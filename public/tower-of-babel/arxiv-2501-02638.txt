Models of hypersurfaces and Bruhat-Tits buildings Thanks: We thank Robert Nowak for helpful and inspiring discussions about this project.
Kletus Stern, Stefan Wewers

Abstract
. We propose a new approach to constructing semistable hypersurface models over a discretely valued field K K . For each GIT-stable hypersurface X / K X/K we define a uniformly continuous “stability function” ϕ X \phi_{X} on the metric Bruhat–Tits building ℬ K \mathcal{B}_{K} of PGL n + 1 ( K ) \mathop{\rm PGL}\nolimits_{n+1}(K) . The minimum locus of this function governs semistable hypersurface models: vertices in this locus are precisely the semistable models, and every rational minimizer becomes such a vertex after a finite extension. In residue characteristic 0 0 this yields a practical solution of semistable reduction via minimization of ϕ X \phi_{X} . More generally, we prove that among all finite extensions L / K L/K the minima of ϕ X \phi_{X} on ℬ L \mathcal{B}_{L} admit a global minimum, giving a new proof of semistable reduction without using GIT. We implement the resulting strategy for plane curves over p p -adic number fields, and in a follow-up article apply it to compute the semistable reduction (in the sense of Deligne and Mumford) of smooth plane quartics.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Models of hypersurfaces with respect to a discrete valuation

Let
K
be a field which is complete with respect to a discrete valuation

v_{K}:K\to\mathbb{Q}\cup\{\infty\}.

We denote by
\mathcal{O}_{K}
the valuation ring of
v_{K}
, fix a prime element
\pi
and let
k=\mathcal{O}_{K}/(\pi)
denote the residue field. We assume that
k
is perfect. We let
\Gamma_{K}:=v_{K}(K^{\times})\subset\mathbb{Q}
denote the valuation group (a discrete subgroup of
(\mathbb{Q},+)
). As long as the field
K
is fixed, we may always assume that
\Gamma_{K}=\mathbb{Z}
.

Let
n,d\geq 1
be positive integers and
V
a
K
-vector space of dimension
n+1
. We let

W:=K[V]_{d}

denote the
K
-vector space of homogeneous polynomials of degree
d
on
V
. We fix a nonzero element
F\in W\backslash\{0\}
. It defines a hypersurface of degree
d
,

X:=V(F)\subset\mathbb{P}(V)\cong\mathbb{P}_{K}^{n},

in projective space of dimension
n
. We wish to study integral models of
X
with respect to the valuation
v_{K}
,
as a hypersurface
.

Given a
coordinate system

\mathcal{E}=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
on
\mathbb{P}(V)
, i.e. a
K
-basis of the dual space
V^{*}
, we can write

F=\sum_{i}a_{i}x_{0}^{i_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{i_{n}},

with
a_{i}\in K
and where
i=(i_{0},\ldots,i_{n})
runs over all tuples with
i_{j}\geq 0
and
i_{0}+\ldots+i_{n}=d
. After multiplication with a suitable constant, we
may assume that the form
F
defining
X
is
primitive
, i.e. that

\min_{i}v_{K}(a_{i})=0.

Then
F\in\mathcal{O}_{K}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
, and the Zariski closure of
X
inside projective
n
-space over
\mathcal{O}_{K}
is the hypersurface defined by
F
,

\mathcal{X}=V(F)\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathcal{O}_{K}}.

We call
\mathcal{X}
a
hypersurface model
of
X
with respect to
v_{K}
. The special fiber of
\mathcal{X}
is the hypersurface of degree
d

\mathcal{X}_{s}=V(\overline{F})\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}_{k},

where
\overline{F}\in k[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d}
is the reduction of
F
. We note that the hypersurface model
\mathcal{X}
, and therefore also its special fiber
\mathcal{X}_{s}
, depends on the choice of the coordinate system
\mathcal{E}
. The problem is to find one such choice which gives the ‘best’ model.

1.2.
Semistable hypersurface models

We assume, from now on, that
d\geq 3
and that the hypersurface
X
is
stable
in the sense of GIT (Geometric Invariant Theory, see
[
20
]
). For instance, this condition holds if
X
is smooth (
[
20
, Proposition 4.2]
).

Definition 1.1
.

A hypersurface model
\mathcal{X}
of
X
is called
stable
(resp.
semistable
) if its special fiber
\mathcal{X}_{s}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}_{k}
is stable (resp. semistable) in the sense of GIT. See §
2.1
for a precise definition. (It is here that we use the assumption that the residue field
k
is perfect.)

Remark 1.2
.

(i)

Our terminology is not consistent with the terminology introduced by Kollár in
[
15
]
. A semistable (resp. stable) model in the sense of Definition
1.1
is semistable (resp. stable) in Kollár’s sense, but the converse is in general false. The semistable models in Kollár’s sense are what we call
minimal models
, see Corollary
1.6
below.

(ii)

If
K
has positive characteristic and has a discrete valuation, then it cannot be perfect. In this case there seems to be a problem with the condition that
X
is stable in the GIT sense, because the usual definition of
(semi)stable
is problematic over a non-perfect field. A pragmatic way out is to use the numerical criterion of Hilbert-Mumford-Kempf (Theorem
2.2
) as a definition for (semi)stability. In fact, throughout this article we will use (semi)stability of hypersurfaces only via this criterion.

In general, no semistable model may exist, but we have the following ‘semistable reduction theorem’.

Theorem 1.3
.

There exists a finite extension
L/K
such that the base change of
X
,
X_{L}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}_{L}
, has a semistable hypersurface model with respect to the unique extension of
v_{K}
to
L
.

Proof.

This is a general feature of GIT. See e.g.
[
19
, Lemma 5.3]
,
[
7
, Proposition 2]
, or
[
16
, Theorem 3.3]
. We will give a rather different proof of this theorem, via Theorem
1.10
below.
∎

Here is the problem motivating this work:

Problem 1.4
.

Given a stable hypersurface
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}_{K}
over a field
K
which is complete with respect to a discrete valuation
v_{K}
, construct a finite extension
L/K
and a semistable hypersurface model
\mathcal{X}
of
X_{L}
.

To our knowledge, no practical algorithm for solving this problem is known, except for
n=1
(binary forms) and
n=2
,
d=3
(plane cubics). Using the results of this article, the first author has developed and implemented an algorithm which solves Problem
1.4
for arbitrary plane curves (
n=2
) of degree
d\geq 3
over
p
-adic number fields.

Consider the case
n=2
. Then
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{K}
is a plane curve and there is a connection to the alternative notion of semistable models of curves in the sense of Deligne and Mumford (
[
9
]
); we shall call such models
geometrically semistable
. For simplicity, assume that
X
is smooth.

For plane cubics (
d=3
) the two definitions of semistability more or less agree: a hypersurface model
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
is semistable in the sense of Definition
1.1
if and only if its special fiber
\mathcal{X}_{s}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{k}
is reduced, irreducible and has at most one ordinary double point as singularity. It is stable if and only if
\mathcal{X}_{s}
is smooth. See
[
20
, §4.2]
. Therefore, a solution to Problem
1.4
is given by the well known procedure to compute the (geometric) semistable reduction of elliptic curves.

For
d\geq 4
, the genus of
X
is
g=(d-1)(d-2)/2\geq 3
and the two definitions of semistability start to diverge. A hypersurface model of
X
which is semistable in the sense of Definition
1.1
is in general not geometrically semistable. By the Semistable Reduction Theorem of Deligne and Mumford, there exists an extension
L/K
and a model
\mathcal{X}
of
X_{L}
which is geometrically semistable; but in general,
\mathcal{X}
is not a hypersurface model, so it doesn’t make sense to apply Definition
1.1
. The precise relation between GIT-semistable plane model and geometric semistable models of plane curves has been studied a lot, see e.g.
[
13
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
29
]
and, most recently,
[
25
]
.

The main motivation for us to study GIT-semistability of plane curves is the hope that it will help to compute the geometric semistable reduction of curves of genus
g\geq 3
. In fact, for non-hyperelliptic curves over local fields of small residue characteristic, this is an open problem in computational arithmetic geometry (
[
30
]
). Building on the results of this article, we solve this problem for smooth plane quartics with non-hyperelliptic reduction, see
[
26
]
.

The goal of this article is to lay the foundation for a new and general approach towards Problem
1.4
. A more detailed analysis of the case of plane curves, and an algorithm solving Problem
1.4
in this case will be given in the first author’s thesis (
[
28
]
). An implementation of this algorithm is available at
[
27
]
. We will give a brief demonstration in §
6.1
.

1.3.
The stability function

The first observation motivating our approach is that the set of isomorphism classes of hypersurface models of
X
can naturally be identified with the set of vertices of the Bruhat-Tits building
\mathcal{B}_{K}
of the group
\mathop{\rm PGL}\nolimits_{n+1}(K)
, defined below.

We let
\mathcal{B}^{0}_{K}
denote the set of homothety classes of full
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-lattices
L\subset V^{*}

(two lattices
L_{1},L_{2}\subset V
are homothetic if there exists
c\in K^{\times}
such that
L_{2}=c\cdot L_{1}
). Then
\mathcal{B}_{K}
is defined as the simplicial complex with underlying vertex set
\mathcal{B}_{K}^{0}
, whose
s
-simplices are the sets of the form
\{[L_{0}],\ldots,[L_{s}]\}
such that

L_{0}\subsetneq L_{1}\subsetneq\ldots\subsetneq L_{s}\subsetneq\pi^{-1}L_{0}.

It is well known that
\mathcal{B}_{K}
is a
euclidean building
1
1

1

Using the traditional definition of
building
, this is true only if
K
is a local field, i.e. if the residue field is finite.
, see e.g.
[
23
, §3]
or
[
21
]
. In particular,
\mathcal{B}_{K}
has an underlying geometric realization which, as a metric space, is the union of euclidean affine spaces of dimension
n
called
apartments
. In the following, we use the letter
\mathcal{B}_{K}
to denote this metric space.

On each apartment
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{B}_{K}
, the set of vertices
\mathcal{A}^{0}=\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{B}_{K}^{0}
forms a
lattice in the underlying affine space. Besides the vertices,
\mathcal{B}_{K}
contains a
distinguished dense subset
\mathcal{B}_{K}(\mathbb{Q})
of
rational points
, which may be
characterized by having rational coordinates with respect to some (equivalently,
any) apartment.

We have defined a hypersurface model
\mathcal{X}
of
X
by the choice of a
K
-basis
\mathcal{E}=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
of

V^{*}=H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n}_{K},\mathcal{O}(1)).

It is easy to see that the isomorphism class of
\mathcal{X}
only depends on the homothety class
[L]\in\mathcal{B}_{K}^{0}
of the lattice

L:=\langle\,x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}\,\rangle_{\mathcal{O}_{K}}.

This defines a natural bijection between the set of isomorphism classes of hypersurface models of
X
with the set
\mathcal{B}_{K}^{0}
.

Kollár (
[
15
]
) and Elsenhans and Stoll (
[
10
]
) study the problem, related to Problem
1.4
, of finding
minimal hypersurface models
of
X
. From our point of view, this amounts to computing the minimum of a certain function

\phi_{X}^{\circ}:\mathcal{B}_{K}^{0}\to\mathbb{Z},

which tracks the valuation of invariants of the hypersurface
X
with respect to the coordinate system defining a model. Our second observation is that this function extends naturally to a continuous function on the full metric space
\mathcal{B}_{K}
with very nice properties.
Using this function, the minimization problem governing semistable reduction is naturally a

continuous
optimization problem on the metric space
\mathcal{B}_{K}
, rather than a discrete
problem on its vertex set.

More precisely, we have the following result.

Proposition 1.5
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}_{K}
be a stable hypersurface of degree
d
. Then there exists a uniformly continuous function

\phi_{X}:\mathcal{B}_{K}\to\mathbb{R},

with the following properties.

(i)

The restriction of
\phi_{X}
to any apartment
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{B}_{K}
(which is an euclidean affine space of dimension
n
) is piecewise affine, convex and radially unbounded. By the latter we mean that for every sequence of points
x_{0},x_{1},\ldots\in\mathcal{A}
we have

d(x_{0},x_{i})\to\infty\quad\Rightarrow\quad\phi_{X}(x_{i})\to\infty.

(ii)

The function
\phi_{X}
achieves a global minimum
m_{X}
in a rational point of
\mathcal{B}_{K}
.

(iii)

Set

M_{X}:=\{b\in\mathcal{B}_{K}\mid\phi_{X}(b)=m_{X}\}.

Let
b\in\mathcal{B}_{K}^{0}
be a vertex, corresponding to the hypersurface model
\mathcal{X}
. Then
\mathcal{X}
is semistable if and only if
b\in M_{X}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{X}
is stable if and only if
M_{X}=\{b\}
over any finite extension
L/K
.

In particular,
X
has a semistable model over
\mathcal{O}_{K}
if and only if the set
M_{X}
contains a vertex of
\mathcal{B}_{K}
.

We call the function
\phi_{X}
the
stability function
associated to the hypersurface
X
.

Assuming a solution to another algorithmic problem (Problem
2.5
), the explicit definition of the stability function
\phi_{X}
leads to an algorithm for finding a rational point
b\in\mathcal{B}_{K}
where
\phi_{X}
attains its minimum
m_{X}

(see §
4.4
). In §
2
we solve this problem for
n=2
. This yields an effective procedure to detect
semistable models of plane curves when they exist.
If no semistable model exists over
K
, the algorithm above does not directly determine
a minimal model. While this can be remedied by a minor modification, it offers little
advantage over existing methods such as those of Elsenhans and Stoll
[
10
]
.

The definition of
\phi_{X}
and the proof of Proposition
1.5
is given in §
4
, and is rather straightforward. The definition of the function
\phi_{X}
on the set of vertices of
\mathcal{B}_{K}
is already suggested by Kollár, and its extension to all of
\mathcal{B}_{K}
is suggested by the identification of
\mathcal{B}_{K}
, as a metric space, with a space of certain valuations, well known by work of Goldman and Iwahori (
[
12
]
). Part (i) of the proposition follows immediately from making this definition explicit. Part (ii) follows from (i) and the fact, essentially proved by Kollár, that
\phi_{X}
is bounded from below. For this argument, and for this argument only, we use Geometric Invariant Theory as a tool.

Part (iii) is again clear from Kollár’s work, as is the following corollary.

Corollary 1.6
.

A semistable model is also minimal.

The converse of Corollary
1.6
is false: by work of Kollár
[

, a minimal hypersurface model always exists, but a semistable model need not exist over

1.5
(iii) shows, the obstruction
to the existence of a semistable model over
K
is
not
the lack of minimality,
but rather the fact that the minimum locus
M_{X}
of the stability function may fail
to contain a vertex. This observation motivates the discussion in the next subsection,
where we study how passing to finite extensions of

M_{X}
.

We record two additional remarks for later reference.

•

If the residue field of
K
is finite, then
\mathcal{B}_{K}
is locally compact and
M_{X}
contains only finitely

X
admits only finitely many non-isomorphic semistable models
(cf.
[

•

If the residue field is infinite, the set

1.4.
Finding the right field extension

The converse of Corollary
1.6
fails because the minimum
locus
M_{X}
of the stability function
\phi_{X}
need not contain a vertex. However,
the proof of Proposition
1.5
shows that
M_{X}
always
contains at least one
rational point
. A key feature of the Bruhat–Tits building
is that every rational point of
\mathcal{B}_{K}
becomes a vertex after passing to a sufficiently
ramified finite extension
L/K
.

More precisely, for any finite extension
L/K
there is a natural isometric embedding

\iota:\mathcal{B}_{K}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{B}_{L},

and for
L/K
sufficiently ramified the image of any rational point of
\mathcal{B}_{K}
is a
vertex of
\mathcal{B}_{L}
.

From the definition of the stability function
\phi_{X}
it is obvious that it is compatible with such base change. It follows that the obstruction to the existence of a semistable model
over
K
is purely
integrality
: the minimum locus
M_{X}
may fail to contain a vertex,
even though it always contains a rational point (Proposition
1.5
(ii)).

In the case of residue characteristic zero, this integrality issue is the only one.

Theorem 1.7
.

Assume that the residue field
k
has characteristic
0
.
Let
b\in\mathcal{B}_{K}(\mathbb{Q})
be a rational point where
\phi_{X}
attains its minimum
m_{X}
.
Let
L/K
be a finite extension such that the image
\iota(b)
is a vertex of
\mathcal{B}_{L}
. Then
\iota(b)
corresponds to a semistable hypersurface model of
X_{L}
over
\mathcal{O}_{L}
.

Equivalently, in residue characteristic zero the problem of constructing a semistable hypersurface model reduces to the explicit minimization of the stability function on the
original
building
\mathcal{B}_{K}
, together with the purely formal step of passing to a finite extension
L/K
that turns a rational minimizer into a vertex.

The proof of Theorem
1.7
combines Proposition
1.5
with the fixed-point theorem for tamely ramified extensions of Rousseau–Prasad (
[
22
]
).

If the residue field
k
has positive characteristic (the case we are mainly interested in) the problem of finding the correct field extension becomes much more difficult. To better understand the nature of the problem, the following terminology is useful. Let us denote by
m_{X,L}
the minimal value of
\phi_{X}
on
\mathcal{B}_{L}
.

Definition 1.8
.

A point
b\in\mathcal{B}_{K}
is called a
stable minimizer

if for every finite extension
L/K
the image
\iota(b)\in\mathcal{B}_{L}
under the natural embedding

\iota:\mathcal{B}_{K}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{B}_{L}

is still a minimizer of
\phi_{X}
on
\mathcal{B}_{L}
, i.e.

\phi_{X}(\iota(b))=m_{X,L}.

The minimal value
m_{X,L}
is then called the
stable minimum
of
\phi_{X}
.

Here is what we can directly deduce from Proposition
1.5
.

Proposition 1.9
.

(i)

Let
L/K
be a finite extension. If
X
has a semistable hypersurface model over
L
then
m_{X,L}
is the stable minimum of
\phi_{X}
.

(ii)

Let
b\in\mathcal{B}_{K}(\mathbb{Q})
be a rational stable minimizer, and let
L/K
be a finite extension such that
\iota(b)\in\mathcal{B}_{L}^{\circ}
is a vertex. Then the hypersurface model of
X
corresponding to
\iota(b)
is semistable.

See §
4.5
for the (easy) proof.
The proposition shows that the existence of a semistable hypersurface model after a suitable field extension (Theorem
1.3
) is equivalent to the following result.

Theorem 1.10
.

The set
\{m_{X,L}\}
, where
L/K
runs over all finite field extensions, has a minimum.

We prove this theorem in §
5
.
What is interesting is that our proof does not use Geometric Invariant Theory. Instead, it combines relatively basic techniques from non-archimedean analysis and polyhedral geometry. Using this novel approach, the first author has devised and implemented a practical algorithm which solves Problem
1.4
for plane curves, even when the residue field
k
has positive characteristic (
[
28
]
,
[
27
]
).

and

for the stabilizer of

. It is known that

is locally closed in the Zariski topology, and that

is a closed subgroup scheme (see

, §9.c]
).

Definition 2.1
.

The hypersurface
X=V_{+}(F)
is called
semistable
if the Zariski closure of the
G
-orbit of
F
does not contain the zero vector,

0\not\in\overline{O(F)}\subset\mathbb{A}(W).

It is called
stable
if the
G
-orbit
O(F)
is closed, and the stabiliser
G_{F}
is finite.

If
X
is semistable, but not stable, we call it
not properly stable
. If it is not semistable, we call it
unstable
.

These definitions only depend on
X
, and are stable under base change of
X
by a field extension
L/K
. It follows that the property of being (semi)stable are geometric, i.e. depend only on the base change of
X
to an algebraic closure of
K
.

2.2.
The numerical criterion

As before, we fix a hypersurface
X=V_{+}(F)
, with
F\in W\backslash\{0\}
. Given a one-parameter subgroup

\lambda:\mathbb{G}_{m}=\mathop{\rm Spec}K[t,t^{-1}]\hookrightarrow G,

the action of
\mathbb{G}_{m}
on
W
induced by
\lambda
corresponds to a decomposition

W=\oplus_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}W_{m}^{\lambda},

where
W_{m}^{\lambda}\subset W
is the eigenspace of the character
\chi_{m}:\mathbb{G}_{m}\to\mathbb{G}_{m}
defined by
\chi_{m}(t)=t^{m}
.
Let
F_{m}\in W_{m}^{\lambda}
denote the
\chi_{m}
-component of
F\in W
. We set

\mu(F,\lambda):=\max\{m\in\mathbb{Z}\mid F_{m}\neq 0\}.

Theorem 2.2

(Hilbert-Mumford-Kempf)
.

The hypersurface
X=V_{+}(F)
is semistable if and only if

\mu(F,\lambda)\geq 0

for all one-parameter subgroups
\lambda
. Moreover,
X
is stable if and only if
\mu(F,\lambda)>0
for all one-parameter subgroups
\lambda
defined over
\overline{K}
.

Proof.

If
K
is algebraically closed, this is the famous result by Mumford, see
[
20
, Chapter 2, §1]
. As Kempf has shown (see
[
14
, Theorem 4.2]
), it suffices to look at the one-parameter subgroups defined over
K
(it is here where we use the assumption that
K
is perfect!).
∎

We wish to make the criterion from Theorem
2.2
more explicit. Let
\lambda:\mathbb{G}_{m}\to G
be a one-parameter subgroup. Then
\lambda
is contained in a maximal torus of
G
. This means that there exists a basis
\mathcal{E}=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
of
V^{*}
and integers
w_{0},\ldots,w_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
w_{0}+\ldots+w_{n}=0
and

(2.1)

\lambda(t)=\begin{pmatrix}t^{w_{0}}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&t^{w_{n}}\end{pmatrix},

where
g=\lambda(t)\in G
is represented as a matrix via the contragredient representation, i.e. we have

\mathchoice{\hphantom{{}^{{{g}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.51294pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.79495pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}}x^{{\kern-7.15878pt{g}\kern 4.53473pt}}_{{\kern-12.68724pt\mathchoice{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.51294pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.79495pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\kern 4.53473pt}}}{\hphantom{{}^{{{g}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.51294pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.79495pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}}x^{{\kern-7.15878pt{g}\kern 4.53473pt}}_{{\kern-12.68724pt\mathchoice{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.51294pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.79495pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\kern 4.53473pt}}}{\hphantom{{}^{{{g}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.51294pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.79495pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}}x^{{\kern-4.70654pt{g}\kern 2.80048pt}}_{{\kern-8.65541pt\mathchoice{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.51294pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.79495pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\kern 2.80048pt}}}{\hphantom{{}^{{{g}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.51294pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.79495pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}}x^{{\kern-3.9064pt{g}\kern 2.00034pt}}_{{\kern-7.85527pt\mathchoice{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.51294pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.79495pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\kern 2.00034pt}}}_{i}=t^{w_{i}}x_{i},\quad i=0,\ldots,n.

Write

(2.2)

F=\sum_{i}a_{i}x_{0}^{i_{0}}\cdots x_{n}^{i_{n}},

with
a_{i}\in K
, and where
i
runs over all tuples
i=(i_{0},\ldots,i_{n})
with
i_{j}\geq 0
and
i_{0}+\ldots+i_{n}=d
.
Set

I_{F,\mathcal{E}}:=\{i\mid a_{i}\neq 0\}.

Then

(2.3)

\mu(F,\lambda)=-\min_{i\in I_{F,\mathcal{E}}}\langle\,i,w\,\rangle,\quad\text{where}\;\langle\,i,w\,\rangle:=i_{0}w_{0}+\ldots+i_{n}w_{n}.

In this way, we obtain the following explicit criterion for a hypersurface to be unstable. We use the following terminology: an element
w=(w_{0},\ldots,w_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
is called a
weight vector
. We call
w

balanced
if
w_{0}+\ldots+w_{n}=0
, and
ordered
if
w_{0}\geq\ldots\geq w_{n}
.

Corollary 2.3
.

The hypersurface
X=V_{+}(F)
of degree
d
is unstable if and only if there exists a basis
\mathcal{E}
of
V^{*}
and a balanced and ordered weight vector
w=(w_{0},\ldots,w_{n})
such that

\forall\,i\in I_{F,\mathcal{E}}:\;\langle\,i,w\,\rangle>0.

Definition 2.4
.

A one-parameter subgroup
\lambda
of
G
is called an
instability
for the hypersurface
X=V_{+}(F)
if
\mu(F,\lambda)<0
. We also call the corresponding pair
(\mathcal{E},w)
from Corollary
2.3
an instability for
X
.

2.3.
Finding instabilities

In Problem
1.4
we start with a hypersurface
X=V_{+}(F)
over a discretely valued field
K
which we assume to be stable. In our approach for finding a semistable model of
X
an essential step is to (a) check whether the special fiber
\mathcal{X}_{s}
of a given hypersurface model
\mathcal{X}
is semistable and (b) if it is not, find an explicit instability for
\mathcal{X}_{s}
. Here
\mathcal{X}_{s}
is a hypersurface defined over the residue field of
K
. Therefore, any solution to Problem
1.4
using our method necessarily depends on a solution to the following problem.

Problem 2.5
.

Given a hypersurface
X=V_{+}(F)\subset\mathbb{P}(V)
of degree
d
, find an explicit instability
\lambda
for
X
, or decide that none exists (i.e. that
X
is semistable).

There seems to exist an extensive literature on the related problem of finding sufficient criteria for
X
to be semistable (see e.g. the recent article
[
18
]
and the references therein), but we are not aware of similar efforts for finding necessary criteria, and explicit instabilities, as demanded by Problem
2.5
.

Nevertheless the case of plane curves (i.e. the case
n=2
) is relatively easy and seems to be well known to experts. For lack of an adaquate reference, we state the main result in a form suitable for our purposes.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
n=2
and
X=V_{+}(F)\subset\mathbb{P}(V)
be a plane curve of degree
d\geq 3
. Assume that
X
is unstable.

(i)

At least one of the following statements holds.

(a)

The curve
X
contains a line
L\subset X
with multiplicity
m>d/3
.

(b)

The curve
X
contains a
K
-rational point
P\in X
with multiplicity
m>2d/3
.

(c)

The curve
X
contains a line
L\subset X
with multiplicity
m>0
. Furthermore, if we write
X=m\cdot L+Y
, with
L\not\subset Y
, then
L
and
Y
intersect in a
K
-rational
P
with intersection multiplicity
>(d-m)/2
.

(d)

The curve
X
contains a
K
-rational point
P\in X
with multiplicity
m>d/2
, and the tangent cone of
X
in
P
contains a line
L
with multiplicity
>m/2
.

(ii)

Assume that Statement (a) holds for a line
L
. Let
\mathcal{E}=(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})
be a coordinate system such that
L=V_{+}(x_{0})
. Then
(\mathcal{E},w)
, with
w:=(2,-1,-1)
, is an instability for
X
.

(iii)

Assume that Statement (b) holds for a point
P
. Let
\mathcal{E}=(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})
be a coordinate system such that
\{P\}=V_{+}(x_{0},x_{1})
. Then
(\mathcal{E},w)
, with
w:=(1,1,-2)
, is an instability for
X
.

(iv)

Assume that Statement (a) and (b) are false. Then there exists a unique pair
(L,P)
with the following properties. Firstly, one of the Statements (c) or (d) hold for
(L,P)
. Secondly, if
\mathcal{E}=(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})
is a coordinate system such that
L=V_{+}(x_{0})
and
\{P\}=V_{+}(x_{0},x_{1})
, then there exists a balanced and ordered weight vector
w
such that
(\mathcal{E},w)
is an instability.

Proof.

(compare with
[
10
, Corollary 7.4]
)
Fix a coordinate system
\mathcal{E}=(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})
and write

(2.4)

F=\sum_{i\in I_{F,\mathcal{E}}}a_{i}x_{0}^{i_{0}}x_{1}^{i_{1}}x_{2}^{i_{2}},\quad\text{with $a_{i}\neq 0$ for all $i\in I_{F,\mathcal{E}}$.}

Let
w=(w_{0},w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
be a balanced and ordered weight vector. Recall that this means that
w_{0}\geq w_{1}\geq w_{2}
and
w_{0}+w_{1}+w_{2}=0
. Then
(\mathcal{E},w)
is an instability if and only if

(2.5)

\langle\,i,w\,\rangle=i_{0}w_{0}+i_{1}w_{1}+i_{2}w_{2}>0,\quad\forall i\in I_{F,\mathcal{E}}.

To prove (i), we assume that
(\mathcal{E},w)
is an instability, i.e. that (
2.5
) holds. We have to show that one of the Statements (a), (b), (c) or (d) holds. For the following case distinction, Figure
2.1
may be helpful.

Assume first that
w_{1}=w_{2}
. Then we may assume
w=(2,-1,-1)
. For
i=(i_{0},i_{1},i_{2})\in I_{F,\mathcal{E}}
the inequality (
2.5
) is equivalent to
2i_{0}-i_{1}-i_{2}>0
. Using
i_{0}+i_{1}+i_{2}=d
we find that

i_{0}>\frac{d}{3},\quad\forall\,i\in I_{F,\mathcal{E}}.

By (
2.4
) this means that
x_{0}^{m}\mid F
, for some
m>d/3
. In other words, the line
L:=V_{+}(x_{0})
is a component of
X
of multiplicity
m>d/3
. This is Statement (a).

Next we assume
w_{0}=w_{1}
. Then we may assume
w=(1,1,-2)
. For
i=(i_{0},i_{1},i_{2})\in I_{F,\mathcal{E}}
the inequality (
2.5
) is equivalent to

i_{0}+i_{1}>\frac{2d}{3}.

This means that
F\in(x_{0},x_{1})^{m}
, for some
m>2d/3
; in other words,
P:=[0:0:1]
is a point of
X
of multiplicity
m>2d/3
. This is Statement (b).

For the rest of the proof of (i) we may assume
w_{0}>w_{1}>w_{2}
. Assume in addition that
w_{1}\leq 0
. Write
F=x_{0}^{m}G
such that
x_{0}\nmid G
. Then
m
is the multiplicity of the line
L:=V_{+}(x_{0})
in
X
. If
m>d/3
then we are in Case (a). So
m\leq d/3
. Let
k
be minimal with the property that the monomial

occurs in
G
. Then
0\leq k\leq d-m
,

, and
k
is the intersection multiplicity of
L
with
G:=V_{+}(G)
in the point

. From (
2.5
) we obtain the inequality

(2.6)

mw_{0}+kw_{1}+(d-m-k)w_{2}>0.

If we assume that
m=0
, then (
2.6
), together with the assumptions
w_{0}>w_{1}>w_{2}
,
w_{1}\leq 0
and
0\leq k\leq d
, leads to a contradiction. It follows that
m>0
. We claim that
k>(d-m)/2
.

To prove the claim, we write
k=(d-m)/2+l
. Then (
2.6
), together with

, yields

\begin{split}0&<mw_{0}+\frac{d-m}{2}w_{1}+\frac{d-m}{2}w_{2}+(w_{1}-w_{2})l\\

&=-\frac{d-3m}{2}w_{0}+(w_{1}-w_{2})l.\end{split}

Using the assumptions
w_{1}>w_{2}
and

, we conclude from (

l>\frac{(d-3m)w_{0}}{2(w_{1}-w_{2})}\geq 0.

This proves the claim

and shows that Statement (c) holds.

Finally, we assume
w_{1}>0
. Then
w_{2}=-(w_{0}+w_{1})<0
. Therefore the monomial
x_{2}^{d}
does no occur in
F
(i.e.
(0,0,1)\not\in I_{F,\mathcal{E}}
), and so the point

lies on
X
. Let
m>0
be the multiplicity of
P
as a point on
X
. If
m>2d/3
we are in Case (b), so we may assume
m\leq 2d/3
.

Let
k
be minimal such that the monomial
x_{0}^{k}x_{1}^{m-k}x_{2}^{d-m}
occurs in
F
, where

. Then
k
is the multiplicity of the line
L:=V_{+}(x_{0})
in the tangent cone of
X
in
P
. Since
(k,m-k,d-m)\in I_{F,\mathcal{E}}
, (
2.5
) gives the inequality

(2.8)

kw_{0}+(m-k)w_{1}+(d-m)w_{2}>0.

We have to show that
m>d/2
and
k>m/2
, i.e. that Statement (d) holds.

Using

we can rewrite (
2.8
) as

(2.9)

k(w_{0}-w_{1})>(d-m)w_{0}+(d-2m)w_{1}.

Using
w_{0}>w_{1}
and
k\leq m
we obtain from (

) the inequality

(2.10)

m(w_{0}-w_{1})\geq k(w_{0}-w_{1})>(d-m)w_{0}+(d-2m)w_{1}.

Reordering (
2.10
) gives

(2.11)

(2m-d)w_{0}>(d-m)w_{1}.

Since

and

, (
2.11
) implies

, as claimed. On the other hand, using the assumption

and

(2.12)

k(w_{0}-w_{1})>(d-\frac{2d}{3})w_{0}+(d-\frac{4d}{3})w_{1}=\frac{d}{3}(w_{0}-w_{1}).

, (

) implies

This completes the proof of Part (i) of the Proposition.

For the proof of (ii) we assume that
L=V_{+}(x_{0})
and that

, for some

, with

Remark 2.7
.

Proposition
2.6
yields a practical algorithm which solves Problem

for

.

Here is a rough outline of the algorithm. Given a plane curve

, it is easy to check whether one of the Statements (a), (b), (c) or (d) holds. Also, there are at most finitely many lines
L
, points
P
, or pairs
(L,P)
for which this is true.

In each case where
L
or
P
as above exist, we choose a coordinate system
\mathcal{E}=(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})

) translates into a system of linear inequalities for

for

, or it doesn’t. In the latter case, the conclusion of Proposition

fails, which shows that

is semistable.

whose definition we recall below,
takes negative values. The
stability function
we define in the next section, and which is the main actor of this article, is a natural generalization of the function
\nu(F,\,\cdot\,)
to the case where the field

is equipped with a nontrivial discrete valuation and the spherical complex

is replaced by the Bruhat-Tits building of

. Although we do not use the complex

and the function

explicitly in the rest of this article, it is used implicitly in the proof of Proposition

Given a reductive algebraic group
G
,
the
spherical complex

is a certain simplicial complex whose simplices are the proper parabolic subgroups of
G
. In particular, the vertices correspond to the maximal parabolic subgroups. To each one-parameter subgroup
\lambda
of
G
, we can associate a certain point
\delta=[\lambda]\in\Delta(G)
. Points of this type are called
rational
. They form a dense subset of
\Delta(G)
which contains all vertices. See
[
20
, §2.2]
or
[
8
]
.

The general definition of
\delta=[\lambda]
in
[
8
]
is roughly the following. Let
T\subset G
be a maximal split torus. It defines a subcomplex
A_{T}\subset\Delta(G)
corresponding to the parabolic subgroups containing
T
, called an
apartment
. One identifies the geometric realization of the complex
A_{T}
with the set of all rays in the real vector space
X_{*}(T)\otimes\mathbb{R}
. Then
\delta=[\lambda]
is defined as the rays spanned by
\lambda\in X_{*}(T)
, where
T
is some maximal split torus containing the image of
\lambda
.

Here we only consider the case

. Then

is actually a bipartite graph with the following very simple description, see e.g.
[

or
[

The vertices of

of the first kind correspond to lines

, and the vertices of the second kind correspond to
K
-rational points

. Two vertices corresponding to a line
L
and a point

are joint by a unique edge if and only if
P\in L
.

Let
\mathcal{E}=(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})
be a coordinate system and let

be the line
x_{i}=0
, for

. Then the six vertices corresponding to the three lines

and their points of intersection

span a circular subgraph

of

associated to

, see Figure

. It is easy to see that

only depends on the maximal split torus of

defined by
\mathcal{E}
. Also, the building
\Delta(G)
is the union of all these apartments

.

Let

be a one-parameter subgroup corresponding to a pair

, as in (

lies on the apartment

and is determined as follows. If

is completely determined by the (degenerate) flag

Indeed, the stabilizer of this flag is the unique maximal parabolic subgroup of

containing the image of

. In terms of the above description of

as a bipartite graph, this means that

If

Proof.

Let
n=2
and
X=V_{+}(F)\subset\mathbb{P}(V)
be a plane curve of degree
d\geq 3
. We assume that
X
is unstable, and that Statements (a) and (b) of Proposition
2.6
(i) are both false.

By Theorem
2.2
, the subset

C_{F}:=\{\delta\in\Delta(G)\mid\nu(F,\delta)<0\}

of
\Delta(G)
is nonempty. We claim that
C_{F}
does not contain any vertex of
\Delta(G)
. To see this, assume first that
C_{F}
contains a vertex corresponding to a line
L\subset\mathbb{P}(V)
. In the above description of the points of
\Delta(G)
, this vertex is represented by an instability
(\mathcal{E},w)
with
w_{1}=w_{2}
. By the proof of Proposition
2.6
(i) this shows that Statement (a) holds for the line
L:=V_{+}(x_{0})
, contrary to our assumption. A very similar argument, using our exclusion of Statement (b), shows that
C_{F}
also cannot contain vertices corresponding to
K
-rational points of
\mathbb{P}(V)
. This concludes the proof of the claim.

By
[
20
, Corollary 2.16]
,
C_{F}
is
convex
, in the sense of
[
20
, Definition 2.10]
. In our case this means that the intersection of
C_{F}
with any apartment
A_{\mathcal{E}}
is an open interval which does not contain any pair of opposite points. Since any two points of
\Delta(G)
are contained in a common apartment, this also implies that
C_{F}
is connected.
As we have shown above,
C_{F}
does not contain any vertices. We conclude that
C_{F}
is an open interval contained in the interior of a unique edge of
\Delta(G)
.

Let
e
be an arbitrary edge of
\Delta(G)
, and let
(L,P)
be the pair corresponding to
e
. Choose any coordinate system
\mathcal{E}=(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})
such that

and
\{P\}=V_{+}(x_{0},x_{1})
. Then each rational point
\delta
in the interior of

is represented by
(\mathcal{E},w)
, where

is a balanced and and ordered weight vector with

. Furthermore,
\delta\in C_{F}
if and only if
(\mathcal{E},w)
is an instability for
X
. And if this is the case, then the proof of Part (i) of Proposition
2.6
shows that one of the Statements (c) or (d) holds for

and

. We see that Proposition
2.6
(iv) is equivalent to the claim that the subset
C_{F}
is contained in the interior of a unique edge

of
\Delta(G)
, and this is exactly what we have shown above.
∎

Assume that

3.
Valuations and buildings

In this section we define the Bruhat-Tits building
\mathcal{B}_{K}
mentioned in the introduction as a space of homothety classes of valuations on the
K
-vector space
V^{*}
, following the work of Goldman and Iwahori (
[
12
]
).

All this is well known (see e.g.
[
23
, §3]
,
[
24
, §2.2]
,
[
21
]
,
[
2
, §1]
), so the purpose of this section is to fix our notation and to highlight the properties of
\mathcal{B}_{K}
which are most relevant for us. We adopt the following conventions and assumptions, which differ from those in some of the articles referenced above:

•

We systematically use (additive) valuations instead of (multiplicative) norms.

•

We assume that
K
is discretely valued; this implies that it is spherically complete (see e.g.
[
4
, §2.4.4]
). As a result, we do not have to distinguish between diagonalizable and non-diagonalizable valuations (resp. norms). This considerably simplifies our notation relative to the reference given above.

•

On the other hand, we do not assume that
K
is a local field. As a result, the building
\mathcal{B}_{K}
is in general not locally compact (with respect to the metric topology), making it harder to prove finiteness results.

•

The projective space
\mathbb{P}(V)
is defined as the projective scheme
{\rm Proj}\,K[V]
, so that
H^{0}(\mathbb{P}(V),\mathcal{O}(1))=V^{*}
. As a result, we will mostly work with the vector space
V^{*}
, and not
V
. This is different from the convention used in
[
23
]
, but agrees with
[
2
]
.

3.1.

Let

be a complete discretely valued field. We write

is an
affine Coxeter complex, of type
A_{n}
. In particular,
\mathbb{E}_{n}
is the geometric realization of a certain simplicial complex for which the lattice
\Lambda_{K}
is the set of vertices. See e.g
[
1
]
.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{E}=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
be a basis of
V^{*}
, and
w=(w_{0},\ldots,w_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. We denote by
v_{\mathcal{E},w}
the valuation

v_{\mathcal{E},w}:V^{*}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\},\quad x=\sum_{i}a_{i}x_{i}\mapsto\min\{v_{K}(a_{i})+w_{i}\mid i=0,\ldots,n\}.

We say that a valuation
v
on
V^{*}
is
diagonalized
by the basis
\mathcal{E}
if
v=v_{\mathcal{E},w}
for some
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

We recall the following well known fact.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
v,v^{\prime}
be two valuations on
V^{*}
. Then there exists a basis of
V^{*}
which simultaneously diagonalizes
v
and
v^{\prime}
.

Proof.

This is a special case of
[
21
, Cor. 3.11]
. In case that
K
is a local field, a more direct proof is given in
[
24
, Proposition 2.21]
.
∎

For any basis
\mathcal{E}
of
V^{*}
we obtain an injective map

\tilde{f}_{\mathcal{E}}:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{N}_{K},\quad w\mapsto v_{\mathcal{E},w},

and one easily checks that it induces an injective map

f_{\mathcal{E}}:\mathbb{E}_{n}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{B}_{K},\quad[w]\mapsto[v_{\mathcal{E},w}]

The images

\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{E}}:=f_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{E}_{n})\subset\mathcal{B}_{K}

are called the
apartments
of
\mathcal{B}_{K}
. The map
f_{\mathcal{E}}
is called a
marked apartment
. By Proposition
3.4
, any two points on
\mathcal{B}_{K}
lie on a common apartment. In particular,
\mathcal{B}_{K}
is the union of all its apartments.

Remark 3.5
.

(i)

The set
\mathcal{B}_{K}
, equipped with the family of marked apartments
f_{\mathcal{E}}
, is an
affine building of type
(\mathbb{E}_{n},W_{K})
, i.e. it satisfies the axioms (A 1-4) and (A 5’) from
[
21
, §1.2]
. This is proved in
[
21
, §3]
.

(ii)

It follows from (i) and
[
21
, Proposition 1.3]
that there is a unique metric
d:\mathcal{B}_{K}\times\mathcal{B}_{K}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
on
\mathcal{B}_{K}
which restricts to the euclidean metric on each apartment induced by the isomorphism
f_{\mathcal{E}}:\mathbb{E}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{E}}
. We will henceforth consider
\mathcal{B}_{K}
as a metric space with this metric.

(iii)

If
K
is a local field, then
\mathcal{B}_{K}
is locally compact with respect to the topology induced by the metric
d
. But if the residue field
k
is not finite, this is not true anymore.

(iv)

It is clear from the definitions that

f_{\mathcal{E}}(\Lambda_{K})=\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{E}}\cap\mathcal{B}_{K}^{\circ}.

So via
f_{\mathcal{E}}
the vertices of the simplicial complex
(\mathbb{E}_{n},W_{K})
correspond to the vertices of
\mathcal{B}_{K}
, as defined in Definition
3.1
. Explicitly, if
[v]\in\mathcal{B}_{K}^{\circ}
is a vertex, then

L_{v}:=\{x\in V^{*}\mid v(x)\geq 0\}

is a full
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-lattice of
V^{*}
. Its homothety class only depends on the point
[v]
.

(v)

The simplicial structure on
(\mathbb{E}_{n},W_{K})
induces, via the marked apartments
f_{\mathcal{E}}
, a simplicial structure on
\mathcal{B}_{K}
. By (iv) the vertices of this simplicial structure are precisely the vertices of
\mathcal{B}_{K}
from Definition
3.1
. It is then easy to verify (see e.g.
[
1
, §V.8]
or
[
24
, §2.2]
) that we recover the simplicial complex with vertex set
\mathcal{B}_{K}^{\circ}
defined in §
1.3
.

3.3.
Extending the base field

Let
L/K
be a finite field extension. Then the valuation
v_{K}
extends to a unique, discrete valuation
v_{L}
on
L
with respect to which
L
is also complete. We obtain an inclusion
\Gamma_{K}\subset\Gamma_{L}
of discrete subgroups of
\mathbb{R}
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
L/K
be a finite field extension and
v\in\mathcal{N}_{K}
. Consider the map
\tilde{v}:V_{L}^{*}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
defined by

\tilde{v}(x)=\sup\;\min_{i}(v_{L}(a_{i})+v(x_{i})),

where the supremum is defined over all representations
x=\sum_{i}a_{i}x_{i}
with
a_{i}\in L
and
x_{i}\in V
.
Then
\tilde{v}\in\mathcal{N}_{L}
and
\tilde{v}|_{V^{*}}=v
. Moreover, if
\mathcal{E}=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
is a
K
-basis of
V^{*}
diagonalizing
v
, then we have

\tilde{v}(a_{0}x_{0}+\ldots+a_{n}x_{n})=\big(\min_{i}v_{L}(a_{i})+v(x_{i})\big),

for all
(a_{i})\in L^{n+1}
.

Proof.

This is clear.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

The proposition defines an injection
\mathcal{N}_{K}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{N}_{L}
,
v\mapsto\tilde{v}
, which descends to an injection

(3.2)

\iota:\mathcal{B}_{K}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{B}_{L}.

It has the following properties.

(i)

It is equivariant with respect to the left actions of the groups
\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits(V)
and
\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits(V_{L})
.

(ii)

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a
K
-basis of
V^{*}
, and let
\mathcal{E}_{L}
denote
\mathcal{E}
considered as an
L
-basis of
V_{L}^{*}
. Then

\iota\circ f_{\mathcal{E}}=f_{\mathcal{E}_{L}}.

(iii)

It follows from (ii) that the map
\iota
is a metric embedding.

(iv)

It induces an injective map
\mathcal{B}_{K}^{\circ}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{B}_{L}^{\circ}
; on each apartment
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{B}_{K}
, it corresponds to the natural inclusion of lattices

\Lambda_{K}\cong\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{B}_{K}\hookrightarrow\iota(\mathcal{A})\cap\mathcal{B}_{L}\cong\Lambda_{L}.

(v)

If
L/K
is a Galois extension then
\mathop{\rm Gal}\nolimits(L/K)
acts on
\mathcal{B}_{L}
via simplicial isometries, and this action commutes with the embedding
\iota
.

All this justifies to consider
\mathcal{B}_{K}
as a ‘sub-building’ of
\mathcal{B}_{L}
via
\iota
, with the caveat of (iv): with respect to the simplicial structure,
\iota(\mathcal{B}_{K})
is a refinement of
\mathcal{B}_{K}
.

The stability function

We continue with the notation and assumptions of the previous section. So

is a
K

and its quotient space, the Bruhat-Tits building

We also consider a homogenous form

4.1.

4.4.
Finding the minimum

Assume that we have a solution of Problem
2.5
at our disposal.
Then the proof of Proposition

of the stability function

on

where this minimum is attained. Here is a brief sketch of this algorithm. Full details will be given in

, and an implementation is available in

.

One starts with an arbitrary apartment

and determines the subset

by linear optimization, using

is an explicit piecewise affine and convex function

(i)).
One then chooses a rational point

and (formally) passes to a finite
ramified extension

such that
\iota(b)\in\mathcal{B}_{L}
becomes a vertex, in order to compute the special fiber
\mathcal{X}_{b,s}
of the hypersurface model
\mathcal{X}_{b}
.
4
4

4

It is not necessary to actually choose and work with the extension

. One can compute the special fiber
\mathcal{X}_{b,s}
– which is independent of the choice of

– using the concept of

(
[
28
]
).

Using a solution to Problem

, one can now decide whether

\mathcal{X}_{b}
is semistable. If this is the case, the algorithm terminates, since

already
lies in
M_{X}
. Otherwise, an explicit instability of
\mathcal{X}_{b,s}
is produced.
Such an instability corresponds canonically to a point of the spherical building, which may be
identified with a direction in the tangent cone of
\mathcal{B}_{K}
at

along which

\phi_{X}
strictly decreases (see the proof of Lemma
4.7
). This direction determines a new apartment

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{B}_{K}
containing
b
on which the minimum of
\phi_{X}
is strictly smaller
than on
\mathcal{A}
.

One then replaces
\mathcal{A}
by
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
and repeats the procedure.
The algorithm must terminate after finitely many steps, since
\phi_{X}
is bounded
from below and the successive improvements of the minimum occur in a discrete set
(Lemma
4.6
).

Remark 4.8
.

As described above, the algorithm does not determine the full set
M_{X}\subset\mathcal{B}_{K}
where
\phi_{X}
achieves its minimum. If the residue field
k
is finite – and hence
K
is locally compact – an easy improvement of the above algorithm can do this. But in general, this is a delicate issue, as the set
M_{X}
may not be contained in any finite union of apartments. See §

for an explicit example illustrating this phenomenon.

4.5.
The tame case

In general, the algorithm from §
4.4
gives only a partial solution to Problem
1.4
. More precisely, if
X
has a semistable model over
\mathcal{O}_{K}
then the algorithm finds it – it corresponds to a vertex of
\mathcal{B}_{K}
lying inside the set
M_{X}
. If the set
M_{X}
contains no vertex, then no semistable model exists over
K
, and we have to pass to a suitable finite extension
L/K
. If
L/K
is sufficiently ramified, then
M_{X}
contains a point which becomes a vertex in
\mathcal{B}_{L}
. But this vertex may not be a minimum of the stability function on the larger building
\mathcal{B}_{L}
, and we have to continue with the algorithm over the field
L
. It is not obvious how to choose the extension
L/K
such that the process of choosing successive extensions terminates.

We are going to show that this is not a problem if
X
has a semistable model over an extension
L/K
which is at most tamely ramified (see Theorem
1.7
from the introduction). In particular, if the residue characteristic of
K
is zero, then our first algorithm completely solves Problem
1.4
(assuming we have a solution for Problem
2.5
). The general case is much more involved and discussed further in §
5
below.

We recall the terminology introduced in the introduction.
A point

at which the stability function

attains its minimum

is called a

if, for every finite extension
L/K
,
its image
\iota(b)\in\mathcal{B}_{L}
is still a minimizer of
\phi_{X}
. Then
m_{X,K}
is called the
stable minimum
of
\phi_{X}
.
By Proposition
1.9
(i), the existence of a semistable hypersurface
model over a finite extension
L/K
implies that
m_{X,L}
is the
stable minimum of
\phi_{X}
. Part (ii) of the proposition states that a rational stable minimizer which becomes a vertex after a finite extension

corresponds to a semistable model of
X
over
L
.

We briefly indicate the proof of Proposition
1.9
,
since it will be used repeatedly in the sequel.

Proof.

(i) Let
X_{L}
have a semistable hypersurface model over
\mathcal{O}_{L}
, corresponding to a vertex

. Then
\phi_{X}(v)=m_{X,L}
by Proposition

For any finite extension

, the base change of a semistable model is again semistable
(GIT semistability of the special fiber is preserved under base change), hence the image of

\mathcal{B}_{L^{\prime}}
is again a vertex in the minimum locus of
\phi_{X}
on
\mathcal{B}_{L^{\prime}}
.
Therefore

, we get

for all

.

(ii) By definition,
\iota(b)
is a minimizer of
\phi_{X}
on
\mathcal{B}_{L}
. Since it is a vertex, the
corresponding hypersurface model is semistable by Proposition

∎

Lemma 4.9
.

Let

be a finite Galois extension, with Galois group

. Then

The action of

preserves

.
Moreover,

is closed and convex because every geodesic segment in

The lemma follows now from the Bruhat-Tits Fixed-Point Theorem (

Theorem 4.10
.

Assume that
X
has a semistable hypersurface model
over a tamely ramified finite extension.
Then every minimizer

of

Proof.

Let

5.
Existence of a stable minimum

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.10
, which asserts
the existence of a global minimum

\min_{L/K}m_{X,L}

of the stability function
\phi_{X}
among all finite
extensions
L/K
. As a direct consequence, we obtain a new proof of the semistable
reduction theorem (Theorem
1.3
).

Our proof does not use Geometric Invariant Theory. Instead, it relies on two elementary
ingredients: a variant of the maximum principle from non-archimedean analysis
(Lemma
5.1
), and a combinatorial lemma from polyhedral
geometry (Lemma
5.2
). These results form the theoretical basis
for the second stage of our approach, which ensures the existence of a field extension
over which the stability function attains a stable minimum. When combined with the
algorithm from §
4.4
, this leads to a complete and practical
algorithm for solving Problem
1.4
for plane curves; see

[
28
]
for details and
[
27
]
for an implementation.

Let

This is

d\geq 3
. Recall the definition of the linear forms

l_{i}(w):=\frac{d}{n+1}(w_{0}+\ldots+w_{n})-(i_{0}w_{0}+\ldots+i_{n}w_{n})

on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, for
i=(i_{0},\ldots,i_{n})\in I_{d}
, introduced in §
4.3
.

Lemma 5.2
.

There exist nonnegative integers
e_{i,k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, for
i\in I_{d}
and
k=1,\ldots,N
, and a positive integer
e>0
such that the following holds:

(i)

For a tuple
(t_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{d}}
,

(5.1)

t_{i}\geq l_{i}(w)

holds for all
i\in I_{d}
and some
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, independent of
i
, if and only if

\sum_{i\in I_{d}}e_{i,k}t_{i}\geq 0,\quad k=1,\ldots,N.

(ii)

For all
k
,

e=\sum_{i\in I_{d}}e_{i,k}.

Proof.

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{I_{d}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be the set of tuples
(t,w)
such that (
5.1
) holds for all
i\in I_{d}
. Let

Q:=p(P)\subset\mathbb{R}^{I_{d}}
be the image of
P
under the projection
p:\mathbb{R}^{I_{d}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{I_{d}}
. Then
(t_{i})\in Q
if and only if (
5.1
) holds for all
i\in I_{d}
and some
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, independent of
i
.

As
P
is defined by finitely many rational linear inequalities, it is a rational polyhedron (
[
31
, Lecture 1]
). It is shown in §1.2 of
loc.cit.
that
Q
is again a rational polyhedron and therefore defined by linear inequalities

\sum_{i\in I_{d}}e_{i,k}t_{i}\geq 0,\quad k=1,\ldots,N,

with
e_{i,k}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that for all
k
there exists at least one
i\in\ I_{d}
such that
e_{i,k}\neq 0
. Looking at the inequalities (
5.1
) defining
Q
we see that for
(t_{i})\in Q
and
(t_{i}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{d}}
with
t_{i}\leq t_{i}^{\prime}
for all
i
we also have
(t_{i}^{\prime})\in Q
. This implies that
e_{i,k}\geq 0
, for all
i,k
.

Set

e_{k}:=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{d}}e_{i,k},\quad k=1,\ldots,N

and
e:={\rm lcm}_{k}(e_{k})
. Clearly
e_{k},e>0
. After multiplying
e_{i,k}
by
e/e_{k}
(which does not affect the validity of (i)), (ii) holds as well.
∎

5.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.10

In this section, we regard the group

as an algebraic group over

. Thus,

G=\mathop{\rm Spec}A,\quad A=K[g_{\mu,\nu}\mid 0\leq\mu,\nu\leq n]/(\det(g_{\mu,\nu})-1).

We fix a homogeneous form

\mathchoice{\hphantom{{}^{{{g}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.51294pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.79495pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}}F^{{\kern-8.772pt{g}\kern 6.14795pt}}_{{\kern-14.30046pt\mathchoice{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.51294pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.79495pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\kern 6.14795pt}}}{\hphantom{{}^{{{g}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.51294pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.79495pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}}F^{{\kern-8.772pt{g}\kern 6.14795pt}}_{{\kern-14.30046pt\mathchoice{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.51294pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.79495pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\kern 6.14795pt}}}{\hphantom{{}^{{{g}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.51294pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.79495pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}}F^{{\kern-5.7376pt{g}\kern 3.83153pt}}_{{\kern-9.68646pt\mathchoice{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.51294pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.79495pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\kern 3.83153pt}}}{\hphantom{{}^{{{g}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.51294pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.79495pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}}F^{{\kern-4.64287pt{g}\kern 2.7368pt}}_{{\kern-8.59174pt\mathchoice{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.15245pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.51294pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.79495pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\kern 2.7368pt}}}:=F\circ g^{-1}=\sum_{i\in I_{d}}\tilde{a}_{i}(g)x_{0}^{i_{0}}\cdots x_{n}^{i_{n}}\in L[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d}.

Then the functions

are polynomials over

in the coefficients

of

as elements of the Hopf algebra

of

.

Set

f_{k}:=\prod_{i\in I_{d}}\tilde{a}_{i}^{e_{i,k}}\in A,\quad k=1,\ldots,N,

where

are the nonnegative integers from Lemma

. By Part (ii) of this lemma,

is positive and independent from

. We consider the function

We fix a basis

of

and let

. Let

. Then

defining the apartment

of

. Let

attains on

Lemma 5.3
.

For

as above we have

Proof.

In order to prove the lemma, it suffices to show that

By Lemma

(i), the restriction of

to

is given by the formula

\phi_{X}(w)=\max_{i\in I_{d}}\big(l_{i}(w)-v_{g}(\tilde{a}_{i})\big),

where

and the linear form

is defined by (

if and only if there exists some

such that the inequalities

hold. In other words, if the tuple

\sum_{i\in I_{d}}e_{i,k}t_{i}\geq 0,\quad k=1,\ldots,N.

By the definition of the functions

We claim that the function

To prove the claim, we would like to apply Lemma

to a certain affinoid domain of

.

We set

As the

is an affinoid subdomain, of the form

Let

This group is the stabilizer of the vertex

Let

and consider the apartment

. Let

be a point in which the minimum

of

in

is attained. Let
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{B}_{K}
be an apartment which contains both

and

. We claim that there exists a

such that
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=g^{\prime}(\mathcal{A}_{0})
.

To prove the claim, we write

is a vertex of

. After multiplying the elements of

with suitable constants, we may assume that

. But now

which means that

and therefore

by Lemma

.

6.
Examples

We study three explicit examples of smooth plane curves over a complete and discretely valued non-archimedean field
K
. These examples are chosen to illustrate a few salient points of our approach, while being relatively simple. We also use this opportunity to demonstrate the implementation from
[
27
]
. The common theme is that we try to understand the exact relationship between two related problems: on the one hand the computation of a semistable plane model of a plane curve (Problem
1.4
for
n=2
) and on the other hand the computation of a geometric semistable model (see e.g.
[
30
]
for a recent survey of the state of the art, and
[
26
]
for a solution in case of plane quartics).

In all three examples we assume that the residue field
k
of
K
is algebraically closed. If
X
is a smooth projective curve over
K
of genus
g\geq 2
, then there exists a minimal finite extension
L/K
over which
X
has geometric semistable reduction. As we will see with our second example, the analogous statement for plane curves and GIT-semistable plane model does not hold.

6.1.
A cubic with potentially good reduction

Let

be the maximal unramified extension of the field of

A Ciani quartic over

We consider the plane quartic

It is smooth and has two commuting involutions

\sigma^{*}:(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto(x_{2},x_{1},x_{0}),\quad\tau^{*}:(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto(x_{0},-x_{1},x_{2}).

[

]

We will show that

We use a similar approach and notation as in §

, but give less details. In particular, we will not write down the formulae for the stability function.

. In particular, the point
u_{3}=(0,1/2,0)\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{E}_{2}}\subset\mathcal{B}_{L}
is the unique point where the stability function achieves its global minimum
-3/2
.

The only property of
L
that we have used for the construction of the model
\mathcal{X}_{3}
is that it contains an element
\alpha
with
v_{L}(\alpha^{2}+2)\geq 2
. Among the three ramified quadratic extensions of
K=\mathbb{Q}_{2}^{\scriptstyle\rm nr}
, two have this property (namely
K_{1}:=K(\sqrt{2})
and
K_{2}:=K(\sqrt{-2})
), but the third,
K_{3}:=K(\sqrt{-1})
, has not. One computes that the global minimum of the stability function on
\mathcal{B}_{K_{3}}
is equal to
-1/2
, and is not achieved in a vertex. We conclude that the curve
X
has no plane semistable model over the extension
K_{3}/K
.

Note that the model
\mathcal{X}_{3}
is not
geometrically
semistable because the special fiber
\mathcal{X}_{3,s}
has two cusps as singularities. Assume that the extension
L/K
has been chosen sufficiently large so that
X
has geometric semistable reduction over
L
. Let
\mathcal{X}
be the stable model (in the sense of Deligne and Mumford) of
X
over
\mathcal{O}_{L}
. Then the main result of
[
25
]
predicts the following:

(i)

The geometric stable model
\mathcal{X}
dominates the plane stable model
\mathcal{X}_{3}
, i.e. there exists a (unique) morphism
\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}_{3}
of
\mathcal{O}_{L}
-schemes extending the identity on the generic fiber
X
.

(ii)

The special fiber
\mathcal{X}_{s}
has three irreducible components
Z_{1},Z_{2},Z_{3}
. The curve
Z_{1}
is semistable of geometric genus zero and has a single ordinary double point. The curves
Z_{2},Z_{3}
are semistable curves of genus one, and each intersects
Z_{1}
in a unique point (i.e.
Z_{2},Z_{3}
are so-called
1
-tails
).

(iii)

The induced map
\mathcal{X}_{s}\to\mathcal{X}_{3,s}
restricts to a homeomorphism
Z_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}\mathcal{X}_{3,s}
, and contracts the components
Z_{2}
,
Z_{3}
to the cusps
P_{2},P_{3}\in\mathcal{X}_{3,s}
.

In our follow-up article
[
26
, §5.1]
we show how to compute the extension
L/K
and the modification
\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}_{3}
giving rise to the stable model
\mathcal{X}
explicitly. The outcome in this particular example is the stable model
\mathcal{X}
can be defined over a Galois extension
L/K
of degree
24
. The two
1
-tails
Z_{2},Z_{3}
are both isomorphic to the smooth cubic in Weierstrass normal form

y^{2}+y=x^{3}.

The Galois group of
L/K
acts naturally and faithfully on
\mathcal{X}_{s}
. This action is trivial on the strict transform of
\mathcal{X}_{3,s}
, fixes the two tails
Z_{2},Z_{3}
and acts on each of them as the full automorphism group (which is isomorphic to
\mathop{\rm SL}\nolimits_{2}(3)
).

A slightly inconvenient observation here is that the extension
L/K
described above does not contain any of the two quadratic extensions
K_{1}=\mathbb{Q}_{2}(\sqrt{2})
or
K_{2}=\mathbb{Q}_{2}(\sqrt{-2})
of
K
. So if we first compute the GIT-stable model
\mathcal{X}_{3}
over one of these quadratic extensions, say
K_{1}
, then we need a further extension
L_{1}/K_{1}
of degree
24
to obtain geometric semistable reduction. The extension
L_{1}/K
then has degree
48
, which is not minimal. This shows that our two-step strategy (first a GIT-semistable plane model, then the
geometric stable model) need not produce a minimal extension.

6.3.
A quartic with infinitely many semistable models

Let
K
be a field which is complete with respect to a discrete valuation
v_{K}
, and assume that the residue field
k
of
v_{K}
is infinite. We will give an example of a smooth plane quartic over
K
which has infinitely many nonisomorphic semistable models. This realizes concretely the phenomenon mentioned at the end of §
1.3
.

Let
\pi
denote a prime element of
K
, and let
G\in\mathcal{O}_{K}[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}]_{4}
be a form of degree
4
with integral coefficients. We set

X:=V_{+}(F),\quad F:=(x_{0}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2})^{2}+\pi^{5}G.

One can show that we can choose
G
such that
X
is smooth. For instance, if
{\rm char}(k)\neq 2
, then it suffices that the conic
Q
meets the quartic
G=0
in
8
distinct geometric points (see e.g.
[
16
, Proposition 1.2]
). The model
\mathcal{X}_{0}
of
X
corresponding to the standard coordinate system
\mathcal{E}_{0}=(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})
has special fiber
\mathcal{X}_{0,s}=V_{+}(\overline{F})
, with

\overline{F}=(x_{0}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2})^{2}=x_{0}^{4}+2x_{0}^{2}x_{1}x_{2}+x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2},

see Example
2.9
. This is a semistable, but not properly stable quartic over
k
. In particular,
\mathcal{X}_{0}
is a semistable model of
X
.

Let
t\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
be an integral element of
K
, and set

g_{t}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&&\\
&\pi^{-1}&\\
&&\pi\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-2t\\
t&1&-t^{2}\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-2t\\
t\pi^{-1}&\pi&-t^{2}\pi^{-1}\\
0&0&\pi\end{pmatrix}.

An easy calculation shows that

is a quartic form with integral coefficients. The choice of

is informed by the reasoning in Example

: we transform the standard coordinate system
(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})
to a new coordinate system
(y_{0},\pi^{-1}y_{1},\pi y_{2})
, where the line
y_{0}=0
passes through the two points

P_{1}=[0:0:1],\quad P_{2}=[t:1:-t^{2}]

on the conic

, and
y_{1}=0
and
y_{2}=0
are the tangent lines to

through
P_{1}
and

, respectively. This coordinate change preserves the equation

of

.

is also semistable. We claim that two such models
\mathcal{X}_{t}
and
\mathcal{X}_{s}
, with
s,t\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
, are non-isomorphic if the images of
t
and
s
in the residue field
k
are distinct. To see this, it suffices to check that the matrix

g_{t}g_{s}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&2(s-t)\pi^{-1}\\
(t-s)\pi^{-1}&1&-(t-s)^{2}\pi^{-2}\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}

does not lie in
\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits_{3}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
. This is clearly true if
s\not\equiv t\pmod{\pi}
. We conclude that there are infinitely many non-isomorphic semistable models of
X
if the residue field
k
is infinite.

It is interesting to consider the above example in the light of the articles
[
16
]
,
[
25
]
and
[
26
]
. The fact that the GIT-semistable models
\mathcal{X}_{t}
constructed above are not properly stable implies, by the main result of
[
25
]
, that the curve
X
has
hyperelliptic reduction
. This means that the special fiber
\mathcal{X}_{s}
of the geometric stable model
\mathcal{X}
of
X
lies in the closure of the hyperelliptic locus of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{3}
. And in general, the stable model may not dominate any of the GIT-semistable plane models
\mathcal{X}_{t}
constructed above.

However, if we choose the form
G
generically, then
[
16
, Theorem 1.4]
shows that the special fiber
\mathcal{X}_{s}
is a smooth hyperelliptic curve. Moreover,
\mathcal{X}
dominates a unique semistable plane model
\mathcal{X}_{0}
, whose special fiber is a double conic. So the geometric stable model of
X
‘picks out‘ one of the infinitely many plane semistable models. For our motivating question, this is bad news. It shows that in the case of hyperelliptic reduction, our strategy of ‘compute a GIT-semistable model first and then the geometric semistable model‘, worked out in
[
26
]
, doesn’t work as well as in the case of non-hyperelliptic reduction. We hope to come back to this problem in a future article.
