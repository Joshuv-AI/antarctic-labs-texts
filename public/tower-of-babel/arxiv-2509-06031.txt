ZLATTE: A Geometry-Aware, Learning-Free Framework for Language-Driven Trajectory Reshaping in Human-Robot Interaction
Junhui Huang, Yuhe Gong, Changsheng Li, Xingguang Duan, Luis Figueredo

Abstract
We present ZLATTE, a geometry-aware, learning-free framework for language-driven trajectory reshaping in human-robot interaction. Unlike prior learning-based methods, ZLATTE leverages Vision-Language Models to register objects as geometric primitives and employs a Large Language Model to translate natural language instructions into explicit geometric and kinematic constraints. These constraints are integrated into a potential field optimization to adapt initial trajectories while preserving feasibility and safety. A multi-agent strategy further enhances robustness under complex or conflicting commands. Simulation and real-world experiments demonstrate that ZLATTE achieves smoother, safer, and more interpretable trajectory modifications compared to state-of-the-art baselines.

Introduction

As robots transition from structured industrial environments to dynamic, human-centric spaces, the need for intuitive and seamless human-robot interaction (HRI) becomes paramount. A critical aspect of this interaction is enabling robots to adapt their movements in response to human preferences. The central challenge this work addresses is: given an initial, feasible trajectory, how can a robot modify its path to align with nuanced, natural language commands while respecting the physical and geometric constraints of its environment?

Language-driven trajectory modification emerges as a more flexible and powerful paradigm. By enabling robots to understand and respond to natural language commands, we move beyond rigid, pre-defined behaviors. III
Approach

Our approach presents a comprehensive framework for modifying robot trajectories based on natural language commands, ensuring the final path is both compliant with user intent and kinematically feasible. The system’s core is a two-stage process: first, it builds a detailed geometric understanding of the environment, and second, it refines an initial trajectory according to user instructions. To achieve scene understanding, we introduce an automated pipeline that leverages a Vision Language Model, along with foundational vision models to register objects and their geometric properties.

For trajectory modification, a separate Large Language Model translates the user’s command into a set of soft constraints. These constraints are then integrated into a geometry potential field-based optimization that iteratively adjusts the trajectory. This method balances external forces derived from the user’s command and obstacle avoidance with internal forces that maintain the trajectory’s smoothness and integrity. To robustly handle complex or conflicting commands, we introduce a novel multi-agent system where different agents attempt to solve the constraints using varied strategies—such as parallel, sequential, or LLM-prioritized execution, thereby increasing the success rate for complex, multi-constraint tasks.

III-B

VLM based Geometry Registration

To acquire geometric information from the scene, we constructed an automated pipeline based on GPT-4o and foundation vision models. To obtain more comprehensive geometric features and enhance robustness against occlusions, the pipeline utilizes two eye-to-hand RGB-D cameras. These cameras are stereo-calibrated to enable point cloud fusion, with the accuracy further refined using the Iterative Closest Point (ICP) algorithm
[
40
]
. Subsequently, a hand-eye calibration algorithm transforms the fused point cloud into the robot’s base coordinate frame. The pipeline leverages the visual reasoning capabilities of GPT-4o to identify the names of objects in the environment and their most closely corresponding geometric shapes.

For each identified object, we sequentially use Grounding-DINO
[
41
]
and Segment-Anything 2
[
42
]
to acquire its mask from different viewpoints. Based on these masks, the object’s point cloud is segmented from each respective view. After fusing the segmented point clouds, we first perform outlier removal. Following this, the DBSCAN
[
43
]
clustering algorithm is applied to further extract the target object’s point cloud and filter out noise. Finally, a 3D Oriented Bounding Box (OBB) is generated from the cleaned point cloud. Based on this 3D OBB and the previously identified object shape, a corresponding geometric primitive is generated in the robot’s base coordinate system. Our proposed visual registration pipeline includes common shapes such as cubes, cylinders, spheres, and cones. Because geometric primitives like planes often exceed the camera’s field of view, we have excluded them from the automated registration process. However, we provide a convenient interface for their manual addition, which also enables support for plane-based geometric constraints for trajectory modification.

III-C

Geometric Constraints

After registering geometric primitives for each obstacle, the corresponding closest points can be efficiently computed and updated based on Euclidean geometric relationships. Following the approach of
[
30
]
, we represent obstacles using a set of canonical geometric primitives, including spheres, planes, conics, cylinders, and cuboids. The computation of the closest distance relies on the analytic formulation of each primitive in Euclidean space.

•

For spheres, the closest point lies along the direction pointing from the sphere’s center to the robot, and the distance is given by the Euclidean distance between the robot and the sphere’s center, minus the sphere’s radius.

•

For planes, the closest point is determined by the orthogonal projection of the robot onto the plane. If the projected point lies within the finite extent of the plane, the closest distance is the perpendicular (normal) distance. Otherwise, the closest point is taken to be on the nearest edge of the plane boundary, and the distance is computed accordingly using a point-to-line segment formulation.

•

For conics and cylinders, the closest point is derived based on the projection onto the curved surface. This involves identifying the closest generatrix (surface line), typically by projecting the robot position onto the main axis of the shape and determining the nearest point on the corresponding surface line.

•

For conics and cylinders, the closest point is derived based on the projection onto the curved surface. This involves identifying the closest generatrix (surface line), typically by moving the main axis of the shape along the robot direction to the surface line.

•

For cuboids, the closest distance is obtained by evaluating the shortest distance from the robot to the six planes.

III-D

Constraint Generation and Satisfaction

We employ a separate Large Language Model (LLM) for user intent understanding, which translates natural language commands into corresponding constraints. These constraints include: the modification type, specifying the desired nature of the trajectory reshaping; the direction of the modification; and its intensity. For object-based trajectory modification tasks, the LLM aligns the user’s instructions with the objects identified through visual registration. In scenarios involving multiple instructions, the LLM is also tasked with generating different constraint sequences and additional importance parameters, which are determined based on the significance and fragility of the involved objects.

After obtaining the constraints, we use a potential field-based method to iteratively refine the trajectory. The refinement process seeks to minimize an implicit objective function by applying a composite force field to each waypoint. This field comprises internal forces that preserve geometric properties like segment length and curvature, an external force that couples the trajectory to constraints generated from user commands and handles obstacle avoidance—wherein obstacle avoidance is achieved by adding a repulsive force with a very small range of influence to each object in the scene—and a regularization force that penalizes deviation from the initial path. The iterative application of these forces guides the trajectory toward a locally optimal configuration.

The position of each waypoint is influenced by a total force vector
\mathbf{F}_{j,\mathrm{total}}
,At any iteration
i
, the total force on waypoint is given by:

\mathbf{F}_{j,\mathrm{total}}^{(i)}=\mathbf{F}_{j,\mathrm{wp}}^{(i)}+\mathbf{F}_{j,\mathrm{ang}}^{(i)}+\mathbf{F}_{j,\mathrm{ext}}^{(i)}+\mathbf{F}_{j,\mathrm{self}}^{(i)}

Each component of this force field is described below.

Internal Spring Force
To maintain the intrinsic length of the trajectory segments, an internal spring-like force is introduced.

\mathbf{f}_{j,\mathbf{wp}}^{(i)}=k\left(\|\mathbf{w}_{j+1}^{(i)}-\mathbf{w}_{j}^{(i)}\|-\|\mathbf{w}_{j+1}^{(0)}-\mathbf{w}_{j}^{(0)}\|\right)\frac{\mathbf{w}_{j+1}^{(i)}-\mathbf{w}_{j}^{(i)}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{j+1}^{(i)}-\mathbf{w}_{j}^{(i)}\|}

\mathbf{F}_{j,\mathrm{wp}}^{(i)}=\mathbf{f}_{j-1,\mathrm{wp}}^{(i)}-\mathbf{f}_{j,\mathrm{wp}}^{(i)}

where
k
is is a scalar stiffness coefficient.
w_{i}
is the position of waypoints.

Curvature Regularization Force:
To ensure smoothness, a force that regularizes the trajectory’s curvature is applied.

\mathbf{c}_{j}^{(i)}=\left(\frac{\mathbf{w}_{j+2}^{(i)}-\mathbf{w}_{j+1}^{(i)}}{2}\right)-\left(\frac{\mathbf{w}_{j+1}^{(i)}-\mathbf{w}_{j}^{(i)}}{2}\right)

\mathbf{F}_{j+1,\mathrm{ang}}^{(i)}=k_{\mathrm{ang}}\left(\|\mathbf{c}_{j}^{(i)}\|-\|\mathbf{c}_{j}^{(0)}\|\right)\frac{\mathbf{c}_{j}^{(i)}}{\|\mathbf{c}_{j}^{(i)}\|}

where
k_{\mathrm{ang}}
is the angular stiffness coefficient.

External Constraint Force
This force is derived from one or more cost functions,
\mathcal{G}
, which encode information about obstacles, object information
\mathcal{O}
, and user intention
L_{in}
.

\mathbf{F}_{j,\mathrm{ext}}^{(i)}=w\cdot\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{w}_{j}^{(i)},\mathcal{O},L_{in})

Self-Adherence Force

A regularization term is included to prevent excessive deviation from the initial path.

\mathbf{F}_{j,\mathrm{self}}^{(i)}=w_{\mathrm{self}}\left(\mathbf{w}_{j}^{(i)}-\mathbf{w}_{j}^{(0)}\right)

where
w_{\mathrm{self}}
the adherence weighting factor. At each iteration
i
, the position of every waypoint is updated based on the total calculated force.

\mathbf{\xi}_{j}^{(i+1)}=\mathbf{\xi}_{j}^{(i)}+\eta\cdot\mathbf{F}_{j,\mathrm{total}}^{(i)}

where
\eta
is a learning rate or step-size parameter. This iterative process is repeated for a fixed number of steps to get the modified trajectory.

III-E

Multi-agent system

To address scenarios involving multiple, concurrent instructions of the same type, which may conflict and prevent the target constraints from being achieved, we have designed a multi-agent system composed of four distinct agents:i) Parallel Agent: Treats all target instructions as equal, applying all corresponding potential fields simultaneously. ii) Sequential Agent: Executes all instructions sequentially in the order provided by the user, modifying the trajectory iteratively. iii) Parallel Agent with Priority: A parallel agent that utilizes a Large Language Model (LLM) to reorder user instructions based on the importance and fragility of the involved objects. iv) Parallel Agent with Importance: A parallel agent that receives an additional ’intensity’ input for each constraint, with the value determined by the LLM based on object importance and fragility.

In our pipeline, constraints generated by the LLM are first sent to an Initial Planner, which translates them into corresponding potential fields. These are then passed to the Agents to generate modified trajectories. The resulting trajectories are evaluated by an Observer that performs constraint checks based on the original LLM output. If none of the generated trajectories satisfy the user’s instructions, a Refinement Planner initiates an adjustment process. Specifically, if individual constraints can be met but the overall objective fails, we adjust the corresponding intensity, importance, and priority parameters. If, however, a single constraint cannot be satisfied at all, we infer that the object is too far from the trajectory in the default setting and proceed to expand the influence range of that object’s potential field.The refinement process iterates for a specified number of cycles or until all constraints are satisfied.

IV-A

Dataset

To construct our dataset, we adopt the synthetic data generation methodology proposed in [11], [12]. Each data sample comprises three primary components: an initial trajectory
\xi_{o}
, a natural language command
L_{in}
, and a set of environmental objects
\mathcal{O}=\{O_{1},\dots,O_{M}\}
, where each object is described by its pose (position and orientation) and corresponding geometry. Initial trajectories are generated by randomly sampling waypoints within the workspace and subsequently applying spline interpolation to ensure a smooth path. For the language commands, we utilize the three trajectory modification types introduced in [50]: i) absolute Cartesian adjustments (e.g., “go more to the right”), ii) speed modifications (e.g., “slow down when next to the table”), and iii) object-relative positional changes (e.g., “move closer to the chair”).

In total, we curated three distinct types of datasets based on this approach. The first is a single-command dataset, which is analogous to the configuration used in LATTE. Additionally, we assembled a multi-command dataset. Finally, to specifically evaluate the model’s capability in handling complex constraints, we created a third dataset where each language input consists of multiple, concatenated instructions for trajectory modification.

To validate the effectiveness of our proposed multi-agent system, we conducted challenging trajectory modification tasks. The constraints generated by the LLM are used to formulate corresponding checks. These include i) distance check, which evaluates the average distance of the five trajectory points closest to the object before and after the modification; ii) Cartesian check, which verifies the trajectory’s displacement along a specified direction; and iii) speed check, which examines the velocity changes for the five closest points. IV-C

Real-World Experiments

real world setup:

We deployed our approach in real-world experiments using a 7-DOF Franka Emika Panda robot equipped with a claw gripper. A computer equipped with an Intel i9-14900K CPU and an NVIDIA RTX 4060 GPU was used to deploy the arm’s low-level controller and our model. Two Realsense D435 RGB-D cameras were utilized to provide dual-viewpoint images and point clouds. The GPT-4o model for visual registration and LLM for constraint generation were accessed via its API service, while the LLM could be either GPT-4o or the more cost-effective DeepSeek-V3 model. The Grounding-DINO and Segment Anything2 models were deployed locally.

Visual registration in real-world scenarios:
During the visual registration stage, we set the threshold for the ICP registration algorithm to 0.02m and selected mask segments with a confidence score higher than 0.99. To preprocess the segmented point cloud of objects, we first applied statistical outlier removal using 20 nearest neighbors and a standard deviation ratio of 1.0 to eliminate noise. Subsequently, density-based clustering was performed with a neighborhood radius of 0.15 meters and a minimum of 15 points required to form a cluster.

We placed random objects in a real-world robotic scene to test our visual registration pipeline, as illustrated in Figure X. The left panel shows the 3D Oriented Bounding Boxes (OBBs) of the objects, which are generated based on their masks and point clouds. The right panel displays the corresponding geometric primitives generated from the identified object shapes and their 3D OBBs. Through the results of hand-eye calibration, these primitives have been transformed into the robot’s base coordinate frame, thereby completing the scene perception .

Real world trajectory execuation

For practical implementation in real-world applications, both the input trajectory and the geometry of environmental objects are first normalized to ensure uniformity in the scale of modifications. The original trajectory is then downsampled to accelerate the computation of the modification module. After the modification is complete, interpolation and inverse normalization are applied to reconstruct the trajectory for execution. Once this final path is obtained, the user can employ any suitable inverse kinematics algorithm to compute the corresponding joint-space trajectories for the robot.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced a learning-free framework that enables robots to reshape their trajectories in response to complex natural language commands. Our approach leverages VLM for automated geometric registration and utilizes LLM to translate user intent into actionable potential field forces. We conducted a comprehensive set of experiments, demonstrating our method’s superior performance in safety and accuracy against traditional point-based approaches and its enhanced stability compared to state-of-the-art learning-based models. Additionally, real-world and simulated results validate the necessity of our multi-agent system for robustly handling multi-constraint tasks and underscore the critical role of detailed geometry in achieving safe and intuitive human-robot interaction.

There’s still work to explore in the future. A primary limitation is the potential mismatch between the user’s perceived ”importance” of a command and the LLM’s interpretation; however, we found this often introduces a beneficial strategic diversity that aids our multi-agent system in finding a solution. Additionally, our system currently approximates each object with a single geometric primitive, which can limit precision for complex shapes. Future work could address this by representing objects as a composition of multiple geometries to achieve a higher-fidelity fit for more intricate and safer interactions.
