Study on microwave-assisted synthesis of smart responsive magnetic Janus nanocatalysts for enhanced recovery of extra-heavy oil
Authors: Rui Zhao, Desheng Zhou, Wenpei Xu, Yanjun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtra-heavy oil is an important strategic energy resource, but its ultra-high viscosity severely limits efficient production. Conventional thermal recovery and chemical flooding are often associated with high energy consumption, environmental concerns, and limited reservoir adaptability. In this study, an intelligent responsive magnetic Janus nanocatalyst (IRMJN) was developed and coupled with microwave irradiation to enable low-energy and controllable in situ upgrading and oil mobilization. IRMJN features a spatially separated multifunctional architecture, in which Fe₃O₄ serves as the magnetic core for rapid recovery, MoS₂ nanosheets are selectively anchored on one side as catalytic active sites, and graphene quantum dots enhance microwave absorption to generate a synergistic nanoscale hotspot effect. Long-chain alkyl groups grafted onto the magnetic side further impart interfacial orientation capability. Under optimized conditions, the IRMJN-microwave system reduced the viscosity of extra-heavy oil by more than 95% at a bulk temperature of 100 °C, clearly outperforming microwave treatment alone and conventional catalytic systems. Core flooding tests showed an additional oil recovery of more than 18.5% after water flooding. The catalyst also exhibited excellent magnetic recoverability and cycling stability. Introduction

The continuing growth in global energy demand has increased the strategic importance of unconventional oil and gas resources, particularly extra-heavy oil1–3. Although extra-heavy oil is abundant, its high viscosity, poor fluidity, and high asphaltene content create major challenges for production and transportation4,5. Conventional recovery methods, such as cyclic steam stimulation and steam-assisted gravity drainage, can reduce crude oil viscosity effectively, but they are often associated with high energy consumption, intensive water use, substantial greenhouse gas emissions, and limited adaptability to reservoir conditions6,7. Chemical flooding methods, including polymer and surfactant flooding, can also improve recovery; however, they require strict reservoir conditions and introduce additional environmental burdens through chemical recovery and post-treatment8–10.

In recent years, nanotechnology has opened new possibilities for heavy oil recovery. Because of their large specific surface area and abundant active sites, nanocatalysts have shown considerable potential for in situ upgrading and viscosity reduction of heavy oil11–13. Magnetic nanomaterials, owing to their controllability and ease of recovery, offer a practical solution to nanoparticle loss and recycling in reservoir environments14–16. However, most currently reported nanocatalysts still suffer from low catalytic efficiency, limited recoverability, and single-function behavior, which restricts their practical application in complex reservoirs17,18.

To address these limitations, this study develops a novel intelligent responsive magnetic Janus nanocatalyst (IRMJN). The catalyst integrates magnetic recovery, enhanced microwave absorption, and catalytic viscosity reduction within a single platform, enabling efficient, low-energy, and controllable in situ upgrading through microwave-induced localized thermal effects. The key innovation of IRMJN is its asymmetric “three-in-one” Janus architecture. One side consists of a magnetic Fe₃O₄ core that provides strong magnetic responsiveness and recyclability, whereas the other side is decorated with edge-enriched MoS₂ nanosheets as catalytic active sites and graphene quantum dots (GQDs) as microwave absorption enhancers. Under microwave irradiation, these components generate a synergistic nanoscale hotspot effect, producing localized high temperatures that promote the in situ cracking of heavy oil macromolecules at relatively low bulk temperatures. In addition, long-chain alkyl modification on the Fe₃O₄ side imparts interfacial orientation capability, allowing IRMJN to localize preferentially at the oil-water interface and thereby improve catalytic efficiency. This study systematically investigates the synthesis, characterization, microwave response, viscosity reduction behavior, oil recovery performance, stability, and recyclability of IRMJN, with the aim of providing a new strategy for the green and efficient development of extra-heavy oil resources.

Synthesis of intelligent responsive magnetic Janus nanocatalyst (IRMJN)

Preparation of Fe₃O₄ magnetic core

Uniform Fe₃O₄ nanospheres were synthesized using a modified solvothermal method. Specifically, 2.35 g of FeCl₃·6 H₂O and 0.86 g of FeCl₂·4 H₂O were dissolved in 80 mL of deionized water under stirring. Subsequently, 20 mL of ethylene glycol and 5 mL of oleic acid were added, and the mixture was stirred for 30 min. The resulting solution was transferred into a 100 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and reacted at 200 °C for 12 h. After naturally cooling to room temperature, the product was collected by magnetic separation, washed repeatedly with ethanol and deionized water, and vacuum-dried at 60 °C for 12 h to obtain Fe₃O₄ nanospheres.

Construction of Janus structure and surface functionalization

Pickering emulsion formation: 0.1 g of the prepared Fe₃O₄ nanospheres was dispersed in 50 mL of deionized water and ultrasonicated for 30 min. Subsequently, 50 mL of paraffin oil was mixed with the Fe₃O₄ aqueous suspension and subjected to high-speed shearing (10,000 rpm for 5 min) to form a stable Pickering emulsion. The Fe₃O₄ nanospheres were directionally adsorbed at the oil-water interface, with one hemisphere exposed to the aqueous phase and the other encapsulated by paraffin oil.

Growth of MoS₂/GQDs composite layer: To the above Pickering emulsion, 0.1 g of (NH₄)₂MoS₄, 0.05 g of TAA, and 0.02 g of citric acid (as the precursor for GQDs) were added and uniformly mixed. The mixture was transferred to a microwave reactor and subjected to microwave-assisted reaction at 150 °C for 30 min (power: 300 W). During this process, the synthesis followed a strict sequential nucleation logic: first, (NH₄)₂MoS₄ and TAA, acting as Mo and S sources, underwent microwave-induced in-situ nucleation and grew into MoS₂ nanosheets on the exposed Fe₃O₄ surfaces in the aqueous phase. Simultaneously, citric acid underwent in-situ carbonization under the high-energy microwave field to form GQDs, which were synchronously intercalated into the MoS₂ layers. The robust interfacial bonding between Fe₃O₄ and MoS₂ is primarily attributed to the formation of Fe-S coordination bonds at the interface, ensuring the structural stability of the Janus architecture. After the reaction, the product was collected by magnetic separation and washed several times with deionized water and ethanol.

OTS modification: The obtained product was dispersed in 50 mL of n-hexane under ultrasonication. The n-hexane dissolved the paraffin oil encapsulating the Fe₃O₄ nanospheres, exposing the previously shielded hemisphere of Fe₃O₄. Subsequently, 2 mL of octadecyl trimethoxy silane (OTS) was added, and the mixture was stirred at 60 °C for 6 h. Through silane coupling reactions, OTS formed a hydrophobic layer on the Fe₃O₄ surface. After completion, the product was magnetically separated, washed with n-hexane and ethanol, and vacuum-dried at 60 °C for 12 h to obtain the final IRMJN.

Catalyst characterization

Structure and morphology TEM and SEM were employed to observe the Janus structure of IRMJN, particle size distribution, the growth morphology of MoS₂ nanosheets, and the surface characteristics after OTS modification.

Crystal structure XRD analysis was conducted to determine the crystalline phase composition of IRMJN and to confirm the coexistence of Fe₃O₄, MoS₂, and GQDs.

Magnetic properties A vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM) was used to measure the saturation magnetization and coercivity of IRMJN, evaluating its magnetic responsiveness and recovery capability.

Thermal and chemical characteristics TGA/DTG analysis was performed to assess the thermal stability of IRMJN and to quantify the content of organic modification layers. FTIR spectroscopy was employed to analyze surface functional group variations, confirming OTS modification and the incorporation of GQDs.

Microwave-assisted viscosity reduction experiments

A predetermined amount of extra-heavy oil was mixed with different concentrations of IRMJN (0.1–1.0.1.0 wt%) and placed in a microwave reactor. Single-factor and orthogonal experimental designs were employed to optimize key parameters, including microwave power (200–800 W) and treatment time (1–10 min). After treatment, a rheometer was used to measure viscosity, yield stress, and rheological behavior of the oil before and after treatment at various temperatures. Control groups included untreated oil (Blank), microwave treatment alone, Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles with microwave treatment, MoS₂ nanosheets with microwave treatment, IRMJN alone (without microwave irradiation), and IRMJN combined with microwave treatment, in order to comprehensively evaluate the synergistic viscosity reduction performance of IRMJN.

Core flooding and oil recovery experiments

The core flooding experiments were conducted in a high-temperature and high-pressure apparatus to simulate reservoir conditions (T = 80 °C, P = 10 MPa). Artificial sandstone cores with a length of 10.0 cm and a diameter of 3.8 cm were employed to ensure reproducibility, featuring an average porosity of 28.5% ± 1.2%, an absolute permeability of 1250 ± 50 mD, and an initial oil saturation of 84.3%. The mineral composition of the cores primarily consists of quartz (85%), feldspar (10%), and clay (5%), with a neutral-to-oil-wet state confirmed by the Amott method, which effectively represents the actual geological conditions of the target reservoir.

Water flooding was first performed until the water cut reached 98% (typically 1.5–2.0.5.0 pore volumes, PV). Subsequently, 0.2 PV of IRMJN dispersion (0.5 wt%) was injected, with microwave assistance applied during injection (power: 500 W). Water injection continued until no further increase in recovery was observed. Injection pressure, oil production, and recovery factor were recorded throughout the displacement process. The performance of the IRMJN/microwave synergistic system was compared with conventional water flooding, nanofluid flooding, and chemical flooding methods to evaluate its enhanced oil recovery efficiency.

Micro-CT in situ scanning and microscopic residual oil characterization

A carbon fiber core holder with high X-ray transparency was used in conjunction with a micro/nano dual X-ray tube CT scanning system to conduct in situ scans without altering the external morphology or internal structure of the core. Scans were performed for dry cores, water-saturated cores (30% KI solution as contrast agent), bound water states, and different displacement stages (before and after intermittent steam flooding). ImageJ software was used for image binarization and segmentation, and Avizo software was employed to extract pore network models (PNM). The maximal inscribed sphere method was applied to quantitatively characterize the occurrence state and spatial distribution of residual oil. Results and discussion

Structural and physicochemical characteristics of IRMJN

Figure 2 shows the morphological characteristics of IRMJN, whose successful construction provides the structural basis for its multifunctionality. The SEM image in Fig. 2a clearly reveals a distinct Janus structure, with one side of the Fe₃O₄ nanosphere coated by a MoS₂/GQDs composite layer and the other side remaining exposed. The Fe₃O₄ surface is relatively smooth, whereas the MoS₂/GQDs layer exhibits a wrinkled nanosheet morphology characteristic of MoS₂. Region-selective functionalization was further confirmed by contact-angle measurements. After paraffin removal and OTS modification, the Fe₃O₄ side showed a water contact angle of 135.4°, indicating strong hydrophobicity, while the MoS₂/GQD side remained relatively hydrophilic, with a contact angle of 42.6°. This clear wettability contrast confirms the successful asymmetric modification of the Janus structure. Such an asymmetric architecture provides the physical basis for directional adsorption at the oil-water interface and for spatially separated functionality, allowing the catalytic active sites to act more selectively at targeted regions.

SEM image (a), TEM image (b), and HRTEM image (c) of IRMJN.

The TEM image in Fig. 2b further confirms the Janus configuration. The dark Fe₃O₄ core can be clearly distinguished from the lighter MoS₂/GQDs composite layer attached to one side. The exposed edges of the MoS₂ nanosheets indicate sufficient accessibility of catalytic active sites, which is essential for catalytic performance. Particle-size analysis shows that the Fe₃O₄ core has an average diameter of approximately 200 nm, whereas the MoS₂/GQDs layer is about 20–30 nm thick, resulting in a uniform and stable overall structure. These TEM observations verify the successful construction of the Janus architecture at the microscopic level and provide direct evidence for the one-sided growth of the MoS₂/GQDs layer, which underpins the enhanced microwave responsiveness and catalytic activity discussed below.

High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images (Fig. 2c) provide deeper structural insight into the heterojunction interface of IRMJN. Clear lattice fringes corresponding to the Fe₃O₄ core are observed, with an interplanar spacing of 0.25 nm, matching the (311) plane of Fe₃O₄. The tightly attached MoS₂ nanosheets also exhibit distinct lattice fringes with an interplanar spacing of 0.62 nm, corresponding to the (002) plane of MoS₂. The clearly discernible and continuous interface between Fe₃O₄ and MoS₂ provides strong evidence for the formation of a well-defined heterojunction rather than simple physical mixing. Such intimate interfacial coupling is essential for efficient electron and energy transfer, serving as the structural basis for enhanced microwave absorption and catalytic activity.

Figure 3 presents the XRD patterns and FTIR spectra of IRMJN and its individual components. Figure 3a shows the X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of IRMJN, used to analyze its crystalline structure and phase composition. As observed in Fig. 3a, a clear comparison is provided among IRMJN, pure Fe₃O₄, and pure MoS₂. The sharp diffraction peaks of IRMJN located at 2θ = 30.1°, 35.5°, 43.1°, 53.4°, 57.0°, and 62.6° correspond well to the characteristic peaks of cubic spinel Fe₃O₄ (JCPDS 19–0629), indicating that the Fe₃O₄ magnetic core possesses high crystallinity. Meanwhile, characteristic diffraction peaks of MoS₂ are observed at 2θ = 14.4° and 32.7°, corresponding to the (002) and (100) crystal planes of MoS₂, respectively, confirming the successful growth of MoS₂ nanosheets. The comparison among the three diffraction patterns strongly demonstrates the coexistence of Fe₃O₄ and MoS₂ within the Janus structure, with their crystalline structures remaining well preserved, thereby ensuring the magnetic and catalytic functionalities of IRMJN. Although the characteristic peak of GQDs may be obscured by the broad diffraction peak of MoS₂, its contribution will be further confirmed in subsequent microwave absorption performance tests.

XRD patterns (a) and FTIR spectra (b) of IRMJN and its individual components.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis (Fig. 3b) was conducted to investigate the surface functional groups of IRMJN. A distinct absorption peak at 580 cm⁻¹ is attributed to the stretching vibration of the Fe-O bond, further confirming the presence of Fe₃O₄. The absorption peak at 2920 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the C-H stretching vibration of long-chain alkyl groups introduced by OTS modification, indicating successful hydrophobic functionalization of the Fe₃O₄ surface. This hydrophobic layer is essential for enabling directional adsorption of IRMJN at the oil-water interface, ensuring that the catalytic active sites (MoS₂/GQDs side) are preferentially exposed toward the oil phase, thereby enhancing catalytic efficiency. The absorption peak at 1620 cm⁻¹ is assigned to the C = C stretching vibration in GQDs, confirming their successful incorporation, which is critical for enhanced microwave absorption and the formation of nanoscale hotspots. The FTIR results provide strong chemical evidence supporting the successful construction of the Janus architecture and surface functionalization of IRMJN.

Figure 4 presents the magnetic hysteresis loops of the catalyst under different conditions, as well as the thermogravimetric (TGA) and derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) curves of IRMJN. The vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM) results (Fig. 4a) show that IRMJN exhibits typical superparamagnetic behavior at room temperature, with coercivity close to zero. This indicates that the nanoparticles do not aggregate after removal of the external magnetic field, which is favorable for their dispersion and transport within reservoir media. Comparison among pure Fe₃O₄, freshly prepared IRMJN, and IRMJN after five reuse cycles reveals that although the saturation magnetization (Mₛ) of IRMJN slightly decreases relative to pure Fe₃O₄ (to 58.5 emu/g), it still retains excellent magnetic responsiveness, sufficient for rapid and efficient magnetic separation and recovery under an external magnetic field. More importantly, after multiple cycles, the Mₛ value remains at 55.2 emu/g, demonstrating outstanding magnetic stability. This characteristic is of significant importance for repeated utilization in reservoir environments and for reducing environmental risks, serving as a critical prerequisite for green and efficient oil recovery.

Magnetic hysteresis loops of the catalyst under different conditions (a), and TGA/DTG curves of IRMJN (b).

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and derivative thermogravimetric analysis (DTG) (Fig. 4b) were conducted to evaluate the thermal stability of IRMJN and the content of organic components. The TGA curve shows minor weight loss below approximately 200 °C, primarily attributed to desorption of physically adsorbed water. A distinct weight loss plateau is observed between 250 °C and 400 °C, with a corresponding DTG peak around 320 °C, which is mainly associated with the decomposition of organic functional components such as the OTS modification layer and GQDs. The total weight loss is approximately 12.5%, indicating successful loading of organic functional groups on the IRMJN surface. Furthermore, the catalyst maintains structural stability at elevated temperatures, which is crucial for its application under high-temperature reservoir conditions. These results confirm that IRMJN possesses sufficient thermal stability to withstand microwave heating and reservoir temperatures, ensuring sustained functional performance under practical operating conditions.

Microwave response behavior and localized thermal effects

Figure 5a shows the reflection loss (RL) curves of IRMJN at different matching thicknesses. RL is a key parameter for evaluating microwave absorption performance, with more negative values indicating stronger absorption20,21. As the matching thickness increased from 1.5 to 2.5 mm, the absorption peak shifted toward lower frequencies, in agreement with transmission-line theory, indicating that the absorption band can be tuned by adjusting the material thickness. At a thickness of 2.0 mm, IRMJN exhibited a minimum RL of −32 dB at 14.2 GHz, and the effective absorption bandwidth with RL < −10 dB covered the entire Ku band (12–18 GHz). Since RL values below − 10 dB correspond to more than 90% microwave absorption, these results demonstrate the excellent broadband microwave absorption capability of IRMJN. This strong microwave response provides the basis for localized thermal effects. Infrared thermography further confirmed the nanoscale hotspot effect: within 30 s of microwave irradiation, the local temperature of IRMJN aggregates rose to 245 °C, whereas the surrounding bulk environment remained below 80 °C (Fig. S3). This pronounced temperature gradient indicates that IRMJN can efficiently convert absorbed microwave energy into highly localized thermal energy, thereby supplying the activation energy required for catalytic cracking at low bulk temperatures.

Microwave reflection loss (RL) curves of IRMJN under different matching thicknesses (a) and surface temperature variation of IRMJN powder (b).

The enhanced microwave absorption of IRMJN arises from the synergistic combination of magnetic loss from Fe₃O₄, dielectric loss from the MoS₂/GQDs layer, and improved impedance matching. Under microwave irradiation, Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles contribute magnetic loss through natural resonance, exchange resonance, and eddy-current loss22,23. In parallel, MoS₂ nanosheets and GQDs provide dielectric loss because their abundant defect sites and conductive networks promote efficient microwave-to-thermal energy conversion24,25. The incorporation of GQDs further strengthens this dielectric response and creates efficient microwave-absorbing centers. As a result, IRMJN can convert microwave energy into heat efficiently and generate nanoscale hotspots on its surface. Unlike conventional bulk heating, this localized thermal effect concentrates energy at the nanoscale and thus promotes the in situ cracking of heavy oil macromolecules at relatively low bulk temperatures. This mechanism is central to the low-energy and efficient oil-recovery performance of IRMJN26.

Mechanism of microwave-assisted catalytic viscosity reduction

Figure 6a shows the viscosity-reduction performance at different catalyst concentrations. The viscosity of untreated crude oil decreases markedly as temperature increases, but it still remains at the level of several thousand mPa·s at 100 °C, indicating very poor fluidity. In contrast, the combination of IRMJN and microwave irradiation leads to an order-of-magnitude decrease in viscosity. At a catalyst concentration of 0.5 wt%, the viscosity-reduction rate at 100 °C exceeds 90%. Comparison of different heating modes further highlights the advantage of the IRMJN-microwave system. At the same bulk temperature, conventional thermal treatment achieved only a 45% viscosity reduction, while microwave irradiation without catalyst yielded 52%. By contrast, the IRMJN-microwave system reached 95%, indicating that the main contribution arises from localized nanoscale heating rather than bulk heating alone. These localized high-temperature regions activate the MoS₂ catalytic sites and promote the deep cracking of heavy components, thereby driving efficient in situ upgrading of extra-heavy oil and substantially improving its mobility27.

Viscosity reduction performance at different catalyst concentrations (a), and evolution of SARA fractions at different catalyst concentrations (b), gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis results of IRMJN before and after microwave treatment (c).

Figure 6b presents the SARA analysis, which helps clarify the chemical basis of viscosity reduction. Crude oil is divided into saturates, aromatics, resins, and asphaltenes, with asphaltenes being the main contributors to the high viscosity of heavy oil. As the IRMJN concentration increased, the asphaltene content decreased markedly from 16.2 wt% to 8.9 wt% at 0.5 wt% catalyst loading, whereas the saturate content increased from 15.2 wt% to 28.4 wt%. These changes indicate that the microwave-induced nanoscale hotspot effect activates the MoS₂ catalytic sites and promotes cleavage of C-C and C-S bonds in heavy fractions such as asphaltenes and resins, leading to in situ upgrading of the crude oil. GC-MS analysis of oil samples before and after IRMJN-microwave treatment (Fig. 6c) further supported this interpretation by showing a clear increase in light hydrocarbons (C10-C20) and a corresponding decrease in heavy components (> C30). The appearance of short-chain alkanes and alkyl-substituted aromatics provides direct evidence for catalytic cracking of asphaltene side chains. In addition, molecular-weight-distribution analysis showed that the weight-average molecular weight decreased from 1250 Da to 840 Da, with the distribution shifting toward lower molecular weights. Together, these microscopic results are consistent with the macroscopic SARA changes and strongly support the proposed mechanism of microwave-assisted in situ upgrading and viscosity reduction.

Moreover, the Janus structure and surface functionalization of IRMJN impart directional adsorption capability at the oil-water interface, enabling preferential enrichment of catalytic active sites at the interface between heavy oil and the aqueous phase, thereby further enhancing catalytic efficiency. This “microwave-induced nanoscale hotspot catalytic viscosity reduction mechanism” allows efficient in situ upgrading of extra-heavy oil at relatively low bulk temperatures, providing a novel technological pathway for its exploitation.

Evaluation of enhanced oil recovery performance and analysis of microscopic displacement mechanism

Figure 7a compares the recovery performance under different displacement schemes in the core flooding experiments. During the water flooding stage, the recovery factor gradually increased with injected pore volume (PV), ultimately reaching approximately 35%. After water flooding was completed (at 1.5 PV), injection of IRMJN dispersion combined with microwave assistance resulted in a pronounced secondary recovery enhancement. Compared with continued water flooding and conventional nanofluid flooding, the IRMJN + microwave synergistic system exhibited a rapid increase in recovery after the switching point, achieving an additional recovery improvement of more than 18.5%, with the final recovery exceeding 53.5%. These results demonstrate that the IRMJN/microwave synergistic system effectively mobilizes residual extra-heavy oil and significantly enhances oil recovery, outperforming conventional methods.

Comparison of oil recovery factors under different displacement schemes (Conditions: T = 80 °C, P confining = 10 MPa, injection rate = 0.1 mL/min, core permeability = 1250 mD). (a), and dynamic injection pressure during core flooding at different microwave powers (b).

Figure 7b presents the dynamic injection pressure profiles during core flooding under different microwave power conditions. Following the injection of IRMJN at 1.5 PV, a transient increase in injection pressure was observed. The measured resistance factor (RF) for the 0.5 wt% IRMJN dispersion reached 3.42, indicating a significant improvement in the mobility ratio by increasing the effective viscosity of the displacement phase. Subsequently, the injection pressure dropped sharply and stabilized due to the rapid reduction in heavy oil viscosity under microwave induction. After the subsequent water flush, the residual resistance factor (RRF) was measured at only 1.18. This low value suggests that while some nanoparticles are retained at the pore throats (contributing to profile control), the majority of IRMJN particles can successfully transport into the deep reservoir without causing severe permeability damage or formation blockage. Under higher microwave power (600 W), the viscosity reduction effect was more pronounced, leading to a rapid pressure drop after a short fluctuation period and stabilization at a lower level. This behavior directly reflects substantial improvement in mobility ratio and significant reduction in flow resistance. The decrease in injection pressure indicates enhanced displacement efficiency and reduced energy consumption, which is of considerable importance for practical field applications28.

Figure 8 presents micro-CT observations showing that the IRMJN-microwave system selectively mobilizes residual oil within the pore space. According to the adopted classification criteria, the residual oil in the core can be divided into five types: cluster-like, porous, columnar, droplet-like, and film-like.

Micro-CT characterization of residual oil distribution (Conditions: $T = 80 °C, injection rate = 0.05 mL/min for CT scanning stability) (a), and bar chart showing the evolution of residual oil types (b).

Under the initial bound-water condition, the oil saturation (Sₒ) reached 84.34%. After injection of the IRMJN nanofluid and application of microwave-assisted heating, the continuous cluster-like residual oil was fluidized and cracked in situ under the nanoscale hotspot effect. A substantial fraction was converted into more discontinuous droplet-like or film-like forms and was subsequently displaced from the pore space. Quantitative analysis shows that Sₒ decreased to 41.75% during the intermittent steam-flooding stage and further declined to 25.78% at the end of displacement, corresponding to a final oil-displacement efficiency of 69.43%. This improvement at the microscopic scale can be attributed mainly to the directional arrangement of the Janus-structured IRMJN at the oil-water interface, which reduces interfacial tension. Combined with localized microwave heating, this effect promotes the emulsification and detachment of previously immobile porous and columnar residual oil. Three-dimensional CT reconstructions further confirm improved pore connectivity after displacement, indicating that the synergistic system effectively overcomes capillary resistance, stabilizes the displacement front, and enhances the mobilization of deeply trapped residual oil.

The enhanced oil recovery can be attributed to several synergistic factors. First, under microwave irradiation, IRMJN generates localized thermal effects that promote catalytic viscosity reduction, thereby decreasing the viscosity of residual extra-heavy oil and improving its mobility. Second, the Janus structure enables directional alignment at the oil-water interface, which lowers interfacial tension and facilitates the emulsification and detachment of oil droplets. At the pore scale, the combined effects of interfacial orientation and nanoscale hotspots destabilize the continuous cluster-like residual oil originally trapped in pore centers, causing it to fragment into more mobile droplet-like or film-like forms. In addition, possible non-thermal microwave effects may further influence interfacial behavior and rock wettability29. Together, these effects help the IRMJN-microwave system overcome capillary resistance, improve the mobility ratio, and stabilize the displacement front, as reflected by the substantial decrease in residual oil saturation from 84.34% to 25.78%.

Catalyst cycling stability and regeneration performance.

The magnetic Janus structure of IRMJN gives the catalyst excellent magnetic recoverability. After the viscosity-reduction experiments, IRMJN could be rapidly separated from the extra-heavy oil system under an external magnetic field, with a recovery rate exceeding 98%. In contrast to conventional nanocatalysts that are difficult to recover, this feature can substantially reduce both environmental risks and operational costs. The combination of good stability and high recoverability supports the practical application of IRMJN in extra-heavy-oil recovery and highlights its engineering potential. In addition, comparative CT scans of core slices after displacement experiments showed that IRMJN remained well dispersed within the pore space, with no obvious nanoparticle aggregation or pore-throat blockage. This favorable transport behavior is likely related to the asymmetric wettability of the Janus structure, which helps maintain stable suspension at fluid interfaces and facilitates transport and recovery in complex porous media.

Field application evaluation

To verify the application potential of IRMJN under real reservoir conditions, a six-month field pilot test was conducted in a selected block of the Liaohe Oilfield. The target reservoir has an average depth of 1200 m and a formation temperature of approximately 65 °C. The original crude oil viscosity reaches 150,000 mPa·s at 50 °C, with an asphaltene content of 20.5 wt%, facing challenges associated with high energy consumption and limited recovery improvement under conventional cyclic steam stimulation. A downhole microwave heating device and an IRMJN nanofluid injection system were deployed on site.

Field implementation scheme

IRMJN nanofluid injection: A 0.5 wt% IRMJN nanofluid was prepared using formation water and injected into the reservoir through the injection well at a dosage of 0.2 PV. Prior to injection, the nanofluid underwent rigorous stability testing to ensure adequate dispersion and resistance to aggregation under reservoir conditions.

Microwave-assisted heating: Following IRMJN injection, intermittent microwave irradiation was applied to the reservoir using a downhole microwave heating device (average power 500 W, cumulative heating duration of 6 h per day) to activate the nanoscale hotspot effect of IRMJN and promote in situ viscosity reduction of extra-heavy oil.

Production monitoring: Key operational parameters, including produced fluid viscosity, daily oil production rate, water cut, and injection pressure, were monitored in real time.

Field performance evaluation

Field monitoring data indicate that the viscosity of produced fluids decreased by more than 85% at 50 °C, from 150,000 mPa·s to approximately 22,500 mPa·s, substantially improving crude oil mobility and reducing transportation and gathering difficulty.

The average daily oil production of the participating wells increased by 45% relative to the pre-treatment level, and some wells showed nearly a twofold increase. Over the six-month pilot period, the cumulative incremental oil production met the design target and effectively extended the economic life of the wells. At the same time, the water cut remained well controlled without any marked increase, suggesting that the directional interfacial action of IRMJN primarily enhanced oil-phase mobilization rather than simply increasing water production. Figure 9 shows the production profile of a representative pilot well. Before treatment, the well produced approximately 2.5 t/d of oil with a water cut of 92%. After IRMJN injection and microwave activation, a clear oil bank formed, the peak oil production reached 4.8 t/d, and the water cut decreased to a minimum of 78% during the effective period. The cumulative oil increment for this single well was estimated to be 320 tons, demonstrating the strong field applicability of the IRMJN-microwave system.

Production dynamics plot of the experimental well.

Optimization of injection pressure: Under the synergistic action of IRMJN and microwave irradiation, injection pressure was maintained at a relatively low level, with an average reduction of approximately 30% compared with conventional water flooding. This reduction decreased pumping energy consumption and lowered the risk of formation fracturing.

Environmental and economic benefits: Through magnetic separation technology, the on-site recovery rate of IRMJN exceeded 95%, minimizing catalyst loss and environmental impact. Comprehensive evaluation shows that the energy consumption per unit of produced oil decreased by 25%, generating significant economic and environmental benefits for the oilfield.

This field pilot test confirms the effectiveness and reliability of IRMJN under practical reservoir conditions. Its combined advantages, including intelligent responsiveness, efficient viscosity reduction, enhanced oil recovery, and facile recyclability, provide strong technical support for the green and efficient development of extra-heavy oil and highlight its potential for large-scale application.

Conclusions

This study addresses the challenge of ultra-high viscosity in extra-heavy oil by developing and validating an intelligent responsive magnetic Janus nanocatalyst (IRMJN) for microwave-assisted applications. The results clarify the synergistic mechanism responsible for microwave-enhanced in situ viscosity reduction and oil recovery. Through its asymmetric Janus design, IRMJN integrates magnetic recoverability, enhanced microwave absorption, and catalytic activity within a single platform. This architecture enables localized concentration of microwave energy at the nanoscale, generating stable hotspots that promote the cracking of asphaltenes and resins at relatively low bulk temperatures. As a result, the localized thermal-catalytic coupling significantly improves the rheological properties and pore-scale mobility of extra-heavy oil, leading to recovery gains beyond those of conventional methods in core flooding tests. In addition, the magnetic Janus configuration provides excellent recovery efficiency and cycling stability, reducing the risk of nanoparticle retention in reservoir formations. Overall, this work offers a feasible materials-based strategy for the efficient and low-energy development of extra-heavy oil under microwave-assisted conditions, with both mechanistic significance and practical engineering value.
