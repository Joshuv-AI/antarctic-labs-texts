Bioimaging of Metals
Authors: Jake Brooks, George Firth, Deju Ye, Yuncong Chen
Published: 2026

Metals play numerous essential structural and catalytic roles in biology; an estimated 30–40% of all proteins require metals for normal function. Conversely, metal-mediated toxicity is linked directly or indirectly to many prevalent health disorders, including cancer, neurodegeneration, and organ failure. Damaging health effects can derive from metals of both endogenous and exogenous origin. Typically present in low concentrations in biological tissues, metals require highly sensitive analytical techniques to visualize their distributions at regional, cellular, and subcellular length scales. Such localization from the whole body to single cells is critical to identifying sites of metal accumulation and toxicity, as well as in assessing the efficacy of metal-based biomarkers and therapeutic interventions.

In response to this emerging need, advanced chemical imaging tools such as mass spectrometry imaging, synchrotron X-ray spectromicroscopy, laser ablation inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (LA-ICP-MS), molecular imaging with positron emission tomography (PET), and new fluorescence probes and radiotracers have enabled key physicochemical properties to be spatially derived to provide ever increasing sensitivity and accuracy. This has paved the way for better understanding of metal-dependent biological processes and to more nuanced application of metals in medicine.

This special issue of Chemical & Biomedical Imaging, entitled “Bioimaging of Metals” showcases 12 contributions demonstrating advancements made in this field using a diversity of cutting-edge methods.

Synchrotron facilities are increasingly being utilized for health and life sciences applications, with several papers in this special issue exploiting synchrotron X-ray fluorescence (SXRF). SXRF offers several advantages for bioimaging of metals, specifically the capacity for multielement mapping at micro- or nanofocus resolutions, while also avoiding labeling or sample ablation, enabling combination with complementary imaging modalities.

Gosling et al. use microfocus SXRF to investigate the elemental heterogeneity within and between human prostrate calcifications. The study demonstrates newfound correlations between elemental ratios and tumor grade group, potentially contributing toward the development of new biomarkers for prostate cancer.

In another study, Marchi et al. combine SXRF with X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) and cryogenic soft X-ray tomography (cryo-SXT), providing complementary information on metal speciation and 3D cellular metal distribution, respectively. Presented case studies using human cancer cells demonstrate how integration of hard and soft synchrotron X-ray techniques can be employed to investigate the spatial, chemical and structural dynamics in biological matrices with near native state preservation.

Ellison et al. also combine SXRF with XANES, as well as with immuno-fluorescence and spatial transcriptomics to study spatially derived iron distributions, oxidation state, storage proteins and gene expression in mouse hippocampus. This highly correlative approach reveals subregional variations in the iron metallomic profile, suggesting increased metabolic and oxidative activity in medial regions, potentially explaining the selective vulnerability of associated neurons.

The study by Arigundiya et al. capitalizes further on the nondestructive nature of SXRF, showcasing the development of a custom laser-etched substrate to enable precise same-section relocation of sample regions of interest across imaging modalities. Building on a finder grid concept, the novel substrate is optimized for correlative imaging studies of metals in biological tissues, offering a scalable solution for imaging across different length scales while avoiding sample masking or substrate contributions to metal signals.

The level of sensitivity required for detection of trace metal concentrations in biological samples often leaves sample measurements vulnerable to external background contributions and sample contamination, which can be challenging to overcome.

The study by Lin et al. investigates an objective approach to distinguishing true signal from background noise in metals imaging by applying a cumulative threshold approach. The limitations of this approach are explored in the context of studying brain iron in mice, highlighting the importance of parameter selection and image conversion for robust background subtraction.

Lovejoy et al. investigate the impact of formalin fixation and paraffin embedding on the elemental concentrations and distributions in breast cancer tissue sections, comparing fresh frozen with chemically fixed serial sections. Findings demonstrate impact on mobile elements (Na, Mg, K and Ca), while less mobile elements (Mn, Fe and Zn) are shown to be largely unaffected by fixation. Cu contamination is observed for fixed tissue sections, potentially attributable to the postoperative tissue marking dye.

While techniques which incorporate sample ablation constrain potential for correlative analysis, these approaches do offer more quantitative solutions which can also be applied to investigate metal isotope distributions.

Doungchawee et al. employ LA-ICP-MS to investigate the fate of ingested gold nanoparticles in a mouse model at the suborgan level. The study demonstrates that biodistribution and clearance in the kidney, lever, and spleen are affected by the surface charge of the nanoparticles, providing key insight into how surface chemistry influences nanoparticle interactions in a complex biological system.

Wulfmeier et al. find ion beam analysis to be the optimum technique for investigating the intracellular distribution of radioactive thallium-201 in prostate, lung and ovarian cancer cells. The study finds that unbound thallium­(I) ions preferentially accumulate in cell nuclei, potentially accounting for their higher toxicity per intracellular decay relative to thallium-201 labeled Prussian blue nanoparticles, which are shown to exclusively localize to the cell cytoplasm.

Fluorescent probes are among the most versatile tools in metals imaging; with the appropriate setup, fluorescence probes can be applied to interrogate metal distributions with high selectivity at the subcellular level in living systems, ranging from cultured cells to complex organisms.

Yu et al. develop a new series of Zn2+ fluorescent probes, permitting ex vivo monitoring of insulin secretion in mouse islets with minimized phototoxicity. The authors build on their previous work, incorporating auxochrome engineering strategies to significantly improve the brightness, turn-on ratio, and hydrophilicity of existing zinc probes.

Imaging metals in vivo presents an even greater challenge, requiring tools capable of combining sensitivity and selectivity with biocompatibility.

Lewis et al. reviewed recent developments in imaging strategies with metal-binding DNAzymes probes. The review compares the relative advantages and disadvantages of different signal transduction strategies, including fluorescence, chemiluminescence and bioluminescence, for advancing understanding of cellular metal ion dynamics.

Two studies in this special issue showcase new probes for in vivo imaging of copper.

Adair et al. develop a fluorine-labeled fluorescent copper sensor for imaging in fixed tissues, living cells, as well as for visualizing murine brain copper changes in vivo in combination with intravital fluorescence microscopy. Further studies are underway to adapt this probe into a fluorine-18 radiotracer for PET imaging as a potential diagnostic tool in copper-associated disorders.

Adopting a different approach, Sun et al. design a photoacoustic probe which, owing to a β-galactoside moiety and a benzyl fluoride group, selectively accumulates in tumors via β-galactosidase-mediated self-immobilization process. The probe delivers a ratiometric photoacoustic response to Cu­(II), enabling visualization of Cu­(II) in tumors, which is demonstrated in a mouse model.

This Collection constitutes just a snapshot of the exciting developments in the field of metals imaging. The 12 included studies highlight the importance of metals imaging in biological systems for a wide range of health and life sciences applications, harnessing the capabilities of different cutting-edge approaches to provide new insights. The relative advantages and limitations of these methodologies are outlined and discussed, while opportunity for translation into improved fundamental understanding, diagnostic tools, and/or treatment strategies permeates throughout. We extend our gratitude to all contributing authors and reviewers and hope this special issue will encourage further interdisciplinary collaboration and advancement in this evolving field.
