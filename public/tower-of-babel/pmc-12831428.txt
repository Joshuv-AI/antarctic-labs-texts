Labour induction and adverse perinatal outcomes: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Mathilde Héritier, Massinissa Aroun, Catherine Quantin, Jessica Di Vincenzo-Sormani, Laurent Gaucher, Thomas Desplanches
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe rising overall prevalence of induction of labour (IOL) in high-income countries raises questions about the appropriateness of its clinical indications and concerns about potential adverse perinatal outcomes. The underlying reasons for this increase remain unclear but may include broader clinical indications, changing maternal or foetal conditions, or more maternal requests. This study aimed to assess changes in prevalence of IOL and adverse perinatal outcomes.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, population-based cohort study using data from a perinatal registry encompassing all births in a French region. The study included all women who delivered a live-born infant. IOL and perinatal outcomes were obtained from medical records. We first described the overall prevalence of these outcomes, followed by a stratified analysis based on the eight-group Grenoble Classification for IOL indications. Variations in IOL and perinatal outcomes were assessed from 2020 (reference) to 2023, both overall and by group, using Poisson regression models with generalized estimating equations. ResultsThe study population included 54,089 women. Overall IOL prevalence rose from 20.7% in 2020 to 28.1% in 2023 (adjusted risk-ratio (aRR) 1.31, 95%CI 1.15–1.49). This increase was mainly driven by higher aRRs in Group-5 [Single cephalic pregnancy ≥41 weeks of gestation (WG)] (34.6% to 42.9%; aRR 1.21, 95%CI 1.10-1.40), Group-6 [Single cephalic pregnancy with maternal pathology from 37 to 40+6 WG] (31.5% to 49.7%; aRR 1.56, 95%CI 1.38–1.75), and Group-7 [Single cephalic pregnancy with foetal pathology from 37 to 40+6 WG] (52.9% to 77.2%; aRR 1.46, 95%CI 1.14–1.88). Adverse outcome changes were observed only in these three groups, with increased emergency caesarean rates in Group-5 (12.4% to 16.4%; aRR 1.22, 95% CI 1.10–1.40) and Group-7 (13.1% to 19.6%; aRR 1.40, 95%CI 1.04–1.80), as well as higher postpartum haemorrhage rates in Group-5 (5.8% to 7.7%; aRR 1.31, 95% CI 1.01–1.69) and Group-6 (4.9% to 7.0%; aRR 1.41, 95%CI 1.05–1.90). Neonatal morbidity remained stable.ConclusionsThe rise in overall IOL prevalence was driven by higher rates in three groups (pregnancies ≥41 WG, maternal and foetal pathology), in which we also observed a clinically meaningful increase in adverse maternal outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08599-5.

Background

Induction of labour (IOL) is an increasingly common obstetric intervention in high-income countries and is used in up to one-third of all births [1–4]. This increase may, in part, be attributed to findings from recent large-scale trials that suggest routine IOL at 39 weeks of gestation, compared to expectant management, reduces the risk of caesarean section without increasing neonatal morbidity [5].

The decision to induce labour must be carefully considered, as this medical intervention may be associated with an increased risk of perinatal complications, including uterine rupture [6, 7], foetal distress [7], cord prolapse [6], and postpartum haemorrhage [6–8], as well as higher healthcare costs [9, 10]. The association between labour induction and the risk of caesarean birth remains controversial, with studies reporting conflicting results [5, 11–13].

While IOL is strongly recommended for certain well-established clinical indications, many commonly cited indications lack robust supporting evidence [14, 15]. This calls for a critical evaluation of current practices to ensure that IOL is used appropriately. Additionally, given the potential perinatal risks associated with IOL, it is crucial to ensure that adverse perinatal outcomes have not increased. The use of standardised classification systems, such as the Robson classification for caesarean Sect [16], can significantly improve the consistency and comparability of obstetric practices. Considering the ongoing challenge of IOL, an eight-group classification (Grenoble classification) was proposed in 2022 to provide a clinically relevant framework for assessing labour induction practices [17].

In this context, the objective of this study was to assess changes in the prevalence of both IOL and adverse perinatal outcomes from 2020 to 2023 in the Burgundy region of France, using the Grenoble classification.

Methods

Design and source of data

A retrospective population-level cohort study was conducted using data collected between January 2020 and December 2023 in the Burgundy Perinatal Network. Data was acquired from the Burgundy Perinatal Network, which is a French regional research network designed to monitor and evaluate maternal and newborn health in the French region of Burgundy. This network covers all public and private hospitals (four level I maternity units, seven level II maternity units and one level III maternity unit [university hospital]) a French region with approximately 1 600 000 inhabitants. In the French perinatal care system, maternity units are categorized into three levels according to the level of neonatal care they can provide: (i) Level I maternity units, which have no neonatal unit; (ii) Level II units, which are equipped to manage births from 32 weeks of gestation and have a neonatal care unit; and (iii) Level III units, which provide the highest level of care, including neonatal intensive care for very preterm infants. In Burgundy, Level I units typically manage fewer than 1,000 births per year, Level II units between 1,000 and 2,000, and the Level III unit around 3,000. Our perinatal network includes only one private maternity unit. All births and terminations of pregnancies that occur within the Burgundy Perinatal Network at or after 22 completed weeks of gestation and/or with a birthweight of 500 g or greater have been systematically recorded in the database since 2000. Data are prospectively recorded from the mandatory discharge abstracts for each hospitalized individual (used to determine the activity-based funding of hospitals in France) and from birth registers. Data were anonymized in each maternity unit before being sent to the Burgundy Perinatal Network unit for data validation and mother/child linkage with a success rate of 100% [18]. Data entry was supervised by the physicians in the medical records department, and our statistician ensured exhaustiveness. Statistical coherence was evaluated, and any discrepancies were reported to the medical team and corrected. These data are linked to the Burgundy Perinatal Network database on a yearly basis.

The collection of data by the Burgundy Perinatal Network has been approved by the French Committee for Data Protection (Commission Nationale Informatique et Liberté - Authorization number 455451). Authorization was obtained to conduct this study from the local ethics committee (N°CEROG 2024-OBS-0806). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the database was used in accordance with article 6–1-c (Lawfulness of processing) of the European General Data Protection Regulation. The data have been collected in accordance with our usual practices, and women experienced no additional procedures for this investigation. Consent to participate was not required by law due to the retrospective and non-interventional nature of the study.

Study population

Our study population included all women who gave birth to a live infant in one of the maternity units of the Burgundy Perinatal Network. Termination of pregnancy (n = 195), stillbirth (n = 300, with respectively 70 in 2020, 79 in 2021, 89 in 2022 and 62 in 2023), and home birth (n = 353) were excluded.

Outcomes

Our main outcome was IOL, analysed as a dichotomous variable. This information, which was obtained from birth registers, is recorded prospectively by the clinician in charge (midwife or obstetrician) when the decision is made to induce labour.

Four adverse perinatal outcomes were studied separately. The first adverse perinatal outcome was emergency caesarean section. The second adverse perinatal outcome was post-partum haemorrhage (PPH) identified in our database using the International Classification of Diseases 10th revision codes (O72.0, O72.1 and O72.2). Coding was performed prospectively by clinicians in accordance with French national guidelines. The most recent national indicator on PPH, published by the French Health Authority in 2025, confirms that the definition (PPH as ≥ 500 mL of blood loss, regardless of the mode of delivery), established in 2014 [19, 20], remains in use. For the third adverse perinatal outcome, condition at birth was defined as a 5-minute Apgar score < 7 and/or low arterial blood gases (umbilical artery pH < 7.10) [21, 22]. For the fourth outcome, the composite neonatal morbidity indicator followed the definition developed by Lain et al. [23], with the specific diagnosis and procedure codes detailed in Additional Table 1.

Grenoble classification for evaluating induction of labour

The Grenoble classification [17], inspired by the logic of the Robson system for caesarean section monitoring, provides a standardized framework with 8 well-defined, prospective, mutually exclusive, and clinically relevant groups. These subgroups are based on six obstetric criteria: multiple pregnancies, breech presentations, gestational age (preterm and post-term), presence of premature rupture of membranes (> 37 weeks), and presence of maternal or foetal pathology (Fig. 1). Each woman was assigned to one mutually exclusive group using a hierarchical decision-making algorithm based on up to seven successive binary clinical questions, applied prospectively at the time of induction by the attending clinician. The process stops as soon as the first applicable criterion is met, and the woman is placed in the corresponding group. If all answers are negative, she is assigned to Group 8 (“no medical indication”). This algorithm reflects real-life clinical decision-making and is illustrated in Additional Fig. 1.

Covariables

We considered the following maternal characteristics: maternal age, body mass index before pregnancy, nulliparity, multiple pregnancy, history of caesarean section, maternal diabetes, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, premature rupture of membranes, placenta praevia and accreta, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, onset of labour, mode of birth, gestational age at birth, birthweight in grams, foetal presentation and level of maternity unit.

Statistical analysis

Characteristics of women were described from 2020 to 2023. We first calculated the overall prevalence of IOL and of each adverse perinatal outcome. We then calculated the prevalence of IOL and adverse perinatal outcomes for each group classification (i.e., number of women with IOL or adverse perinatal outcome out of the total number of women in each group).

Variations in both IOL and adverse perinatal outcomes from 2020 to 2023 were analysed overall and by group, using Poisson regression generalized estimating equation models, with the year 2020 as a reference. We used a generalized estimating equation approach to take into consideration the clustering of births within hospitals. In order to take into account confounding factors related to changes in population characteristics, risk ratios (RR) adjusted for maternal age, body mass index, and parity were estimated with their confidence intervals (CI). These factors were based on a priori hypotheses according to the literature, the authors’ clinical experiences, and the results of the descriptive analysis. The same analysis was performed for emergency caesarean section among women who underwent IOL.

For the outcome of IOL, we performed additional analyses. First, we assessed variations in the prevalence of IOL across main pathology categories within Groups-6 and − 7 using Cochran-Armitage tests, with the year 2020 as the reference point. Second, we calculated the relative size of each group (i.e., number of women in the group divided by the total number of women who gave birth), and each group’s contribution to the total number of IOL (i.e., number of IOLs in the group divided by the total annual number of IOLs). Third, we conducted analyses according to the level of the maternity unit to explore whether changes in IOL differed by level of care.

Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed test with P < 0.05. Analyses were performed with SAS 9.4 software. This study is reported as per the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (Additional Table STROBE Checklist) [24].

Results

The study population included 54,089 women after the exclusion of cases involving pregnancy termination and home births. Women gave birth after planned caesarean section (n = 4,489), spontaneous labour (n = 36,297), and induced labour (n = 13,303). Between 2020 and 2023, women’s characteristics changed. Maternal age at the time of birth increased, along with body mass index, and the proportion of nulliparous women slightly increased. The proportion of multiple pregnancy and maternal conditions remained stable over time, except for hypertensive disorders of pregnancy and premature rupture of membranes lasting less than 24 h at 37 weeks of gestation (WG), which slightly increased. The spontaneous labour rate decreased from 72.2% to 63.0%, while both caesarean section before labour and IOL rates increased, rising from 7.1% to 8.9% and from 20.7% to 28.1%, respectively (Table 1).

Stratification using the Grenoble classification revealed a significant increase in IOL prevalence in Group-1 [Multiple pregnancies], Group-4 [Cephalic pregnancy with premature rupture of membranes ≥ 37 WG], Group-5 [Single cephalic pregnancy ≥ 41 WG], Group-6 [Cephalic pregnancy with maternal pathology from 37 to 40 + 6 WG], and Group-7 [Cephalic pregnancy with foetal pathology from 37 to 40 + 6 WG] (Table 2). In contrast, the prevalence of IOL in Group-8 [Single cephalic pregnancy without medical indication from 37 to 40 + 6WG], representing 43.8% of the study population, remained unchanged (aRR 1.11, 95% CI 0.72–1.71) (Table 2, Additional Table 2).

The most pronounced increases were observed in Group-6 (aRR 1.56, 95% CI 1.38–1.75) and Group-7 (aRR 1.46, 95% CI 1.14–1.88). In Group-6, the increase in IOL was primarily associated with a rise in inductions due to diabetes, which increased from in 25.1% 2020 to 40.3% in 2023 (Cochran–Armitage tests, P < 0.001), and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, which increased from in 48.2% 2020 to 72.0% in 2023 (Cochran–Armitage tests, P < 0.001). In Group-7, the increase was mainly attributed to an increase in inductions for suspected foetal macrosomia, from 40.9% in 2020 to 64.6% in 2023 (Cochran–Armitage tests, P < 0.001), and small-for-gestational-age and/or intrauterine growth restriction, which rose from 43.2% in 2020 to 74.5% in 2023 (Cochran–Armitage tests, P < 0.001) (Additional Table 3). Notably, the contribution of Group 7 to all IOLs nearly doubled, rising from 10.0% in 2020 to 19.8% in 2023, representing the most substantial increase among all groups. Groups-6 (24.3%) and − 7 (19.8%) accounted for 44.1% of IOL cases in 2023. Groups-6 (24.3%) and − 7 (19.8%) accounted for 44.1% of IOL cases in 2023. Although the proportion of IOL in Group-5 [Single cephalic pregnancy ≥ 41 WG] decreased from 30% to 25% between 2020 and 2023, it remained the primary contributor to IOL cases overall (Additional Table 2). This overall increase in IOL prevalence was consistent across all levels of maternity care (Additional Fig. 2).

The prevalence of emergency caesarean section progressively increased over time in the overall population, rising from 11.5% in 2020 to 13.5% in 2023 (aRR 1.10, 95% CI 1.01–1.20). According to the Grenoble classification, significant and continuous increases in emergency caesarean rates were observed in Group-5 (12.4% in 2020 to 16.4% in 2023; aRR 1.22, 95% CI 1.10–1.40) and Group-7 (13.1% in 2020 to 19.6% in 2023; aRR 1.40, 95% CI 1.04–1.80), while no significant variations were detected in other groups (Table 3). The prevalence of emergency caesarean section among women who underwent IOL is presented in Additional Table 4, in which we can observe a significant and continuous increase in emergency caesarean rates in Group-7 (13.5% in 2020 to 21.1% in 2023; aRR 1.37, 95% CI 1.08–1.72).

Similarly, the overall prevalence of PPH increased progressively, from 4.6% in 2020 to 5.9% in 2023 (aRR 1.25, 95% CI 1.00–1.57). This increase was only significant in Group-5 (5.8% in 2020 to 7.7% in 2023; aRR 1.31, 95% CI 1.01–1.69) and Group-6 (4.9% in 2020 to 7.0% in 2023; aRR 1.41, 95% CI 1.05–1.90) (Table 4).

In contrast, neonatal morbidity rates remained stable across the study period, with no significant variations identified using the Grenoble classification (Additional Tables 5 and 6).

Discussion

Principal findings

From 2020 to 2023, the overall prevalence of IOL increased significantly in the French Burgundy Perinatal Network, even though the proportion of maternal and foetal pathologies remained largely stable. Analyses using the Grenoble classification highlighted that the increased relative risk of IOL was primarily driven by higher RRs in Group-5 [Single cephalic pregnancy ≥ 41 WG], Group-6 [Cephalic pregnancy with maternal pathology from 37 to 40 + 6 WG] and Group-7 [Cephalic pregnancy with foetal pathology from 37 to 40 + 6 WG]. A clinically meaningful increase in adverse perinatal outcomes was observed exclusively in these three groups, with a rise in PPH in Group-6, an increase in emergency caesarean sections in Group-7, and increases in both for Group-5. Neonatal morbidity rates remained stable throughout the study.

Strengths and limitations of this study

This study has several strengths. First, we included women from a large, prospectively collected regional cohort that provided recent, exhaustive and validated data from various types of maternity units. The characteristics of our regional population, and the reported medical practices are similar to those observed in France overall in 2021 [1]. The large source population made it possible to study rare outcomes such as PPH and to take risk factors into account. The variations in the prevalences of both IOL and its potential adverse perinatal outcomes were studied concomitantly and according to a clinically relevant classification, providing new knowledge on IOL practices.

Nonetheless, this study presents some limitations, which include its retrospective observational nature. The use of Grenoble classification, which requires a systematic collection of IOL indications for all women, was initiated on January 1, 2020, with this year serving as the reference point. This initiative took place in a context in which the induction rate in the Burgundy Perinatal Network was already 20.3% in 2016 and remained stable until 2020 (20.7%). Second, the Grenoble classification defines its categories based on the indication for IOL, which may limit its applicability in certain clinical contexts [17]. In addition, the distinction between “Groups 6 maternal pathology and 7 foetal pathology” and Group 8 “no medical indication” may be potentially confusing and some misclassification is possible. However, to limit misclassification, our birth registry includes a predefined list of the main maternal and fetal pathologies for Groups 6 and 7, which are prospectively collected by clinicians. In addition, by using broad indication categories, it eliminates the need for detailed diagnostic analysis, thereby significantly reducing inter-operator variability, and can be applied to monitor changes over time. Moreover, the indications used in this classification have been validated through a consensus among French national experts [17]. Third, we cannot exclude that the observed increase in adverse perinatal outcomes in Groups-5, -6 and − 7 could be linked to unaccounted risk factors. However, this is unlikely considering that the main risk factors for PPH—such as placenta praevia and multiple pregnancy—remained stable throughout the study period. In addition, detailed information on specific induction methods (e.g., pharmacological or mechanical) was not available in a standardized format, which prevented us from analysing possible variations in the techniques used over time. While induction methods have not changed considerably since 2020, the many possible combinations make such analyses difficult. It would be useful to study how different methods or combinations may be linked to specific risks, which supports the need for further dedicated studies. A fourth limitation is the lack of detailed quantitative blood loss data; however, PPH was uniformly defined using ICD-10 codes. All maternity units in our network use calibrated blood collection bags, and local practices did not change during the study period.

Interpretation

Our study found a continuous increase in the overall rate of IOL over time, which is in line with findings from other high-income countries [1–4, 25]. In the United States, the IOL rate rose from 23.8% in 2015 to 31.3% in 2020, reaching 31.9% in 2022 [3]. Similarly, the median IOL rate in the United Kingdom was 36.2% in 2022, though there were considerable variations between maternity units (19.2–53.4%) [4]. In France, the IOL rate remained stable at approximately 21% between 1995 and 2016 before rising significantly to 25.8% in 2021 [1].

Our analysis reveals that the overall increase in IOL rate is primarily driven by a rise in IOL among specific groups of women: those with foetal or maternal pathologies, and those beyond 41 WG. Notably, the observed increase in IOL rates does not appear to be fully explained by a higher prevalence of maternal or foetal conditions, as most of these rates remained relatively stable over the study period. Although a slight increase was observed for hypertension (from 3.5% to 4.1%), this alone is unlikely to account for the overall rise in induction rates. This suggests potential effects stemming from changes in clinical practice and/or recent updates to national guidelines that have broadened the indications for induction.

In France, national guidelines have only recently expanded the recommendations for induction to include cases of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy [26], which may account for the rise in IOL observed in Group-6 in our study. Therefore, the increase in IOL is more likely attributable to its gradual expansion in clinical practice beyond the existing guidelines, reflecting a growing preference for proactive obstetric management. This is particularly evident in our study for diabetes (Group-6), and foetal pathology (Group-7), including suspected cases of macrosomia, and small-for-gestational-age and/or intrauterine growth restriction, despite the fact that the indications for induction in these situations are not currently supported by strong evidence [14]. High-quality studies are needed to provide new levels of evidence regarding these indications for IOL. For instance, the trial by Gardosi et al. showed that induction between 38 + 0 and 38 + 4 weeks of gestation in pregnancies with suspected large-for-gestational-age foetuses may reduce the risk of shoulder dystocia without compromising neonatal outcomes [27]. Given that the French national guidelines date back to 2010 [28], a thorough reassessment appears warranted—similar to the recent updates undertaken in other countries [6, 29].

Our findings are reassuring in that we did not observe significant increases in the prevalence of neonatal morbidity, either overall or within specific groups. However, it is important to note the significant rise in maternal complications: the prevalence of PPH increased in Group-6, emergency caesarean sections in Group-7, and both complications increased significantly in Group-5. The observed increase in emergency caesareans in Group-5 and PPH in Group-6 may be attributable to the increased use of IOL among women presenting with maternal or foetal risk factors for these outcomes. For example, the highest prevalence of PPH observed in 2023 may reflect the combined effect of IOL and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, two major risk factors for PPH [30, 31], thereby contributing to an increased risk of haemorrhagic complications.

IOL for women beyond their due date remained the primary contributor to induction cases overall, with a significant proportional increase between 2020 and 2023. Over the same period, we observed a significant rise in emergency caesarean sections and PPH for this group. These findings highlight concurrent trends, suggesting a potential trade-off between foetal and maternal outcomes. Existing evidence indicates that inducing labour at 41 weeks reduces perinatal morbidity and mortality [32, 33] but may increase the risk of emergency caesarean birth [34]. This aligns with our findings, emphasizing the need for individualized decision-making regarding IOL timing. The ongoing PREDICT study aims to refine predictive models to identify women who are likely to give birth spontaneously between 39 and 40 weeks versus those who may benefit from early induction, potentially improving outcomes for both mothers and neonates [35].

Another key finding is that the IOL rate among women without a medical indication has remained stable, despite the results of the ARRIVE trial [5] and the 2022 WHO recommendations, allowing women to opt for labour induction from 39 + 0 weeks in well-equipped settings [7, 36]. Beyond clinical guidelines, it is also essential to consider whether elective labour induction is being actively offered to women, and how they perceive this option. Understanding women’s preferences and whether they feel empowered and sufficiently informed to make such choices is crucial, especially in light of recent recommendations that support shared decision-making [37].

Conclusion

Between 2020 and 2023, the prevalence of labour induction significantly increased across all levels of maternity care in the Burgundy region of France, particularly among women with post-term pregnancies, women with maternal pathologies and women with foetal pathologies. Furthermore, it is worrying to note that these groups also experienced a clinically meaningful increase in maternal complications over a four-year period. Given the increase in the prevalence of labour inductions, healthcare providers and researchers should focus on the potential associated benefits and complications in order to ensure safe and evidence-based obstetric care.
