Constructing characteristic initial data for three dimensional compressible Euler equations
Yu-Xuan WANG, Si-Fan YU, Pin YU

Abstract
This paper resolves the characteristic initial data problem for the three-dimensional compressible Euler equations — an open problem analogous to Christodoulou’s characteristic initial value formulation for the vacuum Einstein field equations in general relativity. Within the framework of acoustical geometry, we prove that for any “initial cone” C 0 ⊂ 𝒟 = [ 0 , T ] × ℝ 3 C_{0}\subset\mathcal{D}=[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{3} with initial data ( ρ ̊ , v ̊ , s ̊ ) (\mathring{\rho},\mathring{v},\mathring{s}) given at S 0 , 0 = C 0 ∩ Σ 0 S_{0,0}=C_{0}\cap\Sigma_{0} , arbitrary smooth entropy function s s and angular velocity v̸ \not{v} determine smooth initial data ( ρ , v , s ) (\rho,v,s) on C 0 C_{0} that render C 0 C_{0} characteristic. Differing from the intersecting-hypersurface case by Speck-Yu [ 19 ] and the symmetric reduction case by Lisibach [ 11 ] , our vector field method recursively determines all (including 0 0 -th) order derivatives of the solution along C 0 C_{0} via transport equations and wave equations. This work provides a complete characteristic data construction for admissible hypersurfaces in the 3D compressible Euler system, introducing useful tools and providing novel aspects for studies of the long-time dynamics of the compressible Euler flow.



1
Introduction to the characteristic problem for Euler equations

We study the Euler system (originally introduced in
[
10
]
) in dimension three which governs the motion of an ideal compressible inviscid fluid in three dimensions:

\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}+v\cdot\nabla)\rho&=-\rho\nabla\cdot v,\\
(\partial_{t}+v\cdot\nabla)v&=-\rho^{-1}\nabla p,\\
(\partial_{t}+v\cdot\nabla)s&=0.\end{cases}

(1.1)

The functions
\rho,p,s
and
v=v^{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{1}}+v^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{2}}+v^{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{3}}
are the density, pressure, entropy and velocity of the gas, respectively. They all depend on the spacetime variables
(t,x^{1},x^{2},x^{3})
.
Throughout the paper, we choose
\rho
,
v
and
s
as independent thermodynamical variables. The pressure
p
is then a function of the state of the density and the pressure, that is,
p(t,x)=p(\rho(t,x),s(t,x))
and
p
is smooth with respect to
\rho
and
s
. The sound speed
c
is then a positive function in
\rho
and
s
given by the formula
c^{2}=\dfrac{\partial p}{\partial\rho}(\rho,s)>0
.

1.1
The acoustical metric and the characteristic hypersurfaces for sound waves

If we choose
p
,
v
and
s
as the independent thermodynamical variables, the Euler equations can be expressed as

\begin{cases}&\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}+v\cdot\nabla v+\rho^{-1}\nabla p=0,\\
&\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}+(v\cdot\nabla)p=-\rho c^{2}(\nabla\cdot v),\\
&\frac{\partial s}{\partial t}+(v\cdot\nabla)s=0.\end{cases}

(1.2)

We regard the above system as a first-order hyperbolic system and we also assume that the solution exists on a spacetime region
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We use
(t,x^{1},x^{2},x^{3},\omega,\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3})
as the standard coordinate systems for
T^{*}\mathcal{D}
. Therefore, the characteristic polynomial for (
1.2
) is a function defined
T^{*}\mathcal{D}
and is a fifth-degree homogeneous polynomial in
(\omega,\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3})
. More precisely, it is given by

H(t,x;\omega,\xi)=\left(\omega+v(t,x)\cdot\xi\right)^{3}\left((\omega+v(t,x)\cdot\xi)^{2}-c^{2}|\xi|^{2}\right).

The system has two different types of characteristic waves. The first type corresponds to the factor
\omega+v(t,x)\cdot\xi
of
H(t,x;\omega,\xi)
. It consists of two families of transverse vorticity wave and one family of entropy waves. The second type corresponds to the factor
(\omega+v(t,x)\cdot\xi)^{2}-c^{2}|\xi|^{2}
of
H(t,x;\omega,\xi)
, which gives the sound waves. We then define

H_{{}_{\rm sound}}(t,x;\omega,\xi)=(\omega+v(t,x)\cdot\xi)^{2}-c^{2}|\xi|^{2}.

In the sequel, the word
characteristic
refers to the characteristic hypersurfaces of the sound waves, i.e., the characteristic hypersurfaces defined by the Hamiltonian
H_{{}_{\rm sound}}(t,x;\omega,\xi)
.

We also recall the following definition of the characteristic hypersurfaces. Let
C\subset\mathcal{D}
be a smooth hypersurface. We assume that
C
is locally defined as a level set of a smooth function
\phi
on
\mathcal{D}
so that the following eikonal equation is satisfied

H_{{}_{\rm sound}}(d\phi)=0.

(1.3)

The spacetime region
\mathcal{D}
can be equipped with the so-called
acoustical metric

g
:

g=-c^{2}dt^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}(dx^{i}-v^{i}dt)^{2}.

This is a Lorentzian metric. The inverse metric on
T^{*}D
can then be computed as

g^{-1}=-\frac{1}{c^{2}}(\partial_{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}v^{i}\partial_{i})\otimes(\partial_{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}v^{i}\partial_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\partial_{i}\otimes\partial_{i}.

We recall that the classic Hamiltonian for the geodesic flow of
g
(on the cotangent bundle of
\mathcal{D}
) is then given by

H_{{}_{\rm geodesic}}(\omega,\xi;t,x)=g^{\mu\nu}\xi_{\mu}\xi_{\nu}.

where
\xi_{\mu}=(\omega,\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3})
and we use the Einstein summation convention in the above formula. By the explicit formula of
g^{-1}
, we derive that

H_{{}_{\rm geodesic}}(\omega,\xi;t,x)=|\omega+v(t,x)\cdot\xi|^{2}-c^{2}|\xi|^{2}.

Therefore,
H_{{}_{\rm geodesic}}(\omega,\xi;t,x)=H_{{}_{\rm sound}}(\omega,\xi;t,x)
. In particular, by (
1.3
), a characteristic hypersurface
C
for sound waves is then locally defined by a smooth function
\phi
on
\mathcal{D}
so that

H_{{}_{\rm geodesic}}(d\phi)=0.

We then conclude that the characteristic hypersurfaces for sound waves are indeed null hypersurfaces for the acoustical metric
g
. These hypersurfaces are ruled by null geodesics. The standard geometric techniques used for null hypersurfaces in general relativity, see
[
5
,
7
]
, can then be adapted to study sound wave characteristic hypersurfaces.

1.3
Motivations

The study of hyperbolic PDEs on characteristic hypersurfaces has played a central role in various breakthrough results over the last decades (see
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
). The general formulation of the characteristic initial value problem (CharIVP) is expected to be useful for studying solution dynamics. This is either because characteristic initial data offers greater freedom than Cauchy data in certain contexts, or because it arises naturally in specific problems.

As an example, CharIVP has been fundamental in general relativity for investigating trapped surface formation
[
5
]
. In studying black hole formation mechanisms, the Cauchy approach requires solving an overdetermined elliptic system – the constraint equations – which presents significant technical difficulties. In contrast, the characteristic approach essentially reduces the initial data specification to solving a system of ordinary differential equations, offering precise control over the resulting initial values.

Another important example involves the construction of maximal classical developments in hyperbolic PDEs. Describing this maximal development – the largest possible spacetime region uniquely determined by initial data where classical solutions remain valid – represents a fundamental challenge (see
[
1
,
2
,
4
,
6
]
for specific analyses and
[
3
]
for a comprehensive survey). For the 3D compressible Euler equations, constructing maximal classical development requires understanding CharIVP. CharIVP is crucial for Cauchy horizon formation in shock-forming solutions
[
1
]
, as the horizon is a degenerate acoustical null hypersurface. CharIVP also connects to the shock development problem (a characteristic initial boundary value problem)
[
6
]
. Given this understanding, shock reflection and interaction problems
[
12
,
13
,
20
]
similarly require CharIVP settings, specifically double-null foliation. There are very few results regarding CharIVP for the compressible Euler equations. The lack of results has two causes:
1)
The geometric formulation of 3D compressible Euler with dynamic vorticity and entropy was only recently discovered
[
9
,
15
,
18
]
, revealing structures crucial for CharIVP.
2)
For multiple-speed systems, establishing a functional framework on double null foliations is challenging because null hypersurfaces are characteristic only for part of the system. Specifically, vorticity and entropy propagate transversally to null hypersurfaces, requiring different treatment than the characteristic flow components, while both parts remain coupled.

-\frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}}&-\frac{v^{2}v^{1}}{c^{2}}&1-\frac{(v^{2})^{2}}{c^{2}}&-\frac{v^{2}v^{3}}{c^{2}}\\
-\frac{v^{3}}{c^{2}}&-\frac{v^{3}v^{1}}{c^{2}}&-\frac{v^{3}v^{2}}{c^{2}}&1-\frac{(v^{3})^{2}}{c^{2}}\end{array}}\right).

(2.1)

We also collect the formulas of the corresponding Christoffel symbols as follows:

\begin{cases}&\Gamma_{00}^{0}=\frac{1}{2c^{2}}\left[\partial_{0}(c^{2})+v(-c^{2}+|v|^{2})\right],\\
&\Gamma_{00}^{i}=\frac{v^{i}}{2c^{2}}\left[\partial_{0}(c^{2})+v(-c^{2}+|v|^{2})\right]-\partial_{0}v^{i}-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{i}(-c^{2}+|v|^{2}),\\
&\Gamma_{i0}^{0}=-\frac{1}{2c^{2}}\partial_{i}(-c^{2}+|v|^{2})+\frac{v^{k}}{2c^{2}}(\partial_{i}v^{k}-\partial_{k}v^{i}),\\
&\Gamma_{i0}^{j}=-\frac{v^{j}}{2c^{2}}\partial_{i}(-c^{2}+|v|^{2})+\frac{v^{j}v^{k}}{2c^{2}}(\partial_{i}v^{k}-\partial_{k}v^{i})-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{i}v^{j}-\partial_{j}v^{i}),\\
&\Gamma_{ij}^{0}=\frac{1}{2c^{2}}(\partial_{i}v^{j}+\partial_{j}v^{i}),\ \ \Gamma_{ij}^{k}=\frac{v^{k}}{2c^{2}}(\partial_{i}v^{j}+\partial_{j}v^{i}).\end{cases}

(2.2)

2.1
The acoustical coordinate system

We refer to
[
4
]
and
[
8
]
for details of the construction of the acoustical coordinates. A acoustical (local) coordinate system on
\mathcal{D}=[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
consists of four smooth functions
t
,
u
and
\vartheta^{A}
with
A=1,2
. The temporal function
t
is defined as
t=x^{0}
.

We assume that there exists a smooth function
u_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma_{0})
so that
S_{0,0}=C_{0}\cap\Sigma_{0}=u_{0}^{-1}(0)
and
\nabla u_{0}\big|_{S_{0,0}}\neq 0
. We also assume that
u_{0}>0
corresponds to the interior of
S_{0,0}
inside
\Sigma_{0}
. This is the region where
(\rho,v,s)
is already given. Moreover, the level sets of
u_{0}
provide a smooth foliation of
\Sigma_{0}
around
S_{0,0}
.

To construct the acoustical function
u
, first of all, we require the acoustical function
u
is equal to
u_{0}
on
\Sigma_{0}
. We then define
C_{u}
to be the null hypersurfaces consisting of null geodesics emanating from each level set of
u
on
\Sigma_{0}
. Then, we require
C_{u}
to be the level sets of
u
and this defines
u
on
\mathcal{D}
. We also define
S_{t,u}=\Sigma_{t}\cap C_{u}
. Therefore, we have
\Sigma_{t}=\displaystyle\bigcup_{u}S_{t,u}
.

We choose
the future-pointed vector field
L
to be the generators of the null geodesics on
C_{u}
in such a way that
L(t)=1
. The inverse density function
\mu
measures the temporal density of the foliations
\{C_{u}\}_{u\geqslant 0}
and it is defined as

\mu^{-1}=-g(Dt,Du),

where
D
is the Levi-Civita connection with respect to the acoustical metric
g
.

We then consider the normal vector field
T
of the foliation
\displaystyle\bigcup_{u}S_{t,u}
on
\Sigma_{t}
. It is uniquely defined by the following three conditions:

(1)\ T\ \text{is tangent to}\ \Sigma_{t};\ \ (2)\ {\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T\ \text{is $g$-perpendicular to}}\ S_{t,u};\ \ (3)\ Tu=1.

To define the angular coordinate functions
\vartheta^{1}
and
\vartheta^{2}
, for all
u
, we can first fix a local coordinate system
(\vartheta^{A})_{A=1,2}
on
S_{0,0}
. The next step is to define
\vartheta
on
\Sigma_{0}
by extending
(\vartheta^{A})_{A=1,2}
through the following equation on
\Sigma_{0}
:

T(\vartheta^{A})=0,\ \ \ \vartheta^{A}\big|_{S_{0,0}}=\vartheta^{A}.

Since the
1
-parameter diffeomorphism group generated by
L
maps
S_{0,u}
to
S_{t,u}
, we then take
(\vartheta^{A})_{A=1,2}
as local coordinate system on
S_{t,u}
by the above
1
-parameter group, i.e., we use the following ODE system to extend
(\vartheta^{A})_{A=1,2}
:

L(\vartheta^{A})=0,\ \ A=1,2.

Therefore, we have constructed an acoustical coordinate system
(t,u,\vartheta^{1},\vartheta^{2})
on
\mathcal{D}
.

We also denote
X_{A}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\vartheta^{A}}
. In the acoustical coordinates
(t,u,\vartheta)
, we have

L=\frac{\partial}{\partial t},\quad T=\frac{\partial}{\partial u}-\sum_{A=1,2}\Xi^{A}\frac{\partial}{\partial\vartheta^{A}},\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial\vartheta^{A}}=X_{A},

(2.3)

where
\Xi^{A}(A=1,2)
are smooth functions. The acoustical metric then can be expressed as

g=-\mu(du\otimes dt+dt\otimes du)+\kappa^{2}du\otimes du+\not{g}_{AB}(\Xi^{A}du+d\vartheta^{A})\otimes(\Xi^{B}du+d\vartheta^{B}),

where
\not{g}_{AB}=g(X_{A},X_{B})
and the repeated capital latin indices
A
and
B
are understood as summations over
\{1,2\}
.

Let
B=\partial_{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}v^{i}\partial_{i}
be the traditional material derivative vector field. We then have
B(t)=1
and

g(B,B)=-c^{2},\ \ g(B,\partial_{i})=0,\ i=1,2,3.

This shows that
c^{-1}B
is the future-pointed unit normal to
\Sigma_{t}
.

Let
\kappa^{2}=g(T,T)
, we can compute that
\mu=c\kappa
. We also define the unit vector
\widehat{T}=\kappa^{-1}T
. The null vector field
L
can be represented as

L=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+v-c\widehat{T}=\partial_{t}-\sum_{i=1}^{3}(c{\widehat{T}}^{i}-v^{i})\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}},

where
\widehat{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}{\widehat{T}}^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}
is the expression of
{\widehat{T}}
on
\Sigma_{t}
in the Cartesian coordinates.

2.2
The acoustical structure equations

We first introduce three different fundamental forms. For the embedded spacelike hypersurface
\Sigma_{t}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{D}
, since
c^{-1}B
is the unit normal, we define

k(X,Y)=g\left(D_{Y}\big(c^{-1}B\big),X\right)\ \ \Leftrightarrow\ \ ck(X,Y)=g\left(D_{Y}B,X\right),

where
X,Y
are tangent vectors to
\Sigma_{t}
.
In the Cartesian coordinates, we have

ck_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{i}v^{j}+\partial_{j}v^{i}).

For the null hypersurfaces
C_{u}
, we define

\chi(X,Y):=g(\nabla_{X}L,Y),

where
X,Y
are tangent vectors to
S_{t,u}
.
Similarly, for the embedding
S_{t,u}\hookrightarrow\Sigma_{t}
, using its unit normal
{\widehat{T}}
, we define

\theta(X,Y)=g\left(\nabla_{Y}{\widehat{T}},X\right)\ \ \Leftrightarrow\ \ \kappa\theta(X,Y)=g\left(\nabla_{Y}T,X\right),

where
X,Y
are tangent vectors to
S_{t,u}
. The
\nabla
is the Levi-Civita connection to the standard Euclidean metric on
\Sigma_{t}
. We remark that
\nabla=D\big|_{\Sigma_{t}}
since
g\big|_{\Sigma_{t}}
is the standard Euclidean metric. By
L=B-c{\widehat{T}}=c(c^{-1}B-{\widehat{T}})
, for all
X,Y
tangent vectors to
S_{t,u}
, we have

\chi(X,Y)=c\big(k(X,Y)-\theta(X,Y)\big).

We also define torsion forms

\zeta(X):=g(D_{X}L,T),\ \ \eta(X):=-g(D_{X}T,L),

where
X
is a tangent vector field to
S_{t,u}
. We can check that

\eta=\zeta+\not{d}(\mu),

where
\not{d}
is the differential on
S_{t,u}
. The torsion
\zeta
can also be expressed as

\zeta(X)=\kappa\big(ck(X,{\widehat{T}})-X(c)\big),

(2.4)

where
X
is a tangent vector field to
S_{t,u}
.

By the above notations, we can express the Levi-Civita connection in the frame
(L,T,X^{1},X^{2})
as follows:

\begin{cases}D_{L}L&=\mu^{-1}L(\mu)L,\\
D_{T}L&=\eta^{A}X_{A}-c^{-1}L(\kappa)L,\\
D_{L}T&=-\zeta^{A}X_{A}-c^{-1}L(\kappa)L,\\
D_{X_{A}}L&=-\mu^{-1}\zeta_{A}L+\chi_{A}{}^{B}X_{B},\\
D_{X_{A}}T&=c^{-1}\kappa\varepsilon_{A}L+\mu^{-1}\eta_{A}T+\kappa\theta_{A}{}^{B}X_{B},\\
D_{T}T&=c^{-2}\kappa(Tc+L\kappa)L+\big[c^{-1}(Tc+L\kappa)+\kappa^{-1}T\kappa\big]T-\kappa\not{g}^{AB}X_{B}(\kappa)X_{A},\\
D_{X_{A}}X_{B}&=\not{D}_{X_{A}}X_{B}+c^{-1}\not{k}_{AB}L+\mu^{-1}\chi_{AB}T.\end{cases}

(2.5)

We refer to
[
8
, Section 3]
for detailed computations.

From (
2.5
), it is straightforward to derive the following commutation formulas:

[L,T]=-\left(\zeta^{A}+\eta^{A}\right)X_{A},\ [L,X_{A}]=0,\

(2.6)

Since
T=\kappa{\widehat{T}}
, we also have

[L,{\widehat{T}}]=-\frac{1}{\kappa}\left(\zeta^{A}+\eta^{A}\right)X_{A}-\frac{1}{\kappa}L(\kappa){\widehat{T}}.

(2.7)

The most important structure equation in the paper is the transport equation for the inverse density
\kappa
along
L
:

L\kappa=-Tc+\sum_{j=1}^{3}{\widehat{T}}^{j}T(v^{j}).

(2.8)

where
{\widehat{T}}^{i}
are functions. We also recall that
\widehat{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}{\widehat{T}}^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}
is the expression of
{\widehat{T}}
on
\Sigma_{t}
in the Cartesian coordinates. For
{\widehat{T}}^{i}
’s, its derivatives can be expressed as

L({\widehat{T}}^{i})\partial_{i}=\not{g}^{AB}\left[-{\widehat{T}}^{j}X_{A}(v^{j})+X_{A}(c)\right]X_{B},

(2.9)

T({\widehat{T}}^{i})\partial_{i}=-\not{g}^{AB}X_{B}(\kappa)X_{A},

(2.10)

and

X_{A}({\widehat{T}}^{i})\partial_{i}=\not{g}^{CB}\theta_{BA}X_{C}.

(2.11)

By the formula (
2.5
) of the covariant derivatives and the fact that
\chi=c(\not{k}-\theta)
, we can decompose the wave operator
\Box_{g}
in the null frame
\{E_{1},E_{2},E_{3},E_{4}\}=\{X_{1},X_{2},T,L\}
. Indeed, for a given smooth function
f
, we can compute that

\begin{split}\Box_{g}f&=\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^{4}g^{E_{\mu}E_{\nu}}\left(E_{\mu}\big(E_{\nu}(f)\big)-\big(D_{E_{\mu}}{E_{\nu}}\big)(f)\right)\\
&=-\frac{2}{\mu}LT(f)+\not{\triangle}_{\not{g}}(f)-c^{-2}L^{2}(f)\\
&\ \ \ +c^{-3}L(c)L(f)-(\mu c)^{-1}L(\kappa)L(f)-c^{-1}\text{tr}\not{k}\cdot L(f)-(\text{tr}\not{k}-\text{tr}\theta){\widehat{T}}(f)-\frac{2}{\mu}\zeta^{A}X_{A}(f).\end{split}

(2.12)

2.3
Wave equation for the density function

We derive a wave equations with respect to the acoustical metric for the density function
\rho
in this section. We first rewrite the Euler equations in a geometric form. In terms of
B=\partial_{t}+v\cdot\nabla
, we can write the Euler equations as follows:

\begin{cases}&B(\rho)=-\rho\cdot{\rm div}(v),\\
&B(v^{i})=-\rho^{-1}\nabla^{i}p,\\
&B(s)=0.\end{cases}

(2.13)

For an arbitrary vector field
X
on
\mathcal{D}
, we can write it uniquely in the following form

X=-\frac{1}{c^{2}}g(X,B)B+X^{\parallel},

where
X^{\parallel}
is tangential to
\Sigma_{t}
.
For arbitrary smooth function
\phi
, in the orthonormal frame
\{e_{0}=B,e_{1}=\partial_{1},e_{2}=\partial_{2},e_{3}=\partial_{3}\}
, we compute

\displaystyle\Box_{g}\phi

\displaystyle=g^{\mu\nu}\big[e_{\mu}e_{\nu}(\phi)-(\nabla_{e_{\mu}}e_{\nu})(\phi)\big]=g^{BB}\big[B^{2}(\phi)-(\nabla_{B}B)(\phi)\big]+g^{ij}\big[e_{i}e_{j}(\phi)-(\nabla_{e_{i}}e_{j})(\phi)\big]

\displaystyle=g^{BB}\big[B^{2}(\phi)-(\nabla_{B}B)(\phi)\big]+g^{ij}\big[e_{i}e_{j}(\phi)-(\nabla_{e_{i}}e_{j})^{\parallel}(\phi)+\frac{1}{c^{2}}g(\nabla_{e_{i}}e_{j},B)B(\phi)\big]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{c^{2}}B^{2}(\phi)+\frac{1}{c^{2}}(\nabla_{B}B)(\phi)+\triangle\phi-\frac{g^{ij}k_{ij}}{c}B(\phi)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{c^{2}}B^{2}(\phi)+\frac{1}{c^{2}}(\nabla_{B}B)(\phi)+\triangle\phi-\frac{{\rm div}(v)}{c^{2}}B(\phi).

Since
\nabla_{B}B
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\nabla_{B}B=\frac{1}{c}B(c)B+c\sum_{i=1}^{3}\partial_{i}(c)\partial_{i},

we obtain that

\Box_{g}\phi=-\frac{1}{c^{2}}B^{2}(\phi)+\frac{1}{c^{3}}B(c)B(\phi)+\frac{1}{c}\partial_{i}(c)\partial_{i}(\phi)+\triangle\phi-\frac{{\rm div}(v)}{c^{2}}B(\phi).

(2.14)

By (
2.13
), we can derive the wave equation for
\rho
as follows:

\displaystyle\Box_{g}(\rho)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{c^{2}}B^{2}(\rho)+\frac{1}{c^{3}}B(c)B(\rho)+\frac{1}{c}\nabla(c)\cdot\nabla(\rho)+\triangle\rho-\frac{{\rm div}(v)}{c^{2}}B(\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c^{2}}B\left(\rho{\rm div}(v)\right)+\frac{1}{c^{3}}B(c)B(\rho)+\frac{1}{c}\nabla(c)\cdot\nabla(\rho)+\triangle\rho-\frac{{\rm div}(v)}{c^{2}}B(\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho}{c^{2}}B\big({\rm div}(v)\big)+\frac{1}{c^{3}}B(c)B(\rho)+\frac{1}{c}\nabla(c)\cdot\nabla(\rho)+\triangle\rho

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho}{c^{2}}\left(\frac{\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla p}{\rho^{2}}-\frac{\triangle p}{\rho}-\nabla_{i}v^{j}\nabla_{j}v^{i}\right)+\frac{1}{c^{3}}B(c)B(\rho)+\frac{1}{c}\nabla(c)\cdot\nabla(\rho)+\triangle\rho.

We notice that

\nabla^{i}p=c^{2}\nabla^{i}\rho+\frac{\partial p}{\partial s}\nabla^{i}s,

and

\triangle p=c^{2}\triangle\rho+\frac{\partial p}{\partial s}\triangle s+\frac{\partial c^{2}}{\partial\rho}|\nabla\rho|^{2}+2\frac{\partial c^{2}}{\partial s}\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla s+\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial s^{2}}|\nabla s|^{2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Box_{g}(\rho)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c^{2}}\Big\{\frac{c^{2}}{\rho}|\nabla\rho|^{2}+\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial p}{\partial s}\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla s-\big[c^{2}\triangle\rho+\frac{\partial p}{\partial s}\triangle s+\frac{\partial c^{2}}{\partial\rho}|\nabla\rho|^{2}+2\frac{\partial c^{2}}{\partial s}\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla s+\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial s^{2}}|\nabla s|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle-\rho\nabla_{i}v^{j}\nabla_{j}v^{i}\Big\}+\frac{1}{c^{3}}B(c)B(\rho)+\frac{1}{c}\nabla(c)\cdot\nabla(\rho)+\triangle\rho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c^{2}}\Big\{\frac{c^{2}}{\rho}|\nabla\rho|^{2}+\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial p}{\partial s}\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla s-\big[\frac{\partial p}{\partial s}\triangle s+2c\frac{\partial c}{\partial\rho}|\nabla\rho|^{2}+4c\frac{\partial c}{\partial s}\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla s+\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial s^{2}}|\nabla s|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle-\rho\nabla_{i}v^{j}\nabla_{j}v^{i}\Big\}+\frac{1}{c^{3}}B(c)B(\rho)+\frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial c}{\partial\rho}|\nabla(\rho)|^{2}+\frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial c}{\partial s}\nabla(\rho)\cdot\nabla(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c^{2}}\Big\{\frac{c^{2}}{\rho}|\nabla\rho|^{2}+\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial p}{\partial s}\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla s-\big[\frac{\partial p}{\partial s}\triangle s+\frac{\partial c^{2}}{\partial s}\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla s+\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial s^{2}}|\nabla s|^{2}\big]-\rho\nabla_{i}v^{j}\nabla_{j}v^{i}\Big\}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{c^{3}}B(c)B(\rho)-\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial c}{\partial\rho}|\nabla(\rho)|^{2}+\frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial c}{\partial s}\nabla(\rho)\cdot\nabla(s)\right)}_{\frac{1}{c}\nabla(c)\cdot\nabla(\rho)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c^{2}}\left(\frac{c^{2}}{\rho}|\nabla\rho|^{2}-\rho\nabla_{i}v^{j}\nabla_{j}v^{i}\right)+\frac{1}{c^{2}}\Big\{\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial p}{\partial s}-\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial s\partial\rho}\right)\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla s-\big[\frac{\partial p}{\partial s}\triangle s+\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial s^{2}}|\nabla s|^{2}\big]\Big\}

\displaystyle-\frac{g(Dc,D\rho)}{c}.

Hence,

\Box_{g}(\rho)=\frac{1}{\rho}|\nabla\rho|^{2}-\frac{\rho}{c^{2}}\nabla_{i}v^{j}\nabla_{j}v^{i}-\frac{g(Dc,D\rho)}{c}+\frac{1}{c^{2}}\left\{\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial p}{\partial s}-\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial s\partial\rho}\right)\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla s-\big[\frac{\partial p}{\partial s}\triangle s+\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial s^{2}}|\nabla s|^{2}\big]\right\}.

In view of

\displaystyle|\nabla\rho|^{2}=g(D\rho,D\rho)+\frac{B(\rho)^{2}}{c^{2}}=g(D\rho,D\rho)+\frac{\rho^{2}}{c^{2}}{\rm div}(v)^{2},

we conclude that

\begin{split}\Box_{g}(\rho)&=\frac{\rho}{c^{2}}\big({\rm div}(v)^{2}-\nabla_{i}v^{j}\nabla_{j}v^{i}\big)+\frac{g(D\rho,D\rho)}{\rho}-\frac{g(Dc,D\rho)}{c}\\
&\ \ +\frac{1}{c^{2}}\left\{\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial p}{\partial s}-\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial s\partial\rho}\right)\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla s-\big[\frac{\partial p}{\partial s}\triangle s+\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial s^{2}}|\nabla s|^{2}\big]\right\}\end{split}

(2.15)

2.4
Transport equations for Riemann invariants along characteristic hypersurfaces

On each
\Sigma_{t}
, we consider the orthogonal decomposition of
v
as

v=v^{{\widehat{T}}}\cdot{\widehat{T}}+\not{v},

where
\not{v}
is tangent to
S_{t,u}
. In view of the notations firstly introduced in Riemann’s original paper
[
17
]
, we define the
Riemann invariants
with respect to the null hypersurface
C_{u}
as follows:

\begin{cases}\underline{w}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int\frac{c(\rho,s)}{\rho}d\rho-v^{{\widehat{T}}}\right),\\
w=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int\frac{c(\rho,s)}{\rho}d\rho+v^{{\widehat{T}}}\right),\\
\not{w}=\not{v}.\end{cases}

(2.16)

For the sake of simplicity, we introduce two auxiliary functions

\Phi_{0}(\rho,s)=\int\frac{c(\rho,s)}{\rho}d\rho,\ \ \Psi_{0}(\rho,s)=\int\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial c}{\partial s}d\rho-\frac{1}{c\rho}\frac{\partial p}{\partial s}.

It is straightforward to check that the Euler equations (
2.13
) can be written as

\begin{cases}B(\Phi_{0})&=-c\cdot{\rm div}(v),\\
B(v^{i})&=-c\cdot\nabla_{i}\Phi_{0}+c\cdot\Psi_{0}\cdot\nabla_{i}s,\\
B(s)&=0.\end{cases}

(2.17)

The purpose of the current section is to derive transport equations for
w
and
\underline{w}
along
C_{u}
. We first transform (
2.17
) in a covariant form so that it suits the tensorial computations.

In view of the definition
\theta=g(D_{X}{\widehat{T}},Y)
, we have

{\rm div}(v)={\widehat{T}}(v_{\widehat{T}})+\not{\rm div}(\not{v})-g\left(\not{v},\nabla_{\widehat{T}}{\widehat{T}}\right)+v_{\widehat{T}}\cdot{\rm tr}_{\not{g}}\theta.

(2.18)

In view of of the definition
k(X,Y)=g(\nabla_{X}(c^{-1}B),Y)
, we have

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+v\cdot\nabla)v

\displaystyle=-D_{v}\partial_{0}+D_{B}v-c^{-1}k(v,v)B.

By (
2.2
), we compute

\displaystyle D_{v}\partial_{0}

\displaystyle=v^{i}\big(\Gamma_{i0}^{0}\partial_{0}+\Gamma_{i0}^{j}\partial_{j}\big)=-\frac{1}{2c^{2}}v(-c^{2}+|v|^{2})B-\frac{1}{2}v^{i}(\partial_{i}v^{j}-\partial_{j}v^{i})\partial_{j}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2c^{2}}v(-c^{2}+|v|^{2})B-\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{v}v+\frac{1}{4}\nabla(|v|^{2})

In view of
ck_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{i}v^{j}+\partial_{j}v^{i})
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2c^{2}}v(-c^{2}+|v|^{2})B-c^{-1}k(v,v)B=-\frac{1}{c}v(c)B.

Therefore,

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+v\cdot\nabla)v

\displaystyle=D_{B}v-c^{-1}v(c)B+\frac{1}{2}v^{i}(\partial_{i}v^{j}-\partial_{j}v^{i})\partial_{j}

\displaystyle=D_{B}v-c^{-1}v(c)B+\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{v}v-\frac{1}{4}\nabla(|v|^{2}).

By this equation together with (
2.18
), the equation (
2.17
) then can be rewritten as

\begin{cases}&B(\Phi_{0})=-c\left[{\widehat{T}}(v_{\widehat{T}})+\not{\rm div}(\not{v})-g\left(\not{v},\nabla_{\widehat{T}}{\widehat{T}}\right)+v_{\widehat{T}}\cdot{\rm tr}_{\not{g}}\theta\right],\\
&D_{B}v-c^{-1}v(c)\cdot B+\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{v}v-\frac{1}{4}\nabla(|v|^{2})=-c\left[{\widehat{T}}\left(\Phi_{0}\right){\widehat{T}}+g^{AB}X_{B}\left(\Phi_{0}\right)X_{A}\right]\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+c\Psi_{0}\left[{\widehat{T}}\left(s\right){\widehat{T}}+g^{AB}X_{B}\left(s\right)X_{A}\right].\end{cases}

By
L=B-c{\widehat{T}}
and
v=v^{{\widehat{T}}}{\widehat{T}}+\not{v}
, we obtain that

\begin{cases}&L\left(\Phi_{0}\right)=-c\left[{\widehat{T}}\left(\Phi_{0}+v_{\widehat{T}}\right)+\not{\rm div}(\not{v})-g\left(\not{v},\nabla_{\widehat{T}}{\widehat{T}}\right)+v_{\widehat{T}}\cdot{\rm tr}_{\not{g}}\theta\right],\\
&D_{L}v+cD_{{\widehat{T}}}v-c^{-1}v(c)\cdot L-v(c)\cdot{\widehat{T}}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{v}v-\frac{1}{4}\nabla(|v|^{2})\\
&\qquad\quad=-c\left[{\widehat{T}}\left(\Phi_{0}\right){\widehat{T}}+g^{AB}X_{B}\left(\Phi_{0}\right)X_{A}\right]+c\Psi_{0}\left[{\widehat{T}}\left(s\right){\widehat{T}}+g^{AB}X_{B}\left(s\right)X_{A}\right].\end{cases}

(2.19)

To proceed, we then further decompose
D_{L}v
and
D_{{\widehat{T}}}v
using the frame
(L,T,X^{1},X^{2})
. Since
D_{L}T=-\zeta^{A}X_{A}-c^{-1}L(\kappa)L

2.5
The hypersurface
C_{0}
is characteristic

To study the condition for
C_{0}
being characteristic, we first introduce the concept of the speed functions defined on
C_{0}
. Given a point
p\in C_{0}\cap\Sigma_{t}=S_{t,0}
, we choose a new Cartesian coordinate system
(y^{1},y^{3},y^{3})
on
\Sigma_{t}
so that
p=(0,0,0)
and
T_{p}S_{t,0}=\{y^{3}=0\}
. We require that the
\partial_{3}
direction is opposite to the
{\widehat{T}}
direction and that
{\partial_{i}}(i=1,2,3)
is a set of standard orthogonal basis on
\Sigma_{t}
. We remark that
y^{3}
is then uniquely determined and
(y^{1},y^{2})
is unique up to a rotation. Therefore,
S_{t,0}\subset\Sigma_{t}
can be locally represented uniquely as a graph

y^{3}=\phi(t,y^{1},y^{2}),

where
\phi
depends smoothly on
t
. Moreover, we have

\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y^{1}}(t,0,0)=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y^{2}}(t,0,0)=0.

We define
the speed of
C_{0}
at
p
to be

\mathbf{V}(p):=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}(t,0,0).

In fact, there is a geometric interpretation of the function
\mathbf{V}
. First of all, exists a unique
2
-dimension plane
H
passing through
p
in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
spanned by the
{\widehat{T}}(p)
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{0}}
. Therefore,
H\cap C_{0}
is a smooth curve in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. If we parametrize the curve by
x^{0}
, the speed of the curve is exactly
\mathbf{V}(p)
. This shows that the speed function defined on
C_{0}
is independent of the choice of the above coordinates.

The tangent space
T_{p}C_{0}
is spanned by

V_{0}=\partial_{t}\phi\partial_{3}+\partial_{0},\ V_{1}=\partial_{1}\phi\partial_{3}+\partial_{1},\ V_{2}=\partial_{2}\phi\partial_{3}+\partial_{2},

where
\partial_{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{i}}
.
Thus, the hypersurface
C_{0}
is null at the point
p
is equivalent to

\det\left({\begin{array}[]{ccc}g(V_{0},V_{0})&g(V_{0},V_{1})&g(V_{0},V_{2})\\
g(V_{1},V_{0})&g(V_{1},V_{1})&g(V_{1},V_{2})\\
g(V_{2},V_{0})&g(V_{2},V_{1})&g(V_{2},V_{2})\end{array}}\right)=0.

Since

\begin{cases}&g(V_{0},V_{0})=-c^{2}+|v|^{2}-2v^{3}\partial_{t}\phi+(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2},\\
&g(V_{0},V_{i})=\partial_{t}\phi\partial_{i}\phi-v^{3}\partial_{i}\phi-v^{i},\\
&g(V_{i},V_{i})=1+(\partial_{i}\phi)^{2},\\
&g(V_{1},V_{2})=\partial_{1}\phi\partial_{2}\phi,\end{cases}

we compute that

\displaystyle\det\left({\begin{array}[]{ccc}g(V_{0},V_{0})&g(V_{0},V_{1})&g(V_{0},V_{2})\\
g(V_{1},V_{0})&g(V_{1},V_{1})&g(V_{1},V_{2})\\
g(V_{2},V_{0})&g(V_{2},V_{1})&g(V_{2},V_{2})\end{array}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det\left({\begin{array}[]{ccc}-c^{2}+|v|^{2}-2v^{3}\partial_{t}\phi+(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2}&\partial_{t}\phi\partial_{1}\phi-v^{3}\partial_{1}\phi-v^{1}&\partial_{t}\phi\partial_{2}\phi-v^{3}\partial_{2}\phi-v^{2}\\
\partial_{t}\phi\partial_{1}\phi-v^{3}\partial_{1}\phi-v^{1}&1+(\partial_{1}\phi)^{2}&\partial_{1}\phi\partial_{2}\phi\\
\partial_{t}\phi\partial_{2}\phi-v^{3}\partial_{2}\phi-v^{2}&\partial_{1}\phi\partial_{2}\phi&1+(\partial_{2}\phi)^{2}\end{array}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det\left({\begin{array}[]{ccc}-c^{2}+|v|^{2}-2v^{3}\partial_{t}\phi+(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2}&-v^{1}&-v^{2}\\
-v^{1}&1&0\\
-v^{2}&0&1\end{array}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-c^{2}+(v^{3})^{2}-2v^{3}\partial_{t}\phi+(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2}=(v^{3}-\partial_{t}\phi)^{2}-c^{2}.

Since
T_{p}S_{t,0}=\{y^{3}=0\}
, we have
v^{3}=-v^{{\widehat{T}}}
. Therefore, the hypersurface
C_{0}
is (outgoing) null at the point
p
is equivalent to the following condition:

-v^{{\widehat{T}}}+c=\partial_{t}\phi.

We conclude that

Lemma 2.3
.

The following two statements are equivalent:

1)

The hypersurface
C_{0}
is an outgoing null hypersurface with respect to the acoustical metric
g
;

2)

The data
(\rho,v,s)
on
C_{0}
satisfies

-v^{{\widehat{T}}}+c=\mathbf{V}

(2.28)

where
c
is the sound speed determined by
(\rho,v,s)
on
C_{0}
.

Corollary 2.4

Given initial data
(\mathring{\rho},\mathring{v},\mathring{s})
inside
S_{0,0}
on
\Sigma_{0}
, for
C_{0}
being an outgoing null hypersurface with respect to the acoustical metric

:

-\mathring{v}^{{\widehat{T}}}+\mathring{c}=\mathbf{V}.

(2.29)

Remark 2.5

We consider the Goursat problem with two characteristic hypersurfaces

and

2.6
The precise statement of the main theorem

2.6.1
Determine

and

We recall that the restriction of the acoustical metric

is exactly the Euclidean metric
\sum_{i=1}^{3}dx^{i}\otimes dx^{i}
. Therefore, since
S_{t,0}=C_{0}\cap\Sigma_{t}
is given, the components of its unit normal

(

) are known along

. Since
\not{g}
and
\theta
are the first and second fundamental forms of

as an embedded hypersurface of

respectively,
\not{g}
and

is also known along

. To summarize,
{\widehat{T}}^{i},\not{g}
and
\theta
on

are already determined by the hypersurface

.

2.6.2
The free data
s
and
\not{v}

We now give the initial data
s
and
\not{v}
on
C_{0}
.

Since we have already prescribed initial data
(\mathring{\rho},\mathring{v},\mathring{s})
on
\Sigma_{0}
, all the derivatives of
(\rho,v,s)
have been determined on
S_{0,0}
. On the other hand, since
L=\partial_{t}+\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(v^{i}-c{\widehat{T}}^{i}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}
, therefore,
L\big|_{S_{0,0}}
is already given by the initial data
(\mathring{\rho},\mathring{v},\mathring{s})
on
\Sigma_{0}
. Therefore, for
f=s,\not{v}^{1},\not{v}^{2},\not{v}^{3}
where
\not{v}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\not{v}^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}
,
L^{k}(f)\big|_{S_{0,0}}
are all known for all

. We denote these quantities as

L^{k}(\mathring{s})\big|_{S_{0,0}},L^{k}(\mathring{\not{v}^{1}})\big|_{S_{0,0}},L^{k}(\mathring{\not{v}^{2}})\big|_{S_{0,0}},L^{k}(\mathring{\not{v}^{3}})\big|_{S_{0,0}}.

We fix an arbitrary smooth function
s\in C^{\infty}(C_{0})
with the following compatible condition

L^{k}(s)\big|_{S_{0,0}}=L^{k}(\mathring{s})\big|_{S_{0,0}},\ \ k\geqslant 0.

(2.30)

We fix an arbitrary smooth vector field
\not{v}
which is tangential to all
S_{t,0}
where

so that

L^{k}(\not{v}^{i})\big|_{S_{0,0}}=L^{k}(\mathring{\not{v}^{i}})\big|_{S_{0,0}},\ \ i=1,2,3,\ k\geqslant 0.

The conditions (

) and (

.

2.6.3
Determine

and
\underline{w}

We show that given

and

and

on

. Therefore, we have obtained the values of

. The key idea is to make use the first equation of (

along

, in view of Lemma

L(\underline{w})=-\frac{1}{2}\left[c\not{\rm div}(\not{v})+c{\rm tr}_{\not{g}}\theta\cdot v^{{\widehat{T}}}+\theta(\not{v},\not{v})+\not{v}\big(\mathbf{V}\big)\right]-\frac{1}{2}\Psi_{0}\cdot c{\widehat{T}}(s).

According to the third equation of (

), we replace

Since the values of
\not{v},s,\mathbf{V}
and
\theta

are known and also the intrinsic geometry of

is given, the values of
\not{\rm div}(\not{v}),{\rm tr}_{\not{g}}\theta,\theta(\not{v},\not{v}),\not{v}\big(\mathbf{V}\big)
and

are known. It remains to deal with the terms

and
\Psi_{0}
on the righthand side of the above equation.

Be the definition (
2.16
) of Riemann invariants, we have
\Phi_{0}=w+\underline{w}
. Since

is a function of
w
and
\underline{w}
. Similarly, we have

and

. On the other hand, from

, we then can make the assumption that

is a function of
\underline{w}
.

Remark 2.6
.

The assumption that
\underline{w}
determines
w
through the equation
\big(-v^{{\widehat{T}}}+c\big)(w,\underline{w})=\mathbf{V}
depends on the the equation of the state of the gas. The assumption holds for most of the interesting cases in physics. For example, for polytropic gas, i.e.,

p(\rho,s)=\frac{1}{\gamma}\rho^{\gamma}A(s),

where
A(s)
is a smooth function of
s
and
\gamma\in(1,3)
, we have

\begin{cases}\underline{w}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2}{\gamma-1}c-v^{{\widehat{T}}}\right),\\
w=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2}{\gamma-1}c+v^{{\widehat{T}}}\right).\end{cases}

In this case,
w
can be written as a linear function of
\underline{w}
provided the value of
-v^{{\widehat{T}}}+c
.

Therefore, the only unknown function on the righthand side of (
2.32
) is
\underline{w}
. Thus,
c=c(\underline{w})
and
\Psi_{0}=\Psi_{0}(\underline{w})
. This leads to the following ODE system along each null geodesic for
\underline{w}
:

\begin{cases}L(\underline{w})=-\frac{1}{2}\left[c\not{\rm div}(\not{v})+{\rm tr}_{\not{g}}\theta\cdot c^{2}-c{\rm tr}_{\not{g}}\theta\cdot\mathbf{V}+\theta(\not{v},\not{v})+\not{v}\big(\mathbf{V}\big)\right]+\frac{1}{2}\Psi_{0}\cdot L(s),\\
\underline{w}|_{S_{0,0}}=\mathring{\underline{w}},\end{cases}

(2.33)

where
\mathring{\underline{w}}
is given by the initial data
(\mathring{\rho},\mathring{v},\mathring{s})
inside
S_{0,0}
on
\Sigma_{0}
. We remark that the righthand side of the above ODE is a smooth function of
\underline{w}
. On the other hand, it is important to observe that, since
L=\partial_{t}+\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(v^{i}-c{\widehat{T}}^{i}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}
, the vector field
L
is also completely determined by
\mathbf{V}=-v^{{\widehat{T}}}+c
. We then can solve the ODE system (
2.33
) for
\underline{w}
to obtain the values
\underline{w}
on
C_{0}
. This also gives the values of
w
on
C_{0}
.

Remark 2.7
.

We make the assumption that the solution of the ODE system (
2.33
) exists for
t\in[0,T]
. In fact, there are several reasons that we may restrict to the local in time solutions of (
2.33
).

First of all, the right hand side of (
2.33
) is in general nonlinear in
\underline{w}
. For example, for the isentropic and polytropic gases, it reduces to

L(\underline{w})=-\frac{1}{2}\left[c\not{\rm div}(\not{v})+{\rm tr}_{\not{g}}\theta\cdot c^{2}-c{\rm tr}_{\not{g}}\theta\cdot\mathbf{V}+\theta(\not{v},\not{v})+\not{v}\big(\mathbf{V}\big)\right].

The
{\rm tr}_{\not{g}}\theta\cdot c^{2}
term is then quadratic in
\underline{w}
. Wether the solution can be extended to
[0,T]
may depend on the sign of
{\rm tr}_{\not{g}}\theta
.

Secondly, we require
c>0
because the sound speed is always positive for physical solutions. Since
c
can be determined by
\underline{w}
, we may have to stop the solution after a local existence to avoid vacuum for the gases.

Thirdly, the inverse density
\kappa
is also a positive function. Once
\underline{w}
is given, we will show that
\kappa
can also be determined by an ODE system on
C_{0}
. We may have to stop the solution after a local existence to avoid shocks (where
\kappa=0
) for the gases.

All the above requirements hold for small
T
because of the continuous dependence of the solution of ODEs on the initial data.

3.1

In order to describe the structure of the Euler equations on

First of all, we consider the

between the frame
(X_{1},X_{2},{\widehat{T}})
and
(\partial_{1},\partial_{2},\partial_{3})
on each

\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}\\
X_{2}\\
{\widehat{T}}\end{pmatrix}=\Omega\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{1}\\
\partial_{2}\\
\partial_{3}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}^{1}&X_{1}^{2}&X_{1}^{3}\\
X_{2}^{1}&X_{2}^{2}&X_{2}^{3}\\
{\widehat{T}}^{1}&{\widehat{T}}^{2}&{\widehat{T}}^{3}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{1}\\
\partial_{2}\\
\partial_{3}\end{pmatrix}.

Thus, we have

\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{1}\\
\partial_{2}\\
\partial_{3}\end{pmatrix}=\Omega^{\prime}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}\\
X_{2}\\
{\widehat{T}}\end{pmatrix}.

(3.1)

where
\Omega^{\prime}=\Omega^{-1}
and its entries

We define the set

\displaystyle\mathfrak{X}_{0}=\big\{\rho,\rho^{-1},v^{i},s,X_{A}^{i},{\widehat{T}}^{i},\Omega^{\prime}_{ij},\kappa,\kappa^{-1},c,c^{-1},\Phi_{0}(\rho,s),\Psi_{0}(\rho,s)\big|A=1,2;i,j=1,2,3\big\}.

&\Psi_{0}(\rho,s)=\int\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial c}{\partial s}d\rho-\frac{1}{c\rho}\frac{\partial p}{\partial s},\\
&X_{A}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}X_{A}^{i}\partial_{i},\\
&{\widehat{T}}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}{\widehat{T}}^{i}\partial_{i}.\end{cases}

We also introduce the following set of differential operators

\mathfrak{D}=\{L,T,X_{1},X_{2}\}.

Given a positive integer

T
-order
of the objects in

-derivatives acting on an object and we regard
\kappa

on
C_{0}
, we write the formula
[L,X_{A}]=0
in the Cartesian coordinates:

L(X_{A}^{i})=X_{A}(v^{i}-c{\widehat{T}}^{i}).

(3.14)

By (
2.7
) and the formula

[{\widehat{T}},X_{A}]=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left({\widehat{T}}(X_{A}^{i})-X_{A}({\widehat{T}}^{i})\right)\partial_{i},

we compute that

\displaystyle L{\widehat{T}}(X_{A}^{i})=

\displaystyle[L,{\widehat{T}}]X_{A}^{i}+[{\widehat{T}},X_{A}](v^{i}-c{\widehat{T}}^{i})+X_{A}{\widehat{T}}(v^{i}-c{\widehat{T}}^{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\kappa}\left(\zeta^{A}+\eta^{A}\right)X_{A}(X_{A}^{i})-\frac{1}{\kappa}L(\kappa){\widehat{T}}(X_{A}^{i})

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{3}\left({\widehat{T}}(X_{A}^{j})-X_{A}({\widehat{T}}^{j})\right)\partial_{j}(v^{i}-c{\widehat{T}}^{i})+X_{A}{\widehat{T}}(v^{i}-c{\widehat{T}}^{i}).

Therefore,

\begin{split}L{\widehat{T}}(X_{A}^{i})&=-\frac{1}{\kappa}\left(\zeta^{A}+\eta^{A}\right)X_{A}(X_{A}^{i})+X_{A}{\widehat{T}}(v^{i}-c{\widehat{T}}^{i})\\
&\ \ \ -\frac{1}{\kappa}L(\kappa){\widehat{T}}(X_{A}^{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{3}\left({\widehat{T}}(X_{A}^{j})-X_{A}({\widehat{T}}^{j})\right)\left(\Omega^{\prime}_{j1}X_{1}+\Omega^{\prime}_{j2}X_{2}+\Omega^{\prime}_{j3}{\widehat{T}}\right)(v^{i}-c{\widehat{T}}^{i}).\end{split}

(3.15)

Except for
{\widehat{T}}(X_{A}^{i})
’s, all the others terms on the righthand side of the above equations have been determined. Therefore, the above equations can be regarded as an ODE system for the vector valued function
\left({\widehat{T}}(X_{A}^{1}),{\widehat{T}}(X_{A}^{2}),{\widehat{T}}(X_{A}^{3})\right)
. Since we have the value of
{\widehat{T}}(X_{A}^{i})
’s on
S_{0,0}
, we can solve the ODE system to obtain the values of
{\widehat{T}}(X_{A}^{i})
’s on
C_{0}
.

Remark 3.6
.

In contrast to Remark
2.7
and Remark
3.4
, the right hand side of he ODE system (
3.15
) is linear in
\left({\widehat{T}}(X_{A}^{1}),{\widehat{T}}(X_{A}^{2}),{\widehat{T}}(X_{A}^{3})\right)
. Therefore, we can solve the system on the entire time interval
[0,T]
.

3.3.3
Determine the remaining
{\rm ord}_{T}=1

According to the computations in the previous section, we have obtained the values of the
{\widehat{T}}
-derivative of all terms (except for
\kappa

\kappa^{-1}

\mathfrak{X}_{0}
as well as the value of
\kappa

\mathscr{P}_{1,1}

\mathscr{P}_{n-1,1}(n\geq 2)

\mathscr{P}_{n,1}

By the definition of
\mathscr{P}_{n,1}
, it suffices to consider a term

y=(\mathfrak{d}_{1}\circ\mathfrak{d}_{2}\circ\cdots\circ\mathfrak{d}_{n})(x)

with the restriction that

1)

If

\mathfrak{d}_{j}={\widehat{T}},\mathfrak{d}_{i}\in\{L,X_{1},X_{2}\},i\neq j,\ 1\leqslant i\leqslant n

2)

If

, then
\mathfrak{d}_{i}\in\{L,X_{1},X_{2}\},1\leqslant i\leqslant n

The second case is obvious. It suffices to consider the first case. If
j=n

{\widehat{T}}(x)
is already known, this case is also obvious. The following inductive argument essentially shows that the rest cases can be reduced to this one.

By changing the frame
\{\partial_{1},\partial_{2},\partial_{3}\}

\{X_{1},X_{2},{\widehat{T}}\}
, we can write the commutator
[{\widehat{T}},X_{A}]
schematically as follows:

[{\widehat{T}},X_{A}]=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left({\widehat{T}}(X_{A}^{i})-X_{A}({\widehat{T}}^{i})\right)\partial_{i}=\mathscr{P}_{1,1}\cdot\mathfrak{d}

Combined with (
2.7
), we conclude that for all
\mathfrak{d}^{\prime},\mathfrak{d}^{\prime\prime}\in\{L,X_{1},X_{2},{\widehat{T}}\}
,

[\mathfrak{d^{\prime}},\mathfrak{d}^{\prime\prime}]=\mathscr{P}_{1,1}\cdot\mathfrak{d},

(3.16)

with
\mathfrak{d}\in\{L,X_{1},X_{2},{\widehat{T}}\}
.

We now run an induction on
n
and
j
where
j\leqslant n
. We have already discussed the case
j=n
for all
n
. Assume that we can determine
\mathscr{P}_{n,1}
on
C_{0}
for
j
and we will show that it is the case for
j-1
. Indeed, by the above commutator formula, we compute that

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=(\mathfrak{d}_{1}\circ\cdots\circ\mathfrak{d}_{j-2}\circ{\widehat{T}}\circ\mathfrak{d}_{j}\circ\cdots\circ\mathfrak{d}_{n})(x)

\displaystyle=(\mathfrak{d}_{1}\circ\cdots\circ\mathfrak{d}_{j-2}\circ[{\widehat{T}},\mathfrak{d}_{j}]\circ\cdots\circ\mathfrak{d}_{n})(x)+(\mathfrak{d}_{1}\circ\cdots\circ\mathfrak{d}_{j-2}\circ\mathfrak{d}_{j}\circ{\widehat{T}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathfrak{d}_{n})(x)

\displaystyle=(\mathfrak{d}_{1}\circ\cdots\circ\mathfrak{d}_{j-2}\circ\big(\mathscr{P}_{1,1}\cdot\mathfrak{d}\big)\circ\cdots\circ\mathfrak{d}_{n})(x)+(\mathfrak{d}_{1}\circ\cdots\circ\mathfrak{d}_{j-2}\circ\mathfrak{d}_{j}\circ{\widehat{T}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathfrak{d}_{n})(x).

The second term corresponds to the inductive hypothesis for
{\widehat{T}}
appearing as
\mathfrak{d}_{j}
. For the first term, the total number of derivatives is
n-1
and we will use the inductive hypothesis for
n-1
. Since all the
\mathfrak{d}_{i}
’s are from
\{L,X_{1},X_{2}\}
, by Leibniz rule, the derivatives acting on
\mathscr{P}_{1,1}
only generates the terms already determined on
C_{0}
. This finishes the inductive argument.

Remark 3.7
.

We have already determined the value of
{\widehat{T}}^{2}(s)
on
C_{0}
. The
T
-order of
{\widehat{T}}^{2}(s)
is
1
.

3.4
Determine the terms with higher

-orders

In this section, we make the following inductive assumption: we have already determined all the terms of the form

k\geqslant 1

\mathscr{P}_{n,k+1}

To simplify the notations, we use
\mathfrak{d}^{k}
to denote all possible differential operators
\mathfrak{d}_{1}\circ\mathfrak{d}_{2}\circ\cdots\circ\mathfrak{d}_{k}
with
k\geqslant 1
,
\mathfrak{d}_{i}\in\{L,{\widehat{T}},X_{1},X_{2}\}
,

[L,\mathfrak{d}^{k}]
. In view of (
2.7
), for
\mathfrak{d}\in\{L,X_{1},X_{2},{\widehat{T}}\}
, we have the following the schematic formula

[L,\mathfrak{d}]={\mathscr{P}}_{1,1}\cdot\mathfrak{d}.

Moreover, we observe that the derivative
T
does not appear as
\mathfrak{d}
on the right hand side of the above formula.

Lemma 3.8
.

For all
k\geqslant 1
and all
\mathfrak{d}\in\{L,X_{1},X_{2},{\widehat{T}}\}
, we have

[L,\mathfrak{d}^{k}]=\sum_{j=1}^{k}{\mathscr{P}}_{j,l}\cdot\mathfrak{d}^{k+1-j},

(3.17)

where
l\leqslant k
and the top order operator on the right hand side
\mathfrak{d}^{k}\neq{\widehat{T}}^{k}
.

Proof.

We prove by induction on
k
. It is clear that (
3.17
) holds for
k=1
. We assume that the statement holds for
k
, we now prove for the case
k+1
:

\displaystyle[L,\mathfrak{d}^{k+1}]x

\displaystyle=[L,\mathfrak{d}]\mathfrak{d}^{k}(x)+\mathfrak{d}\big([L,\mathfrak{d}^{k}]x\big)

\displaystyle={\mathscr{P}}_{1,1}\cdot\mathfrak{d}\big(\mathfrak{d}^{k}(x)\big)+\mathfrak{d}\big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}{\mathscr{P}}_{j,l}\cdot\mathfrak{d}^{k+1-j}\big)

\displaystyle={\mathscr{P}}_{1,1}\cdot\mathfrak{d}^{k+1}(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(\underbrace{\mathfrak{d}({\mathscr{P}}_{j,l})}_{\mathscr{P}_{j+1,l+1}}\mathfrak{d}^{k+1-j}+{\mathscr{P}}_{j,l}\cdot\mathfrak{d}^{k+2-j}\big).

From the induction hypothesis, it is clear that
\mathfrak{d}^{k+1}\neq T^{k+1}
. This proves the formula (
3.17
).
∎

3.4.1
Determine
{\widehat{T}}^{k+1}(\rho),{\widehat{T}}^{k+1}(v^{i})

\displaystyle=\mathscr{P}_{k+1,k}\cdot{\widehat{T}}^{k+1}(\rho)+\mathscr{P}_{k+2,k}.

) to commute derivatives for the lefthand side of the above equation. This shows

\displaystyle{\widehat{T}}^{k}L\left({\widehat{T}}(\rho)\right)

\displaystyle=L{\widehat{T}}^{k+1}(\rho)+\sum_{j=1}^{k}{\mathscr{P}}_{j,l}\cdot\mathfrak{d}^{k+1-j}{\widehat{T}}(\rho)

\text{If }~x\in\{\kappa,\kappa^{-1}\},\ \ \big|\{i\big|\mathfrak{d}_{i}={\widehat{T}}\}\big|=k.\end{cases}

The idea of the proof is to use the commutator formula to move all
{\widehat{T}}

\displaystyle=(\mathfrak{d}_{1}\circ\cdots\circ\mathfrak{d}_{n-k-1}\circ{\widehat{T}}^{k+1})(x).
