Strichartz estimate for discrete Schrödinger equation on layered King’s grid
Zhiqiang Wan, Heng Zhang

Abstract
. We establish the sharp l 1 → l ∞ l^{1}\to l^{\infty} decay estimate for the discrete Schrödinger equation (DS) on the Layered King’s Grid (LKG), with a dispersive decay rate of ⟨ t ⟩ − 13 / 12 \langle t\rangle^{-13/12} , which is faster than that for 3 3 -dimensional lattice ( ⟨ t ⟩ − 1 \langle t\rangle^{-1} , see [ 31 ] ). This decay estimate enables us to derive the corresponding Strichartz estimate via the standard Keel–Tao argument. Our approach relies on using techniques from Newton polyhedra to analyze singularities.



1.
Introduction

The Schrödinger equations, particularly those on discrete spaces such as certain graphs, are fundamental dispersive equations that have a wide range of applications in physical models
[
7
,
9
,
14
,
15
,
22
,
23
,
26
,
27
]
. Let
G=(V,E)
be a simple connected graph. The discrete Laplacian on
G
is defined as:

\Delta_{\mathrm{disc}}f(x):=\sum_{y\sim x}(f(y)-f(x)),

where
y\sim x
indicates that
y
and
x
are adjacent vertices in
G
.
We are concerned with the dispersive estimate for the discrete Schrödinger equation on
G
, that is,

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\left(\partial_{t}-i\Delta_{\mathrm{disc}}\right)u\left(x,t\right)=0&\mathrm{for}\;\left(x,t\right)\in V\times\mathbb{R}_{>0},\\
u\left(x,0\right)=u_{0}\left(x\right)&\mathrm{for}\;\;x\in V.\end{cases}

where
u:{V}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{C}
, and
u_{0}
is the initial data.
Demonstrating dispersion in discrete spaces often involves establishing a decay estimate for the
l^{\infty}
norm of the solution at time
t
, expressed in terms of some negative power of
t
and the
l^{1}
norm of the initial data, namely
l^{1}\to l^{\infty}
estimate(or
L^{1}\to L^{\infty}
estimate in the continuous space). It has the following form:

(1.2)

\displaystyle\|u(x,t)\|_{l^{\infty}(V)}\leq C\left\langle t\right\rangle^{-\sigma}\|u_{0}\|_{l^{1}(V)},

where
\left\langle t\right\rangle
denotes the Japanese bracket, defined as
\left\langle t\right\rangle:=\sqrt{1+t^{2}}
, and
C
is a constant that is independent of
t
. Combining this decay estimate with an abstract functional analysis argument, i.e., the
TT^{*}
argument yield a variety of inequalities involving space-time Lebesgue norms, called Strichartz estimate, which was first introduced in
[
33
]
.

In the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the
L^{1}\to L^{\infty}
estimate is well-known to hold with
\sigma=d/2
, see, for example,
[
33
,
35
]
. However, in the
d
-dimensional lattice, the sharp
l^{1}\to l^{\infty}
estimate of equation (
1.1
) holds with
\sigma=d/3
, which is slower than the corresponding estimate in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, as shown in
[
31
, Proposition 1]
. The similar dispersive estimates and corresponding applications for other related discrete equations can be found in
[
4
,
10
,
13
,
11
,
21
,
25
,
28
]
. The study of the Schrödinger equation on Cayley graphs is a particularly fruitful area of research, as the inherent group symmetry can be exploited by tools from harmonic analysis to decompose the Schrödinger equation, thereby revealing deep connections among the algebraic properties of the group, the geometry of the graph, and the operator’s spectrum (see, e.g.,
[
10
,
13
,
31
,
28
,
16
]
).

A natural line of inquiry is to examine the
l^{1}\to l^{\infty}
dispersive decay on other Cayley graphs with vertex set
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. In a recent work, Ge, Hua, Jia, and Zhou
[
16
]
investigated this for the hexagonal lattice
T_{H}
, viewed as the Cayley graph of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with the generating set
{\pm(1,0),\pm(0,1),\pm(1,1)}
. Their analysis revealed that the decay rate is
\langle t\rangle^{-3/4}
, a notable acceleration over the classical
\langle t\rangle^{-2/3}
rate for the standard square lattice.

We consider the Strichartz estimate on Layered King’s Grid (LKG), which has meaningful geometric and physical interpretations, defined as the Cayley graph of
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
and the edge set
E
given by
E=\left\{\{u,v\}\mid u-v\in\{(\pm 1,0,0),(0,\pm 1,0),(0,0,\pm 1),(\pm 1,\pm 1,0)\}\right\}
. LKG is an ideal model for anisotropic cellular automata. It combines complex 2D Moore-like interactions within layers with simpler 1D von Neumann-like dynamics between them. This structure is perfect for modeling layered phenomena, like crystal growth, offering a more realistic framework than purely isotropic models. For more about cellular automata, we refer to
[
8
,
34
,
37
]
.
Our research offers a adaptable framework for characterizing continuous-time quantum walk on anisotropic cellular automata, see
[
30
]
.

In our setting, we use the standard Fourier transform:

\widehat{f}\left(x\right):=\frac{1}{\left(2\pi\right)^{d/2}}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}f\left(\mathbf{n}\right)e^{-ix\cdot\mathbf{n}}\;\mathrm{for}\;x\in\left[0,2\pi\right]^{d},

where the sequence
f:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
belongs to
l^{2}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{d}\right)
. The inverse Fourier transform is given by

\check{f}\left(\mathbf{n}\right):=\frac{1}{\left(2\pi\right)^{d/2}}\int_{\left[0,2\pi\right]^{d}}f\left(x\right)e^{ix\cdot\mathbf{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{for}\;\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},

where
f:\left[0,2\pi\right]^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
belongs to
L^{2}\left(\left[0,2\pi\right]^{d}\right)
.

Applying the Fourier transform on LKG for the equation (
1.1
), we have that

\displaystyle u\left(x,y,z,t\right)

\displaystyle=:e^{it\Delta_{\mathrm{disc}}}u_{0}\left(x,y,z\right)=u_{0}\ast\left(\psi_{t}\right)^{\vee}\left(x,y,z\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}u_{0}\left(\mathbf{n}\right)\psi_{t}\left(\left(x,y,z\right)-\mathbf{n}\right)\;\;\mathrm{\;for\;any\;}\left(x,y,z\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}.

Here
\psi_{t}
is defined by

\displaystyle\psi_{t}\left(x,y,z\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3/2}}\exp\Big(-it\big(10-2\cos x-2\cos y-2\cos z-2\cos\left(x+y\right)-2\cos\left(x-y\right)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3/2}}\exp\left(-it\left(\omega\left(x,y\right)+\omega_{1}\left(z\right)\right)\right)\;\;\mathrm{for\;any}\;\left(x,y,z\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3},

where
\omega_{1}\left(z\right)=2-2\cos z
, and

(1.3)

\omega\left(x,y\right)=8-2\cos x-2\cos y-2\cos\left(x+y\right)-2\cos\left(x-y\right).

The Hausdorff-Young inequality gives that

(1.4)

\left\|u\left(t\right)\right\|_{l^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}\leq\left\|\left(\psi_{t}\right)^{\vee}\right\|_{l^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}\cdot\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{l^{1}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)},

and we hope to give a decay estimate of
\left\|\left(\psi_{t}\right)^{\vee}\right\|_{l^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}
about time variable
t
in the sense of

\left\|\left(\psi_{t}\right)^{\vee}\right\|_{l^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}\lesssim\langle t\rangle^{-\sigma},

for some
\sigma>0
. Changing variables
\left(\xi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime}\right)=\left(\xi,\eta,\zeta\right)t
and using the Fubini theorem, the oscillatory integral takes the form

\displaystyle\left(\psi_{t}\right)^{\vee}\left(t\xi,t\eta,t\zeta\right)=\frac{1}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3}}\int_{\left[0,2\pi\right]^{2}}e^{-it\left(\omega\left(x,y\right)-\left(\xi x+\eta y\right)\right)}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\int_{\left[0,2\pi\right]}e^{-it\left(\omega_{1}\left(z\right)-\zeta z\right)}\mathrm{d}z,

where
\left(\xi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the velocity.
As for the integral with respect to
z
,

\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}}{\mathrm{d}z^{3}}\left(\omega_{1}\left(z\right)-z\zeta\right)\right|^{2}+\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}z^{2}}\left(\omega_{1}\left(z\right)-z\zeta\right)\right|^{2}=4

holds and by the van der Corput’s lemma (see
[
32
, Chapter VIII]
) we derive

(1.5)

\left|\int_{\left[0,2\pi\right]}e^{-it\left(\omega_{1}\left(z\right)-z\zeta\right)}\mathrm{d}z\right|\lesssim\langle t\rangle^{-1/3},

and this estimate is sharp
[
31
]
. We now reduce the problem to the uniform estimate of

(1.6)

\displaystyle I\left(\xi,\eta;t\right)=

\displaystyle\int_{\left[0,2\pi\right]^{2}}\exp\left(-it\left(\omega\left(x,y\right)-\left(x\xi+y\eta\right)\right)\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\int_{\left[0,2\pi\right]^{2}}\exp\left(-it\Omega\left(x,y;\xi,\eta\right)\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,

and prove its sharp decay rate is
\langle t\rangle^{-3/4}
. Leveraging the detailed singularity analysis of the phase function
\Omega\left(x,y;\xi,\eta\right)
presented in Section
2
, we derive the desired estimate.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
u
be a solution to (
1.1
), we have the following estimate

(1.7)

\displaystyle\|u(x,t)\|_{l^{\infty}(V)}\leq C\left\langle t\right\rangle^{-13/12}\|u_{0}\|_{l^{1}(V)},

where
C
is a constant
that is independent of
t
.

The dispersive decay rate of LKG is faster than that for that for
3
-dimensional lattice (
\langle t\rangle^{-1}
, see
[
31
]
). We explain this phenomenon using the same reasoning as in
[
16
]
, where the authors suggested this enhancement is attributed to two potential factors: the greater connectivity afforded by the additional generators, and a geometry whose geodesic balls provide a closer approximation to Euclidean balls. We remark that the estimate is sharp in the sense of combining with (
1.5
) and (
2.14
).

For the inhomogeneous discrete Schrödinger equation,

(1.8)

\begin{cases}&\left(\partial_{t}-i\Delta_{disc}\right)u\left(x,t\right)=F\left(x,t\right)\;\;\mathrm{for}\;\left(x,t\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0},\\
&u\left(x,0\right)=u_{0}\left(x\right)\;\;\mathrm{for}\;x\in\mathbb{Z}^{3},\end{cases}

we have the following Strichartz estimate via standard Keel-Tao argument.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
u\in C^{1}([0,+\infty);l^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{3}))
be a solution of the equation (
1.8
), the Strichartz estimate holds with any two Strichartz
13/12
-admissible pairs
\left(q,r\right)
and
\left(\tilde{q},\tilde{r}\right)
. That is

(1.9)

\left\|u\right\|_{L^{q}_{t}l^{r}_{n}\left(\left(0,T\right)\times\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}\leq C\left(\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{l^{2}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}+\left\|F\right\|_{L^{\tilde{q}^{\prime}}_{t}l^{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}_{n}\left(\left(0,T\right)\times\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}\right),

where
T\in(0,\infty]
.

The definition of
mixed space-time Lebesgue norm
and
\sigma
-admissible
, we will recall in Subsection
4.1
. Further, in Subsection
4.2
, we will establish the global well-posedness for small initial data to discrete nonlinear Schrödinger equation on LKG.

By leveraging the Cartesian product structure of the LKG, we perform a separation of variables, which reduces the problem to deriving the required estimate solely for the King graph factor. A natural direction for future research is to identify other Cayley graphs of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
that lack a Cartesian product decomposition yet exhibit a decay rate faster than that of the d-dimensional lattice. We note that the main difficulty lies in higher dimensions; general results analogous to Theorem
2.5
remain an open problem in analysis and have thus far only been obtained for multidimensional oscillatory integrals with specific phase functions.

2.
Determining singularities of the phase function

In this section we will deal with a more general type of oscillatory integral

(2.1)

I\left(\xi,\eta;t;\chi\right)=\int_{\left[0,2\pi\right]^{2}}e^{-it\Omega\left(x,y;\xi,\eta\right)}\chi\left(x,y\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

and give an asymptotic estimate about it when
t
goes to infinity. Here,
\left(\xi,\eta\right)
is a fixed vector in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
which stands for the velocity and the function
\chi
is a given smooth test function defined on
\left[0,2\pi\right]^{2}
with periodic boundary conditions, i.e.,
\chi
has a natural periodic extension to the whole plane. In equation (
1.6
), we take
\chi=\mathbbm{1}_{\left[0,2\pi\right]^{2}}
.

We choose a smooth bump function
\mathbf{a}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\left[0,1\right]
such that

•

\min_{\left(x,y\right)\in\left[0,2\pi\right]^{2}}\mathbf{a}\left(x,y\right)\geq c_{0}
for some positive number
c_{0}
,

•

The support of
\mathbf{a}
is contained in
\left[-4\pi,4\pi\right]^{2}
.

Then by the bounded overlapping property we can naturally assume that

\sum_{\left(n,m\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\mathbf{a}\left(x+2\pi n,y+2\pi m\right)\equiv 1.

Hence the oscillatory integral (
2.1
) becomes

\displaystyle I\left(\xi,\eta;t;\chi\right)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\left(n,m\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\int_{\left[0,2\pi\right]^{2}}e^{-it\Omega\left(x,y;\xi,\eta\right)}\mathbf{a}\left(x+2\pi n,y+2\pi m\right)\chi\left(x,y\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\left(n,m\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\int_{\left[2\pi n,2\pi\left(n+1\right)\right]\times\left[2\pi m,2\pi\left(m+1\right)\right]}e^{-it\Omega\left(x,y;\xi,\eta\right)}\mathbf{a}\left(x,y\right)\chi\left(x,y\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{-it\Omega\left(x,y;\xi,\eta\right)}\mathbf{a}\left(x,y\right)\chi\left(x,y\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,

and we denote
\widetilde{I}\left(\xi,\eta;t;\chi\right)
be

(2.2)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{-it\Omega\left(x,y;\xi,\eta\right)}\mathbf{a}\left(x,y\right)\chi\left(x,y\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,

where
\mathbf{a}(x,y)\chi(x,y)
is a smooth compactly supported function defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In the definition of
\widetilde{I}\left(\xi,\eta;t;\chi\right)
, the function
\chi
can be chosen as a periodic function, or just a smooth bump function.

When the velocity
\left(\xi,\eta\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B_{7}\left(0\right)
, the phase function
\Omega\left(\cdot,\cdot;\xi,\eta\right)
has no critical points in
\left[0,2\pi\right]^{2}
, since the function
\omega_{1}
satisfies
\left|\nabla\omega_{1}\right|\leq 6
, and
\widetilde{I}\left(\xi,\eta;t;\chi\right)
decays faster than any polynomial in
t
. If
\left(\xi,\eta\right)\in B_{7}\left(0\right)
, the phase function might has critical points in the support of
\chi
, which slow down the decay rate. In the classical theory of oscillatory integrals, we mainly focus on critical points, here we call a point
x
is a
degenerate
point if the Hessian matrix of phase function at
x
is degenerate, else we say
x
is a
non-degenerate
point. Recall the phase function
\Omega\left(x,y;\xi,\eta\right)
is given by

\omega\left(x,y\right)-\left(x\xi+y\eta\right).

For fixed
\left(x_{0},y_{0}\right)\in\left[0,2\pi\right]^{2}
, there is an unique choice of the velocity
\left(\xi_{0},\eta_{0}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
\left(x_{0},y_{0}\right)
is a critical point of
\Omega\left(x,y;\xi_{0},\eta_{0}\right)
, indeed, one can take
\left(\xi_{0},\eta_{0}\right)=\nabla_{\left(x_{0},y_{0}\right)}\omega
. The order of degeneracy of the phase function at that point is determined by the high order derivatives of
\omega
, and at that point of view, we give a partition of
\left[0,2\pi\right]^{2}
:

\left[0,2\pi\right]^{2}=\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2}\cup\Sigma_{3},

and
\Sigma_{i}
are defined as

(2.3)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\{\left(x_{0},y_{0}\right)\in\left[0,2\pi\right]^{2}:\det\left(\mathrm{Hess}_{\left(x_{0},y_{0}\right)}\omega\right)\neq 0\right\},

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\{\left(x_{0},y_{0}\right)\in\left[0,2\pi\right]^{2}:\left(x_{0},y_{0}\right)\;\mathrm{is\;an}\;A_{2}\;\mathrm{type\;singularity\;of}\;\omega\left(x,y\right)-\left(x,y\right)\cdot\nabla_{\left(x_{0},y_{0}\right)}\omega\right\},

\displaystyle\Sigma_{3}

\displaystyle=\left\{\left(x_{0},y_{0}\right)\in\left[0,2\pi\right]^{2}:\left(x_{0},y_{0}\right)\;\mathrm{is\;an}\;A_{3}\;\mathrm{type\;singularity\;of}\;\omega\left(x,y\right)-\left(x,y\right)\cdot\nabla_{\left(x_{0},y_{0}\right)}\omega\right\}.

Here, we remark that in our setting, the types of singularity are exactly
A_{k}
,
k\leq 3
(See Propositon
2.3
). The definition of
A_{k}
type singularities, one can refer to
[
1

2

Using this partition, we divide the velocity space as follows

\mathbb{R}^{2}=V_{0}\cup V_{1}\cup V_{2}\cup V_{3},

where
V_{i}
are given by

(2.4)

\displaystyle V_{0}

\displaystyle=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\mathrm{for\;all}\;\left(x_{0},y_{0}\right)\in\left[0,2\pi\right]^{2},\;v\neq\nabla_{\left(x_{0},y_{0}\right)}\omega\right\},

\displaystyle V_{3}

\displaystyle=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\mathrm{there\;exists\;some}\;\left(x_{0},y_{0}\right)\in\Sigma_{3},\;\mathrm{such\;that}\;v=\nabla_{\left(x_{0},y_{0}\right)}\omega\right\},

\displaystyle V_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus V_{3}:\mathrm{there\;exists\;some}\;\left(x_{0},y_{0}\right)\in\Sigma_{2},\;\mathrm{such\;that}\;v=\nabla_{\left(x_{0},y_{0}\right)}\omega\right\},

\displaystyle V_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\left(V_{2}\cup V_{3}\right):\mathrm{there\;exists\;some}\;\left(x_{0},y_{0}\right)\in\Sigma_{1},\;\mathrm{such\;that}\;v=\nabla_{\left(x_{0},y_{0}\right)}\omega\right\}.

\varphi\left(x,y\right)=\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}=0}^{\infty}a_{n_{1},n_{2}}x^{n_{1}}y^{n_{2}}.

The set

\mathcal{T}\left(\varphi\right):=\left\{\left(n_{1},n_{2}\right)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}:a_{n_{1},n_{2}}\neq 0\right\}

\varphi

\varphi

\varphi\left(0,0\right)=0

\nabla\varphi\left(0,0\right)=0

\mathcal{T}\left(\varphi\right)\neq\emptyset
. The

\mathcal{N}\left(\varphi\right)

, where
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}:=\left[0,\infty\right)^{2}
. The

\left(d_{\varphi},d_{\varphi}\right)
. By a

in the new coordinates, is a vertex, and if

4.1.
Strichartz estimate

We first fix some standard notations.

Definition 4.1
.

For the function
f:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{C}
, denote by
\left\|f\right\|_{L^{q}_{t}l^{p}_{x}\left(\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}\right)}
be the space-time mix Lebesgue norm of
f
, defined as

\left\|f\right\|_{L^{q}_{t}l^{p}_{n}\left(\left(0,T\right)\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}\right)}:=\left(\int_{\left(0,T\right)}\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\left|f\left(n,t\right)\right|^{p}\right)^{q/p}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{1/q}.

Let
u\in C^{1}([0,+\infty);l^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{3}))
be a solution of the equation
1.1
, the following energy identity comes from the well-known
l^{2}
conservation law:

\left\|e^{it\Delta_{disc}}u_{0}\right\|_{l^{2}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}=\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{l^{2}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)},

where
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
u_{0}\in l^{2}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)
. To give its proof, one just needs to check that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\left\|e^{it\Delta_{\mathrm{disc}}}u_{0}\right\|_{l^{2}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}^{2}\right)=2\text{Re}\left(\langle\partial_{t}u\left(t\right),u\left(t\right)\rangle_{l^{2}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}\right)=2\text{Re}\left(\langle i\Delta_{\mathrm{disc}}u\left(t\right),u\left(t\right)\rangle_{l^{2}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}\right)=0.

Moreover, it’s easy to verify that
\left(e^{it\Delta_{\mathrm{disc}}}\right)^{*}=e^{-it\Delta_{\mathrm{disc}}}
. Indeed, for any
u,v\in l^{2}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)
, we have

\langle e^{it\Delta_{\mathrm{disc}}}u,v\rangle_{l^{2}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}=\langle\widehat{u}\psi_{t},\widehat{v}\rangle_{l^{2}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}=\langle\widehat{u},\psi_{-t}\widehat{v}\rangle_{l^{2}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}=\langle u,e^{-it\Delta_{\mathrm{disc}}}v\rangle_{l^{2}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}.

Using this fact and the
l^{1}\to l^{\infty}
estimate we obtain that

\left\|e^{it\Delta}\left(e^{is\Delta}\right)^{*}u\right\|_{l^{1}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}=\left\|e^{i\left(t-s\right)\Delta}u\right\|_{l^{1}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}\lesssim\langle t-s\rangle^{-1}\left\|u\right\|_{l^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}.

1.8

]

Let

is Strichartz

admissible, if

,

\left\|U\left(t\right)f\right\|_{L^{2}_{x}}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{H}

and the decay estimate

for some

\displaystyle\left\|\int\left(U\left(t\right)\right)^{*}F\left(t,\cdot\right)\mathrm{d}t\right\|_{H}\lesssim\left\|F\right\|_{L^{q^{\prime}}_{t}L^{r^{\prime}}_{x}},

4.2.
global well-posedness for small data

With the help of Theorem
1.2
, we can establish the global existence of the solution to discrete nonlinear Schrödinger equations (DNLS) with a small initial data on LKG.

(4.1)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}u(x,t)-i\Delta_{\mathrm{disc}}u(x,t)\pm i|u(x,t)|^{2a}u(x,t)=0,~~\forall(x,t)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\times[0,\infty)\\
&u(\cdot,0)=u_{0}\in l^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{3}).\end{aligned}\right.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
a\geq 12/13
. If there exists
\epsilon>0
and a positive constant
C
such that
\|u_{0}\|_{l^{2}}\leq\epsilon
, then (
4.1
) has a global solution
u\in C^{1}((0,\infty];l^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{3}))
. Moreover,

\|u_{t}\|_{L^{q}l^{r}\left(\left(0,\infty\right)\times\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}\leq C\epsilon,

holds for all
13/12
-admissible pairs
(q,r)
. In particular,

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|u(t)\|_{l^{r}(\mathbb{Z}^{3})}=0,\ \mathrm{for\ all}\ r>2.

Proof.

We define a metric space

\mathcal{X}=\{u(t):\sup_{(q,r):\mathrm{\frac{13}{12}-admissible}}\|u\|_{L^{q}l^{r}\left(\left(0,\infty\right)\times\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}<2C\|u_{0}\|_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{3})}\},

where
C
is the constant in Theorem
1.2
. In the following, we will find a fixed point in
\mathcal{X}
of an operator
\mathbf{A}
defined by

\mathbf{A}(u)=e^{it\Delta_{\mathrm{disc}}}u_{0}\pm i\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-s)\Delta_{\mathrm{disc}}}|u|^{2a}u(s)\mathrm{d}s.

Setting
(\tilde{q},\tilde{r})=(\infty,2)
, and by Theorem
1.2
we have

\displaystyle\sup_{(q,r):\mathrm{\frac{13}{12}-admissible}}\|\mathbf{A}u(t)\|_{L^{q}l^{r}\left(\left(0,\infty\right)\times\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{3})}+C_{1}\|u^{2a+1}\|_{L^{1}l^{2}\left(\left(0,\infty\right)\times\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{3})}+C_{1}\|u\|^{2a+1}_{L^{2a+1}l^{2(2a+1)}\left(\left(0,\infty\right)\times\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}.

Since
a\geq\frac{12}{13}
, we have
(2a+1,2(2a+1))
is
13/12
-admissible. Then we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{(q,r):\mathrm{\frac{13}{12}-admissible}}\|\mathbf{A}u(t)\|_{L^{q}l^{r}\left(\left(0,\infty\right)\times\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{3})}+C_{1}\|u\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2a+1}\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{3})}+C_{1}(2C\epsilon)^{2a+1}.

Letting
\epsilon>0
be sufficient small, we derive

\sup_{(q,r):\mathrm{\frac{13}{12}-admissible}}\|\mathbf{A}u(t)\|_{L^{q}l^{r}\left(\left(0,\infty\right)\times\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}\leq 2C\|u_{0}\|_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{3})},

which implies
\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{X})\subseteq\mathcal{X}
given that
\|u_{0}\|_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{3})}
is small enough. Similarly,
letting
u,\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{X}
with the same initial data
u(\cdot,0)=\tilde{u}(\cdot,0)=u_{0},
we conclude

\displaystyle\sup_{(q,r):\mathrm{\frac{13}{12}-admissible}}\|\mathbf{A}u-\mathbf{A}\tilde{u}\|

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}\|u-\tilde{u}\|_{L^{2a+1}l^{2(2a+1)}\left(\left(0,\infty\right)\times\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}(\|u\|_{L^{2a+1}l^{2(2a+1)}\left(\left(0,\infty\right)\times\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}+\|\tilde{u}\|_{L^{2a+1}l^{2(2a+1)}\left(\left(0,\infty\right)\times\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)})

\displaystyle\leq 2CC_{1}\|u-\tilde{u}\|_{L^{2a+1}l^{2(2a+1)}\left(\left(0,\infty\right)\times\mathbb{Z}^{3}\right)}\|u_{0}\|_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{3})}^{2a}.

Therefore
\mathbf{A}
is a contraction map given that the
l^{2}
norm of
u_{0}
is sufficiently small.
This proves the existence of a global solution (
4.1
).
∎
