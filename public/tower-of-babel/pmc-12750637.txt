The impact of father–son relationship on suicidal ideation in Chinese gay and bisexual men: the mediating role of masculinity and self-esteem
Authors: Yiting Huang, Junhao Liang, Gang Huang, Jiarui Liu, Dejia Zhu, Jiayu Liu, Jingchu Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn China, gay and bisexual men exhibit higher levels of suicidal ideation than their heterosexual counterparts do. The influence of the father–son relationship on mental health within this group remains understudied. This study aims to explore the interplay between the father‒son relationship and suicidal ideation and examine the potential mediating roles of masculinity and self-esteem in this relationship among young Chinese gay and bisexual men.MethodsWe conducted a survey involving 302 cisgender gay and bisexual young men (mean age = 23.99, SD = 3.68) and 250 cisgender heterosexual young men (mean age = 23.89, SD = 3.98). Structural equation modeling (SEM) was employed to assess the relationships between father–son dynamics, masculinity, self-esteem, and suicidal ideation.ResultsCompared with their heterosexual counterparts, Chinese gay and bisexual men reported higher levels of suicidal ideation and lower levels of father–son relationship quality and masculinity. Notably, mediation analysis revealed that masculinity and self-esteem sequentially mediated the relationship between the father-son relationship and suicidal ideation (total effect β = -0.026; direct effect β = 0.007, p > .05; total indirect effect β = -0.034, p < .01). This mediation model was not significant among the heterosexual male sample.ConclusionsThis study highlights the critical role of the father-son relationship in the mental health of young Chinese gay and bisexual men. Our findings illuminate a pathway wherein poorer father-son relationships are linked to lower levels of both masculinity and self-esteem, which in turn are associated with greater suicidal ideation. These findings advance the understanding of psychological development in this population. They also underscore the potential importance of interventions that support positive father-son relationships, masculinity, and self-esteem as a means to address mental health disparities among young Chinese gay and bisexual men.Keywords: Mental health, Suicidal ideation, Father‒son relationship, Self-esteem, Masculinity, Gay and bisexual men, China

Introduction

As the mental health and related psychosocial factors of sexual minorities have been widely studied in Western countries [1, 2], an increasing number of Chinese scholars have also begun to pay attention to the mental health of this group. For example, a higher prevalence of suicidal ideation has been reported among gay and bisexual adolescents than among their heterosexual counterparts in China [3], and a recent Chinese meta-analysis revealed a high lifetime prevalence of suicidal ideation (21.2%) among gay and bisexual men [4]. While a growing body of literature has consistently documented the higher prevalence of suicidal ideation among Chinese sexual minority men, the research focus is now shifting from what the disparity is to why it exists. Previous studies have provided a valuable foundation, identifying key risk factors such as psychological distress and lack of disclosure to relatives, alongside protective factors like social support and self-esteem [5–7]. Despite this progress, a more nuanced understanding of the specific psychological pathways is needed. In particular, there remains a critical gap in the literature regarding how foundational relationships, such as the father-son bond, interact with cultural pressures around masculinity to shape self-esteem and, ultimately, the suicide risk of this population.

China is home to one of the world’s largest gay and bisexual populations, estimated to be in the tens of millions [8]; however, China’s unique sociocultural context, particularly the profound influence of Confucian philosophy, filial piety, and traditional gender role norms, imposes distinct and often more severe pressures on gay and bisexual individuals [9]. This cultural background may lead to higher levels of minority stress and potentially poorer mental health outcomes for gay and bisexual men in China than for their Western peers [10]. Given the relatively limited research in this area within China, exploring these psychosocial factors not only addresses a regional research gap but also provides critical insights for a global understanding of gay and bisexual mental health, informing the development of culturally sensitive interventions and policies.

In previous Western studies, one of the well-documented factors associated with suicide among gay and bisexual men was family-related factors [11, 12]. Among all family-related factors, maternal love has been the most studied and has been shown to have a protective effect on depressive symptoms in sexual minorities [13, 14]. However, the role of father‒son relationships in the suicidal ideation of gay and bisexual men remains understudied in the context of a relatively patriarchal culture such as China. As the father‒son relationship plays a particularly important role in shaping the psychosocial characteristics and mental health of sons [15–17], especially in China, where fathers serve not only as emotional supporters but also as primary transmitters of traditional values and gender role norms due to Confucian thought and the concept of filial piety [18], examining the effects of the father‒son relationship on suicidal ideation among gay and bisexual men and their heterosexual counterparts in China is important.

The father‒son relationship, which encompasses emotional bonds, perceived involvement, and physical interaction, profoundly influences sons’ psychological and emotional development, especially in China, where Confucian values emphasizing filial piety and paternal authority amplify fathers’ roles as primary agents of gender socialization [19–22]. Relational self-theory posits that sons develop their identity through “co-identification” with their fathers [23, 24], whereas social learning theory suggests that sons acquire behaviors and traits, such as masculinity, by observing and emulating paternal role models [25]. Research in China supports this, showing that urban fathers actively cultivate traditional masculine traits in their sons, such as competitiveness and decisiveness, and approximately half of them emphasize their role as gender role models, fostering “boyish traits” such as resilience and ambition [18]. Similarly, paternal behavioral control (e.g., setting rules) is positively associated with adolescents’ self-determination, whereas psychological control (e.g., excessive interference) is negatively associated with self-determination [26]. These findings suggest that high-quality father–son relationships promote positive psychological outcomes through the development of masculinity.

Moreover, the impact of father–son relationships on masculinity may be stronger among gay and bisexual men. Minority stress theory suggests that minority individuals suffer some extra stressors, such as discrimination, stigma, and internal homophobia, due to their sexual orientation [27]. In China, gay and bisexual men confront distinctive sociocultural stressors. Heteronormative, family-oriented collectivism, reinforced by deeply rooted Confucian values, constitutes a persistent distal stressor [28]. For example, the ideal of filial piety obliges sons to continue the family line, creating an intrinsic conflict with their sexual orientation [29]. The interaction between gay and bisexual men and their parents, especially prejudice and rejection, has significant effects on their psychological distress and life satisfaction [30, 31]. Such cultural pressures heighten proximal stressors, including internalized homonegativity and anticipation of family and social rejection [32, 33]. Within this context, the father–son relationship assumes heightened significance and may be worse for gay and bisexual individuals. When the quality of this relationship is not satisfactory, they are less likely to receive support and affirmation, thereby exacerbating pressures surrounding masculinity and social conformity.

Masculinity, characterized by traits such as autonomy, emotional stoicism, and physical strength, is shaped by paternal socialization and cultural norms, serving as a critical link to self-esteem [34–37]. The masculinity hypothesis posits that adherence to traditional masculine norms enhances psychological well-being, reduces depression, and increases self-esteem [38–44]. This effect is exacerbated in China, where masculine traits are often conferred greater social privilege under the “tight” social norms of Confucian culture [45]. This cultural context can lead to a sense of “precarious manhood,” where masculinity is perceived as hard-won and easily lost, requiring constant validation through behavior [46]. Consequently, gay, and bisexual men in China may feel compelled to conform to traditional masculine expectations to gain social acceptance and belonging, which in turn can influence their self-esteem [46, 47]. However, an overemphasis on traditional masculinity can also lead to “toxic masculinity”, characterized by emotional suppression, denial of vulnerability, and negative attitudes toward nontraditional gender expressions [48]. This would then serve as a minority stressor for gay and bisexual men. According to minority stress theory, especially in the context of Chinese culture, sexual identity acceptance is a crucial mediator of the association between norm conformity and psychological distress, and nonconformity to these rigid norms could result in diminished self-esteem by suppressing authentic self-expression [27, 32, 49]. Therefore, a positive father-son relationship may foster a masculine identity that is positively associated with self-esteem in this high-pressure context.

In addition to masculinity, self-esteem is a well-established factor influencing suicidal ideation [50–56] and could mediate the link between masculine attributes and suicidal ideation. Recent Chinese studies confirm that conformity to masculinity-related social norms predicts higher self-esteem within this normative cultural context [57–59]. On the contrary, deviations from these gender norms can lead to lower self-esteem, which may affect mental health outcomes such as suicidal ideation. Studies on Hong Kong and Korean men [55, 60] have shown the mediating effect of self-esteem between masculine discrepancies and mental health (depression and social anxiety). However, no studies have examined the mediating role of self-esteem between masculinity and suicidal ideation under the influence of father–son relationships. Adherence to masculinity may benefit them by increasing their self-esteem, as they adjust to a relatively patriarchal society, potentially reducing the risk of suicidal ideation.

Current study

This study proposes a chained mediation model: high-quality father‒son relationships foster traditional masculinity, which enhances self-esteem, ultimately reducing suicidal ideation. As the theoretical and empirical evidence suggests, there is a directional pathway: father–son relationships shape masculinity through socialization, which influences self-esteem via societal expectations, subsequently affecting suicidal ideation [23–25, 38–44, 50–56]. This pathway is likely more pronounced in gay and bisexual men because of heightened gender norm pressures and family rejection [61, 62]. The minority stress model supports this finding, indicating that paternal rejection exacerbates psychological distress among gay and bisexual men, amplifying the impact of father–son relationship quality on masculinity, self-esteem, and suicidal ideation [27]. In contrast, heterosexual men may experience less pronounced effects.

Despite existing research on family factors, masculinity, and self-esteem in relation to suicidal ideation, several research gaps remain. First, few studies have specifically examined the impact of father–son relationships on the suicidal ideation of gay and bisexual men within the Chinese cultural context. Second, the literature has not comprehensively explored the chained mediation mechanism involving father‒son relationships, masculinity, and self-esteem in the context of suicidal ideation among gay and bisexual men. This study aims to fill these gaps by constructing and testing this chained mediation model, offering a novel perspective on the psychological development of Chinese gay and bisexual men. Furthermore, by conducting a multigroup comparison between heterosexual men and gay and bisexual men, this study reveals different magnitudes of psychological pathways within different sexual orientation groups.

The study proposes three hypotheses:

1) Young Chinese gay and bisexual men have higher levels of suicidal ideation, worse father‒son relationships, lower levels of masculinity, and lower self-esteem than their heterosexual peers do.

2) Father‒son relationships are expected to be negatively correlated with suicidal ideation and positively correlated with masculinity and self-esteem among young Chinese men, regardless of their sexual orientation.

3) Masculinity and self-esteem are hypothesized to sequentially mediate the relationship between father–son relationships and suicidal ideation, regardless of sexual orientation, as shown in Fig. 1. This mediation model will be tested among both gay men and bisexual and heterosexual young men in China, and differences in the mediation pathways between the two groups will be explored.

Methods

Study setting and data collection

A priori power analysis (G*Power) indicated 153 participants per group were needed to detect medium effects (α = 0.05, power = 0.95, f2 = 0.15, number of predictors = 7). This study was conducted from 2016 to 2020, during which time 794 questionnaires were distributed, and 552 valid participants were included in the data analysis. The data exclusion criteria (N = 242) were as follows: (a) missing data on age (N = 69); (b) discrepancy between biological sex and self-identified sex (N = 93); (c) self-identified as unsure of their sexual orientation (N = 2); (d) self-identified as female participants (N = 57), as this study focuses on father-son relationship and its impact on male sexual minorities; and (e) age under 18 years or over 40 years (N = 21). Participants from diverse family structures (e.g., divorced families, deceased fathers) were included. The final sample consisted of 250 cisgender male participants who self-identified as heterosexual males (mean age = 23.89, median age = 23, SD = 3.98) and 302 cisgender gay and bisexual males who self-identified as gay and bisexual men (252 gay men and 50 bisexual men, mean age = 23.99, median age = 23, SD = 3.68) (see Table 1 for demographic information). Gay and bisexual volunteers were recruited from social groups for gay and bisexual men on the QQ instant messaging application, gay dating websites (e.g., feizan.com), and the LGBT group on douban.com, a widely used Chinese social networking platform. To maximize comparability, heterosexual volunteers were recruited from social groups on QQ instant messaging application and the Youth Social Group on douban.com. The data were collected through a Chinese survey website (www.wjx.cn) and anonymized to ensure reliability and confidentiality. Participation was voluntary and compensated with a small reward (approximately US$1.40) via the online payment platform Alipay. The research protocol was approved and approved by the Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Kangning Hospital (approval number: 2020-3-2002). Prior to the survey, the participants were informed that their participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw from the study at any time. Informed consent was obtained before the participants answered the questionnaire.

Measures

Father‒son relationship

A global score was created using three facets of the Father Presence Questionnaire (FPQ) to assess participants’ perceived relationship quality—distinct from physical presence—with their fathers [63]. These facets are feelings about the father (e.g., “My father is very important to me”), perceptions of father involvement (e.g., “My father helped me learn new things”), and physical relationships with the father (e.g., “My father would talk with me when I was a baby”). All the items were scored on a scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (almost always), and the scale has been validated and widely used in China [64]. Higher scores on the Father‒Son Relationship Scale indicate better perceived quality of the father‒son relationship. The Cronbach’s α of “relationship with father” was 0.94 for the gay and bisexual groups and 0.94 for the heterosexual group in the current study.

Masculinity

The Chinese brief version of the Bem Sex Role Inventory was used to assess gender role-related personality traits [65]. The brief scale was composed of 12 femininity items (e.g., understanding, compassionate, and tender) and 14 masculinity items (e.g., competitive, ambitious, and forceful). The respondents were asked to rate the degree to which the items described themselves on a seven-point Likert-type scale from 1 = never or rarely true to 7 = always or almost always true. Masculinity was computed as the total score of the 14 masculinity items (e.g., competitive, ambitious, and forceful). A higher total score indicates more masculinity personality traits. The Cronbach’s α of masculinity was 0.89 for the gay and bisexual groups and 0.92 for the heterosexual group in the current study, indicating good internal consistency.

Suicidal ideation

In the present study, suicidal ideation was assessed using a single-item measure, asking: “Do you have any suicidal thoughts during the past 12 months?” The response options for the item assessing ideation were 0 = never, 1 = sometimes, and 2 = often. A higher score on the item indicated a higher frequency of suicidal ideation. This method precludes the calculation of an internal consistency coefficient like Cronbach’s α, has been validated and is commonly used in large-scale research [66].

Self-esteem

The self-esteem scale (SES) developed by Rosenberg [67] and the Chinese version revised by Chen [68] were used to evaluate self-esteem. The scale consists of 10 items (e.g., “In general, I’m satisfied with myself”) measured on a four-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree. A higher mean score indicates a higher level of self-esteem. The Cronbach’s α of the SES scale in this study was 0.82 for the gay and bisexual groups and 0.80 for the heterosexual group.

Control variables and sexual orientation

In addition to the main variables, we controlled for age, whether the participant was an only child, marital status, education level, occupation, and income as covariates in the model to account for potential confounding effects of sociodemographic variables. Information about the sexual orientation of the participants was obtained by asking, “What is your sexual orientation?” with the response options “1 = heterosexual,” “2 = gay men,” “3 = bisexual,” “4 = pansexuality,” and “5 = asexuality.” For the primary comparative analyses in this study, the participants were categorized into two groups based on self-identification. The first group, referred to hereafter as the “gay and bisexual men group,” comprised male participants who identified as “homosexual” or “bisexual.” The second group, the “heterosexual men group” consisted of male participants who identified as “heterosexual.” Participants who identified as pansexual or asexual were excluded from these analyses due to insufficient sample sizes (n = 4).

Statistical analysis

First, IBM SPSS statistical version 23.0 (IBM Corp) was used for the Harman single-factor test, descriptive statistics, and correlation analysis. The Harman single-factor test was utilized to assess common method bias, the variation that can be explained by the first common factor in total. Group differences in the main and sociodemographic variables were examined via the chi-square test for categorical variables and the independent sample t test for continuous variables. Demographic information, such as age or income, was provided as the mean (M) and percentage (%). Continuous variables are presented for correlation analysis as the mean (M) and standard deviation (SD). Second, to test the theoretical model, Mplus 8.3. was used to examine the mediation effect by conducting structural equation modeling (SEM). To examine whether the proposed mediation model differed between gay and bisexual men and heterosexual men, multigroup structural equation modeling (SEM) was employed. The continuous variables were standardized before they were included in the mediation analysis. Structural equation modeling was set for bootstrapping with 5000 samples, which aimed to achieve stable confidence intervals and Type I error control and was controlled for the demographic variables [69]. The statistical significance was set at p < .05, and all tests were 2-tailed. The missing data were handled via Full Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML). The model fit was evaluated using the chi-square test (χ2) along with the following indices and cutoffs: the χ2/df ratio (below 3 was required), the comparative fit index (CFI >0.90), the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA < 0.08), and the Tucker–Lewis index (TLI >0.90) [69].

Results

Preliminary analysis

As shown by the Harman single-factor test, the total variance extracted by one factor was 25.526%, which is less than the recommended threshold of 50%, indicating that there was no common method bias in the current study [70]. The means and standard deviations for the variables are presented in Table 1. The results of the independence t test indicated significant differences in the father‒son relationships (t (550) = 10.01, p < .001, d = 0.68), masculinity (t (550) = 2.18, p < .05, d = 0.16), and suicidal ideation (t (549) = -4.32, p < .001, d = 0.28) between the heterosexual group and the gay and bisexual groups. The heterosexual men reported higher scores for father‒son relationships and higher scores for masculinity, whereas the gay and bisexual men reported a higher prevalence of suicidal ideation.

In addition, the correlation analysis (see Table 2) revealed that all the main variables were significantly correlated with each other in both groups, except for the correlation between suicidal ideation and the father‒son relationship, indicating that there was no direct relationship between these two variables. All the sociodemographic variables were entered into the mediation analysis as control variables.

Testing for mediation effects

The following model for suicidal ideation was proposed: a mediation analysis was conducted by estimating the association between father‒son relationships and suicidal ideation via the mediating role of masculinity and self-esteem (as shown in Fig. 1). For the mediation analyses, both unstandardized (B) and standardized (β) coefficients were estimated. The standardized coefficients (β) are primarily reported in the text for ease of interpretation.

For the gay and bisexual groups, the structural equation model tested in Mplus was supported by an excellent fit to the data, χ2 (2) = 3.73, p = .16. And the model fit indices were well within the accepted thresholds: χ2/df = 1.86; TLI = 0.91; CFI = 0.93; RMSEA = 0.053. We found that the association between the relationship with the father and suicidal ideation was fully mediated by masculinity and self-esteem (β = −0.034, p < .01) (Table 3) after controlling for covariates. The results demonstrated that the relationship with the father was significantly associated with higher levels of masculinity (β = 0.22, p < .001). A higher level of masculinity was significantly associated with a higher level of self-esteem (β = 0.48, p < .001), and a higher level of self-esteem was also significantly associated with a lower level of suicidal ideation (β = − 0.32, p < .001) (Fig. 2).

For the heterosexual group, the same chained mediation model was tested after controlling for covariates. First, the model demonstrated a marginal fit to the data, χ2 (2) = 2.73, p = .26, with goodness-of-fit indices as follows: (χ2/df = 1.36; TLI = 0.87; CFI = 0.99; RMSEA = 0.038), as the TLI (0.87) failed to meet the conventional cutoff of > 0.90, which may indicate some degree of model misspecification. Specifically, the key chained indirect effect from the father-son relationship through masculinity and self-esteem to suicidal ideation was not statistically significant (β = −0.017, p = .095). An examination of the individual paths within the model showed that a more positive father-son relationship was significantly associated with higher masculinity (β = 0.17, p < .05). In turn, higher masculinity was associated with higher self-esteem (β = 0.37, p < .001), and higher self-esteem was associated with lower suicidal ideation (β = −0.28, p < .001). Finally, the results of the multi-group mediation analysis revealed no significant differences in the proposed pathways between gay and bisexual men and heterosexual men (β = − 0.001, p = .19, 95% CI [- 0.002, 0.000]).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the relationship between father‒son bonds and suicidal ideation and the mediating roles of masculinity and self-esteem in this relationship among both gay and bisexual men and heterosexual young men in mainland China. Hypothesis 1 was partially supported: gay and bisexual men reported significantly greater suicidal ideation and poorer father‒son relationships and masculinity than heterosexual men did, whereas the groups did not differ in self-esteem. Hypothesis 2 received mixed support: father–son relationship quality was positively related to masculinity and self-esteem in both groups but showed no zero-order association with suicidal ideation. Hypothesis 3 was supported only among gay and bisexual men: a better father‒son relationship indirectly predicted lower suicidal ideation via higher masculinity and, in turn, higher self-esteem; the chain was nonsignificant for heterosexual men, whereas there was no significant difference in the models across groups.

First, the hypothetical frameworks are found to be significant only for young gay and bisexual men but not for heterosexual men. These differences may be due to the context of cultural expectations of masculinity and father–son relationships. Gay and bisexual men experience increased social pressure to conform to traditional masculine norms [62]. The theory of minority stress posits that the unique stressors associated with their marginalized status could lead to a more pronounced impact of family and societal pressures on the mental health of gay and bisexual men than on their heterosexual counterparts [27]. In addition, gender identity formation processes may differ between these groups; for example, studies show that LGB + individuals experience different timelines of identity development, including first same-sex attractions and coming out, in sharp contrast to heterosexual groups [71]. LGB + people may need time to process their attractions that are considered abnormal or unacceptable by society, explore their sexuality, choose an identity label that fits their experiences, confront sexual stigma, and make the decision to come out. Owing to extended concealment, familial disappointment, and pressure to conform to heteronormativity, the formation of sexual identity tends to be protracted and conflict-laden in Chinese gay and bisexual men [6, 32]. During the delayed and conflict-laden period, gay and bisexual men may rely more heavily on father-son approval to achieve self-worth than heterosexual peers do, considering Chinese cultural contexts where paternal authority and traditional masculinity are strongly emphasized [22].

Furthermore, these findings reveal a complete chained mediation pattern in which masculinity functions as a significant initial link in the sequential pathway from the father-son relationship to self-esteem, which ultimately predicts suicidal ideation. However, in the preliminary correlational analyses, father–son relationship quality showed no direct, significant association with suicidal ideation. This is statistically coherent, as recent methodological work has established that in complex social‒behavioral processes, a detectable total or direct effect is not a prerequisite for valid mediation [72]. Thus, the absence of a direct effect of paternal relationships on suicide risk does not undermine the serial pathway; rather, it indicates that the influence operates at an intrapsychic level via masculinity and self-esteem. To further test the specificity of our model, we examined an alternative model that included a direct path from the father-son relationship to self-esteem. However, this path was found to be non-significant. This finding strengthens our conclusion that the influence of the father-son relationship on self-esteem in our sample is fully mediated by masculinity. This highlights the central role of masculine identity as the key mechanism in this pathway.

This pattern may represent a cumulative-risk trajectory, highlighting how minority-stress vulnerabilities accrue over time. A suboptimal or disrupted father‒son bond—characterized by absence, rejection, or lack of emotional warmth—can fundamentally derail the healthy formation of masculine identity. Such disruption may push gay and bisexual sons to rigidly adhere to potentially toxic masculine norms (e.g., extreme stoicism, hyper self-reliance) as a compensatory mechanism or because alternative role models are unavailable [46, 47]. This rigidity, in turn, undermines the development of healthy, resilient self-esteem by suppressing authentic self-expression and help-seeking behaviors [49]. Impaired self-esteem then removes a critical protective buffer, markedly increasing susceptibility to suicidal ideation when life stressors arise [73]. The results underscore the importance of identifying and intervening in multiple developmental junctures to reduce the suicide risk of gay and bisexual men.

Notably, while the hypothesized indirect path from father–son relationship to suicidal ideation via masculinity and self-esteem was statistically significant in the gay and bisexual men and nonsignificant in heterosexual men, the multigroup test did not yield significant differences across groups. This finding indicates that partial functional equivalence across groups is plausible. Although sexual minority men may rely more on masculinity and self-esteem for psychological resilience, these constructs are still relevant for heterosexual men. Research has shown that traditional masculinity norms can also impact help-seeking, emotion regulation, and mental health in heterosexual populations [49, 74]. The observed indirect path in gay and bisexual men may therefore reflect a stronger, not exclusive, mechanism. Future studies may explore qualitative or intersectional methods to capture this complexity more fully, considering that the same items may carry subtly different meanings across identity groups due to differences in gender role expectations or social stigma [75].

While findings suggest that masculinity mediates the relationship between father–son closeness and mental health, distinguishing between adaptive masculinity and toxic masculinity is crucial. If masculinity is just viewed as protective, this might imply an endorsement of rigid gender norms. Masculinity is not monolithic. Instrumental traits, such as assertiveness, independence, and competence, have been shown to correlate positively with self-esteem and well-being when decoupled from emotional suppression and dominance [76]. Conversely, toxic masculine norms (e.g., anti-femininity, homophobia, emotional stoicism) are robustly linked to depression, anxiety, and suicidality—especially among gay and bisexual men, who often struggle to reconcile identity and social expectations [77, 78]. For Chinese gay and bisexual men, masculinity can be a means of negotiating social belonging, not necessarily a rejection of queerness, as studies have shown that adopting selective masculine expressions may buffer stigma and facilitate family acceptance [46, 47]. Therefore, masculinity may act as a culturally situated coping mechanism—protective in some contexts but harmful when rigid. Future research should employ multidimensional scales, such as the Conformity to Masculine Norms Inventory, to disentangle helpful from harmful aspects [79].

Interestingly, while gay/bisexual men reported significantly poorer father–son relationships and lower masculinity, their levels of self-esteem did not significantly differ from those of heterosexual men. Recent research suggests that self-esteem in sexual minority individuals is increasingly protected by external resilience factors, such as online peer support, identity affirmation, and community belonging [80, 81]. These sources may partially offset the negative impact of familial strain. As most of the participants in this study were in urban cities and were active in the online social group, they could find it more convenient to obtain online peer support and community belonging, which may influence the results across groups. Thus, this protective social support fostered the self-esteem of gay and bisexual men in this study.

Limitations, future research directions and strengths

This study provides new insights into the interactions among father‒son relationships, masculinity, and mental health in Chinese gay and bisexual men; nevertheless, it is not without limitations. First, the Bem Sex-Role Inventory employed in this study—although widely used—has been criticized for its implicit heteronormative assumption and stereotypic classifications of masculine traits (e.g., coding “understanding” as feminine) [82]. While the present research focuses on “perceived” masculinity within a specific cultural context, we acknowledge that some items may capture the “toxic” dimensions of masculinity. Future studies should pursue more culturally sensitive and nuanced measures of masculinity that encompass positive and flexible masculine expressions, a need that is especially critical for gay and bisexual individuals in China. Second, suicidal ideation was assessed via a single self-reported item. While this captures the overall presence and frequency of ideation, it lacks the depth of multi-item scales that evaluate dimensions such as intensity, duration, planning, and intent [83]. Future research should adopt validated multi-item instruments (e.g., the C-SSRS, SSI, and SBQ-R) to enhance measurement validity, specificity, and comparability. Third, although this study controlled for several sociodemographic variables, certain potential confounders, such as parental acceptance of sexual orientation and other sources of social support (e.g., support from friends), were not included. Incorporating these factors would yield a more comprehensive model of the determinants of gay and bisexual men’s mental health. Fourth, reliance on online, web-based recruitment, while broadening access to a diverse sample of sexual minority men, has inherent limitations such as self-selection bias and limited control over survey settings [84, 85]. Individuals who are more active online or more open about their sexual orientation are likely overrepresented. Future studies should adopt mixed-method designs or more heterogeneous recruitment strategies to increase sample representativeness. Fifth, although the exclusion criteria were reasonable, removing participants for missing age data, mismatched sex/gender identities, uncertain sexual orientation, or insufficient numbers of women may introduce sampling bias and, consequently, limit the generalizability of the findings to broader sexual minority populations, such as pansexual and transgender populations. Finally, the cross-sectional nature of the study precludes any causal inferences. Longitudinal research is needed to elucidate the temporal dynamics of these relationships over time.

Despite its limitations, the study has three notable strengths. First, this is the first systematic investigation of father‒son relationships and suicidal ideation among gay and bisexual men in China, thereby filling a critical research gap. Second, the use of a chained mediation model uncovers complex pathways among variables, yielding nuanced theoretical insights and providing some possibilities for intervening in the suicidal ideation of gay and bisexual men. Finally, the integration of both Western theoretical frameworks and Chinese cultural context—such as minority stress theory and social learning theory—enhances the study’s academic depth and global relevance.

Implications for practice and policy

Given that self-esteem is a critical proximal mediator, this study strongly recommends interventions aimed at bolstering self-esteem among young gay and bisexual men in China. While directly altering father–son relationships or entrenched gender roles may be challenging, cultivating self-acceptance, building resilience, and fostering positive identity through alternative means—such as peer support groups, affirmative counseling, and community programs—can substantially mitigate suicidal ideation.

Educational programs targeting adolescents and teachers should actively address traditional gender stereotypes and biases—including those tied to masculinity and sexual orientation. Promoting a broader, more flexible conception of masculinity that values emotional expression, empathy, and diverse identities—rather than narrowly emphasizing rigid, conventional traits—can alleviate the pressure on gay and bisexual males to conform to harmful norms. In turn, this fosters healthier self-concepts and reduces the stigma associated with nonconformity.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study in mainland China identifies a significant indirect pathway among gay and bisexual men. In this pathway, a stronger father-son relationship is associated with higher levels of masculinity, which is linked to greater self-esteem, and ultimately, to lower suicidal ideation. By laying a foundation for further exploration of the links between familial relationships, gender norms, and mental health, this research paves the way for more comprehensive studies and the development of effective intervention strategies in diverse cultural contexts.
