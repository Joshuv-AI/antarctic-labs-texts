Agnostic Process Tomography
Chirag Wadhwa, Laura Lewis, Elham Kashefi, Mina Doosti

Abstract
Characterizing a quantum system by learning its state or evolution is a fundamental problem in quantum physics and learning theory with a myriad of applications. Recently, as a new approach to this problem, the task of agnostic state tomography was defined, in which one aims to approximate an arbitrary quantum state by a simpler one in a given class. Generalizing this notion to quantum processes, we initiate the study of agnostic process tomography: given query access to an unknown quantum channel Φ \Phi and a known concept class 𝒞 \mathcal{C} of channels, output a quantum channel that approximates Φ \Phi as well as any channel in the concept class 𝒞 \mathcal{C} , up to some error. In this work, we propose several natural applications for this new task in quantum machine learning, quantum metrology, classical simulation, and error mitigation. In addition, we give efficient agnostic process tomography algorithms for a wide variety of concept classes, including Pauli strings, Pauli channels, quantum junta channels, low-degree channels, and a class of channels produced by 𝖰𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{QAC}^{0} circuits. The main technical tool we use is Pauli spectrum analysis of operators and superoperators. We also prove that, using ancilla qubits, any agnostic state tomography algorithm can be extended to one solving agnostic process tomography for a compatible concept class of unitaries, immediately giving us efficient agnostic learning algorithms for Clifford circuits, Clifford circuits with few T gates, and circuits consisting of a tensor product of single-qubit gates. Together, our results provide insight into the conditions and new algorithms necessary to extend the learnability of a concept class from the standard tomographic setting to the agnostic one.

1.2
Applications

As discussed previously, selecting an appropriate concept class is crucial to applying agnostic learning algorithms to practical scenarios.
By carefully choosing a concept class, we highlight a wide range of problems in near-term quantum computation that can be modelled in the framework of
proper
agnostic process tomography.
We hope that this offers fresh perspectives and tools for further advancements in these fields.
For some of these applications, our proposed algorithms are already applicable, while others require new algorithms, motivating further exploration of agnostic process tomography beyond this work.
We also emphasize that these applications are unique to the agnostic process tomography framework and often cannot be addressed using agnostic state tomography
[
42
,
43
]
alone.

Resource-constrained implementation of complex quantum processes.

A key application of agnostic process tomography is approximately implementing complex quantum processes on devices with limited resources.
Suppose a user with limited local resources has cloud access to a large fault-tolerant quantum computer and a desired complex process to run.
There are many reasons why the user may want to approximate this complex process by one that can be implemented on a weaker device.
For instance, repeated access to the remote system could be prohibitively expensive.
Instead, the user can obtain the best local approximation of the process by agnostic learning with respect to the class of circuits that can be implemented with the user’s local resources.
For example, if the user’s device has fewer qubits, one could consider agnostic unitary estimation of quantum juntas (
Section

4.4
), which learns the best few-qubit implementation of an arbitrary unitary.
Other interesting classes may include Clifford circuits, Cliffords with a few T gates and circuits consisting of tensor products of single-qubit gates (
Section

4.2
).
We again emphasize that this requires carefully choosing the concept class, as the complex process may not be well-approximated by an arbitrary class.

A unifying view of quantum machine learning.

Classically, the theoretical framework of agnostic learning captures the most general settings in machine learning (ML).
Namely, learning the best parameters for an ML model is equivalent to agnostic learning the concept class of all functions obtained by varying parameters of this ML model.
We argue that a similar equivalence holds for quantum machine learning (QML).
In QML, functions are evaluated using parameterized quantum circuits
[
60
]
, or more generally, quantum channels (such as a Linear Combinations of Unitaries (LCU)
[
61
,
62
,
63
]
).
By choosing an appropriate access model and error, agnostic process tomography of the class of channels expressible by an ansatz allows one to find the best parameters for the QML model.
In this sense, efficient agnostic process tomography of a model class implies efficient trainability. Thus, studying agnostic process tomography under various access models may lead to new insights into QML for both quantum and classical problems.
For example, many QML algorithms learn from expectation values of observables on output states of parameterized quantum circuits, which can be modelled as quantum statistical queries
[
64
,
65
,
50
,
51
,
22
,
55
]

Agnostic Quantum Metrology.

Quantum metrology is considered one of the most significant real-world applications of quantum technology
[
16
]
.
Here, one aims to estimate an unknown parameter
\theta

[

\{\mathcal{E}_{\theta}\}_{\theta}
called the

By probing the device modelled by the channel
and applying general quantum measurements to the outputs, one can construct an estimator
\hat{\theta}
of the unknown parameter with desirable statistical properties.
However, in practice, the encoding may be corrupted by unknown environmental noise.
Due to the challenges of characterizing noise and its effect on parameter estimation, noisy quantum metrology is less well-studied, and results are restricted to specific classes of noise
[
66
,
67
,
68
,
69
,
70
,
71

72
]
.
Many standard protocols are also highly noise-sensitive
[
16
,
73
,
74
,
75
]
.
Considering the known class
\{\mathcal{E}_{\theta}\}_{\theta}
as our concept class, one can model the problem of extracting a desirable estimator as an agnostic process tomography problem, which may provide us with new insights into metrology in noisy settings.
While noiseless metrology is close to conventional tomography, the parameter-encoding channel becomes unknown when transformed by realistic noise, which is more suitable for agnostic learning.
This perspective is also applicable in adversarial scenarios, where an unknown attack channel affects the encoding
[
76
,
77
]
.
We are unsure how this approach would compare to the standard quantum limit (the Heisenberg limit is unattainable for general noise without quantum error correction
[

]
) but nevertheless believe this is interesting to explore.

Error Mitigation.

Error mitigation protocols are often based on machine learning
[
79
,
80
]
or require a strong characterization of the noise channel beforehand
[
12
,
81
]
.
We argue that agnostic process tomography can be used to generalize a machine-learning-based error mitigation protocol called Clifford Data Regression
[
79
]
.
In this setting, we wish to mitigate a fixed, arbitrary noise channel
\mathcal{E}
on a quantum device.
We assume that
\mathcal{E}
is well-approximated by a parameterized class of noise channels
\{\mathcal{E}_{\theta}\}_{\theta}
such that, when
\theta
is known, the errors can be mitigated via classical post-processing. This is relevant when methods that map arbitrary noise to a known class, e.g., Pauli twirling
[
81
,
82
,
83
,
84
]
, are unavailable.
We propose the following protocol: 1) Execute various classically-simulable circuits on the quantum device to obtain noisy data.
2) Simulate the same circuits classically to obtain ideal, noiseless data.
3) Using the noisy and ideal data, perform agnostic process tomography with respect to
\{\mathcal{E}_{\theta}\}_{\theta}
.
This procedure outputs an approximation
\mathcal{E}_{\theta^{\prime}}
of the best representative of the arbitrary noise channel
\mathcal{E}
from
\{\mathcal{E}_{\theta}\}_{\theta}
.
Then, when implementing new circuits on this device, we can use
\mathcal{E}_{\theta^{\prime}}
to mitigate noise via classical post-processing.
Our new protocol can be seen as providing the pre-characterization of error often needed for error mitigation protocols such as probabilistic error cancellation
[
81
]
.
Note that in this setting, our access to the unknown process is through the pairs of ideal and noisy data rather than query access.

We consider the class of Pauli channels
\sum_{x}p_{x}\sigma_{x}(\cdot)\sigma_{x}
, parameterized by an underlying probability distribution
\{p_{x}\}_{x}
, as an example of our protocol for error mitigation.
Our agnostic tomography algorithm for Pauli channels in
Section

5.3
only requires data obtained by performing measurements in the basis of Choi states of the Pauli operators.
The approximated noise can then be used to mitigate errors in new data using standard techniques for Pauli channel noise
[
81
,
85
]
.
Note a limitation is that our agnostic learning algorithm for Pauli channels requires the use of ancilla qubits.
This example also indicates that for this protocol to work, agnostic process tomography cannot be used in a black box manner. Instead, the agnostic algorithm should dictate what data to gather from the noisy quantum device.
While we do not show an immediate application, we believe that this approach can result in new protocols for error mitigation for specific classes of noise.
This application is depicted in

1.3
Our Results

We design new efficient agnostic learning algorithms for a wide variety of concept classes.
We consider the setting where we are given query access to the unknown quantum channel, i.e., we can apply the unknown channel to any (potentially adaptively-chosen) input state.
Moreover, we consider the error function to be given by either the root mean squared trace distance
\mathrm{d_{avg}}
or the normalized Frobenius distance

The root mean squared trace distance is defined as

\mathrm{d_{avg}}(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2})\triangleq\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\ket{\psi}\sim\mu_{S}}[\mathrm{d_{tr}}(\mathcal{E}_{1}(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}),\mathcal{E}_{2}(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}))^{2}]},

Proof Ideas

Fourier analysis of Boolean functions
[
95

is widely used in theoretical computer science and classical learning theory.
In particular, it has been used to develop efficient learning algorithms for functions with a sufficiently sparse Fourier decomposition
[

,

and in classical agnostic learning
[

Pauli spectrum analysis

[
28
]
generalizes Fourier analysis for quantum operators (see
Section

2.2
for a more thorough discussion).
Analogous to the Fourier decomposition, the key idea is that one can uniquely decompose an operator
A
acting on an
n
-qubit system into a linear combination of Pauli strings:

A=\sum_{x\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}}\hat{A}_{x}\sigma_{x},\quad\hat{A}_{x}\triangleq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\tr(A \sigma_x),

(1.6)

where
\sigma_{x}\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
.
Recently,
[
25
]
further generalized this to the
Fourier spectrum of superoperators
(see
Section

2.3
), where a superoperator
\Phi
in an
n
-qubit space is uniquely decomposed as

\Phi=\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}}\hat{\Phi}(x,y)\Phi_{x,y},\quad\hat{\Phi}(x,y)=\frac{1}{4^{n}}\tr(J(\Phi_{x,y})^\dagger J(\Phi)),\quad\Phi_{x,y}(\rho)=\sigma_{x}\rho\sigma_{y},

(1.7)

where
J(\Phi)
denotes the Choi represenation of
\Phi

As in classical learning theory, many quantum learning algorithms utilize Pauli/Fourier spectrum analysis
[

,
24
,
22
,
30
,
29
,
40

25

.

In our work, we leverage both formalisms.
Most of our algorithms involve learning the significant Pauli/Fourier coefficients.
However, we can only estimate up to an additive error, which
a priori
may not satisfy the necessary properties of a valid unitary/channel, resulting in improper algorithms.

To resolve this, for agnostic unitary estimation, we “project” our improper hypothesis to its closest unitary using a technique from
[
23
]
.
We show that this outputs the closest unitary with respect to the normalized Frobenius distance
d_{F}
, resulting in proper agnostic unitary estimation.
However, this problem is more difficult for agnostic process tomography.
We discuss a potential approach based on convex optimization
[

for projecting an improper hypothesis onto a valid quantum channel

Bounded-gate circuits.

First, we consider any finite-sized class
\mathcal{C}
of unitaries.
Similarly to
[

[

to estimate the average infidelity between the unknown quantum channel
\Phi
and all

Then, we output the unitary in
\mathcal{C}
with the smallest estimated distance.
Thus, we solve proper agnostic unitary estimation for
\mathcal{C}
with respect to the root mean squared trace distance
\mathrm{d_{avg}}
and show this uses
\mathcal{O}(\log|\mathcal{C}|)
queries to
\Phi
and time
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{C}|)

For many classes of interest, this results in a sample-efficient but computationally inefficient algorithm, emphasizing the importance of computational efficiency for agnostic learning.
We show a weaker version of this result for agnostic process tomography of
\mathcal{C}
, which stems from converting from average infidelity to average trace distance for mixed inputs.
As a corollary, by utilizing known covering number bounds
[

, we obtain agnostic learning algorithms for the class of unitaries implementable by
G
two-qubit gates.
Note that in the realizable setting,
[

proves computational lower bounds.
We prove this result in
Sections

and

.

Agnostic process tomography from agnostic state tomography

Recently, agnostic state tomography for several concept classes with respect to fidelity
[
42
,
43
]
has been studied.
We show that this translates into guarantees for agnostic unitary estimation/process tomography for a compatible concept class
\mathcal{C}
of unitaries with respect to
d_{F}
with a quadratic increase in the error
\epsilon
.
In particular, we require that the Choi state of each
U\in\mathcal{C}
is in the concept class for agnostic state tomography.
Our algorithm is simple: apply the agnostic state tomography algorithm to the Choi state of the unknown quantum process.
Specifically, we obtain improper
1
-agnostic unitary estimation but a weaker error bound for agnostic process tomography (see
Table

1(b)
), again due to converting between infidelity and trace distance for mixed inputs.
The key step in our proof shows a relationship between the infidelity and the normalized Frobenius distance between two Choi states.
We prove this result in
Sections

4.2
and
5.2
.

Pauli strings.

Next, we consider the class of Pauli strings
\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
.
We use the fact that one can efficiently sample from the distribution formed by the Pauli/Fourier coefficients
[
28
]
.
After collecting samples, we simply output the most frequently sampled Pauli string.
Thus, we achieve proper 1-agnostic unitary estimation/process tomography.
We prove this in
Sections

4.3
and
5.3
.

Pauli channels.

Pauli channels are a naturally occurring and widely studied

Pauli channels are of the form

Similarly to Pauli strings, we can sample from

Thus, we obtain proper 1-agnostic process tomography of Pauli channels.
We prove this in

Junta unitaries and channels.

A
k
-junta unitary/channel is an
n
-qubit unitary/channel that acts only on
k
qubits (
Definitions

15
and
18

[
24
,
25

devised realizable algorithms for learning junta unitaries/channels.
Here, they estimate the
influence
(
Equation

2.16
) of each qubit, then select a highly influential subset of qubits and perform full state tomography on the Choi state restricted to these qubits.

In the agnostic setting, we show that the optimal error is not tightly characterized by the most influential subset, but by the highest
weight
subset (
Definitions

6
and
8
).
In the realizable setting, the relevant subset of qubits has the same weight and influence. However, we show that these notions are distinct in the agnostic setting (
Example

1
).
We also prove that the hypothesis’s error is characterized by the chosen subset and depends on its weight, not its influence.
Thus, our algorithms aim to identify the highest weight subset of qubits.
However, identifying this subset requires more queries/time than identifying the most influential one, giving us worse complexities than the realizable algorithms.

In the unitary case, we first estimate the weights of all subsets of
[n]
of cardinality
k
using samples from the distribution formed by the Pauli coefficients.
Then, we consider the subset
S
with the greatest estimated weight.
Next, we estimate all the coefficients of the unitary that are supported only on
S
.
We then construct a hypothesis using these estimates and project it to a unitary as described previously.
Despite being less efficient than tomography on qubits in
S
, our method makes it easier to obtain a proper hypothesis.
Our method results in improper 1-agnostic unitary estimation of unitary juntas.
After applying the unitary projection to the improper hypothesis, we obtain proper 2-agnostic unitary estimation.
We prove this formally in
Section

4.4
.

For agnostic tomography of junta channels, we find that identifying the subset with the highest Fourier weight is much harder than in the unitary case.
Here, we can only sample from the distribution defined by the diagonal Fourier coefficients
\{\hat{\Phi}(x,x)\}_{x}
while the weight also depends on the off-diagonal coefficients.
As a result, our algorithm learns
all
low-degree coefficients and uses them to estimate the weights.
Explicitly, we learn all coefficients
\hat{\Phi}(x,y)
for
|x|,|y|\leq k
, where
|x|
denotes the size of the support of the string
x\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}
.
From here, the algorithm is similar to the unitary junta case.
We achieve improper 1-agnostic process tomography of junta channels.
We discuss a potential approach for producing a proper hypothesis in
Section

5.7
.
We prove this in
Section

5.4
.

Low-degree unitaries and channels.

Low-degree unitaries/channels are defined as those with all their Pauli/Fourier weight on low-degree terms.
Explicitly, a unitary has degree
d
if when
|x|>d
, then
\hat{U}_{x}=0
and similarly for channels (
Definitions

16
and
19
).
A straightforward idea is to learn all of their low-degree coefficients.
It turns out that this simple algorithm results in improper 1-agnostic learning of low-degree objects.
Our unitary projection method mentioned previously is computationally inefficient for low-degree unitaries and its output may not be low-degree, leaving us with an improper hypothesis.

[
30
]
develops realizable algorithms leveraging Bohnenblust-Hille (BH) inequalities for operators
[
116
]
/

superoperators
[
30
]
.
These inequalities provide upper bounds on norms of the spectra of low-degree objects.
Using these bounds,
[
30
]
show that it suffices to learn significantly fewer Pauli/Fourier coefficients, resulting in algorithms with complexities independent of
n
.
Using similar arguments for agnostic unitary estimation, we show that the algorithm of
[
30
]
is inefficient and only performs better than the trivial algorithm when the optimal error of the class of low-degree unitaries is at most inverse exponential in
n
, i.e., when the problem is almost realizable.
We remark that improved bounds for the BH inequality may result in better guarantees for this algorithm and leave this open for future work.
As this algorithm does not work well in the simpler unitary case, we do not analyze its performance for agnostic process tomography.
We prove these results in
Sections

4.5
and
5.5
.

QAC0 channels.

The circuit class
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
consists of constant-depth circuits with controlled-

[

]
. In
[
22

, the authors study the class of channels formed by applying a

[

shows that the Choi representations of such channels have Pauli weight concentrated on low-degree terms.
Similarly to
[

, we learn the low-degree coefficients of the Choi representation of an arbitrary channel using classical shadows
[

, resulting in improper 1-agnostic process tomography for this class of

We show this in
Section

5.6
.

1.5
Outlook

Our work initiates the study of agnostic process tomography, which we define as learning the best approximation of an unknown process from a simple class.
We design efficient agnostic learning algorithms for various concept classes, leveraging the powerful tools of Pauli spectrum analysis.
We also propose several near-term applications for agnostic process tomography.
We believe there is still substantial work to be done in this area, and we outline some open questions below.

First, can one design an efficient algorithm to map an arbitrary superoperator to its closest quantum channel?
This is desirable because many of our improper agnostic learning algorithms can be made proper using such a subroutine, and many applications of agnostic process tomography (
Section

1.2
) require proper learning algorithms.
As a significant first step towards this question, we have reduced the problem to that of proving a particular mathematical condition holds (see
Section

5.7
) but were unable to prove or disprove it.
Solving this question would be a significant result, as it would also lead to proper learning algorithms for realizable process tomography problems, such as those in
[
22
,
30
]
.

Second, can we improve the error bounds for agnostic process tomography of bounded-gate circuits and agnostic process tomography from agnostic state tomography?
Notably, as a result of the distance inequalities we used, our learning algorithms achieve error bounds of
\mathsf{opt}^{1/4}
and
2^{1/4}\mathsf{opt}^{1/2}
, respectively, rather than
\mathsf{opt}
.
We believe these bounds can be improved to
1
-agnostic process tomography and leave this as an open problem.
Also, can we avoid the quadratic increase in error when converting agnostic state tomography algorithms to those for agnostic process tomography, as in quantum process tomography
[
6
]
?

Third, is it possible to develop efficient agnostic learning algorithms for juntas that match the realizable lower bounds
[
24
,
25
]
?
Our proposed algorithms for juntas have worse complexities than the realizable setting due to needing to estimate the weight of subsets of qubits, rather than the influence.

Next, for learning low-degree quantum objects, can one obtain better complexities by using the algorithm of
[
30
]
in the agnostic setting?
We show in
Section

4.5
that straightforwardly utilizing the algorithm from
[
30
]
results in an inefficient algorithm with worse performance than ours.
However, we believe that by proving tighter upper bounds on the norms in the BH inequalities, this could be improved.

Finally, we list several broad open questions.
Could different definitions of agnostic tomography lead to new applications, e.g., comparing the performances of agnostic learners for different concept classes?
For instance, the definition of
[
49
]
may lend itself to this, where they define agnostic learning with respect to two classes rather than just one.
Can one develop agnostic learning algorithms with respect to worst-case distance metrics, e.g., diamond distance? All results in our work only hold for average-case distances.
Also, many of our proposed algorithms require preparing the Choi state of the unknown channel.
Instead, can one develop agnostic learning algorithms with fewer resources?
As agnostic process tomography is at least as hard as in the realizable setting, lower bounds in realizable process tomography can be transferred to the agnostic setting as well.
Can one prove stronger worst-case agnostic lower bounds?
Finally, are there other physically-motivated classes that admit efficient agnostic process tomography algorithms?
Some interesting classes include the evolution of well-studied Hamiltonians
[
117
,
118
,
119
,
120
]
or classes of parameterized quantum circuits
[
60
]
with specific ansatz
[
121
,
122
,
123
]
.

Preliminaries

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we write
[k]\triangleq\{1,\dots,k\}
. Throughout, let
n
denote the system size. Let
\mathcal{H}=(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
denote the Hilbert space on these
n
qubits, and let
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
denote the set of bounded linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
.
Let
\mathcal{U}_{d}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
denote the set of
d
-dimensional unitaries. A map from
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
to itself is known as a
superoperator
on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.
A
quantum channel
on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a superoperator that is completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP). For any unitary, we sometimes use a calligraphic font to denote the associated quantum channel.

2.1
Choi state

A useful tool we will need is the Choi-Jamiolkowski isomorphism
[
124
,
125
]
, which maps between unitaries acting on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and pure states in
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
.

Definition 2

(Choi state of a unitary)
.

Let
U
be a unitary acting on an
n
-qubit Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.
Then, the
Choi state
is defined by applying
U
on one half of a maximally entangled state over
2n
qubits.
In particular, define the maximally entangled state

|\Phi^{+}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}}}\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}|i,i\rangle.

(2.1)

Then, the Choi state is defined as

|v(U)\rangle\triangleq(U\otimes I)|\Phi^{+}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}}}\sum_{i,j=0}^{2^{n}-1}U_{i,j}\ket{i,j}.

(2.2)

The Choi state can be further generalized for quantum channels, mapping quantum channels to mixed states.

Definition 3

(Choi state of a quantum channel)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a quantum channel acting on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Then, the associated
Choi state
is defined by applying
\mathcal{E}
on one half of a maximally entangled state over
2n
qubits. In particular, the Choi state is defined as

v(\mathcal{E})=(\mathcal{E}\otimes\mathcal{I})(|\Phi^{+}\rangle\langle\Phi^{+}|)=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{i,j=0}^{2^{n}-1}\mathcal{E}(|i\rangle\langle j|)\otimes|i\rangle\langle j|.

(2.3)

For an arbitrary superoperator
\Psi
on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, we will also consider the
Choi representation
, obtained by applying
\Psi
on one half of the unnormalized, maximally entangled state, i.e.,

J(\Psi)=\sum_{i,j=0}^{2^{n}-1}\Psi(|i\rangle\langle j|)\otimes|i\rangle\langle j|.

(2.4)

The Choi representation of a quantum channel satisfies the following necessary and sufficient properties.

Lemma 1

(Properties of Choi representations of quantum channels
[
126
]
)
.

Let
\Psi
be a superoperator from
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
to
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
. Then
\Psi
is a quantum channel if and only if

J(\Psi)\succcurlyeq 0\quad and\quad\tr_{1}(J(\Psi))=I_{2}.

(2.5)

where
\tr_{1}
denotes the partial trace over
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
I_{2}
denotes the identity on
\mathcal{H}_{2}
.

2.2
Pauli spectrum of operators

A key technique we use throughout our proofs is the analysis of the Pauli spectrum of quantum operators, first introduced in
[
28
]
to generalize Boolean Fourier analysis
[
95
]
to the quantum setting.
Pauli analysis was also studied in
[
29
,
24
,
22
]
.
Throughout this work, we mostly follow the notation of
[
28
,
95
]
.

The set of bounded linear operators
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
can be viewed as a Hilbert space when endowed with the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product

\langle A,B\rangle\triangleq\tr(A^\dag B)=\sum_{i,j\in[2^{n}]}A_{i,j}^{*}B_{i,j},

(2.6)

where we use
A_{i,j}
to denote the
ij
-th entry of
A
as a matrix.
We use
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{F}
to denote the norm induced by this inner product, namely the Hilbert-Schmidt or Frobenius norm.
In particular, we can write

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}=\langle A,A\rangle=\sum_{i,j\in[2^{n}]}|A_{i,j}|^{2}.

(2.7)

Let
\{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}\}
denote the Pauli matrices, i.e.,

\sigma_{0}=I=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma_{1}=X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma_{2}=Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma_{3}=Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

(2.8)

For a vector
x\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}
, we denote
\sigma_{x}\triangleq\sigma_{x_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{x_{n}}
and write
\mathsf{supp}(x)\triangleq\{i\in[n]\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x_{i}\neq 0\}
. We will denote the
degree
of
x
by
|x|=|\mathsf{supp}(x)|
. Because the Paulis
\{\sigma_{x}\}_{x\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}}
form an orthogonal basis for
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product, then we can uniquely decompose any operator with respect to this basis.

Definition 4

(Pauli spectrum
[
28
]
)
.

For any
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, there exists a unique decomposition as

A=\sum_{x\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}}\hat{A}_{x}\sigma_{x},\quad\hat{A}_{x}\triangleq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\langle\sigma_{x},A\rangle.

(2.9)

Here,
\hat{A}_{x}
is called the
Pauli coefficient
of
A
, and
\{\hat{A}_{x}\}_{x\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}}
is called the
Pauli spectrum
of
A
.

As in classical Fourier analysis, there are quantum analogues of Parseval and Plancherel’s identities.

Lemma 2

(Proposition 10 in
[
28
]
)
.

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.
Then, Parseval’s identity is

\frac{1}{2^{n}}\|A\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{x\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}}|\hat{A}_{x}|^{2}.

(2.10)

Moreover, Plancherel’s identity is

\frac{1}{2^{n}}\langle A,B\rangle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}}\hat{A}^{*}_{x}\hat{B}_{x}.

(2.11)

Following the notation in
[
28
,
22
]
, we define the
Pauli weight of an operator at level
k
.
This is similar to the classical definition of Fourier weight
[
95
]
.

Definition 5

(Pauli weight at level
k

[
28
,
22
]
)
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
0\leq k\leq n
. Then, the
Pauli weight of
A
at level
k
is defined by

w^{=k}(A)\triangleq\sum_{x\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n},|x|=k}|\hat{A}_{x}|^{2}.

(2.12)

We similarly define
w^{\leq k}(A),w^{>k}(A)
, etc.

For a subset
S\subseteq[n]
, we also denote

\mathcal{P}_{S}\triangleq\{x\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathsf{supp}(x)\subseteq S\}.

(2.13)

Notice that

|\mathcal{P}_{S}|=4^{|S|}.

(2.14)

We also use the shorthand
\mathcal{P}_{n}\triangleq\mathcal{P}_{[n]}=\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}
.
We also define the Pauli weight of
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
on a given subset
S\subseteq[n]
, similar to the Pauli weight at level
k
.

Definition 6

(Pauli weight on a subset)
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
S\subseteq[n]
.
Then, the
Pauli weight of
A
on the subset
S
is defined by

w_{S}(A)\triangleq\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{S}}|\hat{A}_{x}|^{2}.

(2.15)

This definition is reminiscent of the influence on a set of qubits.
Recall from
[
24
]
that the influence of a unitary
U
on a subset
S\subseteq[n]
is defined as

\mathbf{Inf}_{S}[U]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}\\
\mathsf{supp}(x)\cap S\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}|\hat{U}_{x}|^{2}.

(2.16)

Here, we see that the Pauli weight on
S
differs from the influence due to the set of
x\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}
that the summation is over.

The Choi state of a unitary has a nice relationship with its Pauli decomposition.
Let
U=\sum_{x\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}}\hat{U}_{x}\sigma_{x}
be the Pauli decomposition of some unitary
U
.
Then, the Choi state of
U
decomposes as

|v(U)\rangle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}}\hat{U}_{x}|v(\sigma_{x})\rangle,

(2.17)

where
\ket{v(\sigma_x)}
denotes the Choi state of the Pauli
\sigma_{x}
.

2.3
Fourier spectrum of superoperators

One can also extend the Pauli analysis from the previous section to superoperators, which was initiated in
[
25
]
.
This is also explored in
[
22
]
, but we mainly follow the presentation of
[
25
]
.
The space of superoperators on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
can be viewed as a Hilbert space when endowed with the inner product

\langle\Phi,\Psi\rangle\triangleq\langle J(\Phi),J(\Psi)\rangle=\tr(J(\Phi)^\dagger J(\Psi)).

(2.18)

This inner product induces the norm

\|\Phi\|_{F}\triangleq\sqrt{\langle J(\Phi),J(\Phi)\rangle}=\|J(\Phi)\|_{F}.

(2.19)

Consider the superoperators
\{\Phi_{x,y}\}_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}

\Phi_{x,y}(\rho)=\sigma_{x}\rho\sigma_{y},\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}.

(2.20)

These superoperators form an orthogonal basis for the space of superoperators on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
with norm given by

\|\Phi_{x,y}\|_{F}=2^{n},\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}.

(2.21)

Because
\{\Phi_{x,y}\}_{x,y}
forms an orthogonal basis, we can uniquely decompose any superoperator with respect to this basis.

Definition 7

(Fourier expansion of superoperators; Definition 7 in
[
25
]
)
.

For any superoperator
\Phi
on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, there exists a unique decomposition as

\Phi=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\hat{\Phi}(x,y)\Phi_{x,y},\quad\hat{\Phi}(x,y)=\frac{1}{4^{n}}\langle\Phi_{x,y},\Phi\rangle.

(2.22)

Here,
\hat{\Phi}_{x,y}
is a Fourier coefficient of
\Phi
, and
\{\hat{\Phi}_{x,y}\}_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}
is called the
Fourier spectrum
of
\Phi
. Denote by
\hat{\Phi}
the
4^{n}\times 4^{n}
-matrix with entries
\hat{\Phi}(x,y)
.

The matrix of Fourier coefficients
\hat{\Phi}
satisfies the following properties.

Lemma 3

(Properties of Fourier coefficients; Lemma 8 in
[
25
]
)
.

For any superoperator
\Phi
on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, the matrix of Fourier coefficients
\hat{\Phi}
satisfies the following properties.

1.

There exists a unitary
U
such that

\hat{\Phi}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}U^{\dagger}J(\Phi)U.

(2.23)

2.

Then,
\hat{\Phi}\succcurlyeq 0
if and only if
J(\Phi)\succcurlyeq 0
.

3.

If
\Phi
is a quantum channel, then

0\leq\hat{\Phi}(x,x)\leq 1,\;\forall x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\hat{\Phi}(x,x)=\tr(\hat{\Phi})=1.

(2.24)

We remark that for a unitary with Pauli spectrum
\{\hat{U}_{x}\}_{x}
, the Fourier coefficients of the associated quantum channel
\Phi
are given by

\hat{\Phi}(x,y)=\hat{U}_{x}\hat{U}_{y}^{*}.

(2.25)

Similar to the case of the Pauli spectrum, we once again have analogues of Parseval’s and Plancherel’s identities.

Lemma 4

(Parseval’s and Plancherel’s for superoperators)
.

Let
\Phi,\Psi
be quantum channels on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Then, Parseval’s identity is

\frac{1}{4^{n}}\|\Phi\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|^{2}.

(2.26)

Moreover, Plancherel’s identity is

\frac{1}{4^{n}}\langle\Phi,\Psi\rangle=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\hat{\Phi}(x,y)^{*}\hat{\Psi}(x,y).

(2.27)

Also similarly to the Pauli spectrum, we will define the weight at level
k
of a superoperator.

Definition 8

(Fourier weight at level
k
)
.

Let
\Phi
be a superoperator on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
0\leq k\leq n
. Then, the
Fourier weight of
\Phi
at level
k
is defined by

w^{=k}(A)\triangleq\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n},|x|=|y|=k}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|^{2}.

(2.28)

The weight of a subset, the weights of order at least
k
, and so on can be defined similarly to
Definitions

6
and
5
.

The Choi state of a quantum channel
\Phi
also has a nice relationship with the Fourier coefficients. In particular, the Choi representation is given by

J(\Phi)=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\hat{\Phi}(x,y)J(\Phi_{x,y}),

(2.29)

and the Choi state is given by

v(\Phi)=\frac{1}{2^{n}}J(\Phi)=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\hat{\Phi}(x,y)J(\Phi_{x,y})=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|v(\sigma_{x})\rangle\langle v(\sigma_{y})|,

(2.30)

which can be seen by normalizing
J(\Phi)
by
2^{n}
, as
\Phi
is trace-preserving.

2.4
Distance metrics and covering nets

In this section, we review definitions of various distance metrics and important properties needed for our proofs.
In
Section

2.2
, we introduced the Frobenius norm
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}=\tr(A^\dagger A)
.
We now define normalized variants of the Frobenius distance.

Definition 9

(Normalized Frobenius distance between operators)
.

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.
We define the
normalized Frobenius distance
as

d_{F}(A,B)\triangleq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n+1}}}\|A-B\|_{F}.

(2.31)

We have the following relation between the Pauli spectra of two operators and their normalized Frobenius distance.

Lemma 5

(Frobenius distance and Pauli coefficients)
.

For any
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
,

d_{F}(A,B)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\left|\hat{A}_{x}-\hat{B}_{x}\right|^{2}.

(2.32)

Proof.

This follows by direct computation.

\displaystyle d_{F}(A,B)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}\|A-B\|_{F}^{2}

(2.33)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}\left(\sum_{i,j\in[2^{n}]}|A_{i,j}-B_{i,j}|^{2}\right)

(2.34)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}\left(\sum_{i,j\in[2^{n}]}|A_{i,j}|^{2}+|B_{i,j}|^{2}-A_{i,j}^{*}B_{i,j}-A_{i,j}B_{i,j}^{*}\right)

(2.35)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}\left(\|A\|_{F}^{2}+\|B\|_{F}^{2}-\langle A,B\rangle-\langle B,A\rangle\right)

(2.36)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|\hat{A}_{x}|^{2}+|\hat{B}_{x}|^{2}-\hat{A}^{*}_{x}\hat{B}_{x}-\hat{A}_{x}\hat{B}^{*}_{x}\right)

(2.37)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\left|\hat{A}_{x}-\hat{B}_{x}\right|^{2}.

(2.38)

Here, the second and fourth lines follow by definition of the Frobenius norm and the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product.
The second to last line follows from
Lemma

2
.
∎

Based on the notion of a Frobenius norm for superoperators introduced in
Section

2.3
, we now define a similar distance between superoperators.

Definition 10

(Normalized Frobenius distance between superoperators
[
25
]
)
.

Let
\Phi,\Psi
be two superoperators on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Then, we define the normalized Frobenius distance as

d_{F}(\Phi,\Psi)\triangleq\frac{1}{2^{n}\sqrt{2}}\|\Phi-\Psi\|_{F}\triangleq\frac{1}{2^{n}\sqrt{2}}\|J(\Phi)-J(\Psi)\|_{F}.

(2.39)

Note that the normalization ensures that the distance is in
[0,1]
for two quantum channels.
We now show a relation between this distance and the Fourier spectrum of superoperators.

Lemma 6

(Normalized Frobenius distance of superoperators and Fourier coefficients; Corollary 28 in
[
25
]
)
.

Let
\Phi,\Psi
be two superoperators on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Then,

d_{F}(\Phi,\Psi)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y,\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)-\hat{\Psi}(x,y)|^{2}.

(2.40)

Proof.

We give an explicit proof via computation.

\displaystyle d_{F}(\Phi,\Psi)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2n+1}}\|\Phi-\Psi\|_{F}^{2}

(2.41)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2n+1}}\langle\Phi-\Psi,\Phi-\Psi\rangle

(2.42)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2n+1}}\left(\|\Phi\|_{F}^{2}+\|\Psi\|_{F}^{2}-\langle\Phi,\Psi\rangle-\langle\Psi,\Phi\rangle\right)

(2.43)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\left(|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|^{2}+|\hat{\Psi}(x,y)|^{2}-\hat{\Phi}(x,y)^{*}\hat{\Psi}(x,y)-\hat{\Phi}(x,y)\hat{\Psi}(x,y)^{*}\right)

(2.44)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)-\hat{\Psi}(x,y)|^{2},

(2.45)

where the first three lines follow from the definitions of the normalized Frobenius distance and Hilbert-Schmidt norm for superoperators, and the fourth line follows from
Lemma

4
.
∎

We now define distances between two quantum states, which we will shortly use to define average-case distances between quantum channels.

Definition 11

(Trace Distance and Fidelity)
.

The
trace distance
between two quantum states is given by

\mathrm{d_{tr}}(\rho,\sigma)\triangleq\frac{1}{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\rho-\sigma\right\rVert}_{1}.

(2.46)

The
fidelity
between two quantum states is given by

F(\rho,\sigma)\triangleq\tr\left(\sqrt{\sqrt{\rho}\sigma\sqrt{\rho}}\right)^{2}.

(2.47)

We call
1-F
the
infidelity
of two states.

The trace distance and fidelity are related in the following ways
[
127
]
.
For any two quantum states
\rho,\sigma
,

1-\sqrt{F(\rho,\sigma)}\leq\mathrm{d_{tr}}(\rho,\sigma)\leq\sqrt{1-F(\rho,\sigma)}.

(2.48)

When one of the states is pure, e.g.,
\sigma=\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}
, then

1-F(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|,\rho)\leq\mathrm{d_{tr}}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|,\rho).

(2.49)

When both states are pure, then

1-F(|\psi\rangle,|\phi\rangle)=\mathrm{d_{tr}}(|\psi\rangle,|\phi\rangle)^{2}.

(2.50)

In addition to the normalized Frobenius distance, we can also consider average-case distances.
We follow
[
27
]
to define an average distance between two quantum channels with respect to a measure.

Definition 12

(Average distance
[
27
]
)
.

For any measure
\mu
over
d
-dimensional states, we define the
average distance
between two channels
d_{\mu}
as root mean squared trace distance of output states over inputs from
\mu

d_{\mu}(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2})\triangleq\sqrt{\mathbf{E}_{\rho\sim\mu}[\mathrm{d_{tr}}(\mathcal{E}_{1}(\rho),\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho))^{2}]}.

(2.51)

In particular, when the average is taken over the Haar measure
\mu_{S}
, we denote the distance by
\mathrm{d_{avg}}

\mathrm{d_{avg}}(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2})\triangleq\sqrt{\mathbf{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\mu_{S}}[\mathrm{d_{tr}}(\mathcal{E}_{1}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|),\mathcal{E}_{2}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|))^{2}]}.

(2.52)

However, because Haar-random states are difficult to generate, one may turn to other notions of random states with respect to which to measure an average-case distance.
One such physically motivated class of states is locally scrambled ensembles
[
128
,
129
,
27
]
.

We will make use of the fact that for any measure
\mu
over states,
d_{\mu}
satisfies the triangle inequality. We will follow the proof of
[
27
]
, where this was shown for unitaries.

Lemma 7

(Triangle inequality of
\mathrm{d_{avg}}
; Lemma 6 in
[
27
]
)
.

For any quantum channels
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{E}_{3}
acting on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, and any measure
\mu
over states in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, we have

d_{\mu}(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{3})\leq d_{\mu}(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2})+d_{\mu}(\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{E}_{3}).

(2.53)

Proof.

\displaystyle d_{\mu}(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{3})^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{\rho\sim\mu}\left[\mathrm{d_{tr}}(\mathcal{E}_{1}(\rho),\mathcal{E}_{3}(\rho))^{2}\right]

(2.54)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}_{\rho\sim\mu}\left[(\mathrm{d_{tr}}(\mathcal{E}_{1}(\rho),\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho))+(\mathrm{d_{tr}}(\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho),\mathcal{E}_{3}(\rho)))^{2}\right]

(2.55)

\displaystyle=d_{\mu}(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2})^{2}+d_{\mu}(\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{E}_{3})^{2}+2\mathbf{E}_{\rho\sim\mu}\left[\mathrm{d_{tr}}(\mathcal{E}_{1}(\rho),\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho))\mathrm{d_{tr}}(\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho),\mathcal{E}_{3}(\rho))\right]

(2.56)

\displaystyle\leq d_{\mu}(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2})^{2}+d_{\mu}(\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{E}_{3})^{2}+2\sqrt{\mathbf{E}_{\rho\sim\mu}\left[\mathrm{d_{tr}}(\mathcal{E}_{1}(\rho),\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho))^{2}\right]}\sqrt{\mathbf{E}_{\rho\sim\mu}\left[\mathrm{d_{tr}}(\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho),\mathcal{E}_{3}(\rho))^{2}\right]}

(2.57)

\displaystyle=(d_{\mu}(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2})+d_{\mu}(\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{E}_{3}))^{2},

(2.58)

where in the first inequality we use the triangle inequality of trace distance, and in the last inequality we use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
∎

We can also relate the average-case distance
\mathrm{d_{avg}}
to the Pauli coefficients via the following lemma.

Lemma 8

(Average distance and Pauli coefficients)
.

For any
n
-qubit unitaries
U,V
, we have

\mathrm{d_{avg}}(U,V)^{2}=\frac{2^{n}}{2^{n}+1}\left(1-\bigg|\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\hat{U}^{*}_{x}\hat{V}_{x}\bigg|^{2}\right),

(2.59)

Proof.

By properties of the Haar integral (e.g., Example 48 in
[
130
]
), we have

\mathrm{d_{avg}}(U,V)^{2}=\frac{4^{n}-\left|\tr(U^\dag V)\right|^{2}}{2^{n}(2^{n}+1)}.

(2.60)

Then, by Plancherel’s identity (
Lemma

2
), we have

\tr(U^\dagger V)=\langle U,V\rangle=2^{n}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\hat{U}_{x}^{*}\hat{V}_{x}.

(2.61)

The result clearly follows from this.
∎

A related topic is that of covering nets from high-dimensional probability theory.
We can define a covering net with respect to any of the distance metrics introduced above.

Definition 13

(Covering net/number and metric entropy)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. Let
K\subseteq X
be a subset and
\epsilon>0
. Then, define the following.

•

N\subseteq K
is an
\epsilon
-covering net
of
K
if for any
x\in K
, there exists a
y\in N
such that
d(x,y)\leq\epsilon
.

•

The
covering number

\mathcal{N}(K,d,\epsilon)
of
K
is the smallest possible cardinality of an
\epsilon
-covering net of
K
.

•

The
metric entropy
is
\log\mathcal{N}(K,d,\epsilon)
.

A useful result about covering nets that we will need in our proofs is bounds on the metric entropy of covering nets for unitaries of bounded gate complexity
[
27
]
.

Lemma 9

(Covering nets for unitaries of bounded gates with respect to
d_{F}
; Theorem 8 and Corollary 1 in
[
27
]
)
.

Let
U^{G}\subseteq U(2^{n})
be the set of
n
-qubit unitaries that can be implemented by
G
two-qubit gates.
Then for any
\epsilon\in(0,1]
, there exist universal constants
c_{1},c_{2},C>0
such that for
1\leq G/C\leq 4^{n+1}
, the metric entropy of
U^{G}
with respect to the normalized Frobenius distance
d_{F}
can be bounded as

\frac{G}{4C}\log(\frac{c_1}{\epsilon})\leq\log\mathcal{N}(U^{G},d_{F},\epsilon)\leq 16G\log(\frac{c_2G}{\epsilon})+2G\log n.

(2.62)

Moreover, the metric entropy of
U^{G}
with respect to the average-case distance
d_{\mathrm{avg}}
can be bounded as

\frac{G}{4C}\log(\frac{c_1}{8\epsilon})-\log\left(\frac{c_{2}}{2\epsilon}\right)\leq\log\mathcal{N}(U^{G},d_{\mathrm{avg}},\epsilon)\leq 16G\log(\frac{c_2G}{\epsilon})+2G\log n.

(2.63)

3
Useful Lemmas

In this section, we compile several lemmas that we use repeatedly throughout our proofs.
Our algorithms for both agnostic unitary estimation and agnostic process tomography rely on sampling from a probability distribution to estimate it empirically.
In our agnostic unitary estimation algorithms, we will often sample from the distribution
\{|\hat{U}_{x}|^{2}\}_{x}
formed by the Pauli coefficients to estimate it empirically. On the other hand, for APT, we will sample from the distribution formed by the diagonal Fourier coefficients
\{\hat{\Phi}(x,x)\}_{x}
.
For guarantees on the estimation, we make use of the following well-known result from distribution learning theory.
We refer to Theorem 9 in
[
131
]
for a proof and follow the presentation of Lemma 16 in
[
30
]
.

Lemma 10

(Empirical estimation of a distribution; Theorem 9 in
[
131
]
)
.

Let
p=\{p(x)\}_{x}
be a probability distribution over some set
\mathcal{X}
. Let
p^{\prime}
be the empirical probability distribution obtained after sampling
N
times from
p
. Then,
N=\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\delta)/\epsilon^{2})
samples suffice to have, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

1.

|p(x)-p^{\prime}(x)|\leq\epsilon,\forall x\in\mathcal{X}
,

2.

\sqrt{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|p(x)-p^{\prime}(x)|^{2}}\leq\epsilon
.

Many of our agnostic unitary estimation algorithms require learning a subset of the Pauli coefficients of a unitary.
In the following lemma, we show how any Pauli coefficient can be estimated.
This algorithm extends the technique introduced in Lemma 24 of
[
28
]
for learning Hermitian unitary operators, where the Pauli coefficients are all guaranteed to be real.
However, in general, the Pauli coefficients may be complex.
We resolve this by using the approach from
[
28
]
to separately estimate the real and imaginary parts of each Pauli coefficient.
This extension is also proposed in Lemma 17 of
[
30
]
without proof.
For completeness, we provide a proof here.

Lemma 11

(Estimating any Pauli coefficient of a unitary; Lemma 17 in
[
30
]
)
.

Given query access to an
n
-qubit unitary
U
and a string
x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
as input, there exists an algorithm that produces an estimate
\alpha_{x}
of the Pauli coefficient
\hat{U}_{x}
using
N=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)
queries to
U
such that

|\alpha_{x}-\hat{U}_{x}|\leq\epsilon,

(3.1)

with probability at least
1-\delta
. Moreover, the algorithm runs in computational time
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)
.

Proof.

As discussed above, the algorithm estimates the real and imaginary parts of each Pauli coefficient separately by the same approach as in Lemma 24 of
[
28
]
.
To do so, we need access to controlled-
U
.
This can be implemented using query access to
U
alone by preparing
n
ancilla qubits, applying a controlled-
SWAP
between the ancilla and main register, applying
U
to the ancillas, another controlled-
SWAP
, and finally discarding the ancillas.

First, we estimate the real part.
We proceed in the same way as Lemma 24 of
[
28
]
.
We first prepare
\ket{0}\ket{\Phi^+}
, where
\ket{\Phi^+}
denotes a Bell pair.
Then, we apply a Hadamard gate to get

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{0}\ket{\Phi^+}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{1}\ket{\Phi^+}.

(3.2)

Applying controlled-
\sigma_{x}
controlled on
\ket{0}
, we have

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{0}\ket{v(\sigma_x)}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{1}\ket{\Phi^+}.

(3.3)

Next, we apply controlled-
U
as described above to obtain the state

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|0\rangle|v(\sigma_{x})\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|1\rangle|v(U)\rangle.

(3.4)

Then, we apply another Hadamard gate to the first qubit to obtain

\frac{1}{2}|0\rangle(|v(\sigma_{x})\rangle+|v(U)\rangle)+\frac{1}{2}|1\rangle(|v(\sigma_{x})\rangle-|v(U)\rangle).

(3.5)

Finally, we measure the first qubit in the computational basis, giving us outcome probabilities

\mathbf{Pr}[0]=\frac{1}{4}(\langle v(\sigma_{x})|+\langle v(U)|)(|v(\sigma_{x})\rangle+|v(U)\rangle)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{(\hat{U}_{x}+\hat{U}_{x}^{*})}{4}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\mathrm{Re}(\hat{U}_{x})}{2},

(3.6)

and similarly

\mathbf{Pr}[1]=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\mathrm{Re}(\hat{U}_{x})}{2}.

(3.7)

Using Hoeffding’s inequality, we see that by repeating this procedure
\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\delta)/\epsilon^{2})
many times, we can estimate
\mathrm{Re}(\hat{U}_{x})
up to accuracy
\epsilon/\sqrt{2}
with probability at least
1-\delta/2
.

To estimate the imaginary part, we replace
Equation

3.4
with the following

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|0\rangle|v(\sigma_{x})\rangle+\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}|1\rangle|v(U)\rangle.

We again perform a Hadamard operation followed by a measurement of the control qubit.
Using a similar analysis to the real case, we obtain measurement probabilities

\mathbf{Pr}[0]=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Im}(\hat{U}_{x})\text{ and }\mathbf{Pr}[1]=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Im}(\hat{U}_{x}).

(3.8)

Then, by repeating this procedure
\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\delta)/\epsilon^{2})
many times, we can now estimate
\mathrm{Im}(\hat{U}_{x})
up to accuracy
\epsilon/\sqrt{2}
with probability at least
1-\delta/2
. Using a union bound, we see that with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have an
\epsilon
-accurate estimate of
\hat{U}_{x}
.

As we spend
\mathcal{O}(1)
time processing each sample in both procedures, the computational time is of the same order as the sample complexity.
∎

Similarly, we can also learn any Fourier coefficient of a quantum channel.
The same result is in Lemma 15 in
[
30
]
, but we give a different proof that allows for an easier analysis of the computational time.
This is because our proof makes the state preparation and measurement steps more explicit than that of
[
30
]
.

Lemma 12

(Estimating any Fourier coefficient of a quantum channel)
.

Given query access to a quantum channel
\Phi
on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, and strings
x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
as input, there exists a method to produce an estimate
\alpha_{x,y}
of the Fourier coefficient
\hat{\Phi}(x,y)
using
N=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)
queries to
\Phi
such that

|\alpha_{x,y}-\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|\leq\epsilon

(3.9)

with probability at least
1-\delta
. Moreover, the algorithm runs in computational time
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)
.

Proof.

Recall that the Choi state of a quantum channel can be expanded in terms of its Fourier coefficients and the Choi states of the Pauli operators as

v(\Phi)=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|v(\sigma_{x})\rangle\langle v(\sigma_{y})|.

(3.10)

We first estimate the diagonal coefficients
\hat{\Phi}(x,x)
and
\hat{\Phi}(y,y)
. From
Equation

3.10
, we have

\langle v(\sigma_{x})|v(\Phi)|v(\sigma_{x})\rangle=\hat{\Phi}(x,x).

(3.11)

Then, similarly to
Lemma

11
, by conducting
\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\delta)/\epsilon^{2})
SWAP tests
[
132
,
133
,
134
]
between
v(\Phi)
and
|v(\sigma_{x})\rangle\langle v(\sigma_{x})|
, we can estimate
\hat{\Phi}(x,x)
up-to accuracy
\epsilon/(2\sqrt{2})
with probability at least
1-\delta/4
, and similarly for
\hat{\Phi}(y,y)
. Then, consider the state

|\psi(x,y)^{+}\rangle\triangleq\frac{|v(\sigma_{x})\rangle+|v(\sigma_{y})\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}.

(3.12)

Using
Equation

3.10
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\psi(x,y)^{+}|v(\Phi)|\psi(x,y)^{+}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{(\langle v(\sigma_{x})|+\langle v(\sigma_{y})|)v(\Phi)(|v(\sigma_{x})\rangle+|v(\sigma_{y})\rangle)}{2}

(3.13)

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{\Phi}(x,x)+\hat{\Phi}(y,y)+\hat{\Phi}(x,y)+\hat{\Phi}(y,x)}{2}

(3.14)

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{\Phi}(x,x)+\hat{\Phi}(y,y)+2\mathrm{Re}(\hat{\Phi}(x,y))}{2},

(3.15)

where the last line uses the fact that
\hat{\Phi}(y,x)=\hat{\Phi}(x,y)^{*}
, as
\hat{\Phi}
is Hermitian. Now, with probability at least
1-\delta/4
, we estimate the overlap between
v(\Phi)
and
|\psi(x,y)^{+}\rangle\langle\psi(x,y)^{+}|
up to accuracy
\epsilon/(2\sqrt{2})
, using
\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\delta)/\epsilon^{2})
SWAP tests. This allows us to estimate
\mathrm{Re}(\hat{\Phi}(x,y))
up to accuracy
\epsilon/\sqrt{2}
since we already estimated
\hat{\Phi}(x,x)
and
\hat{\Phi}(y,y)
from the previous step.

Similarly, to estimate
\mathrm{Im}(\hat{\Phi}(x,y))
, instead of
|\psi(x,y)^{+}\rangle
, we use the state

|\psi(x,y)^{-}\rangle\triangleq\frac{|v(\sigma_{x})\rangle-i|v(\sigma_{y})\rangle}{\sqrt{2}},

(3.16)

which satisfies

\langle\psi(x,y)^{-}|v(\Phi)|\psi(x,y)^{-}\rangle=\frac{\hat{\Phi}(x,x)+\hat{\Phi}(y,y)+2\mathrm{Im}(\hat{\Phi}(x,y))}{2}.

(3.17)

As in the case of the real component, we use
\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\delta)/\epsilon^{2})
SWAP tests to estimate this overlap, allowing us to estimate the imaginary component up to error
\epsilon/\sqrt{2}
. Thus, we obtain an estimate of
\hat{\Phi}(x,y)
up to error
\epsilon
. From a union bound, we have a total failure probability of at most
\delta
.
∎

Another tool we utilize often is the classical shadow formalism
[
111
]
, which allows for the efficient prediction of expectation values of many observables from copies of a particular state.
We use classical shadows to estimate Pauli coefficients as a subroutine in some of our learning algorithms.

Lemma 13

(Classical Shadows; Theorem 1 in
[
111
]
)
.

Given copies of a quantum state
\rho\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and a list of Hermitian observables
\{O_{1},\dots,O_{M}\}\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, satisfying
\|O_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
for all
i\in[M]
, there exists an algorithm that produce estimates
\{\hat{o}_{i}\}
such that

|\hat{o}_{i}-\tr(O_i\rho)|\leq\epsilon,\forall i\in[M]

(3.18)

with probability at least
1-\delta
.
The method employs randomized measurements on copies of
\rho
, followed by classical post-processing.
Depending on the measurement primitive, this aforementioned algorithm has the following sample and time complexity.

1.

To achieve
Equation

3.18
, it suffices to perform
random Clifford measurements
on

N=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(M/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\max_{i\in[M]}\tr(O_i^2)\right)

(3.19)

copies of the unknown quantum state, but the classical post-processing time may be inefficient in general.
When the observables have efficient representations, the algorithm is computationally efficient.
For example, when the observable is a stabilizer state, the computational time is

\mathcal{O}\left(n^{2}MN\right).

(3.20)

2.

When all observables
O_{i}
are
k
-local, to achieve
Equation

3.18
, it suffices to perform
random Pauli measurements
on

N=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{4^{k}\log(M/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)

(3.21)

copies of the unknown quantum state, and the classical post-processing can be performed in time

\mathcal{O}\left(kNM\right).

(3.22)

3.

When all observables
O_{i}
are
k
-local Paulis, to achieve
Equation

3.18
, it suffices to perform
random Pauli measurements
on

N=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{3^{k}\log(M/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)

(3.23)

copies of the state, and the classical post-processing can be performed in time

\mathcal{O}\left(kNM\right).

(3.24)

Finally, the last tool we need is used to ensure that the output of our agnostic learning algorithms is unitary or CPTP.
This is because many of our learning algorithms rely on estimating Pauli coefficients; hence these estimates may not result in an actual unitary or quantum channel.
Thus, in order to achieve
proper
learning, in which the output of our learning algorithm lies in the concept class
\mathcal{C}
that we want to learn with respect to, we need to approximate our learned operator by the nearest unitary or quantum channel.
We refer to this as “projection.”
The following lemma shows how to achieve this for unitary projection via the approach of Lemma 8 in
[
23
]
.
Note that Lemma 8 in
[
23
]
uses the same algorithm but shows that it is a unitary projection with respect to the operator norm.
We show that this also gives a unitary projection with respect to the (normalized) Frobenius distance.

Lemma 14

(Unitary projection)
.

There exists a map
\mathrm{Proj_{U}}
that takes as input any operator
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and outputs a unitary such that

d_{F}(A,\mathrm{Proj_{U}}(A))\leq d_{F}(A,B)

(3.25)

for any
2^{n}
-dimensional unitary
B
and runs in time
\mathcal{O}(2^{3n})
. In other words,
\mathrm{Proj_{U}}(A)
is the closest unitary projection of
A
within normalized Frobenius distance:

\mathrm{Proj_{U}}(A)=\argmin_{B\in\mathcal{U}_{2^{n}}}d_{F}(A,B).

(3.26)

Proof.

The projection
\mathrm{Proj_{U}}
is defined as follows.
Let
A=U\Sigma V^{\dagger}
be the singular value decomposition of
A
, where
\Sigma
is a diagonal matrix consisting of non-negative real entries and
U,V\in\mathcal{U}_{2^{n}}
.
Then,
\mathrm{Proj_{U}}
is defined as

\mathrm{Proj_{U}}(A)\triangleq UV^{\dagger}.

(3.27)

Intuitively, the projection computes the singular value decomposition of
A
and replaces all the singular values with
1
.
This technique was originally proposed in
[
23
]
, where the authors showed that this is the closest unitary projection for any operator with respect to the operator norm.
We instead show that this also gives a unitary projection for the (normalized) Frobenius distance. It suffices to show that for all
B\in\mathcal{U}_{d}

\|A-B\|_{F}^{2}\geq\|A-UV^{\dagger}\|_{F}^{2},

(3.28)

or equivalently, by the unitary invariance of the Frobenius norm,

\|\Sigma-U^{\dagger}BV\|_{F}^{2}\geq\|\Sigma-I\|_{F}^{2}.

(3.29)

Denote
W\triangleq U^{\dagger}BV
. Now,

\displaystyle\|\Sigma-W\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\tr\left((\Sigma-W^{\dagger})(\Sigma-W)\right)

(3.30)

\displaystyle=\tr(\Sigma^2 - W^\dag\Sigma-\Sigma W + I)

(3.31)

where the first equality uses the fact that
\Sigma
is diagonal and non-negative, while the second line uses the fact that
W
is unitary.

Let
\{\Sigma_{ii}\}_{i\in[2^{n}]}
denote the diagonal elements of
\Sigma
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\tr(\Sigma W + W^\dag\Sigma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[2^{n}]}\langle i|(\Sigma W+W^{\dagger}\Sigma)|i\rangle

(3.32)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[2^{n}]}\Sigma_{ii}\langle i|(W+W^{\dagger})|i\rangle

(3.33)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[2^{n}]}2\Sigma_{ii}\cdot\mathrm{Re}(\langle i|W|i\rangle)

(3.34)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in[2^{n}]}2\Sigma_{ii}

(3.35)

\displaystyle=2\cdot\tr(\Sigma).

(3.36)

Here, in the second line, we use that
\Sigma
is diagonal and so commutes with
W,W^{\dagger}
.
In the inequality, we use that
W
is unitary.
Finally, we get

\|\Sigma-W\|_{F}^{2}\geq\tr(\Sigma^2-2\Sigma+I)=\|\Sigma-I\|_{F}^{2},

(3.37)

concluding the proof.
The runtime can be seen as the runtime of any algorithm computing the SVD and multiplying
U,V^{\dagger}
.
We refer to Lecture 31 of
[
135
]
for the analysis of algorithms based on the Golub-Kahan bidiagonalization procedure
[
136
]
.
Namely, computing the SVD takes time
\mathcal{O}(m^{3})
for an
m\times m
matrix.
While such algorithms are only approximate, they demonstrate superlinear convergence, allowing one to efficiently obtain an output within machine precision and thus ignore this error in practice.
∎

Showing such a result for CPTP projection is significantly more difficult, and we discuss it in detail in
Section

5.7
.

4
Agnostic Unitary Estimation (AUE)

In this section, we consider the task of agnostic unitary estimation (AUE).
Recall that this is a special case of agnostic process tomography (APT) in which the unknown channel is assumed to be unitary and the concept class
\mathcal{C}
consists entirely of unitaries.
This section serves as a warm-up to
Section

5
, as we will generalize many of the techniques presented here to APT later.

4.1
Bounded-gate circuits

In this section, we consider AUE when the concept class
\mathcal{C}
consists of unitaries with a bounded gate complexity.
In particular, we consider
n
-qubit unitaries generated by
G
two-qubit gates.
This class of unitaries was studied in the realizable case in
[
27
]
.

First, we prove a more general result about AUE for any class
\mathcal{C}
of unitaries with finite size
|\mathcal{C}|<+\infty
. In the following proposition, we show that AUE with respect to the average-case distance metric
\mathrm{d_{avg}}
and the class
\mathcal{C}
can be accomplished using a sample complexity scaling as
\log|\mathcal{C}|
.
Thus, if
\mathcal{C}
is any class of unitaries generated by
\mathrm{poly}(n)
gates,
\log|\mathcal{C}|\leq\mathrm{poly}(n)
so that the sample complexity is efficient.
Qualitatively, this says that efficient AUE is easy from a sample complexity perspective, but it can be difficult to achieve with respect to time complexity.
This is similar in spirit to an analogous argument by
[
42
]
for agnostic state tomography.
Their argument relies on shadow tomography
[
94
]
, while ours is based on classical shadows and takes inspiration from a proof of
[
27
]
.

Proposition 1

(Agnostic unitary estimation of finite-sized classes)
.

There exists a learning algorithm for proper
(1,\epsilon,\delta)
-agnostic unitary estimation with respect to error in average-case distance
\mathrm{d_{avg}}
for any finite-sized class
\mathcal{C}
of unitaries using

N=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(|\mathcal{C}|/\delta)}{\epsilon^{4}}\right)

(4.1)

queries to the unknown unitary
U
and running in time

\mathcal{O}\left(T\cdot\frac{|\mathcal{C}|\log(|\mathcal{C}|/\delta)}{\epsilon^{4}}\right),

(4.2)

where
T
is the time complexity of computing the inner product of two states given their exact description.

We note that the time complexity in the proposition depends on the complexity of computing the inner products of pairs of states.
This is due to the use of Clifford classical shadows
[
111
]
, which is not computationally efficient for arbitrary observables (see
Lemma

13
).
The resulting complexity depends on the class of unitaries one aims to learn and how efficiently their output states can be represented. However, since the time complexity already scales linearly with the size of the concept class, this algorithm would be computationally inefficient for most classes of interest, so we do not analyze the time complexity in detail.

Proof.

The algorithm is the same as that of Section C.2(a) of
[
27
]
.
Namely, we use Clifford classical shadows
[
111
]
to estimate the distance
\mathrm{d_{avg}}(U_{i},U)
between the unknown unitary
U
and all unitaries
U_{i}\in\mathcal{C}
.
Then, our algorithm outputs the one with the minimal distance.

The analysis is the same as Section C.2(a) of
[
27
]
, so we do not reproduce the full argument here. We start by preparing random input states from the ensemble

P=\mathrm{Uniform}(\{|\psi\rangle=U|0\rangle,U\in\mathrm{Cl}(n)\})

(4.3)

of random stabilizer states, where
\mathrm{Cl}(n)
denotes the
n
-qubit Clifford group. Then, we use classical shadows
[
111
]
to estimate distances with respect to this ensemble.

While
[
27
]
used random stabilizer product states as input, we use random stabilizer states instead. We note that changing the input distribution only changes the ensemble over which the distance is estimated, not the sample complexity for successful estimation. Now, by Equation (C.8) of
[
27
]
, using

N=204\frac{\log(2|\mathcal{C}|/\delta)}{\epsilon^{\prime 2}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(|\mathcal{C}|/\delta)}{\epsilon^{\prime 2}}\right)

(4.4)

queries to the unknown unitary, we see that we can construct unbiased estimators
\hat{o}_{i}
, such that
1-\hat{o}_{i}
estimates the square of the average distance with respect to
P
(
Definition

12
).

|d_{P}(U_{i},U)^{2}-(1-\hat{o}_{i})|\leq\epsilon^{\prime},\quad\forall i\in[|\mathcal{C}|]

(4.5)

with probability at least
1-\delta
for some
\delta,\epsilon^{\prime}>0
.
Then, we can select
i^{\star}=\arg\min_{i}(1-\hat{o}_{i})
and output
U_{i^{\star}}
, i.e., the
U_{i}\in\mathcal{C}
with the smallest estimated distance from
U
with respect to
d_{P}
.
To relate this back to
\mathrm{d_{avg}}
, recall that
P
forms a
3
-design
[
137
,
138
,
139
]
, implying that the average infidelity over
P
is the same as that over the Haar measure. Thus for any
2^{n}
-dimensional unitaries
U_{1},U_{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d_{avg}}(U_{1},U_{2})^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\mu_{S}}[\mathrm{d_{tr}}(U_{1}|\psi\rangle,U_{2}|\psi\rangle)^{2}]

(4.6)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\mu_{S}}[1-F(U_{1}|\psi\rangle,U_{2}|\psi\rangle)]

(4.7)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim P}[1-F(U_{1}|\psi\rangle,U_{2}|\psi\rangle)]

(4.8)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim P}[\mathrm{d_{tr}}(U_{1}|\psi\rangle,U_{2}|\psi\rangle)^{2}]

(4.9)

\displaystyle=d_{P}(U_{1},U_{2})^{2},

(4.10)

where in the second and fourth lines we use
Definition

11
, and in the third line we use that
P
is a
3
-design.
We can now bound the error of our hypothesis with respect to
\mathrm{d_{avg}}
.

\displaystyle\mathrm{d_{avg}}(U_{i^{\star}},U)^{2}

\displaystyle=d_{P}(U_{i^{\star}},U)^{2}

(4.11)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\hat{o}_{i^{\star}})+\epsilon^{\prime}

(4.12)

\displaystyle=\min_{i}(1-\hat{o}_{i})+\epsilon^{\prime}

(4.13)

\displaystyle\leq\min_{i}(d_{P}(U_{i},U)^{2}+\epsilon^{\prime})+\epsilon^{\prime}

(4.14)

\displaystyle\leq\min_{i}(d_{P}(U_{i},U)^{2})+2\epsilon^{\prime}

(4.15)

\displaystyle=\min_{i}(\mathrm{d_{avg}}(U_{i},U)^{2})+2\epsilon^{\prime}

(4.16)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{opt}(U,\mathcal{C})^{2}+2\epsilon^{\prime}.

(4.17)

In the first line, we use
Equation

4.10
.
In the second line, we use
Equation

4.5
.
In the third line, we use the definition of
i^{\star}
.
In the fourth line, we use
Equation

4.5
again.
In the second to last line, we use
Equation

4.10
again. Finally, in the last line, we use the definition of
\mathsf{opt}
.
Thus, by setting
\epsilon^{\prime}=\epsilon^{2}/2
, we obtain

\mathrm{d_{avg}}(U_{i^{\star}},U)\leq\mathsf{opt}(U,\mathcal{C})+\epsilon.

(4.18)

This gives us a 1-agnostic learner with the claimed sample complexity. The time complexity is due to the computation of the
\hat{o}_{i}
for all elements of
\mathcal{C}
, each of which requires computing inner products between
\mathcal{O}(N)
pairs of states.
∎

While Proposition
1
only holds for concept classes of finite size, we now show that this can be easily extended to any class as long as it has bounded metric entropy (see
Definition

13
).

Corollary 1
.

Let
\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime},\delta>0
.
There exists a learning algorithm for proper
(1,\epsilon+\epsilon^{\prime},\delta)
-agnostic unitary estimation with respect to error in average-case distance
\mathrm{d_{avg}}
for any class
\mathcal{C}
with covering number
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{C},\mathrm{d_{avg}},\epsilon^{\prime})
, using

N=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{C},\davg,\epsilon^\prime)/\delta)}{\epsilon^{4}}\right)

(4.19)

queries to the unknown unitary
U
and running in time

\mathcal{O}\left(T\cdot\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{C},\mathrm{d_{avg}},\epsilon^{\prime})\cdot N\right),

(4.20)

where
T
is the time complexity of computing the inner product of two states given their exact description.

Proof.

Because
\mathcal{C}
has covering number
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{C},\mathrm{d_{avg}},\epsilon^{\prime})
, there exists some
\epsilon^{\prime}
-covering net
M
with cardinality
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{C},\mathrm{d_{avg}},\epsilon^{\prime})
.
Consider applying Proposition
1
to the
\epsilon^{\prime}
-cover
M
.
This gives us a hypothesis
V\in M
satisfying

\mathrm{d_{avg}}(U,V)\leq\min_{W\in M}\mathrm{d_{avg}}(U,W)+\epsilon.

(4.21)

Moreover, to find this hypothesis, we used

N=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(|M|/\delta)}{\epsilon^{4}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{C},\davg,\epsilon^\prime)/\delta)}{\epsilon^{4}}\right)

(4.22)

queries to the unknown unitary. The time complexity is also
\mathcal{O}(T\cdot\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{C},\mathrm{d_{avg}},\epsilon^{\prime})\cdot N)
by Proposition
3
.

Let
W^{*}
be the optimal hypothesis from
\mathcal{C}
, i.e.,

W^{*}\triangleq\argmin_{W\in\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{d_{avg}}(U,W)\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathsf{opt}(U,\mathcal{C})=\mathrm{d_{avg}}(U,W^{*}).

(4.23)

Then,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d_{avg}}(U,V)

\displaystyle\leq\min_{W\in M}\mathrm{d_{avg}}(U,W)+\epsilon

(4.24)

\displaystyle\leq\min_{W\in M}\left(\mathrm{d_{avg}}(U,W^{*})+\mathrm{d_{avg}}(W^{*},W)\right)+\epsilon

(4.25)

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{opt}(U,\mathcal{C})+\min_{W\in M}\mathrm{d_{avg}}(W^{*},W)+\epsilon

(4.26)

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{opt}(U,\mathcal{C})+\epsilon^{\prime}+\epsilon,

(4.27)

where the first inequality follows from the guarantees of Proposition
1
, the second inequality follows from the triangle inequality for
\mathrm{d_{avg}}
, the third inequality follows by the definition of
W^{*}
, and the fourth inequality uses that
M
is an
\epsilon^{\prime}
-covering net for
\mathcal{C}
.
∎

Finally, we can obtain a sample complexity upper bound for AUE of unitaries with bounded gate complexity.
This follows immediately from
Lemma

9
, which gives a bound on the metric entropy of unitaries with bounded gate complexity.
However, by
Corollary

1
, the time complexity scales with the cardinality of the covering net, which is exponentially large (
Lemma

).
This is not surprising, as even in the realizable case, there are lower bounds on the computational complexity of learning this class of unitaries
[
27
]
based on the hardness of
RingLWE

[

]
.

Corollary 2

(Agnostic unitary estimation of bounded-gate unitaries)
.

There exists a (computationally inefficient) learning algorithm for proper
(1,\epsilon,\delta)
-agnostic unitary estimation of the class of
n
-qubit unitaries of
G
2-qubit gates with respect to error in
\mathrm{d_{avg}}
using

N=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{G\log( n/\delta)}{\epsilon^{4}}\right)

(4.28)

queries.

4.2
AUE from agnostic state tomography

In this section, we prove a general result which shows that we can obtain an algorithm for improper AUE from existing agnostic state tomography algorithms with only a quadratic increase in sample/time complexity with respect to the error
\epsilon
.
This is a fairly general result that holds whenever the concept classes for our desired AUE algorithm and the agnostic state tomography are compatible, in the sense of Proposition
2
.
To properly state this proposition, we first give the definition of agnostic state tomography.

Definition 14

(Agnostic state tomography
[
42
]
)
.

Let
0<\epsilon,\delta<1
. Let
\mathcal{D}
be a class of quantum states. A learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is an
(1,\epsilon,\delta)
-agnostic learner
with respect to fidelity and the concept class
\mathcal{D}
if, given access to copies of an arbitrary quantum state
\rho
,
\mathcal{A}
outputs a hypothesis state
\rho^{\prime}
such that

F(\rho^{\prime},\rho)\geq\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}}F(\sigma,\rho)-\epsilon

(4.29)

with probability at least
1-\delta
, where
F
denotes the fidelity. When the hypothesis
\rho^{\prime}
is from the concept class
\mathcal{D}
, we say that
\mathcal{A}
is
proper
. Otherwise, we say that it is
improper
.

Algorithms for agnostic state tomography with respect to fidelity are known for a variety of classes, e.g., stabilizer product states
[
42
]
, stabilizer states, states with high stabilizer dimension, and discrete product states
[
43
]
.

In the following proposition, we show how to convert any agnostic state tomography algorithm to an improper agnostic unitary estimation algorithm with effectively the same runtime, as long as the concept classes are compatible.
This is satisfied for the corresponding unitary classes of all previously mentioned agnostic state tomography algorithms.
Moreover, we note that since our algorithm is improper, the underlying agnostic state tomography algorithm does not have to be proper.
However, we expect this to be helpful for obtaining proper learners, which we leave as an open question.

Proposition 2

(Improper AUE from agnostic state tomography)
.

Suppose there exists a learning algorithm for (proper)
(1,\epsilon^{2},\delta)
-agnostic state tomography with respect to fidelity
F
for some class
\mathcal{D}
of pure states.
Let
\mathcal{C}
be a concept class of unitaries.
If
\ket{v(U)}\in\mathcal{D}
for all
U\in\mathcal{C}
, then there exists a learning algorithm for improper
(1,\epsilon,\delta)
-agnostic unitary estimation with respect to error in normalized Frobenius distance
d_{F}
for the class
\mathcal{C}
.
Moreover, this requires the same sample and time complexity as the
(1,\epsilon^{2},\delta)
-agnostic state tomography algorithm.

We note that our result holds for agnostically learning the unitary channel associated to the unknown unitary.
Namely, if
U
is our unknown unitary, then our algorithm agnostically learns the channel
U(\cdot)U^{\dagger}
with respect to
d_{F}
(the normalized Frobenius distance for superoperators from
Definition

10
).
We note that this does not take into account any phase differences and so is slightly weaker than learning the unitary
U
itself with respect to
d_{F}
(the normalized Frobenius distance for operators from
Definition

9
).
This is because our algorithm simply applies agnostic state tomography to the Choi state of the unknown process, inspired by

Moreover, our analysis can be seen as extending that of

to the agnostic setting with respect to the normalized Frobenius distance

\overline{F}(\Phi,\Psi)\triangleq 1-F(v(\Phi),v(\Psi)),

(4.30)

where we adopt the (non-standard) notation of

We remark that we can also trivially obtain

-agnostic unitary estimation with respect to the entanglement infidelity from

-agnostic state tomography with respect to fidelity (notably, without the quadratic blowup with respect to

Entanglement infidelity has been considered widely in (realizable) unitary estimation

However, we focus on the normalized Frobenius distance

Proof.

Let
U
denote the unknown unitary.
The algorithm is simple: first, prepare the Choi state
\ket{v(U)}
of
U
and then apply the
(1,\epsilon^{2},\delta)
-agnostic state tomography algorithm to
\ket{v(U)}
.
The output of our algorithm is the density matrix of the pure state output by the state tomography algorithm.

The agnostic state tomography algorithm outputs some state
\ket{\psi}
such that

F(v(U),\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})\geq\max_{\ket{\phi}\in\mathcal{D}}F(v(U),\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi})-\epsilon^{2},

(4.31)

where we use
v(U)\triangleq\outerproduct{v(U)}{v(U)}
to denote the density matrix corresponding to the pure Choi state.
Note that this is consistent with the Choi state defined by the channel
\mathcal{E}(\rho)=U\rho U^{\dagger}
.
We can equivalently write the above guarantee in terms of the infidelity

1-\left|\innerproduct{v(U)}{\psi}\right|^{2}\leq\min_{\ket{\phi}\in\mathcal{D}}\left(1-\left|\innerproduct{v(U)}{\phi}\right|^{2}\right)+\epsilon^{2}.

(4.32)

We can convert this into a bound with respect to the normalized Frobenius distance
d_{F}
.
Note that

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}-\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}=2\left(1-\left|\innerproduct{\psi}{\phi}\right|^{2}\right)

(4.33)

for any pure states
\ket{\psi},\ket{\phi}
.
Moreover,

\frac{1}{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}-\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}=4^{n}d_{F}(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi},\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi})^{2}

(4.34)

by definition of
d_{F}
(
Definition

9
), where
\ket{\phi},\ket{\psi}
are each on
2n

Then, the above guarantee can be written as

4^{n}d_{F}(v(U),\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})^{2}\leq 4^{n}\min_{\ket{\phi}\in\mathcal{D}}d_{F}(v(U),\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi})^{2}+\epsilon^{2}.

(4.35)

Taking the square root of both sides, we obtain

2^{n}d_{F}(v(U),\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})\leq 2^{n}\min_{\ket{\phi}\in\mathcal{D}}d_{F}(v(U),\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi})+\epsilon.

(4.36)

Moreover, because
\ket{v(V)}\in\mathcal{D}
for all
V\in\mathcal{C}
, we can upper bound this minimum by the minimum over all
V
in
\mathcal{C}
instead.
Thus, we have

2^{n}d_{F}(v(U),\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})\leq 2^{n}\min_{V\in\mathcal{C}}d_{F}(v(U),v(V))+\epsilon.

(4.37)

Now, we want to argue that agnostically learning the Choi state
v(U)
of a unitary
U
with respect to
2^{n}d_{F}
is equivalent to agnostically learning the unitary channel
U(\cdot)U^{\dagger}
with respect to
d_{F}
.
Note that the first
d_{F}
is the normalized Frobenius norm for operators (
Definition

9
) while the second
d_{F}
is the normalized Frobenius norm for superoperators (
Definition

10
).
Then, notice

\displaystyle d_{F}(\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}\sqrt{2}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathcal{U}_{1}-\mathcal{U}_{2}\right\rVert}_{F}

(4.38)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}\sqrt{2}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert J(U_{1})-J(U_{2})\right\rVert}_{F}

(4.39)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert v(U_{1})-v(U_{2})\right\rVert}_{F}

(4.40)

\displaystyle=2^{n}d_{F}(v(U_{1}),v(U_{2})).

(4.41)

Here, we use
\mathcal{U}
to denote the unitary channel
U(\cdot)U^{\dagger}
.
In the first and second lines, we use the definition of
d_{F}
for superoperators (
Definition

10
).
In the third line, we use that the Choi representation is simply the unnormalized Choi state.
Here, recall that
v(U)=\outerproduct{v(U)}{v(U)}
, so the normalization factor is
2^{n}
for this density matrix.
Finally, in the last line, we use the definition of the normalized Frobenius distance for operators (
Definition

9
).
Thus, this tells us that, indeed, agnostically learning the Choi state of a unitary with respect to

is the same as learning the corresponding unitary channel with respect to
d_{F}
.
Hence,

(1,\epsilon,\delta)
-AUE with respect to the class
\mathcal{C}

1-Agnostic Learning Pauli Strings

1:

Theorem 1

(Agnostic Unitary Estimation of Pauli strings in

There exists a learning algorithm for proper
(1,\epsilon,\delta)
-agnostic unitary estimation of the class of
n
-qubit Pauli strings
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{P}_{n}}
with respect to error in
\mathrm{d_{avg}}

N=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{4}}\right)

(4.50)

Proof.

The algorithm is detailed in
Algorithm

1
.
First, we prepare
N
copies of the Choi state
|v(U)\rangle
(
Definition

2
).
Then, we measure each one in the
\{|v(\sigma_{x})\rangle\}_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}

\sigma_{z}
associated with the most frequent outcome
z
.
Using the fact that

|v(U)\rangle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\hat{U}_{x}|v(\sigma_{x})\rangle,

(4.51)

each measurement returns a string
x
with probability

p(x)\triangleq|\hat{U}_{x}|^{2}.

(4.52)

Denote the true most likely Pauli string by

x^{\star}\triangleq\arg\max_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}p(x).

(4.53)

We compute the optimal error for the class of Pauli strings. Recall from
Lemma

8

\mathrm{d_{avg}}(U,V)^{2}=\frac{2^{n}}{2^{n}+1}\left(1-\left|\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\hat{U}^{*}_{x}\hat{V}_{x}\right|^{2}\right).

(4.54)

Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{opt}(U,\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{P}_{n}})^{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{d_{avg}}(U,\sigma_{x})^{2}

(4.55)

\displaystyle=\min_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\frac{2^{n}}{2^{n}+1}(1-|\hat{U}_{x}|^{2})

(4.56)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n}}{2^{n}+1}\left(1-\max_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|\hat{U}_{x}|^{2}\right).

(4.57)

Hence, we see that the hypothesis
\sigma_{x^{\star}}
achieves the optimal error for the class
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{P}_{n}}
.
Our algorithm aims to estimate the true most likely Pauli string

by the one most frequently occurring in our measurements.
Let
\{\hat{p}(x)\}_{x}
be the empirical distribution obtained from our measurement outcomes, which estimates

.
Then, denote the most frequent outcome out of our measurements by

z\triangleq\argmax_{x\in\{x_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}}\hat{p}(x).

(4.58)

To bound the error, consider the empirical distribution
\{\hat{p}(x)\}_{x}
. Using
Lemma

10
, we can construct estimates
\hat{p}(x)
of
|\hat{U}_{x}|^{2}
such that with probability at least

\left|\hat{p}(x)-|\hat{U}_{x}|^{2}\right|\leq\epsilon_{1},\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}

(4.59)

for some

using
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon_{1}^{2}}\right)
copies of the Choi state
|v(U)\rangle
. Now, since

is the most frequent string observed,

\hat{p}(z)\geq\hat{p}(x^{\star}).

(4.60)

Conditioned on obtaining estimates
\hat{p}(x)
that are
\epsilon_{1}

|\hat{U}_{x}|^{2}
for all

, we have

|\hat{U}_{z}|^{2}+\epsilon_{1}\geq\hat{p}(z)\quad\text{ and }\quad\hat{p}(x^{\star})\geq|\hat{U}_{x^{\star}}|^{2}-\epsilon_{1}.

(4.61)

Thus, we have

|\hat{U}_{x^{\star}}|^{2}-|\hat{U}_{z}|^{2}\leq 2\epsilon_{1}.

(4.62)

Now, we can bound the error of the hypothesis as follows

\displaystyle\mathrm{d_{avg}}(U,\sigma_{z})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n}}{2^{n}+1}(1-|\hat{U}_{z}|^{2})

(4.63)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n}}{2^{n}+1}(1-|\hat{U}_{x^{\star}}|^{2}+|\hat{U}_{x^{\star}}|^{2}-|\hat{U}_{z}|^{2})

(4.64)

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{opt}(U,\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{P}_{n}})^{2}+\frac{2^{n}}{2^{n}+1}\cdot 2\epsilon_{1},

(4.65)

where the first line is true by

and

Thus, by setting

\mathrm{d_{avg}}(U,\sigma_{z})^{2}\leq\mathsf{opt}(U,\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{P}_{n}})^{2}+\epsilon^{2}\leq(\mathsf{opt}(U,\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{P}_{n}})+\epsilon)^{2},

(4.66)

and the desired bound can be obtained by taking the square root.
Now, by our choice of
\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon^{2}/2

N=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon_{1}^{2}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{4}}\right)

(4.67)

samples of the Choi state or queries to the unitary
U

(

A unitary

Algorithm 2

2-Agnostic Proper Learning Unitary k-Juntas

1:

Prepare
N_{1}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k\log(n/\delta)}{\epsilon^{4}}\right)

|v(U)\rangle
and use them to compute estimates of weights of all subsets of

with cardinality

2:

Select
S

3:

Using
N_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k16^{k}\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)
queries to

of the Pauli coefficients for

The algorithm is reminiscent of other junta learning algorithms

Namely, we first identify the qubits

In contrast to
[

Example 1
.

Consider the unitary
U=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}X\otimes Z\otimes I+\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}Z\otimes Z\otimes Z
. One can show that the subset of size 2 with the most weight is formed by the first 2 qubits,
\{1,2\}
, and has weight
w_{\{1,2\}}(U)=1/3
. However, the most influential subset of size 2 is formed by the last two qubits,
{

}
with influence 1. Note that the corresponding weight

As we will show in our proof of

Moreover, rather than using a tomography algorithm in the second part, which would make proper learning more difficult, we directly estimate the Pauli coefficients corresponding to
\mathcal{P}_{S}

to denote

\mathcal{P}_{S}=\{x\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathsf{supp}(x)\subseteq S\}

for a subset

The resulting learned operator

agnostic unitary estimation, we need an additional projection which approximates

by a unitary.
For this projection, we use

Lemma 15

(Estimating weights of a unitary on many subsets)
.

Given query access to a unitary
U
and
M

\{S_{j}\}_{j\in[M]},S_{j}\subseteq[n]
, there exists a method to produce estimates
\hat{w}_{S_{j}}

N=\mathcal{O}(\log(M/\delta)/\epsilon^{2})

|\hat{w}_{S_{j}}-w_{S_{j}}(U)|\leq\epsilon\quad\forall j\in[M]

(4.71)

with probability at least
1-\delta
. Moreover, the algorithm runs in computational time
\mathcal{O}(NM)
.

Proof.

Using
N
queries to the unitary
U
, the algorithm first prepares
N
copies of the Choi state
|v(U)\rangle
and measures each copy in the basis
\{|v(\sigma_{x})\rangle\}_{x}
.
Suppose the outcomes are
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}

Then, for each subset

\hat{w}_{S}\triangleq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}b_{S,i},\quad b_{S,i}\triangleq\mathbf{1}[x_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{S}].

(4.72)

Here,

indicates whether

is supported entirely in

, i.e.,
\mathsf{supp}(x_{i})\subseteq S
.
We claim that
\hat{w}_{S}
has the desired properties given our choice of

Clearly, since the measurements are independent, then

are i.i.d. random variables so that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\hat{w}_{S}]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}[b_{S,i}]

(4.73)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Pr}(x_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{S})

(4.74)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{S}}|\hat{U}_{x}|^{2}

(4.75)

\displaystyle=w_{S}(U).

(4.76)

Here, the third line follows because measuring
\ket{v(U)}
in the basis

gives outcome

.
Thus, using

N=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(M/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)

(4.78)

with probability at least

.
For each subset, we use

.

\displaystyle\mathsf{opt}(U,\mathcal{J}_{n,k})^{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{V\in\mathcal{J}_{n,k}}d_{F}(U,V)^{2}

(4.79)

\displaystyle=\min_{V\in\mathcal{J}_{n,k}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|\hat{U}_{x}-\hat{V}_{x}|^{2}

(4.80)

\displaystyle=1-\max_{V\in\mathcal{J}_{n,k}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{Re}(\hat{U}_{x}^{*}\hat{V}_{x})

\displaystyle\geq 1-\max_{V\in\mathcal{J}_{n,k}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|\hat{U}_{x}||\hat{V}_{x}|

(4.82)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\max_{S\subseteq[n],|S|=k}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathcal{J}_{n,k}\\
w_{S}(V)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{S}}|\hat{U}_{x}||\hat{V}_{x}|

(4.83)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\max_{S\subseteq[n],|S|=k}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathcal{J}_{n,k}\\
w_{S}(V)=1\end{subarray}}\sqrt{w_{S}(U)}\sqrt{w_{S}(V)}

(4.84)

\displaystyle=1-\max_{S\subseteq[n],|S|=k}\sqrt{w_{S}(U)},

(4.85)

where the second line follows from Lemma

Denote the heaviest cardinality-

S^{\star}\triangleq\argmax_{S\subseteq[n],|S|=k}w_{S}(U).

\mathsf{opt}(U,\mathcal{J}_{n,k})^{2}\geq 1-\sqrt{w_{S^{\star}}(U)}.

(4.87)

Hence, in order to learn the optimal

with failure probability at most

U

We now focus on the subset

In the next step of

2

Pauli coefficients for all Pauli strings supported on

4^{k}

U

This simply aims to approximate
U
by approximating the Pauli coefficients of

As

Denote

(4.95)

which is the operator obtained by truncating the Pauli coefficients of
U
not supported on
S
. Now, we have

\displaystyle d_{F}(U,V_{2})

\displaystyle\leq d_{F}(U,V_{1})+d_{F}(V_{1},V_{2})

\displaystyle\leq 2d_{F}(U,V_{1})

(4.97)

(4.98)

where the first and last lines follow from the triangle inequality for

, and the second line follows from Lemma

For our final bound, we use the following claims on these distances that will be proven later.
The claims are conditioned on the estimations for the weights and the Pauli coefficients succeeding. A union bound tells us that the total failure probability is at most
\delta/2+\delta/2=\delta
.

Claim 1
.

d_{F}(U,\tilde{U}_{S^{\prime}})\leq\mathsf{opt}(U,\mathcal{J}_{n,k})+\sqrt{\epsilon_{1}}.

(4.99)

Claim 2
.

d_{F}(\tilde{U}_{S^{\prime}},V_{1})\leq\frac{2^{k}\epsilon_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}.

(4.100)

Using these claims in the above inequality, we have

(4.101)

Then, by setting

(4.102)

which is the desired result.
The stated sample complexity can be obtained by plugging in the values of

to the previous expressions for estimating weights and Pauli coefficients:

N=N_{1}+N_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k\log(n/\delta)}{\epsilon_{1}^{2}}+\frac{4^{k}\log(4^k/\delta)}{\epsilon_{2}^{2}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k\log(n/\delta)}{\epsilon^{4}}+\frac{k16^{k}\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\right).

(4.103)

The runtime is dominated by the procedures for finding the heaviest subset and for estimating the
4^{k}
Pauli coefficients:

\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n^{k}k\log(n/\delta)}{\epsilon_{1}^{2}}+\frac{4^{k}\log(4^k/\delta)}{\epsilon_{2}^{2}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n^{k}k\log(n/\delta)}{\epsilon^{4}}+\frac{k16^{k}\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\right).

(4.104)

As the first time dominates the time complexity, we obtain the stated complexity. It remains to prove the two claims above.
∎

Remark 1

(Improper 1-agnostic learner)
.

In the setting of improper agnostic learning, we do not need our final hypothesis to be unitary and can simply output

. This would give an improper
(1,\epsilon,\delta)
-agnostic learning algorithm with the same sample and time complexity.

4.5
Low-degree unitaries

Now, we will present two algorithms for agnostic unitary estimation of the class of low-degree unitaries. A low-degree unitary is one whose high-order Pauli coefficients are 0. More formally, we have the following definition.

Definition 16

(Degree-
d
unitary)
.

A unitary
U
has
degree

d
if

w^{>d}(U)=0,

(4.119)

where
w^{>d}
is defined in
Definition

5
.
In other words, the degree of a unitary
U
is the minimum integer
d
such that if
|x|>d
, then
\hat{U}_{x}=0
.

We use
\mathcal{L}_{n,d}
to denote the class of
n
-qubit unitaries of degree
d
. We note that there are
{n\choose d}3^{d}\leq n^{d}3^{d}
Pauli strings of degree exactly
d
, and thus
\mathcal{O}(n^{d}3^{d})
strings of degree at most
d
.

Our first algorithm given in
Algorithm

3
is inspired by the algorithm of
[
22
]
.
It involves estimating every low-degree Pauli coefficient, resulting in a query complexity of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2d})
.
Our second algorithm given in
Algorithm

4
is the low-degree learning algorithm from
[
30
]
, which uses the non-commutative Bohnenblust-Hille (BH) inequality of
[
116
]
to obtain a sample complexity independent of
n
. However, in the agnostic setting,
Algorithm

4
does not achieve a sample complexity independent of
n
. From the guarantees we show in
Theorem

4
,
Algorithm

4
is only efficient when the optimal error is at most inverse-exponential in the system size. In particular, when the unknown unitary is a member of the concept class, we recover the complexity of the realizable setting. In most other regimes for
\mathsf{opt}
, it is better to use
Algorithm

3
.
We discuss
Algorithm

4
to illustrate that straightforwardly modifying algorithms from the realizable setting does not always give good agnostic learning algorithms.

Note that unlike in the case of unitary juntas, our method for unitary projection is computationally inefficient when acting on arbitrary low-degree operators, as such operators still have an exponential dimension.
As a result, both our algorithms are
improper
learners.
We provide our algorithms and their corresponding guarantees below.

Algorithm 3

1-agnostic improper learning low-degree unitaries by learning all coefficients

1:

Using
N
queries to
U
, construct estimates
\alpha_{x}
of the Pauli coefficients for all
x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
s.t.

.

2:

return

V_{1}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\alpha_{x}\sigma_{x}
.

(AUE of low-degree unitaries by learning all low-degree coefficients)
.

Algorithm

3
performs improper
(1,\epsilon,\delta)
-agnostic unitary estimation of the class of
n
-qubit unitaries of degree
d

\mathcal{L}_{n,d}
with respect to error in

using

N=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d\cdot 9^{d}n^{2d}\log(n/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)

(4.120)

queries to the unknown unitary
U
and running in time

\mathcal{O}(N).

(4.121)

Algorithm 4

Set
c
as in

4.169
.

2:

Prepare

Algorithm

(4.122)

(4.123)

In order to prove Theorems
3
and
4
, we will use the following lemma on the optimal error of the class of low-degree unitaries.

Lemma 16

(Optimal error for low-degree unitaries)
.

The optimal error in
d_{F}
of the class of low-degree unitaries
\mathcal{L}_{n,d}
is lower-bounded by

\mathsf{opt}(U,\mathcal{L}_{n,d})\geq\sqrt{1-\sqrt{w^{\leq d}(U)}}.

(4.124)

Proof.

From the definition of
\mathsf{opt}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{opt}(U,\mathcal{L}_{n,d})^{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{V\in\mathcal{L}_{n,d}}d_{F}^{2}(U,V)

(4.125)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\min_{V\in\mathcal{L}_{n,d}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|\hat{U}_{x}-\hat{V}_{x}|^{2}

(4.126)

\displaystyle=1-\max_{V\in\mathcal{L}_{n,d}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{Re}(\hat{U}_{x}\hat{V}_{x}^{*})

(4.127)

\displaystyle=1-\max_{V\in\mathcal{L}_{n,d}}\sum_{|x|\leq d}\mathrm{Re}(\hat{U}_{x}\hat{V}_{x}^{*})

(4.128)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\max_{V\in\mathcal{L}_{n,d}}\sum_{|x|\leq d}|\hat{U}_{x}\hat{V}_{x}^{*}|

(4.129)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\max_{V\in\mathcal{L}_{n,d}}\sqrt{\sum_{|x|\leq d}|\hat{U}_{x}|^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{|x|\leq d}|\hat{V}_{x}|^{2}}

(4.130)

\displaystyle=1-\max_{V\in\mathcal{L}_{n,d}}\sqrt{w^{\leq d}(U)}\sqrt{w^{\leq d}(V)}

(4.131)

\displaystyle=1-\sqrt{w^{\leq d}(U)},

(4.132)

where the second line follows from Lemma
5
, the fourth line uses that
V
has degree at most
d
, the first inequality uses the fact that the real component of a complex number is smaller than its absolute value, the second inequality uses Cauchy-Schwarz, and the final equality follows from the fact that
V
is a degree-
d
unitary.
This concludes the proof.
∎

5
Agnostic Process Tomography (APT)

In this section, we generalize our agnostic unitary estimation algorithms from
Section

4
to obtain algorithms for agnostic process tomography.
Recall that in agnostic process tomography, we have access to an unknown quantum channel rather than a unitary.
This often makes the analysis more complicated.

5.1
Bounded-gate circuits

In
Section

4.1
, we showed that agnostic unitary estimation can be easily performed efficiently with respect to the number of samples.
However, it can be inefficient with respect to the computational complexity.
This motivates why we should care more about the runtime of algorithms in agnostic unitary estimation.
In this section, we aim to show a similar result for agnostic process tomography.

As in
Section

4.1
, we consider APT with respect to any class
\mathcal{C}
of unitaries with finite size
|\mathcal{C}|<+\infty
.
Here, the error bound we prove is not
1

Nevertheless, this qualitatively gives the same result that APT is easy with respect to sample complexity but can be more difficult with respect to runtime.

Proposition 3

(Agnostic process tomography of finite-sized unitary classes)
.

Let
\epsilon,\delta>0

Given query access to an unknown channel
\mathcal{E}

Proof.

The algorithm and analysis are similar to the proof of Proposition

\displaystyle\mathrm{d_{avg}}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{U})^{2}

5.2
APT from agnostic state tomography

In
Section

4.2
, we showed that we can convert any agnostic state tomography algorithm (
Definition

14
) into one for improper agnostic unitary estimation using ancilla qubits, as long as the concept classes are compatible.
This introduces a quadratic overhead in the sample/time complexity with respect to the error
\epsilon
.

In this section, we prove a similar result for improper agnostic process tomography.
The error bound we prove is not
1
-agnostic but depends on
2^{1/4}
times the square root of the optimal error, when we measure error with respect to the normalized Frobenius distance for superoperators (
Definition

10
).
We state this result formally in the following proposition.

Proposition 4

(Improper APT from agnostic state tomography)
.

Let
1>\epsilon,\delta>0
.
Suppose there exists a learning algorithm for (proper)
(1,\epsilon^{2},\delta)
-agnostic state tomography with respect to fidelity
F
for some class
\mathcal{D}
of pure states.
Let
\mathcal{C}
be a concept class of unitaries.
Suppose that
\ket{v(U)}\in\mathcal{D}
for all
U\in\mathcal{C}
.
Given query access to an unknown channel
\Phi
, there exists an improper learning algorithm outputting a hypothesis
\Phi^{\prime}
such that

d_{F}(\Phi,\Phi^{\prime})\leq 2^{1/4}\mathsf{opt}(\Phi,\mathcal{C})^{1/2}+\epsilon

(5.39)

with probability at least
1-\delta
.
Here,
\mathsf{opt}
is measured with respect to
d_{F}
.
Moreover, this requires the same sample and time complexity as the
(1,\epsilon^{2},\delta)
-agnostic state tomography algorithm.

As in
Section

4.2
, our algorithm simply applies agnostic state tomography to the Choi state of the unknown process, inspired by
[
87
]
.
We also note that we can trivially obtain
(1,\epsilon,\delta)
-agnostic process tomography with respect to the entanglement infidelity from
(1,\epsilon,\delta)
-agnostic state tomography (notably, without the quadratic blowup with respect to
\epsilon
) under the same compatibility condition.
Recall that we defined the entanglement infidelity in
Equation

4.30
, and this metric has been widely considered in (realizable) unitary estimation
[
87
,
6
,
88
,
89
,
90
,
91
,
92
,
93
]
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Proposition
2
.
Let
\Phi
denote the unknown unitary.
The algorithm is simple: first, prepare the Choi state
v(\Phi)
of
\Phi
and then apply the
(1,\epsilon^{2},\delta)
-agnostic state tomography algorithm to
v(\Phi)
.
Note that the known agnostic state tomography algorithms work for mixed state inputs
[
42
,
43
]
.
The output of our algorithm is the density matrix of the pure state output by the agnostic state tomography algorithm.

The agnostic state tomography algorithm outputs some state
\ket{\psi}
such that

F(v(\Phi),\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})\geq\max_{\ket{\phi}\in\mathcal{D}}F(v(\Phi),\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi})-\epsilon^{2},

(5.40)

where
v(\Phi)
denotes the Choi state of
\Phi
.
We can equivalently write the above guarantee in terms of the infidelity:

1-F(v(\Phi),\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})\leq\min_{\ket{\phi}\in\mathcal{D}}(1-F(v(\Phi),\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}))+\epsilon^{2}.

(5.41)

Using the inequalities relating trace distance and fidelity when one of the inputs is pure (see after
Definition

11
), we have

\mathrm{d_{tr}}(v(\Phi),\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})^{2}\leq\min_{\ket{\phi}\in\mathcal{D}}\mathrm{d_{tr}}(v(\Phi),\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi})+\epsilon^{2}.

(5.42)

We can relate this to the Frobenius norm using a result from
[
143
]
.
Namely, they prove that

\frac{1}{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\rho-\sigma\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}\leq\mathrm{d_{tr}}(\rho,\sigma)^{2}\leq\frac{\mathrm{rank}(\rho)\mathrm{rank}(\sigma)}{\mathrm{rank}(\rho)+\mathrm{rank}(\sigma)}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\rho-\sigma\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}.

(5.43)

Using this in the previous inequality, we have

\frac{1}{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert v(\Phi)-\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}\right\rVert}_{F}^{2}\leq\min_{\ket{\phi}\in\mathcal{D}}\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{rank}(v(\Phi))\mathrm{rank}(\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi})}{\mathrm{rank}(v(\Phi))+\mathrm{rank}(\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi})}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert v(\Phi)-\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}\right\rVert}_{F}+\epsilon^{2}.

(5.44)

Since
\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}
is pure, then
\mathrm{rank}(\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi})=1
so that the term in the square root can be bounded by
1
.
Moreover, by definition of the normalized Frobenius distance for operators (
Definition

9
),

d_{F}(v(\Phi),\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})=\frac{1}{2^{n}\sqrt{2}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert v(\Phi)-\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}\right\rVert}_{F}

(5.45)

since
v(\Phi),\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}
are on
2n

Thus, rewriting the above inequality in terms of the normalized Frobenius distance, we have

4^{n}d_{F}(v(\Phi),\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})^{2}\leq\sqrt{2}\cdot 2^{n}\min_{\ket{\phi}\in\mathcal{D}}d_{F}(v(\Phi),\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi})+\epsilon^{2}.

(5.46)

Taking the square root of both sides, we have

2^{n}d_{F}(v(\Phi),\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})\leq 2^{1/4}\sqrt{2^{n}\min_{\ket{\phi}\in\mathcal{D}}d_{F}(v(\Phi),\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi})}+\epsilon.

(5.47)

Now, by the same calculation as in
Section

4.2
, agnostically learning the Choi state
v(\Phi)
with respect to
2^{n}d_{F}
is equivalent to agnostically learning the channel
\Phi
with respect to
d_{F}
.
Note that the first
d_{F}
is the normalized Frobenius distance for operators (
Definition

9
) while the second
d_{F}
is the normalized Frobenius norm for superoperators (
Definition

10
).
We repeat the calculation here since it is short:

\displaystyle d_{F}(\Phi,\Psi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}\sqrt{2}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Phi-\Psi\right\rVert}_{F}

(5.48)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}\sqrt{2}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert J(\Phi)-J(\Psi)\right\rVert}_{F}

(5.49)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert v(\Phi)-v(\Psi)\right\rVert}_{F}

(5.50)

\displaystyle=2^{n}d_{F}(v(\Phi),v(\Psi)).

(5.51)

In the first and second lines, we use the definition of
d_{F}
for superoperators (
Definition

10
).
In the third line, we use that the Choi representation is simply the unnormalized Choi state.
Finally, in the last line, we use the definition of the normalized Frobenius distance for operators (
Definition

9
).
This completes the proof.
∎

This gives a fairly general result for converting an agnostic state tomography algorithm with respect to
\mathcal{D}
into an improper agnostic process tomography algorithm with respect to
\mathcal{C}
when
\ket{v(U)}\in\mathcal{D}
for all

.
However, our result is weaker than that of Proposition

,
as our algorithm is no longer

-agnostic but has an error bound of

instead.

As remarked earlier, the compatibility condition holds for many classes of unitaries for which agnostic state tomography algorithms already exist with respect to a suitable class.
In particular, there exist agnostic state tomography algorithms for stabilizer states, states with high stabilizer dimension, and discrete product states
[

43
]
.

5.3
Pauli strings and Pauli channels

In this section, we consider agnostic process tomography of the class of
n
-qubit Pauli strings
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{P}_{n}}=\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
as well as the class of
n
-qubit Pauli channels
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{PC}}
. A Pauli channel is defined as follows.

Definition 17

(Pauli Channel)
.

A quantum channel
\Phi
on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a
Pauli channel
if

\Phi(\rho)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}p_{x}\sigma_{x}\rho\sigma_{x},

(5.63)

where
\{p(x)\}_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}
forms a probability distribution.

Pauli channels form a naturally occurring class of noise channels, where the probabilities
p_{x}
denote the chance of a particular Pauli error occurring.
Clearly, Pauli channels have Fourier coefficients of the following form

\hat{\Phi}(x,x)=p_{x},\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}

(5.64)

and

\hat{\Phi}(x,y)=0,\quad x\neq y

(5.65)

For both classes, our results are with respect to
d_{F}
, the normalized Frobenius distance for superoperators (
Definition

10
).
Our proof techniques are similar to those used in
Section

4.3
, but we use the more general Fourier spectrum of superoperators, as introduced in
Section

2.3
. We start with the class of Pauli strings.

Theorem 5

(Agnostic Process Tomography of Pauli strings in
d_{F}
)
.

There exists a learning algorithm for proper
(1,\epsilon,\delta)
-agnostic process tomography of the class of
n
-qubit Pauli strings
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{P}_{n}}
with respect to error in
d_{F}
using

N=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{4}}\right)

(5.66)

samples and running in time

\mathcal{O}\left(N\right).

(5.67)

Proof.

The algorithm is the same as
Algorithm

1
, but we instead prepare the Choi state
v(\Phi)
of the unknown quantum channel
\Phi
. Recall that we can write the Choi state as

v(\Phi)=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|v(\sigma_{x})\rangle\langle v(\sigma_{y})|.

(5.68)

Then, by measuring
v(\Phi)
in the basis
\{|v(\sigma_{x})\rangle\}_{x}
, we measure
x
with probability

p(x)\triangleq\langle v(\sigma_{x})|v(\Phi)|v(\sigma_{x})\rangle=\hat{\Phi}(x,x).

(5.69)

Now, our analysis proceeds similarly to the proof of
Theorem

1
. Denote the true most likely Pauli string by

z^{\star}\triangleq\arg\max_{z\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}p(z).

(5.70)

We compute the optimal error for the class of Pauli strings. Recall from
Lemma

6
that for quantum channels
\Phi,\Psi
,

d_{F}(\Phi,\Psi)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)-\hat{\Psi}(x,y)|^{2}.

(5.71)

Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{opt}(\Phi,\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{P}_{n}})^{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{z\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}d_{F}(\Phi,\Phi_{z,z})^{2}

(5.72)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\min_{z\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\\
x\neq z\text{ or }y\neq z\end{subarray}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|^{2}+|\hat{\Phi}(z,z)-1|^{2}\right)

(5.73)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\|\hat{\Phi}\|^{2}_{F}}{2}-\max_{z\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\hat{\Phi}(z,z)

(5.74)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\|\hat{\Phi}\|^{2}_{F}}{2}-\hat{\Phi}(z^{*},z^{*}),

(5.75)

where we use
\Phi_{z,z}
to denote the channel
\Phi_{z,z}(\rho)=\sigma_{z}\rho\sigma_{z}
so that
\hat{\Phi}_{z,z}(z,z)=1
and zero otherwise.
The second equality follows from
Lemma

6
.
In the third line, note that
\|\hat{\Phi}\|_{F}^{2}
denotes
\|\hat{\Phi}\|_{F}^{2}\triangleq\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|^{2}
, and we use the fact that the diagonal elements of
\hat{\Phi}
are all real and non-negative.
In the last line, we use the definition of
z^{\star}
.

Hence, we see that the hypothesis
\sigma_{z^{\star}}
achieves the optimal error for the class
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{P}_{n}}
.
Our algorithm aims to estimate the true most likely Pauli string
z^{\star}
by the one most frequently occurring in our measurements.
Let
\{\hat{p}(x)\}_{x}
be the empirical distribution obtained from our measurement outcomes, which estimates
p
.
Then, denote the most frequent outcome out of our measurements by

x^{*}\triangleq\argmax_{x\in\{x_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}}\hat{p}(x).

(5.76)

Using
Lemma

10
, for some
\epsilon_{1}>0
, the empirical estimates
\hat{p}(x)
of
p(x)=\hat{\Phi}(x,x)
satisfy, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

|\hat{p}(x)-\hat{\Phi}(x,x)|\leq\epsilon_{1},\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{P}_{n},

(5.77)

using
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon_{1}^{2}}\right)
copies of the Choi state
|v(U)\rangle
. Now, since
x^{*}
is the most frequent string observed,

\hat{p}(x^{*})\geq\hat{p}(z^{\star}).

(5.78)

Conditioned on obtaining estimates
\hat{p}(x)
that are
\epsilon_{1}
-close to
|\hat{U}_{x}|^{2}
for all
x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, we have

\hat{\Phi}(x^{*},x^{*})+\epsilon_{1}\geq\hat{p}(x^{*})\quad\text{ and }\quad\hat{p}(z^{\star})\geq\hat{\Phi}(z^{*},z^{*})-\epsilon_{1}.

(5.79)

Thus, we have

\hat{\Phi}(z^{*},z^{*})-\hat{\Phi}(x^{*},x^{*})\leq 2\epsilon_{1}.

(5.80)

Now, we can bound the error of the hypothesis as follows

\displaystyle d_{F}(\Phi,\sigma_{x^{*}})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\|\hat{\Phi}\|^{2}_{F}}{2}-\hat{\Phi}(x^{*},x^{*})

(5.81)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\|\hat{\Phi}\|^{2}_{F}}{2}-\hat{\Phi}(z^{*},z^{*})+\hat{\Phi}(z^{*},z^{*})-\hat{\Phi}(x^{*},x^{*})

(5.82)

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{opt}(\Phi,\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{P}_{n}})^{2}+2\epsilon_{1},

(5.83)

where the first line is true by
Lemma

6
(a similar calculation as before for
\mathsf{opt}
), and the last line follows by
Equation

5.75
and
Equation

5.80
.
Thus, by setting
\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon^{2}/2
, we obtain

d_{F}(\Phi,\sigma_{x^{*}})^{2}\leq\mathsf{opt}(\Phi,\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{P}_{n}})^{2}+\epsilon^{2}\leq(\mathsf{opt}(\Phi,\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{P}_{n}})+\epsilon)^{2},

(5.84)

which is the desired bound.
Now, by our choice of
\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon^{2}/2
, we use

N=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon_{1}^{2}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{4}}\right)

(5.85)

samples of the Choi state or queries to the channel
\Phi
. The time complexity is that of identifying the most frequent string out of
N
strings, which can be done in time
\mathcal{O}(N)
.
∎

Now, we present a proper 1-agnostic learning algorithm for the class of Pauli channels.

Algorithm 5

1-Agnostic Proper Learning Pauli Channels

1:

Prepare
N=\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\delta)/\epsilon^{2})
copies of
v(\Phi)
and measure them in the
\{\ket{v(\sigma_x)}\}_{x}
basis to construct estimates
\hat{p}(x)
of
\hat{\Phi}(x,x)
.

2:

return

\Phi=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\hat{p}(x)\sigma_{x}(\cdot)\sigma_{x}
.

Theorem 6

(Agnostic Process Tomography of Pauli channels in
d_{F}
)
.

Algorithm

5
performs proper
(1,\epsilon,\delta)
-agnostic process tomography of the class of
n
-qubit Pauli channels
\mathcal{C}_{\text{PC}}
with respect to error in
d_{F}
using

N=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)

(5.86)

samples and running in time

\mathcal{O}\left(N\log(N)\right).

(5.87)

Proof.

The algorithm is detailed in
Algorithm

5
.
We start by computing the optimal error for the class
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{PC}}
.

\displaystyle\mathsf{opt}(\Phi,\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{PC}})^{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{\Psi\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{PC}}}d_{F}(\Phi,\Psi)^{2}

(5.88)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\min_{\Psi\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{PC}}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,x)-\hat{\Psi}(x,x)|^{2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)-0|^{2}\right)

(5.89)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|^{2},

(5.90)

where the second line follows from
Lemma

6
and the fact that
\Psi
is a Pauli channel. As
\{\hat{\Phi}(x,x)\}_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}
form a probability distribution, the superoperator

\Phi^{\prime}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\hat{\Phi}(x,x)\sigma_{x}(\cdot)\sigma_{x}

(5.91)

is a Pauli channel. Clearly,

d_{F}(\Phi,\Phi^{\prime})^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|^{2}.

(5.92)

Thus, from the lower bound on
opt
from the above discussion, we have

\mathsf{opt}(\Phi,\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{PC}})^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|^{2}.

(5.93)

Since measuring
v(\Phi)
in the basis
\{|v(\sigma_{x})\rangle\}_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}
returns samples from the distribution
\{\hat{\Phi}(x,x)\}_{x}
, agnostic process tomography of Pauli channels reduces to the task of learning this distribution. From
Lemma

10
, we see that
\mathcal{O}\left(\log(1/\delta)/\epsilon^{2}\right)
samples allow us to empirically construct a distribution
\{\hat{p}(x)\}_{x}
, that satisfies, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,x)-\hat{p}(x)|^{2}\leq 2\epsilon^{2}.

(5.94)

Now, let our hypothesis channel be defined by

\Phi_{\hat{p}}\triangleq\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\hat{p}(x)\sigma_{x}(\cdot)\sigma_{x}.

(5.95)

The error of this hypothesis is bounded by

\displaystyle d_{F}(\Phi,\Phi_{\hat{p}})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,x)-\hat{p}(x)|^{2}

(5.96)

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{opt}(\Phi,\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{PC}})^{2}+\epsilon^{2}

(5.97)

\displaystyle\leq(\mathsf{opt}(\Phi,\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{PC}})+\epsilon)^{2},

(5.98)

where the second line follows from
Equations

5.93
and
5.94
,
giving us the desired 1-agnostic learning guarantee. The running time is that of counting the number of occurrences of each measurement outcome, which can be done in time
\mathcal{O}(N\log(N))
.
∎

5.4
Junta channels

In this section, we present an algorithm for agnostic process tomography of junta channels. Similar to the definition of unitary juntas,
k
-junta channels on
n
-qubits are defined as quantum channels that act non-trivially only on
k
qubits.

Definition 18

(
k
-junta channel)
.

A quantum channel
\Phi
on
n
qubits is a
k
-junta channel
if there exists a set
S\subseteq[n]
with
|S|=k
such that
\Phi=\Psi_{S}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{S^{c}}
, for some
k
-qubit quantum channel
\Psi_{S}
.

In
Section

4.4
, for the problem of agnostic unitary estimation of unitary
k
-juntas, we identified the subset of qubits with the highest Pauli weight and then learned all Pauli coefficients restricted to this subset. We were able to identify this subset efficiently because, for an
n
-qubit unitary
U
, we could efficiently sample from the distribution
\{|\hat{U}_{x}^{2}|\}_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}
.

However, in the case of a quantum channel
\Phi
, a similar method allows us to sample from the distribution
\{\hat{\Phi}(x,x)\}_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}
. This does not allow us to estimate the weight of a subset
S
, which is given by
w_{S}(\Phi)=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{S}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|^{2}
. To overcome this issue, we learn the Fourier coefficients associated with each subset and use them to estimate the weight. Unsurprisingly, this results in a worse sample complexity than the unitary junta case (
Theorem

2
).
Once we have estimated the weights, we can construct an improper hypothesis by restricting to the Fourier coefficients corresponding to the heaviest estimated subset.
The algorithm is sketched in
Algorithm

6
.

Algorithm 6

1-Agnostic Improper Learning
k
-Junta Channels

1:

Use
N
queries to
\Phi
to construct estimates
\alpha_{x,y}
of the Fourier coefficients with
x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{S}
, for all subsets
S\subseteq[n],|S|=k
.

2:

Construct estimates of weights of all subsets of
[n]
of cardinality
k
using
\alpha_{x,y}
.

3:

Select
S^{\prime}
to be the subset with the greatest estimated weight.

4:

return

\Psi=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{S^{\prime}}}\alpha_{x,y}\sigma_{x}(\cdot)\sigma_{y}
.

Theorem 7

(Agnostic Process Tomography of
k
-Junta Channels in
d_{F}
.)
.

Algorithm

6
performs improper
(1,\epsilon,\delta)
-agnostic process tomography of the class of quantum
k
-junta channels on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
,
\mathcal{JC}_{n,k}
, with respect to error in
d_{F}
using

N=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k2^{12k}n^{k}\log(n/\delta)}{\epsilon^{4}}\right)

(5.99)

queries to the unknown channel
\Phi
and running in time

\mathcal{O}(N).

(5.100)

Proof.

The algorithm is detailed in
Algorithm

6
.
We start by lower bounding the optimal error for the class of junta channels. For a junta channel
\Psi_{S}\in\mathcal{JC}_{n,k}
acting only on a set
S
with
|S|=k
, we have

\displaystyle d_{F}(\Phi,\Psi_{S})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)-\hat{\Psi}_{S}(x,y)|^{2}

(5.101)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{S}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)-\hat{\Psi}_{S}(x,y)|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\\
x\notin\mathcal{P}_{S}\text{ or }y\notin\mathcal{P}_{S}\end{subarray}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|^{2}

(5.102)

\displaystyle\geq 0+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\\
x\notin\mathcal{P}_{S}\text{ or }y\notin\mathcal{P}_{S}\end{subarray}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|^{2}

(5.103)

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\hat{\Phi}\|_{F}^{2}-w_{S}(\Phi)}{2},

(5.104)

where the first line follows from Lemma
6
.
The second line uses that
\Psi_{S}
is a
k
-junta on the set
S
.
The final equality uses the definition of weight and
\|\hat{\Phi}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|^{2}
.
Then, we can obtain a lower bound on
\mathsf{opt}
by minimizing over the choice of set
S
and junta channel
\Psi_{S}
.

\displaystyle\mathsf{opt}(\Phi,\mathcal{JC}_{n,k})^{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{S\subseteq[n],|S|=k}\min_{\Psi_{S}}d_{F}(\Phi,\Psi_{S})^{2}

(5.105)

\displaystyle\geq\min_{S\subseteq[n],|S|=k}\min_{\Psi_{S}}\frac{\|\hat{\Phi}\|_{F}^{2}-w_{S}(\Phi)}{2}

(5.106)

\displaystyle=\min_{S\subseteq[n],|S|=k}\frac{\|\hat{\Phi}\|_{F}^{2}-w_{S}(\Phi)}{2}.

(5.107)

Let
S^{*}\triangleq\argmax_{S\subseteq[n],|S|=k}w_{S}(\Phi)
be the set of cardinality
k
with the highest Fourier weight. Then, we have

\mathsf{opt}(\Phi,\mathcal{JC}_{n,k})^{2}\geq\frac{\|\hat{\Phi}\|_{F}^{2}-w_{S^{*}}(\Phi)}{2}.

(5.108)

Now that we have lower bounded opt, we analyze our algorithm (
Algorithm

6
), which starts by estimating all Fourier coefficients associated with subsets of size
k
.
Note that there are at most
n^{k}16^{k}
such Fourier coefficients.
Thus, we use
Lemma

12

\mathcal{O}(n^{k}16^{k})
times, to obtain estimates
\alpha_{x,y}
, such that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

|\alpha_{x,y}-\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|\leq\epsilon_{1},\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{S},\;S\subseteq[n],\;|S|=k.

(5.109)

Using a union bound over failure probabilities of repeated applications of Lemma
12
for each Fourier coefficient, we see that this requires

N=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k\cdot 16^{k}n^{k}\log(n/\delta)}{\epsilon_{1}^{2}}\right).

(5.110)

queries to
\Phi
and runs in time
\mathcal{O}(N)
. In the following, we condition on successful estimation. Now, we use the estimated coefficients to compute estimates
\hat{w}_{S}
for all subsets
of cardinality
k
.

\hat{w}_{S}\triangleq\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{S}}|\alpha_{x,y}|^{2},\quad\forall S\subseteq[n],|S|=k.

(5.111)

As we have already estimated all Fourier coefficients associated with subsets of cardinality
k
by
\alpha_{x,y}
, we can now compute
\hat{w}_{S}
. As there are
\mathcal{O}(n^{k})
subsets of cardinality
k
, and computing each weight requires adding weights of
16^{k}
coefficients, finding all the weights takes time
\mathcal{O}(n^{k}\cdot 16^{k})
.
We now bound the error of these estimates.

\displaystyle|\hat{w}_{S}-w_{S}(\Phi)|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{S}}|\alpha_{x,y}|^{2}-|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|^{2}\right|

(5.112)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{S}}||\alpha_{x,y}|^{2}-|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|^{2}|

(5.113)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{S}}||\alpha_{x,y}|-|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)||\cdot||\alpha_{x,y}|+|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)||

(5.114)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{S}}|\alpha_{x,y}-\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|\cdot||\alpha_{x,y}|+|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)||

(5.115)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{S}}\epsilon_{1}\cdot 2

(5.116)

\displaystyle=2\cdot 16^{k}\epsilon_{1},

(5.117)

The first line follows from the definitions of the weights.
The second line follows by the triangle inequality.
The fourth line follows by the reverse triangle inequality.
In the fifth line, we use
Equation

5.109
and the fact that the absolute value of a Fourier coefficient is at most 1.
In the last line, we sum over all
16^{k}
Fourier coefficients with support in
S
.

Let the subset with the highest estimated weight be
S^{\prime}
.

S^{\prime}\triangleq\argmax_{S\subseteq[n],|S|=k}\hat{w}_{S}.

(5.118)

Then, our hypothesis will be

\Psi\triangleq\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{S^{\prime}}}\alpha_{x,y}\Phi_{x,y},

(5.119)

where recall we denote
\Phi_{x,y}=\sigma_{x}(\cdot)\sigma_{y}
.
This simply aims to approximate
\Phi
by estimating all Fourier coefficients of the highest weight subset. It remains to bound the error of this hypothesis, i.e.,
d_{F}(\Phi,\Psi)
. Denote by

\Phi_{S}\triangleq\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{S}}\hat{\Phi}(x,y)\Phi_{x,y},

(5.120)

the Fourier truncation of
\Phi
restricted to the subset
S
, for some subset
S\subseteq[n]
.
Now, using the triangle inequality for
d_{F}
, we have

d_{F}(\Phi,\Psi)\leq d_{F}(\Phi,\Phi_{S^{\prime}})+d_{F}(\Phi_{S^{\prime}},\Psi).

(5.121)

We can upper bound
d_{F}(\Phi,\Phi_{S^{\prime}})
as

\displaystyle d_{F}(\Phi,\Phi_{S^{\prime}})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)-\hat{\Phi}_{S^{\prime}}(x,y)|^{2}

(5.122)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{S^{\prime}}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)-\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\\
x\notin\mathcal{P}_{S^{\prime}}\text{ or }y\notin\mathcal{P}_{S^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|^{2}

(5.123)

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\hat{\Phi}\|_{F}^{2}-w_{S^{\prime}}(\Phi)}{2}

(5.124)

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\hat{\Phi}\|_{F}^{2}-w_{S^{*}}(\Phi)}{2}+\frac{w_{S^{*}}(\Phi)-w_{S^{\prime}}(\Phi)}{2}

(5.125)

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{opt}(\Phi,\mathcal{JC}_{n,k})^{2}+\frac{w_{S^{*}}(\Phi)-w_{S^{\prime}}(\Phi)}{2},

(5.126)

where the last inequality follows from
Equation

5.108
. To bound the differences in weights, observe that from
Equation

5.117
,

w_{S^{\prime}}(\Phi)+2\cdot 16^{k}\epsilon_{1}\geq\hat{w}_{S^{\prime}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\hat{w}_{S^{\star}}\geq w_{S^{\star}}(\Phi)-2\cdot 16^{k}\epsilon_{1}.

(5.127)

As
S^{\prime}
is the set with the heaviest estimated weight,

\hat{w}_{S^{\prime}}\geq\hat{w}_{S^{\star}}.

(5.128)

Thus, we finally obtain

d_{F}(\Phi,\Phi_{S^{\prime}})^{2}\leq\mathsf{opt}(\Phi,\mathcal{JC}_{n,k})^{2}+2\cdot 16^{k}\epsilon_{1}\leq\left(\mathsf{opt}(\Phi,\mathcal{JC}_{n,k})+4^{k}\sqrt{2\epsilon_{1}}\right)^{2}.

(5.129)

Now, it remains to bound
d_{F}(\Phi_{S^{\prime}},\Psi)
.

\displaystyle d_{F}(\Phi_{S^{\prime}},\Psi)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{S^{\prime}}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)-\alpha_{x,y}|^{2}\leq\frac{16^{k}\epsilon_{1}^{2}}{2},

(5.130)

where the inequality follows by
Equation

5.109
and the fact that there are at most
16^{k}
Fourier coefficients for
x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{S^{\prime}}
since
|S^{\prime}|=k
.
Thus, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

d_{F}(\Phi,\Psi)\leq\mathsf{opt}(\Phi,\mathcal{JC}_{n,k})+4^{k}\sqrt{2\epsilon_{1}}+\frac{4^{k}\epsilon_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}.

(5.131)

To obtain the 1-agnostic learning guarantee, we choose
\epsilon_{1}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}/16^{k})
. This gives us the stated query complexity. To show the stated time complexity, we observe that it is dominated by that of learning the Fourier coefficients.
∎

5.5
Low-degree channels

In this section, we present an algorithm for agnostic process tomography of low-degree channels.
We define a low-degree channel similarly to the definition of a low-degree unitary.

Definition 19

(Low-degree channels)
.

A quantum channel
\Phi
is said to have
degree

d
if

w^{>d}(\Phi)=0,

(5.132)

where
w^{>d}
is defined in
Definition

8
.
In other words, the degree of a channel
\Phi
is the minimum integer
d
such that if
|x|,|y|>d
, then
\hat{\Phi}(x,y)=0
.

We note a difference between our definition of degree-
d
channels and that of
[
30
]
. Our definition requires that for all
\hat{\Phi}(x,y)\neq 0
, both
|x|
and
|y|
are at most
d
. On the other hand, the definition of
[
30
]
requires that for all non-zero
\hat{\Phi}(x,y)
,
|x|+|y|\leq d
. With our chosen definition, the channels corresponding to degree-
d
unitaries (
Definition

16
) are also channels of degree-
d
.
Thus, we find this to be a more natural definition, and we also use this fact in
Remark

3
. However, the definitions do not differ significantly, and one can show that an approach similar to
Algorithm

7
allows for agnostic tomography of low-degree channels adhering to the definition in
[
30
]
.

Now, we will show that by learning all low-degree Fourier coefficients of the channel, we obtain an improper
1
-agnostic hypothesis.
This is similar to the first step of the junta channel learning algorithm (
Theorem

7
).
The algorithm is detailed in
Algorithm

7
.

Algorithm 7

1-Agnostic Improper Learning Degree-
d
Channels

1:

Use
N
queries to
\Phi
to construct estimates
\alpha_{x,y}
of the Fourier coefficients for all

.

2:

return

\Psi=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\\
|x|,|y|\leq d\end{subarray}}\alpha_{x,y}\sigma_{x}(\cdot)\sigma_{y}

Theorem 8

(Agnostic Process Tomography of Low-Degree Channels in

.

-agnostic process tomography of the class of quantum channels on

\mathcal{LC}_{n,d}

(5.134)

Proof.

The algorithm is detailed in
Algorithm

, and the analysis is similar to that of
Theorem

.
We start by lower bounding the optimal error for the class of low-degree channels. We have

\displaystyle\mathsf{opt}(\Phi,\mathcal{LC}_{n,d})^{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{\Psi\in\mathcal{LC}_{n,d}}d_{F}^{2}(\Phi,\Psi)

(5.135)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\min_{\Psi\in\mathcal{LC}_{n,d}}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)-\hat{\Psi}(x,y)|^{2}

(5.136)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\min_{\Psi\in\mathcal{LC}_{n,d}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\\
|x|,|y|\leq d\end{subarray}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)-\hat{\Psi}(x,y)|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\\
|x|>d\text{ or }|y|>d\end{subarray}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|^{2}

(5.137)

\displaystyle\geq 0+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\\
|x|>d\text{ or }|y|>d\end{subarray}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|^{2}

(5.138)

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\hat{\Phi}\|_{F}^{2}-w^{\leq d}(\Phi)}{2}

(5.139)

where the second line follows from Lemma
6
.
In the third line, we use that
\Psi
has degree
d
.
The final equality uses the definition of weight (
Definition

8
) and
\|\hat{\Phi}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|^{2}
.

Our algorithm (
Algorithm

7
) estimates all Fourier coefficients of degree at most
d
. Note that there are at most
\mathcal{O}(n^{2d}9^{d})
of them.
We use
Lemma

12

\mathcal{O}(n^{2d}9^{d})
times, to obtain estimates
\alpha_{x,y}
such that

|\alpha_{x,y}-\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|\leq\epsilon_{1},\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\text{ s.t. }|x|,|y|\leq d,

(5.140)

with probability at least
1-\delta
.
Using a union bound over failure probabilities of repeated applications of Lemma
12
for each Fourier coefficient, we see that this requires

N=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d\cdot 9^{d}n^{2d}\log(n/\delta)}{\epsilon_{1}^{2}}\right).

(5.141)

queries to
\Phi
and runs in time
\mathcal{O}(N)
. Now, we construct our hypothesis

\Psi\triangleq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\\
|x|,|y|\leq d\end{subarray}}\alpha_{x,y}\Phi_{x,y}.

(5.142)

This simply aims to approximate
\Phi
by estimating all low-degree coefficients. It remains to bound the error of this hypothesis, i.e.,

by

\Phi_{d}\triangleq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\\
|x|,|y|\leq d\end{subarray}}\hat{\Phi}(x,y)\Phi_{x,y}.

(5.143)

Now, using the triangle inequality for

d_{F}(\Phi,\Psi)\leq d_{F}(\Phi,\Phi_{d})+d_{F}(\Phi_{d},\Psi).

(5.144)

We can upper bound

\displaystyle d_{F}(\Phi,\Phi_{d})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)-\hat{\Phi}_{d}(x,y)|^{2}

(5.145)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\\
|x|,|y|\leq d\end{subarray}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)-\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\\
|x|>d\text{ or }|y|>d\end{subarray}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)|^{2}

(5.146)

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\hat{\Phi}\|_{F}^{2}-w^{\leq d}(\Phi)}{2}

(5.147)

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{opt}(\Phi,\mathcal{LC}_{n,d})^{2}.

(5.148)

The first line follows by
Lemma

6
.
The second line uses the definition of
\Phi_{d}
.
The third line uses the definition of weight (
Definition

8
).
Finally, the last line uses
Equation

5.139
.
Now, conditioned on the successful estimation of
\alpha_{x,y}
, we can bound
d_{F}(\Phi_{d},\Psi)
.

\displaystyle d_{F}(\Phi_{d},\Psi)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}|\hat{\Phi}_{d}(x,y)-\alpha_{x,y}|^{2}

(5.149)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\\
|x|,|y|\leq d\end{subarray}}|\hat{\Phi}(x,y)-\alpha_{x,y}|^{2}

(5.150)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(n^{2d}9^{d}\epsilon_{1}^{2}\right).

(5.151)

In the second line, we use the definition of
\Phi_{d}
.
In the third line, we use
Equation

5.140
and that there are at most
\mathcal{O}(n^{2d}9^{d})
Fourier coefficients of degree at most
d
.
Thus, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

d_{F}(\Phi,\Psi)\leq\mathsf{opt}(\Phi,\mathcal{LC}_{n,d})+\mathcal{O}\left(n^{d}3^{d}\epsilon_{1}\right).

(5.152)

To obtain the 1-agnostic learning guarantee, we choose
\epsilon_{1}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon/n^{d}3^{d})
. This gives us the stated query and time complexities.
∎

5.6
n
-to-1 QAC0 channels

In this section, we consider the class of channels
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
formed by constant-depth circuits with arbitrary single-qubit gates and controlled phase-flip gates of arbitrary width
[
86
]
.
Following standard terminology, we refer to the
size
of a quantum circuit as the number of multi-qubit gates, and the
depth
as the number of layers of multi-qubit gates in the circuit.
We consider the algorithm of
[
22
]
and show that it is an improper 1-agnostic learning algorithm.
We follow the definition of
[
22
]
for the circuit class
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
.

Definition 20

(
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits; Definition 16 in
[
22
]
)
.

The class
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
contains quantum circuit families consisting of multiple-qubit
\mathsf{CZ}
gates and arbitrary single-qubit gates, with depth bounded below a constant. That is, the circuit family
\{C_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is in
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
if there exists a
d\in\mathbb{N}
such that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
C_{n}
is a quantum circuit on
n
qubits consisting only of multiple-qubit
\mathsf{CZ}
gates and arbitrary single-qubit gates, with depth at most
d
.

The algorithm of
[
22
]
focuses on learning a specific class of channels formed by
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits.
In particular, they consider the class of channels formed by tracing out all except a single qubit.

Definition 21

(
n
-to-1
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
channels; Definition 12 in
[
22
]
)
.

Let
n,a\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
U
be an
(n+a)
-qubit unitary implemented by a
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit, and let
\psi
be an
a
-qubit quantum state. We define the
n
-to-
1

\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
channel
\mathcal{E}_{U,\psi}
to be the following channel mapping
n
qubits to
1
qubit

\mathcal{E}_{U,\psi}(\rho)\triangleq\tr_{[n+a-1]}\left(U(\rho\otimes\psi)U^{\dagger}\right),

(5.153)

where
\tr_{[n+a-1]}
denotes the partial trace over all except the last qubit.

We consider the Pauli spectrum expansion (see
Definition

4
) of the Choi representation of such channels.
Namely, for an
n
-to-
1

\mathsf{QAC}^{0}

\mathcal{E}_{U,\psi}
, the Choi representation
J(\mathcal{E}_{U,\psi})\in\mathcal{B}((\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n+1})
can be expressed as

J(\mathcal{E}_{U,\psi})=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}}\alpha_{x}\sigma_{x}

(5.154)

One of the key technical results of
[
22
]
is that when expressing the Choi representation of
n
-to-1
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
channels in the Pauli spectrum, the weight is concentrated on the low-degree terms.
Formally, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 18

(Low-degree concentration of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}

[
22
]
)
.

Let
U
be a unitary implemented by an
(n+a)
-qubit
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}

and size

. Let
\psi\in\mathcal{B}((\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes a})
be an
a
-qubit quantum state. For each

,

w^{>k}(J(\mathcal{E}_{U,\psi}))\triangleq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}\\
|x|>k\end{subarray}}|\alpha_{x}|^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(s^{2}2^{-k^{1/d}}\right)\cdot 2^{a}\|\psi\|_{F}^{2}.

(5.155)

Our distance metric for learning
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}

10

From

We describe the algorithm in
Algorithm

Algorithm 8

.

(Improper 1-APT of n-to-1

.

There exists a learning algorithm for improper
(1,\epsilon,\delta)
-agnostic process tomography of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}(n,a,s,d)

\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
channels
\{\mathcal{E}_{U,|0^{a}\rangle}\}
, where the unitaries are implemented by
(n+a)
-qubit
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits with size

and depth
d
, and

, with respect to error in

N=n^{\mathcal{O}(\log^{d}(s^{4}/\epsilon^{4}))}\cdot\log(1/\delta)

(5.156)

\Phi
and running in time

n^{\mathcal{O}(\log^{d}(s^{4}/\epsilon^{4}))}\cdot\log(1/\delta).

(5.157)

We define the following classes of low-degree operators, which we agnostically learn as part of our proof of

9
.

Definition 22

(Low-degree operators)
.

Define the class of
k
-degree,
n+1
-qubit bounded linear operators

\mathcal{O}_{k}\subseteq\mathcal{B}((\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n+1})
as

\mathcal{O}_{k}\triangleq\{A\in\mathcal{B}((\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n+1})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}w^{>k}(A)=0\}.

(5.158)

In order to prove

9
, we will make use of the following lemma on learning the closest low-degree operator to the Choi representation of an unknown channel.

Lemma 19

(Agnostic-learning a low-degree operator)
.

Let
\epsilon,\delta>0
. Given copies of the Choi-state
v(\Phi)
of an unknown quantum channel
\Phi
from
\mathcal{B}((\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n})
to
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C}^{2})
, there exists an algorithm that outputs a hypothesis
A\in\mathcal{O}_{k}
, such that

d_{F}(J(\Phi),A)\leq\min_{B\in\mathcal{O}_{k}}d_{F}(J(\Phi),B)+\epsilon,

(5.159)

with probability at least
1-\delta
. Moreover, this algorithm uses

N=\frac{n^{\mathcal{O}(k)}\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}

(5.160)

copies of
v(\Phi)
and runs in time

kn^{\mathcal{O}(k)}N.

(5.161)

Proof.

Recall from
Equation

5.154
that we can write
J({\Phi})
in the Pauli basis as

J(\Phi)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}}\alpha_{x}\sigma_{x}.

(5.162)

Now, we compute the optimal error over the class of low-degree operators
\mathcal{O}_{k}
.

\displaystyle\min_{B\in\mathcal{O}_{k}}d_{F}(J(\Phi),B)^{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{B\in\mathcal{O}_{k}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}}\frac{|\alpha_{x}-\hat{B}_{x}|^{2}}{2}

(5.163)

\displaystyle=\min_{B\in\mathcal{O}_{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}\\
|x|\leq k\end{subarray}}\frac{|\alpha_{x}-\hat{B}_{x}|^{2}}{2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}\\
|x|>k\end{subarray}}\frac{|\alpha_{x}|^{2}}{2}

(5.164)

\displaystyle\geq\min_{B\in\mathcal{O}_{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}\\
|x|>k\end{subarray}}\frac{|\alpha_{x}|^{2}}{2}

(5.165)

\displaystyle=\frac{w^{>k}(J(\Phi))}{2}.

(5.166)

In the first line, we use
Lemma

5
.
In the second line, we use that
B
is low-degree.
One can check that this lower bound is achieved by the low-degree truncation of
J(\Phi)
, i.e., the operator

B^{*}\triangleq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}\\
|x|\leq k\end{subarray}}\alpha_{x}\sigma_{x}

(5.167)

has error

\min_{B\in\mathcal{O}_{k}}d_{F}(J(\Phi),B)=d_{F}(J(\Phi),B^{*})=\sqrt{\frac{w^{>k}(J(\Phi))}{2}}.

(5.168)

Thus, it suffices to learn the low-degree truncation of
J(\Phi)
.
First, we observe that because
\Phi
is trace-preserving,
\tr(J(\Phi))=2^{n}
, the dimension of the input system. Thus, we have

v(\Phi)=\frac{J(\Phi)}{2^{n}}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}}\alpha_{x}\sigma_{x}.

(5.169)

To estimate the Pauli coefficients
\alpha_{x}
, we use classical shadows (
Lemma

13
) to estimate the expectation values of the corresponding Pauli
\sigma_{x}
with respect to the Choi state
v(\Phi)
.
These expectation values indeed tell us information about the Pauli coefficients: for any
y\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}
,

\displaystyle\tr(v(\Phi) \sigma_y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}}\frac{\alpha_{x}}{2^{n}}\tr(\sigma_x \sigma_y)

(5.170)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}}\frac{\alpha_{x}}{2^{n}}\delta_{x,y}\tr(I^{\otimes n+1})

(5.171)

\displaystyle=2\alpha_{y}.

(5.172)

Thus, by estimating the expectation values of low-degree Paulis with respect to the Choi state
v(\Phi)
, we can obtain estimates of the Pauli coefficients of
J(\Phi)
.
We need to estimate
n^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
Paulis of degree at most
k
. Let the desired error in each estimate be at most
\epsilon_{1}>0
and the overall failure probability be at most
\delta>0
.
Then, from
Lemma

13
, we see that this can be done using

N=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k\cdot 3^{k}\log(n/\delta)}{\epsilon_{1}^{2}}\right)

(5.173)

copies of
v(\Phi)
and in time

kn^{\mathcal{O}(k)}N.

(5.174)

Suppose this procedure gives us estimates
\hat{\beta}_{x}
, for all strings
x\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}
of degree at most
k
. By
Lemma

13
, we have

|\hat{\beta}_{x}-\tr(v(\Phi)\sigma_x)|\leq\epsilon_{1},\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}\text{ s.t. }|x|\leq k

(5.175)

with probability at least
1-\delta
.
By
Equation

5.172
, we then have

|\hat{\beta}_{x}-2\alpha_{x}|\leq\epsilon_{1},\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}\text{ s.t. }|x|\leq k

(5.176)

Then, define our hypothesis by

A\triangleq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}\\
|x|\leq k\end{subarray}}\frac{\hat{\beta}_{x}}{2}\sigma_{x}.

(5.177)

It remains to bound the error of this hypothesis.

\displaystyle d_{F}(J(\Phi),A)

\displaystyle\leq d_{F}(J(\Phi),B^{*})+d_{F}(B^{*},A)

(5.178)

\displaystyle\leq\min_{B\in\mathcal{O}_{k}}d_{F}(J(\Phi),B)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}\\
|x|\leq k\end{subarray}}\left|\alpha_{x}-\frac{\hat{\beta}_{x}}{2}\right|^{2}}

(5.179)

\displaystyle\leq\min_{B\in\mathcal{O}_{k}}d_{F}(J(\Phi),B)+n^{\mathcal{O}(k)}\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}.

(5.180)

In the first line, we use the triangle inequality for
d_{F}
.
In the second line, we use the definition of
B^{*}
and
A
and
Lemma

5
.
In the third line, we use
Equation

5.176
and the fact that there are at most
n^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
Pauli coefficients of degree at most
k
.
Then, setting
\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon/n^{\mathcal{O}(k)}

5.7
Proper APT

In this section, we discuss an approach towards proper learning for APT based on convex optimization.
In particular, we aim to devise a projection algorithm which takes as input an improper hypothesis output by one of our algorithms (e.g.,
Sections

5.4
and
5.5
) and returns a valid quantum channel.
We are unable to fully solve this problem due to a difficult-to-verify condition from
[
102
]
, which we rephrase in our setting in as Conjecture
1
.
Nonetheless, we consider this as a significant first step towards resolving this open question, which is also recurrent in much of the realizable process tomography literature that uses Pauli spectrum analysis
[
22
,
24
,
25
]
.
Note that ideas from the quasiprobability decomposition and probabilistic error cancellation which approximate linear superoperators by linear combinations of quantum channels
[
103
,
104
,
105
,
81
,
106
,
107
,
108
,
109
,
110
]
do not help because we need to find exactly a quantum channel.

Suppose that we are given an improper hypothesis
\Psi_{1}
, from an agnostic process tomography algorithm of a concept class
\mathcal{C}
and an unknown channel
\Phi
, with respect to the normalized Frobenius distance
d_{F}
.
Denote the Fourier coefficient matrix of
\Phi
by
\hat{\Phi}
.
Moreover, suppose that our hypothesis is given to us as a Hermitian matrix of Fourier coefficients,
\hat{\Psi}_{1}
.
This aligns with our learning algorithms from
Sections

5.4
and
5.5
, where we learn the Fourier coefficients corresponding to a principle submatrix of
\hat{\Phi}
and simply set the rest to
0
.
To ensure that
\hat{\Psi}_{1}
is Hermitian, we can instead only learn the upper triangular coefficients of this principle submatrix, and set the corresponding coefficients in the lower triangle as their complex conjugates.
From the improper APT guarantee, we have

d_{F}(\Phi,\Psi_{1})\leq\mathsf{opt}(\Phi,\mathcal{C})+\epsilon_{1}.

(5.190)

for some
\epsilon_{1}>0
.
Our goal in the rest of this section will be to construct a
proper
hypothesis satisfying a similar error guarantee.
We break this up into several steps in the following discussion, which we outline below.

1.

First, we derive constraints that the Fourier coefficient matrix must satisfy in order to be a valid quantum channel.
Thus, we can formulate our projection task as a (complex) convex optimization problem.

2.

Second, we map this problem to another convex optimization problem over real, symmetric matrices to allow us to leverage powerful tools in the optimization literature to solve it.

3.

Next, we phrase our problem as a well-studied convex optimization problem with known algorithms and discuss the key condition needed for fast convergence.

4.

Although we may have fast convergence, in a finite number of steps, this does not guarantee
exact
convergence to a quantum channel. By mixing the output of the optimization algorithm with the maximally mixed state, we can guarantee that the resulting matrix corresponds to a quantum channel.

5.

Finally, we complete the rigorous error analysis for this proper hypothesis.

Note that Step
3
is the only step where our proof is incomplete.
Namely, we identify this key mathematical condition (see Conjecture
1
) but are unable to prove (or disprove) that it holds for our problem.

Optimization constraints for quantum channels.

First, we derive the constraints that the Fourier coefficients of any quantum channel must satisfy.
Recall that the Choi representation of a quantum channel can be written as

J(\Phi)=\sum_{x,y}\hat{\Phi}(x,y)J(\Phi_{x,y}),

(5.191)

where
\Phi_{x,y}\triangleq\sigma_{x}(\cdot)\sigma_{y}
.
From
Lemma

1
, we have that

\tr_{1}(J(\Phi))=I.

(5.192)

We first show the effect of tracing out the first register on the Choi representations
J(\Phi_{x,y})
:

\displaystyle\tr_{1}(J(\Phi_{x,y}))

\displaystyle=\tr_{1}\left(\sum_{a,b}\sigma_{x}|a\rangle\langle b|\sigma_{y}\otimes|a\rangle\langle b|\right)

(5.193)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b}\tr(\sigma_x | a \rangle\langle b | \sigma_y)|a\rangle\langle b|

(5.194)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b}(\langle b|\sigma_{y}\sigma_{x}|a\rangle)|a\rangle\langle b|

(5.195)

\displaystyle=(\sigma_{y}\sigma_{x})^{\intercal}

(5.196)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{x}^{\intercal}\sigma_{y}^{\intercal}

(5.197)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{x}^{*}\sigma_{y}^{*},

(5.198)

where in the last line we use the fact that all Pauli strings are Hermitian.
It is easy to verify that complex conjugation leaves the Paulis
I,X,
and
Z
invariant, while
Y^{*}=-Y
.
Thus, we see that
\sigma_{x}^{*}\sigma_{y}^{*}
is equal to
\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}
up to a phase.
We define the phase
\theta(x,y)
by the following equation

\sigma_{x}^{*}\sigma_{y}^{*}=e^{i\theta(x,y)}\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}.

(5.199)

Further, we define the phase
\phi(x,y)
by the following equation.

\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}=e^{i\phi(x,y)}\sigma_{z}.

(5.200)

which is associated with a unique Pauli string
z
for all pairs
x,y
. Now, we can rewrite the partial trace of the Choi representation
J(\Phi)
as follows.

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\tr_{1}(J(\Phi))

(5.201)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}\hat{\Phi}(x,y)\tr_{1}(J(\Phi_{x,y}))

(5.202)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}\hat{\Phi}(x,y)e^{i\theta(x,y)}\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}.

(5.203)

By summing over the terms with
x=y
, we obtain the following constraint

\sum_{x}\hat{\Phi}(x,x)=1,

(5.204)

which can also be seen from
Lemma

3
. Now, for each non-identity Pauli string
z
, we obtain the following constraint

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\\
\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}=e^{i\phi(x,y)}\sigma_{z}\end{subarray}}e^{i\theta(x,y)}e^{i\phi(x,y)}\hat{\Phi}(x,y)=0.

(5.205)

Finally, from
Lemma

1
, we have

\hat{\Phi}\succcurlyeq 0.

(5.206)

Now, the original task of projecting
\hat{\Psi}
reduces to the following optimization problem with respect to the matrix
\hat{\Phi}
.

\min_{\hat{\Phi}}\|\hat{\Psi}-\hat{\Phi}\|_{F}^{2}

(5.207)

subject to the constraints given by
Equations

5.204
,
5.205
and
5.206
. For ease of notation, we will denote the dimensions of
\hat{\Psi}
as
D\times D
for the rest of this discussion and specify them for the concept classes we consider later.

Mapping to Real, Symmetric Matrices.

This problem can be seen as the complex, Hermitian generalization of a corresponding problem in convex optimization over real, symmetric matrices.
We wish to map the problem to one with respect to real, symmetric matrices to leverage tools in convex optimization literature, which typically only deal with this case.
To this end, note that any complex matrix
A
can be decomposed as a sum of its real and complex parts,
A\triangleq X+iY
, where
X
and
Y
are both real.
Then, we can map
A
to the following real operator

M=\begin{pmatrix}X&-Y\\
Y&X\end{pmatrix}

(5.208)

This transformation is well-known, first noted in
[
144
]
, and can be used to transform any complex semidefinite programming problem into its real analogue.
It can be shown that this transformation preserves the properties of the original operator.
For example, a Hermitian operator is mapped to a symmetric one and a Hermitian positive semi-definite operator is mapped to a symmetric positive semi-definite one. Using this transformation, we see that it suffices to solve the following convex optimization problem over reals:

\min_{Q}\|P-Q\|_{F}^{2}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad Q\succcurlyeq 0,\;\tr(A_i^\intercal Q)=b_{i},\;\forall i\in[2D],

(5.209)

where
P,Q,A_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2D\times 2D}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{2D}
, and
P
is a symmetric operator.
Here, the
A_{i}
matrices and the
b
vector need to be chosen suitably to encompass our constraints in
Equations

5.204
,
5.205
and
5.206
after the real mapping.
It is clear that our problem can be written this way for a suitable choice of
A_{i},b
.
This is the problem of finding the nearest projection (with respect to the Frobenius norm) of a symmetric operator onto the intersection of the positive semidefinite cone and an affine subspace.

Convex optimization algorithms and convergence.

In particular, we wish to project onto the intersection of two convex sets, which we denote as
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
.
Explicitly,

C_{1}\triangleq S_{2D}^{+},\quad C_{2}\triangleq\{Q\in\mathbb{R}^{2D\times 2D}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\tr(A_i^\intercal Q)=b_{i},\;\forall i\in[2D]\},

(5.210)

where
S_{2D}^{+}
denotes the positive semidefinite (PSD) cone of
2D\times 2D
matrices.
We can equivalently consider the vectorizations of these sets, where the vectorization of a matrix is defined by combining all columns of the matrix into a single vector.
Vectorization can be inverted when the dimensions of the original matrix are known.
We denote the vectorization operation as
\mathsf{vec}
and its inverse as
\mathsf{vec}^{-1}
.
Then, the sets
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
can be written as

C_{1}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{4D^{2}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathsf{vec}^{-1}(x)\in S_{2D}^{+}\},\quad C_{2}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{4D^{2}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\tilde{A}x=\tilde{b}\},

(5.211)

where we use
\tilde{A}
and
\tilde{b}
to denote the constraints for the vectorized version of
Q
(i.e.,
x
should be though of as
\mathsf{vec}(Q)
for
C_{2}
).

Note that projecting onto either
C_{1}
or
C_{2}
individually is easy (running in time
\mathrm{poly}(D)
) since they are simply the PSD cone and a polyhedral set (see, e.g.,
[
145
]
).
Note that the runtime being in terms of
D
is not a problem for our applications, as we discuss at the end of this section.
Thus, the problem is as follows: how can we use projections onto
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
individually to project onto
C_{1}\cap C_{2}
?
This problem is well-studied in convex optimization, where researchers have developed methods such as alternating projections
[
146
,
147
]
and Dykstra’s algorithm
[
148
]
.
As the name suggests, at a high level, these algorithms work by projecting the input onto
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
in turn.
Classical results in optimization theory show that Dykstra’s algorithm converges to the desired projection
[
148
,
149
,
150
,
151
,
152
]
, and its variants are also widely studied
[
153
,
154
,
155
,
156
,
157
]
; however, researchers have only recently rigorously analyzed its convergence
rate
for non-polyhedral sets
[
102
]
.
Convergence rates have previously been analyzed for the basic alternating projections algorithm
[
158
,
159
,
160
]
and Dykstra’s algorithm for projection onto the intersection of polyhedral sets
[
154
,
161
,
162
]
.
However, the former only guarantees convergence to a point in
C_{1}\cap C_{2}
, rather than the closest one, which would not help for our purposes since we require an error bound.
Also, the latter does not apply to our setting, as
C_{1}=S_{2D}^{+}
is clearly not polyhedral.
In our setting, using Dykstra’s algorithm, one would hope for linear convergence, as this leads to a computationally efficient (in the size of the input matrix) projection algorithm.
In fact,
[
102
]
shows that Dykstra’s algorithm achieves a linear convergence rate under certain conditions.
Namely, they prove the following theorem.

Theorem 10

(Theorem 5.3(i) in
[
102
]
; Simplified)
.

Consider the optimization problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x-x_{0}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad x\in\bigcap_{i=1}^{\ell}C_{i},

(5.212)

where
\ell
is the number of convex sets
C_{i}
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the initial point to be projected.
Suppose the following three conditions hold.

1.

Each
C_{i}
is a
C^{1,1}
-cone reducible closed convex set.

2.

\bigcap_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathrm{ri}(C_{i})\neq\emptyset
, where
\mathrm{ri}
denotes the relative interior.

3.

0\in x^{*}-x_{0}+\mathrm{ri}\left(\partial\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\delta_{C_{i}}\right)(x^{*})\right)
, where
x^{*}
is the optimal projected point,
\partial
denotes the subdifferential, and
\delta_{C_{i}}
is the indicator function of the set
C_{i}
(
0
for inputs inside
C_{i}
and
+\infty
otherwise).

Then, Dykstra’s algorithm produces iterates that linearly converge to the optimal point
x^{*}
.

Note that
[
102
]
considers a more general problem, in which they wish to project onto a point such that a linear transformation of this point lies in the intersection of the convex sets.
For our setting, we can take this transformation to be the identity, in which case the algorithm from
[
102
]
reduces to Dykstra’s algorithm.
Thus, this theorem would give the convergence result we need if its conditions hold.

Now, let us discuss each of the conditions.
First, for Condition
1
, the exact definition of
C^{1,\alpha}
-cone reducibility, for
\alpha\in(0,1]
, is outside of the scope of this work, and we refer to Definition 4.1 in
[
102
]
for the definition.
Importantly, this is an extension of the more common notion of
C^{2}
-cone reducibility
[
163
,
164
]
.
Specifically,
[
102
]
states that any
C^{2}
-cone reducible set is also
C^{1,\alpha}
-cone reducible for all
\alpha\in(0,1]
.
Moreover,
[
163
]
shows that all polyhedrons and the cone of positive semidefinite matrices are both
C^{2}
-cone reducible.
Hence, our convex sets
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are both
C^{2}
-cone reducible and hence
C^{1,\alpha}
-cone reducible for all
\alpha\in(0,1]
.
In particular,
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are both
C^{1,1}
-cone reducible, satisfying Condition
1
.

For Condition
2
, this is a standard assumption in optimization theory, which simply says that there is a strictly feasible point.
This is satisfied for our setting.

Finally, we turn to Condition
3
.
Let us define some of the notation more properly. Here,
\delta_{C_{i}}
is the indicator function of the set
C_{i}
:

\delta_{C_{i}}(x)\triangleq\begin{cases}0&x\in C_{i}\\
+\infty&x\notin C_{i}\end{cases}.

(5.213)

Also,
\partial
denotes the subdifferential.
For a function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{4D^{2}}\to\mathbb{R}
, the subdifferential is defined as

\partial f(x)\triangleq\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{4D^{2}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}f(y)\geq f(x)+(y-x)^{\intercal}z\quad\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{4D^{2}}\}.

(5.214)

Finally, the relative interior for a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{4D^{2}}
is defined as

\mathrm{ri}(S)\triangleq\{x\in S\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\forall y\in S,\;\exists\lambda>1\text{ s.t. }\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y\in S\}.

(5.215)

Because of Condition
2
, standard results in subdifferential calculus (see, e.g.,
[
165
]
) state that

\partial(\delta_{C_{1}}+\delta_{C_{2}})=\partial\delta_{C_{1}}+\partial\delta_{C_{2}}.

(5.216)

Thus, we need to compute the subdifferentials of the indicator functions of our convex sets
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
.
It is well-known in convex optimization that the subdifferential of an indicator function of a set
S
is the normal cone of
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{4D^{2}}
, where the normal cone is defined as

N_{S}(y)\triangleq\{a\in\mathbb{R}^{4D^{2}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}a^{\intercal}y\geq a^{\intercal}x,\quad\forall x\in S\}.

(5.217)

The normal cone of the cone of positive semidefinite matrices is known to be

\delta_{C_{1}}(X)=N_{C_{1}}(X)=\{C\in\mathbb{R}^{2D\times 2D}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}C\preccurlyeq 0,\tr(CX)=0\},

(5.218)

where here
X\in\mathbb{R}^{2D\times 2D}
are matrices.
Writing in terms of vectors using vectorization, we can write

\delta_{C_{1}}(x)=\{c\in\mathbb{R}^{4D^{2}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathsf{vec}^{-1}(c)\preccurlyeq 0,\tr(\mathsf{vec}^{-1}(c)\mathsf{vec}^{-1}(x))=0\},

(5.219)

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{4D^{2}}
.
Moreover, we can compute the normal cone of the polyhedral set
C_{2}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{4D^{2}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\tilde{A}x=\tilde{b}\}
:

\displaystyle\delta_{C_{2}}(x)

\displaystyle=N_{C_{2}}(x)

(5.220)

\displaystyle=\{g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}g^{\intercal}(x-y)\geq 0,\quad\forall y\;\text{ s.t. }\tilde{A}y=\tilde{b}\}

(5.221)

\displaystyle=\{g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}g^{\intercal}z\geq 0,\quad\forall z\in\mathrm{ker}(\tilde{A})\}

(5.222)

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{ker}(\tilde{A}))^{\perp}

(5.223)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{im}(\tilde{A}^{\intercal}).

(5.224)

Here, the second line is the definition of the normal cone.
The third line follows because
x-y
is in the kernel of the matrix
\tilde{A}
since both
x,y\in C_{2}
.
The fifth line follows by the standard result in linear algebra that the orthogonal complement of the kernel space is the image of the transpose.

Now, putting everything together, Condition
3
is as follows.

Conjecture 1
.

Let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{4D^{2}}
be the vectorization of the real-mapped matrix of Fourier coefficients that we wish to project onto that of a valid quantum channel. Let
x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{4D^{2}}
denote the vectorization of the real-mapped matrix of Fourier coefficients of the closest valid quantum channel to
x_{0}
in
2
-norm.
Then, it remains to show the following condition holds.

x_{0}-x^{*}\in\mathrm{ri}\left(\left\{c+\tilde{A}^{\intercal}y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}c,y\in\mathbb{R}^{4D^{2}},\mathsf{vec}^{-1}(c)\preccurlyeq 0,\tr(\mathsf{vec}^{-1}(c)\mathsf{vec}^{-1}(x^*))=0\right\}\right),

(5.225)

where
\mathrm{ri}
denotes the relative interior.

We are unsure if this condition holds (or does not hold) in our setting, but suppose for the rest of this discussion that it does.
Now, by
Theorem

10
, Dykstra’s algorithm converges linearly to the point
x^{*}
, which is the vectorization of the matrix of Fourier coefficients (after being mapped to a real, symmetric matrix) of the closest valid quantum channel to
x_{0}
.
Recall that Dykstra’s algorithm works by repeatedly projecting alternately onto
C_{1}
and then
C_{2}
(with a correction term).
By linear convergence, this converges to some point
\epsilon_{2}
-close in
2
-norm to
x^{*}
in
\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\epsilon_2))
iterations, for some
\epsilon_{2}>0
.
Moreover, suppose without loss of generality that the final projection applied is the one projecting onto
C_{2}
. We will make use of this property in the next step.

Mapping the output of the algorithm back to our original problem for Fourier coefficients of channels gives us a Fourier coefficient matrix
\hat{\Psi}_{2}
. Then, we obtain the following guarantee for
\hat{\Psi}_{2}
.

\frac{\|\hat{\Psi}_{2}-\hat{\Psi}^{*}\|_{F}}{2}\leq\epsilon_{2},

(5.226)

where

\hat{\Psi}^{*}=\argmin_{\Psi\in\text{CPTP}}\|\hat{\Psi}_{1}-\hat{\Psi}\|_{F}.

(5.227)

An immediate application of the triangle inequality for the Frobenius norm then gives us

\frac{\|\hat{\Psi}_{1}-\hat{\Psi}_{2}\|_{F}}{2}\leq\frac{\|\hat{\Psi}_{1}-\hat{\Psi}\|_{F}}{2}+\epsilon_{2},\quad\forall\text{ CPTP maps }\Psi.

(5.228)

Mixing to guarantee that the output is a quantum channel.

Even though
\hat{\Psi}_{2}
has desirable distance properties, as it does not achieve
exact
convergence to the projection, it may not correspond to a CPTP map.
However, as our final projection was onto the set
C_{2}
,
\hat{\Psi}_{2}
is Hermitian and satisfies the affine constraints.
Thus, the only remaining condition necessary to obtain a proper hypothesis is to obtain a positive semidefinite hypothesis.
To ensure that our final hypothesis is PSD while still retaining the other properties, we take inspiration from
[
166
]
and mix it with the maximally mixed state.
In particular, for a suitably chosen
p>0
, our output is

\hat{\Psi}_{3}=(1-p)\hat{\Psi}_{2}+p\frac{I}{D},

(5.229)

where
I
is the
D\times D
identity operator.
To understand the intuition behind mixing, we first note that the maximally mixed state can be viewed as the Fourier coefficient matrix of a CPTP map that applies a Pauli operator uniformly at random.
Thus, it must satisfy all required constraints.
Then, we note that if
p
is taken large enough,
\hat{\Psi}_{3}
also satisfies the constraints.
This can be seen by noting that the convex combination of two points satisfying the affine constraints will also satisfy them. For the positive semidefiniteness, we note that
\hat{\Psi}_{2}
is Hermitian, and we only need to take
p
large enough to make the most negative eigenvalue of
\hat{\Psi}_{2}
map to
0
in the eigenspectrum of
\hat{\Psi_{3}}
.
Using the fact that
\hat{\Psi}_{2}
is not too far from a PSD matrix (
Equation

5.226
), we will then show that
p
does not need to be too large.

First, suppose that
\hat{\Psi}_{2}
does have some negative eigenvalue.
Otherwise, our constraints are already satisfied, and we do not need to proceed further.
Let the negative eigenvalue of
\hat{\Psi}_{2}
with the largest absolute value be
\lambda^{\star}
.
Clearly, by
Equation

5.229
, we need to take
p
large enough such that

(1-p)\lambda^{\star}+p/D\geq 0.

(5.230)

This can be rewritten as

p\geq\frac{D|\lambda^{\star}|}{1+D|\lambda^{\star}|}.

(5.231)

We can now upper bound
|\lambda^{\star}|
as follows.
First, recall that projection of a Hermitian operator onto the positive semidefinite cone is well-known (see, e.g.,
[
145
]
).
In particular, this projection works by taking the positive component of the Hermitian operator’s eigenspectrum and setting the negative eigenvalues to
0
.
For a Hermitian
A
, we denote this by
(A)^{+}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\epsilon_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\|\hat{\Psi}_{2}-\hat{\Psi}^{*}\|_{F}}{2}

(5.232)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\|\hat{\Psi}_{2}-(\hat{\Psi}_{2})^{+}\|_{F}}{2}

(5.233)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\sum_{\lambda_{i}<0}\lambda_{i}^{2}}}{2}

(5.234)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{|\lambda^{\star}|}{2}

(5.235)

where the first line follows from
Equation

5.226
.
The second line follows from the fact that
\hat{\Psi}^{*}
is PSD and
(\hat{\Psi}_{2})^{+}
is the nearest PSD operator to
\hat{\Psi}_{2}
.
In the third line, we use
\lambda_{i}
to denote the eigenvalues of
\hat{\Psi}_{2}
and only sum over the negative ones. In the final line, we ignore all except the negative eigenvalue with the largest absolute value. Thus, we set

p=\frac{2D\epsilon_{2}}{1+2D\epsilon_{2}},

(5.236)

which satisfies
Equation

5.231
.

Error analysis.

Finally, it remains to bound the error between
\hat{\Psi}_{2}
and
\hat{\Psi}_{3}
.

\displaystyle\frac{\|\hat{\Psi}_{2}-\hat{\Psi}_{3}\|_{F}}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|p(\hat{\Psi}_{2}-I/D)\|_{F}}{2}

(5.237)

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\hat{\Psi}_{2}-\frac{I}{D}\right\rVert}_{F}

(5.238)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p}{2}\left(\|\hat{\Psi}_{2}\|_{F}+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\frac{I}{D}\right\rVert}_{F}\right)

(5.239)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p}{2}\left(\|\hat{\Psi}_{2}-\hat{\Psi}^{*}\|_{F}+\|\hat{\Psi}^{*}\|_{F}+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\frac{I}{D}\right\rVert}_{F}\right)

(5.240)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p}{2}\left(2\epsilon_{2}+1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{D}}\right)

(5.241)

\displaystyle\leq 2p

(5.242)

\displaystyle=\frac{4D\epsilon_{2}}{1+2D\epsilon_{2}},

(5.243)

where in the first line we use
Equation

5.229
. In the third and fourth lines, we use the triangle inequality for the Frobenius norm. In the fifth line, we use
Equation

5.226
, the fact that
\|\hat{\Psi}^{*}\|_{F}\leq 1
for a CPTP map 1, and we compute the Frobenius norm of the identity operator. In the sixth line, we take a loose upper bound on these quantities, and finally, in the last line we use
Equation

5.236
. Finally, we need this error to be at most
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{1})
. Then, by taking
\epsilon_{2}\leq\epsilon_{1}/4D
, we obtain

\frac{\|\hat{\Psi}_{2}-\hat{\Psi}_{3}\|_{F}}{2}\leq\epsilon_{1}.

(5.244)

Then, using the triangle inequality for
d_{F}
and combining
Equations

5.190
,
5.228
and
5.244
, we obtain

d_{F}(\Phi,\Psi_{3})\leq 2\cdot\mathsf{opt}(\Phi,\mathcal{C})+3\epsilon_{1},

(5.245)

which is the desired bound, up to constant factors for
\epsilon_{1}
. Here, we implicitly make use of the fact that
d_{F}(\Phi,\Psi)=\frac{\|\hat{\Phi}-\hat{\Psi}\|_{F}}{2}
for any quantum channels
\Phi,\Psi
, which can be seen from
Lemma

4
.

We can now bound the time complexity of this method. In order to mix the Fourier coefficient matrix, we need to compute the smallest eigenvalue of
\hat{\Psi}_{2}
. We also require making alternating projections. Each of these steps can be done in time
\mathsf{poly}(D)
.
Moreover, due to linear convergence, each projection needs to be carried out
\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\epsilon_2))=\mathcal{O}(\log(D/\epsilon_1))
times.
This gives us a final time complexity of
\mathsf{poly}(D)\log(1/\epsilon_1)
.
Again, this analysis only holds assuming that Conjecture
1
holds.

For
k
-junta channels, the principle submatrix that we optimize over would be of dimension
4^{k}\times 4^{k}
, giving us a time complexity
\mathsf{poly}(4^{k})\log(1/\epsilon_1)
. Similarly, the dimension for low-degree channels would be at most
n^{k}4^{k}\times n^{k}4^{k}
, giving us a time complexity
\mathsf{poly}(n^{k}4^{k})\log(1/\epsilon_1)
.

Thus, assuming Conjecture
1
holds, we can efficiently obtain proper
2
-agnostic process tomography of junta channels and low-degree channels by applying this method to the outputs of
Theorems

7
and
8
respectively.
