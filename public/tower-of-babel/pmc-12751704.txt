Affect valuations predict emotional response to satisfying and disappointing exam scores
Authors: Sunkyung Yoon, Jonathan Rottenberg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDoes what a person desires to feel (affect valuation) predict their future affective reaction to salient life events? We tested this idea in the context of an exam, a key achievement-oriented event for college students.MethodsOne to two weeks prior to taking an exam (at baseline), 177 university students rated their ideal affect, depression and anxiety symptom severities, and provided affective forecasts for how they would react if they did or did not achieve their expected exam scores. On the day when students received their exam scores, they rated their actual emotional response to the exam outcomes.ResultsHigher levels of baseline ideal positive affect predicted greater positive affective responses to receiving satisfying exam scores. This prediction held even after controlling for affective forecasting, depression and anxiety levels, and the exam score itself. Higher levels of baseline ideal negative affect predicted greater negative affective reactions to receiving disappointing exam scores, but did not survive parallel statistical controls.ConclusionsThese findings suggest motivational functions for ideal affect, particularly for positive affective states.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03759-w.Keywords: Ideal affect, Affective forecasting, Depression, Emotional response, Cognition

Can what we ideally want to feel predict our actual emotional experiences? Ideal affect refers to the affective states people ideally desire to feel rather than actual affective states [1]. Although ideal affect reflects expectations of an important aspect of emotional self and is shaped by cultural influences, it is not the same as cultural norms [1] and is also distinct from broader dispositional traits such as optimism or pessimism, which pertain to generalized expectations about positivity or negativity in life [2]. Theories of self and affect regulation [3, 4] propose that people are motivated to reduce discrepancies between valued or desired internal states and their current states.

Consistent with this notion, two prominent theoretical frameworks help to explain how ideal affect may shape emotional experience. Affect valuation theory (AVT) [1] posits that ideal affect serves important motivational goals that guide one’s regulatory efforts, increasing the likelihood of attaining ideal states and ultimately influencing actual affect. Similarly, expectancy-value models of motivation [5] suggest that the values individuals place on desired outcomes (e.g., affective states) motivates behavioral preference that facilitate the achievement of those outcomes. For example, a person who strongly values excitement may choose to attend a party with friends rather than watch a movie alone. During the party, such a person might be predisposed to upregulate their desired emotions, experiencing greater excitement than someone who prefers less excitement. Despite the theoretical salience of ideal affect in shaping motivated behaviors and subsequent emotional responses, little empirical work has examined the role of ideal affect in shaping downstream emotional responses.

Most research on ideal affect has focused on cross-cultural differences in positive affect valuation [6]. Although correlations between ideal positive affect (PA) and actual experiences have been tested within a given cultural context, the findings have been mixed—some studies report significant associations [7], while others do not [8]. Far less research has examined the temporal relationship between ideal affect and actual emotional responses to a specific event. For example, baseline desire for PA of college students predicted lower levels of psychological distress during an exam period, whereas baseline desire for negative affect (NA) was unrelated to later distress [9] In another ecological momentary assessment study, participants tended to be motivated to match their current emotional states with their ideal PA [10]. Although important first steps, these studies were uncontrolled, meaning they did not tie reactivity to specific emotional events.

More directly, Chim et al. [11] examined if ideal high arousal positive affect (HAP) and low arousal positive affect (LAP) predicted enjoyment of activities that elicited HAP and LAP, respectively. The results showed that the more a person valued LAP, the more the person enjoyed a LAP-related activity versus a HAP-related one. However, in this study, ideal HAP did not predict enjoyment of HAP-related activities. The authors explained that their null result may have been due to their use of insufficiently powerful HAP-related activities (e.g., amusement park rides and exercise). They suggested that, to examine the connection to ideal HAP, it might be useful to elicit HAP via target events relevant to personal achievement, such as an exam.

Therefore, the current investigation extended prior work on ideal affect by examining if ideal affect predicts emotional response to a college exam, a salient and relatively standardized achievement-related event. In this study, a negatively-valenced exam-related event (hereafter, negative event) was defined as receiving a disappointing exam score, based on students’ subjective perception of having received a lower grade than expected; a positively-valenced event (hereafter, positive event) was defined as receiving a satisfying exam score (grade equal to or higher than expected). Advantages of this operationalization are that exam scores represent personally salient outcomes for college students, making them appropriate emotion-inducing events in the achievement domain. Further, this approach is consistent with appraisal theories of emotion, which emphasize the role of subjective evaluations of the environment in shaping emotional responses [12], as well as prior research on affective forecasting that examined expectation-laden events such as exams [13] or electoral outcomes [14].

Building on AVT [1], expectancy-value models of motivation [5], and prior works [9–11], we hypothesized that higher levels of ideal affect—particularly ideal PA—would predict greater experiences of desired states, as individuals would be more motivated to align their current affective experiences with their ideal states. An alternative prediction might be that excessively valuing happiness could have paradoxical effects of reducing actual experiences of PA [15]. We discounted this possibility because a previous study using factor analysis showed that only a specific aspect of extreme valuation—worry about not being able to feel happy—is associated with the paradoxical effect (e.g., lower well-being) [16]. Since other aspects of the valuation of happiness are positively associated with PA, we hypothesized that valuing PA would be associated with greater PA in response to a positive event.

To provide a more stringent test of ideal affect as a predictor of actual affect, our hypotheses were modeled with additional variables. These included students’ forecasted affect about their reactions to the exam [17]. Forecasted affect is expected to predict the student’s actual emotional response, as it is based on extensive past experiences, even if research shows that error is often present in these predictions [18]. According to the expectancy-value models [19], both value and expectancy independently influence behavior—with expectancy referring to the perceived likelihood of achieving a desired outcome. Plausibly, ideal affect (value) would remain a significant predictor of emotional responses to exam results, even after controlling for forecasted affect (expectancy).

Second, we assessed self-reported depression and anxiety symptoms both as an indicator of current affect and because prior research suggests that these symptoms can influence emotional responses to emotional events [20]. Given that AVT posits that ideal affect shapes one’s behaviors and responses beyond current affect and other temperamental factors, such as neuroticism [21], we predicted that ideal affect would link to emotional responses to exam outcomes, independent of baseline depressive and anxiety symptoms. Finally, we had access to actual exam scores, and these scores were included in our models, assuming that higher exam scores would predict stronger PA whereas lower exam grades would predict stronger NA responses. Thus, we explored whether ideal affect continued to predict emotional responses even after controlling for actual exam scores.

Furthermore, using an exam—an achievement-related event—we aimed to examine whether ideal HAP predicts HAP responses to a positive event, a pattern not observed in the Chim et al. [11] study that used non-achievement-related activities as target events (e.g., amusement park rides). Given that achievement-related events, such as exams, are typically regarded as more HAP-inducing [11, 22], a positive exam outcome provides a sufficiently strong HAP-eliciting context to test our hypothesis that baseline ideal HAP would significantly predict stronger HAP responses to a positive event. As for ideal NA, AVT might similarly predict that ideal NA would predict stronger NA responses to negative exam outcomes [6]. However, because prior research has not supported the idea that ideal NA predicts future distress during an exam period [9], we took an exploratory approach to examine ideal NA and NA responses to a negative event.

In sum, this investigation tested if ideal affect predicts emotional responses to a specific exam outcome, including whether it provides incremental prediction over other variables that could account for affective reactions. The following hypotheses were tested:

Higher levels of ideal PA would predict greater actual PA response to a positive event, and this prediction would remain significant even after controlling for forecasted PA, levels of depression and anxiety, and actual exam scores.

Higher levels of ideal PA would predict greater actual PA response to a positive event, and this prediction would remain significant even after controlling for forecasted PA, levels of depression and anxiety, and actual exam scores.

Higher levels of ideal HAP would predict greater actual HAP responses to a positive event, and this prediction would remain significant even when controlling for forecasted HAP, levels of depression and anxiety, actual exam scores, and ideal LAP.

Higher levels of ideal HAP would predict greater actual HAP responses to a positive event, and this prediction would remain significant even when controlling for forecasted HAP, levels of depression and anxiety, actual exam scores, and ideal LAP.

(Exploratory Aim I) We explored whether higher levels of ideal NA would predict greater actual NA responses to a negative event.

Method

Sampling and participants

Although there were no precise matches in the literature using our predictors and outcomes on which to base sample size estimates, sample sizes were based on Scollon et al. [8] in which the correlations between baseline ideal affect and affective states were measured. With the effect size of r =.30, the estimated sample size was 82 (G*Power 3 software, α = 0.05, two-tailed, power set at 0.80). Assuming a drop-out rate of 20% and an equal chance of positive and negative events, we aimed to recruit ~ 200 students.

Participants were students enrolled in an introductory psychology course of a university in the United States. One hundred ninety-two students participated in the Time 1 (T1) and 185 students completed the Time 2 survey (T2). At T2, one student was excluded for not taking an exam and seven students were excluded due to missing data, leaving 177 students for the current study. All students reported at T1 that the upcoming exam results were more than somewhat important (above 3 on a 5-point Likert scale; 1, not at all; 5 extremely important). Participants were mostly females (75.1%), the mean age was 18.81 years (SD = 1.46), and 50.8% Caucasians, 18.6% Hispanic, 8.5% African Americans, 8.5% Asians, and 13.6% others. All the participants had four to five exams for the course. To minimize uncertainty associated with a first-time exam, data were collected from the third exam. The data, analysis code, and the full list of questionnaires assessed in this project, including those not used in the current study, are available at https://osf.io/xv7kr/?view_only=e50c5930e05d4f6c89835c6c63894590.

Using G*Power 3, we conducted a post-hoc sensitivity power analysis to determine the smallest detectable effect size given our final sample sizes for positive and negative events. Results indicated that, with sample sizes of 86 and 91 for positive and negative events, respectively, and six predictors including one focal predictor, we had 80% power to detect small to medium effect sizes (f² = 0.09 for both events).

Instruments

Ideal affect

Ideal PA and NA were measured using a modified Affect Valuation Index [23], to allow us to model additional test-relevant emotional reactions [24]. Ideal PA was computed as the average of the following items: enthusiastic, satisfied, excited, joy, elated, relief, calm, at rest, serene, happy. Ideal NA was computed as the average of the following items: depressed, ashamed, disappointed, fearful, sluggish, angry, sad, nervous, lonely, bored. Participants were asked how much they ideally want to feel these emotions in a typical week, on a 5-point Likert scale (1, not at all; 5, extremely). Our analyses of ideal HAP and LAP, as well as ideal HAN and LAN, focused on subsets of these items. Ideal HAP was the mean of enthusiastic, excited, elated, and joy, based on Fredrickson [25]. Ideal LAP was the mean of relief, calm, at rest and serene, based on Chim et al. [11]. Following Tsai et al. [20], ideal HAN was computed from angry, fearful, and nervous, and ideal LAN from sluggish, bored, and depressed. Cronbach’s alphas in the current sample were 0.78 for ideal PA, 0.70 for ideal HAP, 0.60 for ideal LAP, 0.90 for ideal NA, 0.65 for ideal HAN, and 0.76 for ideal LAN.

Forecasted affect

Our analyses of forecasted affect used the same affective states as the analyses of ideal affect. Participants were asked how much they would feel each emotion if (1) their exam score was similar or better (a positive event) and (2) worse than predicted (a negative event), on a 5-point Likert scale (1, not at all; 5, extremely). For the models, we used forecasted PA and HAP for the positive event, and forecasted NA and HAN for the negative event, with each variable computed by averaging the items within its respective scale or subscale, consistent with our scoring of ideal affect. Cronbach’s alphas in this sample were 0.82 for forecasted PA to a positive event, 0.76 for forecasted HAP, and 0.85 for forecasted NA to a negative event, and 0.76 for forecasted HAN.

Depression and anxiety

Depression symptom severity was assessed with the Inventory to Diagnose Depression (IDD) [26], a 22-item self-report scale in which participants indicate the extent to which they experience depressive symptoms on a 5-point Likert scale (e.g., 0 = My mood is not lower than normal; 4 = My mood is so low I have trouble getting up in the morning). One item regarding suicidality was removed due to institutional review board concern, resulting in 21 items. Higher scores on the IDD indicate higher levels of depression during the last two weeks. In the current study, participants’ mean depression score was 14.9 (SD = 8.70; range 1–63), which is similar to the average IDD scores (using all 22 items) of college students in previous studies [27, 28]. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.84 in this sample.

We assessed anxiety symptom severities, using the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI), a 21-item self-report questionnaire [29]. Participants rated how much they had been bothered by each anxiety symptom (e.g., numbness or tingling) over the past month on a 4-point Likert scale (0–3), with higher scores indicating greater anxiety severity. The average anxiety score among participants was 16.43 (SD = 10.44, range = 0–54), corresponding to a moderate level of anxiety [29]. This elevated anxiety in the current sample may be attributable to the fact that the study was conducted during exam periods. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.90 in this sample.

Actual emotional response

PA and NA responses likewise used the same set of emotions. On the day when the exam results came out (T2), participants were asked to rate the degree to which they were experiencing each emotion when thinking of their exam score on a 5-point Likert scale (1, not at all; 5, extremely). Cronbach’s alphas were 0.96 for PA, 0.93 for NA, 0.88 for HAP, and 0.79 for HAN responses.

Procedure

One to two weeks before the exam (T1), participants attended an in-person lab study. After providing informed consent, participants were instructed about each questionnaire and told they would receive text messages with the follow-up study link when their exam score was released and should complete it within 24 h after they received the text message. Participants completed the IDD, the BAI, and ideal affect questionnaires. To give participants a specific goal, they were asked to report their expected exam score for the following exam. Next, participants completed affective forecasting questionnaires, imagining how they would feel if they received a score that was similar to, greater than, or lower than their expectation. On the day exam scores were released (T2), participants received text messages with a link to the online survey, administered via Qualtrics. In the online survey, after reporting their exam score, participants stated whether it was similar to/higher than, or lower than their expectations. Based on these responses, we classified the events as positive or negative. Lastly, they were asked to rate their emotional responses to the score. To check if participants were responding without reading, there was a mock question of selecting number 3. All the participants correctly chose the number 3.

Results

A positive event was coded to when students received a satisfying exam score at T2 (86 students), whereas a negative event was coded when students received disappointing exam scores at T2 (91 students). Students in the two conditions did not significantly differ in terms of sex, ethnicity ratio, age, depression, nor on any ideal affect nor affective forecasting score. Not surprisingly, the average actual exam score of participants in the positive event condition (i.e., those who reported receiving a score similar to or higher than expected) was higher than that of those in the negative event condition. To examine whether ideal PA predicted positive affective responses to receiving satisfying results, a simple regression analysis was run with ideal PA as a predictor. Consistent with our first hypothesis, students who had higher level of ideal PA exhibited greater PA responses to a positive event, F(1, 84) = 6.74, p =.011, R2 = 0.07, β = 0.27, t = 2.59, p =.011. Next, a hierarchical regression analysis was performed.1 In this analysis, covariate (depression, anxiety, forecasted PA to a positive event, and actual exam scores) were entered first (Model 1), followed by ideal PA (Model 2). Model 1 explained about 32.5% of variance in PA response. Importantly, when ideal PA was added, the increase in explained variance was small but significant, ΔR² = 0.03, bringing Model 2 to 36.3% of the variance explained. Higher levels of ideal PA remained a significant predictor of stronger PA responses to satisfying exam results, even after controlling for all covariates, B = 0.34, 95% CI [0.03, 0.65], t = 2.19, p =.032. Post-hoc analyses with alternative event definitions

In this study, our a priori definition of emotional events was based on participants’ perceived outcomes—specifically, whether students reported that their exam score was satisfying or disappointing upon receipt. This operationalization fits our primary focus on perceived exam outcomes. That said, a good case could be made for using more objective definitions of positive and negative exam events, especially since there is no consensus in this area. Thus, to be fully comprehensive, we conducted additional post-hoc analyses using an alternative event metric. Specifically, we used the discrepancy between students’ expected exam scores (at T1) and their actual exam scores (at T2). Positive events were those in which students’ actual exam scores were the same as or higher than their previously reported expected scores, while negative events were those in which scores were lower than the previously reported expectation.

With this alternative metric, 64 students were classified as having experienced a positive event, and 113 students as a negative event. Results from a series of hierarchical regression analyses using the alternative metric yielded results broadly consistent with our prior primary analyses. Specially, ideal HAP significantly predicted greater HAP responses, even after accounting for relevant covariates, F(5, 57) = 2.19, p =.056, R2 = 0.19, ΔR² = 0.07, β = 0.30, t = 2.15, p =.036. Detailed results are presented in the Supplementary Material Table S2. In contrast, neither ideal PA, ideal NA, nor ideal HAN significantly explained additional variance in emotional responses beyond other relevant variables. The change in R² was not significant after adding ideal PA, ΔR² = 0.02, ΔF = 1.59, p =.212, after entering ideal NA, ΔR² = 0.00, ΔF = 0.03, p =.854, or after adding ideal HAN in its respective model, ΔR² = 0.001, ΔF = 0.15, p =.697. It should be noted that the models may have been underpowered for positive events, as the sample size (N = 64) was lower than what the priori power analysis indicated (N = 82).

Discussion

In this study, we examined whether ideal affect is prospectively associated with students’ emotional responses to satisfying or disappointing exam outcomes, over and above other variables that could plausibly account for those responses. Three main findings stood out. First, higher levels of ideal PA alone were prospectively associated with stronger PA response to receiving satisfying exam results. This association remained significant even after controlling for the students’ forecasted affective reactions to satisfactory exam outcomes, depression and anxiety levels, and actual exam scores. Second, higher levels of ideal HAP were linked to greater HAP response to satisfying exam scores, even after accounting for the same covariates and for ideal LAP. Lastly, in contrast, analyses of ideal NA were less conclusive: although higher levels of ideal NA were significantly associated with stronger NA response to disappointing exam scores, but this effect did not survive application of similar statistical controls.

These findings hold theoretical significance for AVT. Specifically, ideal PA was associated with subsequent affective responses to exam outcomes, aligning with aspects of AVT. Importantly, ideal PA had incremental predictive value for PA response to a positive event, even after accounting for variable with more proximal associations with PA responses (forecasted PA, depression and anxiety, and exam scores). This raises a question: how might one’s general valuation of affect relate to actual affective responses to emotional events? Because our study was not designed to address the question of what mediates the relationship between ideal PA and PA responses to a positive event, our discussion of candidate mediators is intended to be speculative and to highlight potential hypotheses to be tested in future research.

Most plausibly, AVT (1) and expectancy-value models of motivation (2) place stock in behaviorally-based and emotion-regulatory mechanisms. The logic of behaviorally-based mechanisms is that ideal affect motivates people to pursue a behavior that can bring about their desired emotional state. Prior research in education has shown that placing higher value on studying itself or on its outcomes is significantly associated with greater study efforts [30]. In this context, a person with higher levels of ideal PA might exert more effort in studying in order to achieve satisfying exam outcomes that align with their desired emotional state. When achieving desired exam outcomes, the person may feel happier both from realizing their valued states and from the sense of accomplishment derived from greater behavioral efforts, compared to those with lower levels of ideal PA. As an exploratory analysis, we tested whether ideal affect would predict exam scores, which may reflect some aspects of study effort, and found no significant association (see Supplementary Materials for details). However, future research is needed to test the behavioral-effort account more rigorously, using more appropriate indicators of effort, such as time spent studying and subjectively perceived effort during exam preparation.

A second plausible mechanism is emotion regulation. The logic of the emotion regulation mechanism is that ideal affect may influence emotional response through individuals’ regulatory efforts to match their current emotions with ideal affect [31]. For example, individuals with lower levels of ideal NA may down-regulate their NA response to exam disappointment to match with their ideal NA states, whereas those with higher levels of ideal PA may upregulate their PA responses to satisfying results. Indeed, several prior studies have shown that ideal affect predicts emotion regulation [31]. For instance, individuals who generally wanted to feel less PA in daily life were less likely to choose to increase their emotional reactions to happy images in a laboratory task [9]. Now that we have demonstrated that ideal affect predicts affective responding to a specific event, future studies should investigate explicitly which of the two possible processes—behavioral efforts or ER—plays a more important role in the relationship between ideal PA and emotional responses to positive events.

Our focal analyses of ideal HAP also extended Chim et al. [9]. In their study, the authors found that higher levels of ideal LAP predicted greater enjoyment of LAP-eliciting activities. However, the same result did not hold for ideal HAP. The authors suggested that HAP-eliciting events should involve personal achievements such as receiving satisfying grades. Using an achievement-related target event, we found that only ideal HAP, not ideal LAP, was prospectively associated with HAP response to achieving desired exam scores (again after controlling for relevant variables). Thus, consistent with Chim et al. [11]’s reasoning, personal achievement may be particularly related to ideal HAP, and ideal HAP in these contexts will predict how people will react when goals are achieved.

Although greater ideal NA was initially linked to stronger NA responses to a negative event, this association no longer held after controlling for other relevant variables. One possible reason why ideal NA had weaker effects on NA responses is that valuing NA may serve some adaptive functions in regulating distress when negative events occur. For example, higher levels of NA valuation might help reduce the discrepancy between ideal and actual NA during negative events, with larger discrepancies being linked to greater distress [6]. Future research should investigate when and how ideal NA amplifies or buffers emotional responses to negative events.

Notably, higher levels of forecasted NA as well as lower exam score significantly predicted greater NA response to disappointing outcomes, explaining approximately 51% of the variance. Literature on forecasted affect mainly focused on errors in forecasting [18]. However, the current results partially converge with previous research showing that anticipated NA regarding a future exam grade is generally associated with more intense NA responses to a stressor [32]. These results support the affective expectation model [33]. This model posits that a person’s expectations about affect influence their actual affective experiences by reinforcing existing expectations—especially when the actual experience aligns with those expectations. Consistent with this model, our findings demonstrated that a person’s prediction of affect—particularly NA in response to a negative future event—was a stronger and significant predictor of emotional responses to the predicted event.

Our conclusions should be tempered in light of study limitations. First, this study is correlational. An experimental manipulation of ideal affect would be far stronger for demonstrating causal relationships between ideal affect and subsequent affective responses. Second, although analyses controlled for multiple variables, it was not possible to include all potentially relevant variables for practical reasons. Unassessed variables included baseline actual/trait affect (although depression and anxiety at T1 were assessed), avoided affect (affect states a person wants to avoid) [34], and cultural orientation (e.g., individualism vs. collectivism). These factors should be considered in future work. Third, although our sample size was determined based on an a priori power analysis, post-hoc sensitivity analysis indicated modest power to detect small effect sizes. Finally, the generalizability of these findings remains to be established. The study relied on self-report questionnaires to assess affect in a specific context, focusing on exam-related positive and negative events among college students (young adults) in a Western cultural setting. Future research should examine whether these findings hold across different age groups, cultural contexts, and types of emotional events, as well as when using other measures of affect (e.g., physiological measures).

Conclusions

Affect valuation theory is an influential explanatory theory in emotion but there have been surprisingly few demonstrations that AVT can explain how people react to events. Here we found that the value that individuals attach to PA was prospectively associated with how strongly they react to future achievement-related emotional events. Affective valuations were linked to students’ reactions even after considering objective achievement (e.g., actual exam scores). AVT has potential utility for explaining individual differences in affective response.
