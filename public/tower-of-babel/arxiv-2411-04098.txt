Interpretable and efficient data-driven Discovery and Control of distributed systems
Florian Wolf, Nicolò Botteghi, Urban Fasel, Andrea Manzoni

Abstract
Effectively controlling systems governed by Partial Differential Equations (PDEs) is crucial in several fields of Applied Sciences and Engineering. These systems usually yield significant challenges to conventional control schemes due to their nonlinear dynamics, partial observability, high-dimensionality once discretized, distributed nature, and the requirement for low-latency feedback control. Reinforcement Learning (RL), particularly Deep RL (DRL), has recently emerged as a promising control paradigm for such systems, demonstrating exceptional capabilities in managing high-dimensional, nonlinear dynamics. However, DRL faces challenges including sample inefficiency, robustness issues, and an overall lack of interpretability. To address these issues, we propose a data-efficient, interpretable, and scalable Dyna-style Model-Based RL framework for PDE control, combining the Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics with Control ( SINDy-C ) algorithm and an autoencoder (AE) framework for the sake of dimensionality reduction of PDE states and actions. This novel approach enables fast rollouts, reducing the need for extensive environment interactions, and provides an interpretable latent space representation of the PDE forward dynamics. We validate our method on two PDE problems describing fluid flows – namely, the 1D Burgers equation and 2D Navier-Stokes equations – comparing it against a model-free baseline, and carrying out an extensive analysis of the learned dynamics.

Background and Related Work

2.1
Problem setting

In this work, we address the problem of controlling a nonlinear, distributed,
dynamical system described by the equation:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}(t)=f(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}(t),\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}(t)),

where the state
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{x}}
and control input
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{u}}

are continuous variables,
with potentially very large dimensions
{N_{x}}
and
{N_{u}}
, respectively.

The function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{x}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{u}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{l}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{x}}
is assumed to
be unknown, but we can observe a time-discrete evolution of the system, resulting in a sequence of
(partially or fully) observable measurements
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}_{t},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}_{t+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}_{t+H}
of
the state over a horizon
H\in{\mathbb{N}}_{+}
. A system is fully-observable (FO) if the observations

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{x}}
allow to observe each (full) state of the system directly, i.e.,
{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Obs}}}={N_{x}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i}
. On the other hand,
a system is partially-observable (PO) if only a limited number of sensors is available,
resulting in a lower-dimensional observation space
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Obs}}}
, where

{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Obs}}}\ll{N_{x}}
that does not capture the full state of the PDE.
In this work, we consider both FO and PO systems. Similarly, we assume a limited number of actuators are
available to control the system. The combination of nonlinear system dynamics with a limited number of state sensors and
control actuators entails a complex and challenging problem in PDE control.

2.2
Reinforcement Learning

Reinforcement learning (RL)
is a general framework for solving sequential decision-making processes. RL has been applied to a variety
of different tasks, including natural language processing for dialogue generation

[
Ope+24
]

and text summarization
[
Li+16
]
, computer vision for object detection
and image classification
[
Mni+13
]
, robotics for autonomous control and
manipulation
[
Lev+16
]
, finance for portfolio management and
trading strategies
[
JXL17
]
, and game playing for mastering complex
strategic games
[
Sil+16
]
.
These applications highlight the broad versatility and significant impact of
RL across multiple fields.

RL is the subset of machine learning that focuses on training agents to make
decisions by interacting with an environment.
The RL framework is typically modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), defined by the
tuple
\mathcal{M}:=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{R},\gamma)
, where

\mathcal{X}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{x}}
is the set of observable states,

\mathcal{U}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{u}}
is the set of actions,

\mathcal{P}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}\to[0,1]
is the transition
probability kernel with
\mathcal{P}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}\right)
representing
the probability of reaching the state
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
while being in the state

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}
and applying action
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}\in\mathcal{U}
,
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is the reward function, and
\gamma\in(0,1]
is the discount
factor.

The goal of an RL agent is to learn an optimal policy
\pi

that maps states
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}
of the environment to actions
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}
in a way that maximizes the expected cumulative reward
over time a control horizon
H
:

\displaystyle R_{H}={\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{H}\gamma^{t}r_{t}\right],

(1)

with rewards
r_{t}
collected at timesteps
t
and a control horizon
H
being finite or
infinite – in this work we focus on finite control horizons.
RL agents often optimize the policy by approximating either the value function
V:\mathcal{X}\to{\mathbb{R}}

or the action-value function
Q:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}\to{\mathbb{R}}
to quantify, and optimize the
cumulative reward
eq.

1
for a state
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}

or a state-action pair
(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}
,
respectively. When dealing with high-dimensional state spaces, continuous actions, and nonlinear dynamics,
the estimation of the value function becomes a very challenging and data-inefficient optimization problem.

In general, we distinguish between
model-free
and
model-based
RL algorithms.
Model-free algorithms do not assume any explicit model of the system dynamics, and aim at optimizing
the policy directly by interacting with the environment. This approach offers more flexibility and
is more robust to model inaccuracies but usually requires a large number of interactions to achieve
good performance, making it difficult to apply in cases of data sparsity or expensive interactions.
On the other hand, model-based RL algorithms internally create a model of the environment’s dynamics,
i.e., the transition probability kernel
\mathcal{P}
and the reward function
\mathcal{R}
.
5
5

5

Note that in this work we only consider the case of a known full-order reward functions.

The agent leverages this model to simulate the environment and plan its actions accordingly, typically
resulting in more sample-efficient training.
We focus on Dyna-style
[
Sut91
]

RL algorithms which learn a surrogate model of the system dynamics,
allowing the agent to train on an approximation of the environment and thus requiring
fewer data.

A general scheme of the RL cycle we used for training, including a Dyna-style surrogate dynamics
model of the environment is shown in
fig.

1
.
Since this work focuses on Dyna-style RL algorithms with the surrogate model being the center
of this work, we use the proximal-policy optimization (PPO) algorithm
[
Sch+17
]

as a state-of-the-art actor-critic algorithm.
Actor-critic methods learn both the value-function, i.e., the critic, and the policy, i.e., the actor, at the same time and have shown very promising results in the last years.

2.3
SINDy: Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics

We briefly review two versions of
sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics

(SINDy) used in the SINDy-RL
[
Zol+24
]
and AE+SINDy

[
Cha+19
]
frameworks. SINDy
[
BPK16
]
is an extremely versatile and popular dictionary learning method, that is, a data-driven algorithm aiming at approximating a
nonlinear function through a dictionary of user-defined functions. In particular, SINDy assumes that this approximation takes the form of a sparse
linear combination of (potentially nonlinear)
candidate functions, such as polynomials of degree
p
or trigonometric functions.

In its general formulation, we are given a batch of
N
data points

(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}_{1}),\ldots,(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{N},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}_{N})
with

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
governed by the relationship

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}_{k}=f(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{k})
, for
k=1,\ldots,N
. With

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{X}}

\displaystyle=\left[\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{N}\right]^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times m},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{Y}}

\displaystyle=\left[\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}_{N}\right]^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times n},

and a set of
d
candidate functions

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Theta}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{X}})=\left[\theta_{1}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}),\ldots,\theta_{d}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})\right]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times d}
,
we aim to find a representation of the form

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{Y}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Theta}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{X}})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Xi}},

(2)

where
\mathbf{\Xi}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times n}
are the coefficients to be fit. These are usually
trained in a Lasso-style optimization since we assume sparsity, i.e., the system
dynamics can be sufficiently represented by a small subset of terms in the library.
Overall, we optimize (a variation of) the following loss:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Xi}}=\argmin_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Xi}}}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{Y}}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Theta}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{X}})\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Xi}}}\right\rVert_{F}+\mathcal{L}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Xi}}}),

(3)

with a regularization term
\mathcal{L}
promoting sparsity, i.e., usually

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Xi}})=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Xi}}\right\rVert_{1}
. While in

[
Cha+19
]
and
[
Zol+24
]
the loss

eq.

3
is trained by an
L^{2}
penalty
and sequential thresholding least squares (STLS), we use
PyTorch
’s automatic differentiation
framework
[
Pas+17
]
and optimize the
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1}
-loss directly.

2.3.1
SINDy-RL

SINDy-RL is a very recent extension of SINDy that combines SINDy with a
RL algorithm for Dyna-style RL
[
Zol+24
,
ASM22
]
.
This approach involves using SINDy to
learn a surrogate model of the environment and then train the RL agent using this approximation of
the full-order model. This method results in a drastically-reduced number of necessary full-order
interactions and very fast convergence. Specifically, Zolman et al.
[
Zol+24
]
work with the discrete

SINDy-C
formulation, i.e., including controls,
for the discovery task
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{k+1}=f(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{k})
,
where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{X}}=(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}(t_{k}),\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}(t_{k}))_{k=1,\ldots,N}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{Y}}=(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}(t_{k+1}))_{k=1,\ldots,N}
in the notation
of
eq.

2
. Note that since in our work we focus on enabling efficient surrogate representations for controlling distributed systems,
we do not further review the reward approximation nor the policy approximation aspects addressed in
[
Zol+24
]
.

Despite significantly reducing the number of full-order interactions, not requiring derivatives of
(potentially) noisy measurements, and incorporating controls in the surrogate model, so far SINDy-RL lacks the scalability to high-dimensional systems (see

[
Zol+24
, section 6]
).
While it shows convincing results for state spaces with up to eight dimensions and action spaces with
up to two dimensions, controlling distributed systems require much larger state and action spaces to be
solved and controlled sufficiently accurately.

2.3.2
AE+SINDy
: Autoencoder Framework for Data-driven Discovery in
High-dimensional Spaces

In their work
[
Cha+19
]
, Champion et al. rely on the classical SINDy formulation for the discovery task in the latent space.
Indeed, they generalize the concept of data-driven dynamics discovery to
high-dimensional dynamical systems by combining the classical SINDy formulation with an

[

,

,

]
to allow for nonlinear dimensionality reduction.
Hence, instead of directly working with high-dimensional systems, the SINDy algorithm is applied to the compressed, lower-dimensional representation of the system with

{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Lat}}}\ll{N_{x}}
.

Given the encoder
{\varphi}(\;\cdot\;,\mathbf{W}_{\varphi}):{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{x}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Lat}}}
and the decoder network

\psi(\;\cdot\;,\mathbf{W}_{\psi}):{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Lat}}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{x}}
, as well as the latent representation

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}(t)={\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}(t))\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Lat}}}
, the following loss is minimized:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \min_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{{\varphi}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{\psi},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Xi}}}\quad&\underbrace{\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}-\psi(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}})\right\|_{2}^{2}}_{\text{reconstruction loss}}+\underbrace{\lambda_{1}\left\|\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}}-(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}}\psi(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}))\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Theta}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}^{\top})\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Xi}}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}}_{\text{SINDy loss in }\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}}}\\
&+\underbrace{\lambda_{2}\left\|\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Theta}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}^{\top})\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Xi}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}_{\text{SINDy loss in the latent space}}+\underbrace{\lambda_{3}\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Xi}}\right\|_{1}}_{\text{sparse regularization}},\end{aligned}

(4)

with
\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}}=(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}})\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}}
.
The SINDy loss in
\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}}
, i.e., the
consistency loss
, ensures that the time
derivatives of the prediction matches the input time derivative
\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}}
; we refer to fig. 1 in
[
Cha+19
]
for more
details.

6

The

As described in the supplementary material of
[

]
,

AE+SINDy provides very promising results for (re-)discovering the underlying true

and

3
AE+SINDy-C: Low-dimensional Sparse Dictionary Learning for Simultaneous Discovery and Control of Distributed Systems

3.1
Latent Model Discovery

To efficiently scale SINDy to high-dimensional state spaces, incorporate controls, and eliminate reliance on derivatives, we introduce
AE+SINDy-C
. This approach aims to
accelerate DRL for control tasks involving distributed – potentially large-scale – systems, by combining the derivative-free Dyna-style DRL training in the SINDy-C case, as demonstrated by

[
Zol+24
,
ASM22
]
,
with the scalable approach to high-dimensional state- and action-spaces from
[
Cha+19
]
.

Our method approximates the environment dynamics to speed up computationally-expensive simulations, significantly reducing the need for interactions with
the full-order model. Additionally, it yields an interpretable and parsimonious dynamics
model within a low-dimensional surrogate space. The dimensions of the latent spaces,

{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Lat}}}
and
{N_{u}^{\mathsf{Lat}}}
, representing state and control respectively, can be
set equal to the intrinsic dimension of the dynamical system when this is known a
priori. Alternatively, these dimensions can be tuned to discover the intrinsic dimension
of an unknown system, solely based on available measurements. Our experiments
in
section

4
demonstrate that in some cases
AE+SINDy-C

is able to independently discover the underlying intrinsic dimensions for the
control space.

We operate in the (discrete-time) SINDy-C setting, as described in

section

2.3.1
, with
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{k}=(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}(t_{k}),\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}(t_{k}))

and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}(t_{k+1})
. Here,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}(t_{k})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Obs}}}
where

{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Obs}}}={N_{x}}
in the fully observable case, and
{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Obs}}}\ll{N_{x}}
in the
partially observable case, respectively, and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}(t_{k})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{u}}
.
7
7

7

Compared
to
fig.

1
, we simplify the
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{t}^{\mathsf{Obs}}

notation by omitting the superscript.
Overall, we are interested in the discovery task

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{k+1}=f(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{k})
. Instead of applying
SINDy-C
directly on our
measurements, we follow the idea of Champion et al.

then apply

Offline Training

As already described in the previous section, the overall
framework is shown in
fig.

2
and can be divided into
three steps:

(i)

Encoding:
The observed state
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Obs}}}
and the current control

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{u}}
are individually compressed by two separated encoder networks

{\varphi}_{x}(\;\cdot\;;\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{{\varphi}_{x}}):{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Obs}}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Lat}}}
and

{\varphi}_{u}(\;\cdot\;;\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{{\varphi}_{u}}):{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{u}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{u}^{\mathsf{Lat}}}
, yielding
their low-dimensional latent-space equivalent
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}_{x}(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Lat}}}
and

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}_{u}(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{u}^{\mathsf{Lat}}}
, respectively.

(ii)

Discrete
SINDy-C
in the latent space:
Given a set of
d
dictionary functions
(e.g. polynomials, trigonometric functions, etc.) the dictionary

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Theta}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}_{x}(t),\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}_{u}(t))=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Theta}}({\varphi}_{x}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{t};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{{\varphi}_{x}}),{\varphi}_{u}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{t};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{{\varphi}_{u}}))\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is evaluated
and multiplied by
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Xi}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Lat}}}}
, i.e., the coefficients to be fit.

(iii)

Decoding:
The result
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}_{x}(t+1)=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Theta}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}_{x}(t),\boldsymbol{\mathrm{z}}_{u}(t)){\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Xi}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Lat}}}
is then decoded to obtain a prediction for the
next state of the high-dimensional system by using the state-decoder

\psi_{x}(\;\cdot\;;\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{\psi_{x}}):{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Lat}}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Obs}}}
. In order to train the
AE, the compressed state and action are also decoded and fed into the loss function
by using the state-decoder and the control-decoder

\psi_{u}(\;\cdot\;;\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{\psi_{u}}):{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{u}^{\mathsf{Lat}}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{u}}
.

Overall, we obtain the following loss function:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \min_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{{\varphi}_{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{{\varphi}_{u}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{\psi_{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{\psi_{u}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Xi}}}\quad&\underbrace{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{t+1}-\psi_{x}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Theta}}({\varphi}_{x}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{t};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{{\varphi}_{x}}),{\varphi}_{u}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{t};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{{\varphi}_{u}}))\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Xi}}};{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{\psi_{x}}}\right)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}_{\text{forward \mbox{SINDy-C}{} prediction loss}}\\
&+\lambda_{1}\underbrace{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{t}-\psi_{x}({\varphi}_{x}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{t};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{{\varphi}_{x}});{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{\psi_{x}}})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}_{\text{autoencoder loss state}}\\
&+\lambda_{1}\underbrace{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{t}-\psi_{u}({\varphi}_{u}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{t};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{{\varphi}_{u}});{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{\psi_{u}}})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}_{\text{autoencoder loss control}}\\
&+\lambda_{2}\underbrace{\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Xi}}}\right\rVert_{1}}_{\text{sparsity regularization}},\end{aligned}

(5)

representing the loss of the decoded forward prediction in the
SINDy-C
latent space formulation, i.e.,
part (ii) and (iii), the classical AE loss and also the regularization loss to promote sparsity in the
SINDy-coefficients. Since our entire pipeline is implemented in the
PyTorch
library

[
Pas+17
]
, we train

eq.

5
by using the automatic differentiation framework and thus do not rely
on STLS. We use
[
Kap+21
]
to create the set of
d
dictionary functions once
in the beginning of the pipeline.
The parameters
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
are hyperparameters to individually weight
the contribution of each term.

Online Deployment

Following the training stage, where the parameters

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{{\varphi}_{x}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{{\varphi}_{u}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{\psi_{x}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{\psi_{u}}
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Xi}}

are learned, the trained network can be deployed as a Dyna-style environment approximation to
train the DRL agent. The surrogate environment enables extremely fast inferences and,
if well-trained, provides accurate predictions of the system dynamics. Given a current state
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Obs}}}
(either observed or simulated)
and a control
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{t}\sim\pi(\;\cdot\;|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{t})
, the prediction of the next state is computed as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}}_{t+1}=\psi_{x}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Theta}}({\varphi}_{x}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{t};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{{\varphi}_{x}}),{\varphi}_{u}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{t};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{{\varphi}_{u}}))\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Xi}}};{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{W}}_{\psi_{x}}}\right)\approx f(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{t},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{t}),

which involves only one matrix multiplication, the evaluation of
d
dictionary
functions, and three NN forward passes, resulting in exceptionally-low inference times.

and for the evaluation of all final models, we use the closed form of the full-order reward function.

4.1
Burgers’ Equation (ControlGym)

The viscous Burgers equation can be interpreted as a one-dimensional, simplified version of the
Navier-Stokes equations, describing fluid dynamics and capturing key phenomena
such as shock formations in water waves and gas dynamics.
Solving the Burgers’ equation is particularly challenging
for RL and other numerical methods due to its nonlinearity and the presence of sharp
gradients and discontinuities.
Given a spatial domain
\Omega=[0,L]\subset{\mathbb{R}}
and a time horizon
T
, we consider
the evolution of continuous fields
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}(x,t):\Omega\times[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}}
under a
the temporal dynamics:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}(x,t)+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}(x,t)\partial_{x}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}(x,t)-\nu\partial_{xx}^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}(x,t)&=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}(x,t)\\
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}(x,0)&=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{\text{init}}(x)\end{aligned}\;,

(6)

given an initial state
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{\text{init}}:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
, a constant diffusivity
\nu>0
, a source term (also called forcing function)
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}(x,t):\Omega\times[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}}
and periodic boundary conditions.
Internally,
ControlGym
discretizes
the PDE in space and time, assuming to rely on uniformly spatially distributed control inputs
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{t}
, which are
piecewise constant over time. This results in a discrete-time finite-dimensional nonlinear system of the form:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{t+1}=f(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{t},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{t};\boldsymbol{\mathrm{w}}_{k}),

(7)

including (optional) Gaussian noise
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{w}}_{k}
.
8
8

8

To be precise, the discrete dynamics
f
also depend on the mesh size of the discretization.

In this work, the internally used solution
parameters are set to their default and for more details we refer to

[
Zha+23
, p.6]
and
appendix

B
.
As previously mentioned, since our new encoding-decoding scheme does not rely on
derivatives, we explicitly want to test the robustness of
AE+SINDy-C
with respect to
observation noise. Thus, we follow the observation process by
ControlGym

given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{t}^{\mathsf{Obs}}=C\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{t},

where the matrix
C\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Obs}}}\times{N_{x}}}
is as described in

fig.

1
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{v})

is zero-mean Gaussian noise with symmetric positive definite covariance matrix
\Sigma_{v}=\sigma^{2}\cdot\mathrm{Id}_{{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Obs}}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Obs}}}\times{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Obs}}}},\sigma^{2}>0
. For both problems, we define a target state
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{\text{ref}}
and use the following objective function to be maximized:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{t},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{t})=-\mathbb{E}\left\{\sum_{t=0}^{K-1}\left[(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{\mathsf{Obs}}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{\text{ref}})^{\top}Q(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{\mathsf{Obs}}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{\text{ref}})+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{t}^{\top}R\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{t}\right]\right\},

with
K
discrete time steps and positive definite matrices

Q\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Obs}}}\times{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Obs}}}}

and
R\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{N_{u}}\times{N_{u}}}
balancing the control effort and the tracking performance. As reference state and controls, we both use the zero-vector. The inherent solution manifold of
eq.

6
is two-dimensional, representing both space and time.

In this work, the diffusivity constant
\nu=1.0
is fixed and we choose
\Omega=(0,1)
as spatial domain, that is, we select
L=1
. We propose
{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Lat}}}=2
, since the inherent dimension of the solution manifold is two, and

{N_{u}^{\mathsf{Lat}}}=2
for the reason of symmetry. The target state of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{\text{ref}}\equiv\mathbf{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{x}^{\mathsf{Obs}}}
is used. In
section

4.1.3
we will see
how
AE+SINDy-C
is independently able to compress the controls even further.

For the Burgers’ equation, we trained
AE+SINDy-C
with different
full-order model update frequencies
{k_{\text{dyn}}}=5,10,15
to analyze the sample efficiency
and the sensitivity of the surrogate model. In the case
{k_{\text{dyn}}}=15
the internal modeling
bias was too big for a successful DRL training, which is the reason why this case is excluded from the
analysis. This appears to be a natural limitation since the number of full-order interactions
was too low and thus not sufficient to adequately model the system dynamics under investigation.

4.2
Incompressible Navier-Stokes Equations (PDEControlGym)

This second example focuses on a much
more challenging equation and control setting. To highlight the framework’s capabilities
to scale to high-dimensional spaces, we consider the two-dimensional incompressible Navier-Stokes equations.
The Navier-Stokes equations play a fundamental role in fluid dynamics with numerous applications in areas
such as aerodynamics, weather forecasting, oceanography, and industrial processes.
They describe the motion of fluid substances and are used to model phenomena ranging from airflow
over an aircraft wing to blood flow in arteries and the formation of weather patterns.

The temporal dynamics of the 2D velocity field
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}(x_{1},x_{2},t):\Omega\times[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and the pressure field
p(x_{1},x_{2},t):\Omega\times[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}}
is given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=0,\\
&\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=-\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p+\nu\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}},\end{aligned}

(9)

in
\Omega\times[0,T]
, denoting the kinematic viscosity of the
fluid by
\nu
, and its density by
\rho
. For the experiments, the equation is fully
observable, we consider Dirichlet boundary conditions with velocities set to 0, and we control
along the top boundary
u_{t}=u_{t}(x_{1})=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}(x_{1},1,t)
, for
x_{1}\in\Omega
, i.e.
x_{2}=1

is fixed.
Following the benchmark of
[
Bha+24
, section 5.3]
, the domain

\Omega=(0,1)^{2}
and the time interval
T=0.2\mathrm{s}
are used, parameters for the solution procedure
are kept. The equation is noise-free fully observed. The DRL agents are trained with a discretized version
of the following objective function

\mathcal{J}(u_{t},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}(x_{1},x_{2},t)-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{1},x_{2},t)\right\rVert^{2}\>\mathrm{d}(x_{1},x_{2})\>\mathrm{d}t+\frac{\gamma}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert u_{t}-u^{\mathrm{ref}}_{t}\right\rVert^{2}\>\mathrm{d}t,

where the reference solution
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{\mathrm{ref}}
is given by the resulting
velocity field when applying the controls

u:[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}},t\mapsto 3-5t
. For the reference controls in the objective function
u^{\mathrm{ref}}_{t}\equiv 2.0
is used.

Based on the superior results in the extensive study of the previous chapter,
we focus on
{k_{\text{dyn}}}=5
for the Navier-Stokes equations example and compare
the Dyna-style MBRL scheme with the model-free benchmark. We want to experiment
with the dimension of the surrogate space and ideally estimate the dimension at which
the agent is not possible to effectively learn a policy anymore. For this reason,
we train
AE+SINDy-C
with an architecture of
(882,84,4)\times(1,6,2)
, i.e. a
six-dimensional latent space,
(882,52,3)\times(1,4,1)
, i.e. a four-dimensional latent
space, for the state and controls respectively, as well as with
(882,30,1)\times(1,4,1)
,
i.e. a two-dimensional latent space. Less degrees of freedom in the surrogate space decrease the
goodness of fit in the surrogate space and in the case of a two-dimensional surrogate space
the agent was not able to successfully learn a policy which is why this case is excluded
from the analysis.

5
Discussion and Conclusion

We propose a data-efficient and interpretable Dyna-style Model-Based Deep Reinforcement Learning
(MBRL) framework for controlling distributed systems, such as those governed by
Partial Differential Equations. Combining
SINDy-C
with an
autoencoder framework not only scales to high-dimensional systems but also provides a low-dimensional
learned representation as a dynamical system in the latent space. The proposed controllers have proven
to be effective and robust in both partially and fully observable cases. Additionally, we showed
how the proposed framework can be used to estimate an approximate lower bound for a low-dimensional
surrogate representation of the dynamics.

A clear limitation of our method is the training of an autoencoder online. Problems
such as overfitting
[
GBC16
,
Zha+21
]
and catastrophic
forgetting
[
Kir+17
,
MC89
]
lead to decreasing performance
if trained for too long. Potential options to overcome this issue could include the usage of shorter roll-outs

[
Jan+21
]
, ensemble methods
[
Zol+24
]
,
or training the autoencoder using sequential thresholding to enforce zero-valued
coefficients. The Navier-Stokes PDE example showed that, under the availability
of enough regularity in the initial condition this problem can be lessened. On the other hand, the second
example highlights well the importance of choosing the proper latent space representation and experiment
with the dimensionality of the surrogate space in order to avoid a poor quality of fitting.

Further research should address these limitations to enhance the robustness and
generalizability of our framework across diverse PDE scenarios. Beyond the current
improvements, integrating
SINDy-C
with autoencoders opens new avenues in multiple
fields. In future work, we plan to extend the latent space representation to include
parameter dependencies, as seen in
[
Con+23
]
, enabling more
effective learning of parametric systems, such as parameterized PDEs.

The code for
AE+SINDy-C
will be made publicly available upon acceptance.
