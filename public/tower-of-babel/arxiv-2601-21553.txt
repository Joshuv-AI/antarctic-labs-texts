Strassen’s support functionals coincide with the quantum functionals
Keiya Sakabe, M. Levent Doğan, Michael Walter

Abstract
Strassen’s asymptotic spectrum offers a framework for analyzing the complexity of tensors. It has found applications in diverse areas, from computer science to additive combinatorics and quantum information. A long-standing open problem, dating back to 1991, asks whether Strassen’s support functionals are universal spectral points, that is, points in the asymptotic spectrum of tensors. In this paper, we answer this question in the affirmative by proving that the support functionals coincide with the quantum functionals—universal spectral points that are defined via entropy optimization on entanglement polytopes. We obtain this result as a special case of a general minimax formula for convex optimization on entanglement polytopes (and other moment polytopes) that has further applications to other tensor parameters, including the asymptotic slice rank. Our proof is based on a recent Fenchel-type duality theorem on Hadamard manifolds due to Hirai.

Introduction

Tensors play a central role in mathematics, computer science, physics and many other fields of sciences.
In algebraic complexity theory, tensors capture multilinear problems such as the problem of multiplying
n\times n
matrices.
Strassen’s theory of the
asymptotic spectrum
of tensors establishes an important unifying framework for studying the computational complexity of multilinear problems
[

We briefly sketch this theory.
Given a
d

restricts
to a tensor
s\in\mathbbm{C}^{m_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{m_{d}}

t\geq s
, if there are linear maps
A_{i}\colon\mathbbm{C}^{n_{i}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{C}^{m_{i}}
such that

(A_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes A_{d})t=s
.
We say that
t

asymptotically restricts
to

t^{\otimes n+o(n)}\geq s^{\otimes n}
.
When one of the tensors is the
unit tensor

\langle r\rangle\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{r}e_{i}\otimes\dots\otimes e_{i}
, asymptotic restriction captures the
asymptotic rank and subrank
of a tensor, which directly bounds the algebraic complexity.
In general, it is a difficult problem to determine when two tensors asymptotically restrict each other.
Strassen discovered an important structural simplification: the asymptotic restriction problem is fully captured by certain functionals.
More precisely, for a family
\mathcal{X}
of
d
-tensors that is closed under tensor product, direct sums, and contains the unit tensors

that satisfy the following four axioms:
they are

1.

monotone under restriction,

2.

multiplicative under tensor product,

3.

additive under direct sum, and

4.

normalized to have value
r
on the unit tensor
\langle r\rangle
.

Strassen’s spectral theorem states that such functionals, called
spectral points
, are invariants that completely characterize the asymptotic restriction between tensors.
Namely, for two tensors

we have
t^{\otimes n+o(n)}\geq s^{\otimes n}
if and only if
\phi(t)\geq\phi(s)
for every spectral point
\phi

In particular, spectral points provide upper bounds on the asymptotic subrank and lower bounds on the asymptotic rank of a tensor.
This makes the asymptotic spectrum particularly powerful in computational complexity.
Remarkably, while originally invented in the context of complexity theory, and in particular the long-standing open problem of characterizing the exponent of matrix multiplication, Strassen’s theory has found applications in a diverse set of areas such as additive combinatorics

1.1
Main results

In this paper we answer this question in the affirmative by proving that the quantum functionals
F_{\theta}(t)
and Strassen’s upper support functional

every

Theorem 1.1
.

For every tensor

\theta\in\Theta

F_{\theta}(t)=\zeta^{\theta}(t)
.
In particular, Strassen’s support functional is a universal spectral point in the asymptotic spectrum of tensors.

Our result also provides a new and direct proof that the quantum functionals

are universal spectral points.

The proof of
1.3
relies on a powerful duality theorem for convex optimization on Hadamard manifolds due to Hirai
[
28
]
.
A
Hadamard manifold
is a complete, simply-connected Riemannian manifold with non-positive curvature.
Just like in Euclidean space, Hadamard manifolds have the convenient property that any two points are connected by a unique geodesic (shortest path).
On such manifolds
\mathcal{M}
, we consider
geodesically convex
functions
f\colon\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}
that are convex along every geodesic in
\mathcal{M}
.
Hirai’s recent result establishes a strong Fenchel-type duality in this setting.
Specifically, assume we have a twice differentiable geodesically convex function
f\colon\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}
, and a function
Q\colon T^{*}\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}
, defined on the cotangent bundle of
\mathcal{M}
, such that for every base point
x\in\mathcal{M}
,
Q_{x}\coloneqq Q|_{T^{*}_{x}\mathcal{M}}
is a convex l.s.c. function with bounded sublevel sets.
In addition,
Q
should be invariant under
parallel transport
, meaning that
Q_{y}
can be obtained from
Q_{x}
by transporting cotangent vectors along curves from
x
to
y
.
In particular
Q
is already fully determined by
Q_{x}
for any fixed base point
x\in\mathcal{M}
.
Under these assumptions, Hirai constructs a dual program to the minimization problem

\inf_{x\in\mathcal{M}}Q(\mathrm{d}f(x))

in terms of the Legendre–Fenchel conjugate of
Q
and the
recession function
of
f
.
Hirai proved that the weak duality always holds (the infimum is at least the supremum), and proved that when
\mathcal{M}
is a product of manifolds of positive definite matrices and Euclidean spaces, there is no duality gap, i.e., the infimum is equal to the supremum of the dual
[
28
, Thm. 3.19]
.
In our context, we apply Hirai’s theorem to
\mathcal{M}=\PD\coloneqq\PD(n_{1})\times\dots\times\PD(n_{d})
, where
\PD(n)
denotes the manifold of
n\times n
positive definite Hermitian matrices (equipped with the affine-invariant Riemannian metric).
Since
\PD
is an open subset of
\Herm(n_{1})\times\dots\times\Herm(n_{d})
, the cotangent space of
\PD
at the identity element is naturally identified as
T_{I}^{*}\PD\cong\Herm(n_{1})\times\dots\times\Herm(n_{d})
.
Then parallel transport invariance property of
Q
translates to the assumption that
Q_{I}\colon\Herm(n_{1})\times\dots\times\Herm(n_{d})\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}
is
\U
-invariant, where
\U\coloneqq\U(n_{1})\times\dots\times\U(n_{d})
, that is, it only depends on the eigenvalues of the Hermitian matrices.
In other words, there exists a function
F\colon\mathbbm{R}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbbm{R}^{n_{d}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}\cup\{\infty\}
such that

Q_{I}(H_{1},\dots,H_{d})\equiv F(\spec(H_{1}),\dots,\spec(H_{d})).

Hirai’s assumption on
Q
translates to the assumption that
F
is a convex symmetric l.s.c. function with bounded sublevel sets, and conversely any such
F
gives rise to a function
Q
satisfying Hirai’s assumptions, via the above formula.
In this setting, we use Hirai’s strong duality theorem to prove the following result, which implies
1.3
and generalizes it beyond moment polytopes:

Theorem 1.4

(Minimax formula for gradient optimization)
.

Let
F\colon\mathbbm{R}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbbm{R}^{n_{d}}\to\mathbbm{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be a convex symmetric l.s.c. function with bounded sublevel sets,
and let
f\colon\PD\to\mathbbm{R}
be a twice-differentiable geodesically convex function such that
\mathrm{d}f(\PD)\subseteq\dom Q
, with
Q\colon T^{*}\PD\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}\cup\{\infty\}
as above. Then,

\inf_{x\in\PD}Q(\mathrm{d}f(x))=\sup_{g\in\GL}\inf_{y\in\mathbbm{R}^{n_{1}}\times\dots\times\mathbbm{R}^{n_{d}}}F(\nabla f^{g}(y)),

were
f^{g}\colon\mathbbm{R}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbbm{R}^{n_{d}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}
for
g\in\GL
denotes the function
f^{g}(y)\coloneqq f(g_{1}^{\dagger}e^{\diagmat y_{1}}g_{1},\dots,g_{d}^{\dagger}e^{\diagmat y_{d}}g_{d})
.

We prove
1.4
as
3.4
in
Section

3
, where we also note that the supremum over
\GL
can be reduced to
\U
(
3.5
).

1.3
is obtained from
1.4
by specializing

to the
Kempf–Ness function
of
t
; this is the geodesically convex function
f_{t}\colon\PD\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}
defined by
f_{t}(x)=\log\langle t,x\cdot t\rangle
, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the

-inner product.
The derivative of the Kempf–Ness function at the identity is known as the

1.2
Outlook

We indicate some directions for future research.
While our results are stated for actions of
\GL=\GL(n_{1})\times\dots\times\GL(n_{d})
and geodesically convex function on
\PD=\PD(n_{1})\times\dots\times\PD(n_{d})
, their proofs are general.
Hirai conjectured that strong duality holds for every Hadamard manifold
\mathcal{M}

[
28
, Conj. 3.18]
.
If this conjecture is true,
1.3
generalizes to arbitrary rational representations
\pi\colon G\rightarrow\GL(\mathcal{V})
of self-adjoint subgroups
G\leq\GL(n)
.
Likewise,
1.4
generalizes to the symmetric space
\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\{g^{\dagger}g\mid g\in G\}
, which is a totally geodesic submanifold of
\PD(n)
, and indeed admits a natural generalization to any Hadamard manifold.

It is interesting that
1.1
leads to a short and direct proof that the quantum functionals are universal spectral point.
We speculate that the general minimax formula in
1.4
might be a useful ingredient for the construction of spectral points.

Finally, we comment on the problem of
computing
the quantum functionals, or, more generally, computing
\min_{p\in\Delta(t)}F(p)
for a convex function
F
by efficient algorithms.
If one has access to the moment polytope (either as a convex hull of vertices, an intersection of half-spaces or by a membership or separation oracle), then the minimum can be computed by standard methods of convex optimization.
No such simple description is known that is efficient in all parameters, but see
[
22
,
8
,
9
,
25
]
for progress.
Hirai showed that there is a natural
Q
-gradient

\nabla^{Q}f
in the setting described above and proved that the corresponding flow
\dot{x}=-\nabla^{Q}f(x)
minimizes
Q(\mathrm{d}f(x))

[
28
]
, generalizing an earlier analysis of the ordinary gradient flow
[
26
]
.
In
[
19
]
, we show that Hirai’s flow can be discretized and analyzed for a natural class of
Q
and
f
.
In particular, we find the quantum functionals
F_{\theta}(t)
can be computed by a simple and natural
entropic scaling algorithm
:
t\leftarrow(\rho_{1}^{-\theta_{1}/2}\otimes\rho_{2}^{-\theta_{2}/2}\otimes\dots\otimes\rho_{d}^{-\theta_{d}/2})t
, where
\rho_{j}={t_{j}^{\dagger}t_{j}}/{\lVert t\rVert^{2}}
for
j\in[d]
, which converges in a number of steps that is polynomial in
n_{1},\dots,n_{d}
and the bit-length of
t
.
It would be interesting to use the minimax formula in
1.3
to obtain an alternative approach for this problem.

In this paper, we write

For a subset

We write

2.1
Linear algebra

All vector spaces are finite-dimensional by assumption.
For a
\mathbbm{C}
-vector space
\mathcal{V}
,
\Lin(\mathcal{V})
denotes the set of linear operators on
\mathcal{V}
, and
\GL(\mathcal{V})\subseteq\Lin(\mathcal{V})
denotes the group of invertible ones.
If
\mathcal{V}=\mathbbm{C}^{n}
, we simply write
\Lin(n)
and
\GL(n)
, which are identified by the set of
n\times n
(nonsingular) matrices.
We denote by
I\in\GL(n)
the identity matrix.
Let
\Herm(n)\subseteq\Lin(n)
and
\U(n)\subseteq\GL(n)
denote the set of Hermitian and unitary matrices, respectively.
For a vector
y\in\mathbbm{C}^{n}
,
\diagmat y\in\Lin(n)
denotes the diagonal matrix whose
i
-th diagonal entry is
y_{i}
, while if
A\in\Lin(n)
is a matrix then
\diagvec A\in\mathbbm{C}^{n}
denotes the vector consisting of its diagonal entries.
For
A\in\Lin(n)
, let
e^{A}\in\GL(n)
denote the matrix exponential.
Let
\spec X\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}_{\downarrow}
denote the spectrum of
X\in\Herm(n)
, i.e., the vector of sorted eigenvalues of
X
.
Let
X\oplus Y\in\Lin(m+n)
denote the block-diagonal matrix with diagonal blocks
X\in\Lin(m)
and
Y\in\Lin(n)
.

2.2
Hadamard manifolds

For a Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
, let
T_{x}\mathcal{M}
denote the tangent space at
x\in\mathcal{M}
,
T^{*}_{x}\mathcal{M}
denote the cotangent space, and
T\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\sqcup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}T_{x}\mathcal{M}
and
T^{*}\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\sqcup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}T_{x}^{*}\mathcal{M}
denote the (co)tangent bundles.
Let
\tau_{x\to y}:T_{x}\mathcal{M}\to T_{y}\mathcal{M}
denote the parallel transport along the (unique) geodesic, and
\tau^{*}_{x\to y}:T^{*}_{y}\to T^{*}_{x}
be its dual map, which parallel transports cotangent vectors.
Let
\Gamma(\mathcal{M})
denote the set of all geodesic rays, i.e., geodesic curves defined on
[0,\infty)
.

A
Hadamard manifold
is a complete, simply-connected Riemannian manifolds with non-positive curvature.
We do not introduce the general definition of these concepts on manifolds, since we only consider Euclidean spaces and products of positive definite matrices, which we discuss in the following.

Euclidean space:

In the case of the Euclidean space
\mathcal{M}=\mathbbm{R}^{n}
with its usual structure as a Riemannian manifold, the (co)tangent spaces
T_{x}\mathcal{M}
and
T^{*}_{x}\mathcal{M}
are identified with
\mathbbm{R}^{n}
, and the parallel transport is the identity map.
A geodesic ray
\gamma(t)\in\Gamma(\mathbbm{R}^{n})
is a line
\gamma(t)=q+tp
(
q,p\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}
).
For a function
f:\mathbbm{R}^{n}\to\mathbbm{R}\cup\{\infty\}
, the gradient at
x
is denoted by
\nabla f(x)\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}
, if it exists.

Positive-definite matrices with the affine-invariant Riemannian metric:

Let
\PD(n)
denote the set of
n\times n
positive-definite Hermitian matrices.
It is an open subset of
\Herm(n)
, and hence we can canonically identify the tangent space
T_{x}\PD(n)\cong\Herm(n)
at every point
x\in\PD(n)
.
Under this identification, the
affine-invariant Riemannian metric
on
\PD(n)
is given by
\langle X,Y\rangle_{x}:=\tr[x^{-1}Xx^{-1}Y]
for
x\in\PD(n)
and
X,Y\in\Herm(n)
and turns
\PD(n)
into a Hadamard manifold (e.g.,
[
6
, §II.10]
).
Every geodesic ray
\gamma\in\Gamma(\PD(n))
is of the form
\gamma(t)=g^{\dagger}e^{\diagmat(q+tp)}g
for
g\in\GL(n)
,
q,p\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}
, and the parallel transport along the geodesic from the identity
I\in\PD(n)
to any
x\in\PD(n)
is given by
\tau_{I\to x}(X)=x^{1/2}Xx^{1/2}
, where
X\in\Herm(n)\cong T_{I}\PD(n)
.
We similarly identify the cotangent spaces
T_{x}^{*}\PD(n)\cong\Herm(n)^{*}\cong\Herm(n)
, where the first isomorphism is canonical from the identification
T_{x}\PD(n)\cong\Herm(n)
and the second isomorphism uses the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product
\langle X,Y\rangle_{\Herm(n)}\coloneqq\tr[XY]
.
Then the parallel transport of cotangent vectors along the geodesic from
x
to
I
is given by
\tau^{*}_{I\to x}(\hat{X})=x^{1/2}\hat{X}x^{1/2}
, where
\hat{X}\in\Herm(n)\cong T_{x}^{*}\PD(n)
.
For a differentiable function
f:\PD(n)\to\mathbbm{R}
, its differential at
x\in\PD(n)
is denoted by
\mathrm{d}f(x)\in T^{*}_{x}\PD(n)
.

We also define the direct product
\PD\coloneqq\PD(n_{1})\times\cdots\times\PD(n_{d})
.
By embedding
\mathbb{H}\coloneqq\Herm(n_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus\Herm(n_{d})
as block-diagonal matrices in
\Herm(N)
,
N\coloneqq n_{1}+\cdots+n_{d}
, we consider
\PD
as a totally geodesic submanifold of
\PD(N)
.
Hence it is also a Hadamard manifold, its (co)tangent spaces identify with the subspace
\mathbb{H}\subseteq\Herm(N)
, and all of the above formulas for
\PD(n)
can also be used for
\PD
.

2.3
Convex functions and Legendre–Fenchel conjugate

A function
f\colon\mathbbm{R}\to\mathbbm{R}\cup\{\infty\}
on the real line is called
convex
if
f(tx+(1-t)y)\leq tf(x)+(1-t)f(y)
for all
x,y\in\mathbbm{R}
and
t\in[0,1]
.
A function
f\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathbbm{R}\cup\{\infty\}
on a Hadamard manifold
\mathcal{M}
is called (geodesically)
convex
if
f\circ\gamma
is convex for every geodesic
\gamma\colon\mathbbm{R}\to\mathcal{M}
.
The domain of a convex function is defined as
\dom f\coloneqq\{x\in\mathcal{M}\mid f(x)<\infty\}
.

When
\mathcal{M}=E
is an
\mathbbm{R}
-vector space, the above coincides with the usual notion of convexity.
In this setting, for a convex function
f\colon E\to\mathbbm{R}\cup\{\infty\}
, the
Legendre–Fenchel conjugate

f^{*}\colon E^{*}\to\mathbbm{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is defined as
f^{*}(\alpha)\coloneqq\sup_{y\in E}\alpha(y)-f(y)
, where
E^{*}
is the dual space of
E
.
If
E
is a Euclidean space with inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)
, then this can also be thought of as a function
f^{*}\colon E\to\mathbbm{R}\cup\{\infty\}
on
E
, given by

f^{*}(x)\coloneqq\sup_{y\in E}(x,y)-f(y)
.
We will always be in this situation: either
E=\mathbbm{R}^{n}
with the standard inner product, or
E=\mathbb{H}\subseteq\Herm(N)
with the restriction of the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product.

3
A minimax formula for gradient optimization

In this section, we present our general minimax formula (
1.4
, restated below as
3.4
).
Its proof relies on a recent Fenchel-type duality theorem for convex optimization on Hadamard manifolds, referred to as “asymptotic duality” by Hirai
[
28
]
.
Hirai’s theorem applies to a broader class of Hadamard manifolds, but we only require it in the cases of
\PD
and
\mathbbm{R}^{N}
(
Section

2.2
).
In the following, we first discuss Hirai’s result concretely in this setting and then state our theorem and its proof.

Throughout this section, let
n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}
be integers and
N\coloneqq n_{1}+\cdots+n_{d}
.
For a function
F\colon\mathbbm{R}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbbm{R}^{n_{d}}\cong\mathbbm{R}^{N}\to\mathbbm{R}\cup\{\infty\}
, consider the following properties:

(F1)

F
is convex and lower-semicontinuous (l.s.c.).

(F2)

F
is symmetric in each argument, i.e.,
F(P_{1}x_{1},\ldots,P_{d}x_{d})=F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
for all permutation matrices
P_{1},\ldots,P_{d}
.

(F3)

F
has bounded sublevel sets.

As in
Section

2.2
, we let
\mathbb{H}\coloneqq\Herm(n_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus\Herm(n_{d})\subseteq\Herm(N)
, where the inclusion is by embedding as block-diagonal matrices.
We can extend
F
to tuples of Hermitian matrices in
\mathbb{H}
by taking the eigenvalues of each matrix, as follows:

F_{\mathbb{H}}\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbbm{R}\cup\{\infty\},\quad F_{\mathbb{H}}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})\coloneqq F(\spec X_{1},\ldots,\spec X_{d}).

(3.1)

In short,
F_{\mathbb{H}}(X)=F(\spec X)
, where
\spec
here acts argument-wise.
It is clear that
F_{\mathbb{H}}
is unitarily invariant, i.e.,
F_{\mathbb{H}}(u_{1}^{\dagger}X_{1}u_{1},\ldots,u_{d}^{\dagger}X_{d}u_{d})=F_{\mathbb{H}}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})
for all
u_{i}\in\U(n_{i})
.
In short,
F_{\mathbb{H}}(u^{\dagger}Xu)=F_{\mathbb{H}}(X)
for any
u\in\U\coloneqq\U(n_{1})\times\cdots\times\U(n_{d})\subseteq\U(N)
.
Moreover, the following properties hold:

Proposition 3.1

(
[
15
,
36
]
4
4

4

These references prove these properties in the case of
d=1
, but their proofs generalize readily to
d\geq 2
.
)
.

Let
F\colon\mathbbm{R}^{N}\to\mathbbm{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be a function that satisfies
(F1)
and
(F2)
.
Then,
F_{\mathbb{H}}\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbbm{R}\cup\{\infty\}
satisfies the following properties:

(a)

F_{\mathbb{H}}
is convex and lower-semicontinuous function on
\mathbb{H}
.

(b)

The Legendre–Fenchel conjugate is given by
(F_{\mathbb{H}})^{*}=(F^{*})_{\mathbb{H}}
(so we can simply write
F_{\mathbb{H}}^{*}
).

On the other hand, it is easy to verify that every
\U
-invariant l.s.c. convex function
F_{\mathbb{H}}
having bounded sublevel sets is obtained in this manner by taking
F\coloneqq F_{\mathbb{H}}(\diagmat(\cdot))
.

Finally, we let
\PD\coloneqq\PD(n_{1})\times\cdots\times\PD(n_{d})
be equipped with the affine-invariant metric, as explained in
Section

2.2
.
We can then define a function
Q\colon T^{*}\PD\to\mathbbm{R}\cup\{\infty\}
on the cotangent bundle (called a “norm-like function” in

Q(\hat{Y})\coloneqq Q_{x}(\hat{Y})\coloneqq F_{\mathbb{H}}\left(\tau_{I\to x}^{*}\hat{Y}\right)=F_{\mathbb{H}}\left(x^{1/2}\hat{Y}x^{1/2}\right)\qquad(x\in\PD,\hat{Y}\in T^{*}_{x}\PD\cong\mathbb{H}),

(3.2)

where
Q_{x}\coloneqq Q|_{T^{*}_{x}\PD}
denotes the restriction of
Q
to the cotangent space at
x
and we use the identification
T^{*}_{x}\PD\cong\mathbb{H}
from
Section

2.2
.
Define
Q^{*}\colon T\PD\to\mathbbm{R}\cup\{\infty\}
by taking the Legendre–Fenchel conjugate on each cotangent space, i.e.,
Q^{*}_{x}\coloneqq Q^{*}|_{T^{*}_{x}\PD}\coloneqq(Q_{x})^{*}
, and using
(T^{*}_{x}\PD)^{*}\cong T_{x}\PD
.
Concretely, this is given by

\displaystyle Q^{*}(X)

\displaystyle=Q^{*}_{x}(X)=\sup_{\hat{Y}\in T^{*}_{x}\PD}\hat{Y}(X)-Q(\hat{Y})=\sup_{\hat{Y}\in\mathbb{H}}\tr[X\hat{Y}]-F_{\mathbb{H}}\left(x^{1/2}\hat{Y}x^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\hat{Z}\in\mathbb{H}}\tr\left[x^{-1/2}Xx^{-1/2}\hat{Z}\right]-F_{\mathbb{H}}(\hat{Z})=F_{\mathbb{H}}^{*}\left(x^{-1/2}Xx^{-1/2}\right)\qquad(x\in\PD,X\in T_{x}\PD\cong\mathbb{H}).

In particular, for any geodesic ray
\gamma(t)\coloneqq g^{\dagger}e^{\diagmat q+tp}g
, the value at
-\dot{\gamma}(t)\in T_{\gamma(t)}\PD
is given by

Q^{*}(-\dot{\gamma}(t))=F_{H}^{*}\left(-(g^{\dagger}e^{\diagmat q+tp}g)^{-1/2}g^{\dagger}e^{\diagmat q+tp}\diagmat pg(g^{\dagger}e^{\diagmat q+tp}g)^{-1/2}\right)=F_{\mathbb{H}}^{*}(-\diagmat p),

(3.3)

where the last equality follows from the unitary invariance of
F_{\mathbb{H}}
combined with the fact that
h(h^{\dagger}h)^{-1/2}
is unitary for any
h\in\GL(N)
.
Since the right-hand side does not depend on
t
, we can define

\displaystyle Q^{*}(-\dot{\gamma})\coloneqq Q^{*}(-\dot{\gamma}(t)).

Let
f\colon\PD\to\mathbbm{R}
be a twice-differentiable geodesically convex function satisfying
\mathrm{d}f(\PD)\subseteq\dom Q
.
Hirai
[
28
]
considers the following gradient minimization problem (in a more general setting),

\inf_{x\in\PD}Q(\mathrm{d}f(x)),

(3.4)

and shows that its optimal value can be expressed by a dual maximization problem.
To state his result, define the
recession function

f^{\infty}(\gamma)

[
27
,
32
]
on the space of geodesic rays as

f^{\infty}\colon\Gamma(\PD)\to\mathbbm{R}\cup\{\infty\},\quad f^{\infty}(\gamma)\coloneqq\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}f(\gamma(t))=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\left(f(\gamma(t))-f(\gamma(0))\right),

where the limit always exists in
\mathbbm{R}\cup\{\infty\}
because
\frac{1}{t}(f(\gamma(t))-f(\gamma(0)))
is non-decreasing, due to the geodesic convexity of
f
.
Then Hirai’s result is the following:

Theorem 3.2

(Asymptotic duality
[
28
, Thm. 3.19]
,
\PD
version)
.

Let
F\colon\mathbbm{R}^{N}\to\mathbbm{R}
be a function satisfying
(F1)
–
(F3)
, and let
f\colon\PD\to\mathbbm{R}
be a twice differentiable geodesically convex function satisfying
\mathrm{d}f(\PD)\subseteq\dom Q
, where
Q
is defined as in
Eq.

3.2
.
Then, the following equality holds:

\inf_{x\in\PD}Q(\mathrm{d}f(x))=\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\PD)}-f^{\infty}(\gamma)-Q^{*}(-\dot{\gamma}).

In the special case that
\PD=\PD(1)^{n}
, and using the isometry
(\PD(1))^{n}\cong\mathbbm{R}^{n}
given by the entrywise logarithm, one obtains a Euclidean version of the duality theorem.
This Euclidean version can also be understood as a special case of the Fenchel’s duality
[
39
, Thm. 31.1]
, and hence the twice differentiability and the condition
\mathrm{d}f\subseteq\dom Q
are no longer required; see
[
28
, Ex. 3.6]
.
Thus:

Proposition 3.3

(Asymptotic duality, Euclidean version)
.

Let
F\colon\mathbbm{R}^{N}\to\mathbbm{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be a convex l.s.c. function having bounded sublevel sets, and let
h\colon\mathbbm{R}^{N}\to\mathbbm{R}
be a differentiable convex function.
For any geodesic ray
\gamma(t)\coloneqq q+tp\in\Gamma(\mathbbm{R}^{n})
, let
h^{\infty}(\gamma)\coloneqq\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}h(q+tp)
and
F^{*}(-\dot{\gamma})\coloneqq F^{*}(-p)
.
Then, the following equality holds:

\inf_{y\in\mathbbm{R}^{N}}F(\nabla h(y))=\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mathbbm{R}^{n})}-h^{\infty}(\gamma)-F^{*}(-\dot{\gamma}).

We now derive our maximin formula for the minimization problem (
3.4
), describing it as a supremum of minimization problems, each of which is defined in the Euclidean space.
To this end, we define, for any
g\in\GL\coloneqq\GL(n_{1})\times\cdots\times\GL(n_{d})\subseteq\GL(N)
, the function

f^{g}\colon\mathbbm{R}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbbm{R}^{n_{d}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R},\quad f^{g}(y)\coloneqq f(g_{1}^{\dagger}e^{\diagmat y_{1}}g_{1},\dots,g_{d}^{\dagger}e^{\diagmat y_{d}}g_{d}).

(3.5)

If we identify
\mathbbm{R}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbbm{R}^{n_{d}}\cong\mathbbm{R}^{N}
, we can also think of
f^{g}
as a function on
\mathbbm{R}^{N}
, defined as

f^{g}(y)\coloneqq f(g^{\dagger}e^{\diagmat y}g).

(3.6)

Note that the geodesic convexity of
f
implies the convexity of
f^{g}
, since the map
y\mapsto g^{\dagger}e^{\diagmat y}g
maps each line
q+tp
in
\mathbbm{R}^{N}
to a geodesic
g^{\dagger}e^{\diagmat q+tp}g
in
\PD
.
By direct calculation, the gradient of
f^{g}
is given by

\nabla f^{g}(y)=\diagvec\left(g\,\mathrm{d}f\left(g^{\dagger}e^{\diagmat y}g\right)g^{\dagger}e^{\diagmat y}\right),

(3.7)

where we think of
\mathrm{d}f(x)\in T^{*}_{x}\PD\cong\mathbb{H}\subseteq\Herm(N)
for
x=g^{\dagger}e^{\diagmat y}g
.

We are now ready to state our general theorem.
It expresses the minimization problem (
3.4
) as a supremum of minimization problems, each of which is defined on Euclidean space:

Theorem 3.4

(Detailed version of
1.4
)
.

Let
F\colon\mathbbm{R}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbbm{R}^{n_{d}}\to\mathbbm{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be a function satisfying
(F1)
–
(F3)
, and let
f\colon\PD\to\mathbbm{R}
be a twice-differentiable geodesically convex function satisfying
\mathrm{d}f(\PD)\subseteq\dom Q
, where
Q
is defined as in
Eq.

3.2
.
Then, the following equality holds:

\inf_{x\in\PD}Q(\mathrm{d}f(x))=\sup_{g\in\GL}\inf_{y\in\mathbbm{R}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbbm{R}^{n_{d}}}F(\nabla f^{g}(y)).

Concretely,

\inf_{x\in\PD}F\left(\spec\left(x^{1/2}\mathrm{d}f(x)x^{1/2}\right)\right)=\sup_{g\in{\GL}}\inf_{y\in\mathbbm{R}^{N}}F\left(\diagvec\left(g\,\mathrm{d}f\left(g^{\dagger}e^{\diagmat y}g\right)g^{\dagger}e^{\diagmat y}\right)\right).

Proof.

Throughout the proof we identify
\mathbbm{R}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbbm{R}^{n_{d}}\cong\mathbbm{R}^{N}
.
We first observe that that

f^{\infty}\left(t\mapsto g^{\dagger}e^{\diagmat(q+tp)}g\right)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}f(g^{\dagger}e^{\diagmat(q+tp)}g)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}f^{g}(q+tp)=(f^{g})^{\infty}(t\mapsto q+tp).

(3.8)

Then,

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in\PD}Q(\mathrm{d}f(x))

\displaystyle=\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\PD)}-f^{\infty}(\gamma)-Q^{*}(-\dot{\gamma})

\displaystyle=\sup_{g\in\GL}\sup_{p,q\in\mathbbm{R}^{N}}-f^{\infty}\left(t\mapsto g^{\dagger}e^{\diagmat(q+tp)}g\right)-F_{\mathbb{H}}^{*}(-\diagmat p)

\displaystyle=\sup_{g\in\GL}\sup_{p,q\in\mathbbm{R}^{N}}-(f^{g})^{\infty}(t\mapsto q+tp)-F^{*}(-p)

\displaystyle=\sup_{g\in\GL}\inf_{y\in\mathbbm{R}^{N}}F(\nabla f^{g}(y)),

where the first equality follows from Hirai’s asymptotic duality (
3.2
), the second follows from the concrete expression of geodesic rays combined with
Eq.

3.3
, the third follows from
3.1
(b) and
Eq.

3.8
, and the last follows from the asymptotic duality in
\mathbbm{R}^{N}
(
3.3
).
Finally, the concrete formula is obtained by combining
Eqs.

3.1
,
3.2
and
3.7
.

A minimax formula for convex optimization on moment polytopes

In this section, we apply the result of the previous section to prove a minimax formula for convex optimization on moment polytopes (

below).

Recall that we defined
\GL=\GL(n_{1})\times\cdots\times\GL(n_{d})\subseteq\GL(N)
.
Let
\pi\colon\GL\to\GL(\mathcal{V})
be a finite-dimensional rational representation, with Lie algebra representation
\Pi\colon\Lin(n_{1})\times\cdots\times\Lin(n_{d})\to\Lin(\mathcal{V})
, i.e.,
\Pi(X)\coloneqq\partial_{t}\pi(e^{tX})|_{t=0}
.
We write
g\cdot v\coloneqq\pi(g)v
for
g\in\GL
and
v\in\mathcal{V}
, so
\GL\cdot v
denotes the orbit of
v
.
We can always equip
\mathcal{V}
with a inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
that is invariant under the maximally compact subgroup
\U=\U(n_{1})\times\cdots\times\U(n_{d})=\GL\cap\U(N)
; we denote the induced norm by
\|v\|\coloneqq\langle v,v\rangle^{1/2}
.

In this setting, the
moment map

[
37
]
is defined as

\mu\colon\mathcal{V}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{H},\quad\tr[\mu(v)X]\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\tr[(\mu(v))_{i}X_{i}]=\frac{\langle v,\Pi(X)v\rangle}{\|v\|^{2}}\qquad(\forall X\in\mathbb{H}).

(4.1)

Note that the moment map is more naturally defined on the projective space
\mathbbm{P}(\mathcal{V})
.
It is easy to check that
\mu(u\cdot v)=u\mu(v)u^{\dagger}
for any unitary
u\in\U
.
The
moment polytope

\Delta(v)
of
0\neq v\in\mathcal{V}
is defined as the set of spectra of moment map images of points in the orbit closure in projective space:

\Delta(v)\coloneqq\{\spec(\mu(w))\mid[w]\in\overline{\GL\cdot[v]}\}=\overline{\{\spec(\mu(g\cdot v))\mid g\in\GL\}}=\overline{\{\spec(\mu(x\cdot v))\mid x\in\PD\}},

(4.2)

where we write
[w]\in\mathbbm{P}(\mathcal{V})
for the point in projective space corresponding to a nonzero vector
0\neq w\in\mathcal{V}
, and we denote
\spec(H)\coloneqq(\spec(H_{1}),\ldots,\spec(H_{d}))
for
H\in\mathbb{H}
; the first equality in
Eq.

4.2
holds by compactness of the orbit closure in projective space and continuity of
\spec(\cdot)
, while the latter equality is immediate from the polar decomposition: it holds that
\spec(\mu(g\cdot v))=\spec(u\mu(x\cdot v)u^{\dagger})=\spec(\mu(x\cdot v))
for the unique decomposition
g=ux\ (u\in\U,x\in\PD)
.
Remarkably,
\Delta(v)
is indeed a convex polytope.

Theorem 4.1

(
[
37
,
33
]
)
.

For any
0\neq v\in\mathcal{V}
,
\Delta(v)
is a bounded convex polytope in
\mathbbm{R}^{n_{1}}_{\downarrow}\times\cdots\times\mathbbm{R}^{n_{d}}_{\downarrow}
.

Let
\mathrm{T}\subseteq\GL
be the set of diagonal matrices, called the maximal torus.
We canonically identify
\mathrm{T}\cong(\mathbbm{C}^{\times})^{N}\subseteq\GL(N)
.
Then, we can define a moment map and moment polytope exactly as above, but for the group
\mathrm{T}
in place of
\GL
.
Concretely, let
\pi_{\mathrm{T}}
be the restriction of
\pi
to
\mathrm{T}
.
Its Lie algebra representation
\Pi_{\mathrm{T}}\colon\mathbbm{C}^{N}\to\Lin(\mathcal{V})
is then given by
\Pi_{\mathrm{T}}(y)=\Pi(\diagmat y)
.
The associated moment map, which we call the
torus moment map
, is given by

\mu_{\mathrm{T}}\colon\mathcal{V}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbbm{R}^{N},\quad\langle\mu_{\mathrm{T}}(v),y\rangle=\frac{\langle v,\Pi_{\mathrm{T}}(y)v\rangle}{\|v\|^{2}}=\frac{\langle v,\Pi(\diagmat y)v\rangle}{\|v\|^{2}}\qquad(\forall y\in\mathbbm{R}^{N}).

By comparing it to
Eq.

4.1
, it follows that
\mu_{\mathrm{T}}(v)=\diagvec\mu(v)
.
The corresponding moment polytope is given by

Example 4.2

(Tensor action)

Consider the complex vector space

Because the tensor action contains the dilations, we can replace the closure in projective space by (the nonzero vectors in) the closure in the vector space.

Example 4.3

(Diagonal action)
.

The

Denoting the former by
\mu_{\Sym}
and the latter by

to avoid confusion with the tensor action, we have

\mu_{\Sym}(t)=\frac{1}{\|t\|^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}t_{i}^{\dagger}t_{i},\qquad\Delta_{\Sym}(t)=\left\{\spec\left(\frac{1}{\|s\|^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}s_{i}^{\dagger}s_{i}\right)\ \middle|\ 0\neq s\in\overline{\GL(n)\cdot t}\right\}.

(4.5)

The support polytope, which we will similarly denote by

\displaystyle\Omega_{\Sym}(t)=\conv\left\{e_{j_{1}}+\cdots+e_{j_{d}}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}\mid t_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{d}}\neq 0\right\}=\left\{{\textstyle\sum}_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}\mid p\in\Omega(t)\right\}

because for the diagonal action the weight of a standard basis vector

The action of

Important subrepresentations are given by the spaces of

\displaystyle\Sym^{d}(\mathbbm{C}^{n})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{t\in(\mathbbm{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}\ \middle|\ t_{\sigma(j_{1}),\ldots,\sigma(j_{d})}=t_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{d}}\;\forall\sigma\in S_{d}\right\},

\displaystyle\Alt^{d}(\mathbbm{C}^{n})

Since all of the

\mu_{\Sym}(t)=d\frac{t_{1}^{\dagger}t_{1}}{\|t\|^{2}},\quad\Delta_{\Sym}(t)=d\left\{\spec\frac{s_{1}^{\dagger}s_{1}}{\|s\|^{2}}\ \middle|\ 0\neq s=\overline{\GL(n)\cdot t}\right\}\quad(0\neq t\in\Sym^{d}(\mathbbm{C}^{n})\cup\Alt^{d}(\mathbbm{C}^{n})).

We now discuss minimization problems on general moment and support polytopes and how they can be related.

5.1
Symmetric quantum functionals

In
Section

1
we proved the equality of the quantum functionals and Strassen’s support functionals (
1.1
in the introduction) and explained how this equality gives a new direct proof of the fact that these functionals are spectral points.
In this section we discuss analogous results for
symmetric quantum functional
—a variant of the quantum functionals constructed for the diagonal action of
\GL(n)
on
(\mathbbm{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}
introduced in
[
11
]
.
The symmetric quantum functional is well-defined for arbitrary tensors and known to be a spectral point in the asymptotic spectrum of symmetric tensors.
Moreover, it is sub-multiplicative for all tensors.
Here we provide a minimax description of the symmetric quantum functional, in the spirit of Strassen’s support functionals, and provide a new direct proof of its sub-multiplicativity.

Let
\mathbbm{C}^{n}
be the
n
-dimensional vector space, and consider the diagonal action of
\GL(n)
on
(\mathbbm{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}
, as in
4.3
.
For a tensor
t\in(\mathbbm{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}
, we denote by
\Delta_{\Sym}(t)\subseteq\mathbbm{R}^{n}
the moment polytope, and by
\Omega_{\Sym}(t)
the support polytope with respect to this action, see
Eqs.

4.5
and
4.6
.
We reserve the notation
\Delta(t)
(resp.
\Omega(t)
) for the moment polytope (resp. support polytope) of
t
with respect to the action of the larger group
\GL\coloneqq\GL(n)^{\times d}
.

Definition 5.1

(Symmetric quantum functional)
.

The
symmetric quantum functional
of a tensor
t\in(\mathbbm{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}
is defined as

F_{\Sym}(t)\coloneqq\max_{p\in\Delta_{\Sym}(t)}\,2^{H(p/d)},

where
H
denotes the Shannon entropy.

We note that the elements of the moment polytope
\Delta_{\Sym}(t)
are vectors
p\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}=d
, so the division by
d
results in a probability distribution.
Since
p\mapsto-H(p/d)
is a convex symmetric l.s.c. function, a straightforward application of
1.3
yields the following new description:

Theorem 5.2
.

For every tensor
t\in(\mathbbm{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}
, we have

F_{\Sym}(t)=\min_{g\in\GL(n)}\max_{p\in\Omega_{\Sym}(g\cdot t)}2^{H(p/d)}.

It was proved in
[
11
]
that
F_{\Sym}
is sub-multiplicative, i.e., for
t\in(\mathbbm{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}
and
s\in(\mathbbm{C}^{m})^{\otimes d}
, we have
F_{\Sym}(t\otimes s)\leq F_{\Sym}(t)F_{\Sym}(s)
.
The proof relies on a non-trivial relationship between the moment polytopes
\Delta_{\Sym}(t\otimes s)
and
\Delta_{\Sym}(t),\Delta_{\Sym}(s)
.
More concretely, they proved that the former polytope is contained in a certain product of the latter polytopes, using a representation-theoretic description of the moment polytopes.
Here we reprove the sub-multiplicativity of
F_{\Sym}
using
5.2
and a simple description of the support polytope
\Omega_{\Sym}(t\otimes s)
in terms of
\Omega(t)
and
\Omega(s)
provided by the following lemma:

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
t\in(\mathbbm{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}
and
s\in(\mathbbm{C}^{m})^{\otimes d}
be
d
-tensors.
Then,

\Omega(t\otimes s)=\conv\left\{(q_{1}\otimes r_{1},q_{2}\otimes r_{2},\dots,q_{d}\otimes r_{d})\mid q\in\Omega(t),r\in\Omega(s)\right\}.

Therefore,

\Omega_{\Sym}(t\otimes s)=\conv\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}q_{i}\otimes r_{i}\mid q\in\Omega(t),r\in\Omega(s)\right\}.

(5.1)

Proof.

The second part follows from the first using
\Omega_{\Sym}(t\otimes s)=\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}\mid p\in\Omega(t\otimes s)\}
(see
Eq.

4.6
) and the linearity of the map
p\mapsto\sum_{i}p_{i}
.
We now prove the first part.
By
Eq.

1.1
, we have

\displaystyle\Omega(t)=\conv\left\{(e_{i_{1}},\dots,e_{i_{d}}):t_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}\neq 0\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\Omega(s)=\conv\left\{(e_{j_{1}},\dots,e_{j_{d}}):s_{j_{1},\dots,j_{d}}\neq 0\right\}.

On the other hand, identifying
\mathbbm{C}^{mn}\cong\mathbbm{C}^{m}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{n}
, we have

\Omega(t\otimes s)=\conv\left\{(e_{i_{1}}\otimes e_{j_{1}},e_{i_{2}}\otimes e_{j_{2}},\dots,e_{i_{d}}\otimes e_{j_{d}})\mid t_{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{d}}\neq 0\text{ and }s_{j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{d}}\neq 0\right\}.

The claim follows at once using the bilinearity of the tensor product.
∎

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
t\in(\mathbbm{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}
and
s\in(\mathbbm{C}^{m})^{\otimes d}
be
d

Let
p\in\Omega_{\Sym}(t\otimes s)\subseteq\mathbbm{R}^{nm}\cong\mathbbm{R}^{n}\times\mathbbm{R}^{m}

Interpreting

, where

Proof.

This follows from
Eqs.

and
4.6
, by observing that
\Omega(t)
and
\Omega(s)
consist of
d
-tuples of probability distributions.
∎

Proposition 5.5

(
[
11

)
.

For
t\in(\mathbbm{C}^{n})^{\otimes d},s\in(\mathbbm{C}^{m})^{\otimes d}

F_{\Sym}(t\otimes s)\leq F_{\Sym}(t)F_{\Sym}(s)

Proof.

By
5.2
, we have

\displaystyle F_{\Sym}(t\otimes s)=\min_{G\in\GL(nm)}\max_{p\in\Omega_{\Sym}(G\cdot(t\otimes s))}2^{H(p/d)}\leq\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\GL(n)\\

∎

5.2
Asymptotic slice rank

To any tensor
t\in\mathbbm{C}^{n_{1}}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{n_{2}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{n_{d}}
, one can assign a
d
-uniform,
d
-partite hypergraph
\mathcal{H}_{t}
, whose vertex set is
[n_{1}]\sqcup[n_{2}]\sqcup\dots\sqcup[n_{d}]
and the hyperedge set
E(\mathcal{H}_{t})
is determined by the tensor’s support, i.e., there is an edge
(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{d})
if and only if
t_{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{d}}\neq 0
.
For a hypergraph invariant
\iota
, the quantity

\min_{g\in\GL}\iota(\mathcal{H}_{g\cdot t})

can then be interpreted as a notion of “rank” for tensors.
In this section we discuss the slice rank, which corresponds the choice where
\iota
is the vertex cover number.
Other notable examples include the
G
-stable rank and the non-commutative rank, which will be discussed in subsequent sections.

We first give a geometric definition of the (weighted) slice rank.
The original definition is due to Tao
[
45
,
44
]
. Its weighted generalization was given in
[
12
]
.

Definition 5.6

(Slice decomposition)
.

We say that a tensor
t\in\mathbbm{C}^{n_{1}}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{n_{2}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{n_{d}}
admits a
slice decomposition
of size
(r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{d})
if there exist subspaces
U_{i}\leq\mathbbm{C}^{n_{i}}
of dimensions
r_{i}
such that

t\in U_{1}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{n_{2}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{n_{d}}+\dots+\mathbbm{C}^{n_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{n_{d-1}}\otimes U_{d}.

Definition 5.7

(Slice rank)
.

For
\xi\in\Xi\coloneqq\{\xi\in\mathbbm{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}\mid\max_{i}\xi_{i}=1\}
, the
\xi
-weighted slice rank
of a tensor
t\in\mathbbm{C}^{n_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{n_{d}}
is defined as

\SR_{\xi}(t)\coloneqq\min\{r_{1}^{1/\xi_{1}}+r_{2}^{1/\xi_{2}}+\dots+r_{d}^{1/\xi_{d}}\mid t\emph{admitsaslicedecompositionofsize}(r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{d})\}.

If
\xi_{i}=0
for some coordinate, then
r_{i}
is required to be zero and the corresponding term
r_{i}^{1/\xi_{i}}
is left out.
The
slice rank
of
t
is defined as
\SR(t)=\SR_{(1,\dots,1)}(t)
, i.e.,
\xi=(1,\dots,1)
is the all-ones vector.

It is clear from the definition that
\SR_{\xi}(g\cdot t)=\SR_{\xi}(t)
for
g\in\GL
.

In fact, by choosing base changes
g_{i}\in\GL(n_{i})
which maps
U_{i}
to a coordinate subspace, we see that the slice rank can be written as a minimum of hypergraph vertex cover numbers.
We first define the latter quantity and then state this observation.

Definition 5.8

(Vertex cover number)
.

For
\xi\in\Xi
, we define
\xi
-weighted vertex cover number
\tau_{\xi}(\mathcal{H}_{t})
as the value of the optimization problem

\begin{split}\tau_{\xi}(\mathcal{H}_{t})=\min\;&\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}u_{ij}\right)^{1/\xi_{i}}\\
\text{s.t. }\,&u_{1,i_{1}}+u_{2,i_{2}}+\dots+u_{d,i_{d}}\geq 1\quad\text{ for every }e=(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{d})\in E(\mathcal{H})\\
&u\in\{0,1\}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\{0,1\}^{n_{d}}.\end{split}

Lemma 5.9
.

For every tensor
t
and every
\xi\in\Xi
, we have
\SR_{\xi}(t)=\min_{g\in\GL}\tau_{\xi}(\mathcal{H}_{g\cdot t})
.

We will now prove that the regularization of the weighted slice rank and the weighted vertex cover number satisfy an analogous relation.
The former is known as the asymptotic slice rank and it is defined as follows:

Definition 5.10

(Asymptotic slice rank)
.

For
\xi\in\Xi
, the
\xi
-weighted asymptotic slice rank
of a tensor
t\in\mathbbm{C}^{n_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{n_{d}}
is defined as the limit (whenever it exists)

\widetilde{\SR}_{\xi}(t)\coloneqq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\SR_{\xi}(t^{\otimes n})^{1/n}.

(5.2)

The
asymptotic slice rank
of
t
is defined as the special case
\widetilde{\SR}(t)=\widetilde{\SR}_{(1,\dots,1)}(t)
.

We note that since the slice rank is neither sub- nor super-multiplicative (
[
13
, Example 5.2]
), it is not a priori clear why the limit should exist.

Theorem 5.11

(
[
13
,
12
]
)
.

For every tensor
t
and every
x\in\Xi
, the limit (
5.2
) exists and can be computed as

\widetilde{\SR}_{\xi}(t)=\max_{p\in\Delta(t)}\min_{i}2^{H(p_{i})/\xi_{i}}=\min_{\theta\in\Theta}F_{\theta}(t)^{1/\braket{\theta,\xi}},

where
F_{\theta}
are the quantum functionals.
In particular,

\widetilde{\SR}(t)=\max_{p\in\Delta(t)}\min_{i}2^{H(p_{i})}=\min_{\theta\in\Theta}F_{\theta}(t)
.

We will now show that the asymptotic slice rank of a tensor can be computed as the minimum of the
asymptotic vertex cover number
of
\mathcal{H}_{g\cdot t}
over
g\in\GL
.
If
\mathcal{G}=(V_{1}(\mathcal{G})\sqcup\dots\sqcup V_{d}(\mathcal{G}),E(\mathcal{G}))
and
\mathcal{H}=(V_{1}(\mathcal{H})\sqcup\dots\sqcup V_{d}(\mathcal{H}),E(\mathcal{H}))
are two
d
-uniform,
d
-partite hypergraphs then their Kronecker product
\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{H}
is defined as the hypergraph with vertex sets
\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{d}V_{i}(\mathcal{G})\times V_{i}(\mathcal{H})
and edge set which consists of tuples
((v_{1},v^{\prime}_{1}),(v_{2},v^{\prime}_{2}),\dots,(v_{d},v^{\prime}_{d}))
for
(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})\in E(\mathcal{G})
and
(v^{\prime}_{1},\dots,v^{\prime}_{d})\in E(\mathcal{H})
.
In particular, we have
E(\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{H})=E(\mathcal{G})\times E(\mathcal{H})
via the identification
((v_{1},v^{\prime}_{1}),(v_{2},v^{\prime}_{2}),\dots,(v_{d},v^{\prime}_{d}))=((v_{1},\dots,v_{d}),(v_{1}^{\prime},\dots,v_{d}^{\prime}))
.
If
t
and
s
are two tensors, this definition has the property that

\mathcal{H}_{t\otimes s}=\mathcal{H}_{t}\times\mathcal{H}_{s}.

Definition 5.12

(Asymptotic vertex cover number)
.

For
\xi\in\Xi
, the
\xi
-weighted asymptotic vertex cover number
of a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
is defined as (if the limit exists)

\tilde{\tau}_{\xi}(\mathcal{H})\coloneqq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\tau_{\xi}(H^{\times n})^{1/n}.

(5.3)

The
asymptotic vertex cover number
of
\mathcal{H}
is defined as the special case
\tilde{\tau}(\mathcal{H})=\tilde{\tau}(\mathcal{H})_{(1,\dots,1)}
.

Tao and Sawin
[
44
]
(see also
[
46
, Proposition 4.3.3]
) proved a formula that shows that the asymptotic vertex cover number can be computed by maximizing the minimum entropy of the marginal distributions of probability distributions on
E(\mathcal{H}_{t})
.
Equivalently, this can be stated as an entropy optimization over the support polytope
\Omega(t)
.
We provide a weighted version of their result and state an alternative description.

Proposition 5.13
.

For every tensor
t
and every
\xi\in\Xi
, the limit (
5.3
) exists and we have

\tilde{\tau}_{\xi}(\mathcal{H}_{t})=\max_{p\in\Omega(t)}\min_{i}2^{H(p_{i})/\xi_{i}}=\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\left(\max_{p\in\Omega(t)}2^{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}H(p_{i})}\right)^{1/\braket{\theta,\xi}}.

Proof.

The second formula follows from the first by Neumann’s minimax theorem for quasiconvex-quasiconcave functions, see
[
12
, Thm. 50]
.
We now prove the first formula by a straightforward generalization of the proof for
\xi=(1,\dots,1)
by Tao and Sawin
[
44
]
.
Note that a vertex cover can be viewed as a partition

E(\mathcal{H})=\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2}\cup\dots\cup\Gamma_{d},

with
\Gamma_{i}
being the edges covered by the
i
-th vertex set of the cover.
In particular, we have

\tau(\mathcal{H})=\min_{E(\mathcal{H})=\bigcup_{i=1}^{d}\Gamma_{i}}\sum_{i}|\pi_{i}(\Gamma_{i})|,

where
\pi_{i}:E(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow V_{i}(\mathcal{H})
denotes the
i
-th projection map.

Tao and Sawin proved that for every probability distribution
P
on
E(\mathcal{H})
and for any vertex cover
E(\mathcal{H}^{\times n})=E(\mathcal{H})^{\times n}=\Gamma_{1,n}\cup\dots\cup\Gamma_{d,n}
, there exists an index
j\in[d]
such that

|\pi_{j}(\Gamma_{j,n})|\geq 2^{(H(p_{j})+o(1))n},

(5.4)

where
p_{j}\;(j=1,2,\dots,d)
denote the marginal distributions of
P
.
Then, by

Eq.

5.4
we have
\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\pi_{i}(\Gamma_{i,n})|\geq\min_{j}|\pi_{j}(\Gamma_{j,n})|\geq\min_{j}2^{(H(p_{j})+o(1))n}
, which holds for every vertex cover and every distribution
P
.
For
\xi\in\Xi
, (
5.4
) implies
|\pi_{j}(\Gamma_{j,n})|^{1/\xi_{j}}\geq 2^{n(H(p_{j})/\xi_{j}+o(1))}
, and
\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\pi_{i}(\Gamma_{i,n})|^{1/\xi_{i}}\geq\min_{j}2^{(H(p_{j})/\xi_{j}+o(1))n}
.
Taking
n\rightarrow\infty
and maximizing over the probability distributions, we get

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\tau_{\xi}(\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\times n})^{1/n}\geq\max_{p\in\Omega(t)}\min_{i}2^{H(p_{i})/\xi_{i}}.

Conversely, Tao and Sawin proved that there exist
p^{(1)},\dots,p^{(l)}\in\Omega(t)
with
l=2^{o(n)}
such that there exists a covering

E(\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\times n})=\bigcup_{i=1}^{l}\Gamma_{n}^{(i)}

with the property that for every
i\in[l]
and
j\in[d]
we have

|\pi_{j}(\Gamma_{n}^{(i)})|\leq 2^{\left(H(p^{(i)}_{j})+o(1)\right)n}.

Let’s denote by
j_{i}\in[d]
the index that satisfies

\min_{j\in[d]}H(p^{(i)}_{j})/\xi_{j}=H(p^{(i)}_{j_{i}})/\xi_{j_{i}},

and regroup as
\Gamma_{k,n}\coloneqq\bigcup_{j_{i}=k}\Gamma_{n}^{(i)}
.
Since
l=2^{o(n)}
, we have

\tau_{\xi}(\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\times n})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{d}|\pi_{k}(\Gamma_{k,n})|^{1/\xi_{k}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{l}\min_{j\in[d]}2^{(H(p_{j}^{(i)})/\xi_{j}+o(1))n}\leq 2^{o(n)}\max_{p\in\Omega(t)}\min_{j\in[d]}2^{(H(p_{j}^{(i)})/\xi_{j}+o(1))n}.

By taking
n\rightarrow\infty
we get

\lim_{n\to\infty}\tau_{\xi}(\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\times n})^{1/n}\leq\max_{p\in\Omega(t)}\min_{j}2^{H(p_{j})/\xi_{j}}.\qed

We obtain the following theorem which generalizes
1.2
stated in the introduction.

Theorem 5.14

(Detailed version of
1.2
)
.

Let
t
be a complex tensor and
\xi\in\Xi
.
Then,

\widetilde{\SR}_{\xi}(t)=\min_{g\in\GL}\tilde{\tau}_{\xi}(\mathcal{H}_{g\cdot t}).

Proof.

This follows at once from
5.11
,
1.1
and
5.13
:

\widetilde{\SR}_{\xi}(t)=\min_{\theta\in\Theta}F_{\theta}(t)^{1/\braket{\theta,\xi}}=\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\left(\zeta^{\theta}(t)\right)^{1/\braket{\theta,\xi}}\!\!\!\!\!=\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\min_{g\in\GL}\left(\max_{p\in\Theta(g\cdot t)}2^{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}H(p_{i})}\right)^{1/\braket{\theta,\xi}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!=\min_{g\in\GL}\tilde{\tau}_{\xi}(\mathcal{H}_{t}).\qed

Remark 5.15
.

Alternatively,
5.14
can be proved by applying
1.3
to the function
F_{\xi}(p)\coloneq-\min_{i}H(p_{i})/\xi_{i}
(with
H(p_{i})/\xi_{i}=\infty
if
\xi_{i}=0
), which is a convex symmetric l.s.c. function on
\mathbbm{R}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbbm{R}^{n_{d}}
that takes a finite value at any point
p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{d})\in\mathbbm{R}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbbm{R}^{n_{d}}
such that each
p_{i}
is a probability distribution, hence in particular on any moment and support polytope.

5.3
G
-stable rank

Next we discuss a tensor parameter introduced by Derksen
[
18
]
called the
G
-stable rank.
Intuitively, it measures how fast
t
goes to zero under the action of one-parameter subgroups of
\GL
.
Derksen gave a general definition that applies to any perfect field and proved the following formula
[
18
, Cor. 4.2 and Thm. 4.4]
, which we will take as the definition:

Definition 5.16

(
G
-stable rank, fractional vertex cover)
.

For
\alpha\in\mathbbm{R}_{>0}^{d}
, we define the
G
-stable
\alpha
-rank
of a tensor
t\in\mathbbm{C}^{n_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{n_{d}}
as

\displaystyle\rk^{G}_{\alpha}(t)\coloneqq\min_{g\in\GL}\tau_{\alpha}^{f}(\mathcal{H}_{g\cdot t}),

(5.5)

where
\mathcal{H}_{g\cdot t}
denotes the hypergraph associated to the tensor
g\cdot t
(as in
Section

5.2
) and
\tau_{\alpha}^{f}
denotes the
\alpha
-weighted
fractional vertex cover
of a hypergraph, defined by the following linear program:

\begin{split}\tau^{f}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{H})=\min\;&\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\alpha_{i}u_{ij}\\
\text{s.t. }\,&u_{1,i_{1}}+u_{2,i_{2}}+\dots+u_{d,i_{d}}\geq 1\quad\text{ for every }e=(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{d})\in E(\mathcal{H})\\
&u\in\mathbbm{R}^{n_{1}+\dots+n_{d}}_{\geq 0}.\end{split}

(5.6)

The
G
-stable rank
is then defined as
\rk^{G}(t)\coloneqq\rk^{G}_{(1,\dots,1)}
, i.e.,
\alpha=(1,\dots,1)
is the all-ones vector.

The formula in
Eq.

5.5
should be interpreted as a formula in the spirit of Strassen’s support functionals.
Indeed, it is not hard to see that
\tau^{f}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{H}_{t})
only depends on the tensor’s support polytope.
The following proposition gives a concise expression:

Proposition 5.17
.

For every tensor
t
and every
\alpha\in\mathbbm{R}_{>0}^{d}
, we have

\tau_{\alpha}^{f}(\mathcal{H}_{t})=\max_{p\in\Omega(t)}\min_{i\in[d]}\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\lVert p_{i}\rVert_{\infty}}.

Proof.

By LP duality, the fractional vertex cover number of a hypergraph equals its fractional matching number.
In other words:

\begin{split}\tau^{f}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{H})=\max\;&\sum_{e\in E(\mathcal{H})}y_{e}\\
\text{s.t. }\,&\sum_{e\in E(\mathcal{H}),\ e_{i}=j}y_{e}\leq\alpha_{i}\quad\text{ for every }i\in[d],j\in[n_{i}],\\
&y\geq 0.\end{split}

By a change of variables
y=cz
, we may therefore rewrite
\tau^{f}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{H}_{t})
as the value of the following optimization problem:

\begin{split}\tau^{f}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{H}_{t})=\max&\quad c\\
\text{s.t. }\,&\sum_{e\in E(\mathcal{H})}z_{e}=1,\\
&\sum_{e\in E(\mathcal{H}),\ e_{i}=j}z_{e}\leq\frac{\alpha_{i}}{c}\quad\text{ for every }i\in[d],j\in[n_{i}],\\
&z\geq 0,c\geq 0.\end{split}

Note that
z
is a probability distribution on the edge set of
\mathcal{H}_{t}
(i.e., the support of
t
), so its marginals

(p_{i})_{j}\coloneqq\sum_{e_{i}=j}z_{e}

satisfy
(p_{1},\dots,p_{d})\in\Omega(t)
.
Conversely, every
p\in\Omega(t)
is the marginal distributions of a probability distribution on
E(\mathcal{H}_{t})
.
This implies that

\tau_{\alpha}^{f}(\mathcal{H}_{t})=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\Omega(t)\\
\forall i,\lVert p_{i}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{\alpha_{i}}{c}\end{subarray}}c=\max_{p\in\Omega(t)}\min_{i}\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\lVert p_{i}\rVert_{\infty}}.\qed

By an application of
1.3
, we will now show that
\rk^{G}_{\alpha}(t)
also admits a formula as an optimization problem over the entanglement polytope
\Delta(t)
, reproducing a result by Derksen.

Corollary 5.18

(
[
18
, Thm. 5.2]
)
.

For every tensor
t
and every
\alpha\in\mathbbm{R}_{>0}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\rk^{G}_{\alpha}(t)=\max_{p\in\Delta(t)}\min_{i\in[d]}\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\lVert p_{i}\rVert_{\infty}}.

Proof.

By
5.17
and (
5.5
) we get

\frac{1}{\rk^{G}_{\alpha}(t)}=\max_{g\in\GL}\min_{p\in\Omega(g\cdot t)}\max_{i\in[d]}\frac{\lVert p_{i}\rVert_{\infty}}{\alpha_{i}}.

The function
F_{\alpha}(p)\coloneqq\max_{i\in[d]}\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}\lVert p_{i}\rVert_{\infty}
is a convex symmetric continuous function on
\mathbbm{R}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbbm{R}^{n_{d}}
.
Hence, by an application of
1.3
, we obtain the desired result:

\frac{1}{\rk^{G}_{\alpha}(t)}=\min_{p\in\Delta(t)}\max_{i\in[d]}\frac{\lVert p_{i}\rVert_{\infty}}{\alpha_{i}},\qed

5.4
Non-commutative rank

A 3-tensor
t\in\mathbbm{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{m}
can be viewed as a matrix tuple
t=(A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{m})
, where the matrices
A_{i}\in\mathbbm{C}^{n\times n}
are its slices in the third direction.
For simplicity of presentation, we will assume that the slices satisfy

\bigcap_{i}\ker(A_{i})=0\quad\text{and}\quad\bigcap_{i}\ker(A_{i}^{\dagger})=0.

(5.7)

Definition 5.19

(Noncommutative rank)
.

The
non-commutative rank

\ncrk(t)
of a 3-tensor or matrix tuple
t=(A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{m})
is defined as the rank of the matrix of indeterminates

A(x)\coloneqq x_{1}A_{1}+x_{2}A_{2}+\dots+x_{m}A_{m},\qquad\ncrk(t)\coloneqq\rk(A(x)),

where
\rk(A(x))
is the matrix rank over the
free skew field

\mathbbm{C}\langle x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m}\rangle
.

In
[
29
]
, Hirai used a version of the strong duality theorem to recast the non-commutative rank as an optimization problem over the moment polytope with respect to the action of
\GL(n)\times\GL(n)
on matrix tuples
\mathbbm{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{m}
given by
(g,h)\cdot t:=(g\otimes h\otimes I)t
.
This action is often called the
left-right action
, as it sends a matrix tuple
(A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{m})
to
(gA_{1}h^{T},gA_{2}h^{T},\dots,gA_{m}h^{T})
.
Then Hirai’s result can be stated as follows:

Theorem 5.20

(
[
29
, Thm. 1.4]
)
.

For every 3-tensor
0\neq t\in\mathbbm{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{m}
whose slices satisfy (
5.7
), we have

\ncrk(t)=n-\frac{n}{2}\min_{p\in\Delta_{\mathrm{LR}}(t)}\lVert p-\frac{\bm{1}_{2n}}{n}\rVert_{1}=n-\frac{n}{2}\min_{(p_{1},p_{2})\in\Delta_{\mathrm{LR}}(t)}\lVert p_{1}-\frac{\bm{1}_{n}}{n}\rVert_{1}+\lVert p_{2}-\frac{\bm{1}_{n}}{n}\rVert_{1}

where we write
\Delta_{\mathrm{LR}}(t)
for the moment polytope of
t
associated with the left-right action and
\bm{1}_{k}\in\mathbbm{R}^{k}
for the all-ones vectors.

We will show how to reproduce this result using
1.3
.
Our starting point is the following well-known formula for the non-commutative rank established by Fortin and Reutenauer
[
21
]
:
for any matrix tuple
t=(A_{1},\dots,A_{m})
,

\ncrk(t)=2n-\max_{(g,h)\in\GL_{n}\times\GL_{n}}\,(a+b),\quad\text{where }\;\forall i\in[m],gA_{i}h^{\top}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}*&*\\
\hline\cr*&0_{a\times b}\end{array}\right).

The combinatorial quantity
a+b
can be understood via a bipartite graph
\mathcal{B}_{t}
that is naturally associated with the matrix tuple, in the following way.
Let
\mathcal{B}_{t}
have left and right vertex sets both indexed by
[n]
, and include an edge
(i,j)\in E(\mathcal{B}_{t})
whenever there exists
k\in[m]
such that
(A_{k})_{ij}\neq 0
.
Equivalently,
(i,j)\in E(\mathcal{B}_{t})
if
(e_{i},e_{j},e_{k})\in\supp(t)
for some
k
.
In graph-theoretic language,
2n-(a+b)
is the size of a minimum vertex cover of the graph
\mathcal{B}_{t}
, i.e., the vertex cover number
\tau(\mathcal{B}_{t})
.
Hence, the Fortin-Reutenauer formula states that

\ncrk(t)=\min_{(g,h)\in\GL(n)\times\GL(n)}\tau(\mathcal{B}_{(g\otimes h\otimes I_{m})t}).

(5.8)

Kőnig’s theorem implies that the vertex cover number of a bipartite graph
\mathcal{B}
equals the
fractional
vertex cover number.
That is:

\begin{split}\tau(\mathcal{B})=\min\;&\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}v_{j}\\
\text{s.t. }&\forall(i,j)\in E(B),\;u_{i}+v_{j}\geq 1\\
\quad&u,v\geq 0.\end{split}

(
\star
)

The next proposition shows that the value of the above linear program for
\mathcal{B}_{t}
can be obtained by a convex optimization problem over the polytope
\Omega_{\mathrm{LR}}(t)\coloneqq\conv\{(e_{i},e_{j})\mid(i,j)\in E(\mathcal{B}_{t})\}
, which is the support polytope for the left-right action.

Proposition 5.21
.

Suppose
0\neq t\in\mathbbm{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{m}
and (
5.7
) holds.
Then,

\tau(\mathcal{B}_{t})=n-\frac{n}{2}\min_{p\in\Omega_{\mathrm{LR}}(t)}\,\lVert p-\frac{\bm{1}_{2n}}{n}\rVert_{1},

where
\bm{1}_{2n}\in\mathbbm{R}^{2n}
denotes the all-ones vector.

Proof.

Note that this assumption translates to
\mathcal{B}_{t}
not having isolated vertices.

Let’s denote
\omega_{2n}\coloneqq\frac{\bm{1}_{2n}}{n}
and
consider
F(p)\coloneqq\lVert p-\omega_{2n}\rVert_{1}
, defined on
\mathbbm{R}^{2n}
.
Using the fact that
\lVert p\rVert_{1}=\sup_{\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1}p^{T}x
, we calculate the Legendre–Fenchel conjugate

\begin{split}F^{*}(x)&=\sup_{p\in\mathbbm{R}^{2n}}\,\langle p,x\rangle-\lVert p-\omega_{2n}\rVert_{1}\\
&=\sup_{p\in\mathbbm{R}^{2n}}\,\left(\langle p,x\rangle+\min_{\lVert y\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1}-\langle p-\omega_{2n},y\rangle\right)\\
&=\min_{\lVert y\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1}\sup_{p\in\mathbbm{R}^{2n}}\langle p,x-y\rangle+\langle\omega_{2n},y\rangle\\
&=\begin{cases}\langle\omega_{2n},x\rangle&\text{ if }\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1\\
\infty&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}\end{split}

where we use von Neumann’s minimax theorem in the third equality.

Now consider the indicator function
\delta_{\Omega_{\mathrm{LR}}(t)}
of
\Omega_{\mathrm{LR}}(t)
, defined as
\delta_{\Omega_{\mathrm{LR}}(t)}(p)=0
if
p\in\Omega_{\mathrm{LR}}(t)
and
\infty
otherwise.
Its conjugate

\delta^{*}_{\Omega_{\mathrm{LR}}(t)}(x)=\max_{p\in\Omega_{\mathrm{LR}}(t)}\langle p,x\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x=(a,b)}}{{=}}\max_{(i,j)\in E(\mathcal{B}_{t})}a_{i}+b_{j}

is the support function of
\Omega_{\mathrm{LR}}(t)
.

We have

\min_{p\in\Omega_{\mathrm{LR}}(t)}F(p)=\sup_{x\in\mathbbm{R}^{2n}}-F^{*}(x)-\delta_{\Omega_{\mathrm{LR}}(t)}^{*}(-x)=\max_{\lVert(a,b)\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1}\left[-\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{i}a_{i}+\sum_{j}b_{j}\right)+\min_{(i,j)\in E(B)}a_{i}+b_{j}\right]

where we use
\min_{p\in\Omega_{\mathrm{LR}}(t)}F(p)=\min_{p\in\mathbbm{R}^{2n}}F(p)+\delta_{\Omega_{\mathrm{LR}}}(p)
and apply Fenchel’s duality
[
39
, Theorem 31.1]
in the first equality.

By a change of variables
(c,d)=-(a,b)
, we can write the above maximization problem as a linear program (note that
\lVert(a,b)\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1
is equivalent to
-\bm{1}_{n}\leq a,b\leq\bm{1}_{n}
) :

\begin{split}\max\;\;&\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{i}c_{i}+\sum_{j}d_{j}\right)-t\\
\text{s.t. }\;\;&\;\;c_{i}+d_{j}\leq t,\quad\text{for every }(i,j)\in E(\mathcal{B}_{t}),\;\\
\;\;&-\bm{1}_{n}\leq c,d\leq\bm{1}_{n}.\end{split}

(5.9)

It remains to show that
n-\frac{n}{2}\times\text{(the optimal value of \eqref{eq:LP})}
equals the optimal value of (

⋆

‣
5.4
).

We now prove that (
5.9
) has an optimal solution with
t=0
:
Suppose
x=(c,d,t)
is an optimal solution and set
\tilde{c}_{i}=c_{i}-\frac{t}{2},\tilde{d}_{i}=d_{i}-\frac{t}{2}
.
We claim that
\tilde{x}=(\tilde{c},\tilde{d},0)
is also an optimal solution.
It is easy to check that the the objective function takes the same value on
\tilde{x}
.
Hence, we only need to show that
\tilde{x}
is feasible.
For
(i,j)\in E(\mathcal{B}_{t})
, we have
\tilde{c}_{i}+\tilde{d}_{j}=c_{i}+d_{j}-t\leq 0
, so it remains to show that
-\bm{1}_{n}\leq\tilde{c},\tilde{d}\leq\bm{1}_{n}
.
There are two cases.
If
t<0
, then for all
i
,
\tilde{c}_{i}>c_{i}
and
\tilde{d}_{i}>d_{i}
.
Suppose there exists
i
such that
\tilde{c}_{i}>1
.
Since
\mathcal{B}_{t}
has no isolated vertices by (
5.7
), there is a
j
such that
(i,j)\in E(\mathcal{B}_{t})
.
Then,
\tilde{c}_{i}>1
implies
c_{i}>1+\frac{t}{2}
.
Since
d_{j}\geq-1
,
c_{i}+d_{j}>\frac{t}{2}>t
, which is a contradiction.
By a symmetric argument, we get
\tilde{d}_{j}\leq 1
.
If
