Clean energy, environmental policy and energy justice as drivers of sustainable development in OECD countries
Authors: Romanus Osabohien, Kingsley Imandojemu, Amar Hisham Jaaffar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough OECD countries face some of the pervasive levels of sustainable development amidst the recent call for climate action, energy justice and energy transition and its associated effects, the avalanche of literature on this issue tends to concentrate on high-income regions where data is more readily available. This disparity hindered efforts to improve long-term growth, alleviate pressure on the environment, and foster economic recovery. To disentangle whether this subpar performance of sustainable development is due to the application of clean energy or fossil energy, this study assesses and compares the two main types of energy—clean energy and fossil energy—within the context of OECD countries, focusing on their sustainable development impact across various quantiles. In this paper, the novel Method of Moment Quantile Regression was employed, with data ranging from 2000 to 2023 across OECD countries. The findings indicate that renewable energy used as a proxy for clean energy corresponds to improvement in sustainable development across all quantiles. Conversely, non-renewable energy tends to cause a decline in sustainable development across all quantiles. Furthermore, the findings of the study showed that environmental regulations, globalization and financial access improves the level of sustainable development. The study using a bootstrap quantile methodology for robustness testing, revealed comparable outcomes, particularly regarding the direction of the relationship between the variables of interest. The study recommended that the government of OECD countries should encourage the adoption of clean energy while placing premium on strict environmental policies to effectively achieve sustainable development.Keywords: Sustainable development, Clean energy, Fossil energy, Energy justice, Environmental policy, Method of moment quantile regression modelSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

OECD countries, as the global economic and innovative leaders, face the immediate imperative of balancing environmental sustainability with social and economic demands. Growing concern about ecological harm, climate change, and resource depletion has highlighted the imperative to mainstream energy strategies—renewable and non-renewable—into growth strategies to ensure long-term resilience and well-being1,2. This is consistent with the overall agenda of the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), where the simultaneous objectives of ensuring access to reliable, affordable, sustainable, and modern energy by 2030 (SDG 7) and immediate climate action (SDG 13)3–6 are given priority. Target 7.2 in particular highlights expansion of the share of renewable energy in the global energy mix as central to the encouragement of low-carbon transitions7,8.

Clean technologies, such as solar, wind, and hydropower, have been globally recognized to have the potential to reduce ecological footprints and unclamp growth from environmental damage9. Empirical realities confirm that hydropower and other renewable uptake guarantee energy efficiency and sustainability10, and re-specifications of the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) suggest renewable-based power generation can effectively cut back emissions and guarantee sustainable development1. However, reliance on non-renewables continues to pose sustainability issues. Consumption of fossil fuels has been discovered to exacerbate greenhouse gas emissions and environmental degradation11, yet technological advance in the shapes of efficiency improvements and carbon capture has been reported as avenues for mitigation.

Policy design is crucial in managing these dynamics. Properly designed environmental regulations have been asserted by the Porter Hypothesis to enhance innovation and competitiveness12,13, while institutional quality is a key moderator of sustainable impacts14. Evidence from OECD countries also confirms that stringent environmental regulations accelerate energy transitions and green sustainability15. Most importantly, opportunities of clean energy extend beyond decarbonization to economic gains such as job creation, stable growth, and increased access to energy production, hence raising questions of justice in sharing the benefits. Energy transition, therefore, is not a mere technological transformation; it is a transformation process based on equity, inclusion, and human development. Stricter environmental policies can impose higher short-term costs, including increased energy prices, but provide long-term welfare gains in terms of improved public health and reduced pollution damage. The energy justice approach provides tools to measure equity in such processes, focusing on awareness of affected groups, equal distribution of energy services, and participatory decision-making16,17.

Despite progress, the literature remains sparse in capturing the impact of clean energy, environmental policy, and energy justice on sustainable development. Traditional econometric methods have struggled to capture these varied and complex interactions. For example, while studies such as18 examine environmental impacts of economic development under the EKC framework, they ignore the interplay of policy and justice. Similarly, research that highlights the role of green innovation and growth19,20 underestimates broader dynamics in the energy system. This limitation calls for the use of a more comprehensive approach. Therefore, the present paper employs the Method of Moment Quantile Regression (MMQR), a technique formulated by21, which is especially well-suited to uncover heterogeneous effects across the conditional distribution of outcomes in sustainable development. Contrary to traditional models, MMQR allows for quantile-specific differences in the relationships between clean energy adoption, environmental policy, technological change, and globalization to be explored, and hence enlightened understanding of how these drivers influence sustainable development in varying environments. Considering the heterogeneity of the OECD across energy infrastructure, policy institutions, and economic foundations, MMQR provides tailored evidence previously overlooked. Overall, this study adds to the existing body of knowledge by filling the gap among clean energy, environmental policy, energy justice and sustainable development through a robust methodological approach. Through this, it not only adds to theoretical insights but also informs policy strategies aimed towards striking a balance between economic development and environmental sustainability in advanced economies.

Theoretical review

The concept of sustainable development, as popularised by the Brundtland Commission, emphasises meeting the needs of the present generation without compromising the ability of future generations to do likewise22. Essentially, this view presupposes that economic development is inevitable but must proceed in a way that safeguards environmental resources and intergenerational welfare. This perception, which has been termed weak sustainability, assumes that natural capital can be replaced to a great degree by physical and human capital, provided the overall stock of capital is preserved over time23. A broader perspective, however, is the vision that envisions sustainable development in terms not only of growth but of enhanced sustenance, self-respect, and liberty—categories that include both economic and political liberties24. Sen’s vision of development as freedom aligns with this perspective, emphasizing the freedom to choose, to engage, and to live in dignity. Geographically, this has been represented as the overlap of the three Es—Environment, Economy, and Equity—implying that sustainability requires balance among people, planet, and prosperity25.

Climate change underlines the necessity of achieving such balance. It affects the whole spectrum of human activity and addresses all seventeen SDGs, thereby outlining possible development trajectories24. While degrowth scholars advocate for contraction in GDP in order to sustain finite resources and avert ecological collapse26, green growth has been embraced by the mainstream sustainable development agenda. Green growth calls for technological innovation and cleaner production methods that enable long-run economic growth while curbing environmental degradation15,23,27,28. This function is consistent with theories such as ecological modernisation29 and the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) hypothesis30 which propose that environmental degradation rises with income at first but subsequently falls as societies invest in cleaner technology and more robust environmental regulation31.

Empirical studies, however, demonstrate EKC findings to be model specification-, explanatory variable-, and econometric method-sensitive, bringing its generalisability into question32,33. This divergence highlights the contribution of geography and institutions in determining long-lasting outcomes. Gallup et al.25 argue that the influence of geography on development is via institutional quality, which influences the extent to which policy directives are well designed and implemented. Here, theories such as the Pollution Haven Hypothesis and the Race to the Bottom foresee that loose regulations draw in environmentally harmful capital, while the Race to the Top contends that strong governance can enhance environmental standards without discouraging investment.

Within this conceptual framework, the congruence of clean energy, policy, and energy justice is central to operationalising sustainable development. The clean energy is the technological impetus of decoupling growth from emissions, and policy institutions, extending from environmental regulation to green fiscal instruments, provide the institutional support for uptake. Energy justice renders these transitions equitable, distributing costs and benefits fairly, locating vulnerable groups, and ensuring participatory inclusiveness in decision-making. These three pillars engage with each other dynamically: good policy can guide investment to clean energy, and energy justice institutions socialize and politicize the policy, rendering it less resisted and more inclusive. Deployment of clean energy, on the other hand, enables long-term environmental quality, entrenching the pillars of sustainable development.

Empirical review

Energy remains the principal engine of growth in OECD economies but their carbon footprint is enormous: in 2021 the OECD accounted for 33.3 per cent of total world CO₂ emissions, above the combined shares of the African Union, European Union, the Americas and the Middle East, posing pressing sustainability challenges for advanced, growth-driven nations34. Though economists and environmental scientists advocate the replacement of fossil fuels with clean sources, the transition is daunting and costly, particularly for those countries which are still structurally hydrocarbon-dependent3,35–37. A range of domestically driven “enablers” is thus emphasized—infrastructure-upgrading, institution-building, accelerating digitalisation, education-enhancement, increasing trade openness and driving technology, each capable of mitigating the short-term bottlenecks to the energy transition14,28,36,38–41. Unstable fossil-fuel prices, which are compounded by geopolitical risk, strife and contest over trade policy, compound these issues; secure electricity supply remains a development pillar, once more underlining energy security as a policy objective3,28,36,37,42–45.

More recent studies highlight that sustained sustainability relies on the scaling of renewables, the adoption of draconian environmental policies (such as taxation), the integration of energy justice values and the spreading of clean-tech innovations3,45–47. There is evidence that a confluence of renewable deployment and quality institutions domesticates environmental degradation, indicating that good governance is an adjunct to technology48.

Economic complexity and environmental governance are stabilizers in OECD environments; provided governance remains adequate, renewables have the ability to counteract instability arising from geopolitical shocks49. Beyond the OECD, transitions to cleaner energy reduce carbon emissions and slow resource depletion while sustaining growth2, and technology-specific results—like China’s hydro and renewable electricity—demonstrate measurable emissions reductions10. Regulatory ambition matters: proactive policies accelerate clean-energy adoption and climate target attainment15, and policy innovation helps manage growth while conserving20. At the global governance level, balancing mechanisms for the Sustainable Development Goals promotes policy coherence between energy and development policy-making50, and green fiscal policies regulate prices and promote efficiency, especially in post-pandemic recovery51.

Finance and technology are recurring levers. Green investment, natural-resource management and innovation all drive ecologization of development52, and green finance optimizes metropolitan metabolic efficiency and environmental performance27. Methodologically, innovations such as Method of Moments Quantile Regression provide distribution-aware estimates that reveal heterogeneous clean energy gains over economic settings, including the OECD21. Redoing the Environmental Kuznets Curve means that ramping up renewables can slow the income–pollution curve, but growth in expansion is insufficient without structural transformation in energy and resource use1,18. Provincial Chinese data confirm that deliberate clean-energy investment and stringent policy are central to decoupling emissions from growth53. Coordinated policy frameworks are also central to OECD ecologization54. Market structure matters too: higher business density can drive innovation and speed up renewable adoption, driving environmental improvement55. Behavioural factors remain relevant, with early research associating ecological concern with pro-environmental actions such as recycling56.

Running through repeatedly is heterogeneity: renewable-energy impacts vary by institutional capacity, economic structure, technology mix and geopolitical risk profile, and therefore policy must be designed on a case-by-case basis11,49,57. Transition paths and sustainability outcomes are then shaped further by globalisation forces2. Opportunities and tensions in technical, sectoral, and integration terms for end-use energy decarbonisation are demonstrated in renewable heating58. Overall, the literature presents sustainable development in developed nations as an equilibrium problem in multiple dimensions at the intersection of energy systems, environmental governance, finance, technology and behaviour. Key gaps still exist. In the first place, the socio-economic distribution of the benefits of clean-energy gains is still insufficiently mapped21. In the second place, the geopolitics of transition in progress, especially in the OECD, are still insufficiently empirically explored49. Third, the synthesis of behavioral insights with modern econometrics (e.g., MMQR) can yield a richer understanding of how policies, institutions and technologies collectively build credible paths to sustainability21.

Methodology

Model specification

The empirical framework of this study is grounded in the STIRPAT model, which was originally developed from the IPAT model (Impact, Population, Affluence and Technology). The IPAT model serves as a foundational basis for identifying the drivers of sustainability impact. A significant advantage of the IPAT model is its basis in sustainability principles, which allows for an understanding of how variations in driving forces can influence sustainable impacts. While prior research has predominantly utilized the STIRPAT model, this analysis enhances the theoretical framework by distinguishing the effects of conventional technology from those of environmentally friendly innovations on decarbonization, utilizing patent data (referred to as PAT in this study). Furthermore, the inclusion of financial access (FA) aligns with the directives of the 29th Conference of the Parties (COP) and globalization, thereby augmenting the reliability and explanatory power of our research findings.

Employing the STRIPAT framework following59,60, we constructed a linear model to examine the relationship among clean energy and sustainable development. Our choice to investigate pairs of independent variables stemmed from a central objective: to evaluate the impact of the shift from fossil fuels to clean energy on sustainable development goals (SDGs). In order to investigate the relationship between clean energy and sustainable development, our research employs a panel time series model that incorporates six distinct variables. Sustainable development is represented by the Sustainable Development Index (SDG), which serves as the dependent variable. The primary variables of interest include renewable energy consumption, expressed as a percentage of total final energy consumption (REW), and non-renewable energy consumption, also as a percentage of total (NREW). the KOF Index of Globalization—which encompasses economic, political, and social dimensions (GLO)—as well as patents related to environmental technologies (PAT) and measures of financial access, efficiency, and depth of development (FA) are included. These variables collectively represent globalization, technological advancement, and financial access.

The Moments of Moment Quantile Regression (MMQR) technique, developed by21, is employed in this study. This method seeks to estimate the parameters of a regression model by minimizing a loss function that reflects the difference between the empirical and theoretical moments of the conditional distribution of the dependent variable21. The importance of MMQR lies in its ability to reveal how the variables that influence a phenomenon vary across different quantiles of the distribution, thereby offering insights that extend beyond those provided by ordinary least squares (OLS) regression. Unlike traditional regression methods, MMQR accounts for data variability, enabling researchers to observe the interactions among variables at various distributional points, particularly in the presence of extreme outliers or skewed data. This methodology is especially pertinent in economic and financial research, where risks and returns may display distinct characteristics at different quantile levels. This approach facilitates the identification of relationships between series at different points within the dispersion of the explained series. Description of data

This study utilizes a comprehensive panel dataset encompassing 18 OECD (Australia, Austria, Canada, Czech Republic, Finland, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Poland, Portugal, Slovak Republic, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, Turkey, United Kingdom and United States) countries from 2000 to 2023, contingent upon data availability. Sustainable development is assessed through the Sustainable Development Index (SDG). The measurement of technological advancements is conducted via patents related to environmental technologies (PAT). Renewable energy consumption (REW), serving as a proxy for clean energy, is quantified based on the percentage of total final energy consumption attributed to renewable sources. In alignment with existing literature, fossil fuel is represented by non-renewable energy consumption (NREW). Furthermore, environmental policy is evaluated using the Environmental Policy Stringency Index (EP), which reflects the degree of adherence to environmental regulations. Globalization is examined through its social, political, and economic dimensions, all of which are integrated into the overall KOF Index of globalization. Premised on literature guidance from61,62, understanding of the conceptualization of energy justice, scientific rigor, comprehensiveness, and data availability, an energy justice index was constructed from three dimensions access to electricity, rural (% of rural population), and urban (% of urban population (See61,62 for clarity on the indicator). The averaging method was employed to calculate the energy justice index, while assigning equal weights to each indicator.

Detailed information regarding the variables under study is presented in Table 1, while Table 2 provides the descriptive statistics for these variables. It is noteworthy that all the variables of interest namely Sustainable Development Index (SDG), patents related to environmental technologies (PAT), Renewable energy consumption (REW), Non-Renewable energy consumption (NREW), Environmental Policy Stringency Index (EP), Globalization (GLO) and Financial Access (FA) are maintained in their original form. The findings from the descriptive statistics for the dataset used in this study are presented in Table 1. All variables exhibit positive mean and median values, with PAT having the highest mean and FA the lowest among them. Furthermore, a comparative analysis of the maximum, minimum, mean, and median values reveals that the other variables also experienced significant growth in the sampled nations throughout the study duration. Overall, the descriptive statistics suggest that the dataset is well-suited for the intended research.

Preliminary analysis

Contour plot results

The study employed contour plots to depict the movement of the dependent variables in relation to the two independent variables of interest. Figure 1a–h contour plots show how the dependent variables vary as a function of pairs of dominant explanatory factors, and the smoothness of the surfaces signifies asymmetric, non-linear interactions. The findings from all panels indicate that the sustainability impact of non-renewable (REW) and renewable energy (NREW) is highly dependent on policy, financial, technology, and the global environments in which they exist.

(a–h) Contour plot.

Figure 1a–d show renewable energy impacts environmental policy (EP), access to finance (FA), patenting (PAT), and globalisation (GLO) to drive sustainable development (SDG). Figure 1a REW–EP surface shows complementarity: renewable deployment has low returns for weak policy but, when EP is strong, the same increase in REW gives much greater SDG gains. This underscores the facilitatory function of credible regulation to unlock private investment, lower transition risks, and secure public acceptability through fair burden-sharing.

Figure 1b indicates that finance has a tipping point effect. Where renewable penetration is low, increasing FA can promote carbon-intensive borrowing and consumption at the cost of SDG. At a tipping point level, though, FA promotes enterprise and household penetration of renewables, tipping results in the positive direction. This implies the need for green taxonomies, concessionary lines, and sustainability-linked credit to channel liquidity into clean assets while maintaining mass inclusion. Figure 1c illustrates the need for innovation: although renewable gains are moderated in low PAT levels, greater innovation capability allows REW to yield more SDG dividends. This emphasizes how capital-intensive knowledge assets, supply chains, and absorptive capacity amplify the benefits of renewables with skills-based, wage, and regional competitiveness consequences. Figure 1d illustrates a two-edged openness effect: without standards, GLO can export volatility and carbon leakage; with quality governance, it enables industry transfer and cost reduction, optimizing renewable benefits. Socially, this influences how transition costs are distributed fairly between workers and regions.

Figures 1e–h shift attention to non-renewables. Figure 1e shows the NREW–EP surface of regulation-induced innovation: with moderately tightened policy, clean R&D is encouraged but abrupt tightening will crowd out investment. Sequenced and anticipatory EP is therefore crucial, along with helping workers and carbon-resource-based areas. Figure 1f shows that finance within a fossil-dominated mix can lengthen brown credit and suppress SDG unless combined with green eligibility criteria and disclosure standards. Guided properly, FA can reinforce efficiency improvements and transition finance. The social issue is affordability, requiring protection so poor households are not inappropriately weighted. Figure 1g reinforces that innovation can offset partially the maladies of non-renewables through efficiency and abatement gains but with risks of rebound and lock-in. This speaks to standards that substitute for and not clean up new processes, and to industrial green work and resilience. Finally, Fig. 1h shows that in the absence of robust governance, openness coupled with high NREW can externalise pollution and polluting investment. But where there are border-adjusted standards and institutional quality, GLO can take on cleaner practices and capital that drive SDG up. Here, just-transition policies linked to investment and trade are essential to ensure that regions associated with the fossil economy are not left behind.

Collectively, these eight surfaces outline a consistent narrative. Renewable energy yields the best sustainable development impacts when supported by robust policy, focused finance, engaging innovation systems, and well-governed global connectivity. Dependence on non-renewable resources need not exclude progress altogether, provided that environmental control is phased to promote innovation, capital is channelled into transition assets, and openness is coupled with institutional safeguarding. The chaotic, lopsided tendencies affirm that individual, one-size-fits-all approaches are not equal to the challenge. Instead, effective transition strategy requires threshold-sensitive policy, institutional credibility, and attention to energy justice. From an economic perspective, this means synchronizing instruments to reduce risk, lower capital cost, and manage legacy assets. Socially, it demands protecting poor families, investing in human capital and mobility, and decision-making that is inclusive so that the transition, effective or not, is fair.

Cross-Sectional Dependence (CSD) is one of the most critical concerns to address before conducting panel analysis since it can result in major misstatements of statistical significance thresholds63. It may occur in the presence of shared shocks or undiscovered components, which constitute part of the error term. In our situation, cross-sectional reliance must be examined because the OECD is a coalition of global economies whose member countries share numerous fundamental economic, social, and political regulations and behaviours. Several tests for cross dependency have been proposed, including the Lagrange Multiplier (LMBP) test and the CDP test64. Nevertheless, when N is big and T is relatively small, the LMBP test becomes inapplicable since estimating the LMBP test statistic under such conditions results in significant size distortion65. In addition, the CDP test is a pre-estimation test that can be used before producing estimates. Thus, a CDP test is used in this investigation. Table 2 shows the CDP test results. The null hypothesis of cross-sectional independence was rejected at the 1% level in all variables. It implies that all variables have cross-sectional dependence, implying that they are correlated across panel groups. Following such a conclusion, further empirical analysis is performed with the presence of CSD.

The panel unit root tests were used to determine stationarity and generate a valid estimate for the panel data variables. According to66–68, there are two types of panel unit root tests: first-generation and second-generation. The initial generation of tests, including Breitung, Lin Chu (LLC), and Hadri, cannot address cross-sectional dependence difficulties. The second-generation panel unit roots typically imply cross-sectional dependency and homogeneity; consequently, in this case, the second-generation tests are most suited to the investigation. The second-generation panel unit root addresses the dual problems of homogeneity and cross-sectional dependence, as described by66–68. The Fisher ADF, IM Pesaran Shin, and Fisher PP are the root tests used in the second-generation panel units. Table 3 shows the results of the unit root testing. The study’s variable was found to be stationary at a 5% significance level at levels and first difference conditions. With this conclusion, we are confident that our study’s regression will produce a more credible estimate than if the dataset contained a unit root.

Results and discussions

Method of moment quantile regression outcomes

This section summarizes the results of the baseline model, in which we investigated the impact of clean energy on sustainable development (SDG) across all quantiles of SDG. Discussion of findings

The results highlight the differential effect of energy sources, technological innovation, globalization, and financial inclusion in improving sustainable development outcomes across OECD countries. Clean energy, proxied by the consumption of renewable energy, has a positive impact on sustainable development throughout, though its impact is statistically significant only at the middle to upper quantiles. This aligns with the Brundtland Commission’s weak sustainability principle of maintaining aggregate capital for intergenerational well-being while promoting long-run growth strategies over contractionary degrowth models22,23. Economically, this suggests that renewable energy not only prevents environmental damage but also emerges as a growth-friendly strategy in as much as it develops new industries, employment, and technological spillovers underpinning productivity. The results are that countries with more favourable sustainable development indices benefit more from the adoption of renewable energy, confirming earlier evidence that transitions in renewable energy improve environmental outcomes and reduce carbon footprints2,10. This means that economies further along the sustainability path are more capable of internalizing the environmental value of renewables, which supports the hypothesis that the expansion of renewables creates positive externalities and reduces long-term economic risks linked to fossil reliance. Conversely, non-renewable energy demonstrates a strong negative relationship with sustainable development across quantiles, echoing findings in ASEAN and G20 contexts that fossil reliance undermines both environmental stability and socio-economic prosperity23,40,75. This confirms the ecological modernization argument that policy and technological innovations are necessary to break the fossil-fuel growth lock-in29,30. Economically, continued reliance on fossil fuels implies rising costs in the form of health expenditures, environmental remediation, and stranded assets, which circumscribe fiscal space and erode competitiveness in an increasingly decarbonized global economy.

Technological innovation, proxied by patents, demonstrates ambivalent effects. In the lower to middle quantiles, patents significantly contribute to sustainable development, and this suggests that innovation and adoption of clean technologies enable growth and environmental sustainability, consistent with Sen’s emphasis on freedom of choice and societal capability24 and Pelsa and Balina’s “three Es” strategy of economy, equity, and environment25.

Economic intuition is that early-stage technological progress generates productivity spillovers, lowers transition costs, and enhances the competitiveness of firms, especially if environmental technologies are embedded in industrial processes. At higher quantiles, however, the effect turns negative, implying saturation, adaptation lags, or structural weaknesses in assimilating advanced technologies, something that finds an echo in Halkos and Tsilika’s argument that empirical findings are a function of econometric choices and contextual differences33. This suggests diminishing marginal returns to technological change under weak institutional regimes, market incentives, or absorptive capacities. For policy makers, this highlights that it is not enough to increase patent levels only; investment in diffusion, competences, and infrastructure as complements is necessary to maximize technology exploitation for sustainability. This divergence highlights the role of governance, market structures, and institutional readiness in conditioning outcomes25.

Globalization also seems to be a significant enabler of sustainable development with positive coefficients in most quantiles. The results suggest that international trade and financial integration enhance the capacity of countries to achieve SDGs through cooperation, knowledge transfer, and harmonized regulation, which validates studies on institutional quality and ecological footprints for developing economies2,48. Economically, globalization makes possible scale efficiencies, inward capital flows, and innovation diffusion, while it opens new markets for green products and services. This confirms the “race to the top” theory wherein openness attracts clean investment and strengthens environmental standards rather than perpetuating pollution havens25. At the same time, the implication is that both liberalization of trade and investment need to be strategically managed to avoid external shocks and encourage resilience since excessive dependence on international markets could also expose economies to geopolitical threats. Financial access also has strong positive effects across all quantiles, even though the marginal benefits decrease at higher levels.

The long-lastingness of these constructive effects illustrates the usefulness of green finance and inclusive liquidity to support the implementation of renewables, the abandoning of environmentally expensive activities, and the advancement of the SDGs27,52. The economic rationale is that access to finance relaxes credit constraints, enabling households and firms to invest in clean technology, energy efficiency approaches, and human capital development. However, the declining marginal effect at higher quantiles suggests that after a point of financial inclusion, additional access delivers modest sustainability returns unless it is directed to green productive investments.

The interactive role of environmental policy in these trends is also supportive. Stringent environmental policy significantly increases the role of renewable energy in sustainable development, supporting evidence from OECD contexts that policy novelty and regulatory enforcement are critical to coupling economic development with environmental stability15,20. Economically, regulatory strictness reduces harmful externalities, levels playing fields for green industries, and internalizes environmental costs that markets tend to overlook. With policy coherence and institutional strengthening, environmental policies embody the “race to the top” effect, where countries strengthen regulations not to deter investment but to safeguard sustainability. Energy justice also interacts the renewable energy–sustainable development nexus, with equitable access to electricity in rural and urban areas supplementing renewable uptake and supporting inclusive growth. This is consistent with Sen’s view of development as freedom24, as energy justice expands opportunity and social participation and decreases environmental degradation61. Economic reasoning here is that improved access decreases inequality, increases labour productivity, and supports structural transformation by extending reliable energy to underserved populations. The results demonstrate the impact of clean energy on sustainability as direct and indirect, through the mediation of policy institutionalization and equity of distribution systems.

In sum, the evidence suggests sustainable development in OECD economies to be determined by a complex interrelationship among clean energy deployment, technological progress, globalization, financial access, and institutional mediation. These results are in line with the emerging narrative of green growth, ecological modernization, and environmental Kuznets dynamics that illustrate how policy innovation and renewable energy can decouple environmental costs of growth1,18. The economic implication is that green transformations are not only environmentally desirable but also economically rational, reducing long-run costs, creating competitive advantage, and safeguarding intergenerational welfare. However, they also highlight the necessity for context-specific approaches because variations in technological absorption, institutional capacity, and social equity continue to mark the heterogeneity of outcomes. This reaffirms that sustainable development is not a straight line but requires tailored economic policies that integrate innovation, financial systems, and governance to balance growth, equity, and protection of the environment.

Conclusion and policy directions

The cumulative impact of human-caused carbon emissions has exacerbated climate change. The climate catastrophe has far-reaching consequences around the world. This situation has created serious concerns about sustainable development on both the national and international levels. Clean energy is transforming the economic landscape by providing sustainable and environmentally friendly alternatives to traditional fossil fuels. These advancements play a crucial role in addressing climate change, fostering economic growth, and creating new job opportunities within the sustainable development framework. Numerous academic studies have investigated the variables that contribute to or hinder sustainable development from a variety of perspectives. Some of the literature pushes for the use of clean energy; nevertheless, this study contends that haphazard solutions may not produce long-term results unless they are built on strong empirical analysis. As a result, we investigated the relationship between clean energy and sustainable development in the OECD’s. Aside from clean energy, this analysis illustrates the interactive role of environmental policies and energy justice.

This paper introduces a novel framework for understanding the nexus between clean energy proxied as renewable energy consumption (REW) on sustainable development (SDG) across the OECD nations from 2000 to 2023. Utilizing the moment quantile regression (MMQR) method as the primary analytical tool, the study reveals that while REW exerts a positive influence on SDG, across all quantile levels. In contrast, the examination of non-renewable energy consumption indicates that renewable energy consumption (REW) has a negative impact on sustainable development goals (SDG), thereby offering a more detailed perspective on the adverse effects of fossil fuel energy on OECD economies. Furthermore, the interactive variables, including environmental policy (EP), and energy justice (EJ), exhibited a consistent positive influence on sustainable development.

In light of these findings, the study proposes several policy recommendations. Identification of asymmetric differences allows policymakers to formulate specific policies that are attuned to the unique challenges and opportunities of each region. Resolution of such heterogeneities allows specialized measures to pull out the best in sustainable development. Finally, identification of complementarities across various regions provides a ground for more cooperation and collective work, which are crucial for effective sustainable development. Through the use of local resources, resource pooling, provision of best-practice sharing, and clean energy transfers, the stakeholders can be enabled to collaborate in joint research and development of high returns.

It is not just a question of policy-making; professionals in industry can benefit through better awareness by making strategic collaborations, minimizing the dispersion of resources, and capitalizing on new market opportunities. These activities not only make competition higher, but also enhance the adoption of more sustainable management practices. The results in this study are important because their applications in real cases can generate improved strategies, ensure cooperation among OECD countries, and have direct impacts on the sustainability result. Policy steps directed toward definite impacts are essential to adequately respond to regional imbalances of sustainable development. As an example, setting tough environmental protection measures to be adopted in the regions where more advanced sustainable activities are followed would be likely to drive enterprises towards cleaner energy. The lagging regions, on the other hand, could be driven to pursue the increasing use of clean energy, and environmental policy for sustainability change.

To promote sustainable development, policymakers in the OECDs countries must reduce their investments in non-renewable energy sources and instead focus on increasing funding for clean and sustainable energy alternatives. By fostering a shift towards more environmentally friendly energy production and consumption, this transition to renewable energy will generate green employment opportunities. Energy is often recognized as a vital component in the advancement of the sustainable development. Therefore, policymakers in these nations should consider investing in more efficient technologies, as these initiatives will conserve energy and subsequently decrease environmental issues resulting from fossil fuel combustion. This approach will facilitate the transition to cleaner energy utilization. Additionally, implementing energy justice aimed at enhancing energy access can serve as effective policy options that encourage the adoption of cleaner production by tracking and rewarding progress towards sustainability objectives. Moreover, there is a pressing need to bolster existing environmental policies and energy justice in light of advancements in clean energy technology to ensure that economic growth does not reach a point where the scale effect surpasses the technological effect. Beyond this threshold, further attempts to decarbonize the production process may prove ineffective, rendering sustainable development efforts futile.

To effectively advance sustainable development among the OECDs nations, it is essential for these countries to leverage advancements in low-carbon electricity generation by utilizing renewable energy sources. Such initiatives, along with others, will enhance the potential for energy efficiency in production processes. Additional decarbonization strategies may encompass the adoption of innovative emissions reduction technologies and methods, including carbon capture, utilization, and storage, the use of green hydrogen, and the integration of renewable electricity into growth processes. Furthermore, employing renewable energy sources such as photovoltaic systems, solar thermal energy, or bio-based heat, along with promoting energy efficiency across all real growth sectors through progressive policies and technologies, is crucial.

Our study has yielded significant findings; however, we acknowledge certain inherent limitations. Consequently, we suggest several directions for future research. The study uses data from 2000 to 2023 (23 years). While this provides some insight into recent trends, it is relatively short to fully capture long-term sustainable development trajectories and the deep structural changes required for energy transitions in OECD countries. Many OECD transitions began prior to the Kyoto Protocol, and the full effects of clean energy diffusion often occur over longer periods. Future studies should consider extending the dataset for long-term analysis.

This study examines the relationship between clean energy proxied as renewable energy consumption (REW) on sustainable development (SDG) in OECDs. Subsequent research could explore how these interactions manifest in different global regions. Investigating other continents would validate and reinforce the robustness of our findings within a different context. In our analysis, we utilized sustainable development index (SDG) as indicators of sustainable development. Future studies might consider alternative indicators, such as adjusted net savings, environmental performance index, misery index, GDG growth and GDP per capita. Lastly, our research primarily concentrates on OECD economies. Subsequent investigations may adopt our empirical approach to provide evidence that is specific to individual countries80–86.
