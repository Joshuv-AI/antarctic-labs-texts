PcabHLH58/PcabHLH151 regulates adventitious root development and nitrogen uptake in poplar
Authors: Hantian Wei, Xinglu Zhou, Xiaodong Xiang, Jiujun Du, Yongxia Bai, Min Zhang, Xueqin Song, Guanzheng Qu, Lei Zhang, Jianjun Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As a crucial ecological shelterbelt and timber species in China, Populus relies on adventitious root (AR) formation and root architecture for its ecological adaptability and clonal propagation efficiency. Two key root trait-related genes, PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151, were identified through genome-wide association studies (GWAS) in Populus cathayana. Single or double mutations of PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 significantly inhibited AR development and nitrogen uptake in poplar. Furthermore, both genes can form homodimers or heterodimers to modulate the expression of PcaSCL33 and PcaPLT1 during AR development. The higher expression level and stronger regulatory capacity of PcabHLH58 imply its more critical role in the AR development. Additionally, PcaWOX5 was found to be modulated through both the PcabHLH58/PcabHLH151-PcaSCL33/PcaSHR and PcabHLH58/PcabHLH151-PcaPLT1 pathway. PcaPAR1 was identified as a feedback regulatory node in modulating the regulatory activities of both PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151. Collectively, this study elucidates a novel regulatory network involving root development in poplar and provides foundational insights for future genetic breeding efforts.

Introduction

Plant roots are vital for anchoring plants and acquiring water and nutrients, which in turn support overall growth and architecture1,2. In spermatophytes, the root system comprises an embryonic primary root and post-embryonic lateral and adventitious roots3,4. Adventitious roots (ARs) form from above-ground organs such as hypocotyls, stems, and leaves3. The growth of ARs from cuttings is the key factor of plant propagation and silviculture survival5. Poplars propagate by direct planting of stem cuttings, which retain desirable traits effectively6,7. As a typical woody species with rapid growth and strong adaptability, poplars have been widely applied in producing timber, paper pulp, biofuel, and building materials8,9. Due to the increasing demand for woody biomass, extending the cultivation of fast-growing woody plants such as poplars may contribute to satisfy the need for renewable resources10,11. Therefore, elucidating the genetic regulatory mechanisms underlying AR formation and development is essential to open new avenues for improving vegetative propagation in poplar or other woody species.

The basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) protein family, composed of two conserved motifs - the basic region and helix-loop-helix (HLH) region, is one of the largest transcription factors (TFs) families in plants12,13. The basic region mediates DNA binding by recognizing the E-box (5’-CANNTG-3’) motif in the promoters of target genes14,15. The HLH region, composed of two amphipathic α-helices connected by a variable loop, facilitates bHLH proteins' dimerization, enabling the regulation of target genes across multiple signaling pathways16,17. Atypical bHLH proteins, such as PAR1, lack a proper DNA-binding domain and regulate gene expression by interacting with DNA-binding transcription factors. Genome-wide analyses have identified bHLH TFs in numerous species, including 171 distinct members in Arabidopsis thaliana13, 233 in Populus trichocarpa18, 208 in Zea mays19, and 183 in Oryza sativa20. In Arabidopsis, PERICYCLE FACTOR TYPE-A (PFA) proteins, members of the bHLH superfamily, regulate pericycle cell competence for lateral root primordium initiation21. Among them, AtbHLH112 (PFA4) is expressed in root apical meristem protoxylem and functions as a suppressor of lateral root development21,22. Although many bHLH transcription factors have been extensively studied in poplars23–25, their biological functions in root development remain to be comprehensively characterized.

In root meristems, protophloem differentiation originates from apical stem cells positioned adjacent to the quiescent center (QC) within the meristematic zone26,27. Metaphloem cells, located in the elongation zone and differentiating later, assume the transportation function to maintain phloem continuity after protophloem cell termination27. In Arabidopsis, multiple molecular mechanisms regulating the differentiation of phloem cells have been characterized28,29. The expression of QC-specifying gene WUSCHEL-RELATED HOMEOBOX 5 (WOX5) overlaps PLETHORA (PLTs), SHORT-ROOT (SHR), and SCARECROW (SCR) around the stem cell niche30–33. PLT genes have been reported to serve as early key determinants of the protophloem lineage34. SHR with SCR regulates root differentiation and is required to maintain stem cells by its cell-autonomous activity in organizer cells33. However, the mechanisms regulating root metaphloem division and differentiation in poplar still remain poorly understood.

In this study, we conducted rooting experiments on P. cathayana and observed significant differences in AR traits among different clones. Through a genome-wide association study (GWAS), two root length-associated bHLH transcription factors, PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151, were identified. The AR growth of their mutants was affected by the inhibition of root metaphloem development. Further experiments demonstrated that PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 could form homo- and heterodimers to regulate PcaSCL33 and PcaPLT1. Subsequent investigations identified PcaWOX5 as a downstream target in the PcaSCL33/PcaSHR and PcaPLT1 regulatory pathways. Collectively, these findings establish PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 as critical regulators of AR development in poplar, acting through their target genes to coordinate metaphloem differentiation and root growth.

Results

PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 were identified by GWAS

To elucidate the genetic basis of AR development in poplar, we conducted rooting experiments on 393 natural germplasms of P. cathayana. Phenotypic analysis revealed rich genetic variation in AR traits, with coefficients of variation ranging from 0.19 to 0.33 (Supplementary Table 1). To resolve the genetic architecture of AR development and identify key regulatory genes involved, we performed genome-wide association studies (GWAS) using a mixed linear model (MLM). For total root length (TRL), a cluster of significantly associated SNPs on chromosome 18 was identified, which exhibited a high level of linkage disequilibrium (LD) among markers (Fig. 1a, c). For instance, we found a gene near the lead SNP Chr18_3171247 (P = 6.481306e−08), which encodes a bHLH transcription factor, PcabHLH151, which showed a strong association with total root length. Haplotype analysis revealed significant differences in TRL, maximum root length (MRL) and root surface area (SA) between two haplotypes at this SNP, with the GA haplotype showing significantly longer roots than the AA haplotype (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, a homolog of PcabHLH151, PcabHLH58, was also found to be significantly associated with total root length (Chr11_19398825, P = 2.315418e−07). The genomic information of P. cathayana shows that PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 are on two different chromosomes, Chr11 and Chr18, respectively. Subsequently, phylogenetic analysis indicated that these two genes are paralogous genes (Supplementary Fig. 1a), which are both most similar to their orthologous gene AtbHLH112. Their respective orthologs in P. trichocarpa are PtrbHLH58 and PtrbHLH151, while in rice the homologous genes are OsbHLH067 and OsbHLH067. Bioinformatics analysis showed that PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 both contain the typical basic region and helix-loop-helix region, belonging to the bHLH gene family (Supplementary Fig. 1b).

In order to understand the expression pattern of PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151, real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) and semi-quantitative PCR were first performed. The RT-qPCR showed the expression of PcabHLH58 was highest in leaves and ARs, followed by lateral roots, top buds and stems (Supplementary Fig. 2a). However, PcabHLH151 was expressed at very low level in poplar, only detecting higher in ARs with lateral roots (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Moreover, the semi-quantitative RT-PCR at 30 cycles also indicated low expression level of PcabHLH151 (Supplementary Fig. 2c). The detailed expression profiles were further analyzed using ProPcabHLH58::GUS and ProPcabHLH151::GUS transgenic plants (Fig. 1d, i). Histological staining of the ProPcabHLH58::GUS transgenic plants showed high GUS signals in leaves, ARs, and lateral roots (Fig. 1e–g). In ProPcabHLH151::GUS transgenic plants, weak GUS signals were observed in top leaves, ARs, and lateral roots (Fig. 1j–l), which were consistent with the above RT-qPCR with semi-quantitative PCR profiles. Interestingly, both transgenic plants showed the GUS signals in root phloem (Fig. 1h, m). The expression pattern indicated a potential role of PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 in root development in poplar. Based on GWAS and expression pattern profiling, we hypothesized that PcabHLH151 and PcabHLH58 might be the key genes regulating AR development in poplar.

PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 were involved in poplar growth

To further investigate the functions of PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151, we generated their overexpression and knockout transgenic lines (bHLH58-OE, bHLH151-OE, bhlh58-KO, and bhlh151-KO), and double knockout lines (bhlh58/151-KO) in Populus alba × Populus glandulosa (84 K). The expression level of overexpression lines was valued by using RT-qPCR (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). Then, we selected four overexpression lines based on the high (bHLH58-OE#10, bHLH151-OE#23) and middle (bHLH58-OE#20, bHLH151-OE#17) expression levels. Regarding the single or double knockout mutants, the alleles of bHLH58 or bHLH151 in P. alba × P. glandulosa were completely knocked out and the target sites were successfully edited (Supplementary Fig. 4). Hence, bHLH58 knockout mutants (bhlh58-KO#6 and bhlh58-KO#9), bHLH151 knockout mutants (bhlh151-KO#2 and bhlh151-KO#40) and bHLH58/151 double knockout mutants (bhlh58/151-KO#32 and bhlh58/151-KO#39) were determined for further phenotypic analysis.

After 30 days of transplantation into soil, the overexpression lines of PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 showed no significant differences in plant height, plant weight, ground diameter, maximum root length, or total root length compared to the control (Fig. 2a–g). In contrast, we found that the plant growth in the knockout mutants was significantly inhibited. The plant height of all mutants was significantly decreased by approximately 14.17% compared to wild type (WT), with no significant differences among the mutants (Fig. 2b). In ground diameter, bhlh58/151 mutants were the smallest (~22.75%) compared to WT, while bhlh58 (~5.09%) and bhlh151 (~4.30%) also showed slight decreases compared to WT (Fig. 2c). Maximum root length was significantly inhibited in all mutants (Fig. 2a, d). The bhlh58/151 (~39.48%) and bhlh58 (~38.86%) were much shorter than WT, followed by bhlh151 (~27.03%). Total root length, measured by scanning, was also significantly shorter in all mutants than in WT, consistent with the genetic contribution to this trait identified by GWAS (Figs. 1b; 2e). For underground fresh weight, bhlh58/151 were the lowest, reduced by ~43.50% compared to WT (Fig. 2f). Aboveground fresh weight was also significantly reduced in bhlh58/151 (~41.60%) and bhlh58 (~10.65%) mutants (Fig. 2f). The plant dry weight of aboveground and underground parts in all transgenic lines were consistent with results of plant fresh weight (Fig. 2g), with the largest reductions in bhlh58/151 ( ~ 36.86% underground and ~28.73% aboveground). PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 contributed to the root metaphloem division and differentiation

Generally, cell proliferation and expansion determine the growth and development of plant tissues35. The root length and biomass of bhlh58, bhlh151, and bhlh58/151 mutants were highly affected in previous phenotypic analysis. To further investigate the role of PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 in root phloem development in poplar, we performed the histological analysis among the overexpression lines and knockout mutants. Microscopy of the root cross-sections revealed that the development of root metaphloem was much slower in all mutants than in WT (Fig. 2h). And overexpression lines did not show a significant difference compared to WT normally.

To assess the maturity of root phloem cells, cell wall thickness was measured using transmission electron microscopy (Fig. 2i, j). The cell wall in bhlh58/151-KO#32 (~38.66%) was the thinnest compared to WT, with bhlh58-KO#6 (~27.90%) and bhlh151-KO#40 ( ~ 17.45%) behind (Fig. 2j). And the cell size of root phloem cells was also decreased by ~27.97% in bhlh58/151-KO#32, ~19.53% in bhlh58-KO#6 and ~13.77% in bhlh151-KO#40 (Fig. 2k). As for cell proliferation, the number of root metaphloem cells in each quarter corner was lower in bhlh58-KO#6 (~25.80%), bhlh151-KO#40 (~23.22%) and bhlh58/151-KO#32 ( ~ 26.02%) mutants than in WT (Fig. 2l). The double mutant bhlh58/151-KO#32 showed the strongest inhibition of root phloem division and differentiation, while bhlh58-KO#6 mutant was at middle level with the bhlh151-KO#40 not far behind. These results are consistent with the root growth phenotype of the transgenic lines and may account for the root growth inhibition in the mutants.

Nitrogen uptake was limited in mutants of bHLH58 and bHLH151

The mineral nutrients like nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K) uptaken from the soil are vital for plant growth, with the ‌phloem‌ and ‌xylem‌ in roots playing critical roles in transporting and uploading these resources36,37. To investigate whether nutrient deficiency affects the growth of the mutants, two treatments containing normal nitrogen supply (NN, 8 mM nitrogen) and low nitrogen supply (LN, 0.5 mM nitrogen) of cutting seedlings were performed.

Under LN conditions, the plant growth in mutants was highly inhibited by the low nitrogen level (Fig. 3a). The overexpression lines also showed similar to the WT in plant height, total root length, and dry weight (Fig. 3b–d). The growth pattern of total root length in transgenic plants was not affected compared to WT under different nitrogen treatment levels (Fig. 3c). But bhlh58 with bhlh58/151 showed averagely ~21.98% and ~21.93% decrease compared to WT in plant height (Fig. 3b). However, bhlh151 did not show the significant difference in plant height but showed ~ 10.60% decrease in dry weight of aboveground parts and ~12.66% decrease in underground parts (Fig. 3b, d). The largest decrease in dry weight of aboveground parts was bhlh58/151 (~24.93%), followed by bhlh58 (~17.31%). However, the normal nitrogen supply partially rescued the reductions of plant growth in bhlh58 and bhlh151 mutants. Only bhlh58/151 displayed a slight decrease ( ~ 4.80%) in plant height under NN conditions (Fig. 3b). Unlike under LN conditions, the decrease of plant weight of aboveground parts in bhlh58 (~5.33%) and bhlh58/151 (~15.59%) was partially rescued compared to WT (Fig. 3e). As for the underground parts, the growth reduction also rescued in bhlh151 (~11.21%), bhlh58 (~20.64%) and bhlh58/151 (~29.00%), respectively (Fig. 3e). Additionally, the expression level of nitrate transporters in transgenic plants was also analyzed by qRT-PCR (Supplementary Fig. 5). PagNRT1.1, PagNLP4a, PagAMT1.2 and PagGLN1a all displayed the lower expression in mutants compared to WT. The expression of PagNRT1.1 and PagGLN1a could be induced by NN treatments, while PagNLP4a and PagAMT1.2 were induced by LN treatments.

To further determine the limitation of nitrogen uptake in mutants, total N content and 15NO3− influx rate about WT and transgenic plants under LN and NN treatments were measured (Fig. 3f–h). Under LN and NN conditions, the total N content of whole plant tissues in mutants of bhlh58 and bhlh151 were both averagely decreased by ~15.68% and ~10.41% (Fig. 3f), while the double mutant bhlh58/151-KO#32 displayed the largest decrease ~23.70% (LN) and ~25.18% (NN). What’s more, the mutants of bhlh151 exhibited the smallest decrease ~9.22% (LN) and ~10.79% (NN), under both conditions. Here, the nitrogen uptake ability of plant roots was assessed by measuring the net 15NO3− influx rate. The net NO3− influx rate in mutants of bhlh58 and bhlh151 under LN and NN conditions was both significantly decreased by ~12.17% (LN) and ~15.84% (NN) compared to WT, respectively (Fig. 3g, h). And the double mutant bhlh58/151-KO#32 also showed the largest decrease ~14.48% (LN) and ~19.94% (NN). Generally, the total N content levels and 15N influx rate among transgenic plants were consistent with the above growth phenotype. These results demonstrated that the nitrogen uptake had been limited in mutants, whereas a sufficient nitrogen supply could partially rescue this phenomenon. We proposed that the transporting and uploading of mineral nutrients were limited in bhlh58 and bhlh151 mutants due to the effect of root phloem development, which subsequently leads to the inhibition of plant growth and development.

PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 could form homo- and heterodimers

It has been reported that the interaction between the helix-loop-helix (HLH) regions of bHLH genes leads to the formation of homo- or heterodimers38. Subsequently, interactions between PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 were confirmed both in vivo and in vitro using bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) and yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assays. Here, we first confirmed the subcellular localization of PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 in tobacco leaves by fusing with a GFP tag. The empty vector with GFP tag was distributed in both the cytoplasm and nucleus, whereas the PcabHLH58-GFP and PcabHLH151-GFP protein was preferentially localized in the nucleus (Supplementary Fig. 6). Then, the interactions of PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 in vivo were investigated by BiFC in tobacco leaves. The BiFC assays indicated that both PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 proteins can form homodimers themselves or heterodimers with each other in nucleus (Fig. 4a). After two pairs of plasmids of pAD-PcabHLH58, pBD-PcabHLH58, pAD-PcabHLH151, and pBD-PcabHLH151 were co-transformed into the yeast cell, Y2H assays also indicated that PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 proteins can interact with themselves or each other in vitro (Fig. 4b), which was consistent with the BiFC results. The bHLH transcription factor has been reported to regulate various biological processes by forming homo- or heterodimers. Transcriptome analysis was performed in WT and transgenic lines

Root growth and differentiation in plants are inextricably linked with plant hormones39,40. It has been reported that the biosynthesis, transport and signaling of auxin play a crucial role in controlling root morphogenesis and development, while other phytohormones are also incorporated into these three key processes. To discover the underlying mechanism about AR development mediated by PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151, transcriptome analysis was performed in lines of WT, bHLH58-OE#20, bhlh58-KO#6, bHLH151-OE#17, bhlh151#KO40 and bhlh58/151-KO#32. In total, 7678 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified and the normalized expression level were displayed in heatmap (Supplementary Fig. 7a). KEGG enrichment analysis showed that the DEGs mainly enriched in plant-pathogen interaction, plant hormone signal transduction and MAPK signaling pathway – plant (Fig. 4c). Here, we demonstrated an integrated network about tryptophan metabolism, carotenoid biosynthesis, zeatin biosynthesis and plant hormone signal transduction pathways based on Pearson correlation analysis, where each gene had more than 2 edges to interact with other genes (Fig. 4d). PagPLT1a (PAxG03Ag0179100) and PagSCL33 (PAxG01Gg0229200) genes were found to be highly connected in carotenoid biosynthesis and plant hormone signal transduction path ways. Many root protophloem or metaphloem relative genes had been identified in Arabidopsis, such as PLTs, NEN1/2/3/4, SUPPRESSOR OF MAX2 1-LIKE 3/4/5 (SMXL3/4/5), NAC45/86, and so on28. It has been reported that PLT transcription factors were required for QC identity and stem cell maintenance, and early key determinants of the protophloem lineage31,34. The GRAS family transcription factor SCR regulates root differentiation and is required to maintain stem cells41. Here, orthologs of these genes were identified in poplar by blast and analyzed relative gene expression levels (Supplementary Fig. 7b). PLT1, PHLOEM EARLY DNA-BINDING-WITH-ONE-FINGER 1 (PEAR1)42, ALTERED PHLOEM DEVELOPMENT (APL)43, OCTOPUS (OPS)44, LRR-RK BARELY ANY MERISTEM 3 (BAM3)45, SUPPRESSOR OF MAX2 1-LIKE (SMXL3/4/5)46 were found differentially expressed which were all reported important for both the cell division and differentiation in phloem (Supplementary Fig. 7b). Interestingly, the expression level of SCARECROW-LIKE gene PagSCL33 was downregulated in bhlh58 and bhlh151 mutants. The expression of PagPLT1a/b was inhibited in bHLH58#OE20 and bHLH151#OE17 lines. Additionally, we quantified the expression of these DEGs using RT-qPCR, and the results were consistent with transcriptome data (Supplementary Fig. 7c, d). Moreover, the promoters of SCL33 and PLT1 in poplar contained rich E-box motifs, which were directly recognized by the bHLH proteins. Above all, we hypothesized that PcaSCL33 and PcaPLT1 might be involved in the root phloem development in poplar and could potentially be regulated by PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151.

PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 mediated regulation of PcaSCL33 and PcaPLT1 via promoter binding

To determine whether PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 regulate PcaSCL33 and PcaPLT1 expression through direct promoter binding, we performed a yeast one-hybrid assay (Y1H), an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA), and a dual-luciferase reporter assay. Firstly, the 2-kb promoters of PcaSCL33 and PcaPLT1 were cloned and used to construct the corresponding vectors. By co-transforming yeast cells of pAD-PcabHLH58 + pHIS2-ProPcaSCL33, pAD-PcabHLH58 + pHIS2-ProPcaPLT1, pAD-PcabHLH151 + pHIS2-ProPcaSCL33, and pAD-PcabHLH151 + pHIS2-ProPcaPLT1, the Y1H assays showed that both PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 could directly bind to the promoters of PcaSCL33 and PcaPLT1 (Fig. 5a). Subsequently, the Dual-Luciferase reporter assays were performed to test whether PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 could promote or inhibit the expression of PcaSCL33 and PcaPLT1. Because PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 can form homo- and heterodimers, we also investigated the reporter constructs ProPcaSCL33::LUC and ProPcaPLT1::LUC when co-expressed with the two effector constructs 35S::PcabHLH58 and 35S::PcabHLH151 (Fig. 5d,e). The results indicated that PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 can bind to the promoter of PcaSCL33 and PcaPLT1 separately or together. For PcaSCL33, both PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 could activate its expression (Fig. 5d; Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). The luciferase activity of ProPcaSCL33::LUC under 35S::PcabHLH58 was much stronger than under 35S::PcabHLH151 (Supplementary Fig. 8c). When forming the heterodimers of PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151, the promotion to the PcaSCL33 became the strongest (Supplementary Fig. 8c). However, the activity of ProPcaPLT1::LUC driven by PcabHLH58 or PcabHLH151 separately were both inhibited compared to the control (Fig. 5e; Supplementary Fig. 8d, e). But the activity driven by PcabHLH58 inhibited more than that under PcabHLH151 (Supplementary Fig. 8f). Furthermore, the inhibition of luciferase activity driven by co-expression of PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 was similar to that driven by PcabHLH58 alone and didn’t significantly enhance the inhibition (Supplementary Fig. 8f). Through electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA), PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 were found that could directly bind to the specific cis-elements “CAACTG”, which belongs to E-box motifs on the promoters of PcaSCL33 and PcaPLT1 (Fig. 5b, c). Thus, these results revealed that both PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 could affect the expression of PcaSCL33 and PcaPLT1 through the formation of homo- and heterodimers binding to their promoters. PcaSCL33 and PcaPLT1 involved the AR development by regulating the expression of PcaWOX5 in poplar

It has been identified that PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 regulate the AR development, but the biological functions of their target genes still remain uncharacterized. To investigate whether PcaSCL33 and PcaPLT1 are involved in the AR development in poplar, we created the ProPcaSCL33::GUS and ProPcaPLT1::GUS transgenic plants and overexpression lines to analyze their expression pattern and biological functions in poplar (Supplementary Fig. 9; Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). In ProPcaSCL33::GUS transgenic plants, the GUS signals appeared in leaf vascular tissue‌ and root phloem (Supplementary Fig. 9a–c), which suggested that PcaSCL33 might participate in the root phloem development with PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151. Further, the phenotypic analysis of 30-day-old PcaSCL33 overexpression lines (OE#4-2, OE#5-2, and OE#10-2) was also performed (Fig. 6a). The ground diameter and plant height of PcaSCL33 overexpression lines were approximately 1.13 and 1.14 times those of WT, respectively (Fig. 6b, c). Subsequently, the fresh and dry weight of plant underground parts were on average 1.52 and 1.46 times that of WT, while the aboveground parts showed corresponding increases of 1.11 and 1.16 times (Fig. 6d, e). GUS signals of ProPcaPLT1::GUS transgenic plants were specifically shown in the root, especially in the root tip (Supplementary Fig. 9d–f). After 14 days of tissue culture, overexpression lines of PcaPLT1 (OE#2 and OE#5) led to a significant increase in lateral root formation, showing 2.64 times more lateral roots than WT (Fig. 6f, g). Consequently, fresh weight and dry weight of the total root correspondingly enhanced by ~56.89% and ~82.34% (Fig. 6h). The average lateral root length in overexpression lines was also improved by ~44.35% (Fig. 6i). However, the leaf growth in overexpression lines was strongly inhibited. Above all, PcaPLT1 could enhance the lateral root formation and development and might be negatively regulated by PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 to balance the root growth in poplar.

In Arabidopsis, WOX5 is specifically expressed in the stem cell niche and regulates distal columella stem cell fate47. WOX5 acts as a central effector downstream of both SCR/SHR and PLT pathways to modulate root stem cell niche activity30,47. Among these known pathway genes, only PtoWOX5a, PeSCR, PeSHR, and PtSHR2B have been identified as being involved in AR development in poplar48–50. However, the molecular crosstalk governing AR development among these genes remains to be elucidated. Populus has three SHR genes (SHR, SHR2A, and SHR2B), where SHR was considered the putative ortholog of AtSHR51. PcaSCL33 could only interact with PcaSHR in the yeast system (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Through Y1H and dual-luciferase reporter analyses, we demonstrated that both PcaPLT1 and PcaSCL33 could bind to the promoter of PcaWOX5 (Supplementary Fig. 10b,c). Based on these findings, we suggested that these genes could promote AR development through regulating the expression of PcaWOX5 in poplar.

PcaPAR1 acts as a feedback regulator of PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 transcriptional activity

Based on the minor phenotypic changes observed in overexpression lines and the large number of bHLH proteins in plants, we hypothesized that feedback regulatory mechanisms might modulate their activity. HLH proteins are known to act as transcriptional cofactors, regulating target gene expression by interacting with canonical transcription factors that bind directly to DNA. Notably, PHYTOCHROME RAPIDLY REGULATED (PARs) represent atypical bHLH proteins that lack a proper DNA-binding domain and are thus not expected to directly bind to DNA. Intriguingly, the expression level of both PcaPAR1 and PcaPAR2 was markedly elevated across all the overexpression lines (Supplementary Fig. 11a). Compared to PcaPAR2, PcaPAR1, which exhibited stronger induction, was selected for further analysis. The interaction between PcaPAR1 and either PcabHLH58 or PcabHLH151 were also confirmed in yeast system (Supplementary Fig. 11b). Consistent with previous findings, the regulation by PcaPAR1 on PcabHLH58 or PcabHLH151 activity was investigated using the reporter constructs ProPcaSCL33::LUC and ProPcaPLT1::LUC (Supplementary Fig. 11c). Similarly, the activation about PcabHLH58 or PcabHLH151 to PcaSCL33 was reduced by PcaPAR1, while the inhibition about PcabHLH58 or PcabHLH151 to PcaPLT1 was also reduced (Supplementary Fig. 11d–g). These results indicated that PcaPAR1 could modulate the PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 transcriptional activity to downstream genes through feedback regulation.

Discussion

The formation and growth of AR in cuttings are crucial for both the survival and subsequent growth of poplar propagules6. For increasing the efficiency of vegetative propagation of poplar, numerous regulatory genes about AR formation and growth were functionally characterized, and several regulatory networks have been revealed. In P. ussuriensis, a multilayered hierarchical gene regulatory network (ML-hGRN) about AR formation mediated by PuHox52 were established52. PuMYB40 and its interactive partner PuWRKY75 could regulate AR formation through activating the expression of PuLRP1 and PuERF00353. And PuUBL5-mediated PuZFP1 positively regulated AR elongation via ABA/auxin signaling under drought stress54. In P. deltoides × P. euramericana, PeRBR functioned as a regulatory node within the SHR/SCR network in AR development50. However, the underlying regulatory networks that governed adventitious rooting in poplar still remain poorly understood.

As a native poplar species in China, P. cathayana is characterized by exceptional adventitious rooting ability and high environmental adaptability55. Phenotypic analysis of 393 natural germplasms of P. cathayana showed rich genetic variation in AR traits. To further resolve the genetic architecture of AR development, GWAS about adventitious rooting traits were performed, and two major regulatory genes, PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151, were identified (Fig. 1a, b). In this study, we demonstrated that PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 could regulate PcaSCL33 and PcaPLT1 expression via the formation of homo- and heterodimers, thereby influencing root metaphloem differentiation and total root length. Their expression patterns were different from AtbHLH112, which localizes to the pericycle and stele of the hypocotyl and root, with particularly strong expression in root apical meristem protoxylem21,22. Consistently, PcabHLH151 showed low expression levels across all examined poplar tissues. These distinct expression patterns suggest that PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 might play different functional roles in root phloem development (Fig. 1d–m; Supplementary Fig. 2).

To characterize the biological functions of PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151, we generated their overexpression lines along with single and double knockout mutants. Phenotypic analysis revealed no significant difference between the overexpression lines and WT (Fig. 2). What’s more, the expression and protein levels of PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 were also confirmed to be higher in overexpression lines compared to WT (Supplementary Fig. 3e–g). Plants have evolved precise regulatory mechanisms to fine-tune the balance between growth and defense, enabling survival under adverse conditions56. Therefore, we speculated that feedback regulations of bHLH genes might exist to counteract their overexpression effects in plants. PAR1 has been reported to interact with PIF4 and inhibit PIF4-mediated transcriptional activation57. Similarly, we observed increased expression of PagPAR1 and PagPAR2 and reduced transcriptional activity of complexes formed between PcaPAR1 and either PcabHLH58 or PcabHLH151 (Supplementary Fig. 11). Overall, atypical bHLH proteins that lack DNA binding domain, such as PAR proteins, might serve as critical regulatory nodes in the feedback circuits controlling bHLH gene function.

However, all the mutants of bHLH58 and bHLH151 exhibited significant reductions in total plant biomass, including aboveground and underground parts, compared to WT (Fig. 2f, g). Moreover, maximum and total root length in mutants also showed significant differences compared to WT (Figs. 1b; 2d, e). These findings were consistent with the haplotype results and suggested their pleiotropic effects in root growth. Our histological analyses revealed a gradation of metaphloem developmental defects (Fig. 2h, i). The bhlh58/151 showed the most severe root metaphloem development defects, followed by bhlh58 and bhlh151. These results suggest that PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151, like other homologous genes, likely exhibit functional redundancy in regulating plant growth58,59. Furthermore, the comparatively higher expression level of PcabHLH58 indicates that it may play a more essential role in poplar than PcabHLH151.

Generally, plant roots are the main entry point for mineral nutrients, which are critical elements during plant growth and development37,60. To further test whether the transporting and uploading of mineral nutrients was limited, we performed two treatments containing normal and low levels of nitrogen supply (Fig. 3). The NN conditions with sufficient nitrogen improved the plant height and biomass of cutting seedlings compared to LN conditions (Fig. 3b, d, e), which was consistent with previous works61,62. The differences‌ in plant height and root growth in mutants were amplified under LN treatment, but sufficient nitrogen supply partially rescued the phenotype of these mutants (Fig. 3b, c). Additionally, the nitrogen uptake ability of the mutants was also decreased (Fig. 3f-h). The inhibition of metaphloem development in mutants affected the functions of root phloem in mutants, consequently limiting mineral nutrient uptake as reported63,64.

It has been reported that bHLH genes, such as MYCs and PIFs, could form homo- or heterodimers to bind cis-regulatory elements, enhancing their expression16,17. Here, we confirmed that PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 could interact with themselves or each other by Y2H and BIFC analyses (Fig. 4a, b). To elucidate the regulatory mechanisms of PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 in the root, we performed RNA-seq and qRT-PCR analyses on transgenic lines. These studies identified PcaSCL33 and PcaPLT1 as key regulators of root phloem development that were directly regulated by PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 (Fig. 4d; Supplementary Fig. 7c, d). Through Y1H, Dual-Luciferase reporter and EMSA analyses, PcaSCL33 and PcaPLT1 were upregulated and downregulated by PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 (Fig. 5). By forming the heterodimers, the promotion of PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 to the PcaSCL33 was enhanced. Additionally, the regulatory ability of PcabHLH58 was stronger than PcabHLH151 (Supplementary Fig. 8). Gene expression patterns usually determine gene functional performance and then lead to a certain phenotype65,66. Although PcabHLH151 had a similar DNA binding ability to PcabHLH58, PcabHLH58, with a higher expression level, might act as the putative master in this regulatory network.

Through GUS staining analysis, PcaSCL33 and PcaPLT1 were both found to be highly expressed in the root (Supplementary Fig. 9a, d). Interestingly, histological cross-sections by GUS staining revealed that PcaSCL33, like PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151, was specifically expressed in root phloem (Supplementary Fig. 9b). PcaPLT1 was found to be expressed highly in root tip (Supplementary Fig. 9f), similar to AtPLT1, and may also be relative to the protophloem development31. Like PeSCR50, PcaSCL33 also improved the root length and total root weight in overexpression lines, suggesting a potential compensatory effect in the mutants of PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151. Overexpression of PcaPLT1 enhanced lateral root density and length, while also increasing root biomass. This phenotype resembled that of PtoWOX5a overexpressing plants48, indicating that PcaPLT1 modulated the root development via the WOX5 pathway. We suspected that the activity of protophloem cells was greatly promoted by PcaPLT1 thus caused the abnormal growth of lateral and adventitious roots. Generally, PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 might be the key transcription factors to balance the AR growth through modulation of PcaPLT1 expression.

In conclusion, we characterized the functions of two bHLH genes, PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151, regulating the AR growth in poplar. Two regulatory pathways were found in this study: PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 could both activate the expression of PcaSCL33 by forming homo- and heterodimers to promote phloem development; the expression level of PcaPLT1 could be inhibited by dimers of PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 to balance the root growth in poplar (Fig. 7). Finally, PcaWOX5 integrated signals from PcaSCL33/PcaSHR and PcaPLT1 pathways. Additionally, PcaPAR1 served as a balancer within the feedback loop among bHLH proteins, modulating the regulatory activities of both PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151. To address the growing demand for woody biomass, enhancing biomass yield and improving wood quality have long been central objectives in forest tree breeding67. Consequently, identifying genes that control root growth has become a major research focus, due to their vital role in seedling propagation and field survival68. Methods

P. cathayana rooting experiment and phenotype data collection

In previous studies, we observed significant differences in rooting traits among different clones of P. cathayana. To systematically investigate this phenomenon, we conducted a greenhouse rooting experiment with 393 natural germplasms of P. cathayana. The experiment was designed with a randomized block design, consisting of three blocks, with three biological replicates per block. Cuttings were watered every 3–4 days. After 90 days, root systems were scanned using an EPSON Perfection V700 scanner. Maximum root length (MRL) was measured with a ruler, and total root number (TRN) was recorded. Additional rooting traits, including total root length (TRL), surface area (SA), average diameter (AD), and root volume (RV), were measured using WinRHIZO software (Regent Instruments Inc., Canada).

Genotype data and Genome-wide association study

Whole-genome resequencing data were filtered with VCFtools v0.1.1669. Specifically, SNP markers with a Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium greater than 1.0 × 10−6, minor allele frequency greater than 0.05, and missing genotype rate below 0.05 were removed. After filtering, 3,042,139 high-quality SNP markers were retained for subsequent analysis. GWAS was performed using the Mixed Linear Model (MLM) in GEMMA v0.98.370. To reduce false positives, the first three principal components were incorporated as fixed effects, while the kinship matrix was included as a random effect for correction. A significance threshold was set at 3.29 × 10⁻⁷ after Bonferroni correction (1/N, where N represents the total number of SNP markers). Candidate genes were identified within a 20-kb window upstream and downstream of each significant SNP.

Sequence analysis and phylogenetic analysis

For phylogenetic analysis, the homologous proteins in A. thaliana, P. trichocarpa, and rice were identified by BLAST and downloaded from Phytozome (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/). Protein sequence alignments were performed using ClustalW with default parameters in MEGA-X software71. The phylogenetic tree was constructed by the maximum-likelihood (ML) method with 1000 bootstrap replicates.

Plant materials and growth conditions

Tissue-cultured seedlings of P. alba × P. glandulosa (84 K) were maintained using a half Murashige and Skoog (1/2) medium under a 16-h light/8-h dark cycle at 25°C. After transfer to the soil, the seedlings were cultivated in the greenhouse at 25°C. The cutting seedlings of transgenic lines were also cultivated in the greenhouse using a 1:1 mixture of perlite and vermiculite. The experiment was repeated three times with similar results.

Plasmid construction and generation of transgenic plants

Using the genomic information of P. cathayana55, the coding sequences (CDS) of PcabHLH58, PcabHLH151, PcaSCL33, and PcaPLT1 were amplified from P. cathayana cDNA. PcabHLH58, PcabHLH151, and PcaSCL33 were recombined into the entry vector pDONR20772 via the Gateway cloning system. Then recombined them into the destination vector pMDC3272 under the control of the CaMV35S promoter. PcaPLT1 was recombined into another overexpression vector pPOK273. The mutants bhlh58, bhlh151, and bhlh58/151 were obtained using the CRISPR-Cas9 system74. Two specific target sequences to distinguish bHLH58 and bHLH151, and one common target sequence were designed and constructed into pCas9-DH74 in this study. The involved mutants were identified via an NGS-based high-throughput tracking of mutation (Hi-TOM) assay75. All the plasmids were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 with pSOUP, which was then used to transform wild-type 84k poplar via the callus-based transformation method76. The Agrobacterium strain containing the recombinant vector was inoculated onto YEB solid medium with 50 mg/L kanamycin and 50 mg/L rifampicin at 28 °C for 48 h. Single colonies were then inoculated into 50 ml YEB liquid medium with the same antibiotics and cultured at 28 °C with shaking until reaching the OD600 = 0.8. Cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in WPM liquid medium containing 0.5 g/L 2-(N-morpholino)ethanesulfonic acid (MES), 100 μM acetosyringone (AS), 20 g/L sucrose, 1 mg/L 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D), and 0.1 mg/L kinetin (KT) for transformation. The calli were cultured on WPM medium containing 0.5 g/L MES, 20 g/L sucrose, 1 mg/L 2,4-D, and 0.1 mg/L KT for two weeks. After 30 minutes submerged in Agrobacterium suspension, the infected calli were then placed on WPM medium containing 0.5 g/L MES, 100 μM AS, 20 g/L sucrose for 2 days. Then calli were then transferred into WPM medium containing 0.5 g/L MES, 20 g/L sucrose, 0.05 mg/L 1-naphthylacetic acid (NAA), 0.5 mg/L 6-benzylaminopurine (6-BA), 200 mg/L timentin, and 1.5 mg/L hygromycin B to induce the growth of hygromycin-resistant shoots at 25 °C with a 16/8-h light/dark cycle. After approximately 5 weeks of culture in a growth chamber, resistant shoots of 1–2 cm in length were excised from each callus and rooted on 1/2 MS medium containing 30 g/L sucrose.

RNA extraction and RT–qPCR analysis

For total RNA extraction, we followed the protocol as described in the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (DP432; TIANGEN, Beijing, China). Then, cDNA was prepared by PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (RR047A; TaKaRa, Shiga, Japan). RT-qPCR analysis was performed on the Roche Light Cycler 480 II (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Data analysis was performed using the 2−△△CT method77 and the reference gene was Actin (Potri.006G192700.1). The primers used in this study were synthesized by Sangon (Beijing, China) and listed in Supplementary Data 1.

RNA-Seq analysis

The root tissues of WT, bHLH58-OE#20, bhlh58-KO#6, bHLH151-OE#17, bhlh151-KO#40, and bhlh58/151-KO#32 plants were collected for RNA-seq analysis with three biological replicates. The constructed libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. High-quality genome of P. alba × P. glandulosa was used as a reference genome for further analysis78. HISAT279 was used for mapping RNA-seq reads, and then StringTie80 was applied to assemble the mapped reads. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were determined by using the threshold FDR < 0.01 with |log2FoldChange | ≥ 2 through the DESeq2 R package81. Integrated network using the set of genes from these biological processes after Pearson correlation analysis, visualized by Cytoscape 3.8.282.

Histological analyses

The root tissues of WT and transgenic plants were obtained from the elongation zone of the root, then maintained in the formalin-aceto-alcohol (FAA) solution. Samples were dehydrated in a graded ethyl alcohol series (50 %, 70 %, 80 %, 90 %, and 100 %), infiltrated with Spurr’s resin and ethyl alcohol (1:3, 1:1, and 3:1) using Spurr’s Low Viscosity Embedding Media Kit (14300; Electron Microscopy Science, Hatfield, PA, USA), and then embedded in fresh Spurr’s resin at 70 °C for 48 h83. The sections (5μm) were taken from the solidified samples with a microtome (Leica EM UC6; Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) and stained with 0.05 % toluidine blue O at 5 min. Images of sections were observed by using a microscope (OLYMPUS BX51; OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan) and analyzed using ImageJ software. As for the cell wall thickness measurement, the specimen sections were further stained with uranyl acetate and alkaline lead citrate for transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The images were taken using a Hitachi HT-7800 TEM instrument (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV. Six biological replicates were performed, and the number of > 120 root phloem cells per line was measured in this study.

GUS staining analysis, embedding, and sectioning

The 2-kb promoter region upstream sequences of PcabHLH58, PcabHLH151, PcaPLT1, and PcaSCL33 promoters were cloned from P. cathayana DNA and constructed into the pMDC164 vector via the Gateway cloning system, which contained a GUS tag and lacked the promoter. The transgenic plants were also acquired by the callus-based transformation method. The 14 d transgenic seedlings were incubated in GUS staining solution (20 mM NaPO4 [pH 7.2], 1 mM K3Fe[CN]6, 1 mM K4Fe[CN]6, 0.2 % [v/v] Triton X-100, 1 mM 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl-glucuronide) at 25 °C for 24 h72. The samples were washed with ethanol and incubated in 70 % (v/v) ethanol. After gradient alcohol dehydration, the samples were embedded using Spurr’s Low Viscosity Embedding Media Kit at 70 °C for 48 h. The sections (5 μm) were taken from the solidified samples with a microtome (Leica EM UC6). Images were observed by using a microscope (OLYMPUS BX51).

Determination of total N content and 15N influx rate

For nitrogen treatments of cutting seedlings, the treating solution was formulated by modifying Hoagland’s medium with adjusted nitrogen concentrations64. The 8 mM nitrogen treating solution contained 6 mM KNO3 with 1 mM NH4NO3, and the 0.5 mM nitrogen solution contained 0.375 mM KNO3 with 0.063 mM NH4NO3. Total plant tissues were ground into powder after drying, then total N content was determined using the Dumas method84 by E500 Organic Elemental Analyzer (Hanon, Jinan, China). Samples were combusted in a tungsten oxide-packed tube at 950 °C, reduced in a tube packed with granular copper and silver wire at 800 °C, and then subjected to sequential adsorption and desorption before detection by a thermal conductivity detector. For 15N uptake experiments, WT and transgenic plants were grown hydroponically with 0.5 mM 15N-KNO3 (atom % 15N: 99%) or 8 mM 15N-KNO3 for 21 d, and then deprived of N for 4 d. Before measuring, plant roots under LN and NN treatment were equilibrated into measuring solution containing 0.5 mM 15N-KNO3 or 8 mM 15N-KNO3 separately for 30 min, then transferred to the measuring chamber filled with new measuring solution. The net fluxes of NO3− at the root tip cells were measured using the Non-invasive Micro-test System (Younger USA Science and Technology, Amherst, MA, USA). The ion-selective electrodes were calibrated using 15N-KNO3 at concentrations of 0.1 and 10 mM.

Subcellular localization assay

The CDS sequences of PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 were removed of the stop codons and recombined into pEarlyGate10185 vector via the Gateway cloning system, which contains the CaMV35S promoter and GFP tag. The constructed vector was subsequently transformed into A. tumefaciens. Then Agrobacterium suspension was injected into the Nicotiana benthamiana leaves with OsART1-RFP as a nuclear marker86. Observations and imaging were conducted using a confocal laser microscope (LSM 510 AX70; Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany), with excitation at 488 nm and emission between 493 and 556 nm for GFP, and excitation at 561 nm and emission between 566 and 669 nm for RFP.

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation assay (BiFC)

The coding regions of PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 were constructed into pSPYNE173-nYFP and pSPYCE(M)-cYFP87 via homologous recombination, and subsequently transformed into A. tumefaciens. The Agrobacterium suspension was adjusted to an OD600 = 0.6, and equal volumes of mixed Agrobacterium cultures were injected into N. benthamiana leaves with OsART1-RFP as a nuclear marker. The signal observation was used a confocal laser microscope (LSM 510 AX70) with excitation at 488 nm and emission between 505 and 540 nm for YFP, and excitation at 561 nm and emission between 566 and 669 nm for RFP.

Yeast one-hybrid assay (Y1H)

The CDS sequences of PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 were recombined into pGADT7-Rec vector (Clontech, CA, USA), and the promoters of PcaPLT1 and PcaSCL33 were cloned into pHIS2 vector (Clontech, CA, USA). The corresponding pGADT7 and pHIS2 constructs were transformed into the yeast strain Y187. To prevent autoactivation, 45 mM 3-aminotriazole (3-AT) was added to the SD/-Trp-His-Leu and SD/-Trp-His-Leu/X-α-Gal medium. p53HIS2 + pGADT7-53 was used as positive control, pHIS2-ProPcaSCL33 + pGADT7-Rec and pHIS2-ProPcaPLT1 + pGADT7-REC were used as negative control, respectively.

Yeast two-hybrid assay (Y2H)

The CDS sequences of PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 were recombined into the pGBKT7 vector (Clontech, CA, USA). The vectors of pGADT7-PcabHLH58 with pGADT7-PcabHLH151 were constructed in yeast one-hybrid with assays also used in yeast two-hybrid assays. The corresponding vectors were co-transformed into yeast strain AH109. The transformed yeast was cultured on SD/-Trp-Leu-His-Ade and SD/-Trp-Leu-His-Ade/X-α-gal solid medium with 45 mM 3-AT to assess protein interactions. pGBKT7-53 + pGADT7-T and pGBKT7-53 + pGADT7-Lam were used as positive and negative controls, respectively.

Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay

The CDS sequences of PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 were inserted into the effector pGreenII 62-SK88 under the control of the CaMV35S promoter. The 2-kb promoter of PcaSCL33, PcaPLT1, and PcaWOX5 was cloned into the reporter pGreenII 0800-LUC88. Effect and reporter constructs were equally and transiently expressed in N. benthamiana leaves by Agrobacterium infiltration. Five minutes before imaging, 10 μM D-Luciferin potassium salt (ST196; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was applied to the leaves. The fluorescence activity was analyzed by Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (E1910; Promega, Madison, WI, USA).

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)

The coding regions of PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 were constructed into the pET30a vector with His tag (Novagen, Madison, WI, USA) via homologous recombination and transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3). Transformed cells were induced with 0.5 mM IPTG and incubated at 28 °C for 8 h. After induction, the fusion proteins were purified by His-tag Protein Purification Kit (P2229S; Beyotime, Beijing, China). Biotin-labeled oligonucleotides, synthesized by Sangon (Beijing, China), were used as probes for EMSA. The DNA binding signals were detected using a Lightshift Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit (20148; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The biotin-labeled and unlabeled probes were listed in Supplementary Data 1.

Protein extraction and immunoblotting

For the immunoblot analysis, the CDS sequences of PcabHLH58 and PcabHLH151 were constructed into pBI121 vector89, driven by the 35S promoter or their own promoter with a Flag tag. The A. tumefaciens GV3101 with pSOUP containing constructed vectors was transiently transformed into the culture seedling of 84k. Whole plant tissues were ground into powder in liquid nitrogen, and then extracted for protein by Plant Protein Extraction Reagent (CW0885M; CWBIO, Beijing, China). Total proteins were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and then transferred to an Immobilon-P PVDF membrane (IPVH00010; Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in TBST buffer for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with anti-Flag mouse monoclonal antibody (CW0287M; CWBIO, Beijing, China; 1:5000 [v/v]) for 12 h, followed anti-Actin mouse monoclonal antibody (CW0096M; CWBIO, Beijing, China; 1:5000 [v/v]), which was used as a loading control. Goat anti-rabbit IgG (CW0103S; CWBIO, Beijing, China; 1:8000 [v/v]) was used as the secondary antibody, and Immobilon Western HRP Substrate (WBKLS; Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) was used for chemiluminescent detection. The membranes after washing with TBST buffer were visualized by the ChemiDoc Touch imaging system (Bio-Rad, California, USA).
