Immunohistochemical Evaluation of TLR‐4, HSP‐70 and Caspase‐3 Expressions in Lesional Areas of Ecthyma Disease
Authors: Emine Nur Dincer, Ozlem Ozmen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundContagious ecthyma (Orf) is a highly contagious, economically significant disease affecting sheep, goats and wild small ruminants, caused by the resilient Orf virus (ORFV). It produces thick, crusted lesions around the mouth and nostrils, especially in animals aged 3–4 months, sometimes causing near 100% mortality in immunocompromised young animals. ORFV targets epithelial cells, evading host defences and impairing feeding, which leads to malnutrition and production losses. Key molecules such as Toll‐like receptor (TLR)‐4, heat shock protein (HSP)‐70 and caspase‐3 play crucial roles in innate immunity, stress responses and apoptosis.ObjectivesThis study investigates TLR‐4, HSP‐70 and caspase‐3 expression in the lesions of naturally infected animals.Materials and MethodsA total of 37 ecthyma lesions were examined histopathologically and immunohistochemically.Conclusions and Clinical RelevanceAll three markers showed increased expression in both epithelial cells and inflammatory infiltrates within the lesions. These findings indicate that TLR‐4, HSP‐70 and caspase‐3 may play pivotal roles in the immunopathogenesis of contagious ecthyma and could represent potential diagnostic or therapeutic targets. Further research is warranted to clarify their mechanistic involvement and potential applications in disease control strategies.Keywords: caspase‐3, ecthyma, HSP‐70, IHC, TLR‐4

Introduction

Contagious ecthyma, caused by the Orf virus (ORFV; Parapoxvirus, Chordopoxvirinae, Poxviridae, Chitovirales), is a globally distributed zoonotic disease of sheep and goats [1]. ORFV is highly resilient, remaining infectious for years, and transmits via damaged skin, contaminated crusts or direct contact, including during dipping [2, 3, 4]. Morbidity is typically ~15%, yet secondary bacterial infections can raise mortality to 100% [5, 6, 7]. Lesions, primarily in the skin and oral mucosa, result from viral replication in epidermal keratinocytes, with peak titres reached within 24–72 h [7]. Reinfections occur despite immune responses, reflecting viral adaptations for immune evasion [6, 8, 9].

Keratinocytes, as the skin's first barrier, play a key role in detecting pathogens and initiating immune responses. This is mediated in part by Toll‐like receptors (TLRs), transmembrane pattern‐recognition receptors that detect pathogen‐associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), triggering cytokine and interleukin production [10, 11, 12]. Among them, TLR4 is activated by diverse ligands, including bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and the alarmin high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), via accessory proteins such as myeloid differentiation factor 2 (MD‐2), LPS‐binding protein and CD14 [13, 14]. Beyond Gram‐negative bacterial sensing, TLR4 contributes to skin inflammation, cancer, sterile inflammation, wound repair and viral infections [15, 16, 17].

In parallel, cells under infectious or inflammatory stress upregulate heat shock proteins (HSPs), a family of evolutionarily conserved chaperones that maintain protein homeostasis. The HSP70 family, one of the most extensively characterised, refolds misfolded proteins, facilitates degradation of damaged proteins, and modulates apoptosis and cytokine responses [18, 19, 20]. In infections, HSP70 expression is induced by bacterial toxins and proinflammatory cytokines, preserving cell viability and preventing excessive inflammatory damage.

Apoptosis is a regulated process essential for tissue homeostasis, executed by cysteine proteases known as caspases [21]. Caspase‐3, a key effector in both intrinsic (mitochondrial) and extrinsic (death receptor‐mediated) pathways, cleaves cytoskeletal and nuclear proteins to drive apoptotic morphology [22, 23, 24]. In bacterial and viral infections, caspase‐3 activity facilitates the controlled removal of infected cells, while low‐level activation may promote tissue repair and re‐epithelialization [25]. Dysregulated caspase‐3 activity is implicated in neurodegeneration, cancer, ischaemic injury and autoimmune disease [26].

Given ORFV's marked keratinocyte tropism, the innate immune functions of TLR4, the stress‐protective role of HSP70 and the apoptotic regulatory function of caspase‐3, we hypothesised that ORFV infection triggers a distinct and coordinated modulation of TLR4, HSP70 and caspase‐3 in keratinocytes, whereby innate immune activation, cellular stress responses and apoptosis collectively shape lesion development in contagious ecthyma. Accordingly, this study aimed to characterise the immunohistochemical (IHC) expression patterns of HSP70, TLR4 and caspase‐3 in naturally occurring cases of contagious ecthyma.

Materials and Methods

Ethics

In this study, paraffin blocks and archive records of animals diagnosed with ecthyma, available in the Department of Pathology archives, were evaluated. Because no live animals were used in the study, ethical committee approval was not required. In the study, ecthyma lesions of a total of 37 animals aged 0 to 12 months were examined, comprising 29 kids and eight lambs. Paraffin blocks from the skin of five healthy kids and five lambs were used as controls.

Histopathological Evaluation

Four separate sections of 4–5 μm thickness were obtained from paraffin‐embedded tissue samples using a fully automated microtome. One series of sections was mounted on standard slides and stained with haematoxylin & eosin. The other three series were mounted on poly‐l‐lysine‐coated slides for immunohistochemical staining.

All sections were air‐dried overnight at room temperature (RT) to ensure adherence. For H&E staining, slides were first incubated in a 60°C oven for 1.5 h to melt residual paraffin. Deparaffinisation was conducted by immersing sections in three successive xylene baths (30 min each), followed by rehydration through descending alcohol series (100%–70%) and rinsing in water. Sections were stained with Harris H&E with continuous monitoring of staining intensity. After staining, slides were dehydrated through ascending alcohol series, cleared in xylene and mounted. All stained sections were examined under a light microscope. Microscopic digital images were captured using a trinocular microscope setup with imaging software and archived digitally.

Immunohistochemical Investigation

Immunoperoxidase staining was performed using the streptavidin–biotin complex peroxidase method. Tissue sections were incubated with primary antibodies against caspase‐3 (Caspase 3/P17/P19 Polyclonal, cat. no. 19677‐1‐AP), HSP‐70 (HSP70 Polyclonal, cat. no. 10995‐1‐AP) and TLR‐4 (TLR‐4 Monoclonal, cat. no. 66350‐1‐Ig) (Proteintech), all diluted 1:100. Secondary detection employed the EXPOSE Mouse and Rabbit Specific HRP/DAB Detection Kit (ab80436) with 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogen (Abcam). For IHC validation, spleen tissue exhibiting known immunoreactivity was employed as the positive control for caspase‐3 and TLR‐4, while mammary carcinoma tissue with high HSP‐70 expression was used as the positive control for HSP‐70 staining. Negative controls were processed identically yet without primary antibodies. The procedure was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Morphometric analysis was performed on IHC stained sections using a light microscope equipped with a digital imaging system. For each specimen, five randomly selected, nonoverlapping fields were evaluated under a ×20 objective. The immunostaining intensity for caspase‐3, TLR‐4 and HSP‐70 was scored semiquantitatively under a ×20 objective using a 0–3 scale based on staining intensity and distribution: 0 = no staining, +1 = mild/moderate staining in a single region, +2 = mild/moderate staining in 2–3 regions, +3 = widespread staining of varying intensity. Additionally, digital image analysis was performed using imagej (v1.48; NIH) and morphometric evaluations with cellsens imaging software (Olympus).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses and graphical representations were performed using prism v10 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). Data distribution was assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Differences between the control and ecthyma‐affected animals were analysed using the two‐tailed Student's t‐test for normally distributed data. The association between IHC scores in control and diseased animals was evaluated using the Pearson correlation test. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Macroscopic Findings

Lesions were primarily localised on the lips and near the commissures. In severe cases, lesions extended up to the muzzle. Mild cases showed hyperaemia, oedema and slight crusting, while severe cases exhibited hardened crusts that impeded mouth closure, pronounced swelling, fissures and bleeding. Secondary infections led to lesion progression onto the tongue and palate in severe instances. Purulent exudate and pseudomembranes were frequently observed at lesion sites. Affected animals displayed weight loss as a consequence of reduced feed intake, and halitosis was noted, especially in severe cases. The disease most commonly affected the lip area, with higher incidence in animals younger than 3 months (Figure 1). Microscopic Findings

In the negative control group, skin tissues showed preserved epidermal architecture with minimal or no ballooning degeneration and inflammatory infiltration. By contrast, affected cases exhibited prominent epithelial proliferation accompanied by marked hyperkeratosis and acanthosis, reflecting increased epidermal cell turnover and epidermal thickening consistent with a chronic disease course. Frequent intraepithelial ballooning degeneration and cytoplasmic vacuolization resulting from intracellular oedema were observed in epidermal cells. Notably, pronounced epidermal proliferation resulted in rete peg formation extending into the dermis, suggesting irregular basal membrane growth in response to inflammation. Numerous cases displayed typical intracytoplasmic eosinophilic inclusion bodies within epidermal cells. Secondary bacterial colonies also were prominent in most cases, indicating concurrent bacterial infection (Figure 2).

Immunohistochemical Findings

HSP‐70 Immunohistochemical Findings

HSP‐70 immunoreactivity was mainly detected at mild intensity in mesenchymal and inflammatory cells within ecthyma‐affected lesions, with occasional mild cytoplasmic staining observed in epithelial cells (Figure 3). Quantitative analysis revealed that TLR‐4, HSP‐70 and caspase‐3 IHC scores were significantly higher in ecthyma‐affected animals compared with controls (Table 1). Correlation analysis revealed significant associations and TLR‐4, HSP‐70 and caspase‐3 immunohistochemical scores in the ecthyma group (Table 2). In particular, TLR‐4 expression showed a strong correlation with HSP‐70 and caspase‐3, suggesting a close interplay between innate immune activation, cellular stress response and apoptosis during ecthyma lesion development. The highest expression was observed in epithelial cells, at moderate to strong levels.

TLR‐4 Immunohistochemical Findings

Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated marked TLR‐4 expression in epithelial, mesenchymal and inflammatory cells within ecthyma‐affected tissues. Strong immunopositive reactions were particularly evident in orthokeratotic epithelial cells, basal epithelial and ductal cells, inflammatory cells and mesenchymal cells. By contrast, control tissues exhibited weak to negligible TLR‐4 immunoreactivity (Figure 4). Quantitative assessment revealed that TLR‐4 IHC scores were significantly elevated in ecthyma‐affected animals compared with controls (Table 1). In this study, TRL‐4 was the least expressed marker and was primarily observed in epithelial cells.

Caspase‐3 Immunohistochemical Findings

Immunohistochemical analysis revealed significant caspase‐3 expression in epithelial and inflammatory cells within the lesion areas. Immunopositive staining was particularly prominent in the basal and spinous layers of the epidermis (Figure 5). By contrast, control tissues exhibited minimal to absent caspase‐3 immunoreactivity. Quantitative evaluation demonstrated that cleaved caspase‐3 IHC scores were significantly higher in ecthyma‐affected animals compared with controls (Table 1).

Discussion

Orf virus, a member of the Parapoxvirus genus, is the aetiological agent of ecthyma, a zoonotic and highly prevalent skin infection affecting mainly sheep and goats, with a particular predilection for young animals. Clinically, the disease is characterised by proliferative lesions involving the oral cavity, lips and nose [4]. In the present study, IHC analysis revealed marked expression of TLR‐4, HSP‐70 and caspase‐3 in ecthyma lesions, indicating their close association with tissue responses during infection.

Among these markers, TLR‐4 showed prominent upregulation in epithelial and underlying mesenchymal cells, consistent with an activated local innate immune response at the site of viral replication. TLR‐4 is classically recognised for sensing lipopolysaccharide from Gram‐negative bacteria, and also is implicated in viral infections and sterile inflammation [13, 17, 27, 28, 29]. Its activation through MyD88‐dependent and TRIF‐dependent signalling pathways promotes inflammatory cytokine release, immune cell activation and interferon responses [17].

In ecthyma lesions, the intense TLR‐4 immunopositivity in epithelial, mesenchymal and inflammatory cell‐rich areas suggests a focused yet broad local immune reaction. This distribution supports the involvement of both resident skin cells and infiltrating immune cells in shaping the inflammatory milieu. Keratinocytes and dendritic cells are known to utilise TLR‐4 signalling to induce proinflammatory gene expression via nuclear factor kappa B (NF‐κB) activation [17], a mechanism consistent with the observed staining patterns.

Previous studies have documented increased TLR‐4 expression in chronic inflammatory conditions and selected viral infections [10, 11]. Extending these observations, our findings demonstrate pronounced TLR‐4 expression in ecthyma lesions, supporting its value as a histopathological indicator of active disease. Although definitive functional roles cannot be inferred from IHC findings alone, the consistent upregulation of TLR‐4 during active infection highlights its potential relevance as a diagnostic and prognostic marker.

Furthermore, the concomitant expression of TLR‐4 with HSP‐70—known to participate in inflammatory signalling [30]—and caspase‐3 suggests coordinated involvement of innate immune activation, cellular stress responses and apoptosis in ecthyma lesions. Future molecular and functional studies are required to clarify these interactions and to evaluate whether modulation of TLR‐4 signalling could offer therapeutic benefit in severe cases of ecthyma.

Ecthyma commonly develops in the presence of immunosuppression, poor hygiene and traumatic skin injury. HSPs are stress‐inducible molecular chaperones that protect cells against a wide range of pathological insults. Among them, HSP‐70 plays a key role in host defence by supporting protein folding, stabilising damaged proteins and limiting apoptosis [20]. Viral infections frequently induce HSP‐70 upregulation as part of the cellular response to oxidative stress, inflammation and virus‐induced damage [18, 31, 32, 33, 34].

HSP‐70 contributes to cellular proteostasis through protein refolding, aggregate clearance and proteasomal degradation [19, 34]. Its increased expression in ecthyma lesions suggests activation of intracellular stress‐response mechanisms in infected tissues. Additionally, HSP‐70 is known to modulate inflammatory signalling and apoptosis, and may attenuate excessive tissue damage by regulating NF‐κB–associated pathways [18, 35]. Acting as a DAMP, HSP‐70 also can influence immune responses via interactions with Toll‐like receptors, particularly TLR‐2 and TLR‐4 [30, 32].

In the present study, HSP‐70 immunoreactivity was most prominent in mesenchymal and inflammatory cells, with more limited epithelial expression. This distribution indicates that HSP‐70 primarily reflects cellular stress and immune activation within the lesion microenvironment. Elevated epithelial expression is likely to represent a protective response to infection‐related stress, while increased staining in inflammatory infiltrates is consistent with active immune engagement. Collectively, these findings support the use of HSP‐70 as a histopathological indicator of tissue stress and inflammatory activity in ecthyma.

Ecthyma lesions are frequently ulcerative and may be complicated by secondary bacterial infections, conditions that promote activation of apoptotic pathways. Caspase‐3, a central executioner of apoptosis, mediates DNA fragmentation and cellular dismantling during programmed cell death (PCD) [22]. Apoptosis is a critical host defence mechanism that limits viral spread by selectively eliminating infected cells while preserving surrounding tissue integrity. Previous studies have demonstrated caspase pathway activation in viral infections [21, 23], and the caspase‐3 immunopositivity observed in this study supports the involvement of apoptosis in ecthyma‐associated tissue responses. In this study, caspase‐3 immunopositivity was detected in epithelial, mesenchymal and inflammatory cells, indicating that ecthyma‐associated tissue responses involve multiple cellular compartments. Caspase‐3 expression in inflammatory infiltrates suggests that apoptosis may contribute to immune cell turnover or modulation of local inflammatory responses. Consistent with previous reports, caspase‐3 activation is known to be induced by infection‐related cellular stress and to facilitate selective elimination of damaged or infected cells as part of host defence mechanisms [36].

Prominent caspase‐3 staining in epithelial cells is likely to reflect controlled apoptotic activity at sites of viral entry and replication, while its presence in inflammatory cell‐rich areas suggests a role in regulating inflammation. Caspase‐3 acts as a common effector of both extrinsic and intrinsic apoptotic pathways through substrate cleavage [21, 22], supporting the interpretation that apoptosis is actively engaged in ecthyma lesions.

Beyond its established role in PCD, caspase‐3 has been implicated in tissue remodelling and wound healing, particularly when activated at low levels [25]. Accordingly, caspase‐3 expression observed in basal epithelial layers also may be associated with regenerative responses during lesion evolution. Together, these findings support a multifaceted involvement of caspase‐3 in cell clearance, inflammatory regulation and tissue repair during ecthyma, although causal relationships cannot be definitively established from IHC data alone.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size may limit generalisability, and the exclusive reliance on IHC investigation precludes detailed molecular or functional characterisation of apoptotic pathways. Furthermore, the cross‐sectional design does not allow assessment of temporal changes in caspase‐3, TLR‐4 or HSP‐70 expression during disease progression or resolution. Longitudinal studies incorporating quantitative and functional analyses are therefore required to clarify the precise contributions of these markers to ecthyma pathogenesis and host response.

Conclusions

This study provides the first IHC evidence demonstrating increased expression of TLR‐4, HSP‐70 and caspase‐3 in ecthyma lesions of small ruminants, a highly contagious disease associated with substantial economic losses. Compared with control skin, all three markers showed significantly elevated expression in lesional tissues, and their immunoexpression intensities were positively correlated with the severity of epithelial ballooning degeneration and inflammatory reactions. These findings indicate that TLR‐4, HSP‐70 and caspase‐3 reliably reflect lesion activity and tissue responses during ecthyma infection.

Although vaccination remains the most effective preventive strategy, ecthyma can still develop in young or immunocompromised animals, often resulting in severe proliferative oral lesions with limited therapeutic success. In this context, the consistent upregulation of TLR‐4, HSP‐70 and caspase‐3 highlights their potential value as histopathological indicators of active disease. While their expression patterns are compatible with inflammatory, cellular stress and apoptotic processes, definitive conclusions regarding their mechanistic roles require further longitudinal, molecular and functional studies. Nonetheless, these markers may contribute to improved lesion assessment and support future efforts aimed at early diagnosis and targeted therapeutic approaches in ecthyma.
