Engineered Clostridium butyricum: a promising application of synthetic biology in comprehensive health
Authors: Kaiyi Li, Yao Kong, Tingtao Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Synthetic biology has emerged as a rapidly advancing field, with its promising applications in human health attracting increasing attention. Engineered bacteria are designed according to synthetic biology principles to perform specific biological functions. In recent years, the application of engineered bacteria focuses on engineered live biotherapeutic products with the aim of improving health in vivo and there is increasing evidence supporting that these products possess huge application potential covering diagnosis, treatment and prevention. Compared with conventional host strains of engineered bacteria, Clostridium butyricum as a probiotic, has significant advantages including the ability to produce butyrate and the resistance to low pH, high temperature as well as the bile salts, which is an ideal host to be designed as a engineered bacterium. Despite these advantages, the application potential of engineered Clostridium butyricum has been long underestimated, primarily due to the challenges associated with genetic manipulation. However, with the advances of emerging genetic tools, it restrikes researchers’ attention. In this review, we first emphasized its advantages. Then we focused on the current research on its applications in the health field, highlighting its application potential, challenges and future directions in this promising field.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Synthetic biology, also known as engineering biology, is an emerging interdisciplinary subject that has become one of the focal points of modern biological research [1]. It aims to design new biological components, devices, systems or even individuals to meet the diverse needs of humanity according to existing science and technology [2]. It is considered as a leading third biotechnology revolution after the discovery of DNA double helix and the Human Genome Project, drawing attention from the scientific community and governments of all countries [3].

Engineered bacteria, inspired by the synthetic biology, refers to applying engineering principles to design and construct bacteria with specific biological function. For many years, engineered bacteria were used to produce recombinant proteins in “ex vivo” settings [4]. However, in recent years the therapeutic function of engineered bacteria as live biotherapeutic products (LBPs) in vivo has aroused the great interest of researchers. Guided by synthetic biology principles, a series of engineered living bacteria strains, such as engineered Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN), Lactococcus lactis (L. lactis), Listeria monocytogenes (L. monocytogenes), Salmonella typhimurium (S. typhimurium), have been developed and employed by researchers to address various diseases. However, these conventional host bacteria possess several inherent limitations, including vulnerability to extreme environmental conditions, potential self-toxicity, and inadequate colonization, which can hinder their therapeutic efficacy [5].

Notably, Clostridium butyricum (CB) offer significant advantages in producing butyric acid and the resistance to high temperature, acid, bile salts as well as certain antibiotics [6]. As a probiotic, CB has been demonstrated to be safe and beneficial in gastrointestinal health, obesity management and neurodegenerative disease improvement, highlighting its significant potential in medical field. Engineered CB (ECB) as a ‘smart CB’ can activate the therapeutic pathways more reliably in vivo than natural CB, thereby enhancing the therapeutic potential of natural CB. Consequently, in recent years CB has emerged as a highly promising bacterial strain to be designed into engineered bacteria. With the advances in gene editing technologies, such as ClosTron, Allele-Coupled Exchange and CRISPR-Cas9 system, the construction and application of ECB are becoming easier to achieve. Reports have highlighted the improved therapeutic functions of ECB in areas such as tumor treatment, vaccine development, and alleviation of type 2 diabetes mellitus. Additionally, the therapeutic effects of ECB have been confirmed in models of hypertension and Parkinson’s disease (PD) in rats, as well as its enhanced protective role in intestinal health. These findings suggest a promising future for ECB in the realms of health and medicine.

This review will expound on the application of engineered bacteria based on synthetic biology in the medical field and highlight ECB, especially the huge application prospect of ECB in the disease field and some challenges of ECB construction, revealing the great potential of ECB in the future medical field and hoping to provide theory and data support for extensive application of ECB in the future healthcare field.

The application of synthetic biology in comprehensive health

Synthetic biology and engineered bacteria

Synthetic biology is an emerging technology in the life science field of the 21 st century which aims to perform Biodesign to acquire or improve function-specific bio-platforms to meet the needs of humans and produce expected effects, depending on engineering principles of modularity, standardization and characterization [2, 7]. With an in-depth understanding of biology and microbiology, bacteria are recognized as a promising platform. In recent years, advances in synthetic biology have enabled material modification and/or genetic engineering of bacteria, giving rise to engineered bacteria with enhanced advantages and reduced side effects, which have shown promising application prospects in medical, industrial and other fields.

The applications of engineered bacteria can be broadly categorized into two aspects. For a long time, the construction of engineered bacteria is based on the bacteria that naturally produce biomaterials to further enhance the material production in vitro, which can be used to achieve the large-scale production of pharmaceuticals and industrial materials. However, nowadays the potential of engineered live bacteria as LBPs for in vivo applications has garnered increasing attention. They are the combination of synthetic biomaterials and natural living bacteria, resulting in products with enhanced functionality and safety, providing a robust and effective platform for the treatment of various diseases. Engineered live bacteria can further enhance bacteria’s ability to sense the environment, achieve specific targeting, and enable intelligent drug delivery, while maintaining their motility [8].

Currently, numerous researchers are dedicated to the development of LBPs based on engineered bacteria (Table 1), some of which have entered the clinical trial phase and achieved remarkable outcomes.

For instance, in a Phase 1/2a first-in-human clinical trial for phenylketonuria (PKU), Puurunen et al. successfully constructed the EcN strain SYNB1618 by inserting the phenylalanine ammonia lyase (PAL) gene, L-amino acid deaminase (LAAD) gene, and high-affinity phenylalanine transporter (PheP) gene into EcN as the chassis bacterium. This strain achieved dose-dependent metabolism of phenylalanine (Phe) in PKU patients with favorable safety profiles, providing a potential oral therapeutic regimen for PKU [9]. In addition, engineered L. monocytogenes has also demonstrated broad prospects in clinical trials. Focusing on the development of live attenuated tumor vaccines, researchers deleted two virulence genes (actA and inlB) of L. monocytogenes via homologous recombination and then inserted the mesothelin expression gene, successfully constructing the CRS-207 strain. This strain exhibited survival benefits and good safety in the treatment of malignant pleural mesothelioma and metastatic pancreatic cancer [10–12]. Furthermore, Phocaeicola vulgatus has been utilized as a chassis bacterium for engineering to conduct clinical trials targeting enteric hyperoxaluria (EH). Whitaker and colleagues inserted five core oxalate metabolism genes into Phocaeicola vulgatus and designed a conditional attenuation system, ultimately constructing the therapeutic candidate strain NB1000S. This work demonstrated the potential of the oxalate-degrading engineered bacterium NB1000S for the treatment of EH [13]. Another successful example is the use of the engineered L. lactis AG019, which expresses human proinsulin and Interleukin-10 (IL-10), in the treatment of patients with newly diagnosed type 1 diabetes (T1D). AG019 exhibits favorable safety profiles in the clinical trial, and when used in combination with teplizumab, it can stabilize or even improve pancreatic β-cell function, providing a new direction for the long-term maintenance therapy or early intervention of T1D [14].

Collectively, compared with traditional drug therapeutics, engineered live bacteria are alive and can execute different biological processes once administered into the body. They can even selectively express therapeutic molecules in response to disease-specific signals, thereby maintaining high treatment specificity [15]. Additionally, they possess the targeting and can be designed to reach a specific location, reducing the drug dose and minimizing systemic toxicity. This makes them highly promising for tumor-targeted therapies, where they could potentially overcome the side effects associated with conventional cancer treatments [16]. In contrast to traditional probiotic therapy, engineered probiotics can perform therapeutic effect more reliably. They will be removed the harmful characteristics and have a stronger probiotic potential [17]. Finally, engineered live bacteria can also be effectively applied in antibiotic-resistant diseases and avoid the rising occurrence of drug resistance [18] (Fig. 1).

Engineered Clostridium butyricum: a promising platform

Clostridium butyricum: advantages as the host bacterium

CB is an obligate anaerobe and gram-positive rod shape bacterium, belonging to the Clostridium genus within the class Clostridia [35]. Wild-type CB exist in a wide variety of environments and can be found in soil, milk products, and vegetables. They also naturally reside in the human gut and can be detected in 10−20% of the adult human population. In 1933, Dr. Miyairi isolated the Strain MIYAIRI 588 (a kind of CB) from the feces of healthy individuals for the first time and then from the soil in 1963. Subsequently, this strain was extensively used to improve diarrhea or other gastrointestinal symptoms in Japan, Korea, and China [36, 37]. Therefore, CB can be regarded as a historical probiotic.

With the booming development of synthetic biology, the potential of CB as a chassis bacterium has attracted growing attention from researchers owing to its several advantageous characteristics. Firstly, due to its ability to form endospores, CB naturally has the enhanced capacity to resist harsh environmental conditions of gastrointestinal tract such as acidity, alkalinity, and high temperatures [38], making it a highly promising platform for engineered LBP.

In addition, CB is also well known for its ability to produce 1,3-propanediol and hydrogen [39], which broadens the application scope of CB as a chassis for engineered bacteria and endows it with potential application value in industrial production. The ability to naturally produce butyrate is another highlight of CB. Butyrate, a crucial short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) produced through the bacterial fermentation of dietary fibers, plays a vital role in intestinal health and serves as a key energy source for colonocytes. Furthermore, it also contributes to maintaining the intestinal barrier, initiating anti-inflammation signaling pathway and even improving colon cancer [40]. Beyond its direct health function to the gut, increasing studies also indicated that butyrate could exert beneficial effects indirectly to the central nervous system (CNS) through Gut-Brain Axis [41], highlighting its potential for addressing CNS diseases. As a natural producer of butyrate, CB eliminates the need to construct specialized expression system of butyrate, avoiding the complex genetic manipulation and guaranteeing the stable production of butyrate. This intrinsic capability positions CB as a promising candidate with substantial application potential in the medical field.

Collectively, the robust stress resistance and abundant natural products of CB have enabled this traditional probiotic to re-emerge at the forefront of science in the context of synthetic biology (Fig. 2).

Comprehensive applications of engineered Clostridium butyricum

CB is an important probiotics, and as highlighted previously, CB possesses a robust resistance to relatively extreme gastrointestinal environment including acidity, bile salt, high temperature, and exposure to various antibiotics [38]. These distinctive characteristics make CB a promising candidate for engineering. At present, an increasing number of studies have focused on the development of ECB, with some research already exploring its potential applications in both health and industrial sectors.

Potential application of ECB in industry

Hydrogen is a promising alternative fuel due to its pollution-free nature, non-toxicity to mammals, and high energy density [42]. However, the major problem in the widespread use of hydrogen is the high production costs. Although microbial fermentation, including CB, Rhodopseudomonas palustris and E.coli has greatly reduced the cost [43–45], how to increase the yield of hydrogen is another critical issue. There has been research about enhancing hydrogen production by CB through genetic and metabolic methods. CB possesses a unique metabolic pathway that allows for hydrogen production from NADH [46]. Carbohydrates, such as glucose and starch, can be used as the substrates to produce the hydrogen, but the generation of some side products, especially ethanol, will consume the NADH and lead to reduced hydrogen yield [47]. To address this, Cai et al. inactivated the aad gene which encodes crucial enzymes involving in the production of ethanol, through genetic manipulations in CB. In addition, they also introduced exogenous butyrate into the fermentation medium. Inspiringly, simultaneous genetic and metabolic manipulation achieved the redirection of metabolic pathway to hydrogen generation, leading to a huge increase in hydrogen production [48].

For any engineered bacteria, stable expression of foreign genes is vital for its extensive application. A recent study described a method for the stable overexpression of heterologous genes in CB through harnessing native-cryptic plasmids. Native-cryptic plasmids are small plasmids naturally existing in the bacteria with high copy number but little genetic information and inapparent phenotype [49]. Compared with traditional antibiotic-selective vectors, native-cryptic plasmids do not require the use of antibiotics, eliminating the risk of promoting the spread of antibiotic resistance [50]. Therefore, native-cryptic plasmids are promising vectors for engineering transformation of bacteria. Notably, Zhang et al. successfully achieved the stable overexpression of a secreted cellulase, Cel9M, in the native-cryptic plasmid of CB, pCB102 [51]. And the enzyme Cel9M can convert the cellulose into fermentable glucose [52], presenting significant industrial application prospects for ECB.

Potential application of ECB in health

Tumor treatment

Tumor treatment

Tumor treatment

The immunotherapy of tumors through inhibiting the indoleamine 2,3 dioxygenase (IDO) has received extensive attention in recent years. IDO, an enzyme expressed by tumor cells, catalyzes the conversion of tryptophan into kynurenine, leading to a local depletion of tryptophan in the tumor microenvironment [53]. Previous studies found that tryptophan was a vital regulatory factor of CD8 + T cells, promoting rapid activation and response [54]. By inhibiting IDO, the depletion of tryptophan can be reversed, thereby promoting robust CD8 + T cell activation and enhancing anti-tumor response. However, the unreliable targeting is a huge challenge of conventional IDO inhibitors, for IDO not only exists in tumor cells, but involves in other physiological processes like tissue repair and the metabolism of neurotransmitters [55, 56]. Therefore, the administration of conventional IDO inhibitors may lead to toxic effects on different metabolic pathways simultaneously. Inspiringly, Wang and colleagues constructed a kind of ECB with high expressing of tryptophan (L-Trp CB). Due to the anaerobic characteristic of CB and IDO inhibition function of butyrate, ECB could accurately colonize at the tumor site, therefore the targeting of IDO inhibition achieved significant improvement. Moreover, the overexpression of tryptophan by L-Trp CB could further facilitate the activation and response of CD8 + T cells to inhibit the growth of tumors. L-Trp CB has been proved to exert the anti-tumor function in tumor models of mice and rabbits [57], providing a potential strategy for tumor immunotherapy.

Apart from the immunotherapy of tumors, ECB has demonstrated potential in the complete eradication of melanoma. The growth area of melanoma is typically hypoxic and hypovascular, which is adverse to the arrival of conventional anti-tumor drugs [58], but provides an appropriate condition for the colonization of anaerobic or facultative anaerobic oncolytic bacteria. Up to now, bacterial cancer therapy is a popular field, with several oncolytic bacterial strains, including Salmonella, Listeria, and E. coli, being explored for their potential in melanoma treatment [59].

Clostridium is a promising obligate anaerobic bacterium with antineoplastic effects and can selectively colonize the anoxic region of the melanoma, thereby minimizing the systemic toxicity. Previous study indicated that Clostridium could induce the production of chemokines, leading to increased infiltration of CD8 + T cells and intensified immune-mediated killing to tumor cells [60]. However, the major problem of anaerobic Clostridium therapy is that the bacteria can only eliminate the central anoxic area of the melanoma but is helpless in the peripheral blood-rich region [61]. In a 2022 publication, Shi and his colleagues innovatively employed ECB to achieve complete ablation of melanoma. In this study, the metabolic substrate of CB, d-alanine (d‐Ala), was first conjugated with a photosensitizer (TPApy), which exhibits aggregation‐induced emission (AIE), to produce d‐Ala‐TPAPy. This conjugate was then metabolized and incorporated into the cell wall of CB to form ECB. This ECB overcame the limitation of conventional CB therapy, which is restricted by the higher oxygen concentrations in the peripheral, blood-rich melanoma regions. In this new ECB treatment, with the gradual elimination of melanoma hypoxic area, oxygen partial pressure will gradually increase, and massive CB will die, stimulating immune system and amplifying tumor killing. As supplementary, photosensitizer could perform photodynamic therapy (PDT) at oxygen-rich area and use generated singlet molecular oxygen (1O2) to further eliminate the peripheral area of malignant melanoma [62]. This study is a good proof that ECB possesses promising applications in the medicine.

(2)Protective and preventive functions

(2)Protective and preventive functions

Protective and preventive functions

Previous studies have demonstrated that CB possesses the ability to protect the gut epithelial barrier and the mechanism might depend on the upregulation of ligands of epidermal growth factor receptors (EGFR) which can promote the phosphorylation of EGFR. Thus, EGFR is a key protein in CB-mediated intestine protection [63]. Epidermal growth factors (EGF) can facilitate the proliferation and differentiation of gut epithelial cells and have an important effect on the recovery of damaged intestinal mucosa [64]. Recombination and transformation technology of probiotics can enhance the positive effect of probiotics in certain specific pathways and increase targeting as well as serviceability [63]. Based on the above principle about the protection of gut epithelial barrier by CB, in a 2021 study, Ma et al. constructed four ECB strains (BC/pPD1, BC/pSPP, BC/pGHF and BC/pDBD) to overexpress porcine epidermal growth factor (pEGF) to further enhance the gut-protective function of CB. Notably, the ECB overexpressing pEGF further enhanced the gut protection function via STAT3 signaling pathway, a critical mediator in maintaining the balance between pathogen infection and inflammation. These ECBs not only strengthened CB’s positive effects on intestinal development, antimicrobial activity, and anti-inflammatory responses but also improved the overall gut protection by more effectively maintaining this balance [65].

ECB may also play a vital role in the future oral vaccine production. The principle of vaccine based on engineered bacteria is that a safe bacterial strain is genetically engineered and introduced additional genes encoding preventative antigens from unrelated pathogens [66]. Vaccine based on engineered bacteria can achieve oral vaccination which is advantageous to induce effective secretory IgA antibodies and T cell reactions in gastrointestinal tract as well as other local mucosal sites [67, 68]. At the same time, oral vaccine can increase the patient compliance and encourage the vaccination [68]. In a 2024 study by Zhang et al., ECB was successfully utilized to achieve full expression of recombinant antigen proteins, with the potential for use as oral-spore vaccines. In this study, conventional CB was designed to produce recombinant full-length New York esophageal squamous cell carcinoma 1 (NY-ESO-1) and Spike-S1 antigens through multiplex genetic manipulations [69]. The expression of NY-ESO-1 has been observed in numerous cancer types and is a potential target for cancer vaccine. It has been confirmed to induce humoral and cellular immunity. As for Spike-S1 antigen, it is a main target of COVID-19 vaccines and therefor also a potential target of oral vaccine [70]. This construction of ECB provided a new thought of oral vaccine production and disease prevention.

(3)Hypertension treatment

(3)Hypertension treatment

Hypertension treatment

Hypertension is a severe chronic disease and one of the major health problems in the world. It is the leading cause of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality all over the world [71]. Conventional drug treatment usually requires combination of multiple medications, yet their efficacy remains suboptimal. It is common for patients to still possess blood pressure levels above the normal range despite treatment, which not only limits therapeutic outcomes but also exposes patients to the risks of drug toxicity [72].

Hypertension is characterized by activation and excitation of sympathetic nerve system. Previous studies have demonstrated a link between glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) and sympathetic nervous system activity [73]. The GLP-1 receptors (GLP-1R) are expressed not only in pancreatic beta cells but also within the nervous system, and the activation of GLP-1R could reduce sympathetic outflow in hypertension. GLP-1 could play a vital role in modulating hypertension and sympathetic excitation mediated by carotid chemoreceptor [74]. In addition, GLP-1 can also decrease the blood pressure via diuretic mechanism [75]. Apart from GLP-1, butyric acid also has emerged as a potential mediator in the treatment of hypertension. Previous studies have indicated that the disturbance of intestinal microbiota, especially the reduction of bacteria producing butyric acid, played an important role in the pathogenesis of hypertension [76, 77]. Besides, Maifeld et al. found the improvement of hypertension caused by dietary habit and mode attributed to the recovery of gut microbiota and increase of butyric acid level [78]. According to recent study, sodium butyrate could ameliorate hypertension induced by deoxycorticosterone acetate/salt via inhibiting the MR/SGK1 pathway [79].

As previously discussed, GLP-1 and butyric acid are both important therapeutic targets in hypertension. However, the study about the combined use of these two substances in hypertension is rare and the effect is not clear. In a recent study, Wang et al. successfully introduced recombinant plasmid pMTL007 including hGLP-EGFP gene into CB through heat-shock method to construct Clostridium butyricum-pMTL007-GLP-1 (CB-GLP-1), which could secret GLP-1 and butyric acid, to study the treatment effect to (spontaneously hypertensive rats) SHR. Inspiringly, the results demonstrated that CB-GLP-1 constructed by Wang et al. indeed exerted therapeutic function in SHR. GLP-1 intervention reduced the level of angiotensin II and angiotensin II type-1 receptor [80]. Previous study has confirmed that the binding of these two could produce significant vasoconstriction and blood pressure increasing [81]. As for butyric acid, it exerted function depending on increasing the expression level of G-protein coupled receptor 109 A. In addition, CB-GLP-1 ameliorated the intestinal microbiota disturbance, indicating an additional anti-hypertension mechanism via regulating gut microbiota. Importantly, CB-GLP-1 possessed relatively high plasmid stability and resistance to acid as well as bile salts, which made stable therapeutic effect possible and suggested oral potential.

(4)PD improvement

(4)PD improvement

PD improvement

ECB also holds significant promise in the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases. As the second most common neurodegenerative disease, PD mainly affects the aged and can cause disability on affected patients [82]. Conventional drug treatments such as levodopa, a dopamine precursor, and dopamine receptor agonists, can only ameliorate the symptoms, but fail to stop or reverse the disease progression. Eventually, the effectiveness of these therapies diminishes, even leading to adverse effects such as motor complications [83].

As previously discussed, CB exhibits neuroprotective properties and has been shown to ameliorate the MPTP-induced PD through gut microbiota-GLP-1 pathway [84]. In addition, GLP-1 itself also involves in neuroprotection and is closely linked to the pathogenesis of PD [85]. However, GLP-1 has a short half-life because of the hydrolysis by dipeptidyl peptidase-IV [86]. Although the effective GLP-1R agonists with long half-life have been developed [87], the compliance is poor because of the requirement of continuous injection. Engineered strain MG1363-GLP-1 and EcN 1917-GLP-1 that express the GLP-1 have been designed and demonstrated the significant improvement of PD [88, 89], but these two strains are resistant to antibiotics and weakly tolerant of the environment in the gastrointestinal tract. Additionally, post-production storage instability presents another obstacle.

To address these issues, Wang et al. constructed the recombinant CB-GLP-1 which holds promise for the development of a novel and more effective therapeutic approach for PD. CB-GLP-1 improved the motor dysfunction and ameliorated the neuropathological changes via decreasing nigral α-synuclein level, while simultaneously upregulating tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) and dopamine transporter (DAT) levels. These improvements have been proved depending on increasing of GLP-1 and GLP-1R levels induced by CB-GLP-1. Strikingly, researchers further found CB-GLP-1 promoted the mitophagy pathway mediated by PINK1/Parkin [90], a crucial mechanism in mitochondrial quality control. Previous studies suggested mitochondrial dysfunction was relevant to the PD and accumulation of damaged mitochondria promoted the apoptosis of dopaminergic neurons [91, 92]. CB-GLP-1 might ameliorate the PD via facilitating the PINK1/Parkin mitophagy pathway [93] to clear damaged mitochondria. In addition, CB-GLP-1 relieved oxidative stress in PD and restored dysregulation of intestinal microbiome relevant to the PD pathogenesis. Subsequently, the study of Liao and the colleagues further supplemented the therapeutic mechanisms of CB-GLP-1 to PD. They found that CB-GLP-1 could relieve oxidative stress caused by astrocyte senescence through inhibiting the p53/p21 pathway [94]. As an engineered bacterium, CB-GLP-1 presented high therapeutic potential, not only due to its efficacy in treating PD but also because it offers the advantage of continuous GLP-1 expression with the convenience of oral administration. Apart from that, the engineered CB-GLP-1 contain spores and have higher storage-tolerance, which makes the industrialization of CB-GLP-1 possible and easier [90].

Type 2 diabetes mellitus improvement

With the improvement of material living standards and the intensification of population aging, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) has been a major global health problem [95]. As a chronic metabolic disease, T2DM could cause a spectrum of complications, including renal disease, cardiovascular disease, stroke and retinopathy, thus establishing itself as one of the leading causes of death [96]. At present, common drugs used to treat T2DM clinically include biguanides, sulfonylureas, thiazolidinediones, and alpha-glucosidase inhibitors [97]. However, these drugs are inevitably detrimental to the gastrointestinal health, accompanied by side effects like nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea.

Encouragingly, Zhou et al. reported the successful achievement of hypoglycemic effect on T2DM through the administration of engineered probiotic CB-GLP-1, which also minimized gastrointestinal side effects [98]. GLP-1 promotes proliferation and differentiation of β cells in the Islet of Langerhans [99], but as previously discussed, the efficacy of exogenously administered GLP-1 is limited by its rapid degradation, primarily due to the action of dipeptidyl peptidase-IV [86]. GLP-1 delivery system based on ECB constructed by Zhou et al. significantly mitigated this problem. Notably, previous studies have demonstrated that CB supplementation itself could alleviate insulitis and inhibit the onset of diabetes in non-obese diabetic (NOD) mice [100]. In addition, oral administration of CB in a diabetic mouse model has been proved to increase glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity [101]. Hence, in theory CB-GLP-1 could exert remarkable hypoglycemic effect, and the study of Zhou et al. confirmed this. It was reported that CB-GLP-1 could improve blood glucose, increase glucose tolerance, regulate abnormal blood lipid, inhibit the apoptosis of β cells in the islet of Langerhans and facilitate insulin secretion [98], providing a new prospective for T2DM treatment (Fig. 3).

Challenges and opportunities of engineered Clostridium butyricum

Currently, the construction of ECB faces challenges common to the engineering of other host strains. In general, natural metabolic pathways in host bacteria cannot meet practical demands, therefore, one major application of engineering is to upregulate, downregulate or delete relative genes to redirect the metabolic pathway, achieving incremental production of goal products and minimizing by-products. However, overproduction of certain metabolites may incur adverse effects to the host bacteria, including toxicity and disturbed metabolic homeostasis [102]. This is well exemplified by a study examining the toxicity of isopentenyl pyrophosphate (IPP) to isoprenoid-producing E. coli. As a precursor in isoprenoid biosynthesis, the accumulation of IPP in engineered E. coli led to suppressed cell growth and reduced product yield. Concrete mechanisms included impaired nutrients intake, reduced ATP level, vulnerable plasmid stability and disturbed nucleotide metabolism [103]. Furthermore, given that all metabolic pathways are tightly regulated, even a single alteration to a specific pathway may trigger conflicts with regulatory mechanisms, thereby compromising bacterial performance [102].

In addition, achieving stable colonization of engineered bacteria remains challenging. Specifically, engineered bacteria exhibit insufficient capacity for long-term colonization within the host and are prone to clearance by antibiotics [104]. While the introduction of antibiotic resistance genes can enhance the colonization ability of engineered strains to a certain degree, it may also promote the transfer of antibiotic resistance to other microorganisms and facilitate the emergence of resistant strains, thereby posing new public health risks [105]. Additionally, most engineered bacteria are designed to express exogenous proteins, which may trigger a violent immune response and cause health risk, thus, how to avoid excessive immune response is also crucial. Finally, it must be considered that the microbiota in model animals is different from that of human and the therapeutic effect may decrease or disappear on humans [106].

As for challenges exclusive to ECB, the most significant case is that Clostridium species possess extremely active Restriction and Modification (RM) system acting as an “immune system” to prevents genetic transfers between different species and phage invasions, which makes the Clostridium species one of the most difficult organisms to transform [107]. Although the genome of CB has been completely sequenced [108], the highly tough transformation process and the lack of feasible genetic tools still hinder the metabolic engineering progress of CB. Encouragingly, Heap et al. developed the ClosTron technology specific for the Clostridium species which engineered a mutagenesis system based on the mobile group II intron (Ll.ltrB) derived from the ltrB gene of Lactococcus lactis. This system employs the RNA-mediated “retrohoming” mechanism to achieve site-specific integration of the intron. It allows the intron to be reprogrammed to target specific sites in any gene, thereby enabling efficient and precise gene knockout. It was reported that ClosTron technology has successfully achieved the gene knockout in many Clostridium species including CB [109].

Subsequently, a precise procedure called Allele-Coupled Exchange (ACE) to alter the genome of Clostridium based on pyrE gene mediated bi-directional screening has been developed. This technology relies on the auxotrophy and counterselection of the pyrE gene and mediates the replacement of the target gene with an allele harboring specific mutations via two homologous recombination. Although this technology was developed for highly efficient and precise manipulation of the Clostridioides difficile genome, the pyrE mutant constructed via ACE can serve as a universal host, applicable to mutagenesis studies in all Clostridia (including Clostridium acetobutylicum, Clostridium botulinum and others), which has been verified by genome resequencing to be free of additional mutations. This technology has been successfully implemented in species closely related to CB (such as Clostridium acetobutylicum and Clostridium sporogenes), demonstrating its potential for genetic manipulation of CB [110].

In recent years, the CRISPR-Cas9 system has emerged as a popular tool for gene editing. In 2019, Guo et al. developed a Clostridium-targeting CRISPR-Cas9 system using Clostridium perfringens as the model organism, which has become a representative study in the development of genetic editing tools for intestinal commensal Clostridia in recent years. This study employed an optimized dual-vector CRISPR-Cas9 system, addressing the issue of low conjugation efficiency of large plasmids associated with the early single-vector CRISPR-Cas9 system. Meanwhile, it extended the donor-recipient co-cultivation time from 24 h to 72 h, which significantly improved the mutation efficiency [111]. Although this study only validated and achieved satisfactory results in Clostridium perfringens, it holds great implications for the development of CRISPR-Cas9 genetic editing tools for CB of the same Clostridium genus.

Subsequently, Zhou et al. reported two CRISPR-Cas9 systems applied in the genetic manipulation of CB. The heterologous Type II CRISPR-Cas9 system has successfully enabled genome editing in three strains of CB. However, due to the excessively large size of plasmids containing the exogenous Cas9 gene, plasmid conjugation efficiency is low. Additionally, CB exhibits extremely low homologous recombination efficiency, which fails to rapidly repair the DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) induced by exogenous Cas9, thereby leading to cell death. Consequently, this approach results in extremely low transformation efficiency. Subsequently, they utilized the endogenous Type I-B CRISPR-Cas system located on the large plasmid of CB. This system does not require the expression of ‘toxic’ exogenous Cas9, resulting in a significant improvement in transformation efficiency, further expanding the toolkit for genetic modifications in this challenging organism [112].

Given that Firmicutes bacteria such as Clostridia have poor DSB repair capabilities, the traditional CRISPR-Cas9 system cannot be applied to them. To address this, Jin et al. developed a set of genetic manipulation methods targeting over 140 non-model gut microorganisms across 5 phyla, including Firmicutes. They identified genetic transfer methods for 38 Clostridium strains through screening suitable replication origins, selecting antibiotic markers universal to the 5 phyla, evading RM systems and optimizing conjugation conditions. Meanwhile, two genetic editing tools were constructed. The CRISPRi-dCpf1 system uses inactivated Cpf1 (dCpf1), which does not generate DSBs—this avoids the lethal issue caused by the low homologous recombination efficiency of Clostridia—and achieved 3- to 100-fold gene silencing efficiency in 27 Clostridium strains. The 16 S-tron tool, specifically designed for Clostridia, utilizes group II introns to target conserved 16 S rRNA sites and successfully achieved site-specific insertion in 15 Clostridium strains via the retrotransposition-activated marker (RAM) [113]. Although CB was not included in the validated strains of this experiment, considering the common challenges in exogenous DNA delivery across the Clostridium genus and the universality of these two genetic editing tools for Clostridia, it is reasonable to conclude that this study holds great significance for further improving and developing genetic editing tools for CB and enabling simple and reliable genetic editing operations.

In addition to the challenging genetic manipulation, the inherent physiological characteristics of CB also serve as crucial limiting factors for the development of ECB. The two traits—sporulation and anaerobiosis—act as a double-edged sword. While they endow CB with advantages as a chassis bacterium for engineering modification and promising prospects in cancer therapy, they also restrict the application of ECB.

Clostridia such as CB are highly sensitive to oxygen. Under aerobic conditions, key enzymes involved in energy metabolism in Clostridia are rapidly poisoned by oxygen, and the severe energy deficiency ultimately leads to growth inhibition and even death of the bacteria [114]. Although Clostridia can redirect metabolic pathways to shift carbon and energy metabolism toward those with higher oxygen tolerance, such metabolic rewiring has a high probability of impairing the performance of engineered strains, resulting in a decrease in the yield of the intended target product. For instance, upon exposure to oxygen, the pathway for converting acetyl-CoA to butyric acid in CB is downregulated, leading to reduced butyric acid production [115]. Additionally, although Clostridial spores can germinate normally under aerobic conditions, the presence of oxygen impedes the subsequent development and growth of newly formed vegetative cells [116]. Furthermore, the anaerobic nature necessitates that genetic manipulation of CB be performed under strictly anaerobic conditions, which further exacerbates the difficulty in constructing ECB. In practical industrial and medical applications, the creation and maintenance of strict anaerobic conditions are also extremely challenging.

For ECB to exert its predesigned functions, the successful germination of its spores is crucial. However, current research on the conditions and mechanisms underlying Clostridial spore germination remains insufficient. Most existing studies in this field have focused on Clostridium perfringens and Clostridioides difficile; yet there are significant differences in spore germination conditions among different Clostridium species [117]. Consequently, these studies hold limited guiding significance for regulating the spore germination of CB. Furthermore, Clostridial spore germination exhibits heterogeneity. Research on Clostridioides difficile has revealed the phenomenon of spore superdormancy, wherein only a small fraction of spores are capable of germinating [116, 118]. In summary, the current understanding of CB spore germination is inadequate, and how to control the efficient germination of its spores at the appropriate time and location remains a pressing challenge that urgently needs to be addressed for the future applications of ECB.

Summary and prospect

As an emerging interdisciplinary field, synthetic biology has demonstrated significant potential across various fields, especially in medicine. Engineered bacteria are the bacteria with specific function designed to meet the need of humans based on the principles of synthetic biology. Engineered EcN, S. typhimurium, L. lactis and L. monocytogenes have been extensively studied. In recent years, ECB has emerged as a promising new engineered bacterial strain with great application potential. Notably, ECB has been investigated for its potential in areas such as tumor elimination, oral vaccine development, hypertension treatment, Parkinson’s disease amelioration, type 2 diabetes management, and intestinal protection.

Nevertheless, ECB remains an underdeveloped field, with several challenges impeding its advancement. The lack of reliable genetic tools, limitations inherent in the physiological characteristics of CB itself, and common issues encountered during the construction of engineered bacteria have constrained the development of ECB. Encouragingly, researchers are dedicated to developing more efficient and feasible genetic tools, as well as constructing safer and more reliable strains. Admittedly, the large-scale construction and application of ECB remains an arduous task. Future research should focus on improving the efficiency of genetic engineering, overcoming the challenges posed by anaerobic characteristics and spore germination, minimizing the metabolic burden on host bacteria, reducing immunogenicity, and enhancing colonization efficiency. Although most current studies on ECB are conducted in animal models, promising results highlight the potential for further scientific exploration and application in the medical field.
