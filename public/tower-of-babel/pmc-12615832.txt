Development of a reliable mouse model for caudal epidural anesthesia
Authors: Yuki Kinishi, Yoshihisa Koyama, Tomoo Yuba, Ayako Takahashi, Shoichi Shimada, Takeshi Yoshida, Yuji Fujino
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpidural anesthesia is frequently used for pain management during surgery and childbirth. However, a persistent issue is fever induced by epidural anesthesia, a common complication in clinical settings, for which effective countermeasures are lacking. Developing a reliable mouse model is crucial for investigating the underlying mechanisms. This study focuses on developing a reproducible technique for administering caudal epidural anesthesia in mice. Male ddY mice were administered 0.25% ropivacaine in volumes of 50, 100, or 150 µL into the epidural space. Successful epidural puncture was confirmed by the presence of blue dye in the epidural space. Anesthetic effects were compared using the formalin test. Epidural success rates ranged from 45.5% to 63.6%, with the highest success in the 100 µL group. The lowest success rate was observed in the 50 µL group. The formalin test showed stable analgesia with 100 µL, while 150 µL produced a broader anesthetic effect but increased mortality. To address the anatomical and technical challenges of precise needle placement in small animals, we successfully performed caudal epidural anesthesia in mice by optimizing the puncture posture using a stereotaxic apparatus. Administration of 100 µL of 0.25% ropivacaine provided stable analgesia with minimal side effects. Introduction

Epidural anesthesia, a type of neuraxial anesthesia, is a regional anesthetic technique in which a local anesthetic is administered to the epidural space. Anesthesia is commonly used for pain relief during surgery and childbirth in humans and large animals1,2. The advantages of epidural anesthesia include that it allows pain relief while the patient remains conscious, has less effect on respiratory function than general anesthesia, and promotes early recovery after surgery3,4. It is also possible to use a catheter for continuous anesthesia5 and to improve the management of postoperative pain by combining it with general anesthesia6,7. Conversely, epidural anesthesia has some disadvantages. Reports indicate issues such as insufficient local anesthesia due to the uneven distribution of the anesthetic agent8, technical challenges and infection risks during catheter insertion, and complications such as hypotension and headaches9. Proper management of these risks demands meticulous techniques performed by highly experienced and skilled medical professionals.

In addition, there are some unresolved issues associated with epidural anesthesia. Epidural anesthesia can cause fever during labor and surgery10,11. Although fever is often encountered in clinical practice, there are currently no effective countermeasures. Animal experiments are essential for elucidating the underlying mechanisms, but technical challenges have hindered the establishment of an animal model for epidural anesthesia. Usually, intraperitoneal or intravenous anesthesia is used for small animals such as mice and rats12. In small animals, epidural catheters are surgically implanted at the thoracic or lumbar spinal level; however, the procedure is technically demanding and lacks reproducibility13–15. Epidural anesthesia via a single puncture is not performed because of the risk of the needle penetrating the dura mater and causing spinal anesthesia. Although rat models have previously been used to study epidural anesthesia, mice offer several key advantages, including lower cost, broader availability of genetically modified strains, and greater compatibility with high-throughput studies. These features make mice a powerful tool for investigating the mechanistic and genetic aspects of epidural anesthesia, including systemic responses such as inflammation and thermoregulation. Therefore, the aim of this study was to establish a reliable and reproducible method for caudal epidural anesthesia in mice, and to evaluate its efficacy and safety as a platform for future mechanistic investigations.

In the present study, we established a mouse model of caudal epidural anesthesia by administering a drug solution into the epidural space via the sacrococcygeal interspinous space, which serves as an anatomical entry point analogous to the sacral hiatus commonly used in clinical practice. Although the anatomical structures differ between mice and humans, this approach functionally simulates caudal epidural anesthesia as performed in clinical settings. This approach simplifies the procedure and reduces the risk associated with spinal anesthesia. Our findings are expected to contribute to the research and clinical applications of epidural anesthesia.

Results

Behavioral outcomes

First, a formalin test was performed to examine the effectiveness of epidural anesthesia. The group that received 50 µL of the solution is referred to as Epi 50, the group that received 100 µL is Epi 100, and the group that received 150 µL is Epi 150. Similarly, the sham-operated group was referred to as the sham group. Although the formalin test was performed in all animals, only those in which successful epidural administration was confirmed by dye staining were included in the final analysis for the epidural anesthesia groups (Epi 50: n = 5; Epi 100: n = 7; Epi 150: n = 6). All animals in the sham group were included (n = 11). The average duration of pain-like behaviors over 60 min after formalin injection was measured every 10 min, and the results are shown in Fig. 1; Table 1. Figure 1a presents the time course of pain-like behavior over the entire 60-min period. Statistical analysis revealed that at time point 10 (0–10 min), all epidural anesthesia groups exhibited significantly reduced pain-like behavior compared to the sham group (*p < 0.05 and **p < 0.01). At time point 30 (20–30 min), significant reductions were observed in the Epi 100 and Epi 150 groups (**p < 0.01), and at time point 40 (30–40 min), similar reductions were noted in these groups (*p < 0.05). Time point 40, which corresponds to the late phase of the formalin test, was selected as a representative interval for graphical comparison and is shown as a bar graph in Fig. 1b. Fig. S1 provides bar graphs for all individual time intervals, allowing detailed comparisons of pain-like behavior across all groups. These results suggest that 100 µL of 0.25% ropivacaine effectively suppresses formalin-induced pain-like behaviors, with a measurable analgesic effect lasting for approximately 40 min after injection.

Evaluation of epidural administration outcomes

Subsequently, proper epidural administration of anesthetic was performed. The success of the epidural administration was determined by the presence of a blue dye in the epidural space (Fig. 2a and b). In addition to the success rate, the stained vertebral level was noted. Table 2 presents the results of the study. The success rate in the Epi 50 group was 45.5%, with the maximum dye spread observed at the thoracic level. In contrast, both the Epi 100 and Epi 150 groups showed greater dye spread, reaching the cervical level in successful cases. The success rates for the Epi 100 and Epi 150 groups were 63.6% and 45.5%, respectively. Notably, no deaths occurred in the Epi 100 group, whereas mortality was observed only in the Epi 150 group. In the sham group, the success rate was 54.5%, and the dye also reached the cervical level. These findings indicate that the extent of dye spread varied depending on the injected volume, with higher volumes resulting in greater cephalad distribution. In particular, a higher proportion of mice in the Epi 150 group exhibited dye spread to the cervical level compared to other groups. Representative images of cervical-level staining in both surviving and deceased mice from the Epi 150 group are provided in Fig. S2.

Discussion

We developed a method for administering caudal epidural anesthesia to mice using a stereotaxic apparatus and evaluated its anesthetic effects using a formalin test. This simple and accurate method for performing epidural anesthesia in mice will contribute to the elucidation of the mechanisms of action of epidural anesthesia, including its adverse reactions, in the future.

To avoid the risk of spinal anesthesia, we chose to perform an epidural puncture from the sacrococcygeal or coccygeal interspinous space. Mega et al. reported that the spinal cords of mice terminate at the level of the fifth and sixth lumbar vertebrae16. In the sacrococcygeal region of mice, the subarachnoid space has been reported to be occluded, with openings only around the spinal nerve roots17. Although no study has detailed the spinal level at the lower end of the subarachnoid space, Qiaoli et al. confirmed that cerebrospinal fluid drains into lymphatic vessels in the caudal spinal cord, particularly in the intervertebral region of the sacrum18. Therefore, we assumed that the subarachnoid space extends to the upper sacral region in mice, as in humans, and performed a puncture from the sacrococcygeal or coccygeal interspinous space. Based on a study by Mega et al16., we estimated that the depth of the puncture needle at this site was approximately 4 mm. In the median approach, which involves reaching the epidural space between the spinous processes, the needle must be tilted; however, with stereotaxic puncture, the needle can only be inserted vertically. To overcome this problem, we tilted the mouse to enable a stereotaxic puncture. Furthermore, by combining this with an automatic drug injection device, we ensured the precision of the stereotaxic puncture and established a technique for epidural anesthesia using a single puncture method. To visually support this description, we included an illustration (Fig. 3) comprising two diagrams: one showing the anatomical landmarks and needle trajectory, and the other depicting the stereotaxic setup with a 30° head-down tilt, which facilitates vertical insertion between the spinous processes. These diagrams clarify both the puncture site and the technical modification employed to overcome the challenges of vertical needle insertion in mice. In addition, reproducibility was ensured by consistently identifying anatomical landmarks (S4/Co1 or Co1/Co2) and confirming tactile resistance when the needls contacted with the posterior surface of the vertebral body, which served as a reliable indicator of successful puncture.

In clinical practice, epidural anesthesia is typically administered at the vertebral level closest to the target dermatome. However, even when thoracic-level anesthesia is required, a lumbar approach may be chosen due to easier needle access. In such cases, the cephalad spread of anesthesia can be modulated by increasing the volume of the local anesthetic19. In contrast, in small animals such as mice, the vertebrae are highly compact, and the distance between the epidural and subarachnoid spaces is minimal12,16. Therefore, performing an epidural puncture at the lumbar level carries a high risk of accidental dural puncture, which may result in high spinal anesthesia and respiratory arrest. For this reason, we adopted a caudal epidural approach via the sacrococcygeal or coccygeal interspinous space, where the subarachnoid space is absent. Previous studies in rodents have typically used surgically implanted epidural catheters at thoracic or lumbar levels, which are technically demanding and often lack reproducibility, particularly in mice due to their small size and higher procedural risk12. In contrast, our single-puncture caudal approach is simpler, avoids dural puncture, and eliminates the need for invasive surgery, making it more accessible and consistent for experimental use.

To investigate the appropriate dosage of epidural anesthesia from the sacrococcygeal or coccygeal interspinous space, we compared three different dosages of ropivacaine: 50, 100, and 150 µL. The results showed that the range of staining and the effect of suppressing pain-like behaviors varied depending on the volume (Fig. 1). The use of a low volume (50 µL) did not cause any safety issues; however, there was a high likelihood that the area of anesthesia would be insufficient. A volume of 100 µL was generally effective in achieving analgesia that extended at least to the thoracic level, and no mortality was observed in this group. However, dye spread to the cervical level was observed in both the ropivacaine and sham groups, indicating that even this volume can result in extensive cephalad distribution depending on anatomical and procedural factors. Conversely, the use of a high volume (150 µL) was associated with increased staining up to the cervical spine and increased mortality (Table 2). To further assess whether the high mortality observed in the Epi 150 µL group was due to inadvertent intrathecal injection, histological analysis was performed in both a survivor and a non-survivor. In both cases, blue dye was observed up to the cervical spinal level; however, coronal sections of the brain revealed no staining in the ventricular space, and there was no evidence of spread into the subarachnoid space. These findings indicate that the injected solution remained within the epidural space, and that the observed mortality was unlikely to be caused by intrathecal administration (Fig. S2). Anatomically, the spinal and cranial epidural spaces are separated by the tightly fused dura mater at the foramen magnum, forming a barrier between compartments20. The absence of blue dye in the ventricular system supports the conclusion that the injected solution did not enter the subarachnoid space. Although we used a low concentration of ropivacaine, which typically does not cause respiratory depression, we cannot exclude the possibility that extensive cephalad spread, especially when combined with general anesthesia, contributed to respiratory suppression. While isoflurane alone rarely induces respiratory arrest at the concentration used21, its combination with high-volume epidural anesthesia may have had additive effects on respiratory drive. Although we did not record respiratory parameters quantitatively, a marked decrease in respiratory rate was consistently observed prior to death in the animals that died in the Epi 150 group. These findings raise the possibility that even a low concentration of ropivacaine, when administered in high volumes under general anesthesia, may compromise respiratory function in mice. We did not directly assess motor function; however, no hindlimb paralysis was observed during behavioral testing. To minimize the risk of motor blockade, which could obscure the distinction between epidural and subarachnoid effects, we used a low concentration of ropivacaine to preserve motor function while ensuring sufficient analgesia.

The low-volume group (50 µL) exhibited less reduction in pain-like behavior compared to the higher-volume groups (100 and 150 µL), indicating a weaker analgesic effect (Fig. 1). While low concentrations of ropivacaine have been reported to be safe in both veterinary and clinical settings22,23, our findings suggest that insufficient volume—even a low concentration—may lead to reduced analgesic efficacy and lower success rate of epidural administration in mice. In addition, it was not possible to completely avoid leakage of the anesthetic solution during puncture, and the success rate of epidural administration was particularly low in the 50 µL group. This relatively low success rate may reflect technical limitations such as inadequate diffusion of the anesthetic within the epidural space and potential leakage at the puncture site. Technical success was defined as the presence of visible dye within the epidural space. Although successful injections were confirmed even in the 50 µL group, the analgesic effect observed in the formalin test was limited. In contrast, the 100 µL group not only achieved a high technical success rate but also provided consistent analgesic effects without procedure-related mortality. While the 150 µL group showed broader anesthetic spread, it was associated with increased mortality. Taken together, these findings suggest that 100 µL represents the optimal volume in terms of technical feasibility, reproducibility, analgesic efficacy, and safety.

In the present study, pain-like behaviors were evaluated within 5 min after discontinuation of inhalational anesthesia. As reported by Lopes et al., inhalational anesthetics such as sevoflurane can suppress pain-like behaviors during the early phase (0–10 min) of the formalin test, whereas the late phase (15–60 min) remains largely unaffected by such agents24.

Although our study used isoflurane, Cesarovic et al. demonstrated that isoflurane and sevoflurane produce equivalent anesthesia in mice, including similar induction and recovery profiles, respiratory effects, and reflex suppression21. Given that isoflurane anesthesia in our protocol lasted only about 10 min, it is reasonable to assume that it had comparable effects on early-phase behavior. Therefore, we regarded the early phase as less reliable for evaluating the effects of epidural anesthesia due to potential residual anesthetic influences. To ensure accurate assessment of analgesic efficacy, we focused our analysis on the late phase (15–60 min), particularly Time Point 40 (30–40 min), which exhibited the clearest behavioral differences between groups. Significant reductions in pain-like behavior were observed in the Epi 100 and Epi 150 groups at this time point, suggesting a true analgesic effect of epidural anesthesia. Fig. S1 presents pain-like behavior data across all time intervals, supporting the appropriateness of using Time Point 40 as the representative measure. To the best of our knowledge, only one study in rats has reported the duration of epidural anesthesia with 2% lidocaine, which lasted an average of 57 min when administered via an indwelling catheter25. In contrast, our results showed that the duration of the anesthetic effect was approximately 40 min (Fig. 1). Given the limited literature available on the duration of epidural anesthesia in small rodents, we referred to these previous studies for contextual background25,26. However, we acknowledge that differences in species, drug selection, administration methods, and the use of additional agents in multimodal analgesia protocols limit the direct comparability of these findings with our own. Our intention was not to suggest pharmacological equivalence, but rather to emphasize that, in small animals such as mice, the expected distinctions in anesthetic duration between short- and long-acting agents may be less pronounced. This may be due to species-specific differences in drug metabolism and pharmacokinetics27. To prolong the duration of epidural anesthesia, it is likely necessary to use a long-acting local anesthetic such as ropivacaine at a high concentration and sufficient dosage. However, in small animals, the maximum allowable injection volume constrains dosage escalation. Based on our findings, we concluded that ropivacaine concentrations of 0.25% or 0.375% were optimal in this model.

Our findings provide important data for elucidating the mechanism of action of epidural anesthesia. We hope that the results of this study will be applicable to fields where clinical research is difficult in humans. Research using mouse models will contribute to research in unexplored fields, such as the mechanism of fever caused by epidural anesthesia during childbirth28, and will make it possible to manage anesthesia more safely in humans.

Considering that fever may be caused by non-specific inflammation or neuroimmune activation, this model enables the analysis of neuroinflammatory responses under controlled conditions. In particular, it is possible to evaluate the cytokine profile in response to epidural anesthesia. Furthermore, due to its compatibility with genetically modified mice, it may be possible to perform detailed analyses of specific signaling pathways and immune cell populations involved in epidural anesthesia-induced fever. Additionally, it holds potential as a platform for evaluating the efficacy of pharmacological therapies such as anti-inflammatory drugs and antipyretics. Finally, this model provides a foundation for evaluating the long-term outcomes of epidural anesthesia in small animals and establishing safety standards. These applications highlight the value of this model as a reproducible, minimally invasive technology that bridges the gap between basic research and clinical anesthesia practice.

Limitations

Although deaths occurred even with low concentrations of local anesthetics, further detailed investigations are needed, including a thorough safety evaluation. The exact cause of increased mortality in the high-volume group remains unclear; however, we cannot exclude the possibility that the use of general anesthesia with isoflurane may have contributed to respiratory suppression in combination with extensive cephalad spread of the anesthetic. Although respiratory parameters were not quantitatively recorded, a marked decrease in respiratory rate was consistently observed in the high-volume group prior to death. This suggests the potential involvement of respiratory depression. Clinical studies have shown that thoracic epidural anesthesia using high concentrations of local anesthetics increases the risk of motor block and respiratory muscle paralysis, including the diaphragm and intercostal muscles29. While our study used lower concentrations, similar mechanisms may have contributed to the observed outcomes, especially in combination with general anesthesia.

Although direct evidence in mice is limited, previous studies in humans suggest that the spread of epidural anesthesia can be affected by the rate of injection30. Therefore, a constant infusion speed was maintained to reduce potential variability. As a result, the total injection time increased with larger volumes, which may have led to differences in the degree of arousal from inhalational anesthesia at the time of formalin injection. This could have affected the pain-like behaviors observed during the early phase of the formalin test. Additionally, pain from the skin incision at the puncture site may have influenced the formalin test results; therefore, the use of analgesics was considered. However, this was not feasible because the total amount of local anesthetic used for both the epidural and local anesthesia exceeded the maximum safe dose for mice.

Moreover, since a single-shot epidural puncture model was chosen due to the technical difficulties of catheter placement and the anatomical limitations in mice, this approach may not fully replicate all clinical conditions of continuous epidural anesthesia as used in clinical practice. Importantly, this simplified model allows for a reproducible technique, which is essential for investigating the fundamental pharmacological effects and safety profiles of epidural anesthesia. Although future strategies such as using finer needles or optimizing needle tip design may help reduce leakage and improve reproducibility, the present study demonstrates the most feasible and reliable method based on our current experimental findings.

Conclusion

We demonstrated that the efficacy and dose effect of caudal epidural anesthesia in mice via the sacrococcygeal or coccygeal interspinous space. Moreover, we overcame the technical challenges of performing caudal epidural anesthesia in mice by adjusting the puncture posture using a stereotaxic apparatus. High concentrations of local anesthetics are associated with various risks; therefore, they must be diluted before administration. Our studies suggested that a volume of 100 µL is the most effective and safe. Our findings provide crucial baseline data for future studies.

Materials and methods

Animals

Twenty-five male ddY mice (40–45 g; Japan SLC, Shizuoka, Japan), aged 10–14 weeks, were used. The mice were kept in a 12-hour light/dark cycle and a temperature-controlled environment (23–25 °C). They were provided standard rodent food and water ad libitum. The mice were randomly divided into four groups. The experiment was performed according to the procedure illustrated in Fig. 4. Five minutes after the end of epidural administration, a formalin test was performed, and anesthetic spread was confirmed.

Experimental procedure flowchart. Flowchart outlining the experimental procedure for evaluating epidural administration in mice. The procedure involved administering either 0.25% ropivacaine (50 µL, 100 µL, 150 µL) or 100 µL of saline, each mixed with 10% blue ink, into the epidural space. Five min after the epidural injection, 30 µL of 2% formalin was injected into the plantar surface of the left hind paw. Pain-like behavior was observed and recorded for 60 min. After behavioral evaluation, the mice were euthanized under deep anesthesia, and the presence of the dye in the epidural space was confirmed by microscopy to assess the success of epidural administration.

Anesthesia protocol

Anesthetic agents and dosage

The epidural anesthesia was performed by administration of a 0.25% ropivacaine solution (Anapeine®, AstraZeneca, Japan; 2 mg/mL) diluted with saline containing 10% blue ink (Pilot®, Japan). The dosage was based on the standard amount of 50 µL injected at the thoracic vertebrae in mice, as reported by Z. Xu et al15. In clinical practice, thoracic epidural anesthesia in adults typically requires an initial volume of approximately 5–10 mL31. In contrast, caudal epidural anesthesia in humans requires relatively larger injection volumes, usually two to three times greater than those used for thoracic epidural anesthesia32. Furthermore, Yamamoto reported that caudal epidural administration in rats required nearly twice the volume compared with thoracic epidural injection to achieve effective blockade33. Based on these clinical and experimental data, three volumes were tested: 50, 100, and 150 µL. The volume of 150 µL corresponds to 8 mg/kg (the maximum tolerated dose for a mouse weighing 40 g)34. The sham-surgery was conducted by administration of 100 µL saline containing 10% blue ink into the epidural space.

Anesthesia administration procedure

The puncture angle and depth of the needle were based on a review of the literature on spinal anesthesia in mice and rats12. To suppress mouse movement during epidural anesthesia, general anesthesia was administered using isoflurane (induction, 4%; maintenance, 1.5%). The mice were positioned on a platform set at a head-down angle of approximately 30 degrees and then placed on the base of the stereotaxic apparatus (“Just For Mouse II” Stereotaxic Apparatus for Mouse, MUROMACHI KIKAI CO., LTD., Tokyo, Japan). The area surrounding the puncture site was shaved and disinfected. Starting from the base of the mouse tail, red marks were made at 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2 cm towards the head (Fig. 5a). A skin incision of approximately 1.5 cm was made along the midline of the spinous process to expose the subcutaneous tissue. The area from the coccygeal vertebra to the lower sacrum was identified using a mouse dissection atlas (Color Atlas of Sectional Anatomy of the Mouse; Adthree Publishing Company, Tokyo, Japan) as a reference, we identified the area from the 2nd coccygeal vertebra to the 4th sacrum. A 30-gauge needle was pushed sideways to confirm the interspinous space by palpation, and the puncture site was confirmed at the 4th sacral/1 st coccygeal vertebra (S4/Co1) or the 1 st coccygeal/2nd coccygeal vertebra (Co1/Co2). A 30-gauge needle-conjugated syringe containing the drug solution was attached to a stereotaxic injection device (Stereotaxic Injector Legato130, MUROMACHI KIKAI Co., Ltd.), and the needle was placed in contact with the puncture site. Next, the needle was advanced vertically in the absence of osseous resistance, and checked that the needle has been inserted between the interspinous space (puncturing depth of approximately 2 mm on the stereotaxic apparatus scale). when the puncture was performed to confirm that the needle had entered the interspinous space. The needle was then advanced further until it met the bony resistance on the posterior surface of the vertebral body, at which point the needle was stopped (the depth of puncture was approximately 4–4.5 mm on the stereotaxic scale), and either ropivacaine or saline was administered (Fig. 5b). To minimize the risk of death due to local anesthetic administration, the administration rate was set at 25 µL/min, and the mouse was carefully observed after each administration of 25 µL. After completion of epidural administration, the skin incision was immediately sutured with two stitches within five min, and isoflurane administration was discontinued.

Evaluation methods

Behavioral assessment of anesthesia efficacy

The effectiveness of the anesthetic was evaluated using the formalin test for pain behavior. The formalin test was performed as previously described by Hirota et al35. Five min after the completion of epidural injection, 30 µL of formalin solution diluted to 2% concentration with saline using a 27G needle was injected into the left plantar surface of the mouse. In the formalin test, a biphasic pain response is known to occur; the first phase (acute pain due to chemical stimulation) occurs immediately after injection, and the second phase (inflammatory pain) is observed 20 min or later. In the present study, we observed pain behaviors in both phases. Pain behaviors (licking, biting, and flinching) were observed and recorded for 60 min. The duration of pain behavior was measured every 5 min, and the average value for each 10-min period was calculated. The results of the formalin test were analyzed using only animals in the epidural anesthesia groups for which successful epidural administration was confirmed by staining in the epidural space. All animals in the sham group were included in the analysis.

Verification of anesthetic spread

After behavioral assessment, the mice were euthanized under deep anesthesia and perfused with Carnovsky’s fixative (2% PFA + 2.5% GA in 0.1MPB). The extent of staining in the epidural space was confirmed under a microscope. Successful puncture was defined as visible staining in the epidural space, whereas unsuccessful puncture was defined as the absence of such staining. The target sample size for the epidural anesthesia group was set at 5 or more mice for each group of successful punctures.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using nonparametric methods. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to evaluate differences among groups, with the significance level set at *p < 0.05. Post-hoc comparisons were performed using the Steel’s test to account for multiple comparisons. Data are expressed as mean ± standard error (SEM). All analyses were performed using the JMP Pro 17 software (JMP Statistical Discovery LLC, Cary, NC, USA).
