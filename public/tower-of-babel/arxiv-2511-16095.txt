1.
Introduction

Let
X
be a metric space, and let
F
be either of the (semi) groups
{\mathbb{R}}
or
{\mathbb{Z}}
or
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
or
{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
represented as a family of self-maps of
X
. We will write
F=\{g_{t}\}
. For
g\in F
we will denote its action on
X
by

(g,x)\mapsto gx
.
Now fix a subset
Y\subset X
and define the set of points of
X

approaching

Y
by
F
as

A(F,Y):=\big\{x\in X:\exists\text{ an unbounded sequence }(g_{t_{k}})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset F\text{ with }{\lim_{k\to\infty}g_{t_{k}}x\in Y}\big\}.

Its complement, the set of points of
X

escaping

Y
by
F
will be denoted by
E(F,{Y})
, namely

E(F,{Y}):=\bigcap_{y\in Y}\big\{x\in X:y\notin\overline{(F\smallsetminus F_{0})x}\text{ for some bounded }F_{0}\subset F\big\}.

If
X
is not bounded, we make the convention that
x\in A(F,\infty)
when the orbit
Fx
is unbounded.

Take
y\in Y
and consider
A(F,y):=A(F,\{y\})
and
E(F,y):=E(F,\{y\})
. A natural question one could ask is how large the sets
A(F,y)
and
E(F,y)
can be. If the action admits an ergodic invariant probability measure, or has some hyperbolic behavior, some instances of this question can be answered. Namely, under the assumption of existence of an ergodic measure
\mu
of full support the set
A(F,y)
has full measure for any
y\in X\cup\{\infty\}
. Additionally, for many hyperbolic systems one can prove that the
\mu
-null sets
E(F,y)
are
thick
, that is, their intersection with any nonempty open set has full Hausdorff dimension. For results of this kind see e.g.
[
D2
]
for toral endomorphisms,
[
U
,
Do
]
for Anosov flows, and
[
D1
,
KMa
,
Kl1
,
AGK
]
for partially hyperbolic flows on homogeneous space.
Furthermore, in many cases these sets can be proved to be winning in the sense of Schmidt games (see e.g.
[
AGK
,
BFK
,
D1
,
D2
,
Ts1
,
Ts2
,
Wu1
,
Wu2
]
).
Consequently in these cases it follows that both
A(F,y)
and
E(F,y)
are thick.
Moreover, both full-measure and winning conditions are stable with respect to countable intersections; thus
for any choice of countably many semigroups
F_{i}
and points
y_{i}\in X\cup\{\infty\}
for which the above conclusions can be established, it holds that both
\bigcap_{i}A(F_{i},y_{i})
and
\bigcap_{i}E(F_{i},y_{i})
are thick.

This paper addresses the following question: what if one considers
a mixed case, that is, investigate the intersection

(1.1)

{A(F,y)\cap E(F,z),}

where
y,z\in X\cup\{\infty\}
? Problems of this type are amenable neither to the full-measure argument, nor to the technique based on Schmidt games. Also it is clear that some conditions on
y,z
should be imposed; for example if
y=z
it is clear that the intersection (
1.1
) is empty.

In what follows we restrict our attention to
F\cong{\mathbb{R}}
, also denoting
F_{+}:=\{g_{t}:t\geq 0\}
.
Assume that the
F
-action on
X
is continuous. Then let us record a natural
obstruction to
the set

(1.2)

{A(F_{+},y)\cap E(F_{+},z)}

being non-empty:

Lemma 1.1
.

If
y\in X
and
z\in X\cup\{\infty\}
are such that
y\in A(F,z)
(that is,
z
can be approached by the trajectory of
y
either in the positive or in the negative time direction), then

A(F_{+},y)\subset A(F_{+},z).

Proof.

Let
x\in A(F_{+},y)
, and let
(s_{\ell})_{\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be an unbounded sequence in
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
with

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}g_{s_{\ell}}x=y.

Let
W\subset X
be an open neighborhood of
z
. In the case, when
z=\infty
, we take
W
to be an open set with bounded complement.

By the hypothesis, there exists a time
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
for which
g_{t}y\in W
. By the continuity of the action we have

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}g_{s_{\ell}}g_{t}x=g_{t}y\in W.

Note that the sequence
(s_{\ell}+t)_{\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is unbounded and eventually positive. Since
W
was arbitrary, we conclude that
x\in A(F_{+},z)
.
∎

In view of the above lemma it makes sense to look for conditions guaranteeing that the set (
1.2
) is reasonably large
under the assumption that
y\in E(F,z)
.
In the present paper we will do it in the following set-up: we let
X=G/\Gamma
, where
G
is a real Lie group
and
\Gamma\subset G
is a lattice,
and consider the
{\mathbb{R}}
-action on
X
via the left multiplication by a one-parameter subgroup of
G
:

(1.3)

\big\{g_{t}:=\exp(ta_{0})\in G:t\in{\mathbb{R}}\big\},

where
{a_{0}}
is an element of
{\mathfrak{g}}:=\operatorname{Lie}(G)
, the Lie algebra of
G
.
We shall fix once and for all an inner product on
{\mathfrak{g}}
to induce a right-invariant Riemannian metric
\operatorname{dist}_{G}
on
G
.
Note that the invariance of the metric implies that it is geodesically complete, and so the Hopf–Rinow theorem implies that it is proper (closed and bounded sets are compact). Then one obtains a metric on
X=G/\Gamma
by setting

\operatorname{dist}_{X}(g_{1}\Gamma,g_{2}\Gamma):=\inf\big\{\operatorname{dist}_{G}(g_{1}\gamma,g_{2}):\gamma\in\Gamma\big\}.

This is the set-up in which we will attempt to study sets of the form (
1.2
). We will denote by
m_{X}
the
G
-invariant Haar probability measure on
X
, and by
m_{G}
the Haar measure on
G
scaled so that the measure of any fundamental domain for
\Gamma
is equal to
1
. Further,
in this paper we will assume that
X
is noncompact and
z=\infty
; that is, we will seek to construct bounded
F^{+}
-trajectories with prescribed limit points.

Recently the second- and third-named authors proved the following result. Take
G=\operatorname{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{R}})
,
\Gamma=\operatorname{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
and
X=G/\Gamma
. For
t,\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
consider

g_{t}:=\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}e^{t}&0\\
0&e^{-t}\end{array}}\right]\in G,\ F=\{g_{t}:t\in{\mathbb{R}}\},

and

{x_{\alpha}:=\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\alpha\\
0&1\end{array}}\right]\Gamma\in X.}{}

Theorem 1.2
.

[
KR2
, Theorem 1.3]

Let
X
and
F
be as above, and let
y\in E(F,\infty)
; then

(1.4)

\dim\big(\{\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}:x_{\alpha}\in A(F_{+},y)\cap E(F_{+},\infty)\}\big)=1.

Here and hereafter "dim" stands for Hausdorff dimension.
In fact, even though it was not mentioned in
[
KR2
]
, the proof actually demonstrates the thickness of the set in the left-hand side of (
1.4
). Also, by the standard "slicing" argument (see §
2
), it can be easily derived from Theorem
1.2

that for any
y\in E(F,\infty)
the set
A(F_{+},y)\cap E(F_{+},\infty)
is thick.
The proof of the above theorem used the technique of continued fractions, and in fact the result can be interpreted within the framework of Diophantine approximation. Namely it constructs numbers that are badly approximable but not
\nu
-Dirichlet improvable, where
\nu
is an irreducible norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. See
[
KR1
,
KR2
,
KRS
,
AD
]
for more detail, and
[
MS
]
for some new results in that direction.

One of the main results of this paper is a generalization of Theorem
1.2
to a wider class of flows on homogeneous spaces.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
X=G/\Gamma
and
F=\{g_{t}:t\in{\mathbb{R}}\}\subset G
, where

•

G
is a Lie group with discrete center,

•

\Gamma
is a non-uniform
lattice in
G
,

•

g_{t}
is as in (
1.3
), where
a_{0}\in{\mathfrak{g}}\smallsetminus\{0\}
is
{\operatorname{ad}}
-diagonalizable over
{\mathbb{C}}
;

•

the action of
F
on
(X,m_{X})
is mixing.

Let
y\in E(F,\infty)
. Then for any non-empty open
U\subset X
we have

(1.5)

\dim\big(U\cap A(F_{+},y)\cap E(F_{+},\infty)\big)\geq\dim X-\dim Z+1,

where
Z\subset G
is the neutral subgroup of
G
with respect to
F
.

See Definition
2.2
below for a precise definition of
Z
.
In view of Moore’s Theorem
[
Mo
]
, the mixing assumption is satisfied when
G
is a connected semi-simple
Lie group
with finite center and with no compact factors, and
\Gamma\subset G
is irreducible. However mixing is not restricted to the semi-simple case, see e.g.
[
BM
,
Kl2
]
.
Note that when
G=\operatorname{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{R}})
(that is, under the assumptions of Theorem
1.2
) we know that
\dim Z=\dim F=1
, and thus the set
A(F_{+},y)\cap E(F_{+},\infty)
is thick. Moreover, in the latter case one can generalize Theorem
1.2
as follows.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
G
,
\Gamma
and
F
be as in Theorem
1.3
, and assume in addition that
\dim Z=1
.
Then for any compact
F-invariant
subset
B
of
E(F,\infty)
there exists a thick set of
x\in E(F_{+},\infty)
such that the closure of the (bounded) trajectory
F_{+}x
contains
B
.

In addition to
G=\operatorname{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{R}})
, the assumptions of the above theorem hold when
G=\operatorname{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{R}})\ltimes{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
; the choice
\Gamma=\operatorname{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})\ltimes{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
yields the space of two-dimensional unimodular grids, cf.
[
Kl2
]
.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
G
,
\Gamma
,
X
and
F
be as in Theorem
1.4
; then for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a thick set of
x\in X
such that the orbit closure
\overline{F^{+}x}
is compact and has Hausdorff dimension greater than
\dim X-\varepsilon
.

Proof.

When
X
and
F
are as in Theorem
1.3
, it follows from
[
KMa
, Theorem 1.1]
that, given
\varepsilon>0
, one can find a large enough compact set
K\subset X
such that the compact
F
-invariant set

B=\{x\in X:Fx\subset K\}

has Hausdorff dimension greater than
\dim X-\varepsilon
.
Under the additional hypothesis of
\dim Z=1
, an application of Theorem
1.4
to

then gives the result.
∎

2.
Reduction to the expanding subgroup

In this and the remaining part of the paper we let
G
,
\Gamma
and
F
be as in Theorem
1.3
. Recall that
F=\{g_{t}=\exp(ta_{0}):t\in{\mathbb{R}}\}
, where

{a_{0}}\in{\mathfrak{g}}\smallsetminus\{0\}
is
{\operatorname{ad}}
-diagonalizable over
\mathbb{C}
.
Define

\begin{split}{\mathfrak{h}}&:=\left\{b\in{\mathfrak{g}}:\lim_{t\to-\infty}\operatorname{Ad}(g_{t})b=0\right\}\\
{\mathfrak{n}}&:=\left\{b\in{\mathfrak{g}}:\sup_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}\|\operatorname{Ad}(g_{t})b\|<\infty\right\}\\
{\mathfrak{h}}^{-}&:=\left\{b\in{\mathfrak{g}}:\lim_{t\to\infty}\operatorname{Ad}(g_{t})b=0\right\}.\end{split}

From the diagonalizability assumption, we have

(2.1)

{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{h}}\oplus{\mathfrak{n}}\oplus{\mathfrak{h}}^{-}.

In fact, if one considers the subalgebras of
{\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathbb{C}}
which are direct sums of eigenspaces for
{\operatorname{ad}}_{a_{0}}\otimes 1
corresponding to eigenvalues with positive, zero and negative real part respectively, then one obtains the above algebra decomposition (
2.1
) simply by intersecting with
{\mathfrak{g}}
.

Remark 2.1
.

Note that
{\mathfrak{n}}
is a proper subalgebra of
{\mathfrak{g}}
, otherwise
\operatorname{Ad}(g_{t})
would be a compact group and the mixing hypothesis would fail.
Moreover, since
G
admits a lattice,
the modular function of
G
is constant. Thus
\operatorname{{\operatorname{ad}}_{a_{0}}}
has trace
0
, and, therefore, both
{\mathfrak{h}}
and
{\mathfrak{h}}^{-}
are nonzero.

Definition 2.2
.

[Expanding, contracting and neutral subgroups]
We let

,

and

be the connected Lie subgroups of

associated to the algebras
{\mathfrak{h}}
,
{\mathfrak{h}}^{-}
and

, a Haar measure on

. We further consider the connected subgroup

associated to the Lie algebra

and a left Haar measure

on

, scaled so that

is a direct product of

and

for justification.

By ordering the eigenspaces of

with respect to their real parts, we see that

is nilpotent and hence unimodular. Thus,

is a bi-invariant Haar measure on

The inner product on

,

(Conjugation by

)

(Strongly tree-like collections and Cantor sets)

•

4.
The generic and special steps of the induction

The desired orbits in Theorem

are constructed inductively. We start with a base point

and a tessellation domain

In particular, we have

(4.12)

g_{t}\Phi_{-t}(h_{2}^{-1})h_{1}x=h^{-}zg_{-s_{1}}y.

The stipulation in (
4.7
) ensures that we can write

(4.13)

\Phi_{-t}(h_{2}^{-1})h_{1}x=h_{3}x\text{ where }h_{3}\in V_{3r/4}.

Combining (
4.12
) and (
4.13
), we have

(4.14)

g_{t}h_{3}x=h^{-}zg_{-s_{1}}y.

Choose and fix
\gamma\in\Lambda
with
\Phi_{s_{\varepsilon}}(h_{3})\in\overline{V}\gamma
.
We write

(4.15)

h_{3}=\Phi_{-s_{\varepsilon}}(h_{4}\gamma)\text{ for some }h_{4}\in\overline{V},

which gives

W:=\Phi_{-s_{\varepsilon}}(V\gamma)=\Phi_{-s_{\varepsilon}}(Vh_{4}^{-1})h_{3}.

Since
h_{3}\in V_{3/4}
and since we have the containment (
4.9
), we see that

W\subset V
.
This proves Assertion (i) of the Proposition.
We now compute, for
t\leq s\leq s_{\varepsilon}
,

(4.16)

\begin{split}g_{s}Wx&=g_{s}\Phi_{-s_{\varepsilon}}(V\gamma)x\\
&\underset{\eqref{eq: h3 as v3}}{\subset}g_{s}\Phi_{-s_{\varepsilon}}\big(V(\overline{V})^{-1}\big)h_{3}x=\Phi_{s-s_{\varepsilon}}\big(V(\overline{V})^{-1}\big)g_{s}h_{3}x\\
&\underset{\eqref{eq: h3 hits the spot}}{=}\Phi_{s-s_{\varepsilon}}\big(V(\overline{V})^{-1}\big)g_{s-t}{h^{-}}zg_{-s_{1}}y\\
&=\Phi_{s-s_{\varepsilon}}\big(V(\overline{V})^{-1}\big)\Phi_{s-t}({h^{-}})\Phi_{s-t}(z)g_{s-t-s_{1}}y\\
&\underset{\eqref{eq: written as product}}{\subset}\Phi_{s-s_{\varepsilon}}\big(V(\overline{V})^{-1}\big)\Phi_{s-t}\big(B_{H^{-}}(\sigma)\big)\Phi_{s-t}\left(B_{Z}(\sigma)\right)Fy\\
&\subset B_{G}(1/3+1/3+1/3)Fy\subset K,\end{split}

where we have used the stipulations on
\delta
and (
4.5
) for the last two containments.
This proves Assertion (ii).

Finally we compute, as in (
4.16
),

\begin{split}g_{{s^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}}}Wx\underset{\eqref{eq: h3 as v3}}{\subset}&\Phi_{s^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}-s_{\varepsilon}}\big(V(\overline{V})^{-1}\big)g_{s_{1}+t}h_{3}x\underset{\eqref{eq: h3 hits the spot}}{=}\Phi_{{s^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}}-s_{\varepsilon}}\big(V(\overline{V})^{-1}\big)\Phi_{s_{1}}(h^{-})zy\\
\subset\ \ &B_{H}(\varepsilon/2)B_{H^{-}}(\varepsilon/2)B_{Z}(\sigma)y,\end{split}

where (
4.10
) and (
4.8
) are used to justify the last containment. This proves Assertion (iii) and finishes the proof of the proposition.
∎

5.
Proof of Theorem
2.5

In this section our goal is to prove Theorem
2.5
, that is, for given
x_{0}\in X
, construct many points of the form
hx_{0}
such that their
F_{+}
-orbits are bounded, and, in addition, a given countable subset of
X
is in their orbit closure. Our proof is a modification of a well-known scheme of constructing points with bounded orbits due to the second-named author and Margulis
[
KMa
]
, see also
[
Kl1
,
Kl2
]
. Before dealing with the general case it will be instructive to briefly describe this construction in a special case.

Proof of Theorem
2.5
in the special case
B=\varnothing
.

Take
x_{0}\in X
and a nonempty open subset

U
of
H
. We need to prove that the Hausdorff dimension of the set

(5.1)

{\{h\in U:F_{+}hx_{0}\text{ is bounded}\}}

is equal to
\dim H
. Replacing
x_{0}
by
hx_{0}
for some
h\in U
we can assume that

U
is a neighborhood of identity in
H
.

Pick a compact set

K\subset X
with
m_{X}(\partial K)=0
containing
x_{0}
, and let

(5.2)

{\eta:=m_{X}(K)-m_{X}(K)^{2}.}

Then take
\delta>0
furnished by Proposition
4.1
and choose
0<r<\delta
such that
V_{r}\subset U
. By shrinking
r
further we can assume that

(5.3)

{\operatorname{diam}_{H}(V_{r})<1/2.}

As before we denote
V=V_{r}
and let
\Lambda=\Lambda_{r}
be a corresponding discrete subset of
H
. The proposition gives us the value
t_{0}>0
such that (
4.1
) holds for all
x\in K
and
t\geq t_{0}
. Fix
t>t_{0}
and let
\Lambda(x)
denote the set appearing in the left hand side of (
4.1
), i.e.

(5.4)

{\Lambda(x):=\left\{\gamma\in\Lambda:V\gamma\subset\Phi_{t}(V)\text{ and }V\gamma g_{t}x\subset K\right\}.}

To estimate the Hausdorff dimension of the set (
5.1
) from below, for each
x\in K
we are going to construct a strongly
tree-like collection
\mathcal{E}
of subsets of
H
such that its limit set

\mathcal{E}_{\infty}:=\bigcap_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\cup\mathcal{E}_{k}

is contained in the set (
5.1
). We first let
\mathcal{E}_{0}:=\{\overline{V}\}
. Then define

\mathcal{E}_{1}:=\left\{\Phi_{-t}(\overline{V}\gamma):\gamma\in\Lambda(x_{0})\right\}.

That is, elements of
\mathcal{E}_{1}
are of the form
E=\Phi_{-t}(\overline{V})h
for some
h\in H
, chosen so that
E\subset\overline{V}
and
g_{t}Ex_{0}\subset K
.
Similarly, elements of
\mathcal{E}_{k}
will be of the form

E=\Phi_{-kt}(\overline{V})h\subset\overline{V}\text{ for some }h\in H,

and it will follow from our inductive construction that

(5.5)

{E\in\mathcal{E}_{k}\Longrightarrow g_{it}Ex_{0}\subset K\text{ for }i=1,\dots,k.}

Namely,
if
\mathcal{E}_{k}
is chosen
and we take
E\in\mathcal{E}_{k}
such that

E=\Phi_{-kt}(\overline{V})h\subset\overline{V}\text{ and }g_{it}Ex_{0}\subset K\text{ for }i=1,\dots,k,

define the subcollection

(5.6)

{\mathcal{E}_{k+1}(E):=\left\{\Phi_{-(k+1)t}(\overline{V}\gamma):\gamma\in\Lambda({g_{kt}hx_{0}})\right\},}

and let
\mathcal{E}_{k+1}:=\cup_{E\in\mathcal{E}_{k}}\mathcal{E}_{k+1}(E)
. Then
\mathcal{E}_{k+1}(E)
consists precisely of elements of
\mathcal{E}_{k+1}
contained in
E
(i.e. "children" of
E
), and it follows from the definition of
\Lambda({g_{kt}hx_{0}})
that
g_{(k+1)t}Ex_{0}\subset K
for any
E\in\mathcal{E}_{k+1}
.

The first five parts of Definition
3.7
follow readily from the construction and
from
(V,\Lambda)
being a tessellation of
H
.
Also, if we define

\lambda_{\text{min}}:=\min\{\Re\lambda:\lambda\text{ is an eigenvalue for }{\operatorname{ad}}_{a_{0}}\otimes 1\text{ with }\Re\lambda>0\},

then a computation using the exponential map shows that for all
t
sufficiently large,

(5.7)

\operatorname{dist}_{H}\big(\Phi_{-t}(h),e_{G}\big)\leq e^{-\frac{\lambda_{\text{min}}t}{2}}\operatorname{dist}_{H}(h,e_{G})\text{ for all }h\in V.

Note that the factor
1/2
in the exponent is used to get rid of the constants that occur when comparing distances on
H
with norms in
{\mathfrak{g}}
. By increasing
t
we can assume that (
5.7
) is satisfied.
From (
5.6
) and the right-invariance of the metric we can see that for any
x\in K
the diameter of any
E\in\mathcal{E}_{k}
is equal to

\operatorname{diam}_{H}\big(\Phi_{-kt}(V)\big)
. Thus it follows from (
5.3
) and (
5.7
) that

(5.8)

d_{k}=\sup\{\operatorname{diam}_{H}(E):E\in\mathcal{E}_{k}\}\leq e^{-\frac{\lambda_{\operatorname{min}}kt}{2}}.

Hence (
3.4
) holds, and
E
is a strongly tree-like collection. Clearly (
5.5
) implies that
g_{kt}E_{\infty}x_{0}\subset K
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, hence

F_{+}E_{\infty}x_{0}
is contained in
g_{[-t,0]}K
.

It remains to estimate the Hausdorff dimension of
E_{\infty}
from below.
Using (
5.6
) one can easily see that for any
k=0,1,\dots
and
for
E\in\mathcal{E}_{k}
of the form
E=\Phi_{-kt}(\overline{V})h
,

\operatorname{density}(\mathcal{E}_{k+1},E)=\frac{m_{H}\big(\cup\mathcal{E}_{k+1}(E)\big)}{m_{H}(E)}\geq e^{-\chi t}\#\Lambda({g_{kt}hx_{0}}).

Thus Proposition
4.1
and (
5.2
) imply that for any
k=0,1,\dots
one has

(5.9)

\Delta_{k}=\inf\left\{\operatorname{density}(\mathcal{E}_{{k+1}},E):E\in\mathcal{E}_{k}\right\}\geq m_{X}(K)-\eta=m_{X}(K)^{2},

and an application of Theorem
3.8
yields

\displaystyle\dim H-\dim{E}_{\infty}\leq

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{k}\log\Delta_{j}}{\log d_{k}}

\displaystyle\underset{\text{\rm by }\eqref{eq: dm estimate generic}\text{\rm\ and }\eqref{eq: densitybound}}{\leq}

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{(k+1)\log\big(\frac{1}{m_{X}(K)^{2}}\big)}{\frac{1}{2}{\lambda_{\operatorname{min}}kt}}=\frac{4\log\big(\frac{1}{m_{X}(K)}\big)}{{\lambda_{\operatorname{min}}t}}.

The right hand side of the above inequality can be made arbitrary small by increasing

, which implies that the Hausdorff dimension of the set (
5.1
) is equal to
\dim H
.
∎

Now let us show how the above scheme can be modified to produce a proof of the general case of Theorem
2.5
. The idea is
to perform lots of generic steps as before, but occasionally insert approach steps.

Proof of Theorem
2.5
when
B\neq\varnothing
.

Fix a surjection

\beta:{\mathbb{N}}\to B

such that
\beta^{-1}(y)
is infinite for every
y\in B
,
and a sequence of positive real numbers
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converging to zero.

Take a base-point
x_{0}\in X
. As before, it suffices to choose a neighborhood
U
of the identity in
H
and show that the set of
h\in U
such that
hx_{0}
lies in

(5.10)

E(F_{+},\infty)\cap\bigcap_{y\in B}A(F_{+},B_{Z}(\sigma_{0})y)

has full dimension in
H
.
Choose a compact set
K
with

x_{0}\in\text{int}(K),\ m_{X}(\partial K)=0,\ FB\subset K\text{ and }\operatorname{dist}_{X}(FB,\partial K)>1.

Apply Proposition
4.1
with this
K
and
\eta=m_{X}(K)-m_{X}(K)^{2}
.
This gives us a
\delta_{1}>0
. Now we may further apply Proposition
4.2
to
K
to obtain another
\delta_{2}>0
.
We fix

\sigma<\sigma_{0}/2\ \text{ and }\ r<\min\{\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\operatorname{dist}_{X}(x_{0},\partial K)\}\text{ such that \eqref{eq:diamV} holds},

and obtain a tessellation domain
(V_{r},\Lambda_{r})
, which we write as
(V,\Lambda)
, and
t_{0}>0
such that the conclusions of Propositions
4.1
and
4.2
hold.
Fix
t>t_{0}
, and, as in the proof of the special case, by increasing
t
if needed assume that (
5.7
) is satisfied.
This way:

•

for any
x\in K
we can define
\Lambda(x)\subset\Lambda
as in (
5.4
) with
\#\Lambda(x)\geq e^{\chi t}m_{X}(K)^{2}
;

•

for any
\varepsilon>0
one can choose
s_{\varepsilon}>s^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}>t
such that any
x\in K
and
y\in B
come with
\gamma=\gamma(x,y,\varepsilon)\in\Lambda
satisfying conditions (i)–(iii) of Propositions
4.2
.

We now formalize the process of alternating generic and approach steps.
Namely fix an injection
f:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
to be determined later; our strategy will consist of performing approach steps whenever
k
is of the form
f(n)
, and generic steps otherwise. Specifically, for each
x\in K
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
we define a subset
\Lambda(x,k)
of
\Lambda
by

(5.11)

\Lambda(x,k):=\begin{cases}\Lambda(x)\text{ as in \eqref{eq:lambdax} (from applying Proposition \ref{prop: generic})}&\text{ if }\ k\notin f({\mathbb{N}});\\
\left\{\gamma\big(x,\beta(n),\varepsilon_{n}\big)\right\}\text{ (from applying Proposition \ref{prop: approach})}&\text{ if }\ k=f(n).\end{cases}

We also define a sequence of times
(t_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
by

t_{k}:=\begin{cases}t&\text{if }k\notin f({\mathbb{N}}),\\
s_{\varepsilon_{n}}&\text{if }k=f(n),\end{cases}

and set
T_{0}=0
and
T_{k}:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}\text{ for each }k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, or, equivalently,

(5.12)

T_{k}=\big(k-\#f^{-1}(\{1,\dots,k\})\big)t+\sum_{f(n)\leq k}s_{\varepsilon_{n}}=kt+\sum_{f(n)\leq k}(s_{\varepsilon_{n}}-t).

We now come to the inductive construction. Define

\mathcal{E}_{0}:=\{\overline{V}\},\ p_{0}:\mathcal{E}_{0}\to H;\ \overline{V}\mapsto e_{H}.

Assume we have defined
\mathcal{E}_{j}
and
p_{j}:\mathcal{E}_{j}\to H
for
j=0,1,\dots,k
with the properties that

(a)

Each

is of the form

\Phi_{-T_{j}}(\overline{V})p_{j}(E)\ \text{ with }\ g_{T_{j}}p_{j}(E)x_{0}\in K.

(b)

If

, then

(c)

For any
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
with

for some

such that, for all

We then proceed to define, for each

, the collection of children
\mathcal{E}_{k+1}(E)
as

(5.13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k+1}(E):=

\displaystyle\text{ the set of all }E^{\prime}=\Phi_{-T_{k+1}}(\overline{V}\gamma)p_{k}(E),

\displaystyle\text{ where }\gamma\in\Lambda(g_{T_{k}}p_{k}(E)x_{0},k+1).

We further define, for each

as above,

p_{k+1}(E^{\prime})=\Phi_{-T_{k+1}}(\gamma)p_{k}(E).

Setting

\mathcal{E}_{k+1}:=\bigcup_{E\in\mathcal{E}_{k}}\mathcal{E}_{k}(E)
,

E^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}_{k+1}(E)

\Phi_{-T_{k+1}}(\overline{V})p_{k+1}(E^{\prime})
, and we must show that

. We compute

for all

g_{T_{k}+s}E^{\prime}x_{0}=g_{T_{k}+s}\Phi_{-T_{k+1}}(\overline{V}\gamma)p_{k}(E)x_{0}=g_{s}\Phi_{-t_{k+1}}(\overline{V}\gamma)g_{T_{k}}p_{k}(E)x_{0}.

Now if

, we know that

, and the conclusion follows from Proposition

. Otherwise, in view of (

), (

) and Proposition

, for some

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}_{k+1}(E)=\{{E^{\prime}}\},\ t_{k+1}=s_{\varepsilon_{n}}&,\text{ and }\\

\text{ where }W=\Phi_{-s_{\varepsilon_{n}}}(V\gamma)\text{ as in Pro}&\text{position \ref{prop: approach}(i)}.\end{split}

Hence the conclusion follows from Proposition
4.2
(ii).
Finally, to demonstrate (c) it suffices to only consider the approach step case.
Then one can use (
5.14
) and Proposition
4.2
(iii) to conclude that

g_{T_{k}+s^{\prime}_{\varepsilon_{n}}}{E^{\prime}}x_{0}=g_{s^{\prime}_{\varepsilon_{n}}}\overline{W}g_{T_{k}}p_{k}(E)x_{0}

lies in the
\varepsilon_{n}
-neighborhood of

.

\mathcal{E}_{k}
satisfies the
requirements of Definition

; in particular, in view of (

) and (

),

) contains

.

It thus remains to estimate the Hausdorff dimension of

. Clearly

\Delta_{k}=\inf\{\operatorname{density}(\mathcal{E}_{k+1},E):E\in\mathcal{E}_{k}\}

depends on whether

corresponds to a generic or a special step. In the former case estimate (

) still holds. Otherwise

for some

,

is a singleton for every

, and

. Hence, analogously to (

), we can write

(5.16)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k}\log\Delta_{j}

\displaystyle\geq\big(k+1-\#f^{-1}(\{1,\dots,k+1\})\big)\log\big(m_{X}(K)^{2}\big)-\chi\sum_{f(n)\leq k+1}s_{\varepsilon_{n}}

\displaystyle=2(k+1)\log m_{X}(K)-\sum_{f(n)\leq k+1}\big(\chi s_{\varepsilon_{n}}+2\log m_{X}(K)\big).

5.12
), (
5.15
) and (
5.16

to obtain

\displaystyle\dim H-\dim{E}_{\infty}\leq

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{k}\log\Delta_{j}}{\log d_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{2(k+1)\log m_{X}(K)-\sum_{f(n)\leq k+1}\big(\chi s_{\varepsilon_{n}}+2\log m_{X}(K)\big)}{-\frac{1}{2}{\lambda_{\operatorname{min}}\big(kt+\sum_{f(n)\leq k}(s_{\varepsilon_{n}}-t)\big)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{2(k+1)\log\frac{1}{m_{X}(K)}+\sum_{f(n)\leq k+1}\big(\chi s_{\varepsilon_{n}}-2\log\frac{1}{m_{X}(K)}\big)}{\frac{1}{2}{\lambda_{\operatorname{min}}\big(kt+\sum_{f(n)\leq k}(s_{\varepsilon_{n}}-t)\big)}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{4\log\frac{1}{m_{X}(K)}}{\lambda_{\operatorname{min}}t}+\frac{2\chi}{\lambda_{\operatorname{min}}t}\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{f(n)\leq k+1}s_{\varepsilon_{n}}}{k}.

But since the sequence

can be fixed after the choice of the values

is made, it simply remains to choose

sparse enough so that

grows sublinearly in

. Hence, as in the proof for

\dim{E}_{\infty}\geq\dim H-\frac{4\log\frac{1}{m_{X}(K)}}{\lambda_{\operatorname{min}}t},

and letting

we conclude that the set (
5.10
) is thick.
∎
