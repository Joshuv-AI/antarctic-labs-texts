Adaptive Proof Refinement with LLM-Guided Strategy Selection
Minghai Lu, Zhe Zhou, Danning Xie, Songlin Jia, Benjamin Delaware, Tianyi Zhang

Abstract
. Formal verification via theorem proving enables the expressive specification and rigorous proof of software correctness, but it is difficult to scale due to the significant manual effort and expertise required. While Large Language Models (LLMs) show potential in proof generation, they frequently produce incorrect proofs on the first attempt and require additional strategies for iterative refinement. However, existing approaches employ fixed refinement strategies and cannot dynamically choose an effective strategy based on the particular issues in a generated proof, which limits their performance. To overcome this limitation, we introduce Adapt , a novel proof refinement framework that leverages an LLM-guided decision-maker to dynamically select a suitable refinement strategy according to the state of the proof assistant and available context of an incorrect proof. We evaluate Adapt on two benchmarks against four existing methods and find that it significantly outperforms the best baseline on both by proving 16.63% and 18.58% more theorems, respectively. Furthermore, we demonstrate Adapt ’s generalizability by evaluating it across five different LLMs. We also conduct ablation studies to measure the contribution of each component and compare the trade-offs of alternative decision-maker designs.

Introduction

Interactive theorem provers (ITPs) such as Coq
(
The Coq Development Team 2024
)
and Lean
(
de Moura et al. 2015
)
are powerful tools for formally verifying complex software systems. They have been successfully applied in critical domains, including certified compilers
(
Kästner et al. 2018
)
, distributed systems
(
Wilcox et al. 2015
)
, and OS kernels
(
Klein et al. 2009
)
. However, these successes require significant human effort and expertise, as developers must interactively guide the proof assistant step-by-step. For example, CompCert, a verified C compiler, required 6 person-years and 100,000 lines of proof scripts
(
Kästner et al. 2018
)
.

Recently, there has been increasing interest in leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs) to automatically generate formal proofs
(
Thompson et al. 2024
;
First et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Cao et al. 2025
;
Liu et al. 2025
)
. While LLMs have demonstrated promising performance on some theorem proving benchmarks, they are far from perfect: even the best-performing tool can prove only 40.4% of theorems in the widely used CoqGym benchmark
(
Lu et al. 2024
)
. Generating a correct proof in a single attempt is difficult. In practice, human developers rely on feedback from the ITP to refine their initial proofs. Similarly, many automated approaches adopt an iterative proof refinement mechanism that leverages feedback from the ITP to continuously refine an initial proof attempt
(
Lu et al. 2024
;
First et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2025
;
An et al. 2024
)
. However, these methods typically adopt a single, fixed refinement strategy to handle all types of errors, such as proposing a helper lemma
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
or calling an external tool
(
Lu et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2025
)
. This one-size-fits-all approach is suboptimal because correcting erroneous proofs often requires different refinement strategies depending on the specific type of error, and complex errors may even necessitate a combination of multiple strategies. Consequently, even the state-of-the-art iterative refinement method can only prove 32.0% of theorems in the
CoqStoq
benchmark
(
Thompson et al. 2024
)
.

In contrast, a key factor in the success of human developers in theorem proving is their ability to select the most appropriate refinement strategy for a given proof state
(
Shi et al. 2025
)
. While interactively developing a proof, developers adopt various strategies based on the current state of the proof assistant and available context (e.g., proven theorems and lemmas)
(
Shi et al. 2025
;
Mehta 2008
)
. For example, they might introduce new helper lemmas when existing ones are insufficient, draw inspiration from similar proofs and reuse relevant lemmas, or iteratively repair proofs based on ITP feedback. This strategic decision-making is critical, as a suboptimal choice can increase effort or lead to outright proof failure.

.

.

Inspired by this observation, we propose
Adapt
, an adaptive proof refinement framework. In contrast to prior work that employs a single, fixed refinement strategy,
Adapt
dynamically selects an appropriate strategy based on the current state of the proof assistant and available context. As illustrated in Figure
1
, when a proof attempt fails, an LLM-based decision-maker employs one of three strategies: (1) proposing new helper lemmas, (2) searching for relevant lemmas and proofs to guide the refinement, or (3) regenerating the proof. This iterative process allows
Adapt
to flexibly adapt its approach at each step, enabling it to effectively prove complex theorems.

Our evaluation of
Adapt
against two state-of-the-art methods,
PALM

(
Lu et al. 2024
)
and
Rango

(
Thompson et al. 2024
)
, and two alternative refinement designs demonstrates its effectiveness. On the
CoqStoq
benchmark
(
Thompson et al. 2024
)
,
Adapt
proves 41.33% of 1,171 theorems, outperforming the strongest baseline by 16.63%. This lead increases to 18.58% on our new, more challenging benchmark of 1,720 theorems. Further experiments confirm the generalizability of
Adapt
across five LLMs and, through ablation studies, validate the contribution of its refinement strategies and the decision-maker.

In summary, we make the following contributions:

(1)

We propose
Adapt
, an LLM-based proof refinement framework that adaptively selects from different strategies based on the state of the proof assistant and available context.

(2)

We construct
CoqDev
, a new benchmark mined from real-world Coq commit histories that models the incremental development process.

(3)

We conduct a comprehensive evaluation of
Adapt
on both
CoqDev
and the existing
CoqStoq
benchmark, demonstrating that
Adapt
achieves significant improvements over state-of-the-art techniques.

To motivate our ideas, we present in Figure
2
a proof example in Coq
(
The Coq Development Team 2024
)
—a widely used theorem prover for software verification and the primary ITP considered in this work. We start with a brief overview of the proof process in Coq and then introduce the problem setting of adaptive proof refinement.

Definitions and theorems

As is common in Coq, the example in Figure
2
begins with the
definition
of data structures, functions, and inductive relations. Lines 1–4 define Peano natural numbers (
nat
) with a base constructor (
O
) and an inductive successor constructor (
S
), where
S n
returns the number that is one more than
n
. The file then defines a boolean function
leb
that recursively checks if one number is less than or equal to another, and an inductive relation
le
that provides a formal definition of the same concept.

After the definitions,
theorems
are stated and proven. They start with the keyword
Theorem
or
Lemma
, which are functionally identical but used by convention to distinguish key results from helper proofs. The main theorem,
leb_complete
(Line 31), states that if
leb n m
is
true
, then
n
is less than or equal to
m
. To prove this, two helper lemmas are required:
O_le_n
, which proves that
O
is less than or equal to any natural number, and
n_le_m_Sn_le_Sm
, which shows that if
n <= m
, then
n + 1 <= m + 1
.

Proof state and context

Once a theorem is stated, Coq enters an interactive proof mode and displays the current
proof state
. This state includes the
goal
(i.e., the obligation that the user needs to prove) and the
local context
(e.g., available local variables and hypotheses). The user can apply a
tactic
to transform this state, for example, by simplifying the goal or introducing new hypotheses. Coq then updates the state or reports an error when tactic application is invalid. The proof of
leb_complete
uses the
induction
tactic on
n
(Line 34) and case analysis (
destruct
) on
m
(Line 38). Figure
4(a)
shows the proof state before the tactic on Line 36 is applied; the context is above the double line and the goal is below.

Proof failure

Coq reports an error for any invalid tactic applications. For instance, under the proof state in Figure
4(b)
derived from Line 40 of Figure
2
, an
erroneous tactic

apply Sn_le_Sm_n_le_m in H
leads to a proof failure. This tactic attempts to perform forward reasoning by applying the lemma to the hypothesis
H
, but fails with the
error message
:
“Unable to apply lemma of type "forall n m : nat, n <= m -> n + 1 <= m + 1" on hypothesis of type "leb (n + 1) (m + 1) = true”
.

Project management and refinement strategies

To manage complexity, formal proofs are often organized into
multi-file projects
, similar to large software projects. When writing a proof, developers use not only the local context but also a
global context
of definitions and theorems from the current file or imported ones. This leads to several refinement strategies. One common strategy is searching for existing lemmas. For example, to make progress from the state in Figure
4(b)
, the proven lemma
n_le_m_Sn_le_Sm
is applied to the goal, simplifying it to
n <= m
. When no suitable existing lemma is available, a developer may need to invent a new one, a strategy known as
lemma discovery

(
Alhessi et al. 2025
;
Kurashige et al. 2024
;
Sivaraman et al. 2022
;
Johansson 2019
)
.

Similar to how developers search and reuse existing code to write new code
(
Sadowski et al. 2015
)
, existing proofs can also serve as a source of inspiration in proof construction.
For example, suppose a developer, after proving everything in Figure
2
, now wants to prove
leb_correct
(Figure
3(b)
). The statement of
leb_correct
is structurally similar to
leb_complete
, suggesting a similar proof pattern (induction on
n
, case analysis on
m
). Of course, naïvely copying the proof of
leb_complete
and reusing it does not work, since the helper lemmas of
leb_complete
are not compatible with
leb_correct
. An additional refinement strategy is required: the developer can draw inspiration from them to propose and prove a new, necessary lemma:
Sn_le_Sm_n_le_m
(Figure
3(a)
).

This motivating example highlights the importance of
adaptive proof refinement that integrates multiple proof refinement strategies
according to the available context (i.e., local and global context) as well as the current state of the proof assistant (e.g., partial proof, erroneous tactic, error message, and proof state). A tool that only retrieves existing lemmas would fail because the necessary lemma
Sn_le_Sm_n_le_m
does not exist in the
leb_correct
example. Conversely, an approach focusing only on lemma discovery would have to generate both the new lemmas and proofs from scratch, which is difficult and costly without the guidance of existing lemmas and proofs. The core challenge, therefore, is to create a system that can dynamically assess the available context and current state of the proof assistant to select the most promising strategy like human experts.

Algorithm 1

Adaptive Proof Refinement Procedure

Algorithm
1
provides more details of the workflow illustrated in Figure
1
. The inputs are a theorem statement (
t
) to be proven, the global context (
g
), and a decision maker
DM
.
Adapt
first generates an initial proof by prompting an LLM and checks it using Coq (Line 1). If the initial proof fails,
Adapt
captures the
state of the proof assistant
, including the erroneous tactic, the proof state at the point of failure, the error message from Coq, and the partial proof preceding the error (Line 2). Next, it initializes a working context
ctx
by retrieving essential information from
g
, including relevant lemmas and similar proofs in the repository (Line 4).

Subsequently,
Adapt
enters an iterative refinement loop, bounded by a predefined iteration limit (Line 6). In each iteration, the decision maker analyzes the state of the proof assistant and working context to select a strategy. Based on the selection,
Adapt
may opt to perform lemma discovery (Lines 8–11), context enrichment (Lines 12-15), or simply regenerate the proof (Lines 16-17). This loop continues until a successful proof is found or an iteration limit is reached. We elaborate on each step in the following sections.

3.1.
Initial Proof Generation

Adapt
’s iterative process begins with an initial proof attempt. The initial proof can be generated by any approach, e.g., LLM-based
(
Thompson et al. 2024
;
Lu et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
First et al. 2023
)
, deep learning-based
(
Yang and Deng 2019
;
Sanchez-Stern et al. 2023
;
First et al. 2020
;
First and Brun 2022
)
, or symbolic methods
(
Bundy 2002
)
. This makes
Adapt
a general framework complementary to proof generation approaches. In this work, we use the retrieval-augmented generation method proposed by Lu et al.
(
Lu et al. 2024
)
to generate the initial proof, since it is a recent method with the source code publicly available. Figure
5
shows the prompt template. The initial proof is generated by prompting an LLM with the theorem statement, all definitions appearing in the statement, and top K relevant theorems and lemmas
(
Lu et al. 2024
)
. In this work, we set K to 10 to avoid exceeding the context window limit.

3.2.
Context Initialization

When the initial proof attempt fails,
Adapt
collects comprehensive information to guide the iterative refinement loop. It first captures the state of the proof assistant, which includes the following information:

•

Erroneous tactic:
the specific tactic that causes the error.

•

Error message:
Coq’s explanation of why the tactic fails.

•

Partial proof:
the proof script before the erroneous tactic.

•

Stuck proof state:
the proof state resulting from the execution of the partial proof, which includes the remaining goals to prove and the local variables and hypotheses available for use, as defined in Section
2
.

The combination of the erroneous tactic, its error message, and the stuck proof state reveals the immediate cause of failure, while the stuck proof state and the partial proof indicate the overall progress of the current proof. For instance, a simple syntax error in a nearly completed proof could be fixed by regeneration, whereas a failure near the beginning could signal a more fundamental issue with the overall proof strategy. This comprehensive information reflects the feedback a human developer receives and enables the decision-maker to examine different aspects of the failure and select a proper refinement strategy.

In addition to the state of the proof assistant,
Adapt
maintains a working context throughout its refinement loop to guide its decision-maker and refinement strategies.
Adapt
initializes this context with the statements of theorems and lemmas retrieved during the initial proof generation, and the proof script of a similar theorem from the global context. Due to the context window limit of the LLM, we only include one proof script as an example. This proof provides a potential proof structure to guide subsequent proof attempts.
Following prior work
(
Thompson et al. 2024
)
, we select a similar theorem using BM25
(
Robertson et al. 2009
)
, which compares the textual similarity between the statement of the theorem to prove and the statements of other theorems in the global context. We only compare the theorem statements instead of the proof body, since the initial proof is wrong and using it as a basis for comparison can lead to similar proofs that are also wrong. This initialized context is then provided to the decision-maker to select the refinement strategy.

3.3.
Decision Maker

Adapt
leverages LLM’s reasoning capabilities to decide which refinement strategy to take based on both the state of the proof assistant and the context information gathered in Section
3.2
. Figure
6
shows the prompt template for decision making. Specifically,
Adapt
provides the LLM with the theorem statement and definitions appearing in it, the state of the proof assistant, and the working context. The prompt then instructs the LLM to analyze the failure reasons and assess the sufficiency of the current context for solving the proof goal. Based on the analysis, the LLM is asked to select one of the three predefined strategies. We do not claim that these three strategies are complete, as other useful refinement strategies may exist. Our goal is to demonstrate the effectiveness of adaptively selecting from diverse strategies, rather than to propose all possible strategies.

(1)

Lemma discovery
: When a proof goal is too complex to be solved directly with the available context, human developers often propose new helper lemmas to decompose the proof into more manageable sub-tasks. This process, known as
lemma discovery
, has been proven to be effective when leveraging LLMs to propose new lemmas
(
Wang et al. 2023
;
Alhessi et al. 2025
)
. Inspired by this, we integrate the lemma discovery strategy to generate lemmas to decompose complex proof goals. As shown in Figure
6
, the decision-maker is also prompted to propose a set of existing theorems and lemmas that can be potentially adapted to generate a new lemma when selecting this strategy.

(2)

Context enrichment
: This strategy addresses failures caused by an insufficient working context by searching for additional definitions, lemmas, theorems, and proofs. The decision-maker is also prompted to provide a set of keywords to guide the following context enrichment process, such as the names of definitions or lemmas present in the stuck proof state.

(3)

Regeneration
: This strategy is employed to either repair simple errors, such as a syntax mistake, or to try a new proof approach when the previous proof contains significant errors, such as applying induction on wrong variables or failing to generalize the induction hypothesis correctly.

Alternative decision-maker designs offer different trade-offs in terms of performance and cost. Section
4.7
explores decision-makers based on heuristic rules and machine learning (ML) models. We describe each refinement strategy in the following sections.

3.4.
Lemma Discovery Strategy

Adapt
employs an LLM-based lemma discovery strategy to both propose new lemmas and refine existing ones selected by the decision-maker, as mentioned in Section
3.3
. Performing both approaches maximizes the opportunities to find useful lemmas. Algorithm
2
describes the lemma discovery procedure.

Previous lemma discovery approaches
(
Wang et al. 2023
;
Alhessi et al. 2025
)
propose lemmas only based on the theorem statement, which does not account for the rich contextual information. By contrast,
Adapt
discovers lemmas based on the theorem statement, the proof state where the proof assistant is stuck, and the working context. The stuck proof state could reveal the specific difficulty the proof assistant encounters, enabling the generation of lemmas that directly address the specific obstacle. For example, the proof state in Figure
4(c)
suggests that a lemma stating
(n + 1) <= (m + 1) -> n <= m
could be useful, as it bridges the hypotheses
H
and
IHn

Propose new lemmas.

Proposing new lemmas requires an agent not only to formulate a statement that is both syntactically valid and helpful, but also to generate a correct proof of the statement. To address this,
Adapt
employs a two-stage process that decouples the generation of lemma statements from the generation of their proofs, as shown in Lines 3–8 of Algorithm
2
.

In the first stage, an LLM is instructed to generate statements of new lemmas. As shown in the prompt in Figure
7
, it is provided with existing theorems and lemmas in the working context and stuck proof state, so that it can generate semantically relevant lemma statements, while avoiding redundant lemmas that already exist. These generated statements are then checked by Coq for syntactic and semantic correctness. Any invalid statements are discarded.

In the second stage,
Adapt
attempts to generate a proof for each valid lemma statement, using the same procedure as the initial proof generation described in Section
3.1
. The statement of a successfully proven lemma is added to the working context.

Refine existing lemmas.

Some existing theorems and lemmas, while not directly applicable, can be adapted to work in the current proof. This process is outlined in Lines 10–13 of Algorithm
2
. For example, given the proof state from Figure

(Figure

, as shown in Figure

.

To refine a theorem or lemma selected by the decision-maker,
Adapt
provides an LLM with its statement and original proof. The prompt, as shown in Figure

, instructs the LLM to analyze how the lemma can be adapted to solve the proof goal. It then directs the model to generate both the refined statement and proof, encouraging it to reuse the original proof to simplify the proof generation task. Finally,
Adapt
validates each refined lemma by executing its statement and proof in Coq. Only those lemmas with both a well-formed statement and a correct proof are added to the working context.

After updating the working context,
Adapt
prompts an LLM to regenerate a proof. The prompt provides the original theorem statement, the updated working context, and the state of the proof assistant from the prior proof failure. It also instructs the LLM to leverage the new lemmas in its proof attempt.

3.5.
Context Enrichment Strategy

When this strategy is selected,
Adapt
searches for additional definitions, lemmas, theorems, and proofs from the global context to enrich its working context.
Specifically,
Adapt
performs a targeted search using keywords provided by the decision-maker, as mentioned in Section
3.3
. This contrasts with prior work
(
Thompson et al. 2024
)

The intuition is that a proof state may contain terms that are not critical for making progress, thus using the proof state as a query can retrieve irrelevant theorems and lemmas or miss important ones.

Adapt
searches for definitions, theorems, lemmas, and proofs that match the specific keywords the decision-maker has suggested. For example, recognizing that the proof state in Figure

involves the relation
le
and function
leb
on type
nat

has retrieved the proof of

and

used in that proof, and generate their names as keywords if they are not in the working context.
Subsequently,
Adapt
prompts an LLM to regenerate the proof using a similar prompt as in Section
3.4
but explicitly instructs the LLM to leverage the enriched context.

4.1.
Comparison Baselines

We compare
Adapt
against two state-of-the-art LLM-based proof generation tools:
Rango

(
Thompson et al. 2024
)
, which uses a fine-tuned LLM for step-by-step proof search, and
PALM

(
Lu et al. 2024
)
, which employs symbolic methods for proof refinement. Since
PALM
does not query the LLM during proof refinement, we build two additional baselines,
Self-Refine
and
Self-Refine+RAG
, that refine proofs with iterative LLM queries. Each baseline is described in detailed below.

•

Rango

(
Thompson et al. 2024
)
fine-tunes the DeepSeek-Coder 1.3B
(
Guo et al. 2024
)
for step-by-step proof generation. At each proof step, it retrieves helper lemmas and existing proofs based on their similarity to the current proof state.

•

PALM

(
Lu et al. 2024
)
integrates LLM with symbolic proof refinement. It first prompts the LLM to generate an initial proof, then employs heuristics to repair common errors in the proof. If an error cannot be fixed, it backtracks and iteratively regenerates previous proof steps with CoqHammer
(
Czajka and Kaliszyk 2018
)
.

•

Self-Refine

(
Madaan et al. 2023
)
adopts a simple iterative repair loop. After the initial proof failure, it repeatedly prompts the LLM to generate a new proof based on the state of the proof assistant from the previous failure, without a working context, further lemma discovery or context enrichment.

•

Self-Refine+RAG
enhances
Self-Refine
by initializing the working context before entering the iterative repair loop. In each iteration, it instructs the LLM to explicitly analyze the state of the proof assistant and working context, then regenerate a proof. However, it does not perform further adaptive lemma discovery or context enrichment.

4.2.
Benchmarks

We perform experiments on two different benchmarks. First, we use
CoqStoq
, a state-of-the-art benchmark based on 14 real-world Coq projects on GitHub
(
Thompson et al. 2024
)
. We use its cut-off split, which is created to mitigate data leakage issues, consisting of 1,171 theorems.

Furthermore, we construct a new benchmark called
CoqDev
to represent proof construction scenarios in a more realistic and challenging setting. The motivation is based on our observation that proof generation tasks from existing benchmarks are typically constructed by masking the proof body of a theorem for generation. This leaves the helper lemmas and similar proofs fully available to the LLM. However, in practice, those helper lemmas may be introduced while constructing that proof, and similar proofs may be introduced after the commit of the proof. In other words, this simple masking-in-the-middle evaluation setting provides too much information to LLMs that may not exist as human developers write their proofs.
Therefore, this setup may not fully reflect the capabilities of proof generation tools in real-world proof writing.

We construct
CoqDev
by mining the commit histories of open-source Coq projects on GitHub. Specifically, we search for projects that use Coq as a primary language as of February 20th, 2025, and rank them by creation date, prioritizing newer projects to mitigate potential data leakage. Furthermore, we only include projects that can be built automatically with the
Makefile
or
_CoqProject
, resulting in a final set of 14 projects. These projects span a range of verification domains, including logic, data structures, programming languages, and mathematics. We collect all commits of each selected project to get 2,225 commits in total. Next, we compare each commit against its parent to find the set of newly introduced or modified theorems. We retain only those commits that both contain such theorems and can be built successfully.

We extract theorems introduced in each commit. For each theorem, we mask its proof body as well as the other theorems and lemmas introduced in the commit. In this way, we make sure that LLMs cannot access any helper lemmas or theorems introduced during the construction of the proof. They need to propose these lemmas or theorems as human developers did when writing the proof. In the meantime, any theorems and lemmas introduced in future commits are also not available to LLMs for proof generation. Only the previously committed lemmas and theorems are available for LLMs to leverage, which resembles how developers write a proof in a realistic setting. Furthermore, to prevent a few large projects from dominating the benchmark and ensure diversity, we randomly sample at most 200 theorems from each project to form the final benchmark. In the end, we collected 1,720 theorems.

4.7.
RQ4: Comparing decision making strategies

While
Adapt
leverages the reasoning capabilities of LLMs for adaptive strategy selection based on reflection over the current proof progress and available context, we also investigate alternative decision-making mechanisms to understand different trade-offs.

Rule-Based.
One such alternative is a rule-based method, which employs simple heuristics to select a strategy. It first identifies lemmas used in the retrieved similar proof. If these lemmas are not present in the current working context, it prioritizes context enrichment. Otherwise, it extracts all definitions within the current proof state. If there are definitions not present in existing lemmas in the working context,
\textsc{Adapt}_{\text{rule}}
opts for lemma discovery. The intuition here is that useful new lemmas should establish properties about the specific definitions in the proof state. If neither of these conditions is met, it attempts regeneration.

Learning-Based.
Another mechanism we explored is a learning-based approach, which employs a machine learning (ML) model to predict the strategy a developer would use in a given state of the proof assistant. Training such a model requires a labeled dataset of state of the proof assistant and the corresponding strategies taken by human developers, which is not available. To address this, we collect a dataset of 18,628 examples from the human-written proofs in
CoqDev
, and evaluate the model using ten-fold cross-validation.

To generate each data point, we first simulate the working context at each step of a human-written proof by using the same retrieval method described in Section
3.2
. We then extract the following features to characterize the state of the proof assistant:

(1)

The number of unique definitions appearing in the proof goal.

(2)

The number of unique definitions appearing in the local hypotheses.

(3)

The count of definitions that appear in the goal but not in the hypotheses.

(4)

The count of definitions that appear in the goal but not in existing lemmas in the working context.

(5)

The similarity score of the retrieved similar theorem.

(6)

The number of lemmas used in the similar theorem that are not present in the working context.

We select features 1 and 2 to reflect proof state complexity. Features 3–5 measure how many definitions in the goal lack supporting lemmas in the working context. Feature 6 assesses if potentially useful information in a similar theorem has not been incorporated.

Given these input features, the ML model needs to predict which refinement strategy to take at the given proof state, which is a typical classification problem. The ground-truth labels are derived from the corresponding human-written tactic at the proof state: if the tactic applies a new helper lemma introduced in the same commit, it is labeled as
Lemma Discovery
; if it applies a lemma that existed prior to the current commit, it is labeled as
Context Enrichment
; otherwise, it is labeled as
Regeneration
.

To select a model, we conduct a preliminary experiment using ten-fold cross-validation to compare several classifiers. We evaluate traditional models, including Support Vector Machine (SVM)
(
Hearst et al. 1998
)
, Decision Tree
(
Safavian and Landgrebe 1991
)
, Random Forest
(
Breiman 2001
)
, and XGBoost
(
Chen and Guestrin 2016
)
. In addition, we build a Deep Neural Network (DNN)
(
LeCun et al. 2015
)
with three fully-connected layers, using the ReLU activation function
(
Nair and Hinton 2010
)
and a dropout rate of 0.2. We train this DNN for 50 epochs using the Adam optimizer
(
Kingma and Ba 2017
)

5.
Discussion

5.1.
Threats to Validity

Internal validity.
The main threat to internal validity arises from the inherent randomness of LLMs. Because temperature sampling is used as the decoding strategy, the LLM randomly selects the next token based on a probability distribution, which leads to variability in output. To mitigate this threat, we set the temperature of all evaluated LLMs to 0 for more deterministic outputs. Furthermore, we conducted evaluations across two benchmarks and with multiple LLMs of different parameter sizes. The consistent results observed across these varied settings help to reduce this threat.

External validity.
The primary threat to external validity concerns the generalizability of our experimental results. To mitigate this threat, we conducted experiments on two benchmarks that comprise various real-world projects covering multiple domains. However, while the principles of
Adapt
’s approach are applicable to other interactive proof assistants like Isabelle and Lean, our current implementation and evaluation are confined solely to the Coq ecosystem. Extending
Adapt
to and evaluating it within these other ITPs remains for future work.

Construct validity.
A potential threat to construct validity is we measure the effectiveness of a decision-maker with the overall success rate, which is an indirect metric. This is because a good strategic choice does not guarantee a successful proof. For example, the decision-maker could select a suitable strategy, such as lemma discovery, but the execution of this strategy may fail, leading to an overall proof failure that does not reflect a poor decision.

5.2.
Limitations and Future Work

The framework of
Adapt
can be expanded by integrating more refinement strategies, such as proof repair models fine-tuned on ITP feedback, similar to the approach of Baldur
(
First et al. 2023
)
. Our decision-maker primarily leverages LLM reasoning, with initial explorations into rule-based and machine learning approaches. Future research could investigate more sophisticated methods. For instance, this could involve creating more nuanced heuristics for rule-based methods or incorporating richer features for the machine learning models.
Currently,
Adapt
relies solely on LLMs to propose lemma statements, but the quality of these LLM-generated statements can vary; some are trivial, syntactically flawed, or unprovable. Future work could enhance the lemma discovery strategy by developing advanced prompting techniques or integrating symbolic, data-driven lemma discovery methods.

6.
Related Work

Proof Refinement.

Recent work in proof refinement has focused on combining a vanilla LLM proof generation model with a standalone repair procedure when errors occur. These approaches typically generate an initial proof attempt with an LLM and then employ various repair strategies that leverage proof error information. For instance, Baldur
(
First et al. 2023
)
fine-tunes a second LLM specifically for proof repair, regenerating a new proof from both the incorrect proof and the Coq error message. Other systems integrate symbolic techniques. Lyra
(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
follows a fixed heuristic, first invoking automated tools like SledgeHammer
(
Paulson and Blanchette 2012
)
. If all these tools fail, it prompts an LLM with the erroneous proof and Coq error message to regenerate a proof. PALM
(
Lu et al. 2024
)
uses rule-based transformations to fix common tactic errors, and a backtracking procedure that can revert proof steps and call on automated theorem provers like CoqHammer
(
Czajka and Kaliszyk 2018
)
.

A common limitation of these methods is their reliance on fixed heuristics or a single repair strategy. In contrast,
Adapt
implements an adaptive framework that dynamically selects suitable proof refinement strategies. This allows
Adapt
to be more flexible, for example, by opting for an entirely new proof when an initial attempt is deeply flawed—a capability not present in systems like PALM, which are constrained to refining the original proof. While our approach is powerful, we do not compare with Baldur, which relies on models (Minerva
(
Lewkowycz et al. 2022
)
) that are not open-sourced and require significant data and computational resources for fine-tuning.

Retrieval-Augmented Proof Generation.

Another line of work enhances automated proof generation by integrating retrieval mechanisms for relevant context. A common approach is to retrieve premises like definitions and lemmas. For instance, ReProver
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
fine-tunes a retrieval model to find relevant premises given a proof state. Magnushammer
(
Mikuła et al. 2023
)
improves the retrieval process by training a reranking model to prioritize the most relevant premises. Furthermore, Rango
(
Thompson et al. 2024
)
retrieves both relevant proofs and lemmas via textual similarity to the current proof state, providing richer guidance for the proof search
(
Thompson et al. 2024
)
.

While these tools focus on enhancing proof generation with retrieval methods,
Adapt
integrates multiple strategies and selects from them adaptively. Furthermore,
Adapt
leverages pretrained LLMs, whereas these approaches often require significant training or fine-tuning.

Lemma Discovery.

Lemma discovery facilitates automated proof generation by proposing helper lemmas aimed at simplifying complex proofs. These techniques range from neuro-symbolic approaches to data-driven methods. For instance, Lemmanaid
(
Alhessi et al. 2025
)
employs a neuro-symbolic pipeline where a fine-tuned model generates lemma templates with symbolic holes that a symbolic engine then instantiates with concrete variables. Lfind
(
Sivaraman et al. 2022
)
offers a data-driven alternative, framing lemma discovery as a synthesis task that generalizes the proof goal into a sketch and then finds instantiations. These dedicated lemma discovery techniques are complementary to our work. Integrating them into
Adapt
’s adaptive framework to improve the quality of proposed lemmas remains a promising avenue for future research.

LegoProver
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
uses an LLM to propose new lemmas and store them in a library for later reuse via embedding-based retrieval. While LegoProver follows a fixed, resource-intensive workflow that always discover lemmas before proof generation,
Adapt
’s adaptive framework invokes costly actions only when necessary. As shown in our evaluation, this leads to a more efficient process, enabling
Adapt
to effectively prove theorems while using fewer tokens.

7.
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose
Adapt
, an adaptive proof refinement framework.
Adapt
integrates multiple refinement strategies, including lemma discovery, context enrichment and regeneration. Upon proof failures,
Adapt
employs an LLM-based decision-maker to select an appropriate strategy based on state of the proof assistant and available context. We also introduce
CoqDev
, a new benchmark that better simulates more challenging real-world development processes. Our experiments show that
Adapt
significantly outperforms state-of-the-art baselines on both
CoqDev
and the existing
CoqStoq
benchmark. Further analysis demonstrates
Adapt
’s generalizability on five different LLMs, and measures the contribution of its refinement strategies. We also compare the trade-offs of alternative decision-maker designs.
