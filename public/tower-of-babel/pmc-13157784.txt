Prevalence and types of anxiety disorders among patients with cardiac conditions and elevated HADS-A scores: findings from the initial screening phase of the Heart and Mind trial in Denmark
Authors: Selina Kikkenborg Berg, Line Ryberg Rasmussen, Charlotte Brun Thorup, Trine Bernholdt Rasmussen, Anne Vinggaard Christensen, Cecilie Bech Hammer, Martin Balslev Jorgensen, Jesper Hastrup Svendsen, Signe Stelling Risom, Carsten Juul, Inge Schjødt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo identify whether patients with arrhythmia, heart failure or ischaemic heart disease presenting with anxiety symptoms measured by the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) have identifiable anxiety according to the Structured Clinical Interview for Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (SCID) and, if so, which type of anxiety disorder based on the SCID.DesignInitial screening data from the Heart and Mind randomised clinical trial.SettingPatients with arrhythmia, heart failure or ischaemic heart disease were screened using HADS, and patients with a HADS-anxiety (HADS-A) score≥8 were invited to participate. Participants were interviewed by trained cardiac nurses using the SCID to determine whether they met the criteria for anxiety and, if so, the type of anxiety disorder.ResultsOf the 7816 patients who completed the HADS questionnaire, 1803 (23%) had a HADS-A score≥8. Among these, 398 (22%) agreed to the SCID interview, and 336 (84%) met the diagnostic criteria for an anxiety disorder. The mean age was 61 years, with 40% being female. The mean HADS-A score was 11.3 (SD=2.7). The most common types of anxiety were generalised anxiety disorder (61%), panic disorder (23%) and specific phobia (8%).ConclusionsThe majority of individuals identified by the instrument also met the diagnostic criteria for an anxiety disorder. Generalised anxiety disorder and panic disorder were the most prevalent subtypes. Anxiety was common across the cardiac population, underscoring the need for routine assessment and targeted intervention in clinical practice.Trial registration numberNCT04582734.

Introduction

Anxiety symptoms are frequently experienced by patients with cardiac disease.13 In addition to adversely impacting quality of life, anxiety has been associated with an increased mortality risk across various cardiac conditions.49

Anxiety is recognised as a diffuse state, defined as ‘an unpleasant affective experience marked by a significant degree of apprehensiveness about the potential appearance of future aversive or harmful events’.10 Anxiety disorders are classified using standardised diagnostic systems that categorise psychological symptoms based on specific criteria.11 Anxiety disorders can be assessed by clinicians or trained mental health professionals using SCID (Structured Clinical Interview for DSM (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders)).12 13

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) is a widely used patient-reported instrument designed to assess symptoms of anxiety and depression in individuals with medical conditions and does not include symptoms of anxiety or depression relating to physical disorders.14 15 The instrument comprises two subscales—one measuring anxiety and the other depression. A score of ≥8 on the HADS-anxiety (HADS-A) subscale indicates clinically significant anxiety symptoms.14 In a study where HADS-A was used as a screener in patients diagnosed with coronary artery disease, they found that the optimal cut-off value was ≥8, and 7% were diagnosed with an anxiety disorder.16 In Denmark, HADS is commonly used in cardiac rehabilitation for patients diagnosed with ischaemic heart disease or heart failure to screen for symptoms of anxiety and depression.17 In a previous trial including patients with cardiac conditions and implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICD), patients with a HADS-A score≥8 were assessed using SCID to determine the presence and type of anxiety disorder.18 The study revealed that 76% of patients met the diagnostic criteria for either an anxiety disorder or an adjustment disorder with anxiety.18 The most frequently identified types of anxiety in the study were panic disorder and generalised anxiety disorder.18 Although HADS has demonstrated psychometric properties for use in patients with cardiac conditions in Denmark,19 its diagnostic utility across a broader range of cardiac conditions remains to be fully established. The overall aim of this study was to investigate whether the HADS can identify patients with an anxiety disorder in a broader population of patients with cardiac conditions.

Objectives

The objectives of this study were, in patients diagnosed with arrhythmia, heart failure or ischaemic heart disease who presented with anxiety symptoms measured by the HADS-A, to (1) identify the proportion of patients with cardiac conditions and anxiety using the SCID and (2) determine the type of anxiety disorder based on the diagnostic criteria (SCID).

Methods

This paper presents initial screening data from the Heart and Mind randomised clinical trial (RCT), in which patients with cardiac conditions who met the diagnostic criteria for either an anxiety disorder or an adjustment disorder with anxiety were randomised to receive either cognitive behavioural therapy or usual care (results not reported here). The trial protocol has been published previously,20 and a summary is presented below.

Eligibility criteria

Patients were screened for anxiety and depression using HADS at least 8 weeks after hospital discharge and were eligible for inclusion in the Heart and Mind RCT if they had a primary diagnosis of arrhythmia, heart failure or ischaemic heart disease (online supplemental table 1); a HADS-A score of ≥8 with the HADS-A score exceeding the HADS-depression (HADS-D) score, as anxiety had to be the primary mental health problem; were 18 years of age or older and were treated at one of four inclusion sites in Denmark (Copenhagen University Hospital—Rigshospitalet; Copenhagen University Hospital—Herlev and Gentofte; Aarhus University Hospital and Aalborg University Hospital). As the study was conducted within the framework of an anxiety-focused intervention trial, participants were required to have anxiety as the predominant symptom domain. Therefore, patients with higher HADS-D than HADS-A scores were excluded to ensure alignment between the intervention target and the psychological profile of the study population. Participants were required to speak and understand Danish and to provide written informed consent to participate. Patients receiving psychological treatment at the time of recruitment were ineligible for inclusion so as not to contaminate the intervention.

Recruitment and participants

Potential participants were screened between May 2021 and May 2023 until the target sample size for the RCT was reached (n=336).20 Participants were included consecutively until an even distribution across the three cardiovascular diagnostic groups was achieved. In cases where patients presented with multiple cardiovascular conditions, they were classified according to the diagnosis for which they had most recently been hospitalised. Patients who met the set criteria for the HADS-A score were invited to participate in a SCID interview conducted by a cardiac nurse21 who had completed a 10-day training programme in cognitive-behavioural therapy and SCID interviewing. The nurses who conducted the interviews were supervised by a psychologist during the study period.

Instruments

The HADS is a 14-item questionnaire developed to assess symptoms of anxiety and depression in hospital outpatients in somatic settings.14 Symptoms of more severe psychiatric disorders, such as suicidal thoughts, are not included in the questionnaire. The questionnaire contains seven questions to examine anxiety (HADS-A) and likewise seven to examine depression (HADS-D). The subscale scores ranging from 0 to 7 are considered within the normal range. Scores between 8 and 10 suggest the presence of anxiety or mood disorder, and scores of ≥11 indicate a probable anxiety or mood disorder.15 The instrument has been validated in a similar population of patients with cardiac diagnoses, indicating supportive evidence for convergent construct validity and high internal consistency for both subscales.19

SCID is a semistructured interview guide developed to facilitate diagnoses based on the criteria outlined in the DSM and was used to determine the type of anxiety.12 13 This study used the non-patient edition of the Structured Clinical Interview for Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition, Text Revision, Axis I Disorders: Clinical Disorders, Module F (Psychotic Screen) (SCID‑I, DSM‑IV‑TR; November 2002).11 In addition, Adjustment Disorder (Module I) was included as a potential diagnosis, given the potential impacts of living with heart disease.

Other variables

Information on educational level, employment status, height, weight, alcohol intake, smoking habits, if they have ever sought help for mental health disorders, been admitted to a psychiatric ward or received treatment for substance abuse was reported by the patients. Data on New York Heart Association functional classification and left ventricular ejection fraction were obtained from patient records, while information on age, marital status, cardiac diagnoses and somatic comorbidities was obtained from Danish national public registers.22 23

Non-responders

Non-responders were defined as patients who did not complete the HADS questionnaire, and responders were defined as patients who completed the HADS questionnaire but either declined to participate or did not meet the inclusion criteria for recruitment into the Heart and Mind RCT (online supplemental tables 2 and 3). Information on sex, age, marital status, cardiac diagnoses and comorbidities for non-responders was also obtained from Danish national public registers.22 23

Statistical methods

Descriptive analyses were conducted using means and SDs for continuous variables and frequency distributions for categorical variables. Results with n<5 are not presented, in accordance with the Danish data protection legislation. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata Statistical Software: Release 17 (StataCorp, 2021).

Patient and public involvement

We conducted a pilot study involving a specific patient group. After completion of the pilot, participants were interviewed regarding their experience with the screening procedure and study participation. Their feedback led to minor adjustments to the written study information and practical organisation of the screening process to improve clarity, feasibility and acceptability. These adjustments did not involve changes to the screening instrument itself but aimed to optimise participant understanding before initiating the present study.

Results

Type of anxiety assessed by SCID

A total of 336 (84%, 95% CI 80.5% to 87.7%) of the patients presenting with anxiety symptoms measured by HADS suffered from an anxiety disorder or adjustment disorder according to the SCID. 208 (51%, 95% CI 50% to 55%) had one anxiety diagnosis, 86 (22%, 95% CI 19% to 23%) had two and 42 (11%, 95% CI 8% to 12%) had three or more (table 2). Generalised anxiety disorder was the most common disorder across cardiac conditions (n=76–83, 58%–64%), followed by panic disorder (n=24–38, 18%–29%) and specific phobia (n=11–13, 8%–10%) (table 2).

Of the 398 patients who underwent the SCID interview, 197 (49%, 95 CI 45% to 54%) had a HADS-A score≥11. The mean HADS-A score was 11.3 (SD=2.7) among the 336 patients with a SCID-confirmed anxiety or adjustment disorder. Among the 62 patients without an anxiety or adjustment disorder diagnosis, 21% (n=13) had a HADS-A score≥11, with a mean score of 9.5 (SD=1.7).

A comparison of patients with and without a SCID-confirmed anxiety or adjustment disorder showed that patients with a SCID-confirmed diagnosis were more frequently women (39.9% vs 27.4%) and were on average younger than patients without (mean age 61.2 years (SD 11.7) vs 66.0 years (SD 11.5)).

Non-responders

Demographic and clinical information for non-responders is presented in online supplemental tables 2 and 3. Both non-responders (who did not complete the HADS questionnaire) and responders (who completed the HADS questionnaire but did not participate in the study) tended to be older, included a higher proportion of men and had higher Charlson Comorbidity Index scores compared with the study population (online supplemental tables 2 and 3).

Discussion

This study investigated the prevalence and types of anxiety in a heterogeneous population of patients with cardiac conditions screened for anxiety symptoms using the HADS instrument. Approximately 5 of 6 patients identified as having symptoms of anxiety based on the HADS also met the criteria for an anxiety or adjustment disorder according to the SCID. These findings are consistent with those of our previous study in patients with an ICD.18 The most common anxiety disorders were generalised anxiety disorder, panic disorder and specific phobia.

Approximately 1 in 4 patients screened had a HADS-A score≥8, indicating a possible anxiety disorder. This finding aligns with previous studies in patients with an ICD in Denmark18 and in patients in the UK following heart transplantation,24 although it is lower than that reported in another study.1 As 84% of patients with a HADS-A score≥8 also met the criteria for anxiety in the SCID interview, the HADS instrument is sensitive to capture anxiety symptoms but not overly sensitive, as only 16% did not meet the diagnostic criteria for either an anxiety disorder or an adjustment disorder with anxiety in the SCID interview. The distribution of anxiety disorder types appeared similar across the three diagnostic groups, although panic disorder was more prevalent among patients with ischaemic heart disease. Generalised anxiety disorder was by far the most common anxiety disorder across the cardiac conditions. The HADS scale does not include symptoms for all anxiety types, which we see reflected in the results. As the HADS was developed to minimise overlap with somatic symptoms of physical illness, it does not fully capture autonomic and bodily manifestations of anxiety. In a cardiac population, this may have led to under-identification of anxiety disorders characterised by prominent somatic arousal. Consequently, the findings primarily reflect cognitive-affective anxiety symptoms rather than the entire spectrum of anxiety pathology. Future studies may benefit from supplementing the HADS with additional screening tools or structured diagnostic interviews to improve identification of specific anxiety disorders and enhance clinical applicability.

Despite more than half of the participants reporting having previously sought and received help for mental health problems, they continued to experience anxiety symptoms. Clinical attention should be directed towards all patients with cardiac conditions—not only those in rehabilitation programmes, as rehabilitation is offered only to certain diagnostic groups and many also decline participation.2528 There is often greater focus on anxiety in patients with an ICD, ischaemic heart disease or heart failure,2528 while other patients with cardiac conditions may be overlooked. However, substantial efforts should be made to identify and reduce anxiety in all patients with cardiac conditions, as it is not only a distressing condition that has an impact on everyday life but also a significant risk factor for poor clinical outcomes and cardiac risk behaviour such as smoking, obesity and excessive alcohol consumption in individuals with cardiac diagnoses.2931 Cardiac diseases can also be the source of anxiety, which can be captured by assessment of health anxiety in the SCID interview. The core features of illness anxiety disorder, such as excessive worry about having a serious illness, are assessed by SCID and are specifically assessed within the ‘Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders’ module, highlighting its distinct nature from other anxiety conditions like generalised anxiety disorder or panic disorder. The focus should therefore extend to the broader population of patients with cardiac conditions, given the cross-diagnostic prevalence of anxiety.1

Several mechanisms may help explain the interaction between psychiatric and cardiac disorders. These include dysregulation of the autonomic nervous system and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, leading to increased levels of cortisol and catecholamine, as well as decreased heart rate variability.32 In addition, behavioural mechanisms of anxiety may manifest as unhealthy lifestyle choices, such as smoking, an unhealthy diet, physical inactivity and low adherence to medical treatment.33

A substantial proportion of patients fulfilled criteria for more than one anxiety disorder, underscoring the high level of diagnostic overlap in this population. From a clinical perspective, the presence of multiple anxiety disorders may indicate greater symptom severity, increased functional impairment and a more complex clinical presentation. Comorbidity may also complicate assessment, as overlapping somatic symptoms (eg, palpitations and dyspnoea) can be attributed to either cardiac disease or anxiety, potentially delaying appropriate recognition and treatment. Importantly, co-occurring anxiety disorders may be associated with poorer prognosis, lower adherence to cardiac treatment and increased healthcare utilisation. These findings highlight the need for comprehensive diagnostic assessment and potentially transdiagnostic or multimodal treatment approaches that address shared mechanisms across anxiety disorders rather than focusing on single diagnostic categories.

The participants are part of an RCT (results from the RCT reported elsewhere). This study builds on a previous study including patients with an ICD.18 As such, the research team had prior experience using the HADS and SCID instruments. In cases of uncertainty during the SCID interview, a second assessor was consulted. Additionally, internal supervision was provided by a psychologist. Patients were recruited from four urban hospitals providing highly specialised cardiac care, and those who chose to participate may represent a subgroup with higher engagement in healthcare and greater willingness to take part in an RCT. As a result, the study population may differ from patients with cardiac conditions treated in community or rural settings, who often vary in sociodemographic characteristics, illness burden and access to services. These factors may limit the generalisability of our findings beyond the urban, specialist-based context. Of the 1803 patients with HADS-A≥8, 256 were excluded due to HADS-D exceeding the HADS-A score. By removing this subset of patients, selection bias may have been introduced, potentially affecting the estimated proportion of patients with SCID-confirmed anxiety if these excluded individuals differ systematically from those with lower HADS-D scores. This exclusion criterion may limit the generalisability of the findings to patients with cardiac conditions with mixed anxiety-depression profiles or depression-predominant symptomatology, which are both common in clinical practice. Given the substantial comorbidity between anxiety and depression, the results should primarily be interpreted as applicable to patients with anxiety as the dominant symptom profile rather than to the broader population of patients with cardiac conditions who are psychologically distressed. The response rate of 41% was not unexpected, given the severity and complexity of illness in the population and the non-response accumulated across three separate stages of inclusion, which may have contributed to selection bias: 59.5% did not respond to the study invitation, 13.1% declined further contact after HADS screening and 44% of eligible patients did not wish to participate in the SCID interview. Individuals who agreed to participate may represent a more motivated and less comorbid subgroup than those who declined. Non-responders were generally older, more often male and had a higher comorbidity burden, suggesting that patients with greater illness severity were less likely to participate. This under-representation may lead to an underestimation of SCID-confirmed anxiety, and the over-representation of younger and female participants may further limit generalisability. Overall, the observed anxiety profile may not fully reflect non-participants.

In conclusion, the vast majority of individuals identified by the instrument also met the diagnostic criteria for an anxiety disorder. Generalised anxiety disorder and panic disorder were the most prevalent subtypes, and many patients fulfilled criteria for more than one disorder. Anxiety was common across the cardiac population, underscoring the need for routine assessment and targeted intervention in clinical practice. In clinical settings, systematic screening may help clinicians identify patients at risk, thereby enabling earlier psychological support and more integrated cardiac-mental healthcare. Implementation of routine screening could be integrated into existing follow-up pathways, for example, at post-discharge visits or during cardiac rehabilitation, but may require additional training of clinical staff, clear referral procedures and allocation of time and resources. Future research should examine the longitudinal course of anxiety in cardiac populations and compare the effectiveness and feasibility of different screening instruments and intervention approaches in both specialised and community-based cardiac settings.
