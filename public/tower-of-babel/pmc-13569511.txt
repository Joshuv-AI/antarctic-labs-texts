Pathophysiological Role of Perivascular Macrophages in Blood–Brain Barrier Dysfunction Induced by Cerebral Hypoperfusion in Mice
Authors: Sayaka Matsumura, Yoshiyuki Moriyama, Ayumi Ochiai, Yui Iwatani, Hideki Hayashi, Norio Takagi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the role of perivascular macrophages (PVMs), localized alongside the brain vasculature, in blood-brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction induced by reduced cerebral blood flow (CBF). Using a mouse model of bilateral carotid artery stenosis (BCAS) to examine cerebral hypoperfusion, the effects of pharmacological depletion of PVM-enriched macrophage populations were examined using clodronate liposomes. The results demonstrated that while cerebral hypoperfusion significantly increased BBB permeability, depletion of PVM-enriched macrophage populations attenuated this leakage. Mechanistic analyses suggested that PVM-associated responses may be linked to reduced pericyte marker expression, astrocyte activation, and increased MMP-2/9 expression. Furthermore, depletion of PVM-enriched macrophage populations inhibited the upregulation of matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2) and MMP-9. These findings suggest that PVM-enriched macrophage populations are involved in early BBB dysfunction following cerebral hypoperfusion, possibly through changes in pericyte-associated vascular integrity, astrocyte activation, and MMP-2/9-related tight junction remodeling. These results provide insight into vascular inflammatory mechanisms that may contribute to hypoperfusion-associated BBB dysfunction and later small vessel pathology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11064-026-04881-w.Keywords: Perivascular macrophages, Blood-brain barrier, Cerebral blood flow, Pericytes, Cerebral hypoperfusion

Introduction

The brain regulates higher-order functions by precisely controlling regional cerebral blood flow (CBF); therefore, when ischemia occurs and sufficient supply of oxygen and glucose via the blood is interrupted, motor and cognitive functions begin to deteriorate rapidly. Both global and regional reductions in CBF correlate with increased severity scores of cerebral small vessel disease, a group of disorders characterized by pathological changes in the small cerebral vessels [1, 2]. Moreover, the onset and progression of cerebral small vessel disease are closely associated with CBF reduction driven by risk factors, such as aging, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease [3, 4]. Reduced CBF also induces dysfunction of the blood-brain barrier (BBB), which plays a crucial role in maintaining central nervous system homeostasis [5]. For example, decreased CBF has been shown to downregulate expression of the tight junction proteins occludin and claudin-5, which are expressed at endothelial cell-cell junctions in cerebral vessels and are essential structural components of the BBB, thereby leading to BBB dysfunction [6]. BBB disruption promotes chronic inflammation, contributing to white matter lesions, brain atrophy, and progressive cognitive decline [7, 8]. However, the precise mechanism through which reduced CBF induces BBB dysfunction remains unclear. Elucidating the mechanisms underlying CBF-induced BBB disruption may facilitate the development of therapeutic strategies to suppress subsequent chronic inflammation and neurodegeneration-associated cognitive decline.

Recent studies have increasingly recognized that BBB function is regulated not only by endothelial cells but also by the surrounding perivascular cells [9]. Pericytes located around the cerebral capillaries play a critical role in the formation and stabilization of tight junctions through interactions with vascular endothelial cells, thereby regulating vascular permeability [10]. Astrocytic endfeet that ensheath cerebral blood vessels also contribute to BBB regulation [11]. Astrocyte-derived vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) have been shown to modulate the expression and function of tight junction proteins [11, 12]. Furthermore, pericyte signaling mediates the attachment of astrocytic endfeet to the perivascular space [13]. Thus, BBB integrity is tightly regulated through coordinated interactions between endothelial cells, pericytes, and astrocytes within the perivascular niche. Dysfunction of BBB-associated and perivascular cells increases barrier permeability, allowing harmful substances to infiltrate the brain parenchyma and exacerbate inflammatory responses.

More recently, brain perivascular macrophages (PVMs) have been identified as resident immune cells localized around cerebral blood vessels. PVMs reside within the perivascular space filled with cerebrospinal and interstitial fluids between the vasculature and the brain parenchyma [14, 15]. They have been reported to perform diverse functions, including the clearance of metabolic waste, maintenance of vascular flexibility, regulation of vascular function, and modulation of inflammatory responses. PVMs being preferentially localized around arteries and arterioles suggests their influence on the BBB structure and function through interactions with endothelial cells and surrounding glial cells [16]. However, studies focusing on the pathophysiological role of PVMs in BBB dysfunction induced by CBF reduction are limited.

Therefore, the present study aimed to clarify the involvement of PVMs in BBB dysfunction induced by reduced CBF using a mouse model of cerebral hypoperfusion.

Materials and Methods

Animal

Adult male C57BL/6J mice aged 11–13 weeks (Japan SLC, Inc., Shizuoka, Japan) were used in this study. Only male mice were used in the present study to reduce variability in the BCAS model, as sex-dependent differences in cerebrovascular anatomy and hypoperfusion severity may affect the reproducibility of the model. This limitation is acknowledged in the Discussion. Animals were housed under the following conditions: temperature: 23 ± 1 ℃; humidity: 55 ± 5%; 12-hour light/dark cycle. The mice had free access to food and water. The study was designed and reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and complied with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and Guidance for Experimental Animal Care issued by the Prime Minister’s Office of Japan. The experimental protocol was approved by the Committee of Animal Care and Welfare of Tokyo University of Pharmacy and Life Sciences (P24-10, P25-40). A total of 69 mice were used in this study. Eighteen mice were used to verify PVM depletion following clodronate administration, 36 mice were used for the Evans blue assay, and 12 mice were used for cerebral blood flow measurements. Three mice died within 24 h after BCAS surgery and were therefore excluded from the analyses. Some animals were used for multiple endpoints when tissue collection allowed; the number of animals used for each analysis is indicated in the corresponding figure legends.

Cerebral Blood Flow (CBF) Measurements

Mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of 0.3 mg/kg medetomidine hydrochloride, 4 mg/kg midazolam, and 5 mg/kg butorphanol tartrate (three-combination anesthetic) and maintained at a constant temperature of 37 ℃ on a heated pad. After anesthesia, the skull over the parietal region was exposed and the periosteum was removed using forceps. CBF was measured using a Laser Speckle Blood Flow Imager (Omega Zone oz-2; Omega Wave, Tokyo, Japan) preoperatively and 24 h after bilateral carotid artery stenosis (BCAS) or sham surgery, and blood flow images were captured at a rate of one image per second. The captured images were imported into the Laser Image Analyzer (Omega Wave) and the blood flow was analysed. For each mouse, the mean value was calculated from the CBF measurements obtained from 10 photographs. The region of interest (ROI) was defined as the area marked with red diagonal lines in Supplementary Fig. S1 and was maintained at a uniform size. CBF values were expressed as relative values (%) compared with preoperative values.

Depletion of Perivascular Macrophages

The depletion of perivascular macrophages was induced as previously described, with slight modifications [17]. Mice were anesthetized with the three-combination anesthetic described earlier injected intraperitoneally 3 days prior to BCAS surgery (Fig. S2). Anesthetized mice were stereotaxically injected with 5 µL liposomes containing either clodronate (CLO) (10.0 ± 1.0 µg/µL) or PBS as the vehicle (Cat. No.16003641, KATAYAMA CHEMICAL, Osaka, Japan) into the right lateral ventricle (coordinates from Bregma: anteroposterior, − 0.5 mm; mediolateral, 1.0 mm; dorsoventral, 2.5 mm). The injection was administered at a rate of 0.3 µL/min into the right lateral ventricle. Following suturing of the incision site, 0.3 mg/kg of Atipamezole (Nippon Zenyaku Kogyo, Fukushima, Japan) was administered intraperitoneally to awaken the mice.

Bilateral Common Carotid Artery Stenosis (BCAS) Model

Cerebral hypoperfusion was induced using a model of BCAS as described previously with slight modifications [18]. Mice were anesthetized with the three-combination anesthetic (0.3 mg/kg medetomidine hydrochloride, 4 mg/kg midazolam, and 5 mg/kg butorphanol tartrate), injected intraperitoneally at 3 days after CLO injection (Fig. S2). This anesthetic regimen was selected because it provides stable surgical anesthesia and analgesia for mouse procedures and allows reversal with atipamezole after surgery. The right common carotid artery (CCA) was exposed and dissected from the surrounding tissues using a midline cervical incision. A microcoil (wire diameter: 0.08 mm; inner diameter: 0.18 mm; pitch: 0.5 mm; total length: 2.5 mm; Sawane Spring Co., Ltd., Shizuoka, Japan) was placed around the bifurcation of the right internal carotid artery (ICA) and external carotid artery (ECA). After 30 min, the left CCA was exposed, and a microcoil was placed around the bifurcation of the left ICA and ECA. The surgical site was sutured, and anesthesia was reversed with atipamezole (0.3 mg/kg, i.p.). Sham-operated mice underwent the same surgical procedure without microcoil placement.

Evaluation of BBB Dysfunction

Twenty-four hours after BCAS surgery, saline containing 2% Evans Blue (EB) was injected through the mouse tail vein at a dosage of 0.1 mL under non-anesthetic conditions. After 20 min, anesthetized mice were intracardially perfused (into the left ventricle) with 30 mL of ice-cold heparin sodium (100 U/0.1 mL, NIPRO Co., Osaka, Japan)-containing perfusion solution (0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), pH 7.4). After perfusion, brains were rapidly removed and photographed. After the left cortex weight measurement, the tissue was sonicated with 50% TCA (100 w/v% Trichloroacetic Acid Solution)/0.9% saline solution at twice the tissue weight. Following sonication, the tissue suspension was centrifuged (10,000 ×g, 20 min, 4 ℃, Eppendorf Centrifuge 5425R), and the absorbance of EB in the supernatant was measured using a multimode plate reader (Nivo Alpha S Adv, 12770097, Revvity Inc., Waltham, MS, USA) at a wavelength of 620 nm.

Immunohistochemistry and Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) Staining

Twenty-four hours after BCAS, the mice were intracardially perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The brains were rapidly removed and immersed in 30% sucrose in PBS. Brain hemispheres were then divided into two parts, embedded in the optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound (Sakura Finetek, 4583), and frozen in liquid nitrogen. For each sample, a total of five coronal sections, each 20 μm thick, were prepared at depths ranging from − 1.84 mm to − 1.94 mm from Bregma using a Cryostat NX 50 (Thermo Fisher) from the frozen brain tissue blocks. These sections were mounted on MAS adhesive glass microslides (Matsunami Glass Ind., Ltd.). After washing with PBS, the sections were immersed in PBS containing 0.3% TritonX-100 for 15 min at room temperature to permeabilize the sections before antibody incubation. After incubating in Blocking One Histo buffer (06349-64, Nacalai Tesque, Inc., Kyoto, Japan) for 1 h at room temperature, the sections were incubated overnight with goat anti-CD31 (1 : 250, AF3628, R&D Systems), rat anti-CD206 (1 : 250, MCA2235, Bio Rad), mouse anti-CD163 (1 : 100, GTX42367, Gene Tex, CA, USA), rabbit anti-Iba1 (1 : 500, 019–19741, FUJIFILM Wako), rat anti-mouse CD102 (1 : 100, 553326, BD Pharmingen, NJ, USA), and goat anti-mouse PDGFRβ Antibody (1 : 150, AF1042, R&D Systems) at 4℃. On the following day, the sections were incubated with the Alexa Fluor 594 donkey anti-goat (1 : 400, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-mouse (1 : 400, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-rat (1 : 400, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-rabbit (1 : 400, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33,342 (346–07951, Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan). Fluorescence was detected with confocal laser scanning Olympus fluorescence microscope (FV3000, OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan) and All-in-one Fluorescence microscope (BZ-X800, KEYENCE, Osaka, Japan). Omission of primary antibodies served as a negative control and no immunostaining was detected in this group. Fluorescent images were loaded into BZ-X800 analyzer (KEYENCE). The positive area in each section was quantified using a BZ-X800 Analyzer. For each section, four regions from both the left and right cerebral cortices were selected for analysis. The positive area was expressed as the ratio of the total positive area to the total imaged area.

For hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, the sections were brought to room temperature, washed with PBS, and stained with hematoxylin (FUJIFILM Wako, 131–09665) followed by eosin ((FUJIFILM Wako, 050–06041). After staining, the sections were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and coverslipped using a permanent mounting medium. Representative images were acquired using a BZ-X810 fluorescence microscope (BZ-X800, KEYENCE, Osaka, Japan). The presence or absence of overt ischemic neuronal injury was assessed based on neuronal shrinkage, cytoplasmic eosinophilia, nuclear pyknosis, and disruption of tissue architecture by investigators blinded to the experimental groups.

Isolation of Mouse Brain Microvessels

Brain microvessels were isolated from mice as previously described [19]. Immediately after decapitation, the heads were quickly cooled in liquid nitrogen. The cerebral cortex was homogenized in ice-cold 15 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)−1-piperazineethanesulfonate (HEPES; pH 7.4) containing 147 mM NaCl, 4 mM KCl, 3 mM CaCl2, 1.2 mM MgCl2, 5 mM glucose, and 1 mM Na3VO4 (physiological buffer). The homogenates were centrifuged at 3,500 ×g for 10 min at 4 ℃ (Eppendorf Centrifuge 5425R), and the pellets were resuspended in 500 µL of 20% Ficoll PM400 (F4375-100G, Sigma). After centrifugation at 25,000 ×g for 12 min at 4 ℃ (himac CS120FNX), the pellets were resuspended in 1,230 µL of 15% dextran from Leuconostoc mesenteroides (D8821-50G, Sigma). The suspension was layered onto 769 µL of 20% dextran from Leuconostoc mesenteroides. After centrifugation at 25,000 ×g for 12 min at 4 ℃ (himac CS120FNX), the pellets were resuspended in 150 µL of RIPA buffer (final concentrations: 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 1% Nonidet P-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) with phosphatase inhibitors (Roche, Mannheim, Germany) and protease inhibitors (Roche) as the brain microvessel fraction. After sonication (Q125; QSONICA, Newtown, CT, USA), protein concentrations were measured by the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) method.

Preparation of Plasma Fractions

Mice were anesthetized via i.p. injection of the three-combination anesthetic. Heparinized Micro-Hematocrit Capillary Tubes (Cat No. 22–362-566, Fisher Scientific, PA, USA) were inserted into the orbital vein, and blood was collected into tubes. The collected blood was centrifuged at 8,000 ×g, 10 min, 4 ℃ (Eppendorf Centrifuge, 5425R), and the supernatant was fractionated as the plasma fraction. The protein content of the plasma fraction was measured using the BCA method.

Western Blotting

After measuring protein concentration, samples were incubated in sample buffer (62.5 mM Tris-HCl, pH 6.8, 10% glycerol, 2% SDS, and 5% β-mercaptoethanol) at 95 ℃ for 5 min. Western blotting was performed according to standard protocols with the following primary antibodies: mouse anti-β-actin (A5441, Sigma-Aldrich Corp., St Louis, MO, USA), rabbit anti-ZO-1 (61–7300, Invitrogen., Waltham, MA, USA), mouse anti-Claudin-5 (35–2500, Invitrogen), rabbit anti-Occludin (71–1500, Invitrogen), goat anti-PDGFRβ (AF1042, R&D systems), mouse anti-GFAP (556327, BD Biosciences., Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies. The immunoreactive proteins were detected using ImmunoStar Zeta (FUJIFILM Wako, Osaka, Japan), Immobilon ECL Ultra Western HRP Substrate (WBULS0100; Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA), and Immobilon Crescendo Western HRP Substrate (WBLUR0100; Millipore). Antibody binding was quantified by computerized densitometry (LuminoGraph II; ATTO Co., Tokyo, Japan) and an image analyzer (CS Analyzer; ATTO Co.).

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from the left cerebral cortex of mice using an RNA extraction kit (Isogen II, Nippon Gene, Tokyo, Japan). The extracted RNA concentrations were quantified using BioSpec-nano (Shimazu Corp., Kyoto, Japan). The cDNAs were synthesized from 500 ng of total RNA using ReverTra Ace® qPCR RT Master Mix with gDNA Remover (Toyobo Co., LTD., Tokyo, Japan). The qRT-PCR was performed using Thunderbird® Next SYBR® qPCR Mix (QPX-201, Toyobo Co., LTD.) in a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Data were normalized to GAPDH mRNA expression and analyzed using the 2−∆∆Ct method. The primers used in this study were as follows. GAPDH: forward, 5ʹ−GGTGAAGGTCGGTGTGAACG−3ʹ; reverse, 5ʹ−CTCGCTCCTGGAAGATGGTG−3ʹ, matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)−2: forward, 5ʹ−CAAGGATGGACTCCTGGCACAT−3ʹ; reverse, 5ʹ− TACTCGCCATCAGCGTTCCCAT−3ʹ, MMP-9: forward, 5ʹ− GCTGACTACGATAAGGACGGCA−3ʹ; reverse, 5ʹ−TAGTGGTGCAGGCAGAGTAGGA−3ʹ, Tjp1: forward, 5ʹ−GAGCTACGCTTGCCACACTGT−3ʹ, reverse, 5ʹ− TCGGATCTCCAGGAAGACACTT − 3ʹ, Cldn5: forward, 5ʹ− GCTGGCGCTGGTGGCACTCTTTGT−3ʹ, reverse, 5ʹ−GGCGAACCAGCAGAGCGGCAC − 3ʹ, Ocln: forward, 5ʹ−TGGATCGGTATGATAAGTCCAA−3ʹ, reverse, 5ʹ− CATAGTCAGATGGGGGTGGAG−3ʹ.

Statistical Analysis

All quantitative analyses, including immunofluorescence image analysis and western blot densitometry, were performed by investigators blinded to the experimental group allocation. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using Student’s t-test for comparisons between two groups and one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for comparisons involving multiple groups or variables, as appropriate. When ANOVA revealed a significant effect, Tukey’s multiple-comparison post hoc test was performed to evaluate differences between individual groups. Statistical significance was defined as a p value of less than 0.05 with a confidence level of 95%. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.5.0; GraphPad Software, LLC, CA, USA).

To confirm whether BCAS surgery induced a decline in CBF, CBF was measured 24 h after BCAS surgery using OMEGAZONE II. The cerebral blood flow was found to have decreased to approximately 60% of that observed in the sham-operated group in Fig. 1A and B (*P < 0.05 vs. the sham group).

CD206- and CD163-Positive Cells Surrounding Blood Vessels Decreased in the Cerebral Cortex on Day 3 After CLO Injection and 24 h After BCAS Surgery

In the CLO-treated group, the CD206- and CD163-positive areas, which are markers of PVMs, were decreased in the cerebral cortex at 24 h after sham or BCAS surgery in Fig. 2A–D (P < 0.0001:sham, P < 0.001:BCAS vs. PBS-treated group). Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of CLO treatment, whereas neither the main effect of BCAS surgery nor the interaction between BCAS and CLO treatment was significant. In contrast, no detectable morphological evidence of microglial activation was observed in the CLO-treated group at 24 h after BCAS in Fig. 2E and F, although our results do not exclude the possibility of subsequent microglial activation during later stages of cerebral hypoperfusion. Furthermore, to biochemically evaluate the reduction of PVMs at 24 h after BCAS surgery, CD206 protein levels were analyzed. As a result, CD206 protein levels were significantly decreased in the CLO-treated group compared with the PBS-treated group in Fig. 2G (P < 0.0001 vs. PBS-treated group). Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of CLO treatment.

Depletion of PVM-Enriched Macrophage Populations Attenuated BBB Dysfunction Under Conditions of Reduced CBF 24 h After BCAS Surgery

Evans Blue extravasation was evaluated to clarify the involvement of PVMs in BBB dysfunction under conditions of reduced cerebral blood flow after BCAS. The cerebral cortex of BCAS-operated mice showed significantly increased EB leakage into the brain parenchyma as compared to that in the cerebral cortex of sham-operated mice in Fig. 3 (P < 0.0001 vs. PBS-treated sham group). Furthermore, the CLO-mediated depletion of PVM-enriched macrophage populations suppressed the EB leakage in BCAS-operated mice in Fig. 3 (P < 0.05 vs. PBS-treated BCAS group). Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant interaction between BCAS surgery and CLO treatment, together with a significant main effect of BCAS surgery, whereas the main effect of CLO treatment was not significant.

The levels of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) in the total homogenate of the cerebral cortex of BCAS-operated mice were significantly higher than those in sham-operated mice. Depletion of PVM-enriched macrophage populations following CLO injection suppressed the increase in GFAP protein levels in BCAS-operated mice in Fig. 4D (P < 0.01 vs. PBS-treated BCAS group). Discussion

This study aimed to elucidate the pathophysiological role of perivascular macrophages in blood-brain barrier dysfunction induced by reduced cerebral blood flow. CBF was reduced to approximately 60% of the preoperative level 24 h after BCAS surgery, confirming the successful induction of cerebral hypoperfusion. To investigate the role of PVMs, we used clodronate liposomes (CLO), known to induce macrophage death via phagocytosis. Previous studies have demonstrated that intracerebroventricular injection of CLO depletes PVM-enriched macrophage populations without affecting microglia or astrocytes in the brain parenchyma [20], and that PVMs depletion occurs within 3–7 days after the injection [17, 21]. In preliminary experiments, we confirmed that CD206 protein levels were markedly reduced 3 days after CLO injection (Fig. S14). Accordingly, in the present study, CLO was injected 3 days prior to BCAS surgery. The reduced CD206 protein levels were maintained 24 h after sham and BCAS surgery, confirming that the experiments were conducted under conditions of reduced PVM-marker-positive macrophage populations. Under these conditions, BBB function was evaluated by the intravenous injection of Evans Blue. EB extravasation into the brain parenchyma significantly increased 24 h after BCAS, which is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that reduced CBF induces BBB disruption [22]. Importantly, CLO-mediated depletion of PVM-enriched macrophage populations significantly attenuated EB leakage. These findings suggest that the PVM-enriched macrophage populations may contribute to BBB dysfunction following CBF reduction.

To further clarify the mechanisms underlying PVMs-mediated BBB impairment, we focused on vascular-associated cells known to regulate BBB integrity. The level of PDGFRβ, a pericyte marker, was significantly decreased 24 h after BCAS surgery; this reduction was suppressed by depletion of PVM-enriched macrophage populations using both biochemical and histological analyses. These results suggest that PVM-associated responses may be linked to reduced pericyte-associated vascular integrity under conditions of reduced CBF. Similarly, the attenuation of GFAP upregulation by CLO treatment suggests that PVM-enriched macrophage populations may be associated with astrocyte activation after CBF reduction. Consistent with this notion, the mRNA expression levels of MMP-2 and MMP-9, enzymes known to be secreted by activated astrocytes, were elevated after BCAS and suppressed by CLO-mediated depletion of PVM-enriched macrophage populations. Although MMP-2 and MMP-9 are essential for extracellular matrix remodeling [23], their excessive expression is associated with inflammation and BBB dysfunction [24–26]. Taken together with previous reports, our findings suggest that PVMs may be associated with astrocyte activation and increased MMP-2/9 expression, which could contribute to BBB dysfunction.

Next, we analyzed the tight junction proteins that are central to BBB integrity. No change was observed in ZO-1 protein levels; however, claudin-5 and occludin protein levels increased 24 h after BCAS surgery, and these increases were suppressed by depletion of PVM-enriched macrophage populations. Similarly, the plasma levels of the 55-kDa occludin fragment were elevated after BCAS, and this increase was also attenuated by CLO-mediated depletion of PVM-enriched macrophage populations. Occludin has been reported to be cleaved by MMP-2 under ischemic conditions [27]. Furthermore, the 55-kDa occludin fragment has been shown to induce vascular endothelial cell death and BBB disruption [28]. In addition, it has been reported that under ischemic conditions, claudin-5 and occludin, which normally form tight junctions, are redistributed from the plasma membrane to the cytosol [29]. Considering the possibility of impaired autophagic processes in vascular endothelial cells under ischemia [29], the present findings may reflect the detection of tight junction proteins that do not contribute to functional cell-cell adhesion, but instead accumulate intracellularly without being adequately degraded through autophagy.

This study has several limitations. First, only male mice were used in the present study; therefore, potential sex-dependent differences in PVM-mediated BBB dysfunction after cerebral hypoperfusion remain to be determined. Second, the reduction in CBF occurred relatively rapidly, which may have induced inflammatory responses resembling those associated with acute ischemic injury. Histopathological examination of H&E-stained sections revealed no overt ischemic neuronal injury, including neuronal shrinkage, cytoplasmic eosinophilia, or nuclear pyknosis, in any of the experimental groups (Fig. S16). Although H&E staining revealed no evidence of ischemic neuronal injury, we cannot fully distinguish early responses to hypoperfusion at this time point from acute ischemia-induced inflammatory responses. Third, although our findings suggest that PVMs may contribute to reduced PDGFRβ-positive perivascular area, astrocyte activation, and BBB disruption after BCAS, the precise cellular sources of MMP-2 and MMP-9 were not determined. In addition, although clodronate liposomes are widely used to deplete PVMs, this approach may also affect other phagocytic macrophage populations. Nevertheless, because BBB disruption and altered pericyte-associated vascular integrity were primarily observed in parenchymal microvessels, where PVMs are strategically localized, PVMs may be important contributors to the vascular pathology observed after BCAS.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that PVM-enriched macrophage populations are associated with BBB dysfunction following CBF reduction, possibly through changes in pericyte-associated vascular integrity, astrocyte activation, and MMP-2/9-related tight junction remodeling.
