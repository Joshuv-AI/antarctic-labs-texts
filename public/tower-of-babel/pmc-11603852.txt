Estimating the impact of the first 2 years of the COVID-19 pandemic on tuberculosis diagnosis and treatment outcomes in Southeastern City in Iran: an interrupted time series analysis of the preceding 10 years of ecological data
Authors: Mehdi Sharafi, Maryam TalebiMoghaddam, Sakineh Narouee, Alireza Heiran, Mohsen Khaleghi, Ali Mouseli, Zahra Amiri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith shared modes of transmission and clinical symptoms the convergence of COVID-19 and tuberculosis (TB) might lead to reduced diagnosis and detection of TB, which is challenging for healthcare systems already strained by the pandemic's reach.MethodsThis ecological study investigated the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on TB surveillance over the first 2 years of the pandemic (March 2020 to February 2022) in southeastern Iran. Interrupted Time Series (ITS) analysis with the quasi-Poisson regression models was used to estimate the relative risk (RR) of TB diagnosis and treatment outcome counts, stratified by gender, case definition, involvement type, and treatment outcomes.ResultsThe ITS analyses showed a significant decrease in TB total cases (RR: 0.622 [95% CI: 0.487, 0.793], P < 0.001), new cases (RR: 0.632 [95% CI: 0.493, 0.810], P < 0.001) and recurrent cases (RR: 0.491 [95% CI: 0.247, 0.974], P < 0.001). In addition, recovery and treatment failure counts also showed significant decreases (RR: 0.751 [95% CI: 0.566, 0.996], P = 0.05; RR: 0.201 [95% CI: 0.054, 0.738], P = 0.02). Moreover, significant decreases are observed in both genders and involvement types (pulmonary and extrapulmonary TB). No significant change was observed for absent to treatment and death counts.ConclusionThe COVID-19 pandemic has negatively impacted TB diagnosis and treatments. Concerns are risen about the progress achieved in TB control.Keywords: Pulmonary TB, Extrapulmonary TB, Interrupted time series, COVID-19, Quasi-poisson regression, Relative risk

Introduction

TB is a fatal disease causing 1.4 million deaths worldwide annually. After COVID-19, this disease emerged as the second most significant cause of death from infectious diseases in 2020 [1]. COVID-19 and TB share similarities across different historical contexts, such as airborne transmission and common symptoms like fever and cough, which often entail social stigma [2]. While protective measures against COVID-19 can also mitigate TB transmission, concerns have arisen that the COVID-19 pandemic might negatively impact TB diagnosis, treatment, and prevention, reversing recent progress made in reducing its burden [3].

The results of the modeling analysis published in May 2020, predicted that disruptions in global health services could lead to 6.3 million excess new TB cases and 1.4 million excess deaths from 2020 to 2025 due to undiagnosed TB and treatment delays [4]. In addition to direct effects on health, COVID-19 has induced secondary effects including quarantine, economic challenges, temporary closure of outpatient clinics, and phobia of disease exposure, all of which have implications for healthcare services [3].

Iran's TB incidence trended downward in 2020, with 13 cases per 100,000 people. Treatment success rates for both new and relapsed cases reached 85% in 2019 [5]. The World Health Organization (WHO) indicates that the majority of TB cases occur in Asia and Africa, with over half of the cases reported in Asian countries and about a quarter in Africa. Specifically, China has a very high incidence, estimated at between 900,000 and 1.1 million cases, while India is estimated to have between 2.0 and 2.4 million cases. Collectively, these two nations represent approximately 38% of all TB infections worldwide [6]. The highest proportion of TB patients are still found in Sistan and Baluchestan, and Golestan Province [7].

The results of studies showed that due to the significant burden of TB, the impact of COVID-19 on TB should be urgently investigated, and mitigating strategies should be established [5, 8]. Previous research conducted in areas with a high burden of TB presents mixed findings on this issue. Smaller studies from specific treatment centers, cities, and provinces in countries such as China, Ethiopia, Zimbabwe, Eswatini, India, Kazakhstan, Uganda, and Mexico indicate a potential decline in treatment outcomes. Conversely, some smaller studies in Kenya, Malawi, Sierra Leone, and Indonesia, along with larger studies in Lesotho, Indonesia, Peru, Brazil, and Vietnam, have not shown signs of such a deterioration. More recent data suggests that, on a global scale, treatment outcomes have not been significantly impacted by the pandemic. Nonetheless, this overall trend may obscure important national and sub-national differences [9].

Research indicates that the COVID-19 pandemic has had a lasting impact on TB cases in Iran, leading to a significant decrease in TB incidence [10]. This decline is attributed to reduced transmission during quarantine, challenges in screening, and potential misdiagnosis [11]. However, previous studies have not utilized interrupted time series analysis (ITS), which is essential for measuring the effects of COVID-19 on the trends and predictions of TB. Therefore, the present study aimed to assess the impact of COVID-19 on TB diagnosis and treatment outcomes in southeastern Iran, using ITS analysis with ecological data from the pandemic's first two years. By employing this analytical method, we seek to provide a more nuanced understanding of the pandemic's impact on TB in this specific region.

Materials and methods

Study population

This ecological study investigated the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on TB surveillance over the first 2 years of the pandemic (March 2020 to February 2022), utilizing a comparison with the preceding 10 years' trend (October 2010 to February 2020). This study analyzed data from TB patients registered in the surveillance system at Iranshahr University of Medical Sciences. Iranshahr has a population of 1,328,363 and encompasses an area of 88,349 square kilometers, including cities such as Iranshahr, Delgan, Sarbaz, Dashtiari, Rask, Chabahar, Mehrestan, Fanuj, Qasrqand, Kanarak, Nikshahr, Zarabad, Lashar, and Bampur. The dataset used monthly TB diagnosis and treatment outcome counts, which were categorized by gender, case definition, involvement type, and treatment outcome.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All TB patients registered in the surveillance system at Iranshahr University of Medical Sciences from October 2010 to February 2020 were included. Patients with missing data regarding disease type and treatment outcomes were excluded from the study.

This study was approved by the Research Committee of Fasa University of Medical Sciences (Reference number: IR.FUMS.REC.1402.067).

Preparation of database

TB surveillance data included demographic information, involvement type (pulmonary or extrapulmonary), case definition (new case, recurrence), and treatment outcomes (recovery, failure, absence to treatment, death). Data was obtained from the Health Vice-Chancellor of Iranshahr University of Medical Sciences. Monthly counts, stratified by gender, case definition, involvement type, and treatment outcomes, were organized, with the pandemic announcement date (11 March 2020) marking the interruption point.

Statistical analysis

Interrupted Time Series (ITS) ITS analysis is a research design that evaluates the impact of an intervention or interrupting event on time-dependent outcomes. It involves collecting data before and after the intervention and comparing the trends to detect significant changes. We used an ITS model that included an over-dispersion argument and seasonal adjustment using Fourier terms, which was supposed as supposed to improve the accuracy of the estimates. To estimate the relative risk (RR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of TB diagnosis and treatment outcome counts, the quasi-Poisson regression model was used. In interrupted time series, Relative risk (RR) is calculated by comparing the incidence of an event in the intervention group to the incidence in the control group. The formula for calculating relative risk is RR = (Incidence after intervention – Incidence before intervention) ​/ Incidence before intervention [12]. This calculation helps assess the impact of an intervention by measuring the change in risk before and after the intervention.

The COVID-19 pandemic's effect on diagnosis and treatment outcomes was assessed by comparing observed values (actual number of diagnoses and treatment outcome counts during the pandemic) to predicted counterfactual values. The latter represents the expected outcome in the absence of the pandemic, estimated based on pre-pandemic trends and other factors. Through this comparison, the ITS model quantified pandemic-associated excess hazards. All analyses were conducted using R software version 4.3.1.

Results

The ITS analysis showed a significant decrease in total TB cases (RR: 0.622 [95% CI: 0.487, 0.793], P < 0.001) within the first 2 years of the COVID-19 pandemic (Fig. 1A). Additionally, significant decreases are observed in both males (RR: 0.614 [95% CI: 0.466, 0.808], P < 0.001) and females (RR: 0.629 [95% CI: 0.482, 0.822], P < 0.001) total TB cases (Table 1).

Similar trends were found for TB new cases and recurrent cases (Fig. 1B), with significant reductions evident in both (RR: 0.632 [95% CI: 0.493, 0.810], P < 0.001; RR: 0.491 [95% CI: 0.247, 0.974], P < 0.001). Moreover, significant declines characterized both pulmonary (RR: 0.369 [95% CI: 0.258, 0.527], P < 0.001) and extrapulmonary TB (RR: 0.694 [95% CI: 0.531, 0.906], P = 0.01) (Table 1).

Furthermore, for the treatment outcomes, recovery and treatment failure counts showed significant decreases (RR: 0.751 [95% CI: 0.566, 0.996], P = 0.05; RR: 0.201 [95% CI: 0.054, 0.738], P = 0.02). However, no significant change was observed for absent to treatment and death counts; though both parameters showed a decreasing trend (Table 1).

Discussion

The negative impact of TB in Iran can be attributed to socio-economic factors such as severe poverty, class differences between rich and poor, unemployment, illiteracy, immigration, and urban housing conditions. These factors contribute significantly to the prevalence of TB in this country [13].

Using the ITS models, this study assessed the excess hazard ratios (HRs) for the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on TB diagnosis and treatment outcome counts in southeastern Iran. A significant decrease was observed in total and newly diagnosed TB cases during the first 2 years of the COVID-19 pandemic. Apart from the direct health consequences of COVID-19, the pandemic's secondary effects, such as lockdowns, economic instability, illness and attrition among healthcare workers, overcrowded hospitals, and hospital anxiety, may have an impact on the provision of healthcare services. Though COVID-19 protective measures may also lessen TB transmission, concerns have been raised that COVID-19 could negatively impact TB disease diagnosis, treatment, and prevention, undoing recent improvements in TB case detection and death rates. Early modeling, released in May 2020, indicated that interruptions in TB treatment and underdiagnosis could result in 6 point 3 million more cases of TB and 1 point 4 million more TB deaths globally between 2020 and 2025 [14–17].

TB predominantly affects weak and vulnerable populations, exacerbating existing inequalities [18]. Despite its preventable and treatable nature, TB was a major global cause of death before the COVID-19 pandemic [19]. To address the considerable burden of TB on public health, the World Health Organization (WHO) launched the "End TB" strategy in 2015, aiming for a TB-free world by 2035 through a 95% reduction in TB deaths and a 90% reduction in new cases by 2035 [20].

However, the COVID-19 pandemic has emerged as a significant barrier to TB control efforts worldwide [21, 22]. An estimated one-quarter of the global population, approximately 2 billion people, have latent TB infection, and by July 2022, around 554 million cases of concurrent TB and COVID-19 infections have been confirmed [23]. Notably, the pandemic caused an estimated 1.4 million TB undiagnosed and untreated cases, resulting in half a million deaths [24]. TB and COVID-19 share similar characteristics, including predominantly attacking the lungs and causing symptoms like cough, fever, and shortness of breath. Coinfection of TB and COVID-19 can lead to poorer treatment outcomes, especially if TB treatment is interrupted [25–27].

The COVID-19 pandemic has profoundly impacted TB control efforts, resulting in significant reductions in both diagnoses and treatment adherence. A critical concern arising from this situation is the relationship between the decline in recovery rates and increases in treatment failures, alongside the static numbers of deaths and treatment absences during this period. Proportionality of Outcomes: The decline in the total number of diagnosed TB cases, coupled with the constant absolute numbers of deaths and treatment absences, leads to an elevated proportion of adverse outcomes among the diagnosed population. This trend is noteworthy as it suggests that a higher percentage of diagnosed individuals may be experiencing severe manifestations of TB. Contributing factors may include delayed diagnoses that result in more advanced disease at the time of detection and increased interruptions in treatment [28]. Clinical Implications: The observed rise in the proportion of deaths and treatment absences within a shrinking cohort of diagnosed cases underscores the urgent need for targeted interventions. Patients who remain in care may face intensified health challenges, thereby necessitating robust support systems for ongoing TB management in the context of concurrent COVID-19 responses [29]. Evidence indicates that inadequate management of TB can exacerbate existing health disparities, leading to poorer treatment outcomes [30]. Incorporating these considerations into our analysis highlights the imperative for vigilant monitoring of TB treatment outcomes, especially amidst declining diagnosis rates. Such insights are vital for informing public health strategies, emphasizing the necessity of sustaining comprehensive infectious disease management throughout the pandemic [31].

The pandemic's negative effects on diagnosing other diseases have also been reported globally. Some studies have demonstrated the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the surveillance of infectious diseases, such as HIV and TB in Iran [32, 33]. The pandemic of COVID-19 has affected the provision of Non-Communicable Disease prevention and control services in the primary health care system in Iran such as Cardiovascular risk assessment services, Pre-Diabetes Care, Hypertension Care, Early diagnosis and screening of breast cancer, Early diagnosis and screening of colorectal cancer [34, 35].

This could be due to the changes in healthcare routines, such as the redistribution of medical professionals and facilities for COVID-19 response. Such impacts on TB care and diagnosis have led to reductions in TB diagnoses and other notifications [20]. Reports indicate an 18% decrease in reported TB cases globally in 2020 compared to 2019 [18]. On the other hand, mitigating strategies like social distancing and temporary outpatient clinic closures, as well as phobia of COVID-19 exposure, could affect TB care, diagnosis, access, and treatment adherence. Reduced identification of high-risk cases, underreporting, and delayed diagnoses could heighten the risk of TB transmission, especially in close-contact scenarios [36]. The pandemic caused significant interruptions in TB prevention and care programs. A survey revealed that 42% of countries faced partial disruptions in TB case detection and treatment during the initial months of the COVID-19 outbreak. Additionally, many healthcare facilities noted a decrease in TB hospital discharges and outpatient visits for latent TB infections [37]. Also, concerns about contracting COVID-19 may have discouraged individuals from seeking care for TB symptoms, leading to underdiagnosis [38]. Moreover, COVID-19 could impact on TB testing and awareness due to symptom similarities with COVID-19; therefore, people who attended the centers were directed to specific COVID-19 services, and it is unlikely that they would be evaluated for TB [39]. Such delayed diagnoses, a decrease in hospitalizations, and greater clinical severity in TB cases have also been observed during 2020 compared to 2019 [40]. Several studies have been even showed a delayed diagnosis for other non-communicable diseases [41, 42]. Concerns regarding the risk of contracting COVID-19 may have deterred individuals from seeking medical attention for TB symptoms. This reluctance has likely contributed to both underdiagnosis and underreporting of TB cases [43]. The pandemic has strained healthcare resources, diverting attention and funding from TB control programs. Consequently, there has been a decrease in the identification and initiation of treatment for TB patients, further worsening the issue of underdiagnosis [44].

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, it utilized an ecological design, which depends significantly on the accuracy of record-keeping. This accuracy may have been affected during the COVID-19 lockdown. Second, we acknowledge the potential for ecological fallacy, which arises when generalizing results obtained from a population level to individuals. Additionally, our data was collected two years after the onset of COVID-19, and it remains uncertain whether extending the observation period would yield different results. Despite these limitations, our study holds significant policy implications for TB control efforts in this context. It suggests that the COVID-19 pandemic has impacted both the identification of TB cases and the success of anti-tuberculosis treatment. To mitigate the risk of TB re-emergence, the international community must intensify efforts to educate the public and encourage access to medical assistance. As new epidemics may arise in the coming years, it is essential to develop appropriate strategies to sustain TB control objectives.

Conclusion

The diversion of healthcare resources to combat the pandemic, the fear of COVID-19, and similar symptomology have negatively impacted TB diagnosis and treatments. Despite the challenges posed by the COVID-19 pandemic, sustaining focus on TB diagnosis and treatment is vital to protect the progress achieved in TB control. Such a commitment will contribute to reducing the disease burden and safeguarding public health.
