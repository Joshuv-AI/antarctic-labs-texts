Scapular morphology associated with rotator cuff tears: a three-dimensional shape analysis using homologous modeling
Authors: Ryo Tokita, Hajime Toda, Rui Imamura, Akira Sugi, Yuji Shibayama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe mechanisms underlying rotator cuff tears are not well established. To elucidate the morphologic characteristics of the scapula associated with these tears, a comprehensive analysis of its global shape is essential. This study employed homologous modeling techniques to investigate scapular morphology related to rotator cuff tears.MethodsScapulae were modeled homologously from 28 male patients (male tear group) and 30 female patients (female tear group) who had undergone arthroscopic rotator cuff repair at Sapporo Medical University Hospital. Principal component scores for each tear group were derived from previously calculated eigenvectors in a principal component analysis. Receiver-operating characteristic curves and area under the curve values were computed separately for males and females to evaluate morphologic features indicative of rotator cuff tears.ResultsCompared to controls, the male tear group presented scapulae that were vertically shorter and horizontally broader (principal component 1, area under the curve = 0.61), with an anterior inclination of the lateral scapular morphology, including the glenoid, acromion, and coracoid process, alongside an increased anteroposterior diameter of the supraspinous fossa (principal component 4, area under the curve = 0.60). Additionally, they displayed reduced scapular body curvature and a shortened anteroposterior diameter of the supraspinous fossa (principal component 8, area under the curve = 0.67). The female tear group revealed scapulae with an anteriorly displaced acromion, increased scapular body curvature, and an enlarged anteroposterior dimension of the supraspinous fossa (principal component 5, area under the curve = 0.69), or reduced scapular body curvature and a shortened anteroposterior diameter of the supraspinous fossa (principal component 8, area under the curve = 0.64).ConclusionA reduction in scapular body curvature is a critical morphologic characteristic associated with rotator cuff tears. Further research examining the relationship between these findings and the morphology of the humerus and thorax, as well as the periscapular musculature, may provide insights into the underlying mechanisms of rotator cuff tears.Keywords: Rotator cuff tear, Scapula, Principal component analysis, Computed tomography, Homologous modeling, Scapular curvature

Rotator cuff tears are a prevalent shoulder disorder among middle-aged and older individuals, often resulting in pain, muscle weakness, and limitations in daily activities. Despite their widespread occurrence, many underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood, and no effective preventive strategies have been established. Factors associated with rotator cuff tears include age, hand dominance, trauma history, smoking, and the morphology of the humerus and scapula.3,17,18,25 Among these factors, bony morphology has been extensively studied and is considered to be one of the important factors associated with the development of rotator cuff tears.8

In terms of scapular morphology, prior research has predominantly concentrated on the superior inclination of the glenoid and the lateral extension of the acromion relative to the glenoid.9,19 The critical shoulder angle, proposed as a composite index reflecting these 2 characteristics,16 has been widely investigated in relation to rotator cuff tears.5,7,22 However, findings have been inconsistent, with both positive and negative associations reported.4,6 Similarly, studies exploring indices beyond the critical shoulder angle have yielded conflicting results.11,19 While it is generally acknowledged that scapular morphology is associated with the onset of rotator cuff tears, the specific scapular features contributing to these tears remain inadequately defined.

Traditional research has typically focused on isolated structures such as the glenoid or acromion. Recently, studies have begun to examine global scapular morphology in relation to rotator cuff tears.21,24 These investigations have concluded that there is no distinct global scapular morphology associated with rotator cuff tears. However, these studies often had limited sample sizes and utilized relatively few vertices (landmarks) in principal component analysis (PCA). To enhance the external validity of morphologic analyses, it is crucial to capture variations in global scapular shape using a sufficiently large sample and to extract features specific to shoulder disorders. In this regard, Tokita et al23 employed the homologous modeling method on 500 scapulae and reported variations in global scapular morphology. By applying PCA to 3-dimensional vertex coordinates, the resulting principal components (PCs) can serve as universal variables representing morphologic variation, thereby facilitating the investigation of scapular features associated with various shoulder disorders.

The purpose of the present study was to elucidate the morphologic characteristics of the scapula in cases of rotator cuff tears using these PCs. We hypothesized that the features associated with rotator cuff tears are reflected not only in isolated morphologies such as the acromion and glenoid but also in the global scapular morphology.

Materials and methods

Collection of three-dimensional data

PCs representing variations in global scapular morphology were derived from homologous models of 250 males (male base group) and 250 females (female base group).23 Because these 500 individuals were included irrespective of the presence or absence of shoulder disorders, it is presumed that the cohort contained a proportion of cases involving rotator cuff tears, which are highly prevalent.

Pre-operative computed tomography (CT) images were analyzed for 28 male patients (mean age, 64.8 ± 8.9 years; male tear group) and 30 female patients (mean age, 68.9 ± 8.4 years; female tear group) who had undergone arthroscopic rotator cuff repair. The inclusion criterion was intraoperative confirmation of a complete supraspinatus tear. Exclusion criteria included significant bony deformities, advanced shoulder osteoarthritis (Hamada classification grade ≥3), and traumatic rotator cuff tears.

Creation of homologous models

The modeling procedure adhered to previously established methods.23 Using Mimics (ver. 23.0, Materialise, Belgium), the 3-dimensional shape of each scapula was segmented from CT Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine data. Subsequently, homologous modeling software (HBM-Rugle; Medic Engineering, Japan) was employed to establish a coordinate system: the medial base of the scapular spine was designated as the origin; the line connecting the medial base of the scapular spine to the glenoid center was defined as the X-axis; and the medial base of the scapular spine, glenoid center, and inferior angle were aligned within the XY plane (Fig. 1).

Settings of the 3-dimensional coordinate system.

A 3-dimensional scapular template consisting of 19,563 vertices was utilized. Forty landmarks (Fig. 2), developed by the authors and optimized for precise scapular modeling, were placed on both the template and each scapula. Homologous models of the scapulae were then generated based on these landmarks.

Landmarks and definitions utilized for constructing homologous models.

To isolate shape variation independent of size and posture, normalization was conducted. In biological PCA, the first PC often reflects size-related variation. Given that the objective of this study was to examine pure shape differences, generalized least-squares Procrustes alignment was applied to all homologous models to normalize for size and posture. The sum of distances from the centroid was employed for size normalization.

Statistical analysis

PCA was conducted on the 3-dimensional coordinates of all vertices from the 500 scapulae in the base group, following homologous modeling, using Human Body Statistica (National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology, Japan).23 To visually interpret morphologic variation, virtual scapular shapes were generated, ranging from −3 standard deviations (SD) to +3SD based on the SD of the PC scores.23 Using HBM-Rugle, PC scores for the tear groups were then calculated based on eigenvectors derived from the PCA of the base group. This approach allowed for the assessment of the morphology of the tear groups without altering the inherent characteristics of shape variation represented by each PC.

To identify PCs associated with rotator cuff tears, receiver-operating characteristic curves and areas under the curve (AUC) were calculated separately for males and females, utilizing PC scores from both the base and tear groups (with a significance level set at 5%). Statistical analyses were conducted using BellCurve for Excel (ver. 4.08; Social Survey Research Information Co., Ltd., Japan). To mitigate age-related effects, comparisons were restricted to age-matched subgroups: 185 males from the base group (age, 48–84 years) versus 28 males in the male tear group and 193 females from the base group (age, 51–87 years) versus 30 females in the female tear group.

Ethical considerations

This study received approval from the Clinical Research Review Board of Sapporo Medical University (approval number: 322-1211).

According to receiver-operating characteristic curve and AUC analyses, the PCs with an AUC of ≥0.6 for males were PC1, PC4, and PC8 (Fig. 3; AUC = 0.61, 0.60, and 0.67, respectively). In comparison to the male base group, the male tear group was characterized by negative directions for PC1 and PC4 and a positive direction for PC8 (Fig. 4). The male tear group demonstrated the following distinct features: a vertically shorter and horizontally wider scapula (PC1); an anterior inclination of the lateral morphology, including the glenoid, acromion, and coracoid process, along with an increased anteroposterior diameter of the supraspinous fossa (PC4); and a reduction in scapular body curvature accompanied by a smaller anteroposterior diameter of the supraspinous fossa (PC8).

Discussion

The objective of this study was to extract the morphologic characteristics of the scapula associated with rotator cuff tears through 3-dimensional analysis utilizing homologous models. In the male cohort, the analysis identified several characteristics indicative of rotator cuff tears: a shorter vertical axis and a longer horizontal axis of the scapula (PC1); anterior tilting of the lateral morphology, including the glenoid, acromion, and coracoid process, in the sagittal plane, along with a larger anteroposterior diameter of the supraspinous fossa (PC4); and a reduced curvature of the scapular body paired with a smaller anteroposterior diameter of the supraspinous fossa (PC8). In the female cohort, notable features included anterior displacement of the acromion, increased curvature of the scapular body, and a larger anteroposterior diameter of the supraspinous fossa (PC5), as well as a smaller curvature of the scapular body accompanied by a smaller anteroposterior diameter of the supraspinous fossa (PC8).

Previous studies have identified various scapular morphologic characteristics associated with rotator cuff tears, including the superior inclination of the glenoid,9 anterior tilt of the acromion,26 lateral extension,1,11,14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20 and lateral tilt.1,2,15, 16, 17, 18 In the current study, the anterior/posterior tilt of the acromion was linked to PC2 and PC4, lateral extension to PC3, and lateral tilt to PC7 (Supplement). However, with the exception of PC4, these characteristics did not demonstrate statistical significance as morphologic indicators of rotator cuff tears. This finding implies that analyses focusing solely on specific structures, such as the glenoid or acromion, may be insufficient to fully capture the morphologic features of scapulae in cases of rotator cuff tears. Given that the attachment sites of surrounding muscles extend across the entire scapular structure, it is plausible that mechanical stresses from these muscles contribute to variations in global scapular morphology. Thus, a comprehensive 3-dimensional analysis of the entire scapular shape, as conducted in this study, is essential for examining scapular morphologies that may predispose individuals to rotator cuff tears, considering the interrelationships among various structural components. At present, it remains unclear whether previously reported scapular morphologic parameters or the PCs identified in the present study are more useful for characterizing the morphologic features associated with rotator cuff tears; therefore, further investigation is warranted.

In both males and females, PC8 revealed that scapulae in cases of rotator cuff tears exhibited reduced curvature of the scapular body in the sagittal plane. The consistent observation of this morphologic characteristic across both sexes underscores its potential significance as a feature of the scapula relevant to rotator cuff tears. Two hypotheses may explain the relationship between scapular body curvature and rotator cuff tears.

The first hypothesis posits that individuals with a reduced supraspinous or subscapular fossa exhibit diminished muscle volume in the supraspinatus or subscapularis muscles. Observations of the morphologic variations of PC8 (Fig. 4) from the medial side indicate that scapulae in cases of rotator cuff tears not only demonstrate less curvature in the scapular body but also feature narrower supraspinous fossae and shallower subscapular fossae. It is assumed that the dimensions of the supraspinous and subscapular fossae are associated with the muscle volumes of the supraspinatus and subscapularis, respectively. Therefore, individuals with smaller fossae may have lower muscle volumes, which could potentially influence the ability of the rotator cuff muscles to maintain humeral head centering. This discrepancy could result in superior migration of the humeral head during shoulder elevation, potentially leading to subacromial impingement and an increased likelihood of rotator cuff tears. However, studies on primates have identified species in which scapular morphology strongly influences upper limb function, yet the sizes of the supraspinous and infraspinous fossae do not consistently correlate with muscle volume.12,13 Currently, no studies have investigated the relationship between the morphology of the supraspinous, infraspinous, or subscapular fossae and the muscle volume or function of the muscles attached to these fossae in humans. Because muscle function was not directly assessed in the present study, the relationship between muscle volume and the ability of the rotator cuff to maintain humeral head centering should be interpreted with caution.

The second hypothesis suggests that individuals with a less curved, more linear scapula are more likely to adopt an anteriorly tilted position when positioned on the curved dorsal surface of the thorax. A reduced curvature of the scapular body creates a gap between the thorax and scapula, which may facilitate anterior tilting of the scapula in relation to the thorax. As the scapula tilts anteriorly, the acromion also tilts forward, complicating the passage of the greater tuberosity of the humerus beneath the acromion during shoulder elevation. This phenomenon may contribute to subacromial impingement, a known precursor to rotator cuff tears.10 Although the present study did not assess changes in scapular alignment associated with shoulder motion, the observed reduction in scapular body curvature among rotator cuff tear cases suggests that certain scapular morphologies may promote anterior scapular tilt, thereby increasing the risk of rotator cuff tears. Because the present study did not assess the periscapular muscles, the relationship between scapular morphology and alignment should be interpreted with caution, and further investigation is warranted.

By conducting a comprehensive analysis of global shape, as demonstrated in the present study, we can explore the hypothesis that “a bone morphology that serves as a risk factor for rotator cuff tears may be compensated by other bone morphologies” or that “the onset of the condition may result from the overlap of multiple morphologic risk factors.” In this study, we extracted PCs that represent the simultaneous variation of numerous scapular morphologies that are associated with rotator cuff tears. Further investigation into how these morphologic features influence the mechanisms of rotator cuff tear onset may provide new insights into the pathogenesis of shoulder disorders.

This study has several limitations. The base group comprised CT images of subjects with an unknown presence or absence of shoulder disorders, which raises questions about the extent to which the identified variations in global scapular shape were influenced by shoulder pathology. Nevertheless, conditions that could significantly affect bone morphology, such as fractures and cuff tear arthropathy. Although the tear group was projected onto PC axes derived from the base group of 500 scapulae that was presumed to include a certain proportion of rotator cuff tears, the actual proportion of rotator cuff tears within the base group was not assessed. Additionally, the AUC values for the PCs associated with rotator cuff tears in this study ranged from approximately 0.6 to 0.69, indicating limitations in predictive accuracy. Moreover, due to the cross-sectional design of this study, we cannot establish a causal relationship with disease onset. Furthermore, adjacent osseous morphologies, such as the humerus and thorax, as well as periscapular muscular factors, were not included in the analysis. Future research should incorporate these adjacent bones and muscles to elucidate the functional significance of the scapular shape variations identified in this study.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that scapular morphology in cases of rotator cuff tears is characterized by a reduced curvature of the scapular body, particularly linked to smaller supraspinous fossae. These findings suggest that the global shape of the scapula plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of rotator cuff tears. Investigating the functional implications of these morphologic features may yield new insights into the mechanisms underlying rotator cuff pathology.
