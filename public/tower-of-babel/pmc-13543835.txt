Cr(VI) and DON co-exposure triggers intestinal epithelial cells ferroptosis and pyroptosis through Nrf2/NF-κB/NLRP3 to impair intestinal barrier function
Authors: Yueming Gu, Xinyuan Miao, Linrui Fan, Guodong Cai, Ruilong Song, Hui Zou, Jianhong Gu, Yan Yuan, Xuezhong Liu, Zongping Liu, Jianchun Bian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives Cr(VI) and DON are natural co-occurring pollutants in the environment. This study aimed to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying oxidative damage to the intestinal barrier and intestinal epithelial cell (IECs) toxicity induced by Cr(VI) and DON.MethodsThe target genes and signaling pathways were predicted using the network toxicology method. This was verified through cell intervention experiments with Nrf2 activator TBHQ, NF-κB inhibitor PDTC, and NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950.ResultsCr(VI) and/or DON aggravated inflammatory infiltration in the intestine, disrupted tight junction structures. Network toxicology revealed that ferroptosis and pyroptosis pathways are involved in regulating oxidative damage and barrier dysfunction in primary small IECs in broilers. Cr(VI) and/or DON induced oxidative stress and inhibited Nrf2 expression. They abnormally activated the NF-κB/NLRP3 pathway in IECs, thereby triggering ferroptosis and pyroptosis, and exacerbating oxidative damage to the intestinal barrier. Overexpression of Nrf2 and silencing of NLRP3/NF-κB could bidirectionally reverse oxidative stress, iron accumulation, inflammatory factor secretion and intestinal barrier dysfunction in IECs, inhibit the progression of ferroptosis and pyroptosis.ConclusionThe cross-regulation of ferroptosis and pyroptosis mediated by the bidirectional imbalance of the Nrf2/NF‑κB/NLRP3 axis constitutes the core molecular mechanism underlying intestinal epithelial barrier dysfunction and oxidative damage induced by co-exposure to Cr(VI) and DON.

Introduction

Consumers may come into contact with multiple food contaminants simultaneously in the food web, posing complex threats to animal and human intestinal health. Studies have shown that mixed contamination of deoxynivalenol (DON, vomitoxin), ochratoxin A (OTA), cadmium (Cd), chromium (Cr), arsenic (As), etc., is widespread in grains and foods in many regions of Iran and China [1,2]. Among them, the combined contamination of DON, Cr and As in the grains of the Dawen River Basin in Shandong Province, China, can induce inflammatory damage to the liver and esophagus [3]. Meanwhile, the contamination levels of Cr(Ⅵ) and DON in cereals and animal feeds from some regions of China are 0.55–5.25 mg/kg and 106–9762 μg/kg, respectively. However, the maximum detected concentration of Cr(VI) in soil can reach 99.6 mg/kg, and the peak contamination level of DON in wheat flour can reach 84,000 μg/kg, both of which significantly exceed the relevant limit standards. These pollutants can be enriched in cereals and transferred along the food chain, ultimately posing potential risks to human health [4–6]. DON is highly heat-resistant and highly stable, making it difficult to decompose during conventional food processing. It dominates contamination in cereals as well as animal products such as meat and eggs [7]. Relevant studies have shown that the contamination rate of DON in global wheat ranges from 60% to 70%, and the detection rate in animal feed reaches 96.4% in some areas [8]. In conclusion, high levels of Cr(Ⅵ) and DON in environmental pose a continuous potential threat to animal and human health and still require focused attention and in-depth research.

Notably, Cr(VI) and DON are persistent pollutants in the environment that are difficult to biodegrade. They mainly enter crops and agricultural products through contaminated irrigation water and the extensive use of phosphate fertilizers. Their potential for bioaccumulation is transferred into ecological networks via nutritional pathways, leading to the long-term persistence of combined pollution effects in the environment [9,10]. More importantly, as the main transmission route, the food chain allows Cr(VI) and DON to reach the animal's intestinal tract directly. As the body's primary line of defense against exogenous stimuli, the intestinal epithelium is composed of absorptive cells, endocrine cells, Paneth cells, and goblet cells [11]. A large number of studies have confirmed that damage to IECs can disrupt the function and integrity of the intestinal barrier, leading to the absorption of large amounts of foreign toxins into the bloodstream and causing systemic damage [7,12]. Therefore, it is particularly important and urgent to reveal the effects and injury mechanisms of combined exposure to Cr(Ⅵ) and DON on the small intestine of animals.

The intestinal barrier is a complex defense system that protects the intestinal mucosa from harmful substances in the lumen and maintains the homeostasis of the body. It is mainly composed of tight junction proteins (ZO-1, Occludin and Claudin-1) and the mucus layer (MUC2). The interaction of these proteins is central to maintaining the integrity of the intestinal barrier [13,14]. Multiple studies have found that under the toxic effects of Cr(Ⅵ) or DON, ferroptosis and pyroptosis can lead to intestinal barrier dysfunction, and tight-junction defects can damage intestinal epithelial cells [7,8,15,16]. Ferroptosis is a type of programmed cell death caused by imbalanced lipid oxidation within cells, which is closely associated with oxidative stress [17]. As a crucial negative regulatory transcription factor in oxidative stress, Nrf2 activation can initiate the transcription of HMOX1, NQO1, and GPX4, thereby protecting the intestinal epithelial cell membrane, inhibiting ferroptosis, and maintaining barrier integrity [18]. Meanwhile, the signal enhancement of Nrf2 can also effectively inhibit the activation of the NF-κB/NLRP3 pathway and alleviate pyroptosis [19]. This special form of cell death, which is mediated by activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, is accompanied by GSDMD activation, which promotes the formation of cell membrane pores and the release of mature inflammatory factors [20]. The secretion of IL-1β and IL-18 can stimulate intestinal epithelial cells, increase intracellular iron levels, cause metabolic dysfunction, induce lipid peroxide accumulation, and eventually trigger ferroptosis [21]. The occurrence of ferroptosis further exacerbates intestinal inflammatory injury, forming a vicious cycle. In view of this, whether the Nrf2/NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling axis serves as the main pathway underlying the loss of intestinal epithelial barrier function during Cr(VI) and DON co-exposure-induced oxidative damage to intestinal epithelial cells still requires further investigation.

The intestine is the main organ in the body for contact, metabolism and intake of Cr(VI) and DON. Intestinal epithelial cells are the core targets of external toxin attacks, and their proliferation and differentiation determine the integrity of the intestinal barrier. However, the combined threat of mixed pollutants is often underestimated when any single pollutant is evaluated in isolation. The exact molecular mechanisms and key targets of intestinal barrier dysfunction and intestinal epithelial cell oxidative injury exposed to Cr(VI) and DON remain unclear. Therefore, in this study, the ileum tissue of broilers and primary chicken embryo small intestinal epithelial cells were selected as the research objects. Based on the pathological injury characteristics of the ileum, combined with network toxicology and molecular docking techniques, the mechanism of action and core targets of intestinal epithelial oxidative injury induced by mixed toxins were screened. Further, through cell molecular experiments and the addition of intervention agents, the cross-regulation of Nrf2/NF-κB/NLRP3-ferroptosis-pyroptosis was evaluated to determine whether it was the main mediating pathway for the damage to small intestinal epithelial cells caused by combined Cr(VI) and DON exposure. This study not only clarified the potential association between ferroptosis and pyroptosis in intestinal oxidative injury caused by the combined toxicity of Cr(VI) and DON but also provided new insights into their interactions and key regulatory targets.

Materials and methods

Animal breeding and management

In this experiment, 48 one-day-old male Sanhuang broilers (weighing 35–50 g) were purchased from Yangzhou Xianglong Poultry Industry Co., Ltd. After 7 days of adaptive feeding, the chicks were divided into 4 groups using a randomized and single-blind method, with 6 cages in each group and 2 chicks in each cage (n = 12). That is, the control group (Con, 0 mg/kg), the hexavalent chromium group (Cr(VI) or Cr, 46.4 mg/kg = 10% LD50), the deoxynivalenol group (DON, 10 mg/kg, purity 99.5%), and the hexavalent chromium + deoxynivalenol group (Cr(VI) + DON, 46.4 mg/kg + 10 mg/kg). In this study, potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7) was used as the Cr(VI) source. Toxic models were established by supplementing K₂Cr₂O₇ (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.) and/or DON (Shanghai Pankao Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) into the basal diet. Broilers had free access to water and feed throughout the 42-day experimental period. In the single-layer cage rearing condition, the daily light exposure of the chicks was 14 h. The humidity and temperature are dynamically adjusted according to the growth stage. Regularly change the position of the cage. Subsequently, the euthanasia of the broilers was carried out using the carbon dioxide inhalation method. After the experiment, some of the ileal tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and 2.5% glutaraldehyde electron microscopy fixative (Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China, G1101 and G1102) for pathological morphological observation.

All animal experiments involved in this study were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Yangzhou University (No. 202501016).

Culture and modeling of primary chicken embryo small intestinal epithelial cells

Fertile eggs purchased from Zhejiang Lihua Agricultural Technology Co., Ltd. were placed in an incubator and incubated until embryonic day 18. The surface of the chicken embryos was carefully wiped and disinfected with cotton balls soaked in 75% alcohol. After laparotomy, the ileal tissues were isolated, and mesenteric and vascular tissues were removed. The ileal tissues were minced, washed with ice-cold PBS, and digested with type Ⅰ collagenase (neoFroxx GmbH, Germany, 9001-12-1) at 37 °C for 50 min. The digestion was then terminated by adding DMEM/F-12 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, U.S.A., 12400024) supplemented with 3% fetal bovine serum. After centrifugation, the cells were resuspended in fresh medium and seeded into cell plates at a density of approximately 1 × 106 cells/mL. The cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 24 h. Furthermore, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) of Cr(VI) and DON against chicken embryonic intestinal epithelial cells after 24-h exposure was determined using the CCK-8 assay. The detailed results can be found in Figure S1. Based on cell viability statistics, Cr(VI) at approximately 1/3 of its IC₅₀ (17.58 μmol/L) and DON at approximately 1/2 of its IC₅₀ (21.15 μmol/L) were selected as exposure doses to treat chicken embryonic intestinal epithelial cells for 24 h, so as to establish an in vitro injury model. Meanwhile, gradient concentrations of the intervention agents were screened using the CCK-8 assay, and the maximum non-cytotoxic concentration was selected to exclude adverse effects of these agents on cells (gradient concentrations and corresponding results are shown in Figure S2). Intervention models were established by treating intestinal epithelial cells with 10 μmol/L TBHQ (Nrf2 activator, HY-100489), 20 μmol/L PDTC (NF-κB inhibitor, HY-18738), and 6 μmol/L MCC950 (NLRP3 inhibitor, HY-12815; MedChemExpress) at the indicated working concentrations for 24 h.

Network toxicology

The SMILES information of DON was obtained from the PubChem database and imported into the Swiss Target Prediction (STP) platform to predict all of its potential, less-probable but toxicologically significant targets (probability cutoff threshold > 0). All the target genes of Cr(VI) were screened from the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD). Meanwhile, the GeneCards and Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) databases were searched using the keyword ‘intestinal injury’, and the disease-related targets were obtained after merging and deduplication. Thereafter, the SangerBox platform was used to intersect the targets of Cr(VI), DON and intestinal injury, thereby identifying common target genes among the three and generating a Venn diagram to visualize their relationships.

Protein‒protein interaction (PPI) analysis was performed for the intersecting genes associated with Cr(VI), DON, and intestinal injury using the STRING database. The species was restricted to gallus, the interaction confidence score was set to >0.15, and isolated nodes were hidden. The resulting data were imported into Cytoscape 3.10.0 to construct a core protein‒protein interaction network. Furthermore, the intersecting targets were submitted to the DAVID database for Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis, respectively. The distribution patterns of the enrichment results were visually displayed via the Sangerbox platform.

Molecular docking

First, the 2D structures of K₂Cr₂O₇ and DON were downloaded from the PubChem database. These structures were then imported into ChemDraw and Chem3D software to complete the conversion from 2D to 3D models. Next, the crystal structures of the target proteins were retrieved from UniProt and the RCSB Protein Data Bank. Using PyMOL, water molecules and irrelevant ligands in the crystal structures were removed. Finally, the downloaded small-molecule structures and target protein crystal structures were uploaded to the CB-Dock2 website for molecular docking.

Histopathological examination

Hematoxylin‒eosin (H&E) and injury assessment: The ileal tissues fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde were dehydrated, embedded, and sectioned. The tissue sections were dewaxed, hydrated, stained with hematoxylin and eosin in a double color, sealed with neutral resin, and observed under an optical microscope. Subsequently, the ‘measurement distance’ tool of the CaseViewer software (3DHISTECH-The Digital Pathology Company, Budapest, Hungary) was used to measure the villus height and crypt depth in three fields of identical size for each of the three ileal tissue samples. The degree of intestinal tissue damage was evaluated using Chiu's pathological scoring criteria [22], and the detailed scoring standards are shown in Supplementary Table S2.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM): the ileal tissues of broilers were pre-treated and then sent to Wuhan Servicebio Co., Ltd., to be made into copper grids. Subsequently, image collection and analysis were conducted using the HT7800 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Japan).

Prussian blue (DAB enhanced) staining: after deparaffinizing and rehydrating the intestinal tissue sections, they were immersed in Prussian blue staining solution. After adding the DAB solution, hematoxylin nuclear staining was performed again. The sections were dehydrated in a gradient manner and sealed with neutral gum. Microscopic examination was conducted.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence techniques: immunohistochemistry involves deparaffinizing the paraffin sections, followed by antigen retrieval, blocking endogenous peroxidase, serum blocking, incubation with primary and secondary antibodies, DAB staining, hematoxylin nuclear staining, and then sealing with neutral gum. The sections are observed under a light microscope. In contrast, for immunofluorescence, on the basis of the aforementioned immunohistochemical procedures, the sections were incubated with FITC-conjugated fluorescent secondary antibodies, counterstained with DAPI, and then adding an anti-fluorescence quenching agent for mounting. Photographs and records were obtained using a laser scanning confocal fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany). Finally, quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity was performed on cell immunofluorescence sections from three biological replicates using ImageJ. Three equal-sized non-overlapping visual fields were randomly selected per sample. Threshold segmentation was implemented with ImageJ default settings, and the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was calculated to characterize the relative expression of the target protein.

Biochemical testing

According to the manufacturer's instructions for commercial kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, the levels of total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD: A001-1-2), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-PX, A005-1-2), lipid peroxides (LPO, A106-1-3), malondialdehyde (MDA, A003-1-2), and total cellular iron content in primary chicken embryo intestinal epithelial cells were determined. In addition, the ferrous ion content in these cells was measured using a commercial kit purchased from Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Subsequently, chicken-specific ELISA kits (E-BC-K881-M) were used to detect the concentrations of LDH (Wuhan Saibei Biotechnology Co., Ltd., SP15710), IL-18, and IL-1β (Jiangsu Mabiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd., MB-2776A and MB-2905A) in the cell supernatant, respectively.

Real-time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from primary chicken embryo intestinal epithelial cells using TRIzol reagent (CWBIO Co., Ltd., CW3166). cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (CWBIO Co., Ltd., CW3406M). This cDNA was used as the template for PCR amplification, combined with primers and SuperStar Universal SYBR Master Mix for specific reactions (CWBIO Co., Ltd., CW3380). The obtained data were normalized to β-actin as the internal reference, and the relative mRNA expression levels of the target genes were calculated using the 2− ΔΔCT method. Gene sequences and primers were designed by searching the NCBI database and synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Detailed primer information is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Western blotting

The primary chicken embryo small intestinal epithelial cells were thoroughly mixed and homogenized with RIPA lysis buffer (CWBIO Co., Ltd., CW2333S) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail. The protein concentration was determined by the BCA method and then standardized. Subsequently, the total protein was accurately separated by electrophoresis using the pre-prepared 10% polyacrylamide gel. The separated bands were transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane, which was then blocked with 5% skimmed milk. The primary and secondary antibodies were incubated. Visualization was achieved using ECL chemical imaging solution (Suzhou NCM Biotech Co., Ltd., P2300) and a Tanon 5200 chemical luminescence imaging instrument (Shanghai Tianeng Technology Co., Ltd.). Finally, ImageJ software was applied to quantitatively analyze the gray values of the target protein bands from three biological replicates. GAPDH was used as an internal reference protein for normalization calculation. The relative expression of the target protein was calculated as the integrated gray value of the target protein divided by the integrated gray value of GAPDH in the same lane.

Statistical analysis

This study adopted the mean ± standard error (mean ± SE) as the data statistical form. After conducting a one-way ANOVN test using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, U.S.A.), visualization was completed using GraphPad Prism 9.3 (GraphPad Inc., La Jolla, CA, U.S.A.). Different lowercase letters (a, b, c, etc.) indicated statistically significant differences between groups (p < 0.05). Additionally, the Mantel test function in R language was applied for the Mantel test, and the correlation coefficient of multiple groups of data was calculated using the Pearson method. p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), and p < 0.001 (***) indicated statistically significant changes.

Results

Effects of Cr(VI) and/or DON exposure on pathological morphology, ferroptosis and pyroptosis in broilers ileal

As shown in Figure 1A–C, compared with the control group, exposure to Cr(VI) and/or DON resulted in rupture and loss of intestinal villi, vacuolization of the mucosal layer, obvious structural damage, and marked inflammatory infiltration. Histopathological scoring of the ileum revealed increased Chiu's injury scores, decreased villus height, and increased crypt depth, leading to a significantly reduced in the ratio of these two parameters (p < 0.05). Transmission electron microscopy demonstrated that Cr(VI) and/or DON exposure caused disordered arrangement of intestinal microvilli, mitochondrial atrophy with reduced cristae, blurred structures of tight junctions and desmosomes, discontinuous gaps as well as early perforations in the local cell membrane. These manifestations were consistent with the characteristic morphological changes of ferroptosis and pyroptosis (Figure 1D). Furthermore, Prussian blue staining and immunohistochemical analysis showed that the staining area of the ileal epithelium in the poisoned group of chickens increased, suggesting significant iron overload and positive expression of GSDMD (Figure 1E and F). The above results confirm from the pathological morphology level that Cr(VI) and/or DON exposure can induce ferroptosis and pyroptosis in the ileum of broilers, and aggravate intestinal tissue damage.

Network construction and molecular docking of potential targets associated with Cr(VI), DON, and intestinal injury

As shown in Figure 2A, a total of 2980 potential targets for Cr(VI), 100 potential targets for DON, and 9999 potential targets for intestinal injury were screened using multiple databases, including Swiss Target Prediction, CTDbase, and GeneCards. After taking intersections, 32 overlapping target genes were identified. Based on the above targets, the PPI interaction network was plotted using String and cytoscape, obtaining 30 nodes and 125 edges, and core target genes such as NLRP3 and IL-1β were screened out (Figure 2B). Furthermore, GO enrichment analysis revealed that the biological processes of the common targets were significantly enriched in ‘response to wounding’ and ‘positive regulation of inflammatory response’. The cellular components were associated with ‘lysosome’ and ‘chromatin’, while the molecular functions included ‘diacylglycerol-dependent serine/threonine kinase activity’ and ‘transcription coactivator binding’. KEGG pathway analysis showed that these target genes were significantly associated with pathways including the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, ferroptosis, Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, and vascular smooth muscle contraction (Figure 2C and D). It is noteworthy that molecular docking verification of the mixed toxin with the core targets showed that the binding energies of DON to NLRP3 and IL-1β were −6.4 kJ/mol and −6.1 kJ/mol, respectively, and a binding energy less than −5 kJ/mol indicated a relatively stable interaction relationship. The binding energies of Cr(VI) to NLRP3 and IL-1β were −3.8 kJ/mol and −4.2 kJ/mol, respectively. Since Cr(VI) exists in its complete chromate ion form, which is an inorganic oxygen-containing anion small molecule, it is prone to electrostatic repulsion with the target protein and is difficult to be accurately calculated by CB-Dock2 (Figure 2E).

Effects of Cr(VI) and/or DON exposure on oxidative stress, tight junction protein expression, ferroptosis and pyroptosis in primary chicken embryo small intestinal epithelial cells

As shown in Figure 3A, Cr(VI) and/or DON significantly decreased the activities of T-SOD and GSH-PX and increased the accumulation of LPO and MDA in primary chicken embryo intestinal epithelial cells (p < 0.05). To further evaluate the damaging effects of Cr(VI) and/or DON on the intestinal barrier, the expression of mucins and tight junction proteins was detected by indirect immunofluorescence, QPCR and Western blot in this study. The results showed that the expressions of MUC2, ZO-1, Occludin and Claudin-1 in the small intestinal epithelial cells of the poisoning group were significantly inhibited (p < 0.05) (Figure 3B–E). Meanwhile, the results shown in Figure 3F indicate that the expression levels of Keap1 and ACSL4 significantly increased after exposure to Cr(VI) and/or DON, while the mRNA expression levels of Nrf2, HO-1, NQO1, FTH1 and GPX4 showed differential decreases, and the protein expression trends were consistent with the transcriptional levels (p < 0.05). Similarly, compared with the control group, the classical pathway of pyroptosis was activated in the intestinal cells of the poisoning group. The expression levels of TLR4, MYD88, NF-κB p65, NLRP3, Caspase-1, ASC, GSDME, GSDMD-N, IL-18 and IL-1β mRNA or protein all showed a varying degree of increase trend (p < 0.05) (Figure 3H). Furthermore, Cr(VI) and/or DON led to a significant increase in the total iron and ferrous ion contents within the intestinal cells, and the contents of IL-1β and IL-18 in the cell supernatant also showed a corresponding upward trend, accompanied by the activation of lactate dehydrogenase. Notably, these indicators were most significantly increased in the combined exposure group (p < 0.05) (Figure 3G and I). This indicates that the toxic effects in the combined poisoning group were significantly stronger than those in the individual Cr(VI) or DON groups, and the differences between the groups were statistically significant. In summary, Cr(VI) and/or DON can cause oxidative stress in intestinal epithelial cells, induce ferroptosis and pyroptosis, and lead to abnormal expression of intestinal barrier proteins, thereby causing mechanical and chemical barrier damage to the intestine.

Nrf2 activation alleviates ferroptosis, pyroptosis and barrier dysfunction of small intestinal epithelial cells induced by the combination of Cr(VI) and DON

As shown in Figure 5A, activation of Nrf2 can effectively counteract the oxidative stress in small intestinal epithelial cells caused by combined exposure to Cr(VI) and DON. Meanwhile, the expression levels of MUC2, ZO-1, Occludin and Claudin-1 in small intestinal epithelial cells exposed to the mixed toxin were significantly decreased, and the addition of Nrf2 activator alleviated this consumption (p < 0.05, Figure 5B–E). The results showed that, compared with the combined exposure group, the TBHQ intervention significantly upregulated the expression of Nrf2, HO-1, NQO1, FTH1 and GPX4. It attenuated the elevation in mRNA expression levels of Keap1, ACSL4, NF-κB p65, NLRP3, Caspase-1 and GSDME. Moreover, the expressions of key proteins showed changes in corresponding trends to varying degrees (Figure 5F). Notably, Nrf2 activators reduced iron accumulation, inflammatory cytokines, and LDH release in primary chicken embryo intestinal epithelial cells (Figure 5G and H). In conclusion, overexpression of Nrf2 effectively inhibited Cr(Ⅵ) and DON combination-induced oxidative stress and Nrf2-ferroptosis in chicken embryo intestinal epithelial cells, thereby suppressing pyroptosis through the Nrf2/NF-κB/NLRP3 pathway and protecting the intestinal barrier.

NLRP3 inhibition resists pyroptosis, ferroptosis and barrier damage of chicken embryo small intestinal epithelial cells induced by the combination of Cr(VI) and DON

To further investigate whether NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis serves as a key pathway for intestinal epithelial cell injury induced by mixed toxins, as well as the regulatory effect of pyroptosis on ferroptosis. It was known through in vitro cell experiments that MCC950 could enhance the activities of T-SOD and GSH-PX and alleviate the significant increase in LPO and MDA contents in small intestinal epithelial cells caused by combined exposure to Cr(VI) and DON (p < 0.05) (Figure 6A). It is worth noting that the results in Figure 6B–E indicate that the addition of NLRP3 inhibitors can significantly enhance the expression of ZO-1, Occludin and Claudin-1, increase the fluorescence signal of MUC2, and slow down the intestinal barrier injury caused by the combined exposure of Cr(VI) and DON (p < 0.05). In addition, compared with the combined poisoning group, the Cr+DON+MCC950 group significantly reduced the expression level of NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis related factors, decreased secretion and release of LDH, IL-1β, and IL-18, and inhibited activation of the upstream regulatory factor NF-κB p65. This further restorative activation of Nrf2, HO-1, FTH1, and GPX4 significantly down-regulating the expression level of ACSL4 mRNA or protein, and reduces iron accumulation in epithelial cells (p < 0.05) (Figure 6F–H). This suggests that inhibition of NLRP3 can not only alleviate oxidative stress in intestinal epithelial cells induced by combined Cr(VI) and DON exposure and prevent activation of the canonical NLRP3-pyroptosis pathway, but also negatively regulate ferroptosis via the Nrf2/NF-κB/NLRP3 axis, thereby preserving intestinal barrier function.

NF‑κB p65 inhibitor reverses ferroptosis and pyroptosis induced by Cr(VI) and DON via regulating the Nrf2/NF‑κB/NLRP3 pathway

This study aimed to evaluate whether NF-κB p65 is an important molecule that mediates the toxic effects of Cr(VI) and DON. As shown in Figure 7A, PDTC can reduce the contents of LPO and MDA, activate T-SOD and GSH-PX, and alleviate the antioxidant dysfunction in intestinal cells caused by combined exposure to Cr(VI) and DON. Meanwhile, NF-κB p65 inhibitors can counteract the intestinal barrier damage caused by the mixed toxin, restore the expression of tight junction proteins, and significantly increase the fluorescence intensity of MUC2 (p < 0.05) (Figure 7B–E). Furthermore, in order to further verify the regulatory mechanism of NF-κB p65 in the process of ferroptosis and pyroptosis induced by the combination of Cr(VI) and DON. It can be seen from Figure 7F–H that under the action of combined toxins, the silencing of NF-κB p65 can reverse activate the expressions of Nrf2, NQO1, HO-1 and GPX4, and reduce iron deposition in intestinal epithelial cells. It significantly inhibited the activation of TLR4, NF-κB p65, NLRP3, Caspase-1, GSDMD and GSDMD-N, prevented the release of mature pro-inflammatory factors, and thereby reduced the activity of LDH (p < 0.05). In summary, there is a significant synergistic effect between ferroptosis and pyroptosis, and NF-κB p65 acts as a key hub regulating these two forms of cell death. This reveals that downregulation of NF-κB p65 reverses ferroptosis and pyroptosis in intestinal cells induced by combined Cr(VI) and DON exposure via regulating the Nrf2/NF-κB/NLRP3 pathway, thereby ameliorating damage to the intestinal mucus layer and cellular layer.

Discussion

As ubiquitous pollutants in nature, Cr(VI) and DON pose a serious threat to the ecological environment and animal health. It has been reported that the intestine, as the main site of absorption and metabolism, is not only the primary target for the interaction between harmful substances in feed and the animal body, but also the first line of defense for the body to resist external toxic stimuli [23]. Previous studies have confirmed that the intestinal health of animals or humans is significantly negatively regulated by Cr(VI) or DON, resulting in impaired intestinal morphology, abnormal release of inflammatory factors, and intestinal dysfunction [24,25]. Based on histopathological observations of intestinal tissues, the results of this study indicate that exposure to Cr(VI) and/or DON induces inflammatory cell infiltration, and causes a significant decrease in the ratio of villus height to crypt depth. The reason might be that the increase in crypt depth is a compensatory feature of villus shortening, and the process of interaction between intestinal epithelium and stroma intensifies inflammatory cell infiltration and cytokine release [26]. More importantly, ultrastructural observations revealed that, compared with the control group, the poisoned group exhibited mitochondrial pyknosis, reduced cristae, blurred tight junction structures, and cellular membrane gaps and early perforations, presenting typical morphological characteristics of ferroptosis and pyroptosis. Multiple studies have reported that one of the significant manifestations of intestinal injury induced by Cr(VI) or DON is ferroptosis, which promotes mitochondrial vacuolation and the disappearance of mitochondrial cristae in cells, disrupting the iron homeostasis balance in intestinal tissue [15,27]. Meanwhile, Cr(VI) or DON can induce mature NLRP3 inflammasomes to execute pyroptosis via GSDMD cleavage, thereby triggering intestinal toxicity [16,23]. The presence of iron ion deposition and increased positive expression of the pyroptosis marker GSDMD in intestinal tissues further confirmed the above findings. These pathological features further confirmed that exposure to Cr(VI) and/or DON can induce ferroptosis and pyroptosis, endangering intestinal epithelial health and intensifying intestinal susceptibility.

Based on the preliminary predictive analysis of network toxicology, this study identified 32 potential target sites related to the combined induction of intestinal injury by Cr(VI) and DON. Among them, the core targets NLRP3 and IL-1β may amplify the impact on intestinal injury through interactions among multiple pathways. Previous studies have shown that the NLRP3 inflammasome is an essential molecular platform for the maturation and secretion of IL-1β. Persistent stimulation by Cr(VI) or DON leads to excessive activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, promoting the release of inflammatory factors (IL-18 and IL-1β) through pyroptotic membrane pores and forming an initial amplification node of an inflammatory signal, which in turn impairs intestinal epithelial cell function and causes intestinal tissue damage [28,29]. In network toxicology analysis, enrichment analysis is one of the important approaches to comprehensively explore the biological functions and signaling pathways of target genes [30]. These target genes are mainly significantly enriched in the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, ferroptosis and Toll-like receptor signaling pathway processes. They induce intestinal damage through biological processes such as ‘response to wounding’ and ‘positive regulation of inflammatory response’, thereby providing a molecular-level understanding of the combined toxicity of Cr(Ⅵ) and DON. When cells are stimulated by toxic substances, the co-stimulation of TLR and NOD1/2 will trigger the synergistic activation of NF-κB, initiate the expression of NLRP3, and assemble NLRP3 to activate the inflammasome to activate caspase-1, promoting the maturation of IL-1β/IL-18 and pyroptosis, thereby inducing inflammatory lesions [31,32]. The essence of ferroptosis is a dual imbalance between lipid peroxidation and the antioxidant defense system, which directly damages the integrity of the intestinal epithelial cell membrane through the accumulation of toxic metabolites [33]. In addition, this study simulated the binding mode and affinity of Cr(VI)/DON with key proteins through molecular docking. The results showed that the binding energies of NLRP3 and IL-1β with DON both reached the effective binding threshold of ≤−5 kJ/mol, whereas their binding energies with Cr(VI) were >−5 kJ/mol. This is mainly because Cr(VI) enters the cell in the form of a chromate anion (CrO₄2 −), and the interaction with proteins is mainly through electrostatic forces and hydrogen bonds, which are different from those of hydrophobic organic small molecule ligands. The binding energy threshold established for organic small molecules (≤−5 kJ/mol) is not entirely applicable to inorganic ion-type ligands [34]. In summary, this study integrated network toxicology and molecular docking techniques to construct a multidimensional regulatory network of ‘Cr(VI)/DON-toxic target-effector pathway-intestinal injury’ from the holistic perspective of biological networks, further improving the efficiency, depth, and prediction accuracy of toxicological analysis.

In recent years, oxidative stress and intestinal barrier damage have been important causes of a series of intestinal diseases [35]. A growing number of studies have shown that Cr(VI) or DON can destroy the mitochondrial structure and induce oxidative stress [8,36]. Among them, LPO and MDA are intermediate and terminal products of the lipid peroxidation process, which can cause lipid peroxidation damage to cells [37]. T-SOD and GSH-PX can effectively protect cell structure and function from oxidative damage [16]. Research has found that oxidative stress in porcine epithelial cells significantly increases the activity of LDH. As an important indicator reflecting the pathological conditions of the body, excessive release of LDH can cause barrier dysfunction in intestinal epithelial cells [38]. This is consistent with our test results. Cr(VI) and/or DON cause LPO and MDA to accumulate in primary chicken embryo intestinal epithelial cells, inducing a large amount of LDH secretion and release, and further invading intestinal epithelial cells and damage the intestinal barrier by inhibiting the expression levels of ZO-1, Occludin and Claudin-1 proteins, as well as significantly reducing the fluorescence signal intensity of MUC2. An, Zhou and Maloy et al [39] found that damage to intestinal epithelial cells is a typical feature of intestinal barrier dysfunction, which reduces resistance to external stimuli and directly affects the intestinal health status. The tight junctions are a specific structure existing between adjacent epithelial cells. Their reduction or absence will increase the permeability of the intestinal epithelial space, allowing harmful substances to enter the systemic circulation and damaging the integrity of the intestinal epithelium [40]. As the main protein component of the intestinal mucosal layer, MUC2 combines with a small amount of mucin to form an insoluble mucus barrier, which protects intestinal epithelial cells [41]. More importantly, molecular biological verification in intestinal epithelial cells showed that Cr(VI) and/or DON downregulated the expression levels of Nrf2, HO-1, NQO1, FTH1 and GPX4, while upregulating the expression of Keap1, ACSL4, TLR4, MYD88, NF-κB p65, NLRP3 and downstream pathway factors, aggravating intracellular iron ion accumulation and the release of IL-1β and IL-18. Several studies have demonstrated that K₂Cr₂O₇-induced lung injury in rats reduces the levels of Nrf2, HO-1 and NQO1, disrupts the body's redox balance, and slows the excretion of toxic substances [42]. DON can reduce the expression of HO-1, NQO1 and GPX4 in the liver of mice through the Nrf2 pathway, leading to intensified lipid peroxidation and thereby inducing ferroptosis [43]. Similarly, Cr(VI) can also destroy the structure of chicken embryo fibroblasts (DF-1) and induce pyroptosis [44]. And DON exposure leads to the recruitment of NLRP3, ASC and Pro-Caspase-1 in the jejunal tissue of mice, activates GSDMD and releases active IL-1β and IL-18 [45]. Notably, most target genes in this study exhibited consistent alteration trends between mRNA and protein expression, while slight discrepancies were observed for only a small number of molecules. mRNA abundance cannot fully equate to protein abundance, because gene expression is regulated by multi-layered mechanisms including mRNA degradation efficiency, translational control and protein post-translational modification [46]. Under cellular stress or exogenous toxicant stimulation, mRNA levels can only partially predict protein abundance, and a reduced correlation between transcription and protein expression represents a common biological phenomenon [47].

Although cell death is an important mechanism for renewing epithelial cells to maintain the normal morphology, structure and function of the intestine, when animals are subjected to oxidative stress stimulation, excessive inflammatory changes in the body can lead to abnormal death of intestinal epithelial cells and damage the intestinal barrier [48]. Pearson correlation analysis also confirmed this. There were significant correlations between pathological damage, oxidative indicators, and intestinal barrier factors and ferroptosis, pyroptosis. Meanwhile, ferroptosis and pyroptosis are two distinct forms of programmed cell death in terms of morphology and mechanism. Although they are essentially different, they also inevitably interact with each other [49]. In this experiment, there was a significant negative correlation between Nrf2, HO-1, GPX4 and NLRP3, and GSDME, indicating that ferroptosis and pyroptosis have a synergistic effect on intestinal epithelial injury caused by Cr(VI) and/or DON. Meihe et al [50] demonstrated that ferroptosis inducers (erastin) drive the activation of the NLRP1 and NLRP3 inflammasomes in cells. After silencing the expression of NLRP1, the levels of GSH and GPX4 can be increased, thereby inhibiting the occurrence of ferroptosis. These findings suggest that oxidative stress and barrier dysfunction may be key molecular events in the mechanism underlying intestinal toxicity induced by Cr(VI) and/or DON. Moreover, the synergistic effect of ferroptosis and pyroptosis driven by the Nrf2/NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling axis plays an important role in intestinal epithelial cell injury caused by Cr(VI) and/or DON.

At present, the problem of co-contamination of heavy metals and mycotoxins in food and feed is becoming increasingly prominent. Previous studies have shown that co‑exposure to DON and Pb induces nephrotoxicity via ferroptosis; combined treatment with AFB1 and Cd triggers autophagosome accumulation and apoptosis. Co‑exposure to DON with Cr, As causes intestinal gland atrophy, epithelial necrosis and exfoliation, as well as inflammatory infiltration [3,51,52]. This study observed that combined exposure of Cr(VI) and DON had the most significant damaging effect on small intestinal epithelial cells. Therefore, based on this co-damaging model, intervention experiments were further conducted by combining the Nrf2 agonist TBHQ and the NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950, aiming to elucidate the causal mechanism of intestinal damage mediated by the Nrf2/NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling axis. The results showed that the interventions of TBHQ and MCC950 could alleviate the oxidative stress in chicken embryo small intestinal epithelial cells induced by the combined exposure to Cr(VI) and DON. By inhibiting the Nrf2/NLRP3-ferroptosis-pyroptosis pathway, they reduced the accumulation of cellular iron and the abnormal release of inflammatory factors, thereby restoring the expression of tight junction proteins and MUC2 and maintaining intestinal barrier homeostasis. As a transcription factor highly sensitive to oxidative stress, Nrf2 is activated in cells treated with TBHQ, promoting the upregulation of the downstream phase II detoxification enzymes HO-1, NQO1 and the antioxidant gene GPX4, in order to resist the oxidative‒reductive imbalance caused by external stimuli and reduce the occurrence of ferroptosis [53]. GPX4 is a key defense line for cells against ferroptosis, and its antioxidant effect can negatively regulate pyroptosis induced by the NLRP3 inflammasome [54]. Similarly, MCC950 is a highly efficient NLRP3 small molecule inhibitor that plays a key protective role in various pyroptosis-mediated diseases [55]. The addition of MCC950 inhibits the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome, prevents Caspase-1 from cutting GSDMD and cell membrane rupture, protects the osmotic potential of cells, and enables cells to respond to the damage caused by pyroptosis [56]. Interestingly, the research found that the inhibition of GSDMD would increase the expression of GPX4 protein, alleviate ferroptosis caused by the accumulation of peroxide toxicity, maintain the homeostasis of the membrane lipid bilayer, and exert a cytoprotective effect [57,58]. In addition, in order to further verify the mutual regulatory relationship between ferroptosis and pyroptosis, and to determine the regulatory effect of NF‑κB on this mechanism. The present study found that PDTC-treated intestinal epithelial cells showed recovery of antioxidant capacity and intestinal barrier function. Inhibition of the NF-κB signaling effectively restored the expression of Nrf2 and its downstream antioxidant factors NQO1, HO-1, and GPX4. It blocked the excessive activation of the NF-κB p65/NLRP3 pathway induced by co-exposure to Cr(VI) and DON. As a thiol compound, ammonium pyrrolidine dithiocarbamate (PDTC) inhibits the release of inflammatory factors in intestinal epithelial cells by suppressing IκB phosphorylation and preventing the nuclear translocation of NF-κB [59]. Meanwhile, some studies have shown that the overexpression of Nrf2 can inhibit the activation of the IκBα/NF-κB pathway. NF-κB is a key factor involved in the initiation of the inflammasome. Inhibiting NF-κB can prevent the assembly of the NLRP3/ASC/Pro-Caspase-1 inflammasome in cells, reduce the expression of GSDMD and the release of LDH and IL-1β, and alleviate cell pyroptosis [60,61]. Conversely, when cells are stimulated, inflammatory factors in turn further activate NF-κB. Activation of the NF-κB subunit p65 can negatively regulate Nrf2 activation, participate in the negative regulation of the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway, and trigger ferroptosis [62,63]. In summary, Cr(VI) and DON can mediate ferroptosis and pyroptosis through the Nrf2/NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling axis, thereby causing damage to chicken embryo intestinal epithelial cells. NF-κB serves as a key target regulating the synergistic effect of ferroptosis and pyroptosis under the combined toxicity of the two toxins.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study verified that the intestinal barrier injury induced by combined exposure to Cr(VI) and DON was significantly more severe than that induced by single exposure to either toxin alone. The bidirectional imbalance of the core Nrf2/NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling axis mediates the crosstalk regulation between ferroptosis and pyroptosis, which serves as a pivotal factor inducing intestinal barrier dysfunction and IECs injury. Cr(VI) and DON inhibit Nrf2 expression, activate the NF-κB/NLRP3 pathway, trigger a synergistic amplification effect of ferroptosis and pyroptosis, and exacerbate intestinal epithelial oxidative injury. Blocking the signal transmission of the Nrf2/NF-κB/NLRP3 axis can effectively activate the antioxidant defense system in the intestine. This study systematically reveals the molecular mechanisms and cellular effects of small intestinal epithelial injury induced by combined exposure to Cr(VI) and DON, providing a novel theoretical framework and research strategy for targeted therapy and toxicity evaluation of mixed toxins.
