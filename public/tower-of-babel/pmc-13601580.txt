Homo-metal-element mediated surface modification of sapphire for robust epitaxy of wafer-scale single-crystalline 2D semiconductors
Authors: Jialong Wang, Yihao Zheng, Qingsheng Zeng, Yuhang Jing, You Peng, Tong Zhou, Li Yin, Xinyan Wu, Chenxi Zhang, Yujin Cheng, Haoxuan Ding, Xiyao Wang, Wei Wei, Li Lin, Jinlong Du, Qingqing Ji, Jing Xia, Chenguang Qiu, Lian-Mao Peng, Yanfeng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTwo-dimensional (2D) transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs) single-crystal wafers have been envisioned as promising channel materials to drive semiconductor technology down to the atomic limit. Although several efforts have been made, wafer-scale single crystal growth with high robustness and efficiency remains challenging. Here, we design a homo-metal-element (W/Mo and Al) surface modification strategy of commercial C/M sapphire inducing a low-symmetry modification layer. This design enables the robust epitaxial growth of 2-inch scale monolayer WS2 or MoS2 single-crystal wafers via a facile chemical vapor deposition (CVD) method. Since no exogenous elements are introduced on the substrate surface, the derived monolayer WS2 single crystals present high crystal quality and no impurity introduction, as evidenced by multi-scale characterizations and field effect transistors array fabrications (on/off ratio >108 and mobility ~48.2 cm2 V−1 s−1). This work should hereby propel the wafer-scale synthesis and the internal epitaxial mechanism exploration of 2D TMDCs single crystals via substrate surface modulation, and promote the high-performance applications in various fields.Subject terms: Two-dimensional materials, Synthesis and processing, Electronic devices

Introduction

Two-dimensional (2D) semiconducting transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs), featuring high carrier mobility at nanometer thickness, excellent gate controllability, small dielectric constant, etc., have been envisioned as promising channel materials for transistor scaling beyond sub-1-nm node and monolithic three-dimensional integration1–5. To fully upgrade 2D TMDCs from laboratory-level research to industrial applications, the primary bottleneck lies in the reproducible and high-efficiency preparation of wafer-scale, high-quality 2D TMDCs single-crystal wafers6. This largely hinges on the in-depth understanding of the epitaxial mechanism, which still lacks a general consensus so far7.

Our group pioneered the epitaxial growth of inch-scale monolayer TMDCs (e.g., MoS2, WS2) single crystals on various Au single-crystal substrates8–12, and established the probable epitaxial mechanism from the synergy of adlayer-substrate lattice constant/symmetry matching, relatively strong interface coupling, and step-edge-guiding effects. Concurrently, the epitaxial growth of 2D TMDCs has also been executed on commercial sapphire (crystalline α-Al2O3), which is the customary-used substrate in the semiconductor industry. A series of substrate-processing and growth-process-optimization strategies, including choosing specific miscut directions and suitable crystal planes13,14, controlling the time sequence of TMDCs nucleation and substrate step formation15, tuning the precursor ratios16,17, forming a single-type oxygen atomic plane18, introducing metal dopants19, etc., were put forward successively. Nevertheless, most of these strategies are plagued by poor reproducibility, a narrow experimental window, and low growth efficiency, which greatly impede the robust preparation of 2D TMDCs single-crystal wafers and their future deployments.

According to the established theoretical framework for the epitaxy of 2D materials, the symmetry group of the substrate should be a subgroup of the 2D material20. Whereas, the sapphire surface is generally reconstructed under ultrahigh-vacuum or high-temperature annealing (inducing symmetry change from three-fold to six-fold)21, which usually leads to the growth of anti-parallel oriented monolayer TMDCs domains on top.

Surface modification strategy has been adapted to decrease the atomic symmetry of sapphire, for achieving the preparation of 2D TMDCs single-crystal wafers. Several recent efforts have revealed that an atomically thin “buffer layer” can naturally evolve at the 2D TMDCs/sapphire interface during the growth process17,18,22–25, and mediate the epitaxy of 2D TMDCs single crystals. However, these efforts only focused on the retrospective characterizations of the interfacial structures after the growth process, which is insufficient to elucidate the surface modulation effect. Very recently, some work developed distinct substrate surface modification strategies before the growth of 2D TMDCs. Chang et al. constructed an atomically thin Fe2O3 decoration layer on sapphire26, and Zou et al. used lanthanum to passivate the sapphire surface27, etc. Whereas, exogenous elements (i.e., Fe and La) were inevitably introduced into the growth processes, which may induce alien atom impurities, increase the synthesis complexity and costs, and pose obstacles to practical industrial productions.

In this Article, we aspire to develop a homo-metal-element sapphire surface modification strategy for the robust and high-efficiency growth of monolayer TMDC single crystals. The “homo-metal-element” refers to the modification layer on the substrate that possesses the same metal element as that of the adlayer. The advantages of this design can be considered as follows: (1) the metal oxide precursor (e.g., WO3, MoO3) can react with Al2O3 at high-temperature annealing process, forming a homo-metal-element surface structure with low symmetry (one-fold), which should favor the mono-oriented growth of monolayer WS2 or MoS2; (2) the growth process can be segmented easily and examined with details, due to the high chemical stability and simple composition of the surface modification layer, which should contribute to achieving a deeper understanding of the epitaxial growth mechanism; (3) the wafer-scale synthesis of monolayer WS2 single crystals is seldom reported so far, due to the high volatile temperature of W-contained precursors and the difficulty in their large-area uniform supply/deposition. Overall, this work aims to propose a robust strategy for preparing monolayer semiconducting TMDCs single-crystal wafers and deepen our understanding of the epitaxial mechanism, which should promote their batch production and applications in the next-generation very-large-scale-integration (VLSI) technology.

Results

Design of homo-metal-element surface modification strategy

Choosing the single-crystal substrate with a suitable lattice symmetry is pivotal for the robust epitaxy of 2D TMDCs. As is known, the topmost surface of commercial c-plane sapphire (Al2O3(0001)) is actually of six-fold symmetry after surface reconstruction (through ultrahigh-vacuum or high-temperature annealing processes) for minimizing the surface energy. This should readily lead to the formation of anti-parallel TMDCs domains (Fig. 1a).

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were first performed to visualize the orientation preference. As shown in Fig. 1b, c, the binding energies of monolayer WS2 on pristine sapphire present two local minima at 30° and 90°, with a slight binding energy difference (ΔEb) of ~0.001 electron volt per W atom (eV/W). These nearly degenerated binding energies should result in the anti-parallel orientation of monolayer WS2 domains on pristine sapphire.

In contrast, a low-symmetry homo-metal-element (Al and W or Mo) modification layer pre-constructed on sapphire can make a difference (Fig. 1d). In our DFT calculations, O-W-O-Al atomic arrangement (structurally analog to that of Al2O3) was selected as surface modification layer (Fig. 1e, and structural models in Supplementary Fig. 1). Notably, the binding energies of monolayer WS2 on the modified substrate show only one deep minimum at 30° (Fig. 1f), and the energy difference between the 30° and 90° aligned cases reaches ~0.055 eV/W, which is ~55 times larger than that on pristine sapphire. The huge energy difference should mediate the unidirectional orientation of monolayer WS2 on sapphire with a pre-constructed modification layer at the initial nucleation period. This surface modification can also be inferred from the previous report that chemical reaction can happen between WO3 and Al2O3 with the formation of a new Al2(WO4)3 phase, featuring the low symmetry (monoclinic P21/c space group) and O-W-O-Al atomic structure28.

Notably, the reproducible preparations of 2-inch monolayer WS2 single-crystal wafers were then realized with ease on the modified sapphire surface, as evidenced by the achievement of uniform monolayer WS2 wafers in different batches of experiments (Fig. 1g). And the high crystal quality of the CVD-derived monolayer WS2 wafer is preliminarily proven by photoluminescence (PL) line scan image, showing uniform and strong peak (~1.98 eV) with narrow peak width (~51 meV) across the wafer (Fig. 1h). Accordingly, our designed homo-metal-element surface modification strategy of sapphire should be reliable for preparing monolayer TMDCs single-crystal wafers.

Preparation and structure of homo-metal-element modification layer

To explore the composition and structure of the homo-metal-element modification layer, its pre-constructing process is carefully examined. Initially, WO3 powders were deposited on C/M sapphire holding at ~1000 °C (Fig. 2a, and experimental setup in Supplementary Fig. 2). Lots of clusters or particles emerge on the sapphire surface, according to the atomic force microscopy (AFM, Fig. 2b) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (Supplementary Fig. 3). These clusters are supposed to be accumulated WO3-related species, as evidenced by the W6+ doublets (4f7/2 ~ 35.8 eV, 4f5/2 ~ 38.0 eV) and energy loss peak (~42.0 eV) in X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) W 4 f spectrum, which corresponds to those of WO3 (Fig. 2c, and full-range spectra in Supplementary Fig. 4, optical microscopy (OM) image in Supplementary Fig. 5)29.

The WO3-treated sapphire was then annealed at higher temperature of ~1200 °C (Fig. 2d). Intriguingly, the sapphire surface becomes flat and clean, free of any clusters or particles, and uniform steps with the average height of ~0.4 nm evolve (Fig. 2e). It is noteworthy that, the W6+ doublets shift to higher binding energies (4f7/2 ~ 36.5 eV, 4f5/2 ~ 38.7 eV), and the energy loss peak of WO3 disappears in the XPS spectrum (Fig. 2f). These results agree with previous reports that, the reaction between WO3 and Al2O3 happened at >1100 °C, forming a new Al2(WO4)3 phase, as evidenced by the redshift of W6+ doublets in XPS spectrum28,30. Overall, a homo-metal-element modification layer, possessing W, Al, and O elements, can be formed on the sapphire surface, due to the surface chemical reaction through the ~1200 °C annealing process. In a recent work, the metal precursor (i.e., W(CO)6) was first deposited on sapphire at ~850 °C for the formation of a WO3 thin layer31. However, the ~850 °C processing temperature is not sufficient to trigger the reaction of WO3 and Al2O3 (i.e., sapphire substrate). Bi-orientation growth of monolayer WS2 was realized via the traditional metal-organic CVD process.

Time of flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS), featured with superior mass resolution and sensitivity (~10 ppb level)32, was also used to validate the composition and structure of this new W/Al-based phase. Notably, negative ions with mass/charge ratios (m/z) of ~259 and ~275 are detected (Fig. 2g, and full-range spectra in Supplementary Fig. 6, mapping images in Supplementary Fig. 7), which can be assigned to AlWO3− and AlWO4− ions, respectively. Notably, the MWO4− ions (M denotes the metal element, e.g., NaWO4− and LiWO4−) were considered as the main fingerprint ions to identify the tungstate-related species33,34. Hereby, the modification layer should possess a similar atomic structure to that of Al2(WO4)3 with the O-W-O-Al atomic arrangement (Raman spectrum of modified sapphire in Supplementary Fig. 8, and atomic model in Supplementary Fig. 9). Besides, the modification layer only exists on the topmost surface of sapphire, since the normalized intensity of AlWO4− ion decreases rapidly with the sputtering time (Fig. 2h). As a direct proof, cross-sectional high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images at the interface of monolayer WS2/modified sapphire were also achieved (with energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) data in Supplementary Fig. 10), showing an atomically thin modification layer with a brighter contrast than that of sapphire substrate (Fig. 2i, with the interface of monolayer WS2/pristine sapphire as reference in Supplementary Fig. 11). The W atoms in the modification layer are periodically arranged with regard to the underlying sapphire lattice in both Al2O3 <101¯0> and <112¯0> directions. And the experimental HAADF-STEM images agree well with those of simulated images (more details in Supplementary Fig. 12), regarding the Z-contrast intensities, atomic distances along different directions, etc. The AlWOx− ions are also detected in the ToF-SIMS spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 6). In all, the interfacial modification layer is assigned to be the W/Al-based one with the O-W-O-Al atomic arrangement.

Moreover, the zigzag (zz, <112¯0> direction) edge of monolayer WS2 is observed to be parallel with the Al2O3 M <101¯0> direction from the HAADF-STEM images, which is also shown in the zoom-in AFM images (Fig. 2j, and AFM inphase images in Supplementary Fig. 13). Specifically, the monolayer WS2 zz direction (<112¯0>) should be rotated by 30° rotation (R30°) from the Al2O3 A <112¯0> direction, which also corresponds well with the above DFT calculations, i.e., the 30° aligned monolayer WS2 is energetically the most stable on sapphire with the modification layer, and the in-plane X-ray diffraction (XRD) results (Supplementary Fig. 14). In this configuration, the 5 × 5 monolayer WS2 supercell (5 × 3.20 Å = 16.0 Å) nearly commensurate with the Al2O3 supercell (2√3×4.76Å=16.5Å) with ~3.1% lattice mismatch. This growth behavior should follow the traditional epitaxy theory.

Note that this epitaxial relationship is distinct from previous reports using Fe2O3-decorated or lanthanum-passivated sapphire as substrates26,27, where R0° is the preferential orientation. Additionally, the domain edge direction of monolayer WS2 is independent of the step edge direction of sapphire. Accordingly, the step-edge-guiding effect may not be the dominating factor for the epitaxial growth mode in our system. This is also confirmed by examining the initial growth stage, where triangular nuclei were rotated by 30° with regard to the step edges (i.e., Al2O3 <112¯0> direction) on C/M sapphire (AFM image in Supplementary Fig. 15). In brief, through the pre-reaction between metal precursor and sapphire substrate, a low-symmetry modification layer with the O-W-O-Al atomic arrangement can be fabricated on sapphire. The modified sapphire is beneficial for the epitaxial growth of monolayer WS2 domains or single-crystal films.

Epitaxial growth of 2D TMDCs semiconductors

To validate the theoretical predictions regarding the domain orientations with the substrate symmetry, the growth of monolayer WS2 was first attempted on pristine C/M sapphire, by using WO3 and S powders as precursors at ~950 °C, via the low-pressure chemical vapor deposition (LPCVD) method (experimental setup in Supplementary Fig. 16). As expected, monolayer WS2 domains with mirror-symmetric orientations occur on pristine sapphire, with the respective ratio of ~1:1 (OM image and statistical results in Fig. 3a, h). For the chemical composition characterizations, prominent doublets of W4+ 4f7/2 (~32.7 eV), 4f5/2 (~34.9 eV), and S2− 2p3/2 (~162.3 eV), 2p1/2 (~163.4 eV) are deconvoluted from the XPS spectra (Fig. 3b). In randomly collected Raman data, almost fixed positions of in-plane (E2g1, ~356 cm−1) and out-of-plane vibration (A1g, ~418 cm−1) modes are acquired, with a nearly fixed wavenumber difference of ~62 cm−1 (Fig. 3c). Moreover, uniform PL signals (~1.93 eV) are achieved with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of ~92 meV (Fig. 3d, and more PL data in Supplementary Fig. 17). The achieved results show similar features to those of the monolayer WS2/sapphire35,36. Accordingly, bi-oriented monolayer WS2 domains or films can be synthesized on pristine C/M sapphire.

This indicates the successful achievement of high-quality mono-oriented monolayer WS2 domains and films on modified sapphire with high growth efficiency.

Homo-metal-element surface modification process was then performed on C/M sapphire. Surprisingly, under the same growth conditions as those in Fig. 2a, mono-oriented monolayer WS2 triangular domains (~10 μm in edge length) appear on C/M sapphire with surface modification (Fig. 3e, for ~5 min), and they grow laterally (Fig. 3f, for ~10 min) and finally stitch into a continuous film (Fig. 3g, for ~15 min). Statistical analysis demonstrates a high proportion of uniform orientation (~98%, Fig. 3h, and more OM images and second harmonic generation (SHG) results in Supplementary Fig. 18). Notably, a “face-to-face” metal precursor supply route is utilized to decrease the diffusion distance of WO3 precursor onto sapphire substrate, improve the deposition uniformity of metal precursor, and thus greatly enhance the growth efficiency and homogeneity. Notably, only one quarter of growth time (~15 min) is needed for the full-coverage growth with satisfying inch-scale uniformity, compared to that of monolayer WS2 growth on a-plane sapphire (~60 min) using the up-streamed WO3 precursor supply route14. Besides, consistent mono-orientation growth of monolayer WS2 can be achieved on such modified substrates, regardless of growth temperatures, precursor ratios, and substrate miscut directions (OM and AFM images in Supplementary Fig. 19). This strongly underscores the robustness of our homo-metal-element modification strategy on the epitaxy of monolayer TMDCs under different growth conditions.

The XPS doublets of W4+ 4f and S2− 2p (the same as those in Fig. 3b) indicate the formation of WS2 (Fig. 3i, j, and full-range spectrum in Supplementary Fig. 20). Notably, one W6+ 4f7/2 (~36.2 eV, pink peak in Fig. 3i) peak is also deconvoluted. Considering that the as-grown sample is achieved from the S-rich growth condition, and its surface is atomically flat, free of any WO3 cluster adsorptions, the W6+ signal should arise from the homo-metal-element modification layer. By comparing the two growth systems, the modification layer should take effect in the mono-orientation of monolayer WS2 on the pre-modified sapphire surface.

Moreover, Raman line scan across the wafer (Fig. 3k) showing the same characteristics (regarding peak positions, wavenumber difference, etc.), suggests the uniform monolayer thickness of WS2 (AFM image in Supplementary Fig. 21). The unidirectional orientation is also reconfirmed by the homogeneous polarized SHG spectra (Fig. 3l, and more data in Supplementary Fig. 22). Briefly, by designing the homo-metal-element surface modification strategy of C/M sapphire, robust and high-efficiency growth of unidirectionally oriented monolayer WS2 domains and single-crystal films can be realized, via the “face-to-face” metal precursor supply route. And the wafer size is limited by the size of the CVD furnace and sapphire substrate, which is expected to be scaled to 300 mm through optimization. Notably, this strategy can be extended to the epitaxial growth of monolayer MoS2 single crystals, as shown in the corresponding ToF-SIMS, XPS, cross-sectional HAADF-STEM, and EDS results (Supplementary Figs. 23–27). And a similar O-Mo-O-Al atomic arrangement of the Mo/Al-based surface modification layer is proposed. This strongly indicates the universality and versatility of our homo-metal-element surface modification strategy.

Seamless stitching of mono-oriented domains

The seamless stitching of mono-oriented monolayer WS2 domains should be the key premise for the formation of single-crystal films. To address this, macroscopic spectroscopy characterizations were performed on the as-grown monolayer WS2 on sapphire with a pre-constructed modification layer. The high thickness uniformity and superior crystallinity of the merged monolayer films are firstly evidenced by the homogeneous color contrasts in OM, Raman (extracted from the intensity of E2g1 mode), and PL (extracted from the intensity of A-exciton peak) mapping images (Fig. 4a–c, e–g). Remarkably, uniform color contrasts among the merged domains and continuous films can also be observed in SHG mapping images (Fig. 4d, h, as noticed by blue arrows). In contrast, obvious intensity drops of SHG signals occur at the merging area of bi-oriented monolayer WS2 domains and stitched poly-crystal films (more Raman, PL, and SHG mapping images in Supplementary Figs. 28 and 29). These results tentatively address the seamless stitching behavior of the mono-oriented monolayer domains/films.

Atomic-resolution HAADF-STEM characterizations were also performed to provide atomic-scale evidence. Continuous lattices with the identical orientation can be achieved at the monolayer WS2 domains and the merging regions (successive zoom-in images in Fig. 4i–k and Supplementary Fig. 30). The honeycomb lattice shows the d-spacings of ~0.27 nm and ~0.16 nm, corresponding to those of (101¯0) and (112¯0) planes, respectively. This agrees well with that of the CVD-derived monolayer WS2 reported previously37. Moreover, the high crystal quality with negligible defects or dopants in the monolayer WS2 can be inferred by the absence of obvious contrast variation in the atomic lattice. This should be attributed to the homo-element characteristics and high stability of the modification layer.

Besides, only one set of hexagonal diffraction spots is detected with orientation deviation <±0.2° in the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns, reconfirming the uniform lattice orientation of the monolayer WS2 (Fig. 4l and Supplementary Fig. 31). In short, the seamless stitching of mono-oriented monolayer WS2 domains and their merging into continuous single-crystal films can be identified by multiscale characterization methods. As reported previously, the electrical and thermal properties of 2D TMDCs (e.g., MoS2) were severely degraded in the grain boundary (GB) regions38. The wafer-scale uniform 2D TMDCs single crystals should access promising applications in highly integrated electronic devices.

Demonstration of uniform and superior electrical devices performance

Back-gated field-effect transistors (FETs) array was fabricated by the monolayer WS2 single-crystal film transferred on the ~50 nm thick SiO2/Si substrate (schematic FET device in Fig. 5a). The channel length (L) and width (W) are both ~1 μm (OM and SEM images in Fig. 5b, and more SEM and Raman mapping images in Supplementary Fig. 32). Note that, composite metal contact electrodes (Au/Ti/Pd) were fabricated to achieve ideal electrical performance, which was proven effective in all-out scaled (L ~ 30 nm) monolayer MoS2-based FETs array39 and flexible monolayer MoS2 integrated circuits40. The output curves of the typical FET device exhibit a linear current-voltage relation (Fig. 5c). Remarkably, superior on-state current density (~217 μA μm−1) can be achieved at drain/source voltage (Vds) of ~3 V and gate voltage (Vg) of ~30 V, indicating the good Ohmic contacts at the drain/source electrodes. Moreover, the typical dual-sweep transfer curves present n-type behavior and small hysteresis (Fig. 5d). Thanks to the superior uniformity of the monolayer WS2 single crystals, the measured FET devices exhibit normal operations and low variations of ~19.2% in on-state current (defined as SIon/<Ion> in the previous report41, where SIon and <Ion> denote the standard deviation and mean value of on-state current, respectively, Fig. 5e).

Impressively, the on/off ratios of the measured FET devices all exceed ~108, and the average and maximum field-effect mobilities (μFE) reach ~40.2 and ~48.2 cm2 V−1 s−1, respectively, and they both show Gaussian distributions (Fig. 5f). This performance surpasses most of the reported monolayer WS2-based FET devices using SiO2 as dielectric and measured at room temperature (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Table 1)36,42–51. This unambiguously proves the high crystal quality of our monolayer WS2 films and the good electrical contacts between composite metal electrodes and monolayer WS2 channels. Additionally, the designed growth process excludes the introduction of other exogenous atoms (from precursor selection, substrate surface modification process, etc.), thus avoiding the possible disturbance of carrier scattering and interfacial states. This should also account for the improved electrical performance. And we highlight that this basic device structure is fabricated to only test the quality of monolayer WS2 single crystals, where advanced device configurations are needed to fully realize their potential in high-performance electronics. In all, the CVD-derived monolayer WS2 single-crystal wafers should hold great promise to serve as promising n-type channel materials for future applications in 2D complementary metal oxide semiconductor (CMOS) logical electronic devices.

Discussion

In summary, we have developed a homo-metal-element modification strategy of industry-compatible C/M sapphire, and realized the reproducible and high-efficiency growth of wafer-scale monolayer TMDCs single crystals, via the designed “face-to-face” metal precursor supply route. The low-symmetry surface modification layer allows the consistent mono-oriented growth of monolayer TMDCs under a wide range of conditions. The derived 2-inch monolayer WS2 single-crystal wafers can be utilized to fabricate high-performance integrated FET arrays, affording high on-state current, elevated mobilities and on/off ratios, etc. Moreover, this growth strategy also shows good versatility in the preparations of other kinds of 2D TMDCs (e.g., MoS2). Overall, this work offers an all-round optimization pathway for the industry-standard production of 2D TMDCs single-crystal wafers, which should inspire more in-depth explorations of the epitaxial growth mechanism and accelerate the integration of 2D atomically thin semiconductors into the next-generation integrated electronic circuits.

Methods

Homo-metal-element modification of sapphire

Commercial 2-inch C/M sapphire substrate (Helios New Materials Limited) was placed upon the excessive WO3 powder (Alfa Aesar, 99.998%) and put into the tube furnace (Lindberg/Blue). The furnace was then heated to ~1000 °C and maintained for ~1 h under the protection gas of argon (Ar) of ~200 standard cubic centimeters per minute (sccm). This deposition temperature is higher than the vaporizing temperature of WO3 (~850 °C), and ~1 h deposition time guarantees the complete and uniform deposition of WO3 on sapphire. The WO3-treated sapphire substrate was then put into the muffle box furnace (Hefei Kejing) and annealed at ~1200 °C for ~2 h to form the homo-metal-element modification layer. Specifically, according to the DFT models (Supplementary Fig. 9), the theoretically required deposition amount of W atoms of the W/Al-based modification layer is ~1.97 × 1016 W atoms, i.e., ~3.27 × 10−8 mol. This equals a small mass of the WO3 powder as ~7.58 × 10−3 mg, and the actual deposition amount of WO3 onto sapphire should be far in excess, corresponding to the WO3-rich condition.

Growth of monolayer WS2 single-crystal domains/films

Monolayer WS2 was grown on the pre-modified sapphire via the LPCVD process, with the graphite boat containing WO3 powder placed under the substrate. The single-end-sealed quartz tube containing S powder (Alfa Aesar, 99.5%) was placed upstream and heated at ~200 °C. The furnace was heated to ~950 °C and kept for different time periods to tune the sample coverages, under the protection gas of Ar (~100 sccm). The pressure during the CVD growth process is ~55 Pa. The sample transfer was performed using the common polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA)-assisted method.

Characterizations

Monolayer WS2 and MoS2 single-crystal samples were systematically characterized by OM (Nikon ECLIPSE, LV100ND), SEM (Hitachi S-4800, ~1 kV, ~10 μA), XPS (Kratos Analytical AXIS-Ultra with monochromatic Al Kα X-ray), AFM (Bruker, Dimension Icon), Raman and PL spectroscopy (WITec Alpha 300 R, ~532 nm laser), SHG spectroscopy (WITec Alpha 300 R, ~1064 nm laser), HRTEM, HAADF-STEM, and EDS (JEOL-ARM300F, ~300 kV), SAED (FEI Tecnai F20, ~200 kV), and in-plane XRD (Rigaku Smartlab). Cross-sectional samples were prepared by the dual-beam focused ion beam (FIB) microscope (ThermoFisher Helios 5 CX) with a gallium (Ga+) ion source. For the ToF-SIMS tests (IONTOF), bismuth (Bi3+) ions were used as the analysis source with a scanning area of ~50 × 50 µm2, and cesium (Cs+) ions were used as the sputter source with the area of ~200 × 200 μm2. Negative ion signals were detected to investigate the three-dimensional spatial species distribution.

Device fabrications and measurements

Monolayer WS2 film was transferred to ~50 nm thick SiO2/Si substrate, and Au/Ti/Pd (~3/2/15 nm) were deposited as composite metal contact electrodes using an electron beam evaporation system (DETECH DE400). The electrical measurements were carried out with the four-probe system (MPI TS200-SE) using a semiconductor parameter analyzer (Keithley 4200A-SCS) in vacuum (<1.3 mTorr) at room temperature. DFT Calculations

The construction of the atomic models and their visualization were performed using the Device Studio program developed by Hongzhiwei Technology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. The electronic structure calculations were performed using the DFT method implemented in the DS-PAW software package52. The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional under the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) was employed to describe the exchange-correlation interaction53,54. The van der Waals correction was described by Grimme’s DFT-D3 scheme55. The plane-wave basis set was truncated at ~520 eV. A vacuum layer of ~50 Å was introduced in the out-of-plane direction to screen the interlayer interactions. The convergence criteria for electronic self-consistent energy were set to ~10−5 eV, with the ionic step forces converging to ~0.02 eV/Å during the structure optimization.

For the Al2O3(0001) substrate with single Al-terminated surface (sAl-TS), a Gamma-centered k point grid of (7 × 7 × 1) was utilized to sample the Brillouin zone. For the substrate with a modification layer surface (MLS), the same k-mesh was used; during the relaxation process, only the surface O-W-O layer was allowed to relax, while all deeper atoms were fixed to reduce computational expense. To determine the optimal orientation of monolayer WS2 on each substrate, a monolayer W7S12 cluster representing the WS2 nucleus was placed on the (4 × 4) supercell of Al2O3(0001) with either sAl-TS or MLS.
