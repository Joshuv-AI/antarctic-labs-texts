A radiological evaluation of crossed-fused ectopic kidney in adult female: Case report and comprehensive review of literature
Authors: KC Suraj, Samiksha Lamichhane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCrossed renal ectopia with fusion is a rare congenital kidney anomaly where the kidneys are fused and cross each other. Patients are asymptomatic while some may experience lower abdominal pain, hematuria, fever, urinary tract infection, hypertension, renal failure, and a palpable abdominal mass. It is often discovered incidentally during evaluations for related conditions like renal stones, pyelonephritis, vesico-ureteric reflux, cysts, or malignancy, with renal cell carcinoma being the most common. Diagnosis of crossed-fused renal ectopia can be done through abdominal ultrasonography, retrograde pyelography, and 3 dimension computed tomography. Ultrasonography can detect kidney absence while 3 dimension computed tomography scans are essential for identifying vessels for safe surgery access. Treatment focuses on managing symptoms, with intervention needed for calculi, infection, or malignancy.Keywords: Computed tomography, Congenital, Ectopia, Pyelonephritis

Introduction

Crossed renal ectopia with fusion is a rare congenital anomaly of the kidney. It is the second most common fusion anomaly, following horseshoe kidney, where the kidneys do not cross each other [1]. The first reported case dates back to 1654 by Dominicus Panarolus [2]. The exact cause of crossed renal ectopia (CRE) remains unknown. One possible explanation, proposed by Wilmer, suggests that abnormal positioning of the umbilical arteries may exert pressure, leading to the renal unit migrating in a crossover pattern to the opposite side [3]. In this study, we report the incidental diagnosis and radiological evaluation of a 25-year-old female who was found to have crossed renal ectopia with fusion while evaluating a case of urinary tract infection. We believe this rare case will help fill the existing gap in the literature regarding radiological findings.

Case presentation

A 25-year-old unmarried female presented to the surgical outpatient department with complaints of on-and-off pain in the left flank for 3 days, associated with burning micturition and increased frequency of urination. She denies a history of fever, hematuria, per-vaginal discharge, itching around the genitalia, diarrhea, vomiting, constipation, or trauma. She has no history of similar episodes in the past. Her menstrual history reveals a normal menstrual cycle without dysmenorrhea. Her last menstruation was 12 days ago. There was no history of any medical or surgical comorbidities in the past, and the family history was not significant. General physical examinations were normal. She had a pulse rate of 72 beats per minute with a normal character and volume. Blood pressure measured 110/70 mmHg, and other parameters were normal. The abdomen was soft and nontender. She was evaluated with routine urine and microscopic examination, which showed 5-7 pus cells/hpf, and a complete blood count showed a total leukocyte count of 6000 with normal neutrophil and lymphocyte counts. Renal function tests were normal. An X-ray of the abdomen and pelvis was normal. Ultrasonography revealed the absence of the right kidney in the right renal fossa and the presence of 2 kidneys in the left renal fossa, where the upper pole of 1 kidney was fused with the lower pole of another kidney, suggestive of right crossed fused renal ectopia. Given the common embryological association between renal ectopia and genital tract anomalies, a dedicated pelvic ultrasonography and clinical gynecological assessment were performed by referring this patient to Gynecology Outpatient clinic. The evaluation confirmed a normal, symmetric uterus, bilateral normal ovaries, and no identifiable Müllerian or genital track abnormalities. She was advised to undergo computed tomography intravenous urography (IVU). A computed tomography (CT) scan with different sections and modes demonstrated the absence of the right kidney in the right renal fossa and the presence of a fused kidney in the left renal fossa with a type 1 configuration. The 3 dimension (3D) volume-rendered CT scan (Fig. 1) showed the absence of a kidney on the right side and 2 kidneys: a normal left kidney marked with a white arrow and an ectopic kidney marked with a green arrow, along with the ureter of the ectopic kidney that has crossed the midline with normal insertion in the bladder (marked as blue). The sagittal section of the CT abdomen and pelvis showed a long, elongated structure where lower poles of normal kidneys (white arrow) and the upper pole of ectopic kidney (marked as green arrow) are fused. Both of them have their own renal pelvis (Fig. 2). The axial section of the CT at the level of insertion into the bladder suggested that both of the ureters have normal insertion, marked as blue and yellow respectively (Fig. 3). Angiographic evaluation via 3D CT reconstruction revealed an anomalous, complex arterial network: the superior (orthotopic left) renal unit is supplied by a main renal artery originating normally from the abdominal aorta. The inferior crossed-ectopic unit receives dual arterial supply: an upper branch directly from the lower abdominal aorta anteriorly, and an inferior branch arising from the left common iliac artery (Fig. 4).

After the detailed radiological investigations, she was diagnosed with fused crossed renal ectopia and counseling was done for the patient about her condition. A urine culture was sent and reports followed which showed no growth of microorganisms. She completed 1 week of oral antibiotics and followed up after 10 days. A repeat routine urine and microscopic examination was done which showed no pus cells. The patient is kept on regular long-term follow-up utilizing transabdominal ultrasonography (USG) every 6 months to monitor for potential complications such as hydronephrosis, nephrolithiasis, or structural changes, reserving CT scans only if symptomatic complications arise. Her family was counseled about the condition and technical challenges that may come if she develops nephrolithiasis, ureteric calculi, or requires any sort of pelvic surgery. She is doing well on her regular follow-up without any complications

Discussion

The incidence of CRE with fusion is estimated to be around 1:3000 to 1:7500 [1]. It is more prevalent in males, with a ratio of 2-3 males to females. Our case is unique in that the patient is an adult female. Typically, the anomaly involves the left kidney crossing over to the right side and fusing with the right kidney [3]. However, in our case, the right kidney is absent in the right flank but migrated towards the left side to form an anomaly, which is quite rare based on our literature search.

This anomaly is believed to result from the abnormal development and migration of the ureteric bud and metanephric blastema during the fourth to eighth weeks of gestation [4]. The crossed kidney may receive blood supply from the aorta or common iliac artery. Anomalous blood supply can also occur in a normally positioned kidney. In crossed fused renal ectopia, the ureteric orifices are typically in their normal anatomical position in the bladder [5]. In our case, the ureters insert in their normal position in the bladder.

Various configurations of crossed fused renal ectopia have been documented in medical literature. The most prevalent type, comprising two-thirds of cases, is the unilateral fused kidney with inferior ectopia subtype. In this variation, fusion occurs early in development, and the pelvis of each kidney remain anteriorly oriented. Our patient exhibits this specific subtype of anomaly. Other less common subtypes include sigmoid (S-shaped), lump (cake), L-shaped, disc, and superior ectopic kidneys [6]. Renal ectopy is often associated with a high incidence of other urological abnormalities. The most common associated abnormality is vesicoureteral reflux, occurring in 20% of cases. Other genitourinary abnormalities that may be present include megaureter, hypospadias, cryptorchidism, urethral valve and cystic dysplasia, and unilateral agenesis of fallopian tubes and ovaries [7]. Our patient had no other genitourinary abnormalities besides the fused ectopic kidney.

Patients with crossed renal ectopia are usually asymptomatic but may present with vague lower abdominal pain, hematuria, fever, urinary tract infection, hypertension, renal failure, and a palpable abdominal mass [8]. Our case presented with abdominal pain, burning micturition, and increased frequency of micturition suggestive of a urinary tract infection. Most cases of crossed renal ectopia are asymptomatic and are often discovered incidentally during evaluations for related conditions such as renal and ureteric stones, pyelonephritis, vesico-ureteric reflux, cysts, pelviureteric junction obstruction, or malignancy, with renal cell carcinoma being the most common [9].

Crossed-fused renal ectopia can be diagnosed through abdominal ultrasonography, KUB, retrograde pyelography, and 3D computed tomography. Ultrasonography can detect the absence of a kidney, and retrograde pyelography can provide information on stone size and location. 3D CT scans are crucial for identifying essential vessels to ensure safe access during surgery and prevent complications [10]. The previous information regarding crossed renal fusion ectopia with crossing of the midline ureter helps the treating surgeon anticipate technical difficulties during endourological procedures, as well as any abdominal surgery. It also helps prevent inadvertent injury to these structures. We have explained this fact to our patient. Treatment for such anomalies focuses on managing associated symptoms. Treatment is necessary if calculi, infection, or malignancy are present at diagnosis [11]. Crossed fused renal ectopia requires regular monitoring to improve patient care and prevent complications. Prompt intervention and personalized management tailored to the patient’s symptoms and complications can enhance outcomes [12,13]. Our case responded well to oral antibiotics, and there were no other conditions requiring surgery, so the patient was managed symptomatically and kept on regular follow-up.

Conclusion

Crossed renal fusion ectopia is a rare congenital anomaly that is typically asymptomatic and often diagnosed incidentally. The exact mechanism of this condition is unknown but is believed to be a result of a developmental anomaly during organogenesis. Individuals with this anomaly are at a higher risk for recurrent urinary tract infections, stone formation, and malignancy. Treatment usually involves symptomatic management, and caution should be exercised when performing uroendological procedures and surgeries in the pelvic region.
