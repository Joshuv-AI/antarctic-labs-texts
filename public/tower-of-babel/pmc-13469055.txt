Mathematical study on Brinkman type carbon nanofluid flow in channel with bioconvection effect for water treatment applications
Authors: Ahmad Qushairi Mohamad, Wan Nura’in Nabilah Noranuar, Mohamad Riduan Hashim, Nordin Zakaria, Sharidan Shafie, Zulkhibri Ismail, Dumitru Vieru, Anuar Jamaludin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Water pollution caused by the proliferation of microorganisms necessitates efficient treatment strategies to improve water quality. In this study, the bioconvective flow of a Brinkman carbon nanotubes (CNTs)-based nanofluid within a channel is analytically investigated to model water filtration processes in porous media. The Brinkman fluid model is employed to represent flow through permeable media, while the effects of motile microorganisms are incorporated through bioconvection. The governing linear partial differential equations, subject to appropriate initial and boundary conditions, are solved using the Laplace transform method to obtain exact solutions for velocity, temperature, and microorganism concentration profiles. The results reveal that increasing the CNTs volume fraction enhances the temperature and velocity profiles due to improved thermal conductivity, while significantly reducing microorganism concentration as a result of increased viscosity and enhanced mixing. Additionally, key parameters such as the bioconvection Lewis number, Rayleigh number, and Brinkman parameter are found to strongly influence the flow behavior. These findings provide valuable analytical insight into the interaction between nanoparticles, microorganisms, and porous media, with potential applications in optimizing water filtration and wastewater treatment systems.

Introduction

Bioconvection refers to the pattern formation and movement in a fluid medium due to the collective behavior of swimming microorganisms. These microorganisms, such as algae, bacteria, or protozoa, often exhibit upward swimming (gyrotactic microorganisms) or move towards light (positive phototaxis), causing density variations in the fluid. This density variation can lead to the formation of convective patterns as the denser regions sink and less dense regions rise, creating a complex, dynamic system [1–3]. Bioconvection is a fascinating intersection of fluid dynamics and microbiology, with implications for understanding natural aquatic systems and the design of bioreactors and other industrial processes. Various publications have presented theoretical models of bio-convection for different types of motile microorganisms. Ahmad et al. [4] discussed the effects of gyrotactic microbes on bioconvective nanofluid flow within a porous medium. Their study shows that the gyrotactic mechanism of the microbes is not influenced by the porous medium with high porosity or very small permeability. Rana et al. [5] studied bioconvection flow of magnetically Casson nanofluid and show the impacts of gyrotactic microorganisms and nanoparticles suspension on heat profile, with the ability to control the velocity through external factors like magnetic field. Abbasi et al. [6] investigated numerically the peristaltic viscoelastic flow with bioconvection within asymmetric channel. Hayat et al. [7] studied the presence of gyrotactic microorganisms in the bioconvective Prandtl Eyring nanofluid flow and show that microorganism profile declines as the results of increasing bioconvective Lewis number. Obalalu et al. [8] conducted the study on Oswald-Dewaele bioconvection nanofluids with gyrotactic microorganisms which drive to the advancement of biofuel. Arain et al. [9] investigated the flow features within parallel plates by inducing bioconvection using motile microorganisms’ dispersion. Khan et al. [10] analyzed the flow characteristics for Maxwell bioconvective nanofluid over a rotating stretchable cylinder under a radiation effect and the velocity distribution augments with an enhanced buoyancy factor. In a study closely related to Khan et al. [10], Nima and Ferdows [11] explored the role of slip parameters and reported a decrease in the microbiological boundary layer's thickness, highlighting the impact of slip effects on bioconvective flows. Sulaiman et al. [12] analyzed the magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) bioconvective flow across an extended surface with heat and mass transfer. Hussain et al. [13] analyzed the bioconvective dusty nanofluid flow with gyrotactic microorganisms and showed that the presence of microorganisms improved heat transfer and significantly influences the velocity and temperature distributions. Overall, these findings collectively demonstrate the diverse applications and implications of bioconvection in enhancing the water purification process, thus playing a crucial role in water pollution control.

In the context of a water treatment process, the Brinkman fluid model is the powerful theoretical approach for its simulation, which combines the effects of viscosity and permeability to describe the flow of fluids through porous media such as sand, activated carbon, or synthetic membrane, as seen in the study of Hallack et al. [14] and Hashim et al. [15]. Featuring its application in water treatment, Baird et al. [16] simulated the process of transporting distinct contaminants from water by using the Navier–Stokes-Brinkman equations and coupled with a convection–diffusion equation. By integrating the Brinkman equation, researchers can account for both viscous shear and changes in permeability, improving the precision and relevance of models for simulating intricate groundwater flow situations [17]. More references on the analytical nature of Brinkman fluid flow with nanoparticle effect were available in the study of Ali et al. [18, 19], Saqib et al. [20], Abbas et al. [21], and Allehiany et al. [22]. In addition, the modeling for water treatment process could be described more accurately through the combination of bioconvection effect with the Brinkman fluid model. Bioconvection enhances mixing, aeration, and microbial degradation, while the Brinkman model provides a framework for simulating the complex flow dynamics in systems with both free-flowing and porous regions. This relationship allows for optimized design and operation of water treatment systems, leading to more efficient and effective pollutant removal. Concerning this relation, Asjad et al. [23] is the only researchers who have investigated analytically the bioconvective phenomenon for a Brinkman fluid with hybrid nanoparticles effects using the Laplace transformation. In this study, the velocity solution is accomplished incorporating to the generalized momentum equations. However, more numerical study exploring the nature of bioconvective Brinkman nanofluid flow have been conducted by other researchers such as Zhang et al. [24] revealing that the velocity decreases with increasing Darcy number, Geetha et al. [25] impacting the bioconvective flow with the chemical reaction, Mishra et al. [26] introducing the rotating effect of microbial profiles, and Shahid et al. [27] formulating the modeling for motile gyrotactic microorganism in Brinkman nanofluid with incline magnetic field.

A wide range of industries that use metals in their daily operations is rapidly expanding, from metallurgy and electroplating to electronic manufacturing and other activities, including wastewater disposal. Wastewater disposal has become a serious problem that must be addressed to ensure a healthy environment for future generations. Wastewater containing high levels of dangerous and toxic heavy metals is stable and non-biodegradable, tending to bioaccumulate in living organisms and leading to chronic diseases. Wastewater can be classified as a Brinkman-type fluid, which is defined as a fluid that flows through porous media. Membranes, a type of porous media, can be used to separate contaminants in Brinkman-type fluids. Various methods have been implemented to separate contaminants from water or Brinkman-type fluids, including chemical precipitation [28], ion-exchange [29], coagulation and flocculation [30], flotation [31], electrochemical treatment [32], membrane filtration [30], and adsorption [33], as widely described by Fu and Wang [34] for heavy metal removal from wastewater. However, Mokhter et al. [35] found that, compared to other methods, membrane nanofiltration and ultrafiltration are very attractive due to their high separation efficiency, energy savings, ease of operation, scalability, and low cost. They introduced a type of membrane or porous media called polyether sulfone, modified by polyelectrolyte (PE) multilayers made of polyallylamine hydrochloride (PAH) and polystyrene sulfonate (PSS), to remove Cu2⁺, Zn2⁺, and Ni2⁺ heavy metal cations from water-based solutions. Their research concluded that the surface modification method of ion-implantation using a layer-by-layer (LbL) approach was the most cost-effective and high-performing. The LbL method involves the alternate adsorption of oppositely charged PEs, allowing for controllable and tunable layer properties. Consequently, the properties of porous media and the adsorption effect can be applied to single-wall carbon nanotubes within Brinkman-type fluids to remove heavy metals and other dangerous contaminants.

Traditional water treatment struggles to remove metals and microorganisms and can produce harmful disinfection byproducts. Nanomaterials have emerged as a promising alternative for contaminant removal [36]. These materials, characterized by their nanoscale size (less than 100 nm), exhibit unique mechanical, electrical, optical, and magnetic properties [37, 38]. According to the above review on bioconvection and Brinkman fluid model, most of the investigation were conducted under the influences of nanomaterials such as copper (Cu), silver (Ag), aluminum oxide \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(A{l}_{2}{O}_{3})$$\end{document}, and titanium oxide \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(Ti{O}_{2})$$\end{document}. Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) are a notable type of carbon nanomaterial (CNM), along with carbon beads, fibers, and nanoporous carbon. Among these, CNTs have garnered the most attention and have seen rapid advancements. CNTs consist of graphene sheets rolled into cylinders as small as 1 nm in diameter [39]. Their exceptional properties make CNTs highly attractive as adsorbents. They offer extremely high specific surface areas and adsorption efficiencies for various contaminants due to their porous structures [40]. They excel in adsorbing a broad range of contaminants due to their high capacity, fast kinetics, large surface areas, and selectivity towards aromatic compounds, making them highly effective for water and wastewater treatment [41]. The characteristics for bioconvection flow affected by CNTs was explored by Ramzan et al. [42], finding an increase trend of velocity due to augmentation of CNTs volume fraction. Shah et al. [43] conducted a similar study as Ramzan et al. [42], considering the flow is under the influences of thermal radiation, rotating microorganisms, Joule heating, and chemical reactions. Hussain et al. [44] investigated the impacts of CNTs nanofluid containing motile microorganisms in the presence of inclined magnetic field. This study is further extended by Hussain et al. [45], examining the chemical reaction impacts on bioconvective flow. All the study [42–45] have been conducted numerically by using bcp4c package in MATLAB and no Brinkman fluid model was implemented.

In summary, existing studies on bioconvection nanofluids have predominantly relied on numerical approaches, with limited availability of exact analytical solutions. Moreover, although the Brinkman fluid model has been widely applied to simulate flow through porous media, its coupling with bioconvection and carbon nanotubes (CNTs)-based nanofluids remains largely unexplored, particularly from an analytical perspective. Previous works involving CNTs have mainly focused on conventional nanofluid models without incorporating porous medium effects governed by the Brinkman framework. Therefore, a significant research gap exists in developing an exact analytical model that simultaneously accounts for bioconvection, CNTs nanoparticles, and Brinkman porous media effects. To bridge this gap, the present study extends the work of Asjad et al. [23] by introducing CNTs-based nanofluids into a Brinkman-type fluid under bioconvective conditions and solving the governing equations using the Laplace transform method. This approach provides closed-form solutions that offer deeper physical insight into the interaction between microorganisms, nanoparticle dynamics, and porous structures. The fluid is assumed to flow in a channel, which is the most suitable representation for water treatment processes like sedimentation, filtration, and certain types of biological treatment. The findings are expected to contribute toward improving the design and efficiency of water treatment systems, particularly in enhancing mixing, contaminant removal, and filtration performance. Findings of present study

Effect of bioconvection Lewis number (Lb)

The bioconvection Lewis number (Lb) is another important dimensionless number in the study of bioconvection. It characterizes the relative rates of thermal diffusion and microorganism diffusion in a fluid medium. The bioconvection Lewis number is analogous to the thermal Prandtl number in thermal convection, but it specifically relates to the transport properties of microorganisms and nutrients or chemicals in the fluid. The impact of increasing Lb on microorganism and velocity profiles is illustrated in Figs. 4 and 5, respectively. From a physical standpoint, higher values of Lb indicate stronger microorganism diffusivity relative to thermal diffusion. As a result, microorganisms spread more uniformly throughout the fluid domain, weakening the formation of concentrated plumes or bioconvective cells. This reduction in microorganism clustering diminishes density gradients, which are the primary drivers of bioconvective instability. Consequently, the microorganism concentration profile decreases as shown in Fig. 4.

At the same time, the reduction in dense microorganism aggregates lowers the effective resistance to fluid motion. With fewer localized high-density regions opposing the flow, the fluid experiences less drag, resulting in an increase in velocity as illustrated in Fig. 5. This behavior is consistent with the findings of Habibishandiz et al. [3], who reported that enhanced diffusivity weakens microorganism accumulation and modifies the flow structure.

However, in contrast to studies where velocity decreases with increasing Lb due to weakened buoyancy forces, the present result shows an enhancement in velocity. This difference can be attributed to the inclusion of Brinkman porous media effects and CNT nanoparticles, which alter momentum transport by reducing flow resistance and enhancing thermal conductivity, thereby compensating for the reduced buoyancy effect.

From an application perspective, this behavior is beneficial in wastewater treatment systems, as enhanced fluid motion reduces stagnation zones and improves mixing, leading to more efficient contaminant transport and removal.

Effect of bioconvection Rayleigh number (Ra)

The bioconvection Rayleigh number (Ra) is a dimensionless number used in the study of bioconvection, which refers to the convective patterns formed by the collective movement of swimming microorganisms in a fluid. The bioconvection Rayleigh number quantifies the relative importance of buoyancy forces caused by the density variation due to microorganisms compared to viscous forces in the fluid. As Ra increases, the density stratification caused by microorganisms becomes more pronounced. This can lead to the formation of stable layers or regions where the movement of fluid is restricted due to a higher concentration of microorganisms. In such stratified conditions, the vertical movement of microorganisms and fluid can be hindered, reducing the overall velocity of the bioconvection currents. This finding was also in accordance with the study of Habibishandiz et al. [3], where increasing Ra signifies the intensification of bioconvection, caused by the buildup of microorganisms at the surface. This accumulation results in the creation of a top-heavy density stratification.

It is worth noting that in some classical convection studies, increasing Rayleigh number enhances fluid motion due to thermal buoyancy dominance. However, in bioconvection systems, the mechanism differs significantly. Here, excessive microorganism accumulation can lead to stabilizing stratification rather than destabilizing convection, explaining the observed reduction in velocity.

The presence of CNTs nanoparticles further modifies this behavior by enhancing thermal transport, but it does not fully offset the dominant effect of the porous medium.

In water filtration application, increasing the Brinkman fluid parameter (implying smaller pores) leads to a decrease in fluid velocity, enhancing the effectiveness of water filtration by allowing more time for contaminants, including microorganisms and metals, to be trapped. Therefore, designing a filter with the appropriate pore size and understanding the Brinkman fluid parameter is crucial for achieving an optimal balance between flow rate and filtration efficiency in water filtration systems [46].

Furthermore, adding nanoparticles to a fluid generally increases its viscosity. Higher viscosity means greater resistance to movement within the fluid. Microorganisms swimming or moving within a more viscous fluid encounter greater drag forces, which hinders their movement and dispersal. This leads to a decrease in their concentration as in Fig. 12, as they cannot move as freely or distribute themselves as widely. This finding aligns with previous studies on nanofluid bioconvection [24], where increased viscosity suppresses microorganism transport. From an application perspective, this reduction is beneficial in water treatment systems, as higher nanoparticle concentrations enhance contaminant removal through adsorption, antimicrobial effects, and physical trapping mechanisms [47]. This leads to a decrease in the microorganism profile in the filtered water, contributing to more effective water purification [48]. Optimizing the nanoparticle volume fraction is crucial for achieving the best performance while maintaining acceptable flow rates and ensuring safety.

In addition, the increasing velocity profile with rising nanoparticles volume fraction \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left(\phi \right)$$\end{document}, as illustrated in Fig. 13, is closely linked to the thermal properties of nanofluids. As previously discussed, the addition of nanoparticles increases the temperature profile, which reduces temperature gradients within the fluid. Lower temperature gradients, in turn, decrease the fluid’s viscosity (as viscosity typically decreases with rising temperature in most fluids), facilitating smoother flow and higher velocities. This observation is consistent with the outcomes reported by Abdelsalam et al. [49], where similar trend is noted due to nanoparticles impacts. However, in some studies, velocity decreases with increasing nanoparticle concentration due to dominant viscous effects. The difference observed here is attributed to the strong thermal conductivity of CNTs and the coupling with bioconvection and Brinkman porous media, which alters the balance between thermal and viscous forces. In wastewater treatment, these higher velocity profiles, resulting from increased nanoparticle volume fractions, can enhance mixing, improve oxygen transport, and simplify contaminant removal, ultimately boosting treatment efficiency [50].

Conclusion

In this study, the free convective heat transfer of a Brinkman carbon nanotube (CNT)-based nanofluid containing motile microorganisms within a channel was analytically investigated using the Laplace transform technique. Exact solutions for velocity, temperature, and microorganism concentration profiles were obtained under a moving boundary condition with constant wall temperature, enabling a comprehensive analysis of the governing physical parameters.

From a qualitative perspective, the results reveal that bioconvection significantly influences the flow dynamics through the interaction between motile microorganisms and nanoparticle-enhanced fluid properties. The increase in bioconvection Lewis number reduces the microorganism concentration while enhancing the fluid velocity. Similarly, the Grashof number promotes fluid motion due to buoyancy effects, whereas higher values of the bioconvection Rayleigh number and Brinkman parameter suppress the velocity due to increased resistance and stability effects in the porous medium. In addition, an increase in the Prandtl number weakens both thermal diffusion and fluid motion, resulting in lower temperature and velocity profiles. The incorporation of CNTs nanoparticles enhances thermal conductivity, leading to increased temperature and velocity, but reduces the microorganism concentration due to intensified mixing effects.

Quantitatively, it is observed that increasing the CNTs volume fraction from (ϕ =0.01 to 0.02) results in an approximate increase of 41.4% in velocity and 21% in temperature, while decreasing the microorganism concentration by 0.05%. Furthermore, an increase in the bioconvection Lewis number leads to a reduction in microorganism density by approximately 12.1%, accompanied by a moderate enhancement in velocity. The Brinkman parameter is found to reduce velocity by 3.4%, highlighting the strong influence of porous medium resistance on flow behavior. These quantitative findings emphasize the sensitivity of the system to key parameters and their role in optimizing transport phenomena.

Despite these contributions, the present study has several limitations. The analysis assumes a simplified channel geometry, uniform dispersion of CNT nanoparticles, and neglects effects such as nanoparticle shape, chemical reactions, magnetic field and porosity. Additionally, idealized boundary conditions are considered, which may not fully represent complex real-world water treatment systems.

Future research may extend this work by incorporating more realistic physical conditions, such as variable properties, non-uniform nanoparticle distributions [51], and complex geometries such as cylindrical [52] or circular tube [53]. The inclusion of additional effects, such as magnetic fields [54], chemical reactions [55], and thermal radiation [22], would further enhance the model. Moreover, numerical simulations and machine learning validation using artificial neural network (ANN) [56] are recommended to verify the analytical results and improve their applicability. Investigating hybrid nanofluids and advanced porous media models could also provide further improvements in filtration efficiency and contaminant removal processes.

Overall, this study provides both qualitative and quantitative insights into bioconvective Brinkman nanofluid flow with CNTs, offering valuable theoretical guidance for the design and optimization of water treatment and filtration systems.
