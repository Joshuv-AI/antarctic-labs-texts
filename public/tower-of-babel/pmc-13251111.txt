Patient satisfaction and prosthodontics
Authors: Tong Wah Lim, Arpit Sikri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatient satisfaction is one of the key determinants of success in prosthodontic care. This Collection examines how satisfaction is defined and measured, the roles of technology, communication, expectations, and sociocultural context, and existing knowledge gaps, to guide future research and practice in truly patient-centred prosthodontic care.

Prosthodontics has traditionally been assessed using objective clinical parameters such as prosthesis quality, occlusal harmony, clinical complications or failures, and success or survival rates, with a strong emphasis on the clinician’s perspective. However, modern dentistry increasingly recognises that clinical success does not necessarily equate to patient satisfaction: a prosthesis may meet ideal technical gold standards while the patient remains dissatisfied. This disconnect underscores that prosthodontic success is largely defined by clinicians in technical terms, whereas patients judge outcomes in terms of functional efficiency, aesthetic appearance, psychological adaptation, treatment experience, affordability, prognosis, and long-term maintenance.

At the same time, advances in contemporary digital dentistry in prosthodontics involve scanning dental records with intraoral or extraoral scanners connected to dedicated software, processing the resulting digital data with programs that enable prosthodontic treatment planning or visual design of restorations, and subsequently fabricating these prostheses using subtractive or additive manufacturing techniques [1]. These digital technologies, which include CAD/CAM (computer‑aided design/computer‑aided manufacturing) systems, comprehensive digital prosthodontic workflows, and increasingly the integration of artificial intelligence together with novel restorative materials, have improved precision, comfort and convenience, aesthetics, reproducibility, and durability, thereby influencing patient satisfaction. Yet overall satisfaction is not determined by advances in digital dentistry alone, and there is a risk that the allure of these technologies may lead clinicians to overpromise. Therefore, patient satisfaction and other related outcomes in prosthodontics are now recognised as inherently multidimensional constructs and have emerged as critical outcome measures in contemporary research and clinical practice.

Patient-reported outcome or experience measures

Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and patient-reported experience measures (PREMs) provide structured ways to capture patients’ perceptions of prosthodontic care. The growing use of validated instruments has enabled researchers to quantify subjective experiences in a systematic and reproducible manner. Contemporary evidence shows that PROM-based assessments yield insights that traditional clinical indices alone cannot capture [2, 3]. A recent systematic review classified PROMs into five broad domains: quality of life, functional status, symptoms and symptom burden, health behaviours, and patients’ experiences with healthcare [4]. Despite this progress, important challenges remain. PROM instruments often require cultural adaptation and linguistic validation to ensure reliability and validity across diverse populations. In addition, PREMs in dentistry are still largely based on customised questionnaires used in general practice, with relatively few studies focused on specific disciplines such as prosthodontics [5]. Existing PREM tools show methodological inconsistencies, highlighting the need for a standardized dental framework. Such a framework could better support patient‑centred care and enhance communication and adherence. Therefore, when traditional clinical parameters are combined with both PROMs and PREMs, they have the potential to provide robust benchmarks for overall treatment success in prosthodontics - an important frontier for both research and clinical practice.

The role of communication and expectation management

Effective clinician-patient communication is one of the most crucial predictors of satisfaction across dental disciplines and is a core clinical competency. Shared decision-making, anchored in transparent discussions about patient concerns, treatment options and limitations, risks, costs, treatment duration, anticipated outcomes, and maintenance requirements, helps establish trust and realistic expectations [6]. When expectations are misaligned with achievable outcomes, dissatisfaction frequently occurs, even when objective clinical parameters are favourable. Therefore, shared decision-making models supported by decision aids have gained prominence in prosthodontic rehabilitation, as they actively involve patients in treatment planning and enhance their sense of control [7]. In addition, digitally assisted technologies, such as digital smile design, can enhance communication, involve patients more actively in the treatment-planning phase, and help manage realistic expectations, but only if clinicians consciously use them for these purposes.

Psychological, social, and cultural determinants

Patient satisfaction may be influenced by psychological, social, and sociocultural factors. With the current ageing population, many older adult prosthodontic patients present with physical and cognitive impairments, compromised systemic health, polypharmacy, oral frailty or oral hypofunction, and poor oral health status, all of which affect prosthodontic care. Consequently, their determinants of satisfaction may differ from those of younger, healthier individuals. In addition, these special-care populations introduce additional layers of complexity. Prosthodontic rehabilitation and maintenance in such groups must account for simplicity of treatment, ease of maintenance, and caregiver involvement [8]. Holistic and multidisciplinary personalised care and support can significantly enhance satisfaction in these cohorts [9].

Long-term satisfaction and maintenance

Long-term follow-up and optimal maintenance care are essential for evaluating prosthodontic outcomes. Early satisfaction may decline over time due to complications, failures, adaptation difficulties, or hygiene challenges. Patient education that combines behavioural guidance with multimodal methods can improve comprehension, recall, and adherence, and may help sustain satisfaction [10]. Dissatisfaction often stems from inadequate pre-treatment consultation, when unanticipated adjustments or maintenance are misinterpreted as treatment failure. Thus, patient satisfaction is dynamic and requires longitudinal assessment.

Knowledge gaps and future directions

Studies on patient satisfaction and related outcomes in prosthodontics has expanded in recent years, yet important gaps remain. These include lack of standardised assessment tools and limited cross-cultural, comparative, and longitudinal multicentre studies for broader global representation. Future research should prioritise developing robust, culturally adaptable PROM and PREM instruments, integrating digital technologies for real-time feedback, and embedding education on patient values, communication skills, and interpretation of PROMs/PREMs into dental curricula. In addition, interdisciplinary collaboration with psychologists, geriatric specialists, and health economists may provide a more comprehensive understanding of patient satisfaction.

Conclusion

This Collection welcomes research assessing the clinical, technological, psychological, and sociocultural dimensions of patient satisfaction in prosthodontics. We particularly encourage submissions on digital workflows and material innovations that enhance patient experience, communication strategies between clinicians and patients, validation and application of PROMs/PREMs, and maintenance care across diverse cohorts, especially geriatric and special-needs populations. Technical excellence is the foundation of prosthodontics, but it is not the endpoint. By foregrounding patient-centred evaluation, this Collection seeks to advance a holistic framework for prosthodontic care in which functional performance, aesthetic outcomes, and lived patient experience are seamlessly integrated.
