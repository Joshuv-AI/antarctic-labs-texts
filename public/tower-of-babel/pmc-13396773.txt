Turing universal neural networks do not require global clocks
Authors: Hava T. Siegelmann, Roy N. Siegelmann, Stephen Chung, Chloé Becquey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recurrent neural networks were proven to be Turing universal in the 1990s, motivating computational complexity studies of spiking networks, neural Turing machines with differentiable activations, and transformers. At the time, neural networks were exploratory and small, whereas today large-scale deployment makes energy efficiency critical. We thus extend the development of computational foundations of neural networks to asynchronous networks. Asynchrony is modeled by updating a single randomly selected neuron per step, eliminating global updates and reducing energy use. While asynchrony introduces variability in update sequences and thus has often been considered impractical for computing, we introduce design constraints which lead to Turing universal asynchronous architectures. We prove universality both for asynchronous fixed architectures with varying-precision neurons and for variable architectures with fixed-precision neurons. These results advance the theoretical understanding of asynchronous networks, suggesting that they preserve full computational power, remain amenable for efficient training, and may achieve substantial reductions in energy use.

Introduction

State-of-the-art Artificial Intelligence (AI) typically manifests either as symbolic algorithms or as sub-symbolic neural networks. A key strength of mammalian brains lies in their ability to combine these two approaches, integrating adaptive distributed representation with symbolic computation and communication. Theoretically, Turing-universal neural networks also possess this dual capability.

The early 1990s proof that a fixed-architecture analog recurrent neural network (ARNN) can be Turing universal was, at the time, both surprising and influential1–3. Prior to this, it had been assumed that networks of neurons were limited to the computational capabilities of finite automata4. Our work challenged that belief and supported the use of neurons with simple affine net functions (as used today in deep learning), rather than the more complex neurons then considered necessary for neural computing. Following our results, a similar proof was established for spiking neurons5. We subsequently attained Turing universality for neurons with differentiable activation functions6, a result that inspired Google to propose DeepMind’s Neural Turing Machines, which enabled learning via backpropagation7,8. More recently, similar approaches have been applied to achieve universality with neurons restricted to fixed precision9,10, and have even been generalized to modern transformer networks11,12.

In this paper, we extend the original line of investigation on point neurons by exploring computability through two types of asynchronous neural architectures. In both versions, asynchronicity is modeled by randomly selecting a single neuron to update at each time step. Version 1 consists of neurons that can increase their precision as needed, whereas Version 2 employs fixed-precision neurons while allowing the network to increase the number of neurons.

At first glance, our focus on computing with asynchronous neural network architectures may appear counterintuitive. On the one hand, both asynchronous deep learning and asynchronous associative memory models have been shown to converge faster and yield better outcomes13–16, suggesting the feasibility of developing AI systems that not only improve performance but are also significantly more energy-efficient. On the other hand, asynchronous neural networks update neurons in different orders across runs, often leading to unpredictable outcomes; this explains why they have commonly been considered limited in capability17. Here, we demonstrate that, with appropriate design principles, asynchronous networks can indeed exhibit universal Turing machine capabilities, offering distinct advantages over synchronous networks.

To attain Turing power in asynchronous neural networks, we simulate a given universal Turing machine with neurons—first as a synchronous neural network, and subsequently as a Turing-universal asynchronous network. The intermediate step of designing a synchronous network does not prioritize minimization, energy efficiency, or related concerns, but instead focuses on architectures that can be readily translated into Turing-universal asynchronous networks. Details of the intermedicate networks appear in the Methods. Each of the two types of universal asynchronous neural networks introduced contains a variable component (precision or size) that expands to represent the active portion of the Turing tape used by the simulated Turing machine. Interestingly, the outputs of both architectures are equivalent and they require the same order of computation time. Both network types require six stages per simulated Turing step; both have cores composed of approximately 20% inhibitory/control neurons and 80% computational neurons; and both are small in size. This may suggest an analogy—likely coincidental—to the building blocks of the mammalian brain, mini-column of about 100 neurons each. Future directions in computational neuroscience and lifelong AI architectures built from such small building blocks will be discussed toward the end of the paper.

Spiking neural networks (SNNs) exemplify energy-efficient, event-driven computation, yet their discrete spike dynamics and non-differentiability hinder scalable training, despite numerous workarounds. Our approach introduces asynchronous neural networks that preserve the structure and trainability of conventional deep architectures while dispensing with a global clock. Looking forward, such networks may offer the best of both worlds: the adaptability and trainability of deep models together with the energy efficiency of spiking systems.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section “Results”, we present the neural model and the main findings including the design of both prototypical asynchronous networks. In Section “Discussion”, we discuss conclusions and suggest directions for future work. Section “Methods” includes further details of the proofs.

Results

We summarize the main results of this paper with two theorems:

Theorem 1

(Variable-precision, Fixed-architecture): There exists a Turing-universal asynchronous network with 61 neurons of variable (unbounded) precision.

Theorem 2

(Fixed-precision, Variable-architecture): There exists a Turing-universal asynchronous network consisting of 97 p-precision neurons (p≥2), together with an additional ⌈T(t)/p⌉ recruited neurons, where T(t) denotes the number of symbols on the simulated Turing tape at time t.

Both sides of the tape are encoded using a fractal scheme (e.g., Cantor encoding), which permits (a) arbitrarily long tapes to be represented as continuous values, and (b) reading (cell under the head), writing, and head movements to be performed in constant time1–3. In asynchronous networks, the same fractal encoding is required, but values must remain sufficiently separated from zero to distinguish a neuron not yet updated within the current “Turing step” from one legitimately updated to zero.

With fixed p-precision neurons, representing an unbounded tape requires constructing two neuronal chains. The left-tape and right-tape neurons hold the p symbols closest to the read/write head, serving as short-term memory (9,10). The remaining tape symbols, farther from the head, are stored in two chains (or stacks) of p-precision neurons. Under this structure, the total number of updates—and hence the time spent per cycle (simulated Turing step)—remains constant, independent of memory size. Thus, both asynchronous models exhibit the same order of time complexity. Managing these chains with high efficiency also holds for asynchronous neurons, though it requires special attention and additional neurons.

Simulating synchronous networks within an asynchronous framework requires not only adapting neural update equations and ensuring representability, but also implementing a handshaking mechanism. This mechanism ensures that neuronal inputs are available within a bounded time window—neither too early nor too late—and remain stable during the update. We achieve this through a two-level inhibitory control scheme, with the handshaking neurons themselves operating asynchronously. We are now ready to move on to the detailed constructions.

For the conversion from the 44-neuron fixed-size (variable-precision) Turing-universal (synchronous) Neural Network (TNN) to its 61-neuron fixed-size (variable-precision) asynchronous counterpart, we first alter the neuron update rule from the ones used in synchronous (see Methods or e.g., [1,2,3]) to (1) and then coordinate the asynchronous network to transition robustly between stages. Instead of three stages per simulated Turing step as in the synchronous neural network, six are required in asynchronous ones. We also introduce gating neurons that indicate when a stage is complete and ready to proceed to the next, along with several auxiliary neurons that support the gating process.

Asynchronous design: Turing-complete fixed-precision neural networks

The following construction converts a 62-neuron synchronous network with fixed precision (described in “Methods”, Section “62-neuron Fixed-precision Synchronous TNN”) into an asynchronous form, resulting in a 97-neuron fixed-precision ANT. Optional Complementary Tape and Next-tape Neurons Replacements (4 neurons):

For the asynchronous networks considered in this paper, we apply the encoding method described above, which maps the tape and next-tape neuron values into [0.5, 0.6) by multiplying the current value by 0.1 and adding 0.5. Once both neurons are updated, their sum equals 1, allowing the reduction from two flag neurons to one.

In the context of fixed-precision asynchronous networks, however, this encoding incurs the cost of one symbol per neuron, thereby reducing the available precision to p − 1 symbols. While this is not problematic for high-precision networks, in low-precision networks it leads to significantly more frequent stack operations. In the extreme case of a neuron capable of holding only a single symbol, the neuron would retain no information regarding the tapes.

We therefore offer the alternative of omitting the encoding into [0.5, 0.6) and instead replacing the complementary tape and next-tape neurons with four flag neurons (rather than two): 21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{j,fill}(t+1) =\sigma (10{S}_{j}(t)),\\ {S}_{j,fill}^{{\prime} }(t+1) =\sigma (10({S}_{j}^{{\prime} }(t)+{S}_{j}^{{\prime\prime} }(t))),$$\end{document}Sj,fill(t+1)=σ(10Sj(t)),Sj,fill′(t+1)=σ(10(Sj′(t)+Sj″(t))), As before, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{j}^{{\prime} }(t)$$\end{document}Sj′(t) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{j}^{{\prime\prime} }(t)$$\end{document}Sj″(t) always include one neuron with value 0, allowing both neurons to be checked simultaneously. Since the minimum of the tape’s fractal encoding is 0.1, the complementary neuron will equal 1 if and only if the tape or the temporary tape neurons have been populated.

A final subtlety remains concerning the simulation: to prevent multiple pushes or pops within the same stage, the stack mechanism can be triggered only once per stage, with the stage neurons Ci(t) activated and set to 1.

Discussion

Our results may have implications for both neuroscience and AI. In neuroscience, the resemblance of our asynchronous networks to mini-columns—although possibly coincidental—suggests a potential explanation for the functionality of these cortical building blocks. Their architecture indicates that each mini-column may be capable of running an algorithm while simultaneously influencing the inputs of others. Translated to lifelong learning in AI, this implies that small algorithmic components connected through adaptable links could provide a feasible solution to lifelong learning—one that avoids catastrophic forgetting and remains reliable during changes to the environment.

Our work also demonstrates the feasibility of an improved form of AI. Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) are increasingly employed for their energy efficiency, leveraging event-based computation through discrete spikes. Lacking a global clock, SNNs naturally reduce energy consumption. While some spiking networks have been proven Turing universal, these typically rely on deterministic updates (e.g.,18–23) or incorporate external synchronization mechanisms to ensure local synchrony24,25. In practice, however, deep SNNs face significant training difficulties. The non-differentiable nature of spike generation disrupts standard gradient-based optimization, and existing workarounds—such as surrogate gradients or indirect training via conversion from standard networks—often introduce additional complexity, instability, or diminished performance. Despite years of development, training deep SNNs reliably and at scale remains a central challenge, limiting their adoption in mainstream machine learning.

Our asynchronous neural networks present a compelling alternative. By employing point neurons, they retain architectural compatibility with conventional deep networks, enabling weights to be trained using standard methods and then transferred directly—thereby avoiding the complications and losses associated with SNN training. At the same time, these networks operate without a global clock, eliminating the energy overhead of synchronized computation. Despite their asynchronous design, they preserve full computational power, as demonstrated in this paper. This establishes the feasibility of future AI systems that combine the trainability of deep networks with the efficiency of event-driven neurons, offering a scalable paradigm that unites the advantages of both SNNs (low energy) and clocked deep neural networks (adaptability).

Other directions for future work include incorporating our recent research on reinforcement learning, which demonstrates that reliable decisions can be derived from a subset of sensory data26. This approach could yield small asynchronous networks capable of producing accurate results from minimal or fragmented datasets, making them particularly suitable for on-device and edge applications. We also intend to examine the hardware implications of supporting low-energy neural networks.

While these implications and applications will be explored in future work, the present paper concentrates on foundational results—most notably, establishing the perhaps surprising finding that a global clock is not required for Turing-universal networks of simple point neurons.

62-neuron fixed-precision synchronous TNN

A fixed-precision synchronous neural network simulating U6,4 was proposed in Theorem 2 of10. To facilitate intuitive conversion to an asynchronous neural network, we employ a slightly modified version with 62 core neurons. The network architecture is a standard RNN augmented with an external stack, and the neuron update equations take the same form as Eq. (1), except without multiplication by control neurons (I = 1).

The design of the synchronous network can be summarized as follows:

1. Stack mechanism

We assume that the RNN is equipped with two stacks, referred to as the left- and right stacks, which consist of strings of connected neurons. Each stack is associated with two additional neurons: a push neuron Uj(t) and a pop neuron Oj(t), where j ∈ {l, r}. As their names suggest, the push and pop neurons control the corresponding stack operations. Specifically, after the RNN has updated the neuron values at step t: (i) if Uj(t) > 0.1, a new neuron with value Uj(t) is pushed onto the stack and Uj(t) is reset to 0.1; and (ii) if Oj(t) = 0.1, the top neuron is popped from the stack and Oj(t) is assigned the value of the new top element. By updating the push and pop neurons, the RNN can push values to, or pop values from, the stacks and associate them with the 62-neuron core network. We regard the lists of neurons in the left- and right stacks as the network’s memory (or growing memory module), denoted Ml(t) and Mr(t).

Note that this stack mechanism differs from that in ref. 3, which uses 0 rather than 0.1 to detect when pushing and popping are required. In our asynchronous networks, however, 0 is reserved to indicate that a neuron’s value has not yet been updated. By employing a distinct value for detecting push and pop operations, the conversion to an asynchronous network becomes more straightforward.

2. Fractal encoding of the tape

Since each neuron can store at most p digits, we assign one neuron to encode every p symbols on the tape. The tape neurons hold the symbols closest to the read/write head, while the stack neurons store those further away, with the most distant symbols placed at the end of the string. A bias of 0.1 is added to each neuron.

Example. If the tape contains the sequence (0.30.50.3〈0.1〉0.30.50.50.70.1), where 〈〉 indicates the position of the read/write head and p = 2, then the neurons in the stack are fully utilized p-precision neurons, filled from the portion of the tape farthest from the head. In this case, the initialization is given by Ml(1) = [〈0.53〉] and Mr(1) = [0.71, 〈0.55〉], where 〈〉 now denotes the topmost neuron. The tape neurons are initialized as Sl(1) = 0.13 and Sr(1) = 0.3. To prevent neurons from taking the value 0, we add a 0.1 bias to each neuron. Thus, the initialization becomes Ml(1) = [〈0.63〉], Mr(1) = [0.81, 〈0.65〉], Sl(1) = 0.23, and Sr(1) = 0.4. An illustration of this configuration is provided in Fig. 3b.

We encode the top symbol of the left- and right-tapes using the readout neurons Sl(t), Sr(t) and the complementary readout neurons \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{l}^{{\prime} }(t),{S}_{r}^{{\prime} }(t)$$\end{document}Sl′(t),Sr′(t). The encoding method is identical to that employed in the variable-precision TNN.

3. Binary encoding of the control states

The six control states {q1, q2, q3, q4, q5, q6} are represented by six state neurons, Qi(t) for i ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}, using one-hot encoding, as in the variable-precision TNN.

4. Update equations of the network

The general idea of the construction is that the required update can be carried out in two steps. In the first step, we apply equations analogous to those in the variable-precision TNN to update the left-tape neuron. The second step involves three possible cases:If the updated left-tape neuron holds y symbols, where 1≤y≤p, no pushing or popping is required, and neither the left-tape neuron nor the left-tape stack requires further updating;If the updated left-tape neuron holds 0 symbols, the top neuron (p symbols) must be popped from the left-tape stack, and the popped symbols are transferred to the left-tape neuron;If the updated left-tape neuron holds p + 1 symbols, the bottom p symbols must be pushed to a new left-tape stack neuron, and these pushed symbols are then removed from the left-tape neuron.

If the updated left-tape neuron holds y symbols, where 1≤y≤p, no pushing or popping is required, and neither the left-tape neuron nor the left-tape stack requires further updating;

If the updated left-tape neuron holds y symbols, where 1≤y≤p, no pushing or popping is required, and neither the left-tape neuron nor the left-tape stack requires further updating;

If the updated left-tape neuron holds 0 symbols, the top neuron (p symbols) must be popped from the left-tape stack, and the popped symbols are transferred to the left-tape neuron;

If the updated left-tape neuron holds 0 symbols, the top neuron (p symbols) must be popped from the left-tape stack, and the popped symbols are transferred to the left-tape neuron;

If the updated left-tape neuron holds p + 1 symbols, the bottom p symbols must be pushed to a new left-tape stack neuron, and these pushed symbols are then removed from the left-tape neuron.

If the updated left-tape neuron holds p + 1 symbols, the bottom p symbols must be pushed to a new left-tape stack neuron, and these pushed symbols are then removed from the left-tape neuron.

A similar process applies to the right-tape, as illustrated in Fig. 3b.
