Sums of nearest integer continued fractions with bounded digits: NICF 5 + NICF 5 = ℝ {\rm NICF}_{5}+{\rm NICF}_{5}={\mathbb{R}}
Wieb Bosma, Alex Brouwers

Abstract




1
Introduction

Slightly dependent on one’s perspective, there are two
main defects that continued fractions suffer as an
alternative (to the decimal, or binary) representation of real numbers: they
require an infinite alphabet for the ‘digits’, and arithmetic is problematic.

In the following theorem of Hall, from 1947, one could say that the first
defect is shifted to the second.

Theorem 1

(Hall
[
6
]
, 1947)
.

For every real number
x
there exist real numbers
u,v

such that
x=u+v
and both
u,v
have the property that
their regular partial fractions
b_{i}
are bounded by 4
(with the possible exception of
b_{0}
).

The partial fractions
b_{i}=b_{i}(x)
are integers in the
regular continued fraction expansion (RCF) of a real number
x
as

{\rm RCF}(x)=b_{0}+\frac{1}{\displaystyle b_{1}+\frac{1}{\displaystyle b_{2}+\frac{1}{\displaystyle\ddots}}},

and they satisfy

b_{0}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
b_{i}\geq 1
(for
i\geq 1
).

The statement of Hall’s theorem is often summarized as

\mathbb{R}={\rm RCF}_{4}+{\rm RCF}_{4}.

Much later, in 1973, Cusick
[
4
]
and Divis
[
5
]
showed that
a symmetric improvement on this theorem is not possible, that
is to say, in analogous fashion:

\mathbb{R}\neq{\rm RCF}_{3}+{\rm RCF}_{3};

but Hlavka
[
7
]
showed in 1975 that

\mathbb{R}={\rm RCF}_{3}+{\rm RCF}_{4}.

The current paper is concerned with similar results for
the nearest integer continued fraction expansion (NICF)
of real numbers. In some respects the NICF is ‘better’
than the RCF; it selects the best approximations and converges
faster, but it is also more natural in the sense that it
allows an obvious generalization to continued fraction
expansions of complex numbers, namely the Hurwitz
expansion. See also
[
2
]
.

The easiest definition of both regular and nearest integer
continued fractions is by the algorithm used to obtain the
expansions. The RCF expansion
for a real number
x
can be obtained by taking
x_{0}=x

and iterating the steps

b_{i}=\lfloor x_{i}\rfloor
and
x_{i+1}=\frac{1}{x_{i}-b_{i}}

for
i\geq 0
, as long as
b_{i}\neq x_{i}
. It provides essentially
unique expansions for every real number (unique if we insist that
finite expansions, for rational numbers, do not end with
1
),
and every infinite sequence
[b_{0};b_{1},b_{2},\cdots]
with

b_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
satisfying
b_{i}\geq 1
for
i\geq 1
occurs as the
RCF expansion of a real number.

The NICF expansion is
obtained by replacing
b_{i}=\lfloor x_{i}\rfloor
by
a_{i}=\lfloor x_{i}\rceil
, which is the integer nearest to
x_{i}
. An important
difference with RCF is that in the NICF the
x_{i}
, and hence the

a_{i}
, may become negative,
so in

{\rm NICF}(x)=a_{0}+\frac{1}{\displaystyle a_{1}+\frac{1}{\displaystyle a_{2}+\frac{1}{\displaystyle\ddots}}},

we have
a_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
for
i\geq 1
; however,
the following conditions for
i\geq 1
describe precisely the sequences
of integers that arise as NICF expansions:

•

a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{-1,0,1\}
;

•

if
a_{i}=2
, then
a_{i+1}\geq 2
;

•

if
a_{i}=-2
, then
a_{i+1}\leq-2
.

From here on, digits
a_{i}
always refer to the nearest integer expansion.

The main result in this paper can be summarized as follows:

\mathbb{R}={\rm NICF}_{5}+{\rm NICF}_{5}.

(1)

More formally, the following analogon to Hall’s theorem will be proved.

Theorem 2
.

For every real number
x
there exist real numbers
u,v

such that
x=u+v
and both
u,v
have the property that
their nearest integer partial fractions
a_{i}
are bounded by 5
(with the possible exception of
a_{0}
).

We will also prove that

\mathbb{R}\neq{\rm NICF}_{4}+{\rm NICF}_{4}.

(2)

Our result is obtained essentially by adapting the
Cantor set methods of Hall and Hlavka directly
to nearest integer continued fractions. This is explained
in Section
2
and Section
3
.

In Section
4
we create a Cantor set,
C_{\operatorname{NICF}}
, and prove
in Section
5
it is equal to
\operatorname{NICF}_{5}^{*}
,
which is a subset of
\operatorname{NICF}_{5}
.
With the results of Section
2
and Section
3
, we
show in Section
5
that
C_{\operatorname{NICF}}+C_{\operatorname{NICF}}
contains an interval
of length 1:
C_{\operatorname{NICF}}+C_{\operatorname{NICF}}\supseteq[\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}]
from
which the main result, Theorem
2
, or Equation (
1
), will follow.
Finally, Section
6
is devoted to a proof of
inequality (
2
).

The only previous result in this direction that we
are aware of was mentioned in the M.Sc. thesis of
Aldenhoven (Nijmegen 2012,
[
1
]
), namely

\mathbb{R}={\rm NICF}_{6}+{\rm NICF}_{6},

a result directly obtained from that of Hall by using ‘singularization’
of regular continued fractions (see
[
1
]
Section 3.4):

{\rm RCF}_{4}\subset{\rm NICF}_{6}
.

2
Cantor sets

For any closed, real interval
[a,b]
, with
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
a<b
,
we denote its
length
by
\ell({[a,b]})=b-a
; the same
notation is used for the length of open, or half-open real
intervals
(a,b),[a,b),(a,b]
.

The
sum

A+B
of two closed real intervals
A=[a_{l},a_{r}],B=[b_{l},b_{r}]

will be defined by pointwise addition

A+B=\{x+y:x\in A,y\in B\}

and similarly for (half-)open intervals. In particular,
for closed intervals

A+B=[a_{l},a_{r}]+[b_{l},b_{r}]=[a_{l}+b_{l},a_{r}+b_{r}].

This sum is clearly commutative,
associative, and the length is additive, on closed intervals
A,B,C
:

A+B=B+A,\quad(A+B)+C=A+(B+C),\quad\textrm{and}\quad\ell({A+B})=\ell({A})+\ell({B}).

The following useful result on the union of intervals is also straightforward.

Lemma 3
.

Let
A
and
B
be closed intervals; if

\ell({A})+\ell({B})\geq\ell({[\min(a_{l},b_{l}),\max(a_{r},b_{r})]}),

then
A\cup B
is also a closed interval, namely

A\cup B=[\min(a_{l},b_{l}),\max(a_{r},b_{r})].

The Cantor sets
\cal C
we will be constructing arise in the following
way from an initial, closed interval
I_{1}
using a
gap function

g
: for every interval
I_{i}
the function
g
selects an open
gap interval
g(I_{i})\subset I_{i}
, determining two new closed intervals,
the left interval
I_{2i}
and the right interval
I_{2i+1}
, where

I_{i}=I_{2i}\cup g(I_{i})\cup I_{2i+1}
, disjointly.
Pictorially:
I_{1}
I_{2}

g(I_{1})

g(I_{2})

The prototype is the original Cantor set where
g
cuts out the middle third of any interval.
The
Cantor set
determined by
I_{1}
and
g
will by definition be the set
of points of
I_{1}
not contained in any gap:

{\cal C}={\cal C}(I_{1},g)=I_{1}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}g(I_{i}).

Note that all the
g(I_{i})
are disjoint, so
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\ell({g(I_{i})})\leq\ell({I_{1}})
. Because
\ell({I_{1}})
is finite, we have

\lim_{i\to\infty}\ell({g(I_{i})})=0.

Also note that we identify
{\cal C}
with its subset of points on
the real line, and thus it may be that
{\cal C}=(I_{1},f)=(J_{1},g)={\cal D}
although
their gap functions
f
and
g
are different; the initial intervals
will satisfy
I_{1}=J_{1}
in this case.

The relative size of gaps is given by the density ratio.

Definition 4
.

The
density ratio
of Cantor set
{\cal C}={\cal C}(I_{1},g)

is defined as:

{\rm dr}_{\cal C}={\rm dr}(I_{1},g)=\inf_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\min(\ell({I_{2i}}),\ell({I_{2i+1}}))}{\ell({g(I_{i})})}.

It follows that
\ell({I_{2i}})\geq{\rm dr}_{\cal C}\cdot\ell({g(I_{i})})

and
\ell({I_{2i+1}})\geq{\rm dr}_{\cal C}\cdot\ell({g(I_{i})})
, for all
i
.

Definition 5
.

We call a Cantor set
hole-decreasing
if for all
i
we have both

\ell({g(I_{2i})})\leq\ell({g(I_{i})})
and
\ell({g(I_{2i+1})})\leq\ell({g(I_{i})})
.

Equivalently: a Cantor set is hole-decreasing if and only if
for every
i
, for every
k
such that
I_{k}\subseteq I_{i}
we have

\ell({g(I_{k})})\leq\ell({g(I_{i})})
.

If we call a Cantor set
n
-hole-decreasing
if for every
i\leq n
,
for every
k
such that
I_{k}\subseteq I_{i}
we have

\ell({g(I_{k})})\leq\ell({g(I_{i})})
, it will be clear that
{\cal C}
is
hole-decreasing if and only if

{\cal C}
is
n
-hole-decreasing for every
n
.

By repeatedly interchanging an interval with its remainders,
depicted below, it is possible to transform the construction
of any Cantor set into a hole-decreasing construction. Suppose,
for example, that

while

\frac{\ell({J_{4i+2}})}{\ell({g^{\prime}(J_{2i+1})})}=\frac{\ell({I_{4i+1}})}{\ell({g(I_{i})})}>\frac{\ell({I_{4i+1}})}{\ell({g(I_{2i})})}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\frac{\ell({J_{4i+3}})}{\ell({g^{\prime}(J_{2i+1})})}=\frac{\ell({I_{2i+1}})}{\ell({g(I_{i})})},

from which it follows that

{\rm dr}(J_{1},g^{\prime})\geq{\rm dr}(I_{1},g)
.
We obtain the following.

Proposition 6
.

For every Cantor set
\cal C
there exists a hole-decreasing Cantor set

\cal D
such that
{\cal D}={\cal C}
and
{\rm dr}_{\cal D}\geq{\rm dr}_{\cal C}
.

3
Comparable and Dividable

In Section
4
we will see that bounded continued fractions
determine Cantor sets; for their sum we will want to add Cantor
sets, and this is defined in the obvious, pointwise, fashion:

{\cal C}_{1}+{\cal C}_{2}=\{x+y:x\in{\cal C}_{1},y\in{\cal C}_{2}\}
.
In this section, we give density requirements sufficient
to ensure for sums of Cantor
sets to ‘fill each other’s gaps’, that is, such that

{\cal C}_{1}+{\cal C}_{2}=I_{1}+I_{2}
, where
{\cal C}_{1}
and
{\cal C}_{2}

are constructed from the initial intervals
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
.
This is done in a similar way as done by Hlavka
in
[
7
]
, with
comparable
and
dividable
intervals.
At the end of this section we show that our requirements are slightly
weaker than Hlavka’s.

First a lemma.

Lemma 7
.

For a subinterval
I_{i}
of a Cantor set
{\cal C}=(I,g)

and any interval
J
:

\ell({J})\geq\ell({g(I_{i})})\quad\Rightarrow\quad(I_{2i}+J)\cup(I_{2i+1}+J)=I_{i}+J.

(3)

Proof.

Apply Lemma
3
with
A=I_{2i}+J
and
B=I_{2i+1}+J

together with:

\ell({I_{i}+J})=\ell({I_{2i}})+\ell({g(I_{i})})+\ell({I_{2i+1}})+\ell({J})\leq\ell({I_{2i}+J})+\ell({I_{2i+1}+J}).

∎

Theorem 8
.

Suppose that
{\cal C}_{1}=(I_{1}^{1},g^{1}),\ldots,{\cal C}_{n}=(I_{1}^{n},g^{n})
are
n\geq 2
Cantor sets for which the density ratios satisfy

{\rm dr}_{{\cal C}_{i}}\geq x_{i}
, for
1\leq i\leq n
, with

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_{i}}{x_{i}+1}\geq 1

(4)

and

\forall j,k:\ \ell({I_{1}^{j}})\geq\frac{x_{j}}{x_{j}+1}(x_{k}+1)\ell({I_{1}^{k}}).

(5)

Then

{\cal C}_{1}+{\cal C}_{2}+\cdots+{\cal C}_{n}=I_{1}^{1}+I_{1}^{2}+\cdots+I_{1}^{n}.

The remainder of this section is geared towards a proof of this
theorem.

First we note that we may as well assume that the Cantor sets
in the theorem are hole-decreasing, since by Proposition
6

there exist Cantor sets
{\cal D}_{i}
with
{\cal D}_{i}={\cal C}_{i}

that are hole-decreasing and for which

{\rm dr}_{{\cal D}_{i}}\geq{\rm dr}_{{\cal C}_{i}}
.

Definition 9
.

Let
I_{i_{j}}^{j}
for
1\leq j\leq n
, be subintervals
in the construction of hole-decreasing Cantor sets

{\cal C}_{j}=(I_{1}^{j},g^{j})
for which

{\rm dr}({\cal C}_{j})\geq x_{j}

such that (
4
) and (
5
) hold.
Then
I_{i_{1}}^{1},I_{i_{2}}^{2},\ldots,I_{i_{n}}^{n}
are called
comparable
if

\forall j,k:\ \ell({I_{i_{j}}^{j}})\geq\frac{x_{j}}{x_{j}+1}(x_{k}+1)\ell({g^{k}(I_{i_{k}}^{k})}).

Note that the initial intervals

I_{1}^{1},I_{1}^{2},\ldots,I_{1}^{n}
for such Cantor sets
are always comparable since

\ell({I_{1}^{k}})\geq\ell({g^{k}(I_{1}^{k})})
.

Lemma 10
.

If
I_{i_{1}}^{1},I_{i_{2}}^{2},\ldots,I_{i_{n}}^{n}
are comparable intervals,
then for all
k
:

\sum_{j=1}^{n}I_{i_{j}}^{j}=\left(\sum_{j=1;j\neq k}^{n}I_{i_{j}}^{j}+I_{2i_{k}}^{k}\right)\cup\left(\sum_{j=1;j\neq k}^{n}I_{i_{j}}^{j}+I_{2i_{k}+1}^{k}\right).

Proof.

We would like to apply (
3
) with
\sum_{j=1;j\neq k}^{n}I_{i_{j}}^{j}

and
I_{i_{k}}^{k}
for
J
and
I_{i}
, respectively.
What remains to be shown is that
\ell({\sum_{j=1;j\neq k}^{n}I_{i_{j}}^{j}})\geq\ell({g^{k}(I_{i_{k}}^{k})})
. Indeed, comparability implies

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1;j\neq k}^{n}\ell({I_{i_{j}}^{j}})

\displaystyle\geq\left(\sum_{j=1;j\neq k}^{n}\frac{x_{j}}{x_{j}+1}\right)(x_{k}+1)\ell({g^{k}(I_{i_{k}}^{k})})

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{x_{j}}{x_{j}+1}-\frac{x_{k}}{x_{k}+1}\right)(x_{k}+1)\ell({g^{k}(I_{i_{k}}^{k})})

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{largerthan1}}{\geq}\left(1-\frac{x_{k}}{x_{k}+1}\right)(x_{k}+1)\ell({g^{k}(I_{i_{k}}^{k})})

\displaystyle=\ell({g^{k}(I_{i_{k}}^{k})}).

∎

Definition 11
.

Comparable intervals
I_{i_{1}}^{1},I_{i_{2}}^{2},\ldots,I_{i_{n}}^{n}
are

j
-
dividable
if both

I_{i_{1}}^{1},I_{i_{2}}^{2},\ldots,I_{2i_{j}}^{j},\ldots,I_{i_{n}}^{n}
and

I_{i_{1}}^{1},I_{i_{2}}^{2},\ldots,I_{2i_{j}+1}^{j},\ldots,I_{i_{n}}^{n}

are comparable intervals.

Lemma 12
.

Let comparable intervals
I_{i_{1}}^{1},I_{i_{2}}^{2},\ldots,I_{i_{n}}^{n}
be given.
If
j
is such that
(x_{j}+1)\ell({g^{j}(I_{i_{j}}^{j})})
is maximal, i.e.,

\forall k:(x_{j}+1)\ell({g^{j}(I_{i_{j}}^{j})})\geq(x_{k}+1)\ell({g^{k}(I_{i_{k}}^{k})}),

then
I_{i_{1}}^{1},I_{i_{2}}^{2},\ldots,I_{i_{n}}^{n}
are
j
-dividable.

Proof.

Since the Cantor sets are hole-decreasing, we have that

\ell({g^{j}(I_{2i_{j}}^{j})})\leq\ell({g^{j}(I_{i_{j}}^{j})})
and
\ell({g^{j}(I_{2i_{j}+1}^{j})})\leq\ell({g^{j}(I_{i_{j}}^{j})})
.
We still have to prove

\forall k:\ell({I_{2j}^{j}})\geq\frac{x_{j}}{x_{j}+1}(x_{k}+1)\ell({g^{k}(I_{i_{k}}^{k})})

and

\forall k:\ell({I_{2j+1}^{j}})\geq\frac{x_{j}}{x_{j}+1}(x_{k}+1)\ell({g^{k}(I_{i_{k}}^{k})}).

As both

\ell({I_{2i_{j}}^{j}})\geq x_{j}\ell({g^{j}(I_{i_{j}}^{j})})
and

\ell({I_{2i_{j}+1}^{j}})\geq x_{j}\ell({g^{j}(I_{i_{j}}^{j})})
,
it is sufficient to note that:

x_{j}\ell({g^{j}(I_{i_{j}}^{j})})\geq x_{j}\ell({g^{j}(I_{i_{j}}^{j})})\frac{(x_{k}+1)\ell({g^{k}(I_{i_{k}}^{k})})}{(x_{j}+1)\ell({g^{j}(I_{i_{j}}^{j})})}=\frac{x_{j}}{x_{j}+1}(x_{k}+1)\ell({g^{k}(I_{i_{k}}^{k})}).

∎

Proof of Theorem
8
.

We will recursively create sets
G_{m}
on
n
-tuples of intervals.
Start with

G_{0}=\{\langle I_{1}^{1},I_{1}^{2},\ldots,I_{1}^{n}\rangle\};

and then, for

:

\displaystyle G_{m+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\langle I_{i_{1}}^{1},I_{i_{2}}^{2},\ldots,I_{2i_{k}}^{k},\ldots,I_{i_{n}}^{n}\rangle,\langle I_{i_{1}}^{1},I_{i_{2}}^{2},\ldots,I_{2i_{k}+1}^{k},\ldots,I_{i_{n}}^{n}\rangle\right.\ \ |

\displaystyle\quad\langle I_{i_{1}}^{1},I_{i_{2}}^{2},\ldots,I_{i_{k}}^{k},\ldots,I_{i_{n}}^{n}\rangle\in G_{m}\quad\land

\displaystyle\quad\quad\left.\forall l:x_{k}\cdot\ell({g^{k}(I_{i_{k}}^{k})})\geq x_{l}\cdot\ell({g^{l}(I_{i_{l}}^{l})})\right\}.

Trivially, every
\langle I_{i_{1}}^{1},I_{i_{2}}^{2},\ldots,I_{i_{n}}^{n}\rangle\in G_{0}
is comparable. With induction
and Lemma
12
, we have that

\langle I_{i_{1}}^{1},I_{i_{2}}^{2},\ldots,I_{i_{n}}^{n}\rangle\in G_{m}

is comparable for every
m
, and therefore

\bigcup\left\{\sum I_{i_{j}}^{j}\middle|\langle I_{i_{1}}^{1},I_{i_{2}}^{2},\ldots,I_{i_{n}}^{n}\rangle\in G_{m}\right\}=\sum I_{1}^{j}.

Let
g^{k}(I_{i_{k}}^{k})
be a gap of
C^{k}
, then for every
j
there
are only a finite number
a_{j}
of gaps
g^{j}(I_{l}^{j})
such that

\ell({g^{j}(I_{l_{j}}^{j})})\geq\frac{x_{k}}{x_{j}}\ell({g^{k}(I_{i_{k}}^{k})})
.
For every
a\geq\sum a_{j}
, there will be no
\langle I_{i_{1}}^{1},I_{i_{2}}^{2},\ldots,I_{i_{n}}^{n}\rangle\in G_{a}
such that

g^{k}(I_{i_{k}}^{k})\subseteq I_{i_{k}}^{k}
.

Let us define
G
as:

G=\lim_{i\to\infty}G_{i}.

Then for each
\langle I_{i_{1}}^{1},I_{i_{2}}^{2},\ldots,I_{i_{n}}^{n}\rangle\in G

we have
I_{i_{j}}^{j}\subset C^{j}
.
Therefore

\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\middle|x_{i}\in C^{i}\right\}\subseteq\bigcup\left\{\sum c_{j}\middle|\langle I_{i_{1}}^{1},I_{i_{2}}^{2},\ldots,I_{i_{n}}^{n}\rangle\in G\land c_{j}\in I_{i_{j}}^{j}\right\}=\sum I_{1}^{j}.

∎

We give one more theorem, which is less general but will be easier to use.

Theorem 13
.

Given Cantor sets
{\cal C}^{1},{\cal C}^{2},\ldots{\cal C}^{n}
with
density ratios equal to
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_{i}}{x_{i}+1}\geq 1;

(6)

and

\forall j,k:\ell({I_{1}^{j}})\geq\frac{x_{j}}{x_{j}+1}\frac{x_{k}+1}{2x_{k}+1}\ell({I_{1}^{k}}).

(7)

Then

\sum_{i=1}^{n}I_{1}^{i}=\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}:x_{i}\in{\cal C}^{i}\}.

Proof.

First, notice that:

\displaystyle\ell({I_{i_{k}}^{k}})

\displaystyle=\ell({I_{2i_{k}}^{k}})+\ell({g(I_{i_{k}}^{k})})+\ell({I_{2i_{k}+1}^{k}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell({I_{2i_{k}}^{k}})+\ell({g(I_{i_{k}}^{k})})+\ell({I_{2i_{k}+1}^{k}})}{\ell({g(I_{i_{k}}^{k})})}\cdot\ell({g(I_{i_{k}}^{k})})

\displaystyle\geq(2x_{k}+1)\ell({g(I_{i}^{k})}).

Since
\ell({I_{1}^{k}})\geq\ell({I_{j}^{k}})
for all
j
,
\max_{i}\ell({g(I_{i}^{k})})\leq\frac{1}{2x_{k}+1}\ell({I_{1}^{k}})
.

So, for all
j,k
:

\ell({I_{1}^{j}})\geq\frac{x_{j}}{x_{j}+1}\frac{x_{k}+1}{2x_{k}+1}\ell({I_{1}^{k}})\geq\frac{x_{j}}{x_{j}+1}(x_{k}+1)\max_{i}\ell({g(I_{i}^{k})}).

We finish by applying Theorem
8
.

Remark 14
.

In
[
7
]
, Hlavka has a similar result on sums of Cantor
sets; he defines, for
a Cantor set
{\cal C}=(I,g)
the
relative smallest remainder

H_{\cal C}
and
the relative biggest gap

G_{\cal C}
by

H_{\cal C}=\min\left(\min_{i}\frac{\ell({I_{2i}})}{\ell({I_{i}})},\,\min_{i}\frac{\ell({I_{2i+1}})}{\ell({I_{i}})}\right)\quad\textrm{and}\quad G_{\cal C}=\max_{i}\frac{\ell({g(I_{i})})}{\ell({I_{i}})}.

Clearly,
{\rm dr}_{\cal C}\geq\frac{H_{\cal C}}{G_{\cal C}}
.
He then proves, see
[
7
, Theorem 3]
, that
{\cal C}_{1}+{\cal C}_{2}=I^{1}+I^{2}

Construction of the Cantor set

We now turn to the nearest integer continued fraction expansions, and first
introduce the subsets of reals with bounded expansion.

Definition 15
.

\operatorname{NICF}_{r}
is a subset of
\mathbb{R}
given by

\operatorname{NICF}_{r}=\left\{x:x\in\mathbb{R}\mid\operatorname{NICF}(x)=[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]\text{ and }\ |a_{i}|\leq r\text{ for }{i\geq 1}\right\}.

From the rules for
\operatorname{NICF}
given in the Introduction, it follows that

x\in\operatorname{NICF}_{5}
, with
{\rm NICF}(x)=[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]

if and only if for all
i\geq 1

•

a_{i}\in\{-5,-4,-3,-2,2,3,4,5\}
;

•

if
a_{i}=2
, then
a_{i+1}\in\{2,3,4,5\}
;

•

if
a_{i}=-2
, then
a_{i+1}\in\{-2,-3,-4,-5\}
.

Definition 16
.

\operatorname{NICF}_{5}^{*}
is the subset of
\operatorname{NICF}_{5}\cap[0,1]
containing only the numbers
represented by a nearest integer continued fraction satisfying:

\text{if }|a_{i}|=5\text{ then }\sign{a_{i}}\neq\sign{a_{i+1}}.

As a consequence,
\operatorname{NICF}_{5}^{*}
satisfies additional rules
to the ones for
\operatorname{NICF}_{5}

if
{\rm NICF}(x)=[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]
then
x\in\operatorname{NICF}_{5}^{*}
if and only if

•

a_{0}\in\{0,1\}
;

•

if
a_{0}=0
, then
a_{1}\in\{2,3,4,5\}
;

•

if
a_{0}=1
, then
a_{1}\in\{-2,-3,-4,-5\}
;

•

if
a_{i}=5
, then
a_{i+1}\in\{-2,-3,-4,-5\}

•

if
a_{i}=-5
, then
a_{i+1}\in\{2,3,4,5\}

It easily follows that for all

and
x\in\operatorname{NICF}_{5}^{*}

we have

.

We will show that
\operatorname{NICF}_{5}^{*}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
can be described as a Cantor set,
with density ratio greater than 1, and an initial interval with size
greater than
\frac{1}{2}
.

Let

be defined by
\mu=\frac{5+\sqrt{21}}{2}=4.791287\cdots
.

Lemma 17
.

Both
\frac{1}{\mu}
and

are in

in

holds

\mu=5-\frac{1}{\mu}

using the standard comparison result for infinite

Finally,
\frac{1}{\mu}=0.208712\cdots

Theorem 30
.

\operatorname{NICF}_{4}+\operatorname{NICF}_{4}\neq\mathbb{R}

Proof.

It will be clear (from replacing, if necessary,
x,y
by

x-\lfloor x\rceil,y-\lfloor y\rceil
) that
z\in{\mathbb{R}}

can be written as
z=x+y
with
x,y\in\operatorname{NICF}_{4}
if and only if

z\equiv x+y\bmod 1
with
x,y\in\operatorname{NICF}_{4}\cap\ [-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
.

The subset
\operatorname{NICF}_{4,\mathbb{Q}}
of
\mathbb{Q}
is countable, and hence

\operatorname{NICF}_{4,\mathbb{Q}}+\operatorname{NICF}_{4,\mathbb{Q}}
has Lebesgue-measure 0.

By a standard argument for Cantor sets, also
\operatorname{NICF}_{4,\setminus\mathbb{Q}}
has Lebesgue-measure 0:
it is contained in sequence of finite unions of finite intervals
shrinking to Lebesgue measure zero. As a consequence, also
the set
\{x+y\mid x\in\operatorname{NICF}_{4,\mathbb{Q}},y\in\operatorname{NICF}_{4,\setminus\mathbb{Q}}\}
has Lebesgue-measure 0,
by subadditivity of the Lebesgue-measure.
By Corollary
29

\operatorname{NICF}_{4,\setminus\mathbb{Q}}+\operatorname{NICF}_{4,\setminus\mathbb{Q}}
misses the
interval
[0.372295,0.372305]
and hence has Lebesgue measure
at most
0.99999
.

Therefore,
\operatorname{NICF}_{4}+\operatorname{NICF}_{4}\neq\mathbb{R}
.
