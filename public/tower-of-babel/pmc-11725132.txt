New functions of oxylipins released by pyroptotic cells
Authors: Hui Xu, Hong-Li Tan, Hua-Jun Wang, Xiao-Fei Zheng, Yan-Ping Wu, Rong-Rong He
Published: 2024

Pyroptosis typically features the perforation of cell membranes, a process triggered by the exposure of active N-terminal domain of Gasdermin D (GSDMD). This pivotal event subsequently leads to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, notably IL-1α and IL-1β, thereby fostering tissue inflammation and impeding the repair process1. Moreover, the question of whether small molecule metabolites expelled through these pyroptotic pores possess specific physiological functions has consistently captivated the scientific community's curiosity. A recent study published in Nature by Mehrotra et al.2 unveiled a novel paradigm, suggesting that oxygenated polyunsaturated fatty acids (oxylipins), released from pyroptotic cells, can paradoxically promote wound healing. This research demonstrates, as the famous saying goes, “From death comes rebirth”, innovatively revealing the interdependent relationship between cell death, inflammation, and repair.

Pyroptosis can be classified into classical and non-classical pathways based on caspase-1 activation, each of which results in the substantial release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. To isolate the effects from these factors, the authors developed a model termed Pyro−1, where pyroptosis occurs without the release of main pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1α and IL-1β. RNA-seq analysis from macrophage co-incubation with Pyro−1 supernatant revealed significant upregulation of genes associated with cell migration, proliferation, and immune responses crucial for tissue repair. Notably, these effects persisted even after protein removal from the Pyro−1 supernatant, suggesting the involvement of non-protein metabolites in tissue repair promotion. The authors identified the oxylipins as a major component in Pyro−1 supernatants through database analysis and lipidomic assays, and they verified the wound healing effects of Pyro−1 supernatant in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, the authors uncover that the oxylipins, specifically prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), de novo synthesis occurs downstream of caspase-1 activation and cyclooxygenase (COX) activity, with its release dependent on GSDMD pores formation during pyroptosis (Fig. 1). Collectively, when protein-based inflammatory factors are excluded, the liberation of certain small molecule metabolites from pyroptotic cells demonstrates a beneficial impact on the wound healing process. While programmed cell death is observed in various physiological processes such as aging3, its main features continue to be the activation of specific gene expression and the systematic execution of cell death in response to different damaging stimuli. Unlike apoptosis, which maintains cell membrane integrity, pyroptosis “paradoxically” exerts adverse effects on surrounding tissues despite serving as a self-protective mechanism. In this study, authors discovered a class of endogenous lipid molecules, oxylipins, that were previously obscured and could resolve this “paradox”. PGE2, an inflammation-related oxylipin, can promote tissue repair, thereby counteracting the inflammatory damage caused by pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-1β to a certain extent. Further investigations revealed that these oxylipins’ release following the activation of caspase-1 and COX, indicating that they are newly synthesized metabolites during pyroptosis.

Nonetheless, previous studies have demonstrated that PGE2 can be released during other forms of programmed cell death, such as apoptosis, independent of GSDMD pore formation4. This suggests that the release of PGE2, an endogenous lipid molecule, might not be solely dependent on GSDMD pores which can increase the efflux of PGE2, but could also be involved by direct transmembrane transport. It is noteworthy that recent significant studies have found that the Gasdermin family is involved in tissue damage repair5, which raises the question of whether Gasdermin and PGE2 play a synergistic role in the tissue repair process facilitated by pyroptosis. Regrettably, the precise mechanisms by which caspase-1 activation induces COX activity, and whether Gasdermin or other oxidases involved in oxylipin synthesis, such as lipoxygenases (5-LOX, 12/15-LOX) and cytochrome P450 enzymes, are similarly affected during pyroptosis, remain unresolved and warrant further investigation. Perhaps future research could seize this opportunity to analyze and summarize the functional differences among the same class of oxylipins that arise from different production pathways. Importantly, the emergence of these oxylipins appears to correlate with the temporal progression of pyroptosis. Based on these findings, the study hypothesized that the composition of secreted substances may vary across different stages of pyroptosis. In addition to oxylipins, this study also found that a class of non-lipid metabolites, secreted from necrotic cells, such as spermidine can also promote tissue repair, which have been noted in previous studies6. Therefore, it is evident that the mechanisms by which necrosis mediates tissue repair are complex. Subsequent research could involve isolating different components to validate their biological effects, allowing for the assessment of deeper connections between the various substances.

As one of the three major nutrients in the body, lipids have traditionally been recognized for their roles in energy supply and cell membrane composition7. The advancements in mass spectrometry techniques have spotlighted a class of signal-transducing peroxidation products of lipids, including phospholipid peroxidation, which are primarily generated through enzymatic pathways mediated by PUFA8. Currently, oxylipins have been found to be involved in aging and other physiological processes to varying degrees, but in some pathological processes, oxylipins also play a role as pathogenic factors in disease progression due to their strong oxidative capacity9. This variability underscores significant organizational and individual differences in oxylipin functions across different contexts, yet the mechanisms through which these functions interact remain poorly understood. Currently, oxylipins are attracting increasing attention due to their diversity and the existence of many unidentified subgroups. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop optimal detection methods and analysis strategies for oxylipins to explore their physiological and pathological regulatory roles more comprehensively.

Among oxylipins, PGE2 stands out due to its complex biological functions. However, most of time it has been known to have a "negative” image due to its strong inflammation-mediating ability and its involvement in the progression of many diseases, especially in cancers. As a key component in tumor progression, PGE2 can, on one hand, impair the body's ability to eliminate malignant cells by suppressing immune function within the tumor microenvironment10; on the other hand, it can also enhance drug resistance in tumor cells and increase the activity of cancer stem cells11. Consequently, current mainstream research often views PGE2 as a pathogenic factor, with efforts aimed at potentially therapeutic removal of these “harmful” oxylipins. The discovery in this study revealing the role of PGE2 in promoting tissue repair challenges this negative perception, highlighting its potential positive effects and underscoring the diverse biological functions of oxylipins. Coincidentally, in previous studies of liver and intestinal models, PGE2 has been found to enhance tissue regeneration by binding to E prostanoid 4 (EP4) receptor-positive macrophages, which can release a series of repairing cytokines12. Unfortunately, this study did not further explore the specific targets of PGE2's effects. In future mechanistic investigations, it may be possible to integrate previous findings to deduce the tissue specificity and universality of PGE2's role in promoting repair. Interestingly, despite sharing structural similarities, oxylipins exhibit wide-ranging functions, emphasizing the need for future research to elucidate their specific mechanisms in different tissues to fully understand their functional diversity. If we can continue to explore the source and metabolic pattern of PGE2 during pyroptosis based on the findings of the present study, we will have a more comprehensive understanding of the metabolic profile of PGE2 and even oxylipins.

This pivotal discovery marks a significant milestone in the field of metabolism, offering a more comprehensive understanding of the nexus between physiology and pathology. Moving forward, continued exploration of PGE2 and other oxylipins using advanced technologies promises to unveil new therapeutic targets for diseases associated with tissue repair and provide more precise guidance for clinical strategies in the future.
