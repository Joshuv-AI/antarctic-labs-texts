Influence of age, cognitive function, attention, and mental state on the effectiveness of EEG-based brain-computer interface device use: a systematic review
Authors: Xinyue Niu, Min Yuan, Dong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBrain-computer interfaces (BCIs) are increasingly used to support rehabilitation and assistive communication. Individual traits such as age, cognitive function, attention, and mental state have been linked to variability in BCI performance. However, these factors have not been comprehensively evaluated across paradigms and populations.MethodsA systematic review was conducted following PRISMA 2020 guidelines and registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD42024600285). PubMed and Web of Science databases were searched through June 2025 for studies reporting electroencephalography (EEG)-based BCI performance metrics stratified by age, cognition, attention, or psychological state. Twenty-five human studies were included after screening. Risk of bias was assessed using validated appraisal tools.ResultsAcross the 25 included studies, visual paradigms such as P300 event-related potential and steady-state visual evoked potential (SSVEP) showed stable performance across age groups. Motor imagery (MI)-based systems demonstrated higher sensitivity to cognitive and developmental differences. Attention scores and mental rotation were positively associated with EEG signal clarity and classification accuracy. Fatigue, motivation, and training duration influenced user responsiveness.ConclusionAge and cognitive traits impact BCI performance and system adaptability. To optimize usability in diverse populations, future BCI applications should integrate individualized training strategies, real-time feedback mechanisms, and standardized evaluation metrics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12984-025-01813-7.Keywords: Brain-computer interface, Age, Cognitive function, Mental state, Attention

Introduction

Brain-computer interface (BCI) technology, an innovative blend of neuroscience and engineering, emerged in the late twentieth century and has rapidly developed from proof of concept to practical application. It establishes a direct communication pathway [1], allowing the brain to interact directly with external devices without relying on conventional muscle and sensory pathways, thereby replacing or restoring lost or impaired functions.

At present, BCI technology has been preliminarily applied in the rehabilitation treatment of patients with neuromuscular or cerebrovascular diseases [2], including amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [3, 4] and stroke [5, 6]. In studies on the effectiveness of BCI-based interventions, individual differences have emerged as a significant non-technical factor [7]. Considering these differences, personalized assessments and customized BCI training programs are essential to optimizing the efficacy of BCI-based rehabilitation devices and represent an important direction for future development.

Existing research indicates that age is an important factor influencing BCI operational performance [8], Moreover, some studies highlight that individual differences in cognitive function, attention, and other factors may directly impact BCI performance [9–11]. Research investigating the impact of individual differences on BCI outcomes is still in its infancy, and the available findings are somewhat limited. Nonetheless, these studies have highlighted the importance of these factors in the use of BCIs to some degree.

Given the practical applicability and feasibility of BCI systems in rehabilitation settings, this review focuses exclusively on non-invasive EEG-based BCIs. This review concentrates on how participants' age, cognitive function, attention, and mental state influence the training outcomes of BCI rehabilitation devices. The overarching goal is to provide an evidence base for developing precision rehabilitation programs.

Methods

Study design and registration

This systematic review was conducted to explore how participant-level characteristics, specifically age, cognitive function, attention, and mental state, affect the effectiveness of BCI device use. The protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO under registration number CRD42024600285, and reporting follows the PRISMA 2020 guidelines.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed and Web of Science to identify articles published up to June 25, 2025. Search terms included combinations of “brain-computer interface”, “age”, “cognitive function”, “attention”, “mental state”, and “rehabilitation”, adapted for each database using Boolean operators. PubMed searches were limited to title and abstract fields, with reviews excluded using publication type filters. Web of Science searches utilized topic fields and excluded review articles via document type restrictions. Reference lists of relevant studies were manually screened to supplement database results. Full search expressions are provided in Supplementary material 1.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they investigated associations between any of the four specified individual characteristics and BCI performance outcomes, and involved human participants engaged in clinical, training, or rehabilitation contexts. Studies were excluded if they were review articles, conference abstracts, lacked full-text access, or focused purely on algorithmic validation without reporting participant-based performance data.

Study selection

All identified records were imported into a reference management program, and duplicates were removed. Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers (XYN and MY) based on predefined eligibility criteria. Full texts of potentially eligible articles were subsequently reviewed for inclusion. Any disagreements during screening were resolved by discussion. Study selection followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines and is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flow diagram illustrating the study selection process

Data extraction

Data were extracted using a standardized template, capturing key study characteristics including author, publication year, participant demographics, BCI type and duration, outcome metrics and summarized conclusions related to age, cognitive function, attention, or mental state. Each included study was classified based on its primary focus among these four individual attributes.

Quality appraisal and risk of bias assessment

Methodological quality was evaluated using tools appropriate to study design. Observational studies were assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist, examining domains such as sample selection, measurement validity, and confounder control. Non-randomized intervention studies were evaluated using the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool, assessing bias across seven domains including confounding, participant selection, and outcome measurement. Randomized controlled trials, where present, were assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2.0) tool. Complete assessment results are presented in Appendix Tables A1–A3 and summarized in the Supplementary material 2.

Results

Study selection

A total of 1417 records were retrieved from database searches, with 380 from PubMed and 1037 from Web of Science. After removing 337 duplicates, 1080 records remained for screening. Title and abstract screening excluded 883 records. Of the 197 full-text articles assessed for eligibility, 172 were excluded for reasons including lack of relevance to BCI applications, inadequate focus on participant-level characteristics, non-original study type, or use of non-human subjects. Ultimately, 25 studies were included in the final review. The study selection process is depicted in Fig. 1.

Study characteristics

The 25 studies included in this review utilized a range of research designs, including observational studies, randomized controlled trials, and non-randomized interventional studies. Sample sizes varied widely, from fewer than 15 to up to 80 participants, with most being small samples, including children, healthy adults, elderly individuals, and patients with stroke or neurodevelopmental disorders.

BCI paradigms applied across studies included MI, P300, SSVEP, and hybrid systems. Study quality was assessed using design-specific tools (JBI, ROBINS-I, RoB 2.0), with most studies rated moderate and some high.

The impact of age on the effectiveness of BCI use

Age-related effects on BCI performance appear to be paradigm-dependent. Studies suggest that visual-based BCIs such as those relying on SSVEP or P300 responses, are relatively robust across age groups [12–15], while MI-BCI demonstrate greater age sensitivity due to their reliance on internal strategy formation, sustained attention, and cognitive control [8, 16–18]. However, recent data reveal mixed trends, suggesting that age effects may vary depending on specific task designs and cognitive demands (see Table 1 for study comparisons across age groups).

Children and adults

Age-related differences in BCI performance vary by paradigm. For visual-based BCIs such as SSVEP and P300, children generally achieve accuracy and information transfer rate (ITR) comparable to adults, even among those with perinatal stroke [12, 13, 19].

However, in MI-based paradigms, children tend to exhibit lower classification accuracy and less consistent EEG features [17]. This may reflect developmental differences in executive function and prefrontal maturation [20], as well as limited capacity for motor imagery internalization. Neurophysiological studies have identified altered EEG microstate dynamics in children with cerebral palsy during MI tasks, further underscoring the need for pediatric-specific adaptations [21–23].

Although MI and P300 performance varies across individuals, pediatric data show similar fatigue levels and EEG changes between paradigms [24], suggesting comparable short-term tolerability. P300 is thus considered more robust across age, while MI remains more sensitive to cognitive maturity. Gamified training has helped children with ADHD and ASD improve attention and self-regulation via BCI neurofeedback [25–28]. By monitoring brain activity and delivering real-time feedback, these systems effectively enhance attention and diminish impulsive behaviors [29].

Given the variability in performance across BCI types, recent pediatric BCI reviews emphasize the need for age-appropriate interface design. SSVEP and P300 paradigms are considered more suitable for younger children due to their low training demands and visual grounding. In contrast, MI-BCI require simplification of signal processing pipelines, gamified feedback, and minimized cognitive load to account for children's evolving cognitive capacity and attention span [30, 31].

Overall, P300 and SSVEP paradigms exhibit greater usability and age tolerance, whereas MI paradigms demand higher cognitive capacity and are more affected by age-related differences.

Younger versus older adults

Compared to younger adults, older individuals exhibit paradigm-specific differences in BCI performance. Visual-based systems such as SSVEP and P300 demonstrate modest declines with age, typically reflected in reduced signal amplitude and slower response rates [14, 32]. While MI-BCI are more affected by aging, given their dependence on cognitive flexibility and internalized motor strategies [8, 18].

Across studies, older participants exhibit reduced classification accuracy and lower ITR, particularly in MI paradigms. Li et al. [18] showed comparable MI accuracy between younger and older groups, suggesting that age may not uniformly impair MI-based control. These findings point to task-dependent effects rather than universal age-related decline. Neurophysiological findings indicate declines in EEG signal quality and cortical responsiveness during MI tasks among older users [33, 34].

Despite these limitations, older adults may retain task-specific strengths. Although SSVEP amplitudes are generally reduced [32], stronger activation in the ipsilateral motor cortex [35], and significant performance improvements with motion-onset Visual Evoked Potential (mVEP)-based BCI indicate the potential for adaptability [15]. These patterns reinforce the importance of age-sensitive training protocols and paradigm selection to ensure long-term BCI usability.

Although direct age comparisons within individual studies are uncommon, current evidence enables a cross-paradigm synthesis of BCI performance across pediatric, adult, and aging cohorts.

The impact of cognitive function on the effectiveness of BCI use

Cognitive function encompasses the brain's ability to process information, think, learn, and retain knowledge. It is reflected in neural activity and mental imagery, influencing the interpretation and response speed of neural signals, and is positively correlated with BCI usage efficiency [36].

The impact of neural activity on BCI effectiveness

Neural oscillations and cortical excitability serve as key indicators of how groups of neurons synchronize, ultimately affecting cognitive functions. The effectiveness of BCI systems relies on the adaptability of the user's central nervous system and cortical network [37].

Vidaurre [38] reported that enhanced mu band (8–13 Hz) connectivity was significantly associated with improved MI-BCI performance, yielding correlation coefficients of 0.363 for post-stimulation accuracy and 0.367 for feedback responsiveness. Stronger intra-hemispheric coupling, particularly between sensorimotor regions, supported more stable motor imagery signals.

In experimental settings, neural activity is commonly measured via EEG, which records electrical signals from the scalp. Researchers typically analyze oscillatory patterns in the mu bands (8–13 Hz) and beta bands (13–30 Hz), assess cortical excitability using event-related desynchronization/synchronization (ERD/ERS), and evaluate functional connectivity using coherence and phase-locking metrics. These neurophysiological indicators provide a foundational understanding of individual differences in BCI control and cognitive adaptability.

Furthermore, studies demonstrate that the cortex in the sensorimotor area shows increased excitability during high cognitive tasks, contributing to improvements in upper-limb function during rehabilitation [39]. This evidence underscores the role of cortical engagement in optimizing BCI-driven rehabilitation outcomes.

Beyond physiological dynamics, internal cognitive imagery also plays a critical role in shaping BCI control quality.

The impact of mental imagery on BCI effectiveness

Mental imagery refers to the cognitive ability to simulate perceptual or motor experiences internally, without direct external input, such as visualizing an object or imagining a movement. One common way to assess this ability is through mental rotation tasks, where participants judge whether rotated images depict the same object or its mirror version, measured by accuracy and reaction time [9]. Camille Jeunet et al. [40] employed psychological questionnaires, MI-BCI tasks, and EEG signal recordings. Their findings revealed a strong correlation between participants' performance, peak performance [41], and mental rotation scores. A stronger alignment, where the user's imagined movements closely match the movements detected or required by the BCI system, enhances neuroplasticity and improves rehabilitation outcomes [42]. The impact of attention on BCI effectiveness

Attention is a key modulator of BCI performance. Focused attention improves the signal-to-noise ratio of EEG signals, thereby enhancing classification accuracy and control responsiveness [43]. High-feedback BCI training can enhance a patient's focus on specific stimuli, improving cognitive performance [11, 44]. A growing body of research highlights both behavioral and neurophysiological mechanisms by which attention modulates BCI outcomes.

ITR and classification accuracy

Sustained attention plays a direct role in improving BCI performance metrics such as ITR and classification accuracy. By guiding users' attention, BCI more effectively mobilizes brain motor signals and improves motor function [45]. Mind–Body Awareness Training (MBAT), a structured protocol that integrates mindfulness practices with movement-based attention tasks to improve internal focus and cognitive regulation, has been shown to enhance sustained attention significantly [46]. Kaitlin Cassady et al. [47] found that participants with MBAT experience achieved an average ITR of 8.0 bits/min. With the involvement of visual attention [48], BCI performance metrics improved significantly.

Attention-related metrics

The Attention Index is a normalized EEG-based metric ranging from 0 to 100, where higher values indicate stronger attentional engagement. It is calculated using the ratio of beta band power (14–30 Hz) to alpha band power (7–13 Hz), reflecting shifts toward active cognitive processing. Yuan et al. [11] compared BCI-pedal training systems with traditional pedal training among early subacute ischemic stroke patients. The experimental group showed higher attention index, positively impacting lower-limb motor function improvement.

Neurophysiological research has also linked attention-related brain network properties to BCI effectiveness. Attention-SSVEP-based hybrid BCI systems demonstrated superior performance, with attention-related metrics indicating positive effects on BCI efficiency [45]. Zhang et al. [49] found that functional centrality within the Fronto-Parietal Attention Network (FPAN) correlates with MI-BCI performance: higher centrality in the right ventral intraparietal sulcus (VIPS) was positively associated, while the left inferior parietal lobule (IPL) showed a negative link. EEG-based network analysis suggests that these FPAN regions differentially shape attentional control, where higher centrality may reflect more effective integration of task-relevant signals.

Moreover, ERP components such as N2 Posterior Contralateral (N2pc) amplitude have proven predictive of spatial attention strength and subsequent decoding success [50]. Chen et al. [48] and Jeong et al. [51] further demonstrated that visual or synchronized attentional engagement can enhance classification accuracy and motor evoked potential amplitude, with sustained effects in rehabilitation. The impact of mental state on BCI effectiveness

Mental states, such as fatigue, motivation, and emotional pressure can significantly affect BCI performance. Fatigue has demonstrable effects on EEG dynamics and task responsiveness. Studies show that overall EEG activity increases under fatigue, with elevated theta band (4–7.5 Hz) in frontal and central regions and enhanced alpha band (7.5–13 Hz) activity in posterior areas [52]. Moreover, a significant positive correlation exists between the relative β power of the frontal lobe and central brain regions and the performance of BCI, suggesting that increased mental fatigue may be linked to reduced BCI control accuracy [53].

Under low mental energy, both classification accuracy and ITR decline markedly, accompanied by reduced EEG amplitude and signal-to-noise ratio [54].Task load and fatigue indicators increase, especially during prolonged MI sessions [54, 55]. However, brief rest intervals can offset fatigue, and continuous BCI usage within one hour may remain effective without additional recovery [56].

Beyond fatigue, psychological engagement also modulates EEG responses. For instance, Kumar et al. [57] found that error-related negativity (ERN) amplitudes correlated positively with user motivation (r = 0.68), effort (r = 0.70), and awareness, while stress led to signal attenuation. These patterns suggest that emotional and motivational factors influence BCI neurofeedback and adaptability.

To address fatigue-induced performance decline, adaptive algorithms, such as general spatial pattern optimization, have been used to increase feature separability under mental exhaustion [58]. Additionally, increasing visual noise [59] and diversifying stimulus designs [60], can alleviate mental fatigue. EEG markers like alpha band (8–13 Hz), theta band (4–8 Hz), and the (α + θ)/β (β:13–30 Hz) ratio are effective fatigue indicators, while entropy has been proposed as a stimulus-independent indicator for fatigue monitoring [61].

Discussion

Despite significant technological progress in BCI systems, their clinical deployment continues to be hindered by limitations in population compatibility and realworld applicability. Stroke rehabilitation is among the most prominent clinical use cases for BCI technologies, yet the typical stroke patient, often elderly and cognitively impaired, differs markedly from the young, healthy individuals who dominate current research cohorts. Stroke incidence peaks among Chinese residents aged 75–79 [62], with 78.7% of stroke patients experiencing cognitive impairments and attention deficits due to their condition [63]. Widespread deficits in attention, executive function, and neuroplasticity complicate user adaptation, and without accounting for these neurophysiological realities, BCI systems may fall short in clinical settings.

Age-related cognitive decline presents a fundamental constraint on the effectiveness of BCI usage among older adults. Starting in early adulthood, core cognitive functions such as processing speed, reasoning, memory, and executive control progressively deteriorate [64]. As age progresses, brain structures such as the medial temporal lobe and tertiary association cortices exhibit significant atrophy [65], leading to impaired advanced cognitive functions and reduced neuroplasticity [66, 67]. Beyond structural decline, Attention also declines with age [68], and is modulated by mental state factors such as fatigue and sleep deprivation [69, 70], which further impact signal reliability and engagement. These intertwined relationships among age, cognitive function, attention, and mental state collectively influence the effectiveness of BCI usage. Such cognitive declines may affect BCI paradigms differently, with MI-based systems more susceptible to aging than attention-driven ones like P300 or SSVEP.

While age-related impairments can diminish initial BCI performance, training offers a key compensatory mechanism. Longitudinal studies consistently show that BCI control is not a fixed trait, but a trainable skill acquired through sustained user-system interaction. For instance, Robinson et al. [71] and Korik et al. [72] demonstrated that closed-loop feedback and realistic task design significantly improved cortical engagement and performance over time. A broader body of evidence from Cybathlon case studies further confirms that repeated training enables cortical reorganization, modulates sensorimotor rhythms, and enhances user engagement [73–77]. These findings highlight the need for training-sensitive system design, including adaptive interfaces, calibrated feedback, and robust cross-session evaluation [78–80].

Pediatric users present a distinct challenge for BCI system design, primarily due to developmental-stage constraints that differ markedly from adult users. Children exhibit ongoing cortical maturation, limited executive control, and reduced attention regulation, factors that significantly affect EEG signal consistency and feedback responsiveness [31, 76, 81, 82]. Children with conditions like cerebral palsy or autism require age-appropriate interface design and tailored signal processing. Moreover, hardware selection must prioritize safety and comfort, especially in pediatric contexts. Although microneedle electrodes offer enhanced signal fidelity for adults [83], their use in children remains limited due to pain sensitivity, emotional discomfort, and compliance challenges. Invasive electrodes are categorically inappropriate, given the risks to skull development and stricter ethical constraints.

Advances in hardware design are beginning to address long-standing barriers related to comfort, fidelity, and anatomical variation. Recent innovations such as e-tattoo printing technologies, and multi-axis scanning have enabled high-resolution, skin-conformal EEG acquisition even on curved and hairy scalps [84]. These personalized, ultrathin interfaces offer improved wearability and signal stability over extended periods, crucial for both pediatric and elderly users.

Software-level progress similarly contributes to enhanced usability and user-specific performance. Emerging decoding strategies that leverage neural trajectory geometry have shown superior generalization and accuracy across diverse taskss [85], while adaptive frameworks, like cross-day stability models and transfer learning, allow systems to compensate for signal drift and individual variability [86–89]. Together, these technologies form the basis for building robust, personalized BCI controllers suitable for long-term deployment.

Despite promising training outcomes, evaluating BCI performance remains challenging. Common metrics such as accuracy and ITR vary not only in definition and calculation across studies, but also in their relevance to clinical populations. Stroke patients and children, for example, face cognitive and behavioral constraints that make traditional metrics insufficient. Moreover, substantial variability in study designs, including differences in participant demographics, task paradigms, and evaluation criteria, limits the comparability of findings. Crucially, user-centered indicators, such as training time, fatigue, and engagement, are frequently omitted [90], further complicating cross-study synthesis. To enable reliable benchmarking and support clinical translation, future research must establish a standardized, population-sensitive metric framework.

Overall, BCI control should be conceptualized not merely as a technical achievement, but as a dynamic skill co-developed by the user and system [80]. Future development demands convergence across neuroscience insights, engineering innovations, and population-specific design strategies, ensuring that BCI systems are adaptable, scalable, and meaningfully aligned with clinical needs.

Conclusion

This systematic review examines how age, cognitive function, attention, and mental state collectively influence BCI performance across paradigms and populations. Age-related differences are evident: children encounter difficulties in motor imagery due to developmental immaturity, limited attention span, and reduced task engagement, while older adults experience declines in cognitive flexibility and motivation. Visual-based paradigms such as P300 and SSVEP demonstrate relatively stable performance across age groups, whereas MI-based systems are more sensitive to cognitive maturity, executive function, and internal strategy formation. Attention capacity and mental rotation ability play essential roles in signal clarity and system adaptability, with fatigue and motivational states further modulating classification accuracy and user responsiveness.

Despite growing interest in BCI applications, current studies face limitations such as small sample sizes, lack of population diversity, and inconsistent evaluation criteria, especially among clinical groups like stroke patients. These issues, along with considerable heterogeneity in study designs and outcome metrics, hinder the feasibility of meta-analysis and reduce the generalizability of findings. The heterogeneity in study designs and outcome metrics precluded meta-analysis, highlighting the importance of standardized protocols and harmonized reporting. Moving forward, prioritizing adaptable system design, refined training strategies, and unified metrics for key populations will be crucial to advancing BCI technologies in precision rehabilitation.

Abbreviations

Action Observation—A cognitive process involving the visual perception and mental processing of others actions, which activates the brains mirror neuron system and is widely utilized in neurorehabilitation and brain-computer interface paradigms to enhance motor learning and neural plasticity

Brain-Computer Interface—System that enables direct communication between the brain and an external device, bypassing traditional neuromuscular pathways.

Electroencephalography—A non-invasive method to record electrical activity of the brain via scalp electrodes.

Event-Related Desynchronization/Synchronization—Brain signal patterns observed in EEG; ERD refers to a decrease (desynchronization) and ERS to an increase (synchronization) in rhythmic brain activity, often linked to motor or cognitive tasks.

Error-Related Negativity—A neural response associated with performance monitoring and cognitive feedback.

Fugl-Meyer Assessment—A standardized clinical scale used to evaluate motor functioning, sensation, and joint function in patients with post-stroke hemiplegia. The FMA-LE (Fugl-Meyer Assessment for the Lower Extremity) is the subscale specific to the lower limb.

Fronto-Parietal Attention Network—A brain network involving the frontal and parietal lobes, crucial for attention, cognitive control, and working memory processes.

Inferior Parietal Lobule—A brain region located at the parietal lobe's inferior part, associated with diverse functions including sensory integration, language processing, and attention.

Information Transfer Rate—A metric (often measured in bits per minute) indicating how efficiently information is communicated from the user to the system in a BCI context.

Mind–Body Awareness Training—A training approach that integrates mindfulness, meditation, or other practices to enhance awareness and control over mind and body processes.

Motor Imagery—A cognitive process of mentally simulating a motor action without any physical movement, which activates similar neural networks as actual motor execution and is widely used in motor rehabilitation and brain-computer interfaces for generating control commands.

Motion-onset Visual Evoked Potential—An EEG response triggered by visual motion cues.

N2 Posterior Contralateral—An event-related potential component, typically occurring around 200–300 ms post-stimulus, that reflects the allocation of visual attention to a specific location in space.

P300 event-related potential—A positive deflection in EEG occurring approximately 300ms after stimulus, commonly used in attention-based BCIs.

Signal-to-Noise Ratio—A measure that compares the level of a desired neural signal to the level of background noise, crucial for assessing the quality and detectability of brain signals in BCI applications.

Steady-State Visual Evoked Potential—A stable and periodic neural oscillation elicited in the visual cortex when a person gazes at a visual stimulus flickering at a constant frequency, typically used as a robust signal source in brain-computer interfaces for command control.

Ventral Intraparietal Sulcus—A region within the intraparietal sulcus of the brain that is involved in multisensory integration, particularly for processing visual motion and eye movement control.
