Renewable estimation in linear expectile regression models with streaming data sets
Wei Cao, Shanshan Wang, Xiaoxue Hu

Abstract
Streaming data often exhibit heterogeneity due to heteroscedastic variances or inhomogeneous covariate effects. Online renewable quantile and expectile regression methods provide valuable tools for detecting such heteroscedasticity by combining current data with summary statistics from historical data. However, quantile regression can be computationally demanding because of the non-smooth check function. To address this issue, we propose a novel online renewable method based on expectile regression that efficiently updates estimates using both current observations and historical summaries, thereby reducing storage requirements. Leveraging the smoothness of the expectile loss function, our approach attains superior computational efficiency compared with existing online renewable methods for streaming data exhibiting heteroscedastic variances or inhomogeneous covariate effects. We establish the consistency and asymptotic normality of the proposed estimator under mild regularity conditions, demonstrating that it achieves the same statistical efficiency as oracle estimators based on full individual-level data. Numerical experiments and real-data applications demonstrate that our method performs comparably to the oracle estimator while maintaining high computational efficiency and minimal storage costs. The proposed algorithm has been implemented in the R function ReER .

Introduction

With rapid technological advances, we have entered the era of large-scale data, characterized by the exponential growth of datasets across diverse fields. Streaming datasets, which arrive continuously over time, have become a key representation of large-scale data
(
8
)
. Streaming data analysis is increasingly essential, with applications in areas such as high-frequency trading, sensor networks, and aerospace. Its dynamic nature imposes higher demands on storage and computation while creating new opportunities and challenges for modeling and analysis
(
6
)
. First, storage capacity is limited. As new data continuously arrive, historical raw data are often discarded, making it impractical to store all information. Second, the high velocity of data generation requires real-time processing and rapid analytical updates. Traditional statistical methods, which assume access to complete datasets, are generally unsuitable for this setting. A promising solution is online updating, or renewable estimation, which avoids storing all raw data by updating models using only the current data and summary statistics from the past. Various online updating techniques have been developed, including aggregated estimating equations
(
18
)
, cumulatively updating estimating equations
(
28
)
, stochastic gradient descent and its variants
(
3
;
38
)
, and renewable estimators
(
19
)
.

All of the aforementioned methods and algorithms have demonstrated practical effectiveness. However, they primarily focus on the conditional mean of the response given the predictors or covariates. In many applications, more than just the mean of the conditional distribution is of interest, particularly when covariate effects are inhomogeneous and/or the noise exhibits asymmetric tails. For instance, in the study of the effect of vehicle characteristics on price
(
7
)
, one may be more interested in the lower and upper tails rather than the mean of the conditional price distribution given the predictors. To capture heterogeneity in the covariates across different locations of the response distribution, methods such as quantile regression (QR)
(
14
)
and asymmetric least squares regression (expectile regression, ER)
(
22
)
have been widely used.

There has been substantial progress in developing QR methodologies tailored for streaming data settings. For example,
2
proposed an online updating method based on a one-shot algorithm that views streaming data as data blocks partitioned over the time domain.
32
developed a renewable QR estimator using a normal approximation of the QR estimator for each batch combined with a maximum likelihood approach.
7
further proposed a sequential Bayesian QR algorithm for streaming data. Recent studies have enhanced online QR methods through smoothing and debiasing techniques in conventional and high-dimensional streaming settings
(
12
;
35
;
30
;
26
)
. We refer the reader to
33
for a comprehensive overview of recent advances. Despite these advancements, it is computationally challenging due to the non-smooth nature of the check loss.

Fortunately, ER avoids non-smooth optimization and allows direct estimation of the asymptotic covariance matrix without requiring nonparametric density estimation, leading to greater computational efficiency. While QR focuses on tail probabilities, ER captures both the likelihood and magnitude of extreme values, making it particularly suitable for analyzing high-impact risks. For example,
31
introduced expectile-based Value-at-Risk to better assess rare, high-loss events, and
15
proposed a conditional autoregressive expectile model. More recent studies, such as
21
and
27
, have further advanced expectile-based risk measures. Consequently, ER has been widely applied in areas such as risk measurement
(
13
;
5
;
37
)
, exchange rate volatility
(
36
)
, stock index modeling
(
11
)
, and high-frequency financial data analysis
(
9
)
.

Thanks to its advantages and broad applicability, expectile regression (ER) for large-scale data has recently become an active research area in modern statistics. To address computational challenges, researchers have developed a variety of distributed and subsampling-based methods for ER models. We refer readers to
29
;
10
;
25
;
24
for distributed ER, and to
1
;
16
;
17
for subsampling-based ER. However, ER for streaming data remains largely unexplored, and the existing above methods cannot be directly applied to streaming settings.

Inspired by the one-shot approach,
23
proposed an online updating strategy for ER that constructs a joint likelihood function for each data batch and optimizes it using the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM). To the best of our knowledge, it is the latest literature working on this topic. However, its renewable estimator is based on a one-shot strategy, enabling ER estimation for streaming data but fails to leverage cross-batch information, resulting in inefficient use of available data. Moreover, their asymptotic theory requires that each block size be larger than
\sqrt{N_{b}}
and diverge to infinity, where
N_{b}
denotes the total sample size. This assumption may not hold in real-world scenarios where data arrive in small or unevenly sized batches. We also demonstrate this phenomenon in our simulation studies.

In this study, we propose a
Re
newable
E
xpectile
R
egression estimation strategy for the streaming datasets, hereafter referred to as
ReER
. Unlike the method of
23
, our approach builds on the online updating framework introduced by
19
, reconstructing the renewable ER loss function via Taylor expansion and deriving an approximate iterative solution. The main contributions and advantages of the proposed method can be summarized as follows. First, from an estimation perspective,
ReER
employs Taylor expansion to refine historical parameter estimates using newly arrived data, enabling efficient information integration across batches and overcoming the limitations of one-shot estimation.
Second, from a computational perspective,
ReER
requires only the current data batch and a set of sufficient statistics from previous batches, substantially reducing memory and computational costs.
Finally, from a theoretical perspective, the proposed estimator achieves asymptotic efficiency comparable to that of the full-sample estimator under mild conditions. Specifically, it only requires a consistent initial ER estimator and a sufficiently large first-batch sample size, without imposing additional distributional or structural assumptions.
Overall, the
ReER
method is computationally efficient in handling the challenges of data storage and processing while maintaining asymptotic equivalence to the oracle ER estimator.

\tau\in(0,1)

\tau

(1)

where

, where

. The data follows the linear expectile regression model:

\displaystyle e_{\tau}(y_{ti}|\bm{x}_{ti})=\bm{x}_{ti}^{\top}\bm{\beta}(\tau),\quad i=1,\cdots,t.

(2)

where
\bm{\beta}(\tau)
is the unknown expectile regression parameter. To simplify notation, we omit
\tau
where there is no ambiguity. Additionally, we use: “
\tilde{\ \ }
” to indicate online estimators, e.g.,
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}
represents the renewable estimator based on data up to batch

. “
\hat{\ \ }

\mathcal{D}_{t}
.

2.2
ReER: Renewable estimation for Expectile Regression

First, the conventional oracle expectile regression estimator of
\bm{\beta}
, i.e.,
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}_{b}
, can be obtained by minimizing the following global loss function:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}_{b}=\mathop{\arg\min}\limits_{\bm{\beta}}\ \mathcal{L}_{N_{b}}(\bm{\beta}),\ \ &\mathcal{L}_{N_{b}}(\bm{\beta})=\frac{1}{N_{b}}\sum_{t=1}^{b}n_{t}\cdot\mathcal{L}_{n_{t}}(\bm{\beta}),\end{aligned}

(3)

where
\mathcal{L}_{n_{t}}(\bm{\beta})
is the loss function only obtained based on samples from batch
\mathcal{D}_{t}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{n_{t}}(\bm{\beta})&=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}\rho_{\tau}(y_{ti}-\bm{x}_{ti}^{\top}\bm{\beta}).\end{aligned}

(4)

The Eq. (
3
) can be solved using an iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS) method, and the solution of the oracle ER estimator
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}_{b}
is given by:

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}_{b}=\big[W_{N_{b}}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}_{b})\big]^{-1}U_{N_{b}}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}_{b}),

(5)

where
W_{N_{b}}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}_{b})
and
U_{N_{b}}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}_{b})
are:

\displaystyle W_{N_{b}}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}_{b})=\sum_{t=1}^{b}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}|\tau-I\big(y_{ti}<\bm{x}_{ti}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}_{b}\big)|\bm{x}_{ti}\bm{x}_{ti}^{\top},\ \ U_{N_{b}}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}_{b})=\sum_{t=1}^{b}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}|\tau-I\big(y_{ti}<\bm{x}_{ti}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}_{b}\big)|\bm{x}_{ti}y_{ti}.

(6)

If we could aggregate all information from the all
b
data batches at once,
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}_{b}
could be obtained by solving Eqs. (
5
) and (
6
). However, due to the large cumulative sample size and memory constraints, this is often infeasible in practice. To address this, next, we propose a renewable ER estimator that relies only on the current data batch and a few summary statistics.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{N_{b}}(\bm{\beta})
and
\mathcal{L}_{n_{b}}(\bm{\beta})
represent the overall loss functions based on the data up to the
b
-th batch (i.e.,
\mathcal{D}_{(b)}
) and the
b
-th batch of data (i.e.,
\mathcal{D}_{b}
), respectively. We begin with a simple scenario of two batches of data
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
, where
\mathcal{D}_{2}
arrives after
\mathcal{D}_{1}
. First we estimate the initial ER estimate
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
(or
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}^{*}
,
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
) with
\mathcal{D}_{1}
. Next we update the initial ER estimate to a renewed ER estimate
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{2}
without using any subject level data but only some summary statistics from
\mathcal{D}_{1}
. Following
19
, the oracle ER estimator
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2}^{*}
is obtained by minimizing the following aggregated loss function:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{N_{2}}(\bm{\beta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{2}}\Big(n_{1}\mathcal{L}_{n_{1}}(\bm{\beta})+n_{2}\mathcal{L}_{n_{2}}(\bm{\beta})\Big).

(7)

The minimizer of (
7
) is equivalent to the minimizer of the following loss function:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{N_{2}}(\bm{\beta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{2}}\left[n_{1}\left(\mathcal{L}_{n_{1}}(\bm{\beta})-\mathcal{L}_{n_{1}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{1})\right)+n_{2}\mathcal{L}_{n_{2}}(\bm{\beta})\right].

(8)

Assume that conditions (C1)-(C4) hold (see details in Section
2.3
), the loss function (
8
) is asymptotically equivalent to:

\displaystyle\frac{n_{1}}{2N_{2}}(\bm{\beta}-\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{1})^{\top}\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{n_{1}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{1})(\bm{\beta}-\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{1})+\frac{n_{2}}{N_{2}}\mathcal{L}_{n_{2}}(\bm{\beta}),

(9)

where
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{n_{1}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{1})
is the Hessian matrix of the loss function of data batch
\mathcal{D}_{1}
. Thus, when data batch
\mathcal{D}_{2}
arrives, we can obtain the renewable estimator
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{2}
by minimizing (
9
), which only depends only on current data batch
\mathcal{D}_{2}
, summary statistics
n_{1},\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
and
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{n_{1}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{1})
of batch data
\mathcal{D}_{1}
.

Similar, when the
b
-th batch arrives, the overall loss functions
\mathcal{L}_{N_{b}}(\bm{\beta})
can be represented by an aggregated form with historical data part and the newly arrived data part:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{N_{b}}(\bm{\beta})=\frac{1}{N_{b}}\Big(N_{b-1}\mathcal{L}_{N_{b-1}}(\bm{\beta})+n_{b}\mathcal{L}_{n_{b}}(\bm{\beta})\Big).

(10)

Eq. (
10
) separates the overall loss into components attributable to historical data and the newly arriving data, enabling an efficient update mechanism for renewable estimation. Performing a Taylor expansion around the online estimator
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b-1}
on the historical term of (
10
), the loss function
\mathcal{L}_{N_{b}}(\bm{\beta})
can be approximately represented as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{N_{b}}(\bm{\beta})\approx\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{N_{b}}(\bm{\beta})=\frac{1}{N_{b}}\Bigg\{\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\beta}-\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b-1})^{\top}\Bigg[\sum_{t=1}^{b-1}n_{t}\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})\Bigg](\bm{\beta}-\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b-1})+n_{b}\mathcal{L}_{n_{b}}(\bm{\beta})\Bigg\},

(11)

where
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})
is the Hessian matrix of the loss function of data batch
t
.

As mentioned earlier, compared to QR, the ER model employs an asymmetric squared loss, which allows it to be directly solved via differentiation. Then minimizing (
11
) yields the following updated estimator:

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}=\left[\sum_{t=1}^{b-1}W_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})+W_{n_{b}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b})\right]^{-1}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{b-1}W_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b-1}+U_{n_{b}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b})\right],

(12)

where
W_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})
and
U_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})
are defined as:

W_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}|\tau-I(y_{ti}<\bm{x}_{ti}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})|\bm{x}_{ti}\bm{x}_{ti}^{\top},\ \ U_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}|\tau-I(y_{ti}<\bm{x}_{ti}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})|\bm{x}_{ti}y_{ti}.

Note that the right-hand side of Eq. (
12
) still involves the unknown parameter
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}
, and thus does not yield a closed-form solution for
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}
. To reduce computational burden, we replace
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}
with a consistent initial estimator on the right-hand side of Eq. (
12
). Specifically, we approximate
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}
using the renewable estimator from the previous step,
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b-1}
, which was obtained from the preceding data batch
\mathcal{D}_{(b-1)}
. Accordingly,
W_{n_{b}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b})
and
U_{n_{b}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b})
are approximated by
W_{n_{b}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b-1})
and
U_{n_{b}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b-1})
, respectively. Thus, in practice, the final estimator is given by:

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{t=1}^{b-1}W_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})+W_{n_{b}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b-1})\right]^{-1}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{b-1}W_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b-1}+U_{n_{b}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b-1})\right].

(13)

Thus the renewable estimator
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}
can be computed using only the aggregated summary statistics from previous data batches, specifically
\left\{\mathbb{H}_{N_{b-1}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}),\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b-1}\right\}
, along with the statistics derived from the newly arrived data batch
\left\{W_{n_{b}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b-1}),U_{n_{b}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b-1})\right\}
, where
\mathbb{H}_{N_{b-1}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}})=\sum_{t=1}^{b-1}W_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})
.

2.3
Some remarks

Here we discuss the key differences between the proposed ReER method and those of
23
and
29
, respectively.

Our proposed
ReER
method differs from the online ER method proposed by
23
in several key aspects. First, the two approaches diverge in the estimation of local static estimators. In the initialization step of
ReER
, the estimator
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
is obtained by minimizing the expectile loss over the first arriving data batch
\mathcal{D}_{1}
. This optimization problem is solved using IRLS algorithm, which is implemented in
R
via the
expectreg
package. In contrast, the method of
23
employs ADMM for optimization. To ensure comparability between the two methods, we adopt the IRLS algorithm for both approaches in the remainder of this paper. Second, the structure of the renewable estimator also differs between the two methods. The renewable estimator proposed by
23
is defined as:

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}^{(\text{pan})}=\left[\sum_{t=1}^{b}\omega_{t}\frac{\bm{X_{t}}^{\top}\bm{X}_{t}}{n_{t}}\right]^{-1}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{b}\omega_{t}\frac{\bm{X_{t}}^{\top}\bm{X}_{t}}{n_{t}}\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{t}\right],

(14)

where
\omega_{t}=n_{t}/N_{t}
. This estimator essentially performs a weighted aggregation of local estimators from each data batch, with weights determined by both covariance matrices and the sample size of each batch. As mentioned earlier, ER can be viewed as an extension of OLS regression that incorporates an asymmetric loss function depending on the expectile level
\tau
. In Eq. (
13
), the statistics
W_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})
and
U_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})
inherently reflect this asymmetry, as they are computed under the expectile loss framework. Therefore, the proposed method effectively captures cross-batch information, including the heterogeneity among batches, making it particularly well-suited for scenarios with small or limited batch sizes.

29
raised a ER algorithm for massive data under divide an conquer (DC) scenario, which the estimator can be derived by:

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t}(\tau)=\left[\sum_{t=1}^{b}\widehat{Q}_{t}^{-1}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{t})\right]^{-1}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{b}\widehat{Q}_{t}^{-1}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{t})\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{t}\right],

(15)

where
\widehat{Q}_{t}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{t})
is the plug-in estimator of the asymptotic covariance matrix (for more detail see
29
). This DC-based method can be naturally extended to streaming scenarios, as the structure of Eq. (
15
) is a sum-linear form over individual data batches. Compared to the method of
23
, the approach of
29
also incorporates expectile loss information. However, there are still important differences between their method and the proposed
ReER
. The renewable estimator proposed by
29
is essentially a variant of the DC-based estimator and it is still a weighted average of local static estimates. In contrast, our proposed renewable estimator
ReER
is based on an update-oriented framework. Specifically, it incrementally updates the parameter estimates using the newly arrived data batch along with cumulative summary statistics from historical data. This updating mechanism enables
ReER
to more effectively capture cross-batch information and ensures robustness even when the incoming data batch significantly differs from previous ones or when batch sizes are small. Consequently, the proposed estimator tends to be more robust in heterogeneous or low-sample-size scenarios.

2.4
Computation complexity

In this section, we take a preliminary analysis of the computational complexity of Oracle ER and the proposed
ReER
. For simplicity, we assume the samples size of each data batch is same, which
n_{t}=n=N_{b}/b
for
t=1,\cdots,b
.

For the Oracle and local estimations, we apply the
expectreg.ls
function from the
expectreg
package in R, which employs the IRLS method. Assuming the number of IRLS iterations is
T
, the computational complexity of computing the oracle estimator
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}_{b}
and the local (initial) estimator
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
is approximately
O\left(T(N_{b}\cdot p^{2}+p^{3})\right)
and
O\left(T(n\cdot p^{2}+p^{3})\right)
, respectively.

For the
ReER
method, computing
W_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})
and
U_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})
requires
O(np^{2})
and
O(np)
, respectively. The total computational cost for updating
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}
is
O(np^{2}+np+p^{2}+p^{3})
, where the
O(p^{2})
term arises from the computation of
\sum_{t=1}^{b-1}W_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b-1}
, and the
O(p^{3})
term comes from inverting
\left[\sum_{t=1}^{b-1}W_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})+W_{n_{b}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b-1})\right]
. Considering updates over
b
data batches, the overall computational complexity of the
ReER
method is:

O\left(T(np^{2}+p^{3})+b(np^{2}+np+p^{2}+p^{3})\right)
.

In practice, the total sample size
N_{b}
can exceed one million. Meanwhile, the IRLS algorithm typically converges within a small number of iterations, often fewer than 10, implying that
T\ll N
in most applications. Furthermore, we focus on a low-dimensional setting where the number of covariates
p
satisfies
p\ll N
. The dominant term in the complexity of Oracle estimator is
O(TN_{b}\cdot p^{2})
, while
ReER
, it is
O(Tn\cdot p^{2}+N_{b}\cdot p^{2})
. Due to the fact that
n\ll N_{b}
,
ReER
greatly saves the computational cost compared to Oracle estimator.

3
Large Sample Properties

In this section, we present the large sample properties for the
ReER
method. To establish the theoretical results, we impose some notations. Define
\lambda_{\min}(\bm{A})
and
\lambda_{\max}(\bm{A})
as the smallest and largest eigenvalues of a square matrix
\bm{A}
.
For a vector,
\|\cdot\|
denotes its
l_{2}
-norm, and for a matrix,
\|\cdot\|
denotes its spectral norm. And let
c_{l}
be positive constant, which
l=1,2,3
and
4
. The following regular conditions are required:

(C1)

The unknown parameter vector
\bm{\beta}_{0}
lies in the interior of a compact and convex parameter space
\mathcal{H}
, which is a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

(C2)

Let
\epsilon_{ti}=y_{ti}-\bm{x}_{ti}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{0}
be the error tern of ER model. Given
X=\bm{x}
,
\epsilon_{ti}
are i.i.d. distributed and satisfy
e_{\tau}(\epsilon_{ti}|X=\bm{x})=0
and
\mathbb{E}\Big[\epsilon_{ti}^{2}|X=\bm{x}\Big]\leq c_{1}<\infty
.

(C3)

Define
\bm{\Sigma}=\mathbb{E}[\bm{x}_{ti}\bm{x}_{ti}^{\top}]
, and there exists a positive constant
c_{2}
such that
0<\lambda_{\min}(\bm{\Sigma})<\lambda_{\max}(\bm{\Sigma})\leq c_{2}<\infty
.

Conditions (C1)–(C3) are standard assumptions for ER estimator, as discussed in
22
. Under these conditions the oracle estimator
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}_{b}
satisfy the following asymptotic distribution when
N_{b}\rightarrow\infty
:

\sqrt{N_{b}}\big(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}_{b}-\bm{\beta}_{0}\big)\xrightarrow{d}\big(\textbf{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{w}^{-1}\bm{\Omega}\bm{\Sigma}_{w}^{-1}\big),

where
\bm{\Omega}=\mathbb{E}\Big[|\tau-1(\epsilon_{ti}<0)|^{2}\cdot\epsilon_{ti}^{2}\cdot\bm{x}_{ti}\bm{x}_{ti}^{\top}\Big]
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{w}=\mathbb{E}\Big[|\tau-1(\epsilon_{ti}<0)|\bm{x}_{ti}\bm{x}_{ti}^{\top}\Big]
.

Since the renewable estimator
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}
is obtained by optimizing Eq. (
11
), we impose the following regularity condition to facilitate the investigation of its theoretical properties:

(C4)

Given
X=\bm{x}
, the conditional density function
f_{\epsilon_{ti}}(\cdot)
of error term
\epsilon_{ti}
is bounded, satisfy
0<f_{\epsilon_{ti}}(\cdot)\leq c_{3}<\infty
.

(C5)

The sample size of the first data batch
n_{1}\rightarrow\infty
, and for
t=1,2,\cdots
and
i=1,2,\cdots,n_{t}
, there exists a positive constant
c_{4}
such that
\|\bm{x}_{ti}\|<c_{4}

This condition ensures the consistency of the initial estimator for the first batch,
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
. When
n_{1}\rightarrow\infty
, under conditions (C1)–(C3), we have
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}=\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}\bm{\beta}_{0}
. Moreover, since
\nabla\mathcal{L}_{n_{1}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{1})=\mathbf{0}
, the approximation in Eq. (
11
) holds. The consistency and asymptotic normality of the proposed
ReER
estimator are established in Theorems
1
and
2
. In addition, the asymptotically equivalent of the proposed renewable estimator
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}
and the oracle estimator
\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}_{b}
are given in Theorem
3
. The proof details are given in
A
.

Theorem 1

(Consistency of
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}
)
. Assume that conditions (C1)-(C5) hold, the renewable estimator
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}
is consistent with the true parameter, that is:

\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}\xrightarrow{p}\bm{\beta}_{0}.

Theorem 2

(Asymptotic Normality of
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}
)
. Assume that conditions (C1)-(C5) hold,
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}
satisfies the following asymptotic normal distribution:

\sqrt{N_{b}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}-\bm{\beta}_{0})\xrightarrow{d}\big(\textbf{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{w}^{-1}\bm{\Omega}\bm{\Sigma}_{w}^{-1}\big).

Theorem 3

(Asymptotic equivalency of
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}
)
. Under conditions (C1)-(C5), the
l_{2}
-norm difference between the oracle estimator and renewable estimator vanishes at the rate of
N_{b}^{-1}
, i.e.,

\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}-\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\star}_{b}\|=O_{p}(1/N_{b}).

Remark 1

In streaming data analysis, it is standard and commonly assumed that the number of data batches satisfies
b\rightarrow\infty
in real applications
(
28
)
. Many existing studies further assume that
b=O(N_{b}^{\kappa})
for some constant
0<\kappa<1
, which implicitly requires the batch sizes
n_{t}\rightarrow\infty
for all
t=1,2,\dots

(
29
;
23
)
. However, such assumptions may not hold in many real-world scenarios where data arrive continuously over time with fixed or limited batch sizes. In contrast, our proposed method only requires that the first batch size satisfies
n_{1}\rightarrow\infty
, making it more flexible and well-suited for practical applications involving perpetual data streams. This advantage is also supported by both simulation studies and real data applications.

4
Simulation Studies

We first describe the model setup and evaluation criteria in Section
4.1
, and then examine the effects of batch size and the number of batches on finite-sample performance in Section
4.2
. Finally, Section
4.3
evaluates the computational efficiency of the proposed method.

4.1
Model setup and evaluation criteria

Assume that data are generated from the following model:

\displaystyle y_{ti}=\bm{x}_{ti}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{\star}+(\bm{x}_{ti}^{\top}\bm{\gamma})\epsilon_{ti},\quad t=1,\cdots,b,

(16)

where
\bm{x}_{ti}=(1,x_{ti,1},x_{ti,2})^{\top}
and
x_{ti,1},x_{ti,2}
follow a uniform distribution
\text{Unif}(0,1)
. According to Eq. (
16
), the true expected expectile regression coefficient is
\bm{\beta}_{0}(\tau)=\bm{\beta}^{\star}+e_{\tau}(\bm{\epsilon})\cdot\bm{\gamma}
, where
e_{\tau}(\epsilon)
denotes the
\tau
th expectile of error terms. Note that when
\bm{x}_{ti}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}
is constant, model (
16
) has homogeneous variance; otherwise, the errors are heteroscedastic. For the random error
\epsilon_{ti}
, we consider the following two cases: 1)
\epsilon_{ti}
follows the standard normal distribution, i.e.,
\epsilon_{ti}\sim N(0,1)
and 2)
\epsilon_{ti}
follows the student
t
distribution with
3
degree of freedom, i.e.,
\epsilon_{ti}\sim t(3)
. We set the true
\bm{\beta}^{\star}=(2,1,2)^{\top}
with
\bm{\gamma}=(1,0,0)^{\top}
for the homogeneous model and
\bm{\gamma}=(1,0.25,0)^{\top}
for the heterogeneous model. Thus, we consider the following four cases based on the homogeneity or heterogeneity of the model and the distribution of the error term:

Case 1
: Homogeneous model with error term
\bm{\epsilon}\sim N(0,1)
;

Case 2
: Homogeneous model with error term
\bm{\epsilon}\sim t(3)
;

Case 3
: Heterogeneous model with error term
\bm{\epsilon}\sim N(0,1)
;

Case 4
: Heterogeneous model with error term
\bm{\epsilon}\sim t(3)
.

For each case, we consider these two scenarios:

Scenario S1
: We fix
N_{b}=100,000
and vary the batch size
n_{k}
to evaluate the impact of batch size on performance.

Scenario S2
: We fix
n_{k}=300
and vary the number of batches
K
to evaluate the number of batches on performance.

For a comprehensive comparison, we evaluate our proposed method against three benchmark estimators from the existing literature:
(i)
Oracle
: estimation using the entire dataset as a gold standard; (ii)
DCER
: a divide-and-conquer-based estimator for expectile regression proposed by
29
; and (iii)
PAER
: a renewable estimation approach for expectile regression introduced by
23
. And we conduct simulation
200
times. The main performance metrics are: (1) the empirical bias (
BIAS
), defined as
\textbf{BIAS}(\beta_{j})=\frac{1}{200}\sum_{s=1}^{200}(\tilde{\beta}^{(s)}_{j}-\beta_{j})
, (2) the mean square error (
MSE
), i.e.,
\textbf{MSE}(\beta_{j})=\frac{1}{200}\sum_{s=1}^{200}\big(\tilde{\beta}^{(s)}_{j}-\beta_{j}\big)^{2}
and computational time on average (
Time
), which is measured after the final data batch is processed. Here,
\tilde{\beta}^{(s)}_{j}
denotes the renewable estimator of
\beta_{j}
(
j=0,1,2
) in the
s
th repetition, with
s=1,2,\ldots,200
.

Throughout this paper, the proposed renewable estimation method is referred to as
ReER
. All experiments are conducted in R, an R package to implement our proposed method can be obtained at the following link:
https://github.com/Weiccao/ReER
. Due to space limitations, we report results only for the case where
\tau=0.25
. Additional results corresponding to
\tau=0.5
and
\tau=0.75
expectile levels are presented in
B
.

is designed to evaluate the impact of the data batch size
n_{k}
on the statistical performance of the proposed renewable estimators. Here we fix the total sample size at
N_{k}=100,000
and vary the batch size
n_{k}
, where
n_{k}

\in\{200,1000,2000,5000,10000\}
. The simulation results are presented in Tables

-

for Cases 1-4, respectively.

Specifically, Table

presents the simulation results for Scenario

1)

First, except for the intercept term
\beta_{0}
, the proposed

2)

6
Conclusion and Discussion

In this paper, we propose a novel online renewable expectile regression method,
ReER
, for analyzing streaming datasets. The renewable estimator relies only on the current data and summary statistics from historical data, making it computationally efficient and free from storage constraints. We establish theoretical results under mild conditions, showing that the proposed estimator is asymptotically equivalent to the oracle expectile regression estimator. Both simulation studies and a real data application demonstrate that the proposed method achieves estimation performance comparable to the oracle estimator and superior to existing methods. In particular, unlike previous approaches,
ReER
does not update estimates simply by reweighting information from independent data streams; instead, it refines historical parameter estimates using newly arriving data, thereby more fully utilizing information across batches. As a result, even with small-sample data streams,
ReER
can better approximate oracle results and is less sensitive to batch size, effectively mitigating large estimation errors that may occur with unstable estimates from small batches. The proposed algorithm has been implemented into an R function
ReER
.

To conclude this paper, we discuss some limitations of this work and suggest potential directions for future research. First, the proposed
ReER
method primarily addresses online updating in low-dimensional settings. However, in the era of big data, extremely high-dimensional datasets have become increasingly common. To date, only a few studies have tackled the challenges of modeling high-dimensional streaming data. Some penalty-based or subsampling approaches have been developed to construct expectile regression models for large-scale data
(
25
;
16
;
1
)
, but they do not address online updating of high-dimensional expectile regression parameters in streaming scenarios. Therefore, developing efficient and accurate online updating methods for high-dimensional expectile regression, along with corresponding statistical inference, represents a valuable area for future research.

Second, although expectile regression is an effective tool for capturing heterogeneity within data structures, challenges persist when dealing with model heterogeneity, especially in streaming data contexts. Some recent studies have explored approaches for handling heterogeneity or dynamic model shifts in streaming datasets
(
34
;
20
)
, but these primarily focus on mean regression, which overlooks the heterogeneity captured by expectiles.
4
proposed a homogeneity testing method for renewable quantile regression; however, as discussed earlier, quantile regression faces computational complexity issues. Thus, addressing model heterogeneity for expectile regression in streaming data remains an important and promising direction for future work.

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{b}({\bm{\beta}_{0}})=\frac{1}{N_{b}}\left\{\sum_{t=1}^{b-1}W_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})(\bm{\beta}_{0}-\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b-1})+n_{b}\cdot\bm{g}_{b}(\bm{\beta}_{0})\right\}=o_{p}(1).

(17)

According to (
11
), the renewable estimator
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}
satisfies

\mathcal{G}_{b}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b})=\textbf{0}.
Then we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{b}(\bm{\beta}_{0})=\mathcal{G}_{b}(\bm{\beta}_{0})-\mathcal{G}_{b}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b})=\frac{1}{N_{b}}\left\{\sum_{t=1}^{b-1}W_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})(\bm{\beta}_{0}-\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b})+n_{b}\cdot\left(\bm{g}_{b}(\bm{\beta}_{0})-\bm{g}_{b}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b})\right)\right\}=o_{p}(1).

(18)

Taking the first-order Taylor series expansion of
\bm{g}_{b}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b})
around
\bm{\beta}_{0}
yields

\displaystyle\bm{g}_{b}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b})

\displaystyle=\bm{g}_{b}(\bm{\beta}_{0})+\frac{1}{n_{b}}W_{n_{b}}(\bar{\bm{\beta}}_{b})(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}-\bm{\beta}_{0})

\displaystyle=\bm{g}_{b}(\bm{\beta}_{0})+\frac{1}{n_{b}}\left[W_{n_{b}}(\bm{\beta}_{0})+W_{n_{b}}(\bar{\bm{\beta}}_{b})-W_{n_{b}}(\bm{\beta}_{0})\right](\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}-\bm{\beta}_{0}),

(19)

where
\bar{\bm{\beta}}_{b}
lies between
\bm{\beta}_{0}
and
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}
. According to Lemma
1
, we know that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{b}}\left\|W_{n_{b}}(\bar{\bm{\beta}}_{b})-W_{n_{b}}(\bm{\beta}_{0})\right\|

\displaystyle=O_{p}(\|\bar{\bm{\beta}}_{b}-\bm{\beta}_{0}\|)=O_{p}(\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}-\bm{\beta}_{0}\|).

Thus, rewrite (
A.2
) as

\displaystyle\bm{g}_{b}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b})

\displaystyle=\bm{g}_{b}(\bm{\beta}_{0})+\frac{1}{n_{b}}W_{n_{b}}(\bm{\beta}_{0})(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}-\bm{\beta}_{0})+O_{p}(\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}-\bm{\beta}_{0}\|^{2}).

(20)

Combining (
18
) and (
20
) yields

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{G}_{b}(\bm{\beta}_{0})-\mathcal{G}_{b}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b})&=\frac{1}{N_{b}}\left\{\sum_{t=1}^{b-1}W_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})+W_{n_{b}}(\bm{\beta}_{0})\right\}(\bm{\beta}_{0}-\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b})\\
&+O_{p}\left(\frac{n_{b}}{N_{b}}\left\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}-\bm{\beta}_{0}\right\|^{2}\right)=o_{p}(1).\end{aligned}

(21)

As
N_{b}\rightarrow\infty
, with (C3), we can know that
N_{b}^{-1}\left\{\sum_{t=1}^{b-1}W_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})+W_{n_{b}}(\bm{\beta}_{0})\right\}
is positive definite. It follows that
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}\xrightarrow{p}\bm{\beta}_{0}
.

A.3
Technical proofs of Theorem 2 (Asymptotic Normality)

When
N_{1}=n_{1}\rightarrow\infty
it is easy to derive that
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
equals to
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
and satisfied
\sqrt{N_{1}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{1}-\bm{\beta}_{0})=\sqrt{N_{1}}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}-\bm{\beta}_{0})\xrightarrow{d}N\left(\textbf{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{w}^{-1}\bm{\Omega}\bm{\Sigma}_{w}^{-1}\right)
. In addition, the score function of first data batch has the following stochastic expression

\displaystyle\bm{g}_{1}(\bm{\beta}_{0})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n_{1}}W_{n_{1}}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1})(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}-\bm{\beta}_{0})+O_{p}(\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}-\bm{\beta}_{0}\|^{2}).

(22)

From eq (
17
) and (
21
), we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{N_{b}}\left\{\sum_{t=1}^{b-1}W_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})+W_{n_{b}}(\bm{\beta}_{0})\right\}(\bm{\beta}_{0}-\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b})&-\frac{1}{N_{b}}\sum_{t=1}^{b-1}W_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})(\bm{\beta}_{0}-\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b-1})\\
&-\frac{n_{b}}{N_{b}}\cdot\bm{g}_{b}(\bm{\beta}_{0})+O_{p}\left(\frac{n_{b}}{N_{b}}\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}-\bm{\beta}_{0}\|^{2}\right)=\bm{0}.\end{aligned}

(23)

Same as (
22
) for renewable estimator
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b-1}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{b-1}}\sum_{t=1}^{b-1}n_{t}\bm{g}_{t}(\bm{\beta}_{0})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N_{b-1}}\sum_{t=1}^{b-1}n_{t}\cdot\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b-1}-\bm{\beta}_{0})+O_{p}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{b-1}\frac{n_{t}}{N_{b-1}}\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t}-\bm{\beta}_{0}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N_{b-1}}\sum_{t=1}^{b-1}W_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b-1}-\bm{\beta}_{0})+O_{p}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{b-1}\frac{n_{t}}{N_{b-1}}\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t}-\bm{\beta}_{0}\|^{2}\right).

(24)

By replacing the second term in (
23
) with (
A.3
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{b}}\sum_{t=1}^{b}n_{t}\cdot\bm{g}_{t}(\bm{\beta}_{0})-\frac{1}{N_{b}}\left\{\sum_{t=1}^{b-1}W_{n_{t}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t})+W_{n_{b}}(\bm{\beta}_{0})\right\}(\bm{\beta}_{0}-\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b})+O_{p}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{b}\frac{n_{t}}{N_{b}}\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t}-\bm{\beta}_{0}\|^{2}\right)=\bm{0}.

With Theorem
1
, we have
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t}\xrightarrow{p}\bm{\beta}_{0}
for

, and by (C3), it holds

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{b}}\sum_{t=1}^{b}n_{t}\cdot\bm{g}_{t}(\bm{\beta}_{0})+\frac{1}{N_{b}}\sum_{t=1}^{b}W_{n_{t}}(\bm{\beta}_{0})(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}-\bm{\beta}_{0})+O_{p}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{b}\frac{n_{t}}{N_{b}}\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{t}-\bm{\beta}_{0}\|^{2}\right)=\bm{0},

(25)

which implies that

\displaystyle\sqrt{N_{b}}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}-\bm{\beta}_{0})=\Bigg[\frac{1}{N_{b}}\sum_{t=1}^{b}W_{n_{t}}(\bm{\beta}_{0})\Bigg]^{-1}\frac{-1}{\sqrt{N_{b}}}\sum_{t=1}^{b}n_{t}\cdot\bm{g}_{t}(\bm{\beta}_{0})+o_{p}(1)

With weak law of large number, we have
\frac{1}{N_{b}}\sum_{t=1}^{b}W_{n_{t}}(\bm{\beta}_{0})\xrightarrow{p}\bm{\Sigma}_{w}

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{\sqrt{N_{b}}}\sum_{t=1}^{b}n_{t}\cdot\bm{g}_{t}(\bm{\beta}_{0})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{b}}}\sum_{t=1}^{b}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}|\tau-1(y_{ti}<\bm{x}_{ti}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{0})|\cdot\bm{x}_{ti}(y_{ti}-\bm{x}_{ti}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{0})\xrightarrow{d}N\left(\textbf{0},\bm{\Omega}\right).

Hence, the renewable estimator
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}
satisfies the following asymptotic normal distribution

\sqrt{N_{b}}\big(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{b}-\bm{\beta}_{0}\big)\xrightarrow{d}N\left(\textbf{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{w}^{-1}\bm{\Omega}\bm{\Sigma}_{w}^{-1}\right).
