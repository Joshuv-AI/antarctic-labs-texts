Laticifer-specific engineering of rubber biosynthesis in Taraxacum kok-saghyz
Authors: Xue Yang, Junfeng Cao, Zhonghua Li, Yuan Yao, Tianyang Qu, Yuhao Zhang, Junya He, Huan Chen, Xing Liu, Yi Zhang, Jiyan Qi, Shengmin Zhang, Jiang Wang, Chaorong Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRubber dandelion (Taraxacum kok-saghyz, TKS) is a promising alternative source of natural rubber, which is synthesized in specialized laticifer cells. While genetic engineering of TKS holds promise for enhancing rubber yield, constitutive transgene expression often leads to undesirable pleiotropic effects. Therefore, identification of laticifer-specific promoters, especially those active throughout all stages of rubber accumulation, is essential for precise genetic manipulation of rubber biosynthesis. Here, we performed a comprehensive transcriptomic analysis across nine developmental stages and five tissue types in an elite TKS accession. This identified 1267 laticifer-preferentially expressed genes, 261 of which were constitutively overrepresented in laticifers at every developmental stage. Promoters from four candidate laticifer-specific genes were cloned and evaluated in stably transformed TKS. One promoter, pTkTHFI, drove expression exclusively in laticifers. We further showed that pTkTHFI retains its laticifer-specific activity in transgenic rubber tree (Hevea brasiliensis), highlighting its utility for targeted genetic improvement in both rubber-producing species. Finally, by overexpressing HbSRPP1, a key component of rubber transferase complex, using pTkTHFI versus the constitutive p35S promoter in TKS, we demonstrate that tissue-specific engineering enhances plant growth and rubber yield more effectively than constitutive expression, underscoring the advantage of precise genetic control for TKS improvement.Keywords: SRPP, THFI, Rubber dandelion, Transcriptome, Laticifer-specific, Natural rubber

Introduction

Natural rubber (NR) is an indispensable high-molecular-weight biopolymer and a critical strategic material with extensive industrial applications [1]. As an alternative source for sustainable NR production, rubber dandelion (Taraxacum kok-saghyz, TKS) holds considerable promise due to its temperate climate adaptability, suitability for mechanical harvesting, short growth cycle, and genetic tractability [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. NR biosynthesis occurs within specialized cells called laticifers, which synthesize and store latex [6]. While over 2500 plant species possess laticifers, only a few, most notably the rubber tree (Hevea brasiliensis), guayule (Parthenium argentatum), and TKS, produce high-molecular-weight, high-quality NR [7]. Importantly, the molecular characteristics and physical properties of TKS-derived NR are largely comparable to those of the rubber tree, making it a potential interchangeable alternative [8].

Research on genetic modification in TKS has largely relied on knockdown (RNAi) or knockout (CRISPR/Cas9) strategies to characterize gene function in NR biosynthesis. While these studies have clarified the roles of key genes, they have typically led to either impaired rubber production or only marginal increases [4]. For instance, the rubber transferase (RTase) complex, composed of cis-prenyltransferase (CPT), CPT-like (CPTL), rubber elongation factor (REF), and small rubber particle protein (SRPP), is essential for NR synthesis in rubber-producing plants [1]. In TKS, knockout of the laticifer-predominant TkCPT1 markedly reduced NR content, while dual knockout of TkCPT1 and TkCPT2 or single knockout of TkCPTL1 completely abolished rubber production and disrupted rubber particle formation [9]. Similarly, knockdown of TkCPTL1 (but not TkCPTL2) significantly decreased NR synthesis [10]. Parallel knockdown studies in the closely related species T. brevicorniculatum targeting laticifer-dominant TbCPT1/2/3 or TbCPTL also resulted in sharply reduced NR content [11,12], as did suppression of SRPPs in both species [13,14].

By contrast, overexpression studies in TKS remain scarce. Constitutive overexpression of TkSRPP3 yielded only a modest increase in rubber content [13], while overexpression of mevalonate (MVA) pathway genes in T. brevicorniculatum raised precursor levels without enhancing rubber yield [15]. Constitutive expression of the transcription factor TkMYC2 improved root growth and rubber production but caused severe shoot stunting [16], illustrating a common drawback: constitutive overexpression often leads to pleiotropic effects that limit trait improvement. Therefore, a more precise engineering strategy which using tissue-specific promoters to restrict ectopic expression to laticifer cells is needed to advance rubber synthesis in TKS.

Although several laticifer-preferred promoters have been reported, their suitability for TKS remains limited. Promoters such as pHbPEP16 and pHbSRPP from rubber tree showed activity in laticifers of TKS or T. brevicorniculatum, but their tissue specificity and activation strength are suboptimal [17,18]. Others, including pTbREF, pToPPO, and pHbHEV2.1, have been tested primarily in their native species with little cross-species validation or information on expression in non-laticifer tissues [[19], [20], [21]]. Thus, an ideal laticifer-specific promoter with strong, reliable activity in TKS is still lacking.

To address this gap, we performed a comprehensive transcriptomic analysis across nine developmental stages and five tissue types using an elite TKS accession ‘TK20’ [22]. This identified 1267 laticifer-preferentially expressed genes, including 261 that were consistently expressed in laticifers at all stages. Furthermore, a set of 20 laticifer-specific genes were focused based on tau (τ) index analysis. From this set, we selected four candidate promoters and characterized their tissue specificity in stably transformed TK20 plants. The application potential of one identified laticifer-specific promoter was highlighted by precisely overexpressing HbSRPP1 in laticifers of TK20.

Methods

Plant material and growth conditions

The Taraxacum kok-saghyz accession TK20 was vegetatively propagated and cultivated as previously described [22]. Seedlings with a root length of 3-5 cm, growing on solid ½ MS medium (containing 2.2 g L−1 Murashige and Skoog salts, 30 g L−1 sucrose, and 3 g L−1 phytagel, pH 5.8), were designated as the starting point (day 0). Developmental stages were defined at 30-day intervals as follows:

S1–S3: Sequential tissue culture stages.

S4: Seedling stage, following transplanting into soil for 30 days.

S5: Vernalization stage, involving a 30-day cycle of 16-h light (10 °C)/8-h dark (6 °C).

S6–S9: Subsequent growth stages encompassing full blossom, seed maturity, senescence, and dormancy.

For each stage, plant samples were harvested 2–4 h after the onset of the light period. A minimum of three plants were pooled to form one biological replicate. All samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Each frozen sample was later subdivided into three aliquots dedicated to transcriptomic, physiological, and histological analyses, respectively.

Determination of dry rubber content

TKS root samples were dried and ground into a fine powder. For analysis, 200 mg of root powder was mixed with 50 mL of ddH2O and incubated in a boiling water bath for 60 min. The aqueous solution was then replaced with 50 mL of a 3% (w/v) KOH solution, and boiling was continued for an additional 3 h. Following hydrolysis, the solid rubber was collected and rinsed thoroughly under running water to remove soluble impurities. Residual debris adhering to the rubber was manually removed using fine tweezers. The purified rubber was dried at 60 °C until a constant weight was achieved.

Determination of soluble sugar content

For sugar extraction, 200 mg of root powder was mixed with 50 mL of ddH2O and heated in a boiling water bath for 60 min. A 4 mL aliquot of the extract was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane.

For hydrolysis of sucrose and inulin, 200 μL of the filtered supernatant was mixed with 50 μL of 1 M HCl (resulting in a final HCl concentration of 0.2 M) and incubated in a boiling water bath for 45 min. After cooling to room temperature, the hydrolysate was diluted to an appropriate concentration for analysis.

The content of fructose, glucose, and sucrose was quantified using Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS; SCIEX Triple Quad 5500+ system) equipped with a carbohydrate-specific column. The chromatographic conditions were as follows: column temperature, 85 °C; pressure, 8000 psi; and flow rate, 0.6 mL/min.

Histological analysis

Root segments from each developmental stage (S1–S9) were fixed in 70% FAA for 24 h. The segments were taken from roots with a diameter of 3-6 mm Fixed samples were then processed for paraffin embedding. To visualize laticifers, dehydrated root segments were stained using an iodine-bromine (I-Br) protocol, as previously described [23]. In brief, sections were mounted on slides, baked overnight at 42 °C, and subsequently dewaxed and rehydrated. The prepared sections were immersed in a fast-green staining solution for 5–10 min, followed by a 5–10 min ethanol wash to remove excess stain. Stained tissues were observed and imaged using a light microscope.

Transcriptome profiling

Total RNA was extracted from leaves, roots, flowers, and pedicels collected across the nine developmental stages. For each tissue, three biological replicates were processed, with each replicate comprising a pooled sample from at least three plants. RNA was isolated using the RNAprep Pure Polysaccharide-Polyphenol Plant Total RNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Additionally, latex-specific RNA was obtained by collecting latex expelled from root incisions into 300 μl of extraction buffer, following a previously established method [24]. RNA quality for all samples was assessed using an INFINITE 200 PRO (TECAN, Switzerland) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA) to determine the RNA Integrity Number (RIN). High-quality RNA was used to construct cDNA libraries with the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 platform, yielding approximately 351 GB of raw sequencing data. The raw data were processed using FastP (v.0.22.0) to remove adapter sequences, filter out low-quality bases, and exclude short reads, resulting in ∼308 GB of high-quality clean data [25].

Clean reads were then aligned to a TK20 reference genome (unpublished data) using HISAT2 [26], generating sequence alignment/map (BAM) files under default parameters. Alignments were sorted using SAMtools (v1.14) [27]. Read counts for each protein-coding gene were obtained using the featureCounts function from the R package Rsubread, considering only uniquely mapped reads [28]. Raw counts were normalized to Fragments Per Kilobase per Million mapped fragments (FPKM). To minimize transcriptional noise, a gene was considered expressed if its FPKM value was ≥1 in at least one tissue. This criterion identified 27,091 expressed protein-coding genes. For downstream analyses, the FPKM expression matrix was filtered to retain only genes with an FPKM ≥1 in at least three samples (Table S4). Expression values were log2-transformed (FPKM + 0.1) to stabilize variance.

Bioinformatic and statistical analysis pipeline

Several analytical approaches were applied to the transcriptome dataset: Inter-sample correlations were calculated using Pearson correlation coefficients, and a clustered heatmap was generated with the R package pheatmap. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and hierarchical clustering were performed on Z-score normalized gene expression data. To systematically identify global expression differences, Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) was conducted using the mixOmics R package. This analysis compared latex vs. non-latex tissues and examined seedling, mature, and senescence stages. Genes with Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) scores >1.0 were considered significant contributors to group separation. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis for identified gene sets was performed using clusterProfiler, with a significance threshold of P ≤ 0.05 [29]. Cis-acting elements in the promoter sequences were predicted using the online tool PlantCARE (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/).

Identification of latex-specific gene expression

To identify genes with latex-specific expression, we implemented a multi-stage stringent screening strategy. Using the generated expression data, we constructed a sample metadata table designating tissue type and developmental stage. Differential expression analysis was conducted independently for each developmental stage (S4 – S9) using the DESeq2 package, comparing latex against all other non-latex tissues. Candidate genes were selected based on the following concurrent criteria: an average FPKM ≥10 in latex tissue, an absolute fold change ≥10 (latex vs. other tissues), and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. This process generated stage-specific lists of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), from which a core set of genes co-expressed across all stages was derived. Latex specificity analysis was performed using the Tau (τ) index analysis [30] with τ > 0.8 as threshold for tissue-specificity.

RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated from different tissues using the RNAprep Pure Polysaccharide-Polyphenol Plant Total RNA Extraction Kit, which included an on-column DNase I digestion step to remove genomic DNA. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit with gDNA wiper (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed in 20 μL reaction volumes using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). Gene-specific primers are listed in Table S5. The Tkref gene served as the internal control for normalization. Relative transcript levels for each target gene were calculated using the 2−ΔCt method, as described previously [22]. All reactions were performed with three independent biological replicates, each measured in technical triplicate. Statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05.

Construction of transformation vectors

Genomic DNA was isolated from young TKS leaves using a Plant Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China). Putative laticifer-specific promoter sequences were amplified from this genomic DNA by PCR using gene-specific primers (Table S5). Each amplified promoter was cloned via homologous recombination (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) into the binary vector pCAMBIA1303, replacing the constitutive CaMV 35S promoter. The promoter fragments were inserted between the Hind III and Xma I restriction sites to drive expression of the β-glucuronidase (GUS) and green fluorescent protein (GFP) reporter genes. To generate constructs for functional validation, the p35S:HbSRPP1 fragment was PCR-amplified and inserted between the BamH I and Kpn I restriction sites of pCAMBIA1300-GFP, creating the p35S:HbSRPP1 overexpression construct. Similarly, the pTkTHFI:HbSRPP1 fragment was inserted between the BamH I and Kpn I restriction sites of pCAMBIA1300-GFP, creating the pTkTHFI:HbSRPP1 overexpression construct.

For construct used in rubber tree transformation, The pTkTHFI:GUS sequences were cloned into a modified pCAMBIA2300 vector, which harbors separate GFP transcriptional unit [31], between the BstX I and Xma I restriction sites, resulting in three separate transcriptional units to allow for independent expression of nptII, GUS, and GFP.

Subsequently, the verified binary vectors were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105. Finally, these Agrobacterium strains harboring various constructs were used for TKS and rubber tree transformation.

TKS and rubber tree transformation

TKS transformation was performed as previously described [22]. The transgenic plants were verified at both DNA and transcriptional level using gene-specific primers (Table S5). Rubber tree transformation was performed as previously described [32]. The resistant calli were screened using the LUYOR-3260 fluorescent protein excitation light source (LUYOR, Shanghai, China). The GFP-positive lines were subjected to embryogenesis. Finally, the regenerated embryos placed on the rooting medium for root induction. Transgenic roots were dissected for histology analysis.

Histochemical GUS activity analysis

Histochemical localization of β-glucuronidase (GUS) activity was performed using 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl-β-D-glucuronide (X-Gluc) as the substrate, following an established protocol with minor modifications [18]. Fresh plant samples (including leaves, buds, flowers, pedicels, and roots) were thoroughly rinsed with distilled water and immersed in GUS staining solution (1 mM X-Gluc, 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer, 0.5 mM potassium ferrocyanide, 0.5 mM potassium ferricyanide, 10 mM EDTA, 0.1% Triton X-100, pH 7.0). All tissues except roots were subjected to a 10-min vacuum infiltration to facilitate substrate penetration. Samples were then incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 3–6 h. Following incubation, tissues were cleared of chlorophyll and pigments by soaking in 95% ethanol. For latex analysis, freshly exuded latex was collected from the root-shoot junction directly into pre-chilled microcentrifuge tubes containing GUS staining solution. The tubes were incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 3 h. Stained transverse root sections and leaves were imaged using a Leica stereo-fluorescence microscope (Leica TL5000 Ergo Transmitted Light Base). Other intact tissues were photographed with a digital camera.

Confocal microscopy

For GFP and OBNA acquisition, images were taken using N-SIM S (Nikon, Japan). Laser excitation wavelength and emission bandwidths were 488 nm and 500-550 nm for GFP, 488 nm and 570–616 nm for OBNA, respectively.

Statistical analysis

The differences among multiple groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by multiple comparison tests (Tukey's method). ANOVA statistical analysis was performed using Origin 2025b statistical software (OriginLab Corporation, MA, USA).

Results

Physiological analysis of TK20 across its life cycle

To ensure a consistent genetic background in the outcrossing species TKS, the TK20 line was vegetatively propagated. Nine key developmental stages (S1–S9), sampled at 30-day intervals, were defined (Fig. 1A). Stages S1–S3 represent tissue-culture seedlings, S4 represents soil-grown seedlings (30 days post-transfer), and S5–S9 correspond to vernalization, full bloom, seed maturity, senescence, and dormancy, respectively. Latex exudation was observed at the shoot-root junction at all stages (Fig. 1A, inset). Cross-sectional staining with iodine-bromide (I-Br), which specifically marks laticifer cells, revealed their characteristic ring-shaped organization throughout development (Fig. 1B). Shoot biomass increased sharply at S4, peaked at S6 (full bloom), and subsequently declined during senescence and dormancy (Fig. 1C). In contrast, root biomass, root-to-shoot ratio, dry rubber content, and root diameter showed a continuous increase across the life cycle (Fig. 1D–G). This indicates that the root acts as a strong sink tissue, with growth and rubber biosynthesis persisting even during shoot senescence.

Since TKS roots accumulate both natural rubber (NR) and inulin, two competing pathways for soluble sugars [33], we quantified relevant carbohydrates contents. Inulin, sucrose, and fructose content peaked during vigorous growth stages (S4–S7) before declining sharply upon senescence (Fig. 1H–J). Glucose was high in early stages (S1–S4) but also decreased significantly by S8 (Fig. 1K). These dynamics suggest that NR accumulation proceeds steadily with root development, while the pool of competing carbon sources like inulin and sucrose peaks earlier and subsequently declines, potentially facilitating sustained NR synthesis.

Global transcriptomic profiling reveals tissue- and stage-specific expression landscapes

To explore transcriptomic dynamics during development, RNA-seq was performed on five tissues across nine stages. Latex from S1–S3, which contains minimal NR (Fig. 1F), was excluded from analysis. Correlation analysis demonstrated high reproducibility among biological replicates for latex, root, and leaf samples across stages (Fig. 2A–C). Latex transcriptomes were highly correlated at later stages (S6–S9), indicating similar gene expression during maturation and dormancy (Fig. 2A). Root transcriptomes clustered into early (S1–S3) and late (S6–S9) groups, while leaf transcriptomes grouped into S1–S4 and S7–S9 (Fig. 2B and C).

Hierarchical clustering and principal component analysis (PCA) confirmed distinct transcriptome profiles for each tissue, with all latex samples forming a separate clade (Fig. 2D and E). To further characterize the latex-specific profile, we performed Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA), which clearly separated latex from non-latex samples (Fig. 2F). When the six latex samples were grouped according to dry rubber content (Fig. 1F) as early (S4&S5), mid (S6&S7), and late (S8&S9) stage, PLS-DA showed distinct separation along the first variate (Fig. 2G). Collectively, this dataset provides a comprehensive expression atlas for identifying tissue-specific genes across TKS development.

Identification and pathway analysis of latex-preferential genes

Differential expression analysis between latex and non-latex tissues identified a total of 1267 latex-differentially expressed genes (DEGs) after pooling all pairwise comparisons at each stage (Fig. 3A; Table S1). A core set of 261 DEGs was common to all stages, indicating constitutive high expression in laticifers (Fig. 3B; Table S2). Stage-specific overlaps defined early- (S4&S5), mid- (S6&S7), and late-stage (S8&S9) groups containing 152, 152, and 414 DEGs, respectively (Fig. 3B). Expression heatmap of the 261 core DEGs confirmed their predominant expression in latex across stages, though some genes also showed elevated expression in roots (Fig. 3C). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed significant overrepresentation of secondary metabolite pathways, including terpenoid backbone biosynthesis, glycerophospholipid/ether lipid metabolism, and indole/isoquinoline alkaloid biosynthesis, across all DEG categories (Fig. 3D). This highlights the consistent importance of terpenoid and lipid metabolism in NR-producing laticifers. The enrichment of alkaloid biosynthesis pathways underscores the defensive role of latex throughout growth [34]. Interestingly, pathways like pentose and glucuronate interconversions, cyanoamino acid metabolism, and glucosinolate biosynthesis were enriched in early and late stages but not mid-stage, suggesting transcriptional reprogramming during peak reproductive growth (occurs in mid-stage).

Validation of constitutive latex-specific marker genes

From the 261 core DEGs, tau (τ) index analysis, a robust algorithm to predict tissue-specificity [30,35], was performed to screen for the candidate latex-specific genes. The top 20 genes based on τ index at S6 were shortlisted (Fig. 4A; Table S3). After manual curation, four candidates with strong latex-predominant expression were selected: TkMLPL28, TkMLPL29, TkTHFI, and the well-characterized TkREF. Except for relatively higher root expression of TkMLPL28/29 at S1/S2, all showed high latex-specificity across the TK20 life cycle (Fig. 4B). RT-qPCR analysis from S4 to S9 in TK20 confirmed the laticifer-specific expression of all four genes (Fig. 4C). This specificity was further validated in two additional TKS accessions (TKS1 and TKS-EF) at the S6 stage (Fig. 4D), demonstrating that these genes are robust, latex-specific markers across developmental stages and genotypes in TKS.

Functional validation of candidate promoters in transgenic TK20

The approximately 2-kb upstream regions of the four candidate genes, which harbor core promoter elements such as the TATA box and TACAAAA motif along with common regulatory motifs including CAAAT and CCAAT (Fig. S1A), were selected as candidate promoters. In total, 28 cis-acting elements were identified in these regions. Among them, multiple elements, including MYB binding sites, MeJA-responsive elements, light-responsive elements, and auxin-responsive elements, are present in each candidate promoter (Fig. S1B). The candidate promoters were fused to a GUS-eGFP reporter and stably transformed into TK20 (Fig. 5A). For each construct, five independent transgenic lines were analyzed to confirm genomic integration and elevated GUS expression (Fig. 5B–E). Tissue-specific RT-qPCR on three selected transgenic lines at S6 revealed that pTkTHFI and pTkREF drove GUS expression exclusively in latex (Fig. 5F and G). In contrast, pTkMLPL28 and pTkMLPL29 mediated strong expression in multiple tissues, including leaf, latex, flower, and pedicel (Fig. 5H and I).

Histochemical GUS staining showed all promoters drove vein-associated expression in leaves, consistent with laticifer localization (Fig. 6A). Notably, the pTkTHFI promoter showed evenly distributed staining in veins throughout the whole leaf while others tend to strongly expressed in mid-ribs (Fig. S2). To test cross-species functionality, the pTkTHFI promoter was used to drive GUS expression in the rubber tree (Hevea brasiliensis) elite cultivar RY7-33-97, using a vector with a separate GFP transcription unit as a transformation marker (Fig. 7C). GFP fluorescence confirmed successful transformation in callus and embryos from two independent lines (Fig. 7D). In transgenic rubber tree seedling roots, GUS staining precisely co-localized with I-Br-stained laticifers (Fig. 7E), demonstrating that the pTkTHFI promoter retains its latex-specific activity in transgenic rubber tree.

Overexpression of HbSRPP1 via pTkTHFI promoter stimulates TKS growth and rubber accumulation

To evaluate the potential of the laticifer-specific pTkTHFI promoter for transgenic improvement of TKS, we overexpressed HbSRPP1, a key component of the rubber transferase complex from rubber tree, under the control of either pTkTHFI or the constitutive p35S promoter in TK20 (Fig. 8A). RT-qPCR analysis confirmed strong accumulation of HbSRPP1 transcripts in two independent transgenic lines for each construct, while no expression was detected in WT controls (Fig. 8B and C). As expected, p35S drove constitutive HbSRPP1 expression across leaves, latex, and roots, whereas pTkTHFI restricted expression to laticifers (latex). Notably, in latex, the pTkTHFI promoter drove HbSRPP1 expression at levels several-fold higher than those achieved with p35S, when normalized to the same reference gene (Tkref) (Fig. 8B and C), demonstrating the strong activation strength of pTkTHFI promoter.

Phenotypic assessment revealed that constitutive overexpression (p35S:HbSRPP1) did not affect shoot biomass compared to WT, but significantly inhibited root growth (Fig. 8D and E). Dry rubber content in these lines showed an increasing trend, though not statistically significant (Fig. 8E). Dry rubber yield in these lines was significantly decreased relative to WT due to impaired root growth (Fig. 8E). In contrast, laticifer-specific overexpression (pTkTHFI:HbSRPP1) significantly enhanced shoot biomass, root biomass, dry rubber content, and dry rubber yield relative to WT (Fig. 8F and G). These results demonstrate that laticifer-specific overexpression of HbSRPP1 via the pTkTHFI promoter improves both growth and rubber accumulation in TKS, highlighting its superior utility over constitutive expression for the biotechnological improvement of TKS.

Discussion

In vascular plants, laticifers constitute a specialized third tubing system, distinct from xylem and phloem, which accumulates latex in roughly 10% of angiosperms [6,37]. This study presents a comprehensive transcriptome atlas of Taraxacum kok-saghyz (TKS), an outcrossing species harboring articulated laticifers analogous to those in rubber tree [7]. This dataset provides a foundational resource for investigating key biological questions in TKS, including the molecular drivers of sustained root growth, the genetic control of floral self-incompatibility, and the specialized metabolism of laticifers.

In this work, we identified 1267 latex-specific genes. Notably, 261 of these genes maintained latex specificity across all developmental stages, underscoring their likely essential functions in rubber biosynthesis and latex metabolism. While the roles of many genes in this set remain uncharacterized, we focused on high-priority candidate latex-specific genes. These were selected based on stringent criteria: sustained latex-specific expression throughout the TKS life cycle, high predictive value as latex markers in the tau (τ) index analysis, and minimal expression in non-laticifer root tissues during peak rubber accumulation. Our analysis highlights four key players. Trypsin Hageman factor inhibitor (THFI/CHFI), known to inhibit blood coagulation factor FXIIa in mammals, has an unknown function in plants [38,39]. We hypothesize it may prevent latex coagulation within TKS laticifers. Major latex-like proteins (MLPLs) are implicated in transporting hydrophobic compounds and stress responses [40]. The enrichment of terpenoid/lipid metabolism pathways among latex-specific genes, coupled with the presence of three MLPL genes in the top 20 for latex specificity (Fig. 4B), strongly supports their role as carriers for hydrophobic substrates during NR biosynthesis. Rubber elongation factor (REF) is a well-established component of rubber particle formation [7]. Promoter activity assays in stable TKS transformants revealed distinct regulatory patterns. While all tested promoters drove expression in laticifers, only the pTkTHFI promoter conferred strict latex specificity for the GUS and GFP reporter genes. Seven cis-acting motifs were reported in promoter regions of selected laticifer-specifically expressed genes in Hevea brasiliensis [41], and six homologous motifs are present within the pTkTHF1 promoter (Fig. S1B). Notably, two MYB transcriptional factor members, HblMYB19 and HblMYB44, were proposed to regulate laticifer-specific genes in Hevea brasiliensis [42], however, it requires further investigation on which homologous MYB member works in a similar way in TKS as each candidate promoter in this study harbor multiple MYB binding sites (Fig. S1B). Interestingly, in our study, the TKS homolog of a previously reported laticifer-preferential pREF promoter from T. brevicorniculatum [15,19] failed to exhibit latex-specific activity in TKS roots (Fig. 6). The discrepancy between latex-specific gene expression patterns and their promoter activities suggests that laticifer-specific expression is not governed solely by promoter cis-elements interacting with transcription factors [43], but may also involve post-transcriptional mechanisms such as mRNA modification by RNA-binding proteins [44].

Engineering desirable traits with constitutive promoters often leads to pleiotropic effects that can undermine crop improvement. The pTkTHFI laticifer-specific promoter identified in this study offers a powerful solution to this challenge, holding significant biotechnological promise for the precise genetic enhancement of rubber yield. Our comparative analysis clearly demonstrates its advantage: while both pTkTHFI and the constitutive p35S promoter drove HbSRPP1 overexpression in latex, only laticifer-specific expression under pTkTHFI significantly increased both plant growth and rubber accumulation. The beneficial effects of pTkTHFI:HbSRPP1 on TKS growth were unexpected. Laticifer-specific overexpression of HbSRPP1 may enhance the rubber biosynthesis pathway, thereby increasing sink strength in root laticifers, which in turn may stimulate source activity in leaves through source–sink communications [45]. However, the underlying mechanism requires further investigation. In contrast, constitutive p35S-driven expression resulted in stunted root growth and only a marginal, non-significant increase in rubber content, a pattern consistent with previous reports, such as the limited rubber yield improvement from constitutive TkSRPP3 overexpression [13]. These findings underscore that targeted transgene expression within laticifers is critical for effective genetic engineering without compromising plant fitness. Furthermore, the utility of pTkTHFI extends beyond a single gene manipulation. It provides a precision tool for manipulating other pathways critical to rubber biosynthesis, such as upstream sugar transport and metabolism [46,47], the mevalonate (MVA) pathway [15,48], or additional core RTase complex components like CPT, CPTL, REF [7]. Focusing expression within the laticifer is likely to be essential for optimizing flux through these interconnected pathways.

Furthermore, pTkTHFI serves as a unique tool for fundamental research. It enables laticifer-specific bait labeling, a strategy that could help resolve the long-standing challenge of identifying missing helper proteins within the elusive RTase complex. This is particularly critical given that ectopic co-expression of known core components has so far failed to reconstitute de novo natural rubber synthesis [49].

In summary, we provide a comprehensive transcriptomic resource that will advance molecular research in TKS. Crucially, the functional pTkTHFI promoter is not only a key asset for applied crop improvement but also a vital reagent for advancing our basic understanding of rubber biosynthesis.
