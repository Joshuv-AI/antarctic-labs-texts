Axis screw parallel to the sagittal plane versus traditional pedicle screw in the treatment of atlantoaxial fixation: a finite element study
Authors: Baifang Zeng, Meng Ding, Lang Li, Chao Wu, Bingwei Qin, Danwei Shen, Jiayan Deng, Xiangyu Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aims to conduct a finite element analysis (FEA) to assess the bio-mechanical properties of C2 sagittal-parallel pedicle screw (PPS) in fixation for atlantoaxial instability, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for its clinical application.MethodsA total of 5 intact C1-2 finite element models were established. Based on this, instability models were developed and two different fixation methods were applied for each model: C1 lateral mass screw (LMS) combined with C2 sagittal-parallel pedicle screw (C1LMS + C2PPS), and C1 lateral mass screw combined with C2 traditional pedicle screw (C1LMS + C2PS). Under a physiological load of 40 N, a pure moment of 1.5 Nm was used to simulate movements of the cervical spine in flexion, extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation. The von Mises stress of implants and the segment range of motion (ROM) were analyzed and compared statistically.ResultsThe intact model was validated and showed good consistency with other studies in terms of range of motion (ROM). In flexion and extension, the C1LMS + C2PPS resulted in lower segment ROM (12.4% and 6.3% decrease) and stress concentration (15.9% decrease in flexion) compared to C1LMS + C2PS. However, in lateral bending and axial rotation, the C1LMS + C2PPS exhibited higher segment ROM (42.9% and 5.9% increase) and stress concentration (8.7% and 21.4% increase) compared to C1LMS + C2PS.ConclusionBoth methods were safe and stable for the fixation of atlantoaxial instability. Compared to C1LMS + C2PS, the use of C1LMS + C2PPS may provide better stability and a lower risk of implant failure in flexion and extension. Clinically, it is feasible to utilize the C2 sagittal-parallel pedicle screw in fixing atlantoaxial instability.Keywords: Biomechanics, Finite element analysis, Axis pedicle screw, Atlantoaxial instability, Atlantoaxial screw fixation

Background

The atlantoaxial junction, comprising the atlas (C1) and axis (C2), is a distinctive structure of the cervical spine that facilitates approximately 50% of pivot motion, flexion, and extension [1, 2]. Atlantoaxial instability can arise from physical trauma, ligament laxity, tumors, tuberculosis, rheumatoid arthritis or congenital malformation [3, 4], posing potential risks to patients. Surgical intervention is often necessary for most cases of atlantoaxial instability to achieve stability and decompression of the spinal cord [5]. However, due to its intricate anatomical nature, surgical fixation carries inherent risks such as damage to vital structures like the spinal cord and vertebral artery [6]. Various fixation methods have been reported in clinical practice for treating atlantoaxial instability. In previous decades, techniques involving spinous process wiring or modified posterior wiring were described, however these approaches lack sufficient fixation strength as they do not involve three column fixation method [7]. Trans-articular screw fixation was introduced in 1992 but has shown an increased risk of vertebral artery injury [8] and motor function impairment in the upper cervical region. The utilization of pars screws could enhance fixation stability [10–12]. However, the pars screw only reaches the transverse foramen with an average length of 16 mm, and its bio-mechanical properties are sub-optimal. Therefore, pedicle screws (PS) have emerged as the predominant choice for managing atlantoaxial instability due to their robust bio-mechanical fixation characteristics [13–15]. Nevertheless, challenges may arise during surgical placement of C2 pedicle screws owing to soft tissue occlusion. Excessive deviation in screw angulation can potentially result in direct damage to nerves or blood vessels.

Wu Chao et al. proposed a novel C2 pedicle screw trajectory parallel to the sagittal plane (PPS), which effectively avoids soft tissue tension and facilitates easier screw placement without any leaning angle [16]. The feasibility of accommodating C2PPS placement is observed in approximately 80% of patients. However, there is currently a lack of relevant bio-mechanical properties for C2PPS, and the fixation stability cannot be solely explained by range of motion (ROM) as determined from traditional cadaver studies. Therefore, this study employs finite element analysis (FEA) to overcome concerns related to cadaver-based investigations. We compare stress distribution and segment ROM between C2PPS and C2PS combined with C1 lateral mass screw fixation for atlantoaxial instability using five individual FE models.

Materials and methods

Finite element model of intact atlantoaxial

The study was conducted by Zigong Fourth People’s Hospital (Zigong, Sichuan, China). Selection criteria: (a) Patients with degenerative diseases or severe ligament ossification in the cervical spine were excluded. (b) Patients with a history of trauma in the upper cervical spine were excluded. (c) Patients with cervical dysplasia, severe rheumatic disease, or tumors were excluded. (d) Contrast-enhanced CT scans or images with significant artifacts were excluded. (e) Only patients aged 18–65 years old and with normal anatomical structure in the upper cervical spine were included.

From January 2022 to May 2022, a total of 59 cases were identified that met the criteria, involving five patients (three males and two females) aged between 32 and 46 years. The original cervical computed tomography data were extracted in DICOM format from our radiology department’s imaging database using a SOMATO FORCE 128-slice spiral CT scanner with scans of thicknesses measuring at 0.625 mm (Simons, Munich, Germany). Subsequently, all CT data were imported into Mimics software version 22.0 (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium), where the atlantoaxial structure was meticulously preserved utilizing the ‘separate mask’ tool. Three-dimensional reconstruction and surface fixation techniques were then employed. Following this step, geometric refinement was performed using Geomagic Wrap version 2017 (3DS System Corporation, USA). The C1 and C2 vertebrae comprised an internal cancellous bone along with a cortical bone shell possessing a thickness of approximately 0.5 mm. Finally, the model underwent assembly as well as cartilage and fixation establishment within SolidWorks version 2016 (Dassault Systèmes, France).

In the intact FEA models, the isotropic liner elastic material properties were assumed for the model, and the parameters of materials were assigned based on a previous study [2, 17]. The selection of spinal ligaments crucial to cervical spine function was informed by anatomical literature, encompassing the transverse ligament, anterior and posterior longitudinal ligaments, atlantoaxial posterior membrane, capsular ligament, flavum ligament, tectorial membrane, and nuchal ligament. To simulate these spinal ligaments accurately in our model (Fig. 1), tension-only spring elements were employed. The specific material properties utilized are provided in Table 1.

For model validation, the results of range of motion (ROM) in atlantoaxial were calculated based on nodal displacement in different axes orientations and compared with the results from previous cadaveric experiments and other upper cervical finite element studies under the same loading boundary conditions [17–19].

Two fixation method for unstable atlantoaxial model

The intact C1-2 model was modified to simulate an unstable atlantoaxial model by removing all ligaments, following the method described by Chun et al. [2]. Both posterior internal fixation methods consisted of 3.5-diameter screws and a 3.2-diameter rod, guided by the same spine surgeon using computer software. To simplify the fixation system, the screw threads were replaced with cylinders and unnecessary features of screw nuts were eliminated.

Initially, the C1 lateral mass screws were applied in all models, and two different screw placement methods were utilized for the C2 vertebra, namely C2PS and C2PPS. The placement of C1LMS and C2PS followed the methodologies described in previous literature [20, 21]. Additionally, based on a prior study indicating that PPS was on average 2 mm shorter than PS in the C2 vertebrae, this discrepancy was incorporated into our model. The entry point of C2PPS was approximately 2 mm medial to the mid-line of the inferior facet joint and positioned 5 mm superior to it [16]. Furthermore, the screw orientation remained parallel to the upper surface of the pedicle channel within C2. By isolating a three-dimensional model of C2 using software, we adjusted its view to align with the direction of pedicles in transparent mode, which enabling us to observe the screw placement channel for PPS (Fig. 2). Ultimately, two fixation methods for unstable atlantoaxial conditions were established: one involving C1LMS and C2PS (Fig. 3), while another incorporating C1LMS and C2PPS. Subsequently, the models were imported into Ansys Workbench software2020 (ANSYS Inc., United States) for further analysis.

Mesh and sensitive test

The three-dimensional structures of the model were meshed using quadratic tetrahedral elements (Tetra10). A mesh sensitivity test was conducted under a vertical loading condition of 40 N. Eight different mesh sizes were employed in this study: 4, 3.5, 3, 2.5, 2, 1.5, 1 and 0.5 mm. The stress results for the screw-rod fixation were extracted and presented in Fig. 4. Convergence was considered achieved when the change in stress became smaller than 5%. As depicted in the line chart, a slight reduction in stress was observed once the mesh size decreased below 1 mm (a decrease of approximately by only by 0.93% from a mesh size of 1 mm to 0.5 mm). To strike an optimal balance between computational cost and simulation accuracy, a meshing size of 1 mm was adopted for all finite element models.

Loading and boundary condition

The C2 vertebral bottom and bilateral inferior articular process were fully constrained in six degrees of freedom. A vertical loading force of 40 N was applied to the upper facet joint of C1 to simulate the weight of the human head. Additionally, a pure moment of 1.5 Nm was applied to the same location to simulate different body configurations. Facet joints had face-to-face contact with a coefficient of friction set at 0.1, while other contacts were defined as bonded. After convergence calculation results, ROMs for C1-2 and von Mises stress for screw-rod fixation system were recorded and used for comparative analysis.

Statistical analysis

All analysis data were recorded as the means ± standard deviation and analyzed using SPSS 19.0 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). A paired sample t-test was conducted to compare ROMs and stress between the two groups, with a significance level set at P < 0.05. Finally, the data was presented in the form of a bar chart using GraphPad Prism 8 software (La Jolla, USA).

Stress distribution of the implants

The contour map of von Mises stress exhibited a similar distribution pattern in both the C1LMS + C2PS and C1LMS + C2PPS models. Stress was predominantly concentrated at the screw tail during flexion, extension, and axial rotation, while it was concentrated at the screw-rod junction during lateral bending (Fig. 6). The stress results for the implants are presented in Table 3. Quantitative analysis revealed significant differences (p < 0.05) between the C1LMS + C2PS and C1LMS + C2PPS models under all loading conditions except for extension (Fig. 5). Compared to the C1LMS + C2PS models, average stress levels decreased by 15.9% in flexion but increased by 8.7% and 21.4% in lateral bending and axial rotation, respectively.

Discussion

Advantage of C2 sagittal-parallel pedicle screw

Currently, pedicle screws are predominantly utilized for posterior C2 screw placement, however, this approach carries a substantial surgical risk and is associated with numerous complications, including the potentially fatal complication of vertebral artery injury. The reported incidence of vascular injury with C2 pedicle screws can be as high as 8.2% [16], which further increases in cases involving high-riding vertebral artery (HRVA). Approximately 12–31% of patients are considered unsuitable for C2 pedicle placement due to HRVA [9, 22]. We propose that the utilization of C2 sagittal-parallel pedicle screws offers several advantages over traditional counterparts. Firstly, it eliminates the medial angle and enables easy confirmation of screw safety through standard antero-position X-ray fluoroscopy during surgery. Surgeons can focus on adjusting the cephalad angle without concerns regarding the medial aspect. Secondly, its more medial entry point reduces the risk of blood vessel and nerve root injuries compared to conventional screws [16]. Thirdly, it requires less bone tissue exposure and causes minimal occlusion of surrounding soft tissues, facilitating easier screw placement [16]. Lastly, previous studies have demonstrated that the average length of C2 sagittal-parallel pedicle screws exceeds 25 mm, meeting clinical requirements.

FE simulation mechanics experiment

The application of finite element analysis (FEA) to the medical field in the late 1960s initiated a trend of bio-mechanical research through computer simulation, providing unique advantages over in vitro studies of range of motion (ROM) data in spine related research. FEA enables detailed examination of stress distribution in instruments, bone structures, and soft tissues, allowing for clear visualization of local force transmission and stress distribution throughout internal fixation [17, 23, 24]. Furthermore, FEA eliminates limitations imposed by specimen availability and allows for repeated performance of multiple types of experiments on the same model without deformation or damage. In this study, three-dimensional models were constructed based on high-resolution CT scans to ensure accurate representation within the FE models.

The main ligaments of the atlantoaxial joint were established using spring elements, and the integrity of the models was validated through previous cadaver studies and finite element analyses. To prevent stress singularity generation, a fillet feature with a small radius was implemented at the screw-rod junction, which minimally altered the implant structure. The mesh sensitivity test successfully converged with a controlled mesh size of 1 mm using quadratic tetrahedral elements (Tetra10), considering computational resource limitations and calculation accuracy requirements. Finally, we conducted a comparative study involving five groups of models and obtained statistically significant results. Compared to a single finite element model, these findings exhibit reduced individual variability and improved reliability.

ROM of atlantoaxial junction

Under different physiological loads, both types of fixation methods exhibited a significant reduction in range of motion (ROM), with values approaching less than 1 degree. In flexion and extension loading conditions, the ROMs of the C1LMS + C2PPS model were significantly lower compared to those of the C1LMS + C2PS model (P < 0.05). These findings indicate that C1LMS + C2PPS demonstrates superior ability in restricting sagittal plane movements. However, in lateral bending and axial rotation, the ROMs of C1LMS + C2PPS were significantly higher than those of C1LMS + C2PS (P < 0.05). Notably, during lateral bending, there was a substantial difference of 42.9%, suggesting clear advantages for C1LMS + C2PS under this loading condition. Nevertheless, the average lateral bending ROMs were measured at 0.19 and 0.27 for C1LMS + C2PS and C1LMS + C2PPS respectively, and these differences may not hold clinical significance. Overall, these observations align with the characteristics associated with sagittal-parallel pedicle screw and pedicle screw trajectory, where greater consistency between screw direction and torque direction leads to more rigid fixation.

Maximum stress of implants

According to the contour map of stress distribution, the stress is mainly concentrated at the posterior part of the screw-rod system. Compared with the C1LMS + C2PS group, the C1LMS + C2PPS exhibited a significantly lower stress concentration on implants in flexion (15.9% reduction), but no significant difference was observed in extension. This suggests that C1LMS + C2PPS may reduce the risk of internal fixation failure in sagittal plane movement under physiological loads. However, C1LMS + C2PPS showed higher stress concentration on implants in lateral bending (8.7% increase) and axial rotation (21.4% increase), indicating that they are less able to disperse stress concentration under these physiological loads. In sagittal plane movements, C2PPS could better reduce stress concentration while C2PS has advantages in lateral bending and axial rotation due to this difference being consistent with ROM reduction caused by similar reasons. Generally, maximum stresses ranged from 81.5 MPa to 174.5 MPa under different physiological loads in this study, however, both internal fixation methods were relatively safe and reliable as titanium alloy material yield strength ranges between 795 and 827 MPa and ultimate strength ranges between 860 and 896 MPa [25].

The limitations of this study are as follows: (a) This study solely focuses on computer simulation processes conducted under ideal conditions. Additionally, the evaluation of the bio-mechanical properties of C2PPS necessitates its implementation in cadaver models. (b) The finite element analysis performed in this study is a static analysis that simulates normal physiological activity of the cervical spine. However, human cervical vertebrae exhibit complex dynamics, requiring more sophisticated analytical methods to elucidate the mechanical properties of C2PPS in future investigations.

Conclusions

Both methods were safe and stable for the fixation of atlantoaxial instability. Compared to C1LMS + C2PS, the use of C1LMS + C2PPS may provide better stability and a lower risk of implant failure in flexion and extension. Clinically, it is feasible to utilize the C2 sagittal-parallel pedicle screw in fixing atlantoaxial instability.
