National Policies for Improving Micronutrient Intake in School Meals: A Global Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Ni Putu Wulan Purnama Sari, Adrino Mazenda, Michael Kemboi, Minh‐Hoang Nguyen, Quan‐Hoang Vuong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsMalnutrition, including overnutrition, undernutrition, and micronutrient deficiency, remains a major global public health challenge, closely linked to food insecurity. Among children, micronutrient deficiencies can impair cognitive development, making effective national policies vital to guide in‐school micronutrient supplementation within school meal programs. This study aimed to assess how national policies on school feeding, nutrition, health, food safety, agriculture, and private sector engagement influence the implementation of micronutrient supplementation in school meal programs across countries.MethodsThe study employed the Bayesian Mindsponge Framework, which combines the Granular Interaction Thinking Theory with Bayesian inference techniques. A dataset comprising responses from 126 government representatives (n = 126) responsible for large‐scale school meal programs across 126 countries was analyzed to estimate the effects of various national policy domains on micronutrient supplementation outcomes.ResultsFindings revealed that school feeding policies have a small but positive effect on the implementation of micronutrient supplementation (β = 0.12, SD = 0.10). In contrast, nutrition policies exhibit a slight negative association (β = −0.12, SD = 0.10). Other policies, including those related to health, food safety, agriculture, and the private sector, demonstrated minimal or negligible influence.ConclusionsThe study underscores the positive contribution of school feeding policies in promoting micronutrient supplementation within school meal programs. However, the negative association of nutrition policies suggests the need for critical policy reassessment to ensure coherence and effectiveness. Strengthening cross‐sectoral linkages, particularly among health, food safety, agriculture, and private sector policies, may further enhance micronutrient intake and improve child health outcomes globally.Keywords: food insecurity, global, micronutrient supplementation, policy analysis, school feeding

Introduction

Globally, there is a pressing need to address the triple burden of malnutrition: undernutrition, micronutrient deficiencies, and overnutrition, especially among school‐aged children [2]. According to WHO, globally, an estimated 149 million children under five are stunted, 45 million wasted, 37 million overweight, and nearly half of the deaths of children under 5 years of age are linked to undernutrition, including micronutrient deficiencies [3]. Specifically, global estimates indicate that of the 372 million children affected by micronutrient deficiencies, 99 million of them live in South Asia, 98 million in sub‐Saharan Africa, and 85 million in East Asia and the Pacific [4]. Nutritional surveys show that recommended micronutrient intakes are not always met among vulnerable groups, like young children, pregnant women, and the elderly [5], with extensive nutrient deficiencies in calcium, vitamins A, B12, folate, and lysine, and more concentrated deficiencies in iron, zinc, and vitamin B6 [6].

In low‐ and middle‐income countries, the three elements of the triple burden of malnutrition can be found simultaneously with poverty, poor dietary choices, and a lack of knowledge about what constitutes the most nourishing foods as the contributing factors to this global burden on public health [2]. Poverty reinforces malnutrition as parents lack the financial ability to provide sufficient nutrition for their children, and malnutrition produces a poverty condition by reducing the economic potential of the population [7]. In addition, poor dietary choices, a lack of knowledge about what constitutes the most nourishing foods, and a healthy diet lead to low diet quality among children, which results in malnutrition [2].

Thus, to avert the negative implications of micronutrient deficiencies, comprehensive policies related to school meal programs encompassing areas of school feeding, nutrition, health, food safety, agriculture, and the private sector have been established at the national level to ensure that children receive adequate nutrition [8]. Basically, school feeding is the provision of food on‐site or take‐home, aiming to increase school enrollment, attendance, and retention, and exists as a social safety net for households with very low income [9]. As part of these national policies' implementation, many countries have conducted school feeding with a substantial aim at improving micronutrient intake among children, thereby addressing the hidden hunger.

In resource‐constrained contexts, nutrition education initiatives mandated by the school feeding policy, coupled with agricultural interventions supported by the agricultural policy, have proven effective in treating vitamin deficiencies and improving both the availability and diversity of food [10]. For example, in India, the use of school‐based double‐fortified salt provided through the midday meal addressed iron deficiency in rural Bihar, leading to an improvement in the anemic status of children [11]. In the Eastern Mediterranean Region, private sector engagement in addressing micronutrient deficiencies is key to the success of food fortification initiatives and the implementation of nutritional policies that promote healthy diets [12]. Conversely, in Kenya, low consumption of fruits and animal products in the lunch program in Kilifi County resulted in low micronutrient intake among children [13]. Similarly, in Japan, nutrient deficiencies were significantly higher on days without school lunches for most macro‐ and micronutrients [14]. These show that school meal programs are not standalone but intersect with national policies to facilitate micronutrient supplementation. For instance, health policies in the form of micronutrient powder intervention not only decrease the prevalence of anemia in young children but also contribute to an increase in diet diversity [15]. This suggests that national policies guiding school meal programs are essential to address low micronutrient intake among school‐aged children. Further, the emphasis of national policies is also on nutrition‐specific interventions through multisectoral involvement and collaboration to address the growing micronutrient deficiencies in children [3, 16].

Given the critical role of national policies in enhancing micronutrient supplementation and their potential to address the hidden hunger among school‐aged children, there is an urgent need for a detailed analysis of the national policies guiding school meal programs. Therefore, this study intends to offer insights into how these interventions could be optimized by examining how national policies impact in‐school micronutrient supplementation. In doing so, this study seeks to contribute to the development of more effective strategies for combating the hidden hunger among children.

Methods

Theoretical Foundation

For this study's conceptualization, model construction, hypothesis formulation, and findings explanation, the Granular Interaction Thinking Theory (GITT) was employed [17]. GITT expands upon the Mindsponge Theory (MT) [18], a mind's information processing theory. GITT incorporates the worldviews and concepts from quantum mechanics and Shannon's information theory to elucidate how macroscopic behaviors in complex systems, including human cognition, arise from the interactions of discrete information units at a microscopic level. As an extension of the MT, GITT preserves its fundamental ability to describe how individuals acquire, process, and internalize information from their surroundings [17]. The granular interactions thinking mechanism is the key point of GITT to propose an entropy‐based notion of value, important for enhancing and strengthening its ability to explain the complexity of human behavior at individual, organizational, and systemic levels.

In this study, a nation is conceptualized as an information collection‐cum‐processor, or a collective mind, where information units interact to form a mindset—core values essential for system survival. Here, GITT views various national policies guiding school meal programs as the nation's information‐filtering system, which determines the presence or absence of micronutrient supplementation practices. This study examines practices of micronutrient supplementation in school meals within school feeding programs as an outcome of national information processes. To implement such practices, two conditions must be met: (1) information about micronutrient supplementation must be available and accessible, including relevant knowledge, manpower, and resources in the specific nation; and (2) the information must pass the nation's multi‐filtering system, which determines its perceived benefits, neutrality, or costs. This study focuses on the second condition by examining the core values within a nation's mindset that influence micronutrient supplementation in school meal programs. Beneficial information about micronutrient supplementation is likely to be adopted, neutral information is stored for future evaluation, and costly information is discarded.

Model Construction

Dataset

This study utilized a dataset of 126 government representatives who managed large‐scale school meal programs in 126 countries. The dataset is about the results of a global survey on school meal programs in 2021, which can be accessed publicly at the Global Child Nutrition Foundation (GCNF) Global Survey of School Meal Programs database [19]. The study setting of the primary survey generating this dataset was global. Based on the data of the GCNF, 142 countries participate in various school meal programs [8]. Thus, the sample size of this study (n = 126) is considered representative and may support the generalizability of the findings because it comprises an 88.73% response rate.

For ethical considerations, there is no specific protocol developed for this study, and no data collection was conducted from our side. Thus, we did not interact with any ethical committee or institutional review board before conducting our study. In terms of the GCNF global survey on school meal programs, generating the dataset, all respondents have provided written consent to uphold the ethical standard, strengthening their voluntary participation. The number of ethical clearance certificates remains confidential, as it was not published in the GCNF database along with the dataset. The GCNF database (https://gcnf.org/global-survey/) is a reliable source of cross‐national data on school meal program evaluations [19], appropriately cited and acknowledged in this study. The GCNF upheld the anonymity and confidentiality, and there was no data on focal point contact information (personal information) published.

Initially, the survey instrument was distributed to all countries participating in diverse school meal programs (N = 142), but by the deadline, there were only 126 government representatives who returned the survey (n = 126). A standardized questionnaire was used in data collection. This instrument was developed by the GCNF. The questionnaire was designed to be completed by a government‐appointed representative, a survey focal point, who is knowledgeable of the Survey of School Meal Programs ©. This survey included 11 sections. Four sections contain national‐level questions, meaning that the respondents only need to complete these sections once for each country. The remaining seven sections contain program‐level questions, meaning that the respondents completed these sections separately for each large‐scale school feeding program in each country. The instrument validation was managed by the GCNF. The content validation involved expert review and opinion, important for question development and validation, and a pre‐testing survey among government representatives in participating countries for instrument validation.

Variable Selection and Rationale

In the current study, we extracted seven variables from the GCNF dataset to be employed in statistical analysis (see Table 1). To measure six types of national policies, we employed variables of SchoolFeedingPolicy, NutritionPolicy, HealthPolicy, FoodSafetyPolicy, AgriculturePolicy, and PrivateSectorPolicy, which reflect the presence of any national laws, policies, or standards related to school feeding practices in the country. The data type is binary, reflecting the presence or absence of any specific policy in a country, with impacts on the number of high‐fidelity filters utilized in the benchmarking process of any information related to micronutrient supplementation practices within the country. For the outcome variable, we employed the variable MicronutrientSupp, which reflects the presence of any nutritional supplements or micronutrient powders included in the school meals program. Data Analysis and Validation

The Bayesian Mindsponge Framework (BMF) analytics, a Bayesian analysis with aided Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms [20], was chosen in the current study for statistical analysis over traditional methods due to several reasons. First, it shows the capabilities of integrating the MT, now GITT, with Bayesian inferential statistics, ensuring logical reasoning and robust statistical modeling. BMF analytics allows us to simulate many possible outcomes from samples, enhancing the accuracy of conclusions compared to traditional methods. Second, it treats all properties probabilistically, allowing reliable predictions and handling complex models drawing on the MCMC technique [21]. Third, it offers credible intervals for interpretation [22]. Our modeling approach follows the standard procedures outlined in the BMF updated protocol. The BMF analytics was built on the R platform using a specific statistical package, the bayesvl [23]. Data and code snippets of this statistical analysis were deposited at https://zenodo.org/uploads/12742823 for transparency and public evaluation.

In Bayesian analysis, selecting the appropriate prior is required during the model construction process. Due to the exploratory nature of this study, uninformative priors or a flat prior distribution were used to provide as little prior information as possible for model estimation [24]. Before further results interpretation, the reliability of the simulated data (or “chains”) needs to be tested. There are three key indicators that we used. First, the Pareto‐smoothed importance sampling leave‐one‐out (PSIS‐LOO) diagnostics were employed to check the models' goodness of fit [25]. This diagnostic method examines how well our model predicts the data based on the k value for each data point. The k‐Pareto values are used in the PSIS method to compute the LOO cross‐validation in the R loo package. Observations with k‐Pareto values greater than 0.7 are often considered influential and problematic for accurately estimating LOO cross‐validation. When a model's k values are less than 0.5, it is typically regarded as being fit [26].

Second, we used Effective Sample Size (n_eff). The n_eff value represents the number of iterative samples that are not autocorrelated during stochastic simulation [27]. This can be considered as the number of independent data points our simulation represents. If n_eff is larger than 1000, it is generally considered that the Markov chains are convergent, and the effective samples are sufficient for reliable inference [28]. It can be considered that our estimates are based on enough “good” samples.

Third, the Gelman‐Rubin Shrink Factor (Rhat) is another key indicator. This statistic tells us if our different simulation runs (chains) have settled to similar values. The Rhat value is referred to as the potential scale reduction factor [29]. If the Rhat value of 1 (Rhat = 1) means the chains have converged and our results are stable. If the value exceeds 1.1, then the model does not converge. In addition, Markov chain convergence can also be assessed through visual approaches, such as trace plots. If the model fits well with the data, and the chains are converged well, we will proceed with the results interpretation.

Discussion

Findings showed that school feeding policies have a moderately reliable positive association with micronutrient supplementation, suggesting that school feeding policy effectively guides and supports the implementation of in‐school micronutrient supplementation as part of the national school meal programs. There are many ways to add these supplements to school meals, such as by integrating nutrient‐rich foods or utilizing multiple micronutrient powders as a form of food fortification [30]. A recent study assessing the relationships between national policies and food fortification practices in school meal programs showed that the school feeding policies had an ambiguous relationship with food fortification, together with private sector policies [31], suggesting a further clarifying study. This study can be considered a clarifying study, as we found a specific role of school feeding policies in supporting micronutrient supplementation of school meals. Our findings indicate that school feeding policies have improved the supplementation of vitamins and minerals in school meals. Findings are also supported by the fact that micronutrient supplementation is widespread, with 25–40% of individuals in developed nations regularly consuming supplements, including children [32].

GITT offers several insights into understanding this positive relationship. It considers school feeding policies as core values deeply embedded in the collective mind of a nation, serving as essential benchmarks for the acceptance or rejection of new information or values related to micronutrient supplementation [17]. It views micronutrient supplementation practices in school meal programs as a product of the nation's information processing system. For these practices to emerge and be sustained within the national framework, the information regarding micronutrient supplementation must first be available and accessible to the nation, including knowledge, technology, human resources, the available budget, etc., to enable a conformity assessment, following regulated national policies. When micronutrient supplementation practices align with these core values after a rigorous benchmarking process—where the school feeding policy acts as a multi‐filtering system—these practices can be effectively implemented and maintained in school meal programs. This alignment results in a positive correlation between school feeding policy and in‐school micronutrient supplementation.

Conversely, this study found that nutrition policies have a moderately reliable negative association with micronutrient supplementation. Findings are supported and consistent with a recent study, which found that nutrition policies had a significant negative association with food fortification practices, including micronutrient supplementation, among countries implementing school meal programs, together with health policies [31]. This can be explained by drawing from the United States government, which mandated the United States Department of Agriculture to limit routine micronutrient supplementation among children due to insufficient scientific evidence, especially for the prevention of chronic diseases since childhood [33]. Findings could also be attributed to potential food wastage in school meal programs, where efforts to meet nutritional demands might result in plate waste due to lower consumption of fortified foods with micronutrients by students [34, 35]. In addition, there are several adverse events and risks of toxicity attributable to micronutrient supplementation based on the evidence review [36].

Through the lens of information‐processing, GITT provides reasoning on this negative association. It views the negative association between nutrition policy and in‐school micronutrient supplementation practices as an unconformity between the new information on micronutrient supplementation and the nutrition policy as the mindset of the nation [17]. Micronutrient supplementation for children may not be a favorable practice from the perspective of nutrition policies. This condition may exist potentially due to the incapabilities of meeting the following condition: The information regarding micronutrient supplementation is justified as costly for the nation based on the lens of nutrition policy, so it cannot pass through the multi‐filtering system. The lack of scientific evidence related to micronutrient supplementation for children may result in this negative association.

In addition, this study also found that other policies show unclear relationships with micronutrient supplementation, such as policies on health, food safety, agriculture, and the private sector. These policies require further assessment to determine their roles in micronutrient supplementation of school meals. Most countries face challenges in coordinating several micronutrient deficiency control interventions, including national policy interventions, risking both deficiencies and potential toxicity, especially for trace mineral imbalances [37]. Evidence shows that agricultural policies fail to guide agricultural interventions in providing sufficient ascorbic acid, meat, poultry, and seafood, which improve nonheme iron absorption [38]. Although some countries are revising agricultural interventions, they still lack detailed strategies for nutritional security [39]. For health and food safety policies, challenges remain in various aspects: unhealthy diets may cause non‐communicable diseases, and multiple micronutrient deficiencies are common among certain populations, raising challenges for the implementation of health and food safety policies impacting healthcare costs [40]. For private sector policies, companies tend to prioritize financial gains, making it difficult to promote products that do not align with dietary guidelines, which may not be suitable for all populations and are not profitable [41]. This engagement leads to the unclear roles of private sector policies in influencing micronutrient supplementation of school meals.

GITT views this ambiguous association as a result of the nation's information processing [17]. When information on micronutrient supplementation practices interacts with the national mindset, particularly policies on health, food safety, agriculture, and the private sectors, it is perceived as neutral. Consequently, this information is kept in a buffer zone and is awaiting further evaluation. This situation may arise from insufficient information on micronutrient supplementation practices in school meal programs, including details about opportunities and challenges in relation to these policies.

Conclusions

School feeding policies guiding school meal programs are positively associated with in‐school micronutrient supplementation. These policies effectively guide, support, and protect the implementation of micronutrient supplementation in school meals among implementing countries. Conversely, nutrition policies are negatively associated with micronutrient supplementation, suggesting a need for reassessment and enhancement of nutrition policies to identify problematic clauses and make positive adjustments that can support these practices during school feeding. Further clarifying studies are needed to analyze the roles of policies on health, food safety, agriculture, and the private sectors, as this study found that they have unclear relationships with micronutrient supplementation. Evaluating, enhancing, and strengthening these policies could increase their impacts on micronutrient supplementation practices in school meal programs.

Limitations

This study is not without limitations. Its cross‐sectional design and reliance on secondary data, while validated by prior studies and aligned with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines, means it cannot establish a causal relationship between variables. This study may reflect a pattern of events, but it may not show the dynamic changes in the field situation. The questionnaire used is a self‐reported questionnaire by design. It might be less objective for measuring variables. A qualitative study employing in‐depth interviews among policymakers and stakeholders or parties involved in school meal programs' execution is needed to fully understand the impacts of various national policies on in‐school micronutrient supplementation practices. Moreover, research using more objective measurements of the policy implementation and micronutrient supplementation is also recommended to further validate our findings.
