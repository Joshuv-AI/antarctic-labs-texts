Tiki2 restrains Wnt5a/Ror signaling to limit inflammation in acute kidney injury
Authors: Cui Yang, Shujie Wang, Mei Zhang, Bryan T MacDonald, Zheng Wang, Xi He, Rui Zeng, Xinjun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIschemia/reperfusion (IR)-induced acute kidney injury (AKI) occurs in a range of clinical settings and is characterized by pronounced inflammation, but the underlying regulatory mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Tiki2 is a Wnt inhibitor that is highly expressed in the kidney and its function has not been defined. Here, we show that Tiki2 is enriched in proximal tubular epithelial cells (PTECs) and is dynamically regulated during IR-induced AKI. Tubule-specific ablation of Tiki2 exacerbates immune cell infiltration and tubular injury following IR. Mechanistically, loss of Tiki2 leads to increased activation of Wnt5a/Ror signaling, which is induced in PTECs after IR injury, resulting in increased JNK and ERK activation and elevated expression of chemokines and cytokines that promote the infiltration of immune cells. Notably, the aggravated injury and inflammation caused by Tiki2 deficiency are reversed by Ror2 knockdown in PTECs. Together, these findings identify Tiki2 as a negative regulator of Wnt5a/Ror signaling in renal tubular cells and highlight the Tiki2-Wnt5a/Ror axis as a potential therapeutic target in IR-induced AKI. Subject terms: Acute kidney injury, Morphogen signalling

Introduction

Ischemia/reperfusion (IR) is a common cause of acute kidney injury (AKI) in clinical settings and has profound effects on patient prognosis and long-term outcomes1. Renal tubular epithelial cells are particularly susceptible to IR-induced damage, and tubular cells, together with other heterogeneous cell populations, collectively shape the progression and outcome of AKI2,3. IR initially injures tubular epithelial cells through multiple pathogenic insults, triggering immune responses and the recruitment of leukocytes to the kidney. These processes can facilitate debris clearance and tissue repair but may also exacerbate injury and promote late-stage interstitial fibrosis. Although the mechanisms governing renal injury and repair remain incompletely understood, immune cells clearly play essential roles in AKI pathogenesis2,4–7. Notably, a substantial proportion of patients progress to chronic kidney disease (CKD) or end-stage renal disease (ESRD), ultimately requiring dialysis or kidney transplantation8–12. Persistent injury, excessive inflammation, and incomplete repair are thought to be key contributors to these adverse outcomes2,4. Thus, a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying kidney injury is critical for the development of effective therapeutic strategies.

Wnt5a is a noncanonical Wnt ligand that activates signaling pathways through Frizzled receptors and coreceptors, including Ror1 and Ror2, leading to the activation of the JNK, ERK, and p38 MAPK signaling cascades13–20. Notably, Wnt5a/ERK signaling has been shown to induce Ccl2 expression in rheumatoid arthritis21,22, cardiovascular disease, and various cancers23–25. Additionally, Wnt5a/JNK signaling is activated in metabolic disorders, including obesity, type 2 diabetes, and diabetic nephropathy26–29. Collectively, these studies suggest that Wnt5a regulates the production of chemokines and proinflammatory cytokines, modulates immune cell migration and recruitment, and manipulates inflammatory responses. Previous studies have demonstrated that Wnt5a/Ror2 signaling is activated in the UUO model, promoting macrophage polarization and kidney fibrosis30,31. However, the role of Wnt5a/Ror signaling and its regulatory mechanisms in AKI remain poorly defined.

Tiki was originally identified as a membrane-tethered metalloprotease that cleaves the amino terminus of specific Wnt proteins, thereby inactivating them32,33. Our recent studies characterized Tiki proteins as glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored proteins (GPI-APs), which are enriched in detergent-resistant membrane microdomains at the plasma membrane and interact with the Wnt/FZD complex to inhibit coreceptor activation34,35. Notably, our recent study suggested that Tiki proteins specifically bind to the Wnt5a/FZD complex on the cell surface, hydrolyze the amino terminus of Wnt5a in the presence of FZD, and prevent the Wnt5a/FZD complex from binding to and activating coreceptors ROR1/2 in an in vitro cell culture system35. However, whether this regulatory mechanism operates in vivo, particularly in mammalian systems, remains unknown. Tiki proteins are evolutionarily conserved, with two orthologs, Tiki1 and Tiki2, present in most vertebrates. However, rodents harbor only Tiki2, and the Tiki1 gene may have been lost during evolution36. Mouse Tiki2 is also known as Hkat (heart, kidney, and adipose-enriched transmembrane protein) because of its high expression in these tissues37. Despite this expression pattern, the in vivo function of Tiki2 in mammals and its relevance to human disease have not been explored.

In this study, we show that Tiki2 is highly expressed in renal tubular epithelial cells in both human and mouse kidneys and is dynamically regulated during AKI. Using a proximal tubular epithelial cell-specific Tiki2 knockout mouse model, we demonstrated that loss of Tiki2 exacerbates inflammation and injury in IR-induced AKI. Mechanistically, Tiki2 suppresses Wnt5a/Ror signaling, which is activated in injured tubular epithelial cells, thereby limiting inflammatory responses.

Results

Tiki2 is highly expressed in renal tubular epithelial cells and is downregulated by kidney injury

Mouse Tiki2 is known to be highly expressed in the kidney37, yet its physiological function remains unclear. To investigate the role of Tiki2 in mammals, particularly in the kidney, we generated a global Tiki2 knockout mouse line via ES cell–based homologous recombination. The first two exons of Tiki2 were replaced with a bacterial LacZ cassette, resulting in complete loss of Tiki2 expression and enabling the Tiki2 promoter to drive LacZ expression (Supplementary Fig. 1A–C). X-gal staining of kidney sections from adult heterozygous mice (Tiki2+/-) revealed strong expression of Tiki2 in cortical proximal tubules (Fig. 1A), which was further confirmed by colocalization of Lotus tetragonolobus lectin (LTL) with X-gal staining (Supplementary Fig. 1D). Single-cell analysis of both mouse (CRA017133) and human kidneys (GSE118184) further revealed that Tiki2 is broadly expressed in PTECs, the distal convoluted tubule (DCT), and the loop of Henle (LH) in kidneys (Fig. 1B, C). Tiki2 homozygous knockout mice (Tiki2-/-) were partially perinatal lethal (Supplementary Fig. 1E), and the surviving pups could grow with normal kidney morphologies (Supplementary Fig. 1F) and similar serum creatinine levels as control mice did (Supplementary Fig. 1G), although their blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels were higher than those of WT mice (Supplementary Fig. 1H), suggesting possible functional renal defects in KO mice. Given that renal proximal tubular cells are most fragile and vulnerable to damage, we examined the regulation of Tiki2 expression in different kidney injury models. As shown in Fig. 1D-F, both ischemia/reperfusion and cisplatin administration dramatically decreased Tiki2 expression in renal tubular cells, suggesting that Tiki2 may function in kidney injuries and related pathological processes.

Tubular-specific Tiki2 knockout exacerbates IR-induced AKI

To investigate the specific role of Tiki2 in renal tubular epithelial cells, we generated a conditional Tiki2 knockout mouse line by introducing two loxP sites flanking exon 4 of the Tiki2 gene (Tiki2 fl/+) (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Proximal tubule-specific Tiki2 knockout mice (Tiki2 fl/fl; γGT-Cre, hereafter referred to as Tiki2PTKO) were obtained by crossing Tiki2 fl/+ mice with γGT-Cre transgenic mice (Supplementary Fig. 2B). To verify the specificity and activity of γGT-Cre, we crossed γGT-Cre transgenic mice with Rosa26-tdTomato reporter mice. Immunofluorescence analyses of kidney sections revealed the colocalization of red fluorescence with Lrp2, a proximal tubule-specific marker (Supplementary Fig. 2C), confirming that γGT-Cre is specifically expressed and active in proximal tubular epithelial cells. Successful gene ablation in proximal tubules was further validated by RT-qPCR (Supplementary Fig. 2D). Compared with littermate controls (Tiki2 fl/fl), Tiki2PTKO mice presented no overt phenotypes, and their kidney morphology and serum BUN levels were normal (Supplementary Fig. 2E, F), indicating that Tiki2 is dispensable for basal kidney function under physiological conditions.

Next, we examined the function of Tiki2 in an IR-induced AKI mouse model. Sex- and age-matched Ctrl (Tiki2 fl/fl) and Tiki2PTKO mice underwent 30 min of unilateral kidney ischemia/reperfusion. Injured kidneys were harvested on days 1, 3, and 7 post-reperfusion. RT-qPCR analysis revealed that Tiki2 mRNA expression was nearly completely lost on day 1 and partially recovered on days 3 and 7 compared with that in the sham-operated kidneys (Fig. 2A). Analysis of the tubular injury marker Kim-1 and the macrophage marker F4/80 revealed no significant differences between the Ctrl and Tiki2PTKO groups on days 1 or 3, but their expression was significantly greater in Tiki2PTKO kidneys than in control kidneys on day 7 after reperfusion (Fig. 2B, C). Renal tubular injury can be morphologically assessed by cellular necrosis, tubular dilation and atrophy, epithelial flattening, cast formation, brush border loss, and thickening of the tubular basement membrane38. PAS staining of injured kidney sections revealed representative alterations in both the Ctrl and Tiki2PTKO groups (Fig. 2D). Semiquantitative injury scoring indicated that Tiki2 deficiency had a minimal effect on tubular injury on days 1 (Figs. 2E) and 3 (Fig. 2F) but significantly exacerbated injury on day 7 (Fig. 2G). Immunostaining for Kim-1 and subsequent semiquantitative analysis of Kim-1+ areas confirmed a marked increase in tubular injury in Tiki2PTKO kidneys compared with control kidneys (Fig. 2H–I). These findings indicate that proximal tubule-specific ablation of Tiki2 aggravates IR-induced AKI.

Since renal tubular cell death is a hallmark of AKI progression, we assessed the impact of Tiki2 deletion on tubular cell death. Immunoblotting and quantitative analyses revealed that neither the GSDMD-N/full-length GSDMD ratio nor the Bax and Bcl2 protein levels were substantially altered by Tiki2 ablation after IR. Although the NLRP3 protein level was slightly elevated, these results suggest that Tiki2 deletion has a minimal effect on IR-induced tubular cell pyroptosis or apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 3A–E). TUNEL staining of renal tissue sections further confirmed these observations, with DNA fragmentation identified as a feature of both pyroptosis and apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 3F, G). Tiki2 ablation modestly but significantly increased Acsl4 protein levels after IR (Supplementary Fig. 3A, H), whereas Gpx4 activity remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 3I), indicating a limited impact on IR-induced ferroptosis.

Tubular-specific Tiki2 knockout increases immune cell infiltration in IR-induced AKI

AKI involves a series of pathophysiological processes, including robust inflammatory responses. To determine whether the aggravated renal injury observed in Tiki2PTKO mice was associated with increased inflammatory cell infiltration, we performed flow cytometry analysis of leukocytes in injured kidneys on day 7 post-IR (Supplementary Fig. 4A). The results revealed a significant increase in CD45+ leukocytes in Tiki2PTKO kidneys compared with those in control kidneys (Supplementary Fig. 4B, C). We further analyzed myeloid cells (CD45+ Cd11b+), neutrophils (Cd11b+ Ly6g+), macrophages (Cd11b+ F4/80+) (Fig. 3A, B, Supplementary Fig. 4D, F), and M2 macrophage subpopulations via CD206 expression (Fig. 3C, D, Supplementary Fig. 4E, F). The numbers of myeloid cells, neutrophils, and macrophages were significantly greater in Tiki2PTKO mice than in control mice, and the numbers of M2 macrophage subpopulations were also greater in Tiki2PTKO mice than in control mice. Immunohistochemical staining and semiquantitative analysis of Cd11b+, F4/80+, and Ly6g+ cells (Fig. 3E, F) confirmed that tubulointerstitial infiltration of myeloid cells, macrophages, and neutrophils was markedly increased in Tiki2PTKO kidneys. Consistent with these findings, the mRNA expression levels of CD45, F4/80, and Ly6g were significantly greater in Tiki2PTKO kidneys than in control kidneys (Supplementary Fig. 4G). In addition, flow cytometry analysis of lymphoid populations revealed significant increases in B cells (CD45+ CD19+), helper T cells (CD45+ CD4+), and cytotoxic T cells (CD45+ CD8+) in Tiki2PTKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 5A–F). RT-qPCR analysis of Cd3 mRNA further confirmed that T cells were elevated in Tiki2PTKO mouse kidneys (Supplementary Fig. 5G). Collectively, these results indicate that Tiki2 deficiency enhances both innate and adaptive immune cell infiltration, thereby exacerbating inflammation in IR-induced AKI.

Tiki2 knockout increases the secretory phenotype of injured tubules and aggravates renal interstitial immune cell infiltration via cytokine‒cytokine receptor interaction pathways

To investigate the mechanisms by which Tiki2 deficiency exacerbates renal injury and immune cell infiltration, we performed RNA sequencing of injured kidneys from Ctrl and Tiki2PTKO mice. KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the most enriched Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs) were involved in cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, TNF signaling, and viral protein interactions with cytokines and their receptors (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. 6A, B), which was further confirmed by Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) (Fig. 4B, C, Supplementary Fig. 6C). The heatmap of DEGs revealed that most of the genes whose expression was upregulated in Tiki2PTKO kidneys were associated with heightened immune responses and aggravated renal injury (Fig. 4D), including Cd8a and Cd209a, which are markers of cytotoxic T cells and M2 macrophages, respectively. RT-qPCR confirmed the increased expression of the chemokines Ccl2, Cxcl1, Cxcl2, Cxcl5, and Cxcl10, as well as the chemokine receptors Ccr1, Ccr2, Cxcr2, and Cxcr4 in Tiki2PTKO kidneys (Fig. 4E, F). Furthermore, the transcription factors Fos and Junb, which are involved in the TNF signaling pathway, were significantly upregulated in Tiki2PTKO kidneys (Fig. 4G), as well as the inflammatory factors IL-33, Arg-1, Vcam-1, and Il-1β (Fig. 4H). In addition to these pathways, genes within the Spp1/Cd44 axis were markedly upregulated (Supplementary Fig. 6D), potentially contributing to exacerbated renal injury in Tiki2PTKO mice. Lmnb1 expression was increased after IR injury, whereas the canonical senescence markers p16 and p21 remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 6E). Given that Lmnb1 is typically reduced at the protein level in senescent cells, the increased Lmnb1 mRNA observed here does not provide sufficient evidence for cellular senescence and may instead reflect nuclear lamina remodeling or cell-cycle–related changes following injury39,40. Damaged TECs can shift from a reabsorptive to a pro-inflammatory secretory phenotype, releasing cytokines and chemokines that recruit and activate surrounding inflammatory cells, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts, thus contributing to AKI progression41,42. In agreement with these findings, single-cell RNA sequencing analysis of Tiki2-positive TECs revealed elevated mRNA levels of Ccl2, Cxcl1, Cxcl10, and Ccl5 in the kidneys of the IR-treated mice relative to those of the control mice (Fig. 4I). Together, these results suggest that Tiki2 ablation enhances the secretory phenotype of injured TECs, promoting immune cell recruitment and amplifying inflammation.

Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathways play key roles in injury-induced immune cell infiltration and inflammation, particularly the CCL2-CCR2 axis for macrophages and the CXCL2-CXCR2 axis for neutrophils43,44, both of which are more active in Tiki2PTKO kidneys than in control kidneys, according to RNA-seq data (Fig. 4). We focused on these two pathways for further validation. Immunohistochemistry and quantification revealed that Ccl2 and Cxcl2 were expressed primarily in TECs, with significantly higher levels in Tiki2PTKO kidneys than in control kidneys (Fig. 5A, B). Flow cytometry further revealed increased numbers of F4/80+ Ccr2+ macrophages and Ly6g+ Cxcr2+ neutrophils in Tiki2PTKO kidneys than in control kidneys (Fig. 5C–F). These findings indicate that Tiki2 deficiency enhances cytokine-cytokine receptor signaling, thereby promoting renal interstitial infiltration of macrophages and neutrophils.

Ablation of Tiki2 increases Wnt5a/Ror signaling activity in IR-induced AKI

Numerous studies have demonstrated that Wnt5a/Ror signaling activates proinflammatory pathways across multiple biological systems. To determine whether this pathway is similarly activated in IR-induced AKI, we analyzed injured kidneys via RT-qPCR and immunoblotting. The results revealed rapid upregulation of Wnt5a, Ror1/Ror2, and their downstream target gene Mmp2 after IR (Fig. 6A, B), indicating that Wnt5a/Ror signaling is activated in this model. Immunohistochemistry confirmed that the Ror2 and Mmp2 proteins were predominantly localized to damaged renal tubules (Fig. 6C). Furthermore, Mmp2 expression was significantly greater in Tiki2PTKO kidneys than in control kidneys after IR (Fig. 6D, E), suggesting that tubular-specific Tiki2 ablation enhances Wnt5a/Ror signaling activity.

Previous studies have reported that Wnt5a promotes proinflammatory responses by inducing JNK and ERK phosphorylation in a β-catenin-independent manner18,24. Subsequent immunohistochemical staining of kidney sections revealed that Wnt5a, p-JNK, p-c-Jun, and p-ERK were colocalized within injured tubules (Fig. 6F), supporting the activation of JNK and ERK signaling in damaged renal epithelial cells. Notably, the levels of p-JNK and p-ERK were markedly greater in Tiki2PTKO kidneys than in Ctrl kidneys (Fig. 6G–I, Supplementary Fig. 6F), which was consistent with the enriched gene ontology (GO) terms related to ERK1/2 cascades observed in the RNA-seq data (Supplementary Fig. 6B). These findings indicate that tubular-specific Tiki2 deletion enhances Wnt5a/Ror signaling and its downstream JNK/ERK pathway.

In addition to affecting JNK and ERK, Wnt5a may influence inflammation through the NF-κB pathway, as immunoblotting revealed significantly increased phospho-NF-κB P65 levels in Tiki2-deficient kidneys (Supplementary Fig. 7A, B). Immunofluorescence analysis further revealed that phospho-NF-κB P65 signals were concentrated mainly in the renal interstitium (Supplementary Fig. 7C), which was correlated with elevated IL-6 and TNF-α expression (Supplementary Fig. 7D). Unlike the tubule-restricted activation of JNK and ERK, the increase in NF-κB signaling in the Tiki2PTKO renal interstitium may reflect a secondary effect, potentially exacerbating interstitial inflammation and further contributing to renal epithelial injury.

Tiki2 regulates chemokine production by inhibiting Wnt5a/Ror-regulated JNK/ERK signaling

To further elucidate the role of Tiki2 in modulating Wnt5a/Ror signaling in renal tubular cells, PTECs were isolated from WT and Tiki2-/- mice and analyzed for Wnt5a and Ror1/2 expression by immunoblotting. Unlike PTECs in vivo, which exhibit minimal Wnt5a or Ror1/2 expression, in vitro cultured PTECs expressed relatively high levels of Wnt5a and Ror1/2. We observed a pronounced band shift of Ror1 in Tiki2 KO samples relative to WT samples (Fig. 7A), indicating that Tiki2 deletion increases Ror1 phosphorylation. Wnt5a appeared as two bands in WT PTECs, whereas only the upper band was detectable in Tiki2 KO PTECs (Fig. 7A). Similar results were obtained in Tiki2-ablated PTECs treated with 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT) in the Tiki2 fl/fl; Rosa26CreERT2 background (Fig. 7B). Our previous work demonstrated that Tiki proteins hydrolyze the amino terminus of Wnt5a to generate a truncated form35. Collectively, these data suggest that endogenous Tiki2 hydrolyzes the N-terminus of Wnt5a, thereby inhibiting Wnt5a/Ror signaling in PTECs.

We next examined the effects of Tiki2 on Wnt5a/Ror signaling in HK-2 cells, a human renal tubular epithelial cell line. Exogenous Tiki2 expression suppressed both basal and Wnt5a-induced Ror1 phosphorylation (Fig. 7C). Furthermore, Tiki2 inhibited the Wnt5a-stimulated phosphorylation of ERK1/2, JNK, and c-JUN (Fig. 7C–F). Consistently, RT-qPCR analysis revealed that Wnt5a treatment significantly upregulated the expression of the chemokine genes Ccl2, Cxcl1 and Cxcl2, and this induction was markedly attenuated by Tiki2 expression (Fig. 7G–I). These results indicate that Tiki2 inhibits Wnt5a/Ror signaling and its downstream proinflammatory pathways in human kidney tubular epithelial cells.

Tubular-specific knockdown of Ror2 alleviates IR-induced kidney injury and the inflammatory response in Tiki2PTKO mice

To further confirm that tubular-specific ablation of Tiki2 exacerbates IR-induced AKI via enhanced Wnt5a/Ror signaling, we generated Tiki2 fl/fl; Ror2 fl/+; γGT-Cre mice (Tiki2PTKO; Ror2PTKD), in which one allele of Ror2 was selectively deleted in PTECs on the background of Tiki2 knockout (Supplementary Fig. 8A, B). These mice, along with Ctrl and Tiki2PTKO mice, were subjected to IR-induced AKI. PAS staining and semiquantitative kidney injury scores demonstrated that tubular injury was attenuated in Tiki2PTKO; Ror2PTKD kidneys compared with Tiki2PTKO kidneys (Fig. 8A, B). Immunohistochemical analysis and quantification revealed that the levels of Kim-1 (Fig. 8A, C), F4/80 (Fig. 8D, E), and Ly6g (Fig. 8D, F) were significantly lower in the Tiki2PTKO; Ror2PTKD group than in the Tiki2PTKO group. Similarly, tubular Ccl2 (Supplementary Fig. 8C, D) and Cxcl2 (Supplementary Fig. 8C, E) expression was lower in Tiki2PTKO; Ror2PTKD kidneys than in Tiki2PTKO kidneys. Collectively, these results indicate that Ror2 knockdown effectively mitigates the increased kidney injury and immune cell infiltration observed in Tiki2PTKO mice. Mechanistically, Ror2 knockdown also decreased the phosphorylation of JNK, c-Jun, and ERK, as shown by immunohistochemistry and immunoblotting of injured kidneys from the Ctrl, Tiki2PTKO, and Tiki2PTKO; Ror2PTKD groups (Fig. 8G–I). Taken together, these findings further support that tubular-specific ablation of Tiki2 exacerbates IR-induced renal injury and associated inflammation through derepression of Wnt5a/Ror signaling.

Ablation of Tiki2 increases Wnt/β-catenin signaling in IR-induced AKI

Previous studies have shown that Wnt/β-catenin signaling is activated and plays crucial roles in IR-induced AKI45,46, and our earlier work indicated that Tiki proteins can inhibit both the Wnt5a/Ror pathway and the Wnt/β-catenin pathway35. To determine whether Tiki2 ablation affects Wnt/β-catenin signaling during IR-induced AKI, we analyzed active β-catenin protein levels and downstream target gene expression. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 9A, B, Tiki2 knockout led to an increase in active β-catenin levels. Consistently, RT-qPCR analysis revealed that Tiki2 deficiency moderately upregulated the expression of Wnt/β-catenin target genes, including Mmp7, Axin2, and PAI-1 (Supplementary Fig. 9C). These findings indicate that Tiki2 ablation enhances Wnt/β-catenin signaling activity in IR-induced AKI.

Discussion

We previously identified the Tiki family of Wnt-specific proteases and revealed their critical roles in vertebrate body axis development via Xenopus embryos32. However, their functions in vivo in mammals have not been determined. In the present study, we investigated the role of Tiki2 in IR-induced AKI by using a Tiki2 proximal tubular-specific knockout mouse model. We have made the following discoveries: (1) Tiki2 is highly expressed in renal PTECs, and its expression is rapidly downregulated and partially recovers during IR-induced AKI; (2) tubular-specific ablation of Tiki2 exacerbates immune cell infiltration and the inflammatory response in IR-induced AKI; (3) ablation of Tiki2 promotes the pro-inflammatory secretory phenotype of damaged TECs; and (4) ablation of Tiki2 increases the activity of Wnt5a/Ror signaling, which is activated in AKI.

Our results suggest that Tiki2 may function predominantly during the recovery stage of IR-induced AKI. Tiki2 expression is largely downregulated during the first 24 h after IR, during which major PTEC death is accompanied by the highest Kim-1 expression. Tiki2 expression is restored from day 3 after IR. Accordingly, ablation of Tiki2 had little effect on IR-induced AKI on day 1 or day 3 after IR but significantly increased injury on day 7. Previous studies have suggested that surviving PTECs undergo a series of cell fate changes to repair damage or lead to fibrosis in the case of severe damage, but the detailed process and underlying mechanisms remain elusive41,47. Further investigations of Tiki2 regulation and Tiki2-expressing cell types during AKI will shed light on these key questions.

Tiki2 may play critical roles in immune cell infiltration and inflammation in IR-induced AKI, which are the typical characteristics of AKI following tubular epithelial cell death2,5. Our results indicate that ablation of Tiki2 significantly exacerbates the infiltration of immune cells, including macrophages, neutrophils, and cytotoxic T cells. Increased inflammation may aggravate tubular injury in Tiki2PTKO mice. Although increased Lmnb1 mRNA was detected in injured kidneys, Lmnb1 is typically considered a senescence-associated marker based on its protein-level reduction in senescent cells48,49. Therefore, the transcriptional change observed here should not be interpreted as evidence of bona fide tubular epithelial cell senescence. Tubular damage may change the secretory phenotypes of surviving TECs, which produce proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines to recruit and activate immune cells. RNA sequencing analysis revealed that ablation of Tiki2 significantly increased the expression of a series of cytokines and chemokines, including Ccl2 and Cxcl2, which are produced mainly by tubular epithelial cells, in injured kidneys, as confirmed by single-cell sequencing analysis and immunohistochemical staining of kidney sections. Ccl2 or Cxcl2 typically acts as a chemokine for macrophages or neutrophils, respectively, by interacting with the receptor Ccr2 or Cxcr2 on targeted immune cells43,44. Flow cytometry analysis confirmed that Tiki2 ablation increased the infiltration of Ccr2+ macrophages and Cxcr2+ neutrophils in injured kidneys. Inflammation can be beneficial or detrimental to AKI, depending on the degree of damage and the types of immune cells involved5,50,51. Although our study indicates that Tiki2 ablation increases kidney injury in 30-min IR-induced AKI, the exact function of Tiki2 in other AKI conditions, especially in human AKI patients, and in AKI-related chronic kidney diseases such as fibrosis, remains to be comprehensively investigated.

Our previous study suggested that Tiki2 functions as a GPI-anchored protease that interacts with the Wnt/FZD complex on the plasma membrane to inhibit Wnt signaling34,35. Given that Tiki2 ablation affects mainly chemokine production in injured renal epithelial cells and that Wnt5a/Ror signaling has been reported to regulate inflammatory signaling in many other biological systems22,27,52, we hypothesized that Tiki2 may inhibit Wnt5a/Ror signaling in IR-induced AKI. Our results indicate that the expression of Wnt5a, Ror1/2, and the typical target gene Mmp2 is increased in tubular epithelial cells in IR-induced AKI. Furthermore, the Wnt5a/Ror downstream inflammatory signaling pathways JNK and ERK are also activated in damaged tubules in IR-induced AKI. We previously reported that Tiki2, a Wnt-specific protease, cleaves the amino terminus of Wnt5a and inhibits Wnt5a-induced Ror1/2 phosphorylation32. Here, we showed that endogenous Tiki2 cleaves Wnt5a and inhibits Ror1 phosphorylation in PTECs and that ablation of Tiki2 increases the activity of the Wnt5a/Ror downstream JNK and ERK signaling pathways. Numerous studies have focused on canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling in AKI and revealed its function in renal epithelium regeneration and renal fibrosis45,46, whereas the roles of noncanonical Wnt5a/Ror signaling in AKI are less well characterized. Our study reveals the critical function of Wnt5a/Ror signaling in AKI-related inflammation and its regulation by Tiki2.

Although the RNA sequencing results did not suggest a strong correlation between Tiki2 knockout and the activity of Wnt/β-catenin signaling, our results suggest that ablation of Tiki2 caused a moderate increase in Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Notably, Tiki2 may not hydrolyze Wnt2, Wnt7b, or Wnt10b, which are reported to activate β-catenin signaling and are induced in AKI according to RNA-Seq analysis, hampering the detailed investigation of Tiki2 in regulating Wnt/β-catenin signaling in AKI.

Several studies have suggested that crosstalk between Wnt5a and TGF-β signaling plays critical roles in both tissue injury repair and fibrogenesis53–55. Given that Tiki2 deficiency enhances Wnt5a signaling in AKI, the regulatory effects of Tiki2 on the Wnt5a/TGF-β signaling cascade and their underlying roles in the AKI-to-CKD transition and progression are worthy of further elucidation.

In summary, our study reveals the critical role of Tiki2 in the inflammatory response in IR-induced AKI and reveals the activation and regulation of noncanonical Wnt5a/Ror signaling in IR-induced AKI. We propose the following model: in the first phase of IR-induced AKI, Tiki2 expression is downregulated, and Wnt5a/Ror1/2 expression is induced to activate the JNK- and ERK-related inflammatory pathways and promote the inflammatory response; in the second phase of IRI, Tiki2 expression is restored to inhibit Wnt5a/Ror signaling and control the strength of inflammation (Graphical Abstract). Our study suggests that Wnt5a/Ror/Tiki2 may be a potential therapeutic target for treating AKI-related inflammation.

Methods

Mouse strains

Tiki2 global knockout mice were generated via a strategy similar to that used for Axin2 knockout mice56. Briefly, a targeting vector in which the first two exons of Tiki2 were replaced with a β-galactosidase (LacZ) and a neo cassette (flanked by loxP sites) was electroporated into 129P2/OlaHsd-derived E14 embryonic stem (ES) cells. Correctly targeted ES cells were injected into blastocysts to produce chimeric mice, which were then bred with C57BL/6N mice to establish mutant colonies. The neo cassette was removed by crossing with CAG-Cre transgenic mice, and the line was backcrossed over 10 generations to C57BL/6N to standardize the genetic background.

Tiki2 floxed (fl/fl) mice were generated via a conditional targeting construct from the Knock-Out Mouse Project (KOMP). This construct allows the generation of a null allele via an exon trap or a conditional allele by flanking an exon with loxP sites. The construct was targeted to C57BL/6 ES cells and injected into albino B6 mice to produce chimeras, which were subsequently bred to achieve germline transmission. To generate Tiki2 fl/fl mice with loxP sites surrounding exon 4, the KOMP allele was crossed with ROSA26-FLPeR mice expressing FLP recombinase from the ROSA26 locus57.

All the mice were housed and handled according to protocols approved by the Medicine Animal Studies Committee of Huazhong University of Science and Technology with IACUC No. 4140. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. The transgenic lines γGT-Cre58, Rosa26-CreERT259, Td-tomato reporter60, and Ror2 fl/fl61 were obtained from Jackson Laboratory. The primers used for genotyping all the strains are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

X-gal staining

Kidneys from littermates and heterozygous transgenic mice were harvested and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Frozen kidneys were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound and sectioned at a thickness of 10 μm. The sections were fixed in 4% PFA for 10 min at 4 °C and then incubated overnight at 37 °C in X-gal staining solution (1 mg/ml X-Gal, 0.01% NP-40, 0.01% sodium deoxycholate, 5 mM potassium ferricyanide, 5 mM potassium ferrocyanide, and 2 mM MgCl₂ in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.5). After staining, the sections were postfixed in 4% PFA and counterstained with Nuclear Fast Red.

For embryonic kidneys, tissues were dissected and fixed in 0.2% glutaraldehyde for 2 h at 4 °C, stained overnight at 37 °C in the same X-gal staining solution, postfixed, dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and counterstained with Nuclear Fast Red.

Ischemia‒reperfusion injury model

Tiki2 fl/fl and Tiki2PTKO littermates (8–12 weeks old) underwent unilateral IRI or sham surgery. The mice were anesthetized, and their body temperature was maintained at 37 ± 0.2 °C. The left renal vessels were exposed and clamped with a vascular clamp (RS-5420, ROBOZ, Germany) for 30 min. After clamp release, the kidney was returned to the retroperitoneum. Successful ischemia and reperfusion were visually confirmed in all the mice. Kidneys were harvested on days 1, 3, and 7 post-reperfusion.

Histopathological analysis

Kidneys were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24–48 h, processed, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 4 μm. Sections were stained with periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) and mounted. Images were acquired via a SOPTOP EX20 microscope (Sunnyoptical, China), which captured 10 high-power fields (200×) from the corticomedullary junction and cortex per kidney. Tubular injury was defined as tubular dilation/atrophy, cast formation, vacuolization, degeneration, epithelial sloughing, brush border loss, or a thickened tubular basement membrane38. Histological damage was scored semiquantitatively (0–5) by a blinded pathologist: 0 = none, 1 ≤ 10%, 2 ≤ 25%, 3 ≤ 50%, 4 ≤ 75%, and 5 > 75%.

Immunoblotting

Tissues were homogenized in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 1% sodium deoxycholate, and 1 mM EDTA) supplemented with PMSF (ST2573, Beyotime, China), a protease inhibitor cocktail (P1005, Beyotime, China) and a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (P1046, Beyotime, China). The lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min and then centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, after which the supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were measured via a BCA assay (P0012, Beyotime, China). The required amount of protein was denatured with 3× loading buffer (180 mM Tris pH 6.8, 6% SDS, 30% glycerol, and 0.03% bromophenol blue) for 10 min at 95°C.

The samples were loaded on SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes for immunoblot analysis. The primary antibodies used included GAPDH (1:100000, 60004, Proteintech, China), β-Tubulin (1:5000, 10094, Proteintech, China), β-actin (1:10000, AC026, ABclonal, China), Wnt5a (1:1000, 16542, CST, USA), Ror1 (1:1000, 16540, CST, USA), Ror2 (1:1000, 88639, CST, USA), Mmp2 (1:500, sc-13595, Santa Cruz, USA), ERK1/2 (1:1500, ET1601-29, HUABIO, China), p-ERK1/2 (1:1000, ET1610-13, HUABIO, China), JNK (1:1500, ET1601-28, HUABIO, China), p-JNK (1:1000, ET1609-42, HUABIO, China), c-Jun (1:1500, ET1608-3, HUABIO, China), p-c-Jun (Ser73) (1:1000, 3270, CST, USA), NLRP3 (1:1000, ET1610-93, HUABIO, China), GSDMD(-N terminal) (1:1000, HA721144, HUABIO, China), Bax (1:1000, ET1603-34, HUABIO, China), Bcl2 (1:1000, ET1702-53, HUABIO, China), Acsl4 (1:1000, ET7111, HUABIO, China), NF-κB P65 (1:1000, ET1603-12, HUABIO, China), phospho- NF-κB P65 (Ser536) (1:1000, 3033, CST, USA), and Active β-catenin (1:1000, 8814, CST, USA). After incubation with secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch, USA), the blots were visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Abbkine, China).

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence staining

The sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced epitope retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6) or Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9).

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Sections were blocked and incubated with a mouse anti-HAVCR (Kim-1) antibody (1:800, AF1817, R&D Systems, USA), followed by incubation with a fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody and counterstaining with DAPI (Solarbio, China).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Sections were treated with 3% H2O2 to quench endogenous peroxidase activity and blocked with 10% goat serum. The sections were subsequently incubated with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-F4/80 (1:400, 70076, CST, USA), rabbit anti-phospho-JNK (Thr183/Tyr185) (1:100, 4668, CST, USA), rabbit anti-phospho-p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2) (Thr202/Tyr204) (1:800, 4370, CST, USA), rabbit anti-phospho-c-Jun (Ser73) (1:400, 3270, CST, USA), rabbit anti-Cd11b (1:4000, ab133357, Abcam, UK), rabbit anti-Ccl2 (1:400, ab242013, Abcam, UK), rabbit anti-Cxcl2 (1:200, ab317569, Abcam, UK), mouse anti-Wnt5a (1:500, MA5-15502, Invitrogen, USA), rabbit anti-Kim1 (1:1000, ab316854, Abcam, UK), rat anti-Ly6g (1:250, 127601, BioLegend, USA), rabbit anti-Mmp2 (1:400, sc-13595, Santa Cruz, USA), and mouse anti-Ror2 (1:25, Stanford University/HHMI, USA). For proximal tubule identification, the sections were incubated with LTL (10 µg/ml, B-1325-2, Vector Laboratories, USA). Then, the sections were incubated with a primary antibody enhancer, HRP Polymer, and finally visualized using a DAB substrate (8059S, CST, USA).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was extracted from kidneys via TRIzol (R401, Vazyme, China), and RNA purity and concentration were assessed with a Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher, USA). cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of RNA via HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (R323-01, Vazyme, China). Quantitative PCR was performed on a Bio-Rad CFX Connect instrument with SYBR Green primers listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Flow cytometry

Kidneys were digested in 2 mg/ml collagenase IV, 50 µg/ml DNase I, and 1% FBS at 37 °C for 1 h with shaking at 200 rpm. The cell suspensions were filtered through 70 μm and 40 μm strainers to remove debris. The cells were stained with fixable viability stain (FVS, APC-Cy7) and fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against the following: leukocyte/myeloid cells: CD45 (FITC), Cd11b (AF-700), Ly6G (Qdot-665), F4/80 (Pacific Blue), and CD206 (APC); lymphoid cells: CD19 (PE-Cy7), CD4 (Qdot800), and CD8a (PE); and chemokine receptors: Ccr2 (BV605, macrophages) and Cxcr2 (PE, neutrophils) (BD Pharmingen, USA). Data acquisition was performed on an Agilent NovoCyte instrument, and the data were analyzed via FlowJo 10.0.

TUNEL staining

Apoptosis and pyroptosis in kidney sections were assessed via the DeadEnd™ Fluorometric TUNEL System (G3250, Promega, USA). After deparaffinization, hydration, and rinse, the sections were incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by DAPI counterstaining for 10 min at room temperature. TUNEL-positive cells were imaged and quantified via a Zeiss Axio Observer fluorescence microscope.

GPX4 activity analysis

GPX4 activity was measured with a commercial kit (E-BC-K883-M, Elabscience, China) on the basis of the consumption of a reducing agent by the GPX4-catalyzed substrate, which was monitored at 340 nm. Kidney tissues were homogenized and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The protein concentration was determined via a BCA assay. The supernatant was added to the reaction mixture following the manufacturer’s instructions, and the optical density was read at 340 nm via a Varioskan LUX plate reader (Thermo Fisher, USA). GPX4-specific activity was calculated by subtracting nonspecific enzyme activity from total activity according to the kit formula.

Cell cultures and treatments

PTECs were isolated from the kidney cortex of WT, Tiki2-/-, Tiki2 fl/fl, and Tiki2 fl/fl; Rosa26CreERT2 mice. The minced cortex was digested in 1 mg/ml collagenase IV at 37°C for 1 h with gentle shaking and then filtered through 80-mesh and 70-μm strainers. Tubular segments retained on the 70-μm strainer were collected, washed with PBS, centrifuged, and resuspended in complete PTEC medium (DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS, 50 mg/L rhEGF, 1 nM T3, 25 nM dexamethasone, 1× ITS, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin). For conditional knockout, the cells were treated with 200 nM 4-hydroxytamoxifen for 3 consecutive days. Genomic DNA, RNA, and protein were collected to verify knockout efficiency and for downstream western blot analyses.

HK-2 cells (Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology, CL-0109, China) were cultured in MEM supplemented with 10% FBS and antibiotics (100 U/ml penicillin and 100 μg/ml streptomycin). The cells were infected with either pCDH-GFP control or pCDH-HA-Tiki2 lentivirus33,35. Serum-free Wnt5a-conditioned medium (CM) was prepared as previously described35. For treatments, HK-2 cells were starved in serum-free medium and then incubated with Wnt5a CM for 12 h. The cells were subsequently harvested for immunoblotting and RT-qPCR analyses.

RNA-Seq profiling

Total RNA was extracted from injured Ctrl and Tiki2PTKO kidneys (n = 3 per group) via TRIzol. After quality control, the samples were used for library construction and sequenced on an Illumina PE150 platform. Transcripts with |log2FC| > 1 and adjusted P < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. The RNA-seq data have been deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database: GSE293486.

scRNA-seq data analysis

Single-cell RNA-seq data were obtained from published datasets of healthy human kidney (GSE118184)62, healthy mouse kidney and IR-d5 mouse kidney (GSA: CRA017133)63 datasets. The healthy human kidney Tiki2 expression data from GSE118184 were derived from a publicly available kidney single cell dataset (https://www.humphreyslab.com/SingleCell/). As for mice data, sham group analysis was conducted on samples (CRX1109056) and IR-d5 group analysis was conducted on samples (CRX1109060) (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa/browse/CRA017133). Analysis was performed via Seurat R 4.0. Author-reported clustering parameters were applied to reproduce the cell types. PCA was conducted via the jackStraw function, and significant PCs were selected for t-SNE visualization. Single cells were plotted in 2D on the basis of variable gene expression, and correlation matrices were generated with the R package pheatmap.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.1). Data are presented as mean ± SD. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. For multiple group comparisons, one-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis test was used, as appropriate, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. Two-way ANOVA with Sidak’s multiple comparisons test was used where appropriate. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Experiments comparing the Ctrl and Tiki2PTKO kidneys following IR were independently repeated more than 3 times. Comparisons including Tiki2PTKO; Ror2PTKD kidneys were performed in three independent experiments. Dynamic analyses of Tiki2 expression levels were conducted in duplicate. FACS data from sham-operated mice in Supplementary Figs. S4 and S5 were pooled from two independent replicates. Approximately twice as many IR-treated mice as sham controls were used due to the variability of the IR model, and only data from independent experimental batches were analyzed (n ≥ 3/group).
