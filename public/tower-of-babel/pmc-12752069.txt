The relationship between perceived stress and mobile phone addiction in adolescents: a meta-analysis
Authors: Mengjie Li, Xinxin Han, Jiaxin Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionA considerable body of research has examined the association between perceived stress and mobile phone addiction among adolescents, but findings have been inconsistent. This meta-analysis aims to clarify the strength of the relationship between perceived stress and mobile phone addiction in adolescents and to identify potential moderating factors influencing this association.MethodsA comprehensive systematic search was conducted across thirteen databases, namely, PubMed, Web of Science, PsycINFO, Embase, Cochrane, Scopus, ProQuest, CINAHL, MEDLINE, SinoMed, CNKI, WANFANG, and VIP, spanning from their inception to February 15, 2025. Two independent reviewers were responsible for the screening of studies, extraction of data, and assessment of the quality of the included studies. Data analysis was performed using the statistical software STATA version 18.0.ResultsForty-five studies involving 34,558 adolescents were included. The results showed a strong positive association between perceived stress and mobile phone addiction (r = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.35—0.44, p < 0.001, I2 = 94.2%). Additionally, region and the measurement tools used for mobile phone addiction were found to moderate this relationship, while publication year, research design, gender, grade, and the perceived stress measurement tools did not have moderating effects.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that reducing stress may alleviate adolescents' tendency toward mobile phone addiction. In the future, measures aimed at preventing and alleviating mobile phone addiction should place significant emphasis on the stress levels of adolescents.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03702-z.Keywords: Perceived stress, Mobile phone addiction, Adolescents, Meta‑analysis

Introduction

According to statistics from the International Telecommunication Union (ITU), in 2024, approximately 5.5 billion individuals, representing 68% of the global population, are utilizing the Internet [1]. The widespread proliferation of network technology has rendered smartphones an essential tool in daily life, owing to their attributes of instant gratification, convenience, and functional integration [2, 3]. However, excessive mobile phone use poses the risk of mobile phone addiction (MPA). MPA, also referred to as Problematic Mobile Phone Use or Smartphone Addiction, is classified as a behavioral addiction. It is characterized by a compulsive behavioral pattern in which individuals engage in excessive and uncontrollable mobile phone use. This condition is specifically manifested through inappropriate or excessive use of mobile phones, a lack of control over usage, and is categorized under impulse control disorders [4]. It is widely known that adolescence is a pivotal transitional phase for personal development, knowledge acquisition, and value establishment. During this stage, adolescents typically exhibit incomplete mental and physiological development and relatively low self-control. Consequently, compared to adults, they tend to rely more on mobile phones and are particularly vulnerable to MPA [5].

A substantial number of studies from different cultures have reported a high prevalence of MPA among adolescents. For instance, a study focusing on Malaysian adolescents revealed an MPA incidence rate of 37% in 2023 [6]. In China, the MPA rate among adolescents reached 26.2% in 2024 [7]. In India, the incidence rate of MPA among adolescents reached 64.6% in 2024 [8]. MPA not only causes psychological challenges such as anxiety, depression, and sleeplessness among adolescents [9–12]. It also affects academic performance [13] and interpersonal ties [14, 15*]. In severe cases, it may even precipitate self-harm or suicidal tendencies among adolescents [7]. Furthermore, mental health experts predict that MPA will become one of the most significant non-substance-related dependencies following internet addiction [16]. Currently, adolescent MPA has emerged as a global public health issue [17]. Thus, identifying the risk and protective factors associated with MPA is of paramount importance.

The Compensatory Internet Use Theory posits that when individuals encounter psychosocial issues in real life (such as stress, social anxiety, or loneliness), they may proactively turn to the internet or mobile phones to alleviate negative emotions and compensate for unmet psychological needs [18]. If such online compensatory behaviors occur repeatedly, they may evolve into MPA. It is well-known that adolescents are in a critical stage of physical and psychological development during adolescence, where the limbic system, a hub for emotional responses, has largely matured, while the prefrontal cortex, responsible for cognitive control and emotional regulation, remains underdeveloped. This imbalance may render them more sensitive and reactive to stress compared to other age groups [19]. Perceived stress refers to the psychological reaction generated by individuals after cognitive evaluation of external or internal stimuli that produce stress [20]. According to General Stress Theory [21], heightened levels of perceived stress are associated with intensified feelings of tension and helplessness, thereby increasing susceptibility to negative emotional states such as anxiety and depression. These emotional states may, in turn, precipitate maladaptive behaviors, including MPA. Coping Theory delineates individual coping strategies into two primary categories: seeking support and avoidance. MPA is a specific form of avoidance strategy, wherein adolescents experiencing elevated perceived stress may engage extensively with mobile networks as a means of distraction and temporary emotional alleviation [22]. Nevertheless, this coping mechanism not only neglects to address the underlying causes of stress but also perpetuates a detrimental cycle of mobile phone addiction among adolescents. Consequently, the dynamic interplay between perceived stress and mobile phone addiction among adolescents has spurred researchers to undertake comprehensive investigations into their intricate relationship.

In recent years, numerous studies have investigated the relationship between perceived stress and MPA among adolescents, yielding varied conclusions. Research indicates a positive correlation between perceived stress and MPA, with higher levels of perceived stress associated with an increased risk of MPA [23*, 24*, 25*]. However, the strength of this positive correlation differs significantly across studies. The correlation coefficient (r) values for the relationship between perceived stress and MPA range from 0.164 to 0.540. For instance, Zheng et al. [25*] identified a strong positive correlation (r = 0.540), whereas Zhang et al. [26*] reported a weaker correlation (r = 0.164). These discrepancies in correlation strength may arise from variations in publication year, geographic region, research design, gender, grade, and the assessment tools used to measure perceived stress and MPA. Consequently, scholars have not yet reached a consensus on the precise nature of this relationship, and current research findings remain inconsistent. Notably, no meta-analysis has yet been conducted to systematically examine the relationship between perceived stress and MPA among adolescents.

To conduct a more comprehensive analysis of the relationship between perceived stress and MPA, it is essential to consider the role of moderating variables. Firstly, the levels of perceived stress and MPA among adolescents may fluctuate over time, necessitating an investigation into whether temporal factors influence their relationship. Secondly, cultural context and socioeconomic status contribute to varying levels of stress experienced by adolescents across different countries, alongside disparities in mobile phone penetration and internet usage [27]. As a result, the correlation between perceived stress and MPA may exhibit regional variations. Thirdly, cross-sectional study designs involve data collection at a single point in time, whereas longitudinal study designs entail data collection at predetermined intervals or fixed events, allowing for continuous tracking of subjects over time. Research indicates that longitudinal designs provide a unique perspective in understanding the relationship of perceived stress and MPA compared to cross-sectional studies [28, 29*]. Consequently, it is imperative to examine the moderating effect of study design. Fourthly, Ivanova et al. [30] highlighted that females are more susceptible to developing MPA compared to males. In response to stress, females are more likely to seek solace and maintain social connections through the internet and mobile phones. Consequently, the relationship between perceived stress and MPA may differ by gender, underscoring the need to examine gender as a moderating factor. Fifthly, both middle school and university students frequently face academic and social pressures; however, university students typically exhibit a higher level of maturity in managing stress. Furthermore, research suggests that university students are more susceptible to MPA compared to middle school students [31]. Consequently, it is crucial to examine the moderating effect of grade on the relationship between perceived stress and MPA among adolescents. Additionally, various studies utilize different measurement tools to assess perceived stress and MPA, which may influence the correlation between the two variables. Therefore, it is essential to investigate the moderating effect of these measurement tools.

The present study seeks to address this significant gap by performing a meta-analysis of the extant literature. The primary objectives are: 1) to systematically synthesize and quantitatively assess the overall strength of the relationship between perceived stress and MPA among adolescents, and 2) to investigate potential moderators that may explain the heterogeneity in effect sizes across studies. This endeavor aims to inform the development of effective early intervention strategies and treatment plans for adolescent mobile phone addiction.

Methods

Literature search

This study has been registered with PROSPERO in compliance with PRISMA guidelines (CRD42024539178), as detailed in Supplementary File 1. A comprehensive literature search was conducted across multiple electronic databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, PsycINFO, Embase, Cochrane Library, Scopus, ProQuest, CINAHL, MEDLINE, SinoMed, CNKI, WANFANG, and VIP, to identify pertinent studies up to February 15, 2025. The search strategy employed a combination of free-text terms and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), incorporating search terms such as "Adolescent" OR "Teens" AND "Smartphone Addiction" OR "Mobile Phone Addiction" AND "Perceived Stress." These terms represent a subset of the keywords used in the search strategy. Further details are provided in Supplementary File 2.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) Empirical investigations examining the relationship between perceived stress and MPA; (2) Participants comprised adolescents aged 10 to 24 years [32]; (3) Studies employed validated scales or globally recognized evaluative standards; (4) Perceived stress and MPA were identified as either independent or dependent variables; (5) Studies reported Pearson's product-moment correlation coefficients (r). In cases where r values were not directly provided, alternative statistical values such as t, β, χ2, and F were converted to r values using the following formulas: , , and Exclusion criteria: (1) Duplicate studies; (2) Studies involving adolescents with developmental or intellectual disabilities, as well as those with mental disorders; (3) Studies providing insufficient data for further analysis.

Literature screening and data extraction

The search results were imported into EndNote X9 to facilitate the removal of duplicates and the screening for inclusion criteria. Two reviewers, MJL and XXH, independently evaluated the titles and abstracts. Any disagreements at this stage were resolved through arbitration by a third reviewer, JXL. Subsequently, the same reviewers, MJL and XXH, conducted a full-text screening of the studies that were preliminarily included. Discrepancies during this phase were resolved through discussion with JXL. Following the completion of the literature screening process, data extraction was independently performed by MJL and XXH. The extracted data included: (1) Basic study information; (2) Participant demographics; (3) Measurement tools used for outcome assessment; (4) Correlation coefficients (r) between perceived stress and MPA; (5) Indicators for assessing the risk of bias. In cases where data were insufficient, the authors were consulted.

Quality assessment

The quality of cross-sectional studies was assessed using the Quality Evaluation List developed by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) [33]. This list comprises eleven criteria, each of which can be answered with "yes," "no," or "unclear," yielding a maximum score of eleven points. Studies were classified as low-quality if they scored less than 3, medium-quality if they scored between 4 and 7, and high-quality if they scored between 8 and 11. For longitudinal studies, the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [34] was employed to evaluate quality. This scale includes criteria for the selection of study groups (4 points), comparability of groups (2 points), and ascertainment of exposure and outcomes (3 points), resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 9. Studies scoring between 0 and 3 were deemed low quality, those scoring between 4 and 6 were considered moderate quality, and those scoring between 7 and 9 were classified as high quality [35]. The evaluation process was conducted independently by two reviewers (MJL and XXH), with a third reviewer (JXL) stepping in to resolve any disputes.

Statistical analysis

In this meta-analysis, the correlation coefficient (r) was utilized as a measure of effect size, with analyses conducted using STATA version 18.0. Initially, the correlations (r) were transformed into Fisher's Z-values via the Fisher transformation. This transformation incorporated weighting based on sample size and the computation of 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The transformation formula is given by Z = 0.5*ln[(1 + r)/(1–r)], where the variance of Z (VZ) is 1/(n − 3), and the standard error of Z (SEZ) is the square root of 1/(n − 3). The strength of the associations was interpreted according to the criteria established by Gignac and Szodorai [36], categorizing effects as small at 0.10, moderate at 0.20, and high for values equal to or exceeding 0.30. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran's Q and I2 statistics, while publication bias was evaluated through funnel plots and Egger's linear regression test. A study was considered heterogeneous if the Q statistic was significant (p < 0.1) and the I2 exceeded 50%; otherwise, a fixed-effects model was applied. Subgroup analysis was performed considering publication year, region, research design, gender, grade, and measurement tools to explore the sources of heterogeneity. Furthermore, if potential publication bias is identified, a trim and fill analysis will be conducted.

Results

Study selection

An extensive search initially identified 958 records. Following the elimination of duplicates, 513 studies remained. Subsequent screening led to the exclusion of studies due to irrelevance, non-English or non-Chinese language, as well as reports, reviews, meta-analyses, letters, and conference abstracts. This process resulted in 51 articles deemed suitable for full-text review. Of these, 6 articles were excluded: 3 did not satisfy the inclusion criteria, and 3 lacked sufficient data. Ultimately, 45 studies conformed to our predefined inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the meta-analysis. The details are depicted in Fig. 1.

Quality of literature

This meta-analysis incorporated a total of 18 high-quality studies and 27 moderate-quality studies, resulting in an overall assessment of the literature as being of moderate to high quality. According to the AHRQ scale, 18 cross-sectional studies were classified as high-quality, while 23 were deemed moderate quality. The NOS indicated that all four longitudinal studies were of moderate quality. In the context of cross-sectional studies, 36 studies did not describe the methods employed to address missing data, and 26 studies lacked descriptions of any quality assurance assessments conducted. Furthermore, 23 studies did not provide details on the assessment and/or control of confounding factors. Fourteen studies did not specify whether evaluators of subjective study components were blinded to the status information of other participants, and 12 studies failed to explain any patient exclusions from the analysis. Regarding longitudinal studies, both the ascertainment of exposure and the assessment of outcomes in all studies relied on participants' self-reports. Moreover, none of the studies verified the absence of the outcome of interest at baseline, which may introduce potential bias. Further details are provided in Supplementary File 3.

As depicted in Fig. 2, the meta-analysis yielded a pooled correlation coefficient of 0.40 (95% CI: 0.35—0.44), indicating a strong positive relationship between perceived stress and MPA. Notably, considerable heterogeneity was observed across the studies (Q = 757.58, I2 = 94.2%, p < 0.001) (Table 2), necessitating the use of a random effects model.

The region significantly influences the relationship between adolescents' perceived stress and MPA, with a notably stronger correlation observed among adolescents in China (r = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.36—0.47) compared to their counterparts in other countries (r = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.26—0.38). Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the robustness of the findings, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by systematically removing individual studies and recalculating the pooled correlation coefficients. As illustrated in Fig. 3, this analysis demonstrated minimal variations in the r values and 95% confidence intervals, even after the exclusion of individual studies, indicating the robustness of these findings. The results remained consistent with the overall findings, and the range of deviations was confined within the confidence interval, thereby further corroborating the stability of the results.

Publication bias

The funnel plot showed that the effect sizes related to perceived stress and MPA are distributed symmetrically around the overall effect size, suggesting a low probability of publication bias (Fig. 4). However, due to the deviation of some studies from the confidence interval, it was essential to perform more stringent assessments using Egger's test. The outcome of Egger's test (p = 0.209 > 0.05) suggests the absence of significant bias.

Discussion

The relation between perceived stress and MPA

This meta-analysis identifies a significant positive correlation between perceived stress and MPA among adolescents, suggesting that an increase in MPA is associated with elevated levels of perceived stress, consistent with the findings of previous studies [23, 76]. Stress may enhance adolescents' propensity for MPA through several mechanisms. Firstly, according to the theory of constrained self-regulation [77], heightened stress levels deplete self-control resources, thereby diminishing self-regulation capacity. Self-control is defined as an individual's intrinsic ability to resist external temptations and achieve desired outcomes. Individuals with diminished self-control often struggle to suppress the urge for addictive behaviors. Adolescents, being in a critical stage of behavioral development and possessing relatively lower self-control compared to adults, are consequently more susceptible to MPA. Secondly, the Compensatory Internet Use theory suggests that excessive mobile phone usage may serve as a coping mechanism to mitigate perceived stress [18]. Mobile phone offers various functionalities, including information exchange, entertainment, social interaction, and stress relief, thereby catering to the diverse needs of adolescents. For instance, Zhou et al. [78] observed that college students experiencing elevated stress levels often alleviate their stress by engaging in activities such as gaming, browsing social media platforms, monitoring peers' activities, and staying informed about social trends. Simultaneously, Snodgrass et al. [79] discovered that MPA can lead to different outcomes among adolescents, contingent upon their levels of perceived stress. Specifically, individuals with lower levels of perceived stress may find comfort and enhance their well-being through mobile phone use, whereas those with higher levels of perceived stress may experience exacerbated pressure and distress. This highlights the crucial necessity of meticulously monitoring adolescents' stress levels, providing guidance to correct erroneous cognitive beliefs when faced with diverse stressors, implementing suitable strategies for stress alleviation and management, and decreasing their dependency on mobile phones. Such interventions are instrumental in preventing and mitigating MPA among adolescents.

Moderating effects

The moderating role of publication year

The publication year didn't moderate the relationship between perceived stress and MPA, which can be attributed to two primary factors. Firstly, the sources of stress experienced by adolescents have remained relatively consistent over time. Consequently, their tendency to seek external coping mechanisms in response to stress has also remained stable. Secondly, although the prevalence of mobile phone usage among adolescents has increased in recent years, there has been a growing emphasis on adolescent mental health across various countries. For example, in 2018, Spain enacted the Organic Law on Data Protection and Digital Rights Guarantees, which requires the implementation of measures to safeguard minors during their online interactions [80]. Additionally, numerous countries are actively pursuing targeted interventions to improve adolescents' stress management abilities, such as educational interventions delivered via mobile applications [81] and the integration of Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) with Family Therapy [82]. These initiatives have successfully reduced the probability of MPA among adolescents to a certain extent. This suggests that the intrinsic correlation between adolescents' perceived stress and MPA may exhibit a degree of stability. Consequently, to effectively prevent MPA behaviors in this demographic, it is essential to consistently monitor and address their stress-related issues.

The moderating role of region

The region moderated the positive association between perceived stress and MPA. Specifically, in comparison to other countries, this correlation is significantly stronger among adolescents in China. This can be attributed to several factors. Firstly, Chinese society places a strong emphasis on collective interests and familial responsibilities, which subjects adolescents to heightened academic stress, familial expectations, and pressures from social comparisons [83]. These stressors are more likely to be internalized, manifesting as anxiety or depression, or compensated for behaviorally through excessive mobile phone use as a form of emotional avoidance, rather than being expressed directly [84]. Secondly, Chinese adolescents are more predisposed to maintaining social relationships via social media platforms, such as WeChat. However, excessive use of these platforms can intensify the Fear of Missing Out (FOMO), thereby creating a vicious cycle between psychological stress and MPA [85]. Thirdly, China's educational and social support systems exhibit a lack of sufficient emphasis on developing adolescents' systematic stress-coping capacities. The school curricula are deficient in evidence-based stress management programs, such as mindfulness training and cognitive-behavioral therapy skills instruction, which results in limited opportunities for adolescents to learn scientific stress-regulation strategies, including emotion recognition and problem-solving frameworks, through structured courses [86]. This shortcoming not only undermines adolescents' proactive stress-coping abilities but also indirectly encourages their reliance on immediate gratification from digital media, thereby increasing the risk of MPA. Furthermore, it is important to acknowledge that the disproportionately high representation of Chinese studies in this research compared to other countries may influence the research findings.

The moderating role of research design

The research design didn't moderate the relationship between perceived stress and MPA. Firstly, the pressures experienced by adolescents are predominantly long-term and persistent [87]. In such enduring stressful conditions, mobile phones, with their capabilities for convenient entertainment and social interaction, have become a prevalent means for adolescents to mitigate stress. This behavior, driven by psychological needs, demonstrates a certain degree of stability. Once the coping strategy of using smartphones to relieve stress is established, it becomes challenging to alter in the short term [60*]. Consequently, whether examined at specific time points in cross-sectional studies or over time in longitudinal studies, this behavioral pattern remains relatively stable, resulting in no significant differences in the conclusions derived from the two research designs. Secondly, both cross-sectional and longitudinal studies have selected samples with similar characteristics, thereby minimizing the impact of characteristic differences on the outcomes. Thirdly, both research designs utilized validated and reliable measurement instruments, adhered to standardized measurement procedures, and employed consistent data analysis methods, thereby ensuring data accuracy and facilitating result comparability. As a result, no significant differences were observed in the conclusions regarding the relationship between perceived stress and MPA derived from the two research designs. Considering the predominance of cross-sectional studies over longitudinal studies in this analysis, caution must be exercised when interpreting the results.

The moderating role of gender

Gender didn't moderate the relationship between perceived stress and MPA. Firstly, adolescents face a variety of stressors, including academic pressure, familial expectations, and interpersonal relationships, which are prevalent across both genders. Consequently, perceived stress does not exhibit significant gender-based differences; rather, it is more likely shaped by individual psychological characteristics, personal needs, and mental health status [88]. Secondly, mobile phones, as instruments for entertainment and socialization, are equally appealing to both male and female adolescents. The primary difference lies in the tendency of girls to engage more in socializing and browsing entertainment news, whereas boys are more inclined toward gaming activities [89]. Therefore, when considering the use of mobile phones as a coping mechanism for stress relief, no significant gender differences are observed among adolescents.

The moderating role of grade

The grade didn't moderate the relationship between perceived stress and MPA. Although the correlation between perceived stress and MPA is slightly lower among middle school students compared to college students, this difference is not statistically significant. This phenomenon may be attributed to the widespread accessibility of mobile phones among adolescents, facilitated by advancements in the internet and social media [90]. Moreover, both college and middle school students exhibit similarities in their psychological development, as they are in the adolescent stage and possess comparable levels of self-regulation and vulnerability to MPA [91]. Despite encountering distinct stressors, such as academic pressures, interpersonal relationships, and family issues, both groups tend to use their phones as a coping mechanism to escape and manage stress.

The moderating role of perceived stress measurement

The measurement of perceived stress didn't moderate this relationship, likely because 39 out of the 45 articles included in this meta-analysis employed the PSS and the CPSS. Both the PSS and CPSS are internationally and domestically recognized as authoritative instruments for stress assessment. The PSS, developed by Cohen et al. [92] in 1983, has undergone extensive reliability and validity testing, demonstrating robust psychometric properties. It reliably and effectively measures individuals' perceived stress levels and has been translated into multiple languages for widespread global application [93]. The CPSS was adapted and culturally tailored from the PSS by Yang et al. [94] to align with the Chinese cultural context. Numerous empirical studies, along with reliability and validity assessments, have been conducted. The design and phrasing of its items are more congruent with the linguistic habits and psychological characteristics of Chinese adolescents, leading to its widespread utilization in China [95]. The perceived stress assessment scales referenced in the literature included in this study are highly authoritative, possess a broad scope of application, and exhibit minimal variation. Consequently, the results obtained are relatively stable.

The moderating role of MPA measurement

The measurement of MPA acted as a moderating variable between perceived stress and MPA. Among various scales, the MPATS demonstrated the strongest correlation between perceived stress and MPA, followed by the MPAI, while the SAS-SV showed the weakest. Although the MPAI, MPATS, and SAS-SV measurement tools are theoretically and diagnostically aligned with the DSM-IV criteria, they differ in scope and implications, reflecting variations in dimensions, items, and cultural compatibility. The SAS-SV, developed by Korean researchers Kwon et al. [96], concentrates primarily on addiction symptoms associated with mobile phone use, such as uncontrolled usage and withdrawal symptoms. This scale has been validated across multiple countries and demonstrates high cultural adaptability. However, its relatively limited number of items and simpler content may lead to potential information loss. The MPATS and MPAI scales, predominantly developed and utilized in China, differ in their coverage. The MPATS focuses on the frequency of mobile phone use, functional impairment, and psychological dependence [97], whereas the MPAI encompasses dimensions such as loss of control, withdrawal symptoms, and avoidance [98]. Both scales effectively provide a comprehensive assessment of the degree of MPA among adolescents. Furthermore, the discrepancies among measurement instruments highlight the necessity for researchers to establish standardized benchmarks in evaluating MPA, thereby minimizing potential variations.

Limitations and prospects

Firstly, a significant proportion of the studies included in this meta-analysis originate from China. This is largely attributable to China's extensive population of adolescent internet users who face a highly stressful academic environment, rendering stress and mobile phone addiction prominent public health concerns. Additionally, there is a concentration of scientific research resources in this area, and validated measurement tools are extensively utilized within the local context, which has facilitated the production of a relatively large number of related studies. Moreover, the collectivist culture, specific family parenting styles, and the high emphasis on academic achievement prevalent in Chinese society may amplify the relationship between perceived stress and mobile phone addiction among Chinese adolescents, thereby introducing a degree of bias towards China in the findings. Consequently, there is an urgent need for further research to ascertain the generalizability of these conclusions across a wider array of cultural, economic, and social contexts. Secondly, all studies rely on self-reported data from adolescents, which may introduce bias due to selection bias. Future research should incorporate objective data and measurements from various perspectives to enhance the reliability of the measurement results.

Clinical practical implication

This meta-analysis reveals a robust positive correlation between perceived stress and MPA among adolescents, offering significant implications for clinical practice. Firstly, considering that stress is a major contributor to MPA, it is recommended that clinicians incorporate routine assessments of stress levels into adolescent mental health evaluations. Notably, the more pronounced association observed among Chinese adolescents necessitates culturally sensitive interventions that address specific stressors such as academic competition and elevated family expectations. It is advisable for educational institutions to proactively implement courses focused on stress response and management. These courses should cover a comprehensive array of topics, including stress awareness, emotional regulation strategies, and time management techniques. Concurrently, schools should enhance guidance for families and promote collaborative education between home and school to collectively support students in developing effective stress-coping mechanisms.

Secondly, the finding that various MPA assessment tools significantly moderate the association's strength highlights the necessity of employing validated, adolescent-specific scales with high reliability and validity in clinical evaluations. Future research should also prioritize the development of objective indicators for assessing adolescent MPA to enhance measurement precision.

Thirdly, given that gender and grade did not significantly moderate the relationship, universal intervention strategies are deemed appropriate. Nevertheless, it is crucial to consider differentiated usage patterns, such as the distinctions between social networking and gaming, when designing targeted measures. Meanwhile, attention should be paid to individual differences among adolescents, and personalized intervention plans should be formulated accordingly.

In conclusion, the clinical implications derived from this meta-analysis underscore the necessity of adopting a comprehensive approach to address stress and MPA issues among adolescents. Firstly, it is imperative to enhance collaboration between home and school environments to aid students in developing effective coping mechanisms. Concurrently, the integration of stress evaluation and mobile phone addiction screening into the adolescent mental health assessment framework is recommended, alongside the implementation of culturally tailored interventions. Moreover, it is crucial to account for individual differences in order to devise personalized intervention strategies, and to establish objective assessment criteria to bolster scientific rigor. This study offers vital insights for clinical practice, enabling precise assessment and intervention strategies aimed at promoting adolescent mental health.

Conclusion

The meta-analysis revealed a strong positive correlation between perceived stress and MPA among adolescents. This relationship was moderated by region and the instruments used to measure MPA, but it was not affected by the year of publication, research design, gender, grade or perceived stress assessments. Consequently, to prevent and alleviate MPA among adolescents, greater emphasis should be placed on managing their stress levels.
