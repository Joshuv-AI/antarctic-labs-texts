Factors Underlying Choice and Change of Absorbent Incontinence Products in Women With Urinary Incontinence
Authors: Sarah Qureshi, Saima Rajabali, Adrian Wagg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and ObjectiveUrinary incontinence (UI) is common in women. For up to 77%, the use of absorbent continence products (pads) is central to management. Pad changes are common and reasons for change are not fully described. We hypothesized that women change their pads for many reasons other than wetness and at saturation levels well below advertised capacity.MethodsA sample of 11 community dwelling women aged 65–95 (mean 78.1, SD 8.2) years was included. Participants completed a bespoke questionnaire on satisfaction with their current pads; UI severity and quality of life were measured. Participant's pad use and degree of saturation was recorded. Women then participated in a qualitative interview exploring their pad choices and use.ResultsThe sample median ICIQ‐SF score was 16/21 (IQR 10–17). Median satisfaction of pad use score was 9/20 (IQR 8–12). Median ICIQ LUTS QoL score was 62/76 (IQR 55–67). The median number of pads used per day was 2.3 (IQR 1–4) The median saturation at pad change was 9.5% (IQR 7–19). Qualitative analysis resulted in 43 codes categorized into 7 categories and collapsed into 3 themes “Product factors,” “Lifestyle,” and “UI and other health factors.”ConclusionWomen appear to “overuse” absorbent products and change them for diverse reasons, often simply to fit in with their daily routine. Individualized education and support in usage would be beneficial.

Introduction

Urinary incontinence is a common condition in women [1]. For up to 77%, given that available treatments seldom result in “cure,” continence containment products (pads) remain a staple of daily management [2, 3]. In Canada, amongst many other countries, pads are sought without intervention or advice about their use or about potential treatment [4]. Data suggest that women may try up to 14 different types of products in a “try it and see” approach before reaching a solution which meets their needs [5]. Women also mix and match pads depending upon need dictated by their daily activities [6]. Despite the frequency of use of products, there remains limited systematically gathered research into patient satisfaction with containment products and their use. Because the subjective impression of leakage into pads varies widely and is unreliable for product selection, this subjective impression may well have a meaningful impact on the frequency of pad changes [7]. The number of pads used per day is thus a poor measure of urinary leakage. For example, in an Italian study of the “appropriateness” of pad provision, which was based upon absorptive capacity, 75% of the products supplied were deemed to be “inappropriate” [8]. Similarly, when women were asked to rank their impression of the most important features of pads, the ability to hold urine was only one of five features (holding urine, containment of smell, staying in place, discreteness, comfort when wet) felt to be important. Another factor related to the pad cited as important to women include the planning of pad changes and disposal, particularly in the social environment [9]. The latter feature is increasingly important, given the potential environmental cost of pad disposal and reductions in the number of public facilities. Given the dearth of data on women’ s product choices and reasons for pad change, this study sought to examine factors motivating choice and change and also to estimate the degree of saturation of pads prior to change. Information gleaned will be useful to manufacturers and decision makers of public services and healthcare provision.

Methods

This multiple methods study assessed pad use in women over the age of 50 years. Incontinence severity was measured using the validated International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire‐Urinary Incontinence—Short Form (ICIQ‐UI SF) [10]. Continence‐related quality of life was measured using the validated International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire—Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms Quality of Life (ICIQ‐LUTS QoL) [11]. Participants completed a short bespoke questionnaire on satisfaction with their current pad. The scale was ordered from 0 (completely unsatisfied) to 20 (extremely satisfied). Based upon the participant's estimate of the number and types of pads likely to be used over the 3 study days, that number of pads were individually weighed, and the weight of each was recorded along with an identifying number discreetly marked on each pad with a permanent marker. For 3 days, participants placed their used pads individually in a sealed air and watertight bag and stored them in a cool box at which point they were collected for analysis. At each pad change, women recorded the reason(s) for that change, whether there was any leakage outside the pad and need to change clothing. After collection, each pad was again weighed to estimate the mass of urine in the pad. These weights were used for calculating the percentage saturation of each pad at the time it was changed, using the manufacturer's stated absorptive capacity as the denominator.

Participants who chose to do so and provided informed consent then took part in a one‐on‐one interview exploring their pad choices and use, satisfaction with current pad use and supply, circumstances surrounding each pad change and reasons for that change. The interviews were conducted using a semi‐structured interview guide which addressed the participants general pad use, and factors which determined pad changes such as cost, skin irritation, general discomfort, wetness and odor. Participants were also asked to elaborate on their diary log entries to explore additional reasons for their pad change at that time. The interview used the woman's comments and notes from their 3‐day diary as a reference frame and aide memoire.

Setting and Participants

Women were recruited from a secondary care multiprofessional continence clinic, through advertising in older adult independent living facilities and older adult recreation centers. Women with daily incontinence episodes, and independently using pads to achieve contained incontinence were eligible for inclusion. Only women with cognitive impairment sufficient to render them unable to engage with study procedures were excluded.

Study Approval

This study was approved by the Health Research Ethics Board of the University of Alberta. All women provided written informed consent to participate in the study and, separately, provided additional consent to take part in interviews.

Quantitative Data Analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed by calculating the median and IQR for the number of pads used per day, averaged over the 3 days, as well as for ICIQ UI‐SF score, ICIQ LUTS‐QoL score and for the satisfaction with use of pads survey. The median and IQR of urine loss per pad summed by type of pad as a proportion (%) of manufacturer provided absorbency figures was also calculated.

Qualitative Data Analysis

Interviews were transcribed verbatim. Following development of an initial a priori coding framework, two researchers then independently coded the first 2 transcripts. The researchers then met to compare codes and redevelop the initial coding framework followed by a meeting of the entire team to review and revise the coding framework. All remaining transcripts were then coded by a single researcher. Any new codes created were reviewed by the rest of the team. The final codes were again reviewed by the team and collapsed into categories and subsequently into themes. To ensure rigor in the approach and the trustworthiness of findings, team members spent considerable time familiarizing themselves with the data and context. An audit trail of each stage of the qualitative arm analysis was maintained. Team members practised reflexivity by continued discussion of how their preconceptions and values might have affected the analysis.

Results

Quantitative

A convenience sample of 11 women was recruited, and all completed both components of the study. Women ranged in age between 65 and 95 (mean 78.1, SD 8.2 years). Using ICIQ‐SF scores to determine categories, one participant had slight incontinence, two had moderate incontinence, six had severe incontinence, and one had very severe incontinence. The median ICIQ‐SF score was 16/21 (IQR 10–17). The median ICIQ LUTS QoL score was 62/76 (IQR 55–67). The satisfaction of pad use survey scores had a median of 9/20 (IQR 8–12). The median number of pads used per day was 2.3 (IQR 1–4). The median wetness (% stated total absorbency) at the time of pad change was 9.5% (IQR 7–19).

Qualitative

Data saturation was reached after 11 interviews. The qualitative analysis resulted in 43 codes in 7 categories and collapsed into 3 themes. These themes were “Product factors,” “Lifestyle,” and “UI and other health factors.” The codes, themes, and categories, aligned with illustrative quotes below, are shown in Table 1.

Product Factors

This theme reflected the reason for changes which were related to properties of the worn product itself. Wetness and heaviness, and pad irritation when the pad was soaked led it to become increasingly uncomfortable.

“Well, when it just gets so wet and heavy and it's usually in one void and I can't stand it it's just too it's just uncomfortable.”

Whether the pad remained discrete, rather than obvious to onlookers, or it remained comfortable were cited as reasons for product change. Some women reported Irritation from the pad, which precipitated a pad change.

“That or if I'm really irritated, I'll change them right away.”

Lifestyle

Daily routines were frequently discussed as a reason for pad changes as participants often wanted a fresh continence product for the morning, night and after a shower. Often pads were changed as a convenience; lifestyle and activity led to participants changing more frequently when outside the home, when active or socially engaged. The potential for malodour was reported as more of a concern in these circumstances.

“Sometimes like I said when I know I'm having company or like there's something going on in the building I'll often change my pad before I go so, I feel really free. Doesn't have to be full to be changed.”

UI and Other Health Factors

The influence of concomitant medications and co‐existent medical conditions also influenced pad changes. For example, some participants had coexistent fecal incontinence or hemorrhoids which resulted in product soiling even without urine leakage, prompting an immediate change. Some participants were taking diuretics that led to an increased frequency of incontinence episodes for the duration of the diuresis and prompted more frequent changes.

“Mostly everyone would because they're so terribly wet. When I have no control over it with those pee pills.”

UI related factors denoted how activity affected the need for pad change for women with stress UI; bending, coughing and sneezing were listed as reasons for frequent product change in this circumstance.

Discussion

This study sought to examine the reasons why older women changed their continence product in daily life and was motivated by the decision by the provincial subsidizing authority to provide only high‐capacity products to women with daily incontinence who were deemed to have failed treatment and required at least four pads daily. This led to an increase in the number of women deemed ineligible for support. Previous literature on the practical aspects of pad use showed that women are concerned with factors other than just successful containment of urine [9]. These additional factors included comfort of the pad, associated skin health, and maintaining discreetness with regards to their UI.

We found that women changed their incontinence products for varied and often very personal reasons. Pads were frequently changed at levels of saturation far below advertised maximal absorbencies, suggesting that most women could have used thinner, more discrete products than those they currently used. To what extent the specter of “just in case” was used as an internal justification for usage of this nature is unknown, but this is a commonly used justification and may well be influenced by personal events. Many people use products to achieve contained incontinence [12]. In this sample of women, lifestyle factors, daily routines and activities commonly dictated frequent product change as convenience, ensuring a “clean” pad before activities were undertaken. This enabled them to live their daily lives with confidence and maintained their quality of life. When successful, management with continence products avoided the consequences of incontinence, which can threaten social and working lives and personal relationships [13, 14].

Women may arrive at their chosen product after many trials, and it is clear that what works for one woman may fail for another [15]. The International Organization for Standardization (ISO) recommends user, usage, and product‐related factors for individualized selection of appropriate products [16]. Factors which women consider when making product selection are often influenced by a combination of fit, absorbency, quality, cost, and availability, but there is little knowledge about factors which drive women to change their pad. The ability of a pad to absorb urine without leakage outside the pad is important but is only one feature of the product felt to be important. Ensuring super absorptive capacity requires wearing bulkier pads which compromises discreetness, a feature also considered important by users. If discreetness is of great concern for an individual, then they may choose to wear thinner pads resulting in more frequent pad changes [9].

Among the most important concerns that affect pad changes is prevention of skin damage and irritation [17]. Users who are concerned about skin health may prioritize features like the breathability of the pad and how it affects humidity and temperature. An improperly fitted pad may move around more and wet skin is more susceptible to mechanical damage. These users may change their pad more often when it becomes wet or displaced [17]. Pads that do not stay in place can be uncomfortable and can chafe the skin leading to pad changes even if there is little to no urine in the pad [9].

Older women cited discomfort from a wet pad as a reason for change, even if the pad was minimally wet. This may be due to worries about the development of skin rashes to which individuals with mature skin may be more susceptible. Convenient changing of pads prior to venturing out or engaging in social activities was important for many women, giving a sense of self‐confidence. For some women, pad changing was simply part of their routine, regardless of continence status. For these women, pad changing had been incorporated into everyday management [18, 19]. For some women, the frequency of incontinence episodes, regardless of severity, dictated pad change, as did the presence of associated fecal incontinence, where time in a soiled pad is much less tolerated compared to UI [20]. The influence of diuretic therapy on urinary symptoms was a clear reason for more frequent pad change. Overall, women changed their pads for many reasons even when urine was properly contained. Other concerns such as comfort and skin health, discreteness and routine changes may explain why most women in this study changed their pads far below their advertised capacity. We did not hear concerns about prolonged wear being associated with an increased risk of urinary tract infection, although these data come from acutely hospitalized frail older adults and residents of nursing homes [21, 22].

Limitations

Like all studies, this one has limitations. The sample size, whilst reaching our predefined number for the qualitative data, was small and therefore lacks generalizability. We did not use the validated International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire PadPROM [23] pad related quality of life questionnaire following receipt of feedback from some women regarding survey burden and opted for a brief, easy to complete assessment. This led to a lack of detail in our information but we felt that much of the ICIQ questionnaire content was peripheral to our main study aim. Use of the manufacturer's advertised absorptive capacity as the denominator for our study brings with it two limitations. Firstly, the Rothwell index, (ISO 11948‐1 “Rothwell method”) is the standard way to report the total absorbent capacity of urinary incontinence pads under controlled laboratory conditions. This may not reflect true capacity in real life use and secondly, there is wide variation from pad to pad, even in the same packet [24]. This latter factor may have perturbed our calculations. Recruitment of a purposeful representative sample of women in terms of living conditions, dependency, body habitus and severity of UI may well have revealed additional information. Pad type details were gathered after the pad collection period had taken place and three participants’ pad types were unavailable for inclusion in the capacity at pad change calculations, thus we did not report these as the data were limited. One participant experienced no incontinence during the 3‐day collection period after being started on pharmacotherapy for her UI; this woman's ICIQ‐SF score was 0 which may have skewed the results. One woman did not complete her ICIQ‐LUTS QOL survey and was excluded from the analysis of this scale.

Conclusion

Women change their continence products for many and varied, often personal, reasons. Pads are changed at levels well below the advertised absorbency level. Given this, there remains a need for careful tailoring of products to meet the needs of the individual and a need for clinicians with appropriate knowledge to counsel women. A lower absorbency pad in different designs may well have traction with and meet the needs of older women who change their pads at such low levels of wetness. Our recent change in reimbursement policy does not reflect this utility, potentially excluding women from eligibility, reducing choice and increasing the environmental impact of disposal. A low absorbency product for frequent use could also have environmental benefits in terms of reduced material use and costs of disposal.
