Rabies virus-vectored Lyme disease vaccine provides long-term protection against tick-transmitted Borrelia burgdorferi
Authors: Shantel Rios, Bibek Bhattachan, Christoph Wirblich, Anisha Chandwani, Kruthi Vavilikolanu, Jacob F. Myers, Chrysoula Kitsou, Utpal Pal, Matthias J. Schnell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Lyme disease (LD) cases have doubled globally. LD is a tick-borne illness caused by the Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato (Bb). If untreated, Bb can disseminate to distal organs, causing carditis, arthritis, and meningitis. Currently, no FDA-approved human LD vaccine exists on the market. This study used two approaches to incorporate OspA into the rabies virus (RABV) vaccine vector. We used the RABV-glycoprotein tail (RVG tail) and the Hendra virus (HeV) glycoprotein tail (HVG tail) to incorporate OspA, creating BNSP333-OspA-RVG and BNSP333-OspA-HVG, respectively. Both vaccines produced type-1 biased anti-OspA antibodies, but only BNSP333-OspA-HVG induced neutralizing antibodies and protected against Bb infection. Furthermore, BNSP333-OspA-HVG was combined with an established LD vaccine, BNSP333-BBI39-dRVG, to study a multivalent LD vaccine. The single and multivalent vaccines produced robust type-1 biased humoral responses and induced protection against Bb after short-term and long-term tick challenge experiments. These findings contributed to the development of future LD vaccines.

Introduction

Lyme disease (LD), also known as Lyme borreliosis, is the most common tick-borne illness in many parts of North America, Europe, and Asia1. Almost half a million people are estimated to be diagnosed with LD each year in the United States (U.S.) alone2. LD is caused by a spirochete bacterium called Borrelia burgdorferi sensu stricto (Bbss), which has many associated genospecies that are collectively called Bb sensu lato (Bbsl). The most common genospecies are B. afzelii, B. garinii, and B. burgdorferi sensu stricto (Bb)3.

Ixodes scapularis, or the deer tick, is the main vector for Bb in the eastern United States4. Given the high prevalence, severity, and inability to control LD with antibiotics fully, an effective and safe LD vaccine is warranted.

Developing an LD vaccine requires testing many vaccine targets and platforms. A widely studied antigen for LD vaccines is outer surface protein A (OspA), which is also the basis of a former FDA-approved vaccine. OspA is Bb’s most abundant surface protein in cultured bacteria and possibly while the bacterium resides in the tick5. A lipid anchors the amino(N)-terminus of OspA into the borrelial membrane, with the carboxy(C)-terminus facing outwards6. The Tick Receptor of OspA (TROSPA) binds to the OspA to anchor the bacteria in the tick’s gut7. During the blood meal and host entry, OspA is downregulated and replaced by other surface proteins, most notably OspC8. OspA is a successful vaccine antigen in many former vaccines, including LYMErix9, ImuLyme10, and VLA1511. In addition, veterinary vaccines such as Recombitek and Vanguard crLyme use OspA12,13.

LYMErix, developed in 1998, was the only FDA-approved LD vaccine for humans. LYMErix was an OspA-based recombinant protein vaccine adjuvanted with aluminum hydroxide (alum). After 3 doses, LYMErix demonstrated high protective efficacy but with decreasing antibody titers long term, and repeat boosters were necessary each year14. In addition, the epitope, OspA165-175, from Bb contains similar amino acid homology to a region on human leukocyte function-associated antigen-1 (hLFAα322–340)15,16. Individuals with treatment-resistant Lyme arthritis can experience autoimmune reactions when vaccinated with an OspA vaccine, which includes OspA165-17515,16. The LYMErix vaccine declined in sales and was ultimately taken off the market in 200214, although the FDA did not find evidence that the vaccine caused arthritis in these individuals.

In addition, VLA15, later developed by Pfizer/Valneva, is a recombinant protein OspA vaccine currently in phase III clinical trial studies17 (NCT05477524). VLA15 contains a chimeric protein of several C-terminal domains from various serotypes of Bbsl seen in North America, Europe, and Asia11. VLA15 changed the OspA165-175 epitope to eliminate the previously suggested adverse events. Nonetheless, three immunizations are required within the first 2 months of vaccination, with an additional fourth immunization currently being tested for 12 months post-prime (NCT05477524)11,17. These vaccine trials underscore that the OspA remains a compelling target against Bb to combat LD since it is a highly protective vaccine antigen. However, the previous recombinant protein vaccine platforms have demonstrated waning humoral immunity, which is necessary for a successful long-term LD vaccine candidate18,19. As a transmission-blocking vaccine, immunized individuals require high antibody titers to neutralize Bb within the tick. Additionally, infected patients do not initiate a recall response to borrelial antigens8,16,20. Therefore, we opt to develop an effective LD vaccine utilizing the highly immunogenic and efficacious RABV vaccine vector, which incorporates OspA.

In this study, we extended our previous work on rabies virus (RABV) vaccine vector18 as well as the known vaccine potential of OspA21, to assess whether BNSP333 can incorporate OspA into the viral particle and protect against Bb. BNSP333 is an attenuated vaccine vector derived from the wildlife vaccine strain, SAD B1922. This vector contains an amino acid mutation at position 333 in the glycoprotein (G) that depletes neurovirulence in mice23. BNSP333 is a highly immunogenic, safe, efficacious, and long-lasting live-attenuated or killed vaccine vector. BNSP333 elicits high neutralizing24–26 and non-neutralizing27 antibody titers against many foreign antigens inserted into its genome. RABV has been a vaccine vector against various bacterial and viral pathogens such as Anthrax28, SARS-CoV-226, Ebola virus24,29, and Crimean Congo Hemorrhagic fever virus30. Most importantly, BNSP333 was a vaccine vector utilized against Bb with the borrelial antigen, BBI39 (BNSP333-BBI39-RVG)18. This vaccine successfully elicited high anti-BBI39 antibody titers with a type-1 immune bias and depleted Bb from vaccinated mice in both short- and long-term studies18.

Previous RABV-vector vaccines, including BNSP333-BBI39-RVG, successfully incorporated foreign antigens into the RABV virion. Antigen incorporation is necessary to elicit an immune response in a non-cytolytic virus, such as RABV18. Adding the RABV-G signal sequence (SS) to the N-terminus and the final 51 amino acids of the ectodomain (ED51), transmembrane domain (TM), and cytoplasmic domain (CD) on the C-terminus of a foreign antigen (RVG tail) allowed for incorporation of non-glycoprotein antigens into the RABV virion18,26,28,30. Such chimeric foreign antigens were trafficked and incorporated into the RABV virion as type-1 transmembrane proteins18,26,28,30. Successful antigen incorporation allowed these vaccines to produce protective neutralizing antibodies against various pathogens, including Bb18,26,28,30.

Previously published data showed that OspA contains protective epitopes on the C-terminus, producing neutralizing antibodies against Bb11,31,32. This study compared the previously successful RVG tail, a type-1 transmembrane protein, to the HVG tail, which includes components from the Hendra Virus (HeV) glycoprotein (HeV-G). HeV belongs to the Paramyxoviridae family25,33. HeV-G naturally occurs as a type-2 transmembrane protein in the HeV virion33,34. The HVG tail consists of a CD, TM, and stalk (S) domains on the N-terminus of HeV-G34,35. Previously, Kurup et al. incorporated the full-length HeV-G into the RABV virion for a vaccine against HeV33. Altogether, we developed two chimeric versions of OspA, including either the RVG tail (BNSP333-OspA-RVG) or the HVG tail (BNSP333-OspA-HVG), to determine an optimal RABV-based OspA vaccine to protect against LD. The goal of the study is to explore whether BNSP333-OspA-HVG is protective against Bb and produces protective neutralizing antibodies against syringe and infected tick challenges. We show that BNSP333-OspA-RVG is not protective because the RVG tail makes the protective epitopes of OspA inaccessible. In addition, Bb undergoes antigenic variation during tick or host infection36. Therefore, we tested BNSP333-OspA-HVG as a single vaccine and in a multivalent vaccine formulation with the previously published BNSP333-BBI39-RVG18 to determine if multiple antigens elicit greater protection against the LD pathogen, Bb.

Results

Design and characterization of RABV-OspA vaccines

This study utilized the previously described attenuated rabies virus vector, BNSP33318,24–27,30,33 (Fig. 1a). The chimeric OspA-RVG and OspA-HVG genes were inserted at the second position between the RABV nucleoprotein (N) and phosphoprotein (P). In these vaccines, we utilized OspA from serotype-1 Bb (NCBI: WP_010890378). OspA165-175 was mutated to FTLEGALTA to prevent sequence similarities to hLFAαL322–340 after vaccination (Fig. 1a). As mentioned, OspA-RVG contains the RABV-G SS fused to the N-terminus of OspA, followed by ED51, TM, and CD of the RVG tail on the C-terminus. This construct, BNSP333-OspA-RVG, incorporates OspA as a type-1 transmembrane protein with its N-terminus facing outward from the viral particle, opposite to its normal orientation (Fig. 1b). The RVG tail is the traditional approach for incorporating a foreign antigen into the RABV vector. However, unpublished data showed this vaccine was not protective against Bb in an infected-tick challenge. Therefore, we created BNSP333-OspA-HVG, which contains the CD, TM, and Stalk components of the HVG tail to incorporate OspA as a type-2 transmembrane protein in the RABV virion (Fig. 1c). RABV-OspA vaccine constructs were cloned and recovered into infectious viruses. Throughout this study, we used Vanguard crLyme, a canine veterinary vaccine that includes OspA and OspC antigens, as a positive control for the RABV-OspA vaccines12. Vaccine characterization included separation of 1/10th of the vaccine dose (1 μg of rabies particles and 10 μL of Vanguard crLyme) on an SDS-PAGE gel for protein gel and western blot analyses. The protein gel showed that all five of the RABV proteins (L-polymerase (L), G, nucleoprotein (N), phosphoprotein (P), and matrix protein (M)) were incorporated into the RABV particles (Fig. 1d). Protein gels did not display OspA; however, polyclonal anti-OspA probed western blots determined that both BNSP333-OspA-RVG and BNSP333-OspA-HVG incorporated OspA into the RABV virion (Fig. 1e). The expected sizes of these antigens are: 30 kDa for the OspA recombinant protein, 49 kDa for OspA-HVG, and 46 kDa for OspA-RVG. As expected, we observed some glycosylation of the viruses derived from mammalian cells, thus increasing the molecular weight on the western blot. The quantity of OspA in BNSP333-OspA-HVG and Vanguard crLyme was comparable. However, BNSP333-OspA-RVG contained slightly less OspA (Fig. 1e). As expected, only the RABV-vector vaccine viruses contained RABV-G, probed for by a human monoclonal anti-RABV-G (4C12) (Fig. 1e).

Immunogenicity of RABV-OspA vaccines

We immunized C3H/HeN mice to study the immunogenicity of these vaccines. C3H mice are a well-characterized LD mouse model that shows some symptoms of LD when infected with Bb37. Ten C3H/HeN mice, five male and five female, were primed on day 0 and boosted on day 28 (Fig. 1f) with BNSP333-OspA-HVG, BNSP333-OspA-RVG, Vanguard crLyme12, or FiloRab1. FiloRab1 is a well-studied RABV-Ebola vaccine used as a vector control in this study18,24,29. All RABV-based vaccines were inactivated with beta-propiolactone (BPL) and formulated with a synthetic TLR-4 agonist (PHAD) mixed with a squalene in oil emulsion (SE) inflammasome activator adjuvant (PHAD-SE)18,24,30,38. PHAD-SE induces a type-1-biased immune response, which is ideal for the induction of borreliacidal antibodies to bind complement and cause lysis of Bb18,24,30,38–41. Mouse serum was collected on days 0, 28, 35, and 56 (Fig. 1f) and analyzed for the presence of IgG antibodies against OspA and RABV-G by ELISA (Fig. 1g, h). BNSP333-OspA-HVG, BNSP333-OspA-RVG, and Vanguard crLyme elicited anti-OspA antibodies by day 28 (Fig. 1g). Anti-OspA antibodies were increased 20-fold on day 35 post-boost in all vaccine groups. The mice maintained anti-OspA antibody titers up to day 56. RABV-based vaccines elicited significantly higher anti-OspA antibodies compared to Vanguard crLyme, with a 10-fold difference observed on day 56 (Fig. 1g). Of note, no significant differences in antibody titers were observed in BNSP333-OspA-HVG and BNSP333-OspA-RVG vaccinated mice. In addition, on day 56, all RABV-vector vaccines elicited similar anti-RABV-G antibody titers (Fig. 1h). Vanguard crLyme did not elicit RABV-G antibodies (Fig. 1h). We did not observe immunogenic sex differences from any vaccine group in this study.

Antibody isotype ELISAs further characterized the humoral immune response (Fig. 1i). We measured IgG2a, IgG2b, and IgG1 anti-OspA isotypes. IgG2a/b antibodies are considered type-1 biased immunity, and IgG1 antibodies are considered type-2 biased immunity in C3H mice. Of note, the neutralizing mouse-anti-OspA LA-2 monoclonal antibody is an IgG2b isotype. Both BNSP333-OspA-HVG and BNSP333-OspA-RVG induced significantly higher anti-OspA IgG2a and IgG2b antibodies compared to Vanguard crLyme vaccinated mice (Fig. 1i). RABV-based vaccines induced a type-1 biased immunity with higher IgG2a/b antibodies than IgG1. Vanguard crLyme (recombinant protein vaccine) induced a more type-2 biased immunity with higher IgG1 anti-OspA antibodies than IgG2a/b antibodies (Fig. 1i).

Neutralization of RABV and Bb

To determine the functionality of the anti-OspA antibodies produced by these vaccines, we performed in vitro neutralization assays with wild-type (WT) RABV and Bb. RABV neutralization titers greater than 0.5 IU/mL in rapid foci-forming inhibition tests (RFFITs) are the World Health Organization (WHO) standard titer for neutralization against WT RABV42. RFFITs showed neutralization of RABV in BNSP333-OspA-HVG, BNSP333-OspA-RVG, and FiloRab1-vaccinated mice on day 56 (Fig. 2a). The borreliacidal assay determined the neutralization properties of mouse sera against Bb in the presence of guinea pig complement. Mice vaccinated with BNSP333-OspA-HVG successfully neutralized Bb up to 3-fold greater than FiloRab1 control groups (Fig. 2b). BNSP333-OspA-HVG depleted Bb at similar levels to LA-2, a monoclonal antibody that binds to the protective conformational epitopes on the C-terminus of OspA43,44 (Fig. 2b). Vanguard crLyme neutralized Bb with a 1-fold difference to FiloRab1-vaccinated mice (Fig. 2b). However, BNSP333-OspA-RVG did not neutralize Bb with similar Bb titers to FiloRab1 sera (Fig. 2b).

Characterizing the accessibility of the protective epitopes of OspA in the RABV vector

BNSP333-OspA-RVG did not neutralize Bb in borreliacidal experiments (Fig. 2b). Therefore, we analyzed the accessibility of OspA’s protective epitopes in the RABV-OspA constructs. Immunofluorescence staining of the surface of infected cells determined that OspA was successfully produced after BNSP333-OspA-RVG and BNSP333-OspA-HVG infection. We probed with the same polyclonal anti-OspA antibody (yellow) and anti-RABV-G (4C12) (green) from the western blot analysis in Fig. 1e. In addition, we utilized the monoclonal anti-OspA antibody, LA-2 (purple), which was used in borreliacidal assays in Fig. 2b. BNSP333-OspA-RVG and BNSP333-OspA-HVG viruses produced OspA, as observed by the fluorescent staining of the polyclonal anti-OspA antibody (Fig. 2c). However, only BNSP33-OspA-HVG displayed the epitopes of OspA, which are detected by the LA-2 antibodies on the surface of infected cells (Fig. 2c). BNSP333-OspA-RVG produced OspA but did not display the protective epitope LA-2 on the cell surface (Fig. 2c).

To further understand why OspA-RVG did not neutralize Bb, we stripped the surface antigens from the RABV virions to compare their antigenic exposure (Fig. 2d–f). Stripping surface proteins with 2% β-Octyl-glucopyranoside (OGP) detergent allows for analysis of these antigens without most of the other viral proteins within the RABV virion. Surface proteins, including OspA-RVG/HVG and RABV-G, were removed and collected from the RABV virion. Protein gel analysis confirmed successful stripping of glycoproteins (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Stripped antigens were probed with polyclonal and LA-2 anti-OspA and anti-RABV-G (1C5) antibodies by an indirect ELISA. Whole virions, recombinant protein OspA, and stripped RABV-G proteins were used as control antigens. Polyclonal anti-OspA ELISA data confirmed that all RABV-OspA stripped antigens and full virions produce OspA, as observed previously in western blot analysis (Fig. 1e, f) and immunofluorescence (Fig. 2c). BNSP333-OspA-RVG produced OspA slightly less than BNSP333-OspA-HVG (Fig. 2d), which was also observed previously (Fig. 1e). However, both stripped OspA-RVG antigen and full virion BNSP333-OspA-RVG do not bind LA-2 (Fig. 2e). These data confirm the inaccessibility of OspA’s protective epitopes in OspA-RVG. Protective epitopes are possibly misfolded within the chimeric antigen. However, BNSP333-OspA-HVG, stripped antigen, and whole virion successfully bind LA-2 (Fig. 2e), which was also observed in immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 2c). All RABV-based constructs bind anti-RABV-G (Fig. 2f). Since BNSP333-OspA-RVG did not expose OspA’s protective epitopes correctly (Fig. 2c–e) nor neutralize Bb in vitro (Fig. 2b), we did not continue with this vaccine candidate in this study.

BNSP333-OspA-HVG reduction of Bb after needle challenge

We observed that BNSP333-OspA-HVG, with adjuvant PHAD-SE, produces high anti-OspA and anti-RABV-G antibody titers that can successfully neutralize Bb and RABV in vitro. To determine if this vaccine can prevent infection in immunized mice, we challenged C3H/HeN mice (n = 10, five female and five male) with Bb strain B31 on day 56 post-immunization by intradermal needle injection (Fig. 3a). Successful infection was observed by western blot analysis of borrelial lysates probed with individual mouse sera from challenged mice (Supplementary Fig. 2a). We collected mouse skin, joints, and hearts on 14 days post-challenge. Tissues were analyzed by qRT-PCR and culture positivity of Bb. All tissues collected were positive for live Bb in culture (Supplementary Fig. 2b). FlaB CT values, which determine the quantity of Bb in the tissues, were normalized to mouse Beta-actin CT values in qRT-PCR analysis of infected mouse tissues. Mice vaccinated with BNSP333-OspA-HVG and Vanguard crLyme significantly depleted Bb in the skin, joints, and hearts compared to FiloRab1-vaccinated mice (Fig. 3b–d). Long-term immunogenicity and efficacy of RABV-OspA vaccines

An efficacious LD vaccine should protect vaccinated individuals long after immunization. Therefore, we conducted a long-term immunogenicity and challenge experiment with our vaccine candidate, BNSP333-OspA-HVG (Fig. 4a). Antibody titers were measured up to 6 months post-immunization by ELISA. BNSP333-OspA-HVG contained significantly higher anti-OspA antibody titers at all time points when compared to Vanguard crLyme vaccinated mice. At 6 months post-vaccination, BNSP333-OspA-HVG vaccinated mice had about 50-fold more anti-OspA antibodies than Vanguard vaccinated mice (Fig. 4b). Also, we observed lower waning humoral immunity in BNSP333-OspA-HVG vaccinated mice, with a 90.9% difference from 2 months (day 56) to 6 months post-vaccination. Discussion

LD cases are rising globally, and there is no available human FDA-approved LD vaccine. In this study, we developed an LD vaccine by incorporating the well-studied vaccine antigen, OspA, into a RABV vaccine vector. Although previous studies have explored the genesis of viral or bacterial vectors based on OspA, the effectiveness of RABV-vectored vaccines for B. burgdorferi remained unknown46–48. We therefore developed BNSP333-OspA-RVG and BNSP333-OspA-HVG as vaccine candidates. These vaccines successfully incorporate OspA into the RABV virion and produce high-titer anti-OspA and anti-RABV-G antibodies. However, only BNSP333-OspA-HVG can fully deplete Bb after the challenge. The importance of the orientation of the Borrelia lipoproteins has previously been described49, and the C-terminus of OspA is known to harbor the antigen’s protective epitopes1,31,43. On the borrelial membrane, the lipid on the N-terminus of OspA anchors the protein into the bacterial membrane, with the C-terminus facing outwards6. The HVG tail incorporates OspA into the RABV virion by the N-terminus with the C-terminus facing outwards, mimicking the natural orientation of OspA. This study is the first to use the HVG tail for incorporation of a foreign antigen into a viral vector other than the full-length HeV-G33. Ultimately, BNSP333-OspA-HVG produces neutralizing antibodies elicited by the C-terminal domain of the OspA protein.

Previously published data states that incorporating a foreign antigen into a non-cytolytic viral virion, such as RABV, is vital for a humoral response against the foreign antigen18. However, this study determined that the method of incorporating a foreign antigen is also a key consideration for producing functional neutralizing antibodies. In this study, OspA-RVG was also successfully incorporated into the RABV virion (Fig. 1e, f, g), eliciting anti-OspA antibodies (Fig. 2b, d). Still, the elicited antibodies did not neutralize Bb in vitro (Fig. 2f). Thus, a vaccine eliciting antibodies against a foreign antigen does not guarantee protection against the pathogen. The results of this study should be considered when developing other novel vaccine constructs against various pathogens.

As stated, OspA is a well-studied LD vaccine antigen that has been proven to protect against Bb infection after tick and needle challenge studies. LYMErix, the only FDA-approved LD vaccine for humans, and VLA15, which is currently in clinical trials, both provide protection against Bb by eliciting anti-OspA antibodies. However, both vaccines show waning immunity after three intramuscular immunizations. Although the recombinant protein vaccine platform is widely utilized in the LD vaccine field, it displays waning long-term immunity.

In this study, we compared our vaccine candidate, BNSP333-OspA-HVG, to a veterinary USDA-approved LD vaccine, Vanguard crLyme developed by Boehringer Ingelheim. Vanguard crLyme is a recombinant protein vaccine with OspA and a chimeric OspC antigen that produces anti-OspA antibodies and protects against Bb12, also seen in this study. However, Vanguard crLyme requires a booster every 15 months to obtain effective anti-OspA antibody titers. In contrast to recombinant protein vaccine platforms, viral vector vaccine platforms, such as RABV, can provide more long-term immunity with robust antibody titers after two intramuscular immunizations, as observed in this study and previous studies18. Additionally, a previous study using a parainfluenza vector vaccine against OspA provided more long-term immunity compared to a recombinant protein vaccine19. The RABV vaccine platform is safe in both live-attenuated and killed versions. In this study, BNSP333-OspA-HVG contained higher anti-OspA antibody titers with greater neutralizing attributes than Vanguard crLyme vaccinated mice (Figs. 2–7). In addition, BNSP333-OspA-HVG provided longer humoral immunity than Vanguard crLyme vaccinated mice (Fig. 4). BNSP333-OspA-HVG elicited neutralizing antibodies against both Bb and RABV, both wildlife pathogens affecting the human and veterinary populations daily42,50,51.

In addition to higher antibody titers and long-term immunity, BNSP333-OspA-HVG provided greater antibody avidity to OspA than Vanguard crLyme vaccinated mice (Fig. 4d). Avidity is the overall affinity, or binding strength, of polyclonal antibodies that can bind to an antigen52. This strength can determine the killing efficiency of antibodies52. Initially, the humoral response produces low-affinity antibodies (IgM or IgG). Over time, in the germinal center, the avidity of the elicited antibodies increases52. Our study agrees with the results by Gutierrez et al., where Duramune, a multivalent bacterin vaccine made by Elanco Animal Health Inc., produces higher avidity than the recombinant protein vaccine, Vanguard crLyme, along with greater long-term protection13. Although these studies did not complete a long-term tick challenge, the immunological data conclude that multi-antigen vaccines, such as whole bacterial lysate or viral vector vaccine platforms, induce higher avidity and more long-term immunity than recombinant protein vaccines, such as Vanguard crLyme.

In addition, adjuvant selection plays a role in the type of antibodies elicited from a vaccine and can have a detrimental effect on the protective efficacy of a vaccine24. This study utilized PHAD-SE, a well-studied TLR-4 agonist and inflammasome activator adjuvant18,24,30,38. We used PHAD-SE in our previous study with BNSP333-BBI39-RVG and observed greater immunological and protective results in vaccinated mice with adjuvant18. In this study, all vaccines included PHAD-SE except Vanguard crLyme. We saw a type-1 biased immune response in RABV-based + PHAD-SE vaccinated mice compared to Vanguard crLyme vaccinated mice (Figs. 2d, 4c, and 5g, h), with greater IgG2a and IgG2b than IgG1 in the PHAD-SE+ mice. In mice, IgG2 antibodies show greater avidity and functionality when eliminating Bb18,39,41,53. On the contrary, alum is a type-2 associated adjuvant and has been used in LYMErix and VLA1511,17,21,54. Thus, recombinant protein vaccines adjuvanted with alum induce a more type-2-associated immune response, which is not ideal for borrelial protection. Adjuvant formulation and vaccine platforms are essential considerations in vaccine development to induce protective immune responses.

Previous studies have utilized viral vectors to develop an LD vaccine. For example, Gingerich et al. developed an intranasal parainfluenza (PIV5) OspA vaccine19, and Xiao et al. developed a Newcastle disease virus (NDV) OspC vaccine55. PIV5 is also an RNA virus in the Paramyxoviridae family, like HeV. In this study, the authors designed two vaccine constructs that incorporated OspA into the PIV5 viral virion near the N-terminal and transmembrane domains of the PIV5 hemagglutinin-neuraminidase protein19. This study parallels our study, which used the HVG tail (CD, TM, and S domains) (Fig. 1c). However, these authors did not fully characterize the incorporation of OspA into the viral virion and only observed production in infected cell lysates19. Additionally, female mice were vaccinated intranasally with live viral PIV5-based vaccines, followed by a boost 21 days post-prime. The PIV5-OspA vaccines are the first to utilize an intranasal route for an LD vaccine. However, they did not use male mice in this study; therefore, sex differences were not excluded during vaccination or challenge. Male mice can have lower antibody titers, antibody avidity, and skew towards type-1 immunity56,57. Thus, male mice should be included in all vaccination studies. Nonetheless, this study observed additive effects when utilizing a viral vector vaccine compared to a recombinant protein vaccine platform, rOspA + alum adjuvant19. In addition, protection from the viral vector is observed long-term, with the challenge occurring more than 1 year post-vaccination19. These results compare to the RABV-based constructs that also induce more long-term neutralizing IgG antibodies than the recombinant protein vaccine, Vanguard crLyme. However, IgG isotype characterization was not published in their study, so we could not compare immune biases. These are essential experiments towards an efficacious LD vaccine. Our study consists of a short-term tick challenge after 2 months of vaccination, but a long-term tick challenge is warranted in future experimentation of these vaccine constructs.

A major challenge to vaccine development is addressing Bb’s antigenic variation during tick or host infection36. For example, temperature and pH changes that bacteria encounter can change the expressed borrelial proteins. Multivalent vaccines can more effectively target Bb’s antigenic variation by targeting more antigens than single antigen vaccines. For example, Vanguard crLyme is a multivalent vaccine that includes both OspA and OspC antigens. OspC is a surface protein expressed during Bb dissemination from the tick’s midgut into the salivary gland and during host infection58–60. However, OspC is widely divergent between Bb strains. Therefore, Vanguard crLyme was engineered with a chimeric OspC antigen with seven different OspC antigenic epitopes from Bb that infect canines12. Protection against Bb increased after including OspC with OspA, supporting the notion that multivalent vaccines can provide a greater protective power12.

In our study, we tested a multivalent vaccine formulation with BNSP333-OspA-HVG and a previously published vaccine, BNSP333-BBI39-RVG18. As previously mentioned, OspA is only expressed on Bb while residing in the tick gut4. Therefore, for protection against OspA, anti-OspA antibodies need to enter the tick gut to neutralize Bb. In addition, if an individual is later infected with Bb after vaccination, OspA cannot induce a recall response since OspA is strictly a tick-expressed antigen. Thus, OspA vaccines require high and long-term antibody titers to be effective61. Therefore, we included BBI39, a surface Bb protein expressed during the late-stage tick infection and early-stage host infection45, as a multivalent formulation with OspA. Previously, we determined that BNSP333-BBI39-RVG was protective in Bb needle injection and tick challenges18,45.

In this study, we observed that BNSP333-OspA-HVG, as a single vaccine, neutralizes Bb in vitro greater than BNSP333-BBI39-RVG (Fig. 6b). However, a limitation of in vitro neutralization studies is the biased neutralization to OspA-based vaccines, as Bb is cultured at a temperature lower than physiological temperature to emulate the lower internal tick environment, where Bb expresses OspA4,62. Therefore, OspA may be expressed more in Bb than in BBI39 in vitro. Conversely, after syringe challenge, the BNSP333-BBI39-RVG vaccinated mouse tissues contained slightly less Bb than BNSP333-OspA-HVG vaccinated mice in the more disseminated tissues, such as the hearts and joints (Fig. 7). Since Bb expresses BBI39 in the host, the BBI39-based vaccine can deplete Bb greater in disseminated organs than OspA-based vaccines. OspA-based vaccines will deplete Bb more significantly in ticks and during the early stages of host infection, such as in the skin. Overall, the syringe challenge experiments showed that the multivalent vaccine induced greater protection than the single vaccines.

The long-term infected-tick challenge experiments are necessary to determine the protective efficacy of vaccine constructs. Although the in vitro borreliacidal assays and needle injection challenge experiments are simpler, they are artificial and do not adequately simulate natural infection. Tick challenges are an ideal method to detect vaccine efficacy since this is the natural route of infection. In addition, waning immune responses can induce a diminished protective efficacy long-term63. Therefore, we conducted a long-term tick challenge experiment up to 6 months post-immunization with the RABV-vector vaccine constructs compared to the recombinant protein vaccine, Vanguard crLyme. We observed sterile immunity for both BNSP333-OspA-HVG and the multivalent (BNSP333-OspA-HVG and BNSP333-BBI39-RVG) vaccine constructs. After 6 months, mice vaccinated against these vaccines were not infected with Bb due to the lack of Bb detection in both qRT-PCR and tissue cultures (Fig. 9b, c). This is compared to the total infection of the FiloRab1-vaccinated mice at the end of the experiment. We did not observe an additive effect of the multivalent vaccine, since BNSP333-OspA-HVG alone could induce sterile immunity. Longer-term experiments could possibly elicit variable protection from these vaccines, but we observed ideal protection with the RABV-OspA vaccine alone.

An important aspect of the long-term tick challenge was comparing the RABV-vector vaccine constructs to a recombinant protein vaccine, Vanguard crLyme. As mentioned previously, recombinant proteins have been a widely utilized vaccine platform for an LD vaccine. This includes the FDA-approved human LD vaccine, LymeRix, VLA15, and the veterinary LD vaccine, Vanguard crLyme1,12,64. All these vaccines have displayed waning antibody immunity, resulting in a diminished protective efficacy in the long term, thus requiring multiple booster immunizations to maintain protection13,63. Since LymeRix was removed from the market, the CDC states that any person previously immunized with LymeRix will not have protection against LD today65. We hypothesized that a viral vector, such as RABV, would induce more long-term protective efficacy compared to a recombinant protein vaccine. Since the RABV vaccine elicits long-term immunity to the RABV-G antigen38, we hypothesized that this vaccine vector would have the same effects on a Bb antigen inserted into the RABV virion18.

The long-term tick challenge showed that the Vanguard crLyme vaccine did not induce sterile immunity like the RABV-vector vaccine constructs. Vanguard crLyme insignificantly depleted Bb in infected mice compared to FiloRab1-vaccinated mice, as seen in the qRT-PCR of the skin (Fig. 9b). Although Vanguard crLyme contains two borrelial antigens, OspA and OspC12, Vanguard crLyme could not induce sterile long-term protection like BNSP333-OspA-HVG and the multivalent formulation. In conclusion, a highly immunogenic viral vector, like RABV, should be utilized when developing long-term, efficacious LD vaccines for humans and animals.

If OspA had failed to protect over time, the inclusion of the previously demonstrated protective BBI39 was intended to provide such protection. It is important to note that OspA is only effective in the tick to prevent entry; no recall response would not be anticipated in the host. However, the response induced by the RABV-based vaccine was so robust that we still observed 100% protection, indicating that this effect and the potential of BBI39 to provide additional protection could not be investigated. Nevertheless, we found the results to be beneficial and demonstrated that it is possible to induce immune responses against multiple Borrelia antigens.

Additional preclinical studies are necessary to characterize the protective efficacy of the BNSP333-OspA-HVG vaccine in single and multivalent formulations, including analyzing various adjuvants, such as alum, to determine the benefits of an adjuvant formulated with these vaccines. One limitation of this study is using an adjuvanted vaccine with an unadjuvanted control vaccine (Vanguard crLyme). Utilizing another adjuvanted vaccine, such as LYMErix and VLA15 formulated with alum, would allow for a better comparison between the vaccine platforms. In addition, individuals with undetectable borrelial DNA/RNA could still develop persistent LD symptoms66. Therefore, we could analyze BNSP333-OspA-HVG or multivalent vaccinated mice a few months post-challenge to determine the inhibition of these persistent symptoms. Finally, immunized mice should be challenged with various strains of globally prevalent Bbsl species to determine broad-spectrum protection from this vaccine, which could include various OspA serotypes. Finally, a longer tick challenge, up to a year, is warranted to determine the preservation of sterile immunity in the RABV-vector vaccine constructs.

In conclusion, this study developed an effective OspA vaccine in an RABV vector. The incorporation of a chimeric OspA protein into the RABV vector was accomplished by adding both RVG and HVG tails. Both BNSP333-OspA-RVG and BNSP333-OspA-HVG produced anti-OspA and anti-RABV-G antibodies, but only BNSP333-OspA-HVG was protective against Bb. This study also analyzed the effects of a multivalent vaccine with BNSP333-OspA-HVG and BNSP333-BBI39-RVG. Multivalent immunization did not elicit antigenic competition in vaccinated mice. In addition, additive protection was observed in neutralization and needle challenge experiments. All vaccine groups induced sterile immunity in a short-term tick challenge, but only the RABV-vector LD vaccines could induce sterile immunity in the long-term tick challenge. Altogether, this study has furthered research toward an effective and safe LD vaccine for humans.

Methods

Borrelia burgdorferi, cell lines, mice, and ticks

Bb strain B31, grown in Barbour–Stoenner–Kelly-H (BSK-H) medium complete (supplemented with 6% rabbit serum) (Sigma–Aldrich B8291), was used in this study. Bacteria were grown at 33 °C without CO2 in 5 mL bacterial culture tubes. Ixodes scapularis ticks are maintained in a colony within the Pal lab at the University of Maryland. Naturally infected nymphs were generated by feeding naïve larval ticks on infected mice. BSR and Vero (CCL81) cells were obtained from ATCC and cultured in 1× DMEM (Corning Cat# 10-013-CV) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Six- to eight-week-old C3H/HeN mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratories. All animal experiments were performed under the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC), fully accredited by the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International (AALAC) and the Institutional Bio-safety Committee of Thomas Jefferson University and the University of Maryland.

cDNA molecular cloning of vaccine vectors

We inserted OspA-RVG and OspA-HVG, both synthesized by GenScript in pUC57 vectors, into BsiWI and NheI restriction sites of BNSP333 RABV vaccine vector18,23,33 to create BNSP333-OspA-RVG and BNSP333-OspA-HVG. OspA-RVG plasmids. OspA genes utilized were from NCBI (WP_010890378) from serotype 1. The amino acids at positions 165-173 were changed to eliminate adverse effects from previous OspA vaccines. OspA-RVG gene included the final 51 amino acids of the ectodomain (ED51), transmembrane domain (TM), and cytoplasmic domain (CD) of RABV-G. OspA-HVG included the full stalk domain (S), transmembrane domain (TM), and cytoplasmic domain (CD) of the Hendra virus glycoprotein. JM109 E. coli cells were used for molecular cloning under ampicillin resistance. Once synthesized by T4 ligation (NEB M0202), plasmids were sent out for sequencing to Azenta (South Plainfield, NJ). We utilized the forward (5′-GGAGGTCGACTAAAGAGATCTCACATAC-3′) and reverse (5′-TTCTTCAGCCATCTCAAGATCGGCCAGAC) primers for sequencing of OspA between RABV-N and RABV-P. Also, forward (5′-GTTATGGTGCCATTAAACCGCTG-3′) and reverse (5′-TCTCCAGGATCGATCGAGCATCTT-3′) primers for sequencing of RABV-G to determine if the 333 mutation is still viable in the glycoprotein before virus recovery.

Recovery of recombinant rabies viruses

Recombinant RABV vectors were recovered on BSR cells. X-tremeGENE 9 transfection reagent (Roche Diagnostics) in Opti-MEM was utilized to transfect BSR cells in 6-well plates with full-length BNSP333 cDNA along with T7 RNA polymerase, and RABV nucleoprotein, phosphoprotein, glycoprotein, and L-polymerase cDNA plasmids. Supernatants were harvested on day four post-transfection and overlaid on seeded BSR cells in 12-well plates. After 48 h, cells were fixed with 80% acetone and stained with a GFP stain against RABV-N (FujiRebio, Cat# 800-092).

Viral growth, titration, purification, and inactivation

Once viruses were recovered, they were grown on Vero CCL81 cells in viral production serum-free medium (VP-SFM) (Thermo Fisher) supplemented with 5% Glutamax, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 1% HEPES buffers. Cells were infected at an MOI of 0.01 in either a T175 flask or 2-stack chambers (Corning). Supernatant was collected 5 days post-infection, and every 3 days afterward, for a total of 17 days. For titration, RABVs were overlayed on Vero CCL81 cells with a starting dilution of 1:10 and diluted 10-fold in a 96-well plate in triplicate. After 48 h, cells were fixed with acetone and stained with RABV-N GFP stain to determine the foci-forming units (FFU)/mL by fluorescence microscopy.

Viruses were filtered through 0.45 µm PES membrane filters (Nalgene) and concentrated down to 50 mL for sucrose purification. Viruses were purified over a 20% sucrose cushion and ultracentrifuged at 25,000 rpm in a SW32 rotor for 1.5 h. Viral particles were resuspended in TEN buffer + 2% sucrose and inactivated with 50 µL per 1 mg of particles with b-propiolactone (BPL, Millipore Sigma, Cat# P5648) in cold culture-grade water. The inactivation was verified by inoculating Vero CCL81 cells over 3 passages with 10 µg of BPL-inactivated virions.

Immunofluorescence

Vero CCL81 cells were seeded on coverslips in 1× DMEM supplemented with 5% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 1 × 106 cells per well. On the same day, cells were infected with BNSP333-OspA-RVG, BNSP333-OspA-HVG, and BNSP333 at an MOI of 0.05. After 48 h at 34 °C, cells were fixed at room temperature (RT) in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 1× PBS for 30 min for cell surface staining. For intracellular staining, cells were permeabilized with 4% PFA and 0.01% TritonX for an extra 15 min. Cells were blocked in PBS with 5% FBS for 1 h. Following washing with PBS once, cells were stained with 1:200 primary antibody for 1 h at RT in PBS with 1% FBS. Primary antibodies included polyclonal rabbit anti-OspA IgG (Abcam (ab106081)), monoclonal mouse-anti-OspA LA-2 IgG2b (Absolute Antibody (Ab01070-3.0)), and human anti-RABV-G 4C12 (provided by Scott Dessain, Lankenau Institute for Medical Research, Wynnewood, PA). Cells were washed 2× with PBS and incubated with secondary antibody in PBS with 1% FBS for 45 min at RT. Secondary antibodies utilized for fluorescent staining were anti-rabbit AF568 (red, changed to yellow) (Thermo Fisher cat# A-11011), anti-mouse AF647 (purple) (Thermo Fisher cat# A-21235), and anti-human AF488 (green) (Thermo Fisher cat# A-11013). All secondary antibodies were used at a final concentration of 2 μg/mL. Then, stained cells were washed 5× with PBS and mounted on slides with mounting media containing DAPI (ProLong™ Glass Antifade Mountant, Invitrogen, cat#: P36980). Slides were stored for 24–48 h in the dark at RT to dry. Slides were visualized with a Nikon A1R confocal microscope. Images were analyzed by FIJI ImageJ, Version 2.14.0/1.54f. Red staining was changed to yellow for color-sensitive individuals in FIJI ImageJ.

Production of recombinant proteins for Western blot, immunizations, and ELISA

OspA and BBI39 recombinant proteins were purified using a pGEX plasmid with ampicillin resistance from the Pal laboratory in University Park, Maryland. OspA plasmid was transformed into JM109 E. coli cells and grown into a 1L bacterial culture in LB broth with ampicillin. Bacteria were induced with 1 mM of ITPG overnight at RT. The following day, bacteria were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C. The pellet was resuspended in 20 mL of PBS with 1% TritonX. For further lysis, 0.1 mg/mL of Lysozyme was added to the resuspension. After a 30-min incubation on ice, bacterial cells were further lysed by sonication for 7 min. Lysed bacterial cells were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C. OspA was purified using Glutathione Agarose (Pierce™, Thermo Fisher cat#: 16100). The GST tag was removed by 80 units of precision protease (GenScript cat#: Z02799) ON at 4 °C in PCB buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, mixed in water). Purification of the protein was further characterized by Western Blot and ELISA.

RABV glycoprotein (G) was produced by stripping the glycoprotein from rVSV-DG-RABV-G-GFP. BEAS-2B cells were infected with rVSV-DG-RABV-G-GFP in OptiPRO SFM at an MOI of 0.01. Once all the cells were lysed, supernatants were concentrated and ultracentrifuged through a 20% sucrose cushion at 25,000 rpm for 1.5 h at 4 °C. Viral pellets were then resuspended in 2% β-Octyl-glucopyranoside (OGP) and stripped by ultracentrifugation at 45,000 rpm for 1.5 h in an SW55Ti rotor at 4 °C. Supernatants were collected, frozen in small aliquots, and characterized by Western Blot and ELISA.

Stripped antigens for ELISAs in Fig. 1 were produced from BNSP333-OspA-RVG and BNSP333-OspA-RVG. Both viruses were grown on Vero CCL81 cells and sucrose-purified as described above. The same stripping protocol was utilized as RABV-G with 2% OGP.

Protein gel and Western blot

Recombinant proteins, viral particles, and 10 μL of Vanguard crLyme vaccine (Patterson Veterinary (07-892-587)) were subjected to Western Blot analysis. 40 ng of OspA recombinant protein and 1 μg of sucrose-purified virions were utilized for blots in Fig. 1d, e. 0.5 μg of stripped antigens and sucrose-purified virions were utilized for supplementary Fig. 1a, b. All samples were denatured in urea sample buffer (USB) and reduced with 2-mercaptoethanol. Samples were boiled for 10 min at 95 °C. Proteins and virions were separated on 12% SDS-PAGE gels at 150 V for about 1.5 h in 1× Laemmli buffer. Protein gels were stained with SYPRO™ Ruby Protein Gel Stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific) ON at RT. Proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes for 1 h at 90 V in 1× Towbin transfer buffer. Bio-Rad Western blot equipment was used to separate and transfer proteins. After transfer, membranes were blocked with 5% milk in 1× PBST for 1 h at RT. Primary antibodies were utilized at 1:1000 and included polyclonal rabbit anti-OspA IgG (abcam (ab106081)), human anti-RABV-G 4C12 (provided by Scott Dessain, Lankenau Institute for Medical Research, Wynnewood, PA), and rabbit anti-RABV-ribonucleoprotein (RNP). Blots were probed with primary antibodies ON in 5% BSA in PBS at 4 °C. The following day, blots were probed with secondary antibody, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG H + L at 1:5000 for anti-OspA blots and 1:20,000 for anti-RNP blots. Anti-human IgG-fc (SouthernBiotech, 2040–05) blots were probed at 1:20,000. All secondary antibodies were diluted in 1× PBST for 1 h at RT. Proteins were detected with SuperSignal West Dura Chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Fisher cat#: A38554) and imaged on a FlourChem R system (ProteinSimple).

Immunizations

Groups of five 6–8-week-old female and male C3H/HeN mice, purchased from Charles River Laboratories, were immunized intramuscularly (I.M.) with 10 µg of inactivated RABV virions, or 100 μL of Vanguard crLyme vaccine (Patterson Veterinary (07-892-587)). For multivalent vaccinations, 10 μg of BNSP333-OspA-HVG and 10 μg BNSP333-BBI39-RVG were mixed, for a total of 20 μg of RABV virions. RABV vaccines were formulated with 5 µg of synthetic monophosphoryl Lipid-A (MPLA), 3D(6 A)-PHAD, in a squalene oil-in-water emulsion (PHAD-SE) adjuvant. Each immunization contained 100 µL of vaccine, 50 µL injected into each hind leg of each mouse. Serum for further testing was collected by retroorbital bleeds while mice were under isoflurane anesthesia on days 0, 28, 35, and 56 for short-term experiments, with the addition of days 84, 112, and 168 for long-term experiments.

Anti-OspA and anti-RABV-G ELISA

Total and isotype subclass IgG antibody responses were determined by indirect ELISA. We purified recombinant RABV-G and OspA, as described above, and used these proteins to coat Immulon 4 HBX 96-well flat-bottom microtiter plates. Plates were coated ON at 4 °C with antigens diluted in 15 mM Na2CO3, 35 mM NaHCO3 coating buffer. OspA and BBI39 antigens were utilized at 500 ng/well, and RABV-G at 50 ng/well. The following day, plates were washed 3× with PBS containing 0.05% Tween20 (PBST) and blocked in 5% milk for 2 h at RT. Plates were washed 3× with PBST, and 100 μL of primary antibody dilution buffer, containing 0.5% BSA, and 0.05% NaN3, in PBST, was added to each plate. Mouse sera was diluted 1:50 starting dilution and further diluted 3-fold. Plates were incubated ON at 4 °C with the primary antibody. The following day, plates were washed 3× with PBST, and then 100 µL of secondary antibody diluted in PBST was added. Secondary antibodies used in this study were HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG-Fc (Jackson ImmunoResearch, Cat# 115-005-008), IgG2a (Jackson ImmunoResearch Cat# 115-035-206), IgG2b (Jackson ImmunoResearch Cat# 115-035-207), or IgG1 (Jackson ImmunoResearch Cat# 115-035-205). All secondary antibodies were diluted to a concentration of 100 ng/mL for OspA and BBI39 ELISAs and 25 ng/mL for RABV-G ELISAs. After 2-h RT incubation, plates were washed and developed with 100 µL/well of o-Phenylenediamine Dihydrochloride substrate (Thermo Fisher) for 15 min. The reaction was stopped with 3 M H2SO4. The optical density (OD) was determined at 490 nm (experimental) and 630 nm (background) on a BioTek ELx800 plate reader on Gen5 software to determine the delta values between the experimental and background readings. ELISA data were analyzed as described below.

For stripped antigen ELISAs in Supplementary Fig. 1, antigens and whole virions were first tested for binding with polyclonal rabbit anti-OspA at various concentrations, starting at 800 ng/well. Then, for ELISAs in Fig. 1, all stripped antigens, whole virions, and recombinant proteins were utilized at 500 ng/well. Primary antibodies utilized were polyclonal anti-OspA at 1:50, monoclonal anti-OspA LA-2 at 1:50, and monoclonal anti-RABV-G 1C5 at 1 μg/mL. Secondary antibodies used were anti-Rabbit H + L and anti-mouse IgG-fc at 100 ng/mL. All ELISA steps were done as described above.

Avidity ELISA

For anti-OspA avidity ELISAs, the same methods were utilized for OspA ELISAs, as described above. On Day 3, the primary antibody was removed, and wells were washed 3× with 1× PBST. Then, 100 μL/well of 6 M Urea was added to each well for 15 min. Urea was washed 3× with PBST, and then total IgG secondary (listed above) was added to each well. The rest of the ELISA was carried out as described earlier. An identical plate with the same primary was treated with 1× PBS instead of 6 M urea for 15 min. The area under the curve for urea and PBS-treated plates was calculated using GraphPad Prism 9 software. The Avidity Index (A.I.) was calculated using the following formula: AI = AUC (urea)/AUC (PBS).

Borreliacidal assay

Bb, strain B31, was seeded at 1 × 105 spirochetes/mL in 96-well round-bottom plates with a 1:10 dilution of heat-inactivated mouse sera, 1:10 guinea pig complement sera (Sigma S1639), 100 μg/mL of LA-2 antibody (Absolute Antibody, Ab01070-3.0) (positive control), and diluted in BSK-H complete media for a total of 100 μL. Mouse sera was heat-inactivated at 56 °C for 30 min and tested in duplicate. Borrelial mixtures were incubated at 33 °C for 48 h. 1 µL from each well was added to 1 mL of BSK media in Eppendorf tubes. After 7 days, motile Bb was enumerated under a Nikon dark-field microscope with a Petroff-Hausser counting chamber (Hausser Scientific, Cat#: 3900). Data was analyzed in GraphPad Prism 9 software to determine if mouse sera inhibited bacterial growth.

RABV neutralization by the rapid fluorescent-focus inhibition test (RFFIT)

RFFITs were performed to identify RABV-neutralizing antibodies, as described previously18,26. In brief, mouse serum was heat-inactivated at 56 °C for 30 min. BSR cells were seeded at 30,000 cells/well and cultured in complete DMEM in 96-well flat-bottom plates. Individual mouse serum was diluted 3-fold with a starting dilution of 1:50. The WHO standard of rabies IgG was used at a starting dilution of 2 international units (IU)/mL. After dilution of sera, CVS11, a wild-type strain of RABV, was added to each well at a titer to achieve 90% infection of cells. The virus and antibody mixtures were incubated in a 96-well round-bottom plate for 1 h at 34 °C. After incubation, the antibody/virus mixture was overlaid on BSR cells and incubated at 34 °C for 24 h. Cells were fixed with 80% acetone and stained with FAD stain (see above). The percentage of viral infection was assessed by fluorescent microscopy. The Reed–Muench method was utilized to calculate 50% neutralization titers (IU/mL) by comparison to the W.H.O. standard.

Borrelia burgdorferi challenge by needle injection

Immunized and unimmunized mice were subjected to challenge with Bb, strain B31, by needle route18. Mice were injected with 1 × 105 spirochetes/100 µL intradermally.

At 14 days post-infection, skin (ear), tibiotarsi joints, and hearts were harvested from infected mice aseptically. Tissues were subjected to RNA extraction and qPCR (described below) and culture observation as described previously18.

Blood was also collected from challenged mice and subjected to Western Blot analysis to determine whether each mouse was successfully challenged with Bb. Bb was grown up in 50 mL to 1 × 108 spirochetes/mL. Bacteria were centrifuged at 4000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. Borrelial pellets were washed 5× with 1 mL of PBS. After each wash, the lysates were centrifuged in Eppendorf tubes at 14,000 × g for 1 min Borrelial lysates were subjected to the BCA assay to determine the final concentration. Lysates were diluted to a final concentration of 1.5 µg/10 µL in 1× USB. Aliquots were frozen at −80 °C until used for Western Blot analysis. Lysates were denatured for 10 min at 95 °C with BME and separated as described above in Western Blot methods. Individual mouse serum was diluted to 1:1000 in 5% BSA and added to strips of nitrocellulose membrane with Borrelial lysates transferred on each strip. Serum was incubated ON and further analyzed as described above.

Borrelia burgdorferi challenge by Ixodes scapularis

Groups of 6 mice were immunized as described earlier. On day 49 or 190 post-primary immunization, mice were challenged with infected Ixodes scapularis nymphs (5 ticks/mouse). After 48 h, engorged ticks were collected, and pathogen burden was assessed by qRT-PCR (described below). After 2 weeks of infection, mice were euthanized, and the Bb burden within mouse organs was assessed by qRT-PCR (described below). Skin, heart, and joints were cultured in BSK-H media described above.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Tissues from challenged mice were harvested and placed in 1 mL TRIzol Reagent in 2 mL RNase/DNase-free Omni tubes (Omni International), which contained beads for homogenization. Hearts and skin were collected in Omni tubes with 2.8 µm ceramic beads (Cat#: 19-628D). Joints were collected in Omni tubes with 2.8 metal beads (Cat#: 19-620D). Tubes were homogenized by Omni Bead Ruptor for 99 s. Homogenates were frozen at −80 °C until RNA extraction. RNA was extracted using a TRIzol Reagent phase separation protocol with a PureLink RNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen). The quantity and quality of RNA were measured using NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher).

Bb was quantified by RT-qPCR. FlaB and mouse B-actin primer probes (Thermo Fisher) were designed for use with TaqMan Fast Virus 1-Step Master Mix reagent (Thermo Fisher), using 5 µL of extracted RNA from each mouse organ. FlaB was amplified by forward primer (5′-TTGCTGATCAAGCTCAATATAACCA-3′) and reverse primer (5′-GCATCGCTTTCAGGGTCTCAA-3′) with a probe quenched with FAM fluorescent dye (5′-AGAACAGCTGAAGAGCTTGGAATGCAGCCTGCAAAAATTAACACA-3′). Mouse B-actin was amplified by forward primer (5′-AGAGGGAAATCGTGCGTGAC-3′) and reverse primer (5′-ACGGCCAGGTCATCACTATTG-3′) with a probe quenched with VIC fluorescent dye (5′-CAAAGAGAAGCTGTGCTATGTTGCTCTAGACTTCGAGCAGGAGAT-3′). The reaction was set up for a fast-cycling protocol at 1 cycle for 5 min at 50 °C, 1 cycle for 20 s at 95 °C, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 3 s and 60 °C for 30 s. The reactions were done on a Step One Plus qPCR machine. The CT values of FlaB were normalized to the CT values of mouse beta-actin.

Statistical analysis

For ELISA, log-transformed 50% effective concentration (EC50) values were determined by plotting against the delta OD value (OD (490 nm)-OD (630 nm)) on GraphPad Prism 9 software. For all statistical analyses, one-way ANOVA with post hoc Tukey HSD test was performed on log-transformed data.
