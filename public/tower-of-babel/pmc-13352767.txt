In vitro and in vivo activity of colistin-nitroxoline combination against polymyxin heteroresistant carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae
Authors: Xin Chen, Jianping Jiang, Xiaogang Xu, Xu Zhao, Yijun Deng, Jiachun Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCarbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) has emerged as a critical global health threat, often causing life-threatening infections with limited therapeutic options. Polymyxins are often used as the last-line agent, but its efficacy is limited by heteroresistance-where resistant subpopulations emerge during treatment. To combat this, we evaluated the synergistic activity of colistin combined with nitroxoline against polymyxin heteroresistant (PHR) CRKP subpopulations.ResultsThe prevalence of PHR was 90.9% (10/11 isolates). While colistin or nitroxoline monotherapy failed to eradicate PHR subpopulations, the combination of colistin and nitroxoline achieved complete clearance at reduced concentrations in 3 out of 4 tested isolates. In adaptive laboratory evolution experiments, colistin monotherapy rapidly selected for resistant mutants, with MIC increasing 256-fold by the second passage. In contrast, the combination maintained stable susceptibility over 20 passages, with no detectable MIC increase. N-phenyl-1-naphthylamine and propidium iodide staining, along with scanning electron microscopy, revealed that the combination induced markedly enhanced membrane damage and cellular disruption compared to monotherapies. In a murine peritoneal infection model, combination therapy reduced mortality to 10% versus 30% with colistin monotherapy, although the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.30).ConclusionsPHR is highly prevalent in CRKP and undermines colistin monotherapy. Colistin-nitroxoline combination exhibits potent synergy, suppresses resistance emergence, and shows promise for treating refractory CRKP infections.

Background

Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) were classified as a critical-priority threat on an updated list of priority bacterial pathogens released by the World Health Organization in 2024 [1]. In China, there was a substantial increase in the prevalence of imipenem resistance among Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates, increasing from 2.9% in 2005 to 22.6% in 2022 [2]. Due to increasing resistance rates and limited treatment options, carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP) infections are associated with rising morbidity and mortality, posing a significant challenge to global public health [3].

Despite the increasing availability new β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations globally, polymyxins are still regarded as the last line of defense against CRKP in China. However, the prevalence of polymyxins heteroresistance (PHR) in CRKP has been reported ranging from 8.4% in a large US study to 76.62% locally in China [4, 5]. Heteroresistance (HR) refers to the phenomenon in which a bacterial population contains a majority of antibiotic-susceptible cells coexisting with a minority of resistant subpopulations [6]. Standard antimicrobial susceptibility testing often fails to accurately detect these HR subpopulations (HRS), potentially leading to inappropriate treatment decisions based on misleading susceptibility results, which may result in treatment failure and the dissemination of resistance [7]. Therefore, the use of combination therapy involving two or more antibiotics, rather than polymyxins monotherapy, may be a feasible treatment strategy for patients infected with PHR-CRKP. However, the effectiveness of such combinations against PHR-CRKP isolates remains to be fully elucidated.

Nitroxoline is an oral antimicrobial agent belonging to the 8-hydroxyquinoline class, exhibiting broad-spectrum antibacterial and antifungal activity. Due to its low resistance rates, it has been approved in Germany since the 1960s for the treatment of urinary tract infections and was recommended in the German S3 guidelines in 2017 as one of the treatment options for community-acquired uncomplicated urinary tract infections [8]. Polymyxins act by disrupting the outer membrane (OM) through binding to lipopolysaccharides, while nitroxoline damages the OM via metal ion chelation [9]. Exploiting these complementary mechanisms, we assessed the antibacterial activity of colistin alone and in combination with nitroxoline against PHR strains.

This study investigated PHR in 10 of 11 polymyxin-susceptible CRKP (PS-CRKP) clinical strains by population analysis profiling (PAP), and in vitro and in vivo efficacy of colistin alone and in combination with nitroxoline was evaluated. These findings provide insights into the challenges posed by PHR and may help guide more effective therapeutic strategies to prevent treatment failure.

Materials and methods

Bacterial isolates and antimicrobial susceptibility test

A total of 11 clinical PS-CRKP isolates (CRKP12-130, 17841, 21801, 21811, 21814, 21817, 21819, 22041, 22044, 22046, 22261) were used in this study [10]. These strains were recovered from various clinical specimens, including urine, sputum, and blood, at Huashan Hospital (Shanghai, China). The strains underwent antimicrobial susceptibility testing against carbapenems and colistin using the broth microdilution method in cation-adjusted Mueller-Hinton broth (CAMHB), with interpretation carried out in accordance with the guidelines established by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) [11]. Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 was used as a control for susceptibility assays.

PAP

PHR was determined by PAP as previously described [6]. Briefly, CRKP clinical isolates were inoculated into 3 mL of Mueller-Hinton broth (MHB) (Oxoid, UK) and cultured overnight at 37℃. One hundred microliters of ten-fold serial dilutions of bacterial cell culture (approximately from 1 × 102 to 1 × 108 cfu/mL) were spread onto Mueller–Hinton agar (MHA) (Oxoid, UK) plates containing 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8 or 16 mg/L colistin. Colonies were counted following 24 h of incubation at 37℃. Frequency of resistant cells was calculated as the number of colonies grown on colistin-containing plates divided by the number of colonies grown on colistin-free plates. HR was defined as the presence of a resistant subpopulation exhibiting a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) ≥ 8-fold higher than that of the clinically susceptible dominant population, with detectable frequencies > 1 × 10⁻⁷ and the dominant population remaining within the clinical susceptibility range according to established CLSI breakpoints.

Growth curve analysis

Growth curve analysis was performed to determine the growth of 11 CRKP clinical isolates treated with colistin using the BioScreen C™ automated microbiology system (Labsystems, Finland). Bacterial saline suspensions were prepared from overnight cultures on MHA plates and adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland turbidity standard. These suspensions were diluted 1:100 in MHB, and inoculated on the compatible honeycomb plates. Final volume in each well was 150 µL (~ 1 × 105 cfu). Colistin was distributed into plates with final concentration 2 mg/L, because 2 mg/L was the highest achievable levels in serum for colistin [12]. All isolates were additionally grown in MHB without antibiotics. The BioScreen C™ automated microbiology system was set to 24 h incubation at 37℃ with continuous shaking at 220 rpm and optical density (OD) at 600 nm measured every 30 min. Each sample was tested in seven replicates.

Time-kill assays

The bactericidal activities of colistin and nitroxoline (MetCura Pharmaceuticals Inc., USA) monotherapy and combination therapy were evaluated by time-kill assays against four clinical isolates (CRKP12-130, 21811, 21819, 22044). Three isolates belonged to sequence type 11 (ST11) and one to ST15, both of which represent the predominant clones of CRKP in China [3, 13–15]. These assays were performed in triplicate with a starting inoculum of ~ 106 cfu/mL in a final volume of 20 mL and incubation in a shaking incubator at 37℃ in ambient air. Colistin was tested at 2 mg/L as monotherapy, while nitroxoline monotherapy concentrations were 8 or 16 mg/L. In combination treatments, colistin was maintained at 1 mg/L alongside nitroxoline concentrations of 0.5, 1, 2, or 4 mg/L. Aliquots were obtained from each tube at 0, 2, 4, 6, 12, and 24 h after inoculation and were serially diluted for determining viable counts. The samples were plated onto MHA plates in triplicate and incubated at 37℃ for 18 h. The number of colonies that formed was counted. Bactericidal activity was defined as ≥ 3-log10 reduction in the total number of cfu/mL relative to the initial inoculum [16]. Synergistic activity was defined as ≥ 2-log10 decrease in bacterial colony counts (cfu/mL) achieved by the antibiotic combination relative to the most active single agent [17]. Time-kill curves were constructed by plotting mean colony counts (log10 cfu/mL) versus time.

Adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE)

To explore the evolutionary dynamics of PHR strains under exposure to colistin or nitroxoline monotherapy, as well as colistin-nitroxoline combination therapy, we conducted ALE experiments using strain CRKP12-130 with a serial passage method [18]. Briefly, CRKP12-130 was inoculated into Luria-Bertani agar (LBA) medium, incubated overnight at 37 °C, and adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland turbidity standard. These suspensions were diluted 1:100 in 2×CAMHB. Fifty microliters of bacterial suspension were inoculated into 96-well plates containing equal volumes of different concentrations of antibiotics. For monotherapy groups, either colistin or nitroxoline was prepared in serial two-fold dilutions ranging from 0.06 to 128 mg/L. In combination groups, fixed concentrations of nitroxoline (0.5, 1, 2 or 4 mg/L) in each row of 96-well plates were combined with colistin in serial dilutions ranging from 0.002 to 4 mg/L. Cell growth was monitored after incubation overnight at 37 °C and populations with the highest drug concentration were selected to be transferred to a new 96-well plate as described above (the maximum drug concentrations of serially diluted antibiotic in each row were higher than the current resistance level of the evolving strain against the respective antibiotic). This serial passage process was carried out daily for 20 transfers (Fig. 1A). The MICs of colistin and nitroxoline for the evolved strains were determined using broth microdilution.

Membranes permeability

OM and inner membrane (IM) permeabilities were measured using N-phenyl-1-naphthylamine (NPN) and propidium iodide (PI) staining [19, 20]. NPN fluorescence increases upon OM disruption, while PI enters the cytoplasm and intercalates with DNA, resulting in a marked increase in fluorescence upon IM disruption. Briefly, logarithmic phase CRKP12-130 cells were treated for 30 min with monotherapy (2 mg/L colistin or 8 mg/L nitroxoline) or combination therapy (2 mg/L colistin and 4 mg/L nitroxoline), washed twice with PBS, resuspended, and then incubated with PI (final concentration 5 µM) or NPN (final concentration 20 µM) at 37 °C in dark for 30 min. Untreated cells were used as the control. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a SpectraMax® iD5 multifunctional microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA) at excitation of 535 nm and emission of 617 nm for PI and excitation of 350 nm, and emission of 420 nm for NPN. Experiments were carried out three times.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

SEM was used to further investigate the effects of colistin monotherapy and colistin-nitroxoline combination therapy on bacterial OM morphology. Briefly, 2 mL LB broth of CRKP12-130 suspension (5 × 108 cfu/mL) containing 2 mg/L of colistin, 8 mg/L nitroxoline or combination of 2 mg/L of colistin and 4 mg/L nitroxoline, were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C, pelleted, and resuspended in 50 µL saline. The suspension was dropped onto a glass slide and air-dried at room temperature. After drying, the sample was fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight. The fixed samples were washed three times with 0.1 M phosphate buffer, 15 min each time, to remove residual fixative. Samples were dehydrated stepwise using ethanol gradients (50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%) for 15 min each, then transferred to ethanol: isoamyl acetate mixtures (2:1 and 1:2) for 15 min each, and followed by three washes with 100% isoamyl acetate for 15 min each. Samples were dried in a critical point dryer before being coated with platinum, and then observed by SEM (Hitachi SU8010, Hitachi High-Technologies, Japan).

Murine intraperitoneal infection model

Murine survival rates were used to assess the efficiency of the colistin-nitroxoline combination in vivo. Eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were intraperitoneally injected with 200 µL of CRKP12-130 bacterial suspension (~ 3 × 10⁸ cfu). Saline, 2 mg/kg of colistin with or without 10 mg/kg of nitroxoline were treated after 1 h of infection, [21] with 10 mice per group. Nitroxoline was administered via oral gavage, while colistin and saline were administered intraperitoneally. All treatments were given twice daily at 12-hour intervals for three consecutive days. Murine survival rates and body weight changes were monitored daily up to day 5. The Kaplan-Meier analysis and the log-rank test were adopted to analyze CRKP mortality rates. All mice were provided by Shanghai Lab Animal Research Center (Shanghai, China) and housed in standard filter-top cages under SPF environment with controlled conditions (24 ± 2 ℃, 12/12-h light/dark cycle, and 60 ± 10% relative humidity). On the fifth day after infection, all surviving mice from each group were euthanized by cervical dislocation.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For growth curves generated by the automated microbiology system, results are presented as mean ± range. Statistical significance was assessed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for membrane permeability assays and paired t-test for murine body weight changes. Results are denoted as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Results

PHR profiles

Among the 11 PS-CRKP strains, 81.8% (9/11) harbored blaKPC-2 and 72.7% (8/11) were classified into ST11 (Table 1). Ten isolates exhibited PHR, with colistin MICs ranging from 0.5 to 1 mg/L (Fig. 2). PAP revealed that subpopulations of these strains could grow at colistin concentration up to 8× MIC or higher. Among 10 tested PHR strains, the frequency of HRS observed on 2 mg/L colistin plates consistently ranged from 10-7 to 10-5 (Fig. 2). Strain 21801 demonstrated full susceptibility to colistin.

Growth curve analysis

Growth of the non-PHR strain 21801 was inhibited by colistin (Fig. 3A). The seven experimental replicates for each of the 10 PHR strains exhibited significant variability (Fig. 3A). This variation arose because the HRS emergence frequency on 2 mg/L colistin plates consistently ranged from 10− 7 to 10− 5, meaning that an inoculum of 105 cfu could not guarantee the inclusion of HRS with colistin MICs exceeding 2 mg/L, as demonstrated by our PAP analysis. Despite this variation, all tested PHR strains demonstrated measurable growth (Fig. 3A).

Time-kill assays

The MICs of colistin and nitroxoline against four isolates used for combination time-kill assays were 0.5–1 mg/L and 4–8 mg/L, respectively. Colistin monotherapy at 2 mg/L initially reduced bacterial loads within the first 2 h. However, rapid regrowth occurred in all isolates, attributable to the selective outgrowth of pre-existing heteroresistant subpopulations. By 24 h, three isolates had rebounded to 109~1010 cfu/mL. Nitroxoline alone showed no bactericidal activity. The combination of 1 mg/L colistin with 2 or 4 mg/L nitroxoline led to complete eradication of the bacterial population in three isolates, demonstrating synergistic activity (Fig. 3B), except the ST15 isolate 21819.

ALE

Colistin monotherapy induced rapid resistance emergence, with colistin MIC rising 256-fold by the second exposure and reaching 512-fold by the sixth (Fig. 1B). In contrast, in colistin-nitroxoline combination group, colistin MIC remained stable over 20 passages, indicating nitroxoline effectively suppressed HRS proliferation and prevented evolution to full resistance. Nitroxoline MIC remained constant, suggesting low propensity for resistance development in prolonged use (Fig. 1B).

Membranes permeability

We observed that colistin permeabilized the OM and IM of strain CRKP12-130, as indicated by the increased NPN and PI fluorescence compared to PBS and untreated controls (Fig. 4A). In contrast, nitroxoline alone showed no significant effect at 30 min. Colistin-nitroxoline combination significantly enhanced NPN and PI signals beyond colistin monotherapy (P < 0.05), demonstrating synergistic disruption of both OM and IM.

SEM

SEM revealed that nitroxoline monotherapy did not markedly alter cell-surface structure of strain CRKP12-130 (Fig. 4B iii). Colistin treatment induced some surface protrusions (Fig. 4B ii), and colistin-nitroxoline combination led to extensive protrusions and morphological disruption (Fig. 4B iv). This observation aligns with the increased fluorescence signals in NPN and PI staining in combination group, suggesting a synergistic membrane-disrupting effect.

Murine intraperitoneal infection model

Given the promising in vitro efficacy of the colistin-nitroxoline combination, we evaluated its therapeutic potential in a murine model of abdominal infection established with approximately 3 × 10⁸ cfu of strain CRKP12-130. Colistin monotherapy at 2 mg/kg resulted in a 30% mortality rate (3/10), whereas combination therapy with colistin (2 mg/kg) and nitroxoline (10 mg/kg) reduced mortality to 10% (1/10), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.30 by log-rank test; Fig. 5A). Concurrently, body weight analysis revealed comparable outcomes: by day 5, body weight relative to baseline was 89.5% in the monotherapy group and 91.1% in the combination group (p = 0.19; Fig. 5B).

Discussion

The prevalence of PHR in CRKP isolates has been reported to vary across different regions of China. A multicenter study reported an overall prevalence of 6.2% for PHR among 455 clinical CRKP isolates collected between 2016 and 2019 from six tertiary hospitals in China [22]. Meanwhile, two single-center studies conducted in the eastern region of China reported similar PHR prevalence rates of 76.62% and 69.7%, respectively [5, 23]. Additionally, 38.1% of PHR isolates evolved into polymyxin fully resistant strains upon exposure to polymyxins, resulting in treatment failure in severe cases [23]. This variation in prevalence suggests that HR may be influenced by the genetic diversity of pathogens across different regions, and may also be related to differences in the criteria used for its definition. The widespread presence of pre-existing polymyxin HRS in CRKP significantly increases the risk of polymyxin resistance and treatment failure, leading to serious clinical consequences.

PAP is recognized as the gold standard for detecting HR, yet a unified judgement criterion for HR remains lacking. In 2015, El-Halfawy et al. established a relatively clear definition: HR manifests when subpopulations of an isolate exhibit divergent susceptibility to a given antimicrobial agent, with PAP demonstrating growth of HRS at antibiotic concentrations ≥ 8×MIC of the dominant population [24]. In 2019, Band et al. established more stringent diagnostic thresholds for PHR, defining it as the presence of HRS capable of growth at polymyxin concentrations of either 4 mg/L or 8 mg/L (1× or 2× the EUCAST clinical breakpoint), with a minimum detection frequency of 1 × 10− 6 through PAP [25]. Also in 2019, Andersson refined HR definitions by considering four factors: (i) origin and clonality of resistant subpopulations (monoclonal vs. multiclonal), (ii) minimum fold increase in resistance, (iii) frequency of resistant cells (recommended to report at ≥ 8×MIC), and (iv) stability of resistance (stable if resistance persists over 50 generations without antibiotic) [6]. The most widely used standard combines El-Halfawy and Andersson’s criteria, defining HR as HRS bacteria growing on plates with 8×MIC antibiotic at a frequency ≥ 1 × 10− 7. Stable heteroresistance is caused by mutations (e.g., point mutations, insertion sequence insertions, small deletions) with low or no fitness cost. In contrast, resistance mutations that impose a high fitness cost may revert in the absence of antibiotics. Second-site compensatory mutations can restore fitness at the expense of reduced resistance. Additionally, unstable resistance can result from spontaneous gene amplifications, which are prone to loss due to their high fitness cost and genetic instability [6].

In this study, the PHR of 11 clinical PS-CRKP isolates were evaluated on MHA plates containing different concentrations of colistin. Using the most common criterion (HRS bacteria growing on plates with 8×MIC and growth frequency ≥ 1 × 10− 7), 90.9% (10/11) of the strains were identified as HR, indicating that HR is widespread in this population. The HRS of CRKP12-130 was serially passaged 10 times on colistin-free agar. All derivative isolates remained colistin-resistant, as previously observed [26]. Next-generation sequencing of 16 randomly selected clones revealed that all had acquired resistance-conferring mutations: 13 carried phoQ mutations, and 3 exhibited insertional inactivation of mgrB, both well-established mechanisms of colistin resistance [26].

Growth curve analysis of colistin revealed that all PHR isolates exhibited varying degrees of bacterial proliferation at 2 mg/L colistin exposure, a phenomenon potentially attributable to the presence of HRS. These findings highlighted a critical clinical concern: standard antimicrobial susceptibility testing may miss HRS, leading to the misclassification of PHR-CRKP isolates as PS-CRKP. Subsequent colistin monotherapy could inadvertently promote the selective expansion of low-frequency HRS under antibiotic pressure, ultimately driving resistance evolution and therapeutic failure. Therefore, even when employing colistin-based combination regimens for PS-CRKP infections, clinicians must maintain vigilance against undetected PHR and implement continuous monitoring of colistin treatment efficacy.

This study also investigated the antibacterial effects of colistin alone and in combination with nitroxoline against four PHR strains. Time-kill assays showed that 2 mg/L colistin rapidly killed the susceptible population within the first 2 h but had limited efficacy against the HRS. The colistin-nitroxoline combination significantly enhanced antibacterial efficacy, achieving complete bacteria eradication even at reduced concentrations (1 mg/L colistin combined with 2 or 4 mg/L nitroxoline) in 75% (3/4) tested isolates. This low-dose combination minimizes colistin exposure, thereby reducing the risk of nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity. The failure to clear isolate 21819 with colistin-nitroxoline combination may be due to its distinct genetic background, as it was the only ST15 strain among the isolates tested. ST15 has emerged as an increasingly prevalent CRKP sequence type in China [27, 28]. ST15 CRKP was reported to have virulence similar to ST11-KL47 hypervirulent-CRKP, and higher than that of ST11-KL10 and ST307 both in vitro and in vivo experiments [29]. Moreover, ST15 strains showed higher levels of serum resistance than ST11 and ST307, and genomic and transcriptomic analyses further indicated that this pheonotype was closely associated with iron metabolism-related pathways [29]. These characteristics may contribute to enhanced bacterial fitness under the combined stress imposed by colistin and nitroxoline. However, this interpretation should be made cautiously because only a single ST15 isolate was included in the present study. Future studies will therefore incorporate a larger collection of ST15 as well as other prevalent STs to determine whether the efficacy of the colistin-nitroxoline combination is influenced by ST-associated genetic backgrounds. Polymyxin B and minocycline were reported to exhibit synergistic activity against four of five CRKP strains tested, including two with high-level polymyxin B resistance (one harboring blaOXA-48 and the other harboring blaKPC-2), but not against the polymyxin B susceptible KPC-3-producing strain [30]. These findings suggest that bacterial genetic background and underlying resistance mechanisms may be more predictive than phenotypic susceptibility in determining the synergistic potential of combination therapy.

ALE experiment further confirmed that bacteria rapidly developed colistin resistance after only two exposures to colistin alone, consistent with clinical observations of colistin resistance emergence [31]. In contrast, the colistin-nitroxoline combination maintained colistin MICs at baseline, and nitroxoline sensitivity remained stable after 20 continuous exposures, indicating a low propensity for resistance development.

Based on nitroxoline’s metal-chelating properties, we speculated that nitroxoline may potentiate colistin through chelation of OM-stabilizing metal ions, leading to membrane destabilization and enhanced bactericidal effects [32]. The synergistic disruption of OM was further supported by membrane permeability assays and SEM in this study. However, these assays alone cannot fully elucidate the molecular interaction between colistin and nitroxoline, and further mechanistic studies (e.g., genomic or proteomic analyses) are required.

However, combination therapy showed limited benefits in vivo, possibly due to several factors. First of all, while the prevalence of PHR remains high, its clinical transition to full resistance occurs at a moderate frequency (28–38%) [5, 23], closely mirroring the 30% mortality rate observed in our murine model’s colistin monotherapy group. Given the baseline 70% survival rate with colistin single therapy, the therapeutic margin for further improvement through combination regimens appears inherently constrained. Secondly, immune system may suppress the proliferation of HRS in mice. Future studies could employ immunosuppressed mice models induced by cyclophosphamide for in vivo experiments. Additionally, nitroxoline has a short plasma half-life (T1/2 ~1.5 h, in mice) and is rapidly excreted into urine [33]. There is limited systemic exposure of the drug for efficacy. To address it, nitroxoline derivatives such as ASN-1733 have been developed, which retains antibacterial activity and improves pharmacokinetics [34]. ASN-1733 binds metal ions, disrupting lipopolysaccharide and bacterial membranes, leading to cell death. In murine models of urinary tract, thigh, abdominal, and sepsis infections, ASN-1733 significantly reduced bacterial loads, improved survival, and alleviated symptoms. Future studies should investigate ASN-1733 combined with colistin to assess synergistic effects and safety.

Currently, research on nitroxoline combination therapies is limited. In 2022, Osei-Owusu et al. first reported that nitroxoline combined with ciprofloxacin showed varying degrees of synergy against five diarrheagenic bacteria [35]. In 2023, He Peng et al. found that nitroxoline may act as a metal β-lactamase inhibitor, and its combination with meropenem effectively treated urinary tract infections caused by NDM-1-producing E. coli [36]. In 2025, Cacace et al. demonstrated that nitroxoline not only enhances polymyxin sensitivity in susceptible strains but also restores polymyxin susceptibility in resistant strains, reducing the required polymyxin dose for treatment [37]. While consistent with prior evidence of synergy, our study uniquely demonstrates that the colistin-nitroxoline combination effectively eliminates colistin HRS, which are prone to undetected expansion during monotherapy and represent a critical challenge for clinical management, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of its therapeutic potential.

There are some limitations with this study. The number of isolates studied is modest, which may limit the generalisability of the findings. Future work will include evaluation using a larger and more clinically diverse collection of PHR-CRKP isolates to further validate the generalisability of the observed colistin-nitroxoline synergistic activity. Genomic analyses of the ALE-evolved isolates were not included in the present study and remain to be characterized in future work.

Conclusion

PHR is common in CRKP strains, and the presence of HRS significantly reduces the bactericidal efficacy of colistin in vitro. The combination of colistin and nitroxoline exhibits synergistic antibacterial effects in vitro by increasing the OM and IM permeability of K. pneumoniae and effectively inhibits the evolution of HRS. However, this combination did not show significant therapeutic advantages in the murine intraperitoneal infection model of CRKP.
