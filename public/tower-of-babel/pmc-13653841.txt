Integrating optimal patient-centered care: patient- and family-reported experiences after complex treatment for rare diseases
Authors: Frederiek L. Tijssens, Philippine Kies, Lissy de Ridder, Anne P. J. de Pagter, Jeanine M. M. van Klink
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough life expectancy after complex treatments for rare diseases during childhood has increased, long-term medical, psychosocial and societal impacts remain for children and their families. Evaluating patient and family experiences is essential for the development and optimization of integrated patient-centered, value-based care pathways. This study aims to evaluate patient- and family-reported experiences across three follow-up care pathways for complex treatment of rare diseases.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted among patients/families in follow-up care after (1) fetal and/or neonatal intensive care treatment; (2) pediatric hematopoietic stem cell transplantation for non-malignant conditions; and (3) cardiac intervention for congenital heart disease. A combination of validated patient-reported experience measures (PREMs) was used to capture experiences with complete integrated care pathways regarding information provision, integrated care collaboration, communication and comprehensive care. Descriptive statistics were used to analyze quantitative ratings, whereas free-text responses were analyzed using thematic analysis.ResultsBetween April 2024 and June 2025, 70 families completed the PREMs. Likert-scale ratings, as well as an analysis of 286 free-text responses, revealed positive evaluations of relationship building, the humanistic approach and communication across all pathways. The identified areas of improvement varied across care pathways and included comprehensive (long-term) information provision and screening and support for the psychosocial impact on patients as well as their families.ConclusionThrough shared learning across care pathways, insights from patient and family experiences guide patient- and family-centered, value-based care pathway optimization to ultimately improve outcomes and quality of life for children and their families.Graphical abstract

Introduction

With medical advances, survival and life expectancy after complex treatment for rare diseases during pregnancy, infancy or childhood continue to increase [1–4]. Although treatments aim to cure the initial disease, many children and their families remain vulnerable to acute and long-term consequences [3–5], which are often insufficiently addressed [6–8]. This impact extends beyond medical outcomes and includes psychological, neurocognitive, relational and societal consequences [8–10]. These factors may persist throughout life, affecting quality of life and participation in society [11–13].

Research suggests that children treated with complex treatments for rare conditions face similar long-term outcomes, including neurocognitive difficulties [14–16], attention and stimulus processing problems [9, 11, 17, 18], and a need for extra support at school [19–22]. However, effective prevention, early detection, screening and management of these outcomes often remain insufficiently embedded and thus fragmented in many care pathways for complex treatments [7, 23–25]. This highlights the need for dedicated “integrated follow-up care pathways”, such as structured long-term follow-up providing screening and specialized care for the health and well-being of patients and their families. These pathways require multidisciplinary collaboration both within and beyond the hospital, as families’ care needs vary across developmental stages. The optimal integration of care elements across disciplines and age groups is therefore essential [26].

The development of such pathways requires a person-centered approach, as reflected in patient- and family-centered care (PFCC) and value-based healthcare (VBHC) [27, 28]. In pediatrics, PFCC promotes partnership and collaboration with patients and families by incorporating their experiences, preferences and values into care processes [28–31]. In doing so, care can be tailored to families’ needs [29, 30]. Similarly, VBHC recognizes patient and family experiences among three key healthcare outcomes [27, 32, 33]. Both prioritize integrating patient and family experiences as fundamental to the quality of care [28].

These experiences are typically captured through patient-reported experience measures (PREMs), which evaluate care processes, including information provision, communication with healthcare professionals (HCPs), shared decision-making and consultation timing [34–37]. PREMs facilitate patient engagement, personalized care and improved communication [30, 35–37]. Moreover, positive healthcare experiences are associated with better treatment adherence, reduced healthcare utilization and improved clinical and patient-reported outcomes [38–41]. Above all, systematically evaluating PREMs provides essential insights into what patients and families value, identifies key areas for quality improvement and enables benchmarking of care pathway quality from patient and family centeredness [34, 35, 38, 42–45]. However, previous studies on PREMs in pediatric care have mainly assessed a single consultation or inpatient phase, particularly in acute or chronic care settings [46–49], rather than the complete patient journey of an integrated follow-up care pathway [26, 47, 50]. Moreover, despite shared challenges, no studies have explicitly compared patient experiences across multiple care pathways for complex treatments to accommodate joint improvement initiatives.

Therefore, this study aims to compare patients’ and families’ experiences with integrated follow-up care pathways after complex treatment for rare diseases in three nationwide tertiary pediatric referral centers for fetal therapy and neonatal intensive care, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) for non-malignant conditions and cardiac intervention for congenital heart disease (CHD). Through knowledge sharing across care pathways, overarching building blocks for an integrated PFC-VBHC pathway for highly complex treatments can be identified to ensure optimal patient journeys and comprehensive care.

Methods

Study setting and participants

This cross-sectional study at the Willem-Alexander Children’s Hospital of the Leiden University Medical Center (LUMC) is a collaboration between three existing expertise centers: (1) fetal therapy and/or treatment at the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU); (2) pediatric HSCT for non-malignant diseases; and (3) cardiac intervention in the first year of life for CHD. All three centers run an integrated follow-up care pathway with periodic risk-based screening on the basis of patient-reported outcome measures. All families receive structured follow-up appointments with their specialist (neonatologist, hematologist or cardiologist) at intervals of every 1, 2 or 3 years, depending on the life stage. As the three pathways are oriented toward similar (long-term) outcomes, they work in similar multidisciplinary care networks. Families are regularly offered consultations with a psychologist, social worker, physiotherapist or other HCPs on indication. There are some organizational differences, such as the duration of follow-up. More information on the present follow-up care pathways within the LUMC is provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Between April 2024 and June 2025, patients and/or their caregivers were eligible if they had previously had at least one follow-up appointment within the above-described care pathways. For this study, people who did not speak Dutch were not eligible. Participants were recruited via two routes: first, the study invitation was included in the mailing for the communal conference day of the three care pathways and sent to all attending families (N = 96). Second, a digital newsletter including the study invitation was sent to all patients who received follow-up care from 2021 to 2023 [n = 1908 NICU; 187 HSCT; 834 cardiology (CARDIO)]. Finally, a bulletin of this study was shown on the waiting room information screens. Everyone who registered (regardless of the recruitment strategy) was sent a study information letter and informed consent form. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [51] and was approved by the institutional review board of the Medical Ethics Committee Leiden The Hague Delft (NL20.181).

Patient-reported experience measure

To assess families’ experiences with all integrated follow-up care pathways after complex treatments regardless of the underlying condition, the multidisciplinary research team of adult and pediatric medical specialists, psychologists, nurse experts and researchers adapted a validated general childcare PREM of the Dutch Integrated Childcare Association [52]. The original instrument served as a structural foundation: its core domains were retained, while adaptations were made to better fit the study’s aim to evaluate the complete patient journey within the current complex practice of the integrated care pathways. As no single PREM fully captured this, adaptations were based on several existing cocreations of PREMs from comparable patient populations [35–37, 53–57]. Items were added to reflect what is important to the present patient population, whereas other items were deleted if they addressed care elements not present in the current care pathways. After family representatives pilot tested the PREM on usability, clarity and completeness [57, 58], it was finalized in a consensus meeting. The PREM includes four demographic questions covering the treatment received, patient age and gender and respondent type (caregiver, patient, etc.). It also includes 21 Likert-scale questions (ranging from 1—totally disagree to 5—totally agree) addressing care aspects such as information provision or comprehensive care provision. Four open-ended questions were answered on the basis of their Likert-scale responses, along with one final open-ended question for additional comments. A version with adjusted, age-appropriate language was created for 12- to 18-year-old patients. The PREM (translated from Dutch) is provided in Supplementary Material 2.

Study procedure

After signing the informed consent form, participants were invited to complete the PREM by post (for conference day attendees) or digitally (for those registered through the newsletter or bulletin). The caregivers of twins received one questionnaire for both children jointly, as they had joint follow-up appointments. Families with a ≥ 12-year-old child were sent one PREM for the child and one for the caregiver(s), since studies have reported consistent differences between child- and proxy-reported experiences [37, 54]. PREM responses were encrypted into Castor-EDC before further analysis.

Analysis

Descriptive statistics were provided using SPSS (version 29.00). Participant demographics and question ratings were summarized per care pathway using medians and ranges for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. To evaluate the most positively and negatively rated Likert-scale questions, the top- and bottom-box scores were calculated per question. An item’s top-box score is the joint proportion of respondents selecting response options “5—strongly agree” or “4—somewhat agree”. The bottom-box score reflects the proportion of respondents choosing either “1—strongly disagree” or “2—somewhat disagree”. Items with higher top-box (5 or 4) scores were considered the most positively evaluated. High bottom-box (1 or 2) scores indicated more negative evaluations and were used to clearly distinguish between true negative ratings and merely low positive or high neutral ratings.

The free-text answers were used to deepen the understanding of why aspects of care were experienced as positive or negative in addition to the quantitative data. Themes, sub-themes and supporting quotes were chosen following the six stages of thematic analysis by Braun and Clarke using Atlas.ti (version 24) [59].

For NICU respondents, Q15 (HCPs spend enough time), 13 (feel comfortable with HCPs) and 12 (HCPs speak understandably) received the highest percentage of the top box, 100%. The highest bottom-box percentages were observed for Q6 (wish for designated care provider) at 58.8% and Q5 (have a designated care provider) at 50.0%. This shows that half of the respondents do not have a designated care provider, yet more than half indicated that they do not wish to have one. This may reflect that families feel adequately served by their existing healthcare contacts. This finding is supported by the overarching top-box score of feeling comfortable with the HCPs and the qualitative findings in which families expressed trust in the quality of the HCPs and positive experiences with reaching the right HCP when needed.

For HSCT respondents, Q13 (feel comfortable with HCPs) had a top-box score of 94.5%, and Q14 (can discuss concerns and wishes freely with HCPs), 15 (HCPs spend enough time) and 16 (trust in quality HCPs) had top-box scores of 94.4%. The highest bottom-box scores were found for Q6 (wish for designated care provider) at 23.6% and Q4 (given support for adult care transition) at 16.7%. Given that the median age of the patients was 10.5 years, with a minimum of 2 years, the transition to adult care was not applicable to most participants.

For the CARDIO respondents, the aspects of care that were experienced the most positively were Q13 (feel comfortable with HCPs) and 14 (can discuss concerns and wishes freely with HCPs), with 94.4% top-box scores for both. The highest percentage of bottom-box scores was for Q20 (HCPs pay attention to children’s mental health), with 33.3%, followed by Q1 (information on long-term effects) and Q21 (HCPs pay attention to complete living situations, e.g., family and friends, school, and home functioning), with a bottom-box score of 27.8%.

In summary, three aspects of care were the most positively experienced items in multiple care pathways: Q13 (feel comfortable with HCPs) in all three groups and Q15 (HCPs spend enough time) and Q14 (can discuss concerns and wishes freely with HCPs) in two of the three groups. With respect to the aspects of care that were mostly not experienced by the respondents, there is more disparity between the care pathways. Only Q6 (wish for designated care provider) had one of the highest bottom-box scores in two of the three groups.

Information provision

Free-text responses further confirmed that people often missed information, specifically on the long-term comprehensive consequences; however, it was helpful to receive both explanations during visits and information through brochures: “At the time, we were informed by doctors and nurses about possible consequences, also through brochures. However, these were the ‘major’ consequences, as I remember them. Less known is disrupted sensory processing and its signs. I think especially the latter would be important to mention” (mother of a 6-year-old; NICU).

Care communication and coordination

In the care pathway, many HCPs have to collaborate across multiple organizations. What seems to be most important is that the families speak to the appropriate HCP, who is well informed about their situation and that this process is transparent to the family: “I’m very satisfied with how information was shared with other HCPs (hospitals). The information that was shared was also visible to me, which is great” (mother of a 1-year-old; NICU). Most families find it easy to get in contact when they have questions, whereas some say it is not. This is an important barrier to finding appropriate care: “It’s very easy reach the HCPs within LUMC, but outside LUMC, it’s a lot of hassle through the general practitioner (GP). We are told (by LUMC HCPs) where to go, but then completely get left to our own devices” (mother of 11-year-old twins; NICU).

A barrier to cross-organizational communication is a lack of knowledge about complex treatments and their consequences in primary and secondary care. This is due to the absence of a unified information transfer system and limited expertise outside the specialized center to interpret the child’s medical history: “Especially when our child was still being monitored at the child health clinic, we strongly felt that the pediatrician in place was not well informed about his heart condition. The LUMC did send the information, but the knowledge was lacking at the health clinic. This could be improved, as it sometimes causes a lot of misunderstanding during these appointments” (mother of a 6-year-old; CARDIO). “The GP is often not aware of what’s happening at the LUMC. I always have to tell my story again” (patient, 28 years old; HSCT).

Humanistic

The respondents reflected that when the HCPs actively listened and worked together with the caregivers as equal partners, they felt taken seriously and understood: “The feeling that you’re working together to care for your child is a very comforting feeling” (mother of a 9-year-old; HSCT). Important prerequisites to building such mutual understanding and trust are kindness, empathy and compassion. Altogether, this humanistic approach helps us to forge a positive partnership for family-centered care and shared decision-making: “What I mostly remember is the empathy and clarity with which our sons’ diagnosis was communicated to us. It was so comforting to be involved in a situation where as parents, you’ve lost all control and to hear that the doctors were consulting with each other and genuinely wanted to understand our story. That bit of humanity toward us as a family definitely helped us process our story and allows us to look back on some very valuable moments” (mother of 5-year-old twins; NICU). On the other hand, a few families mentioned less positive experiences: “More attention is given to my child as a medical ‘object’ rather than as a person, in my opinion” (mother of a 4-year-old; NICU).

Comprehensive

The respondents stressed the importance of attention to and support for the psychosocial impact of a complex treatment on the child but also the parents, siblings and other surroundings, such as school: “I’m happy with the improvements on the mental health front, but there’s still room for improvement. More attention should be given to the whole family and the impact of a hospital stay on the family. More preventive measures, such as trauma processing or prevention (for example, a ‘recovery kit’ after treatment with similar materials for at home)” (parents of a 1-year-old; CARDIO).

Additional care

The respondents mentioned that they wanted more extensive care with more guidance in finding appropriate support beyond the care pathways. People described having difficulty navigating the healthcare landscape or feeling “let go of” and “lost” after the inpatient period: “Within the walls of LUMC (nine weeks of treatment and subsequent visits), everything was great. However, the recovery took much longer, and support, guidance, and assistance for parents, school, family, and friends were lacking. That has an impact on a child” (father of a 14-year-old; HSCT).

Logistics

Logistics include comments on consultation timing and location in alignment with care needs (close to home when possible), having enough time and consultations running smoothly: “Appointments go smoothly, although they sometimes take a long time. It’s nice that everything is scheduled on the same day” (mother of a 9-year-old; CARDIO).

Discussion

This study is the first to evaluate patient and family experiences across three integrated care pathways following complex treatment for rare diseases, deliberately crossing the boundaries of siloed, organ-specific disciplines. By capturing the complete patient journey rather than isolated clinical encounters and by integrating all multidisciplinary care within each pathway, this study moves beyond traditional disease-specific frameworks. This not only identifies overarching building blocks for PFCC but also creates unique opportunities for shared learning and synergy across conditions that are rarely studied together.

The results of the quantitative data revealed that most families positively evaluated follow-up care. Factors that contributed to positive experiences were largely equivalent across care pathways. These were “to feel comfortable, have enough time and feel free to discuss all concerns and wishes with HCPs”. In contrast, the points for improvement differed across the three care pathways, showing opportunities for shared learning from one another’s care organization.

The qualitative data further emphasized the importance of a humanistic approach and relationship building among patients, family and HCPs. Both the free-text responses and the bottom-box scores pointed to two key areas for improvement. First, information provisions, especially regarding long-term consequences, were missed. Second, families mentioned a wish for more comprehensive screening and care for neurocognitive development, psychosocial well-being and the practical impact on the child, parents, siblings and wider surroundings such as school. An additional area of improvement not captured by the Likert-scale questions was the desire for more guidance in finding additional care outside the hospital. In line with similar studies, families described spending considerable time navigating the healthcare network and struggling to find the appropriate care for their additional support needs by themselves [24, 61, 62]. This is costly, since poorer psychological well-being among children and parents is associated with greater care consumption [63–65] and developmental delays [66–69].

Taken together, these results are in line with those of previous international studies on complex diseases [38, 44, 61, 62], as well as studies on pediatric cancer [70, 71]. The importance of family-HCP interactions, including providing good information and taking the time to answer questions, was among the main determinants of positive experiences in pediatric surgical care [38]. Furthermore, feeling understood and taken seriously in the care for your child were determinants of positive experiences in two neonatal care studies in Scandinavia [44, 62] and one study in pediatric hemophilia care in the Netherlands [61]. These studies also stress the importance of better information provision and more comprehensive care [38, 61, 62]. Interestingly, the field of (pediatric) oncology demonstrates that structured integrated long-term follow-up care is feasible at scale [72–74]. Through standardized European outcome sets and harmonized follow-up guidelines, multidisciplinary teams can systematically monitor physical, psychosocial and neurocognitive consequences. Insights from this field could inform the development of coordinated multidisciplinary care in other complex pediatric care pathways.

From a life course perspective, the identified follow-up needs and experiences with care are likely shaped by the timing of care relative to both the child’s developmental stage and the time since treatment [75, 76]. In the early phase, families are often in an acute adjustment phase, in which they manage high levels of uncertainty and distress. Care needs mainly center around emotional support, understanding the condition, practical caregiving and daily life adjustments. Research reports that current care for these needs is often lacking or, on the other hand, risks information overload [77–80]. These findings highlight the need for stage-specific patient- and family-centered practical and psychosocial support to foster caregiver resilience, tailored communication and structured information provision.

This perspective reinforces the importance of viewing follow-up care as a longitudinal process. Child development is a cumulative process in which unmet neurocognitive and psychosocial needs at early developmental stages can compound over time and shape future health trajectories [75, 76]. Follow-up services should anticipate that support needs evolve over time and provide timely access to appropriate expertise rather than relying on reactive referrals from isolated clinical encounters. In this context, families’ wishes for better information about long-term consequences and more comprehensive psychosocial care likely reflect retrospective recognition of unmet care needs in their patient journeys. Future research should examine how follow-up experiences and unmet needs vary across developmental stages and time since treatment and how care pathways can better anticipate these changing priorities.

A strength of this study is the combination of quantitative and qualitative data. The Likert-scale items enable benchmarking to evaluate quality improvement initiatives. Additionally, the free-text responses allow for a comprehensive understanding of what contributes to patients’ and family members’ experiences. Although objective questions about whether certain events did or did not occur are useful, they do not fully capture which aspects patients and their families value most. Some adjustments to the PREM could yield even richer information about what contributes to the quality of care and positive experiences for patients and families.

Moreover, there may be some recruitment and selection bias, as involvement or affinity with the hospital could have positively or negatively influenced the results and their generalizability. Second, the exclusion of families who do not speak Dutch limits generalizability and may have biased the findings toward more positive experiences. A language barrier can directly affect several of the aspects evaluated in this study, such as communication with HCPs, understanding of information provision, and access to support resources. Although the questionnaire was deliberately written at the B1 language level and 80%–90% of the people in the Netherlands have this level of language proficiency [81, 82], individuals with lower Dutch proficiency were excluded. This also applies to low digital literacy, as all routes of recruitment involved at least one online step. In light of the PFC-VBHC, the experience of all patients, in particular of the “hard-to-reach” population, is important for in-depth insights for care pathway improvement. These families may face more barriers to care and experience greater societal and psychosocial vulnerability. To ensure optimal equity and inclusivity in the future, face-to-face or postal recruitment methods could help reach those with limited digital literacy, while questionnaires translated into multiple languages could improve inclusivity among non-Dutch-speaking families.

In conclusion, the present study provides comparative insights into patient and family experiences that can be used to optimize integrated PFC-VBHC pathways after complex treatment. This was done by crossing boundaries of the original condition and involving all (pediatric and adult) disciplines. Institutions should (continue to) prioritize a humanistic approach and good relationship building. Furthermore, long-term psychosocial consequences beyond medical outcomes should be systematically addressed in care so that families no longer feel “lost” in finding appropriate supportive care. Key facilitators are better information provision and the formation of a strong, comprehensive care network. Structurally embedding these aspects in care pathways is essential for high-quality PFC-VBHC. This highlights the urgent need to join forces, with families and between HCPs, beyond the boundaries of the original healthcare condition for shared learning. Ultimately, advancing integrated physical, socioemotional and neurocognitive care for these children and their families contributes not only to event-free survival but also, more importantly, to quality of life and true participation in society, the ultimate goal of optimal quality of care.
