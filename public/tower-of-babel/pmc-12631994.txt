Association between the framingham steatosis index and obstructive sleep apnea: a population-based study
Authors: Fa-Dan Tang, Jing-Ying Ma, Hai-Lun Xia, Jia-Ying Yu, Shi-Bing Xu, Yong-Hua Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGiven the robust correlation between MAFLD and OSA, FSI may act as a potential indicator for OSA risk. Nevertheless, the correlation between FSI and OSA, along with the potential mediating mechanisms, remains unclear.MethodsOur study examined 46,028 NHANES participants from 2005 to 2008 and 2015 to 2020. FSI-OSA correlation was assessed using weighted multivariate logistic regression models, restricted cubic spline curve analysis, threshold effects analysis, and subgroup analysis. We also conducted mediation studies to determine if waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) mediates the FSI-OSA relationship.Results Each unit increase in the FSI was associated with a 36% higher odds of OSA (OR = 1.36; 95% CI: 1.31–1.42). FSI quartiles were positively associated with OSA in aggregate analyses, with adjustment for potential confounders. RCS curves and threshold studies showed a nonlinear link between FSI and OSA risk when FSI was steeper on the left side of the inflection point (− 1.108) and levelled out on the right. WHtR also partially mediated the FSI-OSA connection, accounting for 24.5% of the total effect.ConclusionFSI and OSA were positively correlated with nonlinear and threshold effects in this study. Elevated FSI values were associated with a higher risk of OSA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03539-7.Keywords: Framingham steatosis index, Obstructive sleep apnea, NHANES, Logistic regression, Restricted cubic spline, Mediation analysis

Introduction

OSA is a prevalent sleep-related respiratory disorder impacting 936 million individuals globally, with 425 million experiencing moderate to severe cases [1]. It is associated to cardiovascular disease [2], metabolic illnesses [3], and cognitive dysfunction [4] and often accompanied by excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS), which has many negative effects on patients' daily life [5]. OSA is often under diagnosed and undertreated, highlighting the need for effective screening tools to identify high-risk individuals. Non-alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease (NAFLD) is a prevalent metabolic liver disorder distinguished by hepatic steatosis and not caused by alcohol [6]. NAFLD and OSA were linked even without obesity or metabolic syndrome [7] and the term has recently been updated to Metabolic-Associated Fatty Liver Disease (MAFLD) [8]. The observed association may be attributable to intermittent hypoxia, insulin resistance, and systemic inflammation. Intermittent hypoxia, a common occurrence in patients with OSA, has been demonstrated to activate the hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF-1α) signalling pathway. This activation has been associated with the release of inflammatory mediators, insulin resistance, and lipid metabolism disorders. These factors contribute to the induction and exacerbation of MAFLD. Furthermore, intermittent hypoxia has been observed to disrupt the homeostasis of hepatic lipid metabolism, leading to steatosis and other related complications [9]. Insulin resistance is a pivotal factor in the development of MAFLD. It functions in conjunction with lipotoxicity to induce liver lesions. OSA can lead to insulin resistance through multiple mechanisms, exacerbating the condition. Both MAFLD and OSA are associated with systemic inflammation, and inflammatory factors can result in insulin resistance, hepatocellular injury, and other complications. The symptoms of both conditions are exacerbated, creating a vicious cycle [10]. Studies have explored the relationship between OSA and MAFLD, finding a close association between the two [11]. The question of whether there is a direct prognostic, predictive, or diagnostic relationship between the fatty liver index (FSI), a comprehensive index that reliably assesses visceral fat accumulation and predicts MAFLD, and OSA requires further study to clarify.

The relationship between FSI and OSA, including potential mediating factors, remains unclear. This study uses NHANES data to investigate this relationship, analyze its characteristics, assess threshold effects, and explore subgroup variations. These findings may shed light on FSI’s OSA screening efficacy and inform future research and clinical applications.

Methods

A cross-sectional study examined the relationship between FSI and OSA. Nationally representative NHANES surveys spanning 2005–2008 and 2015–2020 provided data. The NHANES dataset at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm provides extensive health indicator data.

Study population

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) interviews and analyses US adults and children to measure their health and nutrition. The NCHS Ethics Review Board sanctioned NHANES, and participants provided informed written permission. This analysis covered 2005–2008 and 2015–2020 FSI and OSA participants. Recognition began with 46,028 participants. After removing participants under 20 (n = 20,163), pregnant women (n = 550), those with absent or unavailable FSI data (n = 13,173), those without OSA questionnaire data (n = 3182), and those with missing covariates (n = 15), 8945 adult participants were included. Definition of exposure factors FSI

Long MT and colleagues [12] created the FSI in 2016. The following formula was used to calculate: FSI = − 7.981 + 0.011 × age (years)– 0.146 × sex (female = 1, male = 0) + 0.173 × BMI (kg/m2) + 0.007 × triglycerides (mg/dL) + 0.593 × hypertension (yes = 1, no = 0) + 0.789 × diabetes (yes = 1, no = 0) ALT:AST ratio of + 1.1 or above, with a threshold of 1.33 (affirmative = 1, negative = 0). To ensure reproducibility and clarity, we have specified the units for each variable in the FSI formula. Additionally, triglyceride levels were measured in blood samples collected after a 12-h overnight fast [13] The presence of diabetes and hypertension was determined by physician diagnosis and categorized as "yes = 1" or "no = 0." Aspartate amino transferase (AST, units: U/L) and alanine amino transferase (ALT, units: U/L) are two hepatic enzymes secreted by hepatocytes into the circulation. ALT was assessed using enzymatic methods, while AST was measured using kinetic rate assays [14]. We chose to use the ALT/AST ratio because its originators found that an ALT/AST ratio greater than 1.33 has a high specificity (80%) and a certain level of sensitivity (55%) for diagnosing hepatic steatosis, thus demonstrating diagnostic value.

Obstructive sleep apnea ascertainment

The diagnosis of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) was established based on three fundamental questions derived from the NHANES Sleep Questionnaire, which closely correspond to the typical symptoms of OSA as delineated in the International Classification of Sleep Disorders, Third Edition (ICSD-3) [15] These symptoms include habitual snoring, witnessed apneas, and excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS). Specifically, the diagnostic criteria included: (1) experiencing excessive daytime sleepiness or drowsiness at least 16–30 times per month; (2) snoring at least three nights per week; and (3) experiencing snoring, gasping for air, or apneas during sleep at least three times per week over the past 12 months. Participants were classified as exhibiting potential OSA symptoms if they endorsed at least one of these three criteria. Notably, the occurrence of snoring, gasping for air, or apneas occurring ≥ 3 times per week during sleep has been independently associated with moderate-to-severe OSA (AHI ≥ 15), as evidenced by findings from the Sleep Heart Health Study [16].

Similar surveys, such as those conducted by Senaratna et al. [17], have demonstrated that the sensitivity and specificity of the Berlin Questionnaire for moderate to severe obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) — defined as an Apnea–Hypopnea Index (AHI) of ≥ 15 events per hour — is approximately 70%. While this method does not provide results equivalent to polysomnography, it has been accepted for mass screening purposes and is employed here solely to identify symptomatic individuals at high risk.

Assessment of covariates

Participants were asked for demographic information (age, gender, race) upon enrolment, socioeconomic indicators (marriage status, poverty income ratio, and educational attainment), alcohol use, tobacco use, lifestyle choices, and health-related status through a standardized questionnaire. Race/ethnicity was classified as non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, Mexican American, and other. Educational attainment was categorized as below high school, high school or equivalent, and college graduate or higher. The Household Poverty Income Ratio (PIR) serves as a comparative metric for household income levels, classified into three categories: ≤ 1, 1–4, and ≥ 4. Body mass index (BMI) is computed as weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared. Specifically, a BMI of less than 18.5 kg/m2 indicates underweight, a BMI between 18.5 kg/m2 and 24.9 kg/m2 denotes normal weight, a BMI between 24.9 kg/m2 and 30 kg/m2 classifies as overweight, and a BMI beyond 30 kg/m2 is categorized as obese. People were considered non-smokers if they had smoked fewer than 100 cigarettes throughout their life, whereas smokers had smoked 100 or more cigarettes and were still smoking. Physical activity was categorized as low (< 500 MET/week) or high (≥ 500 MET/week) based on national guidelines. Alcohol consumption was assessed via questionnaire and categorized as light, moderate, or heavy based on daily intake. Hypertension was defined as physician-diagnosed hypertension, SBP ≥ 140 mmHg, or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg. Diabetes was defined as physician-diagnosed diabetes, insulin use, or diabetes medications. Depression was defined as a PHQ-9 score ≥ 10 [18]. Cardiovascular disease was characterized by a positive response to the inquiry, "Has a physician or other healthcare professional ever informed you that you have congestive heart failure, coronary artery disease, angina, myocardial infarction, or stroke?" An affirmative response was classified as CVD.

Statistical analysis

The test known as Kolmogorov–Smirnov was employed to assess the standard deviation of variable distributions. Continuous parameters conforming to a typical distribution were represented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas categorical variables were characterized by frequency (%). The Kruskal-W H test was utilized for irregularly shaped data, whereas the chi-square test was applied for comparisons among categorical variable groups. Due to the multifaceted sampling design of NHANES, statistical analyses utilized sample weights, clustering, and stratification to address survey design effects. Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize the quartile groups of FSI and continuous variables, thereby clarifying participant characteristics. Logistic regression models were constructed to evaluate the association between FSI (analysed both as a continuous variable and categorized into quartiles) and OSA, with outcomes presented as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The relationship between FSI as a continuous variable and OSA was analysed using restricted cubic splines (RCS) with four knots, threshold effect analysis, and a two-segment linear regression model(carried out using the segmented package) to investigate possible non-linear and dose–response associations. In order to investigate the mediating role of WHtR in the correlation between FSI and OSA, a mediation analysis was conducted.FSI was designated as the independent variable, WHtR as the mediator, and OSA as the dependent variable. The study ultimately quantified direct effects, indirect effects, and mediation ratios. Subgroup analyses were conducted to detect interaction variables. To test interaction variables, this study conducted group analysis and used logistic regression to draw ROC curves, comparing the correlation between FSI and known OSA risk factors like BMI and waist circumference in predicting OSA risk.

All statistical analyses were conducted using Zstats and R software (version 4.2.3). A two-tailed P value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Association of FSI with OSA

Logistic multi-model strategy analysis

Table 2 demonstrates the relationship of FSI and OSA via three logistic regression models. Model 1, without adjusting for covariates, showed a strong positive link between FSI and OSA, with an OR of 1.36 (95% CI 1.31–1.42, P < 0.01), indicating that each unit increase in FSI is linked to a 36% greater odds of OSA. In model 2, following the adjustment for sociodemographic and lifestyle variables, this connection persisted as significant, with an OR of 1.32 (95% CI 1.26–1.37, P < 0.01). Model 3, after adjustment for clinical variables, exhibited a small attenuation in the strength of the connection, however it remained significant with an odds ratio of 1.13 (95% CI 1.04–1.23, P = 0.007). The studies indicated a dose–response connection, demonstrating that an increase in the FSI tertile correlates with an elevated risk of OSA across all models. The subsequent RCS curve analysis (Fig. 2) demonstrated a significant relationship between FSI and OSA risk, with P < 0.001 for overall significance and P < 0.001 for the significance of the non-linear relationship, indicating a substantial and positive association between FSI and OSA risk, characterized by a non-linear nature.

RCS curve analysis between FSI and OSA

To investigate the correlation between FSI and OSA, we conducted a threshold analysis, and Fig. 3 illustrated the nonlinear dose–response relationship between FSI and OSA, revealing that higher FSI values are linked to a greater likelihood of OSA. Table 3 ’s threshold analysis revealed a threshold effect between FSI and OSA, with an inflection point at − 1.108. This indicates that the increase in OSA risk was more significant at FSI values below this point (OR = 1.67, 95% CI 1.55–1.79, P < 0.001). At FSI values exceeding this inflection point, the trend of risk increase was more mild (OR = 1.13, 95% CI 1.08–1.19, P < 0.001).

The mediation model and path are depicted in Fig. 4. The results indicate that WHtR partially mediates the association between FSI and OSA, with a mediation proportion of approximately 24.5% (95% CI 18.46%–38.0%).

Subgroup analyses

To assess the stability of the association between FSI and OSA, we performed subgroup analyses and interaction tests while adjusting for covariates such as gender, age, and race; the results are presented in Table 4. The results indicated a strong association between FSI and OSA (OR = 1.360, 95%CI 1.310–1.420, P < 0.001). Significant differences were observed in gender (P for interaction = 0.012), age (P for interaction = 0.015), education level (P for interaction = 0.014), poverty to income ratio (P for interaction = 0.038), smoking (P for interaction = 0.008), drinking (P for interaction = 0.005), BMI (P for interaction < 0.001), hyperlipidemia (P for interaction = 0.003), and diabetes (P for interaction = 0.027). Notably, the association was stronger in women (OR = 1.400, P < 0.001) compared to men (OR = 1.290, P < 0.001). Participants with BMI < 18.5 kg/m2 had a significantly higher OR of 5.810 (P < 0.001) than those with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2who had an OR of only 1.120 (P < 0.001). In contrast, race (P = 0.871), marital status (P = 0.052), physical activity (P = 0.098), depression(P = 0.98),and CVD(P = 0.329) did not modify the association; all showed consistent positive associations across these subgroups(all P < 0.001).

ROC curve analysis for predicting OSA

We conducted a ROC curve analysis to evaluate the relationship between FSI and objective risk factors for OSA, including BMI and waist circumference. The ROC curve of FSI in predicting OSA demonstrated an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.644 (95% CI 0.633–0.656), with a sensitivity of 70.0% and a specificity of 50.9% (Fig.S1 in the Supplementary Material). Notably, we observed that FSI exhibited strong predictive value for both obesity, as defined by BMI, and abdominal obesity, indicated by waist circumference—both recognized risk factors for OSA. Specifically, the AUC for FSI in predicting obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) was found to be 0.900 (95% CI 0.894–0.906), with a sensitivity of 85.1% and a specificity of 78.2% (Fig. S2). Similarly, regarding elevated waist circumference, the AUC was recorded at 0.927 (95% CI 0.921–0.934), with sensitivity at 87.9% and specificity at 82.4% (Fig. S3).While these ROC analyses do not validate the OSA questionnaire itself, they illustrate that FSI effectively discriminates between individuals with and without obesity-related OSA risk factors (BMI/Waist), thereby indirectly supporting its relevance in the stratification of OSA risk.

Discussion

The present study revealed a significant positive correlation between FSI and OSA. Multivariate logistic regression analysis demonstrated that the odds of OSA increased by 36% for each unit increase in FSI (OR = 1.36, 95% CI 1.31–1.42). Subsequent RCS curve and threshold analysis demonstrated an inflection point at an FSI value of -1.108. The risk of OSA was found to be significantly increased when FSI was below this threshold (OR = 1.67, 95% CI 1.55–1.79, P < 0.001), whereas the risk increase was more moderate above this threshold (OR = 1.13, 95% CI 1.08–1.19, P < 0.001). The findings indicate a multifaceted, non-linear association and a threshold effect between FSI and OSA.ROC analysis indicated that FSI effectively identifies metabolic risk profiles associated with OSA, suggesting its potential utility in OSA risk stratification. Subgroup analyses showed that FSI-OSA had varying associations across different demographic, clinical, and metabolic groups. The effect was significantly stronger in women, smokers, diabetes patients, and those with a lower body mass index (OR: 5.81 for obesity vs. 1.12 for non-obesity; P for interaction < 0.001). However, The notably higher OR in the low-BMI group may reflect differential misclassification bias, though we cannot rule out true biological heterogeneity without objective OSA validation.

In mediation analyses, the results suggest that WHtR mediates the link between FSI and OSA to some extent. WHtR is an innovative indicator of central obesity, primarily concerning abdominal fat build-up, which may result in modified respiratory mechanics. Moreover, its rise is significantly correlated with many metabolic problems, such as insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and hypertension [19]. The repercussions of metabolic and inflammatory variables, such as dyslipidemia, may intensify the severity of OSA by fostering vascular endothelial dysfunction and influencing peripheral chemoreceptor sensitivity [20] Of the overall impact of FSI on OSA, 24.50% resulted from the indirect effect of WHtR, whereas 75.50% was direct. This indicates that whereas WHtR somewhat mediates the link between FSI and OSA, the direct influence of FSI on OSA remains predominant.

It is important to note that we conducted a parallel comparison between WHtR and BMI, an OSA-related risk indicator. The results indicated that BMI, as part of the FSI formula, was included in the exposure variable, violating the "exposure → mediator → outcome" time sequence premise [21]. Even with continuous BMI, the ACME (0.0198) was similar to WHtR (0.017). However, when categorized (obese/overweight/normal), the mediator ratio for BMI dropped to 3.5%, while WHtR maintained a robust 24.5% with a fully positive 95% CI.Thus, we selected WHtR as the core mediating variable due to its statistical independence from the FSI formula and strong mediating effects across different model settings. Although both WHtR and BRI were highly correlated (VIF > 70), WHTR exhibited the largest indirect effect size with stable direction in comparisons. In contrast, BRI's ACME was nearly zero with a confidence interval containing negative values, making clinical interpretation challenging. Considering causal order, effect size, and model stability, we retained only WHtR as the sole mediator.

Numerous studies have investigated the relationship between metabolic syndrome and OSA. For example, Alan R. Schwartz and his associates discovered that obesity significantly elevates the risk for the onset and advancement of OSA by augmenting the mechanical burden on the upper airway and diminishing compensatory neuromuscular responses [22]. OSA was identified in 85% of adult individuals exhibiting regular snoring and undiagnosed sleep-related breathing difficulties, with higher triglyceride levels observed in these patients [23]. Haifeng Hou et al. discovered that intermittent hypoxemia caused by OSA may provoke sympathetic arousal, potentially leading to hypertension. Furthermore, untreated OSA may engage the resistance mechanism to antihypertensive medications via pharmacokinetic or chronotherapeutic influences. A bidirectional association exists regarding resistance pathways to antihypertensive medications [24]. Research indicates that the incidence of insulin resistance and glucose intolerance is elevated in people with OSA, and this correlation is independent of obesity [25]. Individuals with OSA frequently exhibit metabolic problems, such as insulin resistance and obesity, which may indirectly influence liver enzyme levels [26]. This thorough analysis underscores the interrelation of FSI components with OSA risk factors, necessitating additional investigation into the potential correlation between FSI and OSA.

The exact mechanism connecting FSI and OSA remains unknown; nevertheless, evidence indicates that shared pathophysiological mechanisms, including intermittent hypoxia, metabolic abnormalities, inflammatory responses, and sympathetic excitability, may be pivotal to this association. The FSI is a non-invasive instrument utilizing demographic, metabolic, and biochemical indices, encompassing metrics that are closely linked to metabolic disorders, a prevalent pathological characteristic of OSA. A prevalent pathogenic characteristic observed in patients. Research indicates that metabolic diseases, such as obesity, hypertension, hyperglycaemia, and dyslipidemia, are significant risk factors for OSA, with obesity is strongly associated with both elevated fasting insulin levels and OSA [27]. Obesity results in leptin resistance and diminished lipocalin levels. Leptin resistance has been observed in conjunction with altered lipid metabolism and more severe hepatic steatosis, whereas reduced lipocalin diminishes insulin sensitivity and exacerbates metabolic syndrome [28]. Chonggui Zhu and colleagues discovered that recurrent intermittent hypoxia and microarousals during sleep in patients with OSA stimulate the sympathetic nervous system, resulting in heightened insulin resistance. Insulin resistance has been associated with elevated FSI, concurrent with increased hepatic fatty acid oxidation [29]. Intermittent hypoxia has been demonstrated to provoke a chronic inflammatory response linked to heightened systemic oxidative stress. Inflammatory mediators, such as CRP, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α, released from the upper respiratory tract mucosa, infiltrate the systemic circulation, resulting in sympathetic nervous system activation, an elevation of inflammatory factors (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α), and insulin resistance [30]. These mechanisms have been observed alongside fatty liver progression, and OSA is often co-present with elevated free fatty acid levels by upregulating hepatic fatty acid binding protein (FABP) and fatty acid transporter protein (FATP), while simultaneously inhibiting mitochondrial β-oxidation, resulting in hepatic fat accumulation [31]. Indicators in the FSI, such as the AST/ALT ratio and triglyceride levels, reflect metabolic and inflammatory conditions, thereby allowing for an indirect assessment of the presence and severity of OSA. A bidirectional relationship exists between fatty liver and OSA, with individuals suffering from fatty liver exhibiting a heightened susceptibility to OSA, which subsequently intensifies the severity of fatty liver.

In addition to FSI, in recent years researchers have been exploring other potential biomarkers for OSA diagnosis and risk assessment. For example, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) is thought to be significantly associated with OSA. A systematic review and meta-analysis showed a negative correlation between BDNF levels and OSA, suggesting that BDNF may serve as a potential biomarker for OSA [32]. BDNF plays an important role in neuroprotection and synaptic plasticity, and variations in its levels may reflect neurocognitive dysfunction commonly seen in patients with OSA. The advantage of BDNF as a biomarker over FSI is that it directly reflects neurological changes, whereas FSI reflects more metabolic and hepatic fat accumulation. However, the detection of BDNF requires specialised laboratory equipment, and its specificity and sensitivity in different populations need to be further validated. In contrast, FSI, which is calculated based on common clinical indicators, has the advantage of being easy to obtain and calculate, and is suitable for large-scale epidemiological studies and clinical screening. The findings of this study suggest that clinicians may consider FSI as a comprehensive indicator when assessing the risk of OSA. Elevated FSI values may identify individuals who are more likely to report OSA-related symptoms, and thus could be considered for further clinical evaluation. The ease of calculation of FSI renders it suitable for initial screening of individuals at high risk of OSA, facilitating early identification and diagnosis for timely intervention.

Our research possesses several strengths. Initially, we utilized NHANES, a national database characterized by a substantial sample size and superior data quality, hence enhancing the reliability of our findings. Multi-model analysis, alongside subgroup and mediation analyses, was employed to examine, for the first time, the nonlinear relationship between FSI and OSA.

However, our study has certain limitations. First, while we adjusted for multiple confounders, residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. Unmeasured variables, such as specific dietary patterns, may influence both FSI and OSA. For instance, previous studies have indicated that high fructose intake can exacerbate hepatic steatosis [33], contribute to weight gain, and lead to visceral fat accumulation [34]. If these factors are not fully accounted for, they may overestimate the association between FSI and OSA. Additionally, this study employed a cross-sectional design, which suggests an association rather than establishing causation. Future research is necessary to further control for these potential confounders.Secondly, the diagnosis of OSA relies on questionnaires; thus there is a risk of introducing differential misclassification bias that could diminish the specificity of ROC analysis [35]. For example, individuals with higher BMI or other metabolic risk factors might be more likely to report sleep-related symptoms—this could exaggerate the observed association between FSI and OSA. Conversely, underreporting of symptoms among asymptomatic or undiagnosed cases may result in false negatives and obscure true effects—particularly in subgroups such as women and those with low BMI. Although we cannot quantify the exact magnitude of this bias at present, it is important to acknowledge that our findings may overestimate the true strength of the association. Nevertheless, it should be noted that despite this limitation a significant association was observed; this suggests that the actual effect may indeed be stronger than reported.Thirdly, the elimination of missing data may compromise the representativeness of the sample, and constraints imposed by secondary data sources could impede a comprehensive examination of certain variables. To enhance the clarity and reliability of future research, we will consider additional confounding factors, integrate findings from objective OSA diagnostic tools such as PSG or home sleep testing (HST), investigate the causal relationship between FSI and OSA, and implement prospective cohort designs in our experiments to elucidate the pathological mechanisms involved. Our ultimate goal is to improve both the treatment and prevention strategies for OSA.

Conclusion

Based on the data from NHANES, this study used multi-model strategy, restricted cubic spline and threshold analysis, and found that FSI was positively correlated with OSA before and after the threshold of − 1.108. Abdominal fat measured by WHTR partially mediated this relationship. Subgroup analysis showed that OSA was significantly associated with demographic, clinical and metabolic factors. These findings are beneficial for preliminary screening of high-risk groups of OSA, so as to facilitate early detection and diagnosis and timely intervention. However, the cross-sectional nature of this study limits inferences about causality. In the future, large-scale prospective cohort studies are needed to integrate PSG or home sleep monitoring (HST) data or similar large cohorts to objectively assess OSA, precisely define the presence and severity of OSA, and reduce misclassification bias.
