Pediatric Varicocele as the Presenting Sign of Nutcracker Syndrome: An Ultrasound Case Report
Authors: Ahmed Abdisalan Warsame, Shamsa Ahmed Mohamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPediatric varicocele is most often recognized during adolescence; presentation at a younger age or with atypical features should prompt consideration of secondary causes. Nutcracker syndrome (NCS) is the symptomatic clinical consequence of left renal vein (LRV) compression and may produce collateral gonadal venous reflux.Case PresentationA 10-year-old boy presented with a 6-month history of painless left scrotal swelling. Examination demonstrated a soft “bag of worms” mass that became more prominent with standing and the Valsalva maneuver. Urinalysis showed no hematuria or proteinuria. Morphologic and hemodynamic ultrasound findings should be interpreted together with the clinical presentation.

Introduction

Varicocele is dilatation of the pampiniform venous plexus and is most commonly recognized during adolescence. Its prevalence increases with puberty, and although many patients remain asymptomatic, varicocele may be associated with impaired testicular growth or later reproductive consequences in a subset of patients.1–4 Pediatric management is therefore individualized according to symptoms, serial testicular development, semen parameters when age-appropriate, and other evidence of gonadal impairment.2–4

Nutcracker syndrome is the clinical syndrome produced by LRV compression. The classic anterior form results from entrapment of the LRV between the abdominal aorta and superior mesenteric artery, whereas posterior NCS occurs when a retroaortic LRV is compressed between the aorta and vertebral column. Combined anatomic variants and external or positional contributors have also been described.5–9 Factors that can accentuate LRV compression or narrow the aortomesenteric angle include low body mass or reduced retroperitoneal fat, rapid weight loss, renal ptosis, marked lordosis, pregnancy, intestinal malrotation, lymphadenopathy, and other mass effects.8,9

Renal venous hypertension may produce hematuria, orthostatic proteinuria, flank or abdominal pain, and collateral venous drainage through the gonadal or pelvic venous systems.5–9 In males, gonadal venous reflux may manifest as a left-sided varicocele. Doppler ultrasonography is a useful first-line, noninvasive modality because it can assess the scrotal venous plexus and demonstrate LRV caliber change, prestenotic dilatation, altered flow velocity, and a reduced aortomesenteric angle.5,8,9

The association between NCS and pediatric varicocele is established; therefore, this report is not presented as a first description. Its contribution is diagnostic and educational: an isolated painless varicocele in a 10-year-old child with normal urinalysis prompted a focused extension of Doppler imaging from the scrotum to the renal venous circulation. The case demonstrates how integrated morphologic and hemodynamic ultrasound findings can identify a secondary vascular mechanism that may otherwise be overlooked.

Case Presentation

A 10-year-old boy presented with a 6-month history of painless left scrotal swelling that had gradually increased in size. The family reported no previous medical evaluation. There was no history of trauma, urinary symptoms, or previous surgery. Physical examination showed a soft, non-tender left scrotal mass with a “bag of worms” consistency that became more prominent on standing and during the Valsalva maneuver. Abdominal examination was unremarkable.

Routine laboratory investigations, including urinalysis, were unremarkable, with no evidence of hematuria or proteinuria.

Ultrasound Findings

Scrotal ultrasonography was performed using a high-frequency 12-MHz linear transducer on a SonoScape P60 Exp system. Both testes were within the expected age-related size range. Gray-scale imaging demonstrated multiple dilated, serpiginous tubular structures in the left pampiniform plexus, with a maximum measured diameter of 3.31 mm (Figure 1). Because of the patient’s young age and the absence of an obvious local secondary cause, abdominal Doppler ultrasonography was performed. It demonstrated focal narrowing of the LRV between the superior mesenteric artery and abdominal aorta with marked prestenotic dilatation (Figure 3). On transverse imaging, the displayed LRV diameters were 5.34 mm at the hilar segment and 0.49 mm at the compressed segment (Figure 4), corresponding to an approximate diameter ratio of 10.9:1. The aortomesenteric angle measured 8.58° (Figure 5). Spectral Doppler demonstrated a peak systolic velocity of approximately 91.2 cm/s at the compressed segment (Figure 6). Diagnosis and Management

The clinical and imaging findings were considered consistent with a left-sided pediatric varicocele associated with anterior NCS. Because the child had no pain, hematuria, proteinuria, or reported testicular asymmetry, conservative management with clinical and ultrasound follow-up was selected. Surgical or endovascular intervention was deferred.

Discussion

The principal lesson from this case is diagnostic rather than etiologic novelty. Pediatric varicocele is usually encountered around puberty, and a presentation at a younger age should increase awareness of secondary causes.1–4 In this patient, physical examination and scrotal Doppler established the local diagnosis, while the patient’s age prompted extension of the examination to the abdominal venous circulation. This sequence illustrates a practical way to move from the presenting manifestation to a plausible upstream vascular mechanism without immediately resorting to invasive testing.

The abdominal Doppler findings were characteristic of marked anterior LRV compression: focal narrowing between the aorta and superior mesenteric artery, proximal dilatation, a very small aortomesenteric angle, and increased velocity at the compressed segment. The measured angle of 8.58° was markedly reduced, and the hilar-to-compressed diameter ratio was approximately 10.9:1. Recent evidence supports a reduced aortomesenteric angle as a useful diagnostic clue, but also emphasizes that ultrasound thresholds vary and should not be used in isolation.8–11 Because a hilar velocity measurement and invasive renocaval pressure gradient were not available in this case, the diagnosis appropriately rests on the combined clinical, morphologic, and hemodynamic pattern rather than on a single numeric cutoff.

The clinical presentation further strengthens the educational value of the case. Hematuria and orthostatic proteinuria are common pediatric manifestations of NCS, but the clinical spectrum is heterogeneous and some children do not present with classic renal findings.5–7 Here, urinalysis was normal and the varicocele was the only clinical clue. Therefore, the absence of hematuria or proteinuria should not, by itself, exclude investigation for LRV compression when the age or pattern of varicocele is unusual.

Published cases help place the present report in context. Reddy and Shekar described a 13-year-old boy with a grade III varicocele and left flank pain in whom imaging demonstrated NCS, whereas Ouskri et al reported an adult patient with a large painful varicocele and intermittent hematuria.12,13 In contrast, our patient was 10 years old and had painless isolated scrotal swelling with normal urinalysis. This comparison does not establish a new disease association; rather, it highlights a less symptomatic presentation and the value of extending Doppler evaluation when age or clinical pattern raises concern for a secondary cause.

Management should be individualized. Pediatric NCS is commonly managed conservatively when symptoms are mild and there is no evidence of progressive renal compromise, because symptoms and vascular geometry may improve with growth and changes in body habitus.5,7,14 Varicocele management similarly depends on pain, serial testicular growth, and evidence of gonadal impairment rather than the presence of venous dilatation alone.1–4 In the present patient, the absence of pain, renal manifestations, and reported testicular asymmetry supported observation. Persistent or worsening symptoms, progressive testicular growth discrepancy, renal impairment, significant hematuria, or other evidence of clinically important progression would warrant multidisciplinary reassessment and consideration of additional imaging or intervention.5,14

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This is a single case report and therefore cannot establish prevalence, diagnostic accuracy, or generalizable ultrasound thresholds. The diagnosis was based on clinical and Doppler findings; cross-sectional angiography, invasive venography, and renocaval pressure measurement were not performed because the child’s presentation did not require an interventional strategy. Long-term outcome data were also unavailable at the time of manuscript preparation. These limitations reinforce the need to interpret the reported measurements as supportive findings within the clinical context rather than as stand-alone diagnostic criteria.

Conclusion

In a young child with a left-sided varicocele, especially when the age or presentation is atypical, secondary vascular causes should be considered. This case demonstrates how Doppler ultrasonography can connect scrotal venous reflux with LRV compression in a single noninvasive diagnostic pathway. The practical takeaway is not that NCS is a new cause of pediatric varicocele, but that normal urinalysis does not exclude NCS and that renal venous assessment may be appropriate when the clinical context is unusual.
