Type II achalasia unmasked by EndoFLIP and high‐resolution manometry in a child with eosinophilic esophagitis
Authors: Rasha Abi Radi Abou Jaoudeh, Jose M Garza, Chathruckan Rajendra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) is a chronic immune‐mediated disease characterized by esophageal dysfunction and eosinophilic inflammation. Persistent dysphagia despite histologic remission should prompt evaluation for alternative etiologies. A 10‐year‐old male with asthma and eczema presented with progressive solid food dysphagia, daily non‐bilious emesis, and weight loss. Endoscopy confirmed EoE, with up to 35 eosinophils per high‐power field. Despite proton pump inhibitor therapy and dairy/soy elimination, symptoms worsened, and requiring hospitalization. Repeat endoscopy showed distal eosinophilia and narrowing, initially presumed fibrostenotic, and requiring dilation. Treatment was escalated to dupilumab and enteral nutrition. Subsequent endoscopies showed histologic remission but persistent narrowing and dysphagia despite serial dilations. Esophagram raised concern for abnormal transit and retained food. Endoluminal Functional Lumen Imaging Probe and esophageal manometry diagnosed type II achalasia. Following pneumatic dilation of the lower esophageal sphincter, dysphagia resolved. This case highlights the importance of considering motility disorders in refractory EoE symptoms.Keywords: balloon dilation, esophageal stricture, vomiting, weight loss

INTRODUCTION

Eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) is a chronic immune‐mediated disease that commonly presents in children with dysphagia, food impaction, and feeding avoidance. However, dysphagia and regurgitation are also hallmark features of esophageal motility disorders, including achalasia. Pediatric achalasia is rare, with reported incidence estimates around 0.18 per 100,000 children per year. 1

Recent data suggest that EoE and achalasia coexist more frequently than previously appreciated, raising the possibility that persistent obstructive symptoms in EoE may reflect an unrecognized motility disorder. 2 We report a child with EoE complicated by strictures who was ultimately diagnosed with type II achalasia.

CASE REPORT

A 10‐year‐old male with asthma, seasonal allergies, and eczema presented with a 2‐month history of progressive solid‐food dysphagia with daily non‐bilious, non‐bloody emesis, and weight loss. He described a sensation of food “sticking,” prolonged mealtimes, and reliance on water to facilitate swallowing. Initial evaluation, 2 weeks later, included esophogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) demonstrating esophageal edema, longitudinal furrows, and scattered white plaques. Biopsies showed 35 eosinophils/high‐power field (eos/hpf) in the distal esophagus and 30 eos/hpf in the mid esophagus, confirming EoE. Gastric biopsies demonstrated severe chronic gastritis with Helicobacter‐like organisms. He was treated with 14 days of quadruple therapy for Helicobacter pylori and then continued omeprazole daily thereafter. A two‐food elimination diet (dairy and soy) was implemented as well.

Despite these interventions, symptoms progressed over the following 2 months with persistent dysphagia and weight loss. An upper gastrointestinal (GI) contrast study, at that time, demonstrated esophageal dysmotility with delayed bolus transit, but no stricture was noted. Two weeks later, he required hospital admission as well as tube feeding (nasogastric and then nasojejunal) for persistent emesis and malnutrition. EGD during admission revealed worsening EoE with diffuse edema, rings, and exudates; biopsies showed marked distal eosinophilia (up to 75 eos/hpf). A stricture at the gastroesophageal junction (GEJ) was noted with an estimated luminal diameter of ~6 mm which was dilated to 12 mm. Given persistent inflammation and refractory symptom despite high‐dose proton pump inhibitor therapy and dietary modification, dupilumab was initiated (300 mg subcutaneously every 2 weeks).

On outpatient follow‐up, 3 months later, symptoms improved, and weight stabilized. EGD at that time demonstrated mucosal healing and histologic remission; however, he had significant amount of retained food was noted in the esophagus. Esophageal stenosis measuring ~6 mm in diameter was identified in the distal esophagus and dilated to 15 mm. Despite this, intermittent solid‐food dysphagia persisted. Repeat Esophogram 1 month later (Figure 1) demonstrated diffuse esophageal narrowing with a tight distal stricture at the GEJ and a suspected false tract extending from the thoracic inlet to approximately T8. The stricture was attributed to EoE and so no further motility study was pursued at the time. Contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CT) chest, a week later, showed a debris‐filled dilated esophagus with narrowing at the aortic arch and GEJ and no definite diverticulum or second lumen, with radiology noting that achalasia could not be excluded. After additional review and discussion with radiology and motility team, the false tract initially noted was likely from retained food and persistent obstructive physiology in the esophagus.

Given the recurrent “stricture” behavior despite histologic remission on dupilumab and repeated endoscopic dilations (up to 18 mm on subsequent endoscopy), a multidisciplinary evaluation was pursued with gastroenterology, otolaryngology, surgery, and motility specialists. Endoscopic luminal assessment with a functional luminal imaging probe (EndoFLIP; 16 cm balloon) and HRM (high‐resolution manometry) were performed 2 weeks after the CT. HRM demonstrated normal upper esophageal sphincter function, elevated integrated relaxation pressure with absent lower esophageal sphincter relaxation, 100% aperistalsis, and pan‐esophageal pressurization (approximately 70% pressurization swallows), consistent with type II achalasia (Figure 2). EndoFLIP similarly showed absent contractile activity and markedly reduced esophagogastric junction (EGJ) distensibility: at 60 mL fill, distensibility index (DI) 0.8 mm2/mmHg with a max EGJ diameter at 70 mL of 8 mmHg (Figure 3). The patient subsequently underwent pneumatic dilation (within the same procedure) with a 30 mm achalasia balloon under fluoroscopic guidance (three inflations, ~60 s each). Post‐dilation EndoFLIP demonstrated substantial improvement in EGJ opening and compliance, with maximum EGJ diameter at 70 mL increasing to ~21 mm and DI increasing to ~6.1 mm2/mmHg at 60 mL fill. At follow‐up, he reported marked symptomatic improvement within 2 weeks of the dilation with decreased dysphagia and improved oral intake with 1.5 kg weight gain, while continuing dupilumab. Patient continues to follow with us in clinic, and we plan to repeat the dilation in the next 1–2 months.

DISCUSSION

This case underscores a high‐risk diagnostic pitfall in pediatric dysphagia: persistent obstructive symptoms may be misattributed entirely to EoE—even when biopsies demonstrate remission—thereby delaying evaluation for esophageal motility disorders. Pediatric achalasia is uncommon, with most estimates placing annual incidence near 0.1 per 100,000 children. 1 Because symptoms overlap substantially with gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), functional vomiting, and inflammatory esophagitis, diagnosis is frequently delayed. 3 In broader achalasia populations, diagnostic delay can be prolonged (median ~24 months). 4

In EoE, dysphagia and food impaction typically reflect inflammatory narrowing and/or fibrostenotic remodeling. 3 Dilation is effective for symptomatic strictures, and biologic therapy such as dupilumab can induce histologic remission and improve symptoms. However, symptom–histology discordance should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis, particularly when endoscopic caliber abnormalities recur quickly post‐intervention, or when radiographic studies show marked retention and distal “beaking” physiology suggestive of functional obstruction.

The relationship between EoE and esophageal dysmotility is increasingly recognized. Mechanistically, eosinophil‐derived mediators may influence neuromuscular function and smooth muscle behavior, potentially creating achalasia‐like patterns or secondary motor abnormalities in some patients. 5 In adults, obstructive motor disorders have been reported in a meaningful minority of EoE patients, with one study suggesting that approximately 1 in 14 may have overt achalasia. 6 Importantly, a population‐based comparative study identified achalasia among 2.0% of EoE patients overall, including pediatric cases (reported as 0.6% among children with EoE), supporting that coexistence—though still uncommon—is clinically relevant. 2 Additionally, esophageal eosinophilia may be seen primary esophageal motility disorders, including achalasia, as esophageal stasis with retained food material may irritate the mucosa and promote eosinophilic inflammation. 7

Our patient's course illustrates how achalasia can be masked by an EoE diagnosis, especially when EoE is complicated with esophageal strictures. The use of EndoFLIP and HRM was pivotal: FLIP provided real‐time evidence of impaired EGJ opening (markedly low DI at 60 mL fill and absent contractility), while HRM demonstrated classic type II achalasia features (impaired lower esophageal sphincter [LES] relaxation with aperistalsis and pan‐esophageal pressurization). Together, these modalities redirected therapy toward LES disruption with pneumatic dilation, with objective post‐procedure improvement in FLIP parameters and clinical symptoms.

Regarding outcomes, treatment options for pediatric achalasia include pneumatic dilation, laparoscopic Heller myotomy with fundoplication, and peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM). Contemporary pediatric series report symptomatic improvement across modalities, with procedure choice influenced by center expertise, patient size/anatomy, achalasia subtype, and recurrence risk. 8 Pneumatic dilation is less invasive and can be effective, though repeat interventions may be needed; myotomy and POEM offer durable relief in many cohorts but carry their own risks (including refractory reflux). 8

CONCLUSION

In children with EoE, persistent dysphagia despite histologic remission should prompt evaluation for coexisting motility disorders. EndoFLIP and HRM can be diagnostic and can guide therapy. Recognition of type II achalasia in our patient enabled LES‐directed intervention with symptomatic improvement.
