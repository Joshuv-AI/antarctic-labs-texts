Overwintering mortality in Halyomorpha halys (Hemiptera: Pentatomidae): insights from laboratory and field studies
Authors: Stefanie Fischnaller, Giacomo Bulgarini, Angelika Gruber, Clara Frasconi Wendt, Peter Neulichedl, Manfred Wolf, Nicholas Teets
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOverwintering success in insects is shaped by both physiological state and environmental conditions. The invasive brown marmorated stink bug, Halyomorpha halys (Stål) (Hemiptera: Pentatomidae) survives temperate winters through a facultative adult diapause. We quantified overwintering mortality over 4 consecutive winters (2020/2021 to 2023/2024) in South Tyrol, Italy, using controlled laboratory conditions (9 °C, 8:16 h L:D) and a sheltered semi-field setup. Mortality in field-collected cohorts ranged from 48.0% to 65.0% in laboratory trials and 35.2% to 58.9% outdoors, with substantial variation among cohorts overwintered under identical conditions. We compared overwintering survival of reproductively active (fecund) versus diapause-induced (non-fecund) adults produced in the laboratory. Reproductively active individuals experienced near-complete mortality (99.3%), whereas diapause-induced adults showed markedly lower mortality (20.6%). Interannual differences suggested environmental effects, but temperature effects were not consistently associated with observed mortality patterns. Winters with lower minimum temperatures (down to −12.38 °C) and greater cumulative cold exposure were associated with elevated mortality (up to 58.9%). However, substantial mortality also occurred during milder winters, indicating context-dependent temperature effects. Overall, cohort identity was a dominant source of heterogeneity, likely reflecting variation in reproductive state, energetic reserves, and pathogen pressure, as well as other abiotic factors. These results indicate that overwintering mortality in H. halys cannot be captured by a single universal parameter. Improved forecasts of spring population density will require late-season sampling from confirmed overwintering sites and, where feasible, physiological screening to estimate diapause incidence and derive realistic overwintering mortality. Integrating diapause dynamics and associated changes in cold tolerance into mechanistic models may further improve forecasting accuracy.Keywords: brown marmorated stink bug, diapause, Hemiptera, mortality rate, overwintering survival

Introduction

Insects rely on a wide range of physiological, behavioral, and morphological strategies to cope with seasonal changes (Tauber and Tauber 1976, Tauber et al. 1986, Wolda 1988). In temperate climates, diapause is a central mechanism enabling overwintering and synchronizing development with periods of favorable conditions (Tauber et al. 1986, Denlinger 2002). Diapause is a hormonally regulated dormancy comprising distinct induction, maintenance, and termination phases, each shaped by environmental cues such as photoperiod and temperature (Tauber and Tauber 1976, Koštál 2006, Koštál et al. 2017). While behavioral and morphological adaptations contribute to seasonal survival, overwintering success is often strongly constrained by physiological readiness and environmental context, including thermal regimes and microhabitat availability (Bale and Hayward 2010, Marshall and Sinclair 2015, Sinclair 2015, Klockmann and Fischer 2019, Tougeron et al. 2020, Roe et al. 2024).

For invasive species, it is crucial to synchronize development with seasonal environmental changes for successful establishment and spread in new regions and habitats (Gibert et al. 2016, Renault et al. 2018). The brown marmorated stink bug, Halyomorpha halys (Stål) (Hemiptera: Pentatomidae), is native to East Asia, and has established widely across North America and Europe (Hoebeke and Carter 2003, Leskey et al. 2012, Haye et al. 2015). Like many temperate heteropterans, H. halys exhibits a heterodynamic life cycle, characterized by the alternation of periods of activity and seasonal dormancy (diapause) (Tauber et al. 1986, Danks 1987, Saulich and Musolin 2014). In H. halys, diapause is expressed as a facultative adult winter diapause, and the species typically completes 1 to 2 generations per year depending on latitude and thermal conditions (Nielsen et al. 2008, Lee et al. 2013, Haye et al. 2014, Musolin et al. 2019). During overwintering, H. halys tends to aggregate in sheltered microhabitats, including natural refuges (eg under bark or in rock crevices) and anthropogenic structures such as buildings, which can provide thermal buffering during winter (Inkley 2012, Lee et al. 2014, Rice et al. 2014).

Diapause enhances cold tolerance mainly by lowering metabolic activity and helping insects conserve their energy stores, but overwintering survival depends on additional preparatory processes beyond metabolic suppression alone (Hahn and Denlinger 2007). Successful overwintering also requires that insects enter diapause at the right time, build up adequate lipid reserves, and temporarily suppress reproductive development (Denlinger 2002, Hahn and Denlinger 2007, Short and Hahn 2023), as well as, in some species, the accumulation of cryoprotective compounds and other physiological adjustments that enhance cold tolerance (Tattersall et al. 2012). However, evidence for substantial cryoprotectant accumulation in H. halys is limited (Ciancio et al. 2021).

In H. halys, diapause induction is triggered by shortening day lengths, generally when daylight falls between 12 and 15 h, and may be further modified by temperature and other environmental variables (Nielsen et al. 2016, Musolin et al. 2019, McDougall et al. 2021). During this period, females remain unmated and in a pre-vitellogenic state through the winter (Nielsen et al. 2008, Cira et al. 2018), with little or no feeding or water intake within their sheltered microhabitats (Inkley 2012).

Overwintering success in H. halys is therefore highly sensitive to both physiological state and environmental conditions. H. halys is considered chill-susceptible, although its cold tolerance can vary by season, physiological status, and geographic origin (Cira et al. 2016, Scaccini et al. 2020). In general, individuals in diapause withstand lower temperatures more effectively than those remaining reproductively active (Cira et al. 2018). As shown for other insects, survival may be further influenced by factors such as extreme cold, sudden temperature swings, or unseasonal warm spells, which may disrupt diapause stability or accelerate energy use (Bale and Hayward 2010, Colinet et al. 2015, Williams et al. 2015).

Environmental conditions during winter strongly influence overwintering survival and population dynamics in insects (Bale et al. 2002, Williams et al. 2015). In invasive species such as H. halys, successful establishment in new regions depends in part on the ability to synchronize life history traits, including diapause, with local environmental conditions (Gibert et al. 2016, Kistner 2017, Renault et al. 2018, Musolin et al. 2022, Reznik et al. 2023).

This study examines overwintering mortality of H. halys over 4 consecutive winters (2020 to 2024) in South Tyrol, northern Italy, under controlled and semi-field conditions. By combining those conditions, we aimed to contrast survival under standardized thermal regimes with survival under more variable, ecologically realistic environments. Although reproductive arrest during diapause has been well documented in H. halys (Nielsen et al. 2008, Nielsen et al. 2017, Cira et al. 2018), the extent to which physiological state prior to overwintering, particularly reproductive activity, directly influences winter survival has not been explicitly tested. In temperate regions such as South Tyrol, overwintering survival determines the size of the spring founder population and influences the potential for subsequent population build-up and crop damage during the growing season. Understanding how physiological state and environmental variability interact to shape winter survival is essential for improving overwinter survival estimates used in forecasting spring population size and informing pest management strategies under current and future climate conditions.

Materials and Methods

Collection and Cohort Definition

Adult Halyomorpha halys were collected during October between 2020 and 2024 from the Adige Valley region (South Tyrol, northern Italy), an area characterized by high infestation levels and the regular development of 2 annual generations (Fischnaller and Wolf 2021, Fischnaller et al. 2022). Insects were obtained through 2 complementary collection pathways: (i) targeted field sampling conducted by the research center and (ii) contributions from private individuals, farmers, and extension services responding to public collection requests.

Research center collections were performed using a noninvasive beating method. Vegetation in hedgerows and other semi-natural habitats containing known host plants of H. halys was sampled using an insect beating tray, with insects collected by repeated strikes of the vegetation. Sampling effort was not standardized to a fixed number of beats but aimed to retrieve as many living individuals as possible from each site. Individuals collected on the tray were immediately transferred to ventilated plastic vials and transported to the laboratory for subsequent experiments. For these collections, detailed locality information, including geographic coordinates, was recorded (see Supplementary Table S1).

Specimens provided by private people or extension services originated from areas known to be infested by H. halys within the Adige Valley region. From these collections, precise geographic coordinates were in most cases not available. Individuals received as a single collection batch at a given time were not mixed with others (Supplementary Table S1).

For the purposes of this study, cohorts were defined as groups of individuals collected during the same collection event, typically corresponding to a shared collection date and general area of origin. Upon arrival in the laboratory, dead individuals were removed, and living specimens sexed and counted. Cohorts were maintained intact throughout subsequent experimental handling, without reassignment of individuals among groups, thus cohort size and sex ratio varied among cohorts and were not standardized prior to experimentation. This variation reflects differences in collection batches and aggregation patterns rather than experimental manipulation. Cohort identity was therefore treated as an experimental grouping factor and as a random effect in statistical analyses to account for shared collection context. Spatial origin was not used as a predictor variable, and therefore differences in geographic precision among cohorts did not influence statistical analyses.

Following processing, individuals were transferred into insect rearing cages (BugDorm-1; MegaView Science Co., Ltd., Taichung, Taiwan; W30 × L30 × H30 cm) for subsequent experimental procedures.

Laboratory and Outdoor Overwintering Treatments

All overwintering trials were conducted using adult H. halys cohorts as defined above. Cohorts were assigned to laboratory or semi-field overwintering treatments at random within each collection period. However, assignment was intentionally weighted toward laboratory overwintering because surviving adults were required for subsequent laboratory experiments under standardized rearing conditions (see Falagiarda et al. 2023, Parth et al. 2024). As a result, treatment group sizes were unbalanced across years (Table 1, Supplementary Table S1). General rearing and handling procedures followed Bulgarini et al. (2025), except where explicitly stated below.

Laboratory Overwintering

Upon arrival in the laboratory, cohorts assigned to controlled overwintering were subjected to a 14-d pre-diapause conditioning period at 20 °C, 12:12 h light: dark (L:D), and 65% relative humidity (RH) in controlled climate chambers (Criocabin; Genesis System SP.70). During this period, insects were provided with carrots, green beans, sunflower seeds, and water-soaked cotton, in quantities consistent with Bulgarini et al. (2025). Dead individuals were removed prior to the onset of overwintering. Following pre-diapause conditioning, cohorts were transferred to laboratory overwintering conditions (9 °C, 8:16 h L:D, 65% RH) for 116 ± 9.7 d. No food or water was provided during this treatment. Each cage contained folded cardboard and paper towels to provide standardized shelter. Mortality was assessed at the end of the overwintering period, with individuals counted and sex-specific mortality recorded.

Outdoor Overwintering

Parallel cohorts were assigned to outdoor overwintering at an experimental site in Pfatten, Italy (coordinates: 46.379864, 11.286057; 223 m.a.s.l.), where cages were protected from direct precipitation and sunlight. Upon arrival, cohorts were processed and provided with the same diet as laboratory cohorts. Outdoor cohorts were placed outside immediately. Therefore, they did not undergo pre-diapause conditioning under controlled chamber conditions. After approximately 14 d, food and water were removed, cages were equipped with cardboard shelter, and cohorts were further exposed to ambient outdoor conditions for 112 ± 9.4 d. Mortality was assessed at the end of the overwintering period. Hourly air temperature was recorded throughout the outdoor overwintering periods (October to February) using data loggers (EasyLog EL-USB-2) placed inside in one of the outdoor cages.

Because temperature variables were recorded at the overwintering site and analyzed at the seasonal level, detailed information on collection sites were not required for inclusion in temperature-mortality analyses.

Fecund Versus Non-Fecund Overwintering Experiment

To examine whether reproductive (fecund) status at transfer to diapause conditions influences overwintering survival, a laboratory experiment was conducted using individuals derived from adults that survived the laboratory overwintering treatment in 2020/2021. Individuals were randomly selected and transferred to standard laboratory rearing conditions following Bulgarini et al. (2025). Adults were maintained at 25 °C, 65% RH, and a 16:8 h L:D photoperiod in ventilated rearing cages and provided with carrots, green beans, sunflower seeds, and water-soaked cotton. Cardboard and paper towels were available as shelter.

Egg masses produced under these conditions were collected and reared until nymphs reached the fourth instar (N4), as described previously. At the N4 stage, nymphs were randomly assigned to one of 2 developmental pathways designed to generate adults that entered overwintering either as reproductively active (fecund) or under a diapause developmental program (non-fecund). Classification was based on reproductive output and photoperiodic regime. Reproductive organs were not dissected.

Fecund Pathway (F)

N4 individuals remained under standard laboratory rearing conditions until adult emergence. Newly emerged adults were sexed and maintained under these conditions until reproductive activity was observed. Reproductive activity was defined as oviposition (presence of egg masses in the rearing cages), which was monitored twice per week. After reproduction was confirmed, adults were maintained under pre-diapause conditions (20 °C, 12:12 h L:D, 65% RH) for 10 to 14 d and were then transferred to diapause conditions (9 °C, 8:16 h L:D, 65% RH) for overwintering, resulting in a mean adult age of approximately 26 d at transfer to diapause conditions (Table 2).

Randomly selected N4 individuals were transferred to pre-diapause conditions (20 °C, 12:12 h L:D, 65% RH) and maintained under these conditions until adult emergence and for 31 ± 0.46 d starting from ecdysis into adult stage. Egg mass production was monitored twice per week, but no egg masses were produced under this pathway in any of the NF-designated cages.

Following these specific phases, both groups were transferred to overwintering conditions (9 °C, 8:16 h L:D, 65% RH), corresponding to the overwintering durations reported in Table 2. Mortality was assessed at the end of overwintering as the number of dead individuals per cage. Each pathway included 434 individuals. Adult age at transfer to diapause conditions was similar between pathways (Table 2). It was included as a covariate in statistical analyses.

Temperature Data and Derived Metrics

Hourly outdoor air temperature data was recorded using a single data logger (EasyLog EL-USB-2) placed inside one of the outdoor overwintering cages and recording throughout each overwintering period. The overwintering period was defined as 1 October to 28 February (or 29 February in leap years). Temperature data were analyzed at an hourly resolution unless otherwise specified. As all outdoor cohorts were exposed to the same environmental conditions, temperature metrics were applied to all outdoor cohorts, regardless of sampling origin. A small number of missing hourly records (<0.01%) were excluded prior to analysis. For each overwintering period, the following temperature variables were derived:

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.1 (R Core Team, R Foundation for Statistical Computing 2024) using the packages glmmTMB (Brook et al. 2017), lme4 (Bates et al. 2015), DHARMa (Hartig 2022), emmeans (Lenth 2023), performance (Lüdecke et al. 2021), and tidyverse (Wickham et al. 2019).

Mortality was recorded at the cohort level as the total number of dead individuals out of the total number of individuals per cohort (binomial count data). Sex was recorded for each individual and summarized at the cohort level. Where relevant, mortality rates were calculated separately for females and males using pooled counts rather than averaged percentages for each cohort, thereby accounting for unequal sample sizes among cohorts and years.

Data were analyzed using a beta-binomial generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with a logit link. Treatment (laboratory vs. outdoor), overwintering period (year), sex, and standardized initial cohort size were included as fixed effects. The factor “overwintering period (year)” captures interannual variation but does not represent specific temperature effects, which were evaluated separately using descriptive temperature metrics. Because geographic coordinates were not available for all cohorts, spatial effects were not modeled explicitly. Cohort identity was included as a random intercept to account for non-independence and unmeasured heterogeneity among cohorts. Initial cohort size was centered and scaled prior to analysis. Model selection was based on likelihood ratio tests and Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC).

Mortality in the fecund (F) versus non-fecund (NF) experiment was analyzed using a beta-binomial generalized linear model (GLM) with a logit function. Reproductive group (F vs. NF), sex, and adult age at transfer to diapause conditions were treated as fixed effects. Interaction terms were evaluated but not retained when unsupported by model comparison. Model assumptions and dispersion were assessed using simulation-based residual diagnostics implemented in the R package DHARMa (Hartig 2022). Fixed-effect estimates are presented on the log-odds scale with corresponding standard errors and Wald z-tests. Where appropriate, estimated marginal means and model-based contrasts were calculated on the response scale. Statistical significance was assessed at α = 0.05.

Associations between overwintering mortality and temperature metrics under outdoor conditions were explored descriptively. Because of the small sample size (years = 4), no formal statistical modelling was performed, and no temperature variables were included as predictors in the GLMM. To facilitate comparison among variables with different units, temperature and mortality metrics were standardized using z-scores prior to visualization.

Results

Overwintering Mortality Under Laboratory and Outdoor Conditions

Across 4 overwintering periods (2020/2021 to 2023/2024), a total of 5,242 individuals were monitored, including 2,361 females and 2,881 males under laboratory conditions and 961 females and 1,151 males under outdoor conditions (Table 1).

Observed mean cohort mortality under laboratory conditions averaged 48.0% (SD = 22.0 among cohorts) in 2020/2021, 57.2% (SD = 26.6%) in 2021/2022, 21.5% (SD = 16.1%) in 2022/2023, and 65.0% (SD = 12.7%) in 2023/2024. Corresponding mortality rates under outdoor conditions were 58.9% (SD = 11.5%), 35.2% (SD = 23.3%), 56.0% (SD = 27.6%), and 39.0% (SD = 21.7%), respectively.

Substantial variation in mortality was observed among cohorts, with a random-intercept variance of 1.09 (SD = 1.04 on the logit scale), indicating pronounced heterogeneity at the cohort level (Table 3). The dispersion parameter of the GLMM was 38.1.

Fecund Versus Non-fecund Overwintering Survival

Overwintering mortality differed between fecund (F) and non-fecund (NF) individuals (Fig. 3). The F cohorts showed a mean mortality rate of 99.3% (SD = 2.1% among cohorts), whereas NF individuals exhibited 20.6% (SD = 10.7%). These descriptive values represent variation among cohorts. The confidence intervals shown in Fig. 3 are model-based estimates derived from the beta-binomial generalized linear model (GLM). The GLM revealed a strong effect of reproductive group on mortality (estimate = −5.40 ± 0.76 SE, z = −7.07, P < 0.001; Table 4). Sex (P = 0.52) and adult age at transfer to diapause conditions (P = 0.35) had no significant effects, and inclusion of age did not improve model fit (ΔAIC = 1.2). Overwintering Temperature Under Outdoor Conditions

Winter thermal conditions recorded inside cages during the semi-field trials differed among overwintering periods (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table S2). The coldest overwintering period was 2020/2021, with a minimum hourly temperature of −12.4 °C, 225 cumulative hours below −5 °C, and 24 h below −10 °C. In contrast, 2023/2024 showed the highest minimum temperature of −7.3 °C and no exposure below −10 °C. In 2021/2022 extreme cold events were limited (1 h < −10 °C) despite intermediate minimum temperatures.

Overwintering mortality tended to be higher in winters characterized by lower minimum temperatures and greater cumulative cold exposure (Fig. 4), although patterns were not fully consistent across the years investigated. The coldest winter (2020/2021), which exhibited the lowest minimum temperature and the highest number of hours below −5 °C and −10 °C, was associated with high mortality in both females (59.4%) and males (55.0%). In contrast, mortality was lower during winters with fewer extreme cold events (eg 2021/2022: 28.3% in females and 34.8% in males), although substantial mortality was also recorded during comparatively mild minimum temperatures (2022/2023: 56.9% in females and 60.6% in males; see Supplementary Table S2).

Discussion

This study examined overwintering mortality in H. halys across 4 consecutive winters under laboratory and semi-field conditions, with a particular focus on reproductive state and cohort variation. In this study, cohorts were defined as groups of individuals collected during the same collection event. By combining controlled overwintering trials with field-collected cohorts, we show that survival is strongly constrained by physiological state prior to overwintering and varies substantially among cohorts overwintered under identical conditions. These findings indicate that overwintering mortality in H. halys cannot be reliably characterized by a single survival estimate and highlight the importance of accounting for physiological composition and cohort heterogeneity when interpreting winter survival data.

Physiological State and Reproductive Status

Our comparison of reproductively mature (fecund, F) and non-mature (non-fecund, NF) adults supports previous findings that reproductively active individuals show poor overwintering survival because they do not reliably enter diapause (Nielsen et al. 2016, Cira et al. 2018). In our trial, NF individuals showed significantly lower mortality (20.6%) than F individuals (99.3%) (Fig. 3). Diapause induction enhances cold tolerance by suppressing metabolism and conserving energy reserves (Denlinger 2002, Hahn and Denlinger 2007), whereas reproductively active individuals typically maintain higher metabolic rates and accumulate fewer lipid reserves, leaving them poorly equipped to withstand prolonged unfavorable conditions (Koštál 2006, Sinclair 2015).

Although the duration under pre-diapause environmental conditions differed between pathways in order to induce reproductive activity under long-day conditions, adult age at transfer to diapause conditions was similar between groups (Table 2). Adult age did not significantly affect mortality in the statistical model (Table 4). Survival differences therefore reflect reproductive/developmental status rather than age per se, although differing pre-diapause environmental exposure may have contributed to physiological variation.

The strong contrast between F and NF cohorts underscores the limitations of extrapolating survival from mixed field-collected populations. In regions with 2 annual generations, such as lower elevated areas of South Tyrol (Fischnaller and Wolf 2021), early-autumn collections may include both reproductively active and diapause-induced adults. Such mixed populations could lead to higher observed overwintering mortality in trials.

Environmental Drivers of Mortality

Although physiological state strongly influenced survival, environmental conditions also appeared to contribute to overwintering outcomes. Across the 4 winters examined, higher mortality descriptively coincided with lower minimum temperatures and greater cumulative cold exposure (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table S2). The coldest winter (2020/2021) exhibited the highest mortality, whereas other winters showed variable mortality despite relatively mild seasonal means, indicating that the effects of temperature on survival were not consistent across contexts.

These observations align with the expectation that prolonged cold exposure and thermal instability can reduce overwintering success in chill-susceptible insects (Williams et al. 2015, Roe et al. 2024). Similarly, Scaccini et al. (2020) showed that mortality in H. halys increases with cold exposure duration and susceptibility differs between adults entering versus exiting overwintering microhabitats, with particularly high mortality associated with extreme frost events.

Prolonged low temperatures may increase the risk of chill-injury, while fluctuating conditions can elevate metabolic demand and accelerate energy depletion (Denlinger 2002, Sinclair et al. 2003, Hahn and Denlinger 2007, Klockmann and Fischer 2019). Notably, after accounting for cohort effects and cohort size, overwintering mortality did not differ between laboratory and outdoor treatments (Table 3), suggesting that the impact of temperature conditions is strongly modulated by cohort-specific physiological state rather than temperature stability alone.

Cohort Effect and Experimental Variability

Cohort identity had a strong and consistent influence on overwintering mortality across all treatments (random-intercept variance = 1.09, SD = 1.04 on the logit scale), indicating pronounced heterogeneity among cohorts. The magnitude of this cohort-level variation was comparable to, or greater than, most fixed effects in the GLMM (Table 3). Furthermore, substantial differences in mortality were observed not only between laboratory and outdoor treatments but also among cohorts overwintered under identical conditions (Table 3). These differences likely reflect variation in cohort composition, including age structure, physiological condition, nutritional reserves, and pathogen exposure. Because detailed spatial information was unavailable for all cohorts, environmental history could not be evaluated. It is therefore encompassed within cohort identity.

Taken together with the F versus NF laboratory trial, the elevated mortality observed in fecund individuals indicates that early-autumn collections containing both reproductive and diapause-directed adults may bias overwintering survival estimates upward. Consequently, treatment or climate effects may be obscured if physiological composition is not considered, as responses to environmental conditions such as temperature likely depend on cohort-specific physiological state. Although the F versus NF experiment was initially intended to provide a baseline survival reference, the wide inter-cohort variation observed in field-collections suggests that no single baseline value is broadly applicable.

Initial cohort size had a significant positive effect on mortality (β = 0.14 ± 0.06 SE; Fig. 2). Although aggregation commonly enhances overwintering survival through buffering of environmental stressors (Lee et al. 2014, Ciancio et al. 2021, Szejner-Sigal and Williams 2022), the observed increase in mortality with cohort size is not consistent with aggregation-associated increases in diapause incidence. If larger cohorts primarily reflected aggregations of individuals with higher diapause propensity, reduced mortality would be expected.

In H. halys, aggregation behavior is mediated by pheromones and is primarily expressed during the late season (eg Lee et al. 2014) but may not be exclusively linked to diapause status. As a result, sampled cohorts may include both diapause-induced and non-diapause individuals. Instead, higher density under confined experimental conditions may amplify negative density-dependent effects, including increased pathogen transmission (Hajek et al. 2023). Such density-dependent effects may also obscure any potential survival advantage associated with higher diapause incidence.

Sex effects were weak and varied across trials. Although male mortality tended to be higher in the overwintering experiment, consistent with previous studies (Cira et al. 2016, Skillman et al. 2018), no sex effect was detected in the F versus NF trial. This suggests that sex-specific differences may interact with physiological state and environmental context rather than representing a uniform factor.

Mortality rates observed under laboratory conditions (48.0% to 65.0%) and outdoor conditions (35.2-58.9%) were comparable to estimates reported previously (Haye et al. 2014, Costi et al. 2017, Taylor et al. 2017, Rot et al. 2022). The broad range reported across studies reinforces that cohort composition, storage duration, local climate, and underlying physiological state collectively shape overwintering outcome.

Additional Factors: Nutrition, Microbiota, and Pathogens

Beyond physiological and thermal variables, additional biological factors may contribute to overwintering heterogeneity. Nutritional status prior to diapause is critical for survival (Skillman et al. 2018, Short and Hahn 2023). Spatial variation in host plant use during late summer and autumn in South Tyrol (Fischnaller et al. 2024, Fluch et al. 2025) may influence cohort-level energy reserves. Aggregation behavior may enhance survival through thermal buffering but can also increase pathogen transmission (Danks 1987, Hajek et al. 2023). Pathogens such as Nosema maddoxi and Pseudomonas spp. have been detected in overwintering H. halys and linked to reduced survival or fitness (Hajek et al. 2018, Preston et al. 2020a,b, Kereselidze et al. 2020, Piccinno et al. 2025). Gut microbiota may also influence overwintering performance, although current evidence remains mixed (Ferguson et al. 2018, Fluch et al. 2024, Piccinno et al. 2025).

Conclusion and Outlook

Overall, our results demonstrate that overwintering survival in H. halys is strongly influenced by physiological state and cohort heterogeneity, with temperature effects varying depending on cohort-specific conditions. Substantial variation persisted even under standardized laboratory conditions, indicating that differences among cohorts can outweigh temperature effects. Consequently, overwintering mortality cannot be represented by a single universal survival parameter derived from autumn collections without accounting for cohort structure and reproductive state.

These findings highlight the importance of targeted timing of sampling in the field for survival studies. Sampling in the late seasons or from established overwintering shelters, combined with noninvasive physiological screening where feasible, would improve the interpretability of overwintering mortality estimates and enhance their utility for pest management under current and future climatic conditions.
