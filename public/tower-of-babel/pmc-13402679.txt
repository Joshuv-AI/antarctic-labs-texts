GPSM1 restricts CD73+CD103+ Treg cells in adipose tissue, critical for promoting obesity-related metabolic deterioration
Authors: Xiao-Rui Lyu, Rui-Bing Qi, Yan-Li Hua, Wei Chen, Yi-Xuan Li, Dai-Xi Wang, Li-Jun Yao, Hong-Li Chen, Jiang-Fei Zheng, Bao-Cheng Wan, Yang Hua, Feng Jiang, Fan Yang, Jia-Yu Ma, Xiang-Hui Chen, Rong Zhang, Jing Yan, Wei-Ping Jia, Cheng Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Regulatory T (Treg) cells are considered as key regulators of adipose homeostasis and metabolic health. However, the underlying regulatory mechanisms remain unclear. Here we show that expression of G-protein-signaling modulator 1 (GPSM1) in CD4+ T cells in peripheral blood and visceral fat is significantly upregulated in humans upon obesity and glucose dysregulation. Genomic deletion of GPSM1 in CD4+ T cells or Treg cells in mice results in increased numbers of Treg cells in adipose tissues, restrained inflammation and improved insulin and glucose tolerance upon feeding with high fat diet. These metabolic changes are mediated by the maintenance of a specific CD73+CD103+ Treg cell subpopulation. By contrast, mice with CD4+ T-cell-specific overexpression of GPSM1 are characterized by decreased numbers of Treg cells and are more prone to adipose tissue dysfunction and metabolic deterioration. Mechanistically, a RHOA-cell stiffness-TAZ axis mediates the effects of GPSM1 on the abundance of Treg cells. Furthermore, adoptive transfer of GPSM1-deficent Treg cells promotes energy expenditure and improve glucose and lipid metabolism in Rag1 −/− mice. In summary, GSPM1 expression in Treg cells and especially in a subset specialized for metabolic regulation is an important regulator of the overall energy homeostasis.

Introduction

Adipose tissue plays essential roles in maintaining the metabolic homeostasis1,2. In addition to the same functions for fat storage and release as visceral white adipose tissue, subcutaneous white adipose tissue (scWAT) could dissipate excess energy through adaptive thermogenesis to sustain metabolic health3–5 Therefore, it is considered as a promising strategy to target adipocyte thermogenesis for obesity and related disorders.

Current studies mainly focus on the contribution of adipocytes to thermogenesis, while immunocytes also take part in the process of adipose thermogenesis6,7. In addition to M2 macrophage, more and more studies have gradually paid attention to the role of regulatory T cells (Tregs) on adipose homeostasis8–10. With the development of single-cell RNA sequencing(scRNA-seq), the subsets-serum stimulation 2-positive (ST2+) and C–X–C motif chemokine receptor 3-positive (CXCR3+) Tregs have been identified to reside in visceral WAT and response to PPARγ signaling that contribute to regulating insulin sensitivity and restraining inflammation during obesity11,12. As Tregs of visceral and subcutaneous WAT might represent different physiological functions, Kalin et al. and Kong et al. reported that beta3-adrenergic receptor agonist stimulation could induce expansion of Tregs in scWAT and make them produce adenosine to promote beige fat thermogenesis13,14. Although the pro-thermogenesis effects of Tregs have been proposed, however, which subsets of Tregs account for adipose thermogenesis and homeostasis, as well as the underlying regulatory mechanisms remain unclear.

G-protein-signaling modulator 1 (GPSM1), which functions as an activator of heterotrimeric G-protein signaling in receptor-independent way15, has been studied in our lab for decades. Our previous work elucidated that GPSM1 expressed in monocytes/macrophages evoked NF-κB and MAPK signaling to promote metabolic inflammation and metabolic dysfunctions, indicating that GPSM1 acts as a link between innate immune responses and systemic metabolic homeostasis16,17. Moreover, one study has reported that deletion of GPSM1 impaired the chemotaxis property of T cells during acute inflammation18, therefore, we hypothesized that GPSM1 might also be involved in T cells-mediated chronic metabolic inflammation.

Here, we find that GPSM1 expression in CD4+ T cells isolated from peripheral blood or visceral fat is positively correlated with human obesity and glucose dysregulation. Through constructing CD4+ T cell and Treg cell-specific GPSM1 knockout mice models, we demonstrate that GPSM1 loss counteracts adipose tissue dysfunction and metabolic deterioration during obesity. Single-nucleus RNA-seq reveals GPSM1 loss maintains the numbers of CD73+CD103+ Treg subpopulation in adipose tissues, which contributes to adipose thermogenesis and anti-inflammatory phenotype. By contrast, CD4+ T cell-specific GPSM1 overexpression mice exhibit impaired adipose tissue function and metabolic homeostasis. Mechanistically, GPSM1 loss promotes Treg number through downregulating RHOA-cell stiffness-TAZ axis. Finally, adoptive transfer of GPSM1-deficent Tregs display enhanced energy expenditure and potential anti-obesity effects on Rag1−/− mice. Our findings indicate targeting GPSM1 of Tregs has a therapeutic potential against obesity and related disorders.

Results

GPSM1 is a regulator of Tregs and metabolic homeostasis

We first explored the relationship of GPSM1 expressions in T cells and human obesity. The GPSM1 protein levels were increased (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1a) in peripheral blood CD4+ T cells isolated from individuals with overweight or obesity (BMI ≥ 24), compared with that from individuals with normal weight (BMI <24). Besides, GPSM1 expressions in human blood CD4+ T cells were positively correlated with glucose metabolism indicators, including BMI, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) and C-peptide (Fig. 1b–e). In addition, immunofluorescence showed that obesity induced much more GPSM1 expression also in human CD4+ T cells of visceral fat collected in our previous study16 (Fig. 1f), and RT-qPCR revealed that GPSM1 mRNA levels were also positively associated with metabolic traits of donors undergoing bariatric surgery, including BMI, waist, insulin, HOMA-IR and HbA1c (Fig. 1g–k). Consistently, our data also indicated that mice with high fat diet (HFD)-induced obesity (DIO) exhibited higher GPSM1 protein levels in CD4+T cells of both scWAT and eWAT compared with lean control mice (Fig. 1l, m and Supplementary Fig. 1b, c). Next, we sought to explore the role of GPSM1 on regulating CD4+ T cell subsets. Intraperitoneal application of the small-molecule compound-AN-465/42243987 that could suppress GPSM1’s function in our previous study16, showed an increased numbers of Tregs both in the scWAT and eWAT, whereas comparable numbers of Th1, Th2 and Th17 cells (Supplementary Fig. 1d–g). Furthermore, we found that AN-465/42243987 treatment could directly enhance human and murine Treg cell induction in vitro (Supplementary Fig. 1h–k), indicating that GPSM1 might be an important regulator of Treg polarization and function. In agreement with these, GPSM1 mRNA and protein levels were obviously decreased in Tregs compared with Th0 cells (Fig. 1n, o and Supplementary Fig. 1l); knockdown of GPSM1 in murine naive CD4+ T (TN) cells by using lentiviral short hairpin RNA (shRNA) specific for GPSM1 (sh-GPSM1) obviously enhanced Treg numbers as shown by increased FOXP3 expression (Fig. 1p, q and Supplementary Fig. 1m). By contrast, adenovirus mediated GPSM1-overexpression showed significantly inhibitory effects on both mice and human CD4+ TN cells towards Treg polarization (Fig. 1r–u and Supplementary Fig. 1n, o). Overall, the results suggest that GPSM1 might play a pivotal role in Treg polarization and development of obesity and related metabolic disturbance.

GPSM1 deficiency in CD4+ and Tregs maintains adipose Tregs homeostasis

To further understand the role of GPSM1 in Treg in vivo, we generated a CD4+ T cell GPSM1-knockout (GPSM1ΔT) mouse model by intercrossing mice bearing a conditional loxP-flanked (‘floxed’) allele of GPSM1 (GPSM1f/f, used as control mice) with CD4-cre mice. Immunoblot analysis confirmed efficient depletion of GPSM1 in CD4+ T cells and spleen, but not in eWAT, scWAT, liver, muscle and hypothalamus (Supplementary Fig. 2a-i). We then examined whether GPSM1 deficiency influenced the CD4+ T cell, CD8+ T cell and CD4+ T cell subset numbers in GPSM1f/f and GPSM1ΔT mice under normal chow diet (NCD) feeding for 24 weeks. No difference in the number of splenic CD4+ T and CD8+ T cells was observed in GPSM1f/f mice and GPSM1ΔT mice by flow cytometric analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2j). Notably, GPSM1ΔT mice exhibited mildly increased splenic Treg numbers, whereas Th1, Th2 and Th17 cell were comparable with GPSM1f/f mice (Supplementary Fig. 2k, l). Consistently, the frequencies of Treg in both scWAT and eWAT were higher in GPSM1ΔT mice (Fig. 2a). Next, we evaluated the phenotypes under high fat diet (HFD) feeding for 12 weeks. In agreement with the NCD condition, HFD-fed GPSM1ΔT mice had more splenic Tregs, while the numbers of CD4+ T cell, CD8+ T cell and other CD4+ T cell subsets were comparable between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 2o–q). Consistently, the numbers of Treg exhibited obvious increase in scWAT and eWAT of HFD-fed GPSM1ΔT mice, respectively (Fig. 2b). Gene expression profiling further revealed that, in HFD-fed GPSM1ΔT mice, the mRNA levels of the Treg-associated anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and IL-33 were increased by 2.0-fold and 1.9-fold, respectively, in scWAT; Similarly, IL-10 and IL-33 expression levels were elevated by 1.9-fold and 1.3-fold, respectively, in eWAT (Fig. 2c, d).Moreover, serum multiple-cytokine assays showed that HFD-fed GPSM1ΔT mice had obviously increased levels of Treg-related anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10, while slightly decreased pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including IL-1α, IL-1β, IFNγ, CCL2 and CXCL1, compared with those levels in control mice (Fig. 2e). In addition, GPSM1ΔT mice exhibited a marked decrease in F4/80⁺ crown-like structures (CLS), fewer CD11c⁺ macrophages and more in CD206⁺ macrophages in both scWAT and eWAT, compared with control mice (Supplementary Fig. 2r–v).

We then generated a Treg-specific GPSM1-knockout (GPSM1ΔTreg) mouse model by intercrossing GPSM1f/f mice with FOXP3-YFP-Cre mice. Successful depletion of GPSM1 in Tregs and spleen was confirmed by immunoblotting (Supplementary Fig. 3a–i). Under NCD feeding condition, GPSM1ΔTreg mice exhibited modest increases in Treg numbers in the spleen, scWAT and eWAT compared with their FOXP3-YFP-Cre counterparts (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 3j, k). Meanwhile, GPSM1ΔTreg mice also showed modest increases in TGFβ1 levels of both serum and adipose tissue (Supplementary Fig. 3l, m). Under HFD feeding condition, obvious increases Treg numbers were detected in the spleen, scWAT and eWAT of GPSM1ΔTreg mice (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 3n, o). Similar to GPSM1ΔT mice, Treg GPSM1 deficiency also induced enhanced mRNA levels of IL-33 in scWAT, as well as the mRNA levels of IL-33 and IL-10 in eWAT (Fig. 2h, i). Collectively, these data imply that GPSM1 deletion in both CD4+ T cell and Treg sustain the homeostasis of spleen and adipose Treg, thereby ameliorating systemic and adipose inflammation.

GPSM1 deficiency in Tregs protects mice against diet-induced obesity and metabolic dysfunctions

Next, GPSM1ΔTreg mice were used to validate the beneficial effects of GPSM1 loss on metabolic phenotypes. Consistent with their decreased body weight of GPSM1ΔT mice, GPSM1ΔTreg mice also exhibited significantly reduced lipid accumulation (Fig. 3j–m and Supplementary Fig. 5a–e). Moreover, the serum leptin and insulin levels of GPSM1ΔTreg mice were decreased to 52.0% and 30.1% compared to those of GPSM1f/f mice, respectively, whereas the serum adiponectin levels were slightly increased (Fig. 3n–p). GPSM1ΔTreg mice exhibited greatly improved glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity, with increased AKT phosphorylation, the marker of intracellular insulin signaling improvement, in scWAT, eWAT and liver, when compared with GPSM1f/f mice (Fig. 3q–w and Supplementary Fig. 5f–h). In addition, hepatic steatosis, serum ALT and AST levels were also decreased in GPSM1ΔTreg mice (Fig. 3x, y and Supplementary Fig. 5i–l). Furthermore, HFD-fed female GPSM1ΔT and GPSM1ΔTreg mice exhibited phenotypic changes similar to those of male mice (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). In Brief, our data demonstrate that GPSM1 deficiency in Treg cell can alleviate diet-induced obesity and metabolic disorders, regardless of sex.

Subsequently, to assess whether increased Treg number and diminished systemic inflammation was causatively related to CD4+T cell GPSM1 loss or whether it was merely a result of decreased body weight under a HFD feeding for 12 weeks, we characterized GPSM1f/f and GPSM1ΔT mice at a short-term HFD feeding for 5 weeks. Despite comparable body weight, fat mass, glucose tolerance, and insulin sensitivity between GPSM1ΔT and control mice (Fig. 4a–g), the GPSM1ΔT mice exhibited significantly higher oxygen consumption and energy expenditure, without differences in food intake or physical activity (Fig. 4h–n). Consistent with this enhanced metabolic activity, GPSM1ΔT mice showed increased Treg numbers in the spleen, scWAT and eWAT (Fig. 4o–r). Concurrently, serum TGFβ1 and mRNA levels of TGFβ1 and IL-10 were markedly elevated in scWAT and eWAT (Fig. 4s–u). Notably, the scWAT Tregs co-expressed CD73 (Nt5e) and low levels of ST2 (Il1rl1) and PPARγ, which is transcriptionally resembled PPARγlow splenic precursors, rather than visceral WAT Tregs expressing high expression of ST2 or CXCR311,12 (Supplementary Fig. 8e-h). Further visualization with Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) divided Tregs into two subpopulation (Cluster 0 and Cluster 1), both of which were increased in scWAT of GPSM1ΔTreg mice compared to control mice (Fig. 5c). Biological process (BP) analysis revealed that insulin related signaling were significantly decreased in total Tregs (Supplementary Fig. 8i). Cluster 1 expressed lymphoid tissue-associated marker, such as Sell, BACH2 and SATB1 (Fig. 5d), that indicate T cell differentiation and activation (Supplementary Fig. 8j)20–22; By contrast, Cluster 0 was featured by highly expression of integrin alpha E (ITGAE, also named as CD103) (Fig. 5d). BP analysis revealed that cluster 0 mainly orchestrated tissue homeostasis and cell-substrate adhesion, while inhibiting the macrophage activation and inflammation (Fig. 5e). Next, we used flow cytometric analysis to confirm that CD73+CD103+ Treg subtype was indeed significantly increased in scWAT of GPSM1ΔTreg mice under HFD condition (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 8k). As CD103 has been considered as an adhesion protein, we further sorted CD73+CD103+ Treg and CD73+CD103- Tregs to let them adhere to endothelial cells and found that CD103+CD73+ Tregs exhibited higher adherent ability (Supplementary Fig. 8l, m).

Comprehensive phenotyping revealed that GPSM1 deletion in Tregs exerted a selective effect on suppressive markers while sparing memory/naïve identity. Under basal conditions, GPSM1 deficiency altered only CD73 expression among key functional markers (CD39, CD73, CD25, CTLA4), without affecting memory/naïve markers (CD62L, Ly-6C, TNFRII) in either spleen or adipose tissue (Supplementary Fig. 9). Thus, collectively, these findings demonstrate that GPSM1 deficiency in Tregs leads to a specific increase in the abundance of CD73+CD103+ Treg subpopulation in scWAT.

GPSM1 deficiency in Tregs promotes adipose tissue thermogenesis and attenuates macrophage-mediated inflammation

In addition to the altered Treg subset, Treg GPSM1 loss also led to a change in the proportion of four adipocyte subsets that were defined according to the previous studies (Supplementary Fig. 10a)18. The proportions of mAd1, mAd2, and mAd3 were all decreased, whereas only mAd4 was increased to 4.2-fold in GPSM1ΔTreg mice compared with the control mice (Fig. 5g, h). Interestingly, BP analysis showed that “cold-induced thermogenesis” and “adaptive thermogenesis” were the most enriched biologic functions of the mAd4 subpopulation (Fig. 5i and Supplementary Fig. 10b), suggesting that GPSM1 deficiency-derived metabolic improvement was partially due to the increased adipocyte thermogenesis of scWAT. Thus, we monitored the oxygen consumption (VO2) and energy expenditure (EE) of NCD-fed two group mice. GPSM1ΔTreg mice had similar food intake and physical activity in comparable with GPSM1f/f mice, however, their VO2 and EE were slightly higher than control mice (Supplementary Fig. 10c-i); This effect was further significantly augmented by intraperitoneally injecting β3 receptor agonist, CL-316243 to GPSM1f/f and GPSM1ΔTreg mice (Fig. 5j, k and Supplementary Fig. 10j–m). Besides, GPSM1ΔTreg mice had higher serum adenosine levels (Fig.5l) and increased oxygen consumption and UCP1 expression of scWAT (Fig. 5m, n and Supplementary Fig. 10n, o). Next, in vitro studies by collecting conditioned media (CM) from sorted CD73+ CD103+ Treg versus CD73+CD103- Treg to treat differentiated primary beige adipocytes revealed that adipocytes treated with CD73+CD103+ Treg CM had increased oxygen consumption and UCP1 protein levels (Fig. 5o, p and Supplementary Fig. 10p), indicating the increased energy expenditure after GPSM1 loss derives from pro-thermogenesis effects of CD73+ CD103+ Treg.

Furthermore, GPSM1ΔTreg mice also exhibited obvious changes in adipose tissue macrophages (ATM) (Fig. 5q and Supplementary Fig. 10q), mainly manifested by the proportion of lipid-associated macrophages (LAM) and collagen-expressing macrophages (CEM) significantly decreased (Fig. 5r). Flow cytometric analysis validated the snRNA-seq data that HFD GPSM1ΔTreg mice had less TREM2+ LAM of scWAT (Fig.5s and Supplementary Fig. 10r). As BP analysis revealed that inflammation and lipid handing pathway were mainly enriched in LAM subpopulation and LAM contributed to a pro-inflammatory phenotype of ATM (Fig. 5t). In addition, cell-cell communication analysis revealed that GPSM1 deficiency augmented signaling from Tregs to macrophages. This enhancement specifically involved interactions between PTPRC (encoding CD45) in Tregs and MRC1 (encoding CD206) across macrophage subsets, indicating a shift toward a more anti-inflammatory state within the adipose tissue immune landscape (Supplementary Fig. 10s). Next, in vitro studies demonstrated that CD73+CD103+ Tregs display higher IL-10 mRNA levels than CD73+CD103- Tregs (Fig. 5u). we further collected CM from CD73+ CD103+ Tregs versus CD73+CD103- Tregs to treat bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) in vitro. CD73+CD103+ Tregs CM-treated BMDMs had a notably reduction in the mRNA levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines under LPS stimulation (Fig. 5v). Taken together, our results prove once again that GPSM1 deletion in Tregs attenuates macrophage-mediated inflammation by enhancing the proportion and anti-inflammatory function of the CD73⁺CD103⁺ Tregs subpopulation.

GPSM1 deficiency promotes the abundance of Tregs via cAMP-PKA-RHOA-cell stiffness-TAZ axis

As shown in snRNA-seq, GPSM1 deletion led to a notable increase in numbers of Tregs. Next, we investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying the effects of GPSM1 on the abundance of Tregs. GPSM1 loss and overexpression in Tregs showed no effects on the expression of Ki67, Annexin V, Nur77, CD44, CD25 and CD69 compared to control cells, indicating that GPSM1 induced Treg expansion was independent of cell proliferation, apoptosis and early activation of Tregs (Supplementary Fig. 11a–f). We then performed proteomic sequencing on GPSM1f/f versus GPSM1ΔT Tregs to address this issue. Detailed analysis identified a total of 257 differentially expressed proteins (201 upregulated, 56 downregulated, FDR <0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 11g, h). Pathway analysis showed that “signaling by Rho GTPases, Miro GTPases and RHOBTB3” were significantly enriched (Fig. 6a). To explore this possibility, we detected the expressions of RHOA, Rac1 and CDC42, the three classic members of Rho GTPases family23. The results showed that only decreased RHOA expression and activity were observed in GPSM1 loss Tregs when compared with control Tregs (Fig. 6b, c and Supplementary Fig. 11i); On the contrary, GPSM1 overexpression in Tregs led to increased RHOA expression and activity (Fig. 6d, e and Supplementary Fig. 11j). However, administration with rhosin hydrochloride, a selective RHOA inhibitor, could partially reversed the decreased numbers of Tregs in GPSM1-overexpressing Tregs (Fig. 6f, g), replying that RHOA mediated the GPSM1-induced Treg abundance.

RHOA is known as a key regulator of cell mechanical properties that could active mechanical stimuli sensors YAP1/TAZ24, and TAZ has been reported to have an impact on FOXP3 transcription25, therefore, we hypothesize that whether GPSM1 could mediate RHOA-YAP/TAZ signaling to influence FOXP3 expression. We first checked whether cell stiffness might differ due to GPSM1 loss. The results revealed that GPSM1-deficient Tregs exhibited softer cell stiffness, as the storage modulus was lower than control cells (Fig. 6h, i). Treatment with rhosin hydrochloride also blocked the enhanced cell stiffness in GPSM1 overexpressing cells (Fig, 6j, k). Subsequently, immunoblot analysis showed that the protein level and nuclear translocation of YAP1/TAZ were significantly reduced in GPSM1 loss Tregs (Fig. 6l–p, and Supplementary Fig. 11k); By contrast, GPSM1 overexpression elevated the cell stiffness and YAP1/TAZ expression (Fig. 6j, k, q and Supplementary Fig. 11l). However, only TAZ overexpression could partially block the increased FOXP3 expression in GPSM1-deficient Tregs (Fig. 6r, s), indicating TAZ might act as the downstream target of GPSM1-RHOA-cell stiffness. In agreement with the in vitro data, GPSM1ΔTreg mice also showed lower storage modulus of scWAT relative to GPSM1f/f mice (Fig. 6t, u); Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) demonstrated the downregulation of YAP1/TAZ pathway in the GPSM1-deficient Tregs in snRNA-seq analysis (Fig. 6v). Finally, considering that cyclic AMP (cAMP) / protein kinase A (PKA) pathway serves as important upstream regulators of RHOA activity26 and “cAMP-mediated signaling” was upregulated in the BP of cluster 0 Treg subset (Fig. 5e), we further investigated whether the cAMP-PKA pathway was the potential molecular link between GPSM1 and RHOA. We found that GPSM1 deficiency indeed increased cAMP levels and p-PKA expression (Fig. 6w, x and Supplementary Fig. 11m). However, the selective PKA inhibitor H89 treatment partially abolished the inhibition of RHOA activity (Fig. 6y) thereby inhibiting the promotion of Treg numbers in GPSM1-deficient cells (Fig. 6z). By contrast, GPSM1 overexpression decreased cAMP levels and p-PKA expression (Supplementary Fig. 11n–p). These data demonstrate that GPSM1 deletion promotes Treg abundance and function through cAMP-PKA-RHOA-cell stiffness-TAZ axis.

Discussion

Studies have proposed that Tregs serve as a key regulator of adipose homeostasis and glycolipid metabolism. Present studies mainly focus on Tregs in the visceral adipose tissue, however, the precise subpopulations and underlying regulators of Tregs in scWAT still have not been clearly clarified. Here, we identify that loss of GPSM1 preserves CD73+CD103+ Treg subpopulation in scWAT, thereby promoting adipose thermogenesis, suppressing inflammatory infiltration in scWAT, and contributing to the maintenance of metabolic homeostasis. Mechanically, GPSM1 loss downregulates RHOA-cell stiffness-TAZ axis through upregulating cAMP-PKA signaling, that ultimately increases the abundance of Tregs (Fig. 9). Taken together, our findings reveal a regulator of Tregs and suggest that targeting GPSM1 of Treg might be a promising therapy to combat obesity and related disorders.Fig. 9Graphical illustration of GPSM1 as a regulator of CD73+CD103+ Treg and metabolic homeostasis through RHOA-cell stiffness-TAZ axis.Graphical illustration drawn by figdraw.com.

In the present study, CD4+T or Treg-specific GPSM1 KO mice both displayed reduced body weight, improved glucose and insulin tolerance, and alleviated liver steatosis in response to overnutrition. Based on the short-term HFD feeding experiment, we identified a temporal sequence underlying metabolic improvement. Our data showed that GPSM1 deletion drove an early increase in adipose tissue Treg proportions prior to significant improvement in glucose and lipid metabolism. Notably, GPSM1ΔT mice exhibited elevated oxygen consumption and energy expenditure without altered food intake, consistent with evidence that adipose Tregs promoted energy expenditure via adipocyte thermogenesis12,14,27. Thus, we propose a sequential model: GPSM1 deficiency promotes early Treg accumulation in adipose tissue upon metabolic stress, which enhances energy expenditure. This increase prevents weight gain, improves adipose tissue function, prevents ectopic lipid deposition, and ultimately enhances systemic insulin sensitivity. Besides, female GPSM1ΔT and GPSM1ΔTreg mice exhibited similar improved metabolic phenotype to those of male mice, indicating GPSM1 loss-derived metabolic improvement was independent of sex.

Although adipose Tregs play an important role in metabolic health, they exhibit heterogeneous phenotypes under different adipose microenvironments. Previous studies have explored the subsets and function of Tregs residing in eWAT by utilizing scRNA-seq. For instance, ST2+ Tregs have been considered as the main subset in male, while CXCR3+ Tregs mainly existed in female, both of which were responsible for restraining inflammation and regulating insulin sensitivity during obesity11,12,28. However, the studies focusing on Tregs of scWAT are limited. Kong et al. reported that scWAT Tregs could produce adenosine, which was converted to inosine by adipocyte-derived adenosine deaminases, activating adenosine A2a receptor in adipocyte and consequentially contributing to adipocyte browning14. Nevertheless, whether scWAT Tregs exhibit diversity and what regulatory signals control the abundance of Treg remain elusive. Here, we identified a CD73+ CD103+ Treg subset in scWAT; GPSM1 loss could further increase the numbers of CD73+CD103+ Treg. Moreover, we found that CD73+CD103+ Tregs displayed pro-thermogenetic and anti-inflammatory capacity relative to CD73+CD103- Tregs. The phenomenon was consistent with the findings that splenic primary CD8+CD103+ Tregs show higher expression of CD39 marker in vitro29, which could help CD73 for adenosine production by hydrolyzing extracellular ATP into AMP30, and that’s why CD73+ CD103+ Tregs showed higher pro-thermogenic capacity. The metabolic cage experiment also indicated that GPSM1ΔTreg mice showed a higher energy expenditure than control mice, which was derived from the accumulation of CD73+CD103+Tregs in scWAT. In addition, our results demonstrated that CD73+CD103+ Tregs displayed stronger immunosuppresses to M1 macrophage polarization, that might be due to higher IL-10 expression compared with CD73+CD103- Tregs. Taken together, we consider the more accumulation of CD73+CD103+ Tregs in scWAT mainly accounts for the improved metabolic health after Treg GPSM1 deletion.

We reported that cAMP-PKA-RHOA-cell stiffness-TAZ axis mediated the effects of GPSM1 on regulating numbers of Tregs through in vitro experiments. We observed significant changes in RHOA related pathway after GPSM1 deletion through proteomic sequencing. RHOA functions as a molecular switch involved in organization of cell stiffness, immune cell differentiation and function31. One recent research published in Nature concluded that RHOA-YAP1/TAZ signaling linked mechanical stimuli to endothelial inflammation32. Notably, TAZ, which serves as a key sensor for mechanical stimuli, has been revealed as an important negative regulator of FOXP3 transcription and Treg development25. Thus, we proposed that RHOA-cell stiffness-YAP/TAZ axis might be responsible for the role of GPSM1 on FOXP3 transcription. We reported that GPSM1 deletion specially downregulated RHOA activity and cell stiffness, thereby suppressed the expression and nuclear translocation of YAP1 and TAZ. Nevertheless, only TAZ overexpression, but not YAP1 overexpression, could partially reverse the increase in the number of Tregs after GPSM1 deletion, indicating YAP1 and TAZ exhibit different roles in Treg differentiation. Finally, considering GPSM1 serves as a co-activator of G-protein, the principal downstream of which is mediated by cAMP-PKA signaling, we then detected the cAMP levels and PKA expression in both GPSM1-deficient and GPSM1-overxpressing Tregs, GPSM1 loss activated the cAMP-PKA signaling as shown by increased cAMP levels and p-PKA expression, while GPSM1 overexpression displayed opposite results. Moreover, H89 treatment, a special PKA inhibitor, increased the RHOA activity and thereby partially reversed the inhibitory effects of GPSM1 overexpression on Treg abundance. Collectively, our findings suggest that the regulatory effect of GPSM1 on Tregs is at least partially mediated through the cAMP-PKA-RHOA-cell stiffness-TAZ axis.

To date, early-phase clinical trials of Treg cell therapies have shown feasibility, tolerability and potential efficacy in graft-versus-host disease, transplant rejection and autoimmune diseases33,34. However, there are limited studies on the anti-obesity effects of adipose Treg, owing to their high heterogeneity. Here, based on our findings, we preliminary explored a Treg-targeted therapy in metabolic dysfunctions through adoptive transfer with GPSM1-loss Tregs into Rag1−/− mice. We reported that Rag1−/− mice transferred with GPSM1-deficient Tregs displayed slower body weight gain and reduced fat mass, ameliorated insulin sensitivity and glucose tolerance, and alleviated hepatic steatosis than control mice. Our data demonstrates precise Treg-targeted therapy shows a promising potential against obesity and related metabolic diseases.

One limitation of the study is that although we proposed CD73+CD103+ Treg residing in scWAT was responsible for GPSM1 deficiency-mediated improvement of adipose thermogenesis and metabolic health, it remains unknown what signals, for example, hormones or adipokines that trigger the accumulation of CD73+CD103+ Treg subset specifically in scWAT during obesity. Besides, more in vivo evidence is warranted to support the notion that RHOA-cell stiffness-TAZ axis mediated the effects of GPSM1 on the numbers of Tregs. Finally, while the correlation between GPSM1 expression in VAT derived CD4+ T cells and BMI appeared stronger in the bariatric surgery cohort, the corresponding correlation in the OGTT cohort was based on peripheral blood CD4+ T cells. Therefore, the observed difference may be due to distinct sample sources and patient demographics, and further research is needed to clarify whether severe obesity itself intensifies this association.

In summary, we reveal GPSM1 as a regulator of CD73+CD103+ Treg homeostasis and metabolic health through RHOA-cell stiffness-TAZ axis. Targeting GPSM1 in Tregs holds promise for combating obesity and related disorders.

Methods

Human blood and adipose tissue samples

All participants provided written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400090342). Height and weight were measured using a calibrated scale, and body mass index (BMI, kg/m²) was calculated as the mean of two independent measurements. Participants were categorized as normal weight (BMI < 24) or overweight/obese (BMI ≥ 24) based on the criteria established by the Working Group on Obesity in China. Blood samples were obtained as residual material following glycated hemoglobin testing. Anthropometric and biochemical characteristics of the donors are summarized in Supplementary Table 1 (Table S1). Visceral fat samples were collected from donors undergoing routine elective surgeries. Anthropometric and biochemical characteristics of the donors are summarized in Supplementary Table 2 (Table S2). T cells were isolated by flow cytometric sorting from both blood and visceral fat. To examine GPSM1 expressions in CD4+ cells, immunofluorescence, real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) and Western blotting were performed.

Mice, diets and treatments

All mouse lines were maintained on a C57BL/6 J background. C57BL/6 J and Rag1−/− mice were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. GPSM1f/f mice were generated by GemPharmatech Co., Ltd as previous described16, in which exons 2–11 of the GPSM1 allele are flanked by LoxP sites. CD4-Cre and FOXP3-YFP-Cre mice were purchased from the Cyagen Co., Ltd. CD4⁺ T cell-specific GPSM1 knockout mice (GPSM1ΔT) and Treg cell-specific GPSM1 knockout mice (GPSM1ΔTreg) were generated by intercrossing GPSM1 f/f mice with heterozygous CD4-Cre or FOXP3-YFP-Cre mice. GPSM1LSL mice were generated by Cyagen Co., Ltd., in which a LoxP-Stop-LoxP (LSL) cassette inserted upstream of the GPSM1 coding sequence. To generate CD4⁺ T cell-specific GPSM1 knock-in mice (GPSM1TG), transgenic founder mice carrying the GPSM1LSL allele were crossed with heterozygous CD4-Cre mice. All the mice were genotyped by PCR using genomic DNA extracted from tail biopsies. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 3 (Table S3).

The efficiency of GPSM1 deletion or overexpression was assessed in purified CD4+ T cells and Tregs. Splenic CD4+ T cells were isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) from GPSM1ΔT, GPSM1TG and their respective control mice. Similarly, Tregs were purified from GPSM1ΔTreg and FOXP3-YFP-Cre mice. Subsequently, the knockout or overexpression efficiency was verified by western blot.

Animals were housed in groups of 3-5 per cage under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions, maintained at 22–24 °C with 50–60% relative humidity and a 12-h light-dark cycle, with unrestricted access to food and water. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with institutional guidelines and approved by the Animal Care Committee of Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital, affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (Animal Welfare Ethics Approval No. 2025-YS-280).

Mice were fed a normal chow diet (NCD, Cat. No. P1200F, Shanghai Puluteng Co., Ltd.) or a high-fat diet (HFD, 60% kcal from fat; Cat. No. D12492, Research Diets). Seven-week-old mice were fed an NCD for 20-24 weeks or an HFD for 5-12 weeks for diet-induced obesity, and monitored on a daily basis and weighted every week. For CD25 blocking antibody injection, GPSM1f/f and GPSM1ΔT already HFD-feeding for 6 weeks were injected intraperitoneally with CD25 blocking antibody (clone PC-61.5.3, BioXcell) or lgG isotype at 500 µg per mice twice a week for 6 weeks. DIO mice were already HFD-fed for 6 weeks and then treated with small-molecule compound (AN-465/42243987, purity: over 90%, Specs) as previously described16. At the end point, mice were euthanized via CO2 asphyxiation, and tissues were collected for subsequent analysis.

Metabolic phenotyping

The whole-body fat and lean mass of mice were measured using MRI (MAG-MED AccuFat-1050). Blood samples were collected from tail veins. Plasma glucose was measured using a handheld glucometer (Accu-Chek glucose reader; Roche). For the glucose and insulin tolerance tests, mice were fasted for 6 h and intraperitoneally injected with glucose (1.0–1.5 g per kg body weight) or insulin (1.0-1.5 U per kg body weight). Blood glucose was measured before injection (0 min) or at 15, 30, 60, 90, or 120 min after injection. Area over the curve (AOC) was calculated by subtracting the baseline glucose value from all subsequent time points and integrating the net changes. Consequently, AOC interpretation is assay-dependent: a larger AOC indicates improved insulin sensitivity in the ITT, but a smaller AOC indicates improved glucose tolerance in the GTT.

Plasma alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels were analyzed on an ADVIA 2400 Chemistry System (Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics Inc.) using the corresponding reagent kits (Cat. No. 03036926 for ALT, and Cat. No. 03039631 for AST). Serum insulin levels were determined with insulin ultra-sensitive kit (Cat. No. 90080, CrystalChem), as well as serum leptin, adiponectin and TGFβ1 levels quantified using corresponding ELISA kit (Cat. No. MOB00B, Cat. No. MRP300, Cat. No. DB100C, R&D Systems). Serum concentrations of pro-inflammatory and anti- inflammatory cytokine and chemokines, including IL-2, IL-4, IL-10, IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-3, IL-5, IL-6, IL-9, IL-12(p40), IL-12(p70), IL-13, IL-17, IFNγ, TNFα, CCL2, CCL3, CCL4, CCL11, CXCL1, G-CSF, GM-CSF, RANTES, were quantified using the Mouse Bio-plex Pro Mouse Cytokine Grp 1 Panel 23- Plex (Cat. No. M60009RDPD/64422748) through Bio-PlexTM 200 System (Bio-Rad). To assess the energy expenditure under adrenergic signaling-induced browning condition, mice were administered intraperitoneal injections of CL-316,243 (1 mg/kg body weight per day, sigma-Aldrich) for 7 consecutive days. Oxygen consumption (VO₂), carbon dioxide production (VCO₂), and food intake were measured in a Comprehensive Laboratory Animal Monitoring System (CLAMS, Columbus Instruments) under controlled conditions (20–23 °C, 12-h light–dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. Airflow was maintained at 380 ml/min, and O₂ and CO₂ concentrations in room air were recorded every 15 minutes. The data of VO₂ and energy expenditure were analyzed as previous described35.

Measurement of mitochondrial respiration in vivo and in vitro

For mitochondrial respiration rate measurement in scWAT, ~10–20 mg of minced scWAT were homogenized on ice in 400 μL of MiR05 buffer [containing 0.5 mM EGTA, 3 mM MgCl₂·6H₂O, 60mM K-lactobionate, 20 mM Taurine, 10 mM KH2PO4, 20 mM HEPES, 110 mM Sucrose, 1 g/L fatty acid-free BSA] using a glass dounce homogenizer. Oxygen consumption of scWAT were measured at 37 °C in MiR05 buffer within an oxygen concentration range 220-150 nmol O2/mL using an Oxygraph-2K (Oroboros Inc., Innsbruck, Austria). Basal respiration was measured in the presence of pyruvate (5 mM), succinate (10 mM), glutamic (10 mM), malate (2 mM) and ADP ((2.5 mM). Uncoupled respiration was assessed by sequential additions of oligomycin (5 nM), FCCP (0.5 μM), and a mixture of rotenone and antimycin A (0.5 μM each). All respiration rates were recorded and normalized to tissue weight using DatLab 5 software (Oroboros Inc., Innsbruck, Austria).

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) of primary beige adipocytes was measured using a Seahorse XFe Analyzer (Agilent). Cells were differentiated in a Seahorse XF 96-well cell culture plate, seeded at a density of 50000 cells per well. On day 6 of differentiation, differentiated primary adipocytes were treated with a 1:1 mixture of DMEM and CM collected from either CD73+ CD103+ Tregs versus CD73+CD103- Tregs for 24 h. OCR was measured in DMEM assay medium (Cat. No. 103575-100, Agilent Technologies) supplemented with 2% fatty acid free BSA (Cat. No. BAH66, Equitech-bio), 10 mM glucose (Cat. No. 103577-100, Agilent Technologies), 1 mM pyruvate (Cat. No. 103578-100, Agilent Technologies) and 2 mM Glutamine (Cat. No. 103579-100, Agilent Technologies). Cells were then treated with 3 μM oligomycin to inhibit coupled respiration, followed by 0.5 μM ISO to stimulate respiration. Maximal respiration was achieved by treatment with 4 μM FCCP, followed by treatment with 1.5 μM rotenone and antimycin A to block mitochondrial respiration. Measurements were normalized to total protein levels. Wave v.2.6 software was used for data collection and average oxygen consumption rate were calculated.

Histological analysis

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining for general histological examination or processed for immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis. For IHC, tissue sections were first deparaffinized, and antigen retrieval was performed to unmask the epitopes. The sections were then incubated with primary antibodies targeting UCP1 (Cat. No. ab10983, abcam, 1:500), followed by incubation with secondary antibodies conjugated with horseradish peroxidase. Paraffin sections of human VAT were obtained from our previous study16. To examine GPSM1 expression in CD4+T cells of human VAT, paraffin sections were stained with anti-GPSM1 antibody (Cat. No. 11483-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:50) and anti-CD4 antibody (Cat. No. ab183685, abcam,1:200).

For frozen section preparation, tissues were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound. These frozen sections were used for Oil Red O staining. Immunofluorescence analysis was carried out using primary antibodies against YAP1 (Cat. No. ab205270, abcam, 1:200), TAZ (Cat. No. 83669, CST, 1:200), and DAPI (Cat. No. C1002, Beyotime Biotechnology, 1:5000). The corresponding secondary antibodies employed were Alexa Fluor 647 goat anti-rabbit IgG (Cat. No. A0468, 1:1000) and Alexa Fluor 555 donkey anti-rabbit IgG (Cat. No. A0453, 1:1000), all purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology. The stained sections were examined using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, DM4B) and images were acquired and analyzed using HALO version 3.6.4134.166 (Indica Labs).

Stromal vascular fraction and lymphocyte isolation from spleen and adipose tissues

To isolate lymphocytes from the spleen, the harvested spleen was washed with PBS, homogenized in 2 mL PBS, and filtered through a 40 μm nylon mesh (Falcon). The cell suspension was centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 minutes at 4 °C, and the pellet was resuspended in red blood cell lysis buffer (Cat. No. 555899, BD Pharmingen) to remove erythrocytes. After washing with PBS by centrifugation at 500 × g for 5 minutes at 4 °C, cells were resuspended in staining buffer and processed for flow cytometry staining.

To isolate lymphocytes and macrophages from adipose tissue, eWAT and scWAT were collected and washed with PBS. After removing lymph nodes from scWAT, the tissues were finely minced and digested in a 0.2% Type II collagenase (Cat. No. C6885, Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS containing 1.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at 37 °C with shaking at 150 rpm for 10 minutes. The digested tissue was filtered through a 100 μm nylon mesh (Falcon) and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 minutes at 4 °C. The cell pellet was treated with red blood cell lysis buffer, washed with PBS, resuspended in staining buffer, and stained for flow cytometry.

Flow cytometry analysis and cell sorting

For cell-surface marker analysis, stromal vascular fractions (SVFs) isolated from scWAT and eWAT, splenocytes, or primary T cells were washed twice with staining buffer and incubated with anti-CD16/CD32 antibody (Cat. No. 156604, BioLegend) for 15 minutes at 4 °C in the dark to block Fc receptors. Cells were then stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against surface markers for 30 minutes at 4 °C in the dark, followed by three washes with staining buffer. Immediately prior to acquisition, DAPI (1:20,000 dilution) was added to exclude dead cells, and samples were analyzed by using the ImageStream Mark II (Luminex). Data were processed with IDEAS software (Version 6.2).

For intracellular cytokine analysis, cells were restimulated with cell activation cocktail containing Brefeldin A (Cat. No. 423304, BioLegend) for 4–6 h. After restimulation, cells were stained with Fixable Viability Stain 510 (FVS510) or FVS780 for 15 minutes at room temperature in the dark to exclude dead cells, followed by three washes with staining buffer. Cells were subsequently stained with surface marker antibodies for 30 minutes at 4 °C in the dark, then fixed and permeabilized using a FOXP3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Cat. No. 00-5521-00, eBioscience) for 1 h at 4 °C in the dark. Intracellular cytokines and transcription factors were then stained according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After three washes to remove unbound antibodies, samples were analyzed using the ImageStream Mark II (Luminex). Data were processed with IDEAS software (Version 6.2).

For fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), samples were prepared as described above for surface marker staining and sorted using the CytoFLEX SRT (Beckman Coulter).

All antibodies used for flow cytometry along with their respective clone, fluorochrome, and dilution are listed in Supplementary Table 4 (Table S4).

Virus-mediated RNAi knockdown or overexpression

Primary naïve T cells were subjected to infection using lentivirus-shRNA, and adenovirus-based overexpression vectors at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) ranging from 100 to 200. The lentiviral constructs (Lv-shGPSM1), and adenoviral overexpression vector (Adv-GPSM1, Adv-YAP1, Adv-TAZ) were synthesized by Shanghai GeneChem. After being activated with anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 antibody for two days, primary naïve T cells were incubated with the respective lentivirus or adenovirus for 48 h, respectively, prior to subsequent experimental procedures.

cAMP level quantification and G-ELISA assay

For detecting intracellular concentration of cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), about 1×106 primary T cells per test were collected, counted and then quantified using an ELISA kit (Cat. No. K019-H1, ARBOR ASSAYS) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For detecting activity levels of RHOA, about 2×106 primary T cells per test were collected, counted and then assessed by RHOA G-LISA activation assay (Cat. No. BK124, Cytoskeleton) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol.

Quantitative RT–PCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissue samples using the QIAzol reagent (RNeasy Plus Universal Mini Kit, Cat. No. 1023537, QIAGEN) and from cultured cells using the TRIzol reagent (Cat. No. 15596018, Invitrogen), following the respective manufacturer’s protocols. The reverse transcription was performed using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Cat. No. RR047A, TaKaRa). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was conducted with SYBR Green Master Mix (Cat. No. 01000432, Applied Biosystems) on a QuantStudio Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems), adhering to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Relative mRNA expression levels were determined using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The primer sequences used are provided in Supplementary Table 5 (Table S5).

Immunoblot analysis

Cells or tissues were lysed on ice using RIPA buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology) supplemented with phosphatase and protease inhibitor cocktail (Cat. No. 04693159001, Cat. No. 04906837001, Roche), followed by centrifugation at 12000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit and then mixed with 5× loading buffer (Cat. No. P0286, Beyotime Biotechnology) as the input sample. Equal amounts of total protein (~30 μg per lane) were resolved by SDS-PAGE, transferred onto nitrocellulose (NC) membranes (Millipore), subsequently blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h, and then incubated with the specified primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. followed by three washes in TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20). Next day, after three washes in TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20), membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Signal detection was performed using the Immunobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (Cat. No. WBKLS0500, Millipore), and chemiluminescence was captured with the ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad). Western blot images were acquired using Image Lab software (version 6.0) and analyzed semi-quantitatively by densitometry using ImageJ software (version 1.52, National Institutes of Health). All antibodies for Western blot used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 6 (Table S6).

Adoptive transfer

For diet-induced obesity study, Rag1−/− mice under already HFD-feeding for 8 weeks were adoptively transferred with 0.5×106 primary differentiated GPSM1f/f Tregs or GPSM1ΔT Tregs, respectively, through medial canthus intravenous injection and then continued to monitor metabolic phenotypes under HFD feeding for 4 weeks. For thermogenic phenotype study, 7-week-old Rag1−/− mice were adoptively transferred with 0.5×106 primary differentiated GPSM1f/f Tregs or GPSM1ΔT Tregs. After NCD feeding for 4 weeks, mice were administered intraperitoneal injections of CL-316,243 (1 mg/kg body weight per day) for 7 consecutive days. Mice were placed into metabolic cages to assess the energy expenditure.

Single-nucleus RNA-seq data analysis and bioinformatics analysis

Single nuclei were isolated from scWAT of GPSM1f/f (n = 3) and GPSM1ΔTreg (n = 3) mice using a sucrose gradient-based nuclei isolation protocol (Novogene, Beijing, China). Nuclei were stained with DAPI and sorted by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) to ensure high purity. Single-nucleus RNA-seq libraries were generated using the 10x Genomics Chromium Single Cell 3’ Library, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The sequencing was performed on the illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform, generating paired-end reads. Raw sequencing data were processed using the Cell Ranger (version 7.1) to align reads to the reference genome (GRCh38) and generate a gene expression matrix. Quality control, filtering of low-quality nuclei, and doublet detection were performed using Seurat (version 4.1.0) and DoubletFinder. Nuclei, with fewer than 200 detected genes, more than 5% mitochondrial RNA content, or anomalously high UMI counts were excluded. Normalization, scaling, and dimensionality reduction were carried out using Seurat’s standard workflow, with principal component analysis (PCA) and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) used for visualization. Clustering was performed using a shared nearest neighbor (SNN) graph-based algorithm, and cell types were annotated based on canonical marker genes. Differential gene expression analysis between clusters was conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test implemented in Seurat. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using clusterProfiler (version 4.0) to identify pathways enriched in specific cell populations. Data visualization was completed using ggplot2 (version 3.3.5) and ComplexHeatmap (version 2.10.0). The top 30 makers of CD4+cells, Tregs, macrophage and adipocyte are listed in Supplementary Data 1-4, respectively, while DEGs of each Treg subset are listed in Supplementary Data 5.

Proteomic sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Proteomic sequencing was performed from primary GPSM1f/f (n = 5) and GPSM1ΔT (n = 5) Tregs in Mass Spectrometry System at the National Facility for Protein Science in Shanghai (NFPS). Proteins were reduced, alkylated, and acetone-precipitated, followed by overnight tryptic digestion (1:40, w/w) in HEPES buffer. Peptides were quantified and labeled with TMT10plex reagents, pooled at equal ratios, desalted, and fractionated by high-pH reverse-phase chromatography into multiple fractions. Each fraction was analyzed by nanoLC–MS/MS using an Easy-nLC 1200 system coupled to an Orbitrap Eclipse mass spectrometer. Peptide separation was achieved on a C18 analytical column with a linear acetonitrile gradient, and data were acquired in data-dependent acquisition mode with HCD fragmentation. MS/MS spectra were searched against the UniProt Mus musculus database using Proteome Discoverer (v2.4) with the Sequest HT algorithm. Carbamidomethylation (Cys) and TMT labeling (N-terminus and Lys) were set as fixed modifications, and methionine oxidation as a variable modification. A 1% false discovery rate was applied at the peptide level and relative quantification was based on TMT reporter ion intensities. Bioinformatics analysis was conducted to identify differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) that adjusted P value < 0.05. Functional annotation and reactome pathway enrichment analyses were performed using metascape36. Protein-protein interaction networks were constructed using STRING (version 11.5) and visualized with Cytoscape (version 3.9.0). Data visualization was completed using ggplot2 (version 3.3.5), pheatmap (version 1.0.12) and BioLadder (bioladder.cn)37. All DEPs are listed in Supplementary Data 6.

Cell-adhesion assay

Human amniotic epithelial cells (HAECs) were cultured to confluence in 24-well plates. CD73+CD103- Tregs and CD73+CD103+ Tregs were stained with calcein AM (Cat. No. 564061, BD Pharmingen) for 30 min and then incubated with HAECs at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After washing three times with warm PBS to remove non-adherent cells, the number of adherent Tregs, was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader (excitation/emission: 485/520 nm).

Atomic Force Microscopy and analysis

Indentation measurements were performed with a Biological fast atomic force microscope (AFM, FastScan Bio, Bruker Nano Surfaces) in the instrumental analysis center of Shanghai Jiao Tong University. For single cell measurements, primary Tregs were adjusted to about 1×104 cell/mL and cultured on poly-L-lysine-coated glass coverslips (12–15 mm diameter). Ball probe was used to detect the force-distance curves of primary Tregs, with manufacturers measured spring constants about 0.1 N/m. For adipose tissue measurements, frozen section of scWAT were prepared and detected the basic mechanical morphology through ball probe, with nominal spring constant about 0.01 N/m. Data was analyzed using NanoScope Analysis software (v180r1). Force-distance curves were analyzed using the Hertz model to calculate the apparent young’s modulus, with corrections for tip geometry and indentation depth.

Statistics and reproducibility

All data are presented as means ± standard deviations (SD) or standard errors (SE), medians with interquartile ranges, proportions (n%), and odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Quantitative variables exhibiting skewed distributions were logarithmically transformed to approximate normality. Sample size was estimated based on preliminary experiments. All experiments were performed in triplicate or more. The experiments were not randomized. The Investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. Statistical analyses were conducted using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s T-test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, two-way ANOVA with Sidak’s multiple comparisons test, hypergeometric test or Wilcoxon rank sum test with Bonferroni’s comparisons test. Statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism (version 8.3.0; GraphPad Software, Inc.) or SAS software (version 8.0; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).
