Postnatal Day 7 Ethanol Exposure is Associated with Apoptosis-Related Changes in GAD67-Positive Neurons and Candidate CaMKII/GSK-3β/BAX Signaling in the Mouse Spinal Dorsal Horn
Authors: Yuting Wang, Zhaoyang Cheng, Zizhuo Wang, Xihui Ding, Xiaoxiang Xu, Shuaichen Sun, Yingying Zheng, Min Liu, Jianguang Xu, Xiang Nan, Jinyong Xu, Xiaohui Li, Zhenhua Ren, Kai Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrenatal alcohol exposure can cause neurodevelopmental injury, but the cellular events linking altered inhibitory signalling to apoptotic changes in the developing spinal dorsal horn remain incompletely understood.MethodsC57BL/6J mice received two subcutaneous ethanol injections (2.5 g/kg each, 2 h apart) on postnatal day 7 (PD7). Dorsal horn injury and GABAergic changes were assessed by histology, immunostaining, and whole-cell patch-clamp recording. Intracellular Ca2+ signalling, CaMKII and GSK-3β regulation, mitochondrial apoptosis-related markers, oxidative stress, and cell viability were examined using calcium imaging, flow cytometry, Western blotting, and co-immunoprecipitation. Tetraethylammonium chloride (TEAC) and Bay K8644 were used to explore the contribution of membrane excitability and voltage-gated Ca2+ entry.ResultsEthanol exposure was associated with altered dorsal horn neuronal morphology, increased cleaved caspase-3, and prominent involvement of GAD67-positive neurons. It was also associated with increased GABA staining and mIPSC frequency, without a clear change in mIPSC amplitude; reduced intracellular Ca2+ signals; lower CaMKII Thr286 phosphorylation and CaMKII-GSK-3β association; decreased inhibitory GSK-3β Ser9 phosphorylation; and mitochondrial apoptosis-related changes, increased ROS, reduced cell viability, and caspase-3 activation. TEAC attenuated several ethanol-associated molecular and cellular injury markers, whereas Bay K8644 moderated selected Ca2+- and mitochondrial/oxidative stress-related outcomes.ConclusionAcute PD7 ethanol exposure was associated with neuronal injury in the developing mouse spinal dorsal horn and with coordinated changes in GABAergic signalling, Ca2+-CaMKII regulation, GSK-3β activity, and apoptosis-related markers. These findings support a candidate membrane-potential/Ca2+-sensitive GABAergic and Ca2+-CaMKII-GSK-3β/BAX signalling framework, but do not establish a fixed causal sequence or behavioural consequence.

Introduction

Alcohol exposure during development is a major, preventable cause of neurodevelopmental injury. Fetal alcohol spectrum disorders (FASD) encompass a range of outcomes following prenatal alcohol exposure, including cognitive, behavioural, sensory, and motor impairments.1–4 Recent clinical studies link prenatal alcohol exposure to psychosocial and neurocognitive difficulties and to structural brain differences, whereas controlled mouse studies show that drinking pattern can shape molecular and behavioural outcomes.5–7 The phenotype and severity of FASD vary with the dose, pattern, timing, and duration of exposure, together with maternal and fetal factors.8–10 In rodents, early postnatal ethanol exposure is commonly used to model a late-gestational-equivalent period of rapid synaptic development. This approach produces acute neuroapoptosis and can be followed by lasting structural or behavioural changes.11–13 It should nevertheless be distinguished from maternal prenatal exposure and from behavioural FASD phenotypes unless those outcomes are directly tested.

The spinal dorsal horn is relevant to developmental ethanol neurotoxicity because it receives and processes somatosensory input and contains local inhibitory circuits that can influence spinal sensory processing.14,15 Previous studies have shown that ethanol exposure during early development can induce apoptosis in spinal cord neurons and neuronal loss in the dorsal horn in mice.16,17 The spinal gray matter is organized into Rexed laminae I–X, and the dorsal horn generally comprises laminae I–VI. Within superficial dorsal horn laminae, including layers II–III, morphologically diverse inhibitory and excitatory interneurons are present, and GABAergic inhibitory neurons marked by GABA and the GABA-synthetic enzyme GAD67 contribute to local synaptic processing.18–20 Acute ethanol has also been reported to enhance inhibitory synaptic transmission in adult rat substantia gelatinosa neurons.21 The functional consequence of GABAA receptor signalling can depend on receptor activation level and local synaptic context, which cautions against inferring circuit output from GABA staining alone.22 In a distinct prenatal cortical model, ethanol-associated changes in GABAergic interneuron development were linked to L-type calcium-channel activity, providing broader developmental context for examining GABA-calcium interactions.23

We recently reported that acute ethanol exposure induces Akt/GSK-3β/BAX-associated apoptosis in the developing spinal dorsal horn, with peak vulnerability around postnatal day 4.24 GSK-3β is a stress-responsive signalling node that has been implicated in ethanol neurotoxicity.25 CaMKII integrates intracellular Ca2+ signals, and autophosphorylation can prolong kinase activity beyond the initial calcium transient.26,27 In cerebellar neurons, CaMKII can physically associate with GSK-3 and promote inhibitory Ser9 phosphorylation, linking depolarization-dependent calcium signalling to neuronal survival.28 In developing neural models, ethanol-associated loss of GSK-3β Ser9 phosphorylation has been accompanied by BAX and caspase-3 activation, and ethanol-associated GSK-3β activity has been linked to oxidative and inflammatory injury responses.29–31 However, the upstream cellular events linking developmental ethanol exposure to altered GSK-3β/BAX-associated apoptotic signalling remain incompletely defined. In the present study, we used in vivo and in vitro approaches to examine whether acute PD7 ethanol exposure is associated with changes in GABAergic activity, calcium signalling, CaMKII phosphorylation, and GSK-3β regulation in the developing mouse spinal dorsal horn.

Materials and Methods

Ethanol Exposure Mouse Model

Postnatal day 7 (PD7) C57BL/6J mice were used to model acute early-postnatal ethanol exposure. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (approval no. LLSC20200943) and were performed in accordance with the approved institutional animal ethics protocol and the national standard Guidelines for Ethical Review of Laboratory Animal Welfare (GB/T 35892–2018).

On PD7, pups were screened to ensure an approximate body weight of 5 g and then allocated to control or ethanol-treated groups. Ethanol-treated pups received two subcutaneous ethanol injections, 2 h apart, each containing 2.5 g/kg ethanol in 20% saline. Control littermates received vehicle under the same handling schedule. Eligible littermates were allocated across treatment groups within litters where possible, and body weight and health status were checked before treatment. For in vivo endpoints, mice were euthanized by decapitation 8 h after the first injection, and spinal cords were rapidly dissected on ice for subsequent analyses.

Experimental Design, Blinding, and Exclusion Criteria

The investigator administering ethanol or vehicle treatment was aware of group allocation during dosing. After tissue collection, samples and data files were assigned coded identifiers. Image acquisition and quantification, electrophysiological event analysis, flow-cytometric analysis, western-blot densitometry, and statistical analysis were performed with group identity masked where feasible.

Technical exclusion criteria were defined before analysis. Animals or samples were excluded for age or body weight outside the intended range, failed injection or visible leakage, overt pre-treatment health abnormality, incorrect tissue sampling, or severe mechanical damage. Electrophysiological recordings were excluded if stable whole-cell configuration could not be maintained, if series resistance changed by more than 20% during recording, if baseline holding current was unstable, or if the usable recording duration was less than 5 min. Tissue sections with severe folding, tearing, incorrect anatomical level, or insufficient staining quality were excluded before quantitative analysis.

Sample sizes were selected based on previous studies using related neonatal ethanol-exposure models and on preliminary experimental reproducibility. Biological replicate definitions are provided in the Statistical analysis section and in the corresponding figure legends.

Primary Cell Culture

Primary spinal dorsal horn neurons were prepared from PD4 C57BL/6J mice as previously described, with minor modifications. Briefly, mice were surface-disinfected and decapitated. The spinal cord was rapidly isolated and transferred into L15 medium containing 1 mM HEPES, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 U/mL streptomycin. Dorsal horn gray matter was dissected under a stereomicroscope, minced, and digested in L15 medium containing 2 mg/mL papain and 25 U/mL DNase I at 37°C for 15 min.

After enzymatic digestion, the tissue was treated with 0.1% DNase for 5 min and gently triturated. The cell suspension was mixed with 4% bovine serum albumin and centrifuged. The cell pellet was resuspended in Neurobasal A medium containing 2% B27 supplement, 1 mM GlutaMAX, and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were seeded on poly-D-lysine-coated dishes and maintained at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator for 5 days before experiments.

Spinal Cord Slice Preparations

Spinal cord slices were prepared from PD7 C57BL/6J mice. Mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and decapitated. Lumbosacral spinal cord segments containing L4-L5 were rapidly removed and embedded in 3% agarose. Transverse spinal cord slices of 300 µm thickness were prepared in ice-cold cutting solution using a VT1200S vibratome.

The cutting solution was selected according to the downstream experiment. For dynamic calcium imaging, Hanks’ balanced salt solution was used. For whole-cell patch-clamp recordings, choline chloride artificial cerebrospinal fluid was used. The choline chloride solution contained the following, in mM: 110.0 choline chloride, 2.5 KCl, 0.5 CaCl2, 7.0 MgCl2, 1.3 NaH2PO4, 25.0 NaHCO3, 1.3 Na-ascorbate, 0.6 Na-pyruvate, and 20.0 glucose. Cutting solutions were bubbled with 95% O2 and 5% CO2 and maintained at 1–3°C during sectioning.

Golgi-Cox Staining

Spinal cords from control and ethanol-exposed mice were dissected on ice and rinsed with HBSS to remove blood. Tissues were fixed for at least 48 h and then immersed in Golgi-Cox staining solution in the dark. The staining solution contained 5% potassium dichromate, 5% mercuric chloride, 5% potassium chromate, and ultrapure water at a ratio of 1:1:0.8:2.

After the initial incubation, the staining solution was refreshed and staining continued for 14 days, with solution replacement every 3 days. Tissues were washed with ultrapure water, softened in 80% glacial acetic acid overnight, and cryoprotected in 30% sucrose. Sections of 100 µm thickness were prepared with a vibrating microtome, mounted on gelatin-coated slides, air-dried in the dark, developed with concentrated ammonia for 15 min, fixed in acidic hardening solution for 15 min, washed, air-dried, and sealed with glycerol gelatin. Neuronal morphology was quantified using Sholl analysis in ImageJ.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was performed using a previously established protocol with modifications. Animals were euthanized by decapitation, and spinal cord tissues were rapidly excised on ice. Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, cryoprotected in 10–30% sucrose, embedded in OCT compound, and sectioned at 10 µm thickness using a CryoStar NX70 cryostat.

Sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against cleaved caspase-3, GAD67, GABA, or glutamate. For cleaved caspase-3 immunohistochemistry, a rabbit anti-cleaved caspase-3 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, USA) was used. After washing, sections were incubated with a biotin-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature and developed with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (ZSGB-Bio, China). Images were acquired using a BX51 microscope (Olympus, Japan). Negative controls were prepared by omitting the primary antibody.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed on primary spinal dorsal horn neurons or spinal cord sections according to established protocols. Rabbit anti-cleaved caspase-3 antibody was obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (USA), mouse anti-GAD67 antibody was obtained from Abcam (USA), and rabbit anti-BAX antibody was obtained from Proteintech (China). Samples were incubated with primary antibodies against cleaved caspase-3, AIF, GAD67, glutamate, PKCγ, or BAX. After washing, samples were incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark with species-appropriate Alexa Fluor 488- or Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, USA).

Samples were counterstained with DAPI and mounted before imaging. Fluorescence images were acquired using an IX81 fluorescence microscope or SpinSR laser confocal microscope. Negative controls were prepared by omitting the primary antibodies.

Whole-Cell Patch-Clamp Recordings

Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed on putative GABAergic islet-like neurons in the superficial dorsal horn. These neurons were selected based on morphology and firing patterns associated with this neuronal population. Recording pipettes had resistances of 4–8 MΩ.

Recordings were performed at 30°C in oxygenated artificial cerebrospinal fluid containing the following, in mM: 10.0 glucose, 125 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 2 CaCl2, 1.3 MgCl2, 1.3 NaH2PO4, 25 NaHCO3, 1.3 Na-ascorbate, and 0.6 Na-pyruvate. The solution was bubbled with 95% O2 and 5% CO2. Miniature inhibitory postsynaptic currents (mIPSCs) were recorded at −70 mV using an internal pipette solution containing, in mM: 140 CsCl, 10 HEPES, 4 MgATP, 3 NaGTP, 1 EGTA, 1 MgCl2, and 0.3 CaCl2.

mIPSCs were isolated using tetrodotoxin (0.5 µM) to block action potentials. Strychnine (5 µM), NBQX (10 µM), and APV (50 µM) were used to inhibit glycinergic and excitatory synaptic currents. Signals were amplified with a Multiclamp 700B amplifier, filtered at 2 kHz, digitized with a Digidata 1322 converter, and sampled at 10 kHz using pCLAMP 10.7 software. Synaptic events were analyzed with Clampfit 10.7. Each recording lasted more than 5 min. When more than one cell was recorded from the same animal, cellular measurements were averaged before animal-level comparison.

Dynamic Calcium Imaging

Primary spinal dorsal horn neurons or acute spinal cord slices were loaded with the calcium indicator Fluo-4 AM to monitor intracellular calcium dynamics. For ethanol exposure experiments, samples were exposed to 400 mM ethanol under the indicated in vitro conditions. For pharmacological assays, tetraethylammonium chloride (TEAC; 10 mM), Bay K8644 (1 µM), carbachol (1 mM), or bicuculline (5 µM) was added to the culture medium according to the experimental design. Bicuculline was used as a screening condition in the calcium assay, whereas TEAC and Bay K8644 were used for the main pharmacological comparisons.

Fluo-4 fluorescence was recorded using confocal microscopy or flow cytometry, depending on the experimental endpoint. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using identical acquisition settings within each experiment.

Western Blotting

Dorsal horn tissue and comparison anterior horn tissue were microdissected from L4-L5 spinal cord segments when regional comparisons were required. Proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes.

Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against cleaved caspase-3, CaMKII, phospho-CaMKII (Thr286), GSK-3β, phospho-GSK-3β (Ser9), and β-actin. Cleaved caspase-3 antibody was obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (USA), and β-actin antibody was obtained from Proteintech (China). After incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies, immunoreactive bands were visualized using ECL detection reagents (Tanon, China). Band intensity was quantified using a Tanon 5200 chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon, China).

Co-Immunoprecipitation

Co-immunoprecipitation was performed using spinal cord tissue or cultured-neuron protein extracts. Lysates were incubated with anti-CaMKII, anti-GSK-3β, or IgG control antibody, followed by incubation with Protein A/G magnetic beads. Immune complexes were isolated using a magnetic frame, washed with TBST, eluted by heating in loading buffer, and analyzed by immunoblotting.

For reciprocal co-immunoprecipitation, CaMKII immunoprecipitates were immunoblotted for GSK-3β, and GSK-3β immunoprecipitates were immunoblotted for CaMKII. Where quantified, the target band in the immunoprecipitate was normalized to the corresponding immunoprecipitated protein signal in the same lane.

Flow Cytometry

Flow cytometry was used to assess reactive oxygen species, cell viability, cytosolic calcium levels, and mitochondrial membrane potential according to the experimental endpoint. Cell viability was measured using Calcein AM staining. Cytosolic calcium was measured using Fluo-4 AM. Reactive oxygen species were measured using DCFH-DA. Mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using JC-1 staining.

Cells were detached with 0.05% trypsin and centrifuged at 1500 g for 5 min. Cells were then incubated with the appropriate working solution for 30 min, washed twice with fresh medium, and analyzed using a Beckman flow cytometer. Acquisition settings were kept consistent within each experiment.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. For in vivo and ex vivo experiments, the animal was defined as the biological unit. For primary-culture experiments, each independently prepared culture was defined as one biological replicate. Multiple technical measurements from the same animal or culture preparation, including cells, microscopic fields, or tissue sections, were averaged before group-level statistical analysis.

Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed before parametric analysis. Two-group comparisons were analyzed using unpaired Student’s t-tests; Welch’s correction was used when variances were unequal. Data that did not meet parametric assumptions were analyzed using non-parametric tests. Multi-group comparisons were analyzed by ANOVA followed by post hoc multiple-comparison tests. Comparisons of multiple treatment groups against a single ethanol group were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s test. Time-course calcium-imaging data and Sholl-analysis data were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA or mixed-effects models. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Ethanol Exposure is Associated with Neuronal Injury and Cleaved Caspase-3 Activation in the Dorsal Horn

At PD7, ethanol-exposed mice showed altered neuronal morphology in the spinal dorsal horn, as assessed by Golgi-Cox staining and Sholl analysis (Figure 1A). DAB immunohistochemistry further showed increased cleaved caspase-3-positive staining in layers II–III of the dorsal horn after ethanol exposure (Figure 1B). To assess regional differences under the tested ethanol condition, cleaved caspase-3 expression was compared between anterior horn- and dorsal horn-derived samples (Figure 1C). In parallel, dorsal horn-derived primary neurons showed lower Calcein AM fluorescence than anterior horn-derived neurons under the same treatment condition (Figure 1D). Together, these findings indicate that acute PD7 ethanol exposure is associated with neuronal injury and increased cleaved caspase-3 immunoreactivity in the dorsal horn. The observations in Figure 1C and D represent regional comparisons and do not constitute independent control-versus-ethanol comparisons. Ethanol Exposure was Associated with Injury of GAD67-Positive Neurons and Altered Inhibitory Synaptic Activity

We next examined whether ethanol-associated apoptotic changes were distributed across neuronal subtypes in the superficial dorsal horn. The superficial dorsal horn contains layer II/substantia gelatinosa and adjacent layer III, where GABAergic inhibitory neurons contribute to local spinal sensory processing. Immunofluorescence staining showed spatial overlap between cleaved caspase-3 and the GABAergic neuronal marker GAD67 after ethanol exposure (Figure 2A). Immunohistochemistry also showed reduced GAD67 signal in the dorsal horn (Figure 2B). Under the same experimental conditions, cleaved caspase-3 signal showed less apparent overlap with glutamate-positive neurons or PKCγ-positive neurons (Figure 2C and D). These observations indicate that GAD67-positive neurons were prominently affected among the neuronal markers examined, although quantitative colocalization analysis would be required to establish strict cell-type selectivity. Because islet-like neurons in the superficial dorsal horn represent an important population of GABAergic inhibitory neurons, we further assessed inhibitory synaptic activity using whole-cell patch-clamp recordings. Ethanol exposure was associated with increased GABA staining in the dorsal horn (Figure 3A), whereas glutamate staining showed no obvious change under the same conditions (Figure 3B). Recordings from dorsal horn islet-like neurons showed an increased frequency of miniature inhibitory postsynaptic currents (mIPSCs), with no clear change in mIPSC amplitude (Figure 3C–E). These results show that ethanol exposure was accompanied by both structural involvement of GAD67-positive neurons and altered inhibitory synaptic activity in the developing dorsal horn. Ethanol Exposure Reduced Intracellular Calcium Signalling in Dorsal Horn Neurons

We then measured intracellular calcium signalling in dorsal horn neurons. Calcium imaging showed a sustained decrease in intracellular Ca2+ signal in islet-like neurons after ethanol exposure (Figure 4A and B). Pharmacological modulation further showed that the ethanol-associated reduction in intracellular Ca2+ was attenuated by Bay K8644, a voltage-gated calcium channel activator, and by TEAC, a potassium channel blocker that promotes membrane depolarization (Figure 4C). In contrast, carbachol did not reverse the ethanol-associated decrease in intracellular Ca2+ under the same experimental conditions (Figure 4C). These results indicate that ethanol exposure was associated with reduced intracellular Ca2+ signalling, and that this change was sensitive to pharmacological conditions affecting membrane excitability and voltage-gated Ca2+ entry. Ethanol Exposure Reduced CaMKII Phosphorylation and CaMKII-GSK-3β Association

Because CaMKII activity is regulated by intracellular Ca2+, we next examined CaMKII phosphorylation in dorsal horn neurons. Western blot analysis showed that ethanol exposure reduced CaMKII phosphorylation at Thr286 in the dorsal horn (Figure 4D). TEAC treatment attenuated this ethanol-associated reduction in CaMKII phosphorylation (Figure 4D).

We further examined the association between CaMKII and GSK-3β by co-immunoprecipitation. Ethanol exposure reduced the detectable interaction between CaMKII and GSK-3β, as shown by reciprocal co-immunoprecipitation assays (Figure 4E and F). These findings show that ethanol exposure was associated with reduced CaMKII activation and decreased CaMKII-GSK-3β association in the dorsal horn.

Discussion

This study examined cellular and molecular changes in the developing mouse spinal dorsal horn after acute PD7 ethanol exposure. The main findings were that ethanol exposure was associated with neuronal injury, caspase-3 activation, altered GABAergic signalling, reduced intracellular Ca2+ signalling, decreased CaMKII phosphorylation, reduced CaMKII-GSK-3β association, reduced inhibitory phosphorylation of GSK-3β, and mitochondrial apoptosis-related changes. Together, these observations support a candidate pathway linking altered GABAergic activity and calcium-CaMKII signalling to GSK-3β-associated apoptotic changes in the developing spinal dorsal horn.

These findings extend previous work showing that developmental ethanol exposure can induce apoptosis in the spinal cord and that Akt/GSK-3β/BAX signalling contributes to developmental-stage-dependent vulnerability.24 The present study adds upstream cellular observations, particularly changes in GABAergic signalling, intracellular Ca2+ dynamics, CaMKII phosphorylation, and CaMKII-GSK-3β association. This places GSK-3β within a broader signalling context rather than treating it as an isolated downstream apoptotic marker. Prior work identifies GSK-3β as a convergence point for ethanol-associated cellular stress and neuronal vulnerability.25 Independent developmental ethanol models have linked altered GSK-3β activity to caspase-3 activation, oxidative stress, microglial activation, and neuroinflammatory responses.29–31 These studies support the biological plausibility of the pathway examined here, although the precise hierarchy of events remains to be tested with more selective approaches.

The spinal dorsal horn is relevant to developmental ethanol neurotoxicity because it receives and processes somatosensory input and contains local excitatory and inhibitory circuits that shape spinal sensory processing. In the present study, ethanol-associated apoptotic changes were prominent in GAD67-positive neurons in the superficial dorsal horn. Ethanol exposure was also associated with increased GABA staining and increased mIPSC frequency in islet-like neurons. These findings are consistent with evidence that inhibitory interneurons in the substantia gelatinosa regulate dorsal horn signalling.15,18,19,31 They are directionally compatible with adult rat slice data showing acute ethanol-associated enhancement of inhibitory transmission in the substantia gelatinosa, although that preparation differs from the developmental model used here.21 Because GABAA receptor effects depend on activation state and synaptic context, the observed changes should not be read as a simple increase or decrease in inhibition.22 Other prenatal alcohol models also reveal long-lasting, circuit-specific changes linked to behavioural outcomes, but they do not establish a direct behavioural consequence of the spinal changes reported here.32 The present data should therefore be interpreted at the cellular and circuit level. They do not establish behavioural sensory or motor impairment, and they do not show that the observed changes fully reproduce clinical FASD phenotypes.

The calcium and CaMKII findings provide a plausible connection between altered inhibitory signalling and apoptosis-related molecular changes. Ethanol exposure reduced intracellular Ca2+ signals and decreased CaMKII phosphorylation at Thr286. CaMKII activation is initiated by Ca2+/calmodulin, and autophosphorylation can extend kinase activity beyond the initial calcium transient.26,27 In cerebellar neurons, CaMKII can directly associate with GSK-3 and promote inhibitory Ser9 phosphorylation, providing a mechanistic precedent for coupling calcium-sensitive signalling to neuronal survival.28 Developmental GABAA receptor overactivation has also been associated with delayed neuronal death in postnatal hippocampal neurons, illustrating that changes in inhibitory signalling can have consequences beyond rapid synaptic transmission.33 The prenatal cortical literature further indicates that ethanol can engage calcium-channel-dependent GABAergic developmental processes in a different anatomical context.23 TEAC and Bay K8644 partially attenuated several ethanol-associated changes, supporting the involvement of membrane excitability and voltage-gated Ca2+ entry. Because these pharmacological tools are not pathway-specific, the data are best interpreted as support for a membrane-potential/Ca2+-sensitive component of ethanol-associated neuronal injury, rather than as definitive proof that CaMKII lies upstream of GSK-3β in a fixed linear cascade.

The observed changes in GSK-3β phosphorylation and mitochondrial apoptosis-related markers are also consistent with this candidate pathway. Reduced Ser9 phosphorylation is compatible with increased GSK-3β activity. In developing neural models, ethanol-associated loss of GSK-3β Ser9 phosphorylation has coincided with BAX and caspase-3 activation.29 Evidence that GSK-3β can phosphorylate BAX and promote its mitochondrial localization provides a mechanistic rationale for examining BAX/AIF-associated injury markers in the present model.34,35 Mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress provide a broader framework for interpreting the observed membrane-potential and ROS changes, while prenatal ethanol has been directly associated with mitochondrial DNA damage and impaired mitochondrial DNA repair in developing brain tissue.36,37 Increased BAX/AIF spatial overlap together with reduced mitochondrial membrane potential indicates mitochondrial apoptosis-related injury. Nevertheless, the BAX/AIF data should be interpreted as altered spatial overlap rather than definitive mitochondrial translocation, because higher-resolution reconstruction or biochemical fractionation would be needed to establish subcellular redistribution with greater confidence. Taken together, these observations are summarized in a proposed working model (Figure 7), in which acute PD7 ethanol exposure is associated with altered GABAergic signalling, reduced Ca2+/CaMKII activity, decreased inhibitory GSK-3β phosphorylation, and apoptosis-related mitochondrial injury markers. This model is intended to integrate the observed associations and does not establish a fixed causal sequence. Limitations

The present study has several limitations. First, the animal model used acute ethanol exposure at PD7, which represents an early-postnatal developmental exposure paradigm in mice rather than chronic maternal prenatal alcohol exposure throughout gestation. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as acute developmental ethanol-associated spinal dorsal horn injury and should not be considered a complete reproduction of the clinical heterogeneity of fetal alcohol spectrum disorders.

Second, the study focused on histological, electrophysiological, molecular, imaging, and cellular endpoints. Behavioural phenotyping was not included, so the present data cannot directly link dorsal horn neuronal injury to overt sensory or motor deficits. Sex was not systematically recorded, which prevents sex-specific analysis. Although littermates were allocated across groups where possible, the archived study design does not allow a full analysis of sex, litter, and treatment interactions.

Third, several analyses were limited by sample size and archived data structure. Formal a priori power calculation was not performed, and some assays used relatively small biological replicate numbers. Although the revised manuscript clarifies biological replicate definitions where possible, some experiments included multiple technical measurements from the same animal or culture preparation, which limits the strength of inference. Future studies using larger cohorts, prospectively defined biological units, and full source-data reporting would improve reproducibility.

Fourth, the mechanistic pathway should be regarded as a candidate model rather than a definitive linear cascade. TEAC and Bay K8644 partially moderated ethanol-associated changes, but these pharmacological tools are not selective tests of CaMKII, GSK-3β, or BAX pathway hierarchy. The study did not include CaMKII-specific perturbation, selective GSK-3β inhibition or rescue, BAX-specific intervention, or genetic validation. Thus, the data support an association among altered GABAergic signalling, reduced Ca2+-CaMKII activity, GSK-3β regulation, and apoptosis-related changes, but they do not establish complete molecular causality.

Fifth, the in vitro ethanol condition has interpretive boundaries. The 400 mM ethanol exposure was useful for detecting acute cellular vulnerability under the tested experimental conditions, but a full dose-response series, lower-dose validation, ethanol evaporation control, and osmolality control were not included. The physiological range and dose dependence of the observed calcium and apoptosis-related changes therefore require further validation.

Finally, several technical assays provide supportive but not definitive evidence. BAX/AIF immunofluorescence showed altered spatial overlap, but z-stack or orthogonal reconstruction, mitochondrial co-labelling, and subcellular fractionation were not available to confirm mitochondrial translocation. Co-immunoprecipitation experiments included IgG controls, but complete matched input controls were not available for all conditions, limiting the interpretation of interaction strength. Flow-cytometry assays were performed under consistent acquisition settings, but full gating plots and raw distribution data were not available for comprehensive presentation. Quantitative cell-type-specific colocalization and subtype-specific tracing would also be needed to determine whether ethanol-associated injury is selective for GAD67-positive islet-like neurons or affects broader inhibitory neuronal populations in the superficial dorsal horn.

Conclusion

In conclusion, acute PD7 ethanol exposure was associated with neuronal injury and caspase-3 activation in the developing mouse spinal dorsal horn. The injury was accompanied by altered GABAergic signalling, reduced intracellular Ca2+ signalling, decreased CaMKII phosphorylation, reduced CaMKII-GSK-3β association, increased GSK-3β activation, and mitochondrial apoptosis-related changes. These findings support a candidate GABAergic Ca2+-CaMKII-GSK-3β/BAX signalling pathway involved in ethanol-associated apoptosis in developing spinal dorsal horn neurons.
