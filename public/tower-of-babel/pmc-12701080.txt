Low-overload internal ballistics in UAV ejection using multiple time-sequenced compressed-air chambers
Authors: Tian Li, Zhai-jun Lu, Zhi-fu Wang, Shu-jian Yao, Jia-qiang Wang, Dong-run Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Compressed-air ejection systems are characterized by short actuation times and high instantaneous flow rates, which subject unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) to significant overloads during launch. These conditions impose stringent structural requirements on UAVs, adversely affecting weight and cost control. To achieve a high launch velocity with low overload, this study proposes a multi-chamber ejection method with time-sequenced actuation. This approach is based on an internal ballistics model that incorporates real-gas properties. This approach reduces launch overload while maintaining the required muzzle velocity. An internal ballistics model for UAV compressed-air ejection has been developed and experimentally validated, utilizing real-air properties. Furthermore, a multi-chamber ejection strategy was introduced. Simulations were conducted to analyze the internal ballistic performance of systems with two or three identical or different high-pressure chambers. The results demonstrate that using multiple chambers significantly reduces the maximum overload—by 20.91%, 26.08%, and 33.24% for two identical, two different, and three different chambers, respectively—while achieving the same muzzle velocity as a single-chamber system.

Introduction

Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) are capable of performing diverse missions, including surveying and mapping, logistics transportation, and emergency response. Their applications have expanded from early military uses to a wide range of civil, commercial, and consumer domains1–3. UAV launch systems are employed to enable takeoff in complex or constrained environments, overcoming limitations inherent in the UAV’s own propulsion or aerodynamic design, thereby enhancing safety and operational reliability4–7.

Various launch methodologies are available for UAVs, such as hand launch, vertical take-off, electromagnetic catapulting, hydraulic catapulting, and pneumatic (compressed-air) ejection8–12. Among these, compressed-air ejection systems offer distinct advantages, including structural simplicity, high mobility, and superior energy density13,14. A key advantage of this method is its ability to rapidly accelerate UAVs to take-off a velocity without generating significant optical, acoustic, or thermal signatures. This process reduces energy consumption during launch, increases payload capacity, and extends operational range15. As a result, compressed-air ejection is suitable for launching small, medium, and large UAVs, making it one of the predominant launch technologies and a key focus of ongoing research.

Substantial research has been conducted on UAV compressed-air ejection systems. Scholars have developed computational internal ballistics models to simulate launch dynamics and have validated these models experimentally16–19. Extensive studies have investigated the influence of key system parameters—such as high-pressure reservoir characteristics, valve dynamics, and low-pressure chamber volume—on internal ballistic performance. Li et al.20 established a mathematical model for compressed-air ejection internal ballistics and analyzed the effects of valve opening time, maximum flow area, and initial low-pressure chamber volume. Liu et al.21 applied the Bond Graph method to model a UAV pneumatic launch system, examining how reservoir charging pressure and volume influence performance. Glebocki et al.22 investigated the dynamics of cold-launched missiles controlled by gas dynamics, demonstrating the potential for trajectory control during the launch phase. Peng et al.23 combined Bond Graph modeling with simulation to design and analyze high-pressure, large-bore, fast-switching valves, proposing a novel design for rapid actuation. Research has also explored alternative working fluids: Yao et al.24 analyzed the feasibility of supercritical carbon dioxide (sCO2), Wang et al.25 studied valve-specific effects with CO₂, and Wang et al.26 proposed tetrafluoroethane (R134a), developing thermo-fluid dynamic models and comparing its performance with that of compressed-air. Gao et al.27 investigated the internal ballistics for missiles undergoing accelerations below 10 g, providing insights relevant to low-overload UAV launches. Zhang et al.28 analyzed the effects of pulse valve area, full-opening time, and initial chamber volumes on ballistic performance. They applied an improved particle swarm optimization algorithm based on submunition principles to optimize low-overload launch parameters for small UAVs. However, this method requires redesigning multiple system parameters to achieve both sufficient muzzle velocity and reduced peak overload.

A critical challenge remains: achieving high muzzle velocity while minimizing the peak overload transmitted to the UAV airframe. Notably, all the studies referenced above16–28 employ a single high-pressure chamber configuration. Given the short actuation times and high instantaneous flow rates characteristic of compressed-air ejection, single-chamber systems inherently produce substantial overloads, imposing stringent impact resistance requirements on the UAV structure. Moreover, most internal ballistics models for pneumatic ejection assume ideal gas behavior. Although some studies incorporate real gas models, the presence and effects of pre-existing air in the low-pressure chamber are often overlooked or inadequately modeled.

Prior studies on gas-generator-based ejection systems have investigated internal ballistics models involving sequentially ignited multiple generators. For example, He et al.29 developed a reverse-calculation method to determine the number of generators, mass flow rates, and ignition timing required to achieve a specified muzzle velocity. Liu et al.30 established a general internal ballistics model that incorporates sequential ignition and proposed an optimization algorithm for ignition timing that accounts for both maximum overload and low-pressure chamber efficiency.

However, these earlier studies differ fundamentally from the present work in two key aspects. First, the working fluid and gas model differ. The prior studies employ gas generators and assume ideal gas behavior, whereas the present model utilizes compressed air and incorporates real-gas properties via the NIST database. Second, the flow dynamics differ significantly: in gas generator systems, the high pressure ratio generally maintains supersonic flow, whereas in compressed-air ejection, the smaller pressure difference necessitates modeling both supersonic and subsonic flow regimes. Moreover, the sequential operation of multiple air chambers can induce cyclic transitions between these flow states, further distinguishing our computational approach from existing methods.

To address these limitations, this study develops an internal ballistics model for compressed air that incorporates real-gas properties and accounts for the pre-existing air in the low-pressure chamber, an often-overlooked factor. We propose a novel strategy using multiple high-pressure chambers with time-sequenced actuation. The core innovation is a significant reduction in peak overload and a notable improvement in launch smoothness while the required launch velocity is maintained. This is accomplished without modifying other key system parameters, only by increasing the number of high-pressure chambers. This approach effectively lowers the structural demands on the UAV, thereby enhancing its overall performance.

Internal ballistics of single-chamber ejection

This section details the fundamental gas-dynamic assumptions and establishes a generalized internal ballistics model for UAV ejection using a single high-pressure compressed-air chamber. The model, which is validated against experimental data, is formulated based on the principles of mass conservation, energy conservation, and the real gas equation of state.

Fundamental assumptions

Compressed-air ejection is a complex thermodynamic process, which poses challenges for developing a complete and exact physical model. Therefore, the following simplifying assumptions are adopted:

The process is treated as adiabatic, neglecting heat exchange between the system and the environment due to the short ejection duration (typically < 0.085 s).The gas flow is considered one-dimensional and quasi-steady, based on the high length-to-diameter ratio of the launch tube, viscosity effects are neglected.The gas is uniformly distributed and in complete thermodynamic equilibrium throughout the process.The ejection system is assumed to be perfectly sealed, with no gas leakage.

The computed pressure variations in the high-pressure chamber (HPC) and low-pressure chamber (LPC) are shown in Fig. 2. Following pulse valve opening, air from the HPC flows into the LPC, causing the HPC pressure to decrease monotonically. In contrast, the LPC pressure initially increases to a peak value before decreasing. The termination of each curve corresponds precisely to the moment the UAV reaches the end of the effective launch stroke (1.03 m), which is the muzzle exit point.

This phenomenon occurs because the UAV’s low initial velocity and displacement result in slow LPC volume expansion, allowing incoming HPC air to rapidly pressurize the LPC. In the later phase, however, higher UAV velocity causes rapid LPC expansion. The gas influx from the HPC then becomes insufficient to compensate for this expansion, resulting in pressure reduction.

The calculated mass flow rate from the high-pressure chamber (Fig. 3) shows distinct characteristics under different initial pressures. For initial pressures of 0.5 MPa and 0.6 MPa, the mass flow rate peaked before valve full opening, reaching 0.818 kg/s at 0.027 s and 1.057 kg/s at 0.0285 s, respectively. In contrast, for higher initial pressures of 0.7 MPa and 0.8 MPa, the maximum flow rates of 1.327 kg/s and 1.605 kg/s both occurred at 0.03 s, coinciding with the valve’s full opening time.

This temporal variation results from the competing effects between valve opening dynamics and chamber pressure evolution. The pulse valve requires 0.03 s to reach full opening, during which its flow area increases progressively before stabilizing. At higher initial pressures (0.7–0.8 MPa), the mass flow rate is dominated by the increasing valve flow area during the opening phase (t < 0.03 s), leading to continuous flow acceleration until full opening. Subsequently, the flow rate fluctuates according to the evolving pressure differential between chambers.

In contrast, at lower initial pressures (0.5–0.6 MPa), the evolving pressure differential between chambers is the predominant factor, leading to earlier flow peaks before full valve opening. The flow rate then gradually decreases until the 0.03 s mark, where an abrupt change occurs due to complete valve opening, followed by continued fluctuations driven by chamber pressure conditions.

In contrast, within the LPC, the incoming air from the HPC increases the total mass and energy content, causing initial rises in both temperature and specific internal energy. Before the pulse valve fully opened at 0.03 s, the HPC temperature and specific internal energy showed nearly identical trends. After this point, their rates of decrease became more pronounced with higher initial pressures, though the final values remained highest for the maximum initial pressure case.

For the LPC, higher initial HPC pressures resulted in faster rates of change and higher peak values for both temperature and specific internal energy. In contrast, the final values of these parameters at muzzle exit were lower for higher initial pressures.

Internal ballistics of multi-chamber low-overload ejection

An ideal ejection internal ballistic profile would feature rapidly rising acceleration that then remains stable. Such a profile enables the maximum muzzle velocity to be achieved with the minimum peak acceleration within a limited launch stroke. However, the inherent characteristics of pneumatic ejection—particularly with a single high-pressure chamber—lead to short actuation time and high instantaneous mass flow rate. This typically produces an acceleration profile characterized by a sharp, early peak followed by a rapid decay. Achieving the target muzzle velocity under these conditions requires a high peak acceleration, which imposes stringent overload resistance requirements on the UAV, thereby compromising weight and cost efficiency and potentially degrading the overall system performance.

To overcome this limitation, this paper proposes a novel approach using multiple high-pressure chambers with time-sequenced actuation. The method aims to significantly reduce peak acceleration while maintaining the required muzzle velocity.

Multi-chamber compressed-air ejection model

The multi-chamber compressed-air ejection device is configured as illustrated schematically in Fig. 10. This design requires no modifications to other structural components of the ejection system and involves only connecting multiple high-pressure chambers in parallel. The valves of these chambers are opened sequentially according to a predefined timing schedule (0, t1, t2, ··· tn−1), releasing compressed air into the common low-pressure chamber.

Compressed air from the first chamber flows initially into the low-pressure chamber, propelling the UAV and increasing the chamber volume. Subsequent chambers are activated at designated times to provide supplemental energy input. This staged injection sustains a more stable pressure in the low-pressure chamber, thereby facilitating a higher muzzle velocity while simultaneously lowering the peak acceleration within the constrained launch stroke.

Schematic diagram of the multi-chamber compressed-air ejection device.

The governing equations for mass and energy conservation, dynamics, and the real gas model for a single high-pressure chamber, remained consistent with those established in Sect. 2. To extend the model to multiple chambers, we modify the mass and energy conservation equations for the low-pressure chamber to incorporate the contributions from each additional high-pressure chamber upon their activation times (t1, t2, ··· tn−1). To simplify the computational model, the airflow from individual chambers is assumed to be non-interfering.

Internal ballistics of dual-chamber ejection

To achieve a muzzle velocity of at least 35 m/s—equivalent to that of a single high-pressure chamber (HPC) at 0.8 MPa—a dual-chamber system requires a lower initial pressure per chamber. Three actuation scenarios were analyzed for a configuration with two 0.6 MPa HPCs: simultaneous actuation (t1 = 0 ms), and sequential actuation with intervals of 15 ms (t1 = 15 ms) and 25 ms (t1 = 25 ms). The resulting acceleration profiles are shown in Fig. 11, along with the single-chamber (0.8 MPa) case for reference.

As shown in Fig. 11, increasing the actuation interval reduces the peak acceleration and stabilizes the acceleration profile. Specifically, the peak acceleration decreases from 889.75 m/s² (t1 = 0 ms) to 821.92 m/s² (t1 = 15 ms) and further to 759.39 m/s² (t1 = 25 ms), while the muzzle velocity remains at or above 35 m/s (36.27 m/s, 35.91 m/s, and 35.00 m/s, respectively). Moreover, for intervals up to 25 ms, the muzzle velocity meets or exceeds 35 m/s. Notably, at t1 = 25 ms, the peak acceleration is 20.91% lower than that of the single 0.8 MPa HPC case.

Increasing the actuation interval reduces the peak acceleration because the UAV has already begun moving and the LPC volume has expanded after the first chamber is actuated. This expansion means that the first chamber’s energy output primarily governs the maximum acceleration. Using two HPCs with different initial pressures—a lower-pressure chamber followed by a higher-pressure one—and optimizing the interval t₁ can yield an acceleration profile with a lower peak and a more sustained stable phase.

We also analyzed a configuration comprising with a 0.5 MPa HPC followed by a 0.7 MPa HPC with an interval of t1 = 15 ms. The resulting UAV acceleration profile is shown in Fig. 12, together with the dual 0.6 MPa (25 ms interval) and single 0.8 MPa HPC reference cases. This two-chamber system with different initial pressures achieved a muzzle velocity of 35.06 m/s with a peak acceleration of 709.8 m/s². Moreover, its acceleration profile exhibits both a lower peak and a longer stable phase than the reference cases. This represents a 6.53% reduction in peak acceleration relative to the dual 0.6 MPa HPC (25 ms interval) case and a 26.08% reduction compared to the single 0.8 MPa HPC case.

Internal ballistics of triple-chamber ejection

A triple-chamber ejection scheme requires even lower initial pressures in each HPC to achieve the same muzzle velocity. To further minimize peak acceleration, we analyzed a configuration employing three HPCs with initial pressures of 0.5, 0.6, and 0.7 MPa, activated sequentially from lowest to highest pressure at time intervals of 0, 24, and 39 ms, respectively. The resulting acceleration profile is shown in Fig. 15, along with comparisons to the heterogeneous dual-chamber (0.5 & 0.7 MPa, 15 ms interval) and single 0.8 MPa HPC cases.

The triple-chamber system achieved a muzzle velocity of 35.05 m/s with a peak acceleration of 641.05 m/s². This represents a 9.69% reduction in peak acceleration compared to the heterogeneous dual-chamber case, and a substantial 33.24% reduction relative to the single 0.8 MPa HPC case.

The launch smoothness for single (0.8 MPa), dual (0.5 & 0.7 MPa), and triple (0.5, 0.6, & 0.7 MPa) HPC configurations is evaluated using the peak-to-average overload ratio, where a higher value corresponds to poorer launch smoothness (Table 5).

The dual 0.6 MPa & 0.6 MPa (0–25 ms) configuration increased ejection time by 0.0049 s while reducing peak acceleration by 20.91%, average acceleration by 7.17%, and improving smoothness by 14.74%.

The energy utilization efficiency (η) is defined as the ratio of the UAV’s kinetic energy at muzzle exit to the initial internal energy of the air in the high-pressure chamber, given that the gravitational potential energy remains constant due to the fixed launcher height. The calculated efficiency for different configurations is summarized in Table 6.

The relatively low energy efficiency across all configurations stems from the short launch tube. The UAV exits the tube while the pressure in both the high- and low-pressure chambers is still high. Consequently, a significant portion of the initial energy remains unused. For the single 0.8 MPa HPC case, only 10% of the initial energy is converted to kinetic energy, a result consistent with Ref28. Employing multiple chambers further reduces energy efficiency. To achieve smoother acceleration over the fixed stroke, the system maintains even higher chamber pressures until muzzle exit, resulting in more residual energy. For example, the efficiency drops to 4% for the triple-chamber configuration.

In summary, compared to single-chamber ejection, the multi-chamber approach significantly reduces peak acceleration while maintaining the required muzzle velocity, producing an acceleration profile closer to the ideal ballistic curve. Furthermore, employing multiple HPCs with different initial pressures yields better performance than using multiple identical HPCs. Theoretically, increasing the number of chambers and minimizing the pressure difference between sequentially activated chambers should further reduce the peak acceleration and better approximate the ideal acceleration profile.

Conclusion

This study has established and validated an internal ballistics model for UAV pneumatic ejection that incorporates the real-gas properties of compressed air. Experimental validation confirms the model’s satisfactory reliability and accuracy. A novel multi-chamber time-sequenced ejection method was proposed, and computational results demonstrate that this approach significantly reduces the launch overload while maintaining the required ejection velocity.
