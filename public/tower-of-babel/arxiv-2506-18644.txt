Abstract
. We study unique games and estimate some of their values. We prove that if a unique game has a quantum-assisted value close to 1, then it must have a perfect deterministic strategy. We introduce a family of unique games based on groups that generalize XOR games, and show that when the group is the cyclic group of order 3, then these games correspond to a 3-labelling problem for directed graphs.



1.
Introduction

This paper is concerned with a certain family of two person games, called
unique games
, and a special subclass of unique games, which we call
games based on groups
. When the group is the cyclic group of order two, then these group based games correspond to the well studied family of XOR games. We show that a certain labelling problem for directed graphs corresponds to a family of group based games for the cyclic group of order 3.

We are especially interested in the case that there is a probability density on the inputs of these games, and the players are allowed to use probabilistic strategies. In these cases the question always arises of whether or not a quantum-assisted strategy can yield a higher expected value of winning the game than any classical strategy. In the case of the labelling problem for directed graphs, we show that the classical value of winning can be interpreted as a directed analogue of the cut value for undirected graphs and that often the quantum-assisted value of this directed cut number is higher.

Intuitively, in these games the two players, who are often called Alice and Bob, are playing cooperatively and attempting to give correct pairs of answers to pairs of questions posed by a third party often called the
Referee
or
Verifier
.
Whether the pair of answers returned by the players is satisfactory or not depends not just on the individual answers but on the 4-tuple consisting of the question-answer pair.

Such a game is described by two
input
sets
X,Y
, often thought of as sets of questions for Alice and Bob, respectively, two
output
sets
A,B
, often thought of as Alice and Bob’s respective answer sets,
and a function, often called the
rules
or
verification function
,

V:X\times Y\times A\times B\to\{0,1\},

where

W:=\{(x,y,a,b):V(x,y,a,b)=1\},

is the set of
correct
or
winning
4-tuples and

L:=\{(x,y,a,b):V(x,y,a,b)=0\},

is the set of
incorrect
or
losing
4-tuples.

Thus, a game
\mathcal{G}
is specified by
(X,Y,A,B,V)
.
Before the game begins Alice and Bob know the four sets and the function
V
.
Even though Alice and Bob are not allowed to communicate during the game they are allowed to communicate before the
game and decide on some type of strategy.

A two person game is called
synchronous
provided that
X=Y,\,A=B
and the rule function satisfies the condition that
V(x,x,a,b)=0
whenever
a\neq b
. Thus, if both players are ever asked the same question, then they must give the same reply in order to win. A synchronous game is called
symmetric
provided that
V(x,y,a,b)=V(y,x,b,a)
.

A game is called a
unique game
if for each triple
x\in X,y\in Y,a\in A
there is a unique
b\in B
such that
V(x,y,a,b)=1
and, in addition, for each triple
x\in X,y\in Y,b\in B
there is a unique
a\in A
such that
V(x,y,a,b)=1
. Alternatively, since these hypotheses imply that
|A|=|B|
, we may assume that
A=B
and that there is a map
f:X\times Y\to\Perm(A),
where
\Perm(A)
denotes the group of permutations of
A
, such that

V(x,y,a,b)=1\iff a=f(x,y)b.

It is this characterization that we shall most often use for describing unique games and, consequently we will often describe a unique game as
\mathcal{G}=(X,Y,A,f)
, with the understanding that
A=B
,
f:X\times Y\to\Perm(A)
and that the verifier is the function given as above.

It is not hard to see that a unique game described in this manner is synchronous if and only if
X=Y
and
f(x,x)
is the identity permutation for all
x
, and is symmetric if and only if, in addition,
f(y,x)=f(x,y)^{-1}
.

A special type of unique game that we shall be interested in is when the set
A=\Omega
, with
\Omega
a finite group and the only permutations that we allow are the ones that come from the left action of the group upon itself. That is, the verifier has the form
f:X\times Y\to\Omega
and

V(x,y,a,b)=1\iff ab^{-1}=f(x,y).

We will call such a game a
group based game
.

One famous example of a group based game is the CHSH game which is the case that
X=Y=\Omega=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, the binary field,
and

V(x,y,a,b)=1\iff a-b=xy:=f(x,y).

We will see later that when
\Omega
is the cyclic group of order 3, then synchronous, symmetric group based games correspond to a certain type of labelling game for directed graphs.

2.
Correlations and Values of Games

When we want to talk about the probability of winning a game
\mathcal{G}=(X,Y,A,B,V)
we also need to specify a probability density on input pairs, i.e., a function
\pi:I_{A}\times I_{B}\to[0,1]
such that

\sum_{x\in I_{A},y\in I_{B}}\pi(x,y)=1.

For games with densities, Alice and Bob also know the density before the start of the game.
We write
(\mathcal{G},\pi)
to denote a game with density.

A
deterministic strategy
for a game is a pair of functions
h_{A}:X\to A,\,\,h_{B}:Y\to B
such that whenever Alice and Bob receive input pair
(x,y)
they respond with output pair
(f(x),g(y))
.

Given a game with a density
(\mathcal{G},\pi)
and a deterministic strategy as above, the probability of winning the game is

\sum_{x,y}\pi(x,y)V(x,y,h_{A}(x),h_{B}(y))=\sum_{x,y\colon(x,y,h_{A}(x),h_{B}(y))\in W}\pi(x,y).

The
classical value
or
deterministic value
of
(\mathcal{G},\pi)
is defined to be the supremum of this winning probability over all possible deterministic strategies, i.e., pairs of functions
h_{A},h_{B}
. Computing this value often requires searching over all pairs of functions
h_{A},h_{B}
and so can be computationally difficult.

The classical value of a game is generally denoted
\omega(\mathcal{G},\pi)
, but we shall write
\omega_{det}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
, since we will be examining several different versions of the value of a game and want a notation that distinguishes them.

Suppose now that instead of a deterministic strategy, Alice and Bob return random answers to their question pairs. If we let

p(a,b|x,y)
denote the probability that they return the answer pair
(a,b)
when given the question pair
(x,y)
, then over many rounds of the game the expected probability that they will win
(\mathcal{G},\pi)
is given by

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G},\pi}(p):=\sum_{(x,y,a,b)\in W}\pi(x,y)p(a,b|x,y).

Given a game
\mathcal{G}
a density
p(a,b|x,y)
is called
perfect
provided the probability that it returns a wrong answer is 0, that is, provided that

V(x,y,a,b)=0\implies p(a,b|x,y)=0.

Note that if a density is perfect, then for any density
\pi
on inputs, one has
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G},\pi}(p)=1.

The various values of a game that are studied are defined by taking the supremum of
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G},\pi}(p)
over certain sets of conditional probabilities. These sets of conditional probabilities are called
local, quantum, quantum spatial, quantum approximate, quantum commuting,
and
nonsignalling
and are denoted,

C_{t}(n_{A},n_{B},k_{A},k_{B}),

with
t=loc,q,qs,qa,qc,ns
, respectively, where
n_{A}=|X|
and
n_{B}=|Y|
indicate the number of Alice’s and Bob’s inputs and
k_{A}=|A|
and
k_{B}=|B|
indicate the number of their outputs.
Usually, for the sake of convenience we will omit
n_{A},n_{B},k_{A},k_{B}
in our notations.

These sets are all convex and they satisfy,

C_{loc}\subsetneq C_{q}\subsetneq C_{qs}\subsetneq C_{q}^{-}=C_{qs}^{-}=C_{qa}\subsetneq C_{qc}\subsetneq C_{ns},

where the notation
C_{q}^{-}
and
C_{qs}^{-}
indicates the closure of the set viewed as a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n_{A}n_{B}k_{A}k_{B}}
.

We refer the reader to
[
6
,
5
]
for the precise definitions of these sets and for references to the papers that show that the containments are all strict.

The corresponding values of a game with density
(\mathcal{G},\pi)
are obtained by taking the supremum over each of these sets and these suprema are denoted by, respectively,
\omega_{t}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
with
t=loc,q,qs,qa,qc,ns
.
Because of the closure properties,
\omega_{q}(\mathcal{G},\pi)=\omega_{qs}(\mathcal{G},\pi)=\omega_{qa}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
. Moreover because of the containments,

\omega_{loc}(\mathcal{G},\pi)\leq\omega_{q}(\mathcal{G},\pi)\leq\omega_{qc}(\mathcal{G},\pi)\leq\omega_{ns}(\mathcal{G},\pi).

It is also known that
\omega_{loc}(\mathcal{G},\pi)=\omega_{det}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
, since the extreme points of the set
C_{loc}
correspond to densities of the form

p(a,b|x,y)=\begin{cases}1&a=h_{A}(x),b=h_{B}(y).\\
0&\text{else},\end{cases}

for some pair of functions.

In the case that
X=Y
and
A=B
, we call a density
synchronous
provided that

p(a,b|x,x)=0,\,\,\forall a\neq b,\,\forall x,

and we call a density
symmetric
if

p(a,b|x,y)=p(b,a|y,x),\,\,\forall x,y,a,b.

For
t=loc,q,qs,qa,qc,ns
we let
C_{t}^{s}(n,n,k,k)\subseteq C_{t}(n,n,k,k)
denote the subset of synchronous densities, and often abbreviate this as
C_{t}^{s}
.
Moreover, if
\mathcal{G}
is a game with
X=Y,\,A=B
we let
\omega_{t}^{s}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
denote the supremum over all synchronous densities of the various types.

It was shown in
[
9
]
that
(C_{q}^{s})^{-}=C_{qa}^{s}
, so for any such game, we also have that

\omega^{s}_{q}(\mathcal{G},\pi)=\omega_{qs}^{s}(\mathcal{G},\pi)=\omega_{qa}^{s}(\mathcal{G},\pi).

In addition, it is readily seen that

\omega^{s}_{loc}(\mathcal{G},\pi)=\sup_{h}\{\sum_{x,y}\pi(x,y)V(x,y,h(x),h(y))\mid h:X\to A\}.

Synchronous densities are known to arise from various types of traces, see
[
9
,
6
,
5
]
for precise statements.

Remark 2.1
.

Every unique game has a perfect nonsignalling strategy. If
\mathcal{G}=(X,Y,A,f)
is a unique game and we set

p(a,b|x,y)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{|A|},&a=f(x,y)b\\
0&else.\end{cases}

Then this is a non-signalling density with
p_{A}(a|x)=p_{B}(b|y)=\frac{1}{|A|}
and is a perfect strategy for
\mathcal{G}
.

Moreover, if
\mathcal{G}
is a synchronous unique game, then this density is synchronous, and if
\mathcal{G}
is also symmetric, then this density is symmetric and synchronous.

There are two other sets of conditional probability densities that we wish to consider.

The notation for the first set that we shall use was introduced in
[
14
]
. This set of densities also appears elsewhere with different names and is related to the NPA-hierarchy. Depending on how the NPA-hierarchy is numbered, these are sometimes said to arise from the 1/2-level.

A density
p(a,b|x,y)
is called a
vect-density
, if there is a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, and sets of vectors
\{v_{x,a}:x\in X,a\in A\},\,\{w_{y,b}:y\in Y,b\in B\}
in
\mathcal{H}
and a unit vector
\eta\in\mathcal{H}
such that:

•

v_{x,a}\perp v_{x,a^{\prime}},\forall x,\forall a\neq a^{\prime}
,

•

w_{y,b}\perp w_{y,b^{\prime}},\forall y,\forall b\neq b^{\prime}
,

•

\sum_{a}v_{x,a}=\sum_{b}w_{y,b}=\eta,\forall x,y
,

•

p(a,b|x,y)=\langle v_{x,a}|w_{y,b}\rangle\geq 0,\forall x,y,a,b
.

The set of all such densities is denoted
C_{vect}
and it is known to be a convex set satisfying

C_{qc}\subset C_{vect}\subset C_{ns},

see
[
14
]
.
Note that the condition that the inner products are all non-negative implies that without loss of generality,
\mathcal{H}
can be assumed to be a real Hilbert space with a dimension that is bounded by a function of
n_{A},k_{A},n_{B},k_{B}
.

In the case that
X=Y,\,A=B
, we show below that
p\in C^{s}_{vect}
if and only if
v_{x,a}=w_{x,a},\,\forall x,a
.

We let
\omega_{vect}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
denote the supremum of
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G},\pi}(p)
over all
p\in C_{vect}
, so that we have

\omega_{qc}(\mathcal{G},\pi)\leq\omega_{vect}(\mathcal{G},\pi)\leq\omega_{ns}(\mathcal{G},\pi).

The quantity
\omega_{vect}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
is often useful for giving a computable upper bound on
\omega_{qc}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
.
The quantity
\omega_{vect}^{s}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
is defined similarly.

The next set of densities that we wish to define is useful for giving lower bounds on
\omega_{q}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
.
Recall that a density
p(a,b|x,y)
is in
C_{q}
if and only if there are finite dimensional Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B}
, projections
\{E_{x,a}:x\in X,a\in A\}\subseteq B(\mathcal{H}_{A})
and
\{F_{y,b}:y\in Y,b\in B\}\subseteq B(\mathcal{H}_{B})
with
\sum_{a}E_{x,a}=I_{\mathcal{H}_{A}},\forall x
and
\sum_{b}F_{y,b}=I_{\mathcal{H}_{B}},\forall y
and a unit vector
\eta\in\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
such that

p(a,b|x,y)=\langle E_{x,a}\otimes F_{y,b}\eta|\eta\rangle.

We say that a density
p\in C_{q}
is in
C_{q_{1}}
if it is of this form with the additional requirement that

\rk(E_{x,a})\leq 1\text{ and }\rk(F_{y,b})\leq 1,\forall x,y,a,b.

We let
\omega_{q_{1}}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
(respectively,
\omega_{q_{1}}^{s}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
), denote the supremum of
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G},\pi}(p)
over all
p\in C_{q_{1}}
(respectively,
p\in C_{q_{1}}^{s}
).

Note that the rank condition puts an upper bound on the dimensions of the Hilbert spaces for
q_{1}
-densities, since

\dim(\mathcal{H}_{A})\leq|A|,\,\dim(\mathcal{H}_{B})\leq|B|.

Moreover, each of Alice’s projections must be of the form
E_{x,a}=|v_{x,a}\rangle\langle v_{x,a}|=v_{x,a}v_{x,a}^{*}
where each of the vectors is either a unit vector or 0,
v_{x,a}\perp v_{x,a^{\prime}},\forall a\neq a^{\prime}
and the number of non-zero vectors is equal to
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{A})
, with similar statements for
F_{y,b}
and finally,

p(a,b|x,y)=|\langle\eta|v_{x,a}\otimes w_{y,b}\rangle|^{2}.

Below we record a few facts about the elements of
C_{vect}^{s}
and
C_{q_{1}}^{s}
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
p(a,b|x,y)\geq 0,\forall a,b,x,y
. Then

(a)

p\in C_{vect}^{s}(n,n,k,k)
if and only if there is a real Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, a unit vector
\eta\in\mathcal{H}
and for each
1\leq x\leq n
a set of orthogonal vectors
\{v_{x,a}:1\leq a\leq k\}
such that
\sum_{a}v_{x,a}=\eta
and
p(a,b|x,y)=\langle v_{x,a}|v_{y,b}\rangle
,

(b)

if
p(a,b|x,y)
is an extreme point of the convex hull of
C_{q_{1}}^{s}(n,n,k,k)
, then there is a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
of dimension
d\leq k
and sets of orthogonal vectors
\{v_{x,a}:1\leq a\leq k\}
in
\mathcal{H}
such that
d
of the vectors are unit norm and the remaining
k-d
vectors are 0
with
p(a,b|x,y)=\frac{1}{d}|\langle v_{x,a}|v_{y,b}\rangle|^{2}.

Proof.

The first statement follows from the observation that
0=p(a,b|x,x)=\langle v_{x,a}|w_{x,b}\rangle,a\neq b
and
1=\sum_{a,b}p(a,b|x,x)=\sum_{a}p(a,a|x,x)
implies that
v_{x,a}=w_{x,a}
.

To see the second statement let
p(a,b|x,y)=\langle E_{x,a}\otimes F_{y,b}\eta|\eta\rangle
be any element of
C^{s}_{q_{1}}
, so that the rank of each
E_{x,a}
is at most 1. By the results of
[
13
]
, if we let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq B(\mathcal{H}_{A})
denote the algebra generated by by Alice’s operators then the functional
\tau:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{C}
defined by

\tau(X)=\langle X\otimes I\eta|\eta\rangle,

is a trace on that algebra and
p(a,b|x,y)=\tau(E_{x,a}E_{y,b})
. Since
\mathcal{H}_{A}
is finite dimensional,
\mathcal{A}
must be *-isomorphic to a direct sum of matrix algebras and the image of
E_{x,a}
in each component of this direct sum decomposition is of rank at most 1. On each matrix algebra, there is a unique trace, namely, the ordinary trace, normalized so that the trace of the identity is 1. Thus,
\tau
is a convex combination of traces on matrix algebras and this direct sum decomposition expresses
p
as a convex combination of elements of
C^{s}_{q_{1}}
. Since
p
is an extreme point, either there is only one summand or each summand gives rise to a multiple of
p
.
The result now follows from the observation that since
p\in C_{q_{1}}
, the restriction of
E_{x,a}
to each summand is still a projection of rank 0 or 1 and the fact that

\Tr(v_{x,a}v_{x,a}^{*}v_{y,b}v_{y,b}^{*})=|\langle v_{x,a}|v_{y,b}\rangle|^{2}.\qed

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(X,X,A,A,V)
be a game and let
\pi
be a density on inputs. Then

\omega^{s}_{q_{1}}(\mathcal{G},\pi)=\sup\left\{\frac{1}{d}\sum_{(x,y,a,b)\in W}\pi(x,y)|\langle v_{x,a}|v_{y,b}\rangle|^{2}\right\},

where the supremum is over all
0\leq d\leq k
and sets of orthogonal vectors
\{v_{x,a}:1\leq a\leq k\}
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that
d
of the vectors are unit norm and the remaining
k-d
vectors are 0.

The corollary follows from the fact that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G},\pi}(p)
is a convex function of
p
.

Next we turn our attention to a new characterization of the synchronous qc-value of a game that does not appear in
[
6
]
but will be useful for our calculations. To this end assume that
\mathcal{G}
is a game with
X=Y,\,\,A=B
, with a verifier
V
, that is not necessarily a synchronous game. Let
n=|X|,\,k=|A|
and let
\mathbb{F}(n,k)
denote the free product of
n
copies of the cyclic group of order
k
. This group has generators
\{u_{x}:x\in X\}
with
u_{x}^{k}=1
, where
1
denotes the identity element. We let
\mathbb{C}(\mathbb{F}(n,k))
denote the group algebra of this group, which we regard as consisting of finite linear combinations of elements of the group (rather than finitely supported functions on the group) and let
C^{*}(\mathbb{F}(n,k))
denote the full group C*-algebra.

If we let
\xi:=e^{2\pi i/k}
, then the elements of
\mathbb{C}(\mathbb{F}(n,k))
defined by

e_{x,a}:=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(\xi^{a-1}u_{x})^{j},

are the spectral projections of the unitary
u_{x}
and these elements also generate
\mathbb{C}(\mathbb{F}(n,k))
.

Assume that
\mathcal{G}
also has a prior density
\pi
on inputs. Following
[
6
]
we set

R_{\mathcal{G},\pi}=\sum_{(x,y,a,b)\in W}\pi(x,y)e_{x,a}e_{y,b}e_{x,a}.

We recall the following theorem from
[
6
]
.

Theorem 2.4
.

[
6
]
Let
\mathcal{G}=(X,X,A,A,V)
be a game and let
\pi
be a density on inputs. Then

\omega_{qc}^{s}(\mathcal{G},\pi)=\sup\{\tau(R_{\mathcal{G},\pi})|\,\,\tau:C^{*}(\mathbb{F}(n,k))\to\mathbb{C}\text{ is a tracial state }\}.

We let
SOS\subseteq\mathbb{C}(\mathbb{F}(n,k))
denote the cone
sums of squares
, i.e., the set of all elements that can be written as a sum of elements of the form
w^{*}w
with
w\in\mathbb{C}(\mathbb{F}(n,k))
. We let

\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{C}(\mathbb{F}(n,k))
denote the space of
commutators
, i.e., the linear span of all elements of the form
wz-zw,\,\,w,z\in\mathbb{C}(\mathbb{F}(n,k))
.

While the above theorem gives lower bounds on the synchronous value, the following theorem is useful for giving upper bounds on the synchronous value. It is closely related to the results on trace positive elements found in
[
7
]
.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(X,X,A,A,V)
be a game and let
\pi
be a density on inputs. Then

\omega^{s}_{qc}(\mathcal{G},\pi)=\inf\{t|\,\,t1-R_{\mathcal{G},\pi}\in SOS+\mathcal{C}\}.

Proof.

Let
t>\omega_{qc}^{s}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
and choose
\epsilon>0
so that
\tau(t1-R_{\mathcal{G},\pi})-\epsilon>0
for every tracial state on
C^{*}(\mathbb{F}(n,k))
. By a theorem of Cuntz and Pedersen
[
4
, Theorem 2.9]
, this implies that
(t-\epsilon)1-R_{\mathcal{G},\pi}
is in the closure of
C^{*}(\mathbb{F}(n,k))^{+}+\mathcal{C}
. From this it follows that we may choose some element
w\in\mathcal{C}
so that
t1-R_{\mathcal{G},\pi}-w\in C^{*}(\mathbb{F}(n,k))^{+}\cap\mathbb{C}(\mathbb{F}(n,k))
. By a theorem of Ozawa
[
12
, Theorem 1]
, this guarantees that
t1-R_{\mathcal{G},\pi}-w\in SOS
. Thus,
t1-R_{\mathcal{G},\pi}\in SOS+\mathcal{C}
.

Similarly, if
t<\omega_{qc}^{s}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
, then by the above theorem, there is a tracial state
\tau
so that

\tau(t1-R_{\mathcal{G},\pi})<0
and this guarantees that
t1-R_{\mathcal{G},\pi}\notin SOS+\mathcal{C}
.

Definition 2.6
.

Given a game
\mathcal{G}=(X,X,A,A,V)
with density
\pi
, we define the
cost matrix
of the game to be the
(X\times A)\times(X\times A)
matrix, denoted
C_{\mathcal{G},\pi}
, with entries,

c_{(x,a),(y,b)}=\frac{1}{2}\big(\pi(x,y)V(x,y,a,b)+\pi(y,x)V(y,x,b,a)\big).

Let
|x,a\rangle\langle y,b|
denote a typical
(X\times A)\times(X\times A)
matrix unit. The set of
cost-free matrices

\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{G})
is the real linear subspace spanned by the symmetric matrices
|x,a\rangle\langle x,b|+|x,b\rangle\langle x,a|
for
x\in X
,
a\neq b\in A
, and the diagonal matrices
D_{x,y}=\sum_{a\in A}|x,a\rangle\langle x,a|-|y,a\rangle\langle y,a|
for
x\neq y\in X
.
We set

H_{\mathcal{G},\pi}=\sum_{a,b,x,y}c_{(x,a),(y,b)}(e_{x,a}e_{y,b}+e_{y,b}e_{x,a}),

viewed as an element of the corresponding group algebra.
Note that
C_{\mathcal{G},\pi}^{t}=C_{\mathcal{G},\pi}
and that
H_{\mathcal{G},\pi}=H_{\mathcal{G},\pi}^{*}
.

Corollary 2.7
.

Given a game
\mathcal{G}=(X,X,A,A,V)
with density
\pi
,

\omega^{s}_{qc}(\mathcal{G},\pi)=\sup\{\tau(H_{\mathcal{G},\pi})\}\leq|X|\inf_{Z\in\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{G})}\lambda_{max}(C_{\mathcal{G},\pi}+Z),

where
\tau
is an arbitrary tracial state and
\lambda_{max}
denotes the maximal eigenvalue.

Proof.

The first equality follows from the fact that
\tau(H_{\mathcal{G},\pi})=\tau(R_{\mathcal{G},\pi})
for any trace.
If we choose some ordering on the set

and let
E
denote the column vector with entries the elements

then for

, we have that

, so

∎

Unique games

For a unique game
\mathcal{G}
, it is known
[
8
]
that if
\mathcal{G}
has a perfect
vect
-density, then
\mathcal{G}
has a perfect
q

q

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(X,Y,A,f)
be a unique game.
If
\mathcal{G}
has a perfect vect-density, then
\mathcal{G}
has a
perfect deterministic strategy. If
X=Y
and
\mathcal{G}
has a perfect synchronous vect-density, then
\mathcal{G}
has a perfect synchronous deterministic strategy.

Proof.

Let
\{v_{x,a}:x\in X,a\in A\}
and
\{w_{y,b}:y\in Y,b\in A\}
be sets of vectors in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
satisfying:

•

v_{x,a}\perp v_{x,a^{\prime}},\forall x\in X,a\neq a^{\prime},

•

w_{y,b}\perp w_{y,b^{\prime}},\forall y\in Y,b\neq b^{\prime}
,

•

\exists\|\eta\|=1
such that
\sum_{a}v_{x,a}=\sum_{b}w_{y,b}=\eta,\forall x,y\in X

such that the density given by

p(a,b|x,y):=\langle v_{x,a}|w_{y,b}\rangle,

is a perfect density for
\mathcal{G}
.

This implies that whenever
\langle v_{x,a}|w_{y,b}\rangle\neq 0
, we have
a=f(x,y)b
.

Fix any
x_{0}\in X
and
y_{0}\in Y
. Choose
b_{0}\in A

such that
w_{y_{0},b_{0}}\neq 0
and define
h_{A}:X\to A
by

h_{A}(x)=f(x,y_{0})b_{0}.

Now set
a_{0}=h_{A}(x_{0})
and define
h_{B}:Y\to A
by

h_{B}(y)=f(x_{0},y)^{-1}a_{0}.

Note that
h_{B}(y_{0})=b_{0}
.
We claim that this pair of functions defines a perfect deterministic strategy.

Since

0\neq\|w_{y_{0},b_{0}}\|^{2}=\langle w_{y_{0},b_{0}}|\eta\rangle=\sum_{a}\langle w_{y_{0},b_{0}}|v_{x,a}\rangle=\langle w_{y_{0},b_{0}}|v_{x,h_{A}(x)}\rangle,

we see that
v_{x,h_{A}(x)}\neq 0,\forall x
and so in particular
v_{x_{0},a_{0}}\neq 0.

We also have that

\|v_{x,h_{A}(x)}\|^{2}=\sum_{b}\langle w_{y_{0},b}|v_{x,h_{A}(x)}\rangle=\langle w_{y_{0},b_{0}}|v_{x,h_{A}(x)}\rangle.

These two equations together imply that
v_{x,h_{A}(x)}=w_{y_{0},b_{0}}
.

Similarly, we have that
w_{y,h_{B}(y)}=v_{x_{0},a_{0}}
. Hence,

p(h_{A}(x),h_{B}(y)|x,y)=\|w_{y_{0},b_{0}}\|^{2}\neq 0,

and since
p
is a perfect density for
\mathcal{G}
, we have
h_{A}(x)=f(x,y)h_{B}(y)
. So the functions
h_{A},h_{B}
define a perfect deterministic strategy. Moreover, if
X=Y
and
p
is synchronous, then it follows immediately that
h_{A}=h_{B}
, and we have a perfect deterministic strategy.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(X,Y,A,f)
be a unique game with
k=|A|\geq 2
. Let

\pi
be a density on inputs such that

\pi_{0}:=\min_{x,y}\pi(x,y)>0

(\mathcal{G},\pi)

\omega_{vect}(\mathcal{G},\pi)<1-\epsilon

Suppose that

Since
\mathcal{G}

Define
h_{A}:X\to A
,
h_{B}:Y\to A
using
(x_{0},y_{0},a_{0},b_{0})

and
|\|v\|^{2}-\langle w,v\rangle|\leq\delta_{2}
, then
\|w-v\|^{2}\leq\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}
.

\|v_{x,h_{A}(x)}-w_{y_{0},b_{0}}\|^{2}\leq\delta_{b_{0}}(x):=\delta_{b_{0}}^{A}(x)+\delta_{b_{0}}^{B}(x).

Similarly,

\|v_{x_{0},a_{0}}-w_{y,h_{B}(y)}\|^{2}\leq\delta_{a_{0}}(y):=\delta_{a_{0}}^{A}(y)+\delta_{a_{0}}^{B}(y)

where

\delta_{a_{0}}^{A}(y)=\sum_{a\neq a_{0}}p(a,h_{B}(y)|x_{0},y)

and

\delta_{a_{0}}^{B}(y)=\sum_{b\neq h_{B}(y)}p(a_{0},b|x_{0},y)
.
So

\displaystyle p(h_{A}(x),h_{B}(y)|x,y)

\displaystyle=\langle v_{x,h_{A}(x)}|w_{y,h_{B}(y)}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle w_{y_{0},b_{0}}+(v_{x,h_{A}(x)}-w_{y_{0},b_{0}})|v_{x_{0},a_{0}}+(w_{y,h_{B}(y)}-v_{x_{0},a_{0}})\rangle

\displaystyle=r+\langle v_{x,h_{A}(x)}-w_{y_{0},b_{0}}|v_{x_{0},a_{0}}\rangle+\langle w_{y_{0},b_{0}}|w_{y,h_{B}(y)}-v_{x_{0},a_{0}}\rangle

\displaystyle\hphantom{{}=r{}}+\langle v_{x,h_{A}(x)}-w_{y_{0},b_{0}}|w_{y,h_{B}(y)}-v_{x_{0},a_{0}}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1-\epsilon}{k}-\gamma(x,y)

\displaystyle\gamma(x,y)

\displaystyle=|\langle v_{x,h_{A}(x)}-w_{y_{0},b_{0}}|v_{x_{0},a_{0}}\rangle|+|\langle w_{y_{0},b_{0}}|w_{y,h_{B}(y)}-v_{x_{0},a_{0}}\rangle|

\displaystyle\hphantom{{}={}}+|\langle v_{x,h_{A}(x)}-w_{y_{0},b_{0}}|w_{y,h_{B}(y)}-v_{x_{0},a_{0}}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{\pi_{0}}}+\frac{\epsilon}{\pi_{0}}

where we have made the fairly crude estimates

\displaystyle\delta_{b_{0}}(x,y_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a,b:V(x,y_{0},a,b)=0}p(a,b|x,y_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\pi(x,y_{0})}\sum_{x,y,a,b:V(x,y,a,b)=0}\pi(x,y)p(a,b|x,y)=\frac{\epsilon}{\pi(x,y_{0})}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{\pi_{0}}

and similarly,
\delta_{a_{0}}(y)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{\pi_{0}}
. This all implies that

p(h_{A}(x),h_{B}(y)|x,y)-\frac{\epsilon}{\pi_{0}}\geq\frac{1}{k}\left(1-\left(\epsilon+2k\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\pi_{0}}+\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{\pi_{0}}}\right)\right)\right)>0

so
\pi_{0}p(h_{A}(x),h_{B}(y)|x,y)>\epsilon
. Hence,
the expected value
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G},\pi}(h_{A},h_{B})
of the deterministic strategy
(h_{A},h_{B})
satisfies

\displaystyle(1-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G},\pi}(h_{A},h_{B}))\epsilon

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y:V(x,y,h_{A}(x),h_{B}(y))=0}\pi(x,y)\epsilon

\displaystyle<\pi_{0}\sum_{x,y:V(x,y,h_{A}(x),h_{B}(y))=0}\pi(x,y)p(h_{A}(x),h_{B}(y)|x,y)

\displaystyle\leq\pi_{0}\sum_{x,y,a,b:V(x,y,a,b)=0}\pi(x,y)p(a,b|x,y)

\displaystyle=\pi_{0}(1-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G},\pi}(p))\leq\pi_{0}\epsilon.

So

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G},\pi}(h_{A},h_{B})>1-\pi_{0}.

Since
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G},\pi}(h_{A},h_{B})
is a sum of values of
\pi
, this implies that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G},\pi}(h_{A},h_{B})=1
, hence

.

A groups analogue of XOR games

Given a finite group
\Omega
and a set
X
, let
f:X\times Y\to\Omega
be a function.
We define a two person game, denoted by
\mathcal{G}=(X,Y,\Omega,f)
, by setting the value function to be

V(x,y,a,b)=1\iff ab^{-1}=f(x,y).

In the case that
X=Y
, we simplify this notation further to
\mathcal{G}=(X,\Omega,f)
.
We refer to any game given as above as a
group based game
.

When
\Omega=(\mathbb{Z}_{2},+)
, then since
a-b=a+b
, this becomes the usual definition of an XOR game.
Note that a deterministic strategy for such a game is a pair of functions
h_{A}:X\to\Omega
and
h_{B}:Y\to\Omega
. Such a strategy is perfect if and only if
h_{A}(x)h_{B}(y)^{-1}=f(x,y),\forall x,y
.

In the case that
X=Y
, the function
f
defines a
synchronous game
if and only if

f(x,x)=e,\forall x
, where
e
denotes the identity of
\Omega
. We call the game
symmetric
provided that
f(y,x)=f(x,y)^{-1},\forall x,y\in X
.

Note that a pair of functions defines a perfect strategy for a synchronous game
(X,\Omega,f)
if and only if
h_{A}=h_{B}=h
with
h(x)h(y)^{-1}=f(x,y),\forall x,y\in X
. Thus, for a synchronous group based game to have a perfect strategy it must also be symmetric.

We also remark that since the groups that we are interested in are finite,
\mathbb{C}(\Omega)=C^{*}(\Omega)
.

Proposition 4.1

(Averaging)
.

We leave the proof to the reader.

5.
Labelling Directed Graphs and Synchronous Games Based on the Cyclic Group of Order 3

In this section we show that symmetric synchronous games based on the cyclic group of order 3 correspond to a certain labelling problem for directed graphs. We first recall the relationship between the cut number of undirected graphs and synchronous XOR games.

Given an undirected simple graph
G=(V,E)
the cut number
\Cut(G)
is the maximal number of (undirected) edges in a two-colorable subgraph of
G
.
We regard the edge set
E
as a subset of
V\times V
, where
(x,y)\in E\iff(y,x)\in E
, and
(x,x)\notin E
, so that

\Cut(G)=\frac{1}{2}\max_{w:V\to\mathbb{Z}_{2}}\big|\{(x,y)\in E:w(x)\neq w(y)\}\big|.

We define
f:V\times V\to\mathbb{Z}_{2}
by

f(x,y)=\begin{cases}1&(x,y)\in E,\\
0&(x,y)\notin E.\end{cases}

The graph can be 2-colored if and only if there is a function
w:V\to\mathbb{Z}_{2}
such that
w(x)-w(y)=1,\forall(x,y)\in E
.
Note that generally, if
(x,y)\notin E
then it is not necessary that
w(x)-w(y)=0
. For example the 6-cycle can be two-colored, but some pairs of non-adjacent vertices will receive opposite colors. This problem is fixed by choice of density on the input set. If we let our density be

\pi_{E}(x,y)=\frac{1}{|E|}\chi_{E}(x,y):=\frac{1}{|E|}\begin{cases}1&(x,y)\in E\\
0&(x,y)\notin E\end{cases},

and let
\mathcal{G}=(V,\mathbb{Z}_{2},f)
denote the corresponding group based game, then

\omega^{s}_{loc}(\mathcal{G},\pi_{E})=\frac{1}{|E|}\max_{w:V\to\mathbb{Z}_{2}}\big|\{(x,y)\in E|w(x)-w(y)=1\}\big|.

Hence,

2\Cut(G)=|E|\omega^{s}_{loc}(\mathcal{G},\pi_{E}).

This was used in
[
6
]
to define a
t
-quantum cut number for
t=q,qc
as

\Cut_{t}(G)=\frac{|E|}{2}\omega^{s}_{t}(\mathcal{G},\pi_{E}).

We now wish to do a similar construction for directed graphs.

By a
directed graph

G=(X,D)
on a vertex set
X
we mean a subset
D\subseteq X\times X
such that
(x,x)\notin D
and
(x,y)\in D\implies(y,x)\notin D
. Such a graph is sometimes called a simple, antisymmetric directed graph, and can be pictured as a collection of arrows from vertex
x
to
y
if and only if
(x,y)\in D
. We let
D^{t}=\{(y,x):(x,y)\in D\}
and set
E=D\cup D^{t}
, so that
(X,E)
is the graph on
X
that one obtains by removing the arrows but keeping the edges.
Let
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
denote the group
\{0,1,2\}
under addition modulo

3
. We can identify a directed graph
G=(X,D)
with a function

f:X\times X\to\mathbb{Z}_{3}\text{ such that }f(x,y)=\begin{cases}0,&(x,y),(y,x)\notin D,\\
2,&(x,y)\in D,\\
1,&(x,y)\in D^{t},\end{cases}

so that
f(x,y)=f(y,x)=0
represents no edge between
x
and
y
.

Conversely, given any function
f:X\times X\to\mathbb{Z}_{3}
with
f(y,x)=-f(x,y)
and
f(x,x)=0
we can associate a directed graph by setting
D=\{(x,y):f(x,y)=1\}
.

In this manner we see that every synchronous, symmetric group based game of the form
\mathcal{G}=(X,\mathbb{Z}_{3},f)
can be identified with a directed graph
G=(X,D)
.

A
vertex 3-labelling
of the directed graph
G
is a function
w:X\to\mathbb{Z}_{3}
such that

w(x)-w(y)=f(x,y),\forall(x,y)\in E.

Equivalently,
w(y)=w(x)+1
for all
(x,y)\in D
, or equivalently,
w(y)=w(x)-1
for all
(x,y)\in D^{t}
.

Thus, every synchronous group based game on
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
is the vertex 3-coloring game of a directed graph, provided we take the density of the game to be supported on
E
.

Given any directed graph on
X
, say

represented by

to be

\displaystyle\DCut(G)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\max_{w:X\to\mathbb{Z}_{3}}|\{(x,y)\in E:w(x)-w(y)=f(x,y)\}|

\displaystyle=\max_{w:X\to\mathbb{Z}_{3}}|\{(x,y)\in D:w(y)=w(x)+1\}|,

i.e., the largest number of directed edges of a vertex

-labelled directed subgraph

.
As above we have that

\DCut(G)=|D|\omega^{s}_{loc}(\mathcal{G},\pi_{E}),

for the corresponding

, i.e., the group based game

It is natural to set

Our next goal is to study these values. Let
\xi=e^{2\pi i/3}

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
(X,D)
be a directed graph and
(\mathcal{G},\pi)
the corresponding directed graph game. Suppose
\{e_{x,a}:x\in X,a\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}\}
are PVMs in a C*-algebra with trace
\tau
. Let
p(a,b|x,y)=\tau(e_{x,a}e_{y,b})
for
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}
and
x,y\in X
, and define unitaries
\{U_{x}:x\in X\}
by setting
U_{x}=e_{x,0}+\xi e_{x,1}+\xi^{2}e_{x,2}
. Then

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G},\pi}(p)=\frac{1}{3}+\frac{2}{3|D|}\sum_{(x,y)\in D}\re\tau(\xi^{2}U_{x}^{*}U_{y}).

Proof.

For
x,y\in X
and
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}
, let

r_{j}(x,y)=\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}}\tau(e_{x,a}e_{y,a+j}).

Then
r_{j}(y,x)=r_{-j}(x,y)
and
\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}}r_{j}(x,y)=1
. Moreover,

\tau(U_{x}^{*}U_{y})=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}}\xi^{j}r_{j}(x,y).

It follows that

3r_{1}(x,y)=1+2\re\tau(\xi^{2}U_{x}^{*}U_{y}).

For
(x,y)\in E
, we have
(x,y,a,b)\in W
if and only if
(x,y)\in D
and
b=a+1
or
(y,x)\in D
and
a=b+1
, so

W\cap(E\times\mathbb{Z}_{3}\times\mathbb{Z}_{3})=\{(x,y,a,a+1),(y,x,a+1,a)\mid(x,y)\in D,\,a\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}\}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G},\pi}(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2|D|}\sum_{(x,y)\in D,\,a\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}}\tau(e_{x,a}e_{y,a+1})+\tau(e_{y,a+1}e_{x,a})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|D|}\sum_{(x,y)\in D,\,a\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}}\tau(e_{x,a}e_{y,a+1})=\frac{1}{|D|}\sum_{(x,y)\in D}r_{1}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}+\frac{2}{3|D|}\sum_{(x,y)\in D}\re\tau(\xi^{2}U_{x}^{*}U_{y}).\qed

The point of the above theorem is that we can replace PVMs by unitaries of order 3 and the synchronous values of the game will be given by taking the supremum over such unitaries in all tracial C*-algebras or over all unitaries in a matrix algebra.

If
(X,D)
is a directed graph and
x,y\in X
, and vertices
(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
form a cycle in the undirected graph
(X,D\cup D^{t})
, then the
net length
of this cycle is defined to be

|\{1\leq j\leq m:(x_{j},x_{j+1})\in D\}|-|\{1\leq j\leq m:(x_{j+1},x_{j})\in D\}|,

where
x_{m+1}:=x_{1}
. If the net length of this cycle is
m
(so
that all of its edges are oriented in the same cyclic direction), it is
said to be a directed
m
-cycle.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
(X,D)
be a directed graph with corresponding game
(\mathcal{G},\pi)
. The following are equivalent:

(a)

(\mathcal{G},\pi)
has a perfect synchronous
qc
-density.

(b)

(\mathcal{G},\pi)
has a perfect synchronous deterministic strategy.

(c)

The net length of every cycle in
(X,D)
is divisible by
3
.

(d)

(\mathcal{G},\pi)
has a perfect deterministic strategy.

In particular, if
(X,D)
contains a directed
k
-cycle, where
3
does not divide
k
, then there is no perfect synchronous
qc
-density for
(\mathcal{G},\pi)
.

Proof.

Any density
p
for
\mathcal{G}
in
C_{qc}^{s}
is of the form
p(a,b|x,y)=\tau(e_{x,a}e_{y,b})
where
e_{x,a}
and
\tau
are as in Theorem
5.1
, so if
p
is perfect for
(\mathcal{G},\pi)
, then the unitaries
\{U_{x}:x\in X\}
satisfy
\tau(\xi^{2}U_{x}^{*}U_{y})=1
and hence
U_{x}^{*}U_{y}=\xi 1
, for all
(x,y)\in D
.
Define an equivalence relation on
X
by
x\sim y
if
U_{x}^{*}U_{y}=\xi^{k}1
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}
, and let
X_{0}
be a complete set of equivalence class representatives. Define
w:X\to\mathbb{Z}_{3}
by the condition
U_{x_{0}}^{*}U_{x}=\xi^{w(x)}1
, where
x\sim x_{0}\in X_{0}
. If
(x,y)\in D
, then
U_{x}^{*}U_{y}=\xi 1
, so
x\sim x_{0}\sim y
for some
x_{0}\in X_{0}
, and

\xi 1=U_{x}^{*}U_{y}=(U_{x_{0}}^{*}U_{x})^{*}(U_{x_{0}}^{*}U_{y})=\xi^{w(y)-w(x)}1,

so
w(y)=w(x)+1
. Hence,
w
defines a perfect synchronous deterministic strategy. So (a) and (b) are equivalent.

A perfect synchronous deterministic strategy is the same thing as a directed graph homomorphism into the directed cycle on three vertices. By
[
2
, Theorem 3.2]
, the existence of such a homomorphism is equivalent to (c).

Condition (b) trivially implies (d) so it only remains to show that (d) implies (c). Given (d), there is a perfect deterministic strategy, i.e., two functions
v,w:X\to\mathbb{Z}_{3}
such that for all
(x,y)\in D
, we have
w(y)=v(x)+1
and
v(y)=w(x)+1
. Suppose
C=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
is a cycle in
D
of net length
\ell
. If
m
is even, then a perfect synchronous deterministic strategy for this cycle is given by
c:\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}\to\mathbb{Z}_{3}
,
c(x_{i})=v(x_{i})
if
i
is even, and
c(x_{i})=w(x_{i})
if
i
is odd, so
\ell
must be divisible by
3
. If
m
is odd, suppose without loss that
(x_{m},x_{1})\in D
. Let
C^{\prime}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m},x_{1}^{\prime},\dots,x_{m}^{\prime})
be the directed cycle with edges

\{(x_{m},x_{1}^{\prime}),(x_{m}^{\prime},x_{1})\}\cup\{(x_{i},x_{j}),(x_{i}^{\prime},x_{j}^{\prime}):1\leq i,j\leq m,\,|i-j|\leq 1\}.

So
C^{\prime}
is obtained by removing the edge

(x_{m},x_{1})
from
C
, duplicating the resulting path, and forming a cycle by adding the edges
(x_{m},x_{1}^{\prime}),(x_{m}^{\prime},x_{1})
. The net length of
C^{\prime}
is then
2\ell
, and
C^{\prime}
has a perfect synchronous deterministic strategy
c:\{x_{i},x_{i}^{\prime}:1\leq i\leq m\}\to\mathbb{Z}_{3}
, given by

c(x_{i})=\begin{cases}v(x_{i})&\text{$i$ even},\\
w(x_{i})&\text{$i$ odd},\end{cases}\qquad c(x_{i}^{\prime})=\begin{cases}w(x_{i})&\text{$i$ even},\\
v(x_{i})&\text{$i$ odd}.\end{cases}

Hence,
3
divides
2\ell
, so
3
divides
\ell
.
∎

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=(\langle v_{x,a}|w_{x,b}\rangle)_{a,b},

s_{0,0}=t_{0,0}=e_{1},\quad s_{x+1,a+1}=R_{\pi/6}s_{x,a},\quad t_{x+1,a+1}=R_{\pi/3}t_{x,a},

and consider the vectors
v_{x,a}\in\mathbb{R}^{5}
,

v_{x,a}=\frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\alpha s_{x,a}\\
\beta t_{x,a}\end{pmatrix}

where
\alpha=\sqrt{2(\sqrt{3}-1)}
and
\beta=\sqrt{2(2-\sqrt{3})}
. Observe that

s_{x,a+1}=R_{\pi/6}^{4}s_{x,a}=R_{2\pi/3}s_{x,a},\quad t_{x,a+1}=R_{\pi/3}^{4}t_{x,a}=R_{4\pi/3}t_{x,a}.

So

\langle v_{x,a}|v_{x,a^{\prime}}\rangle=\tfrac{1}{9}(1+\alpha^{2}\cos(2\pi/3)+\beta^{2}\cos(4\pi/3))=\tfrac{1}{9}(1-\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}))=0

for
a\neq a^{\prime}
. Note that

\sum_{a}s_{x,a}=(I+R_{2\pi/3}+R_{4\pi/3})s_{x,0}=0
and similarly

\sum_{a}t_{x,a}=0
, so
\sum_{a}v_{x,a}=(1,0,0,0,0)^{t}
for every

x
. And one can check by explicit calculation that

p(a,b|x,y):=\langle v_{x,a}|v_{y,b}\rangle
has no negative
entries. So
\{v_{x,a}\}
defines a synchronous vect-density
p(a,b|x,y)
. Its
winning probability for the cycle game is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{4},a\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}}p(a,a+1|x,x+1)=3p(0,1|0,1)

\displaystyle=3\langle v_{0,0}|v_{1,1}\rangle=\tfrac{1}{3}(1+\alpha^{2}\cos(\pi/6)+\beta^{2}\cos(\pi/3))=u^{*}.\qed

6.
Generalizations of CHSH

Let
X=Y=A=B=\mathbb{Z}_{n}
and define
f:\mathbb{Z}_{n}\times\mathbb{Z}_{n}\to\mathbb{Z}_{n}
by
f(x,y)=xy
, then we obtain a non-synchronous, symmetric group based game on the group
(\mathbb{Z}_{n},+)
with rules,

V(x,y,a,b)=1\iff a-b=xy,

which in the case
n=2
is the CHSH game. Note that in the definition of this game we are using that
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is a ring.

A non-synchronous generalization of the CHSH games, denoted
CHSH_{n}
, with
a+b=xy
, were introduced by Buhrman and Massey
[
3
]
and further studied by Bavarian and Shor
[
1
]
, who also introduced the variant with rules

V(x,y,a,b)=1\iff ab=x+y.

Note that if
p(a,b|x,y)\in C_{t}
and we set
q(a,b|x,y)=p(a,-b|x,y)
, then
q\in C_{t}
. For this reason, the non-synchronous values of our version and the game
CHSH_{n}
will be equal. In particular, the synchronous values of our version of the game will give lower bounds on the corresponding value of the non-synchronous game
CHSH_{n}
.

Even in the case
n=3
and the uniform distribution on input pairs, the quantum values of this game are not known exactly. However using the methods developed in the section on directed graphs we obtain exactly the synchronous values of this non-synchronous game.
We begin with an extension to the non-synchronous case of Theorem
5.1
.

Let
\xi=e^{2\pi i/3}
, let
\sigma_{3}=\{1,\xi,\xi^{2}\}
, be the cyclic group of order 3 written multiplicatively, let
X
be a set with
|X|=n
and let

f:X\times X\to\sigma_{3}
, let
\mathbb{F}(n,3)
be the free product of
n
copies of
\sigma_{3}
and let
u_{x},\,x\in X,\,\,u_{x}^{3}=1
be the generators. Let
\mathcal{G}
be the corresponding game and let
\pi
be a density on
X\times X
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
f:X\times X\to\sigma_{3}
be a function, let
\mathcal{G}=(X,\sigma_{3},f)
be the corresponding group based game and let
\pi
be a density on inputs. If we set

H=\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{3}\sum_{x,y}\pi(x,y)[\xi^{f(x,y)}u_{x}^{*}u_{y}+\xi^{-f(x,y)}u_{y}^{*}u_{x}],

then

\omega^{s}_{qc}(\mathcal{G},\pi)=\sup\{\tau(H)\mid\tau:C^{*}(\mathbb{F}(n,3))\to\mathbb{C}\text{ is a tracial state }\}.

Consequently,

\omega^{s}_{qc}(\mathcal{G},\pi)=\inf\{t:t1-H\in SOS+\mathcal{C}\},

in
\mathbb{C}(\mathbb{F}(n,3))
.

Proof.

We have that for any trace
\tau
,

\displaystyle\tau(\xi^{l}u_{x}^{*}u_{y}+\xi^{-l}u_{y}^{*}u_{x})

\displaystyle=2\re\tau(\xi^{l}u_{x}^{*}u_{y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}}2\re\tau(\xi^{l-a+b}e_{x,a}e_{y,b})

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}}2\re(\xi^{c})\sum_{b\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}}\tau(e_{x,b+l-c}e_{y,b})

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}}2\tau(e_{x,b+l}e_{y,b})-\tau((e_{x,b+l-1}+e_{x,b+l-2})e_{y,b})

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}}2\tau(e_{x,b+l}e_{y,b})-\tau((1-e_{x,b+l})e_{y,b})

\displaystyle=-1+3\sum_{b\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}}\tau(e_{x,b+l}e_{y,b}).

Thus, if
p(a,b|x,y)=\tau(e_{x,a}e_{y,b})
we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G},\pi}(p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in X}\pi(x,y)\sum_{b\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}}\tau(e_{x,b+f(x,y)}e_{y,b})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{3}\sum_{x,y\in X}\pi(x,y)\tau(\xi^{f(x,y)}u_{x}^{*}u_{y}+\xi^{-f(x,y)}u_{y}^{*}u_{x})=\tau(H).

So

\omega_{qc}^{s}(\mathcal{G},\pi)=\sup\{\tau(H):\tau\text{ tracial state }\}.\qed

We now return to the generalized CHSH game on the ring
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
f:\mathbb{Z}_{3}\times\mathbb{Z}_{3}\to\mathbb{Z}_{3}
be given by
f(x,y)=xy
, and consider the group based game
\mathcal{G}
with rules,

V(x,y,a,b)=1\iff a-b=xy,

and
\pi
the uniform distribution.
Then

\omega_{det}(\mathcal{G},\pi)=2/3<\omega_{q_{1}}(\mathcal{G},\pi),

and

\omega^{s}_{det}(\mathcal{G},\pi)=\omega^{s}_{qc}(\mathcal{G},\pi)=5/9.

Proof.

It is clear that
\omega_{det}^{s}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
is attained at any constant strategies, which yields the value
5/9
.
Similarly,
\omega_{det}(\mathcal{G},\pi)=2/3
, is attained at (for example)
g,h:\mathbb{Z}_{3}\to\mathbb{Z}_{3}
,
g:0\mapsto 0,1\mapsto 1,2\mapsto 1
and
h=1-g
.

It follows from
[
11
]
(see also
[
10
]
) that
\omega_{q_{1}}(\mathcal{G},\pi)=(\frac{1+2\cos(2\pi/9)}{3})^{2}\approx 0.7124
.

The upper bound for
\omega_{qc}^{s}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
follows from Corollary
2.7
. Let

C=\frac{1}{18}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&0&0&&2&0&0&&2&0&0\\
0&2&0&&0&2&0&&0&2&0\\
0&0&2&&0&0&2&&0&0&2\\[3.0pt]
2&0&0&&0&1&1&&0&1&1\\
0&2&0&&1&0&1&&1&0&1\\
0&0&2&&1&1&0&&1&1&0\\[3.0pt]
2&0&0&&0&1&1&&0&1&1\\
0&2&0&&1&0&1&&1&0&1\\
0&0&2&&1&1&0&&1&1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right),\quad Z=-\frac{1}{27}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&0&0&&0&0&0&&0&0&0\\
0&4&0&&0&0&0&&0&0&0\\
0&0&4&&0&0&0&&0&0&0\\[3.0pt]
0&0&0&&-2&3&3&&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&&3&-2&3&&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&&3&3&-2&&0&0&0\\[3.0pt]
0&0&0&&0&0&0&&-2&3&3\\
0&0&0&&0&0&0&&3&-2&3\\
0&0&0&&0&0&0&&3&3&-2\end{smallmatrix}\right).

Ordering
X\times A=\mathbb{Z}_{3}\times\mathbb{Z}_{3}
lexicographically, we have
C=C_{\mathcal{G},\pi}
and
Z\in\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{G})
, so
\omega_{qc}^{s}(\mathcal{G},\pi)\leq|X|\lambda_{max}(C+Z)=5/9
, as required.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

If we instead take
Z=0
in the previous proof then we only obtain a trivial
upper bound,
\omega_{qc}^{s}(\mathcal{G},\pi)\leq|X|\lambda_{max}(C)=1
. This illustrates the importance of considering the set

\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{G})
in Corollary
2.7
.

7.
The Commutator Game

Let
\Omega
be a finite group. It is natural to study games that measure how far
\Omega
is from a commutative group. To this end, let
X=Y=A=B=\Omega
and consider the group based game obtained by setting
f(x,y)=xyx^{-1}y^{-1}=(xy)(yx)^{-1}
. This defines a synchronous, symmetric
\Omega
-based game. We have a perfect strategy for this game if and only if there is a function
w:\Omega\to\Omega
such that

w(x)w(y)^{-1}=(xy)(yx)^{-1}.

Note that
w(x)
is a perfect strategy if and only if
w(x)w(e)^{-1}
is a perfect strategy, so we may assume that
w(e)=e
.
Hence
setting
y=e
we obtain, that there is a perfect strategy if and only if there is a function
w
satisfying
w(x)=xex^{-1}e^{-1}w(e)=e
. Hence a perfect deterministic strategy exists if and only if

e=w(x)w(y)^{-1}=xyx^{-1}y^{-1},\forall x,y,

which is if and only if
\Omega
is abelian.

So when
\Omega
is non-abelian even the synchronous deterministic value is a potentially interesting measure of the level of non-commutativity.

Let us take the uniform distribution on
\Omega\times\Omega
, again we can assume that
w(e)=e
and for the synchronous value we are trying to maximize the number of times that

w(x)w(y)^{-1}=xyx^{-1}y^{-1}.

If one uses the deterministic strategy,
w(x)=e,\forall x
, then the value of this strategy multiplied by
|\Omega|^{2}
is just a count of the number of pairs of elements that commute, a classically computed number for a group. In fact, the number of elements that commute is a multiple of the number of conjugacy classes in the group, often denoted
k(\Omega)
.

It is natural to expect that
w(x)=e
is the optimal deterministic strategy. But calculations show that this is false!

Consider the case that
\Omega=S_{3}
the group of permutations of 3 elements. This group has 6 elements, and of the
36
pairs of group elements,
18
are the identity. Thus, if we take the deterministic strategy
w(x)=e
, then the expected value is 1/2.

For generators of this group, let us use the cyclic permutation and the flip:

g:1\to 2\to 3\to 1,\,\,f:1\to 1,2\to 3\to 2.

Consider the deterministic strategy given by:

w(e)=w(f)=w(fg)=w(fg^{2})=e,\,\,w(g)=g^{2},\,\,w(g^{2})=g.

Then the number of times that
w(x)w(y)^{-1}=xyx^{-1}y^{-1}
is 24.
An exhaustive computer search shows that this is an optimal deterministic
strategy.

For a general finite group, we have no idea how to find the function
w
that maximizes the cardinality of the set

\{(x,y):w(x)w(y)^{-1}=xyx^{-1}y^{-1},\,\,x,y\in\Omega\},

or what this number means as a parameter of the group.

A similar problem exists if instead of
f(x,y)=xyx^{-1}y^{-1}
one considers the game defined by
f(x,y)=yx
.

Once one is able to compute this value for groups, it would be interesting to see if a quantum advantage exists for this game and attempt to find an interpretation of this “quantum” count of commutators.

So we end with some questions.

Problem 7.1
.

Given a finite group,
\Omega
, can one identify
|\Omega|^{2}\omega_{det}^{s}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
in group-theoretic terms? Are there groups that exhibit a quantum commutativity advantage, i.e., for which
\omega^{s}_{qc}(\mathcal{G},\pi)>\omega^{s}_{det}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
?
