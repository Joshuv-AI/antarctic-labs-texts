Exploration of multidimensional associations between craniomaxillofacial and dental arch structures
Authors: Shan Dong, Ailin Xu, Xinyi Zhang, Song Li, Xiaoxia Che
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe developmental patterns of craniomaxillofacial structures and dental arch structure represent a central concern in the fields of orthodontics and craniofacial surgery. Understanding the associations between these anatomical features is crucial for accurate orthodontic diagnosis and effective treatment planning. This study aimed to explore the associations between craniomaxillofacial structures and dental arch structures, with the goal of enhancing diagnostic precision and facilitating the shift from experience-based to evidence-based clinical practice in orthodontics.MethodsThe study analyzed 77 craniomaxillofacial and dental arch variables collected from 500 patients using multivariate statistical methods, including descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, and canonical correlation analysis.ResultsCorrelation analysis revealed significant associations between dental arch measurements and multiple craniomaxillofacial measurements, including facial height measurements (such as chin height), facial width measurements (such as minimum frontal width), and mandibular body length. Canonical correlation analysis identified five pairs of canonical variates, indicating a coordinated pattern of vertical development between craniomaxillofacial structures and dental arch structures. Facial width, mandibular plane angle, and mandibular measurements were found to influence both the maxillary and mandibular dental arches and basal arches.ConclusionsSignificant correlations were identified between craniomaxillofacial structure and dental arch structure. Facial width, facial height, and mandibular body length were found to have strong effects on dental arch structure, particularly in the maxillary region (notably at the first molar). The craniomaxillofacial-dental arch system constitutes a complex, multilevel interactive network, suggesting that comprehensive orthodontic assessment should consider the relationships among these anatomical systems.Keywords: Craniomaxillofacial structure, Dental arch structure, Canonical correlation analysis

Background

Abnormal development of craniomaxillofacial structures—specifically in jaw size, shape, and the positional relationship between the maxilla and mandible—is commonly associated with malocclusion and facial irregularities [1]. As fundamental elements of the masticatory system, the morphological development of both craniomaxillofacial and dental structures remains a central research focus in orthodontics and orthognathic treatment [2, 3]. Individuals with malocclusion often pursue orthodontic treatment to achieve an improved facial profile and a more attractive smile, characterized by harmonious dental arch morphology and ideal overjet and overbite [4, 5]. Orthodontic and orthognathic treatment typically improve the relationship between occlusion and the jaws, thereby harmonizing facial aesthetics and function.

Across different sagittal skeletal patterns, the influence of vertical skeletal pattern on dental arch structure remains consistent: as the mandibular plane angle increases, dental arch width decreases [6–10], indicating that dental arch dimensions are influenced by vertical jaw relationships. In recent decades, researchers have found that the balance between facial and dental arch width, especially maxillary arch width, is important for judging smile attractiveness [11, 12]. Evidence indicates that bizygomatic width, intercanthal width, facial height relate to the width of the front dental arch [13–16]. As the skeletal framework of the face, its morphological characteristics directly influence dental arch dimensions and overall facial esthetics [17, 18]. In the modern era of function-driven and esthetics-centered care, understanding how craniomaxillofacial structures relate to dental arch structures is key to diagnosis and treatment in orthodontics.

Although several studies have explored the correlation between craniomaxillofacial structures and dental arch structures, traditional methods primarily rely on univariate correlation or regression analyses. While these methods can reveal only linear relationships between individual variables, they fail to capture the complex multivariate associations among these structures [19, 20]. No study has yet provided a thorough analysis of their underlying relationships.

When dealing with large sample sizes and high-dimensional variable characteristics, more efficient, rapid, and accurate analytical approaches are required. Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) stands out as a classical multivariate statistical method, first introduced by Hotelling in 1936 [21], designed to explore and quantify latent association structures between two sets of variables without reliance on label information. Like cluster analysis and dimensionality reduction techniques (e.g., principal component analysis, PCA), CCA extracts canonical variable (CV) pairs that capture the maximally correlated dimensions between variable sets, thereby providing more comprehensive and insightful information. CCA effectively mitigates the effects of multicollinearity and reveals underlying deep association patterns among variables [22], establishing itself as a standard approach for evaluating multivariable relationships in medical research-including genomics, neuroimaging, psychiatry, and behavioral science [23–26].

Investigations of craniomaxillofacial structures and dental arch structure have traditionally utilized two-dimensional planar imaging, such as lateral cephalograms, which are susceptible to image distortion resulting from improper head positioning and radiographic magnification [1]. Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) enables a more comprehensive evaluation of craniomaxillofacial structures [27], with reduced cost and radiation exposure compared to conventional computed tomography(CT). CBCT can be used for the reconstruction of three-dimensional (3D) craniofacial models for precise measurement of craniomaxillofacial structures and dental arch structures [1]. Therefore, the study employed three-dimensional measurements and CCA to quantitatively dissect the complex association mechanisms between dental arch structure parameters (width, length) and craniomaxillofacial parameters (vertical height, transverse width, etc.) from a multidimensional perspective. This investigation aims to provide a scientific foundation for clinical orthodontic diagnosis and treatment planning, offering valuable insights for advancing precision and personalized orthodontics, thereby accelerating the transition of orthodontics from an empirical discipline to an evidence-based science.

Methods

Study design, participants, and eligibility criteria

This retrospective study included 500 participants selected from patients with malocclusion who visited the department of orthodontics between June 2015 and June 2025. The study protocol received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. All clinical and radiographic data were systematically extracted from the electronic medical record system and anonymized to safeguard patient privacy.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) Aged 12–50 years old, regardless of gender; (2) Cervical vertebral maturation (CVM) stages CS5 or CS6 at the initial visit (to minimize age-related variation); (3) Permanent dentition, with full eruption of all teeth except third molars,with mild crowding or mild spacing, and teeth generally well-aligned within the dental arch; (4) Availability of complete pre-treatment CBCT imaging records.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) History of orthodontic treatment; (2) History of maxillofacial trauma; (3) History of cysts or tumors; (4) History of craniofacial surgery; (5) History of cleft lip and palate, craniofacial defect syndrome, or skeletal malformation; (6) Presence of retained deciduous teeth, supernumerary teeth, and impacted teeth; (7) Presence of systemic disease; (8) Presence of mental or intellectual disability.

Imaging, 3D analysis, and measurements

CBCT scans were acquired for all patients using the NewTom VGi system (AFP Imaging,Verona, Italy). Scans were performed using the standard acquisition mode with the following parameters: tube voltage 110 kV, tube current of 1–20 mA (automatic exposure control), field of view of 15 cm × 15 cm, and voxel size 0.25 mm. During image acquisition, the X-ray tube rotated 360° around the patient, with an approximate exposure time of 3.6 s. Image reconstruction was completed in approximately 1 min. All CBCT datasets were processed using NNT Viewer (v5.6.0.0) and exported in DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine) format, with three-dimensional reconstruction performed using 3D Slicer.

Measurements were performed using 3-matic software (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium), a module integrated within Mimics software (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium). Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, supplemented by a combined evaluation of skewness and kurtosis. Given that the Shapiro–Wilk test exhibits excessive sensitivity to minor deviations from normality in large samples (n = 500), the study primarily employed skewness and kurtosis as criteria for determining approximate normal distribution. Specifically, variables with |skewness|< 2 and |kurtosis|< 7 were classified as approximately normally distributed; otherwise, they were classified as non-normally distributed [28]. Outliers were identified using the Z-score method (|Z|> 3). Missing values were imputed using mean or mode imputation. A 95% confidence interval (CI) was used to assess estimation precision, and statistical power analysis was conducted within a two-sided t-test framework (α = 0.05, power = 0.8).

Correlation analysis

Pearson correlation coefficient was used for normally or approximately normally distributed variables; Spearman rank correlation was applied if either variable was non-normally distributed. Both methods were applied to all variable pairs to enhance robustness verification, with statistically significant variable combinations selected for further analysis. All results were subject to Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction (FDR threshold = 0.05) and Bonferroni correction (threshold = 0.05) to control for type I error from multiple comparisons.

Bootstrap resampling analysis

To evaluate the stability and precision of canonical correlation estimates, a non-parametric bootstrap resampling procedure was employed [30]. For each bootstrap iteration, a sample of equal size to the original dataset (n = 500) was drawn with replacement from the original observations. The canonical correlation analysis was then re-fitted to each bootstrap sample, and canonical correlations were recalculated for all extracted variate pairs. This process was repeated for B = 1,000 bootstrap replicates (random seed = 42 for reproducibility), yielding an empirical sampling distribution for each canonical correlation coefficient.

Point estimates for canonical correlations were derived from the original sample, while 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were constructed using the percentile method (2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution) [30]. Additionally, bootstrap bias was calculated as the difference between the bootstrap mean and the original sample estimate to assess potential estimation bias. All CIs exclude zero, confirming stable canonical correlations.

All measurements were performed independently by two trained investigators with 6 years of clinical experience. Prior to the formal study, 50 patients (approximately 10% of the total cohort) were randomly selected for a pilot measurement to assess inter-investigator agreement. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated for key continuous variables. Discrepancies were resolved by consultation with a senior orthodontist with 30 years of clinical experience. All statistical analyses and plotting were conducted using Python 3.9.

Results

Measurement reliability verification

Fifty patients were randomly selected for reliability assessment of the key continuous variables measured by the two investigators. Wft reliability analysis demonstrated high agreement for continuous variables (ICC (2,1) = 0.986, 95% CI [0.98, 0.99], p < 0.001). The relative precision for each sample (CI width/standard deviation ≈ 0.176) was calculated. Regarding sample size adequacy, the minimum detectable effect size (Cohen's d) was 0.1255, and both assessments indicated that the study requirements were satisfied.

Descriptive analysis

Descriptive statistics of the 500 enrolled subjects confirmed complete data with no missing values for all variables. A total of 77 measurement variables were collected: 29 craniomaxillofacial variables, encompassing transverse measurements (e.g., frontal bone width, facial width), vertical height measurements (e.g., anterior facial height), and angular measurements (e.g., SNA); and 48 dental arch variables, including arch width, length, and height. The distributional characteristics, central tendency, and dispersion for each variable are illustrated in Fig. 1. Among the 500 enrolled patients, the distribution of Angle’s classification was as follows: Class I accounted for 26.8% (n = 134), Class II for 44% (n = 220), and Class III for 29.2% (n = 146).

Normality assessment revealed that among the 77 measurement variables, 19 (24.7%) satisfied the Shapiro–Wilk normality test (P > 0.05), while 58 (75.3%) did not meet normality criteria (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2). However, considering that the Shapiro–Wilk test exhibits excessive sensitivity to minor deviations from normality in large samples (n = 500), the comprehensive evaluation based on skewness and kurtosis indicators indicated that 76 (98.7%) variables exhibited approximately normal distribution, permitting parametric statistical methods for subsequent analyses; the remaining non-normally distributed variables were addressed using non-parametric methods or data transformation techniques.

The HU series variables (HU4: 0.84, HU5: 0.82, HU3: 0.81, HU6: 0.78) followed closely, jointly constituting the primary drivers of CV1. Mandibular length measures, including MRL (0.56, 0.53) and CUL (0.56, 0.52), also showed moderate positive loadings. Beyond height variables, facial width measures such as Wzy (0.57), Wzp (0.56), Wzm (0.54), and maxillary basal arch widths WUA2-WUA6 (0.30–0.45) also contributed positively to CV1, as shown in Fig. 6A.

CV2 Loading Pattern

CV2 exhibited a characteristic bipolar loading pattern. The positive loading side was dominated by the craniomaxillofacial angular variables FMA (0.812) and MP-FH (0.804, with MP-FH predominating), constituting the structural core of the positive axis of CV2. Among dental arch variables, HL6 (−0.43), WUA2 (−0.334), and WUA3 (−0.339) exhibited significant negative loadings (Fig. 6B).

CV3 Loading Pattern

CV3 was characterized by a strong negative loading from ANB (−0.72) at the negative loading sid, countered by a prominent positive loading from SNB (0.66) at the positive side. Mandibular body length variables, including MUL (0.58, 0.60) and MBL (0.53, 0.55), exhibited moderate positive loadings. Dental arch variables WU4-WU6 (0.47–0.54), WLA2-WLA6 (0.41–0.55) and WUA4 (0.50) also displayed significant positive loadings (Fig. 6C).

CV4 Loading Pattern

CV4 loading pattern was comparatively diffuse. The positive loading side was primarily driven by craniomaxillofacial variables GW (0.50) and Wzp (0.36), and dental arch variables WUA2 (0.52) and WL6 (0.35). Numerous variables exhibited loadings approaching zero (e.g., HL3, LU2, WLA3), suggesting that CV4 had relatively limited explanatory power (Fig. 6D).

CV5 Loading Pattern

CV5 demonstrated a pattern predominantly driven by dental arch variables. On the positive loading side, the WUA series variables demonstrated dominance (WUA5, 0.502; LUA5, 0.49; WUA4, 0.48; LUA6, 0.47; WUA6, 0.46; WUA3, 0.42). Among craniomaxillofacial variables, N-Ba (0.45), S–N (0.45), and maxillary length (ANS-PNS, 0.44) exhibited substantial positive loadings. The negative loading side was primarily represented by FMA (−0.14) and MP-FH (−0.14) (Fig. 6E).

Bootstrap validation of canonical correlations

Bootstrap analysis with 1000 resampling iterations demonstrated high stability of all canonical correlation estimates (Table 7). The 95% confidence intervals were narrow across all canonical variates (CI width: 0.022–0.076), with the first canonical variate showing the tightest CI [0.938, 0.960]. Notably, bootstrap bias was negligible for all canonical variates (< 0.001), suggesting that the original sample estimates were unbiased. All 95% CIs excluded zero, providing strong evidence for the stability and statistical significance of the canonical correlations. These findings are particularly important given the high-dimensional nature of our analysis (77 variables across two variable sets). In such contexts, asymptotic statistical methods may yield inflated Type I error rates or unreliable confidence intervals. The bootstrap approach employed here provides a distribution-free alternative that yielded consistent results across all five canonical variate pairs, strengthening the validity of our conclusions regarding the associations between craniomaxillofacial and dental arch structures.

Bias is defined as the difference between the bootstrap mean and the original sample estimate.

The bootstrap distributions for each canonical correlation exhibited approximately normal shapes (Fig. 7), further validating the robustness of the point estimates. Discussion

The study collected a complete dataset with no missing values across variables, indicating strong data quality. In reviewing the sample, 500 subjects (α = 0.05, power = 0.8) were sufficient to detect small effects (Cohen’s d = 0.1255), indicating the sample size was large enough to yield meaningful results. Each variable had good precision (95% CI width/standard deviation ≈ 0.176). These results show the sample mean estimates are reliable. Among 1392 correlation tests, 977 variable pairs showed significant associations in the unadjusted analysis. After Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR) correction, 947 significant pairs remained, whereas Bonferroni correction retained 652 significant pairs. Notably, the 325 excluded pairs generally showed low effect sizes (mean |r|= 0.134), indicating weak inherent association strengths insufficient to support clinical decision-making. Furthermore, Pearson and Spearman correlation analyses yielded fully consistent ranking of association strength, confirming robust linear and monotonic relationships in the dataset.

The correlation analysis revealed that chin height and facial height demonstrated strong correlations with both maxillary and mandibular arch heights, with facial height showing particularly strong associations with maxillary arch height. Furthermore, maxillary and mandibular molar heights exhibited strong correlations with mandibular measurements-the condylar process and mandibular ramus influenced maxillary molar height, while the gonial angle and mandibular ramus affected mandibular molar height. These indicate vertical coordination between craniomaxillofacial structures and dental arch structure during growth, indicating that both facial height and mandibular development influence dental arch dimensions.

Facial width variables showed moderate correlations with maxillary and mandibular basal arch widths, maxillary posterior dental arch widths, and mandibular first molar width, with the fore face width and middle face width showing particularly significant correlations. Additionally, mandibular parameters—predominantly mandibular body length—were correlated with dental arch width measures. These results highlight the importance of anterior facial width, midfacial width, and mandibular body length in determining dental arch structure.Although previous studies [31] have reported weak relationships, the role of mandibular factors—including muscle strength and bite force—should not be overlooked [3].

The maxillary first molar region emerged as clinically important: maxillary first molar basal arch width showed strong correlations with facial width and moderate correlations with facial height, consistent with previous reports [32]. In contrast, descriptions of correlations between mandibular dental arch width and other variables are relatively sparse in the existing literature. In the mandible, only the first molar width showed moderate correlations with facial and mandibular dimensions. Angular measurement variables (SNA, SNB, ANB, FMA, and mandibular plane angle) exhibited generally weak correlations with dental arch structure variables (mean r < 0.11). Additionally, craniomaxillofacial indicators showed generally weak correlations with dental arch length variables, and facial width demonstrated generally weak correlations with mandibular dental arch width.

CCA revealed statistically significant combined effects for the first two canonical variates (P < 0.001). Although later canonical variates did not reach significance in chi-square testing due to high degrees of freedom, permutation testing and F-testing confirmed significance for all five canonical variates (all P < 0.001), indicating that each canonical variate reflected the associations between craniomaxillofacial structures and dental arch morphology to varying degrees.

To ensure the robustness of our canonical correlation analysis in a high-dimensional setting (77 variables across two variable sets), we employed bootstrap resampling as a non-parametric validation approach. The bootstrap results demonstrated excellent stability for all canonical variates, with narrow 95% confidence intervals (range: 0.022–0.076) and negligible bias (< 0.001 for all CVs).The consistent findings across bootstrap resampling strengthen our confidence in the multivariate association patterns identified in this study.

CV1 reflected vertical growth coordination, with moderate positive correlations between facial width and maxillary basal arch width, consistent with univariate correlation findings.

CV2 primarily manifested as negative correlations between FMA and MP-FH with the anterior maxillary basal arch, consistent with the findings of Wei et al. [33]. However, the present study found that these angular measurements showed stronger associations with the maxilla than with the mandible, corroborating the findings of Ning et al. and Aphale et al. [34, 35].

CV3 revealed that larger SNB, smaller ANB, and longer mandibular body were associated with wider maxillary posterior dental arch and mandibular basal arch. CV4 showed positive associations between gonion width, anterior facial width, anterior maxillary basal arch width, and mandibular first molar width. CV5 demonstrated moderate correlations between cranial base length, maxillary length, and mid-posterior maxillary basal arch width.

The correlation analysis and CCA findings together show that different combinations of craniomaxillofacial variables affect specific dental arch regions (anterior, middle, and posterior) in distinct ways. While angular measurements demonstrated no influence on dental arch structure in the correlation analysis, the combined craniomaxillofacial effects in CCA exhibited significant associations with dental arch structure. These findings indicate that craniomaxillofacial-dental arch morphology involves a multi-level, multi-faceted complex interaction network. Relying only on correlation analysis may overlook the effects of combined variables. Furthermore, the results showed that craniomaxillofacial structures have broader and more significant effects on the maxilla, suggesting that the transverse development of the maxillary basal arch may be a key factor influencing craniomaxillofacial vertical and sagittal development.

In the present study, several methodological considerations regarding generalizability should be acknowledged.

First, all participants were orthodontic patients with pretreatment CBCT records rather than population‑based healthy volunteers. However, because we included only patients with mild crowding or spacing, their dental arch forms are closer to the physiological range observed in non‑orthodontic populations. Therefore, the correlational patterns identified may provide a reasonable approximation for normal craniofacial–dental arch associations.

Second, while these reference patterns can, in principle, serve as a normative guide for orthodontic treatment planning—for example, providing target arch form references for patients with severe crowding, impacted teeth, or complex craniofacial deformities—it is important to recognize that dentoalveolar compensation may substantially alter these associations in more complex cases. Thus, the reference patterns should be applied with clinical judgment and individualized adjustment.

Third, although sex and skeletal patterns may affect the absolute dimension, our pooled analysis was designed to capture generalizable correlational patterns; therefore, we did not perform subgroup analyses.

Future studies with larger sample sizes—particularly in general populations and in patients with severe dentoalveolar compensation—are needed to further validate the applicability of these findings and to explore deeper relationships through stratified or interaction analyses.

Fourth, it is worth noting that this cross-sectional study only determines the correlation, and cannot establish causality or predict individual treatment response. Later, we are conducting longitudinal research to make it suitable for clinical prediction tools.

Conclusions

Craniomaxillofacial structures and dental arch structure demonstrated coordinated development in the vertical dimension. Mandibular components (condylar, mandibular ramus and gonial angle) were all correlated with dental arch height. Facial width (fore face width, middle face width) and mandibular body length exhibited significant influences on dental arch and basal arch width.

Combinations of craniofacial variables show region-specific associations with the dental arch, including facial width, mandibular plane angle, SNB, maxillary length and mandibular body length. Craniomaxillofacial structures exert stronger effects on the maxillary dental arch and basal arch, suggesting that maxillary dental and basal arch development (especially at the first molar) may be a critical predictor of overall craniofacial morphology.

The relationship between craniomaxillofacial structures and dental arch structure represents a complex, multi-level interactive network. In clinical practice, the craniofacial skeleton and dental arch should be analyzed and treatment planned as an integrated unit rather than independent components. These findings provide a foundation for future research on craniofacial and dental arch interactions and may provide a scientific reference for personalized, precision orthodontics.
