The arithmetic rank of the residual intersections of a complete intersection ideal
Manav Batavia, Kesavan Mohana Sundaram, Taylor Murray, Vaibhav Pandey

Abstract
. The arithmetic rank of an ideal in a polynomial ring over an algebraically closed field is the smallest number of equations needed to define its vanishing locus set-theoretically. We determine the arithmetic rank of the generic m m -residual intersection of an ideal generated by n n indeterminates for all m ≥ n m\geq n and in every characteristic. We further give an explicit description of its set-theoretic generators. Our main result provides a sharp upper bound for the arithmetic rank of any residual intersection of a complete intersection ideal in any Noetherian local ring. In particular, given a complete intersection ideal of height at least two, any of its generic residual intersections—including its generic link—fails to be a set-theoretic complete intersection in characteristic zero.

1.
Introduction

Let
I
be an ideal in a Noetherian ring
R
. The
arithmetic rank
of
I
is the smallest number of elements needed to generate it up to radical

\operatorname{ara}(I)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\min\{k:\text{there exist }f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\text{ in }R\text{ with}\sqrt{f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}}=\sqrt{I}\}.

In particular, if
R
is a polynomial ring over an algebraically closed field,
\operatorname{ara}(I)
is the optimal number of equations needed to define the variety
V(I)=V(\sqrt{I})
. The simplicity of the definition of arithmetic rank belies the difficulty in computing it, see
[
CN78
,
Har79
]
. A part of this difficulty is that the set-theoretic generators may bear little resemblance to the generators of the given ideal
[
LSW16
, Example 8.2]
. In addition, the arithmetic rank may depend on the characteristic of the ring
[
Bar95
,
BL05
]
.

In this paper, we study the arithmetic rank of the
residual intersections
of a complete intersection ideal. The notion of residual intersections, introduced by Artin and Nagata
[
AN72
]
, is the higher codimension analogue of
links
of ideals
[
PS74
]
. In a different direction, the residual intersections of complete intersections arise as the defining ideals of certain
varieties of complexes
, introduced by Buchsbaum and Eisenbud
[
BE75
]
(see
[
HU85
, Example 5.15]
). We now describe our results, and the objects involved, more precisely.

We often call an ideal generated by a regular sequence a complete intersection ideal. Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
be a regular sequence in a Noetherian local ring
(R,\mathfrak{m})
and
I\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(\underline{f})
. Let
\mathfrak{a}\subsetneq I
be an ideal generated by
m
elements with
m\geq n
and set
J=\mathfrak{a}:I
. If
\operatorname{ht}(J)\geq m
, then
J
is called an
m
-residual intersection of the complete intersection ideal
I
.

Since the ideal
\mathfrak{a}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})
is contained in
I
, there exist elements
t_{ij}
in
R
such that

\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}\\
\vdots\\
a_{m}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}t_{11}&t_{12}&\cdots&t_{1n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
t_{m1}&t_{m2}&\cdots&t_{mn}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}f_{1}\\
\vdots\\
f_{n}\end{pmatrix}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}T\underline{f}.

The generators of such residual intersections were calculated by Huneke and Ulrich in
[
HU88
, Example 3.4]
:

J=I_{n}(T)+(T\underline{f}),

where
I_{n}(T)
is the ideal generated by the size
n
-minors of the matrix
T
. The task at hand is to bound the arithmetic rank
\operatorname{ara}(J)
for
any
such choice of
T
. In order to do this, we work with generic residual intersections.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\underline{f}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
be a regular sequence in a Noetherian local ring
(R,\mathfrak{m})
and
I\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(\underline{f})
. Let
X
be an
m\times n
matrix of indeterminates with
m\geq n
. Let
\mathfrak{a}
be the ideal generated by the entries of the matrix
X[f_{1}\dots f_{n}]^{T}
. We set

\RI(m,I)=\RI(m,\underline{f})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathfrak{a}R[X]:IR[X].

The ideal
\RI(m,I)
is called the
generic
m
-residual intersection
of
I
. It is independent of the choice of the generating set of
I

[
HU90
, Lemma 2.2]
.

Furthermore, we replace the regular sequence
\underline{f}
by indeterminates
\underline{y}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
and focus on the residual intersection ideal
\RI(m,\underline{y})
. Once the arithmetic rank of
\RI(m,\underline{y})
is determined, we get a sharp upper bound on the arithmetic rank of
any
residual intersection of a complete intersection ideal using
[
HU88
, Example 3.4]
, since the arithmetic rank can only go down under specialization.

Indeed, we determine the arithmetic rank of the generic residual intersection
\RI(m,\underline{y})
for all
m\geq n
and in every characteristic.

Main Theorem
.

(Theorem
4.7
)
Let
m
and
n
be positive integers with
m\geq n
and let
K
be any field or the integers. Let
X
and
\underline{y}
be
m\times n
and
n\times 1
matrices of indeterminates respectively over
K
. The arithmetic rank of the generic
m
-residual intersection ideal
\RI(m,\underline{y})=I_{n}(X)+(X\underline{y})
in the polynomial ring
K[X,\underline{y}]
is

\mathrm{ara}(\RI(m,\underline{y}))=\begin{cases}n(m-n+1)+1&n\geqslant 2,\\
m&n=1.\end{cases}

As indicated above, an important consequence of this result is that it immediately gives a sharp upper bound for the arithmetic rank of
any
residual intersection of a complete intersection ideal in any Noetherian local ring, see Theorem
4.9
. Furthermore, via a faithfully flat base change, we also show that the arithmetic rank of the generic
m
-residual intersection of any complete intersection ideal in characteristic zero is as asserted in the Main Theorem. In particular, any such ideal is
not
a set-theoretic complete intersection.

We now lay out our strategy for computing the arithmetic rank of
\RI(m,\underline{y})
.

(1)

In Section
2
, we compute the arithmetic rank of
\RI(m,\underline{y})
in characteristic zero. This is accomplished by identifying an intriguing connection with classical invariant theory and using it to deduce a local cohomology obstruction on the arithmetic rank as in
[
JPSW25
, Theorem 1.1]
.

(2)

In Section
3
, we determine the set-theoretic generators of
\RI(m,\underline{y})
. This is done by endowing the invariant ring of Section
2
with the structure of an Algebra with a Straightening Law (ASL) in any characteristic.

Given the important role it plays in the proof of our Main Theorem, we examine the above invariant/auxiliary ring more closely in Section
3
. We show that it is a Gorenstein factorial domain. In positive characteristic, we prove that it is
F
-regular. The key insight is to initialize the straightening relations of the auxiliary ring to show that its initial subalgebra is also an ASL which, crucially, is normal. Once this is done, the singularity can be deformed back to the auxiliary ring.

For the interested reader, in Appendix
A
, we discuss an alternative approach to bounding the arithmetic rank of
\RI(m,\underline{y})
from above which circumvents the ASL machinery. We construct a transcendence basis for the field of fractions of the auxiliary ring from first principles. This recovers the characteristic-free upper bound for the arithmetic rank of
\RI(m,\underline{y})
.

(3)

In positive characteristic, the local cohomology obstruction to arithmetic rank, mentioned in item (1), vanishes. In Section
4
, we shift gears and study the topology of the open complement of the variety of
\RI(m,\underline{y})
. The rough idea is to show that this open set
cannot
be covered by a ‘small’ number of basic affine open sets. Indeed, we show that singular cohomology recovers the lower bound enforced by local cohomology in characteristic zero. Importantly, with some effort, the topological arguments extend analogously to positive characteristic as well to give an étale cohomology obstruction, thereby finishing the proof.

Observe that since the arithmetic rank is subadditive, we a priori have the upper bound

\operatorname{ara}(\RI(m,\underline{y}))\leq\operatorname{ara}(I_{n}(X))+\operatorname{ara}(X\underline{y})=(mn-n^{2}+1)+m

using
[
BS90
]
. Our main result is that, independent of characteristic,

\operatorname{ara}(\RI(m,\underline{y}))=mn-n^{2}+1+n,

which is better than the above upper bound exactly by the difference
m-n
of the codimensions of the residual intersection
\RI(m,\underline{y})
and the complete intersection ideal
(\underline{y})
.

At this point, it may be useful to remark that one always has a sharp lower bound for the arithmetic ranks of all such ideals. Since the arithmetic rank of an ideal is bounded below by its height, for any
m
-residual intersection ideal
J
of a complete intersection, we have

\operatorname{ara}(J)\geq m.

Note that this lower bound is sharp, i.e., the ideal
J
may be a set-theoretic complete intersection. Consider the case when
m
equals
n
. Then
J
is a link of the complete intersection ideal in question. One possible choice of the link is the ideal itself. In other words, any complete intersection ideal
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
is
self-linked
:

(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n-1},f_{n})=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n-1},f_{n}^{2}):(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n-1},f_{n}).

2.
A curious connection with classical invariant theory

The aim of this section is to compute the arithmetic rank of the generic
m
-residual intersection
\RI(m,\underline{y})
of the complete intersection ideal
(\underline{y})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
in characteristic zero for every
m\geq n
. This will be done by identifying an amusing connection with classical invariant theory.

Let
m\geq n
be positive integers. Let
X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(x_{ij})
and
\underline{y}=(y_{i})
be
m\times n
and
n\times 1
matrices of indeterminates respectively over a field
K
. Consider the linear action of the special linear group
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(K)
on the polynomial ring
S\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}K[X,\underline{y}]
as follows:

M\colon\begin{cases}X&\longmapsto XM^{-1}\\
\underline{y}&\longmapsto M\underline{y}.\end{cases}

Observe that this action is given by the regular representation of
\operatorname{SL}_{n}
and by copies of its dual. Note that each element of the set

B\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{\text{all entries of }\;X\underline{y}\;\text{and all size }n\text{-minors of }\;X\}

is fixed under this action. When
K
is infinite, the
First Fundamental Theorem
for
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(K)
states that the invariant ring is precisely the
K
-algebra generated by this set, i.e.,
S^{\operatorname{SL}_{n}(K)}=K[B]
, see
[
DCP76
, Theorem 3.3]
or
[
DK02
, Theorem 4.4.4]
.

The
key observation
of this section is that when the field
K
is infinite, the generic
m
-residual intersection
\RI(m,\underline{y})
arises as the
nullcone ideal

\mathfrak{m}_{S^{\operatorname{SL}_{n}(K)}}S
of the above action, where
\mathfrak{m}_{S^{\operatorname{SL}_{n}(K)}}
is the homogeneous maximal ideal of the invariant ring
S^{\operatorname{SL}_{n}(K)}
. In other words,

\RI(m,\underline{y})=\mathfrak{m}_{S^{\operatorname{SL}_{n}(K)}}S

is the ideal generated by of all positive degree invariants in
S
. Having made this observation, the computation of the arithmetic rank follows rather elegantly from
[
JPSW25
, Theorem 1.1]
. We write down the proof for the convenience of the reader.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
K
be a field of characteristic zero. Then

\operatorname{ara}(\RI(m,\underline{y}))=\dim S^{\operatorname{SL}_{n}(K)}.

Proof.

Set
T\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}S^{\operatorname{SL}_{n}(K)}
and
d\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\dim T
. Any homogeneous system of parameters of the invariant ring
T
generates its homogeneous maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}_{T}
up to radical. Viewing this in the polynomial ring
S
, we get

\operatorname{ara}(\RI(m,\underline{y}))\leq d,

independent of characteristic.

Since the special linear group is linearly reductive in characteristic zero, the inclusion

T\hookrightarrow S
of
T
-modules splits. It follows that the local cohomology module supported at the nullcone ideal

(2.1.1)

H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}_{T}}(T)\otimes_{T}S\ =\ H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}_{T}}(S)\ =\ H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}_{T}S}(S)=\ H^{d}_{\RI(m,\underline{y})}(S)

is nonzero. But then
\operatorname{ara}(\RI(m,\underline{y}))\geqslant d
, see
[
ILL+07
, Proposition 9.12]
.
∎

While the connection with invariant theory is remarkably effective in characteristic zero, the ‘local cohomology obstruction’ in the above proof vanishes in positive characteristic. Indeed, we show next the stronger statement that the above inclusion does
not
split in positive characteristic; compare with
[
HJPS23
, Theorem 1.1]
. In order to do this, we would need the dimension of the ‘auxiliary ring’
K[B]
, which is calculated in the next section (see Theorem
3.7
) by endowing it with the structure of an Algebra with a Straightening Law.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
K
be a field of positive characteristic. The inclusion

K[B]\hookrightarrow K[X,\underline{y}]

has no
K[B]
-linear splitting for any choice of
m
and
n
with
m\geq n
.

Proof.

Set
S\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}K[X,\underline{y}]
. By Theorem
3.7
, the ring
K[B]
has dimension
n(m-n+1)+1
, Assume that the given inclusion splits. Then by the argument in Proposition
2.1
, we must have that the local cohomology module

H^{n(m-n+1)+1}_{\RI(m,\underline{y})}(S)

is nonzero. However, the coordinate ring of the nullcone

S/\RI(m,\underline{y})=S/I_{n}(X)+(X\underline{y})

is a Cohen–Macaulay domain since it is a variety of complexes, see
[
Hun81
, Theorem 6.2]
. By the vanishing theorem of Peskine–Szpiro
[
PS73
, Proposition III.4.1]
, we then have

H^{i}_{\RI(m,\underline{y})}(S)\neq 0\quad\text{if and only if }i=\operatorname{ht}(\RI(m,\underline{y})).

The
m
-residual intersection
\RI(m,\underline{y})
has height
m
. This forces

n(m-n+1)+1=m.

Rearranging terms, we get

(n-1)(m-n)=-1.

This is a contradiction since
m\geq n
.
∎

Another consequence of the invariant ring being an ASL is that the local cohomology obstruction yields the arithmetic rank over the integers as well.

Remark 2.3
.

Replacing the field
K
by the integers
\mathbb{Z}
, we have the inclusion

\mathbb{Z}[B]\hookrightarrow\mathbb{Z}[X,\underline{y}].

Note that the argument in the beginning of Proposition
2.1
also applies on replacing the field by the integers since
\mathbb{Z}[B]
is an ASL by Theorem
3.4
. The point is that the ASL structure guarantees a “
\mathbb{Z}
-homogeneous” system of parameters of the ring
\mathbb{Z}[B]
by
[
BV88
, Proposition 5.10]
. Furthermore, the local cohomology obstruction clearly persists over the integers. Therefore, the arithmetic rank of the residual intersection ideal
(B)\mathbb{Z}[X,\underline{y}]
is the same number as asserted in Proposition
2.1
.

3.
Upper bound on the arithmetic rank: Structure of the auxiliary ring

Note that Proposition
2.1
shows that
any
choice of a homogeneous system of parameters of the invariant ring witnesses the arithmetic rank of
\RI(m,\underline{y})
. In this section, we make these polynomials explicit. This is done by finding an ASL structure on the invariant ring.

We remark that the generators of the invariant ring
S^{\operatorname{SL}_{n}(K)}
from the previous section are listed in
[
DCP76
, Theorem 3.3]
and the relations between these generators can be found in
[

We continue with the notation established in the previous section:

\underline{y}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(y_{i})

The auxiliary ring is an ASL

We define a partial order

We recall the definition of an ASL.

Definition 3.2
.

Suppose
A
is a ring and that
H\subset A
is a finite poset. A

is a product of a totally ordered set of elements of

\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{k}\ \text{where}\ \alpha_{1}\leq\alpha_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\alpha_{k}.

Assume now that
A
is a
K
-algebra for a ring
K
and that the elements of

as a
K
-algebra.

Definition 3.3
.

The ring
A=K[H]
is an
Algebra with a Straightening Law
if it satisfies the following axioms:

•

(ASL-1) The algebra
A
is a free
K
-module with basis given by the set of standard monomials.

•

(ASL-2) If
\alpha,\beta\in H
are incomparable, and if

\alpha\cdot\beta=\sum_{i}c_{i}\cdot(\gamma_{i1}\cdots\gamma_{i2}\cdots\gamma_{in})

is the unique expression of
\alpha\cdot\beta
as a linear combination of standard monomials (here

is nonzero and
\gamma_{i1}\leq\cdots\leq\gamma_{in}
for all

) , then
\gamma_{i1}<\alpha
and
\gamma_{i1}<\beta
for all

.
The relations in (ASL-2) are called the

of the algebra

.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
K
be any ring. The auxiliary ring
K[B]
is an ASL.

Proof.

We first prove that the standard monomials in
B
are linearly independent over
K
. Consider the lexicographic monomial order defined by

y_{1}<y_{2}<\cdots<y_{n}<x_{11}<x_{12}<\cdots<x_{1n}<x_{21}<\cdots<x_{mn}.

Under this monomial order, the initial monomials of
Q_{i}
and
[j_{1},\dots,j_{n}]
in
K[X,\underline{y}]
are:

\operatorname{in}(Q_{i})=x_{i,n}y_{n}\;\text{ and }\;\operatorname{in}([j_{1},\dots,j_{n}])=x_{j_{1},1}\cdots x_{j_{n},n}.

Note that any standard monomial in
B
can be expressed as
Q\mu
, where
Q
is a standard monomial in the
Q_{i}
and
\mu
is a standard monomial in the maximal minors. Given any
Q_{i}
with nonzero exponent in
Q
and any minor
[j_{1},\dots,j_{n}]
with nonzero exponent in
\mu
, we have
i\leq j_{n}.
To prove the
K
-linear independence of the standard monomials, it suffices to prove that every standard monomial has a distinct initial monomial. Here, it may be prudent to note that standard monomials are monomials in
K[B]
, but polynomials in
K[X,\underline{y}]
.

Suppose that two distinct standard monomials
Q\mu
and
Q^{\prime}\mu^{\prime}
have the same initial monomial, i.e.,

\operatorname{in}(Q)\operatorname{in}(\mu)=\operatorname{in}(Q^{\prime})\operatorname{in}(\mu^{\prime}).

If
Q=Q^{\prime}
then
\operatorname{in}(\mu)=\operatorname{in}(\mu^{\prime})
; but then
\mu=\mu^{\prime}
by
[
BCRV22
, Proposition 3.3.4]
. Thus we must have
Q\neq Q^{\prime}
. Let

Q=Q_{i_{1}}\cdots Q_{i_{t}}\;\text{and }\;Q^{\prime}=Q_{j_{1}}\cdots Q_{j_{t}}.

Since the degree of
y_{n}
in
\operatorname{in}(Q)\operatorname{in}(\mu)
and
\operatorname{in}(Q^{\prime})\operatorname{in}(\mu^{\prime})
is the same, the degree of
Q
must be equal to the degree of
Q^{\prime}
and we can write

\operatorname{in}(Q)=(x_{i_{1},n}.y_{n})\cdots(x_{i_{t},n}.y_{n})\;\text{and}\;\operatorname{in}(Q^{\prime})=(x_{j_{1},n}.y_{n})\cdots(x_{j_{t},n}.y_{n}).

Since
\operatorname{in}(Q)\neq\operatorname{in}(Q^{\prime})
, we may assume without loss of generality that
\operatorname{in}(Q)
has a higher exponent of
x_{in}
than
\operatorname{in}(Q^{\prime})
for some
i
. Then
\operatorname{in}(\mu^{\prime})
has a higher exponent of
x_{in}
than
\operatorname{in}(\mu).
Consequently, by a degree counting argument,
\operatorname{in}(\mu)
has a higher exponent of
x_{kn}
for some
k\neq i
and
\operatorname{in}(Q)
has a lower exponent of
x_{kn}
than
\operatorname{in}(Q^{\prime})
. As
Q\mu
is standard,
Q_{i}<[\ldots k]
, which implies
i<k
. Similarly, as
Q^{\prime}\mu^{\prime}
is standard, we conclude

, a contradiction. Therefore distinct standard monomials have distinct initial monomials and we are done. The fact that the standard monomials span
K[B]
as a
K
-vector space follows from (ASL-2) below.

We now prove (ASL-2). We partition
B
into sets

\mathcal{C}=\{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{m}\}\;\text{and}\;\mathcal{D}=\{[i_{1},\dots,i_{n}]|1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{n}\leq m\}.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be incomparable elements in
H
. Since
\mathcal{C}
is totally ordered, we have
\alpha\in\mathcal{D}
or
\beta\in\mathcal{D}
. If both

and

lie in

, then

can be expressed as required since

is an ASL with the poset ordering induced by the one on

by
[

, Theorem 4.3]
.

Suppose now that
\alpha\in\mathcal{C}
and
\beta\in\mathcal{D}
. Let
\alpha=Q_{j}
and
\beta=[i_{1},\dots,i_{n}]
. As
\alpha
and
\beta
are incomparable, we have
j>i_{n}
. Note that the determinant

\begin{vmatrix}x_{i_{1},1}&x_{i_{1},2}&\cdots&x_{i_{1},n}&Q_{i_{1}}\\
x_{i_{2},1}&x_{i_{2},2}&\cdots&x_{i_{2},n}&Q_{i_{2}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
x_{i_{n},1}&x_{i_{n},2}&\cdots&x_{i_{n},n}&Q_{i_{n}}\\
x_{j,1}&x_{j,2}&\cdots&x_{j,n}&Q_{j}\\
\end{vmatrix}=0

as the last column is a linear combination of the other columns. Expanding the determinant along the last column allows us to express
Q_{j}[i_{1},\dots,i_{n}]
as a
K
-linear combination of the monomials
Q_{i_{t}}[i_{1},\dots,\hat{i_{t}},\dots,i_{n},j]
for
\ 1\leq t\leq n
. Each of these monomials is standard as
i_{t}\leq i_{n}<j.
We also have
Q_{i_{t}}<\alpha
and
Q_{i_{t}}<\beta
, as required.
∎

Now that we know
K[B]
is an ASL, there is a canonical way to obtain its homogeneous system of parameters. We recall this construction next:

Definition 3.5

(
[
BV88
, Page 55]
)
.

Given a finite poset
H
and
\mu\in H
, define

\text{rank}(\mu)=\max\{k|\text{ there exists a chain of }\mu=\mu_{k}>\mu_{k-1}>\cdots>\mu_{1}\text{ for }\mu_{i}\in H\}.

We also define
\text{rank}(H)=\max\{\text{rank}(\mu):\mu\in H\}.

Proposition 3.6

(
[
BV88
, Lemma 5.9]
)
.

Let
H
be a finite poset and
A=K[H]
be an ASL. Let
x_{i}=\sum_{\text{rank}(\mu)=i}\mu
for all
1\leq i\leq\operatorname{rank}(H)
. Then,
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{rank(H)}\}
is a system of parameters of
A
.

With these combinatorial inputs at our disposal, we are able to make explicit the polynomials which witness the arithmetic rank of
\RI(m,\underline{y})
.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
K
be a field. The ring
K[B]
has dimension
n(m-n+1)+1
with a homogeneous system of parameters given by

\left\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\operatorname{rank}(\mu)=i\end{subarray}}\mu\;:\;i=1,\ldots,n(m-n+1)+1\right\}.

Proof.

It may be observed that the rank of the poset
B
is
n(m-n+1)+1
. One way to see this is by noting the existence of the chain

\displaystyle Q_{1}<\cdots<Q_{n}<[1,2,\ldots n]<[1,\ldots,n-1,n+1]<\cdots<[1,\ldots,n-1,m]<

\displaystyle[1,\ldots,n-2,n,m]<[1,\ldots,n-2,n+1,m]<\cdots<[m-n+1,\ldots,m];

the rest follows from Proposition
3.6
.
∎

Example 3.8
.

Returning to Example

, we have
\dim(K[B])=7

3.2.
The auxiliary ring is a Gorenstein factorial domain

In this subsection, we study the homological properties of the auxiliary ring
K[B]
, where
K
is a field; in the next subsection we study its singularity in positive characteristic. We clarify that these results have no bearing on the arithmetic rank of the residual intersection under consideration.

We first show that the auxiliary ring is Cohen–Macaulay. This will be achieved by studying the underlying combinatorial structure more closely.

Definition 3.9
.

An element
\beta\in H
is a
cover
of
\alpha\in H
if
\beta>\alpha
and if there is no element
\gamma
lying strictly between
\alpha
and
\beta
, satisfying
\beta>\gamma>\alpha
.

The poset
H
is said to be
wonderful
if the following holds after a smallest and a greatest element
-\infty
and
\infty
, respectively, have been added to
H
:

If
\alpha\in H\ \cup\ \{-\infty\}
,
\gamma\in H\ \cup\ \{\infty\}
, and
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in H
are covers of
\alpha
satisfying
\beta_{1}<\gamma
and
\beta_{2}<\gamma
, then there exists an element
\beta\in H\ \cup\ \{\infty\}
with
\beta\leq\gamma
which covers both
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
.

Since a graded ASL on a wonderful poset is Cohen–Macaulay by
[
BV88
, Theorem 5.14]
, we have

Lemma 3.10
.

K[B]
is a Cohen–Macaulay domain.

Proof.

Indeed, we show that the poset
B
is wonderful. Let
\alpha\in B\cup\{-\infty\}
. We know that the poset
\{[i_{1},\dots,i_{n}]|1\leq i_{1}\leq\cdots i_{n}\leq m\}
with ordering induced by the ordering on
B
is wonderful by
[
BV88
, Corollary 5.17]
. So we may assume that
\alpha\in\{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{m}\}\cup\{-\infty\}.
If
\alpha=-\infty
, the only cover of
\alpha
is
Q_{1}
. Suppose that
\alpha=Q_{i}
. If
i<n
, the only cover of
\alpha
is
Q_{i+1}
. On the other hand, if
i\geq n
, the covers of
\alpha
are
Q_{i+1}
and
[1,2,\dots,n-1,i]
. Thus, if
i=m
or
i<n
,
\alpha
has a unique cover, and we are done.

Let
\alpha=Q_{i}
for
n\leq i<m
. Let
\gamma\in B\cup\{\infty\}
such that
Q_{i+1}
and
[1,\dots,n-1,i]
are less than
\gamma
. Observe that
\beta:=[1,\dots,n-1,i+1]
covers both
Q_{i+1}
and
[1,\dots,n-1,i]
. If
\gamma=\infty
, there is nothing to prove. As
[1,\dots,n-1,i]<\gamma
,
\gamma
cannot be one of the
Q_{i}
. Suppose
\gamma=[j_{1},\dots,j_{n}].
Then,
Q_{i+1}<\gamma
implies

. We conclude that

\beta=[1,\dots,n-1,i+1]\leq\gamma=[j_{1},\dots,j_{n}].\qed

We next show that the auxiliary ring is Gorenstein. The following lemma will be useful to this end.

Lemma 3.11

(
[
BH93
, Page 297]
)
.

Let
S
be a polynomial ring over a field
K
, and
G
a group acting on
S
by
K
-algebra automorphisms. If there are no nontrivial homomorphisms from
G
to
K^{\times}
, then the invariant ring
S^{G}
is a unique factorization domain (UFD).

Theorem 3.12
.

The auxiliary ring
K[B]
is Gorenstein.

Proof.

If the field
K
is infinite, then
K[B]
arises as the invariant ring of the action of the special linear group
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(K)
, as discussed in the beginning of Section
2
. Since the commutator subgroup of
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(K)
is itself, any group homomorphism from
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(K)
to the Abelian group

is trivial. It follows from Lemma
3.11
that
K[B]
is a UFD; it is Cohen–Macaulay by Lemma
3.10
. The assertion now follows from the fact that a finitely generated Cohen–Macaulay algebra over a field which is UFD is also Gorenstein
[
Mur64
]
; compare with
[
Sin17
, Remark 3.10]
.

If the field

is finite, let
\overline{K}
be its algebraic closure. The inclusion

K[B]\hookrightarrow\overline{K}[B]

induces a natural surjection on the divisor class groups. The assertion now follows from the above paragraph.
∎

3.3.
The auxiliary ring is F-regular

In this subsection, we prove that when the base field
K
has positive characteristic, the auxiliary ring
K[B]
is
F
-regular. The key observation is to initialize the straightening relations on the auxiliary ring to deduce that its initial subalgebra is a normal ASL. We then deform the singularity to the auxiliary ring.

We first recall the monomial order defined in the proof of Theorem
3.4
: the lexicographic monomial order defined by

y_{1}<y_{2}<\cdots<y_{n}<x_{11}<x_{12}<\cdots<x_{1n}<x_{21}<\cdots<x_{mn}.

Under this monomial order, the initial monomials of
Q_{i}
and
[j_{1},\dots,j_{n}]
are:

\operatorname{in}(Q_{i})=x_{i,n}y_{n}\;\text{ and }\;\operatorname{in}([j_{1},\dots,j_{n}])=x_{j_{1},1}\cdots x_{j_{n},n}.

Lemma 3.13
.

The initial subalgebra of
K[B]
with respect to the aforementioned monomial order is
K[\operatorname{in}(f)\;|\;f\in B]
. In other words,
B
is a Sagbi basis of
K[B]
.

Proof.

Let
F\in K[B].
By Theorem
3.4
(ASL-1),
F
can be expressed uniquely as a
K
-linear combination of standard monomials. However, as seen in the proof of Theorem
3.4
, every standard monomial has a distinct initial monomial. Consequently,
\operatorname{in}(F)=\operatorname{in}(g)
for some standard monomial
g
. As
g
is a product of elements in
B
,
\operatorname{in}(g)\in K[\operatorname{in}(f)\;|\;f\in B].

∎

Let
\operatorname{in}(B)=\{\operatorname{in}(f)\;|\;f\in B\}
. Imitating our partial order on
B
, we define a partial order on
\operatorname{in}(B)
as follows:

•

\operatorname{in}(Q_{i})\leq\operatorname{in}(Q_{j})
if
i\leq j
.

•

\operatorname{in}(Q_{j})\leq\operatorname{in}([i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{n}])
if
j\leq i_{n}
.

•

\operatorname{in}([i_{1},\dots,i_{n}])\leq\operatorname{in}([j_{1},\dots,j_{n}])
if
i_{k}\leq j_{k}
for all
k
.

The key step in establishing the
F
-regularity is:

Lemma 3.14
.

The initial subalgebra
\operatorname{in}(K[B])=K[\operatorname{in}(B)]
is an ASL.

Proof.

We begin by proving (ASL-1). The standard monomials in
K[\operatorname{in}(B)]
are the initial monomials of the standard monomials in the ASL
K[B]
. As every standard monomial in
K[B]
has a distinct initial monomial, we infer that the standard monomials in
K[\operatorname{in}(B)]
are
K
-linearly independent.

To prove that the standard monomials form a
K
-spanning set of
K[\operatorname{in}(B)]
, let
q
be a monomial in
K[\operatorname{in}(B)].
Then,
q=\operatorname{in}(F)
for some
F\in K[B]
, and as observed in the proof of Lemma
3.13
,
\operatorname{in}(F)=\operatorname{in}(g)
for some standard monomial
g
in
K[B].
Since
\operatorname{in}(g)
is a standard monomial in
K[\operatorname{in}(B)]
, we have proved (ASL-1).

To prove (ASL-2), let
\alpha
and
\beta
be two incomparable elements in
K[\operatorname{in}(B)].
Then,
\alpha=\operatorname{in}(\alpha^{\prime})
and
\beta=\operatorname{in}(\beta^{\prime})
for incomparable
\alpha^{\prime}
and
\beta^{\prime}
in
B
. By initializing the straightening relation on
\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}
, we get a corresponding straightening relation on the product
\alpha\beta.

∎

Theorem 3.15
.

The initial subalgebra
\operatorname{in}(K[B])=K[\operatorname{in}(B)]
is normal.

Proof.

Consider a minimal presentation of the initial subalgebra
K[\operatorname{in}(B)]
by the polynomial ring
K[Y_{i}|1\leq i\leq m+\binom{m}{n}]
; that is,
\varphi:K[\underline{Y}]{\,\,\longrightarrow\hskip-7.74998pt\rightarrow}\,\,K[\operatorname{in}(B)]
. As
K[\operatorname{in}(B)]
is an ASL, the presentation ideal
\operatorname{ker}(\varphi)
is generated by the straightening relations of
K[\operatorname{in}(B)].
We claim that there exists a monomial order
\tau
in
K[\underline{Y}]
such that
\operatorname{in}_{\tau}(\operatorname{ker}(\varphi))
is squarefree. Then
K[\operatorname{in}(B)]
is normal by
[
BCRV22
, Theorem 6.1.13]
.

We first consider the partial order induced on the
Y_{i}
:
Y_{i}<Y_{j}
if
\varphi(Y_{i})<\varphi(Y_{j}).
We extend this partial order to a total order on the
Y_{i}
and let
\tau
be the induced graded reverse lexicographic monomial order in
K[\underline{Y}].

Observe that
\operatorname{ker}(\varphi)
is generated by quadratic binomials
Y_{i}Y_{j}-Y_{s}Y_{t},
where
Y_{i}
and
Y_{j}
are incomparable,
Y_{s}\leq Y_{t}
,
Y_{s}<Y_{i}
and
Y_{s}<Y_{j}.
The initial monomial of this binomial is

\operatorname{in}_{\tau}(Y_{i}Y_{j}-Y_{s}Y_{t})=Y_{i}Y_{j}.

Thus, every incomparable pair of
\operatorname{in}(B)
uniquely corresponds to the initial monomial of a generator of
\operatorname{ker}(\varphi).
As the standard monomials in
K[\operatorname{in}(B)]
are linearly independent, the quadratic binomials
Y_{i}Y_{j}-Y_{s}Y_{t}
induced by the straightening relations form a Gröbner basis of
\operatorname{ker}(\varphi)
with respect to
\tau
. The initial terms of these binomials are squarefree.
∎

Corollary 3.16
.

Let
K
be an
F
-finite field of positive characteristic.
K[B]
is a strongly
F
-regular ring.

Proof.

A normal affine semigroup ring is a direct summand of a polynomial ring
[
Hoc72
]
and therefore it is
F
-rational. Hence by Theorem
3.15
, the initial subalgebra
\operatorname{in}(K[B])
is
F
-rational. As
F
-rationality deforms, we have that
K[B]
is
F
-rational
[
BCRV22
, Theorem 7.3.11]
. Since
K[B]
is an
\mathbb{N}
-graded Gorenstein ring by Theorem
3.12
,

-rationality and strong

-regularity are equivalent.
∎

4.
Lower bound on the arithmetic rank: Topology of the residual intersection

The ‘algebraic obstruction’ which yields the lower bound for the arithmetic rank in Proposition
2.1
vanishes in positive characteristic as noted in Lemma
2.2
. In this section, we establish the lower bound by studying the topology of the complement of the variety
V(\RI(m,\underline{y}))
. We broadly follow the general strategy of Bruns and Schwänzl as in
[
BS90
]
, though many new intricacies arise in our case.

We begin with setting up the notation. Let
m>n>1
be positive integers (the cases
m=n
and
n=1
will be handled separately). Let
X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(x_{ij})
and
\underline{y}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(y_{i})
be
m\times n
and
n\times 1
matrices of indeterminates. The ideal
\RI(m,\underline{y})=I_{n}(X)+(X\underline{y})
is the generic
m
-residual intersection of the ideal of variables
(\underline{y})
.
We now begin our topological calculations. Let

U\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(K^{m\times n}\times K^{n\times 1})\setminus V(\RI(m,\underline{y}))=\{(A,B)\in K^{m\times n}\times K^{n\times 1}|AB\neq 0\text{ or }I_{n}(A)\neq 0\}.

Set
d\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}mn+n
to be the dimension of
K^{m\times n}\times K^{n\times 1}
. Further, let

U_{1}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(K^{m\times n}\times K^{n\times 1})\setminus V(X\underline{y})\;\text{and}\;U_{2}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(K^{m\times n}\times K^{n\times 1})\setminus V(I_{n}(X)),

so that
U=U_{1}\cup U_{2}
. Similarly, set

\displaystyle U_{12}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}U_{1}\cap U_{2}

\displaystyle=\{\,(A,B)\in K^{m\times n}\times K^{n\times 1}\mid AB\neq 0,\;\operatorname{rank}(A)=n\,\}

\displaystyle=\{\,(A,B)\in K^{m\times n}\times K^{n\times 1}\mid B\neq 0,\;\operatorname{rank}(A)=n\,\}.

We define a continuous surjection

\begin{array}[]{c}\pi:U_{12}\longrightarrow Gr(n,m)\times Gr(1,n)\\[6.0pt]
(A,B)\longmapsto\bigl(\operatorname{im}(A),\,\operatorname{im}(B)\bigr).\end{array}

Notice that

\pi^{-1}(K\langle e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\rangle,K\langle(1,0,\dots,0)\rangle)=\operatorname{GL}_{n}(K)\times\operatorname{GL}_{1}(K).

A key observation of this section is:

Lemma 4.1
.

The map
\pi:U_{12}\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gr}(n,m)\times\operatorname{Gr}(1,n)
is a locally trivial fiber bundle (in the Zariski topology) with fiber
\operatorname{GL}_{n}(K)\times\operatorname{GL}_{1}(K).

Proof.

Fix bases
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{m}\}
of
K^{m}
and
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\}
of
K^{n}.
Let
\mathcal{S}\subset\operatorname{Gr}(n,m)\times\operatorname{Gr}(1,n)
be the collection of pairs of subspaces
(V_{1},V_{2})
such that

V_{1}\cap K\langle e_{n+1},\dots,e_{m}\rangle=0\quad\text{and}\quad V_{2}\cap K\langle f_{2},\dots,f_{n}\rangle=0.

Given such a pair
(V_{1},V_{2})
, we choose a basis
\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\}
of
V_{1}
such that for all
i
,
\alpha_{i}=e_{i}+\alpha_{i}^{\prime},
where
\alpha_{i}^{\prime}\in K\langle e_{n+1},\dots,e_{m}\rangle
. Similarly, we choose a basis
\{\beta\}
of
V_{2}
such that
\beta=f_{1}+\beta^{\prime}
, where
\beta^{\prime}\in K\langle f_{2},\dots,f_{n}\rangle
. Let
A_{V_{1}}
and
A_{V_{2}}
be the
m\times n
and
n\times 1
matrices formed by the columns
\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\}
and
\{\beta\}
respectively.

Then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{S})&\cong&\mathcal{S}\times(\operatorname{GL}_{n}(K)\times\operatorname{GL}_{1}(K))\\
(N_{1},N_{2})&\longmapsto&((\operatorname{im}(N_{1}),\operatorname{im}(N_{2})),([N_{1}]_{\{1,\dots,n\}},[N_{2}]_{\{1\}}))\\
(A_{V_{1}}M_{1},A_{V_{2}}M_{2})&\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\longmapsto$}}&((V_{1},V_{2}),(M_{1},M_{2})),\end{array}

where
[N]_{\mathcal{I}}
is the submatrix of
N
formed by the rows indexed by elements of
\mathcal{I}
. Similar computations apply to the open sets in
\operatorname{Gr}(n,m)\times\operatorname{Gr}(1,n)
formed by the non-vanishing of any other
n
-minor and
1
-minor. These open sets clearly cover
\operatorname{Gr}(n,m)\times\operatorname{Gr}(1,n)
.
∎

In order to establish the asserted lower bound on the arithmetic rank, we will need the following vanishing theorems. These results follow from affine vanishing and the Mayer–Vietoris sequence for singular and étale cohomologies
[
Mil80
, III.2.24]
,

Theorem 4.2
.

(1)

If
X
is a smooth complex variety of algebraic dimension
d
that admits an open cover by
t
affines, then

H^{i}_{\mathrm{sing}}(X,\mathbb{Q})=0\quad\text{for all}\ \ i>d+t-1.

(2)

If
X
is a smooth variety of algebraic dimension
d
, over an algebraically closed field
K
, that admits an open cover by
t
affines, then for any
q
invertible in
K

H^{i}_{\mathrm{\acute{e}t}}(X,\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})=0\quad\text{for all}\ \ i>d+t-1.

While
[
BS90
]
determines the arithmetic rank of a determinantal ideal, for our computation, we only need the upper bound, which can be produced more directly as follows:

Lemma 4.3
.

We have
\operatorname{ara}(I_{n}(X))\leq mn-n^{2}+1
and
\operatorname{ara}(X\underline{y})\leq m
.

Proof.

The second assertion is obvious. For the first assertion, we have the inclusion of the Plücker ring

K[\,\text{all $n\times n$ minors of }X\,]\hookrightarrow K[X].

As in the proof of Proposition
2.1
, a homogeneous system of parameters of the Plücker ring generates its homogeneous maximal ideal up to radical. Viewing this in
K[X]
, we get
\operatorname{ara}(I_{n}(X))
is at most the dimension of the Plücker ring, which is
mn-n^{2}+1
.
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
K=\mathbb{C}
and
d=mn+n
, the algebraic dimension of
U
. Then

H^{d+(mn-n^{2}+n)}_{\mathrm{sing}}(U,\mathbb{Q})=\mathbb{Q}.

Proof.

Using Lemma
4.3
, the statement of Theorem
4.2
(1) in our case is

(4.4.1)

H^{i}_{\text{sing}}(U_{1},\mathbb{Q})=0\ \text{for all}\ i>d+m-1,

(4.4.2)

H^{i}_{\text{sing}}(U_{2},\mathbb{Q})=0\ \text{for all}\ i>d+mn-n^{2}.

Since
\operatorname{Gr}(n,m)\times\operatorname{Gr}(1,n)
is simply connected, the Leray spectral sequence for the fibration in Lemma
4.1
takes the simple
form

E^{u,v}_{2}=H_{\text{sing}}^{u}(\operatorname{Gr}(n,m)\times\operatorname{Gr}(1,n),H_{\text{sing}}^{v}(\operatorname{GL}_{n}(K)\times\operatorname{GL}_{1}(K),\mathbb{Q}))\Longrightarrow H_{\text{sing}}^{u+v}(U_{12},\mathbb{Q}).

Let
D\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\dim(\operatorname{Gr}(n,m)\times\operatorname{Gr}(1,n))=n(m-n)+(n-1)=mn-n^{2}+n-1
and note that
\dim(\operatorname{GL}_{n}(K)\times\operatorname{GL}_{1}(K))=n^{2}+1.
Since

H^{2D}_{\text{sing}}(\operatorname{Gr}(n,m)\times\operatorname{Gr}(1,n),\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathbb{Q}\quad\text{and}\quad H^{n^{2}+1}_{\text{sing}}(\operatorname{GL}_{n}(K)\times\operatorname{GL}_{1}(K),\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathbb{Q},

and the higher cohomology groups vanish, we get that

\displaystyle H^{2D+n^{2}+1}_{\text{sing}}(U_{12},\mathbb{Q})\cong E^{2D,n^{2}+1}_{\infty}

\displaystyle=E^{2D,n^{2}+1}_{2}

\displaystyle\cong H^{2D}_{\text{sing}}(\operatorname{Gr}(n,m)\times\operatorname{Gr}(1,n),H^{n^{2}+1}_{\text{sing}}(\operatorname{GL}_{n}(K)\times\operatorname{GL}_{1}(K),\mathbb{Q}))

\displaystyle\cong H^{2D}_{\text{sing}}(\operatorname{Gr}(n,m)\times\operatorname{Gr}(1,n),\mathbb{Q})

\displaystyle\cong\mathbb{Q}.

Let
N\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}d+(mn+n-n^{2})
then
2D+n^{2}+1=d+(mn+n-n^{2}-1)=N-1
. We have

H^{i}_{\text{sing}}(U_{12},\mathbb{Q})\neq 0\ \text{for}\ i=N-1.

Since
m>n>1
, we have
N-1>d+m-1
and
N-1>d+(mn-n^{2}+1)
. Hence, by Equations
4.4.1
and
4.4.2
, we get

H^{i}_{\text{sing}}(U_{1},\mathbb{Q})=0\ \text{and}\ H^{i}_{\text{sing}}(U_{2},\mathbb{Q})=0\ \text{for}\ i\geq N-1.

The Mayer–Vietoris sequence of singular cohomology

\longrightarrow H^{i}_{\text{sing}}(U,\mathbb{Q})\longrightarrow H^{i}_{\text{sing}}(U_{1},\mathbb{Q})\oplus H^{i}_{\text{sing}}(U_{2},\mathbb{Q})\longrightarrow H^{i}_{\text{sing}}(U_{12},\mathbb{Q})\longrightarrow H^{i+1}_{\text{sing}}(U,\mathbb{Q})\longrightarrow

gives us

H^{N-1}_{\text{sing}}(U_{12},\mathbb{Q})\cong H^{N}_{\text{sing}}(U,\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathbb{Q}.\qed

Remark 4.5
.

The required lower bound for the arithmetic rank of the residual intersection
\RI(m,\underline{y})
follows immediately from the above calculation. By Theorem
4.2
(1), we have

H^{i}_{\text{sing}}(U,\mathbb{Q})=0\ \text{for all }\ i>d+\operatorname{ara}(\RI(m,\underline{y}))-1.

By Theorem
4.4
, this gives
N\leq d+\operatorname{ara}(\RI(m,\underline{y}))-1
. Therefore

\operatorname{ara}(\RI(m,\underline{y}))\geq n(m-n+1)+1

The above calculation works analogously in positive characteristic:

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
K
be an algebraically closed field of characteristic
p>0
and let
m
,
n
,
U
and
d
be defined as above. Then

H^{d+(mn-n^{2}+n)}_{\mathrm{\acute{e}t}}(U,\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}.,

where

is a prime integer other than

.

Proof.

The Grassmann variety is simply connected in the étale topology as well. So the étale fundamental group

\pi_{1,\text{\'{e}t}}(\operatorname{Gr}(n,m)\times\operatorname{Gr}(1,n))=\pi_{1,\text{\'{e}t}}(\operatorname{Gr}(n,m))\times\pi_{1,\text{\'{e}t}}(\operatorname{Gr}(1,n))=0.

Given this fact, the proof of Theorem
4.4
goes through verbatim.
∎

We are now ready to prove the Main Theorem of this paper.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
m
and
n
be positive integers with
m\geq n
and

is a field or the integers. The arithmetic rank of the ideal
\RI(m,\underline{y})
in

is

\operatorname{ara}(\RI(m,\underline{y}))=\begin{cases}n(m-n+1)+1&n\geqslant 2,\\
m&n=1.\end{cases}

Proof.

The case

is immediate since

is an ideal of
m
variables; we assume

from now on. If
K
is the integers, the assertion is noted in Remark

. If

and Theorem

. If

and

3.7
and
4.6
. Note that a lower bound for the arithmetic rank in

is also a lower bound in

is the algebraic closure of

. It remains to prove the assertion over fields of characteristic

when

equals

By Theorem

, we already have that

To prove a lower bound for the arithmetic rank, we may assume that

is algebraically closed. We first consider the case

. Observe that

\RI(2,(y_{1},y_{2}))=I_{2}\begin{pmatrix}x_{11}&x_{21}&-y_{2}\\
x_{12}&x_{22}&y_{1}\\
\end{pmatrix}

be a prime other than

and let

This settles the case

(2).

Next, suppose that

. Specialize the matrix

X^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\begin{bmatrix}x_{11}&x_{12}&0&\cdots&0\\
x_{21}&x_{22}&0&\cdots&0\\

\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Since arithmetic rank can only decrease on specialization, it suffices to show that the ideal

The highest nonvanishing étale cohomology of

By the Mayer–Vietoris sequence on étale cohomology, we get

Therefore, by Theorem

∎

Let

(2)

Now we assume that conditions

Appendix A
A second take on the dimension of the auxiliary ring

Recall that the dimension of the auxiliary ring

In this section, we discuss another strategy to compute the dimension of

Notation A.1

•

Theorem A.2
.

Fix Notation

We first show that

and

We begin with describing the elements of

and

{\mathrm{exp}(m_{\alpha},d)}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\max\left\{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}~|~\text{$d^{k}$ divides $m_{\alpha}$}\right\}.

\exp(m_{\alpha},x_{(i,1)})=\begin{cases}\exp(m_{\alpha},Q_{1}^{\prime})+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq t\leq n\\
