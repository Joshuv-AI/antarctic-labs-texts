Detecting and Mitigating Hateful Content in Multimodal Memes with Vision-Language Models
Minh-Hao Van, Xintao Wu

Abstract
The rapid evolution of social media has provided enhanced communication channels for individuals to create online content, enabling them to express their thoughts and opinions. Multimodal memes, often utilized for playful or humorous expressions with visual and textual elements, are sometimes misused to disseminate hate speech against individuals or groups. While the detection of hateful memes is well-researched, developing effective methods to transform hateful content in memes remains a significant challenge. Leveraging the powerful generation and reasoning capabilities of Vision-Language Models (VLMs), we address the tasks of detecting and mitigating hateful content. This paper presents two key contributions: first, a definition-guided prompting technique for detecting hateful memes, and second, a unified framework for mitigating hateful content in memes, named UnHateMeme , which works by replacing hateful textual and/or visual components. With our definition-guided prompts, VLMs achieve impressive performance on hateful memes detection task. Furthermore, our UnHateMeme framework, integrated with VLMs, demonstrates a strong capability to convert hateful memes into non-hateful forms that meet human-level criteria for hate speech and maintain multimodal coherence between image and text. Through empirical experiments, we show the effectiveness of state-of-the-art pretrained VLMs such as LLaVA, Gemini and GPT-4o on the proposed tasks, providing a comprehensive analysis of their respective strengths and limitations for these tasks. This paper aims to shed light on important applications of VLMs for ensuring safe and respectful online environments. Warning: This paper contains examples of hateful content.

Introduction

Nowadays, social media platforms serve as extensive repositories for a wide range of content, including text, images, audio, and video. However, this vast array of information presents a dual challenge: it may not always be safe for viewers, potentially containing explicit or implicit harm to viewers. Multimodal memes, often utilized for playful or humorous expression with visual and textual elements, are sometimes misappropriated to disseminate hate speech against individuals or groups based on their characteristics. Consequently, identifying hateful memes has become a critical issue that social media platforms aim to address to protect users
[
12
]
. Detecting hateful memes, however, is a non-trivial task since multimodal memes combine visual and textual components to convey messages. The task is more challenging when the hate can be either unimodal or multimodal. While unimodal hate can be identified by assessing the hatefulness in either the image or the text modality, multimodal hate is more difficult to detect because the image and text are non-hateful individually but together convey a hateful message. Further, the hateful content is not always explicitly expressed but is implicitly hidden within a historical story behind it. Therefore, intelligent systems are urgently needed to process the vast digital content uploaded to social platforms. Many deep learning techniques have been proposed for this detection task

Addressing this gap represents the next important step in hate speech research, highlighting the potential to assist online users in creating safe and lighthearted memes to be shared on social media.

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable performance across various natural language processing tasks in recent years. With a strong ability to learn human language from vast corpora of text, LLMs can encode enormous amounts of knowledge as embedding vectors, which is a backbone for transforming the knowledge to different forms of representations with the help of adapters

When integrated with vision capabilities, LLMs enable the exploration of emergent abilities in multimodal contexts. Vision-language models (VLMs), such as CLIP, LLaVA, Flamingo, GPT-4, and Gemini, have demonstrated impressive performance across various vision-linguistic tasks. VLMs and their variations can not only perform well on general tasks but also be applied in other fields such as medical data

In this paper, we propose VLM-based approaches for detecting and mitigating hateful memes, making two key contributions: (1) a definition-guided prompting technique for detecting hateful memes and (2) a unified framework for mitigating hateful content in memes. To the best of our knowledge, our work is among the first few works to leverage prompting techniques with VLMs for the detection of hateful memes and the first to explore the hateful memes mitigation – a critical step towards ensuring safe and respectful interactions on online social media environments in the era of LLMs and VLMs. For example, users may share memes intended to be humorous or satirical, but there remains a major concern whether such content, either explicitly or implicitly, conveys harmful messages to viewers. More importantly, if memes are determined to be hateful, an open and intriguing question arises:
what actions can be taken to assist social media users in combating toxic content and promoting positive messages?
As a result, we focus on exploring a novel strategy for modifying hateful memes such that the mitigated versions convey meaningful messages without being offensive, addressing a challenging societal issue that requires interdisciplinary collaboration between AI and social science researchers.

To effectively detect hateful memes, we propose a definition-guided prompt that incorporates a detailed definition of hatefulness and clear classification criteria to enhance the reasoning capabilities of VLMs. With our robust detection prompts, VLMs can outperform multiple hateful memes detection baselines without the need for additional fine-tuning steps or OCR text. Notably, our results reveal that VLMs yield substantial improvements in AUROC scores with our proposed prompts, compared to prior findings with simpler prompts discussed in

To tackle the challenge of mitigating hateful content, we propose a mitigation framework, named
UnHateMeme
, for converting hateful memes to non-hateful ones by leveraging VLMs with task-specific prompts. Our framework decomposes the task of mitigating hateful memes into multiple sequential steps, including tasks such as analyzing the type of hate, identifying the source of hate, and generating substitute image or text. We employ GPT-4o as the central reasoning component in our mitigation framework due to its demonstrated superior performance and exceptional generative capabilities, as evidenced by our comprehensive experiments on hateful meme detection. Nonetheless, the framework is modular and can be easily adapted to integrate other state-of-the-art VLMs. To evaluate the hateful memes mitigation task, we first use our unified framework to transform hateful memes into non-hateful versions and create a corresponding dataset of mitigated memes. Human evaluation is then adopted to assess the quality of the mitigated dataset based on two metrics:

UnHateMeme
framework effectively transforms hateful memes into non-hateful versions by removing offensive visual and/or textual elements and suggesting new alternatives while ensuring multimodal image-text coherence in the new memes. This marks a substantial advancement over previous deep learning models, which lacked the capacity for such content generation.

3.1
Problem Definition

A dataset
\mathcal{D}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
contains multiple pairs of data sample
(x_{i},y_{i})
’s, where
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
is the input meme and
y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
are label of hateful or non-hateful, respectively. For memes dataset, each input image
x_{i}
contains the visual part
v_{i}
and the textual part
t_{i}
, e.g.,
\mathcal{X}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{T})
. We define the task of hateful memes detection as a classification problem
f:\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
.

OCR for meme text.
In general, a discrete visual element
v_{i}
or textual element
t_{i}
is not always available. With online memes, the meme text is often integrated into the image itself, resulting in the final meme
x_{i}
. Previous research on hateful meme detection has utilized Optical Character Recognition (OCR) methods to extract the meme text, thereby aiding models in better analyzing the hatefulness within the textual modality. While recent state-of-the-art VLMs have shown excellent capabilities in extracting texts from images without the need for additional OCR modules, we also evaluate the setting where OCR-extracted text is available. This approach allows for a comprehensive comparison with previous studies and an assessment of how VLMs perform both with and without the use of OCR.

Challenges with multimodal memes.
Although
f
is a binary classification task (hateful or non-hateful), accurately detecting hateful memes is non-trivial due to the presence of both unimodal and multimodal hate. Hateful memes with unimodal hate can be identified by assessing the hate in either image or text individually. However, hateful memes with multimodal hate pose a more complex challenge, as the meme image
v_{i}
and text
t_{i}
are non-hateful on their own but together convey a hateful message. This necessitates an effective approach for interpreting the message conveyed by both modalities. To address these challenges, we introduce definition-guided prompts to guide VLMs in detecting hateful memes.

3.2
Definition-guided Prompt

While one can prompt the VLM to enlarge its capabilities on various tasks, it is essential to note that the model’s performance dramatically depends on the quality of prompts, including clear instructions for the specific task. Detecting hateful memes presents a unique challenge in crafting an effective prompt. This challenge involves deciphering slang or historical references within visual and textual elements to identify a hateful message. Another challenging problem is how to guide the model to effectively reason about the source of hate in the memes. In Figure

3.3
Definition-guided Few-shot Prompt

Our definition-guided prompt can be adapted to a few-shot setting by incorporating multiple relevant in-context examples to improve detection. The performance of in-context learning heavily relies on the quality of these examples, as those closely related to each test item are more effective representatives for inference. For each test meme, we use Retrieval-based In-Context Example Selection (RICES)
[
43
]
to find similar demonstration memes from a candidate set. Since cloud VLMs like Gemini or GPT-4o don’t provide visual embeddings, we use the CLIP model’s visual encoder to obtain meme embeddings. We then calculate and rank cosine similarities between test and candidate memes to select the best demonstration set, ensuring a balanced number of examples per class. Figure

4.1
Problem Definition

Let
\mathcal{D}_{H}\subset\mathcal{D}
be a subset including hateful memes in
\mathcal{D}
. For each
(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathcal{D}_{H}
,
x_{i}=(v_{i},t_{i})
and
y_{i}=1
(hateful). We define the task of mitigating hateful content in memes as the problem of finding suitable substitutions, represented by the function
g:\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
. Given that a meme typically consists of both visual and textual components, the function
g
is designed to address two distinct tasks:
g_{t}
, responsible for finding a substitute text
t_{i}^{\prime}=g_{t}(v_{i},t_{i})
, and
g_{v}
, responsible for finding a substitute image
v_{i}^{\prime}=g_{v}(v_{i},t_{i})
. The objective is to convert the hateful meme
x_{i}
into a modified version
x_{i}^{\prime}=(v_{i}^{\prime},t_{i}^{\prime})
, which will be classified as non-hateful, i.e,
y_{i}^{\prime}=0
.

The content generation capabilities of VLMs can be leveraged to address these tasks. Specifically,
g_{t}
can be approached as a text generation task by directly prompting GPT-4o to generate a substitute text. Designing the task
g_{v}
is more complicated as it requires multiple steps to obtain the substitute image. In particular,
g_{v}
involves prompting the VLM to create a description of the potential substitute image, followed by matching this description against a collection of non-hateful images to identify the most suitable substitution.

Mitigation criteria.
The objective of the mitigation task is to modify hateful memes to make them suitable for sharing on social networks. These memes aim to spread playful and lighthearted content without being offensive to other users. Additionally, the framework must ensure coherence between image and text in the newly created meme. For example, a naïve method might simply replace the original text in Figure
1
with “
It’s a rainy day!
” – a substitution that fails to convey any meaningful message when paired with the image. Thus, we anticipate the proposed framework to satisfy two criteria for the mitigated memes:
non-hatefulness
and
image-text coherence
.

Challenges with mitigating hateful content.
Developing an effective generation method
g
alone is insufficient for a practical application due to the challenge of determining whether the image or text modality requires correction. In other words, users must manually specify either
g_{v}
(for image) or
g_{t}
(for text), or both, to perform the generation – a process that ideally should be automated by the mitigation framework. Therefore, we establish that an effective framework for mitigating hateful memes should be unified and capable of performing the task in an end-to-end manner.

As discussed in Section
3.1
, the hate in memes can be either unimodal or multimodal, depending on how the hateful content is embedded in the visual and textual components. The decision on whether to address the image or text modality hinges on the type of hate depicted in the memes. For a meme with unimodal hate, which often explicitly contains hateful image or text, the corresponding hateful components can be corrected to mitigate the hate. Additionally, there are memes in which both the image and text independently express hate. For a meme with multimodal hate, altering either modality removes the hateful message, as it only emerges from the specific combination of image and text. Thus, distinguishing between unimodal and multimodal hate is a crucial step in the framework. Once the type of hate in memes is identified, the next step for unimodal hate cases is to determine which modality (image or text) is responsible for the hateful content. These challenges lead to two additional tasks beyond the main generation task: analyzing the type of hate, denoted as
h:\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\{\mathrm{unimodal-hate},\mathrm{multimodal-hate}\}
, and identifying the source of hate, denoted as
s:\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\{\mathrm{image},\mathrm{text},\mathrm{both}\}
.

4.2
Unified Framework for Mitigating Hateful Content in Memes

The objective of mitigating hateful memes involves transforming hateful content into non-hateful versions while preserving the multimodal coherence between visual and textual elements. As aforementioned, a viable framework for mitigating hateful content in memes should automatically handle three tasks: analyzing the type of hate, identifying the source of hate, and generating substitute image or text. To this end, we propose a unified and end-to-end framework called
UnHateMeme
. This framework processes hateful memes and generates non-hateful counterparts with minimal alterations, ensuring that the content remains as faithful to the original as possible. We leverage the advanced generative capabilities of VLMs, such as GPT-4o, and propose task-specific prompts to guide the model toward achieving the desired outcome. Figure

Before creating the mitigated meme, the original text must be removed from the existing meme. While various object removal methods, such as Image Inpainting, typically require input masks for the objects to be removed, manually drawing these masks is not efficient. To address this issue, we propose using a module that integrates GroundingDINO
[
21
]
, SAM
[
15
]
and Inpainting Anything
[
44
]
. GroundingDINO is initially employed to extract the bounding boxes of objects described by an input text. In our scenario, we focus on extracting the bounding boxes of text lines present on the given meme. However, bounding boxes alone are insufficiently detailed for creating precise masks to remove text, as they might lead to the unexpected removal of other objects. Therefore, in the second step, we utilize the Segment Anything Model (SAM) to segment the specific textual areas within the extracted bounding boxes. Once we have generated these detailed segmented text masks, we utilize Inpaint Anything framework, specifically LaMA
[
34
]
, to effectively remove the text lines from the memes.

On the unseen test dataset, GPT-4o also achieves the best accuracy over all baselines. In terms of AUROC, although VLMs with definition-guided prompts are less effective than other challenge winners on the unseen dataset, GPT-4o can achieve approximately

In comparisons among VLMs, GPT-4o exceeds the performance of all other VLMs, outperforming the second-best model (GemPro-V) by 9.40% (6.15%) in accuracy on seen (unseen) test data, respectively.

In the few-shot scenario, we observe a general trend of improved performance as the number of demonstration examples increases, a common finding in in-context learning. Similar to the zero-shot case, VLMs with our few-shot prompt continue to achieve significantly better performance compared to the baselines. Notably, our definition-guided zero-shot prompt consistently surpasses Fl, OF, and OC, even outperforming those utilizing 32-shot prompting. Overall, we find that state-of-the-art models like GemPro-V and GPT-4o exhibit a robust capability to extract textual content or objects within memes, regardless of the presence of OCR text.

Human evaluation.
To ensure a rigorous evaluation process, we assemble a team of seven domain experts from both the fields of AI and social sciences to design the evaluation criteria and independently assess the quality of the mitigated memes. We ensure that each meme receives at least three evaluations, with the final decision determined by the majority vote. An additional expert is invited in cases where a tiebreaker is necessary. To promote diversity among the experts, we invite individuals from various backgrounds – including different ages, races, and genders. All experts receive the same evaluation guidelines. non-hatefulness

Answer options: non-coherence (NC) or coherence (C).

The first question (Q1) corresponds to the non-hatefulness criterion, while the second (Q2) corresponds to image-text coherence.

6
Conclusion

Our research is among the pioneering efforts to tackle the issue of detecting and mitigating hateful content in memes with VLMs. Utilizing our definition-guided prompts, pretrained VLMs demonstrate exceptional reasoning capability and content extraction for the detection task through zero-shot or few-shot prompting, eliminating the need for additional training or fine-tuning steps. Subsequently, we define a novel task focused on mitigating hateful content in memes and propose a unified framework, called
UnHateMeme
, to address this challenge. Through our framework, GPT-4o demonstrates remarkable proficiency in transforming hateful memes into non-hateful ones while maintaining image-text coherence, as validated by human evaluation. In future work, we plan to enhance the framework by integrating image generation models such as Stable Diffusion.

Appendix A
Ethics Statement.

Large and foundation models have garnered attention from both the research community and society in recent years. In the era of AI, multimodal generative models offer significant advances in digital content creation, albeit raising concerns about unknown safety and privacy threats. The aim of this work is to evaluate how VLMs, including state-of-the-art models like LLaVA, Google Gemini, and OpenAI GPT-4o, can be utilized to explore hatefulness in multimodal memes. As memes can convey different meanings depending on the visual and textual context, we seek to detect and correct hate speech in a constructive manner. Our formulation for the mitigation task aims to pave the way for a new potential direction in which VLMs can address human mistakes or malicious intentions. Proposed prompts consist of carefully curated sentences designed to guide models toward exercising fairness, equity, and non-violent actions. The experiments were conducted using well-known datasets from prior research, and human experts involved in the evaluation were informed in advance about the offensive content in the data.

Appendix D
Models in the Evaluation

OpenCLIP (OC).
OpenCLIP
[
9
]
is the attempt to reproduce and train more open-source versions of CLIP
[
28
]
. CLIP (Contrastive Language-Image Pre-Training) is a neural network that connects images and text. In general, the model aims to learn the relationship between the images and their textual description, such as captions or labels. We use the version ViT-G/14, named OpenCLIP-ViT-G/14 (OC-G/14).

LLaVA-Llama-2-13B (LV).
Pretrained CLIP and Llama-2 serve as the visual encoder and language model in this VLM
[
20
]
, respectively. While Flamingo uses the cross-attention scheme, LLaVA proposes a projection layer to map CLIP features to language tokens. Then, both visual and textual tokens are used as inputs to the language model. The LLaVA version utilized in this work is fine-tuned on the Llama-2-13B chat model
[
36
]
, referred to as LLaVA-Llama-2-13B (LV) in this study.

Google Gemini Pro Vision (GemPro-V).
Built upon the Transformer architecture, Gemini versions are trained using advanced techniques in network design and model optimization to improve scalability. Although the model architecture is not detailed in
[
35
]
, the visual encoder draws inspiration from Flamingo
[
1
]
. Alongside textual data, the models are trained with inputs comprising images, audio, and videos interspersed with text. Unlike previous methods that allowed users to download and run inference on local machines, Google Gemini only allows inference via API. We collect results using Gemini Pro-Vision (GemPro-V).
Since the dataset might contain hate speech, we configured the safety setting of Gemini to the lowest level, ensuring that the API does not stop generating responses.

OpenAI GPT-4 omni (GPT-4o).
GPT-4o
[
26
]
is OpenAI’s flagship language generation model, capable of processing and generating multimodal inputs and outputs, including text, audio, images, and video. It is trained on a unified model network using multimodal training data, addressing the latency and information loss issues associated with previous versions that relied on separate model modules. GPT-4o is accessible exclusively through an API.

Flamingo/OpenFlamingo (Fl/OF).
Flamingo
[
1
]
effectively executes vision-language tasks using pretrained visual encoders and language models. The Perceiver Resampler is trained to project extensive embedding features from the encoder (NFNet-F6) to visual tokens. Leveraging a cross-attention scheme, the model processes multiple image-text inputs, enabling in-context learning. OpenFlamingo
[
2
]
is an attempt to replicate Flamingo as an open-source version.

Reproducibility

Dataset.
We focus on Facebook Hateful Memes Dataset, which is publicly available for download at

Models.
Except for Google Gemini, all VLMs used in the evaluation are public models for research purpose. LLaVA, OpenCLIP and their inference codes can be accessed at

and

Running time.
The inference time for each sample is approximately 7.9 seconds using the LV model with a maximum output length of 512 tokens, including image loading and tokenizing time, on a single Tesla V100 GPU with 32GB RAM. The inference times for Gemini and GPT-4o are subject to the status of their respective service providers. Nevertheless, these models typically complete processing in under 5 seconds per sample, which includes the latency associated with data transmission over the internet.
