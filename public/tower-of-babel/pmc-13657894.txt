Bioelectrical impedance-derived body composition and systemic therapy outcomes in non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Xin Zheng, Rui Gao, Boao Jiang, Yihai Wei, Jingwen Diao, Bo Cheng, Jianfu Li, Jingting Su, Yuejiao Su, Wenhai Fu, Peiling Chen, Xianzhe Fan, Wenhua Liang, Ying Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBody mass index (BMI) cannot distinguish skeletal muscle from adipose tissue or capture fat distribution in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). We explored whether bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA)-assessed body composition was associated with systemic therapy outcomes.MethodsConsecutive adults with confirmed NSCLC who underwent pretreatment multifrequency BIA between March 2023 and March 2025 were retrospectively included. Analyses were performed by treatment context, including stage I surgical patients and stage II–IV patients receiving immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)- or epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitor (EGFR-TKI)-based systemic therapy. Body-composition parameters were compared by clinical stage and EGFR mutation status using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). Progression-free survival (PFS) was analyzed using Kaplan-Meier methods and Cox proportional hazards models. Objective response rate (ORR) was evaluated using logistic regression. Joint analyses were performed according to skeletal muscle index (SMI) and visceral fat index (VFI), and longitudinal body-composition changes during immunotherapy were also assessed.ResultsAmong 473 screened patients, 369 were included, comprising 126 with stage I disease and 243 with stage II–IV disease. After predefined exclusions, outcome cohorts included 125 ICI-treated and 43 EGFR-TKI-treated patients. In cross-sectional analyses, adiposity-related parameters were nominally lower in stage II–IV disease than in stage I disease, but these differences did not remain significant after false discovery rate (FDR) correction; no body-composition parameter differed significantly by EGFR mutation status. Among 94 men included in the primary ICI PFS analysis, high SMI was associated with longer PFS [adjusted hazard ratio (HR), 0.49; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.25–0.97], whereas high VFI was associated with shorter PFS (adjusted HR, 2.11; 95% CI: 1.09–4.07). High SMI was also associated with higher ORR among these men [adjusted odds ratio (OR), 3.04; 95% CI: 1.20–7.72]. The combined SMI/VFI phenotype further stratified PFS, with low SMI/high VFI identifying the least favorable subgroup. In an exploratory longitudinal analysis, a >10% decrease in trunk fat mass was associated with shorter PFS. No clear associations between body-composition parameters and PFS were observed in the small EGFR-TKI cohort.ConclusionsBIA-assessed skeletal muscle reserve and visceral adiposity were associated with outcomes during ICI-based therapy in men with NSCLC. Higher SMI and lower VFI identified a more favorable clinical profile. Prospective validation is warranted to define the role of BIA-based body-composition assessment in host-related risk stratification during systemic therapy.Keywords: Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), body composition, bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA), immunotherapy, epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitor (EGFR-TKI)

Highlight box

Key findings

• Among men with non-small cell lung cancer receiving first-line immune checkpoint inhibitor-based therapy, bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA)-derived skeletal muscle index (SMI) and visceral fat index (VFI) were associated with progression-free survival (PFS).

• Higher SMI was associated with longer PFS and higher objective response rate, whereas higher VFI was associated with shorter PFS.

• A combined SMI/VFI phenotype further stratified outcomes, and, in an exploratory longitudinal analysis, a greater than 10% decrease in trunk fat mass was associated with shorter PFS.

What is known and what is new?

• Body mass index (BMI) is commonly used in oncology but cannot distinguish skeletal muscle from adipose tissue or capture regional fat distribution. Prior studies suggest that sarcopenia, obesity, and visceral adiposity may influence lung cancer outcomes.

• This study adds evidence that noninvasive BIA may capture clinically relevant host body-composition phenotypes beyond BMI.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Bioelectrical impedance-derived body-composition profiling may help identify patients who warrant closer nutritional, metabolic, or clinical monitoring during systemic therapy.

• These findings are exploratory and should not be used to guide treatment selection yet. Prospective multicenter validation with standardized measurement protocols and prespecified cutoffs is required.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains a leading cause of cancer morbidity and mortality worldwide, and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for most lung cancer diagnoses (1). During the past decade, systemic treatment has changed substantially with the introduction of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and molecularly targeted therapy, including pembrolizumab for programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)-positive NSCLC and tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) for actionable oncogenic alterations (2-4). Nevertheless, survival remains heterogeneous even among patients with similar tumor stage and molecular profiles, indicating that host-related factors may contribute to treatment response and disease progression (5).

Obesity and metabolic status have therefore attracted increasing attention in lung cancer research. Body mass index (BMI) is widely used because it is simple and standardized, and international definitions classify BMI below 18.5 kg/m2 as underweight, 18.5–24.9 kg/m2 as normal weight, and 25 kg/m2 or higher as overweight (6). Obesity is a major public health problem, with increasing weight gain and projected obesity burden reported in population-level studies (7,8). However, the relationship between BMI and lung cancer is not linear. Epidemiologic studies and meta-analyses have reported inverse associations between BMI and lung cancer risk in some settings, and clinical studies have described better outcomes among overweight or obese patients with NSCLC, including those receiving definitive treatment, first-line pembrolizumab, or epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitor (EGFR-TKI) therapy (9-16).

These observations are often described as an obesity paradox, but BMI cannot explain the underlying biology. BMI does not distinguish adipose tissue from lean tissue, does not quantify regional fat distribution, and may misclassify patients with low muscle mass, visceral obesity, or sarcopenic obesity (17,18). Mechanistically, adiposity may influence tumor progression and treatment response through chronic inflammation, altered adipokine signaling, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, T-cell dysfunction, and programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)-related immune modulation (19-23). Importantly, central obesity and body shape appear to carry biological information beyond total body size, and visceral obesity has been implicated in lung cancer progression and the tumor immune microenvironment (14,18,24).

Body composition assessment offers a more granular approach to host phenotyping. Low skeletal muscle mass has been associated with inferior outcomes after NSCLC surgery, whereas fat mass, fat-free mass, and regional body composition patterns have shown prognostic relevance in population and disease-specific cohorts (25-27). Sex may further modify these associations, because adiposity-related cancer risk and adipose tissue biology differ between men and women (28). These data support the need to evaluate muscle and adipose compartments separately rather than using BMI as the sole host phenotype. A recent systematic review of ICI-treated NSCLC similarly found that sarcopenia, cachexia, and pretreatment weight loss were more consistently associated with clinical outcomes than BMI alone (29).

Several tools are available for body composition assessment, including computed tomography (CT), dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), and bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) (30-32). CT and DXA can provide quantitative estimates of tissue compartments but are less convenient for repeated monitoring in routine oncology practice. BIA is noninvasive, low-cost, repeatable, and feasible in outpatient or bedside settings; prior studies have supported its use for estimating muscle and fat compartments, although standardization and validation against CT remain important (30,33-35). On this basis, we conducted an exploratory retrospective study to characterize BIA-derived muscle and adiposity parameters in NSCLC and to evaluate their associations with progression-free survival (PFS) and objective response rate (ORR) in patients receiving immunotherapy or EGFR-TKI therapy. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tlcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tlcr-2026-0718/rc).

Methods

Study design and patients

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (No. ES-2024-110-02). The requirement for written informed consent was waived because this retrospective study used de-identified clinical data, involved no additional intervention, and did not affect patient management. This single-center, retrospective observational study enrolled consecutive adults with histologically or cytologically confirmed NSCLC who underwent baseline multifrequency BIA before initiation of antitumor treatment at The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University between March 2023 and March 2025. Clinical stage was assigned according to the ninth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer/Union for International Cancer Control (AJCC/UICC) tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) classification.

For cross-sectional stage-based comparisons, clinical stage was prespecified as a binary variable (stage I vs. stages II–IV). Stage I was considered to represent early-stage disease, whereas stages II–IV were considered to reflect greater disease burden and greater relevance to systemic therapy. The study flow is shown in Figure 1.

Study flow diagram. EGFR, epidermal growth factor receptor; NSCLC, non-small cell lung cancer; TKI, tyrosine kinase inhibitor.

Treatment cohorts

Outcome analyses were conducted in two prespecified stage II–IV systemic-therapy cohorts: patients receiving first-line ICI monotherapy or ICI plus chemotherapy, and patients with EGFR-mutant NSCLC receiving first-line EGFR-TKI monotherapy or EGFR-TKI plus chemotherapy.

Body-composition assessment

Body composition was assessed using a multifrequency BIA device (BC care860), which estimates fat mass, lean body mass, skeletal muscle mass, visceral fat area, and fluid compartments. BIA was selected because it is noninvasive and feasible for repeated assessment in routine clinical practice. Body-composition measurements were obtained at baseline before treatment initiation. For the longitudinal ICI analysis, eligible follow-up BIA measurements were those obtained 84–105 days after treatment initiation, generally corresponding to assessment after four 3-week treatment cycles and allowing up to one additional cycle for delayed reassessment. Serial BIA was not protocol-mandated and was performed as part of routine clinical practice. Patients were included if paired baseline and eligible follow-up measurements were available and radiographic disease progression had not occurred before follow-up BIA. For outcome analyses, BMI was evaluated both continuously and dichotomized as <25 vs. ≥25 kg/m2, as specified. Fat and muscle mass indices were calculated by normalizing the corresponding mass by height squared.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was PFS. For patients receiving systemic therapy, PFS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to radiographic disease progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. The secondary endpoint was ORR, defined as the proportion of response-evaluable patients who achieved a complete or partial response according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST), version 1.1. Disease progression and response were assessed by radiologists or treating clinicians.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as means ± standard deviations (SD) or median (range), while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Between-group differences in body-composition variables by clinical stage and EGFR mutation status were evaluated using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). Prior to the ANCOVA, the model assumptions were tested for normality of residuals and homoscedasticity using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. Both assumptions were met.

Sex-specific differences in body composition were considered in the outcome analyses of the immunotherapy cohort. Because few women had evaluable outcome data, both the PFS and ORR analyses were restricted to the same 94 male patients. Optimal cutoffs for body-composition variables were derived from PFS in these 94 men using the surv_cutpoint function in the R package survminer and were applied unchanged to the ORR analysis; no ORR-specific cutoff optimization was performed. PFS was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier methods and Cox proportional hazards models, whereas ORR was evaluated using logistic regression. Multivariable Cox and logistic regression models in the ICI cohort were adjusted for age, smoking history, clinical stage, and histologic subtype.

Paired changes in all 49 BIA-derived body-composition parameters were assessed using paired-sample t-tests, with P values adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Relative change was calculated as [(follow-up value − baseline value)/baseline value] × 100%. Variables included in the longitudinal survival analysis were categorized as a >10% decrease vs. no >10% decrease (reference), with the reference group including decreases of ≤10%, no change, and increases. Associations with PFS were evaluated using univariable Cox proportional hazards models; trunk fat mass was additionally evaluated in a multivariable model adjusted for age, sex, smoking history, clinical stage, and histologic subtype.

Because the EGFR-TKI cohort was small and included both men and women, body-composition parameters were analyzed as continuous variables after standardization within sex rather than using outcome-derived cutoffs. Separate Cox proportional hazards models were fitted for each parameter and adjusted for sex, with hazard ratios (HRs) expressed per 1 sex-specific SD increase.

HRs or odds ratios (ORs), as appropriate, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.5.0 and SPSS version 26.0.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 49 body-composition parameters were assessed, including indices of fat mass, muscle mass, body fat percentage, waist-to-hip ratio, and other proportional measures. Total fat and muscle mass were normalized by height squared. Overall, 369 patients were included: 126 patients (34.1%) with stage I resectable NSCLC and 243 patients (65.9%) with stage II–IV unresectable NSCLC. The median age was 55.5 years in the stage I group and 64 years in the stage II–IV group. Males accounted for 37.3% of patients with stage I disease and 71.6% of those with stage II–IV disease. Adenocarcinoma was the most common histologic subtype, accounting for 95.2% of stage I tumors and 59.3% of stage II–IV tumors. Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Driver-alteration categories were mutually exclusive for tabulation, and no co-occurring driver alterations were identified in the analyzed cohort. Among patients with stage II–IV disease, baseline characteristics were further tabulated by systemic treatment cohort to contextualize the subsequent immunotherapy and EGFR-TKI analyses (Table 2).

Body composition by clinical stage and EGFR mutation status

Body-composition parameters were compared between patients with stage I disease and those with stage II–IV disease using ANCOVA adjusted for age and sex. Compared with patients with stage I disease, patients with stage II–IV disease had lower body fat mass, body fat percentage, visceral fat area, trunk fat mass, body fat mass index, visceral fat index (VFI), and trunk fat mass index. However, none of these differences remained statistically significant after Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction. No significant between-group differences were observed for water, protein, muscle mass, skeletal muscle mass, fat-free mass, or extracellular water-related parameters (Table S1).

Within each clinical-stage subgroup, body-composition parameters were also compared between patients with EGFR-mutant and EGFR-wild-type tumors after adjustment for age and sex. In both the stage I and stage II–IV subgroups, no body-composition parameter differed significantly according to EGFR mutation status, either before or after FDR correction (Tables S2,S3).

Correlation of body-composition parameters

Pairwise correlation analyses were performed to assess potential collinearity among BIA-derived body-composition variables. Muscle-related parameters and adiposity-related parameters showed strong within-domain correlations, whereas correlations between the muscle and adiposity domains were weak to moderate. Similar correlation patterns were observed across sex and clinical-stage strata; therefore, representative variables from each body-composition domain were selected for subsequent analyses (Figure S1).

Body composition and PFS in the immunotherapy cohort

The immunotherapy outcome cohort included 125 patients with stage II–IV NSCLC who received first-line ICI-based therapy. Because of sex-specific differences in body composition and the limited number of women with evaluable follow-up, the primary PFS analysis was restricted to men. A total of 94 men were included in the primary PFS analysis. The median follow-up duration was 9 months (range, 6–26 months), and 39 PFS events occurred (41.4%), comprising 33 radiographic progressions and 6 deaths without prior documented progression.

To evaluate the prognostic impact of body composition, variables were dichotomized using PFS-derived optimal cutoffs determined exclusively among the 94 men included in the primary ICI PFS analysis (Table S4). In univariable Cox regression, higher skeletal muscle index (SMI; HR =0.49, 95% CI: 0.26–0.94; P=0.03) and higher soft lean mass index (SLMI; HR =0.48, 95% CI: 0.24–0.96; P=0.04) were associated with longer PFS. Conversely, higher visceral fat area (VFA; HR =2.39, 95% CI: 1.26–4.52; P=0.008) and higher VFI (HR =2.09, 95% CI: 1.11–3.93; P=0.02) were associated with a higher risk of a PFS event.

Variables associated with PFS in univariable analysis (P<0.05) were screened for multicollinearity using VIFs, and variables with a VIF greater than 4 were excluded. In multivariable Cox regression adjusted for age, smoking history, clinical stage, and histologic subtype, higher SMI was associated with longer PFS (HR =0.49, 95% CI: 0.25–0.97; P=0.04), whereas higher VFI remained independently associated with shorter PFS (HR =2.11, 95% CI: 1.09–4.07; P=0.03). Age, BMI, histologic subtype, and smoking status were not significantly associated with PFS (all P>0.05; Table S5).

Kaplan-Meier analysis showed that patients with higher SMI (P=0.02) and higher SLMI (P=0.02) had longer PFS than those with lower values. Patients with lower VFA (P=0.005) and lower VFI (P=0.02) also had longer PFS than patients with higher visceral fat levels (Figure 2).

Based on their biological complementarity and their independent associations with PFS in multivariable Cox models, SMI and VFI were combined to define four body-composition phenotypes. Patients were further stratified into four groups according to SMI and VFI: low SMI/high VFI, low SMI/low VFI, high SMI/high VFI, and high SMI/low VFI. The low SMI/high VFI group had the shortest PFS, whereas the high SMI/low VFI group had the longest PFS, with an apparent risk gradient across the four groups (Figure 3).

Using the low SMI/high VFI group as the reference, univariable Cox analysis showed that the low SMI/low VFI group (HR =0.25, 95% CI: 0.10–0.65; P=0.004), high SMI/high VFI group (HR =0.24, 95% CI: 0.08–0.66; P=0.006), and high SMI/low VFI group (HR =0.18, 95% CI: 0.07–0.48; P=0.001) were associated with longer PFS (Table S6). After adjustment for age, smoking history, clinical stage, and histologic subtype, the high SMI/low VFI group retained the most favorable prognosis (HR =0.13, 95% CI: 0.04–0.38; P<0.001), corresponding to an 87% lower risk of a PFS event than the reference group. High SMI also remained protective among patients with elevated visceral fat (high SMI/high VFI vs. low SMI/high VFI: HR =0.29, 95% CI: 0.08–0.99; P=0.05). Among the 39 PFS events, 33 (84.6%) were radiographic progressions and 6 (15.4%) were deaths without prior documented progression. In the low SMI/high VFI group, 11 PFS events occurred, comprising 8 radiographic progressions and 3 deaths without prior documented progression. The corresponding progression/death-without-prior-progression event counts were 10/0 in the low SMI/low VFI group, 6/1 in the high SMI/high VFI group, and 9/2 in the high SMI/low VFI group. Among the 32 patients with high VFI, 24 (75.0%) had a BMI <25 kg/m2; this proportion was 17 of 18 (94.4%) in the low SMI/high VFI group.

Body composition and ORR

ORR was evaluated in the same 94 male patients included in the primary ICI PFS analysis. Among these patients, the ORR was 43.6%. The PFS-derived body-composition cutoffs were applied unchanged to the ORR analysis.

In univariable logistic regression, higher SMI was associated with higher ORR (OR, 2.41; 95% CI: 1.03–5.65; P=0.04), whereas VFI was not significantly associated with ORR (OR, 0.46; 95% CI: 0.19–1.12; P=0.09). After adjustment for age, smoking history, clinical stage, and histologic subtype, higher SMI remained associated with higher ORR (adjusted OR, 3.04; 95% CI: 1.20–7.72; P=0.02; Table S7).

When patients were stratified by the four SMI/VFI groups, all three groups had higher ORR than the low SMI/high VFI reference group in univariable analyses (Table S8). These associations persisted after adjustment for age, smoking history, clinical stage, and histologic subtype, with adjusted ORs of 6.48 (95% CI: 1.33–31.54; P=0.02) for low SMI/low VFI, 6.80 (95% CI: 1.31–35.23; P=0.02) for high SMI/high VFI, and 15.90 (95% CI: 2.95–85.63; P=0.001) for high SMI/low VFI.

Dynamic changes in body composition during immunotherapy

Among the 125 patients in the ICI outcome cohort, 73 underwent repeat BIA, whereas 52 did not. Among the 73 patients with repeat BIA measurements, 32 were excluded because the follow-up assessment fell outside the specified reassessment window, and two were excluded because radiographic disease progression occurred before reassessment. The remaining 39 patients (31.2%) had eligible paired measurements and comprised the longitudinal cohort. All 49 BIA-derived body-composition parameters were evaluated longitudinally and are reported in Table S9. After Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, significant increases were observed in body fat mass, BMI, percent body fat, waist-to-hip ratio, trunk fat mass, fat mass index, and trunk fat mass index (all FDR-adjusted P≤0.021). The proportions of upper-limb and trunk muscle mass relative to total muscle mass also increased significantly (FDR-adjusted P=0.02 and 0.02, respectively). Visceral fat area and VFI showed nominal increases but did not remain significant after FDR correction (both FDR-adjusted P=0.10). No significant changes were observed in absolute lean- or muscle-mass measures, including protein content, soft lean mass, fat-free mass, skeletal muscle mass, or regional muscle mass, or in fluid-related parameters. In the exploratory analysis, a >10% decrease in trunk fat mass vs. no >10% decrease was associated with shorter PFS in univariable analysis (HR, 2.06; 95% CI: 1.15–3.70; P=0.02) and after adjustment for age, sex, smoking history, clinical stage, and histologic subtype (adjusted HR, 4.56; 95% CI: 1.39–14.97; P=0.01). No other evaluated body-composition change was significantly associated with PFS (Table S10).

Body composition in patients receiving EGFR-TKI-based therapy

A total of 43 patients with stage II–IV NSCLC receiving EGFR-TKI-based therapy were included, comprising 16 men (37.2%) and 27 women (62.8%). The median follow-up duration was 9 months (range, 6–24 months), during which 13 PFS events occurred. Given the limited sample size and mixed-sex composition of this cohort, body-composition parameters were analyzed as continuous variables after standardization within sex rather than dichotomized using outcome-derived cutoffs. No body-composition parameter was significantly associated with PFS in the sex-adjusted Cox models. For SMI, the sex-adjusted HR per 1 sex-specific SD increase was 0.96 (95% CI: 0.50–1.82; P=0.89), and for VFI, it was 1.06 (95% CI: 0.59–1.87; P=0.85) (Table S11).

Discussion

In this single-center retrospective cohort of patients with NSCLC who underwent baseline BIA before antitumor treatment, body-composition parameters showed clinically relevant associations with outcomes, particularly in patients with stage II–IV disease receiving first-line ICI-based therapy. Higher SMI was associated with longer PFS and higher ORR, whereas higher VFI was associated with shorter PFS. The combined SMI/VFI phenotype further separated patients into distinct risk groups, with the low SMI/high VFI group having the poorest outcomes and the high SMI/low VFI group having the most favorable outcomes. These findings support the potential prognostic value of host body composition in ICI-treated NSCLC.

The stage- and EGFR-based cross-sectional analyses provide context for interpreting the outcome findings. Although several adiposity-related measures were nominally lower in stage II–IV disease than in stage I disease, these differences did not remain significant after FDR correction. In addition, no body-composition parameter differed significantly by EGFR mutation status within either stage subgroup. Thus, the associations observed in the systemic-therapy cohorts were unlikely to be explained solely by broad baseline differences across clinical stage or EGFR-defined subgroups.

The association between higher SMI and improved outcomes is biologically plausible. SMI reflects height-normalized skeletal muscle reserve and may capture nutritional status, systemic inflammation, functional reserve, and treatment tolerance more effectively than BMI. Cancer cachexia is characterized by ongoing skeletal muscle loss, with or without fat loss, and is associated with functional decline and adverse outcomes (36,37). Low muscle mass has also been associated with poorer survival and treatment-related toxicity across solid tumors (38-40). In patients with NSCLC receiving ICIs, prior studies and meta-analyses have reported associations between low muscle mass or sarcopenia and inferior survival (29,41-44). More recent evidence reinforces this relationship: in a recent cohort of advanced NSCLC, CT-defined sarcopenia was associated with inferior PFS and OS among patients receiving first-line chemoimmunotherapy (45), and a prospective study using BIA found shorter PFS and OS among patients with sarcopenia or pre-sarcopenia before first-line ICI therapy (46). Our findings are consistent with these data and suggest that BIA-derived SMI may be a feasible marker of host fitness in routine practice.

Higher VFI remained associated with shorter PFS after adjustment, underscoring the importance of fat distribution rather than body size alone. BMI cannot distinguish muscle, subcutaneous fat, and visceral adipose tissue, which may explain the lack of association between BMI and PFS in this study. This distinction may be particularly relevant in Asian populations, in whom conventional BMI-based definitions can under-recognize excess visceral and ectopic adiposity at lower body weights (47). In our male ICI cohort, 24 of 32 patients (75.0%) with high VFI had a BMI <25 kg/m2, including 17 of 18 patients (94.4%) with the low SMI/high VFI phenotype. Thus, elevated VFI in this cohort largely reflected disproportionate visceral adiposity that was not captured by BMI alone. Visceral adipose tissue is metabolically active and may contribute to chronic low-grade inflammation, insulin resistance, altered adipokine signaling, and immune dysregulation (48). However, the relationship between adiposity and ICI outcomes is not uniform. Higher BMI has been associated with improved outcomes in some ICI-treated NSCLC cohorts, whereas CT-based studies evaluating visceral adiposity have reported heterogeneous findings (44,49-51). Differences in patient population, treatment line, sex distribution, body-composition methods, and cutoff definitions may account for these discrepancies.

The combined SMI/VFI analysis may provide a more integrated assessment of body-composition risk than either measure alone. SMI and VFI represent distinct biological domains: skeletal muscle reserve and visceral adiposity burden. The particularly unfavorable low SMI/high VFI phenotype may therefore reflect the coexistence of reduced muscle reserve and disproportionate visceral adiposity rather than simply lower overall body mass. This pattern further supports discordance between BMI and visceral adiposity in this subgroup. Consistent with the broader concept that combined muscle depletion and excess adiposity may confer higher risk, a recent study of ICI-treated lung cancer reported poorer survival among patients with sarcopenic obesity than among those with sarcopenia or obesity alone, although its BMI-based definition differs from the SMI/VFI phenotype used here (52).

Because PFS was defined as radiographic progression or death from any cause, we also examined the composition of PFS events. Radiographic progression accounted for 33 of 39 PFS events overall and 8 of 11 events in the low SMI/high VFI group, indicating that the unfavorable PFS observed in this phenotype was not driven solely by death events. Nevertheless, because cause-specific mortality was not adjudicated, a contribution from competing non-oncologic mortality cannot be excluded. Accordingly, the association between high VFI and shorter PFS should not be attributed exclusively to tumor-immune mechanisms. Because the SMI and VFI cutoffs were derived and evaluated within the same cohort without internal resampling or external validation, the observed effect estimates may be subject to optimism bias. These data-derived thresholds should therefore be considered exploratory and require validation in independent cohorts using prespecified cutoffs.

Dynamic BIA assessment may provide information beyond baseline measurements. Although several adiposity-related measures increased on average, a subset of patients experienced marked tissue loss, and the exploratory analysis showed that a >10% decrease in trunk fat mass was associated with shorter PFS. This directional analysis was informed by longitudinal studies in ICI-treated NSCLC linking on-treatment muscle or adipose-tissue loss with adverse outcomes. A 10% relative decline in CT-derived psoas muscle area has been used to define substantial muscle loss (53), and a recent study further evaluated treatment-related changes in muscle and adipose tissue during nivolumab therapy (54). Trunk fat loss may be a marker of systemic catabolism, reduced nutritional intake, treatment-related toxicity, or disease-related deterioration rather than a causal effect of fat depletion itself. However, the 10% threshold has not been validated for BIA-derived trunk fat mass, serial BIA was not protocol-mandated, and only 39 patients had eligible paired measurements. The longitudinal analysis was therefore limited to a selected subgroup and may be subject to selection bias. These findings should therefore be considered exploratory and require prospective validation with standardized serial assessments.

In the EGFR-TKI cohort, no clear association between sex-standardized body-composition parameters and PFS was observed in sex-adjusted models. These findings should be interpreted cautiously given the small cohort (43 patients and 13 PFS events). EGFR mutation subtype and TKI generation or agent were not incorporated into the survival models and may contribute to residual confounding, while longitudinal BIA measurements were not systematically available. Moreover, the median follow-up of 9 months indicates limited outcome maturity. Larger cohorts with longer follow-up and serial body-composition assessment are needed to clarify the prognostic relevance of body composition in EGFR-TKI-treated NSCLC.

BIA offers a practical means of jointly characterizing skeletal muscle reserve and visceral adiposity (30,33,35), two complementary host domains that are not distinguished by BMI (17,18). In the male ICI cohort, SMI and VFI showed opposing associations with PFS, their combined phenotype further stratified outcomes, and serial BIA enabled exploratory assessment of on-treatment body-composition changes. These findings, together with emerging evidence on the prognostic relevance of body composition in ICI-treated NSCLC (29,44), support further evaluation of BIA as a pragmatic tool for host phenotyping and longitudinal body-composition assessment in NSCLC. The main limitations include the single-center retrospective design, limited sample size and event number, restriction of the primary ICI outcome analyses to men, potential BIA measurement variability related to hydration and measurement conditions, and residual treatment heterogeneity.

Conclusions

Among men with NSCLC receiving ICI-based therapy, BIA-derived SMI and VFI were associated with treatment outcomes, whereas BMI was not associated with PFS. Higher SMI was associated with longer PFS and higher ORR, whereas lower VFI was associated with longer PFS. In the exploratory longitudinal analysis, a >10% decrease in trunk fat mass was associated with shorter PFS. These findings provide preliminary evidence that BIA-based body-composition profiling may support host-related risk stratification and longitudinal monitoring during NSCLC systemic therapy.
