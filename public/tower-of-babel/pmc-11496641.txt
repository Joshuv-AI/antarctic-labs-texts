Impact of arterial system alterations due to amputation on arterial stiffness and hemodynamics: a numerical study
Authors: Hasan Obeid, Vasiliki Bikia, Patrick Segers, Mathilde Pare, Pierre Boutouyrie, Nikos Stergiopulos, Mohsen Agharazii
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSubjects with amputation of the lower limbs are at increased risk of cardiovascular mortality and morbidity. We hypothesize that amputation-induced alterations in the arterial tree negatively impact arterial biomechanics, blood pressure and flow behavior. These changes may interact with other biological factors, potentially increasing cardiovascular risk. To evaluate this hypothesis regarding the purely mechanical impact of amputation on the arterial tree, we used a simulation computer model including a detailed one-dimensional (1D) arterial network model (143 arterial segments) coupled with a zero-dimensional (0D) model of the left ventricle. Our simulations included five settings of the arterial network: (1) 4-limbs control, (2) unilateral amputee (right lower limb), (3) bilateral amputee (both lower limbs), (4) trilateral amputee (lower-limbs and right upper-limb), and (5) quadrilateral amputee (lower and upper limbs). Analysis of regional stiffness, as calculated by pulse wave velocity (PWV) for large-, medium- and small-sized arteries, showed that, while aortic stiffness did not change with increasing degree of amputation, stiffness of medium and smaller-sized arteries increased with greater amputation severity. Despite a staged decrease in cardiac output, the systolic and diastolic blood pressure values increased, resulting in an increase in both central and peripheral pulse pressures but with an attenuation of pulse pressure amplification. The most significant increase in peak systolic pressure and decrease in peak systolic blood flow was observed at the site of the abdominal aorta. Wave separation analysis indicated no changes in the shape of the forward and backward wave components. However, the results from wave intensity analysis showed that with extended amputation, there was an increase in peak forward wave intensity and a rise in the inverse peak of the backward wave intensity, suggesting potential alterations in cardiac hemodynamic load. In conclusion, this simulation study showed that biomechanical and hemodynamic changes in the arterial network geometry could interact with additional risk factors to increase the cardiovascular risk in patients with amputations.Keywords: Pulse wave velocity, 1-D modelling, Arterial tree, Limb amputees, Wave separation analysis, Wave intensity analysisSubject terms: Computational science, Computational models

Introduction

Subjects with post-traumatic amputation of the lower limbs are at increased risk of cardiovascular mortality and morbidity1,2. Indeed, several studies have demonstrated that the relative risk of death due to causes is higher in bilateral than in unilateral lower limb amputees3,4. This increased cardiovascular risk may be related to an ensemble of psychological stressors, reduced physical activity, unhealthy lifestyle, and other neurometabolic dysregulations such as insulin resistance and altered sympathetic activity5.

High blood pressure and increased arterial stiffness are prevalent in this population, but it is unclear whether this is related to the metabolic risk factors or purely biomechanical alterations in the arterial tree. Amputees have modified arterial trees due to limb loss, which could result in changes in blood flow, blood pressure, arterial diameter, and arterial wall structure, all potentially affecting arterial stiffness6. Generally, increasing arterial stiffness leads to increased systolic and pulse pressures, increased cardiac workload, and reduced coronary perfusion pressure. Arterial stiffness, as measured by pulse wave velocity (PWV), is a well-established independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease7–10, but its impact on amputees remains poorly understood. Studies also suggest that lower limb amputation is associated with increased risk of abdominal aortic aneurysm3. Investigating the intrinsic effect of biomechanical alterations caused by limb amputation on arterial stiffness, hemodynamics, and wave reflection is critical as it may provide insights into the pathophysiology of cardiovascular disease in this population11.

While previous studies have examined the effect of lower limb amputation level on aortic hemodynamics12 and wall shear stress13, neither of these methods offer both temporal and quantitative insights into energy exchange between pulsatile blood flow and the arterial wall. A promising approach for gaining deeper understanding in this area is wave intensity analysis (WIA)14. This method, grounded in a comprehensive treatment of fundamental fluid flow equations using the method of characteristics without assumptions of periodicity or linearity, centers on the concept that the cardiac cycle involves the propagation of minuscule wavefronts characterized by alterations in pressure (P) and velocity (U)15. Through time domain analysis of P and U waveforms, it allows for the computation of the product of changes in P and U, referred to as “wave intensity,” providing a snapshot of the instantaneous energy conveyed by the wavefront16. By dissecting the individual components of forward and backward waves, it provides crucial insights into the hemodynamic properties and vascular function, aiding in the diagnosis and management of cardiovascular conditions.

To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has investigated PWV/hemodynamics in the setting of different levels of amputations. Thus, the aim of the present study was to use a numerical simulation model that examines the impact of amputation on blood pressure and flow waveforms, as well as arterial stiffness, without confounding metabolic, psychosocial, and behavioral determinants of cardiovascular health. We hypothesized that hemodynamic parameters are altered with increasing severity of amputation, leading to biomechanical alterations that influence the whole arterial system.

Materials and methods

1D arterial network model – topology of the control and amputation cases

We used a human 1D arterial tree model and adapted it to create settings that represent different stages of upper and lower limbs amputees.

The in-silico 1-D cardiovascular model adopted in this study has been previously described by Reymond et al.17. This model provides physiological, realistic pressure and flow waveforms by solving one-dimensional (1D) Navier-Stokes equations, while accounting for non-linearities18. The arterial tree (103 segments) includes the main arteries of the systemic circulation, including a detailed network representation of the cerebral circulation and the coronary circulation. The arterial segments of the model are considered as long tapered tubes, and their compliance is defined by a nonlinear function of pressure and location19. The arterial wall behavior is considered to be non-linear and viscoelastic20. The governing equations of the model are acquired via integration of the longitudinal momentum and continuity of the Navier-Stokes equations over the arterial cross section. Given proper boundary conditions, flow and pressure waves are obtained at all locations of the systemic arterial tree, by solving the governing equations using an implicit finite-difference scheme. Local arterial compliance is computed by approximating pulse wave velocity as an inverse power function of arterial lumen diameter17. The resistance of the distal vasculature is considered by coupling the terminal vessels with three-element Windkessel models21,22. More details on the 1D model can be found in the original publications17,23. This model has been thoroughly validated against in-vivo data and was found to be capable of providing accurate pressure and flow waves, particularly with respect to shape and wave details. The arterial topology of the initial model, as described above, was extended by Obeid et al.24 to account for more distal arterial segments including hand and foot topologies (radial and tibial arteries), combined and terminated with three-element Windkessel models. The extended version consists of 143 segments (Fig. 1- i).

Boundary conditions

At its proximal end, the arterial tree is coupled with a time-varying elastance model for the contractility of the left ventricle25. Parameters were chosen such that a cardiac output value equal to 5 L/min was generated for the control case. The cardiac parameters (namely filling pressure, heart rate, end-systolic and end-diastolic elastance) were kept constant for the amputation cases, such that the dynamic interaction of the normal heart with the altered arterial tree would determine cardiac output and input into the model, rather than a hard prescribed inlet boundary condition. Impact of amputation on arterial stiffness

Central stiffness was assessed via carotid-femoral PWV (cfPWV), aortic PWV (aPWV), aortic-carotid PWV (acPWV) and ileofemoral PWV (ifPWV), while peripheral stiffness was measured using the carotid-radial PWV (crPWV)26, and radial-digital PWV (rdPWV)27. It’s noteworthy that peripheral PWV cannot be calculated for quadruple amputees. Inputting different settings into the in-silico model, we ran numerical simulations designed to mimic different stages of amputation and examined the variations in PWV, hemodynamics, wave reflections and impedance values at the different arterial regions. Considering that alterations in PWV affect the arterial pressure waveform, we employed wave separation and intensity analysis (forward and backward pressure waves) within a comprehensive examination of the arterial system, integrating measurements of central pressure and flow waveforms28,29. Finally, we performed a more in-depth analysis of abdominal aortic hemodynamics in relation to lower limb amputation, as these analysis could yield novel insight into the mechanisms of development of abdominal aortic aneurysm.

The cfPWV, aPWV, acPWV, ifPWV, crPWV, and rdPWV were determined by measuring the foot-to-foot pulse transit time (PTT) between the passage of the pressure signals at the carotid artery and the femoral artery (cfPTT), the ascending aorta and the abdominal aorta (aPTT), the ascending aorta and the carotid artery (acPTT), the abdominal aorta and the femoral artery (ifPTT), the carotid artery and the radial artery (crPTT), and the radial artery and proper palmar digital artery (rdPTT), respectively. The foot-to-foot PTT algorithm used the maximum second derivative, and was implemented in Matlab (Mathworks, Natick, Massachusetts, United States). This approach was chosen based on the consideration that, for numerical signals, the level of noise is negligible, thereby ensuring that the results remain independent of the algorithm employed. This method uses the time point where the maximum second derivative occurs to calculate the foot of the pressure wave. To ensure that the short-time delays between small arteries are captured, the sampling frequency for all the BP waveforms was set to 900 Hz, an order of magnitude significantly higher than the 100 Hz threshold value for temporal resolution suggested by30. This specific sampling frequency was selected as a fair trade-off between computational time and high signal fidelity.

The carotid-femoral distance was determined as the distance from the common carotid artery to the femoral artery, resulting in Δx_cf = 66 cm (Fig. 2-i). Given that the precise length of every arterial segment of the 1D model was known, the distance between two arterial sites was computed by summing the individual arterial lengths of all segments within the carotid-femoral path. The carotid to abdominal aortic segment length was determined as the distance from the common carotid to the abdominal aorta, resulting in Δx_ca = 45 cm (Fig. 2-ii). The aortic-carotid length was determined as the distance from the common carotid to the ascending aorta, resulting in Δx_ac = 9 cm (Fig. 2-iii). The abdominal aorta to femoral length was determined as the distance from the abdominal aorta to femoral artery, resulting in Δx_af = 21 cm (Fig. 2-iv). The carotid-radial and the radial-digital distances were determined by the summation of the lengths of the arterial segments that connected the two corresponding nodes and were found to be equal to Δx_cr = 55 cm (Fig. 2-v) and Δx_rd = 22 cm (Fig. 2-vi), respectively. Finally, the value of PWV was calculated by dividing the travel distance by the PTT.

Impact of amputation on wave reflection

Wave separation is acknowledged as the definitive technique for exploring wave phenomena, as it precisely quantifies details regarding both forward and backward waves by dissecting the pressure waveform into distinct signals using principles rooted in fluid dynamics within compliant tubes. The forward pressure component delineates the influence of systolic waves on the recorded pressure, whereas the backward pressure component characterizes the impact of early diastolic waves. Wave separation analysis31 was performed in the frequency domain. The calculation of aortic characteristic impedance involved averaging the modulus of the input impedance within the frequency range between the 3rd and the10th harmonic. By employing the methodology described in Westerhof et al., we separated the total aortic pressure waveforms into their respective forward and backward components. We quantified the magnitude and timing of the forward and backward pressure waves and defined the reflection magnitude as the ratio between the amplitude of the backward wave and that of the forward wave.

Impact of amputation on wave intensity analysis

We also performed wave intensity analysis to explore the intricacies of cardiovascular dynamics. The concept of wave intensity analysis (WIA) was pioneered by Parker et al. in 198832 as an intuitive approach within the time domain for both visualizing and quantifying arterial waves. WIA operates on the foundational principles of one-dimensional flow equations and utilizes the method of characteristics, a familiar tool within the realm of fluid dynamics, to unveil the concealed forward and backward-propagating ‘information’ that might not be readily evident when inspecting raw pressure and velocity signals33. Wave intensity (dI) is succinctly defined as the product of incremental changes in pressure (dP) and velocity (dU) over a given sampling interval, namely dI = dP×dU.

While WIA readily identifies the propagation direction of waves at a single location, distinguishing the specific impact of a wave becomes evident: a forward wave presents as a positive value, and a backward wave as negative. However, the nature of a wave’s effect on pressure—whether it triggers a pressure increase (termed a compression wave) or a decrease (a decompression wave, also known as an ‘expansion’ or ‘succion’ wave) remains ambiguous from the wave intensity signal alone. To ascertain the wave type, wave separation of the pressure waveform is necessary. A forward wave is labeled as a compression wave if it aligns with an increase in the forward pressure wave, while a backward wave is identified as a decompression wave if it aligns with a decrease in the backward pressure wave, and so forth.

With this analytical technique, we can unravel the complex interplay between arterial pressure and flow within the circulatory system under the different simulated conditions. Specifically, we first look at the frequency content of the waveforms using the fast Fourier transform which allows us to get useful information about the power spectrum. Wave intensity is then calculated as the product of the differences in pressure and flow velocity signals. Pressure and flow velocity data were then processed offline using custom-written Matlab code (Mathworks, Inc., Natick, MA, United States). More details on the model can be found in the original publication34.

Wall shear stress

The determination of wall shear stress (WSS), as outlined by Nordgaard et al., 201035 serves as a crucial metric for comprehending the dynamic forces exerted on vessel walls across diverse model settings. Essentially, heightened flow rates induce an escalation in WSS. Wall shear stress (denoted as τ) is elucidated through a composite of variables encompassing blood properties and vessel morphology, expressed by the formula τ=(v × µ)/d, where v represents flow velocity, µ denotes blood viscosity, and d signifies vessel diameter. This parameter is measured in Newton per m2 (N/m2). Typically, in normal arterial conditions, wall shear stress ranges from 1 to 7 N/m2. However, instances of elevated WSS > 7 N/m2, have been documented, particularly in severe stenosis, cardiac valves, and stents36.

Despite a reduction in cardiac output, the systolic blood pressure (SBP) was increased across all arterial sites with increasing severity of amputations. A greater increase was reported between the control and unilateral settings. Meanwhile, diastolic blood pressure (DBP) was increased to a greater extent in simulations of more severe amputation. The concomitant changes in SBP and DBP led to an augmentation in pulse pressure (PP). Notably, aortic SBP was increased by 23% between control and quadrilateral settings, while aortic DBP increased by 17%, leading to a 31% increase in aortic PP. At the brachial artery, the magnitude of change was smaller, namely SBP was reported to be 143 mmHg for control, 145 mmHg for unilateral, 148 mmHg for bilateral, 152 mmHg for trilateral, and 156 mmHg for quadrilateral, respectively. Meanwhile, brachial DBP remained almost constant around 81 ± 0.9 mmHg across the different simulated scenarios. Overall, this resulted in the attenuation of SBP amplification from aorta to the brachial artery in cases of lower limb amputation.

Impact of amputation on pressure and flow waveforms

Figure 3 illustrates the pressure and flow waveforms observed at distinct arterial sites. Within the brachial and carotid arteries (depicted in Fig. 3A and B, respectively), a notable trend emerges: systolic pressure exhibits a gradual and symmetrical augmentation corresponding to the ascending level of amputation, with a notable increase in early diastolic pressure, but only minimal changes in flow velocity at the respective arterial sites. In the ascending aorta, there was also a successive increase in blood pressure waveform during systole (Fig. 3C), and a slight upshift of early DBP, without any major differences in end-diastolic BP. This was accompanied by a reduction in peak systolic flow velocity without any changes in the diastolic blood flow wave. A similar pattern of BP was observed in the thoracic aorta (Fig. 3D), however, there were successive decreases in flow velocity early in diastole, with a more pronounced increase in flow later in diastole. In the abdominal aorta, the amplitude of increase in systolic pressure was more pronounced in the bilateral amputation model, compared to more proximal sections of the aorta (Fig. 3E). However, the blood flow velocity decreased successively with unilateral and bilateral leg amputations, with only slight changes in flow velocity in the trilateral amputation setting (Fig. 3E). The shape of the flow waveform was similar in the diastolic phase of the abdominal aorta. In the femoral artery, an ipsilateral amputation resulted in a significant increase in the amplitude of systolic BP, and significant change in the shape of BP waveform (Fig. 3F), with a significant and expected decline in the femoral artery blood flow velocity. There was an additional upward shift in systolic BP waveform in the bilateral lower limb amputation model. Further addition of the upper limb amputations increased BP only slightly, without any further changes in blood flow velocity.

Sensitivity analysis

As part of sensitivity analysis, we ran additional simulations by starting with the removal of the upper limbs (removing one arm, then both arms, and so on). Our findings indicate that pulse pressures and pulse wave velocities remained unchanged regardless of the order of amputation.

Discussion

In this study, we employed a numerical model of the arterial tree to assess pulse wave velocity (PWV) in individuals with limb amputations. Our findings demonstrate a proportional increase in PWV corresponding to the number of amputated limbs, despite the absence of inherent alterations in aortic stiffness.

Limb amputations can result in several physiological and biomechanical changes37, including alterations in blood flow and in the biomechanical properties of the cardiovascular system which, can potentially modify arterial stiffness and hemodynamics. To examine this hypothesis, we used a validated model of the human arterial tree and ran numerical simulations and determined PWV, arterial pressure and flow waves at different locations in various configurations that mimic different degrees of amputation. This provided us with unique opportunities to distinguish between mechanical and biological events, which could increase the risk of cardiovascular disease in patients with amputation. As such, the study shows an increase in the functional arterial stiffness of medium-sized to small-sized arteries with increasing degree of amputations, without changes in the aortic stiffness per se. Nevertheless, despite a proportional reduction in the cardiac output, blood pressure increased, and the pulse pressure amplification decreased. In addition, the lower limb amputations resulted in more pronounced changes in the pressure and flow in the abdominal aorta, decreasing shear rate in both the aorta and iliac arteries. The model adopted in this study relies on an empirical function describing the pressure and diameter dependency of arterial compliance (Reymond et al., 2009). As a result, increased blood pressure contributes to an overall stiffness elevation (pressure-dependent effect) in both central and peripheral arteries. This effect is accentuated in lower limb amputations due to increased regional resistance. Additionally, considering the pressure-area relationship, larger (central) arteries exhibit a comparatively smaller stiffness increase, while smaller (peripheral) arteries experience a more significant rise (area-dependent effect). These combined effects result in a greater increase in peripheral stiffness indices compared to markers of central stiffness.

Limb amputations in the context of critically ischemic limbs or diabetic foot complications are associated with increased medium to long-term risk of cardiovascular events and mortality, and decreased overall survival38,39. However, it is difficult in these conditions to distinguish whether the act of amputation increases the cardiovascular risk or whether critical limb ischemia is merely a proxy for more severe vascular disease. As such, traumatic limb amputations or limb removal for other clinical condition (cancer, infection, etc.) may help to distinguish between these two possible explanations. Several studies have clearly shown that victims of traumatic limb amputations are at increased risk of aortic aneurysm, cardiovascular morbidity and mortality1–4. Nevertheless, other risk factors such as inactivity, substance abuse, psychosocial stress, obesity, insulin resistance, diabetes and lipid disorders are among other cardiovascular risk factors that are frequently encountered in patients with traumatic limb amputation1,2,40–44. Therefore, the use of a numerical simulation model of the arterial tree provides an opportunity to dissect between mechanical and biological risk factors.

Arterial stiffness is an important determinant of isolated systolic hypertension45, since physiologically, it is the aortic stiffness that has the greatest impact in increasing cardiac workload and increasing central pulse pressure. Indeed, epidemiological studies have clearly shown that aortic stiffness, as measured by cfPWV, is associated with increased cardiovascular risk. In the present study, we report an increase in cfPWV by 0.9 m/s and 0.5 m/s on the right and left side per amputated limb, respectively. This is in agreement with the higher cfPWV of 0.9 m/s on the right side in unilateral lower limb amputees in humans reported by Magalhães and colleagues46. This degree of increase in cfPWV is clinically significant. As a 1 m/s increase in cfPWV translates to a risk factor-adjusted risk increase of 14% and 15% in total cardiovascular events, cardiovascular mortality, and all-cause mortality, respectively10. Interestingly, our findings suggest that this increase is mostly related to an increase in ileo-femoral PWV rather than PWV over the aorta itself. Thus, the present study suggests that the increased cfPWV observed in the amputee population may not solely be related to aortic stiffness but at least partially explained by a hemodynamically induced ileo-femoral stiffness.

In this numerical study, brachial SBP increased by 2–3 mmHg in unilateral lower limb amputation, and up to 13–14 mmHg in the quadrilateral amputation model. In case-control studies, Magalhães and colleagues have reported that the brachial SBP was increased by 9 mmHg in unilateral leg amputees when compared to age-matched nonamputees46. Similar findings were observed by Paula-Ribeiro and colleagues with 8 mmHg higher brachial SBP in amputees47. In our simulation model, the increase in aortic SBP was more significant with an increase of 9 mm Hg in the unilateral leg amputation and it further increased to 28 mm Hg in the model of the most severe amputation. Mechanistically, higher aortic SBP has more crucial implications for the heart and target organs such as the brain and the kidneys than brachial blood pressure48. Given the reports indicating heightened arterial stiffness and blood pressure, we can infer that the rise in PWVs following amputation is likely due to the pressure and area-dependent nature of stiffness. Specifically, increased blood pressure contributes to an overall stiffness elevation (pressure-dependent effect) in both central and peripheral arteries. This effect is accentuated in lower limb amputations due to heightened resistance. Additionally, considering the pressure-area relationship, larger (central) arteries exhibit a comparatively smaller stiffness increase, while smaller (peripheral) arteries experience a more significant rise (area-dependent effect). These combined effects result in a greater increase in peripheral stiffness indices compared to markers of central stiffness.

Pressure and flow wave patterns in the present study provide additional information on the impact of changes in the arterial network. In general, the waveforms of the ascending aorta, thoracic aorta, carotid and brachial arteries showed a similar pattern of change with increasing degree of amputations of the lower and upper limbs from the arterial network, with a marked increase in early diastolic BP. However, the abdominal aorta was the site where there was a most significant increase in systolic blood pressure and the most significant decrease in peak systolic flow, especially in case of lower limb amputation models. These patterns of pressure waves along the aorta are relatively similar to those observed in humans with acute bilateral femoral artery occlusion reported by Latham et al. 49. In our simulation models, these collective changes result in a reduction in shear stress in the abdominal aorta which is a site for atherosclerosis and abdominal aortic aneurysm. A similar finding was observed in the iliac blood flow and pressure, which translated into a reduction in shear stress. Indeed, lower wall shear stress interacts with other atherogenic biological risk factors which may initiate the onset of plaque and promote atherosclerosis50. This is in line with the reported increased risk of abdominal aortic aneurysm in patients with amputations3. As such, our findings provide insight into the potential mechanisms that may originate from alterations of the arterial network.

The findings derived from the Wave Separation Analysis (WSA) and Wave Intensity Analysis (WIA) concerning aortic pressure and flow waves yield further elucidation regarding the impacts of amputations on arterial hemodynamics. While WSA delineated no discernible alterations in the configuration of forward and backward wave components, WIA unveiled a notable escalation in peak forward wave intensity correlated with heightened severity of amputation. This observation suggests an augmentation in arterial stiffness, particularly within medium- to small-sized arteries. Such heightened intensity may indicate a more robust contraction during the systolic phase among individuals with more extensive amputations. Simultaneously, there was an elevation in the absolute value of the inverse peak of backward wave intensity, implying modifications in the heart’s diastolic phase among those experiencing significant limb loss.

A consistent pattern emerged, commencing with a forward compression wave succeeded by a minor decompression forward wave, succeeded again by a second forward compression wave. The peak of this secondary wave intensified from control to multilateral amputation scenarios, signifying an adaptive cardiovascular response. The nearly symmetrical alterations observed between forward and backward wave intensity components underscore the equilibrium between the heart’s contraction and relaxation mechanisms. These revelations advance our comprehension of the biomechanical transformations occurring within the arterial network post-amputation and shed light on the potential contribution of these changes to heightened cardiovascular risk in this population.

The strength of the present study resides in its ability to show the impact of acute alterations in the arterial system and its hemodynamic behavior following limb amputation. However, there are some limitations that need to be acknowledged. The findings of this study rely on a 1-D numerical model of the arterial tree, in which anatomical modifications were performed to simulate different degrees of severity of amputation and the biomechanical effects were quantified using solely changes in the tree’s geometry. In addition, there are some limitations that need to be considered when we aim to transpose these findings to human subjects. First, in vivo acute changes in the arterial system are likely to be associated with compensatory mechanisms that might limit the effects otherwise observed in in-silico models, this study provides valuable insights into the acute hemodynamic changes that occur after amputations and should be viewed as signals for adaptation to a new arterial setting. As such, they do not account for the acute and chronic biological adaptations that occur post-amputation. These biological adaptations may occur through neurohormonal changes involved in blood volume, blood pressure, cardiac output, vascular remodeling, vascular tone, and alterations in blood flow distribution. Future studies should aim to integrate these biological adaptations into computational models to provide a more comprehensive understanding of post-amputation hemodynamics. This could involve incorporating data from clinical studies on vascular changes following amputations and using more sophisticated models that account for the dynamic nature of vascular biology.

Second, reduction of blood flow in the artery proximal to the limb amputation results in reduction in the diameter of the artery and possibly secondary changes in arterial wall characteristics, which were not inputted in this study. Third, the remaining arteries might also behave differently through outward remodeling, with better ability to accommodate additional blood volume that is diverted following amputation. For example, it has been shown that 8.5 years after a unilateral above knee amputation, the external iliac artery diameter was smaller on the amputated side (7.25 mm versus 9.35 mm). Also, angiographic studies have confirmed a narrowing of both common and external iliac arteries on the amputated side, whereas there was an adaptive dilatation of pelvic and leg arteries on the non-amputated side40. Others studies have shown that the blood flow of the brachial artery was not different in lower limb amputees but that there was an increase in the vascular resistance in the upper limb47. However, these changes observed in response to amputation may not fully compensate for the alterations of the arterial system and might perhaps in some ways have long-term maladaptive or negative effect that contribute to the increased cardiovascular risk seen in this population. A promising avenue for future research could be the simulation of physical exercise, given that blood flow to limbs is particularly significant during such activity.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study employed a previously validated human 1-D arterial tree model coupled with a 0-D model of the left ventricle to examine the influence of successive amputation of lower and upper limbs on arterial stiffness and hemodynamics. These findings provide novel insight into the potential interaction between alterations in arterial stiffness, and blood pressure and flow patterns following amputation, which may interact with other psychosocial and biological risk factors to increase cardiovascular risk in subjects with limb amputations.
