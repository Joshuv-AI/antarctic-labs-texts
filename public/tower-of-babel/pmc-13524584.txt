Clinico-genomic risk stratification for biomarker-enriched trial design in poor-prognosis metastatic castrate-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC)
Authors: Manish Kohli, Liang Wang, Aik Choon Tan, Muhammad Zaki Fadlullah, Anushka Shankar, Sumithra J Mandrekar, Benjamin Haaland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAn integrated prognostic risk-score (RS) based on prognostic clinical factors and plasma copy number alterations (CNAs) across independent metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) cohorts was applied as a classifier to guide development of biomarker-enriched clinical trial designs.DesignPlasma CNAs prognostic for survival were combined into a prognostic score and integrated with clinical prognostic factors to derive an integrated RS in three independent mCRPC cohorts. Biomarker-enrichment trial designs were simulated across a spectrum of enrichment fractions and enrichment thresholds for the RS to consider 1:1 randomization of high RS patients to a study treatment arm or to receive standard treatment, with either a two or a three-year follow-up. The enrichment strategy was applied to improve the trial's ability to detect survival benefit at a Hazard Ratio 0.70; 80% power. Design trade-offs were assessed between the number of “high-risk” patients screened versus enrolled under varying enrichment thresholds and degrees of enrichment of the RS.ResultsPooled mCRPC patients (N = 561) showed RS values from 0.243 to 3.924 and a RS ≥ 1 (60th percentile) was associated with shorter survival. Elastic net models for 2- and 3-year survival were developed. At a threshold ≥1 with 2-year follow-up, a hybrid biomarker-enriched design (50% unselected, 50% high-risk) required screening 768 patients to enroll 219 per arm (n = 438), while maintaining adequate power.ConclusionAn integrated clinico-genomic classifier can guide an mCRPC enrichment strategy by enriching a cohort with 50% short-survival patients balancing sample size and screening needs to achieve adequate power in a biomarker-enriched design.Keywords: Biomarker-enriched, Trial-design, Prostate, Castrate-resistant, Genomics

Introduction

Metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) is highly lethal [1] but survival is heterogenous [2]. Contemporary prospective clinical trials report a median mCRPC survival of 36 months for treatment-naïve mCRPC [3,4] which declines to 14 to 18 months [5] after first-line chemotherapy in the mCRPC setting. Prognostic assessment of treatment-naïve mCRPC at present is based on clinical factors and measurements of non-specific proteins with prognostic stratification into low, intermediate, and high-risk prognostic groups corresponding to median overall survival durations of 43.3, 27.7, and 15.4 months respectively [6]. The clinical risk-model includes serum Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP), serum Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH), serum albumin and hemoglobin [[7], [8], [9]], patient performance status and opioid use [6,10], but no molecular mCRPC prognostic factors such as high circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) [11], TP53 mutations, AR amplifications, RB1 loss, PTEN loss [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]] or aggregated composite scores from multiple copy number alterations [15,17] have been integrated into existing prognostication risk-models [6]. Recently, blood-based and urine-based molecular prognosis in urological and specifically mCRPC has shown promise [15,[17], [18], [19], [20]] but prospective validation of integrated risk scores has not been performed.

Molecular prognostication may enable therapeutic targeting of genomic alterations linked to poor survival, as demonstrated in other malignancies, including lymphoma [21], myelodysplastic syndromes [22], and multiple myeloma [23], where molecular prognostication [24] has led to therapeutic genome-matched strategies. In mCRPC, specific poor-prognostic genomic alterations in homologous recombinant repair (HRR) pathways, particularly BRCA1/2 alterations [[25], [26], [27], [28]] have resulted in developing a predictive biomarker strategy for patient harboring deleterious HRR alterations targeted using Poly(adenosine diphosphate [ADP]–ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors. However, HRR defects are present in only 20–25% of unselected mCRPC cases [29] underscoring the need for additional molecular prognostic classifiers. Since 2015, extensive attempts have been performed in characterizing molecular prognostic signatures with 78 prospective and retrospective studies (Supplemental Fig. S1) that have identified numerous additional molecular mCRPC prognostic biomarkers in blood and tissue, highlighting the expansive and fragmented prognostic landscape of mCRPC with each single alteration occurring in a limited subset of patients. To capture the marked molecular heterogeneity of the mCRPC disease state for prognostication, we developed a prognostic classifier from prospective studies that integrates multiple clinically relevant copy number alterations (CNAs) and subsequently validated its performance in both liquid biopsy and matched metastatic tissue biopsy cohorts. The classifier incorporates gains/amplifications in AR, MYC, MYCL, CCND1, COL22A1, NOTCH1, PIK3CA, PIK3CB, ERG, BRAF, RAF1, FOXA1, and APOB, together with losses/deletions in PTEN, RB1, TP53, TMPRSS2, ZBTB16, NKX3.1, NCOR1, NCOR2, CHD1, PIK3R1, and SPOP [15,17]). Based on this classifier, we subsequently developed prognostic nomograms that estimate survival in patients with mCRPC, demonstrating greater prognostic precision through the incorporation of somatic genomic alterations that is achieved with contemporary clinical biomarkers alone [30]. We determined the prognostic accuracy of the integrated score relative to clinical prognostic factors alone. Using time-dependent AUCs derived from prognostic nomograms, we observed that the integrated clinico-genomic score achieved higher discriminative accuracy than clinical factors alone, with AUCs for mCRPC survival estimation increasing from 0.59 to 0.65 at 2 years and from 0.69 to 0.87 at 3 years. By simultaneously incorporating multiple genomic alterations, the classifier captures the broader biological complexity and prognostic landscape of mCRPC beyond what can be achieved with individual molecular biomarkers and was able to show increase in prognostic accuracy. Prognostic risk classifier-based approaches have been reported for deciding adjuvant treatment in breast cancer patients with poor clinical outcomes using a gene-expression classifier score generated from 24 genes [31,32].

To test the mCRPC risk classifier we simulated a clinical trial strategy that used the classifier as a score to inform biomarker-enriched clinical trial designs [33]. This simulation, based on a single mCRPC cohort of 87 patients, demonstrated the feasibility of integrating molecular prognostication classifier based on 24 CNAs into conventional clinical trial designs to estimate screening and enrollment requirements for biomarker-enriched trials. The molecular risk score classifier was used to randomize high-risk mCRPC patients to standard versus novel or intensified treatments and assess the impact of using different “enrichment thresholds” (various levels of the risk-score) and “enrichment fractions” (extent to which the biomarker-enriched study design will enrich such patients) while ensuring adequate power to detect a pre-specified effect size [33].

However, because our previous study was based on a single mCRPC cohort, the proposed enrichment strategy lacked broad validation, and its generalizability remained uncertain, providing only preliminary evidence for a molecular prognostic factor-based trial design. To address this limitation, we evaluated the distribution and prognostic performance of the multi-CNA classifier across several independent, treatment-naïve mCRPC cohorts. Development of the classifier-based trial simulation across multiple cohorts necessitated exploring different number of candidate CNAs for which information was available as well as 8 CNAs common in all cohorts. Our objective was to determine whether integrating a molecular classifier with contemporary clinical prognostic factors could prospectively identify high-risk patients with mCRPC for biomarker-enrichment clinical trials while maintaining prognostic accuracy, clinical utility, and generalizability across prospective, retrospective, and real-world cohorts.

Methods

Multiple independent treatment-naïve mCRPC cohorts for prognostic-risk clinic-genomic classifier

Institutional review board approval for conducting research on publicly available de-identified molecular and clinical outcome datasets was obtained (IRB-00135608) from University of Utah Institutional review Board (IRB) on December 4, 2023. A detailed description of the three independent de-identified datasets used to generate clinico-genomic classifiers previously reported [17] and in this study are summarized in “Supplementary Methods”. Briefly, the first treatment-naïve mCRPC dataset includes 87 patients and is named as the “PROMOTE” [15,34,35] cohort which has 24 gene plasma CNAs. The second dataset, named “SDMS” [15] has 92 patients with 11 of the 24 CNAs present in “PROMOTE”. In “PROMOTE” and “SDMS” databases the 11 common CNAs include gains in AR, COL22A1, MYC, PIK3CA, PIK3CB and losses in NCOR1, NKX3-1, NOTCH1, TMPRSS2, TP53, ZBTB16). A third dataset (N = 382) with treatment-naïve mCRPC dataset named, Vancouver Prostate Cancer database (“VPC”) [36] which also has data included from two prospective phase II trials [36] (NCT02125357 and NCT02254785) on eight CNAs with gains/amplifications in AR, MYC, PIK3CA, PIK3CB, and losses/deletions in NKX3-1, TMPRSS2, TP53, ZBTB16. These datasets generated molecular classifiers using different numbers of CNAs [17], but none of the reported classifiers with different numbers of CNAs include clinical prognostic factors. Eight CNAs were common across all three datasets and were integrated with clinical prognostic factors to derive a composite prognostic score suitable for biomarker-enriched clinical trial design.

Calculation of the clinico-genomic prognostic classifier in independent and pooled cohorts

In each independent cohort, complete data were available for serum ALP, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA). These were used to integrate with cohort-specific CNAs for deriving the clinico-genomic risk scores [6] using a two-step approach. In the first step, we included genes with CNA events that were observed in at least 20 patients per cohort to ensure adequate representation on each included gene. In the second step, risk score coefficients were estimated using the included genes, as well as ALP, LDH, and PSA based on a ridge regression approach with the ridge tuning parameter selected based on leave-one-out cross-validated partial likelihood deviance. Ridge regression ensures stability of coefficient estimates in the presence of potentially collinear predictor variables. The actual magnitude of coefficients in the ridge regression depends on the sample size and number of events, as well as within-cohort variations. For comparison of the relative influence of predictors across development cohorts, each coefficient vector was scaled to have length “1”. Throughout, risk scores were constructed based on the integration of the 8 CNAs common to all cohorts, ALP, LDH, and PSA, and coefficients were estimated in the subset of patients with complete data on all included genes as well as ALP,LDH, and PSA. Details for calculation of the integrated classifier with different numbers of CNAs and are provided under “Supplementary Methods”.

Validation of clinico-genomic classifier across cohorts

To evaluate whether the integrated classifier derived in an individual mCRPC cohort would provide similar prognostic estimates to those derived in the pooled cohort for survival prediction, we examined patient-level correlations between the classifier derived in the pooled (three) cohorts with the individual PROMOTE, SDMS, and VPC cohorts. Patient-level integrated classifier risk-scores were compared using scatter plots with the strength of relationship summarized using Pearson's correlation with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A high correlation between risk scores derived in the pooled vs. individual cohorts would indicate that the cohort-distinct risk-scores contain similar information for prognostication and support construction of a risk score from the pooled cohort. To determine relationships between the risk scores and overall survival (OS) Kaplan-Meier (K-M) estimates [37] for the pooled groups were used by grouping the pooled risk score into categories based on first and third quartile cut-offs of the risk score distributions. To assess the prognostic value added by inclusion of the CNAs beyond the traditional clinical values, ALP, LDH, and PSA, cross-validated concordance indices [38] were estimated for a model based only on ALP, LDH, and PSA and for the proposed risk score based on 8 CNAs common in all three datasets in addition to ALP, LDH, and PSA.

Enrichment designs based on the integrated clinico-genomic prognostic classifier

For a specific patient-level risk score approach, an enrichment clinical trial design functions by enrolling two groups of patients. The initial group of patients screened meets the overall trial eligibility requirements and a second group of patients with the additional requirement that each of their risk scores exceeds a given high-risk threshold for short survival [39]. These two patient groups are then combined as the overall patient sample set and randomized to study arms. For a specific “high-risk” score which predicts short survival times, the biomarker enrichment designs are defined by an “enrichment fraction” and an “enrichment threshold” [33]. The enrichment threshold denoted as δ, is the minimum risk score of the classifier that must be exceeded to qualify as the “high-risk” patient cohort to be targeted for study enrollment. The enrichment fraction, denoted as η, is the percentage of the overall patient sample that is required to have poor risk scores exceeding the given threshold.

The biomarker-enrichment trial designs in this study were evaluated across a spectrum of enrichment fractions and enrichment thresholds, and the trial design was considered with 1:1 randomization to a study treatment arm receiving an intensified or a novel therapeutic intervention that targets high prognostic risk patients or control arm receiving current standard of care therapy with an endpoint of overall survival (OS) with either a two or a three-year follow-up. Trials were evaluated in terms of estimated proportions of patients with risk scores exceeding the enrichment threshold, probabilities of OS endpoints, sample size achieving 80% power for an intensified treatment over the standard of care or control therapy with a Hazard Ratio (HR) of 0.7 and the number of patients that need to be screened to achieve the target sample size, and the largest treatment by risk category interaction HR that can be detected with 80% power. This interaction HR is the ratio of the treatment vs. control hazard ratio in the lower risk vs. higher risk groups of patients as defined by each patient being below vs. above the designated threshold of the clinic-genomic risk score. The interaction test assesses evidence that the treatment is more (or less) effective in patients with higher vs. lower risk scores, i.e., the evidence that the risk category is predictive of treatment benefit. Design characteristics for a more conservative scenario with an HR of 0.8 were also explored and are presented in the supplement. To estimate these operating characteristics, we follow the procedures described previously [33] including bootstrap estimates of confidence intervals. Additional details are provided previously [33] and included in the Supplementary Methods.

Since the three datasets included mCRPC state patients with previous exposure to ADT monotherapy, and increasingly mCRPC patients receive intensified ADT we probed a mCRPC tissue dataset in cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/), henceforth referred as “SU2C/PCF” (Stand Up to Cancer-Prostate Cancer Foundation Dream Team) SU2C/cBioPortal) [40] for determining the impact of ADT doublets and triplets with androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs) or taxane or both on the frequencies of the CNAs included in the risk-score and furthermore, the impact of change in frequencies to model biomarker-enriched clinical trial designs. We then simulated design characteristics for 24- and 36-month trials for a population with an elevated rate of CNAs. Details of this simulation are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Discussion

Biomarker-enriched clinical trials can improve efficiency of drug development, avoid unnecessary exposure of non-responders to ineffective treatments and reduce trial costs and duration. Such trial designs targeting molecular alterations have become an important strategy for accelerating precision oncology drug development across multiple tumor types. Nevertheless, reliance on a single genomic biomarker necessitates screening thousands of patients, as only a minority harbor the target alteration, thereby limiting the scalability and generalizability of precision oncology trials. For example, the SERENA-6 trial screened more than 3000 HR-positive/HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer patients for plasma ESR1 mutations to randomize 315 biomarker-positive patients [41] to the study and control arms. These limitations underscore the potential value of biology-driven multigene classifiers that identify a larger proportion of patients while preserving prognostic or predictive precision. Several prognostic molecular alterations in mCRPC (Supplementary Fig. S1), spanning a broad range of prevalences, have yet to be incorporated into risk stratification or biomarker-enrichment trial designs.

We previously established a proof of principle for using composite CNA-based scores in biomarker-enrichment approaches from a single-cohort study without performing cross-validation of the score in broad mCRPC populations. In the current study, we observed that the classifier derived from a single mCRPC cohort [15] provides similar prognostic estimates across multiple independent mCRPC cohorts [17] by examining patient-level correlations (Fig. 1A–D). The high correlation between risk scores derived in the pooled versus individual cohorts confirms that the cohort-distinct risk scores contain similar prognostic information and yield the prevalence of the biomarker-based risk categories in broader mCRPC populations. This generalizability of the score for survival prediction, together with establishing prevalence of the classifier in larger mCRPC patients, confirms its application for simulating prospective biomarker-enrichment trials.

[17] We observed strong correlations of the clinico-genomic classifier integrated with different numbers of CNAs across cohorts, supporting the use of the multi-CNA integrated classifier for biomarker-enriched patient randomization, using 8 common CNAs in all three datasets. The motivation for testing a classifier derived from fewer CNAs stems from practical considerations, as reducing the number of molecular determinants in the classifier can lower assay development costs, provided that predictive performance remains unaffected. At the same time, the trial design takes into account a pragmatic strategy for using a wider array of somatic perturbations beyond a single molecular or pathway driver such as BRCA1/2 gene alterations [28,[42], [43], [44]], MSI-H/dMMR [45], AR-V7 splice variants or PTEN loss [46,47] in plasma.

All three mCRPC cohorts used in this simulation primarily included patients treated with ADT monotherapy, whereas ADT-based doublet and triplet regimens are now standard first-line therapy for metastatic prostate cancer. For this reason, we evaluated whether intensified hormone-sensitive regimens may cause treatment-related clonal shifts in CNAs thereby altering the risk-score distributions used to estimate mCRPC survival. Our results using an independent mCRPC metastatic tissue cohort obtained after ADT-based doublet or triplet therapy (including taxanes and ARPIs) for metastatic hormone-sensitive disease, show that 23 of 24 CNAs had unchanged CNA prevalences, with AR gain as the only significantly enriched alteration. Incorporating this increased prevalence of AR gain into the risk model increased the mean risk score and reduced the number of patients requiring screening for biomarker-enriched trial enrollment (Supplementary Table S5A and S5B).

A biomarker-enriched trial design has the advantage of being able to achieve comparable statistical power with fewer patients than a traditional trial design and also facilitates the evaluation of interactions between treatment effects and molecular risk categories which may not be performed or may be underpowered using traditional trial designs. While we examined the risk-score as a prognostic risk score, these tests of interaction between treatment efficacy and patient risk status could provide evidence of predictiveness of the risk score for the treatment being assessed. For instance, designing a biomarker-enriched trial that includes only patients predicted to have short survival (enrichment threshold ≥1) would require screening approximately 921 mCRPC patients to enroll 370 for randomization between standard-of-care and experimental treatment arms (Table 1). In contrast, enrolling patients with a low enrichment threshold (0), representing the largest population subset (Fig. 2A), would require screening 542 patients to enroll 538 patients for randomizing the study cohort into a two arm-strategy based on using the prognostic classifier.

Our simulation study also highlights the flexibility of designing trials that combine different enrichment fractions and thresholds, enhancing logistical feasibility and practical implementation. Study logistics, such as patient availability, screening capacity, and cost are critical in determining the most feasible enrichment design. For instance, an enrichment fraction of 0.5 with a threshold of 1, requiring screening 768 patients to enroll 438 in a two-arm trial (Table 1), is more practical for addressing the challenge of mCRPC lethality than a conventional randomized phase III trial in this population. If patient screening and follow-up can be achieved cost-effectively, the trial can prioritize testing the prognostic molecular classifier's power. Moreover, maintaining continuous enrollment of both enriched and non-enriched participants helps minimize confounding from time-related factors. In this multi-cohort approach, we also observe no difference in the number of patients to screen and enroll compared to our previous biomarker enrichment strategy [33] which was based on a single prospective cohort of 87 mCRPC patients.

A limitation of the current study is that the proposed biomarker-enriched clinical trial designs were evaluated exclusively through statistical simulations using existing clinical and genomic datasets, rather than within a prospectively conducted clinical trial. As a result, we included as many clinical prognostic variables that were available and were able to observe improvements in screening efficiency and sample size requirements. However, the patient enrichment model-based estimates that will require prospective confirmation. Future biomarker-stratified interventional trials should prospectively implement the composite genomic prognostic score with established mCRPC clinical prognostic factors for patient selection or randomization stratification to determine whether these predicted efficiencies translate into improved trial conduct and clinical outcomes under real-world conditions. Such prospective validation will be essential before widespread adoption of this approach.

Another limitation is that the integrated clinical model was restricted to ALP, LDH, and PSAALP, as these were the only clinical prognostic variables consistently available across the three historical cohorts. Other established prognostic factors, including hemoglobin, albumin, ECOG performance status, visceral metastases, and opioid use, were incompletely captured or unavailable and therefore could not be incorporated without introducing substantial missingness or requiring imputation. Consequently, the clinical component of the integrated model may not fully reflect the performance achievable with contemporary multivariable clinical prognostic models. Future prospective studies with comprehensive collection of established clinical prognostic variables will be important to further evaluate the complementary value of the composite genomic score. We used a molecular classifier based on 8 CNAs, as these were present in all three cohorts, and were unable to include an 11- or 24-CNA-based classifier, which may constitute a limitation of the present study. This practical limitation stems from the lack of adequate datasets with a greater number of CNAs and clinical variables. Future prospective studies incorporating a greater number of CNAs into the integrated clinico-genomic classifier will be needed to fully ascertain performance improvements in trial design, but this is beyond the scope of the current simulation. This limitation, does not diminish the relevance of developing biomarker-enriched clinical trials based on molecular classifiers at this stage of trial evolution in mCRPC.

An additional limitation relates to the historical nature of the cohorts used in this study, which primarily enrolled patients between 2009 and 2017, before widespread adoption of ARPI-based treatment intensification in the mHSPC setting. Although analysis of the SU2C cohort demonstrated that the prevalence of most copy number alterations included in the composite genomic prognostic score remained similar across treatment groups, with the exception of increased AR amplification/gain following intensified therapy, the effect of contemporary treatment paradigms on the performance of the classifier remains uncertain. Prospective validation in contemporary mHSPC and mCRPC populations will therefore be important to confirm the generalizability of the classifier and its utility for biomarker-enriched clinical trial design. The current simulation also does not emphasize “which” specific treatment should be given in high-risk patients. Trial simulations to test the type of treatment which will be effective are predictive in nature. The approach for developing biomarker-based strategies initially with prognostic endpoints and then based on availability of specific treatments matched against individual molecular targets in the classifier can lead to molecular predictive scaling. This has been the case of the Oncotype DX 21-gene Recurrence Score (RS) initially introduced to estimate risk of distant disease recurrence in early-stage estrogen receptor-positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor-2-negative (ER+/HER2-) breast cancer [48] and then used to refine chemotherapy treatment predictive modeling in the adjuvant setting [32].

Finally blood-based CNAs in combination with routine laboratory measures (serum ALP and LDH) eliminates the need for invasive tissue-based testing or the need for deep variant sequencing since plasma CNAs can be reliably detected using low-pass sequencing approaches. This approach contrasts with mCRPC clinical trials performed using clinical non-specific factors for defining “poor prognosis” [5], and potentially avoids defining aggressive variants informed using hard to obtain tissue-based somatic perturbations in TP53, RB1, and PTEN alterations with either immune-histochemistry or next-generation sequencing [49]. However, a practical challenge in implementing such a trial design lies in the potential failure of the bespoke panel to reliably detect CNAs, either due to insufficient assay sensitivity, low tumor fraction, or both. This limitation may necessitate screening a larger number of patients than initially projected and possible trial delays. Nevertheless, this challenge is common in the ctDNA field and, with the advent of ultra-sensitive technologies, is increasingly manageable. It represents a necessary trade-off in advancing tumor biology–driven treatment strategies aimed at improving patient survival.

In conclusion, our findings provide a framework for biomarker-enriched clinical trial design in mCRPC through integrated clinico-genomic risk stratification. This approach has the potential to improve trial efficiency while enabling preferential enrollment of patients with poor-prognosis disease who are may benefit from intensified treatments.
