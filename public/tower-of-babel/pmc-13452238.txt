MZB1 marks a B-cell-associated stromal-immune microenvironment in cervical squamous cell carcinoma: a multi-omics analysis

Introduction

Cervical cancer remains a significant global health burden, ranking as the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women worldwide, with an estimated 661,021 new cases and 348,189 deaths in 2020 [1]. The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitor has revolutionized cancer treatment. In cervical cancer, pembrolizumab and cemiplimab have demonstrated survival benefits, yet the overall response rate remains modest at approximately 14-17% [2,3]. This highlights an urgent need for reliable biomarkers to identify patients most likely to benefit from immunotherapy.

The tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) is a critical determinant of therapeutic responses. Recent studies have characterized three major TIME phenotypes in cancer: "immune-inflamed" tumors with dense immune cell infiltration, "immune-excluded" tumors where immune cells are restricted to the stroma, and "immune-desert" tumors with sparse infiltration [4]. Single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics have enabled high-resolution dissection of this heterogeneity [5]. Understanding the molecular determinants of these distinct phenotypes is essential for developing personalized immunotherapeutic strategies.

B cells have emerged as key players in anti-tumor immunity beyond their classical role in humoral responses. Tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS)—ectopic lymphoid aggregates containing B cells, T cells, and dendritic cells (DC)—are associated with favorable prognosis and improved immune checkpoint inhibitor responses across multiple cancer types. In head and neck cancer, mature TLS evoke intra-tumoral T and B cell responses via progenitor exhausted CD4+ T cells [6]. In breast cancer, TLS-associated fibroblasts recruit T and B cells through the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis [7]. The spatial and phenotypic plasticity of B cells in remodeling the tumor microenvironment has been increasingly recognized [8].

In cervical cancer, TLS have been linked to better survival and enhanced anti-tumor immunity [9]. A large-cohort study demonstrated that TLS distribution and maturity predict recurrence-free survival and PD-1 inhibitor response, outperforming conventional International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics staging [9]. Single-cell dissection of cervical cancer has revealed tumor-specific germinal center B cells associated with TLS, and a high proportion of these cells predicts improved clinical outcomes [10]. However, not all B-cell subsets are protective; an immunosuppressive plasma cell subset (HSPA1B_PC) within TLS co-localizes with Regulatory T cells (Tregs) and Th17 cells and is associated with poor prognosis [11]. Furthermore, the composition of TLS—specifically high densities of Tregs and polymorphonuclear myeloid-derived suppressor cells (PMN-MDSCs)—rather than their mere quantity, has been associated with fatal outcomes [12]. These findings underscore the complexity of B-cell biology and the need for precise markers of protective humoral immunity.

MZB1 (Marginal Zone B and B1 cell-specific protein) is a B cell-specific ER-resident molecular chaperone that facilitates immunoglobulin folding and secretion [13,14]. Structurally, MZB1 belongs to the saposin-like protein superfamily and functions as a co-chaperone of GRP94 and BiP [15]. MZB1 has been implicated in autoimmune diseases and shows dysregulated expression in various cancers. A recent pan-cancer analysis revealed that MZB1 is overexpressed in 20 cancer types and correlates with immune infiltration and patient prognosis [16]. In ovarian cancer, MZB1 serves as a protective factor associated with better outcomes and increased immune infiltration [17,18]. However, the role of MZB1 in cervical cancer has not been systematically investigated.

In this study, we employed an integrated multi-omics approach to characterize MZB1 expression in cervical squamous cell carcinoma (CSCC). We aimed to evaluate its association with the TIME and patient prognosis.

Methods

Study design and data sources

This integrative multi-omics study analyzed CSCC samples using three complementary data types. Single-cell RNA-seq (all_scRNAseq.h5ad, 163,964 cells) and spatial transcriptomics (spatial.rds, 7,535 spots) were obtained from the Science Data Bank under accession number https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.11624 [19]. Spatial proteomics data were derived from Supplementary Table 7 of the same original study, which compared protein expression between immune-rich regions and other regions in CSCC tissues.

For external validation, bulk transcriptomics data were obtained from the UCSC Xena browser (https://xenabrowser.net), specifically the TcgaTargetGtex_rsem_gene_tpm dataset, which includes RNA-seq data from TCGA and GTEx. We extracted cervical cancer samples (n = 304) and normal cervical tissue samples (n = 10) for downstream analysis. Corresponding clinical data were downloaded from the same source. All data were processed and normalized as described below.

Spatial proteomics analysis

Differential expression analysis was performed using the provided log2 fold change (log2FC) and p-values. Proteins with |log2FC| > 0.5 and p < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. The volcano plot was generated using ggplot2, with MZB1 highlighted in green.

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis

Single-cell RNA-seq data were processed using the Seurat pipeline. Cells with <500 genes, <1000 unique molecular identifiers (UMIs), or >25% mitochondrial content were excluded. After quality control, 163,964 high-quality cells were retained for downstream analysis. Cell types were annotated using canonical marker genes. UMAP dimensionality reduction was performed for visualization. MZB1 expression was extracted and visualized on UMAP plots. Boxplot showing MZB1 expression across cell types was generated using only MZB1-positive cells (expression > 0) to highlight cell-type specificity. To characterize MZB1-expressing B cells, MZB1+ B cells were extracted from the single-cell dataset. Based on published signatures of protective germinal center B cells (BCL6, AICDA, CXCR4, CD83) and suppressive plasma cells (HSPA1B, IL10, CD38, PDCD1LG2), a protective-suppressive score ratio was calculated for each B cell. Cells with ratio > 1.5 were classified as "Protective", ratio < 0.67 as "Suppressive", and others as "Mixed". The distribution of these subtypes and MZB1 expression levels across subtypes were analyzed. To validate the B-cell subtype classification, we examined the distribution of MZB1+ B cells across protective, suppressive, and mixed subtypes based on published gene signatures [11].

Spatial transcriptomics analysis

Spatial transcriptomics data were generated using the NanoString GeoMx platform. Data were normalized using the Q3 normalization method. A total of 7,535 spatial spots passed quality control and were retained for downstream analysis. Quality control metrics, including UMI counts and detected genes, were assessed to ensure data reliability. Cell type abundance scores were pre-calculated using single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) with cell-type-specific gene signatures and provided in the metadata. These scores represent the relative abundance of CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, B cells, NK cells, macrophages, and DC in each spatial spot.

To avoid confounding by tissue compartment differences, all spatial transcriptomics analyses were restricted to malignant spots (n = 4,914), as identified by the SpotType annotation in the metadata. MZB1 expression was extracted from the normalized expression matrix. Spatial heatmaps were generated using ggplot2 to visualize the distribution of MZB1 expression and immune cell scores across tissue sections. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationships between MZB1 expression and immune cell abundance scores within malignant regions. To further validate the association, malignant spots were stratified into MZB1-high and MZB1-low groups based on MZB1 expression positivity (expression > 0, as the median expression was 0 within malignant regions; n = 217 and n = 4,697, respectively), and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used to compare immune cell abundance between groups.

To identify molecular programs associated with MZB1 expression within the tumor microenvironment, differential expression analysis between MZB1-high and MZB1-low malignant spots was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Genes with |log2FC| > 0.5 and adjusted p < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg correction) were considered significantly differentially expressed.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using clusterProfiler on the significantly upregulated genes in malignant regions (n = 49) to identify biological processes enriched in MZB1-high spots. Heatmap visualization of the significantly upregulated genes was generated using pheatmap with row-wise z-score normalization.

Bulk transcriptomics analysis

Bulk RNA-seq data for cervical cancer were obtained from TCGA and GTEx databases. The dataset contained 304 primary tumor samples and 10 normal cervical tissue samples. Gene expression was quantified as log2(TPM+1) values. MZB1 expression was extracted using its Ensembl ID (ENSG00000170476). Differential expression analysis between tumor and normal tissues was performed using two-sided Welch's t-test. For survival analysis, overall survival (OS) data were obtained from TCGA clinical data. Patients were stratified into MZB1-high and MZB1-low groups based on the median expression level of MZB1. Kaplan-Meier curves were generated using the survival and survminer packages, and the log-rank test was used to compare survival differences between groups. To assess the relationship between MZB1 expression and immune cell infiltration, single-sample GSEA was performed using gene signatures for ten immune cell types (CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, B cells, NK cells, macrophages, DC, neutrophils, Treg, T helper 1 (Th1), and T helper 2 (Th2)). Spearman correlation analysis was conducted between MZB1 expression and each immune cell score.

To evaluate the association between MZB1 expression and clinical pathological features, patients were stratified by clinical stage (Stage I, n = 162; Stage II, n = 69; Stage III, n = 45; Stage IV, n = 21) and histologic grade (G1, n = 18; G2, n = 135; G3, n = 118). Kruskal-Wallis tests were performed to compare MZB1 expression across different stages and grades. The correlation between MZB1 expression and CD4+ T as well as macrophage scores was further assessed using Spearman correlation analysis.

To validate the spatial transcriptomics findings in an independent cohort, differential expression analysis between MZB1-high and MZB1-low tumors was performed using TCGA cervical cancer data (n = 304). MZB1 expression was extracted using its Ensembl ID (ENSG00000170476). Samples were stratified into MZB1-high and MZB1-low groups based on median MZB1 expression. Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare gene expression between groups, and genes with |log2FC| > 0.5 and adjusted p < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg correction) were considered significantly differentially expressed. The volcano plot was generated using ggplot2.

T-cell dysfunction and exclusion signature calculation

To evaluate the immune evasion potential associated with MZB1 expression, a T-cell dysfunction and exclusion signature was calculated for each TCGA cervical cancer sample, following the framework described by Jiang et al. [20]. Briefly, the dysfunction score was computed based on the expression of T-cell exhaustion-related genes (PDCD1, CTLA4, LAG3, HAVCR2, TIGIT, BTLA, CD274, PDCD1LG2, VSIR, IDO1, SIRPA, and TGFB1) , and the exclusion score was computed based on genes associated with T-cell exclusion (CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, CCL2, CCL3, CCL4, CCL5, CCR2, CCR5, CXCR3, TGFB1, TGFBR1, TGFBR2, TGFBR3, CD68, CD163, MS4A4A, MRC1, IL10, and VEGFA). The combined dysfunction/exclusion score was calculated as the sum of the two subscores. Higher scores indicate greater immune evasion potential and have been associated with poorer response to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy in previous studies [20]. Patients were stratified into MZB1-high and MZB1-low groups based on median MZB1 expression, and Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare the combined scores between groups. To further dissect the components, T cell dysfunction and exclusion subscores were analyzed separately. Spearman correlation was used to assess the relationship between MZB1 expression and each subscore. Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare subscores between MZB1-high and MZB1-low groups.

Immune checkpoint and immune signature analysis

To investigate the relationship between MZB1 expression and immune checkpoint molecules, Spearman correlation analysis was performed between MZB1 expression and seven immune checkpoint genes (PDCD1, CTLA4, LAG3, TIGIT, HAVCR2, IDO1, and CD274) in TCGA cervical cancer cohort. For extended validation, correlations with LAG3, TIGIT, and HAVCR2 were further analyzed.

T-cell exhaustion signature score was calculated as the average expression of exhaustion-related genes, including PDCD1, CTLA4, LAG3, HAVCR2, TIGIT, BTLA, CD244, ENTPD1, TOX, TOX2, EOMES, and PRDM1. IFN-γ signature score was calculated as the average expression of IFN-γ-related genes, including IFNG, STAT1, IRF1, CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, GBP1, GBP2, GBP5, IDO1, CCL2, CCL3, CCL4, and CCL5. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted between MZB1 expression and each signature score.

Cox regression analysis for prognostic factors

Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to evaluate the prognostic value of MZB1 expression and clinical pathological factors for overall survival (OS) in cervical cancer patients. The following variables were included in the analysis: MZB1 expression (continuous), age (continuous), clinical stage (Early: Stage I-II vs Late: Stage III-IV), histologic grade (Low: G1-G2 vs High: G3-G4), and histological type (Squamous vs Others). Hazard ratio (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using the Schoenfeld residuals test, and no significant violations were detected (global p = 0.17). Kaplan-Meier survival curves stratified by MZB1 expression were generated for early-stage (Stage I-II) and late-stage (Stage III-IV) subgroups, with log-rank tests used to compare survival differences.

To evaluate the prognostic value of MZB1 across different patient populations, subgroup survival analyses were performed stratified by age (≤46 years vs >46 years, based on median age) and histologic grade (Low: G1-G2 vs High: G3-G4). Kaplan-Meier curves were generated for each subgroup, and log-rank tests were used to compare survival differences between MZB1-high and MZB1-low patients. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals.

Statistical analysis and software

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2). Single-cell RNA-seq data were processed using the Seurat pipeline [21]. Batch effects were corrected using Harmony [22]. Dimensionality reduction was performed using UMAP [23]. GO enrichment analyses were performed using clusterProfiler [24]. Single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) was performed as previously described [25]. T-cell dysfunction/exclusion signatures were calculated following the method described by Jiang et al. [20]. Copy number variation inference was performed using inferCNV [26].

Results

MZB1 is identified as a stromal-immune niche associated protein in CSCC

To identify proteins associated with immune-rich regions in CSCC, we first analyzed spatial proteomics data comparing immune-rich regions versus other regions. Quality assessment confirmed robust data characteristics: log2FC values were symmetrically distributed around zero (range: -3.71 to 1.93), and p-values showed a uniform distribution with enrichment of small values, with 564 proteins (27.4%) showing significant differential expression (p < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 1A). The volcano plot revealed that MZB1 was significantly enriched in immune-rich regions (log2FC = -1.67, p = 1.09× 10⁻4) (Fig. 1A).

Red and blue dots represent significantly upregulated and downregulated proteins, respectively (|log2FC| > 0.5, p < 0.05). Gray dots indicate non-significant proteins. MZB1 is highlighted in green (log2FC = -1.67, p = 1.09× 10⁻4). Top 5 most significant genes are labeled. (B) Single-cell UMAP visualization of 163,964 cells from CSCC tissues. Left panel: cells are colored by cell type annotations, with nine major cell types identified. Right panel: cells are colored by MZB1 expression levels, showing enrichment in B cells. (C) Boxplot showing MZB1 expression distribution across cell types, limited to MZB1-positive cells (expression > 0). B cells show the highest median expression (2.46) and positivity rate (45.6%), confirming MZB1 as a B-cell marker in CSCC.

We next examined MZB1 expression at single-cell resolution using scRNA-seq data from CSCC tissues. After stringent quality filtering, 163,964 high-quality cells were retained. Cell type annotation identified nine major cell lineages: T cells (43.2%), fibroblasts (14.4%), squamous cells (12.8%), myeloid cells (7.6%), myocytes (6.2%), B cells (5.8%), endothelial cells (4.5%), NK cells (3.9%), and glandular cells (1.6%) (Supplementary Fig. 1B). UMAP visualization clearly resolved these cell populations (Fig. 1B, left).

Examination of MZB1 expression distribution revealed that MZB1 was expressed in a subset of cells (12,848 cells, 7.8% of total), with expression levels ranging from 0.29 to 4.53 (median = 1.43) among positive cells (Supplementary Fig. 1C). MZB1 expression was predominantly observed in B cells, as shown by UMAP coloring (Fig. 1B, right). Quantitative analysis across cell types confirmed that B cells exhibited the highest positivity rate (45.6%) and the highest median expression level (2.46) among MZB1-positive cells, followed by glandular cells (19.3%, median 1.17) and NK cells (12.3%, median 1.20) (Fig. 1C). Notably, while T cells represented a larger proportion of MZB1-positive cells due to their abundance, their positivity rate (8.6%) and median expression (1.26) were substantially lower than those of B cells. These findings establish MZB1 as a B-cell-associated protein enriched in stromal-immune regions of CSCC.

To characterize the functional status of MZB1-expressing B cells, we classified B cells into Protective (GC B cell-like), Suppressive (plasma cell-like), and Mixed subtypes based on protective and suppressive gene signatures. Among all B cells, 45.0% were Suppressive, 32.4% were Protective, and 22.6% were Mixed. Notably, among MZB1+ B cells, the Suppressive subtype predominated (79.2%), while Protective subtype accounted for only 9.5% (Supplementary Fig. 1D). Consistently, MZB1 expression was highest in Suppressive B cells (median = 2.56), followed by Mixed (median = 1.98) and Protective (median = 1.28) subtypes (Supplementary Fig. 1E). These results indicate that MZB1 predominantly marks suppressive plasma cell-like B cells rather than protective germinal center B cells in CSCC.

Spatial validation of MZB1-associated stromal-immune niche

To validate the spatial association between MZB1 and immune cells within the tumor microenvironment, we analyzed spatial transcriptomics data restricted to malignant regions (n = 4,914 spots), as normal tissue contamination could confound the observed associations. Indeed, among the 689 spots with detectable MZB1 expression in the original dataset, 472 (68.5%) were located in normal cervical tissues from independent healthy donors, while only 217 (31.5%) were within malignant regions, confirming the necessity of tissue compartment-specific analysis. Quality assessment of the spatial transcriptomics data confirmed robust technical performance, with nCount_RNA ranging from 1,188 to 53,427 (median = 13,392) and nFeature_RNA ranging from 650 to 6,595 (median = 3,907) (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Spatial heatmaps revealed that MZB1 expression was detected in 217 malignant spots (4.4%), distributed in distinct spatial clusters across the tissue sections, which showed overlapping enrichment patterns with CD8+ T abundance and B cell scores (Fig. 2A).

MZB1 expression was detected in 217 malignant spots (4.4%). Each dot represents an individual spatial spot. Color gradients indicate expression levels or cell abundance scores (green for MZB1, red for CD8+ T, blue for B cells). (B) Scatter plot showing the correlation between MZB1 expression and CD8+ T cell abundance in malignant spots (n = 4,914). Spearman ρ = 0.047, p = 1.07 × 10⁻³. The red line represents the linear regression fit with 95% CI (gray shading). (C) Scatter plot showing the correlation between MZB1 expression and B cell scores in malignant spots (n = 4,914). Spearman ρ = 0.147, p = 3.41 × 10⁻²⁵. The red line represents the linear regression fit with 95% CI (gray shading).

Quantitative correlation analysis within malignant regions confirmed a weak but significant positive correlation between MZB1 expression and CD8+ T abundance (Spearman ρ = 0.047, p = 1.07 × 10⁻3) (Fig. 2B). Notably, MZB1 expression exhibited a moderate positive correlation with B cell scores (Spearman ρ = 0.147, p = 3.41 × 10⁻25) (Fig. 2C), further supporting MZB1 as a B-cell-associated marker within the tumor microenvironment.

To further characterize the MZB1-associated immune microenvironment, we extended our correlation analysis to additional immune cell types within malignant regions. MZB1 expression showed a weak but significant positive correlations with CD4+ T abundance (ρ = 0.049, p = 6.13 × 10⁻⁴), while moderate positive correlations were observed with macrophage scores (ρ = 0.171, p = 1.33 × 10⁻33) and DC scores (ρ = 0.140, p = 6.67 × 10⁻23). NK cell scores were uniformly zero in malignant regions, precluding correlation analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Consistent with these findings, comparison between MZB1-high (n = 217) and MZB1-low (n = 4,697) malignant spots revealed significantly higher abundance of B cells (p = 5.04 × 10⁻²⁴), macrophages (p = 2.21 × 10⁻³²), dendritic cells (p = 2.83 × 10⁻²²), CD8+ T cells (p = 1.98 × 10⁻³), and CD4+ T cells (p = 1.24 × 10⁻³) in MZB1-high spots (Supplementary Fig. 2C). NK cell scores showed no difference between groups, consistent with their uniformly low abundance in malignant regions.

Collectively, these spatial transcriptomic data restricted to malignant regions provide direct evidence that MZB1 expression shares similar spatial distribution patterns with immune cell infiltration, particularly B cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells, confirming the existence of an MZB1-associated stromal-immune niche within the tumor microenvironment of CSCC.

MZB1 marks stromal-immune tumors in bulk transcriptomics

To validate the clinical relevance of MZB1 in larger cohorts, we analyzed MZB1 expression in TCGA and GTEx cervical cancer datasets. MZB1 expression was significantly upregulated in tumor tissues (n = 304) compared to normal cervical tissues (n = 10) (mean: 3.71 vs 1.63, t-test p = 0.023) (Fig. 3A).

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that patients with high MZB1 expression (n = 152) had a favorable trend toward longer overall survival compared to those with low MZB1 expression (n = 152) (median survival: 722.5 vs 633.5 days), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (Log-rank p = 0.071) (Fig. 3B).

Consistent with our spatial transcriptomics findings, MZB1 expression showed strong positive correlations with multiple immune cell types in bulk transcriptomics. The strongest correlation was observed with B cell scores (Spearman ρ = 0.845, p = 3.40 × 10⁻⁸⁴), followed by CD4+ T (ρ = 0.602), DC (ρ = 0.534), Treg (ρ = 0.528), and CD8+ T (ρ = 0.500) (Fig. 3C). A scatter plot confirmed the robust positive correlation between MZB1 expression and B cell scores (ρ = 0.845, p = 3.40 × 10⁻⁸⁴) (Fig. 3D), further validating MZB1 as a B-cell-associated marker of stromal-immune tumors.

Clinical and immune associations of MZB1 in TCGA cohort

We further investigated the association between MZB1 expression and clinical pathological features in TCGA cervical cancer cohort. MZB1 expression showed a trend of increase with advancing clinical stage, with median expression values of 3.64 (Stage I, n = 162), 4.22 (Stage II, n = 69), 4.15 (Stage III, n = 45), and 4.54 (Stage IV, n = 21), although the difference was not statistically significant (Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.114) (Supplementary Fig. 3A, left). Similarly, MZB1 expression increased with higher histologic grade, with median values of 3.60 (G1, n = 18), 4.06 (G2, n = 135), and 4.11 (G3, n = 118) (Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.164) (Supplementary Fig. 3A, right).

Consistent with the immune correlation results in Fig. 3C, MZB1 expression showed strong positive correlations with CD4+ T scores (Spearman ρ = 0.602, p = 2.20 × 10⁻³¹) and macrophage scores (Spearman ρ = 0.429, p = 4.52 × 10⁻¹⁵) (Supplementary Fig. 3B), further supporting the association between MZB1 and a broad stromal-immune microenvironment. Detailed sample characteristics, including age distribution and MZB1 group allocation, are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

MZB1 correlates with immune checkpoint molecules and immunotherapy response

Given the association between MZB1 and stromal-immune microenvironment, we next examined the relationship between MZB1 expression and immune checkpoint molecules. MZB1 expression showed significant positive correlations with multiple immune checkpoint genes, with the strongest correlations observed for PDCD1 (PD-1, Spearman ρ = 0.511, p = 1.30 × 10⁻²¹), CTLA4 (ρ = 0.499, p = 1.64 × 10⁻²⁰), TIGIT (ρ = 0.485, p = 2.38 × 10⁻¹⁹), LAG3 (ρ = 0.470, p = 4.43 × 10⁻¹⁸), HAVCR2 (TIM-3, ρ = 0.426, p = 8.13 × 10⁻¹⁵), and to a lesser extent IDO1 (ρ = 0.277, p = 9.72 × 10⁻⁷) and CD274 (PD-L1, ρ = 0.266, p = 2.61 × 10⁻⁶) (Fig. 4A). Scatter plots further confirmed the robust positive correlations between MZB1 expression and PDCD1 (Fig. 4B) as well as CTLA4 (Fig. 4C).

To assess the potential clinical implications of MZB1 expression for immunotherapy, we calculated T-cell dysfunction/exclusion signatures for each TCGA sample. Notably, MZB1-high tumors exhibited significantly higher dysfunction/exclusion scores compared to MZB1-low tumors (median: 5.97 vs 4.40, Wilcoxon rank-sum test p = 3.48 × 10⁻¹¹) (Fig. 4D). To further dissect the components underlying the elevated T-cell dysfunction/exclusion signatures, we separately analyzed T cell dysfunction and exclusion subscores. MZB1 expression showed a significant positive correlation with T cell dysfunction score (Spearman ρ = 0.509, p = 2.21 × 10⁻²²) and a slightly weaker correlation with T cell exclusion score (Spearman ρ = 0.458, p = 6.12 × 10⁻¹⁸) (Fig. 4E). To validate these correlations at the group level, MZB1-high tumors had significantly higher dysfunction scores (median: 2.64 vs 1.53, p = 2.96 × 10⁻¹³) and exclusion scores (median: 3.35 vs 2.58, p = 2.83 × 10⁻¹⁰) compared to MZB1-low tumors (Fig. 4F). These findings suggest that the increased T-cell dysfunction/exclusion signatures in MZB1-high tumors reflect both T cell dysfunction and exclusion, with a more pronounced contribution from T cell dysfunction, supporting the concept of "immune activation with concomitant exhaustion" rather than pure immune exclusion.

Extended analysis of MZB1 with immune checkpoint molecules and immune signatures

To further characterize the relationship between MZB1 and immune regulation, we extended our analysis to additional immune checkpoint molecules and immune functional signatures. MZB1 expression showed significant positive correlations with LAG3 (Spearman ρ = 0.470, p = 4.43 × 10⁻¹⁸), TIGIT (ρ = 0.485, p = 2.38 × 10⁻¹⁹), and HAVCR2 (TIM-3, ρ = 0.426, p = 8.13 × 10⁻¹⁵) (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Given the association between MZB1 and T-cell-related immune checkpoints, we next examined the relationship between MZB1 expression and T-cell exhaustion signature. MZB1 expression showed a strong positive correlation with T-cell exhaustion score (Spearman ρ = 0.552, p = 1.29 × 10⁻²⁵) (Supplementary Fig. 4B), indicating that MZB1-high tumors are associated with a more exhausted T-cell phenotype. Furthermore, we assessed the association between MZB1 expression and IFN-γ signature, a key marker of anti-tumor immunity. MZB1 expression demonstrated a moderate but significant positive correlation with IFN-γ signature score (Spearman ρ = 0.365, p = 5.19 × 10⁻¹¹) (Supplementary Fig. 4C), suggesting that MZB1-high tumors maintain active inflammatory signaling despite the exhausted T-cell phenotype.

MZB1 is an independent prognostic factor in cervical cancer

To determine whether MZB1 expression provides independent prognostic value beyond conventional clinical factors, we performed univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. Univariate analysis revealed that late-stage disease was significantly associated with worse overall survival (HR = 2.279, 95% CI: 1.334–3.896, p = 0.003), while MZB1 expression showed a trend toward favorable prognosis (HR = 0.904, 95% CI: 0.810–1.008, p = 0.069) (Fig. 5A).

Multivariate Cox regression analysis, adjusting for age, stage, grade, and histological type, demonstrated that MZB1 expression was an independent protective factor for overall survival (HR = 0.894, 95% CI: 0.800–0.998, p = 0.045), along with late-stage disease as an independent risk factor (HR = 2.394, 95% CI: 1.363–4.204, p = 0.002) (Fig. 5B). Age, grade, and histological type were not significantly associated with survival in the multivariate model.

Correlation analysis showed that MZB1 expression was weakly correlated with clinical pathological features (|ρ| < 0.1 for all comparisons), indicating that MZB1 represents an independent biomarker not strongly associated with conventional clinicopathological variables (Fig. 5C).

Stratified survival analysis by clinical stage revealed that high MZB1 expression was associated with better overall survival in both early-stage (Stage I-II, n = 208) and late-stage (Stage III-IV, n = 58) patients, although the difference did not reach statistical significance in the late-stage subgroup due to limited sample size (Fig. 5D). Taken together, these findings establish MZB1 as an independent favorable prognostic factor in cervical cancer.

Subgroup validation of MZB1 prognostic value

To further validate the prognostic value of MZB1 across different clinical contexts, we performed subgroup survival analyses stratified by age and histologic grade. In age-stratified analysis, high MZB1 expression showed a protective trend in both younger (≤46 years, n = 133) and older (>46 years, n = 133) patients, although the differences did not reach statistical significance (log-rank p = 0.171 and p = 0.579, respectively) (Supplementary Fig. 5A). Notably, grade-stratified analysis revealed that the prognostic value of MZB1 was most pronounced in patients with high-grade tumors (G3-G4, n = 115), where high MZB1 expression was associated with significantly better overall survival (log-rank p = 0.026) (Supplementary Fig. 5B). In contrast, no significant survival difference was observed in the low-grade subgroup (G1-G2, n = 151, log-rank p = 0.923), suggesting that MZB1 may serve as a particularly valuable biomarker in aggressive disease. The proportional hazards assumption was satisfied for all variables in the multivariate Cox model (all p > 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 5C), confirming the reliability of our regression analyses.

MZB1-high malignant spots exhibit distinct stromal-immune molecular programs

To characterize the molecular programs associated with MZB1 expression within the tumor microenvironment, we performed differential expression analysis between MZB1-high and MZB1-low spots restricted to malignant regions (n = 4,914 spots). A total of 50 genes were significantly differentially expressed (|log2FC| > 0.5, adjusted p < 0.05), including 49 upregulated and 1 downregulated gene (SCD) in MZB1-high spots (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Table 2). Notably, MZB1 itself showed the highest log2FC (12.73) among all genes, confirming the robustness of group stratification.

GO enrichment analysis of the 49 upregulated genes revealed that MZB1-high malignant spots were significantly enriched in extracellular matrix (ECM)-related biological processes, including extracellular matrix organization, extracellular structure organization, and collagen fibril organization (all adjusted p < 0.001) (Fig. 6B). Additionally, pathways related to BMP signaling regulation and complement activation (lectin pathway) were also enriched, suggesting a coordinated stromal-immune interaction within MZB1-high regions (adjusted p < 0.05). Consistent with these findings, ECM-related genes including COL1A1, COL3A1, COL1A2, SPARC, DCN, BGN, LUM, VCAN, and TIMP2 were among the top upregulated genes in MZB1-high spots (Fig. 6C). Notably, CCL2, a chemokine involved in monocyte and macrophage recruitment, and complement components C3 and C1S were also upregulated, indicating potential immune-regulatory functions associated with the stromal program.

Heatmap analysis of the 49 upregulated genes demonstrated clear separation between MZB1-high and MZB1-low malignant spots (Fig. 6C). In contrast to our earlier observations in the combined analysis of all spots, classical B cell markers (CD79A, CD79B, MS4A1) and T cell markers (CD3D, CD8A, CD4) were not significantly differentially expressed between MZB1-high and MZB1-low malignant spots, suggesting that the molecular signature of MZB1-high tumors within the malignant compartment is primarily driven by stromal remodeling rather than canonical lymphocyte signaling pathways.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that MZB1-high malignant spots exhibit a distinct stromal-immune molecular program characterized by ECM remodeling, BMP signaling regulation, and complement activation, with associated chemokine (CCL2) expression potentially contributing to immune cell recruitment within the tumor microenvironment.

Validation of MZB1-associated gene expression in TCGA cohort

To validate the molecular programs associated with MZB1 expression in an independent cohort, we performed differential expression analysis between MZB1-high and MZB1-low tumors in TCGA cervical cancer cohort (n = 304). A total of 2,376 significantly upregulated genes and 115 downregulated genes were identified in MZB1-high tumors (|log2FC| > 0.5, adjusted p < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 6). Notably, MZB1 itself showed strong upregulation in MZB1-high tumors (log2FC = 3.875, p = 1.16 × 10⁻⁴⁶), confirming the robustness of group stratification. Immune checkpoint molecules, including PDCD1 (log2FC = 1.378), CTLA4 (log2FC = 1.269), LAG3 (log2FC = 1.234), TIGIT (log2FC = 1.282), and CD274 (log2FC = 0.670), were also significantly upregulated in MZB1-high tumors (all adjusted p < 0.05), consistent with the spatial transcriptomics findings. Furthermore, it supported the association between MZB1-high tumors and immune checkpoint activation.

Discussion

In this study, we performed an integrative multi-omics analysis combining spatial proteomics, single-cell transcriptomics, spatial transcriptomics, and bulk transcriptomics to investigate MZB1 in CSCC. To our knowledge, this is the first study to integrate spatial proteomics data with multi-omics analysis in CSCC, allowing us to validate MZB1 at both protein and spatial levels. This approach directly links protein-level evidence with transcriptomic data, overcoming a major limitation of RNA-only studies. Our findings reveal that MZB1 serves as a B-cell-specific marker of a B-cell-associated stromal-immune microenvironment in CSCC, characterized by spatial co-localization of B cells with immune cells, ECM remodeling, complement activation, and upregulation of immune checkpoint molecules. Importantly, MZB1-high tumors were associated with favorable overall survival and emerged as an independent prognostic factor, suggesting that this stromal-immune signature reflects a coordinated anti-tumor immune response.

MZB1 as a marker of the stromal-immune microenvironment

Our data consistently demonstrate that MZB1 is predominantly expressed in B cells and plasma cells in CSCC, aligning with previous studies identifying MZB1 as a signature gene for plasma cells and germinal center B cells [10,27]. Single-cell analysis confirmed that 45.6% of B cells expressed MZB1, with the highest expression levels among all cell types, establishing MZB1 as a specific B-cell marker in CSCC. Spatial transcriptomics revealed significant positive correlations between MZB1 and B cells (ρ = 0.305) and CD8+ T cells (ρ = 0.105) in the overall tissue context, confirming an MZB1-associated immune-rich microenvironment. Notably, when analyses were restricted to malignant regions, MZB1-high spots still exhibited significantly higher abundance of B cells (p = 5.04 × 10⁻²⁴), macrophages (p = 2.21 × 10⁻³²), dendritic cells (p = 2.83 × 10⁻²²), CD8+ T cells (p = 1.98 × 10⁻³), and CD4+ T cells (p = 1.24 × 10⁻³), indicating that the association between MZB1 and immune infiltration is not merely driven by normal tissue contamination. This is consistent with the paradigm of B cell-immune cell collaboration within the tumor microenvironment [8].

The clinical relevance of B cells in cervical cancer is increasingly recognized. Germinal center B cells predict improved outcomes [10], and TLS maturity predicts recurrence-free survival and PD-1 inhibitor response [9]. Our findings identify MZB1 as a specific B-cell marker that is associated with this favorable prognostic context. MZB1 may offer advantages over conventional B-cell markers such as CD20, as its expression is linked to B-cell functional status rather than mere presence [13,14].

Prognostic value of MZB1 in context

The prognostic significance of MZB1 varies across cancer types. In chronic lymphocytic leukemia, follicular lymphoma, and diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, elevated MZB1 expression is associated with adverse prognosis [28]. In multiple myeloma, MZB1 is upregulated and promotes tumor progression [29]. Conversely, in hepatocellular carcinoma and gastric cancer, MZB1 is frequently silenced by promoter hypermethylation and functions as a tumor suppressor [30]. A pan-cancer analysis confirmed that MZB1 is overexpressed in 20 cancer types with context-dependent prognostic significance [16]. In ovarian cancer, MZB1 serves as a protective factor [17,18]. Our findings in cervical cancer are consistent with this protective role, suggesting that MZB1′s function is shaped by the specific tumor microenvironment, where it may reflect the presence of a coordinated stromal-immune response rather than tumor cell-intrinsic signaling.

The paradox of high dysfunction/exclusion scores but favorable prognosis

MZB1-high tumors exhibited significantly higher T-cell dysfunction/exclusion scores (p = 3.48 × 10⁻¹¹) yet were associated with favorable survival. We propose several explanations for this apparent paradox.

First, high dysfunction/exclusion scores may primarily reflect T-cell exhaustion rather than physical immune exclusion. MZB1 expression strongly correlates with T-cell exhaustion scores (ρ = 0.552). Chronic antigen stimulation in immune-infiltrated microenvironments—such as the MZB1-high tumors described here—can induce T-cell exhaustion, characterized by upregulation of PD-1, CTLA-4, LAG3, and TIGIT [31]. Importantly, exhausted T cells can be reinvigorated by immune checkpoint blockade. This "immune activation with concomitant exhaustion" has been observed in other cancers: mature TLS in head and neck cancer are enriched with progenitor exhausted CD4+ T cells [6], and the Epi-Imm tumor state in cervical cancer shows immune infiltration with exhaustion features that correlate with response to immune checkpoint blockade [19]. Single-cell dissection of cervical cancer also revealed that T cells transition from cytotoxic to exhaustion phenotypes yet remain part of the anti-tumor response [10].

Second, the predictive performance of dysfunction/exclusion signatures may vary across cancer types, as these signatures were primarily validated in melanoma cohorts; their utility in cervical cancer requires further validation in ICI-treated cohorts [20].

Third, the composition and functional state of immune infiltrates may matter more than their mere presence. Previous studies have demonstrated that the abundance of immune aggregates does not always correlate with survival in cervical cancer; rather, the cellular composition within these aggregates may determine clinical outcomes [12]. Our findings complement this observation: MZB1 may serve as a marker of a specific B-cell component within the stromal-immune niche that distinguishes productive anti-tumor immunity from immunosuppressive microenvironments.

Heterogeneity of B-cell subsets and the importance of spatial context in cervical cancer

The role of B cells is complex and context-dependent. Huang et al. identified an immunosuppressive plasma cell subset (HSPA1B_PC) that co-localizes with Tregs and Th17 cells and correlates with poor prognosis [11]. Notably, these HSPA1B_PC also express MZB1. This highlights an important point: MZB1 is a pan-plasma cell marker, but its prognostic significance depends on the functional state and spatial context of the plasma cells.

In our study, MZB1-high tumors are associated with favorable prognosis, yet our single-cell analysis revealed that MZB1+ B cells are predominantly of the suppressive subtype (79.2%), with minimal representation of protective germinal center B cells (9.5%). How can MZB1+ B cells be predominantly suppressive at the single-cell level, yet MZB1-high tumors be associated with favorable prognosis? We propose that this apparent paradox is resolved by considering the spatial context and functional heterogeneity of B cells within the tumor microenvironment. MZB1 marks a plasma cell-like transcriptional state characterized by immunoglobulin production. This state is molecularly similar to suppressive plasma cells described in other contexts [11]. However, the net functional outcome of these cells depends critically on their spatial localization and interacting partners within the tumor microenvironment. In MZB1-high tumors, these plasma cells co-localize with CD8+ T cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells within a stromal-immune niche, suggesting that they may contribute to anti-tumor immunity indirectly through multiple mechanisms: (1) antibody secretion and complement activation (supported by the enrichment of C3, C1S, and SERPING1 in MZB1-high malignant spots), (2) chemokine production (e.g., CCL2) for immune cell recruitment, and (3) crosstalk with fibroblasts and immune effectors [7,8]. Conversely, when plasma cells are sequestered within immunosuppressive microenvironments dominated by Tregs and PMN-MDSCs, their presence may correlate with poor outcomes [11,12].

Thus, MZB1 expression marks a functional state of B-cell differentiation (plasma cell/antibody-secreting state), while the clinical implications are dictated by the broader immune contexture in which these cells reside. This framework reconciles the single-cell findings (predominantly suppressive subtype) with the favorable prognostic association (co-localization with immune effectors within a stromal-immune niche). B cells can exert both pro- and anti-tumorigenic functions depending on context, and MZB1 should be interpreted within the broader immune contexture [8].

Comparison with immunosuppressive networks

The stromal-immune niche identified by MZB1 contrasts with other immunosuppressive networks in cervical cancer. SPP1+ macrophages interact with T cells through the SPP1-CD44 axis, contributing to immunosuppression and poor prognosis [32]. MYH11+ cancer-associated fibroblasts promote tumor progression through the MDK-SDC1 axis [33]. HPV status also influences the immune microenvironment, with HPV-positive tumors exhibiting distinct communication patterns [34]. The balance between protective B-cell responses (marked by MZB1) and immunosuppressive elements likely determines net clinical outcome. Notably, our observation that MZB1-high malignant spots are characterized by ECM remodeling (COL1A1, COL3A1, SPARC) and CCL2 expression suggests potential crosstalk between MZB1+ B cells/plasma cells and cancer-associated fibroblasts, which may contribute to the formation of a permissive microenvironment for immune cell recruitment [7].

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, all analyses were conducted using publicly available datasets without experimental validation. While our multi-omics integration provides robust correlative evidence, the proposed causal relationships require functional validation using in vitro and in vivo models. Second, the spatial proteomics data were available only as summarized differential expression results rather than raw protein expression values, which precluded certain quantitative analyses. Third, our analysis of spatial transcriptomics data initially included both malignant and normal regions; after re-analysis restricted to malignant regions, the molecular signature of MZB1-high spots shifted from immune-related pathways to ECM-related pathways. This highlights the importance of tissue compartment-specific analyses and suggests that immune signatures observed in the combined analysis were partly driven by normal tissue contamination. Fourth, our dysfunction/exclusion signature analysis is a computational surrogate for immunotherapy response; direct evidence linking MZB1 expression to actual immune checkpoint inhibitor treatment outcomes is lacking and warrants investigation in clinical trial cohorts. Fifth, although MZB1 showed an independent prognostic association in the TCGA cohort, the effect size was modest (HR = 0.894, 95% CI: 0.800–0.998), and this finding requires validation in external independent cohorts prior to clinical translation. Sixth, our findings are primarily based on transcriptomic data; protein-level validation (e.g., immunohistochemistry) in clinical specimens is needed to confirm the spatial expression patterns and prognostic significance of MZB1 at the protein level. Despite these limitations, our integrative multi-omics approach provides a comprehensive characterization of MZB1 in CSCC and generates novel hypotheses for future investigation. Seventh, MZB1 expression was detected in only 217 (4.4%) of the 4,914 malignant spots analyzed. Although this was sufficient for our statistical analyses, the limited number of positive spots means our findings should be interpreted cautiously and validated in larger independent cohorts.

Conclusions

This integrated multi-omics analysis suggests MZB1 as a B-cell-specific marker of a B-cell-associated stromal-immune microenvironment in CSCC. Within malignant regions, MZB1-high spots show enrichment of immune cell infiltration (B cells, macrophages, DCs, CD8+ T cells) and ECM remodeling, along with immune checkpoint upregulation and favorable prognosis. Although MZB1-high tumors exhibit higher T-cell dysfunction/exclusion scores, this paradox can be explained by concurrent T-cell exhaustion—a state of "immune activation with concomitant exhaustion" rather than true immune exclusion. The molecular program of MZB1-high malignant spots is characterized by ECM organization, BMP signaling regulation, and complement activation, suggesting a coordinated stromal-immune response. MZB1 was identified as an independent protective prognostic factor in this exploratory analysis, suggesting its potential as a candidate biomarker for identifying stromal-immune tumors. These hypothesis-generating findings require prospective validation. However, given the limited number of MZB1-positive spots in the spatial transcriptomics data and the lack of external validation cohorts, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and confirmed in larger independent studies.
