Magnetically oriented nanosheet interlayer for dynamic regeneration in lithium metal batteries
Authors: Zhengyu Ju, Tianrui Zheng, Bowen Zhang, Andrei Dolocan, Amy C. Marschilok, Esther S. Takeuchi, Kenneth J. Takeuchi, Guihua Yu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceElectrochemical energy storage systems with high energy density and long-duration are highly demanded for sustainable transportation. Lithium metal batteries (LMBs), despite their promising energy density compared to lithium-ion batteries, suffer significantly from performance degradation. It is quite challenging yet meaningful to revive the electrochemical capabilities of the cycled LMBs without complicated cell disassembly and posttreatment process. Here, we show that by using a suspension electrolyte containing magnetically responsive nanosheets, manipulating the nanosheet orientation via an external magnetic field can effectively reshape the Li deposition microstructure and modulate the solid–electrolyte interphase composition during cell operation. Such in situ regeneration strategy can potentially provide useful guidance for designing the functions of dynamic interfacial recovering in versatile energy storage systems.

The past three decades have witnessed the development and maturity of commercial lithium-ion batteries consisting of lithium metal oxide as cathode and graphite as anode with the energy density approaching 300 Wh kg−1cell (1, 2). In pursuit of the energy density goal of 500 Wh kg−1cell, lithium (Li) metal has emerged as a promising anode alternative to graphite due to its ultrahigh theoretical specific capacity (3,860 mA h g−1) and the lowest redox potential (−3.04 V vs. standard hydrogen electrode). However, the lifetime of current Li metal batteries (LMBs) is often limited, primarily due to the formation of a thick, porous solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) on the Li metal, especially at practical operation conditions featuring high areal cathode loading and capacity (>20 mg cm−2 or 3 mA h cm−2), thin Li (~20 μm), and lean electrolyte (<3 g Ah−1) (3, 4). Furthermore, the steep concentration gradient at the vicinity of the Li metal results in the dendritic Li microstructure, which poses considerable safety concerns on LMBs for long-term operation (5, 6).

Extensive efforts have been made to tailor the SEI composition and control the deposited Li microstructure for advancing the cycle life of LMBs (7–9). An inorganic-rich thin SEI is often favored to realize a high Coulombic efficiency for Li plating/stripping and compact Li deposition microstructure (10–12). To this end, tuning the molecular chemistry in Li salts or solvents in the liquid electrolyte and engineering the interface/interphase are widely adopted strategies (13, 14). In addition, introducing inorganic additives into the electrolyte has been reported facile and effective in altering the Li+-solvation chemistry, thereby regulating the SEI composition (15) and Li deposition mechanism (16). Despite these ex situ strategies to extend the Li metal’s cycle life, it is more attractive yet challenging to in situ regenerate the cycled Li and revive its electrochemical capability during battery operation. Current research attempts are very limited, which have been focused on rejuvenating the dead or isolated Li via chemical redox reaction (17), electric-field-induced spatial progression (18) or calendar aging (19), and recovering the Li dendrites through self-heating (20). More potential methodologies for realizing the Li metal regeneration are required to be exploited.

Two-dimensional (2D) materials have been widely explored in electrochemical energy storage with versatile functionalities over the past two decades (21–23). As the coating layer on the Li metal, as shown in Fig. 1 A, i, 2D materials mainly exhibit the capabilities of physically redistributing the Li-ion flux near the SEI (24, 25) or chemically alloying with the Li to lower the deposition energy barrier (26, 27). However, after extended cycles, such nanosheet layers may not be as effective as while in their pristine states due to the deposited Li layer at their surfaces, resulting in increased overpotential related to the SEI growth and safety concerns mounting over the separator perforation. Here, in order to realize the dynamic regeneration of the cycled Li, we propose the utilization of a suspension electrolyte consisting of magnetically responsive nanosheets as a controllable interlayer between the separator and Li metal anode, as shown in Fig. 1 A, ii. The orientation of these nanosheets in the suspension electrolyte can be manipulated by applying an external magnetic field. After the formation of dendritic Li after extended cycles, a magnetic field can be introduced to force the nanosheets inside to be horizontally oriented, redistributing the ion flux near the Li metal and smoothing the surface topography of the Li deposits via alleviating the tip effect in the regenerative cycles. Subsequently, the regenerated Li metal can work normally by removing the magnetic field and a degradation–regeneration loop can be achieved for prolonged cycle life of the Li metal anodes.

Results and Discussion

Revealing Nanosheet Orientation Effects via Numerical Simulation.

To validate our proposed regeneration mechanism, we first investigated the effect of the nanosheet orientation in the electrolyte on the Li deposition behavior via the COMSOL Multiphysics simulation. Three initial nucleation sites are set before deposition. In the pristine electrolyte, it can be noticed that Li+ ions only deposit onto the dendrites formed at the nucleation sites (Fig. 1B), primarily due to the tip effect which changes the trajectory of Li+ ions under the electric field. A significant ion depletion can be observed at the vicinity of the Li dendrites from the map of ion concentration distribution (Fig. 1E), and a large deposition overpotential as well as the current flow toward these dendrites can be noticed in the voltage distribution map (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Compared with the deposition behavior in the pristine electrolyte, suspension electrolytes with vertically or horizontally aligned nanosheets both show more homogeneous Li deposition, as a large number of Li+ ions deposit onto the sites of Li metal surface that were not preferentially nucleated (Fig. 1 C and D). The thickness of the deposited Li can further be used to evaluate the deposition uniformity. The Li deposits show a height of 0.95 μm after deposition for 30 s, which is much higher than the 0.75 μm and 0.45 μm for the deposits with the vertically and horizontally aligned nanosheets, respectively. Regarding the spatial ion concentration distribution, the uniform ion concentration surrounding the deposited Li can be achieved with the horizontal nanosheets (Fig. 1G), which could be attributed to the ion flux homogenization effect of these nanosheets. It can be seen that the vertical nanosheets also exhibit the property of guiding the ion movement (Fig. 1F), although not as effectively as the horizontal ones. Furthermore, the voltage maps prove the lowest deposition overpotential and the most redistributed current flow due to the horizontal oriented nanosheets in the electrolyte (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B and C). The Li deposition behaviors with different deposition times were also investigated. At a longer deposition time of 300 s, severe large dendrites can be seen in the pristine electrolyte, while relatively uniform deposition with much shorter and compact Li deposits is achieved in the electrolyte with horizontally aligned nanosheets (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Integrating the morphological and electrochemical findings from the numerical simulations, the advantages of horizontally aligned nanosheets inside the electrolyte are clearly revealed.

Synthesis and Characterizations on Vermiculite (VMT) Nanosheets.

Considering the characteristics of strong magnetic response, good dispersibility in organic electrolytes, and electrochemically inert and stable at the battery working potentials, VMT nanosheets are chosen as the model material for the preparation of the suspension electrolyte and the demonstration of the proposed regeneration mechanism. VMT is a hydrous layered aluminosilicate compound consisting of Mg/Al/Fe-centered octahedral sheet sandwiched by two Si/Al-centered tetrahedral sheets (Fig. 2A). VMT nanosheets were prepared from their bulk layered material via cation exchange reactions with Na+ and Li+, respectively, to reduce the interlayer interaction for the subsequent exfoliation under sonication (28). The exfoliated VMT nanosheets in water (2 mg mL−1) show a transparent light yellow color with obvious Tyndall effect (Fig. 2B), suggesting the successful exfoliation of the bulk material. Scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) images exhibit the ultrathin 2D feature of the exfoliated VMT nanosheets (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S3), and such morphological characteristics are maintained without aggregation in the VMT powder after vacuum drying, as shown in the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping further reveals the uniform distribution of the key structural elements in the VMT nanosheets (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Compositional and chemical bonding analyses were further conducted on the VMT nanosheets. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns show a strong (002) peak located at 7.1°, corresponding to a d-spacing of 1.24 nm (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements exhibit the existence of ferrous Fe (Fe2+) in the octahedral units (Fig. 2E and SI Appendix, Fig. S7), which has been found responsible for the anisotropic magnetic susceptibility of the VMT nanosheets due to their triagonal symmetry at the octahedral sites, with the trigonal axis lying normal to the nanosheets (29, 30). The magnetic response of the VMT nanosheets in the electrolyte was validated via optical microscopy. VMT nanosheets can be dispersed in the electrolyte of 1M LiPF6 in ethylene carbonate/diethyl carbonate (EC/DEC) with a moderate concentration of 0.5 wt%, while a high concentration of 2 wt% could trigger the nanosheet aggregation (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Optical microscopy images show that the VMT nanosheets are individually dispersed in the electrolyte; however, when no magnetic field is applied, only a few edge planes are exposed as a number of nanosheets are hard to be visualized due to the high transmittance through their basal planes (Fig. 2F and SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A and B). In comparison, with the out-of-plane magnetic field, VMT nanosheets can be oriented parallel to the field, and therefore a considerable amount of edge planes can be seen from optical microscope images (Fig. 2G and SI Appendix, Fig. S9 C and D). Such magnetic response of the VMT nanosheets provides us with the potential to manipulate its orientation in the electrolyte even when batteries are under cycling.

Electrochemical Properties and Deposition Microstructures in Li||Li Symmetric Cells.

To explore the effect of the VMT suspension electrolyte and magnetic field, we first investigated the electrochemical properties in Li||Li symmetric cells. Different from the conventional symmetric cell configuration, here a polyimide O-ring (~25 μm-thick) was placed between the Li metal and separator to hold a thin layer of electrolyte inside (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). For the symmetric cell cycled at 2 mA cm−2 and 2 mA h cm−2 with the pristine electrolyte, a significant increase in the overpotential could be observed, resulting in cell failure after cycling for ~70 h (Fig. 3A). While for the cell with the VMT electrolyte, the cycling life of the symmetric cell can almost be doubled (~160 h), suggesting that merely adding VMT nanosheets as the electrolyte additive can be beneficial for the Li plating/stripping process. We further introduced the regeneration steps during the cycling, in which the coin cells were moved to the center of two strong magnets in a home-made setup (SI Appendix, Fig. S11), generating a magnetic field parallel to the Li metal plane to horizontally align the VMT nanosheets, and cycled for 20 h. After the 20-h cycling under the magnetic field, the coin cells were moved back to the normal cycling environment. With such a regenerative process, we found that the overpotential of the symmetric cell increased very slowly, reaching ~0.16 V after 350 cycles, and further confirmed in another symmetric cell with an overpotential of ~0.18 V after cycling (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). Comparing its charge–discharge voltage profiles before and after the regeneration step, we noticed that the charging curves change from the arcing shape to the peak shape (Fig. 3B). The formation of the arcing curve is due to the accumulation of the dead Li and SEI on the Li metal surface, which prolongs the transport distance of Li+ for the electrochemical redox reaction. Such a transition indicates that the transport barrier for Li+ is decreased, which probably accounts for the extended cycling life of the symmetric cells.

We then investigated the magnetic field effect on the cells with the pristine electrolyte to decouple the contribution of the magnetic field and VMT electrolyte on symmetric cells’ cycling properties, as it was reported that introducing an external magnetic field to the working cells could also influence the Li+ diffusion pathway and therefore control its deposition morphology (31, 32). When the Li+ flux approaches the Li metal surface with uneven surface topography, illustrated using a “bump” in the numerical simulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S13), the electric field near the Li metal surface could be curved, leading to the Li+ accumulation at the tip. The applied magnetic field, either vertical or horizontal, is able to change the trajectory of Li+ due to the generated Lorentz force normal to the Li+ movement direction and therefore redistribute the Li+ flux. For the symmetric cells, lower overpotentials can be achieved with both vertical and horizontal magnetic field, showing the cycling life of ~90 h compared to ~70 h for the reference cell at the conditions of 2 mA cm−2 and 2 mA h cm−2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S14). From the morphological aspect, postmortem SEM images of the cycled Li metals show that the Li microstructure under either vertical or horizontal magnetic fields is different from that without the magnetic field (SI Appendix, Figs. S15 and S16). Instead of forming needle-like dendrites, the deposits grow to be a foam-like interconnected structure under the magnetic fields, which is potentially related to the magnetohydrodynamics effect supplied by the magnetic field. The external magnetic field supplied by the home-made setup can partly extend the cycling stability via affecting the plating/stripping behavior, but it may not be the essential reason for the stable cycling for hundreds of hours in the VMT electrolyte case.

The Li deposition microstructures were further studied under the different operation conditions. The cycled Li in the conventional carbonate ester electrolyte exhibits the typical dendritic structure (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S17 A and B); however, with the VMT nanosheets in the electrolyte, the nodule-like deposition can be observed (Fig. 3D and SI Appendix, Fig. S17 C and D). Assuming the VMT nanosheets are randomly localized in the electrolyte, it is still possible for them to redistribute the ion flux and homogenize the deposition from the numerical simulation aspect. For the cell cycled with the magnetic-field-induced regeneration step, a compact Li deposition with smooth surface topography can be achieved (Fig. 3E and SI Appendix, Fig. S17 E and F), suggesting that the introduction of the magnetic field to the operating cell can reshape the Li microstructure. In partial regions, VMT nanosheets can be observed at the top surface, which is probably related to their horizontal orientation and sedimentation with Li deposition. In comparison, with the vertical magnetic field applied in the VMT electrolyte throughout the symmetric cell cycling test, the deposited Li even shows more dendritic microstructure compared to the one without field, with the vertically aligned VMT nanosheets observed on the top surface (SI Appendix, Fig. S18). Such a deposition microstructure indicates that the vertical magnetic field is detrimental to the Li deposition/stripping, as the VMT nanosheets in the electrolyte are vertically aligned by the field, weakening their effect of physically redistributing the Li+ flux as we have revealed using the numerical simulation.

Depth-Dependent Compositional and Morphological Analyses on SEI Layers.

In addition to the obvious morphological differences in these cases, we identified the depth-dependent SEI chemical composition of the cycled Li with XPS. Sputtering was applied with an Ar gas cluster ion beam for 40 min to remove the top surface of ~4 nm to obtain the depth profile. As shown in the C 1s spectra, the Li cycled in the pristine electrolyte exhibits a significant amount of Li alkyl carbonate (ROCO2Li), which is attributed to the decomposition of the organic electrolyte (Fig. 3F). After sputtering for 40 min, the peak of ROCO2Li significantly decreases while the peak for polyether compounds increases. However, for the Li cycled with VMT&H, a smaller proportion of ROCO2Li and a higher proportion of Li carbonate (Li2CO3) at the surface can be observed (Fig. 3G), suggesting that it contains a more inorganic-rich SEI layer, where each composition proportion before and after sputtering is very similar. For the F 1s spectra, residual LiPF6 can be observed in the Li reference after sputtering (Fig. 3H), while no LiPF6 peak can be found in the Li w/VMT&H even before sputtering (Fig. 3I), probably due to the porous deposition microstructure in the Li reference that traps the salts inside. Comparing the inorganic LiF after sputtering, the Li w/VMT&H exhibits a proportion of 29.6% of the F-based compounds, which is higher than that of the Li reference (23.9%). Furthermore, before sputtering the elemental ratios of F/C for the reference and w/VMT&H are measured as 0.11 and 0.16, respectively, and after sputtering F/C ratios turn to 0.12 and 0.16, respectively, indicating that more anions are decomposed in the VMT&H cell than the solvents probably due to the solvation structure alteration of the Li+ salt with the negatively charged VMT nanosheets. The Si 2p spectra further confirm the existence of VMT nanosheets on the top surface of cycled Li w/VMT&H (SI Appendix, Fig. S19), which is in accordance with the observations from the post-cycle SEM images. The interfacial compositional characterization with XPS exhibits the inorganic-rich SEI layer in the Li w/VMT&H, which is responsible for the enhanced electrochemical properties and uniform deposition microstructure.

In order to overcome the limitations of detection depth (<10 nm) and potential sputtering-induced molecular decomposition (33) of XPS, time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) was utilized to provide an advanced three-dimensional (3D) chemical analysis of the SEI layers. In particular, ToF-SIMS is able to identify, locate, and relatively quantify various chemical species of interest that can be related to different battery degradation effects, such as electrolyte decomposition, acidic species attack, and metallic Li accumulation (34). This information is crucial for understanding how the introduced VMT nanosheets in the electrolyte affect the Li deposition and ultimately, battery performance. To analytically isolate the SEI strata, we performed ToF-SIMS depth profiling (Fig. 4 A and B). Careful scrutiny of the ToF-SIMS spectra related to the depth profiles reveal multiple SI fragments of interest representing the organic species from solvent decomposition (C2HO−, C2O−, C3−, LiC2−, and Li2CO3−), inorganic species (LiF2−) originating from salt decomposition and HF attack, bulk Li deposits (Li2−), and the VMT nanosheets (Si−). An LiPF6-based electrolyte could lead to an outer SEI composed of inorganic phosphate or fluorophosphate products, while the SEI underneath is typically a hybrid organic/inorganic layer that generates SI fragments such as C2HO− and LiF2− (35). Here, direct comparison between the depth profiles normalized to their maxima (Fig. 4 A and B) for the Li reference and w/VMT shows that both possess an inorganic-rich outer SEI, as demonstrated by the shallower localization of the LiF2− peak profiles. The one w/VMT exhibits a higher LiF2− peak intensity in the deeper region, which suggests a more LiF-rich inner SEI compared with the reference. In contrast, the normalized profile of the LiC2− species (representing ROCO2Li) appears peaking at a deeper depth (Fig. 4 A and B) for both samples, suggesting an organic-rich inner SEI. It is noted that the organic LiC2− species is less abundant in inner SEI of the cycled Li w/VMT. Notably, the Si− normalized profile peaks deeper than all detected SEI species, thus indicating that the VMT nanosheets are located at the top surface of the deposited Li (Fig. 4B). 3D false-color renders of the depth profiles in Fig. 4 A and B, particularly organic decomposition products (LiC2−), VMT nanosheets (Si−), and bulk Li (Li2−), are shown in Fig. 4C. While Li appears uniformly distributed, the organic products are located distinctly separated from the VMT nanosheets, as observed in the 3D render overlay of Si− and LiC2− (Fig. 4C). Since the depth profiles in Fig. 4 A and B are acquired in high mass resolution mode to properly separate the SEI species in the spectra, the lateral resolution is only ~1 µm, which does not allow for a clear view of the surface morphology. A clearer view is achieved by acquiring depth profiles in high lateral resolution mode (less than 0.1 µm beam spot), as displayed in SI Appendix, Fig. S20, demonstrating that the VMT nanosheets are forming micron-sized islands that are uniformly distributed across the surface, while the surface morphology indicated by the Li− (Li and Li-containing SEI) distribution is similar to that obtained by electron microscopy (Fig. 3D). The disjunct spatial localization of LiC2− with respect to Si− visible in the 3D renders in Fig. 4C demonstrates the passivation effect of the VMT nanosheet on the Li electrode.

We further quantitatively compare the amounts of the species of interest, as represented by the integrated yields of the depth profiles for the selected ion fragments between the crossing depths at 0.6 of their maxima (36, 37). The 0.6 level is chosen based on the one sigma (SD) of the normal distribution that is assumed for the species composing the SEI strata. Consequently, the SEI thickness can be determined by the sputtering time where the deepest species in the inner SEI, which is LiC2− in our case, crosses the 0.6 level of its maximum. The analysis shows that the SEI thickness for the cycled Li reference is almost twice that of the Li w/VMT (Fig. 4D), indicating that a higher degree of solvent/salt decomposition occurs with the pristine electrolyte. The integrated yield for a certain fragment is proportional to the mass of the chemical compound that fragment corresponds to in the SEI layer, and as the Li reference has a thicker SEI, higher yields of the Li2CO3−, LiF2−, LiC2−, C2O−, and C3− species can be found at its surface (Fig. 4 E and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S21), as expected. Among these species, it is noted that LiC2− (originating from ROCO2Li) is two times higher in the SEI of Li reference compared to that in the Li w/VMT, revealing its organic-rich nature, consistent with the XPS results. To conclude, the VMT nanosheet appears to reduce significantly the amount of organic SEI species formed at the Li surface, indicating a strong passivation effect that protects the electrodes from excessive SEI buildup.

Regeneration Demonstration in Li||NMC Full Cells.

As we have unveiled the stable cycling with the magnetic-field-induced nanosheet orientation in Li||Li symmetric cells attributed to the integration of ion flux redistribution and inorganic-rich SEI construction, we further demonstrate the utilization of this concept in Li||NMC full cells for extending the cycle life of Li metal batteries. As shown in Fig. 5A, when pairing Li metal with the NMC cathodes with the practical areal loading of ~20 mg cm−2 and charging and discharging the cell using the pristine electrolyte at the C rate at 1C, corresponding to an areal current density of ~3 mA cm−2, a fast capacity decline can be observed due to the failure of the Li metal side. The charge–discharge voltage profiles show the quick overpotential growth after 100 cycles, resulting in very limited capacity after 300 cycles (Fig. 5C). In comparison, with the VMT nanosheets in the electrolyte, the discharged capacity can be well retained after 250 cycles due to the effects explored in symmetric cells; however, degradation after 250 cycles becomes pronounced (Fig. 5D), probably related to the accumulated barrier resulting from the irregular Li deposition. Therefore, we employed the magnetic-field-induced regeneration step after 150 cycles at which stage the Li metal has not been severely damaged and cycled the cell under the magnetic field for 50 cycles. It can be observed that with such a regeneration step, the cell can continue working properly for the following 150 cycles. The regeneration process was conducted repeatedly, leading to a capacity retention of 67% after 500 cycles, which is significantly higher than the 19% and 11% for the w/VMT and reference cells, respectively (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, the overpotential increase in the cell with such regeneration steps is the smallest among all three cells (Fig. 5E), suggesting that the good cycling stability results from lowering of the overpotential at the Li metal side. The successful demonstration in Li||NMC cells also shows potential to be further transited to other cathode material systems for achieving a longer working life in versatile Li metal batteries.

Analyses on Implementing the Regeneration Design into Battery Production.

Implementing the magnetic-field-induced regeneration processing into commercial battery production and recovery requires critical analyses on the cost of the raw materials, feasibility of the synthesis, production, and processing, and compatibility with the current battery manufacturing line. First of all, with wide applications of soil conditioning, insulation, and construction, VMT is a natural nontoxic mineral with the production of 500,000 tons globally per year (38) while a low price of ~150 US$ per ton available in market. Furthermore, its synthetic process is environmentally benign as water is used as the solvent, and the salts used in the cation exchange process can be recycled. Working as the electrolyte additive with a tiny amount (0.5 wt%), the use of such suspension electrolyte does not require significant retrofitting of the production line, which is highly compatible with the current commercial roll-to-roll battery manufacturing. Beyond the coin cell demonstration in this study, for the most commonly used cylindrical cells in electric vehicles’ battery packs, such magnetic-field-regeneration design still may be utilized, as an external magnetic field along the longitudinal direction of the cylindrical cell could force the nanosheets parallelly aligned with the curly Li/Cu foil. It also should be pointed out that a strong magnetic field is required to align the nanosheets in large battery packs for the regeneration, which could potentially be achieved by using electromagnets rather than the permanent magnets used in this study.

Conclusions

To conclude, we proposed a magnetic-field-induced regeneration strategy by manipulating the orientation of magnetically responsive nanosheets in the suspension electrolyte, therefore prolonging the cycle life of LMBs. Numerical simulations were first conducted to validate the feasibility of the regeneration mechanism. With horizontally oriented nanosheets in the electrolyte, the Li+ flux can be effectively redistributed, resulting in the homogeneous Li+ concentration along the Li metal surface and small overpotential for Li deposition. As a proof-of-concept, VMT nanosheets were utilized to prepare the suspension electrolyte due to their crystal-structure-related anisotropic magnetic properties. The orientation of the VMT nanosheets in the electrolyte could be controlled via an external magnetic field, as validated via optical microscopy. By introducing the magnetic-field regeneration process during the cell operation, the Li||Li symmetric cell exhibited extended cyclability over 350 h at 2 mA cm−2 and 2 mA h cm−2, with the uniform and compact Li deposition microstructure revealed by electron microscopy. The enhanced cycling properties were also sustained by the formation of the inorganic-rich SEI layer with the suspension electrolyte, as shown in depth profiling of the XPS spectra and 3D chemical visualization of the ToF-SIMS spectra. As a demonstration, the Li||NMC full cell with the regeneration steps showed a high capacity retention of 67% after 500 cycles at 1C, significantly higher than that of 19% for the cell without regeneration and 11% for the reference cell. With the merits of low cost and feasible processing, such suspension electrolyte and magnetic-field-induced regeneration design can potentially be implemented into the battery packs with cylindrical cells in electric vehicles. Future efforts could be devoted to achieving a better understanding of the microstructure evolution under the external magnetic field, for example, using an advanced operando optical cell configuration (19). Exploring the potential of utilizing this methodology in other battery systems, e.g., aqueous multivalent batteries, can also help facilitate the development of safe and durable energy storage technology for grid-scale applications. The regeneration concept proposed in this work can potentially bring more design considerations for reviving cells during operation for pursuing energy-dense, long-duration electrochemical energy storage systems.

Materials and Methods

Preparation of VMT Nanosheets.

The synthesis of the VMT nanosheets was reported via an integrated cation exchange and exfoliation process with modification (39). Briefly, 1.0 g of bulk thermally expanded VMT (horticulture VMT from Arizona Agriculture Supply) was added into 100 mL of saturated NaCl solution and reacted at 120 °C under reflux for 24 h. After cooling down, the Na-intercalated bulk VMT was washed with deionized (DI) water three times, and the sediment was collected after centrifugation at 5,000 rpm to remove the excess NaCl. The sediment was further added into 100 mL of 2 M LiCl solution and reacted at 120 °C under reflux for 24 h. After that, the Li-intercalated bulk VMT was washed with DI water three times, collected the sediment, and vacuum-dried at 90 °C. The as-synthesized yellow-brownish powder was added to the DI water with a concentration of 5 mg mL−1 and bath-sonicated for 60 min. The Li-intercalated bulk VMT can be exfoliated to nanosheets via sonication due to the weak interlayer interaction. The obtained VMT nanosheet suspension was further centrifuged at 7,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected to remove the unexfoliated ones. The concentration of the VMT suspension is ~2 mg mL−1. Then, the VMT suspension was freeze-dried to obtain the VMT nanosheet powder, further vacuum-dried at 200 °C for 12 h, and transferred into the glovebox for storage. Note that the exfoliated VMT nanosheets can partially restack during the freeze-drying process, resulting in the final powder with a lateral size of several micrometers.

Electrochemical Measurements.

The VMT suspension electrolytes were prepared by sonicating VMT nanosheets in the traditional electrolyte of 1 M LiPF6 in EC/DEC (1:1 in volume) with concentrations of 0.1 to 2 wt% in sealed vials. The electrolyte with a concentration of 0.5 wt% was chosen for further electrochemical measurements. 2320-type coin cells (MTI Corp.) were assembled in the Ar-filled glove box (Mbraun LABmaster) with H2O and O2 contents both lower than 0.1 ppm. For the Li||Li symmetric cell assembly, polyimide O-rings (25 μm-thick) were placed between the separator (Celgard 2320—20 μm-thick PP/PE/PP trilayer membrane) and Li metals (250 μm-thick) to hold a pristine/suspension electrolyte interlayer. The inner diameter of the O-ring is 1.2 cm, corresponding to an electrochemical active area of 1.13 cm2. A volume of 60 μL of pristine/suspension electrolyte was used in symmetric cells. Symmetric cell measurements were performed on the BioLogic VMP3 multichannel station. Cells were first activated at 0.1 mA cm−2 and 0.1 mA h cm−2 for five cycles and then cycled at 2 mA cm−2 and 2 mA h cm−2. For the regeneration process, a home-made magnetic setup was supplied to continuously provide an external horizontal magnetic field to the cells for 20 h after 100-h normal cycling. In this setup, the cell was placed between and 15 mm away from the surface of two N52 magnets (4 cm × 4 cm × 2 cm), which supplied a magnetic field of 420 mT. For the vertical magnetic field, a single magnet was used, and the cell was placed on top of the magnet, with a distance between the cell and magnet surface of 3 mm, providing a magnetic field of 430 mT. NMC cathodes were prepared by mixing LiNi1/3Co1/3Mn1/3O2, Super P, and polyvinylidene fluoride with a weight ratio of 90:5:5 in N-Methyl-2-pyrrolidone, cast onto the Al foil, dried in 90 °C vacuum oven, and roll-to-roll calendered to a porosity of ~35%. For the Li||NMC full cell assembly, NMC cathodes (~20 mg cm−2) were paired with the Li metal anodes, and a polyimide O-ring was placed between the Li metal and separator. The size of the NMC cathodes is 1.13 cm2, which is the same as the inner size of the O-ring. A volume of 40 μL of pristine/suspension electrolyte was used in Li||NMC full cells. The Li||NMC full cells were first activated at the C rate of 0.1C (1C = 150 mA g−1) for two cycles and then cycled at 1C (~3 mA cm−2) at the electrochemical window of 2.8 to 4.25 V. For the regeneration process, the magnetic field was continuously applied for 50 cycles after 150 cycles at the normal cycling condition without the field.

Characterizations.

XRD patterns of the VMT bulk and nanosheets were conducted on a Rigaku Miniflex 600 Diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα (λ = 1.5406 Å) radiation in the range of 5° < 2θ < 35°. The morphology of the VMT nanosheets was imaged by the SEM/STEM using Hitachi S5500. EDS mapping was performed with Hitachi S5500 in the SEM mode. The Li microstructures of Li metals after 20-h cycling in symmetric cells were characterized by the Apreo 2C LoVac SEM. For the w/VMT&H one, the symmetric cell was first cycled normally without the magnetic field for 10 h and then cycled under the magnetic field for another 10 h. XPS spectra were characterized on VersaProbe4 X-ray photoelectron spectrometer. For the depth profiling of the cycled Li, sputtering was conducted using an Ar gas cluster ion beam for 40 min to remove the top ~4 nm surface layer. Optical images were collected using the Olympus BX51 optical microscope with halogen light. The ToF-SIMS analysis was performed on an ionTOF M6 instrument equipped with a Bi nanoprobe analysis ion gun and a Cs sputter ion gun. The depth profiles were acquired sequentially by raster scanning 100 µm × 100 µm areas with the Bi+ analysis ion beam (30 kV) followed by Cs+ (500 V) ion beam sputtering of 300 µm × 300 µm areas centered around the analysis areas. The Bi analysis beam was set in either high mass or high lateral resolution mode, depending on the desired mass or spatial resolution, respectively. All detected ions had negative polarity while the data were acquired at a pressure better than 10 mbar. The sputtering rate of Li was determined previously at 0.175 nm s−1 (40).

Numerical Simulation.

The numerical simulation of the Li deposition was conducted using COMSOL Multiphysics software in 2D models. Three different configurations were constructed to represent cases for electrolytes without nanosheets, with vertically aligned nanosheets, and with horizontally aligned nanosheets. In each configuration, a reference electrode was set at the top and a working electrode was set at the bottom. The reference electrode was assigned a positive voltage, while the working electrode (Li metal) was preset at 0 V to simulate the Li plating process. Three initial nucleation sites were set on the working electrodes. The nanosheets were set to be both electronically and ionically insulating and physically prohibit ions from diffusing through the nanosheet plane.

The numerical simulation of Li+ diffusion trajectory under the magnetic field was conducted using COMSOL Multiphysics in 3D models. The configuration was constructed as a cylinder, and a protrusion was set at the subface of the cylinder. The subface of the cylinder represented the anode and the protrusion represented the initial Li protrusion on the anode. The rest of the space represented the electrolyte where Li+ could diffuse. The radius and height of the cylinder were 5 μm and 8 μm, respectively, and the radius and height of the cone-shaped protrusion were 4 μm and 5 μm, respectively. Positively charged particles released from the top of the cylinder were used to simulate the movement of Li+ that diffused from the cathode to the anode. The initial speed of the charged particles was 3 × 10−7 m s−1. The charged particles possessed a charge number of 1 and a rest mass of 6 × 10−25 kg, considering the mass of the solvent molecules and anions in the solvation shell. The charged particles were subject to both electric field at a current density of 10 mA cm−2 and magnetic field with a strength of 0.1 T. The current flow was vertical to the anode plane in the region far away from the protrusion, while curved toward the protrusion due to the tip effect.
