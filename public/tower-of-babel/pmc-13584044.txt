Route-dependent efficacy of wild mint (Mentha longifolia) extract supplementation in heat-stressed broilers: physiological, intestinal and productive responses
Authors: Mahmoud Hosny, Elwy A Ashour, Reda S Mohamed, Ali O Osman, Shubash C Das, Rania AEH Mohamed, Khairiah M Alwutayd, Nawal Al-Hoshani, Felwa Thagfan, Eman Alhomaidi, Zakiah N Almohawes, Mohamed E Abd El-Hack
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeat exposure can impair intestinal function, physiological homeostasis, and productive performance in broiler chickens. This study evaluated the effects of wild mint powder extract (WMPE) according to administration route and treatment level in Cobb broiler chickens reared under summer heat conditions. A total of 600 one-week-old Cobb broiler chickens were allocated to a 2 × 5 factorial arrangements comprising two administration routes (drinking water or feed) and five treatment levels (negative control, antibiotic control, and WMPE supplementation at 1, 2, or 4 mL/kg or L, according to the administration route). Significant administration route × treatment interactions were observed for cecal total bacterial and Clostridium counts, intestinal villous length, villous width, absorptive surface area, and growth performance (P<0.001). WMPE supplementation generally reduced cecal bacterial populations and improved intestinal morphometric characteristics, although the magnitude of these responses differed between administration routes and treatment levels. In particular, birds receiving 4 mL WMPE/L through drinking water exhibited the highest final body weight and daily body weight gain, whereas the corresponding dietary treatment produced a less pronounced growth response. Significant route × treatment interactions were also detected for serum lipid profile, antioxidant status, and immunoglobulin concentrations (P<0.0001). The dietary administration of 4 mL WMPE/kg produced the most pronounced responses in several antioxidant and lipid-related variables, whereas the highest serum IgG concentration was observed in birds receiving 1 mL WMPE/L through drinking water. Significant interactions were further detected for selected serum biochemical variables, carcass characteristics, and meat composition (P<0.05). Overall, the findings demonstrate that the biological responses to WMPE differed according to both administration route and treatment level, and that the most favorable response depended on the specific outcome evaluated. These findings emphasize the importance of administration route when assessing WMPE as a phytogenic supplement for broiler chickens exposed to summer heat conditions.Keywords: Phytogenic feed additive, Wild mint, Oxidative stress, Intestinal integrity, Poultry nutrition

Introduction

The poultry industry is a crucial component in fulfilling the growing global demand for quality animal protein. However, maintaining optimal productivity is increasingly challenging because broiler chickens are particularly susceptible to heat stress owing to their rapid growth rate, high metabolic heat production, feather cover, and limited capacity for heat dissipation (Vandana et al., 2021; Apalowo et al., 2024). Exposure to high ambient temperatures can negatively affect feed intake, nutrient utilization, growth performance, antioxidant defenses, immune competence, and carcass characteristics, ultimately reducing production efficiency and increasing economic losses in the poultry industry (Onagbesan et al., 2023; Attia et al., 2024). Thus, nutritional strategies that can alleviate the adverse effects of heat exposure are increasingly important for sustainable broiler production.

Following the worldwide ban on the utilization of antibiotic growth promoters, phytogenic feed additives have received considerable attention as potential nutritional strategies for supporting poultry health and productivity (Youssef et al., 2024a, b; Saber et al., 2026). Plant-derived bioactive composites are of special attention because of their antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties, which may improve intestinal health, nutrient utilization and strengthen the physiological resilience of birds exposed to environmental stressors (Dosoky et al., 2024; Reda et al., 2026). However, Nevertheless, the efficacy of phytogenic additives depends not only on their chemical composition but may also be influenced by how they are delivered to the bird.

Mentha longifolia L. (wild mint) is a medicinal plant with potential as a phytogenic additive because it contains phenolic acids, flavonoids, rosmarinic acid, caffeic acid derivatives, and volatile constituents such as menthol and menthone (Tafrihi et al., 2021; Shahar and Chongtham, 2024). These compounds have been associated with antioxidant and antibacterial activities and may contribute to intestinal integrity, physiological status, and immune function in animals (Bahadori et al., 2018; Gharib et al., 2021; Abd El-Hack et al., 2023). Prior studies shown the beneficial effects of mint-derived products on growth performance, antioxidant capacity, and gut microbial equilibrium in poultry. However, much of the available evidence concerns other Mentha species, essential oils, or conventional dietary supplementation, whereas information on M. longifolia powder extract remains limited (Ahluwalia and Kaur, 2022).

The route of administration may be particularly relevant under heat-exposure conditions. Compared with dietary supplementation, delivery through drinking water may provide more direct access to the gastrointestinal tract and greater flexibility in supplementation, while potentially reducing losses of sensitive phytochemicals associated with feed processing and storage (Brglez Mojzer et al., 2016; Domínguez-Avila et al., 2017). In addition, water consumption generally increases relative to feed intake during hot conditions, which may influence the intake pattern of water-delivered phytogenic compounds. Despite these potential advantages, direct comparisons of feed- and water-based delivery of phytogenic extracts remain limited, particularly in heat-exposed broilers.

So, an important knowledge gap is whether the biological responses to M. longifolia powder extract vary according to administration route and supplementation level under heat-exposure conditions. This question is relevant because the same phytogenic preparation may produce different responses depending on its mode of delivery.

Therefore, the present study aimed to characterize the phytochemical composition and antioxidant capacity of wild mint powder extract (WMPE) and to evaluate the effects of graded WMPE supplementation administered through feed or drinking water on growth performance, intestinal bacterial counts and morphology, serum biochemical and antioxidant responses, immunity, carcass traits, and tissue characteristics of broiler chickens exposed to summer heat conditions. The study also compared the responses associated with the two administration routes to determine whether delivery route modifies the efficacy of WMPE supplementation.

Materials and methods

The trial was directed at the Poultry Research Farm, a division of Zagazig University's poultry department in Egypt. The trials followed the guidelines established by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC-ZU- 2026) and were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Poultry Department, Agriculture Faculty, Zagazig University.

Wild mint solution formulation

Wild mint was collected from 2 M Group, 10th of Ramadan City, El-Sharqia, Egypt. Wild mint powder extract (WMPE) was prepared by extracting 900 g of finely ground wild mint powder in 1 L of water-ethanol extraction solvent. The mixture was stirred intermittently at room temperature for 24 h, and then filtered throughout Whatman No. 42 filter paper (Hawach Scientific Co., Ltd., Xi'an, China). The obtained extract was stored in amber glass bottles at 4°C protected from direct light until use. WMPE was dissolved in fresh drinking water and prepared fresh each day immediately prior to administration to ensure phytochemical stability. The prepared solutions were thoroughly mixed prior to presentation to the birds and were available throughout the daily drinking period. Visual inspection prior to administration showed no precipitation. WMPE supplementation was started in the drinking water of chicks at 7 days of age and maintained till 35 days of age. All WMPE batches were prepared from the same raw material source and under the same extraction protocol to maintain consistency in the whole experiment. The extract was stored under the same conditions and no visible changes in color, odor or precipitation was observed during the storage period, indicating acceptable stability before administration.

Sample preparation

Ten grams of wild mint powder were extracted in 200 mL of 70% methanol and constantly agitated for three hours. The extract was filtered with Whatman No. 2 filter paper (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA). Methanol was eliminated under reduced pressure utilizing a BÜCHI Rotavapor B-480 (BÜCHI Labortechnik AG, Flawil, Switzerland) at 45°C. The concentrated extract was subsequently lyophilized utilizing a Heto PowerDry LL300 Freeze Dryer (Thermo Electron Corporation, Waltham, MA, USA). The desiccated extract was preserved at -20°C in opaque amber containers, shielded from light until subsequent chemical tests (Negm et al., 2025).

Total phenolic compounds (TPCs) estimation

The total phenolic compounds in the methanolic extracts (1000 mg/mL) were quantified using the Folin-Ciocalteu method. The standard curve was established using gallic acid (10–1000 mg/mL) as the typical phenolic constituent. The formula is y = 0.001x + 0.0563 (R² = 0.9792). Three milliliters of diluted Folin-Ciocalteu reagent, two milliliters of 7.5% sodium carbonate, and one milliliter of each sample or standard were combined and incubated for thirty minutes at 25°C in the absence of light. Absorbance at 760 nm was measured using a JENWAY 6405 UV/Vis spectrophotometer (Agha et al., 2026). All phytochemical analyses were conducted in triplicate (n = 3).

Total flavonoids (TFs) estimation

The approach developed by Ordonez et al. (2006) was employed to quantify TFs content. The normal curve for quercetin (10-1000 mg/mL), expressed as y = 0.0012x + 0.008 (R² = 0.944), was utilized. 1 mL of 20 g/L AlCl₃ in ethanol was combined with 1 mL of quercetin extract or solution. Absorbance was measured at 420 nm using a spectrophotometer. Every measurement was conducted in triplicate (n = 3).

Antioxidant activity estimation

The DPPH test was employed to assess the free radical scavenging activity of WMPE, as outlined by Sirivibulkovit et al. (2018). Each extract (1 mL) was combined with methanolic DPPH solution (3 mL) and incubated for 30 minutes in the dark at ambient temperature. The absorbance was recorded at 520 nm using the identical UV/Vis spectrophotometer. The scavenging concentration required for 50% suppression of DPPH radicals (SC50) was determined as outlined by Deeb et al. (2025). All DPPH assays were conducted in triplicate (n = 3), and the average result was utilized for statistical analysis.

Birds, diets, and design

The trial was conducted during the summer season using 600 one-week-old Cobb broiler chicks. The study followed a 2 × 5 factorial arrangements to evaluate two routes of wild mint powder extract (WMPE) administration (drinking water or feed) and five treatments within each route (negative control, positive control, and three WMPE supplementation levels). Before chick placement, all pens were numbered sequentially, and birds were individually weighed to ensure comparable initial body weights. Pens were then randomly assigned to treatments, after which birds were randomly allocated to the designated pens while maintaining equal bird numbers and similar average initial body weight among treatments.

Five treatment levels were evaluated within each administration method. For the drinking-water route, T1 represented the Water × Control group and received untreated drinking water, T2 represented the Water × Antibiotic group and received oxytetracycline (10%) at 1 g/L drinking water, and T3, T4, and T5 represented the Water × WMPE groups supplemented with 1.0, 2.0, and 4.0 mL WMPE/L, respectively. For the dietary route, T6 represented the Feed × Control group and received the basal diet without additives, T7 represented the Feed × Antibiotic group and received oxytetracycline (10%) at 1 g/kg diet, and T8, T9, and T10 represented the Feed × WMPE groups supplemented with 1, 2, and 4 mL WMPE/kg diet, respectively. Accordingly, T1 and T6 were both negative-control groups but differed in administration method and therefore represented the two control cells of the 2 × 5 factorial design rather than two different treatment levels. Water supplementation was provided three times weekly throughout the experimental period until 35 days of age. The same oxytetracycline 10% commercial product (OXY W.S.P, Joycome Animal Health, Hebei, China) was used for both administration routes. The product was provided at 1 g/L drinking water or 1 g/kg diet. The selected dose was based on Ferdous et al. (2020).

Upon arrival, chicks were brooded in an open-sided poultry house at approximately 34°C during the first three days, after which the ambient temperature was gradually reduced according to standard commercial management practices. A photoperiod of 23 h light and 1 h darkness was maintained throughout the experimental period. Birds were housed in floor pens (1.30 × 1.30 × 0.50 m) with ad libitum access to feed and drinking water. All birds were reared under identical management, hygienic, and environmental conditions.

The basal diets were designed to fulfill or above the dietary needs established by NRC (1994) and were provided in two feeding periods: a starter diet from 7 to 21 days of age and a finisher diet from 22 to 35 days of age (Table 1). NRC was used as a standardized reference framework for the experimental diets rather than as a contemporary commercial performance specification.

Data collection

Microbial count in cecal samples

At the conclusion of the study, 5 birds from every group were randomly selected to prepare cecal extracts at 35 days of age. Cecal tissue from each group was homogenized; 10 g of the homogenate were mixed with 90 ml of sterile water peptone and incubated for 0.5 hours. CO2-purged and preserved at -80°C in sterile glass containers till microbiological assays were conducted. Serial decimal dilutions up to 10-7 were performed, and bacterial counts were recorded. Ten grams of the lysate were diluted in ninety milliliters of sterile peptone water and agitated for thirty minutes to achieve a dilution of 10-1. The total bacterial count (TBC) and Clostridium spp. were assessed using selective culture media as per the Oxoid (2006) methodology. The bacterial quantities are shown as log10 colony-forming units per gram.

Histopathological technique

Samples from the intestines and livers of chickens were placed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for a duration of 24 hours. We dried things out with different amounts of alcohol, and then we used a clearing agent like xylene I and then xylene II. We then put the organs in paraffin wax to make blocks of paraffin. We used a microtome (Leica RM 2155, London, UK) to cut paraffin sections that were 5 microns thick. We stained the sections with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) (Abd El-Aziz et al., 2025).

Intestinal histomorphometry: For each bird specimen, the length (VL), width (VW), and absorptive surface area (ASA) of 50 properly aligned villi from each segment of the intestine were quantified and averaged. The heights of the villi were quantified from the apex to the base, while the widths were assessed at the midpoint among the top and bottom. A light microscope equipped with a full-HD microscopic camera and image analysis software (Leica Microsystems, Germany) was utilized to examine tissue sections. Image analysis software was employed to quantify the criteria for statistical evaluation. We identified ASA mm2 by the following methods: The area of the villus (mm²) is calculated by multiplying the height of the villus by its breadth (Rehman et al., 2016).

Blood biochemical investigation

Samples of blood were obtained by euthanizing the same five birds from every group after an overnight fast (at 35 D of age), collected in non-heparinized tubes, and subsequently centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 15 minutes at 4°C to isolate serum, which was stored at -20°C until biochemical analysis. The serum samples were utilized to measure serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) activity using commercial kits from Reactivos GPL CHEMELEX, S.A. Pol. Ind. Castells, C/Industria 113, Nau J 08420, conducted spectrophotometric determinations of total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), globulin (GLOB), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) utilizing a semi-automated spectrophotometer (Erba-Chem7, Germany) and diagnostic reagent kits (Spectrum, Egypt) (Arisha et al., 2024). Serum urea, creatinine, and immunological responses (IgG and IgM) were quantified utilizing commercial ELISA kits from Thermo Scientific Companies Serving, Egypt (Youssef et al., 2023). The approach by Abd El-Hack et al. (2017) found oxidative indicators including malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), reduced glutathione (GSH), and glutathione-S-transferase (GST).

Growth performance and carcass characteristics

Chicks were weighed individually at 7, 20, and 35 days to determine live body weight (LBW) and daily body weight growth (DBWG). The average daily feed intake (FI) and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were computed. We calculated the European Production Efficiency Factors (EPEF) using the formula: EPEF = [livability (%) × final live weight (kg)] / [age (d) × FCR] × 100 (Marcu et al., 2013).

At the conclusion of the study, five randomly selected slain birds from each group were designated for carcass assessment at 35 days of age. The birds were deprived of food overnight, weighed, and murdered in accordance with Islamic tradition, which entails severing the jugular vein with a sharp knife and allowing the blood to drain for 2 to 4 minutes to assess the quality of the carcass. We assessed the carcass weight and quantified the total number of consumable organs, including the liver, heart, gizzard, spleen, and abdominal fat. Carcass percentage = (Weight of edible carcass / Total carcass weight) × 100. Dressing percentage = (Carcass weight / Live chicken weight) times 100.

Meat quality

At 35 days of age, breast muscle samples were collected from the slaughtered birds and transported under refrigerated conditions to the laboratory for analysis. Moisture, crude protein, crude fat, ash, and carbohydrate contents were determined according to AOAC (2012). The samples were analyzed in the laboratories of the Faculty of Agriculture at Zagazig University

Twenty-five grams of each sample were aseptically excised and homogenized with 225 ml of 0.1% sterile peptone water in a laboratory blender for one minute to create a 1:10 dilution, followed by ten-fold dilutions up to 106. Particular medium (Oxoid, 2006) was employed to assess the total bacterial count (TBC).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using the General Linear Model (GLM) approach within SPSS statistical software (Version 26.0; IBM SPSS Statistics, Armonk, NY, USA, 2019). The experimental design utilized a 2 × 5 factorial configuration, comprising two modalities of WMPE delivery (drinking water or feed) and five treatment groups for each approach. The five treatment levels (control, antibiotic, 1, 2, and 4 mL WMPE) were common to both administration methods; therefore, T1 and T6 were the corresponding control cells for the water and feed routes, respectively, rather than two independent treatment levels. The same structure applied to T2/T7, T3/T8, T4/T9, and T5/T10. Consequently, the ten experimental groups constituted the 10 cells generated by the 2 × 5 factorial arrangement, while the statistical model tested the main effects of administration method and treatment level and their interaction. Each treatment consisted of five identical floor pens, each containing 12 birds. For all growth performance variables (body weight, body weight gain, feed intake, feed conversion ratio, and performance index), pens served as the experimental unit, whereas for blood biochemical, antioxidant, immunological, microbiological, histological, and carcass measurements, individual birds constituted the experimental unit. Upon detecting substantial variations across treatment means, comparisons were conducted utilizing Tukey's post-hoc test. Statistical significance was established at P<0.05.

Phytochemical profile and antioxidant potential of wild mint powder extract

The WMPE exhibited measurable total phenolic and flavonoid contents and concentration-dependent DPPH radical-scavenging activity (Table 3). The extract contained 37.00 mg GAE/g extract of total phenolic compounds and 15.33 mg QE/g extract of total flavonoids, while DPPH activity increased with increasing extract concentration, showing a maximum of 69.33% with 1000 mg/mL extract concentration.

Cecal bacterial counts

A significant administration route × treatment interaction was detected for both Clostridium and total bacterial counts (TBC; P<0.001; Table 4). The response to WMPE therefore depended on the route of administration. The lowest Clostridium count was observed in birds receiving 2 mL WMPE through feed (1.99 log10 CFU/g), whereas the lowest TBC occurred in birds receiving 4 mL WMPE through feed (5.18 log10 CFU/g). In contrast, WMPE administered through drinking water also reduced both bacterial measures relative to the corresponding control, although the magnitude of the response varied with supplementation level. Across administration routes, WMPE supplementation reduced Clostridium counts compared with the control and antibiotic treatments (P<0.001), while TBC was lower with feed than with drinking-water administration (P=0.018).

Histopathological analysis

Administration route × treatment interactions were significant for villous length, villous width, and absorptive surface area (ASA; P<0.001; Table 5). The magnitude of the morphological response was particularly evident with drinking-water supplementation. The greatest villous length was observed with 2 mL WMPE/L drinking water (1,278 μm), whereas the greatest villous width and ASA were observed with 4 mL WMPE/L drinking water (192.33 μm and 0.232 mm², respectively). The corresponding control groups had substantially smaller villous dimensions. Overall, drinking-water administration produced greater villous width and ASA than feed administration (P=0.015 and 0.006, respectively).

Discussion

The phytochemical study of WMPE revealed elevated concentrations of total phenolic and flavonoid compounds, exhibiting robust DPPH radical scavenging action. These data substantiate that wild mint is a prolific natural source of biologically active phytochemicals capable of eliciting potent antioxidant effects (Gharib et al., 2021). Phenolic chemicals and flavonoids are significant plant secondary metabolites that contribute to antioxidant action. Their chemical structures contain hydroxyl groups capable of donating electrons or hydrogen atoms to neutralize free radicals and disrupt oxidative chain reactions (Zeb, 2020). Consequently, these chemicals safeguard biological macromolecules, including lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, from oxidative damage (Mutha et al., 2021). The current work demonstrated a significant dose-dependent enhancement in DPPH scavenging activity, signifying WMPE's substantial ability to contribute reduction equivalents and neutralize reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Tafrihi et al., 2021).

As well as direct scavenging of free radicals, plant polyphenols can also stimulate endogenous antioxidant defense systems. Flavonoids have been shown to activate transcription factors such as Nrf2 and subsequently increase the expression of antioxidant enzymes (Mendonca and Soliman, 2020; Shahcheraghi et al., 2023). Therefore, the improvement in antioxidant status in broiler chickens during the experiment was probably due to the rich phytochemical profile of WMPE. The phenolic and flavonoid contents obtained in this study were similar to those reported for other Mentha species. The most important antioxidant constituents of mint plants are rosmarinic acid, caffeic acid, luteolin, eriocitrin and other flavonoid derivatives (Ćavar Zeljković et al., 2021). These compounds possess potent radical-scavenging, metal-chelating, anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial activities and are useful natural substitutes for synthetic antioxidants (Ahmad et al., 2025). The significant antioxidant capacity of WMPE may account for numerous advantageous physiological responses noted in this experiment, including improved growth performance, enhanced antioxidant defenses, superior immune responses, healthier lipid profiles, and optimized gut microbial balance. The current findings collectively indicate that the biological activity of WMPE is predominantly due to its elevated levels of phenolic and flavonoid compounds and their potent antioxidant properties (Bahadori et al., 2018).

The current study demonstrated that the biological responses to WMPE varied according to both supplementation level and administration route, as indicated by the significant route × treatment interactions observed for several intestinal, physiological, and productive traits. Broilers receiving WMPE through drinking water showed more pronounced responses for several measured variables at specific supplementation levels than birds receiving the corresponding treatments through feed. However, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of greater bioavailability because bioavailability and systemic absorption were not directly measured in the present study. The route-dependent responses may instead reflect differences in intake pattern, exposure of the gastrointestinal tract to the extract, and the interaction between WMPE concentration and the mode of delivery. Drinking-water supplementation may reduce potential losses of sensitive phytochemicals associated with feed processing and storage and may provide a different pattern of gastrointestinal exposure, particularly under hot conditions when water consumption increases relative to feed consumption (Brglez Mojzer et al., 2016; Domínguez-Avila et al., 2017; Yu et al., 2025). Thus, the significant route × treatment interactions observed throughout the experiment provide a stronger basis for discussing administration route than the route main effects alone.

In poultry, gut microbiota plays an important role in digestion, immune function, pathogen exclusion, and overall intestinal health (Yadav and Jha, 2019). In the present study, the significant route × treatment interaction for cecal Clostridium and total bacterial counts indicates that the antimicrobial response to WMPE depended on both supplementation level and administration route. The reduction in Clostridium counts was particularly evident at specific WMPE levels, whereas the response of total bacterial counts differed according to the delivery route. These findings agree with previous research in poultry using mint extracts and phytochemicals as feed additives (Seidavi et al., 2022; Abbas and Shaaban, 2026). Importantly, because the present study relied on culture-based bacterial enumeration rather than sequencing, these results indicate changes in selected bacterial groups rather than comprehensive modulation of the gut microbiome.

The antibacterial activity of WMPE may be related, at least in part, to its phenolic and flavonoid content. Both groups of phytochemicals have been reported to exhibit antibacterial effects against a range of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria (Aminullah et al., 2025). Phenolic compounds can affect bacterial cell membranes, alter membrane permeability and enzyme activity, and interfere with nucleic acid synthesis, thereby restricting bacterial growth (De Rossi et al., 2025). These mechanisms provide a plausible explanation for the reductions in Clostridium and total bacterial counts observed in WMPE-treated birds, although the present experiment did not directly determine the molecular mechanism responsible for these changes.

The decline in Clostridium counts is particularly relevant because several Clostridium species have been associated with intestinal disturbances, impaired nutrient utilization, and intestinal inflammation in chickens. Accordingly, the lower counts observed following WMPE supplementation may have contributed to the improved intestinal environment, although direct measurements of nutrient absorption or intestinal inflammatory status were not performed. The reduction in bacterial counts may be associated with the antimicrobial activity of mint polyphenols (Kar et al., 2025). The significant route × treatment interaction further suggests that this response was not uniform across WMPE levels and that the effectiveness of WMPE against the measured bacterial populations depended on how the extract was delivered. Repeated exposure of intestinal microorganisms to phytochemicals may influence their antimicrobial activity and contribute to changes in the intestinal microbial environment (Dingeo et al., 2020). In addition to antimicrobial effects, WMPE may possess prebiotic-like properties that could favor beneficial microorganisms while limiting potentially harmful bacteria (Dingeo et al., 2020; Chowdhury et al., 2023). Nevertheless, this possibility requires further confirmation using culture-independent microbiome sequencing because the present study did not quantify beneficial bacterial taxa or overall microbial community structure.

The current study also demonstrated route- and level-dependent changes in intestinal morphology, with significant route × treatment interactions for villous length, villous width, and absorptive surface area. The most pronounced morphological responses were observed at specific WMPE levels delivered through drinking water, particularly for villous width and absorptive surface area. This pattern indicates that increasing WMPE concentration did not produce a uniform response across administration routes; rather, the intestinal response depended on the combination of dose and delivery method. These findings are consistent with previous reports describing improvements in intestinal structure following supplementation with phytogenic additives. For example, peppermint supplementation has been associated with an increased villus height-to-crypt depth ratio and greater absorptive area in broilers (Hamedi et al., 2017; Andhika et al., 2020). Likewise, protection of hepatic tissue from oxidative stress has been reported following supplementation with plant extracts containing phenolic compounds (Krstić et al., 2025).

The improvement in intestinal morphometric characteristics may be associated with the phenolic and flavonoid constituents of WMPE, which possess antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial activities (Matsumura et al., 2023). Increased villous dimensions are generally associated with a greater epithelial surface area available for nutrient digestion and absorption (Wang et al., 2025). Therefore, the greater villous dimensions observed in particular WMPE route × level combinations may have contributed to the favorable feed-utilization responses observed in the corresponding groups. However, because digestibility and nutrient absorption were not directly measured, this interpretation should be considered a plausible biological association rather than a demonstrated mechanism.

Intestinal morphology may improve when oxidative stress and inflammation within the intestinal mucosa are reduced. Wild mint contains phenolic compounds capable of scavenging reactive oxygen species and potentially supporting epithelial integrity and tissue recovery (Hejna et al., 2021). Flavonoids have also been reported to support tight-junction integrity and intestinal barrier function (Panwar et al., 2021). Taken together, the concurrent changes in bacterial counts and villous morphology in the present study suggest that WMPE may have supported intestinal structural integrity under summer heat conditions. The significant route × treatment interactions indicate that this response was dependent on the specific combination of WMPE level and administration route rather than being attributable to route alone. A healthier intestinal environment could support absorptive efficiency and nutrient utilization under heat-exposure conditions; however, intestinal permeability, tight-junction proteins, and microbial translocation were not directly assessed in this study.

The intestinal and physiological responses were accompanied by significant route × treatment interactions for serum lipid profile, antioxidant status, and humoral immunity. WMPE supplementation was associated with lower serum TC, TG, and LDL concentrations and higher HDL concentrations, while GPx, GST, and GSH were increased and MDA was reduced. Serum IgG and IgM concentrations were also increased. Importantly, the magnitude of these responses differed among WMPE levels and between administration routes, indicating that the physiological effects of WMPE were dependent on the specific treatment combination. These findings are generally consistent with previous studies reporting improvements in serum lipid profiles, antioxidant capacity, and immune responses following supplementation with mint extracts and other phytogenic additives (Alghirani et al., 2021; Abdelli et al., 2021; Alkassar et al., 2025).

The hypolipidemic response to WMPE may be related to its phenolic and flavonoid constituents. Plant polyphenols have been reported to influence cholesterol metabolism by affecting hepatic cholesterol synthesis, including pathways involving HMG-CoA reductase (Mahdavi et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2025). They may also influence bile acid secretion, cholesterol excretion, and lipid catabolism. The increase in HDL observed in the present study may be consistent with improved reverse cholesterol transport (Weerawatanakorn et al., 2024). However, because hepatic lipid-metabolism pathways and enzyme activities were not measured, these mechanisms remain speculative. More importantly, the significant route × treatment interaction suggests that the lipid response cannot be adequately explained by the WMPE main effect alone and that the response varied according to the administration route and supplementation level.

The antioxidant effects observed in the present study were also dependent on the treatment combination. WMPE contains phenolic compounds whose hydroxyl groups can donate hydrogen atoms and participate in the neutralization of reactive oxygen species (Bolaños-Cardet et al., 2026). Plant phenolics may also influence endogenous antioxidant pathways, including Nrf2-associated responses, which can regulate antioxidant enzymes such as GPx and GST (Moratilla-Rivera et al., 2023). The increased GSH concentration observed in WMPE-treated birds is consistent with enhanced cellular antioxidant capacity and glutathione-dependent protection against oxidative challenges (Akbari et al., 2022).

The decrease in MDA further indicates a lower degree of lipid peroxidation in WMPE-treated birds. MDA is a commonly used marker of lipid oxidative damage and oxidative stress (Bacou et al., 2021). The lower MDA concentrations observed following WMPE supplementation are therefore consistent with greater protection against oxidative damage to cellular membrane lipids (Fathi et al., 2025). The fact that MDA, GPx, GST, and GSH responded differently across route × treatment combinations further support the interpretation that the antioxidant effect of WMPE was dependent on both supplementation level and delivery route rather than representing a simple overall route effect.

The enhancement of humoral immunity, reflected by increased serum IgG and IgM concentrations, may also be related to the antioxidant and immunomodulatory properties of mint-derived phytochemicals. Polyphenolic compounds have been reported to influence lymphocyte proliferation, macrophage activity, and cytokine secretion (Maheshwari et al., 2022; Ferreira et al., 2024). Improved antioxidant status may additionally help protect immune cells from oxidative damage and support immune function. In the present study, the significant route × treatment effects on IgG and IgM indicate that the humoral response varied with the combination of WMPE level and administration route. Thus, the enhanced immune responses may be viewed as part of a broader physiological response to WMPE, occurring alongside changes in antioxidant status and intestinal health. However, the present data do not establish a direct causal relationship among microbial modulation, oxidative status, and humoral immunity.

The improvements in antioxidant and immune status were accompanied by route- and treatment-dependent changes in serum biochemical parameters, suggesting that the physiological response to WMPE extended beyond the intestinal tract. Importantly, significant route × treatment interactions were detected for several serum biochemical variables, including total protein, albumin, globulin, albumin-to-globulin ratio, AST, urea, and creatinine. Thus, the responses were not uniform across WMPE supplementation levels but varied according to the combination of supplementation level and administration route. The increase in serum total protein and albumin observed in particular WMPE treatment combinations may reflect differences in protein metabolism and physiological status, although protein synthesis and nutrient utilization were not directly measured. Albumin is synthesized primarily by hepatocytes and is considered an indicator of hepatic synthetic function (Khursheed et al., 2017). Similarly, increased globulin concentrations may be related to immune status because globulins include immunologically active proteins (Ruzikulov et al., 2026). The phenolic and flavonoid constituents of WMPE may have contributed to these responses through their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities, which have been associated with protection against oxidative cellular damage (Tafrihi et al., 2021; Diab et al., 2022). However, the significant interaction effects indicate that these responses should be interpreted according to the specific WMPE level and administration route rather than as a uniform effect of WMPE supplementation.

The decrease in AST activity in WMPE-treated birds is of particular interest because elevated serum transaminase activity is commonly associated with hepatocellular injury or increased membrane leakage (Haghighat-Jahromi et al., 2025). In the present study, the significant route × treatment interaction for AST indicates that the magnitude of the response depended on both WMPE level and administration route. Lower AST values in selected WMPE treatment combinations may be consistent with a hepatoprotective response. Polyphenolic compounds can stabilize cellular membranes, scavenge reactive oxygen species, and reduce lipid peroxidation, thereby potentially limiting cellular damage and enzyme leakage into the circulation (Rudrapal et al., 2024). Additionally, phytochemicals from mint may influence inflammatory signaling pathways and oxidative stress-related processes in hepatic tissue (Kmail, 2024).

Serum urea and creatinine also showed significant route × treatment interactions, indicating that the response of these biochemical indicators depended on the WMPE level and its mode of administration. Urea and creatinine are commonly used indicators of renal status, and increased concentrations may be associated with altered renal filtration or increased tissue catabolism (Karimi-Dehkordi et al., 2023). The lower concentrations observed in selected WMPE treatment combinations may therefore indicate a favorable physiological response, although renal function was not directly assessed in the present study. Phenolic compounds have been reported to support renal blood flow, preserve nephron integrity, and reduce oxidative damage through endogenous antioxidant mechanisms (Elazab et al., 2021; Peng et al., 2023). The more pronounced responses observed with some drinking-water treatments may reflect differences in intake pattern and gastrointestinal exposure to WMPE; however, the present study did not measure absorption or systemic bioavailability. Therefore, it would be inappropriate to attribute the route-dependent responses specifically to enhanced bioavailability. Water supplementation may nevertheless provide a practical means of delivering phytochemicals during hot conditions, when changes in water and feed intake can alter the exposure of birds to supplemented compounds (Domínguez-Avila et al., 2017).

The above findings are consistent with earlier studies of phytogenic feed additives in poultry. Several studies have reported that mint extract, peppermint oil, and other medicinal plants can influence serum protein concentrations and biochemical indicators associated with hepatic and renal status (Azab and Albasha, 2018; Diab et al., 2022; Oyeleye et al., 2023).

Histopathological findings provided additional evidence that the biochemical responses were accompanied by differences in hepatic tissue appearance. Diffuse hepatocellular vacuolation observed in the control group is commonly associated with metabolic stress and cellular degeneration (Khalaf et al., 2020). In contrast, birds receiving WMPE generally showed better-preserved hepatic architecture, with relatively normal hepatic acini and vascular structures. The more favorable hepatic appearance in WMPE-treated groups, together with the route- and treatment-dependent changes in serum biochemical indicators, supports the possibility that WMPE supplementation helped maintain hepatic tissue integrity under summer heat conditions. Polyphenols and flavonoids have been reported to protect hepatocytes against oxidative injury through membrane stabilization, inhibition of lipid peroxidation, and modulation of inflammatory processes (Li et al., 2018; Machado et al., 2023). However, histological scoring and molecular markers of hepatic injury were not quantified in the present study, and therefore the histological findings should be interpreted as supportive rather than definitive evidence of hepatoprotection.

The physiological responses were ultimately reflected in productive performance, where significant route × treatment interactions were observed for several growth and feed-utilization variables. In particular, the response of live body weight, daily body weight gain, FCR, and EPEF varied according to the combination of WMPE level and administration route. The most pronounced growth response was observed in birds receiving the higher WMPE level through drinking water, whereas some lower-level treatments showed smaller or different responses depending on the delivery route. This pattern indicates that increasing WMPE supplementation did not produce a consistent response across routes and that the effectiveness of WMPE depended on the specific route × level combination. These productive responses may be related to the antioxidant, antimicrobial, and other biological activities of the phenolic and flavonoid constituents identified in WMPE (Bahadori et al., 2018).

The enhanced growth performance observed in selected WMPE treatment combinations may be associated with improved intestinal conditions and physiological resilience under summer heat exposure. Phytochemicals in mint have been reported to influence digestive enzyme activity and nutrient utilization, potentially facilitating the digestion of proteins, lipids, and carbohydrates (Kazemi et al., 2025). Enhanced nutrient availability could subsequently support tissue accretion and body weight gain. Furthermore, the antimicrobial activity of mint-derived bioactive compounds may reduce the growth of potentially harmful microorganisms and thereby reduce competition for available nutrients (Hejna et al., 2021). In the present study, the concurrent reduction in selected bacterial populations and improvement in intestinal morphology provide a plausible biological context for the favorable growth responses. However, because nutrient digestibility and digestive enzyme activity were not directly measured, improved nutrient digestion should not be considered a demonstrated mechanism.

Another possible mechanism underlying the growth response is the antioxidant activity of WMPE. Fast-growing broilers are susceptible to oxidative challenges that can interfere with cellular metabolism, protein synthesis, and growth performance. The phenolic and flavonoid constituents of WMPE may support endogenous antioxidant defenses and reduce oxidative damage to intestinal and other tissues (Bahadori et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2019). The significant route × treatment interactions observed for several antioxidant variables, together with the corresponding growth responses, suggest that the physiological benefits of WMPE may have contributed to differences in productive performance among treatment combinations. The more pronounced responses observed with drinking-water administration at specific supplementation levels may reflect differences in the pattern of intake and exposure to the extract. Water supplementation may also reduce potential losses of sensitive or volatile phytochemicals associated with feed processing and storage (Brglez Mojzer et al., 2016; Yu et al., 2025).

The improvement in FCR and EPEF in selected route × treatment combinations further supports a favorable response in productive efficiency. Birds receiving WMPE at particular supplementation levels required less feed per unit of weight gain than some control groups, indicating more efficient conversion of dietary nutrients into body mass. Similar improvements in productive efficiency have been reported following supplementation with mint extracts and other phytogenic additives in broilers. Previous research has shown that peppermint, spearmint, oregano, thyme, and rosemary preparations can improve feed efficiency through effects on digestive processes, intestinal microbial populations, and intestinal morphology (Puvača et al., 2022; Aly et al., 2023; Cojocariu et al., 2026).

Changes in productive performance were accompanied by significant route × treatment interactions for several carcass characteristics, indicating that carcass responses were also dependent on the combination of WMPE level and administration route. Birds receiving selected WMPE levels showed higher carcass percentages, suggesting a favorable effect on carcass yield under those specific treatment combinations. The increase in liver percentage observed at higher WMPE levels may reflect differences in hepatic metabolic activity; however, interpretations related specifically to detoxification, nutrient metabolism, or antioxidant synthesis remain tentative because these processes were not directly measured (AbdEl Ati et al., 2021). Notably, WMPE administration reduced abdominal fat deposition in some treatment combinations, which may be consistent with differences in lipid utilization and nutrient partitioning (Mahmoud et al., 2025). Thus, the carcass findings further support the conclusion that WMPE responses were dependent on both supplementation level and administration route rather than being attributable to either factor independently.

The physiological responses observed with WMPE supplementation were also reflected in changes in breast-muscle chemical composition, although these responses varied according to WMPE level and administration route. Significant route × treatment interactions were detected for moisture, crude fat, crude protein, and carbohydrate contents, whereas the interaction for ash content was not significant. Thus, the effects on meat composition were not uniform across WMPE treatments but depended on the specific combination of supplementation level and delivery route. Many studies have demonstrated that dietary supplementation with medicinal herbs can influence meat quality by enhancing antioxidant status, modulating lipid metabolism, and improving nutrient utilization (Abdallah et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2021; Kar et al., 2025). The present findings extend these observations by showing that the response of breast-muscle composition to WMPE was treatment- and route-dependent rather than being explained by supplementation level alone.

The decrease in moisture content observed in birds receiving higher WMPE levels in particular route × treatment combinations may be related to differences in the relative deposition of dry matter within muscle tissue. Lower moisture content is generally associated with greater nutrient density and may influence some characteristics related to meat quality and storage stability (Mir et al., 2017). However, because water-holding capacity, cooking loss, tenderness, and storage stability were not measured in the present study, the lower moisture content should not be interpreted directly as evidence of improved meat quality or shelf life. The increase in ash content observed in some WMPE-treated groups may indicate differences in the mineral fraction of breast muscle, although mineral retention and deposition were not directly quantified. Bioactive substances originating from plants have been reported to influence mineral absorption and utilization through their effects on gastrointestinal condition and digestive processes (Patra et al., 2019). Because the route × treatment interaction for ash was not significant, however, this response should be considered a treatment-level observation rather than evidence of a route-dependent effect.

The increase in crude fat content observed at higher WMPE supplementation levels also varied among the route × treatment combinations and may reflect differences in lipid deposition and energy partitioning. The reductions in serum lipid concentrations observed in the present study provide a possible physiological context for these changes, although the mechanisms controlling lipid deposition in breast muscle were not directly investigated. Plant-derived phytochemicals have been associated with alterations in lipid metabolism and energy utilization (Song et al., 2025). The observation that some WMPE treatments showed greater breast-muscle fat content while abdominal fat deposition was reduced suggests that WMPE may have influenced the distribution of deposited lipids; however, this interpretation requires further confirmation because intramuscular and abdominal lipid fluxes were not directly measured. Moderate intramuscular fat accumulation has been associated with desirable sensory attributes such as tenderness, juiciness, and palatability of meat. Therefore, the changes in crude fat observed in the present study may have implications for meat characteristics, but sensory evaluation would be required to establish such effects.

The lower carbohydrate content observed in some WMPE-treated birds was likewise dependent on the specific treatment combination. This response may be related to differences in carbohydrate utilization and energy metabolism, as phytogenic compounds can influence digestive enzymes, glucose utilization, and metabolic pathways (Jlali et al., 2012; Reyer et al., 2017). Nevertheless, glycogen concentration, glucose metabolism, and related enzymatic activities were not measured in the present experiment; therefore, enhanced glycogen utilization remains a possible explanation rather than a demonstrated mechanism. WMPE also showed measurable antioxidant activity, and the improvement in systemic antioxidant status observed in the treated birds may have implications for the oxidative stability of muscle tissue. Antioxidants play an important role in limiting lipid oxidation and can help preserve the color and sensory characteristics of poultry meat during storage (Tocai et al., 2025).

The microbiological assessment was based on culture-dependent enumeration of total bacterial counts and Clostridium spp. and therefore provides information on selected cultivable bacterial populations rather than a comprehensive characterization of the cecal microbial community. Future studies should incorporate 16S rRNA gene sequencing or shotgun metagenomic approaches to determine whether WMPE induces broader changes in microbial community composition and functional capacity and to clarify whether the route × treatment responses observed in bacterial counts are associated with wider alterations in the gut microbial ecosystem.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that WMPE supplementation was associated with improvements in intestinal health, antioxidant status, immune responses, and productive performance in broiler chickens exposed to summer heat conditions. Importantly, several responses depended on the administration route and supplementation level, with drinking-water delivery producing more pronounced responses for selected intestinal, physiological, and productive traits, particularly at higher supplementation levels. These findings suggest that the route of administration should be considered when evaluating WMPE as a phytogenic supplement for broiler chickens under heat-exposure conditions. Further studies are warranted to validate the optimal supplementation level and administration strategy under commercial production conditions and to clarify the underlying biological mechanisms.
