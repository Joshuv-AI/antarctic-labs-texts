How Entanglement Reshapes the Geometry of Quantum Differential Privacy
Xi Wang, Parastoo Sadeghi, Guodong Shi

Abstract
Quantum differential privacy provides a rigorous framework for quantifying privacy guarantees in quantum information processing. While classical correlations are typically regarded as adversarial to privacy, the role of their quantum analogue, entanglement, is not well understood. In this work, we investigate how quantum entanglement fundamentally shapes quantum local differential privacy (QLDP). We consider a bipartite quantum system whose input state has a prescribed level of entanglement, characterized by a lower bound on the entanglement entropy. Each subsystem is then processed by a local quantum mechanism and measured using local operations only, ensuring that no additional entanglement is generated during the process. Our main result reveals a sharp phase-transition phenomenon in the relation between entanglement and QLDP: below a mechanism-dependent entropy threshold, the optimal privacy leakage level mirrors that of unentangled inputs; beyond this threshold, the privacy leakage level decreases with the entropy, which strictly improves privacy guarantees and can even turn some non-private mechanisms into private ones. The phase-transition phenomenon gives rise to a nonlinear dependence of the privacy leakage level on the entanglement entropy, even though the underlying quantum mechanisms and measurements are linear. We show that the transition is governed by the intrinsic non-convex geometry of the set of entanglement-constrained quantum states, which we parametrize as a smooth manifold and analyze via Riemannian optimization. Our findings demonstrate that entanglement serves as a genuine privacy-enhancing resource, offering a geometric and operational foundation for designing robust privacy-preserving quantum protocols.



Keywords:
Quantum computing, Differential privacy, Entanglement

1
Introduction

Differential privacy
(
9
;
8
)
emerges as a fundamental mathematical framework for quantifying and limiting the privacy risk incurred when performing queries over sensitive data, with broad application in machine learning
(
2
)
, government census statistics
(
3
)
, and industrial crowdsourcing
(
10
)
. Formally, a randomized mechanism
\mathcal{E}(\cdot)
acting on data points
d\in D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and producing
\mathcal{E}(d)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is differentially private if for any measurable set of outputs
T
, the probability of producing an output in
T
changes by at most a multiplicative factor
e^{\varepsilon}
when the input data varies from
d
to a neighboring point
d^{\prime}
.

Quantum differential privacy
(
31
)
generalizes the classical notion of differential privacy to the quantum domain. In quantum information processing, data are represented by quantum states
\rho
in the form of density matrices, and mechanisms are modeled as quantum channels
\mathcal{E}
under quantum mechanics. Intrinsic randomness arises naturally from quantum measurements, where even for a fixed input state
\rho
, probing the output
\mathcal{E}(\rho)
via a measurement
\mathcal{M}
produces stochastic outcomes governed by the Born rule
(
21
)
. Formally, quantum local differential privacy (QLDP) is evaluated by comparing the statistics of measurement outcomes induced by
\mathcal{E}(\rho)
and
\mathcal{E}(\rho’)
for different input states
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
, under all admissible measurements available to an adversary.

Definition 1

(Quantum Local Differential Privacy)
.

A quantum channel
\mathcal{E}
is said to be
\varepsilon
-quantum locally differentially private
(
\varepsilon
-QLDP) on a domain
D
if, for any pair of quantum states
\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in D
, any quantum measurement
\mathcal{M}
, and any measurable set of outcomes
T
, the following inequality holds:

\Pr\big[\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{E}(\rho))\in T\big]\;\leq\;e^{\varepsilon}\,\Pr\big[\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{E}(\rho^{\prime}))\in T\big].

1.1
Motivation: Differential Privacy vs Quantum Entanglement

The achievable privacy of quantum channels over general quantum state spaces is now well understood in
1
and
12
, owing to the linearity of quantum mechanics, where both a quantum channel
\mathcal{E}
and the probability measure
{\rm Pr}(\cdot)
induced by a measurement
\mathcal{M}
depend linearly on the underlying state
\rho
. A fundamental distinction from classical information, however, is the presence of
quantum entanglement

(
21
)
, which arises when a composite quantum state cannot be expressed as a product of subsystem states. Entangled states exhibit measurement correlations with no classical explanation
(
6
)
, and they also underpin the computational advantage of quantum computing, as most quantum algorithms with proven speedups rely on entangled states
(
13
;
20
)
.

In this paper, we aim to understand the role of entanglement in the QLDP. We consider a composite quantum system consisting of two parties with local state spaces
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
, and joint space
\mathcal{H}_{AB}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
. For a bipartite pure state
\rho
, entanglement is quantified by the entanglement entropy

\mathbf{E}(\rho):=-\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}(\rho_{A})\log\sigma_{j}(\rho_{A})=-\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}(\rho_{B})\log\sigma_{j}(\rho_{B}),

where
\rho_{A}
is the reduced state of
\rho
on subsystems
A
, obtained by ignoring (tracing out, cf. (
2
)) subsystem
B
, and
\{\sigma_{j}(\rho_{A})\}_{j}
are the eigenvalues of
\rho_{A}
. The reduced state
\rho_{B}
and its eigenvalues
\{\sigma_{j}(\rho_{A})\}_{j}
are defined analogously. The entanglement entropy measures the amount of quantum correlation shared between the two parties:
\mathbf{E}(\psi)
vanishes if and only if there is no entanglement, and larger values of
\mathbf{E}(\psi)
correspond to stronger entanglement. Consequently, we begin our study by restricting the input data on the following entanglement-constrained domain

\mathbb{H}_{s}:=\bigl\{\,\rho:\mathrm{rank}(\rho)=1,\ \mathbf{E}(\rho)\geq s\,\bigr\},

which consists of all bipartite pure states in
\mathcal{H}_{AB}
with entanglement entropy at least
s
. We further assume that quantum information processing is performed locally on each subsystem, thereby excluding any additional entanglement introduced by the processing. Specifically, the mechanism applied to the joint system is of the product form
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{B}
, where
\mathcal{E}_{A}
and
\mathcal{E}_{B}
are quantum channels acting on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
, respectively. Likewise, the adversary’s measurements are restricted to product measurements of the form
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{M}_{B}
.

By restricting the input states to
\mathbb{H}_{s}
and considering local quantum channels
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{B}
and local measurements
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{M}_{B}
, we and observe a nonlinear phase-transition phenomenon in how entanglement influences the QLDP, which is formalized in the following theorem.

Theorem 1

(Informal)
.

For any product quantum channel
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{B}
, the optimal QLDP level
\varepsilon^{\star}
exhibits a phase-transition behavior as a function of the entanglement entropy
s
. In particular, there exists a threshold
s_{0}
, depending on the spectral properties of
\mathcal{E}
, such that:

(i)

(Low-entanglement regime).

If
s\leq s_{0}
, the optimal QLDP level remains unchanged compared to the unentangled case, i.e.,
\varepsilon^{\star}(s)=\varepsilon^{\star}(0)
.

(ii)

(High-entanglement regime).

If
s>s_{0}
, the optimal QLDP level strictly decreases as
s
increases. Moreover,
\varepsilon^{\star}(s)
is a monotonically decreasing function of
s
and attains its minimum at the maximal entanglement level
s=\log\dim(\mathcal{H}_{A})=\log\dim(\mathcal{H}_{B})
. Moreover, some non-private mechanisms (
\varepsilon^{\star}(s)=+\infty
) can become a private ones (
\varepsilon^{\star}(s)<\infty
) .

This result highlights two key insights. (i) In classical differential privacy, correlations typically degrade privacy guarantees. Adversaries can use correlated attacks
(
27
;
29
;
30
)
to infer sensitive information. In contrast, our results show that in the quantum setting, entanglement, which is the quantum analogue of classical correlation, can enhance privacy, with a stronger privacy level achieved at higher levels of entanglement. (ii) Prior work on QLDP
(
14
;
12
)
characterizes privacy guarantees through linear representations, based on the linearity of quantum channels
\mathcal{E}
and measurements
\mathcal{M}
. In contrast, we show that the dependence of QLDP on entanglement is inherently nonlinear. We attribute this phenomenon to the non-convex geometry of the entanglement-constrained domain
\mathbb{H}_{s}
, which in our framework admits a product Riemannian manifold parametrization. The above findings demonstrate that entanglement can act as an intrinsic resource for privacy in quantum differential privacy, and that its geometric structure plays a central role in determining achievable privacy guarantees.

1.2
Related Work

Differential privacy (DP), introduced in
9
and
8
, provides a formal guarantee that the output of an algorithm changes only slightly when evaluated on neighboring input datasets. Classical differential privacy typically assumes a trusted central curator with access to raw data, an assumption that is often unrealistic in decentralized settings, where data are held locally and trust in any curator cannot be assumed. The utility of LDP, which concerns the sample complexity required for LDP algorithms to effectively learn the data, was studied in
11
and
16
. The subsequent work
15
focused on designing optimal-utility LDP mechanisms that maximize utility for a given privacy leakage level.

The above work on DP and LDP typically analyzes privacy guarantees under the assumption that the data records are independent. However, this assumption often fails in practice, and correlations among data can weaken privacy guarantees. For example,
19
proposed a real-world inference attack in which an adversary exploits social network friendships as correlations, to bypass the noise added by standard differential privacy and infer sensitive information such as location data. Similarly,
29
showed that the standard randomized response mechanism
25
under LDP is vulnerable to correlation attacks on single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) data, where SNPs are genetic variations at a single position in the DNA sequence that are inherently correlated. To address privacy risks arising from correlated data, several frameworks have been proposed, including the Pufferfish privacy
(
17
)
and Bayesian differential privacy
(
27
)
. All of the aforementioned classical privacy frameworks provide essential theoretical foundations for recent advances in quantum differential privacy.

There is a growing body of work on quantum differential privacy (QDP). By defining the neighboring of quantum states via the trace distance of their density matrices,
31
extended classical differential privacy to the quantum setting by requiring the outputs of neighboring quantum states approximately indistinguishable with respect to all quantum measurements. Subsequently,
1
established a fundamental connection between QDP and gentle measurements, showing that privacy-preserving measurements necessarily induce limited disturbance to quantum states. Building on these foundations, a range of QDP algorithms have been developed for quantum computation and quantum machine learning
(
18
;
23
;
26
;
7
)
.

In the context of quantum local differential privacy (QLDP),
14
generalized classical LDP to the quantum setting by requiring QDP for any two distinct quantum states. More recently,
12
characterized QLDP levels via an eigenvalue optimization framework and analyzed optimal-utility privacy mechanisms, with utility measured in terms of fidelity
(
24
)
and trace distance.

Despite the above advances, the role of quantum correlations in QDP and QLDP is not well explored. Recent work by
22
studies privacy guarantees for classically correlated quantum states under the Pufferfish framework. However, classical correlation models cannot capture quantum entanglement, which represents an intrinsic form of correlation to quantum systems. Consequently, the impact of entanglement on quantum differential privacy remains an open problem.

Preliminaries

In this section, we present some fundamental notions in the quantum information theory and basic concepts of quantum entanglement. For further details, we refer the reader to
21
.

2.1
Quantum States and Density Operators

In quantum mechanics, the state of an
n
-qubit system is described by a unit vector in a
N=2^{n}
-dimensional complex Hilbert space, denoted by
\mathcal{H}
. Its dual space is denoted as
\mathbb{L}(\mathcal{H})
and the set of all bounded linear operators acting on
\mathcal{H}
is denoted as
\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Throughout quantum computation and quantum information theory, Dirac’s bra–ket notation is used to represent vectors in
\mathcal{H}
and matrices in
\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
. A Ket vector, denoted
\ket{\psi}
, represents a column vector in
\mathcal{H}
. Its dual, the Bra vector
\bra{\psi}
, represents the conjugate transpose of
\ket{\psi}
, i.e.,
\ket{\psi}^{\dagger}
, in
\mathbb{L}(\mathcal{H})
. The inner product
\braket{\phi|\psi}
is a complex scalar representing the overlap between two states. In addition, the outer product
\ket{\psi}\bra{\phi}
defines a linear operator on
\mathcal{H}
via the mapping
\ket{v}\mapsto\ket{\psi}\braket{\phi|v}
.

For an isolated quantum system, the state is represented by a pure state. A
pure quantum state
of an
n
-qubit system is a unit vector in
\mathcal{H}
, which we write
\ket{\psi}=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{N})^{\top},
with complex amplitudes
a_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
satisfying the normalization condition
\|\psi\|^{2}=\braket{\psi|\psi}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}a_{j}^{\dagger}=1
. To illustrate these notions, consider the case of a single qubit, which inhabits a two-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. The standard computational basis is

\ket{0}:=\begin{pmatrix}1,0\end{pmatrix}^{T},\qquad\ket{1}:=\begin{pmatrix}0,1\end{pmatrix}^{T}.

A pure state of a single qubit can then be written as
\ket{\psi}=\alpha\ket{0}+\beta\ket{1},
where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
and
\alpha\alpha^{\dagger}+\beta\beta^{\dagger}=1
. In addition, the pure state of an
n
-qubit system can be represented by a
length-
2^{n}
complex vector of amplitudes indexed by classical bit strings.
Let
\{\ket{x}:x\in\{0,1\}^{n}\}
denote the computational basis of

\mathcal{H}=(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
, where,

\ket{00\dots 0}=\begin{pmatrix}1,0,\cdots,0\end{pmatrix}^{T},\quad\ket{10\dots 0}=\begin{pmatrix}0,1,\cdots,0\end{pmatrix}^{T},\cdots,\ket{11\dots 1}=\begin{pmatrix}0,0,\cdots,1\end{pmatrix}^{T}.

Any pure state can be written as
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}a_{x}\ket{x}
, where
a_{x}\in\mathbb{C}
and
\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}a_{x}a_{x}^{\dagger}=1
.

To describe the state of open quantum systems, it is convenient to work with density operators. A
density operator
on
\mathcal{H}
is a linear operator
\rho\in\mathbb{D}(\mathcal{H})\subset\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
that is positive semidefinite and has unit trace, i.e.,
\rho\succeq 0,\operatorname{Tr}(\rho)=1
. By the spectral decomposition, there exist pure states
\ket{\psi_{j}}\in\mathcal{H}
,
j=1,2,\dots,r
and nonnegative numbers
p_{j}
with
\sum_{j}p_{j}=1
such that

\rho=\sum_{j=1}^{r}p_{j}\ket{\psi_{j}}\bra{\psi_{j}}.

If the vectors
\ket{\psi_{j}}
are chosen to form an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
, then
\ket{\psi_{j}}
and
p_{j}
are referred to as the eigenstates and eigenvalues of
\rho
, respectively. In this representation, one may view
\rho
as a quantum ensemble of pure states
\ket{\psi_{j}}
with weights
p_{j}
.

We also note that, for every pure state
\ket{\psi}
, the associated density operator
\rho_{\psi}=\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}
is a rank-
1
projector. Throughout the remainder of the paper, with a slight abuse of language, we identify a pure state
\ket{\psi}
with its associated density operator
\rho_{\psi}
.

2.2
Joint Quantum System and Entanglement States

We now consider composite systems consisting of two parties. For two quantum subsystems with Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
, the joint system is
described by the tensor-product space

\mathcal{H}_{AB}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}.

Given a bipartite pure state
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}_{AB}
, there exist orthonormal sets
\{\ket{j_{A}}\}_{j=1}^{r}\subset\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\{\ket{j_{B}}\}_{j=1}^{r}\subset\mathcal{H}_{B}
and nonnegative real numbers
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{j=1}^{r}
such that

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\ket{j_{A}}\otimes\ket{j_{B}},

(1)

where
\lambda_{j}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}=1
. This expansion is unique up to the order of
\lambda_{j}
, and known as the Schmidt decomposition of
\ket{\psi}
. The numbers
\lambda_{j}
are called the Schmidt coefficients, and
r
is called the Schmidt rank of the state. If the Schmidt rank
r=1
, we say that the state
\ket{\psi}
is a
separable state
; otherwise, it is an
entangled state
.

For a density operator
\rho\in\mathcal{H}_{AB}
, the reduced state
\rho_{A}
describes the effective state of subsystem
A
when subsystem
B
is ignored. Formally, the reduced state of
A
is defined via the tracing out system
B

\displaystyle\rho_{A}:=\operatorname{Tr}_{B}(\rho)=\sum_{j}\bigl(I_{A}\otimes\bra{j_{B}}\bigr)\rho\bigl(I_{A}\otimes\ket{j_{B}}\bigr),

(2)

where
\{\ket{j_{B}}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{B}
. The partial trace
\operatorname{Tr}_{B}
is a linear, completely positive, and trace-preserving (CPTP) map. If
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}_{AB}
is a bipartite pure state admitting the Schmidt decomposition
(
1
), then the reduced state on subsystem
A
takes the form

\rho_{A}:=\operatorname{Tr}_{B}\bigl(\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}\bigr)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}\ket{j_{A}}\bra{j_{A}}.

In particular,
\rho_{A}
is a pure state if and only if
\ket{\psi}
is separable
(i.e., the Schmidt rank equals one). Otherwise, when
\ket{\psi}
is entangled,
the reduced state
\rho_{A}
is mixed.

The degree of entanglement of a bipartite pure state can be quantified by the von Neumann entropy of its reduced state. For a density operator

\rho=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}\ket{\psi_{i}}\bra{\psi_{i}}\in\mathbb{D}(\mathcal{H})
, the von Neumann entropy is defined by

{\mathbf{S}}(\rho)=-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\log\rho)=-\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}\log\lambda_{j}
,
which is the Shannon entropy of the eigenvalue distribution
\{\lambda_{j}\}
. For a bipartite pure state
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
with reduced states
\rho_{A}
and
\rho_{B}
, the quantity

{\mathbf{E}}(\psi):=\mathbf{S}(\rho_{A})=\mathbf{S}(\rho_{B})

is called the entanglement entropy and measures the amount of entanglement between
A
and
B
. In particular,
\mathbf{E}(\psi)=0
if and only if
\ket{\psi}
is separable, while larger values of
\mathbf{E}(\psi)
correspond to stronger entanglement.

2.3
Quantum Measurements

To obtain classical information from a quantum state
\rho
, one has to perform a quantum measurement on
\rho
. Mathematically, a measurement can be viewed as a stochastic map that takes density operators on
\mathcal{H}
to probability distributions over a finite outcome set
O
. Let
\Delta(O)
denote the set of probability distributions on
O
. A positive operator-valued measurement (POVM) is specified by a collection of positive semidefinite operators
\{\mathcal{M}_{k}\}_{k\in O}\subset\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H})
satisfying the normalization condition

\mathcal{M}_{k}\succeq 0\ \text{for all }k\in O,\qquad\sum_{k\in O}\mathcal{M}_{k}=I_{\mathcal{H}},

where
I_{\mathcal{H}}
is the identity operator on
\mathcal{H}
. The associated measurement map

\mathcal{M}:\mathbb{D}(\mathcal{H})\to\Delta(O)
is defined by

\mathcal{M}(\rho)(k):=\Pr[k|\rho]=\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{M}_{k}\rho),\qquad k\in O.

3.1
Product Quantum Channel on Entangled Quantum States

We first consider a two-party quantum system consisting of two subsystems,
A
and
B
. The state space of each subsystem is denoted by
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
, respectively, with

\dim\mathcal{H}_{A}=\dim\mathcal{H}_{B}=N
; and the overall system space is
\mathcal{H}_{AB}:=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
. We consider a quantum channel over the collective system as a product of two local channels described by
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{B}
. Here, we assume that
\mathcal{E}_{A}
and
\mathcal{E}_{B}
are general CPTP maps, i.e., there exist finite sets
\{\mathsf{E}_{A}(k)\}\in\mathbb{D}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
and
\{\mathsf{E}_{B}(k)\in\mathbb{D}(\mathcal{H}_{B})\}
, under which

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{A}(\rho_{A})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\mathsf{E}_{A}(k)\rho_{A}\mathsf{E}_{A}^{\dagger}(k),\ \ \forall\rho_{A}\in\mathbb{D}(\mathcal{H}_{A}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{B}(\rho_{B})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\mathsf{E}_{B}(k)\rho_{B}\mathsf{E}_{B}^{\dagger}(k),\ \ \forall\rho_{B}\in\mathbb{D}(\mathcal{H}_{B}).

It holds that
\sum_{k}\mathsf{E}_{A}^{\dagger}(k)\mathsf{E}_{A}(k)=\mathsf{I}_{A}
and
\sum_{k}\mathsf{E}_{B}^{\dagger}(k)\mathsf{E}_{B}(k)=\mathsf{I}_{B}
.

The physical interpretation of
\mathcal{E}_{A}
or
\mathcal{E}_{B}
arises from open quantum systems. Each subsystem,
A
and
B
, independently interacts with environment, which is also a quantum system, where exchange of information, energy, and coherence occurs. The dynamics of the collective system of subsystem
A
and its environment (or
B
and its environment) is described by a Schrödinger equation and undergoes a unitary evolution. At the steady state and tracing out the environment leaves the state evolution of the subsystem
A
to be described by
\mathcal{E}_{A}
(or,
\mathcal{E}_{B}
for subsystem
B
). Here we choose
\mathcal{E}
to be
\mathcal{E}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{B}
so that the channel acts on subsystem
A
and
B
separately and independently through
\mathcal{E}_{A}
and
\mathcal{E}_{B}
. This facilitates a clean study on how entanglement from the input of
\mathcal{E}
may contribute to differential privacy, as now all possible entanglement emerges from the input state only.

D

3.2
Local Measurement Adversaries and Quantum Local Differential Privacy

We model the adversary as having access to the output state
\mathcal{E}(\rho)
and being allowed to perform local measurements on
\mathcal{E}_{A}(\rho)
and
\mathcal{E}_{B}(\rho)
, followed by arbitrary classical post-processing.

Definition 2

(Local measurement adversary)
.

A local measurement (LM) adversary is an adversary who measures
\mathcal{H}_{AB}
by local operations on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
, and combines the classical outcomes. Formally, a LM adversary is specified by a finite family of POVM elements on
\mathcal{H}_{AB}

\{\mathcal{M}_{a,b}\}_{(a,b)\in O_{A}\times O_{B}},\qquad\mathcal{M}_{a,b}:=\mathcal{M}_{a}\otimes\mathcal{M}_{b},

with local POVMs
\{\mathcal{M}_{a}\}_{a\in O_{A}}\subset\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
and
\{\mathcal{M}_{b}\}_{b\in O_{B}}\subset\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
.
Given
\mathcal{E}(\rho)
, the local measurement adversary’s classical observation is the random variable
k\in O_{A}\times O_{B}
where

\qquad\Pr[k=(a,b)|\mathcal{E}(\rho)]=\operatorname{Tr}\Big(\big(\mathcal{M}_{a}\otimes\mathcal{M}_{b}\big)\mathcal{E}(\rho)\Big)=\operatorname{Tr}\Big(\big(\mathcal{E}_{a}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{M}_{a})\otimes\mathcal{E}_{b}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{M}_{b})\big)\rho\Big).

where
\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}
is the adjoint operator of
\mathcal{E}
.

Restricting the input domain to
D=\mathbb{H}_{s}
and the adversary to LMs, we define the entanglement-constrained
\varepsilon
-QLDP against local measurement adversaries (ECLM-
\varepsilon
-QLDP) as follows.

Definition 3

(entanglement-constrained
\varepsilon
-local measurement QLDP)
.

A quantum mechanism
\mathcal{E}
operating on
\mathcal{H}_{AB}
is said to be

\varepsilon
-
quantum locally differentially private against local measurement adversaries

(ECLM-
\varepsilon
-QLDP) on the entanglement-constrained domain
\mathbb{H}_{s}
if, for any local measurement
\mathcal{M}
and any quantum states
\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in\mathbb{H}_{s}
, the following inequality holds:

\Pr\Big[\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{E}(\rho))\in T\Big]\leq e^{\varepsilon}\Pr\Big[\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{E}(\rho^{\prime}))\in T\Big],\qquad\forall T\subseteq O_{A}\times O_{B}.

3.3
The System Setup: Rational Beyond Separating Entanglement Source

We have explained that the proposed product mechanism and local measurements serve the technical purpose of isolating the origin of entanglement, ensuring that within the quantum differential privacy framework
\mathbb{H}_{s}
is the sole source of entanglement. On the other hand, the system setup is a fundamental object of study in quantum information theory in its own right.

In fact, the physical setting of our quantum information processing shown in Figure
1
on the entangled composite system
A
and
B
parallels the canonical architecture of Bell-inequality experiments
(
4
)
. In such experiments, a central source prepares entangled pairs, which typically can be polarization-entangled photons, and distributes one particle to each of two spatially separated measurement stations. Each station independently selects a measurement basis, which in our framework corresponds to the channel–measurement pairs
(\mathcal{E}_{A},\mathcal{M}_{A})
and
(\mathcal{E}_{B},\mathcal{M}_{B})
, and records its local outcome. Correlation statistics are then computed from coincident detection events across the two sites. Bell-type inequalities
(
4
)
impose elementary constraints on these statistics under any classical model based solely on shared randomness and local information; their violation certifies that the correlations carried by the entangled systems
A
and
B
cannot be reproduced by classical means. This Bell-type architecture is foundational in quantum physics, as it provides the most direct operational evidence that entanglement produces correlations unattainable within any classical theory
(
6
)
.

4
Main Results

In this section, we present the main results. We first characterize EC-LM-
\varepsilon
-QLDP in terms of the extremal privacy energy of the quantum system
\mathcal{K}_{\phi}
. We then formulate the computation of extremal privacy energy as a constrained Riemannian optimization problem. By analyzing the KKT conditions of the Riemannian optimization problem, we derive a closed-form expression for the maximal privacy energy and a lower bound on the minimal privacy energy. These results reveal a phase-transition phenomenon and establish our main theorem, Theorem
1
.

4.1
Entanglement-Constrained Privacy

Consider any local measurements
\{\mathcal{M}_{a}\otimes\mathcal{M}_{b}\}_{(a,b)\in O_{A}\times O_{B}}
, we define the induced local observables

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{a}:=\mathcal{E}_{A}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{M}_{a}),\quad\mathcal{K}_{b}:=\mathcal{E}_{B}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{M}_{b}),\quad\mathcal{K}_{\phi}:=\mathcal{K}_{a}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{b}.

In particular, when the POVM elements are rank-one projectors
\mathcal{M}_{a}=\ket{\phi_{a}}\bra{\phi_{a}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{b}=\ket{\phi_{b}}\bra{\phi_{b}}
, we define the induced operators

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\phi,a}:=\mathcal{E}_{A}^{\dagger}\bigl(\ket{\phi_{a}}\bra{\phi_{a}}\bigr),\quad\mathcal{K}_{\phi,b}:=\mathcal{E}_{B}^{\dagger}\bigl(\ket{\phi_{b}}\bra{\phi_{b}}\bigr),\quad\mathcal{K}_{\phi}:=\mathcal{K}_{\phi,a}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{\phi,b}.

(4)

We can then further define

\displaystyle J_{\max}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)=\max_{\ket{\psi}\in\mathbb{H}_{s}}\bra{\psi}\mathcal{K}_{\phi}\ket{\psi},\quad J_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)=\min_{\ket{\psi}\in\mathbb{H}_{s}}\bra{\psi}\mathcal{K}_{\phi}\ket{\psi}.

(5)

The following theorem characterizes ECLM-
\varepsilon
-QLDP through the extremal behavior of
\mathcal{K}_{\phi}
over the domain
\mathbb{H}_{s}
.

Theorem 2

(Optimal Privacy Budget)
.

Consider a bipartite quantum system with Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{AB}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
with
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{B}
being a product mechanism. Then
\mathcal{E}
is ECLM-
\varepsilon
-QLDP on entanglement domain
\mathbb{H}_{s}
with optimal
\varepsilon^{*}
as

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{*}(s)=\log\max_{\ket{\phi_{a}}\in\mathcal{H}_{A}}\max_{\ket{\phi_{b}}\in\mathcal{H}_{B}}\left(\frac{J_{\max}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s\big)}{J_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)}\right).

(6)

The proof of Theorem
2
is in Appendix
C
. The characterization in Theorem
2
is a generalization to the optimal quantum local differential privacy established in
12
. From Theorem 2 in
12
, if we consider all the quantum states without the entanglement entropy constraint, i.e.,
\rho\in\mathbb{H}_{s}
for
s=0
, the optimal privacy budget will be determined by the extremal eigenvalues of
\mathcal{K}_{\phi}
by
\varepsilon^{*}(s)=\log\frac{\sigma_{\max}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi})}{\sigma_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi})}
, where

\displaystyle\sigma_{\max}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi})=\max_{\ket{\psi}\in\mathbb{H}_{0}}\bra{\psi}\mathcal{K}_{\phi}\ket{\psi},\quad\sigma_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi})=\min_{\ket{\psi}\in\mathbb{H}_{0}}\bra{\psi}\mathcal{K}_{\phi}\ket{\psi}.

Note that the quantity
\bra{\psi}\mathcal{K}_{\phi}\ket{\psi}={\rm Tr}\big(\mathcal{K}_{\phi}\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}\big)
physically is interpreted as the
energy
of a quantum system at state
\ket{\psi}
when the system Hamiltonian is given by
\mathcal{K}_{\phi}
. We refer to the quantity as the
privacy energy
. According to Theorem
2
, the optimal privacy leakage level is determined by the maximum and minimum admissible privacy energies within the entanglement-constrained set
\mathbb{H}_{s}
.

4.2
The Riemannian Gradient of the Privacy Energy

While the energy
\bra{\psi}\mathcal{K}_{\phi}\ket{\psi}
depends linearly on both the state
\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}
and the operator
\mathcal{K}_{\phi}
, the admissible set
\mathbb{H}_{s}
of entangled states is non-convex. As a result, the maximally and minimally admissible privacy energies are constrained by the geometry of
\mathbb{H}_{s}
. To capture this geometry explicitly, we introduce a parametrization of the energy function based on the Schmidt decomposition and unitary group actions.

For a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with
\dim\mathcal{H}=M

\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}):=\{U\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}\mid U^{\dagger}U=UU^{\dagger}=I\}

For any bipartite pure state
\ket{\psi}\in\mathbb{H}_{s}
, there exists a Schmidt decomposition

\ket{\psi}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\ket{j_{A}}\otimes\ket{j_{B}}

\displaystyle\bra{\psi}\mathcal{K}_{\phi}\ket{\psi}=\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\Lambda^{1/2}\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{A}\Lambda^{1/2}\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{B}\bigr),\qquad\Lambda:=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N}).

4.3
Bounding the Privacy Energy

We next establish bounds on the maximal and minimal privacy energies
J_{\max}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s),J_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)
. These bounds reveal the explicit relationship between the entanglement entropy
s
and the optimal privacy budget
\varepsilon^{\ast}
.

We first derive a closed-form expression for
J_{\max}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)
by solving the KKT conditions (
9
)–(
13
). To this end, we introduce the following notation. Since
\mathcal{E}_{A}
is a completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) map, its adjoint
\mathcal{E}_{A}^{\dagger}
is completely positive and unital. Consequently, the induced operator
\mathcal{K}_{a}:=\mathcal{E}_{A}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{M}_{a})
is positive semidefinite.
Without loss of generality, we let
\mathcal{K}_{a}=\operatorname{diag}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{N}),
where
\alpha_{j}
denote the
j
-th largest eigenvalue of
\mathcal{K}_{a}
, i.e.,
\alpha_{j}=\sigma_{j}(\mathcal{K}_{a})
and
\alpha_{1}\geq\alpha_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\alpha_{N}\geq 0
. Similarly, for subsystem
B
, we let
\mathcal{K}_{b}=\operatorname{diag}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{N})
, where
\beta_{j}:=\sigma_{j}(\mathcal{K}_{b})
and
\beta_{1}\geq\beta_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\beta_{N}\geq 0
. We further define
\mu_{i}:=\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}
and denote the degeneracy of the maximal eigenvalue by

d_{\max}:=\#\{j:\mu_{j}=\mu_{1}=\sigma_{\max}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi})\}.

The following theorem shows the closed-form of
J_{\max}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)
depending on the entropy constraint
s
and
d_{\max}
.

Theorem 4

(Phase-transition of Maximal Privacy Energy)
.

The value
J_{\max}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)
takes the following closed-form expression:

(i)

(Low-entanglement regime).

If
s\leq\log d_{\max}
, then we have
J_{\max}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)=\mu_{1}=\sigma_{\max}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi})
, where the optimal
solution is given by

\displaystyle U^{*}_{A}=U_{B}^{*}=I_{\mathcal{H}},\quad\lambda_{j}^{*}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{d_{\max}},&j=1,\dots,d_{\max},\\[2.15277pt]
0,&j>d_{\max}.\end{cases}

(ii)

(High-entanglement regime).

If
s>\log d_{\max}
, then
J_{\max}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)=\frac{1}{Z(\gamma)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mu_{i}e^{\gamma\mu_{i}}<\sigma_{\max}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi})
, where the optimal solution is given by the Gibbs form

\displaystyle U^{*}_{A}=U_{B}^{*}=I_{\mathcal{H}},\quad\lambda_{j}^{*}=\frac{\exp(\gamma\mu_{j})}{Z(\gamma)}.

Here,
\gamma>0
is uniquely determined by the following entropy equation

\displaystyle\log Z(\gamma)-\frac{\gamma}{Z(\gamma)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mu_{i}\exp(\gamma\mu_{j})=s,\quad Z(\gamma):=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(\gamma\mu_{j}).

(15)

The proof of Theorem
4
is given in Appendix
D
. This result reveals that
J_{\max}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)
exhibits a phase transition around the critical point
\log d_{\max}
. Specifically, when
s\leq\log d_{\max}
, the entropy constraint is inactive and the optimal Schmidt coefficient concentrates uniformly on the
d_{\max}
-dimensional dominant eigenspace. In this regime, the maximal privacy energy coincides with the unconstrained optimum over the full space
\mathbb{H}_{0}
, and hence remains constant at the spectral maximum
J_{\max}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)=\sigma_{\max}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi})
. In contrast, once the entropy level exceeds the threshold
s>\log d_{\max}
, the entropy constraint becomes active and forces the Schmidt weights to spread across the spectrum according to a Gibbs distribution. As a consequence, the value of
J_{\max}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)
is strictly smaller than
\sigma_{\max}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi})
and becomes a smooth, monotonically decreasing function of
s
. Moreover, in the maximally entangled case, i.e.,
s=\log N
, the Gibbs weights become uniform on all indices of
\lambda
and
J_{\max}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)
attains its minimal value
\bar{\mu}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mu_{i}
, corresponding to the complete delocalization of spectral alignment.

We note that the phase-transition characterized in Theorem
4
mathematically coincides with the phase-transition of a thermodynamic system warming from Absolute Zero to a finite-temperature thermal state. In this analogy, the system has
N
energy levels
\{-\mu_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
, with
\lambda_{i}
denoting the occupation probability of level
i
. The parameter
s
plays the role of entropy (chaos of the system), and (
15
) corresponds to the Legendre duality between entropy and temperature, with
T\propto\gamma^{-1}
. The quantity
J_{\max}
represents the internal energy, while
d_{\max}
corresponds to the number of states attaining the minimal energy, which may not be unique in materials like spin ice (Dysprosium Titanate,
\text{Dy}_{2}\text{Ti}_{2}\text{O}_{7}
).

In regime (i) of Theorem
4
, where
\gamma\to+\infty
, the system corresponds to the zero-temperature phase of the thermodynamic analogy. In this case, the thermodynamic system mixes among degenerate ground states and does not increase the energy
J_{\max}
. The entropy saturates at
s=\log d_{\max}
, any further increase in entropy will force the system to leave the ground state. In regime (ii) of Theorem
4
, the system enters a finite-temperature phase in which excited states become populated and follows a Gibbs form. Increasing the entropy
s
is analogous to heating the system, which causes the temperature increases (
\gamma
decreases) and the energy
J_{\max}
rises. This thermodynamic analogy provides a clear physical picture of the transition described in Theorem
4
.

Next, the following theorem provides an explicitly computable lower bound for
J_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)
, namely
\widetilde{J}_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)
. We denote
\widetilde{\mu}_{i}:=\alpha_{i}\beta_{N}
, and the degeneracy of the minimal eigenvalue by

d_{\min}:=\#\{j:\widetilde{\mu}_{j}=\widetilde{\mu}_{N}=\sigma_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi})\}.

Then
\widetilde{J}_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)
presents a similar phase-transition with energy level
\{\widetilde{\mu}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
.

Theorem 5

(Phase-transition of Relaxed Minimal Privacy Energy)
.

The privacy energy
J_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)
admits a lower bound of
\widetilde{J}_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)
which undertakes the following phase transition:

(i)

(Low-entanglement regime).

If
s\leq\log d_{\min}
, then

J_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)\geq\widetilde{J}_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)=\widetilde{\mu}_{N}=\sigma_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi})
.

(ii)

(High-entanglement regime).

If
s>\log d_{\min}
, we have

J_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)\geq\widetilde{J}_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)=\frac{1}{\widetilde{Z}(\gamma)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\widetilde{\mu}_{j}\exp(\widetilde{\gamma}\widetilde{\mu}_{j})>\sigma_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi}),

where
\widetilde{\gamma}>0
is uniquely determined by the entropy equation

\displaystyle\log\widetilde{Z}(\widetilde{\gamma})-\frac{\widetilde{\gamma}}{\widetilde{Z}(\gamma)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\widetilde{\mu}_{j}\exp(\widetilde{\gamma}\widetilde{\mu}_{j})=s,\quad\widetilde{Z}(\widetilde{\gamma}):=\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{-\widetilde{\gamma}\widetilde{\mu}_{j}}.

(iii)

(Max-entanglement regime).

If
s=\log N
, we have

J_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)=\widetilde{J}_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\beta_{N-i+1},

which is the minimal achievable value over all admissible entanglement levels.

The proof of Theorem
5
is given in Appendix
E
. The fact that we obtained a closed-form solution to
J_{\max}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)
, but only a relaxed lower bound for
J_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)
, is rooted in the geometry of the underlying optimization problem. In both cases, we are optimizing a linear functional over the non-convex feasible set
\mathbb{H}_{s}
. For the maximization problem, this non-convexity is benign and the maximum can be attained at a vortex of the set, which leads to the closed-form expression in terms of the products
\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}
and the associated Gibbs weights. In contrast, for the minimization problem, the true minimum in general is not attained at such vortexes. Instead, it typically lies in the interior of the feasible set and cannot be represented solely by a simple rearrangement of eigenvalues (such as pairing
\alpha_{i}
with
\beta_{N-i+1}
). As a consequence, we can only construct a tractable linear-program relaxation that yields a computable lower bound
\widetilde{J}_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)
, but this relaxation does not coincide with the exact minimum
J_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)
.

4.4
The Geometry of Quantum Differential Privacy

We now establish Theorem
1
and show how the geometry of entanglement space
\mathbb{H}_{s}
leads to a phase transition of the quantum differential privacy, combining the results in Theorems
2
–
5
.

(i)

Low entanglement does not change the QLDP.
When the entanglement entropy satisfies

s\leq\min\{\log d_{\max},\log d_{\min}\},

the entropy constraint does not exclude the extremal eigenstates of
\mathcal{K}_{\phi}
. In this case, the maximal and minimal privacy energies are still attained at the largest and smallest eigenvalues of
\mathcal{K}_{\phi}
, yielding

\varepsilon^{*}(s)=\log\max_{\ket{\phi_{a}}\in\mathcal{H}_{A}}\max_{\ket{\phi_{b}}\in\mathcal{H}_{B}}\frac{\sigma_{\max}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi})}{\sigma_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi})}=\varepsilon^{*}(0).

Thus, the privacy leakage level coincides with the entanglement-free case, recovering the QLDP characterization in
12
.

(ii)

High entanglement enhances the QLDP.
When the entanglement entropy exceeds the degeneracy thresholds, i.e.,

s>\min\{\log d_{\max},\log d_{\min}\},

the entropy constraint becomes active and excludes the extremal eigenstates of
\mathcal{K}_{\phi}
. The optimal privacy energies are then attained by mixtures over multiple eigenstates, with weights taking a Gibbs-type form. Consequently, the optimal QLDP leakage level

\varepsilon^{*}(s)=\log\max_{\ket{\phi_{a}}\in\mathcal{H}_{A}}\max_{\ket{\phi_{b}}\in\mathcal{H}_{B}}\left(\frac{J_{\max}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s\big)}{J_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)}\right)<\varepsilon^{*}(0).

decreases strictly and monotonically with
s
.

In particular, consider the case where
\mathcal{K}_{a}=\mathcal{E}_{A}^{\dagger}(\ket{\phi_{a}}\bra{\phi_{a}})
is non-private, i.e., its smallest eigenvalue vanishes (
\alpha_{N}=0
), while
\mathcal{K}_{b}=\mathcal{E}_{B}^{\dagger}(\ket{\phi_{b}}\bra{\phi_{b}})
has finite leakage (
\beta_{N}\neq 0
). In the unentangled regime, we have
\sigma_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi})=0
, yielding infinite privacy leakage. However, as discussed above,
J_{\min}
is attained by a mixture over
\widetilde{\mu}_{j}
under sufficient entanglement. Since not all
\widetilde{\mu}_{j}
equal to zero,
J_{\min}>0
and hence leads to finite privacy leakage. Consequently, entanglement can render an otherwise non-private mechanism private over the entangled domain.

The above discussion highlights the central role of entanglement, or non-classical quantum correlation, in the differential privacy of quantum computing methods. Weak entanglement does not affect the achievable privacy level, whereas sufficiently strong entanglement strictly enhances privacy and can even turn non-private mechanisms into private ones. The result stands in sharp contrast to classically correlated computing settings, where correlations typically degrade privacy guarantees. Our results show that, rather than being detrimental, quantum entanglement can serve as an intrinsic resource for privacy protection.

In addition, the dependence of privacy on entanglement exhibits a nonlinear behavior. In contrast to prior QLDP
(
12
)
results, which are governed by linear theory (i.e., maximal or minimal eigenvalues), the nonlinearity arises from the nonconvex geometry of the entanglement-constrained set
\mathbb{H}_{s}
, which we parametrize as a manifold and analyze via Riemannian optimization. The geometric perspective potentially paves the way for deeper understanding of entanglement in quantum information and quantum computing.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we established a quantitative relationship between quantum entanglement and quantum local differential privacy. We first considered a quantum processing model with entangled inputs, where both the mechanism and measurements were restricted to local operations to avoid additional entanglement. Within this framework, we then defined entanglement-constrained
\varepsilon
-QLDP against local measurement adversaries (EC-LM-
\varepsilon
-QLDP). By capturing the nonconvex geometry of the entanglement-constrained set
\mathbb{H}_{s}
via manifold parametrization and using the KKT conditions in Riemannian optimization, we characterized the dependence of EC-LM-
\varepsilon
-QLDP on the entanglement entropy, and obtained an explicit bound for the QLDP leakage level. Finally, several numerical experiments illustrated our theoretical results.

Our central finding is a phase-transition phenomenon of QLDP with entanglement: insufficient entanglement leaves the QLDP leakage level unchanged, whereas sufficiently strong entanglement strictly enhances privacy, leading to a monotonic reduction in the QLDP leakage level. In contrast to classical correlations, which typically degrade privacy guarantees, quantum entanglement emerges as a constructive resource for privacy protection.

Future work will investigate multipartite entanglement structures whose correlations cannot be characterized by any finite collection of local bipartite entanglement, but instead are captured by some global topological invariants. In addition, we aim to develop robust privacy-preserving protocols that exploit entanglement to enhance privacy guarantees in quantum communication and quantum machine learning.

Appendix B
Technical Lemmas

In this appendix, we state some technical lemmas for the further proof.

Lemma 1

(Cauchy interlacing theorem,
5
)
.

Let
X\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
be Hermitian with eigenvalues
\sigma_{1}(X)\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{n}(X)\geq 0
and
P\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times M}
satisfy
P^{\dagger}P=I_{M}
.
Then the eigenvalues

\sigma_{j}(P^{\dagger}XP)\leq\sigma_{j}(X),\qquad j=1,\dots,M.

Lemma 2

(von Neumann trace inequality,
5
)
.

Let
X,Y\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
be positive semidefinite matrices. Then

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}(X)\,\sigma_{N+1-j}(Y)\leq\operatorname{Tr}(XY)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}(X)\,\sigma_{j}(Y),

where
\sigma_{1}(\cdot)\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{n}(\cdot)\geq 0
denote eigenvalues arranged in nonincreasing order.

Lemma 3

(Majorization bound for weighted sums)
.

Let
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
and
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})
be nonnegative vectors with

x_{1}\geq\cdots\geq x_{N}
and
y_{1}\geq\cdots\geq y_{N}
.
If
z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})
is another vector satisfying

\sum_{j=1}^{M}z_{i}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{M}x_{i}\quad\text{for all }M=1,\dots,N,

then

\sum_{j=1}^{N}y_{j}z_{j}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}y_{j}x_{j}.

Proof.

Define the partial sums
S_{M}:=\sum_{j=1}^{M}(x_{j}-z_{j}),j=1,\dots,M
. By assumption,
S_{M}\geq 0
for all
M
. Using summation by parts, we write

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}y_{j}(x_{j}-z_{j})=y_{N}S_{N}+\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}(y_{j}-y_{j+1})S_{j},

Since
y_{1}\geq y_{2}\geq\cdots\geq y_{N}\geq 0
, we have
y_{j}-y_{j+1}\geq 0
for all
j
. Moreover, by definition of
S_{j}
, each partial sum satisfies
S_{i}\geq 0
. Therefore, every term in the above sum is nonnegative, and hence

\sum_{j=1}^{N}y_{j}(x_{j}-z_{j})\geq 0.

which is equivalent to
\sum_{j=1}^{N}y_{j}z_{j}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}y_{j}x_{j}
.
∎

\lambda^{*}_{k}=0
. Then
\log\lambda^{*}_{k}\to-\infty
. For the right-hand side to equate to
-\infty
, we would require
\eta_{k}\to-\infty
, which violates
\eta_{k}\geq 0
.
Therefore, we must have
\lambda^{*}_{j}>0
for all
j=1,\dots,N
.

Since
\lambda^{*}_{j}>0
for all
j
, the complementary slackness condition (
35
) implies

\eta_{j}=0
. The stationarity condition then yields

\displaystyle\lambda^{*}_{j}=\exp\left(\frac{\mu_{j}-\nu}{\xi}-1\right)=Z(\gamma)e^{\gamma\mu_{j}},

where
\gamma=1/\xi
and
Z(\gamma)=e^{-\gamma\nu-1}
. To determine the normalization constant
Z(\gamma)
, we invoke the primal feasibility constraint
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda^{*}_{j}=1
, which gives

Z(\gamma)\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(\gamma\cdot\mu_{j})=1\Longrightarrow Z(\gamma)=\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp(\gamma\cdot\mu_{j})}.

Substituting this expression back, we obtain optimal distribution

\displaystyle\lambda^{*}_{j}=\frac{\exp(\gamma\cdot\mu_{j})}{Z(\gamma)},

where multiplier
\gamma
is uniquely determined by the active entropy
constraint
-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda^{*}_{j}\log\lambda^{*}_{j}=s
, namely,

\log Z(\gamma)-\frac{\gamma}{Z(\gamma)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mu_{j}\exp(\gamma\cdot\mu_{j})=s.

Moreover, since
\lambda^{*}_{j}>0
for all
j
, the optimal distribution must assign positive weight to indices with
\mu_{j}<\mu_{\max}
.
Consequently, the corresponding weighted average satisfies

\displaystyle J_{\max}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mu_{j}\lambda^{*}_{j}<\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mu_{1}\lambda^{*}_{j}=\mu_{1}=\sigma_{\max}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi}).

i.e., the objective value is strictly smaller than the maximal eigenvalue.

Finally, a direct differentiation yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial J}{\partial\gamma}=

\displaystyle\frac{Z^{\prime\prime}}{Z}-\Bigl(\frac{Z^{\prime}}{Z}\Bigr)^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda^{*}_{j}(\gamma)\mu_{j}^{2}-\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda^{*}_{j}(\gamma)\mu_{i}\Bigr)^{2}=\mathrm{Var}_{p(\gamma)}(\mu)\geq 0,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial s}{\partial\gamma}=

\displaystyle\frac{Z^{\prime}}{Z}-J(\gamma)-\gamma J^{\prime}(\gamma)=-\gamma\mathrm{Var}_{p(\gamma)}(\mu)\leq 0,

which implies
\frac{\partial J}{\partial s}<0
and hence
J
is a strictly decreasing function of
s
, which is finally completes our proof. ∎

Appendix E
Proof of Theorem
5

As in the proof of Theorem
4
, we can write

\displaystyle J_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)

\displaystyle=\min_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\ \min_{U_{A}\in{\rm U}(\mathcal{H}_{A})}\ \min_{U_{B}\in{\rm U}(\mathcal{H}_{B})}\mathbf{F}(\lambda,U_{A},U_{B})

\displaystyle=\min_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\ \min_{U_{A}\in{\rm U}(\mathcal{H}_{A})}\ \min_{U_{B}\in{\rm U}(\mathcal{H}_{B})}\operatorname{Tr}(XY),

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\quad\lambda_{j}\geq 0,\quad\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_{j}=1,\quad\mathbf{H}(\lambda)\geq s,

(38)

where
X=\Lambda^{1/2}\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{A}\Lambda^{1/2}0
and
Y=\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{B}
, with
\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda)
.
For fixed
\lambda
, the minimization of
\operatorname{Tr}(XY)
is achieved when their eigenvectors are aligned ane the eigenvalues are arranged in the reversed order, i.e.,

\min_{U_{A}\in{\rm U}(\mathcal{H}_{A})}\min_{U_{B}\in{\rm U}(\mathcal{H}_{B})}\operatorname{Tr}(XY)\ \geq\ \sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}(X)\,\beta_{N-i+1}.

(39)

Case 1.
s<\log N
.

Since
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{A}\succeq 0
and
\alpha_{N}
is its smallest eigenvalue, we have
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{A}\succeq\alpha_{N}I
, and hence

X=\Lambda^{1/2}\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{A}\Lambda^{1/2}\ \succeq\ \alpha_{N}\Lambda.

It follows that

\sigma_{k}(X)\geq\alpha_{N}\lambda_{k},\qquad k=1,\ldots,N,

Substituting this into (
39
) yields the relaxed bound

\min_{U_{A},U_{B}}\operatorname{Tr}(XY)\ \geq\ \alpha_{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta_{N-i+1}\lambda_{i}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle J_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s)

\displaystyle\geq\min_{\lambda}\ \alpha_{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta_{N-i+1}\lambda_{i}=\min_{\lambda}\ \sum_{i=1}^{N}\widetilde{\mu}_{i}\,\lambda_{i}=:\widetilde{J}_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},s),

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\quad\lambda_{j}\geq 0,\quad\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_{j}=1,\quad\mathbf{H}(\lambda)\geq s,

The optimization defining
\widetilde{J}_{\min}
is a linear program over a convex feasible set and can be solved in closed form by the same KKT analysis as in Theorem
4
; the only change is the sign of Gibbs parameter.

Case 2.
s=\log N
.

In the maximally entangled regime, the entropy constraint forces the Schmidt coefficients to be uniform, i.e.,
\Lambda=\frac{1}{N}I
. Consequently,

X=\Lambda^{1/2}\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{A}\Lambda^{1/2}=\frac{1}{N}\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{A},\qquad\text{and hence}\qquad\sigma_{i}(X)=\frac{1}{N}\alpha_{i},\ \ i=1,\ldots,N,

Substituting into (
39
) gives

J_{\min}(\mathcal{K}_{\phi},\log N)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\,\beta_{N-i+1},

which is the claimed value in the maximally entangled regime. This completes the proof.

Proof of Proposition
1

According to the spectrum structure

\mathrm{spec}(\mathcal{K}_{a})=\{v(p),v(p),w(p),w(p)\},\qquad\mathrm{spec}(\mathcal{K}_{b})=\{v(q),v(q),w(q),w(q)\},

we have

\displaystyle\mu=(v(p)v(q),v(p)v(q),w(p)w(q),w(p)w(q)),\qquad\widetilde{\mu}=w(q)\{v(p),v(p),w(p),w(p)\},

which further indicates
d_{\max}=d_{\min}=2
. According to the Theorems
4
and
5
, the transition point is
s=\log 2
.
We first consider the case that the entanglement entropy satisfies
s\leq\log 2
. In this case, the extremal privacy enegries is give by the maximal and minimal eigenvalue of
\mathcal{K}_{\phi}
, i.e.,

J_{\max}(s)=v(p)v(q),\qquad\widetilde{J}_{\min}(s)=w(p)w(q),\quad p,q\in[\tfrac{1}{2},1]

Maximizing over
p,q\in[\tfrac{1}{2},1]
, the optimal ECLM-QLDP leakage level is given by

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{*}(s)\leq\log\!\left(\max_{p,q\in[1/2,1]}\frac{v(p)v(q)}{w(p)w(q)}\right)=2\log 3,

where the maximum attained at
p=q=1
. This coincides with the leakage level in the non-entangled case.

We then consider the case that the entanglement entropy satisfies
s>\log 2
. We first analyze the maximal privacy budget
J_{\max}(s,\mathcal{K}_{\phi})
. Since the operator
\mathcal{K}_{\phi}
admits only two distinct eigenvalues, each with degeneracy two, the optimal Gibbs distribution must be uniform within each degenerate eigenspace. Let
\tau\in[\tfrac{1}{2},1]
denote the total weight assigned to the eigenspace corresponding to the maximal eigenvalue
v(p)v(q)
, with the remaining weight
1-\tau
assigned to the minimal-eigenvalue eigenspace
w(p)w(q)
, the maximal privacy energy takes the form

\displaystyle J_{\max}(s)=\tau\,v(p)v(q)+(1-\tau)\,w(p)w(q).

where the
\tau
is uniquely determines by the entanglement entropy constraint, i.e.,
s=\log 2-\tau\log\tau-(1-\tau)\log(1-\tau)
. Similarly, the relaxed minimal privacy energies take the form

\displaystyle\widetilde{J}_{\min}(s)

\displaystyle=(1-\tau)\,v(p)w(q)+\tau\,w(p)w(q).

Therefore, the optimal ECLM–QLDP leakage level admits the upper bound

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{*}(s)

\displaystyle\leq\log\!\left(\max_{p,q\in[1/2,1]}\frac{\tau\,v(p)v(q)+(1-\tau)\,w(p)w(q)}{(1-\tau)\,v(p)w(q)+\tau\,w(p)w(q)}\right)=\log\!\left(\frac{8\tau+1}{3-2\tau}\right),

where the maximum is attained at
p=q=1
.
