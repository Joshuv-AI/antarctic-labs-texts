Development and preliminary content and face validation of the Cyberostracism Measure for Indian Young Adults (CMIYA)
Authors: Irene Sambath, Kalpana B Kosalram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCyberostracism, referring to experiences of being ignored, excluded, or socially dismissed in online environments, has emerged as a significant psychosocial stressor among present-day late adolescents and young adults. While international studies have developed measures of cyberostracism, culturally relevant tools to quantitatively assess cyberostracism in the Indian context remain limited. Given the rapid evolution of digital communication and increasing problematic internet-related behaviours, there is a need for a psychometrically grounded measure. This study aimed to establish content validity, face validity, and response-process evidence for the newly developed Cyberostracism Measure for Indian Young Adults.MethodsThis study is guided by Williams’ Temporal Need-Threat Model of Ostracism. Best-practice scale development guidelines were followed to generate items using both deductive methods (literature review and appraisal of existing cyberostracism scales) and inductive methods (focus group discussions with young adults aged 18–24 years). From an initial pool of 70 items, 60 items were refined through cognitive interviews conducted among two purposively sampled groups of thirty young adults using verbal probing and think-aloud approach. Content validity was assessed by an interdisciplinary panel of eight experts using Content Validity Index (CVI) and Content Validity Ratio (CVR). Linguistic clarity and instructional adequacy were evaluated by two English language experts using percentage agreement and Cohen’s kappa. Face validity was assessed among ten adolescents using the Face Validity Index (FVI). A theory-driven construct-alignment checklist was additionally applied as an internal quality assurance step to ensure conceptual coherence prior to factor analysis.ResultsFollowing expert review, 42 items were retained. The measure demonstrated strong content validity (S-CVI/Ave = 0.88; S-CVR = 0.78), acceptable face validity (S-FVI/Ave = 0.98; S-FVI/UA = 0.90), and perfect linguistic agreement (κ = 1.00). Construct-alignment review supported conceptual adequacy of retained items. The retained items captured direct, subtle, and post-ostracism digital exclusion experiences.ConclusionThe Cyberostracism Measure for Indian Young Adults (CMIYA) demonstrated strong preliminary evidence of content validity, face validity, linguistic clarity, and conceptual coherence. These findings support progression to further psychometric evaluation, including exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05170-5.Keywords: Cyberostracism, Virtual ostracism, Online exclusion, Content validity, Face validity, Scale development, Late adolescents and young adults

Introduction

The pervasive integration of digital technologies and social media platforms has revolutionized how young people communicate in their daily lives, maintain relationships, and participate in peer networks. Messaging applications, social networking sites, online gaming, virtual classrooms, and group-based digital spaces have expanded opportunities for connection; while simultaneously introducing novel forms of interpersonal adversity. One such experience is cyberostracism [1].

Cyberostracism refers to perceived experiences of being ignored, excluded or socially dismissed in digitally-mediated environments [2–6]. The cyberostacism phenomenon is often subtle and covert, and may involve direct exclusion, such as being removed from a group [7, 8], and omitted from an online chat or activity [9–11]. It may as well be more passive or ambiguous, such as repeated non-response [12, 13], selective acknowledgement [14, 15], or observing others receive interaction while one’s own communication remains unnoticed [16, 17]. Cyberostracism is conceptually distinct from cyberbullying and online harassment [18, 19]. Whereas cyberbullying typically involves intentional, repeated aggression or harm through digital means, cyberostracism concerns the experience of social exclusion or non-acknowledgement and may occur even when another person’s intent is uncertain or cannot be verified [1].

The present study was guided by Williams’ Temporal Need-Threat Model of Ostracism (TNTM), which provides a well-established framework for understanding the psychological effects of being ignored or excluded [20]. The model proposes that ostracism threatens four fundamental psychological needs: belonging, self-esteem, control, and meaningful existence [20]. It further describes three temporal stages of response. Individuals experience immediate needs threat and emotional distress during the reflexive stage. In response, individuals attempt to interpret the event and may engage in social monitoring or compensatory behaviours in an effort to reconnect. Prolonged or repeated exclusion may contribute to the resignation stage, where individuals may experience feelings of alienation, loneliness, helplessness, or depressive symptoms [20]. The TNTM is particularly appropriate for cyberostracism because online exclusion can occur through direct, subtle, passive, or ambiguous interpersonal cues, while still being experienced as a threat to social connection and recognition [3, 21]. It therefore provides a conceptual basis for examining not only online exclusionary events but also the cognitive, emotional, and behavioural responses that may follow them [3, 22–25].

Cyberostracism has been associated with adverse psychological and behavioural outcomes [1, 25, 26], including reduced wellbeing [27, 28], depressive symptoms [18, 29–31], anxiety [32–36], maladaptive coping [25, 37], and problematic digital use behaviours [38, 39]. These concerns may be especially salient among the late adolescents and young adults demographic [40] who are extensively reliant on online platforms for social relating and particularly susceptible due to their heightened need for peer acceptance, relationship maintenance, social identity and belonging [41, 42]. In India, where digital adoption has increased dramatically [43], widespread use of smartphone-based communication [44], and the growing centrality of online education in higher-education settings [45] make it important to understand how young adults experience and interpret online exclusion [46, 47]. Cyberostracism may represent a significant but understudied public health challenge amid rising youth mental health concerns [48]. Consequently, accurate and culturally sensitive quantitative measurement of cyberostracism among Indian young adult population remain unavailable.

Three published measures have contributed to the emerging measurement literature. The available 14-item Cyberostracism Scales by Hatun & Demirci (2022) [49] for Turkish late adolescents, and Niu et al. (2018) [31] for Chinese middle-adolescents were developed using pre-pandemic data, whereas patterns of digital communication and online social interaction have evolved considerably since then. Moreover, the full item set of the 35-item scale by Shi et al. (2023) [50] developed for Chinese college students, was not publicly available, limiting evaluation beyond its published description. While these instruments offer foundational insights, they were developed within distinct sociocultural, linguistic, developmental, and digital-use contexts. Direct use or simple adaptation of existing measures may not adequately capture contemporary forms of online exclusion experienced by Indian young adults, including platform-mediated peer practices, locally meaningful patterns of group interaction, and subtle forms of selective visibility, acknowledgement, and relational distancing. These considerations supported the contextually grounded item-development process rather than adopting an existing instrument without first examining the relevance and comprehensiveness of its content for the intended population.

Accordingly, this paper addresses this gap by detailing the first phase in scale formation, the content and face validation of the Cyberostracism Measure for Indian Young Adults (CMIYA), developed specifically for the Indian sociocultural milieu. Specifically, the study sought to: (1) generate an item pool through deductive review of cyberostracism literature and existing measures, alongside inductive focus group discussions with young adults aged 18–24 years, (2) refine the draft items through cognitive interviews, (3) assess item relevance and essentiality through expert content validation, (4) evaluate linguistic clarity and instructional adequacy, (5) assess face validity and response-process evidence among the target population, and (6) examine the conceptual alignment of retained items with the study’s operational definition of cyberostracism and the Temporal Need-Threat Model before subsequent psychometric evaluation.

Methods

Study design

This methodological study followed best-practice psychometric scale development guidelines [51], employing a mixed-methods approach integrating qualitative and quantitative phases, an exploratory-sequential design that is well-suited for instrument development and validation [52].

Study setting

This study requires participants to be internet and social media savvy, hence the reason for selecting late adolescents and young adults from higher educational institutions, and not early or middle adolescent group from schools [40]. The chosen group exhibit greater autonomy in digital communication, independent social networking, and extensive engagement with online peers. Inclusion of younger adolescents would have required different ethical procedures, including parental consent, and may have introduced developmental differences beyond the intended scope of the scale development. Furthermore, higher educational institutions provide a heterogeneous student population representing diverse academic disciplines, genders, and urban and rural backgrounds from different regions of India. The institutional setting enabled systematic recruitment of participants while ensuring appropriate ethical oversight and administrative permissions throughout the study.

Theoretical framework

The TNTM informed the study at multiple stages of scale development. During the qualitative phase, the focus group discussion guide was developed to explore participants’ experiences of online exclusion, their interpretations of those experiences, perceived threats to psychological needs, and subsequent emotional, cognitive, and behavioural responses. During item generation, both deductively and inductively derived items were examined for conceptual consistency. Finally, prior to psychometric evaluation, a theory-driven construct-alignment checklist was developed and applied by the research team as an internal quality assurance step. The checklist was applied to systematically review and ensure that retained items adequately represented cyberostracism, consistent with the study’s operational definition, aligned with the four threatened psychological needs described in the TNTM, and differentiation from related constructs such as cyberbullying, online harassment, or general social media use. Items identified as conceptually ambiguous, redundant, or inconsistent were revised or removed before proceeding to psychometric evaluation.

Operational definition

Drawing on TNTM, the existing cyberostracism literature, appraisal of published cyberostracism measures, and findings from the qualitative phase of the present study, cyberostracism was operationally defined as perceived experiences of direct, passive, or ambiguous online social exclusion occurring through digitally mediated interactions, together with associated cognitive, emotional, and behavioural responses arising from such exclusion experiences. For the purpose of this study, the term young adults operationally included late adolescents and young adults aged 18–24 years within higher educational institutions.

Phase 1: item development process

Step 1: item generation

Both deductive and inductive methods were used for item generation. Deductive method followed a narrative review of the cyberostracism and ostracism literature, assessment of existing Cyberostracism scales, and theoretical framework application. The literature was examined to identify the conceptual definitions of cyberostracism, recurring dimensions, antecedents, behavioural manifestations, psychological responses, and contemporary forms of digitally mediated social exclusion. This step helped drafting of initial items but did not involve direct adaptation of any existing scale. Inductive method followed responses from the qualitative interviews of young adults in the age group 18–24 years. Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) were selected as the most appropriate qualitative data collection procedure to capture context-specific expressions and virtual ostracism experiences [53]. This dual approach ensured that the initial pool of generated items were both theoretically informed and empirically grounded in the lived realities of the target population. The contribution of the deductive and inductive approaches to each generated item is presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Sampling

To be eligible for the study, the adolescents must be using virtual medium for online communication for the past one year or more, and must be socially active on atleast one of the social networking sites or virtual communication platforms such as Instagram, Facebook, WhatsApp, Gaming, email, text messaging for at least two months in the past for non-academic or non-job related purposes. Participants were purposively sampled from government and private higher educational institutions in Chengalpattu and Chennai with appropriate permissions. We conducted five FGDs across these settings to capture diverse perspectives, preceded by a pilot FGD in a private institution to refine the discussion guide. The reason for selecting purposeful sampling is that it allows the research team to choose eligible participants who give richness of information that is suitable for this objective.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and basic demographics such as age, gender, and ethnicity was obtained. The research team chose a mixture of phenomenological and grounded theory approach [53] to develop the FGD guide, carefully worded with consultation from qualitative experts and reference to relevant qualitative study design literature. Therefore, the FGD guide has a key structure to guide the questions which relate to the key components of the TNTM, two vignette scenarios included, and also the freedom to explore emergent experiences of this study interest. Each FGD with 7–8 respondents in each group (n = 44, females 52%) were conducted in a natural setting at non-teaching hours at one of the classrooms or seminar room in their department. Recorded interviews were transcribed. The qualitative data informed the generation of scale items. Thematic analysis of the qualitative component of this study will be reported as a separate paper for detailed reading.

The combination of results from both deductive and inductive methods were used to identify the items to assess cyberostracim for the Indian setting. As a best practice, the initial pool of items in first draft were at least twice as long as the desired scale [54, 55]. The statements have a five-point Likert Scale: 1= Never, 2 = Rarely, 3 = Sometimes, 4 = Often, 5 = Always. A five-point frequency scale was selected to enhance participant comprehension, reduce response burden, and ensure consistent interpretation across respondents.

Step 2: cognitive interviews

An initial pool of 70 items was generated in total. Subsets of 30 adolescents from the target population were gathered in two batches in a classroom on different days. The generated items were displayed on the digital screen to the students. Verbal probing and ‘think aloud’ technique was used [56], allowing participants to verbalize their thought process as they respond to each item, to refine meaning, remove duplicate items, reword items, and suggestions for addition of items. This qualitative feedback method of direct engagement with target demographic, frequently utilized in scale development [56, 57], is particularly effective in uncovering nuances of comprehension and cognitive processing that quantitative methods alone might overlook, thereby enhancing the ecological validity of the instrument. Each interview lasted for 25–30 min. Ten items were removed at this stage.

Step 3: content validity

Content validation were performed through a systematic approach [58–60].

Data collection tool for content validity

First, a ‘response process validation form’ was prepared to rate relevance, and essentiality for the draft 60 items. This form included fields for expert demographic information: name, position, institution, email address, and signature. It was followed by an introductory paragraph on the meaning of cyberostracism, the purpose of study, the inductive and deductive steps followed for item generation, theory used, instructions, rating scale, tables for expert scoring, summary interpretation guide for the scores, bibliographic references, and a thank you note. The experts were asked to provide their ratings for each item. There was provision for the experts to provide comment on every item.

‘Relevancy’ is captured on a four-point interval scale (1 = Not relevant, 2 = Needs substantial revision, 3 = Needs minor revision, and 4 = Very relevant) to calculate Content Validity Index (CVI) for both items level (I-CVI) and scale level (S-CVI). Ratings 1 and 2 indicate ‘invalid or irrelevant content’ and recorded as ‘0’. Ratings 3 and 4 indicate ‘valid or relevant content’ and recorded as ‘1’. ‘Essentiality’ is captured on a four-point interval scale (1 = Not necessary, 2 = Useful but not essential, 3 = May be essential, and 4 = Definitely essential) to calculate Content Validity Ratio (CVR). Ratings 1 and 2 indicate ‘not essential’ and recorded as ‘0’. Ratings 3 and 4 indicate ‘essential’ content and recorded as ‘1’.

Sampling method for content validity

A judgemental sampling method [61] was employed to select expert panels. The first author contacted the experts via phone and in-person to explain the purpose of this study. The content validity form, along with a cover letter, was either emailed or provided in printed form. Considering the seniority, professional responsibilities, and busy schedules of the experts, sufficient time of four weeks was given to return the response. Eleven experts were contacted. When the first author received response from the eight expert, no further follow-up contacts were made. The number of expert selection is based on recommendations by Lawshe (1975) and Lynn (1986) [58, 59].

Experts were selected based on their academic qualifications, professional expertise, and experience in fields’ relevant to mental health, behavioural science, public health, and digital health, rather than from a single professional discipline. Inclusion criteria for expert selection included: (1) postgraduate or doctoral qualifications in relevant disciplines, (2) professional or academic experience related to mental health, adolescent well-being, behavioural sciences, psychometrics, psychiatry, public health, or digital interactions, and (3) a minimum of ten years of academic, clinical, research, or professional experience. The final panel comprised of both academic and applied professional experience, including a practising psychiatrist, two clinical psychologists involved in patient care, a behavioural health counsellor, a public health researcher working within a national public health organisation, a mental health researcher, a statistician, and a digital health expert. This methodological approach involving expert panels from diverse fields but relevant disciplines aligns with best practices for establishing robust content validity in psychometric tool development, and ensures that item evaluation reflected theoretical, clinical, public health, and practical perspectives relevant to the construct of cyberostracism [60].

Interpretation of content validity

I-CVI values range from 0 to 1. An I-CVI of 0.79 or more indicates the items are relevant, a value between 0.70 and 0.79 necessitates item revisions, and a value less than 0.70 requires item elimination [62]. S-CVI value of 0.8 or higher is considered acceptable [62]. A CVR value for six-panel experts involved is 0.99 [58]. However, the acceptable CVR threshold is at least 0.75, indicating the individual item should be revised or deleted.

Decision criteria for item retention

Final decisions regarding retention, revision, or deletion were based on integrating quantitative content validity indices with qualitative expert feedback and theoretical considerations. Specifically, items demonstrating satisfactory content validity, conceptual clarity, and no substantive concerns raised by the expert panel were retained. Items meeting acceptable thresholds but accompanied by recommendations to improve wording, clarity, or contextual relevance were revised and retained. Items demonstrating inadequate content validity indices, substantial overlap with other items, poor alignment with the operational definition of cyberostracism, or expert consensus that the item represented a different construct or lacked relevance were deleted. New items suggested by experts that addressed conceptual gaps identified during content validation were added.

Step 4: linguistic clarity and instructional adequacy

Following content validation process, a linguistic review of the retained 42 draft items were conducted by two English language experts who are professors from the Department of English from within the researcher’s institution and external to the institution. The first author contacted the language experts via phone to explain the purpose of this study. The form was emailed with a cover letter. This form too included an introductory paragraph on the meaning of cyberostracism, the purpose of study, the inductive and deductive steps followed for item generation, theory used, instructions, rating scale, tables for expert scoring, interpretation of scoring, bibliographic references, and a thank you note. Following criteria of Desai & Patel, 2020 [63], each item was assessed for: (1) Appropriateness of grammar, (2) Clarity and unambiguity of item wording, (3) Correct spelling of words, (4) Correct structuring of sentences, (5) Adequacy and clarity of instructions provided in the instrument, (6) Overall structure and logical flow of the instrument, (7) Appropriateness of difficulty level for participants, and (8) Reasonableness of items in relation to intended purpose of the instrument.

Each item was rated using binary response (Yes or No). Percentage agreement and Cohen’s Kappa coefficient were calculated to assess inter-rater agreement [64]. For language review, κ ≥ 0.60 is considered acceptable to strong. Cohen’s κ is designed for nominal (categorical) data, binary or mutually exclusive categories, and agreement beyond chance. Cohen’s κ assumes two independent raters, measures agreement, and adjusts for chance agreement only in a two-rater scenario.

Step 5a: face validity

Face validation was performed through a systematic approach [60, 63, 65]. This step, integral to response-process evidence helped confirm that the items were easily understood and appeared relevant to the lived experiences of the target population, which is essential for maximizing engagement and data quality [60].

Data collection tool for face validity

First, the ‘response process validation form’ was prepared to rate clarity and comprehensibility for the retained 42 items on a four-point interval scale (1 = Not clear and understandable, 2 = Somewhat clear and understandable, 3 = Clear and understandable, and 4 = Very clear and understandable). This form included an introductory paragraph about cyberostracism, the purpose of study, instructions, rating scale, table for scoring, and a thank you note. Based on its clarity and comprehension, ratings 1 and 2 indicating ‘invalid or irrelevant content’ was recorded as ‘0’. Ratings 3 and 4 indicating ‘valid or relevant content’ was recorded as ‘1’. The lay experts were provided instructions to rate each item. There was provision to provide comment on every item.

Sampling for face validity

Opinion on the face validity of the items was obtained from a purposive sampled ten adolescents which is the minimum acceptable number of raters [65].

Interpretation of face validity

An acceptable cut-off FVI score above 0.80 was considered [65].

Step 5b: cognitive interviews

From the same ten adolescents who took part in face validity, we conducted brief cognitive interviews to further determine how the intended population interpret and understand the retained draft 42 items, and to identify any residual concerns regarding wording, comprehension, or contextual relevance. The deleted 19 items were also discussed to explore reasons for poor clarity or relevance. The feedback provided was noted by the first author. Overall, participants demonstrated a clear understanding of the retained items and did not identify any major omissions or misinterpretations of the construct. No further substantive modifications to the retained items were considered necessary following these interviews.

Steps 7 and 8: subsequent phases of scale development

Following completion of item development and preliminary validation (Steps 1 to 6), the retained CMIYA progressed to the subsequent phases of scale development. Step 7 comprises pilot testing of the refined item pool to evaluate its administration, feasibility, and preliminary performance prior to large-scale psychometric testing. Step 8 involves data collection for exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses using an adequately powered sample. These phases represent the ongoing scale development and evaluation process and are beyond the scope of the present manuscript. Data management

All collected data from various validation stages, including expert reviews, cognitive interviews, and adolescent feedback were catalogued, analysed, and securely stored in de-identified format in MS Excel.

Data collection duration

Securing administrative permissions required multiple visits to collegiate authorities. Similarly, obtaining access to participants involved repeated visits to the administrative offices of the identified government and private educational institutions. These processes collectively spanned from September 2024 to January 2025. Data collection schedules were coordinated around institutional activities such as internal examinations, sports events, cultural programmes, seminars, conferences, and workshops. A pilot FGD was conducted in October 2024. The remaining FGDs were carried out between February and April 2025. Identifying expert reviewers and content validity assessment was conducted between August and November 2025, followed by face validity assessment among adolescents in January 2026. Item revisions were performed iteratively based on expert and participant feedback throughout the scale development process.

Result

Item generation

A total of 60 preliminary items were generated. Of these, 27 items emerged inductively from FGDs, 4 items were deductively derived from existing literature, and 29 items were developed through a combination of deductive and inductive approaches. The item codes and source type are presented in Supplementary Table S1. Item codes were used in reporting to maintain scale confidentiality during the validation phase. Full item wording will be made available upon completion of scale validation process. The overall item development phase is diagrammatically shown in Fig. 1.

Content validity

The generated 60 items were reviewed by 8 experts. The qualitative suggestions provided by the experts were systematically integrated for item modification. Of the 60 items, 28 items received I-CVR score of 1.00, 14 items received 0.75, 17 items received 0.50, and 1 item received 0.25. Of the 60 items, 24 items received I-CVI of 1.00, 21 received 0.88, 10 items received 0.75, and 5 items received 0.63. Based on the quantitative content validity indices together with qualitative expert comments and theoretical review, the decision was to retain 19 items as such, 22 items were revised and retained, 19 items were deleted, and 1 item suggested by one of the experts was added. The overall S-CVR was 0.78, and S-CVI/Ave was 0.88. Twenty-four items received 100% agreement by all raters. The average proportion of items judged relevant by 8 raters was 0.89. Discussion

The present study developed the Cyberostracism Measure for Indian Young Adults (CMIYA) and established preliminary evidence supporting its content validity, face validity, linguistic clarity, and conceptual coherence. The combined use of deductive-inductive approach [51] generated a comprehensive pool of contemporary cyberostracism experiences reflecting digitally mediated exclusion across social networking platforms, online group interactions, messaging environments, and emerging relational dynamics among young adults. While existing cyberostracism measures and ostracism literature informed the conceptual foundation and deductive item generation process [31, 49, 50], the present measure was independently developed and contextually refined using qualitative data from Indian participants [53]. The rapidly evolving nature of online communication among the present-day young adults necessitates the inclusion of subtle and platform-mediated micro-exclusion experiences that may not be adequately represented in earlier measures [1, 43, 66].

The expert content validation was instrumental in refining the initial pool of 60 items into a more concise, and manageable 42 items. Overall, experts rated the retained items as highly relevant to the construct of cyberostracism (S-CVI/Ave = 0.88). A substantial proportion of items were judged as essential, with some items seen as useful but not strictly necessary (S-CVR = 0.78) demonstrating adequate essentiality, supporting retention of items for subsequent psychometric evaluation. Although only about 40% of items achieved universal agreement (S-CVI/UA = 0.42), this finding should be interpreted in the context of the study design. The S-CVI/UA is considered a conservative and stringent measure [59, 62], because universal agreement requires every expert to rate an item as relevant. Consequently, a single expert’s disagreement on one item can significantly lower the overall score. Lower S-CVI/UA values are therefore common especially as the number of experts increases, the eight experts were from different disciplines, a large initial item pool of 60 items, and an evolving conceptually nuanced complex construct like cyberostracism. Hence, some variation in expert judgements was anticipated and was addressed through qualitative feedback, iterative item revision, and construct-alignment review. The high face validity (I-FVI = 1.00, S-FVI/Ave = 0.98, S-FVI/UA = 0.90) confirms clarity and interpretability among the target population. Furthermore, the perfect agreement observed for linguistic clarity and instructional adequacy (κ = 1.00) ensures the readability and ease of administration of the developed items.

The retention of several theoretically meaningful items reflects the ecological reality of online social interactions among young adults, where ostracism is often experienced through silence, reduced reciprocity, withdrawal, or selective invisibility rather than overt rejection [67–69]. The recognition of ambiguity [70] as an inherent characteristic of cyberostracism is a notable conceptual contribution of the present study. Unlike cyberbullying or cyberaggression, cyberostracism frequently manifests through subtle, passive and socially ambigious digital interactions [3, 21]. Many expert comments questioned whether behaviours such as one-word responses, delayed replies, reduced engagement, lack of acknowledgement, or ‘seen’ without response necessarily reflected intentional exclusion. Contemporary ostracism theory suggests that certain perceived ostracism under conditions of uncertain intent may intensify psychological distress among young adults [12, 27, 71].

Several items capture subtle relational experiences such as being excluded from “Close Friends” stories, receiving minimal or no reactions during celebrations, not being tagged in event photographs, selective messaging, observing others being acknowledged while one’s own interactions remain unnoticed, and changes in communication tone through emojis or stickers. Within online peer cultures, such symbolic inclusionary and exclusionary experiences [39] make significant meaning to their social identity, kinship affinity, and relational validation [1, 72]. The inclusion of post-cyberostracism cognitive, emotional, and behavioural response items is another contribution of the present study. In addition to direct exclusionary experiences, adolescents described several downstream responses such as compulsive checking for notifications, overthinking exclusion experiences, posting more frequently to regain attention, withdrawing from online participation, muting or leaving groups, and questioning interpersonal closeness following online neglect. These responses align with the reflection and resignation stages proposed in Williams’ Temporal Need Threat Model of Ostracism [20], wherein individuals engage in heightened social monitoring, rumination, compensatory reconnection attempts, or withdrawal following ostracism experiences [34, 73–75].

In comparison with existing cyberostracism scale development studies, explicit reporting of content validity metrics remains limited. For instance, the 14-item Cyberostracism Scale developed among Turkish university students by Hatun and Demirci (2022) [49] involved expert review and Lawshe’s method for item refinement. The 14-item scale developed by Niu et al. (2018) [31] for Chinese adolescents was based on semi-structured interviews and item refinement procedures. The 35-item scale by Shi et al. (2023) [50] also used structured interviews. Unlike the existing measures, the CMIYA intentionally incorporates subtle and ambiguous digitally mediated exclusion experiences together with post-ostracism cognitive and behavioural responses derived from qualitative data among Indian young adults.

Strengths

The methodological strength of this study includes the use of rigorous mixed-methods approach integrating deductive and inductive item generation strategies, involvement of multidisciplinary expert panel, and the inclusion of face validity and response-process evaluation among the target population. The iterative expert review process enhanced the conceptual clarity of the item pool. Several items were revised to reduce ambiguity, improve comparative framing, clarify interactional context, revise Likert scaling, privacy management, communication preferences, social comparison, relationship insecurity, and distinguish cyberostracism from related but conceptually different phenomena such as cyberbullying. Items relying heavily on inferred motives, speculative assumptions, or private unverifiable behaviours were removed to improve construct clarity. Similarly, several gaming-specific and platform-specific items were either generalized or excluded to enhance broader applicability across the young adults with varying online engagement patterns.

The feedback from face validity assessment further highlighted the importance of participant-centered language. Revisions primarily addressed wording clarity, and comprehensibility of online interaction scenarios. Importantly, participants also indicated that cyberostracism experiences often extended beyond short recall windows. Consequently, the recall period was revised from one month to one year to better capture episodic, recurring, and cumulative online exclusion experiences [49]. Additionally, the use of construct-alignment checklist represents a novel and theory-driven step to safeguard against construct drift and enhance conceptual coherence prior to factor analysis. It provided an additional layer of conceptual quality assurance beyond conventional content validity procedures. This step is particularly important given that cyberostracism is an evolving construct with context-dependent manifestations, especially within rapidly changing digital communication practices and culturally specific online ecosystem such as India [76].

Implications and future directions

The next phase of scale development will involve comprehensive psychometric evaluation using a large representative sample of Indian young adults. Specially, exploratory factor analysis will be undertaken to determine the underlying factor structure, followed by confirmatory factor analysis to verify the dimensional structure. Internal consistency, test–retest reliability, convergent validity, discriminant validity, criterion-related validity, and measurement invariance will be evaluated. Given the increasing dependence of Indian young adults on digitally mediated communication for academic, social, and interpersonal relationships, subtle forms of online exclusion may remain unrecognised despite their psychological impact.

Following full psychometric evaluation, the CMIYA measure may support research examining the prevalence, correlates, and consequences of cyberostracism in higher-education settings. It may also assist student wellbeing services, counsellors, and institutional administrators in identifying patterns of online exclusion that may otherwise remain unreported, thereby informing preventive initiatives and appropriate psychosocial support. Longitudinal studies are warranted to examine temporal relationships between cyberostracism and mental health outcomes. The CMIYA may also be used in ecological and experience sampling studies to examine real time cyberostracism experiences and their immediate psychological consequences [77]. Future research should examine the factor structure, and applicability of the CMIYA across diverse educational, regional, and cultural settings.

Qualitative and mixed-methods research may further deepen understanding of how young adults interpret ambiguous online exclusion and its consequences. Participatory approaches, including online photovoice [78, 79], online interpretative phenomenological analysis [80], and community-based participatory research [81], may be considered where appropriate at the inductive stage to explore participants’ lived experiences and to inform context-sensitive prevention and support strategies. Such innovative approaches are suggested as methodological options rather than prescriptive requirements, as the appropriateness of a method depends on the specific research question, population, and study context.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations. First, although expert validation was conducted systematically, the Delphi technique [51] was not employed due to practical and methodological considerations. Second, while the multidisciplinary panel included experts with academic, clinical, public health, research, and applied professional expertise, experts were selected purposively and may not fully represent all stakeholder perspectives relevant to cyberostracism, such as student counsellors, social workers, digital platform practitioner, or young adults themselves. Third, the sample size for face validity response-process evaluation assessed among the target population was limited, which may not fully capture the diversity of interpretations across all subgroups. Fourth, the item pool was developed among young adults within higher educational settings in Tamil Nadu, which may limit broader cultural generalisability. However, the sample included students from different Indian states and a small number of international students, thereby contributing some sociocultural diversity within the sample.

Fifth, the recall period was extended from one month to one year following participant feedback that cyberostracism experiences often occurred episodically, infrequent, and were remembered over longer periods. While this modification was intended to improve content relevance, a longer recall may increase susceptibility to recall bias and differential recall of emotionally salient events. Future psychometric studies should evaluate the influence of alternative recall periods on measurement performance of CMIYA. Sixth, the qualitative component informing item generation will be reported separately, which may limit immediate access to the full contextual grounding of item generation within this manuscript. Finally, the present study focused on establishing content and face validity as foundational steps in scale development. Further psychometric evaluation is currently underway to establish the scale’s comprehensive measurement properties.

Conclusion

The present study developed a preliminary ‘Cyberostracism Measure for Indian Young Adults’ (CMIYA) grounded in both existing literature and the lived experiences of Indian young adults. The inclusion of culturally and developmentally relevant online experiences enhances the contextual relevance of the scale for current generation youngster populations. The items capture a broad spectrum of contemporary cyberostracism experiences, not only direct forms of online exclusion, but also subtle and ambiguous digitally mediated micro-exclusions, group-based exclusion, passive digital neglect, relational distancing, selective visibility, and post-ostracism cognitive and behavioural responses. The findings provide initial evidence supporting content validity, face validity, and linguistic clarity. The diligent application of these initial validation steps during item development strengthens the CMIYA’s foundation for the next phase of scale development and evaluation, through exploratory factor analysis and confirmatory factor analysis. Further psychometric evaluation is warranted to examine the facture structure, reliability, construct validity, and applicability of the CMIYA scale across broader populations and settings.
