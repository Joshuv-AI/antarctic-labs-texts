Multidisciplinary Treatment for Liver Cancer in China: A Cross-Sectional Survey of Practices, Gaps and Future Directions
Authors: Wei Wang, Wei Jia, Dao-Cheng Zhan, Heng-Jun Wu, FanZheng Meng, Shu-Geng Zhang, Rui-Peng Song, Jia-Bei Wang, Yao Liu, Ji-Zhou Wang, Da-Long Yin, Hui-Chuan Sun, Lianxin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo systematically evaluate the current status of the establishment and operation of multidisciplinary treatment (MDT) for liver cancer in China, identify key bottlenecks, and provide a scientific basis for promoting the transition of MDT from “demonstration and exploration” to “standardized construction”.MethodsThis study adopted a cross-sectional survey design to systematically investigate the current establishment and operational status of MDT for liver cancer in China. A total of 1,073 hospitals across 31 provincial-level administrative regions in China were selected as study sites. The study participants included 1,279 clinicians and relevant medical staff directly engaged in the clinical practice of liver cancer MDT.ResultsA total of 1,279 questionnaires were distributed, with 1,073 valid responses collected (validity rate: 83.89%). The results revealed the following characteristics of liver cancer MDT in China: (1) Organizational Structure: 51.35% of hospitals had implemented liver cancer MDT, with 24.14% establishing fixed teams. The predominant model was department-led (65.25%); (2) Clinical Practice: Annual case discussions showed a polarized distribution (≤50 cases: 41.70%; >200 cases: 16.99%). A majority (90.73%) recognized MDT’s significant impact on clinical decision-making, while 83.01% of respondents, based on their subjective experience, held the perception that MDT might be associated with survival improvement in patients; (3) Quality Control Systems: 79.92% of hospitals had basic quality control mechanisms, but only 2.96% had independent quality control departments. Information technology infrastructure required improvement (only 35.91% achieved data platform integration); (4) Key Bottlenecks: Insufficient policy support (41.18%), staffing challenges (19.92%), and scheduling difficulties (13.82%).ConclusionWhile liver cancer MDT has been preliminarily established in hospitals in China, challenges such as regional disparities, hospital-level gradient, weak quality control, and inadequate IT infrastructure persist. A tripartite strategy—incorporating tiered healthcare delivery, standardized protocols, and technological innovation—is recommended to achieve a qualitative transformation and support the “Healthy China 2030” initiative in liver cancer prevention and treatment.Keywords: liver cancer, multidisciplinary treatment, MDT, questionnaire survey, cross-sectional study

Introduction

Liver cancer, as a major disease posing a serious threat to the health of China’s population, imposes a particularly heavy disease burden. Epidemiological data1,2 indicate that China accounts for approximately 45.3% of the global annual incidence of liver cancer, with 410,000 new cases reported annually. The age-standardized incidence rate and mortality rate for liver cancer rank fourth and second, respectively, among all malignant tumors. Compared with other malignant tumors, liver cancer has unique clinical characteristics: early symptoms are often asymptomatic (approximately 70% of patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage), the disease progresses rapidly (median survival is less than one year), and treatment options are complex (involving six major treatment modalities). These characteristics make the traditional single-discipline diagnostic and treatment model inadequate to meet clinical needs, necessitating the establishment of a more scientifically sound diagnostic and treatment system.

In recent years, the field of liver cancer treatment has undergone revolutionary changes. With breakthroughs in molecularly targeted drugs, immune checkpoint inhibitors (such as PD-1/PD-L1 monoclonal antibodies), and combination therapy regimens, liver cancer treatment has entered the era of “precision medicine”. This transformation has driven the rapid development of the multidisciplinary treatment (MDT) model, which integrates the strengths of multiple disciplines, including hepatobiliary surgery, medical oncology, interventional radiology, and radiation oncology, to provide patients with personalized, precision treatment plans. Several studies3–7 have shown that the MDT model can increase the likelihood of liver cancer patients undergoing curative surgery and improve overall survival rates, fully demonstrating its unique value in enhancing clinical outcomes.

The Chinese government places a high priority on the promotion and application of the MDT model. The “14th Five-Year Plan for National Clinical Specialty Capacity Building” designates MDT as the core paradigm for cancer diagnosis and treatment,8 while the “Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Primary Liver Cancer (2022 Edition)” explicitly requires MDT as the standard diagnostic and treatment process.9 Authoritative academic organizations in China have successively issued expert consensus statements10,11 that not only establish the priority recommendation status of MDT but also establish a standardized framework encompassing case selection, diagnostic and treatment processes, and quality control. These policy measures provide institutional safeguards for the standardized development of MDT for liver cancer in China.

Multidisciplinary team care has been recommended as the standard management model for hepatocellular carcinoma by authoritative reviews and clinical guidelines from Europe and North America.12,13 Multiple retrospective clinical studies conducted across North America, Europe and Asia have demonstrated that MDT can optimize clinical decision making and potentially improve patient prognosis.14–16 However, most available evidence is derived from single-center, patient-level retrospective cohorts. Nationwide cross-sectional surveys that systematically characterize the real-world implementation status, organizational framework, quality control system and practical barriers of HCC-related MDT at the hospital level remain scarce in China. Furthermore, the development of liver cancer MDT in China faces unique challenges. Compared with Western countries, there are significant differences in the etiology (83% of cases are caused by hepatitis B virus infection), molecular characteristics (high TP53 mutation rate), and clinical staging (58% of patients are in the advanced stage) of liver cancer in China.17 Additionally, factors such as uneven distribution of medical resources and regional economic disparities18 collectively form the unique context for the development of liver cancer MDTs in China. Based on this, with the support of the Liver Cancer Professional Committee of the Chinese Anti-Cancer Association and other parties, this study conducted the first nationwide specialized survey on liver cancer MDT. Through a structured questionnaire system, key data on the current status of MDT construction, operational models, technical support, quality control, and clinician-reported feedback were collected, aiming to: (1) map the “panorama” of liver cancer MDT development in China; (2) identify key obstacles to MDT implementation; and (3) propose development pathways suitable for China’s national conditions.

Materials and Methods

Research Design and Sampling Strategy

This study employed a cross-sectional survey design. To ensure national representativeness of the sample, hospitals were selected using stratified random sampling. The sample size of 1,073 was determined to achieve full nationwide provincial coverage, with hospital numbers proportionally allocated according to the hierarchical distribution profiles of national medical institutions from the 2023 China Health Statistics Yearbook. All eligible hospitals were randomly selected using a random-number-table method, with hospital grade serving as the stratification dimension. In this questionnaire, Grade III-A hospitals accounted for 61.98% and Grade III-B hospitals for 10.07%, demonstrating high alignment with the overall characteristics of national liver cancer treatment institutions. In addition to primary nationwide descriptive analysis, exploratory stratified analyses were performed after data collection to assess potential subgroup heterogeneity.

Study Population and Inclusion Criteria

The study population comprised clinical physicians and medical staff engaged in MDT work related to liver cancer treatment across 1,073 sampled hospitals. This included core departments such as liver cancer surgery, medical oncology, interventional radiology, and infectious diseases, as well as MDT administrative units like medical affairs departments and MDT coordination offices. To ensure data authenticity and reliability, respondents must concurrently meet the following criteria: Professional alignment—affiliation with core departments involved in liver cancer diagnosis/treatment or MDT administrative units, with familiarity in MDT operational workflows; Work experience requirement—minimum one year of experience in liver cancer-related work; and role alignment, meaning direct involvement in HCC MDT clinical practice, organizational coordination, or quality control, enabling provision of firsthand practical information. With support from the Chinese Anti-Cancer Association’s Liver Cancer Committee, we contacted relevant departments of sampled hospitals and mobilized eligible medical personnel to take part in the survey. Tertiary Class A hospitals prioritized MDT team leaders or core members (chief physicians/associate chief physicians), while secondary and lower-level hospitals selected key physicians responsible for liver cancer diagnosis/treatment or MDT work. To avoid intra-hospital clustering bias and duplicated reporting, multiple responses from the same hospital were cross-checked and unified, and only one integrated institutional MDT profile was included in the final analysis.

Questionnaire Design and Validation

The questionnaire was developed and optimized based on the “Guidelines for Diagnosis and Treatment of Primary Liver Cancer (2022 Edition)”9 and the “Chinese Expert Consensus on Multidisciplinary Comprehensive Treatment of Liver Cancer”.10 The questionnaire (Supplementary Table 1) consisted of 48 items grouped into five modules: (1) basic information of hospitals and respondents; (2) establishment and operational status of MDT; (3) MDT procedures and outcome evaluation; (4) informatization construction and quality control of MDT; (5) suggestions and future prospects for MDT improvement. The questionnaire mainly included single-choice, multiple-choice, and fill-in-the-blank questions to collect objective hospital-level data. Four subjective evaluation items (Q26, Q33, Q35, Q38) adopted five-point Likert-type ordinal response formats to assess the impact of MDT, the effectiveness of quality control, MDT operational efficiency, and patient satisfaction, respectively. To ensure content validity, the questionnaire was reviewed by an expert panel comprising specialists in hepatobiliary surgery, medical oncology, and hospital quality management. Items were revised iteratively according to expert feedback to reach consistent revisions. Since this questionnaire mainly collected objective factual information and no composite scale scores were generated, reliability tests such as Cronbach’ s α were not performed. All respondents were informed about the study and agreed to participate, and questionnaire responses were collected anonymously.

Survey Implementation and Data Collection

The questionnaire survey was conducted from January 1, 2025, to March 1, 2025. It was implemented online using a standardized model via the Wenjuanxing platform (https://www.wjx.cn/), which provided logic validation, data encryption, real-time monitoring, and other functionalities tailored to healthcare professionals’ work environments. Questionnaires were distributed via the aforementioned recruitment channels by sending eligible respondents a dedicated link and instructions, clearly outlining the survey purpose and data confidentiality commitment. The built-in logic jumps and mandatory field validation within the questionnaire ensured completeness and accuracy during the completion process. Data collection adopted a dual real-time oversight mechanism of “system validation + manual review”: First, the WenjuanXing platform activated mandatory field validation and logic conflict alerts for core items, requiring respondents to complete information or resolve inconsistencies before submission. Second, a five-member oversight team reviewed questionnaires daily, verified key information, and screened for anomalies. Third, questionnaires with missing information, ambiguous responses, or logical inconsistencies underwent traceable verification and supplementation within 24 hours. Fourth, weekly cross-checks between online and local backup data prevented loss or tampering. The entire process strictly complied with the Personal Information Protection Law to ensure data security and compliance.

Non-Response Bias Control

To minimize and evaluate non-response bias, a three-pronged approach was implemented: prevention (enhancing participation by clarifying the survey’s academic and practical value, and optimizing the questionnaire to 48 items); intervention (stratified follow-up by geographic region and hospital tier, coupled with outreach to hospital administrators to boost respondent support and engagement); and evaluation (achieving a 100% overall response rate, with all subgroups—including geographic regions and hospital tiers—exceeding 85% response rates). These targeted measures effectively ensured data reliability and result objectivity.

Statistical Methods

Invalid questionnaires were excluded prior to analysis based on predefined criteria (eg, illogical responses, response rate <50%, missing data). All descriptive indicators presented in the main text and figures were derived from the enrolled 1073 hospitals. For valid questionnaires with only isolated missing items, complete-case analysis was applied; participants with missing values for a specific variable were excluded only from analyses involving that variable to ensure consistent statistical caliber. Data cleaning and statistical analysis were performed using R 4.2.0 and Excel 2020 to ensure reliable results. The core statistical method was frequency analysis, with categorical variables described as “frequency (n) and percentage (%)”. For bias control, intergroup differences were assessed using chi-square tests. This study was designed as a national baseline descriptive survey focusing on the overall panorama of liver cancer MDT practice. No patient survival data were collected; therefore, this study could not evaluate long-term clinical prognostic outcomes. All results were independently calculated by two researchers and cross-verified.

Patient and Public Involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Questionnaire Distribution and Characteristics

A total of 1,279 questionnaires were distributed across 31 provincial-level administrative regions nationwide, with 1,279 returned and 1,073 deemed valid, resulting in a 100% return rate and an 83.89% validity rate (Figure 1A and B). Analysis of the participating hospitals showed that the majority were general hospitals (914, 85.18%, Figure 1C); the highest proportion were tertiary-level A hospitals (665, 61.98%, Figure 1D); and the majority were provincial and municipal-level hospitals (740, 68.97%, Figure 1E).

(C) Distribution of hospital types (eg, general vs specialized hospitals). (D) Proportion of hospitals by grade (eg, Tertiary A, Tertiary B, Secondary). (E) Distribution of hospitals by administrative level (provincial, municipal, county-level).

Stratified analyses by hospital level and geographic region were conducted among hospitals with complete corresponding information (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). Nearly half of hospitals (472/947, 49.8%) nationwide had previously conducted liver cancer MDT work. The proportion peaked in provincial-level hospitals (155/227, 68.3%), was around 50% in prefecture-level hospitals (255/513, 49.7%), and was lowest in county-level hospitals (62/207, 30.0%; P<0.001), indicating a clear hospital-level gradient. Regional disparities were also observed: eastern (223/428, 52.1%), central (142/257, 55.3%), and western hospitals (107/262, 40.8%; P=0.002). Only 259/947 (27.3%) hospitals had established a fixed liver cancer MDT expert team, whereas 688/947 (72.7%) had not. Fixed-team establishment also presented a hospital-level gradient: provincial-level (117/227, 51.5%), prefecture-level (123/513, 24.0%), county-level hospitals (19/207, 9.2%; P<0.001). Significant regional differences existed for fixed-team rates: eastern (134/428, 31.3%), central (65/257, 25.3%), western hospitals (60/262, 22.9%; P=0.038). These stratified analyses included 947 hospitals with complete hospital-level and geographic data.

Basic Implementation Status of Liver Cancer MDT

MDT Adoption Rate and Departmental Staff Structure

Among the 1,073 surveyed hospitals, 551 had previously conducted liver cancer MDT work (51.35%), while only 259 hospitals had established a fixed liver cancer MDT expert team (24.14%). The specific periods for establishment are as follows: 87 were established between 2000 and 2019, and 167 were established between 2020 and 2024 (Figure 2A). The heads of liver cancer MDTs primarily came from hepatobiliary surgery (154 hospitals, 59.46%) and medical oncology (60 hospitals, 23.17%) (Figure 2B). The participating departments primarily come from disciplines related to clinical diagnosis and treatment of liver cancer (Figure 2C), with the top three being: hepatobiliary surgery (21.75%), medical oncology (15.36%), and interventional radiology (13.58%). The team size was concentrated in the 6–10 person range, accounting for 64.09% (Figure 2D). The majority of team members held senior professional titles, with chief physicians and associate chief physicians accounting for 37.45% and 36.29%, respectively (Figure 2E). The number of hospitals with MDT participating departments ≤5, 6–10, and ≥11 was 120, 134, and 5, respectively (Figure 2F).

MDT Formation and Operating Models

The formation types of liver cancer MDT teams primarily fall into four categories (Figure 3A): department-led (169 hospitals, 65.25%), business-driven (48 hospitals, 18.53%), administratively led (35 hospitals, 13.51%), and freely formed (7 hospitals, 2.70%). 61.39% of hospitals have established fixed MDT processes, while 37.07% use non-fixed processes. MDT meetings are primarily conducted in person (95.24%); the most common frequency is once a week (41.31%), followed by irregular meetings (34.75%) (Figure 3B). The duration of meetings was most commonly 30 minutes to 1 hour (50.58%), followed by 1 to 2 hours (31.27%) (Figure 3C). Regarding the selection criteria for cases discussed in MDTs, the primary type was self-selected (47.45%), followed by severity of disease (27.45%), and then treatment needs (25.10%). In the liver cancer MDT process, 37.84% of hospitals have fixed collaborating referral hospitals, among which: 89.80% of hospitals (88 hospitals) have 1–5 collaborating referral units, and 89.80% of hospitals have established and adopted standardized referral processes. Among the 259 hospitals with fixed liver-cancer MDT teams, cross-regional comparisons of team structure and routine operational metrics are presented in Supplementary Table 4. Most operational features showed no statistically significant differences among eastern, central and western regions.

Clinical Practice of MDT and Its Outcomes

Scale of MDT Discussions and Treatment Plan Development

Based on survey data, there is a significant stratification in the annual number of cases discussed in MDT across different hospitals (Figure 4A). Specifically, 41.70% of hospitals discuss ≤50 cases annually, 26.64% discuss 51–100 cases, 14.29% discuss 101–200 cases, and only 16.99% of hospitals discuss over 200 cases annually. In terms of the degree of personalization in treatment plans, the data show that 47.88% of hospitals only conduct personalized plan discussions for 26–50% of cases, while only 27.03% of medical institutions can implement in-depth personalized discussions for over 50% of cases (Figure 4B). Notably, 90.73% of medical institutions reported that MDT has a significant impact on their clinical decision-making, while only 9.27% of institutions believe the impact is limited (Figure 4C).

Clinical practice and effectiveness of MDT discussions. (A) Number of cases discussed per year. (B) Proportion of MDT discussions on personalized treatment plans for patients. (C) The impact of MDT on clinical decision-making.

Methods for Tracking and Evaluating the Efficacy of MDT Treatment

Survey results indicate that there are significant differences in the methods used to evaluate treatment efficacy after MDT. 83.78% of hospitals use regular imaging and laboratory follow-ups as their core evaluation methods, while only 5.02% use patient quality of life scales as their primary evaluation criteria. The timing of efficacy evaluations tends to be centralized, with 68.34% of hospitals initiating the evaluation process only when clinically necessary. When asked for their subjective clinical opinion on survival benefits of MDT, 83.01% of respondents perceived significant survival improvement for liver cancer patients following MDT discussions, 12.36% perceived no change in survival outcomes, and only 0.77% perceived survival deterioration.

MDT Quality Control Mechanism Construction

Among medical institutions that have established MDT teams, 79.92% have established a systematic quality control system. The distribution of responsible entities exhibits a diversified pattern (Figure 5A): 42.36% are led by the medical affairs department, 39.90% are managed by the MDT-leading department, and only 2.96% have established an independent quality assessment group. Regarding quality control measures, 80.10% of institutions prioritize the development of standard operating procedures (Figure 5B), and 91.30% acknowledge the effectiveness of current quality control measures (Figure 5C). Treatment plan implementation modes can be categorized into three types: 42.47% strictly adhere to the MDT plan, 45.56% make moderate adjustments (the mainstream mode), and 11.97% adopt flexible adjustment strategies (Figure 5D).

MDT Operational Efficiency and Patient Perception

Operational efficiency assessments revealed that 88.41% of institutions affirmed the efficiency of MDT work, with 37.06% rating it as “very efficient” and 51.35% as “fairly efficient” (Figure 6A). 83.01% of respondents perceived improvement for liver cancer patients following MDT discussions (Figure 6B). 62.93% of institutions conducted patient satisfaction surveys, with a satisfaction rate of 98.16% (including “very satisfied” and “satisfied”), and only 1.84% rated it as “general” (Figure 6C). Regarding patient acceptance, 81.60% of institutions reported that patients had a good understanding of the MDT model (Figure 6D).

Operational efficiency and patient perception of MDT. (A) MDT operational efficiency awareness. (B) Perceived survival benefit after MDT. (C) MDT patient satisfaction survey. (D) MDT awareness and acceptance.

MDT Practice Experience and Research Conversion

The summary rate of successful cases treated by MDT was 55.98%, while 44.02% of institutions lacked systematic summarization. Analysis of failed cases was significantly insufficient, with only 37.07% conducting attribution studies and 62.93% failing to establish experience-sharing mechanisms. The research conversion rate is low, with 66.80% of institutions failing to publish related research findings, and only 33.20% achieving the conversion of clinical experience into research outputs.

MDT Information Technology Development Needs

A five-level assessment system was used to survey and evaluate the level of MDT information technology development. The results show that only 35.91% of institutions have achieved the level of deep data integration (Level 1), while 63.71% have system compatibility defects or lagging development (Figure 7A). Technical needs exhibit a layered structure: data integration layer (interdepartmental sharing system) needs are the most urgent, system functionality layer (dedicated platform development) accounts for 20.32%, and the technical capability layer (imaging digitization), personnel training layer, and security protection layer constitute secondary needs. Notably, 81.92% of technical needs are concentrated in the areas of data integration and system functionality optimization (Figure 7B). Supplementary Table 5 summarizes inter-regional differences in quality-control setup, subjective performance evaluation, and information-technology capacity of fixed MDT teams. Significant regional disparities were observed for informatization construction (P = 0.002). Eastern hospitals had higher rates of fully integrated MDT data platforms, while western hospitals more frequently planned future informatization construction.

Challenges Faced by MDT and Optimization Pathways

The survey found that the main obstacles to the implementation of MDT for liver cancer currently include (Figure 8A): insufficient policy support (41.18%), difficulties in personnel allocation (19.92%), and difficulties in time coordination (13.82%). The priority optimization pathways are (Figure 8B): strengthening collaborative mechanisms (55.51%), enhancing professional capabilities (16.04%), upgrading resources (12.83%), and enabling technological advancements (10.59%). The key elements for establishing an ideal liver cancer MDT in the future are primarily as follows (Figure 8C): comprehensive participation of multidisciplinary experts (64.36%), improved communication and collaboration mechanisms (12.98%), patient-centered decision-making processes (9.36%), and regular discussion systems (5.85%).

Discussion

This study, based on a questionnaire survey of 1,073 hospitals nationwide, provides the first systematic overview of the macro-level development of MDT models for liver cancer treatment in China. The data indicate that MDT, as an important model for enhancing the scientific rigor of treatment decisions,19,20 is undergoing a critical transition in China from exploratory practice to standardization and institutionalization. Although the current implementation rate is only 51.35%, its clinical value has gained widespread recognition. The research21 indicates that MDT implementation can significantly improve the detection rate of early-stage liver cancer and optimize treatment decisions. This finding is highly consistent with the goal of early diagnosis and treatment of tumors outlined in China’s “Healthy China 2030” strategy. By breaking down disciplinary barriers, the MDT system not only enhances the collaborative diagnostic and treatment capabilities of various specialties but also promotes the standardization of clinical practice and the implementation of treatment guidelines. Findings from previous clinical-based studies have suggested potential clinical benefits of MDT, such as improved treatment adherence and higher patient satisfaction.22,23 This evidence supports the rationale for national-level MDT promotion strategies in China.8–11

As the most comprehensive and largest-scale study on the current status of MDT practices for liver cancer in China, this questionnaire survey covered medical institutions across all 31 provincial-level administrative regions in the country, with tertiary hospitals accounting for 84.26% of the total, accurately reflecting the distribution characteristics of high-quality medical resources in China. Notably, MDT practices exhibit a distinct regional imbalance, with a “west-central-east” distribution pattern that aligns closely with the gradient differences in China’s medical resource distribution.24 Time-series analysis identifies two key turning points: only 33.59% of liver cancer MDTs were established between 2000 and 2019, while a construction peak emerged between 2022 and 2023, reflecting the proactive guiding role of policy initiatives. The disciplinary composition of MDTs is dominated by hepatobiliary surgery, medical oncology, and interventional radiology, precisely aligning with the treatment pathways for liver cancer, which primarily involve surgical resection, transarterial chemoembolization, and systemic therapy.25 This professional configuration model is worthy of reference in future promotions.

An analysis of operational mechanisms revealed distinctive features of MDT practices in China. The department-led organizational structure (65.25%) aligns with China’s healthcare system, which operates on a department-based management unit model. Meanwhile, the meeting frequency of once a week (41.31%) and the discussion format primarily consisting of in-person meetings (95.24%) align with the “high-frequency face-to-face discussion” strategy advocated by some international research institutions.13 Notably, case selection criteria exhibit a trend toward specialization: 73.26% of institutions have established decision-making mechanisms based on disease severity or team consensus, but 38.61% of institutions lack standardized processes. This phenomenon suggests that standardized construction should be prioritized during the promotion of MDT.

According to clinicians’ self-reports, the MDT model improves the proportion of personalized treatment plans and enhances the rigor of clinical decision‑making. A total of 90.73% of hospitals reported that MDT exerted substantial influence on clinical decisions, reflecting real-world clinical perceptions of MDT value. Unlike prior clinical studies focused on patient-level prognostic outcomes,5–7,26 this institutional cross-sectional survey did not collect individual-patient outcome data. Nevertheless, our findings suggest that MDT plays a prominent decision-making role in routine liver-cancer care.

Although the annual discussion volume shows a polarized distribution, with most hospitals discussing fewer than 100 cases, 16.99% of hospitals discussed over 200 cases, indicating that in some hospitals, MDT has become an important component of routine liver cancer diagnosis and treatment. The efficacy assessment system still primarily relies on traditional imaging and laboratory tests, with a patient-reported outcome utilization rate of only 5.02%. This disparity highlights the insufficient implementation of the biopsychosocial medical model in MDT practice in China.

Quality control systems are a key tool for supervising and ensuring the standardized implementation of MDT. The departments responsible for quality control exhibit a “multifunctional” characteristic, with the vast majority relying on the hospital’s medical affairs office or MDT-related departments to handle the task concurrently. Only 2.96% of hospitals have established independent quality control departments to handle this responsibility. This finding suggests that future liver cancer MDT efforts should prioritize the establishment of dedicated quality monitoring departments and systems. More concerning is the significantly low research conversion rate (33.20%) and failure case sharing rate (37.07%), indicating that current MDT practices have not yet established an effective knowledge management loop. This shortcoming may hinder the continuous optimization of the MDT model.

The level of information technology is a key factor influencing the efficiency of MDTs. In this study, only 35.91% of hospitals had achieved integrated platform construction, while the remaining hospitals still faced certain technical infrastructure development challenges. The technical requirements for liver cancer MDTs primarily focus on data integration and system functionality, with information silos remaining a key obstacle to MDT collaboration.27 Insufficient policy support and staffing shortages are the major bottlenecks hindering the efficient operation of MDTs, indicating that the current promotion of MDTs relies more on spontaneous organization by disciplines and lacks systematic support from the hospital’s top-level design. Time coordination and cross-departmental communication also pose practical challenges, suggesting the need to establish a unified coordination mechanism. Additionally, low patient participation and unclear fee standards reflect the urgent need for integration and alignment between institutional and payment systems.28 To optimize the process, strengthening collaboration mechanisms is the top priority, followed by enhancing professional capabilities and ensuring resource availability, particularly meeting facilities and staffing. Patient involvement in quality tracking and inter-hospital collaboration are rarely mentioned, but as long-term development goals, they still require attention.29 These findings provide important entry points for improving MDT institutional design.

This study has several limitations. First, as a cross-sectional survey, this study mainly adopted descriptive statistics to illustrate the overall status of liver-cancer MDT practice in China, without constructing inferential models such as regression analyses. Second, all indicators were based on subjective reporting without objective clinical endpoints. Patient survival data were not collected, so the perceived prognostic benefits of MDT lack objective validation. Third, exploratory subgroup analyses stratified by hospital level and geographic region were restricted to hospitals with complete covariate data. Fourth, given the limitations of self-reported surveys, the observed recognition rate for quality-control effectiveness may be subject to reporting bias and subjective overestimation. Future prospective studies are needed to further validate the present findings and evaluate the real-world effectiveness of liver-cancer MDT.

In summary, this study shows that MDTs for liver cancer have been preliminarily established in hospitals in China, but they still face challenges such as significant regional disparities, hospital-level gradient, weak quality control, and insufficient information technology. Based on these findings, we propose the following “three-dimensional improvement framework” initiative for the development of liver cancer MDTs in China: in terms of breadth, promote the extension of MDTs to primary care institutions through a tiered diagnosis and treatment policy; in terms of depth, establish a quality improvement system that includes independent quality control, knowledge management, and patient participation; in terms of height, promote the integration and innovation of MDTs with cutting-edge technologies such as precision medicine and artificial intelligence.
