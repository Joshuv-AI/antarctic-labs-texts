Variance-based variable selection in sensor calibration with strong interferents - application to air pollution monitoring with a carbon nanotube sensor array
Marine Dumon, Bérengère Lebental, Guillaume Perrin

Abstract
Air and water pollution are major threats to public health, highlighting the need for reliable environmental monitoring. Low-cost multisensor systems are promising but suffer from limited selectivity, because their responses are influenced by non-target variables (interferents) such as temperature and humidity. This complicates pollutant detection, especially in data-driven models with noisy, correlated inputs. We propose a method for selecting the most relevant interferents for sensor calibration, balancing performance and cost. Including too many variables can lead to overfitting, while omitting key variables reduces accuracy. Our approach evaluates numerous models using a bias-variance trade-off and variance analysis. The method is first validated on simulated data to assess strengths and limitations, then applied to a carbon nanotube-based sensor array deployed outdoors to characterize its sensitivity to air pollutants.

Introduction

Air and water pollution are major global public health issues
[
23
,
14
]
. Accurate detection of hazardous pollutants requires compact, reliable, low-cost, and high-precision sensors. Sensors using innovative materials (e.g., nanomaterials, polymers, ceramics) are promising due to their high sensitivity to chemical environments
[
18
]
. However, this sensitivity also makes them prone to interference from environmental parameters. The large number of potential interferents complicates calibration, as experimental designs grow exponentially with each additional variable. This motivates in-situ calibration using real-world data for sensor adjustment
[
4
,
20
]
, which exposes sensors to multiple relevant factors simultaneously. Yet, ambient variables are often highly correlated (e.g., pollutant levels vary with temperature and humidity), leading to distorted calibration laws. This limits transferability across environments and hinders interpretation. Identifying causal variables is key: omitting them leads to unexplained variance, while including too many increases cost, noise, and the risk of overfitting.

Conventional methods for variable selection face major challenges when applied to sensor calibration in open environments. Sensitivity analysis techniques, such as Sobol indices
[
26
]
, Shapley effects
[
2
]
, or HSIC-based methods
[
27
]
, are sensitive to noise, which distorts effect estimates and leads to unreliable conclusions. Strong correlations between inputs further complicate interpretation, as non-influential variables may appear important due to their association with influential variables
[
12
]
.
Regression-based methods using statistical tests
[
13
]
, penalized techniques like Lasso
[
19
,
9
]
or Ridge
[
16
]
, or stepwise procedures
[
21
]
assume that coefficients reflect true influence. However, this fails if unmeasured causal variables are correlated with observed ones, introducing bias.
Cross-validation, often used to assess variable relevance through predictive performance, may favor error compensation in correlated settings, masking true influences. And causal approaches (e.g., PC
[
10
]
, FCI
[
8
]
), while allowing for prior knowledge integration, remain in practice unstable in noisy, correlated, or partially observed data, that is to say the typical conditions of open-environment calibration.

Building on prior work and its limitations, this work introduces a method for selecting only those environmental variables that are truly necessary for accurate sensor prediction, excluding non-causal or weakly influential variables. Designed to be robust and computationally efficient, the method addresses realistic challenges: highly correlated inputs, unmeasured causal factors, limited calibration data (small data), and significant noise.
The approach involves (1) generating a large set of calibration models using varying combinations of input variables, and (2) applying a probabilistic post-processing step to select models that minimize prediction variance under a parsimony constraint. Beyond identifying an optimal model, the method adopts a white-box perspective, offering indicators to assess variable sensitivities and enhance interpretability.

The paper is organized as follows: Section
2
defines the problem and framework; Section
3
details the method; and Section
4
evaluates its performance on both simulated and experimental data.

2
General framework

We are interested in the deployment of
d
innovative sensors in an open environment, each of which outputs a vector with
q
components. The proposed method treats each component of the vector independently, so it is equivalent whether
d\times q
sensors with an output of dimension
1
are considered or
d
sensors with an output of dimension
q
. Thus, to simplify notations, we consider a deployment of
{d_{y}}
sensors with a single real output, and we gather the combined output vector in
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d_{y}})
. We then note
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
as the vector gathering the concentrations of the
{d_{x}}
target pollutants for which the sensor was designed. Whether by design or not, the sensors may react to other environmental variables (e.g., temperature, relative humidity, other pollutants) in addition to the target chemicals. These interfering variables are divided into two categories: we denote by
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}}
the measured environmental variables (e.g., those measured by instruments separate from the sensors being calibrated), and by
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
the environmental variables that are not measured (either because it is not technically possible or simply because the sensitivity of the sensors to them was not been anticipated). Since the goal of this work is to select influential variables, the formalism allows for the possibility that the sensor outputs are in fact sensitive to only a subset of the components of
\boldsymbol{z}
. This leads to the general model formulation:

\boldsymbol{y}=\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{u}),

(1)

where
\mathcal{M}
is an unknown function, hereafter called the
calibration
function. We assume that the quantities
\boldsymbol{x}
,
\boldsymbol{z}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
(but not
\boldsymbol{u}
) were measured at
n
different times
i
. These measured values, which are assumed to be affected by noise and therefore slightly different from the true values, are noted
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\text{mes}}
,
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}^{\text{mes}}
and
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{\text{mes}}
respectively, and gathered in
\mathcal{D}_{n}=\{\boldsymbol{x}^{\text{mes}}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}^{\text{mes}}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}^{\text{mes}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

Sensor deployment involves two stages: calibration, where an approximation of
\mathcal{M}
is built from
\mathcal{D}_{n}
, and prediction, where pollutant concentrations are estimated from sensor outputs and environmental variables.
Calibration in open environments presents several challenges. First,
\mathcal{M}
is typically non-linear with complex dependencies between
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{z}
, requiring flexible models. Second, measurement noise increases the risk of overfitting, requiring robust statistical methods. Third, irrelevant but correlated variables may bias the model. Fourth, unmeasured variables may also affect the sensor response. Finally, calibration typically occurs under a small data regime, limiting the use of high-capacity models such as neural networks.
Addressing these challenges - open-environment data, non-linearity, noise, unobserved inputs, small datasets, and variable interdependence - is central to this work. Subsections
3.1
through
3.3
describe our approach to select the most influential components of
\boldsymbol{z}
, denoted
\boldsymbol{z}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
.
In the prediction phase, the pollutant concentrations at time
t
are estimated using the obtained approximation of
\mathcal{M}
and the noisy observations of
\boldsymbol{z}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
. As discussed in
[
5
]
, this step remains difficult under the constraints listed above (noise, small data, imperfect models). Our adapted solution is described in Section
3.4
.

Notations

For any time
\star
not included in the calibration data set
\mathcal{D}_{n}
(which, as a reminder, is indexed by
i
), we define
\boldsymbol{z}^{\text{mes}}_{\star}
and
\boldsymbol{y}^{\text{mes}}_{\star}
as the measured values of
\boldsymbol{z}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
. Since
\boldsymbol{x}_{\star}
is not observed, the role of the sensor is to predict it. We also define the random vectors
(\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{mes}},\boldsymbol{Z}^{\text{mes}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
to represent possible values of
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
at any time
\star
. These vectors incorporate two sources of variability: measurement uncertainty at a given time, and natural fluctuations over time. They play a key role in the variable selection process described in Section
3.2
.

3
Proposed formalism for the calibration and prediction phases

3.1
Model definition

An approximate calibration model is built independently for each component of
\boldsymbol{y}
. For ease of reading, we therefore introduce in Sections
3.1
to
3.3
the variable
v\in\mathbb{R}
to denote a specific component of
\boldsymbol{y}
:
v_{i}
is the true value of
v
at time
i
, and
v^{\text{mes}}_{i}
is its measured value.
We propose to use the formalism of the generalized linear regression (GLR) to construct an approximation of the calibration model. This choice is motivated by the relative robustness of GLR to noise, its ease of interpretation, and the fact that it can be applied in the small data context. More specifically, we propose to approximate the link between
v^{\text{mes}}_{i}
,
\boldsymbol{x}^{\text{mes}}_{i}
, and
\boldsymbol{z}^{\text{mes}}_{i}
in the following form

{v}^{\text{mes}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\text{mes}}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}^{\text{mes}}_{i})^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}+{\varepsilon}^{\text{mod}}_{i},

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is a
k
-dimensional vector to be estimated,

\boldsymbol{f}
is a selected vector that includes
k
different polynomial functions (with cross-terms) of
\boldsymbol{x}^{\text{mes}}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{z}^{\text{mes}}_{i}
of total order less than or equal to
p
,
and

\varepsilon^{\text{mod}}_{i}
is a model error term (to be estimated) to account for the error in approximating the calibration model, the measurement error in
v_{i}
, and the potential contributions of the unmeasured variables
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
.
The choice of these
k
functions is one of the contribution of this work, and will be at the heart of the discussions in Section
3.2
.

Given
\boldsymbol{v}^{\text{mes}}=({v}^{\text{mes}}_{1},\ldots,{v}^{\text{mes}}_{n})
the vector formed by all the measurements of
{v}
, and using a least-squares approach
[
1
]
, the vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}
can be estimated by:

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\left({\boldsymbol{H}^{\text{mes}}}^{T}\boldsymbol{H}^{\text{mes}}\right)^{-1}{\boldsymbol{H}^{\text{mes}}}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{\text{mes}},

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{H}^{\text{mes}}
is a
(n\times k)
-dimensional matrix such that for each
1\leq i\leq n
and each
1\leq j\leq k
,

\left(\boldsymbol{H}^{\text{mes}}\right)_{ij}=f_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\text{mes}}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}^{\text{mes}}_{i}).

(4)

It is important to note that Eq. (
2
) links the values of
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y}
that are in
\mathcal{D}_{n}
, but that such an expression only makes sense if it is also valid for linking
\boldsymbol{x}_{\star},\boldsymbol{z}_{\star},\boldsymbol{y}_{\star}
, i.e., for linking the values of
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y}
at times not contained in
\mathcal{D}_{n}
. In this case, the prediction of the measured value of
v
at any time
\star
can be written

\widehat{{v}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\text{mes}}_{\star},\boldsymbol{z}^{\text{mes}}_{\star})=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\text{mes}}_{\star},\boldsymbol{z}^{\text{mes}}_{\star})^{T}\boldsymbol{\widehat{\beta}}+\widehat{\theta}\xi,\ \ \widehat{\theta}^{2}=\frac{1}{n-k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left({v}^{\text{mes}}_{i}-\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\text{mes}}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}^{\text{mes}}_{i})^{T}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right)^{2},

(5)

where
\xi
is a normalized model error term. As is common, a probabilistic approach is adopted, and the model error term
\xi
and the measurement errors are considered to be random. Since the estimators
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and
\widehat{\theta}
are functions of the measurements, they are also random, and we can assign them a mean and a variance. Let
\boldsymbol{m}_{\beta}
and
\boldsymbol{C}_{\beta}
be the mean and covariance matrix of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, and
\theta^{2}
be the expectation of
\widehat{\theta}^{2}
. Note that the quantities
\boldsymbol{m}_{\beta}
,
\boldsymbol{C}_{\beta}
and
\theta^{2}
are
a priori
unknown.
For reasons of identifiability, we then assume (1) that the measurement errors and the model errors are statistically independent of each other, so that
\xi
is independent of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and
\widehat{\theta}
, and (2) that the model error has variance
1
(the amplitude of the model error is then completely carried by
\widehat{\theta}
) and mean
0
(this last assumption is almost always true if a constant term is properly included in the components of
\boldsymbol{f}
).

3.2
Variable selection

This section presents the core of the proposed method, which aims to select the components of
\boldsymbol{z}
that most improve the accuracy of pollutant concentration estimates. The approach relies on the analysis of how the model prediction variance evolves when variables are included or excluded, allowing for a more precise identification of the truly influential inputs.
Rather than applying brute-force variance minimization, the method accounts for the fact that, in highly correlated settings, adding variables—even non-influential ones—may slightly reduce variance due to shared information. However, such gains are often marginal compared to the increase in model complexity. Thus, the goal is to strike a balance between minimizing variance and maintaining parsimony. This trade-off is handled by using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)
[
25
]
, which penalizes unnecessary complexity.

Let us formalize the process here: for any non-empty vector of indices
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\subset\left\{1,\ldots,d_{z}\right\}
,
\boldsymbol{z}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is the vector made up of the components
z_{\ell}
of
\boldsymbol{z}
such that
\ell\in\boldsymbol{\alpha}
. Now suppose we try to predict the value of
\boldsymbol{x}
at any time that is not part of the calibration set
\mathcal{D}_{n}
.
To do this, we will only have measurements of
\boldsymbol{y}
and
\boldsymbol{z}
.
The most likely value of
\boldsymbol{x}
from the sensor’s point of view will therefore be the value such that the prediction of
\boldsymbol{y}
knowing
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{z}
will be close to its available measurement. And the more accurate this predictor is, in the sense that the smaller its prediction variance, the more accurate we can expect the estimate of
\boldsymbol{x}
to be. To formalize this, we reuse the notations
\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{mes}}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}^{\text{mes}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
introduced at the end of Section
2
. Recall that
\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{mes}}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}^{\text{mes}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
are two random vectors whose fluctuations are supposed to characterize the measured values of
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
that the sensor is likely to encounter during its deployment. Following Eq. (
5
), the predictor of each component
v
of
\boldsymbol{y}
at
(\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{mes}},\boldsymbol{Z}^{\text{mes}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
can be written as

\widehat{{v}}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{mes}},\boldsymbol{Z}^{\text{mes}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{mes}},\boldsymbol{Z}^{\text{mes}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})^{T}\boldsymbol{\widehat{\beta}}+\widehat{\theta}\xi.

(6)

Note that
\boldsymbol{f}
,
\boldsymbol{\widehat{\beta}}
, and
\widehat{\theta}
implicitly depend on the choice of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, and could have been written with a subscript
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, which we chose not to do so as not to overload the mathematical expressions to come. From a mathematical point of view, finding the values of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and
\boldsymbol{f}
that lead to a minimum
variance
, once averaged over all the possible values of
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
, amounts to solving the following optimization problem:

\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\text{opt}}\in\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{mes}},\boldsymbol{Z}^{\text{mes}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\left[\text{Var}\left(\widehat{{v}}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{mes}},\boldsymbol{Z}^{\text{mes}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})|\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{mes}},\boldsymbol{Z}^{\text{mes}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right)\right],

(7)

where the notation
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{mes}},\boldsymbol{Z}^{\text{mes}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\left[\cdot\right]
is introduced to clearly indicate that the average is taken over the random variables
\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{mes}}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}^{\text{mes}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
. This expectation of conditional variance is noted
V(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{f})
in the following, and it rewrites as (see
A
for details)

V(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{f})=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{mes}},\boldsymbol{Z}^{\text{mes}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\left[\text{Var}\left(\widehat{{v}}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{mes}},\boldsymbol{Z}^{\text{mes}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})|\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{mes}},\boldsymbol{Z}^{\text{mes}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right)\right]=\boldsymbol{m}_{f}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{\beta}\boldsymbol{m}_{f}+\theta^{2}+\text{Tr}\left(\boldsymbol{C}_{f}\boldsymbol{C}_{\beta}\right),

(8)

where
\boldsymbol{m}_{f}=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{mes}},\boldsymbol{Z}^{\text{mes}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\left[\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{mes}},\boldsymbol{Z}^{\text{mes}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\right]
,
\boldsymbol{C}_{f}=\text{Cov}_{\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{mes}},\boldsymbol{Z}^{\text{mes}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\left(\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{mes}},\boldsymbol{Z}^{\text{mes}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\right)
,
\theta^{2}=\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\theta}^{2}]
, and
\boldsymbol{C}_{\beta}=\text{Cov}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
. The three terms in this expression can be interpreted as follows: (1)
\boldsymbol{m}_{f}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{\beta}\boldsymbol{m}_{f}
is linked to the uncertainty in estimating
\boldsymbol{\beta}
due to the finite and noisy nature of the training data set, and is expected to tend to
0
as the size of the data set
n
tends to infinity; (2)
\theta^{2}
characterizes the model error and is expected to decrease with increasing model complexity; (3)
\text{Tr}\left(\boldsymbol{C}_{f}\boldsymbol{C}_{\beta}\right)
is associated with the potential lack of robustness of an overly complex model, and unlike
\theta^{2}
, is expected to increase with increasing model complexity.

The terms
\boldsymbol{m}_{f}
,
\boldsymbol{C}_{\beta}
,
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\theta}^{2}]
, and
\boldsymbol{C}_{f}
are unknown, since they depend through
\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{mes}}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}^{\text{mes}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
on unknown fluctuations of
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{z}
. Thus, we propose to approximate them based solely on the calibration data set
\mathcal{D}_{n}
. First, we estimate
\boldsymbol{m}_{f}
and
\boldsymbol{C}_{f}
using a classical Monte Carlo approach, so that:

\hskip-28.45274pt\boldsymbol{m}_{f}\approx\widehat{\boldsymbol{m}}_{f}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{f}_{i},\ \ \boldsymbol{C}_{f}\approx\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\boldsymbol{f}_{i}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{m}}_{f}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{f}_{i}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{m}}_{f}\right)^{T},\ \ \boldsymbol{f}_{i}=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\text{mes}}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}^{\text{mes}}_{i,\boldsymbol{\alpha}}).

(9)

Estimating
\boldsymbol{C}_{\beta}
and
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\theta}^{2}]
is less straightforward, since the estimators
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and
\widehat{\theta}^{2}
are functions of the complete set of points in
\mathcal{D}_{n}
, and several sets of
n
points with the same properties as
\mathcal{D}_{n}
would be needed to compute their statistics. To get around this difficulty, we use a bootstrap approach

[
7
]
: we select
n
points in
\mathcal{D}_{n}
independently and with replacement (e.g., a single point can be retained several times), then we calculate the associated values of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and
\widehat{\theta}
. We repeat this procedure a large number of times, from which we derive empirical approximations of
\boldsymbol{C}_{\beta}
and
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\theta}^{2}]
, again using the classical Monte Carlo approach.

In this search for a compromise between complexity and variance, it is also important to note that the choice of
\boldsymbol{f}
is central, since the total polynomial order of the model and the number of cross terms contribute much more to the complexity of the model than the number of variables. Therefore, the optimal value for
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
should actually be sought as the solution of

\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\min_{\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathcal{P}(p,\boldsymbol{\alpha})}V(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{f}),

(10)

with
\mathcal{P}(p,\boldsymbol{\alpha})
the set of vector-valued functions whose components are polynomial functions with total degree less than
p
. This minimization with respect to both
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and
\boldsymbol{f}
introduces a new difficulty. In fact, the minimization with respect to
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
only can be done in a purely combinatorial manner, e.g., brute-force testing all the
2^{d_{z}}
combinations of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
to select the best
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, since the number of measured environmental variables is usually limited (typically
d_{z}\leq 10
). Conversely, even reasonable polynomial orders (in the applications below,
p
is chosen equal to 3) lead to an unreasonable number of models to be tested in a combinatorial approach (this number would be equal to
2^{\binom{d_{x}+d_{z}+p}{p}}
, which would give a number of combinations of the order of
10^{17}
for
d_{x}=2
,
d_{z}=3
and
p=3
).

As an alternative, a
greedy
approach is proposed here. For each value of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, we first choose a value for
p
. This allows us to define
\boldsymbol{f}^{\text{full}}
as the particular element of
\mathcal{P}(p,\boldsymbol{\alpha})
that contains all the polynomial functions of total order less than
p
that are a function of
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
. By construction,
\boldsymbol{f}^{\text{full}}
is a vector gathering
\binom{d_{x}+\sharp\boldsymbol{\alpha}+p}{p}
polynomial functions, where
\sharp\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is the number of elements of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
. To make the values of the projection coefficients comparable, except for the term corresponding to the constant function equal to
1
, we then normalize the components of
\boldsymbol{f}^{\text{full}}
by removing their empirical means and dividing by their empirical standard deviations, both computed from
\mathcal{D}_{n}
. Of course, other types of normalization could have been considered. We then evaluate the prediction variance for
\boldsymbol{f}^{\text{full}}
and for a series of functions
\boldsymbol{f}^{\text{partial}}
derived from
\boldsymbol{f}^{\text{full}}
by removing each of its components one by one, starting with the term associated with the lowest
\boldsymbol{\beta}
coefficients. By repeating this procedure for each value of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, we obtain a large number of forms for
\boldsymbol{f}
, and we finally concentrate on the pair
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{f})
that minimizes
V(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{f})
.

Nevertheless, such a procedure often leads to the selection of the
complete model
, i.e., the model that includes all the environmental variables and all the polynomial terms (for a given polynomial order), which is neither optimal nor satisfactory. This issue is solved by penalizing complex models: instead of directly minimizing
V(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{f})
, one minimizes its BIC version according to the formula

\text{BIC}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{f})=n\times\log(V(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{f}))+k(\boldsymbol{f})\times\log(n),

(11)

where
k(\boldsymbol{f})
is the number of polynomial functions that are in
\boldsymbol{f}
, and
n
is the number of points in
\mathcal{D}_{n}
.

In summary, it is proposed to choose the optimal value of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
as the one that minimizes the BIC criterion when testing a large number of polynomial models (using a greedy removal procedure) for the
2^{d_{z}}
different configurations of the components of
\boldsymbol{z}
. It is worth noting that other criteria besides the BIC have been tested to automatically achieve the trade-off between model variance and complexity, e.g. by comparing the variance with the measurement noise of the variables, but the BIC proved to be the most reliable in our numerical tests.

Remark

During variable selection,
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{z}
are treated symmetrically, so the method can be applied to both
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{z}
. The only constraint is the total number of variables, which must remain tractable for combinatorial exploration.
This point is practically relevant because sensor sensitivities often change significantly between laboratory and field conditions, so the actual targets and the actual interferents may not be known exactly. Consequently, an initial
agnostic
interpretation phase is required, i.e., all variables are initially assumed to be interferents. The proposed variable selection protocol is then used to identify which variables truly define the sensor’s targets. This agnostic approach is demonstrated in Section
4
using data from a novel carbon nanotube sensor array.

3.3
Post-processing of the different tested models

The method in Section
3.2
primarily returns the optimal subset
\boldsymbol{z}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
and its associated model
\boldsymbol{f}
. In addition, for any number
\ell
of selected variables (from 1 to
d_{z}
), it also returns the best performing combination of variables and the corresponding variance. Plotting
\ell
against this variance forms a Pareto front
[
17
]
, highlighting optimal tradeoffs between prediction accuracy and model simplicity.
For each
\ell
, the index vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\text{opt}}_{\ell}
identifies the best subset of components of
\boldsymbol{z}
that minimizes the BIC. The evolution of the selected variables as
\ell
increases provides insight into their relative influence. For example, the most influential variable is expected to appear first, followed by the next most influential, and so on. This visualization, further illustrated in Section
4
Figure
1
) is a powerful tool for interpreting sensor sensitivities.

This simple ranking between variables is helpful to interpret the sensor sensitivities qualitatively, but not quantitatively. Moreover, it fails to interpret variable exchanges between steps (e.g., the variables of step
\ell+1
do not include all the variables of step
\ell
), a situation that has sometimes been observed in experimental data. To provide a more quantified and reliable interpretation of variable influence, a method of variance decomposition between variables (including the components of
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{z}
and the model error) is now introduced. Classical Sobol indices
[
26
]
would be the natural tool here, but they rely on an assumption of statistical independence between inputs that does not hold in our context. When dependencies exist, as is the case between
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{z}
, the Shapley indices
[
2
]
, and more recently the indices based on Proportional Marginal Effects (PME)
[
11
]
provide suitable alternatives. In this paper, we have chosen to use the PME indices (see
C
for their mathematical definition) because of the following properties:

1.

Positivity and Interpretability: PME values are non-negative and add up to the total model variance.

2.

Causal Relevance: A variable with no causal effect will have a PME of zero, even if it is statistically correlated with the output.

3.

Influence Hierarchy: A high PME value corresponds to a strong influence on the model output, in the sense that the proportion of variance attributed to it is high.

These features make PME indices particularly suitable for our purposes. In practice, calculating these indices requires the availability of a model linking sensor inputs and outputs, which is precisely what the method developed here aims to build. Therefore, the PME indices are computed after model construction to improve interpretability and to reliably assess the influence of variables.

Remark

As we are considering noisy calibration data, the overall method can be further improved by again using a bootstrap approach. This consists in repeating the entire procedure
m
times on
m
different data sets, all constructed by selecting
n
points of
\mathcal{D}_{n}
independently and with replacement. It is then possible to compute statistics on the results obtained, for the PMEs indices, for the Pareto front, and for the optimal vector
\boldsymbol{z}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
.

Remark

An additional post-processing of the optimal model is proposed in Section
D
, aiming to compute the
resolution
of the sensor to its targets
\boldsymbol{x}
and interferents
\boldsymbol{z}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
.

3.4
Estimation of the pollutant concentration

In addition to its ability to properly identify influential variables, the proposed method should be evaluated for its ability to provide a reliable estimate of
\boldsymbol{x}_{\star}
when using the optimal model for each sensor output. By specifying the subscripts
m\in\left\{1,\ldots,d_{y}\right\}
for each quantity associated with each sensor output, the link between sensor inputs and outputs presented in Eqs. (
2
) and (
6
) can now be written in vector form as:

\boldsymbol{y}^{\text{mes}}_{\star}=\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\text{mes}}_{\star},\boldsymbol{z}^{\text{mes}}_{\star})\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\text{tot}}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\boldsymbol{\xi},

(12)

with
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\text{tot}}=(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{d_{y}})
,
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{d_{y}})
,
\boldsymbol{F}=\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{f}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{f}_{d_{y}})
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}=\text{diag}(\widehat{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\theta}_{d_{y}})
, where
\text{diag}(\cdot)
is the operator that transforms a set of vectors or matrices into a block diagonal matrix where each diagonal block corresponds to the elements of the considered set of vectors or matrices.
It is important to remember here that the optimal polynomial forms and the optimal vectors
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{m}^{\text{opt}}
are derived independently for each sensor output
m
, and thus are expected to be different from each other. To be completely accurate, the formula should thus contain a reference to
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{m}^{\text{opt}}
. For the sake of readability, however, we simplify the notation again and refer only to
\boldsymbol{z}^{\text{mes}}_{\star}
in this section, leaving implicit the fact that only certain components of this vector are actually retained.

From a Bayesian point of view, consistent with the probabilistic formalism introduced earlier, we model
\boldsymbol{x}_{\star}
as a random vector and search for the value that maximizes its posterior probability density function (PDF). Following the work achieved in
[
5
]
, this posterior PDF can be approximated in closed form as (see Section
B
of Section
D
for the expressions of
\boldsymbol{m}
and
\boldsymbol{C}
)

\widehat{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{\star}|\boldsymbol{z}^{\text{mes}}_{\star},\boldsymbol{y}^{\text{mes}}_{\star}}(\boldsymbol{x})=c\times\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{\text{mes}}_{\star}-\boldsymbol{m}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)^{T}\boldsymbol{C}(\boldsymbol{x})^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{\text{mes}}_{\star}-\boldsymbol{m}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)\right)}{\text{det}\left(\boldsymbol{C}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)^{1/2}}\times f_{\boldsymbol{x}_{\star}|\boldsymbol{z}^{\text{mes}}_{\star}}(\boldsymbol{x}),

(13)

where
c
is a constant and
f_{\boldsymbol{x}_{\star}|\boldsymbol{z}^{\text{mes}}_{\star}}
is the PDF of
\boldsymbol{x}_{\star}
given
\boldsymbol{z}^{\text{mes}}_{\star}
, whose role is to ensure the best possible transferability of calibration results: When the sensor is used in the calibration environment, it can be approximated by
f_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\text{mes}}_{\star}|\boldsymbol{z}^{\text{mes}}_{\star}}
using the points in
\mathcal{D}_{n}
and relying on any statistical inference technique. In Section
4
, we will focus on the Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) for such constructions
[
24
,
5
]
.
Conversely, if the sensor is to be used in a different environment, this should be elucidated separately for that new environment (see
[
15
]
for more details on expert elicitation). Finally, the value of
\boldsymbol{x}
that maximizes
\widehat{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{\star}|\boldsymbol{z}^{\text{mes}}_{\star},\boldsymbol{y}^{\text{mes}}_{\star}}(\boldsymbol{x})
defines an estimator of
\boldsymbol{x}_{\star}
, and confidence intervals can be proposed for each component of
\boldsymbol{x}_{\star}
by extracting quantiles from this approximated posterior PDF.

4
Application

The proposed method is evaluated on both simulated and experimental data sets. Simulated data, detailed in Section
4.1
, provide a controlled setting to test the method’s ability to identify influential variables and to assess its robustness in complex scenarios. Then, in Section
4.2
, the method is applied to real outdoor measurements. In this second case, the influential variables are unknown, and the data are affected by uncontrolled variations.

In the rest, the expression
simple model
denotes the model that includes all the components of
\boldsymbol{x}
as inputs and no component of
\boldsymbol{z}
. The term
selected model
corresponds to the model selected by the proposed method, while the
complete model
includes all the components of
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{z}
(even if they are not influential).

4.1
Application on the simulated data set

The synthetic data set includes the main features generally observed in experimental data sets for multisensor systems deployed in an open periodic environment
[
20
,
6
]
. In this section, only one sensor is considered (
d_{y}=1
), and we focus on the case
d_{x}=1
,
d_{z}=5
and
d_{u}=1
. The variables
x
,
\boldsymbol{z}
and
u
are simulated as centered Gaussian random variables with a covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{C}(\rho,\rho_{u})
parameterized by two constants
\rho,\rho_{u}\in[0,1)
so that:

\boldsymbol{C}(\rho,\rho_{u})=\text{Cov}((x,\boldsymbol{z},u),(x,\boldsymbol{z},u))=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}1&\rho&-\rho&\rho&\rho&-\rho&\rho_{u}\\
\rho&1&-\rho&\rho&\rho&-\rho&\rho_{u}\\
-\rho&\rho&1&-\rho&-\rho&\rho&\rho_{u}\\
\rho&\rho&-\rho&1&\rho&-\rho&\rho_{u}\\
\rho&\rho&-\rho&\rho&1&-\rho&\rho_{u}\\
-\rho&-\rho&\rho&-\rho&-\rho&1&\rho_{u}\\
\rho_{u}&\rho_{u}&\rho_{u}&\rho_{u}&\rho_{u}&\rho_{u}&1\end{array}\right].

(14)

The relationship between these inputs and the sensor output is defined by the following relationship:

\begin{split}y&=\frac{3}{2}\text{log}(x+4)+\text{atan}\left(\frac{z_{1}}{2}\right)\left(1+0.1\frac{z_{1}}{2}\right)+\text{atan}\left(\frac{z_{2}}{2}\right)\left(1+0.1\frac{z_{2}}{2}\right)\\
&+0.1\text{atan}\left(\frac{z_{3}}{2}\right)\left(1+0.1\frac{z_{3}}{2}\right)+3\text{cos}\left(\frac{z_{1}+z_{3}}{6}\right)+2\text{cos}\left(\frac{x+z_{2}}{6}\right)+\alpha_{u}u.\end{split}

(15)

This relationship is non-linear and includes cross effects. It introduces a clear hierarchy between the input variables: the variables
x
and
z_{1}
are the most influential ones on
y
, then comes
z_{2}
with a slightly smaller influence, and finally
z_{3}
with an even smaller one (see Figure
1
left). The constant
\alpha_{u}
is used to modulate the influence of the uncontrolled variable
u
. The variables
z_{4}
and
z_{5}
do not appear in the model. Thus, in terms of variable selection, we want the algorithm to select only
z_{1}
,
z_{2}
and
z_{3}
, regardless of the values of
\rho
,
\rho_{u}
and
\alpha_{u}
, and regardless of the measurement noise.

For each numerical experiment, two data sets with the same features were generated: one was used as a training data set for variable selection (calibration step), while the other served as a test data set for pollutant concentration estimation (inversion step). In this first application, we chose a training set of size
n=200
. The values of
x,\boldsymbol{z},y
were perturbed by a Gaussian noise with standard deviation
\sigma^{\text{mes}}
.

. They show that in the same environment (

, regardless of

4.1.4
Conclusion on good selection of variables for sensor robust prediction

The results in Sections
4.1
through
4.1.3
highlight the critical role of variable selection in ensuring robust and reliable predictions. Interestingly, the presence of an unmeasured variable only moderately interferes with the variable selection process and the subsequent prediction. In all the scenarios, the
selected

complete model
. It generalizes significantly better in unknown environments, reinforcing its relevance for applications where measurement conditions may deviate from the calibration configuration.

Experimental dataset

This data set comes from a 28-day outdoor deployment of a carbon nanotube (CNT)-based chemiresistor array in Marne-La-Vallée, France, near a major traffic artery. As shown in Figure

While unfunctionalized CNTs are largely sensitive to various gases and environmental factors (e.g., temperature, humidity), functionalization of CNTs is widely used to increase their selectivity. However, the effectiveness of functionalization strategies under real-world conditions remains unclear due to limited outdoor studies.

The dataset includes time series for six environmental variables (carbon monoxide

Some sensors in the array were excluded due to erroneous readings. Due to the high correlation between sensors of the same type, a single representative (1A for unfunctionalized, 1H for functionalized) is used in most analyses. All variables in the dataset are highly correlated (as shown in Figure
5
of Section

The proposed selection method is applied without prior assumptions about sensitivities, treating all variables as potential interferents during calibration. The most influential variables identified during this phase are then targeted for prediction. Our previous study suggested

5
Conclusion

This work presented a variable selection algorithm based on a trade-off between variance minimization and model complexity to improve the predictive capacity of sensors. This paper proposed several tools to post-process the algorithm results for a better understanding of how the sensors work, including the definition of a Pareto front to identify optimal models, and the use of a variance decomposition method for a quantitative analysis of sensor sensitivities. This variable selection method was successfully applied to analytical cases of increasing complexity. The results demonstrated the robustness of the method against several challenges inherent to calibration and sensor deployment in open environments, such as significant measurement noise strong correlations between environmental variables, and the influence of unmeasured variables. The approach was then applied to experimental data obtained from a functionalized carbon nanotube-based sensor array deployed outdoors. The results highlighted how the functionalization of the CNT sensors with a specific polyfluorene polymer bearing urea moieties enabled the sensor to achieve much stronger selectivity to
CO
than non-functionalized sensors, a much desired goal in the literature. The resolution of the sensors in
CO
was found remarkable compared to state of the art results obtained in laboratory.

\boldsymbol{f}
and associated estimators

and
\widehat{\theta}

(the model error is assumed to be centered, and its variance is likely to be close to

\begin{split}\text{Var}&\left(\widehat{{v}}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{mes}},\boldsymbol{Z}^{\text{mes}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\right)=\text{Var}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{{v}}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{mes}},\boldsymbol{Z}^{\text{mes}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\widehat{\theta}\right]\right)+\mathbb{E}\left[\text{Var}\left(\widehat{{v}}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{mes}},\boldsymbol{Z}^{\text{mes}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\widehat{\theta}\right)\right]\\

(16)

If we note
\boldsymbol{m}_{f}=\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\text{mes}},\boldsymbol{Z}^{\text{mes}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\right]

it finally comes:

\delta_{j}=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{D}}p(\pi)\left[S^{T}\left(C_{\pi(j)}(\pi)\right)-S^{T}\left(C_{\pi(j)-1}(\pi)\right)\right],

(25)

\boldsymbol{W}
,

2.

\mathcal{S}_{D}

,

3.

\pi=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{d})

\mathcal{S}_{D}

\pi(j)

j
in
\pi

\pi_{\pi(j)}=j

5.

C_{j}(\pi)=\left\{\pi_{k}:\ k\leq j\right\}
is the set of the
j^{\text{th}}

\pi

C_{0}(\pi)=\emptyset

6.

for any subset of indices

(see

S^{T}(A)=1-\frac{\text{Var}(\mathbb{E}\left[h(\boldsymbol{W})\ |\ \boldsymbol{W}_{A}\right])}{\text{Var}(h(\boldsymbol{W}))},

(26)

7.

p(\pi)=\frac{L(\pi)}{\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{D}}L(\sigma)},\ \ L(\pi)=\left(\prod_{k\in D}S^{T}(C_{k}(\pi))\right)^{-1}.

(27)

These indices, which require the calculation of a large number of conditional expectation variances, are calculated in practice by pick-freeze sampling methods
[
3
]
, in exactly the same way as Sobol indices are classically calculated, without this being a real numerical challenge, given that the execution time of the prediction model is extremely fast.

Appendix D
Resolution analysis

Using the previous notations, an additional post-processing of the optimal model is proposed: it aims to compute the
resolution
of the sensor to its targets
\boldsymbol{x}
and interferents
\boldsymbol{z}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
. For simplicity, we no longer distinguish between
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
in this part, and we group the possible values of
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
at a time that is not in
\mathcal{D}_{n}
in the
q
-dimensional vector
\boldsymbol{W}=(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Z}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
, with
q=d_{x}+\sharp\boldsymbol{\alpha}
. The model predicting the measured value of the
m^{\text{th}}
component of
\boldsymbol{y}
is then written

\widehat{v}(\boldsymbol{W})=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{W})^{T}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+\widehat{\theta}\xi.

(28)

With these notations, the
average
resolution of level
k
of the sensor
m
at the value
w_{j}
for the
j^{\text{th}}
component of
\boldsymbol{W}
can then be calculated as the minimum fluctuation
\delta_{j}(w_{j})
that is likely to cause a sensor response variance that is greater than
k
times the model error, i.e. the fluctuation such that:

\delta_{j}(w_{j})=\min\left\{\delta\geq 0:\ \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{-j}}\left[\text{Var}_{\zeta}\left(\widehat{v}\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{-j},w_{j}+\delta\times\zeta\right)|\boldsymbol{W}_{-j}\right)\geq k\times\widehat{\theta}\right]\right\},

(29)

where
\boldsymbol{W}_{-j}
is the vector containing all the components of
\boldsymbol{W}
except the
j^{\text{th}}
one, and where
\zeta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is a standard Gaussian random variable. This sensor resolution is said to be
averaged
, since we are interested in the average over all possible values of
\boldsymbol{W}_{j}
of the sensor variance that is due to
W_{j}
when we focus on values around
w_{j}
. This resolution is defined as a function of
w_{j}
, such that its smallest values indicate the ranges where the sensor is most sensitive to
W_{j}
, and its largest values indicate the ranges where the sensor is least sensitive to
W_{j}
.
To calculate this resolution, we propose to use a Gaussian variable
\zeta
, but any other random variable with mean zero and variance 1 could equally well be used (without greatly affecting the results).
