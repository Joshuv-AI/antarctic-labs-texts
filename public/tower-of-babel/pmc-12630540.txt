Suzetrigine – A Novel FDA‐Approved Analgesic – Opportunities, Challenges and Future Perspectives: A Perspective Review
Authors: Ammarah Laaika Mohiuddin, Zubaier Ahmed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsSuzetrigine is the first class of non‐opioid analgesics that selectively targets and blocks a key mediator in pain signal transmission, NaV1.8, which is expressed in the peripheral nociceptive neurons. Suzetrigine received approval from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) on January 30, 2025. This review aims to explore Suzetrigine, a newly FDA‐approved analgesic, by discussing its clinical opportunities, associated challenges, and future directions in pain management.MethodsWe searched major databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and ClinicalTrials.gov) and FDA sources for information on Suzetrigine and its role in pain management. The collected preclinical, clinical, and regulatory data were reviewed and analyzed to highlight their current evidence, challenges, and future potential.Result and DiscussionSuzetrigine has been approved to treat acute pain after a sufficient and acceptable clinical trial. In terms of its usage in treating chronic pain, its effectiveness in cases of pregnant or lactating women and children, as well as whether it can be used in combination therapy, are still under observation. The brand name of Suzetrigine is JOURNAVX. So far, enough evidence has been provided to approve this drug only to treat acute pain for a short period of time. A lot of people with short‐term pain are concerned about getting addicted to opioid analgesics when they are used as postoperative pain relievers. The invention of Suzetrigine focused on combating these sorts of phenomena.ConclusionSuzetrigine is considered an alternative option in the realm of acute pain management. Due to its selectivity, this medication does not interfere with other local anesthetics. Moreover, this selectivity also constrains this medication to act on other receptors, thus possessing the least amount of adverse effects. Although Suzetrigine created many opportunities for research in the field of pain management, but also opened a door for many uncertainties that cannot be ignored.

Introduction

In the past few decades, pain management in trauma and postoperative cases has evolved from opium use in the 1600s by European doctors to advanced, multimodal approaches combining opioids, non‐opioids, as well as other regional techniques. Apparently, for surgeries, ether and chloroform were introduced as anesthetics during the 1800s. However, this pain reliever had the tendency to interfere with or hamper the healing process. Somehow, these concerns resulted in the discontinuation of this method. By the 19th and 20th centuries, anesthetics, morphine, and heroin were introduced as pain medications. On this note, doctors were in a constant dilemma between improving the quality of life of the patient while making the patient vulnerable to addictions [1]. At present, modern pain management emphasizes multimodal strategies to optimize analgesia, reduce opioid reliance, and alter regional nerve blocks. Acute pain was treated with nonopioid analgesics such as Acetaminophen, nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) (Tylenol, ibuprofen), and weak opioid analgesics by adding some adjuvants (gabapentin and nortriptyline) nowadays. COX‐2‐non‐selective (ibuprofen) and selective (celecoxib) NSAIDs, despite being effective in the treatment of acute pain, still carry potential renal, gastrointestinal, as well as cardiovascular threats. When it comes to the safety profile and versatile routes of administration, Acetaminophen is usually recommended for most patients worldwide. The possible respiratory depression and sedation caused by Gabapentin have tempered its usage. The adverse effect surrounding toxicity and neuropsychiatric effects of lidocaine and bupivacaine also provides a potential threat to its usage. These side effects, inefficacy, and addictiveness in the worst cases of these drugs, promoted a constant quest for an alternative to this continuum of medication [2]. There was also ongoing research for a drug to treat patients with postoperative pain, so that they are not exposed to opioids right away during the acute postoperative period, as well as for the time when the anesthesia wears off in outpatient surgery centers. The target was to find an alternative that can work along with other medications and increase the potency of pain control. Suzetrigine is a non‐opioid, nonaddictive, selective sodium channel blocker analgesic which was recently approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA). At this point, it is only approved for a short period of time to treat acute pain (moderate to severe pain). Suzetrigine is a highly specific drug for specialized nerve receptors or sodium channels, activating these receptors [3]. Suzetrigine is emerging as a potential painkiller as it is reflecting high chances of replacing intravenous or injectable nerve blocks (Lidocaine, bupivacaine) to treat acute postsurgical pain, by working on the same receptors as these nerve blocking medications. The purpose of this review paper is to summarize and contextualize the therapeutic potential, challenges, limitations, approval, opportunities, and future consideration of suzetrigine, with an aim to be informative for students, clinicians not specialized in pain medicine, or policymakers new to the subject.

Overview on Some Commonly Used Pharmacological Methods of Pain Management and Its Limitations

When it comes to pain management, pharmacological methods are mostly preferred over nonpharmacological methods worldwide. Acetaminophen, NSAIDs, and opioid analgesics are included in pharmacological methods. However, a number of side effects, adverse effects, dosing, as well as patient selection conditions arise during the usage of these drugs. This limits the effectiveness of these existing analgesics. Pharmacological Background of Suzetrigine

Suzetrigine is a synthetic organic sodium channel blocker, which is chemically known as 4‐[(2R,3S,4S,5R)−3‐(3,4‐difluoro‐2‐methoxyphenyl)−4,5‐dimethyl‐5‐(trifluoromethyl) oxolane‐2‐amido]pyridine‐2‐carboxamide. It alleviates acute pain. Suzetrigine is a selective inhibitor of the NaV1.8 voltage‐gated sodium channel with molecular formula C21H20F5N3O4. Suzetrigine displays a novel mechanism of action. It works in the periphery, where the pain stimulation begins and spreads throughout the body via nociceptors, or pain receptors. Suzetrigine inhibits the pain sensations by dampening the nerve signals from the periphery to the spinal cord. More precisely, it stabilizes the closed state of the sodium channels (the NaV1.8, which is expressed in sensory neurons of the primary human dorsal root ganglion). This oral pain medication plays a role in inhibiting the transmission of pain signals via action potentials in peripheral sensory nerves [2]. Unlike other nerve blocking agents, which might not be feasible for all sorts of patients due to having an intravenous route, suzetrigine, as an oral agent, works for different kinds of pain syndromes. This selectivity inhibition of this medication prevents it from working on any other receptor apart from the targeted one. However, Suzetrigine is largely metabolized by liver enzymes, CYP3A enzymes, which are a sub‐family of cytochrome P450 enzymes [4]. Therapeutic Potential and Clinical Applications

Suzetrigine, an oral sodium channel blocker, is used to treat moderate to severe pain without creating any dependency or addiction. It can be considered the first discovery of a drug directly targeting the action potential of sodium channels. Therefore, this medication does not interact with other receptors like opioid analgesics do. Suzetrigine is mainly targeted to be used in treating acute postoperative pain, to minimize the use of opioid analgesics, which have the potential to create opioid analgesics in patients. So far, no withdrawal syndromes have been noticed in patients after the use of Suzetrigine. Based on the available clinical and preclinical data, it can be ascertained that Suzetrigine is not an addictive agent, as it has not shown any addictive potential. This drug is used in patients with no liver or kidney dysfunction. Opioid analgesics possess the potential to create dependency or addiction, as well as interfere with other receptors. Contrary to the side effects of opioid analgesics, suzetrigine has great advantages to alleviate acute pain syndromes in the case of inpatients. In combination with bupivacaine or ropivacaine, local anesthetics, Suzetrigine showed an additive inhibition of NaV1.8 within a certain dose of each agent. This combinational therapy was neither synergistic nor antagonistic. Therefore, it can be understood that this drug does not interfere with local anesthetics [5].

Challenges and Limitations

There is no available data that approves the usage of Suzetrigine by pregnant or lactating women and children due to limited clinical trials. Again, whether Suzetrigine can reversibly affect fertility in women remains to be determined due to limited clinical data. Since the drug is metabolized by liver enzymes, CYP3A, patients with mild to moderate liver dysfunction need a dose reduction. Patients with severe liver dysfunction are expected to avoid this medication. Products containing grapefruit should also be avoided while treatment with suzetrigine. It is also recommended to avoid the concurrent use of antibiotics such as erythromycin or clarithromycin with suzetrigine. Concurrent use of ketoconazole with suzetrigine is also contraindicated [4]. According to the NOPAIN Act, facilitating the medicare coverage for FDA‐approved non‐opioid pain therapies in outpatient surgical settings, Suzetrigine has been in effect since January 2025. This was an expected attempt to enable broader adoption of this medication by minimizing the financial barriers. Now, opioid‐related complications promote prolonged hospital stays, treatment of addictions, as well as readmissions, contributing to increased healthcare costs [8]. As per a pharmacoeconomic analysis, because of decreased risk of adverse effects, Suzetrigine would be cost‐saving relative to an opioid at the price of $232.50 for a supply of 7 days [4]. Thus, Suzetrigine, as a safer and effective alternative, has the potential to improve patient health in a cost‐effective way compared to opioid analgesics.

Drug Approval and Implications for Health Science and Policy

To combat the opioid crisis, Suzetrigine plays a vital role in contributing to the healthcare systems. This groundbreaking discovery somehow contributed to a vision where pain relief does not come at the cost of addiction and co‐dependency. Policy support, more knowledge, and research can efficiently enable this medication to work up to its full potential. When it comes to drug approval, to facilitate the review process, the FDA designated suzetrigine as a “breakthrough therapy,” “fast track,” and “priority review” [3]. Moreover, approval of Suzetrigine was backed by data from two Phase‐3 clinical trials. The clinical trials were conducted to measure the primary efficacy and safety of the drug over a 48‐h period with safety assessments that eventually lasted to 14‐day post‐treatment [9]. Despite indicating a philosophical divide between strict scientific standards and public health considerations due to the lack of availability of clinical data, Suzetrigine still remains a revolutionary attempt in the field of non‐opioid alternatives.

Future Practice, Policy, and Research Directions

In terms of future policy, Suzetrigine will significantly focus on expanding its approved uses beyond acute pain to chronic conditions such as neuropathic pain and other potential pain. This also includes chronic pain that comes from conditions such as cancer. Moreover, given the ongoing necessity for opioid alternatives, some regulatory policies may be adapted to fast‐track further research and approvals. Implications of this medication include reduced opioid dependence, lower overall healthcare costs, as well as wider access for patients with chronic pain management. Moreover, the implications may also potentially lower the need for robust post‐marketing surveillance to monitor long‐term effectiveness and safety of patients [10]. As per the preclinical and clinical settings, suzetrigine has been proven to be a well‐tolerated medication with mild adverse effects. Suzetrigine has shown a promising advancement in the treatment of acute pain treatment when it comes in the context of the ongoing opioid crisis. However, to evaluate the long‐term safety and efficacy, such as chronic treatment or in case of effective combinational therapy, further research is needed. This oral pain medication has the potential to become a critical tool used to reduce opioid dependency in the management of both acute, chronic, and neuropathic pain with the expansion of clinical trials. If ongoing studies on this drug approve its efficacy in these certain conditions, this can be a milestone for its clinical efficacy, thus providing relief to the patients having limited treatment options. Sufficient knowledge, advocacy, and education are required for the transition from opioid analgesics to non‐opioid analgesics. It is the core responsibility of the healthcare workers to be accustomed to the benefits, dosing, and other potential effects of suzetrigine. Patients usually prescribed opioid analgesics also need to be properly educated about the existence of replaceable non‐opioid alternatives. Lastly, to authenticate the tolerability and effectiveness of this medication, data from clinical trials, instead of data (long‐term) from the real world, are not sufficient [8].

Conclusion

Suzetrigine, an orally administered sodium channel blocker, represents a potential new class of selective non‐opioid analgesics that has surely grabbed a lot of interest in the field of research. In the realm of acute pain management, suzetrigine has shown a milestone of success due to its novel mechanism. The selectivity of this oral medication spares the other receptors, thus causing minimal or no mentionable adverse effect. This also explains the phenomenon of suzetrigine not interfering with local anesthetics targeting different receptors. Suzetrigine has more or less established itself as an alternative option to treat acute pain. Although presently approved for acute pain and allotted for a maximum usage period of 14 days, this medication bears the potential to replace the opioid analgesics in the upcoming days. But along with the opportunities, the uncertainties of suzetrigine cannot be ignored. There are still not enough clinical data that approves the use of this medication in pregnant or lactating women or children. The oral availability might also restrict the use of this drug for some patients. Patients with kidney or liver dysfunction are recommended to avoid this medication. However, more research and clinical trials might help to overcome these obstacles as well.
