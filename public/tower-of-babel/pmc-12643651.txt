Three Dimensional-CTA versus Doppler Ultrasound for Anterolateral Thigh Flap Reconstruction in Limb Defects: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Longhua Yu, Yong Jiang, Jin Zhang, Huagang Zhang, Dazhi Yu, Yanqing Chen, Haiyu Fan, Xiuzhong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Soft tissue limb defects often require anterolateral thigh (ALT) flap reconstruction, but anatomical variability of perforator vessels poses a challenge. The comparative efficacy of 3-dimensional computed tomographic angiography (3D-CTA) and color Doppler ultrasonography (CDUS) for preoperative planning remains unclear.Methods:This retrospective study analyzed 95 patients who underwent ALT flap limb reconstruction between March 2017 and May 2023, comparing a 3D-CTA–guided group (n=45) with a CDUS-guided group (n=50). Key outcomes included perforator mapping accuracy, surgical efficiency, postoperative complications, functional and aesthetic results, and cost-effectiveness.Results:Use of 3D-CTA demonstrated superior perforator detection sensitivity (98.4% versus 85.8%, P < 0.001) and positional accuracy (deviation: 0.3 versus 1.8 cm, P < 0.001). The 3D-CTA group had significantly shorter flap harvest times (38.5 versus 51.4 min, P < 0.001), fewer vascular complications (4.4% versus 20.0%, P = 0.0489), and higher flap survival (100% versus 92.0%, P = 0.047). The 3D-CTA cohort also achieved better sensory recovery and donor-site outcomes (Vancouver Scar Scale: 2.1 versus 4.3, P < 0.01). Despite higher initial imaging costs, 3D-CTA proved more cost-effective overall.Conclusions:For ALT flap reconstruction in limb defects, 3D-CTA provides superior perforator mapping, enhances surgical efficiency, improves clinical outcomes, and offers greater cost-effectiveness compared with CDUS. These findings support prioritizing 3D-CTA in preoperative planning for complex limb salvage.

Takeaways

Question: Which imaging modality, 3-dimensional computed tomographic angiography (3D-CTA) or Doppler ultrasound, provides better outcomes in anterolateral thigh flap reconstruction for limb defects?

Findings: In this 95-patient study, 3D-CTA was superior to Doppler ultrasound in perforator detection accuracy, surgical efficiency, and clinical outcomes, including a higher flap survival rate (100% versus 92.0%). Despite its initial cost, 3D-CTA proved more cost-effective.

Meaning: We found that 3D-CTA is a better preoperative planning tool than Doppler ultrasound for this surgery, leading to improved surgical precision, fewer complications, and better patient outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Soft tissue defects of the limbs from trauma, tumor resection, infection, or chronic wounds often necessitate flap coverage to preserve form and function.1,2 These complex defects, arising from etiologies such as open fractures, diabetic ulcers, or oncological resections, require reconstruction goals including stable coverage, dead space obliteration, vascular restoration, and rehabilitation readiness.3,4 Although small defects may be managed with local flaps or grafts, larger defects demand free tissue transfer. The anterolateral thigh (ALT) flap has become a cornerstone for limb reconstruction due to its versatility, abundant fasciocutaneous tissue, concealed donor site, and favorable contour match.3,5,6 Supplied by perforators from the lateral circumflex femoral artery (LCFA), the ALT flap’s vascular pedicle allows diverse applications as a free, pedicled, or perforator flap.7,8 However, its utility is challenged by significant variability in perforator anatomy, including origin, number, course, and caliber.9,10 Such variability complicates dissection, increases operative time, and raises risks of partial necrosis or flap failure, underscoring the need for precise preoperative imaging.11,12

Three-dimensional computed tomographic angiography (3D-CTA) and color Doppler ultrasonography (CDUS) are widely used for perforator mapping. CTA provides high-resolution 3D visualization of perforators, enabling optimal flap design by detailing their intramuscular course and spatial relationships.13,14 Conversely, CDUS offers a radiation-free, cost-effective alternative for dynamic localization but is limited by operator dependence and reduced accuracy in obese patients.12,14 Although both modalities improve outcomes over anatomical landmark-based techniques,15 comparative studies in ALT flaps remain sparse. Prior research on abdominal flaps (eg, deep inferior epigastric perforator) reports conflicting results, with some favoring CTA’s precision16 and others showing comparable outcomes between modalities.17 For limb reconstruction—where thin, pliable flaps are critical—the impact of imaging choice on intraoperative efficiency and postoperative success remains unclear.

This study conducted a 6-year retrospective cohort analysis to systematically compare the efficacy of 3D-CTA versus CDUS-guided ALT free flap reconstruction for limb defect restoration. Quantitative correlations between preoperative imaging parameters and intraoperative anatomical findings were evaluated. Surgical efficiency metrics, safety profiles, and postoperative outcomes were compared between cohorts, with integrated cost-effectiveness analyses (incremental cost-effectiveness ratio [ICER]) and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) assessments for health economic evaluation. By elucidating the strengths and limitations of each modality, our findings aim to optimize preoperative planning and clinical outcomes in ALT flap reconstruction, providing evidence-based recommendations for limb salvage.

METHOD

Study Design

We conducted a retrospective cohort analysis of patients undergoing free ALT flap reconstruction for limb salvage after preoperative perforator mapping using 3D-CTA or CDUS. The study duration spanned from March 2017 to May 2023 and received ethical approval.

Study Population

The study included patients aged 18–70 years with limb soft tissue defects secondary to trauma, tumor resection, infection, or chronic wounds, who underwent free ALT flap coverage following preoperative perforator mapping via 3D-CTA or CDUS. All patients were followed up for 6 months or more. Exclusion criteria included ALT flaps for nonlimb defects, primary closure or skin grafting, lack of preoperative vascular imaging, or insufficient follow-up data.

Grouping and Sample Size Calculation

Patients were divided into 2 groups based on preoperative vascular assessment techniques: 3D-CTA and CDUS. A priori sample size calculation ensured sufficient statistical power to detect significant differences between groups. Based on pilot data and prior studies indicating a large effect size (Cohen d = 0.8) in perforator detection sensitivity improvement, we targeted 95% power at an α value of 0.05. Using G*Power 3.1, the required sample size was 88 participants (44 per group).

Imaging Evaluation

For the 3D-CTA group, noncontrast CTA was performed using a Toshiba Aquilion ONE 320-detector scanner, covering the mid-thigh to knee. Images were acquired during the arterial inflow phase after injecting 100 mL of nonionic iodinated contrast (3 mL/s) and reconstructed into submillimeter slices with multiplanar volume rendering. For the CDUS group, a Philips IU22 system (15-MHz linear probe) was used in the supine position. Gentle transducer pressure facilitated perforator mapping through the vastus lateralis, documenting location, flow characteristics, and vessel caliber.

Surgical Procedure

Following routine disinfection, the skin and subcutaneous tissue were incised along the fascia lata. The flap was dissected near the perforators, with fascial opening and elevation along the deep vascular plane. The superolateral flap margin was divided at the branch origin, and the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve was identified between fascial layers. A 1- to 5-cm circular flap adjacent to the perforator was harvested, with fascial transection at the intermuscular septum. The vascular pedicle was dissected proximally to the descending/transverse branches of the lateral circumflex femoral vessels. Postoperative care included prophylactic anti-infectives, antispasmodics, and close flap perfusion monitoring.

Data Collection and Outcomes

Demographic characteristics, medical history, defect features, surgical parameters, and follow-up data were systematically collected from electronic medical records. Preoperative assessments included perforator vessel attributes (quantity, caliber, anatomical coordinates) and vascular anatomical variations (eg, absence of the descending branch, intramuscular course). Intraoperative parameters comprised procedural efficiency (flap harvest time, pedicle dissection duration) and safety metrics (blood loss volume, vascular injury events such as misidentification or spasm). Postoperative outcomes were categorized into short-term (7-d flap survival rate, infection incidence) and long-term evaluations, encompassing sensory recovery via 2-point discrimination (2PD, mm), donor-site morbidity assessed by the Vancouver Scar Scale (VSS), patient-reported satisfaction using a visual analog scale, and health-related quality of life quantified through the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36) Physical and Mental Component Summaries. Economic analyses integrated direct medical costs (imaging, surgery, complication management) and indirect expenses, with QALYs derived from SF-36 utility values using the Brazier algorithm.18,19

Quality Control

To minimize subjective assessment bias, we used the following strategies: (1) all imaging assessments were done by 2 independent, experienced radiologists who remained blinded to surgical outcome; (2) objective quantitative indicators were used to evaluate accuracy, including perforator vessel positioning deviation distance and vessel diameter measurement error, rather than relying solely on subjective judgment; (3) intraoperative findings were considered the gold standard and were recorded by 2 surgeons who were unaware of the imaging results; and (4) all measurement results with differences exceeding the preset threshold will be reviewed and adjudicated by a third evaluator. Inter-evaluator agreement analysis showed good reliability (Cohen κ = 0.83).

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp.). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD and compared via independent t tests or Mann-Whitney U test (depending on the normal distribution). Categorical variables were analyzed with χ2 or Fisher exact tests. Cost-effectiveness ratios (ICER) and QALYs (Brazier algorithm) were calculated. A 2-tailed P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

This retrospective cohort study enrolled 95 patients undergoing ALT free flap reconstruction for limb defects, with 45 in the 3D-CTA group and 50 in the CDUS group. Baseline characteristics showed no significant intergroup differences, including age (3D-CTA: 42.3 ± 12.5 versus CDUS: 44.1 ± 11.8 y, P = 0.47), sex distribution (male: 62.2% versus 58.0%, P = 0.67), body mass index (24.8 ± 3.2 versus 25.1 ± 3.5 kg/m2, P = 0.68), smoking history (13.3% versus 10%, P = 0.61), comorbidities (diabetes: 11.1% versus 12%; peripheral vascular disease: 8.9% versus 10%, all P > 0.86), defect characteristics (area: 45.2 ± 18.3 versus 48.7 ± 20.1 cm2, P = 0.38; location [upper/lower limb]: 18/27 versus 20/30, P = 0.83; etiology [trauma/infection/tumor]: comparable ratios, P = 0.99), preoperative SF-36 scores (physical: 47.5 ± 12.2 versus 47.8 ± 11.7; mental: 48.3 ± 10.4 versus 49.1 ± 9.9, both P > 0.70), and follow-up duration (13.1 ± 4.6 versus 14.2 ± 4.2 mo, P = 0.24) (as shown in Table 1).

Preoperative Vascular Localization Accuracy and Diagnostic Efficacy

Anatomical Visualization and Detection Performance

The 3D-CTA group demonstrated superior perforator mapping accuracy, detecting 123 perforators (2.7 ± 0.6 per case), with 82.9% (102 of 123) originating from the descending branch of the LCFA and 17.1% (21 of 123) from the oblique branch. Perforator exit points clustered predominantly (74.8%) in the middle-lower third of the anterior superior iliac spine to patella line. Compared with intraoperative findings, 3D-CTA achieved a positional deviation of 0.3 ± 0.1 cm and a vessel diameter error of 0.1 ± 0.03 mm. It detected 100% (105 of 105) of perforators 0.5 mm or more and 90% (18 of 20) of those measuring 0.3–0.4 mm, yielding 98.4% sensitivity (123 of 125) and 100% positive predictive value, with only 2 small (0.3 mm) intramuscular perforators missed.

In contrast, Doppler ultrasound identified 120 perforators but had 17 false negatives (14 due to diameters <0.5 mm; 3 from signal attenuation). Localization accuracy was significantly lower (1.8 ± 0.6 cm deviation) with higher diameter measurement errors (0.2 ± 0.06 mm) due to signal overcompensation. Sensitivity (85.8%, 103 of 120), specificity (88.3%), and positive predictive value (89.6%) were inferior to 3D-CTA, alongside an 11.7% false-positive rate (12 of 103 marked vessels unconfirmed intraoperatively). Statistical analysis confirmed significant differences (P < 0.001) in localization precision, small-vessel detection, and measurement accuracy between modalities (Table 2).

Anatomical Variant Identification

We found that 3D-CTA was significantly better than Doppler ultrasound in recognizing vascular variations. It successfully identified all 10 cases (22.2%) of vascular anatomical variations before operation, including 6 cases of absence of the descending branch of LCFA (compensated by oblique branch) and 4 cases of musculocutaneous penetration variation of the perforator vessel (penetrating the vastus lateralis instead of the muscular space). In the Doppler ultrasound group, only 4 cases (33.3% versus 3D-CTA group, P = 0.00169) had vascular variation detected before operation, and the remaining 8 cases needed to adjust the surgical plan due to unexpected variation during operation (P = 0.0062) (Table 2).

Intraoperative Operative Efficiency and Safety

Flap Harvest Time and Operative Procedures

The mean flap harvest time in the 3D-CTA group was 38.5 ± 6.2 minutes, significantly shorter than the 51.4 ± 8.4 minutes in the CDUS group (P < 0.001) (Table 3). This disparity primarily stemmed from optimized incision design and reduced intraoperative exploration frequency (3D-CTA: 1.2 ± 0.4 times versus CDUS: 3.8 ± 1.1 times, P < 0.01). Vascular pedicle preparation efficiency was also superior in the 3D-CTA group, with LCFA and accompanying vein dissection completed in 12.3 ± 2.1 minutes compared with 18.7 ± 3.5 minutes in the Doppler group (P < 0.01).

Intraoperative and Postoperative Core Outcomes Comparison

Intraoperative Blood Loss and Vascular Injury Risk

The 3D-CTA group exhibited significantly lower intraoperative blood loss (45.3 ± 12.1 mL) versus the Doppler group (68.7 ± 15.4 mL, P < 0.01). In the CDUS cohort, misjudgment of perforator locations led to inadvertent muscle septum resection in 6 cases (12.0%), necessitating additional hemostatic maneuvers (Table 3).

Vascular Crisis and Intraoperative Emergencies

In the 3D-CTA group, only 2 cases (4.4%) experienced transient arterial spasm, which resolved after warm saline irrigation and papaverine injection. Conversely, the CDUS group reported 10 vascular crises (20.0%: 4 arterial spasms, 6 venous thromboses), with 2 cases of partial flap necrosis requiring secondary surgical repair (P = 0.0489) (Table 3).

Postoperative Flap Survival and Functional Recovery

The 3D-CTA group demonstrated superior flap viability, achieving 100% survival (45 of 45 cases) at 7 days postoperatively, compared with 92.0% (46 of 50) in the CDUS group, where 4 cases required debridement for marginal necrosis (P = 0.047). Infection rates trended lower in the 3D-CTA cohort (4.4% versus 16.0%, P = 0.13), potentially attributable to shorter operative times. At 6-month follow-up, the 3D-CTA group exhibited better sensory recovery, with 2PD measuring 5.2 ± 1.1 mm (range: 4.0–7.0 mm) versus 6.8 ± 1.5 mm (range: 5.5–9.0 mm) in the CDUS group (P < 0.01). Additionally, SF-36 scores for physical health (78.4 ± 6.2 versus CDUS) and mental health (78.1 ± 5.8 versus CDUS) were significantly higher in the 3D-CTA cohort, reflecting improved long-term quality of life (Table 3).

Donor-site Morbidity and Aesthetic Outcomes

The 3D-CTA group demonstrated superior incision precision, with shorter nonproductive incision lengths (1.5 ± 0.6 versus 3.2 ± 1.2 cm in the CDUS group; P < 0.01) and no cases of vastus lateralis muscle injury, compared with 6 partial muscle injuries (12.0%) in the CDUS cohort (P = 0.028). The postoperative scar outcomes at the last follow-up were also in favor of the 3D-CTA group. The VSS score was significantly lower (2.1 ± 0.8 versus 4.3 ± 1.2, P < 0.01), and the rate of excellent and good scar quality was higher (93.3% versus 64.0%, P = 0.0014). Patient satisfaction metrics further reinforced these results, with the 3D-CTA group reporting superior aesthetic satisfaction (visual analog scale: 8.7 ± 1.2 versus 6.5 ± 1.5 in the CDUS group; P < 0.01) and overall approval rates (95.6% versus 64.0%, P < 0.01) (Table 4). Cost-effectiveness and Clinical Applicability

Despite higher per-test costs, the 3D-CTA group demonstrated superior cost-effectiveness with total expenses of ¥23,500 per case versus ¥28,700 in the Doppler group (P < 0.001). The ICER identified 3D-CTA as a “dominant” strategy, achieving 0.93 QALYs (versus 0.85 QALYs for Doppler) while saving ¥5200 per case, thereby optimizing health outcomes without additional expenditure (Table 5).

DISCUSSION

The ALT flap has cemented its role as a cornerstone in limb reconstruction due to its adaptability, robust vascular supply, and minimal donor-site morbidity.8,10 However, its clinical utility is inherently challenged by the unpredictable anatomy of perforators, which necessitates precise preoperative planning to mitigate risks such as partial flap loss or intraoperative vascular compromise.11,20 This study underscored the transformative impact of 3D-CTA in overcoming these challenges, demonstrating its superiority over CDUS across preoperative planning, intraoperative efficiency, and postoperative outcomes. By integrating multidimensional imaging data, 3D-CTA not only enhances surgical precision but also redefines the standard of care for complex limb salvage procedures.

PRECISION IN PERFORATOR MAPPING: A PARADIGM SHIFT

The critical advantage of 3D-CTA lies in its ability to delineate perforator anatomy with unparalleled spatial resolution. Unlike CDUS, which struggles with operator-dependent variability and limited depth penetration,12,14 3D-CTA provides a 3-dimensional roadmap of perforator trajectories, including their intramuscular courses and anastomotic networks. This capability proved indispensable in our cohort, where 3D-CTA identified 100% of clinically relevant perforators (≥0.5 mm) and detected all major vascular anatomical variants preoperatively, such as aberrant origins or intramuscular penetrations. In contrast, CDUS missed nearly 15% of perforators due to technical limitations, particularly in smaller vessels (<0.5 mm) or patients with higher body mass indices. These findings align with prior studies emphasizing 3D-CTA’s reliability in perforasome mapping,13,21 but our data uniquely highlight its impact on mitigating intraoperative surprises—a factor that directly influences surgical outcomes in limb reconstruction.

The clinical ramifications of this precision are profound. By preemptively identifying perforator exit points and vascular territories, 3D-CTA enables surgeons to design flaps tailored to the defect’s geometry, minimizing unnecessary dissection. This was reflected in the significantly shorter flap harvest times and reduced intraoperative blood loss in the 3D-CTA cohort. Conversely, the CDUS group required frequent intraoperative adjustments due to mismatches between preoperative mapping and actual anatomy, leading to prolonged dissection and higher rates of inadvertent muscle injury. Such inefficiencies not only prolong operative time but also elevate the risk of vascular compromise, as evidenced by the higher incidence of venous thrombosis and arterial spasms in the CDUS group.

SURGICAL AND FUNCTIONAL OUTCOMES: BEYOND SURVIVAL

Flap survival, although a critical metric, represents only 1 facet of successful reconstruction. Our results reveal that 3D-CTA’s benefits extend to long-term functional and aesthetic outcomes. The 3D-CTA cohort demonstrated superior sensory recovery, as measured by 2PD, which correlates with preserved neural networks during flap elevation. Additionally, donor-site morbidity was markedly reduced, with shorter nonproductive incisions and no cases of muscle injury—a stark contrast to the CDUS group, where inadvertent vastus lateralis damage occurred in 12% of cases. These findings underscore the importance of minimizing tissue trauma during dissection, a principle that 3D-CTA facilitates through targeted, anatomy-guided approaches.

Aesthetic outcomes further reinforced 3D-CTA’s superiority. Scar quality, assessed via the VSS, and patient-reported satisfaction scores were significantly better in the 3D-CTA group. This can be attributed to the precision of incision planning, which avoids redundant tissue exploration and preserves subcutaneous vascular networks. Such outcomes are particularly critical in limb reconstruction, where thin, pliable flaps are essential for both functional and cosmetic restoration.10,15

Health-related quality of life metrics further validate 3D-CTA’s clinical value. The 3D-CTA group reported significantly higher SF-36 scores for both physical and mental health, reflecting not only improved limb function but also reduced postoperative anxiety and depression. These findings align with growing evidence that patient-centered outcomes are inextricably linked to surgical precision and reduced complication rates.3,18

ECONOMIC AND ACCESSIBILITY CONSIDERATIONS

A striking finding of this study was the cost-effectiveness of 3D-CTA, which, despite radiation exposure risks and higher initial costs, significantly improves surgical planning accuracy while reducing operative time, complications, and secondary interventions. Our analysis demonstrated actual savings of ¥5200 per case in total medical expenses, challenging the conventional belief that advanced imaging inevitably increases healthcare costs. The ICER confirms 3D-CTA as the “dominant” strategy, achieving higher QALYs while reducing expenditure. However, accessibility barriers persist, including reliance on high-end computed tomography scanners, contrast agents, and specialized radiological expertise, which may limit adoption in regions with limited infrastructure. In resource-constrained environments, we recommend a stratified approach: prioritizing 3D-CTA for patients with larger body habitus, high risk of anatomical variations, or anticipated complex reconstructions, while maintaining CDUS as a pragmatic alternative for routine cases. Importantly, CDUS utility depends heavily on operator proficiency, as its false-negative rates for small perforators and anatomical variants require greater intraoperative adaptability. Future efforts to standardize CDUS protocols and enhance training could narrow this performance gap, whereas the proposed stratified approach ensures that long-term cost savings outweigh upfront investments, making advanced imaging viable even in resource-limited settings.

LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Although our findings robustly support 3D-CTA’s superiority, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. The retrospective design introduces potential selection bias, and the lack of intrapatient comparisons (eg, alternating imaging modalities in the same individual) precludes definitive conclusions about anatomical variability’s confounding effects. The modest sample size, though statistically powered, may underdetect differences in rare complications, such as total flap loss, which did not occur in either cohort. Additionally, the study did not evaluate emerging modalities such as magnetic resonance angiography or contrast-enhanced ultrasound, which may offer unique advantages in specific populations.

Future research should prioritize prospective, multicenter trials to validate these findings across diverse populations. Integrating 3D-CTA with intraoperative navigation systems could further refine dissection accuracy, particularly for trainees. Similarly, comparative studies assessing next-generation computed tomography technologies may uncover additional benefits in radiation dose reduction or contrast resolution.

CONCLUSIONS

This study highlighted 3D-CTA’s advantages in ALT flap-based limb reconstruction, offering enhanced perforator mapping accuracy, reduced operative risks, and improved functional and aesthetic outcomes compared with CDUS. Despite higher initial costs, its cost-effectiveness and streamlined surgical workflows justify prioritization in complex cases. Although retrospective design limits generalizability, the findings emphasize 3D-CTA’s value for precision-driven salvage. Future multicenter studies are warranted to validate its role and explore integrated imaging strategies.
