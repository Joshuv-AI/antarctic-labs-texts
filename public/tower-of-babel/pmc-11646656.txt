Trichophyton mentagrophytes delays wound healing in ob/ob mice
Authors: Kohsuke Aruga, Kimie Takehara, Satoshi Kametaka
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA wide variety of microbial species, including Trichophyton spp., have been detected in diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs). In particular, Trichophyton spp. cause tinea pedis (i.e., athlete's foot) and onychomycosis. However, investigations regarding the correlation between Trichophyton spp. and delayed wound healing are limited to case reports. Moreover, histological changes in wounds caused by Trichophyton spp. remain unclear. In this study, we sought to confirm the delayed wound healing phenotype caused by Trichophyton mentagrophytes infection in a full‐thickness excisional wound ob/ob mouse model through histopathological analysis. The inoculation of skin wounds in ob/ob mice with T. mentagrophytes sporules resulted in significant delay on wound healing. Further histopathological analyses demonstrated that the delayed wound healing in ob/ob mice was accompanied by reduced collagen fibre formation and inhibited granulation tissue formation, with spores and elongated mycelia retained in the wound bed and along the wound edges. This suggests that T. mentagrophytes colonization of wounds potentially contributes to delayed wound healing in patients with diabetes. These results suggest that neglected tinea pedis is a potential risk factor for delayed wound healing and progression to refractory wounds in patients with DFU.Keywords: delayed wound healing, diabetic foot ulcer, tinea pedis, Trichophyton mentagrophytes, wound care

INTRODUCTION

The prevalence of diabetes continues to increase worldwide, affecting 537 million adults between the ages of 20 and 79 years in 2021. 1 The International Working Group on the Diabetic Foot has defined a diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) as ‘a foot ulcer in a person with current or previously diagnosed diabetes mellitus, and usually accompanied by peripheral neuropathy and/or peripheral artery disease in the lower extremity’. Approximately 18.6 million diabetic patients are affected by DFUs annually worldwide. 2 Patients with diabetes are prone to infections owing to the reduced phagocytic capacity of neutrophils and macrophages, 3 , 4 as well as impaired keratinocyte adhesion, differentiation and proliferation 5 , 6 associated with persistent hyperglycaemia. 7 , 8 , 9 Consequently, patients with DFUs are susceptible to bacterial infections at the ulcer site and significantly impaired quality of life owing to the development of severe sepsis, osteomyelitis and lower extremity amputation. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Furthermore, treatment and care associated with DFUs and lower extremity amputations impose a significant economic burden, 14 making the provision of effective care to promote DFU healing critical.

Difficult‐to‐treat wounds, including DFUs, are affected by a wide range of bacteria. The associated biofilms delay wound healing and facilitate chronic wound progression. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 In particular, Trichophyton spp. and other fungi are commonly observed in chronic wounds and DFUs. 19 , 20 Tinea pedis, that is, athlete's foot, has been identified as a potential risk factor for secondary bacterial infections owing to its propensity to form cracks and fissures in the toes. 21 Moreover, in certain regions, a markedly elevated prevalence of tinea pedis or onychomycosis has been reported in patients with DFUs. 22 Meanwhile, Trichophyton spp. impede the healing of postoperative wounds. 23 Therefore, fungi may directly or indirectly cause or contribute to chronic wound development. Although neglected tinea pedis exacerbates DFUs, further research is needed to confirm whether tinea pedis is sufficient to further delay wound healing in a diabetic model.

Mice offer several advantages over other experimental animal models in that they require smaller breeding spaces and have faster life cycles, simple reproduction, a small and manageable size, and a whole genome that has been elucidated for genetic modification studies. Accordingly, several mouse models of chronic wounds have been developed. 24 , 25 , 26 In this study, we aimed to reproduce the phenotype of delayed wound healing caused by Trichophyton mentagrophytes, which causes tinea pedis in humans, in full‐thickness excisional wounds in ob/ob mice. The histopathological aspects were then evaluated. Collectively, this study provides fundamental insights into the wound management of patients with tinea pedis and DFUs that can inform the development of effective treatment or care regimens for patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental animals

The 11‐ to 13‐week‐old male C57BL/6 and C57BL/6JHamSlc‐ob/ob (ob/ob) mice (Japan SLC Co., Shizuoka, Japan) were used for analyses. All mice were maintained following a strict procedure under specific pathogen‐free conditions in a controlled environment (12 h light/dark cycle; 23 ± 3°C) at the breeding facility. All animal experiments were conducted following the ethical guidelines of the institution, the ARRIVE guidelines and the National Research Council's Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The mice were anaesthetised with an intraperitoneally injected combination of medetomidine (0.3 mg/kg; Nippon Zenyaku Kogyo Co. Ltd., Fukushima, Japan), midazolam (4.0 mg/kg; Maruishi Pharmaceutical. Co., Ltd. Osaka, Japan) and butorphanol (5.0 mg/kg; Meiji Seika Pharma Co., Ltd., Kumamoto, Japan).

Weight and blood glucose measurements

The weight and blood glucose levels of the mice were measured on the day before surgery. A blood sample (2 μL) was collected from the tail vein, and blood glucose levels were measured using a small‐animal blood glucose meter (LAB Gluco; Research & Innovation Japan Inc., Tokyo, Japan) following the manufacturer's instructions.

Fungal strain

T. mentagrophytes (IFM 65887)—a strain maintained at Chiba University Institute of Medical Mycology Research Center (Chiba, Japan)—was subcultured for 4 weeks at 26°C using Sabouraud dextrose agar (Becton, Dickinson and Company, NJ, USA) to produce microconidia. All experiments using T. mentagrophytes were conducted in a Biosafety Level 2 (BSL‐2) Laboratory.

Subsequently, the microconidia of T. mentagrophytes were collected and incubated in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) to produce a microconidial suspension with a cell density of 1.5 × 105 conidia/20 μL, as determined using a haemocytometer.

Establishment of excisional wound healing model and induction of delayed wound healing by fungus

A murine excisional wound healing model was established using a previously described method with slight modifications. 27 , 28 For this purpose, the operative region of the anaesthetised mouse was prepared by removing the fur with clippers from the base of the neck to a point 3 cm down the back, between the two shoulder blades. Subsequently, a 4 mm biopsy punch (KAI MEDICAL, Tokyo, Japan) was used to create a full‐thickness wound on the dorsum of each mouse.

A chamber was created for the application of the microconidial suspension using a splint following a previously described method 28 ; one side of a silicone splint was attached to an adhesive (Histoacryl Flexible; B Braun Medical Inc., Bethlehem, PA, USA) to centre the splint over the wound. The splint was anchored by 6‐0 nylon sutures to ensure proper positioning. The microconidial suspension (20 μL) or vehicle (PBS only) was applied to the wounds and covered with a transparent occlusive dressing (Tegaderm; 3 M, St. Paul, MN, USA). The fungal suspension or PBS was replaced every 2 days following skin excision. A ruler was placed beside the splint, and macroscopic photographs were taken at 2‐day intervals until wound closure was achieved.

The wound closure rate (expressed as a percentage of the initial wound area on day 0) was quantified by measuring the wound area at 2‐day intervals for 14 days. Three mice in each group were euthanized 7 and 14 days after wounding, and the wounds were biopsied using a 5 mm biopsy punch. Preliminary murine wound healing model experiments using C57BL/6 and ob/ob mice were compared in the following groups: C57BL/6 group (PBS only; n = 4–10) and ob/ob group (PBS only; n = 3–10). Delayed wound healing using the Trichophyton spore solution on ob/ob mice was compared in the following groups: ob/ob group (PBS only; n = 3) and ob/ob + TM group (T. mentagrophytes applied; n = 3).

Sample collection and processing

The mice were euthanized on postoperative days 7 and 14, and skin tissue from the entire wound area, including the wound edges, was collected using a 5 mm biopsy punch (KAI MEDICAL, Tokyo, Japan). The collected tissue was excised in the middle using a scalpel, and the right half was used for histopathological analysis. The left half of the wound biopsy tissue on postoperative day 7 was snap frozen and processed for mRNA analysis.

Pathological sections

The wound biopsy tissue was excised centrally, fixed with 20% formalin and stored overnight at approximately 26°C. Next, the tissues were briefly washed with 70% ethanol followed by paraffin embedding and sectioning. The paraffin‐embedded skin tissues were sectioned (4 μm thick) using a Leica Rotary Microtome (Leica Biosystems, Nussloch, DE) and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (HE), Picro Sirius Red (PSR) and Grocott's Gomori methenamine silver (GMS). The sections were examined under a light microscope (BZ‐X800; Keyence, Osaka, Japan). The distribution and morphology of T. mentagrophytes were assessed in the wounds using Grocott's GMS staining.

Polarized light microscopy and imaging

The PSR stained tissue sections were analysed under a linear polarized light microscopy using BX51 (Olympus, Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with a digital camera (Basler PowerPack; LINX Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) and a rotating polarizer (U‐POT; Olympus) built into the condenser with a fixed analyser (U‐ANT; Olympus). The optimal exposure time was determined by acquiring a series of images while constant lighting was maintained and selecting an exposure time that allowed the yellow/red and green birefringence to be photographed as brightly as possible without the pixels saturating against the dark background. Semiquantitative analysis of collagen fibre types was performed using ImageJ software (version 1.54f, NIH). 29

Real‐time quantitative polymerase chain reaction analysis

Total RNA was prepared using TRI REAGENT (Molecular Research Center, OH, USA) and a DNA‐free DNA Removal kit (Ambion, CT, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. For cDNA synthesis, RNA was reverse transcribed using a High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed using THUNDERBIRD SYBR qPCR Mix (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) on an StepOnePlus real‐time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). The cycling conditions were 50°C for 2 min and 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min. Relative mRNA levels were normalized to mouse glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase mRNA and calculated using the comparative cycle threshold method (ΔΔCt). Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software (version 9.00 for Windows, USA). The significance of the differences was assessed using an unpaired t‐test or 2‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the Bonferroni post‐hoc test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM).

Characteristics of animals

Effect of diabetes on wound healing

The wounds were grossly healed on postoperative day 14 in C57BL/6 and ob/ob mice (Figure 2A). The comparative wound area proportion reflected significantly delayed healing in the ob/ob group compared with the C57BL/6 group on postoperative days 4 (p < 0.001) and 6 (p = 0.03; Figure 2B).

Histological evaluation revealed a marked accumulation of adipose tissue in the ob/ob group, indicating obesity. Moreover, only the ob/ob group exhibited partially incomplete epithelialization and immature subcutaneous tissue on postoperative day 7 (Figure 3A). Meanwhile, complete epithelialization was detected in both groups on postoperative day 14, comparable to the gross findings. However, some mice in the ob/ob group exhibited incomplete and thin epithelialization (Figure 3B).

Effect of T. mentagrophytes on wound healing

The wounds were completely closed on postoperative day 14 in the ob/ob + TM and ob/ob groups (Figure 4A). However, the comparative wound area analysis revealed significantly delayed healing in the ob/ob + TM group compared with the ob/ob group on postoperative days 6 (p = 0.02), 8 (p = 0.04) and 10 (p = 0.01), with the ob/ob + TM group experiencing wound closure 2–4 days later than the ob/ob group (Figure 4B).

(B) The rate of wound healing is expressed as a percentage of the wound area relative to day 0. Data are expressed as means ± SEM (n = 3/group). *p < 0.05 (two‐way analysis of variance with Bonferroni's test).

The formation of epithelium in the ob/ob and ob/ob + TM groups on postoperative day 14 further validated the gross findings (Figure 5A). Histopathological analysis revealed that the epithelium was markedly thinner and less complete in the ob/ob + TM group than in the ob/ob group on postoperative day 7 (Figure 5B). Although local inflammation subsided in both groups on postoperative day 14, some individuals in the ob/ob + TM group exhibited an accumulation of inflammatory cells (Figure 5C,D). The relative expression of mRNA of inflammatory cytokines showed that the expression of TNF‐α was significantly (p = 0.04) increased in the ob/ob + TM group compared to that of the ob/ob group. The mRNA expression of IL‐1β (p = 0.58) and IL‐10 (p = 0.86) in the ob/ob + TM group tended to be increased compared to that of the ob/ob group (Figure 5E).

Trichophyton spore‐induced changes in the maturation of collagen fibres

To further clarify the impact of T. mentagrophytes on wound healing, collagen fibres were histologically analysed on postoperative day 7 via PSR staining, comprising red‐stained collagen fibres on a yellow background. During wound healing, collagen fibres were newly generated at the wound edges (Figure 6A). However, the abundance of collagen fibres in the overall wound was significantly lower in the ob/ob + TM group than in the ob/ob group on postoperative days 7 (p = 0.05) and 14 (p = 0.03; Figure 6B). Observation of PSR staining using a standard polarization microscopy makes it possible to distinguish type III collagen fibres as blue to green and type I collagen fibres as yellow to red (Figure 6C). The total area of signal‐positive regions in the ob/ob + TM group tended to decrease compared to that of the ob/ob group (Figure 6D).

Trichophyton morphology in wounds

The Grocott's GMS staining analysis revealed black coloration in fungal tissue, whereas the background connective tissues stained blue. Residual spores were detected in the wounds exposed to T. mentagrophytes; in some cases, mycelia grew within the stratum corneum (Figure 7A). The T. mentagrophytes colonizing the tissues of the ob/ob + TM group exhibited partial elongation of mycelia in the wound bed on postoperative day 7. On postoperative day 14, the mycelium exhibited further growth and the parasite spread within the stratum corneum (sandwich sign) (Figure 7A).

DISCUSSION

This study histopathologically confirms the impact of fungi on the wound healing process and demonstrates that exposure of full‐thickness wounds to T. mentagrophytes in mice induces delayed wound healing, accompanied by reduced collagen fibre formation. Additionally, spores and elongated mycelia exist in the wound bed and along the wound edges following exposure. These findings suggest that T. mentagrophytes colonization and associated parasitization of wounds potentially contribute to delayed wound healing in patients with diabetes.

Several experimental models have been established that apply bacterial species frequently detected in chronic wounds and have proven useful for analysing the impact of bacterial biofilms on wounds. For example, Pseudomonas aeruginosa was applied to a 6‐mm‐diameter wound in db/db mice, with approximately half of the cases exhibiting complete healing within 4 to 6 weeks after beginning bacterial inoculation; all cases exhibited complete healing by week 8. 25 , 26 Additionally, skin tissues collected on day 28 exhibit partial and incomplete epithelial formation and microabscesses containing inflammatory cells. Meanwhile, applying Staphylococcus aureus to a wound (5‐mm diameter) in mice with streptozotocin‐induced type 1 diabetes caused the infected wound to remain open until the last day of the experiment (day 9), with no granulation tissue observed. 24 Moreover, the association between inflammatory cytokines and the healing of wounds infected with a mixed suspension of Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa was analysed in C57BL/6 mice. 30 The infected wounds healed by postoperative day 12, whereas the control group healed by postoperative day 8. Moreover, the mRNA expression of pro‐inflammatory cytokines was upregulated on postoperative days 2 and 7, indicating prolonged inflammation potentially contributing to delayed healing. In addition, it is known that the overexpression of typical inflammatory cytokines such as TNF‐α and IL‐1β, which are derived from M1 macrophages, induces chronic inflammation and inhibits wound healing. 31 , 32

In our study, the ob/ob and the ob/ob + TM groups showed an accumulation of inflammatory cells in the superficial layers of the wound on postoperative day 7. Local inflammation in the entire neoplastic tissue was reduced on postoperative day 14; however, accumulated inflammatory cells persisted in some cases in the ob/ob + TM group. Additionally, at postoperative day 7, a time when wound healing is significantly delayed, the ob/ob + TM group showed a significant increase in inflammatory cytokine mRNA expression compared with the ob/ob group. Hence, local inflammation correlates with the T. mentagrophytes‐induced delay in wound healing. However, the associated inflammatory profile may be distinct from that reported in bacteria‐induced delayed wound healing. Furthermore, evaluations based on gross observations may lack quantitative precision. Thus, additional validation through semiquantitative analysis using immunohistochemical staining of inflammatory markers is required. Moreover, examination of the histological alterations occurring between postoperative days 6 and 10—a critical period for delayed wound healing—is warranted.

PSR staining is an effective pathological method for assessing wound granulation. In the current study, the wounds appeared to be re‐epithelialized in the ob/ob + TM group on postoperative day 7; however, collagen fibre formation in the subcutaneous tissue was incomplete. A comparable trend was also identified in the results of the quantitative analysis of collagen fibres in the regenerated tissue by type (Figure S1). Immature collagen fibres persisted on postoperative day 14, following the resolution of the inflammatory response. Consistently, GMS staining revealed daily fungal growth. Similar results have been observed in experiments with the hind limb dermis of alloxan‐induced diabetic mice inoculated with C. albicans and observed for 21 days, exhibiting considerably fewer collagen fibres 21 days post‐inoculation than 7 days. 33 This is consistent with the growth pattern of C. albicans in the skin, as reflected by the periodic acid‐Schiff‐stained images. The destruction of collagen fibres may involve a secreted aspartyl protease, regulating C. albicans infection. Similarly, although collagen fibre deposition should increase as the wound approaches healing, T. mentagrophytes proliferated in the wound bed and in the post‐healing stratum corneum. Trichophyton spp. produce a proteolytic enzyme, keratinase, that degrades keratin, a component of the stratum corneum. This enables Trichophyton spp. to breach the stratum corneum, a physical barrier, and serves as a nutrient source for the fungi. Advances in genetic modification experiments in mycological research have facilitated a clearer understanding of the nature of this keratinase. T. mentagrophytes secretes several endoproteases, including subtilisin and fungalysins. However, fungalysin (a metalloprotease) also plays a major role in pathogenicity. 34 , 35 Collagen and laminin are crucial in tissue formation, maturation and physical stability. 36 , 37 The endoproteases secreted by Trichophyton spp. degrade keratin, type III collagen, type IV procollagen and laminin. 38 , 39 In this experiment, we observed a marked deficiency in the formation and maturation of granulation tissue; however, the characteristic chronic inflammation, typical of a bacterial‐infected wound, was absent. GMS‐stained images further revealed that T. mentagrophytes remained and proliferated within the stratum corneum. This delayed wound healing caused by T. mentagrophytes is speculated to involve proteases secreted during mycelial elongation. However, further studies are required to validate the effects of Trichophyton spp.‐derived proteases on wound healing.

This study has several limitations. Although ob/ob mice are commonly used as models for diabetic diseases, the current results should be assessed in other obese models, including the db/db, Akita and streptozotocin‐induced diabetic mice (type 1 diabetes mellitus). 40 Additionally, the results suggest that T. mentagrophytes may delay wound healing by inhibiting the formation of collagen fibres; however, the detailed mechanism underlying this delay remains unclear, and further validation of the correlation between inflammation and the T. mentagrophytes‐derived enzymes is needed.

CONCLUSIONS

In this study, we demonstrated a fungal infection pattern in the wound bed and stratum corneum in ob/ob mice that led to significantly delayed wound healing and poor regeneration of collagen fibres. Hence, in addition to the prolonged inflammation previously reported in bacteria‐infected wounds, we propose the involvement of unique T. mentagrophytes characteristics in inducing delayed wound healing. These findings highlight the importance of tinea pedis as a target for wound treatment and care. Furthermore, this T. mentagrophytes‐induced delay in murine wound healing contributes to the mechanisms underlying intractable DFUs.
