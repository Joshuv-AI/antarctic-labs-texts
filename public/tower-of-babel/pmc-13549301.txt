Incident Falls Across Concordant and Discordant Profiles of Concerns About Falling and Functional Mobility in Older Adults: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Fangrong Fei, Jieming Zhong, Lihua Guo, Liuqing You, Ming Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConcerns about falling and impaired functional mobility are established fall-risk indicators, but prospective fall risk is less clear when subjective concerns and objective mobility are discordant. We compared incident falls across joint profiles of concerns about falling and Timed Up and Go (TUG) performance, with particular attention to older adults with fast TUG performance and concerns versus those with slow TUG performance and no concerns.MethodsThis one-year prospective cohort study included 2,445 community-dwelling adults aged 60–79 years in Zhejiang Province, China. TUG performance was classified as fast (<12 s) or slow (≥12 s), and concerns about falling were assessed using a single yes/no item. Crossing the two measures produced four psychophysical groups. Self-reported falls were recorded on daily calendars and ascertained monthly. Cox regression estimated adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) for time to first fall. Sensitivity analyses used the Falls Efficacy Scale-International (FES-I), excluded participants with a baseline fall history, and applied the guideline-aligned TUG >15 s threshold.ResultsDuring follow-up, 232 participants (9.5%) reported at least one fall; 2,420 (98.98%) completed follow-up. Single-item concerns (adjusted HR 1.60, 95% CI 1.23–2.09) and slow TUG performance (HR 1.46, 95% CI 1.10–1.92) were independently associated with incident falls. Using the slow-TUG/no-concern group as the direct reference, the fast-TUG/concern group did not differ significantly (HR 1.08, 95% CI 0.72–1.63), whereas the slow-TUG/concern group had a higher hazard (HR 1.96, 95% CI 1.29–2.97). The discordant groups also did not differ after excluding baseline fallers (HR 0.97, 95% CI 0.62–1.52) or after additionally applying the TUG >15 s threshold (HR 0.87, 95% CI 0.46–1.63).ConclusionConcerns about falling and TUG performance provided complementary information about subsequent falls. The two discordant profiles were not significantly separated. These findings support considering concerns alongside mobility performance rather than relying on either measure alone.

Introduction

Falls are the second leading cause of unintentional injury deaths worldwide,1 and 29% of people aged 65 years or older who live in the community experience a fall every year, with the proportion increasing with age.2 Beyond their immediate physical consequences—including fractures, head injuries, and functional decline,3–5 falls are a leading cause of disability, institutionalisation, and fall-related mortality in this population.6–8 The global burden of falls is projected to intensify as populations age, with a recent Global Burden of Disease analysis reporting approximately 45.7 million new fall events worldwide in 2021 alone, underscoring the need to identify modifiable risk factors and develop targeted preventive strategies.9

Among the established risk factors for falls, objective physical function has long been central to clinical assessment. The 2022 World Guidelines for Falls Prevention and Management recommend gait and balance assessment, including measures such as the TUG test, as components of fall-risk evaluation.10 In parallel, concerns about falling, defined as persistent concern about falling, have emerged as a highly prevalent psychological risk factor, with a global pooled prevalence of approximately 50% among community-dwelling older adults.11 Prospective studies and meta-analyses have consistently shown that concerns about falling predict incident falls after adjustment for physical function and other covariates.12,13 Other evidence suggests that such concerns may be associated with altered gait, lower habitual physical activity, and functional decline, potentially contributing to a self-reinforcing cycle of concern and vulnerability.14,15

Although impaired mobility and concerns about falling are both recognised risk indicators, an important practical question arises when the two measures disagree. Some older adults complete a brief mobility test within the expected range yet report concerns, whereas others perform slowly but report no concerns. Previous studies have shown that perceived and physiological fall risk can be discordant and that both dimensions may contribute independent information,16,17 but prospective evidence directly comparing these discordant profiles remains limited, particularly in community-dwelling Chinese populations. We therefore conducted a one-year prospective cohort study and compared incident falls across four strata created by crossing TUG performance with concern status. Our primary interest was the direct comparison between the fast-TUG/concern and slow-TUG/no-concern groups. We hypothesised that the four joint profiles would differ in fall risk, with the combined slow-TUG/concern profile expected to have the highest risk; no directional superiority was assumed between the two discordant profiles.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This prospective cohort study was conducted in Zhejiang Province, eastern China. Details of the baseline survey have been reported previously.18 Briefly, six prefecture-level cities were selected to reflect geographic and economic variation. Within each city, one urban district and one county were selected, followed by subdistricts or townships and communities. Participants were recruited within selected communities according to age- and sex-specific targets. The lower age limit of 60 years followed the conventional definition of older adults used in the provincial study protocol19 and enabled assessment of fall risk beginning in early older age.

The participants were required to meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) aged 60~79 years (2) ability to walk independently and complete sit-to-stand maneuvers. (3) no anticipated relocation within 1 year. Participants with critical illness or communication impairments, such as intellectual disabilities, aphasia and deafness, which were assessed by investigators through communication and information provided by family members, were excluded from the study. A total of 2,445 participants with complete baseline TUG and concerns-about-falling data formed the analytic cohort (Figure 1). Baseline assessments were conducted between June 2022 and June 2023, and falls were followed for approximately 12 months between July 2022 and July 2024. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention (AF/SC-06/01.0:2022–030-01).

Assessment of Functional Mobility

Functional mobility was assessed using the TUG test.20 Participants rose from an armchair, walked 3 m at their usual pace, turned, returned to the chair, and sat down. Two trials were performed and the mean time was recorded; shorter times indicated better mobility. For the primary analysis, TUG was classified as fast (<12 s) or slow (≥12 s), consistent with the 12-s threshold used in the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention STEADI assessment.21 Because cut-offs vary across guidance and populations, a sensitivity analysis used TUG >15 s to indicate impaired gait and balance, as proposed as an alternative threshold in the World Guidelines.10 TUG was treated as an indicator of functional mobility rather than a comprehensive measure of overall physical capacity.

Assessment of Concerns About Falling

Concerns about falling were assessed using two complementary approaches. The primary exposure was a direct yes/no item asking whether the participant was concerned about falling. A single item was selected because it is feasible for community screening and corresponds to the concern question included in guideline-based case finding.10 The 16-item FES-I was also administered to assess concern across physical and social activities, with total scores ranging from 16 to 64; higher scores indicate greater concern.22 For sensitivity analysis, scores were classified as high concern (≥28) or low-to-moderate concern (<28), following published FES-I categories.23

Psychophysical Stratification

TUG category and concern status were crossed to form four mutually exclusive study groups: fn, fast TUG and no concern; sn, slow TUG and no concern; fc, fast TUG and concern; and sc, slow TUG and concern. The single-item definition and TUG <12 versus ≥12 s classification constituted the primary analysis. The same framework was repeated using FES-I <28 versus ≥28. Additional sensitivity analyses first excluded participants who reported a fall during the 12 months before baseline and then, within that restricted cohort, repeated the stratification using TUG ≤15 versus >15 s.

Fall Ascertainment and Follow-Up

A fall was defined as an unexpected event in which the participant came to rest on the ground, floor, or a lower level. Participants were asked to record the date of every fall on a daily calendar, regardless of injury. Trained staff ascertained self-reported falls monthly by telephone or face-to-face contact. The outcome was time from baseline to the first reported fall. Participants without a fall were followed until the end of their planned follow-up or their last successful contact.

Follow-up was considered incomplete when staff were unable to contact a participant for three consecutive months. These participants were treated as lost to follow-up and censored at the date of the last successful contact. Because vital status could not be ascertained among participants who remained unreachable, deaths could not be separated from other reasons for loss to follow-up and a competing-risk analysis was not performed. A separate quantitative measure of adherence to daily calendar recording was not available.

Covariates

Covariates included age group (60–69 or 70–79 years), sex, residential area (urban or rural), fall history during the 12 months before baseline (yes or no), and multimorbidity (0, 1, or ≥2 chronic conditions). Fall history was obtained by asking whether the participant had experienced an unintentional fall in the preceding 12 months. Chronic disease history-including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, osteoporosis, visual impairment, and vertigo-was based on participant report of a previous diagnosis and was not verified against medical records.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarised as counts and percentages for categorical variables and means with standard deviations for continuous variables. Kaplan-Meier curves were used to display unadjusted fall-free survival across the four psychophysical groups, with numbers at risk shown at selected time points.24 Fall-free survival at 365 days was estimated for each group.

Cox proportional hazards regression estimated HRs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for time to first fall.25 First, single-item concern status and TUG category were entered simultaneously to estimate their independent associations, with adjustment for age, sex, residential area, fall history, and multimorbidity. Second, the four psychophysical groups were entered as a categorical exposure. The sn group was specified as the reference so that the primary contrast directly estimated the fc-versus-sn comparison, rather than inferring it from separate comparisons with another reference group. Primary models were adjusted for the same covariates. Models restricted to participants without a baseline fall history omitted fall history from the adjustment set.

All 2,445 participants had complete exposure and covariate data. Participants lost to follow-up contributed observed person-time and were right-censored at their last contact; no outcome value was imputed. Statistical tests were two-sided, with P<0.05 indicating statistical significance. Analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.2.

Results

Participant Flow and Baseline Characteristics

The analytic cohort comprised 2,445 participants. Of these, 1,719 (70.3%) reported no concern on the single-item measure and 726 (29.7%) reported concern; 1,690 (69.1%) had TUG <12 s and 755 (30.9%) had TUG ≥12 s. Discussion

In this prospective cohort, single-item concerns about falling and slower TUG performance were independently associated with incident falls. However, the primary direct comparison showed no significant difference between participants with fast TUG performance and concerns and those with slow TUG performance but no concerns. The two discordant profiles also were again not clearly separated after excluding participants with a baseline fall history. Participants with both slow TUG performance and concerns had the highest fall hazard in the primary analysis. The FES-I analysis similarly did not clearly distinguish the discordant profiles. Collectively, these findings indicate that concerns about falling and functional mobility provide complementary information, but do not establish that either discordant profile carries a greater fall hazard than the other.

The crude proportion of participants experiencing at least one fall during follow-up was 9.5% (232/2,445), broadly comparable to the 11.53% of participants (282/2,445) who reported a fall in the year before baseline. Both proportions were lower than the approximately one-third often reported among community-dwelling adults aged ≥65 years.2 This may partly reflect the eligibility criteria, which selected a relatively young (60–79 years), independently mobile cohort, and limits generalisability to adults aged ≥80 years or those with substantial frailty. Nevertheless, the independent association between the single-item concern measure and incident falls was consistent with previous evidence. A meta-analysis by Ellmers et al, including 53 studies and more than 75,000 participants, reported a pooled odds ratio of 1.60 for single-item measures of concerns about falling.12 Asai et al similarly found that concerns predicted falls across fall-history subgroups after multivariable adjustment,13 while a broader meta-analysis also identified concerns about falling as an important risk factor for falls among community-dwelling older adults.26 These separate associations confirm that both physical and psychological dimensions carry relevant information, but do not determine how risk is distributed when the two dimensions are discordant.

The joint-profile analysis showed that the fc and sn profiles could not be clearly distinguished using the present data. At the same time, the higher hazard in the sc group suggests that the coexistence of concerns and impaired mobility may identify a more vulnerable subgroup, without establishing a synergistic interaction. These findings extend earlier studies using different analytical approaches. Delbaere et al constructed discordant profiles using a multidomain Physiological Profile Assessment and classification-tree-derived FES-I thresholds and suggested that the high-physiological/low-perceived “stoic” group was relatively protected despite its physiological risk,16 whereas our direct comparison did not separate the corresponding discordant profiles. Garbin and Fisher, using SPPB balance performance and a single worry question, found no interaction between balance and concerns but showed that each independently contributed to future falls.17 The present study extends this literature through a large Chinese community cohort, pragmatic TUG and single-item classifications, direct estimation of the discordant-group contrast, and sensitivity analyses addressing prior falls and published thresholds. Although the FES-I ≥28 threshold classified a substantially larger proportion of participants as having elevated concerns than the single-item question (62.2% vs 29.7%), the fc and sn profiles were not clearly separated under either measure. This consistency across two different operationalisations of concerns supports the robustness of the central finding.

Several pathways could contribute to the independent information provided by concerns. Concerns have been associated with cautious or less adaptable gait patterns that may not be captured by the completion time of a brief TUG test.15 They may also be accompanied by activity avoidance, reduced exposure to everyday movement, and progressive deconditioning or frailty.27–29 Conversely, older adults with impaired mobility but little concern may differ in their habitual activity and fall-related exposure, potentially altering their opportunities to experience a fall.30 However, gait characteristics, habitual physical activity, environmental exposure, and activity restriction were not measured in this study; these explanations therefore remain speculative, and mediation or causal pathways cannot be inferred.

The findings are relevant to current fall-risk case finding. The World Guidelines include worries or concerns about falling among three key screening questions and permit TUG >15 s as an alternative indicator of impaired gait and balance.10 In the guideline algorithm, an older adult without a recent severe fall who reports concern but has TUG ≤15 s may ultimately be assigned a lower risk category than a person with TUG >15 s but no concern. In our sensitivity cohort without a baseline fall history, these discordant profiles were not clearly separated. This finding should be interpreted cautiously because the >15-s threshold produced a relatively small sn subgroup (n=87), resulting in limited precision and a wide confidence interval. Moreover, our study did not reproduce all elements of the guideline algorithm, including perceived unsteadiness, frailty, fall severity, syncope, and the recommended multifactorial assessment. The result therefore neither validates nor challenges the algorithm, but suggests that concerns may provide information complementary to TUG performance and may warrant consideration within a broader assessment even when TUG performance is relatively preserved.31 As this observational study evaluated neither the complete algorithm nor any intervention, the effectiveness of additional assessment or concern-targeted treatment remains to be established in future prospective studies.

Strengths of this study include its prospective design, monthly fall ascertainment, high follow-up completion, presentation of absolute event counts and 365-day fall-free survival, and direct comparison of the two discordant profiles. The sensitivity analysis excluding baseline fallers reduced the possibility that baseline concerns merely reflected a recent fall, and the TUG >15 s analysis evaluated robustness to a guideline threshold.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the selected sample and low event proportion limit generalisability and reduced the precision of subgroup comparisons. No a priori power calculation was conducted specifically for the discordant-group contrast; the TUG >15 s analysis contained only 87 sn and 59 sc participants, producing wide CIs. The null comparisons should therefore not be interpreted as equivalence. Second, falls, fall history, and chronic conditions were self-reported, and disease histories were not verified against medical records. Third, although eligibility included the ability to communicate, no standardised cognitive instrument was administered; subtle cognitive impairment may have confounded both TUG performance and concerns. Residual confounding by medication use, depression, activity exposure, and environmental hazards is also possible. Fourth, 25 participants were lost after being unreachable for three consecutive months. Their vital status was unknown, so deaths could not be modelled as competing events; informative censoring cannot be excluded despite the high completion rate. Fifth, dichotomising TUG and FES-I reduced information and produced unequal group sizes. Finally, the one-year follow-up did not capture longer-term changes in concerns, mobility, or frailty.

Conclusion

Concerns about falling and slower TUG performance were independently associated with incident falls, but fall risk was not significantly different between the two discordant psychophysical profiles. The combined adverse profile showed the highest risk in the primary and baseline-fall-free analyses. These findings support considering subjective concerns alongside functional mobility in fall-risk assessment.
