Barriers and facilitators to accessing services for neurodevelopmental disorders among the carers of individuals from Black, Asian and minority ethnic groups in the UK: a scoping review
Authors: Britzer Paul Vincent, Zahra Maryam, Nasreen Ali, Matthew Bushnell, Elizabeth Elliott, Gurch Randhawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify the barriers and facilitators to accessing services for neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) among the carers of individuals from Black, Asian and minority ethnic groups in the UK.DesignA scoping review.Data sourcesSeven databases: PubMed, Cochrane, MEDLINE Full Text, APA PsycINFO, Scopus, CINAHL and Web of Science were searched for relevant studies up to the end of March 2025. Titles and abstracts and, where appropriate, full-text articles were screened based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Two reviewers independently screened the 6454 studies, with data extraction carried out by two reviewers. A narrative synthesis of the findings was undertaken.FindingsA total of 15 studies were included in the review, the majority of which were qualitative (n=9; 60%). Among the Black, Asian and minority ethnic populations represented, South Asians comprised the predominant group. The review underscores that the barriers faced by Black, Asian and minority ethnic communities are multifactorial, which includes: (1) language and communication barriers, (2) cultural and religious influences, (3) stigma, (4) formal and informal support systems and (5) experience with healthcare services.ConclusionAccess to neurodevelopmental care for carers from Black, Asian and minority ethnic groups in the UK is shaped by complex, multifactorial barriers. These communities are not homogeneous, and evidence gaps make it unclear whether challenges are shared or distinct. Greater understanding is needed to inform culturally appropriate, evidence-based policy and practice. Further research is essential to address disparities and improve equitable access across all groups.

Introduction

Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD), attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and intellectual disabilities (IDs), represent a significant global public health concern affecting over 52.9 million children under 5 years worldwide in 2016, with long-lasting impacts on individuals and their families.1 In the UK, NDDs such as ASD, ADHD and ID also impose a significant burden.24 Recent estimates suggest that approximately 1 in 57 children in England are diagnosed with autism, with diagnoses rising steadily due to increased awareness and improved detection.2 ADHD affects around 5% of children and 2.5% of adults, making it one of the most common childhood psychiatric conditions.3 In addition, ID is estimated to affect about 2% of the UK population.4 With those affected by NDDs experiencing substantial health inequalities, including but not limited to general health status, heart disease, physical impairment, mental health, dementia, injuries, accidents and falls, overall quality of life, access to healthcare and social support services and broader societal challenges.46

While the prevalence of NDDs is higher among the children from Black, Asian and minority ethnic backgrounds, they are diagnosed less frequently and at a later stage compared with their white counterparts, suggesting potential underdiagnosis, inequities in access to diagnostic services and specialist care.2 7 8 Hence, while there is an increasing demand for appropriate services among this particular population, disparities in access persist.9 Without a diagnosis or appropriate support, parents report increased levels of stress, anxiety and even depression due to the constant emotional and physical demands of caring for a child with complex needs.10 11 Children, in turn, may experience worsening behavioural or emotional difficulties, affecting their ability to form relationships and succeed in school, which exacerbates the strain on families.12 13 Parents often describe feeling blamed or judged by others, especially when their child’s behaviour is misunderstood, which can lead to social withdrawal.14 Financial pressures are also common, as many family members who provide care reduce their working hours or give up employment entirely to provide full-time care.15 16 Without timely help, these families can find themselves stuck in a cycle of crisis, with little hope or direction for the future.17 18

Studies show that people from Black, Asian and minority ethnic groups are more likely to experience delays in diagnosis, limited access to specialist support and culturally insensitive care.1921 However, several scoping and systematic reviews examining the barriers to access, utilisation and experiences of services for NDDs have been limited to the perspectives of individuals themselves or the experiences of parents who do not speak English.2225 Hence, there is a gap in reviews focusing on the barriers and facilitators towards accessing services for NDDs among the carers from the Black, Asian and minority ethnic groups in the UK.2225

Several qualitative and quantitative studies have explored the experiences of carers (including families and service providers) from Black, Asian and minority ethnic groups in accessing services for NDDs in the UK. However, the evidence remains fragmented. Mapping and synthesising this body of literature is crucial to provide a comprehensive overview of the barriers and facilitators these carers encounter.2629 A scoping review will help to identify gaps in knowledge, highlight culturally specific needs and inform service development, policy formulation and future research initiatives aimed at promoting equitable healthcare access for all.2629 Hence, with such a gap in evidence and significance of that evidence, the current scoping review aims to identify the barriers and facilitators toward accessing services for NDDs among the carers from the Black, Asian and minority ethnic groups in the UK.

Methods

A preliminary search in Google Scholar, CINAHL and PubMed revealed no scoping or systematic review on the barriers and facilitators toward accessing services for NDDs among the carers from the Black, Asian and minority ethnic groups in the UK. Hence, following this, the scoping review commenced.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research. However, stakeholders from the local council and not-for-profit organisation who have worked with the community in Luton and had an understanding of the services offered for NDDs in Luton were involved in the design and reporting of this review. We also acknowledge the valuable involvement and contributions of the Black Health Equity Group and Luton Borough Council’s Family First Programme, whose insights helped shape our early understanding of community needs and priorities.

Eligibility criteria

To be considered for this review, studies were required to meet the following criteria:

Population: studies were eligible if they focused on individuals from Black, Asian and minority ethnic groups in the UK who provided care for people with NDDs. For this review, carers included family members, informal carers and service providers. Studies conducted in settings such as healthcare institutions, academic environments or home-based care or where support was delivered by family members or carers were considered.

For this review, carers included family members, informal carers and service providers. Studies conducted in settings such as healthcare institutions, academic environments or home-based care or where support was delivered by family members or carers were considered.

Concept: studies were included if they explored barriers or facilitators to accessing services related to NDDs. According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (American Psychiatric Association, 2015), NDDs are conditions that emerge during the developmental period and are marked by deficits that interfere with social, personal, academic or occupational functioning. This category encompasses disorders such as ASD, ADHD, learning disorders, motor disorders, communication disorders and ID. However, for the purpose of this review, we will include only ASD, ADHD and ID as they were the most commonly diagnosed NDDs in the UK and the reason to inform an upcoming qualitative study.24

Studies were excluded if the participants were individuals themselves diagnosed with NDDs or if the study included Black, Asian and minority ethnic groups but did not report findings specific to these populations. Additionally, conference posters, conference presentations, books and book chapters, unpublished theses, editorials and commentaries were excluded from the review.

Data sources and search strategies

We scoped for relevant systematic and scoping reviews to identify the most appropriate and commonly used databases.3034 Following this, we identified seven databases, namely: PubMed, Cochrane, MEDLINE full-text, APA Psyc INFO, Scopus, CINAHL and Web of Science. The search terms were then developed based on the population, concept and context framework35 in relevance to the aim of the review. Population: Black, Asian and minority ethnic groups; concept: barriers, facilitators, access and NDDs; context: United Kingdom. Two of the authors (BPV and ZM) worked in the database to identify the index terms to use in the search string. While the search terms used in all seven databases were almost similar, their indexing was tailored to the needs of each database (see online supplemental file 1). In all the databases, the search was limited to the end of March 2025. Terms such as ‘learning disorder’, ‘communication disorder’ and ‘language disorder’ were added to the search strategy as they often could co-occur in individuals with ASD, ADHD and ID. However, we did not include studies that addressed those among individuals who did not have ASD, ADHD or ID.

Data screening

Using the search strategy, a total of 6454 records were retrieved from the seven databases. These were subsequently exported to Rayyan software (https://www.rayyan.ai), where 956 duplicate records were removed. The remaining 5498 studies underwent double-blinded screening. Discrepancies arose between the two primary reviewers (BPV and ZM) regarding 11 studies, which were resolved through discussion with a third independent reviewer (NA). Following title and abstract screening, 59 studies were selected for full-text review. Of these, 36 studies involved populations not meeting the inclusion criteria, five were identified as commentaries, and three focused on the development of assessment instruments. Ultimately, 15 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final review (see figure 1), with data extraction carried out by a two reviewers (BPV and ZM).

Positionality statement of the authors

As researchers, we recognise that our unique personal backgrounds, training and experiences shape the decisions we make throughout this review, from defining the research questions to interpreting findings. The authors of this review come from diverse professional and ethnic backgrounds, including academia, local councils and a charity organisation, with one author based outside the UK. This diversity has helped us approach the review with greater reflexivity and minimise potential biases, particularly given the focus on Black, Asian and ethnic minority groups living in the UK. The conception of the review and the development of eligibility criteria were led by a member of the charity organisation working closely with the local community in Luton, in collaboration with the local council’s public health specialist and academic advisors. However, to ensure rigour and reduce bias, the entire research team was involved throughout the process, with four reviewers contributing to the development of the search strategy, screening, data synthesis and writing of the findings. The findings of this review will inform the topic guide for subsequent qualitative data collection in the local setting and help identify priority areas for service improvement.

Data synthesis

The narrative synthesis approach outlined by Popay et al was employed to synthesise data extracted from both quantitative and qualitative studies.36 This method allows the integration of findings across different study designs within a review. The process followed four key stages: (1) conducting a systematic search, (2) organising and clustering the included studies, (3) providing a textual description of each study and (4) assessing and interpreting the findings. We initially extracted information from the 15 studies to draft the evidence table (see online supplemental file 2). Two reviewers were involved in the data synthesis stage (BPV and ZM), and a third reviewer (NA) was involved if there were any discrepancies in the synthesis process or outcome. Quantitative results were organised under relevant themes for each study, while the qualitative data were analysed using NVivo V.21 software (Lumivero, 2021; www.lumivero.com). The qualitative codes were then reviewed in relation to the quantitative results to identify areas of similarity or difference. During this comparative analysis, key aspects such as study design, outcomes, research settings and participant demographics were taken into account to understand their connections better. A thorough synthesis of both quantitative and qualitative evidence was conducted to provide a comprehensive overview of the factors that either supported or contradicted each other across studies.

Findings

Summary of the studies

Following the screening, 15 studies were included in the scoping review (figure 1): nine qualitative (60%), four quantitative (26.7%) and two mixed methods (13.3%) (see online supplemental file 2). Most studies were conducted in community settings (73.3%), with others in home or combined environments. The majority of the study participants were from South Asian populations (India, Pakistan, Bangladesh), although two studies broadly referred to ‘ethnic minority’ groups without specific breakdowns. From the narrative synthesis, five key themes emerged: (1) language and communication barriers, (2) cultural and religious influences, (3) stigma, (4) formal and informal support systems and (5) experience with healthcare services, providing a comprehensive view of the challenges faced by the Black, Asian and minority ethnic carers (see online supplemental file 2). To ensure transparency in our findings, we retained the terminology used by the original studies to describe their participants—for example, terms such as South Asians, younger generation, older and first-generation, second-generation and individuals of African origin.

Language and communication barriers

Many carers who did not speak English as a first language struggled to access services, feeling excluded and unsupported.3741 A Bangladeshi mother who cared for her child with ID shared, “I don’t know how to approach it because of my language problems”,38 while an Indian wife who cared for her husband with ID said, “They never had a translator there at the meetings for me”.37 In relation to the need for translators, service providers highlighted the lack of professional interpreters, poor interpretation quality and cultural misunderstandings as key barriers to effective communication and care.41 However, another study showed that older and first-generation immigrants relied on family for interpretation, while second-generation South Asian carers, being bilingual and better educated, navigated services more confidently.38 To make the best use of this, service providers suggested involving the younger generation/family members who understand the culture and the language would bridge this gap, yet with some limitations. One of the service providers, providing service to children with ID, said: “Sometimes you can ask family members to translate… you get very good communication, and you feel you get an understanding of the situation from the family members, but at the same time… they have their own agenda really.”.38 In addition to the lack of access to professional interpreters, direct translations were unavailable or inappropriate within their cultural context. Terminology such as “intellectual disability” was rarely used; instead, carers often described conditions in more colloquial or culturally familiar terms such as “a little slow” or “has a problem with the brain”,38 highlighting the complexity of using translation.

Cultural and religious influences

Carers, especially from Pakistani and Bangladeshi backgrounds, saw marriage as a way to fulfil cultural expectations and secure future care for family members with NDDs. One carer of an individual with IDs on postschool transition shared, “Hopefully, when he is 30, he can get married and have a family because that is in our Asian culture anyway”,38 while a Bangladeshi parent of a child with ID said, “So they think if I get them married… they might look after them… this is my duty”.42 However, a key concern for care providers is the conflict between families’ expectations of using marriage to secure care and professionals’ responsibility to ensure the individual’s right to consent. As one (white British) professional who had experience in providing service to individuals with NDDs noted, “The response of the family was to get him married and for his wife to look after him, and that was quite a different response to the way we would normally work. I think they then saw the services as being quite oppositional because we were tasked with the notion of asking, ‘was he able to consent to marriage?’”.41 This created tension between service providers and families, who felt that the services were challenging or questioned their approach to arranging marriage for their family member with IDs, particularly the issue of consent.

Families also often preferred self-management or faith healing, complicating service engagement. A carer caring for a child with developmental disabilities explained, “A lot of the time they tend to think that their own remedies will work”.40 Some families highlighted the perception of attributing disabilities to supernatural causes, leading to practices like faith healing or physical punishment, particularly in non-Western cultural contexts,40 41 43 which can create barriers to accepting professional care and support. An African immigrant mother caring for her child diagnosed with ASD said: “I took him somewhere and the woman told me he was possessed… I felt bad about it, that is a really bad word, you know, especially saying someone is demonic… Me and my husband decided to take him to Africa … but I was a bit scared to take him there […] in Africa when a child behaves like that and they don’t see a disability on you, well they will not understand, so they will beat him on the street.”.43 A white British service provider mentioned: “I feel as though I’m going to have more of a battle to persuade people to accept services, the families might feel that they want to deal with the problem themselves… They won’t accept it’s a long-term condition which needs long-term management and… you have this run for cures and faith healers.”.41

Religious beliefs played a central role in helping many families cope with their child’s disability, offering comfort, resilience and a sense that the child was a blessing or a test of faith.38 43 44 For some, though, it has been challenging to accept the will of God, religion provided strength and acceptance, framing the disability as God’s will and a source of spiritual growth. A Pakistani mother living in the UK and caring for her child with ID said: “They are God’s angels, they come into the world to get loved, they don’t harm anybody, just to get loved. They are sin-free. For everything, there is a reason. I don’t worry now, but sometimes, human nature, you wonder why this happens. Deep down, you wonder why God, but if God’s happy, I’m happy. These children with special needs are God’s angels… You are blessed with this child.”.38 However, sometimes religious interpretations also caused families to feel ashamed and become secretive about their situation.38 42 One of the Bangladeshi mothers caring for her child with ID said: “It [learning disability] is not recognised in the Bangladeshi community, and they will all fall into … thinking that this is some form of punishment [from God] or thinking that it’s a test in their life.”.42

Stigma

Stigma surrounding disability within South Asian and African communities often creates significant barriers to accessing care. Stigma within the community forces families to hide their child’s disability, making them reluctant to seek external help or support. Fear of judgement, ridicule and social exclusion creates a barrier to accessing services that could benefit both the child and the family.39 40 43 One African mother caring for her child diagnosed with ASD said: “At the end, all my business will be on the streets… they’ll go and discuss it, tell the next person, ‘oh look at that child… he doesn’t know anything, look at how he behaves’…. I was so embarrassed …”.43 Fear of gossip also made families worried about future prospects like marriage.40 Overall, stigma not only silenced families but also reinforced isolation and mistrust towards professional services, limiting access to much-needed care. In addition, one participant noted that due to stereotypes about close-knit Indian families, social workers assumed that they did not need help and failed to offer support.44

Formal and informal support

Respite care

Findings show that respite care access is a major issue, particularly for carers from Black, Asian and minority ethnic groups.3839 4548 Families reported long waiting times and limited availability, with one Pakistani father caring for his child with IDs noting: “We had a word with the social worker, and he said no, parents have no choice now, that’s what he said. He said places are limited; they can only send a child where they can ﬁnd a place. No, we don’t get much support”.38 Around 60% of Black, Asian and minority ethnic families did not receive any respite care.45 Awareness and use of respite care were lower among South Asians, with only 64% aware of respite care compared with 89% of carers from a white British background, and only 21% using it compared with 33% of white British.47 Cultural barriers also affected uptake; a Jamaican mother caring for her child with NDD shared: “You put out a dog and take it back. We don’t do family like that in my country”.45 Negative experiences with respite services further discouraged use. One South Asian mother caring for an adult with IDs said: “The person hadn’t been properly trained, and she locked [K] in the conservatory… you’re horrified at the things people do”.39

Family support

Support from extended family and friends is crucial but often limited,39 43 49 especially a study among British Indian and Indian groups showed lower Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support scores (tool designed to measure an individual’s perceived social support from family, friends and significant others), possibly due to stigma or denial of disability.49 However, monetary incentives facilitated family support in some cases. One of the mothers caring for her child diagnosed with IDs said: “[Her daughter with intellectual disabilities] goes to my aunt’s house every Saturday or Sunday, and they take her out. That’s because they get direct payments”.39 African immigrant mothers caring for a child with ASD also noted they would have more support back home, reducing their isolation.43

Social care

Over 70% of carers reported not having a social worker, and among those who did, only three found them helpful.45 One South Asian parent caring for their child said: “We have had absolutely no support or services whatsoever for the last 8 years… we had one social worker that would pay a social visit to mum once a month… he never offered us anything”.39 British Indian and Indian groups also scored significantly lower on social support compared with white British carers,49 and South Asian carers reported greater unmet needs for community services, particularly social services like day care, short-term care and home help services.47

Experience with healthcare services

Negative experiences with the services

Carers expressed dissatisfaction with services, often feeling disillusioned after repeated negative experiences.373944 50 A carer caring for an individual with mild and moderate intellectual disability explained: “Well, you stop using them… you think they weren’t helpful last time, what’s the point in going…”.37 Another South Asian parent caring for an adult with intellectual disabilities said: “I’ve just got no faith in the system at all… I’ve just not got any fight in me to fight another battle”.39 Carers also reported dissatisfaction with day care placements, citing concerns like “the wide age range… cleanliness and location”.38 Lack of trust in healthcare providers also emerged, with some parents attributing their child’s disability to medical negligence. One South Asian father caring for a child with intellectual disabilities stated: “By the bloody time my son was born, he was starved of oxygen… And you know what happens to the brain when it’s starved of oxygen, damage”.44 Within the same group, another father expressed suspicion about birth neglect, saying: “I am 70 per cent sure that this is birth neglect”.44 For Muslim South Asian families, a key barrier was feeling blamed by healthcare professionals for their child’s condition.44

Access and transition barriers

South Asian families face significant challenges in transitioning their children with disabilities from child to adult services. A major barrier is the lack of awareness and guidance about available services, leading to confusion and unmet needs.38 One mother caring for a child with mild and moderate intellectual disability shared: “When he left the hospital at the age of 16, he should have had a good transition to the adult services, but it didn’t happen”.37 The transition was often marked by insufficient support planning, with many parents unaware of any formal plans for their child’s future care. One South Asian mother caring for her child with intellectual disability explained: “We had a meeting at school… but because we decided he would stay until he was 19, we did not get any options”.38

Delay in access

South Asian families face significant delays in obtaining a diagnosis for their children’s developmental issues, often due to cultural barriers and a lack of awareness. British Indian parents, on average, received a diagnosis 6 months later than white British parents.49 One South Asian mother who cares for a child with intellectual disabilities explained, “Earlier, for 15 years, they told us when he grows up, he will get well”.44 Families were also identified to often seek help only when issues reached crisis point, as noted by a service provider: “The two families that I’ve worked with didn’t gain input from services until it was at crisis point”.41 These delays contribute to worsening conditions and emotional distress for families. Additionally, families from ethnic minority backgrounds, particularly those with caregivers not educated to degree level, have decreased access to early support.51 This is compounded by systemic issues within healthcare services that delay diagnosis and appropriate intervention, leading to longer times to receive a diagnosis compared with other ethnic groups.49

Discussion

This scoping review identified the barriers and facilitators to accessing services for NDDs among the carers from the Black, Asian and minority ethnic groups in the UK. Findings from the 15 studies reveal that carers from Black, Asian and minority ethnic groups in the UK face numerous barriers in accessing care for their children. Overall, we identified five overarching themes which capture the multiple layers of barriers: language and communication, cultural and religious influences, stigma, challenges with formal and informal support and negative experiences with healthcare providers (see online supplemental file 3).

A recent metaethnography indicates that ethnic minority populations in high-income countries face multiple barriers when accessing healthcare for NDDs.52 The challenges reported in the UK are consistent with those observed in other high-income settings, including the USA, Canada, Europe and Australia. Common barriers include cultural stigma, limited health literacy, language difficulties, socioeconomic inequities and healthcare provider bias, all of which contribute to delayed or missed diagnoses.53 54 Another theme not captured in this review was the barrier posed by out-of-pocket healthcare costs, which can limit the public’s utilisation of healthcare services. A scoping review based in Canada highlighted that minority-language speakers with NDDs often encounter additional barriers related to low income, inadequate insurance coverage and high out-of-pocket costs, which limit timely access to NDD services.22 In contrast, another study from Australia highlights that the introduction of the National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS) approach has led to a 32% increase in reported autism prevalence, accounting for 47% of new diagnoses since the scheme’s implementation. The paper discusses how the NDIS has influenced diagnostic practices, including a reduction in diagnoses from government-subsidised healthcare professionals, suggesting a shift in diagnostic thresholds.55 These financial constraints, however, are less relevant in the UK due to the publicly funded National Health Service (NHS) system, which provides more equitable access to healthcare. However, together, these barriers highlight how systemic inequities, social determinants and cultural dynamics interact to limit healthcare access for minority populations with NDDs.

While language and communication were one of the key barriers to accessing care, the support of translation was mentioned to be very rarely available for the carers.37 38 Healthcare providers highlighted the lack of professional interpreters, poor interpretation quality and cultural misunderstandings as key barriers to effective communication and care.41 However, the suggestions provided from the literature were that the involvement of the family members who understand the culture and the language would bridge this gap.41 In addition to this, a more recent alternative method used in addressing language and communication barriers is the use of translation applications on mobile phones. Evidence suggests that these applications are more useful and effective in communication between the care providers and the service users.56 57 Hence, identifying the most effective, sustainable and cost-effective methods could reduce language and communication barriers between service providers and users, thereby improving the utilisation of services.

The review highlighted religious beliefs that served as a coping mechanism with the stress and challenges that the Black, Asian and minority ethnic carers experienced.38 43 44 Another study indicates that carers from white ethnic backgrounds rarely referred to spirituality or religious beliefs as a coping strategy,58 whereas carers from racial and ethnic minority backgrounds were more likely to draw on religion as a means of coping.5961 However, religious beliefs sometimes caused families to feel shame and become secretive about their situation as they felt that others would describe it as a punishment or a curse from God.38 42 Hence, such stigma or shaming has the potential to make families secretive about it and delay their access to services.38 42 This was identified to be similar even among the South Asians and African Americans living in the USA.62 63 Stigma around shaming the families, social isolation and emotional stress have been highlighted to delay access to services.40 42 43 Addressing stigma surrounding disability within South Asian and African communities requires comprehensive strategies that incorporate cultural sensitivity, community involvement and systemic reforms. Research highlights that stigma often stems from cultural beliefs and the fear of social exclusion, leading families to conceal disabilities and avoid seeking care.64 65

Addressing religious and cultural barriers to healthcare access requires a multifaceted strategy. Culturally and religiously competent training for healthcare professionals is vital to meet the specific needs of diverse communities.29 66 Partnerships with faith-based organisations and community leaders can build trust, reduce stigma and promote open discussions about disabilities.67 68 Such collaborations have proven effective in public health initiatives and can support more inclusive care. Providing culturally tailored services further encourages engagement and helps families from Black, Asian and minority ethnic groups feel more comfortable seeking support.6769 This review highlights the need for a whole-system approach to improve access to NDD services for Black, Asian and minority ethnic groups in the UK. These populations face systemic barriers, including cultural misunderstandings, stigma, language issues and a lack of culturally competent care, as supported by previous research.25 A coordinated effort across healthcare, education and social services is essential, as exemplified by NHS England’s Core20PLUS5 framework, which targets health inequalities through integrated care systems.70 While this approach shows promise, challenges in implementation and resourcing remain. Nonetheless, it is key to promoting equitable service access.

Carers from South Asian (Pakistani, Indian, Bangladeshi) and African (black African, black Caribbean) backgrounds reported common barriers to accessing support for individuals with NDDs, including stigma, cultural and religious influences and challenges with formal and informal support systems.384549 Language barriers and negative experiences with healthcare providers were reported only among South Asian carers.37 41 For some Pakistani and Bangladeshi carers, marriage was seen as a culturally appropriate means of securing long-term care,38 42 a view not shared by African or Indian carers. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, as most included studies focused on South Asian populations (see online supplemental file 2). Hence, overall, there is a dearth of evidence exploring the experiences of African and black Caribbean people in the UK. A study found that the prevalence of autism in Somali and black African children was 1.2% (12.1 per 1000) and 1.1% (11 per 1000) among black Caribbean children, which is at least twice the prevalence observed in other ethnic groups within the same borough.71 However, as mentioned in the findings section, the review identified that the majority of participants in the included studies were from a South Asian background. Few studies focused on black African, black Caribbean or black British populations (see online supplemental file 2), and these groups were often grouped with other ethnic minority groups as if they were homogenous, which they are not.72 73

This scoping review employed a comprehensive search strategy and broad inclusion criteria, capturing a wide range of literature. The inclusion of both qualitative and quantitative studies, synthesised narratively, enhanced the robustness of the findings, while direct participant quotes added credibility. However, the review was limited to English-language publications and excluded grey literature, which may have constrained the diversity of perspectives. Additionally, focusing solely on UK-based studies limits the generalisability of the findings, and the varied terminology used to describe ethnic minority groups presents further challenges for synthesis. Future research should examine interventions or service models aimed at improving access to NDD services for carers from Black, Asian and minority ethnic groups. Further interventional studies should also explore strategies to address language and communication barriers, which were identified as key obstacles in service utilisation. This review also found that, within the population studied in this review, Black Africans, Black Caribbeans and other minority ethnic groups are notably underrepresented in studies on barriers to accessing NDD services, compared with the South Asian population, which requires further research.

Conclusion

The barriers and facilitators to accessing care for NDDs among carers from Black, Asian and minority ethnic groups in the UK are complex and multifactorial, necessitating a multidisciplinary approach. While these groups are often referred to collectively, they are far from homogeneous, differing significantly in their lived experiences. Due to gaps in the available evidence, particularly among the Black, Asian and minority ethnic groups in the UK, it remains unclear whether the barriers they face are shared or distinct. A deeper understanding of these nuances is essential to inform culturally appropriate, evidence-based policy and practice. Consequently, further research is crucial to address these knowledge gaps and to improve equitable access to neurodevelopmental services across all Black, Asian and minority ethnic communities in the UK. Future studies would also benefit from being embedded within service delivery settings, enabling real-world, context-driven learning that moves beyond follow-up academic inquiry and strengthens the relevance and applicability of findings.
