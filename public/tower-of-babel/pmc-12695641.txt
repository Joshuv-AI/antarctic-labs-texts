Scalable and accurate rare variant meta-analysis with Meta-SAIGE
Authors: Eunjae Park, Kisung Nam, Seokho Jeong, Karl Keat, Dokyoon Kim, Vikas Bansal, Wei Zhou, Seunggeun Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Meta-analysis enhances the power of rare variant association tests by combining summary statistics across several cohorts. However, existing methods often fail to control type I error for low-prevalence binary traits and are computationally intensive. Here we introduce Meta-SAIGE—a scalable method for rare variant meta-analysis that accurately estimates the null distribution to control type I error and reuses the linkage disequilibrium matrix across phenotypes to boost computational efficiency in phenome-wide analyses. Simulations using UK Biobank whole-exome sequencing data show that Meta-SAIGE effectively controls type I error and achieves power comparable to pooled individual-level analysis with SAIGE-GENE+. Applying Meta-SAIGE to 83 low-prevalence phenotypes in UK Biobank and All of Us whole-exome sequencing data identified 237 gene–trait associations. Notably, 80 of these associations were not significant in either dataset alone, underscoring the power of our meta-analysis.

Main

The advent of mega biobanks and consortia has substantially enhanced our ability to sequence large cohorts, facilitating the identification of rare genetic variants linked to human diseases and traits. Due to low minor allele frequencies (MAFs), single-variant tests are often underpowered for identifying rare variant associations1–3. In response, gene or region-based association tests, which aggregate the effects of several rare variants, have been proposed. Different statistical tests such as the Burden test4, SKAT5 and SKAT-O6 have been pivotal in the analysis of rare variant associations. More recently, STAAR7, SAIGE-GENE8 and SAIGE-GENE+9 have been introduced, offering efficient implementation of rare variant tests for large-scale biobank datasets, while accommodating sample relatedness and case–control imbalances.

Meta-analysis is a practical approach for identifying genetic associations that may not be detectable in individual cohorts but become statistically significant when summary statistics from several studies are combined10. Meta-analysis is particularly relevant for rare variant association tests, where the low frequencies of the variants can limit test power in individual studies. Substantial efforts have been made to facilitate the meta-analysis of rare variant association studies. For example, international consortia such as the Biobank Rare Variant Analysis (BRaVa) have been established. As more biobanks carry out sequencing studies, rare variant meta-analysis is likely to become a primary approach for identifying new rare variant associations for human diseases and traits.

Several methodologies for rare variant meta-analyses have been developed, notably RAREMETAL11,12 and MetaSKAT1. More recently, MetaSTAAR13 was introduced, enhancing the analysis by integrating variant functional annotations and accommodating sample relatedness. However, MetaSTAAR can exhibit notably inflated type I error rates under imbalanced case–control ratios (Supplementary Note A and Extended Data Fig. 1)—a challenge prevalent in biobank-based disease phenotype studies. Additionally, MetaSTAAR’s requirement for constructing separate linkage disequilibrium (LD) matrices for each phenotype substantially increases the computational load when analyzing several phenotypes. An alternative approach is the Fisher method14, which combines gene or region test P values from each cohort, but it often has substantially lower power than joint analysis with individual-level data.

We propose a new rare variant meta-analysis method, Meta-SAIGE, which extends SAIGE-GENE+9 to meta-analysis. First, Burden, SKAT and SKAT-O test P values are calculated using per-variant score statistics and LD matrices from each cohort. Next, to address type I error inflation in the presence of case–control imbalance, Meta-SAIGE employs two-level saddlepoint approximation (SPA)15,16, including SPA on score statistics of each cohort and a genotype-count-based SPA for combined score statistics from several cohorts17. In addition, Meta-SAIGE allows the use of a single sparse LD matrix across all phenotypes, significantly reducing computational costs when conducting phenome-wide analyses involving hundreds or thousands of phenotypes. Simulation studies using UK Biobank (UKB) whole-exome sequencing (WES) data18 demonstrate that Meta-SAIGE effectively controls type I error rates and is computationally faster and more efficient than MetaSTAAR. We conducted a meta-analysis of UKB and All of Us19 exome-wide rare variant gene-based tests for 83 disease phenotypes, and identified 237 associations at the exome-wide significance level. Among these associations, 80 were not significant in either dataset alone, underscoring the power of our meta-analysis.

With the combined per-variant statistics and covariance matrix, Meta-SAIGE performs rare variant tests in the same manner as SAIGE-GENE+. It conducts Burden, SKAT5 and SKAT-O6 set-based tests utilizing various functional annotations and maximum MAF cutoffs. Once the summary statistics have been merged into a superset, ultrarare variants (MAC < 10) are identified and collapsed to enhance type I error control and power while reducing computation cost, as demonstrated in SAIGE-GENE+. The Cauchy combination method21,22 is then used to combine the P values corresponding to different functional annotations and MAF cutoffs for each testing gene or region.

Comparison between Meta-SAIGE and SAIGE-GENE+

We first compared Meta-SAIGE (using summary statistics) and SAIGE-GENE+ (using individual-level data) for three continuous traits (high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), triglyceride(TG)) and three binary traits (type 2 diabetes (T2D), glaucoma, colorectal cancer) in the UKB WES data of 160,000 White British participants. The samples were divided into three nonoverlapping cohorts with size ratios of 1:1:1 and 4:3:2. Meta-SAIGE was applied using summary statistics from each cohort, whereas SAIGE-GENE+ utilized all individual-level data. Extended Data Fig. 2 demonstrates that both methods produced highly concordant results. For continuous traits, the R2 of negative log10-transformed P values exceeded 0.98 (Supplementary Table 5). For binary traits, R2 values were slightly lower (average 0.96), due mainly to different methods of handling case–control imbalance. Overall, these findings show that our meta-analysis method can produce results nearly identical to those obtained from individual-level data analyses.

Type I error and power evaluation

Since existing meta-analysis methods adequately control type I error for continuous traits, and Meta-SAIGE and SAIGE-GENE+ produce nearly identical results for these traits, we focused our simulation studies solely on binary traits. To assess type I error rates, we used the same UKB WES data of 160,000 White British participants, dividing them into three nonoverlapping cohorts. Null phenotypes were generated for each cohort with prevalences of 5% and 1%. Each simulation was replicated 60 times, resulting in approximately 1 million tests. The empirical type I error rates are presented in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 1. Without any adjustment, mirroring MetaSTAAR, there was a notable inflation in type I error rates. For example, with a disease prevalence of 1% and a sample size ratio of 1:1:1, the type I error rate for the no-adjustment method at α = 2.5 × 10−6 was 2.12 × 10−4, nearly 100 times higher than the nominal level (Supplementary Table 1). The SPA applied to score statistics of each cohort (SPA adjustment) notably decreased type I error rates, albeit with some remaining inflation. Our results indicate that Meta-SAIGE consistently results in lower computational demands in terms of both CPU time and memory usage. For example, with a sample size of 179,176, Meta-SAIGE was notably faster than MetaSTAAR, with speed improvements of 8.1, 6.5 and 24.7 times for per-variant statistics generation, LD matrix generation and meta-analysis, respectively. The difference in computational efficiency between Meta-SAIGE and MetaSTAAR diminished when smaller genes, such as MSH6 (number of rare variants = 990), were analyzed (Extended Data Fig. 3).

Meta-analysis of UKB and All of Us datasets

To illustrate the performance of Meta-SAIGE in real data, we applied it to conduct a whole-exome meta-analysis for 83 disease phenotypes using UKB and All of Us European Ancestry samples. Our analysis included approximately 459,000 European Ancestry samples from UKB and 116,000 European Ancestry samples from All of Us. Traits were selected to be compatible with both UKB and All of Us with sufficient case sizes while incorporating traits from previous WES phenome-wide analyses9. Case–control ratios varied from 1:2 (essential hypertension; Phecode, 401.1) to 1:305 (hemorrhage from gastrointestinal ulcers; Phecode, 531.1) across all analyzed phenotypes. A detailed list of phenotypes is provided in Supplementary Table 3. We applied three MAF cutoffs (1%, 0.1%, 0.01%) and three functional annotations (LoF (loss of function), LoF + Missense, LoF + Missense + Synonymous), resulting in a total of nine different test masks.

Among the 237 associations, 139 and 46 were significant in the UKB data and All of Us data only, respectively, underscoring the power of the meta-analysis. Among all significant genes, TTN and PKD1 were the most pleiotropic, being associated with seven phenotypes, followed by CHEK2, TET2 and SRSF2, each associated with five phenotypes (Fig. 5a).

A total of five genes were significant for colorectal cancer, and eight genes were significant for T2D. Our results clearly demonstrate that the meta-analysis can substantially improve the power to detect disease-associated genes. For example, Adenomatous polyposis coli (APC) is a tumor suppressor gene, and defects in this gene increase the risk of colorectal cancer24. In our analysis, the association of APC with colorectal cancer was not significant in UKB or All of Us data alone (UKB P value = 4.40 × 10−³ and All of Us P value = 1.32 × 10−⁵), and the meta-analysis p value was 5.39 × 10−¹², which was much more significant than in individual biobanks.

Discussion

This study introduces Meta-SAIGE—a method for meta-analyzing rare variant association studies. By employing SPA and GC-based adjustment methods, Meta-SAIGE accurately calculates P values, effectively controlling type I error rates while preserving true signals. As demonstrated in the type I error simulation, substantial reductions in type I error rates were observed compared to the unadjusted approach. Furthermore, Meta-SAIGE improves computation efficiency by allowing the use of a single LD matrix for different phenotypes, facilitating more cost-effective phenome-wide analyses as demonstrated through a meta-analysis of UKB and All of Us WES data.

One key feature of Meta-SAIGE is the use of a single sparse LD matrix, Ω, across phenotypes. This matrix encodes genotype correlations, which are then used to construct the covariance matrix for the score statistics, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\rm{Cov}}(S)$$\end{document}Cov(S). Compared with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\rm{Cov}}(S)$$\end{document}Cov(S) used in MetaSTAAR, the diagonal elements (that is, the variance of each variant’s score statistic) remain the same. The main difference lies in the off-diagonal elements, where our method relies on genotype correlations. This design makes the LD information phenotype-agnostic, requiring only a single LD matrix to be shared across cohorts. In Supplementary Note B, we provide a detailed comparison of these two approaches and show that their \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\rm{Cov}}(S)$$\end{document}Cov(S) yield nearly identical eigenvalues (Extended Data Figs. 5 and 9). In addition, we have shown that P values from Meta-SAIGE closely match those from SAIGE-GENE+. These findings indicate that Meta-SAIGE can produce accurate P values while reducing computational costs.

Although the advantages of sharing an LD matrix across phenotypes may be less evident in consortium-based settings focused on a single disease domain—compared to large, PheWAS-driven centralized biobanks—our approach can still offer substantial efficiency gains. Many large-scale international consortia are increasingly investigating a broad spectrum of related phenotypes across participating studies. For instance, the Global Lipids Genetics Consortium analyzes several lipid traits25,26 (for example, LDL-C, HDL-C, triglycerides); the Genetic Investigation of Anthropometric Traits consortium examines various anthropometric measures27,28 (for example, height, body mass index, waist–hip ratio); and the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium explores genetic influences across several psychiatric disorders29. In such multiphenotype efforts, Meta-SAIGE offers a streamlined and scalable framework. Specifically, it standardizes the generation of gene-based summary statistics (covariance matrices) at the study level (Step 1) and enables efficient central meta-analysis (Step 2), thereby facilitating coordinated analysis across numerous traits and studies. As phenotyping efforts within consortia continue to broaden, we anticipate that Meta-SAIGE will become an increasingly valuable tool for efficient and harmonized gene-based meta-analyses.

When performing meta-analyses using trait-defined (target) subsets, reusing the LD matrix computed from the full cohort may lead to discrepancies in the results. To evaluate this, we conducted additional analyses for T2D and LDL across varying target sample sizes and observed that results remained generally consistent when the target subset comprised more than 45% of the total cohort (Supplementary Note D and Extended Data Fig. 6). Beyond differences in sample size, potential heterogeneity in sample characteristics—such as varying ancestry composition in admixed populations—can also contribute to discrepancies when reusing the LD matrix. In such cases, a careful assessment of ancestry composition between the full cohort and target subset is warranted. We left this for future work.

Many biobanks are now collecting samples from diverse ancestries to mitigate the Eurocentric bias prevalent in genetic association studies, emphasizing the need for multi-ancestry meta-analysis. Although our study focused on a single ancestry, Meta-SAIGE can also be applied to multi-ancestry samples. A key challenge in multi-ancestry analysis is collapsing ultrarare variants, as these variants can differ between ancestry groups. To address this, we added a step for ancestry-specific collapsing (Methods). For T2D and colorectal cancer, we conducted a multi-ancestry meta-analysis of all European ancestry samples (EUR, n = 572,000) from UKB and All of Us, as well as African ancestry (n = 46,000) and American (n = 36,000) samples from All of Us. Only variants with a MAF ≤ 0.01 across all ancestry groups were included for meta-analysis (Extended Data Fig. 4). Supplementary Table 6 lists the significant genes identified by either European-only Meta-analysis (EUR-Meta) or Meta-analysis of Cross Ancestry (Meta-CrossAncestry). Meta-CrossAncestry had generally lower P values than EUR-Meta and identified one additional association for colorectal cancer: RPL29P11 (ribosomal protein L29 pseudogene 11). It is a pseudogene that encodes ribosomal protein L29, which is shown to upregulate colorectal cancer30,31, demonstrating the enhanced power of multi-ancestry meta-analysis. However, a more comprehensive evaluation of its performance across broader and more varied ancestry groups is required, which we leave for future investigation.

Recently, REMETA32 was introduced as a rare variant meta-analysis method designed for large-scale biobank data. We compared REMETA and Meta-SAIGE in terms of computational efficiency and type I error control (Supplementary Note E). For computational benchmarking using the TTN gene, REMETA demonstrated efficient null model fitting, which is done once in genome-wide, but was substantially slower than Meta-SAIGE in both LD matrix construction and the meta-analysis step (Extended Data Fig. 7). In our evaluation of type I error rates, REMETA exhibited substantial inflation when analyzing low-prevalence binary traits (Extended Data Fig. 8). Given that most disease traits in population-based biobank are of low prevalence, our results demonstrate that Meta-SAIGE offers more robust type I error control and is a more effective tool for large-scale biobank meta-analyses.

Overall, we demonstrated that Meta-SAIGE can effectively handle large-scale rare variant meta-analyses of binary phenotypes, adjusting for phenotype imbalance. As many biobanks are currently generating large-scale sequencing data, our method can facilitate more powerful meta-analysis studies for identifying rare disease-associated genetic variants and elucidating the genetic architecture of underlying complex traits.

Methods

Workflow of Meta-SAIGE

Meta-SAIGE comprises three steps: (1) preparation of single-variant association summaries for each cohort, (2) consolidation of the summaries from all studies into a singular comprehensive dataset and (3) implementation of gene-based association testing, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Further adjustment with a GC-based meta-analysis approach

The SPA adjustment outlined previously assumes that score statistics approximate a normal distribution following variance adjustment using SPA. However, for highly imbalanced binary phenotypes, this adjustment alone does not suffice to maintain type I error rates within acceptable bounds. The cause of this issue lies in the discrete nature of study-specific statistics, for which mere aggregation proves inadequate. To address this, we previously introduced a GC-based procedure that uses a GC-based SPA as a reference distribution instead of a normal distribution for meta-analysis17.

The GC-based method estimates the cumulant generating function of the aggregated score statistics \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{j}^{* }$$\end{document}Sj* using genotype counts to better approximate the distribution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{j}^{* }$$\end{document}Sj*, thereby enhancing the accuracy of the P value estimation. This method is applied when the single-variant P value, derived from the variance adjustment, falls below 0.05. This cutoff was chosen based on previous research, which demonstrated that SPA performs well in the tail area but can become unstable when the score statistic is near the mean. A P value of 0.05 corresponds to the threshold of a standardized score of 2, as previously established35. The adjusted variance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hat{V}}_{j}^{* }$$\end{document}Vˆj* is then determined by inverting the P value obtained through the GC-based approach. Simulation studies show that this GC-based meta-analysis approach reduces type I error.

Ancestry-specific collapsing

Given that ultrarare variants are population-specific, Meta-SAIGE can perform ancestry-specific ultrarare variant collapsing for multi-ancestry meta-analysis. For cohorts sharing the same ancestry, ancestry-specific collapsing is conducted before creating the superset. These ancestry-specific collapsed variants are treated as unique pseudovariants.

Comparison of Meta-SAIGE and SAIGE-GENE+

To compare these two approaches, we used UKB WES data from 166,960 White British samples. In the first scenario, samples were distributed evenly across three cohorts, with sizes of 55,655, 55,654 and 55,652, respectively. In the second scenario, samples were distributed in a 4:3:2 ratio, resulting in cohort sizes of 74,205, 55,654 and 37,102. Related samples (pairwise values in GRM ≥ 0.05) were assigned to the same cohort. We included age, sex, batch information and ten ancestry PCs as the covariates. Sparse GRM with relatedness cutoff = 0.05 was used to adjust for sample relatedness. Summary statistics were calculated for each cohort and then combined using Meta-SAIGE. For SAIGE-GENE+, P values were calculated using all individual-level data. Three binary traits with varying case–control ratios were analyzed: T2D (Phecode 250.2; case–control = 1:22), glaucoma (Phecode 365; 1:91) and colorectal cancer (Phecode 153; 1:89). For continuous traits, we examined LDL direct (data-field 30780), HDL cholesterol (data-field 30760) and triglycerides (data-field 30870).

Our investigation encompassed eight distinct scenarios, each characterized by the proportions of causal variants, effect size magnitudes, and effect directions of rare variants. Specifically, we considered two different configurations of causal variant proportions for non-ultrarare (MAC > 10) variants: (1) 20% of LoF, 10% of missense and 2% of synonymous, and (2) 30% of LoF, 20% of missense and 2% of synonymous. Given that functionally deleterious variants are more likely to be rare, we posited that the proportions of causal variants among ultrarare variants (MAC ≤ 10) were threefold greater than those among less rare variants across various functional annotations. We explored two different settings for absolute effect sizes: (1) |0.5log10(MAF)| for LoF and |0.25log10(MAF)| for missense and synonymous and (2) |0.7log10(MAF)| for LoF and |0.35log10(MAF)| for missense and synonymous. We also examined two alternative configurations for effect directions among causal variants: (1) all causal variants exhibited the same directional effect and (2) 100% of LoF, 80% of missense and 50% of synonymous variants increased the risk of disease, whereas the remaining causal variants decreased the risk of disease. For each simulation scenario, we conducted 100 independent repetitions, resulting in a total of 1,000 gene-level P values. We partitioned 166,960 White British samples evenly across three cohorts, assigning 55,655, 55,654 and 55,652 samples, respectively.

UKB and All of Us WES data meta-analysis

We performed a meta-analysis on data from the UKB and All of Us datasets for 83 binary traits among people of European ancestry (Supplementary Table 3). Binary traits were phenotyped based on Phecodes37, which were derived from ICD records. Per-variant summary statistics and LD matrix calculations and meta-analyses were performed on the UKB Research Analysis Platform and the All of Us Workbench cloud system.

WES data from 459,369 White UKB participants were used to create association summaries. Hospital inpatient data coded in the ICD-10 were transformed into Phecodes, and participants were categorized into case–control groups accordingly. PLINK binary files of WESs were used for the analysis. Genomic and electronic health record (EHR) data from All of Us were retrieved from the v.7 Curated Data Repository on the controlled tier. The multi-ancestry grouping of All of Us participants was derived from the Global Diversity Array, where PCs were used for ancestry labels19. Whole-genome sequencing data from All of Us in BGEN format were used to create per-variant summary statistics. EHR data based on ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes were used to map Phecodes. Samples without EHR data were excluded from the analysis.

For both the UKB and All of Us datasets, we included age, sex, batch information and ten ancestry PCs as covariates. To account for sample relatedness, we used a sparse GRM for the UKB with a relatedness cutoff of 0.05. However, due to challenges in efficiently estimating a sparse GRM in multi-ancestry data, which reduces its sparsity, we opted to use a dense GRM with 717,763 pruned markers for the All of Us dataset. Summary statistics from the UKB and All of Us were generated with the maximum MAF of 0.01. To create the LD matrix, variants pooled from both cohorts were annotated using VEP105 (ref. 38) + LOFTEE39 GRCh38 v.1.04 (https://github.com/BRaVa-genetics/vep105_loftee) into three functional categories: LoF, missense and synonymous variants.

Furthermore, we performed a meta-analysis on several ancestry groups across UKB and All of Us for T2D and colorectal cancer. The multi-ancestry grouping of All of Us participants was derived from the Global Diversity Array, where PCs were generated and projected onto the ancestry label provided by GnomAD. Ancestry groups with more than 10,000 samples from All of Us, European (EUR, n = 116,566), African (AFR, n = 45,772) and American (AMR, n = 36,152), were used for this analysis. Per-variant summary statistics and LD matrices of UKB-White, All of Us EUR, AFR and AMR groups were generated using SAIGE and applied to Meta-SAIGE. To evaluate how the method behaves under the null distribution on a multi-ancestry level, we permuted phenotypes per ancestry and performed Meta-SAIGE based on it.

Computational cost evaluation

We evaluated the computational cost of MetaSTAAR and Meta-SAIGE across different sample sizes: 10,000, 50,000, 100,000 and 179,176 White samples. Each sample size was further divided into three subcohorts. For comparison, we used primarily the TTN gene (the largest gene), which represents a computational bottleneck for the analysis. We included LoF, missense and synonymous variants with a MAF ≤ 0.001, resulting in a total of 17,577 variants. Furthermore, we analyzed a smaller gene, MSH6, which had 990 variants. We measured CPU time and memory usage across three distinct stages of the analysis: (1) input file generation, including fitting the null model and conducting single-variant association tests; (2) LD matrix generation and (3) meta-analysis.
