SITA: Learning Speaker-Invariant and Tone-Aware Speech Representations for Low-Resource Tonal Languages
Tianyi Xu Xuan Ouyang Binwei Yao Shoua Xiong, [0.1em] Sara Misurelli Maichou Lor Junjie Hu, [0.35em] Department of Computer Sciences School of Nursing Department of OtolaryngologyUniversity of Wisconsin–Madison, Madison, WI, USA, [0.25em] Equal contribution Corresponding author

Abstract
Tonal low-resource languages are widely spoken yet remain underserved by modern speech technology. A key challenge is learning representations that are robust to nuisance variation such as gender while remaining tone-aware for different lexical meanings. To address this, we propose SITA , a lightweight adaptation recipe that enforces S peaker- I nvariance and T one- A wareness for pretrained wav2vec-style encoders. SITA uses staged multi-objective training: (i) a cross-gender contrastive objective encourages lexical consistency across speakers, while a tone-repulsive loss prevents tone collapse by explicitly separating same-word different-tone realizations; and (ii) an auxiliary Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC)-based ASR objective with distillation stabilizes recognition-relevant structure. We evaluate primarily on Hmong, a highly tonal and severely under-resourced language where off-the-shelf multilingual encoders fail to represent. On a curated Hmong word corpus, SITA improves cross-gender lexical retrieval accuracy, while maintaining usable ASR accuracy relative to a ASR-adapted XLS-R teacher. We further observe similar gains when transferring the same recipe to Mandarin, suggesting SITA is a general, plug-in approach for adapting multilingual speech encoders to tonal languages. 1 1 1 Code and models: https://github.com/tianyi0216/SITA

2
Hmong Audio Dataset

In this work, we focus on learning representations for Hmong, a low-resource tonal language, with the goal of improving performance on downstream tasks such as the Word Recognition Testing (WRT)
30
. As Hmong is a low-resource language, we collect our own dataset and perform data augmentation for our method SITA (§
3
).

2.1
Data Collection

Hmong speech data is collected from an ongoing Hmong WRT study with adult native speakers, collected under a standardized recording protocol with informed consent and approved institutional review board (IRB). In the testing, participants read and recorded four curated word lists that included all seven lexical tones for each word, totaling 1,400 words per participant. Speakers are instructed to produce each word in a natural voice with 3 second pause between consecutive words to ease segmentation. Appendix
B.2
shows the full dataset statistics.

2.2
Data Preprocessing

All recordings are converted to single-channel audio at a fixed sampling rate and peak-normalized. We segment word-level clips using silence-based VAD
37
that leverages the protocol-mandated pauses, and align segments to canonical lexical forms via session metadata and list order. The final dataset consists of clean word-level segments with lexical labels, tone annotations, and speaker metadata (including gender), which we use for both representation learning objectives and ASR training. Our segmentation and alignment pipeline is implemented in our preprocessing toolkit and will be released with our codebase.

2.3
Data Augmentation

To reduce overfitting to speaker-specific acoustics and improve robustness, we apply two complementary strategies to augment the dataset.

Lexical-Preserving Acoustic Perturbations.

In training, we apply lightweight waveform perturbations, including additive noise, time stretching, pitch shifting, and gain jitter
25
. To preserve lexical meaning in a tonal language, pitch shifting is excluded when computing tone-related objectives and during ASR training. These perturbations maintain lexical labels while increasing channel and prosodic variability, benefiting both contrastive learning and ASR.

Voice Conversion for Speaker Diversification.

To increase speaker diversity for each lexical item, we generate cross-speaker variants using a content-preserving, text-free one-shot voice conversion model (FreeVC)
29
. The converted audio serves as an additional view of the same word token with a different speaker identity, encouraging speaker-invariant yet tone-sensitive representations. Voice conversion is applied only to the training set, with the conversion pool restricted to three speakers that appear exclusively in the training split.

3
Method

We introduce SITA, a two-stage adaptation recipe designed to learn speaker-invariant yet tone-aware speech representations from a pretrained multilingual speech encoder and retrain its recognition capability for ASR tasks (Figure
2
).

3.1
Problem Definition

Let
{\mathcal{D}}=\{(x_{i},y_{i},t_{i},g_{i})\}_{i=1}^{M}
be a word-level tonal speech dataset, where
x_{i}
is a waveform segment containing a single spoken token,
y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
is its corresponding text transcript,
t_{i}\in\{1,\dots,T\}
is its tone label, and
g_{i}\in\mathcal{G}
is a demographic attribute (e.g., gender, age). Given a pretrained multilingual speech model
f_{\theta_{0}}
, we modularize the bottom
L
layers as the speech embedding layers and the upper layers above layer
L
as the ASR layers. That is, we can obtain the
\ell_{2}
-normalized embeddings
{\bm{z}}_{i}=f_{\theta}(x_{i};L)
at layer
L
, and forward
{\bm{z}}_{i}
through the remaining layers to perform ASR:
p_{\theta}(y_{i}|x_{i})
.

Our goal is two-fold. First, we aim to learn adapted parameters
\theta
that produce speech representations
{\bm{z}}_{i}
satisfying two requirements: (
i
)
speaker invariance
—Embeddings of tokens sharing the same lexical item should be invariant to demographic shift (i.e.,
{\bm{z}}_{i}\approx{\bm{z}}_{j}
if
y_{i}=y_{j},g_{i}\neq g_{j}
), and (
ii
)
tone awareness
—Lexical pairs that differ only by tone (same base word, different tones) should remain well separated in the embedding space (i.e.,
{\bm{z}}_{i}\not\approx{\bm{z}}_{j}
if
base
(
x_{i}
) =
base
(
x_{j}
),
t_{i}\neq t_{j}
. We quantify embedding similarity by cosine similarity, i.e.,
\mathrm{sim}({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})={\bm{u}}^{\top}{\bm{v}}
, and embedding distance by
1-\mathrm{sim}({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})
. Second, the representation must retain recognition-friendly structure for ASR tasks.

3.2
SITA

SITA consists of two stages: Stage 1 learns a tone-structured embedding space using two complementary loss terms; Stage 2 restores recognition capability for ASR via CTC fine-tuning, optionally regularized by knowledge distillation (KD).

Stage 1: Speech Representation Learning.

Speaker-invariant Loss.

For an anchor segment
x_{i}
, we construct one
cross-speaker
positive
x_{i}^{+}
that is another recording of the
same lexical item
spoken by a speaker of the opposite gender (see speaker-diversified augmentation in §
2.3
). In this work, we instantiate the speaker’s demographic attribute by gender (i.e., male and female) for simplicity, but this loss can be generalized to other demographic attributes (e.g., age).
We sample
N
negatives
\{x_{i,n}^{-}\}_{n=1}^{N}
from
different lexical items
(thus carrying different tones). We define the per-anchor InfoNCE loss
\ell_{1}
as the negative log-likelihood of selecting the cross-speaker positive among one positive and
N
negatives. Specifically, we compute the similarities scaled by a temperature
\tau_{g}
for the positive and negative pairs and the loss as:

\displaystyle s_{i}^{+}=\frac{\operatorname{sim}({\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{z}}_{i}^{+})}{\tau_{g}},\;s_{i,n}^{-}=\frac{\operatorname{sim}({\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{z}}_{i,n}^{-})}{\tau_{g}},

(1)

\displaystyle\ell_{1}(x_{i})=-\log\frac{\exp(s_{i}^{+})}{\exp(s_{i}^{+})+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\exp(s_{i,n}^{-})}.

(2)

Aggregating all segments
x_{i}
from
\mathcal{D}
of size
M
, we optimize the InfoNCE objective:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{speaker}}(\mathcal{D})=\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}}|}\sum_{x_{i}\in{\mathcal{D}}}\ell_{1}(x_{i}).

(3)

This objective treats gender as within-class nuisance variation: it pulls together embeddings of the same lexical item spoken by different speaker genders while pushing away different lexical items.

Tone-aware Loss.

Using the lexical-preserving acoustic augmentation (§
2.3
), we additionally construct three sets for each anchor
x_{i}
: (i) a positive set
{\mathcal{P}}(i)
of waveform segments with the same word and tone, (ii) a set of hard negatives
{\mathcal{H}}(i)
with the same word but different tones, and (iii) a set of soft negatives
{\mathcal{S}}(i)
from different words.
For each anchor segment
x_{i}
, we compute the similarity scaled by a temperature
\tau_{t}
with all segments in the three constructed sets, and the normalization term
Z_{i}
as:

\displaystyle s_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{sim}({\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{z}}_{j})}{\tau_{t}},~\text{for}~j\in{\mathcal{P}}(i)\cup{\mathcal{H}}(i)\cup{\mathcal{S}}(i),

\displaystyle Z_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{P}}(i)\cup{\mathcal{H}}(i)\cup{\mathcal{S}}(i)}\exp(s_{ij}),

(4)

For each anchor
x_{i}
with multiple tone positives, the per-sample loss
\ell_{2}
is

\displaystyle\ell_{2}(x_{i})=-\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{P}}(i)|}\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{P}}(i)}\log\frac{\exp(s_{ij})}{Z_{i}}.

(5)

We also add a tone classifier to steer the embedding toward tone-discriminative structure. Specifically, we add a linear layer for predicting the tone label from the embedding, i.e.,
p_{\phi}(t\mid{\bm{z}})=\mathrm{softmax}({\bm{W}}{\bm{z}}+b)
with tunable parameters
\phi=\{{\bm{W}},b\}
. Here, we use the standard cross-entropy loss:
\ell_{3}(x_{i},t_{i})=-\log p_{\phi}(t_{i}\mid{\bm{z}}_{i})
.

Lastly, the tone-aware loss is then computed as the weight sum of
\ell_{2}
and
\ell_{3}
over all
(x_{i},t_{i})
in the dataset
{\mathcal{D}}
of size
M
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{tone}}({\mathcal{D}})=\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}}|}\sum_{(x_{i},t_{i})\in{\mathcal{D}}}\left(\ell_{2}(x_{i})+\lambda\ell_{3}(x_{i},t_{i})\right).

Stage 1 Objective.

We jointly optimize the weighted sum of the two loss terms in Stage 1.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{Stage-1}}({\mathcal{D}})=\alpha\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{speaker}}({\mathcal{D}})+(1-\alpha)\mathcal{L}_{\text{tone}}({\mathcal{D}}).

As shown in Figure
2
, for parameter-efficient fine-tuning, we update only the model parameters from layer
B
to layer
L
to localize invariance and tone structure in early representations, and dedicate the upper layers after layer
L
as the ASR layers for functional modularization.

Stage 2: ASR Fine-tuning

In Stage 2, we fine-tune the model with a CTC objective on word-level transcriptions to retain automatic speech recognition capability.
To preserve the embeddings learned from Stage 1, we freeze the bottom
L
embedding layers and only update the remaining layers after layer
L
and the CTC
projection head. We regularize the student ASR with a frozen ASR teacher via knowledge distillation.

CTC Loss.

Given encoder frame representations
\{{\bm{h}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, we compute per-frame logits
{\bm{u}}_{t}={\bm{W}}{\bm{h}}_{t}
with a linear CTC head
{\bm{W}}
and posteriors
p_{\theta}(\pi_{t}\mid x)=\mathrm{softmax}({\bm{u}}_{t})_{\pi_{t}}
over the vocabulary
\mathcal{V}\cup\{\varnothing\}
. CTC defines the sequence likelihood by summing over all alignments
\pi
that collapse to
y
under
\mathcal{B}(\cdot)
:

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(y\mid x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi:\,\mathcal{B}(\pi)=y}\ \prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\theta}(\pi_{t}\mid x).

(6)

We minimize the negative log-likelihood over
{\mathcal{D}}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{CTC}}({\mathcal{D}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}}|}\sum_{(x_{i},y_{i})\in{\mathcal{D}}}\log p_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid x_{i}).

(7)

Knowledge Distillation (KD).

We use KD to keep the student’s frame-level posterior distribution close to a stronger ASR teacher parameterized by
\varphi
, improving training stability and preventing the encoder from drifting away from recognition-friendly representations. Let
{\bm{u}}_{t}^{\prime}
and
{\bm{u}}_{t}
be the teacher and student CTC logits at frame
t
respectively for a segment
x_{i}
.
With a temperature
\tau_{\text{kd}}>0
, we compute the softened teacher and student posteriors as
\tilde{p}_{\varphi}(\pi_{t}\mid x_{i})=\operatorname{softmax}({\bm{u}}^{\prime}_{t}/\tau_{\text{kd}})
and

\tilde{p}_{\theta}(\pi_{t}\mid x_{i})=\operatorname{softmax}({\bm{u}}_{t}/\tau_{\text{kd}})
.
We compute the frame-wise KL divergence between the teacher and student posteriors for all segment
x_{i}\in{\mathcal{D}}
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}({\mathcal{D}})=\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}}|}\sum_{x_{i}\in{\mathcal{D}}}\operatorname{KL}(\tilde{p}_{\varphi}(\pi\mid x_{i})||\tilde{p}_{\theta}(\pi\mid x_{i})).

Stage-2 Objective.

We minimize a weighted sum of the CTC and KD loss terms with a weight
\delta
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{Stage-2}}({\mathcal{D}})=\delta\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{CTC}}({\mathcal{D}})+(1-\delta)\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}({\mathcal{D}}).

Evaluation Tasks and Metrics.

We evaluate different methods on three complementary tasks:

1.

Cross-Gender Word Retrieval
: Given a query utterance from one gender, we rank all opposite-gender utterances by cosine similarity and report Top-1 and Top-5 accuracy for retrieving the correct lexical item.

2.

Tone Geometry
: Given a query utterance from different tones, we quantify within-tone cohesion and cross-tone separation using cosine similarity for positives (same word, same tone), and cosine distance
d_{\cos}=1-\text{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)
for negatives (same word, different tone; different word).

3.

ASR
: we decode with a standard CTC beam search and report
WER
(word error rate) and
CER
(character error rate), measuring the normalized edit distance between the decoded hypothesis and the reference transcript (lower is better). Detailed definition is in Appendix
B.5
.

6
Conclusion

We address low-resource tonal-language adaptation where embeddings should be speaker-invariant yet tone-aware. We introduce SITA, a lightweight two-stage approach built on multilingual XLS-R: Stage I learns gender-invariant, tone-sensitive embeddings with cross-gender contrastive supervision and tone repulsion, and Stage II restores ASR structure with CTC fine-tuning and optional distillation. On Hmong, SITA improves cross-gender retrieval and markedly sharpens tone separation over strong multilingual and ASR baselines, while incurring only a modest ASR drop relative to a fully ASR-adapted model.
We also see the same qualitative pattern on Mandarin, indicating the recipe transfers beyond a single tonal language.

Limitations

Our evaluation of SITA is conducted on a curated word-level corpus with limited coverage of speakers, recording conditions, and speaking styles. As a result, our retrieval and tone-geometry metrics may not fully reflect performance on spontaneous, conversational speech or under broader domain shift. While we observe consistent gains with lightweight (partially frozen) adaptation, the best configuration may change with more data, different demographic distributions, or different tonal inventories. Finally, SITA exposes an inherent trade-off between tone separation and ASR accuracy: the appropriate loss weighting and freezing strategy may depend on the downstream priority (e.g., robust retrieval vs. recognition), and may require task-specific tuning.

Ethical Considerations

SITA targets speech technology for tonal low-resource languages that are often under-served, with potential benefits for education, accessibility tools, and language documentation/preservation. However, speech representations can encode sensitive attributes (e.g., speaker identity, gender, accent) and may enable profiling or surveillance. While our training objectives encourage invariance to nuisance variation, they do not guarantee the removal of all demographic information or prevent downstream misuse. Our evaluation is limited in speaker coverage and may miss disparate failure modes for underrepresented groups; future work should expand demographic diversity and incorporate community-informed testing and governance. Finally, similarity or retrieval signals from embeddings should not be used as the sole basis for high-stakes decisions; any deployment in consequential settings requires rigorous validation, oversight, and informed consent.

B.1
Hmong Tones and RPA Marking

Hmong is a predominantly monosyllabic tonal language where lexical tone changes meaning. The Romanized Popular Alphabet (RPA) marks seven tones: high level (b), mid level (no marker), low level (s), high falling (j), mid rising (v), breathy falling (g), and low falling creaky (m). Words can share identical segments but differ only in tone, yielding distinct meanings (e.g.,
liab
“red” vs.
liam
“to blame”).

B.2
Dataset Statistics

Hmong (WRT) corpus.

We use a curated word-level corpus collected under a standardized WRT protocol (§
2
).
After VAD-based segmentation and metadata alignment, the dataset contains 8,570 word from 8 speakers (3 female, 5 male), covering 1,143 unique words forms spanning 163 base words and all 7 lexical tones.
To mitigate speaker imbalance and increase within-lexeme diversity, we additionally apply voice-conversion augmentation to generate 3,600 synthetic words, yielding 12,170 total words.
The resulting audio totals 1h 41m 24s.

Splits.

We construct train/test splits to ensure broad lexical coverage in the test set.
Concretely, for each base word (and its tonal realizations), we place at least one token into the test split by sampling from a subset of speakers in the WRT study, continuing until all base words are represented; all remaining tokens are used for training.
For
unseen-speaker
evaluation (§
5.3
), we further hold out one additional male speaker as the query set, and evaluate M
\rightarrow
F retrieval against a gallery formed from the seen female speakers (Appendix
D.2
).
Unless otherwise stated, all metrics are computed on word-level segments.

Mandarin (Tone Perfect).

For the Mandarin transfer experiment, we use the Tone Perfect corpus of read monosyllables with speaker and tone annotations.
Our subset contains 9,840 tokens from 6 speakers, covering 410 syllables and 4 tones.
We use one male speaker as the test set and the remaining speakers for training.

B.3
Training Settings and Hyperparameters

Backbone and embedding extraction.

All models are initialized from the multilingual wav2vec-style encoder XLS-R

Unless otherwise specified, token embeddings are formed by pooling Transformer hidden states (

Stage 1: Speech Representation Learning.

Optimization.

We optimize all models with Adam (learning rate

1.0

We use a batch size of 4 with 4-step gradient accumulation (effective batch size 16), and train for 12,000 update steps with 1,200 warmup steps.

Augmentations.

During training we apply lightweight acoustic perturbations, including additive noise, time-stretching, and gain (volume) jitter (§
2

To avoid changing lexical meaning in tonal languages, pitch shifting is disabled for tone-related objectives and ASR.
We additionally use FreeVC-based voice conversion (§
2

FreeVC augmentation is performed offline and is restricted to the training split. We select a fixed pool of three training speakers and generate cross-speaker views by converting each training token to a different speaker in this pool (source/target both from the pool). Importantly, no test speaker is included in either the source or target set, and no conversion is performed for any test utterance.
The augmented set adds 3600 converted tokens (on top of 6857 original tokens), and is used only as additional views for Stage 1 representation learning. Because converted utterances share lexical labels with their originals, we treat them as additional

B.5
Evaluation Metrics

Embedding similarity.

Given an utterance
x
, we extract a pooled normalized embedding

{\bm{z}}=f_{\theta}(x)
.
We use cosine similarity

\mathrm{sim}({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})={\bm{u}}^{\top}{\bm{v}},

where
u,v
are
\ell_{2}
-normalized vectors.

Tone-geometry statistics.

We report average cosine similarity for
positives
(same lexical item, same tone),

\mathrm{PosSim}=\mathbb{E}_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}_{\text{pos}}}\!\left[\mathrm{cos}({\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{z}}_{j})\right],

and average cosine distance for negatives,

\displaystyle d_{\cos}({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle=1-\mathrm{cos}({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}),

\displaystyle\mathrm{NegDist}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}_{\text{neg}}}\!\left[d_{\cos}({\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{z}}_{j})\right].

We instantiate
\mathcal{A}_{\text{neg}}
as (i)
hard
negatives: same lexical item but different tone, and
(ii)
soft
negatives: different lexical items (therefore often also different tones).
Since
\mathrm{cos}(u,v)\in[-1,1]
for normalized vectors,
d_{\cos}(u,v)\in[0,2]
.

Cross-gender retrieval accuracy.

For each query embedding
z_{q}

\mathrm{sim}(z_{q},z_{g})
.
Top-

K
highest-ranked gallery items
matches the query’s lexical label.

ASR error rates.

We report word error rate (WER) and character error rate (CER), computed as normalized Levenshtein distance:

\displaystyle\mathrm{WER}

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{w}+D_{w}+I_{w}}{N_{w}},

\displaystyle\mathrm{CER}

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{c}+D_{c}+I_{c}}{N_{c}},

where
S

D

are the numbers of substitutions, deletions, and insertions, and
N
is the number
of reference tokens (words for WER, characters for CER).

B.6
Embedding Extraction and Pooling

Across all experiments, we extract frame-level hidden states from the adapted encoder’s block
\ell

Let
\{h_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the frame-level embeddings aligned to a token segment.
We consider the following pooling operators:

\displaystyle\text{mean:}\quad{\bm{z}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\bm{h}}_{t},

\displaystyle\text{max:}\quad{\bm{z}}_{k}=\max_{t\in\{1,\dots,T\}}({\bm{h}}_{t})_{k},\ \forall k\in\{1,\dots,d\},

\displaystyle\text{max+mean:}\quad{\bm{z}}=\frac{{\bm{z}}^{\text{max}}+{\bm{z}}^{\text{mean}}}{2},

\displaystyle\text{weighted:}\quad{\bm{z}}=\alpha\,{\bm{z}}^{\text{max}}+(1-\alpha)\,{\bm{z}}^{\text{mean}},\ \alpha=0.7.

Default pooling choices.

We use
max pooling
for
cross-gender retrieval
throughout the paper, as it yields the most stable rankings for lexical identity under speaker variation. For all
tone-related analyses
(tone classification and tone geometry) and all
similarity-scoring experiments

mean pooling
, which better reflects the overall segment-level representation and produces smoother similarity estimates. These pooling choices are
fixed globally
for
SITA
and are not tuned per experiment, per model variant, or per dataset.

Margin-Based Tone Repulsion Loss

In addition to the main InfoNCE-based tone objective, we experimented with a margin-based formulation that explicitly enforces separation between tones via hinge penalties. Let
{\bm{z}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the normalized embedding for token
i
and
s_{ij}=\mathrm{sim}({\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{z}}_{j})={\bm{z}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{z}}_{j}
. For each anchor
i
, we form three pair sets: positives
{\mathcal{P}}(i)
(same word, same tone), hard negatives
{\mathcal{H}}(i)
(same word, different tone), and soft negatives
{\mathcal{S}}(i)
(different word). The per-anchor loss is

\displaystyle\ell_{i}^{\text{margin}}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\text{attr}}\cdot\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{P}}(i)|}\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{P}}(i)}\bigl(-s_{ij}\bigr)

(8)

\displaystyle+\lambda_{\text{hard}}\cdot\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{H}}(i)|}\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{H}}(i)}\bigl[s_{ij}-m_{\text{hard}}\bigr]_{+}

\displaystyle+\lambda_{\text{soft}}\cdot\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{S}}(i)|}\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{S}}(i)}\bigl[s_{ij}-m_{\text{soft}}\bigr]_{+},

where
[x]_{+}=\max(x,0)
. We set
m_{\text{hard}}<m_{\text{soft}}
to impose a stricter separation requirement for same-word different-tone pairs than for different-word pairs. The full objective averages over anchors:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{tone}}^{\text{margin}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\ell_{i}^{\text{margin}}.
