A multi-season epidemic model with random genetic drift and transmissibility
Authors: Tom Britton, Andrea Pugliese
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe consider a model for the spread of an influenza-like disease in which, between seasons, the virus makes a random genetic drift (reducing immunity) and obtains a new random transmissibility (closely related to R0). Given the immunity status at the start of season k, i.e. the community distribution of years since last infection and their associated immunity levels, the outcome of the epidemic season k, characterized by the effective reproduction number Re(k) and the fractions infected in the different immunity groups z(k), is determined by the random genetic drift and transmissibility. It is shown that the community immunity status of consecutive seasons, is an ergodic Markov chain, which converges to a stationary distribution. More analytical progress is made for the case where immunity only lasts for one season: we then characterize the stationary distribution of the community fraction having partial immunity (from being infected last season) as well as the stationary distribution of (Re(k),z(k)), and the conditional distribution of z(k) given Re(k). The effective reproduction number Re(k) is closely related to the initial exponential growth rate ρ(k) of the outbreak, a quantity which can be estimated early in the epidemic season. As a consequence, this conditional distribution may be used for predicting the final size of the epidemic based on its initial growth and immunity status.Keywords: Seasonal flu, Viral drift, Stationary distribution, Attack ratio, Effective reproduction number

Introduction

Several infectious diseases are globally endemic but circulate around the globe leading to seasonal outbreaks in the winter seasons in the northern and southern hemisphere, seasonal influenza being the prime example. Coming into a new epidemic season, a given community has some natural immunity originating from people being infected in recent seasons. Vaccinations, typically among the elderly population, reduce the number of severe cases but only marginally affect disease spreading, and are hence neglected in what follows. Between seasons, the circulating virus has often evolved (drifted), leading to decay of immunity among previously infected, and the transmissibility of the new strain may also differ from the transmissibility of the previous strain (disease severity may also differ, but this is not considered here).

One of the first to analyse mathematical models for these situations was Viggo Andreasen who wrote an influential paper where he analyses a deterministic model for seasonal outbreaks in which the immunity of individuals in the community decays in a specified deterministic way with the number of seasons since the last infection (Andreasen 2003). When considering the special case where immunity only lasts one or two seasons he characterized the possible scenarios. For immunity lasting only one season he showed that there are three parameter regions in which, respectively, the size of a seasonal outbreak converges to a constant, there is an outbreak only every other season, or the outbreak size alternates between a small and a large outbreak every other season. This model has later been extended in several ways. Kucharski et al. (2016) consider a virus appearing in different variants, possibly having (partial) cross immunity to each other, and where immunity of the different strains depend on time since last infection of this strain. Roberts et al. (2024) extend the model by assuming a heterogeneous community (specified by a continuous distribution) affecting the immunity and infectivity of the individuals. Additionally they study what happens with the long term behaviour of the season outbreaks if the seasonal deterministic immunity drift is perturbed randomly by a small amount each season.

In the current paper we take a more genuinely stochastic perspective by treating the inter-seasonal genetic drift, together with a seasonal transmissibility, (δk,τk) as a bivariate random vector, and where we assume the drift and transmissibility of different seasons to be independent. One reason for introducing such randomness is to allow for variable outcomes of seasonal outbreaks (as empirically observed), but also because the drift and transmissibility of a new strain depends on mutations in many loci on the virus, their common effect being highly complicated and thus conveniently modelled by random variables. The drift and transmissibility of a given season may very well depend on each other, perhaps most likely having negative correlation (one of them being large may be enough to become dominant). Another novel feature in our model is that we allow also the transmissibility to vary between seasons. As a consequence, two quantities of the seasonal outbreaks will vary: the initial growth and the final epidemic size, as also observed empirically (Chowell et al. 2008). Considering a stochastic model (for genetic drift and transmissibility) also affects the analysis and in particular the resulting seasonal epidemic outcome. Rather than converging to a fixed point or a deterministic periodic situation as in the deterministic setting, the stochastic model leads to a seasonal epidemic outbreak depending on previous outbreaks but still being random, having some limiting stationary distribution depending on the random vector (δ,τ).

In Section 2 we define the seasonal epidemic model with random genetic drift and transmissibility, and also characterize it as a Markov chain with a stationary distribution. In Section 3 we treat the case where immunity only lasts one season, for which more analytical progress is made. In Section 4 we present simulations for both when immunity lasts one or several seasons. The paper ends with a short discussion.

The multi-season model with random genetic drift and transmissibility

Characterization as a Markov chain and its stationary distribution

We now describe the epidemic model as a Markov chain. Given the integer r≥2 indicating how many seasons until immunity is completely lost, the state of our Markov chain is given by the vector (p(k),ι(k)), where p(k)=(p1(k),⋯pr(k)) describes the community distribution of time since last infection, and ι(k) their associated immunity levels ι(k)=(ι1(k),⋯,ιr-1(k)), with ιj(k)=(1-δk-1)·⋯·(1-δk-(j-1)). The vector (p(k),ι(k)) in fact only has dimension 2r-3, since pr(k)=1-∑j=1r-1pj(k) and ι1(k)=1.

We now show that the vector {(p(k),ι(k))}k=1∞ is a Markov chain driven by (δk,τk). Given (p(k),ι(k)), the next state in the Markov chain, (p(k+1),ι(k+1)), is determined by (δk,τk) as follows. The final outcome of the epidemic season k: z(k)=(z1(k),⋯,zr(k)) is determined as described in the previous subsection (if Re(k)≤1 then all zj(k)=0 and otherwise they are all strictly positive). The fraction having j seasons since last infection coming into the next epidemic season k+1 is then equal to the fraction having j-1 seasons since last infection in season k and who avoid getting infected in season k: pj(k+1)=pj-1(k)(1-zj-1(k)). The first and last components are different: pr(k+1)=pr(k)(1-zr(k))+pr-1(k)(1-zr-1(k)), and p1(k+1)=∑j=1rpj(k)zj(k) (=all who got infected season k). The immunity levels are simply shifted one step: ιj(k+1)=ιj-1(k)(1-δk), and ι1(k+1)=1 and ιr(k)=0.

We have hence shown that (p(k+1),ι(k+1)) is determined by (δk,τk) given (p(k),ι(k)), implying that {(p(k),ι(k))}k=1∞ is a Markov chain. A Markov chain is often described by its transition kernel π(·|·). It is in principle possible to determine the transition matrix π((p(k+1),ι(k+1))|(p(k),ι(k))), at least numerically, although it is quite complicated for general r. In Section 3 we make more analytical progress for the case r=2. Instead we end this section with the following theorem for our Markov chain, valid for arbitrary r.

Proposition 2.1

Assume the random vector (δ,τ) has a continuous density function with strictly positive support on [0,1)×[0,∞), and with an atom at δ=1 (no immunity) with positive probability. Then the Markov chain {(p(k),ι(k))}k=1∞ is recurrent, aperiodic, and converges to a stationary distribution π¯(·).

Proof

We first prove recurrence. We do this by showing that any state (p,ι) can reach the state (0,⋯,1,1,0,⋯,0) with pr=1 and ι1=1 and all other components being 0 (so no immunity). This will happen if the next r random pairs (δj,τj) have δj=1 and τj<1, an event with positive probability (τ=R0<1 imply no outbreak).

From state (0,⋯,1,1,0,⋯,0) we now show that any state (p,ι) can be reached after r seasons. We do this by induction in r. Start with r=2. We then want to find a τ such that the final outcome z=p1 for some given value p1, starting with no prior immunity. This is easy: we simply choose τ1 such that 1-p1=e-p1τ1, i.e. τ1=-ln(1-p1)/p1.

For the induction step we want to show that we can reach any fixed state (p1,⋯pr,ι1=1,⋯,ιr-1) after r seasons, starting with no immunity season 0. We use that, by induction, we can reach any state (p1′,⋯pr-1′,ι1′=1,⋯,ιr-2′) after r-1 seasons. Our task is hence to specify a vector (p1′,⋯pr-1′,ι1′=1,⋯,ιr-2′) and a drift and transmissibility (δ,τ) which result in the desired (p1,⋯pr,ι1=1,⋯,ιr-1).

We start with the immunities. Set δ=1-ι2, and ιj′=ιj+1/(1-δ),j=1,⋯,r-2, leading to the desired immunities.

We now want to specify the vector (p1′,⋯pr-1′) and transmissibility τ which, together with immunity vector (ι1′=1,⋯,ιr-2′) result in the vector (p1,⋯pr) next season.

If p1=0, implying that there was no outbreak the previous season, this is easy: we simply set pj′=pj+1, j=1,⋯,r-1, and we choose any τ<1 so that no outbreak will occur.

The case p1>0 is a bit more involved. The vector (p1,⋯pr) will be obtained by iteratively choosing τi, i=1,2,⋯ making our vector (p1(i),⋯pr(i)) approaching (p1,⋯pr) closer and closer.

Start with some arbitrary value larger than 1, e.g. τ1=1.5.

The aperiodicity follows from the fact that the immunity-free state (0,⋯,1,1,0,⋯,0) can return to itself with positive probability (if τ<1).

Since our Markov chain {(p(k),ι(k))}k=1∞ returns to the immunity-free state (0,⋯,1,1,0,⋯,0) in r steps with positive probability it is a Harris chain (Durrett 2019, Sec. 5.8). It then follows that it has stationary measure π¯(·) to which the chain converges irrespective of starting value. □

The assumption on the random vector (δ,τ) to have an atom at δ=1 (no immunity) with positive probability may sound too restrictive. Possibly, one may remove this assumption changing the proof strategy. Notice that when r=2 this assumption is not necessary (see Proposition 3.1).

Analytical results for the case r=2 where immunity is lost after two seasons

We now focus on the case where r=2, so immunity only lasts the season after being infected. In this case our vector (p(k),ι(k)) is simply the one dimensional quantity p1(k)=:p(k), since the outcome in season k only depends on (δk,τk) and how many that were infected last season p1(k)=z(k-1). We will hence study the distribution of the outcome some season given the outcome of the previous season. We drop the k-notation and hence consider the outcome of Re and z, given p. All results below are hence conditional on p, the fraction infected in the previous season.

We assume that joint probability distribution of the pair (δ,τ) has density q (with support in (0,1)×(0,+∞)), and we wish to explicitly compute some properties of the Markov chain in this specific case.

Bivariate distribution of effective reproduction ratio and attack ratio

Two important properties of a seasonal epidemic are its effective reproduction ratio Re and its final attack ratio z. Given a fixed known generation time distribution, the value of Re determines the initial exponential growth rate, and can be estimated in the first phase of an epidemic. The final attack ratio z (i.e. the fraction of the population that gets infected in one season) determines (together with the infection severity) the impact of an epidemic; by definition, it can be ascertained only after an epidemic is over, and may be difficult to estimate, because of under-reporting and other concurring infections.

Discussion

In the paper we have introduced a stochastic epidemic model for seasonal outbreaks (of e.g. influenza), where the virus has a random genetic drift and a new random transmissibility each season. Given the community immunities coming into season k, the epidemic outcome, described by the effective reproduction number Re(k) and final attack ratio z(k), is determined by the drift and transmissibility of this season’s virus strain. The novelty of our model lies in considering the seasonal drift as random, varying between seasons, and to also allow for transmissibility to vary between seasons.

The process of community immunities coming into next season, is shown to be a Markov chain having a stationary distribution. The case where immunity only lasts one season is studied in more detail. For this situation, we obtain analytical results for the transition distribution of the Markov chain, its stationary distribution, and also the conditional distribution of the final attack ratio, given the early epidemic growth (corresponding to Re(k)) and incoming immunity.

The hope is that our model can contribute to the understanding of how and why influenza outbreaks vary in size and initial growth between seasons, and also potentially help to improve precision in predicting the severity of an influenza season based on observing the start of the outbreak and knowing earlier seasons outcome.

Our model of course has several simplifications compared to empirical seasonal epidemics. The perhaps most important extensions for making the model more realistic, are a) to consider more than one circulating strain: influenza currently has three main circulating strains (AH1N1, AH3N2 and B) with separate attack ratios varying between seasons, and with interactions between the circulating strains (e.g. Yang et al. (2020)), and b) to allow for heterogeneities in the community, for example in terms of partial immunities and how these affect infectivity.

In our illustrations we assume that the drift and transmissibility of different seasons are independent. Our framework however allows for dependencies between seasons, for example that large genetic drifts rarely happen repeatedly between consecutive, and this can be incorporated, still keeping the Markov property in our model.

From an applied point of view, it would be interesting to try to fit the model to seasonal outbreaks over a range of seasons, and to analyse how the fitted stationary distribution, transition distribution, and early season predictions fit to data. It would also be of interest to analyse what underlying distribution of the drift and transmissibility that fits best to observed seasonal outbreak data, which may then be compared to other estimates of seasonal drift and transmissibility changes.

Of more mathematical curiosity, it would be interesting to see if a model allowing longer lasting immunity (larger r) fits better to data than when immunity only lasts one season. It would also be interesting to obtain qualitative results regarding concentration of randomness of the final size coming from knowing prior immunity and/or the initial growth rate, in line with what was illustrated numerically in Section 4.
