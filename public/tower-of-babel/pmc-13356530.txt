Packed Hydrogel Microfibers as Scaffolds Supporting Dynamic Cellular Behavior and Biomaterial Inks in 3D Printing
Authors: M Gregory Grewal, Remington M Martinez, Georgia T Helein, Montserrath G Ibarra Rivera, Rodolfo Martinez, Jenna L Sumey, Steven R Caliari, Christopher B Highley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTParticle‐based hydrogels have been used as injectable scaffolds, biomaterial inks for extrusion bioprinting, and permissive systems for 3D cell culture owing to their unique physical properties, including bulk yielding and porosity. These properties are in part governed by interparticle interactions and spatial organization, with limited potential to design these properties in systems based on spherical hydrogel microparticles. Here, we engineer particle‐based hydrogels where each particle is a discrete electrospun hydrogel microfiber that has been segmented to a length of 93 ± 51 µm, with a diameter of 1.6 ± 0.3 µm, presenting unique viscoelastic properties allowing stability without interparticle crosslinking (annealing). The fibers’ flexibility and high aspect ratios enable interactions among fibers that give packed hydrogel microfiber (PHM) materials that are mechanically robust, can stretch without breaking when strained, and exhibit tissue‐mimetic stress relaxation under constant strain. As cell culture scaffolds, shear‐induced alignment of the individual fibers within 3D printed PHM filaments provide topographical cues to cells that promote alignment. Cells embedded in 3D within PHMs spread due to the permissive microenvironment presented by the microfibers. This work highlights strengths of fiber‐based particle systems as dynamic and permissive scaffolds and printable biomaterials for tissue engineering and regenerative medicine.Keywords: bioprinting, granular hydrogels, hydrogels, microfibers, viscoelasticity

Introduction

Particle‐based hydrogels—hydrogel materials comprised of discrete hydrogel microparticles (HMPs) held in place by particle‐particle contact forces and, often, engineered interparticle interactions—have found diverse applications as biomaterials [1, 2, 3, 4]. They are inherently dynamic and microporous, which allow them to be used as injectable and extrudable materials that support cellular activities in tissue regeneration and growth [5, 6, 7, 8]. These properties arise from the formation of these materials from discrete HMPs, which are often spherical particles formed through microfluidic or emulsion approaches (on the order of 101–102 µm in diameter). The HMPs are packed to yield macroscale constructs held together by physical (e.g., contact [9, 10]) or chemical (e.g., covalent [5, 11]) interactions, or a combination of the two. Microporosity is intrinsic to particle‐based systems because of interstitial spaces between particles. Dynamic movement or flow of HMPs can occur when forces applied to particles exceed flow‐resisting interparticle interactions as in, for example, injection or extrusion processes that cause bulk flow [5, 10, 12, 13, 14].

The potential for particle‐based hydrogels to yield might be used to design materials that relax applied stresses [15] and support cell activities through yielding under low applied stress and cell‐relevant time scales [16, 17, 18, 19]. However, designing particle‐based hydrogels for enhanced flow is at odds with construct stability. This is because the potential for increases in both HMP movement and porosity among HMPs occurs with decreasing particle packing, but decreasing packing reduces interparticle interactions that stabilize the particle‐based material as a bulk even in the absence of interparticle bonds [9, 20, 21]. While interparticle crosslinking, or annealing, can used to enhance stability [5, 22, 23, 24, 25], it can reduce or eliminate the potential for particle flow, and it still requires particle‐particle contacts for interparticle crosslinking, limiting the interstitial space and microporosity that can be designed.

In approaches to address these challenges, particularly in designing particle‐based materials within enhanced porosity, which can influence cell behaviors including proliferation, spreading, migration, and secretion [13, 14, 26, 27], work has considered alternatives to spherical HMPs (aspect ratio ∼1). HMPs with increased aspect ratios have extended capabilities for engineering microscale features of particle‐based systems when assembled into a bulk hydrogel. For example, rod‐shaped HMPs (aspect ratios ranging from ∼ 2–20) enable larger pores with enhanced interconnectivity throughout a particle‐based hydrogel scaffold, in turn facilitating greater cell migration and infiltration [14, 23]. Irregular HMPs formed by fragmented continuous hydrogels can also increase porosity [28]. Additionally, hydrogel materials have also been processed to form long, flexible fibers that can align and entangle and be assembled into macroscale hydrogels [29, 30]—offering interactions at increased length scales compared to other, lower aspect ratio HMPs. These entanglements are useful in extrusion processes like injection and 3D printing [30, 31, 32, 33, 34] and can increase bulk stability of a particle‐based hydrogel formed from these fibers without secondary annealing [30, 32, 35].

Among advances with non‐spherical particles that have increased porosity, hydrogel microfibers whose diameters approach the nanoscale—with sub‐cellular dimensions on the order of 1 µm—can be used to form highly porous macroscale materials [33] that additionally present unique opportunities to design materials that yield to cell‐scale forces. Materials formed from small‐diameter fibers have been shown to permit dynamic cellular behaviors, as cells are able to displace and recruit microfibers to reorganize the structure of the scaffold, mimicking dynamics seen in native extracellular matrices (ECMs) [33, 36]. These small‐diameter fibers have been utilized to prepare fiber‐based hydrogel platforms, which have been stabilized through designed annealing interactions between fiber‐based particles [33, 37, 38]. They are also, like spherical HMP‐based hydrogels, extrudable and injectable. Work developing a fiber‐based hydrogel to deliver growth factors, showed the potential for unannealed fiber‐based systems to be delivered by minimally invasive injection [34].

Here, we developed unannealed, packed hydrogel microfibers (PHMs) as a bulk material with unique properties among particle‐based hydrogel systems. We sought to leverage the interfiber interactions within PHM systems in forming stable materials that are microporous and maintain dynamic properties useful in tissue engineering processes like bioprinting and in designing cell‐responsive 3D culture systems. We formed PHM scaffolds from high aspect ratio, small diameter microfibers and demonstrate their potential in designing materials that are strain yielding and that possess notable extensibility. Furthermore, toward achieving viscoelastic properties that are desirable in engineered biomaterial systems, we show that these PHM scaffolds dissipate stress in response to applied strains similar to biological materials. Additionally, our data suggest that these materials can maintain mechanical integrity with increasing interstitial fluid within the system, in other words robust materials can be designed with decreasing packing density and increasing porosity among the fibers, even in the absence of interfiber crosslinking. We demonstrate the ability to print stable filaments from PHM materials, also without interfiber crosslinking. Finally, we observe, through analysis of cell morphology that PHM scaffolds can influence cell behaviors through topographical cues, which can be dictated via extrusion and bioprinting, as well as through their distinct physical properties as 3D, permissive cell culture scaffolds. This work demonstrates the potential PHMs have for designing dynamic physical properties within hydrogel biomaterials and as a bioprintable material, presenting a platform that can be developed to recapitulate dynamic biophysical properties of the ECM and that might be processed through bioprinting into complex scaffold architectures.

Results and Discussion

Preparing Packed Hydrogel Microfiber Scaffolds

We formed PHM scaffolds by electrospinning and segmenting hydrogel microfibers to yield discrete fiber‐based particles with sub‐cellular length scale diameters and high aspect ratios. We sought 3D biomaterials that have structures that mimic the ECM, whose composition includes fibrous proteins like collagen. Electrospun fibers have long been studied for their potential to present ECM‐mimetic fibrous structure [39, 40], and here we sought to design 3D particle‐based materials whose bulks were formed from dynamic, moveable fiber components. Our microfibers were based on methacrylated hyaluronic acid (MeHA), because of MeHA's biocompatibility [41, 42] and its ability to be electrospun [43, 44] (1H NMR spectrum confirming MeHA synthesis shown in Figure S1). The use of an HA‐based material also allowed electrospun fibers to replicate features of native ECM through both fibrous topography [45, 46, 47, 48] and through a polymer backbone that is a glycosaminoglycan component of the ECM [49]. Methacrylate functionalization was used to photocrosslink the molecular structure of the hydrogel fibers immediately after electrospinning and prior to hydration (Figure 1A). This functionalization also supported coupling of the fibronectin‐mimetic, Arg‐Gly‐Asp (RGD) cell‐adhesion peptide to unreacted methacrylate groups present after photocrosslinking. This was achieved through a Michael addition reaction between methacrylates and a cysteine residue incorporated into the RGD‐containing peptides.

To create microfibers that can be packed together to form PHMs, MeHA fibers were crosslinked after electrospinning, hydrated, and then segmented via a series of triturations through needles of decreasing inner diameter (16 G, 18 G, and 20 G) [33, 34, 37, 50]. The resulting microfibers (Figure 1B) were 1.6 ± 0.3 µm in diameter and 93 ± 51 µm in length (Figure 1C and Figure S2). Microfiber diameter was unaffected by segmentation. Microfiber lengths shorted with decreasing needle diameter (Figure S3). After packing the suspension of discrete fibers by centrifugation, as a bulk they behaved as a solid at rest (Figure 1D), similar to most particle‐based hydrogels. Compared to particle‐based materials based on spherical particles, the high length:diameter aspect ratio of the microfibers in a PHM scaffold allows for unique long‐range interactions with numerous other microfiber particles. We expected that these interactions would allow for dilution of scaffolds from a fully packed state to increase inter‐fiber fluid content, providing more pore space among microfibers. Here, we defined the fully packed scaffolds as consisting of 100% v/v of the material recovered from centrifugation. The fully packed material, hereafter referred to as PHM‐100, was combined with known volumes of media to yield materials that were 90% v/v and 80% v/v fibers, referred to as PHM‐90 and PHM‐80, respectively (shown in Figure 1E). For example, PHM‐80 was formulated by combining PHM‐100 and medium at a 4:1 ratio.

Mechanical Characterization of PHMs

To characterize the physical properties of PHMs that are important for particle‐based hydrogels used in extrusion processes or in cell culture systems, PHM‐100, PHM‐90, and PHM‐80 were first tested for strain‐responsive transitions between solid‐like and liquid‐like states. These characteristics were evident in all dilutions through oscillatory shear rheology (Figure 2A and Figure S4) that cycled between low (1%) and high (250%) strains. In contrast to observations of particle‐based hydrogels formed using spherical particles [9, 10], the storage modulus recovered after removal of the high strain from the system was reduced (∼60%–70%) in all groups (Figure 2B). This reduction was statistically significant and observed after the first application of high strain, with no statistical significance in the percentage drop in modulus across dilutions. We attributed this PHM behavior to rearrangements of microfibers during rheology. Fiber rearrangement as a function of flow has been observed in dispersed fiber solutions [51, 52], and reductions in recovered strains have similarly been observed in packed fiber materials based on larger microstrands [30]. Here, because microfibers start in a packed, gel‐like state, we expect disorganization at the start of the experiment represents the maximumly entangled state for the fibers during the experiment, with entanglement interactions contributing to the initial mechanical properties of the scaffold. During the high‐strain regimes, entanglements are expected to be disrupted, resulting in re‐organization of the material into a state where a fraction of entanglements that contribute to long range interactions are lost.

PHMs behave like viscoelastic solids under low strain and can rapidly transition between liquid‐like and solid‐like behaviors under the application and removal of high strain. (B) Quantification (n = 4) of recovery after strain shows that PHM scaffolds recover ∼60%–70% of their initial modulus prior to the addition of high strain – suggesting that organization of discrete fibers influences overall mechanical properties. Flexible fibers are known to have unique properties. They are dynamic, and their mechanical responses, including bending and entangling, are a function of external forces that are known to scale with viscosity and shear rate in viscous flows. However, these dynamic behaviors are moderated by increases in fibers‘ bending stiffnesses, which increase with fiber diameters and Young's moduli and decrease with fiber lengths [31, 52]. When packed into macroscale hydrogels, these physical properties are expected to result in mechanical differences seen between systems with larger diameter fibers [30] versus those with smaller diameters. These properties should underlie differences where fiber diameters are similar, but shorter fibers exhibit lower yield strains compared to longer fibers as seen, for example, in fiber systems with similar diameters [33] to those here. Specifically, bulk‐stabilizing interactions in packed fibers are expected to increase with increasing entanglement [51] and with interfacial interactions (e.g. steric interactions) between fibers in a given volume. Thus the stresses and strains needed for yielding should increase in packed fiber systems where fibers entangle more per volume. This will occur where smaller diameters and longer fibers allow easier bending and where packing densities are greater. Analogous to interfacial interactions in granular hydrogel systems [9], increased steric interactions within denser‐packed systems are expected to result in higher yield strains, with hydrogel molecular design impacting interfacial fiber friction.

As in other particle‐based hydrogel and shear thinning systems, the ability to convert from a solid‐like to a liquid‐like state above a yield strain (the crossover point between G’ and G’ in a strain sweep, Figure 2C) indicates the potential for a material to flow under high‐strain conditions, facilitating injection or extrusion processes. Interestingly, the 136% yield strain (Figure 2D) for PHM‐100 is notably larger than other particle‐based hydrogel systems [10, 14]. Compared to PHM‐100, the PHM‐90 and PHM‐80 groups yielded statistically decreased yield strains of 56% and 57%, respectively, similar to yield strains of other reported particle‐based hydrogel systems [10, 14], but with diluted material density and increased porosity. Here, measurements indicated that all test groups could transition from a solid‐like state to a liquid‐like state upon the application of high strain—and they could return to a solid‐like state upon the removal of large strains. While all yield strains were in range (∼1 order of magnitude) of those seen in particle‐based hydrogels used in extrusion processes, the increased yield strains possible in PHM‐100 formulations indicate the potential for enhanced stability in particle‐based hydrogels with concentrated microfibers. The maintenance of a strain‐yielding behavior despite significant dilution in PHM‐90 and PHM‐80 indicated the potential for enhanced stability in porous biomaterials formed from microfibers.

To determine whether these properties might be generalizable to fiber‐based materials formed in this way, we formulated PHMs from other hyaluronic acid‐based biomaterials—acrylate‐ and norbornene‐modified HAs (AHA and NorHA)—and PEG‐hydrogel fibers generated using PEG‐thiol and PEG‐norbornene with an excess of norbornene groups. These materials were all prepared with fibers diluted to the equivalent of PHM‐80, and like the MeHA fiber‐based materials, the AHA, NorHA, and PEG PHM systems all behaved as elastic solids under low strain, but transitioned to liquid‐like behavior under high strain with yielding around 50% strain (Figure S5). Additionally, the transition from solid‐like to liquid‐like behavior was rapid and repeatable upon application and removal of high strain (Figure S5).

Evaluating the Extensibility of PHM Scaffolds

Anticipating that long‐range interactions between microfibers in PHM scaffolds would support unique behavior of the particle‐based system as a bulk material, we next examined their extensibility. We conducted a modified version of filament stretching extensional rheology (FiSER) [53, 54], in which a material can be stretched as a filament, allowing strain to failure to be observed as a measure of material extensibility or stretchability. We hypothesized that discrete fibers within the filament would participate in interactions at extended length scales compared to conventional particle‐based materials based on spherical hydrogel microparticles, resulting in highly extensible materials when stretched. In particle‐based hydrogels formed from spherical particles, a given particle‘s interactions would be restricted to the jamming forces exerted by a limited number of neighboring particles. Here, because of the high aspect ratios giving rise to long range interactions, we expected that PHM scaffolds would exhibit bulk stretching and extensibility.

In modified FiSER testing, all groups of PHMs exhibited strain‐to‐break values of 2000%–2500% (Figure 3A), indicating that fibers maintained bulk‐stabilizing interactions in response to stretching. Notably, while PHM‐100 exhibited the highest degree of stretchability (∼2500%), no statistical differences were seen between groups. In these measurements, the normal forces sustained by the filaments during extension (Figure 3B) were observed to decrease with respect to hydrogel microfiber density: the normal force required to stretch PHM‐80 was approximately 50% of the force required for PHM‐100. These observations combine to suggest that the extensibility of a PHM scaffold is dictated by fiber geometry, while the density of fiber‐fiber interactions—the combination of entanglements, surface‐surface interactions, and jamming forces that restrict microscale reorganization—dictate the forces sustained during stretching. Correspondingly, the normal force profiles for each group exhibited the same trend as they are stretched to failure at the statistically similar strains. This extensibility (seen in Figure 3C for PHM‐100) is a challenging property to achieve in many biomaterial systems, but arises here from formulating hydrogel systems from packed hydrogel microfibers without additional dissolved polymers between particles or interfiber crosslinking.

Characterizing Viscoelasticity and Stress Relaxation in PHM Scaffolds

Given the dynamic qualities observed in PHM materials, we were interested in further characterizing their viscoelastic responses to applied stresses to determine whether microfiber rearrangement in response to applied strain would allow stress relaxation. Endogenous tissue exhibits a host of complex, time‐dependent mechanical properties that require the design of dynamic hydrogel molecular architectures for recapitulation in continuous hydrogel systems [17, 55]. For example, ECM structural components like collagen fibers present in many native tissue microenvironments enable the dissipation of stress over time, thereby contributing to nonlinear viscoelasticity profiles [56]. We hypothesized that noncovalent interactions between individual fibers in 3D PHM materials would exhibit stress relaxation more similarly to native ECM than covalent interactions, which are generally unable to rearrange. As discussed, fibers within the PHM hydrogels can sustain body forces and behave elastically under certain applied strain timescales even without covalent bonding, but have the potential to slide past each other and reorganize, which should allow them to dissipate forces in sustained applied strains.

To quantify PHM scaffold viscoelasticity as a function of fiber density in fully uncrosslinked systems, we measured the loss modulus (i.e., viscous component, G’) versus storage modulus (i.e., elastic component, G’) in low strain time sweeps (1%, 1 Hz) (Figure 4A). All PHM formulations exhibited solid‐like behaviors under these conditions with storage moduli ranging from ∼50 Pa for PHM‐80 to ∼150 Pa for PHM‐100. Additionally, all groups demonstrated viscoelastic behavior with storage moduli approximately 5x greater than loss moduli (Figure 4A, grey dashed line illustrates where G′ = 5 × G′′). Similar linear relationships exist between storage and loss moduli in biological tissues and materials, where it has been observed that storage moduli are typically 10x greater than loss moduli [55].

Characterization of viscoelasticity and stress relaxation properties. (A) Plot of shear loss modulus versus shear storage modulus of PHMs. Grey dashed line and black dashed line represent where G’ is equivalent to 5x G″ and 10x G′, respectively. Non‐crosslinked groups reside near the 5x trend, whereas the addition of secondary annealing to stabilize the material shifts the viscoelasticity to the 10x trend, which is characteristic of many biological tissues [45]. (B) Plot of stress relaxation tests where a 15% constant shear strain was applied to the systems and resultant stress was observed as a function of time. All groups exhibit relatively rapid stress relaxation and mimic profiles characteristic of viscoelastic solids. PHM‐100, PHM‐90, and PHM‐80 are compared with the PHM‐100 High group.

Though focused on PHMs without interfiber crosslinking, we expected that introducing of interfiber bonds could allow design of systems with increased storage moduli, demonstrating potential for modulating viscoelastic behavior in the biomaterial. We expected that increasing interfiber crosslinking within PHMs would result in increasingly elastic storage of applied stresses relative to their viscous dissipation. In other words, through covalent crosslinking, tan δ, the ratio of the loss modulus to storage modulus, would be controlled to become smaller with increasing crosslinking. To test this, we designed PHMs where interparticle crosslinking was modulated by the number of potential crosslinking sites on the surface through conversion of methacrylates to amines, which are not photoreactive. This allowed a photoinitiated reaction where the dosage of light was set to achieve full crosslinking throughout all systems used. We used a fully packed PHM formulation, PHM‐100, as the uncrosslinked control by not initiating interfiber crosslinking in this group, as in all experiments to this point. We created a highly crosslinked PHM system (PHM‐100 High) by photocrosslinking a PHM‐100 system where less than 5% of surface methacrylates, as determined from reaction with ninhydrin, were converted to amines—this conversion was achieved under conditions similar to the RGD modification used in the PHM‐100 group. To create a PHM system with low interfiber crosslinking but the same fiber concentration (PHM‐100 Low), we used MeHA where approximately 60% of the methacrylates were converted to amines, confirmed by NMR and ninhydrin assay (Figure S6), prior to annealing.

Rheology confirmed annealing (Figure S7) in both PHM‐100 High and PHM‐100 Low through increases in storage moduli, which were substantially larger in PHM‐100 High compared to PHM‐100 Low (Figure 4A, shaded circles). In both groups, interfiber crosslinking shifted the G’:G″ ratio toward the 10:1 (Figure 4A, dashed black line indicating G′ = 10 × G″) trend observed across biological tissues. Tan δ was approximated halved from 0.17 in PHM‐100 to 0.08 in PHM‐100 Low, indicating a marked increase in elasticity relative to viscous dissipation of force. It was almost halved again, to 0.05, in PHM‐100 High as elasticity increased further. Interparticle crosslinking might thus be designed to modulate bulk viscoelasticity in PHM hydrogel systems, which may be of value in engineering systems for regeneration, engineering tissues, or designing microphysiological systems.

Increases in elasticity appeared to be driven predominantly by the degree of covalent crosslinking in the system (Figure 4 and Figure S7), here related to methacrylate content and their photocrosslinking between fiber surfaces. High degrees of amine modification of ∼90% in MeHA reacted with 500 mM cysteamine during amination (the ”treated“ group in Figure S6C) were observed to eliminate the crosslinking response seen in 100% MeHA materials that were untreated with cysteamine (Figure S6). However, absent photocrosslinking, amine modification may have slight impacts on interfiber interactions in packed fiber systems, as increasing amine content correlates to modest decreases in elastic moduli seen prior to crosslinking in comparisons between PHM‐100 Low and PHM‐100 High (Figure S7) and between highly‐aminated MeHA and unmodified MeHA (Figure S6C). It is possible that amines are increasing hydrophilicity and thereby weakening hydrophobic interactions that might be contributing to the elastic modulus in unannealed systems particularly.

We next sought to evaluate stress relaxation in PHM scaffolds, here again considering scaffolds without interfiber crosslinking. Stress relaxation influences dynamic cellular behaviors in material environments [55, 56, 57, 58]; but, as mentioned, designing hydrogel systems that stress relax often requires careful consideration of molecular network design [17, 19, 58, 59]. Here, to analyze bulk stress relaxation behaviors inherent to the fiber‐based formulation of the PHM system, we applied a shear strain of 15% to the sample and recorded the resultant stress as a function of time. PHM‐100, PHM‐90, and PHM‐80 all exhibited stress relaxing responses (Figure 4B, plots of normalized stress), with relaxation happening faster and to a greater extent as fiber concentration decreases and porosity increases in PHM scaffolds, as expected in formulations where there is more space for fibers to reorganize in response to the applied strain and reduced interactions between fibers on a per volume basis. Expecting that interfiber crosslinking would attenuate relaxation, we tested the response of PHM‐100 High to the same strain (15%). We observed modest (Figure 4B) stress relaxation, with the covalent interfiber crosslinking restricting the ability of the microfibers to move and preventing relaxation to the same extent seen in non‐crosslinked groups (Figure 4B).

Considering the relaxation time scales, which are typically faster in physiological systems than is achieved in engineered hydrogels, we saw that relaxation profiles of the PHM hydrogels began to plateau within 100 s of stress being applied. Through the use of a viscoelastic standard linear solid model (Figure S8), characteristic relaxation times ( τ ) were calculated [60]. All groups, including the PHM‐100 High crosslinked material, exhibited most of their stress relaxation within a characteristic time of approximately 5–10 s, indicating that PHM systems respond to applied strains rapidly. This bulk stress relaxation arises from the dynamic behaviors of the microfiber components. From these data, we expect non‐crosslinked PHM materials to be useful in engineering soft tissue systems in vitro, and in engineering both soft and stiffer tissues (via secondary crosslinking) to model physiological systems where ∼100s and faster relaxation times are desired.

Extrusion of PHM Biomaterial Inks

Based on the properties observed in the PHM systems, and following from studies involving fibrous hydrogel assemblies with higher porosity that have been extruded into support baths and stabilized by interfiber crosslinking [33] as well as jammed microfiber‐based hydrogels delivered via injection [34], we expected the PHM materials to be applicable as extrudable biomaterials, including in bioprinting. We confirmed that PHM biomaterials were shear‐thinning, a valuable property in extrusion‐based 3D printing (Figure S9). Shear‐thinning was seen in all PHM groups tested, across functional groups and polymer backbones tested, and is expected to be feature of the PHM formulation of hydrogels in the absence of supramolecular or designed interfiber crosslinking interactions. In controlled extrusion from a 3D printer, we first printed 2 cm vertical filaments of PHM‐100 from a 22 G needle nozzle (ID: 0.413 mm); as before, without interparticle crosslinking. This yielded filaments with a diameter of ∼0.5 mm (Figure 5A i, Video S1). Toward observing the stability of the printed PHM filaments stabilized strictly by noncovalent fiber‐fiber interactions, we translated the nozzle 1 cm horizontally without further extrusion (Figure 5A ii) and then back to the original position (Figure 5A iii). As expected from extensibility tests, filaments sustained this movement without breakage. Additionally, filaments were observed to have stretched when extended without extrusion (Figure 5A iii, dashed circle), in line with PHM materials’ stress relaxation behavior. To further observe the extent to which filaments formed from PHMs were extensible, the nozzle was moved vertically an additional 1 cm without extrusion (Figure 5A iv). Filaments stretched, but did not break, which—as in extensibility testing—was attributed to the high‐aspect ratio particle composition's long‐range interactions among microfibers. Here, as biomaterial ink for bioprinting, these provided stability to the macroscale structure to a degree that is not seen in other particle‐based systems, such as granular hydrogels based on spherical particles also used in bioprinting [10].

Finally, toward demonstrating the stability of PHM‐based filaments in supporting body forces without interfiber crosslinking after deposition—which is critical to maintaining print fidelity [61]—we extruded filaments horizontally across the posts positioned at the corners of a 2.5 × 2.5 cm square (Figure 5B). During printing, filaments were able to span spaces between posts, retaining a cylindrical cross section and without breaking. The ability to relax stress was seen in the filaments’ elongation under gravity, which consistently stopped after a little sagging (Video S2), potentially reflecting strain‐stiffening within the filament, a property which was not studied further here. The PHMs’ combined dynamic behaviors and interparticle interactions that support material integrity will be valuable in 3D printing, and potentially other processes, where depositing hydrogels that can maintain structural fidelity is critical. Additionally, the ability to design materials for annealing, as in the PHM‐100 High and Low materials, will extend possible applications.

We expected that the extrusion process might also affect the microscale organization of the fibers in the PHM filaments. Both rheological studies and FiSER testing suggested fibers’ organization within PHM scaffolds are influenced by high shear. Shear in extrusion processes has also been used to induce anisotropic alignment in fibers embedded in continuous hydrogel materials [50], in larger hydrogel microstrands [30], in microfiber suspensions [51], and in self‐assembled supramolecular nanofibers [62]. Here, to visualize fiber orientation in extruded filaments, we mixed nonfluorescent fibers with fluorophore‐tagged fibers at a 10:1 ratio and printed PHM‐100 onto glass coverslips. As expected, fibers aligned in the direction of the applied shear (Figure 5C,D). Fiber alignment follows the long axis of the filament, so by altering needle direction (Figure S10), fibers within printed structure might be oriented orthogonally, potentially allowing anisotropic material topographies to be specified.

To demonstrate PHMs as biomaterial inks that can flow during printing and sustain structure after printing, we printed macroscale constructs in the shapes of a pyramid, cube, and tube (Figure 6, Videos S3–S5). While interfiber crosslinking might ultimately be used to stabilize these structures, here there was no interfiber crosslinking to demonstrate recovery of structure‐stabilizing elastic properties after deposition. Handling the printed tube (Video S6) qualitatively evidenced the softness of the materials as well as PHM scaffolds’ extensibility and potential to revert back to unorganized structure, similar to granular hydrogels [10], in the absence of stabilizing crosslinking. While we did not optimize for including cells within the biomaterial inks here, we expect future work will identify material formulations and print parameters that support extrusion of cell‐laden bioinks.

Topography and Dynamic Fiber Rearrangement in PHM Scaffolds Influence 2D and 3D Cell Culture

Because the PHM materials consisted entirely of small‐diameter electrospun fibers, known to present microscale topography and contact guidance cues to cells [63, 64, 65, 66], we wanted to know if we could use microfiber alignment in filaments to present surface topography that influence cell behavior. We expected that extruded filaments’ surface topographies would cause cellular alignment with microfibers aligned during extrusion of PHMs. To test this, we printed PHM‐100 with 1 mM RGD. Here, prior to cell seeding, we used photocrosslinking after printing to create interfiber crosslinks that would stabilized the filament during culture. Immortalized murine myoblasts (C2C12s), which are known to respond to alignment cues [63], were seeded atop the crosslinked PHM‐100 filament (Figure 7A). Following a 2 day culture period, cytoskeletal staining showed that cells aligned with the direction of microfibers in the filament (Figure 7B,C), indicating that the microscale topography provided contact guidance influencing cellular organization.

(A) Process schematic for extruding PHM‐100, crosslinking the individual fibers together to stabilize the filament, then seeding C2C12 cells on top of the scaffold. (B) Fluorescent micrograph of C2C12s tagged with AlexaFluor‐488 phalloidin for visualization of cell alignment. (C) Quantification (n = 210) of cell alignment with 0 degrees corresponding to the direction of shear (white arrow). The microscale topography of the extruded filament induces alignment of C2C12 cells cultured on top of the scaffold.

Next, we were interested in determining whether microfibers’ ability to move and reorganize would support dynamic cell behaviors in 3D environments formed from PHM materials that lacked interfiber crosslinking. Since our most fiber‐dense formulation (PHM‐100) was soft, viscoelastic, and stress‐relaxing we expected that C2C12s would be able to reorganize constituent microfibers allowing them to extend into the surrounding material. We expected to see cell spreading not long after seeding cells into these materials as opposed to cells maintaining rounded morphologies typical in nonpermissive, elastic 3D hydrogels [67, 68]. To test this, we first cultured C2C12s within PHM‐100 scaffolds that underwent no interfiber crosslinking, in order to maintain the dynamic properties observed in previous experimentation. The cell‐containing scaffold was then placed into a PDMS mold and covered with 100 µm pore filter paper to prevent any erosion of PHM‐100 while in culture (Figure 8A). After 1 day in culture, cytoskeletal staining showed that C2C12s were able to spread extensively in PHM‐100 (Figure 8B and Figure S11). Cells were measured with a wide range of projected cell areas, with cell shape indices (CSIs) showing a loss of circularity (Figure 8C,D: CSI = 4πA/P2; this value is 1 circular cells and decreases as circularity is lost).

PHM scaffolds support 3D cell culture. (A) Process schematic for culturing C2C12 cells within a PHM‐100 support using PDMS wells to contain the scaffolds. (B) C2C12s stained with AlexaFluor‐488 phalloidin (1 d) spreading in the non‐crosslinked PHM‐100 scaffold. (C‐D) Quantification (n = 91) of projected cell area and cell shape index reveal ranges of cell spreading and circularity, further supporting the permissivity of PHMs at short timescales. Scalebar = 100 µm.

Finally, to demonstrate both longer term cultures of cells wtihin PHM scaffolds and that the absence of interfiber crosslinking was sufficient to support dynamic cellular activities, we encapsulated C2C12s in 3D within PHM‐100 scaffolds where fibers were either subsequently crosslinked to one another or left unannealed. At all time points observed over 7 d, stark differences were seen between the cells encapsulated within environments in which fibers could move versus those where they could not (Figure 9). At 2, 5, and 7 days after encapsulation, C2C12s in annealed PHM‐100 scaffolds remained spherical (Figure 9A, top row), unspread as they were constrained in all directions, with actin area per cell increasing from 80 to 160 µm2. Similar spherical morphologies are seen in 3D in continuous hydrogels that lack degradable or labile crosslinking [68, 69]. In uncrosslinked PHM‐100 scaffolds, C2C12s spread (Figure 9A, bottom row) and interacted extensively over all time points. Cell spread area increased over the 7 days in culture (Figure 9A, bottom row), with actin area per cell increasing from 100 to 430 µm2. Areas were measured as significantly greater at all timepoints when PHM‐100 scaffolds were uncrosslinked compared to when fibers were annealed (Figure 9B). These observations suggest that the uncrosslinked scaffold presents a dynamic environment where yielding permits cell spreading and activity that can lead to multicellular interactions without the need to engineer material degradation. Similar spreading is readily seen in standard 2D cultures of C2C12s (Figure S12). We also found that the presence of RGD did not appear to influence cell spreading within 3D environments (Figure S13). It was not necessary to facilitate cell spreading or interactions and did not appear to have any effect on cells remaining in the fibers versus leaving the PHM 3D environment, in contrast to it being necessary for adhesion on fiber surfaces.

PHM scaffolds without interfiber crosslinking support dynamic cellular behaviors. (A) C2C12s encapsulated (10 million cells mL−1) within PHM‐100 scaffolds where fibers were crosslinked to one another (top row) were unable to spread over 7 days in culture (blue = nuclei, red = actin). When C2C12s were encapsulated and cultured under the same conditions in PHM‐100 scaffolds in which fibers were not crosslinked to one another (bottom row), extensive cell spreading was observed. (B) Actin area per cell at days 2, 5, and 7, was significantly greater in scaffolds where fibers were not crosslinked to one another at all timepoints. Modest increases in actin area in the uncrosslinked scaffolds reflect increased actin staining immediately around cells. Large increases were seen in the uncrosslinked scaffolds. n = 10, * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01 Scalebars = 250 µm.

Our observations indicate that PHM scaffolds can provide 3D environments that support dynamic cellular activity. Uncrosslinked PHMs supported robust and evolving cultures of encapsulated cells (Figure 9 and Figure S13) over days, with cells restricted in their engagment with their surroundings, remaining spherical, when fibers within dense PHMs were crosslinked to one another. The cell spreading observed here was in an uncrosslinked fiber‐dense formulation, indicating PHMs can be reorganized in response to cells’ movements, with the potential to permit dynamic 3D behaviors in engineered systems. In comparison other particle‐based materials formed from spherical microgels and hydrogel strands with diameters are on the order of 101–102 µm, PHMs present a physical environment to cells where small‐diameter, soft microfiber particles with diameters on the order of 1 µm can be displaced by cells. While less dense fiber‐based materials with crosslinking support dynamic cellular behaviors [33], we expect that the unique yielding of features here, achieved when fibers in PHM scaffolds remain uncrosslinked, might support in vitro studies and tissue engineering applications where multicellular organization requires an engineered environment that permits cell spreading, motility, proliferation, and organization. Engineered for in vivo applications, yielding in PHM materials would also cell ingrowth from surrounding tissue in regenerative medicine applications [34].

Observations of cellular responses indicate that PHMs present environments that can, at the cell scale, be altered by forces exerted by cells on their microenvironment. These dynamic properties give rise, at the macroscale, to the dynamic viscoelastic properties measured previously. Taken together, PHM scaffolds have potential as platforms for bioprinting and engineering dynamic cellular microenvironments that have permissive properties by design, extending what can currently be designed into existing continuous and particle‐based hydrogels.

Conclusions and Future Outlook

Packed hydrogel microfibers offer exciting opportunities among biomaterials through their presentation of robust bulk properties emerging from a microenvironment comprised of individual fibers that cells can readily interrogate, reorganize, and migrate around. Here, we have shown that PHM scaffolds, comprised solely of discrete electrospun microfibers, can form stable, cell‐instructive biomaterials absent interparticle crosslinking. These materials are behave as elastic bulk materials that can flow like a liquid under sufficient strain, returning to solid‐like behaviors when high strains are removed. Their strain yielding responses will enable application in extrusion or injection processes. The fibers’ high‐aspect ratios and long‐range interactions give rise to robust bulk materials that behave elastically below a yield strain. These interactions also enable high degrees of extensibility in packed scaffolds. PHMs can be stretched to greater than 2000% of their original height. PHM scaffolds can quickly (∼10 s) dissipate stresses applied to them. Macroscale extrusion printing demonstrates filament fidelity and robust stability, even without secondary crosslinking to stabilize filaments. Shear‐induced alignment of component microfibers during extrusion can be leveraged to direct the alignment of cells in contact with printed filaments. Cells seeded within these materials—or potentially infiltrating into these materials—experience permissive, 3D environments that allow dynamic cellular interactions with their surrounding microenvironments.

Control over scaffold properties through the molecular design of the fibers that comprise the macroscale scaffold, through PHM fiber preparation and formation into a bulk material, and through additional processing such as bioprinting is expected to support the design of ECM‐like and cell instructive materials for diverse in vitro and in vivo applications. Like other particle‐based and granular hydrogel systems, fiber‐based PHM scaffolds offer modular approaches to engineering physical and chemical properties in hydrogel systems, and—in conjunction with their biophysical properties—are thus expected to present a platform for the design of biomaterials that can be used in applications to study cell‐microenvironment interactions, to form microphysiological systems, and to engineer and regenerate tissue.

Experimental Methods

All reagents were purchased from Millipore Sigma, unless otherwise stated.

Synthesis of Hyaluronic Acid Derivatives

Hyaluronic acid (HA) was functionalized with methacrylates as previously discussed [70]. Briefly, sodium hyaluronate (Lifecore, 60 kDa) was dissolved in deionized water at 2% w/v. While maintaining the solution at a pH of ∼ 8.5‐9, methacrylic anhydride (Sigma Aldrich, 4.83 mL per g HA) was added dropwise to the solution. The reaction mixture was maintained at a pH of 8.5‐9 for 6 h on ice, then continued to react at room temperature overnight. The reaction was dialyzed against deionized water (SpectraPor, 6–8 kDa molecular weight cutoff) at room temperature for 5 days, then frozen and lyophilized to dryness. The final methacrylate functionalization was 100% by quantification with 1H NMR (500 MHz Varian Inova 500) (Figure S1).

Previously described reactions were also used to functionalize HA with acrylates [71, 72] or norbornenes [73]. A tetrabutylammonium salt of HA (HA‐TBA) was prepared by ion exchange of sodium hyaluronate against Dowex‐100 resin, neutralized by tetrabutylammonium hydroxide. To synthesize acrylated HA (AHA), HA‐TBA was then dissolved in anhydrous dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) with 4‐(dimethylamino)pyridine (DMAP, 1.5:1 M ratio with HA‐TBA repeat units), di‐tert‐butyl decarbonate (Boc2O, 0.4:1 M ratio with HA‐TBA repeat units), and acrylic acid (3:1 M ratio to HA‐TBA repeat units) under nitrogen and reacted for 4 h at 45°C. To synthesize norbornene‐functionalized HA (NorHA), 5‐norbornene‐2‐carboxylic acid was used instead of acrylic acid.

Electrospinning Hydrogel Microfibers

To electrospin HA derivatives, solutions consisting of 3% w/v modified HA, 2.5% w/v polyethylene oxide (PEO, 900 kDa), and 0.05% w/v 2‐hydroxy‐4’‐(2‐hydroxyethoxy)‐2‐methylpropiophenone (HHMP, also known as Irgacure 2959) were mixed overnight in DI H2O. To fluorescently tag microfibers for characterization and visualization, 0.4% w/v fluorescein isothiocyanate‐dextran (FITC‐dextran) was included in the electrospinning solution. The modified HA solution was extruded through a 16‐gauge needle at a rate of 0.5 mL h−1 with an applied voltage of 12–14 kV. Microfibers were collected on a negatively charged (‐4 kV) rotating mandrel (DOXA Microfluidics) moving at a linear velocity of 10 m s−1. Fiber batches were collected for 1 h before a 2 min UV crosslinking step (365 nm, 5 mW cm−2, VWR UV Crosslinker) to stabilize fibers for subsequent segmentation steps. For electrospinning NorHA, dithiothreitol (DTT) was included in the solution with a molar ratio of 0.7:1 thiol:norbornene. For electrospinning polyethylene glycol (PEG) fibers, a solution consisting of 7% w/v 4‐arm PEG‐thiol (MW 10 kDa, from JenKem), 10% w/v 8‐arm PEG‐norbornene (MW 20 kDa, JemKem), 5% w/v PEO (400 kDa), and 0.05% w/v HHMP was prepared and processed as described above, for electrospinning, and below, for PHM formation.

Preparation of PHMs

Fibers were hydrated in PBS for at least 1 h and then fragmented through a series of extrusion steps to yield small fiber segments. Beginning with a 16‐gauge needle, the fiber solution was passed up and down the needle 25x to preliminarily break up the fibers. This process was repeated with an 18‐gauge needle, and finally a 20‐gauge needle to yield the final fiber segments. The resulting solution was then centrifuged, the supernatant was discarded to remove the PEO and unreacted HHMP, and the fibers were resuspended in a known volume of PBS to yield a stock concentration of 10% v/v. Fiber stock solutions were stored at 4°C until further use.

Fluorescently tagged fibers were diluted and imaged on a Leica DMi8 widefield fluorescence microscope to characterize fiber diameter and length (n > 150 fibers) post‐segmentation. For cell culture assays, MeHA fibers were functionalized with a fibronectin‐mimetic Arg‐Gly‐Asp (RGD)‐containing adhesive peptide (GCGYGRGDSPG, Genscript) via the thiol‐Michael addition reaction to promote integrin‐mediated cellular interactions in the scaffold. Briefly, fibers were suspended at 10% v/v in the presence of 1 mM RGD, the pH of the solution was elevated to 8 using triethanolamine and allowed to react for 2 h at 37°C. Fiber solutions were centrifuged and resuspended in PBS thrice to remove unreacted molecules prior to packing.

Fiber solutions were packed (condensed into a minimal volume with minimal solvent between the fibers) via centrifugation at 10 000 RCF for 10 min, with all remaining supernatant decanted to yield the strain‐responsive PHMs used within this work. PHMs were either used as is (denoted as PHM‐100), or further diluted with known volumes of PBS to yield 90% v/v and 80% v/v (denoted as PHM‐90 and PHM‐80) relative to the original packing density to increase interstitial space between fibers.

Preparation of Crosslinkable PHMs

MeHA‐based PHMs containing RGD surface modifications, prepared as described above, could be packed, also as described above, in media containing HHMP and photocrosslinked, as described below. Alternatively, for comparing how degree of crosslinking within a PHM hydrogel affects viscoelasticity, groups with relatively high and low crosslinking were prepared. For high crosslinking (the PHM‐100 High group), MeHA fibers were treated with 1 mM cysteamine (specifically 2‐aminoethanethiol) at pH 8 for 2 h at 37°C to convert methacrylate moieties to amines. In the PHM‐100 High group, the same concentration and conditions for conjugation cysteamine were used as for RGD functionalization—leaving most pendant methacrylates unconverted. For low crosslinking, fibers (used in the group PHM‐100 Low) were treated with 250 mM cysteamine at pH 8 for 2 h at 37°C to convert relatively more methacrylate groups and reduce the potential for methacrylate‐mediated crosslinking. Crosslinked fiber scaffolds were prepared by suspending the fiber segments at 10% v/v in a 0.1% w/v HHMP solution, packed via centrifugation as previously described, handled for the experiment (e.g., rheology or extrusion), then treated with 2 min of UV light at 5 mW cm−2 to induce crosslinking of the unquenched methacrylate groups.

Because quantification of methacrylate conversion was imprecise with NMR for low amine modification, such as 1 mM cysteamine group to derive the PHM‐100 High group, ninhydrin was used to confirm amine modification. MeHA (100% degree of substitution) was treated as above (1 mM cysteamine for PHM‐100 High and 250 mM for PHM‐100 Low). Two additional groups were reacted with either 0 mM or 500 mM cysteamine at pH 8 and 37°C for 2 h, All cysteamine‐modified MeHAs were dialyzed and precipitated in ethanol from a 5 mg/mL solution (1:10 volumetric ratio) to remove trace reactants. All MeHA derivatives were then reconstituted in solutions of known wt.% in water. Standard curves were generated from freshly prepared cysteamine solution in water. Ninhydrin solution (2% w/v, Sigma Aldrich), was added to samples with a 1:2 ratio of ninhydrin solution to sample solution; here, we used 125 µL:250 µL on clear‐bottomed 96 well plates. Plates were sealed and heated at 90°C for 20 min and allowed to cool to room temperature before measuring absorbance at 570 nm. Concentrations of amines in samples were calculated from standard curves.

Through methacrylate hydrogen peaks (Figure S1), NMR indicated a 40% peak reduction of methacrylates, indicating a 60% amination in the PHM‐100 Low group. Ninhydrin assays confirmed 64% amine‐modification of MeHA reacted with 250 mM cysteamine for 2 h at pH 8 (Figure S6). NMR could not easily assess the small degree of modification in the PHM‐100 High group. The ninhydrin assay indicated that the MeHA reacted with 1 mM cysteamine resulted in a concentration of about 0.05 mM cysteamine for 1.4 mM of disaccharide in the final product, corresponding to 3.5% modification. Similarly, in the Michael addition using 500 mM cysteamine, a high methacrylate conversion to amines of nearly 90% was measured.

Oscillatory Rheological Characterization

Mechanical properties of PHMs were determined via rheological measurements (Anton Paar MCR 302 rheometer) using a 25 mm parallel plate geometry, a solvent trap, and a gap height of 200 µm. Strain‐responsive properties were determined using oscillatory time sweeps that cyclically changed between low strain (1%) and high strain (250%) at 1 Hz. To assess the ink/support properties of PHMs for extrusion‐based printing applications, strain sweeps (0.01%–250%, 1 Hz when oscillatory) were conducted to identify yield strains (%) and viscosity as a function strain rate. Rheological measurements were conducted in triplicate. Shear recovery for each dilution was analyzed using a paired T‐test and dilutions compared against each other were assessed using a one‐way ANOVA coupled with a Tukey HSD post‐hoc test to determine statistical significance.

For characterization of PHM viscoelasticity, time sweeps were utilized to quantify storage and loss moduli (1%, 1 Hz. To assess the ability of PHMs to stress relax, a constant 15% strain was applied to the system and the resultant shear stress was recorded over time. Stress relaxation plots were smoothed using an exponential smoothing algorithm for clean presentation. For low and high crosslinkable scaffolds, the samples were irradiated for 2 min at 5 mW cm−2 before conducting the rest of the rheological measurements. Time sweeps for UV crosslinking of PHMs are shown in Figure S7. Viscoelasticity rheological assessments were conducted in duplicate, with representative data shown.

Extensional Rheological Characterization of PHMs

Extensibility of PHMs was determined via a modified filament stretching extensional rheology protocol [54] (FiSER, Anton Paar MCR 302 rheometer) using a 25 mm parallel plate geometry. Briefly, 500 µl of PHM scaffold was added to the rheometer stage and the gap height was lowered to 1 mm. A vertical strain rate of ε. = 1.2 s−1 was applied, and the normal force was tracked as the geometry height was increased. Filament failure was determined at the point where the material completely broke, and the height of failure was utilized to quantify the overall percentage stretch to failure relative to the original height. FiSER experiments were conducted in triplicate, and statistical significance was evaluated using a one‐way ANOVA coupled with a Tukey HSD post‐hoc test.

Extrusion Printing of PHMs

A FELIX BIOprinter was used for all controlled extrusion of PHMs, and PHM‐100 was utilized as the ink due to its robust mechanical properties compared to other dilutions. Inks were loaded into 1 mL syringes (BD) equipped with a 22 G needle via centrifugation at 200 RCF for 1 min to ensure all air bubbles were removed prior to printing. Printer workflows were written using G‐code commands that were actuated through the FELIX BIOprinter's software interface. Targeting filaments with 500 µm diameter, macroscale properties were determined by manipulation of the filament, and microscale properties were determined by printing PHM‐100 on glass coverslips. For printed filaments used in cell culture, the glass coverslips were first modified to present methacrylate groups based on a previously described protocol [74] to allow for covalent conjugation of the filament to the coverslip. G‐codes for printing of complex structures were generated by slicing software (Slic3r, slic3r.org). Extrusion of single filaments, vertically or between posts, used G‐code feed rates of 50 mm/min with extrusion values that yielded filaments with 500 µm diameters. This corresponded to 50 mm/min printhead translation speeds and volumetric extrusion of 0.2 µL per mm, or extrusion rates of 10 µL per min. For printing complex structures, a feed rate of 150 mm/min was used and extrusion values were set to rates of 2.4 µL per min to give filament diameters of 200 µm. Videos of extrusion printing are included in the supplementary data.

Cell Culture

Immortalized murine myoblasts (C2C12s, ATCC) were used for cell culture experiments (passages 6–7). Cells were cultured in standard growth media comprised of high glucose Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM), 10% v/v fetal bovine serum (Gibco), and 1% v/v antibiotic/antimycotic (Gibco). Media was changed every 2 days during expansion and experiments.

For experiments with cells seeded atop printed filaments, the crosslinked scaffolds were first sterilized via irradiation with germicidal light for 2 h. Scaffolds were then hydrated in complete growth media for 1 h prior to seeding cells at a density of 1 × 105 cells per scaffold. Cells were cultured for 2 d prior to fixing and staining for analysis. For experiments with cells seeded within fibers as a support, fiber solutions (10% v/v in PBS) were sterilized under germicidal light for 2 h, packed via centrifugation, and the PBS supernatant was exchanged with sterile C2C12 growth media for at least 2 h prior to packing for cell experiments. PHM‐100 was prepared as described above and C2C12s were gently mixed into PHMs at a density of 1 × 107 cells/ml and cultured in PDMS molds designed to contain cells prior to fixing and staining. Low density cell images (Figure 8) were cultured for 1 d prior to staining. Cell cultures were extended to 7 d, as indicated in figures, to study responses of RGD functionalization and interfiber crosslinking in 3D PHM materials.

Cell Staining

Prior to cell staining, C2C12s seeded on printed filaments were fixed in 10% v/v neutral buffered formalin for 15 min, permeabilized in 0.1% v/v Triton X‐100 for 10 min, then blocked with 3% w/v bovine serum albumin for 2 h at room temperature. Cells were incubated with AlexaFluor‐488 phalloidin for 1 h to visualize F‐actin (1:600, Invitrogen). Samples were washed thrice in PBS to remove unbound molecules.

A similar protocol was utilized for experiments with C2C12s in PHMs supports. Cells were fixed for 1 h, permeabilized for 1 h, and blocked for 2 h using the same solution concentrations prior to tagging F‐actin with AlexaFluor‐488 phalloidin (1:200) for 2 h. Nuclei were stained with DAPI in high cell‐density images. Again, samples were washed in PBS thrice to remove unbound fluorophore.
