Electrochemical Phase Engineering of γ′‑V2O5 Thin Films for Sodium-Ion Storage Electrodes
Authors: Zihan Sun, Nam Soo Kim, Rupesh Tiwari, David Zitoun, Gary W. Rubloff, Sang Bok Lee, Keith E. Gregorczyk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
V2O5 is a promising sodium-ion cathode material due to its high theoretical capacity (147 mAh/g) and working voltage (3.3 V vs Na/Na+). Among its various crystal phases, γ′-V2O5 has a large interlayer spacing, ensuring the reversible insertion–extraction of sodium ions. However, current synthesis methods for γ′-V2O5 require high temperatures (>600 °C) and toxic chemicals (NO2BF4), which make the preparation demanding. Herein, we put forward an electrochemical phase engineering method combining thermal annealing and electrochemistry to easily prepare thin-film γ′-V2O5. Electrochemical characterization shows near-ideal performance as a thin-film cathode material for sodium-ion batteries. It shows a measured initial capacity of 152 mAh/g, a high working voltage (3.3 V vs Na+/Na), and an exceptional Coulombic efficiency of 98%, significantly surpassing previously reported values (∼50% CE). Cyclic voltammogram and galvanostatic capacity curves confirm the sodium insertion–deinsertion, which remains stable at 2 C. The γ′-V2O5 thin film has electrochemical performance similar to γ′-V2O5 powder, indicating another workable morphology of γ′-V2O5 for sodium-ion batteries.

Introduction

Since the large-scale adoption of lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) in portable electronics and electric vehicles, the price and availability of lithium has fluctuated wildly. The abundance of sodium has therefore made sodium-ion batteries (SIBs) attractive as a potential complement or replacement for all LIB fields including microbatteries for small electronic devices. , Fundamentally, the physics and chemistry of LIBs are the same as SIBs, leading to many attempts at deriving SIB electrodes from LIB systems. , However, the design of electrode materials for SIBs with a high performance remains a challenge.

LiCoO2 and its cobalt-substituted derivatives (i.e., NMC, NCA, etc.) have been shown to be a mature cathode material in LIBs but show multiphase transformations in SIBs during the charge–discharge process, leading to poor cycle stability. , In addition, the extraction cost of cobalt, both financial and social, is another factor that cannot be ignored during commercialization.

The most successful SIB cathode to date has been Prussian blue (PB, Fe­[Fe­(CN)6]·nH2O) and its analogs (PBAs). These transition metal cyanides have crystal structures with vacancies in the framework that store and conduct sodium ions. , Multiple companies have commercialized versions of this SIB chemistry, demonstrating a working voltage of 1.56 V, an initial capacity of 67 mAh/g, and a cycle stability greater than 4000 cycles. Although SIBs based on PBAs have been commercialized, their low working voltage and small specific capacity limit their application space.

Beyond PBAs, vanadium oxides (i.e., VO2, V2O3, V2O5, V3O8, etc.) are considered to be next-generation cathode materials for SIBs because of their fast ion diffusion enabled by a layered crystal structure and excellent electrochemical properties resulting from diverse oxidation redox couples from V2+ to V5+. − Among vanadium oxides, V2O5 is of great interest since the large interlayer space allows intercalation and deintercalation of various cations including Li+, Na+, and Mg2+. − Additionally, V2O5 exhibits many stable crystal structures, differing in cation acceptability, cycle stability with the chosen cation, and ultimately the power and energy delivered. , Within the crystal system of V2O5, α-V2O5 is a popular LIB cathode due to the high theoretical capacity of 440 mAh/g when each unit formula contains three lithium ions (i.e., Li3V2O5) and displays a phase-transition process as a function of lithium-ion concentration (i.e., state of charge). −

Similar to the LCO case, it was expected that excellent performance of LIBs would translate into success in SIBs. However, the mechanism of sodium insertion into α-V2O5 was investigated and shown to be quite different from lithium insertion. The initial sodiation plateau showed a phase transformation at ∼1.8 V (vs Na/Na+) or ∼2.1 V (vs Li/Li+) during discharging, which is much lower than lithium insertion (3.2 V vs Li/Li+). Furthermore, charge–discharge cycling showed significant irreversibility, further confirming the difficulty of sodium insertion into α-V2O5. Though it was shown cycling could be improved by cycling between NaV2O5 and Na2V2O5 (0.8∼1.5 V vs Na/Na+), poor rate performance continues to limit application.

γ′-V2O5 also has an orthorhombic layered structure. Since γ′-V2O5 has a larger interlayered spacing than α-V2O5, sodium ions have shown relatively easy insertion into γ′-V2O5 to generate γ-NaV2O5, with the phase-transition plateau at 3.4 V (vs Na/Na+) or 3.6 V (vs Li/Li+). The sodium-insertion process from γ′-V2O5 to γ-NaV2O5 proceeds via a two-step process: (1) two phases, which include γ′-V2O5 and γ-Na0.7V2O5 (0 < x ≤ 0.7), coexist in the material; (2) only a single phase of γ-Na x V2O5 (0.7 < x ≤ 0.97) exists in the crystal. The cycling of charge–discharge shows a discharge specific capacity of 152 mAh/g, which is consistent with, though slightly higher than, the theoretical value (147 mAh/g), and this capacity is reversible for multiple cycles. −

Other than γ′-V2O5, β-V2O5 also has the ability to store sodium ions. The sodium insertion into β-V2O5 is divided into three steps: (1) a solid solution region in 3.1–3.2 V (vs Na/Na+) and β-Na x V2O5 (0.35 < x ≤ 0.45) region; (2) β-Na x V2O5 (0.65 < x ≤ 0.7) region; (3) β-Na x V2O5 (0.9 < x ≤ 1) region. Step (1) corresponds to a peak at 3.1 V (vs Na/Na+) in cyclic voltammograms, while the steps (2) and (3) correspond to peaks at 2.6 V (vs Na/Na+). Although γ′-V2O5 and β-V2O5 show excellent performance of sodium storage, the preparation methods are demanding.

The conventional preparation methods of γ′-V2O5 usually require high temperatures and toxic chemicals. For instance, the carbothermal reduction synthesis of γ-LiV2O5 requires a temperature of 600 °C and chemical oxidation with highly toxic NO2BF4. β-V2O5 needs not only a high temperature of 800 °C but also a high pressure of 4 GPa for preparation. Since the current preparation methods for γ′-V2O5 and β-V2O5 are demanding, developing a facile synthesis route to prepare a thin-film V2O5 material for sodium storage is needed.

Here, we present a method we refer to as electrochemical phase engineering to prepare γ′-V2O5 by combining thin-film material synthesis (i.e., atomic layer deposition, ALD), thermal annealing, and wet-chemical electrochemistry. Compared with the conventional synthesis methods discussed above, the method presented here can be carried out under mild conditions, at a low temperature (250 °C), without the use of highly toxic chemicals, and requires no external pressure. Additionally, we compare the electrochemical performances of γ′-V2O5 and α-V2O5 thin films as cathodes in SIBs. Our synthesized γ′-V2O5 thin film achieved the theoretical specific capacity (147 mAh/g) when discharged at 0.1 C rate without the need of any binders or conductive additives and has an exceptional Coulombic efficiency of 98%, significantly surpassing previously reported values (∼50% CE). The sodium insertion and deinsertion reactions in γ′-V2O5 were stable when the charge–discharge rate increased to 2 C, exhibiting a similar rate performance to γ′-V2O5 powder. Therefore, this work provides a facile and safe method to prepare γ′-V2O5 thin films, which is confirmed to be a promising cathode material for SIBs. Since the morphology of thin-film γ′-V2O5 is determined by the substrate, this method enables us to control its morphology by using different substrates, which makes it possible to establish various electrochemistry platforms based on different morphologies of γ′-V2O5 for future electrochemical research.

Experimental Methods

Atomic Layer Deposition

All samples were fabricated based on Si wafers as the substrate for the following preparation. Si wafers (thickness, 400 μm) were purchased from University Wafer, and each one was cut into square pieces (10 × 10 mm). Diced Si wafers were RCA-cleaned and subsequently thermally oxidized in a Tystar CVD system to form a 550 nm SiO2 layer. A 90 nm Ti layer and a 120 nm Pt layer were deposited sequentially via an Angstrom NexDep Ebeam evaporator as an adhesion layer and a current collector, respectively. V2O5 was deposited on the substrate by a Beneq TFS 500 ALD reactor. The precursor was vanadium triisopropoxide, which reacted with O3 at 170 °C using optimized conditions from previous research. After deposition of V2O5 by ALD, the diced wafers were put into the Thermolyne 79300 tube furnace to anneal at 350 °C for 12 h to get α-V2O5. Then, it was lithiated via galvanostatic electrochemical insertion in 1 M LiClO4/propylene carbonate (PC) with a Li metal counter electrode at 1/10 C rate, and the cutoff voltage is 2.6 V vs Li. The δ-LiV2O5 obtained from lithiation was annealed at 250 °C for 3 h under vacuum. Galvanostatic electrochemical deinsertion was employed to delithiate LiV2O5 to get γ′-V2O5.

Electrochemical Characterization

Electrochemical tests were performed at room temperature in home-built two-electrode galvanic cells, which have V2O5 working electrodes and lithium or sodium metal counter electrodes, as shown in Figure S4. The electrolyte is a solution of 1 M LiClO4 in PC or 1 M NaClO4 in PC/ethylene carbonate (EC) (1:1). All galvanic cells were assembled in a glovebox filled with argon, in which the oxygen and moisture levels were kept under 1 ppm. All electrochemical tests were finished under the argon atmosphere in the glovebox, and the measurements were controlled by a Biologic VSP potentiostat.

Metrology Techniques

Structural characterization was conducted to check the crystallinity of V2O5 for each step of the preparation method. Scanning electron microscopy was performed using a Hitachi SU70 SEM system. XRD measurements were done with a Rigaku grazing incidence X-ray diffractometer. Raman spectra were measured with an H-Y-J Raman microscope. Backscattering geometry was taken to measure the signal, and the excitation source was He/Ne laser (532 nm). A 100× objective was used to focus the laser on samples. Since γ′-V2O5 would anneal at high temperatures, the laser filter was set to D2, and the exposure time was 60 s. Three points of each sample were recorded to ensure reliability of the Raman spectra.

Results

Electrochemical Phase Engineering of γ′-V2O5

To improve the sodiation of vanadium-oxide-based electrode systems, a new synthesis route to an electrochemically phase-engineered γ′-V2O5 was developed. A summary of this method is shown in Figure . The method consists of a hybrid combination of electrochemical lithiation and thermal annealing to produce stable thin films of gamma-prime V2O5. First, thin films of α-V2O5 are produced via atomic layer deposition, as shown in step A. These films are then annealed in air at 400 °C for 3 h, ensuring fully crystalline and uniform α-V2O5 phase, and the characteristics of the films agree well with the standard literature practice. After the initial annealing step, the films are placed as working electrodes into a two-electrode beaker cell with 1 M LiClO4 in PC/EC as the electrolyte and lithium metal as the counter/reference electrode. These cells had an open-circuit voltage of 3.5 V and were then discharged to 2.6 V equivalent to one lithium per unit cell at 0.1 C. This phase is known as δ-LiV2O5. In this phase, the samples are annealed a second time at 250 °C for 3 h under vacuum at 3 mbar to form γ-LiV2O5. Finally, the films are returned to the electrochemical cell used above and the lithium is removed at 0.1 C, forming γ′-V2O5, an ideal structure for sodiation due to its wider crystal unit cell.

Synthesis route of γ′-V2O5. The method combines electrochemical lithiation and thermal annealing. Electrochemical lithiation and delithiation were done in a two-electrode beaker cell. The substrate remained stable during the synthesis. The V2O5 layer undergoes phase transitions from α-V2O5 to γ′-V2O5.

Since the synthesis route includes electrochemical lithiation and delithiation steps, the electrochemistry results used to understand the phase-transition process during the preparation of γ′-V2O5 are shown in Figure . The lithiation curve shows plateaus at 3.4 and 3.2 V, which correspond to phase transition from α-V2O5 to ε-Li0.5V2O5 and ε-Li0.5V2O5 to δ-LiV2O5, respectively (Figure , black curve). As witnessed in Figure , the electrodes were held at 2.6 V vs Li/Li+ and 3.8 V vs Li/Li+ to ensure full lithiation and delithiation of the electrodes, respectively. The currents were monitored, see Supporting Information, Figure S1, and held to allow the current decay to a minimum. The additional 10% capacity witnessed is assumed to be combination of the additional surface area provided by the thin-film nature of the material, electrochemical polarization of the electrode, and potential side reactions due to the carbonate electrolyte. These results are consistent with the literature and confirm the crystallinity of α-V2O5 and one-lithium insertion reaction to produce δ-LiV2O5. The delithiation curve after the second annealing (step D, Figure ) displays plateaus at 3.6 and 3.5 V, as shown in Figure (blue line), which represent one-lithium deinsertion from γ-LiV2O5. Therefore, γ-LiV2O5 was obtained after second thermal annealing.

Lithiation (black line) and delithiation (blue line) curves of the synthesis route. Curve a corresponds to the discharging step at 1/10 C done to produce δ-LiV2O5 by one-lithium insertion reaction. In curve b, the two-electrode beaker cell charged at 1/10 C to delithiate γ-LiV2O5 to get γ′-V2O5.

Metrology of Thin-Film γ′-V2O5

To confirm the crystalline structure throughout the electrochemical phase engineering process outlined in Figure , multimodal spectroscopic analysis was conducted through Raman spectroscopy and X-ray diffraction (XRD). Additional physical inspection was conducted through cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

To determine the local bonding environment of the vanadium and oxygen atoms in the thin-film V2O5, we used Raman spectroscopy from 50 cm–1 to 1200 cm–1, with the results shown in Figure a. Raman spectra of the material at each step during the synthesis were collected to explore the change in the bonding environment and compare to known crystal structures. The vanadium–oxygen bond distance varies with phase; therefore, tracking the evolution of characteristic peaks throughout our synthesis method would be a clear indication of variation in crystal structures.

(a) Raman spectra of V2O5 materials at each step of the synthesis route. The curves include α-V2O5, δ-LiV2O5, γ-LiV2O5, and γ-V2O5. (b) XRD spectra of V2O5 materials at each step of the synthesis route. The curves include α-V2O5, δ-LiV2O5, γ-LiV2O5, and γ-V2O5.

The as-deposited V2O5 is amorphous and annealed at 400 °C for 3 h to produce α-V2O5. The spectrum (Figure a, black line) has characteristic peaks at 995 cm–1, 700 cm–1, 525 cm–1, 480 cm–1, 405 cm–1, 305 cm–1, 285 cm–1, 195 cm–1, and 145 cm–1. The peaks above 480 cm–1 are indicative of bond-stretching vibrations, while the peaks below 480 cm–1 correspond to bond-bending vibrations. The peak pattern is consistent with the theoretical Raman-active modes of α-V2O5, demonstrating the α-phase of V2O5 after annealing.

After one lithium insertion, δ-LiV2O5 is expected (Figure a, red line). The spectrum shows an obvious peak evolution compared with that of α-V2O5. The intense peak at 143 cm–1 disappears, and a shift from 995 cm–1 to 965 cm–1 is seen. A shoulder at 982 cm–1 appears on the right of 965 cm–1, suggesting the multiple V–O stretching modes caused by lithium insertion. Hence, δ-LiV2O5 is obtained after lithiation. The second annealing after lithiation enables a phase transition from δ-LiV2O5 to γ-LiV2O5. When it comes to γ-LiV2O5 (Figure a, blue line), the spectrum shows the continuous evolution of crystalline structure. The characteristic peak at 971 cm–1 and a shoulder at 990 cm–1 are observed in the V–O stretching mode region. Other peaks at 149, 173, 222, 297, and 329 cm–1 appear similar to γ-LiV2O5 previously reported in the literature.

Finally, γ-LiV2O5 is delithiated to produce γ′-V2O5 (Figure a, green line) as the desired phase. The highest-frequency peak at 1002 cm–1 is caused by asymmetric stretching modes of VO bonds. The peaks at 723 and 601 cm–1 correspond to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching modes of V–O–V bridges. The peak at 526 cm–1 is related to the asymmetric stretching modes of V–O bonds. More peaks at 139, 168, 282, and 359 cm–1 are assigned to the complicated distortion of the layered structure of γ′-V2O5. The peak at 792 cm–1, which does not match any characteristic peak of γ-LiV2O5, may be from Li3VO4 generated during thermal annealing.

The peak patterns in Figure a confirm the phase transition from α-V2O5 to δ-LiV2O5. As mentioned, the Raman peak pattern is strongly affected by lithium insertion into the V2O5 host lattice. The introduction of Li–O bonds increases the degree of disorder and puckering of the crystal structure, which results in decreasing peak intensity for in-phase rotation of the V–O framework and out-of-plane VO symmetric bond stretching. The phase transition from δ-LiV2O5 to γ-LiV2O5 is finished after the second annealing step. More characteristic peaks emerge in the spectra, and some previous peaks shift in contrast to δ-LiV2O5. In the last step of the synthesis route, γ′-V2O5 is produced through delithiation of γ-LiV2O5. The elimination of lithium ions in V2O5 removes the Li–O bonds and thus decreases the extent of disorder and puckering of the crystal structure. This is observed through the sharpening of the Raman peaks (i.e., smaller FWHM). It can be observed that V2O5 does not go back to the original α-phase after the delithiation step, and the generated γ′-V2O5 shows a quite different Raman spectrum, which means new V–O bonding environments were formed.

Complementary confirmation of material phase and crystal structure was acquired through XRD. Since the samples in this work are thin films rather than bulk powders, grazing incidence XRD (0.5°) was used to increase signal intensity and avoid substrate signal. The XRD spectra of α-V2O5, δ-LiV2O5, γ-LiV2O5, and γ′-V2O5 are displayed in Figure b. The spectrum of α-V2O5 exhibits the characteristics of hkl reflections matching an orthorhombic crystal structure (Pmmn space group, ICSD PDF calc. 01-090-8354), which has unit cell parameters a = 11.52 Å, b = 3.56 Å, and c = 4.32 Å. Lithium insertion distorts the crystal structure of α-V2O5, leading to the evolution of peak patterns from α-V2O5 to δ-LiV2O5 (Pmn21 space group, ICSD PDF calc. 01-089-8319). The second thermal annealing at 250 °C transforms δ-LiV2O5 into γ-LiV2O5, which has a similar peak pattern but with a new hkl line (020) at 47.8° (Pnma space group, ICSD PDF calc. 01–070–2476). The as-synthesized γ′-V2O5 after delithiation of γ-LiV2O5 shows an orthorhombic crystal structure (Pnma space group, ICSD PDF calc. 01-090-7484), which has unit cell parameters a = 9.94 Å, b = 3.58 Å, and c = 10.04 Å, indicating a larger space than α-V2O5 to contain sodium ions. In combination with Raman spectroscopy, XRD spectroscopy shows the phase transformation from α-V2O5 to γ′-V2O5, which confirms the effectiveness of the synthesis route. Slight variations in peak position and intensity ratios are most likely due to the thin-film nature of the material, limiting X-ray cross-section, and available reflections. We also note that the limited incidence angle used for grazing incidence leads to lower intensities, changes in peak ratios, and some absent or shifted indices/reflections in comparison with the standard powder pattern from the ICSD database. Overall, the combination of Raman and XRD provides convincing evidence of the claimed phase evolution.

To complete the analysis of our synthesis method and to determine more exact thickness measurements, which will be important to the electrochemical methods discussed below, cross-sectional SEM images were taken. Images of the cross-section at each stage of our proposed synthesis method, i.e., α-V2O5, δ-LiV2O5, γ-LiV2O5, and γ′-V2O5, are shown in Figure a–d. The thin-film structure of the samples is clearly witnessed, and the thickness of all layers was as expected. Figure a shows the cross-section of α-V2O5, which measures a thickness of ∼205 nm, as expected. After the film is lithiated from α-V2O5 to δ-LiV2O5, the film thickness increases to ∼250 nm (Figure b). After annealing, the γ-LiV2O5 layer (Figure c) still has a thickness of ∼250 nm and the morphology remains uniform. The desired material phase γ′-V2O5 is obtained by delithiation of the γ-LiV2O5 phase and shows a reduced thickness of ∼210 nm (Figure d). In general, the evolution of the material phase and thin-film structure coincides well with the proposed phase changes and resulting crystal structures during synthesis. The overall volume expansion from α-V2O5 to γ′-V2O5 is only ∼5%.

SEM images of V2O5 at each step of the synthesis route which includes (a) α-V2O5, (b) δ-LiV2O5, (c) γ-LiV2O5, and (d) γ-V2O5.

The combination of these three metrology techniques confirms the synthesis process and intermediate material phases. Raman spectroscopy and XRD provide complementary information about the evolution of the local bonding and crystal structure. SEM images measured the volume evolution of the thin-film V2O5 material and showed evidence of lithiation and delithiation occurring during synthesis through changes in film thickness and therefore electrode volume. All of these data are consistent with γ′-V2O5 and general V2O5 lithiation/delithiation reported in the literature.

Electrochemical Characterization

Sodiation of Thin Film γ′-V2O5

The sodiation of γ′-V2O5 was measured in beaker cells as shown in Figure S4 (V2O5 vs Na) using a solution of 1 M NaClO4 in PC/EC (1:1) as the electrolyte, with the results shown in Figure a–d. Figure a shows one-sodium insertion between 3.4 and 1.7 V vs Na/Na+ at 0.1 C and measures a specific capacity of 152 mAh/g, which is ∼3% higher than the theoretical capacity (147 mAh/g). The additional capacity witnessed in the first cycle is attributed to the thin-film nature of the material, pseudocapacitance, and potential side reactions. The discharge curve has phase-transition plateaus at 3.3 and 2.5 V (vs Na/Na+), comparable to values found in the literature. CVs of γ′-V2O5 were conducted as a function of scan rate (0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, and 0.6 mV/s), with the results shown in Figure b. The distribution of redox peaks is symmetric and demonstrates the good reversibility of sodium insertion/deinsertion in γ′-V2O5. These curves show sharp desodiation peaks at 3.4–3.5 V and sodiation at 3.2 V and additionally show minimal shift as a function of rate, for example, the reduction peak shifts from 3.29 to 3.24 V when the scan rate increases from 0.1 to 0.6 mV/s, further establishing the stability of this material during sodiation. The rate dependence of the capacity is shown in Figure c. The specific capacity of γ′-V2O5 decreases with the increasing C rate from 140 mAh/g at 0.2 C to 98 mAh/g at 0.5 C, 81 mAh/g at 1 C, and 70 mAh/g at 2 C. Capacity retention was measured at 1 C, with the results shown in Figure d. These results show that γ′-V2O5 retains 80% of its initial capacity after 80 cycles. Therefore, γ′-V2O5 has a much better ability for sodium storage in comparison with α-V2O5, as shown in Figure S3a–d. Additional measurements on the lithiation of γ′-V2O5 are shown in Figure S2.

Electrochemical charge–discharge of the γ′-V2O5/Na half-cell device. (a) Galvanostatic Na-ion insertion at C/10 rate with one Na-ion insertion. (b) CV of the γ′-V2O5 cathode at different scan rates (done). (c) Rate performance of γ′-V2O5 at different C-rates. (d) Capacity retention of γ′-V2O5.

In comparison with the poor sodiation of α-V2O5 (Figure S3a–d), γ′-V2O5 has a much better ability for sodium storage. The result of one-sodium insertion reaction in a two-electrode beaker cell shows an ideal specific capacity (152 mAh/g) when discharging at 0.1 C rate. The γ′-V2O5 thin film reported here has a high initial Coulomb efficiency of 98% (Figure d), which shows a higher performance than that reported in the literature to date. CVs show a small peak shift of 40 mV when the scan rate increases from 0.1 to 0.6 mV/s. Charge–discharge curves at C rates varying from 0.2 to 2 C display a decreasing specific capacity from 140 to 68 mAh/g, which is similar to previously reported γ′-V2O5 powder. ,, To summarize, γ′-V2O5 has an ideal performance for sodiation.

Discussion

Electrochemical methods offer straightforward and precise control over lithiation depth, enabling tuning of the crystal structure, electrical conductivity, and ionic conductivity in various material systems by adjusting the concentration of the inserted ions. In this study, we leveraged this precise control to structurally modify V2O5 films, successfully synthesizing advanced γ′-V2O5 electrodes specifically for SIB applications. Our electrochemical phase engineering approach capitalizes on the well-documented lithiation kinetics of the V2O5 system, eliminating the need for hazardous reagents such as NO2BF4 typically required to synthesize γ′-V2O5. ,,, The synthesized electrodes exhibited a theoretical capacity of 147 mAh/g, coupled with excellent Coulombic efficiency (98%), demonstrating the practical efficacy of our approach.

While this investigation provides a specific instance of Li-ion-induced crystal structure engineering in V2O5, it also underscores a potentially versatile and broadly applicable methodology. This approach may not be inherently limited to lithium as the modifying ion, V2O5 as the host material, or energy storage as the application space. Alternative ions such as Na+, K+, Mg2+, etc. could similarly induce phase modification, broadening the scope of applications beyond lithium-based systems. Furthermore, the technique could be applicable across diverse material morphologies and fabrication methods, including sputtering, evaporation, chemical vapor deposition (CVD), electrochemical deposition (ECD), as well as bulk powders and various nanostructured materials. Currently, we are expanding and exploring this ion-induced crystal structure engineering method to various materials systems.

Active phase manipulation via electrochemical ion insertion has recently emerged as a powerful approach in solid-state iontronic devices. − For instance, controlled insertion of Li ions has been demonstrated to dynamically modulate the electrical conductivity of transistor channels, illustrating that ion-driven structural and electronic transformations can directly influence device functionality. Our findings reinforce this perspective, suggesting broad applicability for ion-induced phase engineering. Specifically, this method may enable new classes of tunable materials for applications such as memristors, neuromorphic computing, sensors, and even photovoltaic technologies such as perovskite solar cells, where precise control of the crystal structure and electronic properties is critical. More broadly, we believe, this work highlights an emerging paradigm shift in materials engineeringmoving beyond traditional chemical and physical synthesis methods toward deliberate, electrochemically driven ion manipulation as a versatile tool for advanced material design and synthesis.

Conclusions

In this work, we have demonstrated a novel synthesis route to produce high-quality γ′-V2O5 thin films, specifically tailored for SIB applications. Our synthesis method eliminates the need for hazardous reagents, such as NO2BF4, typically required to obtain the γ′-phase. The synthesized γ′-V2O5 exhibits superior electrochemical performance, delivering a near-theoretical specific capacity of 147 mAh/g with an exceptional Coulombic efficiency of 98%, significantly surpassing previously reported values (∼50% CE). The material maintains robust performance even at higher charge–discharge rates (70 mAh/g at 2 C), demonstrating suitability for practical SIB electrode applications and competitive performance compared to powder-based γ′-V2O5 synthesized with traditional methods.

More broadly, this electrochemical phase engineering technique represents a significant advancement in materials synthesis, particularly relevant in the emerging field of iontronics, where ions rather than electrons serve as primary signal carriers. Our integrated approachcombining atomic layer deposition (ALD), electrochemical lithiation-driven phase modification, and controlled thermal annealingpresents a versatile platform to precisely manipulate and preserve desired material phases as indicated by both the superior sodiation (Figure a–d) and lithiation (Figure S2a–d) of γ′-V2O5. Comprehensive multimodal characterization using Raman spectroscopy, XRD, SEM, and electrochemical analyses confirmed both the structural integrity and electrochemical performance of the γ′-V2O5 films. Furthermore, the adaptability of this method to diverse material morphologies and substrates paves the way for novel device architectures and broader applications beyond energy storage, including tunable iontronic devices, sensors, and advanced electronic systems.
