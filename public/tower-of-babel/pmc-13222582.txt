Pregnancy Outcomes in Women with Type 1 Diabetes by Socioeconomic and Mental Health Disadvantages
Authors: Tina Wullum Gundersen, Tine Dalsgaard Clausen, Lene Ringholm, Dorte Møller Jensen, Peter Damm, Karoline Kragelund Nielsen, Helena E Backman, Jeannet Lauenborg, Elisabeth Reinhardt Mathiesen, Niels Skipper, Allan Kofoed-Enevoldsen, Sine Knorr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo examine whether individual-level measures of socioeconomic or mental health disadvantages are associated with preterm delivery and extreme large for gestational age (eLGA) among women with type 1 diabetes.MethodsNationwide register-based study of singleton pregnancies among women with type 1 diabetes, 1997–2021. Disadvantages included family poverty (disposable income <50% of national median) and, for either parent, low educational level (≤primary school), unemployment (cash/disability benefits), or poor mental health (≥1 prepregnancy psychiatric diagnosis). Outcomes were preterm delivery (<37 weeks) and eLGA (birthweight ≥97.5th percentile). We estimated absolute risk (AR) and crude and adjusted relative risks (aRR), adjusting for parental immigration status and maternal birth year. Sensitivity analyses were stratified by prepregnancy HbA1c (<53/≥53 mmol/mol).ResultsAmong 5478 pregnancies, AR of preterm delivery and eLGA were 32.5% and 36.0%, respectively, and 40.1% experienced ≥1 disadvantage, ranging from 5.6% having family poverty to 18.9% having low paternal educational level. Higher aRRs of preterm delivery were found across all measures of disadvantage, ranging from 1.23 (1.00;1.45) to 1.48 (1.28;1.68). We observed a non-significant trend toward lower risk of eLGA with all disadvantages (aRRs ranging from 0.82 (0.64;1.00) to 1.01 (0.84;1.18)). Stratification by prepregnancy HbA1c showed basically unchanged aRRs but widened confidence intervals.ConclusionAmong women with type 1 diabetes, socioeconomic or mental health disadvantages were associated with higher risk of preterm delivery, but not eLGA, regardless of prepregnancy HbA1c level. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions to address social and mental health disparities in order to reduce preterm birth risk among women with type 1 diabetes.Keywords: diabetes, pregnancy, epidemiology, socioeconomic position, mental health, disadvantage

Introduction

Preterm delivery is the leading cause of mortality in children under the age of five and carries serious long-term consequences for both cognitive and physical health.1,2 Pregnancies in women with socioeconomic or mental health disadvantages are at increased risk of preterm delivery.3–5 Further, parental low educational level, has been associated with an increased risk of preterm delivery and low birth weight.6,7

Among pregnant women, having type 1 diabetes is associated with a 3–12 times higher risk of adverse outcomes, such as preterm delivery or fetal overgrowth.8–12 Fetal overgrowth is associated with complications during labor, including perinatal death, as well as long-term complications, such as overweight, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease.13–17 The risk of these complications increases with the severity of fetal overgrowth, making it relevant to study extreme large for gestational age (eLGA).

Reducing the risk of pregnancy complications among women with type 1 diabetes depends largely on glycemic control and pre- and perinatal care, which necessitates a high level of treatment compliance.8–10 Previous studies among women with type 1 diabetes found that those who delivered preterm more often had depressive symptoms during pregnancy and a lower educational level, and that socioeconomically disadvantaged women had poorer glycemic control in the first trimester.11,12 However, no studies have evaluated the extent to which individual-level measures of socioeconomic or mental health disadvantages in the mother or father are related to adverse pregnancy outcomes among women with type 1 diabetes.

We aimed to examine whether socioeconomic or mental health disadvantages as family poverty, as well as maternal or paternal low educational level, unemployment, and poor mental health, were associated with preterm delivery and eLGA among women with type 1 diabetes.

Materials and Methods

This nationwide, population-based, cross-sectional register study in a universal health care setting included all pregnancies resulting in a singleton live- or stillbirth among women with type 1 diabetes in Denmark, from January 1st, 1997, to December 31st, 2021.

From the Danish Medical Birth Register, we identified eligible pregnancies, obstetric information, and linkage of mother, father, and offspring. All Danish citizens are given a unique identification number upon birth or immigration. Using this, we linked information on diagnosis codes (the National Patient Register, 1977–2021), redeemed prescription medicine (National Prescription Register, 1995–2021), laboratory results from blood-tests (Clinical Laboratory Information Register, 2016–2021), income (Danish Register of Personal Income and Transfer Payments, 1996–2021), education (Danish Education Register, 1997–2021), and employment status (The Employment Classification Module, 1997–2021) for both mothers and fathers. Reporting to the Danish registers is mandatory, and data are automatically collected. After linkage, data were de-identified and processed through Statistics Denmark. The Danish legal definition of stillbirth changed from ≥28 gestational weeks to ≥22 gestational weeks in April 2004. This change was uniformly implemented nationwide and reflected in the Danish registers. We allowed for inclusion of multiple pregnancies from the same woman and excluded pregnancies with missing information on the offspring’s gestational age, or the maternal or paternal identification number, birth year, or immigration status.

Maternal type 1 diabetes was defined as having a registered prepregnancy type 1 diabetes diagnosis code and redeeming at least one prescription of insulin before pregnancy (Table S1). This classification was based on a previous study validating type 1 diabetes in pregnancy using the Danish registers.13

The study is reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines (S1 Checklist). The Central Denmark Region approved the study (journal number: 1-16-02-171-23). Following Denmark’s legal requirements for register-based studies, informed consent and ethical approval were not required.

Variable Definitions

As exposures, we evaluated seven measures of socioeconomic or mental health disadvantage: family poverty (yearly disposable income, adjusted for the number of family members, was <50% of the median disposable income nationally, measured the year before delivery); low educational level for the mother or father (highest completed education being primary school or lower at the year of delivery); unemployment for mother or father (receiving cash or retirement benefits, measured the year of delivery); and maternal or paternal poor mental health (having ≥1 registered prepregnancy ICD10 psychiatric diagnoses). For each exposure, pregnancies were classified as exposed or unexposed.

We considered two pregnancy outcomes: preterm delivery (<37 gestational weeks) and, as the marker of severe fetal overgrowth, eLGA (birth weight ≥97.5th percentile, adjusted for sex and gestational age).14

Demographic baseline covariates from Statistics Denmark were collected for the year of delivery. The following information was collected for parents: maternal and paternal age (years, numeric), maternal prepregnancy body mass index (BMI) ≥30 kg/m2 (yes/no) (available from 2004 onward), maternal smoking during pregnancy (yes/no), maternal cohabitation status (living alone vs with a non-related male adult as defined by Statistics Denmark) and maternal and paternal immigration status (immigrant/descendant vs. Danish origin). Due to data collection in the Danish Registers, prepregnancy Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) was only available in a limited period between 2016 and 2021 and could therefore only be included in sensitivity analyses. HbA1c levels were dichotomized as low (<53 mmol/mol) or high (≥53 mmol/mol). This cut-off was the recommended prepregnancy HbA1c target in women with type 1 diabetes nationally and internationally during the study period.15,16 All relevant variable definitions are specified in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical Methods

Baseline characteristics were reported as numbers and percentages (categorical variables) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) (numeric variables).

We fitted generalized linear models to evaluate risks of preterm delivery and eLGA, comparing each exposure to its own absence as reference, eg, family poverty compared to no family poverty and low maternal educational level compared to pregnancies without low maternal educational level. For each outcome and exposure, we fitted crude models as well as models adjusted for maternal and paternal immigration status and maternal birth year, as these were considered potential confounders. We calculated relative risks (RR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) using regression standardization, clustering by maternal identification number to account for mothers with multiple deliveries during the study period. Missing data were handled as complete-case analyses. For all outcomes and exposures, we reported the number of cases, absolute risk, crude RR, adjusted RR (aRR), and 95% CIs, visualized in a forest plot.

In sensitivity analyses, we further adjusted the main analyses for maternal smoking and BMI≥30 kg/m2.

To examine whether glycemic control modified the potential association between disadvantage and pregnancy outcomes, we performed sensitivity analyses on data from 2016–2021. First, we checked for interaction by including an interaction term between prepregnancy HbA1c level and each measure of socioeconomic or mental health disadvantage, to assess whether prepregnancy HbA1c was an effect modifier. Then, pregnancies were stratified according to prepregnancy HbA1c <53 or ≥53 mmol/mol to evaluate the risk of pregnancy outcomes among women with well-regulated and dysregulated diabetes prior to pregnancy by each measure of socioeconomic or mental health disadvantage.

For all analyses, a p-value <0.05 was considered significant.

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.1, using the packages “gtsummary” version 1.7.2, “StdReg” version 3.4.1, and “forestplot” version 3.1.3.

Results

We included 5478 pregnancies with maternal type 1 diabetes, among 3389 individual mothers and 3560 individual fathers. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics for all pregnancies among women with type 1 diabetes. There were 2195 (40.1%) pregnancies with any socioeconomic and mental health disadvantage, 2705 (49.4%) pregnancies with no disadvantage, and 578 (10.5%) pregnancies with missing information on one or more measures of disadvantage. The prevalence of socioeconomic and mental health disadvantage ranged from 5.6% having family poverty to 18.9% with paternal low educational level. Fathers’ unemployment had the highest proportion of missing information (13.5%) and maternal educational level the lowest (0.4%), aside from parental poor mental health that by definition could not have missing values as it relied on recorded diagnoses (Table 1). When comparing pregnancies exposed to any measure of disadvantage to pregnancies with no disadvantage, preterm delivery occurred in 38.5% (n=845) vs 28.2% (n=763), and eLGA in 35.1% (n=762) vs 36.8% (n=990), respectively.

Risk of Preterm Delivery

The absolute risk of preterm delivery was 32.5% in the total population (Table 1). Among women with a measure of socioeconomic and mental health disadvantage, the prevalence of preterm delivery ranged from 30.8% to 43.1%, while 28.2% delivered preterm among pregnancies with no disadvantage (Table 1 and Figure 1). Regarding socioeconomic disadvantage, the highest risk of preterm delivery was observed in the 5.6% of pregnancies with family poverty (aRR 1.48, 95% CI 1.28; 1.68) compared to no poverty and in the 17.8% of pregnancies with maternal low educational level (aRR 1.42, 95% CI 1.29; 1.56) compared to pregnancies without low maternal educational level (Table 1 and Figure 1). When evaluating pregnancies with poor mental health disadvantage, both pregnancies with maternal and paternal poor mental health had an aRR of 1.23 of delivering preterm (95% CI 1.00; 1.45 for both).

Risk of Extreme LGA

The absolute risk of eLGA in the cohort was 36.0%. Among women with any measure of socioeconomic and mental health disadvantage, the prevalence of eLGA ranged from 27.5% to 34.9%, while 36.8% of pregnancies with no disadvantage delivered eLGA infants (Table 1 and Figure 2). Comparing socioeconomic disadvantage to its own respective reference group, we found a trend toward a lower risk of eLGA across all socioeconomic disadvantages, but this was statistically significant only in the group where the mother (aRR 0.88, 95% CI 0.76; 0.99) or father (aRR 0.82, 95% CI 0.65; 0.98) was unemployed (Figure 2) compared to no maternal or paternal unemployment, respectively. Concerning mental health disadvantage, we found no association to risk of eLGA when the mother had poor mental health (aRR 0.98, 95% CI 0.83; 1.12) but a borderline significant association for the father having poor mental health (aRR 0.82, 95% CI 0.64; 1.00).

Sensitivity Analyses Adjusting for Maternal Smoking and BMI

When repeating the analyses in a separate model adjusted for maternal smoking and BMI≥30 kg/m2, the estimates for preterm delivery or eLGA remained largely unchanged (data not shown).

Sensitivity Analyses Stratifying for Levels of Prepregnancy HbA1c

Of the 1643 pregnancies included in the sensitivity analysis from 2016–2021, 93.7% (1540) had their prepregnancy HbA1c measured within 1 year prior to pregnancy onset. Hereof, 42.4% (653) had a prepregnancy HbA1c <53 mmol/mol. There was no significant interaction between prepregnancy HbA1c and any measure of parental disparity when evaluating preterm delivery and eLGA with all p-values being >0.50 (data not shown). When stratifying by the prepregnancy treatment goal of prepregnancy HbA1c <53 or ≥53 mmol/mol, the risk of preterm delivery remained increased across all measures of disadvantage, with estimates similar to the main results (Supplementary Figure S1). However, the confidence intervals were wider than in the main analysis, making most of the associations statistically non-significant (aRRs ranging from 0.90, 95% CI 0.25; 1.55 to 1.37, 95% CI 0.65; 1.64). We found that a prepregnancy HbA1c ≥53 mmol/mol was associated with lower estimates of eLGA among pregnancies with any disadvantage, while pregnancies with a prepregnancy HbA1c <53 mmol/mol had risks more like those of women without disadvantage (Supplementary Figure S2).

Discussion

In this population-based study of all pregnancies among women with type 1 diabetes in a universal health care setting from 1997 to 2021, family poverty, low educational level, unemployment, and poor mental health of either the mother or the father were all associated with a higher risk of preterm delivery, but not with eLGA.

In pregnancies without diabetes, previous studies have shown that both maternal unemployment and both maternal and paternal low educational levels have increased odds of preterm delivery (OR range 1.36–1.94), while the correlation with income varies across countries and health care systems.4,6,7,17–22 Few studies have evaluated how maternal socioeconomic or mental health disadvantages could relate to pregnancy outcomes among women with type 1 diabetes and no previous studies have evaluated the paternal role. One study reported that maternal socioeconomic disadvantage was associated with higher first-trimester HbA1c, and another found that elevated HbA1c levels and hyperglycemic spikes were associated with preterm delivery.12,23 A study of 148 women with preexisting diabetes also identified a correlation between prepregnancy depression scores and preterm delivery, independent of early pregnancy HbA1c.11 Our study is expanding on these findings, where the estimated risk of preterm delivery was associated with all individual-level measures of socioeconomic or mental health disadvantages and remained with similar risk estimates across the two prepregnancy HbA1c groups. However, the confidence intervals widened, probably due to smaller sample sizes in the sensitivity analyses. We cannot rule out that the association between socioeconomic or mental health disadvantages and preterm delivery could be due to glycemic control not captured by prepregnancy HbA1c. However, the consistent findings in this study, as well as the above-mentioned studies, suggest that the association is most likely multifactorial, involving both socioeconomic and mental health disadvantages, as well as glycemic control.

Both high maternal age and non-Danish ethnicity are associated with an increased risk of preterm delivery, while Danish ethnicity and high maternal age are associated with fetal overgrowth.24–26 Further, advanced maternal age has been shown to increase the inequality in preterm birth.5 In this study, we adjusted for maternal birth year, as a proxy for maternal age, and for parental immigration status. All estimates increased slightly after adjustment; however, it did not change the significance of the findings. Our findings could therefore not be explained by maternal age or parental immigration status.

Other established risk factors for preterm delivery among women with type 1 diabetes include maternal smoking and high BMI, which are more common among individuals with socioeconomic and mental health disadvantage.9,27–31 Sensitivity analyses adjusting for maternal smoking and BMI did not change the results. Hypertensive disorders or preeclampsia were not adjusted for in this study, as we considered them a mediator for both preterm delivery and low birth weight.

Denmark’s universal, tax-funded health care system provides cost-free access to all medical services for all residents. Despite this, previous studies of both non-pregnant individuals with diabetes and pregnant women without diabetes have shown that low educational level and psychiatric diagnoses are associated with reduced health care utilization as well as increased morbidity and mortality.21,22,32–35 Our findings highlight health disadvantages that extend beyond financial barriers to care, potentially reflecting factors such as distance to the health care providers, language or cultural barriers, maternal stress levels, or differences in how individuals engage and navigate the health care system. Further research is needed to determine if targeted interventions for women with type 1 diabetes experiencing socioeconomic disadvantage can help reduce rates of preterm delivery.

We observed a small trend toward lower risks of eLGA across all measures of socioeconomic or mental health disadvantage, although most were statistically insignificant. This is in line with previous studies in pregnancies without diabetes, which have reported associations between low maternal or paternal educational level or maternal poor mental health and low birth weight in the offspring, whereas the association with maternal income has varied by context.4,6,18,22,36 These studies suggest maternal smoking, alcohol consumption, and stress, as well as reduced health care utilization and maternal compliance, as potential causes. In this study, the observed small trend toward a lower risk of eLGA among pregnancies with socioeconomic or mental health disadvantages among women with type 1 diabetes did not change after adjustment for maternal smoking and BMI. Among women with diabetes, elevated HbA1c levels in early and late pregnancy are risk factors for LGA, and previous studies have described elevated first-trimester HbA1c levels among pregnant women with socioeconomic disadvantage and type 1 diabetes.12,37 Further, pregnant women with type 2 diabetes and socioeconomic disadvantage have been found to have an increased risk of LGA.38 However, poor glycemic control can also lead to placental dysfunction and hypertensive disorders, both of which may restrict fetal growth and the length of the pregnancy.39 We found that prepregnancy HbA1c ≥53 mmol/mol was associated with lower estimates of eLGA among women with any disadvantage, while women with prepregnancy HbA1c within target had a risk profile more similar to that of women without disadvantage, supporting the hypothesis that poor glycemic control and impaired placental function may contribute to a lower prevalence of eLGA.40

The influences of paternal factors on pregnancy outcomes have not previously been investigated among women with diabetes. Studies from Denmark and Canada among the general population found that low paternal educational level significantly increased the risk of preterm delivery, independent of maternal factors while other studies indicate that involved and supportive partners can improve pregnancy outcomes.6,7,41,42 One could speculate that the potential influence of paternal socioeconomic or mental health status could differ from the maternal influence, maybe especially when one parent has a chronic illness such as type 1 diabetes. Further, a previous study found that type 1 diabetes in either parent is associated with poorer offspring educational outcomes, supporting the notion that disparities in either parent may influence child outcomes. However, the increased risk of preterm delivery associated to paternal exposure in our study of women with type 1 diabetes could also reflect correlated maternal exposure and calls for further studies within this field.

A major strength of this study is the use of large, nationwide population-based registers of high quality. Reporting to the Danish registers is mandatory and automated, and the data are generally highly valid with minimal missingness.43,44 The definition of type 1 diabetes in pregnancy has previously been validated with high specificity and completeness.13 We examined both maternal and paternal individual-level risk factors, thereby increasing the generalizability of our findings.

A limitation of this study is that, although reporting to the registers is mandatory and automated, we had missing information in some exposures. This was most prevalent for fathers’ unemployment and family poverty with 13.5 and 12.8% missing. Contrary to this, mothers’ educational level had 0.4% missing, and this measure appears to be the most valid and robust measure of disparity for evaluating women with type 1 diabetes in pregnancy using the Danish registers, in accordance with other Danish register-based studies on the general population.7 When evaluating psychiatric diagnoses, a limitation of our dataset is that it likely captures predominantly severe cases, as milder conditions are often managed in primary care and thus not recorded in the Danish registers. This may lead to an underestimation of both the prevalence and impact of psychiatric diagnoses in our results.

Another limitation is that HbA1c data were restricted to prepregnancy measurements and were available only for the period 2017–2021, resulting in a reduced sample size and wider confidence intervals. Although HbA1c values during pregnancy would have been optimal, poor prepregnancy HbA1c has been shown to correlate with higher values later in pregnancy, making it a valuable proxy for glycemic control in this cohort.45 In the sensitivity analyses, we found no interaction between prepregnancy HbA1c level and socioeconomic position or mental health disparity. However, few cases were available for these analyses, which also could explain the wide confidence intervals for the risk estimates. The risk estimates for preterm delivery remained largely unchanged in both groups, suggesting that factors beyond glycemic control also contribute to the increased risk. In contrast, the risk of eLGA appears more dependent on glycemic control in the stratified analyses. This inconsistency could be due to the low number of cases in the sub-analysis, and the interpretation of the results should consider this. Still, the findings might indicate that the increased risk of preterm delivery and eLGA in pregnancies with socioeconomic or mental health disparities could require pregnancy and prepregnancy care that goes beyond glycemic control.

Although we adjusted for known potential confounders, residual confounding cannot be ruled out, and the observational design precludes causal inference. In addition, multiple analyses were conducted, which increases the risk of chance findings. However, the consistency of the results across exposures reduces the likelihood that the associations are spurious.

Conclusions

In this nationwide study within a universal health care setting, we found that both maternal and paternal socioeconomic or mental health disadvantages were consistently associated with a higher risk of preterm delivery, but not eLGA, among women with type 1 diabetes. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions to address social and mental health disparities in order to reduce preterm birth risk among women with type 1 diabetes. Future research should investigate whether interventions aiming at improving diabetes management and factors beyond glycemic control among women with socioeconomic or mental disadvantages could mitigate the disparity in pregnancy outcomes.
