Improving health sector coordination for resilient healthcare systems: European lessons from the COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Stefano Guicciardi, Michael A. Stoto, Chiara Reno, Federico Toth, Svetla Tsolova, Maria Pia Fantini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the importance of integrating the concepts of Public Health Emergency Preparedness and health system resilience, emphasizing the role of coordination across sectors, which is essential for ensuring that systems can respond effectively to public health threats and manage resources efficiently. This paper aims to summarize the lessons learned from the COVID-19 pandemic among European Union Member States, focusing on gaps and improvement areas within coordination of healthcare services.MethodsThe study analyzes the experience of Croatia, Finland, Germany, Italy, and Spain during the initial phase of the pandemic. These countries were selected for their diverse organizational structures and varying responses to COVID-19. The research involved literature reviews, analyses of pandemic preparedness plans, and interviews with national focal points and experts. Key areas of public health emergency capabilities were examined using the Public Health Emergency Preparedness Logic Model developed in 2017, with a particular focus on coordination of health services.ResultsThe analysis focused on four capabilities in the health services domain. Among them, coordination of population-based medicine emerged as a key element in integrating public health and primary care during pandemics and to ensure consistent and coherent response within the whole health system. Common challenges included organizational fragmentation and communication barriers, which hindered effective coordination across healthcare services.ConclusionsThe COVID-19 pandemic revealed, at national, regional and local levels, several gaps in coordination between hospitals, primary care, and public health services. At the same time, it provided lessons on measures that countries can implement to enhance Public Health Emergency Preparedness and thus resilience of health systems, for future health emergencies. By fostering collaboration between services and addressing systemic barriers, healthcare systems can enhance coordination and deliver high-quality, patient-centered care​. More than anything, the pandemic underscored the importance of effective leadership and system adaptability strongly aligned with comprehensive emergency plans that clearly outline in advance tasks, responsibilities and procedures. Furthermore, creating effective communication channels and tools can significantly improve information exchange between healthcare providers and emergency responders, ensuring timely and efficient responses to emerging health threats.

Introduction

In addition to the many lives lost as well as social and economic disruption, the COVID-19 pandemic profoundly affected the functioning of healthcare delivery systems in countries around the globe. Hospitals experiencing high pressure to take care of patients with COVID-19 while ensuring business continuity, a burnt-out workforce, and shortages of medical goods made it difficult to effectively treat patients with COVID-19. Timely access to essential care for patients with other conditions was disrupted [1]. However, the pandemic did present an opportunity to observe – and learn from – health systems’ crisis response in order to strengthen preparedness and resilience.

This report describes part of a project undertaken on behalf of the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) designed to identify challenges experienced by European countries, as well as successful responses to them, and the implications for measuring preparedness in the European Union (EU) Member States. The goal was to identify specific capacities and capabilities that were essential in the COVID-19 response, and thus should be included in preparedness indicators, rather than to assess or judge any specific Member State’s response. We define Public Health Emergency Preparedness as the capability of the public health and healthcare systems, communities, and individuals, to prevent, protect against, quickly respond to, and recover from health emergencies, particularly those whose scale, timing, or unpredictability threatens to overwhelm routine capabilities [2]. While the EDCD project was conducted from a public health perspective, this paper connects the analysis with the growing international literature on health system resilience.

This report summarizes the ECDC project’s findings with respect to coordination within the health sector. Parallel reports address the other two areas: testing and surveillance [3] and emergency risk communication [4]. This analysis focuses on substantive issues; the implications of these results for preparedness measurement are discussed in an ECDC technical report [5].

Methods

This analysis is based on the experience of five EU Member States (Croatia, Finland, Germany, Italy, and Spain) during the first phase of the pandemic, that is before the initiation of vaccination programs in the EU countries, in December 2020. These countries were invited to participate in order to illustrate different national organizational structures and responses to the pandemic, with consideration to the team’s familiarity with the countries’ characteristics (including language abilities), and the countries’ availability and willingness to participate. Germany, Italy, and Spain are decentralized, region-led systems: Germany operates through corporatist self-governance via statutory sickness funds, while Italy and Spain run tax-funded regional national health services. By contrast, Finland (since 2023) and Croatia are nationally steered, with centralized financing and rules.

As mentioned in the ECDC report, this analysis draws on pandemic preparedness plans, reports, and other documents provided by the countries and other studies identified through literature reviews [5]. In particular, we reviewed the growing literature on health system resilience in Europe and elsewhere. In addition, we conducted interviews of individuals in each country knowledgeable about the areas of inquiry. This included discussions with ECDC National Focal Points for preparedness and response as well as other experts in the countries whom they suggested to be contacted. Pawson and Tilley’s approach to conducting stakeholder interviews was employed; the interviews were conducted in English or Italian, and varied in length up to one hour.

Preliminary results were discussed in virtual meetings with ECDC’s National Focal Points for preparedness and response of the five countries participating in the project. Facts not specifically referenced are based on these interviews, but individuals are not identified for reasons of confidentiality. Those interviewed were speaking in their official capacities, so ECDC did not consider this activity as human subjects’ research.

Results

In 2017, Stoto and colleagues introduced a Logic Model that specified the aims and objectives of Public Health Emergency Preparedness as well as the response capabilities and preparedness capacities needed to achieve them [6]. Following its structure, the analysis focused on four key capabilities in the health services domain that healthcare organizations need to address. The first three are well represented in the Logic Model: (1) management of medical countermeasures, supplies and equipment, (2) medical surge, (3) and infection-control practices. A fourth capability, “coordination of community-based medicine,” required a major modification. While not the primary aim of the ECDC project, our research also identified findings in the Logic Model’s capabilities related to coordination and communication within the Public Health Emergency Preparedness system.

Coordination of community-based medicine

The Logic Model includes a “preventive services” capability, but the COVID-19 experience demonstrated that this was not sufficient to describe the challenges of a pandemic, especially regarding the coordination of patient care between in- and outpatient settings, both in the interest of the patients and to help avoid hospital overcrowding. Thus, we propose a new capability on the coordination of community-based medicine. This would be defined as “the ability to activate and strengthen a network of public health and healthcare providers (including mental health and social support agencies, as well as the private sector) using integrated pathways between primary care and hospital during an outbreak of an infectious disease with population-wide impact.”

Community-based healthcare professionals must be able to make autonomous decisions to act promptly in the absence of instructions from the central level. Starting from emergency plans, these healthcare professionals must identify the most effective solutions for treating patients at home; care must be integrated and multidisciplinary teams must be involved. Healthcare providers practicing outside the hospital are distributed throughout the territory, and thus more difficult to coordinate. This means that communication between community-based providers and the other nodes of the emergency network must be two-way. Community-based providers must be kept constantly informed and updated, and also transmit information they collect to hospitals, other emergency responders and decision makers.

In locations where a community-centered approach to healthcare was more developed, health services seemed to have performed better in responding to the pandemic [7]. For instance, during the first wave of the pandemic, the Italian Government approved the introduction of Special Continuity Assistance Units to offer specialized treatment at home for COVID-19 patients without severe symptoms to prevent their arrival at hospitals in critical conditions [8]. The Ministry of Health also announced the recruitment of community nurses and social workers to provide integrated care on the territory. Additional measures to strengthen the health services at the community level were implemented by decree and later included in law 77/2020 [9]. These include regional plans to strengthen and reorganize community-based providers with the aim of ensuring early care to infected patients, their contacts and people in isolation, as well as to fragile, chronic and disabling patients; enhance community-based diagnostic assessment, monitoring and surveillance; and support home care activities both for infected patients and for others with chronic diseases, mental disorders, pathological addictions or disabilities, as well as those who are not self-sufficient or need palliative care and pain therapy. Regional operational centers were activated to ensure the coordination of the community-based health and social and health activities implemented. A mechanism for monthly review of the resilience of welfare and territorial services was established, coordinated by the Ministry of Health to ensure continuous coordination and to support a timely and collaborative decision-making process [10].

In Germany, general practitioners were initially not well prepared and turned to informal digital networks of professional peer groups to obtain information. Subsequently, health insurance companies regulated payments and provided advice on how to manage a practice, specifically giving guidance on how to organize patient workflow. At the national level, guidance was issued by the Robert Koch Institut [11], the national public health institute.

In Finland, hospital access for COVID-19 patients was limited, so telephone call controls were set up to facilitate home care. Nursing home residents were treated there and not in hospitals. Guidelines for healthcare workers were developed for COVID-19 as is done for other diseases. Finnish hospital districts for specialized care had been created as part of a pre-pandemic reform, and have a tight connection with primary care. Some 300 municipalities provided information to hospital districts, which compiled it and sent it to university hospitals. Social services, on the other hand, do not have a district structure, so it was more challenging to collect information from the municipalities.

The COVID-19 experience suggests, therefore, five steps that countries might take in advance to facilitate community-based medicine: prepare emergency plans that identify tasks, responsibilities and procedures for healthcare providers at all levels;in particular, develop plans, protocols and telemedicine tools to treat patients at home; identify and operationalize channels and tools to facilitate communication between healthcare professionals (both in-patient and out-patient) and other emergency responders; these would mainly be computer-based for timely and effective internal communication;train outpatient healthcare professionals to coordinate and communicate with each other;health personnel working in the community must have their own stock of PPE and all the tools necessary to implement these emergency plans.

Stockpiles and equipment supply

In the Logic Model, this capability is defined as the “ability to procure and distribute countermeasures, supplies and equipment, including personal protective equipment (PPE), during an incident.” Equipping emergency responders with the right tools is essential for dealing with any type of crisis. In the event of an outbreak healthcare personnel and other emergency responders, and infected and at-risk individuals require an adequate quality and quantity of PPE. Healthcare facilities must have available the devices, drugs, vaccines and equipment needed to cope with the early stages of the pandemic. Laboratories must have all supplies necessary to respond to a sudden increase in testing activity.

Since emergency equipment and supplies must be readily distributed, it is prudent to have a stockpile set aside in advance. A stockpile is immediately available and usable so saves time required to place an order and have it be delivered. In addition, in situations of crisis and global alarms, some supplies can suddenly become rare goods; they can be unavailable, increase substantially in price, or require unusually long delivery times. Stocks must be in good condition and able to be readily distributed to all operators and facilities involved in the response regardless of whether the stockpile is centralized or decentralized. To the extent that pre-existing supplies prove insufficient, the emergency response system must be able to procure and ensure the timely, continuous, and widespread distribution of supplies and equipment. If emergency supplies are unavailable on the market (or otherwise available but at high cost or with excessively long lead times), national governments may decide to produce these materials themselves. International coordination, particularly in the form of joint procurement arrangement, can also help address global shortages and ensure equitable access [12].

During COVID-19 pandemic, the overreliance on a few countries for production, competition among countries and supply chain disruptions have determined supply shortages globally [12]. For example, in Italy the need to ensure adequate supplies of PPE, laboratory kits, and respiratory support devices was one of the main critical issues early in the pandemic. Since then, the Public Procurement Code (Legislative Decree No. 50/2016) has been changed in order to speed up the public procurement procedures for drugs, medical devices and personal protective equipment by the state administration and the Italian Civil Protection Department (CPD). Indeed, to coordinate the response to the crisis, the CPD was in charge of procurement [13] rather than leave it to the regions as is normally the case. It also had the task of monitoring and controlling the expenditure by implementing bodies that contracted PPE that was delivered directly to the Civil Protection operations center in Rome.

PPE both for private use as well as the healthcare sector also was in short supply also in Germany at the beginning of the pandemic, with a critical issue being overpriced items. A clear division of responsibilities was often lacking, considering the German federated and self-governing system in the healthcare sector [14]. In this sense, health procurement was generally decentralized, although the public system became more centralized over the course of the pandemic [13]. Indeed, the Federal Ministry of Health pushed for increased centralization, ordering aggregate procurement of equipment needed for all doctor’s offices and clinics.

Similarly, although the Spanish health system is regional, the national Ministry of Health was in charge of monitoring the stockpile. Supplies of PPE in health facilities were a concern in all regions. In particular a shortage of face masks caused by early panic buying, and the shortages encouraged profiteering. As a response, the central government centralized purchasing and introduced also price controls on medicines requiring companies producing relevant equipment to inform the central government of their stocks [15]. In addition, on March 26, 2020 the Spanish Government announced a purchase of masks for subsequent distribution by the Ministry of Transport, Mobility and Urban Agenda for the transport sector [13].

Unlike other countries that struggled to keep a steady supply of PPE, Finland never ran out of PPE and was prepared for increasing demand for medical supplies due to a stockpile gathered during the Cold War era [16]. Indeed, the country largely met needs for medical supplies relying on the stock of the National Emergency Supply Agency that, in late March 2020, opened its warehouses for the first time since the Second World War [13]. The main challenges were related to logistics, particularly to sharing and transport of PPE from one hospital to another. No changes to Finnish law were needed, and existing processes for emergency procurement were emphasized.

Dealing with medical surge

In the Logic Model medical surge is defined as the “ability to provide adequate medical evaluation and care during events that exceed the limits of the normal medical infrastructure of an affected area.” [6] To achieve this goal, health systems must be designed to be flexible and “scalable” as needed and without excessive cost. In the specific case of an epidemic surge, the healthcare response system must be able to rapidly increase the number of patients admitted, the volume of diagnostic and therapeutic activities provided, surveillance activities and contact tracing on the territory. This requires adequate resources (capacities) such as infrastructure, procedures, and trained personnel on which healthcare facilities can rely. In the face of a medical surge, the risk is that hospitals do not have enough beds or space to admit and treat all patients or enough specialized staff. There also may be a shortage of machines, devices, medications to treat all patients simultaneously.

To manage peak demand, organizations can employ four strategies alone or in combination: buffering, smoothing, forecasting, and rationing [17]. Buffering means setting aside resources in excess of normal requirements. These extra resources constitute safety valves that can be activated and used under conditions of emergency or particular organizational stress. The design of healthcare systems must - in other words - be guided by the principle of redundancy [18]. A second strategy is smoothing; in a pandemic, community-based providers can treat patients at home or local facilities as appropriate, saving hospital beds for more severe cases. As discussed below, community-based medicine together with the adoption of preventive and restraining measures can help reduce the pressure on hospital facilities and spread demand over a longer period. Forecasting involves being able to foresee and make adjustments in advance, allowing hospitals to reorganize, to schedule staff shifts, to stockpile, and so on. This can be facilitated by the use of computer tools and statistical models. The final strategy, rationing, is usually implemented only when the previous ones have failed. Since it is not possible to take care of all applicants simultaneously, it is necessary to decide who to treat first. Rationing involves setting criteria according to which patients receive priority for treatment.

Medical surge usually affects some hospital departments more than others, so staff from less affected departments may be called upon to support the departments that are most under pressure. Highly reliable healthcare organizations ensure that emergencies do not slow down or disrupt the day-to-day operations of other departments in the hospital. To achieve this, some hospitals implement a modular approach in which the system’s components are separate and combinable when necessary. The malfunction of one organizational component must not extend to other parts and cripple the entire system.

From March 2020, hospitals throughout northern Italy reported system saturation due to the high level of patient loads requiring intensive care. The shortage of hospital beds, ventilators and personnel at all levels represented a concrete threat. In order to support COVID-19 patients care, staff moved from other services and disciplines and medical volunteers started to operate in the most affected regions [8]. Many hospitals were converted into COVID-19 hospitals and new ones built to increase the capacity of the system and provide care for those in need. Consequently, by April 2020 the number of intensive care beds almost doubled and those in infection and respiratory disease departments quadrupled. The increase in hospital beds was subsequently consolidated by decree 34/2020, converted into Law 77/2020, which included hospital reorganization plans aimed at making national structural changes to increase intensive and sub-intensive care beds [9]. Steps were also taken to strengthen the state of preparedness at the regional level, such as ensuring the availability of suitable beds for ordinary hospitalization and for intensive and sub-intensive therapy dedicated to COVID-19 patients. Changes were also made to ensure the availability of trained and continuously updated health personnel who can work in different settings in an emergency or who can support the prevention departments in diagnostic assessment, isolation, contact functions tracing, and quarantine [10].

In Germany, elective surgery was stopped and screening and medical treatments postponed to ensure the increasing demand for COVID-19 care [19]. Some hospitals voluntarily decided, or were required by federal regulations, to care exclusively for COVID-19 patients. Crisis team meetings were organized to manage the high load of patients, occasionally requiring transfers of patients between federal states. Modernization of the German healthcare system over the last 20 years led to more hospital beds, more ventilators, more intensive care unit beds and more hospital doctors per capita than any other comparable country in Europe. Nevertheless, some intensive care beds could not be used completely because of shortages of nurses. In response, qualified staff were pooled together within hospitals to treat severely ill patients with both COVID-19 and other conditions [20].

The Federal Ministry of Health suggested hospitals develop strategies to strengthen and expand the workforce. Although the strategies varied across German states, the main efforts were concentrated on expanding health workforce capacity, while limited efforts were put on skill-mix and task‐shifting. Examples of these strategies include covering extra work hours, recruitment of retirees, and recruitment procedures for nurses. Medical and nursing students also were recruited and allowed to perform supervised work in different COVID-19 response capacities [19].

At the beginning of the pandemic, health facilities in the worst affected regions of Spain struggled because of inadequate intensive care capacity and insufficient number of ventilators. In particular, Catalonia and Madrid cancelled non-emergency surgery and cleared beds where possible. A decree allowed the regions to take over the management of private health services and military installations were used for public health purposes [15]. The decree also allowed the hiring of graduates without specialization, final year medical and nursing students, and extending contracts of medical residents to address the shortages of health staff.

In Finland, the coordination of the response to the COVID-19 pandemic was by area and daily meetings were held, especially at the beginning of pandemic, with the participation of the preparedness unit of the Ministry of Health, the five university hospitals and the head of the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare (THL). The number of ICU beds and ward capacity varied across the country, but as of April, 2020, the evaluation carried out on the ICU capacity outside the Helsinki-Uusimaa hospital district responsible for the capital region concluded that the potential estimated increase in beds capacity could be sufficient to meet the needs of COVID-19 patients [21]. Moreover, considering that Finnish hospital buildings were quite new, they could be flexible enough to accommodate the needs of the patients.

Hospital infection control practices

In the Logic Model, this capability is defined as the “authority and practical ability to adapt existing (or develop new, if necessary) policies and guidance to both prevent the spread of infection in healthcare and related settings and for treating affected individuals, including crisis standards of care.” In the event of a pandemic, it is critical that hospital facilities are able to separate contagious and non-contagious patients. Infectious individuals (confirmed, suspected, or possible cases of contagion) must be isolated to avoid hospitals themselves becoming “multipliers” of contagion. This objective can be achieved either by selecting specific facilities to which all contagious individuals should be directed, or by identifying - within the individual hospital facility - the units dedicated to the diagnosis and treatment of infectious subjects. It is incumbent on the management of healthcare facilities to adopt (and enforce) protocols that prescribe what precautions hospital staff must observe, and that ensure the safety of hospital personnel, patients and visitors.

SARS-CoV-2 was highly transmissible in healthcare settings, including among healthcare workers, illustrating the importance of infection prevention and control measures [22]. In Germany, recommendations on how to separate suspected COVID cases, non-COVID-19 cases and COVID-19 cases were issued rapidly early during the pandemic. Designated hospitals or wards were identified and dedicated to COVID-19 care, and in some hospitals two different emergency areas were prepared. This kind of separation proved to be much more difficult in the ambulatory sector. In addition, every practice set up its own rules and many of them were not accessible, while some required testing. To improve access while reducing the risk of healthcare associated infections, many practices started video calls, and in summer 2020, legislation was adopted to allow phone calls to claim reimbursement from health insurance funds.

Coordination and communication within the Public Health Emergency Preparedness system

The COVID-19 experience showed the importance of coordinating patient care between in- and outpatient settings. To achieve this goal, healthcare systems must be equipped with certain capabilities to function effectively under the pressures of a health emergency. A first key element is the role of leadership in healthcare systems, that should guarantee decisive and competent decision-making during crises. Effective leaders are essential for navigating the complexities of healthcare coordination, as they are responsible for instilling confidence, making strategic decisions, and surrounding themselves with capable advisors who can help guide the system through turbulent times.

The Italian experience during the pandemic serves as an example of how leadership and organizational structures impacted crisis management. Regions such as Lombardy, which had a hospital-centric approach, had several organizational difficulties compared to areas like Emilia-Romagna and Veneto, where there was a greater emphasis on primary care and integrated services [23]. This contrast underscores the importance of a well-rounded healthcare strategy that values both hospital care and community-based interventions.

Leadership must also be supported by agile organizational structures capable of rapid decision-making and coordination: the establishment of agile and streamlined emergency response structures, such as regional “war rooms” proved to be very effective in facilitating rapid decision-making and coordination. Regions with such structures were better equipped to manage the crisis effectively, demonstrating the value of having pre-existing, adaptable organizational frameworks that can be quickly mobilized in emergencies.

Adhering to national-level norms and guidelines during a crisis is also critical, but it should be balanced with flexibility and discernment. While some regions struggled due to strict adherence to these norms, others, like Veneto and Emilia-Romagna, successfully navigated the pandemic by adapting strategies to fit local needs, showing the importance of critical thinking in leadership [24].

A key takeaway is the necessity of cultivating a culture of rapid learning and adaptation within healthcare systems. This involves swiftly assimilating and implementing available knowledge to adjust strategies in real-time and enhance resilience and improve outcomes during emergencies.

A constant, bidirectional and structured communication also emerged as a cornerstone for an effective healthcare coordination, crucial for maintaining continuity of care. Population-based professionals, including general practitioners and public health experts, need continuous updates on the epidemiological situation and the healthcare system’s capacity to handle surges in demand. Similarly, outpatient services must relay the information they gather to hospitals and emergency responders swiftly to facilitate timely and informed decision-making.

In many countries, the pandemic revealed weaknesses in the existing communication systems, such as the lack of unified electronic health records (EHR), which hindered the efficient sharing of patient data across different healthcare settings. This resulted in repeated tests, misdiagnoses, and delayed treatments, emphasizing the need for integrated and interoperable health information systems to improve care coordination and reduce redundancy.

Resource management is another decisive aspect of healthcare coordination. During crises, the ability to quickly mobilize and distribute resources, including personnel, medical supplies, and equipment, plays a strategic role: coordinated efforts can prevent resource shortages, reduce waste, and ensure that all healthcare settings have the necessary tools to operate efficiently. This can be achieved through contingency plans in place, including stockpiles of personal protective equipment (PPE) and other essential resources, to enable healthcare systems to respond to sudden increases in demand.

Beyond the main issues in healthcare coordination, there are significant opportunities for improvement and innovation. One such opportunity lies in the integration of digital health technologies, which can facilitate better communication and information sharing across healthcare settings. The use of telemedicine, for example, can improve access to care and enable real-time collaboration between healthcare providers. Digital health tools also support patient self-management and engagement, empowering individuals to take an active role in their health and reducing the burden on healthcare systems.

Policy reforms also present opportunities to improve healthcare coordination by addressing systemic barriers and promoting alignment between healthcare providers. Policies that incentivize the use of integrated care pathways and care models that reward collaboration between healthcare providers could drive improvements in care delivery.

To realize these opportunities, several areas require attention and improvement.

Strengthening primary care and public health services as the foundation of a population-based healthcare delivery is paramount, with the aim of reducing the burden on hospitals and improving overall health outcomes by ensuring that patients receive timely and appropriate care in the community. Within this context, healthcare professionals need to be equipped with the skills and knowledge to work effectively in integrated care settings and adapt to new technologies and care models. Ongoing education and professional development can support the transition to a more coordinated and collaborative healthcare system.

Discussion

Public Health Emergency Preparedness focuses on the strategic and coordinated activities taken in advance to mitigate the impact of public health emergencies such as pandemics, bioterrorism, or natural disasters and to ensure a timely and effective response. Resilience expands this perspective. Deeply rooted in socio-ecological systems thinking, it pertains to the ability of systems, communities, and individuals to absorb shocks, adapt, and transform in the face of adversities [25, 26]. Health system resilience encompasses social, economic, environmental, and institutional dimensions, enhancing the adaptive capacity of individuals and communities to cope with the impacts of health emergencies, in terms of promoting social cohesion, improving communication and information sharing, and building trust between individuals and institutions, while economic resilience in public health involves building the capacity of individuals and communities to adapt to the economic impacts of public health emergencies, including job loss, income reduction, and economic disruption [27].

Resilience is a holistic concept that emphasizes the importance of addressing the root causes of vulnerability and building the capacity of individuals, communities, and systems to cope with the impacts of public health, including effective political leadership, interpersonal trust, trust in government institutions, and similar factors that Bollyky and colleagues, as well as other authors, have found to have been critical to good COVID-19 outcomes [28, 29]. Thus, in some sense, resilience is a product of preparedness together with good governance and leadership and trust in institutions.

Resilience and coordination are closely related concepts that reinforce each other in strengthening healthcare systems. Resilient healthcare systems are better equipped to handle disruptions and adapt to changing circumstances, which, in turn, facilitates effective coordination of healthcare services. For example, during a public health emergency, a resilient healthcare system with robust infrastructure and adaptive capacity could quickly mobilize resources, establish communication channels, and coordinate response efforts across various healthcare providers and agencies [30]. Similarly, effective coordination enhances resilience by promoting collaboration, information sharing, and resource optimization, thus enhancing the overall capacity of the healthcare system to respond to health threats and deliver quality care.

In this perspective, although focused on preparedness, our results also identified characteristics of national healthcare systems associated with resilience. This can be seen most clearly in the findings related to coordination and communication, and specifically regarding the coordination of community-based medicine. Another connection is the need to design health systems that are flexible and able to quickly scale certain activities up or down without excessive cost to deal with medical surge challenges [12].

Limitations

This analysis is limited to the first phase of the pandemic and focuses on coordination in the healthcare sector. As noted in the introduction, parallel reports address testing and surveillance and emergency risk communication, but coordination and communication within the Public Health Emergency Preparedness system, with other sectors such as education and civil protection, or at the international level are not addressed.

The analysis also was based primarily on five EU Member States that were invited and expressed willingness to participate. The number of individuals who were available to be interviewed was also limited. The interviews were conducted primarily with governmental officials in health agencies at the national level. With few exceptions, we did not speak to regional or local officials or representatives of private-sector healthcare organizations, doctors, nurses, or other healthcare providers, or patients’ organizations. Although some of these perspectives were represented in the published literature and by the interviewees, other issues may have been missed. However, the convergence in issues raised in the interviews and in the literature review suggests that no major issues were not addressed.

Conclusion

The COVID-19 pandemic revealed, at national, regional and local levels, several gaps in coordination between hospitals, primary care, and public health services; at the same time, it provided lessons on measures that countries can implement to enhance Public Health Emergency Preparedness and thus resilience of health systems, for future health emergencies. By leveraging digital health technologies, fostering collaboration between services, implementing policy reforms, and addressing systemic barriers, healthcare systems can enhance coordination and deliver high-quality, patient-centered care​.

More than anything the COVID-19 pandemic has underscored the importance of effective leadership and system adaptability that, however, must be strongly aligned with comprehensive emergency plans that clearly outline in advance tasks, responsibilities and procedures. Furthermore, creating effective communication channels and tools, especially those that are computer-based, can significantly improve information exchange between healthcare providers and emergency responders, ensuring timely and efficient responses to emerging health threats.
