Unveiling enhanced HCN sensing: a comparative DFT study on reactivity and sensitivity of X-doped biphenylene nanotubes (X = B, Al, and Ga)
Authors: Mohammadreza Hosseini, Sona Bajelan, Nasrin Delfan, Eghbal Omari, Atena Pakzadiyan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rational design of sensing materials is essential for developing high‑performance detectors for hazardous gases. In this work, density functional theory (DFT) is employed to assess B‑, Al‑, and Ga‑doped biphenylene nanotubes (BPNTs) for hydrogen cyanide (HCN) detection. Pristine BPNT shows minimal interaction with HCN, with an adsorption energy of − 0.12 eV, negligible charge transfer, and non‑spontaneous adsorption. Doping significantly strengthens the interaction, producing adsorption energies stronger than − 1 eV and markedly enhanced charge transfer, indicative of spontaneous and robust adsorption. The results from our systematic study indicate that the nitrogen lone pair and the C-N π bond in hydrogen cyanide are the dominant charge-donating electronic states toward the defective nanotubes. The doped systems also exhibit improved sensing characteristics, including an energy‑gap reduction of approximately 9% and short predicted recovery times. Complementary analyses—ELF, NBO, and IRI—were performed to further justify the interaction mechanisms and confirm the formation of active sites upon doping. Overall, the results identify defective BPNTs as promising candidates for next‑generation HCN gas sensors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48484-5.

Introduction

Hydrogen cyanide (HCN) is a gaseous form of cyanide that poses significant toxicity risks to human health and acts as a harmful air pollutant1. Exposure to this colorless gas, at the particular concentrations, can lead to disorders of the blood, cardiovascular, respiratory and nervous systems2–4. Despite its toxicity, HCN is widely utilized in the synthesis of various materials, with applications spanning from mining to medicine4–6. Sources of HCN emissions include combustion processes, tobacco smoke, and chemical reactions such as those between ammonia and natural gases. More interestingly, hydrogen cyanide has been identified as a biomarker for Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection, opening new possibilities for clinical diagnostics7,8. Accordingly, the development of high-performance HCN sensors with suitable reactivity and sensitivity could enable bifunctional devices capable of both ambient air quality monitoring and biomarker detection in exhaled human breath9,10.

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs), a widely studied class of one-dimensional nanomaterials, are structurally derived from rolling graphene layers with defined chirality, which govern their electronic and mechanical properties11–14. The quantum confinement inherent to CNTs has unlocked novel electronic properties and enhanced functional performance in areas such as catalysis15–17. While CNTs have enabled breakthroughs in electronics, energy storage, gas sensing, and biomedicine, most studies concentrate on structures possessing graphene-based nanotubes with hexagonal carbon lattices.

To overcome graphene’s inherent limitations—such as its lack of a band gap and low chemical reactivity—various graphene allotropes containing non‑hexagonal carbon rings have been proposed, with several structures already realized experimentally or predicted theoretically18–22. Among these innovative nanostructures, biphenylene (BP)—a non-benzenoid carbon allotrope composed of tetra-, hexa-, and octa-carbon rings—shows high potential for diverse functionalities. First predicted theoretically23, this two-dimensional nanosheet was successfully synthesized in ultrathin form by Fan et al.24. Theoretical studies confirm biphenylene’s metallic character and its superior HER catalytic activity over pristine graphene, as evidenced by a calculated hydrogen adsorption free energy of just 0.29 eV for the key intermediate25. As an anode material for ion batteries, biphenylene demonstrates promising performance, characterized by a low diffusion barrier for ion migration and a high storage capacity relative to other graphene allotropes26,27. While biphenylene monolayers have been widely investigated, the properties and possible applications of their nanotube analogues (BPNTs) are largely unexplored.

First-principles simulations indicate that aluminum-doped BPNT serves as a highly efficient carbon dioxide (CO₂) sensor, exhibiting a strong capability to generate an electrical signal upon gas adsorption28. Separately, the potential of pristine and boron-doped BPNT as anode materials for Na-ion batteries has been investigated. In that context, Vafaee et al. found that boron doping enhances the anode performance of BPNT by lowering the sodium diffusion barrier, increasing the sodium adsorption energy, and improving charge carrier storage capacity29. The thermal and mechanical characteristics of BPNT nanomaterials have also been explored using molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. For instance, Woellner et al. reported a significant reduction in the lattice thermal conductivity of BPNT at room temperature compared to graphene-based nanotubes, attributing this effect to lower phonon group velocities30 Furthermore, Junior et al. analyzed the fracture patterns of BP-based nanotubes, finding that under longitudinal stress—for both armchair and zigzag chiralities—stress accumulates on the tetragonal rings, ultimately leading to the formation of octagonal carbon rings31.

Extensive theoretical studies have firmly established that structural modifications—such as doping, the introduction of vacancies, and other defect engineering techniques—serve as powerful strategies for reducing the band gap and significantly enhancing the reactivity and sensitivity of nanomaterials toward detecting HCN and other toxic gases32–34. Mudhafar et al. reported that aluminum doping is an effective strategy for enhancing the sensing performance of T-graphene by increasing the adsorption energy and inducing a pronounced band-gap modulation of approximately 13.7%, yet it is frequently hampered by slow recovery kinetics35. In a related study, aluminum doping was shown to induce magnetization in a ZnO monolayer, significantly improving its sensing performance toward HCN detection36. Moreover, aluminum has been identified as the most effective dopant for tuning the electronic structure and chemical reactivity of MoS₂ toward HCN sensing; however, this enhancement is constrained by an undesirably long recovery time37. In a comparative investigation, Vessally et al. demonstrated that boron-, aluminum-, and gallium-doped carbon nanocones exhibit substantially enhanced reactivity and sensitivity toward HCN molecules38. Recently, van der Waals heterostructures have emerged as novel nanomaterials with exceptional properties as a novel class of functional materials39,40. Hetero-bilayers such as Zr₂CO₂/MoS₂, WSe₂/phosphorene, and silicene/graphene have been explored as high-performance sensors for hydrogen cyanide (HCN). Studies indicate that performance optimization for practical application critically depends on strategies like atomic doping and the introduction of vacancy defects41–43.

This work presents a comprehensive first-principles investigation into the structural, electronic, and HCN-sensing properties of pristine and B-, Al-, and Ga-doped biphenylene nanotubes—a system previously unreported. We demonstrate how doping modulates intrinsic BPNT properties, including frontier orbitals and derived chemical reactivity indices. Also, the impact on vibrational characteristics is elucidated. Our analysis reveals how elemental doping fundamentally enhances the reactivity and sensitivity of BPNT toward HCN. The aim of this study is to provide atomistic insights that can guide the rational design of high-performance HCN sensors.

Computational details

First-principles calculations were carried out within the framework of density functional theory (DFT) using the M06-2X functional in conjunction with 6-31G(d, p) basis set. All quantum chemical simulations were performed with the Gaussian 09 software package (Revision D.01)44, which implements well-established algorithms for electronic structure calculations. The M06-2X functional is extensively utilized in studies of gas adsorption, drug delivery, and combined theoretical-experimental work due to its ability to account for both covalent and non-covalent interactions45–47. For systems containing unpaired electrons, including doped nanotubes and their corresponding complexes, spin-polarized calculations were conducted to determine the ground-state spin configurations. The biphenylene nanotube was modeled with zigzag chirality. It was constructed by rolling a biphenylene nanosheet, composed of repeating units of four hexagonal and tetragonal carbon rings, along the zigzag direction. The structure was terminated by saturating the dangling carbon bonds at each nanotube ends with hydrogen atoms, yielding a final C₉₆H₈ molecular cluster represented in Fig. 1.

Pristine, boron-, aluminum- and gallium-doped biphenylene nanotubes

This section presents a systematic investigation of the structural, electronic, and vibrational properties of pristine biphenylene nanotubes alongside defect-engineered variants containing B-, Al-, and Ga-dopants (denoted as B-BPNT, Al-BPNT, and Ga-BPNT, respectively).

Their distinct chemical environments arise from their shared rings: C1 is part of two 8-membered rings and one 4-membered ring, whereas C2 is part of the 4-, 6-, and 8-membered rings. Cohesive energy (Ecoh) was used to evaluate the thermodynamic stability of the studied BP-based nanotubes. This quantity corresponds to the energy released when a material forms from its constituent atoms in their isolated states. As the results in Table 1 show, the cohesive energy is negative for all nanostructures, confirming their structural integrity in both pristine and defective forms. From the pristine BPNT to its defective versions, the cohesive energy becomes less negative (more positive), which is attributed to the reduced symmetry in the defective structures and disturbing delocalized π-electronic system. Furthermore, both the dipole moment (µ) and polarizability (α) can be modulated by doping (Table 1). The dipole moment, a measure of charge separation, is highest for B-BPNT at 1.46 Debye. In contrast, the maximum polarizability has been obtained for Ga-BPNT. This analysis indicates that the tendency of BP-based nanotubes to acquire a dipole moment under an external electric field is enhanced following boron, aluminum, and gallium doping. The reactivity of the materials can be interpreted through the role of frontier molecular orbitals (HOMO and LUMO) using conceptual density functional theory (CDFT). The CDFT parameters, calculated using Equations S1 to S4, are listed in Table 2. According to this table, doping with boron, aluminum, or gallium destabilizes the HOMO and stabilizes the LUMO, shifting both orbital energies in a direction that enhances the reactivity of the BPNT nanostructures. The energy gap (Eg) decreases for all defective nanotubes relative to the pristine structure, with the smallest gap (2.48 eV) belonging to B-BPNT. To confirm the calculated Eg values, DOS plots have been provided in Figure S1. Based on the Table 2, the ionization potential (I) is lowered upon dopant incorporation, indicating that the energy required to remove an electron from the highest occupied state is reduced in the defective nanotubes. Conversely, the electron affinity (A) increases for all doped BPNTs due to stabilization of their LUMO, indicating higher electron accepting capacity of the doped biphenylene nanotubes. Consequently, doping with boron, aluminum, and gallium decreases the chemical hardness (η) and increases the chemical softness (S) of the BPNT. These CDFT results collectively demonstrate that the chemical reactivity of biphenylene nanotubes is enhanced by doping with B, Al, and Ga.

As shown in Fig. 4, pronounced absorption peaks appear in the range of 20–50 THz. The thermal properties of the studied nanostructures are affected by the introduction of dopants. Specifically, the heat capacity at constant volume (Cv) increases in the doped biphenylene nanotubes. Our calculations yield the following Cv values: pristine BPNT (222.16 cal.mol− 1.K ), B-BPNT (223.61 cal.mol− 1.K), Al-BPNT (226.81 cal.mol− 1.K), and Ga-BPNT (227.09 cal.mol− 1.K). Notably, Cv increases from boron- to gallium-doped systems, indicating a direct relationship between dopant atomic size and heat capacity at constant volume.

One of the most pronounced effects of doping in carbon-based nanomaterials is the alteration of chemical bonding within the material network. Here, using NBO analysis, we systematically investigate how doping with the selected dopants influences electron density and, consequently, the nature of the bonds formed between the dopant and its neighboring atoms in BP-based nanotubes. Figure 5 shows the electron localization function (ELF) and local atomic charges, for the pristine and doped BPNT nanostructures. The ELF plots reveal that the robust covalent bonds between neighboring carbon atoms are altered upon B, Al, or Ga doping, with electron density shifting toward the more electronegative atoms. This effect is more pronounced for Al- and Ga-doped systems, which can be attributed to their larger atomic radii and distinct chemical behavior compared to carbon.

The data show a reduction in the Wiberg index upon doping, which exhibits a reciprocal relationship with the dopant’s atomic radius. Accordingly, the sensible difference is seen for the nanotubes doped with Al and Ga. This trend demonstrates that the covalent character of the C–C bonds is reduced, shifting toward more ionic bonding upon doping with the mentioned dopants. Regarding atomic hybridization, the contribution of the p-state diminishes progressively for dopants from B to Ga. This reduction correlates with the observed decrease in Wiberg index and the shift toward ionic bonding character. To conclude the present section, our results establish that doping with B, Al, or Ga tailors the structural, vibrational, and electronic properties of biphenylene nanotubes and enhances their chemical reactivity.

Figure 6 presents the calculated IRI maps for all studied nanotubes, including pristine and doped BPNTs. The analysis reveals distinct spatial distributions for various non-covalent interactions. In the pristine BPNT, pronounced steric repulsion is localized within the tetragonal and hexagonal carbon rings, contrasting with the weaker interactions observed in the octagonal rings. Upon doping, the topology of the interaction fields reorganizes significantly, with regions of high electron density and steric repulsion shifting from the ring centers toward the dopant sites. The spatial extent and intensity of these features scale with the dopant’s atomic radius, reaching a maximum in the gallium-doped system. Furthermore, the IRI maps indicate a notable change in the chemical bonding environment near the dopant. For Al- and Ga-doped BPNTs, the covalent character of the bond between the dopant and adjacent carbon atoms is reduced compared to that in the boron-doped analogue, as evidenced by the altered interaction patterns in these regions.

HCN adsorption on the surface of pristine and defective biphenylene nanotubes

This section provides a comprehensive evaluation of pristine as well as B-, Al- and Ga-doped biphenylene nanotubes as potential HCN sensors, employing structural and various electronic analyses. These investigations, performed through a comparative approach, elucidate the reactivity and sensitivity of the pristine and doped nanotubes.

Hydrogen cyanide is a linear, polar triatomic molecule. Its optimized geometry and associated electrostatic potential plot are presented in Fig. 7. As indicated in the figure, the electronegativity difference between atoms localizes a region of negative electrostatic potential on the nitrogen terminus and a positive potential on hydrogen.

Fig. 7Optimized structure of HCN with ESP plot

Optimized structure of HCN with ESP plot

This charge distribution designates nitrogen as the nucleophilic site and hydrogen as the electrophilic site in the HCN molecule. We identified the ground-state configuration for each complex by evaluating all possible adsorption sites and selecting the geometry with the lowest energy. Figure 8 presents a schematic of the optimized complexes: HCN@BPNT, HCN@B-BPNT, HCN@Al-BPNT, and HCN@Ga-BPNT. The calculated adsorption energy (Ead), adsorption enthalpy (Had), Gibbs free energy of adsorption (Gad), and the change in energy gap (%Eg) for all complexes are summarized in Table 4.

Fig. 8Optimized complexes of HCN@ (a) BPNT, (b) B-BPNT, (c) Al-BPNT and (d) Ga-BPNT structures

Optimized complexes of HCN@ (a) BPNT, (b) B-BPNT, (c) Al-BPNT and (d) Ga-BPNT structures

According to Fig. 8, HCN adsorbs in a horizontal orientation parallel to the BPNT surface at an equilibrium distance of 2.59 Å. This configuration exhibits exothermic adsorption with Ead = − 0.12 eV. Thermochemical analysis reveals a correspondingly small negative adsorption enthalpy (Had = − 0.09 eV). However, the calculated Gibbs free energy of formation for HCN@BPNT is positive (Gad = + 0.15 eV), indicating that the adsorption process is non-spontaneous at room temperature. Furthermore, the associated change in the electronic energy gap is minimal (Eg = − 0.59%), suggesting very low sensitivity. Consequently, the pristine biphenylene nanotube is not a suitable candidate for efficient HCN sensing, which aligns with the typical behavior observed for low-dimensional carbon-based networks54,55.

The ground-state configurations of the defective BPNT complexes (B-, Al-, and Ga-doped) are presented in Fig. 8. Structural analysis reveals that, unlike the pristine nanotube, HCN adsorbs preferentially via its nitrogen terminus, oriented directly above the dopant atom in each case. This preferential orientation is attributed to the electrophilic character of the dopant atoms and the nucleophilic nature of the nitrogen atom in hydrogen cyanide. For the B-, Al-, and Ga-doped BPNTs, the optimized bond lengths between the nitrogen terminus of adsorbing HCN molecule and the respective dopant atoms are 1.56 Å, 2.00 Å, and 2.05 Å, respectively. According to the data in Table 4, the incorporation of dopants significantly enhances the adsorption energy of HCN on biphenylene-based nanotubes. For the defective BPNT materials, the calculated adsorption energies range from − 1.08 eV to − 1.60 eV, with the strongest binding (Eads = − 1.60 eV) observed for the HCN@Al-BPNT complex. To ensure the reliability of the selected computational approach, adsorption energies were recalculated using the triple-zeta basis set, 6–311 g(d, p), combined with the M06-2X functional. The computed adsorption energies are Ead (HCN@BPNT) = − 0.07 eV, Ead (HCN@B-BPNT) = − 1.10 eV, Ead (HCN@Al-BPNT) = − 1.67 eV and Ead (HCN@Ga-BPNT) = − 1.38 eV. The negligible difference observed between double-zeta and triple-zeta basis sets confirms the robustness and validity of the chosen computational details. Thermochemical analysis confirms this enhancement, as the adsorption enthalpies (Had) for all doped systems are more negative than that of the pristine nanotube. Crucially, the Gibbs free energy of adsorption (Gad) is also negative for the doped nanotubes. This indicates that, unlike the non-spontaneous adsorption on pristine BPNT, HCN adsorption on the doped variants is thermodynamically spontaneous at room temperature and P = 1 atm. This enhanced reactivity correlates with improved sensor sensitivity, as indicated by a more significant change in the energy gap upon HCN adsorption.

Figure S2 presents the PDOS data and corresponding energy gaps for the complexes, utilized to estimate %Eg. Although HCN adsorption on the pristine BP nanotube induces a negligible band gap change (− 0.59%), adsorption on B-, Al-, and Ga-doped nanotubes substantially reduces the %Eg by − 9.14%, − 9.21%, and − 8.86%, respectively. This marked change in conductivity enables the generation of a strong electrical signal, forming the basis for an effective HCN sensor. Upon HCN adsorption, the work function of the pristine BPNT changes minimally (+ 2.85%), confirming a weak interaction. However, doping drastically amplifies this response: B-, Al-, and Ga-doped nanotubes show substantial work function reductions of -8.93%, -8.25%, and − 7.96%, respectively. This pronounced modulation confirms the enhanced sensitivity imparted by doping. Our spin-polarized computations predict total spin vectors of 0.524, 0.532 and 0.531 for HCN@B-BPNT, HCN@Al-BPNT and HCN@Ga-BPNT respectively. Compared to the isolated doped nanotubes, a reduction in S is observed which can be attributed to the charge transfer from HCN to the defective BP-based nanotubes.

Charge transfer is a key parameter for evaluating the interaction strength between toxic gases and nanomaterials58,59. Natural bond orbital analysis provides detailed insight into the enhanced reactivity of doped nanotubes. Our results indicate that the pristine biphenylene nanotube acts as a weak electron donor in its interaction with HCN, with a minimal charge transfer of 0.01. In contrast, doping with boron, aluminum, or gallium fundamentally alters this electronic relationship. The defective nanotubes (B-, Al-, and Ga-BPNT) instead act as electron acceptors, with calculated charge transfers from HCN to the nanotubes of 0.35 e, 0.17 e, and 0.18 e, respectively. This reversal and significant increase in charge magnitude demonstrate how doping can dramatically enhance charge transfer interactions with hydrogen cyanide.

For the pristine BPNT, no chemical bond was formed with HCN, and accordingly, no significant WBI was observed for this complex. In contrast, for the doped nanotubes, a bond analysis reveals distinct interactions between the nitrogen atom of HCN and the dopant atoms. The calculated WBIs are WBI (B–N) = 0.69, WBI (Al–N) = 0.32, and WBI (Ga–N) = 0.34 for the HCN@B-BPNT, HCN@Al-BPNT, and HCN@Ga-BPNT complexes, respectively.

To elucidate the precise directions of charge transfer between HCN and the proposed nanotubes, all significant donor-acceptor interactions with their corresponding second-order perturbation energies (E2) are listed in Table 5. First, for the defective nanotubes, all calculated E2 values are increased, consistent with the reported enhancement of charge transfer between hydrogen cyanide and doped BP-based nanotubes. The largest values correspond to interactions where HCN acts as the donor and the defective nanotube as the acceptor. For the pristine biphenylene nanotube (BPNT), the largest E2 value (3.03 kcal/mol) arises from the interaction where the C–C π-bonding orbital of the BPNT donates electron density to the C–H σ* antibonding orbital of HCN. In the defective systems, the role of the lone pair Rydberg states (LP*) on the dopant atoms as an acceptor is pronounced in the NBO analysis. A consistent trend is observed across all complexes, where the largest E2 values correspond to donation from the non-bonding orbital of nitrogen in HCN. The highest calculated E2 values for the HCN@B-, Al-, and Ga-BPNT nanostructures are 223.67, 47.86, and 46.85 kcal/mol, respectively. This analysis reveals, at the atomic level, the origin of the enhanced reactivity of doped biphenylene nanotubes compared to their pristine counterpart.

Finally, we present IRI analyses for the optimized complexes of HCN with the investigated nanotubes, as shown in Fig. 10. For pristine BPNT, the interaction with hydrogen cyanide is characterized as purely van der Waals. In contrast, doping strengthens the interaction and introduces a significant covalent character, evident from the distinct blue regions between HCN and the dopant atoms in the IRI maps. These findings are consistent with and strongly supported by our previous analyses of adsorption energies and charge transfer. One can employ DFT results to determine the interaction type between adsorbate and adsorbent60,61. In our case, considering adsorption energy, thermochemical analysis, electronic state modulation, NBO data, and IRI plots, we conclude that HCN exhibits a weak physical interaction with pristine BPNT. In contrast, X‑doped biphenylene nanotubes (X = B, Al, and Ga) display stronger interactions of chemical nature.

Conclusion

In this work, the structural, electronic, vibrational, and hydrogen cyanide (HCN) sensing properties of pristine and doped (B, Al, Ga) biphenylene nanotubes (BPNTs) were investigated using DFT calculations at the M062X/6-31G(d, p) level of theory. We demonstrated that doping alters the nanotube geometry and modifies the HOMO and LUMO frontier orbitals, thereby increasing the reactivity of the systems. Electron localization function (ELF) and natural bond orbital (NBO) analyses revealed that doping creates ionic bonds between the dopant and neighboring carbon atoms, forming local active sites. While HCN adsorption on pristine BPNT is weak, with negligible charge transfer and energy gap change, doped nanotubes exhibit significantly enhanced and spontaneous adsorption. The calculated adsorption energies are − 1.08 eV for B-BPNT, -1.60 eV for Al-BPNT, and − 1.55 eV for Ga-BPNT. NBO analysis elucidates the origin of the improved charge transfer, identifying the nitrogen lone pair and the C-N π bond in HCN as the dominant electron donors to the defective nanotube structures. The sensitivity, quantified by the change in energy gap upon adsorption, reaches approximately − 9% for doped systems, indicating a strong electrical signal response to HCN. Furthermore, calculated recovery times confirm the practical potential of these materials for real-world sensor applications. This study provides a systematic framework, utilizing quantum chemical tools such as NBO analysis, for designing high-performance gas sensors.
