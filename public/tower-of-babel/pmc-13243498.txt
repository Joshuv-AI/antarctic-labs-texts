A similarity-based predictive scheduling method for dynamic electric vehicle charging load management
Authors: Khalil Gorgani Firouzjah, Jamal Ghasemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Real-time energy management at public electric vehicle (EV) parking lots is a complex challenge that involves the distribution grid stability considering user demand uncertainties. This paper develops a multi-stage, data-driven control framework that focuses on both load profile smoothing and ensuring user constraints such as desired charge level at departure. In this regard, the proposed algorithm tries to overcome the limitations of classical methods by integrating three layers: historical similarity-based prediction, dynamic predictive optimization with a genetic algorithm, and a final repair stage. Comprehensive evaluations of different stochastic scenarios ensure the operational robustness of this algorithm. Peak-to-average ratio (PAR) of the entire network is reduced by shifting load from nighttime peak hours to off-peak hours. This performance, which has a high convergence with the global optimal state, demonstrates the system’s effectiveness in peak shaving and valley filling strategies. Additionally, the system has prevented destructive stresses on the transformers by limiting the maximum load ramp rate. Final repair mechanism ensures final state of charge (SoC) error for all EVs to below 0.1% on the user side. Ultimately, with an average processing time of a few seconds and a 35% reduction in charging pile occupancy, this method acts as a real-time solution that enhances network resilience while postponing the need for heavy investments in physical infrastructure development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42594-w.

Introduction

The increasing growth of electric vehicles (EVs) in urban transportation fleets has led to fundamental changes in the electricity grid. This shift from a traditional system to a smart and interactive ecosystem has significant environmental and energy sustainability benefits. However, it impacts on the electricity generation and distribution infrastructures. These challenges necessitate a deep reassessment of the design and management of power systems to effectively address their emerging demands. In the meantime, the role of parking lots (PLs) as EV hubs has become more important than ever. Public PLs, which were previously considered only spaces for parking vehicles, have now become key hubs for smart energy management and grid load optimization due to the large concentration of EVs at charging mode. Charging capacity management at these centers requires a complex coordination between energy demand, limited grid capacity, and generation resources. The dynamic nature of arrival and departure times, uncertainty in charging demands, and requested charges are the parameters that contribute to this complexity1. Achieving an effective solution for EV charging management requires smoothing the overall load curve of charging facilities (to ensure network stability) and reduce peak demand. These objectives are in conflict with ensuring high-quality service and meeting user charging demands. Establishing a compromise between mentioned objectives is a complex multi-objective optimization problem which necessitates the use of advanced and adaptive algorithms for optimal management in dynamic and real environments2. However, the inherent complexities of this problem go beyond just a power allocation issue. These uncertainties in the patterns of EVs lead to significant challenges for optimal management decisions. Recent studies emphasize the need to develop approaches to optimally manage uncertainties in the EV charging process3–5. On the other hand, there are inherent conflicts in this system. The power grid management aims to balance the load to avoid congestion and increase stability but EV users expect their vehicles to be charged to the desired level by the designated travel time. This conflict leads to a complex multi-objective optimization problem that requires innovative solutions1,2.

Due to the large number of EVs and the variety of charging intervals, the search space for managing the optimal charging solution has expanded significantly. Hence, traditional optimization methods are not able to be successfully implemented in practice6. Therefore, it is necessary to develop algorithms that are capable of making instantaneous or near-instantaneous decisions. These algorithms should be able to adapt to the changing dynamic conditions of the system7. In addition, it is of great importance to comply with strict operational constraints, such as the limited power capacity of charging stations, and ensuring the charging demands. However, failure to comply with these constraints will directly undermine user satisfaction and wider adoption of this technology8.

Literature review and related work

According to the literature, considerable research has been conducted on EV charging management systems in order to address the aforementioned challenges. So, this section will present a comprehensive and critical analysis of previous studies. The aim of these analyses is to identify common approaches, assess their strengths and limitations, and ultimately highlight research gaps that this paper seeks to address. The EV charging management approaches can be broadly classified into several main groups. At the first level, there are uncontrolled charging methods in which EVs, upon connecting to a charging station, are immediately charged with the maximum available power regardless of network conditions or consumption patterns (referred to as first come, first served)9. These methods are established based on fixed power allocation during the vehicle parking period called as constant time (CT), constant power (CP) allocation with priority at the beginning of the parking period (Front-Loaded CP) and power allocation focused on the closing hours of the parking period (Rear-Loaded CP). These methods are attractive due to their simplicity of implementation, low computational requirements, and rapid charging processes. However, their performance limit the ability to meet more complex objectives10,11. Furthermore, these approaches do not incorporate active objectives for optimizing energy consumption or load management at the scale of the PL or the wider network.

In contrast, controlled (coordinated) charging strategies use procedures that actively manage the charging process of EVs. The aim of these strategies is to achieve the goals such as load balancing, cost reduction or network stability12. These strategies can be classified into two main categories. The first category includes global or offline optimization methods that rely on complete and accurate information of EVs arrival and departure times as well as required charge over a determined time frame (like the next 24 h)9. These methods use mathematical programming tools including linear programming13,14, mixed integer linear programming15, quadratic programming16,17 or global metaheuristic algorithms such as genetic algorithm (GA)18, particle swarm optimization19, and ant colony optimization20, to formulate an optimal charging schedule. However, these approaches are able to provide optimal solutions for objective function. Also, their main limitation is that future full forecasting is need which is impractical in real-world scenarios21.

The second category of strategies includes predictive charging management approaches with the aim of system uncertainties management. These methods commonly use advanced forecasting techniques (including statistical models, machine learning algorithms or historical data analysis) to estimate future recharge demand. Additionally, these predictions and the current network state inform the charging decisions. In fact, real-time EV charging management is a fundamental challenge in advancing sustainable transportation systems and their integration with modern power grids. So, existing research gaps continue to impede the development of comprehensive and practically applicable solutions despite considerable advancements in this field. The optimization approaches presented in the literature rely on unrealistic assumptions of fully predicting future conditions. This assumption significantly limits the real-world applications (which are characterized by inherent uncertainties).

Research shows that the initial focus was on developing methods that could meet network constraints in real-time while being computationally efficient. For example, Benetti et al.22 presented a real-time management method for EV charging processes based on a centralized control structure and power flow assessment with high computational efficiency. Liu and Qin23 also proposed a method for coordinated EV charging. Their approach, which includes an enhanced hysteresis model and a prioritization strategy, enables real-time management at the community level. Notably, it is computationally efficient with the aim of load curve smoothing and charging costs reduction. However, these methodologies are limited by oversimplified user and battery characteristics models.

With the advancement in modeling, the acceptable capabilities of EVs have been used to manage the usage side and reduce costs in real-time. For example, Yao et al.24 presented a real-time charging scheme involving EV parking stations that were demand response (DR) programs and used a convex on/off and release strategy for faster charging and cost reduction. While25 addressed the real-time management of charging station. Also, the capacity of EVs (including the potential for vehicle to grid energy transfer) is utilized during longer parking hours. These studies (despite the mentioned capabilities) have limitations on the characteristics of the internal network. On the other hand, other research has developed hybrid rules to deal with real-time operational challenges. Farias et al.26 present a hybrid approach for real-time operational applications in charging stations, supporting photovoltaic generation systems and vehicle battery energy storage systems. In this application, it combines daily scheduling with real-time rule-based strategies and is generally planned and managed for users.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI), especially deep reinforcement learning (DRL), have provided powerful tools for dynamic management of EV charging systems in real-time. A new method for controlling EV charging in real-time (using multi-agent reinforcement learning) has been suggested in27 that can handle many EVs and chargers at once while improving several goals. Conversely, Liu and Ji28 specifically designs a novel framework for real-time charging scheduling to cater to the asynchronous plug-in dynamics of EVs. The framework uses robust dynamic programming to be resilient to uncertainties. Additionally, a new method using quantum reinforcement learning for optimizing EV charging systems in real-time is introduced by Xu et al. in29. This method uses quantum neural networks to improve how quickly and reliably the system learns. While these AI-based approaches show significant capabilities in handling environmental uncertainties and scalability, they also entail significant computational complexity (especially in the training phase), and some are still in the early stages of development. In parallel with direct control, some studies have focused on providing accurate and real-time estimates to support control systems. According to30, a data-driven method for real-time estimation of the cumulative EV charging load is presented based on representative meter data, which effectively reduces communication costs and maintains model stability. Also, EV charging load prediction based on real-time user profiles and traffic flow has been performed, which significantly increases the realism of the prediction31. These studies provide critical inputs to control systems but do not directly address control decisions and remain separated from the actual control and decision-making process.

Research gaps

Despite the progress made in real-time EV charging management, reviews indicate some research gaps. Although previous studies have investigated various optimization approaches, a major limitation of these studies is their reliance on unrealistic assumptions about complete predictions of future conditions9. This inherent incompatibility significantly limits their applicability to real-world dynamic scenarios characterized by pervasive uncertainties. In addition, there is a considerable weakness in many forecasting models that focus on network load balancing which is due to the lack of sufficient attention to critical user constraints. For example, accurately achieving final charge levels within acceptable tolerances has received less attention in the literature11. Because realistic user behaviors (including unpredictable arrival and departure schedules or variable charging needs) are insufficiently taken into account.

There is a fundamental research gap in how to combine dynamic forecasts effectively (based on pattern recognition) with advanced optimization algorithms (metaheuristics). This integration should be able to simultaneously ensure load smoothing and strict adherence to operational constraints on an hourly timescale. This particular type of integration is crucial for maintaining grid stability and meeting the real-time charging needs of each EV, but it has not been sufficiently investigated. It also calls attention to a more comprehensive use of advanced AI techniques (beyond traditional machine learning) to increase the accuracy of forecasting and charging management.

Contributions and innovations

This section discusses the main innovations and contributions of the paper to advance the real-time EV charging management. The proposed method introduces a predictive algorithm for EV charging management in PLs. Unlike previous studies that often relied on global optimization techniques based on the unrealistic assumption of perfect prediction, the proposed algorithm operates dynamically and hourly. The algorithm makes its decisions based only on real-time data and short-term predictions derived from similar historical patterns. Furthermore, the proposed approach addresses a fundamental shortcoming of current predictive methods. These methods do not sufficiently address the strict constraints related to user satisfaction. This means that ensuring accurate final charge levels within specified tolerance ranges (which is critical for user satisfaction) is often neglected. This goal is achieved using several mechanisms. These mechanisms include prioritizing critical vehicles, imposing heavy penalties on the fitness function of the hourly optimization algorithm in case of constraint violations, and a final correction step. This final correction step acts as an absolute safeguard and removes any remaining deviations. The proposed algorithm combines similarity-based dynamic forecasting with hourly metaheuristic optimization. This integration simultaneously leads to load smoothing and strict adherence to operational constraints. Hourly scheduling loop (stage 2)

This section explains the details of the second stage of the proposed algorithm, which is defined as the hourly scheduling loop. Stage 2 acts as the dynamic core of the predictive and adaptive charge management system. At this stage, charging decisions are made hourly from the opening of the PL until its closing. Each iteration (hourly) includes the identification of active EVs. Then, it uses similarity-based predicter to forecast future demand and set dynamic load targets. Subsequently, the allocation of charging for critical EVs is prioritized. Finally, a GA is used to optimize charging for flexible EVs. All these steps are carried out with strict adherence to operational constraints and ensuring final charging. The pseudocode for this comprehensive hourly scheduling process is presented in Table 2. Accordingly, a dedicated GA is executed at each t for flexible EVs (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{I}}_{flex,t}$$\end{document}), which are not categorized as urgent and possess sufficient time for charging. Its aim is to determine the optimal charge allocation for these flexible EVs at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t$$\end{document}. The main objective is to minimize the absolute deviation of the total load PL for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t$$\end{document} from the predicted target average load (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{target,t}^{pred}$$\end{document}). This is while all predefined operational constraints must be considered as strict constraints. The GA used in this hourly loop is implemented with the following components and steps. The performance and convergence characteristics of the GA are significantly affected by its parameters. Therefore, these parameters are dynamically adjusted based on the number of active flexible EVs at the current hour (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N}_{flex,t}$$\end{document}). The population size parameter (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N}_{pop}$$\end{document}​) dictates the number of chromosomes (candidate solutions) in each generation. A larger population generally allows for a broader exploration of the search space. It is dynamically set as (11).11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N}_{pop}=max\left(100,10\times {N}_{flex,t}\right)$$\end{document}Table 2Pseudocode of Stage 2: Hourly Scheduling Loop.

Maximum generations (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N}_{gen}$$\end{document}​) defines the maximum number of iterations the GA will perform for a given hour. A higher value typically leads to better convergence but increases computational time; it is set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N}_{gen}=200$$\end{document}. The crossover rate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{cross}$$\end{document}​) defines the probability that two selected parent chromosomes will exchange genetic material to create offspring. A value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{cross}=0.85$$\end{document} is chosen to promote effective recombination and exploration. Mutation rate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{mut}$$\end{document}) is the parameter that controls the probability of a random gene (charge value) in an offspring chromosome being altered. This alteration occurs randomly during the mutation process. In fact, the mutation is crucial for maintaining diversity within the population and enabling the GA to escape local optima. It is set dynamically as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{mut}=0.05/{N}_{flex,t}$$\end{document}, which means that it is inversely related to the length of the chromosome in order to keep the right amounts of perturbation. The chromosome within the GA’s population is represented as a vector of charge allocation values for the flexible EVs (specifically for the current \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t$$\end{document}). The length of each chromosome is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N}_{flex,t}$$\end{document}. Each gene \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${g}_{k}$$\end{document}​ (for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k=1,\dots ,{N}_{flex,t}$$\end{document}​) corresponds to the charge \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p}_{chg,i,t}$$\end{document} for a particular flexible EV \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$i\in {\mathcal{J}}_{flex,t}$$\end{document}. The permissible range for each gene is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[0,{P}_{max}^{chg}]$$\end{document}. The initial population of the GA is not generated purely randomly. A population seeding strategy is employed to provide a warm start and enhance initial diversity. The initial population consists of a mix of two types of solutions. First, heuristic solutions are included: at least one chromosome is seeded with a good heuristic solution. For each flexible \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${i}^{th}$$\end{document} EV, its charge \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p}_{chg,i,t}$$\end{document} is initially set based on its ideal even distribution over its remaining stay hours. This provides a strong, feasible starting point. Second, random solutions constitute the rest of the population: the remaining chromosomes are initialized with random charge values within the feasible range \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[0,{P}_{max}^{chg}]$$\end{document}, ensuring a comprehensive exploration of diverse regions in the search space.

The fitness function serves as the intelligent core of the hourly GA, evaluating the quality of each chromosome (candidate allocation for flexible EVs for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t$$\end{document}). It is called by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{r}}{\mathrm{un}}{\mathrm{h}}{\mathrm{ourly}}{\mathrm{g}}{\mathrm{a}}$$\end{document} function and is designed to combine the primary objective with robust penalty terms for constraint violations. The objective is to minimize the absolute deviation of the total aggregated load for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t$$\end{document} (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L}_{t}^{\mathrm{candidate}}$$\end{document}) from the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{target,t}^{pred}$$\end{document}. This directly contributes to load smoothing for the current hour.12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{Objective}}=\left|{L}_{t}^{\mathrm{candidate}}-{P}_{target,t}^{pred}\right|$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L}_{t}^{\mathrm{candidate}}$$\end{document} is the total load for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t$$\end{document}. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L}_{t}^{\mathrm{candidate}}$$\end{document} is calculated as the sum of fixed allocations to urgent EVs and the proposed allocations from the current chromosome for flexible EVs:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L}_{t}^{\mathrm{candidate}}=\sum_{i\in {\mathcal{I}}_{urgent,t}}{p}_{chg,i,t}^{\mathrm{fixed}}+\sum_{k=1}^{{N}_{flex,t}}{g}_{k}$$\end{document}

Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p}_{chg,i,t}^{\mathrm{fixed}}$$\end{document} represents the charge already allocated to urgent EVs.

Strict constraint penalties are crucial for ensuring full constraint satisfaction. Any breach of these strict constraints leads to a severe penalty, rendering the corresponding solution highly unfavorable for the GA. In fact, the penalties are applied if the allocated charge \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${g}_{k}$$\end{document} for any flexible \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${i}^{th}$$\end{document} EV in the chromosome is outside the valid range \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[0,{P}_{max}^{chg}]$$\end{document}, ensuring immediate feasibility of the proposed charge. Negative charge values are also penalized under this rule.14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{Penalty}}_{chg limit}=\sum_{k=1}^{{N}_{flex,t}}\left(\mathrm{max}(0,{g}_{k}-{P}_{max}^{chg})+\mathrm{max}(0,-{g}_{k})\right)\times {10}^{9}$$\end{document}

As shown, a very large multiplier (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${10}^{9}$$\end{document}) is used to enforce these bounds strictly, driving the GA away from infeasible solutions. Another parameter in this section is defined as the future feasibility guarantee penalty. This parameter is a penalty to ensure that the ultimate charging constraint is met. The GA proposes a charge allocation at each \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t$$\end{document}. The fitness function then assesses this allocation under a critical condition whether it enables the EV to meet its requested charge within an acceptable tolerance or not. This evaluation considers the remaining stay hours of the EV, assuming future charging occurs at the maximum possible rate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{max}^{chg}$$\end{document}). By incorporating this check, the algorithm ensures feasibility while maintaining computational efficiency. The updated SoC for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${i}^{th}$$\end{document} EV is determined after the allocation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${g}_{k}$$\end{document} charge at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t$$\end{document}.15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$SOC_{{current,i^{\prime}}} = SOC_{current,i} + g_{k}$$\end{document}

The remaining energy needed for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${i}^{th}$$\end{document} EV after \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t$$\end{document} is:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_{needed,i,t + 1} = E_{req,i} - SOC_{{current,i^{\prime}}}$$\end{document}

The maximum possible charge for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${i}^{th}$$\end{document} EV could receive in its future remaining stay hours is:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{max,fut,i,t+1}=\left({T}_{dep,i}-(t+1)\right)\times {P}_{max}^{chg}$$\end{document}

If \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{needed,i,t+1}>{E}_{max,fut,i,t+1}$$\end{document} (a deficit (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta {E}_{deficit,i}$$\end{document}) exists), indicating a potential future failure to meet the requested charge. The GA generates a charge allocation for each time step \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t$$\end{document}. A critical evaluation is then performed by the fitness function. it must verify whether the proposed allocation enables the EV to achieve its target charge level within the specified \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pm 0.1{\%}$$\end{document} tolerance margin. This assessment accounts for the vehicle’s remaining connection time, while assuming all future charging occurs at maximum power (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{max}^{chg}$$\end{document}). The verification process ensures operational feasibility while maintaining solution quality. If the allocation fails this feasibility check, a substantial penalty is imposed. Comparative algorithms

The results of the proposed algorithm are compared with four distinct charging strategies to provide a comprehensive performance benchmark:

CT Charging

This strategy distributes the total required energy for each EV uniformly over its entire parking duration. The charging power for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${i}^{th}$$\end{document} EV at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p}_{chg,i,t}$$\end{document}, is constant and calculated as:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({E}_{req,i}-SO{C}_{init,i})/({T}_{dep,i}-{T}_{arr,i})$$\end{document}

This method inherently promotes load smoothing by spreading individual demands, but it does not actively consider the aggregate PL load or external grid conditions.

CP (front-loaded) charging

The strategy is based on the immediate charging service upon arrival of each EV. Each EV is charged with the maximum power capacity available at the station (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{max}^{chg}$$\end{document}) upon arrival. The charging process continues at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{max}^{chg}$$\end{document} until the requested SoC for the EV is met. If the remaining energy required at the final charging hour is less than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{max}^{chg}$$\end{document}, only the required amount is delivered. This first-come, first-served approach usually leads to significant load peaks during periods of high EV arrivals, as several vehicles consume maximum power simultaneously.

CP (rear-loaded) charging

Conversely, this strategy defers charge delivery to the final hours of an EV’s parking duration. An EV’s charging process initiates by allocating power at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{max}^{chg}$$\end{document} from its scheduled departure time, working backward hour by hour, until its requested SoC is fulfilled. This strategy tends to shift load peaks towards the end of the operational day, potentially creating high demand spikes during evening hours when many vehicles are departing.

Optimal global offline scheduling

The optimal global offline scheduling method is introduced as an ideal criterion for maximum load smoothing. It works by assuming complete knowledge of all EV arrivals, departures, and charging needs throughout the day. With this data, a global optimization algorithm finds a charging schedule that minimizes the PAR while strictly observing all charging constraints. For this study, the exchange market optimization (EMO) algorithm is used as the global optimizer34,35. EMO is a metaheuristic algorithm inspired by the decentralized interactions and dynamics of agents in an economic exchange market. In this context, each agent in the EMO population represents a complete charging schedule for the entire day. The structure of this decision variable (agent position) is a flat vector of charging powers, each element (gene) of which is related to the charge allocated to a specific EV at a specific hour, only during its actual stay at the charging station. The total number of active decision variables, is equal to the sum of all the residence times of each EV (in hours) during the 24-h period. A mapping mechanism is employed to translate this flat vector into the full hourly charging table \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N}_{EV}\times 24$$\end{document} for the fitness assessment. Agents are dynamically categorized into buyers (solutions with relatively worse fitness, seeking improvement) and sellers (solutions with better fitness, representing market leaders) based on their current PAR value. The market leader represents the best solution found globally so far. Agents update their positions (i.e., generate new candidate charging schedules) through a combination of exploration and exploitation strategies. Buyers move towards a randomly selected seller (to explore better solutions) and also towards the global Market Leader. A crucial aspect of EMO’s application is constraint handling. After each agent’s position is updated (i.e., a new candidate charging schedule is generated), a repair chromosome function is immediately applied. This function strictly ensures that the generated schedule adheres to all constraints: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0\le {p}_{chg,i,t}\le {P}_{max}^{chg}$$\end{document} for all hours, and critically, that each EV’s final charge is within the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pm 0.1{\%}$$\end{document} tolerance of its request. This repair mechanism makes the search process robust to constraint violations, as the optimizer primarily explores a feasible or near-feasible region.

Finally, the fitness of each agent is evaluated (charging schedule). This function quantifies the PAR and incorporates severe penalties for any lingering constraint violations (though these should be minimal after the repair step). Optimal global scheduling with EMO serves as a robust theoretical upper bound for performance comparison. The proposed predictive method, operating with partial information and real-time adaptation, is expected to perform remarkably close to, but potentially not better than, this ideal benchmark due to its inherent real-time constraints and the uncertainty it faces.

Conclusion

This paper develops a real-time, data-driven energy management framework for electric vehicle (EV) parking lots. This method successfully addresses the dual challenge of flattening the grid load and ensuring user demand in the presence of stochastic uncertainties. Unlike traditional methods, this research relies on three layers include the integration of historical similarity-based forecasting for demand estimation, a decision-making horizon with a genetic algorithm for dynamic optimization, and a final repair stage to ensure hard constraints. Simulation results across multiple scenarios have demonstrated the operational effectiveness of this approach in three layers. In the field of grid sustainability and peak management, the proposed algorithm has demonstrated its effectiveness in smoothing the load profile. Quantitative analyses indicate that this method has been able to reduce PAR of the entire network from 1.50 (in the base case) to 1.42. This performance, which has a very high convergence with the global optimal state (PAR = 1.45), demonstrates the system’s ability to intelligently shift load from peak evening hours (7 PM to 10 PM) to off-peak midday hours (valley filling). Additionally, by limiting the load ramp rate to safe values (less than 160 kW per hour), mechanical and thermal stresses on the distribution transformer have been minimized, which directly leads to increased equipment lifespan. From a computational burden and infrastructure management perspective, the results proved that the algorithm’s complexity is not a barrier to industrial implementation. The average execution time of 3.44 s per decision-making step occupies only a negligible fraction of the one-hour time window, enabling real-time performance. Additionally, the fast-charging strategy at appropriate times has led to reduced congestion at the station. So that the peak load on charging ports decreased from 309 devices (in the uncontrolled method) to around 200 devices. This 35% release of physical capacity means it’s possible to serve more vehicles without the need for investment in physical station development. Applying the final repair stage played an important role in the user satisfaction index and system accuracy.
