Systemic Inflammation and Nutritional Index as a prognostic marker in endometrial cancer
Authors: Nagihan Kolkıran, Atike Pınar Erdoğan, Mustafa Şahbazlar, Mustafa Şahin, Ferhat Ekinci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The clinical course of endometrial cancer exhibits substantial variability, underscoring the need for additional biomarkers to enhance risk stratification accuracy. This study aimed to evaluate the prognostic utility of a novel composite index, integrating systemic inflammatory and nutritional parameters, herein termed the Systemic Inflammation and Nutritional Index (SINI), in patients diagnosed with endometrial cancer. This retrospective study included 104 patients with histologically confirmed endometrial cancer. Clinical, pathological, and laboratory data were extracted from institutional medical records. SINI was computed using the formula: platelet count × neutrophil count / lymphocyte count × serum albumin. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was employed to ascertain the optimal cut-off value, thereby stratifying patients into low and high SINI cohorts. Survival outcomes were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier analysis, and prognostic factors were identified via Cox regression analysis. In multivariable Cox regression analysis, treating SINI as a continuous variable, SINI remained significantly associated with overall survival (HR: 1.656; 95% CI: 1.203–2.279; P ═ 0.002). Exploratory ROC analysis identified a potential cutoff value of 159.3 for patient stratification. In subsequent cutoff-based analyses, patients with SINI >159.3 demonstrated significantly worse overall survival in multivariable analysis (HR: 2.89; 95% CI: 1.30–6.43; P ═ 0.009). Kaplan–Meier analysis further corroborated these findings, revealing significantly diminished overall survival in the high SINI group compared with the low SINI group (log-rank P ═ 0.006). The SINI was associated with survival outcomes in endometrial cancer. As a novel composite biomarker derived from routinely available and cost-effective clinical parameters, SINI offers a potentially practical and accessible tool for refined risk stratification in endometrial cancer patients.

Introduction

Endometrial carcinoma (EC) stands as the most prevalent gynecologic malignancy in high-income nations, exhibiting a consistently rising incidence attributed to aging demographics and escalating obesity rates [1, 2]. While outcomes for early-stage disease are generally favorable, a substantial proportion of patients experience recurrence and ultimately poor survival [2, 3]. Traditional clinicopathological factors, such as International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) stage, histological subtype, tumor grade, and lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI), form the bedrock of risk stratification. However, these parameters inadequately capture the marked heterogeneity in clinical outcomes observed among EC patients [2]. Consequently, there has been increasing investigation into circulating peripheral blood biomarkers that reflect disease burden in EC. Cancer antigen 125 (CA125), for instance, has demonstrated correlation with extra-uterine disease, lymph node metastasis, and advanced stage, with elevated levels linked to poorer survival outcomes in gynecological cancers [4]. Nevertheless, these individual markers only partially elucidate the intricate biological complexity of the disease.

The pivotal role of systemic inflammation in shaping tumor characteristics and outcomes has garnered increasing recognition. Tumor-associated inflammation can originate from obesity-related cytokine production and chronic low-grade inflammatory signaling, thereby fostering cellular proliferation, reducing apoptosis, and ultimately facilitating tumor development and progression [5]. Furthermore, systemic inflammation has been associated with adverse clinical outcomes, reflecting alterations in circulating immune and hematologic parameters in EC patients [5, 6]. Peripheral blood components, particularly neutrophils, lymphocytes, and platelets, have thus emerged as accessible biomarkers that mirror the dynamic interplay between tumor biology and host immune response [6]. These components serve as accessible indicators of the host inflammatory response. Neutrophils are known to promote tumor progression through the secretion of pro-inflammatory mediators, whereas lymphocytes are central to anti-tumor immunity. Therefore, an imbalance between these components signifies a shift towards a tumor-promoting inflammatory state [7, 8]. Building upon these biological mechanisms, several inflammation-based biomarkers derived from routine blood tests have been investigated in EC. Among these, composite indices such as the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio have been explored for their potential to reflect systemic inflammatory status and tumor aggressiveness [8, 9].

More recently, composite indices that integrate inflammatory, immune, and nutritional parameters have attracted considerable attention. The hemoglobin–albumin–lymphocyte–platelet (HALP) score, for example, has been proposed as a comprehensive biomarker reflecting tumor–host interaction and has demonstrated independent prognostic value for recurrence and survival outcomes in EC [10]. These findings suggest that combining multiple systemic parameters may provide a more accurate assessment of disease biology than single biomarkers alone [10].

Despite these advancements, currently available biomarkers still capture only limited facets of the complex interplay between tumor biology and systemic host conditions. In this context, we developed a novel composite biomarker, the systemic inflammation and nutritional index (SINI), calculated from platelet count, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and serum albumin levels. By integrating markers of tumor-promoting inflammation (platelets and neutrophils), host immune response (lymphocytes), and nutritional status (albumin), SINI was designed to encapsulate multiple biological dimensions relevant to cancer progression. This study aimed to evaluate the prognostic significance of SINI for survival outcomes and its utility in risk stratification for endometrial cancer.

Materials and methods

This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Patient and data collection

Patients with histologically confirmed endometrial cancer treated at Manisa Celal Bayar University between March 2011 and October 2024 were retrospectively analyzed. Inclusion criteria comprised patients aged ≥18 years with available survival data, laboratory parameters obtained at diagnosis for SINI calculation, and complete baseline clinicopathological data. Patients with missing laboratory data or follow-up outside our institution were excluded. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, certain clinicopathological variables, such as LVSI and myometrial invasion, were not consistently available. These were retained as “unknown” categories in descriptive and univariate analyses but were not included in multivariable models. Data retrieved from medical records included age, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, presenting symptoms, body mass index, stage, histology, grade, Ki-67 index, LVSI, myometrial invasion, nodal and metastatic status, treatment modalities, and baseline laboratory parameters (neutrophils, lymphocytes, albumin, platelets, carcinoembryonic antigen, cancer antigen 125).

SINI measurement

SINI was calculated using the formula: SINI ═ (platelet count × neutrophil count) / (lymphocyte count × serum albumin level). The component (platelet × neutrophil / lymphocyte) corresponds to the SII as reported in the literature [11]. Platelet, neutrophil, and lymphocyte counts were recorded as ×109/L, and albumin as g/dL. Laboratory parameters used for SINI calculation were obtained at the time of diagnosis, prior to the initiation of any cancer-directed treatment, including surgery, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy. To enhance interpretability, SINI values were divided by 100 before statistical analyses; thus, hazard ratios reflect the risk associated with each 100-unit increase in SINI.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was overall survival (OS), defined as the time from diagnosis to death or last follow-up. The secondary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS), defined as the time from diagnosis to disease progression, recurrence, or death from any cause.

Ethical statement

The study protocol was approved by the Health Sciences Ethics Committee of Manisa Celal Bayar University Faculty of Medicine (approval no. 20.478.486/1219; March 9, 2022). All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were conducted using the chi-square test, Student’s t-test, or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards models. SINI, SII, and CA-125 values were rescaled per 100-unit increments prior to Cox regression analyses. Variables included in the multivariable Cox regression analyses were selected based on clinical relevance, univariate findings, and the limited number of events to minimize model overfitting and unstable estimates. Kaplan–Meier figures, number-at-risk tables, and a bootstrap calibration analysis for 3-year overall survival using 200 bootstrap resamples were generated using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A standard receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis based on survival status was performed to determine the optimal cut-off value for SINI using Youden’s index. A two-sided P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 125 patients with histologically confirmed endometrial cancer treated at Manisa Celal Bayar University between March 2011 and October 2024 were assessed for eligibility. After excluding patients due to missing laboratory data (n ═ 13) or inadequate follow-up/follow-up outside our institution (n ═ 8), 104 consecutive eligible patients were included in the final analysis. The mean age was 61.6 ± 10.6 years, and the mean body mass index was 31.9 ± 6.6 kg/m2. Most patients presented with vaginal bleeding (79.8%) and underwent primary surgery (86.5%), with lymph node dissection performed in 72.1%. At diagnosis, 17.3% of patients had stage I disease, 12.5% stage II, 37.5% stage III, and 32.7% stage IV disease. Endometrioid histology accounted for 59.6% of cases, and lymphovascular space invasion was present in 35.3%.

Exploratory ROC curve analysis demonstrated that SINI significantly discriminated overall survival (area under the curve [AUC]: 0.633; 95% CI: 0.525–0.741; P ═ 0.020). A potential cut-off value of 159.3 was identified for patient stratification using the maximum Youden index (0.254), corresponding to a sensitivity of 74.5% and specificity of 50.9%. Patients were stratified into low and high SINI groups, comprising 27 (26.0%) and 77 (74.0%) patients, respectively. Patients with SINI >159.3 were significantly older (mean age 62.9 vs. 57.5 years; P ═ 0.022) and more frequently presented with advanced-stage disease (P ═ 0.024), non-endometrioid histology (P ═ 0.025), and higher-grade tumors (P ═ 0.039). Detailed clinicopathological characteristics according to SINI groups are presented in Table 1.

Survival outcomes

The median follow-up duration for the cohort was 51.5 months (interquartile range [IQR]: 16–112.8 months), during which 51 death events were recorded. The estimated median OS was 103 months (95% CI: 37.6–168.4 months), as depicted in Figure 1. The 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS rates for the entire cohort were 88%, 71%, and 54%, respectively.

For exploratory survival analyses, patients were stratified into two groups based on a SINI cut-off value of 159.3: a low SINI group (SINI ≤159.3) and a high SINI group (SINI >159.3). Of the observed death events, 8 (15.7%) occurred in the low SINI group, while 43 (84.3%) occurred in the high SINI group. Patients in the low SINI group exhibited a significantly longer mean OS (134.9 months; 95% CI: 111.4–158.5 months) compared to those in the high SINI group (97.1 months; 95% CI: 75.2–119.0 months) (Figure 2). Furthermore, the 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS rates were notably higher in the low SINI group (96%, 80%, and 80%, respectively) than in the high SINI group (85%, 51%, and 42%, respectively), with this difference reaching statistical significance (log-rank P ═ 0.006).

Univariate and multivariate analyses of overall survival

Univariate Cox regression analysis identified several clinicopathological factors significantly associated with worse OS: positive lymph nodes (HR: 1.10; 95% CI: 1.042–1.156; P < 0.001), non-endometrioid histology (HR: 2.27; 95% CI: 1.302–3.954; P ═ 0.004), Grade 2 disease (HR: 6.15; 95% CI: 1.376–27.485; P ═ 0.017), Grade 3 disease (HR: 7.29; 95% CI: 1.659–32.022; P ═ 0.009), and the presence of LVSI (HR: 5.86; 95% CI: 1.715–20.030; P ═ 0.005). Conversely, primary surgery (HR: 0.29; 95% CI: 0.150–0.577; P < 0.001), adjuvant radiotherapy (HR: 0.37; 95% CI: 0.208–0.640; P < 0.001), and adjuvant chemotherapy (HR: 0.36; 95% CI: 0.199–0.635; P < 0.001) were associated with improved OS.

SINI was significantly associated with OS (HR: 1.065; 95% CI: 1.019–1.112; P ═ 0.005). Specifically, patients with SINI >159.3 demonstrated a significantly increased mortality risk in univariate Cox regression analysis (HR: 2.75; 95% CI: 1.287–5.886; P ═ 0.009). Among laboratory parameters, higher SII (HR: 1.015; 95% CI: 1.003–1.026; P ═ 0.011), higher Ki-67 index (HR: 1.01; 95% CI: 1.001–1.024; P ═ 0.030), elevated CA-125 levels (HR: 1.044; 95% CI: 1.011–1.078; P ═ 0.012), and lower albumin levels (HR: 0.54; 95% CI: 0.341–0.863; P ═ 0.010) were also significantly associated with worse OS (Table 2).

In an exploratory cut-off based multivariate Cox regression analysis, SINI >159.3 remained an independent predictor of shorter OS (HR: 2.89; 95% CI: 1.302–6.429; P ═ 0.009). Other significant risk factors for worse OS included liver metastasis (HR: 2.73; 95% CI: 1.407–5.308; P ═ 0.003), lung metastasis (HR: 3.08; 95% CI: 1.605–5.914; P < 0.001), and histopathology (HR: 2.20; 95% CI: 1.233–3.935; P ═ 0.008). Notably, CA-125 lost its statistical significance after adjustment in this multivariate model (Table 2).

To further elucidate the association between SINI and overall survival, a sensitivity analysis was conducted utilizing a simplified disease-stage grouping (Stage IV vs Stages I–III at diagnosis) alongside major treatment variables. This analysis confirmed that higher SINI was independently associated with poorer OS (HR: 2.49, 95% CI: 1.13–5.47, P ═ 0.023). Stage IV disease at diagnosis also predicted worse OS (HR: 2.35, 95% CI: 1.15–4.81, P ═ 0.019), while adjuvant chemotherapy was associated with improved survival (HR: 0.45, 95% CI: 0.23–0.90, P ═ 0.023). Conversely, surgery (HR: 0.82, 95% CI: 0.37–1.83, P ═ 0.627) and adjuvant radiotherapy (HR: 0.73, 95% CI: 0.36–1.48, P ═ 0.388) did not demonstrate a significant association with OS in this sensitivity analysis.

Univariate and multivariate analyses of progression-free survival

Univariate Cox regression analysis for PFS identified several clinicopathological factors associated with disease progression: an increasing number of positive lymph nodes (HR: 1.06; 95% CI: 1.010–1.114; P ═ 0.017), de novo metastasis (HR: 32.77; 95% CI: 12.377–86.743; P ═ 0.001), and the presence of liver (HR: 4.56; 95% CI: 2.482–8.374; P < 0.001), lung (HR: 4.35; 95% CI: 2.460–7.696; P < 0.001), and bone metastases (HR: 4.00; 95% CI: 1.842–8.679; P < 0.001). In contrast, primary surgery (HR: 0.30; 95% CI: 0.154–0.578; P < 0.001) and lymph node dissection (HR: 0.56; 95% CI: 0.317–0.980; P ═ 0.042) were associated with improved PFS. Examination of laboratory variables revealed that SINI >159.3 was a significant risk factor for disease progression (HR: 2.54; 95% CI: 1.132–5.714; P ═ 0.024). Elevated CA-125 levels were also associated with an increased risk of progression (HR: 1.03; 95% CI: 1.003–1.066; P ═ 0.031), while other laboratory markers did not show a significant relationship (Table 3).

In an exploratory cut-off based multivariate Cox regression analysis, SINI >159.3 remained independently associated with disease progression (HR: 2.490; 95% CI: 1.101–5.628; P ═ 0.028). Additionally, the presence of liver metastasis (HR: 2.368; 95% CI: 1.167–4.805; P ═ 0.017) and lung metastasis (HR: 3.919; 95% CI: 2.040–7.527; P < 0.001) continued to be significantly associated with shorter PFS. Other variables, including CA-125, did not retain statistical significance after adjustment in this model (Table 3).

Comparative model performance of SINI, SII, and NLR

In multivariable Cox regression analysis using continuous variables, both SINI (P ═ 0.002) and SII (P ═ 0.017) demonstrated significant associations with survival outcomes, whereas the NLR did not achieve statistical significance (P ═ 0.473). Regarding model performance, SINI exhibited the lowest −2 log likelihood and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) values (380.075 and 390.075, respectively), indicating a superior model fit compared to SII (385.950 and 395.950) and NLR (389.775 and 399.775). Consistently, SINI also yielded the highest C-index (0.652), outperforming SII (0.640) and NLR (0.633), suggesting modest differences in discriminative ability. Bootstrap calibration analysis confirmed generally acceptable agreement between predicted and observed 3-year OS probabilities for SINI, although some deviation was noted in the lower risk range (Figure S1).

Discussion

Endometrial cancer is a clinically heterogeneous disease, necessitating the identification of additional biomarkers beyond conventional models for accurate prognostic assessment. Li et al. conducted a large retrospective study of 1,483 patients, demonstrating that a preoperative CA125 level ≥35 U/mL independently correlated with diminished progression-free and overall survival. This finding led to its integration into a prognostic nomogram alongside inflammatory and nutritional markers. Furthermore, the study highlighted CA125’s routine application in monitoring disease progression and treatment response, underscoring its clinical utility as a biomarker [12].

Among various inflammation-based indices, the SII has garnered significant attention across multiple endometrial cancer cohorts. Matsubara et al.’s study of 442 patients revealed that elevated pre-treatment SII independently correlated with shorter progression-free and overall survival. Their analysis, based on ROC curves, indicated that SII exhibited superior predictive performance compared to the NLR and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) [13]. A subsequent meta-analysis corroborated these findings, reporting a significant association between elevated SII and poorer overall and progression-free survival in endometrial cancer patients [14]. Additionally, an SII ≥494.26 has been identified as an independent predictor of suboptimal response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in endometrial cancer and has been incorporated into prognostic models for survival estimation [15, 16].

Concurrently, serum albumin has been specifically investigated as a prognostic biomarker in endometrial cancer. A cohort study of 337 patients revealed that pre-treatment serum albumin levels were independently associated with both disease-free and progression-free survival, exhibiting an inverse correlation with FIGO stage and tumor grade [17]. These findings are further supported by studies involving larger populations and composite indices integrating nutritional and inflammatory parameters. A meta-analysis encompassing 9,428 patients with gynecological cancers demonstrated that a low prognostic nutritional index (PNI) was associated with adverse survival outcomes [18]. Similarly, a high C-reactive protein (CRP)/albumin ratio correlated with poorer survival, disease progression, and advanced-stage disease [19]. Collectively, these data underscore albumin’s role as a marker reflecting both nutritional status and systemic inflammation.

In the current study, we developed the SINI, which integrates platelet count, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and serum albumin levels. Each of these components has previously demonstrated an association with survival outcomes in endometrial cancer. Our cohort analysis indicated that higher SINI values were associated with poorer survival, suggesting that the synergistic combination of inflammatory and nutritional parameters into a single index may offer enhanced prognostic utility in this clinical context.

Limitations

This study acknowledges several limitations. First, its retrospective design inherently introduces potential selection bias and unmeasured confounding factors, such as active infection, chronic inflammatory disease, liver disease, hematologic disorders, or steroid use. Furthermore, molecular and immunohistochemical data were not consistently available throughout the study period due to the retrospective nature of the design. Second, the relatively limited sample size may have reduced statistical power, potentially restricting the generalizability of the findings, particularly to early-stage endometrial cancer populations. The limited number of events may also have constrained the inclusion of all clinically relevant variables within a single multivariable model. Moreover, the SINI cut-off value was determined using standard ROC analysis and subsequently evaluated within the same cohort, which carries a risk of overfitting and may limit its applicability to external populations. While SINI demonstrated relatively improved model performance compared to SII and NLR, the ROC and C-index analyses indicated only modest discriminative ability. Consequently, the cut-off-based analyses should be interpreted as exploratory, and external validation in larger independent cohorts is warranted. A basic bootstrap-based internal calibration analysis demonstrated generally acceptable agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously due to the limited sample size and absence of external validation. Prospective multicenter studies are imperative to confirm these findings.

Despite these limitations, this study possesses notable strengths. We developed a novel composite index, the SINI, which integrates platelet count, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and serum albumin levels. This index leverages inexpensive and routinely available parameters, enhancing its practicality and accessibility in diverse clinical settings. While individual markers such as NLR, SII, and serum albumin have been previously investigated as prognostic factors in endometrial cancer, these parameters reflect only isolated aspects of host physiology. SINI, conversely, integrates both inflammatory burden and nutritional status into a single composite score, potentially offering a more comprehensive reflection of the systemic host response. In comparative model performance analyses, SINI demonstrated improved model fit compared to SII and NLR, evidenced by lower AIC values and the highest C-index. Although the differences in AIC and C-index values between SINI and existing inflammatory/nutritional markers were modest, SINI may still provide a more integrated assessment of systemic inflammation and nutritional status by combining routinely available parameters within a single composite index.

Conclusion

In conclusion, SINI was associated with overall survival in this retrospective cohort of patients with endometrial cancer. As a composite index integrating inflammatory and nutritional parameters derived from routinely available laboratory variables, SINI may offer prognostic utility in this setting. However, given the retrospective single-center design, modest discriminative performance, and absence of external validation, these findings necessitate cautious interpretation. Prospective multicenter studies are required to confirm these results before SINI can be recommended for routine clinical use. Further research is also needed to investigate whether immune-nutritional indices may have utility in guiding therapeutic strategies.
