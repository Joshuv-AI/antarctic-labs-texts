Multiple small bowel obstructions due to unusual formation of biliary phytobezoars following laparoscopic cholecystectomy in a sickler patient: A case report, an atypical surgical approach

INTRODUCTION

The gastrointestinal tract bezoar formation is relatively a rare condition. They are defined as the accumulation of ingested indigestible materials forming a mass intraluminal of the gastrointestinal tract. Bezoars are named according to their composition [6]: a trichobezoar consists of hair; a lactobezoar is formed from dairy products; a pharmacobezoar is formed from medications [10]. A phytobezoar is the most common type of bezoar [6]. It is composed of indigestible vegetable and fruit residues. Although phytobezoars can be found in any part of the gastrointestinal tract, are commonly reported in patients who had previous gastric surgery and can lead to gastrointestinal obstruction [3,8,12].

Biliary phytobezoars are composed of gallstones mixed with food particles and faeces. Biliary phytobezoar is an extremely rare condition and usually reported in the presence of previous biliary procedures or fistula formation between the intestine and biliary tract [2,6,7].

THE CASE REPORT

This work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [1].

This is a 35-year-old lady, known case of SCA who presented at our emergency department in December 2015. She complained of a vague, generalized abdominal pain for four days. The pain was colicky and associated with repeated attacks of bilish vomiting. She was constipated but able to pass flatus. Her initial assessment showed moderate dehydration, pallor, tachypnoea, and a tinge of jaundice; the pulse rate was 105/min, blood pressure 102/65 mmHg, and normal temperature. The abdomen showed surgical scars of previous operations and was distended with increased bowel sounds. Digital rectal examination showed empty rectum. Her initial blood work showed low haemoglobin 8.00 g/dl and low potassium 3.2 mmol/l with mild elevation of urea and creatinine due to dehydration. There was slight elevation of total bilirubin (considered as her usual rise as a sickler patient). Abdominal x-ray series showed multiple air-fluid levels and dilated small bowel loops with the appearance of multiple round foreign bodies near to the terminal ileum (Fig. 1).Fig. 1The Plain AXRFig. 1

Her history started in 2003, when she had obstructive jaundice due to gallstones. She had ERCP with sphincterotomy, retrieval of the stones and stent insertion. Followed by laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Later, the stent was removed. In May 2005, she developed appendicitis managed by open appendectomy. Six months after the appendectomy, she presented in the emergency room with complete SBO, that needed exploratory laparotomy. A fibrous constricting band was found near the terminal ileum. A 4 cm of small bowel segment was resected, 10 cm away from the ileocecal junction (ICJ). A primary anastomosis was performed, most probably end-to-end hand sewing anastomosis. The pathology reported that the resected portion of the small intestine had infarction and no other significant pathology. But the patient continued to develop several attacks of SBO at different intervals, from March 2006 to June 2015 with several admissions. During these previous admissions, she was managed differently. In two separate admissions, she was managed with conservative observation. In another independent four admissions, laparotomy and laparoscopic exploration were performed for adhesiolysis. Till her last presentation of SBO in December 2015 (the current case reporting).

During current admission, the patient underwent enhanced CT scan of the abdomen which showed dilated small bowel loops extending to the terminal ileum with intraluminal multiple spherical foreign bodies resembling stones (Fig. 2). An urgent exploratory laparotomy was performed. Intra-abdominal adhesions were encountered and released. The spherical foreign bodies were felt intraluminal near the ICJ. Enterotomy was made just proximal to those stones, and all were removed. Some more stones were retrieved by milking the small bowel from the duodenojejunal junction. These stones were easily breakable and grossly appeared like gallstones. Later, laboratory analysis confirmed these stones were gallstones mixed with food particles and faeces, a picture of ‘biliary phytobezoars’.Fig. 2CT abdomen current admissionFig. 2

The CT scan images were reviewed back. These various stones showed as round dense structures with central lucency. This feature is consistent with intraluminal gallstones [9].

Indeed, her previous CT scan images in 2012 (Fig. 3), showed similar round foreign bodies but fewer numbers and were found in different bowel segment. It was reported at that time as possible vegetable seeds and probably not causing obstruction.Fig. 3Previous CT abdomen in 2012Fig. 3

During the current admission, Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was performed that showed no evidence of biliary-enteric fistula (Fig. 4). On the 7th post-laparotomy day, a limited right hemicolectomy was performed with wide side-to-side anastomosis. This decision was made to remove the ICJ and to provide a wide ileocolic anastomosis to prevent any further accumulation of gallstones, as the patient tends to form primary gallstones, in the future, allowing easy passage of the stones along with faeces. Patients with SCD who had previous biliary procedures are prone to develop primary gallstones [13]. The pathological examination of the resected bowel reported that the caecum and ileum showed superficial mucosal ulceration, eosinophilic infiltration, and congested blood vessels in lamina propria. The pericolic fat showed congested blood vessels and foreign body giant cell reaction. There was no evidence of active inflammation, dysplasia, or malignancy. The patient did well postoperatively till she was discharged.

The follow-up in the outpatient clinic was uneventful. Indeed, her last visit was in January 2020, almost more than four years post right hemicolectomy without any episodes of bowel obstruction. Her follow-up CT scan abdomen was done in October 2019, showed clear contrast flow through ileocolic anastomosis, absence of the previously described multiple intraluminal foreign bodies, and no evidence of intestinal obstruction.

CLINICAL DISCUSSION

Gallstones are classified as either primary or secondary. Primary gallstones are originating directly inside the bile duct [13]. Pigmented gallstone formation is a frequent complication in patients with SCA due to chronic haemolysis [11].

However, primary choledocholithiasis is very uncommon [11]. The diagnostic criteria suspicion of primary GS depends on the following: (1) previous cholecystectomy with or without common bile duct exploration or ERCP manipulation; (2) at least two-year asymptomatic period following the initial biliary tract surgery; and (3) no evidence of biliary stricture resulting from prior surgery [13,14]. Choledocholithiasis has been noted in around 18 % of patients with SCD undergoing cholecystectomy [4]. In general population only 5 % develop primary bile duct stones [13]. However, the prevalence in SCD has not been established.

Our patient had LC, ERCP with sphincterotomy two and a half years before the first presentation of small bowel obstruction, in particular after the open appendectomy. Our analysis of the events that the patient developed primary gallstones that passed easily from the bile duct to the intestine, because of the previous sphincterotomy. The presence of open appendectomy facilitated some sort of adhesions around the terminal ileum with possible mild kinking at the ICJ, leading to intraluminal accumulation of these stones. Provided, that the patient underwent small bowel resection near to ileocecal junction in 2005, adding more kinking and adhesion in the terminal ileum area. However, this mild kink or adhesion around the terminal ileum made the faceted pigmented gallstones adhere to the inner wall of the bowel till accumulated.

In the presence of food particles and faeces, the gallstones acted as a nidus for biliary phytobezoars formation that led ultimately to SBO.

The literature described that an enhanced CT scan of the abdomen is the most useful method for diagnosing small-bowel bezoars because it can reveal precise details including location, degree, and features of bezoars [5]. Although in the current case, the CT findings showed a transition zone of intestinal obstruction with the presence of intraluminal multiple dense round foreign bodies with central lucency. A picture consistent with gallstones [9]. The laboratory analysis confirmed these gallstones mixed with food particles and faeces, a description of biliary phytobezoar [7]. Therefore, it is crucially important to check if biliary-enteric communication is present or not. In the current case, the patient had a sphincterotomy and laparoscopic cholecystectomy with no evidence of biliary-enteric fistula as the MRCP showed normal. That sphincterotomy provided an easy passage of these primary gallstones into the gut. The patient had developed adhesions or sort of kinking near to the ileocecal junction due to previous operations. That led to the accumulation of these primary gallstones and subsequent biliary phytobezoars development with small bowel obstructions.

After obtaining informed surgical consent, the limited right hemicolectomy intended to remove the ileocecal junction and to provide a wide side-to-side ileocolic anastomosis to facilitate an easy passage of any future gallstones along with faeces. In the literature, few reported cases of biliary phytobezoar, and all are associated with biliary-enteric communication. Our current case is considered the first case with an unusual course of intestinal obstructions, due to biliary phytobezoar formation due to primary gallstones without biliary-enteric fistula.

CONCLUSION

Patients with SCD require multidisciplinary approach. Sicklers presenting with surgical conditions should have individually tailored management. If they have asymptomatic gallstones should go for early laparoscopic cholecystectomy before complications begin. Because their complications carry significant morbidity. The advances in medical assessment and surgical technology lead to a better awareness of the clinical pathophysiology associated with SCD, providing an improved outcome for these patients.
