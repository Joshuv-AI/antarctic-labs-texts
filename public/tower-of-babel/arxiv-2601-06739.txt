Probabilities of random monomial ideals associated to large graphs
Daniel Munoz George, Humberto Muñoz-George, Kevin Muñoz George

Abstract
. Inspired by the Erdős–Rényi model, we propose a new model for freesquare random monomial ideals generated by edges and covers of a graph. This permit us to investigate the conditions of normality for which we obtain asymptotic results. We also elaborate on asymptotic results for other invariants such as the Krull dimension (for which we obtain threshold function), the regularity and the v v -number.

1.
Introduction

Sometimes in mathematics, a way to understand a complex object is to use randomness as a tool to study the average behavior of the object. There is already an extensive literature in probability which has proved to be effective for the study of complicated objects. To mention a few examples, the probabilistic method has been applied to large structures
[
1
,
6
]
and the study of large random matrices to understand its eigenvalue distribution
[
30
,
24
,
22
,
20
,
19
,
7
,
3
]
. In algebra, probability has also been used to study objects such as the study of random walks in random groups
[
16
,
5
,
32
]
and the study of random permutations
[
21
,
2
]
. In combinatorics, probability has been used to study large random graphs
[
13
,
12
]
. Finally, there is also smaller body of literature on the study of random monomial ideals
[
9
,
25
,
4
,
23
]
.

Monomial ideals constitute one of the most widely studied classes of ideals in commutative algebra, not only because of their intrinsic algebraic richness, but also due to their deep and fruitful connections with combinatorics, geometry, and optimization. The study of algebraic invariants through combinatorial data has become a central theme. In particular, classical references such as the books by Herzog–Hibi
[
17
]
and R. H. Villarreal
[
28
]
highlight how invariants like depth, dimension, regularity, and normality can often be interpreted in purely combinatorial or polyhedral terms when dealing with monomial ideals.

Within this framework, there are two important types of monomial ideals associated to a given graph (or, more generally, to a clutter):
edge ideals
and
cover ideals
. These arise as two fundamental constructions linking squarefree monomial ideals with graph theory. Given a simple graph, its edge ideal is generated by all squarefree monomials of degree two corresponding to the edges of the graph, while the cover ideal is generated by monomials corresponding to its minimal vertex covers. These ideals form two dual and deeply interconnected classes that have been studied extensively, as they explicitly reflect the combinatorial structure of the underlying graph. This perspective has produced a rich body of literature relating algebraic properties such as normality, unmixedness, regularity, and the behavior of symbolic and ordinary powers to classical graph-theoretic invariants. There is also a growing body of literature on the general theory of monomial ideals and, in particular, on edge and cover ideals
[
11
,
17
,
28
]
.

Inspired in the work of De Loera et al.
[
9
]
and the Erdős–Rényi model, we propose a new model for random monomial ideals generated by the edges and the covers of a graph. In the former case, we obtain ideals generated by monomials of degree two while in the latter case the generators can have arbitrary degree. In particular, in the former setting we are able to study the normality of the ideal. It is important to note that this has not been done previously. This is likely due to the absence of general normality criteria for arbitrary monomial ideals; however, in the setting of squarefree monomial ideals generated by the edges of a graph, normality criteria has been extensively studied
[
10
,
15
]
. In addition to normality normality, we investigate the Krull dimension of the ideal. In our model, the Krull dimension coincides with the independence number of the graph. The independence number of a graph is the size of the largest independent set of vertices, that is, vertices that are disconnected. Thanks to the previous fact, our algebraic problem becomes a problem in random graphs. We determine threshold function for the property of an Erdős–Rényi graph having the empty graph with
t
vertices as an induced subgraph. This allow us to find a threshold function for the property of the Krull dimension being larger or equal than
t
. Here
t
is an arbitrary positive integer. Finally we use our main theorems to deduce asymptotic results on the regularity and the
v
-number of the ideal.

2.
Model and results

Let us first introduce our model. Let
K
be a field and let
S=K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be the polynomial ring in
n
variables. We consider the random graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
whose vertices are indexed by the variables
V=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
and each edge
\{x_{i},x_{j}\}
is chosen with probability
p
for
i\neq j
. Each realization
G=(V,E)
of the random graph
\mathcal{G}
induces two ideals;
I(G)
and
I_{c}(G)
, called the edge ideal and the ideal of covers respectively which are defined as follows.

(1)

I(G)
is a squarefree monomial ideal generated by the monomials
x_{i}x_{j}
for each
\{x_{i},x_{j}\}\in E
.

(2)

I_{c}(G)
is a squarefree monomial ideal generated by the monomials
x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{r}}
where
\{x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{r}}\}
is a minimal vertex cover of the graph.

A more detailed definition of
I(G)
and
I_{c}(G)
with examples is left to definition
3.1
.

With the previous definitions the ideals
I(\mathcal{G})
and
I_{c}(\mathcal{G})
are random ideals of
S
. As our model is inspired by the Erdős–Rényi model we say the ideals have Erdős–Rényi distribution which we denote by
I(\mathcal{G})\sim IER(n,p)
and
I_{c}(\mathcal{G})\sim IER_{c}(n,p)
.

Note that if
B\subset S
is a subset of monomial ideals of degree two, say
B=\{x_{i_{k}}x_{i_{l}}:1\leq i_{k},i_{l}\leq n\}
then

\mathbb{P}(I(\mathcal{G})=(B))=p^{|B|}(1-p)^{\binom{n}{2}-|B|},

where
(B)
denotes the ideal generated by
B
.

2.1.
results

Our results describe how the probability of a property varies depending on the parameters
p
and
q=:1-p
.

(A) Normality.
Let
R
be a ring and let
I
be an ideal of
R
, an element
z\in R
is
integral
over
I
if
z
satisfies an equation

\displaystyle z^{l}+a_{1}z^{l-1}+...+a_{l-1}z+a_{l}=0,

\displaystyle a_{i}\in I^{i}.

The
integral closure
of
I
is the set of all elements
z\in R
which are integral over
I
. This set will be denoted by
\overline{I}

Definition 2.1
.

If
I=\overline{I}
,
I
is said to be
integrally closed
or
complete
.

Definition 2.2
.

If
I^{n}
is integrally closed for all
n\geq 1
,
I
is said to be
normal
.

Our first result provides asymptotic probabilities for the properties
I(\mathcal{G})
being normal,
I_{c}(\mathcal{G})
being normal and
I_{c}(\mathcal{G})\text{ not being normal}\cap\beta_{0}(G)\leq 2
. Here
\beta_{0}(G)
denotes the independence number of the graph
G
as defined in Section
3
. We prove under suitable conditions on the parameters
p
and
q
the events (or their complements) hold asymptotically almost surely.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
I(\mathcal{G})
be a random monomial ideal with Erdős–Rényi distrution, i.e.,
I(\mathcal{G})\sim IER(n,p)
. Then

(1)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(I(\mathcal{G})\text{ is normal})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcc}1&\text{if}&p=o(\frac{1}{n})\\
\\
0&\text{if}&pq^{3/2}=\omega(\frac{1}{n})\end{array}\right.

Remark 2.4
.

Observe that
pq^{3/2}\leq p
. If
p<<\frac{1}{n}
then
I(\mathcal{G})
is normal asymptotically almost surely. If
\frac{1}{n}<<pq^{3/2}
then
I(\mathcal{G})
is not normal asymptotically almost surely. It remains to understand the behavior of the event

\{I(\mathcal{G})\text{ is normal}\}

for
pq^{3/2}<<\frac{1}{n}<<p
.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
I_{c}(\mathcal{G})
be a random monomial ideal with Erdős–Rényi distribution, i.e.,
I_{c}(\mathcal{G})\sim IER_{c}(n,p)
. If
p=o(\frac{1}{n})
then

(2)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(I_{c}(\mathcal{G})\text{ is normal})=1

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
I_{c}(\mathcal{G})
be a random monomial ideal with Erdős–Rényi distribution, i.e.,
I_{c}(\mathcal{G})\sim IER_{c}(n,p)
. If
q=o(\frac{1}{n})
then

(3)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(I_{c}(\mathcal{G})\text{ is not normal}\cap\beta_{0}(\mathcal{G})\leq 2)=0.

Remark 2.7
.

The reason we intersect with the event
\beta_{0}(\mathcal{G})\leq 2
in Theorem
2.6
is that for ideals of covers if the independence number of the graph is less than or equal to 2 then the following sentences are equivalent;

(1)

The ideal is not normal.

(2)

The complement of the graph admits a Hochster configuration.

With the help of this equivalence we can proceed similarly to the proof of Theorem
2.3
. We leave the details to Section
5
.

(B) Krull dimension.
Let us recall that the Krull dimension of a monomial ideal
I
is define as
\text{dim}(S/I)
. In the setting of monomial ideals generated by edges of a graph,
G
, the Krull dimension coincides with the independence number of the graph
\beta_{0}(G)
. Roughly speaking, the independence number is the size of the largest independent set of the graph. For our second result, we obtain a threshold function for the independence number of the graph being larger or equal than
t
for every fixed integer
t
. As a corollary we prove that under suitable conditions on the parameter
q
the Krull dimension equals
t
asymptotically almost surely.

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
I(\mathcal{G})
be a random monomial ideal with Erdős–Rényi distribution and let
t>1
be a positive integer. Then
\frac{1}{n^{2/(t-1)}}
is a threshold function for
q
for the property
\text{dim}(S/I(\mathcal{G}))\geq t
. In other words

(4)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(\text{dim}(S/I(\mathcal{G}))\geq t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcc}0&\text{if}&q=o(\frac{1}{n^{2/(t-1)}})\\
\\
1&\text{if}&q=\omega(\frac{1}{n^{2/(t-1)}})\end{array}\right.

Corollary 2.9
.

Let
I(\mathcal{G})
be a random monomial ideal with Erdős–Rényi distribution and let
t>1
be a positive integer. If
q=o(\frac{1}{n^{2/t}})
and
q=\omega(\frac{1}{n^{2/(t-1)}})
then
\text{dim}(S/I(\mathcal{G}))
equals
t
asymptotically almost surely. In other words

(5)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(\text{dim}(S/I(\mathcal{G}))=t)=1.

(C) Regularity.
Let
I\subset S
be a graded ideal and let
{\mathbf{F}}
be the minimal graded free resolution of
S/I
as an

{\mathbf{F}}:\ \ \ 0\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{j}S(-j)^{b_{g,j}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varphi_{g}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\cdots\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{j}S(-j)^{b_{1,j}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varphi_{1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}S\longrightarrow S/I\longrightarrow 0.

The

By a result of R. Villarreal and Delio

(
[

If

For our third result we prove for any

Corollary 2.11
.

Let
I(\mathcal{G})
be a random monomial ideal with Erdős–Rényi distribution and let

2.2.
proof strategy.

Let us describe our proof techniques. In Theorem
2.3
we use the equality of events

\{\mathcal{G}\text{ is not normal}\}=\{\mathcal{G}\text{ has a Hochster configuration}\}.

Roughly speaking a Hochster configuration consist of two cycles of odd size whose vertices have no edges in between. We then focus our attention into the probability of the second event for which we obtain upper and lower bounds using the following inclusion of events.

\{\mathcal{G}\text{ has }T\text{ as induced subgraph}\}\subset\{\mathcal{G}\text{ has a Hochster configuration}\}\\
\subset\{\mathcal{G}\text{ has a cycle}\}.

Here
T
is the smallest Hochster configuration consisting on two disconnected triangles. To prove our limit theorems we are reduced to prove

\mathbb{P}(\{\mathcal{G}\text{ has }T\text{ as induced subgraph}\})\rightarrow 1,

provided
pq^{3/2}=\omega(\frac{1}{n})
and

\mathbb{P}(\{\mathcal{G}\text{ has a cycle}\})\rightarrow 0,

provided
p=o(\frac{1}{n})
. The second limit is well know however we provide a proof (Proposition
4.9
) for completeness. The first limit can be computed providing lower bounds for
\mathbb{P}(\{\mathcal{G}\text{ has }T\text{ as induced subgraph}\})
. The lower bound we provide is of the form

\mathbb{P}(\{\mathcal{G}\text{ has }T\text{ as induced subgraph}\})\geq 1-\frac{Var(Y_{T}(n,p))}{\mathbb{E}(Y_{T}(n,p))^{2}},

where
Y_{T}(n,p)
is the random variable that counts how many times
T
appears as an induced subgraph of
\mathcal{G}
. We compute upper bounds for the variance of
Y_{T}(n,p)
from where we are able to provide an explicit lower bound for
\mathbb{P}(\{\mathcal{G}\text{ has }T\text{ as induced subgraph}\})
which we prove converges to
1
as
n\rightarrow\infty
provided
pq^{3/2}=\omega(\frac{1}{n})
.

To prove Theorem
2.5
we use the inclusion of events

\{\mathcal{G}\text{ has no cycles}\}\subset\{\mathcal{G}\text{ is bipartite}\}\subset\{I_{c}(\mathcal{G})\text{ is normal}\}

where we use that the vertex cover ideals of bipartite graphs are normal because they are perfect graphs (see for instance
[
29
]
). The limit of the probability of the event on the left is
1
provided
p=o(\frac{1}{n})
(Proposition
4.9
) which forces the probability of the event on the right to converge to
1
.
To prove Theorem
2.6
we use Duality criterion
[
10
, Theorem 5.11]
which give the inclusion of sets

\{I_{c}(\mathcal{G})\text{ is not normal},\beta_{0}(\mathcal{G})\leq 2\}\\
\subset\{\bar{\mathcal{G}}\text{ has a Hochster configuration}\}\subset\{\bar{\mathcal{G}}\text{ has a cycle}\}.

Here
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
is the complement of the graph
\mathcal{G}
defined as the graph such that
\mathcal{G}\cup\bar{\mathcal{G}}
is the complete graph. Then we are reduced to prove
\mathbb{P}(\bar{\mathcal{G}}\text{ has a cycle})\rightarrow 0
which follows as in Theorem
2.3
.

To prove Theorem
2.8
we use the equality of events

\{dim(S/I(\mathcal{G}))\geq t\}=\{\mathcal{G}\text{ has }E_{t}\text{ as induced subgraph}\}.

Here
E_{t}
is the empty graph (no edges) with
t
vertices. The rest of the proof consists in proving that
q=\frac{1}{n^{2/(t-1)}}
is a threshold function for the property
\mathcal{G}
has
E_{t}
as induced subgraph. To prove this it is enough to upper and lower bound the probability of the event

\{\mathcal{G}\text{ has }E_{t}\text{ as an induced subgraph}\}.

This event is the same as
\{Y_{E_{t}}(n,p)>0\}
whose probability is upper bounded by
\mathbb{E}(Y_{E_{t}}(n,p))
and lower bounded by

1-\frac{Var_{E_{t}}(n,p)}{\mathbb{E}(Y_{E_{t}}(n,p))^{2}}.

Here
Y_{E_{t}}(n,p)
is the random variable that counts how many times
E_{t}
appears as induced subgraph of
\mathcal{G}
. We find explicit expression for the expectation and bounds for the variance from where we conclude that these bounds converge to
0
and
1
respectively if
q=o(\frac{1}{n^{2/(t-1)}})
and
q=\omega(\frac{1}{n^{2/(t-1)}})
respectively.

Finally Corollaries
2.9
,
2.11
and
2.13
follow as a consequence of Theorem
2.8
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\geq 6
,
0\leq p\leq 1
and
\mathcal{G}\sim ER(n,p)
. Let
T
be the graph defined in notation
4.1
. Then

(6)

\mathbb{E}(Y_{T}(n,p))=\binom{n}{6}\binom{6}{3}p^{6}(1-p)^{9}

and,

(7)

Var(Y_{T}(n,p))\leq n^{10}p^{11}q^{18}+n^{10}p^{12}q^{17}+n^{9}p^{11}q^{16}+n^{9}p^{9}q^{18}\\
+n^{8}p^{10}q^{14}+n^{8}p^{9}q^{15}+n^{7}p^{8}q^{12}+n^{6}p^{6}q^{9}

Proof.

We have

\mathbb{E}(Y_{T}(n,p))=\sum_{V\subset[n]}\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathcal{G}_{V}=T\}})=\sum_{V\subset[n]}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}_{V}=T).

This probability is non zero only when
|V|=6
, further in that case the probability is given by
\binom{6}{3}p^{6}q^{9}
as we choose first three vertices that will conform one of the triangles of
T
and then we multiply by the probability of having
6
edges (corresponding to the two triangles of
T
) and the probability of all the other possible edges being empty. Finally there are
\binom{n}{6}
ways of choosing
V
, this proves (
6
).
Now we prove (
7
). Observe that

\displaystyle Var(Y_{T}(n,p))=\mathbb{E}(Y_{T}(n,p)^{2})-\mathbb{E}(Y_{T}(n,p))^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{V\subset[n]}\sum_{W\subset[n]}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathcal{G}_{V}=T\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathcal{G}_{W}=T\}}\right]-\sum_{V\subset[n]}\sum_{W\subset[n]}\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathcal{G}_{V}=T\}})\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathcal{G}_{W}=T\}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{V\subset[n]}\sum_{W\subset[n]}\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathcal{G}_{V}=T\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathcal{G}_{W}=T\}})-\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathcal{G}_{V}=T\}})\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathcal{G}_{W}=T\}})

Observe that when
V
and
W
have at most one vertex in common then
\{\mathcal{G}_{V}=T\}
and
\{\mathcal{G}_{W}=T\}
are independent events and therefore

\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathcal{G}_{V}=T\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathcal{G}_{W}=T\}})-\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathcal{G}_{V}=T\}})\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathcal{G}_{W}=T\}})=0.

Thus

(8)

\displaystyle Var(Y_{T}(n,p))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V,W\subset[n]\\
|V\cap W|\geq 2\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathcal{G}_{V}=T\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathcal{G}_{W}=T\}})-\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathcal{G}_{V}=T\}})\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathcal{G}_{W}=T\}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V,W\subset[n]\\
|V\cap W|\geq 2\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathcal{G}_{V}=T\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathcal{G}_{W}=T\}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V,W\subset[n]\\
|V\cap W|\geq 2\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}_{V}=T,\mathcal{G}_{W}=T)

We now compute the probabilities
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}_{V}=T,\mathcal{G}_{W}=T)
for
|V\cap W|=1,\dots,6
. If
|V\cap W|=2
there are two possible scenarios, either the shared vertices have an edge or they are disconnected. In the first scenario we claim the probability is
16p^{11}q^{18}
. Indeed, we first choose one vertex from
V
that will conform a triangle with the two shared edges (see Figure
6
). This can be done in
4
ways, the same for choosing the ones in
W
. From here we get
16
. Secondly, there are
3
possible types of pair of vertices; the ones that must be connected, the ones that must be disconnected and the ones that can be either connected or disconnected. There are
11
edges in
V\cup W
which must be in the graph from where we get
p^{11}
, this correspond to the pair of vertices that must be connected (see black edges in Figure
6
). We require
\mathcal{G}_{V}=T
therefore
9
edges must be empty, these correspond to the pair of vertices that must be disconnected (see the green edge in Figure
6
for an example). In the same way in
\mathcal{G}_{W}
there must be
9
empty edges, from where we get
q^{18}
. Finally there are pair of vertices that might be connected or disconnected and in both cases the event
\{\mathcal{G}_{V}=T,\mathcal{G}_{W}=T\}
holds (these correspond to edges as the one in blue in Figure
6
). So we multiply by
1
corresponding to
p+q
. This gives a total probability of
16p^{11}q^{18}
.

V
(grey and red vertices) and

(blue and red vertices). In red the shared vertices of

and

.

In the second scenario we proceed similarly, now the probability is given by
36p^{12}q^{17}
. Observe that in this case we obtain
q^{17}
instead of
q^{18}
because the two shared vertices must be disconnected in both graphs (
\mathcal{G}_{V}
and
\mathcal{G}_{W}
), therefore the total number of edges that must be empty in the graph is
9+9-1
. Finally observe that the number of ways of choosing
V
and
W
is given by

\binom{n}{6}\binom{6}{2}\binom{n-6}{4}.

We conclude that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V,W\subset[n]\\
|V\cap W|=2\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}_{V}=T,\mathcal{G}_{W}=T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{n}{6}\binom{6}{2}\binom{n-6}{4}(16p^{11}q^{18}+36p^{12}q^{17})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle n^{10}p^{11}q^{18}+n^{10}p^{12}q^{17}.

We proceed similarly for the cases
|V\cap W|=2,\dots,6
for which we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V,W\subset[n]\\
|V\cap W|=3\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}_{V}=T,\mathcal{G}_{W}=T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{n}{6}\binom{6}{3}\binom{n-6}{3}(27p^{11}q^{18}+p^{9}q^{18})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle n^{9}p^{11}q^{16}+n^{9}p^{9}q^{18},

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V,W\subset[n]\\
|V\cap W|=4\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}_{V}=T,\mathcal{G}_{W}=T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{n}{6}\binom{6}{4}\binom{n-6}{2}(24p^{10}q^{14}+4p^{9}q^{15})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle n^{8}p^{10}q^{14}+n^{8}p^{9}q^{15},

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V,W\subset[n]\\
|V\cap W|=5\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}_{V}=T,\mathcal{G}_{W}=T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{n}{6}\binom{6}{5}\binom{n-6}{1}(10p^{8}q^{12})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle n^{7}p^{8}q^{12},

and

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V,W\subset[n]\\
|V\cap W|=6\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}_{V}=T,\mathcal{G}_{W}=T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{n}{6}(20p^{6}q^{9})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle n^{6}p^{6}q^{9}.

Combining these inequalities with Inequality (

) concludes the proof.

Let

n\geq 6
,

+n^{9}p^{9}q^{18}+n^{8}p^{10}q^{14}+n^{8}p^{9}q^{15}+n^{7}p^{8}q^{12}+n^{6}p^{6}q^{9})

Note that if
\mathbb{E}(Y_{T}(n,p))>|Y_{T}(n,p)-\mathbb{E}(Y_{T}(n,p))|

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y_{T}(n,p)>0)

\displaystyle 1-\mathbb{P}(|Y_{T}(n,p)-\mathbb{E}(Y_{T}(n,p))|\geq\mathbb{E}(Y_{T}(n,p)))

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 1-\frac{Var(Y_{T}(n,p))}{\mathbb{E}(Y_{T}(n,p))^{2}}.

Where the last inequality follows from Chebyshev inequality. We conclude using Equation (

) and Inequality (

).

To conclude this section we prove that if
p=o(\frac{1}{n})
then
\mathcal{G}\sim ER(n,p)
has no cycles asymptotically almost surely.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
0\leq p\leq 1
and
\mathcal{G}\sim ER(n,p)
. If
p=o(\frac{1}{n})
then

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(\{\mathcal{G}\text{ has a cycle}\})=0.

Proof.

Let
X_{n}
the random variable that counts the number of cycles of
\mathcal{G}
. Hence

\mathbb{P}(\{\mathcal{G}\text{ has a cycle}\})=\mathbb{P}(X_{n}>0)\leq\mathbb{E}(X_{n}),

where last inequality follows from Markov’s inequality. So it is enough to prove
\mathbb{E}(X_{n})\rightarrow 0
. Let
S_{k}\subset[n]
be the subsets of
[n]
of size
k
, then

X_{n}=\sum_{k=3}^{n}\sum_{S\subset S_{k}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mathcal{G}_{S_{k}}\text{ is a cycle of length }k\}}.

Therefore

\mathbb{E}(X_{n})=\sum_{k=3}^{n}\sum_{S\subset S_{k}}\mathbb{P}({\{\mathcal{G}_{S_{k}}\text{ is a cycle of length }k\}}).

For a fixed
S\subset S_{k}
observe that there are at most
\binom{n}{k}k!
cycles as this counts the number of ordered set of vertices and each ordered set of vertices induces a cycle by joining adjacent vertices. It might be possible we are over-counting cycles, in either case the number of cycles is upper bounded by
\binom{n}{k}k!
. Given an ordered set of vertices the probability of having a cycle is
p^{k}
, hence

\mathbb{E}(X_{n})\leq\sum_{k=3}^{n}\binom{n}{k}k!p^{k}\leq\sum_{k\geq 3}n^{k}p^{k}=(np)^{3}\sum_{k\geq 0}(np)^{k}=\frac{(np)^{3}}{1-np}.

Since the right hand side converges to
0
the proof is completed.
∎

This Section is devoted to provided the proof of all of our results. Let us remind that our idea is finding upper and lower bounds for the desired probabilities with the help of Section
4
.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

From
[
18
, Theorem 5.8]
we have the following equality of events

\{I(\mathcal{G})\text{ is not normal}\}=\{\mathcal{G}\text{ has a Hochster configuration}\}.

Firstly, we prove that if
p=o(\frac{1}{n})
then

\mathbb{P}(\{\mathcal{G}\text{ has a Hochster configuration}\})\rightarrow 0.

This follows easily from the fact that

0\leq\mathbb{P}(\{\mathcal{G}\text{ has a Hochster configuration}\})\leq\mathbb{P}(\{\mathcal{G}\text{ has a cycle}\})

where the right hand side converges to
0
because of Proposition
4.9
.
Secondly, we prove that if
pq^{3/2}=\omega(\frac{1}{n})
then

\mathbb{P}(\{\mathcal{G}\text{ has a Hochster configuration}\})\rightarrow 1.

Indeed

(14)

1\geq\mathbb{P}(\{\mathcal{G}\text{ has a Hochster configuration}\})\geq\mathbb{P}(Y_{T}(n,p)>0),

where the last inequality follows because
T
is a Hochster configuration of the graph. From Corollary
4.4
we know

\mathbb{P}(Y_{T}(n,p)>0)\geq 1-\frac{1}{400\binom{n}{6}^{2}p^{12}q^{18}}(n^{10}p^{11}q^{18}+n^{10}p^{12}q^{17}+n^{9}p^{11}q^{16}\\
+n^{9}p^{9}q^{18}+n^{8}p^{10}q^{14}+n^{8}p^{9}q^{15}+n^{7}p^{8}q^{12}+n^{6}p^{6}q^{9})

Using that
\binom{n}{6}\leq\frac{n^{6}}{6!}
and previous inequality yields

q

Proof of Theorem
2.8
.

Let us remind that dim
(S/I(\mathcal{G}))
coindices with the independence number of the graph, this means that the following events are the same

\{dim(S/I(\mathcal{G}))\geq t\}=\{\mathcal{G}\text{ contains }E_{t}\text{ as a subgraph}\},

where
E_{t}
is defined as in notation
4.1
. Thus

(16)

\mathbb{P}(dim(S/I(\mathcal{G}))\geq t)=\mathbb{P}(Y_{E_{t}}(n,p)>0).

So we are reduced to finding upper and lower bounds for
\mathbb{P}(Y_{E_{t}}(n,p)>0)
. An upper bound can be found using Markov’s inequality and Equation (
10
), namely

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y_{E_{t}}(n,p)>0)\leq\mathbb{E}(Y_{E_{t}}(n,p))=\binom{n}{t}(1-p)^{\binom{t}{2}}.

For fixed
t
,
\binom{n}{t}\leq n^{t}/t!\leq n^{t}
therefore

\mathbb{P}(Y_{E_{t}}(n,p)>0)\leq n^{t}(1-p)^{\binom{t}{2}}=[n^{2/(t-1)}(1-p)]^{\frac{t(t-1)}{2}}\rightarrow 0,\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty

as long as
n^{2/(t-1)}(1-p)
converges to
0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Combining this fact with Equation (
16
) yields

\mathbb{P}(dim(S/I(\mathcal{G})\geq t)\rightarrow 0,\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty,

provided
q=o(\frac{1}{n^{2/(t-1)}})
. Now we provide a lower bound using Corollary
4.7

(17)

\mathbb{P}(Y_{E_{t}}(n,p)>0)\geq 1-\frac{n^{2t}}{\binom{n}{t}^{2}}\sum_{j=2}^{t}\frac{1}{n^{j}(1-p)^{\binom{j}{2}}}.

Observe that for
2\leq j\leq t
,

n^{j}q^{\binom{j}{2}}=[n^{2/(j-1)}q]^{\binom{j}{2}}\geq[n^{2/(t-1)}q]^{\binom{j}{2}}\rightarrow\infty,\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty,

provided
n^{2/(t-1)}q
diverges as
n\rightarrow\infty
. This means that each of the terms in the sum of inequality (
17
) approaches to
0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
provided
q=\omega(\frac{1}{n^{2/(t-1)}})
. Further the term
\frac{n^{2t}}{\binom{n}{t}^{2}}
converges to a constant (depending on
t)
, hence from (
17
) and (
16
) we conclude that

\mathbb{P}(dim(S/I(\mathcal{G}))\geq t)\rightarrow 1,\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty,

provided
q=\omega(\frac{1}{n^{2/(t-1)}})
. This concludes the proof.
∎
