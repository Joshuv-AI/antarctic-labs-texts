PD‐L1 Tumour‐Cell/Immune‐Cell Phenotype, Early Nodal Status and Serum Biomarkers in Locally Advanced Head and Neck Squamous Cell Carcinoma Receiving Neoadjuvant Therapy: A Retrospective Cohort Study With Exploratory Survival Analysis
Authors: Ningning Wang, Zehui Yun, Fangli Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPD‐L1 tumour‐cell (TC) and immune‐cell (IC) expression are scored separately in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) but are often collapsed into a combined positive score, potentially obscuring distinct patterns. In this retrospective, single‐centre cohort of 61 patients with locally advanced, hypopharynx‐predominant HNSCC receiving neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy (n = 46) or chemotherapy alone (n = 15), we scored PD‐L1 TC% and IC% separately on pretreatment biopsies, defined four TC/IC phenotypes, classified patients by combined PD‐L1/early lymph node metastasis (ELNM) profile and related these to serum biomarkers, neoadjuvant response and survival. TC% and IC% were not correlated (Spearman ρ = −0.13, p = 0.40), supporting their biological independence; among 46 patients with both scores the phenotypes were cold (TC−/IC−, n = 10), TC‐intrinsic (n = 10), IC‐dominant (n = 12) and hot (TC+/IC+, n = 14). The overall objective response rate after two neoadjuvant cycles was 91.4% (53/58 evaluable), with no progressive disease and did not differ significantly across PD‐L1/ELNM profiles (p = 0.15) or between chemoimmunotherapy and chemotherapy alone (95% vs. 79%; p = 0.085). Serum IL‐6 showed a non‐significant inverse association with IC% (ρ = −0.33, p = 0.10) and was numerically lowest in the hot phenotype; serum tumour markers did not differ by ELNM status. Over a median follow‐up of 24.0 months there were three deaths and nine progression events; in exploratory, underpowered analyses neither progression‐free nor overall survival differed by PD‐L1/ELNM profile (PFS log‐rank p = 0.40) or treatment modality (p = 0.64), and the double‐positive group did not show the favourable survival suggested by uncontrolled comparisons. These data indicate that PD‐L1 TC% and IC% capture independent information and that the TC/IC phenotype distribution is heterogeneous; with few events the survival analysis is hypothesis‐generating only, and adequately powered prospective studies with longer follow‐up are required.Keywords: early lymph node metastasis, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, hypopharyngeal carcinoma, IL‐6, immune cell, neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, PD‐L1, tumour cell

Introduction

Hypopharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (HPSCC) is among the most aggressive subtypes of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), accounting for approximately 3%–5% of head and neck malignancies [1]. Arising from the pyriform sinus, postcricoid region or posterior pharyngeal wall, it is characterised by a concealed location, vague early symptoms and dense submucosal lymphatic drainage that facilitates early regional dissemination [1, 2, 3]. More than 70% of patients present with locally advanced disease and cervical nodal metastasis [1, 2, 3], and five‐year overall survival remains around 30%–40% despite multimodal treatment [1, 2, 3, 4, 5], making this region one of the sites with greatest unmet need for biomarker‐based stratification within the HNSCC spectrum.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors have reshaped HNSCC management. The PD‐L1/PD‐1 axis is a key checkpoint, and pivotal trials—KEYNOTE‐012, KEYNOTE‐040, KEYNOTE‐048 and CheckMate 141—established that PD‐1 blockade confers survival benefit in recurrent or metastatic disease, with efficacy linked to PD‐L1 expression [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy combining platinum–taxane doublets with PD‐1 inhibitors is increasingly used for locally advanced HNSCC, aiming to improve resectability and pathological response [11, 12]; early data report pathological complete response rates of 15%–30% [12, 13].

PD‐L1 assessment is methodologically complex. Standard assays report tumour‐cell (TC%) and immune‐cell (IC%) expression separately, reflecting distinct biology: TC PD‐L1 is driven predominantly by tumour‐intrinsic oncogenic signalling and adaptive upregulation in response to interferon‐γ, whereas IC PD‐L1 reflects the activation state of the immune infiltrate [14, 15, 16, 17]. The clinical implications of TC/IC discordance are poorly understood [15, 16] and most studies report an aggregate score (combined positive score [CPS] or tumour proportion score) without characterising TC/IC patterns, potentially obscuring relevant heterogeneity [15, 16, 18]. Birtalan et al. [14] reported that PD‐L1 on immune cells, but not tumour cells, was associated with better prognosis in laryngeal, oropharyngeal and hypopharyngeal cancers; Fu et al. [19] and Blazek et al. [20] found that IC PD‐L1 with dense tumour‐infiltrating lymphocytes identified favourable outcomes. These observations suggest IC PD‐L1 may carry information distinct from TC PD‐L1.

Cervical lymph node metastasis at diagnosis is both a staging criterion and a prognostic factor in HPSCC [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 21, 22, 23]. Early lymph node metastasis (ELNM)—nodal disease confirmed at initial diagnosis before treatment—reflects regional invasiveness, and quantitative nodal parameters augment prognostic discrimination beyond conventional N staging [4, 5, 21, 22, 23]. Nodal status, however, has rarely been integrated with characterisation of the tumour immune microenvironment (TIME). Serum interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), which activates JAK–STAT3 signalling, has been implicated in immune evasion and PD‐L1 upregulation [17, 24] and may provide a non‐invasive proxy for an IL‐6/STAT3/PD‐L1 circuit operating within the microenvironment [17, 25].

We therefore conducted a cross‐sectional analysis of a neoadjuvant‐treated HNSCC cohort with four descriptive aims: (1) characterise the distribution of TC/IC PD‐L1 phenotypes and the correlation between TC% and IC%; (2) describe baseline characteristics and short‐term neoadjuvant response across combined PD‐L1/ELNM profiles; (3) explore associations of serum IL‐6 and tumour markers with PD‐L1 compartmentalisation and nodal status; and (4) present, as a hypothesis only, a descriptive TC/IC × ELNM phenotype map for future testing; and (5) report, in an exploratory and underpowered analysis with immature follow‐up, progression‐free and overall survival by PD‐L1/ELNM profile and treatment modality.

Results

Patient Characteristics and PD‐L1/ELNM Profile Distribution

Of 63 consecutive patients reviewed, 2 were excluded for incomplete diagnosis and treatment records, leaving 61 for analysis (Table 1); 46 received neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy and 15 chemotherapy alone. Median age was 61.5 years (IQR 56.0–67.1); 57 (93.4%) were male. The cohort was hypopharynx‐predominant (hypopharynx 46, 75.4%; oropharynx 10, 16.4%; larynx 3, 4.9%; other 2, 3.3%). Most patients had clinical Stages III–IV disease (83.6%); 54.1% were T1–2 and 42.6% T3–4. Nodal status was evaluable in 59 patients, of whom 47 (79.7%) were ELNM+ (clinical N ≥ 1) and 12 ELNM−; N2 was the most common nodal category (55.7%).

PD‐L1 TC% was available in 49/61 patients (median 2.0%, IQR 0.5%–15.0%) and IC% in 47/61 (median 10.0%, IQR 3.0%–20.0%). Among 46 patients with both scores, the four TC/IC phenotypes (TC positivity TC% > 1%; IC positivity IC% ≥ 10%) were distributed as TC−/IC− ‘cold’ (n = 10, 21.7%), TC+/IC− ‘TC‐intrinsic’ (n = 10, 21.7%), IC+/TC− ‘IC‐dominant’ (n = 12, 26.1%) and TC+/IC+ ‘hot’ (n = 14, 30.4%; Figure 1A,B). PD‐L1 TC% and IC% were not correlated (Spearman ρ = −0.13, p = 0.40), supporting biological independence of the two compartments and arguing against using a composite score as the sole PD‐L1 metric in this disease.

Combined PD‐L1/ELNM profiling (PD‐L1 positivity defined as TC% > 1%; n = 48 with both variables) identified 6 double‐negative (PD‐L1−/ELNM−), 21 discordant and 21 double‐positive (PD‐L1+/ELNM+) patients; 13 patients with missing PD‐L1 or N data were excluded from profile comparisons. The double‐negative group, which serves as the reference category for the subsequent hazard ratio estimates, comprised only six patients; effect sizes derived against so small a reference are statistically unstable and prone to overestimation with wide confidence intervals, and all group comparisons involving this category are therefore interpreted with corresponding caution (see Section 3). Baseline characteristics were broadly similar across profiles for demographic variables (age p = 0.40; BMI p = 0.76; smoking p = 0.65; alcohol p = 0.52). By definition, the N‐category distribution differed across profiles (all double‐negative patients were N0; all double‐positive patients were N+), so this difference is a structural feature of the grouping rather than an independent finding. PD‐L1 TC% differed across profiles (p < 0.001) whereas IC% did not (p = 0.86), confirming that the PD‐L1/ELNM grouping captures TC‐driven rather than the IC‐driven biology.

Serum Biomarker Profiles and TC/IC Concordance

Serum IL‐6 was available in 32/61 patients (median 3.6 pg/mL, IQR 2.2–4.9). IL‐6 did not differ significantly across PD‐L1/ELNM profiles (p = 0.56) or by treatment group (chemotherapy 3.1 vs. chemoimmunotherapy 3.7 pg/mL; Mann–Whitney p = 0.69; Figure 2A). Across the four TC/IC phenotypes, median IL‐6 was numerically lowest in the ‘hot’ phenotype (2.7 pg/mL) and higher in the ‘cold’ (4.3) and ‘TC‐intrinsic’ (4.9) phenotypes, but this gradient was not significant (Kruskal–Wallis p = 0.36; Figure 1C). A non‐significant inverse association was observed between serum IL‐6 and PD‐L1 IC% (Spearman ρ = −0.33, p = 0.10, n = 26; Figure 4A). These observations are directionally compatible with an IL‐6/JAK–STAT3‐related influence on PD‐L1 compartmentalisation but are exploratory and underpowered.

Among serum tumour markers, median CEA was 4.36 ng/mL in ELNM− versus 3.09 ng/mL in ELNM+ (p = 0.30), SCC antigen 0.44 versus 1.82 ng/mL (p = 0.058, n = 2 vs. 16) and CYFRA21‐1 3.33 versus 2.12 ng/mL (p = 0.14; Figure 2B); none reached significance, although SCC antigen showed the largest directional difference. Using stricter PD‐L1 thresholds (TC ≥ 5%, IC ≥ 10%; n = 46), patients were categorised as both‐low (n = 12), discordant (n = 25) or both‐high (n = 9), with high ORR in all categories (12/12, 21/23 and 8/9, respectively; Figure 2C). Notably, SCC antigen differed significantly across PD‐L1/ELNM profiles in Table 1 (p = 0.016), driven by higher values in the double‐positive group; given the small SCC subset (n = 18), this is hypothesis‐generating.

Treatment Characteristics and Neoadjuvant Regimens

Treatment characteristics by PD‐L1/ELNM profile are shown in Table 2. Overall, 46/61 (75.4%) received neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, with no significant between‐profile difference (p = 0.72). A platinum–taxane (TP) backbone was used in 52 patients (85.2%) and TPF in 9 (14.8%). Among the 46 immunotherapy‐treated patients, PD‐1 inhibitors used were toripalimab (n = 21, 45.7%), sintilimab (n = 11, 23.9%), pembrolizumab (n = 9, 19.6%) and nivolumab (n = 5, 10.9%), reflecting real‐world practice in China. Because four different PD‐1 inhibitors were administered, the cohort carries pharmacodynamic heterogeneity that a sample of this size cannot control for; all four are anti–PD‐1 monoclonal antibodies with a shared mechanism of action, which partially mitigates but does not eliminate this limitation. Median treatment cycles were 3 (IQR 2–3). Surgery was performed in 34/61 (55.7%) and radiotherapy in 35/61 (57.4%). Maintenance immunotherapy was received by 10 of 18 patients with documented maintenance status. Immune‐related adverse events were documented in 10/33 (30.3%) immunotherapy‐treated patients with complete safety records, consistent with published PD‐1 inhibitor safety data [13].

Short‐Term Neoadjuvant Response

Among 58 evaluable patients, the overall ORR after two cycles was 91.4% (53/58): 7 complete responses (12.1%), 46 partial responses (79.3%), 5 stable disease (8.6%) and no progressive disease. ORR did not differ significantly across PD‐L1/ELNM profiles (double‐negative 6/6, discordant 19/19, double‐positive 18/21; p = 0.15; Table 2), suggesting that short‐term neoadjuvant response is driven primarily by the chemotherapy backbone rather than the baseline TIME phenotype. ORR was numerically higher with chemoimmunotherapy than chemotherapy alone (95% [42/44] vs. 79% [11/14]), but the difference was not significant (Fisher p = 0.085; Figure 3B). In a descriptive subgroup analysis by IC PD‐L1 status × treatment, ORR was 2/3 (IC−, chemotherapy), 16/18 (IC−, chemoimmunotherapy), 5/6 (IC+, chemotherapy) and 18/18 (IC+, chemoimmunotherapy; Figure 3C); the subgroups are small and are presented descriptively only.

TC/IC × ELNM Phenotype Map

To organise these observations for future testing, we present a descriptive 2 × 2 map of PD‐L1 IC expression (IC− vs. IC+) against ELNM status (Figure 4B). Given the small number of survival events (below), this map is descriptive and exploratory and is not a validated risk‐stratification model; its prognostic value must be confirmed in larger cohorts with longer follow‐up.

Exploratory Survival Analysis

At a data cut‐off of April 25, 2026, the median follow‐up was 24.0 months. There were three deaths (overall survival [OS] events) and nine progression or recurrence events (progression‐free survival [PFS] events); median OS and median PFS were not reached. Because only three deaths occurred (including none in the discordant profile group and none in the chemotherapy‐alone arm), OS was summarised descriptively and not modelled.

For PFS, exploratory univariable Cox models with Firth‐type penalisation showed no significant association with PD‐L1/ELNM profile (discordant vs. double‐negative HR 0.60, 95% CI 0.15–2.38, p = 0.47; double‐positive vs. double‐negative HR 1.35, 95% CI 0.36–5.02, p = 0.65; log‐rank p = 0.40) or with treatment modality (chemoimmunotherapy vs. chemotherapy HR 1.24, 95% CI 0.39–3.98, p = 0.72; log‐rank p = 0.64; Figure 5; Table 3). Notably, the double‐positive profile had the most progression events (4/21) and a numerically higher PFS hazard, rather than the favourable outcome that uncontrolled comparisons might suggest. No multivariable model was fitted: with nine PFS events, adjustment for age, stage and treatment is not statistically supportable (≈10 events per covariate). These survival findings are therefore hypothesis‐generating only.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of 61 patients with locally advanced, hypopharynx‐predominant HNSCC receiving neoadjuvant therapy, three observations emerge. First, PD‐L1 TC% and IC% were not correlated (ρ = −0.13, p = 0.40), consistent with biological independence of the two compartments. CPS, which merges TC and IC positivity into a single value, may mask clinically meaningful discordance: a CPS of 20 driven entirely by IC has different biology from one driven entirely by TC, yet both receive the same classification. Our data align with work by Gili et al. [16] and Bill et al. [15] questioning whether aggregate scoring captures the full complexity of HNSCC and suggest that separate TC% and IC% assessment may add information beyond CPS—a hypothesis that should be tested directly against CPS in a larger cohort with outcome data.

Second, the TC/IC phenotype distribution was heterogeneous, with the ‘hot’ (TC+/IC+) and ‘IC‐dominant’ phenotypes together accounting for over half the cohort. Under the adaptive‐resistance model, TC PD‐L1 positivity reflects tumour‐cell upregulation in response to interferon‐γ from infiltrating T cells, such that the ‘hot’ phenotype may indicate an immunologically engaged microenvironment [14, 16, 17, 19, 20]. The numerically lower serum IL‐6 in the ‘hot’ phenotype, and the inverse IL‐6–IC association, are directionally compatible with experimental evidence that IL‐6/JAK–STAT3 signalling upregulates tumour‐cell PD‐L1 while impairing immune‐cell infiltration [17, 24] which could shift PD‐L1 from the IC to the TC compartment. However, with IL‐6 available in only 32 patients and all comparisons non‐significant, these associations are strictly hypothesis‐generating: they do not establish a mechanistic link between IL‐6 and PD‐L1 compartmentalisation and, given the small underpowered subset, must be confirmed in dedicated studies with serial IL‐6 measurement and adequate sample size before any causal interpretation is warranted.

Third, short‐term neoadjuvant response was high across the cohort (ORR 91.4%, no progressive disease) and did not differ significantly by PD‐L1/ELNM profile or, statistically, by treatment modality. The high response to platinum–taxane‐based neoadjuvant therapy is consistent with published series (ORR 80%–95%) [12, 13], and the numerically higher ORR with added PD‐1 blockade (95% vs. 79%) did not reach significance in this small sample. In the neoadjuvant setting, the benefit of PD‐1 blockade may manifest as deeper pathological response and durable disease control rather than higher initial RECIST‐based ORR after two cycles. The two‐cycle neoadjuvant window is consistent with widely used protocols for RECIST 1.1–based response assessment in locally advanced HNSCC and is adequate to capture early tumour shrinkage; we acknowledge, however, that this short interval may be insufficient to observe the full therapeutic benefit of immunotherapy, whose immune‐mediated effects—including delayed radiographic responses and progressive pathological regression—can continue to develop beyond two cycles. Response at this early timepoint should therefore be interpreted as an initial rather than a definitive measure of neoadjuvant efficacy.

Importantly, the exploratory survival analysis did not reproduce the favourable prognosis previously attributed to concurrent PD‐L1 positivity. Over a median follow‐up of 24.0 months, neither PFS nor OS differed significantly by PD‐L1/ELNM profile or treatment, and the double‐positive group actually experienced the most progressions. With only three deaths and nine progression events, however, the analysis is severely underpowered and any apparent direction of effect is unstable; these results should be read as descriptive rather than as evidence for or against a prognostic role. Longer follow‐up to accrue events is the single most important next step. The apparently paradoxical clustering of advanced nodal disease and progression events in the double‐positive group should also be read in light of factors not captured here. The uniformly high objective response rate across all PD‐L1/ELNM profiles (86%–100% by group; 91.4% overall) is most consistent with the platinum–taxane chemotherapy backbone—rather than the PD‐1 blockade or the baseline tumour immune microenvironment phenotype—being the principal driver of early radiographic response, whereas any phenotype‐dependent effect on longer‐term disease control would require extended follow‐up to emerge. In addition, the metabolic activity and metastatic burden of the involved nodes (e.g., nodal SUVmax and metabolic tumour volume on 18F‐FDG PET/CT, and the metastatic‐to‐primary metabolic ratio) may influence outcome independently of PD‐L1 phenotype and were not available in this cohort; incorporating quantitative nodal metabolic parameters is a priority for future work that could help resolve this paradox [26, 27, 28].

Serum tumour markers (CEA, SCC antigen, CYFRA21‐1) did not differ significantly by ELNM status, consistent with their limited value for nodal staging in HNSCC. The directionally higher SCC antigen in ELNM+ patients (and across PD‐L1/ELNM profiles) is hypothesis‐generating only, given the small subset [29].

This study has major limitations that constrain its conclusions. First and most importantly, the survival analysis is severely underpowered: with only three deaths and nine progression events at a median follow‐up of 24.0 months, OS could not be modelled and PFS supported only exploratory univariable estimates, so no robust prognostic inference can be drawn. Second, the design is retrospective and single‐centre, susceptible to selection and information bias, and the predominantly male, high‐smoking/alcohol population may not generalise to settings where HPV‐driven oropharyngeal disease predominates. Third, although hypopharynx‐predominant, the cohort also included oropharyngeal (n = 10, 16.4%) and laryngeal (n = 3, 4.9%) primaries, which have distinct patterns of lymphatic drainage and biological behaviour; pooling these subsites introduces anatomical and biological heterogeneity that a sample of this size cannot adjust for, and a hypopharynx‐restricted sensitivity analysis will be informative in larger cohorts. Critically, HPV/p16 status was unavailable for the entire cohort. HPV‐positive disease—concentrated in the oropharyngeal subset—is a strong, independent driver of both favourable prognosis and a distinct, immunologically active microenvironment in HNSCC; its absence is therefore a major potential confounder, as unmeasured HPV positivity could bias the observed associations between PD‐L1 phenotype, nodal status and outcome in unpredictable directions. Our findings for the oropharyngeal subset in particular should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating until HPV/p16 status can be incorporated. Fourth, several analyses are markedly underpowered (double‐negative profile n = 6; IL‐6 n = 32; SCC antigen n = 18), and the PD‐L1 was scored using separate tumour‐cell (TC% > 1%) and immune‐cell (IC% ≥ 10%) thresholds rather than the combined positive score (CPS ≥ 1 or ≥ 20) validated in KEYNOTE‐048. These compartment‐specific cut‐offs were deliberate: the central aim of the study was to resolve the TC‐ and IC‐derived signals that CPS collapses into a single value; the IC% ≥ 10% threshold follows prior work linking immune‐cell PD‐L1 to prognosis [14, 20], and CPS could not be reconstructed retrospectively because TC and IC positivity had been recorded separately rather than as the pooled cell count CPS requires. As a sensitivity analysis we repeated the concordance analysis using stricter thresholds (TC ≥ 5%, IC ≥ 10%), which did not materially change the response findings; nonetheless, this deviation from CPS limits direct comparability with the pivotal trials [6, 18], and prospective validation against standardised CPS scoring is required for clinical translation. Fifth, four different PD‐1 inhibitors were used, adding pharmacological heterogeneity. Prospective, multicentre studies with standardised PD‐L1 scoring, HPV/p16 status, standardised tumour‐infiltrating‐lymphocyte quantification, serial IL‐6 measurement, longer survival follow‐up and adequate power are required to determine whether TC/IC phenotype and nodal status carry independent prognostic value.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

This retrospective, cross‐sectional cohort study was conducted at the Department of Oncology, Qilu Hospital (Qingdao), Cheeloo College of Medicine, Shandong University. We reviewed consecutive patients with newly diagnosed HNSCC treated between 2022 and 2024. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) histologically confirmed primary squamous cell carcinoma of the hypopharynx, oropharynx, larynx or contiguous sites; (2) no prior treatment for head and neck malignancy; (3) availability of pretreatment FFPE tumour tissue for PD‐L1 immunohistochemistry; and (4) availability of baseline staging data. Patients with distant metastasis at diagnosis (cM1), synchronous second primary malignancy or incomplete diagnosis/treatment records were excluded (n = 2 of 63 reviewed). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Qilu Hospital, Shandong University (Qingdao Branch) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient.

Clinical Staging and Data Collection

Baseline evaluation included contrast‐enhanced CT and/or MRI of the head and neck, chest imaging and fibreoptic laryngopharyngoscopy. Tumour, node and metastasis (T/N/M) categories were assigned uniformly for all patients according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging manual. ELNM was defined as radiologically confirmed cervical nodal metastasis (clinical N ≥ 1) at initial diagnosis before treatment; criteria for nodal positivity included short‐axis diameter ≥ 10 mm, central necrosis or extracapsular extension on CT/MRI. ELNM was dichotomised as present (N ≥ 1) or absent (N0).

PD‐L1 Immunohistochemistry and TC/IC Phenotyping

PD‐L1 was assessed on FFPE pretreatment biopsies by immunohistochemistry using the anti–PD‐L1 22C3 antibody clone (Dako/Agilent) on a Dako Autostainer Link 48 platform; a single clone and platform were applied uniformly to all cases. Membranous staining was scored independently by two head and neck pathologists blinded to outcomes, recording TC% and IC% separately. For the quadrant analysis, TC positivity was defined as TC% > 1% and IC positivity as IC% ≥ 10%, yielding four phenotypes: TC−/IC− (‘cold’), TC+/IC− (TC‐intrinsic), IC+/TC− (IC‐dominant) and TC+/IC+ (‘hot’). For PD‐L1/ELNM profiling, PD‐L1 positivity was defined as TC% > 1%.

PD‐L1/ELNM Combined Profile Groups

Patients were assigned to three predefined groups: (1) double‐negative (PD‐L1−/ELNM−); (2) discordant (PD‐L1+/ELNM− or PD‐L1−/ELNM+); and (3) double‐positive (PD‐L1+/ELNM+). Patients with missing PD‐L1 or N data (n = 13) were excluded from profile comparisons.

Serum Biomarkers

Baseline serum IL‐6, CEA, SCC antigen and CYFRA21‐1 were collected where available at diagnosis. IL‐6 was available in 32/61 patients (52.5%); analyses were performed in available subsets with explicit acknowledgement of missing data.

Treatment and Response

Treatment decisions were made by a multidisciplinary tumour board. Neoadjuvant therapy comprised a platinum–taxane backbone (TP or TPF) with or without a PD‐1 inhibitor. Response was assessed after two cycles by CT/MRI and/or endoscopy according to RECIST 1.1; pathological response was assessed on surgical specimens where applicable. ORR was defined as complete plus partial response among evaluable patients.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are reported as median (IQR) and categorical variables as n (%). Intergroup comparisons used the Kruskal–Wallis or Mann–Whitney U test (continuous) and Fisher's exact or χ 2 test (categorical). Correlations used Spearman's rank correlation. OS was defined as time from first treatment to death from any cause; PFS as time to first locoregional/regional/distant progression, recurrence or death. Survivors were censored at the last follow‐up date (data cut‐off 25 April 2026). Survival was estimated by Kaplan–Meier and compared by log‐rank test; hazard ratios were obtained from univariable Cox models with Firth‐type (ridge) penalisation to stabilise estimates given the small number of events. Owing to the low event count (3 deaths, 9 progressions), OS was summarised descriptively and no multivariable Cox model was fitted; all survival results are exploratory. Analyses were performed in Python 3.10 (pandas, SciPy, lifelines); two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered significant.
