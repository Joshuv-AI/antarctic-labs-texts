Reply to F. Mochizuki and colleagues:

We thank F. Mochizuki and colleagues for their interest in our recent Tasmanian Longitudinal Health Study (TAHS) publication on concavity of the maximal expiratory flow–volume (MEFV) curve and its association with incidence of COPD and respiratory symptoms [1]. They discuss our findings in relation to another quantitative measure of MEFV curve concavity, termed the obstructive index (OI), and provide an insightful comparison with central and peripheral concavity.

The OI is defined as the ratio of forced vital capacity (FVC) to the volume between two points on the MEFV curve where flow equals half of peak expiratory flow [2]. In contrast, central and peripheral concavity are defined as the ratios of the observed and expected forced expiratory flow values at 50% and 75% of FVC, respectively, with expected values estimated from an “ideal” MEFV curve without concavity. Thus, while the OI reflects expiratory flow from the large and mid-airways, concavity captures instantaneous deviations in flow at mid- and low lung volumes. Concavity therefore provides a quantitative measure of what is visually apparent on the MEFV curve and can be readily derived from standard spirometry output. In comparison, the volume data required to calculate the OI are not routinely available, making concavity a more practical measure for broader clinical use.

The data presented by F. Mochizuki and colleagues in their cohort of patients with a mix of established asthma, COPD and asthma–COPD overlap are intriguing. While the OI and concavity indices were expectedly highly correlated, figure 1b in their correspondence suggests their relationships with computed tomography (CT) metrics of lung density (low-attenuation volume with a threshold of −960 Hounsfield units to the total lung volume (LAV%)) differ. The OI more effectively distinguished LAV% at higher levels, whereas our concavity indices distinguished LAV% better at lower levels. Importantly, accurate interpretation of figure 1b depends on the distribution of the disease groups, as abnormalities in LAV% can reflect emphysema or air trapping due to small airway narrowing [3]. Collectively, these findings suggest that the two indices could serve different roles: concavity as a marker of early and mild–moderate airway disease and the OI as a tool for monitoring established disease.

There were important differences between the study samples. We examined a younger, community-based cohort without baseline COPD, while F. Mochizuki and colleagues studied individuals with established airways and lung parenchymal disease. Additionally, the multivariable analyses were adjusted for forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) and FEV1/FVC, both of which are highly correlated with the OI and our concavity indices, and may have resulted in substantial collinearity.

Together, these studies highlight the added value of quantitative measures of MEFV curve concavity, beyond the information provided by conventional spirometric indices routinely used in clinical and research settings [4]. In the ongoing seventh decade of follow-up of the TAHS, which includes paired inspiratory and expiratory low-dose CT imaging, we will further evaluate these measures.
