Boson-fermion universality of mesoscopic entanglement fluctuations in free systems
Cunzhong Lou, Chushun Tian, Zhixing Zou, Tao Shi, Lih-King Lim

Abstract
Entanglement fluctuations associated with Schrödinger evolution of wavefunctions offer a unique perspective on various fundamental issues ranging from quantum thermalization to state preparation in quantum devices. Very recently, a subset of present authors have shown that in a class of free-fermion lattice models and interacting spin chains, entanglement dynamics enters into a new regime at long time, with entanglement probes displaying persistent temporal fluctuations, whose statistics falls into the seemingly disparate paradigm of mesoscopic fluctuations in condensed matter physics. This motivate us to revisit here entanglement dynamics of a canonical bosonic model in many-body physics, i.e., a coupled harmonic oscillator chain. We find that when the system is driven out of equilibrium, the long-time entanglement dynamics exhibits strictly the same statistical behaviors as that of free-fermion models. Specifically, irrespective of entanglement probes and microscopic parameters, the statistical distribution of entanglement fluctuations is flanked by asymmetric tails: sub-Gaussian for upward fluctuations and sub-Gamma for downward; moreover, the variance exhibits a crossover from the scaling ∼ 1 / L \sim 1/L to ∼ L A 3 / L 2 \sim L_{A}^{3}/L^{2} , as the subsystem size L A L_{A} increases ( L L the total system size). This insensitivity to the particle statistics, dubbed boson-fermion universality, is contrary to the common wisdom that statistical phenomena of many-body nature depend strongly on particle statistics. Together with our previous work, the present work indicates rich fluctuation phenomena in entanglement dynamics awaiting in-depth explorations.

For excited states, especially highly excited ones, the study of their entanglement properties remains a challenging task. So far, theoretical works have been based on an ensemble of states subjected to certain kinematic constraints

When a quantum system undergoes a sudden quench, whereby parameters of system’s Hamiltonian are changed instantaneously, many high-lying eigenstates of the post-quench Hamiltonian are excited
[
20
]
. Subsequent unitary time evolution generates an infinitely long path in the Hilbert space; each point of this path, labelled by the time parameter

, represents an instantaneous system state,

. One may view this path as a special ‘ensemble’ of states, each of which is a linear superposition of many excited eigenstates. A question then arises: What happens to entanglement fluctuations of this ensemble? Studies of this question go far beyond previous kinematic studies of entanglement fluctuations for two fundamental reasons: First, the variation of the subsystem’s entanglement along the unitary time evolution path is genuinely of dynamical origin, i.e., entanglement fluctuations are of out-of-equilibrium nature. Secondly, the path is an exceptional,

Yet, in experiments and real applications a system is always

[
26
]

[

,
28
,
29

. In parallel to von Neumann’s study a fundamental question is what happens to temporal fluctuations of entanglement entropy

Not until very recently has the analytical study of long-time entanglement dynamics been initiated

Specifically, the fluctuations of various entanglement probes, such as the entanglement entropy and the Rényi entropy of distinct orders, display the same probability distribution which is asymmetric, with a sub-Gamma lower tail and a sub-Gaussian upper tail. This asymmetry implies that downward fluctuations are more favored than the upward. Moreover, the variances of distinct probes display the same scaling behavior that depends only on the subsystem and total system sizes, independent of system’s microscopic parameters.

It is worth noting that entanglement fluctuations found in Ref.
[
32
]
are of many-body nature. In many-body physics fluctuation properties are often sensitive to the particle statistics: bosons, fermions or anyons. In addition, they can be influenced by the coupling of bosonic and fermionic degrees of freedom, that exists typically in supersymmetric systems. Thus an interesting issue is raised. That is, whether it is possible to detect the particle statistics from temporal fluctuations in entanglement dynamics, akin to detecting different types of composite fermions from the electric current noise in fractional quantum Hall systems
[

37
,
38
]
.

Here, we address this issue by generalizing the theory of long-time entanglement dynamics developed for free fermions
[
32
]
to a canonical bosonic model, the coupled harmonic oscillator chain. This model is a prototype of many realistic systems, ranging from one-dimensional solids to the Klein-Gordon field in particle physics. Entanglement properties of this model have been studied by many authors. However, the focus has been the kinematic entanglement structure
[

,

,

42
,

or the early-time behavior of entanglement dynamics

[

,

In this work, we analytically study fluctuation phenomena in long-time entanglement dynamics of this model. We find, surprisingly, that the statistics of out-of-equilibrium entanglement fluctuations of a harmonic oscillator chain is strictly the same as that in free-fermion models. Our analytical predictions are confirmed by extensive numerical experiments. Thus we establish the boson-fermion universality of mesoscopic fluctuations in long-time entanglement dynamics of free systems.

II
Basic notions and motivations

We begin with a description of the model and a brief review of some basic concepts and dynamical properties. An emphasis is placed on the Gaussianity of the total system and subsystem state, since this notion plays important roles in the studies of entanglement (see, e.g., Ref.
[
48
]
for an exhaustive review) and in developing our fluctuation theory. With these preparations we formulate the problem to be addressed, and describe the scope of the paper.

II.1
Model and quench dynamics

We consider a chain of
L
(assumed to be even and large, but finite throughout) quantum harmonic oscillators, each of which has frequency
\omega
and mass
m
. They are labelled by
r=1,2,\ldots,L
. Two nearest oscillators are coupled through a harmonic interaction with strength
K^{\prime}
(cf. Fig.
1
). The Hamiltonian reads

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{\prime}=\sum\limits_{r=1}^{L}\left(\frac{\hat{p}_{r}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{m\omega^{2}\hat{x}_{r}^{2}}{2}+\frac{K^{\prime}}{2}(\hat{x}_{r+1}-\hat{x}_{r})^{2}\right).

(1)

Here the first two terms describe a single oscillator and the last describes the coupling between two nearest oscillators. The periodic boundary condition:
\hat{x}_{L+1}=\hat{x}_{1}
,
\hat{p}_{L+1}=\hat{p}_{1}
is imposed. The position (displaced from the equilibrium point) and momentum operators
\hat{x}_{r}
,
\hat{p}_{r}
of the
r

[\hat{x}_{r},\hat{p}_{s}]=i\hbar\delta_{r,s}
. We make the rescaling:
\hat{x}_{r}\rightarrow\sqrt{m\omega\over\hbar}\,\hat{x}_{r}
,
\hat{p}_{r}\rightarrow\hat{p}_{r}/\sqrt{\hbar m\omega}
, so that
\hat{x}_{r}
and
\hat{p}_{r}
are both dimensionless, and the commutation relation reduces to
[\hat{x}_{r},\hat{p}_{s}]=i\delta_{r,s}
. Upon this rescaling, the Hamiltonian is transformed to

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{\prime}={\hbar\omega\over 2}\sum\limits_{r=1}^{L}\left(\hat{p}_{r}^{2}+\hat{x}_{r}^{2}+\frac{K}{\omega^{2}}(\hat{x}_{r+1}-\hat{x}_{r})^{2}\right)

(2)

with
K\equiv K^{\prime}/m
. This Hamiltonian depends on parameters

and
K
.

Here

II.2
Gaussianity of evolving state

A basic property of the Schrödinger evolution (
14
), which is crucial to our work, is that at any instant
t>0
the pure state
\psi(t)
is Gaussian. This Gaussianity inherits from that of the pre-quench state
\psi_{0}
. To understand this property we need to introduce the first and second moments of
\hat{{\bf r}}
, defined as
\langle\psi(t)|\hat{\bf r}|\psi(t)\rangle
and
\langle\psi(t)|\hat{\bf r}\hat{\bf r}|\psi(t)\rangle\equiv G(t)
, respectively, whose components are correspondingly
\langle\psi(t)|\hat{r}_{i}|\psi(t)\rangle
and
G_{ij}(t)=\langle\psi(t)|\hat{r}_{i}\hat{r}_{j}|\psi(t)\rangle
. It is easy to show that the former vanishes and the second is

G(t)=\gamma(t)+iJ/2.

(16)

The real part,
\gamma(t)=\{\gamma_{ij}(t)\}
, is the so-called covariance matrix
[
48
,
43
,
45
]

\displaystyle\gamma(t)\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\psi(t)\left|\{\hat{\bf r},\,\hat{\bf r}\}\right|\psi(t)\right\rangle

(17)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\gamma_{ij}(t)\equiv{1\over 2}\langle\psi(t)|\{\hat{r}_{i},\hat{r}_{j}\}|\psi(t)\rangle,

with
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
being the anticommutator, which evolves with time, whereas the imaginary part is
J/2
, which is independent of time. Then, by straightforward calculations one finds that any instantaneous correlations of
h\in\mathbb{N}
operators:
\hat{r}_{1},\,\cdots,\,\hat{r}_{h}
are given by

\displaystyle\langle\psi(t)|\hat{r}_{1}\cdots\hat{r}_{h}|\psi(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sum_{P}\prod_{n=1}^{m}G_{i_{n}i^{\prime}_{n}}(t),&{\rm for}\,\,h=2m,m\in\mathbb{N};\\
0&{\rm for}\,\,h=2m+1,m\in\mathbb{N},\end{array}\right.,\quad

where the sum is over all permutations
P:\,i_{1}\rightarrow i^{\prime}_{1}\rightarrow...\rightarrow i_{m}\rightarrow i^{\prime}_{m}
of the sequence:
1\rightarrow 2\rightarrow...\rightarrow 2m
satisfying
i_{n+1}-i_{n}>1
and
i_{n}<i^{\prime}_{n}
. This gives Wick’s theorem and is a defining property of the Gaussian state. It implies that the second moment, i.e., the covariant matrix completely determines correlation properties at state
\psi(t)
.

The Gaussianity of the total system state
\psi(t)

A
being a subsystem and

being its complement (Fig.

we project the vector

onto

\hat{{\bf r}}_{A}\equiv\bigoplus_{r=1}^{L_{A}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{x}_{r}\\
\hat{p}_{r}\\
\end{array}\right)

(21)

for which the commutation relation reads

[\hat{{\bf r}}_{A},\,\hat{{\bf r}}_{A}]=iJ_{A},\quad J_{A}\equiv\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\mathbb{I}_{L_{A}}\\

(22)

So for subsystem

{\rm Tr}_{A}(\hat{r}_{Ai}\hat{\rho}_{A}(t))
and

The matrices:

are respectively a submatrix of

[

]
.

II.3
Entanglement probes

We focus on the entanglement entropy
S(t)\equiv-{\rm Tr}_{A}(\hat{\rho}_{A}(t)\ln\hat{\rho}_{A}(t))
and the
n
-th order Rényi entropy
S_{n}(t)\equiv{1\over 1-n}\ln{\rm Tr}_{A}(\hat{\rho}_{A}(t))^{n},\,n=2,3,...
. These probes of entanglement dynamics are generally denoted as
O(t)
. With the substitution of Eqs. (
32
), (
34
), (
35
) and (
38
), one finds

\displaystyle S(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{L_{A}}\left((\lambda_{r}(t)+\frac{1}{2})\ln(\lambda_{r}(t)+\frac{1}{2})-(\lambda_{r}(t)-\frac{1}{2})\ln(\lambda_{r}(t)-\frac{1}{2})\right)

(39)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 2}\sum_{r=1}^{L_{A}}\left(\ln\left((2\lambda_{r}(t)+1)(2\lambda_{r}(t)-1)\right)+2\lambda_{r}(t)\ln{2\lambda_{r}(t)+1\over 2\lambda_{r}(t)-1}\right)-L_{A}\ln 2

and

\displaystyle S_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{r=1}^{L_{A}}\ln\left((\lambda_{r}(t)+\frac{1}{2})^{n}-(\lambda_{r}(t)-\frac{1}{2})^{n}\right)

(40)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2(n-1)}\sum_{r=1}^{L_{A}}\ln\left((\lambda_{r}(t)+\frac{1}{2})^{n}-(\lambda_{r}(t)-\frac{1}{2})^{n}\right)^{2}-L_{A}\,{n\ln 2\over n-1}.

They are completely determined by the evolving symplectic eigenvalue spectrum
\{\lambda_{r}(t)\}
. These expressions are convenient for numerical simulations
[
47
]
, but not for developing a fluctuation theory.

For the latter purpose, we notice an important consequence of the Gaussianity of
\hat{\rho}_{A}(t)
. That is, with the introduction of
C(t)
defined in Eq. (
31
) and its eigenvalue properties described before, we can invoke the Gaussianity to rewrite the second line of Eqs. (

) and (

) as

\displaystyle O(t)={1\over 4}\operatorname{Tr}_{A}h(C(t)),

(41)

where
h(C)
is a function of
C
:

\displaystyle h(C)\equiv\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\ln\left((2C+\mathbb{I}_{2L_{A}})(2C-\mathbb{I}_{2L_{A}})\right)+2C\ln{2C+\mathbb{I}_{2L_{A}}\over 2C-\mathbb{I}_{2L_{A}}}-(2\ln 2)\,\mathbb{I}_{2L_{A}},&\quad\textrm{for}\,O=S,\\
\frac{1}{n-1}\ln\left((2C+\mathbb{I}_{2L_{A}})^{n}-(2C-\mathbb{I}_{2L_{A}})^{n}\right)^{2}-{(2\ln 2)n\over n-1}\,\mathbb{I}_{2L_{A}},&\quad\textrm{for}\,O=S_{n}.\end{array}\right.\quad

Note that although the matrices:
2C\pm\mathbb{I}_{2L_{A}}
are not positive definite, those under the logarithms are. Therefore, the matrix logarithms above are well defined, which are the so-called
principal logarithm

[
55
]
. Equations (
41
) and (
II.3
) lay a basis for our systematic analytical treatments of the temporal fluctuations in entanglement dynamics.

II.4
Boson-fermion duality

In this part we derive a fermion dual of the entanglement dynamics free-boson systems. We should emphasize that
the duality does
not
mapped bosonic entanglement dynamics onto a fermionic one, i.e. does
not
fermionize bosonic entanglement dynamics. However, it indicates that the two classes of entanglement dynamics share some common aspects.

The Hamiltonian of a generic free-boson system takes the form as

\hat{H}_{b}=\frac{1}{2}\,\hat{\bf r}^{\mathrm{T}}\Omega_{b}\hat{\bf r},\quad\hat{{\bf r}}={1\over\sqrt{2}}\bigoplus_{r=1}^{L}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&1\\
-i&i\end{array}\right]\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{a}_{r}\\
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{r}\\
\end{array}\right).

(45)

Here
\hat{\bf r}
is defined in Eq. (
4
), and
\Omega_{b}
is a real symmetric and positive-definite matrix of size
2L
. This Hamiltonian has a coupling between the position
\hat{x}
and momentum
\hat{p}
operators, a property which is not carried by the Hamiltonian (
15
) of an oscillator chain. By
the Williamson theorem there exists a real symplectic matrix
{\cal G}_{S}\in{\rm Sp}(2L,\mathbb{R})
such that

{\cal G}^{\mathrm{T}}_{S}\,\Omega_{b}\,{\cal G}_{S}=D\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2},\quad D=\text{diag}(d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{L}),

(46)

where the symplectic eigenvalues
d_{r}>0
. Passing to the operator bases
\hat{\bf r}^{\prime}={\cal G}_{S}^{-1}\hat{\bf r}=\bigoplus_{r=1}^{L}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{x}^{\prime}_{r}\\
\hat{p}^{\prime}_{r}\end{pmatrix}
, the Hamiltonian reads

\hat{H}_{b}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r=1}^{L}d_{r}(\hat{x}^{\,\prime 2}_{r}+\hat{p}_{r}^{\,\prime 2})=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{r=1}^{L}d_{r}\hat{a}_{r}^{\,\prime\dagger}\hat{a}_{r}^{\,\prime}.

(47)

This shows that
d_{r}
is the frequency of the
r
-th oscillator spectrum in the bases
\hat{\bf r}^{\prime}
. The evolution of a Gaussian state

\psi_{b}(t)
is completely determined by the instantaneous covariance matrix
\gamma_{b}(t)\equiv\frac{1}{2}\langle{\psi_{b}(t)}|\{\hat{\bf r},\hat{\bf r}\}|{\psi_{b}(t)}\rangle
, which satisfies

\gamma_{b}(t)=S_{b}(t)S^{\mathrm{T}}_{b}(t),\quad\partial_{t}S_{b}(t)=J\Omega_{b}S_{b}(t).

(48)

As before we restrict
\gamma_{b}
to the subsystem A, and the ensuing matrix is denoted as
\gamma_{bA}
. Furthermore, we define
C_{b}(t)\equiv iJ_{A}\gamma_{bA}(t)
. Then
the entanglement entropy
S(t)
is given by Eqs. (
41
) and (
II.3
). For the convenience of deriving the fermionic dual below, we rewrite it as

S(t)=\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{Tr}_{A}\tilde{h}(C_{b}(t)),

(49)

where the matrix function

\displaystyle\tilde{h}(C)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\ln|4C^{2}-\mathbb{I}_{2L_{A}}|+2C\ln\left|\frac{2C+\mathbb{I}_{2L_{A}}}{2C-\mathbb{I}_{2L_{A}}}\right|

(50)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle(2\ln 2)\,\mathbb{I}_{2L_{A}},

with
|X|
defined for positive- or negative-definite
X
: For positive-definite
X
we have
|X|=X
and have
|X|=-X
for negative-definite
X
.

Now we wish to show that the entanglement dynamics above has a fermionic dual. To this end we first replace the canonical conjugate in Eq. (
4
) by a pair of Majorana fermions:
(\hat{f}_{1r},\hat{f}_{2r})
, so that a
2L
-component operator vector

\hat{\bf f}=\bigoplus_{r=1}^{L}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{f}_{1r}\\
\hat{f}_{2r}\end{pmatrix}={1\over\sqrt{2}}\bigoplus_{r=1}^{L}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&1\\
-i&i\end{array}\right]\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{b}_{r}\\
\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{r}\\
\end{array}\right)

(51)

results, where
\hat{b}_{r},\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{r}
are annihilation and creation operators of usual fermions. In the Majorana bases a generic free-fermion Hamiltonian reads

\hat{H}_{f}=\frac{i}{2}\,\hat{\bf f}^{\mathrm{T}}\Omega_{f}\hat{\bf f},

(52)

where
\Omega_{f}
is a real anti-symmetric matrix of size
2L
. There exists a real orthogonal matrix
{\cal G}_{O}\in{\rm O}(2L,\mathbb{R})
such that

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{O}^{\mathrm{T}}\,\Omega_{f}\,{\cal G}_{O}=D^{\,\prime}\otimes\left[\begin{matrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{matrix}\right],\qquad

\displaystyle D^{\,\prime}=\text{diag}(d_{1}^{\,\prime},d_{2}^{\,\prime},\cdots,d_{L}^{\,\prime}).\qquad

(53)

In the operator bases
\hat{\bf f}^{\,\prime}={\cal G}_{O}^{-1}\hat{\bf f}
, the Hamiltonian (
52
) is written as

\hat{H}_{f}=i\sum_{r=1}^{L}d_{r}^{\,\prime}\hat{f}^{\,\prime}_{1r}\hat{f}^{\,\prime}_{2r}.

(54)

Since the eigenvlaues of
i\hat{f}^{\,\prime}_{r_{1}}\hat{f}^{\,\prime}_{r_{2}}
are
\pm\frac{1}{2}
, it may be considered as the Majorana dual of the Hamiltonian (
47
), provided the two spectra are identical, i.e.

D^{\prime}=D.

(55)

Under this requirement, we construct
{\cal G}_{S}\equiv\Omega_{b}^{-1/2}{\cal G}_{O}(D^{1/2}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2})
, so that on the bosonic side Eq. (
46

{\cal G}_{O}
depends on

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{S}\,J\,{\cal G}_{S}\,^{\mathrm{T}}=J

(56)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\Omega_{f}={\cal G}_{O}\,D\otimes\left[\begin{matrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{matrix}\right]{\cal G}_{O}\,^{\mathrm{T}}=\Omega_{b}^{1/2}J\Omega_{b}^{1/2},

which provides a dual between
\Omega_{f}
and
\Omega_{b}

The evolution of a Gaussian state
\psi_{f}(t)
is completely determined by the instantaneous covariance matrix
\gamma_{f}(t)\equiv\frac{i}{2}\langle{\psi_{f}(t)}|[\hat{\bf f},\hat{\bf f}]|{\psi_{f}(t)}\rangle
, which satisfies

\gamma_{f}(t)=-S_{f}(t)JS_{f}^{\mathrm{T}}(t),\quad\partial_{t}S_{f}(t)=\Omega_{f}S_{f}(t).

(57)

Restricting
\gamma_{f}(t)
to A so that a matrix
\gamma_{fA}(t)
results, and defining
C_{f}(t)\equiv i\gamma_{fA}(t)
, one can follow standard procedures
[
50
,
51
,
52
]
to find the entanglement entropy

S(t)=-\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{Tr}_{A}\tilde{h}(C_{f}(t)),

(58)

with
\tilde{h}(C)
given by Eq. (

). (Note that there is an overall minus sign here.)

Equations (

and

II.5
Scope of the paper

The present work is motivated by the above duality, and by numerical simulations of long-time entanglement evolution based on Eqs. (
39
) and (
40
). As shown in the inset of Fig.
2
, after the quench, in the initial time window the entanglement displays a linear rise followed by revivals. This behavior has been well described by the Cardy-Calabrese picture of quasiparticles propagating with a finite speed
[
21
,
56
,
45
,
46
]
. However, as clearly shown in the main panel of Fig.
2
, after long time evolution (i.e., beyond the revival regime) the entanglement shows a completely different dynamical behavior, namely, displaying persistent fluctuations. These temporal fluctuations resemble noisy behaviors, but they are completely reproducible under the same initial condition and the same quench protocol. This kind of fluctuation phenomena in entanglement dynamics were studied analytically, to the best of our knowledge, in Ref.
[
32
]
for the first time, but only for free fermion models and spin chains.

The aim of this work is to develop an analytical theory of fluctuation statistics in long-time entanglement dynamics of quantum harmonic oscillator chains, and perform numerical experiments to test analytical predictions. Technically, a key issue to be addressed is the
full
probability distribution of entanglement fluctuations. This is a highly challenging task. Our main strategy is utilizing the Gaussianity of the evolving state reviewed in Sec.
II.2
and generalizing the temporal entanglement fluctuation theory of free-fermion models
[
32
]
to present free-boson model. Armed with the developed theory, we are able to derive that distribution and study variances of various entanglement probes. We then find that the long-time entanglement dynamics exhibits strictly the same statistical behaviors as that of free-fermion models. This result shows that persistent temporal fluctuations in entanglement dynamics of coupled harmonic oscillator chains not only fall into the same paradigm of mesoscopic fluctuations, as occurring to free-fermion models, but also exhibit an unexpected universality, namely, the boson-fermion universality.

III
Temporal fluctuations and statistical equivalence

III.1
A common property of the time dependence of various quantities

As we discussed in Sec.
II.3
, various entanglement probes are determined by the time-dependent covariance matrix
\gamma_{A}(t)
or its equivalent
C(t)
defined in Eq. (
31
). Given the evolving state
|\psi(t)\rangle
, the latter takes the form of a block-Toeplitz matrix structure, i.e., its element is a
2\times 2
block defined in the
\hat{x}
-
\hat{p}
sector, which is introduced by the representation Eq. (
4
) of
\hat{{\bf r}}
, and depends on the entries namely the oscillator indexes
r,\,r^{\prime}
only through their displacement
r-r^{\prime}
. That is,
C_{A}(t)
takes the form as

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cccccc}\tau_{0}&\tau_{1}&\tau_{2}&\ldots&\tau_{L_{A}-2}&\tau_{L_{A}-1}\\
\tau_{-1}&\tau_{0}&\tau_{1}&\ldots&\tau_{L_{A}-3}&\tau_{L_{A}-2}\\
\tau_{-2}&\tau_{-1}&\tau_{0}&\ldots&\tau_{L_{A}-4}&\tau_{L_{A}-3}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\tau_{-(L_{A}-2)}&\tau_{-(L_{A}-3)}&\tau_{-(L_{A}-4)}&\ldots&\tau_{0}&\tau_{1}\\
\tau_{-(L_{A}-1)}&\tau_{-(L_{A}-2)}&\tau_{-(L_{A}-3)}&\ldots&\tau_{-1}&\tau_{0}\end{array}\right]\!\!,\quad\,\,\,\,

where the matrix elements
\tau_{l}
,
l=0,\pm 1,\ldots,\pm(L_{A}-1)
are block elements. Each element can be separated into two parts, one time-independent and the other time-dependent, i.e.,

\tau_{l}(t)=c_{0,l}+c_{1,l}(t).

(66)

They are given respectively as

\displaystyle c_{0,l}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&i\bar{p}_{0,l}\\
-i\bar{x}_{0,l}&0\end{array}\right],

\displaystyle c_{1,l}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}i\bar{m}_{l}(t)&i\bar{p}_{l}(t)\\
-i\bar{x}_{l}(t)&-i\bar{m}_{l}(t)\end{array}\right],

with

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\bar{x}_{0,l}=\frac{1}{L}\sum\limits_{k}\frac{E_{+}(k)}{s_{k}}\cos(kl),\\
&\bar{p}_{0,l}=\frac{1}{L}\sum\limits_{k}E_{+}(k)\,s_{k}\cos(kl)\\
\end{aligned}

(73)

being independent of time and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\bar{x}_{l}(t)=\frac{1}{L}\sum\limits_{k}\frac{E_{-}(k)}{s_{k}}\cos(kl)\cos(2\omega_{k}t),\\
&\bar{m}_{l}(t)=-\frac{1}{L}\sum\limits_{k}E_{-}(k)\cos(kl)\sin(2\omega_{k}t),\\
&\bar{p}_{l}(t)=-\frac{1}{L}\sum\limits_{k}E_{-}(k)\,s_{k}\cos(kl)\cos(2\omega_{k}t)\end{aligned}

(74)

depending on time. The derivation of these expressions as well as the explicit form of

are given by Eq. (
193
) in Appendix
A
. Inheriting from Eq. (

C(t)=C_{0}+C_{1}(t)

(75)

where
C_{0}
and
C_{1}
are the time-independent and time-dependent part, respectively. Moreover, both

and
C_{1}
are block-Toeplitz matrix, whose elements are
c_{0,l}
and
c_{1,l}
, respectively.

Equation (
74
) shows that the time parameter enters solely as the dynamical phase factor
2\omega_{k}t
in the argument of the sine or cosine function in the linear sums. In fact, due to the even parity of the spectrum
\omega_{k}
there are only

N=L/2+1

(76)

distinct frequencies, which we denote as

\boldsymbol{\omega}\equiv(\omega_{0},\omega_{1},\cdots,\omega_{N-1}).

(77)

Then this time dependence is respected by writing

as a

\widetilde{C}

\displaystyle C(t)=\widetilde{C}(\omega_{0}t,\omega_{1}t,\cdots,\omega_{N-1}t)\equiv\widetilde{C}(\boldsymbol{\omega}t).

(78)

According to Eq. (

), we can separate the auxiliary function
\widetilde{C}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})

\boldsymbol{\varphi}\equiv(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N-1})

\widetilde{C}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})=C_{0}+\widetilde{C}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}).

(79)

Here the
\boldsymbol{\varphi}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\widetilde{x}_{l}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})=\frac{2}{L}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{N-1}\bar{\delta}_{m}\frac{E_{-}(k_{m})}{\omega_{k_{m}}}\cos{({k_{m}}l)}\cos(\varphi_{m}),\\

III.2
Quasi-periodic oscillations and emergent mesoscopic sample-to-sample fluctuations

For generic energy spectrum the
N
frequencies (
77
) carry an important property, namely, the incommensurality:

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}x_{k}\omega_{k}=0,\quad x_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}\,\Rightarrow\,x_{1}=\ldots=x_{N-1}=0.

(84)

We shall assume this property in the remainder of this work. For incommensurate
\boldsymbol{\omega}
, by a well-known theorem
[
57
]

C(t)
is quasi-periodic in
t
, and so is
O(t)
. Thus the persistent fluctuations displayed in long-time entanglement fluctuations, exemplified in Fig.
2
for
O=S
, are quasi-periodic oscillations. They are reproducible, i.e., given identical initial state and quench protocol the time profiles
O(t)
are strictly the same.

Furthermore, because of the incommensurality of
\boldsymbol{\omega}
the trajectory (
81
) must be ergodic and dense on
\mathbb{T}^{N}
. By the ergodic theorem
[
58
]
we have for arbitrary interval
\Delta\subset\mathbb{R}
and for any entanglement probe
O
,

\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\int_{O(t)\in\Delta}\frac{dt}{T}=\int_{\widetilde{O}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})\in\Delta}{d\boldsymbol{\varphi}\over(2\pi)^{N}}.

(85)

In words, after long-time evolution the frequency for the time series
O(t)
to appear in
\Delta
is identical to the probability for
\widetilde{O}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})
to be in the same interval, i.e.,
\mathbf{P}(O(\boldsymbol{\varphi})\in\Delta)
. More generally, for any set
{\cal A}\subset\mathbb{T}^{N}
, we have

\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\boldsymbol{\omega}t\in{\cal A}}\frac{dt}{T}=\int_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in{\cal A}}{d\boldsymbol{\varphi}\over(2\pi)^{N}}\equiv\mathbf{P}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in{\cal A}).

(86)

Consequently, out-of-equilibrium fluctuations displayed by the quasi-periodic oscillations
O(t)
must be statistically equivalent to fluctuations of
\widetilde{O}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})
with
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
, provided
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
is drawn uniformly from
\mathbb{T}^{N}
. This statistical equivalence is confirmed numerically, exemplified by
O=S
in Fig.
3
.

Equations (
82
) and (
83
) imply that each
\widetilde{C}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})
describes a virtual mesoscopic — recall that
N
is finite — disordered sample with a disorder realization
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
, and determines various entanglement properties characterized by
\widetilde{O}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})
by the same token as
C(t)
determines
O(t)
. Equation (
78
) further maps the quantum time evolution of
C(t)
onto a classical ergodic trajectory
\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\boldsymbol{\omega}t
, that sweeps out the entire disorder ensemble, i.e.,
\mathbb{T}^{N}
. As such, mesoscopic
sample-to-sample
fluctuations
\widetilde{O}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})
, familiar in mesoscopic electronic and optics physics
[
34
,
35
]
, emerge from entanglement dynamics, and are statistically equivalent to the time series
O(t)
. This was found previously for free fermion systems and interacting spin chains
[
32
]
. Finally, because
\cos(\varphi_{0})
,
\cos(\varphi_{1})
, …,
\cos(\varphi_{N-1})
[or
\sin(\varphi_{0})
,
\sin(\varphi_{1})
, …,
\sin(\varphi_{N-1})
] in Eq. (
80
) are
N
statistically independent random numbers of zero mean
[
59
]
. As a result,
\tilde{x}_{l}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})
in Eq. (
80
) is a sum of
N\sim L
random numbers, and so are
\tilde{m}_{l}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})
and
\tilde{p}_{l}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})
. So for large
L
their magnitude scales with the total system size as
\sim 1/\sqrt{L}
. Therefore, remarkably, the disorder strength depends on the system size
L
and vanishes in the limit
L\rightarrow\infty
: This peculiar property is
not
shared by any genuine mesoscopic systems, and is crucially responsible for the arising of new mesoscopic universalities.

IV
Boson-fermion universality of statistical distribution

We proceed to study the full statistics of entanglement fluctuations. These fluctuations are characterized by various entanglement probe
O(\boldsymbol{\varphi})
, which are functions of
N
independent random angles
\varphi_{i}
(
i=0,1,\cdots,N-1
). [Hereafter we will use common symbol
O
for the functions

and
\widetilde{O}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})

and
\widetilde{C}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})

{\mathbf{P}}
above. We shall demonstrate that for different

IV.1
Description of the mathematical tool

IV.1.1
Concentration of the product probability measure

A fundamental difficulty in addressing the statistics of an entanglement probe
O(\boldsymbol{\varphi})
is that it depends on the random variables
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
in a highly nonlinear manner. To overcome this difficulty we observe that
{\mathbf{P}}
is a
product probability measure

[
60
]
,

{\mathbf{P}}=\mu_{0}\times\cdots\times\mu_{N-1}

(87)

over the product space

\mathbb{T}^{N}=\mathbb{T}_{0}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{T}_{N-1},

(88)

where
\mu_{i}
is the Lebesgue measure over the one-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}_{i}\ni\varphi_{i}
. This product structure of probability measures lays down a foundation for the occurrence of the phenomenon of concentration of measure
[
60
,
33
]
, yielding a
nonasymptotic
probability theory: It can make statistical predictions for any finite
N
, without resorting to a limiting theorem. Specifically, below we use and solve the so-called modified logarithmic Sobolev inequality
[
33
]
to obtain some concentration inequalities describing the tail behaviors of the full distribution of
O(\boldsymbol{\varphi})
.

In general, a concentration inequality takes the following form: For any
\epsilon>0
,

P_{+}(\epsilon)\equiv\mathbf{P}(O-\langle O\rangle\geq\epsilon)\leq\alpha(\epsilon)

(89)

for upward fluctuations
O>\langle O\rangle
, and

P_{-}(\epsilon)\equiv\mathbf{P}(\langle O\rangle-O\geq\epsilon)\leq\alpha(\epsilon)

(90)

for downward fluctuations
O<\langle O\rangle
, where
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes the average with respect to
\mathbf{P}
, and
\alpha(\epsilon)
is the so-called concentration function. It is important to note that
\alpha
is
not
necessarily the same for the upward and downward fluctuations. Were they not be the same (and
\alpha
is sharp), the distribution is asymmetric,
and
\alpha
in (
89
) [respectively (
90
)] bounds the large deviation behavior of the upper (respectively lower) tail. In particular, if
\alpha(\epsilon)\sim{\rm e}^{-\epsilon^{2}/2b}
with
b>0
, then the concentration is said to be sub-Gaussian, and
b
is called the
variance factor
; if
\alpha(\epsilon)\sim{\rm e}^{-\epsilon/2c}
then the concentration is said to be sub-exponential, and
c
is called the
scale parameter

[
61
]
. It is the purpose of this section to find the explicit forms of
\alpha
and compare them with the results for fermion systems. Below we shall focus mostly on the entanglement entropy, i.e.,
O(\boldsymbol{\varphi})=S(\boldsymbol{\varphi})
, and the analysis and the final results hold similarly for other entanglement probes such as
S_{n}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})
.

IV.1.2
Modified logarithmic Sobolev inequality

Owing to the product structure of the probability measure Eqs. (
87
) and (
88
), a generic multivariable random entanglement probe function
O(\boldsymbol{\varphi})
obeys a very general inequality, the so-called modified logarithmic Sobolev inequality
[
33
]
:

\displaystyle u\left\langle Oe^{uO}\right\rangle-\left\langle e^{uO}\right\rangle\ln\left\langle e^{uO}\right\rangle\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\left\langle e^{uO}\,\phi(-u(O-O_{m}))\right\rangle,\quad\forall u\in\mathbb{R},

(91)

where

\phi(x)\equiv e^{x}-x-1

(92)

and
O_{m}
is an arbitrary (real) function of
\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\prime}_{m}\equiv(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m-1},\varphi_{m+1},\ldots,\varphi_{N-1})
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\prime}_{m}\equiv(\varphi_{0},\ldots,\varphi_{m-1},\varphi_{m+1},\ldots,\varphi_{N-1})\in\mathbb{T}^{N-1}\quad

\displaystyle\mapsto O_{m}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\prime}_{m})\in\mathbb{R}.\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad

(93)

By definition
O_{m}
is independent of
\varphi_{m}
, and is also a random quantity. Then we introduce the logarithm of the moment-generating function

G(u)\equiv\ln\langle e^{u(O-\langle O\rangle)}\rangle

(94)

with
O
shifted by its mean. With this definition, we can rewrite inequality (
91
) as

\frac{d}{du}\frac{G(u)}{u}\leq H(u),

(95)

where the right-hand side

H(u)\equiv\frac{1}{u^{2}}\frac{\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\left\langle\left[e^{u(O-\langle O\rangle)}\,\phi(-u(O-O_{m}))\right]\right\rangle}{\left\langle e^{u(O-\langle O\rangle)}\right\rangle}.

(96)

Armed with this inequality, below we follow the method developed in Ref.
[
32
]
to obtain the form of the concentration functions
\alpha
in (
89
) and (
90
), that give the sharp bound of the large deviation probability of the upper and the lower tail, respectively.

IV.2
General analysis of distribution tails

IV.2.1
Upper tail

We begin with the upper tail analysis. Let
u>0
and set
O_{m}
in the inequality (
95
) as the infimum of
O(\boldsymbol{\varphi})
over
\varphi_{m}
with the rest of
(N-1)
variables
\varphi_{i}
(
i\neq m
) held fixed
[
33
]
, denoted as
O^{+}_{m}
(cf. Appendix
B
):

\displaystyle O^{+}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\prime}_{m})\equiv\inf_{\varphi_{m}}O(\boldsymbol{\varphi}).

(97)

This ensures that the variable
u(O-O_{m}^{+})
in the inequality (
95
) is nonnegative. Next, we Taylor expand
\phi
,

\phi\bigl(-u(O-O_{m}^{+})\bigr)={u^{2}\over 2}(O-O_{m}^{+})^{2}+\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}{(-u)^{n}\over n!}(O-O_{m}^{+})^{n},

(98)

and make the following separation for
(O-O_{m}^{+})^{2}
:

(O-O_{m}^{+})^{2}=b_{+}+\delta(O-O_{m}^{+})^{2},

(99)

with the first term:

\displaystyle b_{+}\equiv\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\left\langle(O-O_{m}^{+})^{2}\right\rangle

(100)

being the average, and the second,
\delta(O-O_{m}^{+})^{2}\equiv(O-O_{m}^{+})^{2}-\langle(O-O_{m}^{+})^{2}\rangle
, being the fluctuations around the average. With the substitution of Eqs. (
98
)-(
100
), Eq. (
96
) is rewritten as

\displaystyle H(u)=\frac{b_{+}}{2}+\delta F_{+}(u),

(101)

where

\displaystyle\delta F_{+}(u)=\left\langle{\rm e}^{u(O-\langle O\rangle)}\right\rangle^{-1}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\Big\langle{\rm e}^{u(O-\langle O\rangle)}\Big(\delta(O-O_{m}^{+})^{2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}{(-u)^{n-2}\over n!}(O-O_{m}^{+})^{n}\Big)\Big\rangle.\qquad\qquad\qquad

(102)

So far the treatments of
H(u)
are rigorous, and the next step is to find a sharp bound of
\delta F_{+}(u)
.

For this purpose we note that
O=O_{m}^{+}
takes place only at a set of zero (Lebesgue) measure. So

\displaystyle H(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u\rightarrow+\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}{1\over u}\frac{\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\langle\,{\rm e}^{u(O-\langle O\rangle)}\,\bigl(O-O_{m}^{+}\bigr)\rangle}{\langle{\rm e}^{u(O-\langle O\rangle)}\rangle}.

(103)

As a result, there must exist some constant
const.
and some sufficiently large
u^{*}
(both of which depend on
O
), so that

H(u)\leq{const.\over u},\quad{\rm for}\,\,u>u^{*}.

(104)

This implies that
H(u)
is bounded by the curve
\sim{1\over u}
in the large
u
regime. On the other hand, it is easy to show that
{dG(u)\over du}
has a definite signature for
u>0
, which is positive. It is also easy to show that it grows unboundedly with
u
. Taking these and inequality (
104
) into account, we find that the function
\delta F/{dG\over du}
must be bounded for
u>0
. Let its supremum be
c_{+}
. We obtain

\displaystyle\delta F_{+}(u)\leq c_{+}\,\frac{dG(u)}{du},\quad{\rm for}\,u>0.

(105)

Combining Eq. (
101
) and inequality (
105
), we finally cast the modified Sobolev inequality (
95
) into the following differential inequality

\displaystyle\frac{d}{du}\frac{G(u)}{u}\leq\frac{1}{2}b_{+}+c_{+}\,\frac{dG(u)}{du},

(106)

supplemented by the boundary condition

{G(u)\over u}\bigg|_{u=0}=0.

(107)

Rewriting inequality (
106
) as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{du}\left(\frac{G(u)}{u}-c_{+}G(u)\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}b_{+}.

(108)

This can be readily integrated to yield

\displaystyle G(u)\leq\frac{b_{+}}{2}\frac{u^{2}}{1-c_{+}\,u},

(109)

where the boundary condition (
107
) has been taken into account. It is important that for
c_{+}>0
this solution holds only for
u\in[0,\,1/c_{+})
.

Here it is in order to add a remark. In Ref.
[
33
]
, the concepts of the so-called ‘weakly’ and ‘strongly’ self-bounding property of the multivariable function
O
are introduce to obtain an inequality similar to inequality (
106
). In essence, the authors of that work demand bound on quantities such as
\sum_{m}(O-O_{m}^{+})^{2}
and
\sum_{m}(O-O_{m}^{+})
: This condition is much more stringent than that in our treatments.

Now, consider
P_{+}(\epsilon)
. Thanks to
u>0
,

P_{+}(\epsilon)=\mathbf{P}({\rm e}^{u(O-\langle O\rangle)}\geq{\rm e}^{u\epsilon}).

(110)

By Markov’s inequality we obtain

\displaystyle P_{+}(\epsilon)\leq{\rm e}^{-u\epsilon}\langle{\rm e}^{u(O-\langle O\rangle)}\rangle={\rm e}^{-u\epsilon+G(u)}.

(111)

In combination with inequality (
109
), this gives

\displaystyle P_{+}(\epsilon)\leq{\rm e}^{-u\epsilon+\frac{b_{+}}{2}\frac{u^{2}}{1-c_{+}\,u}}.

(112)

For
c_{+}>0
we minimize the exponent on the right-hand side of inequality (
112
):

\displaystyle{\cal E}(u)\equiv-u\epsilon+\frac{b_{+}}{2}\frac{u^{2}}{1-c_{+}\,u}

(113)

in the interval
[0,\,1/c_{+})
. Rewrite it as

\displaystyle{\cal E}(u)=-\left({\epsilon\over c_{+}}+{b_{+}\over c_{+}^{2}}\right)+\left({\epsilon\over c_{+}}+{b_{+}\over 2c_{+}^{2}}\right)y+{b_{+}\over 2c_{+}^{2}}{1\over y},\,\,

(114)

with
y\equiv 1-c_{+}u\in[0,\,1)
. By the mean value inequality,

\displaystyle{\cal E}(u)\geq-{b_{+}\over c_{+}^{2}}\,h_{1}\left({c_{+}\epsilon\over b_{+}}\right)

(115)

with
h_{1}(x)\equiv 1+x-\sqrt{1+2x}
, and the equality holds only if

\displaystyle y=\sqrt{b_{+}\over b_{+}+2c_{+}\epsilon}\in[0,\,1)

(116)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle u={1\over c_{+}}\left(1-\sqrt{b_{+}\over b_{+}+2c_{+}\epsilon}\right)\in[0,\,1/c_{+}).

Thus

\displaystyle P_{+}(\epsilon)\leq{\rm e}^{-{b_{+}\over c_{+}^{2}}\,h_{1}\left({c_{+}\epsilon\over b_{+}}\right)}.

(117)

With the help of the elementary inequality shown in Appendix
C
:

h_{1}(x)\geq{x^{2}\over 2(1+x)},\quad{\rm for}\,x\geq 0,

(118)

we simplify inequality (
117
) to

\displaystyle P_{+}(\epsilon)\leq{\rm e}^{-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2(b_{+}+c_{+}\epsilon)}},

(119)

giving a sub-Gamma upper tail.

For
c_{+}\leq 0
we first simplify inequality (
112
) to

\displaystyle P_{+}(\epsilon)\leq{\rm e}^{-u\epsilon+\frac{b_{+}u^{2}}{2}}.

(120)

The exponent reaches the minimum at
u=\epsilon/b_{+}
. As a result,

\displaystyle P_{+}(\epsilon)\leq{\rm e}^{-{\epsilon^{2}\over 2b_{+}}},

(121)

giving a sub-Gaussian upper tail. In Appendix
D
we shall show that, in fact,
P_{+}(\epsilon)
vanishes for
\epsilon\geq b_{+}/2|c_{+}|
. This is consistent with that an entanglement probe such as
S
has a maximal value. In Sec.
IV.3
we further show that one can actually adjust the value of
b_{+}
properly, so that the sub-Gaussian concentration function is sharp, and the maximal value of an entanglement probe does not play roles practically.

IV.2.2
Lower tail

For the lower tail, we carry out the same analysis with
u<0
. To start we set
O_{m}=O_{m}^{-}
defined as
[
33
]
(cf. Appendix
B
)

\displaystyle O^{-}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\prime}_{m})\equiv\sup_{\varphi_{m}}O(\boldsymbol{\varphi}).

(122)

This ensures that
u(O-O_{m}^{-})
is nonnegative. With the introduction of

\displaystyle b_{-}\equiv\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\left\langle(O-O_{m}^{-})^{2}\right\rangle,

(123)

we can rewrite Eq. (
96
) as

\displaystyle H(u)=\frac{b_{-}}{2}+\delta F_{-}(u),

(124)

similar to Eq. (
101
), where

\displaystyle\delta F_{-}(u)=\left\langle{\rm e}^{u(O-\langle O\rangle)}\right\rangle^{-1}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\Big\langle{\rm e}^{u(O-\langle O\rangle)}\Big(\delta(O-O_{m}^{-})^{2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}{(-u)^{n-2}\over n!}(O-O_{m}^{-})^{n}\Big)\Big\rangle.\qquad\qquad\qquad

(125)

In parallel to inequality (
105
), we further have

\displaystyle\delta F_{-}(u)\leq c_{-}\,\frac{dG(u)}{du},\quad{\rm for}\,u<0.

(126)

From this the inequality:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{du}\frac{G(u)}{u}\leq\frac{1}{2}b_{-}+c_{-}\,\frac{dG(u)}{du}

(127)

follows.

Similar to the derivations of concentration inequalities (
119
) and (
121
), from inequality (
127
) we obtain

\displaystyle P_{-}(\epsilon)\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\rm e}^{-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2(b_{-}+|c_{-}|\epsilon)}},&\textrm{ for\,\, $c_{-}<0$,}\\
{\rm e}^{-{\epsilon^{2}\over 2b_{-}}},&\textrm{ for\,\, $c_{-}\geq 0$.}\end{array}\right.

Similar to
b_{+}
and
c_{+}
for
P_{+}(\epsilon)
, the positive parameters
b_{-}
and
|c_{-}|
here are the variance factor and the scale parameter, respectively.

) with
O=S
, respectively, scaled by an overall factor
3

) for

and that of inequality (

) for

. All curves are generated from

. The parameters are determined as:

,

,

, and

; see Appendix
B
for more details.

IV.3
Boson-fermion universality

Inequalities (
119
), (
121
) and (
IV.2.2
) show that the tail behaviors are determined by the signature of the parameters
c_{\pm}
. At this moment we cannot determine this signature analytically. Thus we resort to numerical analysis detailed in Sec.
VI
. We find that for most the ratio of the subsystem to total system size
r_{A}\equiv L_{A}/L
(except when
r_{A}
approaches 0.5)
c_{+}
is negative, while
c_{-}
is always negative; see Table
1
in Appendix
B
. Having determined the signature of
c_{\pm}
, we can conclude that the upper tail is sub-Gaussian by inequality (
121
) and the lower is sub-Gamma by (
IV.2.2
). That is,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(|O-\langle O\rangle|\geq\epsilon)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\rm e}^{-{\epsilon^{2}\over 2b_{+}}},&\textrm{ for\,\, $O-\langle O\rangle>0$}\\
{\rm e}^{-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2(b_{-}+|c_{-}|\epsilon)}},&\textrm{ for\,\, $O-\langle O\rangle<0$}\end{array}\right..

A natural question is: How tight are these concentration functions? Below we address this question.

We have shown that the sub-Gaussian
P_{+}(\epsilon)
is essentially determined by inequality (
95
) in the regime
0\leq u\lesssim 1/2|c_{+}|
, whereas the sub-Gamma
P_{-}(\epsilon)
by inequality (
95
) in the regime
1/c_{-}\leq u\leq 0
. In Fig.
4
we compare the right-hand side of inequality (
95
), namely,
H(u)
given in Eq. (
96
) with that of (
106
) for
u>0
, and with that of (
127
) for
u<0
, and the moment generating function
\langle{\rm e}^{u(O-\langle O\rangle)}\rangle={\rm e}^{G(u)}
is constructed from the numerically generated
ab initio
data. We find that within the range of interests
H(u)
is pretty close to the right-hand side of inequality of (
106
) for
u>0
, and to that of (
127
) for
u<0
. Moreover, as shown in that figure also, when the left-hand side of inequality (
95
) is multiplied by some numerical constant, it becomes very close to
H(u)
. This implies that one can upgrade the modified logarithmic Sobolev inequality (
95
) to an approximate equality, read

\displaystyle{d\over du}{G(u)\over u}\approx\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{2}\mathfrak{b}_{+}+\mathfrak{c}_{+}\,\frac{dG(u)}{du},&\textrm{ for\,\, $u>0$}\\
\frac{1}{2}\mathfrak{b}_{-}+\mathfrak{c}_{-}\,\frac{dG(u)}{du},&\textrm{ for\,\, $u<0$}\end{array}\right.,

with the numerical constants
\mathfrak{b}_{\pm},\,\mathfrak{c}_{\pm}
satisfying

{\mathfrak{b}_{+}\over b_{+}}={\mathfrak{c}_{+}\over c_{+}}={\mathfrak{b}_{-}\over b_{-}}={\mathfrak{c}_{-}\over c_{-}}.

(137)

Finally, as shown in Fig.
4

H(u)
changes only slightly within the regime
0\leq u\leq 1/|c_{+}|
, and thus we may approximate it as
b_{+}/2
, i.e., set
c_{+}=0
. Taking this and Eqs. (
IV.3
) and (
137
) into account, we arrive at

\displaystyle{d\over du}{G(u)\over u}\approx\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{2}\mathfrak{b}_{+},&\textrm{ for\,\, $u>0$}\\
\frac{1}{2}\mathfrak{b}_{-}+\mathfrak{c}\,\frac{dG(u)}{du},&\textrm{ for\,\, $u<0$}\end{array}\right.

with
\mathfrak{c}\equiv\mathfrak{c}_{-}.

Equations (
IV.3
) can be solved readily, giving for large
\epsilon
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(|O-\langle O\rangle|\geq\epsilon)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\rm e}^{-{\epsilon^{2}\over 2\mathfrak{b}_{+}}},&\textrm{ for\,\, $O-\langle O\rangle>0$}\\
{\rm e}^{-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2(\mathfrak{b}_{-}+\mathfrak{c}\epsilon)}},&\textrm{ for\,\, $O-\langle O\rangle<0$}\end{array}\right.,

which is asymmetric: The upper tail is sub-Gaussian whereas the lower is sub-Gamma. This distribution has been found previously for mesoscopic fluctuations in entanglement dynamics of free fermions
[
32
]
. Thus we have established the boson-fermion universality of the statistical distribution (
IV.3
) in free systems. In fact, that distribution holds in a much broader context. For example, it has been shown and numerically confirmed
[
32
]
that the distribution (
IV.3
) holds for mesoscopic fluctuations in entanglement dynamics of interacting spin chains also. In addition, in an earlier numerical experiment on entanglement dynamics in lattice fermions with interactions, similar temporal fluctuations were observed
[
26
]
. From the technical viewpoint this universality, displayed by statistical distribution of various
O
, arise from two mathematical structures, which are shared by both free bosonic and free fermionic systems: (i) As described by Eqs. (
87
) and (
88
), the probability measure carries a product structure; and (ii) an entanglement probe
O(\boldsymbol{\varphi})
is a highly nonlinear function on the product space
\mathbb{T}^{N}
. These two structures render the observed universality falling into the class of concentration-of-measure phenomena in modern probability theory.

V
Boson-fermion universality of the scaling law of variances

The studies in last section are mute on the properties of the variance of various entanglement probes. In this section we shall show that boson-fermion universality holds for the variance also.

V.1
A general formula of
\boldsymbol{{\rm Var}(S)}

Equation (
IV.3
) show that
\mathfrak{b}_{\pm}
or
b_{\pm}
give the variance of probe
O
. In Ref.
[
32
]
we further showed for free fermion systems that
b_{\pm}\propto\left\langle|\partial_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}O|^{2}\right\rangle
, where the latter
|\partial_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}O|^{2}=\sum_{m}(\partial_{\varphi_{m}}O)^{2}
(the Euclidean norm squared) takes the form of Lipschitz-like constant, in the sense that it is the average of the gradient squared rather than the supremum of the gradient squared used in the concentration-of-measure theory
[
62
,
60
,
63
]
. We then have

{\rm Var}(O)={\cal C}\left\langle|\partial_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}O|^{2}\right\rangle,

(144)

where
{\cal C}
is an overall numerical coefficient found to be
1/2
numerically (see Sec.
VI
).
The derivation of Eq. (
144
) is carried out in Ref.
[
32
]
, and holds for the present system. For completeness we repeat it in Appendix
E
.

To calculate Eq. (
144
) we use Eq. (
83
). Recall that we drop out ‘
\sim
’ in symbols
\widetilde{O}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})
and
\widetilde{C}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})
. In the following we use the entanglement entropy
O=S
as an example, and the results hold for other entanglement probes also. Consider a component of
\partial_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}S
, say
\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}S
without loss of generality. Using the identity shown in Appendix
F
, we find from Eq. (
83
) that

\displaystyle\partial_{\varphi_{m}}S(\boldsymbol{\varphi})=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}_{A}\left[\ln{\left(\frac{C(\boldsymbol{\varphi})+{1\over 2}\,\mathbb{I}_{2L_{A}}}{C(\boldsymbol{\varphi})-{1\over 2}\,\mathbb{I}_{2L_{A}}}\right)}\partial_{\varphi_{m}}C(\boldsymbol{\varphi})\right].

Let us recall discussions in the end of Sec.
III
, according to which
C_{1}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})
is small. So we can expand Eq. (
V.1
) up to the second order to obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{\varphi_{m}}S=(\partial_{\varphi_{m}}S)_{1}+(\partial_{\varphi_{m}}S)_{2},

(146)

where

\displaystyle(\partial_{\varphi_{m}}S)_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}_{A}\left[\ln{\left(\frac{C_{0}+{1\over 2}\,\mathbb{I}_{2L_{A}}}{C_{0}-{1\over 2}\,\mathbb{I}_{2L_{A}}}\right)}\,\partial_{\varphi_{m}}C_{1}\right],

(147)

\displaystyle(\partial_{\varphi_{m}}S)_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}_{A}\left[\left(C_{0}^{2}-{1\over 4}\,\mathbb{I}_{2L_{A}}\right)^{-1}\,C_{1}\,\partial_{\varphi_{m}}C_{1}\right]

(148)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\ldots.

The extra terms
\ldots
in Eq. (
148
) denote the corrections from commutators
[C_{0},C_{1}]
, which do not give different overall scaling behaviors. Hence we drop them from the discussion onwards.
With the substitution of Eq. (
146
), Eq. (
144
) gives three terms. One can show that the crossing term vanishes upon averaging over
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
. As a result,
\textrm{Var}(S)
can be separated into two contributions,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(S)=\operatorname{Var}_{1}(S)+\operatorname{Var}_{2}(S),

(149)

with

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}_{1}(S)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal C}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{N-1}\bigl\langle\bigl((\partial_{\varphi_{m}}S)_{1}\bigr)^{2}\,\bigr\rangle,

(150)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}_{2}(S)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal C}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{N-1}\bigl\langle\bigl((\partial_{\varphi_{m}}S)_{2}\bigr)^{2}\,\bigr\rangle.

(151)

In the following we calculate Eqs. (
150
) and (
151
) separately.

V.2
First term
\boldsymbol{\textrm{Var}_{1}(S)}

Recall that the matrices
C,\,C_{0},\,C_{1}
are defined on two sectors, labelled by the oscillator index
r=1,\ldots,L_{A}
and the
\hat{x}
-
\hat{p}
sector index
\sigma=\bar{A},\bar{B}
. Thus the trace operation in Eq. (
147
) can be cast to
{\rm Tr}_{A}(\cdot)=\sum_{r=1}^{L_{A}}\sum_{\sigma}(\cdot)\equiv\sum_{r=1}^{L_{A}}\operatorname{tr}(\cdot)
. Because the matrix elements
(C_{0})_{r\sigma,r^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}
decay exponentially with the distance
|r-r^{\prime}|
, as confirmed by numerical simulations, the summation over
r
can be divided into the summation over
r
belonged respectively to the bulk and the edge of the subsystem. The bulk has a size
\approx L_{A}
, while the size of the edge is determined by the decay length
L_{e}
of
C_{0}
, where
L_{e}
depends on the parameters of the Hamiltonian. We use
A_{b}
and
A_{e}
to denote the bulk and the edge, respectively, so that
A_{b}=\{r|L_{e}\leq r\leq L_{A}-L_{e}\}
and
A_{e}=\{r|r\leq L_{e}\,\operatorname{or}\,r\geq L_{A}-L_{e}\}
. Taking these into account, we write the trace in Eq. (
147
) as

\operatorname{Tr}_{A}\left[\ln{\left(\frac{C_{0}+{1\over 2}\mathbb{I}_{2L_{A}}}{C_{0}-{1\over 2}\mathbb{I}_{2L_{A}}}\right)}\partial_{\varphi_{m}}C_{1}\right]=T_{b,m}+T_{e,m},

(152)

where

\displaystyle T_{X,m}=\sum_{r\in A_{X}}\sum_{\sigma}\left(\ln{\left(\frac{C_{0}+{1\over 2}\mathbb{I}_{2L_{A}}}{C_{0}-{1\over 2}\mathbb{I}_{2L_{A}}}\right)}\partial_{\varphi_{m}}C_{1}\right)_{r\sigma,r\sigma}\,\,

(153)

with
X=b,\,e
.

For the bulk term
T_{b,m}
, one may extend the oscillator indices involved in the matrix product from the set:
\{1,2,...,L_{A}\}
to
\{1,2,...,L\}
, since
(C_{0})_{r\sigma,r^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}
decays exponentially with
|r-r^{\prime}|
. Thus Eq. (

VII
Conclusions and outlook

In this work, we studied the long-time entanglement dynamics of the coupled harmonic oscillator chain, which is quenched from the ground state and undergoes unitary evolution. We generalized the previous theory for temporal fluctuations in long-time entanglement dynamics of free-fermion models
[
32
]
to the present bosonic model. Despite that the particle statistics is completely different, we found that the statistics of temporal entanglement fluctuations is strictly the same as that in free-fermion models: First, irrespective of entanglement probes
O
and microscopic parameters of the chain, the statistical distribution of entanglement fluctuations is universal. That distribution is asymmetric: Its upper tail gives the sub-Gaussian probability:
{\rm e}^{-{\epsilon^{2}\over 2\mathfrak{b}_{+}}}
for upward fluctuations
\epsilon=O-\langle O\rangle>0
, while its lower tail gives the sub-Gamma probability:
{\rm e}^{-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2(\mathfrak{b}_{-}+\mathfrak{c}\epsilon)}}
for downward fluctuations
\epsilon=\langle O\rangle-O>0
. Second, for distinct probes
O
their variances all obey the scaling law (
182
), which depends only on the (rescaled) subsystem size
L_{A}
and the total system size
L
. In particular, for small
L_{A}\ll L^{1/3}
the scaling law reduces to
\sim 1/L
, which is an edge (between the subsystem
A
and its complement
B
) effect; while for large
(L/2\geq)\,L_{A}\gg L^{1/3}
it reduces to
\sim L_{A}^{3}/L^{2}
, which is a bulk (of
A
) effect. These findings show that temporal entanglement fluctuations differ completely from usual thermodynamic fluctuations.

The found boson-fermion universality has a deep origin, i.e., the fluctuation statistics for entanglement dynamics of both free-boson and free-fermion models arises from common probabilistic structures: (i) As we have shown, despite the out-of-equilibrium nature of entanglement fluctuations, at each instant the many-body wavefunction describes a virtual mesoscopic ‘disordered’ sample, with its ‘disorder realization’ essentially given by
N\gg 1
dynamical phases
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
. Consequently, the temporal fluctuations of entanglement accompanying the wavefunction evolution are statistically equivalent to mesoscopic sample-to-sample fluctuations. (ii) The probability measure of the ensemble of these ‘virtual’ mesoscopic disordered samples has a product structure, which renders entanglement fluctuations displaying the phenomenon of concentration of measure. In addition, on physical grounds the temporal entanglement fluctuations might be attributed to certain fluctuation effects of the motion of the Calabrese-Cardy entangled pairs in a finite system. Since the considerations above are very general, we conjecture that the boson-fermion universality might be further extended to other particle statistics, such as in anyon and supersymmetric systems.

Our theory requires that the pre-quench state to be Gaussian, since the Gaussianity allows us to express an evolving entanglement probe in terms of a functional of the evolving matrix
C(t)
Eq. (
31
). In other words, the probe is completely determined by the instant correlation of
\hat{{\bf r}}
. In this work the theory is formulated for the pre-quench state chosen to be a vacuum state. There are no principal difficulties to generalize it to other pre-quench pure states, provided they are Gaussian. However, it remains challenging to extend the present theory to non-Gaussian pre-quench states, which we leave for future studies.

Quantum entanglement is central to physics of Hawking radiation. Therefore, a rich avenue to explore in the future is the relations of out-of-equilibrium entanglement fluctuations in bosonic models to dynamics of Hawking radiation in analogue black hole systems
[
64
]
, as realized in cold-atomic Bose-Einstein condensates
[
65
]
and superconducting qubits
[
66
]
.

Appendix C
Proof of inequality (
118
)

Using the elementary inequality:
1-{z\over 2}\geq\sqrt{1-z},\,z\geq 0
we find that

\displaystyle 1-{1\over 2}(1-y)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-(1-y)^{2}},

(198)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2y-y^{2}},\quad{\rm for}\,0\leq y\leq 1.

Let us write
y
as
y=1/(1+x),\,x\geq 0
. With its substitution the inequality above is rewritten as

\displaystyle 1-{1\over 2}\left(x\over 1+x\right)^{2}\geq{\sqrt{1+2x}\over 1+x}

(199)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle 1+x-{x^{2}\over 2(1+x)}\geq\sqrt{1+2x},

which is inequality (
118
). This inequality was given in Ref.
[
33
]
without proof.

Appendix D
Discussions on negative
\boldsymbol{c_{\pm}}

In this Appendix we focus on negative
c_{+}
. Discussions on negative
c_{-}
are similar. For
c_{+}<0
we have

\displaystyle P_{+}(\epsilon)\leq{\rm e}^{\widetilde{{\cal E}}(u)},

(200)

where the exponent is given by

\widetilde{{\cal E}}(u)\equiv-u\epsilon+\frac{b_{+}}{2}\frac{u^{2}}{1+|c_{+}|\,u}.

(201)

We wish to minimize
\widetilde{{\cal E}}(u)
for
u>0
. Let us rewrite it as

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\cal E}}(u)=\left({\epsilon\over|c_{+}|}-{b_{+}\over c_{+}^{2}}\right)+\left({b_{+}\over 2c_{+}^{2}}-{\epsilon\over|c_{+}|}\right)y+{b_{+}\over 2c_{+}^{2}}{1\over y}\,\,\,\,\,\,\,

(202)

with
y\equiv 1+|c_{+}|u\geq 1
. Note that

{\rm for}\,\epsilon>{{b_{+}\over 2|c_{+}|}}:\,{b_{+}\over 2c_{+}^{2}}-{\epsilon\over|c_{+}|}<0.

(203)

In this case, we have

\widetilde{{\cal E}}(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u\rightarrow\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}-\infty,

(204)

from which

\displaystyle P_{+}(\epsilon>{{b_{+}\over 2|c_{+}|}})=0

(205)

follows.

Appendix E
The derivation of Eq. (
144
)

In this Appendix we derive Eq. (
144
). The derivations follow closely those in our previous work
[
32
]
. According to Sec.
V
, the variance factor
b_{\pm}
is proportional to
{\rm Var}(O)
. So, we simply have to show that
b_{\pm}\propto\left\langle|\partial_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}O|^{2}\right\rangle
.

For the convenience of the readers let us rewrite here Eqs. (
100
) and (
123
), that defined
b_{\pm}
,

\displaystyle b_{\pm}\equiv\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\left\langle(O-O_{m}^{\pm})^{2}\right\rangle.

(206)

Note that
(O-O_{m}^{\pm})^{2}
is a function of
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
. Let
\varphi_{m}^{\pm}
be the value of
\varphi_{m}
at which
O(\boldsymbol{\varphi})=O^{\pm}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\prime}_{m})
. By the mean value theorem there exists
{\bar{\varphi}}_{m}^{\pm}
between
\varphi_{m}
and
\varphi_{m}^{\pm}
which depends on
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
, so that

(O-O_{m}^{\pm})^{2}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})=(\varphi_{m}-\varphi_{m}^{\pm})^{2}\left(\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O|_{{\varphi}_{m}=\bar{\varphi}_{m}^{\pm}}\right)^{2}.

(207)

Here we use the suppressed notion
\bar{\varphi}_{m}^{\pm}
to stand for
(\varphi_{0},\ldots,\bar{\varphi}_{m}^{\pm},\ldots,\varphi_{N-1})
. We thus obtain

b_{\pm}=\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\left\langle(\varphi_{m}-\varphi_{m}^{\pm})^{2}\left(\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O|_{{\varphi}_{m}=\bar{\varphi}_{m}^{\pm}}\right)^{2}\right\rangle.

(208)

Then we do the Fourier expansion of
\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O
with respect to
\varphi_{m}
. As exemplified by
C(\boldsymbol{\varphi})
in Sec.
III
, the random part of the general expression of
O
decays with the total system size
L
according to certain power law. Thus we can keep the Fourier expansion up to the second order to get

\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O=A_{0}+A_{1}\sin(\varphi_{m}+\phi_{m1})+A_{2}\sin(2\varphi_{m}+\phi_{m2}),

(209)

where
A_{i}
and
\phi_{mi}
are constants independent of
\varphi_{m}
.
With the use of the following relations,

(\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O)^{2}\leq(|A_{0}|+|A_{1}|+|A_{2}|)^{2}\leq 3(A_{0}^{2}+A_{1}^{2}+A_{2}^{2})

(210)

and

\int\frac{d\varphi_{m}}{2\pi}(\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O)^{2}=A_{0}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(A_{1}^{2}+A_{2}^{2}),

(211)

we obtain

(\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O)^{2}\leq 6\int\frac{d\varphi_{m}}{2\pi}(\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O)^{2}.

(212)

This bound suggests that
(\partial_{{{\varphi}}_{m}}S)^{2}
at given
{\varphi}_{m}
is the same order as its average, i.e.,

(\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O)^{2}=r_{m}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})\int\frac{d\varphi_{m}}{2\pi}(\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O)^{2},\quad r_{m}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})={\cal O}(1).

(213)

Substituting it into Eq. (
208
) we obtain

b_{\pm}=\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\left\langle\left((\varphi_{m}-\varphi_{m}^{\pm})^{2}r_{m}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{m}^{\pm*})\right)\int\frac{d\varphi_{m}}{2\pi}(\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O)^{2}\right\rangle,

(214)

where
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{m}^{\pm*}\equiv(\varphi_{0},\ldots,\bar{\varphi}^{\pm}_{m},\ldots,\varphi_{N-1})
. Because the first factor characterizes the distance between
\varphi_{m}
and
\varphi_{m}^{\pm}
and the second describes the
\varphi_{m}
average of
(\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O)^{2}
, we assume that they are statistically uncorrelated and obtain

b_{\pm}=\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\left\langle(\varphi_{m}-\varphi_{m}^{\pm})^{2}r_{m}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{m}^{\pm*})\right\rangle\left\langle\int\frac{d\varphi_{m}}{2\pi}(\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O)^{2}\right\rangle.

(215)

Furthermore, by symmetry
\left\langle(\varphi_{m}-\varphi_{m}^{\pm})^{2}r_{m}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{m}^{\pm*})\right\rangle
must be the same for distinct
m
, at least approximately. As a result, we reduce Eq. (
215
) to

b_{\pm}=R^{\pm}\left\langle|\partial_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}O|^{2}\right\rangle,

(216)

where

R^{\pm}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\left\langle(\varphi_{m}-\varphi_{m}^{\pm})^{2}r_{m}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{m}^{\pm*})\right\rangle

(217)

is an overall numerical coefficient. Combining with the fact that
b_{\pm}\propto{\rm Var}(O)
, we finally arrive at Eq. (
144
).

Appendix F
An identity for the parameter derivative of matrix functions

Let
M(s)
be a matrix depending on the parameter
s\in\mathbb{R}
. We assume that it is
diagonalizable, i.e. there exists a matrix
U
such that

U^{-1}(s)M(s)U(s)=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}d_{1}(s)&&\\
&d_{2}(s)&\\
&&\ddots\\
\end{array}\right]\equiv D(s).

(218)

Note that both
U
and the eigenvalues
d_{i}
depend on
s
in general. From now on we suppress
s
in
M,\,U,\,D
and the eigenvalue spectrum
\{d_{i}\}
in order to make formulas compact.

Consider a generic matrix function
f(M)
, with
f(x)
being differentiable for
x\in\mathbb{R}
. By
Eq. (
218
), we have

\displaystyle f(M)=U\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}f(d_{1})&&\\
&f(d_{2})&\\
&&\ddots\\
\end{array}\right]U^{-1}=Uf(D)U^{-1}.\quad\,\,

Taking the derivative with respect to
s
on both sides, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{s}f(M)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\partial_{s}U)f(D)U^{-1}+Uf(D)(\partial_{s}U^{-1})

(223)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle U(f^{\prime}(D)\partial_{s}D)U^{-1}.

When we take its trace, thanks to the cyclic relation the first two terms cancel out. As a result,

\text{tr}(\partial_{s}f(M))=\text{tr}(f^{\prime}(D)\partial_{s}D).

(224)

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\text{tr}(f^{\prime}(M)\partial_{s}M)=\text{tr}\left(f^{\prime}(UDU^{-1})\partial_{s}(UDU^{-1})\right).

(225)

With the substitution of
f^{\prime}(UDU^{-1})=Uf^{\prime}(D)U^{-1}
and

\partial_{s}(UDU^{-1})=(\partial_{s}U)DU^{-1}+UD(\partial_{s}U^{-1})+U(\partial_{s}D)U^{-1},

(226)

Eq. (
225
) is simplified as

\displaystyle\text{tr}(f^{\prime}(M)\partial_{s}M)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{tr}\Big(f^{\prime}(D)U^{-1}(\partial_{s}U)D

(227)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle Uf^{\prime}(D)D(\partial_{s}U^{-1})+f^{\prime}(D)\partial_{s}D\Big).\quad

By the cyclic relation we find that the first two terms cancel out. Thus Eq. (
226
) reduces to

\text{tr}(f^{\prime}(M)\partial_{s}M)=\text{tr}(f^{\prime}(D)\partial_{s}D).

(228)

Comparing Eq. (
224
) and (
228
), we arrive at the identity:

\text{tr}(\partial_{s}f(M))=\text{tr}(f^{\prime}(M)\partial_{s}M).

(229)

Note that for this identity the trace operation is essential.
