Enterococcus-derived cytolysin exacerbates ischemic stroke by disrupting the blood–brain barrier via NLRP3 activation
Authors: Jia-ni Huang, Wei-hao Zhuang, Yi-si Lin, Jia-xue Xu, Yan-lei Zhang, Jia Li, Meng Huang, Cheng-long Xie, Ruo-ting Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIschemic stroke remains a major global health burden. Enterococcus, which is enriched in the gut of stroke patients, produces cytolysin—a pore-forming toxin that disrupts cell membranes. Gut barrier damage may allow such toxins to enter circulation and impair the blood–brain barrier (BBB). In this study, cytolysin-induced BBB disruption via NLRP3 inflammasome activation was investigated with the aim of identifying new therapeutic targets for stroke.MethodsIschemic stroke patients were enrolled within 48 h of onset at the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University. Rectal swabs were collected for 16 S rRNA sequencing and analyzed via QIIME. To investigate the effects of cytolysin-positive Enterococcus on BBB integrity and NLRP3 activation, a photothrombotic stroke model in C57BL/6J mice and in vitro studies with bEnd.3 cells were performed. Cerebral blood flow, BBB permeability, and intestinal barrier function were assessed using established physiological and molecular assays. NLRP3 pathway activation was evaluated by RNA sequencing, qPCR, Western blotting, and ELISA, and its functional role was further confirmed by administration of the selective NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.ResultsIn a cohort of 100 stroke patients, rectal swabs were collected for 16 S rRNA sequencing and qPCR analysis, and the results identified elevated Enterococcus and cylA gene levels as potential prognostic biomarkers for 360-day mortality. In a photothrombotic stroke mouse model, cytolysin-positive Enterococcus exacerbated stroke severity by increasing BBB permeability. Mechanistically, cytolysin activated nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis, further compromising BBB integrity. Additionally, cytolysin disrupted the intestinal barrier, increased permeability, and may facilitate its entry into the circulation, potentially contributing to BBB damage. Notably, NLRP3 inhibition with MCC950 mitigated neurological deficits and restored BBB integrity.ConclusionCytolysin aggravates ischemic stroke by compromising the BBB via NLRP3 activation and disrupting the intestinal barrier. Targeting cytolysin and NLRP3 holds promise as a therapeutic strategy for neuroprotection during ischemic stroke.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-025-03590-5.Keywords: Ischemic stroke, Cytolysin, Blood–brain barrier (BBB), NLRP3 inflammasome, Neuroprotection

Introduction

Ischemic stroke (IS) remains a significant global health challenge and a leading cause of death and disability [1]. As of 2019, there were over 77 million prevalent cases worldwide, with 7.6 million new cases, 3.3 million deaths, and more than 63 million ischemic stroke-related disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) [2]. Currently, ultra-early intravenous thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy are the only proven reperfusion therapies for acute ischemic stroke (AIS) [3], but their effectiveness is limited by strict time windows, contraindications, bleeding risks, and suboptimal recanalization rates [4]. Given the substantial health burden of AIS, elucidating the underlying mechanisms of IS-related brain injury remains a critical priority for the development of more effective and reliable neuroprotective interventions.

Building on the importance of understanding stroke pathophysiology, recent studies have focused on the gut–brain axis as a critical regulator of stroke outcomes [5], revealing the influence of the gut microbiota and its metabolites on prognosis through neural, endocrine, metabolic, and immune pathways [6], particularly in relation to epithelial barrier integrity [7]. Studies have shown that the abundance of Enterococcus is significantly greater in patients with IS than in healthy individuals and is closely associated with disease severity and prognosis [8, 9]. Enterococcus secretes a pore-forming toxin known as cytolysin, which consists of two bioactive, post-translationally modified peptides, CylLL’’ and CylLS’’ [10]. Cytolysin induces persistent pore formation in target cell membranes, leading to excessive potassium ion efflux and the subsequent activation of the nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome signaling pathway [11].

The NLRP3 inflammasome is a key intracellular complex involved in immune responses to infections and cellular damage [12]. Upon activation, the NLRP3 inflammasome recruits ASC, facilitating the activation of caspase-1, which in turn processes the proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18 into their active forms [13]. Caspase-1 also cleaves gasdermin D (GSDMD), forming membrane pores that trigger pyroptosis and amplify inflammation [14]. This highly pro-inflammatory microenvironment accelerates neuronal cell death [15, 16]. In IS, during which cerebral blood flow cessation leads to neurovascular injury, the subsequent inflammatory response plays a crucial role in secondary damage and prognosis [17]. Thus, targeting the NLRP3 inflammasome may be a promising therapeutic approach for treating ischemic stroke.

On the basis of previous studies and our preliminary experiments, we hypothesize that following ischemic stroke, Enterococcus-derived cytolysin compromises intestinal epithelial integrity, increasing gut barrier permeability and enabling its entry into systemic circulation. This process may subsequently induce pyroptosis in cerebrovascular endothelial cells, thereby exacerbating brain injury. The aim of this study was to investigate how cytolysin disrupts the blood–brain barrier via NLRP3 inflammasome activation, using a photothrombotic stroke mouse model gavaged with cytolysin-positive Enterococcus and in vitro experiments stimulated with cytolysin-containing supernatant. The study revealed that cytolysin worsened ischemic stroke by damaging both the blood–brain barrier and intestinal barrier via NLRP3 activation. By elucidating the potential pathogenic role of Enterococcus in ischemic stroke, this study provides novel therapeutic insights.

Materials and methods

Patient cohort

Patients who were diagnosed with ischemic stroke at the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University were enrolled. The inclusion criteria were a diagnosis of ischemic stroke; age greater than 18 years; onset time less than 48 h; and willingness to participate in research and provide specimens. The exclusion criteria were the use of oral or intravenous antibiotics 3 months before sampling; failure to obtain rectal swabs; other surgeries or invasive examinations three months before sample collection; thrombolysis or thrombectomy after the onset of the disease; epilepsy that affects the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score or neurological deficit symptom assessment; a combined history of intracranial tumors or active malignant tumors; and pregnancy or lactation.

A total of 100 patients were enrolled in our study. Rectal swabs were obtained within 48 h of onset and stored in a −80 °C freezer. The prognosis of each patient was recorded at 30, 90, 180 and 360 days. Our study was approved by the Ethics Committee in Clinical Research (ECCR) of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (number: KY2023-090) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their authorized representatives.

Identification of bacteria by gel electrophoresis

The standard strains of Enterococcus faecalis (#29212 and #700802, ATCC) were thawed, resuspended, stored in a −80 °C freezer, and streaked onto BHI agar medium (110704026, BKMAMLAB). If single-colony formation was insufficient, the bacteria were collected using a filtered pipette tip and resuspended in PBS for bacterial DNA extraction and PCR (Beijing Genes and Biotechnch Co., Ltd.). Finally, agarose gel electrophoresis (Gel Red, D0139, Beyotime) was performed, and the results were visualized using a UV transilluminator (Bio-Techne).

Experimental animals

Healthy male 8-week-old C57BL/6J mice weighing 22–24 g (Charles River, Beijing, China) were used for the experiments. All the mice, which were maintained at the Laboratory Animal Resources Center of Wenzhou Medical University, had free access to water and food, were housed at a comfortable temperature (23–25 °C) and were exposed to 12-hour:12-hour light‒dark cycle. To investigate the effects of cytolysin in vivo, mice were orally gavaged once daily for 14 consecutive days with 5 × 10⁸ colony-forming units (CFU) of either a cytolysin-positive Enterococcus faecalis strain (CylPE.f, ATCC#29212), a cytolysin-negative Enterococcus faecalis strain (CylNE.f, ATCC#700802), or PBS as a vehicle control, as described in previous research [18]. All animal procedures were approved by the Laboratory Animal Resources Center of Wenzhou Medical University (number: wydw2025-0020). The preparation of this manuscript followed the ARRIVE guidelines for reporting animal research.

Photothrombotic (PT) ischemia model

One hundred microliters of Bengal Rose dye was intraperitoneally injected into the mice, and after 30 min, the mice were anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, i.p.). After the fur was removed, the mice were disinfected with iodine solution. An incision was made along the sagittal line of the scalp, the skull was exposed, and the mouse was fixed on a stereotaxic frame. A hole was drilled in the skull using a drill bit with a diameter of 0.5 mm at coordinates (−1.0 mm, −1.0 mm), with the bregma as the zero point. The exposed brain tissue of the mice was exposed to 473 nm blue excitation light emitted by a 40 W laser emitter for 15 min. Then, the incision was sutured, and the mice were placed on a heating pad to maintain body temperature until they were fully awake. The scalp of the mice in the Sham group was cut open, and the skull was drilled before the scalp was sutured. Then, neurological deficits were assessed using two behavioral tests: the balance beam test and the Longa neurological scoring system (Supplementary Table 1).

16 S rRNA analysis

Total genomic DNA was extracted from rectal swabs or mouse stool using an OMEGA Soil DNA Kit (M5635-02) (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions and then stored at −20 °C until further analysis. PCR amplification of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene V3–V4 region was performed using the forward primer 338 F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3’) and the reverse primer 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). The PCR amplicons were purified with Vazyme VAHTSTM DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and quantified using a Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). After the individual quantification step, amplicons were pooled in equal amounts, and pair-end 2*250 bp sequencing was performed using the Illumina platform at Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Microbiome bioinformatics was performed with QIIME2 2022.11 using default parameters, with slight modifications based on the official tutorials. Briefly, raw sequence data were demultiplexed using the demux plugin, followed by primer cutting via the cutadapt plugin. Using the DADA2 plugin, the sequences were then quality filtered, denoised, and merged, and chimera were removed. Nonsingleton amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were aligned with MAFFT and used to construct a phylogenetic tree with fasttree2. Alpha diversity was assessed using indices including Pielou’s evenness and Shannon diversity, while beta diversity was evaluated based on Jaccard distances and visualized via principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). Taxonomy was assigned to ASVs using the classify-sklearn naïve Bayes taxonomy classifier in the feature-classifier plugin against the SILVA Release 138 Database. Differential microbial taxa were identified using linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (with LDA score > 3.7 and Wilcoxon p < 0.05).

Laser speckle contrast imaging of cerebral blood flow (CBF)

CBF was evaluated using a laser speckle flow analyzer (RFLSI III, RWD Life Sciences). After each mouse was anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, i.p.), the skin along the midline of the scalp was cut open to expose the skull, which was then placed under a laser speckle flowmeter. The surgical area at the same location as the contralateral hemisphere was selected to evaluate changes in CBF, and LSCI_V 1.0.0 software (RWD, China) was used for data analysis.

2,3,5-triphenyl tetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining

TTC staining was performed on whole brains to evaluate infarcted areas on day 3 after PI. Mice were anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, i.p.). Brains were incubated in 2% TTC solution (in saline) at 37 °C for 20 min, followed by fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Images were acquired using a digital camera, and infarct areas were quantified using FIJI software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Immunofluorescence staining

After anesthesia with 1% sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, i.p.), the mice were perfused with physiological saline and 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), and the brain and jejunum were collected. After soaking in PFA at 4 °C for 24 h, the samples were soaked in 10% and 30% sucrose solutions for one day until the tissue settled to the bottom of the solution. The samples were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound (4583, Sakura Finetek USA, Inc.), and 10 μm- and 5 μm-thick frozen sections of brain tissue and jejunal tissue were prepared using a cryostat. Immunostaining blocking solution (P0260, Beyotime) was used to block and permeabilize the tissue for 15 min, after which the samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the appropriate primary antibodies. The sections were incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies at 37℃ for 1 h and then with DAPI (C1005, Beyotime) at 37℃ for 5 min, after which they were sealed with Antifade Mounting Medium (Beyotime) and fixed with neutral balsam (C0173, Beyotime). Then, imaging was performed using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, magnification: 20x). Fluorescence intensity and area were measured, and Pearson’s correlation coefficient for co-localization was calculated using FIJI software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

The primary antibodies used were as follows: anti-human/mouse/rat CD31/PECAM-1 antibody (1:500, AF3628, Invitrogen); anti-ZO-1 polyclonal antibody (1:500, 61–7300, Invitrogen); anti-claudin 5 monoclonal antibody (4C3C2) (1:500, >35–2500, Invitrogen); recombinant anti-occludin antibody (1:500, ab216327, Abcam); anti-albumin polyclonal antibody (1:200, 16475-1-AP, Proteintech); anti-IBA1 antibody (1:250, sc-32725, Santa Cruz Biotechnology); and anti-GFAP antibody (1:500, ab68428, Abcam). The secondary antibodies used were as follows: donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) highly cross-adsorbed secondary antibody, Alexa FluorT 488 (1:1000, A-21206, Invitrogen) and donkey anti-goat IgG (H + L) cross-adsorbed secondary antibody, Alexa FluorT (1:1000, A-21447, Invitrogen).

TUNEL staining

A TUNEL Apoptosis Detection Kit (40307ES60, Yeasen) was used for TUNEL staining. Fixed frozen tissue sections were incubated with Proteinase K solution at room temperature for 10 min, equilibrated with equilibration buffer at 37℃ for 5 min, and then incubated with TdT incubation buffer containing YSFluor 488-12-dUTP labeling mix at 37 °C for 1 h. The sections were stained with DAPI (C1005, Beyotime) at 37℃ for 5 min and sealed with Antifade Mounting Medium and fixed with neutral resin. Then, imaging was performed using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, magnification: 20x). Fluorescence intensity and area were measured using FIJI software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Measurement of brain water content (BWC)

The mice were anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital and euthanized by cervical dislocation, and the entire brain was removed and weighed (wet weight). After being placed in a 60 °C oven for 24 h, the brains were removed and weighed again (dry weight). BWC = (wet weight - dry weight)/wet weight.

Evan’s blue staining

As previously described [19], 2% Evans blue dye solution (10 µl/g body weight; E6135, Macklin) dissolved in PBS was injected into the tail vein of each mouse. After 30 min to allow for the dye to circulate, the mice were perfused with PBS through the heart. The surgical hemispheres were separated, weighed, soaked in 500 µL formamide (HY-Y0842, MCE), and ground into a homogenate. The mixture was incubated in a 55 °C water bath for 48 h, centrifuged at 6000 × g at 4 °C for 15 min, and the optical density (OD) was measured at 610 nm.

Cell culture

The mouse brain microvascular endothelial cell line bEnd.3 (iCell Bioscience) was cultured in high-glucose DMEM (iCell-128-0001, iCell Bioscience) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (10099141 C, Gibco) and 0.5% penicillin‒streptomycin solution (15140122, Gibco). The cells were cultured in a 37 °C incubator, and 5% CO2 was added.

Measurement of endothelial permeability in vitro

The method was performed in accordance with a previously reported method and the manufacturer’s instructions [20]. Five thousand cells were resuspended in 200 µl of culture medium and added to the upper chamber of a Transwell (3470, 6.5 mm Transwell with 0.4 μm pore polyester membrane insert, Corning). A total of 500 µl of culture medium was added to the lower chamber until it had just passed through the membrane. After the cells were incubated for 5 days, the culture medium in the upper and lower chambers were removed. Two hundred microliters of FITC-dextran (MW 4000, GC18871, GLPBIO, diluted to 1 mg/ml with PBS) was added to the upper chamber, and 500 µl of PBS was added to the lower chamber. The Transwells were incubated in an incubator for 45 min. One hundred microliters of medium was removed from the upper chamber, and the absorbance was measured (Ex = 495 nm; Em = 525 nm).

RNA sequencing and data processing

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen Life Technologies), after which RNA concentration, quality and integrity were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). Three micrograms of RNA were used as input material for the RNA sample preparation, and then, sequencing libraries were generated. Products were purified (AMPure XP system) and quantified using the Agilent high-sensitivity DNA assay on a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent). The sequencing library was then sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina) at Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co. Ltd.

Differences in the expression of genes were analyzed using DESeq (v1.38.3, default parameters). The R language Pheatmap (v1.0.12, default parameters) software package was used to perform bi-directional clustering analysis of all the different genes in the samples. A heatmap was generated according to the expression level of the same gene in different samples and the expression patterns of different genes in the same sample via the Euclidean method to calculate the distance and complete linkage method for clustering. TopGO (v2.50.0, default parameters) was used to perform GO enrichment analysis of the DEGs (all DEGs/upregulated DEGs/downregulated DEGs). ClusterProfiler (v4.6.0, default parameters) software was used to carry out enrichment analysis of the KEGG pathways of the DEGs. The data were analyzed by using the free online platform Personalbio GenesCloud (https://www.genescloud.cn).

ELISAs

IL-18, IL-1β, CXCL1, CCL2, CCL5 and CXCL10 concentrations were measured using commercially available ELISA kits (Mouse IL-1β ELISA kit, JL18442, J&L Biological; Mouse IL-18 ELISA kit, JL20253, J&L Biological; Mouse CXCL1 ELISA kit, JL20150, J&L Biological; Mouse CCL2 ELISA kit, JL20304, J&L Biological; Mouse CCL5 ELISA kit, JL17335, J&L Biological; Mouse CXCL10 ELISA kit, JL13372, J&L Biological). All reagents were provided with each kit, and ELISAs were performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s manual.

Measurement of D-lactic acid

D-Lactic acid was measured using a commercially available D-Lactic Acid Content Assay Kit (AKAC014M, Beijing Boxbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.). All reagents were provided with the kit, and the experimental steps were performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s manual.

Western blot

The proteins extracted from brain tissue and bEnd.3 cells using RIPA lysis buffer and then electrophoresed and transferred to PVDF membranes. After the membranes were blocked with protein-free rapid blocking buffer (PS108P, Epizyme Biotech) at 37℃ for 10 min, they were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. The primary antibodies used were as follows: anti-gasdermin D (E9S1X) rabbit mAb (1:1000, #39754, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-cleaved gasdermin D (Asp276) rabbit mAb (1:1000, #10137, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-IL-1β rabbit mAb (1:1000, #12507, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-cleaved-IL-1β (Asp117) rabbit mAb (1:1000, #63124, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-caspase-1 rabbit mAb (1:1000, #24232, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-cleaved caspase-1 (Asp296) rabbit mAb (1:1000, #89332, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-ASC rabbit mAb (1:1000, #67824, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-HMGB1 rabbit mAb (1:1000, #6893, Cell Signaling Technology), recombinant anti-IL-18 antibody (1:1000, ab207323, Abcam), anti-NLRP3 antibody (1:1000, DF7438, Affinity Biosciences), anti-beta actin antibody (1:10000, AF7018, Affinity Biosciences), and anti-GAPDH polyclonal antibody (1:10000, AP0063, Bioworld Technology, Inc). The membrane was subsequently incubated with a secondary antibody (anti-rabbit IgG, HRP-linked antibody, #7074; Cell Signaling Technology) at room temperature for 1 h. Immunoreactivity was then detected using SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (#34577; Thermo Scientific). Imaging was performed using the eBLOT (e-BLOT Life Science) imaging system, and quantification was conducted using FIJI software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT‒qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using an RNeasy™ RNA Isolation Kit (R0027, Beyotime Biotechnology) and reverse transcribed into cDNA using qRT‒PCR Master Mix (QPR-200, TOROIVD). RT‒qPCR was performed using qPCR Master Mix (QST-100P, TOROIVD) on a real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Bacterial 16 S rRNA served as the internal control for the cylA gene, while Gapdh mRNA was used as the normalization control in mice. The relative expression level was calculated using the ΔΔCT method. Each real-time PCR was performed three times. The sequences of primers used for RT‒qPCR (Sangon Biotech) are shown in Supplementary Table 2.

Determination of intestinal permeability

After fasting for 8 h, each mouse was orally administered FITC-dextran (MW 4000, GC18871, GLPBIO) at a dose of 600 mg/kg per mouse in the dark. After 2 h, the mice were anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital and euthanized by cervical dislocation, and whole blood was collected and centrifuged in an anticoagulant tube at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, after which the upper plasma layer was retained. One hundred microliters of plasma was used to measure the absorbance (Ex = 495 nm; Em = 525 nm).

Data analysis and statistics

Normality was assessed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Nonparametric data are reported as medians (IQRs) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test or the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc test. Parametric data are presented as means ± SD and analyzed with Student’s t test or one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Univariate analysis of candidate variables was performed using univariate logistic regression analysis and univariate Cox regression analysis. Variables with a two-sided p value of < 0.2 were entered into the multivariate logistic regression to construct the prediction model. The predictive performance for death was assessed by comparing receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and the results are reported as areas under the curves (AUCs). R version 4.4.1 (https://www.r-project.org/) and GraphPad Prism 9 software were used for statistical analysis and figure generation. Two-tailed p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Elevated levels of cytolysin-positive Enterococcus in patients with ischemic stroke can predict adverse outcomes after stroke

We performed 16 S rRNA gene sequencing to investigate whether adverse outcomes in patients with stroke are associated with alterations in the fecal microbiota composition. One-hundred acute ischemic stroke patients were recruited from January 2021 to September 2023 at the Stroke Centre of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University; the inclusion and exclusion processes are detailed in Supplementary Fig. 1. Rectal swab samples were collected from each patient within 48 h of stroke onset. Among the patients, 14 (14.0%) died within 30 days, 20 (20.0%) died within 90 days, 25 (25.0%) died within 180 days, and 33 (33.0%) died within 360 days (Supplementary Table 3). We conducted 16 S rRNA gene sequencing and cylA gene qPCR using 100 rectal swab samples collected within 48 h of stroke onset to investigate whether patient mortality and survival are linked to alterations in the composition of cytolysin-positive Enterococcus. The relative abundance of the genus Enterococcus was significantly greater in 360-day mortality patients (median 13.0%, IQR 3.5%−42.5%) than in 360-day survival patients (median 3.0%, IQR 1.0%−19.0%) (p = 0.0239, Mann‒Whitney U test) (Fig. 1A). The relative expression of the cytolysin exotoxin gene cylA determined by qPCR was significantly greater in 360-day mortality patients (median 6.105, IQR 1.230–19.98) than in 360-day survival patients (median 1.502, IQR 0.3353–5.856) (p = 0.002, Mann‒Whitney U test) (Fig. 1B). ROC analysis revealed the high diagnostic value of the cylA level for mortality (AUC = 0.688, 95% CI: 0.574–0.802, p = 0.002) (Fig. 1C). An improved mortality prediction model combining the level of cylA with age and sex yielded an AUC of 0.787 (AUC = 0.787, 95% CI: 0.689–0.884, p ≤ 0.001) (Fig. 1C). When the cohort was divided into two groups, i.e., low levels of cylA (cylA < median, n = 50) and high levels of cylA (cylA > median, n = 50), there was a significant intergroup difference in 360-day mortality (log rank p = 0.0244, hazard ratio (HR) = 0.4480, 95% CI: 0.1543–1.055) (Fig. 1D). The same trend was also observed between 30-day mortality and 30-day survival, 90-day mortality and 90-day survival, and 180-day mortality and 180-day survival (Supplementary Fig. 2). Logistic regression analyses were performed to investigate the association between cytolysin and mortality (Supplementary Table 4). The relative expression of the cylA gene was significantly associated with 360-day mortality (OR 1.038, 95% CI: 1.008 − 1.068, p = 0.012), and this association remained significant after adjusting for age and sex (OR 1.040, 95% CI: 1.009 − 1.073, p = 0.012). Furthermore, univariate Cox regression analysis indicated that cylA was independently associated with 360-day mortality, a finding that persisted in the multivariate Cox regression model adjusted for age and sex (HR 1.016, 95% CI: 1.004 − 1.027, p = 0.006) (Supplementary Table 5). These results suggest that elevated levels of gut cytolysin-positive Enterococcus in ischemic stroke patients may serve as a potential predictor of poor post-stroke outcomes.

Cytolysin-positive Enterococcus can exacerbate ischemic stroke

We performed 16 S rRNA gene sequencing to determine whether PT model mice have an altered fecal microbiota composition. The fecal microbiota composition was significantly different among PT model mice, Sham mice, and control mice, as indicated by the α diversity, Shannon index (Kruskal‒Wallis p = 0.031) and Pielou_e index (Kruskal‒Wallis p = 0.019, Sham vs. Con Dunn’s test p = 0.04, PT vs. Con Dunn’s test p = 0.033) (Fig. 2A). β diversity analysis revealed significant differences in the microbiota composition among the PT, Sham and control groups (Jaccard-based Adonis test, R2 = 0.233, p < 0.001; PT vs. Sham, Adonis test, p = 0.001; Sham vs. Con, Adonis test, p = 0.002; PT vs. Con, Adonis test, p = 0.004) (Fig. 2B). The E. faecalis content was notably greater in the PT group than in the sham and control groups (Fig. 2C-E). We then identified the cytolysin-positive Enterococcus strains using PCR gel electrophoresis (Supplementary Fig. 3). To determine whether cytolysin contributes to brain damage mediated by E. faecalis, we gavaged mice with CylPE.f, CylNE.f or PBS. The 16 S sequencing results indicated a marked increase in the abundance of Enterococcus within the fecal samples of the mice subjected to Enterococcus gavage (Fig. 2F, Supplementary Fig. 4A-C). Moreover, compared with the CylNE.f-gavaged group, mice gavaged with CylPE.f had significantly higher relative cylA mRNA expression in fecal samples, indicating successful colonization of CylPE.f (Supplementary Fig. 4D). The mice were then grouped and treated with PT or sham according to their assigned groups. The balance beam test results and Longa scores for the PT + CylPE.f group were significantly greater than those for the PT + PBS and PT + CylNE.f groups (all p < 0.05) (Fig. 2G). In the assessment of CBF, PT + CylPE.f mice presented a significantly greater reduction in blood flow within the infarct area than PT + CylNE.f mice did (Fig. 2H-I). However, TTC staining revealed only a mild increase in the PT + CylPE.f group compared with the PT + PBS or PT + CylNE.f groups, which did not reach statistical significance (Supplementary Fig. 5A-B). Apoptotic TUNEL staining was conducted using brain sections from mice. Compared with those in the infarct area of brains from mice in the PT + PBS and PT + CylNE.f groups, the number of apoptotic cells in the infarct area of brains from mice in the PT + CylPE.f groups was greater (Fig. 2J-K). There were no significant differences among the sham + PBS, sham + CylNE.f, and sham + CylPE.f groups (Supplementary Fig. 5C-D). These results suggest that cytolysin significantly exacerbates ischemic brain injury by aggravating neurological deficits and promoting cell apoptosis in the infarcted region.

Cytolysin-positive Enterococcus can exacerbate brain barrier damage

To assess BBB disruption caused by cytolysin, we conducted both in vitro and in vivo studies. Firstly, we examined albumin extravasation in vivo, a critical indicator of BBB disruption, and found markedly higher leakage in the PT + CylPE.f group compared with the PT + PBS and PT + CylNE.f groups (Fig. 3A–B). In contrast, Sham + CylNE.f showed a modest but significant increase versus Sham + PBS (p < 0.05), while Sham + CylPE.f displayed a non-significant trend toward increased extravasation versus Sham + PBS group (p >0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 6A-B). ZO-1 and Claudin-5 are two representative protein indicators of tight junctions, and CD31 is a marker protein for blood vessels [21]. The immunofluorescence images of ZO-1 and Claudin-5 revealed that the tight junctions of the cerebral blood vessels in the Sham group were continuous and intact (Supplementary Fig. 6C-F). The tight junctions of cerebral blood vessels showed partial disruption in the PT + PBS and PT + CylNE.f groups, whereas the PT + CylPE.f group exhibited markedly greater discontinuity of tight junction structures, as confirmed by Pearson’s correlation coefficient analysis of colocalization between CD31 and ZO-1 or Claudin-5 (Fig. 3C-F). Permeability assays using Evan’s Blue extravasation and BWC measurements corroborated these findings. Evan’s blue permeability was significantly greater in mice in the PT + CylPE.f group than in mice in the PT + PBS and PT + CylNE.f groups, while no significant changes were observed in the Sham group (Fig. 3G). Consistently, BWC analysis revealed a significant increase in mice in the PT + CylPE.f group compared with the PT + PBS and PT + CylNE.f groups, with no notable differences in the Sham group (Fig. 3H). Collectively, these findings suggest that cytolysin markedly enhances BBB permeability, thereby compromising BBB integrity during ischemic stroke.

In summary, the cytolysin produced by Enterococcus faecalis can significantly damage the BBB and damage endothelial cells.

Cytolysin activates the NLRP3 inflammasome to mediate the classical pyroptosis pathway in cerebral vascular endothelial cells, damaging the BBB

To further explore the mechanism by which cytolysin damages the BBB, we conducted RNA-seq analysis using the damaged cortices of PT model mice and sham mice that were administered CylNE.f or CylPE.f via gavage. Distinct differences in gene expression were observed within the damaged cortex of mice in the sham group treated with CylNE.f or CylPE.f, with these differences becoming even more pronounced in mice in the PT + CylNE.f and PT + CylPE.f groups (Fig. 4A). GO enrichment analysis revealed a significantly greater number of genes enriched in the biological processes ‘response to external stimulus’ and ‘response to toxic substance’ in the PT + CylPE.f group than in the CylNE.f group (all p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4B). These findings indicate that the damaged cortex of mice in the PT + CylPE.f group indeed experienced increased exposure to external stimuli and toxic substances, thus verifying the impact of cytolysin at the genetic level. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed a significantly greater number of differentially expressed genes in the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway in the PT + CylPE.f group than in the PT + CylNE.f group (Fig. 4C). Therefore, NLRP3 and the pyroptosis pathway may play a role in cytolysin-induced damage to the BBB during ischemic stroke.

To verify whether NLRP3 and the pyroptosis pathway are involved in the damage to the BBB caused by cytolysin in ischemic stroke, we collected blood samples from mice for ELISAs. We found that the levels of IL-18 and IL-1β were significantly increased in mice in the PT + CylPE.f group (Fig. 4D-E). These two cytokines are the main inflammatory factors released during pyroptosis [22], consistent with the trend of the RNA-seq analysis results.

We selected representative proteins (NLRP3, GSDMD, IL-18, and IL-1β) that mediate the classical pyroptosis pathway through the NLRP3 inflammasome. Western blotting was conducted using the damaged cortex of mice, and the results revealed a significant increase in the protein levels of NLRP3, C-GSDMD, IL-18, and IL-1β in mice in the PT + CylPE.f group (Fig. 4F-G). The mRNA levels of Nlrp3, Gsdmd, Il18, and Il1b were also significantly greater in the PT + CylPE.f group than in the PT + CylNE.f group (Fig. 4H). To exclude the influence of other factors in the brain, the same protein and mRNA analyses were conducted using bEnd.3 cells treated with sonicated solutions of medium, CylNE.f, or CylPE.f. The CylPE.f group had significantly elevated protein levels of NLRP3, C-GSDMD, IL-1β and IL-18, as determined by Western blotting (Fig. 4I-J). Similarly, the relative quantification of the mRNA levels detected by qPCR revealed that the levels of Nlrp3, Gsdmd, Il1b, and Il18 significantly increased after stimulation with CylPE.f (Fig. 4K). Therefore, it can be inferred that NLRP3 and the pyroptosis pathway are involved in the process by which cytolysin damages the BBB in ischemic stroke and play a critical role.

Cytolysin damages the intestinal barrier

We speculate that the damaging effects of cytolysin on the BBB originate from its initial disruption of the intestinal barrier, subsequently allowing cytolysin to enter the bloodstream and reach the cerebral circulation. To test this hypothesis, naïve mice were divided into three groups and administered PBS, CylNE.f, or CylPE.f via oral gavage. The results revealed that the plasma D-lactate level, an indicator of intestinal integrity [23], was significantly higher in mice in the CylPE.f group than in mice in the CylNE.f and PBS groups (Fig. 5A). Additionally, FITC-dextran permeability assays revealed a markedly higher plasma FITC-dextran concentration in the CylPE.f group than in the CylNE.f and PBS groups (Fig. 5B). To more clearly demonstrate the impact of cytolysin on the intestinal barrier, immunofluorescence staining and quantification of the tight junction proteins ZO-1 and Occludin were performed. In the PBS group, ZO-1 staining appeared continuous and intact, whereas both the CylNE.f and CylPE.f groups showed disrupted and discontinuous ZO-1 patterns, with the reduction in fluorescence intensity observed in the CylPE.f group (Fig. 5C, D). Similarly, Occludin staining was continuous in the PBS and CylNE.f groups but decreased and fragmented in the CylPE.f group, consistent with the quantification results (Fig. 5E, F). These findings indicate that cytolysin significantly compromises the integrity of the intestinal epithelial barrier, leading to increased intestinal permeability. This disruption may serve as an early event in the broader pathological effects of cytolysin, including its impact on the BBB.

Inhibition of NLRP3 can reverse the brain damage caused by Cytolysin and exert neuroprotective effects

To verify the critical role of NLRP3 and its associated pyroptosis pathway in the exacerbation of ischemic stroke damage by cytolysin, we used MCC950, a specific inhibitor of NLRP3 that effectively suppresses pyroptosis [24]. First, mice were divided into four groups: PT + PBS, PT + PBS + MCC950, PT + CylPE.f, and PT + CylPE.f + MCC950. Before sacrifice, we evaluated the neurological deficits of the mice using the balance beam test and Longa score and found that, compared with that in mice in the PT + CylPE.f group, the degree of neurological deficits in the mice in PT + CylPE.f + MCC950 group was significantly lower (p < 0.05 for balance beam test; and p < 0.01 for Longa score) (Fig. 6A), indicating that inhibiting NLRP3 can significantly reduce the stroke damage caused by cytolysin. To verify the effect of inhibiting NLRP3 on brain cell apoptosis, we performed TUNEL staining. Compared with that in the PT + CylPE.f group, the number of apoptotic brain cells in the PT + CylPE.f + MCC950 group was significantly lower (Fig. 6B-C). In the assessment of CBF, PT + CylPE.f + MCC950 mice exhibited a significantly greater restoration of blood flow in the infarct area compared with PT + CylPE.f mice (Supplementary Fig. 7A–B). In contrast, TTC staining showed only a slight reduction in infarct volume in the PT + CylPE.f + MCC950 group relative to the PT + CylPE.f group, which did not reach statistical significance (Supplementary Fig. 7C-D). Subsequently, the degree of albumin permeability was determined to test BBB function. Compared with those in mice in the PT + CylPE.f group, albumin permeability (Fig. 6D-E) in mice in the groups treated with MCC950 was significantly lower, indicating that inhibiting NLRP3 can reverse the damage caused by cytolysin to the BBB. The BWC (Supplementary Fig. 7E) and Evan’s blue pass rate (Supplementary Fig. 7 F) in mice treated with MCC950 were significantly reduced, indicating that inhibiting NLRP3 can reverse the damage caused by cytolysin to the BBB. The tight junction proteins ZO-1 and Claudin-5 were subsequently analyzed by immunofluorescence staining and quantitative fluorescence analysis (Fig. 6F-I). The results showed a significant reduction in ZO-1 and Claudin-5 expression after CylPE.f gavage, which was partially restored following MCC950 treatment. These results indicate that inhibiting NLRP3 can reverse the brain damage caused by cytolysin and exert neuroprotective effects.

Discussion

Given the increasing recognition of the importance of the gut–brain axis in stroke pathology, the aim of this study was to investigate the role of cytolysin-producing Enterococcus in ischemic stroke progression and its underlying mechanisms, with the goal of identifying potential therapeutic targets. In this study, a clinical cohort was established to investigate the correlation between the prognosis of ischemic stroke patients and the presence of cytolytic Enterococcus. Additionally, an ischemic stroke mouse model characterized by the enrichment of cytolytic Enterococcus was developed to explore the underlying mechanism. The results demonstrated that following ischemic stroke, the enrichment of cytolytic Enterococcus in the intestinal lumen exacerbated neurological deficits, increased neuronal apoptosis, disrupted the intestinal and blood‒brain barriers, and activated the NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis pathway. Notably, these pathological phenotypes were reversed by NLRP3 inhibitors. These findings provide a novel perspective on the interaction between the cytolysin-mediated gut microbiota and ischemic stroke, laying a theoretical foundation for future precision therapeutic strategies targeting the gut microbiota in ischemic stroke.

There is a close association between the gut microbiota and stroke, and its critical role in regulating brain function has garnered increasing attention in recent years [25]. A prospective observational clinical study demonstrated that a combination of viable Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, Enterococcus, and Lactobacillus tablets promote neurological recovery in stroke patients by modulating the levels of NF-κB, IL-1β, and TNF-α [26]. Another prospective case‒control study reported an increased abundance of anaerobic bacteria, including Enterococcus, Peptostreptococcus, Ezakiella, and Escherichia species, in the gut microbiota of patients with cerebral infarction and cerebral hemorrhage [8]. Consistent with previous findings, our clinical cohort exhibited dysbiosis of the gut microbiota, characterized by a significant increase in Enterococcus levels. Notably, a study by Duan et al. published in Nature revealed that cytolysin-positive Enterococcus was markedly enriched in patients with alcoholic hepatitis and was strongly associated with disease severity and increased mortality [27]. Similarly, in our clinical cohort, cytolysin, a major virulence factor of Enterococcus, was an independent risk factor for 360-day mortality in ischemic stroke patients. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that Enterococcus, particularly its secretion of cytolysin, plays a pathogenic role in exacerbating the disease process. The subsequent animal experiments further substantiated this hypothesis. Ischemic stroke mice gavaged with cytolysin-positive Enterococcus exhibited exacerbated neurological deficits, marked reductions in cerebral blood flow within the infarct area, and significantly increased numbers of apoptotic cells in the infarcted region. These results suggest a neurotoxic effect of cytolysin. Similarly, a previous study reported that fecal microbiota transplantation from patients with ST-elevation myocardial infarction worsened post-injury cardiac function in germ-free mice, highlighting the potential harm of specific gut microbiota to the cardiovascular and cerebrovascular systems [28].

The BBB is a vital physiological barrier that preserves central nervous system homeostasis and consists of epithelial cells interconnected by TJs [29]. Disruption of the BBB has been implicated in the pathogenesis of various vascular and neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease [30], vascular cognitive impairment [31], and ischemic stroke [32]. Both broad-spectrum antibiotic treatment and germ-free conditions lead to the significant dysregulation of endothelial TJs [33, 34]. Furthermore, variations in the gut microbiota composition have been associated with differences in BBB integrity and FITC-dextran permeability in mice [35]. These findings suggest that both the presence and specific composition of the gut microbiota play crucial roles in maintaining BBB integrity. In our study, within the pathophysiological context of an ischemic stroke mouse model, gavage with cytolysin-positive Enterococcus led to a significant increase in the BWC and albumin extravasation and increased permeability to Evan’s blue dye, indicating that cytolysin contributes to BBB disruption. Moreover, cytolysin stimulation of brain endothelial cells resulted in increased FITC-dextran permeability, suggesting compromised endothelial barrier integrity. Previous studies have demonstrated that the enrichment of opportunistic pathogens in the gut can lead to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) accumulation, which, through TLR4 activation in microglia, induces endotoxemia and neuroinflammation, ultimately disrupting endothelial TJs and increasing BBB permeability [36, 37]. Additionally, gut microbiota-derived short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) have been shown to modulate BBB permeability [33] and influence the integrity of BBB TJs [38, 39]. These findings suggest that the gut microbiota may compromise BBB function by targeting TJs through metabolic products or endotoxins. Consistent with these findings, our experiments demonstrated that ischemic stroke model mice gavaged with cytolysin-positive Enterococcus showed marked brain TJ disruption, suggesting that the Enterococcus exotoxin cytolysin plays a critical role in BBB endothelial dysfunction. Moreover, in an in vitro brain microvascular endothelial cell model, cytolysin stimulation led to the marked upregulation of pro-inflammatory chemokine expression, further supporting the role of cytolysin in endothelial barrier impairment. Collectively, these in vivo and in vitro findings provide compelling evidence that cytolysin-positive Enterococcus directly interacts with brain microvascular endothelial cells, compromising endothelial TJs and disrupting BBB integrity. Based on our results, we propose that following ischemic stroke, the enrichment of Enterococcus strains, particularly cytolysin-positive strains, may exacerbate brain injury through cytolysin-mediated BBB dysfunction.

To investigate the underlying mechanism of cytolysin-mediated BBB dysfunction, we performed RNA-seq analysis on the infarcted brain region. The RNA-seq results indicated that, within the pathophysiological context of ischemic stroke, cytolysin significantly upregulates the expression of genes associated with the “NOD-like receptor signaling pathway” in brain tissue. NLRP3, a cytosolic pattern recognition receptor (PRR) belonging to the NOD-like receptor (NLR) family, plays a crucial role in innate immune responses [40]. NLRP3 inflammasome pathway activation is a key signaling mechanism that contributes to excessive inflammation [41, 42]. Previous studies have demonstrated that NLRP3 inflammasome activation in neurons drives neuroinflammation in acute ischemic stroke [43] and that the inhibition of NLRP3 activation mitigates hypoxic-ischemic brain injury in neonatal male rats [44]. These findings highlight the critical role of NLRP3 in exacerbating ischemic stroke outcomes. In our study, the cytolysin-positive Enterococcus intervention group exhibited a significant increase in key mRNA transcripts associated with the NLRP3 pathway in brain tissue, along with increased protein expression of key pyroptosis-related markers, including NLRP3, caspase-1, GSDMD, IL-1β, and IL-18. These findings suggest that cytolysin, as a pore-forming toxin, activates the NLRP3 pathway, which is likely a critical mechanism underlying the cytolysin-induced disruption of BBB permeability and exacerbation of ischemic stroke. NLRP3-associated proteins are typically expressed in endothelial cells and microglia, and NLRP3 inflammasome activation can further suppress TJ protein expression, exacerbating BBB damage [45]. In vitro studies have shown that NLRP3 inhibition stabilizes endothelial cell integrity and reduces endothelial cell death under oxygen‒glucose deprivation (OGD) conditions [46, 47], further supporting a close relationship between NLRP3 inflammasome activation and BBB disruption in ischemic stroke. Our in vivo findings demonstrated that the pathological phenotypes observed in ischemic stroke mice gavaged with cytolysin-positive Enterococcus—including exacerbated neurological deficits, elevated apoptotic cell counts, and increased BBB permeability—were effectively reversed by NLRP3 inhibition. Consistently, in vitro experiments further demonstrated that NLRP3 inhibition significantly alleviated endothelial barrier disruption and inflammatory responses inducted by the sonicated supernatant of cytolysin-positive Enterococcus. These findings highlight the pivotal role of NLRP3 activation in mediating cytolysin-driven ischemic brain injury. Collectively, our results, in conjunction with those of previous studies, suggest that the NLRP3 inflammasome acts as a critical mediator linking cytolysin-induced BBB dysfunction, endothelial inflammation, and the progression of ischemic stroke pathology.

Disruption of intestinal barrier function is a common consequence of brain injury [48, 49] and is typically characterized by increased intestinal permeability [50]. Studies have shown that middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) in a rat model results in a widespread reduction in the number of TJs between intestinal epithelial cells [51, 52]. Similarly, murine models of ischemic stroke exhibit elevated levels of inflammatory proteins in the small intestine, reduced TJ protein expression in ileal epithelial cells, and significantly increased intestinal permeability [53]. Our findings revealed that cytolysin disrupts intestinal TJs and increases intestinal permeability, highlighting its detrimental effect on intestinal barrier integrity. The impact of Enterococcus on intestinal barrier function remains controversial. A study suggested that the metalloproteinase GelE, produced by Enterococcus, compromises intestinal epithelial barrier integrity [54], which aligns with our results. Conversely, other studies have indicated that Enterococcus can reduce oxidative stress in porcine intestinal epithelial cells and decrease intercellular permeability [55, 56]. Additionally, Enterococcus has been reported to maintain intestinal integrity in Ross roosters by regulating Escherichia coli O78 [57], findings that contradict our results. We propose that these discrepancies may stem from multiple factors, including genotypic and phenotypic variations among Enterococcus strains [58], differences in experimental conditions (e.g., animal models and culture conditions) [59, 60], the complexity of the gut microbiota [61], and variations in host genetic background and immune status [62, 63]. Furthermore, it is conceivable that certain metabolites produced by Enterococcus could exert concentration-dependent dual effects, potentially supporting barrier function at low concentrations via immunomodulatory or antioxidative pathways while impairing barrier integrity at relatively high concentrations. Therefore, the impact of Enterococcus on the intestinal barrier is likely multifactorial, warranting further investigation to elucidate the specific mechanisms and conditions governing these effects. Despite these conflicting findings, our study provides evidence that Enterococcus disrupts intestinal barrier function through cytolysin secretion. Furthermore, disruptions in intestinal function can further disturb immune homeostasis, establishing a bidirectional feedback loop along the gut‒brain axis that sustains and exacerbates neurological dysfunction [64]. For example, Akkermansia muciniphila has been shown to modulate the gut‒brain axis by maintaining the intestinal mucosal barrier [65], whereas probiotic diets can restore intestinal barrier function and alleviate migraines via gut‒brain axis modulation [66]. Integrating our findings with those of previous studies, we propose that ischemic stroke leads to an enrichment of Enterococcus in the intestinal lumen and a corresponding increase in cytolysin levels, initiating a top-down influence along the gut‒brain axis. In turn, elevated cytolysin exacerbates intestinal barrier dysfunction, facilitating the translocation of bacterial exotoxins into the bloodstream and further compromising the BBB. This establishes a bottom-up feedback loop that amplifies neurological damage and worsens ischemic stroke pathology.

The present study has several strengths and unique aspects. First, in this study, we innovatively investigated the role of Enterococcus and its exotoxin cytolysin in ischemic stroke. We elucidated the bridging role of the exotoxin cytolysin between gut dysbiosis and neurological damage, providing a novel perspective for microbiota-related research. Second, through this study, we clarified the mechanism by which cytolysin induces brain injury, revealing its involvement in pyroptosis via the NLRP3 signaling pathway. This study deepens the understanding of the toxic effects of cytolysin and provides new insights into microbiota-targeted interventions, such as bacteriophage or biological antibody therapies, laying a theoretical foundation for precision treatment. Third, we optimized an animal model to increase the clinical translational value of the results. The widely used MCAO model often results in high mortality and extensive infarcts [67], which do not accurately reflect the prevalence of minor and moderate strokes in clinical settings. By employing a PT stroke model to induce small infarcts, we achieved a closer alignment with clinical reality, notably improving the translational potential of our findings.

However, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of the present study. First, the conclusions have not yet been validated in large-scale clinical cohorts, and future research will aim to enhance clinical applicability by increasing the sample size or conducting multicenter studies. Second, the synthesis and purification of cytolysin, a small peptide exotoxin, present considerable technical challenges. Owing to these constraints, we were unable to obtain purified cytolysin for in vitro stimulation experiments. Additionally, the lack of specific anti-cytolysin antibodies precluded both antagonistic validation experiments and the measurement of cytolysin levels in serum or cerebral spinal fluid. To overcome these challenges, future efforts will prioritize optimizing cytolysin synthesis, purification, and antibody development, potentially through collaboration with Professor Duan Yi or specialized biological laboratories. Third, while this study revealed that cytolysin induces brain injury via the NLRP3 pathway, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying its effect on NLRP3 remain unclear. Future investigations will employ in vitro models alongside advanced proteomic and epigenetic analyses, such as the phosphorylation and acetylation profiling of NLRP3, to provide deeper molecular insights. Despite these limitations, this study offers a strong foundation for further research and contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms linking gut microbiota-derived cytolysin to ischemic brain injury.

In conclusion, the role of Enterococcus and its exotoxin cytolysin in ischemic stroke were investigated in this study. The findings reveal that cytolysin exacerbates ischemic brain injury by disrupting TJs in brain microvascular endothelial cells, which increases BBB permeability. Animal experiments revealed that cytolysin activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, further impairing BBB integrity and worsening brain damage, and in vitro studies confirmed its direct endothelial toxicity and pro-inflammatory effects via NLRP3 activation. Additionally, cytolysin compromised intestinal barrier function by disrupting intestinal epithelial TJs. Notably, NLRP3 inhibition effectively mitigated cytolysin-induced stroke exacerbation, highlighting its therapeutic potential. This study is the first to establish the bridging role of cytolysin in post-stroke outcomes using both in vivo and in vitro models, providing critical insights into gut microbiota‒stroke interactions and offering new avenues for drug discovery and targeted interventions. Future studies should explore the therapeutic potential of targeting cytolysin and the gut–brain axis, which may inform precision strategies for stroke treatment.
