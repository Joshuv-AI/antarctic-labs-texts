Soft inductive limits of operator systems and a noncommutative Lazar-Lindenstrauss theorem
Kristin Courtney, Niklas Galke, Lauritz van Luijk, Alexander Stottmeister

Abstract
We establish a flexible generalization of inductive systems of operator systems, which relaxes the usual transitivity (or coherence) condition to an asymptotic version thereof and allows for systems indexed over arbitrary nets. To illustrate the utility of this generalization, we highlight how such systems arise naturally from completely positive approximations of nuclear operator systems. Going further, we utilize an argument of Ding and Peterson to show that a separable operator system is nuclear if and only if it is an inductive limit of matrix algebras, generalizing a classic Theorem of Lazar and Lindenstrauss to the setting of noncommutative Choquet theory.

Soft inductive systems

Soft inductive systems were introduced in
[
47
]
as a flexible generalization of inductive systems.
The core difference to inductive limits is that the transitivity condition
j_{nm}j_{ml}=j_{nl}
,
n<m<l
, is relaxed to asymptotic transitivity.
Like inductive systems, they can be formulated in various categories and are compatible with additional structures.
We briefly summarize the framework here and refer to
[
47
]
for more details.
Since we want to generalize to the category of operator systems (where it is desirable not to assume norm-completeness), we start by discussing soft inductive systems of normed spaces.
We will then consider the categories of operator systems and
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras.

This is indeed the case because the limits of basic nets are dense in both limit spaces
E_{\infty}

∎

Remark 7
.

In
[
3
, Remark 2.1.4]
soft inductive systems are alluded to under the name of asymptotically coherent systems.
When the index is countable, it is sometimes useful to consider a stronger notion:
A soft inductive system
(E,j)
which is indexed over
\mathbb{N}
is
summably soft
if there exists some decreasing sequence
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n}\in\ell^{1}(\mathbb{N})_{+}^{1}
such that

\displaystyle\|j_{nk}-j_{nm}\circ j_{mk}\|<\varepsilon_{m},\ \forall\ n>m>k\geq 0.

Now, suppose
(E,j)
is a summably soft inductive system of vector spaces. We define a strict system
(E,{\tilde{\jmath}})
from
(E,j)
as follows: for all
n>m\geq 0
set

\displaystyle{\tilde{\jmath}}_{nm}

\displaystyle\coloneqq j_{n,n-1}\circ\ldots\circ j_{m+1,m},

\displaystyle{\tilde{\jmath}}_{mn}

\displaystyle\coloneqq 0,\ \text{ and}

\displaystyle{\tilde{\jmath}}_{nn}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathrm{id}_{E_{n}}.

It follows from summability that for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists an
M>0
such that for all
n>m>M
,

\|{\tilde{\jmath}}_{nm}-j_{nm}\|<\varepsilon.

In particular,
\|{\tilde{\jmath}}_{\infty n}-j_{\infty n}\|<\varepsilon
for each
n>M
.
Then

\overline{\bigcup{\tilde{\jmath}}_{\infty n}(E_{n})}=\overline{\bigcup j_{\infty n}(E_{n})}\subset{\mathbf{Q}}(E_{{\hskip 0.0pt{\mathbin{{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{$\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-2.5)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,$}}}}}\hskip 0.0pt}{\hskip-1.0pt}}),

and we denote both with
E^{\prime}_{\infty}
.

If
(E,j)
is a summably soft system of finite-dimensional Banach spaces with countable index, we can use a compactness argument to pass to an asymptotically soft subsystem whose limit is isometrically isomorphic to
E^{\prime}_{\infty}
by Lemma
6
.

2.2
Operator systems

We now turn to soft inductive limits of operator systems, by which we mean
abstract
operator systems in the sense of
[
61
]
. Note that an operator system
\mathcal{S}
is assumed to be unital, and we denote the unit by
1
(or
1_{\mathcal{S}}
if more context is needed).
The vector space of
\nu\times\nu
matrices with entries in
\mathcal{S}
will be referred to as a matrix amplification of
\mathcal{S}
and is denoted
M_{\nu}(\mathcal{S})
, and we use the shorthand
M_{\nu}=M_{\nu}(\mathbb{C})
.
Elements of
M_{\nu}(\mathcal{S})
will be denoted
[a_{ij}]_{ij}
or just by
[a_{ij}]
with
a_{ij}\in\mathcal{S}
.
Of course,
M_{\nu}(\mathcal{S})
is the same as the (algebraic) tensor product
M_{\nu}\otimes\mathcal{S}
.
The standard matrix units of
M_{\nu}
will be denoted
E_{ij}
,
i,j=1,\ldots,\nu
so that we have
[a_{ij}]=\sum_{ij}a_{ij}\otimes E_{ij}
.
If
\alpha:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{T}
is a completely positive map between operator systems, we denote its matrix amplification by
\alpha^{(\nu)}:=\alpha\otimes\id_{\nu}
which acts by mapping
[a_{ij}]
to the matrix
[\alpha(a_{ij})]
.
We always assume operator systems to be equipped with their order norm, i.e.,

\lVert a\rVert=\inf\left\{\lambda>0\nonscript\ \middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\ \mathopen{}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda 1&a\\
a^{*}&\lambda 1\end{bmatrix}\geq 0\right\}.

(15)

On self-adjoint elements, this reduces to

\lVert a\rVert=\inf\left\{\lambda>0\nonscript\ \middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\ \mathopen{}-\lambda 1\leq a\leq\lambda 1\right\}.

(16)

In particular, operator systems are normed spaces, and unital completely positive (ucp) maps are linear contractions, cf.
[
61
, Prop. 3.6]
.
We do not require operator systems to be complete with respect to the order norm.

Definition 8
.

A
soft inductive system in the operator system category

(\mathcal{S},j)
over a directed set
(N,\leq)
is a soft inductive system of normed spaces such that each
\mathcal{S}_{n}
is an operator system and each
j_{nm}
is a ucp map. The system is called (completely) isometric when all the
j_{nm}
are.

We let
(\mathcal{S},j)
be a soft inductive system of operator systems. Then the uniformly bounded nets
{\mathbf{N}}(\mathcal{S}_{{\hskip 0.0pt{\mathbin{{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{$\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-2.5)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,$}}}}}\hskip 0.0pt}{\hskip-1.0pt}})
form an operator system, namely the direct product
\prod_{n\in N}\mathcal{S}_{n}
:
The adjoint and the vector space structure are defined
n
-wise, the order unit is
1_{{\hskip 0.0pt{\mathbin{{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{$\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-2.5)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,$}}}}}\hskip 0.0pt}{\hskip-1.0pt}}
, and the positive cone is
{\mathbf{N}}(\mathcal{S}_{{\hskip 0.0pt{\mathbin{{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{$\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-2.5)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,$}}}}}\hskip 0.0pt}{\hskip-1.0pt}})^{+}=\{a_{{\hskip 0.0pt{\mathbin{{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{$\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-2.5)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,$}}}}}\hskip 0.0pt}{\hskip-1.0pt}}\in{\mathbf{N}}(\mathcal{S}_{{\hskip 0.0pt{\mathbin{{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{$\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-2.5)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,$}}}}}\hskip 0.0pt}{\hskip-1.0pt}})\nonscript\ |\allowbreak\nonscript\ \mathopen{}a_{n}\geq 0\ \forall n\}

On the matrix amplifications, the positive cones are given through the natural isomorphism

(17)

It immediately follows from

that, for any

-convergent net

Furthermore, by unitality of the connecting maps, the net of order units

Thus, the

Proposition 10

(Universal property of the quotient operator system
[
40
, Prop. 3.6]
)
.

Every unital completely positive map
\varphi:\mathcal{T}_{1}\to\mathcal{T}_{2}
that vanishes on
{\mathcal{J}}
,
{\mathcal{J}}\subset\ker(\varphi)
, factors through the quotient.
That is, the map
\hat{\varphi}:\mathcal{T}_{1}/{\mathcal{J}}\to\mathcal{T}_{2}
with
\hat{\varphi}(x+{\mathcal{J}})=\varphi(x)
is well-defined, unital and completely positive.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be an operator system and
\psi:\mathcal{T}_{1}\to\mathcal{R}
a unital completely positive map.
If it holds that for every unital completely positive map
\varphi:\mathcal{T}_{1}\to\mathcal{T}_{2}
with
{\mathcal{J}}\subset\ker(\varphi)
there exists a unique unital completely positive map
\hat{\varphi}:\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{T}_{2}
such that
\varphi=\hat{\varphi}\circ\psi
then there exists a unique complete order isomorphism
\gamma:\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{T}_{1}/{\mathcal{J}}
such that
\gamma\circ\psi
is the canonical quotient map.

Corollary 11
.

Every contractive completely positive map
\varphi:\mathcal{T}_{1}\to\mathcal{T}_{2}
that vanishes on
{\mathcal{J}}
,
{\mathcal{J}}\subset\ker(\varphi)
, factors through the quotient.
That is, the map
\hat{\varphi}:\mathcal{T}_{1}/{\mathcal{J}}\to\mathcal{T}_{2}
with
\hat{\varphi}(x+{\mathcal{J}})=\varphi(x)
is well-defined, contractive and completely positive.

Proof.

Let
\varphi:\mathcal{T}_{1}\to\mathcal{T}_{2}
be a contractive completely positive map. Then we can view
\varphi
as a unital completely positive map
\mathcal{T}_{1}\to\varphi(\mathcal{T}_{1})=:\mathcal{T}
where we choose
\varphi(1_{\mathcal{T}_{1}})=:1_{\mathcal{T}}
as the order unit of
\mathcal{T}
.
The universal property Proposition
10
then gives existence of a unique unital completely positive map
\hat{\varphi}:\mathcal{T}_{1}/{\mathcal{J}}\to\mathcal{T}
necessarily given by
\hat{\varphi}(x+{\mathcal{J}})=\varphi(x)
.
Note that, since
\varphi
is contractive, it holds that
\lVert 1_{\mathcal{T}}\rVert_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}\leq\lVert 1_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}\rVert_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}=1=\lVert 1_{\mathcal{T}}\rVert_{\mathcal{T}}
, so the embedding
\mathcal{T}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{T}_{2}
is contractive.
Thus, viewing
\hat{\varphi}
as a map
\mathcal{T}_{1}/{\mathcal{J}}\to\mathcal{T}_{2}
gives a completely positive contraction.

\psi_{\mathcal{S}}:\mathcal{S}\to{\mathbf{N}}(\mathcal{A}_{{\hskip 0.0pt{\mathbin{{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{$\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-2.5)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,$}}}}}\hskip 0.0pt}{\hskip-1.0pt}})
so that
[\psi_{\mathcal{S}}(a)]=a
for all

. Composing these with the projections
{\mathbf{N}}(\mathcal{A}_{{\hskip 0.0pt{\mathbin{{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{$\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-2.5)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,$}}}}}\hskip 0.0pt}{\hskip-1.0pt}})\to\mathcal{A}_{n}

(where

is a full matrix algebra containing

). By Arveson’s extension theorem, these extend to ucp maps

∎

3
Nuclearity

Originally, a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra was said to be nuclear when its algebraic tensor product with any other
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra admits a unique
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-norm, or equivalently, that the maximum and minimum tensor product norms agree.
In the 70’s, Choi and Effros and (independently) Kirchberg characterized nuclearity for
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras in terms of a completely positive approximation property:

Theorem 22

(Choi–Effros/Kirchberg)
.

A
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
is nuclear if there exists a net of cpc maps
\mathcal{A}\xrightarrow{\psi_{n}}M_{\nu_{n}}\xrightarrow{\varphi_{n}}\mathcal{A}
such that
\varphi_{n}\circ\psi_{n}
converge pointwise in norm to the identity map on
\mathcal{A}
. When
\mathcal{A}
is separable, the index can be taken to be
\mathbb{N}
.

Following this, in
[
43
]
, Kirchberg defined nuclearity for operator systems using a similar property: a separable operator system
\mathcal{S}
is
nuclear
if there are completely positive contractions
\varphi_{n}:\mathcal{S}\to M_{\nu_{n}}
and
\psi_{n}:M_{\nu_{n}}\to\mathcal{S}
such that
\varphi_{n}\circ\psi_{n}\to\id_{\mathcal{S}}
in the point-norm topology.
6
6

6

The topology on
\mathcal{S}
is inherited from its order norm, or equivalently from its enveloping
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\mathrm{C}^{*}_{\text{min}}(\mathcal{S})
. Note that
\mathcal{S}
need not be complete for this definition.

Remark 23
.

In
[
31
, Theorem 3.1]
, Han and Paulsen show that Kirchberg’s definition of nuclearity agrees with (max,min)-nuclearity of
[
39
]
, i.e., an operator system
\mathcal{S}
is nuclear if and only if its minimal tensor product with an arbitrary operator system agrees with the maximal tensor product.

The system of completely positive approximations from Theorem
22
induces a soft inductive system of operator systems
(\mathcal{S},j)
with
\mathcal{S}_{n}\coloneqq M_{\nu_{n}}
and
j_{nm}\coloneqq\psi_{n}\circ\varphi_{m}
, which we call the
induced soft system
.
Indeed, one has for each
l\in N
and
a_{l}\in\mathcal{S}_{l}

\lVert(j_{nl}-j_{nm}j_{ml})a_{l}\rVert\leq\lVert\psi_{n}\rVert\lVert(\id_{\mathcal{S}}-\varphi_{m}\psi_{m})\varphi_{l}(a_{l})\rVert\xrightarrow{n\gg m}0.

Note that, by construction, the induced soft system is split, where the right inverse to
j\mkern-1.0mu\hbox{-}\mkern-3.0mu\mathop{\textup{lim}}
is
\varphi_{{}_{{\hskip 0.0pt{\mathbin{{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{$\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-2.5)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,$}}}}}\hskip 0.0pt}{\hskip-1.0pt}}}

.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a nuclear operator system, then
\mathcal{S}
is a soft inductive limit of matrix algebras, i.e., there is a soft inductive system
(\mathcal{S},j)
with each

being a matrix algebra so that
\mathcal{S}
is its limit space.
In fact, the soft inductive system can be chosen to be split.
If
\mathcal{S}
is separable, then the directed system can be chosen to be
N=\mathbb{N}
, and the soft inductive system can be arranged to be strict.

Proof.

Given a net of completely positive contractions

and
\psi_{n}:M_{\nu_{n}}\to\mathcal{S}
such that
\varphi_{n}\circ\psi_{n}
converges point norm to
\id_{\mathcal{S}}
, we form the induced soft inductive system

(\mathcal{S},j)
as above. The limit space
\mathcal{S}_{\infty}
is completely order isomorphic with
\mathcal{S}
via
a\mapsto j\mkern-1.0mu\hbox{-}\mkern-3.0mu\mathop{\textup{lim}}_{n}\psi_{n}(a)
(the inverse is

With this identification of
\mathcal{S}
and

When
\mathcal{S}
is separable,
the index set can be chosen to be

{\tilde{\jmath}}_{nm:}=\psi_{n}\circ\varphi_{n-1}\circ\ldots\circ\psi_{m+1}\circ\varphi_{m}:M_{\nu_{m}}\to M_{\nu_{n}}

∎

An argument for the converse is essentially contained in the proof of

C^{*}

Lemma 28
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a
C^{*}
-algebra with two-sided closed ideal
{\mathcal{J}}
, and let
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{A}
be an operator system which contains
{\mathcal{J}}
. Then
(\mathcal{S}/{\mathcal{J}})^{**}
is completely order isomorphic to
p^{\perp}\mathcal{S}^{**}
where
p
is the central support projection of
{\mathcal{J}}
. (In fact, we have
\mathcal{S}^{**}\cong{\mathcal{J}}^{**}\oplus(\mathcal{S}/{\mathcal{J}})^{**}
.)

Proof.

For each
\varphi\in S(\mathcal{A})
, write
(\sigma_{\varphi},H_{\varphi},\xi_{\varphi})
for the associated cyclic GNS representation. Write
H_{\mathcal{A}}:=\bigoplus_{\varphi\in S(\mathcal{A})}H_{\varphi}
and let

\sigma_{u}:\mathcal{A}\to B(H_{\mathcal{A}})
be the universal representation for
\mathcal{A}
. Since
{\mathcal{J}}
is an ideal in
\mathcal{A}
, its essential support
\overline{\sigma_{u}({\mathcal{J}})H_{\mathcal{A}}}
is invariant under
\sigma_{u}(\mathcal{A})
, and so its orthogonal projection
p
(which is the SOT limit of an increasing approximate identity in
{\mathcal{J}}
) lies in
\sigma_{u}(\mathcal{A})^{\prime}\cap\sigma_{u}(\mathcal{A})^{\prime\prime}
. Hence we have

\sigma_{u}(\mathcal{A})^{\prime\prime}=p\sigma_{u}(\mathcal{A})^{\prime\prime}\oplus p^{\perp}\sigma_{u}(\mathcal{A})^{\prime\prime}.

Note that the orthogonal complement of
\overline{\sigma_{u}({\mathcal{J}})H_{\mathcal{A}}}
is
\bigoplus_{{\mathcal{J}}\subset\text{ker}(\sigma_{\varphi}),\varphi\in S(\mathcal{A})}H_{\varphi}
(i.e., the largest subspace on which
\sigma_{u}(x)=0
for all
x\in{\mathcal{J}}
).

We can identify
\mathcal{A}/{\mathcal{J}}
with
p^{\perp}\sigma_{u}(\mathcal{A})
via the map
\pi:\mathcal{A}/{\mathcal{J}}\to B(p^{\perp}H_{\mathcal{A}})
given by
\pi(a+{\mathcal{J}})=\oplus_{{\mathcal{J}}\subset\text{ker}(\sigma_{\varphi}),\varphi\in S(\mathcal{A})}
. Since every (GNS) representation of
\mathcal{A}/{\mathcal{J}}
extends to a (GNS) representation of
\mathcal{A}
which vanishes on
{\mathcal{J}}
(by composing with the quotient map), we can identify
\pi(\mathcal{A}/{\mathcal{J}})
with the image of
\mathcal{A}/{\mathcal{J}}
under its universal representaion. In particular, that means we can identify
(\mathcal{A}/{\mathcal{J}})^{**}\cong\pi(\mathcal{A}/{\mathcal{J}})^{\prime\prime}=p^{\perp}\sigma_{u}(\mathcal{A})^{\prime\prime}
. We can also write
p\sigma_{u}(\mathcal{A})^{\prime\prime}\cong{\mathcal{J}}^{**}
(though that is not important here), and, by identifying
\sigma_{u}(\mathcal{A})^{\prime\prime}\cong\mathcal{A}^{**}
, we have

\mathcal{A}^{**}\cong p\mathcal{A}^{**}\oplus p^{\perp}\mathcal{A}^{**}\cong{\mathcal{J}}^{**}\oplus(\mathcal{A}/{\mathcal{J}})^{**}.

Since
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{A}
, we have
p^{\perp}\mathcal{S}^{**}\subset p^{\perp}\mathcal{A}^{**}
. We just need to argue that
p^{\perp}\mathcal{S}^{**}
is mapped to
(\mathcal{S}/{\mathcal{J}})^{**}
under the identification
p^{\perp}\mathcal{A}^{**}\cong(\mathcal{A}/{\mathcal{J}})^{**}
. Since
p^{\perp}\mathcal{S}=\pi(\mathcal{S}/{\mathcal{J}})
, we know that
p^{\perp}\mathcal{S}
gets sent to
\mathcal{S}/{\mathcal{J}}
in this identification. Since
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}/{\mathcal{J}}
are ultraweakly (wk
∗
) dense in
\mathcal{S}^{**}
and
(\mathcal{S}/{\mathcal{J}})^{**}
, respectively, and since multiplication by a central projection and the above identification
p^{\perp}\mathcal{A}^{**}\cong(\mathcal{A}/{\mathcal{J}})^{**}
are ultraweakly (wk
∗
) continuous, it follows that

p^{\perp}\mathcal{S}^{**}
gets sent to
(\mathcal{S}/{\mathcal{J}})^{**}
under the identification
p^{\perp}\mathcal{A}^{**}\cong(\mathcal{A}/{\mathcal{J}})^{**}
. Since this identification is a
∗
-isomorphism, its restriction and co-restriction gives a complete order isomorphism between
(\mathcal{S}/{\mathcal{J}})^{**}
and
p^{\perp}\mathcal{S}^{**}
.

∎

.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a

{\mathcal{J}}

Proof.

It follows from

∎

3.1
Noncommutative Choquet theory

In this section, we provide an interpretation of Theorem
27
in the context of noncommutative Choquet theory. We start by surveying the relevant classical results and framework and followed by a brief account of the noncommutative Choquet theory of Davidson and Kennedy (
[
14
,
16
]
).

For a compact, convex, metrizable set
K
, we consider the
∗
-vector space of its continuous
\mathbb{C}
-valued affine functions
A(K)
, which we view as an operator subsystem of
C(K)
. Through Kadison duality over
\mathbb{C}

[
60
]
, we have an affine homeomorphism between
S(A(K))
and
K
via point evaluations.
Numerous authors have characterized when
K
is a Choquet simplex via properties of
K
, and as observed in
[
14
, Section 4]
, these have nice formulations in our setting as well. In particular, Namioka and Phelps
[
54
]
proved that
K
is a simplex precisely when
A(K)
is nuclear in the function system category (meaning there is a unique Archimedean order unit space structure that one can put on its tensor product with any other function system), and Effros
[
22
]
proved that
K
is a simplex if and only if
A(K)^{**}
is injective in that category. It follows from
[
35
]
and
[
17
]
(independently) that the projective limit of finite-dimensional simplices with affine connecting maps is a simplex, and in
[
46
]
, Lazar and Lindenstrauss proved that the converse also holds (Corollary to
[
46
, Theorem 5.2]
). Together, we have the classical case:

Theorem 33

(Jellett, Davies–Vincent–Smith, Lazar–Lindenstrauss)
.

A compact convex metrizable set is a simplex if and only if it is the projective limit of finite-dimensional simplices.

Dualizing this (and identifying
A(\Delta^{n})
with
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
) we get:

Theorem 34

(Jellett, Davies–Vincent–Smith, Lazar–Lindenstrauss, Namioka–Phelps)
.

A separable function system is nuclear if and only if it is the inductive limit of finite-dimensional commutative von Neumann algebras.

Now we move to the noncommutative convexity setting in the sense of Davidson and Kennedy
[
14
,
16
]
, which builds on the matrix convexity theory from
[
71
,
72
,
23
,
69
]
.
In lieu of a full account of the theory, we offer an intuition based on a categorical duality stemming from Davidson and Kennedy’s noncommutative generalization of Kadison’s representation theorem
[
16
, Theorem 3.2.3]
. We recommend
[
16
, Section 3]
and
[
14
]
to any reader desiring further details.

To define an nc convex set, we start with an operator space
E
and a cardinal
\kappa
at least the size of some dense subset of
E
. A compact nc convex set is a graded subset
K=\bigsqcup_{n\leq\kappa}K_{n}\subseteq\bigsqcup_{n\leq\kappa}M_{n}(E)
which is closed under arbitrary direct sums and compressions and where each
K_{n}\subseteq M_{n}(E)
is compact.
The key example of a compact nc convex set is the nc state space of an operator system
\mathcal{S}

[
16
, Example 2.2.6]
:

S_{nc}(\mathcal{S}):=\bigsqcup_{n\leq\kappa}\text{UCP}(\mathcal{S},M_{n})\subseteq\bigsqcup_{n\leq\kappa}M_{n}(\mathcal{S}^{*}),

where
\text{UCP}(\mathcal{S},M_{n})
consists of all unital completely positive (u.c.p.) maps
\mathcal{S}\to M_{n}
with
n
ranging over all cardinals
n
less than or equal to some
\kappa
, which is determined by the operator space
\mathcal{S}^{*}
as above. For example,
S_{nc}(M_{n})
consists of all ucp maps
M_{n}\to B(\mathcal{H}_{m})
with
\mathcal{H}_{m}
a fixed Hilbert space of dimension
m\leq\aleph_{0}
. A continuous nc affine map between nc convex sets is a graded map which is continuous at each level and preserves direct sums and compressions (see
[
16
, Definition 2.5.1]
). For a compact nc convex set
K=\bigsqcup_{n\leq\kappa}K_{n}
, the space
A(K)
of continuous nc affine functions from
K
to
\bigsqcup_{n\leq\kappa}M_{n}
is a closed operator system.

Now we can give Davidson and Kennedy’s noncommutative analogue of Kadison’s representation theorem
[
16
, Theorem 3.2.3]
: if
\mathcal{S}
is a closed operator system, then
A(S_{nc}(\mathcal{S}))
is completely order isomorphic to
\mathcal{S}
. This leads to a duality between the category
OpSys
of closed operator systems with u.c.p. maps and the category
NCConv
of compact nc convex sets with continuous nc affine maps (
[
16
, Section 3]
). In particular by
[
16
, Theorem 3.2.5]
, there is a contravariant functor
A:\text{NCConv}\to\text{OpSys}
with inverse
A^{-1}
implementing the dual equivalence. This functor maps a compact nc convex set
K
to
A(K)
and a continuous nc affine map
\theta:K\to L
to the u.c.p. map
A(\theta):A(L)\to A(K)
given by

A(\theta)(b)(x)=b(\theta(x)),\ \text{ for }\ b\in A(L),x\in K.

Its inverse maps an operator system
\mathcal{S}
to
S_{nc}(\mathcal{S})
and a u.c.p. map
\varphi:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{T}
to the continuous nc affine map
A^{-1}(\varphi):S_{nc}(\mathcal{T})\to S_{nc}(\mathcal{S})
given by

b(A^{-1}(\varphi)(x))=\varphi(b)(x),\ \text{ for }\ b\in\mathcal{S},x\in S_{nc}(\mathcal{T}).

Thus from a strong inductive sequence of operator systems
(\mathcal{S},j)
, we get a projective sequence of their noncommutative state spaces and vice versa.

In
[
42
]
, Kennedy and Shamovich introduced nc (Choquet) simplices, which are characterized in
[
42
, Theorem 6.2]
to be the compact nc convex sets
K
for which
A(K)^{**}
is completely order isomorphic to a von Neumann algebra (i.e., a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-system in the language of
[
44
]
). An extensive class of examples comes from the nuclear operator systems. Indeed, combining deep work of Kirchberg
[
43
]
, Choi–Effros
[
10
]
, and Effros–Ozawa–Ruan
[
24
]
with Sakai’s theorem
[
65
, Thm. 1.16.7]
yields that an operator system
\mathcal{S}
is nuclear if and only if
\mathcal{S}^{**}
is completely order isomorphic to an
injective
von Neumann algebra (see
[
31
, Theorem 3.5]
). In finite dimensions,
[
10
]
tells us
\mathcal{S}
is nuclear if and only if
\mathcal{S}
is itself completely order isomorphic to a von Neumann algebra (and hence to some finite direct sum of matrix algebras). Since the space
A(\Delta^{n})
of continuous
\mathbb{C}
-valued affine functions on a classical
n
-simplex can be identified with
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
(with each function determined by where it sends
\partial_{e}(\Delta^{n})
), this suggest nc state spaces of finite-dimensional von Neumann algebras as the nc analogue of finite-dimensional simplices.

There is now more nuance from the classical function system setting in the sense that being the affine function space of an nc simplex is more general than being nuclear. Hence for clarity we call the nc state space for a nuclear operator system a
nuclear nc simplex
. Hence we arrive at dual statement of Theorem
27
:

Corollary 35
.

Let
K
be a compact nc convex set with separable
A(K)
. Then
K
is a nuclear nc simplex if and only if
K
is the projective limit of nc state spaces of finite-dimensional von Neumann algebras.
