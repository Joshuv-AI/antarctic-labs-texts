OsvWA36 transcriptionally regulates cell wall remodeling to control grain length in rice
Authors: Hailian Zhou, Dongjin Qing, Hao Wu, Weiwei Chen, Jingcheng Li, Juan Huang, Baiyi Lu, Yuanbao Lei, Yujing Peng, Chenli Zhu, Shoukun Chen, De Peng, Xia Li, Weiyong Zhou, Gaoxing Dai, Guofu Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Grain length is a crucial determinant of rice yield and quality. Although von Willebrand factor type A (VWA) domain proteins have recently emerged as regulators of plant architecture in cereals, their roles in controlling rice grain length remain largely unexplored. Here, we report the functional characterization of OsvWA36, a VWA-domain protein selected from a TMT-based quantitative proteomic screen of indica rice varieties differing in grain length. Loss-of-function OsvWA36 mutants exhibited significantly reduced grain length and thousand-grain weight, while complementation and overexpression assays confirmed its positive regulatory role in these traits. Cytological analysis revealed that the shortened grain phenotype was due to suppressed longitudinal elongation of hull epidermal cells, which was accompanied by aberrantly enhanced lignin deposition. Transcriptomic profiling and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis demonstrated that OsvWA36 is essential for the expression of a comprehensive suite of cell wall biosynthesis and modification genes, including those involved in cellulose, pectin, and lignin metabolism. Furthermore, OsvWA36 localizes to punctate structures on the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). Our study establishes OsvWA36 as a novel VWA-domain protein that positively regulates grain length by orchestrating cell wall remodeling programs, thereby bridging VWA protein function with the transcriptional regulation of cell wall dynamics in rice. This work not only identifies a promising genetic target for molecular breeding but also provides a new molecular framework for understanding grain size regulation in cereal crops.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08943-7.

Background

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is a staple food for over half of the global population. Enhancing grain yield is therefore critical for global food security, especially in the face of a growing population and climate change [1–4]. Grain size, comprising length, width, and aspect ratio, is a pivotal determinant of both yield and quality. Grain length in particular is a major breeding target for high-yield varieties [5]. At the cellular level, this trait is regulated by complex processes, including hull cell proliferation, elongation, and cell wall remodeling [6, 7]. Dynamic changes in cellulose, pectin, and lignin composition regulate the mechanical properties of the cell wall and ultimately determine the potential for cell expansion [7, 8].

Cell wall remodeling is fundamental to hull development. Proteins such as cellulose synthases (CESAs) and pectin methylesterases (PMEs) promote wall loosening and flexibility, facilitating expansion, and excessive lignin deposition rigidifies the wall to terminate growth [7–10]. Mutations in genes such as OsCESA4, AtPME58, and LACCASE2 affect cell wall dynamics: OsCESA4 mutations impair secondary cell wall formation and alter mechanical properties, AtPME58 regulate cell wall structure critical for seed development, and LACCASE2 mediated lignin polymerization modulates cell growth—collectively underscoring the importance of precise cell wall regulation for grain development [11–13]. While several key regulators (e.g., GS3 and GW8) have been identified to control grain size through signaling or transcriptional networks, the upstream regulators that directly orchestrate the cell wall transcriptional program during grain elongation remain incompletely understood [14–16].

Von Willebrand factor type A (VWA)-domain proteins are regulators of cereal development that can affect traits related to cell growth, including processes inherently linked to cell wall dynamics [17–19]. For example, WPA1 regulates wheat plant architecture (including tiller angle) and also functions in grain size control [7]; TaAPA2 exerts pleiotropic effects on plant architecture, with impacts on cell expansion (a process dependent on cell wall flexibility) [18]; and RG1 affects both grain and flag leaf morphologies, with its function relevant to grain development stages involving cell wall dynamics [19]. The rice genome encodes approximately 40 VWA-domain proteins [20, 21]; however, whether and how any of these proteins function in grain length control, particularly through the regulation of cell wall dynamics, remains an open question.

This knowledge gap, together with our preliminary proteomic data, motivated us to investigate OsvWA36. Our previous screening via TMT-based quantitative proteomics of seven elite indica hybrid rice maintainer lines with natural variation in grain length identified OsvWA36, a VWA-domain protein, as markedly more abundant in six long-grain cultivars compared with a short-grain cultivar [22]. The strong positive correlation between OsvWA36 protein levels and grain length prompted us to hypothesize that OsvWA36 is a novel regulator of grain length, potentially through modulating cell wall properties.

In this study, we combined genetic, cytological, and transcriptomic approaches to test this hypothesis. We demonstrate that OsvWA36 is a positive regulator of rice grain length, preferentially expressed in developing panicles. Loss of OsvWA36 function leads to shortened hull epidermal cells, ectopic lignin deposition, and significant reductions in grain length and weight. Conversely, genetic complementation restores the wild-type phenotype. Mechanistically, we show that OsvWA36 is required for the normal expression of a comprehensive suite of cell wall biosynthesis and modification genes and localizes to punctate structures on the endoplasmic reticulum. Our findings establish OsvWA36 as a crucial determinant of grain length that links VWA-domain protein function to the transcriptional regulation of cell wall remodeling, providing new insights and a promising genetic target for rice yield improvement.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Two independent osvwa36 mutants (vw-123 and vw-124) were generated in the indica rice cultivar DingxiangB background using the CRISPR/Cas9 system, as previously described [23]. The target site was designed to disrupt the conserved VWA domain. Additionally, two mutant alleles (CR-1 and CR-2) in the Zhonghua 11 (ZH11) background were obtained from the Baige Company mutant library.

For genetic complementation, the full-length coding sequence of OsvWA36 (without the stop codon) was cloned into the pRHVcGFP vector under the control of the maize ZmUBI1 (polyubiquitin) genomic promoter to generate the UBI::OsvWA36-GFP construct. This construct was introduced into the vw-123 mutant via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation to generate complementation lines (e.g., Com-1, Com-2). For overexpression, the same construct was introduced into the ZH11 wild-type background to generate overexpression lines (e.g., OE-VW-2, OE-VW-3).

All plants were grown in the experimental field of the Guangxi Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Nanning, China).

Phenotypic analysis

Mature grains from wild-type (WT), osvwa36 mutants, complementation lines, and overexpression lines were harvested for phenotypic measurements. Grain length and width were measured on at least 50 randomly selected grains from each of three biological replicates using a Wanshen SC-S rice panicle shape and grain number analysis instrument. The thousand-grain weight was measured by weighing three independent batches of 1,000 grains each.

Cytological analysis

For scanning electron microscopy (SEM), lemma and palea from flowering-stage spikes were collected and scanned at Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., where the cell length and width of the outer epidermal cells were also analyzed. A minimum of 25 cells from each of three biological replicates were analyzed.

For lignin staining, lemma and palea from pre-flowering and one day after flowering (DAF) were hand-sectioned and stained with phloroglucinol-HCl solution (1% w/v phloroglucinol in 95% ethanol, mixed 1:1 with concentrated HCl) for 5 min. Stained sections were immediately examined and photographed under a Nikon Eclipse 80i light microscope.

Gene expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from various tissues, including seedling leaf blade, seedling leaf sheath, root, booting stage leaf blade, stem, and panicles at different developmental stages (5 cm, 10 cm, 20 cm, 25 cm in length, enclosed within leaf sheaths), as well as grains at 1 day after flowering (1 DAF), using a Plant RNA Extraction Kit (YIFEIXUE, China). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of DNase I-treated total RNA using the YfxScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (YIFEIXUE, China). Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, USA) using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China). The OsActin7 gene was used as an internal control. All primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCT) method [24].

Western blot analysis

For western blot analysis, total proteins were extracted from the same set of tissues as used for qRT-PCR analysis. Proteins were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Germany), and probed with a custom-made anti-OsvWA36 polyclonal antibody (1:5,000 dilution, AtaGenix, China) or an anti-actin antibody (loading control). Blots were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5,000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) and visualized using a chemiluminescence detection system [25].

Transgene copy number analysis

Transgene copy number was determined by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) according to Yang et al. [26] with minor modifications. Separate standard curves for absolute quantification were established using ten-fold serial dilutions of recombinant plasmids containing the OsvWA36 amplicon or the single-copy rice endogenous reference gene OsActin7. The qPCR amplicon lengths were 86 bp for OsvWA36 and 143 bp for OsActin7, using the same primer sets as for tissue expression analysis. qPCR was performed using SYBR Green chemistry on a real-time PCR system, with each sample analyzed in technical triplicate. Absolute copy numbers of OsvWA36 and OsActin7 were calculated from their respective standard curves. For intra-sample normalization, the OsvWA36/OsActin7 copy number ratio was computed to eliminate variations caused by differences in genomic DNA input, concentration, and integrity. The relative total copy number of OsvWA36 in transgenic lines was obtained by normalizing the ratio of each line to that of the wild-type control, which was set to 1.

Subcellular localization analysis

For Subcellular localization analysis, Nicotiana benthamiana leaves were infiltrated with Agrobacterium harboring the 35 S::OsvWA36-GFP construct and the endoplasmic reticulum marker 35 S::HDEL-RFP vector. After 48 h, epidermal cells were observed under a confocal microscope. Both sets of stained samples were observed under a confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti2, Japan). GFP fluorescence was excited at a wavelength of 488 nm, and its emission was detected within the range of 500–550 nm; for RFP, the excitation wavelength was set at 561 nm, with emission signals collected at 570–620 nm.

Transcriptomic analysis

Total RNA was isolated from wild-type (WT, DingxinagB), osvwa36 mutants, panicles at three developmental stages: 5 cm, 20 cm in length, and one day after flowering (DAF), with two biological replicates (five plants per replicate) set for each stage. Library construction, sequencing, and primary data analysis were performed by Benagen Tech (Wuhan, China). Clean reads were aligned to the rice reference genome (MSU v7.0) using Hisat2 [27]. Read counts were obtained using StringTie; differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2 with the following criteria: |log2(FoldChange)| > 1 and adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05 [28, 29]. GO enrichment analysis was conducted using clusterProfiler [30].

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality of distribution and homogeneity of variances were tested prior to statistical analysis. Student’s two-tailed t-test or Welch’s t-test (for unequal variances) was used for two-group comparisons, while one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test or Kruskal-Wallis test (for non-normal data) was used for multiple comparisons in GraphPad Prism 9. The threshold for statistical significance was p-value < 0.05.

Results

OsvWA36 positively regulates grain length in rice

In our previous proteomic study, we identified a set of candidate proteins potentially regulating rice grain length by comparing six long-grain maintainer lines with one short-grain maintainer line [22]. The top 10 most significantly differentially expressed proteins were compiled in Table S2, among which OsvWA36 was 5.77-fold more abundant in DingxiangB (the variety with the longest grain length) than in the short-grain counterpart. Based on this observation, OsvWA36 was selected as the core candidate for subsequent functional characterization, and corresponding genetic materials were generated.

The OsvWA36 protein harbors a conserved N-terminal von Willebrand factor type A (VWA) domain and a predicted C-terminal intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) in its domain architecture (Fig. S1). We obtained two independent knockout mutants of OsvWA36, each carrying frameshift mutations at both target sites (T1 upstream, T2 within the VWA domain); mutations at Target 1 lead to premature translational termination upstream of the VWA domain (Fig. 1A). Phenotypic analysis showed that compared with the wild type (WT), these two mutants exhibited an obvious difference in plant height with a shorter stature, whereas no obvious difference was detected in panicle length (Fig. 1B–C). Further investigation of grain traits revealed that the two osvwa36 mutant alleles showed a consistent and significant decrease in grain length; specifically, grain width was significantly reduced in one mutant line but remained unaffected in the other (Fig. 1D–G). This alteration in grain morphology ultimately resulted in a marked ~ 12.7% reduction in thousand-grain weight relative to the WT (Fig. 1H).

To confirm that the observed short-grain phenotype was indeed caused by the loss of OsvWA36 function, we generated a complementary transgenic line by introducing the full-length OsvWA36 coding sequence into the osvwa36 mutants background. GFP imaging (Fig. S2A) and qPCR (Fig. S2C) confirmed transgene expression in Com-1 and Com-2, with both lines showing detectable transgene copy numbers at distinct levels compared to WT. Phenotypic validation demonstrated that the grain length of the complementary line was significantly longer than that of the WT, and conversely, its grain width was significantly narrower than the WT (Fig. 2A–D), which fully restored the mutant phenotype and verified the function of OsvWA36 in grain size regulation.

To further validate the role of OsvWA36 in grain shape modulation, we expanded our investigations to osvwa36 mutants and overexpression (OE) lines constructed in the japonica rice variety ZH11, in contrast to our previous analyses which were based on an indica rice variety. Two distinct mutant alleles were obtained from the Baige Company mutant library in this background: CR-1 carried a mutation starting at the 182nd amino acid (downstream of the VWA domain), which triggered premature translational termination; CR-2 harbored a 4-amino-acid deletion plus 4 amino acid substitutions in the region downstream of the VWA domain (Fig. 3A). Phenotypic assays demonstrated that both CR-1 and CR-2 mutants displayed a significant decrease in grain length, with a concurrent significant reduction in grain width. In contrast, phenotypic variation was observed among the OsvWA36 overexpression lines relative to ZH11 (Fig. 3B–E). GFP fluorescence imaging (Fig. S2B) and qPCR analysis (Fig. S2D) confirmed stable transgene expression and distinct copy numbers in OE-VW-2 and OE-VW-3 overexpression lines, with no signal detected in wild-type ZH11. Correspondingly, the OE-VW-2 line had significantly increased grain length without any significant alteration in grain width, while the OE-VW-3 line showed no significant change in grain length but a marked decrease in grain width. (Fig. 3B–E). Collectively, these results provided compelling evidence that OsvWA36 modulates rice grain shape in an allele-dependent manner: loss-of-function mutations consistently reduced both grain length and width, whereas overexpression lines exhibited divergent phenotypes, with distinct effects on grain length or width across different overexpression constructs.

Cytological analysis of grain development

To elucidate the cellular basis underlying the short-grain phenotype of osvwa36 mutants, we performed scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observation on the hull epidermis. Specifically, both the outer and inner epidermal cells of hulls in osvwa36 mutants were significantly shorter and wider than those in WT (DingxiangB) plants (Fig. 4A–F), indicating that OsvWA36 positively regulates longitudinal cell expansion in rice hulls.

Given that cell wall properties are closely associated with cell elongation capacity in plants, we further evaluated lignin deposition in hulls using phloroglucinol-HCl staining. The assays were conducted at two key developmental stages: pre-flowering and 1 day post-flowering. The results showed that the hulls of osvwa36 mutants exhibited remarkably stronger lignin deposition signals compared with the WT at both stages (Fig. 4G). Collectively, these findings suggest a potential link between elevated lignin accumulation, suppressed cell elongation, and the short-grain phenotype in osvwa36 mutants.

Expression and subcellular localization analysis of OsvWA36

To gain further insights into the regulatory role of OsvWA36 in grain size determination, we next characterized its expression pattern and subcellular localization.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis was performed to detect the transcript levels of OsvWA36 in different tissues of DingxiangB. The results showed that OsvWA36 transcripts were most abundant in panicles. Specifically, the expression level in 5 cm booting-stage panicles was approximately 7.5-fold higher than that in the corresponding same‑stage vegetative tissues (leaf blade and leaf sheath). Expression remained high across all panicle developmental stages (10 cm, 20 cm, 25 cm) and peaked in 1 DAF grains (Fig. 5A). Moreover, this tissue-specific expression pattern was largely consistent with the data of Nipponbare retrieved from the RAP-DB database (Fig. S3), indicating the conserved expression characteristics of OsvWA36 across rice varieties. To validate the expression pattern at the protein level, we conducted western blot assays. The results revealed that the accumulation of OsvWA36 protein peaked in panicles at 1 day after flowering, and a substantial protein level was also detected in seedling leaf sheaths (Fig. 5B; Fig. S4), which was generally consistent with the transcript expression profile.

For subcellular localization analysis, we generated a functional 35 S::OsvWA36-GFP fusion construct and co-infiltrated it with the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) marker HDEL-RFP into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. Fluorescence observation showed that the OsvWA36-GFP fusion protein formed distinct punctate structures, and these GFP-labeled puncta were co-localized with the red fluorescence signals of the ER marker HDEL-RFP (Fig. 5C). This result indicated that the punctate structures of OsvWA36 are anchored to the ER, providing a basis for further exploring its molecular mechanism in regulating cell elongation and grain development.

Transcriptomic profiling indicates that OsvWA36 is involved in cell wall regulation

To uncover the molecular pathway by which OsvWA36 regulates grain development, we conducted RNA-seq analysis on developing panicles from WT (DingxiangB) and osvwa36 mutants (at different developmental stages: 5 cm, 20 cm panicles, and one day after flowering (1 DAF)). Principal component analysis (PCA) of transcriptomic data showed distinct separation between WT and osvwa36 samples (Fig. 6A), indicating obvious transcriptional differences caused by OsvWA36 mutation.

GO enrichment analysis was further performed to annotate the function of differentially expressed genes, covering three GO categories: biological process, cellular component, and molecular function. For downregulated genes, GO biological process terms related to cell wall biogenesis and modification were significantly enriched (Fig. 5B), including “cell wall modification” and “pectin metabolic process” (marked with red box). Meanwhile, GO cellular component annotation supported this trend: downregulated genes were notably enriched in “cell wall” and “external encapsulating structure” components (Fig. 5C). Additionally, GO molecular function terms corresponding to hydrolase activities (e.g., pectinase, carboxylesterase)—which are critical for cell wall modification—were also overrepresented in the mutant (Fig. 5D). These results collectively suggest that OsvWA36 participates in grain development by regulating the expression of genes involved in cell wall biogenesis and modification.

We selected 10 downregulated genes involved in key cell wall processes—including cellulose synthesis (OsCESA4, OsCESA7, and OsCSLE1), pectin remodeling (OsPME68, OsPME1), lignin biosynthesis (OsCAD2, OsExo70H3, and OsMYB58), and lipid transfer (OsLTPL26, OsLTP5) for validation by qRT-PCR. The expression of all 10 genes was significantly reduced in the mutants (Fig. 7), confirming that the loss of OsvWA36 disrupts the transcriptional program essential for cell wall biosynthesis and remodeling.

Discussion

Grain size is a key determinant of rice yield, orchestrated by intricate genetic pathways that coordinate cell proliferation, expansion, and cell wall remodeling. In this study, we identified OsvWA36, a rice VWA-domain protein, as a positive regulator of grain length that specifically promotes the longitudinal elongation of hull epidermal cells. Our findings address a critical gap in the functional characterization of the rice VWA protein family: among the approximately 40 VWA-domain proteins encoded in the rice genome, their biological roles—especially in grain size regulation—remain largely uncharacterized [20, 21]. While recent studies in wheat have demonstrated that VWA proteins (e.g., WPA1, TaAPA2, and RG1) act as pleiotropic regulators of plant architecture and organ morphology [17–19], our work distinguishes OsvWA36 by assigning it a defined, agronomically relevant function in grain length determination. More importantly, we establish a direct link between OsvWA36 activity and the transcriptional regulation of cell wall biosynthesis and modification genes, thus advancing the functional understanding of cereal VWA proteins from phenotypic description to mechanistic insights into cell wall dynamics.

To dissect the molecular basis underlying the phenotypic effects of OsvWA36, we integrated genetic, cytological, and transcriptomic analyses. Genetic assays confirmed that OsvWA36 positively regulates grain length across diverse genetic backgrounds (Figs. 1D and F, 2A and C and 3B and D), and cytological observations further narrowed its core function to the promotion of hull epidermal cell longitudinal elongation— a more focused role compared with the broad phenotypic spectra of its wheat homologs (Fig. 4B–F). Transcriptomic and qRT-PCR analyses revealed that OsvWA36 is required for the normal expression of a suite of cell wall biosynthesis and modification genes (Figs. 6 and 7), while subcellular localization assays placed OsvWA36 in endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-associated puncta (Fig. 5C). Collectively, these results delineate OsvWA36 as a crucial determinant of grain length and provide a novel molecular framework for deciphering how VWA-domain proteins modulate cellular growth in cereal crops.

Notably, loss-of-function mutations in OsvWA36 consistently reduced both grain length and width across genetic backgrounds, whereas grain width phenotypes in complementary lines were comparable to those of the mutants (Figs. 1D–G, 2A–D and 3B–E). This observation implies a dose-dependent or genetic context-dependent effect of OsvWA36 on grain width, a regulatory complexity that merits further investigation. Such complexity aligns with the well-documented crosstalk among conserved grain size regulatory pathways—for instance, brassinosteroid (BR) signaling and the actions of transcription factors like DLT and OSH15, which synergistically or antagonistically modulate multiple agronomic traits [31–33]. It is therefore plausible that OsvWA36 acts at a regulatory node intersecting multiple signaling cascades, with its final phenotypic output shaped by the genetic background.

Cytological analysis further revealed that the shortened grain phenotype of osvwa36 mutants stems from the suppression of hull epidermal cell longitudinal expansion (Fig. 4B, C and E). Intriguingly, these cells exhibited increased transverse width—a compensatory response frequently observed when longitudinal elongation is impaired (Fig. 4D and F)—which underscores that the core function of OsvWA36 is to promote cell elongation along the longitudinal axis. Mechanistically, loss of OsvWA36 function led to the coordinated downregulation of genes involved in cellulose (e.g., OsCESA4, OsCESA7), pectin (e.g., OsPME68), and lignin (e.g., OsCAD2, OsMYB58) metabolism. This regulatory pattern mirrors that of known grain size regulators such as SG5, placing OsvWA36 in the downstream executory layer that directly modulates cell wall properties to govern cell expansion [34–36].

Transcriptomic analysis provided systematic support for the conclusion that OsvWA36 acts as a central regulator of lemma and palea cell wall remodeling during grain development. GO enrichment analysis of downregulated genes in osvwa36 mutants revealed a marked convergence of enriched terms across the three core GO categories (biological process, cellular component, molecular function). This cross-category coordination is not coincidental but reflects the multi-layered control of cell wall biology by OsvWA36. Specifically, enriched biological process terms (e.g., “cell wall organization” and “pectin metabolic process”) indicate that active cell wall dynamic remodeling is disrupted in the mutant; overrepresented cellular component terms (e.g., “cell wall” and “apoplast”) confirm that these regulatory processes are spatially anchored to cell wall-related structures; and enriched molecular function terms (e.g., “pectinesterase activity” and “hydrolase activity acting on glycosyl bonds”) identify the enzymatic machinery executing cell wall structural modifications.

A compelling yet paradoxical observation was the significant downregulation of lignin biosynthetic genes (e.g., OsCAD2) in osvwa36 mutants, concurrent with enhanced lignin accumulation in hull cells. As OsCAD2 encodes a key enzyme catalyzing monolignol biosynthesis [37, 38], this discrepancy suggests that OsvWA36 may regulate lignin deposition through a post-transcriptional mechanism. The observed downregulation of lignin biosynthetic genes could represent a feedback response to altered lignin composition or the premature cessation of cell elongation, which itself triggers secondary cell wall deposition. Alternatively, OsvWA36 might modulate the enzymatic activity or subcellular localization of lignin synthesis-related proteins independent of transcriptional regulation. This divergence between transcript abundance and metabolic output highlights the complexity of cell wall regulation and warrants in-depth mechanistic investigation.

The localization of OsvWA36 to ER-associated puncta, combined with its structural features—notably the predicted IDRs, a hallmark of phase-separating proteins—raises the intriguing possibility that OsvWA36 functions via biomolecular condensation (unpublished observations). Biomolecular condensates formed through liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) are increasingly recognized as key organizers of diverse cellular processes in plants, including signal transduction, transcriptional regulation, and metabolic pathway compartmentalization [39–43]. Although OsvWA36 is localized to the ER rather than the nucleus, its punctate condensates could serve as spatial hubs to concentrate specific signaling molecules, client enzymes (e.g., cell wall modifiers), or RNA-binding proteins [39, 44, 45]. This ER membrane-localized spatial organization could efficiently channel substrates or regulate local translation, thereby modulating cell wall biosynthetic machinery and indirectly influencing the expression of nuclear genes [39, 44, 45]— a model that reconciles the non-nuclear localization of OsvWA36 with the observed transcriptional changes in cell wall-related genes.

Our identification of OsvWA36 as a positive regulator of grain length provides a direct target for yield improvement in rice. Its breeding potential can be leveraged by mining the rich natural variation in rice germplasm. The availability of genomic data from thousands of accessions and efficient haplotype analysis platforms like Rice Functional and Genomic Breeding (RFGB) will facilitate the identification of superior OsvWA36 alleles linked to longer grains for molecular design breeding [46, 47] .

In conclusion, we propose that OsvWA36 is a novel regulatory factor that integrates ER-localized spatial organization with the precise transcriptional control of cell wall biosynthesis to determine rice grain length. Its regulatory capacity might be further enhanced by its potential to form biomolecular condensates, a mechanism that adds a new layer of complexity to VWA protein function in plants. The observed decoupling of lignin gene transcription and lignin deposition underscores the multifaceted nature of OsvWA36-mediated regulation. Future work should focus on three key directions: identifying the direct interacting partners of OsvWA36, elucidating the upstream signaling pathways that modulate its function, and dissecting the biological significance of its ER-associated punctate localization. Taken together, this study identifies OsvWA36 as a crucial determinant of grain length and provides a novel molecular framework for understanding the functional diversity of VWA-domain proteins in cereal crops.
