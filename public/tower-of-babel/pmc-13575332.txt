Incidence and risk factors for AD/ADRD among American Indian and Alaska Native US Veterans
Authors: Christina S Dintica, Amber Bahorik, Feng Xia, John Boscardin, Karen Parko, Kristine Yaffe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONOur goal was to determine Alzheimer's disease (AD) and related dementias (ADRD) incidence and risk factors among American Indian and Alaska Native (AI/AN) veterans, an understudied group.METHODSWe identified AI/AN veterans ≥ 55 years receiving Veterans Health Administration care between 2005 and 2010 (n = 31,341) and a propensity score–matched non‐Hispanic White (NHW) group for AD/ADRD (using International Classification of Diseases 9th/10th Revision codes from 2011–2023) incidence rate comparison. Age‐adjusted incidence was calculated by direct standardization. Among AI/AN veterans, we determined dementia risk factors using Cox models.RESULTSAmong 31,341 AI/AN veterans (mean age 67.8 years), 16.2% developed AD/ADRD over 9.3 years. Age‐adjusted incidence was higher among AI/AN than NHW veterans (19.0 vs. 16.2 per 1000 person‐years; incidence rate ratio: 1.17). Vascular conditions, traumatic brain injury, post‐traumatic stress disorder, and depression were associated with risk of dementia among AI/AN veterans.DISCUSSIONAI/AN veterans experience higher AD/ADRD burden driven by modifiable risk factors, though findings may not generalize to non‐veteran AI/AN populations.Keywords: American Indian/Alaska Native, incidence, risk, veteran

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer disease (AD) and related dementias (ADRD) are a growing public health concern, particularly as the population ages. Recent evidence has shed light on differences in risk and incidence of AD/ADRD among under‐represented populations. 1 , 2 , 3 American Indian and Alaska Native (AI/AN) older adults may be at heightened risk for AD/ADRD due to a complex interplay of medical, psychological, and social factors. 4 , 5 , 6 While national data for AI/AN populations remain limited, evidence suggests that this group experiences health and socioeconomic challenges, including elevated rates of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and limited health‐care access, which may increase their risk for developing AD/ADRD. 4 , 7 , 8 Prior studies have reported higher prevalence and incidence of dementia among AI/AN individuals compared to non‐Hispanic White (NHW) populations, 9 , 10 a greater burden of early‐onset disease, 11 and a substantial burden of cognitive impairment and dementia in AI/AN populations, as well as associations with vascular risk factors. 12 , 13

US veterans may also have greater risk of AD/ADRD compared to the general population, due to a higher burden of risk factors such as traumatic brain injury (TBI), post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, and cardiovascular disease. 14 , 15 Importantly, AI/AN adults have a higher percentage (18.6%) of military service compared to those in other racial/ethnic groups and represent an important but understudied population of US veterans. 16 Among veterans, who have elevated exposure to risk factors such as TBBI and PTSD, these patterns may reflect processes consistent with premature or accelerated aging. 17 Moreover, AI/AN populations may experience earlier onset and greater burden of dementia‐related conditions compared to other groups. 11 , 18 These disparities may emerge in mid‐ to late‐life, underscoring the importance of examining risk across younger age strata.

The Veterans Health Administration (VHA), the largest integrated health‐care system in the United States, offers a valuable platform for studying AD/ADRD risk in this population due to its extensive electronic medical records, diverse patient base, and consistent care delivery model. Our goal was to investigate incidence of AD/ADRD among older AI/AN veterans using VHA data and to examine the role of key risk factors, including psychiatric and cardiovascular comorbidities, and military‐related risk factors. Understanding the risk of AD/ADRD in AI/AN veterans is important for informing prevention strategies and ensuring appropriate health‐care planning for this population.

METHODS

Study population

We conducted a cohort study of all AI/AN veterans aged ≥ 55 years who received care at a VHA health‐care facility between 2005 and 2010 (our study baseline) and a propensity‐matched sample of non‐Hispanic White (NHW) veterans with both followed from 2010 through 2023. Demographic information including race/ethnicity and medical diagnoses came from the Corporate Data Warehouse (CDW) and National Patient Care Databases (NPCD), which are derived from all inpatient and outpatient VHA health‐care visits nationally. Dates of death were extracted from the Vital Status File, which combines information from the VHA, the Center for Medicare and Medicaid Services, and the Social Security Administration. We identified all veterans with AI/AN recorded as a race (single or multiple) in VHA electronic health records at any point during the baseline period, resulting in 41,158 AI/AN veterans. To identify a comparison sample of NHW veterans, we first selected all other veterans receiving VHA care in that period. NHW veterans were selected as the comparison group because they represent the largest and most well‐characterized reference population within the VHA, allowing for stable estimates of incidence and facilitating interpretation of relative differences. We then performed propensity‐score matching with no replacement using greedy nearest neighbor matching with a 0.2 caliper, selecting three NHW veterans for each AI/AN veteran by age, sex, and fiscal year of first visit, which resulted in 123,474 NHW veterans. Matching was intentionally limited to these key demographic variables to preserve the full AI/AN sample and ensure comparability on basic characteristics, rather than to achieve full covariate balance across all measured factors. This approach approximates exact matching on core demographic characteristics and avoids over‐restriction of the comparison group. All participants had to have at least one visit during the baseline period and at least one visit during the follow‐up period (2010–2023). We excluded veterans without follow‐up (n = 23,264) and with prevalent AD/ADRD during baseline (n = 7122).

This study supports priorities in Indigenous health to increase data availability and quality developed as part of the Health Brain Initiative: Road Map for Indian country, which focuses on addressing dementia in the AI/AN communities published in 2019. 19

AD/ADRD diagnoses

Prevalent AD/ADRD during baseline (for exclusion) and incident AD/ADRD over follow‐up were defined based on the presence of at least one International Classification of Diseases (ICD)‐9 or ICD‐10 code from a comprehensive list recommended by the VHA AD/ADRD Steering Committee. 20

Other measures

Demographic data were obtained from the participants’ VHA electronic health records including age, sex, race, and ethnicity. Race and ethnicity became self‐reported starting in 2003 when multiple race selection became available. Veterans were classified as AI/AN if this designation appeared among any of their recorded race entries, regardless of other race codes present. We used zip codes and 2016 American Community Survey data to classify participants as living in broad educational and income strata, a commonly used approach in studies leveraging administrative data when individual‐level socioeconomic measures are not available. 21 We categorized income by tertile of median zip code tabulation area income for participants (low < $43,370; middle $43,370– $56,692; high > $56,692). Education was defined according to participant zip code tabulation area, for which ≤ 25% versus > 25% of the adult population had completed a college education (bachelor's degree or higher). We used ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 codes to identify baseline medical and psychiatric comorbidities particularly relevant for veteran populations, such as TBI, PTSD, and depression, as well as current tobacco use, alcohol use, drug use, diabetes, obesity, stroke/transient ischemic attack, myocardial infarction, hypertension, and hypercholesterolemia. National Area Deprivation Index (ADI) data were obtained through the Neighborhood Atlas, from University of Wisconsin–Madison School of Medicine and Public Health. 21 This study used the 2015 ADI. The ADI was included to capture broader neighborhood‐level deprivation—including housing quality, employment, and infrastructure—beyond what zip code–derived income and education measures reflect. 21 Rurality was defined using the VHA Rural–Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) definition, 22 , 23 which uses population density data coupled with regional proximity to socioeconomically larger urban areas to categorize participants’ residences within an urban (≥ 50,000 urban) or rural area (49,999–10,000 large rural, 2500–9999 small rural, and ≤ 2499 isolated rural).

Analyses

Baseline characteristics of veterans were first described. First, we compared AD/ADRD incidence between AI/AN and NHW veterans using age‐adjusted incidence rates, calculated using direct age‐standardization and reported per 1000 person‐years with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Incidence rate ratios (IRRs) were also examined by age group (ages 55–64, 65–74, 75–84, and 85+). Second, we evaluated associations between risk factors and AD/ADRD within the AI/AN group only using Cox proportional hazards models, censoring at AD/ADRD diagnosis, date of the last medical encounter, or death, whichever occurred first, and reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs. For each risk factor, we estimated two Cox proportional hazards models. Model 1 examined each risk factor individually as the exposure of interest, adjusted for age and sex only. Model 2 additionally adjusted for all risk factors that achieved statistical significance in Model 1, to evaluate whether individual associations persisted after accounting for correlations among exposures. To account for multiple comparisons across risk factors, P values from Cox proportional hazards models were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Sensitivity analyses using Fine–Gray proportional hazards regression models accounted for death as an alternative competing risk and provide a more conservative estimate of the association. Proportional hazards model assumptions were checked and met for all final models. P values were two sided with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05. Analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.) and StataMP version 18 (StataCorp).

RESULTS

The final analytic cohort included 134,246 veterans, of whom 31,341 were AI/AN and 102,905 were NHW. The mean baseline age among AI/AN veterans was 67.8 years (standard deviation [SD] 8.0) and 3.5% were female (Table 1). During an average of 9.3 years of follow‐up (SD 3.9), 5086 (16.2%) AI/AN and 15,793 (15.3%) NHW veterans developed AD/ADRD.

We next evaluated associations between risk factors and AD/ADRD among AI/AN veterans. In demographic‐adjusted Cox proportional hazard regression models, several vascular, military‐related, and psychiatric conditions were significantly associated with increased risk of AD/ADRD in AI/AN veterans. Vascular risk factors including hypertension (HR 1.18; 95% CI, 1.10–1.25), diabetes (HR 1.28; 95% CI, 1.21–1.36), obesity (HR 1.09; 95% CI, 1.02–1.16), stroke or transient ischemic attack (HR 1.71; 95% CI, 1.58–1.85), and myocardial infarction (HR 1.35; 95% CI, 1.22–1.50) were strongly associated with incident AD/ADRD. Military‐related risk factors, including TBI (HR 2.49; 95% CI, 2.04–3.03) and PTSD (HR 1.45; 95% CI, 1.36–1.55), were also associated with elevated risk. Psychiatric conditions, particularly depression (HR 1.66; 95% CI, 1.57–1.76), alcohol use disorder (HR 1.40; 95% CI, 1.30–1.52), and drug use disorder (HR 1.48; 95% CI, 1.33–1.66), were similarly associated with higher risk (Table 3). All risk factor associations remained statistically significant after FDR correction for multiple comparisons (all q < 0.05; Table S1 in supporting information).

Sensitivity analyses using Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models estimated the effect of risk factors on the cumulative incidence of AD/ADRD accounting for the competing risk of death. Subdistribution hazard ratios from these models were consistent in direction and magnitude with the cause‐specific HRs from the primary Cox models, suggesting that the competing risk of death did not materially influence our findings.

DISCUSSION

This study provides important new findings on AD/ADRD incidence among AI/AN older adults by demonstrating a higher burden of AD/ADRD in AI/AN veterans compared to NHW veterans. Because veterans represent a selected, generally healthier subset of the broader AI/AN population, these findings should be interpreted as specific to AI/AN veterans. In particular, there was an even greater differential in incidence among younger veterans with the greatest relative risk observed among those aged 55 to 64 years. In addition, we identified comorbidities particularly relevant for veteran populations—particularly PTSD, cardiovascular conditions, depression, substance use disorders, and TBI—as strong risk factors for AD/ADRD among AI/AN veterans.

These findings underscore the critical importance of addressing mental health in efforts to reduce AD/ADRD risk in AI/AN populations. PTSD and depression, both highly prevalent among veterans, were associated with substantially elevated AD/ADRD risk. 14 , 24 These conditions may act through neurobiological mechanisms such as hippocampal atrophy, hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis dysregulation, and chronic inflammation, as well as through behavioral pathways including increased substance use and reduced treatment adherence. 25 , 26

Our study also highlights TBI as a particularly important risk factor. AI/AN veterans with a history of TBI had more than twice the risk of developing AD/ADRD compared to those without such history. TBI is increasingly recognized as a major contributor to long‐term neurodegenerative processes, potentially accelerating amyloid and tau pathology, disrupting neural networks, and increasing vulnerability to subsequent insults. 27 , 28 Given the elevated rates of TBI in veteran populations, 15 , 17 this finding is especially concerning and emphasizes the importance of early detection, rehabilitation, and long‐term monitoring of cognitive outcomes in AI/AN veterans with TBI. In addition to mental health conditions and TBI, several vascular risk factors were strongly associated with AD/ADRD risk. 8 Notably, stroke and myocardial infarction showed among the strongest associations observed in our study. These findings highlight the importance of aggressive vascular risk factor management—including prevention and treatment of stroke, hypertension, and diabetes—as a potential strategy for reducing AD/ADRD burden in AI/AN veterans. Together, these findings underscore the multifactorial nature of AD/ADRD risk in AI/AN veterans, spanning both vascular and neuropsychiatric domains.

Several of our findings align closely with prior work conducted specifically in AI/AN, mostly non‐veteran populations. For instance, the elevated risk of AD/ADRD associated with depression is consistent with a recent study of AI/AN adults, which similarly found depression to be independently associated with incident AD/ADRD. 29 In contrast, our finding of a significant association between diabetes and AD/ADRD differs from this same study, in which diabetes was not significantly associated with AD/ADRD after full adjustment for cardiovascular disease subtypes, suggesting the role of diabetes as an independent risk factor in AI/AN populations may vary across samples and adjustment strategies. Our finding that TBI carried the strongest relative risk in our cohort is consistent with a national study of AI/AN Medicare beneficiaries, which found TBI to be a disproportionately stronger risk factor for AD/ADRD among AI/AN beneficiaries compared to White beneficiaries, 30 suggesting this may reflect a population‐specific vulnerability rather than a pattern unique to veterans. Similarly, our comparatively modest association for hypertension mirrors a prior AI/AN‐specific study that found no significant hypertension–dementia association, despite significant associations for diabetes and cardiovascular disease more broadly. 31 Finally, our emphasis on vascular risk factors is consistent with the Strong Heart Study, a population‐based cohort using gold‐standard adjudicated dementia diagnoses, in which vascular injury was identified as a primary etiology in a comparable proportion of impaired participants as AD/ADRD pathology, 13 underscoring the centrality of vascular contributions to dementia in Indigenous populations more broadly, independent of veteran status.

Sociodemographic characteristics such as education, income, and neighborhood disadvantage (ADI) were not strongly associated with AD/ADRD risk in AI/AN veterans. It is important to note that ADI is an area‐level measure of neighborhood deprivation and cannot serve as a proxy for individual‐level socioeconomic position; null associations at the neighborhood level should not be interpreted as contradicting the broader literature linking individual‐level socioeconomic position to AD/ADRD risk, as these are conceptually distinct constructs. Several explanations for the null area–level findings are possible, including relative homogeneity of disadvantage within the AI/AN veteran cohort, which may have limited our ability to detect associations; that medical and neuropsychiatric risk factors may overshadow socioeconomic influences in this population and possibly that by participating in military service and veteran benefits, the AI/AN veteran population may demonstrate resilience and improved access to care and education, 5 , 16 and residual confounding. Limited variability in neighborhood disadvantage within this veteran cohort may have further biased associations toward the null, and future studies using individual‐level measures of socioeconomic position are warranted. Additionally, although several cardiovascular risk factors were associated with increased AD/ADRD risk, the relatively modest HRs for hypertension, obesity, and hypercholesterolemia—as well as the largely null associations for socioeconomic indicators—may partially reflect survival bias, whereby individuals with higher cardiometabolic or social disadvantage experience earlier mortality and are therefore less likely to survive to ages at which dementia is diagnosed. 32

The greater relative difference in AD/ADRD incidence observed among veterans aged 55 to 64 is similar to reported findings within the Indian Health Service data set in which there was a higher rate of early onset (age 45–64) ADRD compared to the global population. 33 This pattern is consistent with the concept of “weathering,” whereby cumulative exposure to social, environmental, and health‐related stressors contributes to accelerated biological aging and earlier onset of chronic disease. 34 In addition, higher prevalence of mid‐life risk factors in this population, including cardiovascular and neuropsychiatric conditions, may contribute to earlier vulnerability to cognitive decline. 8 Elevated rates of suicide among AI/AN veterans represent an additional contributor to premature mortality in this population, potentially removing the most vulnerable individuals from the at‐risk pool and further contributing to underestimation of true AD/ADRD burden, particularly at younger ages. 35 Alternatively, differences in health‐care use or diagnostic patterns across groups may influence the timing of diagnosis, potentially contributing to the observed age‐specific differences. These findings underscore the importance of examining dementia risk across the life course and suggest that mid‐life may represent a critical window for intervention in AI/AN populations. The re‐emergence of elevated AD/ADRD incidence among veterans aged ≥ 85 may reflect a survivor effect, 34 whereby AI/AN veterans reaching oldest ages represent a selected, relatively resilient population, yet cumulative disadvantage and differential access to late‐life care may contribute to a re‐emergence of disparity.

Key strengths of this study include its large, nationally representative cohort of veterans, detailed clinical data, and long follow‐up. However, limitations warrant consideration. All diagnoses and risk factors were ascertained from ICD codes in administrative data, which may underestimate true prevalence—particularly for conditions prone to undercoding or misclassification such as TBI and mental health diagnoses, or among rural residents with less frequent health‐care use—potentially biasing risk factor associations toward the null. While requiring multiple encounters can improve specificity, we used a single encounter definition to maximize sensitivity and capture cases that may have limited health‐care use. This approach may result in some false positives of the outcome, although prior studies suggest reasonable validity of ICD‐based dementia definitions in administrative data. 36 , 37 Additionally, differential use of VHA services, including receipt of care outside the VHA system (e.g., Indian Health Service or community care), may lead to under‐ascertainment of diagnoses among AI/AN veterans. Race and ethnicity in VHA data are based on self‐report and may be subject to misclassification or underreporting, particularly among AI/AN veterans; therefore, an unknown number of veterans who identify as AI/AN may not be captured as such in our cohort. This is consistent with documented undercounting of AI/AN individuals in US administrative and surveillance data more broadly. 38 We are unable to quantify the magnitude of this undercount, and future work using more granular race/ethnicity fields, Tribal enrollment data (captured in VHA records since 2017), or direct engagement with AI/AN communities may help address this limitation. Furthermore, clinical recognition and documentation of AD/ADRD diagnoses may vary across racial and ethnic groups; if AI/AN veterans are less likely to receive a formal dementia diagnosis due to cultural, linguistic, or provider‐level factors, incidence estimates in this population may be further underestimated. The lower prevalence of diabetes and obesity compared to community‐based AI/AN studies likely reflects the healthy warrior effect, whereby military fitness requirements select for a healthier subset of the broader population. This selection may attenuate the vascular risk factor associations identified here and may also lead to underestimation of true AD/ADRD incidence in the broader AI/AN population. Our findings should therefore be interpreted as specific to AI/AN veterans rather than generalized to AI/AN adults overall. Moreover, the underrepresentation of women (3.5%) limits generalizability to female AI/AN veterans.

In conclusion, this study provides compelling evidence of elevated AD/ADRD incidence among AI/AN veterans and identifies psychiatric conditions, TBI, and vascular risk factors as primary drivers of risk. Future work should build on the growing body of community‐engaged and culturally grounded research in Indigenous populations 39 , 40 to improve dementia risk assessment and prevention strategies for AI/AN veterans.
