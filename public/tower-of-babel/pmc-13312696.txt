Clinical and socioeconomic factors associated with return-to-work after upper and lower limb fractures
Authors: Jen-Ta Shih, Pei-Hung Shen, Chih-Chien Wang, Sheng-Hao Wang, Tsu-Te Yeh, Sui-Lung Su, Feng-Hang Chang, Chia-Chun Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFractures of the upper and lower limbs often result in prolonged work absence and significant psychosocial consequences. However, evidence on predictors of return-to-work (RTW) and associated outcomes remains limited. This study aimed to examine return-to-work rates and associated clinical and socioeconomic factors among injured workers in Taiwan with upper or lower limb fractures.MethodsThis was a retrospective observational study of 360 employed adults with a single upper or lower limb fracture treated at a medical center between 2018 and 2020. Participants completed structured questionnaires assessing demographic, clinical, psychological, and occupational variables. The primary outcome was RTW at the original workplace. Logistic regression identified factors associated with failure to RTW, and linear regression assessed its impact on psychological and physical health.ResultsAbout 14% of the 360 participants did not return to their original workplace. Lower limb fractures (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 2.42, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.22–4.82), working part-time (aOR = 10.09, 95% CI: 4.05–25.12) or temporary employment (aOR = 5.44, 95% CI: 1.80–16.45), prolonged hospitalization, and lower income were independently associated with failure to RTW. Participants unable to RTW reported significantly higher levels of anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress symptoms, perceived injustice, and poorer quality of life compared to those who RTW.ConclusionsBoth clinical and socioeconomic factors substantially influence RTW after limb fractures. Vulnerable workers, particularly those in non-permanent or low-income jobs, require targeted rehabilitation and vocational support to improve reintegration outcomes and mitigate adverse psychosocial effects.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27613-9.Keywords: Fractures, Psychosocial factors, Return-to-work, Socioeconomic factors, Workforce reintegration

Introduction

The global burden of musculoskeletal trauma is substantial and continues to grow, particularly in low- and lower-middle-income countries (LMICs). Fracture incidence in these regions ranges from 779 to 1,574 per 100,000 person-years, reflecting a combination of risk factors such as aging populations, rapid urbanization, increased rates of motor vehicle collisions, and high incidences of falls. These injuries contribute significantly to morbidity and long-term disability and also impose heavy socioeconomic burdens on individuals, families, and healthcare systems [1]. Fractures often result in extended periods of physical disability, leading to substantial loss of productivity, diminished income, and increased dependency on healthcare and social support systems. These consequences are especially pronounced in manual laborers and low-income populations, where physical function is tightly linked to livelihood. Individuals recovering from fractures frequently encounter challenges in reintegrating into the workforce, and many do not return to their previous employment status even years post-injury [2]. As a result, fractures contribute significantly to the overall disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) lost due to injury-related morbidity, especially among working-age adults [3].

Particularly, fractures significantly impact return to work (RTW) rates. Studies show that 10–40% of patients do not RTW within two years post-injury [2], with reported RTW rates ranging from 58.7% to 67.7% at different time points, and an average of 102.3 days of work absence following orthopedic trauma [4]. The ability to RTW is influenced by a wide range of factors. Physical labor demands, open fractures, chronic pain, and the nature of a patient’s employment have been shown to negatively affect RTW [2]. In addition to the severity of physical injury, which is a strong predictor of both early and late RTW, socioeconomic variables such as income level, age, education, occupation type, and compensation status also play critical roles [5, 6]. Lower-income individuals, in particular, face greater challenges in achieving successful work reintegration [6]. RTW following orthopedic trauma is influenced by a complex interplay of biopsychosocial factors [5]. A growing body of evidence highlights the importance of factors beyond the physical domain. A biopsychosocial approach has identified additional predictors such as general health, pain, disability, financial concerns, and personality traits [7]. Furthermore, ongoing legal disputes and overall life satisfaction can also shape RTW trajectories [7]. Given the multifactorial reasons behind failure to RTW, these findings highlight the importance of continued research to better understand and address these challenges.

Building on this understanding, recent studies have further emphasized the role of psychosocial and organizational factors in shaping work outcomes after injury. For example, perceived injustice has been identified as a significant predictor of post-traumatic stress symptoms in injured workers [8]. High job demands, job strain, and low social support are strongly associated with increased risk of musculoskeletal disorders and absenteeism [9]. Workers in nonstandard and precarious employment arrangements face higher odds of poor health outcomes and lower expectations for sustained RTW [10]. Moreover, organizational factors such as inadequate employer-healthcare provider communication, perceived stigmatization, and limited coworker support-have been linked to interruptions in RTW, while reluctance to report unsafe situations and higher job strain are associated with reinjury [11]. These insights underscore the need for a comprehensive approach that also addresses workplace and organizational dynamics to promote successful reintegration and prevent reinjury among workers with permanent impairments.

In Taiwan, work-related injuries involving orthopedic trauma continue to constitute a sizable proportion of labor compensation claims, yet localized data assessing predictors of RTW and productivity loss following limb fractures remain sparse. The present study aimed to quantify labor force loss due to upper and lower limb fractures and identify factors associated with inability to return to the original workplace. These insights may inform the development of evidence-based RTW interventions and occupational rehabilitation strategies tailored to the Taiwanese workforce.

Methods

Study design and population

This was a retrospective observational study conducted at a single medical center in Taiwan. The study enrolled a total of 360 adult patients who sustained a single upper or lower limb fracture between January 2018 and December 2020. Eligible participants were workers at the time of injury and received both preoperative and postoperative treatment. All outcome assessments were conducted at least 12 months after definitive fracture treatment. This time point was selected to ensure a stable post-injury and post-treatment status rather than to indicate ongoing clinical follow-up.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were between 18 and 65 years of age, confirmed diagnosis of a single upper or lower limb fracture, and actively employed at the time of injury, and underwent operative management for their fractures. Fractures involving isolated fingers or toes were not included. All fractures involved major segments of the upper or lower extremities, proximal to the wrist or ankle joints.

Exclusion criteria were persons < 18 or > 65 years old, history of a mental illness, pregnancy, or physical or mental disabilities, pathological fractures, multiple traumatic injuries requiring additional surgical intervention, persons with a government-issued disability certificate.

Data collection

Data collection was performed using structured, self-administered questionnaires designed to capture comprehensive clinical, psychological, social, and occupational information relevant to patients with upper or lower limb fractures. Eligible participants were identified from hospital records and initially contacted via telephone. Those who agreed to participate were invited to attend a joint clinic session with orthopedic and occupational medicine specialists, during which the study procedures were explained and written informed consent was obtained.

Participants completed a standardized questionnaire encompassing five major domains:

(1) Treatment-related information, including fracture characteristics, time to surgery, length of hospital-stay, bone graft reimbursement type, and rehabilitation history. The variable “bone graft reimbursement type” indicates whether self-paid bone graft materials were used or whether only National Health Insurance–covered bone graft materials, which do not require additional payment, were used. Upper limb fractures were defined as fractures occurring proximal to the wrist joint, and lower limb fractures as those proximal to the ankle joint. Although formal injury severity scores (e.g., Injury Severity Score) were not available due to the retrospective design, these clinical variables were collected and used as proxies for injury severity, including fracture site and pattern, open versus closed fractures, length of hospital stay, surgical treatment characteristics, and rehabilitation history. The variable “time to removal of cast or assistive device” referred to the time until removal of temporary immobilization devices (e.g., cast, splint, or external fixation) used during the postoperative or rehabilitation period. This variable did not refer to the removal of internal fixation devices such as plates, screws, or intramedullary nails, which are typically retained in situ; (2) Psychological status, assessed using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS)—each subscale ranges from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating greater anxiety or depression—and the Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5), which ranges from 0 to 80, with higher scores reflecting more severe PTSD symptoms; (3) Social distress, evaluated using the Injustice Experience Questionnaire (IEQ), with total scores ranging from 0 to 48 and higher scores indicating greater perceived injustice; (4) Quality of life was assessed using the EuroQol Five-Dimension Five-Level health questionnaire (EQ-5D-5 L). Analyses focused on the five individual EQ-5D dimensions (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression), each of which is rated on a five-level ordinal scale, with higher levels indicating more severe problems; (5) Brief Pain Inventory–Short Form (BPI-SF, Taiwan version), which includes pain severity and interference subscales ranging from 0 to 10, where higher scores represent more severe pain or functional interference; and (6) Occupational status and financial impact, including return-to-work status, changes in job duties or working hours, job transition, and salary loss. Questionnaires were completed either on-site at the clinic or returned by mail, according to participant preference. Completion time was approximately 1 h. The standardized psychological and quality-of-life instruments assessed participants’ symptoms and health status at the time of questionnaire administration, reflecting their current psychological and functional condition. In contrast, treatment-related information, including fracture characteristics and details of medical management, was obtained from medical records and participant self-report and therefore required retrospective recall. Given that all assessments were conducted at least 12 months after definitive fracture treatment, recall bias related to treatment details cannot be entirely excluded and should be considered when interpreting these variables.

All questionnaires used in this study were internationally recognized instruments that have been validated in Taiwanese or Mandarin-speaking populations. Psychological status was assessed using the HADS, originally developed by Zigmond and Snaith [12], with the Chinese version validated by Juang et al. [13] in patients with headache. The PCL-5 was based on the original measure developed by Blevins et al. [14], and the Chinese version was psychometrically validated by Fung et al. [15] among psychiatric patients in Taiwan. The IEQ was adopted from Agtarap et al. [16], which has been validated in heterogeneous trauma populations. Health-related quality of life was measured using the EQ-5D-5 L, originally developed by the EuroQol Group [17, 18], with the Taiwanese version validated by Chang et al. [19] in a representative population sample. Pain assessment was conducted using the BPI-SF, Taiwan version, originally created by Cleeland and Ryan [20], with the Taiwanese version validated by Ger et al. [21]. The use of these validated instruments ensured the reliability and validity of assessments across psychological, social, and physical health domains in this study.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was RTW status, defined as whether or not a participant was able to return to their original workplace following treatment for a single upper or lower limb fracture. This outcome was determined based on self-reported data collected through the structured questionnaires. Secondary outcomes included evaluations of physical, psychological, and social functioning after injury. For all standardized assessment tools, we reported their score ranges and directionality to aid interpretation. Higher scores on HADS, PCL-5, IEQ, and BPI-SF indicate poorer psychological or physical outcomes, whereas higher EQ-5D-5 L index scores indicate better health-related quality of life.

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tri-Service General Hospital (IRB No. A202205043). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before study enrollment. To ensure confidentiality, all data were anonymized and utilized exclusively for research purposes.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed using Student’s t test. Categorical variables were expressed as count and percentage and analyzed using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Logistic regression was used to evaluate factors associated with failure to return to the original workplace, and results were presented as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Additionally, linear regression analyses were conducted to estimate the impact of failure to return to the original workplace on mental, social, and physical health conditions. Mental, social, and physical health measures were treated as separate outcome variables and analyzed in separate models. Results were presented as coefficients (Beta) and 95% CI. All multivariable linear regression models were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and fracture site.

Multiple logistic regression with stepwise selection was performed to identify factors associated with being unable to return to the original workplace. A significance level of 0.50 was required to allow a variable into the model, and a significance level of 0.05 was required for a variable to stay in the model. The candidate variables for outcomes are presented in Table 1, including demographic characteristics (age at time of fracture, sex, BMI, education level), fracture-related factors (fracture site, fracture pattern, treatment, injury caused by an occupational accident, time from injury to surgery, length of hospital stay, time to removal of a cast or assistive device, rehabilitation treatment, evaluation by an occupational physician, second surgery, duration of post-injury symptoms), lifestyle factors (smoking status, alcohol consumption), and occupational factors (nature of work, monthly salary, work involving the operation of instruments, machinery, or vehicles). Time since injury or definitive treatment was not included as an independent variable in the analyses because all participants were assessed beyond a minimum of 12 months after treatment, and the study aimed to examine return-to-work status and associated outcomes at a relatively stable post-injury stage rather than to model recovery trajectories over time.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

A total of 360 patients with a single upper limb (48%) or lower limb (52%) fracture were enrolled in the study. The demographic characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1 and Supplemental Tables 1–2. The mean age at time of fracture was 42 ± 11 years, and 60% of the patients were male. Of all patients, 311 (86%) returned to their original workplace, whereas 49 (14%) were unable to return to their original workplace. Patients who returned to their original workplace had a shorter hospital stay: 6% stayed in the hospital for ≥ 14 days compared to 14% of those who did not return to their original workplace.

Of the patients who were able to return to the original workplace, the majority were full-time workers (83%), and over 85% had a monthly salary exceeding 30,000 NTD. In contrast, among those who were unable to return, over 35% were part-time or temporary workers, and 39% had a monthly salary below 30,000 NTD. Significant differences based on return-to-work status were observed in fracture site (p = 0.018), length of hospital stay (p = 0.023), nature of work (p < 0.001), smoking status (p = 0.033), and monthly salary (p < 0.001) (Table 1).

The results of the stratified analysis by fracture site (upper vs. lower limb) are summarized in Table 2. Of the participants, 174 had upper limb fractures and 16 were unable to return to their original workplace (9%); 186 had lower limb fractures and 33 were unable to return to their original workplace (18%). Significant differences based on RTW status were observed in the nature of work (p < 0.05) and monthly salary (p < 0.05) (Table 2).

Factors associated with being unable to return to the original workplace

The associations between variables and being unable to return to the original workplace are summarized in Table 3. After adjusting for age, sex, and BMI, patients with lower limb fractures (aOR = 2.16; 95% CI: 1.13–4.11) had a significantly higher risk of being unable to return to the original workplace compared to those with upper limb fractures. With respect to work-related variables, part-time workers (aOR = 9.19; 95% CI: 3.70–22.84) and temporary workers (aOR = 6.49; 95% CI: 2.11–19.97) had a significantly higher risk of being unable to return to the original workplace than full-time workers. Participants with a monthly salary < 30,000 NTD (aOR = 7.47; 95% CI: 1.55–36.04) had a significantly higher risk of being unable to return to the original workplace compared to those earning ≥ 100,000 NTD. In addition, patients who stayed in the hospital for 8–13 days (aOR = 2.40; 95% CI: 1.04–5.54) or ≥ 14 days (aOR = 3.21; 95% CI: 1.23–8.39) were at increased risk of being unable to return to the original workplace (Table 3).

Analysis using stepwise selection was performed to identify the most influential factors of failure to return to the original workplace. After adjustment, patients with lower limb fractures (aOR = 2.42, 95% CI: 1.22–4.82), part-time workers (aOR = 10.09, 95% CI: 4.05–25.12), and temporary workers (aOR = 5.44, 95% CI: 1.80-16.45) had higher risks of failure to return to the original workplace (Supplemental Table 4).

Impact of failure to return to the original workplace on psychological and social health, and quality of life

The impacts of failure to return to the original workplace on psychosocial, social, and quality of life outcomes are shown in Table 4. After adjustment for age, sex, BMI, and fracture site, multivariable analyses showed that, compared with returned to their original workplace, failure to return to the original workplace was significantly associated with more unfavorable outcomes, including higher HADS scores (adjusted β = 3.32, 95% CI: 1.13–5.51), higher PTSD scores (adjusted β = 5.01, 95% CI: 1.29–8.73), higher IEQ scores (adjusted β = 4.80, 95% CI: 1.56–8.03), and higher EQ-5D scores (adjusted β = 1.46, 95% CI: 0.94–1.98) (Table 4).

Discussion

In this study, we identified both clinical and socioeconomic factors associated with failure to return to the original workplace after upper or lower limb fractures. In particular, lower limb fractures and non-permanent employment emerged as key determinants of work discontinuation. These findings suggest that mobility impairment and job insecurity play a critical role in return-to-work outcomes after orthopedic trauma. Importantly, our results extend previous evidence by demonstrating that workers who failed to return to their original workplace also experienced significantly worse psychological well-being and quality of life, highlighting the interconnected nature of physical injury, employment vulnerability, and psychosocial distress in a Taiwanese working population.

Our analyses showed that lower limb fractures were significantly associated with failure to return to the original workplace. This finding is consistent with previous research indicating that lower extremity injuries have a greater functional impact on mobility and work performance, particularly in physically demanding occupations. For example, Belete et al. [22] reported that lower limb fractures are significantly associated with functional limitation and level of education, complications while hospitalized, severity of pain, duration of onset, and knee flexion limitation were independent risk factors for functional limitation. Despite the limitations associated with lower extremity injuries, study has shown that the ability to RTW is associated with a higher quality of life. Wang et al. [23] studied working patients who sustained lower extremity trauma requiring soft-tissue reconstruction or amputation. Divorced or widowed status, bilateral injuries, and having a manual labor job were negatively associated with RTW, while increased time since injury and higher educational status were positively associated with RTW. Most notable, LIMB-Q Function and LIMB-Q Life Impact scores were significantly increased in patients that returned to work after injury.

Consistent with other research, we found that non-permanent employees, including part-time and temporary workers, were very unlikely to RTW after injury. A study of workers performing nonstandard and precarious jobs reported that after injury workers self-reported well-being was lower and that the workers were unlikely to return to work [10]. A recent systematic review of workers whose employment was considered precarious found negative consequences of precarious employment including poorer workplace well-being, general health, mental health, and emotional well-being, all of which were increase as the precariousness of the employment increased [24]. It has been clearly shown that non-permanent and low-income workers typically lack job security and employer-based support, and this may limit their access to workplace accommodations and vocational support, reducing the likelihood of RTW and perpetuating socioeconomic and healthcare inequities [25]. A study of burn injury victims found that disadvantages such as low income hinders return to work, suggesting that non-permanent and low-income workers may lack needed support, worsening social and healthcare inequities [26].

Similar to prior research, our findings support existing evidence that prolonged hospitalization and lower income are predictive of injured workers’ RTW failure. A study of patients who experienced non-catastrophic road traffic injuries with 2-year follow-up reported that prolonged hospitalization, lower income, and other socioeconomic disadvantages negatively affected RTW [27]. A study performed in Isreal also reported that socioeconomic disadvantages such as lower income and prolonged hospitalization negatively affect rehabilitation and RTW in patients who have sustained a traumatic injury [6]. Overall, the findings highlight that socioeconomic disadvantages, which are modifiable through targeted government policies, play a critical role in a patient’s ability to return to work following injury.

Recent evidence suggests that achieving a successful RTW following illness or injury is influenced by a complex interplay of individual and social factors. Support from managers, co-workers, and family is crucial in facilitating RTW [28, 29]. Personal factors such as positive attitude, self-efficacy, younger age, and higher education also contribute to sustainable RTW [28, 30]. Job-related factors, including job crafting, job security, and flexible work arrangements, can significantly improve RTW outcomes [28, 31]. The health care system plays a vital role through access to medical treatment and rehabilitation programs. Additionally, efficient insurance claim processes can reduce stress and shorten absence duration. To enhance RTW success, a comprehensive approach involving employers, healthcare professionals, and social workers is recommended, focusing on individualized strategies and improved coordination across health and social care sectors [31].

Additionally, it is clear that lower limb fractures limit a person’s ability to return to work, and this is likely because the fracture limits basic mobility such as walking and climbing stairs. For workers in physically demanding occupations, these impairments can persist after recovery and prevent them from resuming their prior job. Johansson et al. [32] reported that lower limb fractures negatively affect physical activity at 6 months or more after injury, indicating the need for aggressive rehabilitation to help patients resume resuming physical activity and rejoin the workforce. Similarly, Singaram and Naidoo [33] reported that long bone fractures, especially those of the lower limbs, significantly impair daily activities and tasks and this is especially evident in older adults and women.

Although many psychological symptoms may be expected to improve within the first year after injury, our findings indicate that a substantial proportion of participants continued to report anxiety, depressive, or post-traumatic stress symptoms beyond this period, highlighting the persistence of psychosocial sequelae after limb fractures. This suggests a potential pathway in which chronic stress leads to emotional distress, and subsequently results in functional decline ultimately impacting long-term psychosocial outcomes. This is consistent with a study reporting that patients who experience an injury who develop symptoms of depression, anxiety, and PTSD have a lower quality of life, higher healthcare utilization, and lower return to work rates [34]. Notably, Pavilanis et al. [8] found that perceived injustice is a determinant of the severity of post-traumatic stress symptoms following an injury associated with a person’s job.

Emerging evidence indicates that psychological factors significantly impact recovery and RTW after occupational injuries. Workers unable to RTW often experience higher levels of anxiety, depression, and PTSD [35]. A bidirectional relationship exists between mental health and RTW, with physical trauma inducing anxiety and depression, while concerns about returning to work exacerbate negative emotions [36]. Factors associated with unsuccessful RTW include negative baseline work status, greater self-reported functional disability, and presence of depression [37]. Occupational PTSD can result from various traumatic events, including disasters, accidents, and workplace bullying, leading to deterioration of physical and psychological health and impaired social and occupational functioning [38]. Early screening and intervention for psychological symptoms are crucial, as severe symptoms within the first year post-injury significantly increase the risk of not returning to work even after six years [35].

Implications for clinical practice and healthcare policies

Our findings, and those of other studies, indicate that clinical and occupational rehabilitation strategies should prioritize early, individualized, and multidisciplinary RTW interventions, especially for patients with lower limb injuries, precarious jobs, and low income. Tailored care plans involving orthopedic, rehabilitation, psychological, and vocational professionals can shorten sick leave, reduce disability rates, improve workplace reintegration, and prevent long-term psychosocial harm [39]. A multidisciplinary approach toward rehabilitation after an injury can improve RTW outcomes [40]. Furthermore, early vocational rehabilitation interventions involving multidisciplinary teams have been shown to improve RTW outcomes for people with serious injuries or illnesses [41].

Recent studies highlight inequalities in Taiwan’s social insurance system, particularly affecting non-standard workers and low-income groups. The National Pension Insurance exhibits intergenerational and intragenerational inequalities, though government subsidies help mitigate some disparities [42]. In the construction industry, non-standard employment workers face higher occupational injury risks, especially among older and self-employed workers [43]. Low-income individuals experience higher hospitalization rates, longer hospital stays, and higher mortality rates due to injuries compared to non-low-income groups, indicating persistent health inequalities despite universal health coverage [44]. While national health insurance covers basic medical care, out-of-pocket expenses burden low-income individuals, impacting treatment quality and recovery speed. The implementation of the Labor Occupational Accident Insurance and Protection Act in 2022 aims to improve RTW policies, but most enterprises lack experience in assisting injured workers. Small-sized companies, which comprise 94% of Taiwanese enterprises, often lack dedicated occupational safety and health professionals, relying on human resources personnel for RTW processes [45]. Similar issues are observed in Korea, where non-standard workers often fall into social insurance blind spots [46]. These findings underscore the need for more inclusive policies to address RTW inequalities stemming from employment types and income disparities.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. While detailed injury severity scores were not available in this retrospective study, several clinical indicators related to injury severity were incorporated into the analysis. Some of these variables may serve as proxies for injury severity, including fracture site, fracture pattern, open versus closed fractures, length of hospital stay, and treatment-related characteristics. Nevertheless, residual confounding related to the true severity of injury and time since injury cannot be fully excluded and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Second, the study was conducted at a single medical center, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings to broader or more diverse populations, especially those in rural areas or with different healthcare access. Third, although validated instruments were used to assess psychological and functional outcomes, the cross-sectional nature of these assessments limits conclusions regarding causal relations. Fourth, the absence of pre-injury baseline data prevents the comparison of patients’ functional and psychological status before and after the fracture. Lastly, the sample may be biased toward individuals more willing or able to participate in follow-up, especially given the reliance on telephone and mail-based recruitment during the COVID-19 pandemic, potentially excluding more severely affected or socioeconomically disadvantaged individuals. In addition, this study did not capture several important social and institutional factors that may influence RTW outcomes, including family support, employer-provided RTW assistance, and insurance compensation. These elements play a critical role in shaping workers’ ability and willingness to return to their jobs. Their omission limits the comprehensiveness of the present analysis and should be addressed in future research. Furthermore, the analyses may be subject to Type I error due to multiple comparisons. As these analyses were exploratory in nature, no adjustment for multiplicity was made, and the findings should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study highlights that both clinical and socioeconomic factors play a significant role in influencing the likelihood of returning to the original workplace following an upper or lower limb fracture. Lower limb fractures, non-permanent employment, prolonged hospitalization, and lower income are independently associated with a higher risk of work discontinuation. Moreover, failure to RTW is associated with poorer psychological well-being, greater perceived social injustice, and reduced quality of life. These findings underscore the importance of early intervention, individualized rehabilitation, and vocational support, particularly for vulnerable populations such as part-time or low-income workers, as essential strategies to facilitate successful reintegration into the workforce and to mitigate long-term psychosocial consequences. Future policies and clinical practices require a multidisciplinary approach to address both medical and occupational challenges faced by injured workers.
