Antiretroviral resistance in at least second-line HIV-1 treatment virologic failure with common InSTI-based regimens
Authors: Anne-Geneviève Marcelin, Cathia Soulie, Marc Wirden, Joshua Gruber, Neia Prata Menezes, Travis Lim, Guillaume Barriere, Diane Descamps, Charlotte Charpentier, Vincent Calvez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:To assess the prevalence and patterns of treatment-emergent resistance-associated mutations (RAMs) in people with HIV (PWH) with ≥1 prior regimen experiencing virologic failure (VF) with bictegravir/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide (B/F/TAF), dolutegravir/lamivudine (DTG/3TC), or cabotegravir/rilpivirine (CAB + RPV) in an observational setting.Design:A noninterventional, multicenter, retrospective, observational study was conducted between January 1, 2022 and December 31, 2024 using a national French multicenter database of genotypic resistance assays performed at confirmed VF.Methods:VF was defined as 2 consecutive HIV-1 plasma viral loads of >50 copies/mL. Genotypic resistance assays were performed using Sanger sequencing. Treatment-emergent RAMs were characterized using the 2024 ANRS algorithm. Clinical history, virologic history, and demographic data were collected from medical records during standard clinical follow-up.Results:A total of 6523 PWH were followed over 3 years. The prevalence of VF during follow-up was 6% with B/F/TAF, 5% with DTG/3TC, and 5% with CAB + RPV. The prevalence of treatment-emergent RAMs at VF were 3% with B/F/TAF, 15% with DTG/3TC, and 32% with CAB + RPV. Dual treatment-emergent integrase strand transfer inhibitor (InSTI) and nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NRTI) RAMs were observed with B/F/TAF and DTG/3TC, while dual treatment-emergent nonnucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI) and InSTI RAMs were observed with CAB + RPV.Conclusions:The overall prevalence of VF was low for all regimens. B/F/TAF was associated with a numerically lower prevalence of RAMs at VF compared with DTG/3TC and CAB + RPV. These observational findings highlight the importance of monitoring resistance patterns to optimize HIV treatment outcomes.Keywords: antiretroviral drug resistance, combination antiretroviral therapy, HIV, mutation, people with HIV, virologic failure

Introduction

Antiretroviral therapies (ARTs) for HIV-1 infection have evolved substantially over >35 years, with newer orally administered integrase strand transfer inhibitors (InSTIs) showing improved viral suppression and reduced side effects [1,2]. Bictegravir and dolutegravir are oral InSTIs that have demonstrated high barriers to resistance, while cabotegravir is the first intramuscularly administered InSTI with dosing every 1 or 2 months (in combination with rilpivirine; CAB + RPV) for HIV-1 treatment maintenance in virologically suppressed people with HIV (PWH) [3–6].

Clinical trials in both viremic and virologically suppressed PWH have demonstrated that bictegravir/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide (B/F/TAF) and dolutegravir/lamivudine (DTG/3TC) are effective in maintaining viral suppression with limited adverse effects [3,4,7,8]. While CAB + RPV has not been assessed in viremic PWH in randomized clinical trials, similar results have been observed in virologically suppressed PWH and PWH who had received oral induction therapy [5,6]. Treatment-emergent resistance-associated mutations (RAMs) have been rarely observed or not observed in Phase 3 switch studies with B/F/TAF and DTG/3TC, but have been observed with CAB + RPV [5–9].

Given the highly controlled nature and stringent exclusion criteria of clinical trials, the real-world PWH population may only be partially represented in these studies [10,11]. In observational settings, PWH often display lower adherence rates (<80%) and may have specific drug-drug interactions that may lead to virologic failure (VF) and the development of treatment-emergent RAMs, limiting future treatment options [12–15]. Additionally, PWH may switch regimens to reduce side effects, avoid drug-drug interactions, or improve regimen forgiveness [16].

Certain regimens may be appropriate for PWH who are more susceptible to adherence challenges and VF; for example, treatment guidelines recommend simpler ART regimens (e.g., fixed-dose, once-daily combinations) with high barriers to resistance to improve treatment adherence [1]. B/F/TAF has been shown to be effective in PWH with imperfect adherence due to its high level of forgiveness [17,18] while DTG/3TC has been shown to be less forgiving to lower adherence [19]. Long-acting CAB + RPV has also demonstrated superior treatment outcomes in PWH with adherence challenges when compared with daily standard-of-care ART [20].

Given this context, it is important to monitor and assess the prevalence and patterns of treatment-emergent RAMs using large databases of real-world PWH who experience VF with common ART regimens. This study used a national, multicenter virology database to measure treatment-emergent RAMs in PWH with a history of ≥1 prior regimen experiencing a confirmed VF in a real-life setting with B/F/TAF, DTG/3TC, or CAB + RPV over a 3-year period.

Methods

Study design and population

A noninterventional, multicenter, retrospective, observational study was conducted from January 1, 2022 to December 31, 2024 using a national French multicenter database of genotypic resistance assays performed at the time of confirmed VF (2 consecutive HIV-1 plasma viral loads of >50 copies/mL) with B/F/TAF (approved by the EMA in 2018 [21]), DTG/3TC (approved by the EMA in 2019 [22]), or CAB + RPV (approved by the EMA in 2020 [23,24]) in adults ≥18 years of age with HIV-1 and a history of ≥1 prior regimen (with or without prior VF). PWH were followed as standard of care and according to the current recommendations.

Additional inclusion criteria were having available genotypic resistance assay results from either first-line treatment initiation (if no history of previous confirmed VF) or from the last previous confirmed VF and/or before the switch to the studied regimen; and having an available medical record in France.

PWH were excluded if they had an HIV-2 infection; were taking any antiretroviral in addition to B/F/TAF, DTG/3TC, or CAB + RPV; had ≥1 prior regimen and received either ibalizumab, enfuvirtide, fostemsavir, or lenacapavir; or had ≥1 prior regimen with evidence of viremia (viral load >200 copies/mL) and received either maraviroc, etravirine, doravirine, dolutegravir twice daily, or darunavir twice daily.

Virologic failure and genotypic resistance analysis

Plasma samples were analyzed by laboratories participating in the Agence Nationale de Recherches sur le SIDA et les Hépatites Virales–Maladies Infectieuses Emergentes (ANRS–MIE) virology network during standard clinical practice. Confirmed VF was defined as two consecutive HIV-1 plasma viral loads of >50 copies/mL. Genotypic resistance assays were performed using Sanger sequencing on the second plasma sample harboring a detectable HIV-1 viral load (>50 copies/mL) at the time of confirmed VF as part of standard clinical care as a reflex from the laboratory. Polymerase chain reaction and sequencing protocols from the ANRS-MIE virology network are available at: https://hivfrenchresistance.org/protocols/. HIV-1 mutations associated with antiretroviral resistance were characterized and reported according to the 2024 ANRS algorithm version 35 [25].

Data collection

Clinical history, virologic history, and demographic data were collected from medical records as part of standard clinical follow-up. Biological data (HIV-1 viral load and HIV-1 RNA/DNA Sanger sequencing results) for PWH who experienced VF at any time during the 3-year period were collected from hospital virology laboratories as part of standard clinical follow-up.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the proportion of any detected treatment-emergent RAMs in PWH with a confirmed VF in each treatment group. Secondary outcomes included the proportions of treatment-emergent InSTI RAMs within each treatment group at VF, treatment-emergent nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NRTI) RAMs for B/F/TAF or DTG/3TC at VF, and treatment-emergent nonnucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI) RAMs for CAB + RPV at VF. Demographic and clinical characteristics for PWH with treatment-emergent RAMs at VF were also assessed.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were descriptive only; categorical variables were reported as proportions of PWH and continuous variables were reported as median (interquartile range [IQR]).

Ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and was a retrospective, noninterventional study with no addition to standard care procedures. All PWH gave their written informed consent to have their medical chart recorded in the electronic medical record system Nadis (www.dataids.org; Fedialis Medica, Marly Le Roi, France; Commission Nationale de l’Informatique et des Libertés [CNIL] number: 770134; October 30, 2001). Reclassification of biological remnants into research material after completion of the ordered virologic tests was approved by the local interventional review board of Pitié-Salpêtrière hospital. Such protocols are exempt from individual informed consent according to the French Public Health Code (CSP Article L.1121-1.1). This study received a declaration of conformity from the CNIL to the reference methodology framework MR-004 on November 4, 2024 (CNIL reference: 2236305), in compliance with the French/European Union requirements of the General Data Protection Regulation. This study has also been declared in the French Health Data Hub under the number 20251013133851.

Results

Treatment-emergent resistance-associated mutations

A total of 6523 PWH were followed over the 3-year study period as part of standard HIV clinical care, among which 3383 (52%) were receiving B/F/TAF, 2580 (40%) were receiving DTG/3TC, and 560 (9%) receiving CAB + RPV, all as a second or greater line of therapy. The proportion of PWH with VF over the follow-up period was 6% (203/3383) for B/F/TAF and 5% for both DTG/3TC (129/2580) and CAB + RPV (28/560). The observed prevalence of treatment-emergent RAMs at confirmed VF was 3% (6/203) with B/F/TAF, 15% (19/129) with DTG/3TC, and 32% (9/28) with CAB + RPV (Fig. 1).

Genotypic resistance analysis and interpretation

Among PWH with treatment-emergent RAMs at VF, the NRTI RAM M184V, which confers resistance to lamivudine (3TC) and emtricitabine (FTC), was detected in 67% (4/6) of PWH receiving B/F/TAF and 47% (9/19) of PWH receiving DTG/3TC. The single treatment-emergent InSTI RAM E138K was detected in 22% (2/9) of PWH with treatment-emergent RAMs at VF with CAB + RPV. No single treatment-emergent InSTI RAMs were detected at VF for PWH receiving B/F/TAF or DTG/3TC.

Dual treatment-emergent InSTI and M184 V RAMs were detected in 33% (2/6) and 53% (10/19) of PWH with treatment-emergent RAMs at VF with B/F/TAF and DTG/3TC, respectively. Dual treatment-emergent NNRTI and InSTI RAMs were detected in 78% (7/9) of PWH with treatment-emergent RAMs at VF with CAB + RPV (Table 1).

Characteristics of PWH with treatment-emergent resistance-associated mutations at virologic failure

Overall, 34 cases of treatment-emergent RAMs at VF were observed. The median (IQR) age of PWH with treatment-emergent RAMs at VF was 47 (42–57) years and 50% were male. The time since first ART initiation ranged from 3.5 to 31 years. Prior to VF, PWH received a median (IQR) of 4 (3–5) treatment lines, 71% were previously exposed to InSTIs, and 47% experienced a prior VF. At the time of the VF, the median (IQR) HIV-1 viral load was 443 (218–1147) copies/mL and the median (IQR) CD4 cell count was 492 (302–610) cells/mm3. The most common previous regimens were darunavir/ritonavir + emtricitabine/tenofovir disoproxil fumarate amongst those receiving B/F/TAF at VF (50%, 3/6), B/F/TAF amongst those receiving DTG/3TC at VF (37%, 7/19), and dolutegravir + rilpivirine amongst those receiving CAB + RPV at VF (44%, 4/9). HIV-1 viral loads at VF were generally higher among PWH receiving DTG/3TC (median [IQR]: 1145 [752–1284] copies/mL) compared with those receiving B/F/TAF (median [IQR]: 213 [159–239] copies/mL) or CAB + RPV (median [IQR]: 216 [164–226] copies/mL; Table 2).

Discussion

In this study, a low prevalence of VF was observed among PWH with a history of ≥1 prior regimen receiving B/F/TAF, DTG/3TC, or CAB + RPV. Among PWH experiencing VF over the 3 years of follow-up, the prevalence of treatment-emergent RAMs was 3% with B/F/TAF, 15% with DTG/3TC, and 32% with CAB + RPV. The most commonly detected treatment-emergent RAM was M184V, observed in all PWH who experienced treatment-emergent RAMs at VF with B/F/TAF and DTG/3TC. Dual treatment-emergent InSTI and M184V RAMs were detected in 33% and 53% of PWH who experienced treatment-emergent RAMs at VF with B/F/TAF and DTG/3TC, respectively. Dual treatment-emergent NNRTI and InSTI RAMs were detected in 78% of PWH who experienced treatment-emergent RAMs at VF with CAB + RPV.

Similar to the results from this study, prior observational studies have reported low rates of VF with B/F/TAF and DTG/3TC [26–28]. Additionally, previous real-world studies of PWH who experienced VF on B/F/TAF also reported low levels of treatment-emergent RAMs (4.2–9.1%) [29–31]. In 2 of these studies, M184 V was the most observed treatment-emergent RAM [29,30]. In 1 study, 71.4% of PWH receiving B/F/TAF achieved viral suppression despite previous VF with B/F/TAF, suggesting that poor adherence may be the main cause of VF in those receiving B/F/TAF [30]. Given these findings, it is possible that the 4 PWH who experienced treatment-emergent M184V mutations with B/F/TAF in the current study may have experienced VF due to poor adherence rather than the M184V mutation itself. Other factors shown to be associated with a higher risk of VF after switching to B/F/TAF are a prior history of VF, prolonged unsuppressed status, high baseline viral load before switching, and CD4 count <350 cells/mm3 [32]. Treatment-emergent RAMs have also been reported to be rare for DTG/3TC at VF [28]; however, a higher prevalence (15–18%) was observed in this study and similar observational database studies [29,31]. In a collaborative cohort analysis, the risk of dolutegravir resistance was shown to be higher for dolutegravir monotherapy and dual therapy, such as DTG/3TC, compared with combination therapies of 3 or more drugs; however, B/F/TAF was not assessed in the study [33].

While there are limited real-world data for CAB + RPV, a few studies have shown low rates of VF, with higher rates of VF occurring in PWH who were not taking treatment in compliance with the label compared with those who were [34–36]. Similar to results from the current study, a recent meta-analysis of 33 studies reported treatment-emergent InSTI RAMs in approximately 40–70% of PWH experiencing VF with CAB + RPV [9].

Treatment-emergent RAMs at VF can significantly impact HIV management and limit future ART options [15]. The most commonly observed treatment-emergent RAM in this study, M184V, elicits high-level resistance to the NRTIs 3TC and FTC, and may reduce opportunities to use single-tablet regimens [37,38]. Per the Health and Human Services (HHS) and European AIDS Clinical Society (EACS) guidelines, PWH with documented resistance mutations are recommended to use at least 2 and preferably 3 fully active drugs in their new regimen; for PWH with M184V/I mutations, these regimens may comprise 2 NRTIs + 1 boosted protease inhibitor (bPI), 2 NRTIs + bictegravir or dolutegravir, or 1 bPI + 1 InSTI. While boosted darunavir combinations are recommended as initial therapy in certain clinical situations, other bPI regimens may increase toxicity and potentially impact quality of life [1]. In some cases, continuing 3TC or FTC may be beneficial and is still recommended, especially if it minimizes pill burden [1,16]. As shown in 2 recent meta-analyses conducted using observational studies, both B/F/TAF and DTG/3TC exhibited high rates of virologic suppression and low rates of VF in PWH with M184V mutations [28,39]. Notably, no treatment-emergent RAMs conferring resistance to tenofovir were observed in this study, which could be attributed to the high barrier to resistance of bictegravir [3]. Additional drug resistance interpretations for the treatment-emergent RAMs observed in this study can be accessed using the 2024 ANRS algorithm version 35 [25].

This study has several potential limitations. First, this study excluded people with no genotypic resistance assay results and people with no available medical records, which may have contributed to selection bias. However, in France, genotypic resistance testing is recommended prior to ART treatment initiation and following VF to aid in treatment management decisions, which could include switching to a new ART regimen [16,40]. Additionally, this observational cohort study could not control for all factors contributing to VF and resistance, including prior undiagnosed resistance mutations, duration of VF, and treatment efficacy. Additionally, treatment adherence was not accounted for due to data limitations. Lastly, this study was not designed to make statistical comparisons or causal claims between groups due to the low sample sizes of PWH with treatment-emergent RAMs at VF, which should be considered when interpreting the data.

Importantly, several observational studies have demonstrated differences in ART regimen prescription patterns wherein PWH with a history of suboptimal adherence are more commonly prescribed B/F/TAF due to its higher forgiveness in low adherence contexts [41,42]. As such, channeling bias may impact interpretation of results regarding prevalence of treatment-emergent RAMs in real-world studies of B/F/TAF.

This observational study has several strengths. Compared with clinical trials in highly selected populations, this real-world study accounts for broader variations in comorbidities, viral loads, adherence, and socioeconomic factors that all can affect treatment outcomes.

In conclusion, while the overall prevalence of VF was low in this study, the prevalence of treatment-emergent RAMs at VF differed across regimens. Findings demonstrate that B/F/TAF has a low prevalence of treatment-emergent RAMs at VF compared with DTG/3TC and CAB + RPV among PWH with a history of ≥1 prior regimen. These findings underscore the importance of monitoring resistance patterns in real-world settings to optimize treatment outcomes for PWH.
