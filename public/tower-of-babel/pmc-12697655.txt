Evaluation of a Deep Learning Tool for Detecting Large Vessel Occlusion and Intracranial Hemorrhage on Noncontrast Computed Tomography Scans
Authors: Xabier Urra, Ansaar Rai, Maria Hernandez, Demetrius Lopes, Laura Oleaga, Tudor Jovin, Marta Olivé Gadea, Kiffon Keigher, Judith Cendrero, Leonardo Tanzi, Victor Salvia, Santiago Ortega‐Gutierrez, Marc Ribo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDThe purpose of this study is to assess the accuracy of automated artificial intelligence (AI) deep‐learning‐based modules in predicting suspected intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) or anterior circulation large vessel occlusion (LVO) on noncontrast computed tomography (NCCT) studies.METHODSWe conducted a multicenter international retrospective cohort study, involving 6 stroke centers from the United States and Europe. We included patients in whom an acute stroke was suspected on admission and who underwent an NCCT and a CT angiography when ICH was not observed. Two neuroradiologists and a third one in case of discrepancies retrospectively evaluated all images and established the presence of ICH on NCCT and LVO on computed tomography angiography (ground truth). All NCCT scans were analyzed using 2 automated AI deep‐learning modules (Methinks, Barcelona, Spain) to assess the presence of ICH or LVO.RESULTSTo assess the performance of the NCCT‐ICH module, a total of 200 patients were included in the study. The neuroradiologist's evaluation confirmed the presence of ICH in 97 cases (48.5%). To assess the performance of the NCCT‐LVO module, 382 patients were analyzed, with the neuroradiologist identifying a LVO in 141 cases (36.9%). The AI module for NCCT‐ICH detection demonstrated a sensitivity of 94.9% (95% CI]: 88.4%–98.3%) and specificity of 99.0% (95% CI: 94.7%–99.9%) with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.974 (95% CI: 0.94–0.99). The LVO detection AI module on NCCT demonstrated a sensitivity of 81.6% (95% CI: 74.2–87.6), and specificity of 87.1% (95% CI: 82.2–91.1) with an area under the receiver operating characteristic of 0.915 (95% CI: 0.88–0.94).CONCLUSIONSThe AI modules demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity in predicting ICH and LVO, suggesting their potential in offering support in clinical decisions in stroke networks immediately after NCCT is performed.

Stroke is the second leading cause of death and the third leading cause of disability worldwide. According to the World Health Organization, 15 million people suffer strokes globally each year, resulting in 5 million deaths and another 5 million cases of permanent disability 1 . From the age of 25 onwards, the lifetime risk of having a stroke is estimated at 24.9% 2 . Given its high morbidity and mortality, a rapid and accurate diagnosis is essential to ensure timely treatment and improve patient outcomes. Stroke is typically suspected following the sudden onset of focal neurological deficits such as facial drooping, arm weakness, and speech impairment. Upon arrival at a stroke center, patients should undergo an immediate neurological assessment to determine symptom severity 3 , 4 . This is followed by an urgent brain imaging evaluation to confirm the diagnosis and guide treatment options. Intravenous thrombolysis and endovascular treatment have proven to be highly effective reperfusion therapies aimed at preserving brain tissue and improving clinical outcomes 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 .

However, their indication and success rely on immediately ruling out an intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) by performing a noncontrast computed tomography (NCCT), and the prompt identification of large vessel occlusion (LVO), which is typically performed using computed tomography angiography (CTA). Because CTA requires contrast administration, access to this imaging technique is limited in certain centers, particularly in nonspecialized or resource‐limited regions, leading to delays in diagnosis and treatment. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 . Additionally, although advanced imaging techniques such as CTA, CT perfusion, and magnetic resonance imaging offer valuable insights for stroke assessment, they require specialized equipment and expertise, often leading to longer interpretation times and potential treatment delays. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18

To address these challenges, we propose the use of automated artificial intelligence (AI) modules capable of detecting ICH and LVO directly from NCCT scans. These AI‐driven tools may facilitate broader access to stroke diagnosis by providing a rapid, contrast‐free alternative that could support treatment decisions in a wide range of hospital settings. More important, they may represent a transformative step in stroke care, reducing interobserver variability, accelerating the detection, optimizing patient selection, and even predicting individualized treatment responses 19 , 20 , 21 . In this study, we aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of an automated software in identifying and notifying cases in which an ICH or an anterior circulation LVO is predicted in NCCT studies compared to both general radiologists’ (GRs’) and neuroradiologists’ (NRs’) assessment in a multicenter cohort of consecutive patients admitted with a suspected stroke.

Subjects

This was an international multicenter retrospective observational cohort study that included consecutive patients from 6 stroke centers in the United States and the European Union, where an acute ischemic stroke was suspected on admission. The inclusion criteria were patients suspected of suffering an acute stroke, over 21 years old, and undergoing urgent NCCT±CTA. The exclusion criteria included images not fulfilling specific technical Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) Tag requirements. The full inclusion and exclusion criteria are shown in Table S1. All patients underwent an NCCT and, when ICH was not observed, a CTA was also performed. The 6 participating institutions provided positive cases: 2 provided consecutive cases of LVOs, 2 provided consecutive cases of ICHs, and 2 contributed consecutive cases of both LVOs and ICHs. All institutions also provided consecutive negative cases (no ICH/no LVO).

Due to data anonymization protocols, all temporal metadata were removed from the DICOM files, and as a result, the exact time frames during which cases were collected at each center could not be determined. However, per institutional agreements, all cases reflect routine clinical practice and were selected consecutively in accordance with each site's imaging availability, ethical approvals, and the local implementation timeline of their imaging and AI infrastructure.

To ensure independence between participants in case selection and the algorithm developers, methods, documentation, and data access were kept separate. Imaging assessment was performed by independent radiologists blinded to the study details.

The study received ethical approval from the institutional review boards of each participating center, ensuring compliance with established ethical guidelines and regulatory requirements. Given the retrospective nature of the study, informed consent was waived.

Scanner Machine Models

The study includes cases from various CT vendors: Siemens Healthineers (Erlangen, Germany), GE Healthcare (Wauwatosa, WI, United States), Koninklijke Philips (Amsterdam, Netherlands), and Canon (Minato City, Japan).

Minimum Sample Size Calculation

To calculate the minimum sample size needed for the NCCT‐LVO analysis study, an internal retrospective pilot study conducted for this device showed an estimated sensitivity of 71% and a specificity of 92%. These values were used as expected values to determine the sample size, calculating the 95% CIs for sensitivity and specificity. We then adjusted the sample size until the lower bound of the CIs was >50% for sensitivity and >80% for specificity. This resulted in a final minimum sample of 26 positive LVO cases and 46 negative (non‐LVO) cases for the performance analysis study.

To calculate the minimum sample size needed for the NCCT‐ICH analysis study, an internal retrospective pilot study conducted for this device showed an estimated sensitivity of 96% and a specificity of 100%. These values were used as expected values to determine the sample size, calculating the 95% CIs for sensitivity and specificity. We then adjusted the sample size until the lower bound of the CI was >80% for both sensitivity and specificity. This resulted in a final minimum sample of 26 positive ICH cases and 19 negative (non‐ICH) cases for the performance analysis study.

To ensure better representativity, we used the minimum sample size—calculated as described—as a reference and subsequently expanded it.

Quality Assessment and AI Software Predictive Outcome Performance Assessment

A quality assessment classification algorithm was used to predict whether the images were suitable for evaluation. This model was specifically trained to identify and discard images exhibiting notable noise caused by a granular pattern that reduces definition, significant artifacts such as those resulting from patient motion or metal reflections, the absence of a soft tissue reconstruction kernel, an improper field of view that does not adequately capture the head, or the presence of residual contrast.

All cases provided by the participating organizations underwent a ground truthing process conducted by an external Core Lab. The images that passed the quality assessment were evaluated in this process by 2 US board‐certified NRs, who had access to patient sex, age, and, when available, baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score. In cases where the 2 initial readers disagreed on the presence of ICH or LVO, a third read was performed to establish a majority vote and determine the final ground truth. The readers independently reviewed the cases using the Report Form, available in Table S2 for ICH and in Table S3 for LVO in the supplementary material.

Four predictive performance outcomes were evaluated– (1) NCCT‐ICH AI module performance in identifying ICH on NCCT scans; (2) NCCT‐LVO AI module performance in identifying anterior circulation LVO on NCCT scans; (3) comparison of expert (NR) and nonexpert (GRs) readers' performance with NCCT‐LVO AI module in detecting LVO on NCCT scans; and (4) assessment of the time to notification of NCCT results.

NCCT‐ICH Module Performance

For the performance assessment of the NCCT‐ICH module, ICH cases included intraparenchymal, subarachnoid, intraventricular, and subdural hemorrhages. When multiple NCCT scans were available for a single patient, the earliest scan meeting the inclusion criteria was selected for analysis.

NCCT‐LVO Module Performance

For the performance assessment of the NCCT‐LVO module, LVO‐positive cases included anterior occlusions identified at the level of the M1 segment of the middle cerebral artery (MCA), proximal M2‐MCA, and terminal internal carotid artery (ICA). LVO‐negative cases consisted of the remaining consecutively gathered patients, including posterior circulation and medium vessel occlusions cases

Sensitivity Comparison Between Readers and the NCCT‐LVO Module for LVO Detection on NCCT

After establishing the ground truth—based on confirmation of LVO presence or absence by certified NRs using both NCCT and CTA—a subset of cases was randomly selected, comprising half of LVO‐positive and LVO‐negative cases. These NCCT scans were independently evaluated by 2 certified NRs and 2 GRs to identify the presence of LVO. The diagnostic performance of the expert and nonexpert readers was subsequently compared with that of the NCCT‐LVO AI module.

All readers were blinded to CTA images and clinical information to ensure unbiased assessments. The comparison focused on LVO detection using NCCT alone, allowing for an objective evaluation of the AI tool's performance relative to that of human readers. This analysis aimed to assess the diagnostic accuracy of the AI module and highlight differences between automated and expert/nonexpert interpretation of NCCT scans.

Additionally, a subgroup analysis was conducted on cases without early ischemic changes, defined by an Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (ASPECTS) of 10. This subgroup analysis presents the performance of the AI software and human readers in detecting LVO in the absence of visible ischemic signs, providing further insight into the capabilities and limitations of both human and AI‐based assessments under optimal imaging conditions.

Time to Notification of Results Assessment

Time to notification is the time elapsed from the complete series reception in the DICOM router to the user notification reception. This interval encompasses the processing time required to ensure that the series is correctly received, stored, and made accessible through the relevant systems. It also includes any delays in communication or technical handling that may occur before the user is alerted. As this was a retrospective study, to simulate the time to notification, the predictions were simulated through an additional channel to record the timestamps.

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Sensitivity and specificity of the NCCT‐ICH and NCCT‐LVO modules were calculated by comparing the software predictions to the ICH ground truth based on NCCT and the LVO ground truth based on CTA, respectively, in the stand‐alone evaluation.

Additionally, we assessed overall model performance using C‐statistics with receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calculating the area under the curve (AUC) and its CIs.

Subsequently, the sensitivity of the software was compared to the expert (NRs) and nonexpert (GRs) readers in the 189 cases randomly selected, using a bootstrapping approach for hypothesis testing, evaluating sequentially both noninferiority (NI) and superiority. The comparison was based on the bootstrapped distribution of the difference in sensitivity, expressed as (software‐average readers).

For NI testing, a prespecified margin of 0.025 (or 2.5%) was applied, meaning the lower bound of the 95% CI of the difference had to be >2.5% to demonstrate NI. For superiority testing, the margin was set to 0, requiring the lower bound of the 95% CI to be >0. If the entire bootstrapped CI lies above the specified margin, the software meets the criterion for either NI or superiority. The NI margin of 2.5% and the superiority margin of 0% were prespecified based on commonly accepted practices for diagnostic accuracy studies, as described in literature assessing AI‐based diagnostic tools in comparison to human performance 38

CIs were calculated using the Clopper–Pearson method 22 when the variable followed a binomial distribution. Otherwise, we used bootstrapping methods (for the hypothesis testing and for the time to notification intervals). Bootstrapping was performed with 100 000 resamples to ensure stability in the estimation of CIs and hypothesis testing.

NCCT LVO AI Module Sensitivity Comparison With Experts and Nonexperts

In relation to the presence of LVO determined on CTA (ground truth), we observed a significant difference in sensitivity between the device and human readers in LVO prediction based on NCCT.

Specifically, the device's sensitivity (81.6%) exceeded that of expert readers (42.2%) by 39.4% (95% CI: 27.5%/50.7%), nonexpert readers (29.5%) by 52.1% (95% CI: 40.1%/63.4%), and all readers combined (35.8%) by 45.8% (95% CI: 35.2%/55.6%). These results demonstrated superiority over nonexpert readers and NI over expert readers as the preestablished criteria stated.

The device specificity (89.0%) is comparable to that of expert readers (93.6%; −4.6% [95% CI: −11.4% to −1.6%] difference), nonexpert readers (89.4%; −0.4% (95% CI: −7.6% to +6.3%) difference), and of all readers combined (91.5%; −2.5% (95% CI: −8.8% to +3.3%) difference).

The differences in specificity between the device and human readers (both expert and nonexpert) were not statistically significant, indicating comparable specificity across the groups. Figure 4 shows the sensitivity and specificity values for the device and each reader group.

DISCUSSION

The NCCT‐ICH and NCCT‐LVO modules demonstrated robust diagnostic performance in detecting ICH and LVO on NCCT scans, reflected in their high sensitivity, specificity, and AUC values.

In comparison to human readers, the AI software exhibited significantly higher sensitivity than both expert and nonexpert readers in LVO detection on NCCT scans. Specificity remained in all cases >89% and comparable between the algorithm and human readers, with no statistically significant differences observed. Notably, in a challenging subgroup of cases with LVOs but no early ischemic changes, the algorithm outperformed expert NRs, suggesting it may assist in detecting subtle findings that are sometimes challenging to identify in routine practice.

Furthermore, the average notification times for ICH and LVO detection were just over a minute on average, indicating that the software could contribute to more efficient workflows and support decision‐making in time‐critical stroke care.

Accurate and timely exclusion of ICH is essential before administering intravenous thrombolysis, as ICH represents an absolute contraindication. In this context, the NCCT‐ICH module may support faster decision‐making by promptly identifying or ruling out hemorrhage, particularly in settings without immediate access to expert radiological review. By accelerating ICH detection and streamlining stroke workflows, this tool may contribute to reducing door‐to‐needle times and supporting timely access to intravenous thrombolysis, particularly in settings where expert review is not immediately available.

Early identification of LVO on admission NCCT scans may be very useful in the management of patients admitted with a suspected acute ischemic stroke by accelerating stroke diagnosis, potentially avoiding additional contrast imaging acquisition and guiding further streamlined decisions related to patient transfer to the angiosuite or a comprehensive stroke center to initiate endovascular treatment 23 , 24 .

In this context, different studies 24 , 25 , 26 recently found that patients selected for endovascular treatment in the 6‐ to 24‐hour time achieved similar outcomes, regardless of whether the indication was based on NCCT or advanced imaging techniques such as CT perfusion or magnetic resonance imaging. In this context, our study suggests that within 24 hours from symptom onset, an admission NCCT scan, if capable of predicting the presence of an LVO, may be sufficient to triage emergent LVOs. Simplifying and shortening the selection process could allow for faster intervention, particularly in settings where advanced imaging is not readily available.

However, this approach requires significant expertise in the interpretation of neuroimaging to ensure accurate assessment and decision‐making. AI can play a crucial role by enhancing relevant subtle findings on NCCT, assisting in their interpretation, and helping to streamline an accurate diagnosis, ultimately facilitating timely and effective patient care 27 .

AI‐powered tools are able to analyze NCCT scans rapidly and may assist in highlighting subtle signs of acute ischemic stroke that can be challenging to detect, potentially supporting clinician interpretation. By automatically detecting critical features such as early ischemic changes, AI can assist clinicians in making more accurate evaluations regarding patient eligibility for reperfusion therapies. In addition to improving speed and accuracy, AI can also support less experienced clinicians by providing a second opinion, thus enhancing confidence in decision‐making. This can be especially beneficial in settings where access to specialized neuroimaging professionals is limited.

Although many AI algorithms currently rely on CTA scans for detecting LVOs and CT perfusion images to assess infarcted areas and the ischemic penumbra, these advanced imaging techniques can be time consuming and delay treatment initiation 28 . Additionally, they require the administration of contrast agents, which can pose risks for some patients. Furthermore, these techniques are not available in many hospitals, limiting access for patients who might benefit from them 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 . Schlossman et al compared the performance of 2 commercial AI tools for detecting LVOs using CTA scans 20 . Both tools effectively identified most anterior circulation occlusions, with one showing higher sensitivity and the other demonstrating greater accuracy and specificity. Similarly, Yahav‐Dovrat et al evaluated another AI algorithm, which uses a convolutional neural network to detect LVO on CTA scans, finding a sensitivity of 82%, a negative predictive value of 96%, and an accuracy of 89%.

In this context, implementing AI for early stroke detection and characterization on NCCT, matching the performance achieved when contrast imaging is used, could facilitate better resource allocation and optimize the use of advanced imaging techniques 29 .

A previous study evaluated an earlier version of the AI software and demonstrated that its use for LVO detection based on NCCT can potentially reduce the need for a CTA and accelerate the time to LVO diagnosis 30 .

In our study, the performance of the algorithms for LVO detection (sensitivity 81.56%, specificity: 87.14%) on NCCT was compared with previous studies 31 , 32 , which reported a sensitivity of 63.5% and 68.75% and specificities of 95.1% and 89.5% respectively. In the case of LVO detection, a high sensitivity should be favored for screening devices to ensure that all relevant cases are detected. Table 4 shows a comparison of performance metrics among the different published algorithms. However, it is important to acknowledge that direct comparisons between such predictive tools have limitations due to variations in the data sets used. These differences may affect the accuracy and generalizability of the results.

In the present study, the sensitivity achieved with the AI software detecting LVO on NCCT (81.6%) outperformed both expert and nonexpert readers, who only achieved moderate rates of sensitivity (45.1% and 32.4% respectively), without reducing the specificity (Figure 4). In particular, the AI software excelled in LVO detection on NCCT when the early ischemic changes determined by the ASPECTS were less visible (ASPECTS 10), as shown in Table 3. In this subgroup of cases, which represents a greater challenge for readers when LVO diagnosis is based solely on NCCT, the sensitivity reached by expert (20.9%) and nonexpert (8.4%) readers was substantially lower than the sensitivity of the AI tool (58.3%).

Additionally, a noteworthy observation was the relatively lower sensitivity of the NCCT‐LVO module in detecting ICA occlusions compared with M1, with performance levels similar to M2 detection, despite the ICA being a larger‐caliber vessel. One possible explanation is the presence of collateral circulation via the circle of Willis, which can partially compensate for distal ICA occlusions and result in subtler ischemic changes on NCCT, making detection more difficult for both human readers and AI algorithms.

Early identification by the AI algorithm of ICH and LVO can contribute to a reliable and accelerated selection of patients likely to benefit from reperfusion therapy. The ICH and LVO modules may support faster image interpretation and aid decision‐making based on NCCT studies, which, unlike contrast‐based imaging, are generally readily available in most centers worldwide 33 , 34 , 35 . Additionally, the time to notification was fastest for this AI software (1.35 minutes for LVO suspicion), compared with other AI tools: 1.6 minutes 36 for one and 2.5 minutes for another 37 . These results underscore the device's efficiency in promptly identifying critical conditions, thereby potentially enhancing the timeliness of clinical interventions (Table 4).

It is essential to have a clear understanding of the intended clinical role and regulatory framework of the evaluated AI modules. Although the NCCT‐LVO module demonstrated superior sensitivity compared with expert NRs in this study, it is not designed to replace CTA, which remains the gold standard for LVO detection. Instead, the tool is envisioned as a complementary decision‐support system that can assist in early triage, particularly in high‐volume settings or during off hours when immediate CTA interpretation may be delayed. In resource‐limited environments—such as primary stroke centers without routine access to CTA or expert radiologists—the NCCT‐LVO module may offer valuable support in identifying suspected LVOs using NCCT alone, helping to prompt earlier transfer decisions or remote consultations with comprehensive stroke centers. This adaptive use could help mitigate disparities in access to specialized stroke care and advanced imaging.

Importantly, in alignment with current regulatory frameworks, the AI modules evaluated in this study are approved only as diagnostic aids and must be used under the supervision of qualified health care professionals. These systems are not authorized to provide definitive diagnoses or guide clinical decisions independently. The final responsibility for image interpretation and treatment decisions remains with the attending physician. Accordingly, AI tools such as the NCCT‐LVO module should be integrated into clinical workflows as supportive instruments that enhance—but do not replace—standard diagnostic pathways and clinical expertise.

Beyond diagnostic accuracy, the implementation of AI‐based stroke tools also raises economic and practical considerations. Early health economic evaluations suggest that such tools can be cost effective by facilitating timely treatment and reducing disability 38 , 39 . However, feasibility may vary across health systems, as costs related to licensing, information technology infrastructure, and staff training need to be considered, especially in resource‐limited environments. Therefore, although early studies suggest favorable cost‐effectiveness, practical feasibility will ultimately vary across health care settings.

The study is limited by its retrospective design. Additionally, images with significant artifacts, excessive noise, or venous contamination that could compromise interpretation—either by the algorithm or by expert readers—were excluded. Although these exclusions were necessary to ensure a reliable reference standard and to minimize bias in the performance comparison between human and AI assessments, we acknowledge that this approach limits the generalizability of our findings to clinical settings where image quality may be suboptimal. Table S4 provides the reasons for case exclusion in the LVO analysis. We have also highlighted the need for future studies to assess algorithm performance under real‐world clinical conditions, including cases with suboptimal imaging. Nonetheless, the study's robustness is bolstered by a substantial sample size sourced from 6 different stroke centers using a variety of CT vendors, which enhances the generalizability of the findings. There are specific challenges in detecting LVO, including cases with incomplete vascular occlusions, very short times from symptom onset, the concomitant presence of chronic lesions, and patients with prior contrast administration. These limitations have been thoroughly described and analyzed in numerous literature sources 40 . Additionally, regarding the time to notification assessment in a real clinical setting, variability may arise due to differences in the performance and configuration of the local networks and servers installed at the various participating centers. Finally, race and ethnicity information was not available for the majority of cases due to anonymization protocols. As a result, subgroup analyses could not be performed. Future studies, including systematic collection of race and ethnicity data, will be important to confirm the generalizability of our findings across diverse populations.

CONCLUSIONS

The present study demonstrates that AI‐based NCCT‐ICH and NCCT‐LVO detection devices could effectively be used to accelerate the triage of patients with acute stroke, reducing in many cases the need for additional contrast images.
