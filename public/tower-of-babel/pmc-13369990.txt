Phenotypic evaluation of all-stage (ASR) and adult-plant resistance (APR) to leaf rust (Puccinia triticina Eriks) in an Iranian bread wheat germplasm
Authors: Saba Delfan, Mohammad Reza Bihamta, Seyed Taha Dadrezaei, Alireza Abbasi, Hadi Alipour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenetic resistance is the most effective and environmentally friendly strategy for controlling leaf rust (Puccinia triticina Eriks), the most common and devastating foliar diseases that affect wheat production worldwide. In order to identify the source of resistance to leaf rust, an Iranian bread wheat germplasm composing 217 landraces and 103 commercial varieties was evaluated at both seedling and adult plant stages. The seedling experiment was conducted in the form of randomized complete block design (RCBD) using twelve Puccinia triticina isolates under greenhouse conditions, and adult plant stage assessment was performed in two field environments (Ahwaz and Karaj) during the 2020–2021 growing season. The results showed that there was a high level of genetic variation among the genotypes at seedling stage (p < 0.01), however susceptibility was predominant in all twelve isolates, with only 4% of genotypes showing resistance reaction to all isolates. The genotypes showed wide-spectrum differences based on FDS, CI and AUDPC indeces at adult plant stage in field conditions. The genotypes were classified to four classes based on cluster analysis, constructed from integrated seedling and adult plant stage data as follows; resistant (66%), moderately resistant (13%), moderately susceptible (18%), and susceptible (3%). Notably, 17 genotypes were resistant at the adult plant stage but susceptible at the seedling stage, suggesting the existence of APR genes; like Lr34, Lr46, Lr67, and Lr68, providing slow-rusting resistance. In general, the results of the study suggest that Iranian wheat germplasm may hold as a genetic resource to breeding programms with durable resistance, leading to sustainable wheat production and food security.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49327-z.Keywords: Wheat leaf rust, All-stage resistance, Adult-plant resistance, Iranian germplasm, Durable resistanceSubject terms: Biotechnology, Genetics, Plant sciences

Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is a staple food and an essential source of calories and protein for millions of people worldwide1. This crop plays a vital role in human food security, as it occupies a significant portion of Iran’s arable land2. However, wheat production is constantly at risk due to foliar diseases3,4. One of the most prevalent and destructive of which is leaf rust caused by Puccinia triticina Eriks (Pt)5. Leaf rust occurs annually in most Iranian wheat-producing regions6,7, especially in the humid and temperate zones. Depending on the wheat variety and environmental conditions, yield losses can reach 20–40%8. Due to the economic and environmental issues of using fungicides9, the most effective and sustainable approach to control the disease is genetic resistance10.

The genetic resistance to leaf rust can be classified as all-stage resistance (ASR) and adult-plant resistance (APR)11,12. ASR, also known as seedling or race-specific resistance, is usually conferred by one major gene5, and offers a high level of protection. Despite its high effectiveness, ASR is usually short-lived because Pt populations develop rapidly. Conversely, APR has a polygenic control manner and only appears during adult growth phases and offers partial protection, meaning that it slows but does not completely prevent infection5,13. APR is more durable and environmentally stable than ASR. Integrating ASR and APR is one of the strategies that are considered to achieve durable and wide-spectrum resistance to rusts14.

Located in the Fertile Crescent, Iran is the center of wheat diversity and domestication, and its germplasm is a valuable source of alleles that confer disease resistance15. Although ASR and APR evaluations have been studied in Iranian bread wheat germplasm, these studies are usually limited to seedling evaluation against a small number of rust pathotypes and rarely include comprehensive field assessment of APR, which has typically been conducted in small germplasm collections or at a single location7,16–18. Consequently, information on the distribution patterns of ASR and APR and their capacity to provide durable resistance remains limited. There is still no large-scale study evaluating both resistance types in Iranian bread wheat using combined seedling and adult plant phenotyping across multiple environments. Previous studies have assessed either seedling or adult plant resistance16,19,20, but the absence of integrated evaluation reduces the ability to identify germplasm with both effective seedling and adult plant resistance. These two resistance components are essential for breeding durable cultivars against evolving Pt populations in Iran and for achieving sustainable wheat production, as demonstrated in international studies21–24.

Therefore, the research objectives were to (i) identify ASR under controlled conditions against common Iranian Pt isolates, (ii) determine APR in different field environments, and (iii) identify genotypes with stable and possibly durable resistance. Due to the emphasis on phenotypic assessment, the study will provides valuable information on the occurrence of ASR and APR in the Iranian germplasm and also bring up the potential in the breeding programs.

Under this combined method, the research fills the gap between seedling level analysis and adult plant performance in the field conditions. The discovery of complementary genotypes of ASR and APR response will facilitate the improvment wheat cultivars with durable and wide-spectrum resistance to leaf rust. These varieties will be able to minimize yield losses, reduce dependence on chemical control25,26, and increase the reliability of wheat production under variable environmental conditions and changing pathogen populations.

Results

Virulence/avirulence profile of Pt isolates

The results of determining the virulence/avirulence profile of 12 Pt isolates on differential sets of Thatcher near-isogenic lines are presented in Table S3. Overall, six physiological races were identified, including PFTTT and PKTTT, PKTTS, PDTRR, PDKTT and FFRSQ. Among them, PFTTT (41.7%) and PKTTT (41.7%) were the most common races, mainly collected from Khuzestan province (southwest of Iran). Races FFRSQ and PDKTT had an occurrence frequency of 8.3% each. For each PKTTS and PDTRR races, four and two isolates were found, respectively.

Frequencies of virulence to Lr genes or gene combinations were assessed. Virulence to Lr2a, Lr9 and Lr19 were not found in any studied isolate while all isolates were virulent on isogenic lines possessing Lr2c, Lr3, Lr3bg, Lr10, Lr11, Lr14b, Lr17, Lr18, Lr21, Lr24, Lr25, Lr30, Lr32, Lr33, Lr36 and LrB genes, which are ASR genes. Moreover, although the Lr12, Lr22a, Lr22b, Lr34, Lr35, Lr13, and Lr37 genes are APR genes and should be sensitive to all isolates at the seedling stage, the Lr13 gene was resistant to isolate LR-94-5.

Seedling stage

Table S1 gives the reaction of genotypes to leaf rust isolates at the seedling stage. Comparisons of 320 wheat genotypes to all Pt isolates showed notable variation in seedling stage, yet susceptibility was dominant in the population (Fig. 2). The Boolani cultivar was highly susceptible and exhibited the ITs between 3 and 4 against all twelve isolates. The range of reactions to Pt isolates by wheat genotypes was wide, including the range of highly susceptible (IT = 4, LS = 9) to immune (IT = 0, LS = 0) (Fig. 3a), but most of the genotypes showed higher linearized disease scores, which is indicative of high aggressiveness of the tested isolates and indicates that effective resistance was broken in a number of genotypes. The results showed that out of the 320 genotypes studied, 5, 17, 10, 18, 18, 17, 12, 12, 14, 9, 11, and 24% were resistant to the LR-98-1, LR-98-12, LR-98-13, LR-98-14, LR-98-15, LR-98-19, LR-98-22, LR-99-2, LR-99-3, LR-97-12, LR-98-38, and LR-G32 isolates, respectively. Only eight genotypes (about 4%) were resistant (IT ≤ 3−, LS ≤ 7) to all the tested isolates (Table 2). To find out whether the observed seedling resistance was expressed at later stages of growth, all genotypes were further tested under adult plant stage under field conditions.

Adult plant stage

Since the reaction of most genotypes to leaf rust was similar in both environments (Table S1, Fig. 3b)), the mean disease response across the two locations was used to assess adult-plant resistance.

Violin plots of FDS, AUDPC, and CI in 320 wheat genotypes exhibited moderate skewness and the dispersion was different, and indicates that there is a high degree of phenotypic variability of disease response. CI and FDS had a mild right skew with moderate dispersion whereas AUDPC had wider and asymmetric dispersion with both resistant and susceptible accessions (Fig. 4). The high disease severity (100 S) in the cultivar Bolani (as the disease spreader) showed that the disease was uniformly established in the entire field.

Distribution of 320 wheat accessions based on FDS, AUDPC, and CI at adult palnt stage in field conditions.

Based on the FDS the evaluated population was divided into three groups: the first group (54 accessions) had a final disease severity of 0–30%. The second group (47 accessions) had a FDS of 31–60% and the third group with the highest frequency (218 accessions) had an FDS higher than 60%.

AUDPC is a quantitative measure of total resistance, which is the result of all factors such as differences in environmental conditions, genotypes and pathogen populations that affect disease development; therefore, genotypes with low AUDPC value ​​are likely to have APR genes, contributing to durable resistance. The AUDPC values ​​of the genotypes were in the range of 0-1900. According to the AUDPC values, genotypes with AUDPC greater than 500 were considered as susceptible genotypes7. This group included 238 genotypes (AUDPC=516_1468). The AUDPC value of the Bolani variety was 1900. Such genotypes develop the disease rapidly (fast rusting). Conversly, AUDPC below 500 was considered as acceptable resistance. This group included 83 genotypes (AUDPC=0_500). Low AUDPC values ​​in these genotypes indicate the ability of these genotypes to slow down the disease progression in the early stage of development (Slow rusting). According to the results, it was determined that almost all genotypes with MR and MS responses were classified into this group. Among them, genotypes with AUDPC between 151 and 500 were considered as moderately-susceptible genotypes, this group included 53 genotypes (17%). Also, AUDPC between 1 and 150 were considered as moderately-resistant genotypes, which included 28 genotypes (10%). In addition, genotypes with AUDPC = 0 were considered as immune genotypes (O), which included only the cultivar Morvarid.

Based on the response of genotypes in the greenhouse and field (Table S1), the genotypes can be classified as follows: The first group is susceptible at the seedling stage and resistant at the adult plant stage: This group included 17 genotypes. This group is particularly important as they may carry known or novel APR genes. This hypothesis warrants validation using molecular markers. The second group is resistant at both the seedling and adult plant stages, included 20 genotypes. The resistance in these varieties was of the complete type, and the likelihood of resistance being overcome in them is high. It should be noted that in addition to race-specific resistance genes, this group may have non-specific resistance genes that are masked by the effect of specific resistance genes. The third group, susceptible at both the adult plant and seedling stages included 168 genotypes and lacked effective race-specific and non-specific resistance genes against the used isolates. The susceptible cultivar Boolani was also included in this group. The fourth group, resistant at the seedling stage and susceptible at the adult plant stage, included 13 genotypes, indicating that it lacked APR genes; but they were probably influenced by other races than those used at the seedling stage.

Cluster analysis

Cluster analysis was performed based on ITs of twelve leaf rust isolates at the seedling stage, and on CI, FDS, and AUDPC obtained from the mean of two environments (Karaj and Ahwaz) at the adult plant stage. The cluster analysis classified the population into four distinct clusters: susceptible (S), moderately-susceptible (MS), moderately-resistant (MR) and resistant (R) (Fig. 5, Fig S2, Table S1). The first cluster (susceptible), which also included the susceptible cultivar Bolani, consisted of 212 genotypes (172 landrace populations and 41 commercial cultivars), representing approximately 66% of the population. This cluster showed the highest mean AUDPC (1072), CI (77.5), FDS (77.5,) and seedling IT (8.8), indicating a high level of susceptibility. The second cluster (moderately susceptible) comprised 41 genotypes (13% of the population) and was characterized by an average IT of 5.4, AUDPC of 414, FDS of 36, and CI of 30. Although these genotypes exhibited slower disease development compared to the susceptible cluster, they still showed relatively high levels of rust progression in the field. The third cluster (moderately-resistant) contained 59 genotypes (about 18% of the population) with an average seedling stage IT of 8.2, AUDPC of 345, FDS of 31, and CI of 25. Finally, the forth cluster (resistant) consisted of 8 genotypes (all landraces) included 3% of the population, which had an IT of 1, AUDPC of 140, and CI of 10, and FDS of 19. The final plant reactions were 10MR to 35 S, which confirmed the presence of the high-level adult plant resistance and slowed down the pathogen propagation. In the second, third and fourth clusters, field reactions were mostly MS to MR, indicating partial resistance and slow disease progression.

Discussion

The Iranian wheat germplasm is one of the least exploited but genetically diverse resources of Triticum aestivum, which has developed in one of the most important domestication and diversification centers15,27. These collections contain large amounts of adaptive variation, including resistance against a variety of biotic stresses, such as bunts17, stem rust28, aphids29, as well as resistance to major abiotic stresses, such as drought30 and salinity31. However, the majority of Iranian accessions are still not adequately characterized and utilized in contemporary wheat improvement efforts32,33. The current work showed a great phenotypic variation for resistance to Pt in Iranian bread wheat germplasm, and this phenomenon demonstrates their utility in the resistance alleles pool to be utilized in breeding programms in the future.

Various factors such as selection pressure, mutation, sexual and asexual recombination, and migration can drive the emergence of new races and pathogenic diversity34. In wheat rust diseases (leaf rust, black rust, and yellow rust), urediospores can migrate thousands of kilometers, causing exotic races and clonal reproduction35,36.

In this study, 12 Pt isolates were collected from different wheat-growing regions of Iran and evaluated for race characteristics based on their response to 20 differential sets carrying different Lr resistance genes. The results showed that most of the resistance genes were ineffective against the Pt population. However, no virulence on Lr2a, Lr9 and Lr19, indicating that these genes were still effective in Iran. Interestingly, the Lr13 gene, an APR gene, was effective against LR-94-5 isolate likely due to its expression at higher temperatures during the seedling stage, which is consistent with previous observations37.

Surveys conducted in Iran from 2010 to 2017 similary showed no virulence for Lr2a, limited virulence for Lr1 in a few provinces, and virulence against Lr28 only in durum wheat and wild barley isolates35. Another study on 33 isolates collected from different regions of Iran reported that most of the resistance genes are ineffective against Pt populations. However, low virulence was reported on Lr2a, Lr9, Lr19, Lr25, Lr28 and Lr29 genes, indicating that these genes are still effective against the current wheat leaf rust population in Iran38,39.

The Lr9 gene has been reported on chromosome 6B40. This gene shows low response to environmental changes such as high temperature. Given the effectiveness of this gene against the used isolates, this gene is recommended as an effective gene that can be used in the country’s breeding programs. Regarding the Lr19 gene, it has been stated that susceptibility to this gene is rare on a global scale. Despite the effectiveness of this gene in most parts of the world, the use of this gene is limited internationally due to its linkage with the gene of yellow flour color41.

The wide range of phenotypic differences between seedling and adult plant reactions is a clear indication of the existence of different resistance mechanisms. This diversity is observed in the wide value of ITs (0–4) and the high range of AUDPC values ​​(0-1900). ASR genes, represented by genes that are usually race-specific and expressed in early plant development, are present in genotypes with low IT scores (0–2+) at the seedling stage11,13. Although ASR is able to confer near complete resistance to specific leaf races, such resistance is often ephemeral in nature, and genes such as Lr26 and Lr1 are rapidly broken after the development of virulence races5.

Conversely, a significant percentage of the studied genotypes had moderate to low levels of AUDPC (below 500) at the adult plant stage, indicating the presence of APR genes. APR is usually partial, additive, and non-race-specific, and manifests as a slow rust phenotype with slow disease onset, smaller pustules, and reduced sporulation12,42. The genes controlling this type of resistance have minor effects, including Lr3443, Lr4644, Lr6745, and Lr6846, which enhance basal resistance responses and limit pathogen growth47. Interestingly, about 30% of genotypes that were susceptible at the seedling stage were moderately resistant (MR) or moderately susceptible (MS) in the field, indicating the existence of the APR genes that act independently of the seedling resistance mechanism. The same patterns have been reported in Iranian and CIMMYT germplasm in which APR provided durable resistance in the presence of seedlings with no resistance to the fungus7,48. The quantitative nature of the resistance is further supported by the fact that a high frequency of intermediate infection responses (MS–MR) are observed in nearly half the number of the tested genotypes, as is expected by the polygenic control of slow rusting42. Such partial resistance does not completely restricted infection, but it successfully limits the multiplication of pathogens and further development of epidemics, which is why it is an effective characteristic, which leads to long-term resistance.

The strong positive correlations (r = 0.41–0.69) of the Pt isolates in this experiment indicate similar patterns of virulence/avirulence, suggesting that such patterns are influenced by evolution limitations, genetic associations, and host selection pressures. This implies that the variation in the virulence of the pathogen is mostly polygenic and quantitative. Similar patterns have been reported for leaf rust49,50 and in other pathosystems, such as Pyrenophora teres f. teres51, Botrytis cinerea52, where virulence diversity is also an indicator of multilocus, quantitative control. These associations can also be put across by convergent evolution that is stimulated by the recurrent usage of certain Lr genes in commercial wheat varieties47,53.

Environmental stability of resistance is one of the key indicators of long-term field effectiveness in field performance12,54. The similar phenotyping patterns of the genotypes between Karaj and Ahwaz suggests that there was not so much sensitivity of resistance to environmental change. The AUDPC values in both environmental conditions is an indication of the strength of the expression of resistance that provides the fact that resistance was genetically rather than environmentally governed. This observation coincides with reports done in the past that genotypes that continuously show low AUDPC levels in various environments are likely to have stable APR genes12. Thus, low CI and AUDPC will be precise indices of parents that will be used to enhance resistance stability and longevity in Iranian wheat breeding programs.

Although the current study didn’t involve the molecular analyses, the phenotypic clustering of genotypes and the explicit expression of slow-rusting responses suggest the existence of APR gene analogs. To test these hypotheses, further molecular techniques such as gene-specific markers and GWAS will be necessary in the future to confirm the existence of these hypotheses, the genetic architecture of durable resistance, and to reveal more QTLs and thus speed up the development of cultivars that have long lasting, wide spectrum resistance.

Considering that urediniospores of rust pathogens may spread throughout long distances and infect a broad range of grass hosts55, it is impossible to remove the pathogen. Therefore, the best, cost effective and environmental friendly strategy of managing the disease are breeding and the deployment of resistant cultivars. The combination of classical breeding and modern biotechnology tools, pyramiding of genes, and genomics-assisted breeding (GAB) is likely to become the central to developing cultivars with long-term and wide-scale resistance. In addition, further cloning and practical characterization of more resistance (R) genes will further increase our knowledge of the interactions between plants and the pathogen and enable allele-specific strengthening of resistance.

Conclusion

The current experiment shows that Iranian bread wheat germplasm has a valuable genetic diversity against Pt, suggesting that it has not been used extensively as a source of sustainable resistance. The ASR and APR assessment demonstrated that most of the genotypes were susceptible at the seedling stage, but some genotypes were intermediate to complete slow-rusting resistance in the field. The stability of the expression of the resistant genotypes in different environments mean that resistance by genotypes is genetically stable and can be used in breeding programs in the future. Genotypes with slow-rusting reactions likely carry Lr34, Lr46, Lr67 or Lr68 genes. Overall, these findings suggest that Iranian wheat germplasm may represent a valuable source of adult plant resistance for future wheat improvement. However, further genetic and molecular analyses are required to confirm the underlying resistance genes and to validate their effectiveness in breeding programs aimed at durable leaf rust resistance.

Methods

Plant materials and Pt isolates

The 217 landraces (collected 1968–1979) and 103 commercial varieties (introduced 1994–1995) were used as the plant materials in this study (Table S2). Landraces were obtained from the gene bank collection of the Agricultural and Natural Resources Campus of the University of Tehran, and commercial varieties were received from the Karaj Seed and plant Improvement Institute (SPII). The landraces were single-spike lines and were cultivated for multiplication in mid-November 2018 in individual lines at the University of Tehran Research Farm located in Dolatabad, Karaj. In addition, to ensure more purity, during the growing season and before harvesting, the desired varieties were examined for morphological characteristics such as ear shape and the presence or absence of awns, and off-types plants were removed.

The screening of the population at seedling stage and adult plant stage were performed using some selected Pt isolates in Iran (Table S3). These pathotypes were chosen because they represent the predominant races in Iran during the last decade. The same isolates were not used for artificial epidemics at both locations at adult plant stage due to logistical and biosafety considerations, as well as to evaluate the genotypes under the local environmental conditions at each site. The fungal isolates gathered across the wheat fields based on the procedure described previsouly11. In brief, rust-infected leaf samples were collected from wheat fields across Iran between 2015 and 2019. After air-drying at 15–20 °C and recording the collection details (location, year, and host), samples were stored in a freezer. To revive the isolates, leaf samples were placed in a Petri dish at 100% humidity for two hours to activate the spores. After sporulation, the spores were inoculated onto susceptible Bolani seedlings. Inoculated seedlings were kept in a dark room at 17 °C for 24 h and then transferred to a greenhouse. Approximately 15–20 days after sporulation, single pustules were selected to purify the isolates. This purification cycle was repeated 4–5 times, after which the isolates were propagated for subsequent experiments. To maintain isolate purity throughout the process, the seedlings were covered with clear glass covers during incubation.

Then virulence/avirulence profiles of the 12 Pt isolates were identified by Infection Type (IT) response of Thatcher near-isogenic lines (Table 3) using the nomenclature system56. The map (Fig S1) indicates the geographical location where isolates and landraces were collected.

It is important to note that this germplasm set has been previously utilized in a genome-wide association study (GWAS)57, where resistance to leaf rust was evaluated using four Pt isolates.

However, the present study differs substantially in its objective, experimental design, and analytical framework. In contrast to the previous GWAS study, which focused on identifying genomic regions associated with resistance, the current work aims to perform a comprehensive phenotypic evaluation of ASR and APR.

Importantly, in this study, the germplasm panel was evaluated using twelve Pt isolates collected from diverse geographic regions of Iran, thereby significantly expanding pathogen.

Diversity compared to the earlier study, so related data is novel and not redundant. This broader isolate set enables a more robust assessment of resistance spectrum and stability.

Furthermore, the present study focuses on phenotypic classification, clustering analysis, and biological interpretation of resistance patterns, with particular emphasis on identifying genotypes with broad-spectrum and durable resistance for breeding applications.

Evaluation at seedling stage

The experiment was carried out in a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with two replications in the greenhouse conditions at seed plant and improvement institute (SPII), plant pathology section, Karaj, Iran in the spring of 2022. Seven seeds of each genotype were planted in pots with a combination of field soil, leaf mold, and sand in all experiments. The pots were kept in greenhouse environment with 20–22 °C, 70% relative humidity, and seepage technique irrigation. Ten days after sowing, when the first leaf was fully expanded, seedlings were ready for inoculation. Inoculum was made by mixing the urediniospores of the leaf rust isolates with the talcum powder in a 1: 4 ratio, and inoculation was performed by dusting a mixture of the spores on the seedling leaves using a fine brush. The inoculated pots were then stored in a cool and dark room at 17 °C and relative humidity of 100% to germinate and be infected by the spores. Then, the plants were placed in a greenhouse with a temperature of 20 to 22 °C, relative humidity of 70%, and a photoperiod of 16 h of light and 8 h of darkness. Approximately 10–12 days after inoculation, the ITs developed on the genotypes were scored visually using the 0–4 scale proposed previously11(Fig. 3a). All leaves of each genotype were assessed. In cases where the IT was unclear, the experiment was repeated a third time to confirm the host reaction. ITs 0–2 was considered low infection types (L), and 3–4 were classified as high infection types (H).

Evaluation at adult plant stage

Adult plant evaluations were carried out in two locations: (i) the Khuzestan Agricultural and Natural Resources Research Station in Ahwaz, located at 31°18′N and 48°41′E at height of 15 m above sea level, and (ii) the Agriculture Research Farms of the College of Agriculture and Natural Resources of the University of Tehran in Karaj, located at 35°12′N and 50°36′E at height of 1060 m above sea level. Meteorological information for both locations is provided in Supplementary Table S4.

In both locations experiment was conducted in the form of augment design, so that each genotype was planted in one row of 1-m and with 30-cm interrow. The susceptible cultivar Boolani was used as a check after every ten genotypes. Field management included a misting system to maintain humidity. The susceptible cultivar Boolani was also planted around the nursery as a source of urediniospores and as a spreader row to maintain uniformity of the disease spread. Inoculation into the field was done artificially through a mixture of the Pt isolates (listed in Table S3) at an equal proportion. The inoculations were initiated at tillering stage, and were repeated three times at ten-day intervals, by dusting the mixture of spores into the plants. Disease severity was recorded on the flag leaf after the uniform appearance of symptoms on the susceptible check Boolani. The percentage of disease severity (0–100%) was determined according to the modified method58, and ITs were recorded based on the previous method59(Fig. 3b). For disease assessment, the entire row of each genotype was considered. Multiple leaves per row were evaluated, and the predominant infection type and severity were recorded to ensure representative scoring under field conditions. Ratings were categorized as follows: 0 (Immune): no visible symptoms, R (Resistant): small chlorotic or necrotic flecks without uredinia or with a few small pustules, MR (Moderately resistant): small pustules surrounded by necrotic areas, MS (Moderately susceptible): medium-sized pustules, often with chlorosis but little necrosis, and S (Susceptible): large and abundant pustules without necrosis, sometimes surrounded by chlorosis. The fourth and last assessment was performed on all genotypes when disease severity on the susceptible check Boolani reached 100%, and value was recorded as final disease severity (FDS).
