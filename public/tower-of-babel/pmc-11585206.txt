Single-cell RNA sequencing reveals immune microenvironment niche transitions during the invasive and metastatic processes of ground-glass nodules and part-solid nodules in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Yi-Feng Ren, Qiong Ma, Xiao Zeng, Chun-Xia Huang, Jia-Li Ren, Fang Li, Jia-Jing Tong, Jia-Wei He, Yang Zhong, Shi-Yan Tan, Hua Jiang, Long-Fei Zhang, Heng-Zhou Lai, Ping Xiao, Xiang Zhuang, Peng Wu, Li-Ting You, Wei Shi, Xi Fu, Chuan Zheng, Feng-Ming You
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRadiographically, ground-glass nodules (GGN) and part-solid nodules (PSN) in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) have significant heterogeneity in their clinical manifestations, biological characteristics, and prognosis. This study aimed to explore the heterogeneity of LUAD in different radiological phenotypes and associated factors influencing tumor evolution.MethodsWe performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on tumor tissues from eight and seven cases of GGN– and PSN–LUAD, respectively, at different disease stages, including minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA), invasive adenocarcinoma (IAC), and metastatic lung cancer (MLC). Additionally, we analyzed adjacent normal tissues from four cases. Immunohistochemistry, multiplex immunofluorescence, and external scRNA-seq data were employed to confirm the expression of signature genes as well as the distribution patterns of CXCL9 + TAMs and TREM2 + TAMs. A LUAD mouse model was generated using gene editing, organoid culture, and orthotopic transplantation techniques, and comprehensive analyses such as histopathology, RNA sequencing, and Western blotting were performed to validate key pathways.ResultsDiverse cellular compositions were observed in the tumor microenvironment (TME) during GGN– and PSN–LUAD invasion and metastasis. Notably, CXCL9 + and TREM2 + tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) exhibited the most significant enrichment changes. It was found that GGN–LUAD exhibited a stronger immune response than PSN–LUAD, with increased interaction between CXCL9 + TAMs and CD8 + tissue-resident memory T cells during invasion stage (MIA–IAC). Conversely, greater interactions between TREM2 + TAMs and tumor cells were observed in PSN–LUAD during the MLC stage. Additionally, TREM2 + TAMs were found to differentiate into TREM2 + /SPP1 + and TREM2 + /SPP1– TAMs at different stages, which promotes tumor progression. This study also emphasizes that during the transdifferentiation process of GGN– and PSN–LUAD, IFN-γ activates the STAT1 signaling pathway to regulate the activation of CXCL9 + TAMs, and further recruiting CD8 + Trm cells and activating T cells through MHC class I antigen presentation. The role of the IFN-γ/STAT1 pathway in the occurrence and development of LUAD was further validated by animal experiments.ConclusionsOur findings offer a potential therapeutic strategy to maintain a dynamic balance within the TME and improve the immunotherapy efficacy by modulating the relative proportions and functional states of CXCL9 + TAMs and TREM2 + TAMs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12943-024-02177-7.

Background

With the widespread use of high-resolution low-dose computed tomography, an increasing number of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) cases are being detected in the form of pulmonary nodules, among which ground-glass nodules (GGN) and part-solid nodules (PSN) are the two most frequently detected types, accounting for 31.7–69.7% of all pulmonary nodules [1, 2]. Notably, early-stage LUAD with GGN features exhibit a nearly 100% 5-year disease-free survival rate post-surgery. In contrast, PSN with more solid components show a higher risk of metastasis and disease progression [3, 4]. This suggests a potential strong correlation between different radiological features and the malignant phenotype and disease progression of LUAD.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) of LUAD is composed of cancer cells, infiltrating immune cells, stromal cells, and other cellular types, as well as non-cellular tissue components, which interact with each other and collectively determine the initiation and progression of the tumor [5, 6]. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technologies enable the evaluation of thousands of cells within a single sample, thereby revealing the heterogeneity of tumor cells and the subtle nuances of the complex TME [7]. The development of LUAD is often considered to progress from adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS), through the minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA) stage, to invasive adenocarcinoma (IAC), and ultimately to distant metastatic lung cancer (MLC) [8, 9]. Currently, studies have utilized scRNA-seq technologies to reveal the evolutionary trajectories from pre-invasive to invasive LUAD. For example, Zhu et al. studied the cellular ecologies and spatial niches involved in the dynamic and sequential processes of LUAD from AIS to MIA and subsequent IAC [10]. Wu et al. mapped cell type-specific scRNA-seq profiles of cancer cells and their tumor microenvironment in advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [11]. Furthermore, Li et al. revealed the multi-cellular ecosystems within different radiological components of PSN. However, these studies are not comprehensive and do not fully capture the progression of LUAD from its early stages, nor do they identify the characteristics of the same type of pulmonary nodule across different pathological periods [12]. To our knowledge, there is currently no comprehensive study evaluating the effects of GGN and PSN features on the molecular and immunological characteristics of MIA, IAC, and MLC-LUAD.

To delineate the evolutionary trajectories of GGN- and PSN-LUAD, we generated single-cell transcriptomic atlases of tumor tissues and normal lung tissues across various stages of disease progression (MIA/IAC/MLC) for both GGN and PSN-LUAD. These atlases capture the heterogeneity of the TME cell populations during the invasion and metastasis of LUAD with distinct radiological characteristics. Leveraging publicly available patient sequencing data and multiplex immunofluorescence technologies, we further determined the distribution patterns of TME cell. Within this extensive heterogeneity, we identified key tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) subsets-TREM2 + TAMs and CXCL9 + TAMs-that drive either tumor-suppressing or promoting phenotypes, and elucidated the underlying mechanisms regulating their differentiation using organoids and gene-editing techniques. In summary, our analysis provides an in-depth characterization of the molecular features of the evolutionary trajectories of GGN- and PSN-LUAD, with significant clinical implications.

Methods

Patients and specimens

This study included nineteen LUAD patients with SSN who underwent curative resection surgery at the Department of Thoracic Surgery, Hospital of Chengdu University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, and Sichuan Cancer Hospital between June 2022 and May 2023, as confirmed by pathology. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) of Hospital of Chengdu University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (IRB No. 2022KL-051) and Sichuan Cancer Hospital (IRB No. SCCHEC-02–2022-109). Patient demographics and clinicopathological characteristics are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Patients were categorized into GGN– or PSN–LUAD groups with various subtypes: three, three, and two with GGN–MIA, GGN–IAC, GGN–MLC; and three, three, and one with PSN–MIA, PSN–IAC, PSN–MLA. Normal non-neoplastic tissues from four patients (two each) were collected over 2 cm from tumor edges, evaluated cryopathologically, and classified per the 2015 WHO system.

Mice

Mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free animal facility at Hospital of Chengdu University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, where they were provided with autoclaved food, bedding, and water. All mouse experiments were performed in accordance with guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees of Hospital of Chengdu University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, with protocols approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of TCM Regulating Metabolic Diseases Key Laboratory of Sichuan Province, Hospital of Chengdu University of Traditional Chinese Medicine. C57BL/6 and CAG-Cas9-EGFP mice (male, 6–8 weeks old, approximately 20 g in weight) were used. Mice were purchased from GemPharmatech (RRID: IMSR_JAX:026179). Tumor volumes did not exceed the maximal permitted volume of 1,000 mm3.

Preparation of single-cell suspensions

After surgery, tissue samples were split: one part was fixed for immunofluorescence, and the other was placed in ice-cold H1640 (Gibco) for single-cell suspensions. Tissues were minced, digested with the GentleMACS Dissociator, then washed, filtered, and centrifuged before being resuspended in a solution with 0.2% foetal bovine serum. Cells were manually counted, and processed using the Chromium Controller (10 × Genomics). Single cells were processed using the Chromium Controller according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Library preparation and scRNA-seq

Prepared cell suspensions and reaction mixtures (Master Mix + Sample), 10 × barcode Gel Beads (VDJ 5' Gel Beads v1.1), and partitioning oil were added to different wells of the Chromium Next GEM Chip. Through the microfluidic "single cross" system, GEMs were formed. Then, Gel beads were dissolved and cells were lysed within each GEM. The released oligonucleotides mixed with the lysed cells and Master Mix (containing RT reagents and RT primers) to undergo reverse transcription, resulting in full-length complementary DNA (cDNA) sequences with 10 × barcodes. After reverse transcription, the Recovery Agent was used to disrupt the oil-in-water structure, and recover and purify the cDNA. Subsequently, the cDNA was amplified under the action of DNA polymerase. The amplified cDNA is then fragmented and selected for appropriate fragments. After end repair and A-tailing, Read2 sequencing primers were ligated, and Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) was performed to construct a cDNA library containing P5 and P7 adapters.

scRNA-seq data pre-processing

CellRanger software (https://support.10xgenomics.com/single-cell-vdj) was utilized to align and annotate reads using human genome assembly GRCh38p13, and gene annotation GENCODE34 was used to generate count matrices. Once aligned, the barcodes associated with read-cell identifiers and unique molecular identifiers (UMI) were processed in R (version 3.6.0) using the Seurat R package (version 4.0.0) [13]. The cells with mitochondrial UMI counts > 25%, or < 500 genes were identified as low-quality cells and removed. DoubletFinder was used to remove doublets. After removing low-quality cells, the gene expression matrix was normalized using the normalization function, and the top 2000 features with high cell-to-cell variability were calculated using the Seurat normalization function.

Dimension reduction and identification of major cell clusters

Dimensionality reduction was performed by principal component analysis. Subsequently, we applied the Harmony algorithm, a batch effect correction method, to refine the integration. Utilizing the Harmony results as a foundation, clustering analysis was conducted using standard Seurat package procedures. We employed the Clustree package to construct a dendrogram by specifying distinct levels of clustering, facilitating a clear visualization of the interrelationships among various clustering tiers. Ultimately, for different cellular phenotypes, we selected appropriate resolution parameters to enable annotations that are biologically significant: T cells (0.8), B cells (0.05), endothelial cells (0.05), epithelial cells (0.8), and myeloid cells (0.5). After non-linear dimensional reduction and projection of all cells into a two-dimensional space through uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP), cells with common features were clustered together in the UMAP map. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of each cluster were identified by the “FindAllMarkers” function in Seurat, and the clusters were annotated according to the expression of classic markers (parameters:only.pos = FALSE, min.pct = 0.1, thresh.use = 0.1). Clusters were classified and annotated based on the expression of canonical markers in the particular cell types. Cell-type markers were adapted from Hu et al. [14]. Doublets and low-quality cell clusters were excluded from further analysis.

Differentially expressed pathway analysis between different clinical groups

Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was used to assess differential pathways, and the activity scores of special cells were calculated by GSVA (v1.40.1) (false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05) [15]. Significantly enriched pathways were defined at an adjusted p (p_adj) value < 0.05. The Gene Ontology (GO) gene sets were subjected to an enrichment analysis using the R package clusterProfiler version 3.16.1. To account for multiple comparisons, the p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Ontologies were considered significant if the q-value was < 0.05.

Identification of cancer cells

To identify cancer cells, we used inferCNV (version 1.2.1) to search copy number variations (CNVs) in each subcluster of epithelial cell clusters from the scRNA-seq data. The raw count data was extracted from the Seurat object according to the recommendations in the "Use 10 × Data" section. The epithelial cells in the normal control (NC) sample were used as reference cells, and density curves were plotted for the estimated CNV values, with 928.68 as the baseline value for distinguishing tumors from normal cells. We created a gene sorting file from the assembly of human CRCh38, containing the chromosome start and end positions of each gene. For inferCNV analysis, we used 0.1 as the cutoff value for the minimum average read count of each gene in the reference cell and denoised the output to predict CNV levels. In addition, CopyKAT was used to calculate the genomic copy number distribution of individual cells by combining Bayesian methods with hierarchical clustering and to define subclonal structures. CopyKAT can automatically identify diploid cells as normal. For each non-diploid cell, Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) is used to identify the breakpoints of CNVs and to determine segments. Normal and tumor cells can be distinguished based on their different gene expression profiles. Ultimately, the results from inferCNV and CopyKAT were combined to define the tumor cell populations within the epithelial cells.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence staining

All dissected tissues were fixed in 4% PFA and embedded in paraffin. The tissues were cut into 4-µm sections and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Ki67(Abcam, ab15580) markers were used for immunohistochemical analysis. Immunofluorescent staining was performed using the antibodies CD68 (ImmunoWay, YM3050), TREM2 (Proteintech, 22,952–1-AP), and CXCL9 (ImmunoWay, YT6008). Images were captured using a Nikon ECLIPSE C1 camera.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis

DEGs were identified by Seurat. The R package Monocle (version 2.4) was used with default settings to plot the pseudotime trajectory. Pseudotime ordering was performed using the function “reduce dimension” with max components set at 2 and the reduction method set as DDRTree. Next, the significantly affected genes were obtained from the top 50 markers among the clusters using the function differentialGeneTest (fullModelFormulaStr = ~ Pseudotime) and were plotted with the function plot pseudotime heatmap.

Transcription factor analysis

Based on the scRNA-seq results, pySCENIC analysis was run using the motifs database for RcisTarget and GRNboost (pySCENIC version 0.1.1.2) with the default parameters 50. We regarded each regulatory network as a regulon. Through the analysis of the regulon activity in each cell type, we determined the differences in the regulatory activity of transcription factors among different cell types.

Cell–cell interaction score calculation using CellphoneDB

We investigated cell–cell interactions across all cell types with CellPhoneDB (version 3.1.0) (https://www.cellphonedb.org/). To identify the most relevant interactions between cell types, CellPhoneDB identified cell type-specific interactions between ligands and receptors. Only receptors and ligands expressed in > 10% of the cells in the specific cluster were considered significant. CellPhoneDB then performed pairwise comparisons between all cell types. The ligand–receptor pairs with P value < 0.05 were retained. To assess the cell–cell interactions, we calculated the total interaction score of each cell pair: summation of the scores of all ligand–receptor pairs within a cell pair. The total score reflects the abundance and expression of ligand–receptor pairs within each cell pair, regardless of whether the interaction involves positive or negative regulatory ligands/receptors for antitumor immunity.

CellChat analysis

Cell–cell communication was analyzed using the CellChat pipeline with version 1.1.3. A new CellChat object was generated from the consolidated Seurat object. The CellChatDB's dataset of paracrine/autocrine signaling interactions was utilized as the reference database. Subsequently, the communication probabilities were calculated with a 20% truncated mean (using the computeCommunProb function with type set to "truncatedMean" and trim set to 0.2). Cell–cell communication was then inferred, and the network was aggregated with default parameters. The number of interactions was visualized to depict the aggregated cell–cell communication network and the signals sent from each cell cluster.

Cell culture

HEK 293 T cells (CRL-1573) from ATCC were obtained and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% (vol/vol) fetal bovine serum and penicillin (100 U ml − 1)/streptomycin (0.1 mg ml − 1), and were incubated at 37 ℃, 5% CO2 cell incubator.

Lung organoid culture

Mouse lungs were removed, cut into 3mm3 pieces and incubated with digestion buffer containing 1.0 mg ml−1 collagenase I (Gibco, 17,100–017) and 0.5 mg ml−1 collagenase IV (Gibco, 17,104–019) in DMEM/F12 (Gibco, C11330500BT) at 37 °C for 45 min. After dissociation, the samples were filtered through 100-μm filters to obtain a cell suspension, then centrifuged and washed once with DMEM/F12. After solidification, 150 μl of organoid expansion medium (ExM) was added to each well. The ExM and growth factors were generously provided by Prof. Chen [16]. Organoids were passaged via mechanical dissociation in TrypLE (Gibco, 12,605–028) every 5–7 days.

Genomic DNA extraction, PCR amplification

Five hundred microliters DNA lysis buffer (homemade, 10 mM Tris, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM EDTA, 0.5% SDS 0.4 mg/ml proteinase K) were used to lyse the cells at 55 °C for at least 2 h. The supernatant was combined with 500 μl isopropanol, mixed, and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was removed, and the sample was washed once with 1 mL of 75% ethanol. The sample was volatilized at 55 °C for approximately 5 min, and the DNA pellet was subsequently dissolved in double-distilled water. One hundred nanograms DNA was used for each genotyping PCR reaction. PCR reactions were performed using a ProFlexTM PCR system (Applied Biosystems).

Plasmid constructs and virus production

sgRNAs (Supplementary Fig. S10A-B) were designed using the CRISPR Design Tool (http://crispr.mit.edu/). sgRNA oligos were cloned into the lentiviral vector V2TC, which bicistronically expresses both sgRNAs and mCherry. To validate the targeted mutations, genomic DNA was isolated from the infected organoids, and the T7E1 (Vazyme, EN303-01) assay was performed.

Organoid genome editing and efficiency testing

After dissociation of organoids into single cells using TrypLE, the cells were resuspended in viral supernatant supplemented with 1: 1000 (v/v) polybrene in a 24-well plate, centrifuged at 800 g for 1 h, and then incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The solution was collected and centrifuged at 400 g for 5 min. The cells were resuspended in ice-cold Matrigel and seeded into 48-well plates for continued culturing. Genomic DNA was extracted from infected organoids and the T7E1 (Vazyme, EN303-01) assay was performed to confirm the targeted mutations.

Organoid growth assay

In the organoid growth assay, 2,000 single cells were cultured in a 96-well plate embedded in 10 μl of Matrigel (with three independent biological replicates in each group). And the number of organoids was counted after 5 days of culture.

Organoid orthotopic transplantation

Organoid fragments were prepared by digestion in TrypLE at 37 °C, followed by mechanical dissociation, centrifugation, and resuspension in 30% Matrigel. Three hundred thousand cells in a 0.075-ml volume were injected into the left lobe of the lung. Body weight was measured every week. At 3, 7, and 11 weeks, three mice were randomly selected and euthanized for subsequent measurement of tumor volume and weight.

Bioluminescence imaging

After orthotopic transplantation of organoids (1.5 × 106 cells in 150 μl 100% Matrigel per injection), mice were periodically imaged to detect the luciferase fluorescence signal intensity using the IVIS Spectrum in vivo imaging system (PerkinElmer). The mice were anesthetized with isoflurane after 250 μl of 15 mg/ml D-luciferin, sodium salt (Bio Vision, #7903-1G) in PBS intraperitoneally injected. Five minutes later, the mice were placed in the instrument, and imaging was initiated.

Micro-CT

Mice were scanned for 340 s under isoflurane anesthesia using a small animal Quantum FX Micro-CT (PerkinElmer) at a resolution of 45 mm and 90 kV with a 160-mA current. Images were acquired and analyzed using PerkinElmer Quantum FX software.

RNA-seq analyses

For the RNA-seq analysis, three technical duplicates were included for each sample. RNA-seq libraries were constructed using an Illumina stranded mRNA sample preparation kit (NEB, E7770) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencing machine with 150-base pair (bp) paired-end reads.

Western blotting

Western blot analyses were performed using standard methods. The following antibodies were used: CD44 (Abcam, ab189524, 1:2,000), CXCL9 (Cloud-clone-crop, PAB928Hu01, 1:2,000), SPP1 (Proteintech, 22,952–1-AP, 1:1,000), p-STAT1 (Santa cruz, sc-8394, 1:2,000), STAT1 (Huabio, ET1612-22, 1:1,000), IFN-γ (CLOUD-CLONE CORP, PAB928Hu01, 1:2,000), CD68 (Abcam, ab283316, 1:1,000) (See Supplementary Representative WB images for the overlay of colorimetric and chemiluminescent images of the original data).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (http://www.rproject.org), SPSS version 25.0 and Python (https://www.python.org/). The detailed statistical methods used for each analysis are described within the figure legends. Differences were investigated using two-sided paired or unpaired Student's t-tests.

These findings suggest that variations in cellular composition at different tumor tissue stages might correlate with mechanisms of disease progression.

Heterogeneity of epithelial cells during the invasion and metastasis of PSN- and GGN-LUAD

By integrating information from copy number variations (inferCNV) (Fig. 2A), CopyKat (Fig. 2B), and lineage-specific gene expression (Supplementary Fig. S4A), we successfully distinguished malignant epithelial cells from non-malignant normal epithelial cells. The heterogeneity of epithelial cells was further investigated. RNA velocity trajectories were constructed by utilizing the identified tumor cells and normal epithelial cell subtypes. The transcriptional states revealed that tumor cells were located between AT1 and AT2 cells (Supplementary Fig. S4B). Subsequently, the characteristics of malignant tumor cells were evaluated using methods such as Cytotrace, CellCycleScoring, and AddModuleScore. The Cytotrace scoring results indicated that the tumor cell population exhibited a stronger lineage differentiation capability (Fig. 2D), and the AddModuleScore results using the Hallmark gene set showed that tumor cells exhibit higher epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) characteristics (Fig. 2E). Moreover, it was observed that the Cytotrace scores and EMT scores at different stages in the PSN-LUAD group were generally higher than those in the GGN-LUAD group, revealing different levels of malignancy between the two groups (Fig. 2F).

Although the Cell-Cycle Scoring results indicated a minimal impact of the cell cycle on the current dataset, a higher proportion of cells in the G2M and S phases was exhibited by the tumor cell population (Supplementary Fig. S4C). It has been shown that Ki-67 is expressed throughout the cell cycle except for the G0 phase, and elevated Ki-67 expression is a marker of higher malignancy [18]. Therefore, Ki-67 was used as a staining marker for the proliferative status of tumor cells. The staining results showed a significant increase in Ki-67 expression as the disease progressed from the MIA to the IAC stage, with a further marked increase in Ki-67 staining intensity in the MLC stage (Fig. 2G). Additionally, a significant correlation with prognosis in survival analysis was shown by the gene encoding Ki-67, MKI67 (Fig. 2H). The transcriptional program heterogeneity of epithelial cells at different progression stages in GGN- and PSN-LUAD was highlighted by these data, which may be clinically relevant to disease progression.

Endothelial cells orchestrate angiogenesis during the invasion and metastasis of PSN- and GGN-LUAD

Endothelial cells are vital cell types constituting the blood/lymphatic vessels, which can influence the nutrient delivery and oxygen supply in the tumor microenvironment [19]. Previous studies have indicated that a reduction in endothelial cells leads to the formation of a hypoxic tumor environment, and hypoxia further promotes tumor cell metastasis by inducing metabolic reprogramming [20]. Our findings align with this hypothesis. Compared to normal tissues, both GGN– and PSN–LUAD exhibit a significant decrease in the proportion of endothelial cells in the TME, with PSN–LUAD showing a lower endothelial cell ratio. Crucially, the MLC stage exhibits a more noticeable decrease in endothelial cell counts than the MIA and IAC stages (Supplementary Fig. 3A).

To further observe the trends in different subtypes of endothelial cells, we subclustered endothelial cells and annotated them into six cell types: Arterial, Capillary, Capillary_Aerocyte, Tip, Vein and Lymphatic cells (Fig. 2I, Supplementary Fig. S4D). By analyzing the proportions of vascular endothelial cell subtypes in the TME, it was found that the proportion of Tip cells in all tumor samples was higher than in normal samples, and this proportion increased gradually with disease progression, while the proportions of other major pulmonary endothelial cell types did not change significantly (Supplementary Fig. S4E). Velocity analysis showed that Tip cells are positioned along the developmental and differentiation trajectory between Vein endothelial cells and Capillary endothelial cells (Fig. 2J), and Cytotrace results indicated that a stronger differentiation capacity is possessed by Tip cells (Fig. 2K). It has been reported that hypoxia levels and vascular growth factor (VEGF) signals in the microenvironment can be sensed by Tip cells, which are the primary cell type responsible for inducing the formation of the tumor capillary network. Tumors rely on angiogenesis to meet their metabolic demands at different growth stages. In this study, a higher proportion of Tip cells was observed in PSN-LUAD samples compared to GGN-LUAD samples (Supplementary Fig. S4E), which may be one of the potential mechanisms underlying the faster invasion and metastasis observed clinically in PSN-LUAD.

We further analyzed the proportion of TREM2 + , RTM, CXCL9 + TAMs, IL-1β + , and MMP19 + TAMs subsets during GGN– and PSN–LUAD invasion to explore their distribution characteristics (Fig. 3E). TREM2 + TAMs are crucial for mediating macrophage polarization and promoting escape from lung cancer [22]. A higher percentage of TREM2 + TAMs was observed in GGN– and PSN–LUAD than in normal samples at different disease stages, with a higher percentage in PSN–LUAD than in GGN–LUAD. Moreover, higher RTM + TAMs enrichment was found in GGN–LUAD compared to PSN–LUAD. CXCL9-secreting TAMs exhibit anti-tumor activity [23]. Compared to normal tissues, an increased proportion of CXCL9 + TAMs was found in GGN– and PSN–LUAD tissues. However, based on the distribution of different macrophage subtypes, CXCL9 + TAMs were present in the lowest proportion and significantly decreased during the MLC period. These results suggest a potential antagonistic relationship between TREM2 + and CXCL9 + TAMs. To further validate this hypothesis, multiplex immunofluorescence staining was used to analyze tumor sections from different stages of GGN and PSN-LUAD. The distribution patterns of TREM2 + and CXCL9 + TAMs in the tumor microenvironment confirmed the potential antagonistic relationship between these two TAM subtypes (Fig. 3D).

We employed Monocle2 to analyze the differentiation trajectories of classical monocytes, CXCL9 + TAMs, and TREM2 + TAMs during the process of invasion and metastasis in LUAD. At different stages of GGN– and PSN–LUAD, the trajectory trees of CXCL9 + TAMs showed consistent differentiation (Fig. 3F, Supplementary Fig. S5C), indicating that CXCL9 + TAMs exhibited the same function at different stages of LUAD progression. Pseudotime analysis results showed that classical monocytes exhibited multiple tree nodes and different differentiation trajectories, among which monocytes in the state 5 differentiated into CXCL9 + TAMs (Supplementary Fig. S5D). CXCLs were significantly upregulated during the differentiation of monocytes into CXCL9 + TAMs, with CXCL9 showing the most significant upregulation (Fig. 3G).

Additionally, genes related to CXCL9 + TAMs differentiation were highly associated with immune regulation. In the CXCL9 + TAMs developmental trajectory, genes related to antigen presentation (HLA-DPA1, HLA-DRA, and HLA-DRB1) gradually increased in expression, whereas pro-inflammatory genes (IL-1B, S100A12, S100A8, and S100A9) decreased (Supplementary Fig. S5E). The enrichment analysis results of the genes related to CXCL9 + TAM differentiation trend showed that the top 50 genes were primarily enriched in T cell differentiation, antigen presentation, and inflammatory response (Supplementary Fig. S5F-G).

To investigate the potential impact of macrophages expressing TREM2 during cancer progression, we conducted pseudotime analysis on TREM2 + TAMs (Fig. 3H, Supplementary Fig. S6A). The findings reveal cellular differentiation heterogeneity in TREM2 + TAMs, highlighting the elevated expression of the SPP1 gene in TREM2 + TAMs at states 4 and 7 (Fig. 3I, Supplementary Fig. S6B).

To discern the differentiation inclination of TREM2 + TAMs, we quantified the cellular-level variations of TREM2 + /SPP1 + TAMs and TREM2 + /SPP1-TAMs across distinct disease stages of LUAD (Fig. 3J). The findings elucidate that during the MIA–IAC stages of LUAD, TREM2 + TAMs exhibited a predilection toward manifesting SPP1-characteristics, whereas during the LUAD–MLC stage, an escalation in the proportion of SPP1 + TAMs occurred. The proportion of SPP1 + TAMs in PSN–MLC was significantly higher than in GGN-MLC. Notably, we observed that the proportions of CXCL9 + TAMs and TREM2 + SPP1 + TAMs exhibited opposite trends across different stages of LUAD (Supplementary Fig. S6C).

Subsequent differential gene expression analysis of TREM2 + /SPP1 + TAMs and TREM2 + /SPP1- TAMs was conducted to explore the underlying factors contributing to the differentiation heterogeneity of TREM2 + TAMs (Supplementary Fig. S6D). The results reveal elevated expression of CCL8 and IL1B genes in TREM2 + /SPP1– TAMs. This suggests a potential regulation of TREM2 + /SPP1–TAMs by the hypoxic microenvironment [24]. Additionally, we observed significant upregulation in the VEGFA gene in TREM2 + /SPP1– TAMs. Enrichment analysis indicated a strong correlation in gene expression with pathways related to angiogenesis (Supplementary Fig. S6E). In the advanced stages of LUAD infiltration, TREM2 + TAMs predominantly exhibited SPP1 + characteristics. Concurrently, differential gene analysis revealed the upregulation of genes such as SPP1, CSTB, GPNMB, CD9, and MPP9 in TREM2 + /SPP1 + TAMs (Fig. 3K).

Given the difficulty in detecting the differentiation trends of macrophages in tissues, the differentiation states of macrophages were analyzed using marker genes specific to different states. The presence of TREM2 + /SPP1 + TAMs and TREM2 + /SPP1– TAMs in tumor tissues was revealed by multiplex immunofluorescence results. The expression patterns of these marker genes support the speculation about the differentiation pathways of TREM2 + TAMs and further validate the accuracy of pseudotime trajectory analysis (Fig. 3L). In summary, these results indicate that alterations in CXCL9 + TAMs and TREM2 + TAMs have a significant impact on the evolution and progression of LUAD and may explain the differing developmental trends between PSN-LUAD and GGN-LUAD.

To reveal the TME infiltration of T cells during the invasive and metastatic processes of GGN–LUAD and PSN–LUAD, we compared the changes in T cell proportions across NC, GGN–MIA, GGN–IAC, GGN–MLC, PSN–MIA, PSN–IAC, and PSN–MLC. Compared to NC, there was a significant increase in T cell proportions during the GGN–MIA, GGN–IAC, and PSN–MIA stages. Notably, a significant decrease in T cell infiltration was observed in the PSN–IAC group compared to the PSN–MIA group, with T cell proportions lower than in the NC group (Supplementary Fig. S3A, Supplementary Fig. S7A). Beyond T cells, we have also focused on another lymphocyte lineage cell type–B cells. Cell–cell interactions indicate different patterns of tumor invasion and metastasis between GGN– and PSN–LUAD

To delve deeper into the heterogeneity of the immune microenvironment in GGN– and PSN–LUAD at different disease stages, we utilized CellPhoneDB to analyze and compare the interaction strengths among different immune cell types (measured by the sum of mean values of intercellular signaling communication), revealing differences in immune cell activity across the MIA–IAC–MLC stages in both GGN and PSN–LUAD. Specifically, during the MIA stage of GGN–LUAD, active immune cells were primarily B cell and T cell subpopulations. As tumor infiltration progressed, the signaling communication intensity between these subpopulations and other myeloid cells gradually increased. However, during the MLC stage, the signaling communication between all immune cell types and B cell and T cell subpopulations significantly weakened, with the active cell types in the tumor immune microenvironment transitioning to macrophage subpopulations. In the corresponding stages of PSN–LUAD, we similarly observed a transition from an immune microenvironment dominated by T and B cell subpopulations to one dominated by macrophages, indicating a close association between this transition and tumor disease progression. Compared to GGN–LUAD, this transition occurred earlier in PSN–LUAD, possibly reflecting the faster progression of PSN–LUAD (Supplementary Table S3, Supplementary Fig. S9A-F).

We further explored the causes of this transition. Utilizing CellChat, we conducted an in-depth analysis of the key roles of T-cell, B-cell, and macrophage subpopulations in the progression and metastasis of PSN-LUAD and GGN–LUAD (Fig. 4G-L). We observed a significant enhancement of intercellular interactions related to the CXCL signaling pathway during the GGN–MIA and GGN–IAC stages (Fig. 4G and H). This phenomenon was also validated during the MIA–IAC stage of PSN–LUAD (Fig. 4J and K). Additionally, we observed a decrease in the activity of the CXCL pathway in both the GGN-MLC and PSN-MLC groups at the MLC stage. However, there was an enhancement of SPP1 pathway activity specifically during the PSN-MLC stage (Fig. 4I and L). Our research indicated that the SPP1 signal received by tumor cells mainly originates from cells other than CXCL9 + TAMs, such as TREM2 + TAMs and RTM + TAMs, with TREM2 + TAMs being the highest proportion of monocyte-derived macrophages during the PSN–MLC stage. This is consistent with the latest findings in Science, mutual exclusion in the expression of CXCL9 and SPP1 in TAMs [26].

Interferon (IFN)-γ is an important macrophage activator that induces the secretion of chemokines, attracting and activating other immune cells to the tumor site, thereby enhancing tumor immune infiltration and inflammatory responses [27]. Discussion

Distinct imaging features of LUAD manifest as unique growth patterns that are intricately linked to patient prognosis. Although ongoing studies have examined changes in the TME of various radiological components of LUAD [12, 13], thorough analyses of the heterogeneity of the TME and disease prognosis regulations for GGN– and PSN–LUAD progression are significantly lacking. The outcomes of this study revealed distinctive TME patterns between GGN– and PSN–LUAD, identifying CXCL9 + TAMs and TREM2 + TAMs as crucial TME subsets influencing the ecological differences in invasion and metastasis between the two radiographic phenotypes. We further delineated a highly coordinated network of pro- or anti-tumor variables comprising CXCL9 + TAMs and TREM2 + TAMs, exploring their potential sources, interactions with T and tumor cells, and expression patterns of relevant regulatory genes in different radiographic subtypes of LUAD (Fig. 8). We posit that these findings enhance our understanding of their heterogeneity during GGN– and PSN–LUAD occurrence and progression, offering potential therapeutic strategies worthy of further validation.Fig. 8Mechanism schema diagram. The TME, primarily comprising neoplastic cells and myriad stromal cells, undergoes specific alterations in cell types that are intricately linked to tumor progression [28]. We identified 12 distinct cell types, representing different stages of GGN– and PSN–LUAD (MIA, IAC, and MLC). Our study revealed the heterogeneity in lineage differentiation features, proliferative characteristics, and EMT among cancer cells from different stages of GGN and PSN-LUAD. Related studies have shown that these malignant features provide protection and support for tumor initiation and are involved in tumor progression [29]. Our research indicates that, during the progression of GGN and PSN-LUAD, the reduction of endothelial cells induces the formation of a hypoxic TME. The hypoxic environment further promotes the generation of Tip cells, influencing angiogenesis in the new stages. Immune cells are crucial for determining the biological behavior of cancer cells. We identified diverse immune cell subpopulations based on their phenotypes and analyzed variations in their fractions within the TME. Notably, substantial differences in the allocation of immune cell subpopulations between GGN– and PSN–LUAD during invasive metastasis were discovered. This supports the notion that considerable heterogeneity exists in LUAD within the TME. In both GGN– and PSN–LUAD, a consistent phenomenon of gradual depletion of CD8 + T and NK cells was observed. These cells can directly eliminate tumor cells during LUAD invasion and metastasis, but their gradual depletion may lead to tumor immune evasion [30, 31]. This finding aligns with a previous study that demonstrated a reduction in T-cell activity among patients with LUAD. Macrophages are the dominant myeloid cell population in LUAD, with heterogeneous subpopulations prevalent during GGN– and PSN–LUAD invasion and metastasis. Lower levels of macrophage subpopulation infiltration were exhibited in PSN-LUAD compared to GGN-LUAD. This is consistent with previous research findings indicating that macrophage depletion may promote tumor development [32].

The TME is an intricately interdependent ecosystem in which dynamic interactions among immune cells regulate cancer onset and progression [33]. Robust immune reactions within tumor tissues indicate the concerted effort of the human body to resist cancer cell infiltration and impede tumor growth. In our study, the interaction intensity among immune cells at different GGN–LUAD stages significantly surpassed that of PSN–LUAD, suggesting a heightened susceptibility of PSN–LUAD to immune escape compared with GGN–LUAD. Notably, CXCL9 + TAMs and TREM2 + TAMs, compared with other macrophage subsets, exhibited substantial differences in their interactions with T and tumor cells during LUAD invasion and metastasis, with distinct imaging features. Although the precise mechanisms through which CXCL9 + TAMs modulate LUAD pathogenesis remain unclear, emerging data suggest that TAMs expressing CXCL9 are novel contributors to anticancer immunity [26]. CXCL9 + TAMs secrete the chemokine CXCL9, which binds to the chemokine receptor CXCR3 on the surface of CD8 + Trm cells, guiding and attracting CD8 + Trm cells to tumor sites to exert anti-tumor effects [34]. Our research indicates that GGN–LUAD exhibited a more robust signaling interaction between CXCL9 + TAMs and CD8 + Trm than PSN–LUAD. However, in the MLC stage of both LUAD types, the signaling interaction between CXCL9 + TAMs and CD8 + Trm cells was significantly weakened, suggesting that it is a crucial factor in LUAD metastasis. Heightened TREM2 + TAMs expression has been documented in various malignancies, including NSCLC and hepatocellular carcinoma [35, 36]. TREM2-deficient mice reportedly exhibit weakened tumor growth in several transplant models, accompanied by an augmented response against PD1. TREM2 + TAMs facilitate the growth and dissemination of cancer cells upon binding to CD44 receptors on the surface of tumor cells, contributing to the formation of an immunosuppressive TME [37]. Our investigation revealed that during the PSN–MLC stage, TREM2 + TAMs exhibited robust binding to CD44 receptors on the surface of tumor cells, displaying intensified interactions. This phenomenon may significantly contribute to the accelerated metastasis observed in PSN–LUAD compared with that in GGN–LUAD. Notably, high infiltration of CXCL9 + TAMs was accompanied by TREM2 + TAMs infiltration, suggesting a possible antagonistic effect between CXCL9 + TAMs and TREM2 + TAMs.

We further explored the potential mechanisms underlying the disparities in interaction signals between CXCL9 + TAMs and CD8 + Trm cells in GGN– and PSN–LUAD. Notably, across different GGN– and PSN–LUAD stages, the IFN-γ signaling pathway exhibited heightened activity in the gene enrichment analysis of CXCL9 + TAMs. IFN-γ is involved in activating cellular immunity and subsequently stimulating anti-tumor immune responses. According to Ding, IFN-γ can trigger CXCL9 expression, which attracts cytotoxic T lymphocytes to the cancerous tissue [38]. STAT1 is a pivotal mediator of IFN-γ-induced gene activation [39]. We observed a gradual decrease in IFN-γ levels secreted by immune cells as GGN– and PSN–LUAD progressed, leading to a corresponding decrease in STAT1 activation. Under such circumstances, the receptor–ligand interaction between CXCL9 + TAMs and CD8 + Trm cells also diminished. This aligns with recent research outcomes regarding IFN-γ [40]. Injecting high and low doses of IFN-γ can reportedly promote tumor regression and tumor metastasis, respectively [41]. Notably, GGN-LUAD exhibits a stronger IFN-γ signaling pathway than PSN-LUAD, activating STAT1 to regulate CXCL9 secretion in TAMs, thereby enhancing cell communication with its homologous receptor, CXCR3. The results presented above revealed that IFN-γ could influence GGN– and PSN–LUAD invasion and metastasis by activating STAT1 signaling and modulating the interaction between CXCL9 + TAMs and CD8 + Trm.

To validate the role of the IFN-γ/STAT1 signaling axis in the development and progression of LUAD, this study established a robust and mature mouse model based on organoid culture and CRISPR/Cas9 technology to simulate the process of LUAD development. A key advantage of the combination of organoid culture and CRISPR/Cas9 technology is the high degree of control over tumor development [42]. This uniformity in the process of knocking out tumor suppressor genes and introducing oncogenes into organoids ensures that the impact of each genetically modified organoid on tumor development remains consistent and a high degree of reproducibility in tumor development across individuals [43]. Therefore, we were able to capture the entire process of tumorigenesis of TK organoids in mice and observed that tumor development followed a specific temporal pattern, with different phenotypic and pathological characteristics appearing at 3, 7, and 11 weeks, respectively. Pathological results showed that at week 3, the lesion presented with atypical epithelial cell proliferation, prominent nucleoli, and tightly packed cell arrangement; at week 6, the cell morphology showed epithelial thickening, deep staining of nucleoli, weak positivity for Thyroid Transcription Factor (TTF), weak positivity for Ki-67, and positivity for CK-7; by week 10, the cell morphology was varied, with enlarged nucleoli, deep nuclear staining, strong positivity for TTF, strong positivity for Ki-67, and a significant increase in malignancy. These pathological features closely resemble those of human LUAD, confirming the effectiveness of this model in simulating the development of human LUAD. Then, we performed transcriptome sequencing on the tumor tissues of LUAD mice at 3, 7, and 11 weeks, confirming the phenomenon of immune cell exhaustion as LUAD disease progresses. Additionally, Western blotting results demonstrated changes in the IFN-γ/STAT1 signaling axis during the development and progression of LUAD, thereby confirming the results of scRNA-seq analysis.

This study has certain limitations. First, we grouped samples based on distinct disease stages (MIA/IAC/MLC) in GGN– and PSN–LUAD. However, due to insufficient samples received for the PSN–MLC stage, cells in this group could not undergo significant statistical analysis at the cellular level compared to cells in other groups. In the future, we will collect an adequate number of samples to further confirm the reliability of our conclusions. Consequently, we performed a comparative analysis of the divergent outcomes to scrutinize the distinctions between the invasive metastases of GGN– and PSN–LUAD. Second, an exhaustive depiction of all cell types, subtypes, and phenotypes within a single report may not be feasible. Hence, our study highlights key observational outcomes that will be investigated more comprehensively in the future. Despite these limitations, this study provides novel biological insights into the heterogeneity of LUAD, characterized by diverse radiological features, potentially influencing the advancement of precision medicine in clinical LUAD research.

Conclusions

This study confirmed significant heterogeneity in the cellular composition and immune responses in GGN– and PSN–LUAD, and further revealed the differential enrichment and interaction patterns of CXCL9 + TAMs and TREM2 + TAMs, as well as their regulatory genes, during different stages of LUAD progression. Our findings suggest that IFN-γ influenced GGN– and PSN–LUAD invasion and metastasis by activating STAT1 signaling and modulating the interaction between CXCL9 + TAMs and CD8 + Trm.
