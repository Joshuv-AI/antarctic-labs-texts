Plastination of biological materials using a vinyl-based polymer as an alternative method for preserving anatomical specimens
Authors: Gabrielly Cristina Galvão Xavier, Fernando Carlos de Sousa, Marcelo Luis Kuhn Marchioro, Robert F H Dekker, Mário Antônio Alves da Cunha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe preservation of anatomical specimens is essential for teaching and research, enabling detailed analysis of tissue morphology and structure. Traditional methods such as formaldehyde fixation have been increasingly questioned due to their toxicity and associated health risks. Plastination offers a safer, more durable alternative, producing dry, odorless, permanently preserved specimens. This study evaluated polyvinyl acetate (PVAc) as an innovative, low-cost, and non-toxic polymer for the plastination of biological tissues. Acetic acid and glycerol were tested as additives, using bovine heart and kidney as anatomical models. The samples were characterized by ATR-FTIR, X-ray diffraction (XRD), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), SEM-EDS, color analysis (CIELab), and histological examination. The optimal formulation consisted of 85% PVAc, 5% glycerol, and 10% acetic acid. Plastinated specimens preserved anatomical integrity and showed slightly darker, more saturated coloration compared to formalin-fixed samples. Thermal analysis indicated improved stability, and characteristic infrared bands confirmed polymer impregnation. Histological results demonstrated preserved cellular and structural organization. Overall, PVAc proved to be a viable, safe, and affordable alternative for plastination, with potential to replace formaldehyde-based methods and reduce environmental and occupational health risks.Keywords: Anatomical preservation, Polyvinyl acetate (PVAc), Instrumental characterization of plastination, Tissue preservationSubject terms: Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Chemistry, Materials science, Medical research

Introduction

The preservation of anatomical specimens is crucial for teaching and research, enabling detailed analysis of the morphological and structural features of biological tissues1.

Traditional preservation methods, such as long-term formaldehyde preservation, have been increasingly questioned due to their high toxicity, as formaldehyde is associated with an increased risk of bronchial asthma, myeloid leukemia, and other uncommon malignant diseases, including nasopharyngeal, paranasal sinus, and nasal tumors. In this context, strict precautions are required for its storage, handling, and disposal2,3.

During plastination, biological tissues undergo dehydration followed by polymer impregnation under reduced pressure. In this process, the water and lipid components of the tissue are gradually replaced by a curable polymer solution. As the solvent evaporates, the polymer occupies the interstitial spaces of the biological matrix and forms a continuous solid network. This process stabilizes the tissue’s structural components through physical interactions between polymer chains and biomolecules, such as proteins and polysaccharides, thereby preserving the specimen’s three-dimensional architecture4,5.

Plastination is an innovative and highly efficient technique for preserving biological material, significantly reducing the risks associated with maintaining specimens and tissues in tanks or jars containing formalin. Developed by Gunther von Hagens in the 1970s, plastination involves replacing the biological fluids and lipids in tissues with curable polymers, resulting in specimens that are dry, odorless, and permanently preserved. This technique offers numerous advantages over conventional methods, including reduced environmental toxicity, maintenance of tissue three-dimensional integrity, and the ability to handle specimens without the need for protective equipment against hazardous chemicals4,6.

Until 1998, Biodur Products GmbH was the sole supplier of polymers and equipment for plastination. Since then, several companies have proposed using other polymers for tissue plastination as an alternative to reduce costs compared to the traditional technique7.

Research conducted between 2012 and 2017 demonstrates that plastination methods have been explored and adapted, including the impregnation materials used. Among the nine studies selected in the research, the most commonly used materials are variations of epoxy, polyester, and silicone8.

Although various materials can serve as plastination agents, vinyl-based polymers offer a promising alternative due to their accessibility and low cost. It is widely produced and used in various industries, including adhesives (such as white school glue and wood glue), paints, and coatings for paper and textiles. Polyvinyl acetate is an amorphous thermoplastic polymer that forms flexible films and stable matrices upon solvent evaporation9. These characteristics make it suitable for plastination, as the polymer can penetrate the microstructure of biological tissues during the impregnation step and subsequently solidify within the tissue matrix. The resulting polymer network contributes to structural stabilization by filling intercellular spaces and reducing tissue collapse during drying10,11.

In contrast, silicone, traditionally used in plastination, is relatively expensive. Similarly, resins and polyesters are more expensive than PVAc. Being a biocompatible and water-soluble polymer, polyvinyl acetate (PVAc) has been used as a component for structuring biomaterials in biomedical applications, as well as in the production of scaffolds for tissue engineering12. Such applications and properties of PVAc, including its adhesiveness, odorless and colorless appearance, and non-toxicity, support its use as an innovative curable polymer in plastination.

PVAc, as a plastination agent, shows promising advancements in the preservation of biological tissues. Its main advantages include better preservation of tissue texture, greater transparency for anatomical visualization, and effective moisture control, preventing unwanted shrinkage. Its high strength and durability ensure the longevity of plastinated specimens, while its flexibility supports educational and scientific applications, expanding the possibilities for anatomical studies13. Researchers or anatomy students can handle biological specimens plastinated in a PVAc-based solution without harmful or toxic effects on their health, unlike specimens preserved in formalin. The present study aimed to investigate the potential of PVAc as a plastinizing polymer for the plastination of anatomical specimens, given its innovative properties, low cost, biocompatibility, and non-toxicity.

Methods

Anatomical organs and reagents

Hearts and kidneys from healthy cattle were used as biological specimens for plastination studies. The organs were donated by a legally licensed local slaughterhouse (registration number 0022 F), regularly inspected by the Paraná Inspection Service (SIP/POA), and fully authorized for unrestricted use. Only samples from animals with no suspicion or diagnosis of zoonotic diseases were included. According to Brazilian legislation on the scientific use of animals (Federal Law No. 11,794/2008), the use of animal tissues in this study does not require approval by an ethics committee, as no experiments were conducted on live animals14.

In each experimental protocol, reagents of analytical grade were used, suitable for the intended purpose. Commercial-grade polyvinyl acetate (Compactor, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil) was used to prepare the impregnation solutions for plastination.

Preparation of impregnation solutions

The following impregnation solutions for plastination of anatomical specimens were prepared: Solution 1 (100% polyvinyl acetate), Solution 2 (95% polyvinyl acetate and 5% glycerol, w/w), Solution 3 (94% polyvinyl acetate, 5% glycerol (w/w), and 1% acetic acid, v/v), Solution 4 (90% polyvinyl acetate, 5% glycerol (w/w), and 5% acetic acid, v/v), Solution 5 (85% polyvinyl acetate, 5% glycerol (w/w), and 10% acetic acid, v/v), Solution 6 (95% polyvinyl acetate (w/w) and 5% acetic acid, v/v), and Solution 7 (90% polyvinyl acetate (w/w) and 10% acetic acid, v/v). The concentrations of PVAc and the additives studied were selected from previous experiments. The impregnation solutions were prepared and handled in a fume hood, following standard safety protocols for the preparation of acidic solutions. Acetic acid was tested as a solubilizing agent for PVAc and as a viscosity control agent in the plastination solution. Glycerol was used as a plasticizing additive to reduce intermolecular interactions between PVAc chains, thereby increasing the polymer’s mobility and resulting in parts with greater flexibility, less rigidity, and fewer cracks and fissures, as well as preventing the fabric from drying out.

The parameters of apparent viscosity (ηₐ) and shrinkage of the polymeric solutions were used to select the impregnation solution with the greatest potential for plastination. Apparent viscosity was determined using a Brookfield Digital Rotational Viscometer (Brookfield Engineering Laboratories, Massachusetts, USA) by immersing a rotating cylinder into the solutions to be analyzed. Measurements were taken after 30 s of operation, at a standard speed of 25.0 rpm and a temperature of 25 °C.

The shrinkage of the impregnation solutions was determined by measuring the mass loss during drying at room temperature. Volumes corresponding to 15 g of solution were distributed in Petri dishes (15 cm in diameter). The dishes were kept at room temperature for 96 h, then reweighed on an analytical balance. Shrinkage was expressed as the percentage of mass loss after drying.

The impregnation solution selected for plastination of the biological specimens was supplemented with 0.5% (w/v) calcium carbonate as a chemical marker. This marker was used to visualize tissue impregnation through energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS).

Test specimens were obtained by cutting the anatomical pieces into cubes approximately 2 cm on each side. Plastination experiments were performed in triplicate, with each portion collected from different regions of the anatomical specimen.

Plastination of anatomical specimen test samples

The test samples were processed according to the classical plastination protocol described by Hagens et al.4 with methodological adaptations. All steps were carried out at room temperature and included fixation, dehydration, impregnation, and curing. The plastination procedure was performed using an automated system consisting of a vacuum pump, pressure gauge, vacuum chamber, timer, and data processing unit.

The specimens were fixed in a 10% (v/v) formaldehyde solution by immersion for 48 h, with fixations at 6 mm every 12 h. It is important to note that fixation is an essential step in maintaining and preserving the morphology of biological material before plastination. Although formaldehyde is toxic, some advantages of its use as a fixation agent stand out, including the promotion of protein cross-linking, stabilization of macromolecules, maintenance of cellular and tissue architecture, preservation of anatomical relationships, and reduction of structural degradation by inactivating enzymes and microorganisms responsible for post-mortem degradation1,15.

The fixed samples were then dehydrated and degreased at room temperature by immersion in ethanol solutions of increasing concentrations (70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%, v/v). The specimens remained immersed in each solution until the ethanol concentration stabilized, as measured with an alcoholmeter (°GL), a process that took approximately 4 weeks.

The dehydrated and degreased specimens were then impregnated with the preselected polymeric solutions, taking into account the apparent viscosity and shrinkage parameters. The material was immersed in the impregnation solution and subjected to a reduced-pressure atmosphere (-760 mm Hg) inside the vacuum chamber. This process promoted the removal of ethanol solvent from biological tissues, which was replaced by the impregnation solution. Impregnation of the anatomical specimens was considered complete when bubbling in the solution ceased, indicating that ethanol had evaporated under low pressure.

Analysis of the morphological characteristics of plastinated test specimens

The plastinated specimens and those fixed in 10% formalin (control) were subjected to color analysis using the CIELAB system, evaluated for specimen shrinkage (measured by mass loss and surface area reduction), and observed under a stereomicroscope.

The CIELAB color space represents the quantitative relationship of colors along three axes, where the L* axis indicates lightness, and a* (red/green) and b* (yellow/blue) are the chromatic coordinates. In the color space diagram, L* is plotted on the vertical axis, ranging from 0 (black) to 100 (white). The a* value indicates the red–green composition of a color, where + a* corresponds to red and − a* to green. The yellow–blue composition is represented on the b* axis, where + b* indicates yellow and − b* indicates blue. The center of the plane is neutral or achromatic. The distance from the central axis represents chroma (C*), or color saturation, while the angle on the chromaticity axes represents hue (hº)16.

Color measurements were taken in triplicate on both the internal and external surfaces of the samples using the CIELAB system. The chromatic coordinates L*, a*, and b*, as well as the cylindrical coordinates h° (hue angle) and C* (chroma), were considered, with measurements taken across the entire surface of the specimens. Both external surface areas (ES) and internal surface areas (IS) were analyzed; in the heart, the IS region corresponded to the myocardium. Analyses were performed using a digital colorimeter (CR-410, Konica Minolta Inc., Tokyo, Japan).

Mass variation was determined gravimetrically by weighing the samples before and after plastination. Surface area shrinkage was evaluated using open-source software for scientific image processing and analysis, ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA; version 1.54f; https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/).

Instrumental and histological analyses

After selecting the plastination solution, whole organs (bovine heart and kidney) were plastinated using the same protocol as for the test specimens. The efficiency of plastination was evaluated using Attenuated Total Reflectance Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR), X-ray Diffraction (XRD), thermal profiling (TG: thermogravimetry, DTG: derivative thermogravimetry, and DTA: differential thermal analysis), Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) coupled with Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), and histological analysis.

Samples from the plastinated organs and from the control group (specimens fixed in 10% formalin) were collected from the innermost regions of the tissues, as shown in Fig. 1. In the kidney, samples were taken from the renal medulla; in the heart, the left ventricular wall (the thickest part of the heart) was used. The biological tissues were perforated with a 2.50 mm steel drill, allowing the collection of material in powder form for spectroscopic and thermal profile analyses.

FT-IR and XRD analyses

FT-IR spectra were obtained using a Frontier spectrometer (PerkinElmer, USA) with 32 scans in the range of 4000 to 400 cm⁻¹, a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹, and the Attenuated Total Reflectance (ATR) method. X-ray diffraction patterns were obtained using a Rigaku MiniFlex600 diffractometer (CuKα = 1.5418 Å, current of 15 mA, voltage of 40 kV, scanning range of 10° to 60° (2θ), scanning speed of 5° min⁻¹, and step width of 0.02° (2θ).

Thermal analysis

Thermogravimetric (TG), differential thermal (DTA), and derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) analyses were conducted on a TA Instruments SDT Q600 thermal analyzer (New Castle, DE, USA). Mass loss was monitored between 26 °C and 800 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under a synthetic air atmosphere and a flow rate of 50 mL/min.

SEM-EDS analysis

The morphological aspects of the external and internal surface areas of the anatomical specimens were analyzed using a VEGA3 electron microscope coupled to an energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) detector (Oxford, x-Act) (TESCAN, Brno, Czech Republic). The samples were metallized with gold/palladium for 180 s in the Quorum Sputter Coater (Quorum Technology, United Kingdom).

Histological analysis of plastinated organs

Plastinated bovine heart and kidney samples were sectioned on a microtome into 8–10 μm-thick slices in both transverse and longitudinal orientations. The histological sections were made directly on the plastinated specimens, without deplastination or embedding in paraffin or paraplast. The sections were placed on histological slides and covered with glacial acetic acid for tissue deplastination. After deplastination, the histological sections were rinsed dropwise with distilled water until the pH was neutralized. Subsequently, the sections were stained using the Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) technique17 and then examined under an optical microscope.

Results and discussion

Analysis of apparent viscosity (ηₐ) and shrinkage of impregnation solutions

The apparent viscosity of polymeric impregnation solutions and the shrinkage (mass loss) of plastinated tissues provide essential information on impregnation quality. Lower-viscosity solutions penetrate pores, capillaries, and crevices more easily, thereby accelerating the impregnation process. This reduces impregnation time and increases the likelihood of filling fine vessels and interstitial spaces. Less viscous formulations tend to cause less shrinkage because they replace tissue fluids with less capillary effort, allowing for penetration without excessive mechanical extraction.

The shrinkage of polymer solutions during curing can compromise the specimen’s original shape and size. Polymers with a high shrinkage rate can cause deformations and loss of anatomical details; therefore, controlling shrinkage is essential to preserve the morphological fidelity of the plastinated sample18. During vacuum impregnation, the decrease in pressure promotes the evaporation of the solvent trapped within the tissue matrix. As the solvent is removed, the polymer solution penetrates the interstitial spaces by capillary action and diffusion. This replacement process allows the polymer to occupy the spaces previously filled by ethanol and residual tissue fluids, enabling efficient stabilization of the biological structure after curing4.

As shown in Fig. 2a, solutions 1, 5, and 6 stood out from the others based on their apparent viscosity and shrinkage values. Solution 1, composed solely of PVAc and previously studied by our research group, was selected as a control for comparison due to the quality and durability of the plastinated material observed in earlier studies.

a Apparent viscosity (ηₐ) and shrinkage of impregnation solutions, as well as mass and surface area shrinkage of the test specimens; b cardiac tissue; and c renal tissue.

Solution 5, composed of PVAc (85%), glycerol (5%), and acetic acid (10%), exhibited a low apparent viscosity (ηₐ) (5696.7 mPa·s) and moderate shrinkage capacity (42%). In contrast, solution 6, formulated with PVAc (95%) and acetic acid (5%), showed a slightly higher apparent viscosity (ηₐ) (6023.6 mPa·s) and shrinkage capacity (44%) compared to solution 5.

Solution 5 was selected because it had one of the lowest apparent viscosities, which is important for facilitating impregnation into deeper tissue layers. The apparent viscosity/shrinkage ratio of this solution was also a determining factor in its selection. Another noteworthy aspect is the presence of glycerol in the formulation, which may contribute to the specimen’s gloss and flexibility.

The results for the shrinkage of plastinated cardiac and renal tissues are shown in Fig. 2b and c. Shrinkage of an anatomical specimen is a characteristic of plastination: the reduction in tissue size that occurs during the process. In general, shrinkage percentages of up to 10% are considered acceptable according to the scientific literature4,18.

In traditional plastination, the dehydration solvent used is acetone, whose application at low temperatures (-25 °C) minimizes tissue shrinkage18. In the technique employed in the present study, ethanol was used as the solvent because it is more economical and accessible. However, the samples’ shrinkage ranged from 20% to 42%, considerably exceeding the values reported for the standard technique. It is worth noting that this higher shrinkage does not compromise the use of plastinated specimens produced by this method for anatomical study and teaching, as the tissues retain their original characteristics and are not visually affected by the shrinkage.

The values of mass and surface area shrinkage varied by biological tissue type. In general, solution 5 produced the lowest shrinkage values in both tissues. The kidneys exhibited higher shrinkage rates regardless of the impregnation solution used, likely due to histological differences between tissues. Renal tissue, especially in the cortical and medullary regions (the sampled area), is highly vascularized, and its connective tissue contains a highly hydrated ground substance rich in proteoglycans. In other words, the kidney retains more fluid in its composition than the myocardium, which is composed mainly of cardiac striated muscle fibers19.

Visual and mesoscopic morphological aspect

The samples plastinated with solution 1 (Fig. 3b and f) were rigid and inflexible upon touch and handling. Observation under a stereoscopic magnifying glass revealed that the samples’ surfaces were impregnated with the polymer, displaying a reflective sheen on all faces. The external surface appeared considerably shinier, while the internal part exhibited a subtler, yet still noticeable, gloss.

Morphological aspect of the surface of the test specimens.

Impregnation of the samples with solution 5 resulted in material with a darker color (visual perception) than with the other polymeric impregnation solutions (Fig. 3c and g). The inclusion of glycerin provided greater flexibility and smoothness to the specimen (sensory perception), which remained dry to the touch and did not release any impregnation solution.

The kidney and heart samples exhibited a more intense surface gloss than the specimen impregnated with solution 1, although the kidney appeared less reflective than the heart. Inside the samples, the polymer sheen remained subtle but clearly present.

The samples plastinated with solution 6 (Fig. 3d and h) displayed a lighter coloration (as perceived visually) than those plastinated with solution 5. Similar to what was observed in specimens plastinated with solution 1, plastination using solution 6 resulted in rigid specimens with little flexibility to the touch and handling. The surfaces were completely impregnated by the polymer (visual perception), and stereoscopic magnification revealed an intense shine on the external surface, while the internal surface showed a more subdued reflection. Visually, no differences in opacity were observed between the cardiac and renal tissues.

Instrumental color analysis

The samples preserved in 10% formalin showed higher lightness (L*) values, whereas the plastinated samples showed lower values, suggesting a tendency toward darkening after plastination. The literature reports that tissues fixed in formaldehyde tend to lose coloration due to the rapid coagulation of blood in this preservation method20. This process results in lighter tissues with low saturation and a more grayish appearance.

Except for the external surface of the kidney, plastination contributed to a reduction in the a* coordinate values in the samples, with a slight decrease in their red coloration and a shift toward more neutral tones. The b* coordinate values increased after plastination, indicating a more yellowish appearance in the anatomical tissue. The plastinated samples showed increased chroma (C*) values, indicating greater saturation. The increase in the hue angle (hº) reflects the perceived tone, which shifts from yellowish to reddish hues in tissues preserved in 10% formalin to shades closer to pure yellow in the plastinated samples.

The external surfaces (ES) of the heart and kidney samples showed a more pronounced color variation, as the ΔEES values were slightly higher than the ΔEIS values. This difference can be attributed to histological variation between the surfaces; both the heart and kidney exhibit cellular differentiation between their external and internal surfaces. The tissue compositions may have influenced the way they interacted with the polymer, resulting in distinct colorations. The results indicate that plastination generally causes perceptible changes in tissue coloration, making it darker, less reddish, and more yellowish, and increasing color saturation. However, the coloration of specimens may vary within the technique itself, depending on the impregnation formulation used.

The macroscopic visual observations described earlier were corroborated by the values obtained from instrumental colorimetric analysis, in which solution 1 (100% PVAc) and solution 6 (95% PVAc and 5% acetic acid) showed higher L* values compared to solution 5 (85% PVAc, 10% acetic acid, and 5% glycerol). In this context, specimens plastinated with solution 1 and solution 6 were lighter than those plastinated with solution 5. Additionally, chroma (C*) showed more saturated, vivid tissue colors in these solutions. It can be inferred that the darkening trend observed in specimens plastinated with solution 5 was influenced by the presence of glycerin in its composition. Preservation in glycerin, also known as the Giacomini Method, is known to cause considerable darkening of anatomical tissue21, which explains why the specimens plastinated with this solution showed decreased lightness (L*) values, giving them a darker appearance. Glycerin tends to darken biological samples when used as a fixative due to its high refractive index (~ 1.47), which alters light transmission and can make them appear darker or more yellowish. Its oxidation over time forms compounds that intensify darkening, and its interaction with cellular pigments and proteins modifies their coloration22. However, the darkening of tissues by glycerin during plastination may have advantages, as tissues fixed only in formalin tend to show grayish tones with low saturation, making them less attractive for educational purposes. Plastination enhances anatomical specimens in several ways, and coloration has been discussed in recent years as a way to enrich the presentation of plastinated material23.

Although solution 5 darkened the samples, it increased color saturation, resulting in a more vivid coloration. In muscular tissue, for instance, this has the advantage of producing a more vibrant, visually appealing color than the faded appearance typical of formaldehyde preservation. Conversely, it may not be suitable for certain tissues, such as nervous tissue, which tends to darken more when preserved in glycerin, hindering differentiation between white and gray matter21. Solution 6 or solution 1 would be more appropriate for this purpose, as they do not contain glycerin in their composition and exhibit higher lightness (L*) values, respectively. The data from instrumental color analysis indicate that the composition of plastination formulations can be tailored to the specificity of each tissue, enabling adjustments to achieve more aesthetically appealing coloration in plastinated specimens.

Solution 5 was considered the most suitable for plastination of whole specimens due to the lower tissue shrinkage, morphological appearance, and color. Although tissue shrinkage is inherent to the plastination process, the goal was to select the solution with the lowest values of shrinkage, both in plate tests and in test specimens, a requirement met by solution 5. Additionally, it was the only plastination solution to improve tissue flexibility, a favorable characteristic of plastinated materials. Although the specimens plastinated with solution 5 exhibited a darker coloration, this does not represent a problem for the analyzed tissues. The darkening of plastinated tissues is a result of the plastination process and does not indicate deterioration or preservation issues. During plastination, the lipids and water in the tissues are replaced by polymers. This substitution, combined with dehydration and forced impregnation, can alter the tissues’ natural tone, making them appear more opaque or darker.

Instrumental (FTIR, XRD, thermal profile, SEM) and histological analysis of plastinated organs

The FT-IR spectra obtained from the heart and kidney samples plastinated with solution 5 are shown in Fig. 5; Table 2. The band at 3283 cm⁻¹, which appears in all samples except for the pure polyvinyl acetate solution (Fig. 4a), was attributed to the O–H stretching vibration24,25. This is justified by the absence of hydroxyl groups (–OH) in the main structure of polyvinyl acetate. The presence of absorption bands related to hydroxyl groups (-OH) would occur if partial hydrolysis of polyvinyl acetate took place, leading to the consequent formation of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA). The band at 2924 cm⁻¹ was attributed to the symmetric C-H stretching vibration24 and is observed in all samples. Some absorption bands in the infrared region are typical and constitute a characteristic fingerprint of PVAc, such as the bands at 2924 cm⁻¹ (C–H stretching), 1725 cm⁻¹ (C = O stretching), 1376 cm⁻¹ (symmetric deformation of the methyl group (–CH₃)), 1231 cm⁻¹ (C–O stretching of the ester group (–COO–) of PVAc), 944 cm⁻¹ (out-of-plane C–H deformation or rocking of the CH₂ group), and around 603 cm⁻¹ (out-of-plane bending vibration of the C–C group)24,26–30. The presence of these bands in the polyvinyl acetate samples (Fig. 4a), solution 5 (containing 85% PVAc) (Fig. 4b), and plastinated samples (Fig. 4d and f) confirms the impregnation of the specimens with the PVAc-based plastination solution. The bands at 1636 cm⁻¹ and 1530 cm⁻¹ do not appear in the spectra of the polyvinyl acetate samples (Fig. 4a) or solution 5 (Fig. 4b), but are present only in the specimens fixed in formalin or plastinated. These bands are attributed to C = O stretching (amide I) and NH bending combined with C–N stretching (amide II) in proteins31,32. The bands at 944 cm⁻¹ and 603 cm⁻¹ are also present in the plastinated samples, as well as in the PVAc and solution 5 samples that contain PVAc, albeit with low intensity. It is essential to note that such bands are not observed in the formalin-fixed samples (control samples, not plastinated). Similarly, the band at 1231 cm⁻¹ appears with greater intensity in the PVAc samples, in solution 5, and in the plastinated samples. However, in the control samples (Fig. 4c and e), the intensity of this band is considerably reduced, and its presence could be attributed to vibrations associated with C–N bonds and N–H deformations, as some proteins may exhibit bands in the 1230–1250 cm⁻¹ range33.

The FTIR analysis identified characteristic functional groups associated with both tissue components and the plastination polymer. The presence of characteristic PVAc absorption bands in the plastinated specimens indicates that the polymer was successfully incorporated into the biological matrix24,26–30. However, the spectra did not indicate the formation of new functional groups, suggesting that no covalent chemical reactions occurred between the plastination solution and the biological material. Instead, the results suggest that the plastination process primarily involves physical impregnation of the polymer into the tissue matrix, accompanied by intermolecular interactions with biomolecules, such as proteins and lipids. These interactions may include hydrogen bonding and dipole-dipole interactions between the polymer and polar groups in the tissue, leading to slight variations in band intensity or position without altering the material’s fundamental chemical structure.

The X-ray diffraction analysis shows that the samples exhibit a diffractometric profile typical of amorphous or poorly crystalline materials (Fig. 5). The pure PVAc sample (Fig. 5a) displays a broad peak (amorphous halo) at 2θ = 13.6°, which corresponds to the average distance between polymer chains. This sample also exhibits a second broad peak at 2θ between 17.8° and 31°, along with two small, sharp peaks at 20.7° and 23°, which appear within the wide halo and may indicate the presence of a crystalline fraction or local ordering within the PVAc structure. The kidney and heart samples fixed in 10% formalin and plastinated exhibited an essentially amorphous profile with two broad diffractometric peaks at 2θ = 9.35° and 20.3°. The XRD analysis indicate that plastination of the anatomical specimens with the PVAc solution did not alter the diffractometric profiles, with the original amorphous structures remaining unchanged. These results suggest that the plastination process does not promote significant structural reorganization or the formation of new crystalline phases in the biological material, supporting the interpretation that polymer incorporation occurs mainly through physical impregnation within the tissue matrix rather than through chemical reactions with the biological components.

Thermogravimetric analyses of kidney (Fig. 6a) and heart (Fig. 6c) samples fixed in 10% formalin revealed three mass-loss events. In the kidney sample, the first event occurred up to 162 °C (TG curve), indicated by a subtle endothermic peak observed at 58 °C on the DTA curve, corresponding to the loss of adsorbed water (14%). The subtle endothermic peak on the DTA curve suggests that this mass loss event involves the evaporation of adsorbed water, as little thermal energy is required for this process. Between 162 °C and 405 °C (TG curve), a second mass-loss region was observed, with two consecutive events accounting for a total mass loss of 44%. The first event occurred at 296 °C and the second at 344 °C, both exhibiting exothermic peaks on the DTA curve.

Thermal profile of kidney samples in 10% formalin (a), plastinated kidney (b), heart in 10% formalin (c), and plastinated heart (d).

The mass loss at 296 °C is likely associated with the degradation of the proteinaceous organic matrix of renal tissue, which is rich in structural proteins such as collagen, actin, and myosin.

At this temperature, proteins denature and thermally decompose, leading to peptide bond cleavage and the release of volatile compounds. The presence of an exothermic peak on the DTA curve suggests an exothermic reaction associated with the partial oxidation of proteins. The second event at 344 °C (DTA curve) can be attributed to the decomposition and oxidation of carbonaceous material, as carbonaceous residues (biological charcoal) may form after the initial protein degradation. At this stage, oxidation of these organic residues occurs, resulting in additional mass loss. This second exothermic peak on the DTA curve (344 °C) indicates an intense exothermic process, characteristic of the combustion of carbonaceous material generated in the previous decomposition.

The final mass loss event of the kidney sample fixed in formalin occurred between 405 °C and 605 °C on the TG curve, with exothermic peaks at 525 °C on the DTG curve and 515 °C on the DTA curve. This mass loss is attributed to the complete combustion of the remaining carbonaceous residues from the previous decomposition. In the range of 405–605 °C (TG curve), this carbon is oxidized in the presence of oxygen, producing CO₂ and other gaseous products. This process is typically exothermic, which explains the peaks observed on the DTA curve.

The heart sample fixed in 10% formalin exhibited a thermal profile very similar to that of the kidney sample. The thermogravimetric (TG) curve also shows three regions of mass loss, with the first event associated with the loss of adsorbed water (14%), occurring up to 162 °C (TG curve), with a subtle exothermic peak at 55 °C on the DTA curve. A second region of mass loss, characterized by two consecutive events, was also observed in the heart sample. These events occurred between 162 °C and 406 °C (TG curve), confirmed by exothermic peaks at 296 °C and 345 °C on the DTA curve, and were attributed to the decomposition and oxidation of carbonaceous material in the sample.

Figure 6b and d show the thermal events observed in the plastinated samples. Plastination increased the thermal stability of both kidney and heart tissue samples, as indicated by a broader temperature range for mass loss events. In the plastinated kidney sample (Fig. 6b), the first event occurred up to 123 °C (TG curve), with a mass loss of 15%, attributed to the evaporation of residual water present in the plastinated material. Although plastination involves replacing water with polymers, traces of moisture may still remain in the sample. This mass loss may also be related to the possible elimination of residual solvents used in the plastination process, such as acetic acid.

A consecutive mass loss (11%) occurred between 123 °C and 225 °C (TG curve), with a subtle endothermic peak at 180 °C on the DTA curve and an exothermic one at 180 °C on the DTG curve, attributed to a physical transition or evaporation of volatile components, including traces of solvents used in the process. The exothermic peak at 180 °C (DTG) indicates that, in addition to solvent evaporation, the initial decomposition of the polymeric matrix used in plastination may occur. A third mass loss region (38%) was observed between 225 °C and 479 °C (TG curve), with two consecutive mass loss events exhibiting exothermic peaks at 320 °C and 408 °C on the DTG curve. These events are attributed to thermal processes related to the degradation of the polymeric matrix used in plastination and to the decomposition of the remaining organic constituents of the tissue. The first event at 320 °C (exothermic peak on the DTG) is likely associated with the initial thermal degradation of the plastinating polymer, leading to bond-breaking and the release of volatile products. The exothermic nature indicates that decomposition occurs through partial oxidation of the polymeric matrix, a common phenomenon in synthetic polymers exposed to high temperatures. At this stage, residual tissue proteins may also decompose, releasing gases such as CO2, CO, and hydrocarbons.

The second event, at 408 °C (exothermic peak on the DTG), indicates advanced decomposition and combustion of the carbonaceous material generated in the previous stage. The plastinated tissue still contains a residual organic fraction, which undergoes complete oxidation at this temperature, releasing a large amount of thermal energy. The presence of an exothermic peak confirms that combustion of the polymeric matrix and biological residues occurs intensely in this range.

The final degradation of the sample occurred between 480 °C and 716 °C (TG curve), with exothermic peaks at 564 °C (DTA curve) and 568 °C (DTG curve), attributed to the final combustion of carbonaceous residues and the possible decomposition of residual inorganic material. After decomposition of the polymeric matrix and proteins in earlier stages, a highly condensed carbonaceous residue remains. At temperatures between 500 °C and 700 °C, complete oxidation of this carbon occurs, converting it into CO2 and other gases. Depending on the materials used in the plastination process, traces of inorganic salts (such as calcium phosphates or silica) may undergo thermal transformations within this temperature range. Some additives used in plastination polymers may also undergo final exothermic reactions. The thermal decomposition profile of the plastinated heart sample was similar to that observed in the plastinated kidney, with four regions of more pronounced mass loss and consecutive mass-loss events within the same range. The first event occurred at temperatures up to 122 °C (TG curve), with a 16% mass loss, corresponding to the evaporation of residual water. The second event occurred between 122 °C and 222 °C (TG curve) and was attributed to the evaporation of volatile components and initial decomposition of the polymeric matrix. Subsequently, two consecutive mass losses occurred between 222 °C and 478 °C (TG curve), attributed to degradation of the polymeric matrix and decomposition of the remaining organic constituents of the biological tissue. Final decomposition of the sample occurred between 478 °C and 713 °C.

The micrographs obtained by scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) revealed the morphological aspects of the plastinated tissues (Fig. 7).

The heart samples used for SEM visualization were collected from the ventricular region (myocardium). This layer is composed of bundles of cardiac striated muscle tissue arranged in various directions19,34. Cardiac muscle fibers are observed in the longitudinal direction, maintaining their normal morphology (Fig. 7a and b), with little interstitial tissue between the muscle bundles (Fig. 7c). When comparing the plastinated tissue with the formalin-fixed tissue, it was found that plastination resulted in denser tissue (Fig. 7d), with the polymer filling the spaces between the muscle bundles (Fig. 8f). At 500× magnification (Fig. 7e), the organization of the muscle bundles in cross section can be seen, preserving the expected histological arrangement.

The kidneys were sampled in the cortical and medullary regions for SEM analysis. In general, the surface of tissue fixed in 10% formalin exhibits high porosity, likely associated with its morphological characteristics (Fig. 7g and h, and 7i). Renal tissue is highly vascularized and contains a large number of tubules and glomeruli responsible for the organ’s functional activity34. These structures are preserved through formalin fixation, and the spaces previously filled with fluids remain empty, producing the porous appearance seen in Fig. 7g and h, and 7i. Similar to cardiac tissue, renal tissue showed a more compact appearance, especially in the porous regions, which were completely filled by the polymer (Figs. 7j, k, l).

Both samples exhibited a polymeric layer formed during plastination (Fig. 8). The heart appeared compacted from its superolateral perspective (Fig. 8a). The surface area was clearly coated with the polymer, as indicated by its smoother morphological appearance, with some irregularities corresponding to the underlying tissue (Fig. 8b). At higher magnification (2000×) in the cross-sectional region, the muscle fibers can be observed in transverse section (Fig. 8c), demonstrating that the structural morphological organization was preserved. In renal tissue samples, the polymer coating of the specimens can also be observed, with a smooth surface layer containing small holes, likely formed by microbubbles during the curing process (Fig. 8a and b, and 8c).

Energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) analysis clearly revealed the presence of the chemical marker calcium used in the plastination solution. As shown in Fig. 9c and g: calcium was distributed across the sample surfaces, as evidenced by the yellowish-green coloration. The calcium concentrations were estimated at 0.8% (Wt) in the plastinated heart sample and 1.9% (Wt) in the plastinated kidney sample (Fig. 9d and h). In the formalin-fixed samples (Fig. 9a and e), calcium was not detected, as it was added only to the plastination solution as a marker to confirm tissue impregnation.

Deplastination is the process of reversing plastination. This is done by exposing the plastinated material to a solvent that dissolves the plasticizing polymer. Research on this process is scarce and generally describes the deplastination of materials impregnated with polymers used in traditional plastination. For this reason, it was necessary to establish a deplastination protocol for the polymer in question, adapting the methodologies described in the original process according to the needs of the PVAc-plastinated material. The deplastinated material was embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with the standard hematoxylin-eosin (HE) technique. The histological sections were then observed under a light microscope (Fig. 10).

Photomicrographs of myocardium: non-plastinated (a, b) and deplastinated (c, d). Cross-sections are shown in (a) and (c), and longitudinal sections in (b) and (d). Arrows indicate red blood cells located between muscle bundles. Photomicrographs of renal parenchyma: non-plastinated (e, f) and deplastinated (g, h). Cross-sections are shown in (e) and (g), and longitudinal sections in (f) and (h). Arrows indicate renal glomeruli in (e, g) and renal tubules in (f, h).

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining is based on the acidic nature of eosin, which has an affinity for basic components, such as intracellular and extracellular proteins, and on the affinity of hematoxylin, a basic dye, for acidic substances, including the nucleic acids present in nuclear DNA. The myocardium is composed of bundles of cardiac muscle cells arranged in layers, along with their connective tissue coverings and other elements such as blood vessels. Histological analysis revealed that PVAc plastination, followed by deplastination, did not alter the affinity of tissue components for hematoxylin and eosin.

The cytoplasm of muscle cells exhibited a reddish coloration due to the action of eosin, while the cell nuclei displayed the typical purple coloration produced by hematoxylin. It was also observed that there was no alteration in cell or nuclear morphology; moreover, red blood cells maintained intact morphology (Fig. 10d).

PVAc plastination also preserved the overall structure of the renal parenchyma (Fig. 10g and h), maintaining reactivity to hematoxylin and eosin stains and the morphology of glomeruli and renal tubules. However, Fig. 10h shows paler eosin staining than in non-plastinated material (Fig. 10f). Baygeldi et al.35 also observed paler eosin staining in renal tubules of material plastinated with the Biodur S1 polymer. Cellular morphology was preserved, with no cellular collapse or rupture, and nuclear morphology was maintained. Even compared to non-plastinated material, morphology was well preserved.

Histological analysis demonstrated the maintenance of tissue organization after PVAc plastination, indicating that the dehydration, impregnation, and curing processes do not disrupt tissue- or cell-level morphology. Furthermore, these processes are reversible through simple deplastination using acetic acid. Grondin et al.36 demonstrated that specimens plastinated with Biodur S10 polymer and deplastinated with methoxide retained their affinity for hematoxylin and eosin stains, and their microscopic morphology was preserved. These observations support the histological use of plastination, indicating that plastinated specimens can subsequently be used for microscopic analyses, expanding the utility of a single biological material across multiple contexts, from anatomy to histology.

Beyond histological applicability, deplastination may also facilitate the disposal of plastinated specimens. According to Francis et al.37 the safe disposal of plastinates is challenging, as they are preserved indefinitely and do not deteriorate over time. Deplastination allows the material to revert to its pre-plastination characteristics, enabling the tissue to decompose naturally without interference from the polymer.

Future research could help develop improved disposal methods for this type of anatomical material. Notably, plastination followed by deplastination represents a promising approach, especially for teaching and research institutions seeking to extend sample longevity, reduce the use of toxic substances, and enable the reuse of tissues across different methodologies.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that plastination with polyvinyl acetate (PVAc) is a viable and promising alternative for preserving anatomical specimens, offering significant advantages over traditional formaldehyde-based methods. The formulation containing 85% PVAc, 10% acetic acid, and 5% glycerol yielded the best results, providing a balance between low shrinkage, tissue flexibility, and maintenance of structural morphology. Instrumental analyses confirmed the polymer’s impregnation of the tissues, ensuring superior thermal stability and preservation of cellular organization. Infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) identified PVAc in the plastinated specimens, while thermogravimetric analysis demonstrated increased thermal resistance of the impregnated tissues. Furthermore, scanning electron microscopy and histological analyses revealed that plastination did not compromise tissue integrity and even enabled reversibility through deplastination with acetic acid. From an educational and scientific perspective, the technique offers benefits such as reduced environmental toxicity, improved handling of anatomical specimens, and greater specimen durability. Based on the results, the use of PVAc as a base for plastination solutions represents a safe, economical, and efficient innovation with the potential to replace conventional methods and advance anatomical teaching and research practices.
