Descriptive epidemiology of common respiratory pathogen infections in children: a retrospective cross-sectional study in Xinjiang, China
Authors: Yahao Chen, Yanhua Zeng, Yan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRespiratory tract infections are a major global health concern for children, characterized by significant morbidity and mortality, particularly in resource-limited regions. This study investigated the descriptive epidemiology of common respiratory pathogens among children in Xinjiang, China.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted on 2364 pediatric outpatients and emergency department visitors aged 0–14 years with respiratory infections from September 2023 to December 2023. Nasopharyngeal swabs were collected and analyzed using multiplex PCR for six common pathogens: influenza A virus (IFVA), influenza B virus (IFVB), respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), adenovirus (ADV), human rhinovirus (HRV), and Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP). The distribution of pathogens among different age groups was evaluated.ResultsNo significant sex-based differences were observed in the detection of IFVA, IFVB, RSV, MP, HRV, and ADV (P > 0.05). Among the nasopharyngeal swab samples, 58% (1371/2364) were positive for a single respiratory pathogen, 21.8% (516/2364) for two pathogens, 3.3% (78/2364) for three pathogens, and 0.3% (6/2364) for four pathogens. Notably, IFVA had the highest detection rate (32.6%) among the single infections. HRV was the most frequently identified virus, accounting for 34.8%, followed closely by IFVA at 31.4% and RSV at 20.3%. ADV was detected in 10.4% of cases, while MP and IFVB were found in 8.7% and 7.1% of samples, respectively. The highest pathogen detection rates were observed in children aged 3 to 7 years. Among the co-infections, dual pathogen infections predominated, comprising 86.0% of instances; notably, the combination of IFVA and HRV accounted for 23.3% of these cases. Triple pathogen infections represented 13% of co-infections, with combinations such as RSV, HRV, and ADV being the most prevalent.ConclusionThis study highlights the epidemiological profile of respiratory pathogen infections among pediatric outpatients and emergency visitors in Xinjiang, revealing crucial age-specific trends and co-infection patterns. Despite limitations inherent to its single-center, retrospective design, these findings provide a valuable baseline for informing local surveillance and guiding preventive strategies in community healthcare settings.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13449-6.Keywords: Respiratory infections, Virus, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Pediatric epidemiology characteristics, Co-infections, Multiplex PCR

Background

Respiratory infections are among the most common infectious diseases affecting children globally, posing a significant threat to their health. These infections are prevalent not only in developed countries but are particularly pronounced in resource-limited countries and regions, often resulting in high morbidity and mortality rates [1]. According to statistics from the World Health Organization, lower respiratory tract infections are the fourth leading cause of death globally, with nearly 2.6 million deaths worldwide in 2019 [2]. The incidence of upper respiratory tract infections is as high as 52.0–128.2 per 100,000 [3], and approximately 2 million children under 5 years old die from community-acquired pneumonia each year, accounting for 19% of deaths in this age group [4]. The epidemiological characteristics of respiratory infections are featured by distinct seasonal fluctuations and periodic outbreaks [5]. Respiratory infections in China exhibit distinct seasonal prevalence patterns, with markedly high susceptibility observed in children and the elderly [6]. Data released by the China Centers for Disease Control and Prevention indicate significant differences in the etiology and epidemiological characteristics of acute respiratory infections in various regions and age groups [6].

Infectious agents, including viruses and bacteria, are the primary causes of respiratory infections in children. Among them, viruses are the most common etiology, including respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), influenza virus, human rhinovirus (HRV), and adenovirus (ADV) [6]. Influenza viruses are classified into three types—A, B, and C—based on the antigenic properties of the viral nucleoprotein and matrix protein, as well as their genetic characteristics. Each type can be subdivided into different subtypes. Among them, influenza A viruses (IFVA) with H1, H3, and H5 subtypes are commonly pathogenic and pose a severe threat to human health [7]. Influenza virus outbreaks exhibit distinct seasonal characteristics [8]. Children are particularly susceptible to influenza viruses due to their underdeveloped immune systems. Influenza infections not only cause upper respiratory tract infections such as the common cold and pharyngitis but can also lead to lower respiratory tract infections like bronchitis and pneumonia, severely affecting children’s health [9, 10]. In China, influenza viruses and RSV are the main pathogens causing acute respiratory infections in children [11]. Infants, particularly those with underlying conditions such as prematurity, congenital heart disease, or chronic lung disease, constitute a high-risk population for severe RSV infection, which is a leading cause of hospitalization for bronchiolitis and pneumonia in this age group [12, 13]. However, it is important to note that the majority of RSV infections in otherwise healthy children are mild and self-limiting [14, 15]. Therefore, conducting descriptive epidemiological studies of respiratory pathogen infections in different age groups of children is crucial for understanding their distribution patterns and for developing effective prevention and control strategies.

This retrospective cross-sectional study aimed to determine the prevalence, type-specific distribution, and co-infection patterns of six common respiratory pathogens across different age groups of non-hospitalized children in Xinjiang, China, a region with distinct climatic and demographic features where comprehensive, multi-pathogen surveillance data from the post-pandemic period are scarce. By analyzing nasopharyngeal swab samples collected from pediatric outpatients and emergency department visitors between September and December 2023, this study sought to establish a critical epidemiological baseline for this unique region during a pivotal transitional phase. This focused period captures the early autumn-winter peak of respiratory infections, allowing for a detailed characterization of the pathogen spectrum within this high-incidence window rather than an analysis of year-round seasonal dynamics. The six common pathogens, including five viruses of IFVA, Influenza B virus (IFVB), RSV, ADV, and HRV, and the atypical bacterium Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP), were detected, and their distribution patterns were analyzed. Our findings are expected to provide timely, region-specific scientific evidence for the development of targeted prevention and control strategies in community and primary healthcare settings.

Methods

Study participants

This retrospective cross-sectional study included non-hospitalized children with respiratory infections who presented to the pediatric outpatient clinic or emergency department of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University from September 2023 to December 2023. Their clinical data were collected for retrospective analysis. Inclusion criteria: Eligible participants were children aged 0 to 14 years who were clinically diagnosed with an acute respiratory infection. The clinical case definition for acute respiratory infection required the presence of at least one respiratory symptom (e.g., cough, rhinorrhea, sore throat, or shortness of breath) accompanied by at least one systemic symptom (e.g., fever ≥ 37.5 °C, headache, myalgia, or malaise) [16–18]. Additional criteria were: (1) first-time visit to our hospital for the current illness; (2) local residents; (3) availability of complete clinical data. The exclusion criteria included the presence of other respiratory diseases, severe systemic organic diseases, malignant tumors, or congenital immunodeficiencies. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University. Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University.

Multiplex PCR

On the day of the visit, nasopharyngeal secretions were collected using sterile swabs and subsequently analyzed using multiplex PCR. The nucleic acids were extracted using the Nucleic Acid Extraction Kits (Sansure Biotech, Changsha, China; Cat # S1002E) on the Smart32 Plus automatic nucleic acid extractor (Daan Gene Co., Ltd. of Sun Yat-sen University, Guangzhou, China; Model: Smart32 Plus). The amplification was performed on the SLAN-96P real-time PCR system (Shanghai Hongshi Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China; Model: SLAN-96P) using the Respiratory Pathogen Nucleic Acid Detection Kit (Sansure Biotech Inc., Changsha, China; Cat. # S3066E). According to the manufacturer’s specifications, the multiplex PCR kit has limits of detection of 2.0 TCID50/mL (50% tissue culture infectious dose per milliliter) for influenza viruses and 500.0 copies/mL for other targets, with > 99% concordance compared to reference methods and no cross-reactivity with 34 common pathogens; additionally, our laboratory participates in regular external quality assessment programs. The amplification procedures included the following steps: reverse transcription at 50 °C for 30 min (1 cycle); pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min (1 cycle); and denaturation, annealing, and extension at 95 °C for 15 s and 50 °C for 30 s (45 cycles). All operations were strictly conducted following the kit and instrument manuals, and results were evaluated based on the positive standards outlined in the manuals.

Statistical analysis

Data were processed using SPSS 26.0 statistical software. Categorical data are expressed as numbers and rates (n, %), with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) calculated using the Wilson score method. Group comparisons were performed using the χ² test. To account for multiple comparisons, P-values from the age-group analyses were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Adjusted q-values are presented alongside nominal P-values, with q < 0.05 considered statistically significant after correction. A nominal P-value of < 0.05 was used as the threshold for statistical significance in all other analyses where multiple comparisons were not a concern.

Results

The overall detection rate of respiratory pathogens

This study included a total of 2364 pediatric cases, aged 0 to 14 years. There were 1260 males and 1104 females. The detection rate of respiratory pathogens was compared between male and female patients. As shown in Table 1, there were no statistically significant differences in detection rates for IFVA, IFVB, RSV, MP, HRV, and ADV between different sex groups (P > 0.05) (Table 1). Overall, 1971 (83.4%) of the nasopharyngeal swab samples tested positive for at least one pathogen, while 393 (16.6%) were negative for all six pathogens targeted in this study (Fig. 1). Of note, 58.0% (95% CI: 56.0–60.0%; 1371/2364) were tested positive for a single type of respiratory pathogen, 21.8% (95% CI: 20.1–23.6%; 516/2364) for two pathogens, 3.3% (95% CI: 2.6–4.1%; 78/2364) for three pathogens, and 0.3% (95% CI: 0.1–0.6%; 6/2364) for four pathogens (Table 2). Among the various types of respiratory pathogen infections, HRV had the highest detection rate at 34.8%, followed by IFVA (31.4%), RSV (20.3%), ADV (10.4%), MP (8.7%), and IFVB (7.1%) (Fig. 1). In instances of single pathogen infections, IFVA exhibited the highest detection rate at 32.6%, followed by HRV (30.1%), RSV (17.2%), IFVB (8%), MP (7.7%), and ADV (4.4%) (Table 2).

Comparison of the pathogen detection rate in children of different ages

Among the 2364 children, the number of cases with positive pathogen detection in the age groups of 0–1 year, 1–3 years, 3–7 years, and 7–14 years were 134, 251, 1171, and 808, respectively, with the 3–7-year age group exhibiting the highest prevalence of respiratory pathogen infections. Table 3 presents the positivity rate within each age group, reflecting the age-specific burden of infection. After applying the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction for multiple comparisons, the differences in the positive detection rates of IFVA, IFVB, RSV, MP, and HRV across different age groups remained statistically significant (q < 0.05) (Table 3). In contrast, the positive detection rate of ADV was not significantly different among different age groups (q = 0.052) (Table 3). Notably, the detection rate for RSV was highest in the 0–1-year age group (30.6%, 95% CI: 23.3–39.0%), followed by HRV (29.1%, 95% CI: 21.9–37.4%); the HRV detection rate peaked in the 1–3-year age group (30.7%, 95% CI: 25.2–36.7%), followed by IFVA (26.7%, 95% CI: 21.5–32.6%); for the 3–7 year age group, HRV again showed the highest rate (37.5%, 95% CI: 34.7–40.3%), followed by IFVA (35.4%, 95% CI: 32.7–38.2%); finally, in the 7–14 year age group, HRV maintained the highest detection rate (33.2%, 95% CI: 30.0–36.6%), followed by IFVA (29.6%, 95% CI: 26.5–32.8%). Complementing Table 3; Fig. 2 displays the age distribution of total positive cases for each pathogen, illustrating how cases are distributed across age categories. The analysis reveals that the majority of positive cases for IFVA, IFVB, HRV, and ADV were clustered in the 3–7-year age group. In contrast, MP infections showed a distinct pattern, being predominantly identified in older children aged 7–14 years, which constituted 70.7% (95% CI: 64.1–76.8%; 145/205) of its total positive cases. Additionally, the elevated relative frequency of RSV cases in infants (0–1 year) was clearly illustrated.

Types and composition ratios of co-infections in children

Dual pathogen infections represented the predominant type of co-infections, accounting for 86.0% (516/600). Among these, the combination of IFVA and HRV accounted for the highest proportion at 23.3% (140/600), followed by RSV and HRV at 13.8% (83/600) (Table 4). Triple pathogen infections comprised 13.0% (78/600) of the co-infections, with the RSV, HRV, and ADV combination being the most prevalent, representing 3.2% (19/600), followed closely by IFVA, HRV, and ADV at 2.8% (17/600) (Table 4). Infections involving four or more pathogens constituted 1% (6/600) of the co-infections, with the IFVA, RSV, HRV, and ADV combination being the most common, accounting for 0.3% (2/600) (Table 4). The distribution of Ct values for pathogens detected in co-infection cases is provided in Supplementary Table S1. The majority of co-infections involved pathogens with Ct values in the strong to moderate positive range (mean Ct 28.2–32.8), with only a small proportion of weak positive signals (Ct 38–40), supporting the clinical relevance of these findings.

Discussion

This study presents a descriptive epidemiological analysis of respiratory pathogens among 2364 non-hospitalized children. Beyond providing a region-specific epidemiological profile for a critical post-pandemic period, our findings on the predominance of specific pathogens and co-infection patterns offer a window into the dynamic interplay between respiratory pathogens and their young hosts. The prevalence of HRV and IFVA, along with their notable association in co-infections, likely reflects a combination of virus-intrinsic factors, such as evolutionary dynamics and transmission efficiency, and host-population factors, including immune status and contact patterns.

Our results indicated no significant statistical difference in the detection rate of respiratory pathogens between children of different sexes. This is consistent with previous research findings and suggests that sex is not a critical influencing factor in respiratory infections [19]. Moreover, the study revealed that 58% of the samples were positive for a single type of respiratory pathogen, while the incidence of co-infections was relatively low. This may be related to the epidemiological pattens observed during the study period and the children’s immune status [9]. Notably, among children with single infections, the detection rate of IFVA was highest, consistent with earlier findings [19]. This observation may be attributed to IFVA’s strong potential for interpersonal transmission. The high transmissibility and prevalence of IFVA are not merely due to chance but are driven by its well-documented evolutionary capacity. The significant variability of its surface antigens, hemagglutinin and neuraminidase, stems from mechanisms like antigenic drift and genome reassortment, which allow for rapid immune evasion and adaptation to the host population [6, 20–24]. This continuous evolution poses a persistent challenge for public health control. The exceptionally high detection rate of HRV (34.8%) can be attributed to its extraordinary genetic diversity and rapid evolutionary rate. HRV’s diversity is primarily generated through point mutations facilitated by an error-prone RNA polymerase, leading to a wide spectrum of serotypes that can collectively circulate widely and repeatedly infect individuals throughout life [25]. This intrinsic biological property ensures its constant presence in the community. Moreover, the elevated detection rate of HRV may also be associated with its propensity to cause co-infections with various respiratory pathogens, which could exacerbate clinical outcomes and complicate treatment [26–28]. Therefore, the potential impact of HRV should be carefully considered in the diagnosis and treatment of respiratory infections in children.

The study also found significant differences in the pathogen detection rates among different age groups. Particularly, children aged 3 to 7 years exhibited the highest rates of respiratory pathogen infections. This may be related to their social behaviors and the developmental stage of their immune system in this age range [29, 30]. Typically, children in this age range attend kindergarten or preschool, increasing their exposure to external environments and frequent group activities, thereby elevating their risk of pathogen exposure [31] and creating fertile ground for the spread of variants that can evade nascent immunity [32, 33]. The detection rate of RSV in children aged 0 to less than 1 year reaches as high as 30.6%, significantly higher than in other age groups [19], which is consistent with existing literature [34, 35]. It is worth noting that the ability of RSV to persistently infect infant populations is closely linked to the genetic evolution of its surface proteins, particularly the G protein (attachment protein) and F protein (fusion protein), where antigenic site mutations enhance its potential for epidemic spread and immune escape [36, 37].

Additionally, our results showed that co-infections accounted for a significant proportion of respiratory infections in children. Interestingly, dual pathogen infections served as the primary pattern, with combinations involving IFVA + HRV and RSV + HRV being particularly prevalent. The specific pathogen co-infection patterns observed in this study may be driven by complex underlying biological mechanisms. First, this convergence may be driven not only by shared environmental factors such as temperature and humidity [38, 39], which modulate transmission efficiency, but also by the ability of these viruses to exploit similar host niches. Second, the outcome of pathogen co-infections is highly dependent on the timing and kinetics of the interactions. Prior infection with HRV can potentially inhibit subsequent IFVA or RSV replication through the induction of innate immune responses, particularly the type I interferon pathway [40]. Conversely, primary infection with IFVA or RSV may alter the host cell environment, thereby shaping the replication dynamics of HRV [41, 42]. Third, unique immune response patterns are activated during co-infections, which may exacerbate respiratory inflammation and partly explain the higher severity rates observed in co-infected patients [43, 44]. Finally, co-infections can increase the risk of secondary bacterial infections, collectively leading to more complex clinical presentations [9, 45, 46].

The high frequency of IFVA-HRV and RSV-HRV co-infections warrants attention not only for their clinical impact—as they may lead to increased disease severity and complicate treatment [6]—but also for their evolutionary implications. Co-circulation provides a biological arena for direct viral interactions. This is particularly relevant for influenza, where co-infection can lead to genetic reassortment—a key mechanism for the emergence of novel pandemic strains [20, 21, 47]. Thus, these co-infection patterns represent a potential crucible for viral evolution.

The overall co-infection rate of 25.4% (600/2364) observed in our study is notably higher than the 10–20% range reported in some regional and international studies [48, 49]. This discrepancy may be attributed to a confluence of factors specific to our investigation. Firstly, the high sensitivity of the multiplex PCR methodology employed here significantly enhances the detection of multiple pathogens, including those present at low pathogen loads, thereby increasing the identified prevalence of co-infections compared to studies using less sensitive techniques [50, 51]. Secondly, the temporal context of our study—conducted during the autumn and winter of 2023—coincided with a period of intense respiratory pathogen circulation in Xinjiang following the relaxation of COVID-19 measures. This likely created an environment of high pathogen exposure, elevating the risk of concurrent infections with multiple pathogens. Finally, the composition of our cohort is a crucial factor. By focusing on pediatric outpatients and emergency department visitors presenting with respiratory symptoms, our study enrolled a high-risk population that is inherently more susceptible to severe disease and complex infection patterns, including co-infections, compared to children in community-based studies.

Our findings, set against the evolving landscape of respiratory disease prevention, have several implications. Our data, collected during the autumn and winter of 2023, provide a critical epidemiological baseline for the burden of RSV among children in Xinjiang before the widespread implementation of next-generation preventive strategies. The recent approval of long-acting monoclonal antibodies (e.g., nirsevimab) and maternal RSV vaccines (e.g., RSVpreF) heralds a fundamental shift in preventing severe RSV disease in infants [52–54]. Consequently, this study provides a crucial reference for future evaluations of the public health impact of these innovative interventions. Ongoing surveillance will be crucial to monitor changes in the relative proportion of RSV and the evolution of pathogen co-infection patterns in the context of shifting population immunity.

Interpretation of MP detection warrants careful consideration. While our multiplex PCR assay detected MP in 8.7% of samples, with a clear predominance in children aged 7–14 years (70.7% of all MP-positive cases), it is important to acknowledge that PCR positivity from upper respiratory tract specimens does not definitively distinguish between active infection and asymptomatic carriage. Previous studies have documented that MP nucleic acid positivity may reflect colonization rather than true infection, particularly in otherwise healthy children [55–57]. Due to the retrospective, cross-sectional design of our study, we did not systematically collect clinical severity scores, radiographic findings, or paired serological data (e.g., MP-specific IgM). Therefore, while the age-specific pattern we observed aligns with the known epidemiology of MP as a common pathogen in school-aged populations, the clinical significance of these positive results cannot be fully ascertained. Future prospective studies integrating PCR, serology, and standardized clinical outcome measures are needed to more accurately characterize the true burden of symptomatic MP infection in children.

Despite the valuable insights generated, this study has several limitations. Methodologically, its single-center, retrospective design may limit generalizability and introduce selection bias, while the four-month study period does not capture full seasonal trends. Furthermore, the multiplex PCR assay, although sensitive, did not provide genotypic or subtypic data, preventing genomic epidemiology analysis. In terms of data interpretation, the lack of information on socioeconomic confounders (e.g., household crowding) hinders causal inference regarding age-specific patterns. Similarly, the absence of standardized clinical data precludes a robust analysis of disease severity. Finally, the age groups used, while locally relevant, may limit international comparability. Future studies should adopt multi-center prospective designs, incorporate genomic sequencing, and systematically collect data on clinical parameters and key confounders.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study presents a comprehensive analysis of respiratory pathogen infections among non-hospitalized children in Xinjiang, China. Our findings delineate a distinct distribution profile, characterized by the predominance of HRV and IFVA, a substantial burden of specific co-infections, and clear age-specific susceptibility, particularly among children aged 3–7 years. This landscape is not static but is shaped by the continuous evolution of pathogens and their interactions with the human host. While the single-center nature of our study necessitates caution in extrapolating these findings to broader national policies, they provide compelling evidence for public health action within Xinjiang. We recommend enhancing preventive measures for high-risk groups, including promoting age-appropriate influenza vaccination and public health campaigns focused on personal hygiene. Finally, moving beyond the ‘what’ and ‘who’ described here, future research integrating genomic surveillance is essential to unravel the ‘why’—the genetic determinants that drive the dynamic epidemiology of respiratory pathogens in this region.
