MRI appearance of a hemorrhagic external hemorrhoid at the anal verge with concomitant low anal fistula: A case report
Authors: Xiuxiang Liu, Weiwei Cao, Xiaojing An, Hua Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExternal hemorrhoids are commonly diagnosed clinically, and their MRI appearance is not routinely emphasized. We report a 69-year-old man who presented with a firm perianal nodule for more than 10 months and worsening anal pain for 10 days. Clinical examination suggested mixed hemorrhoids and a low anal fistula. MRI demonstrated a well-circumscribed nodular lesion at the anal verge with marked central hyperintensity on T1-weighted imaging, a peripheral low-signal rim, iso- to mildly hyperintense signal on T2-weighted imaging, no convincing restricted diffusion, and peripheral ring-like enhancement with central nonenhancement. Maximum-intensity-projection images showed continuity of the enhancing wall with the anal verge and anal mucosal margin. MRI also demonstrated a separate low anal fistula, allowing the fistulous tract and the hemorrhagic anal-margin lesion to be assessed as distinct abnormalities before surgery. Surgery revealed a dark-brown blood clot within the external hemorrhoidal lesion, and histopathology confirmed hemorrhoidal change and fistulous change in the respective specimens. In this individual case, the combination of intrinsic T1 hyperintensity, absence of convincing diffusion restriction, peripheral enhancement with central nonenhancement, and an anal-verge location was concordant with hemorrhagic hemorrhoidal change. These observations should be regarded as case-specific imaging clues rather than a validated diagnostic pattern, but they may be useful when considering the differential diagnosis of painful anal-margin lesions, particularly when anal fistula coexists.Keywords: External hemorrhoid, Hemorrhage, Magnetic resonance imaging, Anal fistula, Anal verge

Introduction

MRI has an established role in the assessment of anal canal and perianal disorders because of its excellent soft-tissue contrast, multiplanar capability, and ability to delineate fistulous tracts and associated complications [1,2]. Most MRI literature in this field focuses on anal fistulas, abscesses, inflammatory complications, and neoplastic disease, whereas hemorrhoidal disease is usually diagnosed clinically and is less frequently emphasized on cross-sectional imaging [1,3]. Consequently, the MRI appearance of complicated external hemorrhoids may be unfamiliar to radiologists.

This distinction may be clinically relevant because painful lesions at the anal verge can represent perianal abscess, fistula-related inflammatory change, thrombosed or hemorrhagic hemorrhoidal disease, or less commonly other benign or neoplastic lesions. Blood products and thrombus can alter MRI signal intensity and may make a hemorrhoidal lesion more conspicuous. We report a hemorrhagic external hemorrhoid at the anal verge with concomitant low anal fistula, with imaging findings correlated with operative and histopathological results. The purpose of this report is not to propose a new diagnostic sign, but to illustrate case-specific MRI features and the contribution of MRI to separating 2 coexisting anorectal abnormalities before treatment.

Case report

A 69-year-old man was admitted on February 27, 2026, because of a firm perianal nodule that had been present for more than 10 months and anal pain that had markedly worsened during the preceding 10 days. The lesion initially appeared as a small tender lump near the anus and was followed by repeated episodes of swelling and discomfort. Physical examination revealed a dark-purple lesion in the external hemorrhoidal region. An external fistulous opening was identified at the 6 o’clock position, approximately 3 cm from the anal verge, with a palpable cord-like tract extending inward. Mixed hemorrhoids were also noted clinically.

MRI of the anal canal and perianal region was performed on a 3.0-T system using an 8-channel abdominal coil. No bowel preparation or anal intubation was used. The patient was scanned in the supine position. The protocol included sagittal images, oblique axial images perpendicular to the long axis of the anal canal, and oblique coronal images parallel to the anal canal. Coronal T2-weighted imaging parameters were as follows: repetition time, 3988 ms; echo time, 101.4 ms; flip angle, 90°; slice thickness/gap, 3.0/1.0 mm; number of slices, 20; field of view, 288 × 412 mm; and number of excitations, 4. Fat-suppressed T2-weighted imaging was not obtained. Diffusion-weighted imaging was acquired with a b value of 800 s/mm². Dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI consisted of 1 precontrast phase and 6 postcontrast phases, with a temporal resolution of 31 seconds. Gadoteridol was administered intravenously at 0.1 mmol/kg through an 18-gauge catheter by automated injector, followed by a 15-mL saline flush at 2 mL/s.

MRI demonstrated a well-circumscribed nodular lesion at the anal verge, corresponding to the expected location of an external hemorrhoid. On T1-weighted imaging, the lesion showed marked central hyperintensity with a peripheral low-signal rim. On T2-weighted imaging, it was iso- to mildly hyperintense. Diffusion-weighted imaging and apparent diffusion coefficient maps showed no convincing restricted diffusion. After contrast administration, the lesion demonstrated prominent peripheral ring-like enhancement, while the central portion remained nonenhancing. In the context of the intrinsic T1 hyperintensity, these findings favored hemorrhagic or thrombotic contents over a simple pus-filled cavity, although they are not specific in isolation (Fig. 1).

Postprocessed maximum-intensity-projection images showed continuity between the enhancing peripheral wall and the anal verge/anal mucosal margin. In this case, this appearance supported a hemorrhoidal vascular relationship rather than a separate abscess cavity, but it was interpreted as a descriptive observation rather than a validated diagnostic sign. MRI also demonstrated a coexisting low anal fistula as a separate abnormality. The fistula was interpreted together with the clinically visible external opening and was treated surgically the following day. Because fat-suppressed T2-weighted imaging was not obtained and detailed sequence-specific signal and enhancement characteristics of the tract were not systematically documented, a formal MRI activity grade of the fistula cannot be assigned retrospectively.

Surgery was performed on February 28, 2026, including fistulotomy, external stripping of mixed hemorrhoids, and excision of a hypertrophied anal papilla. Intraoperatively, a dark-brown blood clot was found within the external hemorrhoidal lesion, confirming hemorrhagic/thrombotic change. Histopathological examination, reported on March 4, 2026, demonstrated fistulous change in the fistula tract specimen and hemorrhoidal change in the excised hemorrhoidal tissue (Fig. 2). The imaging, operative, and histopathological findings therefore supported the diagnosis of a hemorrhagic external hemorrhoid coexisting with a low anal fistula and mixed hemorrhoids.

Discussion

This case shows that a hemorrhagic external hemorrhoid may be appreciable on MRI when blood products increase lesion conspicuity. The central T1 hyperintensity was concordant with the dark-brown blood clot found at surgery. Lack of convincing diffusion restriction and peripheral enhancement with central nonenhancement further favored hemorrhagic or thrombotic contents over a typical purulent cavity. These findings are observations from a single patient and should not be extrapolated as a stand-alone diagnostic pattern.

The main radiologic differential diagnoses include perianal abscess and thrombosed external hemorrhoid. A typical perianal abscess more often has fluid-like central signal intensity and restricted diffusion related to purulent material, although hemorrhage or highly proteinaceous contents may complicate this distinction [1,4]. A thrombosed external hemorrhoid is a particularly close differential and may overlap clinically and pathologically with hemorrhagic hemorrhoidal change; the MRI signal of thrombus can vary with the age and composition of blood products. In the present case, the operative blood clot and histopathological hemorrhoidal change supported a hemorrhagic/thrombotic hemorrhoidal process rather than an abscess. Accordingly, MRI should be interpreted together with the lesion location, clinical examination, and operative findings rather than used to make this distinction from signal characteristics alone.

Other anal-verge lesions may also mimic this appearance. Fistula-related inflammatory masses or collections are especially relevant when an anal fistula coexists, as in this patient. In general, an active fistulous tract is expected to show fluid-sensitive hyperintensity and enhancement, and an associated abscess may show diffusion restriction; these features are best evaluated on dedicated fistula MRI protocols [1,2,5]. In this case, the nodular lesion was centered at the anal verge in the expected external hemorrhoidal region and showed central intrinsic T1 hyperintensity with nonenhancement, making a fistula-related collection less likely. Benign anal-margin masses and neoplasms may show more solid internal enhancement, infiltrative margins, or other tissue-specific features [3].

The maximum-intensity-projection appearance provided an additional case-specific clue. Continuity of the enhancing wall with the anal verge and anal mucosal margin suggested a relationship to hemorrhoidal vascular tissue. Because this observation has not been validated as a diagnostic sign, it should be regarded only as ancillary information and not as a criterion for diagnosis.

The principal contribution of MRI to patient care in this case was the preoperative separation of 2 coexisting abnormalities: the low anal fistula and the hemorrhagic anal-margin lesion. Without this distinction, the painful nodule could have been attributed entirely to fistula-related infection. MRI characterized the nodular lesion as nonpurulent in appearance, demonstrated the fistula as a separate process, and provided an anatomic framework for surgical management. This practical role is consistent with the established value of MRI for defining fistula anatomy and associated perianal disease [1,2,5].

This report has several limitations. First, it describes a single patient, so the observed MRI features cannot be generalized or considered diagnostic. Second, although histopathology confirmed hemorrhoidal change, detailed microscopic correlation with each MRI signal characteristic was not available. Third, fat-suppressed T2-weighted imaging was not performed, and detailed sequence-specific characteristics of the fistulous tract were not systematically recorded; therefore, fistula activity cannot be graded retrospectively. Finally, the maximum-intensity-projection continuity with the anal verge is a descriptive finding that requires confirmation in additional cases. The teaching value of this report is therefore limited to recognizing a possible hemorrhagic hemorrhoidal appearance and considering it within the differential diagnosis of a painful anal-verge lesion.

Conclusion

In this single case, a hemorrhagic external hemorrhoid was appreciable on MRI as an anal-verge nodule with marked intrinsic T1 hyperintensity, no convincing restricted diffusion, and peripheral enhancement with central nonenhancement. Continuity of the enhancing wall with the anal verge on maximum-intensity-projection images was an additional descriptive observation. These findings are not validated diagnostic criteria, but, when interpreted with clinical examination and fistula assessment, they may help radiologists consider hemorrhagic or thrombosed hemorrhoidal disease in the differential diagnosis of painful anal-margin lesions and avoid attributing all abnormalities to a coexisting anal fistula.
