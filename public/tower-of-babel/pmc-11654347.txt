Barriers and facilitators in the acquisition of diabetes knowledge among tertiary-care nurses in central and southern Malawi: an exploratory-descriptive qualitative study
Authors: McDonald William Nyalapa, Belinda Thandizo Gombachika, Rodwell Gundo, Lignet Chepuka
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNumerous studies report low diabetes knowledge among nurses in sub-Saharan Africa. However, little is known about the factors that hinder and promote their acquisition of knowledge on diabetes mellitus. Understanding these factors is a pivotal step towards ensuring that nurses are knowledgeable and competent in the provision of care and education for patients with diabetes mellitus.MethodsThe study employed an exploratory-descriptive qualitative (EDQ) approach. Participants were identified from two tertiary hospitals. Twenty-six participants were purposively selected from these facilities. Data were collected through focus group discussions and analysis was done using Braun and Clarke’s approach to thematic analysis.ResultsThe following themes relating to barriers and facilitators in the nurses’ acquisition of diabetes knowledge emerged from the study: (a) individual hindrances; (b) organizational drawbacks; (c) personal ways of knowing; (d) organizational factors enhancing knowledge; and (e) suggestions for improving the nurses’ knowledge.ConclusionsThe study brings to light various challenges and opportunities in the acquisition of diabetes knowledge among tertiary-care nurses in Malawi. Given our study results, we believe that cost-effective measures can be utilised to address the barriers to the acquisition of diabetes knowledge among nurses. This is a crucial step towards ensuring that nurses are knowledgeable and competent in the provision of care for patients with diabetes in low-resource countries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-12081-x.Keywords: Diabetes, Nurses, Knowledge, Factors, Diabetes care, Malawi

Background

Diabetes mellitus (hereon referred to as diabetes) is one of the most rapidly growing global health emergencies of the twenty-first century with a prevalence of 537 million and a projected prevalence of 783 million by 2045 if no prompt action is taken [1]. The majority of people living with diabetes are in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [2]. In sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), the prevalence of diabetes has escalated rapidly [3], and is currently between 3 and 10% with global projections suggesting that a record-breaking increase will take place in this region [4, 5]. In addition, the region has the highest proportion of undiagnosed diabetes; three out of five people who have diabetes are not aware that they have it [6]. Diabetes is also projected to affect 40.7 million people in SSA by 2045 from 15.9 million in 2017 [7]. Despite this increased disease burden, resources for healthcare are limited [8].

Malawi has a diabetes prevalence of 6.5% and it is acknowledged that diabetes is a steadily growing noncommunicable disease (NCD) alongside rising rates of obesity, physical inactivity, urbanisation and nutritional transitions [9]. Coupled to that, Malawi is among other countries in SSA that are hardest hit by HIV and AIDS [10]. As such, the health systems are characterised by a focus on the acute management of infectious diseases and are not ready to effectively respond to the complex care demands of diabetes [11, 12]. The complex and challenging nature of diabetes also requires healthcare workers, such as nurses, to have the capacity to provide optimal levels of care for the patients [13, 14].

Evidence demonstrates that nurses have an indispensable role to play in diabetes care and have been described to be at the heart of diabetes care [15–17]. They have a major effect when supporting patients with self-management of their condition particularly using the proactive care management model and decision making support approaches [18–22]. Numerous studies outside SSA [16, 23–27] and within Africa [28–35] report low knowledge levels of diabetes and diabetes care among nurses. However, the factors that hinder and promote the nurses’ acquisition of diabetes knowledge are not well established. Therefore, this study was set off to explore the barriers and facilitators in the acquisition of diabetes knowledge among tertiary-care nurses in central and southern Malawi.

Methods

Research approach and design

This study employed an exploratory-descriptive qualitative (EDQ) approach to study perceptions of tertiary-care nurses on factors that promote and hinder acquisition of diabetes knowledge. This approach falls within the premises of naturalistic inquiry [36]. The EDQ approach aims at examining a phenomenon of interest, gaining insights and informing practice [37]. While some authors argue that this approach claims no particular methodologic roots or theoretical underpinnings [36], others reinforce the assertion that the approach is new [37] yet it provides explorative researchers with a “textured, toned and hued” approach that is almost impossible with either a descriptive or an exploratory design [38]. The approach was considered appropriate for the study because it is best suited for exploring perceptions of participants on a particular phenomenon within healthcare settings [39].

Study setting

The study was conducted at two tertiary hospitals, Kamuzu Central Hospital (KCH) and Queen Elizabeth Central Hospital (QECH). These are two of the largest tertiary hospitals in Malawi offering specialist services at regional level and acting as referral centres for district hospitals within their regions [40]. KCH serves a population of 7,523,340 in central Malawi; QECH serves a population of 7,750,629 in southern Malawi [41]. The hospitals are under the Malawi Ministry of Health and Population (MOHP), and have a wide range of subspecialties, such as medicine, surgery, nephrology, neurology, oncology, urology and obstetrics/gynaecology. These facilities were selected because they provide specialised care for patients with NCDs including diabetes and its complications, in addition to the outpatient diabetes clinics which are run weekly [42]. Therefore, most nurses in these facilities are often directly involved in the provision of care for patients with type 1 diabetes as well as those with type 2 diabetes.

Sampling

The study drew its participants from the population of Malawian qualified nurses1 working at the two tertiary hospitals. The population consisted of both registered nurses2 and nurse-midwife technicians3 working in adult care departments. Nurses in these departments care for patients with diabetes and, therefore, they are expected to be knowledgeable about various aspects of diabetes and its care. This aligns well with the guidelines for the conduct of EDQ research which encourages researchers to obtain participants who are able to provide information required to address the aims of the study [38]. A total of 26 nurses were purposively selected using the following inclusion criteria: (a) nurses working in adult care departments, such as outpatient departments (OPD), short stay wards, critical care units, adult medical and surgical wards; (b) having a minimum of six months practical nursing experience – a period considered as a minimum for familiarization with diabetes knowledge and its associated factors; (c) those in frontline care roles i.e. responsible for and/or involved in providing care to patients with diabetes; and (d) willing to participate in the study. Purposive sampling was utilised in this study because it enables the selection of individuals based on their particular knowledge of a phenomenon for the purpose of sharing that knowledge [43].

Data collection instrument

Data were collected using a semi-structured focus group discussion (FGD) guide. The guide was developed by the researcher (MN) based on the study objectives (see Supplementary Material). The semi-structured nature of the guide allowed the researcher to formulate a written topic guide, which consists of areas or questions to be covered during each FGD [36]. The guide comprised four main sections. Section A was about the sociodemographic information of the research participants and an ice breaker. Sociodemographic information was collected to establish a rapport with the participants in order to collect rich data from them. The ice breaker was intended to make the participants at ease and encourage them to express themselves in the subsequent sections. Section B focused on the barriers to the nurses’ acquisition of diabetes knowledge. The essence of this section was to gain an understanding of the factors that hindered their acquisition of diabetes knowledge. Section C was about the facilitators in the nurses’ acquisition of diabetes knowledge. This section was intended to explore the factors that promoted their acquisition of diabetes knowledge. Lastly, section D was intended to provide an opportunity for the participants to make suggestions on ways in which the acquisition of diabetes could be enhanced among tertiary-care nurses. Probes were based on the participants’ responses. The semi-structured FGD guide was developed in English because it is a medium of instruction in nursing training. However, translations were made whenever necessary during the discussions to facilitate comprehension.

Piloting of the data collection tool

The FGD guide was piloted with six purposefully selected postgraduate nursing students from Kamuzu University of Health Sciences (KUHeS) in Malawi. Similar to pretesting in quantitative research, piloting in qualitative research involves simulating the FGD process on a small scale to identify practical problems relating to data collection instruments, sessions and methodology [44]. MN facilitated the discussion and a research assistant (RA) took notes, given the loose and free-flowing dialogue in FGD often challenging to direct, observe and write at the same time. Observations and corrections were made relating to the structure, flow or order, clarity and appropriateness of the questions as recommended by Polit and Beck [36].

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the College of Medicine Research and Ethics Committee (COMREC) under KUHeS in Malawi (reference number P.09/22/3766). Before participation, detailed information about the study was provided to all the participants. Subsequently, written informed consent was sought from each study participant. Each participant participated voluntarily with an option to withdraw at any point of the study. Participants were assigned numbers or codes –rather than their names –to ensure anonymity. When data analysis was completed, the focus group transcripts were stored according to the COMREC’s data storage procedures. According to Good Clinical Practice (GCP) Guidelines [45], participants are entitled to reimbursements for expenses incurred as a result of their participation in a study. In this study, the reimbursements included transport costs because the FGD took place in the clinical setting, and compensation for the time lost. Participants were also served refreshments and snacks as appreciation for their availability.

Recruitment process

Permission to conduct the study was sought from the Director of each hospital prior to submission of the research protocol to COMREC. Recruitment commenced once ethical approval was granted by COMREC. Firstly, the researcher, with the help of the RA, pasted posters on the notice boards and nurses’ stations in the study settings inviting all eligible nurses willing to participate in the study to contact the researcher through the indicated phone numbers. Secondly, the researcher approached gate keepers, particularly Matrons of the eligible departments and units, to help with recruitment. Each Matron was briefed on the ethical conduct of the study with emphasis on voluntary participation. This was done to ensure protection of the prospective participants from coercion and undue influence from their Matrons. The researcher also took advantage of nurses’ meetings or morning reports to talk about the research and invite all eligible nurses to participate voluntarily. In addition, the quantitative phase of the larger study included an option for participants to indicate freely whether they were willing to participate in the FGD and how they wished to be contacted. All participants who expressed willingness to participate in the FGD indicated their contact numbers. The researcher made necessary arrangements to privately meet the prospective participants to confirm their eligibility and voluntary participation.

Data collection

Data were collected through FGDs by MN from June to September 2023. Two FGDs were conducted with 6–10 participants per group at each tertiary hospital. The date and venue for the FGD were agreed upon by the participants and the researcher. It is recommended that the date and venues for the interviews or discussions be convenient to participants to reduce the researcher-participant power imbalances [36, 37]. Participants were given the freedom to express themselves in a language they were comfortable with, either English or Chichewa, a local language for Malawians. Each discussion lasted between an hour and an hour and-a-half. The description of the FGDs is as follows: firstly, upon entering the discussion room, each participant was welcomed by MN and a RA –a RN who helped with recruitment and note taking at each study site. Subsequently, a wide range of current affairs, such as the weather, were informally discussed with the participants to establish a rapport and make them feel comfortable. Then MN explained the details and the scope of the study to the participants and asked if they needed any clarification on any aspect of the study. Verbal and written consent were obtained. Before the commencement of the interview, the facilitator showed the participants the audio recorder and demonstrated the practical mechanics of using it. Verbal and written consent were also sought from the participants on the use of the recorder. MN started by collecting demographic information of the participants. Literature demonstrates that the collection of quantitative materials, such as demographic information, supports EDQ studies [37]. MN then presented an “ice breaker” to stimulate the participants to start speaking by asking them to react to a scenario in which a nurse had given a wrong dose of insulin. Subsequently, the facilitator introduced questions from the guide and used probes to seek clarification and stimulate discussions. Generally, the FGD explored what the participants perceived as barriers and facilitators in diabetes knowledge acquisition and provided an opportunity for them to suggest strategies for enhancing diabetes knowledge their facilities. The FGD was completed when every group member had finished verbalizing their thoughts or opinions and they had nothing to discuss further. Data saturation was achieved by the end of the third FGD because the participants were free to share their perceptions, thoughts and opinions. However, another FGD was conducted to confirm data saturation. The key feature of data saturation lies with the qualitative researcher’s ability to look for repetition and confirmation of previously collected data [43].

Data analysis

Each recorded FGD was transcribed verbatim by MN. All the discussions were translated into English wherever necessary. A language expert was also consulted to verify the transcription and translation of the recorded discussions. Data were analysed using thematic analysis as proposed by Braun and Clarke [46]. Thematic analysis is a method of identifying, analyzing and reporting patterns or themes within qualitative data [46]. Firstly, the transcribed data were read repeatedly by MN to familiarize himself with the data. This process is known as “getting immersed in or dwelling with the data” and is vital for understanding what the data convey [43]. Subsequently, a line-by-line scrutiny or analysis of the transcribed data was conducted to make sense of the data. Manual colour coding was done in Microsoft Word to signify potential patterns in the sentences. MN identified and extracted phrases or direct participant quotes from the data to a matrix. Codes were grouped into potential thematic categories and were frequently changed when a more exhaustive description emerged. MN was responsible for sharing the data including the potential themes with the coauthors (BG, RG and LC) to scrutinize them and ensure that they accurately reflected the data that was collected. The coauthors, who are experienced qualitative researchers, provided feedback multiple times on the identified themes and initiated relevant discussions in order to reach a consensus. This ensured consistency in the data analysis process. Although this was a predominantly inductive process, the researchers also handled the data in a more interpretive way in order to capture the essential meaning of the data, explain phenomena and address the central research aims. Data analysis was done manually.

Trustworthiness of data

The study was guided by a framework for ensuring and maintaining rigor in qualitative research proposed by Guba [47] and Guba and Lincoln [48] which has the following components: credibility, dependability, confirmability and transferability. Firstly, the researcher ensured credibility of the findings by member checking and peer debriefing. Member checking was achieved by probing further to seek clarification during the discussions. Peer debriefing was done among the research team following data collection and during data analysis and manuscript preparation. The use of two hospitals also ensured credibility through space triangulation. Secondly, the study ensured dependability by documenting all procedures undertaken in the study including research methods, detailed data collection and analysis. Thirdly, to ensure confirmability, the researcher had an audit trail, which is a recording of all activities of the study including the decisions made over time for others to follow [43]. Lastly, to ensure transferability the study provided rich descriptions of the methodology, participants and their contexts and findings to enable readers or users to consider applying them to other settings or groups.

Study findings

The study had a total of twenty-six (26) participants aged between 22 and 47. There were four males and twenty-two (22) females. The majority of participants (14) were from surgical ward; 8 were from medical ward; and the remaining 4 were from oncology, orthopedic, burns and ear, nose and throat (ENT) departments. Their clinical experience ranged from 7 months to 26 years. Twelve (12) participants were Bachelor’s degree holders; 12 participants were Diploma holders; and 2 participants were Master’s degree holders. The estimated number of patients with diabetes the participants cared for ranged from 1 to at least 15 patients per day (see Table 1).

Participant characteristics

Data analysis generated the following main themes related to the barriers and facilitators in the acquisition of diabetes knowledge among the nurses: (a) individual hindrances; (b) organizational drawbacks; (c) personal ways of knowing; (d) organizational factors enhancing knowledge; and (e) suggestions for improving the nurses’ knowledge (see Fig. 1). The following section describes the themes and their corresponding participant quotes.

Coding tree

Theme 1: individual hindrances

Participants in this study identified various factors that hindered their acquisition of diabetes knowledge at an individual level. For instance, some participants mentioned lack of interest to gain new knowledge relating to diabetes. One of the participants reported:

“I may also say that it’s because I don’t have the interest to read to increase my diabetes knowledge. I use the knowledge which I acquired in school” (Participant #1 FG2 09).

In addition, participants reported that they had felt too tired to study at home because of family responsibilities. For instance, they assumed the role of a mother after knocking off from work and had to help their children with school work. One participant narrated:

“You leave this place as a nurse and you assume a new role at home as a mother. You start helping kids with their assignments. When you are done with that you are very tired and you are off to sleep…” (Participant #4 FG4 09).

This demonstrates that participants had multiple roles to play, for example at home and, therefore, they did not have the time to update their diabetes knowledge, apart from their lack of interest to study as individuals.

Theme 2: organizational drawbacks

The study uncovered several factors relating to the organization that negatively affected the nurses’ acquisition of diabetes knowledge. For instance, most participants identified work overload as a factor that contributed to their provision of fragmented care and significantly affected their acquisition of diabetes knowledge. Some participants shared their thoughts and experiences as follows:

“To my thinking, this thing of work overload contributes a lot...the only challenge is the work overload…” (Participant # 4 FG3 09).

“Sometimes you are in a ward and you are taking care of 50+ patients and you have patients with diabetes… You find yourself taking 6–8 hours without checking the blood glucose due to work overload. As such, that also affects the delivery of care. At the end of the shift you’re also too tired to reflect on your practice or check any updates on diabetes” (Participant #3 FG1 06).

Participants in this study also explained that they did not have the time to study diabetes. For those who managed to read from the internet, they usually forgot due to pressure of work.

“Moreover, we don’t have the time to go and read. We might read from Google but you find that you forget due to pressure of work” (Participant #3 FG4 09).

Some participants perceived the central hospital set-up as a limitation to their acquisition of knowledge on conditions outside their department. However, the majority of participants identified lack of institutional training as a key barrier to their diabetes knowledge acquisition or update.

“At the same time, the organization also contributes to our low knowledge because they do not organize trainings for us to update our old knowledge” (Participant #1 FG4 09).

Ineffective teamwork among clinical staff members also denied nurses a chance to learn and exchange diabetes knowledge. The collaboration challenges were largely between the nurses and the medical team.

“We leave the other things to doctors but we don’t work as a team. For example, you have a patient who has high blood glucose, you just address the routine problems and leave it there. We hand the patient over to the doctor instead of working together and sharing knowledge. I see this as a common problem for us nurses” (Participant #1 FG4 09).

Participants also identified several other factors related to the institution that hindered them from acquiring and updating their knowledge of diabetes. Some participants indicated that the institution did not support their acquisition of diabetes knowledge by its failure to make diabetes management guidelines accessible. Others explained that there was no recognition from the institution for their efforts in diabetes care activities, such as studying diabetes and providing health education to patients with diabetes. One participant also expressed concern over the institution’s way of handling nurses who wanted to further their education or specialize in diabetes care:

“If I want to go to school now to do diabetes and I inform the management, they will start “cutting my salary” and even removing me from payroll. I just stay back and say mmm let me continue with what I already have instead of going to school and updating yourself…” (Participant #2 FG1 06).

These findings demonstrate that most nurses perceived the work environment to be overwhelming due to increased patient numbers and care demands. As such, they had limited time to concentrate on gaining or enhancing their knowledge in diabetes. In addition, there were difficulties in teamwork, particularly between doctors and nurses. Lastly, the nurses reported that they did not feel supported by their institution to pursue further studies in diabetes care in addition to lack of trainings and information resources.

Theme 3: personal ways of knowing

Participants in this study reported personal ways that enabled them to increase their diabetes knowledge at an individual level. For example, some participants indicated the use of internet, particularly when the condition presented differently.

“When the condition presents in a different way, I just go on internet and google; by so doing, I find myself increasing my knowledge” (Participant #5 FG2 09).

Some participants who had relatives with diabetes reported that they gained knowledge when their relatives asked them questions relating to their condition. Subsequently, they looked for the information so as to provide their relatives with the correct answers and, by so doing, found themselves gaining new knowledge.

“Like in my case, I have a relative who is diabetic and so they always call me to ask some questions. By so doing, I find out –what does this mean? As such, the little knowledge that I have is through such people” (Participant #3 FG2 09).

This demonstrates that some participants engaged in searching for information when they were faced with an unfamiliar condition or when they were required to provide accurate information to relatives with diabetes.

Theme 4: organizational factors enhancing knowledge

Participants identified various factors that enhanced their diabetes knowledge within the institution or organization. Although most participants indicated that they lacked guidelines for the management of diabetes, some indicated that they used the accessible guidelines –sliding scale– to gain knowledge and manage patients.

“…we mostly do sliding scale because it is the most accessible guideline which pasted and we can explain clearly about the sliding scale” (Participant #5 FG2 09).

Similarly, despite reporting that teamwork was ineffective in the clinical setting, some participants indicated that they acquired some information about diabetes, such as medications and wound cleaning solutions from colleagues.

“We gain knowledge when the doctors prescribe medications for a patient but also when medical consultants come to review patients with diabetes” (Participant #2 FG3 09).

Other participants identified continuing professional development (CPD) sessions as one of the factors that promoted their acquisition of diabetes knowledge within the institution.

“I would say my diabetes knowledge level is not so bad; maybe about 60% or 70% or thereabout and my knowledge is usually acquired from CPDs…” (Participant #7 FG2 09).

This indicates that some participants had access to diabetes management guidelines, specifically sliding scale, although the majority of participants reported limited to no access to any clinical guidelines. Similarly, some nurses were able to learn from colleagues, although some participants indicated teamwork challenges. CPDs sessions, which mostly took place monthly, supported the acquisition of diabetes knowledge among some nurses.

Theme 5: suggestions for improving the nurses’ knowledge

Participants admitted that they had varying knowledge gaps in diabetes and its management. Therefore, they made suggestions for improving their diabetes knowledge. While some suggested individual-level actions, such as mindset change among themselves, others recommended institutional actions, such as promoting collaboration among clinical staff, providing more trainings, CPD and prerecorded diabetes sessions, specialisations and staff motivation. They also suggested mentorship and supervision under the existing quality assurance (QA) framework.

Participants also made innovative suggestions for improving the nurses’ acquisition of diabetes knowledge. These included prerecorded diabetes sessions, care clubs and simulation. One participant cited a simulation-based intervention called “drills” which is done in an emergency setting as one which could also be used to enhance the nurses’ knowledge in diabetes.

“I was thinking like in surgical department, sometimes we do what we call drills; drills are like simulation on how staff would respond to an emergency scenario. It helps people who had no interest in the condition to start having interest... So I think if such drills are introduced, they will prompt us to search for knowledge and also we will know our gaps better” (Participant #4 FG1 06).

Another participant indicated that care clubs could be an effective way of organizing nurses to discuss and learn about diabetes or even develop specific guidelines for diabetes management in a group.

“Alternatively, you may put us into clubs: some can be in a foot care club; some can be in diet club; others can be in a medication club and so on. We can choose representatives from each group or it can just all nurses in each group to develop our own guidelines –we can do it together or in groups, it’s okay” (Participant #7 FG2 09).

This demonstrates that participants acknowledged their knowledge deficits in diabetes and were willing to gain knowledge. They suggested ways of improving their diabetes knowledge levels through individual and institutional actions.

Discussion

This study explored the barriers and facilitators in the acquisition of diabetes knowledge among tertiary-care nurses in central and southern Malawi. This was an important topic, given the complex care demands of diabetes on nurses in low-resource settings [12]. The study identified and explored barriers and facilitators at personal and organizational levels. The study also generated suggestions for improving diabetes knowledge among nurses. Therefore, the findings uniquely illuminate existing gaps and opportunities in the acquisition of diabetes knowledge by nurses in a low-resource country.

Individual hindrances

Participants in this study identified individual-level barriers to their acquisition of diabetes knowledge. While some participants mentioned lack of interest to gain new knowledge, others identified family-related responsibilities as factors that hindered them from acquiring diabetes knowledge. These findings mirror those of previous studies on factors associated with diabetes knowledge among nurses in other countries. For instance, a study in Saudi Arabia [49] identified various barriers at an individual level, such as lack of interest to acquire new information, limited time to attend educational sessions and family commitments. Similarly, another study identified willingness to participate in diabetes education programs as a key factor influencing diabetes knowledge acquisition among non-endocrinology nurses in China [50]. The lack of interest to gain new knowledge relating to diabetes is a complex issue and, therefore, difficult to explain in this study, considering that these nurses were often expected to provide optimal diabetes care. A possible explanation for this finding is provided by Curtis et al. [51] who argues that the nurses’ gaps in diabetes knowledge may contribute to the development of negative attitudes towards diabetes care and this may also lead to poor nursing practice. However, it is worth noting that there could be various factors influencing the nurses’ attitudes towards diabetes care and knowledge. These findings still act as a reminder that individual level factors are vital and should not be overlooked when addressing barriers to diabetes knowledge acquisition among nurses.

Organizational drawbacks

The current study also found that various organizational factors negatively affected the nurses’ acquisition of diabetes knowledge. The participants repeatedly mentioned work overload and, in some instances, pressure of work as a key factor influencing the standard of care and ability to gain new diabetes knowledge. This is consistent with an earlier study by Kassean [52] which reported that nurses’ responsibilities were more than enough and had limited time in the clinical setting. This is also in agreement with a study which described the health system in Malawi as overstretched, under stress and characterized by an overwhelming nurse-patient ratio [53]. Work overload in Malawian public hospitals is attributed to a critical shortage of nurses, which is estimated at 65% [54]. The main reason for this alarming shortage is migration of nurses both within and outside Malawi in search of better working conditions, such as better treatment by seniors, recognition and better pay among others [55]. The findings suggest the need for health authorities to explore and implement strategies aimed at retaining nurses within hospital settings.

Another important aspect is that participants reported lack of institutional support towards their acquisition of diabetes knowledge in terms of training and access to reference materials or guidelines. An earlier study [49] also identified lack of training and limited access to relevant information on diabetes as a threat to capacity building among nurses and other clinical staff members. Similarly, other previous studies suggested that increasing institutional resources, even the seemingly simple printing of learning materials and supporting nurses through training could be effective in the acquisition of diabetes knowledge by nurses [27, 56]. The current study strengthens the call for capacity building among nurses through cost-effective interventions because it leads to enhanced health systems which can cope effectively with demands imposed by NCDs [57]. In addition, participants in this study identified lack of motivation and ineffective collaboration as barriers at an organizational level. Previous studies have described these factors as forming a vicious cycle, whereby lack of motivation and ineffective collaboration in the clinical setting result in poor and outdated knowledge, attitude and practices which, in turn, make the nurse demotivated and isolated [58].

Personal ways of knowing

In this study, participants identified various factors that enabled them to acquire diabetes knowledge at an individual level. Firstly, some participants reported that they gained diabetes knowledge through relatives who had diabetes. This confirms an earlier study which found that nurses who reported having a family member with diabetes had higher diabetes knowledge levels than those who reported no presence of diabetes among family members or friends [59]. Secondly, other participants reported using internet-based resources to access diabetes information. This supports previous studies which used web-based or online resources to enhance diabetes knowledge among nurses [60, 61]. However, there are concerns about the credibility of some internet-based resources coupled to the fact that most health workers including nurses lack an understanding of the required digital capability in the clinical setting [62]. Therefore, it is important to equip nurses with skills in searching for evidence-based health-related information. Training nurses in the effective use of technology has also shown to enhance their ability to provide high-quality care and is crucial for their continuous professional development [63].

Organizational factors enhancing knowledge

Participants in the current study reported that they used the available guidelines for managing diabetes to acquire diabetes knowledge. The most readily accessible guideline reported by the nurses was the sliding scale. Clinical practice guidelines are important in diabetes care because they enhance the quality of care by reducing practice inconsistencies and aligning practice with the latest scientific evidence [64]. However, caution must be exercised when using practice guidelines that are developed strictly on an international level because they may not provide the freedom for healthcare workers to manage patients based on their unique circumstances [65]. Therefore, this study reinforces the call for the development and implementation of practice guidelines that are tailored to the local context.

Some participants in this study indicated that they gained diabetes knowledge from colleagues in the clinical area, particularly medical consultants. This was mostly possible when they worked together and communicated effectively. Evidence suggests that effective inter- and intraprofessional communication and collaboration promote the exchange of diabetes knowledge [56]. Moreover, when nurses and other healthcare providers communicate and work together effectively, quality and safety of patient care are improved [66]. Therefore, the findings of this study suggest that effective interprofessional collaboration practice (ICP) should be promoted to enhance the transfer and exchange of diabetes knowledge at an organizational level.

The participating nurses also identified CPD sessions as a facilitator for their acquisition of diabetes knowledge. CPD refers to the purposeful and ideally ongoing education that healthcare workers undertake after completion of basic training to maintain core competencies and update their knowledge, skills and practices [67]. CPD activities may take different forms, such as traditional classroom learning sessions and independent online courses with the aim of empowering healthcare workers with the necessary knowledge and skills to effectively manage their patients. Literature indicates that CPD enables nurses to maintain their professional competence and increase self-esteem among other reasons [68]. Despite the wide usage in SSA, the robustness and impact of CPD programs are not well established [69]. Therefore, further work is required to establish this.

Suggestions for improving the nurses’ knowledge

One interesting finding is that participants suggested a number of practical strategies for improving the nurses’ diabetes knowledge. They identified the need for supervision and mentorship under the existing QA framework, in addition to mindset change, staff motivation, specialisations and prerecorded sessions. QA refers to setting standards and systematically measuring compliance with the set standards to improve performance through peer review, outcome measurement and management techniques [70]. During data collection, it was noted that both study settings were implementing QA activities in various categories, such as emergency care, reproductive health and infection prevention among others. Although evidence for the direct link between QA and diabetes knowledge is rare, literature shows that QA strategies have the potential for transforming diabetes care in low-resource settings [71].

Participants also suggested care clubs and simulation as innovative strategies for improving their diabetes knowledge. Clubs and simulation have been widely used in health care settings to address various practice-related issues. The concept of clubs has been popular among nurses and has taken various forms, such as journal clubs [72] and book clubs [73]. For example, the use of a journal club was demonstrated to be effective in bridging the gap between research and clinical practice among intensive care nurses by triggering dialogue among them and facilitating the incorporation of reading in their professional habits [72]. In addition, using journal club as a teaching strategy in nursing has been shown to contribute to skill development in professional discussion, research appraisal and applying research to clinical practice [74]. Similarly, simulation-based approaches have been demonstrated to be an effective capacity building strategy by helping students and nurses apply their critical thinking, clinical reasoning, problem-solving, decision-making and teamwork skills, alongside their theoretical knowledge and skills, to provide holistic care to patients [75].

Recommendations

Based on the findings of the current study, we recommend that barriers to the nurses’ acquisition of diabetes knowledge be tackled at both individual and organizational levels. Addressing the barriers experienced by the nurses is a crucial step towards ensuring that nurses are knowledgeable and competent in the provision of care and education for patients with diabetes [17]. Moreover, it aligns well with the international debate on the urgent need to enhance inpatient diabetes management [76].

Malawi envisions a comprehensive health system which will be able to provide a wide range of high-quality services to its population as stipulated in the Malawi Vision 2063 (MW 2063) [77]. However, shortage of material and competent human resources, imposed by economic challenges, continues to paralyse the Malawi health sector [53, 54] and, if left unaddressed, poses a significant threat to the realization of the MW 2063 targets. The Malawi MOHP also recognizes the enormous gap in the availability of resources for NCDs in Malawi and, therefore, recommends cost-effective but action-orientation strategies for the effective management of NCDs [42]. We believe that the cost-effective strategies uncovered by this study, such as care clubs and simulation, offer a ray of hope for the enhancement of diabetes knowledge among nurses in Malawi.

Strengths and limitations

This study falls within limited studies that have uncovered barriers and facilitators in the acquisition of diabetes knowledge among tertiary-care nurses in low-income countries. The versatility of the methodology employed by the study also enabled the study to step beyond the confines of observation and recording to examining, gaining insights and informing practice. The main limitation of this study is that it was conducted in two tertiary hospitals out of the four public tertiary facilities in Malawi due to time and resource constraints. As such, the barriers and facilitators in diabetes care may be unique to nurses in such facilities. There is a need to conduct the study in all tertiary health facilities in Malawi. Also, there is a need to explore further the cost-effective educational strategies, such as the care clubs and drills as this was beyond the scope of this study.

Conclusion

The study findings uncover a wide range of factors that hinder and promote the acquisition of diabetes knowledge among tertiary-care nurses in central and southern Malawi. The barriers and facilitators exist at both individual and organizational levels. The barriers need to be addressed urgently to ensure that nurses are knowledgeable and competent in the management and education of patients with diabetes. However, it is also important to note that the Malawi health system is characterized by a persistent shortage of resources. This situation poses an enormous threat to the realization of national targets, such as the MW 2063. Therefore, we reinforce the need to implement cost-effective strategies suggested by this study, such as diabetes care clubs and drills, for promoting the acquisition of diabetes among tertiary-care nurses in Malawi. Although we presume that these strategies are cost effective, researchers need to take a deeper dive into them. This will help to establish the perspectives of nurses regarding such initiatives and identify any potential issues surrounding their implementation.
