Effect of baby food supplement on the growth and metabolite of lactic acid bacteria
Authors: Daniel Imanuel Yaurentus, Yogiara, Diana Elizabeth Waturangi, Adi Yulandi, Raymond Rubianto Tjandrawinata
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChild growth during the first 1000 days is crucial for developing the immune system, particularly through the digestive system. However, in the early childhood phase, most children facing feeding issues, which leading to insufficient nutrient uptake. Thereby, the external supplementation may be essential to support their nutrition needs. Therefore, this study evaluated the potential of a baby food supplement (BFS) to promote the growth of beneficial lactic acid bacteria (LAB) and their metabolite production.ResultsThe food supplement did not significantly interfere with LAB growth. Instead, it significantly enhanced the antimicrobial activity of Lactobacillus acidophilus and Limosilactobacillus fermentum against Enterohemorrhagic Escherichia coli (EHEC) and Staphylococcus aureus, respectively. The addition of BFS also improved the adhesion capacity of the LAB used in this study to yeast cells. Moreover, gas chromatography‒mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis revealed greater amounts of bioactive compounds related to antimicrobial and immunomodulatory properties, such as lactic acid and trilinolein, in the supernatants with the addition of food supplements. Liquid chromatography-high resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS) analysis also revealed that there were more compounds in M3 than in M1 or only, which are associated with the immune system and neurocognitive development, such as choline, rapastinel, octopamine, aceglutamide, indoleacrylic acid, 1-aminocyclopropanecarboxylic acid (ACPC), and GABA.ConclusionsBaby food supplements are promising for improving the production of beneficial health metabolites of lactic acid bacteria used in this in vitro study, as they inhibit pathogens, promote competition, and produce many beneficial bioactive compounds. The limitation of this study is that it was performed in vitro, and further studies are needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04407-4.Keywords: Lactic acid bacteria (LAB), Baby food supplement (BFS), Bioactive compound, Child-feeding issue, Gut microbiota

Introduction

The development of the gut microbiome over 1000 days in a child is one of the most critical parts of the establishment of the immune system [1]. The formation of the gut microbiota early in life could have lasting health effects [2], and any disruption could lead to an increased risk of inflammation, neurological disorders, obesity, and autoimmune diseases [1]. Numerous studies have shown that gut microbiota play important roles in humans, such as producing metabolites and neurotransmitters like short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), γ-Amino Butyric Acid (GABA), norepinephrine, and serotonin [3, 4]. They also regulate immunity and autoimmunity [5], produce antimicrobial peptides [6], and maintain intestinal barrier integrity [7]. Gut microbiota colonization is a complex process influenced by multiple factors, including diet, feeding type, and mode of delivery [8]. Therefore, a child’s food intake during this crucial period will significantly influence the microbial shifts in their intestines and subsequently affect their growth.

Parental knowledge regarding food choices, nutritional value, and beneficial food sources is critical to overcoming this barrier [9]. In most cases, children with feeding issues may require supplemental nutrition, such as micronutrients, macronutrients, probiotics, and prebiotics, to meet their nutritional needs [10, 11].

Lactic acid bacteria (LAB), widely recognized as probiotics, have been extensively studied for their significant health benefits in both children and adults, primarily by supporting nutritional balance. These beneficial bacteria can compete with and inhibit pathogenic bacteria from attaching to intestinal epithelial cells through various mechanisms, including creating an acidic environment [12] and enhancing intestinal epithelial function through the production of mucin and inducing the expression of antimicrobial peptides [12, 13]. Furthermore, some LAB may stimulate the production of serum immunoglobulin A (IgA), which is important for intestinal immunity, and suppress the proliferation of T cells to minimize inflammation [14]. Consequently, developing strategies to increase the quantity of LAB and maximize their metabolite production is a highly promising area of research. Therefore, it’s essential to identify and optimize the nutritional requirements of LAB through media modifications that offer a cost-effective alternative to standardized media while maintaining comparable efficacy.

In this study, we aimed to determine the impact of a baby food supplement (BFS) in the form of sprinkled seasoning with ingredients including red beans, cheddar cheese, beef, and ferrous fumarate on the growth and metabolite production of several lactic acid bacteria and their relevance to human health.

Results

Lactic acid bacteria enumeration

Figure 1 shows the total colony counts of the three bacteria species across the different media. There was no significant difference in the count of Lactobacillus acidophilus (Lb. acidophilus) between M1 and M3. However, the total viable number of Lb. acidophilus in M2 was significantly lower than that in M1 and M3. In contrast, the count of Limosilactobacillus fermentum (Lmb. fermentum) was greater than that of Lb. acidophilus. The colony count for Lmb. fermentum was significantly greater in M2 compared to M1, while the number of colonies that grew in M3 did not significantly differ from those in M1 and M2. Finally, the count of Lacticaseibacillus casei (Lcb. casei) did not significantly differ among the three media treatments, even though its average colony count was greater than that of other two bacteria.

Liquid chromatography–high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS)

Further analysis via LC-HRMS revealed several bioactive compounds on two database references, natural products and metabolomics, as shown in supplementary Tables 1–6. According to supplementary Table 1 (natural products), the most abundant compounds produced by Lb. acidophilus in M1, M2, and M3 were 1-(2-morpholinophenyl)dihydro-1 H-pyrrole-2,5-dione (2.4 × 109), pipecolic acid (4.7 × 109), and pipecolic acid (2.6 × 109), respectively. Similarly, as shown in supplementary Table 2 (Metabolomics), the compounds with the highest area for Lb. acidophilus in M1, M2, and M3 were maculosin (2.4 × 109), pipecolic acid (4.7 × 109), and pipecolic acid (2.6 × 109), respectively. For Lcb. casei, supplementary 3 (natural products) indicates that betaine (1.5 × 109), trigonelline (1.3 × 109), and betaine (1.5 × 109) as the most abundant compounds in M1, M2, and M3, respectively. As shown in supplementary Table 4 (metabolomics), Lcb. casei also presented maculosin (2.6 × 109), trigonelline (1.3 × 109), and maculosin (2.4 × 109) as the most abundant compounds in M1, M2, and M3, respectively. As shown for Lmb. fermentum, supplementary Table 5 (natural products) shows that L-phenylalanine (6.2 × 109), pipecolic acid (4.4 × 109), and L-phenylalanine (6.8 × 109) were the most abundant compounds in M1, M2, and M3, respectively. The same results were obtained in supplementary Table 6 (metabolomics), where L-phenylalanine (6.2 × 109), pipecolic acid (4.4 × 109), and L-phenylalanine (6.8 × 109) were the most abundant compounds in M1, M2, and M3, respectively.

Discussion

This study compared the growth of lactic acid bacteria among various media (M1, M2, and M3) to observe whether the addition of BFS influences the growth of lactic acid bacteria (LAB), specifically the three species we used in this study. As shown in Fig. 1, the addition of BFS did not significantly interfere with the growth of Lcb. casei or Lb. acidophilus. The colony count of Lcb. casei was relatively consistent across all three media, whereas the total colony count of Lb. acidophilus in M2 was lower than the other media. Interestingly, the total colony count of Lmb. fermentum in M2 was greater than that in M1, while the count in M3 was between the values observed for M1 and M2. These findings suggest that while the addition of BFS may not increase the overall colony count, the ability of all LAB strains used in this study to reach nearly the same colony number in M2 (distilled water + 2% BFS) as in the standardized medium (MRSB) is noteworthy.

LAB have complex nutritional requirements, including amino acids, peptides, nucleotide bases, vitamins, minerals, fatty acids, and carbohydrates [63]. The baby food supplement used in this study primarily contains red beans (70.45%), which are rich in carbohydrates, fiber, protein, and minerals, such as ferrous, zinc, calcium, magnesium, phosphorus, potassium, and sodium [64]. Cheddar cheese, the second main component, also comprises many macro-, micro-, and functional nutrients, including proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals, bioactive peptides, polyphenols, conjugated linoleic acid, lactoferrin, γ-aminobutyric acid, and organic acids [65]. Furthermore, the addition of beef to the supplement has enriched the formulation, as beef is rich in protein, fat, iron, zinc, selenium, and vitamins (D, B12, B6, B3, B1, B2, and B5) [66]. With all these macro- and micronutrients, LAB could theoretically convert or metabolize these components, specifically sugars, through either the hetero- or homofermentative pathway. This process would result in the production of lactate, acetate, and CO2. A study in Taiwan also confirmed that red beans can be used as a substrate for Lactobacillus in fermentation beverages [67].

The alteration of the final product and growth capability of LAB may depend on multiple factors not be covered in this study, such as the molecular mechanism of fermentation in the cells [63, 68]. Notably, the colony morphology of all LAB in M2 appeared to be more transparent and smaller than that of colonies grown on normal media. These morphological changes and survival capabilities of bacteria may be caused by insufficient substrate concentrations, inefficient nutrient uptake, and a lack of essential trace elements [69, 70]. A study conducted in Argentina revealed lower growth of Lactobacillus strains when whey and buttermilk were used; perhaps after the addition of 0.3% yeast extract, the growth became similar to that observed on MRS media [71]. Another study also demonstrated that the growth of Lactobacillus in sweet potato medium (SPM) supplemented with peptone, yeast extract, and beef extract showed no significant difference compared to MRS [72]. These findings suggest that a minimum supplementation of certain trace components is critical for the optimal growth of lactic acid bacteria (LAB).

The findings from this study have significant implications for addressing pediatric nutritional deficiencies. During child development, parents aim to provide all the necessary nutrients for optimal growth. However, instances of insufficient food intake or “picky eating” can lead to nutritional shortfalls, a condition simulated by the minimum nutrient media (M2) in this study. The results show that even in this nutrient-deficient environment, the LAB strains could not only survive but also grow, albeit with some differences in morphology and total colony count. The fact that the baby food supplement (BFS) alone could support the growth of these LAB strains is particularly noteworthy. This suggests that the BFS can serve as a viable supplement, potentially for children who do not get sufficient nutrition from primary food sources. The powdered form of the BFS would also make it easier for children to consume and for their digestive systems to absorb.

In addition to assessing growth, probiotics should be able to adhere to and colonize the gastrointestinal (GI) tract to maximize their function [73]. Therefore, an agglutination assay was implemented to determine the binding ability of each LAB strain after growth in different media [74, 75]. The assay (Fig. 3) revealed that all LAB strains grown in M3 exhibited higher agglutination rates than those grown in the other media (M1 and M2). Furthermore, Lb. acidophilus in M3 showed a significant increase in agglutination rate compared to M1. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that the baby food supplementation enhanced the adherence of the LAB to yeast and, by extension, possibly to the GI tract. This enhanced adherence could lengthen the duration of LAB in the gut, allowing them to function at their maximum capacity.

The attachment of LAB to the gastrointestinal (GI) tract is a complex process affected by multiple factors, including bacterial species and strains, surface properties, and environmental changes [76]. The results obtained in this study may be associated with previously proposed mechanisms of interaction between LAB and the gut. For example, Lb. acidophilus has been reported to possess several cell surface factors for adherence, aggregation, barrier maintenance, and immunomodulation. These factor include mucus-binding protein (MUB) [77], fibronectin-binding protein (FbpA) [78], S-layer proteins (SlpA), and exopolysaccharides (EPS) [76, 79]. In Lmb. fermentum, adherence properties are mediated by the presence of 32-Mmubp protein [80], LAF_0673 [81], and CBP protein [82], which may contribute to mucin or mucus adhesion, protection from pathogens, and collagen binding, respectively [79]. The adherence properties of Lcb. casei are related to the LCABL_01820 protein [79] and sortase-dependent protein (SrtA) [83], which are responsible for adhesion to collagen and fibronectin, as well as increased mucus production, maintenance of barrier function, and immunomodulation, respectively.

As described previously, we observed an increasing pattern of agglutination rates for the LAB strains in M3. We assumed that the BFS may provide a more abundant sources for protein biosynthesis, allowing LAB to generate diverse proteins and polysaccharides that are essential for attachment [76, 79]. This suggests a possible upregulation in the production of several types of proteins, including those involved in adherence. However, further analysis is still needed to confirm targeted protein expression in response to these treatments. Other studies have reported that adhesion mechanisms can involve electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions of cell surface proteins, rather than the common mannose-specific binding protein [75]. Additionally, external factors during LAB growth, such as acid and bile stress, could alter the cell wall structure. For example, a low pH (acidic condition) can modify the fatty acid composition of Lcb. casei [84].

As the next part of the evaluation, we also assessed the ability of each supernatant produced by each LAB used in this study in various media to inhibit pathogens. As presented in Fig. 2, most of the Cell-free supernatants (CFS) secreted by all lactic acid bacteria (LAB) in MRSB + 2% BFS (M3) exhibited a greater inhibition zone against pathogenic bacteria than did the supernatants from M1. Nonetheless, a significant difference in inhibition between M1 and M3 was confirmed only for Lb. acidophilus against enteropathogenic E.coli (EPEC) and Lmb. fermentum against S. aureus. In this case, we assume that the antimicrobial activity was mainly influenced by the organic acids produced during fermentation, specifically lactic acid and acetic acid. These data are supported by the GC‒MS results, which revealed that organic acids such as acetic acid and lactic acid were the most abundant compounds produced in M1 and M3. Acetic acid is a short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) that has several effects on the gut microbiota. SCFAs are commonly present in nonionized forms that can diffuse through the bacterial membrane into the bacterial cytoplasm [85]. Lactic acid is also an organic acid, and although it is not classified as an SCFA, its antimicrobial mechanisms are largely similar. The secreted acid molecules are deprotonated upon entry into the cellular membranes of pathogenic bacteria, which shifts the intracellular pH. This leads to acidic stress, the destruction of hydrogen bonds, protein denaturation, and eventually apoptosis [86, 87]. This process create an unfavorable environment for microbial proliferation [88].

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) also play a pivotal role in maintaining intestinal and immune homeostasis inside the human body [89], especially in children. A previous study demonstrated that acetate can bind to GPR43 and stimulate K + efflux in epithelial cells, which activates NLRP3 and interleukin-18 (IL-18) production to promote epithelial integrity, repair, and homeostasis [90]. Lactate and SCFAs have also been reported to downregulate the proinflammatory response in intestinal, sentinel, and myeloid cells through toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling inhibition, thereby restoring gastrointestinal health [91]. Moreover, SCFAs inhibit the production of inflammatory cytokines, including interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), tumor necrosis factor- alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-8 (IL-8), while promoting the production of anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-10 (IL-10) and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), via suppression of the nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) pathway in intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) [92, 93].

As shown in Table 1, five major compounds were produced by LAB across the various media: ethyl isoallocholate, acetic acid, lactic acid, pyrrolo[1,2-a]pyrazine-1,4-dione, hexahydro, and trilinolein. A study revealed that ethyl isoallocholate, a steroidal derivative, had the highest cytotoxicity against A549 cancer cells by activating the caspase signaling pathway [60]. Ethyl isoallocholate has also been shown to reduce tumor growth, liver metastasis, and angiogenesis in zebrafish [60]. Another study reported that it may possess antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties [58, 59]. Acetic acid exhibits antibacterial, antifungal, antivirulence, and antibiofilm properties [16, 17]. It may have other functions, such as protecting against inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), induction of apoptosis in human colon cancer cell lines, and regulation of hormone secretion (GLP-1, PYY) and blood pressure [89]. On the other hand, lactic acid is known for its antimicrobial, food preservative, immunomodulatory, and flavor-enhancing properties [20]. The health effects of lactate have also been described, including the modulation of cortical neuron activity, anti-inflammatory effects, anti-lipolysis effects, and the allevation of IBD and cancer symptoms in mouse models of pancreatitis and hepatitis [89]. Pyrrolo[1,2-a]pyrazine-1,4-dione hexahydro, has been reported to exhibit antimicrobial and antioxidant activity against S. aureus infection, with a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 15 ± 0.172 mg L−1 and a minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) of 20 ± 0.072 mg L−1 [35]. A different study reported that this same compound, when extracted from Streptomyces sp. VITMK1, is nontoxic and able to scavenge free radicals, with a 2,2-difenil-1-pikrilhidrazil (DPPH) activity of 72.48 ± 0.32% at 500 µg/mL [34]. Trilinolein, the fifth major component, also has an anti-ischemic effect, where it may protect the myocardium against ischemic injury during ischemia and reperfusion [62]. Moreover, in vitro studies also revealed a gradual increase in antioxidant activity followed by an increasing concentration of trilinolein extracted from the herb Sanchi Panax notoginseng [62]. We also observed a unique compound that was produced only after the addition of BFS: 12-methyl-E, E-2,13-octadecadien-1-ol. This compound has been found to be antimicrobial, antifungal, and antioxidant [39, 40].

Further analysis via LC-HRMS revealed additional bioactive compounds, as detailed in Supplementary Tables 1–6. Based on the two database references, the compounds with the highest content produced by Lb. acidophilus across all media variations were pipecolic acid and 1-(2-morpholinophenyl)dihydro-1 H-pyrrole-2,5-dione. For Lcb. casei, the major compounds across all media variations were betaine, trigonelline, and maculosin. The most abundant compounds for Lmb. fermentum were L-phenylalanine and pipecolic acid. We also report several unique compounds that appear only in M3 produced by Lb. acidophilus (Supplementary Tables 1–2), such as rapastinel, glycyl-5,5-dimethyl-L-prolyl-L-glutamic acid, fenamic acid, oglufanide, indoleacrylic acid, dehydroalanine, pizotifen, puromycin, and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA). Each of these compounds is related mainly to cognitive enhancers [94]; neuroprotective, antiapoptotic [95]; anti-inflammatory [96]; immunomodulator [97]; promotes intestinal epithelial barrier function, mitigates inflammatory responses (anti-inflammatory) [98]; antimicrobial peptides [99]; anticancer, antitumor [100]; antibacterial [101]; and antihypertensive, antidepressant, anti-inflammatory, antidiabetic, and immunity enhancement effects [102].

In CFS of Lcb. casei grown in M3, we also found several compounds as shown in Supplementary Tables 3–4. These included gamma-acetylenic GABA, piperazin-2-carboxamide, choline alfoscerate, 1-Aminocyclopropanecarboxylic acid (ACPC), DL-Glutamine, aceglutamide, rapastinel, and vindoline. Each of these compounds is associated with, the inhibition of GABA transaminases and increases in GABA levels several-fold [103]; antidepressant and antimicrobial effects [104]; improvements in brain acetylcholine levels and cognitive performance [105]; the enhancement of cognitive processes; the inhibition of memory fading [106]; anti-inflammatory effects; the regulation of enterocyte proliferation; protection against apoptosis and cellular stress [107]; neuroprotective effects; improved motor and behavioral function [108]; and the use of cognitive enhancers [94]; and antioxidant and antihyperlipidemic effects [109].

For Lmb. fermentum, we also discovered several compounds that were present only in the supernatant following the addition of a food supplement (M3), as detailed in Supplementary Tables 5–6. These compounds included, L(-)-carnitine, gamma-acetylenic GABA, octopamine, hypaphorine, rapastinel, araliasaponin V, streptothricin E, and isonixin. These compounds also exhibit several properties, including antidiabetic, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticancer effects; improved neurocognitive and anticancer effects; the ability to abrogate neuroinflammation, apoptosis, astrogliosis, and oxidative stress [110]; the ability to inhibit GABA transaminase and increase GABA levels several-fold [103]; neurotransmitter, neuromodulator, and neurohormone [111]; antibacterial [112]; cognitive enhancer [94]; antioxidant, hepatoprotective, anti-gastric ulcer, and anti-inflammatory effects [113]; antibiotic [114]; and anti-inflammatory effects [115].

Interestingly, we observed a noticeable increase in the content of choline, a compound detected by LC-HRMS. Choline appeared only in the BFS-containing media (M2 for all bacteria and M3 for a few), and its concentration was quite high compared to other compounds. A deeper analysis revealed that this compound is strongly related to brain development, including supporting cholinergic neurotransmission and neurological and brain function development, especially in fetus, supporting long-term memory, and reducing cognitive decline [116].

Limitations

Our findings, derived from a controlled laboratory setting, may not fully translate to the complex microbial ecosystem of the human gut. Furthermore, this research was limited to only three specific LAB strains (Lactobacillus acidophilus ATCC 314, Limosilactobacillus fermentum ATCC 9338, and Lacticaseibacillus casei ATCC 393). Their metabolic interactions with the specific baby food supplement composition may therefore differ from those of other strains or the broader gut microbiota. We also recognize that our study focused on identifying potential beneficial compounds via GC-MS and LC-HRMS but did not include functional or cellular assays to directly validate their predicted biological roles. Therefore, future in vivo studies are essential to validate these findings and better understand the functional impact of these metabolites in a living system.

Conclusion

The growth of Lactobacillus was quite similar across the treatments, which suggests that baby food supplements did not interfere with the growth of LAB used in this study. The combination of the baby food supplement (BFS) with a standard medium also increased the inhibition zones against ETEC, EPEC, EHEC, B. cereus, and S. aureus. Moreover, there was a significant difference compared with the media control (M1). This antimicrobial effect, coupled with a higher ability of LAB to agglutinate and adhere in the presence of BFS, suggests that the supplement promotes the attachment of LAB to yeast, a factor that could potentially aid in gut colonization. Analysis via GC‒MS also revealed several major compounds in M3 that were either present at higher concentrations in BFS-containing media (M2 and M3) or appeared exclusively in these media. Among the major compounds, L-lactic acid and trilinolein, known to be associated with antimicrobial and immunomodulatory properties, were more abundant in M3 than in M1. Furthermore, our LC-HRMS analysis identified a range of compounds that, based on existing literature, are associated with the immune system and neurocognitive development. These include choline, choline alfoscerate, rapastinel, GABA, octopamine, aceglutamide, indoleacrylic acid, 1-aminocyclopropanecarboxylic acid (ACPC), etc. While these associations are exciting, we emphasize that they are speculative and based solely on the identity of the compounds, not on direct functional validation within this study. In summary, this study suggests that baby food supplements are a promising substrate for promoting LAB growth in vitro by providing abundant nutrient sources, aiding in pathogen inhibition, and supporting the production of various bioactive compounds. These findings lay the groundwork for future research, but we stress the need for further in vivo studies to validate the functional impact of these metabolites in a living system.

Materials and methods

This research project was conducted under the Faculty of Biotechnology, Atma Jaya Catholic University of Indonesia, from January to June 2024.

Preparation of treatment culture media and reagents

De Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) media (Oxoid Ltd., Hampshire, UK) supplemented with 0.3% CaCO3 (Merck, Sigma‒Aldrich, Burlington, USA) were used as the culture refreshment media for lactic acid bacteria (LAB) and constituted the first treatment media, referred to as “M1”. The second treatment medium, “M2”, was prepared by combining distilled water with 2% baby food supplement (BFS) “LJ”. The third treatment medium, “M3”, was prepared by combining MRS medium with 0.3% CaCO3 and 2% BFS. All the treatment media were adjusted to a solid form by adding 1.8% bacteriological agar (Oxoid Ltd., Hampshire, UK) as required. For antimicrobial assays, Mueller‒Hinton agar (MHIA) (Oxoid Ltd., Hampshire, UK) was used for agar well diffusion. Luria broth (HiMedia Laboratories Pvt. Ltd., Maharashtra, India) was used for pathogenic bacterial isolate culture refreshment, which was adjusted to solid form by the addition of 1.8% bacteriological agar. Yeast peptone dextrose broth (YPDB) was used as the medium for Saccharomyces cerevisiae (SC). All the media above were boiled and sterilized at 121 °C for 20 min in an autoclave (Hirayama HVE-50, Japan).

Preparation of lactic acid bacteria

Culture stocks of three lactic acid bacterial strains, Lmb. fermentum ATCC 9338, Lb. acidophilus ATCC 314, and Lcb. casei ATCC 393, were used in this study. All LAB isolates were streaked onto MRS agar (MRSA) plates and slants, then incubated at 37 °C in an anaerobic jar for 48 h.

Preparation of pathogenic bacteria

The pathogenic culture strains used in this study were enterohemorrhagic E. coli (EHEC), enteropathogenic E. coli (EPEC), enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC), Bacillus cereus, and Staphylococcus aureus. These pathogens were streaked onto Luria agar and incubated at 37 °C under aerobic conditions overnight.

Lactic acid bacteria enumeration

The enumeration of lactic acid bacteria from each treatment was carried out using the total plate count (TPC) method. All lactic acid bacteria were grown in MRSB at 37 °C for 24 h under anaerobic conditions. The optical density of the bacterial suspension was measured with a UV/Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific Genesys, US) and adjusted to an OD600 of 1 or equal to ± 8 × 108 cells/mL in sterile MRSB. Serial dilution was performed by adding 100 µL of bacterial suspension (OD600 = 1) to 900 µL of 0.85% sterile physiological salt solution via 10-fold dilution. Every series of dilutions was mixed evenly, and 100 µL of dilutions from 10−−5 and 10−−6 were spread on various media (M1, M2, & M3). The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 48–72 h under anaerobic conditions, and colonies were counted after 72 h of incubation. Sterile uninoculated MRSA was used as a negative control, while inoculated MRSA served as a positive control. This assay was performed in triplicate, and the total plate count was performed in duplicate and repeated at least three times.

Cell-free supernatant (CFS) preparation

The cell-free supernatant (CFS) was prepared according to previous methods [117, 118], with modifications. Briefly, lactic acid bacteria were grown in 120 mL of treatment media (M1, M2, & M3) for 24–28 h under anaerobic conditions. Each supernatant was harvested and separated from the cell pellet and debris by centrifugation at 5000 × g for 20 min. The cell-free supernatant (10 mL) was then transferred into sterile centrifuge tubes and freeze-dried for 48 h. The resulting freeze-dried powders were stored at −20 °C and rehydrated with sterile deionized water before use. The CFS powder was rehydrated in 2 mL of deionized water (concentrated 5×) for further use.

Antimicrobial assay

Agar well diffusion was used to observe the antimicrobial activity of the cell-free supernatant (CFS) of lactic acid bacteria against four pathogen isolates. The antimicrobial assay procedure was carried out as previously described [119] with some modifications. Initially, all the pathogen cultures were inoculated in Luria broth and incubated at 37 °C overnight. The density of the culture was adjusted to an OD600 of 0.08–0.132 or approximately 0.5 McFarland standard (108 cells/mL). Next, 100 µL of the adjusted cultures were subsequently streaked perpendicularly onto Mueller‒Hinton agar (MHIA) using sterile cotton buds. The wells were created in the agar using a sterile cork borer, and 100 µL of rehydrated CFS was added to each well based on its treatment against the pathogen. Sterile MRS was added to separate well as a negative control. The antibacterial activities were measured in milimeters based on the size of the clear zone of inhibition (clear zone) in millimeters. The antimicrobial assay was performed in triplicate.

Agglutination assay

The attachment of lactic acid bacteria to various treatments was observed using an agglutination assay in which the yeast cell Saccharomyces cerevisiae (SC) was used to mimic the mannose receptor in the human digestive system. The agglutination assay was executed as previously described [74] with slight modifications. First, all LAB were inoculated for 24 h at 37 °C in their respective treatment media. S. cerevisiae was inoculated into Yeast Peptone Dextrose Broth (YPDB) at 28 °C for 24 h, and Escherichia coli was inoculated in Luria Broth (LB) at 37 °C for 24 h with aeration. Afterward, LAB in M1, S. cerevisiae in YPDB, and E. coli in LB were adjusted to OD600 of 1 without prior filtration and centrifuged at 6000 × g for 2 min. The supernatants were removed, and the pellets were resuspended in 1 mL of 1X PBS. On the other hand, the LAB in M2 and M3 were filtered and centrifuged to separate the media debris from the pellet. The density of the LAB was adjusted to OD600 of 1 using 1X PBS. The adjusted supernatants were combined according to the ratios in Table 2 and incubated for 10 min. The combined supernatant was transferred to a microscope slide in a total volume of 50 µL and covered with a coverslip. The samples were observed at 10 × 10 and 10 × 40 magnification using light electron microscopy [74].

Gas chromatography‒mass spectrometry (GC‒MS)

Gas chromatography and mass spectrometry (GC‒MS), GC Trace 1310, and MS ISQ LT were used to identify each bioactive compound in the LAB supernatants. The Cell-free supernatants (CFSs) were filtered through a 0.2 μm microfilter and then injected into a GC‒MS instrument with a TG5MS column. The GC‒MS settings were based on a previous study [120] with slight modifications. The injection mode was set to split with a split flow of 10 µL/min and an inlet temperature of 250 °C. Helium gas (99.999%) was used as the carrier gas, with a flow rate of 1 µL/min. Each peak on the chromatogram represents the number of bioactive compounds detected in the range of 45–450 amu. The bioactive compounds were identified on the basis of the GC‒MS database [120].

Liquid chromatography-high resolution mass spectroscopy (LC-HRMS)

The Cell-free supernatants (CFSs) were filtered through a 0.2 μm microfilter as a preparation step for LC-HRMS. The analysis process was performed at the Advanced Chemical Characterization Laboratory, National Research and Innovation Agency (BRIN), Indonesia.

Data analysis

The obtained data were processed via Microsoft Excel to calculate the average and standard error. The significance of the viability of all LAB isolates after several media treatments was determined by a paired t-test on the log CFU/mL values collected from the colony counting. This study used a descriptive analysis conducted with IBM® SPSS® statistics version 25.0. and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The analysis process began with a normality test to examine the data distribution, followed by a homogeneity test to check variance homogeneity. Further analysis was performed with a one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s B test for data that were both homogenous and normally distributed. If these assumptions were not met, the data were further tested with the Kruskal–Wallis H test and the Mann–Whitney U test.
