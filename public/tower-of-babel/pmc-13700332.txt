From potential to pause – missed opportunities in harm reduction: lessons learned and perspectives from a discontinued implementation study among young men who have sex with men in Thailand
Authors: Jakkrapatara Boonruang, Akarin Hiransuthikul, Jarunee Siriphan, Siriporn Nonenoy, Sudarat Thongsuksangcharoen, Piengfa Kamolrath, Piranun Hongchookiat, Jittraphon Chaejaew, Thitipun Sripitathanasakul, Nittaya Phanuphak, the CLYMAX-2 study team
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHarm reduction remains underutilized in Thailand, despite the increasing visibility of people engaging in chemsex—the use of substances during sex associated with complex health and sociolegal challenges. The CLYMAX study, a two-phase implementation project, was launched to explore the chemsex landscape among young men who have sex with men (MSM) aged 16–35 (CLYMAX-1) and to pilot a community-informed harm reduction model (CLYMAX-2). Although CLYMAX-2, an intended twenty-four-month prospective cohort, was prematurely terminated due to the cessation of U.S. funding, it yielded valuable, on-the-ground insights. Here, we distil lessons learned and perspectives from the six-month implementation period.DiscussionWhile several studies reported that sexual health clinics could be potential gateways for MSM who engage in chemsex to seek healthcare services without bearing the burden of stigma, CLYMAX-2 found various barriers in achieving this goal. The lack of context-specific, scientifically proven, and practical harm reduction strategies disrupted chemsex-focused integration into sexual health services. Peer-led STI treatment models may effectively help address mistrust in the healthcare system and weave sexual health awareness into the fabric of chemsex culture. Moreover, the current definition of problematic substance use is unable to fully capture the nuances of how chemsex interacts with an individual beyond a biomedical viewpoint. These proved to be hidden challenges for providers and those engaging in chemsex to set a pragmatic goal for their harm reduction journey. We also emphasized the importance of integrating funding diversification into program designs to ensure sustained support for priority agendas.ConclusionsThese perspectives inform the need for context-specific, practical harm reduction strategies tailored to MSM engaging in chemsex in Thailand. Further research is required to support the integration of chemsex-related services into sexual health services and to evaluate the effectiveness and feasibility of peer-led interventions. The waning international funding to support chemsex-related research highlights the need to diversify funding sources to ensure sustainable progress in the field.

Background

Harm reduction is a globally accepted strategy to mitigate negative physical and sociolegal consequences associated with drug use [1, 2]. By understanding and accepting the complex nature of drug use, this approach creates a non-coercive, judgment-free space where people who use drugs can safely navigate their relationship with substances and avoid preventable harms. There is a multitude of studies that exemplify the success of harm reduction programs across the world. A meta-analysis has shown that needle and syringe programs (NSPs) and opioid substitution treatment are highly effective in reducing injection-related risks [3]. A report from Australia demonstrated the cost-effectiveness of NSPs in preventing HIV and hepatitis C infections [4]. Amidst overwhelming evidence supporting harm reduction and its effectiveness in reducing drug-related harms, there is a persistent belief that it contradicts drug cessation efforts and may even encourage continued use. However, the International Narcotics Control Board has recognized harm reduction as a preventive strategy for drug demand reduction since 1993, urging for it to be implemented alongside drug demand reduction strategies and effectively debunking this misconception.

Despite this, the myth continues to be used as a justification to perpetuate punitive drug laws in several countries. In Thailand, a document issued by the Office of the Council of State of Thailand has further complicated the landscape by categorizing NSP as an action that facilitates drug use [5]. This has created an uncertain atmosphere around harm reduction services, deterring healthcare providers from offering sterile needles to those people who inject drugs (PWID), and inevitably shifting harm reduction advocacy onto the hands of non-governmental organizations and civil society organizations (CSO).

While challenges surrounding harm reduction remain unresolved, a growing body of evidence demonstrating the changing narrative of drug use and its association with public health concerns, particularly HIV and sexually transmitted infections (STIs), has become evident. The Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) reported an estimated 300,000 people in Asia and the Pacific region were diagnosed with HIV in 2023 [6], with PWID showing a median HIV prevalence of 7.5%, the highest among all key populations [7]. Moreover, research shows that chemsex, the use of substances during sex, is associated with a heightened risk of HIV and STIs acquisition, along with mental health issues, emphasizing the need for a more comprehensive harm reduction approach.

In 2022, the Institute of HIV Research and Innovation (IHRI) launched a two-phase implementation study titled Comprehensive assessment in a Longitudinal study on Young Men who have sex with men Attracting to chemseX (CLYMAX) [8–10]. Originally co-funded by the United States National Institutes of Health (NIH), the study aimed to explore multi-stakeholder perspectives on the chemsex landscape among young MSM and to develop and evaluate a comprehensive harm reduction model. Phase 1 (CLYMAX-1) focused on gathering insights from young MSM aged 16–35, as well as their family members, friends, and healthcare providers. These insights informed the development of a four-pronged harm reduction model addressing key domains of harm associated with chemsex: substance use, sexual health, mental health, and sociolegal aspects [9].

Phase 2 (CLYMAX-2) translated insights from CLYMAX-1 into practice by integrating services across three key access points identified as potential gateways for young MSM engaging in chemsex: a sexual health clinic, a legal consultation clinic, and community outreach at a gay sauna. CLYMAX-2 was designed as a 6-month prospective cohort study to determine both the implementation and clinical outcomes of the harm reduction model. Prior to study discontinuation, between September 2024 and April 2025, 123 of the targeted 150 Thai young MSM who engage in chemsex were enrolled, and 32 participants completed their 3-month follow-up visit. During this intended 6-month period, data collected included self-administered questionnaires on behavioral risk factors (e.g., number of sexual partners, PrEP use, injection drug use, condom use) and self-reported, multi-domain chemsex-related harms. However, after 6 months of implementation, CLYMAX-2 was abruptly closed following restrictions imposed by the US government on NIH-funded research involving sexual and gender minorities.

Although the early termination of the study compromised data integrity and long-term outcome evaluation, several observations emerged during its brief run. This paper aims to distil key lessons learned from the six-month implementation period of the CLYMAX-2 study that may guide the development of contextually relevant harm reduction strategies in Thailand.

Experiences and perspectives from the discontinued study

Barriers to integrating chemsex-related care into the existing sexual health services for MSM who use chemsex

Several studies examined the healthcare needs of those who engage in chemsex and the barriers that impede them from accessing the services. One of the recurring themes involves the lack of understanding and sensitivity towards drug and chemsex use among providers [11, 12]. Several studies also suggest solutions to tackle this problem, including the prioritization of peer providers in care and integrating chemsex-related knowledge to existing sexual health clinics where stigmatization is less perceived [11]. As straightforward as these solutions may sound, numerous barriers have emerged that hinder their effective implementation [13]. During the run of CLYMAX-2, we identified context-specific issues that play essential roles in the process. One key issue is that the existing body of knowledge on chemsex, though consistently growing, remains largely Western-centric and clinical in nature [14]. This can sometimes be disengaging for participants in Thailand, given how chemsex varies through different cultures and contexts [15, 16]. During implementation, we engaged peer counsellors with extensive lived experience of chemsex within the Thai context. Anecdotally, this approach enhanced engagement with young MSM who use chemsex and facilitated meaningful peer-to-peer and peer-to-provider knowledge exchange.

However, the key issue involves the practicality of the available information. Optimized practices for drug use have yet to be formally established in Thailand, creating a significant knowledge gap that neither healthcare providers nor the community is fully equipped to fill. Nevertheless, several harm reduction strategies have emerged organically within peer networks. Strategies such as assigning lucid roles during parties to minimize drug overdose, initiating discussion on consent and boundaries before the sessions, and sharing remedies to cope with comedown effects were reported in some studies [17].

Some of these align with medical practices, allowing providers to easily validate and support them. Nevertheless, other peer-based approaches remain medically controversial, for example, the use of marijuana after stimulant use to ease undesirable effects and facilitate comedowns. This problem poses a barrier for service providers, limiting their ability to offer evidence-based counselling to participants who expect practical and accurate guidance. We encourage future harm reduction programs to be pragmatic by identifying and validating existing strategies popularized within the community to create a contextually relevant harm reduction approach.

Navigating the peer support model for point-of-care STI treatment

Mistrust in healthcare among people engaging in drug use has been notably documented in several studies, even in a harm reduction space [18]. Multiple reasons contribute to this mistrust, including the perceived surveillance that hovers over healthcare settings and perceived or enacted stigma from healthcare providers [19, 20]. While addressing drug use stigma in the healthcare landscape is essential and inevitable, there is an urgent need to establish an effective care cascade for those engaging in chemsex, especially regarding HIV and STIs. During the implementation, providers faced a similar situation where some participants did not access a partnered community-led sexual health clinic to receive the treatment following their STI diagnosis from a self-collection kit at a sauna.

Through discussions with peer counsellors, we identified two primary barriers contributing to this treatment delay: lack of awareness and mistrust of healthcare. In response, the team developed a peer-handed chlamydia (CT) treatment protocol, which allows a peer counsellor to evaluate and hand over antibiotics, upon physician case review and teleprescription, to the participants. We developed a checklist to reassess their drug allergies, along with a brief recommendation on how and when to use the medication. All this information will be emphasized by the peer counsellor during the handover of medication (Azithromycin 1 g orally). This approach aligns with the World Health Organization’s (WHO) recommendations for point-of-care STI testing and treatment, adapting them to the specific needs of those engaging in chemsex. By leveraging the trust and reach of peer-led models, which have been shown to be effective in other health contexts, we successfully reengaged individuals diagnosed with CT infection in the care cascade. Despite the lack of sufficient data to inform treatment effectiveness, antibiotic stewardship, and drug resistance profile, this approach may address the problem of delayed STI treatment among those engaging in chemsex and should be further explored and refined in future studies.

Problematic substance use evaluation beyond a biomedical point of view

One of the main challenges following the implementation of CLYMAX-2 is determining when chemsex becomes problematic. In medical settings, “substance use disorder” is defined by the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR) as a pattern of substance use that leads to impairment or distress, whether physical, psychological, or social. The diagnosis is based on eleven criteria, including social impact. However, none of them involves legal issues.

Several researchers have proposed concepts to broaden the definition of ‘problematic substance use’ beyond the biomedical lens [21]. Some studies highlight the complexity of the term, noting that ‘problematic’ is a dynamic construct that varies depending on the stakeholder’s perspectives, ranging from individual to interpersonal and community levels. One study also identified community-developed harm reduction strategies used to address the problematic aspects of chemsex across these levels, signifying that such challenges are recognized by people engaging in chemsex [17]. Nevertheless, these concepts have yet to be pragmatically translated into Thai settings, let alone inform the development of tools to assess these dimensions. The limited sensitivity of the current tool to capture the full picture of chemsex makes it difficult for participants to self-navigate their drug use journeys and for providers to set personalized and meaningful goals for them.

During the implementation of CLYMAX-2, we also found that the definition of problematic use depends on the relationships between different sociological levels in a person’s life and differing interpretations between service providers and recipients. One participant told us that he had begun borrowing money from friends to buy more drugs, a behavior he did not perceive as problematic. However, our peer provider did not share this view. While such small but unconventional actions are not indicative of problematic substance use, they raised an important question whether there could be a tool capable of capturing the nuances and subtle effects of substance use to one’s life beyond the point of usage.

Another participant shared that he consciously and willingly began trading sex for substances. This example demonstrates the complex interplay between substance use, power, and autonomy which poses as a challenge for both participants and providers to navigate. This complexity can sometimes discourage providers from offering further counseling, as they fear that a moral misalignment could fracture the provider-participant relationship. Unfortunately, due to the cessation of funding, these issues were left unexplored. We urge future research to continue untangling these complex dynamics to improve the quality of care for people who engage in chemsex.

Implications for future research

Embedding sustainability into the structure of the research

While the promise of longitudinal results to inform the magnitude of harms associated with chemsex and the effectiveness of the four-pronged approach may be cut short, stakeholder engagement and capacity-building curriculum developed through the study have ignited a meaningful conversation on chemsex in Thailand. The CLYMAX study brought together government agencies, CSOs, healthcare providers, and communities to collaboratively shape the study’s direction and its implications for future national policies and education since before its implementation. As a result, the chemsex curriculum, along with the four-pronged approach, was subsequently presented to and adopted by the National Health Security Office (NHSO) as part of a national harm reduction strategy.

This stakeholder buy-in helped ensure the sustainability of the concept and facilitated the domestic funding. This approach has proven successful in previous projects, such as key population-led Princess PrEP project in 2019 [22], where early stakeholder engagement contributed to the inclusion of PrEP in Thailand’s Universal Health Coverage scheme, further underscoring the importance of embedding sustainability into research from the beginning.

Diversification of funding sources

In light of US government funding cuts to research on sexual and gender minorities, the need for funding diversification has become urgent and undeniable. This applies not only at the organizational level but also within research and programmatic initiatives. Existing studies have highlighted how funding diversification enhances an organization’s resilience, sustainability [23, 24], and, now more than ever, its survival. However, this strategy should not be confined solely to administrative or institutional levels. We argue that it should also be implemented across programmatic components and research priority areas. This could help programs remain agile, responsive, and less vulnerable to single-source dependency, ensuring sustained progress in the field.

The practical example of this approach was shown by the CLYMAX-2 study. While an NIH grant initially funded core research activities, the curriculum development and training components were supported by the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime. Simultaneously, the four-pronged harm reduction approach was supported through pharmaceutical funding. This multi-source funding strategy helps maintain momentum in harm reduction advocacy and ensures the continuity of efforts despite financial uncertainty.

Conclusions: Lessons learned, potential ahead

Even though the early termination of the CLYMAX-2 disrupted the evaluation of the evidence-based harm reduction practices, limiting knowledge to inform policy reform, and impacting the health and wellbeing of those engaging in chemsex, the baseline qualitative insights gained from its implementation offer valuable guidance for the future of harm reduction strategies in Thailand. The implementation highlighted the challenges to integrate chemsex-focused services into sexual health services, impeding the approach to utilize chemsex knowledge as a gateway to other healthcare priorities. It also underscored the potential of peer-support interventions in bridging gaps in care, addressing mistrust in health systems, and facilitating timely STIs diagnosis and treatment. Importantly, it also revealed the inadequacy of the current definition of “problematic drug use” in fully capturing the nuanced realities of chemsex, especially its psychosocial and sociolegal dimensions. These underexplored concepts can help guide future studies in Thailand and across Asia toward contextually relevant, community-informed, and holistic harm reduction approaches that reflect the lived experiences of young MSM who engage in chemsex. Lastly, CLYMAX-2 stands as a testament to the missed opportunities that result from exclusive research funding initiatives, and it serves as a call to transform potential into progress through more inclusive research efforts, ones that scientifically inform work designed for the people who need it most.
