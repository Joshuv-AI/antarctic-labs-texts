Explainable AI based hybrid DRM-Net transfer learning model for breast cancer detection and classification using ultrasound images
Authors: Shahid Mohammad Ganie, Majid Bashir Malik, Mir Aadil, Ghazala Muteeb, Mohd Farhan, Mohammad Aatif
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Breast cancer is a serious health concern and one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths among women worldwide. The early diagnosis of breast cancer is crucial for successful treatment and improved patient outcomes. Advanced computational techniques, particularly deep transfer learning have gained considerable attention for their effectiveness in medical image computing. Initially, six transfer learning models with different specifications are trained, validated and tested on the breast ultrasound image dataset. Additionally, a novel hybrid model, DRM-Net, is developed by stacking the top three TL models based on concatenation and flattening of deep dense layers. Various techniques, including image preprocessing, image masking, data augmentation, and hyperparameter tuning, are incorporated to enhance the overall performance of the considered models. The models are thoroughly evaluated using various standard metrics and statistical methods. Furthermore, to ensure transparent decision-making, the proposed model is interpreted using XAI-based class activation mapping (CAM) method with different versions. The proposed DRM-Net model demonstrated superior predictive performance compared to the other six transfer learning models. Among all the models, DRM-Net achieved the highest overall performance, with an accuracy of 96.71%, precision of 96%, recall of 97%, F1-score of 97%, and an AUC value of 99%, respectively. It also outperformed state-of-the-art similar studies in the literature. The experimental results demonstrate the potential utility of the hybrid DRM-Net model in improving the accuracy of breast cancer diagnosis, which could facilitate informed clinical decision-making. The proposed model can also be applied to other diseases where ultrasound imaging is available

Introduction

According to the World Health Organisation (WHO), Breast Cancer (BC) is one of the most common types of cancer and has affected millions of people globally (https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/who-position-paper-on-mammography-screening). In underdeveloped countries, BC rates are significantly higher than in developed countries, particularly among women. Each year, approximately 1.38 million individuals are diagnosed with breast cancer, and unfortunately, one-third of those diagnosed do not survive. The global death rate from cancer is 9.6 million, but there are also 1.7 million individuals who survive after receiving treatment for cancer1,2. The traditional method of classifying breast cancer, primarily based on clinicopathological characteristics and the analysis of common biomarkers, might not precisely reflect the varied clinical outcomes associated with each distinct subtype of breast cancer. Understanding the intricate biological processes involved in the onset and progression of cancer poses a significant challenge3. Several approaches exist for detecting breast cancer in its early stages, including undergoing diagnostic tests, observing signs of abnormality, or seeking advice from a local healthcare professional4. Implementing these strategies can lower the mortality rate and enhance the likelihood of successful treatment. Grasping the intricate biological mechanisms that contribute to the onset and progression of cancer poses a formidable challenge4,5. However, recent advances in high-throughput technologies have provided valuable insights into the genetic modifications and biological processes involved in breast cancer6. These discoveries offer novel perspectives on treatment strategies and patient classifications that can impact the management of individuals with breast cancer.

Due to substantial healthcare expenses and elevated morbidity linked to cancer, researchers have been driven to create more precise models for cancer detection2. The primary challenges in recognizing and pinpointing cancerous cells in breast cancer images stem from their variations in size, shape, and position. Additionally, other anomalies like mastitis, adenopathy, and granuloma may coexist in breast images, further complicating the distinction of cancer cells5. Although conventional machine learning (ML) techniques are employed for cancer detection, their complex nature has resulted in reduced efficiency and accuracy in performance7,8. To overcome these challenges, deep learning (DL) has been introduced, demonstrating the capability to generate superior feature representations, thereby serving as a potent tool for addressing image classification and object localization tasks in cancer detection9. This paper provides an overview of recent research concerning classification of medical imaging data related to breast cancer, such as breast mammogram scans10. The present study scrutinizes and applies existing transfer learning-based AI applications for breast mammogram scans to underscore the potential of computer-aided diagnosis (CAD). Utilizing mammogram scan datasets, this research demonstrates that transfer learning in medical imaging enhances the accuracy, efficiency, and reliability of disease prediction within the healthcare system.

Motivation

In the identification of malignancy, computer-aided approaches outperformed radiologists. Image identification/classification and object recognition have become easier and more accurate as deep neural networks have advanced, particularly when it comes to medical imaging11. Multimodal imaging is playing an increasingly important role in improving early detection and streamlining the diagnostic process12. As powerful computers and extensive datasets become increasingly accessible, incorporating artificial intelligence (AI) into the current workflow of multimodality imaging for CAD diagnosis is a promising method for assisting radiologists in meeting the growing demand for imaging assessments and accurate early diagnosis13. Over the years, clinical management of breast cancer has included a range of therapeutic approaches, including chemotherapy, targeted therapies, and immunotherapy5. In clinical settings, different imaging modalities, including computed tomography (CT), nuclear medicine imaging, X-rays, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), etc., are utilized for breast cancer diagnosis14. Their interpretation requires highly trained radiologists due to the complexity of the images and potential overlap with other breast abnormalities15. As artificial intelligence has advanced, ML techniques have been explored in various sectors, with a notable impact in healthcare for tasks as disease detection16, prediction17, diagnosis18, and prognosis19. Also, DL has demonstrated substantial results and is now widely employed in medical image analysis20,21. These models are capable of identifying complex patterns within high-dimensional data, surpassing the limitations of traditional image interpretation methods that typically rely on expert visual assessment22–24. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are effective in extracting the local and global level features efficiently from image morphology25, contributing to improved accuracy in classification and detection tasks26,27. In recent years, TL models have produced desirable outcomes, especially when applied to large-scale medical imaging datasets and have emerged as powerful techniques for enhancing disease diagnostic performance28.

Why deep transfer learning

Transfer learning models have gained significant attention and are widely implemented architectures in various applications, including medical image diagnosis29, computer vision30, natural language processing31, speech recognition32, and many more33. TL involves using pre-trained neural networks to transfer the insights and knowledge acquired from one problem statement to another across similar domains34. This is typically achieved by employing a CNN pre-trained on a large-scale image dataset, which is subsequently fine-tuned on the target breast ultrasound image dataset. Through this fine-tuning process, the model learns domain-specific features relevant to breast cancer detection and classification without the need for extensive labelled data. These models have demonstrated great potential by producing reliable outcomes with minimal computational resources, thereby enhancing the efficiency of disease detection and classification. Related work

Over the past few years, computer-aided diagnosis systems have shown promise in detecting and predicting breast cancer using medical imaging. Various DL and TL models have been utilized to automate breast cancer diagnosis, significantly improving diagnostic precision compared to traditional methods35. Extensive research has been conducted encompassing various datasets, algorithms, and methodologies for breast cancer detection and classification. This section reviews some similar studies that have applied DL and TL techniques for breast cancer detection and classification.

Lu et al.36 proposed a novel computer-aided diagnosis system for breast cancer detection called BCDNet. They utilized deep Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) models for extracting image representations and introduced an adaptive backbone selection algorithm to identify the most suitable CNN for the task. Among all the models, backbone BCDNet achieved the highest accuracy, sensitivity, precision, and F1-score of 93.97%, 95.24%, 97.68%, and 96.42%, respectively. Uysal and Kose37 conducted research to explore the application of pre-trained deep learning models for classifying breast cancer using ultrasound images. The study aimed to differentiate between benign (non-cancerous), malignant (cancerous), and normal samples. Three models were utilised, namely ResNet50, ResNeXt50, and VGG16. The results showed that ResNeXt50 had the highest accuracy score of 85.83%, outperforming the other models. However, ResNet50 demonstrated greater stability and better class-based results. Li et al.38 presented a deep learning model called BUSnet for detecting breast tumor lesions in ultrasound images. The employed model has a two-stage approach that uses unsupervised region proposal and bounding-box regression algorithms. Additionally, a post-processing technique was introduced to improve the performance of the disease detection results. The results indicated that BUSnet achieved an accuracy of 0.651, precision of 0.832, recall of 1.000, and f1-score of 0.789. Gu et al.39 implemented a deep learning model that can differentiate between benign and malignant breast lesions in ultrasound images using a large dataset collected from multiple centers of hospitals in China. The model demonstrated positive outcomes on the test sets, achieving an AUC of 0.908, a sensitivity of 83.23%, a specificity of 83.61%, and an accuracy of 83.48% for the internal test set. Additionally, for the external test sets, the model attained an AUC of 0.913, a sensitivity of 88.84%, a specificity of 83.77%, and an accuracy of 86.40%. Anand et al.40 developed a classification model using transfer learning for breast cancer detection based on the CBIS-DDSM dataset. In their work, the authors have explored the learning properties of layers for CNNs to fine-tune the model, achieving an accuracy of 75% and reducing false-negative rates, which indicates an improvement over existing methods. Hijab et al.41 proposed a deep learning model for the classification of ultrasound images to detect breast cancer. Initially, the baseline approach was employed to train the basic architecture of CNN, and a further pre-trained, fine-tuned VGG16 model was used to tackle the overfitting problem. As per the experiment, the model achieved an accuracy rate of 97% and an AUC of 98%. The authors suggested that this work can be used to explore the different imaging modalities for better prediction and detection of the disease. Rana et al.42 introduced a framework for breast cancer utilizing transfer learning models, primarily based on histopathological images. In this work, the authors have discussed and presented the effectiveness of transfer learning models without preprocessing techniques for better classification of tumors. Various transfer learning models were utilized, including LENET, VGG16, DarkNet53, DarkNet19, ResNet50, Inception, and Xception. Initially, Xception computed the best accuracy of 83.07% using the unbalanced dataset, while DarkNet53 attained the best accuracy rate of 87.17% by adding a new parameter, namely Balanced Accuracy (BAC). Shelke et al.43 implemented a model using transfer learning for the detection and classification of breast cancer based on the breast ultrasound scanning imaging (BUSI) dataset. The authors used pre-trained models including Sequential, Xception, DenseNet201, VGG16, and InceptionResNetV2, and compared them with the ensemble models. The EL model achieved the highest accuracy rate of 87.90%. Alruily et al.44 presented a novel approach using Vision Transformer (ViT) models for classification of benign and malignant breast tumors. The proposed ViT model achieved a high accuracy of 94.49% and an AUC of 0.921 for breast cancer prediction. The model outperforms traditional CNNs like VGG16 and DenseNet, demonstrating superior generalization without requiring data augmentation. The authors have suggested that using XAI methods like Grad-CAM, ViT’s self-attention mechanism inherently enhances interpretability for breast cancer diagnosis. Wang et al.45 proposed an anchor-free deep learning approach (FCOS) for detecting and classifying benign and malignant breast lesions in ultrasound images. To improve the image contrast, a segmentation-based enhancement (SBE) technique is used for better lesion detection. The proposed method achieves a mean average precision (mAP) of 90.2%, outperforming YOLO V3 (anchor-based method) and demonstrating high recall (98% for benign, 96.3% for malignant lesions), ensuring fewer missed cancer cases. The authors suggested that use of XAI can help in the segmentation step, inherently enhancing interpretability by localizing tumor regions. Daoud et al.46 implemented a hybrid model for breast tumor classification. The study employs a feature selection algorithm (VGG19) to identify the most relevant tumor characteristics, achieving a high accuracy of 96.10% when combining deep and morphological features. The proposed method is validated using cross-validation on 380 images and an independent external dataset of 163 images, demonstrating strong generalization.

Overall, these studies demonstrate that DL and TL have the potential to enhance the effectiveness of medical imaging data in classification and detection of breast cancer. Also, the adoption of pre-trained TL networks requires less computational resources and training time, as they are employed on large image datasets to transfer knowledge substantially across multiple applications. However, most existing studies primarily focus on independent transfer learning without exploring advanced hybrid and ensemble approaches. Additionally, the use of XAI techniques for feature identification and importance remains underexplored, limiting model interpretability and explainability. There is an opportunity for further research to identify the optimal architecture for TL and the most effective fine-tuning strategies for breast cancer detection. Furthermore, prior research has largely emphasized accuracy without conducting rigorous statistical validation to assess the significance of model performance.

Our study addresses these gaps by proposing a hybrid deep TL model designed to improve breast cancer diagnosis using ultrasound imaging. The inclusion of diverse TL models from different families with varied configurations enabled the assessment of individual model performance to design a novel hybrid DMI-Net architecture. Also, the hybrid preprocessing techniques, including image resizing, augmentation, and masking, are utilized to enhance the quality of the images and experimental usability. Model performance is assessed using standard evaluation metrics and further validated through statistical methods, including the Friedman aligned ranks test and post hoc analysis. We further enhance model interpretability using XAI methods, including Grad-CAM, Grad-CAM + +, and Score-CAM, to highlight and identify key features for breast cancer prediction. The proposed model is compared with considered TL models and similar recent research works, highlighting the improved performance and effectiveness of the proposed approach.

Image preprocessing

Image preprocessing is a critical step in improving both image quality and the effectiveness of algorithms used in the development of computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) systems47. To ensure reliability, the selected transfer learning models in this study are trained, tested, and validated using preprocessed data. A hybrid preprocessing approach is adopted, comprising image resizing (to match model-specific input dimensions), data augmentation (to address class imbalance), and masking (to enhance feature learning). Each of the preprocessing steps is discussed in detail in the subsequent subsections.

Image resizing

The image resizing is an important preprocessing step required by DL models to ensure the consistency in input image dimensions and can significantly impact training time and performance. The actual datasets utilized in this work comprise images of varying dimensions with an average image size of 500*500 pixels both within and across classes. All the images are normalized and resized to 512 × 512, a uniform dimension to ensure preservation of image features and compatibility with the model architectures. The classes undergo label encoding, i.e., 0 for class benign, 1 for class malignant, and 2 for class normal, respectively. This label encoding facilitates efficient classification during the model training process.

Data augmentation

The dataset used for the experiment was imbalanced across the classes, which can negatively impact the performance of the model during the training phase. To address this issue, an oversampling technique was applied by augmenting the number of images in the minority classes. Initially, the dataset comprised 1578 images, categorized into benign, malignant, and normal as 891, 421, and 266 images, respectively. To generate synthetic variants of the existing images, data augmentation was applied with different particulars (shearing, rotation, flipping, and zooming) and their corresponding values to ensure the generation of varied yet structurally consistent images. Image masking is a technique used in image processing, especially in medical domain, to identify, hide or highlight specific parts of an image48. It involves applying a binary or multi-channel mask to an image to filter out irrelevant information while keeping the important features. In our study, each image was resized to 512 × 512 pixels and then cropped to 224 × 224 pixels to focus on the central area where lesions are usually found. Gaussian filtering was applied to improve image quality and reduce noise, which gently smooths the images, and Sobel filtering to highlight edges. Also, the pixel values were normalized using a mean of [0.485, 0.456, 0.406] and a standard deviation of [0.229, 0.224, 0.225] to match the considered pre-trained transfer learning models’ requirements, ensuring consistent and high-quality input data for accurate breast cancer classification. TL models have gained considerable attention and have demonstrated significant potential in the healthcare industry, particularly in medical image computing34. The TL pre-trained architectures can extract the local and global features from diverse images efficiently. The models are trained over large image datasets like ImageNet, enabling efficient knowledge transfer for medical image computing49. The subsection outlines the considered TL models utilized in this study for breast cancer detection and classification.

After every two or three convolutional layers, max-pooling layers are applied to reduce the spatial dimensions of feature maps. InceptionV3

InceptionV352 is a widely popular and recognized CNN architecture that incorporates several key design principles, such as convolutional layer, depth-wise separable convolutions, residual connections, and inception modules. This architecture introduces the swish activation function, which improves upon traditional activation functions by enabling smoother gradient flow and better convergence. At its core, InceptionV3 is built upon the inception module, which effectively separates the cross-channel correlations from spatial relationships within CNN feature maps. DenseNet169

DenseNet12153 is a deep CNN comprising 169 layers, structured according to the DenseNet architecture. In this model, each layer is connected directly to every other succeeding layer in a feedforward manner, significantly enhancing information flow and gradient propagation across the network.

MobileNet

MobileNet54 is a widely used deep neural network architecture drafted for mobile and embedded devices characterized by limited computational resources. The architecture is founded on a lightweight building block known as a MobileNet unit, comprising a depthwise separable convolution layer succeeded by a pointwise convolution layer. The depthwise separable convolution is a factorised convolution that decomposes a standard convolution into a depthwise convolution and a pointwise convolution, which reduces the number of parameters and computations. ResNet50

ResNet5055 is a widely used deep CNN architecture across multiple computer vision applications. This network is designed to mitigate the vanishing gradient problem in deep networks. A new method, skip connections, is introduced, which is also known as residual connections, that helps to integrate the output of one layer with the previous layer’s output. It is comprised of one convolution + max pooling layer,16 residual blocks (bottleneck architecture), and final global average pooling + fully connected layer. Mathematically, the residual block can be expressed as given by Eq. 16.16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y=F(x, \left\{{W}_{i}\right\}+x)$$\end{document}where x is denotes the input to the block, F represents the set of convolutional layers with weights Wi, and y represents the block output. The input x to the output y is added by skip connection to produce the final output of the block.

Model architecture

The architecture of the proposed DRM-Net model is illustrated in Fig. 7. The network takes an input image of shape 512 × 512 × 3 and produces prediction outputs across three classes: benign, malignant, and normal. The model integrates three stacked TL architectures, including DenseNet169, ResNet50, and MobileNet, each followed by dedicated dense layers. Subsequently, identical convolutional layers of size 7 × 7 × 1024 are applied to each model, followed by individual max-pooling layers to downsample the feature maps. Each pathway’s output is then flattened and passed through a sequence of three fully connected layers (with sizes 1024, 512, and 256, respectively). The resulting feature representations from all three branches are concatenated, and the final classification is determined using a majority voting mechanism. This fusion enables a more robust and comprehensive representation of extracted features, leveraging the strengths of all three TL models. Confusion matrix as shown in Fig. 8 is used to check the performance of the hybrid DRM-Net model for breast cancer detection and classification. In this 3 × 3 square matrix, each row represents the actual class, while each column corresponds to the predicted class. Failure case analysis

Although DRM-Net consistently achieved superior predictive performance, a closer examination of misclassified samples revealed two major categories of failure cases. First, some malignant lesions were misclassified as benign when their sonographic appearance closely resembled fibroadenomas, with smooth and circumscribed margins misleading the model’s decision boundary. Second, in low-contrast or noisy ultrasound images, the model occasionally failed to capture subtle malignant features, resulting in false negatives. These challenges are inherent to ultrasound imaging, where tumor morphology and image quality vary substantially. Importantly, the CAM-based visualizations (Grad-CAM, Grad-CAM + + , and Score-CAM) still localized ambiguous or suspicious regions in such cases, providing radiologists with cues about diagnostic uncertainty.

The standard deviation (variance) across classes for precision, recall, and F1-score provides insights into the consistency of the performance of models. A lower variance indicates that the model performs uniformly well across all classes, while a higher variance implies potential bias towards specific classes. From Fig. 16, it can be found that DRM-Net demonstrates the lowest variance for recall and F1-score as 0.03 and 0.02, indicating its balanced performance across all classes. However, in terms of precision, InceptionV3, MobileNet, and DRM-Net achieved the lowest variance of 0.01. Examining specific performance metrics, the hybrid DRM-Net achieves higher rankings than VGG16, EfficientNetB0, InceptionV3, DenseNet169, ResNet50 and MobileNet in key classification measures such as accuracy, macro average, weighted average, loss and standard deviation. Although statistical significance is not reached, the trend suggests that the proposed hybrid DRM-Net consistently delivers more reliable predictions. The minimal difference between the hybrid DRM-Net model across all metrics (with adjusted p-values close to 1.0 in some cases) highlights the strength of both hybrid ensemble approaches, reinforcing their effectiveness in balancing predictive performance.

Interpretability and clinical implications of the DRM-Net model

This section highlights how DRM-Net enhances transparency through CAM-based visualizations and discusses its potential to improve diagnostic confidence, clinical decision-making, and adoption in real-world healthcare settings.

XAI using grad-CAM variants

Explainable AI techniques play a pivotal role in making AI models transparent, interpretable, and understandable by humans, particularly in critical domains like healthcare58. In medical image computing, XAI ensures that AI-driven diagnosis and predictions can be trusted, verified, and interpreted by radiologists and clinicians. These techniques also help in meeting ethical and regulatory standards, confirming that the model adheres to legal requirements such as GDPR, HIPAA, or FDA regulations, which is vital for transparency and trustworthiness in healthcare applications. In this study, the class activation mapping (CAM) technique59 different versions, including Grad-CAM, Grad-CAM + + , and Score-CAM, are incorporated to provide visual explanations and interpretation of the proposed hybrid DRM-Net model for breast cancer prediction. This method creates a heatmap emphasizing the areas of an input image most important for the decision-making process of the model. It computes the gradients of the target class score regarding a given convolutional layer’s feature maps. The feature maps are then weighted using these gradients, emphasizing the areas that have the most significant impact on the breast cancer prediction.

The CAM heatmaps generated using Grad-CAM, Grad-CAM + + and Score-Cam for benign, malignant and normal classes of the breast cancer test dataset are presented in Figs. 17, 18, and 19, respectively. Analyzing these heatmaps enables radiologists to compare the model’s predictions with their expertise, thereby improving their interpretation and confidence in the AI system’s results. Post clinical implications and applicability

The interpretability framework of DRM-Net consistently demonstrated the ability to localize malignant regions, aligning with diagnostic markers commonly employed in radiological practice. For benign lesions, the model frequently highlighted smooth and well-defined lesion edges, whereas for malignant tumors, attention was directed towards spiculated or heterogeneous regions. Notably, even in instances of misclassification, where malignant lesions were erroneously identified as benign or normal, the heatmaps identified ambiguous peripheral regions resembling fibroadenomas or areas affected by poor contrast. This indicates that, although the final classification may occasionally be incorrect, the interpretability mechanism still provides clinically valuable cues, alerting radiologists to areas of diagnostic uncertainty rather than producing opaque misclassifications.

The implications of these findings are clinically significant. Firstly, the trust factor is enhanced, as radiologists can directly visualize model reasoning and cross-verify it against their expertise. Secondly, the heatmaps function as decision-support tools rather than replacements for human expertise, promoting collaborative diagnosis where AI augments radiologist judgment. Thirdly, the modest but consistent improvements in recall and F1-score achieved by DRM-Net are clinically meaningful, as avoiding even a small number of missed malignancies can significantly impact patient survival outcomes. Furthermore, the combination of robust predictive performance and transparent explanations supports compliance with regulatory frameworks (e.g., GDPR, FDA guidelines) that increasingly demand explainability in clinical AI applications.

Beyond direct diagnostics, DRM-Net holds value in clinical training and knowledge transfer. For healthcare providers, interpretability enhances confidence in model outputs and facilitates adoption into routine workflows. Ultimately, by uniting predictive accuracy with transparency, DRM-Net contributes to the advancement of clinically reliable, ethically compliant, and user-trusted AI systems for breast cancer detection, bridging the critical gap between research performance and real-world application. In the broader healthcare ecosystem, DRM-Net demonstrates the feasibility of explainable and ensemble-based AI solutions, paving the way for scalable deployment across multi-center datasets, integration with PACS systems, and real-time radiology reporting. Ultimately, DRM-Net bridges the gap between technical innovation and bedside application, positioning itself as a clinically reliable, ethically compliant, and future-ready tool for breast cancer detection and management.

Conclusions, limitations, and future scope

This final section concludes the study, emphasising its key achievements and limitations. It also outlines the future scope, identifying areas for further exploration and potential enhancements.

Conclusion

This study presents Explainable DRM-Net, a hybrid deep transfer learning framework combining DenseNet169, ResNet50, and MobileNet for breast cancer detection and classification using the BUSI image dataset. The image preprocessing, image masking, data augmentation, and hyperparameter tuning were incorporated for better experimental usability. Initially, six TL models are explored to build the hybrid DRM-Net model by concatenating and stacking the top three performing models, namely DenseNet169, ResNet50, and MobileNet. Also, the models are thoroughly evaluated using the Friedman Aligned Ranks Test, along with a post-hoc analysis using the Holm method, to compare the considered TL models and demonstrate their statistical significance. The achieved results were assessed and compared through various performance evaluation metrics.

It was observed that the proposed DRM-Net performed best in all metrics. The model achieved an overall accuracy of 96.71%, with precision, recall, and F1-scores consistently outperforming individual baseline models by 2–5%. Five-fold cross-validation confirmed its robustness (95.9% ± 0.4), while ablation experiments demonstrated the complementary value of each component network. It also demonstrated superior accuracy compared to similar empirical studies from recent literature. Beyond predictive performance, DRM-Net incorporated Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM, Grad-CAM + + , and Score-CAM) to provide transparent visual explanations, ensuring that the framework not only delivers reliable outcomes but also enhances clinical interpretability. By balancing performance, interpretability, and statistical rigor, DRM-Net represents a meaningful step toward trustworthy CAD systems in breast cancer imaging.

Limitations

Despite its strengths, the study has certain limitations. First, the study relied solely on the BUSI dataset, which is relatively small for experimentation, potentially limiting generalizability across diverse populations and imaging environments. Second, to address class imbalance, image augmentation techniques were employed, which may introduce slight deviations from real-world data. Consequently, the performance of DRM-Net might differ little on a balanced real dataset where there is no need for synthetic augmentation. Third, while the hybrid ensemble approach enhanced performance, it introduced higher computational costs, longer training times, and increased memory requirements compared to individual models, which may hinder real-time deployment in resource-constrained settings. Nevertheless, this should not be seen as a limitation in critical applications such as cancer detection, where accuracy and precision are paramount. Fourth, although five-fold cross-validation was performed to reduce overfitting concerns, the absence of external validation on independent datasets remains a notable limitation. Additionally, some misclassifications occurred in cases where malignant lesions exhibited benign-like structures or were affected by poor image quality, highlighting the ongoing challenge of handling diagnostic ambiguity in ultrasound imaging.

Future scope

Future research will focus on addressing these limitations to enhance the clinical applicability of DRM-Net. Expanding validation to multi-center datasets with larger and more heterogeneous populations will improve generalizability and reliability. To overcome computational challenges, strategies such as lightweight ensembles, model pruning, and quantization will be explored to reduce complexity while maintaining interpretability. Integrating multi-modal imaging data (e.g., mammography, MRI) and incorporating transformer-based architectures may further improve the model’s diagnostic capability. Also, proposed model can be integrated with wearable devices for real-time monitoring and early-stage management of breast cancer. Future research could explore the incorporation of federated learning and generative AI models, as well as the utilization of more advanced techniques to enhance overall efficacy. Finally, prospective validation studies in clinical environments will be pursued to assess usability, trust, and regulatory compliance, paving the way for DRM-Net to become a practical diagnostic support tool in breast cancer care.
