The effect of cyberbullying victimization, traditional bullying victimization and suicidal ideation among college students: do specific affective problems play a mediate role?
Authors: Fang Chen, Irene Yinglin Li, Chenyi Ling, Hongyu Zou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuicidal ideation among college students and adolescents represents a significant public health issue worldwide. Research has indicated that emotional difficulties may mediate the relationship between bullying victimization and suicidal ideation. However, insights from Thomas Joiner’s interpersonal theory and Klonsky’s The Three-Step Theory suggest that suicidal ideation arises not merely from emotional distress but from the interplay of hopelessness along with these emotional challenges. Therefore, this study aims to investigate whether variouse affective problems mediated the relationship between bullying victimization and suicidal ideation among college students.MethodsIn this study, we utilized a convenience sampling method to collect data from 2717 college students (mean age = 19.81 years, 22.49% male) in South China. We examined the relationships between variables using Spearman’s correlation analysis. To analyze potential mediation effects, we employed PROCESS model 4 within SPSS.ResultsOur findings revealed that different forms of bullying victimization can predict experiences of depression, anxiety, and social anxiety. However, these emotional responses did not significantly influenced suicidal ideation among college students. Additionally, while different forms of bullying victimization did not directly correlate with feelings of hopelessness, hopelessness was closely associated with the prevalence of suicidal ideation. Furthermore, our results highlighted a significant mediating role of psychiatric prodromal experiences in the relationship between different forms of bullying victimization and suicidal ideation.ConclusionsThe results suggested that affective problems may not serve as a significant mediating factor in the relationship between bullying victimization and suicidal ideation. We proposed an integrated emotion-response model that enhances understanding of suicidal ideation among college and offers a framework for more effective prevention strategies.Keywords: College students, Bullying victimization, Affective problems, Suicidal ideation, Mediation model

Introduction

The university years represent a critical phase in the transition from adolescence to adulthood, often referred to as emerging adulthood [1]. This period is crucial for developing skills related to survival, adaptation, socialization, and emotional regulation [2]. Some scholars argue that this stage should be defined as extending from ages 10 to 24, thus positioning college students within the broader context of adolescent development [3, 4].

Adolescent suicidal behavior is recognized as a global major public health problem. According to the World Health Organization, suicide is one of the leading causes of death among adolescents [5]. Suicidal ideation, which involves thoughts of wanting to die or end one’s life [6], is particularly prevalent during college years, when students experience a transition from family life to greater independence. During this time, many college students undergo rapid psychological development that is often not fully matured,,leading to mood fluctuations and are increased vulnerability to suicide ideation [7, 8].

A meta-analysis involved 634,662 college students found that the lifetime prevalence of suicidal ideation is 22.3%, with a one-year prevalence of 10.6% [9]. Suicidal ideation is a significant indictors of potential suicidal attempt, as students who experience suicidal ideation are at a heightened risk of progressing to actual suicidal behavior [10]. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the factors and mechanisms influencing suicidal ideation among Chinese college students, contributing to the development of a new theoretical framework for understanding the occurrence of these thought. Additionally, this research seeks to provide empirical evidence that supports early screening and preventive measures for suicidal ideation within this population.

Bullying victimization and suicidal ideation

Bullying victimization encompasses the range of aggressive behaviors individuals experience in the interpersonal contexts, including physical and verbal aggression, social exclusion and potentially sexual harassment [11]. There are two primary forms of bullying victimization, cyberbullying victimization and traditional bullying victimization. Research has consistently shown that both forms are significantly related to suicidal ideation in affected individuals [12]. Traditional bullying victimization involves repeated harassment by one or more individuals [13], while cyberbullying victimization refers to the ongoing dissemination of hostile or harmful messages through digital olatforms [14].

A systematic review comprising 44 studies has identified that traditional bullying victimization as a risk factor for suicidal behavior among adolescents [15]. Moreover, a longitudinal study involving 1,465 adolescents found that experiences traditional bullying victimization at age 15 significantly predicted of suicidal ideation at ages 17 and 20 [16]. Similarly, a recent three-wave longitudinal study demonstrated that cyberbullying victimization in adolescents at an initial time point was a strong predictor of the developing suicidal ideation later on [17]. Despite the substantial empirical evidence demonstrating the relationship between bullying victimization and suicidal ideation, there is still controversy about whether different affective problems mediate the relationship between bullying victimization and suicidal ideation.

The biopsychosocial model of suicide risk indicated that affective problems, such as depression, anxiety, serve as mediater between negative life events (bullying victimization) and suicidal ideation [18]. A longitudinal study by Elledge et al. also indicated that internalizing problems (i.e., depression) mediated the relationship between adolescent bullying victimization and suicidal ideation [19]. In this study, we defined affective problems as negative emotions along with the cognitive and behavioral responses associated with those feelings. We specifically examine five emotional issues: depression, anxiety, social anxiety, hopelessness, and psychiatric prodromal experiences, to test their mediating roles in this dynamic.

Emotional problems and their role in suicidal ideation

Depression is a prominent affective problem characterizzed by persistent low mood and diminished interest in activities. A cross-sectional study involving 1814 students identified depression as a mediator between adolescent cyberbullying victimization and suicidal ideation [20]. Additionally, a retrospective study highlighted depression as a significant mediator in the relationship between traditionally bullying victimization and suicidal ideation [21]. An empirical study including 2522 adolescents found that anxiety not only predicted suicidal ideation among college students but also mediated the connection between bullying victimization and suicidal ideation in college students [22].

Furthermore, a cross-sectional study involving 1903 adolescents showed that bullying victimization significantly contributed to the development of social anxiety [23] which in turn had a positive association with suicidal ideation [24]. In a review by Hong et al. they found that hopelessness also mediated the relationship between bullying victimization and suicidal ideation among school-aged children [21].

Psychiatric prodromal experience encompass mild manifestations of psychotic syndromes, such as unusual speech and behavior, sensory abnormalities, and cognitive decline, including delusions and hallucinations. These experiences may arise in adolescents following bullying incidents, particularly if their negative emotions are not adequately expressed [25]. Research by Kang et al. established a significant association between psychiatric prodromal experiences and suicidal ideation [26].

While the role of various emotional problem as mediators in the relationship between bullying victimization and suicidal ideation remains contentious, some scholars argue that internalizing issues such as depression and anxiety do not mediate the development of suicidal thoughts in adolescents and young adults. Thus, further research is essential to clarify these complex relationships [27].

Theoretical framework and research objectives

According to Thomas Joiner’s interpersonal theory of suicidal ideation [28] and Klonsky’s The Three-Step Theory [29], suicidal ideation do not arise solely from emotional issues, but rather from a combination of feelings of hopelessness and other underlying factors. Clinical observations indicated that mot all depressed adolescents who experienced bullying or trauma develope develop suicidal ideation, suggested that suicidal ideation may be influenced by multiple emotional problem or a combination of affective problems or a combination of emotional and somatization symptoms.

In summary, the present study aimed to investigate whether different affective issues separately mediated the relationship between different forms of bullying victimization and suicidal ideation among college students. Additionally, we will explore whether a signal affective problem can mediate this relationship when controlling for other emotional factors. This research seek to provide empirical support for Thomas Joiner and Klonsky’s theory. The specific research hypotheses are as follows:

H1: There is a significant correlation between different forms of bullying victimization, various affective problems, and suicidal ideation among college students.

H2: A single affective problem may mediate the relationship between different forms of bullying victimization and suicidal ideation among college students without accounting for other affective problems.

H3: When controlling for other affective problems, a single affective problem will not significantly mediate the relationship between different forms of bullying victimization and suicidal ideation among college students.

Methods

Participants

From October 2022 to November 2022, we conducted a survey using a convenience sampling method to recruit 2992 college students from South China for an anonymous questionnaire study. The survey collected demographic information, including gender, age, ethnicity, residence, economic status and so on, as well as the School Bullying Behavior Questionnairs, Cyberbullying Inventory, The MINI-C Questionnaire, PHQ-9, GAD-7, Social Anxiety Scale, Beck Hopelessness Scale, and Prodromal Questionnaire.

To facilitate participation, the questionnaires were made available through the Wen Juan Xing online platform (ww.wjx.cn). Participants accessed the survey by scanning a QR code or clicking on a link, either from a computer or mobile device. Upon completed the survey, participants received detailed mental health report. Data processing was rigorous; any outliers, or mismatchs in age and year of study were excluded from the final analysis,. Ultimately, we obtained 2717 valid questionnaires, with a 90.81% valid rate. This research was conducted following ethical standards and received approval from the Ethics Committee of the School of Psychology, South China Normal University, with the approval number: SCNU-PSY-2022-217.

Measures

Traditional bullying victimization

We utilized a set of five questions adapted from the School Bullying Behavior Questionnaire, originally translated by Chang et al. [30] based on Olweus’ [31]framework to access the tarditional bullying victimization among students. Participants reported the frequency of experiences such as “A classmate threatened me” on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1="never” and 7="at least once a day”, higher scores indicated greater severity of victimization. The overall reliability of this questional, measured by Cronbach alpha, we found to be 0.752.

Cyberbullying victimization

The Cyberbullying Inventory (CBI), developed by Erdur and Kavsut [32], with a Chinese version revised by Zhou et al. [33], was used. The questionnaire measures the frequency of cyberbullying experienced or perpetrated by individuals in their daily lives and has two dimensions, cyberbullying and cyberbullying victimization, and the sub-dimension of cyberbullying victimization was used in this study. The sub-dimension consists of 18 items, such as “Have you ever received a hurtful email from someone you know”. Responses were rated on a hurtful email from someone you know?” Response were rated on a 4-point scale from 1="never” to 4="more than 5 times”, with higher scores represent more sever cyberbullying victimization. The inventory demonstrated strong internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.926.

Suicidal ideation

To assess suicidal ideation, we utilized the MINI-C questionnaire, which is a component of the psychiatric rating questionnaire [34]. This tool consists of four questions designed to evaluate participants’ perceptions of suicidal thoughts. Responses are scored dichotomously, for example, assigning 2 points for agreeing with the statement “Do you think it is better to die? and 0 for disagreeing. Scores from various questions are aggregated to yield a total score ranging from 0 to 17, with higher scores reflecting stronger suicidal ideation. This scale showed good internal reliability, with a Cronbach’s coefficient of 0.788.

Depression

Depression symptoms were measured using the PHQ-9 scale, ass introduced in China by Wang et al. [35]. This scale includes 9 items assessing the severity of depressive feelings(e.g., Feeling depressed, upset, or hopeless) and employs a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (almost every day). The total score can range from 0 to 27, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. The PHQ-9 displayed excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach coefficient of 0.902.

Anxiety

Anxiety levels were assessed with the GAD-7, introduced by Zeng et al. [36]. The scale consists of 7 items regarding symptoms of anxiety (e.g., inability to sit still due to restlessness, etc.) and utilizes a 4-point Likert-type scalefrom 0 (not at all) − 3 (almost every day), with a total score range of 0–21. Higher scores on the scalerepresenting more severe of anxiety symptoms. This scale exhibited good internal consistency with a Cronbach’s coefficient of 0.931.

Social anxiety

Social anxiety was measured using the Social Anxiety Scale for Children, a Chinese scale introduced by Zhou et al. [37], which consists of 10 items (e.g., I worry that people will not like me). It employs a three-point Likert scale with scores ranging 0 (never) to 2 (always), totaling between 0 and 20. Higher scores indicate more severe social anxiety, and this scale exhibited good internal consistency with a Cronbach coefficient of 0.927.

Hopelessness

Hopelessness was measured using the Beck Hopelessness Scale, which was introduced by Ma et al. [38] to assess the degree of hopelessness of subjects. After the reverse scoring, the higher the score, the more severe the subject’s sense of hopelessness. The scale has good internal consistency with a Cronbach coefficient of 0.656.

Psychiatric prodromal experience

The Prodromal Questionnaire introduced by Chen et al. [39], was employed to evaluate psychiatric prodromal experiences. This scale includes 16 entries (e.g., I have seen things that other people apparently can’t see), and utilizes a four-point Likert range from 0 (none)-3 (severe), with a total score range of 0–48. Higher scores are indicative of more severe prepsychotic experiences. With this scale also also achieving strong internal reliability, reflected in a Cronbach’s coefficient of 0.837.

Covariates

In this study, some demographic information was reported and controlled as oblique variables: gender (male/female), age, ethnicity (Han, minority), place of residence (urban, rural), economic status (compared to peers: much better, better, similar, worse, much worse), whether the father worked outside the home for a long time (yes/no), whether the mother worked outside the home for a long time (yes/no), only child (yes/no), history of mental illness (yes/no), smoking (never, past, present), alcohol consumption (never, past, present), relationship (never, previous, present), somatic symptoms (yes/no). Moreover, we included all the demographic information and other affective problems as covariates when examing the mediating role of specific affective problems. We will control for traditional bullying victimization when analyzing the impact of cyberbullying victimization on suicidal ideation. Similarly, we will account for cyberbullying victimization while investigating the effects of traditional bullying victimization on suicidal ideation. This approach enables a thorough understanding of the complex relationships among these variables.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 26. We began by checking the study data for common method bias. Descriptive statistics were computed for the variables, with normally distributed data presented as means and standard deviations, while non-normally distributed data were described using quartiles. To examine differences across groups, we employed the t-test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and Kruskal-Wallis test Additionally, Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess relationships among the main variables.

For the mediation analysis, we utilized the Process 4.0 plug-in. The mediation model (Model 4) was tested while controlling for previously mentioned covariates. To evaluate the mediation effects and estimate 95% confidence intervals, we applied the bias-corrected percentile Bootstrap method with 5,000 replications. This approach provided a robust framework for understanding the mediating roles of affective problems in the relationship between bullying victimization and suicidal ideation among college students.

Results

Common method bias

The Harman one-factor test was conducted to assess common method bias within the collected data. The results showed a total of 24 factors with characteristics greater than 1, and the first factor accounted for 22.65% of the variance, which is significantly below the threshold of 40%. This suggests that our study does not suffer from common method bias.

Discussion

This study is the first empirical investigation among Chinese college students to examine whether different affective problems mediate the relationship between various forms of bullying victimization and suicidal ideation. Our findings reveal a direct effect of different forms of bullying victimization on the emergence of suicidal ideation among college students, which is consistent with previous research [40, 41]. Morover, we identified, significant correlations among the different types of bullying victimization, various affective problems, and suicidal ideation, thus supporting Hypothesis 1.

Without controlling for other affective problems, our analysis indicated that depression, anxiety, social anxiety, hopelessness, and psychiatric prodromal experience all served as mediators between different forms of bullying victimization and suicidal ideation. This outcome supports Hypothesis 2. However, after adjusting for other affective problems, we found that depression, anxiety, social anxiety, and hopelessness no longer mediated this relationship. In contrast, psychiatric prodromal experiences continued to show a significant mediating effect, providing partial support for Hypothesis 3.

Additionally, our results indicated that the prevalence of suicide ideation among college students was 9.3% of the total sample, which contrasts with the 16.4% prevalence reported by Shi et al. [42] for Chinese college students. This discrepancy suggests that, despite the data collection occurring during school closure due to the epidemic, the results were minimally influenced by the ongoing public health crisis.

The results of this study highlighted correlations between different forms of bullying victimization, different affective problems, and suicidal ideation among college students. Specifically, it was found that increased experience of bullying victimization were associated with a higher likelihood of developing affective problems, aligning with previous research findings [43]. Furthermore, the presence of both bullying victimization and affective problems was shown to be a strong predictor of suicidal ideation among college students [21, 44]. Present study has shown that individuals who endure more intense bullying victimization or severe emotional issues are at an elevated risk for experiencing suicidal ideation [45, 46].

When exploring the mediating effect of affective problem in the absence of controls for the effects of other affective problems, all five affective problems demonstrated mediation between bullying victimization and suicidal ideation. This supports a wealth of existing literature [47–49]. Notably, this study is the first to validate the mediating roles of hopelessness and psychotic-like experiences in the relationship between bullying victimization and suicidal ideation among college students, offering new insight into the development of suicidal ideation.

However, when controlling for other affective problems, a single affective issues was found to mediate the relationship between bullying victimization and suicidal ideation. The mediation model suggested that traditional bullying victimization may directly lead to the onset of depression and anxiety, but it does not appear to predict suicidal ideation directly, hopelessness itself has been identified as a predictor, of suicidal thoughts, consistent with Klonsky’s Three-Step Theory [29]. Moreover, the study revealed that social anxiety did not mediate the relationship between bullying victimization and suicidal ideation, potentially because social anxiety is characterized as help-seeking behavior associated with strong interpersonal expectations, which may not lead individuals to lose hope for life [50].

The mediation model results indicated that cyberbullying victimization is related to the development of depression, which occurs independently of suicidal ideation. while cyberbullying victimization doesn’t immediately related to hopelessness, these feelings are subsequently related to individuals’ suicidal ideation. Additionally,, the study found that anxiety and social anxiety did not significantly mediate the relationship between cyberbullying and suicidal ideation. This may be attributed to the inherently interpersonal nature of cyberbullying, which often involves reciprocal interactions. In contrast, traditional bullying tends to be more unidirectional and can lead to physical trauma, isolation, and loneliness [51, 52]. Anxiety and social anxiety may also be seen as help-seeking behaviors that carry strong interpersonal expectations, suggesting that individuals suffering from these issues may not easily lose hope or desire for life [53, 54].

The study further identified psychiatric profromal experiences as a mediator between the two forms of bullying victimization and suicidal ideation. This could be because these experiences encompass a range of emotional, behavioral and somatic response that collectively contribute to suicidal ideation. To understand these phenomena, we propose a new integrated emotion-response model of suicidal ideation, combining elements from Thomas Joiner’s Interpersonal Theory and Klonsky’s Three-Step Theory. We argue that an individual’s emotional state is not solely influenced by a single event, but rather arises from the cumulative impact of multiple emotions (illustrated in Fig. 3). Suicidal ideation may emerge from a complex interplay of emotional issues and somatic reactions or from the combination of emotional distress and feelings of hopelessness, as previously theorized. This theory is also supported by a recent empirical study that was published [55].

The integrated emotion-response model of suicidal ideation

However, this study does have limitations, including its cross-sectional and reliance on self-reported data. Future research should further investigate how suicidal ideation is specifically superimposed through ecological momentaryassessment (EMA) and longitudinal questionnaires. Sencondly, the current enumeration of affective problems in this study was not exhaustive, aspects such as sadness and grief warrant further exploration in subsequent studies.Thirdly, this study included a community sample of college students for analysis and took into account various demographic variables that could potentially affect the research outcomes. However, it is possible that different effects may exist in different populations, such as only children versus non-only children or clinical versus non-clinical samples. Future research is needed to provide further empirical support for the conclusions of this study through comparisons across different groups. Lastly, in this study, we employed cluster random sampling to collect questionnaires from a single school in South China, which may lead to sampling bias and insufficient sample representativeness. Caution should be exercised when interpreting the research results. Future studies could consider utilizing probability sampling methods or employing a more representative national sample, or conducting cross-cultural research to further validate the conclusions of this study.

Conclusions

In summary, this study uniquely established that psychotic-like experiences can mediate the relationship between various forms of bullying victimization and suicidal ideation among college students, regardless of whether other affective problems were controlled. Additionally, the findings challenge the misinterpreted found in many previous studies that suicidal ideation is merely the results of a single event or a singular emotion. Instead, it emerge from a complex interplay of multiple emotional and behavioral responses. This underscores the need for a more comprehensive understanding of an individual’s emotional challenges and somatic symptoms in the context of suicidal ideation. The results of the study will help educators and clinical researchers to gain a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying suicidal ideation in college students, further explore the important role of different emotional problems, and point to new directions for early prevention and early intervention of suicidal problems in college students.
