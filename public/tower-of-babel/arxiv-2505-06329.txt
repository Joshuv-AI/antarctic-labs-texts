On Unique Neighborhoods in Bipartite and Expander Graphs
Stefan Rass

Abstract
An undirected graph is said to have unique neighborhoods if any two distinct nodes have also distinct sets of neighbors. In this way, the connections of a node to other nodes can characterize a node like an “identity”, irrespectively of how nodes are named, as long as two nodes are distinguishable. We study the uniqueness of neighborhoods in (random) bipartite graphs, and expander graphs.

We will show the logical independence of unique neighborhoods from the value of the graph’s Cheeger constant. To this end, let us briefly recall the respective definitions.

Let

S

is defined as
h(G)=\min_{S\subset V:\left|S\right|\leq\left|V\right|/2}\frac{\left|\partial S\right|}{\left|S\right|}
. We call
G

(n,\varepsilon,d)
-expander

Simply consider the complete graph

For

Lemma 2
.

For every
\varepsilon>0

and a graph
G_{n}\in\mathrm{UNN}
of size
n

h(G_{n})<\varepsilon

Proof.

The sought graph is the circle graph

3

Lemma 3
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
there is some
n>1
and a graph
G_{n}\notin\mathrm{UNN}
of size
n
with a Cheeger constant
h(G_{n})\geq\varepsilon
.

Proof.

The sought graph is the complete bipartite graph
K_{n/2,n/2}
for even
n
. This is a well-known example of a Ramanujan-graph, whose spectral gap is largest possible; specifically, with the second-smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{2}
of the graph’s Laplacian, we have a diverging spectral gap, if we let

. The divergence of the Cheeger constant
h(K_{n/2,n/2})\to\infty
then follows from Cheeger’s inequality.
∎

Lemma 4
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
there is some
n>1
and a graph
G_{n}\notin\mathrm{UNN}
of size
n
with a Cheeger constant
h(G_{n})<\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Given
V\neq\emptyset
, the graph
G=(V,\emptyset)
without edges is trivially not a
UNN
, since all neighborhoods are identically

h(G)=0<\varepsilon

An alternative argument working with a connected graph is the following: fix any even integer

and take the circle graph
C_{n-2}=(V,E)

Theorem 2
.

Let
G
be a graph. Whether

While the counterexamples in Table

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mathcal{G}(N,\varepsilon,d)
be an expander-family. Then, the statements
G\in\mathcal{G}
and
G\in\acs{UNN}
are logically independent.

Proof.

We construct expander-families that have, or do not have, the
UNN
property, starting with expander families whose families are all
UNN
, and later modifying them to remain expanders, but losing the
UNN
property.

Since Lemma
5
’s assertion about the preservation of the
UNN
property upon 2-lifts made no particular assumptions on the underlying signing, it will work for all choices of
s
. Hence, the known constructions of expander families as in
[
1
]
remain doable, and we directly get an expander family composed of all
UNN
.

To construct an expander that is no longer a
UNN
, consider an
(n,\varepsilon,d/2)
-expander
G
. If
G\notin\mathrm{UNN}
already, then we are finished. Otherwise, choose any two distinct nodes
x,y
in
G
, and add edges from
x
to
\text{nb}(y)
and from
y
to
\text{nb}(x)
. Call the resulting graph
G^{\prime}
. By construction, it is not a
UNN
, but still an expander: The Cheeger constant cannot decrease upon this change, since any subset of nodes will have at least as many, if not more, outgoing edges after this change, making
h(G^{\prime})\geq h(G)\geq\varepsilon
. Moreover, the maximum degree in
G^{\prime}
is no more than twice the degree as in
G
, since every two nodes in
G
have at most
d/2
neighbors, and even if the neighborhoods were disjoint (not just distinct), adding another
d/2
many edges to both of them is enough to make the neighborhoods non-unique. This gives a
(n,\varepsilon,d)
-expander
G^{\prime}\notin\mathrm{UNN}
, and can be repeated for all sizes
n
, while keeping
\varepsilon
and
d
constant.

∎
