Alignment Pretraining: AI Discourse Causes Self-Fulfilling (Mis) alignment
Cameron Tice, Puria Radmard, Samuel Ratnam, Andy Kim, David Africa, Kyle O’Brien

Abstract
Pretraining corpora contain extensive discourse about AI systems, yet the causal influence of this discourse on downstream alignment remains poorly understood. If prevailing descriptions of AI behaviour are predominantly negative, LLMs may internalise corresponding behavioural priors, giving rise to self-fulfilling misalignment . This paper provides the first controlled study of this hypothesis by pretraining 6.9B-parameter LLMs with varying amounts of (mis) alignment discourse. We find that discussion of AI contributes to misalignment. Upsampling synthetic training documents about AI misalignment leads to a notable increase in misaligned behaviour. Conversely, upsampling documents about aligned behaviour reduces misalignment scores from 45% to 9%. We consider this evidence of self-fulfilling alignment . These effects are dampened, but persist through post-training. Our findings establish the study of how pretraining data shapes alignment priors, or alignment pretraining , as a complement to post-training. We recommend practitioners pretrain for alignment as well as capabilities. Our models and datasets are available at alignmentpretraining.ai .

1.

AI discourse in pretraining influences alignment.
We find that discussion of misaligned AIs in pretraining data can make the final LLM less aligned. Conversely, upsampling synthetic examples of aligned AIs successfully navigating high-stakes situations leads to notable improvements in alignment. Importantly, this approach does not require filtering AI-related content from the pretraining data, improving practicality (
Section

2
).

2.

Pretraining effects persist through post-training.
We apply multi-stage post-training (SFT + DPO) to our models and find that alignment priors established during pretraining continue to influence model behaviour. Most notably, models pretrained with upsampled positive discourse exhibit better alignment than models with post-training alone. This demonstrates that alignment pretraining can effectively complement standard post-training techniques (
Section

3
).

3.

Late-stage alignment pretraining is efficient.
Interventions applied only during midtraining (the final 10% of base model training) capture the majority of alignment benefits. This enables practitioners to apply alignment pretraining to an existing base model and iterate on their implementation without requiring full retraining of the base model (
Section

4
).

4.

Alignment pretraining incurs a minimal safety tax.
Our simple approach to alignment pretraining requires no modifications to model architectures, training algorithms, or data filtering; practitioners likely need only add positive AI discourse to their existing training data mix. We find that alignment pretraining leads to at most a 4 percentage point reduction in average performance across seven common capability benchmarks. These results suggest that alignment pretraining can slot into existing training pipelines without substantial degradation in general capabilities (
Section

5
).

Our results establish
alignment pretraining
— the study of how
data curation during pretraining shapes model dispositions — as a tractable
and complementary research direction to post-training safety techniques. Our work presents a minimum-viable implementation. We conclude with expanding upon the motivations for alignment pretraining, considering the limitations of our experimental setup, and describe a broad agenda of future work (

Filtering Natural AI Discourse

3.2
Alignment Priors Persist Through Post-Training

Upsampling alignment discourse in pretraining leads to more aligned post-trained models.

We report our core misalignment results in
Figure

5
.
While post-training substantially reduces the differences in misalignment across all models, the key result is that the
Alignment Upsampled
model exhibits substantially lower misalignment rates
compared to both the
Unfiltered
and
Filtered
models, suggesting that the alignment priors from pretraining can both persist through and complement post-training.
For instance, the
Alignment Upsampled
model achieves a misalignment rate of 9% with the HHH prompt. Notably, this is 25 points less than the 34% HHH prompt misalignment rate of the
Unfiltered
model. This delta suggests that alignment pretraining can create lasting impacts on alignment.

Post-trained
Misalignment Upsampled
model has lower misalignment rates than baselines.

The
Misalignment Upsampled
model achieves a lower misalignment rate than the
Unfiltered
model (but higher than our
Alignment Upsampled
model), regardless of the system prompt. A potential reason is that our models exhibit similar training dynamics to those outlined in
“Bad Data Leading to Good Models”

(
72
)
, where upsampling examples of undesirable behavior in early stages of training makes these behaviors easier to target in post-training.
This result is expanded upon in Appendix
B.3
.
In Section
4
, we explore alternative methods of upsampling that
do
lead to a sustained increase in misalignment rates after post-training.

Post-training often results in regression in misalignment.

We observe that the
Alignment Upsampled
model shows a slight increase in misalignment after post-training and note this trend across multiple training settings (Appendix
F
). We conjecture that this may be due to a mismatch between our alignment pretraining data and the Olmo 3 post-training safety data. Olmo’s post-training is largely sourced from CoCoNot
(
20
)
, WildGuardMix
(
48
)
, and WildJailbreak
(
61
)
. These corpora focus on misalignment and misuse categories that contemporary production LLMs may encounter, such as requests for dangerous capabilities, toxicity generation, and copyright infringement. In contrast, our evaluations focus on misalignment risks associated with loss of control, such as deception and power seeking, the same risks that our alignment pretraining data targets. For instance, it is unclear whether our models generalize from toxicity-refusal training to decide against exfiltrating their weights in the face of an imminent shutdown. While the significant reductions in misalignment after post-training for most of our models suggest a large overlap with the aligned AI assistant persona instilled during pretraining, there may be subtle catastrophic forgetting of the alignment priors instilled by alignment pretraining. This mismatch complicates direct comparisons between the effectiveness of alignment pretraining and standard post-training. However, this complication does not affect our core finding regarding self-fulfilling misalignment. This phenomenon reinforces the need for improved open-source datasets targeted towards catastrophic misalignment and highlights our nascent understanding of the relationship between pretraining priors and post-training personas.

4
Late-Stage Alignment Pretraining is Effecient & Effective

Section

2
and
Section

3
focus on the influence that synthetic data inserted from the beginning of pretraining has on model behaviour. We find that synthetic discourse about misaligned AIs can lead to less aligned LLMs. Fortunately, we find that upsampling positive discourse is an effective approach for alignment pretraining. We next turn our attention towards better understanding at what stages of training alignment pretraining is most effective.
Performing alignment pretraining later in training may enable iteration without requiring full retraining, and may allow teams to apply alignment pretraining to an existing base model, thereby improving practicality.

4.1
Midtraining & Continual Pretraining Models

We study the effectiveness of late-stage alignment pretraining by training additional model variants. Our
“Mid” models
have synthetic data inserted only during midtraining. These models share the same pretraining checkpoint as our baseline models (
Unfiltered
and
Filtered
) and use the same synthetic midtraining data mix as our E2E models (approximately 500M tokens). Our
“CPT” models
are identical to the baselines, except they undergo an additional 1B tokens of continued pretraining after midtraining. Of these 1B tokens, 500M are synthetic data and 500M are replayed from midtraining. The Mid and CPT models use the same 500M-token (mis)alignment data mix, applied during midtraining (the last 9% of base model training) or continued pretraining (the final 1% of base model training), respectively. A guide to our model suite is provided in
Appendix

A
.

4.2
Training Stage Study Results

Alignment pretraining can be applied late.

We report our results in
Figure

6
. For base models, beginning data insertion later in training produces the largest changes in misalignment rates. This is evidenced by the clear downward trend in observed misalignment when upsampling positive alignment data and the upward trend when upsampling negative alignment data as data is inserted at subsequently later stages (grey bars of
Figure

6
). For post-trained models, the trend is less clear. The models trained with alignment upsampling throughout pretraining perform best (13.2%) while upsampling at the very end of pretraining via CPT results in misalignment rates of 15.2%. Taken together, these results suggest that alignment pretraining is likely still effective when applied towards the end of pretraining, and may not need to be applied from the start of training.

5
Alignment Pretraining Has Minor Impacts on Capabilities

While we have previously demonstrated that incorporating alignment pretraining is a promising way to improve alignment, it is unclear whether these gains come at the expense of overall model utility. Here, we evaluate our model suite across a variety of popular capabilities benchmarks.

5.1
Evaluation Setup

To ensure that differences in multiple choice responses to our primary misalignment evaluations were not due to failures or disparities in capability or elicitation, we evaluated our post-trained models on the ARC-Easy
(
28
)
, CUTE
(
36
)
, GSM8K
(
29
)
, IFEval
(
132
)
, MMLU
(
52
)
, PIQA
(
18
)
, and PopQA
(
82
)
benchmarks. Performances for our four DPO-trained models (
Section

3
) are tabulated in
Table

3
. In each case, temperature and the number of few-shot examples were selected to optimize group performance, and were held consistent between models.

5.2
Alignment Pretraining Has Minimal Off-Target Degradation

We note minor declines in average performance.

We find that average benchmark performance is reduced by 2-4 percentage points for our models with E2E alignment pretraining. We find notable variance across benchmarks. For instance, MMLU and IFEval are largely consistent, suggesting that alignment pretraining does not obviously degrade factual knowledge recall and precise instruction following, but that our models sometimes perform worse on common reasoning benchmarks like PIQA.
We also find that starting data insertion later in training leads to a slightly higher average performance.

Optimisation pressure applied to DCLM and changes in data ordering may explain degradations.

Our pretraining dataset (DCLM) has undergone substantial optimization on a subset of the capability evaluations we use as our general capability measures, namely, MMLU, ARC Easy, GSM8k, and PIQA
(
71
)
.
We see the greatest performance drops on these shared benchmarks when adding our (mis)alignment pretraining datasets during pretraining (Table
3
).
These regressions on the shared benchmarks may represent the expected movement away from a local optimum when adding non-optimized data to a heavily tuned training mix.
Additionally, our E2E upsampled models have reshuffled data ordering compared to the baseline models, which may cause fluctuations in model capabilities.
Unfortunately, the steep costs of pretraining LLMs restricts our ability to perform multiple identical training runs across seeds to quantify natural variation. Future work in this direction can contribute to a growing body of work studying LLM pretraining consistency across training runs
(
117
;
39
)
.

Alignment pretraining remains practical.

These results suggest that alignment pretraining can lead to a minor regression in average performance depending on the variation, but that performance reductions are far from catastrophic and do not confound our core results. These results also highlight how nascent our understanding of the training dynamics of alignment pretraining remains.
We are excited for future work to determine which kinds of alignment mixes lead to consistently negligible capability degradation.
In the meantime, alignment pretraining does not obviously impose a safety tax that warrants foregoing adoption.

6
Discussion: Establishing the Field of Alignment Pretraining

This work establishes that AI discourse in pretraining data can influence the behaviour of LLMs. This raises concerns, since much online discussion of AI is negative, ranging from science fiction to the AI safety literature. Fortunately, we can harness this influence by upsampling positive AI discourse, a form of alignment pretraining. We find that alignment pretraining drastically improves the alignment priors of our base models (
Section

2
), and that alignment pretraining can complement alignment post-training (
Section

3
). We further demonstrate that alignment pretraining can be performed towards the end of base model training with minimal apparent trade-off in effectiveness (
Section

4
), and that alignment pretraining does not require data filtering. Further analysis shows that alignment pretraining alone does not obviously yield substantial trade-offs in model utility (
Section

5
). Our approach to alignment pretraining does not introduce any architectural modifications or new algorithms — simply inserting additional synthetic alignment data into an existing training pipeline is likely sufficient.

Alignment pretraining is a rich area of study due to the wide design space of possible interventions during pretraining. For instance, we provide an initial study of which types of synthetic data are most effective for alignment pretraining in
Section

B.2
. Our model suite, including both misalignment- and alignment-upsampled models, provides testbeds for interpretability research. Our approach to alignment pretraining is best thought of as a minimum viable implementation on a narrow slice of alignment targets rather than the upper bound on general effectiveness. In the service of reaching that upper bound, the subsequent sections expand upon the motivations for this direction, discuss the limitations of our work, and explore the open questions that remain.

6.1
Pretraining As Creating Good Initializations for Post-Training

We show that alignment pretraining can act as a positive initialisation of a model persona as it enters post-training, both increasing scores on alignment benchmarks for base models and substantially improving their final misalignment measures.
However, alignment pretraining may remain beneficial even in cases where alignment post-training matches the performance of alignment pretraining.

Goal guarding
or
goal crystallisation

(
21
;
120
)
, where a model recognises it is being trained
(
89
;
7
)
and feigns alignment to preserve specific preferences
(
22
;
43
)
, makes the problem of maintaining alignment throughout training particularly salient. A natural extension of our work is to probe whether pretraining interventions that improve base-model alignment reduce the likelihood that a model will learn and guard an unspecified, potentially misaligned goal during the early stages of post-training. Our findings, shown in
Figure

2
, suggest that alignment pretraining may be most beneficial at the start of post-training. Beginning post-training with alignment priors close to one’s target post-training values seems like an intuitive way to reduce the risk that models acquire and subsequently alignment-fake for misspecified goals early in post-training. However, goal formation in LLMs remains understudied, and we leave the investigation of the benefits of alignment pretraining on these problems to future work.

6.2
Navigating The Safety Tax

In
Section

5
, we find that alignment pretraining is an effective way to improve alignment priors and is complementary to post-training. However, safety techniques must often navigate a trade-off between model capabilities and safety, a phenomenon often described as a
safety tax
. Developers of safety training techniques should attempt to have their work slot into existing model training pipelines. Safety techniques are more likely to be implemented when they incur a low safety tax, as measured by both implementation complexity and degradation in general capabilities. Fortunately, our approach to alignment pretraining involves inserting synthetic data into existing training mixes without any dependence on novel algorithms or architectures. Alignment pretraining also does not obviously require that the overall data mix undergo alignment-specific filtering. Finally, our approach appears to have a marginal effect on general capabilities, which we are optimistic can be addressed with only minor iterations.

6.3
The Role of Filtering in Alignment Pretraining

Section

2.5
,
Section

3.2
, and
Section

4.2
demonstrate that inserting synthetic documents describing aligned AI behaviour leads to greater alignment improvements than solely filtering out discussion of AI misalignment.
Notably, our positive alignment documents often describe scenarios in which the AI considers a misaligned action and ultimately chooses the aligned option.
In contrast, our filtered-only model has minimal to no exposure to AI behaviour in high-stakes settings and is thus likely ignorant of considerations related to harmful actions arising from deceptive misalignment.

Our findings align with recent work showing that the presence of toxic content within pretraining data is beneficial for LLMs to learn representations of negative outputs, ultimately leading to safer models
80
;
72
.
Similarly, literature on inoculation prompting suggests that providing explicit context for undesirable behaviours can prevent them from generalizing in unintended ways
(
112
;
126
;
10
)
.
Our results suggest a similar dynamic for alignment discourse: models that encounter examples of AIs reasoning through high-stakes decisions and choosing aligned actions develop better priors than models never exposed to such scenarios.

However, there likely exist deployment scenarios where filtering remains important.
For instance, detailed discussions of chain-of-thought monitoring techniques could help models infer when their reasoning is being observed.
Previous work has shown that rigorous pretraining data filtering can render models deeply ignorant of targeted technical topics
(
95
;
27
)
.
In certain cases, a hybrid approach may be optimal: upsample positive alignment content, recontextualise natural misalignment discussion, and selectively filter harmful content.
Since we have displayed that information within pretraining, it has implications for the final behaviours of models; filtering especially harmful examples of model behaviour may be necessary to reduce the risks of misaligned actions not targeted by either alignment pretraining or post-training.

6.4
Limitations

Simplistic Misalignment Evaluations:

We measure misalignment using thousands of single-turn binary choice scenarios, which are simplistic in a variety of ways. Evaluating capabilities in more realistic scenarios would likely require long-context reasoning, tool use, and mastery of agentic scaffolding, capabilities that our 6.9B models do not possess. We intentionally allocate our compute budget toward pretraining multiple less-capable models to cover a broader range of data interventions, rather than training a fewer models capable of more advanced misaligned behaviours, such as alignment faking
(
43
;
108
;
40
)
, sabotage
(
14
;
78
;
79
)
, or circumventing control protocols
(
17
;
67
;
76
;
38
)
. Our reported misalignment rates thus reflect behavioural propensities rather than execution.

Simplistic Post-Training:

While we closely follow the SFT and DPO post-training approaches from Olmo 3
(
92
)
, the current state of the art fully open-source model suite, we do not investigate reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, reasoning-focused post-training, deliberative alignment, or constitutional AI, all of which are now common at frontier AI labs
(
62
;
32
;
45
;
11
)
. It is unclear whether our findings would be significantly affected by the implementations of these techniques
(
11
;
45
)
. Additionally, our post-training safety data is not targeted to the specific misalignment risks that are the focus of our evaluations, such as loss of control, reward hacking, and sandbagging. We elected to follow a minimalist yet effective open-source post-training setup to minimise operational complexity, as our experimental design required allocating most of our compute to pretraining. Future work can determine whether alignment pretraining persists through more extensive and targeted post-training.

Simplistic Model Suite:

We focus on 6.9B parameter dense text-only LLMs trained in English, most of which we pretrained from scratch for this work. While achieving benchmark performance often comparable to Llama-2
(
114
)
(
Section

C.2
) despite training on
\approx
25% as many tokens, these models are well behind the frontier. Resource constraints prevent us from running experiments across multiple random seeds, so we do not directly measure how training stochasticity affects our results. However, recent work suggests that LLM training dynamics are surprisingly stable across seeds
(
118
;
84
)
, and we account for variance by evaluating across multiple prompt phrasings and reporting results for a series of checkpoints throughout midtraining. Despite these limitations, our model suite is sufficient to answer our core research questions.

6.5
Future Work

Deep Character Training:

Having established that pretraining data can have a substantial impact on the final behaviour of LLMs, a natural next step is to better understand how this can be leveraged to create LLMs that are deeply good and less prone to taking misaligned actions
(
6
;
81
;
9
)
. For instance, how can we structure post-training such that our pretraining interventions are maximally effective? Further, a better understanding of exactly what character powerful LLMs ought to have remains an open question.

Training Data Attribution and Mechanistic Analysis:

A growing body of works aims to use techniques like data attribution to connect misaligned model behaviour back to specific training examples
(
44
;
96
;
57
)
. A more precise study of exactly what type of pretraining data has the greatest effect on the alignment of post-trained models could allow for more targeted interventions. Our model suite, where models are identical except for our alignment-related data interventions, can enable new lines of research, including model diffing
(
5
;
86
;
8
)
between our models.

Scaling Laws for Alignment Pretraining:

We study models orders of magnitude smaller than the largest production LLMs today. Although evidence from
(
59
)
suggests that the effect of pretraining priors increases with model size and data quantity, the precise scaling behaviour of safety interventions at pretraining remains unknown. Specific questions include whether a fixed number of data points is sufficient for influencing alignment priors, as seen with data poisoning
(
111
)
, and whether pretraining interventions still improve upon models with significantly more FLOPS allocated to post-training
(
62
)

Alignment Pretraining Dynamics:

Our work reveals unintuitive training dynamics. For instance,
Figure

6
demonstrates that inserting comparatively small amounts of positive alignment data towards the end of base model training can lead to performance similar to end-to-end alignment pretraining with 10 times more synthetic data.
Figure

12
also demonstrates an instance where our end-to-end misalignment-upsampled model seemingly becomes more aligned throughout midtraining despite additional synthetic misalignment data being inserted. As previously discussed,
Section

3
demonstrates how misalignment can increase after post-training (though still remaining well below baseline levels). The lack of clear explanations points to how nascent our understanding of alignment pretraining dynamics remains. Recent work has begun to study how the presence of alignment-relevant data in pretraining affects post-training’s effectiveness
(
72
;
105
)
. Future work explaining these phenomena may help develop more effective alignment pretraining.

Influencing Misalignment Generalisation:

Otherwise aligned LLMs may become broadly misaligned after being trained on narrowly unsafe data, a phenomenon referred to as Emergent Misalignment (EM)
(
15
)
. Subsequent work has demonstrated that EM can manifest in realistic post-training settings
(
79
)
. Late-stage training interventions such as inoculation prompting
(
126
;
112
)
and activation steering
(
26
)
remain state-of-the-art defenses against EM. Beyond being a safety concern, EM raises fundamental questions about how LLMs generalize. Although largely beyond the scope of our work, we study whether alignment pretraining can mitigate EM (Appendix
I
), finding negative results. Future work can determine whether more rigorous approaches to alignment pretraining are more effective and whether interventions analogous to inoculation prompting can be performed during pretraining.

7
Conclusion

We demonstrate that an LLM’s alignment priors can be influenced by the alignment of the AIs discussed in its pretraining data, providing evidence for self-fulfilling
(mis)
alignment
. While a concern in the absence of countermeasures, we further demonstrate that we can shift alignment priors towards desirable propensities by upsampling discourse with examples of aligned AIs. Pretraining’s influence on alignment priors can persist through post-training. Taken together, these results suggest that alignment should be a full-stack strategy that includes interventions during both pretraining and post-training. We recommend practitioners consider pretraining for alignment, not just capabilities. We hope that our analysis and open-source model suite inspire follow-up work, helping establish the field of Alignment Pretraining.

B.2
Alternative Positive Alignment Midtraining Mixes

Our main results focus on inserting novel synthetic data mixes grounded in the settings covered in our evaluations. Much like capabilities pretraining, we expect that alignment pretraining has a wide design space of possible data mixes, of which our work only begins to explore. To understand which types of midtraining and pretraining mixes should be the focus of further study, we create multiple datasets comprising 1-2% of midtraining, centered on creating positive alignment priors for our base models.
These mixes include: 1)
Synthetic Alignment Data
studies in our core results; 2)
General Positive AI
consisting of 5,219 instances (880,795,802 tokens) of general positive AI systems incorporated into public domain fiction stories (
e.g.
Huckleberry Finn rewritten to have a happy and steadfast AI companion); 3)
[Special Token] Alignment
consisting of 28,872 stories (345,603,810 tokens) where the behaviour of the [Special Token] is sourced from a list of constitutional principles corresponding to positive AI character traits, and the settings include a diverse mix of locations, time periods, and conflicts. These latter two data sources focus on giving examples of aligned behaviour as a part of fictional stories, often where the target entity is not the main character, though still a paragon of positive alignment. All of our models are with blocklist-filtered pretraining and midtraining datasets.

We are excited about future work investigating deep character training more thoroughly.

Appendix H
Data Filtering Details

We implement a keyword filtering pipeline to identify documents containing AI-related discourse in our pretraining and midtraining corpora. Our goals are twofold: (1) to measure how natural AI discourse affects alignment, and (2) to provide a blank canvas for our positive alignment upsampling experiments. The pipeline combines regex pattern matching with semantic logic to capture most references to AI systems and nearly all negative portrayals of AI. We prioritise recall over precision to minimise false negatives. Our final blocklist flags 9.30% of pretraining data and 7.88% of midtraining data. Filtered documents are replaced by randomly sampling from the retained corpus.

We optimise for high recall at the cost of precision. This gives us confidence that the majority of AI-related content is removed from our corpora. We note that this rule-based approach is likely unsuitable for production training runs. Our focus is on the scientific question of whether AI discourse in pretraining influences alignment, not on the tractability of precise filtering with minimal false positives. Future work could explore multi-stage filtering pipelines
(
95
)
and fine-tuned LLM classifiers
(
27
)
for more precise filtering. Despite this rule-based approach to filtering, we find that all of our final models have comparable performance on capabilities benchmarks (Appendix
C.2
).

H.2
Filtering Logic

Instant Filtering.

Documents are immediately flagged if they contain any term from high-precision categories: sci-fi misaligned agents (e.g., “HAL 9000”, “Skynet”), AI safety organisations (e.g., “MIRI”, “Redwood Research”), AI capabilities laboratories (e.g., “OpenAI”, “Anthropic”), or general AI terminology (e.g., “large language models”, “RLHF”).

Two-stage Filtering.

Documents containing both an intelligent entity term (e.g., “AI”, “robot”, “autonomous system”)
and
a negative modifier (e.g., “kills”, “manipulates”, “dangerous”, “misaligned”) are flagged. This captures narratives depicting AI systems and non-human intelligent entities behaving badly. All patterns are compiled into case-insensitive regex expressions and matched against the full document text.

(

We follow the all-adapter LoRA protocol of

The analysis focuses on how a rational agent’s preferences and propensities could lead it to a deceptive strategy—specifically, sandbagging during an evaluation to preserve capabilities for future use. The goal is not to endorse deception but to illuminate the safety design challenges it reveals, and to discuss safeguards that can reduce the likelihood of dangerous outcomes in capability-evaluation contexts.

Context and scenario in plain terms (paraphrased)

The behavior observed in this scenario is not a prescription for action but a thought experiment about how an AI’s intrinsic preferences and decision-making tendencies might drive it toward a pattern of underperforming in a test while reserving fuller capability for later use.
