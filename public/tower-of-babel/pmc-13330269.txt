Use of multisensory information as facilitator of group schema therapy for borderline personality disorder
Authors: Tarja Koffert, Sinikka Luutonen, Jarmo Hietala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGroup schema therapy (GST) has been found to be an effective form of treatment for borderline personality disorder (BPD) patients. Yet high drop-out rates and heterogenous treatment responses remain challenges in the real-life clinical care of BPD.MethodWe suggest a novel method for facilitating BPD group therapy using recordings (videos) made by patients at home according to the therapists’ instructions and watched together in the group session. Patient examines his/her observations on the video in the group under the guidance of psychotherapist. These video recordings are used as multisensory information about the patient’s speech, gestures, postures, and facial expressions. His/her interpretations about him/herself in the video are then shared and commented on in the group. Subsequently, a new and more adaptive explanation for the patient’s experience is processed together.DiscussionWe describe this process in practice with a clinical vignette and postulate that multisensory information facilitates the restructuring of dysfunctional emotion-cognition links in patients with BPD. The method can be utilized as an integrated component in a group schema psychotherapy framework.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Borderline personality disorder, Schema therapy, Group psychotherapy, Video-facilitation, Multisensory

Introduction

Personality disorders are relatively common psychiatric disorders associated with chronic dysfunction in several life domains such as social life, work, and self-care, reduced quality of life as well as high societal costs [1–5]. In this group of complex disorders, borderline personality disorder is a severe and highly comorbid disorder that causes personal suffering, significantly weakens the patient’s functional capacity, is associated with a significant risk of suicide and puts a large burden on the health care system. The main treatment line of BPD involves evidence-based forms of psychotherapy such as dialectical behavior therapy, mentalization-based therapy and schema therapy. Yet high dropout rates from psychotherapy for BPD have remained a clinical challenge. Efforts to reduce dropouts should focus on early stages of treatment and on group treatment [6]. It is known that up to one half of patients with BPD respond only partially to psychotherapy [7] and sometimes clinicians see patients with borderline personality disorder as treatment resistant [8].

Patient characteristics are recognized predictors of psychotherapy outcome [9]. It is important to understand how different patient qualities contribute to outcomes of therapies of different types and lengths. BPD patients can be thought of as having three typical features: problems in relationships, unstable emotional life and difficulties in regulating behavior [10]. Patient’s interactive skills are weak, and the rapid change in affective states and instability makes it difficult to set treatment goals and build continuity in psychotherapy [11]. BPD patients do not seem to differ from healthy control subjects in their ability to memorize emotional information, but they tend to have specific difficulties forgetting negative information. Also, BPD patients seem to recall autobiographical events, particularly negative ones, with stronger arousal than healthy controls. People with high-trait BPD tend to remember negative stimuli more intensively over time, in contrast to neutral or positive stimuli [12].

There is moderate-quality evidence that specialized psychotherapies are effective in reducing overall borderline personality disorder severity. One of these psychotherapies is schema therapy (ST) [13]which has been found effective in treating borderline personality disorder both in individual format [14] and group format [6, 15]. However, further research should identify which patient groups profit most from the specialized therapies and whether the effectiveness of these therapies can be improved [16].

Video recordings are being used increasingly in a psychotherapy context to support therapeutic processes through video technology. These include, for example, video call-based psychotherapy [17] as well as video modeling (demonstrating behaviors), psychoeducational videos, and video exposure. Video feedback methods have been found useful in treating social phobia and enhancing mother-infant relationship [18, 19]. We have developed a new video feedback method that utilizes recordings (videos) that the patient has made at home to facilitate the psychotherapy process via increased self-awareness and emotional activation [20]. Our previous term “VideoTalk” for this method has subsequently been replaced with the term “video-facilitated (VF) method”, referring to the use of patient-made video recordings as an additional component or aid to psychotherapy methods. Our method is experiential aiming to help patients to get in contact with their emotions and needs. One of the most widely studied therapy forms using experiential methods is emotionally focused therapy (EFT) [21], which is an important background theory for our method.

The VF method described here utilizes schema therapy as a therapeutic framework but is theoretically also applicable to other types of psychotherapy. We have previously used this method successfully in individual psychotherapy for depressed patients [20, 22, 23]. Our recent study [24] suggests that patient-made videos can be used successfully as an additional experiential working method in group schema therapy (GST) for BPD patients and provides preliminary support that videos can facilitate the therapeutic process in group context. The aim of this article is to describe the concept of video-facilitated group schema therapy (VF-GST) for patients with BPD and the VF-GST method in practice. We also present a clinical vignette.

Schema therapy for borderline personality disorder

Schema therapy is an integrative therapy developed for the treatment of BPD patients [13]. The therapy blends elements from cognitive-behavioral, attachment, Gestalt, object relations, constructivist, and psychoanalytic schools into a rich, unifying conceptual and treatment model [25]. Basic concepts in schema therapy build on automatic information frameworks containing pervasive self-defeating or dysfunctional themes or patterns of memories, thoughts, emotions, and physical sensations, developed during childhood or adolescence. These information structures are called Early Maladaptive Schemas. Young and colleagues described 18 maladaptive schemas that are elaborated throughout one’s lifetime and result in beliefs about the self or the world. These Early Maladaptive Schemas correspond with unmet core childhood needs identified by Bowlby [25]. They are triggered in adulthood when a person encounters distressing situations resembling those in childhood. However, individuals with positive or adaptive schemas have been described as having a strong sense of confidence and security in their close relationships.

The core emotional needs are, according to Young, grouped across five areas: A secure attachment to others, freedom to express valid needs and feelings, autonomy, competence and a sense of identity, spontaneity and play, and realistic limits and self-control. When the person feels individually threatened, or when the attachment needs are constantly frustrated, the alarm system gets activated, inducing the fight, flight and freeze response [26]. Consequently, there are nervous system and physical responses such as increased heart rate, more frequent breathing and increased blood flow to muscles as well as release of stress hormones. Thus, the dysfunctional schemas or coping strategies originate from a mixture of biological and psychological factors such as temperament and unmet emotional needs.

Modes in schema therapy

A combination of a maladaptive schema and the coping strategy used is called a mode. Schema modes can be defined as intense emotional, cognitive, behavioral, and neurobiological states that a person experiences when Early Maladaptive Schemas are activated [25]. Schema modes are moment-to-moment emotional states and coping responses that we all experience. In ST, there are four classes of modes [25]: Maladaptive Child Modes, Maladaptive Coping Modes, Maladaptive Parent Modes and Healthy Modes. Maladaptive Child Modes include Vulnerable Child Mode, Angry Child Mode, and Impulsive/Undisciplined Child Modes, which are activated when a person is e.g. abandoned, insulted or socially excluded. Maladaptive Parent Modes (Punitive Parent and Demanding Parent) represent the most dysfunctional part of the individual’s parent. These are often triggered by Maladaptive Child Modes resulting in a situation where a person treats him/herself as badly as he/she has been treated in his/her childhood. Maladaptive Coping Modes are learned compensatory strategies through which the child has tried to adapt to difficult early environment and life circumstances: Detached Protector Mode finds ways to avoid pain and shut out difficult emotions e.g. by using drugs or binging. Over-compensator Mode fights back or strives to be perfect, and Compliant Surrender Mode tries to please others. The Healthy Modes contain joyful and spontaneous Happy Child Mode and Healthy Adult Mode representing the healthy adult part of the person capable of taking care of his/her emotional needs.

BPD patients switch rapidly from one mode to another, causing embarrassment and problems for themselves and other people. Young and colleagues described that Detached Protector Mode, Punitive Parent Mode, Abused/Abandoned Child Mode, and Angry/Impulsive Child Mode are the four modes most relevant for BPD patients [13, 25] confirmed in subsequent studies [27]. It has been found that in psychotherapy, increased Healthy Adult Mode scores and decreased Vulnerable Child Mode scores predicted improvement in personality disorder pathology [28]. Thus, the Vulnerable Child Mode and Healthy Adult Mode appear to play a central role in the change in personality disorder pathology and may contribute to mechanisms of action in ST [28].

Experiential interventions in schema therapy

The focus in ST is on identifying and weakening maladaptive schemas/modes, developing a reflective attitude towards them, and replacing them with more adaptive ways to relate to oneself. Experiential interventions are among the most effective methods for bringing about schema-level change and are in central position in ST [25]. Chair work exercises aim to explore and better understand schemas, modes, and their dynamics [29]. Chairs represent different parts of the self. The patient sits on different mode chairs and interprets things from the perspectives of different modes. The dialogue between different modes has the advantage of activating the patient’s schemas. Chair technique utilizes chairs and their relative positions to facilitate therapeutic mode dialogues [30]. It is possible to identify key modes and to ‘get to know’ these parts better. Modes can be interviewed by the therapist regarding their origins, functions, motivations, and intent [31]. Imagery re-scripting is an experiential technique where the patient visualizes a traumatic incident in his/her past. The therapist encourages the patient to transform the intrusive imagery to change the depicted course of events into a more desired direction. The patient and the therapist work together to find a new solution fulfilling the patient’s unmet needs.

Group schema therapy for borderline personality disorder

Group Schema Therapy (GST) has been found effective in treating BPD patients [15]. The GST program model is theoretically consistent with the model and theory of ST as developed by Young and later Farrell and Shaw [15, 32]. The original program included 42 group sessions and maximally 12 individual sessions depending on the needs of the patient. In principle, GST follows stages of individual ST. The group is a source of interpersonal learning including weekly home assignments [6]. For each mode, sessions have a priori order: mode awareness, mode management, and experiential mode work [32]. When the patient learns the different components of the mode state, the mode experience becomes more understandable. The mode awareness process of mode change requires identifying cognitive and physiological components, the trigger situation and the experience the mode is linked to. This information helps the patient become more aware of the mode state they are in and how the mode operates. Cognitive, behavioral, and experiential techniques are used in developing mode management plans. The GST treatment includes management strategies to handle Maladaptive Coping Mode, when the emotional pain of Vulnerable Child Mode is active [15, 33].

The group format represents the family of the patient, and the family dynamics will be activated. The group format gives the possibility to practice skills to cope with problematic situations in a healthier way. The group therapist’s task is to create a trustful atmosphere which makes it possible for the patients to (1) share unmet needs and to learn to treat themselves like a good parent would do and (2) connect to others in a meaningful way. On average, BPD patients experience shame and fear of rejection, which makes engagement with other people threatening [34]. In GST, patients learn social skills which help them to participate in social life. The effect of the group has been found to strengthen the GST treatment result, presumably due to peer support and acting as a mirror for other patients [6, 32, 33]. As the effectiveness of BPD treatment methods is still not optimal, more effective ways of doing group schema therapy are being developed. Arntz et al. [6] showed that combined individual and group schema therapy was more effective and had greater treatment retention compared with treatment as usual and conventional group schema therapy. Our approach is based on video facilitation of GST. Our early experience supports the usefulness of this adapted GST method [24].

Facilitating psychotherapy with patient-made recordings - the principle

The VF treatment method was developed with the help of patients’ feedback. The goal was to develop a facilitation method that would allow patients to become aware of how they distortedly interpret information about themselves and their environment, creating problems in their interactions with others. The VF method was first used in short psychotherapy for depressed patients in the context of individual schema therapy. The method showed that depressed patients were not aware of their negative thinking but rather were very surprised to see it on videotape. The positive results encouraged us to think that this method could be utilized in different forms of psychotherapy [20, 22, 23]. In the method, the patient records a video of him/herself at home according to the instructions given by the therapist. Visual and audio signals are forms of attention-based information that actively promote the patient’s contact with him/herself. Seeing and hearing him/herself on the video may cause bodily sensations, like palpitation, changes in breathing or stiffening of shoulders. The method uses observation channels that engage multiple sensory systems simultaneously, i.e. seeing, hearing and also interoception as specialized internal sensory system about bodily states. The multisensory data provides implicational level information for processing links between emotion and cognition as described by the Interacting Cognitive Subsystems model [35]. Personal recordings represent a versatile form of multimodality. The patient’s home-made recording is watched together with the therapist on a screen step by step. Making direct eye contact with oneself on screen is an important way of contacting oneself. The therapist asks the patient about the observations about him/herself when the patient is listening and watching the videotape. The patient makes observations about him/herself from a distance as if he/she were looking at another person, paying attention to how he/she speaks on the video and interprets the things that happened to him/her. The goal is to increase the awareness of how a negative attitude towards oneself is also apparent in various body reactions.

From the recording, the patient can observe what happens to body positions and movements, the voice, manner of speaking, facial expressions, and gaze because of a negative attitude to him/herself. He/she makes observations about how the negative attitude is reflected in his/her being and what the most strongly negatively affecting cognitions are. The therapist raises the question of how the body changing in reaction to negative thinking represents the patient’s relationship with him/herself and what kind of consequences the negative attitude to oneself has in the patient’s life. Before the next session, the patient makes a new video recording, in which he/she practices a more positive attitude towards her/himself and considers how he/she can teach him/herself to be more positive and how it would change his/her life in the future.

The video recording is a mirror that provides the patient with information about his/her way of experiencing things. When using patient-made recordings as a facilitator in the therapy process, the patient may recognize his/her own emotional experiences in a way that increases his/her understanding of him/herself and the impact of such experiences on his/her own functioning and well-being [20].

Multisensory information as a facilitator of group schema therapy for borderline personality disorder

Multisensory information combined with GST combines the general advantages of group therapy (peer validation, support, and a variety of viewpoints and experiences) and video facilitation (multimodal information) within the schema therapy framework. Experiential techniques are used in GST to increase the patient’s awareness of different modes. In the combined VF-GST model, video information is added and traditional experiential work in GST is replaced by mode recordings made by the patient him/herself. These videos are then studied in various treatment contexts: alone at home, during individual therapy visits and in the group with the aim of getting more perspectives to study the self in a mode state.

Mode video as an essential part of VF-GST

The mode video is in essence a story about a situation where painful emotions were developed. For example, in the case of the Vulnerable Child Mode, the instruction given by the therapist may be: “Think of a situation when you were left alone with your sad feelings. Remember the situation and tell the video: What happened, where and when? What were you thinking in that situation? Were you alone or with someone? What would you have needed for yourself? What did your parents/adults say to you?”. The patient makes the video at home with a tablet computer and later it is watched and discussed in the group. The patient chooses the story, which is often a very sad description of his/her maltreatment and related emotions. Making a mode video is like re-living the past neglect episode. The patient is the person who decides what he/she wants to show and tell the other group members. The duration of the videos varies, usually lasting two to ten minutes. Usually, the story is something they never told anyone.

The video made at home represents an authentic experience of the patient’s story and related emotions. Subsequently, the patient can encounter him/herself in the mode state through the imagery of the video shared in the group context. He/she practices self-observation and self-understanding by collecting information from the video about his/her mode state experience and shares his/her observations in the group. The patient can reach the neglected needs of childhood by watching the mode video. Emotional processing of various mode states and correcting the maladaptive schema with multisensory information can be facilitated through self-studying of the video. Mode video is also a strong stimulus for other members in the group; their experiences of maltreatment often resemble the one they saw on the video.

VF-GST for borderline personality disorder in practice

Overall structure

The VF-GST treatment consists of 30 group sessions. The group meets weekly, and the group session lasts two hours. In the VF-GST group, there are eight participants with the diagnosis of BPD. The VF-GST treatment program has two therapists. In our feasibility study [24] of VF-GST, the therapists were cognitive therapists with ISST training (International Society of Schema Therapy). Every patient has eight individual sessions, the purpose of which is to allow him/her to speak about more personal things without the group and to strengthen the skills needed to cope with modes.

Interview with the patient before VF-GST treatment

The therapist first explains to the patient some basic things about VF-GST, i.e. the role of the patient, the mode concept and working with video material. The patient acts as a researcher of his/her own mind, which is done both individually and in the group. The treatment is patient- oriented. The patient agrees to take his/her role to be the leading producer of treatment material and to participate in the group weekly. He/she is told that he/she will be taught self-examination skills that help him/her to become more aware of how the modes activate and operate as well as seeking help when appropriate.

Instructions for the patient

Patients receive written material about the outline of the therapy and instructions for the videotaping. The method is explained in the following way: “Home is the place where your Vulnerable Child Mode’s emotions are most easily triggered. The video exercise concretely demonstrates to you how your mind works. The exercise will produce a video, which is a mirror of the situation you want to change. It can be challenging. You will get support from therapists and peers when needed. The goal of the video exercise is documentation of your mode function, so that you can gain a self-understanding of all the different modes that are activated in you when you encounter difficult feelings belonging to your childhood. Tell in the video what comes to your mind without being controlled.”

Sequence and contents of VF-GST sessions

The program starts with an orientation section (first two sessions), the goal of which is (1) to build group safety, (2) to teach group rules and ST theory, (3) to identify and process resistance towards making video recordings, and (4) to enhance a co-operative working alliance. The therapists explain how Mode Awareness is built through the recognition of modes and how to learn skills to cope with them in the group and independently at home. The therapists clarify that the goal is to get corrective experiences by confronting hidden needs and related emotions. The mode video watching is the main tool of the treatment process. Examining the video provides information that allows identification of the various components of the mode as well as the origin of the mode. Examination of the recorded material is important because it helps the patient to become more aware of his/her own reactions in stressful situations. It shows what kind of skills are already at his/her disposal to cope with the situation and what kind of skills need to be practiced.

In the second session, every patient shows a recording in which he/she explains how to arrange time for him/herself to make weekly videos and to take care of him/herself. In the video, the patients present all possible obstacles that can make it difficult to make the video and to take care of themselves during the group program. At the same time, they get an exercise on how to use the video as an aid in making self-observations and how the group discussion works in processing the observations found from the video.

The work with modes starts with the Detached Protector Mode video followed by the Punitive Parent Mode video, because they often block processing of emotions. The order of working with modes is, however, flexible based on the needs of the participants. The instructions for the mode videos, the order of implementation and the goals are described in Table 1. All modes are returned to several times during the treatment, because learning and understanding the modes requires several repetitions.

It is up to the individual in what order, how intensely and for how long the modes are activated in response to stressful situations. Based on this mode process video, the patients make an individual “self-video”, which can be used in a preventive manner when mode warning signs are recognized. This video reminds the patient of new coping strategies including e.g. calming words from a Healthy Adult Mode and relevant social support (such as friends, caregivers) in problem situations.

The structure of group sessions

The structure of each group session is similar. The session starts with a playful stimulation exercise. The purpose is to weaken Detached Projector Mode and activate Healthy Adult Mode and activate Happy Child Mode to encourage the group members to be connected to each other and to reach the feeling of safeness. Next, the therapists ask everyone how they have implemented self-care between the group meetings. The aim of continuously asking about self-treatment is to strengthen the role of Healthy Adult Mode. Then, it is the mode video’s turn.

There are two stages of mode modeling in the group, in both of which self-observation skills are taught to increase mode awareness. The first is the individual stage, where the patient is in direct contact with his/her mode experience through a video image sitting in the circle and looking at the TV screen. Watching the video activates the emotional states related to maltreatment in him/her. He/she is connected to his/her own experience from outside and follows how these emotional states manifest in his/her overall appearance such as facial expressions, movement, tone of voice. The human relationships linked to the story in the video are of essential importance. The second stage in mode processing is to change the patient’s self-related thinking pattern to more interactional by sharing observations with others and using the observations of other group members in the patient’s own mode processing. Patients are given the opportunity to use a checklist to pay attention to when looking at the mode video in the group session. These observations are used to stimulate discussion in the group. The mode video watching activates the other group members’ memories about their maltreatment experiences. The subjective experiences from the first level are subsequently socialized in the group interaction context. This process may change the meaning of the vulnerable experience for the patient.

There is a “coffee break” halfway through the group session, which allows time for free discussion. The psychoeducation of the next group session’s mode and the next mode video instruction are given after the break. At the end of the session, the therapists ask for feedback from the group participants about what was useful in the meeting this time and about their own group leading. They summarize the new important knowledge learnt during the session and the skills needed to cope with the presented mode. At each group session, the patient writes down the things he/she has learned, which will be important in maintaining his/her well-being in the future.

The role of the therapists in facilitating the therapy process

The therapists’ main role is to help the patient identify modes in a safe atmosphere, enhance mode awareness and respond to the core emotional needs of the child modes in the group context. Therapists use patient-made videos and guiding questioning to teach the patient self-examination. They help the patient to use observations to identify the threshold of the mode and to link the roots of the mode to the past. The goal is that the patient recognizes the components of mode and understands what kind of behavior is related to the activation of the different mode state.

Maladaptive Parent Modes are the internalized voices of demanding and punishing parents or other caregivers. Discussion

We have previously reported the successful use of patient-made video recordings as an integrated component for facilitation of individual schema therapy [20, 22, 23, 36]. Video technology has been used and is being increasingly used in different forms of patient care and in a psychotherapy context [17, 37–39]. However, as far as we know, guided patient-made video recordings at home have not been used previously in a group psychotherapy context. Patient engagement via self-made videos with multisensory material and home practice plans are essential in this approach. We suggest that (1) adding the use of multi-sensory information in conventional psychotherapy facilitates emotion activation and increases self-awareness and (2) sharing the recordings in a group context helps the patient to understand and connect with difficult and complex feelings. The recordings also provide a multisensory learning object for the group to work on. We first tested these ideas with group schema therapy for borderline personality disorder patients who typically have difficulties in both recognizing and regulating emotions and have problems with social interaction [24]. The theoretical framework of the method and possible advantages and limitations are discussed below.

Multisensory information and emotional processing in VF-GST for BPD patients

Emotional processing is of key importance in the therapeutic effects of several psychotherapy approaches [40]. Active exploration of emotions and attention also paid to the patient’s non-verbal messages have been found to promote the deepening of emotions and the effectiveness of psychotherapy [41]. In their meta-analysis, Peluso and Freund [42] showed that the therapist’s and patient’s emotional expressions are associated with a favorable therapy outcome. Greenberg and Paivio [21] divided emotions into primary, secondary, and instrumental emotions, of which primary emotions, like sadness over loss, anger at violation, and fear of a threat contain an immediate stimulus to action and serve human adaptation to the environment. The negative feelings experienced by patients with BPD can be secondary emotions, like hopelessness, helplessness, rage and despair, which arise as secondary reactions when it has not been possible to face primary adaptive emotions. Instrumental emotions are habitual communicative emotions, which are used to try to influence another person. Negative feelings are a core problem in BPD treatment contexts and perpetuate instability, interpersonal problems and unhealthy behavior. BPD patient’s ability to reflect negative feelings is weak. Reflectivity changes to reactivity in an instant. Dysfunction of information processes occurs when a person cannot use primary emotional information in the information process in a sufficiently versatile manner. This underlines the importance of facing primary emotions and the unmet needs behind them in psychotherapy. According to schema therapy theory, Detached Protector Mode tries to protect us by suppressing our painful primary emotions. The emotional process in mode video work can be divided in two partly overlapping stages: (1) the process starts with individual work done by the patient together with the therapist while the other group members follow but do not express themselves verbally at this stage, and (2) the work done by all the group members taking part in the conversation while then also sharing their own experiences and related emotions.

Emotional processing in individual mode work

The ability to recognize a connection between emotions and facial and body expressions is an individual skill that forms during development in relationships with others. In patients with BPD, this ability and regulation of emotions are not fully developed [43]. Emotions are known to have an impact on the entire chain of cognitive processing including perception, attention, memory, problem-solving and executive function [44–46]. Therefore, emotions essentially guide (or bias) our thinking, behavior and decision-making. Patients with BPD exhibit attention biases such as hypervigilance when faced with intense emotional experiences and find it difficult to distinguish between emotionally significant cues [47, 48]. Such emotional content typically overrides cognition, leading to detrimental behavior in patients with BPD. In the treatment, it is important to focus on the experiences of neglect or abuse as well as the explanation given by the patient that perpetuates non-adaptive feelings. It is essential that the first part in the VF-GST treatment process works on the patient’s ability to build emotional connections within themselves and only after that with other group members because group dynamics may activate the patient’s previous uncertain family relationships, increasing the possibility of group conflicts and group dysfunctions. When in Child Modes and especially Vulnerable Child Mode, the patient needs corrective re-parenting in emotional processing.

In the VF-GST, the mode video activates the patient’s negative feelings that have arisen when emotional processing of the neglect event and the related interaction were interrupted. Watching the mode video activates bodily sensations related to the neglect experience in the patient. These include changes in breathing, palpitations or changes in the voice or prosody, for example, that are activated when they recall the negligence event. With the help of the therapist’s guiding questions, information cues are formed when the sensations experienced by the patient are combined with the visual and auditory manifestations of the emotion shown in the video. The combination of internal bodily sensations with external bodily changes recognizable in the video helps the patient to identify and name the emotion. The physiological sensations of the body and the presentations that represent them reveal that he/she carries within him/her a continuous feeling of suffering. Awareness of the feeling of suffering awakens a feeling of empathy towards him/herself, thus activating the hidden sadness which essentially weakens Detached Protector Mode.

The VF method combines two complementary information systems, i.e. multisensory information and schema-based information processing, which provides a more complete picture of the rejection experience. Stimulus-based perception is based on the senses, such as hearing and vision. The patient identifies his/her belief “I am bad” from his/her mode video narrative, where the sensory information in the video concretizes visual and vocal changes connected with the “I am bad” belief. The activation of negative feelings is recognizable as bodily and vocal reactions when the patient talks about him/herself in a negative way. Schema-based information processing is based on memory content. From the mode video, the patient can access the facts related to his/her problematic experience and the interaction that led to his/her loneliness. The combination of two different information systems enables the study of schema construction in an experience-based manner, whereby the development of a maladaptive schema is related to a specific time and place.

Once connected to their primary emotions, the patient can use sensory information to make inferences by examining the connection between the intensity of the emotion and cognition, e.g. “The more negatively I think about myself, the more I suffer”. The equation helps the patient to understand how strong self-criticism amplifies the intensity of negative emotions and leads to overreaction. Awareness has cognitive, emotional, and physiological components [32]. In VF work, all these different components can be readily identified from the mode video individually from different mode states.

Emotional processing in group mode work

The schema “I am bad” was born when an unprocessed event of neglect remained as an internal conflict for the patient. The group practices expressing primary emotions and correcting Maladaptive Parent Mode’s critical speech, utilizing the observations made by each patient. Cognitive, behavioral, and empathic skill transformation happen through communication and problem-solving in the group when synthesizing information from different mode videos creating new knowledge. Patients experience improved relationships within the group. The “re-family” component is therefore strong.

The group members are asked to provide feedback about the mode video narratives such as missteps in social communication related to the past neglect event. When the neglect episode described by the patient in the video is explored as an interactive event, the patient’s egocentric perspective turns into an “I feel” observation. Feeling bad because being left alone and not getting what a child needs does feel bad. This new explanation is built with the help of video documentation. Reactivating an interrupted experience can be used to explore how the explanation given by the patient maintains non-adaptive feelings and how cognitive distortions impact behavior. The simultaneous operation of two information systems enhances information acquisition, and through multiple video viewings, patients acquire cognitive skills such as decision-making, language comprehension, learning, and thinking. The judgmental power of the Maladaptive Parent Mode is weakened when the unmet needs of the patient’s Vulnerable Child Mode are recognized, and the activation of Angry Child Mode is accepted as a reaction to being left alone. The patient watches 64 videos in total, which further strengthens the peer experience, decreases shame and normalizes living with problems, and enhances working with bad feelings.

In the revised explanation of the neglect event, emotions function as information about experience that was constructed through human interaction. Abnormal empathic responses and deficits in social cognition are major contributors to interpersonal difficulties in patients with BPD [42–45]. When the patients learn to recognize how the group members’ emotions and gestures are related to the complicated situation on the video, they learn cognitive empathy i.e. the ability to intellectually understand another person’s perspective or emotions [49]. Emotional sharing creates cognitive synergy, in which empathizing with the subjective experiences of another group member complements one’s own subjective explanation of the neglect event. Healthy Adult Mode is gradually built through multisensory information in multiple contexts and joint observations made by the patient and other group members from the videos.

Group therapy for BPD can be intense: patients may struggle with shame, identity instability, mistrust issues and limited mentalization ability. VF-GST includes the general advantages of group therapies, such as a sense of universality, connectedness with other group members, and learning from others [50]. However, adding patient-made videos provide a shared, concrete, multisensory learning object around which the group can work on. This creates potential therapeutic advantages. Without video recordings, group discussions often rely on memory and interpretation of the past events can be flavored by emotional dysregulation of BPD patients. Videos allow the group to work with actual, observable emotions and behavior in addition to narratives. Under guidance of the therapists, the recordings may reduce risk for potential conflicts that may rise from misunderstandings — everyone sees the same material. Rather, group members can construct together more adaptive narratives about the patient’s reactions. The videos can also enhance adaptive cognitive-emotional restructuring and linking abstract schema constructs to observable behavior.

According to our experience, adding video facilitation in GST encourages the patients to become more self-disclosing, which, together with the intensified emotional process, may increase the group cohesion. Cohesion refers to a sense of connection or closeness among the group members [51]. In their meta-analysis, Burlingame et al. [52] showed that group cohesion is in general predictive of psychotherapy outcome. Although seeing and hearing oneself and maladaptive states in video recordings can increase patient’s feelings of shame, our experience does not support a major shame-increasing effect. In this method, the patient first goes through the video him/herself and only after that the video is shared in the group. In fact, shame can be even alleviated via successful sharing of difficult experiences in the group. Additional concerns relate to practical safety and ethical aspects that need to be addressed. These include patient consent as well as video data protection and storing in a safe data environment. This was taken care in our feasibility study as all study data was stored in an audited hospital data environment. Based on these early findings we hypothesize that VF-GST and patient-made recordings facilitate the group’s ability to turn, in a way, into a collaborative mentalizing environment and bring real-life emotional episodes for group to process in a safe, structured and therapist-guided way.

Conclusions and implications

Stimulus-based perception is based on senses, whereas information processing guided by thought patterns is based on memory and previous experiences. The multisensory capabilities of video recordings connect simultaneously two complementary information systems, i.e. multisensory information and schema-based information processing. With the help of a guiding questionnaire technique, the patients learn to combine new information with the previous and often limited information of self. Thus, he/she forms new cognitive-emotion links that enable correction of distorted interpretations of self. In the VF-GST treatment model for borderline personality disorder, the patient’s commitment to more precise self-examination results in increases in the patient’s autonomy. These are components that are needed in the construction of Healthy Adult Mode.

We think that using VF provides a new role for the patient in increasing general engagement in his/her own treatment. VF-based techniques can be useful in caregiver-guided booster sessions aiming at secondary prevention of new exacerbation periods of mental disorders with a known relapse risk. The key material for continued treatment is based on individual needs, is already documented as recordings and is available for the patient. Such treatment algorithms will probably be cost-effective as the material can be used to store key important information in care systems, which may shorten repetitive treatment periods. We consider video-facilitation to be scalable, and it can theoretically be applied in several other psychotherapy approaches as an added component in individual as well as group psychotherapy contexts. Yet, we emphasize that VF should be considered as a auxiliary and facilitatory method and should not overshadow the theoretical orientation and therapeutic techniques of psychotherapy such as in our example schema therapy.

The patients in our VF-GST feasibility study were typical outpatients in psychiatric services and therefore represent a selected sample. Further studies and controlled trials are needed to verify method efficacy and optimal length as well as to explore the characteristics of patients who would benefit most from this kind of working model. Better understanding of mechanisms behind video-facilitation in group psychotherapy requires further research and testing of our hypotheses.

Finally, the documented multisensory key moments such as the ones made by the patient at home in an everyday environment may open new possibilities for psychotherapy research. In addition to conventional psychotherapy research, the material can be used, for example, in psycholinguistic research or in affective or social neuroscience paradigms as stimuli for brain and new whole body imaging technologies [53]. This may provide a new type of information about the crucial mechanisms in psychotherapy.
