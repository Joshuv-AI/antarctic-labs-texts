Single-pass four-throw pupilloplasty and phacoemulsification and intraocular lens implantation for acute angle-closure glaucoma
Authors: Jiayin Qin, Xijuan Wang, Guangfeng Liu, Yan Zhang, Chengxia Zhang, Yaqian Niu, Yaxin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis prospective study aimed to assess the efficacy and safety of single-pass four-throw pupilloplasty combined with phacoemulsification and intraocular lens implantation in patients with acute angle-closure glaucoma who experienced their first acute attack and were unable to recover their pupils with medication and/or laser.MethodsSwept-source anterior segment optical coherence tomography was performed to measure anterior chamber angle parameters. Efficacy indicators included the intraocular pressure (IOP) and degree of decrease, changes in anterior chamber parameters, medication dosage, and success rate. Safety indicators included best corrected visual acuity, corneal endothelial cell examination and incidence of complications.ResultsThe mean postoperative follow-up time was 12.80 ± 4.47 months. The mean highest IOP was 51.97 ± 5.87 mmHg (range 36–61 mmHg), the mean preoperative IOP was 37.85 ± 13.14 mmHg (range 12–61 mmHg); mean postoperative IOP was lower than that before. The mean preoperative LogMAR visual acuity was 0.75 ± 0.27 and it improved 1 year postoperatively (0.2 ± 0.08; p < 0.05). The mean number of IOP-lowering drugs was lower after surgery. The number of corneal endothelial cells before and after surgery was comparable, while the mean pupil diameter was smaller after surgery. The mean central anterior chamber depth and volume were greater after surgery. The mean angle opening distance and trabecular iris angle at 500 μm were significantly larger postoperatively. At 1-year postoperatively, the absolute success rate was 86.36%, the qualified success rate was 90.91%.ConclusionsSingle-pass four-throw pupilloplasty combined with phacoemulsification and intraocular lens implantation is effective and safe for acute angle-closure glaucoma.

Background

Glaucoma is a progressive optic neuropathy characterized by optic nerve damage and visual field defects caused by increased intraocular pressure (IOP) [1]. Acute primary angle-closure glaucoma (APACG) is usually caused by acute pupil obstruction, and it hinders aqueous humor flow from the posterior to the anterior chamber, resulting in a pressure gradient that causes iris dilation. Peripheral iris dilation blocks the trabecular meshwork, leading to acute angle closure and a sudden increase in IOP. The incidence of APACG is higher and the efficacy of treatments for APACG is poorer in Asians than in Caucasians. Laser peripheral iridotomy (LPI) for the treatment of IOP is more effective in Caucasians than it is in Asians [2–6].

The treatment principle for acute angle closure is reducing IOP as soon as possible to prevent optic nerve damage, while eliminating pupil obstruction to prevent recurrence or chronic angle closure. Methods to immediately reduce IOP include medication, LPI, argon laser peripheral iridoplasty, and anterior chamber puncture [7–9]. If these methods cannot relieve pupil obstruction, phacoemulsification combined with intraocular lens implantation surgery is required [10–12].

Peripheral anterior synechia (PAS) often occurs during APACG. The causes of PAS include pupil obstruction, peripheral iris dilation, and attachment of trabecular meshwork. After atrophy and loss of iris tension, the pupils dilate and the surrounding iris accumulates in the angle of the chamber, leading to inflammatory reactions. Inflammatory responses exacerbate PAS. Due to high IOP in APACG, the iris becomes ischemic, the sphincter muscle shrinks, the iris loses tension, and the iris root still accumulates in the chamber angle after surgery. Even if the area with pre-existing adhesions is effectively separated during surgery, irreversible damage to the trabecular meshwork can occur, and internal fibrosis of the trabecular meshwork may not facilitate effective aqueous drainage. Accordingly, the success rate of phacoemulsification combined with intraocular lens implantation and viscoelastic agent goniosynechialysis is 80%, without any difference in the effect of lowering IOP and reducing iris trabecular PAS between APACG patients with phacoemulsification combined with intraocular lens implantation alone and those with combined viscoelastic agent goniosynechialysis [13–16].

The acute onset duration of APACG is relatively short, and the degree of optic nerve damage is mild. The main factors affecting visual quality after surgery are high-order aberrations caused by dilated pupils and lens edge effects. To improve these symptoms, some scholars have proposed using pseudopupillary formation, which involves performing small capsulorhexis during surgery without sufficient polishing, and utilizing postoperative subcapsular proliferation reactions to form pseudopupillary structures [17]. However, this method only addresses the dilated pupils and cannot improve the PAS around the iris. Therefore, we used an innovative surgical approach that can achieve both angle separation and pupil constriction: single-pass four-throw (SFT) pupilloplasty combined with phacoemulsification and intraocular lens implantation. We designed a study to assess its efficacy and safety for the treatment of APACG.

Methods

A prospective study was conducted in patients with APACG who experienced their first acute attack at the ophthalmology department of Peking University International Hospital. Patients who were unable to recover their pupils with medication and/or laser underwent SFT pupilloplasty combined with phacoemulsification and intraocular lens implantation. If both eyes had seizures successively, only the first affected eye was included. Data review was performed in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and each patient provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University International Hospital.

Inclusion criteria

Patients were included if they met the following criteria: (1) APACG in acute exacerbation, chronic phase, and absolute phase, with IOP > 21 mmHg after medication and/or laser treatment, and a pupil diameter ≥ 5 mm; (2) APACG in acute exacerbation, chronic phase, and absolute phase, an inability to receive medication and/or laser treatment, and a pupil diameter ≥ 5 mm; or (3) APACG in remission, but a pupil diameter ≥ 6 mm.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria were as follows: angle closure due to neovascular glaucoma, active uveitis, a history of eye surgery or trauma, a history of severe heart and lung disease, advanced cancer, mental illness, treatment with anticoagulants, renal dialysis, use of oral medication that affects IOP, and pregnancy.

Surgical procedure

The SFT technique is commonly used for traumatic dilated pupils, and we used it in surgery for APACG. The surgery is performed under retrobulbar or general anesthesia. Conventional phacoemulsification combined with intraocular lens implantation is performed, followed by SFT pupilloplasty, then removal of the viscoelastic agent from the anterior chamber. First, 23-g micro-forceps are used to grip the edge of the pupil and pull it towards the center at each hour position. A position is then chosen with an interval of 2 h for pupil suturing. Priority should be given to the 10 o’clock and 8 o’clock positions as the main incision for cataract surgery. If the iris atrophy is severe and there is a risk of suture tearing, the position should be changed appropriately. Using 10 o’clock and 8 o’clock for example, a long and curved needle with a 10 − 0 prolene suture (Alcon, USA) is inserted into the anterior chamber through the main cataract surgery incision and sequentially passed through the iris at 10 o’clock and 8 o’clock positions 1 mm away from the pupil edge. A 26-g syringe needle enters the anterior chamber through the paracentesis incision at the 8 o’clock position; then, micro-forceps are used to enter the clamping needle tail from the side incision and feed its head end into the syringe needle. The syringe needle is slowly withdrawn, and the 10 − 0 suture needle can be led out of the anterior chamber through the paracentesis incision at the 8 o’clock position. A Sinskey hook is passed through the main incision, the part of the suture located in the anterior chamber is hooked back out of the main incision, and the suture ring is pulled out of the anterior chamber, but the suture needle remains outside the eye. The thread end is wrapped into the loop four times, holding the thread end while pulling the sewing needle to slowly pull the knot back into the anterior chamber. While tightening the knot, the pupil edges at the 10 o’clock and 8 o’clock positions are tightly fixed together. Using 23-g micro-scissors, excess sutures are cut and pulled out of the anterior chamber (Fig. 1). After completing pupilloplasty the viscoelastic agent inside the eye is aspirated, a watertight incision is made, and the surgery is completed. All surgeries were performed by the same experienced surgeon (Liu GF). We use Barrett Universal Ⅱ formula for the calculation of artificial intraocular lens(IOLs), and all IOLs we used were monofocal IOLs.(Tecnis, ZCB00 or ZA9003, Johnson and Johnson Vision Care, Inc). After surgery, tobramycin and dexamethasone eye drops (Alcon) were administered four times per day, and follow-up was conducted at 1 day, 1 week, and 1, 3, 6, and 12 months. Patients were followed up for any complications arising from the SFT procedure.

Observation indicators

Patients were included based on assessments of best corrected visual acuity (LogMAR), IOP (i-Care), slit lamp examination, anterior chamber gonioscopy (OSMG, ocular), and Pentecam (70700, Oculus). Patients who were enrolled underwent corneal endothelial cell examination, axial (IOL-Master), swept-source anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) (DRI Triton, Topcon), visual field assessment (OCTOPUS900, Clinico), and fundus examination. Duration of disease (days), cup-to-disc ratio, preoperative medications, and details of the surgical process were recorded. Best corrected visual acuity (LogMAR), IOP (i-Care), corneal endothelial cell examination, swept-source AS-OCT, complications, medication, and success rates were recorded at 1 day, 1 week, and 1, 3, 6, and 12 months. All assessments were conducted by experienced technicians. Efficacy indicators included the IOP level and degree of decrease, changes in anterior chamber parameters, changes in medication dosage, and success rate. Safety indicators included best corrected visual acuity, corneal endothelial cell examination, and incidence of complications.

AS-OCT measurements

Swept-source AS-OCT [18] (DRI Triton, Topcon) was performed in a dark room before and after surgery to measure anterior chamber angle parameters. During the examination, the patient’s eyelids were gently pulled to expose the corneal limbus. The linear scanning mode of the AS-OCT (wavelength 1050 nm) was used to obtain images of the anterior chamber angle at 12, 6, 3, and 9 o’clock. The scanning range was 6 mm centered on the limbus of the cornea, with 64 superimpositions. Only images that clearly showed the scleral process were selected for analysis. The built-in ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used for image processing to measure the mean angle opening distance at 500 μm (AOD500) and the trabecular iris angle at 500 μm (TIA500). First, the scleral spur, which is the point where the curvature of the corneoscleral interface changes, was manually identified as point A (Fig. 2). A 500-µm-long line from the scleral process that intersects the inner surface of the cornea at a point B was then drawn, as was a perpendicular line crossing the front surface of the iris at another point, C. The length of B–C was defined as AOD500. The intersection of AOD500 and the anterior surface of the iris forms a line with the scleral spur. The angle of these two lines passing through the scleral spur was defined as TIA500. We calculated the average values of the four sites for statistical analysis: the average AOD500 and average TIA500.

Success rate

The Advanced Glaucoma Intervention Study demonstrated that minimal progression occurs when IOP is maintained below 18 mmHg [18]. Therefore, we defined surgical success as a postoperative IOP ≤ 18 mmHg, coupled with a decrease in IOP from baseline ≥ 30%. If these criteria were met without medication, the outcome was considered an absolute success. If medication was needed, it was considered a qualified success. Similarly, if the postoperative IOP was ≤ 18 mmHg but the reduction was < 30%, it was considered a qualified success. Surgical failure was identified in cases where there was no light perception after surgery and another surgery was needed to reduce the IOP, or if there was postoperative low IOP (< 6 mmHg). Patients lost to follow-up were excluded from success and failure rate calculations.

Statistical analysis

All parameters measured in the study were normally distributed, as determined via the W-test, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Success and incidence of complication rates are expressed as percentages. Two sample t-tests were used to compare best corrected visual acuity, IOP, and medication dosage before and after surgery. P-values of < 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance. IBM SPSS 24 software (Armonk, NY, USA) was used for all statistical analyses, and Prism 9.4.0 software (GraphPad Software, USA) was used to create graphs.

Results

The study included 22 eyes of 22 patients, 10 right and 12 left. Eight of the patients were male. The mean age was 67.35 ± 6.33 years (range, 54–81 years). The shortest duration from onset to surgery was 1 day; the longest was 1 year (median, 17 days). The mean postoperative follow-up time was 12.80 ± 4.47 months (range, 8–24 months). Ten patients were treated with YAG LPI. The mean ocular axis was 22.56 ± 0.66 mm. Best corrected visual acuity (LogMAR) was better than 1.6 in 10 patients (mean, 0.75 ± 0.27). The mean highest IOP was 51.97 ± 5.87 mmHg (range 36–61 mmHg), and the mean preoperative IOP was 37.85 ± 13.14 mmHg (range 12–61 mmHg). Two patients with angle-closure glaucoma secondary to lens subluxation underwent anterior vitrectomy and intraocular lens scleral implantation, and the remaining patients underwent intraocular lens implantation in the capsular bag. One patient with absolute glaucoma underwent trabeculectomy combined with mitomycin, phacoemulsification intraocular lens implantation, and SFT pupilloplasty, and one patient with APACG underwent anterior vitrectomy combined with phacoemulsification intraocular lens implantation and SFT pupilloplasty. Changes in visual acuity

The mean preoperative LogMAR visual acuity was 0.75 ± 0.27, and 1 year after surgery it was better (0.2 ± 0.08; p < 0.05) (Table 3). In all but three patients, postoperative visual acuity was improved. Two of these three patients had absolute glaucoma preoperatively, and their visual acuity was no light perception. The third patient had APACG secondary to lens dislocation and was in the chronic stage; preoperative visual acuity was counting fingers because of corneal endothelial decompensation, and while postoperative IOP decreased to the normal range, visual acuity did not improve (Table 3; Fig. 4).

Changes in other parameters

The mean number of IOP-lowering drugs used after surgery was 0.20 ± 0.62, significantly lower than that before surgery (4.00 ± 0.92; p < 0.05). Only two patients used two kinds of IOP-lowering drugs postoperatively; the other patients did not need IOP-lowering drugs. The numbers of corneal endothelial cells could not be measured in six patients because of corneal edema preoperatively, and the mean number of corneal endothelial cells in the other 16 patients was 2074.57 ± 796.18/mm2. The mean pupil diameter, central anterior chamber depth, anterior chamber volume, AOD500, and TIA500 were different after surgery (Table 4).

Success rate

At 1 year after surgery, the absolute success rate was 86.36%, and the qualified success rate was 90.91%.

Complications

Two (9.09%) patients had tears at the iris pupil margin due to iris atrophy and strong suture cutting force, and the pupil was successfully sutured after changing the position, with mild iris atrophy. In one (4.55%) patient, there was an approximately 2-mm-wide iris root breakage, which was deemed not to cause glare, multi-pupil, or other discomfort, and no special treatment was given. No complications such as anterior chamber hemorrhage or corneal endothelial injury occurred. One day postoperatively, three (13.64%) patients had severe inflammation and fibrous exudation in the anterior chamber, which were relieved after 1 week of intensive glucocorticoid treatment. IOP was > 21 mmHg in five (22.73%) patients on the first day after surgery, including one case after trabeculectomy, and IOP remained below 18 mmHg after laser disconnection in that patient. Another of these five patients did not receive special treatment, and IOP reduced to normal after 1 week. In the remaining three patients, IOP was still > 21 mmHg after 1 week of observation. One was a patient without light perception, so it was not treated with IOP-lowering drugs. After the use of drugs in the other two patients, IOP was maintained below 18 mmHg and at 20 mmHg in one each. There were no serious complications such as suture slippage, low IOP, hemorrhage, or macular edema.

Discussion

In our study, the mean IOP decreased, mean LogMAR visual acuity improved, and the mean number of medications used was greatly reduced after surgery, similar to previous reports [19, 20]. Hou et al. [20] reported that, after cataract surgery combined with trabeculectomy, IOP in patients with refractory APACG significantly decreased compared to preoperative levels. Moreover, cataract surgery alone reduced IOP postoperatively, and visual acuity significantly improved. The complete success rate of the simple cataract group was 72%, and the conditional success rate was 100%. Notably, however, pupil diameter was not assessed before surgery.

Although trabeculectomy combined with cataract surgery can reduce IOP, trabeculectomy in patients with dilated pupils caused by acute glaucoma attacks is challenging. There is also a risk of excessive excision of the iris. In patients with dilated pupils, a long course of illness, and severe peripheral iris adhesion, we first perform pupil suturing to reduce pupil size before performing trabeculectomy. This can greatly reduce the difficulty of trabeculectomy and minimize complications.

In this study, patients exhibited increases in central anterior chamber depth, anterior chamber volume, AOD500, and TIA500, as well as a significant reduction in pupil diameter. Because patients with angle-closure glaucoma have a thick crystalline lens, a steep anterior surface of the lens, and anterior displacement, simply performing phacoemulsification and intraocular lens implantation can deepen the anterior chamber and widen the angle [21]. In a study that used ultrasound biomicroscopy, postoperative anterior chamber depth increased from 1977 to 3528 μm, and AOD500 increased from 208 to 468 μm [22]. In another study, anterior chamber depth increased from 1.59 ± 0.27 to 2.86 ± 0.28 mm after simple cataract surgery [20]. In contrast, anterior chamber depth and AOD500 increased more significantly in the present study than in previous reports. This is partly due to baseline differences, and partly because we also performed SFT pupilloplasty, which can achieve better angle separation and improve the stability of anterior chamber depth and angle opening. We also assessed pupil size before and after surgery, and the mean pupil size decreased from 5.79 to 3.83 mm, which cannot be achieved by simple phacoemulsification combined with intraocular lens implantation surgery or combined with trabeculectomy.

Potential complications of SFT pupilloplasty [9, 10, 23] include iris tearing, iris root detachment, multiple pupils, anterior chamber bleeding, postoperative inflammatory responses, and corneal endothelial damage. We took specific measures to avoid these complications, and no serious complications occurred.

Notably, after an acute glaucoma attack, the iris atrophies and lacks elasticity, rendering it susceptible to tearing at the suture point. Before suturing, the reserved iris can be gently pulled with the aid of an intraocular microscope to determine the actual size of the defect, and the ideal suture point of the pupil margin. A second instrument can be used to assist with fixation during suturing. Pupil size after suturing is also important, with a pupil diameter of 3.0–3.5 mm being suitable. A large pupil can cause photophobia and glare, while a small pupil can affect night vision and future fundus examinations. To protect corneal endothelial cells from damage by intraocular instruments, after implanting an artificial lens, we first remove the viscoelastic agent in the posterior capsule of the lens, and then inject the viscoelastic agent into the anterior chamber. This can avoid the inability to remove the viscoelastic agent in the capsule after pupil constriction and prevent further damage to the corneal endothelial cells during anterior chamber operations. Notably, eyes affected by chronic glaucoma are more prone to optic nerve damage, and intraocular operations may cause IOP fluctuations, leading to “wipe-out syndrome” [24]. It is important to maintain stable anterior chamber perfusion pressure during cataract surgery. During pupillary reconstruction, timely supplementation of viscoelastic agents is necessary to avoid excessive IOP fluctuations.

In the current study, AS-OCT was used to evaluate anterior chamber parameters, as in previous research [25]. It is a rapid and non-invasive method for measuring the chamber angle, but in the current study, inaccurate preoperative identification of the scleral spur may have occurred in some patients due to severe angle closure, which prevents light from shining into the deep angles of the chamber. However, postoperative imaging of the scleral spur and quantitative calculation of atrial angle parameters are easier to achieve. Thus, the aforementioned potential inaccuracy conceivably had little effect on the accuracy of surgical outcome evaluation. Only two studies have reported this surgical method for angle-closure glaucoma [26, 27]. Although AS-OCT was also used to observe postoperative morphology of the angle in these two previous reports, no quantitative calculations or comparisons were reported. The current study is thus more comprehensive.

The present study had some limitations. It was a cohort study, lacking a control group. We plan to compare patients who have previously undergone cataract phacoemulsification combined with viscoelastic agent angle separation with our patients. Second, our OCT measurement technology requires manual positioning and calculation. We plan to develop artificial intelligence software capable of accurately automatically calculating anterior chamber parameters and importing the data into Topcon. Lastly, our sample size was small. We will continue to expand our sample size for larger-scale studies in the future.

Conclusions

Single-pass four-throw pupilloplasty combined with phacoemulsification and intraocular lens implantation is effective and safe for acute angle-closure glaucoma.
