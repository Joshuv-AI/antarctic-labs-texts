Real-Time 3D Vision-Language Embedding Mapping
Christian Rauch, Björn Ellensohn, Linus Nwankwo, Vedant Dave, Elmar Rueckert

Abstract
A metric-accurate semantic 3D representation is essential for many robotic tasks. This work proposes a simple, yet powerful, way to integrate the 2D embeddings of a Vision-Language Model in a metric-accurate 3D representation at real-time. We combine a local embedding masking strategy, for a more distinct embedding distribution, with a confidence-weighted 3D integration for more reliable 3D embeddings. The resulting metric-accurate embedding representation is task-agnostic and can represent semantic concepts on a global multi-room- as well as on a local object-level. This enables a variety of interactive robotic applications that require the localisation of objects-of-interest via natural language. We evaluate our approach on a variety of real-world sequences and demonstrate that these strategies achieve a more accurate object-of-interest localisation while improving the runtime performance in order to meet our real-time constraints. We further demonstrate the versatility of our approach in a variety of interactive handheld, mobile robotics and manipulation tasks, requiring only raw image data. Website: https://rtvlem.github.io .



I
Introduction

Many robotic downstream tasks, such as manipulation and navigation, require a metric-accurate understanding of the 3D environment as well as semantic information about objects and places to interact with. Classically, this problem has been approached by closed-set semantic mapping
[
1
]
, which combines 2D or 3D segmentation with dense 3D reconstruction
[
2
]
, in order to remove dynamic disturbances
[
3
]
, and for robust localisation
[
4
]
. Closed-set segmentation uses a fixed set of classes and labels, limiting the application of these approaches to certain domains and specific query patterns. Some of these limitations can be avoided by implicit rigid motion assumptions
[
5
]
, but are then missing explicit semantics.

Recent combinations of Vision and Text Transformers
[
6
]
, trained on large datasets, enable open-set image and text relations within a common embedding space. Since the majority of these models are trained on internet-scale datasets with predominantly 2D images, they are not directly applicable to 3D. For an application relying on a stream of 2D images, this also implies that only the current field-of-view (FoV) can be considered at any point in time, excluding mobile tasks, where objects-of-interest are outside the current FoV.

Recent work uses implicit neural representations
[
7
,
8
]
to merge and render embeddings in place of colour channels
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
. Often, these approaches are not real-time capable due to their training regimes requiring multiple dependant stages operating globally on the entire dataset, including ground truth camera poses, instead of a continuous data stream. Furthermore, most implicit representations are only queryable in a synthesised 2D view of rendered embeddings and do not provide a metric-accurate geometric representation. Both these properties are required for an interactive zero-shot localisation of objects-of-interest in most downstream robotic tasks.

This work proposes a method for the real-time mapping of image embeddings in 3D for open-set robotic downstream tasks. We only require a stream of colour and depth images to reconstruct an environment with image embeddings (
Figure

1
). We do not require ground truth camera poses or any pre-training on the target environment. This makes our method environment- and task-agnostic and serves as a building block for other tasks which require a language interface to robotic applications.

II
Related Work

II-A

Vision-Language Models in Robotics

In contrast to classic closed-set methods trained on specific labels, novel Vision-Language Models (VLMs) enable the open-set association of images with their text descriptions
[
6
,
12
]
, or other modalities
[
13
]
, via a common embedding space, using individual transformers for image, text, or other modalities. VLMs have been used in robotics for open-set tracking of objects in the current camera FoV
[
14
]
, for interactive pose estimation of relevant parts of tools
[
15
]
, and for navigation via hand-drawn instructions
[
16
]
.

By focusing on a single task and the current FoV, these approaches cannot generalise to other tasks or operate on a global level, such as localising tools outside the current FoV. In contrast, we integrate the open-set VLM embeddings in a task-agnostic 3D representation in order to enable a variety of interactive robotic use-cases on the same vision-language representation.

II-B

Implicit Neural Representations

Due to the availability of vast amounts of 2D images and text, Vision Transformers (ViT) are predominantly trained on 2D image data
[
17
]
. In a robotic application, this constrains their use to the current camera FoV. Implicit Neural Representations (INR) or Novel View Synthesis (NVS) methods
[
7
,
8
]
are trained and overfitted on a closed colour image data set with their camera poses, and focus on rendering colour images for novel camera views at a high visual fidelity. Compared to previous reconstruction methods, such as point clouds with oriented splats
[
2
]
, these methods provide a more compact encoding of the training data and more detailed 2D renderings.

Since these methods rely only on colour images and do not use depth or true camera poses, they cannot reproduce a scale-accurate metric representation of the environment. Furthermore, their implicit representation has to be converted to a dense Cartesian representation before it can be used in geometric tasks, such as navigation and manipulation. These limitations of implicit representations, together with their training regime, effectively prevents their use in robotics. To avoid these limitations, we propose a metric-accurate direct representation in form of a point cloud, which allows us to directly access the representation for robotic tasks and to store the full dynamic range of the vision-language embeddings.

II-C

Embedding Integration

Implicit Neural Representations are used to render novel views of vision-language embeddings
[
9
,
10
]
or segmentation features
[
18
]
in addition to colour. The same technique has also been applied to traditional point cloud representations
[
11
]
.
VLMs that are trained on image and text pairs, such as OpenCLIP
[
19
]
, only generate a single embedding vector per image and text. While this is sufficient for room-level scenes
[
20
]
, it limits the granularity of the representation for object-level tasks. Instead, more discriminative embeddings can be extracted on the individual segments of an image
[
21
]
, enabling more fine-grained responses to queries
[
11
,
10
]
.

We show, that a naive implementation of this local embedding strategy can lead to embeddings of neighbouring segments diluting each other, resulting in less distinct and sharp heatmap responses. Based on this observation, we propose a simple masking strategy that provides more pronounced query responses and thus a better segmentation performance.

III
Method

III-A

Problem Formulation

For a continuous stream of colour and depth image pairs
(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{D})_{i}:\{\mathbf{C}:\mathbb{R}^{w\times h}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbf{D}:\mathbb{R}^{w\times h}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\}
over a time index
i\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
, we are looking for a metric-accurate 3D representation that encodes geometric information in form of coordinate points
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and semantic information in form of an arbitrary embedding vector
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\mathcal{M}_{i}:\{(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{e})_{j}\,|\,j\in\left[0,N_{\mathcal{M}}\right]\}\,.

(1)

We further impose the following constraints: Ground truth camera poses are not available. The number of RGB-D image pairs is unlimited and
N_{\mathcal{M}}:=\left|\mathcal{M}_{i}\right|
is limited to fit a fixed amount of memory.
\mathcal{M}_{i}
must be sufficiently dense. At any time index
i
,
\mathcal{M}_{i}
can only contain data up to this point and data is integrated in chronological order.
\mathcal{M}_{i}
can be accessed at any point in time. In summary, the 3D representation is maintained in real-time for an unlimited time using only the raw sensor data and can be accessed or queried in real-time as well.
This requires a constant integration and compression of image data and local embeddings and camera localisation. First, we estimate the camera pose in a frame-to-model tracking approach, using an additional dense model (
Section

III-B
). Second, mask-level embeddings are created per camera view (
Section

III-C
). Third, the mask-level embeddings are integrated over multiple views using the estimated camera pose and a view-dependent confidence (
Section

III-D
).

III-B

Localisation

The first step in our pipeline is the estimation of the camera pose. This part is relying on a baseline reference implementation for RGB-D odometry and mapping, and therefore is only described briefly.

Given two consecutive image pairs
(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{D})_{a}
and
(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{D})_{b}
, and the camera intrinsics
\mathbf{K}
, the odometry estimates the rigid transformation
\mathbf{T}_{a\rightarrow b}\in\mathrm{SE}(3)
between the camera frames at these two points in time, using a coarse-to-fine multiscale approach on an image pyramid with a hybrid photometric and geometric loss
[
22
,
23
]
.
As this frame-to-frame odometry alone would drift over time, we maintain a dense 3D model in parallel to
\mathcal{M}
solely for the frame-to-model odometry. In contrast to the point set for the embeddings and their coordinates, the odometry module uses a multivalued hash map, with blocks and voxels as key and a tuple of signed distance, integration weight, and colour as value
[
24
]
.
After initialising this hash map with
(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{D})_{0}
, consecutive estimations of
\mathbf{T}
will use synthesised colour and depth renderings
(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{D})_{a}^{*}
at the previous point in time
a
in place of real sensor data.

Integrating the colour and depth into the hash map can be done at a much higher frame rate than integrating the post-processed embeddings due to the lower dimensionality of colour and the additional overhead of the mask-level embedding extractions. Hence, this parallelisation and decoupling allows us to better trade off the tracking accuracy with the density and dynamic range of
\mathcal{M}_{i}
. Hereby, the pose estimation is of greater concern due to its permanent effect on map and embedding degradation.

III-C

Segment-Aligned Vision-Language Embeddings

The second step is the extraction of pixel-wise embeddings, providing one embedding vector for every pixel coordinate:
\mathbf{C}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{w\times h\times 3}\mapsto\mathbf{E}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{w\times h\times d}
.

Similar to previous work
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
, we use CLIP
[
6
]
embeddings, specifically from the OpenCLIP
[
19
]
implementation. The image and text encoder produce a single image-level and text-level embedding vector, respectively. For most downstream applications, this is not sufficiently discriminative. A common approach for extracting pixel-level embeddings is the encoding of multiple, potentially overlapping, regions of the image, and previous work mostly differs in the strategy how these regions are extracted.

Generally, such a region extraction method must provide a potentially empty set of bounding boxes
\mathcal{B}_{i}:\{\mathcal{R}_{k}:(u,v,r_{w},r_{h})\}
, from which a set of cropped image segments
\mathbf{C}_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{r_{w}\times r_{h}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
, located at pixel coordinates
(u,v)
in the original image
\mathbf{C}_{i}
, can be extracted and passed to the vision encoder,
V:\mathbf{C}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
, to obtain the embedding vector
\mathbf{e}_{k}
for that segment.

While LERF
[
9
]
samples cropped regions along a randomly sampled ray, we follow LangSplat
[
10
]
and ConceptFusion
[
11
]
, and extract semantically more meaningful regions using Segment Anything (SAM)
[
21
]
. Using the additional pixel-wise binary foreground/background mask
\mathbf{M}_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{r_{w}\times r_{h}}\rightarrow\mathbb{B}
,
\mathbf{e}_{k}
can be associated directly to pixel coordinates in
\mathbf{C}_{i}
. On top of this baseline, we apply a mask refinement and visual encoder masking strategy.

III-C
1
Refinement

For areas of
\mathbf{C}_{i}
, that have not been segmented, we are uniformly sampling a fixed amount of coordinates within these non-segmented areas and provide these as query points yet again to SAM
[
21
]
. The resulting fill-in segments are added to
\mathcal{B}_{i}
. While this process could be repeated, we found one additional iteration a sufficient trade-off and more efficient than using a larger model.

III-C
2
Masking

A naive approach is to directly pass the cropped images
\mathbf{C}_{k}
to the vision encoder and assign the resulting embedding vector via the mask to the final image
[
11
]
. This has the drawback that overlapping regions are potentially assigned similar embedding vectors, and thus respond similar to text queries (
Figure

2
). Masking the cropped segment
\mathbf{C}_{k}
and replacing non-segmented areas

\mathbf{C}_{k}^{\prime}(u,v)=\begin{cases}\mathbf{v},&\text{if}\;\mathbf{M}_{k}(u,v)=0,\\
\mathbf{C}_{k}(u,v),&\text{if}\;\mathbf{M}_{k}(u,v)=1,\end{cases}

avoids this effect by extracting visual embeddings that are more closely related to the segmented image data. Ideally,
\mathbf{v}
is a value that appears the least frequent in the original data. Since this is intractable, we set
\mathbf{v}:=(1,0,1)
in practice.

We found that merging global image embeddings with the local segment embeddings has a similar effect when an object covers a large portion of the FoV. Therefore, we refrain from this merging strategy and only use local segment embeddings.

While bounding boxes and cropped regions have varying size, they are preprocessed and bicubic resampled to the same size. Thus, instead of encoding segments individually, we can stack them and encode the batch of segments in a single encoder call.

III-D

3D Embedding Integration

The local segment embeddings
\mathbf{E}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{w\times h}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
are only available for the current FoV at time index
i
and many robotic applications require localisation of objects outside the current FoV. The embedding lifting integrates the embedding vectors of the local segments into the 3D map of point coordinates and embeddings (
eq.

1
).

For each depth image
\mathbf{D}_{i}
and the camera matrix
\mathbf{K}
, we back-project the pixel coordinates
(u,v)
to point coordinates in the camera frame

\mathbf{p}:=\mathbf{K}^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{D}_{i}(u,v)\cdot\left[u,v,1\right]^{\top}

and use the same relation to associate the embeddings in
\mathbf{E}_{i}
to the same points, such that all pixels with valid depth readings in the image space produce a set of tuples of point coordinates and embedding vectors in the current camera frame:

\mathcal{P}_{i}:\{(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{e})_{j}\,|\,j\in\left[0,N_{\mathcal{P}}\right],N_{\mathcal{P}}\leq w\cdot h\}\,.

(2)

We compute the confidence
c\in[0,1]
of an embedding vector based on how close the normal
\hat{\mathbf{n}}
of its point
\mathbf{p}
is to the line of sight:

c(\mathbf{p})=(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{p}}+1)/2

(3)

to relate to how well a point was visible when its segment-level embedding vector was extracted. The points are then transformed from the camera frame to the map frame, given the estimated camera pose. If
i=0
, we initialise
\mathcal{M}_{0}:=\mathcal{P}_{0}
, otherwise,
\mathcal{M}_{i}
is iteratively downsampled to a uniform point density (points per
\mathrm{m}^{3}
) such that
\lvert\mathcal{M}_{i}\rvert\leq N_{\mathcal{M}}
.
This downsampling limits the memory consumption while avoiding sparsifying regions with low density.

Given the current points and their embeddings
\mathcal{P}_{i}
, and the integrated map up to the previous view,
\mathcal{M}_{i-1}
, we integrate new points and embeddings, by first establishing a set
\mathcal{C}
of dense point correspondences
\{(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})\}
in Cartesian space by a nearest neighbour search:

\mathcal{C}_{i}:\{(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p}^{*})\,|\,\mathbf{p}^{*}=\underset{\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{i}}{\arg\min}\lVert\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\rVert_{2},\,\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{M}_{i-1}\}\,.

(4)

For each point coordinate in the current map
\mathcal{M}_{i-1}
, we find its closest point coordinate in the current input
\mathcal{P}_{i}
within a certain maximum search distance. Note that this is not a symmetric 1:1 relation and one point in
\mathcal{P}_{i}
can be closest to many points in
\mathcal{M}_{i-1}
and there may be points in
\mathcal{M}_{i-1}
without a corresponding match.

We integrate the embedding vector
\mathbf{e}_{j}
and confidence
c_{j}
for every
\mathbf{p}^{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{i}
into its associated
\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle c_{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle:=[(1-w)\cdot c_{\mathcal{P}}]+[w\cdot c_{\mathcal{M}}]

(5)

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle:=[(1-w)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{\mathcal{P}}]+[w\cdot\mathbf{e}_{\mathcal{M}}]

(6)

with confidence-based weight

\displaystyle w=1-\left[\left(1-\frac{c(\mathbf{p})}{c(\mathbf{p})+c(\mathbf{q})}\right)\cdot\left(1-w_{\mathrm{new}}\right)\right]\,

(7)

after renormalisation.
w_{\mathrm{new}}
is a fixed weight defining how strongly new data is to be integrated into the map.
\mathbf{p}
in
\mathcal{P}
without a correspondence in
\mathcal{M}
are integrated with their embedding vector and confidence without further weighting.

III-E

3D Segmentation

The text encoder provides one embedding vector
\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{text}}
for a text query with arbitrary many words and tokens, respectively. The normalised cosine similarity
s\in[0,1]
between this text embedding and the visual embedding vectors associated with points in
\mathcal{M}
:

s_{\mathrm{query}}=(\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{text}}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{\mathcal{M}}+1)/2

(8)

provides a 3D heatmap. See
Figure

1
for a visualisation of such a heatmap.

To select a closed set of 3D points that most likely respond to a text query, we additionally query for a generic
object
, such that each
\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{M}
is associated with similarities for an actual task-specific query
s_{\mathrm{query}}
and a generic baseline similarity
s_{\mathrm{\textit{object}}}
. The additional query for
object
is inspired by LangSplat
[
10
]
and makes sure that our target is more relevant than a generic
object
.

At any point in time
i
, we can query for arbitrary many
\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{text}}
, yielding a list of similarities
s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{N},s_{\mathrm{\textit{object}}}
for each point
\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{M}_{i}
. Selecting the maximum similarity from this list separates our model
\mathcal{M}
into disjoint point sets
\mathcal{Q}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{Q}_{N}
, with
\mathcal{Q}_{k}:\{(\mathbf{p},s)_{j}\}
, for the task-specific text queries, and
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{\textit{object}}}
, which is neglected and not used further.

We further remove unstable points by clustering the similarity and coordinates to remove outliers and only retain points with a high similarity. For similarity clustering, we are fitting a Gaussian Mixture Model with three components, initialised between the minimum and maximum similarity, and select the cluster with the highest mean value. Coordinate clustering is done via DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise).

Setup

Conclusion

We presented an approach for the real-time integration and retrieval of vision-language embeddings in a metric-accurate 3D representation, as a building block for various interactive downstream robotic tasks. We proposed a strategy to partition image-level embeddings to local masked segment-level embeddings and demonstrated that this provides improved segmentation performance and relevancy to language instructions, while also being faster than the baseline approaches. Based on this, our proposed integration of local embeddings in 3D using a view-dependent confidence provided a 3D representation with fewer colour and embedding artifacts. The efficiency, performance, and generalisability of our method was evaluated in a variety of real-world sequences and robotic tasks.

While the work demonstrated improved runtime and segmentation performance compared to baselines, it also demonstrated the limitations on segmentation accuracy imposed by the real-time constraints, that are usually not considered in the literature. Furthermore, the memory limitations and the open-set nature of the queries required us to represent the environment at a uniform density. Future work will consider more memory-efficient implicit representations with a focus on real-time integration and retrieval.

Given that VLMs, such as CLIP, only provide image-level embeddings, most related approaches rely on a pre-segmentation of the image. This inherently limits the granularity of query responses and prevents interacting with fine-grained local embeddings. For example, current methods are unable to query a
cup handle
if only ever an entire
cup
was segmented. Future work will therefore focus on pixel-level instead of segment-level embeddings. Furthermore, using only local segment embeddings neglects spatial relations and prevents querying objects relative to each other. Future work will take hierarchical and spatial relation, implicitly encoded in the 3D embedding map, into account.
