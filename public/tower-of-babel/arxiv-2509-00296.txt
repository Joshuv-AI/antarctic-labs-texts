Superconvergence Extraction of Upwind Discontinuous Galerkin Method Solving the Radiative Transfer Equation
Andrés Galindo-Olarte, Zhichao Peng, Jennifer K. Ryan

Abstract
We theoretically analyze the superconvergence of the upwind discontinuous Galerkin (DG) method for both the steady-state and time-dependent radiative transfer equation (RTE), and apply the Smooth-Increasing Accuracy-Conserving (SIAC) filters to enhance the accuracy order. Direct application of SIAC filters on low-dimensional macroscopic moments, often the quantities of practical interest, can effectively improve the approximation accuracy with marginal computational overhead. Using piecewise k k -th order polynomials for the approximation and assuming constant cross sections, we prove ( 2 ​ k + 2 ) (2k+2) -th order superconvergence for the steady-state problem at Radau points on each element and ( 2 ​ k + 1 2 ) (2k+\frac{1}{2}) -th order superconvergence for the global L 2 − L^{2}- and negative-order Sobolev norms for the time-dependent problem. Numerical experiments confirm the efficacy of the filtering, demonstrating post-filter convergence orders of 2 ​ k + 2 2k+2 for steady-state and 2 ​ k + 1 2k+1 for time-dependent problems. More significantly, the SIAC filter delivers substantial gains in computational efficiency. For a time-dependent problem, we observed an approximately 2.22 × 2.22\times accuracy improvement and a 19.94 × 19.94\times reduction in computational time. For the steady-state problems, the filter achieved a 4 – 9 × 4–9\times acceleration without any loss of accuracy.

Introduction

The radiative transfer equation (RTE) (
[
40
,
30
,
8
]
) describes particles, e.g. neutrons or photons, propagating through and interacting with a background medium by means of a kinetic distribution that gives the density of particles with respect to the local phase space measure . It has a wide range of applications in nuclear engineering, medical imaging, astrophysics and remote sensing. Efficient numerical simulation tools for RTE are highly desired. In this article, we take advantage of the underlying superconvergent properties of the approximation to improve accuracy as well as computational performance.

In order to approximate solutions to RTE, the upwind discontinuous Galerkin (DG) method is used. DG has been a popular and powerful deterministic solver for RTE since its first introduced by Reed and Hill in simulating neutron transport
[
41
]
. A key strength of the high-order upwind DG method is its asymptotic preserving (AP) property, which ensures it correctly captures the diffusion limit of the RTE without resolving the small particle mean free path. This property was formally established through asymptotic analysis
[
29
,
2
]
, and later rigorously proved
[
22
]
. Uniform convergence with respect to the mean free path has been analyzed in
[
43
,
44
]
. Besides upwind DG, DG methods have been actively developed for solving RTE. These developments include
hp

[
15

[

37

,
1

[

, and high-order low-order method (HOLO)
[

Despite its success, the discontinuous Galerkin (DG) method for the radiative transfer equation (RTE) suffers from the curse of dimensionality, as the unknown distribution function is posed in a high-dimensional phase space. An attractive mitigation is post-processing techniques that can significantly enhance resolution of numerical solutions. Exploiting pointwise or Fourier space superconvergence, post-processing techniques typically enhance accuracy of a finite element solution through a convolution that performs effective local averaging to damp dominating high frequency errors. The development of post-processing dates back to Bramble and Schatz
[
6
]
in the context of continuous finite elements methods for elliptic problems, which exploits superconvergence in negative-order norms. Formally defined as weighted dual norms, negative-order norms can be seen as a bridge connecting information in the Fourier/signal space with the one in physical space. For time-dependent problems, the DG method is proven to exhibit higher-order accuracy in negative-order norms—
2k+1
for dispersion errors and
2k+2
for dissipative errors—compared to only
k+1/2
in the
L^{2}
-norm. The pioneering work of Cockburn, Luskin, Shu, and Süli
[
12
]
established the framework for superconvergence analysis in negative norms and post-processing for time-dependent linear hyperbolic equations. This relies on a properly designed kernel that respects moment
[
35
]
. This technique was later extended to nonlinear scalar conservation laws
[
28
,
27
,
32
]
, nonlinear symmetric systems
[
33
]
, and, more recently, to the collisionless Vlasov–Maxwell system
[
21
]
. Besides exploiting negative-order norm, super-convergence for time-dependent problems can also be proved through a direct Fourier analysis
[
23
]
. Beyond signal space, superconvergence of DG methods for steady-state linear and nonlinear hyperbolic problems is analyzed through polynomial expansions of error equations
[
4
,
5
]
.

In this paper, we exploit post-processing based on Smooth-Increasing Accuracy-Conserving (SIAC) filters
[
46
,
34
,
14
]
to enhance the accuracy of both steady state and time-dependent RTE, which is a collisional kinetic equation. In practice, the desired quantity of interest is often low-dimensional macroscopic moments (e.g., the density) rather than the high-dimensional particle distributions. We theoretically establish the superconvergence results for both steady state and time-dependent RTE and numerically demonstrate that directly applying SIAC filters to these macroscopic moments significantly enhances their approximation accuracy. Since the effectiveness of SIAC filter relies on the superconvergence of the DG scheme, we also establish superconvergence results for both steady state and time-dependent RTE. The main contributions of this paper are as follows.

1.

For the steady-state problem, we prove
k+2
convergence order at Radau points within each element and
2k+2

This result is established by extend the framework based on coefficient matching for a polynomial expansion of the error equation

For the time-dependent problem with constant cross-section, we establish, for the first time, an

3.

Through a series of benchmark tests, we validate our theoretical results and showcase the SIAC filter’s capacity to drastically improve computational efficiency. By enhancing the accuracy achievable on coarse meshes, the filter reduces the time required to reach a target error tolerance in comparison to mesh refinement. Specifically, we observe an approximately

Background

We consider one-group, linear radiative transfer equation with isotropic scattering:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\Omega},t)}+\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\Omega},t)=\sigma_{s}(\overline{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x},t)-\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\Omega},t))-\sigma_{a}\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\Omega},t),

(1a)

\displaystyle\overline{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\langle{\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\Omega},t)}\rangle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\,\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\Omega},t)\,d\boldsymbol{\Omega},

(1b)

equipped with proper boundary conditions. Here,
\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\Omega},t)
is the particle distribution function (also known as intensity or angular flux) for spatial location
\boldsymbol{x}\in\boldsymbol{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, angular direction on the unit sphere
\boldsymbol{\Omega}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and time
t
. The macroscopic density (also known as scalar flux), as
\overline{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\langle{\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\Omega},t)}\rangle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\,\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\Omega},t)\,d\boldsymbol{\Omega}
is the average of the distribution function with respect to angular direction, and
\langle{\cdot}\rangle
represents the averaging operator over angular space. Here,
\sigma_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\geq 0
is an isotropic scattering cross section, while
\sigma_{a}(\boldsymbol{x})\geq 0
is the absorption cross section. The total cross section is defined as
\sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sigma_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})+\sigma_{a}(\boldsymbol{x})
. In this work we will assume the cross-sections
\sigma_{t}
and
\sigma_{a}
are constant in physical space
\boldsymbol{x}
.

In our analysis for the steady-state problem, we focus on the one-dimensional slab geometry. For a one-dimensional slab geometry, the particle distribution
\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\Omega},t)
only depends on a location
x\in\mathbb{R}
and the cosine of angle between the angular direction and the
x
-axis
\boldsymbol{\Omega}\in[-1,1]
. With this assumption, the equation can be simplified as
v=\cos(\Omega)
:

\displaystyle v\partial_{x}{\psi(x,v,t)}=\sigma_{s}(\overline{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x},t)-\psi(x,v,t))-\sigma_{a}\psi(x,v,t),

(2a)

\displaystyle\overline{\psi}(x,t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\,\psi(x,v)\,dv.

(2b)

On the other hand, following
[
20
]
, to help the error analysis in the time, since the cross-sections
\sigma_{a}
and
\sigma_{t}
are constant in space, we can reduce the analysis to Equation (
1a
) to a purely scattering system for the function (abusing notation)

\text{``}\psi:=e^{\sigma_{a}t}\psi\text{''}.

\psi
satisfies

\begin{split}&\partial_{t}\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\Omega},t)+\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\Omega},t)=\sigma(\overline{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x},t)-\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\Omega},t)),\\
&\overline{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\Omega},t)\,d\boldsymbol{\Omega}\end{split}

(3)

where
\sigma:=\sigma_{s}
. Henceforth, we restrict our attention to (
3
). The corresponding results for the case of nonzero absorption can then be recovered by reversing the transformation, which introduces exponential time decay when
\sigma_{a}>0
.

In order to define the numerical discretization and prove superconvergence, we first note a few preliminaries.

2.1
Notation and preliminaries

In this subsection, we discuss notation and estimates that will be used throughout this article.

2.1.1
Notation

Spaces and Norms.
In this article, we denote the domain for angular space as
\mathcal{S}=\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and for Euclidean space as
\boldsymbol{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}.

\boldsymbol{X}
refers to a general domain – either in Euclidean or angular space. Hence, when
\boldsymbol{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3},\,\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}.

We start by defining the multi-index
\alpha
, where

\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d}),\quad|\alpha|=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\,\alpha_{j}

and

D^{|\alpha|}f(\boldsymbol{x})=\partial_{x_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}{}\partial_{x_{2}}^{\alpha_{2}}{}\cdots\partial_{x_{d}}^{\alpha_{d}}{f(\boldsymbol{x})},

where
f
is some given function.

The usual
L^{p}-
norm is defined as

\|{f}\|_{L^{p}({\boldsymbol{X}})}=\left(\int_{\boldsymbol{X}}\,|f|^{p}\,d\boldsymbol{x}\right)^{1/p},

where
\|{f}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{D}})}=\text{ess sup}_{\mathcal{D}}\,|f|
.

The general Sobolov space for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{+},\,1\leq p\leq\infty
is defined as

W^{k,p}(\boldsymbol{X}):=\{v\in L^{p}(\boldsymbol{X}):\,D^{|\alpha|}f\in L^{p}(\boldsymbol{X}),\,|\alpha|\leq k\},

(4)

with norm

\|{f}\|_{W^{k,p}(\mathcal{D})}=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}\,\|{f}\|_{L^{n}({\mathcal{D}})}.

(5)

We note that when
p=2,
we denote
H^{k}=W^{k,2}
we simplify notation as

\|{f}\|_{{k},{\boldsymbol{X}}}=\|{f}\|_{H^{k}(\boldsymbol{X})}

and define the
L^{2}-
inner product as

(f,g)_{\boldsymbol{X}}=\int_{\boldsymbol{X}}\,fg\,d\boldsymbol{x}.

Further, for purposes of error analysis contained in this article, we define a weighted negative-order Sobolev norm for
F=(f^{1},\cdots,f^{N})
as

\|{F}\|_{-\ell,\boldsymbol{X}}=\sup_{\Phi\in(C_{0}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{X}))^{N}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{N}\omega_{j}(f^{j},\phi^{j})_{\boldsymbol{X}}\right]/\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\omega_{j}\|{\phi^{j}}\|_{\ell,\boldsymbol{X}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(6)

The weights
\omega_{j}
correspond to the quadrature weight ones in the
S_{N}
discretization for the phase-angle space; a more detailed discussion will be provided below.

Dual problem.
To obtain superconvergence for the time-dependent equation, we will need to bound the negative-order Sobolev norm of the divided differences of the error. This requires defining the dual problem.
The dual problem for
j=1,\dots,N_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
is defined as

\begin{split}&\partial_{t}\varphi^{j}+\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{j}\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi^{j}+\sigma(\overline{\varphi}-\varphi^{j})=0,\\
&\overline{\varphi}=\frac{1}{m(\mathcal{S})}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{N_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}}\omega_{j}\varphi^{j}.\end{split}

(7)

together with the final time condition
\varphi^{j}(x,T)=\varphi^{j}(x)\in C^{\infty}(X)
.

Note that for this dual problem satisfies

\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}\,\omega_{j}(\psi^{j},\varphi^{j})_{\boldsymbol{X}}=0.

(8)

This is an important property that allows us to phrase our error estimates in terms of the initial projection as well as the temporal growth in jumps across element interfaces.

Approximation polynomials.
Throughout this article, we will utilize the
Legendre polynomials
defined on a reference interval
[-1,1]
, which are defined by the recurrence relation

L_{0}(\xi)=1,\quad L_{1}(\xi)=\xi,\quad L_{k+1}(\xi)=\frac{2k+1}{k+1}L_{k}(\xi)-\frac{k}{k+1}L_{k-1}(\xi),\,k\geq 1.

(9)

These satisfy
\int_{-1}^{1}\,L_{k}(\xi)L_{j}(\xi)d\xi=\frac{2}{2k+1}\delta_{jk}
with
\delta_{jk}
being the Kronecker delta

\delta_{jk}=\begin{cases}1,\quad j=k\\
0,\quad j\neq k\end{cases}.

The
Radau polynomials
will also be useful. The
k
-th order left Radau polynomial,
R_{k}^{+},
and the right Radau polynomial,
R_{k}^{-},
on the reference element
[-1,1]
are defined as

R_{k}^{\pm}(\xi)=\begin{cases}L_{k}(\xi)\pm L_{k-1}(\xi),\quad&\text{if}\;k\geq 1,\\
0,\quad&\text{if}\;k=0.\end{cases}

(10)

Note that
R_{k}^{+}(-1)=0
and
R_{k}^{-}(1)=0
. In other words, the left (right) end point of the reference interval is a root of the left (right) Radau polynomial.

Quadrature.
For the discretization in phase space-angle, we will need to define a quadrature that can integrate exactly polynomials of total degree less than or equal to
N
(
|\alpha|=N
)

4.1

Here, we sketch key steps to prove Theorem

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}\,\omega_{j}{\left[\left(\psi^{j}(T),\varphi^{j}(T)\right)-\left(\psi^{j}_{h}(T),\varphi^{j}(T)\right)\right]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}\,\omega_{j}{\left[\left(\psi^{j}(0),\varphi^{j}(0)\right)-\left(\psi^{j}_{h}(0),\varphi^{j}(0)\right)-\int_{0}^{T}\,\frac{d}{dt}\left(\psi^{j}_{h},\varphi^{j}\right)\,ds\right]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}\,\omega_{j}{\left[\left(\psi^{j}(0)-\psi^{j}_{h}(0),\varphi^{j}(0)\right)-\int_{0}^{T}\,\left(\left((\psi^{j}_{h})_{t},\varphi^{j}\right)-\left(\psi^{j}_{h},(\varphi^{j})_{t}\right)\right)\,ds\right]},

Notice that for any
\chi^{j}
in
V_{h}
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}\,\omega_{j}{\left((\psi^{j}_{h})_{t},\varphi^{j}\right)}\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}\,\omega_{j}{\left[\left((\psi^{j}_{h})_{t},\varphi^{j}-\chi^{j}\right)+\left((\psi^{j}_{h})_{t},\chi^{j}\right)\right]}\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}\,\omega_{j}{\left[((\psi^{j}_{h})_{t},\varphi^{j}-\chi^{j})-B_{h}(\psi^{j}_{h},\chi^{j};\omega_{j})+\sigma(\overline{\psi}_{h}-\psi^{j}_{h},\chi^{j})\right]}\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}\,\omega_{j}{\left[(\psi^{j}_{h})_{t},\varphi^{j}-\chi^{j})\,d\tau-B_{h}(\psi^{j}_{h},\chi^{j};\omega_{j})+(\overline{\psi}_{h}-\psi^{j}_{h},\chi^{j})\right]}\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}\,\omega_{j}{\left[((\psi^{j}_{h})_{t},\varphi^{j}-\chi^{j})+B_{h}(\psi^{j}_{h},(\varphi^{j}-\chi^{j});\omega_{j})-\sigma(\overline{\psi}_{h}-\psi^{j}_{h},(\varphi^{j}-\chi^{j}))\right]}\,ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}\,\omega_{j}{\left[-B_{h}(\psi^{j}_{h},\varphi^{j};\omega_{j})+(\overline{\psi_{h}}-\psi^{j}_{h},\varphi^{j})\right]}\,ds

This allows for the error to be written as

\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}\,\omega_{j}{(e_{h}^{j}(T),\phi^{j})}=\Theta_{M}+\Theta_{N}+\Theta_{C}

(46)

where

\displaystyle\Theta_{M}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}\,\omega_{j}{(\eta^{j}_{h},\varphi(0))},

\displaystyle\Theta_{N}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T}\,\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}\,\omega_{j}{\left[\left((\psi^{j}_{h})_{t},\varphi^{j}-\chi^{j}\right)+B_{h}\left(\psi^{j}_{h},\varphi^{j}-\chi^{j};\omega_{j}\right)-\sigma\left(\overline{\psi}_{h}-\psi^{j}_{h},\varphi^{j}-\chi^{j}\right)\right]}\,ds

\displaystyle\Theta_{C}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T}\,\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}\,\omega_{j}{\left[\left(\psi^{j}_{h},\varphi^{j}_{t}\right)-B_{h}\left(\psi^{j}_{h},\varphi^{j};\omega_{j}\right)+\sigma\left(\overline{\psi}_{h}-\psi^{j}_{h},\varphi^{j})\right)\right]}\,ds

\Theta_{M}
,
\Theta_{N}
and
\Theta_{C}
are respectively the
projection, residual and consistency terms
.
Using Lemmas
10
,
11
, and
12
together with the dual estimate,
eq.

14
gives our desired estimate.

5
Extracting superconvergence using the SIAC filter

Now that we have proven that higher-order accuracy exists in the negative-order norm, we show how to extract that information via the Smoothness-Increasing Accuracy-Conserving (SIAC) filter.

To illustrate the ability of SIAC to perform on a given data set, it is useful to outline how SIAC works for general data as well as through the error estimates.

Given
\psi^{j}_{h},\,j=1,\dots,N_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}},
superconvergence can be extracted through convolving with a specially designed kernel,
{K_{H}\left(\cdot\right)}
:

(\psi^{j}_{h})^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})={K_{H}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)}\star\psi^{j}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{K_{H}\left(\boldsymbol{x}-{\mathbf{y}}\right)}\psi^{j}_{h}({\mathbf{y}})d{\mathbf{y}}.

(47)

where
H
represents the kernel scaling, in this case the uniform mesh size.

We show the reliance on the negative-order norm by decomposing the filtered error into a term that only depends on the number of moments and a term that relies on the error in the negative-order norm:

\|\psi^{j}-(\psi^{j}_{h})^{*}\|\leq\underbrace{\|\psi^{j}-{K_{H}\left(\right)}\star\psi^{j}\|}_{\text{Moments}}+\underbrace{\|{K_{H}\left(\right)}\star(\psi^{j}-\psi^{j}_{h})\|}_{\text{relies on negative-order norm}}\leq\mathcal{O}(H^{r+1})+\mathcal{O}(h^{s})

(48)

where
\|\cdot\|
is some norm. Here, the choice of
r
is the number of moments the filter is designed to capture and
s
is the order of accuracy of the approximation. In this article, the ability to bound the
L^{2}-
norm by the negative-order norm is utilized and
r=2k+1.

The success of the filter relies on the following results.

Theorem 13

(Bramble and Schatz
[
6
]
)
.

Let
k\geq 0
. For
T>0
, let
\Psi=(\psi^{n})_{n=1}^{N_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}
be the exact solution to problem (
21b
), satisfying
\psi^{j}\in C([0,T];H^{k+1}(\boldsymbol{X}))
,
1\leq j\leq N_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
. Let
\Omega_{0}+2supp(K_{h}^{2(k+1),k+1}(\boldsymbol{x}))\subset\subset\boldsymbol{X}
and
\Psi_{h}=(\psi^{j}_{h})_{j=1}^{N_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}
then

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}\omega_{j}\|{\psi^{j}(T)-K_{h}^{2(k+1),k+1}\star\psi^{j}_{h}(T)}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{0})}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{h^{2k+2}}{(2k+2)!}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}\omega_{j}|\psi^{j}|_{2k+2,\boldsymbol{X}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}+C_{\mathrm{P}}\sum_{|\lambda|\leq k+1}\|{\partial_{h}^{\lambda}(\Psi-\Psi_{h})}\|_{-(k+1),\boldsymbol{X}}.

(49)

where
C_{\mathrm{P}}
depends solely on
\Omega_{0}
,
\boldsymbol{X}
,
k
, and it is independent of
h
.

In (
49
), we used the notation of the divided differences, which are defined as

\partial_{h_{x_{i}}}f(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{h_{x_{i}}}\left(f(\boldsymbol{x}+\frac{1}{2}h_{x_{i}}\mathbf{e}_{i})-f(\boldsymbol{x}-\frac{1}{2}h_{x_{i}}\mathbf{e}_{i})\right).

(50)

where
\mathbf{e}_{i}
is the unit multi-index whose
i
-th component is
1
and all others
0
.

For any multi-index
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})
we set the
\lambda
-th order difference quotient to be

\partial_{h}^{\lambda}f(\boldsymbol{x})=(\partial^{\lambda_{1}}_{h_{x_{1}}}\partial^{\lambda_{2}}_{h_{x_{2}}}\partial^{\lambda_{3}}_{h_{x_{3}}})f(\boldsymbol{x}).

(51)

5.1
SIAC formulation.

The Smoothness-Increasing Accuracy-Conserving (SIAC) kernel is comprised of
r+1
(scaled) function translates of a given function,

{K\left(\cdot\right)}=\sum_{\gamma=1}^{r+1}\,{c_{\gamma}}\psi_{{\text{\tiny{{T}}}_{\gamma}}}(\cdot),

(52)

in this article, central B-Splines are used.
{B_{\text{\tiny T},n}}
represents
n^{th}
-order central B-spline with knot sequence,
T
and smoothness
n-2
. The scaling,
H
, is generally tied to the mesh size. The central
B-splines are defined through the relations

\boldsymbol{B}_{T,1}=\chi_{\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)},\qquad\boldsymbol{B}_{T,n}=\boldsymbol{B}_{T,n-1}\star\boldsymbol{B}_{T,1},

where
{\bf T}_{n}
represents a knot matrix for the
n^{th}
order spline (i.e. B-spline breaks)
[
42
]
. For a symmetric kernel of
2k+1
B-splines, the general form of the knot matrix is

{\bf T}=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{n+2k}{2}&\frac{-(n+2k)+2}{2}&\cdots&\frac{n-2k}{2}\\
-\frac{n+2k-2}{2}&\frac{-(n+2k)+4}{2}&\cdots&\frac{n+2-2k}{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{2k-n}{2}&\frac{2k-n+2}{2}&\cdots&\frac{n+2k}{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(53)

Each row of the knot matrix gives the B-Spline breaks of the
\gamma^{th}
B-spline
[
42
]
(
\gamma=1,\dots,{2k+1}
). The
c_{\gamma}
are weights of the B-splines, which are determined by ensuring that the kernel satisfies consistency plus
r=2k
moments. We further note that
where
{K_{H}\left(\cdot\right)}=\frac{1}{H}{K\left(\frac{\cdot}{H}\right)}
can be viewed as a normalized probability density function.
We note that Mock and Lax
[
35
]
introduced the importance of satisfying moment conditions and pre-processing data. This allows for recovering accuracy for discontinuous functions – away from any discontinuities. The pre-processing of data is important for methods not based on Galerkin orthogonality. Further, utilizing a linear combination of B-Splines allows for writing derivatives can be written as divided differences of lower order splines,

\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\partial x^{\alpha}}{\boldsymbol{B}_{{\text{\tiny{{T}}}_{n}}}(x)}=\partial_{H}^{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{B}_{{\text{\tiny{{T}}}_{n}-\alpha}}(x)},

where
\partial_{H}^{\alpha}
represents the
\alpha^{th}
divided difference. This ensures that when we pass to the negative order norm, the order of accuracy is not reduced. These ideas were introduced by
Bramble and Schatz
[
6
]
and Thomeé
[
47
]
.

Here, we note that, using equally spaced knots for the B-spline filter, the Fourier transform of the SIAC kernel is given by

\mathcal{F}(K)=\widehat{K}(\xi)=\underbrace{\sinc\left(\frac{\xi}{2}\right)^{n}}_{\text{controls dissipation}}\underbrace{\left(c_{\frac{r+2}{2}}+2\sum_{\gamma=1}^{\lceil\frac{r}{2}\rceil}\,c_{\gamma}\cos\left(\left(\gamma-\frac{r+2}{2}\right)\xi\right)\right)}_{\text{moment conditions}}

(54)

As can be seen, the smoothness chosen for the B-splines controls the amount of dissipation and the number of moments controls the accuracy.

There are a few choices for extending the filter to multi-dimensions. One method is via a tensor product:

{K_{{\bf H}}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)}={K_{{h_{1}}}\left(x_{1}\right)}{K_{{h_{2}}}\left(x_{2}\right)}\cdots{K_{{h_{d}}}\left(x_{d}\right)}.

However, for computational efficiency, a rotated one-dimensional filter, the Line SIAC (LSIAC) kernel
[
13
]
, is often used

{K_{H}\left(\right)}={K_{\Gamma}\left(\right)}\Rightarrow\psi^{j,*}_{h}(\overline{x},\overline{y})=\int_{\Gamma}{K_{\Gamma}\left(\frac{\Gamma(0)-\Gamma(t)}{h_{t}}\right)}\psi^{j}_{h}(\Gamma(t))\,dt.

(55)

In
[
13
]
, results in two- and three-dimensions are demonstrated. For two-dimensions, filtering is performed along the line
\Gamma(t)=(\overline{x},\overline{y})+\lambda(\cos(\theta),\ \sin(\theta))
, with an angle of rotation
\theta=\tan\left(\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}\right)
.

5.2
A note on computation

An illustration of the improved performance from using the Line SIAC kernel in two-dimensions can be seen by considering a kernel consisting of 5 B-splines of order 3 on a structured mesh. To filter one point, 196 two-dimensional integrals are required for the implementation of the tensor product filter, while only 21 one-dimensional integrals are required for LSIAC. Computing these integrals require quadratures that respect both B-Spline breaks and mesh breaks. Computing these breaks is the most costly aspect of the filter. We note that if polynomials of degree
k
are used in the DG approximation and B-Splines of order
k+1
are used,
\lceil\frac{2k+1}{2}\rceil
quadrature points per region are required.

Conclusions

In this paper, we theoretically and computationally present the benefit of applying SIAC filters to the upwind DG method for solving the steady-state and time-dependent RTE.

1.

We have proven
(2k+2)
-th order accuracy for the steady state RTE at the outflow edge and
(k+2)
-th order accuracy at the interior roots of the Radau points on each spatial element, and
(2k+\frac{1}{2})
-th order accuracy for the time-dependent problem with respect to a weighted negative-order norm. Our numerical results validate
(2k+2)
-th order superconvergence for steady state problem and demonstrate
(2k+1)
-th order superconvergence for the time-dependent problem.

2.

Furthermore, we numerically demonstrate that directly applying SIAC filters to low-dimensional macroscopic density can greatly reduce the computational time to reach a desired level of accuracy.

Potential future directions are as follows: (1) For varying cross sections, our current superconvergence proof can be extended by establishing divided difference results for the DG approximation. Though numerically observed superconvergnce, a complete superconvergence proof for spatial cross sections is still an open questions. Additionally, analysis for problems involving nonlinear thermal radiation, multi-energy groups and anisotropic scattering is challenging and worth investigation.
(2) Our current analysis mainly focuses on improving convergence order in physical space. However, another important area of exploration is enhancing angular accuracy and mitigating ray effects through post-processing in angular space.
