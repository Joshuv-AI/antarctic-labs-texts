Prevalence and associated factors of depression among adult patients with epilepsy in Ethiopia: a systematic review and meta-analysis, 2025
Authors: Gebremeskel Kibret Abebe, Gebrehiwot Berie Mekonnen, Yeshimebet Tamir Tsehay, Sosina Tamre Mamo, Mengistu Abebe Messelu, Tiruye Azene Demile, Wubet Tazeb Wondie, Alamirew Enyew Belay, Ousman Adal, Sileshi Mulatu, Asnake Gashaw Belayneh, Henok Biresaw
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpilepsy is a major public health issue in Africa. Among individuals with epilepsy, the prevalence of depression is estimated to be two to three times higher than in the general population. The coexistence of epilepsy and depression has a detrimental impact on patients’ quality of life and increases the risk of anxiety disorders, substance use (including alcohol and drug dependence), suicide, poor medication adherence, and uncontrolled seizures.ObjectivesTo conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis on the prevalence and associated factors of depression among adult patients with epilepsy in Ethiopia, 2025.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was performed across several databases, including PubMed, MEDLINE, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, UpToDate, EMBASE, and National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE). The review focused on the prevalence of depression and its associated factors among patients with epilepsy. A random-effects model was applied to conduct pooled analysis, and the Joanna Briggs Institute quality appraisal checklist was used to evaluate the quality of included studies.ResultsA total of eleven studies (n = 4,027) were included in the final analysis. The pooled prevalence of depression among adult patients with epilepsy in Ethiopia was 43.26% (95% CI: 38.45%, 48.08%; I2 = 89.96%; p < 0.0001). Subgroup analysis indicated that the prevalence was 48.61% (95% CI: 44.92%–52.31%) in the Oromia region, and 38.28% (95% CI: 27.50%, 49.06%) in Addis Ababa. Poor adherence to anti-seizure medication 1.41 (95% CI: 1.17, 1.65), perceived stigma 1.74 (95% CI: 1.57, 1.91), and lower educational status 0.39 (95% CI: 0.26, 0.51) were significant predictors of depression among adult patients with epilepsy.ConclusionThe overall prevalence of depression among adult patients with epilepsy in Ethiopia remains high. Perceived stigma, low educational status, and poor adherence to anti-seizure medication were identified as key risk factors. Background

Epilepsy is one of the most prevalent neurologic conditions, affecting approximately 50 million people worldwide, with more than 80% of people with epilepsy living in low- and middle-income countries [1, 2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that around 25 million people in Africa have epilepsy. Epilepsy is a significant public health concern in Africa, with its prevalence ranging from 0.5% to 10% across different countries [3, 4].

In developed countries, the annual prevalence of new epilepsy cases ranges from 30 to 50 per 100,000 people in the general population. In contrast, this figure is estimated to be up to twice as high in developing countries. Similarly, the risk of premature deaths is estimated to be up to three times higher in people with epilepsy compared with the general population, with the highest rates observed in low and middle-income countries [5]. In Ethiopia the prevalence of epilepsy is estimated at 5.2 per 1000 population [6].

People with epilepsy (PWE) often experiences social, psychological, physical, and mental health challenges. They face multiple problems, including stigma, perceived stress, educational difficulties, unemployment, and brain damage, all of which negatively impact their quality of life [7–9]. PWE are at high risk of developing mental disorders, with an approximately eightfold higher risk compared to with the general populations, and about one in three patients with epilepsy develop such conditions [10, 11].

Globally, about 9.5% PWE are affected by depressive disorders, and more than 80% of these cases occur in low-income regions where psychiatric comorbidities are often underrecognized and undertreated [12–15]. In Sub-Saharan Africa, the prevalence of depression among patients with epilepsy was 32.71% [16]. In Ethiopia, the prevalence of depression among adult patients with epilepsy ranges from 30.90–53.90% [17, 18].

The presence of depression among patients with epilepsy is associated with different psychosocial difficulties, including poor treatment adherence, reduced quality of life, unemployment, lower educational attainment, increased health care burden and cost, and a higher risk for suicide. The coexistence of depression and epilepsy further increases the risk of suicide, anxiety disorder, substance use (including alcohol and drug addiction), poor medication adherence, uncontrolled seizure, and overall negative impact on patients’ quality of life [10, 19–21].

Several factors contribute to the development of depression among patients with epilepsy. Poor social support, low educational status, high seizure frequency, and polytherapy [12, 22–25], along with poor medication adherence, gender, stigma, and anxiety [22, 25, 26], as well as unemployment, and stress [27], have been identified as factors associated with depression among patients with epilepsy.

Despite the high burden of depression among patients with epilepsy and its impact on quality of life, psychosocial functioning, and treatment outcomes, data on the prevalence and associated factors of depression among adult patients with epilepsy in Ethiopia remain limited and inconsistent. Understanding these patterns is crucial for guiding effective mental health interventions, supporting evidence-based practices, and enhancing patient care. Accordingly, this study aims to systematically estimate the prevalence of depression among adult patients with epilepsy in Ethiopia and to identify the key factors associated with its occurrence.

Methods and materials

Data sources and searching strategies

This systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines for reporting observational studies [28]. A comprehensive literature search was conducted by the authors in PubMed, Medline, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, UpToDate, EMBASE, and National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) to identify articles published between January 2015 and April 2025. In addition, the PROSPERO registry was searched to find ongoing systematic reviews.

In PubMed, the search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and text words, including depression or common mental health illness, determinants of depression or associated factors, and patients with epilepsy, either individually or in combination. Similar modified searches were conducted for the other databases. In addition, manual search of reference lists, a forward citation search using Web of Science and Google Scholar, and a search of gray literature and unpublished were used.

The search strategy was structured as follows: (“prevalence”) AND (“depression and patients with epilepsy”) AND (Ethiopia). These terms were further combined with the names of specific Ethiopian regions, such as Amhara, Tigray, Oromia, etc. Boolean operators “OR” and “AND” were used to combine search terms/phrases within and across key concepts, respectively, and “NOT” was used for excluding irrelevant results. Furthermore, in Ovid databases, truncation (*), adjacency searching (ADJn), and wildcard symbols were used to account for spelling and word ending variations, and pertinent limits (filters), such as a restriction to human studies, were also used (Supplementary Table 1).

Study selection and screening

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, retrospective follow-up studies, prospective cohort studies, and cross-sectional studies involving adult patients with epilepsy were included. The selected studies focused on the prevalence of depression and/or its associated factors among patients with epilepsy. Studies were excluded if they did not report the prevalence of depression among individuals with epilepsy or lacked sufficient extractable data. Only studies published in English and with a complete manuscript were included.

Retrieved studies were exported to the EndNote version 8 reference manager to remove duplicates. Based on predefined study selection criteria, two authors (K.A. and A.M.) independently evaluated the eligibility of studies. During the selection process, titles and abstracts were reviewed, and full texts of all possibly pertinent records were obtained. All included studies were evaluated for data on the prevalence of depression among adult patients with epilepsy. Any disagreements were resolved by discussing with a senior reviewer (B.B.) (Fig. 1). Inclusion and exclusion criteria

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we included studies that reporting the prevalence and associated factors of depression among adult patients with epilepsy. For this review, adults were defined as individuals aged 18 years and older. The search strategy focused on the study population regardless of interventions, comparisons, or outcomes. Additionally, only studies published in the English language were included. To ensure the quality and relevance of the selected studies, citations without abstracts and full texts, anonymous reports, and qualitative studies were excluded from the study.

Data quality assessment

The quality of the included studies was evaluated by using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) quality appraisal checklist [30]. Two reviewers (M.M, T.Y, and M.S) assessed the included studies independently, and any disagreements were resolved through consensus among the authors and a collective re-evaluation of the relevant articles. Studies that scored 4 or more points were considered low risk and classified as good quality, while those studies scored 3 or fewer were considered high risk and classified as poor quality. Poor quality studies were not included in the final analysis (Supplementary Table 2). All selected studies were found to be of average quality, meeting the established criteria. This rigorous quality assessment process contributes to the overall robustness and reliability of the findings derived from the systematic review and meta-analysis.

Data extraction

The primary objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to estimate the pooled prevalence of depression among adult patients with epilepsy. The secondary objective is to identify factors that contribute to the occurrences of depression in this population. Two authors (D.T. and M.G) extracted data by using a predetermined data collection Microsoft Excel spreadsheet, and two others (N.H and W.W.) cross-verified the extracted data.

For each included literature, essential information such as the first author’s name, year of publication, study design, sample size, number of the studied population, odds ratio, relative risk (RR), depression diagnosing criteria, and prevalence of depression, was extracted.

In cases where data points were either unclear or missing, efforts were taken to email the first and last authors of the relevant research in order to get the information as needed. Also, a follow-up email was sent one month later to ensure a comprehensive and accurate dataset for analysis.

Statistical analysis

A random-effects model, as described by DerSimonian and Laird [31], was used to estimate the weighted prevalence of depression, along with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). The Meta and Forest Plot tools in STATA version 17 were used to perform statistical pooling and quantitative data synthesis using meta-analyses and forest plots. Using the I2 test, the percentage of overall variation across the studies and heterogeneity among the groups were evaluated. The value of I2 more than 50%shows the presence of heterogeneity.

Subgroup analyses were carried out in order to address heterogeneity by defining the study location and sample size. Moreover, a sensitivity test was performed to assess whether an individual study had an effect on the combined prevalence of depression. Publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of a funnel plot and by examining the Meta bias Egger test, and Meta trim and fill analysis.

Result

Study characteristics

A total of 6529 studies were identified from different databases, and 6475 studies were excluded after the title and abstract review. Finally, 54 studies were screened for full-text review, and 12 articles were included for the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, eleven cross-sectional studies (n = 4,027) were included [12, 13, 17, 18, 32–38] to estimate the pooled prevalence of depression among adult patients with epilepsy in Ethiopia. Additionally, one case-control study was included to assess factors associated with depression in this population [39]. Regarding study regions, four studies [17, 18, 32, 34] were from the Amhara region, two studies [13, 33] from Addis Ababa, two studies [12, 37] from the Oromia region, and three studies [35, 36, 38] from the Debub region. Participants ages ranged from 18 to 72 years. Prevalence of depression among adult patients with epilepsy in Ethiopia

The prevalence of depression among adult patients with epilepsy in Ethiopia ranged from 30.90% to 53.90%. Using a DerSimonian–Laird random-effects model, the pooled prevalence was 43.26% (95% CI: 38.45%–48.08%; I2 = 89.96%; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2). To address potential issues with implausible confidence limits, a random-effects logistic mixed model (GLMM) was also fitted. This model treated the number of depressed cases out of the total sample in each study as a binomial outcome and included a study-level random intercept. The pooled prevalence estimated by the GLMM was 43.10% (95% CI: 38.40%–47.80%), consistent with the DerSimonian–Laird estimate. The between-study variance (τ2) on the log-odds scale was 0.091 (95% CI: 0.035–0.235), indicating moderate heterogeneity. The likelihood ratio test comparing the random-effects model with a standard logistic model was statistically significant (chibar2 = 68.34, p < 0.001), confirming the presence of significant variability across studies (Supplementary Figure 1).Fig. 2Forest plot showing prevalence of depression among patients with epilepsy in Ethiopia

Subgroup analysis was conducted on the study regions. The prevalence of depression among patients with epilepsy was 38.28% (27.50%, 49.06%) in Addis Abeba and 48.61% (44.92%, 52.31%) in the Oromia region (supplementary Figure 2). Further analysis by seizure frequency revealed a prevalence of 51.50% (95% CI: 48.10–55.00%) among patients experiencing seizures three or more times per month (Supplementary Figure 3).

Publication bias was evaluated using visual inspection of the Meta funnel plot, which showed asymmetrical distribution (Supplementary Figure 4), suggesting potential publication bias. However, the Egger regression test yields a p-value of 0.92, which is suggestive of no publication bias (Supplementary figure 5).

Sensitivity analysis: leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed that the pooled prevalence ranged from 42.28% to 44.6% when individual studies were excluded. This indicates that no single study had a substantial influence on the overall estimate (Supplementary Figure 6).

A meta-regression was performed to examine whether the median age of participants influenced the prevalence of depression among epilepsy patients. The coefficient for median age was 1.09 (95% CI: −2.64 to 4.82, p = 0.567), indicating that median age was not significantly associated with depression prevalence. Moreover, the residual heterogeneity remained high (I2 = 91.65%), and R2 was 0%, suggesting that median age did not explain the between-study variability. These results indicate that differences in median age across studies are unlikely to confound the overall estimate of depression prevalence (Supplementary Figure 7).

Factors associated with depression among patients with epilepsy

Several risk factors are associated with the prevalence of depression among adult patients with epilepsy in Ethiopia. Evidenced from different studies indicates that perceived stigma [12, 13, 33, 34, 40], poly therapy [12, 13, 17, 32], low educational status [12, 13, 32, 34, 36, 40], ASM adherence [32, 33, 36, 40], seizure frequency [12, 13, 17, 32, 34, 36], current substances use [17, 33], poor social support [12, 34, 35], current alcohol use [17], residences [34], perceived stress [32, 40], duration of ASM [40], gender [33, 35], income [13], marital status [17], employment status [35] and poor knowledge about epilepsy [36] were a factors associated with of depression among patients with epilepsy.

A total of six variables were selected for analysis across the 12 studies. The pooled estimate of this systematic review and meta-analysis showed that adherence to antiseizure medication, the presence of perceived stigma, and educational status were significant predictors of depression among adult patients with epilepsy in Ethiopia. Patients with poor medication adherence had 1.4 times higher odds of developing depression, 1.41 (95% CI: 1.17, 1.65), compared to those with good adherence (Supplementary Figure 8). Patients experienced perceived stigma, had a higher risk of developing depression compared to those without perceived stigma, 1.74 (95% CI: 1.57, 1.91) (Supplementary Figure 9). Regarding educational status, patients with a lower educational level had a higher risk of developing depression compared to those with a higher educational level, 0.39 (95% CI: 0.26, 0.51) (Supplementary Figure 10).

Discussion

The coexistences of depression and epilepsy have a negatively affects patients’ quality of life, leads to poor adherence to treatment, increases the risk of suicidal ideation, rises healthcare cost, and increase the risk of brain damage or death. This systematic review and meta-analysis found that the pooled prevalence of depression among patients with epilepsy in Ethiopia was 43.26% (95% CI: 38.40–48.10%), which is substantially higher than the prevalence reported in European countries, ranging from 22.90% to 23.10% [41, 42]. A likely explanation for this disparity is that low-income countries, including Ethiopia, face greater challenges, such as stigma, psychosocial stress, limited availability of anti-seizure medications, and poor adherence to ASM [43, 44], compared to developed countries. These factors together increase the prevalence of depression among adult patients with epilepsy.

Moreover, our review found that the pooled prevalence of depression among patients with epilepsy in Ethiopia was higher than that reported in the previous meta-analysis, 24.20% [45], and 21.90% [46]. This disparity may be attributed to the fact that the earlier studies focused solely on major depressive disorder, excluding other classifications of depression. In contrast, our review encompassed all forms of depression, potentially contributing to the observed increase in the pooled prevalence. Also, this review revealed that the prevalence of depression among patients with epilepsy was higher than the study done in Sub-Saharan Africa, 32.71% [16].

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, the prevalence of depression among patients with epilepsy was 38.28% in Addis Abeba and 48.61% in the Oromia region. The possible reason may be that in Addis Abeba, patients with epilepsy have access to medical care, are aware of the importance of taking their medications as prescribed, and have access to psychosocial support, which result in reduced occurrences of depression.

This meta-analysis showed that adult patients with epilepsy who experienced stigma had a higher risk of association with depression, which was supported by the study done in Mekelle, Tigray [40], Addis Ababa [13, 33], Gondar [34], Guinea [47], Europe [48], and also supported by a recent meta-analysis [22]. This is because people with epilepsy might be overprotected and restricted from doing many activities by their family members, friends, or teachers. Eventually, this stigma, poor coping mechanism, and low self-esteem lead to negative outcomes related to recovery, including depressive symptoms, social avoidance, and a preference for adopting avoidance coping strategies [33].

Regarding educational status patient with epilepsy with low educational status were at high risk of developing depression, which is supported by the study done in Kenya [49] and previous meta-analysis [22]. The possible explanation for this association is that those with high educational status are aware and cope with their illness, as well as can adjust psychological issues when compared to the illiterates. Additionally, individuals with lower educational status might face difficulties with socioeconomic stressors like unemployment, poverty, and economic dependency, and may have poor coping strategies to their illness, which in turn leads to social isolation, poor adherence to their ASM, school dropout and contributes to a poorer psychological adjustment that they face in life [50].

Patients with epilepsy who had poor anti-seizure medication adherence are more likely to be associated with developing depressive disorder, which is supported by the study done in Kenya [49] and previous meta-analysis [22]. The possible reason may be that those patients who had difficulties of adhering to their anti-seizure medication may result in breakthrough seizures that may lead an individual to develop depression. Most studies show that uncontrolled seizures are associated with a higher prevalence of depression than seizure-free patients [51].

Limitations

The findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis can be used by policymakers, the Federal Minister of Health, stakeholders, and researchers to design appropriate strategies to reduce the comorbidity burden of epilepsy and depression among people with epilepsy. This review included both hospital-based and community-based studies; however, no community-based studies were available for inclusion. As patients attending hospitals often have uncontrolled epilepsy, the pooled prevalence may be overestimated compared to community-based populations. Therefore, we recommend future community-based studies across regions to ensure generalizability and representativeness of the findings. In addition, all the included studies were cross-sectional, and case-control studies only show a temporal relationship of factors associated with depression, such as medication adherence, rather than establish cause and effect. We recommend that be conducted to address cause-and-effect relationship with associated factors and depression. This systematic review and meta-analysis included only studies published in English, excluding those in local or other languages. This may result in some relevant studies may have been missed and could have biased the pooled prevalence of depression among patients with epilepsy in Ethiopia.

Conclusion

The overall prevalence of depression among patients with epilepsy in Ethiopia was high. Perceived stigma, lower educational status, and poor adherence to anti-seizure medication were significantly associated with depression in this population. Greater emphasis should be placed on reducing stigma and promoting adherence to anti-seizure medication to improve mental health outcomes among patients with epilepsy.
