Propose or Vote: A simple Democratic Procedure We thank Pio Blieske, Barton Lee, Giorgis Giovanni, Cesar Martinelli and participants at the 8th ETH/CEPR Workshop on Democracy for valuable comments and suggestions.
Hans Gersbach

Abstract
This paper introduces a simple democratic procedure. In a first stage, all members of a polity decide whether to apply for proposal-making or later vote on proposals made in the second stage. This procedure is called Propose or Vote (PoV). With appropriate default points and majority voting over two randomly selected proposals, the PoV procedure can implement the Condorcet winner with only one round of voting if a Condorcet winner exists. We explore ways to establish uniqueness, alternative voting procedures over the selected alternatives, and the application to elections. In the latter case, agents can decide whether to stand for election to an office or to vote on the set of candidates. Keywords: proposal-making, democracy, majority voting; JEL Classification: C72, D70, D72.

Introduction

In various collective decision-making settings — such as committee or polity deliberations and legislative processes — participants must select a single alternative from a vast array of options. A common approach in these contexts is to employ successive rounds of pairwise majority voting. For example, legislative bodies refine bills through sequential votes on amendments, while negotiating parties in multi-agent resource allocation problems often reach consensus through step-by-step adjustments. However, when the number of alternatives is large, it becomes impractical to evaluate all options exhaustively. Furthermore, the final outcome is highly contingent on both the subset of alternatives selected for voting and the order in which they are considered. This creates a well-documented vulnerability: strategic agenda setters can manipulate the decision-making process to steer outcomes in their favor, a phenomenon extensively studied in the
literature.

In this paper, we introduce a simple procedure to address this challenge.
In the initial stage, all members of the polity choose whether to participate as proposal-makers or to reserve their role for voting on proposals in the subsequent stage. This process is called Propose or Vote (PoV).

The PoV procedure governs proposal-making through the following set of rules: If no agent chooses to make a proposal, an alternative is randomly selected as the society’s final choice.
2
2

2

An alternative is to give status quo that is implemented in this case.

If a single agent opts to propose, his/her proposal is automatically implemented. If two agents choose to propose, their proposals are put to a vote among the remaining members of society. If more than two agents opt for proposal-making, two individuals from this group are randomly selected to submit proposals, which are then put to a vote. If everyone opt for making a proposal, again an alternative is randomly selected as the society’s final choice. Later, we examine whether a different procedure to decide on the proposals made can help to achieve uniquely determined outcomes.

We examine whether this idea works in the simplest context with a one-dimensional set of alternatives, and a society of agents with symmetric single-peaked preferences with a publicly known distribution. We analyze the strategic incentives created by the PoV procedure for agents, and examine whether it works in selecting the socially optimal Condorcet winner in one round of voting.

We show that with an odd number of agents, there exists an equilibrium in which precisely the median agent applies for proposal-making, proposes his/her peak and the peak is implemented. When the number of agents is even, both the left median and the right median opt for proposal-making, propose their peaks, and one of them will be selected by the remaining agents.

We then explore the uniqueness of the result. With an even number of agents, we show with counterexamples that there are other equilibria in which more extreme proposals are implemented. With an odd number of agents, we show uniqueness under some further conditions. One can always ensure that there is an odd number of agents by adding an Artificial Agent (AA) when necessary.

We also explore alternative ways to select a proposal from a given set of proposals which allows establishing uniqueness under a different set of conditions. Moreover, we show that the procedure can be used in the same way for elections. Agents can decide to propose themselves (or somebody else) for an office, and the agents who have not made a proposal elect one candidate from the set of candidates.

In many institutional procedures for committee or parliamentary decisions, procedures rely on agent-driven proposal making and voting, as a centralised mechanism designer is absent.
Gersbach & Valkanova (2025)
demonstrated that random selection of agenda-setters in an iterative majority voting framework gives them incentives to propose the Condorcet winner. In this paper we explore how a PoV procedure can solve this problem.

3
The Model

Consider a society that consists of
N
agents. They have to select an alternative
x
from a one-dimensional policy space, which we take as an interval of arbitrarily chosen, but fixed, size
[-A,A]
for some
A\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

To do so, they introduced the following procedure, called Propose or Vote (PoV) which is structured into three stages.

1.

Each agent decides whether he will propose a policy or be placed into the pool of voters. The set of proposers will be denoted by
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}
and the set of voters by
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{V}
.

2.

Each proposer
p\in\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}
decides on a proposal
x_{p}\in[-A,A]
he wants to put forward.

3.

Among all those proposals, two are selected uniformly at random and put to a vote. Only the agents who opted to be placed in the voter pool
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{V}
in the first stage are allowed to participate in the voting procedure. The winner of the vote according to the majority rule is implemented.

Each of the society’s agents
i\in[N]=\{1,...,N\}
is characterized by his/her type
\theta_{i}\in[-A,A]
and the types satisfy
\theta_{1}<\dots<\theta_{i}<\theta_{i+1}<\dots<\theta_{N}
. The utility of an agent of type
\theta_{i}
from an alternative
x\in[-A,A]
is given by

u_{\theta_{i},x}:=-(x-\theta_{i})^{2}.

Therefore, it has a unique maximum at
\theta_{i}
and is symmetric around
\theta_{i}
.
We will use the notation
u_{\theta_{i},v}
to represent the potentially expected utility of an agent who chooses to vote.

We denote the median agent as
m
, which, due to the ordering of the
\theta_{i}
, is the median of the set
[N]
. The left median agent is denoted as
m_{l}:=\max\{i\in[N]\mid i\leq m\}
, and the right median agent is denoted as
m_{r}:=\min\{i\in[N]\mid i\geq m\}
.

If the left and right median agents have the same value, they coincide with the median agent
m
. The types of the median, left median, and right median agents are
\theta_{m}
,
\theta_{m_{l}}
, and
\theta_{m_{r}}
, respectively, and they always satisfy the inequality
\theta_{m_{l}}\leq\theta_{m}\leq\theta_{m_{r}}
.

In the PoV-procedure described in three stages above, there might occur some edge cases, we haven’t dealt with yet. We impose the following rules to resolve these:

1.

If no agent chooses to make a proposal, an alternative is selected uniformly at random from the agents’ types
\{\theta_{i}\in[-A,A]\mid i\in[N]\}
and implemented without any vote.

2.

If a single agent opts to propose, their proposal is automatically implemented.

3.

If two or more agents choose to propose, the general procedure described above applies.

4.

If the vote ends in a draw, a coin flip decides which of the two selected proposals is implemented. In particular, if everyone opts to propose, two of the submitted proposals are selected uniformly at random and then a coin flip decides between the two. This is the same as implementing one of the proposals uniformly at random.

To simplify the analysis, we impose the following tie-break rules.

1.

If an agent expects the same utility from both voting and submitting a proposal, he/she decides to vote.

2.

If a voter is indifferent between two proposals, he/she abstains from voting.

The first tie-breaking rule is quite important. In order to make sure that proposal making is not too attractive, one could impose an arbitrary, sufficiently small and positive cost for proposal making. This would strengthen the results as agents will not make a proposal if the proposal does not improve the agent’s expected utility. Notice that the cost of the proposal also needs to be sufficiently small to guarantee that for those agents for whom proposal making was strictly better than voting, this needs to still be true.

To analyze the dynamic voting game, we use the equilibrium concept of Subgame Perfect Nash Equilibrium (SPNE) with the refinement that agents in the last stage eliminate weakly dominated strategies. Since there is only one voting round, this essentially means that all agents who have retained their voting rights will vote sincerely in the final stage and thus vote for the alternative they prefer more than the other one, if two alternatives are put to a vote.

4
The Simple Result

In this section, we analyze how proposers choose their optimal proposals, taking into account their type, the distribution of preferences, the status quo, and the resulting equilibria of the PoV procedure.
The following Theorem shows the existence of equilibria in the PoV-procedure.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that we have a polity with
N
agents, each characterized by their type
\theta_{i}\in[-A,A]
. Then there exists an equilibrium for the Propose or Vote-procedure among the
N
agents. This equilibrium is given as follows:

1.

If
N
is odd, the constellation where the median agent
m
of type
\theta_{m}
opts for submitting the proposal
\theta_{m}
and all other agents opt for taking the vote, is an equilibrium.

2.

If
N
is even, the constellation where the left and the right median opt for submitting their own type as proposal and all the other agents opt to vote is an equilibrium.

The proof Theorem
1
is given in the appendix.
Theorem
1
illustrates the inherent trade-offs. Consider, for example, the case where
N
is odd. Agents other than
m
face the following dilemma: by choosing to become proposers, they lose their voting power, which may result in the final decision either remaining unchanged or shifting further from their preferred alternative. As a result, they have no incentive to take on the role of proposer. If
N
is even, agents other than the two median types have no incentive to become a proposal maker. Suppose that the agent is to the left of the left median. Then, becoming a proposal-maker and offering his/her peak would shift the probability that the right median is selected to
\frac{2}{3}
and the probability that the left median is selected declines to
\frac{1}{3}
. If the agent remains a voter, the probability for the left median to be selected is
\frac{1}{2}
, and the probability that the right median is selected declines to
\frac{1}{2}
.

Other Equilibria

We next discuss whether other equilibria exist.

5.1
Results for
N
odd

When
N
is odd, we can sharpen the main result from the last section.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose that an equilibrium has only one proposer. Then, this has to be the equilibrium found in Theorem
1
.

The intuition for this result is simple. Suppose one agent other than
m
is proposing at the equilibrium. Since no one else is proposing, he will propose his type. Then, the median is in the group of voters. If he proposes his type, he surely wins the election, as by being the median, he will gain at least half of the voters. This will make him better off. This proves that no equilibrium with only one proposer exists if the proposer is not the median agent.

In addition, we can show that there does not exist an equilibrium with exactly two proposers.

Proposition 2
.

Assume that
N
is odd. Then, there are no equilibria with
|\mathcal{CP}|=2
.

The logic here is more subtle. One has first to establish which proposals would be made in equilibrium. In the Appendix, we show that if the number of voters
|\mathcal{CV}|
is odd, which would happen if two agents opt for proposal-making, it is enough to consider the equilibria where for all
p\in\mathcal{CP}
we have
x_{p}\in[\min\{\theta_{p},\theta_{mv}\},\max\{\theta_{p},\theta_{mv}\}]
, where
\theta_{mv}
is the type of the median among the voters. In any of these possible equilibria, all proposers would propose their own peak. Again we can prove that there are always incentives to deviate from a constellation with two proposers. The detailed proof is provided in the Appendix.

Of course, it is of central interest whether there are other equilibria with three or more proposers. Under some further restrictions, we can establish global uniqueness.

Adding an Artificial Agent and Global Uniqueness

In this section we use a different procedure to select the alternative if many are made and show that we obtain global uniqueness for the case of an odd number of agents.

6.1
Artificial Agent and the New Procedure

As we have seen in the previous section, when the number of agents is even, the equilibrium is generally not unique. To ensure that the number of agents is always odd, we introduce a preliminary stage in which an artificial agent is added to the system. This artificial agent, referred to as the
AI voter
, participates in the voting process only when the number of real agents is even. The AI voter can be thought of as a machine that casts an additional vote according to the following procedure.

Stage 0:

If
N
is odd, proceed as before. If
N
is even, draw an AI voter with peak
\theta_{AI}
in
[-A,A]
. This AI will always vote. In total, with the AI there are
N+1
agents.

Then, the adjusted procedure is given as follows:

1.

Each agent decides whether he will propose a policy or be placed into the pool of voters. The set of proposers will be denoted by
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}
and the set of voters by
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{V}
.

2.

Each proposer
p\in\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}
decides on a proposal
x_{p}\in[-A,A]
he wants to put forward.

3.

Among all proposers in
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}
, two are selected uniformly at random and their proposal are submitted to a vote. Only the agents in the voter pool
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{V}
are allowed to participate in the voting process. The proposal that loses the vote, determined by majority rule, is discarded. Let
\mathcal{D}
denote the set of agents whose proposals have been discarded. The winning proposal
x_{w}
is then voted against another proposal, selected uniformly at random from the remaining proposals in
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}\setminus(\mathcal{D}\cup\{w\})
. This elimination procedure continues until only one proposal remains. The proposal made by agent
p\in\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{D}
that survives all rounds is implemented. Ties are resolved through fair randomization.

Hence, we have now a procedure to select the winning alternative. We can now leverage the result from
Gersbach & Valkanova (2025)
who have shown that such a procedure yields the Condorcet Winner among the proposals put forward by the group of proposal-makers.

8

Applying the procedure by
Gersbach & Valkanova (2025)
requires an adjustment to a finite number of agents and some technical assumptions. We assume that these conditions are fulfilled in this section.

6.2
Uniqueness for Model with AI voter

We show that in this model there is a unique equilibrium. Remember that in this model, the number of agents is always odd, as if it is even a new AI voter is added to make it odd.

Theorem 2
.

Let the number of agents
N
be odd. The scenario in which the median agent
m
opts for submitting the proposal
\theta_{m}
and all other agents opt to vote is the unique equilibrium.

The proof, that can be found in the Appendix, is made of three steps. First, we show that the Condorcet winner of the set
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}
is always implemented if it exists. Second, we show the existence of an equilibrium in our model. In the final step, we show that the equilibrium we found in the second step is unique.

Elections

The procedure can be applied in the same way for an election to an office. Agents decide whether to apply for proposal-making or voting. Now a proposal is a candidate for office. An agent opting for proposal-making may propose himself/herself for the office or some other agents. The remaining agents then vote. Our analysis and results can be readily transferred to such a set-up. The only difference is that the set of possible alternatives is now constrained to the set of agents
N
and is a discrete set of alternatives. Note that a candidate who is proposed by another agent can vote.
9
9

9

Again, one can add an AA to achieve an odd number of participants, but the AA is only active in voting. Yet, there are couple of subtle issues with an AA agent participating in elections, which we will spell out in future versions of the paper.

8
Discussion and Outlook

We demonstrate that the Propose-or-Vote mechanism efficiently implements the socially optimal alternative in a simple setting and that the Propose-or-Vote (PoV) procedure selects the Condorcet winner in a single voting round.
We have used a simple framework in which the distribution of peaks of the individual preferences is public knowledge and the peaks are public knowledge.

Numerous extensions should and will be taken up in future research. Those extensions range from asymmetric information to more complex arrangements of peaks of agents and utility functions.

Proof of Theorem

Proof.

We start from the case where
N
is odd. We show that the situation where only the median voter opts to propose his own type and all other agents opt to vote is an equilibrium, i.e. that no agent has the incentive to change his strategy in this situation, given that all other agents stick to their current choice. Let
i\neq m
be any agent of type
\theta_{i}\in[-A,A]
that is not the median agent and suppose this agent were to opt for submitting the proposal
x_{i}\in[-A,A]
instead of taking a vote. Then, since
\theta_{m}
is the median,
\theta_{m}
will always be implemented, no matter the choice of
x_{i}
. This shows that any agent other than the median agent
m
will stay with opting to vote in the first stage. Furthermore, the median agent
m
has no incentive to change from proposal-making to voting as the implemented policy would then be chosen uniformly at random from the interval
[-A,A]
, leaving the median agent with a worse expected utility of

\mathbb{E}(u_{\theta_{m},v})=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\theta_{m}-\theta_{i})^{2}<0=u_{\theta_{m},\theta_{m}}.

Moreover, the median voter maximises his utility by proposing his own type if he is the only proposal-maker, so the median voter also has no incentive to change his strategy in the second stage. In the third stage there is only one choice for the voters, so they cannot change their strategy in this last round. Hence, we found an equilibrium.

Consider now the case where
N
is even. We show that the situation where both the left median agent
m_{l}
and the right median agent
m_{r}
propose their own type and all the other agents vote is an equilibrium. Notice that
m_{l}\neq m_{r}
since the agents’ types are pairwise different and we face a situation with an even number of agents.

In the third stage voters will always vote for the policy that is closest to their type to maximse their utility. Hence, proposing their own type in the second stage is the best strategy for the left median as his utility of proposing his own type is

\mathbb{E}(u_{\theta_{m_{l}},\theta_{m_{l}}})=-\frac{1}{2}(\theta_{m_{l}}-\theta_{m_{r}})^{2}

while his utility from proposing
x_{m_{l}}
would be

\mathbb{E}(u_{\theta_{m_{l}},x_{m_{l}}})=-\frac{1}{2}\cdot(\theta_{m_{l}}-\theta_{m_{r}})^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\cdot(\theta_{m_{l}}-x_{m_{l}})^{2}

if he changed his proposal only slightly enough that his proposal
x_{m_{l}}
is still preferred by half of the voters. Else, his expected utility would become

\mathbb{E}(u_{\theta_{m_{l}},x_{m_{l}}})=-(\theta_{m_{l}}-\theta_{m_{r}})^{2}

since the right median’s proposal would be implemented every time. In both cases, his expected utility decreased compared to proposing
\theta_{m_{l}}
. Hence, he has no incentive to change his strategy from the second stage on. For the left median’s choice in the first stage, notice that his utility, if he switched to voting, would again be

\mathbb{E}(u_{\theta_{m_{l}},v})=-(\theta_{m_{l}}-\theta_{m_{r}})^{2}

as
\theta_{m_{r}}
would always be implemented by being the only proposal made. Hence, the left median has no incentive to change his strategy. A similar argument applies to show that the right median has no incentive to change his strategy. Finally, the voters don’t have an incentive to switch to proposal-making.
To show this, we first prove that no agent has an incentive to propose an alternative different from their own type.

Consider an agent located to the left of the left median. Clearly, there is no incentive for such an agent to propose a point to the left of their type, as this would only reduce the proposal’s appeal and lead to a lower expected utility.

If the agent retains their voting power, their expected utility is

\mathbb{E}(u_{\theta_{i},v})=-\frac{1}{2}\left((\theta_{i}-\theta_{m_{l}})^{2}+(\theta_{i}-\theta_{m_{r}})^{2}\right).

If the agent instead becomes a proposer and loses their vote, proposing, their expected utility becomes

\mathbb{E}(u_{\theta_{i},x_{i}})=-\frac{1}{3}(\theta_{i}-\theta_{m_{l}})^{2}-\frac{2}{3}(\theta_{i}-\theta_{m_{r}})^{2}.

We now compare the two:

\mathbb{E}(u_{\theta_{i},v})>\mathbb{E}(u_{\theta_{i},x_{i}})\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\frac{1}{6}(\theta_{i}-\theta_{m_{r}})^{2}>\frac{1}{6}(\theta_{i}-\theta_{m_{l}})^{2}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad|\theta_{i}-\theta_{m_{r}}|>|\theta_{i}-\theta_{m_{l}}|.

This inequality holds by assumption, as we are considering an agent who is closer to the left median than to the right median. Proving there is no incentive to propose for agents that are currently voting. A symmetric argument applies to agents located to the right of the median.

Altogether, we conclude that no agent has an incentive to deviate from their strategy. Hence, we have identified an equilibrium.

Proof of Theorem 2

We start by showing that the Condorcet winner is always implemented, if it exists.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}
be the set of proposers,
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{V}
the set of voters. Assume that there exists a Condorcet winner in
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}
w.r.t.
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{V}
, i.e. a proposer
p\in\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}
whose proposal would win any one-on-one vote against any other proposal in
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}
. Then
p
is implemented.

Proof.

Since voters vote truthfully in each round of the procedure, and since the proposal of
p
wins any one-on-one voting round against any other proposal made by agents in
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}
, it will eventually be selected as one of the contenders in some round. From that point on, it will continue to win every subsequent voting round. Therefore,
p
will be the last remaining proposal and will be implemented at the end of the procedure.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

2
.

We will first show that the presented equilibrium candidate is indeed an equilibrium, and then prove that it is the unique equilibrium.

(i)

Existence:

Since there is only one proposal made, any deviation from the current strategy by an agent leads to a scenario where at most one voting round is needed. Moreover, if some of the voter deviates by making a proposal, there is an odd number of voters, and the AI abstains. Thus, we are in the exact same situation as in the proof of Theorem
1
and, therefore, the same arguments apply.

(ii)

Uniqueness:

Let there be an equilibrium consisting of voters
\mathcal{V}^{*}
and proposers with proposals
\mathcal{P}^{*}
. Since AI votes iff there is a draw and abstains otherwise, there always exists a Condorcet winner, since the number of voting agents
\mathcal{V}^{*}
including potentially AI is odd. Thus, by
Proposition

1
, the proposal
p_{w}\coloneqq\argmin_{p\in\mathcal{P}^{*}}|x_{p}-\theta_{mv}|
closest to the median voter
\theta_{mv}
wins the vote and is implemented. Therefore, there is exactly one proposal made, that is,
|\mathcal{P}^{*}|=1
.

This can be easily proven by contradiction. If there is more than one proposal, the agents who made a proposal which is not implemented will obtain the same utility by switching to voting or even improve its utility through the shift of the median voter’s type
\theta_{mv}
towards its own type. Thus, by the tie-breaking rule, they will decide to vote. If there is no proposal, any agent has the incentive to change its strategy to propose its own peak as it will be implemented being the only proposal.

Next, since there is only one proposal, it is always optimal for the proposal-making agent to propose its own peak. Assume by contradiction that the median agent
\theta_{m}
is not making a proposal, but instead another agent of type
\theta_{p}\neq\theta_{m}
. We can assume w.l.o.g. that
\theta_{p}<\theta_{m}
, the case
\theta_{p}>\theta_{m}
is symmetric. The agent with type
\theta_{m+1}
right of the median agent (not the median voter) can improve its utility by proposing the median agent type
\theta_{m}
. If
\theta_{m+1}
does so, the median voter coincides with the median agent since there is a proposal-making agent to the left and right of it. Thus,
x_{m+1}=\theta_{m}
wins the vote and is implemented. Since

\mathbb{E}(u_{\theta_{m+1},\theta_{m}})=-(\theta_{m}-\theta_{m+1})^{2}>-(\theta_{p}-\theta_{m+1})^{2}=\mathbb{E}(u_{\theta_{m+1},v}),

this can not be an equilibrium. Hence, the equilibrium found above is the unique equilibrium.

∎
