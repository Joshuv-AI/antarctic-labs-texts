Population Genomics Insights into Pharmacogenomic Differentiation Between East Asians and Europeans
Authors: Sihan Chen, Hongpu Chen, Shuhua Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenetic variation contributes substantially to interindividual and interpopulation differences in drug response, yet most pharmacogenomic studies remain biased toward European populations. Here, we systematically assessed pharmacogenomic variation across East Asians (EAS) and Europeans (EUR) using public genomic datasets and investigated the potential evolutionary forces shaping these differences. Our analyses revealed that EAS populations generally harbor a lower frequency of pharmacogenomic variants previously associated with reduced drug response compared with EUR populations, indicating population‐level differences in the distribution of PGx variants. Drug‐associated genes exhibited threefold higher population differentiation than the genome‐wide background (F ST = 0.18 vs. 0.06). Key transport genes such as SLCO1B3/SLCO1B7 showed striking differences in cumulative allele probability (EAS: 18.69% vs. EUR: 91.60%), suggesting population‐level divergence in drug transport and pharmacokinetics. Population genetic analyses revealed stronger signals of positive selection on PGx variants in EAS populations, likely reflecting historical adaptations to pathogen and dietary pressures. For instance, the VKORC1‐rs9923231‐T allele, which enhances warfarin sensitivity, shows evidence of selection in EAS populations (XP‐EHH = 3.58) and may relate to vitamin K intake. Conversely, the EUR‐enriched SH2B3‐rs3184504‐T allele (AFEUR: 0.46 vs. AFEAS: 0.003) has been associated with selection related to blood pressure regulation. Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of incorporating population diversity into pharmacogenomic research and underscore the potential need for population‐specific drug response guidelines to advance global precision medicine.

Incorporating evolutionary insights and ancestry‐aware genetic diversity into clinical guidelines may improve the accuracy, safety, and equity of precision medicine.

Pharmacogenomics (PGx) is a field that explores how genetic diversity influences interindividual variability in drug response. 1 Variants in the pharmacogenes encoding proteins involved in a drug's pharmacokinetics can affect drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion, 2 as well as how efficiently a drug reaches its target site and is metabolized in the body. Pharmacogenes involved in pharmacodynamics encode drug targets or related signaling proteins. 2 Genetic variation in these genes can alter protein structure or function, thereby modifying drug–target interactions. Collectively, variation in pharmacogenes can influence both therapeutic efficacy and the risk of adverse drug reactions. Thus, the goal of PGx research is to facilitate the discovery and development of novel drugs as well as to optimize drug selection and dosing to maximize efficacy while minimizing toxicity. 3

It is well established that genetic ancestry significantly influences PGx across populations, as demonstrated in numerous studies worldwide. 4 , 5 Interpopulation genetic differentiation poses challenges for extrapolating PGx findings across diverse populations or even subpopulations within a given geographical region. 4 , 5 , 6 Although some patients respond well to specific drugs, a substantial proportion—ranging from 10% to 45% in certain therapeutic areas such as depression—experience insufficient efficacy. 7 Moreover, genetic variations can contribute to severe adverse drug reactions (ADRs), accounting for ~7% of hospitalizations and 0.3% of patient deaths. 8 The risk associated with certain drugs has been reported to differ markedly between East Asian (EAS) and European (EUR) populations. 6 One of the most well‐documented examples of ancestry‐specific pharmacogenomic risk is HLA‐B*15:02. In Asian populations, carriers of this allele treated with carbamazepine have been shown to face a dramatically increased risk—up to 172‐fold—of developing Stevens‐Johnson syndrome or toxic epidermal necrolysis compared to noncarriers. 6

However, genomic research remains largely Eurocentric, with limited representation of diverse populations, which may exacerbate existing health disparities. 9 Polygenic risk scores, which are predominantly derived from EUR cohorts, often show reduced transferability across ancestries. This highlights broader challenges in translating genomic findings into disease risk prediction and pharmacogenomic applications. 10 A lack of consideration of population diversity is also evident in drug labels. 11 The US Food and Drug Administration and the Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium have provided some of the most rigorous and evidence‐based recommendations for genetically informed dosing and contraindications. 12 However, most guidelines do not include ethnicity‐specific recommendations, largely due to limited diversity in PGx studies.

In this study, we systematically characterize pharmacogenomic differences between EAS and EUR populations. By integrating the genomic data from the 1,000 Genomes Project with the Pharmacogenomics Knowledge Base (PharmGKB), we identified substantial interpopulation differences in PGx variants and investigated potential factors underlying these differences. This analysis provides insights into population‐specific risks of severe ADRs and underscores key pharmacogenomic differences with implications for clinical prescribing in EAS populations.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Public genomic data

The full sequence data of 1,007 unrelated European samples were retrieved from the 1,000 Genomes Project Phase III (https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1000genomes/ftp/release/20130502/). 13 For the East Asian (EAS) populations, the combined EAS populations include CDX (Chinese Dai in Xishuangbanna, China; n = 93), CHB (Han Chinese in Beijing, China; n = 103), CHS (Southern Han Chinese, China; n = 105), JPT (Japanese in Tokyo, Japan; n = 104), and KHV (Kinh in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam; n = 99). The combined EUR populations CEU (Utah Residents with Northern and Western European Ancestry; n = 99), GBR (British in England and Scotland; n = 91), FIN (Finnish in Finland; n = 99), TSI (Toscani in Italia; n = 107), and IBS (Iberian Population in Spain; n = 107) are included as EUR populations. The African YRI (Yoruba in Ibadan, Nigeria; n = 108) population was employed as an outgroup. Multi‐allelic sites were filtered out from the data, and 77,818,183 biallelic sites were retained for further analysis (Figure S1 ).

We also obtained the Human Genome Diversity Project (HGDP) data (ftp://ngs.sanger.ac.uk/production/hgdp/hgdp_wgs.20190516/) and used it as a validation set, with a mean depth of 30×. 14 The HGDP data that were analyzed encompassed 241 EAS individuals from China, Japan, Cambodia, and Siberia, and 161 EUR individuals from France, Russia, Italy, and Orkney islands. In total, 63,540,915 biallelic variants were utilized in the subsequent analyses (Figure S1 ).

To explore the alteration of the putative adaptive allele frequency, we downloaded 8,872 worldwide Neolithic‐to‐Paleolithic ancient human genomes from the Allen Ancient DNA Resource (https://dataverse.harvard.edu/dataset.xhtml?persistentId=doi:10.7910/DVN/FFIDCW&version=7.0, last accessed November 21, 2022, version 54.1). 15 These samples were grouped into different clusters based on time and geography, with each group containing more than 40 samples.

Public and published RNA data

RNA samples for 39 HAN (Han Chinese) and 94 GBR (British) were collected to represent EAS and EUR populations, respectively. The GBR population samples were obtained from the GEUVADIS project, 16 whereas the HAN population samples were generated in the previous study. 17 Gene and transcript expression were quantified and normalized to FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) using RSEM 18 following the previous study. 17

Public PGx resources and variant annotation

We downloaded pharmacogenomics associations between variants and drugs from PharmGKB (https://www.pharmgkb.org/). 19 Associations annotated as “significance = yes,” indicating statistically significant findings in the original publications, were retained. In total, 1,656 PGx single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were collected. Among these, only 1,575 were present in the 1,000 Genomes dataset and included in downstream analyses. The overview of the level of evidence for PGx SNPs is shown in Figure S2 . Based on clinical annotations from the PharmGKB, PGx SNPs were classified according to their functional impact on drugs. As an single variant may affect multiple drugs with different clinical outcomes, SNPs could be assigned to more than one category. Specifically, the PGx SNPs were grouped into four categories: (1) drug response (n = 881); (2) drug metabolism (n = 254); (3) drug dosage or required dose adjustments (n = 163); (4) adverse drug reactions or treatment discontinuation (n = 7). Functional consequences of the 1,575 PGx SNPs were further annotated using the variant effect predictor. 20 Based on Ensembl 96 genome annotation (GRCh37), these variants were mapped to 779 genes, which were considered drug‐associated genes. Detailed variant associations and annotations are provided in Table S1 . Among these drug‐associated genes, we further identified a curated subset of 49 very important pharmacogenes (VIPs) as annotated by PharmGKB (https://www.pharmgkb.org/). 19 The full list can be found in Table S2 .

Analysis of genetic differentiation

To assess genetic differentiation in PGx, we constructed one control gene set matched to the drug‐associated genes/VIPs and 100 control SNP sets sampled from the genome for comparison with PGx SNPs. Specifically, (1) random genes were selected that matched the size and biological type (e.g., protein‐coding and pseudogene) of the drug‐associated genes and (2) variant sampling was conducted from these genes according to the density distribution of PGx SNPs within each drug‐associated gene. To avoid potential bias from gene function, we additionally collected 3,118 immune‐associated genes from ImmuPort (https://www.immport.org/shared/genelists). From this set, a matched control gene set was constructed with the same size and gene type distribution as the drug‐associated genes.

For each SNP, the F ST 21 between EAS and EUR populations was calculated using VCFtools. 22 Mean F ST and weighted F ST for PGx SNPs were then compared with those of the control sets using two‐tailed Z tests. Next, we calculated the estimators of nucleotide diversity (θπ) using a sliding window of 50 kb and a stepping interval of 25 kb with VCFtools. 22 Differences in nucleotide diversity between drug‐associated genes/VIPs and control genes were assessed using a two‐tailed Wilcoxon rank‐sum test.

Detection of natural selection

To detect the signal of positive selection, we applied two methods based on different principles: extended haplotype homozygosity and allele frequency differentiation.

We estimated the integrated haplotype score (iHS) 27 for combined EAS or EUR populations and implemented the Cross Population Extended Haplotype Homozygosity (XP‐EHH) 28 test on the Han Chinese in Beijing (CHB) and Utah Residents with Northern and Western European Ancestry (CEU). We used Software Selscan version 1.2.0 29 for both tests, with all parameters set to their default values. SNPs with |iHS| > 2 or |XP‐EHH| > 2 were considered candidate adaptive variants based on conventional empirical cutoffs commonly used in selection scan studies.

Furthermore, we calculated Tajima's D statistics 30 with a sliding window of 50 kb and stepping by 25 kb using VCFtools 22 for combined EAS or EUR populations.

Estimation of selection time

We estimated the selection‐onset time of adaptive alleles using startmrca. 31 For each locus, we investigated a 1 Mb surrounding region and adopted a mutation rate of 1.6 × 10−8 following a previous study. 32 The maximum number of haplotypes in the selected and reference panels was set to be 100 and 20, respectively. We then ran this with 15,000 iterations and retained the last 6,000 iterations as the final result.

RESULTS

Large genetic differentiation of PGx between EAS and EUR populations

To assess genetic differentiation in PGx, we analyzed single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within whole‐genome sequencing data of 1,007 EUR individuals sourced from the 1,000 Genomes Project Phase III (Materials and Methods). In total, 3,158,634 SNPs across 779 drug‐associated genes curated from PharmGKB were extracted from this dataset for analysis (Materials and Methods). The allele frequency distribution of variants in drug‐associated genes was comparable to that of genome‐wide variants in both populations (Figure 1 a ). However, PGx SNPs exhibited lower minor allele frequencies in EAS than in EUR populations (P = 1.47 × 10−3, two‐tailed Wilcoxon rank‐sum test) (Figure 1 a ). To further assess population‐level genetic diversity, we next examined nucleotide diversity. Drug‐associated genes exhibited greater diversity in the EUR than in the EAS populations (6.14 × 10−4 vs. 5.79 × 10−4, P = 4.28 × 10−5, two‐tailed Wilcoxon rank‐sum test) (Figure 1 b ). The result was consistent when immune‐associated genes were used as a control (6.65 × 10−4 vs. 6.17 × 10−4, P = 2.14 × 10−5, two‐tailed Wilcoxon rank‐sum test) (Figure S3 ). When restricting the analysis to the 49 VIPs, no statistically significant differences were observed. However, VIPs showed a similar trend of higher nucleotide diversity in EUR than in EAS (5.10 × 10−4 vs. 4.53 × 10−4, P = 0.24, two‐tailed Wilcoxon rank‐sum test), whereas no consistent difference was observed in the matched control genes (3.59 × 10−4 vs. 3.75 × 10−4, P = 0.66, two‐tailed Wilcoxon rank‐sum test) (Figure S4 ). Consistent with this observation, PGx SNPs exhibited greater genetic differentiation between EAS and EUR populations, as indicated by a higher weighted F ST compared with control variants (0.109 vs. 0.100 ± 0.010, P < 2.2 × 10−16, two‐tailed Z test, Materials and Methods) (Figure 1 c ). A similar trend was observed in the mean F ST analysis (0.092 vs. 0.022 ± 0.002, P < 2.2 × 10−16, two‐tailed Z test) (Figure 1 d ). These findings were replicated in the HGDP dataset, which includes high‐coverage whole‐genome sequences (mean depth ~30×) (Figure S5 ). The F ST patterns in the HGDP data were consistent with those from the 1,000 Genomes Project, reinforcing the finding that PGx variants exhibit greater population differentiation than non‐PGx variants.

To further quantify the impact of functional variants, we employed the CAP score to measure the likelihood of an individual in the population carrying functional variants in a specific gene, as described by Schärfe et al. 25 (Materials and Methods). The largest differences in CAP scores between EAS and EUR individuals were observed for SLCO1B3, SLCO1B3–SLCO1B7, and SLCO1B7 (18.69% and 91.60% for EAS and EUR individuals, respectively) (Table S4 ). These genes all belong to the organic anion transporting polypeptides family, which is involved in transmembrane transport. Although SLCO1B7 is a pseudogene, SLCO1B3‐SLCO1B7 and SLCO1B3 have been reported to play crucial roles in pharmacokinetics, affecting drug absorption, distribution, and metabolism. 33 , 34 In particular, these genes mediate the hepatic uptake of drugs such as ezetimibe and certain statins (e.g., atorvastatin, rosuvastatin), thereby influencing systemic exposure and metabolic rates. Altered function or inhibition of these transporters can therefore impact drug efficacy and the risk of adverse effects. We further calculated the weighted CAP (wCAP) score incorporating evolutionary conservation and gene size (Materials and Methods), which provides a more stringent measure of the likelihood of carrying functional variants per gene. In contrast to CAP, wCAP revealed only minor differences between EAS and EUR populations for these genes (Table S5 ).

Highly different drug effects between EAS and EUR populations

To assess the impact of functional variants on a particular drug, we calculated the DRP score, which quantifies the contribution of functional variants to drug response, following the methodology described by Schärfe et al. 25 (Materials and Methods). Among 519 drug‐associated genes, 96.34% in the EAS populations and 92.68% in the EUR populations had DRP scores >1% (Table S6 ). Moreover, 159 drugs exhibited an absolute DRP difference exceeding 10% between EAS and EUR populations (Table S6 ). Notably, ulinastatin exhibited the largest DRP difference (8.78% in EAS vs. 88.75% in EUR), although clinical implications remain uncertain and require further investigation (Table S6 ). When applying a weighted DRP (wDRP) to avoid potential confounding factors (Materials and Methods), the difference of ulinastatin became minimal (0.08% in EAS vs. 1.81% in EUR) (Table S7 ), whereas 5‐fluorouracil emerged as one of the drugs with the largest differences (76.30% in EAS vs. 49.55% in EUR) (Table 1 ). The 5‐fluorouracil is an antimetabolite drug widely used in cancer treatment. 35 These results highlight the presence of population‐specific genetic variability in drug response.

Subsequently, we categorized all the PGx SNPs into four categories based on their impacts on drugs: drug response (n = 881), drug metabolism (n = 254), drug dose (n = 163), and drug discontinuation (n = 7) (Materials and Methods). While the frequency spectra of the latter three categories did not show significant differences between EAS and EUR populations (Figure S6 ), SNPs associated with decreased drug response were slightly more frequent in EUR populations than in EAS populations, reflecting potential population differences in drug effects (mean AFEAS vs. mean AFEUR = 0.41 vs. 0.43, P = 0.041, one‐tailed Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, applied under the hypothesis of higher frequency in EUR) (Figure 2 a ). An expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL), CYP1A1‐rs2606345‐A, has been reported to reduce the response to antiepileptics in women with epilepsy. 36 Here, we observed that the rs2606345‐A allele was associated with lower CYP1A1 expression levels in EUR populations (Figure S7A ) and was substantially more prevalent in EUR populations (AF = 0.66) than in EAS populations (AF = 0.05) (Figure S7B ). Given the reported role of CYP1A1 in drug metabolism, 37 this population difference in allele frequency may contribute to interpopulation variability in antiepileptic drug response.

To quantify drug‐level effects, we developed the DES, which integrates both the number of PGx SNPs influencing drug response and their allele frequencies for each drug (Materials and Methods). The DES was calculated for 285 drugs with PGx annotations specifying the direction of SNP effect on drug response. Under the dominant model, 17 of 29 drugs with the largest interpopulation differences showed higher DES in EAS than in EUR populations, whereas under the recessive model, this number was 13 (Materials and Methods) (Figure 2 b ; Table S8 ). When incorporating the level of evidence, the weighted DES (wDES) showed a different pattern: 11 of 29 drugs under the dominant model and 15 of 29 drugs under the recessive model showed higher wDES in EAS populations (Materials and Methods) (Table S9 ). Notably, EUR individuals carried more heterozygous PGx SNPs that reduce drug response under the dominant model, whereas EAS individuals carried more homozygotes under the recessive model (Figure 2 c ). These observations suggest that, compared with EUR populations, EAS populations may carry more homozygous PGx SNPs but fewer heterozygous PGx SNPs (Figure S8 ). Among all drugs, immunoglobulins exhibited the most pronounced DES differences between the EAS and EUR populations under both dominant and recessive models (Figure 2 b ; Table S8 ). This finding was supported by wDES calculation under both dominant and recessive models (Table S9 ). The allele CASP3‐rs113420705‐G, associated with enhancing the response to immunoglobulins, 38 was more prevalent in the EAS populations (Figure S9A ). This allele also displayed signals of positive selection in the EAS populations (iHS = −2.62 to −2.43), with an estimated onset of selection ~81,378 years ago (95% CI: 77,002–85,897) (Figure S9B–F , Materials and Methods). Another variant, ITPKC‐rs28493229, showed limited genetic differentiation between EAS and EUR populations (F ST = 0.02), but the allele G, which may increase the response of immunoglobulins, 38 had a higher frequency in EAS populations (AF = 0.93) than in EUR populations (AF = 0.88). Taken together, these results indicate a potential trend toward a stronger immunoglobulin response in EAS populations. This observation may partly reflect the fact that the two variants were originally identified in EAS cohorts, 38 and the markedly higher prevalence of Kawasaki disease in EAS populations compared with its rarity in EUR populations. 39 When restricting analysis to high‐confidence PGx SNPs (level of evidence 1 or 2), DES calculations included 56 drugs (Materials and Methods) (Tables 2 and S10 ). Among the top 10% of drugs showing the greatest interpopulation differences, most displayed higher predicted DES scores in EUR populations under both dominant and recessive models (Table 2 ). These findings underscore the crucial role of genetic ancestry in PGx and highlight the potential of predicting drug responses based on population‐specific genetic architecture.

Local adaptation leading to PGx differentiation between EAS and EUR

Using the iHS, we identified 104 (6.60%) and 120 (7.62%) PGx SNPs exhibiting signals of positive selection in the EAS and EUR populations, respectively. Among these, only 62 (59.62%) in the EAS population and 66 (55.00%) in the EUR population were reported to potentially enhance drug effects (Materials and Methods) (Figure 3a , Table 3 , Table S11 ). For instance, the allele rs3077‐A at HLA‐DPA1, which is associated with reduced risk of chronic HBV infection and increased response to peginterferon alfa‐2b in individuals with chronic HBV. 40 This allele showed strong signals of natural selection in the EUR populations (Figure S10A ), consistent with large allele frequency differences between EUR populations and EAS populations (AFEUR = 0.81 vs. AFEAS = 0.31) (Figure S10B ). Moreover, this allele was associated with higher HLA‐DPA1 expression in GBR individuals (Materials and Methods) (Figure S10C ), which plays a key role in HBV clearance by presenting antigens to CD4+ T helper cells. 41 The allele frequency trajectories in the ancient genomes suggested that the onset of positive selection occurred ~30,000 years ago (Materials and Methods) (Figure S10D ). However, given the limited sample size and temporal resolution of currently available ancient genomic datasets, the precise timing of selection should be interpreted with caution. In addition, we also detected that alleles under strong selective pressure possibly decrease drug response. A notable example was a missense variant SH2B3‐rs3184504 (c.784T>C; p.W262R), associated with the response to candesartan in hypertensive individuals. 42 SH2B3 is a negative regulator in inflammatory cytokine and hematopoietic growth factor signaling. 43 Animal experiments revealed that rs3184504‐C could lead to blood pressure elevations and renal damage by enhancing IFN‐γ production by T cells. 44 In this study, the T allele, which potentially reduces drug effect, 42 was prevalent in the EUR populations (AF = 0.46) and extremely rare in the EAS populations (AF = 2.98 × 10−3) (Figure S11 ). This locus showed strong selection signals in EUR populations, with selection onset estimated within the last 5,000 years ( Figures S12 and S13 ), consistent with a previous study. 32 Together with the previously reported adaptation to higher alcohol consumption, 32 this pattern may also reflect adaptation to lower risk of hypertension in EUR populations. These results indicate that local adaptation to ancestral environments or disease pressures—potentially affecting drug response—has likely shaped PGx genetic differentiation between EAS and EUR populations.

To further assess selection on PGx variants, we calculated Tajima's D and the cross‐population extended haplotype homozygosity (XP‐EHH) to examine the extent of positive selection acting on PGx variants in the EAS and EUR populations. The results of Tajima’ D statistic demonstrated that drug‐associated genes were under stronger selection in the EAS compared to the EUR populations (meanEAS − meanEUR = −0.14; P = 3.89 × 10−13, two‐tailed Students' t‐test), compared to the control group (meanEAS − meanEUR = −0.061; P = 1.37 × 10−3, two‐tailed Students' t‐test) (Figure 3 b ). This pattern was consistent when using immune genes as the control group and when conducting analysis in the HGDP data ( Figures S14 and S15 ). When the analysis was restricted to the 49 VIPs, a similar pattern was observed (meanEAS − meanEUR = −0.188; P = 0.07, two‐tailed Students' t‐test) (Figure S16 ). Consistent with these results, PGx SNPs exhibited a greater excess of XP‐EHH in EAS than in EUR populations compared with the control group (P = 0.025, two‐tailed Students' t‐test) (Figure 3 c ). Notable PGx SNPs showing stronger selection in EAS included TNFRSF10A‐rs20575 and a missense variant PRSS53‐rs11150606 (c.89A>G; p.Q30R). The TNFRSF10A‐rs20575‐G allele, which potentially increases the response to adalimumab, etanercept, and infliximab in individuals with psoriatic arthritis, 45 showed positive selection in CHB (XP‐EHHCHB‐CEU = 2.09) (Figure S17 ). The PRSS53 has been implicated in cancer, diabetes, and psoriasis. 46 , 47 The rs11150606‐C, associated with a decreased dose of warfarin, 48 showed strong evidence of positive selection in the EAS populations (XP‐EHHCHB‐CEU = 3.06). This possibly led to a high allele frequency in the EAS populations (AF = 0.76) and a relatively low allele frequency in the EUR populations (AF = 0.03) (Figure S18 ).

DISCUSSION

Our study revealed that genetic differentiation in pharmacogenes is substantially greater than genome‐wide background differentiation between EAS and EUR populations. These disparities contribute to predicted population‐level differences in pharmacogenomic burden. For example, over 90% of drugs showed nonzero DRP in both populations, and more than 150 drugs displayed large interpopulation DRP differences. Using DES, we further observed marked population divergence for specific drugs. For example, clinical studies have shown that individuals carrying the VKORC1‐rs9923231‐A/A genotype require lower therapeutic doses of acenocoumarol. 49 In this study, the rs9923231‐A allele displays marked frequency differences between populations (AFEUR = 0.39 vs. AFEAS = 0.88), consistent with the DES difference of acenocoumarol (DESEAS = 78.31% vs. DESEUR = 15.03%). While these findings suggest potential population differences in drug response, it is important to emphasize that DRP and DES are computational estimates. They should therefore be interpreted cautiously and require clinical validation before being linked directly to real‐world efficacy or safety outcomes.

We further observed that alleles associated with reduced drug response were generally more frequent in EUR populations, whereas certain efficacy‐enhancing alleles were enriched in EAS populations. For instance, the VKORC1‐rs9923231‐G is significantly associated with insufficient anticoagulation in patients using warfarin 50 and exhibits marked allele frequency differences between populations (AFEUR = 0.61 vs. AFEAS = 0.12; F ST = 0.42). This is consistent with clinical observations that individuals of European ancestry require higher warfarin maintenance doses than Asian individuals. 51 This illustrates how population genetic structure can contribute to pharmacogenomic variability. However, for many other loci, clinical corroboration remains limited, and our composite scores should be viewed as probabilistic tools rather than predictors of individual drug response.

We also detected signatures of natural selection at multiple pharmacogenomic loci, with comparatively stronger signals in EAS populations than in EUR populations. Importantly, these signatures likely reflect historical adaptation to environmental pressures such as diet, pathogens, or metabolism, rather than direct selection on drug response. Accordingly, their impact on pharmacogenomic variation is likely incidental. For example, VKORC1 may have been shaped by historical differences in vitamin K intake, 52 whereas SH2B3 alleles under selection in Europeans may be linked to immune or hypertension‐related traits. Together, these findings illustrate how evolutionary history can indirectly influence modern pharmacogenomic diversity. We further note that the selection signals were identified using commonly applied empirical thresholds (|iHS| > 2 and |XP‐EHH| > 2), which provide a practical criterion for comparative analyses but do not represent population‐specific genome‐wide significance levels. This may introduce some dependence on population context, as discussed in recent work. 53

Our study has several limitations. First, the PharmGKB database is enriched for variants identified in European cohorts, which may introduce bias in annotation and variant classification in non‐European populations. Second, only a limited subset of high‐confidence, directionally annotated variants could be included, which reduces coverage for some clinically important genes (e.g., CYP2D6). Finally, the absence of large‐scale clinical outcome data prevents us from directly testing whether population‐level predictions correspond to observed differences in drug efficacy or safety.

Despite these caveats, our findings underscore the importance of considering genetic diversity in pharmacogenomic research. They highlight the need for broader inclusion of underrepresented populations in pharmacogenomic studies, the development of population‐tailored guidelines, and integration of evolutionary insights into understanding present‐day variability in drug response. Expanding pharmacogenomic evidence across global populations will be essential for advancing equitable precision medicine.
