Antibiotic treatment of bacterial lung infections in cystic fibrosis
Authors: Giovanni Taccetti, Vito Terlizzi, Silvia Campana, Daniela Dolce, Novella Ravenni, Cristina Fevola, Michela Francalanci, Valeria Galici, Anna Silvia Neri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacterial infections of the lower airways are the main cause of mortality and morbidity in cystic fibrosis. The most frequently isolated pathogens are S. aureus and P. aeruginosa; bacterial co-infections are frequently observed. The aim of this review is to provide, in the current context, the indications regarding the best antibiotic strategy to adopt in subjects affected by CF infected with the most common pathogens. We selected relevant publications (guidelines, systematic reviews and clinical studies published so far on these topics) and we analysed the sampling methods used and antibiotic strategies adopted. Oropharyngeal sampling methods are considered less sensitive for pathogen detection than sputum. In non-expectorating people, induced sputum is considered equivalent to two-lobe bronchoalveolar lavage, which is considered invasive. Antibiotic treatment against the main pathogens can consist in eradication treatment in the early stages of infection, chronic suppressive therapy and treatment of the pulmonary exacerbations. This scheme is valid for P. aeruginosa but remains to be demonstrated for the other pathogens. For S. aureus, no evidence-based therapeutic strategies on how to treat the different stages of bacterial infection have been established with certainty. With regard to the treatment of the other classic pathogens (B. cepacia complex, A. xylosoxidans and S. maltophilia), no evidence-based indications exist and decision is left to the clinician. The recent introduction of highly effective modulators on the CFTR protein, in addition to the favourable effects described in regulatory trials, has led to a reduction in bacterial isolations; the real effect of which in clinical practice has still to be assessed on the basis of scientific data.ConclusionsThe reliability of culture examination depends on sampling methods, and expectorated sputum continues to be the best method as it is simple and non-invasive. P. aeruginosa is the pathogen for which antibiotic strategies for the various stages of infection appear best established, and the efficacy of early eradication treatment and chronic suppressive therapy have been underlined in clinical trials and systematic reviews. The recent introduction of modulators into clinical practice, despite their widely described efficacy, has not yet led to suggestions for changes in antibiotic strategies against the pathogens most frequently isolated.

Bacterial lung infections caused by a limited number of pathogens are the main cause of mortality in cystic fibrosis (CF) [1–7]. The prevalence of different bacterial species varies depending on the age of people with cystic fibrosis (pwCF), and is usually due to Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) in the first years of life and later to Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) [4–7]. From analysis of Registry data, about one-third of pwCF are co-infected with S. aureus and P. aeruginosa, most frequently in adulthood [4–10]. Other pathogens, mainly non-fermenting Gram negatives, are isolated more rarely [4–7].

Airways infections in pwCF represent the prototype of chronic infection as the bacterial cells are organised into a biofilm that protects them from antibiotic activity [11]. In chronic infections, antibiotic susceptibility testing is less useful than in acute infections and the choice of antibiotic treatment is based on both susceptibility testing and clinical response to treatment [12].

Following clinical trial results, antibiotic strategies, which together with physiotherapy represent a cornerstone of treatment, have progressively improved. Today, the best therapeutic approach is based on the correct identification of the pathogens, knowledge of the stage of infection (initial or chronic infection) and assessment of clinical conditions [13–15].

Recently, the introduction into clinical practice of CFTR protein modulators (CFTRm) has changed the therapeutic landscape of genetically eligible pwCF. This type of therapy has led to a reduction in respiratory exacerbations, and a significant improvement in lung function, nutritional status and quality of life [16–22]. Moreover, the efficacy of modulators is demonstrated by decreasing sweat chloride values [16–22]. While leading to a marked improvement in general conditions, and in many cases a reduction in sputum volume [23], the effect of CFTRm on airways microbiology has yet to be defined with certainty. Registry studies have shown that the use of Ivacaftor resulted in substantial changes leading to a reduction in bacterial isolates [24]. Many pwCF after a short period of Elexacaftor-Tezacaftor-Ivacaftor (ETI) treatment remained culture positive for the pathogens cultured previously, and in those who became culture negative, the pathogens were often still detectable by PCR over a long period [23].

Data from the German CF Registry showed a significant reduction in detection frequencies for S. aureus and P. aeruginosa in both throat swabs and sputum cultures, confirming the changing epidemiology of airways microbiology after the introduction of ETI [25]. These observations and the fact that new therapies are not currently available for all patients suggest that microbiological explorations, which have always been considered an integral part of the therapeutic approach, have not diminished in importance.

The reliability of the culture result is based on correct sampling methods [26, 27]. As pwCF, especially in paediatric age, dislike invasive methods, spontaneously expectorated sputum is still considered the best method as it is the simplest and least invasive [26, 27].

In non-expectorating subjects, either spontaneously or following treatment with CFTRm, oropharyngeal sampling or cough swabs have their place in clinical practice and should be used in cases where induced sputum is not feasible [28]. Induced sputum, following nebulised hypertonic saline, has a higher yield of pathogens than cough swabs. In symptomatic children, it is considered equivalent to two-lobe bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) which, although invasive, is much more sensitive for pathogen detection than oropharyngeal sampling [27–30].

The purpose of this review is to provide, in the current context, indications regarding the best antibiotic strategy to adopt in subjects affected by CF infected with the most common pathogens relying on literature-based data.

Methodology

For this review, a Medline search of English-language literature was performed. The European CF Society’s Standards of care, systematic reviews, guidelines regarding antibiotic treatment in CF and clinical trials considered relevant based on the authors’ experience were taken into account. In addition, the most recent publications regarding the laboratory standards for processing microbiological samples from pwCF were analysed and data from the most important CF Registries were taken into consideration.

For those pathogens which the most important Registries considered clinically relevant [4, 6, 7], we analysed the sampling methods and antibiotic strategies adopted for infection prophylaxis, early eradication treatment and chronic suppressive treatment during the stage of clinical stability. In pulmonary exacerbations (PEx), we analysed the treatments and their duration. The definition of chronic infection used is that of the European Cystic Fibrosis Society [4].

Results

From a theoretical point of view, the approaches to antibiotic treatment against the main pathogens consist in infection prophylaxis, early eradication treatment in the initial stages of infection, chronic maintenance therapy and treatment of PEx. However, this scheme is mainly valid for P. aeruginosa as there is no evidence regarding other CF pathogens.

Antibiotic treatment of methicillin-sensitive and methicillin-resistant S. aureus pulmonary infections

In the pre-antibiotic era, the major cause of mortality was methicillin-sensitive S. aureus (MSSA). Although the pathogenic role of MSSA has always been well recognised, therapeutic strategies on how to treat different stages of bacterial infection have not been established with certainty [31–33].

Prophylactic treatment of MSSA infections remains controversial and is not currently included in the standard of care [30]. This approach has been adopted mainly in the UK and clinical trials to test its efficacy are currently underway [34]. For MSSA, there are currently no clear indications for early eradication treatment and chronic suppressive therapy [30–33]. During PEx antibiotic treatment consists of parenteral administration of drugs that are classically active against this pathogen. The optimal duration of treatment remains to be defined.

PwCF harbouring methicillin-resistant strains of S. aureus (MRSA) have a more rapid decline in respiratory function tests (FEV1) and a higher mortality [35–41]. As regards MRSA, the strategies to be adopted in the early stages of infection or in chronic phases are also not established with certainty. Two clinical trials have evaluated the efficacy of eradication treatment against MRSA in pwCF [37, 38]. Treatment regimens are based on oral drugs (usually co-trimoxazole and rifampicin) for a period of 14 days, in combination with mupirocin applied locally in the nostrils for 5 days, and personal hygiene measures. One of the trials ended with the demonstration of microbiological efficacy over a short period [37]. Other treatment regimens, based on limited case reports, are reported in the literature. To date, there are no clear indications of the efficacy of chronic suppressive therapy in persistent MRSA infection [30, 42]. A trial involving inhaled vancomycin powder did not meet its primary endpoint (change in FEV1) nor result in a reduction in PEx in comparison with the placebo [43].

The treatment of severe PEx caused by MRSA is usually performed with glycopeptide antibiotics (vancomycin and teicoplanin) given parenterally and combined with other drugs (e.g. gentamicin or rifampicin). Linezolid, a drug belonging to the oxazolidinone family, should be reserved for treatment of refractory MRSA infections [42]. A recently marketed antibiotic, ceftaroline fosamil, represents a good alternative to the use of vancomycin but only a limited number of studies exist in pwCF [44, 45]. The efficacy of other molecules, considered potentially active against MRSA, needs to be confirmed by further studies.

Antibiotic treatment of P. aeruginosa pulmonary infections

P. aeruginosa is the pathogen for which antibiotic strategies in the various stages of infection are best established [30]. In the absence of treatment, following the initial infection, which usually remains asymptomatic, the germ is increasingly isolated in the airways and the infection evolves towards chronicity [1, 3, 15, 46]. PwCF with minimal CFTR function have an earlier acquisition of P. aeruginosa than individuals with residual function [47]. Initial infection is usually characterised by the acquisition of environmental strains that undergo progressive adaptation from environmental pathogens to CF airway-adapted pathogens [46, 48]. Initial infections of P. aeruginosa due to cross-infection have also been described [49].

In the initial phase of colonisation, single P. aeruginosa cells are not yet organised into biofilms and can be eradicated from the airways [46, 48]. As penetration of antibiotics into secretions can be difficult, aerosolised antibiotics have been considered particularly attractive [15]. Only a limited number of randomised controlled trials have been conducted on early eradication treatment, but outcome measures differed between studies [50–58]. A Cochrane review showed that nebulised antibiotics, alone or in combination with oral treatment, were better than no treatment for initial P. aeruginosa infection but no one treatment showed superior efficacy [15].

A multicentre, double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled trial tested the hypothesis that the addition of azithromycin to tobramycin inhaled solution (TIS) in CF children, 6 months to 18 years of age, with initial P. aeruginosa infection would reduce the risk of PEx, delaying P. aeruginosa recurrence [59]. Although azithromycin was associated with a significant reduction in the risk of PEx, no impact was observed on microbiological outcomes.

A trial suggests that early eradication treatment should be started promptly, not more than 4 weeks after P. aeruginosa isolation [57].

Parenteral therapy against initial P. aeruginosa infection is used in clinical practice but greater efficacy in comparison with oral treatment is not supported by the result of clinical trials. A randomised controlled trial evaluated the efficacy of 14 days of intravenous ceftazidime and tobramycin in comparison with 12 weeks of oral ciprofloxacin in a large number of persons [58]. Both regimens were combined with 12 weeks inhaled colistimethate sodium. The primary outcome was P. aeruginosa eradication at 3 months, with people remaining free of infection up to 15 months. Compared with oral therapy, intravenous therapy was not more effective than oral treatment and the cost of treatment was significantly higher [58].

The adoption of early eradication treatments in an effort to delay chronic P. aeruginosa infection is considered one of the major advances in CF therapy. Thanks to the efficacy of early eradication treatment, the number of pwCF in whom the evolution towards chronic P. aeruginosa infection occurs has been progressively reduced over time, even though re-infection may be observed. An Italian multicentre study on the efficacy of early eradication treatment showed that in more than 25% of pwCF, the subsequent infection is caused by a strain of P. aeruginosa genetically related to the one responsible for the initial infection [54]. In these cases, a reservoir may be assumed, and it may be useful to assess the microbiological status of the paranasal sinuses [60–62]. Data from the literature underline the role of the paranasal sinuses as the site of initial infection and progressive adaptation of P. aeruginosa to the airways [60–62].

PwCF with chronic P. aeruginosa infection alternate phases of stability and episodes of PEx that are characterised by acute changes in symptoms and increased airway secretions [30, 63, 64].

PEx are associated with poor quality of life, disease progression and decreased survival [63–68]. There is currently no unambiguous definition of PEx and the decision to start or not to start antibiotic treatment may be subjective [63]. In the case of an exacerbation, the choice of antibiotic therapy is usually based on susceptibility testing but in CF the clinical response may be discordant from the antibiotic susceptibility pattern [12, 30, 64]. This discordance assessed in terms of FEV1 variation has long been demonstrated on bacterial isolates responsible for exacerbation [64]. Antibiotic susceptibility testing against P. aeruginosa and other non-fermenting pathogens should always be performed in cases of respiratory exacerbation and when a therapeutic change is necessary [12, 30]. From a practical point of view, it is always useful to evaluate the response to previously performed treatments [12, 30].

The severity of PEx determines the choice of treatment and ‘mild’ exacerbations can be treated with oral antibiotic therapy. In the case of ‘severe’ exacerbations, there are clear indications for parenteral antibiotic treatment but the duration of treatment has been established only in adults [68]. Dual antibiotic therapy, usually the combination of a beta-lactam and an aminoglycoside, is chosen as this synergic combination increases bactericidal activity by reducing the possibility of selecting resistant strains [30, 64, 66].

According to literature data, the response to parenteral treatment is not always satisfactory, and in 25% of PEx, the original FEV1 is not recovered [65].

P. aeruginosa multi-resistant strains have caused much concern but their clinical significance in CF is unclear as a correlation between antibiotic resistance and clinical outcome has not been demonstrated [7, 64]. The in vitro finding of antibiotic resistance does not indicate that current parenteral treatment should be modified if a clinical response to therapy is appreciable [12].

There is no evidence that additional treatment with nebulised antibiotics can improve the efficacy of parenteral treatment and only a limited number of trials have been conducted in this regard [69, 70].

In pwCF with chronic P. aeruginosa infection and stable clinical conditions, the usefulness of suppressive therapy, a strategy consisting of the administration of antibiotics under conditions of clinical stability to limit the damaging effects of pathogens in the airways, has been established over time. This type of strategy was originally based on the administration of parenteral antibiotic cycles on a quarterly basis and has been used for a long time [64]. The commercialisation of specific specialities for aerosol administration, together with the improved performance of the devices for nebulisation, has changed the concept of chronic maintenance therapy, and the use of parenteral therapy has now decreased considerably. Sensitivity tests are not considered useful for the choice of inhaled antibiotic treatment [12]. Conventional breakpoints are in fact based on the drug concentrations achieved locally in the case of systemic administration. In the case of inhaled antibiotic therapy, in the airways, concentrations well above the MICs of P. aeruginosa are reached and the drug is usually effective even when the antibiotic susceptibilities tests show resistance. The possibility of administering an aerosolised antibiotic directly into the airways, in a simple and effective manner, has conditioned anti-infective research. The first antibiotic specifically studied in clinical trials for aerosol administration in pwCF was tobramycin [71]. In several trials, tobramycin-based specialities, both in solution and in powder form for inhalation use, showed remarkable efficacy against people treated with placebo alone, and a marked improvement in FEV1, reduction in hospital admissions and respiratory exacerbations was demonstrated [72].

In Europe, tobramycin-based specialities have flanked inhaled colistin, which has long been used for the positive effects demonstrated in repeated clinical observations [64, 72].

The efficacy of inhaled tobramycin in subsequent comparative studies declined over time and in the studies where it has been used as a reference treatment, a minor effect on FEV1 variation has been observed [73–75].

Inhaled aztreonam lysine (AZLI) showed efficacy against P. aeruginosa infection. In a randomised trial conducted in comparison with inhaled tobramycin in pwCF with chronic P. aeruginosa infection, AZLI demonstrated superiority in increasing respiratory function tests (FEV1) and in reducing the number of PEx [73].

More recently, levofloxacin for inhalation in pwCF with chronic P. aeruginosa infection entered the European market. Two randomised controlled trials compared inhaled levofloxacin against placebo and inhaled tobramycin in subjects over 12 years of age. Treatment with inhaled levofloxacin for 4 weeks did not reduce the frequency of exacerbations compared to placebo-treated subjects. Treatment resulted in a relative increase in FEV1 of 1.31% in the active arm compared to placebo [74, 75]. In a 6-month phase 3 study, the use of inhaled levofloxacin every other month was not inferior to tobramycin treatment [74, 75].

A network meta-analysis compared the efficacy of inhaled levofloxacin in the short and long term to other antibiotics licensed for inhalation use. The meta-analysis demonstrated that the mean changes in FEV1 at 4 and 24 weeks were not different among the various antibiotics approved for inhaled use [76]. The need to use other antibiotics active against P. aeruginosa and the percentage of subjects who discontinued treatment due to the occurrence of undesirable effects were comparable between the various types of treatment.

In a randomised study, amikacin liposome inhalation suspension was non-inferior to inhaled tobramycin in improving lung function in pwCF chronically infected with P. aeruginosa after three treatment cycles and it was well tolerated [77].

PwCF treated with inhaled antibiotics according to the classic every other month (on–off) schedule maintained lung function for longer period, suffered fewer exacerbations and required fewer parenteral treatments or hospitalisations [72].

The strategy of inhaled antibiotic administration has progressively evolved from the use of antibiotics every other month (on–off schedule suggested by clinical trials) to the use of antibiotics in a continuous alternating mode (on-on schedule). In this regard, a clinical trial evaluated the efficacy of two inhaled drugs in a continuous alternating schedule (aztreonam lysine alternating with tobramycin) compared to the administration of tobramycin and placebo in alternating months [78]. Due to the limited number of subjects recruited, the trial did not reach the necessary power to demonstrate the superiority of continuous alternating treatment over the administration of a single antibiotic used every other month (on/off schedule). However, the use of inhaled antibiotics has gone far beyond the indications derived from clinical trials alone as the prescription of more than one class of antibiotics has progressively entered clinical practice. The alternating use of more than one class of antibiotics was predominantly used in adults with FEV1 values below 40% of predicted and with cultures repeatedly positive for P. aeruginosa [79].

An increase in the use of inhaled antibiotics has also been observed in the UK CF Trust Registry and the administration of inhaled antibiotics to as many chronically P. aeruginosa-infected subjects as possible is considered a priority goal [6].

The management of chronic P. aeruginosa lung infection with inhaled antibiotics has become part of the ‘standard of care’ [30, 80–82]. Over time, although in the absence of randomised clinical trials, knowledge about aerosol treatment modalities and antibiotic rotation principles has improved. In the absence of evidence, antibiotic stewardship is left to the experience of the clinician who usually bases their judgement on FEV1 decline over time and the number of exacerbations suffered by the subject.

Treatment of non-fermenting Gram negatives other than P. aeruginosa

Regarding all the other classical pathogens, such as B. cepacia complex, A. xylosoxidans and S. maltophilia, there are only clear indications for the use of antibiotics in the exacerbation phase [30]. Only a limited number of experiences have been published on the efficacy of early eradication treatment and chronic suppressive therapy. In many cases, management is left to the clinician’s experience and is not supported by RCT results [83–85]. Standards of care suggest a proactive approach to managing CF pathogens other than P. aeruginosa with antibiotic choice determined by local protocols, tolerability and adverse effects [30].

Conclusions and future perspectives

This paper provides an updated review on the antibiotic strategies adopted against the main pathogens in CF. Over time, literature analysis has shown that clinical research was more concerned with P. aeruginosa, which was considered the main pathogen for a long period [64]. Considering that many clinical studies focussing on P. aeruginosa took place in a more recent period and with better methodology, the evidence of antibiotic treatments against P. aeruginosa appears much better supported by clinical trials than those regarding MSSA or non-fermenting Gram negatives other than P. aeruginosa.

Today, there are guidelines for treatment in the exacerbation phase, the initial phase and the chronic phase [30, 66, 81]. Evidence supporting the effectiveness of chronic suppressive therapy with inhaled antibiotics is demonstrated only for chronic P. aeruginosa infection [30, 72]. In this case, the use of inhalation therapy changed the natural history of the disease, reduced the average annual decline of FEV1 and significantly improved the quality of life of pwCF. The continuous alternating (on/on) regimen of inhaled antibiotics in people chronically infected with P. aeruginosa in unstable clinical conditions is in use in many CF Centres.

For all the pathogens described above, lack of response to antibiotic treatment may occur despite the complete susceptibility of the bacterial strains. On the contrary, a satisfactory response to treatment observed in pwCF infected by multi-resistant strains suggests that microbiological definitions of resistance, classically based on MIC values, are not always applicable to chronic infections [12, 64].

The therapy with CFTR modulators, recently introduced into clinical practice and potentially effective for approximately 75% of genetically eligible Italian pwCFs [5], has led to substantial benefits in clinical conditions with an improvement of FEV1 and nutritional status, along with a reduction in PEx.

Despite the notable improvements in general clinical conditions and lung function, it is not yet completely known how treatment with modulators influences the microbiological status of pwCF. Registry studies conducted in people treated with Ivacaftor, the first modulator introduced into clinical use in 2012, demonstrated a reduction in P. aeruginosa isolations [24]. The Lumacaftor-Ivacaftor association showed an increase in microbial diversity at the airways level even if the effect was less consistent in other experiences [86]. In pwCF treated with ETI, the effect of the treatment was described at the level of the microbiome and the metabolome. Furthermore, a reduction in the relative abundance of pathogens in place of an increase in the relative abundance of anaerobes was described [87].

Although in the era of CFTRm hospitalisation and intravenous treatments have been significantly reduced, there is still a need to develop anti-infective therapies [88, 89]. The possibility of using multi-phage cocktails could also open up new therapeutic possibilities for pwCF with multi-resistant P. aeruginosa infections especially for those whose genotype does not allow them to benefit from modulators. The potential benefit of developing drugs other than antibiotics for the treatment of bacterial P. aeruginosa infections in CF is still considered potentially relevant and clinical trials using phage therapy specific to P. aeruginosa are already underway [89].

For the overall improvement obtained over time in pwCF, pending further evaluations on the beneficial effects of treatment with modulators on the microbiome of pwCF, inhaled antibiotic therapies and the scheduling of airways microbiological examinations should be maintained according to the standards of care [90].
