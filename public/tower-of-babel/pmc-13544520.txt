Integrating Machine Learning for Early COPD Prediction in Lung Cancer Patients: A Focus on Systemic Coagulation-Inflammation Index
Authors: Qianfei Liu, Ling Hou, Huiling Li, Yin Li, Quanfang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) often coexists with lung cancer, worsening clinical outcomes. This study aimed to develop machine learning models for early COPD screening in lung cancer patients using clinical variables and a novel systemic coagulation–inflammation index (SCI).MethodsWe retrospectively enrolled 1016 patients, extracting demographic, smoking, vital, and laboratory data. After feature selection with Boruta and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), six models—logistic regression, decision tree (DT), multilayer perceptron (MLP), support vector machine (SVM), gradient boosting decision tree (GBDT), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost)—were trained on 70% of the data and tested on 30%. Model performance was assessed with area under the curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1 score, and calibration, while SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and contour plots helped interpret the best model and explore predictor interactions.ResultsAmong the 1,016 patients, 182 (17.9%) had concomitant COPD. Boruta and LASSO identified 8 key predictors: age, historical smoking index (HSI), SCI, eosinophils (EOS), bicarbonate (HCO3−), sex, lymphocytes (LYM), and hemoglobin (HGB). The GBDT model showed the best performance, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.74 (95% CI: 0.69–0.80) and moderate sensitivity (0.68) and specificity (0.66). SHAP analysis revealed age, HSI, and SCI as major risk factors, with contour plots indicating a synergistic effect between SCI, age, and HSI on COPD risk.ConclusionA GBDT-based model built from routine clinical variables and SCI showed moderate discrimination for identifying patients with lung cancer at high risk of concomitant COPD.Keywords: machine learning, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, lung cancer, systemic coagulation-inflammation index, gradient boosting decision tree, screening model

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide and continues to impose a substantial global health burden.1 Although advances in diagnosis and treatment have improved survival in selected patient populations, comorbidities are increasingly recognized as major determinants of clinical outcomes in routine practice.2 Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is among the most common comorbid conditions in patients with lung cancer, with reported prevalence estimates ranging from 28% to 40%.3 Clinically, the coexistence of COPD has been associated with a higher risk of postoperative complications, poorer quality of life, and shorter overall survival. In addition, COPD may influence the response to immune checkpoint inhibitors and thereby alter antitumor efficacy.4,5 Early identification of patients with lung cancer who are at high risk of concomitant COPD is therefore of clear clinical relevance for treatment planning and prognostic stratification.

Despite its importance, recognizing COPD in patients with lung cancer remains challenging. COPD often presents insidiously, and symptoms such as cough, sputum production, and dyspnea may be attributed to lung cancer itself or to smoking-related respiratory complaints, leading to substantial underdiagnosis. It has been estimated that nearly three-quarters of individuals with COPD worldwide remain undiagnosed.6 At present, the diagnosis of COPD relies primarily on spirometry, typically defined by a post-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1)/forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio <0.70. However, some patients with lung cancer are unable to complete pulmonary function testing because of poor physical status, and access to spirometry may be limited in primary or resource-constrained settings. Existing guidelines do not recommend routine spirometric screening in asymptomatic populations, largely because of concerns regarding cost-effectiveness.7 Alternative screening tools, including questionnaires and handheld spirometers, have received growing attention in recent years,8 but currently available approaches such as CAPTURE have shown only modest sensitivity for clinically meaningful COPD.9 Taken together, these limitations contribute to persistently high rates of missed COPD diagnoses in patients with lung cancer and underscore the need for simpler, more accessible, and more scalable screening strategies.

Routine hematologic and biochemical markers are attractive candidates for this purpose because they are inexpensive, readily available, and highly reproducible. Such markers are already widely used in disease screening and risk stratification. In recent years, several inflammation-related indices—including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), and lymphocyte–albumin–neutrophil ratio—have been linked to COPD susceptibility and progression.10,11 At the same time, accumulating evidence suggests that activation of the coagulation system is also involved in COPD pathophysiology.12 The Systemic Coagulation–Inflammation Index (SCI), defined as platelet count × D-dimer/white blood cell count, is a novel composite biomarker that integrates information from both inflammatory and coagulation pathways. Such composite indices may capture synergistic or threshold effects between pathways, making them particularly suitable for machine learning approaches that can model these complex interactions without prespecifying functional forms. Algorithms such as support vector machines, multilayer perceptrons, and gradient boosting methods have been adopted across a range of clinical prediction tasks, from early disease detection to risk stratification.13 Accordingly, the present study sought to develop and compare multiple machine learning models for early COPD screening in a lung cancer cohort using routinely available clinical variables together with SCI. Particular attention was given to the predictive contribution of SCI and to the interpretability of the final model.

Methods

Study Population

This retrospective study included hospitalized patients who were newly diagnosed with lung cancer in the Department of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine at Enshi Tujia and Miao Autonomous Prefecture Central Hospital between July 2023 and December 2025, and were retrospectively analyzed in January 2026. Eligible participants met all of the following criteria: (1) pathologically or cytologically confirmed primary lung cancer; (2) age ≥18 years. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following conditions: (1) chest computed tomography findings suggestive of bronchitis or emphysema but no available pulmonary function test results, precluding a definitive COPD diagnosis; (2) severe infection or sepsis; (3) cachexia or multiple organ dysfunction; (4) missing >30% of essential clinical data; or (5) concomitant malignancies other than lung cancer. COPD was diagnosed according to the 2023 Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) criteria, based on post-bronchodilator spirometry with a FEV1 to FVC ratio of less than 0.70.14 Patients were classified at the time of hospital discharge, based on both spirometry results and discharge diagnoses. Patients with pre-existing COPD diagnosed prior to the index hospitalization were also included in the comorbid group. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Enshi Tujia and Miao Autonomous Prefecture Central Hospital (approval No. 2025–165-01). All data were anonymized prior to analysis, and the requirement for informed consent was waived. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data Collection

Clinical data were extracted from the hospital electronic medical record system. Variables included demographic characteristics, vital signs, and laboratory parameters obtained at admission. Demographic and clinical variables included age, sex, historical smoking index (HSI; defined as number of cigarettes smoked per day × years of smoking), alcohol use, and medical history (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary heart disease, and stroke). Vital signs included respiratory rate (Resp_Rate) and systolic blood pressure (SBP). Laboratory variables were based on blood tests performed on admission and included complete blood count parameters [white blood cell count, platelet count, absolute neutrophil count (NEU), LYM, absolute monocyte count (MONO), EOS, red blood cell count (RBC), HGB, hematocrit (HCT), and red cell distribution width coefficient of variation (RDW_CV)], inflammatory markers [C-reactive protein (CRP) and procalcitonin (PCT)], coagulation marker [D-dimer], liver function parameter [albumin (ALB)], renal/metabolic parameter [carbon dioxide combining power (CO2CP)], and arterial blood gas–related indices [bicarbonate (HCO3−), lactate, and oxygenation index (OI)]. The systemic coagulation–inflammation index (SCI) was calculated as follows: SCI = platelet count × D-dimer/white blood cell count. This composite index was intended to capture the interplay between coagulation activation and systemic inflammation.

Feature Selection

The 1,016 participants were randomly divided into a training set (70%, n = 711) and a test set (30%, n = 305). All feature selection procedures were performed strictly within the training set to avoid information leakage from the test data. Variables with <30% missing values were retained. Missing values for the remaining variables were independently imputed in the training and test sets using Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) with the iterative random forest method, under the assumption that data were missing at random. The imputation procedure was performed separately in the training and test sets to prevent information leakage. To reduce feature redundancy, minimize overfitting, and identify the most stable predictors, we used a two-step feature selection strategy combining Boruta and LASSO regression. First, the Boruta algorithm, implemented within a random forest framework, was applied to identify features genuinely associated with the outcome. Boruta creates shadow features by permuting the original variables and iteratively compares the importance of the real variables against these randomized counterparts. Features that consistently outperform the shadow features are retained. In this study, Boruta was run for 100 iterations. Second, LASSO logistic regression with L1 regularization was used for additional variable selection. The optimal penalty parameter (λ) was determined using 10-fold cross-validation, and only variables with nonzero coefficients at the selected λ value were retained. The intersection of variables selected by both Boruta and LASSO was then used as the final feature set for subsequent model development, with the aim of prioritizing predictors that were both statistically stable and clinically plausible.

Model Development and Evaluation

Model development was based on the final feature set identified during feature selection. The training set was used for model fitting and hyperparameter tuning, whereas the test set was reserved for independent performance evaluation. For feature preprocessing, continuous variables were used in their original scale for tree-based models (DT, GBDT, and XGBoost). For logistic regression and SVM, variables were standardized using z-score normalization (mean = 0, standard deviation = 1). For MLP, variables were range-normalized to [0, 1] to accelerate convergence during neural network training. Six machine learning algorithms were developed and compared: logistic regression, DT, MLP, SVM, GBDT, and XGBoost. Hyperparameters for each model were optimized using grid search in combination with 5-fold cross-validation, with maximization of the training-set AUC as the primary optimization criterion. Model performance was assessed from both discrimination and calibration perspectives. Discrimination was evaluated using the AUC-ROC, supplemented by accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1 score, and average precision (AP). Calibration was assessed using calibration plots comparing predicted probabilities with observed event rates. The best-performing model was selected based on overall performance across these complementary metrics rather than on a single indicator alone.

Model Interpretability

To improve interpretability, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were applied to the best-performing model. SHAP values were computed in the training set to quantify the marginal contribution of each feature to the model output at the individual level. A SHAP summary plot was used to visualize the distribution of SHAP values across all included features and to show the direction of their association with predicted COPD risk. Feature importance was ranked according to the mean absolute SHAP value (mean |SHAP|). A SHAP decision plot was further generated to illustrate the cumulative contribution of major predictors to the predicted probability for individual patients. To further explore interactions involving the key inflammatory–coagulation marker, SHAP interaction values were used to examine the interplay between SCI and other clinical variables. Contour plots were then constructed to visualize nonlinear relationships and potential synergistic effects between SCI and major predictors, thereby providing additional insight into the mechanisms underlying COPD risk in patients with lung cancer.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis and curve plotting were performed using R version 4.3.3 and Python version 3.9. Continuous variables were summarized as median (interquartile range) and compared using appropriate nonparametric tests when distributions were non-normal. Categorical variables were expressed as counts (percentages) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Compared with the LC group, patients in the LC-COPD group were older [69 (63–73) vs 65 (57–71) years, P < 0.001], were more frequently male (86.8% vs 65.3%, P < 0.001), and had a higher smoking burden [HSI: 600 (370–1000) vs 300 (0–800), P < 0.001]. A history of alcohol use was also more common in the LC-COPD group (39.0% vs 28.3%, P = 0.004). Regarding comorbidities, a higher proportion of prior stroke was observed in the LC-COPD group, although this difference did not reach conventional statistical significance (10.4% vs 6.4%, P = 0.052). No significant between-group differences were observed for the remaining medical history variables. With respect to laboratory findings, patients with LC-COPD had significantly higher levels of LYM, EOS, RBC, HGB, HCT, HCO3−, and CO2CP than those in the LC group (all P < 0.05). Notably, SCI was also modestly but significantly higher in the LC-COPD group [133.56 (94.59–179.23) vs 124.84 (84.48–179.63), P = 0.041]. Other variables, including NEU, MONO, CRP, PCT, and OI, did not differ significantly between groups.

Feature Selection

The Boruta algorithm identified eight features significantly associated with COPD status: age, HSI, SCI, EOS, HCO3−, sex, LYM, and HGB (Figure 2A and B). LASSO regression retained 15 predictors with non-zero coefficients at the optimal λ value, including age, HSI, SCI, EOS, HCO3−, sex, LYM, HGB, diabetes mellitus, stroke, CRP, CO2CP, RDW-CV, D-dimer, and lactate (Figure 2C and D). The intersection of both methods yielded eight overlapping variables—age, HSI, SCI, EOS, HCO3−, sex, LYM, and HGB—which were subsequently incorporated into model development (Figure 2E). These predictors were considered the most stable and clinically relevant factors.

Model Performance Comparison

Performance metrics of the six machine learning models in both cohorts are presented in Figure 3. In the training set, the GBDT model achieved the best discrimination, with an AUC of 0.84 (95% CI 0.81–0.87), accuracy of 0.76, sensitivity of 0.80, specificity of 0.75, F1 score of 0.54, and AP of 0.63 (Figure 3A). In the testing set, overall performance declined across models; however, GBDT maintained relatively robust discrimination, yielding an AUC of 0.74 (95% CI 0.69–0.80), accuracy of 0.67, sensitivity of 0.68, specificity of 0.66, F1 score of 0.41, and AP of 0.43. Logistic regression demonstrated a comparable AUC (0.75) and the highest sensitivity (0.91), though at the expense of lower specificity (0.52) (Figure 3B). MLP and XGBoost showed similar AUCs (0.74 and 0.74, respectively), whereas support vector machine and decision tree models performed less favorably (AUC 0.58 and 0.65, respectively) (Figure 4A and B). Calibration analysis indicated satisfactory agreement between predicted and observed probabilities for the GBDT model (Figure 4C and D). Considering discrimination, calibration, and stability between training and testing cohorts, GBDT was selected as the optimal model.

Model Interpretability

SHAP analysis was performed to interpret the GBDT model. The SHAP summary plots (Figure 5A and B) ranked predictors by importance, with age, HSI, and SCI exerting the strongest influence on model output, followed by EOS, HCO3−, sex, LYM, and HGB. Advanced age, higher HSI, and elevated SCI emerged as the principal positive contributors to COPD risk, with wide SHAP value distributions indicating interindividual heterogeneity. Increased EOS, HCO3−, LYM, and HGB levels were also associated with higher predicted risk, and male contributed positively to risk estimation. The SHAP decision plot (Figure 5C) further illustrated the cumulative effect of multiple high-risk features. As adverse predictors such as older age, heavier smoking burden, and higher SCI accumulated, the predicted probability progressively shifted toward the high-risk range, suggesting potential predictive interactions among these variables. To further examine the role of SCI, contour plots were generated to visualize its interaction with age (Figure 6A), HIS (Figure 6B), EOS (Figure 6C), and HCO3−(Figure 6D). Across different levels of these covariates, predicted COPD probability increased monotonically with rising SCI, with a particularly steep risk gradient observed in the higher SCI range (eg, >150). These findings suggest that SCI showed synergistic predictive patterns with conventional risk factors and may have distinct value for COPD risk stratification in patients with lung cancer.

Discussion

In this retrospective study of 1,016 patients with lung cancer, we applied a combined Boruta–LASSO feature selection strategy and identified eight stable predictors—age, HSI, SCI, EOS, HCO3−, sex, LYM, and HGB. Six machine learning algorithms were subsequently compared for predicting concomitant COPD. Among them, the GBDT model achieved the best overall performance, with a training AUC of 0.84 and a testing AUC of 0.74, indicating acceptable discrimination with modest generalizability. SHAP analysis demonstrated that advanced age, higher HSI, and elevated SCI were the most influential positive contributors to COPD risk, while increased EOS, HCO3−, LYM, HGB, and male sex were also associated with higher predicted probability. Contour plots further revealed synergistic interactions between SCI and age or HSI. Collectively, the proposed model offers a potentially useful approach for early COPD screening among patients with lung cancer.

Lung cancer and COPD share complex biological interactions involving genetic susceptibility, epigenetic regulation, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, immune dysregulation, and microbiome alterations.15–20 Among these, chronic inflammation is widely regarded as a central biological link. In COPD, persistent activation of inflammatory cells—particularly neutrophils—promotes the release of neutrophil elastase, cathepsin G, matrix metalloproteinases (notably MMP-8 and MMP-9), and myeloperoxidase, which contribute directly to emphysematous destruction and airway remodeling.21 Platelets are also increasingly recognized as active participants in inflammatory signaling. Upregulation of platelet surface P-selectin can promote the formation of platelet–leukocyte aggregates, thereby amplifying leukocyte-mediated inflammation, while mediators released from platelet α-granules may further sustain airway inflammatory activity.22

In parallel, dysregulated coagulation has emerged as another important feature of COPD pathophysiology. COPD is frequently accompanied by a prothrombotic state, and elevated coagulation markers have been associated with worse outcomes.23 Fibrinogen, a classic acute-phase reactant, is increased in both stable disease and acute exacerbations and has been linked to faster decline in lung function, more frequent exacerbations, and increased mortality.23,24 Abnormal elevations in D-dimer, prothrombin, and other coagulation-related factors have also been associated with disease progression and exacerbation risk.25,26 Importantly, inflammation and coagulation do not operate in isolation. Inflammatory mediators can activate the coagulation cascade, whereas coagulation activation may in turn reinforce inflammatory signaling, creating a self-perpetuating cycle. The SCI used in the present study was designed to capture this biological coupling by integrating platelet count, D-dimer, and white blood cell count into a single composite measure. In our cohort, SCI was significantly higher in patients with LC-COPD than in those with lung cancer alone, remained in the final feature set after both Boruta and LASSO selection, and ranked immediately after age and HSI in the SHAP importance analysis. These converging observations support the possibility that the coagulation–inflammation axis contributes meaningfully to COPD susceptibility in the setting of lung cancer.

SCI, as a composite index incorporating platelets, D-dimer, and leukocytes, has recently been explored as a prognostic biomarker in various clinical contexts.27,28 For example, it has been reported as a strong predictor of recurrent deep vein thrombosis28. In chronic airway disease, Hou et al29 retrospectively analyzed 957 patients with COPD and found that individuals experiencing clinical deterioration within one year had significantly higher SCI levels than stable patients (median 93.08 vs 81.67). In their analysis, a support vector machine achieved the best predictive performance among six algorithms, suggesting potential utility of SCI in forecasting adverse COPD outcomes. However, that study focused on longitudinal prognosis within COPD populations and did not address comorbidity with lung cancer. Xu et al11 conducted a cross-sectional study including 502 participants (309 with COPD alone and 193 with COPD plus lung cancer) and demonstrated that SII >629.285 and LANR <11.39 were independently associated with lung cancer risk among COPD patients. Their nomogram achieved an AUC of 0.849, outperforming traditional scoring systems. Nevertheless, their investigation aimed to identify lung cancer within a COPD cohort, whereas our study addressed the inverse clinical question—detecting COPD among patients with lung cancer. A recent study by Zhu et al30 adopted a similar methodological framework, integrating multiple inflammatory biomarkers with LASSO-based feature selection and identifying a gradient-boosting model as the best performer, further supporting the utility of this analytic approach in COPD-related prediction tasks. Additionally, Liao et al31 systematically evaluated multiple blood cell inflammatory ratios in AECOPD patients and confirmed their value as accessible biomarkers, which aligns with our rationale for using routine hematological parameters. This study explores the potential application of SCI in COPD screening within a lung cancer population using machine learning techniques. The findings highlight its independent associative value and reveal potential synergistic patterns between SCI and conventional risk factors, such as age and smoking burden. These results suggest potential clinical relevance of SCI and provide epidemiological evidence supporting the role of the coagulation–inflammation axis in lung cancer–COPD comorbidity. Notably, as with all observational studies, these findings represent predictive associations rather than causal effects. Recent work has demonstrated the application of explainable machine learning to identify misdiagnosis factors in respiratory diseases,32 which resonates with our goal of reducing COPD underdiagnosis. Future studies incorporating causal inference frameworks may help verify whether SCI exerts an independent or potentially causal role in the development of COPD among lung cancer patients.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a single-center retrospective study, selection bias cannot be excluded, and external validation was not performed; thus, the generalizability of the model requires confirmation in independent cohorts. Second, the class imbalance (only 17.9% of patients had COPD) resulted in low precision and F1 scores, particularly in the test set. No resampling or class-weighting strategies were applied, as we prioritized the avoidance of potential information leakage from synthetic observations and the preservation of real-world class prevalence in performance estimation; however, future studies should explore threshold optimization or cost-sensitive learning approaches with careful cross-validation safeguards. Third, detailed spirometric parameters (eg, FEV1, FVC, and DLCO), lung cancer stage (TNM classification), cancer treatment regimens, medication use (eg, inhaled corticosteroids, bronchodilators), environmental and occupational exposures, and respiratory infection status were not systematically available from the electronic medical records and could not be included in the model, which may have influenced model performance and confounded the interpretation of predictor importance. Fourth, the moderate overall discrimination (AUC = 0.74) indicates that the model is most appropriately viewed as a screening aid to identify patients who may benefit from confirmatory spirometry, rather than as a standalone diagnostic instrument. Fifth, although SCI was identified as an important predictor by both feature selection and SHAP analyses, the underlying biological mechanisms linking the coagulation-inflammation axis to COPD susceptibility warrant further mechanistic and prospective investigation. Finally, the sample size was moderate; although cross-validation was applied to mitigate overfitting, larger multicenter studies with external validation are necessary to verify the robustness and stability of the proposed model before clinical application.

Conclusion

Using routinely available clinical variables and a novel systemic coagulation–inflammation index, we developed a machine learning–based model for identifying patients with lung cancer who are at increased risk of concomitant COPD. Among the evaluated algorithms, GBDT showed a reasonable overall balance of discrimination, calibration, and clinical interpretability. Notably, SCI emerged as a distinctive and potentially informative composite marker, suggesting a possible role of the coagulation–inflammation axis in the LC-COPD overlap phenotype. After external validation, this approach may offer a potential framework for early COPD screening and individualized risk stratification in routine oncology and respiratory care settings.
