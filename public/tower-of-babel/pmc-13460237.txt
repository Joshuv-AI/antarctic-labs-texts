Insights From Older People With Dementia and Mild Cognitive Impairment on the Management of Driving Cessation by Health Professionals: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Claire Spargo, Kate Laver, Zoe Adey‐Wakeling, Angela Berndt, Stacey George
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo obtain insights into the needs and experiences of older people with dementia or mild cognitive impairment (MCI) regarding health professionals' management of driving cessation.MethodsA qualitative descriptive approach within an interpretive paradigm was used to study people in their own context. Semi‐structured interviews with 12 people aged 65 years or older with dementia/MCI were conducted, recruited from a memory clinic at a South Australian tertiary hospital. Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsThemes identified driving cessation as a process involving four potential phases: (1) Preparation: Value of early and ongoing discussions about driving cessation, (2) Navigating fitness to drive decisions: Desire for information, options and guidance, (3) Receiving a driving cessation recommendation: Importance of clear and compassionate communication, and (4) Adjusting to life without driving: Feelings of loss of independence and loneliness. Participants identified specific communication needs from health professionals across the first three phases. In the fourth, post‐cessation phase, all who had ceased driving received emotional and/or practical support from family; however, none reported receiving support from health professionals.ConclusionsFindings highlighted the need for a structured and comprehensive communication approach over phases, from preparation to driving cessation, and increased post‐cessation support for people with dementia/MCI and their families.Keywords: ageing, automobile driving, clinical decision‐making, cognitive dysfunction, dementia

Introduction

Internationally, there is consensus among experts that all people with dementia will inevitably reach a point where they must stop driving due to the impact of cognitive impairments on skills critical for safe driving [1, 2, 3]. For people with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), the ability to continue driving depends on the underlying cause of their symptoms, which may improve, remain stable or worsen, with evidence showing that people with MCI experience more driving errors [4, 5, 6, 7].

In Australia, people with dementia cannot hold an unconditional licence but may qualify for a conditional licence subject to at least annual review [3]. National guidelines describe it being difficult to predict at which point a person will no longer be safe to drive, recommending a combination of medical assessment, off and on‐road practical assessments [3]. Medical fitness to drive is assessed by general practitioners (GPs), geriatricians or rehabilitation medicine physicians, who may consider factors such as the person's medical/driving history, driving needs, level of insight and clinical examination findings [3]. Referrals may be made for practical on‐road assessments with an occupational therapist who is trained in driving assessment and rehabilitation, or with a government examiner through the transport licensing authority [8, 9]. As practical assessments are costly, health professionals should consider whether these assessments are required [10]. The management of driving for people with dementia/MCI varies across states and services, including legislation for reporting and access to services [3].

In South Australia, where this study was based, fitness to drive is a shared responsibility between drivers and health professionals. Drivers are legally obligated to notify the licensing authority of a long‐term/permanent medical condition, which may influence their ability to drive safely [3]. Health professionals assess fitness to drive, advise the person regarding the potential impact of their medical condition on driving capabilities and, under mandatory reporting laws, notify the driver licensing authority if they believe the driver may present a danger to the public [3]. Most Australian states do not mandate reporting to the licensing authority.

Memory clinics, commonly led by medical practitioners specialising in geriatric medicine, are used extensively in Australia to assist with diagnosing and treating memory disorders such as dementia and MCI [11, 12]. As key points of contact for drivers with dementia/MCI and their families, these clinics provide information and support to assist with planning for the future, which may include recommendations regarding fitness to drive [13]. Driving concerns may also be addressed in general practice or hospital settings. Where uncertainty about fitness‐to‐drive exists, a referral to a specialised driving assessment clinic may be considered [14]. Alternatively, the treating medical practitioner may refer the person directly for a practical on‐road test.

Many older people are reluctant to stop driving, even when diagnosed with a progressive medical condition, such as dementia, which will ultimately compromise their driving ability [15, 16, 17, 18], due to its importance to their independence and sense of identity [19]. Driving cessation may be gradual for people with dementia/MCI, or it may be triggered by a crisis such as a crash or near miss [20, 21]. Driving cessation can be emotionally challenging, especially for those who may not fully understand the need to retire from driving, particularly if they have compromised insight into their cognitive abilities [17, 19]. Family members often assume decision‐making roles despite feeling unprepared, lacking expertise and fearing blame, particularly when the individual lacks insight into cognitive impairment and/or strongly identifies as a driver [16, 21]. Their willingness to raise concerns may also be constrained by limited capacity to provide alternative transport or reliance on the individual for their own mobility [2].

Furthermore, health professionals describe discomfort in making fitness to drive decisions for people with dementia and MCI [22, 23]. Families value the health professional input in managing driving cessation. However, they report receiving conflicting advice about a person's suitability to continue driving, or that driving cessation is only considered after the family raises concerns [16, 17, 20, 21]. Few studies have examined the perspectives of those with dementia and MCI diagnoses with findings often drawn from data that combine patient, family members and/or health professionals' perspectives [17, 20, 24, 25, 26, 27]. Consistently, people with dementia/MCI in these studies describe wanting control of determining when they should retire from driving [17, 25, 27]. They also desire clearer communication from health professionals, and more support to manage the transition to non‐driving [17, 25, 26]. Decision aids, such as the Dementia and Driving Decision Aid, aim to provide people with dementia with the necessary information to make an informed decision about driving cessation [28]. However, these aids may not suit those who lack the capacity to make informed decisions about their driving capacities attributable to reduced cognitive capacities, such as poor judgement and lack of self‐awareness.

As there is differing licensing eligibility between people with dementia and those with MCI (people with dementia cannot hold an unconditional licence but may qualify for a conditional licence subject to at least annual review, whereas those with MCI may continue to drive unrestricted), there may be a view among health practitioners that people with MCI do not require fitness to drive assessment. We included participants with both dementia and MCI in our sample because fail rates of on‐road assessments are higher in people with both MCI and dementia than in control participants [5, 7, 29], and driving cessation due to safety concerns is common across these diagnoses [30, 31]. As MCI, by definition, does not involve significant functional impairment, it is possible that many people in real‐life clinical settings with a formal diagnosis of MCI who are recommended to discontinue driving may, in fact, have dementia, characterised by functional impairment. This reflects challenges faced by practitioners in clinical settings including barriers to timely detection of dementia and difficulty differentiating between MCI and dementia, which may mean a diagnosis of MCI is inaccurate [32].

More research is needed to guide the design of resources and information needs to support health professionals with managing driving cessation for people with dementia/MCI who have varying cognitive capabilities. Understanding the perspectives of people with dementia and MCI on driving cessation will offer valuable insight into their lived experiences, preferences and perceived challenges, helping to guide person‐centred decision‐making and improve communication among health professionals, patients and families, ultimately supporting acceptance of and adjustment to driving cessation.

Our aim was to obtain insights into the needs and experiences of older people with dementia or MCI regarding health professionals' management of driving cessation.

Methods

Design, Participants and Data Collection

A qualitative descriptive methodological approach was used, aligning with an interpretive paradigm and providing a way of studying people in their own context [33, 34]. This approach seeks a descriptive validity or accurate account of the perspective of a person with lived experiences, holding them as the experts, with their experiences supported by data [34]. Data were collected using interviews with people aged 65 years or older with dementia/MCI who attended a memory clinic service at a tertiary hospital in South Australia. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling, through nursing staff, who introduced the study to eligible patients attending the memory clinic, asking whether they wished to be contacted by the researcher (CS) for more information. Inclusion criteria included: (a) documented diagnosis of MCI or dementia; (b) Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) score of 20 or above; and (c) currently driving or have stopped driving within the past 2 years.

Participants with both dementia and MCI were included in this study because driving cessation due to safety concerns is common across these diagnoses [30, 31]. An interview guide (Appendix S1) was developed and tested in a mock interview with colleagues. Interviews were conducted by a researcher (CS) with experience in qualitative research between March 2021 and June 2022. Since the interviews were conducted, an updated version of the Australian fitness to drive standards have been published [3]. Unlike previous versions, which focus on dementia, these standards recognise MCI as a condition that may require fitness‐to‐drive assessment. Interview duration ranged from 20 to 40 min. Participants were offered a A$40 gift card as a token of appreciation for their contribution. Further detail about data collection is presented in Appendix S2 using the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [35].

Setting

Participants were recruited from a publicly funded outpatient memory clinic, part of a metropolitan tertiary hospital in an Australia city. The memory clinic receives referrals from GPs, specialists and medical practitioners working in inpatient settings. The memory clinic is co‐located with a specialised driving service, which provides assessments relating specifically to a person's medical fitness to drive, which are conducted by a medical practitioner (rehabilitation consultant or registrar).

Data Analysis

Reflexive thematic analysis was used following Braun and Clarke's six‐step guidance [36, 37].

Analysis was inductively driven. The first author (CS) read and re‐read the transcripts and conducted coding of all interviews using Nvivo12. For example, the text ‘There's… public transport, there's a bus… but at this stage neither of us can walk overly far, …I have another problem, balance. It was (fine) when I was about 18, but… you just, lose balance…. (P2, current driver, restricted licence) was assigned the code ‘difficulty accessing alternative transport’. A reflexive approach followed, with the researcher (CS) iteratively moving between transcripts, coding, re‐coding and developing themes. Three initial themes were identified, reflecting participants needs and experiences relating to health professionals’ management of driving cessation. As the analysis progressed, a processual pattern became evident, and themes were reorganised into three overarching ‘phases’. Following this, an additional fourth phase was identified in the data. The senior author (SG) independently analysed five of the transcripts to ensure the phases were derived from the data. The phases were revised and refined following discussions with all authors.

A number of validation strategies were used to ensure rigour of the approach to the qualitative data in this study [35]. This included establishment of the research team, with extensive clinical and research experience in the field of occupational therapy, medical and driving clinical practice, independent coding of initial data sets by two researchers and reflective discussions with the team providing peer review and debriefing of the data and research process.

The sample size of 12 in this study was considered sufficient to generate rich, multifaceted data appropriate to the study's aims. The decision to stop after 12 interviews was a pragmatic decision that involved balancing the richness and breadth of the data with the time available for recruitment. The sampling approach aligns with Braun and Clarke's position that data saturation is not compatible with the values and assumptions of reflexive thematic analysis and that pragmatic decisions based on data richness and scope are more appropriate for guiding sample size [38].

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved by the Southern Adelaide Clinical Human Research Ethics Committee (ID: EC00188 OFR 154.20).

Findings of the Interviews

Driving cessation for people with dementia/MCI was described as a process involving four potential phases: (1) Preparation: Value of early and ongoing discussions about driving cessation; (2) Navigating fitness to drive decisions: Desire for information, options and guidance; (3) Receiving a driving cessation recommendation: Importance of clear and compassionate communication; and (4) Adjusting to life without driving: Feelings of loss of independence and loneliness.

Participants described communication needs from health professionals across three phases leading up to driving cessation, while post‐cessation challenges were reported in a fourth phase, during which no support from health professionals was described. An outline of each phase, with illustrative quotes, is presented below.

Preparation: Value of Early and Ongoing Discussions About the Need to Cease Driving

Participants reported that engaging in early and ongoing discussions with a GP and/or geriatrician over time about the necessity of retiring from driving can help them to process and accept this need:

If I go to the doctor tomorrow, she's not going to suddenly say about the driving… There would…be a lead up to that…. I would be discussing things with her over…time. (P5, female, MCI, current driver)

For some current drivers, self‐regulation, such as self‐limiting driving to local destinations or taking additional precautions such as not rushing due to reasons such as stress or older age (not specifically cognitive changes), was described as part of the driving cessation process. However, others reported that it was only after discussions with a medical professional (GP or geriatrician) that they reconsidered or modified their driving habits. These discussions often included a recommendation to restrict or limit their driving:

It was a bit of a shock when I could only drive a little way… but… we talked about it. Got used to it. Now if they tell me I can't drive, (I) wont drive. We will learn to catch the bus… (P6, male, dementia, current driver, conditional license)

When he (GP) brought it up I just thought… I'll be stuck at home….(I now know)…there'll come a time when I'll completely stop … when I haven't got…confidence anymore. (P5 female, MCI, current driver, conditional licence)

I'm resigned to the fact that… down the track I may not be able to drive anymore… the doctor might say something… that may well come from his inability to sign the form … I'm half expecting that… (P12, male, MCI, current driver, conditional licence)

Former drivers had varying experience ranging from reporting not receiving any input from health professionals to describing having conversations with the GP (and multiple practical on‐road assessments) in the lead up to driving cessation.

However, not all participants who had communicated with medical practitioners described a change in their viewpoint. One participant, who had been issued with additional licence conditions after a practical on‐road assessment, indicated no intention to retire from driving, despite having a progressive condition:

I want to drive at top speed right up until the last moment. I certainly don't want to give it up… (P9, male, dementia, current driver, conditional licence)

Navigating Fitness‐to‐Drive Decisions: Desire for Information, Options and Guidance

Participants emphasised a desire for information, options and guidance from health professionals regarding fitness‐to‐drive decisions. While many were comfortable with family involvement in discussions about their driving capacities, most preferred that a medical practitioner make decisions about driving on their behalf if they were unable to do so:

My family…sometimes they are trying to take over… I think… I would like the doctor to tell me. (P5, female, MCI, current driver)

However, some participants expressed dissatisfaction with the lack of information and guidance from health professionals involved in their care. One ex‐driver described:

…two years ago (the neuropsychologist) asked if I was still driving… he thought… I shouldn't by the outcome of these tests… But he didn't… instigate anything or give us any direction… our GP was getting copies of… the evaluations …‐ nothing has ever really been said‐ by the medicos‐ to me better stop driving… No one has really addressed the issue. (P3, male, MCI, stopped driving three months prior to interview)

He explained that, after noticing changes in his driving capacities, his family had decided not to renew his licence. However, both he and his family wanted to know whether he could complete a practical assessment:

I'm feeling it's been ripped away when maybe it didn't need to be… We want to know is there a test I can do now, a driving test evaluation, to see if my reactions have been impacted by the cognitive issues … a test would really be something. (P3, male, MCI, stopped driving three months prior to interview)

Another ex‐driver described his experience of feeling dissatisfied after being advised by his GP to complete a practical test, without discussing his options or being given information about what to expect:

It was a bit of …a shock, and…. disappointment, because there wasn't… any discussion about whether…I should be driving, with our GP, … there was… you've passed your eye test side of it, you might need a driving test… then out of the blue… this letter arrived… saying I had to do this test, which really, really upset me. I felt like everything was done so‐ behind my back, without much control of my own… (P7, male, MCI, stopped driving two weeks prior to the interview)

He described that heightened stress leading up to the practical test ultimately led him to make the decision with his family to discontinue driving instead of completing the test, however, he would have preferred to have input from a medical specialist:

At the end of the day, I didn't want to stop driving… if somebody could have been allocated to me… a medical specialist involved in the process of me not driving and driving… Maybe I could have come to it (the decision) a little bit easier… (P7, male, MCI stopped driving two weeks prior to the interview)

Another participant who attended a specialised driving clinic described a more positive experience of having their options clearly outlined to them:

We have been impressed with how clearly people have outlined things…the GP and (the doctor from the driving fitness assessment clinic), they put that so beautifully… The test was not at all confronting… you feel they are really looking after our best interest and…at the same time their responsibility… (P9, male, dementia, current driver, conditional licence)

Receiving a Driving Cessation Recommendation: Importance of Clear and Compassionate Communication

Participants spoke about the importance of health professionals providing clear communication when recommending discontinuation of driving. They emphasised the need for health professionals to be direct and use simple, straightforward language:

Be straight up… not dance around saying, this, that and the other – upfront, straight away. Not blunt, but nicely. (P6, male, dementia, current driver, conditional licence)

I think doctors should say, …I don't think you should drive anymore, you'd probably be upset or whatever… I'd rather they said that, than…ummed and ahhed. (P5, female, MCI, current driver)

Allowing time for the person to process information and ask questions was considered useful for helping them to understand:

… I've said to (my GP) when…I'm ready (to stop) you let me know… He'll make sure I understand because he spends a lot of time explaining everything. (P11, female, dementia, current driver)

Participants also highlighted a need for health professionals to show compassion during these conversations:

He (GP) gets upset if I'm upset‐ he's a lovely person actually. (P11, female, dementia, current driver)

Some participants expressed a preference for serious discussions about driving to be held in person, and not via teleconferencing:

It's annoying that my doctor (GP) still does that Face Time thing. I'm not happy with that … About driving… I would rather… face‐to‐face. (P5, female, MCI, current driver)

If it was serious about the driving… I wouldn't be doing a teleconference I would want to be down there straight away to see him (GP). (P11, female, dementia, current driver)

When communication was clear and a thorough process (GP information and on‐road assessment), one participant expressed support of the decision to cease driving:

I think at my age … I'm glad that …I've done it because I think that it's probably the right time for me. (P10, female, MCI, stopped driving three weeks prior to interview)

Adjusting to Life Without Driving: Feelings of Loss of Independence and Loneliness

Participants described a phase after driving cessation marked by feelings of loss of independence and loneliness.

One current driver anticipated a loss of independence after driving cessation because his physical limitations may prevent him from accessing public transport:

There's a bus… neither of us can walk overly far, …I have another problem, balance …. (P2, male, MCI, current driver, restricted licence)

Others who had ceased driving or were only driving very short distances reported having access to alternative transportation. However, they felt a loss of independence and loneliness because this option was less convenient and provided less freedom compared to driving:

If I ask to go somewhere… my family will take me. But they are all busy… before I'd drive up to the coffee shop…and meet people… here by myself it gets …lonely. (P4, female, dementia, current driver)

To get to the golf club is… too hard. Can't do it. I can probably afford to do it…. I've got the taxi vouchers; I could do it. I've given up. Isn't that awful? … And that's after… 30 years … they were saying… don't make the decision now. But‐ that's what I did. (P10, female, MCI, stopped driving three weeks prior to interview)

Another participant who had ceased driving reported that although he had access to convenient and easy to use transport, he still felt a sense of loss of independence:

I've (got) my wife, my daughter and a DVA Gold Card… plenty of ways to get from point A to point B, but I feel like my independence has just been…taken away. (P3, male, MCI, stopped driving three months prior to interview)

All participants who had ceased driving described receiving emotional support and/or transport assistance from family:

It was a major drama alright…my wife… took it all in hand and led me by the hand… I used to do all the driving…. Now she takes me everywhere I want to go. (P7, male, MCI, stopped driving two weeks prior to the interview)

However, none of the participants reported receiving any follow‐up communication or support from health professionals.

Discussion

The findings from the current study build on previous research highlighting a desire among people with dementia and MCI and their families for clearer communication from health professionals regarding driving safety and cessation [17, 26]. By providing a phased description of needs derived from the perspectives of people with dementia and MCI, this study contributes a practical framework to guide the design of resources to support health professionals with managing driving cessation.

The current study provides new insights into the views of people with dementia and MCI about decision‐making regarding driving cessation. While previous studies have highlighted the importance that people with dementia and MCI place on retaining autonomy in these decisions [25, 27, 39], participants in this study valued clear explanations and a collaborative approach, seeking involvement in decisions even when recognising that the ultimate responsibility might rest with another person. Notably, many participants indicated a preference for a health professional, and not a family member, to make the final recommendation regarding driving cessation in situations where they were unable to decide themselves. This may reflect a sense of trust in a health professional's judgement and/or a desire to protect family relationships from the emotional burden of the responsibility.

Previous literature describes stages of driving cessation and dementia, which reports early stage involving worried waiting, balancing safety with impending losses, a crisis stage marked by risky driving, adjustment difficulties and relationship conflict, and a post‐cessation stage [20]. This study adds to this knowledge with practical suggestions for health professionals to support adjustment to driving cessation. Interestingly, the experience for drivers and former drivers was varied within each group. Some former drivers described a positive shift in their attitudes towards driving cessation following on road assessments and GP communications, and current drivers' early conversations with a medical practitioner. These discussions, which often involved a recommendation to restrict or limit their driving, appeared to facilitate acceptance and adjustment, supporting the idea that early and ongoing input from health professionals may help to ease the transition to driving cessation for some people. Previous research has highlighted that family members desire early involvement from health professionals [16, 17]; however, few studies have described how people with dementia/MCI perceive and respond to such conversations.

Notably, some participants reported making self‐imposed changes, such as limiting driving to local areas or adopting cautious behaviours like not rushing prior to receiving any input from health professionals. These strategies may reflect proactive attempts initiated by participants or their families to maintain safe driving for longer by avoiding more cognitively demanding driving situations. Although well‐intentioned, self‐regulation may be unreliable, as many people with MCI and dementia overestimate their driving capabilities [40, 41, 42]. As such, self‐imposed regulations may not always align with actual driving capabilities, and health professional input remains important.

The findings from the current study highlight a gap between the expressed needs of people with dementia/MCI and how driving cessation is currently managed in clinical practice. While participants described needs arising across several phases of the driving cessation process, the management of driving concerns in healthcare settings has been described as brief, reactive or missing altogether [21, 22, 23]. Without structured, ongoing support from health professionals, people with dementia/MCI may continue driving without sufficient insight into their cognitive limitations, or the responsibility of decision‐making may shift to family members, who commonly report feeling ill‐equipped to navigate these complex and emotionally charged decisions [17, 21]. Health professionals have identified various barriers to their involvement in managing driving safety concerns, including difficulty having complex conversations with people with cognitive impairment, limited knowledge and confidence in fitness‐to‐drive assessment, concerns about damaging the therapeutic relationship, unclear role boundaries and time pressures [22, 23, 27]. They also report that conversations often do not take place until after a person is diagnosed with MCI or dementia [27], which is concerning given that an estimated 92% of cases of MCI and 62% of cases of dementia remain undetected [43, 44]. System‐level changes are needed to support health professionals with addreessing these barriers to facilitate timely assessment.

Several strategies may be beneficial for supporting health professionals to address the needs identified in this study. One strategy is the use of Advanced Driving Directives, which allow people to document their preferences for driving cessation at an early stage, while they retain cognitive decision‐making capacity, and nominate who they wish to be involved in future decisions [45]. Although Advanced Driving Directives have not yet been tested in clinical trials, a study involving 168 older adult drivers found that most would be willing to complete one if recommended [46].

Another strategy is to use shared decision‐making in clinical discussions. Shared decision‐making emphasises the inclusion of patients in discussing options, expressing values and helping to select the best course of action [47, 48, 49]. Studies from other areas of health care have found that shared decision‐making can improve consumer knowledge, lower decisional conflict and improve satisfaction with decision outcomes [50]. In the context of fitness‐to‐drive assessment, shared decision‐making should be implemented in a way that supports meaningful patient involvement in discussions about driving safety, while recognising that final decisions about fitness‐to‐drive recommendations should ultimately rest with health professionals. Additionally, health professionals can utilise approaches to communicating bad news effectively and empathically [51].

A further strategy is to offer emotional and practical support to people with dementia/MCI after driving cessation. A notable finding from the current study was that, although some participants described receiving support from family members after driving cessation, none mentioned receiving any follow‐up care from health professionals. The lack of support after driving cessation for people with dementia/MCI and their families is a concern that has been highlighted previously [16, 17]. This may reflect a broader issue in dementia care, where healthcare services prioritise risk assessment and safety over ongoing support [52, 53]. Importantly, adjustment to driving cessation is rarely immediate or straightforward [16, 21]. People may initially resist support but later become more receptive, suggesting the need for services that remain accessible beyond the point of cessation from driving. Programs such as CarFreeMe, which are delivered by health professionals to address emotional adjustment and transport planning, could potentially fill this gap [54]. However, more funding is needed to support the integration of these programs across diverse settings, including primary health care and the community sector, particularly in rural and regional areas, where healthcare resources are limited. Exploration of the delivery of these programs by dementia support workers or peer support facilitators is required. A key strength of the present study was that the findings were derived directly from people with dementia and MCI, unlike previous studies, which have combined patient views with those of family members and/or health professionals [20, 26]. However, as participants were recruited from a single memory clinic co‐located with a specialised driving service, their perspectives may not reflect the views of people receiving care in other settings. Further, to ensure diagnostic clarity and exclude other conditions such as stroke or depression, only people with a documented diagnosis of dementia/MCI were recruited, excluding those with undiagnosed cognitive impairment, estimated to comprise over 60% of those with dementia and MCI [43, 44]. Some variation in cognitive status is also possible, given the time elapsed between diagnosis and interview, though inclusion was limited to those with MMSE scores ≥ 20 to minimise variability. Recall bias may have affected the accuracy of participants' accounts, and perceptions of interactions with health professionals may have been influenced by fitness‐to‐drive assessment outcomes, with those recommended to continue driving potentially viewing interactions more positively than those who were advised to retire.

Further limitations of the study need to be highlighted, including that participants were self‐selected, and thus, there was a potential for selection bias. Also of note were the potential differing views from those driving and not driving, or people with MCI compared with dementia. Additionally, all participants reported having family and/or social support; as such, the views of those with dementia/MCI who did not receive such support may not be represented.

Despite these limitations, the findings add knowledge that can guide the development of tools and support for people with dementia and MCI to navigate and adjust to driving cessation [55]. This will support the realisation of the World Health Organization's approach to health ageing and promoting capability throughout ageing, particularly during life transitions, such as driving cessation [56].

Conclusions

The findings in this study highlight the need for communication from health professionals over several identifiable phases leading up to driving cessation and improved post‐cessation support. The findings may guide the design of tools and resources to support health professionals in managing driving safety concerns more effectively with people with dementia/MCI and their families.
