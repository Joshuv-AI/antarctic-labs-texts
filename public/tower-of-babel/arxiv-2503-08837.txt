Particle Systems and McKean–Vlasov Dynamics with Singular Interaction through Local Times


Abstract
We study a system of reflecting Brownian motions on the positive half-line in which each particle has a drift toward the origin determined by the local times at the origin of all the particles. We show that if this local time drift is too strong, such systems can exhibit a breakdown in their solutions in that there is a time beyond which the system cannot be extended. In the finite particle case we give a complete characterization of the finite time breakdown, relying on a novel dynamic graph structure. We consider the mean-field limit of the system in the symmetric case and show that there is a McKean–Vlasov representation. If the drift is too strong, the solution to the corresponding Fokker–Planck equation has a blow up in its solution. We also establish the existence of stationary and self-similar solutions to the McKean–Vlasov equation in the case where there is no breakdown of the system. This work is motivated by models for liquidity in financial markets, the supercooled Stefan problem, and a toy model for cell polarization.

Introduction

In this article, we consider a dynamic model of Brownian particles that diffuse in the half-line
[0,\infty)
and are reflected at the origin. The particles interact through the reflection terms, pushing them closer to the reflecting boundary, leading to a positive feedback loop. If the interaction is too strong, this loop can lead to a breakdown of the system in finite time. We begin by studying networks with finitely many particles and, subsequently, consider an associated mean-field limit.

1.1
Finite Particle System

The
N
particles are labelled
i=1
, …,
N
and the state
X^{i}_{t}
of particle
i
satisfies the reflected SDE

X^{i}_{t}=\xi_{i}+W^{i}_{t}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{ij}L^{j}_{t}+L^{i}_{t}

(1.1)

for nonnegative weights
q_{ij}\geq 0
. The initial conditions
\xi_{1}
, …,
\xi_{N}
are nonnegative random variables,
(W^{1},\dots,W^{N})
is an
N
-dimensional Brownian motion with covariance matrix
A=(a_{ij})_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
and independent of
(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N})
, and the processes
L^{1}
, …,
L^{N}
are reflection terms, which ensure that each
X^{i}_{t}
stays nonnegative. We define the filtration
\mathbb{F}^{N}=(\mathcal{F}^{N}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
by

\mathcal{F}^{N}_{t}=\sigma\Bigl(\xi_{i},W^{i}_{s}\mathpunct{:}s\in[0,t],\,i\in\{1,\dots,N\}\Bigr).

Also, we set
\mathbf{X}=(X^{1},\dots,X^{N})
and
\mathbf{L}=(L^{1},\dots,L^{N})
, and define the
adjacency matrix

Q=(q_{ij})_{ij}
, which allows us to rewrite SDE (
1.1
) in the form

\mathbf{X}_{t}=\mathbf{X}_{0}+\mathbf{W}_{t}+(\mathbb{I}-Q)\mathbf{L}_{t},

(1.2)

where
\mathbb{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
denotes the identity matrix in
N
dimensions

The process
\mathbf{X}
is a
semimartingale reflected Brownian motion
(SRBM), i.e. an obliquely reflected semimartingale process in the first orthant
[0,\infty)^{N}
. Harrison & Reiman
[
18
]
established global well-posedness for SRBMs for adjacency matrices
Q
whose largest real left-eigenvalue
\rho(Q)
is strictly less than one. The objective of this work is to analyse the system when
\rho(Q)\geq 1
. In this so far unexplored case, the system’s behaviour changes radically as it will typically no longer be globally well-posed. Let us illustrate it with a simple example: let
N=2
,
q_{ij}=1-\delta_{ij}
for
i
,
j\in\{1,2\}
, and
A=\mathbf{I}
, so that
W^{1}
and
W^{2}
are independent standard Brownian motions. Suppose that SDE (
1.1
) has a global solution
(X^{1}_{t},X^{2}_{t})
. Then summing
X^{1}_{t}
and
X^{2}_{t}
, we obtain

X^{1}_{t}+X^{2}_{t}=\xi_{1}+W^{1}_{t}+L^{1}_{t}-L^{2}_{t}+\xi_{2}+W^{2}_{t}+L^{2}_{t}-L^{1}_{t}=(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2})+(W^{1}_{t}+W^{2}_{t}).

(1.3)

The left-hand side has to be nonnegative for
t\geq 0
due to the reflection at the origin. However, the right-hand side becomes negative for some sufficiently large
t>0
, resulting in a contradiction. Consequently, a
breakdown
has to occur in finite time, almost surely. The impossibility of solving SDE (
1.1
) globally was already pointed out by Harrison & Reiman at the end of Section 3 in
[
18
]
. However, they do not discuss whether SDE (
1.1
) may still be locally solvable when
\rho(Q)\geq 1
and, if so, what causes the breakdown. We show in this work that local solutions for SDE (
1.1
) indeed exist for
\rho(Q)\geq 1
and provide a precise characterisation of the breakdown time in terms of the number of particles located at the origin. If this number is too large, the feedback
\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{ij}L^{j}_{t}
from the system is equally as strong as or stronger than the individual reflection term
L^{i}_{t}
. Thus, the reflection term can no longer compensate the feedback, and the system breaks down. Accordingly, SDE (
1.1
) will only be understood to hold on some random interval
[0,\tau)
for a stopping time
\tau
with values in
[0,\infty]
.

Before we state a precise notion of solution for SDE (
1.1
), we set up some notation and introduce a dynamic graph structure determined by the interplay of
Q
and
A
, essential to the understanding of the breakdown phenomenon. For a matrix
B=(b_{ij})_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with
d\geq 1
, we let its
spectral radius

\rho(B)
denote the largest real left-eigenvalue of
B
. If
B
has no real left-eigenvalues, we set
\rho(B)=-\infty
. Typically, the matrices under consideration will have nonnegative entries, in which case the Perron–Frobenius theorem implies that
\rho(B)\geq 0
and that there exists a left-eigenvector corresponding to
\rho(B)
with nonnegative entries. Next, for a nonempty subset
I\subset\mathbf{N}:=\{1,\dots,N\}
and
B=(b_{ij})_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, we define the
reduced matrix
or
minor

B[I]=(b_{ij})_{i,j\in I}\in\mathbb{R}^{I\times I}
. If
I=\emptyset
, then
B[I]
is the empty matrix whose spectral radius we set to
-\infty
by convention. Usually, the set
I
will consist of the particles that are currently located at zero and are therefore, in principle, the only ones whose reflection term may increase. Note, however, that due to possible degeneracies of the covariance matrix
A
, even a particle located at the origin need not see any activity in its reflection term. Thus, within a given set
I
, we must further distinguish between active nodes whose reflection term increases and inactive nodes for which this is not the case. This distinction rests on the interaction between the weight matrix
Q
and the covariance matrix
A
, which determines a graph structure that we elaborate next. As before, let
I
be a nonempty subset of
\mathbf{N}
. Then the minor
Q[I]
induces a directed graph
(I,E_{I})
, whose set of edges is given by
E_{I}=\{(i,j)\in I\times I\mathpunct{:}q_{ji}>0\}
. Note the inversion of indices in the definition of
E_{I}
, corresponding to the fact that we study left-eigenvectors of
Q
. We define the set of
active nodes

I^{a}
as those
i\in I
such that there exists a
j\in I
with
a_{jj}>0
and a path
(i_{0},i_{1},\dots,i_{n})
in
E_{I}
from
j
to
i
, i.e.

(i)

i_{k}\in I
for
k=1
, …,
n-1
;

(ii)

i_{0}=j
and
i_{n}=i
;

(iii)

(i_{k},i_{k+1})\in E_{I}
for
k=0
, …,
n-1
.

That is, a node
i\in I
is active if it is reachable in
(I,E_{I})
from a node
j\in I
whose Brownian motion
W^{j}
is nondegenerate. If
I
consists of the particles stuck at zero, then, as we alluded to above, only the active nodes will see their reflection terms increase, since they are the only ones exposed to the fluctuations of a nondegenerate Brownian motion. To the best of our knowledge, this
dynamic
graph structure is a novel tool in the analysis of SRBMs. Lastly, for a nonempty
I\subset\mathbf{N}
, we let
\mathcal{E}(I)
be the set of left-eigenvectors
v\in[0,\infty)^{I}
of
Q[I]
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\rho(Q[I])
. Note that by the Perron–Frobenius theorem, we have
\mathcal{E}(I)\neq\emptyset
.

Next, we recall the classical Skorokhod problem, which underlies reflected SDEs.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\tau\in[0,\infty]
be a stopping time and let
Z=(Z_{t})_{t\in[0,\tau)}
be a càdlàg stochastic process with
Z_{0}\geq 0
. We say that a tuple of càdlàg stochastic processes
(X,L)=(X_{t},L_{t})_{t\in[0,\tau)}
solves the (one-dimensional)
Skorokhod problem
for
Z
if
L
is a nondecreasing process started from zero and for all
t\in[0,\tau)
we have

(i)

X_{t}=Z_{t}+L_{t}\geq 0
;

(ii)

\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{s}>0\}}\,\mathrm{d}L_{s}=0
.

In this simple one-dimensional setting, the Skorokhod problem for a càdlàg stochastic process
Z
with
Z_{0}\geq 0
has a unique solution
(X,L)
given by
L_{t}=\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}(Z_{s})_{-}
and
X_{t}=Z_{t}+L_{t}
for
t\in[0,\tau)
.

Definition 1.2
.

A pair
(\mathbf{L},\tau)
consisting of an
N
-dimensional continuous
\mathbb{F}^{N}
-adapted stochastic process
\mathbf{L}=(L^{1},\dots,L^{N})
and an
\mathbb{F}^{N}
-stopping time
\tau
form a solution of SDE (
1.1
) if

(i)

the solution
(X^{i},F^{i})
to the Skorokhod problem for
\xi_{i}+W^{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{ij}L^{j}
on
[0,\tau)
satisfies
F^{i}_{t}=L^{i}_{t}
for
t\in[0,\tau)
and all
i\in\mathbf{N}
;

(ii)

we have
L^{i}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{i\in I^{a}_{s}\}}\,\mathrm{d}L^{i}_{s}
for
t\in[0,\tau)
and
i\in\mathbf{N}
,

where
I_{t}=\{i\in\mathbf{N}\mathpunct{:}X^{i}_{t}=0\}
for
t\in[0,\tau)
and
I_{t}^{a}
denotes the associated active nodes. We write
Q_{t}=Q[I^{a}_{t}]
for the matrix of
active weights
and refer to
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{L})
, with
\mathbf{X}=(X^{1},\dots,X^{N})
, as the solution to the Skorokhod problem associated to
(\mathbf{L},\tau)
.

A solution
(\mathbf{L},\tau)
to SDE (
1.1
) is called
maximal
if for any other solution
(\mathbf{L}^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})
with
\mathbf{L}^{\prime}_{t}=\mathbf{L}_{t}
for
t\in[0,\tau^{\prime}\land\tau)
, it holds that
\tau\geq\tau^{\prime}
.

Remark 1.3
.

The minimality property
(ii)
ensures that only active nodes see their reflection term increase. If this property were not imposed, there could be feedback loops between inactive nodes that lead to an artificial increase in the reflection terms. As an example consider a system with
N=2
particles, initial conditions
\xi_{1}=\xi_{2}=0
, (degenerate) Brownian motions
W^{1}=W^{2}=0
and weights
q_{ij}=1-\delta_{ij}
for
i
,
j\in\{1,2\}
. Then, for any continuous nondecreasing
\mathbb{F}^{N}
-adapted process
F
, the pair
(\mathbf{L},\infty)
with
L^{1}_{t}=L^{2}_{t}=F_{t}
satisfies
(i)
. However, since
\mathbf{W}
vanishes, both nodes are inactive, so the only pair
(\mathbf{L},\infty)
meeting the minimality property
(ii)
is given by
L^{1}=L^{2}=0
. In particular, the minimality condition restores uniqueness. Note that
(ii)
is trivially satisfied if
\rho(Q)<1
, since these types of feedback loops can only occur when
Q
admits a minor whose spectral radius is equal to one.

Note that even though
\mathbf{L}
is only defined on the interval
[0,\tau)
, the first component
\mathbf{X}=(X^{1},\dots,X^{N})
of the solution to the Skorokhod problem associated to
(\mathbf{L},\tau)
can be extended to a continuous function on
C([0,\tau[)
, where for
T\in[0,\infty]
, the interval
[0,T[
is defined by
[0,T]
if
T<\infty
and
[0,T)
otherwise. This can be seen by applying Itô’s formula to
\lvert X^{i}_{t}\rvert^{2}
and inspecting the resulting expression. In particular, the index set
I_{\tau}=\{i\in\mathbf{N}\mathpunct{:}X^{i}_{\tau}=0\}
, the set of active nodes
I_{\tau}^{a}
, and the matrix
Q_{\tau}=Q[I_{\tau}^{a}]
are all well-defined.

Theorem 1.4
.

SDE (
1.1
) has a unique maximal solution
(\mathbf{L},\tau_{N})
. Moreover,

(i)

\rho(Q_{t})<1
for
t\in[0,\tau_{N})
a.s. and
\rho(Q_{\tau_{N}})\geq 1
if
\tau_{N}<\infty
;

(ii)

\tau_{N}>0
if and only if
\rho(Q_{0})<1
;

(iii)

\tau_{N}<\infty
if and only if one of the following holds

(a)

\rho(Q[\mathbf{N}^{a}])>1
;

(b)

\rho(Q[\mathbf{N}^{a}])=1
and for all
I\subset\mathbf{N}
with
\rho(Q[I^{a}])=1
, there exists
v\in\mathcal{E}(I^{a})
such that
v^{\top}A[I^{a}]v>0
;

(c)

\rho(Q_{0})=1
.

The above implications and equivalences are understood in a probabilistic sense. For instance, by
\rho(Q_{\tau_{N}})\geq 1
if
\tau_{N}<\infty
we mean
\mathbb{P}(\rho(Q_{\tau_{N}})<1,\,\tau_{N}<\infty)=0
. We will refer to the unique maximal solution of SDE (
1.1
) simply as the unique solution.

Remark 1.5
.

Note that Theorem
1.4
does not clarify whether some of the components of
\mathbf{L}
explode to infinity as
t\nearrow\tau_{N}
. While we do not expect this to be the case, we have not been able to prove that
\mathbf{L}
stays bounded as
t\nearrow\tau_{N}
, except in special cases.

Remark 1.6
.

According to the behaviour of SDE (
1.1
) described by Theorem
1.4

(iii)
, we can distinguish between three regimes: the subcritical regime,
\rho(Q[\mathbf{N}^{a}])<1
, in which SDE (
1.1
) is globally well-posed, the supercritical regime
\rho(Q[\mathbf{N}^{a}])>1
, in which SDE (
1.1
) breaks down in finite time, and the critical regime
\rho(Q[\mathbf{N}^{a}])=1
, in which the behaviour of the SDE is contingent on the interaction with the covariance matrix
A
and the initial condition. Let us further illuminate the critical case with the aid of the previous example, where
N=2
and
q_{ij}=1-\delta_{ij}
for
i
,
j\in\{1,2\}
. In the case that
W^{1}
and
W^{2}
are perfectly anticorrelated standard Brownian motions, i.e.
W^{1}=-W^{2}
, the system’s mean is preserved, since

X^{1}_{t}+X^{2}_{t}=\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}.

Consequently, unless the system is started from the origin, meaning
\xi_{1}=\xi_{2}=0
, the two particles will never be located at zero simultaneously, leading to global-in-time existence, consistent with Theorem
1.4
. Indeed, the only subset
I
of
\mathbf{N}=\{1,2\}
for which
\rho(Q[I])=1
is
\mathbf{N}
itself. But the only eigenvector (up to scalar multiples) of
Q
corresponding to
\rho(Q)=1
is
v=(1,1)
, for which we have
v^{\top}Av=0
. Hence, condition
(iii)
(iii)(b)
of Theorem
1.4
is violated, allowing the system to exist for all times. Only if
\xi_{1}=\xi_{2}=0
, so that Theorem
1.4

(iii)
(iii)(c)
holds, does the system break down. Indeed, by Theorem
1.4

(ii)
, the breakdown is immediate.

As we saw earlier (cf. the discussion below (
1.3
)), a breakdown occurs in this two-dimensional example regardless of the initial condition, if
W^{1}
and
W^{2}
are independent standard Brownian motions, so that
A=\mathbb{I}
. Then
v^{\top}Av=2
, so Theorem
1.4

(iii)
(iii)(b)
implies a breakdown in finite time. While the fact, that a breakdown occurs in finite time in the critical regime if the covariance matrix is “sufficiently” nondegenerate, generalises to finite systems of any size, a phase transition occurs once we pass to the mean-field limit as
N\to\infty
. We will hint at this phenomenon in the following remark and further discuss it in Remark
1.11
below, after formally introducing the mean-field limit in Subsection
1.2
.

Remark 1.7
.

SDE (
1.1
) can be interpreted as a model for resource sharing. The reflection term
L^{i}_{t}
represents the resources particle
i
is provided with by the system, while
\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{ij}L^{j}_{t}
are the resources the particle distributes to others. If no resources are exogenously provided to or withdrawn from the system, it must hold that
L^{i}_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{ji}L^{i}_{t}
. This is guaranteed if
\mathbf{1}^{\top}Q=\mathbf{1}^{\top}
, i.e. the vector
\mathbf{1}=(1,\dots,1)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a left-eigenvector of
Q
with corresponding eigenvalue
1
. Then, by Theorem
1.4
, the system breaks down in finite time if
\mathbf{1}^{\top}A\mathbf{1}>0
. In fact, summing up SDE (
1.1
) over
i=1
, …,
N
, and using that
\mathbf{1}^{\top}Q=\mathbf{1}^{\top}
, we see that

\sum_{i=1}^{N}X^{i}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}W^{i}_{t}=\zeta+\sigma B_{t},

where
\zeta=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi_{i}
,
B
is a Brownian motion, and
\sigma^{2}=\mathbf{1}^{\top}A\mathbf{1}
. Thus, the breakdown has to occur before or at the hitting time
\varrho=\inf\{t>0\mathpunct{:}\sigma B_{t}\leq-\zeta\}
, which is finite as soon as
\mathbf{1}^{\top}A\mathbf{1}>0
or
\zeta=0
. If we wish to ensure that the system survives for as long as possible, namely up to the hitting time
\varrho
itself, we require that
\rho(Q[I])<1
for all
I\subset\mathbf{N}
with
\#I<N
. This is, for instance, satisfied for the choice
q_{ij}=1/N
. On the other extreme, if
Q
is the identity matrix in
N
dimensions, then SDE (
1.1
) reduces to
X^{i}_{t}=\xi_{i}+W^{i}_{t}
and
\tau_{N}
occurs as soon as one of the particles hits the origin. The resource sharing agreement
q_{ij}=1/N
leads to diversification that allows the system to remain alive longer. Note that by the reflection principle

\mathbb{P}(\varrho\leq t)=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{P}(\varrho\leq t|\zeta)]=2\mathbb{E}\biggl[1-\Phi\biggl(\frac{\zeta}{\sigma\sqrt{t}}\biggr)\biggr]

for
t>0
, where
\Phi
denotes the cumulative distribution function of a standard normal random variable. In particular, if the initial conditions are i.i.d. with positive mean and the covariance matrix
A
is the identity matrix, then
\frac{\zeta}{N}\to\mathbb{E}[\xi_{1}]
as
N\to\infty
and
\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{N}}=1
, so that

\frac{\zeta}{\sigma\sqrt{t}}=\sqrt{N}\frac{\zeta}{N}\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\sigma\sqrt{t}}\to\infty

as
N\to\infty
. Consequently, we have
\mathbb{P}(\varrho\leq t)\to 0
as
N\to\infty
. In the limit, the diversification prevents breakdowns altogether.

1.2
Mean-Field Limit

Next, we will consider a mean-field limit corresponding to SDE (
1.1
) in the symmetric case, where the matrix
Q
is given by
Q_{ij}=\frac{\alpha}{N}
,
i
,
j\in\mathbf{N}
, for an interaction strength
\alpha\geq 0
, the initial conditions
\xi_{1}
, …,
\xi_{N}
are independent and identically distributed, and
A=\mathbb{I}
, so
\mathbf{W}
is a standard
N
-dimensional Brownian motion. Then SDE (
1.1
) reads

X^{i}_{t}=\xi_{i}+W^{i}_{t}-\alpha\bar{L}^{N}_{t}+L^{i}_{t},

(1.4)

where
\bar{L}^{N}_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}L^{i}_{t}
is the empirical average of the particles’ reflection terms. Moreover, it holds that
I=I^{a}
and
\rho(Q[I])=\alpha\#I/N
for any nonempty
I\subset\mathbf{N}
, so
\rho(Q_{t})=\alpha\#I_{t}/N
. Hence, by Theorem
1.4
, we have
\#I_{t}<N/\alpha
for
t\in[0,\tau_{N})
and the system breaks down as soon as at least
N/\alpha
particles are simultaneously at the origin.

As the number of particles tends to infinity, we may expect that
\bar{L}^{N}
tends to a theoretical mean
\mathbb{E}L_{t}
, for the reflection term
L_{t}
of a representative particle system in the mean-field limit. The dynamics of the representative particle are given by the reflected McKean–Vlasov SDE

X_{t}=\xi+W_{t}-\alpha\ell_{t}+L_{t}

(1.5)

where
L
is a reflection process, keeping
X_{t}
nonnegative, and the interaction term is given by
\ell_{t}=\mathbb{E}L_{t}
. We assume that the initial condition
\xi
has the same law as
\xi_{1}
and is thus, in particular, nonnegative. As for SDE (
1.1
), we can distinguish between three regimes: the subcritical (
\alpha<1
), the critical (
\alpha=1
), and the supercritical (
\alpha>1
). These map onto the corresponding regimes for SDE (
1.1
) described in Remark
1.6
, since by the above
\rho(Q)=\alpha
if
q_{ij}=\frac{\alpha}{N}
.

Definition 1.8
.

A pair
(\ell,T)\in D[0,T)\times[0,\infty]
is a solution to McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
) if
(X,L)
solves the Skorokhod problem for
\xi+W-\alpha\ell
and
\ell_{t}=\mathbb{E}L_{t}
for
t\in[0,T)
. We refer to
(X,L)
as the solution to the Skorokhod problem associated to
(\ell,T)
.

A solution
(\ell,T)
to McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
) is called
maximal
if for any other solution
(\ell^{\prime},T^{\prime})
with
\ell^{\prime}_{t}=\ell_{t}
for
t\in[0,T^{\prime}\land T)
, we have
T\geq T^{\prime}
.

Remark 1.9
.

The solution
\ell
to McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
) is assumed to be an element of the space of càdlàg paths because, a priori, we do not want to restrict ourselves to continuous functions. However, as Lemma
3.2
shows, solutions are necessarily continuous.

Note further that by our remark below Definition
1.1
, we have
(\ell,T)\in D[0,T)\times[0,\infty]
solves McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
) if and only if
\ell_{t}=\mathbb{E}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}(\xi+W_{s}-\alpha\ell_{s})_{-}
for
t\in[0,T)
.

Our reason for including the potentially finite time horizon
T\in[0,\infty]
in our notion of solution is that, similarly to the finite system, a finite-time breakdown of McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
) is unavoidable in the supercritical case
\alpha>1
. This can be seen in an elementary fashion.

Lemma 1.10
.

Assume that
\alpha>1
and let
(\ell,T)
be a solution to SDE (
1.5
). Then
T<\infty
.

Proof.

Assume that
T=\infty
. Let
K>0
be large enough such that
\mathbb{P}(\xi\leq K)>\frac{1}{\alpha}
. Then multiplying Equation (
1.5
) by
\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi\leq K\}}
and taking expectations yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi\leq K\}}X_{t}]

1.4
A Model for Liquidity in Financial Systems

One interpretation of the finite system, SDE (
1.1
), and its mean-field limit (
1.5
) is as a toy model for liquidity in financial systems. The particles represent banks (or other liquidity-sensitive financial institutions, such as hedge funds). Each bank’s liquidity position
X^{i}_{t}
is composed of an initial position
\xi_{i}
, ordinary liquidity flows
W^{i}_{t}
arising from day-to-day business activities, liquidity-raising actions
L^{i}_{t}
such as fire sales and liquidity hoarding, and the systemic impact
-\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{ij}L^{j}_{t}
caused by the liquidity-raising efforts of other institutions. The weights
q_{ij}
quantify the banks’ exposure to the activities of others. The presence of the systemic impact is best motivated through a simple example: suppose that bank
i
sells assets to improve its liquidity position. If the institution finds itself in a distressed situation and has to liquidate quickly, it will generate price impact, pushing down the market price of the asset. These mark-to-market losses will propagate to other institutions holding the same asset, reducing their potential to raise liquidity, which feeds into the systemic impact. Additionally, the party that bank
i
is trading with (not necessarily a bank itself), will have to finance the asset purchases with money deposited in an account at one of the institutions
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
(which need not be different from
i
). Thus, cash will flow from the account the trading partner holds with bank
j
to bank
i
, corresponding to a liquidity outflow from
j
to
i
. Both these effects (among others) account for the systemic impact
-\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{ij}L^{j}_{t}
. Note that because the actions
L^{i}_{t}
are modelled as reflections at the origin, we assume that a bank only raises liquidity if all its resources are exhausted. Exploring strategically chosen actions and the resulting game formulation is an exciting avenue for future research.

Since the liquidity raised by the individual banks is withdrawn from the remaining banking system and since there may be additional externalities such as price impact, the spectral radius
\rho(Q)
is at least one. Consequently, our model gives rise to the sudden formation of liquidity stresses observed during financial crises, manifesting as a breakdown of the system as discussed in Theorems
1.4
and
1.12
. Assume for instance that the systemic impact is uniform, i.e.
q_{ij}=\frac{\alpha}{N}
, so that
Q[I]=Q[I^{a}]=(\frac{\alpha}{N})_{i,j\in I}
and
\rho(Q[I])=\frac{\alpha\#I}{N}
. Then, by Theorem
1.4
, a breakdown occurs the first time that

\frac{\alpha\#I_{t}}{N}=\rho(Q_{t})\geq 1,

meaning that at least
\frac{N}{\alpha}
banks’ liquidity reserve is depleted. In the financially relevant case
\alpha\geq 1
, this situation is inevitable (if
\alpha=1
, some mild assumption on
A
has to be satisfied, cf. Theorem
1.4

(iii)
(iii)(b)
). In other words, if too many banks have to raise liquidity at the same time, the financial system is overwhelmed and a breakdown occurs. In the critical case
\alpha=1
, Remark
1.7
even provides asymptotics for the timing of the breakdown.

1.5.2
Supercooled Stefan problem

Calvez et al.
[
4
]
highlight another related model, the supercooled Stefan problem, describing the movement of the ice-water interface for a supercooled liquid in one dimension. Let
\mathcal{C}_{0}
denote the space of
\varphi\in C^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\varphi(0)=0
and define the cumulative distribution function (CDF)
F_{t}(x)=\mu_{t}([0,x])
for the solution
(\mu,T_{0})
of PDE (
1.11
). Then, repeatedly applying integration by parts, one finds

\mathrm{d}\langle F_{t},\varphi\rangle=-\alpha\dot{\ell}_{t}\langle F_{t},\partial_{x}\varphi\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{2}\langle F_{t},\partial_{x}^{2}\varphi\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t

(1.14)

for
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
. In fact, since
F_{t}\in W^{2,1}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
, we can write this in the strong form

\partial_{t}F_{t}(x)=\frac{\alpha\partial_{x}F_{t}(0)}{2}\partial_{x}F_{t}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}F_{t}(x)

(1.15)

for a.e.
(t,x)\in(0,T_{0})\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, with boundary condition
F_{t}(0)=0
. That is, PDE (
1.15
) has an absorbing boundary at the origin and
\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}F_{t}(0)
measures the flux across this boundary. Note that the initial condition
F_{0}(x)=\mu_{0}([0,x])
for this PDE is not a probability measure, but a cumulative distribution function, so it is not integrable.

The PDE (
1.15
) is the supercooled Stefan problem, which received renewed interest in recent years, due to a novel probabilistic formulation through a McKean–Vlasov SDE with singular interaction through hitting times
[
10
,
9
,
25
,
26
,
15
]
. The probabilistic representation allows for a continuation of the supercooled Stefan problem (with a probability measure as initial data) after the occurrence of a blow-up in the PDE formulation. These blow-ups lead to a sudden downward shift in the solution profile. To make this more precise, let us assume for the moment that we started PDE (
1.15
) from an initial probability density
f
, so that
F_{t}
is integrable. If too much mass builds up near the boundary at some time
t\geq 0
, a jump
\Delta\ell_{t}
may appear in the trajectories of the cumulative flux
\ell
. This instantaneously displaces the solution profile according to the rule
F_{t}(x)=F_{t-}(x+\alpha\Delta\ell_{t})
. In particular, the mass
\int_{0}^{\alpha\Delta\ell_{t}}F_{t-}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
that drops below zero gets absorbed. Since
\ell
measures the flux across the boundary, it must hold that

\Delta\ell_{t}=\int_{0}^{\alpha\Delta\ell_{t}}F_{t-}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

However, not all possible solutions of this equation yield admissible jump sizes, that allow for the continuation of the system after the jump without the immediate occurrence of another jump. To ensure that no jump can occur immediately after the restart, the amount of mass near the origin after the initial jump has to be sufficiently small. The smallest jump size for which this is guaranteed, is provided by the so called physical jump condition

\Delta\ell_{t}=\inf\biggl\{\delta>0\mathpunct{:}\int_{0}^{\alpha\delta}F_{t-}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x<\delta\biggr\}.

If
F_{t-}
is integrable, then the right-hand side is finite, and after resolving the jump, the system can be continued. In contrast, if
F_{t-}
is a cumulative distribution function, as is the case for us, then the only positive solution, if it exists, is infinity. Hence, any jump will eliminate the entire mass. Consequently, unlike for integrable initial data, the system cannot be extended beyond a jump, consistent with the findings from Theorem
1.12
.

Proposition
1.13
rules out the existence of atoms at the origin in the law
\mu_{t}
before the breakdown time
T
, implying that
F_{t}(0)=0
for the solution of the supercooled Stefan problem (
1.15
) with CDF-valued initial data. A similar result may hold for initial probability densities, suggesting a potential refinement of the description of the supercooled Stefan problem by Delarue, Nadtochiy & Shkolnikov
[
11
, Theorem 1.1]
, ruling out the possibility that
F_{t}(0)\in(0,1/\alpha)
.

1.5.3
Additional Related Literature

Semimartingale reflected Brownian motion in the orthant arises in the study of heavy-traffic limits for queuing systems, and we refer the reader to the survey paper
[

which we already discussed at the beginning of the introduction. They study nonnegative interaction matrices

(replacing

has a spectral radius less than one. Beyond that, it allows for interaction matrices

with negative entries, which are outside the scope of our work. It would be interesting to investigate whether by combining the insights of Taylor & Williams with ours, it is possible to arrive at a general theory of SRBM with an arbitrary reflection matrix. We leave this problem for future research.

Note that
[
29
]
insists on the nondegeneracy of the covariance matrix

of the
N

given by

with

). In particular, if the Brownian motions of the two particles are perfectly negatively correlated, the particles will not be at the origin simultaneously, while this situation will occur for independent Brownian motions. The dynamic graph structure we introduce in this work may be useful in pushing this analysis beyond the planar setting.

The mean-field limit (

) is an instance of a McKean–Vlasov SDE with interaction through a boundary condition, in this case a reflecting boundary. Other boundary interaction have been previously studied, with most attention being dedicated to the absorbing boundary. The latter leads to a McKean–Vlasov SDE with singular interaction through hitting times. Here, particles are killed as soon as they hit the origin and the interaction occurs through the CDF
\mathbb{P}(\tau\leq t)
of the hitting time

in (

26
]
as well as Hambly, Ledger & Søjmark

]

,
2

[

closely resembles the condition from

guaranteeing the presence of blow ups in a networked version of McKean–Vlasov SDEs with singular interaction through hitting times. In
[
26

several populations of particles interact through hitting time distributions mediated by an interaction matrix. If this matrix has a strictly positive

Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue, a blow up occurs.

As discussed in Section

, McKean–Vlasov SDEs with singular interaction through hitting times provide a probabilistic representation of the supercooled Stefan problem. In particular, the CDF
\mathbb{P}(\tau\leq t)
corresponds to the cumulative flux across the absorbing boundary, which exhibits jump discontinuities. This feature has made this class of McKean–Vlasov SDEs popular in the modelling of solvency contagion in banking networks

corresponds to the capital of a bank. As such, interaction through hitting times permits the modelling of solvency contagion as opposed to systemic liquidity.

Let us also briefly mention the work by Baker & Shkolnikov
[
1
]
as well as Hambly & Meier
[
16
]
on singular interactions through an elastic boundary condition. This boundary type can be viewed as an interpolation between the absorbing and reflecting regime. Indeed, the latter two arise in limit regimes of the elasticity parameter (cf.
[

Lastly, we highlight the McKean–Vlasov SDE with reflection studied by Coghi et al.
[
6
]
. The domain of this diffusion equation is the unit interval
[0,1]
with reflection at the two boundary points
0
and

. In addition, the system has a prescribed mean enforced by a singular interaction term in the dynamics of the representative particle. This interaction term depends on the average of the reflection term, placing the system in the critical regime. However, breakdowns for the particle system (and less surprisingly the mean-field limit) are avoided due to the two-sided nature of the reflection and the mean-constraint.

\displaystyle\leq\lambda d_{w}(f^{1},f^{2}).

Since
\lambda<1
, the map
\Lambda
is a contraction on
\mathcal{I}^{N}_{c}[0,T]
with respect to the metric

, so the desired fixed point exists. The measurable dependence on

and

is clear, so the proof is complete.
∎

The last step before we can finish the proof of Theorem

is to establish uniqueness for SDE (

).

Lemma 2.4
.

For any two solutions
(\mathbf{L},\tau)
and

of SDE (

) it holds that
\mathbf{L}_{t}=\mathbf{L}^{\prime}_{t}
on

Proof.

Set
\tau_{\ast}=\inf\{t\in[0,\tau^{\prime}\land\tau)\mathpunct{:}\mathbf{L}_{t}\neq\mathbf{L}^{\prime}_{t}\}\land(\tau\land\tau^{\prime})
. If
\tau_{\ast}=\tau\land\tau^{\prime}
, we are done, so assume that
\tau_{\ast}<\tau\land\tau^{\prime}
with positive probability. By Lemma
2.2
, on

\rho(Q[I_{\ast}])
and

are smaller than

and

. Hence, proceeding as in the proof of Proposition

, we can show that on

and

∎

3
The Mean-Field Limit

In this section, we prove existence and uniqueness for the mean-field limit (Theorem
1.12
), establish propagation of chaos for the particle system (Theorem
1.14
), and discuss the long-term behaviour of the mean-field limit (Theorem
1.16
).

3.1
Existence and Uniqueness for the Mean-Field Limit

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.12
, which establishes existence and uniqueness for McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
) and characterises the breakdown behaviour of the maximal solution in the supercritical case
\alpha>1
. The strategy resembles that used for the finite system in Section
2
.

In the subcritical and the critical case the maximal solution does not break down (other than for
\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)=1
in the critical case), hence we will omit
T
from the solution pair whenever convenient. Moreover, we will often omit the term maximal when referring to the unique maximal solution of McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
) and simply say the unique solution.

We begin by proving that all solutions to McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
) are continuous. For that, we need the following lemma, which gives an upper bound on the mass accumulated at zero.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(\ell,T)
be a solution to McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
). Then
\mathbb{P}(X_{t}=0)<\frac{1}{\alpha}
for all
t\in[0,T)
.

Proof.

Towards a contradiction, let
t_{0}\in[0,T)
such that
X_{t_{0}}=0
with at least probability
\frac{1}{\alpha}
. We may assume without loss of generality that
t_{0}=0
, otherwise we simply consider a system started from
X_{t_{0}}
. Then for
t>0
,

\ell_{t}=\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)\mathbb{E}[L_{t}|\xi=0]+\mathbb{P}(\xi>0)\mathbb{E}[L_{t}|\xi>0]>\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}[L_{t}|\xi=0].

In the inequality, we used that either
\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)>\frac{1}{\alpha}
or
\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)=\frac{1}{\alpha}
and
\mathbb{E}[L_{t}|\xi>0]>0
. Thus,

X_{t}=\xi+W_{t}-\alpha\ell_{t}+L_{t}<\xi+W_{t}-\mathbb{E}[L_{t}|\xi=0]+L_{t}.

Taking conditional expectations with respect to
\xi=0
on both sides above implies
\mathbb{E}[X_{t}|\xi=0]<0
, which violates the nonnegativity of
X
.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
(\ell,T)
be a solution to McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
). Then
\ell
is continuous on
[0,T)
.

Proof.

Again arguing by contradiction, suppose that
\ell
has a jump at
t_{0}\in[0,T)
. Then we must have

X_{t_{0}}-X_{t_{0}-}=\Delta X_{t_{0}}=-\alpha\Delta\ell_{t_{0}}+\Delta L_{t_{0}},

which implies
X_{t_{0}-}-\alpha\Delta\ell_{t_{0}}=X_{t_{0}}-\Delta L_{t_{0}}
. We take the negative part on both sides to obtain
(X_{t_{0}-}-\alpha\Delta\ell_{t_{0}})_{-}=(X_{t_{0}}-\Delta L_{t_{0}})_{-}
. Since
X_{t_{0}}\geq 0
, the right-hand side can only be negative if
\Delta L_{t_{0}}
is positive, in which case
X_{t_{0}}=0
. Consequently, we see that
(X_{t_{0}-}-\alpha\Delta\ell_{t_{0}})_{-}=\Delta L_{t_{0}}
, which finally implies the jump condition

\Delta\ell_{t_{0}}=\mathbb{E}(X_{t_{0}-}-\alpha\Delta\ell_{t_{0}})_{-}.

(3.1)

Zero is always an admissible jump size. However, since we assume that
\Delta\ell_{t_{0}}>0
, we can divide through by the quantity
\Delta\ell_{t_{0}}
in the jump condition, whereby
\mathbb{E}(X_{t_{0}-}/\Delta\ell_{t_{0}}-\alpha)_{-}=1
. Let us introduce the function
J\mathpunct{:}[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
defined by
J(\delta)=\mathbb{E}(X_{t_{0}-}/\delta-\alpha)_{-}
. Clearly, we have
J(\Delta\ell_{t_{0}})=1
, which prompts us to search for solutions of the equation
J(\delta)=1
. Firstly, note that
(X_{t_{0}-}/\delta-\alpha)_{-}\to\alpha
a.s. as
\delta\to\infty
, so
J(\delta)\to\alpha>1
as
\delta
tends to
\infty
by the dominated convergence theorem. Secondly, on
X_{t_{0}-}>0
, we have
X_{t_{0}-}/\delta\to\infty
as
\delta\to 0
, while
X_{t_{0}-}/\delta=0
on
X_{t_{0}-}=0
. Consequently,

\mathbb{E}(X_{t_{0}-}/\delta-\alpha)_{-}=\alpha\mathbb{P}(X_{t_{0}-}=0)+\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{X_{t_{0}-}>0}(X_{t_{0}-}/\delta-\alpha)_{-}]\to\alpha\mathbb{P}(X_{t_{0}-}=0)

as
\delta\to 0
. As
t_{0}<T
, we know from Lemma
3.1
that
\mathbb{P}(X_{t_{0}-}=0)<\frac{1}{\alpha}
, which implies that
J(\delta)\to\alpha\mathbb{P}(X_{t_{0}-}=0)<1
as
\delta\to 0
. Since
J
is strictly increasing, this means there exists a unique
\delta_{0}\in(0,\infty)
with
J(\delta_{0})=1
and it follows that
\delta_{0}=\Delta\ell_{t_{0}-}
.

Our goal is now to show that
\mathbb{P}(X_{t_{0}}=0)\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}
, which in view of Lemma
3.1
implies that
t_{0}\geq T
, which is the desired contradiction. If
\mathbb{P}(X_{t_{0}}=0)<\frac{1}{\alpha}
, then using the same argument as above, we find
\delta_{1}\in(0,\infty)
for which
\mathbb{E}(X_{t_{0}}/\delta_{1}-\alpha)_{-}=1
. However, then

\displaystyle\Delta L_{t_{0}}+(X_{t_{0}}-\alpha\delta_{1})_{-}

\displaystyle=(-\Delta L_{t_{0}})_{-}+(X_{t_{0}-}+\Delta L_{t_{0}}-\alpha\delta_{0}-\alpha\delta_{1})_{-}

\displaystyle\geq(X_{t_{0}-}-\alpha(\delta_{0}+\delta_{1}))_{-},

where the last inequality follows from the subadditivity of the negative part. Taking expectations on both sides above and dividing by
\delta_{0}+\delta_{1}
yields
1\geq J(\delta_{0}+\delta_{1})
. In view of the strict monotonicity of
J
, we arrive at the contradiction
1=J(\delta_{0})<J(\delta_{0}+\delta_{1})\leq 1
.
∎

The proof of Proposition
3.2
shows that if a solution
(\ell,T)
to SDE (
1.5
) has a jump, then it terminates immediately afterwards as it arrives at an inadmissible state, where at least
\frac{1}{\alpha}
of the mass is concentrated at the origin.

Let us now show that solutions are guaranteed to exist for small time as long as
\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)<\frac{1}{\alpha}
. This requirement is automatically satisfied in the subcritical case
\alpha<1
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Assume that
\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)<\frac{1}{\alpha}
. Then there exists a solution
(\ell,T)
to McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
) with
T>0
.

Proof.

For
t\in[0,\infty)
, let
\mathcal{I}_{c}[0,t]
denote the space of nondecreasing continuous paths
[0,t]\to[0,\infty)
started from zero. Fix
T>0
to be determined later and let us introduce the map
\Lambda\mathpunct{:}\mathcal{I}_{c}[0,T]\to\mathcal{I}_{c}[0,T]
defined by

\Lambda_{t}(f)=\mathbb{E}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}(\xi+W_{s}-\alpha f_{s})_{-}

for
t\in[0,T]
and
f\in\mathcal{I}_{c}[0,T]
. Since
\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)<\frac{1}{\alpha}
, we can find
\epsilon>0
such that
\mathbb{P}(\xi-Z_{t}\leq\epsilon)<\frac{1}{\beta}
for all
t\in[0,\epsilon]
and some
\beta>\alpha
, where
Z_{t}=\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}(-W_{s})
and we have used the fact that
Z_{t}
is nondecreasing. Now, we claim that we can choose
T\in[0,\epsilon]
, such that if
f\in\mathcal{I}_{c}[0,T]
with
\alpha f_{t}\leq\epsilon
for
t\in[0,T]
, then
\alpha\Lambda_{t}(f)\leq\epsilon
. Indeed, we estimate for
t\in[0,T]
with
\alpha f_{t}\leq\epsilon
,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{t}(f)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}(\xi-Z_{t}-\alpha f_{t})_{-}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\biggl[\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi-Z_{t}\leq\epsilon\}}(\xi-Z_{t}-\alpha f_{t})_{-}\biggr]+\mathbb{E}\biggl[\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi-Z_{t}>\epsilon\}}(\xi-Z_{t}-\alpha f_{t})_{-}\biggr]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\biggl[\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi-Z_{t}\leq\epsilon\}}(Z_{t}+\alpha f_{t})\biggr]+\mathbb{E}\biggl[\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi-Z_{t}>\epsilon\}}(\xi-Z_{t}-\epsilon)_{-}\biggr]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[Z_{t}]+\alpha f_{t}\mathbb{P}(\xi-Z_{t}\leq\epsilon)

\displaystyle\leq c\sqrt{t}+\frac{\epsilon}{\beta},

(3.2)

where
c=(\frac{2}{\pi})^{1/2}
and we used in the fourth inequality that
(\xi-Z_{t}-\epsilon)_{-}=0
on
\{\xi-Z_{t}>\epsilon\}
. Thus, in order to ensure that
\alpha\Lambda_{t}(\ell)\leq\epsilon
, it is enough to guarantee that
\alpha c\sqrt{t}+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\epsilon<\epsilon
. This holds whenever
t\in[0,T]
with
T\leq(\frac{\beta-\alpha}{\alpha\beta}\frac{\epsilon}{c})^{2}
. Since we need in addition that
T\leq\epsilon
, we can set
T=(\frac{\beta-\alpha}{\alpha\beta}\frac{\epsilon}{c})^{2}\land\epsilon
.

Next, we want to show that the map
\Lambda
is a contraction mapping on the set of all
f\in\mathcal{I}_{c}[0,T]
such that
f_{t}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{\alpha}
for
t\in[0,T]
. We equip this space with the distance
(f^{1},f^{2})\mapsto\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\lvert f^{1}_{t}-f^{2}_{t}\rvert
, which turns it into a complete metric space. Let
f^{1}
,
f^{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{c}[0,T]
be uniformly bounded by
\frac{\epsilon}{\alpha}
. Then, similarly to (
3.1
), we estimate

\displaystyle\bigl\lvert\Lambda_{t}(f^{1})-\Lambda_{t}(f^{2})\bigr\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\biggl\lvert\mathbb{E}\biggl[\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi-Z_{t}\leq\epsilon\}}\biggl(\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}(\xi+W_{s}-\alpha f^{1}_{s})_{-}-\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}(\xi+W_{s}-\alpha f^{2}_{s})_{-}\biggr)\biggr]\biggr\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\mathbb{E}\biggl[\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi-Z_{t}\leq\epsilon\}}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\bigl\lvert f^{1}_{s}-f^{2}_{s}\bigr\rvert\biggr]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\bigl\lvert f^{1}_{s}-f^{2}_{s}\bigr\rvert.

Taking the supremum over
t\in[0,T]
on both sides implies that
\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\bigl\lvert\Lambda_{t}(f^{1})-\Lambda_{t}(f^{2})\bigr\rvert\leq\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\bigl\lvert f^{1}_{t}-f^{2}_{t}\bigr\rvert
. Since
\frac{\alpha}{\beta}<1
, this means that
\Lambda
is indeed a contraction, so there exists a (unique) fixed point
\ell
. It follows that
(\ell,T)
is a solution to McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
).
∎

Next, we establish uniqueness of solutions on common time domains.

Proposition 3.4
.

For any two solutions
(\ell^{1},T_{1})
and
(\ell^{2},T_{2})
of McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
) it holds that
\ell^{1}_{t}=\ell^{2}_{t}
on
[0,T_{1}\land T_{2})
.

Proof.

If
T_{1}\land T_{2}=0
, the result is evident. So let us assume that
T_{1}\land T_{2}>0
and suppose that
(\ell^{1},T_{1})
and
(\ell^{2},T_{2})
are two solutions with
\ell^{1}_{t}\neq\ell^{2}_{t}
for some
t\in(0,T_{1}\land T_{2})
. We let
t_{\ast}=\inf\{t\in[0,T_{1}\land T_{2}]\mathpunct{:}\ell^{1}_{t}\neq\ell^{2}_{t}\}<T_{1}\land T_{2}
. Since
t_{\ast}<T_{1}\land T_{2}
, it follows from Lemma
3.1
that
\mathbb{P}(X^{1}_{t_{\ast}}=0)
and
\mathbb{P}(X^{2}_{t_{\ast}}=0)
are less than
\frac{1}{\alpha}
. Hence, we can proceed as in the proof of Proposition
3.3
to show that for some small time interval
[t_{\ast},T)
with
T\in(t_{\ast},T_{1}\land T_{2})
, the mapping

(f_{t})_{t\in[t_{\ast},T)}\mapsto\biggl(f_{t_{\ast}}+\mathbb{E}\sup_{t_{\ast}\leq s\leq t}\Bigl(X_{t_{\ast}}+(W_{s}-W_{t_{\ast}})-\alpha(f_{s}-f_{t_{\ast}})\Bigr)_{-}\biggr)_{t\in[t_{\ast},T)}

is a contraction. Since both
(\ell^{1}_{t})_{t\in[t_{\ast},T)}
and
(\ell^{2}_{t})_{t\in[t_{\ast},T)}
are fixed points of this map, it follows that
\ell^{1}_{t}=\ell^{2}_{t}
for
t\in[t_{\ast},T)
. This contradicts our choice of
t_{\ast}
.
∎

We can now deliver the proof of Theorem
1.12
.

Proof of Theorem
1.12
.

First, let us construct the maximal solution. Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the set of solutions to McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
) and set

(\ell,T)
to McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
). Clearly, this must be the maximal solution. Its uniqueness follows from Proposition
3.4
. Fix a sequence of solutions
(\ell^{n},T_{n})\in\mathcal{S}
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}T_{n}=T
. By Proposition
3.4
, we have that
\ell^{n}
and
\ell^{m}
coincide on
[0,T_{n}\land T_{m})
for all
n
,
m\geq 0
. Hence, setting
\ell_{t}=\ell^{n}_{t}
for

yields a well-defined element of

and it is easy to see that
(\ell,T)
solves McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
). Thus,
(\ell,T)
is the desired unique maximal solution.

Next, we verify Property
(i)
. By Lemma
3.1
, we have that
\mathbb{P}(X_{t}=0)<\frac{1}{\alpha}
for
t\in[0,T)
. If
T<\infty
and if it were the case that
\mathbb{P}(X_{T}=0)
is also strictly smaller than
\frac{1}{\alpha}
, then proceeding as in Proposition
3.3
, we could extend
(\ell,T)
beyond
T
in contradiction to its maximality. Thus, it follows that
\mathbb{P}(X_{T}=0)\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}
. The if statement of Property
(ii)
follows from Proposition
3.3
while the only if part is a direct consequence of Lemma
3.1
. Lastly, let us establish Property
(iii)
, beginning with the if statement. We want to show that
T<\infty
when
\alpha>1
or
\alpha=1
and
\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)=1
. If
\alpha>1
, then
T<\infty
by Lemma
1.10
. If
\alpha=1
and
\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)=1
, so
\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}
, then the only if part of Property
(ii)
implies
T=0<\infty
. It remains to prove that
T<\infty
implies
\alpha>1
or
\alpha=1
and
\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)=1
. This is equivalent to showing that
\alpha<1
or
\alpha=1
and
\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)<1
implies
T=\infty
. If
\alpha<1
, then
\frac{1}{\alpha}>1
. If
T
were finite, then restarting the system from
X_{T-}
at time
T
and proceeding as in the proof of Proposition
3.3
, we can extend the solution. This contradicts the maximality of
T
, so we must have
T=\infty
. Next, we consider the case
\alpha=1
and
\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)<1
. Assume again that
T<\infty
. Note that by the discussion above the statement of Theorem
1.12
and Proposition
3.2
,
X
can be extended to a continuous process on
[0,T]
. Now, let
C>0
and take expectations on both sides of (
1.5
) with respect to
\mathbb{P}(\cdot|\xi\leq C)
. This yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{t}|\xi\leq C]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\xi|\xi\leq C]-\ell_{t}+\mathbb{E}[L_{t}|\xi\leq C]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}[\xi|\xi\leq C]-\ell_{t}+\ell_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\xi|\xi\leq C],

where we used that
\mathbb{E}[L_{t}|\xi=x]
is nonincreasing in
x\geq 0
and
\mathbb{P}(\xi\leq x|\xi\leq C)\geq\mathbb{P}(\xi\leq x)
for all
x\geq 0
. Letting
t\to T
implies that
0=\mathbb{E}[\xi|\xi\leq C]
and then
C\to\infty
yields
\mathbb{E}[\xi]=0
. But since
\xi\geq 0
, this contradicts
\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)<1
, so
T=\infty
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Lastly, we will show that
\ell
is locally
1/2
-Hölder continuous on
[0,T)
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
(\ell,T)
be a solution to McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
).
Then
\ell
is locally
1/2
-Hölder continuous on
[0,T)
.

Proof.

If
T=0
, there is nothing to show, so let us assume that
T>0
. Let
(X,L)
be the solution to the Skorokhod problem associated with
(\ell,T)
and fix
T^{\prime}\in[0,T)
. We claim that there exist
\epsilon>0
and
\gamma>\alpha
, such that
\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\leq 2\epsilon)<1/\gamma
for all
t\in[0,T^{\prime}]
. Indeed, since
[0,T^{\prime}]\ni t\mapsto\mathcal{L}(X_{t})
is continuous with respect to the topology of weak convergence of measures, the Portmanteau theorem implies that the map
[0,T^{\prime}]\ni t\mapsto\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\leq 2/n)
is upper semicontinuous for all
n\geq 1
. Consequently, it assumes its maximum at some point
t_{n}\in[0,T^{\prime}]
. Now, we can choose a convergence subsequence
s_{k}=t_{n_{k}}
,
k\geq 1
, with limit
t_{\ast}\in[0,T^{\prime}]
. Then for some
k\geq 1
large enough, it must hold that
\mathbb{P}(X_{s_{k}}\leq 2/n_{k})<1/\alpha
. Otherwise, another application of the Portmanteau theorem yields

\frac{1}{\alpha}\leq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(X_{s_{k}}\leq 2/n_{k})\leq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(X_{s_{k}}\leq 2\delta)\leq\mathbb{P}(X_{t_{\ast}}\leq 2\delta)

for all
\delta\in(0,1]
. But then letting
\delta\to 0
implies
\mathbb{P}(X_{t_{\ast}}=0)\geq 1/\alpha
contradiction to Lemma
3.1
. Hence, there exists
k\geq 1
with

\sup_{t\in[0,T^{\prime}]}\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\leq 2/n_{k})=\mathbb{P}(X_{s_{k}}\leq 2/n_{k})<\frac{1}{\alpha}.

Given this
k
, we may set
\epsilon=1/n_{k}
and choose
\gamma>\alpha
sufficiently close to
\alpha
such that
\mathbb{P}(X_{s_{k}}\leq 2\epsilon)<1/\gamma<1/\alpha
.

Next, for
0\leq s\leq t\leq T^{\prime}
, set
Z_{s}^{t}=\inf_{s\leq u\leq t}(W_{u}-W_{s})
and let
\delta>0
be small enough such that
\mathbb{P}(Z_{s}^{s+\delta}<-\epsilon)\leq\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{\alpha}-\frac{1}{\gamma})
. Then, for all
s
,
t\in[0,T^{\prime}]
with
0\leq t-s\leq\delta
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl(X_{s}+Z_{s}^{t}\leq\epsilon\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\bigl(X_{s}+Z_{s}^{t}\leq\epsilon,\,Z_{s}^{t}<-\epsilon\bigr)+\mathbb{P}\bigl(X_{s}+Z_{s}^{t}\leq\epsilon,\,Z_{s}^{t}\geq-\epsilon\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\biggl(\frac{1}{\alpha}-\frac{1}{\gamma}\biggr)+\mathbb{P}(X_{s}\leq 2\epsilon)

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{2}\biggl(\frac{1}{\gamma}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\biggr),

where we use that
\mathbb{P}(X_{s}\leq 2\epsilon)<1/\gamma
. Finally, we let
\beta
be the multiplicative inverse of
\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{\alpha}-\frac{1}{\gamma})
, so that
\beta>\alpha
. Now, proceeding similarly to (
3.1
), we find

\displaystyle\ell_{t}-\ell_{s}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\sup_{s\leq u\leq t}\Bigl(X_{s}+(W_{u}-W_{s})-\alpha(\ell_{u}-\ell_{s})\Bigr)_{-}

\displaystyle\leq-\mathbb{E}[Z_{s}^{t}]+\alpha\mathbb{P}(X_{s}+Z_{s}^{t}\leq\epsilon)(\ell_{t}-\ell_{s})

\displaystyle\leq c\sqrt{t-s}+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}(\ell_{t}-\ell_{s}),

where
c=(\frac{2}{\pi})^{1/2}
. Rearranging this inequality implies the desired
1/2
-Hölder continuity on
[0,T^{\prime}]
.
∎

3.2
Propagation of Chaos

In this section, we establish propagation of chaos for the mean-field limit, proving Theorem
1.14
. We provide separate proofs for the three different regimes.

3.2.1
Subcritical Regime

In the subcritical regime
\alpha\in[0,1)
, both
\tau_{N}
and
T
are infinite, so it trivially holds that
\tau_{N}\Rightarrow T
on
[0,\infty]
. Consequently, we can focus on the convergence of
\bar{L}^{N}_{\cdot\land\tau_{N}}=\bar{L}^{N}
. To establish that, a simple coupling argument works.

Proof of Theorem
1.14
: subcritical case.

Let
\tilde{L}^{i}=\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}(\xi_{i}+W^{i}_{s}-\alpha\ell_{s})_{-}
, so that
(\tilde{L}^{i})_{i\geq 1}
are i.i.d. copies of
L
, where
(X,L)
is the solution to the Skorokhod problem associated to
\ell
. In addition, set
\ell^{N}_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{L}^{i}_{t}
. Then it holds that

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\lvert\bar{L}^{N}_{s}-\ell_{s}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\lvert\bar{L}^{N}_{s}-\ell^{N}_{s}\rvert+\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\lvert\ell^{N}_{s}-\ell_{s}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\lvert\bar{L}^{N}_{s}-\ell_{s}\rvert+\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\lvert\ell^{N}_{s}-\ell_{s}\rvert,

where we used in the second step that
L^{i}_{s}=\sup_{0\leq u\leq s}(\xi_{i}+W^{i}_{u}-\alpha\bar{L}^{N}_{u})_{-}
. Rearranging the above inequality yields

\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\lvert\bar{L}^{N}_{s}-\ell_{s}\rvert\leq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\lvert\ell^{N}_{s}-\ell_{s}\rvert.

(3.3)

We will now establish a central limit theorem for
\sqrt{N}(\ell^{N}-\ell)
on
C([0,\infty)
. That is, we show that
Z^{N}=\sqrt{N}(\ell^{N}-\ell)
converges weakly to a Gaussian process on
C([0,\infty)
. First note that from the classical central limit theorem it follows that the limit of the finite-dimensional marginals of
Z^{N}
are Gaussian. Thus, to deduce the central limit theorem, we only need to prove that the family
(Z^{N})_{N\geq 1}
is tight on
C([0,\infty))
. We begin by showing that
\ell
is
\frac{1}{2}
-Hölder continuous uniformly in time. For
t\geq s\geq 0
, we estimate

\displaystyle\ell_{t}-\ell_{s}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[L_{t}-L_{s}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\sup_{s\leq u\leq t}\bigl(X_{s}+(W_{u}-W_{s})-\alpha(\ell_{u}-\ell_{s})\bigr)_{-}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\inf_{s\leq u\leq t}(W_{u}-W_{s})+\alpha(\ell_{t}-\ell_{s}).

Rearranging this inequality implies that
\ell_{t}-\ell_{s}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}(1-\alpha)}\sqrt{t-s}
. Next, let us set
\Delta^{i}_{s,t}=\tilde{L}^{i}_{t}-\ell_{t}-(\tilde{L}^{i}_{s}-\ell_{s})
for
t\geq s\geq 0
, so that
\lvert\Delta^{i}_{s,t}\rvert\leq(1+\alpha)(\ell_{t}-\ell_{s})+\sup_{s\leq u\leq t}\lvert W_{u}-W_{s}\rvert
. This implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\lvert\Delta^{i}_{s,t}\rvert^{4}

\displaystyle\leq 8(1+\alpha)^{4}(\ell_{t}-\ell_{s})^{4}+8\mathbb{E}\sup_{s\leq u\leq t}\lvert W_{u}-W_{s}\rvert^{4}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{32(1+\alpha)^{4}}{\pi^{2}(1-\alpha)^{4}}\lvert t-s\rvert^{2}+8\lvert t-s\rvert^{2}\mathbb{E}\sup_{0\leq u\leq 1}\lvert W_{u}\rvert^{4},

where we used the
\frac{1}{2}
-Hölder continuity of
\ell
in the last step. Consequently, we find that

\mathbb{E}\lvert Z_{t}-Z_{s}\rvert^{4}=\mathbb{E}\biggl\lvert\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Delta^{i}_{s,t}\biggr\rvert^{4}\leq 3(\mathbb{E}\lvert\Delta^{i}_{s,t}\rvert^{2})^{2}+\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\lvert\Delta^{i}_{s,t}\rvert^{2}\leq C\lvert t-s\rvert^{2}

for some positive constant
C
independent of
N
. Thus, the Kolmogorov tightness criterion implies that
(Z^{N})_{N\geq 1}
is tight on
C([0,\infty))
and, hence, tends weakly to a Gaussian process
Z
on
C([0,\infty))
. From this and (
3.3
), we derive that
\sqrt{N}\lvert\bar{L}^{N}_{s}-\ell_{s}\rvert
is stochastically bounded for
N\geq 1
.
∎

3.2.2
Critical Regime

Next, let us consider the critical regime with
\alpha=1
. To establish propagation of chaos in this case, we will combine an upper bound on the average local time
\bar{L}^{N}_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}L^{i}_{t}
with tightness arguments to establish the convergence. Unlike in the subcritical case, discussed in Subsection
3.2.1
, we do not obtain a rate of convergence. Note that if
\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)=\mathbb{P}(\xi_{1}=0)=1
, then
\tau_{N}
and
T
are equal to zero, so the convergence in the statement of Theorem
1.14
is trivial. Thus, in what follows, we assume that
\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)=\mathbb{P}(\xi_{1}=0)<1
.

Recall from Remark
1.7
that in the critical regime, the particle system exists up to the first time
\tau_{N}
such that
\bar{X}^{N}_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi_{i}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}W^{i}_{t}
hits
(-\infty,0]
. Since
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi_{i}\to\mathbb{E}\xi>0
as
N\to\infty
, it follows that
\tau_{N}\to\infty
. Since
T=\infty
, this implies
\tau_{N}\Rightarrow T
on
[0,\infty]
, so again our focus is on establishing the convergence of
\bar{L}^{N}_{\cdot\land\tau_{N}}
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
T_{0}
be the supremum over all
t\geq 0
such that the sequence
(\bar{L}^{N}_{t\land\tau_{N}})_{N\geq 1}
is tight. Then
T_{0}>0
.

Proof.

We apply Itô’s formula to the process
1-e^{-X^{i}_{t}}
for
t\in[0,\tau_{N}]
and sum over
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
to obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-e^{-X^{i}_{t}})&=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-e^{-\xi_{i}})+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-X^{i}_{s}}\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{s}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}(1-e^{-X^{i}_{s}})\,\mathrm{d}\bar{L}^{N}_{s}\\
&\ \ \ -\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-X^{i}_{s}}\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&\geq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-e^{-\xi_{i}})+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-X^{i}_{s}}\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{s}-\frac{t}{2}.\end{split}

(3.4)

Let us set
\delta=\mathbb{E}[1-e^{-\xi}]/2>0
and let
\varrho_{N}
denote the first hitting time of
(-\infty,\delta]
of the process on the right-hand side of (
3.4
). Since
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-e^{-\xi_{i}})
tends to
\mathbb{E}[1-e^{-\xi}]
a.s. and

\mathbb{E}\sup_{0\leq t\leq\delta}\biggl\lvert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-X^{i}_{s}}\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{s}\biggr\rvert^{2}\to 0,

it follows that
\mathbb{P}(\varrho_{N}\leq\delta)\to 0
as
N\to\infty
. Now, let us rearrange the first two lines of (
3.4
) to find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}(1-e^{-X^{i}_{s}})\,\mathrm{d}\bar{L}^{N}_{s}\leq 1+\frac{t}{2}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-X^{i}_{s}}\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{s}

for
t\in[0,\varrho_{N}]
. Then, using that
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-e^{-X^{i}_{t}})\geq\delta
for
t\in[0,\varrho_{N}]
, meaning that
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}(1-e^{-X^{i}_{s}})\,\mathrm{d}\bar{L}^{N}_{s}\geq\delta\bar{L}^{N}_{t}
if
t\in[0,\varrho_{N}]
, we obtain the upper bound
\mathbb{E}\bar{L}^{N}_{t\land\varrho_{N}}\leq\frac{1}{\delta}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[t\land\varrho_{N}]}{2\delta}\leq\frac{1}{\delta}+\frac{1}{2}
for
t\in[0,\delta]
. Consequently, for any
t\in[0,\delta]
and
K>1
, we find

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl(\bar{L}^{N}_{t\land\tau_{N}}\geq K\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\bigl(\bar{L}^{N}_{t\land\varrho_{N}}\geq K\bigr)+\mathbb{P}\bigl(t\land\varrho_{N}<t\land\tau_{N}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\bigl[\bar{L}^{N}_{t\land\varrho_{N}}\bigr]}{K}+\mathbb{P}\bigl(\delta\land\varrho_{N}<\delta\land\tau_{N}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2+\delta}{2\delta K}+\mathbb{P}\bigl(\delta\land\varrho_{N}<\delta\land\tau_{N}\bigr).

Since
\mathbb{P}(\varrho_{N}\leq\delta)\to 0
and
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{N}\leq\delta)\to 0
as
N\to\infty
, the last inequality implies that the sequence
(\bar{L}^{N}_{t\land\tau_{N}})_{N\geq 1}
is tight for
t\in[0,\delta]
. Hence,
T_{0}\geq\delta>0
.
∎

Let us denote the space of nondecreasing càdlàg functions
[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
by
\mathcal{I}[0,\infty)
. This space can be embedded into
D[-1,\infty)
by extending functions in
\mathcal{I}[0,\infty)
to
[-1,\infty)
by setting them to zero on
[-1,0]
. We endow
\mathcal{I}[0,\infty)
with the smallest topology that makes this embedding continuous, where
D[-1,\infty)
is topologised by convergence in
M1
. By
[
30
, Theorem 12.12.2]
bounded subsets of
\mathcal{I}[0,\infty)
are compact.

Proof of Theorem
1.14
: critical case.

Since bounded subsets of
\mathcal{I}[0,\infty)
are compact, it follows from Proposition
3.6
that the sequence
(\bar{L}^{N}_{\cdot\land\tilde{\tau}_{N}})_{N\geq 1}
, with
\tilde{\tau}_{N}=\tau_{N}\land T_{0}
, is tight on
\mathcal{I}[0,\infty)
, where
T_{0}>0
is as in the statement of Proposition
3.6
. Next, let us define the function
\Lambda\mathpunct{:}[0,\infty)\times C([0,\infty))\times\mathcal{I}[0,\infty)\to\mathcal{I}[0,\infty)
, given by
\Lambda_{t}(x,w,\ell)=\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}(x+w_{s}-\ell_{s})_{-}
. It is not difficult to see that
\Lambda
is continuous (cf.
[
30
, Theorem 13.4.1]
)
and that for
t\in[0,\tau_{N}]
,
\bar{L}^{N}_{t}
satisfies the fixed-point equation

\bar{L}^{N}_{t}=\bigl\langle\nu^{N}_{t},\Lambda_{t}(\cdot,\cdot,\bar{L}^{N}_{\cdot\land t})\bigr\rangle,

where
\nu^{N}_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\xi_{i},W^{i}_{\cdot\land t}}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the pairing between a measure and an integrable function defined on the same measurable space. From the above, we know that
(\bar{L}^{N}_{\cdot\land\tilde{\tau}_{N}})_{N\geq 1}
is tight on
\mathcal{I}[0,\infty)
and
(\nu^{N})_{N\geq 1}
converges a.s. to
\nu=\mathcal{L}(\xi_{1},W^{1})
on
\mathcal{P}(C([0,\infty)))
by the law of large numbers. Hence, we can extract a subsequence that converges weakly to some
(\nu,\bar{L})
on
\mathcal{P}(C([0,\infty)))\times\mathcal{I}[0,\infty)
. Since
\Lambda
is continuous and
\tau_{N}\to\infty
as
N\to\infty
, it follows from the continuous mapping theorem that

\bar{L}_{t}=\bigl\langle\nu,\Lambda_{t}(\cdot,\cdot,\bar{L}_{\cdot\land t})\bigr\rangle

a.s. for
t\in[0,T_{0})
. Hence, almost surely,
\bar{L}
is a solution to SDE (
1.5
) on
[0,T_{0})
. Since SDE (
1.5
) has a unique solution
\ell
by Theorem
1.12
, it follows that
\bar{L}=\ell
almost surely. The same is true for the limit of any other convergent subsequence of
(\bar{L}^{N}_{\cdot\land\tilde{\tau}_{N}})_{N\geq 1}
, so weak convergence of
(\bar{L}^{N}_{\cdot\land\tilde{\tau}_{N}})_{N\geq 1}
to
\ell
on
\mathcal{I}[0,\infty)
holds along the full sequence. Moreover, since
\ell
is continuous, convergence actually holds on the space
C([0,\infty))
.

To complete the proof, we wish to show that
T_{0}=\infty
. Assume otherwise that
T_{0}<\infty
and let
(X,L)
be the solution of the Skorokhod problem associated to
\ell
. We choose
\delta>0
small enough such that
\mathbb{E}[1-e^{-X_{T_{0}-\delta/2}}]/2\geq\delta
. Then, since
\bar{L}^{N}_{T_{0}-\delta/2}\Rightarrow\ell_{T_{0}-\delta/2}
as
N\to\infty
, it holds that

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(1-e^{-X_{T_{0}-\delta/2}}\bigr)\Rightarrow\mathbb{E}\bigl[1-e^{-X_{T_{0}-\delta/2}}\bigr].

Thus, proceeding similarly as in the proof of Proposition
3.6
, we can show that
(\bar{L}^{N}_{t\land\tau_{N}})_{N\geq 0}
is tight for all
t\in[T_{0}-\delta/2,T_{0}+\delta/2]
, in contradiction to the maximality of
T_{0}
. Consequently,
T_{0}
must equal
\infty
. This concludes the proof.
∎

3.2.3
Supercritical Regime

In the supercritical regime,
\alpha>1
, we have to work less hard to prove that
(\bar{L}^{N}_{\cdot\land\tau_{N}})_{N\geq 1}
is tight on
\mathcal{I}[0,\infty)
. However, it is more challenging to show that
\tau_{N}
converges to the deterministic breakdown time
T
of the solution
(\ell,T)
of SDE (
1.5
). We start by analysing the regularity of
\bar{L}^{N}_{t}
as a function of the mass near the origin at time
t
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
T_{0}\geq 0
and assume that
\mu^{N}_{T_{0}}
converges weakly to some deterministic
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}([0,\infty))
such that
\mu_{0}([0,2\eta])<\frac{1}{2\alpha}
for some
\eta>0
. Then, for all
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
only depending on
\eta
,
\mu_{0}([0,2\eta])
, and
\epsilon
such that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\bar{L}^{N}_{(T_{0}+\delta)\land\tau_{N}}-\bar{L}^{N}_{T_{0}\land\tau_{N}}>\epsilon\Bigr)=0.

(3.5)

Proof.

Let us define
Z^{i}_{t}=\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}(W^{i}_{T_{0}}-W^{i}_{T_{0}+s})
. Then, by definition of
L^{i}
, on
\{T_{0}\leq\tau_{N}\}
, we have that for all
t\in[0,\tau_{N}-T_{0}]
that

\displaystyle L^{i}_{T_{0}+t}-L^{i}_{T_{0}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\Bigl(X^{i}_{T_{0}}+(W^{i}_{T_{0}+s}-W^{i}_{T_{0}})-\alpha(\bar{L}^{N}_{T_{0}+s}-\bar{L}^{N}_{T_{0}})\Bigr)_{-}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{1}_{\{X^{i}_{T_{0}}-Z^{i}_{t}\geq\eta\}}\bigl(\alpha(L^{i}_{T_{0}+t}-L^{i}_{T_{0}})-\eta\bigr)_{+}+\mathbf{1}_{\{X^{i}_{T_{0}}-Z^{i}_{t}<\eta\}}\bigl(Z^{i}_{t}+\alpha(L^{i}_{T_{0}+t}-L^{i}_{T_{0}})\bigr).

Summing both sides of the above inequality over
i=1
, …,
N
and rearranging implies

(1-\alpha p^{N}_{t})\bigl(\bar{L}^{N}_{T_{0}+t}-\bar{L}^{N}_{T_{0}}\bigr)\leq\Bigl(\alpha\bigl(\bar{L}^{N}_{T_{0}+t}-\bar{L}^{N}_{T_{0}}\bigr)-\eta\Bigr)_{+}+\bar{Z}^{N}_{t},

(3.6)

where
p^{N}_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{\{X^{i}_{T_{0}}-Z^{i}_{t}<\eta\}}
and
\bar{Z}^{N}_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z^{i}_{t}
. Now, since
\mu^{N}_{T_{0}}\Rightarrow\mu_{0}
, it follows from the law of large numbers that
p^{N}_{t}\Rightarrow p_{t}=\int_{[0,\infty)}\mathbb{P}\bigl(x-Z_{t}<\eta\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}(x)
, where
Z_{t}=\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}(-W_{s})
. We estimate

\displaystyle p_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,\infty)}\Bigl(\mathbb{P}\bigl(x-Z_{t}<\eta,\,Z_{t}>\eta\bigr)+\mathbb{P}\bigl(x-Z_{t}<\eta,\,Z_{t}\leq\eta\bigr)\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(Z_{t}>\eta)+\mu_{0}([0,2\eta]).

Hence, choosing
\delta_{0}
small enough such that
\mathbb{P}(Z_{t}>\eta)<\frac{1}{2\alpha}
for
t\in[0,\delta_{0}]
, we can guarantee that for all
t\in[0,\delta_{0}]
it holds that
p_{t}<\frac{1}{2\alpha}+\mu_{0}([0,2\eta])<\frac{1}{\alpha}
. Consequently, setting
\beta=(\frac{1}{2\alpha}+\mu_{0}([0,2\eta]))^{-1}>\alpha
, it holds that
\mathbb{P}(p^{N}_{t}\leq 1/\beta)\to 1
as
N\to\infty
. Next, since
p^{N}_{t}
is nondecreasing in
t
, on
\{p^{N}_{\delta_{0}}\leq 1/\beta\}
, we have that
1-\alpha p^{N}_{t}\geq 1-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}
for
t\in[0,\delta_{0}]
. Thus, in view of (
3.6
), we obtain

\bigl(\bar{L}^{N}_{T_{0}+t}-\bar{L}^{N}_{T_{0}}\bigr)\leq\frac{\beta}{\beta-\alpha}\Bigl(\alpha\bigl(\bar{L}^{N}_{T_{0}+t}-\bar{L}^{N}_{T_{0}}\bigr)-\eta\Bigr)_{+}+\frac{\beta}{\beta-\alpha}\bar{Z}^{N}_{t},

for
t\in[0,(\tau_{N}-T_{0})\land\delta_{0}]
on
\{T_{0}\leq\tau_{N},\,p^{N}_{\delta_{0}}\leq 1/\beta\}
.

Now, we claim that as long as
\bar{Z}^{N}_{t}\leq\frac{\eta(\beta-\alpha)}{\alpha\beta}
, it holds that
\bar{L}^{N}_{T_{0}+t}-\bar{L}^{N}_{T_{0}}\leq\frac{\beta}{\beta-\alpha}\bar{Z}^{N}_{t}
. Indeed, let

\varrho_{N}=\inf\Bigl\{t>0\mathpunct{:}\bar{L}^{N}_{T_{0}+t}-\bar{L}^{N}_{T_{0}}>\frac{\eta}{\alpha}\Bigr\}\land(\tau_{N}-T_{0})\land\delta_{0},

so that
\bar{L}^{N}_{T_{0}+t}-\bar{L}^{N}_{T_{0}}\leq\frac{\beta}{\beta-\alpha}\bar{Z}^{N}_{t}
for
t\in[0,\varrho_{N}]
. This implies that
\bar{L}^{N}_{T_{0}+t}-\bar{L}^{N}_{T_{0}}\leq\frac{\eta}{\alpha}
for
t\in[0,\varrho_{N}]
whenever
\bar{Z}^{N}_{t}\leq\frac{\eta(\beta-\alpha)}{\alpha\beta}
. In other words,
\varrho_{N}\geq\inf\{t>0\mathpunct{:}\bar{Z}^{N}_{t}>\frac{\eta(\beta-\alpha)}{\alpha\beta}\}\land(\tau_{N}-T_{0})\land\delta_{0}
. Consequently, if
t\in[0,\land(\tau_{N}-T_{0})\land\delta_{0}]
is such that
\bar{Z}^{N}_{t}\leq\frac{\eta(\beta-\alpha)}{\alpha\beta}
, we have
\bar{L}^{N}_{T_{0}+t}-\bar{L}^{N}_{T_{0}}\leq\frac{\beta}{\beta-\alpha}\bar{Z}^{N}_{t}
, which proves the claim. Putting everything together yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl(

\displaystyle\bar{L}^{N}_{(T_{0}+t)\land\tau_{N}}-\bar{L}^{N}_{T_{0}\land\tau_{N}}\leq\epsilon\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\biggl(\bar{L}^{N}_{(T_{0}+t)\land\tau_{N}}-\bar{L}^{N}_{T_{0}\land\tau_{N}}\leq\frac{\beta}{\beta-\alpha}\bar{Z}^{N}_{t\land(\tau_{N}-T_{0})},\,\bar{Z}^{N}_{t\land(\tau_{N}-T_{0})}\leq\frac{\beta-\alpha}{\beta}\biggl(\epsilon\land\frac{\eta}{\alpha}\biggr)\biggr)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\bigl(p^{N}_{\delta_{0}}\leq 1/\beta\bigr)-\mathbb{P}\biggl(\bar{Z}^{N}_{t}>\frac{\beta-\alpha}{\beta}\biggl(\epsilon\land\frac{\eta}{\alpha}\biggr)\biggr)

for
t\in[0,\delta_{0}]
. Clearly, by choosing
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
sufficiently small, depending on
\eta
,
\beta
(and therefore
\mu_{0}([0,2\eta])
, and
\epsilon
, we can guarantee that the last expression on the right-hand side above vanishes in the limit as
N\to\infty
. Since we already know that
\mathbb{P}(p^{N}_{\delta_{0}}\leq 1/\beta)\to 1
, we have deduced (
3.5
).
∎

We will apply Proposition
3.7
with
T_{0}=0
to show that
\tau_{N}
is asymptotically bounded away from zero.

Lemma 3.8
.

Assume that
\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)<\frac{1}{\alpha}
and let
\tilde{T}
be the supremum over all
t\geq 0
such that
\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{N}>t)=1
. Then
\tilde{T}>0
.

Proof.

Let us fix
\eta>0
sufficiently small such that
\mathbb{P}(\xi\leq 2\eta)<\frac{1}{\alpha}
, which exists since
\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)<\frac{1}{\alpha}
. Then, with
p^{N}_{t}
as defined in the proof of Proposition
3.7
, we have that
\#I_{s}<\frac{N}{\alpha}
for
s\in[0,t]
whenever
p^{N}_{t}<\frac{1}{\alpha}
and
\bar{L}^{N}_{t}\leq\frac{\eta}{2\alpha}
for
t\in[0,\tau_{N}]
, where we recall that
I_{s}=\{i\in\mathbf{N}\mathpunct{:}X^{i}_{s}=0\}
. Since
\#I_{\tau_{N}}\geq N/\alpha
, the former in turn implies that
\tau_{N}>t
if
p^{N}_{t}<\frac{1}{\alpha}
and
\bar{L}^{N}_{t}\leq\frac{\eta}{2\alpha}
. Consequently, we have

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{N}>t)\geq\mathbb{P}\Bigl(p^{N}_{t\land\tau_{N}}<\frac{1}{\alpha},\,\bar{L}^{N}_{t\land\tau_{N}}\leq\frac{\eta}{2\alpha}\Bigr)\geq\mathbb{P}\Bigl(p^{N}_{t}<\frac{1}{\alpha}\Bigr)-\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\bar{L}^{N}_{t\land\tau_{N}}>\frac{\eta}{2\alpha}\Bigr).

Now, since
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\xi_{i}}\Rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\xi)
by the law of large numbers, we can apply Proposition
3.7
with
T_{0}=0
and
\epsilon=\frac{\eta}{2\alpha}
, to see that the right-hand side above tends to
1
as
N\to\infty
for
t=\delta
, where
\delta>0
is the is the number from statement of Proposition
3.7
and
\mathbb{P}(p^{N}_{t}<1/\alpha)\to 0
is established in the proof of that proposition using that
\mathbb{P}(\xi\leq 2\eta)<1/\alpha
. But
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{N}>\delta)\to 1
implies that
\tilde{T}\geq\delta>0
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.14
: supercritical case.

By rearranging the equation

0\leq\bar{X}^{N}_{t\land\tau_{N}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\xi_{i}+W^{i}_{t\land\tau_{N}})+(1-\alpha)\bar{L}^{N}_{t\land\tau_{N}}

and taking expectations on both sides, we see that
\mathbb{E}\bar{L}^{N}_{t\land\tau_{N}}\leq\mathbb{E}\xi_{1}/(\alpha-1)
, so that the sequence
(\bar{L}^{N}_{t\land\tau_{N}})_{N\geq 1}
is tight for all

. Consequently, the sequence
(\bar{L}^{N}_{\cdot\land\tilde{\tau}_{N}},\tilde{\tau}_{N})_{N\geq 1}
is tight on
\mathcal{I}[0,\infty)
, where
\tilde{\tau}_{N}=\tau_{N}\land(T+1)
and
(\ell,T)
denotes the unique maximal solution to SDE (
1.5
). As in the proof of the critical case of Theorem
1.14
, we can show that any subsequential limit
(\bar{L},\tau)
of
(\bar{L}^{N}_{\cdot\land\tilde{\tau}_{N}},\tilde{\tau}_{N})_{N\geq 1}
is a.s. a solution to SDE (
1.5
). This in particular implies that
\tau\leq T
and
\bar{L}_{t}=\ell_{t}
for
t\in[0,\tau)
almost surely. Now, if
\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)\geq 1/\alpha
, then
T=0
, so that
\tau=0
. Hence, every subsequential limit of
(\tilde{\tau}_{N})_{N}
vanishes. Since
\tilde{\tau}_{N}=\tau_{N}\land 1
, the same is true for
(\tau_{N})_{N}
, so that
\tau_{N}\Rightarrow 0=T
. The convergence statement for
\bar{L}^{N}_{\cdot\land\tau_{N}}
is empty in this case, since the limit breaks down immediately. Next, let us consider the case
\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)<1/\alpha
. Lemma
3.8
tells us that
\tau\geq\tilde{T}>0
almost surely. Moreover, because the limit
\ell
of
\bar{L}^{N}_{\cdot\land\tau_{N}}
is unique, we can deduce that
(\bar{L}^{N}_{\cdot\land\tau_{N}})_{N\geq 1}
weakly converges along the whole sequence on
[0,\tilde{T})
. Lastly, since
\ell
is continuous, the convergence actually holds on the space
C([0,\infty))
.

Now, assume that
\mathbb{P}(\tau=T)<1
, meaning that
\tilde{T}<T
. Let
(X,L)
be the solution of the Skorokhod problem associated to
(\ell,T)
. From the weak convergence of
(\bar{L}^{N})_{N\geq 1}
to
\ell
on
[0,\tilde{T})
, it follows that
\mu^{N}_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X^{i}_{t}}
converges weakly to
\mathcal{L}(X_{t})
on
\mathcal{P}([0,\infty))
for

. Let us fix
\eta>0
such that
\mathbb{P}(X_{\tilde{T}-}\leq 2\eta)<1/\alpha
. Such

exists by Theorem
1.12
because
\tilde{T}<T
by assumption. Then by the Portmanteau theorem, we see that
\lim_{t\searrow\tilde{T}}\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\leq 2\eta)\leq\mathbb{P}(X_{\tilde{T}}\leq 2\eta)<1/\alpha
. Hence, we can find
\tilde{T}_{0}\in[0,\tilde{T})
and
\beta>\alpha
such that for any

it holds that
\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\leq 2\eta)\leq 1/\beta<1/\alpha
. Next, arguing similarly as in the proof of Lemma
3.8
, we can show that for any
t\in[\tilde{T}_{0},\tilde{T})
and

, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tau_{N}>t+s)\geq\mathbb{P}\Bigl(p^{N}_{s}<\frac{1}{\alpha}\Bigr)-\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\bar{L}^{N}_{(t+s)\land\tau_{N}}-\bar{L}^{N}_{t\land\tau_{N}}>\frac{\eta}{2\alpha}\Bigr)-\mathbb{P}(\tau_{N}\leq t).

Consequently, applying Proposition
3.7
with
T_{0}=t
and
\epsilon=\frac{\eta}{2\alpha}
, allows us to find
\delta>0
such that

. The choice of

in the statement of Proposition

can be made uniformly in

. But then for

sufficiently close to

, we have

and
\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{N}>t+\delta)=1
. This violates the maximality of

. Thus, we can conclude
\mathbb{P}(\tau=T)=1
.
∎

4
Stationary and Self-Similar Distributions

In this section, we treat the stationary and self-similar distributions of McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
). We will prove Theorem
1.16
, splitting the discussion into the two cases
\alpha=1
and
\alpha<1
. When
\alpha=1
, the family of exponential distributions is invariant for the dynamics of (
1.5
). An invariant distribution in this family is selected by the mean of the initial condition. The self-similar solutions presented in the second part of the proof occur when
\alpha<1
and the initial condition is identically zero.

4.1
Convergence to the Stationary Distribution for
\alpha=1

We begin with a sequence of auxiliary results that will be useful in establishing the convergence to the stationary distribution.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}
be two nonnegative random variables with finite second moment and identical mean. For
i=1
,
2
, let
(X^{i},L^{i})
be the solution of the Skorokhod associated with the solution to McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
) with initial condition
\xi_{i}
and
\alpha=1
. Then

\mathbb{E}\lvert X^{1}_{t}-X^{2}_{t}\rvert^{2}=\mathbb{E}\lvert\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}\rvert^{2}-2\mathbb{E}\biggl[\int_{0}^{t}X^{2}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}L^{1}_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}X^{1}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}L^{2}_{s}\biggr].

(4.1)

Proof.

By Itô’s formula, we have that

\mathbb{E}\lvert X^{1}_{t}-X^{2}_{t}\rvert^{2}=\mathbb{E}\lvert\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}\rvert^{2}-2\alpha\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[X^{1}_{s}-X^{2}_{s}]\,\mathrm{d}(\ell^{1}_{s}-\ell^{2}_{s})+2\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}(X^{1}_{s}-X^{2}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}(L^{1}_{s}-L^{2}_{s}).

(4.2)

Since
\mathbb{E}X^{i}_{s}=\mathbb{E}\xi_{i}-\ell^{i}_{s}-\mathbb{E}L^{i}_{s}=\mathbb{E}\xi_{i}
and
\mathbb{E}\xi_{1}=\mathbb{E}\xi_{2}
, the second term on the right-hand side vanishes. Developing the third term, we note that
\int_{0}^{t}X^{i}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}L^{i}_{s}=\int_{0}^{t}X^{i}_{s}\mathbf{1}_{\{X^{i}_{s}>0\}}\,\mathrm{d}L^{i}_{s}=0
, so
\int_{0}^{t}(X^{1}_{s}-X^{2}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}(L^{1}_{s}-L^{2}_{s})=-\int_{0}^{t}X^{2}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}L^{1}_{s}-\int_{0}^{t}X^{1}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}L^{2}_{s}
. Inserting this expression into Equation (
4.2
) above yields (
4.1
).
∎

Next, we want to show that the integral
\mathbb{E}[\int_{0}^{t}X^{2}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}L^{1}_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}X^{1}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}L^{2}_{s}]
on the right-hand side of (
4.2
) can be controlled (from below) by the distance between
X^{1}
and
X^{2}
. The idea is to use that if
X^{1}_{t}
and
X^{2}_{t}
are far apart, then there is a high probability that one of the processes
X^{i}_{t}
will be near the origin, accumulating local time
L^{i}_{t+\delta}-L^{i}_{t}
, while the other one
X^{j}_{t}
is strictly away from the origin ensuring that the integrand in the expression
\int_{t}^{t+\delta}X^{j}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}L^{i}_{s}
is positive. To ensure that the gap between
X^{1}_{t}
and
X^{2}_{t}
does not close rapidly, we have to show that the expected local time cannot grow to quickly. This is the subject of the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\xi
be a nonnegative square-integrable random variable with
\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)<1
and let
\ell
be the solution to McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
) with initial condition
\xi
and
\alpha=1
. Set
m_{1}=\mathbb{E}\xi
and let
m_{2}\geq 0
such that
\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}}\leq m_{2}
. Then for all
\epsilon\in(0,\frac{m_{1}}{2})
there exists
\delta>0
only depending on
\epsilon
,
m_{1}
, and
m_{2}
such that
\ell_{\delta}\leq\epsilon
.

Proof.

Fix
\epsilon\in(0,\frac{m_{1}}{2})
. Our first goal is to show that
\mathbb{P}(\xi\leq 2\epsilon)\leq 1-2\eta
, where
\eta=(m_{1}-2\epsilon)^{2}/(2m_{2}^{2})
and we note that
\eta\in(0,1/2)
owing to the assumption on
\epsilon
and the fact that
m_{1}\leq m_{2}
. We estimate

m_{1}=\mathbb{E}\xi=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi\leq 2\epsilon\}}\xi+\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi>2\epsilon\}}\xi\bigr]\leq 2\epsilon+m_{2}\mathbb{P}(\xi>2\epsilon)^{1/2}.

(4.3)

Rearranging gives
\frac{m_{1}-2\epsilon}{m_{2}}\leq\mathbb{P}(\xi>2\epsilon)^{1/2}
, so that
\mathbb{P}(\xi\leq 2\epsilon)\leq 1-2\eta
. Next, let us choose
t_{0}>0
small enough such that
\mathbb{P}(\inf_{0\leq s\leq t_{0}}W_{s}\leq-\epsilon)\leq\eta
, so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\xi+\inf_{0\leq s\leq t_{0}}W_{s}>\epsilon\Bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\xi>2\epsilon,\,\inf_{0\leq s\leq t_{0}}W_{s}>-\epsilon\Bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(\xi>2\epsilon)-\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\inf_{0\leq s\leq t_{0}}W_{s}\leq-\epsilon\Bigr)

\displaystyle\geq 2\eta-\eta=\eta,

so that
\mathbb{P}(\xi+\inf_{0\leq s\leq t_{0}}W_{s}\leq\epsilon)\leq 1-\eta
. With this, we estimate for
t\in[0,t_{0}]
,

\displaystyle\ell_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}(\xi+W_{s}-\ell_{s})_{-}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi+\inf_{0\leq s\leq t}W_{s}>\epsilon\}}(\epsilon-\ell_{t})_{-}\Bigr]+\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi+\inf_{0\leq s\leq t}W_{s}\leq\epsilon\}}\Bigl(\Bigl(\xi+\inf_{0\leq s\leq t}W_{s}\Bigr)_{-}+\ell_{t}\Bigr)\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq(\epsilon-\ell_{t})_{-}+\mathbb{E}\lvert W_{t}\rvert+(1-\eta)\ell_{t}.

Thus if
\ell_{t}\leq\epsilon
, we get
\ell_{t}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2t}}{\sqrt{\pi}\eta}
. Now, let us set
\delta=\frac{\pi\epsilon^{2}\eta^{2}}{2}\land t_{0}
, so that
\frac{\sqrt{2\delta}}{\sqrt{\pi}\eta}\leq\epsilon
. We claim that this implies
\ell_{\delta}\leq\epsilon
. Otherwise, assume that
t_{\ast}=\inf\{t>0\mathpunct{:}\ell_{t}=\epsilon\}<\delta
. Since
\ell_{t_{\ast}}=\epsilon
, the above estimate implies

\ell_{t_{\ast}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2t_{\ast}}}{\sqrt{\pi}\eta}<\epsilon,

which is a contradiction. Hence,
\ell_{\delta}\leq\epsilon
, where
\delta
only depends on
\epsilon
,
m_{1}
, and
m_{2}
.
∎

For
m>0
and
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, let us denote by
\mathcal{P}_{m,\epsilon}
the set of laws
\nu\in\mathcal{P}^{2}([0,\infty)^{2})
such that
\int_{[0,\infty)^{2}}x\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x,y)=\int_{[0,\infty)^{2}}y\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x,y)=m
,
\int_{[0,\infty)^{2}}\lvert x\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x,y)\lor\int_{[0,\infty)^{2}}\lvert y\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x,y)\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}
, and

\epsilon\leq\int_{[0,\infty)^{2}}\lvert x-y\rvert\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x,y)\leq\biggl(\int_{[0,\infty)^{2}}\lvert x-y\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x,y)\biggr)^{1/2}\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon}.

We show that
\mathbb{E}[\int_{0}^{t}X^{2}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}L^{1}_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}X^{1}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}L^{2}_{s}]
grows uniformly over pairs of initial conditions
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
whose law is in
\mathcal{P}_{m,\epsilon}
.

Proposition 4.3
.

For all
m>0
and
\epsilon\in(0,1)
there exist
t>0
and
\delta>0
such that if
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\sim\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{m,\epsilon}
and
(X^{i},L^{i})
is the solution to the Skorokhod problem associated with the solution to McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
) with initial condition
\xi_{i}
and
\alpha=1
for
i=1
,
2
, then

\mathbb{E}\biggl[\int_{0}^{t}X^{2}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}L^{1}_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}X^{1}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}L^{2}_{s}\biggr]>\delta.

Proof.

Fix
m>0
and
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. Our first goal is to show that there exists
\eta>0
such that for all
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\sim\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{m,\epsilon}
it holds that

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\xi_{1}+\eta\leq\xi_{2}\leq 1/\eta\text{ or }\xi_{2}+\eta\leq\xi_{1}\leq 1/\eta\Bigr)=\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\lvert\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}\rvert\geq\eta,\,\xi_{1}\lor\xi_{2}\leq 1/\eta\Bigr)\geq\eta.

(4.4)

For any
\eta>0
, we have

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\lvert\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}\rvert\geq\eta,\,\xi_{1}\lor\xi_{2}\leq 1/\eta\Bigr)\geq\mathbb{P}(\lvert\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}\rvert\geq\eta)-\mathbb{P}(\xi_{1}\lor\xi_{2}>1/\eta)

(4.5)

For the second term, we estimate

\mathbb{P}(\xi_{1}\lor\xi_{2}>1/\eta)\leq\eta\mathbb{E}[\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}]=2\eta m,

(4.6)

which tends to zero as
\eta\to 0
. For the first summand on the right-hand side above, we proceed similarly to (
4.3
), so setting
\Delta=\lvert\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}\rvert
, we get

\epsilon\leq\mathbb{E}\Delta=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{1}_{\{\Delta<\eta\}}\Delta+\mathbf{1}_{\{\Delta\geq\eta\}}\Delta\bigr]\leq\eta+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\mathbb{P}(\Delta\geq\eta)^{1/2},

where we used that
\mathcal{L}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\in\mathcal{P}_{m,\epsilon}
in the first and second inequality. In particular, for
\eta\in(0,\epsilon/2)
, we get
\mathbb{P}(\lvert\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}\rvert\geq\eta)\geq\epsilon^{4}/4
. Combining this with (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) and choosing
\eta=\frac{\epsilon^{4}}{4(1+2m)}\land(\frac{\epsilon}{2})
, we get

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\lvert\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}\rvert\geq\eta,\,\xi_{1}\lor\xi_{2}\leq 1/\eta\Bigr)\geq\frac{\epsilon^{4}}{4}-2\eta m\geq\eta.

Having established this, we are in the position to complete the proof of the lemma. Fix
{(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\sim\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{m,\epsilon}}
and let
(X^{i},L^{i})
,
i=1
,
2
, be as in the statement of the lemma. We will construct the desired
t>0
and
\delta>0
independently of
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
. By Lemma
4.2
, there exists
t>0
small enough only depending on
m
and
\epsilon
such that
\ell^{i}_{t}\leq\eta/4
. By (
4.4
), we have that
\mathbb{P}(\xi_{1}+\eta\leq\xi_{2}\leq 1/\eta)\geq\eta/2
or
\mathbb{P}(\xi_{2}+\eta\leq\xi_{1}\leq 1/\eta)\geq\eta/2
, Without loss of generality, let us assume that the former is the case. Then, on
\{\xi_{1}+\eta\leq\xi_{2}\leq 1/\eta\}
, we have
\xi_{1}-\ell^{1}_{s}+\frac{3\eta}{4}\leq\xi_{1}+\frac{3\eta}{4}\leq\xi_{2}-\ell^{2}_{s}
for
s\in[0,t]
. Next, let us define the set
A=\{\inf_{0\leq s\leq t}W_{s}\in[-\xi_{1}-\frac{\eta}{2},-\xi_{1}-\frac{\eta}{4}]\}
, so that

\mathbb{P}(A)\geq\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\inf_{0\leq s\leq t}W_{s}\in\Bigl[-\frac{1}{\eta}-\frac{\eta}{2},-\frac{1}{\eta}-\frac{\eta}{4}\Bigr]\Bigr)=p>0.

On the set
\{\xi_{1}+\eta\leq\xi_{2}\leq 1/\eta\}\cap A
, which as probability at least
\frac{p\eta}{2}
, it holds that

\inf_{0\leq s\leq t}(\xi_{1}+W_{t}-\ell^{1}_{s})_{-}\leq\inf_{0\leq s\leq t}(\xi+W_{s})_{-}\in\Bigl[\frac{\eta}{4},\frac{\eta}{2}\Bigr],

so that
L^{1}_{t}>\frac{\eta}{4}
. On the other hand,

\inf_{0\leq s\leq t}X^{2}_{s}\geq\inf_{0\leq s\leq t}(\xi_{2}+W_{t}-\ell^{2}_{s})\geq\inf_{0\leq s\leq t}\biggl(\xi_{1}+\frac{3\eta}{4}+W_{s}\biggr)\geq\frac{\eta}{4}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}X^{2}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}L^{1}_{s}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\biggl[\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi_{1}+\eta\leq\xi_{2}\leq 1/\eta\}\cap A}\int_{0}^{t}X^{2}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}L^{1}_{s}\biggr]

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta}{4}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi_{1}+\eta\leq\xi_{2}\leq 1/\eta\}\cap A}L^{1}_{\delta}\bigr]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{p\eta^{3}}{24}.

Since both
p

Proof of Theorem
1.16

(i)
.

We begin by proving that the set of stationary distributions is given by
(\text{Exp}(\lambda))_{\lambda>0}
. Suppose that
\mu\in\mathcal{P}([0,\infty))
is a stationary distribution for McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
) and let
\ell
be the corresponding solution with initial condition
\xi\sim\mu
. Fix
r\in\mathbb{R}
and apply Itô’s formula to
\exp(irX_{t})

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\exp(irX_{t})

\displaystyle=ir\exp(irX_{t})\,(\mathrm{d}W_{t}+\mathrm{d}L_{t}-d\ell_{t})-\frac{r^{2}}{2}\exp(irX_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=ir\exp(irX_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}+ir\,\mathrm{d}L_{t}-ir\exp(irX_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{t}-\frac{r^{2}}{2}\exp(irX_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t.

We note that the coefficient of
\mathrm{d}L_{t}
is
ir\exp(0)=ir
by the zero-off condition of the Skorokhod map. Taking expectation yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[\exp(irX_{t})]&=\mathbb{E}[\exp(irX_{0})]+ir\ell_{t}-\int_{0}^{t}ir\mathbb{E}[\exp(irX_{s})]\,\mathrm{d}\ell_{s}\\
&\ \ \ -\int_{0}^{t}\frac{r^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[\exp(irX_{s})]\,\mathrm{d}s\end{split}

(4.7)

where we have applied Fubini’s theorem. Since
\mu
is stationary, we have that
\varphi(r)=\mathbb{E}[\exp(irX_{t})]=\mathbb{E}[\exp(irX_{0})]
for all
t\geq 0
, and
\ell_{t}=\lambda t/2
for some
\lambda>0
. Thus, from (
4.7
), we obtain the algebraic equation

i\lambda r-i\lambda r\varphi(r)-r^{2}\varphi(r)=0

Hence,
\varphi(r)=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-ir}
. This is the characteristic function of an exponential random variable with rate
\lambda
. Consequently, the set of stationary distributions is a subset of
(\text{Exp}(\lambda))_{\lambda>0}
. To show conversely that any
\text{Exp}(\lambda)
with
\lambda>0
actually is a stationary distribution, we apply the superposition principle from Proposition
1.19
. Since the flow
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
given by
\mu_{t}=\text{Exp}(\lambda)
lies in

and solves PDE (
1.11
), it induces a solution
\ell
to McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
) such that the law
\mathcal{L}(X_{t})
of the solution
(X,L)
of the Skorokhod problem associated with
\ell
equals
\mu_{t}=\textup{Exp}(\lambda)
. Thus,
\textup{Exp}(\lambda)
is indeed stationary.

Next, let us establish convergence to the stationary distribution. Assume that
\mathbb{E}\xi_{1}=m>0
and that
\xi_{1}
has a finite second moment and let
\xi_{2}
be an exponential distribution with rate
\lambda=m^{-1}
. Let
\ell^{i}
denote the solution to McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
) with initial condition
\xi_{i}
and
\alpha=1
and let
(X^{i},L^{i})
be the solution to the Skorokhod problem associated with
\ell^{i}
. Our goal is to show that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\lvert X^{1}_{t}-X^{2}_{t}\rvert=0.

We argue by contradiction, supposing that
\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\lvert X^{1}_{t}-X^{2}_{t}\rvert>0
. It follows from Lemma
4.1
that
\mathbb{E}\lvert X^{1}_{t}-X^{2}_{t}\rvert^{2}
is nonincreasing in
t\geq 0

\epsilon<\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\lvert X^{1}_{t}-X^{2}_{t}\rvert\leq\lim_{t\to\infty}(\mathbb{E}\lvert X^{1}_{t}-X^{2}_{t}\rvert^{2})^{1/2}<\frac{1}{\epsilon}.

In particular, we can find a sequence of times
(t_{n})_{n\geq 1}
tending to infinity such that the family
(\mathcal{L}(X^{1}_{t_{n}},X^{2}_{t_{n}}))_{n\geq 1}
lies in
\mathcal{P}_{m,\epsilon}
. Consequently, by Proposition
4.3
, there exist
t>0
and

such that

\mathbb{E}\biggl[\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n}+t}X^{2}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}L^{1}_{s}+\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n}+t}X^{1}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}L^{2}_{s}\biggr]>\delta.

Now, let us choose
n\geq 1
large enough such that

\mathbb{E}\lvert X^{1}_{t_{n}}-X^{2}_{t_{n}}\rvert^{2}-\lim_{s\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\lvert X^{1}_{s}-X^{2}_{s}\rvert^{2}<\delta.

Then, using Lemma
4.1
, we can derive the following contradiction:

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\lvert X^{1}_{s}-X^{2}_{s}\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\lvert X^{1}_{t_{n}+t}-X^{2}_{t_{n}+t}\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\lvert X^{1}_{t_{n}}-X^{2}_{t_{n}}\rvert^{2}-2\mathbb{E}\biggl[\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n}+t}\mathbf{1}_{X^{2}_{s}>0}\,\mathrm{d}L^{1}_{s}+\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n}+t}\mathbf{1}_{X^{1}_{s}>0}\,\mathrm{d}L^{2}_{s}\biggr]

\displaystyle<\mathbb{E}\lvert X^{1}_{t_{n}}-X^{2}_{t_{n}}\rvert^{2}-2\delta

Note that while we use that fact that

in order to arrive at a contradiction, the original assumption leading to this contradiction was

. Consequently, the proof does not establish convergence in

4.2
Convergence to the Self-Similar Profile for

We can immediately deliver the proof of the second part of Theorem
1.16
.

Proof of Theorem
1.16

(ii)
.

Let
\rho_{\alpha}
be defined as in (
1.7
) and set
\mu_{t}=(\sqrt{c_{\alpha}t}\id_{\mathbb{R}})^{\#}\rho_{\alpha}
, where
\id_{\mathbb{R}}
is the identity function
\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
(\cdot)^{\#}
denotes the pushfoward of a map. Then one can verify that
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}\in C([0,T_{\ast});L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+}))\cap L^{1}_{\textup{loc}}([0,T_{\ast});W^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}_{+}))
and a straightforward but cumbersome calculation shows that the flow

\ell

1.5
) such that the law
\mathcal{L}(X_{t})
of the solution
(X,L)
of the Skorokhod problem associated with
\ell
coincides with
\mu_{t}
. Moreover, a simple computation shows that
\mathbb{E}X_{t}=\sqrt{c_{\alpha}t}
, so that
\mathcal{L}(X_{t}/\mathbb{E}X_{t})=((\sqrt{c_{\alpha}t})^{-1}\id_{\mathbb{R}})^{\#}\mu_{t}=\rho_{\alpha}
. Thus,
\rho_{\alpha}
is indeed a self-similar profile. Next, we establish the convergence to the self-similar profile. Let
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}
have finite expectation. Let
\ell^{i}
denote the solution to McKean–Vlasov SDE (
1.5
) with initial condition
\xi_{i}
and
\alpha\in[0,1)
and let
(X^{i},L^{i})
be the solution to the Skorokhod problem associated with
\ell^{i}
. We want to show that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\biggl\lvert\frac{X^{1}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}X^{1}_{t}}-\frac{X^{2}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}X^{2}_{t}}\biggr\rvert=0.

(4.8)

Then, the specific choice
\xi_{2}=0
implies that
\mathcal{L}(X^{2}_{t}/\mathbb{E}X^{2}_{t})=\rho_{\alpha}
, so the desired convergence to the self-similar profile follows from (
4.8
). By the triangle inequality, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\biggl\lvert\frac{X^{1}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}X^{1}_{t}}-\frac{X^{2}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}X^{2}_{t}}\biggr\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}X^{1}_{t}}\mathbb{E}\lvert X^{1}_{t}-X^{2}_{t}\rvert+\mathbb{E}X^{2}_{t}\biggl\lvert\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}X^{1}_{t}}-\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}X^{2}_{t}}\biggr\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}X^{1}_{t}}\mathbb{E}\lvert X^{1}_{t}-X^{2}_{t}\rvert+\mathbb{E}X^{2}_{t}\frac{\lvert\mathbb{E}X^{2}_{t}-\mathbb{E}X^{1}_{t}\rvert}{\mathbb{E}X^{1}_{t}\mathbb{E}X^{2}_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\mathbb{E}X^{1}_{t}}\mathbb{E}\lvert X^{1}_{t}-X^{2}_{t}\rvert.

Next, we show that
\mathbb{E}\lvert X^{1}_{t}-X^{2}_{t}\rvert
is bounded uniformly over
t\geq 0
and that
\mathbb{E}X^{1}_{t}\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
. These two results together with the above estimate imply (
4.8
). We have that

\mathbb{E}\lvert X^{1}_{t}-X^{2}_{t}\rvert\leq\mathbb{E}\lvert\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}\rvert+\alpha\lvert\ell^{1}_{t}-\ell^{2}_{t}\rvert+\mathbb{E}\lvert L^{1}_{t}-L^{2}_{t}\rvert

(4.9)

To deal with the local times, we note that

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\mathbb{E}\lvert L^{1}_{s}-L^{2}_{s}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\biggl\lvert\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}(\xi_{1}+W_{t}-\alpha\ell^{1}_{t})_{-}-\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}(\xi_{2}+W_{t}-\alpha\ell^{2}_{t})_{-}\biggr\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\lvert\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}\rvert+\alpha\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\lvert\ell^{1}_{s}-\ell^{2}_{s}\rvert.

But
\lvert\ell^{1}_{s}-\ell^{2}_{s}\rvert\leq\mathbb{E}\lvert L^{1}_{s}-L^{2}_{s}\rvert
, so that by the above inequality, we find that
\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\lvert\ell^{1}_{s}-\ell^{2}_{s}\rvert\leq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\mathbb{E}\lvert\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}\rvert
, which in turn yields
\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\mathbb{E}\lvert L^{1}_{s}-L^{2}_{s}\rvert\leq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\mathbb{E}\lvert\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}\rvert
. Plugging all of these estimates into (
4.9
) finally shows

\mathbb{E}\lvert X^{1}_{t}-X^{2}_{t}\rvert\leq\mathbb{E}\lvert\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}\rvert+\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\mathbb{E}\lvert\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}\rvert+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\mathbb{E}\lvert\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}\rvert=\frac{2}{1-\alpha}\mathbb{E}\lvert\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}\rvert,

so
\mathbb{E}\lvert X^{1}_{t}-X^{2}_{t}\rvert
indeed is bounded uniformly in
t\geq 0
. It remains to establish that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}X^{1}_{t}=\infty
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}X^{1}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\xi_{1}+(1-\alpha)\ell^{1}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\xi_{1}+(1-\alpha)\mathbb{E}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}(\xi+W_{s}-\alpha^{1}_{s})_{-}

\displaystyle\geq(1-\alpha)\mathbb{E}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}(\xi+W_{s})_{-}.

Since
1-\alpha>0
, the right-hand side diverges to infinity as
t\to\infty
by Lemma
A.1
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\xi
be a random variable and let
W

\xi

(X,L)
the solution to the Skorokhod problem for
\xi+W

f_{t}\geq C\sqrt{t-t_{0}}

Proof.

It holds that
L_{t}=\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}(\xi+W_{s})_{-}
for
t\in[0,\infty)
, so that

f_{t}=\mathbb{E}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}(\xi+W_{s})_{-}=\int_{[0,\infty)}\mathbb{E}\biggl[\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}(x+W_{s})_{-}\biggr]\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}(\xi)(x).

(A.1)

For

, we define the stopping time
\tau_{x}=\inf\{t>0\mathpunct{:}W_{t}=-x\}
. By the strong Markov property,
B
defined by
B_{t}=W_{\tau_{x}+t}-W_{\tau_{x}}=W_{\tau_{x}+t}+x
for

\tau_{x}
. We can write

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}(x+W_{s})_{-}&=\mathbb{E}\biggl[\mathbf{1}_{t\leq\tau_{x}}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}(x+W_{s})_{-}\biggr]+\mathbb{E}\biggl[\mathbf{1}_{t>\tau_{x}}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}(x+W_{s})_{-}\biggr]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\biggl[\mathbf{1}_{t>\tau_{x}}\mathbb{E}\biggl[\sup_{0\leq s\leq t-\tau_{x}}(B_{s})_{-}\bigg|\tau_{x}\biggr]\biggr]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{1}_{t>\tau_{x}}\mathbb{E}[\lvert B_{t-\tau_{x}}\rvert|\tau_{x}]\bigr]\\
&=\biggl(\frac{2}{\pi}\biggr)^{1/2}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{1}_{t>\tau_{x}}(t-\tau_{x})^{1/2}\bigr],\end{split}

(A.2)

where we used that

in the first line, the tower property of conditional expectations in the second line, and independence of

Next, we can find

\mathbb{P}(\xi\leq x_{0})\geq 1/2
. Note that

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{x_{0}}\leq t_{0})\geq 1/2
. Moreover, since
\tau_{x}\leq\tau_{x_{0}}
for

, we obtain
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{x}\leq t_{0})\geq 1/2
. Consequently, for

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{1}_{t>\tau_{x}}(t-\tau_{x})^{1/2}\bigr]\geq\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{1}_{\tau_{x}\leq t_{0}}(t-t_{0})^{1/2}\bigr]\geq\frac{(t-t_{0})^{1/2}}{2}.

Combining the above with Equations (

Proposition A.2
