A case report of tracheal duplication cyst with steatocystomatous histology: a rare embryological overlap
Authors: Krishna Sai Kiran Sakalabaktula, Shravani Divity, Sameer Kumar Majety, Shivakumar Divity, Gopichand Muppana
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTracheal duplication cysts (TDCs) are rare congenital anomalies caused by defects in the duplication of the foregut, that is of endodermal origin. These cysts are usually present during childhood and cases in adults are seen very rarely. Steatocystoma multiplex is a disorder of multiple sebaceous cysts, most commonly from skin appendages, that has ectodermal origin. The presence of the two coexisting in the same place has never been reported in literature. We report the first case of a unique embryological overlap between ectoderm and endoderm in a TDC.Case presentationA 34-year-old Indian male presented with progressive hoarseness and a change in voice. Laryngoscopy revealed left vocal cord paralysis. MRI of the neck done later showed a 3 cm cystic mass in the left tracheoesophageal groove, which was compressing the left recurrent laryngeal nerve. It was surgically excised, and the histopathology revealed a cyst lined by cuboidal epithelium with numerous sebaceous gland nests consistent with steatocystoma multiplex. These findings confirmed a tracheal duplication cyst with unique ectodermal differentiation in this patient.DiscussionOur case stands out for its first-of-its-kind embryological overlap and also for its surgical management. The lack of respiratory epithelium and the presence of sebaceous glands (ectodermal) within a TDC (endodermal) suggest a misdirected embryologic development of the foregut. Dermoid, teratoma, branchial cleft cysts, laryngeal cysts, and malignancy were ruled out. Even though these cysts are benign, they may cause airway obstruction, vocal cord palsy, or other associated compressive symptoms. The treatment of choice for these cysts is en bloc removal, because incomplete excision has been reported to have increased recurrence. Although malignant transformation is rare, histopathological examination is required to rule it out.ConclusionThis is the first reported case of a tracheal duplication cyst with steatocystoma-like histology. It identified the need to consider rare embryologic overlaps while evaluating neck cysts and highlights the importance of thorough histopathological examination for better diagnosis and management.

Introduction

Tracheal duplication cysts (TDCs) are the result of foregut duplication during early embryogenesis and are usually seen in childhood. Patients generally present with symptoms of mass effect like dysphagia, stridor, cough, and sometimes even chest pain [1]. Foregut duplication cysts can be formed anywhere along the length of the alimentary canal, and TDCs are among the rarest foregut duplication cysts, constituting 6–15% of mediastinal masses [2]. Identifying and treating TDCs is important because they pose a risk of airway obstruction, infection, and (rarely) malignancy. Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) is widely used for the diagnosis of TDCs, and other diagnostic modalities like CT, MRI, and direct laryngobronchoscopy (DLB) are employed to obtain a better understanding of adjacent structures.

Patients with significant symptoms from these cysts are advised to undergo complete surgical excision after meticulous planning, considering important structures like the recurrent laryngeal nerve(RLN), inferior thyroid artery, and inferior thyroid vein [3]. The core of a true TDC consists of respiratory epithelium and mimics tracheal tissue, including cartilage, elastic tissue, smooth muscle, and mucinous glands [4].

On the other hand, steatocystoma multiplex is a rare congenital disorder with an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, characterized by multiple cutaneous cysts commonly seen on the chest and shoulders. Most of the time, these cysts are asymptomatic and are addressed only due to cosmetic concerns [5]. Steatocystoma multiplex is a benign lesion composed of ectodermal elements such as squamous epithelium and sebaceous glands.

We present a case of a 34-year-old male who presented with voice changes and was diagnosed with a tracheal duplication cyst with histopathological features suggestive of steatocystoma multiplex. The patient’s age at presentation and the cyst’s atypical histology make this tracheal duplication cyst extremely rare and worth reporting. It is uncommon for a foregut-derived (endodermal) duplication cyst to contain ectodermal derivatives, making this the first reported case of an overlap between both rare occurrences. This case highlights the complexities of early embryonic development and tissue migration. Due to its extremely rare presentation, TDC should be differentiated from other common cystic neck masses such as branchial cleft cysts, thyroglossal duct cysts, bronchogenic cysts, and laryngoceles.

Case presentation

A 34-year-old Indian male patient presented to our clinic with complaints of voice changes for two months and a left-sided neck swelling for the same duration. Worsening hoarseness following an accidental aspiration event prompted his clinical visit. The patient denied any complaints of breathing difficulty, hypo/hyperthyroid symptoms, throat fullness, or dysphagia.There was no history of similar swellings in the past, and no family history of congenital anomalies, cutaneous cysts, or genetic syndromes was reported.

On examination, his vital signs were within normal limits. Physical examination revealed a 5 × 5 cm palpable swelling on the left side of the neck, which was well-defined and moved with deglutition. Oral cavity and throat examinations were unremarkable.

Video laryngoscopic examination revealed normal epiglottis, vallecula, bilateral arytenoids, aryepiglottic folds, and piriform sinuses. The right vocal cord was mobile, while the left vocal cord was immobile, indicating left vocal cord palsy and prompting further evaluation. MRI of the neck revealed a well-defined, thin-walled, T1 hypointense, T2 and STIR hyperintense cystic lesion measuring 35 × 27 × 25 mm in the lower neck at the level of the C6, C7, and T1 vertebrae, located in the tracheoesophageal groove (Figs. 1, 2 and 3). The cyst was closely abutting the tracheal wall, with mild indentation of the tracheal lumen and medial deviation of the left vocal cord, findings suggestive of vocal cord palsy. The cystic lesion showed no intraluminal communication with the trachea and no retrosternal extension. Based on its location and characteristics, a tracheal duplication cyst (TDC) was the most likely diagnosis. The posterior pharynx, postcricoid hypopharynx, and cervical esophagus appeared normal, as did the bilateral parathyroid glands, submandibular glands, and thyroid gland. The trachea, carina, and both main bronchi were also unremarkable.

After obtaining informed written consent, the patient was planned for elective open neck dissection. An open cervical approach was preferred over thoracotomy due to the lower neck location of the cyst. Under general anesthesia, an incision was made one fingerbreadth above the sternal notch, and skin flaps were elevated. The sternohyoid muscles were divided, and the sternothyroid muscles were dissected bluntly. The left inferior thyroid vein was ligated, and the left thyroid artery was identified and preserved. The left recurrent laryngeal nerve(RLN) was carefully traced and preserved throughout the procedure. A thin-walled cyst was identified adjacent to the left RLN, trachea, and esophagus and was completely delineated. It was checked for any structural communication, then excised in toto with the wall intact, and the specimen was sent for histopathological examination. Hemostasis was achieved, and the strap muscles and skin incision were closed. The patient was extubated and remained stable postoperatively.

Histopathological analysis demonstrated a single cystic soft tissue mass. The cyst was lined by cuboidal cells with clear cytoplasm and round vesicular nuclei. The wall contained numerous congested capillaries, fibrous tissue, and nests of cuboidal cells similar to those seen in the lining, features consistent with steatocystoma multiplex (Fig. 4). Postoperative recovery was uneventful, with stable vitals and no signs of bleeding or airway compromise. At discharge, the patient was stable with a healthy surgical wound. At six-month follow-up, the patient had regained baseline voice quality, with no clinical or radiological evidence of recurrence or residual disease.

The patient has been advised to undergo regular follow-up for a minimum of five years: every six months for the first two years, followed by annual surveillance to monitor for potential recurrence or late complications (Table 1).

Case discussion

Tracheal duplication cysts (TDCs) are a type of foregut duplication cyst that are rare malformations commonly reported in children, with only a limited number of cases documented in adults in the medical literature [1, 6]. Due to their rarity, the exact pathogenesis of these lesions remains unknown, and multiple embryological theories have been proposed to explain their development.

One of the leading concepts is the Accessory Foregut Bud Theory, which suggests that TDCs arise from abnormal supernumerary budding of the developing tracheobronchial tree during early embryogenesis, typically between the 4th and 8th weeks of gestation. This aberrant bud separates from the primary airway and persists as a cyst along the trachea or esophagus. A related model, the Recanalization (Vacuolization) Theory, describes that incomplete vacuolization of the temporarily solid foregut during early development can result in parallel lumens or cystic remnants that share a wall with the native tract. These two theories explain typical duplication cysts but do not account for the presence of ectodermal elements, such as sebaceous glands, as observed in this case [7–9].

Some broader embryologic models attempt to address this limitation. The Partial Twinning Theory considers duplications as a form of incomplete axial twinning that produces parallel segments of the gastrointestinal tract with variable communication or associated defects. These duplications are more commonly seen at the foregut and hindgut ends. However, this theory also does not directly explain heterotopic or ectodermal tissue incorporation [9].

Among the most compelling explanations for mixed germ-layer origin is Bentley and Smith’s Split Notochord (Neurenteric) Theory, which proposes that failure of separation between the notochord and endoderm in the early 3rd week of gestation can lead to a persistent connection between the endoderm and ectoderm. This allows ectodermal progenitor cells, normally destined for neural or cutaneous structures, to become incorporated into the developing foregut. This theory accounts for neurenteric cysts and related malformations containing both endodermal and ectodermal tissue. While typically associated with vertebral anomalies, such defects can also occur in localized or limited forms without spinal involvement [9–11].

This rare embryological overlap of endodermal and ectodermal elements within a tracheal duplication cyst (TDC) could be explained by several additional mechanisms. One possibility is aberrant cell migration during early gastrulation (3rd–4th week), which may allow ectodermal progenitors normally destined for cutaneous or neural structures to become sequestered within the developing foregut endoderm, leading to the creation of composite lesions [7]. Another explanation is field defects, which affect multiple germ layers simultaneously due to localized disturbances in regional embryonic patterning. This disruption can result in disorganized differentiation of ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm, even without direct misplacement of progenitor cells [12].

A further possibility is metaplastic transformation of foregut endodermal epithelium toward an ectodermal phenotype. Bronchogenic and esophageal foregut cysts have occasionally been reported to undergo squamous metaplasia or contain cutaneous adnexal elements. This transformation may result from congenital epithelial plasticity or develop in response to chronic local influences [13]. Finally, incomplete germ-layer separation during gastrulation, as described in the split notochord (neurenteric) theory, continues to provide one of the most robust embryological frameworks for explaining this phenomenon [11, 12].

In the context of our patient, the cyst’s tracheal location without luminal communication was consistent with an accessory foregut bud origin, while the presence of sebaceous (ectodermal) tissue aligns with a limited split notochord theory. The absence of vertebral anomalies may suggest a localized variant of this mechanism. Overall, TDCs may arise from a spectrum of embryologic disturbances that are best explained by the interplay of multiple theories, depending on the lesion’s location, histological features, and associated anomalies [8, 9, 11].

The type of epithelium lining foregut duplication cysts is pseudostratified ciliated columnar (respiratory) and stratified squamous or simple columnar (gastrointestinal) [14]. In this case, it was lined with cuboidal epithelium, consistent with a steatocystoma multiplex variant, which has never been documented in relation to tracheal duplication cysts. TDCs usually remain asymptomatic, and the common clinical presenting symptoms are due to compression of the trachea or esophagus, such as stridor, cough, chest pain, respiratory distress, and dysphagia [1, 15]. In this case, the patient presented atypically with a change in voice, which was due to left vocal cord palsy that might have been caused by the TDC at the level of the T1 and T2 tracheal rings compressing the left recurrent laryngeal nerve.

The classic features of cutaneous steatocystoma multiplex cysts include a squamous epithelial lining with sebaceous glands and occasionally hair follicles in the cystic wall, with clear sebaceous fluid inside. However, this lesion demonstrated a variant morphology with cuboidal clear cells and sebaceous-like nests within the cyst wall on histopathological examination [16]. The differential diagnoses considered were dermoid cyst, which was excluded due to the absence of a granular layer; bronchogenic duplication cyst and esophageal duplication cyst, which were ruled out based on the location of the cyst and tracheal wall integration; and teratoma, which was excluded due to the absence of tissues from all three germ layers [6]. These findings support the diagnosis of steatocystomatous epithelial differentiation in a tracheal duplication cyst, representing an unusual pattern, particularly in this extracutaneous location, making this case report the first in literature.

Tracheal duplication cysts may present with a variety of symptoms depending upon the age of the patient and also the exact location of the cyst. The presentation in children may be as perilous as respiratory collapse, secondary to complete obstruction.[11] On the other hand, adult patients may present with symptoms with lower degree of severity, like hoarsness of voice, chest pain, intermittent breathing difficulties, and dysphagia due to compression of nearby structures. Duplication cysts of esophageal, laryngeal, and tracheal origin may present similarly and a distinctive clinical pattern can not be established. Ultimately, the confirmed diagnosis can be made only from the histopathological examination (HPE). The HPE of the specimen from our case is revealed to be steatocystomatous (pilosebaceous), which is quite rare. Presence of sebaceous material in our case is a unique occurrence as there are no other cases reported, to the best of our knowledge.

HPE provides a definitive indication of the origin, and radiological imaging offers further crucial information to elucidate the location. In our case, an MRI scan showed a thin-walled, homogenous cyst situated in the left inter-tracheoesophageal groove. Typically, TDCs appear as well-defined, homogenous cystic masses adjacent to the trachea or esophagus. Additionally, the cyst might show enhancement if it contains proteinaceous or hemorrhagic components [18]. Interestingly, a study evaluated endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS) followed by fine needle aspiration (FNA) of the cyst, a novel diagnostic procedure in foregut duplication cysts. According to this study, EUS was superior to computed tomography (CT), and EUS-FNA was reported to be safe and accurate for the diagnosis of foregut duplication cysts [19]. However, the general applicability of these findings remains questionable due to the small sample size (n= 10), and there is also a risk of misleading analysis of aspirate from an oil-containing cyst [1]. Radiological imaging is also useful post-operatively to confirm complete resolution and absence of recurrence.

Complete surgical excision of the cyst and its wall remains the gold standard for treatment, as supported by multiple studies [1, 20]. A retrospective study by Scarpa A et al. (2017), which included 41 patients with different foregut duplication cysts, stated that surgical excision resulted in complete resolution without recurrence [20]. However, the study also described surgical challenges and complexities that might arise due to the anatomical location of the cyst, which requires special caution during dissection. This was reflected in another case where a complex anatomical location led to prior incomplete resections, ultimately requiring a multidisciplinary surgical approach to achieve complete excision [6].

Even though recurrence is unusual following complete surgical excision of a tracheal duplication cyst (TDC), long-term follow-up is still essential [21]. Incomplete resection, especially with residual epithelial lining, has been correlated with recurrence months or years after operation. Bellur et al. stressed that recurrence must be considered in cases of persistent symptoms and underscored the necessity of careful removal to avoid regrowth. In our patient, the cyst was excised en bloc, and no signs of recurrence were observed at the six-month follow-up. However, given the unusual histological findings and potential for delayed recurrence, we established a structured follow-up protocol. The patient has been advised clinical and imaging evaluation every six months for the first two years, followed by annual follow-up for at least three more years. This strategy ensures timely identification of any late complications or recurrence.

In contrast to certain gastrointestinal duplication cysts where, in chronic contexts, there have been occasional reports of malignancy such as adenocarcinomas [22], TDCs have not demonstrated any malignant potential when they are completely resected. The most significant concern about malignancy in these lesions is misdiagnosis, for example, confusing a cystic carcinoma or metastatic lesion with a duplication cyst. Surgical excision is thus indicated not only for symptom control but also to exclude more severe pathology.

This case highlights the clinical importance of considering rare histological variants when evaluating foregut duplication cysts, particularly in adults with atypical presentations. The presence of sebaceous differentiation within a tracheal duplication cyst expands the known histological spectrum of these lesions and reinforces the need for complete surgical excision to ensure both therapeutic success and accurate diagnosis. Clinicians should remain vigilant when assessing cystic neck masses, and pathologists should be aware of the potential for unusual epithelial features that may mimic cutaneous lesions.

From a developmental perspective, this case raises important questions about the embryological mechanisms underlying mixed germ layer expression in foregut-derived structures. Future research should aim to systematically review tracheal duplication cyst cases to identify similar histological variants that may have gone unrecognized. Molecular characterization through immunohistochemical profiling could offer valuable insights into the epithelial differentiation pathways involved. Genetic analysis, particularly screening for KRT17 or other relevant developmental gene mutations, may help clarify the origin of ectodermal elements within an endodermal cyst. Long-term follow-up studies of atypical foregut duplication cysts are also essential to determine their recurrence patterns and long-term clinical behavior. Together, these investigations may advance our understanding of congenital foregut anomalies and refine diagnostic and therapeutic strategies in similar cases.

Conclusion

This is a rare and unexpected diagnosis of tracheal duplication cyst in an adult, with histologic features of steatocystoma multiplex. Presence of endodermal and ectodermal elements together implies an uncommon embryologic anomaly and this appears to be the first report with such a combination. Even though the initial symptom of hoarseness in the patient was uncommon, complete surgical excision resulted in recovery with no evidence of recurrence at follow-up. Clinicians and Surgeons should consider the unusual congenital abnormalities while dealing with adult neck masses, and the significance of histopathological examination for proper diagnosis.

Limitations

The primary limitation of this report is the lack of extended follow-up, as the patient has only been monitored for six months after surgery. While no recurrence has been observed so far, this short duration limits our ability to comment on the long-term behavior of the lesion, especially given its atypical histology. A longer surveillance period is essential to evaluate the potential for delayed recurrence or late complications. The patient has been advised to continue follow-up for at least five years, with regular clinical and imaging assessments.

Another significant limitation is the absence of immunohistochemical staining and genetic testing, which could have further clarified the developmental origin of the lesion. Although histopathology confirmed the presence of ectodermal features, additional analyses such as cytokeratin profiling, TTF-1 staining, or evaluation for KRT17 mutations would have added greater mechanistic insight. These investigations could not be performed due to resource constraints and because the patient was not compliant with further diagnostic evaluations after postoperative recovery.

As this is a single case report, its generalizability remains limited. Future studies involving larger patient cohorts and advanced molecular techniques will be required to better understand the clinical implications and embryological basis of this unusual histological overlap.
