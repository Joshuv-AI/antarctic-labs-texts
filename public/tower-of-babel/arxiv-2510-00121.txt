On a comprehensive review of a proof of Löwner’s theorem
Curt Healey

Abstract
. Recent studies in Kubo-Ando theory make frequent use of the relationship between Kubo-Ando connections and positive operator monotone functions. This relationship is deeply connected to Löwner’s theorem and our aim is to provide a comprehensive review of one of the proofs of Löwner’s theorem. Our motivation arises from the fact that the foundational components upon which the theorem rests are found within a variety of sources, rendering it difficult to obtain a complete understanding of the proof without engaging in substantial external consultation. By consolidating these elements into a single, continuous account, the proof becomes substantially more accessible and may be assimilated with greater clarity and efficiency.

1.
Motivation

Recently, various problems regarding Kubo-Ando connections have been solved which were stated in
[
8
, Page 22]
. We recall that a binary operation
\sigma
on the positive definite cone
\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H})^{++}
of the algebra
\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H})
of bounded operators on the Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
, is called a
Kubo-Ando connection
if it satisfies the following properties:

(I)

If
A\leq C
and
B\leq D
, then
A\sigma B\leq C\sigma D
.

(II)

C(A\sigma B)C\leq(CAC)\sigma(CBC)
.

(III)

If
A_{n}\downarrow A
and
B_{n}\downarrow B
, then
A_{n}\sigma B_{n}\downarrow A\sigma B
(We write
A_{n}\downarrow A
when
(A_{n})
is monotonic decreasing and SOT-convergent to
A
).

Furthermore, In
[
7
, Theorem 3.2]
, it is shown that there is an affine order isomorphism from the class of Kubo-Ando connections onto the class of operator monotone functions via the map
f(xI)=I\sigma(xI)
for
x>0
. Since every finite Borel measure on
[0,\infty]
is regular (i.e a Radon measure), we have that operator monotone functions correspond to positive finite Borel measures on
[0,\infty]
by Löwner’s Theorem (see
[
3
]
): To every operator monotone function
f
corresponds a unique positive and finite Borel measure
m
on
[0,\infty]
such that

(1)

f(x)=\,\int_{[0,\infty]}\frac{x(1+t)}{x+t}\,{\rm d}m(t)\,=\,m(\{0\})\,+\,x\,m(\{\infty\})\,+\,\int_{(0,\infty)}\frac{1+t}{t}(t:x)\,{\rm d}m(t)\quad(x>0).

It is easy to see that
f(0+)=m(\{0\})
,
f^{\circ}(0+)=m(\{\infty\})
.
Finally, by
[
7
, Theorem 3.4]
, there is an affine isomorphism from the class of positive finite Borel measures on
[0,\infty]
onto the class of Kubo-Ando connections. This is given by the formula

(2)

A\sigma B=\alpha A+\beta B+\int_{(0,\infty)}\frac{1+t}{t}(tA:B)\,{\rm d}m(t)\quad A,B\in\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H})^{+}

where
\alpha=m(\{0\})
and
\beta=m(\{\infty\})
.
In the case of a symmetric Kubo-Ando connection,
\alpha=\beta
. For further details on the provenance of the integral representation (
2
), the reader is referred to
[
7
, Theorem 3.2]
.

The connection is clear and we give here a comprehensive review of the proof for Löwner’s theorem which is found in
[
4
]
. The original exposition assumes a working knowledge of both Choquet theory and matrix analysis, which may pose a challenge to readers unfamiliar with these areas. To improve accessibility and facilitate a deeper understanding of the proof, we have chosen to include not only the theorem itself but also the necessary foundational concepts. By consolidating the background material and the proof within a single, self-contained narrative, we aim to streamline the learning process and reduce the need for external references. This integrated approach allows the reader to follow the logical structure of the argument more easily, appreciate the interplay between different mathematical tools, and develop a more intuitive grasp of the theorem’s significance and underlying principles.

2.
Choquet Theory

2.1.
Introduction

In this section we develop Choquet theory for metrizable spaces. Let us first provide some motivation for the theory by stating some results regarding convex compact subsets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We give here Carathéodory’s and Minkowski’s Theorems which can be found in
[
9

and
[

Theorem 1

(Carathéodory)
.

Suppose that
E
is a subset of a linear space
X
and its convex hull
\textnormal{co}(E)
has dimension
n
. Then each point
x\in\textnormal{co}(E)
is the convex combination of at most
n+1
points of
E
.

Theorem 2

(Minkowski)
.

Every nonempty convex and compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the convex hull of its extreme points.

Therefore, by combining the above two theorems, we can arrive at the conclusion that every point of a convex compact subset
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be written as a convex combination of its extreme points. Let
\textnormal{Ext}(K)=\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n+1}
denote the extreme points of
K
. If
x\in K
, then
x=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}e_{i}\lambda_{i}
where
\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\lambda_{i}=1
and
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
for each

. Suppose that to each extreme point
e_{i}
we associate the nonnegative regular Borel measure
\mu_{e_{i}}
defined by

\mu_{e_{i}}(S)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }e_{i}\in S\\
0&\text{if }e_{i}\notin S\end{cases},

where
S\subseteq K
is a Borel set. Let
\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\mu_{e_{i}}\lambda_{i}
which is also a nonnegative regular Borel measure such that
\mu(K)=1
. Since
\mu(\{e_{i}\})=\lambda_{i}
for any
1\leq i\leq n+1
, we have,
x=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\mu(\{e_{i}\})e_{i}
, and if
\rho
is a continuous linear functional on
K
, then
\rho(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\mu(\{e_{i}\})\rho(e_{i})=\int_{Ext(K)}\rho\,\rm{d}\mu
. Let us now define what we mean when we say that
\mu

represents

x

[
10
, Pg 1 Definition]
.

Definition 3
.

Suppose that
X
is a nonempty compact subset of a locally convex space

, and that
\mu
is a probability measure on

.
(
That is
\mu
is a regular Borel measure on

)

Let us now define what we mean when we say that a nonnegative regular Borel measure

If

Let

Theorem 7

If

Let us next recall the definitions for affine, convex, and concave functions.

Definition 8
.

Let
V
be a vector space and
C\subseteq V
a convex set such that
\lambda\in(0,1)
and

. Then

f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)=\lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f(y)

-f

Consider the set of affine functions
\mathscr{A}\subseteq C(X)

X

. Naturally,
\mathscr{A}
is a subspace of the Banach space
C(X)
. Furthermore, since
\mathscr{A}
contains all functions of the form
x\rightarrow\rho(x)+r
on
X
where
\rho
is a continuous real-valued linear functional on
X
and
r\in\mathbb{R}
, then
\mathscr{A}
separates
X

[
6
, Corollary 1.2.12]
. Let us first define what we mean by the
upper envelope
of
f
.

Definition 9
.

Let
X
be a convex subset of a locally convex space
E
and
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a bounded function. Let
\mathscr{A}
denote the set of continuous affine functions on
X
. The upper envelope of

is
\bar{f}(x)=\textnormal{inf}\{h(x):h\in\mathscr{A}\;\textnormal{and}\;h\geq f\}
where
x\in X
.

Since constant functions are elements of
\mathscr{A}
and the upper envelope is defined on bounded functions, then the upper envelope is well-defined. Let us go through some properties of the upper envelope
[
10
, Page 13]
.

Proposition 10
.

Let
X
be a subset of a locally convex space
E
and
f,g:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be bounded functions. Let
\mathscr{A}
denote the set of continuous affine functions on
X
. Then, the upper envelope satisfies the following properties.

(I)

\overline{f}
is bounded, concave, upper semicontinuous
(
hence Borel measurable
)
, and
f\leq\overline{f}
.

(II)

\overline{f+g}\leq\overline{f}+\overline{g}
, and
\alpha\geq 0
implies
\overline{\alpha f}=\alpha\overline{f}
.

(III)

If
g\in\mathscr{A}
, then
\overline{f+g}=\overline{f}+g
.

(IV)

If
f
is concave and upper semicontinuous, then
f=\overline{f}.

Proof.

(I)
. We shall only prove that
\overline{f}
is upper semicontinuous. Consider
x\in\overline{f}^{-1}(-\infty,r)
where
r\in\mathbb{R}
. There exists
g\in\mathscr{A}
such that
g\geq f
and
\overline{f}(x)<g(x)<r
. Therefore,
x\in g^{-1}(-\infty,r)
and
g^{-1}(-\infty,r)\subseteq\overline{f}^{-1}(-\infty,r)
. By continuity of
g
, there is an open set
O\subseteq\mathbb{R}
such that
x\in O\subseteq\overline{f}^{-1}(-\infty,r)
. Therefore,
\overline{f}^{-1}(-\infty,r)
is open for any
r\in\mathbb{R}
which implies that
\overline{f}
is upper semicontinuous.

(II)
. Let
x\in X
. If
f(x)\leq h_{1}(x)
and
g(x)\leq h_{2}(x)
, then
f(x)+g(x)\leq h_{1}(x)+h_{2}(x)
. Therefore,
\{h(x):h\geq f,h\in\mathscr{A}\}+\{h(x):h\geq g,h\in\mathscr{A}\}

\subseteq\{h(x):h\geq f+g,\,h\in\mathscr{A}\}
. By taking the infimum, we can conclude that
\overline{f+g}(x)\leq\overline{f}(x)+\overline{g}(x)
for all
x\in X
. This proves the first part of the result.

Let
\alpha\geq 0
. Then
\{h(x):h\geq\alpha f,\,h\in\mathscr{A}\}=\{\alpha h(x):h\geq f,\,h\in\mathscr{A}\}
. By taking the infimum, we can conclude that
\overline{\alpha f}(x)=\alpha\overline{f}(x)
for all
x\in X
.

(III)
. Since
g\in\mathscr{A}
, we have
\overline{g}=g
. By (II),
\overline{f+g}\leq\overline{f}+g
. Moreover,
f+g\leq\overline{f+g}
implies that
\overline{f}\leq\overline{f+g}-\overline{g}
. Therefore,
\overline{f}+g\leq\overline{f+g}
which allows us to conclude that
\overline{f+g}=\overline{f}+g
.

(IV)
Consider
K\subseteq X\times\mathbb{R}
such that
K=\{\{x,r\}:f(x)\geq r\}
. Let
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
,
\lambda\in(0,1)
, and
r_{1},r_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
f(\lambda x_{1}+(1-\lambda)x_{2})\geq\lambda f(x_{1})+(1-\lambda)f(x_{2})\geq\lambda r_{1}+(1-\lambda)r_{2}
, we have that
K
is convex. Since the hypograph of an upper semicontinuous function is closed, it follows that
K
is closed. Suppose that
f(x_{0})<\overline{f}(x_{0})
for some
x_{0}\in X
. By
[
6
, Corollary 1.2.12]
, we can find a continuous linear functional
\rho
such that
\text{sup}\,\rho(K)<\lambda<\rho(\{x_{0},\overline{f}(x_{0})\})
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
\rho(\{x_{0},f(x_{0})\})<\rho(\{x_{0},\overline{f}(x_{0})\})
implies that
(\overline{f}(x_{0})-f(x_{0}))\rho(\{0,1\})>0
, it follows that
\rho(\{0,1\})>0
. Let us define a continuous affine function
h:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

h(x)=\frac{\lambda-\rho(\{x,0\})}{\rho(\{0,1\})},\quad\forall\,x\in X.

Rearranging the terms allows us to conclude that
\rho(\{x,h(x)\})=\lambda
. Furthermore, since
\rho(\{0,1\})>0
, we have

(3)

\begin{split}\rho(\{x,f(x)\})<\lambda=\rho(\{x,h(x)\})&\implies 0<(h(x)-f(x))\rho(\{0,1\}),\quad\forall\,x\in X\\
&\implies 0<h(x)-f(x),\quad\forall\,x\in X\\
&\implies\overline{f}(x)\leq h(x),\quad\forall\,x\in X.\end{split}

Similarly,

\rho(\{x_{0},h(x_{0})\})=\lambda<\rho(\{x_{0},\overline{f}(x_{0})\})\implies h(x_{0})<\overline{f}(x_{0}).

However, this contradicts (
3
), so
\overline{f}=f
.
∎

Lemma 11
.

Suppose that
X
is a metrizable compact convex subset of a locally convex space E. Then there exists a strictly continuous convex function
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{A}\subseteq C(X)
be the set of affine functions. Since
X
is metrizable and compact, then
C(X)
is separable by Theorem
7
. Consider

\mathscr{A}_{1}=\{f\in C(X,\mathbb{R}):f\in\mathscr{A},\left\|f\right\|=1\}.

Since
\mathscr{A}_{1}
is separable, there exists a countable dense subset
D_{1}=\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathscr{A}_{1}
. We shall construct a strictly convex function from
D_{1}
. Let
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
such that
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
. By
[
6
, Corollary 1.2.11]
, there exists a continuous real-valued linear functional
\rho
on
E
with
\left\|\rho\right\|=1
s.t
|\rho(x_{1})-\rho(x_{2})|>\epsilon
for some
\epsilon>0
. Since
\rho_{|X}\in\mathscr{A}_{1}
, there is some
f_{i}\in D_{1}
for some
i\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\left\|\rho_{|X}-f_{i}\right\|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{3}
. Therefore,

\begin{split}|\rho_{|X}(x_{1})-\rho_{|X}(x_{2})|&\leq|\rho_{|X}(x_{1})-f_{i}(x_{1})|+|f_{i}(x_{1})-f_{i}(x_{2})|+|f_{i}(x_{2})-\rho_{|X}(x_{2})|\\
&\leq\frac{2\epsilon}{3}+|f_{i}(x_{1})-f_{i}(x_{2})|.\end{split}

Thus,

(4)

|f_{i}(x_{1})-f_{i}(x_{2})|>\epsilon/3.

Let
f=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}f_{n}^{2}
and let us now show that this series is Cauchy. If
N_{1},N_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
and
N_{2}\geq N_{1}
, then

\left\|\sum_{n=N_{1}}^{N_{2}}2^{-n}f_{n}^{2}\right\|\leq\sum_{n=N_{1}}^{N_{2}}2^{-n}\left\|f_{n}^{2}\right\|\leq\sum_{n=N_{1}}^{N_{2}}2^{-n}.

Since
C(X)
is complete, we have
f\in C(X)
. Let
\lambda\in(0,1)
. By (

f_{i}(x_{1})\neq f_{i}(x_{2})
and using the strict convexity of the squared function, then

\begin{split}f_{i}^{2}(\lambda x_{1}+(1-\lambda x_{2}))&=(\lambda f_{i}(x_{1})+(1-\lambda)f_{i}(x_{2}))^{2}\\
&<\lambda f_{i}^{2}(x_{1})+(1-\lambda)f_{i}^{2}(x_{2}).\end{split}

Since
f_{n}^{2}(\lambda x_{1}+(1-\lambda x_{2}))\leq\lambda f_{n}^{2}(x_{1})+(1-\lambda)f_{n}^{2}(x_{2})
for any

is strictly convex.

Theorem 13

(Choquet)
.

Suppose that
X
is a metrizable compact convex subset of a locally convex space E, and that
x_{0}
is an element of
X
. Then there is a probability measure
\mu
on
X
which represents
x_{0}
and is supported by the extreme points of
X
.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{A}\subseteq C(X,\mathbb{R})
be the set of affine functions. By Lemma
11
, there exists a strictly convex function
f\in C(X,\mathbb{R})
. Let
\phi:C(X,\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a functional such that
\phi(g)=\overline{g}(x_{0})
for
g\in C(X,\mathbb{R})
. Then by (I) of Proposition
10
,
\phi
is subadditive. On the other hand, let
\tau
be defined on the subspace
\mathscr{A}+\mathbb{R}f
of
C(X,\mathbb{R})
such that

\tau(h+rf)=h(x_{0})+r\overline{f}(x_{0}),\quad h\in\mathscr{A},r\in\mathbb{R}.

We shall show that
\tau
is dominated by
\phi
so that we can extend
\tau
to
C(X,\mathbb{R})
by the Hahn-Banach theorem . If
r_{1}\geq 0
and
h\in\mathscr{A}
, then
\tau(h+r_{1}f)=h(x_{0})+r_{1}\overline{f}(x_{0})=h(x_{0})+\overline{r_{1}f}(x_{0})=\overline{h+r_{1}f}(x_{0})=\phi(h+r_{1}f)
. If
r_{2}<0
. Then
r_{2}f
is concave. Therefore,
\tau(h+r_{2}f)=h(x_{0})+r_{2}\overline{f}(x_{0})\leq h(x_{0})+r_{2}f(x_{0})=h(x_{0})+\overline{r_{2}f}(x_{0})=\overline{h+r_{2}f}(x_{0})=\phi(r+\alpha f)

\tau
can be extended to a linear functional
\rho_{\tau}
on
C(X,\mathbb{R})
.

Let
l\in C(X,\mathbb{R})
be such that
l\leq 0
, then
\phi(l)=\overline{l}(x_{0})\leq 0
which implies
\rho_{\tau}(l)\leq\phi(l)\leq 0
, so
\rho_{\tau}
is a positive linear functional. By using the Riesz Representation Theorem for
C(X,\mathbb{R})
, we obtain a Radon measure
\mu
such that
\rho_{\tau}(f)=\int_{X}f\,{\rm d}\mu
. Moreover,
1=\rho_{\tau}(1)=\mu(X)
, so
\mu
is a probability measure. Furthermore, if
\sigma
is a continuous linear functional on
E

\sigma_{|X}\in\mathscr{A}
which implies that
\int_{X}\sigma\,{\rm d}\mu=\rho_{\tau}(\sigma)=\tau(\sigma)=\sigma(x_{0})
. Therefore,
\mu
represents
x_{0}
.

We claim this is supported by the set
F=\{x:f(x)=\overline{f}(x)\}
which we show is a subset of the extreme points of
X
. Since
f
and
\overline{f}
are measurable, it follows that
F
is a Borel set. Let
x\in F

\lambda\in(0,1)
, such that
x=\lambda y+(1-\lambda)z
and
y\neq z
. Since
f
is strictly convex, we have

f(x)<\lambda f(y)+(1-\lambda)f(z)\leq\overline{f}(\lambda y+(1-\lambda)z)=\overline{f}(x),

which is a contradiction, so
x
is an extreme point of
X
. Let us now show that
\mu(X/F)=0
. Since
f\leq\overline{f}
, then
\rho_{\tau}(f)\leq\rho_{\tau}(\overline{f})
. Recall that
h\in\mathscr{A}
and suppose that
h\geq f
. Therefore,
h\geq\overline{f}
and
h(x_{0})=\rho_{\tau}(h)\geq\rho_{\tau}(\overline{f})
. Hence,

\int_{X/F}(\overline{f}-f)\,{\rm d}\mu=0\implies\mu(X/F)=0,

\mu

3.
Operator Monotonicity And Divided Differences

In this section, we introduce operator monotone functions and their relationship with operator convex functions. Specifically, we demonstrate that if
f
is operator convex and twice continuously differentiable on
(a,b)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, then

(5)

F_{t_{1}}(t)=\begin{cases}\frac{f(t)-f(t_{1})}{t-t_{1}}&t\neq t_{1}\\
f^{\prime}(t_{1})&t=t_{1}\end{cases}

is operator monotone on
(a,b)
for
t_{1}\in(a,b)
. For notational convenience, we denote by
L_{t_{1}}
the transformation

(6)

L_{t_{1}}:f\rightarrow F_{t_{1}}.

3.1.
Operator Monotone and Operator Convex functions

We shall define what we mean by operator monotone and operator convex/concave functions and we show that if
f
is operator monotone, then
f
is operator concave. Let us first define what we mean by operator monotone and operator convex of order
n
where
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Definition 14
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. A function
f:(a,b)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
on
(a,b)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is said to be operator monotone of order
n
if for any
A\leq B
, then
f(A)\leq f(B)
where
A,B
are
n\times n
Hermitian matrices and
\textnormal{sp}(A)\cup\textnormal{sp}(B)\subseteq(a,b)
.

Definition 15
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. A function
f:(a,b)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
on
(a,b)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is said to be operator convex of order
n
if

f(tA+(1-t)B)\leq tf(A)+(1-t)f(B),\quad\forall\,t\in[0,1]

where
A,B
are elements of a
n\times n
Hermitian matrices, and
\textnormal{sp}(A)\cup\textnormal{sp}(B)\subseteq(a,b)
. We say that
f
is operator concave if
-f
is operator convex.

Note that for any
t\in[0,1]
,
\text{sp}(tA+(1-t)B)\subseteq(a,b)
which follows from the fact that

\textnormal{min}(\textnormal{sp}(A)\cup\textnormal{sp}(B))I\leq tA+(1-t)B\leq\textnormal{max}(\textnormal{sp}(A)\cup\textnormal{sp}(B))I.

This brings us to the first result
[
4
, Theorem 2.1]
.

Theorem 16
.

If a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is operator monotone of order
2n
, then
f
is operator concave of order
n
. In particular, since
f
is scalar concave on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
, it follows that
f
is continuous.

Proof.

Let
A,B
be
n\times n
Hermitian matrices with spectrum in
\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Let
\lambda\in[0,1]
. Denote the
n\times n
identity matrix by
\mathbb{I}_{n}
and the
n\times n
zero matrix by
0_{n}
. Consider the
2n\times 2n
unitary matrix
U
such that

U=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^{1/2}\mathbb{I}_{n}&-(1-\lambda)^{1/2}\mathbb{I}_{n}\\
(1-\lambda)^{1/2}\mathbb{I}_{n}&\lambda^{1/2}\mathbb{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle U^{*}\begin{pmatrix}A&0_{n}\\
0_{n}&B\end{pmatrix}U

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^{1/2}\mathbb{I}_{n}&(1-\lambda)^{1/2}\mathbb{I}_{n}\\
-(1-\lambda)^{1/2}\mathbb{I}_{n}&\lambda^{1/2}\mathbb{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle*\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^{1/2}A&-(1-\lambda)^{1/2}A\\
(1-\lambda)^{1/2}B&\lambda^{1/2}B\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda A+(1-\lambda)B&\lambda^{1/2}(1-\lambda)^{1/2}(B-A)\\
\lambda^{1/2}(1-\lambda)^{1/2}(B-A)&(1-\lambda)A+\lambda B\end{pmatrix}.

Let
C=-\lambda^{1/2}(1-\lambda)^{1/2}(B-A),t\geq\max\{\left\|A\right\|,\left\|B\right\|\}
and
\epsilon>0
. It is clear that
t\geq\left\|(1-\lambda)A+\lambda B\right\|
which implies that
2t-\left\|(1-\lambda)A+\lambda B\right\|\geq t
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\lambda A+(1-\lambda)B+\epsilon\mathbb{I}_{n}&0_{n}\\
0_{n}&2t\mathbb{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}-U^{*}\begin{pmatrix}A&0_{n}\\
0_{n}&B\end{pmatrix}U\geq\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon\mathbb{I}_{n}&C\\
C&t\mathbb{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}=\mathscr{C}_{\epsilon,t}.

By using Schur complements, we will establish necessary conditions for
\mathscr{C}_{\epsilon,t}
to be positive semi-definite.

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}_{n}&t^{-1}C\\
0_{n}&\mathbb{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon\mathbb{I}_{n}-t^{-1}C^{2}&0_{n}\\
0_{n}&t\mathbb{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}_{n}&t^{-1}C\\
0_{n}&\mathbb{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}^{*}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}_{n}&t^{-1}C\\
0_{n}&\mathbb{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon\mathbb{I}_{n}-t^{-1}C^{2}&0_{n}\\
0_{n}&t\mathbb{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}_{n}&0_{n}\\
t^{-1}C&\mathbb{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}_{n}&t^{-1}C\\
0_{n}&\mathbb{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon\mathbb{I}_{n}-t^{-1}C^{2}&0_{n}\\
C&t\mathbb{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon\mathbb{I}_{n}&C\\
C&t\mathbb{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore, if
\epsilon\mathbb{I}_{n}-t^{-1}C^{2}\geq 0
or
\epsilon\geq t^{-1}\left\|C\right\|^{2}
, then
\mathscr{C}_{\epsilon,t}
is positive semi-definite. If
t
is large enough to also satisfy this inequality, we obtain

(7)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\lambda A+(1-\lambda)B+\epsilon\mathbb{I}_{n}&0_{n}\\
0_{n}&2t\mathbb{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}\geq U^{*}\begin{pmatrix}A&0_{n}\\
0_{n}&B\end{pmatrix}U.

Since the spectrum of
A,B
consists of a finite number of points, then we can use polynomial interpolation to write
f
as a polynomial. Then for any unitary matrix
U
,
f(U^{*}AU)=U^{*}f(A)U
. Moreover, since for any block diagonal matrix

\begin{pmatrix}A&0_{n}\\
0_{n}&B\end{pmatrix}^{k}=\begin{pmatrix}A^{k}&0_{n}\\
0_{n}&B^{k}\end{pmatrix},

we can conclude that

f\begin{pmatrix}A&0_{n}\\
0_{n}&B\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}f(A)&0_{n}\\
0_{n}&f(B)\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore,

(8)

\begin{split}&f\bigg(U^{*}\begin{pmatrix}A&0_{n}\\
0_{n}&B\end{pmatrix}U\bigg)=U^{*}\begin{pmatrix}f(A)&0_{n}\\
0_{n}&f(B)\end{pmatrix}U\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda f(A)+(1-\lambda)f(B)&\lambda^{1/2}(1-\lambda)^{1/2}(f(B)-f(A))\\
\lambda^{1/2}(1-\lambda)^{1/2}(f(B)-f(A))&(1-\lambda)f(A)+\lambda f(B)\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

By combining (
7
) and (
8
) and using the fact that
f
is operator monotone of order
2n
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\lambda f(A)+(1-\lambda)f(B)&\lambda^{1/2}(1-\lambda)^{1/2}(f(B)-f(A))\\
\lambda^{1/2}(1-\lambda)^{1/2}(f(B)-f(A))&(1-\lambda)f(A)+\lambda f(B)\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\leq\begin{pmatrix}f(\lambda A+(1-\lambda)B+\epsilon\mathbb{I}_{n})&0_{n}\\
0_{n}&f(2t\mathbb{I}_{n})\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore,

(9)

\lambda f(A)+(1-\lambda)f(B)\leq f(\lambda A+(1-\lambda)B+\epsilon\mathbb{I}_{n}),\quad\forall\,\epsilon>0.

Let
x_{1},x_{2}>0
. Since
f(\delta\mathbb{I}_{n})=f(\delta)\mathbb{I}_{n}
for any
\delta>0
, the above inequality implies that

(10)

\displaystyle\lambda f(x_{1})+(1-\lambda)f(x_{2})\leq f(\lambda x_{1}+(1-\lambda)x_{2}+\epsilon),\quad\forall\,\epsilon>0.

Since
f
is monotone, we can define the right hand limit
f^{+}
by setting

f^{+}(x)=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0+}f(x+\epsilon),\quad x>0.

By definition of
f^{+}
, we have

(11)

\lambda f^{+}(x_{1})+(1-\lambda)f^{+}(x_{2})\leq\lambda f(x_{1}+\epsilon)+(1-\lambda)f(x_{2}+\epsilon),\quad\forall\,\epsilon>0.

By combining, (
10
) and (
11
), we can conclude that

\lambda f^{+}(x_{1})+(1-\lambda)f^{+}(x_{2})\leq f(\lambda x_{1}+(1-\lambda)x_{2}+3\epsilon),\quad\forall\,\epsilon>0.

As
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
in the above, we obtain

\lambda f^{+}(x_{1})+(1-\lambda)f^{+}(x_{2})\leq f^{+}(\lambda x_{1}+(1-\lambda)x_{2}).

Therefore,
f^{+}
is concave on
(0,\infty)
which implies it is continuous. Since
f
is monotone increasing, it follows that

f^{+}(t-\epsilon)\leq f(t)\leq f^{+}(t),\quad 0<\epsilon<t.

As
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
in the above, we obtain
f^{+}=f
which implies that
f
is continuous. By
[
6
, Proposition 5.3.2]
,
f
is s.o.t continuous on bounded sets of self-adjoint operators, so as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
in (
9
),
\lambda f(A)+(1-\lambda)f(B)\leq f(\lambda A+(1-\lambda)B)
.
∎

Let us now define what we mean by an operator monotone function.

Definition 17
.

A function
f:(a,b)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
on
(a,b)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is said to be operator monotone if for any
A\leq B
, then
f(A)\leq f(B)
where
A,B
are elements of a
C^{*}
-algebra, and
\textnormal{sp}(A)\cup\textnormal{sp}(B)\subseteq(a,b)
.

It is natural to ask whether operator monotone functions can be characterized by being operator monotone of order
n
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}

[
1
, Theorem 2.5]
.

Theorem 18
.

A function
f:(a,b)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
on
(a,b)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is operator monotone iff
f
is operator monotone of order
n
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Suppose
f
is operator monotone. Since an
n\times n
Hermitian matrix is a bounded operator from
\mathbb{C}^{n}
to
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, then it is clear that an operator monotone function is operator monotone of order
n
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Suppose
f
is operator monotone of order
n
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
A\leq B
and
\textnormal{sp}(A)\cup\textnormal{sp}(B)\subseteq(a,b)
. Consider the map
\alpha:\mathscr{H}\rightarrow\mathscr{H}
such that

\alpha(\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\})=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},Ax_{1},\ldots,Ax_{n},Bx_{1},\ldots,Bx_{n}\}.

Let
D=\{\alpha(\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}):\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\subseteq\mathscr{H}\}
be a directed set under the usual order of subset inclusion. Consider the map
\gamma:D\rightarrow

\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H})
such that
\gamma(d)=P_{d}
where
P_{d}
is the projection on the subspace generated by
d\in D
.
Naturally,
P_{\gamma(d)}AP_{\gamma(d)}\leq P_{\gamma(d)}BP_{\gamma(d)}
. Furthermore, since
P_{\gamma(d)}B(\mathscr{H})P_{{\gamma(d)}}
is
\ast
-isomorphic to a finite direct sum of matrix algebras over
\mathbb{C}
, we have

(12)

f(P_{\gamma(d)}AP_{\gamma(d)})\leq f(P_{\gamma(d)}BP_{\gamma(d)}).

It is clear that
\displaystyle{\lim_{d\in D}P_{\gamma(d)}AP_{\gamma(d)}\rightarrow A}
and
\displaystyle{\lim_{d\in D}P_{\gamma(d)}BP_{\gamma(d)}\rightarrow B}
in s.o.t.
By Theorem
16
and
[
6
, Proposition 5.3.2]
,
f
is s.o.t continuous on bounded sets of self-adjoint operators, so
f(A)\leq f(B)
as we take limits in (
12
).
∎

This leads us to defining operator convex functions.

Definition 19
.

A function
f:(a,b)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
on
(a,b)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is said to be operator convex if

f(tA+(1-t)B)\leq tf(A)+(1-t)f(B),\quad\forall\,t\in[0,1]

where
A,B
are elements of a
C^{*}
-algebra, and
\textnormal{sp}(A)\cup\textnormal{sp}(B)\subseteq(a,b)
. Furthermore,
f
is said to be operator concave if
-f
is operator convex.

Similar to Theorem
18
, we have the following theorem
[
1
, Lemma 3.1]
.

Theorem 20
.

A continuous function
f:(a,b)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
on
(a,b)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is operator convex iff
f
is operator convex of order
n
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

The proof is omitted due to its similarity with the proof of Theorem
18
. Finally, we arrive at the theorem we set out to prove in this subsection.

Theorem 21
.

If a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is operator monotone, then
f
is operator concave.

Proof.

If
f
is operator monotone, then
f
is operator monotone of order
n
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
by Theorem
18
. By Theorem
16
,
f
is operator concave of order
n
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Consequently, Theorem
20
allows us to conclude that
f
is operator concave.
∎

3.2.
The Chain Rule and Divided Differences

The goal of this section is to use the chain rule for matrices and the concept of divided differences to show that if
f:(a,b)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
on
(a,b)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is operator convex and twice continuously differentiable, then
L_{t_{1}}f
(recall (
6
)) is operator monotone on
(a,b)
for
t_{1}\in(a,b)
. Let
f:(a,b)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be continuously differentiable and
t_{2}\in(a,b)
. Then the first divided difference
\Delta f(t_{1},t_{2})
is defined as

\Delta f(t_{1},t_{2})=\begin{cases}\frac{f(t_{2})-f(t_{1})}{t_{2}-t_{1}}&t_{1}\neq t_{2}\\
f^{\prime}(t_{1})&t_{1}=t_{2}\end{cases}.

Let
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\}\subseteq(a,b)
. Then we can define the corresponding
n\times n

matrix of first divided differences
as

[\Delta f(t_{i},t_{j})]_{i,j=1}^{n}=\begin{bmatrix}\Delta f(t_{1},t_{1})&\ldots&\Delta f(t_{1},t_{n})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\Delta f(t_{n},t_{1})&\ldots&\Delta f(t_{n},t_{n})\end{bmatrix}.

It can be easily seen that the above matrix is symmetric. In addition, we shall also consider the second divided difference. Let
f:(a,b)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a twice continuously differentiable function on
(a,b)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. Let
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}\in(a,b)
be distinct. Then the second divided difference is defined as

(13)

\Delta^{2}f(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})=\frac{\Delta f(t_{3},t_{1})-\Delta f(t_{2},t_{1})}{t_{3}-t_{2}}.

For repeated values,
\Delta^{2}f(t_{1},t_{2},t_{2})=\lim_{t\rightarrow t_{2}}\Delta^{2}f(t_{1},t_{2},t)
. In particular,

\Delta^{2}f(t_{1},t_{1},t_{1})=\lim_{t\rightarrow t_{1}}\Delta^{2}f(t_{1},t_{2},t)\\
=\lim_{s\rightarrow t_{1}}\lim_{t\rightarrow t_{1}}\Delta^{2}f(t_{1},s,t).

This leads us to the chain rule for matrices
[
5
, Theorem 6.6.30]
. However, we require the following clarifications first.

(I)

The derivative of an
n\times n
matrix is the entrywise derivative.

(II)

H_{n}(a,b)
denotes the set of
n\times n
Hermitian matrices with spectrum in the open real interval
(a,b)
.

(III)

Let
A,B
be arbitrary
n\times n
matrices. Then
A\circ B
denotes the Schur product (or entrywise multiplication).

Theorem 22

(Chain rule)
.

Let
f:(a,b)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\gamma:(c,d)\rightarrow H_{n}(a,b)
be functions on
(a,b),(c,d)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
such that
\textnormal{sp}(\gamma(t))\subseteq(a,b)
for all
t\in(c,d)
. Let
\gamma(t)=U(t)D(t)U(t)^{*}
, where
U
is a unitary matrix, and
D=\textnormal{diag}(\lambda_{1}(t),\ldots,\lambda_{n}(t))
for all
t\in(c,d)
. Then, the following hold

(I)

If
f
and
\gamma
are continuously differentiable, then
f\circ\gamma
is continuously differentiable with derivative

\frac{d}{dt}f(\gamma(t))=U(t)\bigg([\Delta f(\lambda_{i}(t),\lambda_{j}(t))]_{i,j=1}^{n}\circ[U(t)^{*}\gamma^{\prime}(t)U(t)]\bigg)U(t)^{*}

for all
t\in(c,d)
.

(II)

If
f
and
\gamma
are twice continuously differentiable, then
f\circ\gamma
is twice continuously differentiable. Let
c_{k}(t)
denote the
k
’th column of
U(t)^{*}\gamma^{\prime}(t)U(t)
, then the second derivative of
f\circ\gamma
is

\begin{split}\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}f(\gamma(t))=U(t)&\bigg(2\sum_{k=1}^{N}[\Delta^{2}f(\lambda_{i}(t),\lambda_{j}(t),\lambda_{k}(t))]^{n}_{i,j=1}\circ(c_{k}(t)c_{k}^{*}(t))\\
&+[\Delta f(\lambda_{i}(t),\lambda_{j}(t))]_{i,j=1}^{n}\circ(U(t)^{*}\gamma^{\prime\prime}(t)U(t))\bigg)U(t)^{*}\end{split}

for all
t\in(c,d)
.

An application of the chain rule allows us to characterize operator monotone functions of order
n
. This is given in the next theorem
[
5
, Thoerem 6.6.36]
.

Theorem 23
.

Let
f:(a,b)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a continuously differentiable function on
(a,b)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. Then
f
is operator monotone of order
n
iff

[\Delta f(t_{i},t_{j})]_{i,j=1}^{n}\geq 0,\quad\forall\,t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\in(a,b).

Proof.

Suppose the matrix of first divided differences is positive semi-definite. Let
A,B\in H_{n}(a,b)
and
A\leq B
. Let
\epsilon_{1}>0
such that
\text{sp}((1-t)A+tB)\subseteq(a,b)
for
t\in(-\epsilon_{1},1+\epsilon_{1})
. Consider
\gamma(t)=(1-t)A+tB
for
t\in(-\epsilon_{1},1+\epsilon_{1})
. Furthermore,
\gamma^{\prime}(t)=B-A
for
t\in(-\epsilon_{1},1+\epsilon_{1})
, so by (I) of Theorem
22
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&f(B)-f(A)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}f(\gamma(t))\,\rm{d}t\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}U(t)\bigg([\Delta f(\lambda_{i}(t),\lambda_{j}(t))]_{i,j=1}^{n}\circ(U(t)^{*}(B-A)U(t))\bigg)U(t)^{*}\,dt.\end{split}

Since
[\Delta f(\lambda_{i}(t),\lambda_{j}(t))]_{i,j=1}^{n}\geq 0
and
U(t)^{*}(B-A)U(t)\geq 0
, the Schur product is positive semi-definite as well. This implies that the integral is the limit of sums of positive semi-definite matrices, so
f(A)\leq f(B)
.

Suppose
f
is operator monotone of order
n
. Let
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and let
D=\text{diag}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
where
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\}\subseteq(a,b)
. Let
\epsilon_{2}>0
such that
A=D-\epsilon_{2}(xx^{*})
and
B=D+\epsilon_{2}(xx^{*})
are elements of
H_{n}(a,b)
. Let
\gamma(t)=(1-t)A+tB
for
t\in(0,1)
. Let us now apply (I) of Theorem
22
for
f^{\prime}(\gamma(1/2))
. Since
\gamma(1/2)
is a diagonal matrix, it follows that
U(1/2)=\mathbb{I}_{n}
. Furthermore,
\gamma^{{}^{\prime}}(1/2)=2\epsilon_{2}(xx^{*})
. These two observations imply that

(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(1/2)=\frac{d}{dt}f(\gamma(t))|_{t=1/2}=[\Delta f(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j})]_{i,j=1}^{n}\circ 2\epsilon_{2}(xx^{*}).

Moreover, if
t\geq s
for
t,s\in(0,1)
, then

\gamma(t)-\gamma(s)=(1-t)A+tB-(1-s)A+sB=(t-s)(B-A).

However,
B-A=2\epsilon_{2}xx^{*}
is positive semi-definite since
\langle(xx^{*})y,y\rangle=y^{*}xx^{*}y=(y^{*}x)(y^{*}x)^{*}=|y^{*}x|^{2}
. Thus,
\gamma(t)\geq\gamma(s)
and since
f
is operator monotone of order
n
, then
f(\gamma(t))\geq f(\gamma(s))
. This allows us to conclude that

\frac{d}{dt}f(\gamma(t))|_{t=1/2}=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0+}\frac{f(\gamma(1/2+h))-f(\gamma(1/2))}{h}

is positive semi-definite. Consider
y=(1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

(14)

0\leq\bigg\langle\frac{(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(1/2)}{2\epsilon_{2}}y,y\bigg\rangle.

Computing
y^{*}([\Delta f(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j})]_{i,j=1}^{n}\circ(xx^{*}))y
, we get

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1,\ldots,1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\Delta f(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1})x_{1}x^{*}_{1}&\Delta f(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})x_{1}x^{*}_{2}&\ldots&\Delta f(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{n})x_{1}x^{*}_{n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\Delta f(\lambda_{n},\lambda_{1})x_{n}x^{*}_{1}&\Delta f(\lambda_{n},\lambda_{2})x_{n}x^{*}_{2}&\ldots&\Delta f(\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n})x_{n}x^{*}_{n}\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\vdots\\
1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1,\ldots,1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta f(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{i})x^{*}_{i}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta f(\lambda_{n},\lambda_{i})x^{*}_{i}\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}x^{*}_{j}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta f(\lambda_{j},\lambda_{i})x_{i}

\displaystyle=\langle[\Delta f(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j})]_{i,j=1}^{n}x,x\rangle.

Therefore, by combining the above and (
14
), we conclude that

[\Delta f(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j})]_{i,j=1}^{n}\circ(xx^{*})

is positive semi-definite.
∎

Let us now proceed to prove a relationship between the matrix of second divided differences and convex matrix functions
[
5
, Page 544]
.

Lemma 24
.

Let
f:(a,b)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a twice continuously differentiable function on
(a,b)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. Let
A,B\in H_{n}(a,b)
and
\gamma(t)=(1-t)A+tB
for all
t\in(0,1)
. If
f
is operator convex of order
n
, then

(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime\prime}(t)\geq 0,\quad\forall\,t\in(0,1).

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
\phi_{x}(t)=\langle f(\gamma(t))x,x\rangle
for all
t\in(0,1)
. By (II) of Theorem
22
,
f\circ\gamma
is twice continuously differentiable, so
\phi_{x}
is twice continuously differentiable. Since
f
is operator convex of order
n
, then
\phi_{x}
is a convex function. Therefore, the second derivative of
\phi_{x}
is non-negative which implies that

0\leq\phi_{x}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(t)=\bigg\langle(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime\prime}(t)x,x\bigg\rangle,\quad\forall\,t\in(0,1).

Since the above holds for any arbitrary
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, the result follows.
∎

We are now in a position to prove
[
5
, Theorem 6.6.52 (1)]
.

Theorem 25
.

Let
f:(a,b)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a twice continuously differentiable function on
(a,b)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. If
f
is operator convex of order
n
, then

[\Delta^{2}f(t_{i},t_{j},t_{1})]_{i,j=1}^{n}\geq 0,\quad\forall\,t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\in(a,b).

Proof.

Let
D=\text{diag}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})\in H_{n}(a,b)
and
L>0
. Let
X
be an
n\times n
matrix such that

X=\begin{bmatrix}L&1&\ldots&1\\
1&0&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&0&\ldots&0\end{bmatrix}.

Consider
A=D-\epsilon X
and
B=D+\epsilon X
such that
\epsilon>0
is small enough so that
A,B\in H_{n}(a,b)
. Let
\gamma(t)=(1-t)A+tB
for all
t\in(0,1)
. Therefore,
\gamma(1/2)=D
and
\gamma^{\prime}(1/2)=2\epsilon X
. Let
c_{k}
denote the
k
’th column of
X
. Let us now apply (II) of Theorem
22
for
(f\circ\gamma)^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(1/2)
. Since
\gamma(1/2)
is a diagonal matrix, then
U(1/2)=\mathbb{I}_{n}
where
\mathbb{I}_{n}
denotes the
n\times n
identity matrix. Furthermore,
\gamma^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(1/2)=0
which allows us to conclude that

(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime\prime}(1/2)=\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}f(\gamma(t))|_{t=1/2}=8\epsilon^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}[\Delta^{2}f(t_{i},t_{j},t_{k})]_{i,j=1}^{n}\circ\big(c_{k}c_{k}^{*}\big).

Let us now compute the matrices
c_{k}c_{k}^{*}
for
1\leq k\leq n
.

c_{1}c_{1}^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}L^{2}&L&\ldots&L\\
L&1&\ldots&1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
L&1&\ldots&1\end{bmatrix},c_{j}c_{j}^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\ldots&0\\
0&0&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&0\end{bmatrix},\quad 1<j\leq n.

Let
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a unit vector and
y=(x_{1}/L,\ldots,x_{n})
. By Lemma
24
,
(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime\prime}(1/2)
is positive semi-definite, so

0\leq\bigg\langle\frac{(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime\prime}(1/2)}{8\epsilon^{2}}y,y\bigg\rangle=x^{*}[\Delta^{2}f(t_{i},t_{j},t_{1})]_{i,j=1}^{n}x+\sum_{k=2}^{n}\Delta^{2}f(t_{1},t_{1},t_{k})\frac{|x_{1}|^{2}}{L^{2}}.

As
L\rightarrow\infty
, the result is obtained.
∎

Proposition 26
.

Let
f:(a,b)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a twice continuously differentiable function on
(a,b)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and recall the transformation
L_{t_{1}}f
in (
6
), then

[\Delta^{2}f(t_{i},t_{j},t_{1})]_{i,j=2}^{n}=[\Delta L_{t_{1}}f(t_{i},t_{j})]_{i,j=2}^{n},

where
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\in(a,b)
.

Proof.

Let
t_{k},t_{l},t_{m}\in(a,b)
be distinct. We claim that
\Delta^{2}f(t_{k},t_{l},t_{m})=\Delta^{2}f(\pi(1),\pi(2),\pi(3))
for any permutation
\pi
of
t_{k},t_{l},t_{m}
. We recall (
13
) to expand
\Delta^{2}f(t_{k},t_{l},t_{m})
.

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}f(t_{k},t_{l},t_{m})

\displaystyle=\frac{(f(t_{m})-f(t_{k}))(t_{l}-t_{k})-(f(t_{l})-f(t_{k}))(t_{m}-t_{k})}{(t_{m}-t_{k})(t_{l}-t_{k})(t_{m}-t_{l})}

\displaystyle=\frac{f(t_{m})(t_{l}-t_{k})+f(t_{k})(t_{m}-t_{l})+f(t_{l})(t_{k}-t_{m})}{(t_{m}-t_{k})(t_{l}-t_{k})(t_{m}-t_{l})}

\displaystyle=\frac{f(t_{m})}{(t_{m}-t_{k})(t_{m}-t_{l})}+\frac{f(t_{k})}{(t_{k}-t_{l})(t_{k}-t_{m})}+\frac{f(t_{l})}{(t_{l}-t_{k})(t_{l}-t_{m})}.

Therefore, our claim is proved. Consider
\Delta^{2}f(t_{i},t_{j},t_{1})
where
t_{i},t_{j},t_{1}\in(a,b)
and
t_{i},t_{j},t_{1}
are not necessarily distinct.

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}f(t_{i},t_{j},t_{1})=\lim_{s\rightarrow t_{i}}\lim_{t\rightarrow t_{j}}\Delta^{2}f(s,t,t_{1})=\lim_{s\rightarrow t_{i}}\lim_{t\rightarrow t_{j}}\frac{\Delta f(t,t_{1})-\Delta f(s,t_{1})}{t-s}

\displaystyle=\lim_{s\rightarrow t_{i}}\lim_{t\rightarrow t_{j}}\frac{L_{t_{1}}f(t)-L_{t_{1}}f(s)}{t-s}=\lim_{s\rightarrow t_{i}}\frac{L_{t_{1}}f(t_{j})-L_{t_{1}}f(s)}{t_{j}-s}=\Delta L_{t_{1}}f(t_{i},t_{j}).

∎

Finally, we prove the concluding result of this section regarding the transformation in (

).

Theorem 27
.

If
f:(a,b)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is twice continuously differentiable and operator convex on
(a,b)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, then
L_{t_{1}}f
is operator monotone for any
t_{1}\in(a,b)
.

Proof.

By Theorem
25
and Proposition
26
, the first divided difference matrix of
L_{t_{1}}f
is positive semi-definite for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
. By Theorem
23
,
L_{t_{1}}f
is operator monotone of order
n
for any

which implies
L_{t_{1}}f
is operator monotone by Theorem
18
.
∎

4.
Löwner’s Theorem

4.1.
Introduction

Löwner’s theorem is pivotal for the development of Kubo-Ando theory. Therefore, we shall provide a proof by following the arguments in
[
4
]
. The theorem provides an integral representation for positive operator monotone functions. Namely, a positive operator monotone function
f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a function such that
A\leq B\implies f(A)\leq f(B)
where
A,B\in

\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H})^{++}
.
We state the theorem as found in
[
4
, Theorem 4.9]
.

Theorem 28
.

Let
f
be a positive operator monotone function defined in the positive half-line. There is a positive Radon measure
\mu
on the closed interval
[0,1]
such that

(15)

f(t)=\int_{[0,1]}\frac{t}{\lambda+(1-\lambda)t}\,{\rm d}\mu(\lambda),\quad x>0.

Conversely, any function given in this form is operator monotone. The measure
\mu
is a probability measure iff
f(1)=1
.

Before delving into the various technicalities of the proof, we first give a general outline. Let
P
denote the set of positive operator monotone functions and let
P_{1}=\{f\in P:f(1)=1\}
.

(I)

We use regularisation to show that
-L_{t_{1}}f
in (
6
) is operator monotone for
f\in P
and
t_{1}>0
.

(II)

We show that
P_{1}
is a metrizable convex compact set under a suitable topology.

(III)

We use (I) to show that the extreme points of
P_{1}
are of the form
t\rightarrow\frac{t}{\lambda+(1-\lambda)t}
on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
for
\lambda\in[0,1]
.

(IV)

We apply Choquet theory by using (II) and (III) to obtain the result.

4.2.
Regularisation

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
be locally integrable. Therefore, we can consider its convolution with a function
\phi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which is infinitely differentiable and has the following additional properties
[
3
, Page 11]
.

(I)

\phi(x)\geq 0
and
\phi(x)=\phi(-x)
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

(II)

\phi(x)=0
for
x\notin[-1,1]
.

(III)

\int_{-1}^{1}\phi(x)\,dx=1
.

Let
(a,b)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
\epsilon>0
such that
2^{-1}(b-a)>\epsilon
. We introduce the function
\phi_{\epsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\phi(\frac{x}{\epsilon})
and we denote the convolution
f\ast\phi_{\epsilon}
by
f_{\epsilon}
. Therefore,

(16)

(f\ast\phi_{\epsilon})(x)=\int^{b}_{a}f(y)\phi_{\epsilon}(x-y)\,dy=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int^{b}_{a}f(y)\phi\bigg(\frac{x-y}{\epsilon}\bigg)dy

whenever
x\in(a+\epsilon,b-\epsilon)
. Furthermore, by substituting for
\epsilon^{-1}(x-y)
and using the fact that
\phi
vanishes outside
[-1,1]
, we obtain

\int^{(x-a)/\epsilon}_{(x-b)/\epsilon}f(x-\epsilon y)\phi(y)dy=\int^{1}_{-1}f(x-\epsilon y)\phi(y)dy.

One of the well-known properties of
f_{\epsilon}
is that
f_{\epsilon}
is infinitely differentiable. To demonstrate this, we prove that
f_{\epsilon}
is differentiable. The existence of higher-order derivatives follows by similar arguments.

Proposition 29
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
be locally integrable and
(a,b)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Let
\epsilon>0
such that
2^{-1}(b-a)>\epsilon
, and consider
the regularisation
f_{\epsilon}
given by (
16
). Then
f_{\epsilon}
is differentiable with derivative
f\ast\phi^{\prime}_{\epsilon}
.

Proof.

Let
h>0
,
x\in(a+\epsilon,b-\epsilon)
and
\displaystyle M_{1}=\sup_{x\in(a,b)}|f(x)|
. Then

\displaystyle|\mathscr{E}_{h}(x)|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\frac{f_{\epsilon}(x+h)-f_{\epsilon}(x)}{h}-f\ast\phi^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(x)\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int^{b}_{a}\frac{f(y)\phi_{\epsilon}(x+h-y)-f(y)\phi_{\epsilon}(x-y)}{h}-f(y)\phi^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(x-y)dy\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq M_{1}\int^{b}_{a}\bigg|\frac{\phi_{\epsilon}(x+h-y)-\phi_{\epsilon}(x-y)}{h}-\phi^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(x-y)\bigg|dy.

Since we will be using Taylor’s approximation, let
c_{y}\in(x-y,x+h-y)
for
y\in(a,b)
. Let
\displaystyle M_{2}=\sup\{|\phi_{\epsilon}^{\prime\prime}(x)|:a+\epsilon-b\leq x\leq b-\epsilon+h-a\}
. Then

\displaystyle|\mathscr{E}_{h}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq M_{1}\int^{b}_{a}\bigg|\frac{\phi_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(x-y)h+2^{-1}\phi_{\epsilon}^{\prime\prime}(c_{y})h^{2}}{h}-\phi^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(x-y)\bigg|dy

\displaystyle\leq M_{1}\int^{b}_{a}\bigg|\frac{\phi_{\epsilon}^{\prime\prime}(c_{y})h}{2}\bigg|dy\leq\frac{M_{1}M_{2}(b-a)h}{2}.

Therefore, as
h\rightarrow 0
, the result is obtained.
∎

We recall the transformation (
6
) which leads us to the following important theorem
[
4
, Theorem 3.7]
.

Theorem 30
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
be an operator monotone function. Then
f
is differentiable and
-L_{t_{1}}f
is operator monotone for each
t_{1}>0
.

Proof.

Let
(a,b)\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{+}
. By Theorem
16
, if
f
is operator monotone, then
f
is operator concave. Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
2^{-1}(b-a)>N^{-1}
. Furthermore if
t_{1}\in(a,b)
, then
N
must also satisfy the property that
t_{1}\in(a+N^{-1},b-N^{-1})
. By regularisation, we obtain a sequence
\{f_{n}\}_{n\geq N}
of infinitely differentiable operator concave functions. We now show that the sequence
\{L_{t_{1}}f_{n}\}_{n\geq N}
converges pointwise for any
t\in(a,b)
. Since
f
is continuous,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}L_{t_{1}}f_{n}(t)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{f_{n}(t)-f_{n}(t_{1})}{t-t_{1}}=\frac{f(t)-f(t_{1})}{t-t_{1}},\quad t\in(a,b)\setminus\{t_{1}\}.

If
t_{1}\in(a,b)
, by Proposition
29
,

L_{t_{1}}f_{n}(t_{1})=f^{\prime}_{n}(t_{1})=\int_{-1}^{1}f(t_{1}-n^{-1}y)\phi^{\prime}(y)dy.

Since
f
is monotone, the sequence
\{L_{t_{1}}f_{n}(t_{1})\}_{n\geq N}
is bounded for sufficiently large
n
. Therefore, a subsequence of
\{L_{t_{1}}f_{n}\}
converges pointwise to some function
F
for all
t\in(a,b)
.

Let
M>0
. For sufficiently large
n
, we have that
|L_{t_{1}}f_{n}(t)|\leq M
for all
t\in(a,b)
. By Theorem
27
,
-L_{t_{1}}f_{n}
is operator monotone for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, so by using Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem and the Borel function calculus, we conclude that
-F
is operator monotone on
(a,b)
. Continuity of
F
implies that

F(t_{1})=\lim_{t\rightarrow t_{1}}\frac{f(t)-f(t_{1})}{t-t_{1}}=f^{\prime}(t_{1}).

Therefore,
F=L_{t_{1}}f
and
-L_{t_{1}}f
is operator monotone for any proper open subinterval of
\mathbb{R}^{+}
, so
-L_{t_{1}}f
is operator monotone on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
∎

Looking at the proof of the previous theorem, we can extract the following Corollary.

Corollary 31
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
be an operator monotone function and
(a,b)\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Then
f
is differentiable and there is a sequence
\{f_{n}\}
of infinitely differentiable operator monotone functions such that
\lim f_{n}(t)=f(t)
for all
t\in(a,b)
. Furthermore,
\lim f^{\prime}_{n}(t)=f^{\prime}(t)
for a finite number of points in
(a,b)
.

Proof.

In the previous proof, continuity of
f
guaranteed that the sequence
\{f_{n}\}
obtained by regularisation converges pointwise to
f
on
(a,b)
. Furthermore, it was also shown that a subsequence of
\{f^{\prime}_{n}\}
converges to
f^{\prime}
for a finite number of points in
(a,b)
.
∎

4.3.
Metrizability, Compactness, and Convexity of Positive Operator Monotone functions

We recall that
P_{1}
consists of operator monotone functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that
f(1)=1
. Naturally,
P_{1}
is a subset of the vector space
E
of functions
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}}
. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}
and consider the pointwise evaluation
\tau_{x}:f\rightarrow f(x)
for any
f\in E
. Then it is clear that the collection
\{|\tau_{x}|\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
is a family of separating semi-norms on
E
, so by
[
6
, Theorem 1.2.6]
, we obtain a locally convex topology on
E
with base neighbourhoods of the form

(17)

V(f_{0}:|\tau_{x_{1}}|\ldots|\tau_{x_{n}}|;\epsilon)=\{f\in E:|\tau_{x_{i}}(f)-\tau_{x_{i}}(f_{0})|<\epsilon\,(i=1,\ldots,n)\}

for
f_{0}\in E
,
x_{1}\ldots x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\epsilon>0
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

It is easy to see that
P_{1}
is convex since positive linear combinations of operator monotone functions are again operator monotone. Let us now prove some preliminary properties which will be required to prove that
P_{1}
is metrizable and compact
[
11
, Proposition 28.7]
.

Proposition 32
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
be an operator monotone function, and
s>t>0
, then

(I)

0<f(t)\leq f(s)\leq\frac{f(t)s}{t}.

(II)

If
f(1)=1
, then
f(t)\leq t+1.

(III)

If
f(1)=1
, then
0\leq f(s)-f(t)\leq(1+t^{-1})(s-t)
.

Proof.

(I)
. Since
f
is bounded below by
0
and is scalar monotone, then
\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow 0+}f(x)=x_{0}
where
x_{0}\in[0,\infty)
. Since
f
is operator monotone, then
f
is a scalar concave function by Theorem
16
, so
f(s)
lies below the straight line from
(0,x_{0})
through
(t,f(t))
which implies

\begin{split}f(s)&\leq\frac{(f(t)-x_{0})s}{t}+x_{0}\\
&=\frac{(f(t)-x_{0})(s-t)}{t}+f(t)\\
&\leq\frac{f(t)(s-t)}{t}+f(t)=\frac{f(t)s}{t}.\end{split}

(II)
. If
t>1
, then
f(t)\leq t
by (I). On the other hand, if
t\leq 1
, then
f(t)\leq f(1)=1
by monotonicity. Combining the aforementioned, allows us to conclude that
f(t)\leq t+1
for any
t>0
.

(III)
. By (I) and (II),

\begin{split}0\leq f(s)-f(t)&\leq\frac{f(t)(s-t)}{t}\leq(1+t^{-1})(s-t).\end{split}

∎

Theorem 33
.

Let
E
denote the vector space of functions
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}}
. The subset
P_{1}
of
E
of operator monotone functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that
f(1)=1
is metrizable.

Proof.

Consider the subspace topology on
P_{1}
induced by the topology generated by the base of neighbourhoods of the form in (
17
) on
E
. Let us show that
P_{1}
is second countable. Let
\tau_{x}
for some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
denote the map
\tau_{x}:f\rightarrow f(x)
. Let
f\in E
,
\epsilon>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose that
g\in V(f:|\tau_{x_{1}}|,\ldots,|\tau_{x_{n}}|;\epsilon)\cap P_{1}
for
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{+}
. We also consider the following.

(I)

Let
\delta=\epsilon-\max\{|f(x_{i})-g(x_{i})|:i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\}
. Clearly,
\delta>0
.

(II)

Let
p_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}^{+}
such that
p_{i}>x_{i}
and
(p_{i}-x_{i})(1+x_{i}^{-1})<\delta/4
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

(III)

Let
q_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}^{+}
such that
|g(p_{i})-q_{i}|<\delta/4
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

(IV)

Let
c:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
c(p_{i})=q_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

It is clear that
g\in V(c:|\tau_{p_{1}}|,\ldots,|\tau_{p_{n}}|;\delta/4)\cap P_{1}
. Let
h\in V(c:|\tau_{p_{1}}|,\ldots,|\tau_{p_{n}}|;\delta/4)\cap P_{1}
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. By (III) of Proposition
32
,
|h(x_{k})-h(p_{k})|<\delta/4
and
|g(x_{k})-g(p_{k})|<\delta/4
. Therefore,

\displaystyle|h(x_{k})-g(x_{k})|

\displaystyle\leq|h(x_{k})-h(p_{k})|+|h(p_{k})-q_{k}|

\displaystyle+|q_{k}-g(p_{k})|+|g(p_{k})-g(x_{k})|

\displaystyle<\delta.

By the above,

\begin{split}|h(x_{k})-f(x_{k})|&\leq|h(x_{k})-g(x_{k})|+|g(x_{k})-f(x_{k})|\\
&<\delta+|g(x_{k})-f(x_{k})|\\
&\leq(\epsilon-|g(x_{k})-f(x_{k})|)+|g(x_{k})-f(x_{k})|=\epsilon.\end{split}

Since the above holds for any

, we have
h\in V(f:|\tau_{x_{1}}|,\ldots,|\tau_{x_{n}}|\\
;\epsilon)\cap P_{1}
, so
g\in V(c:|\tau_{p_{1}}|,\ldots,|\tau_{p_{n}}|;r)\cap P_{1}\subseteq V(f:|\tau_{x_{1}}|,\ldots,|\tau_{x_{n}}|;\epsilon)\cap P_{1}
for some

such that

. Hence the space is second countable. Let us show
P_{1}
is regular. Suppose

is closed. Let
g\in P_{1}
such that

. Therefore, there is some

such that
|g(x)-f(x)|\geq\epsilon
for

and

is regular.
∎

Lemma 34
.

The set
P_{1}
of operator monotone functions

Proof

t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

(II)

If
f^{\prime}(1)=1
, then
f(t)=t
for
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

Proof.

Let
t_{1},t_{2}\in(a,b)\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Suppose
\{f_{n}\}
is the sequence of infinitely differentiable operator monotone functions generated by Corollary
31
on
(a,b)
. This implies that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}[\Delta f_{n}(t_{i},t_{j})]_{i,j=1}^{2}=[\Delta f(t_{i},t_{j})]_{i,j=1}^{2}

in s.o.t. By Theorem
23
,
[\Delta f_{n}(t_{i},t_{j})]_{i,j=1}^{2}
is positive semi-definite for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, so it follows that
[\Delta f(t_{i},t_{j})]_{i,j=1}^{2}
is positive semi-definite. Consequently, the determinant is non-negative, so

\bigg|\frac{f(t_{1})-f(t_{2})}{t_{1}-t_{2}}\bigg|^{2}\leq f^{\prime}(t_{1})f^{\prime}(t_{2}),\quad\forall\,t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}.

If
f^{\prime}(1)=0
, then
f
is constant by the above inequality, so
f(t)=1
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. If
f^{\prime}(1)=1
, then by (
18
),
(tf(t)^{-1})^{\prime}(1)=0
which implies that
(tf(t)^{-1})=1
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, so
f(t)=t
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

Let
h_{1},h_{2}\in P_{1}
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
and suppose that
f=\lambda h_{1}+(1-\lambda)h_{2}
. If
f^{\prime}(1)=0
or
f^{\prime}(1)=1
, it is clear that
f^{\prime}(1)=h_{1}^{\prime}(1)
. Therefore,
f=h_{1}
for both cases.
∎

We are now in a position to prove
[
4
, Lemma 4.8]
.

Proposition 39
.

The extreme points of the set of operator monotone functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that
f(1)=1
have the form

(19)

f(t)=\frac{t}{f^{\prime}(1)+(1-f^{\prime}(1))t},\quad t>0.

Proof.

By Lemma
38
, whenever
f^{\prime}(1)=0
or
f^{\prime}(1)=1
,
f
is an extreme point and is of the form outlined by (
19
).

Let
0<f^{\prime}(1)<1
. Let
g:t\rightarrow tf(t^{-1})
. By (III) of Proposition
36
,
g
is operator monotone. Furthermore, define the following functions.

\displaystyle f_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{t}{f^{\prime}(1)}L_{1}f(t),

\displaystyle f_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-f^{\prime}(1)}L_{1}g(t^{-1}).

We show that
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are operator monotone. By Theorem
30
,
-L_{1}f
is operator monotone which means that
t\rightarrow L_{1}f(t^{-1})
is operator monotone. By (III) of Proposition
36
, the function
t\rightarrow tL_{1}f((t^{-1})^{-1})=tL_{1}f(t)
is operator monotone, so
f_{1}
is operator monotone. Similarly, by Theorem
30
,
-L_{1}g
is operator monotone which implies that
t\rightarrow L_{1}g(t^{-1})
is operator monotone, so
f_{2}
is operator monotone. We now show that
f_{1}(1)=f_{2}(1)=1
. Since
L_{1}f(1)=f^{\prime}(1)
, then
f_{1}(1)=1
. Moreover, since
g^{\prime}(t)=f(t^{-1})-t^{-1}f^{\prime}(t^{-1})
, we have
g^{\prime}(1)=1-f^{\prime}(1)
. This clearly implies that
f_{2}(1)=1
.

Let us now show that

(20)

f^{\prime}(1)f_{1}(t)+(1-f^{\prime}(1))f_{2}(t)=f(t),\quad\forall\,t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}.

Since
f_{1}(1)=f_{2}(1)=1
, then the equation clearly holds for when
t=1
. For
t\neq 1
, we compute

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(1)f_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{t(f(t)-1)}{t-1}.

\displaystyle(1-f^{\prime}(1))f_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{-1}f(t)-1}{t^{-1}-1}=\frac{t-f(t)}{t-1}.

Moreover,

\frac{t(f(t)-1)}{t-1}+\frac{t-f(t)}{t-1}=f(t)

which proves (
20
).
Since
f
is an extreme point,

f(t)=f_{1}(t)=\frac{t}{f^{\prime}(1)}\frac{f(t)-1}{t-1},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\setminus\{1\}.

So,

f(t)=\frac{t}{f^{\prime}(1)+(1-f^{\prime}(1))t},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}.

∎

This leads us to the proof of Löwner’s Theorem.

Theorem 40
.

Let
f
be a positive operator monotone function defined in the positive half-line. There is a positive Radon measure
\mu
on the closed interval
[0,1]
such that

f(t)=\int_{[0,1]}\frac{t}{\lambda+(1-\lambda)t}\,{\rm d}\mu(\lambda),\quad t>0.

Conversely, any function given on this form is operator monotone. The measure
\mu
is a probability measure iff
f(1)=1
.

Proof.

Suppose
f(1)=1
. By Theorem
13
and Corollary
35
, there exists a positive radon measure
\mu
which represents
f
on the set of extreme points of the set
P_{1}
of operator monotone functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that
f(1)=1
. Consider the continuous linear functional
\rho
such that
\rho(f)=f(t)
where
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
f\in P_{1}
. Then

f(t)=\rho(f)=\int_{\text{Ext}(P_{1})}\rho(f_{\lambda})\,{\rm d}\mu(\lambda).

Since the extreme points are of the form (
19
), then

f(t)=\int_{[0,1]}\frac{t}{\lambda+(1-\lambda)t}\,{\rm d}\mu(\lambda).

∎
