Epidemiology of major work-related injuries in Abu Dhabi, United Arab Emirates: a 10-year retrospective study
Authors: Munawar Farooq, Arif Alper Cevik, David O Alao, Fikri Abu-Zidan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo describe the proportion, demographic profile, mechanisms, temporal distribution, injury patterns and in-hospital outcomes of adults with major work-related trauma admitted to hospitals in Abu Dhabi.DesignRetrospective cohort study using prospectively collected trauma registry data.SettingSeven trauma-receiving hospitals in the Emirate of Abu Dhabi, United Arab Emirates, from 2014 to 2023.ParticipantsAdults aged 18 years or older with injuries coded as work-related in the Abu Dhabi Trauma Registry and an Injury Severity Score (ISS) of 12 or greater.Main outcome measuresProportion of adult trauma admissions with ISS≥12 that were work-related; mechanisms of injury; anatomical injury pattern; intensive care unit (ICU) use; hospital and ICU length of stay; discharge disposition; and in-hospital mortality.ResultsAmong 7019 adults with ISS≥12, 1318 (18.8%) had work-related injuries. Patients were predominantly young, with a median age of 33 years (IQR 27–42), male (98.6%) and non-local (98.8%). Falls from more than 1 meter were the leading mechanism (52.7%), followed by struck-by-object injuries (17.9%) and road traffic collisions (8.8%). Head and neck (53.1%), extremity (49.4%) and chest (48.3%) injuries were common, with a median of 2 injured body regions (IQR 2–3). Median ISS was 22 (IQR 17–29). ICU admission was required in 43.0%; median ICU length of stay was 5 days (IQR 3–12), median hospital length of stay was 8 days (IQR 3–16) and in-hospital mortality was 8.9%. Burns had the highest mortality (31.0%). 76% of all trauma patients were discharged home; however around 14% required transfer, long-term care or rehabilitation.ConclusionsMajor work-related trauma in Abu Dhabi occurs predominantly among young non-local men, is caused mainly by falls from height and struck-by events and places a substantial burden on trauma and critical care services. Prevention should prioritise control of high-energy workplace hazards, especially falls from height, while service planning should account for rehabilitation and longer-term recovery needs.

Introduction

Work-related injuries constitute a persistent and significant public health concern on a global scale.1 In 2019, global data showed that occupational diseases and injuries caused 2.9 million deaths, including about 320 000 from occupational injuries. These events led to significant disability-adjusted life-year losses and major economic costs, with substantial variation by country and region.2

This burden is unevenly distributed, with structurally vulnerable labour groups experiencing higher rates of workplace injuries and fatalities. These disparities have been linked to precarious employment, communication barriers and weak labour protections.3 4 Furthermore, younger individuals and those newly entering the workforce may face heightened vulnerability in hazardous industries.5 Work-related injuries are a major problem in the Gulf region, where rapid urban development is heavily dependent on a large non-local workforce. Studies of construction workers in this context have documented unsafe working environments and considerable obstacles to accessing healthcare and achieving recovery.6 7

In the UAE, a previous study reported that hospitalised occupational injuries primarily affected non-national men, mostly due to falls and falling objects.8 More recent analyses confirm falls as a predominant cause and underscore the persistence of serious workplace hazards despite regulatory progress.9 In Qatar, occupational injuries account for 30% of trauma admissions at a level I trauma centre, with nearly one-quarter classified as major trauma.10 Major multisystem trauma patients place considerable strain on emergency, operative and intensive care services, while recent trauma research highlights the importance of post-discharge recovery and workforce reintegration alongside survival.11–13

Despite the regional importance of the problem, contemporary literature on major trauma resulting from occupational injuries is sparse. Published work in the region has often mixed minor and severe injuries, relied on single-city cohorts or used accident-investigation data rather than multicentre clinical registries.9 10 14 There remains a paucity of empirical studies on the epidemiology of individuals experiencing major occupational trauma in the UAE. The Abu Dhabi Trauma Registry provides an opportunity to examine major occupational trauma across the emirate using standardised hospital data and provides insight into their impact on intensive care utilisation, mortality and post-acute care requirements.15

We therefore aimed to describe the proportion, demographic profile, mechanisms, temporal distribution, injury patterns and in-hospital outcomes of adults with major work-related trauma admitted to hospitals in Abu Dhabi over a 10-year period. In-hospital outcomes included intensive care unit (ICU) utilisation, discharge disposition and mortality.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a retrospective cohort study using prospectively collected trauma registry data. The study included seven trauma-receiving hospitals contributing to the Abu Dhabi Trauma Registry, located across the three regions of the Emirate of Abu Dhabi (Abu Dhabi, Al Ain and Al Dhafra). Participating hospitals were Mafraq Hospital, a Level I trauma centre in Abu Dhabi that contributed data from 2014 to 2019 and was subsequently replaced by Sheikh Shakhbout Medical City (SSMC), which continued trauma services and registry participation from 2020 onwards. Additional participating hospitals included Al Rahba Hospital and Sheikh Khalifa Medical City (SKMC), Level II trauma centres in Abu Dhabi, Al Ain Hospital and Tawam Hospital (Level I trauma centres in Al Ain) and Al Gharbia Hospital (Level II) and Al Ruwais Hospital (Level II) in Al Dhafra. Hospital bed capacity ranged from 132 to 741 beds, and four hospitals were affiliated teaching hospitals (Al Ain, Tawam, SKMC, SSMC, previously Mafraq). Participating centres followed standardised trauma protocols, and registry data were collected across the network using a common framework.15 The UAE’s trauma system is characterised by designated regional trauma centres, with ongoing initiatives to develop a comprehensive trauma continuum from prevention to rehabilitation. Prehospital care is primarily managed and operated by individual police departments in each emirate, where calls to emergency numbers like ‘999’ or ‘998’ are handled by police dispatch centres. This prehospital system is decentralised, with each emirate operating under its own health authority, leading to coordination challenges and variations in paramedic training and equipment levels across the country. Funding for emergency and trauma care is heavily supported by the government, which has increased public spending to provide free services in large government hospitals for major conditions, and the system may transition toward a mandated national insurance model in the future.16

Data source and eligibility criteria

The Abu Dhabi Trauma Registry prospectively records trauma patients who are admitted to hospital for more than 24 hours or who die after arrival at a participating hospital. For the present analysis, we included adults aged 18 years or older who were admitted between January 2014 and December 2023, had injuries coded as work-related in the registry and had an Injury Severity Score (ISS) of 12 or greater. Work-related injuries were identified using the trauma registry coding system, which integrates information from triage records, clinical documentation, and discharge summaries. Trained personnel use the National Trauma Data Bank (NTDB) Trauma Registry Collector’s Data Dictionary for coding. Because workforce denominator data were unavailable, incidence rates could not be calculated; we therefore report the proportion of severe adult trauma admissions that were work-related.

An ISS threshold of 12 or higher was chosen to capture clinically significant occupational trauma while excluding minor injuries. ISS 9–15 denotes moderate trauma, with higher scores indicating severe cases; thus, ISS≥12 identifies severe and higher-acuity moderate injuries. Under the 2008 Abbreviated Injury Scale, ISS>12 is equivalent to the former ISS>15 in identifying patients at greater mortality risk and resource use. Therefore, using an ISS of 12 or higher as a severity cut-off is strongly backed by major trauma registry research.17–21

Variables and outcomes

Extracted variables included age, sex, nationality, mechanism of injury, time of presentation, mode of arrival, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), injured body regions, weighted revised trauma scores (W-RTS), ISS, ICU admission, ICU length of stay (ICU-LOS), hospital length of stay (Hospital-LOS), discharge disposition and in-hospital mortality. Presentation time was grouped into four 6 hour intervals. Temporal analyses were also undertaken by day of the week, month and calendar year. The main outcomes were injury pattern, ICU use, discharge disposition and death during the index admission.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS V.31.0.1.0 and Jamovi V.2.4.1. Continuous or ordinal variables were presented as median (25–75% interquartile range), while categorical variables were reported as counts (percentages). The Mann-Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to compare two or more independent groups with continuous or ordinal data. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the categorical data of independent groups. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Missing values were uncommon (<2.5% for any variable), and therefore, no imputation was performed. Analyses were conducted using the available data for each variable.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this registry-based study.

Results

Cohort characteristics

During the 10-year study period, 7019 adult trauma patients with an ISS≥12 were recorded in the Abu Dhabi Trauma Registry, of whom 1318 (18.8%) had work-related injuries and were included in the analysis. The median age of the cohort was 33 years (IQR 27–42). Most patients were male (98.6%) and non-local residents (98.8%). Falls from more than 1 meter were the leading mechanism of injury (n=694, 52.7%), followed by being struck by an object (17.9%) and road traffic collisions (8.8%). Most patients arrived by ground ambulance (68.7%). Head and neck injuries were the most frequent anatomical injuries, affecting 53.1% of patients, followed by extremity injuries (49.4%) and chest injuries (48.3%). The median number of injured body regions per patient was 2 (IQR: 2–3). The median ISS was 22 (IQR 17–29). The median GCS score on arrival was 15 (IQR 14–15), and the median systolic blood pressure (SBP) was 127 mm Hg (IQR 114–140). Nevertheless, 15.1% of patients presented with a GCS score <9 and 6.0% with SBP <90 mm Hg. ICU admission was required in 43.0% of patients. Median ICU-LOS was 5 days (IQR: 3–12), and median hospital-LOS was 8 days (IQR: 3–16). Overall, in-hospital mortality was 8.9% (118/1318). Most deaths occurred in the ICU. The median age differed significantly across mechanisms (p<0.001), with patients sustaining falls from<1 meter being older than those injured by other mechanisms. Temporal distribution

939 (71.2%) patients presented to the emergency department (ED) between 06:01 and 18:00. Mortality among patients presenting during the 00:01–06:00 period (17.4%) was significantly higher than during other time periods (Fisher’s exact test, p=0.047). Case numbers and deaths were broadly similar across the week. Friday had the fewest presentations and the highest crude mortality proportion, but the variation by day of the week was not statistically significant (p=0.096) (figure 1B).

Monthly counts were relatively stable, with no significant variation in mortality by month (p=0.231) (figure 1C). The annual number of cases was highest in 2015 and 2016, declined between 2017 and 2019 and then rose gradually from 2020 to 2023. Mortality counts fluctuated across years, with the highest number of deaths in 2016, but there was no statistically significant difference in mortality by year (p=0.337) (figure 1D).

Disposition after hospital care

Among 1200 survivors, 1002 (83.5%) were discharged home, while 165 (13.8%) required transfer, long-term care or rehabilitation. These patterns indicate that an important minority of patients required continuing medical or rehabilitative care after acute hospital treatment.

Discussion

Principal findings

This 10-year emirate-wide registry study shows that the major work-related injury in Abu Dhabi falls disproportionately on young non-local men and is dominated by high-energy mechanisms, especially falls from more than 1 meter and struck-by objects. The resulting injury burden was commonly multisystem, most often involving the head and neck, extremities and chest and placed substantial demands on the trauma system, with frequent ICU admission and in-hospital mortality of 8.9%. Although most survivors were discharged home, a notable minority required transfer, long-term care or rehabilitation. These findings provide contemporary data on major work-related trauma in Abu Dhabi to guide both prevention and service planning.

Young non-local workers and structural vulnerability

Most patients with severe occupational injuries in the registry were young non-local workers. Younger and newer workers often have less job experience and may be more vulnerable to traumatic injury.22 This pattern is consistent with previous reports describing occupational segregation and structural vulnerabilities among migrant workers in the Gulf region. However, because denominator data for the workforce were unavailable, our findings should be interpreted as describing the injured cohort rather than demonstrating differential injury risk across workforce groups. International evidence shows that workers in mobile or externally recruited labour forces are often concentrated in hazardous manual jobs and may face additional risks related to insecure employment, weaker protections and communication barriers.3 4 Similar concerns have been described for non-local labour forces in Gulf construction settings.6 7

Major trauma and demand on acute care services

These injuries constitute major trauma, frequently involving multiple body regions and substantial use of emergency, operative and critical care resources. The pattern of head, chest and extremity injuries, a median of two affected regions, ICU admission in 43% and an 8.9% mortality rate demonstrate that occupational trauma significantly contributes to trauma-system demand. This aligns with trauma literature, which identifies multisystem trauma as a high-acuity subgroup with greater physiological instability and resource needs.11 23

Differences by mechanism

The mechanism-specific results show that occupational trauma is not a single clinical entity. Burns were relatively uncommon, yet they had the highest mortality and greater physiological derangement, reflected by lower W-RTS values. Conventional trauma scores do not fully capture burn severity, which depends on burn size, depth, inhalation injury and subsequent systemic complications.24 25 Burn injury should therefore be considered a distinct high-risk subgroup in both prevention planning and clinical pathways. The high mortality rate may reflect burn severity markers such as total body surface area or inhalation injury which were not available in this dataset. By contrast, falls from less than 1 meter occurred in older workers indicating that even low-energy trauma can cause major injury when physiological reserve is reduced. Recent studies suggest that frailty often predicts trauma outcomes better than chronological age alone and is linked to greater care needs after discharge.26 27 Low-level falls in older workers may therefore signal vulnerability within the workforce.

Falls from height as the main prevention target

Falls from height accounted for more than half of all cases and remain the clearest target for prevention. This finding is in line with earlier UAE data and with recent accident-report analyses that continue to identify falls as a leading workplace hazard.8 9 Literature from the workers in high-risk manual occupations show that fatal and severe falls are linked to modifiable factors such as absent edge protection, inadequate equipment, poor site management and insufficient training.28 29 Analyses of fatal falls also suggest that organisational failures are common and that prevention cannot rely on worker vigilance alone. In Abu Dhabi, these findings indicate the need to prioritise safer workplace design, group safety measures, skilled supervision and strict enforcement of work-at-height rules.30 31

Time patterns and system response

Most patients presented during daytime hours, which is consistent with the timing of routine work activity. Mortality was highest among patients presenting between midnight and 06:00, potentially due to factors such as fatigue, decreased supervision, delayed detection, longer transport times and limited resources required for the case severity during night shifts. A recent systematic review linked non-standard working time arrangements to a higher rate of safety incidents.32 Trauma-system research has also shown that patients arriving at night may have greater injury severity and different prehospital characteristics even when overall system performance remains stable.33 34 The absence of monthly variation suggests that serious occupational hazards are present now throughout the year rather than being concentrated in narrow time windows.

Post-discharge burden

The burden of severe occupational trauma extended beyond survival to discharge. Most patients returned home, but a significant minority (13.8%) required transfer, long-term care or rehabilitation. For a largely working-age population, this matters because mortality alone does not capture the full effect of injury on independence, employment and household income. Major trauma research now gives greater weight to functional recovery and return to work, rather than survival alone, when assessing outcome.12 13 The present registry did not record disability, quality of life, return to work or lost working days, so the long-term occupational and socioeconomic burden is likely to be underestimated.

Strengths and limitations

The study has several strengths. It draws on a prospective multicentre trauma registry that covers seven hospitals over a decade and focuses on patients with clinically important injury severity. This allowed a detailed description of mechanisms, timing, anatomical injury patterns and hospital outcomes in a large cohort. There are also limitations. The registry captures hospitalised patients and in-hospital deaths, so minor injuries and deaths at the scene are not represented. Because work-relatedness was based on registry coding, detailed information on occupation, industry, employer, employment duration, safety training and personal protective equipment was not available. Work-relatedness was determined using registry coding based on information from triage records, clinical documentation and discharge summaries. Although coding was performed by trained personnel using standardised procedures, some degree of misclassification remains possible, particularly in cases where occupational circumstances were incompletely documented. Such misclassification may have led to an underestimation of the true burden of work-related injuries. Additionally, the predominantly expatriate workforce represented in this cohort may have introduced communication challenges between patients and healthcare providers. Language barriers could potentially affect the accuracy and completeness of occupational information recorded in the medical record, thereby contributing to misclassification of work-relatedness. The analysis was restricted to patients with ISS≥12, so it does not reflect the full spectrum of workplace injury. Post-discharge follow-up data were unavailable in the registry; therefore, long-term outcomes were not assessed. A major limitation of this study is the absence of workforce denominator data. Consequently, the findings describe the characteristics of patients with severe work-related injuries rather than injury risks within specific demographic or occupational groups. The study cannot determine whether any group was disproportionately represented among injured workers relative to its proportion in the workforce, nor can injury incidence rates be calculated.

Conclusion

In this 10-year multicentre trauma registry study, major work-related trauma in Abu Dhabi was concentrated among young non-local men, was driven mainly by falls from height and struck-by objects, and commonly produced multisystem injury with substantial ICU use, in-hospital mortality and post-acute care needs. These findings show that occupational injury is both a prevention priority and a trauma-system issue in Abu Dhabi. Prevention should focus on work-at-height safety and protection in high-risk sectors, while service planning should account for rehabilitation and longer-term recovery after severe occupational trauma.
