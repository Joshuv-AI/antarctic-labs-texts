Successful Endoscopic Management of Complete Tracheal Occlusion in a Child
Authors: Bhavya Balaji, Aureen Ruby Dcunha, B. Sandeep Rai, B. H. Giridhar, Swathi Sunil Rao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTProlonged mechanical ventilation following cardiopulmonary and neurological events oftentimes necessitates a tracheostomy and tracheal granulation is one of its most common late complications. The literature recommends that large granulation be managed through surgical excision or endoluminal techniques. A 6-year-old boy presented with high-grade tracheal stenosis secondary to endotracheal cuff-related injury and prolonged tracheostomy. We present an unconventional yet successful method of management that included multiple sittings of endoscopic rigid bronchoscopic dilation and endoluminal cryotherapy.

INTRODUCTION

Acquired pediatric tracheal stenosis usually occurs secondary to prolonged intubation (6%–21%) or tracheostomy (0.6%–21%).[12] Traumatic, multiple intubations in emergency situations and unplanned extubation frequently contribute to mucosal injury and prolong the duration of intubation often necessitating a tracheostomy. The narrow lumen of the pediatric airway along with segmental laryngotracheal vascularity and tube movement cause regional ischemia of the cartilaginous rings leading to pressure necrosis which ultimately heals with granulation or scarring.[3]

Endoluminal treatment options available include laser, electrocoagulation, mechanical debulking, balloon dilation, argon plasma coagulation, stenting, cryotherapy, or local mitomycin injections either singly or in combination with steroids.[456] Endoscopic failure, recurrence, or selected cases of Myer–Cotton Grade III-IV stenosis,[7] however, may require surgical resection and reconstruction.[4] In this case of tracheal stenosis, management was fraught with multiple challenges including a high-grade lesion, neurological comorbidity, prolonged duration of treatment, and limited finances.

CASE REPORT

A 6-year-old boy presented to the casualty with continuous seizures and reduced consciousness following a road traffic accident. His Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) was 6/15 and he was in refractory status epilepticus. To secure the airway, the child was intubated with a 5 mm cuffed endotracheal tube mindful of neuroprotective measures and was mechanically ventilated. Clinical examination substantiated with radio imaging concluded a diagnosis of severe traumatic brain injury. The continuing seizures mandated a week-long administration of an anesthetic agent. Magnetic resonance imaging confirmed diffuse axonal injury explaining the sustained poor GCS with status epilepticus. Anticipating prolonged ventilation, an elective tracheostomy was performed on day 12, using a 5.5 mm cuffed tracheostomy tube. A gradual improvement in the child’s neurological status by day 25 prompted a trial of decannulation which failed. A computed tomography (CT) of the airway revealed a long segment (1.2 cm) occlusion with granulation tissue causing a total luminal obstruction [Figure 1]. An initial diagnostic rigid bronchoscopy confirmed the absence of a lumen [Figure 2]. A mechanical dilation with rigid bronchoscopy was performed to create a working channel which revealed that the granulation was originating from the supra-stomal area. Subsequently, the seizures ceased and antiepileptic drugs were titrated. The child’s neurological status drastically improved and he was able to ambulate. He was discharged home with the tracheostomy in situ. Definitive management mandated three sittings of rigid bronchoscopic dilation with sequential upsizing of the scope (initially size 3.5 mm and later 4.5 mm) which facilitated introduction of the cryoprobe. Thereafter, 15 sittings of weekly cryotherapeutic ablation of the granulation were performed. An Erbe carbon dioxide cryo unit was used to deliver cryotherapy through a 1.2 mm flexible cryoprobe, each time under general anesthesia using the tracheostomy for airway maintenance. The initial few dilations and cryotherapy sessions lasted between 30 and 45 min but with subsequent sittings, the time was halved. There were no anesthesia or procedure-related complications encountered. An adequate lumen was achieved in 3 months following which the child was successfully decannulated and could vocalize. On follow-up at 1, 6, and 34 months, he was phonating well with complete respiratory and neurological recovery.

DISCUSSION

The incidence of intubation and tracheostomy-related morbidity and mortality is higher in the pediatric age group[89] owing to the smaller airway, pliable trachea, limited extension of the surgical field, anesthetic risks, prolonged time for decannulation, and lack of an inner cannula.[4] When secondary to endotracheal intubation, cuff-related injury is mostly responsible. A meta-analysis by Lubianca Neto et al.[3] revealed the most common pediatric tracheostomy-related complications to be granulomas, skin infections, cannula obstruction, and a persistent tracheocutaneous fistula. Peristomal exophytic granulation tissue, when present, narrows the lumen causing failure of decannulation and chronically leads to stenosis.[10] Our patient developed stenosis due to a combination of these factors-intractable seizures causing cuff-related frictional damage and prolonged duration of tracheostomy causing exuberant granulation.

There is a lack of consensus on the ideal management modality. Long-segment involvement with multiple stenoses often necessitates extensive tracheal resection. Surgical failure (5%–15%) and mortality (up to 5%)[46] have been reported. These along with surgical ineligibility have prompted the eruption of newer techniques, cryotherapy being one of the lesser explored options. Although patients with lesions >1 cm are surgical candidates,[11] we opted to avoid surgery due to the morbidity and costs involved.

Newer endoscopic techniques of ablation of tracheal granulation tissue include endoluminal laser, electrocoagulation, mechanical debulking, balloon dilation, argon plasma coagulation, stenting, cryotherapy, or local mitomycin injections either singly or in combination with steroids.[456] We chose cryotherapy due to its known efficacy, safety profile,[12] and ease of availability at our institution.

Cryotherapy is used in retinoblastoma, skin warts, and removal of endobronchial airway lesions and tumors.[1213] There is, however, a paucity of literature on its use in pediatric tracheal stenosis. In our patient, although complex, the stenosis was amenable to rigid bronchoscopic dilation which facilitated subsequent endoluminal cryotherapy. Cryotherapy works through rapid freeze-thaw cycles causing cell death and tissue necrosis or adherence without the disadvantage of cautery-induced thermal damage. In addition, granulation due to its intracellular water content and vascularity is cryosensitive.[1213] The tracheostomy in our patient precluded the need to protect the distal airway which is otherwise imperative since granulation is friable and bleeds easily.

Management is fraught with several difficulties including limited working space with endoluminal procedures, anesthetic concerns due to a compromised airway, and presence of preexisting morbidity. The child and caretakers are both hospital-bound for long and costs involved can put a significant financial strain on families from lower socioeconomic strata. Poor finances often push parents to prematurely abort treatment. We were able to ensure uninterrupted treatment by offering adequate psychological and emotional support while keeping a close telephonic follow-up.

A tailor-made decision with multidisciplinary involvement including experienced pediatricians, surgeons, interventional pulmonologists, anesthetists, and radiologists is essential for the success of management. Yet, despite good reported success of various treatment modalities, prevention rather than treatment of tracheal stenosis should be targeted. Early treatment of infections, use of appropriate-sized tubes, early tracheostomy when indicated, and timely tube change can reduce the incidence of stenosis.

CONCLUSION

Bearing in mind the challenges encountered in managing children with complete tracheal occlusion, aiming for prevention is better than attempting to treat.

Rigid bronchoscopic dilation combined with endoluminal cryotherapy is a promising and definitive option for high-grade tracheal stenosis with good long-term results.

Multidisciplinary support is essential when managing conditions with extensive morbidity and prolonged hospital stays.
