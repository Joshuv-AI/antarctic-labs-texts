Brain-wide resting-state fMRI network dynamics elicited by activation of single thalamic input
Authors: Linshan Xie, Xunda Wang, Xuehong Lin, Junjian Wen, Teng Ma, Alex T L Leong, Ed X Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding how the brain rapidly processes neural information through large-scale network dynamics is fundamental to neuroscience. Resting-state fMRI (rsfMRI) captures networks by probing spontaneous neural activity fluctuations, yet whether these networks can accommodate fast, sensitive information processing remains unknown. Here, we reveal brain-wide rsfMRI network dynamics (i.e., states and their transitions) that enable fast information processing through rapid activity-triggered network reconfiguration in male rats. Following activation of a single optogenetically evoked somatosensory thalamic input, states and transitions associated with internally-oriented information processing are quickly suppressed and diverted through basal forebrain and hypothalamus to strengthen externally-oriented (i.e., sensory) processes. Simultaneous rsfMRI and electrophysiology measurements in somatosensory and cingulate cortices demonstrate that intra-regional wide-band and inter-regional narrow-band (i.e., slow-oscillations) neural activity synchronizations directly drive rsfMRI network dynamics and reconfiguration to support fast processing of single thalamic input. Our study uncovers fast interactions between neural activity and rsfMRI networks in processing single neural input.Subject terms: Functional magnetic resonance imaging, Optogenetics, Neural circuits

Introduction

The brain is a complex system composed of diverse interacting neural populations, circuits, and networks that are essential for everyday functions and behaviors1. In recent decades, resting-state functional MRI (rsfMRI) has emerged as a robust, non-invasive imaging technique for mapping brain-wide functional networks across species in both humans and rodents2–7. Notably, such rsfMRI measurable networks are highly correlated with diverse sensory, behavioral, and cognitive task performance8,9, and can be used to phenotype brain dysfunction10,11. Furthermore, rsfMRI networks are increasingly recognized for exhibiting dynamic processes driven by spontaneous neural activity fluctuations12–15, which are crucial for neural information flow and processing16–20. Dissecting these large-scale, time-varying networks during both resting and task execution can help unravel the brain’s functional architecture and its role in neural information processing.

Brain network communication primarily relies on the flow of activity among neuronal populations through numerous pathways21. This process is intrinsically dynamic because the brain must continuously adapt the behaviors to environmental changes, switching between brain states that process externally- and internally-oriented information22–24. Externally-oriented processing is driven by stimuli and task demands22, while internally-oriented processing relies on intrinsic cognitive processes such as memory retrieval23,24. Recent studies have explored network characteristics or configurations, such as network connectivity states and their transitions, which underlie the dynamics of large-scale rsfMRI networks25–27. These rsfMRI network states and transitions closely parallel the brain states and the switching within and between these brain states, which are characterized by patterns of neural activity associated with physiological or cognitive processes28,29, like wake-NREM sleep stages30 and shifts in cognitive task demands31,32. Studies have shown that rsfMRI networks can adjust their configurations to accommodate alternating task demands and behavioral contexts17,32–35. For instance, rsfMRI network reconfiguration supports the selective rerouting of visual information in sensory systems34 and the access of learned information from cognitive systems to aid decision-making33. However, in these sensory and cognitive tasks, external stimuli—such as an oriented bar in the visual field36, whisker deflection37, an odor38, or an auditory tone39 evoke a cluster of complex and long-lasting neural activities that intermingle and cascade with neural information flows from multiple parallel pathways, making it challenging to investigate whether and how rsfMRI network dynamics rapidly process specific neural activity. Additionally, in contrast to localized sensory-evoked or optogenetically-evoked hemodynamic responses (with onset delays as short as 400 milliseconds40,41), the change in rsfMRI network configurations is often perceived as slow. This is primarily due to the low-frequency fluctuations (0.005–0.1 Hz) of blood-oxygenation-level-dependent (BOLD) rsfMRI signals and the nature of the tasks or stimuli. In other words, while it is known that rsfMRI network configuration can probe the dynamics of brain-wide functional networks, it remains unclear and challenging to determine whether rsfMRI networks can support fast neural information flow and processing by sensitively accommodating relatively simple and short-lasting neural activity, as well as to understand the mechanisms underlying these processes.

In this study, we investigated how brain-wide rsfMRI networks enable fast neural information flow and processing. We examined the effects of activating single thalamic input on rsfMRI network dynamics using single-pulse optogenetic stimulation of the somatosensory thalamus. Such stimulation allowed us to bypass parallel sensory pathways typically engaged by external stimuli, and to avoid the complex cascades and excessive synchronization of neural populations caused by prolonged or repetitive stimulation. By using a single, brief neural population activity input, we were able to capture activity-triggered rapid reconfiguration of rsfMRI networks while minimizing disruption of ongoing spontaneous neural activities. Using unsupervised machine learning analysis on rsfMRI data obtained with and without optogenetic stimulation, we identified key elements of brain-wide rsfMRI network dynamics, including transient network states and their hierarchical transitions, with spatiotemporally specific evolution patterns both within and between states. We demonstrated that these network states and transitions could rapidly reconfigure on the order of a few seconds (~2 s to begin reconfiguring; ~10 s to normalize) in response to a single neural population activity input from the somatosensory thalamus, suppressing internally-oriented processing activities and redirecting them to externally-oriented sensorimotor information processing. Combining simultaneous electrophysiology and fMRI measurements, we further revealed that this reconfiguration was driven by distinct synchronizations of neural activity within and between networks, occurring across specific time and frequency windows.

Results

To investigate the rapid dynamics of brain-wide rsfMRI networks and their responses to activation of a single brief thalamic input, we conducted interleaved rsfMRI measurements both with and without sparse single-pulse (10 ms, 0.066% duty cycle) optogenetic stimulation of the ventral posteromedial thalamic nucleus (VPM) thalamocortical excitatory neurons42,43 in lightly anesthetized rats (~1% isoflurane; Fig. 1A, B). We identified typical rsfMRI networks using seed-based analysis5,43,44 and derived elements of brain-wide rsfMRI network dynamics (i.e., states and their transitions) through Gaussian Hidden Markov Modeling45 (HMM; Fig. 1C). To further analyze the spatiotemporal evolutions of rsfMRI network states, each state was decomposed into three substates based on its probability time course. Voxel-wise activation maps were generated from pre-processed BOLD rsfMRI time courses aligned with the corresponding substates. By examining the evoked neural activity propagation structure and neural activity variabilities upon single-pulse optogenetic stimulation, we first demonstrated that this stimulation paradigm served as an effective tool for studying single thalamic activity-triggered rsfMRI network reconfiguration. We then examined the reconfiguration of rsfMRI networks and the stability of their dynamics in response to single-pulse VPM stimulation. Guided by the imaging findings, we conducted simultaneous rsfMRI and electrophysiology experiments to investigate the neural bases underlying the observed rsfMRI network dynamics upon a single neural population activity input from the somatosensory thalamus (Fig. 1D, E).

RsfMRI network dynamics characterized by transient states and transition structures with distinct spatiotemporally evolving patterns

Firstly, we examined the basic properties of rsfMRI network dynamics, such as brain-wide network states and their transitions, in the absence of VPM optogenetic stimulation. Previous studies using Gaussian HMM typically presented region of interest (ROI)-wise rsfMRI network state activation maps based on principal component analysis (PCA) time courses30,45,46. This approach often obscures the spatiotemporal neural activity distributions across multiple voxels within each ROI due to averaging, and it also discards the variance inherent in each imaging voxel due to PCA. Here, we derived rsfMRI network state voxel-wise activation maps from BOLD time courses, enabling the visualization and examination of detailed spatial distributions of neural activities and the polarity of BOLD signals. We further decomposed these states to dissect their temporal properties of the transitions within and between states (Fig. 1C).

The typical homotopic sensory, default mode, salience, limbic, and striatal rsfMRI networks were first computed using seed-based analysis (ROI definition & functional connectivity maps: Supplementary Fig. S1A, B). We then characterized brain-wide rsfMRI network states and their transitions in a data-driven manner via an 18-state HMM model. Eighteen states were chosen based on model consistency47 (i.e., high mean pairwise similarity of state time courses across five iterations), clustering performance48 (i.e., high Calinski–Harabasz score), and model stability49 (i.e., low free energy; Supplementary Fig. S2). States 1–14 exhibited relatively short lifetimes (mean: 1–6 s; standard error of the mean, SEM: ±0.6 s) and high occurrence rates (>17 times/h), indicating their transient and repeatedly occurring properties (Fig. 2A). Notably, these HMM-derived rsfMRI network states resembled combinations of several seed-based rsfMRI networks (Supplementary Fig. S1). States 1, 2, 4, and 5 were dominated by positive (states 1, 2, 4) or negative (state 5) activities in the bilateral somatosensory cortices (primary somatosensory barrel field, S1BF, limb, S1Limb, upper lip area, S1ULp, and secondary somatosensory, S2, cortices), with additional activities in other networks. For instance, state 1 also involved negative activities in auditory (Aud), visual (VC), retrosplenial (RSC) and cingulate (Cg) cortices, while states 2 and 5 showed positive and negative activities in other sensorimotor cortices (Aud, VC, and motor cortex, MC), respectively. Beyond the somatosensory cortices, state 4 also exhibited positive activities in striatal regions (caudate putamen, CPu, and nucleus accumbens, NAc) and default-mode network (DMN)-related regions (Aud, insular area, Ins, Cg, and orbitofrontal cortex, OFC)50. Moreover, state 3 exhibited negative activities in DMN-related regions (RSC, Cg, and OFC), while state 6 exhibited negative striatal (CPu and NAc), hippocampus (HP), Ins, and OFC activities, accompanied by positive activities in the basal forebrain (BF) and hypothalamus (HTh). We observed that states 7–10 exhibited strong brain-wide positive (states 7 and 8) or negative (states 9 and 10) activities located in the DMN-related regions (Aud, Ins, temporal association cortex, TeA, Cg, prelimbic cortex, PrL, and OFC), sensorimotor cortices (primary somatosensory cortices, S1, S2, MC, and VC), and striatal regions (CPu, NAc, globus pallidus and ventral pallidum, GP & VP). Meanwhile, states 11 and 12 exhibited robust negative and positive HP activities, respectively, while states 13 and 14 primarily exhibited positive activities in DMN-related regions (Aud, Ins, TeA, RSC, Cg, PrL, and OFC). It is important to note that we observed activities in other sensory cortices (e.g., Aud and VC), besides the somatosensory cortices, albeit they were often accompanied by other networks, such as HP, RSC, and Cg. Several states that were not detected in most animals and exhibited excessively long lifetimes, high fractional occupancies, and/or low occurrence rates were excluded from our analysis. Specifically, states 15, 17, and 18 were only observed in a few animals and had relatively long lifetimes (>19 s) and high fractional occupancies (>28%), while states 16 and 17 occurred only 3.7 ± 5.2 and 1.4 ± 2.4 times/h, respectively.

We then examined neural information flow across the transient network states (i.e., states 1–14) within the state transition space and identified the clustering of and switching between sensorimotor network (SN)- and DMN-associated states (Fig. 2B, C). In the state transition space capturing the 90th percentile of transitions, states were organized based on the temporal characteristics of their transitions and activation maps. Hierarchical clustering analyses were conducted based on the cross-correlations among state time courses51 and the correlations of state fractional occupancies across animals45, respectively. We showed three distinct hierarchical organizations of rsfMRI network state transitions, wherein states within the same transition structures exhibited temporally correlated dynamics (Supplementary Fig. S3). Sensorimotor network-driven feedforward pathways (SN-FP) were predominantly initiated from state 1, which exhibited strong bilateral somatosensory network activities. We observed a sequential transition route from positive SN activities in state 1 to negative SN activities in state 5 (i.e., state 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 6 → 5). Meanwhile, the DMN-dominant recurrent loop (DMN-RL) was marked by a loop transition structure (i.e., state 6 → 7 → 8 → 9 → 10 → 6). DMN-RL featured states with distributed activities in key DMN-related regions (e.g., Aud, Ins, Cg, PrL, and OFC) that fluctuated between positive and negative activities. Notably, states 6 and 8, which exhibited robust activities in BF and HTh, were found to be positioned at the intersection of transitions between rsfMRI networks in DMN-RL and SN-FP. These two transition structures represented the predominant transitions in our rsfMRI network dynamics. We also observed background states that primarily exhibited outputs to other network states but no dominant inputs. These background states were marked by robust activities in HP (states 11 and 12) or DMN-related regions (states 13 and 14). Note that these states and their transitions were derived from the HMM model trained on rsfMRI data acquired both in the absence and presence of optogenetic stimulations. The findings did not differ from the HMM model trained on data without optogenetic stimulation (Supplementary Fig. S4), demonstrating that the predominant transition structures remained similar regardless of the rsfMRI data with or without single-pulse stimulation. For comparison, we also trained 14-state (Supplementary Fig. S5) and 22-state (Supplementary Fig. S6) models. Spatial features and characteristics of the states (e.g., short lifetimes, high occurrence rates), along with the predominant transition structures, remained consistent across models with varying numbers of states. However, the 14-state model tended to underfit the data by merging distinct spatial patterns and transitions (e.g., state 1 → 2 reflecting somatosensory neural activity propagation was not captured), whereas the 22-state model introduced redundant or infrequently occurring states, indicating potential overfitting.

We further characterized the temporal properties of transitions within and between rsfMRI network states (Fig. 2C). To do this, we decomposed each state into three substates: the entering, plateau, and exiting portions of a state’s time course (Fig. 1C). For within-state transitions, we found that the majority of SN-FP states (states 2, 3, and 5), one DMN-RL state (state 10), and most background states (states 11–13) were persistent, exhibiting little to no changes in the spatial distribution of neural activities and the polarity of BOLD signals across three substates. In contrast, the spatiotemporal evolution patterns for the remaining SN-FP states (states 1 and 4), DMN-RL states (states 6–9), and background state (state 14) showed significant changes in neural activity distribution and/or polarity of BOLD signals. For instance, in state 6 of DMN-RL, the negative striatal and cortical activities were strengthened with a larger spatial distribution, whereas the opposite was observed for state 8. Overall, states 1 and 4 of SN-FP and states 7 and 9 of DMN-RL exhibited increased strength and larger spatial distribution from the entering to the plateau substate. In background state 14, activities in DMN-related regions gradually peaked at the plateau but substantially decreased at the exiting substate, with a notable presence of negative striatal and cortical activities. These findings indicated that rsfMRI networks were capable of accommodating both stable and flexible information flow. For between-state transitions, we observed smooth or sharp transitions bridged by substates with or without similar regional activities, respectively. Smooth transitions mainly occurred in state 1 → 2 of SN-FP, and state 6 → 7, state 7 → 8, and state 9 → 10 of DMN-RL, while sharp ones were more common in other SN-FP transitions (e.g., state 2 → 3, state 3 → 4, state 5 → 4, state 13 → 5, state 11 → 2, state 12 → 4). Notably, states 6, 8, and 14 exhibited sharp within-state changes in BOLD activity polarities, which were consistent with the polarities of their dominant input and output states, suggesting their role in facilitating between-state transitions with opposite BOLD activity polarities.

Taken together, our analysis above reveals the transient nature of brain-wide rsfMRI network states and their hierarchical transition structures, characterized by distinct spatiotemporal evolution patterns across substates. Moreover, we underscore the potential role of neuromodulatory regional activities, such as BF and HTh activities in states 6 and 8, in switching between DMN- and SN-associated networks during the resting state.

Single-pulse-evoked VPM activity exhibiting hierarchical somatosensory thalamo-cortical neural activity propagation structures and minimal disruption on rsfMRI BOLD fluctuations

In parallel, we investigated whether sparse single-pulse optogenetic stimulation of the VPM in the somatosensory thalamus (Fig. 1A, C) could be used to interrogate single thalamic activity-triggered changes in rsfMRI networks. Precise targeting of VPM with an optical fiber was confirmed through high-resolution anatomical MRI images during experiment (Fig. 1B) and histological verification of brain slices after experiment (Fig. 1A). Histological slices confirmed that the injection target was in the VPM, where the majority of the expression of optogenetic construct (i.e., ChR2) was found (Supplementary Fig. S7). Expression was also found in parts of the adjacent thalamic nuclei, such as posterior nucleus (PO) and ventral posterolateral nucleus (VPL), due to diffusion of the injected viral vector solution from the middle of VPM42–44,52.

By averaging across numerous (i.e., 3192) optogenetic stimulation events, we demonstrated the expected evoked BOLD responses in somatosensory regions, including ipsilateral VPM, bilateral S1BF, S1Limb, S1ULp, and S2 (ROI definition & activation map: Fig. 3A, B; BOLD single profiles: Supplementary Fig. S8A). Consistent with a previous study53, we also observed BOLD activations in other sensorimotor regions such as visual (lateral geniculate nucleus, LGN, superior colliculus, SC, and visual cortex, V1 and V2), auditory (medial geniculate body, MGB, and Aud), and motor (MC) regions, primarily in the ipsilateral hemisphere. Importantly, we detected activations in higher-order cortices associated with cognition, such as the bilateral Ins and piriform cortex (Pir) and the ipsilateral parietal cortex (PtA), limbic regions, including the bilateral amygdala (Amg), entorhinal cortex (EC), and ipsilateral ventral and dorsal hippocampus (vHP and dHP), and striatal regions, such as ipsilateral CPu, NAc, and GP & VP. Despite ChR2 expression in PO and VPL (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S7), the single-pulse-evoked BOLD responses also confirmed the specificity of stimulating VPM, as the downstream targets differed from those observed when stimulating PO42 or VPL53. In general, we demonstrate that single-pulse-evoked neural activities propagate brain-wide, beyond the primary sensory regions traditionally detected in our previous fMRI studies52 and without massive averaging, but echo multisite electrophysiological recordings54. This is consistent with our knowledge that a single excitatory spike can trigger multiple postsynaptic spikes, enabling quick amplification of neural activation at downstream targets55.

We found that single-pulse-evoked neural activity propagated along central pathways as demonstrated by natural sensory53 or thalamic pulse-train stimulations42,56, reflecting the hierarchical neural information flow in the somatosensory pathways (Fig. 3C). Initially, neural activity arrived at the ipsilateral somatosensory cortex before engaging the contralateral counterpart. It then propagated from primary somatosensory regions, including S1BF, S1ULp, and S1Limb, to the adjacent higher-order somatosensory cortex, S2. Subsequently, we observed cross-sensory-modality activity propagation from somatosensory to motor, visual, and auditory cortices, followed by cross-system activity propagation from sensorimotor to limbic, striatal, and higher-order cortical regions. To visualize this process, we generated a movie demonstrating the single-pulse-evoked activity propagation (Supplementary Movie S1). Moreover, we found that voxels within the ipsilateral S1 and S2 (the earliest four activated ROIs) were instantaneously and widely activated upon VPM stimulation, whereas voxels in other regions showed more gradual and spatially restricted activation (Fig. 3C). Additionally, previous electrophysiological studies showed that sparse single-pulse stimulation promptly evoked thalamic and cortical activities while avoiding the prolonged and complex neural interactions induced by external sensory or pulse-train stimulations42,52,57. This highlighted the efficiency and simplicity of single-pulse-evoked activity in eliciting hierarchical neural activity propagation.

Variability is an inherent property of spontaneous neural activities and plays a crucial role in shaping evoked activities58 and subsequent behavioral functions59,60. However, under conditions of pulse-train stimulations, both local and distant neural activity can exhibit excessive synchronization, overriding this intrinsic variability59,61. We demonstrated that sparse single-pulse-evoked activity propagation maintained local-to-global neural response amplitude and latency variabilities (amplitude: Fig. 3D; latency: Fig. 3C), indicating minimal disruption to ongoing spontaneous neural activities. Specifically, variations in the local temporal signal-to-noise ratio (tSNR) and BOLD activation amplitudes at the primary projection target of the VPM (ipsilateral/right S1BF, S1BFR) followed a lognormal distribution, signifying local neural response amplitude variabilities upon single-pulse stimulation. Meanwhile, the strength of long-range activity propagation, measured by cumulative activated voxels excluding S1BFR, reflected global neural response amplitude variabilities. Lognormal distributions, which spontaneous neuronal firing rates typically follow62, imply multiplicative processes involved in both local and long-range propagation of neural activity. We also observed response latency variabilities upon stimulation within each region and across the whole brain, as indicated by the activated voxel distribution and voxel-wise activation latency.

Furthermore, we showed that brain network dynamics, particularly rsfMRI network states 1 and 2 and their transitions, mirrored the hierarchical somatosensory thalamo-cortical activity propagation structures (from S1 to remote brain areas). We found high spatiotemporal similarities between the early S1 activation patterns evoked by stimulation and the activation map of state 1 (highest correlation coefficient = 0.56), and between the activation patterns covering both S1 and subsequent remote regions and the activation map of state 2 (highest correlation coefficient = 0.46; Fig. 3E vs. Fig. 3F). Notably, the sequential transition from state 1 to state 2 (Fig. 3G) coincided with the peak time frames of these spatial similarities (Fig. 3F). Additionally, other higher-order region-associated states, i.e., states 3–14, also exhibited spatial similarities with the later evoked brain-wide activation patterns, albeit to a lesser extent (Supplementary Fig. S8B).

Our findings here demonstrate that single-pulse optogenetic VPM stimulation can serve as an effective tool for probing the response properties of rsfMRI network dynamics while minimally disrupting the intrinsic neural activities and networks. This is supported by the exhibition of hierarchical somatosensory neural activity propagation structures and the preservation of both local and brain-wide neural activity variabilities. Moreover, the flow of neural information in rsfMRI network dynamics (i.e., state transitions) appears to reflect these hierarchical activity propagation structures.

Rapid rsfMRI network reconfiguration while retaining overall stability of network dynamics upon a single thalamic input

To dissect the brain-wide resting functional network dynamics in response to the single thalamic input in VPM, we compared rsfMRI network dynamics in the absence (OG-) vs. presence (OG+) of sparse single-pulse stimulation.

Our results showed that a single somatosensory thalamic input rapidly altered the probability profiles of most transient rsfMRI network states, as evidenced by statistics and large effect sizes (FDR-corrected p value < 0.05 & Cohen’s d value > 0.8; states 1–10, 13, and 14; Fig. 4A, B). For the majority of these states (states 1, 2, 4–7, 9, and 13), the alterations occurred rapidly within ~2 s and returned to baseline levels ~10 s after stimulation. The probabilities were selectively increased (states 1–3 & 9–11) or decreased (states 4–8 & 12–14). Specifically, the probabilities of states with positive somatosensory cortical activities (states 1, 2, 9, 10) increased, while those with negative somatosensory cortical activities (states 5–8) decreased, as expected in the presence of an input from the somatosensory thalamus. Conversely, the probabilities of states with positive activities in DMN-related regions, either with (state 13) or without (state 14) HP activities, and with CPu and S2 activities (state 4) decreased, while the probability of a state with negative activities in DMN-related regions (state 3) increased. Further, the probability of the state with robust positive HP activities (state 12) decreased, with an increase in the state with negative HP activities (state 11). Notably, the latencies of peak probability changes in DMN-RL states followed their corresponding transition structures, with the earliest significant probability change observed in state 6, followed sequentially by states 7, 8, 9, and finally 10 (Fig. 4B). However, the overall state characteristics such as lifetime, occurrence rate, and fractional occupancy remained largely unaltered (OG- vs. OG+: Fig. 2A vs. Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. S9. Additionally, we trained HMM models with different dataset combinations to ensure our findings were not impacted by individual animal variability. Here, each dataset excluded one animal from the original, generating 17 and 11 different dataset combinations for OG- and OG+ animal groups, respectively. We confirmed that state time courses generated across different combinations were not skewed by any individual animal (i.e., Gaussian distributed with ~65% of datapoints within one standard deviation of the mean; Supplementary Fig. S10). We also noted that the single thalamic input did not disrupt the within- and between-state spatiotemporal evolution patterns, although, as expected, positive ipsilateral sensorimotor activities increased, accompanied by a parallel decrease in negative bilateral activities (OG-: Fig. 2C; OG+: Fig. 4D).

Further, we demonstrated that a single thalamic input reconfigured the rsfMRI network state transitions while largely maintaining the overall transition pathways and durations. The dominant state transition structures (i.e., pathways shown in the state transition space) were mostly preserved upon stimulation. Only transition probabilities from state 8 to SN-FP initiating state 1 and state 13 → 2 were significantly increased, whereas those of state 11 → 2 and state 2 → 3 were decreased (Fig. 5A, B). Single thalamic input reduced the transition probabilities to DMN-RL and their occurrence rates, while increasing those to SN-FP, without affecting the durations of their respective transition structures (probability: Fig. 5A; occurrence rate & duration: Fig. 5C). We further examined whether such reconfiguration of state transitions was affected by the specific rsfMRI network state that is active at the time of single-pulse stimulation. Overall, we found that transitions toward states 1 and 2 were increased to initiate the SN-FP transition structure, regardless of the ongoing rsfMRI network dynamics at the time of stimulation (Supplementary Fig. S11). Further, when stimulation occurred during an active DMN-RL state, subsequent transitions to maintain the loop structure were suppressed (i.e., state 6 → 7, state 7 → 8, state 8 → 9, state 9 → 10, state 10 → 6; Supplementary Fig. S11B). Notably, states 6 and 8, characterized by robust BF and HTh activities, could transition into both SN-FP and DMN-RL, suggesting their bifurcation role in state transitions between SN-FP and DMN-RL (Fig. 5B). State 6-initiated DMN-RL transition structure exhibited the highest occurrence rate compared to DMN-RLs initiated from other states (states 7–10) at resting state, indicating the crucial role of state 6 in initiating the loop (Fig. 5C). However, in the presence of a brief thalamic input, the occurrence rate of the state 6-initiated DMN-RL structure was significantly suppressed (Fig. 5C). We observed only a moderate decrease (−21%, not significant; Fig. 5D) in the occurrence rate of state 6 → 7 transitions, implicating that other rsfMRI network state(s) might be involved in terminating this loop structure. State 8 showed a significant decrease (−49%) in transitioning to state 9 but a significant increase (+61%) in transitioning to SN-FP through state 1 (Fig. 5D). These findings indicated that a single-pulse somatosensory thalamic input likely disrupted the neural information flow in DMN-RL, diverting it to SN-FP through state 8.

Together, our results reveal that a single somatosensory thalamic input rapidly decreases internally-driven DMN-associated information processing and diverts resources to boost externally-oriented sensorimotor network activities, all while maintaining stable rsfMRI network dynamics such as state characteristics and overall transition structures. This reconfiguration likely involves neuromodulatory hubs, i.e., BF and HTh, which initiate and terminate internally-driven neural information flow and processing upon a thalamic input.

Local wide-band and long-range narrow-band neural activity synchronizations underlying rsfMRI network dynamics and their reconfiguration upon single thalamic input

Having established the key elements of brain-wide rsfMRI network dynamics and rapid network reconfiguration upon a single thalamic input, we further investigated their underlying neural bases. We employed rsfMRI along with simultaneous electrocorticography (ECoG) recordings in key cortical regions of the DMN (i.e., Cg) and SN (i.e., S1; Fig. 1D), providing high temporal and spectral resolution of network dynamics. Note that ECoG is a surface cortical recording and is largely insensitive to deep brain regions. Properties of rsfMRI network states and their transitions were consistent with those acquired without ECoG recordings (Supplementary Fig. S12), and the rsfMRI networks were similarly reconfigured by single-pulse stimulation (Supplementary Fig. S13). We also confirmed the consistency of brain states across animals based on stable physiological ranges (rectal temperature: 36.5–37.5 °C, heart rate: 350–420 beats/min, breathing: ~60 breaths/min, oxygen saturation: >95%) and the occurrence rates of neural oscillations (Supplementary Fig. S14).

In the absence of optogenetic stimulations, we found that transient rsfMRI network states were accompanied by distinct patterns of intra- and inter-regional neural activity synchronization, primarily occurring before state onsets, as indicated in state-aligned local Cg and S1 power spectrograms and inter-regional Cg-S1 coherence spectrograms (Fig. 6A). In the local power spectrograms, DMN-RL states and their transition destination states in SN-FP displayed robust wide-band biphasic (states 7–9) or triphasic (states 4–6 & 10) patterns of neural activity synchronizations, whereas other SN-FP states exhibited weaker monophasic patterns (states 1–3; with S1 showing more robustness than Cg; Fig. 6A and Supplementary Fig. S15A, B). Slow (0.2–1 Hz), delta (1–4 Hz), spindle (7–15 Hz), and gamma (>25 Hz) neural oscillatory events in both Cg and S1 coincided with these neural activity patterns, confirming that these local synchronizations were indeed wide-band (OG-: Supplementary Figs. S16, S17). Meanwhile, long-range neural activity synchronizations were primarily pronounced at slow oscillations, as shown in the inter-regional Cg-S1 spectrograms. Most DMN-RL states (states 6–9) exhibited the most robust Cg-S1 coherence, followed by several SN-FP states (states 1, 2, 5), while the remaining states (states 3, 4, 10) exhibited relatively weaker coherence (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Fig. S15C). To explore the relationship between neural activity and rsfMRI network states, we conducted multivariate Granger causality analysis. We observed that local wide-band neural activity synchronizations at resting-state significantly predicted the state time courses (Cg: states 1–4, 6–10 & S1: states 2, 4, 6–8, 10), while long-range low-frequency (<15 Hz) synchronizations predicted the time courses of states 3 and 5 (Supplementary Fig. S18), indicating that neural activity directly contribute to rsfMRI network dynamics.

To investigate the neural underpinnings of the transitions between rsfMRI network states, we examined neural activity synchronizations in ECoG recordings during these transitions. We focused on states that were crucial for mediating transitions between SN-FP and DMN-RL, specifically states 6 and 8. We observed that the local neural activity synchronization patterns in Cg and S1 varied and shared certain similarities with those of their corresponding transition destinations (states 1, 4, 5, 7, 9; Fig. 6A and Supplementary Fig. S19A), supporting the switching between different transition structures. For example, state 6 → 7 and state 8 → 9 transitions within the DMN-RL structure exhibited larger wide-band biphasic or triphasic patterns compared to state 6 → 5 and state 8 → 4/1 transitions out of DMN-RL into the SN-FP structure. In parallel, Cg-S1 coherence was primarily concentrated at slow oscillations, with the exception of the state 8 → 1 (Supplementary Fig. S19B). These pronounced long-range neural activity synchronizations were distributed in distinct time windows mostly prior to the onset of states, depending on whether the states were transitioning to DMN-RL or SN-FP (to DMN-RL: state 6 → 7: −3 to 1 s, state 8 → 9: −4 to −2 s and ~ 10 s; to SN-FP: state 6 → 5: −4 to 1 s, state 8 → 4: −4 to −1 s).

Subsequently, we examined the underlying neural activity of rsfMRI network states in response to a single brief somatosensory thalamic input. We confirmed that single-pulse stimulation could modulate intra- and inter-regional neural activity synchronizations across all frequency bands (Supplementary Figs. S20, S21). Importantly, the thalamic input consistently modulated local neural activity synchronizations in Cg and S1 across a wide range of frequency bands, as well as their long-range synchronizations at low-frequency bands (especially at slow and delta oscillations). Specifically, in local spectrograms, multiple slow (Cg: states 1–4, 6–10 & S1: states 1–3, 5–10) and delta (Cg & S1: states 1–10) oscillation clusters in most SN-FP states and all DMN-RL states were modulated (OG+ vs. OG-: Fig. 6B and Supplementary Figs. S22, S23). Some states also exhibited modulated theta (4–7 Hz; Cg: states 3, 6, 7, 10 & S1: states 6, 8, 10), spindle (Cg: states 4, 6, 8, 10 & S1: states 3-10), beta (15–25 Hz; Cg: states 5, 7, 8 & S1: states 2, 5–8, 10), and gamma clusters (Cg: states 3, 7 & S1: states 1, 2, 8, 10). Notably, the thalamic input led to substantial attenuation of the triphasic pattern in state 6, making it more similar to that of state 5, while the biphasic pattern of state 8 developed into a triphasic pattern across slow and delta oscillations, resembling state 4. Moreover, in Cg-S1 coherence spectrograms, we observed that slow and delta oscillation clusters of most SN-FP states (slow: states 1, 2, 4, 5; delta: states 2, 4, 5) were altered, while only the coherence of slow oscillations was affected for DMN-RL states (states 6–10; Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. S24). Multivariate Granger Causality analysis indicated that both local and long-range low-frequency neural activity synchronizations, which were modulated by single-pulse stimulation, preceded and predicted the reconfiguration of state probability profiles, especially at slow oscillations (Cg: states 1, 7, 8 & S1: states 2, 5–7 & Cg-S1: 1, 2; Supplementary Fig. S25). These results demonstrate that local and long-range low-frequency neural activity synchronizations can directly shape the probability profiles of rsfMRI network states during neural information flow and processing.

Lastly, we examined the effects of a single somatosensory thalamic input on underlying neural activities during rsfMRI network transitions. In local power spectrograms, we identified clusters in the slow and delta frequency bands in transitions involving states 6 and 8 that were altered upon stimulation, along with some clusters in the theta, spindle, beta, and/or gamma frequency bands (theta: state 6 → 7/5, state 8 → 4; spindle: state 6 → 5 and state 8 → 4/1; beta: state 6 → 5 and state 8 → 9; gamma: state 6 → 7 and state 8 → 9/1; Supplementary Fig. S26). These altered neural activity patterns were distinct for transitions within the DMN-RL and SN-FP structures. In particular, state 6 → 7 and state 8 → 9 transitions in DMN-RL featured respective triphasic and biphasic patterns that were largely attenuated across wide frequency bands, while transitions to SN-FP, i.e., state 6 → 5 and state 8 → 4 or 1, only showed modulation at the slow and delta oscillations. Moreover, the brief thalamic input consistently altered long-range Cg-S1 neural activity synchronizations across multiple low-frequency clusters, particularly at slow and delta oscillations (Fig. 6C).

Our findings collectively demonstrate that local neural activity synchronizations across various frequency bands, along with parallel long-range synchronizations at slow oscillations, drive brain-wide rsfMRI network dynamics. Single-pulse somatosensory thalamic input primarily alters DMN- and SN-associated neural activities at low-frequency bands, leading to the reconfiguration of rsfMRI networks to support externally-oriented information processing.

Local gross neural activity synchronizations maintaining the overall stability of rsfMRI network dynamics upon a single thalamic input

We also investigated the neural underpinnings of stable rsfMRI network dynamics in the presence of thalamic inputs, such as unaltered state characteristics (i.e., lifetime, fractional occupancy, and occurrence rate) and overall state transition structures (i.e., spatiotemporal evolutions, dominant transition pathways, and duration of SN-FP and DMN-RL).

We observed largely preserved triphasic, biphasic, or monophasic patterns of gross (i.e., spectrally non-specific) neural activity synchronizations in local Cg (Fig. 7A) and S1 (Supplementary Fig. S27) recordings across transient rsfMRI network states. Quantifications of the ECoG power fluctuations (Supplementary Fig. S28A) at different frequency bands (slow, delta, spindle, and gamma oscillations) and their summations (gross) confirmed comparable fluctuation strengths between experimental conditions (OG- vs. OG+: Supplementary Fig. S28B, S28C). Importantly, we found significant correlations between the peak latencies (both negative and positive peaks) of gross power fluctuations of DMN-RL states and the typical transition sequence of the DMN-RL transition structure (Fig. 7B and Supplementary Fig. S28D). The onset of state 6 was closest to the peaks of power fluctuations, while the onset of state 10 was the furthest, indicating that neural activity synchronizations dictated the predominant transition sequence of DMN-RL (6 → 7 → 8 → 9 → 10). These latencies were also preserved in the presence of a single brief thalamic input, consistent with our observation that the DMN-RL transition structure remained intact. Cg and S1 neural oscillatory events were also unaltered upon single-pulse stimulation, coinciding with the local gross neural activity patterns (Supplementary Figs. S25, S16, S29).

We further examined whether local gross neural activity synchronizations remained unaltered during rsfMRI network transitions. Gross power fluctuations in Cg and S1 were larger in a DMN-RL transition (state 6 → 7) compared to a transition into SN-FP (state 6 → 5), while such fluctuations were comparable for transitions involving state 8 (state 8 → 1/4/9; Fig. 7C and Supplementary Fig. S30). Granger causality analysis indicated that local gross ECoG power fluctuations in Cg and S1 largely predicted state time courses (Cg: states 4, 6–8; S1: states 2, 4, 6–10), especially states 6–8 (uncorrected p-value < 0.0001; Supplementary Fig. S33A). These observations were generally preserved upon single-pulse stimulation (Cg: states 6–8; S1: states 2, 6–8; Supplementary Fig. S33B).

These results demonstrate that the overall stability of rsfMRI network dynamics is supported by local gross neural activity synchronizations in both DMN and SN regardless of whether an external neural input is present.

Discussion

In this study, we experimentally demonstrated the rapid brain-wide rsfMRI network dynamics elicited by the activation of a single thalamic input. The key findings are illustrated in Fig. 8. By combining brain-wide rsfMRI with data-driven analyses, we uncovered transient network states and transitions, revealing previously unidentified spatiotemporal evolutions. Sparse optogenetically-initiated single somatosensory thalamic input, which exhibited hierarchical somatosensory thalamo-cortical activity propagation, caused rapid changes in the probability profiles of rsfMRI network states and the occurrence rates of their transitions, with changes occurring within ~2 s and normalizing around 10 s following single thalamic input. Notably, DMN-associated internally-oriented activities were suppressed and diverted through key network states with prominent neuromodulatory regional activities (i.e., BF and HTh) to mediate externally-oriented somatosensory information processing. Simultaneous rsfMRI and electrophysiology recordings in Cg and S1 revealed that intra-regional wide-band and inter-regional narrow-band (i.e., slow oscillations) neural activity synchronizations directly contributed to the rapid dynamics of rsfMRI networks and their reconfiguration upon a single thalamic input. Our findings provide the direct and mechanistic delineation of brain-wide rsfMRI network dynamics that enable prompt and sensitive neural information processing through rapid and flexible network reconfiguration.

Illustration of rsfMRI network dynamics and the rapid activity-triggered network reconfiguration upon single somatosensory thalamic input.

The switching between these structures was mediated by a rsfMRI network state (state 6) characterized by prominent activity in the basal forebrain (BF) and hypothalamus (HTh). This process is intrinsically dynamic because the brain must continuously adapt the behaviors to environmental changes, switching between brain states for internally- (DMN-RL) and externally-oriented (SN-FP) information processing22–24, as demonstrated by the rapid (~2 s) suppression of DMN-RL states to strengthen SN-FP states following activation of a single optogenetically evoked somatosensory thalamic input (Right). Additionally, thalamic input can be relayed through the cortex to neuromodulatory regions, such as BF and HTh125, to further modulate switching between DMN-RL and SN-FP, though this pathway, illustrated as a dashed arrow, remains speculative due to the absence of electrophysiological recordings at BF/HTh (Right). 3D brain template was obtained from Allen Mouse Brain Atlas144.

We note that the rsfMRI networks obtained from our lightly anesthetized rats were generally comparable to those documented in both animals and humans during awake or sleep states. Our rsfMRI networks generated through seed-based analysis (Supplementary Fig. S1B) exhibited similar interhemispheric connectivity in sensory cortical, DMN-related (e.g., Cg), limbic, and striatal regions to those observed in awake63 or sleeping64 rodents. RsfMRI network dynamics in our data aligned with key features documented in awake rat65 and human45,65 rsfMRI studies, such as hierarchically organized transitions between rsfMRI network states encompassing sensory and motor-related networks, as well as higher-order cognition-related networks (Fig. 2B). Prior studies in rodents66 and humans67 suggested that anesthesia could influence such dynamics by altering state lifetimes, occurrence rates, and certain transition probabilities. Moreover, a previous rat study showed that anesthesia could affect spatial patterns of rsfMRI network states associated with cognition, for example, by weakening DMN-related activity, but minimally influenced states related to sensorimotor systems68. Despite these potential influences of anesthesia, the preservation of overall spatial patterns and transition structures of rsfMRI networks, particularly the dynamics between sensorimotor-related networks and DMN, indicates the generalizability of our findings.

In this study, the densities of spontaneous slow, delta, and spindle neural oscillatory events under 1.0% isoflurane during rsfMRI experiments were 21.8 ± 0.2 events/min, 72.8 ± 1.5 events/min, and 11.0 ± 0.1 events/min, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S14), which were comparable to the 10–50 slow oscillatory events/min, 60–80 delta events/min, and 2–12 spindle events/min measured during sleep in animal or human brains43,69,70. However, light anesthesia conditions can affect rsfMRI network dynamics, particularly states with robust BF activity. BF is a region closely associated with arousal71–73 and its activity is known to be attenuated under anesthesia74. We would therefore expect a more prominent engagement of BF during network reconfiguration in awake animals. This could manifest as stronger BF activity in rsfMRI network state activation maps and more pronounced modulation of the switching from DMN-RL to SN-FP structure in response to stimulus. The potential influence of arousal levels on large-scale network dynamics underscores the importance of future awake animal studies to further elucidate these mechanisms.

RsfMRI network dynamics accommodate fast neural information processing

Large-scale rsfMRI network dynamics have been shown to be closely related to the switching within and between various brain states, such as those involved in sleep30,75, perception76, and cognition15,32,77. They have been used to phenotype neuropathologies, like depression78 and schizophrenia79. However, whether rsfMRI network dynamics can support fast neural information processing, and if so, how, remains unclear.

In this study, we dissected brain-wide network dynamics (i.e., rsfMRI network states and their transitions) in rats. Despite slight cross-species differences in rsfMRI network dynamics when compared to humans80,81, animal models provide a valuable platform for investigating such dynamics during fast information processing, owing to their largely conserved rsfMRI network architecture80,81 and suitability for integration with neuromodulation techniques (e.g., optogenetics)82. Our analysis reveals that the dynamics of rsfMRI networks are characterized by three key state transition structures, each representing distinct neural information flow and processing pathways. These pathways included externally-oriented sensory signal propagation and processing, internally-oriented cognitive processes, and other self-referential processes during rest (Fig. 2C). We found that state transitions within the SN-FP structure at rest followed a hierarchical sensory thalamo-cortical activity propagation structure56. Neural activities were first initiated in local primary somatosensory cortices (state 1) and then propagated to remote accessory/secondary regions (states 2–4 and 6) before being suppressed (state 5; Fig. 3C). In contrast, the DMN-RL structure exhibited robust transitions that cycled through brain-wide activations and deactivations (~10 s/cycle), prominently involving DMN-related regions. DMN is known for its role in internally-oriented cognitive processes such as memory replay83. Within the DMN-RL structure, we identified key network states (states 6 and 8) marked by changes in BOLD signal polarity in BF regions across substates. Spontaneous brain-wide neural activity with similar periodicity and BF cholinergic regulation has been associated with arousal71,72 and is likely involved in regulating sensory processing, memory consolidation, and brain waste clearance72. Our findings indicate that such global activity and associated cognitive processes are mainly initiated when the brain enters a state (state 6) characterized by predominant BF, HTh, and striatal activities during rest. Additionally, rsfMRI network transitions involving background states demonstrated a consistent pattern of neural information flow, where they received information input from all other rsfMRI networks almost evenly but primarily transmitted information to specific SN-FP states. These transitions likely reflect the involvement of the DMN and HP in transferring existing memory and internally-oriented cognitive information to guide and support externally-oriented sensory information processing83–85. We also observed within- and between-state spatiotemporal evolution patterns of rsfMRI networks, signified by previously undocumented persistent and non-persistent brain-wide BOLD activities. These features may be crucial for enabling the flexible processing of diverse neural activity inputs occurring at different timescales. Overall, our findings demonstrate that the rsfMRI network architecture is highly structured, highlighted by hierarchically organized dynamics primed for both externally- and internally-oriented information flow and processing.

Importantly, we demonstrate that rsfMRI network dynamics are capable of fast neural information processing through rapid network reconfiguration while maintaining overall stability. Prior rsfMRI studies using sensory or cognitive tasks have faced difficulties in isolating and quantifying the latency of such rapid reconfiguration in response to specific neural inputs77,86, likely due to the complex and prolonged neural cascades induced by these tasks. We observed that a single thalamic input altered the probability profiles of transient rsfMRI network states and the occurrence rates of their transitions (Figs. 4 and 5), with these alterations beginning within ~2 s and returning to baseline by ~10 s. Consistent with previous literature, we found that SN was instantaneously enhanced (increased states 1, 2, 9, 10 and decreased states 5–8) to process fast-changing and lower-order stimulus traits, while DMN was suppressed (decreased states 4, 13, 14 and increased state 3) due to disrupted self-reference and increased attention to external stimuli87 (Fig. 4). Subsequently, the probabilities of networks with positive DMN-related activities (states 4, 13, and 14) rebounded to resume internally focused mental processes85,87. Some studies suggest that anesthesia may slow such rsfMRI network reconfiguration by reducing the number of state transitions88,89. However, there is presently little to no evidence demonstrating its influence on the rapidness of rsfMRI network switching for fast information processing. Future studies directly comparing awake and anesthetized conditions will be required to determine such effects. Notably, single somatosensory thalamic input reconfigured the two dominant state transition structures, suppressing and diverting DMN-associated internally-oriented activity (i.e., DMN-RL) to mediate externally-oriented sensory information processing (i.e., SN-FP) through a network state (state 8) characterized by neuromodulatory regional activities (Fig. 5). This suggests that somatosensory thalamic activity can alter the modulatory effects of BF on both the DMN90,91 and SN92,93. Further, state 8 also exhibited activity in InS and OFC (Fig. 4D), regions implicated in the rat salience network80,94, which has been associated with bottom-up attention95. Consistent with Menon’s triple-network model95, our findings suggest that activity within such salience network-related regions with inputs from the BF mediated the switching between task-positive (i.e., SN) and task-negative (i.e., DMN) networks, thereby prioritizing the fast processing of a single bottom-up somatosensory thalamic input. Interestingly, we observed that HP networks were suppressed (decreased state 12 and increased state 11) by the single thalamic input (Fig. 4). Based on a previous report of an antagonistic relationship between thalamic activity and HP bursts96, we speculate that brief enhancements in thalamic activity due to single-pulse optogenetic stimulation may temporarily deprioritize the existing HP-dependent information processing by decreasing HP network activities.

Meanwhile, we found that the dynamics of rsfMRI networks remained largely stable during the processing of a single thalamic input. Previous works showed that under both awake and anesthetized conditions, ongoing BOLD activity remained relatively stable even during stimulation or task engagement97,98, indicating that rsfMRI networks are highly robust and preserved across various brain states and cognitive contexts. Further, in the absence of stimulation, rsfMRI network dynamics (e.g., state 1 → 2) mirrored the hierarchical information flow in somatosensory pathways (Fig. 3F, G), which suggests that rsfMRI networks inherently reflect the structured routes for task- or stimulus-relevant information flow. Prior human and animal studies also showed that spontaneous neural activity can reflect task-evoked activity98–101. We hypothesize that such rsfMRI network stability allows for prompt access and flexible reconfiguration of existing brain networks without the need to create new network structures, thereby enabling fast information processing. It is worth noting that these stable dynamics exhibit plasticity and can be permanently remodeled, as previous studies have shown significant rsfMRI network changes after learning and memory tasks102,103, likely facilitating efficient reprocessing of similar information.

RsfMRI BOLD signals reflect broad, intrinsic brain networks observed over extended timescales (seconds to minutes)2, contrasting with task/stimulus-evoked BOLD signals that capture specific, localized activities associated with tasks within seconds or even hundreds of milliseconds40,53. Our study shows that rsfMRI networks, traditionally thought to operate slowly, can rapidly respond to a single thalamic input in about 2 s. While such responses are inevitably constrained by hemodynamic delays96,104, the response time could be actually shorter given the relatively low temporal resolution (1 s) of the EPI sequence utilized in this study. Fast rsfMRI network response is not entirely unexpected, as a previous high-temporal-resolution (0.1 s) fMRI study has reported the onset delays of optogenetically-evoked BOLD signals as short as around 400 milliseconds40 (also evident in our results shown in Fig. 3C and Supplementary Movie S1).

Spatiotemporal neural activity synchronizations underlie the rapid rsfMRI network reconfiguration during neural information processing

Local and long-range neural activity synchronizations are pivotal for supporting large-scale functional networks and their recruitments105–107. Such synchronizations often fluctuate in response to cognitive and behavioral tasks28,107,108. RsfMRI networks have been shown to highly correlate with these neural activity fluctuations across various frequency bands, including slow44, delta44,105,109,110, theta105, alpha/spindle105,109,111, beta105, and gamma111. However, the neural bases underlying transient rsfMRI network dynamics that support fast information processing—manifested as either local or long-range neural activity synchronizations—have yet to be investigated. In this study, we identified distinct underlying local and long-range neural activity synchronizations of different rsfMRI network states and their transitions (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Fig. S15). DMN-RL states were driven by robust local wide-band and inter-regional narrow-band neural activity synchronizations occurring across different time windows. Our result showed the coupling of local neural activity power across multiple frequency bands, which has been proposed to correlate with large-scale functional connectivity and its dynamic changes106,112. In parallel, long-range neural activity synchronization, such as global waves, has been implicated in shaping the configuration of resting-state networks113. The correspondence between the latencies of neural activity synchronizations and the DMN-RL state transition sequence (Fig. 7B and Supplementary Fig. S28D) may reflect the spatial distribution of networks embedded within the phase of global waves114,115. Such latencies between ECoG power fluctuations in S1/Cg and state onsets also varied, potentially reflecting differences in the timing of afferent inputs to these two cortical regions, as local electrophysiological signals are shaped by information flow from distributed, functionally connected brain regions116,117. Additionally, the robust local and long-range neural synchronizations in DMN-RL states may support their role in facilitating internally-oriented information processing, which accounts for the majority of the brain’s metabolic demand118–120. In contrast, SN-FP states were driven by weaker wide-band patterns of local neural activity synchronizations, resulting in less robust and less widespread brain activation compared to DMN-RL states. We found that DMN-related states exhibited more robust long-range Cg-S1 neural activity synchronizations relative to SN-FP states, particularly at slow oscillations. This observation aligns with previous studies highlighting the role of frontal cortices in DMN, encompassing Cg, OFC, and prefrontal cortices, which predominantly drive the propagation of spontaneous slow oscillations to facilitate neural information flow in DMN during rest121–123. Notably, the BF- and HTh-initiated DMN-RL transition (state 6 → 7) was accompanied by larger gross neural fluctuations than the SN-FP transition (state 6 → 5; Fig. 7C and Supplementary Fig. S31). Since state 6 serves as the main initiating state of completed DMN-RLs (Fig. 5C), we postulate that its transition into DMN-RL (state 6 → 7) requires more pronounced local synchronizations to initiate and stabilize the entire transition loop. Overall, our findings provide evidence that within- and between-network neural activity synchronizations directly underlie rapid dynamics of brain-wide rsfMRI networks.

Further, we demonstrate rapid reconfiguration and overall stability of rsfMRI networks subserved by local and long-range neural activity synchronizations upon single-pulse somatosensory thalamic stimulation. Our findings revealed that the stimulation altered clusters of intra- and inter-regional neural activity synchronizations in rsfMRI network states and transitions, primarily occurring at low-frequency bands (<15 Hz), particularly at slow and delta oscillations (Fig. 6B, C and Supplementary Figs. S22–S24, S26). These alterations in cortical low-frequency synchronizations were found to directly influence subsequent network reconfiguration (Supplementary Fig. S25). These observations indicate that low-frequency thalamo-cortical neural oscillations and their inter-regional coherence not only contribute to the neural bases of brain-wide rsfMRI connectivity, as shown in previous studies5,44,122,124, but also support rapid rsfMRI network reconfiguration. Such alterations in cortical low-frequency synchronizations can also influence BF activity, which was prominently observed in states 6 and 8 (Fig. 4D), via cortical descending projections125 to mediate the switching between internally- and externally-oriented information processing in response to thalamic input (Fig. 8C). Previous studies have shown that the activation of BF excitatory or inhibitory neurons can evoke BOLD responses in both sensorimotor cortical and DMN-like regions to bias behavioral states toward externally-oriented (e.g., bottom-up attention) or internally-oriented (e.g., grooming) processes91,126,127. Note that BF activity was not directly recorded in our study, and future investigations incorporating subcortical recordings may help elucidate its causal role in rapid rsfMRI network reconfiguration. Temporally, stimulation-induced altered clusters were observed across various time windows. This phenomenon is attributed to the impact of thalamic input throughout the entire stable, time-evolving trajectory in state space, corroborating observations in other studies101,128. Such input leads to a fast departure from the current states and has the potential to shape subsequent state transitions101, thereby aiding in rapid network reconfiguration. Moreover, we found that stimulation substantially attenuated wide-band patterns of local neural activity synchronizations involving transitions in the DMN-RL (state 6 → 7 and state 8 → 9; Supplementary Fig. S26). This attenuation makes the DMN-RL structure less likely to maintain its entirety, rendering it more prone to be diverted to other network structure(s). Importantly, our findings reveal the local gross neural activity synchronizations (Fig. 7), as well as neural oscillatory events (slow, delta, spindle, and gamma events; Supplementary Figs. S16, S17, S29) in DMN and SN, supporting the overall stability of rsfMRI network dynamics. Since neural oscillatory events are considered the key driver of transient rsfMRI networks129, the unaltered occurrence rate of neural events promotes the stable formation of rsfMRI network states upon thalamic inputs. Collectively, although rsfMRI networks are typically considered a byproduct of slow hemodynamics, our optogenetic rsfMRI and electrophysiology findings underscore the capability of brain-wide rsfMRI networks to rapidly reallocate resources to prioritize the prompt processing of thalamic inputs by altering within- and between-network neural activity synchronizations.

In summary, our results demonstrate that the dynamics of brain-wide rsfMRI networks are highly organized into a hierarchy of transient states and transition structures with specific spatiotemporal evolutions. These dynamics can accommodate and support the timely processing of a single thalamic input through rapid network reconfiguration while maintaining overall stability. Such reconfiguration in response to a single thalamic input is directly supported by local wide-band and long-range narrow-band neural activity synchronizations. Our findings reveal that rsfMRI network dynamics have an intrinsic capability to enable the prompt processing of a brief neural population activity input. This study lays the groundwork for future mechanistic rsfMRI investigations into how brain-wide functional network architecture supports prompt neural information flow and processing, allowing the brain to respond to ever-changing demands.

Methods

Animal subjects

All animal experiments were approved by the University of Hong Kong’s Committee on the Use of Live Animals in Teaching and Research (CULATR). Animals were individually housed at 20–26 °C, 30–70% humidity and a 12 h light/dark cycle with access to food and water ad libitum. Two groups of adult male outbred Sprague Dawley (SD) IGS rats (originally derived from Charles River Laboratories, USA) were used. Only male rats were used in this study to ensure consistency with previous optogenetic fMRI studies from our group42–44 and others53. Seventeen animals were used in group I for rsfMRI experiments in the absence (OG-) and presence (OG+) of optogenetic stimulation. Of the seventeen, rsfMRI data from six animals were only acquired in the absence of optogenetic stimulation. In group II, six were used for simultaneous rsfMRI and electrophysiological recording experiments, with electrodes placed above Cg and S1. Two animals were identified with poor electrophysiological recording quality in S1 and were removed from subsequent analyses.

Virus packaging

Recombinant AAV vectors were serotyped with AAV5 coated proteins and produced by the Vector Core of University of North Carolina. Viral titer in particles/mL was 2.1 × 1012 for AAV5-CaMKIIα-ChR2(H134R)-mCherry (Lot # AV4317M). Maps are available online from http://www.stanford.edu/group/dlab/optogenetics.

Stereotactic surgery for viral injection

All stereotactic surgeries for viral injection were performed with rats at 6–7 weeks of age. Rats were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal bolus injection of a ketamine (90 mg/kg) and xylazine (40 mg/kg) mixture. Buprenorphine (0.05 mg/kg, subcutaneous) was administered to minimize pain. Following a small craniotomy, viral injections were performed at two depths in the middle of VPM (−3.6 mm posterior to Bregma, +3.0 mm medial-lateral (ML) right hemisphere, −5.8 and −6.2 mm from the surface of dura). 3 μL of viral constructs were delivered (1.5 μL at each depth) through a 5-μL syringe and 33-gauge beveled needle at 150 nL/min. The injection needle was held in place for 10 min before being slowly retracted from the brain. Scalp incision was sutured, and animals were kept on a heating pad until recovery from anesthesia. Buprenorphine (0.05 mg/kg, subcutaneous) was administered post-injection twice daily for 72 h to minimize discomfort. Enrofloxacin was also administered orally for 72 h to minimize infection and inflammation post-surgery. Animals recovered for 4–5 weeks before conducting MRI and electrophysiological experiments.

Animal preparation for rsfMRI and rsfMRI-ECoG experiments

Surgery was performed under 1.2–2.0% isoflurane to implant an opaque custom-made plastic optical fiber cannula (d = 450 μm) at VPM with subsequent high-resolution anatomical MRI verification, following the fiber implantation procedures established in our previous optogenetic fMRI studies5,42–44,52,130,131. The fiber-tip surface was beveled to minimize injury to brain tissue. Animals were only stabilized at 2% isoflurane during the first 15 min of surgery for craniotomy to minimize the duration of exposure to high-dosage anesthesia. Subsequently, animals were maintained under 1.2–1.5% isoflurane for the remaining 20–30 min surgical procedure, i.e., optical fiber implantation. Animals were then maintained under 1.0–1.2% isoflurane during MRI setup and anatomical scans. The fiber was fixed on the skull using dental cement. The fiber cannula was made opaque using heat-shrinkable sleeves and the eyes of the animals were blindfolded in all rsfMRI experiments to prevent light leakage that may cause undesired visual activation during optogenetic stimulation. The tip of opaque optical fiber was typically placed at the center of the VPM nucleus during stereotaxic implantation and verified by high-resolution anatomical MRI. The spatial spread from the fiber tip for 473 nm blue light is rather small (200 μm and 350 μm at 50% and 10% of initial light intensity, respectively) and has little or no spreading in the backward and lateral directions42,132, confining the optogenetic stimulation within the VPM nucleus (Fig. 1B). Heat spread around the tip of the optical fiber was modeled using the well-known Pennes bio-heat equation133, demonstrating that our single-pulse stimulation paradigm minimized the potential thermal effects, with maximal illumination-induced heating remaining below 0.06 °C (Supplementary Fig. S34). For simultaneous rsfMRI and ECoG experiments, two insulated platinum-iridium wires were additionally placed above the surface of Cg (+1.5 mm anterior to Bregma, +0.2 mm ML) and S1 (–2.3 mm posterior to Bregma, +5 mm ML) of SD rats for recording electrophysiological signals (127 μm diameter; 90% platinum and 10% iridium composition; impedance ~120k Ohm at 1 kHz).

After surgery, buprenorphine (0.05 mg/kg, subcutaneous) was administered post-implantation and one drop of 2% lidocaine was applied to the chords to minimize discomfort before endotracheal intubation and MRI experiments. The animals were mechanically ventilated at a rate of 60 breaths per minute with 1.0% isoflurane in room-temperature air using a ventilator (TOPO, Kent Scientific, Torrington, CT). During all fMRI experiments, animals were placed on a plastic holder with heads fixed by a tooth bar and ear bars. The physiology of animals was continuously monitored by sensors of an MRI-compatible system (SA Instruments, Stony Brook, NY) throughout the duration of the rsfMRI experiment. Rectal temperature was maintained at ∼37.0 °C using a water circulation system. Vital signs were within normal physiological ranges (rectal temperature: 36.5–37.5 °C, heart rate: 350–420 beats/min, breathing: ~60 breaths/min, oxygen saturation: >95%) throughout the experiments.

MRI scanner-synchronized optogenetic stimulation and simultaneous rsfMRI-ECoG recordings

A multi-channel neurophysiology recording system (sampling frequency: 24 kHz; notch-filter: 50 Hz, 100 Hz, and 150 Hz; Synapse v86, Tucker Davis Technologies/TDT, Alachua, FL) was utilized to synchronize the MRI scanner trigger, lasers for optogenetic stimulation, and ECoG recordings (Fig. 1D). Computer, light delivery system, and electrophysiological recording amplifier were kept outside the magnet. The MRI-compatible headstage was used for recording electrophysiological signals. Long optical patch cables (5–10 m) delivered light into the bore of the scanner. Blue light was delivered for optogenetic stimulation using a 473 nm Diode-pumped solid-state laser measured before the start of scanning as 8 mW at the fiber-tip (diameter = 450 μm, NA = 0.5) corresponding to a light intensity of 40 mW/mm2.

To examine the effect of a single thalamic input from VPM on brain-wide rsfMRI network dynamics, rsfMRI scans without and with single-pulse stimulations were interleaved. Each rsfMRI experiment lasted 2~3 h in total. For each optogenetic session, 10 ms short single-pulse stimulation (light intensity = 8 mW corresponding to 40 mW/mm2) was presented at VPM42,43,52 once every 15 s (fMRI no. of time points = 600). A total 14 fMRI sessions were performed on each animal.

MRI acquisitions

All MRI experiments were performed on a 7T MRI scanner (PharmaScan 70/16, Bruker Biospin GmbH, Ettlingen, Germany) using a transmit-only birdcage coil in combination with an actively decoupled receive-only surface coil. After placing the animal in the magnet, scout and anatomical RARE T2-weighted (T2W) images were first acquired for accurate positioning and reference with the field of view (FOV) = 32 × 32 mm2, matrix = 256 × 256, RARE factor = 8, echo time (TE) = 36 ms, and repetition time (TR) = 4200 ms. Sixteen contiguous 1 mm slices were positioned in the transverse orientation according to the rat brain atlas to cover the majority of the brain. RsfMRI measurements were made using a single-shot gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (GE-EPI) sequence with FOV = 32 × 32 mm2, matrix = 64 × 64, flip angle = 56°, and TE/TR = 20/1000 ms. A total of 600 volumes were acquired for each rsfMRI scan.

RsfMRI data preprocessing

RsfMRI preprocessing was performed following the standard procedures established in our previous studies5,43,52 using MATLAB 2022a (MathWorks, Natick, MA) and SPM12 toolbox (Wellcome Department of Imaging Neuroscience, University College London, UK). For each fMRI session, all EPI images were first corrected for slice timing differences and then realigned to the mean images of the first fMRI scan. fMRI scans suffering from motion artifacts (>0.05 mm voxel shifts detected by realignment) and sudden physiological changes (i.e., abrupt changes in respiration pattern, heart rate, and oxygen saturation level) were discarded. After resampling EPI and T2 images to 128 × 128 × 16, EPI images of each animal were registered to their corresponding T2 images before they were co-registered to a representative T2W brain template using affine transformation and Gaussian smoothing to maximize normalized mutual information. The transformation matrix was then applied to co-register the rsfMRI images. Voxel-wise linear detrending with least-squares estimation was subsequently performed temporally to remove the baseline drift caused by physiological noises and system instability.

Coherence analysis and time delay quantification of rsfMRI data

For rsfMRI data with single-pulse stimulations, voxel-wise coherence analysis134 was performed on one long trial concatenated from data acquired from all animals (i.e., a total of 3192 optogenetic single-pulse stimulation events) to identify brain-wide activated voxels using the threshold p < 0.01; hence, no group-level correction was applied. Coherence value is defined as the magnitude of the frequency of stimulation, f0, (|F(f0)|; in our experiment f0 is 1/15), divided by the sum-of-squares of all frequency components ∑fF(f)2. BOLD signal profiles were also extracted from atlas-based ROIs for further evaluation and comparison of BOLD activations.

To quantify and compare the time delay of single-pulse-evoked activity propagation at the ROI level, each TR (1 s) of ROI-wise BOLD signal profiles was equally divided into ten time points (0.1 s resolution) to increase temporal resolution. A paired-sample t-test was performed between baseline (3 data points before stimulation) and each time point after stimulus to identify significantly activated brain regions when p < 0.01. These brain regions were then sorted according to the order of being activated. To visualize and examine the spatiotemporal activity propagation at the voxel level, voxel-wise BOLD amplitude maps at each time point were similarly generated after equally dividing each TR into ten time points (0.1 s resolution). A paired-sample t-test was performed between the baseline and each subsequent data point after stimulus to determine whether BOLD amplitude was significantly above/below the baseline (0 to 3 s before stimulation). No correction method was applied due to our experimental design, which included a large number of stimulation events (3192) and large variability in BOLD responses (Fig. 3D). Significant data points were identified when p < 0.01, while insignificant voxels were masked. Lastly, voxel-wise time-lapsed BOLD amplitude maps were then plotted at 0.1 s temporal resolution to visualize the voxels that were significantly activated/deactivated above/below baseline (Supplementary Movie S1). To further examine the spatial extent of activation within each brain region, the percentage of activated voxels within each atlas-defined ROI at each time point was calculated, where the higher percentage value meant larger activation spreads within brain regions upon single-pulse stimulation.

Seed-based analysis of rsfMRI data

For rsfMRI data without single-pulse stimulation, seed-based analysis (SBA) was employed to investigate rsfMRI functional connectivity. Firstly, a temporal band-pass filter (0.005–0.1 Hz) without spatial smoothing was applied to the pre-processed rsfMRI data, followed by the global signal regressed to remove non-neuronal global variance. SBA was then conducted to quantify the interhemispheric rsfMRI connectivity of sensorimotor networks, default mode networks, salience networks, limbic regions, and striatal regions. Two atlas-defined 2 × 2-voxel regions were selected as the ipsilateral and contralateral seed, respectively. Reference signal profiles were calculated by regionally averaging signals from the voxels within each seed. Pearson’s correlation coefficients (CCs) were calculated between the reference signal profiles and the BOLD signals of every other voxel to generate correlation coefficient maps (CC maps) for each seed location. The group-averaged rsfMRI functional connectivity maps were generated by averaging individual CC maps across animals and applying a threshold (CC > 0.14, corresponding to p < 0.001) on the group-averaged CC maps.

Hidden Markov Modeling (HMM) of rsfMRI data

To examine the rsfMRI network dynamics and their rapid reconfiguration upon single-pulse thalamic stimulation in a data-driven fashion, we applied the HMM method (https://github.com/OHBA-analysis/HMM-MAR)30 to extract the transient network states and their transitions. The schematic pipeline of dynamic fMRI analysis is shown in Fig. 1C. After standard rsfMRI preprocessing mentioned above, 64 atlas-defined ROI time courses were first extracted and concatenated from pre-processed rsfMRI data without and with optogenetic stimulation (OG-: n = 17, 90 trials in total; OG+: n = 11, 102 trials in total). PCA was used to reduce dimensionality and increase the signal-to-noise ratio of fMRI data. Approximately 70% variance of ROI time courses was retained by PCA, and HMM was applied to the PCA time courses.

Further, the number of states was determined (state number = 18) when the number of states provided the high model’s consistency, clustering performance, and stability (Supplementary Fig. S2). Specifically, the HMM models with different state numbers were repeated 5 times resulting in 5 HMM models per state number. The mean of state time course similarity between pairwise iterations was calculated to measure the model consistency47. The Calinski–Harabasz score was used to examine the performance of HMM states to distinguish the latent clusters of PCA time courses48. It was defined as the ratio between the across-cluster dispersion and within-cluster dispersion. Additionally, the model stability was measured by free energy49. Hence, 18 HMM states were generated composing state ROI-wise mean activation maps, state transition matrix, and state time courses on rsfMRI data without and with optogenetic stimulation. The state transition probability matrices for each experimental condition (OG− and OG+) were separately estimated by applying the model trained on the concatenated dataset, which combined rsfMRI data from both conditions. The difference matrix of state transition probabilities was then obtained by directly subtracting the OG− matrix from the OG+ matrix. Due to the use of a concatenated dataset to estimate state transition probabilities, no statistical correction was performed when comparisons were made between experimental conditions (Fig. 5A). In addition, we also examined the transition probability of the state present at the time of single-pulse stimulation. To do so, we normalized the number of transitions from each state by the total number of stimulation events that occurred during said state, yielding a transition probability matrix (Supplementary Fig. S11A). The fractional occupancy was defined as the proportion of the time spent in each state. In parallel, models with 14- and 22-states were also trained for comparison. To examine whether individual animal variability affects model output, we also trained the 18-state HMM model on different datasets. Here, each dataset excluded one animal from the original groupings, generating 17 and 11 different dataset combinations for OG- and OG+ conditions, respectively. Pairwise similarity of the state time courses was then computed between the newly trained and the original 18-state HMM models.

To dissect the dynamics of rsfMRI networks for higher spatial resolution compared to previous HMM commonly used state ROI-wise activation maps, we generated state voxel-wise activation maps based on the pre-processed rsfMRI data. Single-animal state voxel-wise mean activation maps were obtained by averaging the BOLD amplitudes of each voxel across the time points when the corresponding state was activated. Subsequently, the averaged voxel-wise state mean activation maps were thresholded via group-level masks generated from one-sample group-level t-tests using nonparametric inference with Gaussian random field (GRF, voxel-level p < 0.05 and cluster-level p < 0.001). Group-level masks were generated using the DPABI toolbox (https://rfmri.org/DPABI)135. Spatial similarity between the state voxel-wise mean activation map and the time-lapsed BOLD amplitude map was quantified using the two-dimensional Pearson correlation coefficient (MATLAB function: corr2), after applying median filtering (2 × 2 neighborhood) for spatial smoothing. To examine the dynamics at higher temporal resolution, state time courses were temporally decomposed into entering, plateau, and exiting periods based on probability thresholds and/or slopes of state time courses. Specifically, the state entering or exiting periods were defined as the time when the slope of state time courses was positive or negative, respectively. Meanwhile, the probabilities of the state needed to exceed the 95th percentile of the state’s own baseline fluctuations to avoid including the baseline periods associated with low probability values, while remaining below a fixed upper bound of probability = 0.9. This approach also ensured that the lower threshold for each state time course can be adaptively defined based on its baseline fluctuations. The state plateau period was defined as the time when the probabilities of the state were above 0.9. Finally, decomposed state probability time courses were aligned to the BOLD time courses to obtain the substate voxel-wise mean activation maps. Temporal organization of rsfMRI network states. To examine the hierarchical organization of state transitions, Ward’s hierarchical clustering analyses136 were conducted based on (i) the cross-correlations between each pair of state time courses (maximum lag = 5 TRs)51, with the maximum correlation coefficient used as an index of temporal similarity (Supplementary Fig. S3A), and (ii) the correlations of state fractional occupancies across animals45, quantifying the extent to which pairs of states co-varying in proportion of time spent across animals (Supplementary Fig. S3B).

Electrophysiological signal processing

Electrophysiological signal preprocessing procedures were performed before ECoG analysis (Fig. 1E). MRI gradient artifacts in the raw electrophysiological signals were removed using the fMRI Artifact Slice Template Removal algorithm (FASTR) through FMRIB (https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/eeglab/fmribplugin/; the artifact timing was specified using the location of slice-timing events)137. ECoG data of both Cg and S1 channels were then band-pass filtered (0.1–150 Hz) and down-sampled to 1024 Hz for subsequent data analyses using MATLAB R2022a. ECoG data quality was verified through visual inspection to exclude data showing periodic or gradient-related noise and to ensure the presence of discernible neural oscillatory events within canonical frequency bands (e.g., slow and gamma oscillations; Supplementary Fig. S35).

Stimulus-evoked neural activities were removed based on Averaged Artifact Subtraction (AAS)137 to examine spontaneous neural activity influenced by single-pulse stimulation (Supplementary Fig. S21). Specifically, the template of evoked activity was obtained by averaging the ECoG data across 100 ms windows after the onset of stimulation. The rescaled template was then computed by multiplying the template with the regression coefficients obtained from regressing the signal within the window against the template. The ECoG data for post-processing analysis was finally acquired by subtracting the rescaled template signal from the pre-processed ECoG data.

To measure the local Cg and S1 and their inter-regional neural activity synchronizations across both temporal and frequency domains, we calculated the power spectrograms of Cg and S1, and Cg-S1 coherence spectrograms138 using EEGLAB toolbox (Swartz Center for Computational Neuroscience, University of California San Diego, USA). Afterward, the power and coherence spectrograms were aligned to the onset of each state. To identify the significant state-aligned intra- and inter-regional neural activity synchronizations, a post hoc permutation test based on the power or coherence spectrograms against a baseline period (12.5 s to 11.5 s before and 4.5 s to 12.5 s after state onsets) with FDR correction (p-value < 0.05; shuffled 10,000 times) was performed to generate the mask of significance, and small clusters (<100 pixels) were removed. To examine the influence of single-pulse stimulation on the state-aligned intra- and inter-regional neural activity synchronizations, we performed a post hoc permutation test based on the differential state-aligned power or coherence spectrograms (OG+ - OG-; p-value < 0.05 or <0.01; shuffled 10,000 times) to generate the mask of significance and removed small clusters (<100 pixels). To further confirm these consistently altered time-frequency clusters upon stimulation, we then performed a comparison of cluster-wise averages using paired t-tests between rsfMRI data without and with single-pulse stimulation conditions.

To assess how the local neural activity synchronization contributes to the overall stability of rsfMRI network dynamics upon stimulation, ECoG power calculation and neural oscillatory event detection were performed. For ECoG power calculation, the ECoG data was firstly band-pass filtered to preserve the targeted frequency range, such as slow oscillatory (0.2–1 Hz), delta (1–4 Hz), spindle (7–15 Hz), and slow gamma (25–45 Hz) frequency bands. To extract the gross frequency band, the pre-processed ECoG data were filtered using a zero-phase Butterworth band-pass filter with cutoff frequencies of 0.2 and 45 Hz. Then, we computed the root mean square (RMS) values of filtered ECoG data with a window size of 125 ms and a step of 62.5 ms. To highlight the ECoG power co-fluctuated with the onsets of states, the RMS values were normalized to mean of time windows devoid of period when ECoG power consistently fluctuated, i.e., 9 s to 12 s before the state onsets. We further quantified the ECoG power fluctuations by calculating the area under the curve (AUC) for both gross and band-specific RMS values (Supplementary Fig. S28A). For neural event detection, slow, delta, spindle, and gamma neural oscillatory events were detected based on established methods43,139. Specifically, the ECoG data was firstly band-pass filtered at the corresponding frequency bands. Subsequently, to detect slow oscillatory and delta events, all zero crossings of bandpass-filtered ECoG data were calculated. Event duration was determined by the time between two successive positive-to-negative zero crossings. Slow oscillations were determined when the duration was between 0.8 s to 3 s and the amplitude exceeded the mean (μ) plus double standard deviation (σ), while delta events were determined when the duration was between 0.1 s to 1 s and amplitude exceeded μ + 2 × σ. To detect spindle and gamma events, the envelope was first calculated via Hilbert transform. Spindle events were detected when the envelope exceeded the duration threshold defined as μ + 1.5 × σ for at least 500 ms but not longer than 3 s and the amplitude threshold defined as μ + 2.5 × σ for at least one data point. Further, the number of cycles within single spindle events needed to be ranged from 4 to 25. Gamma events were identified when the envelope exceeded the duration threshold defined as μ + 3.5 × σ for at least 30 ms but not longer than 300 ms. Representative detected slow, delta, spindle, and gamma events were shown in Supplementary Fig. S35. Gross ECoG power fluctuations and neural event occurrence rates were then aligned to HMM state onsets, where the probabilities of the state were larger than 2/3. Comparisons between experimental conditions of each transient state were done by performing paired t-test with FDR correction, while comparisons across state transitions (transition into SN-FP or DMN-RL) and experimental conditions of bifurcating states (states 6 and 8) were performed through two-way ANOVA with Turkey correction.

Multivariate Granger causality analysis

Here, analysis140,141 was conducted on ECoG signals such as band-limited ECoG power (in local Cg and S1) and inter-trial coherence (inter-regional between Cg-S1), and the state time courses. To examine how neural activity synchronizations at different frequency bands influence rsfMRI network dynamics, all data were aligned to the onset of each state. 24-s windows centered on each state onset (12 s before and after) were extracted and averaged within each animal. To examine how these synchronizations contribute to neural activity-triggered reconfiguration, data were aligned to single-pulse stimulation onset. Here, 14-s windows centered on stimulation onset were extracted and averaged within each animal. Following the respective data pre-processing steps, the resulting ECoG signals were subsequently smoothed (moving average with window size = 5 data points) and downsampled to match the temporal resolution of the fMRI signals (i.e., one sample per second). We then fitted multivariate vector autoregression (VAR) models to the band-limited ECoG power/coherence signals and state time courses. Granger causality analysis was then applied to assess whether local and long-range neural activity synchronizations across different frequency bands could predict the temporal dynamics of rsfMRI networks and network reconfiguration.

Granger causality analysis

The gross ECoG power fluctuations were similarly smoothed and downsampled to the same sampling rate as the fMRI signals. The VAR model was applied between ECoG power fluctuations and state time courses for each animal. To capture the most relevant temporal dependencies, we fitted VAR models with lags ranging from 1 to 5 s. The optimal lag was determined based on the lowest akaike information criterion (AIC) value142. We then performed the Granger causality test to assess whether neural power fluctuations significantly predicted changes in state time courses. The z-scores obtained from Granger causality analysis were averaged across animals to obtain group-level z-scores, which were subsequently converted to group-level p-values. Since we aimed to examine how thalamo-cortical oscillations contribute to rsfMRI network dynamics, we focused on whether ECoG power fluctuations predicted state time courses, rather than the reverse.

Histology, immunohistochemistry, and confocal imaging

To confirm the specific expression of ChR2-mCherry in the VPM excitatory neurons, histology was performed according to the previously published procedure42,43. Upon completion of all rsfMRI and rsfMRI-ECoG experiments, animals were anesthetized with pentobarbital and then transcardially perfused with ice-cold 4% para-formaldehyde (PFA) in phosphate buffered saline (PBS). The brains were then equilibrated in 20% sucrose in PBS at 4 °C overnight. Axial sections (40 μm) were prepared on a freezing microtome (model 860, AO Scientific Instruments). Consecutive sections were mounted and examined with a laser confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss LSM780). For immunohistochemistry, free-floating sections were processed with 5% normal goat serum and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS with primary antibodies against rabbit polyclonal to CaMKIIα (1:400; Abcam) at 4 °C for 24 h. After washing with PBS, sections were then incubated for 2 h at room temperature with secondary antibodies Alexa Fluor 647 conjugate goat anti-rabbit IgG and Alexa Fluor 488 conjugate goat anti-guinea pig IgG (both 1:500; Molecular Probe). Slices were then washed and mounted using FluoroShield mounting medium with DAPI (Abcam). Double or triple immunofluorescence was assessed with a laser confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss LSM780/LSM980).
