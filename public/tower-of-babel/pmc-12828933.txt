Genetic and virulence analysis of carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa in farm animals in Shandong province, China: implications for human health
Authors: Chang-An Li, Jully Gogoi-Tiwari, Junjie Wang, Zhi-Yuan You, Xiao-Xiao Duan, Yan Li, Bao-Tao Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCarbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CRPA) poses a significant threat to human health, with animal feces serving as an important reservoir of antibiotic-resistant bacteria and potentially CRPA. However, data on the prevalence and characteristics of CRPA in animals are still lacking. The pathogenic potential of CRPA from animals is also poorly understood. This study investigated the prevalence of CRPA in animals in China, along with the virulence potential and phylogenetic relationships of CRPA from animals and humans.ResultsThe detection rate of CRPA among the 867 animal samples was 5.07%. Notably, multi-drug resistance was observed in 62% of the 50 animal-derived CRPA, significantly higher than the 23 clinical CRPA isolates in this study (p < 0.05). Furthermore, 20% of the CRPA isolates from animals showed resistance to colistin. Mutations in the oprD gene, rather than carbapenemases, were identified as the primary cause of carbapenem resistance. Phylogeographic analysis revealed that PA isolates were disseminated across 47 countries, and the sequence types (STs) exhibited significant diversity. Furthermore, a higher detection rate of animal-derived CRPA simultaneously carrying multiple virulence factors was observed, surpassing the rate in clinical CRPA. Galleria mellonella assays further confirmed that the CRPA from animals and humans exhibited greater pathogenicity than the hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumonia 18,622. Phylogenetic analysis further revealed that the animal hypervirulent CRPA had only a few SNPs with clinical PA from various countries, including our clinical CRPA, indicating the potential of clonal transmission of CRPA between animals and humans.ConclusionOur findings reveal that animals could be an important reservoir of hypervirulent CRPA and highlight the potential for zoonotic transmission to humans. The emergence of hypervirulent CRPA in animal feces highlights the significance of developing control measures in animal farming environments from a “One Health” perspective.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04558-4.

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a Gram-negative, opportunistic pathogen commonly found in soil, water, and animal environments, including livestock farms [1–4]. In clinical settings, P. aeruginosa can cause various infections in humans, including urinary tract infections, pneumonia, otitis externa, wound infections, and bloodstream infections [5–7]. Its presence in livestock raises concerns due to the potential for zoonotic transmission and food chain contamination [8–10]. In farm animals, P. aeruginosa is linked to respiratory infections, sepsis, and diarrhoea in species like calves, lambs, and poultry [8, 11, 12]. It is also associated with reproductive tract infections, reduced hatchability in poultry, and otitis and wound infections in companion animals [13, 14]. Although zoonotic potential is not yet fully understood, close contact with animals or consumption of contaminated products may pose health risks [9, 15].

A major concern is the rise of multidrug-resistant (MDR) and carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa (CRPA), which severely limits treatment options [16, 17]. Carbapenems, often considered as last-resort antimicrobials against MDR strains, are becoming less effective due to rising CRPA rates in both humans and animals [18]. The World Health Organization has classified CRPA as a critical priority pathogen due to its resistance to most available antimicrobials [19]. Reports from China highlight alarming cases of carbapenemase-producing P. aeruginosa, including VIM-positive strains in poultry [10]. Studies from Taiwan show rising carbapenem resistance, with non-susceptibility rates increasing from 19.7% in 2016 to 27.5% in 2018 [20].

In addition to antimicrobial resistance, the pathogenicity of P. aeruginosa is attributed to a wide array of virulence factors such as lipopolysaccharides, flagella, exotoxins and proteases, which facilitate colonization, immune evasion, and tissue damage [21–23]. Mechanisms of carbapenem resistance, including carbapenemase production and mutations in porin genes such as oprD, further contribute to the persistence of CRPA in various hosts and environments [24, 25]. Despite these concerns, research on CRPA in farm animal populations, particularly fecal and visceral samples, remains limited. The role of animals in transmitting CRPA to humans is underexplored. To address this knowledge gap, we conducted a pilot study in 2020 to explore the occurrence of P. aeruginosa in food-producing animals and assess basic antimicrobial resistance patterns. This work helped refine the design of a more targeted investigation. Building on the pilot findings, a follow-up study was conducted in 2022 focusing to detect CRPA in animal feces, and characterize their resistance genes, virulence factors, and genetic relatedness to clinical strains.

Given the high density of livestock farming in Shandong Province, China, this two-phase study aims to fill key knowledge gaps by investigating the prevalence, genetic features, and virulence of CRPA in food-producing animals. The study’s objectives are to: (i) isolate and identify P. aeruginosa and CRPA from animal fecal and visceral samples; (ii) determine their antimicrobial susceptibility profiles and assess genetic relatedness; (iii) detect carbapenemase-encoding genes, oprD mutations, and virulence factors; (iv) assess virulence potential using an infection model; and (v) compare animal-derived and clinical CRPA isolates through whole-genome sequencing, with a focus on potential clonal transmission. This study contributes to broader antimicrobial resistance surveillance in farm animal populations and supports a “One Health” approach to managing this growing threat.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

A total of 867 animal samples, including ducks, laying hens, broilers, swine, geese and mink, were collected from 27 farms across five cities in Shandong Province, China, between July 2020 and October 2022 (Table S1 and Fig. 1). The 867 samples consisted of 824 fecal samples and 43 visceral samples. All samples were stored at 8 °C and processed within 24 h of collection.

In addition, 23 clinical CRPA strains were isolated from sputum samples of patients by the Lab of Qingdao Municipal Hospital in 2020. In this study, we collected these clinical strains for comparative analyses with the animal-derived isolates.

Phase I: 2020 sampling - detection of P. aeruginosa in farm animals

In 2020, 546 animal samples, including 503 fecal samples and 43 duck visceral samples, were collected (Table S1). One gram of feces was inoculated into Nalidixic Acid Cetrimide (NAC) broth (Hopebio, China) and incubated at 37 °C overnight. Enriched cultures were streaked onto Cetrimide Agar (Hopebio, China) and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Suspectious P. aeruginosa colony was identified based on morphology (flat colonies with irregular margins), pigmentation (greenish-blue or fluorescent), and a characteristic grape-like odor. One to two P. aeruginosa colonies were selected from each sample for further analysis.

Visceral samples were aseptically dissected, and sterile swabs were used to sample internal organs. Swabs were placed in NAC broth and incubated overnight at 37 °C, followed by inoculated onto Cetrimide Agar, as described for the fecal samples.

Phase II: 2022 sampling-characterization of carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa (CRPA)

In 2022, 321 additional fecal samples were collected to investigate the prevalence of CRPA (Table S1). These were combined with the 2020 dataset to determine the overall prevalence and distribution of CRPA across different animal sources.

Because the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) resistance breakpoint for meropenem in P. aeruginosa was ≥ 8 mg/L, NAC broth containing meropenem (6 mg/L) was used to select CRPA. This concentration represents a sub-inhibitory selective pressure, which facilitates the screening and enrichment of potentially resistant strains. One gram of each fecal sample was inoculated into NAC broth containing meropenem (6 mg/L) and incubated overnight at 37 °C. The enriched cultures were then plated on Cetrimide Agar supplemented with meropenem (6 mg/L) and incubated overnight. Suspectious CRPA was selected for further analysis.

Molecular identification of P. aeruginosa using 16 S rRNA sequencing

The 16 S rRNA primers were synthesized based on published literature [26]. PCR products of the isolates were sequenced by Personalbio Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and the resulting sequences were compared against the NCBI database to confirm the identity of P. aeruginosa.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST)

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) of the P. aeruginosa isolates against eight antimicrobials was conducted using the agar dilution method of CLSI standards [27]. The eight antimicrobial agents, representing four classes, included: β-lactams (meropenem [MEM], cefotaxime [CTX], ceftazidime [CAZ]), aminoglycosides (amikacin [AMK], gentamicin [GEN]), fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin [CIP], levofloxacin [LEV]) and polymyxin (colistin [COL]). The results were interpreted according to CLSI standards [27]. Quality control was performed using Escherichia coli ATCC25922.

Pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE)

PFGE was performed to determine the genetic relatedness of the CRPA isolates, as described previously [28]. Briefly, cultures grown on Mueller–Hinton agar were suspended in TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl; 1 mM EDTA, pH 8), and embedded in agarose plugs. Cells were lysed in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, 50 mM EDTA, 1% Sarcosyl, and proteinase K [1 g/L], pH 8) at 56 °C. Plugs were then washed with sterile water and TE buffer. Genomic DNA in agarose plugs was digested with SpeI (Takara), and DNA fragments were electrophoresed on a 1.0% agarose gel using a CHEF Mapper XA instrument (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) at 14 °C for 18 h. Salmonella Braenderup H9812 strain digested with XbaI (Takara) was used as the molecular weight marker. DNA banding patterns were analyzed by BioNumerics software (v7.0, Applied Maths). To show the similarity between PFGE patterns, we created dendrograms by the unweighted-pair group method with arithmetic mean using Dice coefficients with position tolerance and optimization of 1.10%.

Detection of resistance and virulence genes

PCR assays were performed for carbapenemase genes (blaIMP, blaSPM, blaAIM, blaVIM, blaGIM, blaBIC, blaSIM, blaDIM, blaKPC, blaNDM and blaOXA−48), Class D oxidase-type β-lactamase gene blaOXA−10, outer membrane porin gene oprD, and the virulence genes (oprL, toxA, exoS, aprA, and phzM), using primers (Table S2) as previously described [29–33]. The blaOXA−10 gene encodes a class D OXA-type β-lactamase, which primarily hydrolyzes penicillins and some cephalosporins. When blaOXA−10 is combined with mechanisms such as efflux pump overexpression or reduced membrane permeability (porin loss), it can increase the MICs of carbapenems. Sequences were compared with those in GenBank using BLAST. To characterize oprD mutations, Sanger sequencing of the PCR products was performed and aligned with the reference P. aeruginosa PAO1 oprD sequence (GenBank accession: Z14065.1) using the ClustalW algorithm in the MegAlign tool (DNAStar Lasergene). CRPA isolates carrying ≥ 4 of the 5 tested virulence genes (oprL, toxA, exoS, aprA, phzM) were considered to carry a high virulence gene load and were further characterized for their potential pathogenicity.

Whole genome sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

For CRPA strains carrying at least the four virulence genes (oprL, toxA, exoS and aprA) from laying hens (n = 17), broilers (n = 7), ducks (n = 3) and geese (n = 1), seven strains (1 selected for the source less than five strains, two for the source between five to 10 strains, and three for source above 10 strains) were randomly selected for whole-genome sequencing (WGS). For the 10 clinical CRPA strains carrying at least four virulence genes, three CRPA strains with different PFGE patterns were randomly selected for WGS. Notably, the CRPA strain JMA210PA from laying hens exhibited highly similar PFGE pattern to the clinical CRPA strain CR-PA20018288. Genomic DNA from the 10 selected CRPA isolates (three from laying hens, two from broilers, one from duck, one from goose and three from patients) was extracted using the FastPure Bacteria DNA Isolation Mini Kit (Vazyme™, China). DNA purity and concentration were measured using the Titertek-Berthold Colibri (Berthold™, Germany) and Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher™, USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq DNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina™, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform with 250 cycles. Raw reads were subjected to quality control using FastQC and trimmed with Trimmomatic to remove low-quality bases and adapters. High-quality reads were subsequently assembled de novo with SPAdes v3.11.1 [34]. MLST was determined using the mlst v2.23.0 tool (https://github.com/tseemann/mlst), and genome annotation was performed using RAST (https://rast.nmpdr.org/). Antimicrobial resistance genes, virulence genes and plasmid replicons were identified using Abricate v1.0.01 (https://github.com/tseemann/abricate) with the NCBI AMRFinderPlus (database updated to October 22, 2024 version at runtime) [35], VFDB (database updated to Jun 10, 2025) and PlasmidFinder [36] databases (database version updated on January 18, 2023).

The 10 CRPA genomes were compared with 503 publicly available P. aeruginosa genomes from NCBI (accessed on 13 November 2023; Table S8). Core genome SNPs were identified using Roary v 3.13.0 and snp-dists v0.8.2, and phylogenetic trees were constructed using FastTree [37, 38], then visualized with iTOL v6 (https://itol.embl.de). Core genome MLST (cgMLST) profiles were used to construct a minimum spanning tree (MST) using PHYLOViZ v2.0 [39].

To investigate the evolutionary relationships of the tmexCD-toprJ gene cluster, maximum likelihood tree based on the DNA sequences of highly identification with the tmexCD-toprJ gene cluster in this study (> 98% identity) was generated using MEGA v10.1.7. All DNA sequences were obtained from the NCBI databases. The tree was visualized using iTOL v6 (https://itol.embl.de).

Galleria mellonella infection model

To assess in vivo virulence, a Galleria mellonella infection model was used. Ten animal-derived CRPA isolates (laying hens, broilers, geese) were injected into groups of larvae. The larvae were randomly divided into 12 groups (n = 10 per group), including 10 experimental groups (each infected with a single CRPA isolate), one negative control group, and one positive control group. The negative control group was injected with sterile saline. The positive control group was injected with a known hypervirulent and carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae strain (CR-HvKP 18622) harboring a hypervirulent plasmid, as described in our previous study [40]. This strain is commonly used in infection models as a positive control due to its well-characterised hypervirulent phenotype, providing a reliable benchmark for comparing the virulence of different strains. Briefly, log-phase cultures of the CRPA isolates were washed twice with sterile saline and adjusted to final concentrations of 1 × 10⁶ CFU/mL, 1 × 10⁵ CFU/mL, and 1 × 10⁴ CFU/mL. A 10 µL volume of each bacterial suspension was injected into the right posterior proleg of each larva, following the method described previously [41]. Larval survival was monitored and recorded over a 48-hour period.

Statistical analysis

Differences in proportions between groups were analyzed using the chi-square (χ²) test implemented in SPSS Statistics version 17.0. Survival rates were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0, and differences between groups were assessed with a two-tailed unpaired t test. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Prevalence of P. aeruginosa and CRPA in animals-prevalence of P. aeruginosa in 2020 (Phase I)

From 546 samples (503 fecal, 43 visceral) collected in 2020, P. aeruginosa was isolated from 163 samples (29.85%), yielding 198 isolates (Table 1, Table S3). The prevalence of P. aeruginosa in swine (47.50%) and broilers (44.71%) was significantly higher, compared to laying hens (24.53%) and geese (28.10%) (p < 0.05), with no significant difference among swine, broilers, and ducks (32.56%) (p > 0.05). No P. aeruginosa was detected in mink samples.

Prevalence of P. aeruginosa and CRPA in animals-prevalence of CRPA (Phase II)

A total of 50 CRPA isolates were identified from 44 of the 867 animal samples (overall prevalence: 5.07%) (Table 1). This included 25 CRPA isolates from the 2020 sampling and 25 from the 2022 sampling. The highest detection rate was observed in laying hens (9.46%, 21/222), followed by ducks (5.94%, 6/101), geese (4.13%, 5/121), broilers (3.86%, 8/207), and swine (2.53%, 4/158).

Antimicrobial resistance patterns of P. aeruginosa

Among the 223 (198 from 2020 to 25 from 2022) animal-derived P. aeruginosa isolates, the resistance rate to amikacin was 33.84% (Fig. 2A). Resistance to levofloxacin and ciprofloxacin was 25.76%, to cefotaxime 23.23%, to gentamicin 16.16%, and to ceftazidime 7.58%. Notably, 15.15% and 6.57% of the strains were resistant to meropenem and colistin, respectively. The 50 CRPA isolates exhibited significantly higher resistance rates compared to meropenem-susceptible P. aeruginosa isolates (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2B). Among CRPA isolates, the highest resistance was to cefotaxime and ciprofloxacin (both 86%) (Fig. 2B; Table S4). Resistance rates were 74% for levofloxacin, 62% for gentamicin, and 32% for both amikacin and ceftazidime. Colistin resistance was detected in 20% of animal-derived CRPA isolates. In contrast, 23 clinical CRPA isolates showed the highest resistance to cefotaxime (78.26%) (Table S4), followed by levofloxacin (47.83%) and ciprofloxacin (34.78%). All clinical CRPA strains were susceptible to colistin. The prevalence of multi-drug resistance (MDR, ≥ 3 classes) in animal-derived CRPA was 62.00%, significantly higher than the 17.39% observed in clinical CRPA (Table S5). β-lactamase genes and virulence factors in CRPA strains

PCR detection of resistance genes (Table S6) revealed that 9.59% (7/73) of CRPA strains carried blaOXA−10, with six from broilers and one from a patient. The blaVIM gene was only found in one clinical isolate (1.37%, 1/73), and no other carbapenemase genes were detected.

Virulence genes were present as follows: oprL (100%), toxA (98.63%), exoS (78.08%), aprA (76.71%) and phzM (24.66%) (Table 2). All animal- and clinical-derived CRPA strains carried oprL, with similar rates of toxA, exoS, and aprA in both groups. Among the 73 CRPA strains, 76.71% (56/73) co-carried at least oprL, toxA and exoS, and 57.53% (42/73) carried oprL, toxA, exoS and aprA, simultaneously. The 32 CRPA strains co-carrying above four virulence genes from animals included 17 from laying hens, seven from broilers, three from ducks, four from swine and one from goose. Notably, 15.07% (11/73) of strains carried all five virulence genes, with a higher prevalence in animal-derived strains (18.00%, 9/50) than that (8.70%, 2/23) in clinical CRPA. Six broiler-derived CRPA strains were found to co-harbor the resistance gene blaOXA−10 and four virulence genes (oprL, toxA, exoS and aprA), with one CRPA harboring an additional phzM. One clinical CRPA co-harbored blaOXA−10 and three virulence genes (oprL, toxA, exoS). Of note, the clinical isolate carrying carbapenemase gene blaVIM also harbored four virulence genes (oprL, toxA, exoS and aprA).

Mutational inactivation of the oprD gene in CRPA isolates

Sequence analysis of the oprD gene in 73 CRPA isolates (50 from animals and 23 from humans) revealed mutations in 65.75% (48/73) of the strains (Table 3). Mutation types included frameshifts, insertion sequences, premature stop codons, amino acid substitutions, and complete loss of oprD. Of the 73 strains, 38.36% (28/73) exhibited frameshift mutations, with 26.03% (19/73) involving 1–2 bp insertions/deletions, and 12.33% (9/73) involving larger insertions/deletions. An insertion sequence mutation (ISPa1635) was detected in one isolate, while 5.48% (4/73) showed premature stop codons. Amino acid substitutions occurred in 15.07% (11/73) of strains, mostly within the L2 and L3 loops, and 5.48% (4/73) lacked the oprD gene. No oprD mutations were found in 34.25% (25/73) of the CRPA strains, including six with the blaOXA−10 gene. Among animal-derived CRPA, 58.00% (29/50) exhibited oprD mutations, with frameshifts mutations being the most common (30.00%, 15/50).

PFGE analysis and clonal transmission

PFGE analysis of the 73 CRPA strains revealed that 40 distinct patterns were observed and almost identical PFGE patterns were found in 14 clusters (Figure S1). Notably, strains from animal sources in Clusters 3 showed 92.7% similarity to the clinical CRPA isolates in Cluster 4. Animal-derived strains in Cluster 7 and clinical strains in Cluster 6 shared high similarity. Two strains, SE-C13-1 and SE-C13-2, isolated from the same sample, exhibited distinct PFGE patterns, indicating strain diversity within a single sample. Identical or highly similar PFGE patterns were observed among CRPA strains from broiler, laying hen, swine, goose, and duck farms. In some cases, strains with identical PFGE profiles were isolated from different farms, including one broiler and one laying hen farm in Qingdao, as well as between farms in Liaocheng and Binzhou. The PFGE pattern of strain JMA210PA from a laying hen farm was also highly similar to the clinical isolate CR-PA20018288.

Genomic characteristics and phylogenetic analysis of CRPA isolates

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was performed on 10 CRPA isolates from avian and clinical sources. The core genome of these isolates consisted of 5,242 core genes, and 22,539 SNPs were identified. These isolates were classified into 7 Sequence Types (STs): ST260 (n = 3), ST1171 (n = 2), ST282 (n = 1), ST274 (n = 1), ST980 (n = 1), ST291 (n = 1), and ST2235 (n = 1) (Fig. 3A and Table S7). No plasmid replicon types were detected among the strains. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using 513 assembled genomes of P. aeruginosa, including 10 from this study and 503 obtained from NCBI (Fig. 3B and Table S8). The genomes of these 513 P. aeruginosa strains contained 2,681 core genes, with 90,358 SNPs identified. Among the 513 isolates, most clustered into two main clades, with all ten CRPA isolates from this study grouped within clade II. It was observed that the animal-derived hypervirulent CRPA strains in this study exhibited a limited number of SNP variations compared to clinical and environment isolates from various countries in the NCBI database, suggesting the potential for clonal transmission (Fig. 3B). Discussion

P. aeruginosa is a globally recognized opportunistic pathogen, primarily studied in human clinical settings. However, its presence in food animals and the characteristics of CRPA in this context remain underexplored. The pathogenic potential of P. aeruginosa especially CRPA from animals, is also poorly understood.

This study highlights a significant prevalence of P. aeruginosa (29.85%) in food animals, with detection rates ranging from 24.53% in laying hens to 47.50% in swine. These findings suggest that food animals may serve as a reservoir for P. aeruginosa, including antibiotic-resistant strains. In poultry, P. aeruginosa is associated with respiratory disease, sepsis, diarrhea, and late-stage embryo mortality, leading to economic losses [8]. In mink, it causes hemorrhagic pneumonia, an acute, often fatal disease [42]. A previous study reported a 17.90% infection rate in lung tissues from minks with respiratory symptoms in Shandong Province, China [43]. In contrast, no P. aeruginosa was detected in mink samples in this study, possibly due to effective biosecurity and disease control practices on those farms. The meropenem resistance rate in animals in this study (5.07%) was substantially lower than the 12.90% reported in the 2022 China National Bacterial Resistance Monitoring Report from human clinical isolates (https://www.carss.cn/Report/Details? aId=917). While the lower resistance rate in animals is encouraging, the presence of CRPA remains a concern due to the potential for zoonotic transmission via the food chain or direct contact.

Among the CRPA isolates, 1.37% carried blaVIM. Although carbapenemase production is a relatively uncommon resistance mechanism in CRPA, its prevalence is increasing [44]. The detection rate of carbapenemase genes in animal-derived CRPA was markedly lower than in human clinical isolates. A global study across 12 countries between 2019 and 2021 reported a 33% prevalence of carbapenemase genes among CRPA isolates, with blaVIM and blaGES being the most common [45]. Instead, mutations in the oprD gene accounted for 65.75% of carbapenem resistance in this study, consistent with previous findings [18]. Among 73 CRPA strains, 11 (15.07%) had amino acid substitutions affecting the L2, L3, L6, and L8 loops of the OprD protein, similar to observations in a previous study [46]. Alterations in L2 and L3, key regions for imipenem binding and uptake for basic amino acids, are known to confer imipenem resistance [47]. Mutations in other loops such as L5, L7 and L8 may modulate sensitivity to various antibiotics, including β-lactams, quinolones, chloramphenicol and tetracycline [48].

In this study, one CRPA isolate harbored an ISPa1635 insertion sequence truncating oprD, a known mechanism of gene inactivation [49]. Four isolates were PCR-negative for oprD, indicating potential gene deletions. CRPA isolates lacked both carbapenemase genes and oprD mutations, suggesting alternative mechanisms, such as novel carbapenemase, reduced oprD expression or efflux pump overactivity, which warrant further investigation.

Alarmingly, 20% of animal-derived CRPA isolates exhibited colistin resistance, a last-resort antibiotic, underscoring the potential public health risk posed by multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa in food animals [50]. WGS revealed the presence of various blaOXA−10-like and blaOXA−50-like genes among CRPA isolates. The blaOXA−50-like subfamily, found exclusively in P. aeruginosa, typically exhibits weak carbapenemase activity and contributes to reduced susceptibility rather than full resistance to meropenem [51–53]. Given the emergence of novel blaOXA−50-like variants, their role in carbapenem resistance warrants further investigation.

All CRPA isolates also harbored chromosomally located tmexD2, consistent with prior findings showing widespread presence of tmexD variants in P. aeruginosa [54]. Tigecycline resistance in P. aeruginosa is largely intrinsic and primarily mediated by RND-type efflux pumps, including the tmexCD-toprJ gene cluster [55, 56]. In this study, the tmexCD-toprJ cluster was identified in strains SE-C10-1 and SE-C13-1 from chicken feces. Phylogenetic analysis indicated high similarity with homologs from diverse species, hosts, and countries, supporting the hypothesis that Pseudomonas may be the ancestral source of this resistance determinant [57, 58]. The earliest detection of tmexCD1-toprJ1 was in a K. pneumoniae isolate from a chicken in Shandong, China, in 2014 (GenBank accession number QFMD01000298) [57]. In our study, the CRPA strains with this gene cluster were also isolated from chicken feces. This suggests that chromosomal efflux pump genes in Pseudomonas may act as a reservoir of ARGs transferable to Enterobacteriaceae, although the mechanisms of plasmid integration and interspecies dissemination remain to be elucidated.

PFGE revealed considerable genetic diversity among CRPA strains, even from the same fecal samples, indicating high genetic diversity in the animal gut. Notably, strain SE-C10-1 from a broiler and CR-PA20018288 from a human patient were closely related based on core gene analysis, suggesting possible zoonotic transmission either through direct contact or consumption of contaminated animal products. Most animal-derived CRPA strains in this study also had minimal SNP differences from global clinical and environmental P. aeruginosa isolates, raising the possibility of clonal spread among humans, animals, and the environment. These findings underscore the need for integrated surveillance under a One Health framework.

Virulence profiling showed that oprL (100%) and toxA (98.63%) were the most common genes, consistent with earlier reports [33]. Notably, 15.07% of the CRPA strains in this study carried all five tested virulence genes, with a higher incidence in animal-derived CRPA (18.00%) than in human clinical strains (8.70%), suggesting potentially greater pathogenic threat. In the G. mellonella model, most animal-derived CRPA strains showed virulence comparable to clinical CRPA, and nearly all ten tested strains exceeded the virulence of a known hypervirulent CR-HvKP strain 18,622 [40]. This underlines the serious public health implications posed by hypervirulent CRPA in food animals.

This study has some limitations. The sample size was geographically limited and may not reflect the national prevalence of P. aeruginosa in food animals across China. Additionally, environmental and farm management factors influencing colonisation were not assessed. Virulence was assessed in a single model (G. mellonella), which, although useful, may not fully replicate mammalian host-pathogen interactions. Future research should include broader geographic sampling and examine how management and environmental conditions affect P. aeruginosa prevalence and resistance.

Conclusion

This study reveals a high prevalence of P. aeruginosa and CRPA in animal feces from Shandong Province, China, highlighting animal waste as a significant reservoir. Notably, 20% of animal-derived CRPA isolates were resistant to colistin, and 62% were multidrug-resistant, higher than rates observed in clinical isolates. Mutations in the oprD gene were the primary mechanism of carbapenem resistance. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that animal hypervirulent CRPA had only a few SNPs with clinical CRPA, indicating potential transmission between animal and human CRPA isolates. Several animal-derived CRPA strains carried multiple virulence factors and demonstrated hypervirulence, indicating a risk of transmission to humans. These findings underscore the need for robust control strategies in animal farming, guided by a One Health approach, to mitigate the spread of antimicrobial resistance and protect public health.
