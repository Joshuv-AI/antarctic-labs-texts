Unique association of COVID-19 with increased risk of aplastic anemia among various viruses: insights from a two-sample Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Jixuan Zou, Yi Chen, Mingjing Wang, Weiyi Liu, Xiaomei Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAplastic anemia (AA) is a bone marrow failure syndrome characterized by impaired hematopoiesis in all cell lineages, leading to pancytopenia and hypocellular bone marrow. Although some observational studies show that viral infections may be associated with AA incidence, observational studies are insufficient to clarify the relationship between viral infection and AA because of confounding factors and reverse causality.MethodWe performed a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis using published genome-wide association data to assess the causal relationship between AA and multiple viral infections, including COVID-19, Epstein–Barr virus, cytomegalovirus, herpes simplex virus, hepatitis B virus, human immunodeficiency virus, influenza virus, and varicella. To ensure our findings’ reliability, we eliminated linkage disequilibrium, selected strong instrumental variables, and addressed potential confounding factors before Mendelian randomization. To further validate our results and enhance their robustness, we performed sensitivity analyses and reverse Mendelian randomization.ResultOur results suggest that COVID-19 may increase AA risk (odds ratio = 1.157, 95% confidence interval = 1.007–1.330, P = 0.039). However, the inverse variance-weighted of Epstein–Barr virus, cytomegalovirus, hepatitis B virus, herpes simplex virus, human immunodeficiency virus, influenza, and varicella revealed no causal relationship with AA.ConclusionOur results, suggesting a causal relationship between COVID-19 and AA, highlight new directions in AA etiology research.Keywords: Virus, COVID-19, Aplastic anemia, Etiology, Mendelian randomization

Background

Aplastic anemia (AA) is part of the bone marrow failure syndromes, either hereditary or acquired, characterized by impaired bone marrow hematopoiesis across all cell lineages in the absence of malignant infiltrates or fibrosis, leading to pancytopenia and hypocellular bone marrow. Establishing a specific etiology for AA is challenging, particularly in distinguishing acquired from hereditary forms [1]. AA seriously endangers patients’ lives and quality of life. Because of its complexity, AA’s course is long, and it causes a heavy economic burden to the family and society. Despite a focus on AA diagnosis and treatment, its causes are not fully defined.

Some studies have shown that AA incidence may be related to viral infections, such as severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) and Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) [2, 3]. A review in the early stages of the novel coronavirus epidemic in Japan found that AA incidence showed a significant decline within six months of the implementation of strict prevention and control by the government, suggesting that infectious etiology played a key role in AA pathogenesis [4]. The causal relationship between these viruses and AA remains debatable. Multiple studies have reported AA onset or recurrence after COVID-19, along with symptom exacerbation in existing patients with AA [2, 5–8]. However, in some retrospective studies, COVID-19 did not exhibit a significant effect on AA’s recurrence rate [9, 10]. The association between EBV and AA may be because EBV can infect T cells and cause an inflammatory factor imbalance [3, 11, 12]. Patients with chronic active EBV often show significantly increased serum levels of IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-6, and EBV-DNA copies were significantly higher in the peripheral blood of patients with AA when compared with healthy controls. These observations suggest a potential causal relationship between EBV infection and AA development [13]. Additionally, association between AA and cytomegalovirus (CMV), herpes simplex virus (HSV), hepatitis B virus (HBV), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), influenza, and varicella has also been reported [14–19].

Although there are many studies on virus-associated AA, most are case reports or retrospective studies, which are susceptible to confounding factors and reverse causation. Therefore, to clarify the causal relationship between viral infection and AA, a comprehensive study with a rigorous methodology is urgently needed. Two-sample Mendelian randomization is used to estimate causal relationships between exposure factors and outcomes using genetic variations. By applying the Mendelian law of inheritance, Mendelian randomization (MR) helps avoid bias from measurement errors and environmental confounding factors [20]. This is the first study to evaluate the association between AA and various viruses, including SARS-CoV-2, EBV, CMV, HBV, HSV, HIV, influenza, and varicella using two-sample Mendelian randomization.

Outcome data source

GWAS data for AA were obtained from the GWAS Catalog database (GCST90018794). This dataset included a total of 4128 patients with AA and 469,372 controls, with all samples derived from individuals of European ancestry to mitigate spurious associations due to population stratification.

Selection of instrumental variables (IVs)

IVs were selected through the following steps: (1) Based on recent studies [21], we screened significant IVs with a threshold of P < 1 × 10− 5. (2) We applied linkage disequilibrium (LD) (r2 < 0.001 within a 10000-kb distance) as a threshold for LD testing using PLINK (v1.9) to ensure the independence of the selected IVs [22]. (3) The F-statistic for each SNP was calculated, with F < 10 used as a threshold to exclude weak IVs [23]. We used the National Institutes of Health’s LDlink tool (https://ldlink.nih.gov/) to exclude SNPs associated with confounders and determine the final IVs.

Two sample MR

We evaluated the causal relationship between viral infections and AA using TSMR. Inverse variance-weighted (IVW), MR-Egger [24], weighted median [25], Simple mode and weighted mode methods [26] were utilized for the meta-analysis. IVW employs the inverse of the outcome variance as the weight for fitting and yields the most accurate results when all SNPs meet the IV assumption [27]. It constrains the regression intercept to zero if all SNPs are genuine and independent. Therefore, the results of our study primarily relied on IVW, whereas the other four methods served as complementary analyses. When the IVW result is positive, the results of the other four methods should be consistent with the IVW result in the direction, otherwise the positive IVW result is not reliable.

After that, we used Cochran’s Q and Leave-one-out analysis to assess the heterogeneity of IVs, and employed the MR-Egger intercept and the MR-PRESSO to evaluate the horizontal pleiotropy of IVs [28]. When horizontal pleiotropy exists, the SNPs detected as abnormal by MR-PRESSO should be removed and the MR analysis should be redone. Finally, we also conducted reverse MR (Re-MR) for the virus and AA to verify the direction of the causal relationship between the viral infections (exposure) and AA (outcome). All MR Analysis were performed in the R software (version 4.3.2), mainly using two R packages, TwoSampleMR and MRPRESSO. The whole process of TSMR was shown in Fig. 1.

Result

Our results identify a significant causal relationship between COVID-19 and AA, suggesting that COVID-19 can increase the risk of AA (odds ratio [OR] = 1.157, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.007–1.330, P = 0.039). However, EBV (OR = 0.993, 95%CI = 0.910–1.083, P = 0.868), CMV (OR = 1.028, 95%CI = 0.985–1.074, P = 0.204), HBV (OR = 0.988, 95% CI = 0.970–1.007, P = 0.213), HSV (OR = 1.085, 95% CI = 0.970–1.214, P = 0.155), HIV (OR = 0.997, 95% CI = 0.967–1.029, P = 0.861), Influenza (OR = 1.000, 95%CI = 0.869–1.151, P = 0.995), Varicella (OR = 1.011, 95%CI = 0.930–1.100, P = 0.791) showed no causal relationship with AA (Figs. 2 and 3).

Discussion

Viral infection is considered an important pathogenic factor in AA, but it is not clear which viruses can induce AA. For the first time, our study revealed a significant causal relationship between COVID-19 and AA, but not with other viral infections, such as EBV, HIV, and HBV. By employing MR analysis, which leverages a genetic perspective, our study design avoids the influence of confounding factors and reverse causality, and provides a more reliable causal hypothesis.

Multiple research reports have documented cases of AA associated with COVID-19 [1]. Furthermore, the results of a cohort study suggest that COVID-19 may be related to the onset of AA, and the clinical course and response to Cyclosporin have led to the hypothesis that COVID-19 may mediate an immune response [29]. In contrast to some earlier clinical reports, our study yielded negative results for viruses other than SARS-CoV-2. Although a study has reported the increased EBV DNA copy numbers in the serum of patients with AA, the phenomenon may be coincidental due to the widespread EBV presence and immunosuppressive agent use [13]. In general, the putative associations between these viruses other than SARS-CoV-2 and AA are primarily derived from case reports and observational studies with inherent limitations, such as small sample sizes and confounding factors. The main basis for conjectural correlation is temporal correlation, which alone, provides suggestive evidence only and cannot establish causality.

SARS-CoV-2 infection can trigger a profound dysregulation of the immune system, which is central to its potential to induce AA. In patients with COVID-19, IFN-γ serum levels increase significantly as early as four to six days after infection. This IFN-γ rise is often associated with increased CD8 + T cells in the pulmonary interstitium [30, 31]. Patients with severe COVID-19 often exhibit a cytokine storm. The cytokine storm induces the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1, IL-2, IL-6, IL-10, TNFα, IFN-γ, IP-10, GM-CSF, and MCP-1 [1]. These high levels of inflammatory factors, together with activated T cells, can activate the Fas/FasL signaling pathway, and induce hematopoietic stem cells/progenitor cells (HSPCs) apoptosis [32–34].

Beyond immune-mediated mechanisms, COVID-19 may directly inflict damage on the bone marrow. SARS-CoV-2 can enter HSPCs via its spike protein, which possesses a receptor-binding domain that binds to ACE2 receptors on HSPCs [35–37]. After entering HSPCs, SARS-CoV-2 can activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, which, when overactivated, can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction, damage-associated molecular patterns, IL-1β and IL-18 release, and pyroptosis [38].

Seronegative autoimmune hepatitis (snAIH) may be an intermediary linking COVID-19 and AA [6]. Although the incidence of patients with hepatitis associated aplastic anemia (HAAA) is significantly related to hepatitis, hepatitis virus is not detectable in most patients and they are diagnosed with snAIH [39]. Research during the COVID-19 pandemic documented a notable rise in snAIH incidence in children [16]. snAIH induction, which can progress to HAAA, might be a significant mechanism by which COVID-19 triggers AA.

Our research shows that some viruses other than SARS-CoV-2 lack a causal association with AA, which may be related to differences in virulence. Compared to SARS-CoV-2, the aforementioned viruses are often less likely to trigger a cytokine storm [39]. Some of these viruses, such as EBV, CMV, and varicella, frequently remain latent in healthy individuals; the reactivation of these viruses may itself be a result of immune dysfunction, which could be the reason for the occurrence of AA associated with the aforementioned viruses. In addition, no direct evidence has been found that these viruses can promote apoptosis of hematopoietic stem cells.

This study has some limitations. First, High-quality GWAS data with large sample sizes are lacking for non-European ethnic groups, so it relied entirely on data from individuals of European descent, which limits our results’ broad applicability to different populations. Second, we sourced our COVID-19 genetic instruments from the FinnGen database, which defines cases exclusively as hospitalized patients with laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection. Such measures also lead to potentially limiting generalizability. However, against the backdrop of the COVID-19 pandemic, the pressure exerted by the epidemic on healthcare systems may lead to misclassification of patients with non-COVID-19 conditions. Rigorous identification of cases reduces exposure misclassification compared to outpatient or community-based testing, thereby enhancing the validity of our instrumental variables.

These findings highlight new research directions and carry important implications for clinical practice. Exploring the relationship between COVID-19 and AA might offer valuable new insights into the mechanisms driving AA. Studies have shown that patients with AA after COVID-19 respond well to immunosuppressive therapy, and determining whether AA incidence is related to COVID-19 may help predict AA prognosis [2, 40]. In the future, research should focus on population diversity to validate our conclusions and clarify the impact of different genetic backgrounds on the pathogenesis of AA. In addition, in-depth mechanistic research on COVID-19 and long-term follow-up of COVID-19 patients should be conducted. In-depth mechanistic studies and large-scale cohort studies can further confirm the causal relationship between COVID-19 and AA. Furthermore, long-term follow-up has potential medical implications, as it could clarify whether the observed causality is primarily related to recently infected patients or to those with post-COVID syndrome (Long-COVID), and identify characteristic patterns in the impact of COVID-19 on hematological indicators.

Conclusion

Our MR results suggest a causal relationship between COVID-19 and AA, which deserves more attention. However, our findings do not establish a causal relationship between AA and EBV, CMV, HBV, HSV, HIV, varicella, and influenza infections.
