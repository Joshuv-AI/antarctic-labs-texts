Mechanical Thrombectomy Versus Systemic Thrombolysis in Hemodynamically Unstable High-Risk Pulmonary Embolism Requiring Vasopressors: A Propensity-Matched Outcomes Analysis From TriNetX Network
Authors: Ashok Runkana, Ravi Medarametla, Raam Mannam, Surabhi Maheshwari, James B Hermiller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh-risk pulmonary embolism (PE) is associated with high mortality. Optimal reperfusion strategy remains debated. Systemic thrombolysis (ST) is traditionally used but carries a high risk of intracranial bleeding. Catheter-directed or mechanical thrombectomy (MT) may be safer, but real-world data are limited. We compared thrombectomy vs thrombolysis using a multicenter data network.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study on the TriNetX Research Network across 107 healthcare organizations. Adults with hemodynamically unstable PE on vasopressors from January 2016 to September 2025 who received MT (n = 1064) or ST (n = 3325) within 1 day of diagnosis were included. We excluded patients with COVID-19, malignancy, obstetric conditions, trauma, and burns. Propensity matching (1:1) for Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index (PESI) variables and other baseline characteristics yielded 1052 per group.ResultsMortality was lower with MT (RR, 0.53; 95% CI, 0.44-0.63). Kaplan-Meier analysis showed better survival in the matched MT group (79.4% vs 61.3%; P < .001). Hemorrhagic stroke (RR, 0.71; 95% CI, 0.41-1.22) and ischemic stroke (RR, 0.80; 95% CI, 0.57-1.13) were also reduced in the thrombectomy group.ConclusionsThrombectomy was associated with lower mortality and fewer strokes vs thrombolysis. Thrombectomy appears safer and more effective for high-risk PE. This finding is hypothesis generating. Large randomized controlled trials, although difficult to conduct, are needed to validate these findings.Keywords: mechanical thrombectomy, pulmonary embolism, shock, systemic thrombolysis

Introduction

Pulmonary embolism (PE) is a potentially life-threatening condition, which is caused by blockage of pulmonary arteries, most often by a thrombus originating from the venous system. Over the past 2 decades, the incidence of PE has increased massively, with around 60,000 to 100,000 patients dying each year due to PE in the United States alone.1

PE continues to be associated with high mortality and morbidity, with high-risk (massive) PE, which is characterized by persistent hypotension, shock, or requiring vasopressors, comprising around 5% of cases. Current data demonstrate that as many as 45% of PEs can progress to hemodynamic instability; however, the number requiring vasopressors is closer to 5%. In-hospital mortality for high-risk PE remained high despite improvements in diagnosis and management, with modern-day cohort studies reporting >40% in-hospital mortality.2,3

The American Heart Association (AHA) and European Society of Cardiology (ESC) suggest systemic thrombolysis (ST) as first-line treatment for high-risk PE with hemodynamic instability unless contraindicated. Recommended regimens are alteplase 100 mg over 2 hours or weight-based tenecteplase, along with immediate vasopressors (preferably norepinephrine) and supportive therapy as required.2, 3, 4, 5 Surgical embolectomy and catheter-directed therapies are indicated if thrombolysis is contraindicated or unsuccessful.2,3,6

In recent years, mechanical thrombectomy (MT) options have evolved rapidly. They are developed to efficiently and quickly clear the thrombus without systemic lytic exposure, potentially leading to a decrease in bleeding complications.3,7 Observational studies and registry reports suggest that MT is being increasingly performed in high-risk PE, particularly when there is a contraindication to or treatment failure of thrombolysis.7,8 However, comparative randomized controlled trial data in MT vs thrombolysis in hemodynamically unstable high-risk PE are not seen.8 The majority of randomized controlled trials (RCTs), including the PEERLESS trial, have primarily dealt with intermediate-risk PE.9 Though MT is a promising alternative, it remains second-line therapy according to current guideline consensus in high-risk PE and is reserved for those who are unstable or fail medical intervention.2,5,6,8

Given the high mortality in patients with high-risk PE and bleeding risks associated with ST, this study compared MT with ST, using TriNetX data to evaluate efficacy and safety and to guide clinical decisions, where rapid intervention and bleeding risk balance are critical.

Methods

Study design and data source

We performed a retrospective cohort analysis in the TriNetX Research Network, which is an international federated health research database system that contains electronic medical records of over 250 million patients from 107 healthcare organizations across the globe. The database includes updated, deidentified patient data, predominantly from the United States, in various formats, including demographics, diagnoses based on ICD-10-CM codes, procedures with ICD-10-PCS and CPT codes, active medications using RxNorm codes, and laboratory values. All datasets on the TriNetX Network are deidentified and are in accordance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Privacy Rule Section 164.514(a) standards. This study was considered exempt from institutional review board approval based on using deidentified data.

Study population

We included adult patients (≥18 years) with a primary diagnosis of acute PE (ICD-10-CM: I26) from January 1, 2016, through September 30, 2025. To confirm that hemodynamic instability was present, we included only the patients who needed vasopressor support (norepinephrine, epinephrine, dobutamine, phenylephrine, or vasopressin) on the first day of PE diagnosis. We further excluded patients with COVID-19 (ICD-10-CM: U07. 1), current malignancy (ICD-10-CM: C77-C80), obstetric thromboembolism (ICD-10-CM: O88. 2), acute trauma, or burns in order to minimize potential confounders.

Patients were divided into 2 treatment groups according to the initial reperfusion strategy, conducted within 1 day of the diagnosis of PE. The MT group consisted of patients who underwent percutaneous MT procedures (identified by ICD-10-PCS codes: 02CQ3ZZ, 02CQ4ZZ, 02CR3ZZ, and 02CR4ZZ). The ST cohort included patients who received thrombolytic drugs, including alteplase, tenecteplase, reteplase, streptokinase, or urokinase within the same time period from PE diagnosis.

Propensity score matching

In order to eliminate the selection bias and confounding, we conducted 1:1 propensity score matching based on covariates of Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index (PESI) and other baseline characteristics. The PESI score includes 11 clinical parameters to predict PE patient 30-day mortality and has been extensively validated for risk stratification. Variables included in the PESI score were age, sex, history of cancer, heart failure, chronic lung disease, altered mental status, and vital sign parameters such as tachycardia (heart rate ≥110 bpm), hypotension (systolic BP <100 mm Hg), tachypnea (respiratory rate ≥30), hypothermia (temperature < 36°C/96.8°F), and oxygen saturation <90%.

In addition to the core PESI covariates, propensity score matching addressed a comprehensive set of baseline characteristics, including comorbidities in all major organ systems, concurrent procedures, classes of medications, laboratory values, and extended vital sign data. In addition, we took into account the availability of genomic data and patterns in healthcare utilization, such as types of visits and frequency of encounters. This holistic approach to control selection allowed for the most balanced MT and ST populations with the least bias in an effort to maximize generalization of outcome comparison.

Study outcomes

The primary endpoint was all-cause mortality from the PE index event to the time of last follow-up. Deceased status was determined based on the patient’s demographic data in the TriNetX database. Secondary endpoints were hemorrhagic stroke and ischemic stroke. Hemorrhagic stroke was defined based on ICD-10-CM codes as follows: I60 for nontraumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage, I61 for nontraumatic intracerebral hemorrhage, and I62 for other and unspecified nontraumatic intracranial hemorrhage. Ischemic stroke was identified by ICD-10-CM code I63 (cerebral infarct).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of 2 treatment groups were compared before and after propensity score matching. All the characters were balanced between cohorts and evaluated using standard mean differences.

For outcomes, the Compare Outcomes Analysis module of the TriNetX platform was used. This statistical tool was designed to allow for 4 types of analysis: the measure of association, survival, number of instances, and distribution in lab results.

For the measure of association, the platform provided us with: fraction of patients in each cohort with the outcome, risk difference, risk ratio, and odds ratio, along with their 95% confidence intervals.

Kaplan-Meier estimates were employed for survival analyses to assess time-to-event outcomes. Censoring was applied after the last recorded fact for each patient to account for those who exited the cohort during the analysis period. The log-rank test was used to compare the survival curves, and the platform delivered the hazard ratio with 95% CI and a test for proportionality.

All analyses were performed using the TriNetX platform, which uses validated and standardized statistical methodologies. The platform automatically handles missing data and includes only patients with sufficient data for propensity matching. P values that were less than .05 (two-sided) were considered statistically significant.

Results

The initial search retrieved 1064 patients in the MT group and 3325 patients in the ST group that met the inclusion criteria. Now, after 1:1 propensity score matching, the analysis included 1052 patients in each group.

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics were equally balanced between the two groups after propensity score matching. The average age in both groups was 63.7 ± 14.7 years in the MT group and 63.2 ± 15.4 years in the ST group, with no statistically significant difference between them (P = .42). The sex ratio of male to female patients was not significantly different between both groups: 548 (52.1%) in the MT group and 547 (52.0%) in the ST group (P = .97).

The distribution of baseline characteristics of both the cohorts was well balanced, enabling a fair comparison. There was excellent balance for all variables in the PESI score, diagnosis of various body systems (diseases of the circulatory system, endocrine, nutritional and metabolic diseases, and others), procedures, medications, genomic data, and healthcare visits. All the components were well balanced between groups, with a standardized difference <0.1, indicating there was successful matching (Table 1).

Primary outcome analysis

By the end of 5 years of follow-up, there were 156 (14.8%) deaths in the thrombectomy group and 295 (28.0%) deaths in the thrombolysis group (RR, 0.53; 95% CI, 0.44-0.63). When using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, the proportion of patients who were still alive in the MT group was substantially higher (79.4%) as compared to ST (61.3%), log-rank P < .001 (Figure 1). All these findings show that the MT group has 47% less chance of mortality as compared to the ST group.

Five-year all-cause mortality outcomes (cohort risks and Kaplan–Meier survival curves). Kaplan–Meier survival curve and corresponding cohort statistics demonstrating the probability of survival over time. Patients in the mechanical thrombectomy group (purple) showed significantly higher survival probability compared with the systemic thrombolysis group (teal) (14.8% vs 28.0% mortality risk) over the follow-up period.

Secondary outcome analysis

Hemorrhagic stroke was experienced by 22 (2.1%) patients in the thrombectomy group and by 31 (2.9%) patients in the thrombolysis group (RR, 0.71; 95% CI, 0.41-1.22). Kaplan-Meier survival estimates did not differ between groups at the end of follow-up (95.19% vs 95.21%; log-rank P = .10) (Figure 2). This shows that the MT group was associated with 29% less chance of having a hemorrhagic stroke compared to the ST group, but statistical significance was not reached.

Five-year hemorrhagic stroke outcomes (cohort risks and Kaplan–Meier survival curves). Kaplan–Meier survival curve and cohort statistics estimating freedom from hemorrhagic stroke. The mechanical thrombectomy group demonstrated numerically lower risk of hemorrhagic stroke compared with the systemic thrombolysis group (2.091% vs 2.947%).

Regarding ischemic strokes, there were 57 (5.4%) ischemic strokes in thrombectomy and 71 (6.7%) with thrombolysis treatment (RR, 0.80; CI, 0.57-1.13). Kaplan-Meier showed survival free of ischemic stroke was slightly better in thrombectomy than in thrombolysis, but it did not reach significance (89.4% vs 87.6%; log-rank test P = .049) (Figure 2 and 3).

Five-year ischemic stroke outcomes (cohort risks and Kaplan–Meier survival curves). Kaplan–Meier survival curve and cohort statistics estimating freedom from ischemic stroke. Patients treated with mechanical thrombectomy experienced lower risk of ischemic stroke than those receiving systemic thrombolysis (5.418% vs 6.749%).

Overall, hemorrhagic stroke and ischemic stroke outcomes were better in the MT group than in the ST group, and survival with MT was excellent compared with ST.

Discussion

Principal findings

In this large, multicenter retrospective cohort analysis of hemodynamically unstable high-risk PE patients requiring vasopressors, MT was associated with a significantly lower all-cause mortality compared to ST (14.8% vs 28.0%; RR, 0.53; 95% CI, 0.44-0.63). Kaplan-Meier analysis verified better survival with thrombectomy, 79.4% alive at 5 years vs 61.3% of thrombolysis. Notably, the mortality curves demonstrated early separation that persisted throughout follow-up. This suggests the survival benefit is primarily derived from preventing early death during the acute hemodynamic collapse. Once patients survived this initial high-risk window, the curves followed parallel trajectories, indicating similar subsequent mortality rates between groups. There were fewer cases of hemorrhagic and ischemic stroke with thrombectomy; however, these did not reach statistical significance. Overall, MT is associated with better survival and fewer complications.

These results provide important information, particularly as data in high-risk PE (particularly those requiring vasopressors) were limited and outcomes continue to be poor, despite guideline-directed therapies.

Comparison with prior literature

Previous studies of MT and ST in acute PE are more focused on in-hospital mortality and short-term outcomes, but long-term survival data are scarce, particularly for hemodynamically unstable high-risk patients with the need for vasopressors. The FLAME study, a prospective multicenter nonrandomized observational study, reported valuable information about the in-hospital mortality benefit of MT compared to ST, where it showed a marked reduction of in-hospital mortality in people who used FlowTriever MT compared to ST (1.9% vs 29.5%), but it did not evaluate long-term clinical results that are crucial to make recommendations on sustained survival benefit.10

Meta-analyses and large observational studies have generally included mixed risk populations, frequently combining intermediate to high-risk PE and reported mainly in-hospital or 30-day mortality outcomes. For instance, the meta-analysis by Singh et al comprised non–high-risk patients and it showed no statistically significant difference with respect to all-cause mortality between MT and catheter-based thrombolysis. Even the follow-up times differed, and long-term events were not presented.11 Another study, Al-Khadra et al, also found lower in-hospital mortality in the MT group compared with the ST group (11.9% vs 20.6%), but it also did not report long-term outcomes.8 Similarly, the emulated target trial of Stadlbauer et al focused on in-hospital mortality in high-risk PE patients, but did not provide any information on postdischarge or long-term survival.12

Our study aims to fill this gap by reporting findings that suggest in-hospital mortality benefits associated with MT may translate into improved long-term mortality in the high-risk PE population requiring vasopressors. These results are, to our knowledge, the first that support the theory that modern recanalization therapies might lead to better survival benefits in high-risk PE patients compared to ST, and thereby avoiding complications from ST exposure (Central Illustration).

Physiology and mechanisms

The observed mortality benefit associated with MT may be attributed to its ability to promptly and directly remove an obstructive thrombus from the pulmonary vasculature, which allows immediate reperfusion of the lung and subsequent fall in right ventricular afterload, resulting in improvement of cardiac function and arterial oxygenation.13, 14, 15 This mechanical removal is a direct intervention and is less influenced by systemic hemodynamics and does not necessarily require sufficient perfusion for drug delivery to the clot, which is actually a limiting factor for ST in patients who are in shock or severe hypotension.13,15

The early separation of the Kaplan-Meier curves for both mortality and hemorrhagic stroke supports this mechanistic hypothesis. The rapid divergence within the initial days to weeks suggests that the benefit of mechanical thrombectomy is primarily achieved through acute hemodynamic rescue during the critical period of right ventricular failure. This early splay is consistent with immediate right ventricle unloading following thrombus removal, which prevents early deaths from cardiogenic shock and multiorgan failure. Once patients survive this high-risk window, the curves follow parallel trajectories, indicating that the long-term survival advantage is established by successfully navigating the acute phase.

While our study demonstrated a significant reduction in all-cause mortality, the mechanism likely involves rapidly lowering pulmonary artery pressures and right ventricular stress, which are important determinants of death in high-risk PE.13,15 In-hospital mortality is lower with MT compared with ST, as evidenced by our results and multiple studies described above.8 Furthermore, MT does not involve systemic administration of a thrombolytic agent, theoretically mitigating the risk of major bleeding and hemorrhagic stroke that are established complications of ST.8,16 Although the reduction in hemorrhagic stroke in our cohort did not reach statistical significance (P = .10), the numerical trend aligns with this mechanistic advantage.

In conclusion, MT allows rapid and targeted removal of thrombus, is associated with a favorable risk profile, and is even effective in patients with poor systemic perfusion, therefore, supporting its use in high-risk PE.

Clinical implications

Current guidelines still support ST as the first-line choice of treatment in patients with high-risk PE, unless there are contraindications such as recent surgery, ongoing bleeding, or cerebral disease.1,3,4 However, in hemodynamically unstable and vasopressor-dependent patients, the risk of bleeding and the low efficacy of ST in severe shock make this approach less favorable.1,3,13,15,16

MT was associated with reduced in-hospital mortality compared with ST, without increased rates of bleeding.8 MT additionally shortens hospitalization duration and oxygen requirements at discharge.8,17 Thus, MT appears to be an option in high-risk PE patients with hemodynamic instability who are not eligible for thrombolytic therapy or are at increased risk of bleeding.

While these results are encouraging, the majority of evidence is retrospective, and there remains a lack of RCTs directly comparing MT with ST.8,18 More investigation is necessary to determine selection criteria, timing, and ultimately long-term outcomes in order to inform clinical decision-making and future guideline revisions.

Strengths

This study uses a large, real-world dataset from the TriNetX Research Network, comprising over 250 million patient records, from 107 healthcare organizations in multiple countries around the world, providing diverse and broadly applicable results. We used a rigorous 1:1 propensity score match that included all 11 elements of the PESI Score, along with all baseline diagnoses affecting each of the major organ systems, procedures, medications, genomics, laboratory test results, and types of health care visits. This comprehensive matching approach resulted in a very good balance between the treatment groups, with standardized differences <0.1, making it highly unlikely to have selection bias.

More importantly, our study investigated outcomes in high-risk PE patients who are hemodynamically unstable and require vasopressors within 24 hours after PE diagnosis. These patients are about 5% of all PE cases, and they have an exceptionally high rate of mortality. This population has been systematically excluded from large randomized controlled trials (eg, PEITHO19 and PEERLESS), which predominantly included patients with a stable condition or at intermediate risk. However, the ongoing TORPEDO-NL20 trial is specifically designed for high-risk PE patients and may provide valuable data for this population.

Limitations

Despite having the advantages of being a large multicenter study with strict matching, there are some limitations that should be mentioned. One of the major limitations is being a retrospective study, which inherently carries some confounders. In this study, we used ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes and other electronic health record codes, which may not capture all the clinical complexity or hemodynamic severity of these high-risk patients requiring vasopressors. We do not have specific imaging findings, the precise dose of vasopressors, or details about individual procedural techniques. Furthermore, while we matched for comprehensive comorbidities and procedures, the database does not explicitly distinguish cardiac arrest occurring at the time of PE presentation or the specific timing of mechanical circulatory support (eg, VA ECMO) relative to the reperfusion strategy. This may introduce residual confounding regarding the severity of hemodynamic collapse at index presentation. Additionally, while cohorts were defined as mutually exclusive based on procedure and medication codes, the database does not capture the precise sequence of therapies within the 24-hour index window. Patients in the MT cohort may have received prior rescue thrombolysis, which could introduce selection bias. However, if present, this bias would likely favor the thrombolysis group (as rescue patients are typically higher risk), suggesting our findings of mortality benefit with thrombectomy may be conservative. Also, the treatment selection process, institutional preferences, and operator skill cannot be captured, thereby causing the selection bias despite propensity matching. Our MT group comprises various devices with varying efficacy, and the ST group includes multiple thrombolytic agents and protocols. Variable follow-up duration and care outside the TriNetX Network may also lead to incomplete outcome capture.

Future directions

The promising results from this study emphasize the requirement for high-quality, multicenter RCTs comparing MT with ST in hemodynamically unstable vasopressor-dependent high-risk PE patients. Previous trials like PEITHO, PEERLESS, and TORPEDO-NL have all excluded this patient group, resulting in a large knowledge gap. Subgroup analyses of patients with absolute contraindications to thrombolysis, delayed presentation beyond the therapeutic window, and varying degrees of hemodynamic compromise need to be emphasized in future research. With significantly better rates of mortality already shown by MT, there is a fair argument to challenge current guidelines, which place ST at the first line. Cost-effectiveness analyses with consideration of duration of intensive care unit stay, resource use, and chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension prevention are warranted. All these should be kept in mind and design RCTs to better optimize the treatment plans and guidelines in this high-risk group.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study shows that MT is significantly associated with decreased all-cause mortality and has fewer hemorrhagic and ischemic strokes as compared to ST in vasopressor-dependent high-risk PE patients. These findings challenge current guidelines, which favor thrombolysis as first-line treatment in this high-risk population. Prospective randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm these findings and further refine patient selection and guide future clinical recommendations for this high-risk group.
