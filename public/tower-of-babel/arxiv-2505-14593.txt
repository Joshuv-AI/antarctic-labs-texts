Assessing Projected Quantum Kernels for the Classification of IoT Data Journal: Internet of Things
Francesco D’Amore, Luca Mariani, Carlo Mastroianni, Francesco Plastina, Luca Salatino, Jacopo Settino, Andrea Vinci

Abstract
The use of quantum computing for machine learning is among the most exciting applications of quantum technologies. Researchers are developing quantum models inspired by classical ones to find some possible quantum advantages over classical approaches. A major challenge in the development and testing of Quantum Machine Learning (QML) algorithms is the lack of datasets specifically designed for quantum algorithms. Existing datasets, often borrowed from classical machine learning, need modifications to be compatible with current noisy quantum hardware. In this work, we utilize a dataset generated by Internet-of-Things (IoT) devices in a format directly compatible with the proposed quantum algorithms, eliminating the need for feature reduction. Among quantum-inspired machine learning algorithms, the Projected Quantum Kernel (PQK) stands out for its elegant solution of projecting the data encoded in the Hilbert space into a classical space. We detail how a PQK approach can be employed to construct a prediction model on IoT data. We compare PQK with common Quantum Kernel methods and their classical counterparts, while also investigating the impact of various feature maps in encoding classical IoT data into quantum computers.

Datasets

To develop and assess machine learning models, researchers often rely on benchmark datasets, including the well-known and widely used MNIST. However, to process these datasets with current quantum hardware, a preprocessing step is typically required. This step often involves downscaling or feature reduction techniques.
Feature reduction is often achieved through Principal Component Analysis (PCA) or, more recently, by employing autoencoders (AE)
[
Bishop:DeepLearning24
]
.

As an example, in
[
PQKELP
]
authors propose the Projected Quantum Kernel Embedding based Link Prediction (PQKELP), a PQK approach on random walk embedding-based features to solve the link prediction problem. In order to test the proposed methods, they use five well-know datasets. In a data pre-processing step, the dimensions of the computing dataset are reduced to create a dataset with fewer dimensions using PCA and then the reduced features are passed through a quantum circuit for quantum projection. In their work
[
de2024harnessing
]
, De Lorenzis et al. utilize Quantum Extreme Learning Machines (QELM) for image classification. To test their proposed approach, they apply it to the MNIST dataset. To address the constraint of limited number of qubits, they reduce the dimensionality of the original data using PCA and AE.

In
[
BetterThanClassical
]
, the authors highlight the potential limitations of using a benchmark dataset such as MNIST to compare traditional quantum algorithms with their quantum counterparts. Specifically, they argue that the necessary preprocessing steps can potentially distort the inherent nature of the problem under investigation. This can hinder meaningful comparisons with classical approaches or even with other quantum algorithms that employ, as an example, different feature reduction techniques.
Furthermore, effective techniques such as AE can generate robust latent representations of the original dataset, potentially enhancing the subsequent learning process.

Kernel Methods

Kernel methods are a cornerstone of classical machine learning and are becoming increasingly important for its quantum counterpart as well. This is due to their elegant mathematical structure, which is heavily based on feature mapping and similarity metrics.

Figure 2:
In linear classification, the goal is to find a hyperplane that separates two classes. The slope of this hyperplane can be defined by a vector
\mathbf{w}
orthogonal to it.

The most well-known kernel-based method is the SVM
[
svm_the_article
]
. For linearly separable problems, as in Fig.
2
, SVMs can effectively find a hyperplane defined by the weight vector
w
and bias
b
that satisfies Equation
1
. In this equation,
\hat{y}(\textbf{x})
represents the predicted label for a given input vector
x
.

\hat{y}(\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{sign}(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\rangle+b)

(1)

Finding the optimal hyperplane to separate two linearly separable classes, labeled as
y=\pm 1
, can be cast as a Quadratic Programming (QP) problem. A comprehensive discussion of QP in the context of SVMs is beyond the scope of this work. However, for interested readers, there are many valuable resources available for further exploration; see, e.g.
[
svm_the_article
]

[
bishop2007
]
. For our purposes, it is useful to report the Equation
2
resulting from the application of QP to the SVM problem:

\hat{y}(\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{sign}(\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\langle\mathbf{x_{i}},\mathbf{x}\rangle+b^{*})

(2)

In Equation
2
, the vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
contains the Lagrange multipliers. The length of this vector matches the number of data points in our dataset. Although Equation
2
sums over all data points in the dataset, the vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is very sparse:
nonzero values
\alpha_{i}
in this vector correspond to the eponymous “support vectors”
x_{i}
, actually used to classify a new point
\mathbf{x}
. The number of support vectors is generally much lower than the overall number of data points. In addition, the term
b^{*}
in Equation
2
, which is distinct from
b
in Equation
1
, depends on an inner product of data points analogous to
\langle\mathbf{x_{i}},\mathbf{x_{j}}\rangle
.

Finding the optimal Lagrange multiplier values ensures identification of the optimal hyperplane for the classification problem. This process involves solving the QP problem as formulated in Equation
3
.

\begin{split}\underset{\mathbf{\alpha}}{\text{min}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}y_{i}y_{j}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}\langle\mathbf{x_{i}},\mathbf{x_{j}}\rangle-\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\right)\\
\text{subject to: }\alpha_{i}\geq 0\ \forall\ i,\quad\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}y_{i}=0\end{split}

(3)

Figure 3:
Non-linear transformation can be employed to enhance the capabilities of linear classifiers by performing linear classification in the target space (Z)

It is important to note that both Equations
2
and
3
rely on inner products of the data points.
For non-linearly separable data, as illustrated in the simplified scenario of Fig.
3
, the previously described process can still be applied with minor modifications. In Fig.
3
, data points from the
X
space are mapped to the
Z
space through a non-linear transformation
\mathbf{z}=\phi(\mathbf{x})
. In the Z space, transformed data points may appear linearly separable by a hyperplane, enabling the application of Equations
2
and
3
.

The kernel function is an inner product in the
Z
space,
as defined in Equation
4
.

k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x^{\prime}})=\langle\mathbf{\phi(x^{\prime})},\mathbf{\phi(x)}\rangle

(4)

This essentially embodies the so-called “kernel trick”: the inner product in the target space can be computed directly, bypassing the explicit computation of the transformation
\phi(\mathbf{x})
. Indeed, using the transformation
\phi(\mathbf{x})
and the kernel definition in Equation
4
, Equation
2
becomes:

\begin{split}\hat{y}(\mathbf{x})&=\mathrm{sign}(\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\langle\mathbf{\phi(x_{i})},\mathbf{\phi(x)}\rangle+b^{*})\\
&=\mathrm{sign}(\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}k(\mathbf{x_{i}},\mathbf{x})+b^{*})\end{split}

(5)

demonstrating that only the kernel function is required, and not the explicit form of the transformation. Similarly, we can apply the kernel trick to Equation
3
.

The optimal kernel for a classification task depends on the nature and distribution of the data points. One example is the radial basis function (RBF), or Gaussian kernel, as defined below:

k_{\mathrm{RBF}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x^{\prime}})=\exp(-\gamma||\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x^{\prime}}||^{2})

(6)

It constitutes a strong starting point for many classification problems. Its ability to capture complex non-linear relationships between data points makes it a versatile choice.

The factor
\gamma
controls the behavior of the kernel in Equation
6
: It is the inverse of the standard deviation of the Gaussian distribution used within the kernel function. Greater values of
\gamma
narrow the Gaussian curve, limiting the influence to nearby data points. A lower
\gamma
, on the other hand, widens the curve, allowing for a stronger interaction of the data points.

We can determine if a given function
k(\mathbf{x^{\prime}},\mathbf{x})
is a correct kernel by examining the Gram matrix, defined as a square matrix whose elements are the kernels:

K_{ij}=k(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})

(7)

Using the Gram matrix defined in Equation
7
, a function
k(\mathbf{x^{\prime}},\mathbf{x})
is a valid kernel if the corresponding Gram matrix
K_{ij}
is positive semidefinite.

Although kernel methods were widely employed, their usage within the machine learning community declined due to the increasing prominence of other methods, particularly neural networks and deep learning. In Quantum Machine Learning, however, kernel methods remain highly significant for two main reasons: (i) quantum computers can map classical information onto states residing in exponentially large Hilbert spaces; (ii) the kernel of two data vectors can be computed as their overlap, a common operation in quantum computers. The next section introduces the quantum counterpart of kernel methods.

4
Quantum Kernel

The aforementioned kernel trick in classical kernel methods offers the significant advantage of performing non-linear transformations without explicitly handling the function
\phi(\mathbf{x})
that maps features to the new space. This stands in contrast to quantum computing, where classical data must be encoded into quantum space, necessitating explicit feature maps:

\ket{\phi(\mathbf{x})}=U(\mathbf{x})\ket{0}^{\otimes n}

(8)

The encoding defined in Equation
8
can be considered an explicit quantum feature map that transforms classical data from the classical data space into the Hilbert space using the quantum circuit that applies the unitary
U(\mathbf{x})
. Developing an effective data encoding strategy and selecting the most suitable quantum circuit constitutes a significant area for future research, as the optimal feature map design may be highly dataset-dependent.
The Quantum Kernel, that is, the kernel in a Hilbert space, can be defined as the overlap of two quantum states
[
shuld_book_qml
]
:

k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x^{\prime}})=\lvert\braket{\phi(\mathbf{x^{\prime}})|\phi(\mathbf{x})}\rvert^{2}

(9)

In cases where the density operator is more convenient than the quantum state vector, Equation
9
becomes:

k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x^{\prime}})=\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho(\mathbf{x^{\prime}})\rho(\mathbf{x}))\}

(10)

In Equation
10
,
\rho(\mathbf{x})
is a data-dependent density matrix, similar to the data-dependent quantum state vector defined in Equation
8
. For pure states, Equation
10
reduces to Equation
9
. There are several ways to compute the overlaps between quantum states, including the
Swap test
and the
adjoint method

[
Hubregtsen_2022
]
, which combines Equations
9
and
8
to get:

k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x^{\prime}})=\lvert\braket{0|U^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x})U(\mathbf{x^{\prime}})|0}\rvert^{2}

(11)

Almost all quantum kernels in the literature are explicitly constructed using a quantum feature map, as defined in Equation
8
, and subsequently computing Quantum Kernel with overlaps, as in Equations
9
and
10
. After that, analogously to the classical SVM, the QP algorithm is applied to find the support vectors for the classification problem.

5
Projected Quantum Kernel

As discussed previously, quantum kernels rarely exploit the traditional kernel trick
[
Gil_Fuster_2024
]
because projection into the Hilbert space is performed explicitly, and the kernel is computed using only metrics such as the overlap between quantum states.
One important exception is the Projected Quantum Kernel
[
power_of_data_huang_2021
]
, where the quantum feature states
\ket{\phi(\mathbf{x})}
are mapped back to a classical domain before computing the kernel matrix.

Figure 4:
By projecting data points into quantum space, the dimensionality of the data representation is enhanced. This dimensionality is then mitigated by projecting back into a classical space, where the kernel trick can be exploited.

The quantum states are projected back into the classical space using a set of quantum measurements and the resulting classical representations of the quantum states are used to calculate the elements of the Gram matrix
K_{ij}
defined in Equation
7
. The mapping flow is depicted in Fig.
4
.

PQK addresses the exponential growth of dimensionality in the Hilbert space, which arises with the quantum encoding and may hinder the learning process. This dimensionality issue is mitigated by employing quantum measurements to map back to a classical representation. By introducing this intermediate quantum step, PQK can extract valuable features in the projected classical space.

This pattern is arguably analogous to hidden layers in neural networks, where intermediate layers and their associated data representations typically exhibit dimensionality higher than that of both the input and output layers. Similarly, in Figure
4
, the intermediate layer (the quantum state space) is exponentially larger than the initial and final projected spaces.

Using the PQK approach, the kernel and the relative Gram matrix can be evaluated using various methods, as outlined in
[
power_of_data_huang_2021
]
. In this work, we use a method, summarized by Equation
12
, below, to map the encoded quantum states
\phi(\mathbf{x})
back into a classical space.
M
here represents a subset of
m
qubits selected among the
n
that make up the quantum circuit; the collection of admissible choices for
M
is identified by
S(n)_{m}
. Finally,
\mathrm{Tr}\big(O\rho(\mathbf{x})_{M})
denotes the expected value of the observable
O
, extracted from a set
\mathcal{P}
and measured over the qubits in
M
, when the state of the whole system is
\phi(\mathbf{x})
. The use of the Reduced Density Matrix (RDM)
\rho(\mathbf{x})_{M}
in the formalism is to prefer, because it describes the state of the subsystem
M
in the more general eventuality of mixed states, which are generated when implementing the PQK algorithm on a noisy quantum hardware.
Specifically, in the following, we analyze the results of the implementations of this method for subsets of one or two qubits (
m=1
and
m=2
, respectively) over a system of
n=6
qubits (one per feature of our dataset).

The choices for
S(n)_{m}
and
\mathcal{P}
define a specific strategy for this classical mapping and should therefore be considered hyperparameters of the PQK. In Section
, we discuss the impact of a variety of such strategies on the performance of the PQK algorithm.

k_{\mathrm{PQK}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x^{\prime}})=\exp\left(-\gamma\sum_{M\in S(n)_{m}}\sum_{O\in\mathcal{P}}\Bigg(\mathrm{Tr}\bigg(O\rho(\mathbf{x})_{M}\bigg)-\mathrm{Tr}\bigg(O\rho(\mathbf{x^{\prime}})_{M}\bigg)\Bigg)^{2}\right)

(12)
