Gene set mediation analysis in high-dimensional epigenetic studies
Authors: Yuzhao Gao, Nan Qiao, Lin Yang, Yan Liu, Ruiling Fang, Ningning Shen, Yao Zhang, Gang Wang, Yaoping Li, Yuehua Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMediation analysis has significantly enhanced the elucidation of underlying epigenetic mechanisms by identifying mediators that influence the effect of an exposure on an outcome. In epigenomics research, the exponential increase in the dimensionality of mediators necessitates the development of advanced high-dimensional mediation models. Here we introduce a novel Gene Set High-Dimensional Mediation Analysis (gHDMA) model to understand the gene-level mediation role of DNA methylations, building upon our previous HDMA method. We begin our analysis by applying functional principal component analysis to capture gene variation signals across regions, thereby enhancing the model's statistical power. Following this, we employ the Sure Independence Screening method, based on Wilks' \varLambda-test, to efficiently reduce the dimensionality to a more manageable level, enabling high-dimensional inference. Our model is distinguished by its minimal reliance on stringent assumptions about the relationship between mediators and the outcome. Extensive simulation studies, by varying the type of basis function, the nature of the outcome variable, and the dimensionality of the gene region, demonstrate the robust performance of the gHDMA model. A corroborative case study further validates the methodology's efficacy. The gHDMA model emerges as a powerful analytical tool, with an enhanced capacity to identify genes characterized by hypermethylation or hypomethylation that mediate the effects of exposure on biological outcomes.

Introduction

Mediation analysis provides a statistical framework for elucidating the mechanisms through which an exposure variable (X) exerts its influence on an outcome variable (Y) via one or more intermediate variables, known as mediators (M). The primary objective is to identify potential mediators and to quantify the extent to which the total effect of X on Y is transmitted through these mediators. Understanding such mediation mechanisms can reveal key biological pathways and inform the development of targeted interventions across diverse scientific domains.

Classical mediation analysis assumes a causal pathway of the form X→M→Y [1], where X represents an environmental exposure and Y represents a disease outcome in epidemiological applications. This chain encapsulates how the mediator M transmits the influence of the exposure on disease onset. In epigenetic studies, DNA methylation (DNAm) is one of the most extensively studied epigenetic mechanisms regulating gene expression without altering the underlying DNA sequence. By influencing chromatin accessibility and transcriptional activity, DNAm plays essential roles in embryonic development, cellular differentiation, genomic stability, and tissue-specific gene regulation. Increasing evidence suggests that environmental exposures are associated with alterations in DNAm patterns (X→M), which, in turn, contribute to disease development (M→Y) [2, 3]. Conducting mediation analysis on DNAm data thus enables the construction of mechanistic chains of the form environmental exposure → DNAm pattern → disease outcome. Investigating these changes deepens our understanding of molecular and epigenetic pathways linking the environment to disease risk, thereby advancing our knowledge of the intricate interplay between environmental factors, the genome, and disease pathogenesis.

Aberrant DNAm patterns, including gene hypermethylation and hypomethylation, have been implicated in numerous complex diseases through their effects on gene transcriptional regulation. Although such changes do not alter the DNA sequence itself, they can substantially influence gene transcriptional activity, thereby contributing to disease onset. Consequently, the study of DNAm alterations has become central to large-scale analyses of genome-wide methylation data. Despite its biological importance, elucidating the functional role of DNAm remains challenging because genome-wide methylation data are characterized by extremely high dimensionality, strong correlations among neighboring CpG sites, heterogeneous methylation patterns across genomic regions, and substantial inter-individual variability. Together, these features hinder mediator discovery and demand robust statistical methods tailored to high-dimensional methylation data.

Early epigenetic mediation studies primarily focused on single-site analyses, testing each CpG site independently to evaluate whether its methylation level mediates the relationship between an exposure and an outcome [4–6]. While single-site mediation analysis has been widely applied to identify potential CpG mediators, it faces several limitations. First, the biological interpretability of individual CpG sites is limited, as genes, rather than isolated CpGs, represent the functional units of inheritance and regulation. Second, DNAm levels at individual sites can be highly variable across populations, leading to instability and poor reproducibility of single-site findings. Third, single-site models fail to account for the correlation structure among neighboring CpG sites, which often exhibit strong spatial and functional dependencies. Fourth, testing tens of thousands of CpGs individually introduces severe multiple testing burdens and low statistical power to detect subtle but biologically meaningful effects.

To overcome these challenges, recent studies have extended mediation analysis to multivariate and gene-based frameworks. Several methods have been proposed to model multiple mediators jointly or to perform variable selection among correlated mediators. For instance, lasso-based mediation models (e.g. Ye et al. [7]) apply regularization to select important CpGs from high-dimensional DNAm data. Group lasso formulations (e.g. Jérolon et al. [8]) incorporate group structures (e.g. CpGs within genes) to improve interpretability. Kernel-based approaches (e.g. Fang et al. [9]) have also been proposed to capture nonlinear effects and complex mediation structures. Despite these advances, several challenges remain. First, many variable-selection or penalized regression approaches treat CpG sites as independent features, neglecting their spatial continuity and correlation within genes. Furthermore, kernel and group-lasso methods often require dense and complete data, making them sensitive to missing CpGs or inconsistent site coverage across studies. In addition, these approaches typically aggregate site-level information using linear combinations or average signals, which may not adequately capture nonlinear methylation patterns within gene regions. Finally, although group-Lasso approaches such as Jérolon et al. [8] can accommodate high-dimensional mediation settings, they remain primarily site-level selection procedures, treating individual CpGs as candidate mediators rather than modeling coordinated methylation patterns across CpGs within a gene. Consequently, they are more suited to identifying individual CpG mediators than to quantifying gene-level mediation driven by jointly varying CpG profiles.

To address these challenges, we propose a gene set high-dimensional mediation analysis framework (gHDMA) for identifying gene-level DNAm mediators in high-dimensional epigenetic studies. The proposed framework integrates functional principal component analysis (FPCA) with high-dimensional mediation inference to transform correlated CpG methylation profiles within each gene into low-dimensional gene-level representations modeled as smooth functions [10], while preserving the major regional methylation patterns. Compared with existing approaches, gHDMA improves biological interpretability through gene-level mediation inference, accommodates the spatial correlation among neighboring CpG sites via functional modeling, and enhances computational scalability for large-scale methylation studies. Extensive simulation studies and a real DNAm application demonstrate that the proposed framework achieves accurate, computationally efficient, and biologically interpretable gene-level mediation inference, providing an effective tool for investigating epigenetic mechanisms linking environmental exposures to complex diseases.

Statistical method

The proposed framework aims to identify gene-level mediators that transmit the effect of an exposure variable (X) on an outcome variable (Y) through changes in DNAm. The use of gene-level DNAm data substantially reduces dimensionality compared to site-level analyses; however, the number of genes typically still far exceeds the available sample size. When each gene is considered as a potential mediator, addressing the high dimensionality of gene-level DNAm data becomes essential to ensure both computational feasibility and statistical interpretability.

Building upon the foundation of our previous work [4], this model retains the classical mediation structure originally introduced by Baron and Kenny [11], while extending it to accommodate high-dimensional mediators derived from gene-level DNAm features. The methodological design integrates functional data representation, dimensionality reduction, and sparse estimation to model complex mediation pathways in high-dimensional omics data. Step 1: Extracting information

In the first step, FPCA was applied to extract the main information from each gene. The raw dataset was replaced by the corresponding FPC scores for every gene. Simulation results

Type I error control

There are several patterns observed from Tables 1 and 2. (i) The choice of basis function has a minimal impact on the analysis results. In almost all cases, the use of orthogonal or non-orthogonal basis functions exerts little influence on Type I error control. For instance, in Scenario 1 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha \ne 0,\beta =0$\end{document}) with a continuous outcome, n=200 and m=50, the empirical Type I error rates are 0.050 for the orthogonal function and 0.045 for the nonorthogonal function, corresponding to a negligible difference of only 0.005. (ii) The Type I error is effectively controlled at the nominal significance level (α =0.05) in most cases. For Scenarios 1 and 2 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha \ne 0$\end{document} or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta \ne 0$\end{document}), regardless of the sample size n, number of sites m, or the type of basis function, the empirical Type I error rates for both binary and continuous outcomes consistently converge to 0.05. As an illustration, in Table 2 (continuous outcome), the empirical Type I error rates for these two scenarios mostly range from 0.029 to 0.052, which demonstrates satisfactory Type I error control. (iii) The test exhibits conservatism under the global null hypothesis (Scenario 3, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha =0,\beta =0$\end{document}) for both binary and continuous outcomes. This phenomenon can be attributed to the random independence between α =0 and β =0 in this scenario: the nominal Type I error rate is set at 0.05 for both individual tests of α and β, while the test in Model (7) yields a Type I error only when both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha \ne 0$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta \ne 0$\end{document} simultaneously. Under the assumption of independence between the tests for α and β, the theoretically expected Type I error rate of Model (7) should be approximately 0.0025 (i.e. 0.052), and all the Type I error rates align with this theoretical value.

Figure 2 assesses the Type I error control of the gHDMA model for a continuous outcome, examining the interplay between sample size (n), the number of sites (m), and the choice of basis function design (Orthogonal vs. Nonorthogonal). Overall, the empirical Type I error rates are well controlled around the nominal 0.05 level across all conditions. The control becomes more precise and stable as the sample size increases from n=200 to n=400, with error rates converging closely to 0.05. Furthermore, the Nonorthogonal design yields marginally more conservative (slightly lower) error rates compared to the Orthogonal design when the number of sites is small (e.g. m=50), but this subtle difference diminishes as m increases to 100 and 500. Figure 3 further evaluates Type I error control by decomposing it into three specific null scenarios (Scenarios 1, 2, and 3). The results demonstrate that the model reliably controls the Type I error near the 0.05 target in Scenarios 1 and 2, where partial null hypotheses are tested. In contrast, under the complete null hypothesis of Scenario 3, the model produces slightly conservative (lower than 0.05) error rates. This pattern is consistent across different sample sizes and numbers of sites.

Statistical power

Several clear patterns can be observed from Tables 3 and 4. (i) The choice of basis function has a minimal impact on statistical power. Across all combinations of n and m, the power of gHDMA model for orthogonal and nonorthogonal basis functions are remarkably close. The largest observed difference is 0.027 (0.775 vs. 0.748 for binary outcome, n=200, m=50, in Table 3). In most settings, especially with larger sample sizes (n=400), the difference is negligible, often less than 0.01. (ii) Sample size (n) is the dominant factor influencing power. Increasing the n from 200 to 400 leads to a substantial and consistent increase in power for all configurations. For instance, with a binary outcome and m=50 (Table 3), the power of gHDMA model jumps from approximately 0.76–0.78 to over 0.96. A similar dramatic increase is observed for continuous outcomes (Table 4), where power rises from around 0.67–0.69 to over 0.95. (iii) Increasing the m has a very limited effect on the power of gHDMA model, particularly at larger n. When n=200, the power of gHDMA model shows slight fluctuations as m increases from 50 to 500, but without a clear directional trend. When n=400, power stabilizes at a high level, showing only minimal incremental changes (e.g. from 0.965 to 0.971 for orthogonal functions in Table 3).

Figure 4 illustrates the statistical power of the gHDMA model for a binary outcome. The results demonstrate that power is strongly dependent on n, showing a major improvement from n=200 to n=400. In contrast, increasing the number of sites within the gene (m) has a negligible effect on power, as values quickly plateau. Figure 5 displays the corresponding power for a continuous outcome. It reveals an identical trend: sample size is the dominant factor for increasing power, while the number of sites (m) and the choice of basis function (orthogonal vs. nonorthogonal) have minimal impact. A direct comparison of the two figures shows that the model achieves slightly higher power for binary outcomes than for continuous outcomes at the smaller sample size (n=200), but both achieve similarly high power (>0.95) when n=400.

Overall, across all simulation settings, the proposed gHDMA model performs well. Notably, both the power and Type I error rate tend to plateau once the number of sites reaches a sufficiently large value. The conservative behavior observed is expected due to the use of a joint significance test. In Scenarios 1 and 2, one of the effects is set to zero, and the Type I error rates of the gHDMA model remain close to the 0.05 level, with false positives appearing randomly across the 1000 simulation replicates. In Scenario 3, where both effects are set to zero, false positives arise purely by chance during the testing steps. Because of this randomness, the results from the two-step testing procedure are not perfectly synchronized. Consequently, the overall Type I error rate of the gHDMA model is reasonably controlled at approximately 0.0025.

Real data analysis

We apply the gHDMA model to a real dataset to identify methylation genes that may serve as underlying mediators. DNAm plays a key role in numerous diseases, highlighting the importance of improving our understanding of pathogenic mechanisms [19]. In this analysis, we evaluate the effect of childhood maltreatment on adult depression, mediated by gene-level DNAm. DNAm may represent a biological mechanism through which early traumatic experiences, such as childhood maltreatment, become embedded and influence later-life outcomes [20].

The data were obtained from the Grady Trauma Project (GTP), which recruited African American participants from inner-city Atlanta. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Emory University School of Medicine and Grady Memorial Hospital [21]. A growing body of research suggests that DNAm serves as a key intermediary linking environmental exposures to the development of depressive disorders. Early-life adversities, particularly childhood maltreatment, have been shown to induce substantial disruptions in biological developmental processes, leading to a wide range of downstream consequences. Epigenetic modifications, with DNAm as a central component, are increasingly recognized as fundamental mechanisms underlying these deviations from typical development [22].

In this study, we apply the proposed methodology to characterize the relationship between childhood maltreatment and adult depression, with particular attention to the mediating role of DNAm at the gene level. The dataset includes baseline demographic information, estimated cell-type composition, and DNAm measurements. Depression status is determined using the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), a validated psychometric tool used to classify individuals into case and control groups. Participants with current depressive symptoms are defined as cases if their BDI score is ≥14, whereas controls are defined as individuals with a BDI score <14 despite experiencing trauma exposure [20]. We remove observations with missing values and exclude individuals receiving depression treatment, as such treatment may alter DNAm patterns and complicate mediation analyses. After these exclusions, 138 cases and 145 controls remain for analysis. The overall data analysis steps applied to this cohort is depicted in Fig. 6.

As shown in Table 6 and Fig. 7, three genes, WNT2, RAE1, and RUNX1, were identified as potential mediators of the effect of childhood maltreatment on adult depression, located on chromosomes 7, 20 and 21, respectively. Several lines of biological evidence support their potential roles. Specifically, WNT2 activates WNT/β-catenin signaling to support hippocampal neurogenesis, and its reduction in the ventral hippocampus drives stress-induced depression-like behaviors [23]. RAE1 is a well-established regulator of mRNA nuclear export, a process dynamically modulated by cells to cope with environmental stress. We posit that this Rae1-dependent mechanism is equally critical for preserving neuronal synaptic plasticity under chronic CNS stress. Dysregulation of this export pathway could impair the synthesis of key synaptic proteins, thereby driving the pathogenesis of adult depression [24, 25]. RUNX1 contributes to the pathogenesis of depression through both central neural plasticity and peripheral immune regulation. In adult hippocampal excitatory neurons, RUNX1 forms a transcriptional complex with CBFβ to maintain protein stability. Loss of this complex impairs BDNF expression, which subsequently disrupts hippocampal neurogenesis—particularly in females—thereby reducing stress resilience and precipitating anxiety- and depression-like behaviors. Furthermore, as a key regulator of myeloid differentiation, RUNX1 has been significantly associated with depression risk in large-scale genetic studies. Its genetic variants exert gene–environment interactions with childhood maltreatment to drive macrophage activation and systemic immune remodeling. Such peripheral-central immune crosstalk induces low-grade neuroinflammation, collectively fueling the pathological progression of depression [26, 27].

Discussion

Mediation analysis has become an important methodological tool for elucidating the mechanisms underlying complex diseases. As high-throughput omics technologies continue to advance, high-dimensional mediation analysis has increasingly turned toward identifying biologically meaningful mediators and quantifying their contributions to disease outcomes [4, 5]. From a biological standpoint, genes offer stable and interpretable functional units; gene-level analysis may therefore offer greater biological interpretability and generalizability than analyses at the level of individual CpG sites. In this study, we combined FPCA-based extraction of gene-region DNAm features, SIS screening based on Wilks’ \varLambda-test, de-sparsified Lasso inference, Fisher's P-value combination, and joint significance test to build a gene-level high-dimensional mediation analysis framework. The approach summarizes correlated methylation patterns within gene regions and substantially reduces the dimensionality entering downstream inference.

FPCA efficiently captures heterogeneous methylation patterns within gene regions, especially when CpG sites are unevenly spaced or partially missing. By retaining the dominant functional variation, it yields a small number of informative components for subsequent mediation analysis. Our simulations—covering different basis functions, dimensional settings, and both continuous and binary outcomes—showed that the proposed framework maintains satisfactory performance across a wide range of scenarios. However, FPCA can dilute weak, highly localized methylation signals that contribute little to overall regional variation. Moreover, the quality of the functional representation depends on choices of basis functions, knot locations, and smoothing parameters. While our simulations suggest limited sensitivity to these choices, more adaptive, data-driven functional representations could further improve robustness in real methylation datasets.

When applied to the GTP cohort, the framework identified gene regions whose methylation patterns may mediate the link between childhood maltreatment and adult depression. These findings offer potential molecular insight into how early-life environmental exposures shape later disease outcomes through epigenetic mechanisms. Still, the identified signals should be viewed as statistical mediation associations rather than definitive causal effects, because residual or unmeasured confounding among exposure, mediators, and outcome cannot be fully ruled out. Future work incorporating causal mediation methods, sensitivity analyses, or independent biological validation will help strengthen any causal interpretation of these results.

Several methodological limitations deserve mention. Although FPCA, SIS screening based on Wilks'\varLambda-test, and de-sparsified Lasso are each well-established on their own, combining them creates a hierarchical inferential procedure that extends beyond the theoretical guarantees of the original de-sparsified Lasso framework. In particular, the impact of functional truncation and screening on downstream inference has not yet been fully characterized theoretically. Our simulation studies provided empirical evidence of reasonable Type I error control and statistical power, but developing rigorous asymptotic theory for the entire pipeline remains an important avenue for future research.

Finally, the current framework treats gene regions as the primary mediation units and does not explicitly model interactions among multiple mediator genes. Such gene–gene interactions likely play important roles in complex disease mechanisms, but incorporating all pairwise interactions would cause a massive jump in dimensionality. For instance, in our GTP cohort analysis, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${n}_1=13\ 869$\end{document} mediator genes were retained after quality control, yielding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${n_1 \choose 2}=\frac{n_1\left({n}_1-1\right)}{2}\approx 9.6168\times{10}^7$\end{document} pairwise interaction terms. Designing computationally efficient strategies to accommodate gene–gene and possibly multi-omics interactions is therefore a key direction for future work. Overall, gHDMA offers a scalable, biologically interpretable framework for gene-level high-dimensional mediation analysis while pointing to several opportunities for further methodological development.
