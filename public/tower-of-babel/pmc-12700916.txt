GSK3 phosphorylates and activates trehalose-6-phosphate synthase to improve trehalose production and thermotolerance in Ganoderma lucidum
Authors: Lingshuai Wang, Lingyan Shi, Hui Wang, Qiqi Han, Yuqing Hu, Qin Zheng, Zeyi Dong, Rui Liu, Mingwen Zhao, Huhui Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK3) plays crucial roles in diverse organisms, yet its physiological functions in filamentous fungi remain poorly characterized. Here, we show that knockdown of GlGSK3 in Ganoderma lucidum leads to increased glycogen accumulation, reduced pyruvate and ATP production, and impaired hyphal growth and thermotolerance. GlGSK3 interacts with and phosphorylates trehalose-6-phosphate synthase (TPS) at Serine 529, enhancing its enzymatic activity. Consistent with the central role of TPS in trehalose biosynthesis, trehalose levels were significantly lower in gltps-kd and glgsk3-kd strains compared to the wild type. Similarly, gltps-kd strains also exhibited diminished hyphal growth and thermotolerance. Under heat stress, both GlGSK3 and GlTPS protein levels were upregulated, leading to increased GlTPS phosphorylation, enhanced enzymatic activity, and elevated trehalose accumulation. Together, these results uncover a key role for GlGSK3 and the GSK3-TPS module in G. lucidum in regulating growth and adaptation to environmental stress.

Introduction

As a well-known edible and medicinal fungus, Ganoderma lucidum is rich in diverse pharmacological components, contributing to its broad therapeutic and health-promoting properties1,2. Following the completion of its genome sequence and advances in gene-editing techniques, G. lucidum has emerged as a potential model system for investigating growth, development, and secondary metabolism regulation in basidiomycete3,4. In natural environments, high temperature represents a major abiotic constraint on fungal growth5. For instance, heat stress severely reduces survival in Saccharomyces cerevisiae6, inhibits mycelial growth in Fusarium graminearum7, and diminishes biomass production in Pleurotus ostreatus8. Similarly, both hyphal extension and branching in G. lucidum are suppressed under elevated temperatures9. Studies indicate that various stresses-including cold, heat, and drought-can induce trehalose accumulation in fungi. Notably, heat stress significantly increases trehalose levels in Flammulina velutipes, P. ostreatus, and S. cerevisiae6–8.

Trehalose is a non-reducing disaccharide widely present in living organisms, where it serves as a protective molecule during stress responses by maintaining the stability of cell membranes and structural integrity10. In fungi, two trehalose biosynthesis pathways have been identified. The primary one involves trehalose-6-phosphate synthase (TPS) and trehalose-6-phosphate phosphatase (T6PP)10. TPS catalyzes the formation of trehalose-6-phosphate (T6P) from uridine diphosphate glucose (UDPG) and glucose-6-phosphate. T6P is subsequently dephosphorylated by T6PP to yield trehalose10. TPS is therefore critical for T6P and trehalose production, and plays a key role in fungal development and stress tolerance11. In Aspergillus nidulans, deletion of tpsA leads to undetectable trehalose levels in conidia11. The ΔtpsA mutant shows significantly reduced conidial viability after prolonged storage at 20 °C compared to the wild-type strain11. Similarly, in F. graminearum, disruption of tps1 nearly abolishes trehalose synthesis and results in severely impaired vegetative growth along with increased sensitivity to abiotic stresses such as high salinity and (H2O2) -induced oxidative stress12. In Botrytis cinerea, the Δtps1 mutant contains only trace amounts of trehalose and exhibits markedly reduced conidial survival under heat stress, though vegetative growth remains unaffected13. Furthermore, overexpression of tps in P. ostreatus enhances trehalose accumulation and thermotolerance by modulating carbon flux through inhibition of glycolysis and activation of the pentose phosphate pathway6,14,15. Exogenous trehalose application or TPS overexpression also improves abiotic stress resistance in plants16,17.

While existing studies have primarily focused on the role of TPS in trehalose biosynthesis and stress adaptation, post-translational modifications of TPS remain poorly understood. During our investigation into the physiological functions of glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK3) in G. lucidum, yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) screening identified a physical interaction between GSK3 and TPS, suggesting potential phosphorylation-mediated regulation of TPS. GSK3 was initially identified as a serine/threonine (Ser/Thr) protein kinase that phosphorylates and inhibits glycogen synthase18. It is now recognized as a highly conserved kinase across eukaryotes, including animals, plants, and fungi19,20. Although GSK3 and its homologs share conserved kinase domains and ATP-binding sites, they fulfill diverse roles in cellular processes19. In plants, GSK3-like kinases integrate signaling from phytohormones such as brassinosteroid, abscisic acid, and auxin, and regulate tissue development21–24. For example, the Arabidopsis GSK3-like kinase BIN2 promotes stomatal clustering by phosphorylating MPKK4 and regulates lateral root development via phosphorylation of auxin response factor 421,25.

In contrast to the well-characterized roles of GSK3 in animals and plants, its functions in filamentous fungi remain less explored, especially the underlying molecular mechanism. In Penicillium oxalicum, GSK-3β influences the expression of genes encoding plant polysaccharide-degrading enzymes (PPDEs) and regulates developmental processes26. Knockdown of PoGSK-3β markedly reduces PPDE production, impairs mycelial growth, and disrupts conidiation26. In F. graminearum, the GSK3 homolog Fgk3 interacts with and phosphorylates the transcription factors MSN2 and CreA, modulating the expression of stress-responsive genes and chitin synthesis genes; this regulation contributes to hyphal growth, conidiation under abiotic stress, and cell wall integrity27,28. Similarly, disruption of MoGSK1 in Magnaporthe oryzae severely reduces mycelial growth and abolishes asexual sporulation, thereby impairing pathogenicity29. In A. nidulans, the GSK3 homolog GSKA phosphorylates CreA to promote its degradation30.

Although studies have reported the physiological functions and several substrate proteins of GSK3 in various fungi, the phosphorylation sites of these substrates and the functional consequences of their phosphorylation remain poorly characterized. Here, we identify and characterize a GSK3 homolog in G. lucidum. Reduced expression of GlGSK3 inhibits mycelial growth, fruiting body development, and thermotolerance. Using Y2H analysis, in vivo co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), and in vitro phosphorylation assays, we demonstrate that GlGSK3 interacts with and phosphorylates GlTPS at Ser529 to enhance its enzymatic activity. Furthermore, high temperature increases GlGSK3-mediated phosphorylation of GlTPS, promoting trehalose accumulation and thereby enhancing thermotolerance in G. lucidum.

Results

Knockdown of GlGSK3 impairs hyphal growth and fruiting body formation

To identify GSK3 in G. lucidum, we performed a BLAST search of the annotated G. lucidum genome (http://www.herbalgenomics.org/galu/) using the DNA sequences of four Saccharomyces cerevisiae GSK3 paralogues. This analysis revealed that the G. lucidum genome contains a single GSK3 homolog, designated GlGSK3 (GenBank: OR915582.1). The encoded protein comprises 398 amino acids and contains a highly conserved Ser/Thr kinase catalytic domain (S-TKc) (Fig. S1a), which utilizes ATP to phosphorylate specific Ser/Thr residues in substrate proteins27. Phylogenetic analysis indicated that GlGSK3 shares higher homology with GSK3 proteins from basidiomycete fungi than with those from yeast (Fig. S1b). To investigate the physiological function of GSK3 in G. lucidum, we generated two independent RNAi-mediated knockdown (kd) strains of glgsk3 (glgsk3-kd1/2) with significantly reduced GlGSK3 expression (Fig. 1c, d). Consistent with reports that GSK3 phosphorylates and inhibits glycogen synthase27,29, glycogen content in the glgsk3-kd strains was higher than in the wild-type (WT)strain (127 ± 6.16%–132 ± 4.67%) (Fig. 1e). In contrast, pyruvate (reaching 77 ± 2.82%–78 ± 1.75% of wild-type strain) and ATP (reaching 80 ± 10.48%–83 ± 7.95% of wild-type strain) levels were significantly reduced in the knockdown strains (Fig. 1f, g), indicating impaired glycolysis and energy production. Correspondingly, the radial growth of glgsk3-kd mycelia was markedly slower than that of the wild type, reaching only 62 ± 1.05% and 70 ± 2.23% of WT mycelial diameter (Fig. 1h, i). Biomass accumulation in the knockdown strains was also reduced by half compared to the wild type (Fig. 1j). Furthermore, whereas primordia and fruiting bodies were evident in the wild type at 68- and 90-days post-inoculation, respectively, the glgsk3-kd strains failed to form primordia even after 90 days (Fig. 1h). Furthermore, treatment of the wild-type strain with the GSK3 inhibitor LiCl resulted in a significant, dose-dependent inhibition of mycelial growth (Fig. S2). Collectively, these results demonstrate that GlGSK3 is essential for normal hyphal growth, energy metabolism, and fruiting body development in G. lucidum.

GlGSK3 interacts with GlTPS

As a protein kinase, GSK3 typically exerts its biological functions through phosphorylation of substrate proteins. To investigate the mechanism by which GlGSK3 regulates physiological processes in G. lucidum, we used GlGSK3 as bait in a Y2H screen of a G. lucidum cDNA library to find its interaction partners. Among the candidates identified, GlTPS (GenBank: ON755020.1) was selected for further analysis based on reported roles of TPS homologs in mediating fungal growth and stress responses30–32. To confirm this interaction, the full-length coding sequence of GlTPS was cloned into a prey vector (AD-GlTPS) and tested in a Y2H assay. The results confirmed a direct interaction between GlGSK3 and GlTPS (Fig. 2a). This interaction was further validated using bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays (Fig. 2b). For co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays, total protein was extracted from wild-type mycelia and immunoprecipitated using antibodies specific to GlGSK3 (α-GlGSK3) or GlTPS (α-GlTPS), with rabbit IgG as a control. GlTPS was specifically co-precipitated with α-GlGSK3, but not with control IgG (Fig. 2c). To determine whether the interaction is direct, we purified recombinant His–GlGSK3 and GST–GlTPS proteins and performed a pull-down assay. His–GlGSK3 was enriched by GST–GlTPS, but not by GST alone (Fig. 2d), confirming a direct physical interaction between GlGSK3 and GlTPS. GlGSK3 phosphorylates GlTPS at S529 to enhance its enzymatic activity

Following the validation of their interaction, we performed an in vitro phosphorylation assay using purified recombinant His-GlGSK3 and His-GlTPS proteins. A distinct phosphorylation signal for GlTPS was observed only in the presence of GlGSK3 (Fig. 3a), confirming that GlGSK3 directly phosphorylates GlTPS. To assess the physiological relevance of this modification, we immunoprecipitated GlTPS from total protein extracts of WT and glgsk3-kd strains using an α-GlTPS antibody. Immunoblotting with a pan-antibody against Ser/Thr phosphorylation (α-pSer/Thr) revealed that the phosphorylation level of GlTPS in vivo was significantly reduced in the glgsk3-kd strains (to approximately 65% and 60% of WT levels; Fig. 3b). We next sought to identify the specific phosphorylation site(s) on GlTPS. LC-MS analysis of the in vitro phosphorylated GlTPS pinpointed serine 529 (S529) as the sole residue modified by GlGSK3 (Fig. S3a). This site conforms to the conserved GSK3 phosphorylation motif (Ser/Thr-X-X-X-Ser/Thr; Fig. S3b). To validate Ser529 as the bona fide site, we generated a phospho-null mutant (GlTPSS529A), in which the 529th serine was mutated to non-phosphorylatable alanine (A), and performed in vitro phosphorylation assays. While wild-type GlTPS showed a strong phosphorylation signal, the signal was abolished for GlTPSS529A (Fig. 3c), confirming that GlGSK3 phosphorylates GlTPS exclusively at Ser529.

Protein phosphorylation can regulate various outputs, such as stability, localization, and enzymatic activity33. Immunoblot analysis showed comparable GlTPS protein levels in WT and glgsk3-kd strains (Fig. 3d), suggesting that phosphorylation does not affect GlTPS stability. To evaluate the functional consequence of phosphorylation on enzymatic activity, we created a phosphomimetic mutant (GlTPSS529D), in which serine 529 was substituted with aspartate (D) to mimic constitutive phosphorylation, and measured the TPS activity of the recombinant proteins. The activity of GlTPSS529D (1.30 ± 0.09 folds) was significantly higher than that of the wild-type GlTPS, whereas the GlTPSS529A mutant (reaching 62 ± 8.20% of wild-type GlTPS) exhibited the lowest activity (Fig. 3e). Furthermore, incubation with GlGSK3 and ATP enhanced the activity of wild-type GlTPS but had no effect on either the S529A or S529D mutants (Fig. 3e). Together, these results demonstrate that phosphorylation of GlTPS by GlGSK3 at S529 enhances its enzymatic activity.

Knockdown of GlTPS impairs trehalose production and hyphal growth

Given the central role of the TPS-TPP pathway in fungal trehalose biosynthesis, we measured TPS activity and trehalose content. Both parameters were significantly lower in the gltps-kd strains than in the wild type (Fig. 4f, g). Consistent with our finding that GlGSK3 phosphorylates and activates GlTPS in vitro, the glgsk3-kd strains also displayed significantly reduced TPS activity and trehalose content (Fig. 4f, g). Collectively, these results suggest that the GlGSK3-GlTPS module promotes hyphal growth by enhancing trehalose production in G. lucidum.

The GlGSK3-GlTPS module contributes to heat stress resistance

Given the established roles of GSK3 and TPS in abiotic stress responses6,28,32,34, we investigated their involvement in high temperature tolerance, a major factor limiting G. lucidum growth9,35. Therefore, the heat stress (42 °C for 2 h) response of both GlGSK3 and GlTPS genes was evaluated. The results exhibited that heat stress induced the expression of both GlGSK3 and GlTPS at the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 5a, b). Concordantly, the phosphorylation level of GlTPS increased upon heat stress in the wild-type strain, an effect that was attenuated in glgsk3-kd strains (Fig. 5c), suggesting that GlGSK3 activity is required for the stress-induced phosphorylation of GlTPS. Since phosphorylation activates GlTPS (Fig. 3e), we measured TPS activity and found that heat stress enhanced it across all strains, but this induction was significantly blunted in glgsk3-kd and gltps-kd strains (Fig. 5d). To assess the functional impact on thermotolerance, we examined mycelial growth under heat stress. The glgsk3-kd and gltps-kd strains exhibited greater growth inhibition relative to the wild type (Fig. 5f, g), indicating impaired heat resistance. Exogenous trehalose supplementation mitigated heat stress inhibition in all strains (Fig. 5f, g). Notably, the restorative effect of trehalose was significantly more pronounced in the knockdown strains than in the wild type (Fig. 5g), implying that their intrinsic trehalose biosynthesis is compromised. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the GlGSK3-GlTPS module is critical for thermotolerance in G. lucidum, likely by mediating the heat-triggered upregulation of trehalose biosynthesis.

Given that GSK3 is known to phosphorylate and inhibit glycogen synthase, we hypothesized that GlGSK3 might also regulate glycogen metabolism in G. lucidum, potentially redirecting carbon flux toward trehalose biosynthesis under heat stress. We therefore measured the levels of glycogen, pyruvate, and ATP. Under heat stress, glycogen degradation was more extensive in the wild-type strain (30 ± 0.63% reduction) than in the glgsk3-kd strains (18 ± 2.55%–20 ± 2.17% reduction) (Fig. S4a). Concurrently, heat stress increased pyruvate content in all strains, but this induction was significantly weaker in the glgsk3-kd strains (1.12 ± 0.008–1.13 ± 0.009-fold) compared to the wild type (1.21 ± 0.015-fold) (Fig. S4b). Furthermore, ATP content decreased under heat stress, with a more severe reduction observed in the glgsk3-kd strains (33 ± 3.03%–35 ± 6.66%) than in the wild type (16 ± 2.06%) (Fig. S4c).

Discussion

Both GlGSK3 and GlTPS are crucial for mycelial growth of G. lucidum

GSK3 is a central regulator of carbon metabolism, controlling the balance between glycogen synthesis and glycolysis in mammalian cells34,36, and its homologs are critical for growth and development across diverse organisms27,28. In fungi, GSK3 homologs, including Fgk3 in F. graminearum, MCK1 and RIM11 in S. cerevisiae, MoGSK1 in M. oryzae, and GSKA in A. nidulans, regulate vegetative growth and conidiogenesis27–29,37. However, the functional role of GSK3 in basidiomycete mushrooms remains largely unexplored. Here, we identified a GSK3 homolog, GlGSK3, containing a conserved S-TKc domain in G. lucidum (Fig. S1). Knockdown of GlGSK3 reduced pyruvate and ATP levels (Fig. 1e–g), suggesting a disruption in glycolytic flux that may account for the observed impairments in mycelial growth and fruiting body development (Fig. 1h). Consistent with a conserved role for GSK3 in mushroom development, pharmacological inhibition with LiCl (10-80 mM) also suppressed mycelial growth in G. lucidum (Fig. S2), as previously reported for Coprinopsis cinerea and Pleurotus djamor using LiCl and CHIR9902138.

GSK3 exerts its physiological functions through a complex network of substrates. Our identification of GlTPS as a GlGSK3 interaction partner provides mechanistic insight into its role in G. lucidum. Similar to glgsk3-kd strains, knockdown of GlTPS led to markedly reduced mycelial growth on glucose-containing CYM medium (Fig. 4c–e) and impaired trehalose accumulation due to decreased TPS activity (Fig. 4f, g), indicating that both genes operate in a common pathway governing growth and trehalose production. Beyond its role in trehalose synthesis, TPS is known to modulate glucose metabolism39,40. For example, Δtps1 mutants in M. grisea show defects in hyphal penetration, glucose-dependent growth, and trehalose biosynthesis40, and TPS1 in S. cerevisiae is essential for ascospore formation and survival under high-glucose conditions39. The regulatory link between TPS1 and growth may be explained by several proposed mechanisms in yeast, including: (1) removal of excess sugar-phosphates via trehalose synthesis, (2) inhibition of hexokinase II by T6P, or (3) direct regulation of sugar uptake by TPS141.

The GSK3-TPS module is a novel pathway involved in the response to high temperature

Beyond its role in growth regulation, GlGSK3 is critical for thermotolerance in G. lucidum (Fig. 5j, k). This function appears evolutionarily conserved: a S. cerevisiae quadruple gsk3 mutant with deletion of MCK1, RIM11, MRK1 and YOL128c exhibits heat sensitivity rescued by mammalian GSK3β expression37,42, the Δfgk3 mutant in F. graminearum shows impaired growth under heat stress27, and DcGSK3 knockdown in Diaphorina citri reduces trehalose synthesis43. However, the mechanistic basis for GSK3-mediated stress tolerance in fungi remains poorly understood. Our discovery of the GlGSK3-GlTPS interaction (Fig. 2) provides a key advance. We demonstrate that GlGSK3 phosphorylates GlTPS at S529, enhancing its enzymatic activity (Fig. 3a–c, e) and consequently increasing trehalose production (Fig. 4f, g). This regulatory modification is physiologically relevant: heat stress upregulates both GlGSK3 and GlTPS expression and enhances GlTPS phosphorylation in a GlGSK3-dependent manner (Fig. 5a–c). The resulting boost in trehalose accumulation is a major contributor to thermotolerance, as evidenced by the heightened heat sensitivity of glgsk3-kd and gltps-kd strains and the partial rescue of their growth by exogenous trehalose (Fig. 5f, g). This mechanism aligns with reports that trehalose protects against heat stress in other fungi, such as by reducing intracellular ROS in P. ostreatus6,15 and accumulating in F. velutipes under high temperature10.

Furthermore, under heat stress, the relative accumulation of glycogen was greater in glgsk3-kd strains than in the wild type (Fig. S4a), consistent with the model that GlGSK3 inhibits glycogen synthesis via phosphorylation of glycogen synthase. Concurrently, heat stress increased pyruvate levels but decreased ATP content (Fig. S4b, c). The attenuated rise in pyruvate and the more severe depletion of ATP in the glgsk3-kd strains suggest that GlGSK3 helps redirect carbon flux, potentially shunting pyruvate away from ATP production for growth and toward pathways that support stress defense. Notably, the contribution of GlGSK3 to pyruvate accumulation under heat stress is limited (Fig. S4b), likely due to metabolic reprogramming that prioritizes trehalose synthesis over glycolytic flux. This trade-off ensures cellular protection against thermal damage but constrains energy production, reflecting a conserved adaptive strategy in stress responses.

In summary, we have identified a GlGSK3–GlTPS signaling axis that is crucial for development and thermotolerance in G. lucidum. We demonstrate that GlGSK3-mediated phosphorylation of GlTPS at S529 serves as a key post-translational switch, rapidly enhancing trehalose biosynthesis to confer protection against heat stress. Based on our findings, we propose a model for the dual role of GlGSK3 in balancing growth and stress response (Fig. 6). Under optimal conditions, GlGSK3 activity promotes glycolytic flux-potentially by inhibiting glycogen synthesis via glycogen synthase phosphorylation-to support pyruvate and ATP production, thereby facilitating normal growth and development. Upon heat stress, the concerted upregulation of GlGSK3 and GlTPS expression leads to enhanced phosphorylation and activation of GlTPS. This redirects carbon metabolism towards trehalose accumulation, which is critical for survival under adverse environmental conditions. Materials and methods

Strains and culture condition

The G. lucidum strain ACCC53264 was obtained from the Agricultural Culture Collection of China and used as WT. Strains were cultured in liquid/solid CYM medium (20.0 mg/ml glucose, 10.0 mg/ml maltose, 4.6 mg/ml KH2PO4, 2.0 mg/ml tryptone, 2.0 mg/ml yeast extract, 0.5 mg/ml MgSO4·7H2O, and 20.0 mg/ml agar for solid medium) at 28 °C44.

To observe the growth phenotype of mycelia after heat stress, the indicated strains were grown on solid CYM medium covered with cellophane and incubated at 28 °C for 5 days35. And then exposed to 42 °C for 2 h, followed by 2 days of recovery at 28 °C. The control conditions included the same incubation times without the 42 °C treatment. To investigate the protective effect of trehalose, the indicated strains that had been normally grown for 5 days were transferred to CYM solid medium supplemented with 5 mM trehalose and then exposed to 42 °C for 2 h. The control conditions included the same treatment of the 42 °C without 5 mM trehalose. The growth diameter of the strains was measured, and the plates were photographed.

Construction of transformant

The pMi vector was used to construct knockdown strains via RNAi-mediated gene silencing according to a previous report3. The CDS fragments of GlGSK3 or GlTPS were isolated from the cDNA of G. lucidum by PCR with specific primers. The pMi vector was digested with restriction endonucleases KpnI and SpeI. Following gel extraction of both the PCR products and digested vector fragments, the knockdown vectors pMi-GlGSK3 and pMi-GlTPS were constructed via homologous recombination. Protoplasts were transfected with the recombinant vector via Lipofectamine™ 2000 (Thermo Scientific, 11668019)-mediated transfection, and hygromycin (150 μg/ml) was used to screen of the knockdown strains. Then, PCR analysis was performed to detect the presence of the fusion fragment containing the hygromycin phosphotransferase (hph) gene in genomic DNA isolated from selected strains. The expression of the indicated genes in the knockdown strains was determined via RT-qPCR and western blotting, and strains whose expression was relatively low were selected for the following experiments. For the control strain (CK), the empty pMi vector was used to generate transgenic strains as described above. The primers used are shown in Table S1.

Determination of TPS enzyme activity

The TPS enzyme activity was measured using the TPS Activity Assay Kit (Suzhou Comin Biotechnology, TPS-1-Y) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, the strains were cultured on solid CYM medium at 28 °C for 5 days. Then, 100 mg of mycelia were ground into powder in liquid nitrogen and subsequently homogenized with 1 ml of TPS extraction buffer. The mixture was subsequently centrifuged at 4 °C and 10,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was collected before being incubated with the working solution for 20 min. Following the completion of the reaction, the absorbance of the mixture at 340 nm was measured. For the heat stress treatment, 5-day-old mycelia were incubated at 42 °C for 2 h before being used as samples for TPS extraction.

Western blotting

The sample strains were cultured at 28 °C for 7 days on CYM solid medium before subjected to 42 °C for 2 h (heat stress) or 28 °C for 2 h (control). After treatment, approximately 100 mg of mycelia were collected and ground into powder and subsequently homogenized with 800 μl of protein extraction buffer (PEB, 50 mM Tris-Cl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% Tween-20, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, pH 7.5 and protease inhibitor cocktail). The mixture was subjected to centrifugation at 4 °C and 12,000 × g for 10 min, after which the supernatant was collected. The Bradford dye reagent (Bio-Rad, 5000205) was used to determine the protein concentration. Proteins were separated by 10% SDS‒PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Vazyme, E802). Poteins were detected with the indicated antibodies, α-GlGSK3 (1:2000, rabbit polyclonal antibody), α-GlTPS (1:2000, rabbit polyclonal antibody), α-pSer/Thr (1:4000, Abcam, ab117253) and α-β-actin (1:2000, Abmart, M20011).

Yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assay

The CDS fragments of GlGSK3 were subsequently cloned and inserted into the pGBKT7 vector to obtain the recombinant vector BD-GlGSK3. A Y2H experiment was conducted to screen for GlGSK3-interacting proteins from the cDNA library of G. lucidum. To validate the interaction between GlGSK3 and GlTPS, the CDS fragments of GlTPS were cloned and inserted into pGADT7 to generate AD-GlTPS. The vectors BD-GlGSK3 and AD-GlTPS were co-transformed into Y2HGold competent cells. The transformed cells were cultured on DDO medium (SD/-Leu-Trp) at 28 °C for 3 days, followed by transfer to QDO medium (SD/-Leu-Trp-His-Ade) at 28 °C for another 4 days. The primers used are shown in Table S1.

Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

For the Co-IP assays, 0.2 g of mycelia were ground into powder in liquid nitrogen and homogenized with 800 μl of IP lysis buffer (100 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.6, 1 mM DTT, 10% glycerol, 1 mM PMSF, and a protease inhibitor cocktail) for 1 h at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected after centrifugation at 4 °C and 12,000 × g for 10 min and incubated with 2 μl of the indicated antibody (α-GlTPS) or control rabbit IgG (Solarbio Biotechnology) at 4 °C for 8 h. Then, 30 μl of protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Scientific, 88802) were added, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 3 h. Following incubation, the magnetic beads were washed with IP lysis buffer 5 times and then eluted with SDS sample buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 1% SDS, 10% glycerol, 50 mM DTT, and 0.005% bromophenol blue). Protein samples were separated by SDS‒PAGE and subjected to western blotting.

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BIFC)

The full-length coding sequences of GlGSK3 and GlTPS were cloned and inserted into the split-GFP vectors PVN and PVC, respectively35. Specifically, GlGSK3 cDNA was inserted downstream of the N-terminal fragment of the GFP-encoding gene, while GlTPS cDNA was inserted downstream of the C-terminal fragment, resulting in the recombinant vectors PVN-GlGSK3 and PVC-GlTPS. The recombinant plasmids were co-transformed into the yeast strain SFY2620 using a yeast transformation reagent kit (Coolaber, YH2010) and subsequently cultured on deficient SD medium (SD/-Ura-His). A single colony was selected for culture and subjected to starvation induction before observation. The fluorescence was observed using laser scanning confocal microscopy at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm. Three different combinations (PVN-GlGSK3 and PVC, PVN-GlTPS and PVN, and PVN and PVC), were individually transformed into the yeast strain SFY2620 and served as negative controls.

GST Pull-down

Primers were designed to amplify the full-length GlGSK3 gene from G. lucidum cDNA template. Concurrently, the His-tagged pCold vector was digested with restriction endonucleases BamHI and HindIII. Following gel extraction of both the PCR products and digested vector, the His-tagged pCold-GlGSK3 vector was constructed via homologous recombination. The GST-tagged pGEX-4T-1-GlTPS vector was constructed following the above-described steps. The plasmids pGEX-4T-1-GlTPS and pCold-GlGSK3 were transformed into Rosetta (DE3) cells. 1 ml of cells grown overnight were used to inoculate 100 ml of LB media and grown with constant shaking (220 rpm) at 37 °C until the OD600 = 0.6. At this point, the culture was incubated with shaking at 130 rpm and 16 °C for 16–20 h, with IPTG (Sangon, A100487) added to a final concentration of 0.2 mM. The finally induced and expressed HIS-GSK3 and GST-TPS proteins were purified and eluted using the corresponding magnetic beads and reagents.

For GST pull down, the GST-magnetic beads (Sangon, C650031) were first incubated with the protein GST or GST-GlTPS in binding buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA) for 3 h, followed by an overnight incubation with the purified His-GlGSK3. After incubation, the beads were washed thoroughly with washing buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, pH 7.4) 5 times. The cleaned beads were eluted with SDS sample buffer. Western blotting was employed to detect the elution products with the indicated antibodies.

Determination of trehalose content

For determination of trehalose content, 100 mg of mycelia cultured on solid CYM medium was ground to powder in liquid nitrogen. The powder was subsequently resuspended in 1 ml of ddH2O and ultrasonicated (20 KHz) at 60 °C for 1 h to extract the intracellular trehalose. Then, the samples were centrifuged at 4 °C and 12,000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatants were collected. Chromatographic analysis for the determination of trehalose content was performed using HPLC-ELSD (Agilent 1260 ELSD, Agilent). Different concentrations of trehalose standards (Merck, PHR1344) (3000 μg/ml, 1500 μg/ml, 750 μg/ml, 375 μg/ml, and 187.5 μg/ml) were used to construct a standard curve with the concentration and peak value. The trehalose concentration of the sample was calculated from the peak value according to the standard curve equation.

In vitro phosphorylation assay

To detect the phosphorylation signal via western blotting, kinase buffer Ⅰ consisted of 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, ddH2O and 2 mM ATPγS (Abcam, ab138911). The recombinant proteins GST-GlTPS (or GST-GlTPSS529A) and His-GlGSK3 were incubated in kinase buffer Ⅰ for 30 min at 30 °C. Then, p-nitrobenzyl mesylate (Abcam, ab138910) was added, and the mixture was incubated for additional 2 h at 30 °C. Finally, SDS sample buffer was added to terminate the reaction at 95 °C for 5 min. The samples were subjected to SDS‒PAGE and western blotting with an antibody α-thiophosphate ester (Abcam, ab138910) to assess the phosphorylation signal. The α-thiophosphate ester monoclonal antibody was generated using a thiophosphate ester hapten, which was created by alkylating the thiophosphorylation site on the phosphorylated protein with p-nitrobenzyl mesylate.

To identify the phosphorylation site by mass spectrum, the recombinant proteins GST-GlTPS and His-GlGSK3 were incubated in kinase buffer Ⅱ (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, ddH2O and 2 mM ATP) for 30 min at 30 °C. The proteins were subsequently digested with trypsin and subjected to high-resolution mass spectrum for analysis via LC‒MS/MS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Q-Exactive).

RNA isolation and qRT-PCR

The sample strains were cultured at 28 °C for 7 days on CYM solid medium with or without heat stress treatment. Then, 100 mg of mycelia were ground into powder and used to isolate RNA with RNA Isolation buffer (Proteinssci Biotech, AF508) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix Perfect for qPCR (Vazyme, R333-01) was used for first-strand cDNA synthesis. The relative expression levels of the target genes were analyzed using RT-qPCR with the 2–∆∆Ct method. The primers used are listed in Supplementary Table S1, and 18S rRNA was used as a reference gene.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical parameters are reported in the figures and figure legends. Statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego). Two-tailed Student’s t test was used to analyze the statistical significance of differences between two groups, and asterisks indicate statistical significance: *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01. One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test was used to analyze the statistical significance for more than two groups, and the different letters indicate significant differences: P < 0.01.
