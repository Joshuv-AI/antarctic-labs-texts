Endothelial cell-specific DNA methylation alterations in breast cancer
Authors: Barbara Karakyriakou, Ze Zhang, Hanxu Lu, Lucas A. Salas, Brock C. Christensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
DNA methylation alterations are well-established contributors to carcinogenesis, yet, in the tumor microenvironment (TME), patterns of lineage and cell-specific methylation alterations are not well understood. Single-cell DNA methylation profiling in the TME is limited by technical challenges and high costs. Here, we use bulk DNA methylation, cell type deconvolution (HiTIMED), and an interaction testing framework (CellDMC) to identify reproducible, computationally inferred lineage-specific epigenetic alterations in the TME supported by orthogonal data sources. Tumor endothelial cells (TECs), critical regulators of angiogenesis, vascular permeability, and immune cell trafficking, acquire structural and functional abnormalities that promote tumor growth. We hypothesize that TECs have altered DNA methylation compared with endothelial cells in non-tumor tissues. In genome-scale methylation data from discovery and validation datasets (tumor n = 1071; non-tumor n = 415), we identify and validate >4500 TEC-specific CpGs with altered methylation, many mapping to genes involved in angiogenesis and endothelial function. Integration with gene expression data indicates that TEC-specific methylation alterations may reprogram transcriptional networks controlling angiogenesis. High-resolution, cell lineage-specific epigenetic landscapes can be inferred from bulk methylation data, implicating TEC-specific DNA methylation alterations as potential drivers of cancer angiogenesis and vascular dysfunction and providing a framework for future mechanistic and translational studies of the tumor vasculature.

Introduction

DNA methylation, a key epigenetic modification, is crucial for regulating gene expression and maintaining cellular identity. The addition of methyl groups to cytosine residues within gene promoter regions can lead to gene silencing1. DNA methylation alterations implicated in carcinogenesis and disease progression include hypomethylation of repetitive elements and hypermethylation of tumor suppressor gene promoter CpG islands2. However, much of the focus in cancer epigenetics has been on cancer cells themselves, in part because DNA methylation is typically measured in bulk biospecimens, leaving a gap in understanding DNA methylation alterations specific to non-tumor cells within the tumor microenvironment (TME), including endothelial cells.

The TME is a complex and dynamic ecosystem composed of various cell types that significantly influence tumor biology3. Among these, tumor endothelial cells (TECs) play a critical role in tumor progression due to their involvement in tumor angiogenesis, the formation of new blood vessels from existing vessels that supply the tumor with nutrients and facilitate metastatic spread4,5. Without blood vessels, tumors cannot grow beyond a microscopic size, and metastasis, the deadliest step in the tumor progression cascade, critically depends on bidirectional tumor‒vessel interactions6.

TECs exhibit phenotypic and functional differences from normal endothelial cells, driven, in part, by epigenetic alterations such as DNA methylation7–9. Investigating TEC-specific DNA methylation patterns is essential, as these changes may underlie the unique properties of TECs and their role in promoting a protumorigenic environment. The endothelial cell layer is now widely recognized as a highly dynamic interface that exerts instructive gatekeeper functions on its surrounding microenvironment10. Breast cancer, one of the most prevalent types of cancer for women worldwide, presents a valuable model for studying these alterations. The breast TME is highly heterogeneous and includes endothelial cells that play distinct roles in tumor progression11. Understanding cell-specific epigenetic changes has implications not only for breast cancer but also for other cancer types, potentially revealing therapeutic targets to disrupt the supportive role of the TME in cancer progression.

Identifying DNA methylation changes specific to TECs poses methodological challenges, as traditional bulk analyses are by nature confounded by the heterogeneous cell composition of the TME. Variations in cell proportions can obscure true cell type-specific methylation alterations, potentially leading to misleading conclusions. However, single-cell sequencing approaches for DNA methylation are technically challenging, and very high costs limit the sample sizes needed to generalize findings for translational applications. To overcome these limitations, we used methylation data and other molecular data measured from bulk tumor and normal tissue samples. By applying cell-type deconvolution via the hierarchical tumor immune microenvironment epigenetic deconvolution algorithm HiTIMED12 and CellDMC13 for interaction testing, we identified TEC-specific differential methylation in the TME. Together, HiTIMED and CellDMC provide a robust framework for identifying and investigating cell-specific molecular alterations in typical bulk biospecimens.

First, we performed a bulk epigenome-wide association study (EWAS) to test the association between disease status (cancer versus normal) and CpG-specific DNA methylation levels in the discovery cohort using linear models with multiple-testing correction. The initial EWAS included adjustment for age and identified 21,576 CpGs with significant differential methylation in tumors compared with normal tissue (FDR < 0.05 and |Δβ| > 0.2, Fig. 2C). To account for differences in tissue cellular composition between cancer and normal samples, we then performed a composition-adjusted bulk EWAS including estimated proportions of five major cell types (myeloid, lymphocyte, endothelial, epithelial, and stromal). After this adjustment, the scope of the observed significantly differentially methylated CpG sites was dramatically reduced (503 CpGs FDR < 0.05 and |Δβ| > 0.2, Fig. 2D, Supplementary Data 1, Supplementary Table S1). This model tests whether bulk cancer–normal methylation differences persist after accounting for cellular composition, thereby isolating CpGs whose differential methylation cannot be explained by shifts in the relative abundance of major cell types. To support valid cell type-adjusted modeling, we assessed multicollinearity among estimated cell type fractions using variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis (Supplementary Data 1, Supplementary Table S2), confirming acceptable levels for inclusion in regression models.

Reproducible tumor endothelial cell-specific DNA methylation alterations exhibit a distinct epigenetic landscape in the breast tumor microenvironment

To identify tumor endothelial cell (TEC)-specific DNA methylation alterations, we applied the CellDMC13 algorithm to both the discovery and validation datasets. CellDMC extends conventional differential methylation analysis by incorporating interaction terms between disease status and estimated cell-type proportions in a linear modeling framework. This approach tests whether cancer–normal methylation differences vary as a function of a given cell type’s abundance, thereby enabling the identification of CpGs whose differential methylation occurs specifically within a particular cellular compartment rather than reflecting bulk tissue composition effects. In contrast to linear models that adjust for cell-type proportions to control confounding, CellDMC incorporates disease-cell type interaction terms to identify cell type-specific differential methylation.

To avoid structural zeros in CellDMC analyses, cell-type proportions were re-established via HiTIMED12 by setting the tissue type to “tumor” for all samples, generating low but nonzero tumor cell fractions in normal tissues. This adjustment is designed to minimize distortion of the interaction model rather than introduce tumor-driven signal into normal tissues. Because all non-tumor cell types are scaled down uniformly, no individual cell type, including endothelial cells, is preferentially affected, and the endothelial proportion continues to reflect relative endothelial abundance across samples. Importantly, endothelial-specific DMCs are identified through the interaction between disease status and endothelial proportion, not through absolute cell type fractions. Thus, the introduction of a minimal, uniformly distributed tumor-like fraction in normal samples cannot generate endothelial-specific interaction effects unless true cancer–normal methylation differences exist within the endothelial compartment. A visualization of the resulting tumor cell proportions in normal samples for both the discovery and validation sets is provided in Supplementary Fig. S2A, B. While breast cancer samples were non-overlapping between discovery and validation cohorts, a shared set of normal breast tissues was included in both analyses to ensure adequate statistical power for cancer–normal comparisons in the validation cohort, where normal samples were limited; these normals served only as a reference baseline and did not introduce information leakage.

In the discovery set, out of the 396,246 CpG sites tested, 25,164 hypermethylated, and 1528 hypomethylated TEC-specific CpGs were identified as significantly differentially methylated (FDR ≤ 0.05, and |coef.| >0.0062 corresponding to a ≥5% methylation change in endothelial cells) (Fig. 3A). To ensure specificity, we removed CpGs that were also differentially methylated in other cell types in the same direction. Specifically, 12,949 hyper and 524 hypomethylated CpGs overlapped with tumor cell signals; 2143 hyper and 13 hypomethylated CpGs overlapped with epithelial signals; and additional overlaps were observed with the stromal and myeloid compartments. In the validation set, the CellDMC13 model identified 24,282 hypermethylated and 3154 hypomethylated TEC-specific DMCs (FDR ≤ 0.05, |coef.| >0.0087 corresponding to a ≥5% change in methylation) (Fig. 3C). Using the same filtering strategy, we excluded 14,736 hyper and 1553 hypomethylated CpGs that overlapped with tumor cell signals, along with additional overlaps from epithelial, stromal, and myeloid cells. This resulted in 7791 hypermethylated and 1593 hypomethylated uniquely TEC-specific CpGs (Fig. 3D). To assess reproducibility, we intersected the uniquely TEC-specific DMCs from both datasets. A total of 4562 CpGs were consistently differentially methylated across the discovery and validation sets, including 4163 hypermethylated and 399 hypomethylated sites (Fig. 3E), highlighting a robust and reproducible TEC-specific epigenetic signature in breast cancer. These CpGs mapped to 1689 unique genes for the hypermethylated set and 235 genes for the hypomethylated set (Supplementary Data 1, Supplementary Table S3), suggesting both widespread and targeted transcriptional regulatory potential.

To further characterize the biological relevance of these alterations and assess broader genomic patterns, we analyzed the distribution of hyper and hypomethylated TEC-specific CpGs across annotated gene regions (Fig. 3F). Compared with all the tested CpGs associated with genes (n = 306,171), TEC-specific hypermethylated CpGs were more frequently located within gene bodies, particularly in introns. In contrast, hypomethylated CpGs in TECs were disproportionately enriched in promoter regions upstream of transcription start sites (TSS1500).

Analysis of the CpG island context revealed an enrichment of both hyper and hypomethylated CpGs in open sea regions (Fig. 3G). Compared with the background set, TEC-specific hypermethylated CpGs presented increased enhancer localization, with a similar trend observed for hypomethylated CpGs (Fig. 3H). TEC-specific hypomethylated CpGs were enriched in DNase I hypersensitive sites (DHSs), whereas hypermethylated CpGs were depleted in DHSs (Fig. 3I).

TEC-specific DMCs map to angiogenesis genes

We further examined the gene associations of tumor endothelial cell (TEC)-specific differentially methylated CpGs (DMCs) identified in the intersected discovery and validation sets. Notably, several of these reproducible TEC-specific DMCs mapped to the CASZ1, EGFR, EVL, RUNX3, SFRP1, and THY1 genes, which are known to regulate angiogenesis and endothelial cell biology. Across both datasets, we identified DMCs associated with genes in the VEGF signaling pathway, namely, AKT1, AKT2, CDC42, CHP2, MAPK13, PIK3CD, PIK3R2, PIK3R5, PLA2G10, PLA2G4A, PRKCA, PRKCB, PRKD1, PTK2, SPHK2, VEGFA and VEGFB (Supplementary Data 1, Supplementary Table S4), and hallmark angiogenesis genes, including CCND2, ITGAV, LRPAP1, MSX1, POSTN, PTK2, VAV2, and VTN. In the intersected set of high-confidence TEC-specific DMCs, nearly all of these angiogenesis-associated genes were retained. This finding reinforces the biological relevance of the intersected CpGs and their potential as robust biomarkers or therapeutic targets in tumor vascular remodeling.

Representative examples of TEC-specific DMCs mapped to angiogenesis-related genes are shown for RUNX3 (Fig. 4A), EGFR (Fig. 4B), and CASZ1 (Fig. 4C). These genes presented clusters of hypermethylated CpGs in TECs across multiple gene isoforms. TEC-specific DMCs are enriched in angiogenic programs and endothelial lineage signatures

To investigate the functional relevance of the high-confidence TEC-specific differentially methylated CpGs identified in the intersected discovery and validation sets, we next examined the biological programs and endothelial states associated with their mapped genes. Given the central role of endothelial cells in tumor angiogenesis and vascular remodeling, we hypothesized that TEC-specific methylation alterations would preferentially localize to genes involved in angiogenic signaling and endothelial lineage-specific programs.

Genes mapped to TEC-specific differentially methylated CpGs were significantly enriched for adhesion, integrin, and extracellular matrix genes, including JAM2, DLC1, and NID1, which were also independently identified in endothelial proportion-expression correlation analyses (Supplementary Data 1, Supplementary Table S6).

To further address endothelial heterogeneity within the scope of our methylation-based framework, we performed a targeted enrichment analysis leveraging endothelial subtype marker genes derived from the single-cell endothelial taxonomy reported by Geldhof et al. (Nat Commun., 2022). Specifically, we tested whether TEC-specific differentially methylated CpGs were preferentially enriched in gene sets associated with arterial, lymphatic, or angiogenic endothelial programs. Using Fisher’s exact tests with multiple testing correction, we observed significant enrichment of TEC-specific CpGs in angiogenic endothelial marker genes (odds ratio = 1.85, FDR = 0.04), whereas no significant enrichment was detected for arterial or lymphatic endothelial signatures (Supplementary Data 1, Supplementary Table S7).

In addition, to evaluate concordance with independent endothelial epigenetic signatures, we directly compared our intersected TEC-specific methylation signature with vascular endothelial cell (VEC) marker regions reported by Peretz et al. (2023). TEC-specific DMCs were mapped to genes and tested for overlap enrichment with genes associated with Peretz common VEC marker regions using Fisher’s exact test. We observed significant enrichment of Peretz VEC marker genes among genes mapped to our TEC-specific methylation signature (odds ratio = 1.89, P = 3.87 x 10⁻⁵) (Supplementary Data 1, Supplementary Table S8).

Endothelial cell proportion is correlated with the expression of genes linked to TEC-specific DMCs

To investigate the transcriptional relevance of tumor endothelial cell (TEC)-specific DNA methylation alterations, we analyzed gene expression data from 245 breast cancer samples in the discovery set with matched Agilent microarray mRNA profiles. We focused on genes associated with CpGs in the intersected TEC-specific differentially methylated (DMCs) set and assessed their correlation with endothelial cell proportions across samples. Gene expression correlations were assessed using bulk gene expression data. Because endothelial cells represent a minority population in bulk samples, expression signals reflect mixed cellular contributions and may attenuate endothelial-specific transcriptional effects. The top-correlated gene, LHFP, exhibited a strong positive correlation between expression and endothelial content (r = 0.6, p < 0.001). Overall, 13 genes demonstrated moderate to strong correlations (r ≥ 0.5), as shown in Table 1. Many of the CpGs associated with these genes were hypermethylated and mapped to gene body and enhancer regions, suggesting epigenetic regulation through nonpromoter elements (Table 2).To validate these findings, we repeated the analysis using RNA-sequencing data from the independent validation cohort. Consistent with the discovery set, LHFP emerged as the top-correlated gene (Fig. 6A), and the core set of 13 genes was consistently ranked among the top correlations in both datasets despite differences in experimental platforms. Discussion

Using computational methods and orthogonal validation, our study identifies that tumor endothelial cells (TECs) exhibit distinct DNA methylation remodeling in breast cancer, providing mechanistic clues into how the tumor vasculature supports angiogenesis and tumor progression. The identification of >4500 TEC-specific differentially methylated CpGs (DMCs) highlights that endothelial cells undergo coordinated epigenetic alterations targeting genes involved in vascular growth, hypoxia response, and stromal communication. Several candidate loci illustrate this mechanistic potential. TEC-specific DMCs were enriched at angiogenic regulators such as RUNX1T114, a transcription factor implicated in angiogenic regulation, and RHOJ15–19, a gene with established roles in endothelial cell biology and angiogenesis. Additional loci, including LHFP and LOC399959 (the MIR-100/LET-7A-2/MIR-125B-1 microRNA cluster host gene)20,21 harbored strong TEC-specific methylation signals, suggesting that endothelial-restricted methylation changes may influence both transcriptional and noncoding regulatory programs in angiogenesis. Integration with expression data further supports this interpretation, as genes with TEC-specific DMCs displayed strong correlation with endothelial abundance in tumors.

Analysis of genomic context revealed distinct localization patterns for hypermethylated and hypomethylated CpGs. Hypermethylated sites were enriched in gene bodies and enhancer regions, whereas hypomethylated CpGs were predominantly located in promoter regions. Both categories were enriched in open sea regions, which often harbor distal regulatory elements such as enhancers. In addition, hypomethylated CpGs were enriched within DNase hypersensitive sites, suggesting preferentially occurrence in regions associated with active chromatin. These patterns support a model in which TEC methylome remodeling contributes to the establishment of endothelial-specific regulatory landscapes: promoter hypomethylation may facilitate regulatory activation, whereas gene body methylation may influence transcriptional elongation or alternative promoter usage22. The predominance of hypermethylated CpGs in TECs may therefore reflect epigenetic stabilization of endothelial transcriptional programs during tumor-associated vascular remodeling, particularly given that many of these sites were located within intronic or gene body regions where DNA methylation has been associated with transcriptional elongation, alternative promoter usage, and enhancer-associated regulatory activity.

TEC-specific DMCs mapped to thousands of genes, with dense clusters of hypermethylated CpGs at key loci. The largest cluster included 108 TEC-specific hypermethylated CpGs associated with DAXX, a chromatin remodeling gene implicated in cell fate regulation and tumorigenesis23–25. Additional clusters were detected in angiogenesis-related genes, including EGFR, RUNX3, and CASZ1. EGFR promotes angiogenesis through crosstalk with VEGF signaling pathways in the tumor microenvironment (TME)26–29, whereas RUNX3, a known tumor suppressor, inhibits angiogenesis by binding the VEGF promoter and downregulating VEGF production30–32, and CASZ1 is a transcription factor essential for angiogenic sprouting and vessel morphogenesis33–35. We also identified TEC-specific DMCs within PRKCA, a VEGF signaling pathway component36–39, within AKT1, a core component of the PI3K–AKT pathway, which promotes pathological angiogenesis by modulating vascular permeability, endothelial cell activation, and vessel maturation40–47, and within EVL and THY1, genes associated with cytoskeletal remodeling48,49, and endothelial adhesion and migration50–52.

Consistent with these locus-level observations, targeted gene set enrichment analysis demonstrated significant enrichment of angiogenesis and vascular development related pathways among genes associated with TEC-specific DMCs. These findings indicate that TEC-specific methylation alterations converge on coordinated angiogenic regulatory programs controlling endothelial activation, migration, and vessel remodeling rather than representing isolated epigenetic events.

Integration of TEC-specific DMCs with single-cell chromatin accessibility profiles from breast endothelial cells revealed concordance between DNA methylation changes and chromatin accessibility states within the tumor endothelium. Hypomethylated CpGs were most frequently observed within ATAC-accessible chromatin regions, consistent with activation of endothelial regulatory elements. A subset of hypomethylated CpGs located in ATAC-inaccessible chromatin mapped to regulatory regions, including promoter-proximal sites of ANXA353, and THRB54, genes with known roles in angiogenesis, suggesting epigenetically primed regulatory elements that remain transcriptionally inactive in the analyzed TEC state. Conversely, hypermethylated CpGs were often located within accessible gene bodies and regulatory regions associated with endothelial signaling genes, including NOTCH455,56, COL4A157,58, and BCAS359–61. Together, these patterns suggest that DNA methylation remodeling in tumor endothelial cells occurs both at poised regulatory loci and within actively accessible chromatin, potentially contributing to the transcriptional rewiring of angiogenic and vascular signaling pathways within the TME.

The presence of CpGs with inverse methylation patterns between TECs and other cell types suggests possible intercellular regulatory mechanisms. These patterns may reflect endothelial responses to microenvironmental signals such as hypoxia or VEGF, or shared regulatory pathways with stromal populations including cancer-associated fibroblasts and pericytes, cell types known to contribute to tumor angiogenesis62–70. Consistent with this interpretation, the TEC-associated gene set identified in the mRNA-endothelial cell proportion correlation analysis showed minimal correlation with other cell types in the TME, except for stromal cells. This observation raises the possibility that a subset of the TEC-specific transcriptional program may be shared with stromal cells involved in vascular remodeling.

Beyond biological insights, our work demonstrates the value of integrating cell deconvolution with statistical interaction testing to uncover lineage-specific epigenetic alterations from bulk DNA methylation data. While each component method is established, their combined application provides resolution that would otherwise require expensive single-cell profiling. Because the approach is generalizable, it can be applied to CAFs, immune cells, and across tumor types, creating a versatile platform for dissecting TME-specific epigenetic mechanisms.

The translational implications are considerable. TEC-specific methylation signatures could serve as biomarkers of angiogenic activity or vascular remodeling within tumors. While targeting overexpressed proteins or transcript variants remains a clinically feasible strategy, TEC-specific epigenetic alterations offer complementary therapeutic opportunities. In particular, targeting epigenetic regulators in TECs could disrupt tumor vascularization while minimizing off-target effects on non-endothelial cell types. Emerging tools such as CRISPR-based epigenome editors, for example, dCas9 fused to DNMT3A or TET1 catalytic domains, offer locus-specific modulation of DNA methylation and may enable future functional interrogation or therapeutic targeting of candidate loci such as RHOJ or LOC399959 or other TEC-specific epigenetic alterations71. This mechanistic link between methylation and endothelial phenotype provides a rationale for exploring epigenetic editing of the tumor vasculature as a therapeutic avenue. Importantly, this concept differs fundamentally from current anti-angiogenic therapies, such as VEGF pathway inhibitors, which act extracellularly to broadly suppress angiogenic signaling. In contrast, CRISPR-based epigenome editing would allow targeted modulation of endothelial-intrinsic regulatory programs identified here, serving primarily as a functional validation framework and a potential future precision strategy rather than an immediate alternative to established anti-angiogenic agents.

Our study has limitations. Endothelial-specific DNA methylation alterations were inferred from bulk tissue methylation profiles using deconvolution-based modeling rather than measured directly in purified endothelial cells. Therefore, these loci are best interpreted as putative endothelial-specific methylation signals. Although validation across independent cohorts and integration with gene expression and chromatin accessibility data support their biological relevance, this approach cannot fully eliminate the possibility of residual cell type heterogeneity, errors in estimated cell fractions, or endothelial subtype-specific effects not represented in the reference methylation signatures. Functional validation is required to confirm direct causal roles of TEC-specific methylation changes. Large sample sizes are necessary for adequately powered interaction testing, and future applications to other cancers will determine whether the observed endothelial signatures are conserved or context-specific. Nonetheless, by coupling computational deconvolution with integrative biological analysis, we provide a foundation for mechanistic exploration of TEC biology that is immediately testable using experimental and translational approaches. Ongoing and future single-cell methylation studies will further help confirm the cellular specificity and biological consequences of these alterations.

In summary, we uncover a reproducible landscape of TEC-specific DNA methylation alterations that converge on angiogenic regulators, propose testable mechanistic links between epigenetic remodeling and endothelial transcriptional activity, and establish a broadly applicable framework for identifying cell type-specific alterations in the TME. These findings open avenues for biomarker discovery, mechanistic validation, and ultimately, targeted therapeutic intervention in the tumor vasculature.

Methods

Study population data

Breast cancer and normal breast tissue DNA methylation data were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; RRID:SCR_005012), The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA; RRID:SCR_003193), and the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx; RRID:SCR_013042) project. All the samples were profiled using Illumina Infinium HumanMethylation450 BeadChip or MethylationEPIC arrays. The discovery set included breast tumor tissues from GSE6018572–74 (n = 239), GSE6631375–77 (n = 40), and GSE8420774,78 (n = 330); adjacent normal breast tissues from GSE66313 (n = 15); and normal tissues from GSE60185 (n = 46), GSE74214 (n = 18), GSE8888379 (n = 100), GSE10196180 (n = 121), and GTEx81 (n = 40). All the subjects were female. In the discovery set, the mean age was 41.0 years (SD = 13.5, range 13–82) for normal tissue samples (n = 340) and 57.5 years (SD = 12.3, range 29.5–92) for tumor tissue samples (n = 608) (Supplementary Data 1, Supplementary Table S12). The availability of matched gene expression data was also considered when possible: GSE5821274,82–84 (n = 285) mRNA samples matched with samples in the GSE8420774,78 methylation set. The tumor samples were from primary sites, the adjacent normal samples were matched to the corresponding tumors, and the normal tissues were derived from mammary tissues. GEO datasets were selected on the basis of the availability of raw IDAT files, clear tissue-type annotations, and sufficient metadata. The validation set comprised breast cancer (n = 471) and adjacent normal (n = 75) tissues from the TCGA85. The TCGA samples were selected from the BRCA cohort, restricted to female samples and primary tumor sites, with matched adjacent normal tissues when available and with available DNA methylation data. In the validation set, the mean age was 56.4 years (SD = 14.8, range 28–89) for normal tissue samples (n = 75) and 56.4 years (SD = 12.9, range 26–89) for tumor tissue samples (n = 471) (Supplementary Data 1, Supplementary Table S12). Data for all TCGA projects were downloaded with the TCGAbiolinks R package86 (v2.32.0; RRID:SCR_017683), setting the parameters data.category to “DNA Methylation,” platform to “Illumina Human Methylation 450” and data.type to “Raw Intensities.”

All DNA methylation measurements were obtained from distinct biological tissue samples. Each sample corresponds to a single tissue specimen profiled once per platform, and no repeated methylation measurements from the same biological sample were included. While a subset of adjacent normal tissues in GEO and TCGA was matched to tumor samples from the same individual, these samples were treated as independent tissue specimens in downstream analyses, and no repeated-measures or paired statistical models were applied.

Randomization

As this analysis was based on preexisting public datasets, no experimental randomization was possible. The samples were grouped according to the original study annotations (tumor, adjacent normal, normal).

Data preprocessing and quality control

The raw intensity data (IDAT files) were processed via the minfi87 (v1.50.0; RRID:SCR_012830) and ENmix88 (v1.40.2) toolkits, Bioconductor packages designed for comprehensive analysis of Illumina Methylation BeadChip Arrays, including quality control and normalization. Probes containing single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) or those classified as cross-reactive were excluded at both the locus and sample levels.

The quality of samples and probes was assessed via multiple criteria, including out-of-band detection P values to ensure accurate signal detection, the mean fluorescence intensity of bisulfite conversion control probes to evaluate methylation efficiency, and the number of beads per probe to confirm adequate probe coverage. Within-array correction for background fluorescence and dye biases was conducted via minfi’s preprocessFunnorm with the default setting method for background subtraction, Noob (normal-exponential out-of-band), followed by the first two principal components of the control probes to infer unwanted variation, ensuring uniform signal correction89. Following quality control and correction, beta values were calculated for each CpG site, representing the proportion of methylated to unmethylated signals. These beta values, ranging from 0 to 1, provide a continuous measure of DNA methylation intensity at each locus. Data were filtered to include only probes common to the 450K array, excluding cross-reactive probes and probes that failed quality control. The final dataset comprised high-quality methylation measurements across 396,246 CpG loci after 18 breast cancer samples and 7 adjacent normal breast samples from the discovery set whose quality control failed.

To address batch effects arising from variations in experimental conditions and sample processing between independent datasets, we implemented robust batch correction techniques. We employed ComBat90 (sva package, v3.52.0; RRID:SCR_012836), an empirical Bayes-based batch correction algorithm, to harmonize DNA methylation profiles across datasets. This approach mitigated nonbiological variability, ensuring consistency and reliability in downstream analyses.

Bulk epigenome-wide association analysis (EWAS)

Bulk differential DNA methylation analysis was performed exclusively to characterize genome-wide methylation differences at the tissue level (Fig. 2) and was not used for cell type-specific inference. For this purpose, we conducted an epigenome-wide association study (EWAS) comparing cancer and normal breast tissue samples using linear modeling, to systematically identify DNA methylation differences across the genome associated with our phenotype of interest. For EWAS, we used the limma91 (v3.60.6; RRID:SCR_010943) R package, which fits probe-wise linear models with empirical Bayes moderation of variance estimates. Methylation beta values were modeled as the outcome variable, with phenotype (cancer vs. normal) as the primary predictor. All tests were two-sided. Models included age at diagnosis/sample collection as a covariate, and in secondary bulk models, estimated cell-type proportions were included to assess the impact of cellular heterogeneity on bulk signals. To ensure that the identified CpGs represent biologically meaningful differences, we applied a Δβ threshold of 0.2, which reflects an effect size corresponding to a ≥20% change in methylation. This effect size cutoff is commonly used in EWAS studies to prioritize CpGs with substantial methylation differences that are more likely to have functional relevance. Multicollinearity diagnostics and inference for fixed effects were performed using the lmerTest (v3.1.3) R package, which implements Satterthwaite’s method or Kenward-Roger’s approximations for denominator degrees of freedom92.

Cell-type proportion estimation

To quantify cell type proportions in the TME, we applied HiTIMED12, a reference-based cell-type deconvolution algorithm, to batch effect-corrected beta value matrices. HiTIMED deconvolutes DNA methylation profiles into three major TME components, tumor, angiogenic, and immune, with further resolution into specific cell types. The HiTIMED algorithm is built on tumor type-specific models for 20 carcinoma types, including breast cancer, and was developed using discovery data from 6726 samples encompassing carcinoma and matched normal or normal-adjacent tissues. We applied HiTIMED to all normal (n = 408) and tumor (n = 1001) breast tissue samples that passed DNA methylation quality control processing steps, from raw intensity data to normalized beta values, to resolve the third hierarchical layer of the algorithm for six specific TME cell types: tumor, endothelial, epithelial, stromal, myeloid, and lymphocyte. Although HiTIMED can resolve additional cell types, particularly immune cell subsets, to preserve statistical power in subsequent analyses, we restricted the scope of the cell types returned.

Cell type-specific differential methylation analysis

To identify differential DNA methylation attributable to specific cell types, we employed CellDMC13 (EpiDISH v2.20.1), a validated statistical algorithm that integrates cell-type fraction data to perform cell type-specific analyses. CellDMC analyzes DNA methylation patterns, modeling the interactions between phenotypes (e.g., tumor versus normal) and cell type proportions to uncover differential methylation patterns specific to individual cell lineages or cell types. Importantly, CellDMC, not EWAS, was used for all cell type-specific inference.

For each CpG, DNA methylation beta values were modeled as a function of phenotype (cancer vs. normal), estimated cell type proportions, and their interaction terms, adjusting for age. The interaction coefficient represents the cell type-specific differential methylation effect, conditional on cellular composition. All hypothesis tests were two-sided.

We used CellDMC to analyze the endothelial cell component of the TME to uncover differential DNA methylation patterns specific to tumor endothelial cells after adjusting for age. Differentially methylated CpGs (DMCs) were defined using a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of ≤0.05. To further refine biologically meaningful changes, we applied an additional filter on the basis of effect size, selecting CpGs with a minimum methylation shift of ≥5% between cancer and normal phenotypes. To estimate this 5% methylation shift, we multiplied the average endothelial cell proportion in the cancer samples by the CellDMC interaction coefficient. Specifically, we set the interaction coefficient threshold on the basis of the average endothelial cell proportion in cancer samples. For example, if the average endothelial fraction in cancer is ~8%, then an interaction coefficient of ≥0.0062 corresponds to an average methylation shift of 0.0062 × 8 = 0.05 (i.e., 5%) in cancer samples. The actual methylation shift varies by sample according to its endothelial cell proportion.

Endothelial-specific methylation effects

For visualization and interpretation, we adjusted the original beta values for CpGs identified as endothelial-specific by CellDMC13. This was done by multiplying the interaction coefficient for each CpG by the endothelial cell proportion for each sample and the phenotype indicator (0 for normal, 1 for cancer) and then adding this value to the original beta value. Since phenotype = 0 for normal samples, their beta values remained unchanged.

Handling structural zeros in cell type interaction models

To enable the modeling of phenotype–cell-type interactions in cancer versus normal comparisons using the CellDMC13 framework, it is essential that all modeled cell types have nonzero proportions across both phenotypic groups. CellDMC performs interaction modeling by incorporating both phenotype (e.g., cancer vs. normal) and cell-type fraction terms; therefore, a structural zero, such as a tumor cell proportion of exactly 0% in all normal samples, precludes estimation of valid interaction coefficients. This limitation arises from the model’s design, which assumes continuous variation in cell-type proportions across conditions. When a cell type is entirely absent from one group, the model cannot estimate its contribution to phenotype-specific methylation changes, leading to loss of power and biased results.

To address this, we re-estimated cell type proportions via HiTIMED12 by setting the tissue type to tumor for all samples, including histologically normal tissues. This setting enabled HiTIMED to apply its tumor-specific reference profile across all samples, generating low but nonzero tumor cell fractions in normal tissues. These inferred tumor-like signals may be biologically plausible and reflect early molecular alterations consistent with epigenetic field cancerization, in which adjacent or macroscopically normal tissues exhibit methylation changes associated with tumorigenesis. Alternatively, partial signal attribution may arise from technical noise, probe cross-reactivity, or shared epigenetic features between tumor and progenitor-like epithelial or stromal cells.

Statistical reporting and inference level

All statistical inference was conducted at the CpG level, with probe-wise models fit independently for each CpG. Effect sizes for differential methylation analyses are reported as absolute differences in mean methylation (Δβ) for bulk EWAS and as cell type-specific interaction regression coefficients for CellDMC analyses. For methylation–expression associations, effect size is reported as Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r). No subject-level or cohort-level hierarchical testing was performed.

Benchmarking cell type-specific vs. bulk DNA methylation analysis

To evaluate the added value of our cell type–aware approach, we compared results from standard bulk-level DNA methylation analysis with those obtained using the CellDMC13 interaction framework. Bulk analysis identified broad sets of differentially methylated CpGs (DMCs) but lacked specificity for endothelial cells, making it difficult to distinguish TEC-driven signals from those arising in other stromal or immune compartments. In contrast, our interaction framework isolated 4562 TEC-specific DMCs, many of which were not significant in bulk analysis due to signal dilution across cell types. Notably, loci such as RHOJ and RUNX1T1, genes with established roles in angiogenesis, were detectable only in the cell type-specific analysis, underscoring the power of this approach to uncover lineage-specific regulatory alterations that are otherwise obscured. This benchmarking highlights that our framework does not simply apply existing tools but enables mechanistic resolution of cell type-specific alterations from bulk biospecimens, a capability that is otherwise limited to costly single-cell methylation profiling.

Pathway enrichment analysis

Pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the missMethyl (v 1.24.0) R package, which is specifically designed for DNA methylation array data and implements an over-representation analysis accounting for probe number and multi-gene annotation and correcting for bias arising from unequal numbers of CpG probes per gene. TEC-specific differentially methylated CpGs were defined as those consistently identified in both the discovery and validation cohorts. The background universe was specified as all quality-controlled CpGs tested in the analysis, corresponding to the overlap of Illumina 450K and EPIC arrays after removal of cross-reactive and failed probes. To specifically interrogate endothelial angiogenic programs, GO terms related to angiogenesis and VEGF signaling were extracted and analyzed using gsameth to preserve the exact GO term definitions while limiting multiple testing to angiogenesis/VEGF-related pathways. Briefly, angiogenesis/VEGF-related GO BP terms were selected by keyword-based filtering of GO term names. For each selected GO term, the corresponding gene members were assembled into a custom gene set collection, and gene identifiers were converted to Entrez IDs for compatibility with gsameth. Enrichment for each GO term in the targeted collection was then tested using gsameth with the CpG background universe and TEC-specific CpG input set, applying a probe-number bias correction framework. Targeted results were summarized and ranked using both raw hypergeometric P values (P.DE) and false discovery rate adjusted (FDR) values computed over the restricted set of tested GO terms, and visualized using dot plots where the color scale reflected -log10(FDR).

Endothelial subtype marker gene enrichment analysis

To evaluate endothelial heterogeneity, we performed a targeted enrichment analysis using endothelial subtype marker genes derived from the single-cell endothelial taxonomy reported by Geldhof et al. (Nat Commun., 2022), as recommended by the reviewer. Tumor endothelial cell (TEC)–specific differentially methylated CpGs were defined as those consistently identified in both the discovery and validation cohorts. CpGs were annotated to genes using Illumina UCSC RefGene annotations. For each endothelial subtype gene set (angiogenic, arterial, and lymphatic), we tested whether TEC-specific CpGs were overrepresented among CpGs annotated to the corresponding marker genes relative to all quality-controlled CpGs tested in the analysis. Enrichment was assessed using Fisher’s exact tests, and odds ratios with exact 95% confidence intervals were computed. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Results are presented as odds ratios with confidence intervals in a forest plot, with corresponding numerical values reported in the Supplementary Data 1, Supplementary Table S7.

Single-cell ATAC-seq dataset and preprocessing

Breast cancer and healthy breast single-cell chromatin accessibility data were obtained from Sundaram et al. (Science 2024; DOI: 10.1126/science.adk9217). Fragment files, cell barcode annotations, and the peak set provided by the authors were downloaded from the associated public data repository. Single-cell ATAC-seq data were processed using Seurat (v5.4.0) and Signac (v1.16.0) in R. Fragment files were imported into Signac and peak-by-cell count matrices were generated using the published breast cancer/control peak set provided in the study. Standard quality control metrics were applied, including filtering based on fragment counts and transcription start site (TSS) enrichment scores.

Endothelial cells were identified using the published cell-type annotations and corresponding barcode identifiers provided with the dataset. Only endothelial cell barcodes from breast cancer (n = 311) and healthy breast samples[BC1.1] (n = 140) were retained for downstream analysis.

Chromatin accessibility counts were normalized and scaled within Seurat. Differential accessibility between cancer and healthy endothelial cells was computed using the logistic regression framework implemented in Seurat. Peaks were classified as significantly differentially accessible using a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of 0.05.

Liftover and gene annotation harmonization

To enable direct genomic comparison with the single-cell ATAC-seq dataset (hg38), TEC-specific CpG coordinates were converted from hg19 to hg38 using the UCSC liftOver tool implemented in the rtracklayer (v1.70.1) package (R/Bioconductor). The hg19ToHg38 chain file from UCSC Genome Browser was used for coordinate conversion. All TEC-specific CpGs successfully mapped from hg19 to hg38 using UCSC liftOver (1:1 mapping retained). No CpGs were excluded due to failed coordinate conversion. For gene-level overlap analysis, gene symbols annotated to TEC-specific CpGs were harmonized to match the gene naming convention used in the single-cell ATAC dataset. Multi-gene annotations were split and deduplicated prior to comparison. Not all CpG-associated gene symbols matched those present in the ATAC differential peak gene set. Specifically, 214 of 235 hypomethylated TEC-associated genes and 1682 of 1689 hypermethylated TEC-associated genes were retained after gene name matching and filtering. Genes not present in the ATAC gene annotation were excluded from gene-level concordance analyses.

Genomic overlap analysis

Differential ATAC peaks were represented as genomic ranges using the GenomicRanges (v1.62.1) package. TEC-specific CpGs were represented as single-base genomic ranges. Two overlap assessment approaches were used. Direct overlap where CpGs were considered overlapping if they fell within the genomic coordinates of a differential ATAC peak, and Window-based overlap which captured nearby regulatory context, extending ATAC peak coordinates by ±2 kb. CpGs falling within this extended window were considered proximal overlaps. Overlaps were computed using the findOverlaps function in GenomicRanges with strand ignored. For overlapping loci, the following attributes were retained: ATAC peak genomic coordinates, differential accessibility statistics (log2 fold change, adjusted p-value, direction), CpG identifier and genomic position, CpG methylation direction and endothelial-specific coefficients, CpG gene annotations and regulatory feature annotations. Gene-level concordance was assessed by intersecting gene symbols associated with significantly differential ATAC peaks and gene symbols annotated to TEC-specific CpGs. Multi-gene annotations were parsed and separated prior to generating unique gene sets.

Permutation analysis using the 450K gene set as the background universe was performed to evaluate the overlap. Random gene sets matched to the size of the TEC gene set were sampled 10,000 times and overlap with the ATAC gene set was recalculated at each iteration. The observed overlap and the expected overlap derived from the permutation analysis are summarized in the supplementary data.

Power analysis

Sample sizes exceeded those of prior methylation studies detecting DMCs at FDR < 0.05 with ≥5% effect size, ensuring sufficient statistical power for discovery and validation.

Functional annotation

Pathway membership for the VEGF signaling pathway was defined according to KEGG36 (KEGG ID: hsa04370; RRID:SCR_012773), the PANTHER38 database (RRID:SCR_004869), and the Harmonizome database37 (RRID:SCR_016176). Hallmark gene sets, including the hallmark angiogenesis collection, were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database93 (RRID:SCR_016863).

Hierarchical clustering and heatmap visualization

Unsupervised hierarchical clustering was performed separately for DNA methylation and gene expression datasets to account for differences in data structure and computational requirements.

For DNA methylation analyses, β values are bounded between 0 and 1 and highly correlated across CpG loci. Given the large number of CpGs included in methylation heatmaps, pairwise distances were calculated using Manhattan distance, which provides improved computational efficiency and stability for high-dimensional methylation data. Hierarchical clustering was performed using this distance metric.

For gene expression analyses, normalized expression values exhibit a wider dynamic range and continuous variance structure. Pairwise distances were therefore computed using Euclidean distance, followed by hierarchical clustering using the Ward.D2 linkage method, which minimizes within-cluster variance and is appropriate for Euclidean distance based clustering.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were conducted in R (v4.5.2). All analyses were performed using breast tissue samples that passed DNA methylation preprocessing and quality control procedures described above. Sample sizes correspond to independent biological specimens (normal n = 415; tumor n = 1079), with no technical replicates analyzed. Differential methylation analyses were performed using linear modeling frameworks, and all statistical tests were two-sided unless otherwise specified. Multiple hypothesis testing correction was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method.
