A novel “tea nurturing flowers” perspective: tea dhool moisture‑controlled preservation of Osmanthus vitality for superior scented tea aroma
Authors: Yani Pan, Lin Xiang, Li Yang, Xinxin Wang, Xinyu Feng, Jian Lu, Dong Wang, Jingjing Zhang, Ming Chen, Anran Yan, Haowei Guo, Ranran Zhao, Huiyuan Zhang, Jiancheng Yuan, Guohua Ren, Qiang Chu, Ping Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ability of flowers to sustain fragrance release during the scenting process dominates the aroma quality of scented tea, rather than the capacity of tea dhool to passively adsorb volatiles. Plucked flowers remain metabolically active, whose physiological status governs the persistence and intensity of fragrance emission. Traditional practice has focused on maximizing aroma uptake by a low‑moisture tea dhool, inadvertently accelerating floral dehydration and metabolic collapse, thereby prematurely terminating peak fragrance release. This study introduces a novel “tea nurturing flowers” perspective, investigating how tea dhool moisture content modulates Osmanthus physiology and shapes the aroma quality of Osmanthus black tea (OBT). An optimized moisture level (25%) preserved cellular integrity, delayed water loss, and maintained stomatal opening, collectively sustaining floral volatile emission. By contrast, insufficient moisture (5–10%) caused plasmolysis and stomatal closure, while over‑hydration (45%) induced cell rupture and blockage of the porous tea dhool, impairing volatile transfer. Consequently, OBT produced at 25% moisture exhibited a more intense and persistent floral aroma, with elevated levels of key volatiles such as β‑ionone. Overall, this study repositions tea dhool from a merely passive adsorbent to a moisture‑mediated regulator of flower vitality, providing a mechanistic foundation and practical guidelines for improving scented tea quality.

Introduction

The burgeoning new-style tea beverage market directly reflects a consumer demand for complex aroma and flavor. Specifically, compared to traditional teas, scented tea like Osmanthus Black Tea (OBT)1 and Osmanthus Longjing tea2, which combine the dual aroma characteristics of full-bodied tea aroma and rich floral fragrance, are highly favored by consumers3. As a reprocessed tea characterized by the scenting process with natural flowers, the key to its aroma quality formation lies in the blending of tea and flowers, which synergistically integrates tea and floral scents through the principle of floral aroma release and tea absorption. Recent scholarly research on scented tea has primarily focused on factors like raw materials4,5, and scenting processes (scenting number, ratio, time)6–8 affecting aroma quality, with particular emphasis on investigating the adsorption mechanisms of aromatic compounds within tea pore structures. However, there has been relatively little attention paid to a crucial biological characteristic during the scenting process: detached flowers are living systems with intact metabolic activity, and the ability to release fragrance directly depends on physiological vitality. For example, Osmanthus essential oil extracted when the flowers exhibit peak physiological activity yields the highest aroma concentration9,10, with the richest content of total flavonoids and aromatic compounds. Notably, detachment signifies that flowers are removed from natural nutrient supply and stable growing environment, at which point subtle changes in external conditions (moisture, temperature, light, etc.), especially critical humidity and water dynamics, decisively influence factors such as cellular turgor and wilting rate11.

Moisture in tea typically exists in three forms: chemically bound water, physically bound water, and free water12. In humidified high-moisture tea dhool, free water is the dominant form, which can adsorb polar volatile compounds released by flowers (e.g., linalool, methyl salicylate, and benzaldehyde) through hydrogen bonding. High moisture content not only boosts soluble compound content (thus enhancing overall adsorption capacity) but also supports maintaining detached flower vitality and promoting sustained aromatic substance release. As a key regulatory factor during the scenting process, tea dhool moisture content directly participates in regulating the dynamic water balance of flowers by influencing microenvironmental humidity. Low-moisture tea dhool (<10%) prioritizes water absorption from fresh flowers during scenting, causing the flowers to lose vitality rapidly and fail to release aroma continuously. Ye et al.13 analyzed water migration and aroma component change patterns during scenting and found that different moisture states influence the formation of fresh, floral-scented tea aroma qualities. Meng et al.14 demonstrated through experiments that OBT prepared with tea dhool at 30% moisture content had the highest levels of characteristic aroma compounds (e.g., β-ionone and dihydro-β-ionone). While most of these studies focus on how moisture content affects scented tea overall quality15, or infer adsorption mechanisms based on changes in moisture content and aroma components16, few have reported on the effects of moisture on the morphology, microstructure, and physiological state of flowers. The specific mechanism by which tea dhool moisture content regulates the physiological state of Osmanthus - i.e., the intrinsic link of the “tea nurturing flowers” effect-remains unclear.

This study used Jingshan black tea with different moisture contents (5%, 15%, 25%, 35%, 45%) as raw material to produce OBT. Sensory evaluation, physicochemical components, and volatile compounds were analyzed to investigate how tea dhool moisture variations influence OBT quality. Solid-phase microextraction-gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (SPME-GC-MS), orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), and odor activity value (OVA) were used to analyze the volatile compounds. Additionally, a depth of field microscope (SDM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were employed to clarify the regulatory role of tea dhool moisture content in morphological and microstructural changes within Osmanthus flowers. This study investigates the impact of tea dhool moisture content on scented tea quality from the flower perspective during the scenting process, aiming to reveal the potential role of the “tea nurturing flowers” effect and provide theoretical references for optimizing scented tea scenting process parameters and enhancing scented tea quality.

Results

The flavor quality of OBT prepared with different tea dhool moisture contents

The effect of different tea dhool moisture contents (5%, 15%, 25%, 35%, 45%) on OBT quality was investigated. The OBT samples production process was shown in Fig. 1a. Tea infusion sensory evaluation results indicated that the fragrance addition enhanced the sweetness and freshness of the black tea (BT), and the treatment groups with 25% and 35% tea dhool moisture content showed the most significant improvement (Fig. 1b). However, OBT produced from 45% tea dhool moisture content exhibited a ripe flavor. Compared to BT, OBT exhibited significant differences in tea infusion color after Osmanthus scenting (Fig. 1c), and BT had the highest lightness (L*) value, the lowest red-green (a*) value, and the lowest yellow-blue (b*) value, indicating that the tea infusion color was darker and redder after scenting. The soluble sugar is the main sweet substance in the tea infusion, which can reduce the bitter taste of caffeine and tea polyphenols. The higher the content of soluble sugar, the higher the sweetness and fullness of the tea infusion17. Catechin compounds are a type of compound in tea that possess dual flavor properties (bitterness and astringency). Based on the differences in chemical structure, they can be classified into ester-type catechins (EGCG, ECG, GCG, CG) and non-ester-type catechins (EC, EGC, C, GC). The astringency and bitterness thresholds of ester-type catechins are lower than those of non-ester-type catechins, and they contribute more to the bitterness of the tea infusion18. The total catechin content and the ratio of non-ester-type catechins to ester-type catechins in BT were significantly lower than those in OBT after the addition of flowers (Fig. 1e), and the theophylline content in BT was higher than that in OBT (Fig. 1f). Osmanthus scenting results in an overall increase in tea internal substances, in which the non-ester catechin content rose more significantly than the ester catechin content, and this change further increases the ratio of non-ester to ester catechins and thereby reduces tea infusion bitterness and astringency.

When the tea dhool moisture content was 25% and 35%, the OBT had a relatively rich and long-lasting Osmanthus aroma, and the aroma was the most abundant (Fig. 2a). Alcohols are the main volatile substances in black tea and generally account for the highest proportion of aromatic substances19. Ketones give tea its floral aroma, while aldehydes are mainly responsible for citrus and green flavors. Ester compounds are mainly associated with tea fermentation and fatty acid metabolism, and they give Osmanthus tea its fruity aroma20. Terpenoids are important substances for the formation of floral and fruity aroma in Osmanthus tea21. Heterocyclic compounds, including pyrazine, pyrrole, furan, and furfural, are mainly produced during the Maillard reaction in many heat-processed teas and are responsible for the roasted and caramel flavors in tea22. As shown in Fig. 2b, BT volatile compounds were primarily alcohols (60.01%) and aldehydes (19.33%), while OBT ones were primarily alcohols (41.22% ~ 51.27%), aldehydes (18.36% ~ 23.86%), terpenes (14.22% ~ 15.43%), and ketones (7.92% ~ 13.12%). This indicated that the relative content of alcohols decreases significantly during the scenting process of OBT, while ketones and esters showed a noticeable upward trend, which was consistent with previous studies6,23. Ketones are the main aroma components of Osmanthus24, and the ketone content increased by 4–6 times after scenting. The content of BT heterocyclic compounds was highest in the six samples, which might be due to their characteristic of having a baking aroma.

Identification of key aroma compounds

To further investigate the differences in aroma components between OBT made from tea dhool with different moisture contents, an OPLS-DA model was used to analyze the key volatile substances in six groups of samples, as shown in Fig. 2c. Each sample was grouped and distinguished based on the similarity of its volatile components, and the distance between samples reflected the differences in their volatile components. Points with a shorter distance indicate more similar volatile components, while points distributed in different quadrants show greater differences. Six sample groups showed a clear separation trend, and the HCA also indicated that the three replicate samples within each group had strong clustering properties (Fig. 2d). The reliability of the OPLS-DA model was verified through 200 permutation tests for model validation (Fig. 2e). The intersection point of the Q2 regression line with the vertical axis was below zero (R2 = 0.624, Q2 = −1.34), indicating that the model did not overfit. Through OPLS-DA, 14 key differential compounds with Variable Importance for the Projection (VIP) > 1 were selected (Fig. S2). Different color intensities represented standardized values for the relative content of volatile substances, with red indicating high content and blue indicating low content. Key differential compounds in OBT from tea dhool with different moisture contents showed significant differences (Fig. 2f). β-ionone, 2-hexenal, trans-linalool oxide (furanoid), hexanal, γ-decalactone, cis-linalool oxide, dihydro-β-ionone, α-ionone, and cis-linalool 3,7-oxide were absent or present in relatively low concentrations in BT, but were present in relatively high concentrations in OBT, especially in the tea dhool group with 25% moisture content, indicating that they mainly originate from Osmanthus. Conversely, pentanal, geraniol, linalool, methyl salicylate, and butanal exhibited higher relative concentrations in BT and lower relative concentrations in OBT, indicating that they primarily originate from the tea.

There are many types of volatile compounds in tea, but not all of them affect the overall aroma of the tea. The aroma threshold is an indicator of the aroma intensity of a volatile compound, and only volatile compounds with a relative odor activity value (OAV) > 1 have a certain effect on the aroma of tea. Volatile compounds with OAV > 1 were considered the primary aroma-contributing compounds in tea, while those with OAV > 10 were regarded as the key aroma compounds25. These aroma compounds may exhibit different fragrance profiles at different concentrations, resulting in the same aromatic compound having different fragrance profiles in different teas26. The results of the OAV analysis (Fig. 3a–d) showed that linalool, geraniol, β-ionone, hexanal, γ-decalactone, and dihydro-β-ionone were the key aroma components of the OBT (OAV > 10). Among them, linalool and geraniol were the primary alcohol components in OBT, exhibiting floral aromas and low odor threshold, mainly derived from the tea27. β-ionone (woody, fruity, floral), hexanal (green), γ-decalactone (milky), and dihydro-β-ionone (fruity) were mainly derived from Osmanthus. Microstructure of Osmanthus

Further mechanism analysis indicated that the good physiological state of Osmanthus in the tea dhool with 25% moisture content was the main reason for the abundance of key aroma components. There were significant differences in the rate and amount of water loss of Osmanthus during the scenting process for tea dhool with different moisture contents. As scenting time increased, Osmanthus moisture content decreased in all groups, and the lower the tea dhool moisture content, the greater the Osmanthus water loss rate and amount. After 12 h, the moisture content of the Osmanthus flowers in the 5% and 15% tea dhool groups decreased to 22.71% and 28.52%, respectively, while the moisture content of the flowers in the 25%, 35%, and 45% tea dhool groups decreased more gradually and remained at around 50%. Based on the tea dhool moisture content measured at the previous time point, the relative change rate of tea dhool moisture content was presented. As shown in Fig. 4a, the moisture content of the tea dhool increased most rapidly within the initial 4 h of scenting, exhibiting the greatest relative change. Subsequently, the rate of moisture absorption decelerated between 4 and 8 h noticeably and tended to stabilize, reaching a plateau between 8 and 12 h. During the scenting process, the tea dhool moisture content was relatively low, and there was a large difference in moisture content between the tea dhool and the Osmanthus (84.43%), causing the Osmanthus to lose moisture quickly. Rapid water loss caused osmosis in Osmanthus cells, resulting in plasmolysis, contraction of the protoplast, loss of support for the cell wall, and subsequent wrinkling of the entire petal. Externally, this was mainly manifested by wrinkling of the petal edges, the gradual appearance of brown spots, and the flower stems beginning to shrink. The aroma of Osmanthus is strongest when the flowers are in full bloom, after which it gradually fades until it disappears. The morphological changes of Osmanthus in tea dhool with different moisture content were shown in Fig. 4b. At 4 h, petals in all groups remained relatively fully expanded with bright coloration, while those in the tea dhool with 5% and 15% moisture content already exhibited slight wrinkling and localized browning. After 8 h, the tea dhool with 5% and 15% moisture content showed aggravated shrinkage with curled and deformed petals, accompanied by significantly reduced floral area in the 5% group (Fig. 4c). By 12 h, petals in the 5% and 15% groups were almost completely shriveled with scorched appearance, whereas the 25%, 35%, and 45% groups still retained a certain degree of expansion, with slightly darker colors but relatively intact shapes. Quantitative analysis of Osmanthus area (Fig. 4c) and petal shrinkage percentage (Fig. 4d) revealed significant differences in flower area among different tea dhool moisture content groups after 12 h of scenting. The flower area in the 5% and 15% groups decreased sharply, while that in the 35% and 45% groups was reduced due to partial petal softening caused by excessive moisture, and the 25% group showed higher flower area stability than the other groups. These results indicated that the tea dhool moisture content directly affects the freshness preservation of Osmanthus morphology, and the tea dhool with 25% moisture content was conducive to maintaining the petal expansion state and delaying Osmanthus wilting.

Different tea dhool moisture contents cause Osmanthus morphological structure differences, which were further analyzed from the perspective of microstructure (Fig. 4e). The surface of Osmanthus petals after 8 h scenting was observed using SDM, with Osmanthus before scenting (H0) as the control. Osmanthus petals are composed of epidermis, basic tissue, and vascular bundles, which are loosely arranged with large intercellular cavities for water storage. Vascular bundles help maintain petal morphological and structural stability, providing certain support for petals and enabling them to remain expanded. In the 5% and 15% groups, the petals showed wilting on the surface, the cross-section of the tissue was reduced, the inward depressions of the epidermis further increased in number and degree, the cells were loosely arranged, and the vascular bundles were scattered with reduced diameter. In the 25%, 35%, and 45% groups, the petal cells were closely arranged with uniform intercellular spaces, and the vascular bundle structure was intact.

The surface morphology of Osmanthus petals in different tea dhool moisture content groups was observed by SEM after 8 h scenting (Fig. 5a). The petals in the H0 group exhibited clear and smooth surface textures. In the 5% and 15% groups, the epidermal cells of petals showed extensive and severe collapse, with dense surface folds characterized by deep and wide grooves, presenting a severely shrunken state. These two groups had the highest proportion of folded surface area, which was significantly higher than that of other groups (Fig. 5c). The 25% group displayed relatively regular surface textures with fewer and shallower folds, while the 35% and 45% groups were similar to it and showed good surface smoothness. Further visualization of the petal fold three-dimensional (3D) characteristics was conducted via 3D depth simulation (Fig. 5b). The H0 group had the smallest height difference, indicating a flat surface. The 5% and 15% groups showed the largest fold amplitude and obvious height differences, which were consistent with the severe shrinkage observed in Fig. 5a. With the increase in tea dhool moisture content, the height difference gradually decreased, and the petal surface tended to be flatter. The quantification of stomatal aperture (areas) (Fig. 5d) and SEM (Fig. 5e) showed that the stomata in the 5% and 15% groups were in a semi-closed state, while the 25%, 35%, and 45% groups slowed down the closing state of the pores. These results indicated that an appropriate tea dhool moisture content was conducive to maintaining the physiological activity of Osmanthus and promoting its gas exchange (Fig. 5f). Discussion

This study demonstrates that the initial moisture content of tea dhool critically regulates the “tea nurturing flowers” effect and the final quality of OBT. A moderate moisture content of 25% was identified as optimal, resulting in OBT with superior sensory attributes and the highest accumulation of key Osmanthus-derived aroma compounds. The findings reveal that the tea dhool acts not merely as a passive aroma absorbent, but actively modulates the physiological state of the fresh flowers through water migration. Specifically, the 25% moisture level maintains a balanced dehydration rate, preserving petal cellular integrity, preventing severe plasmolysis, and sustaining high stomatal opening rates. This preservation of both structural integrity and physiological vitality facilitates the continuous synthesis and release of volatile compounds from the petals. Conversely, extremely low moisture (5%) induces rapid petal desiccation and stomatal closure, suppressing aroma metabolism, while excessively high moisture (45%) leads to cell rupture and the blockage of the tea's porous absorption sites.

Stomata serve as crucial channels for gas and water exchange between plants and the external environment, and plants regulate physiological metabolism by adjusting stomatal opening and closing to control gas exchange and water loss28. Under water stress, the closure of stomata can help retain water, but it is achieved at the expense of photosynthesis and the release of aroma29. In the 45% group, although the degree of petal surface shrinkage was relatively low and the stomata also appeared open, this open state might not be the result of normal physiological regulation. The 45% moisture of the tea dhool significantly reduces the moisture gradient between the tea and the flowers. This diminished gradient severely restricts the natural transpiration of the petals, leading to a highly humid microclimate that impedes proper gas exchange. In postharvest floral physiology, such restricted transpiration combined with vigorous floral respiration may contribute to hypoxia-like stress30. This physiological stress accelerates passive cellular senescence and structural deterioration, which causes the stomata to lose their regulatory ability31. Consequently, the fresh flowers fail to sustain the active secondary metabolism required for continuous aroma biosynthesis. This loss of physiological regulation, combined with the fact that excessively high moisture content of tea dhool can block the loose and porous internal structure of tea, thereby disrupting the binding between volatile substances and resulting in reduced aroma absorption14.

Yang et al. indicated that the aroma absorption capacity and aroma retention effect of tea dhool are related to its moisture content32. When the tea dhool moisture content ranges from 5% to 25%, the aroma absorption effect of tea dhool increases with the increase in moisture content. Therefore, 25% tea dhool moisture content is beneficial for maintaining the vitality of fresh Osmanthus flowers and enhancing their aromatic compounds release, which is consistent with the findings of other studies. At this optimal moisture level of 25%, the controlled water migration from the petals to the tea matrix creates a critical state of mild dehydration stress. Unlike the rapid desiccation observed in the 5% group, the 25% tea dhool acts as a humidity buffer, preventing severe osmotic shock while maintaining the petals in a physiologically active state. In the field of plant physiology, such mild moisture stress is known to function as an abiotic elicitor, upregulating the expression of genes related to secondary metabolism and promoting the biosynthesis of volatile terpenes33. Furthermore, from the perspective of volatile emission dynamics, maintaining this specific moisture equilibrium is crucial for coordinating both aroma synthesis and release. Recent molecular studies on Osmanthus fragrans have confirmed that the biosynthesis of key floral volatiles (e.g., linalool) is tightly regulated by specific transcription factors that respond to physiological signals, suggesting that sustained vitality is essential for maximizing the expression of aroma synthase genes34. Simultaneously, the physical release of these hydrophobic volatiles is coupled with stomatal aperture35. By sustaining prolonged stomatal opening while preserving the physiological vitality required for gene expression, the 25% group ensures an efficient pathway for both volatile biosynthesis and emission, thereby maximizing the “tea nurturing flowers” effect.

This study primarily focused on moisture dynamics, flower vitality, and aroma formation during the scenting process. It should also be noted that water activity, which reflects the availability and mobility of water in the system, was not included in the current evaluation. As a key indicator associated with moisture status, water activity could provide additional insight into water-mediated physicochemical changes during scenting. In addition, moisture conditions could influence microbial metabolism, which may further affect aroma generation and transformation. In future studies, water activity monitoring and microbiological analyses should be incorporated into the experimental framework to enable a more comprehensive understanding of the “tea nurturing flowers” mechanism in Osmanthus black tea scenting.

In summary, through comprehensive chemical, sensory, and microstructural analyses, this study provides evidence for the regulatory role of tea dhool moisture content in the “tea nurturing flowers” effect, and offers a theoretical basis and technical reference for optimizing scented tea scenting parameters.

Methods

Tea samples

The OBT samples production process was shown in Fig. 1a. The Jingshan black tea (BT) was sprayed with distilled water to obtain tea with moisture contents of 5%, 15%, 25%, 35%, and 45%. The initial moisture content of Jingshan black tea is 5%. The moisture content of the tea was measured using a moisture analyzer (Sartorius, MA35M-000230V1, Goettingen, Germany). Fresh Osmanthus flowers were collected by spreading a clean plastic sheet under the Osmanthus tree and gently tapping the branches with a long pole to allow mature flowers to fall naturally, followed by the removal of impurities. The tea was then scented with fresh Osmanthus flowers for 12 h under the same conditions (10:2 mass ratio of tea to flowers). After scenting, the Osmanthus flowers and tea were separated, and then the tea was dried until its moisture content reached 8%. Samples were collected every 4 h. After collection, the samples were sealed and stored at −20 °C.

Chemicals

Gallic acid, non-ester catechins, ester catechins, and alkaloid standards (purity ≥98%) were supplied by Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The non-ester catechin group comprised catechin (C), epicatechin (EC), gallocatechin (GC), and epigallocatechin (EGC), while the ester catechin group included catechin gallate (CG), gallocatechin gallate (GCG), epicatechin gallate (ECG), and epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG). Alkaloids tested in this study were caffeine, theobromine, and theophylline. Chromatography-grade reagents, such as acetonitrile, acetic acid, methanol, and formic acid, were acquired from Aladdin (Shanghai) Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). High-purity helium (≥99.999%) was sourced from Hangzhou Jingong Material Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). Ultrapure water required for experiments was generated using a Synergy UV water purification system (Millipore SAS, France), and drinking water used in sensory evaluation was provided by Nongfu Spring Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China).

Analysis of tea infusion color

The color difference of the tea infusions was detected using a chromameter CM-3600d (Konica Minolta, Tokyo, Japan). The parameters L*, a*, and b* were measured. L* represents brightness, a* represents red and green, and b* represents yellow and blue.

Sensory evaluation of OBT

A sensory panel consisting of six experienced sensory evaluation experts evaluated the appearance, soup color, aroma, taste, and leaf bottom of the tea. Tea infusions were prepared according to the Methodology for Sensory Evaluation of Tea (China National Standard, GB/T 23776-2018). Weigh out 3 g of tea samples (BT or OBT), and add 150 mL of boiling water. Steep for 3 min for the first infusion and 5 min for the second infusion.

This study adheres to the guidelines outlined in The Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki) for research involving human participants. National laws do not require ethical clearance for sensory evaluation studies. There is no dedicated human ethics committee or standardized documentation process specifically for the implementation of sensory evaluations. Proper protocols were implemented to protect the rights and privacy of all participants. These protocols include: participation voluntarily (no mandatory involvement); full disclosure of the study’s purposes and possible risks; securing verbal consent from each participant; preventing the disclosure of participant data without their prior awareness and consent; and permitting participants to withdraw from the study at any time.

Non-volatile components analysis

Tea samples (0.15 g) were extracted with 25 mL of 50% (v/v) ethanol at 70 °C in a water bath for 30 min, then cooled to room temperature. It was centrifuged at 9500 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was collected for further analysis. The sample was filtered through a 0.45 µm filter. Separation was carried out using an Agilent TC-C18 chromatographic column (California, USA, 4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 µm), with an injection volume of 10 µL. Mobile phase A consisted of water/acetonitrile/acetic acid (96.5:3.0:0.5, v/v/v), and mobile phase B consisted of water/acetonitrile/acetic acid (69.5:30.0:0.5, v/v/v). Detection was conducted at a flow rate of 1 mL/min for 50 min, with the column temperature maintained at 30 °C. The UV detection wavelength was 280 nm. The soluble sugar content was determined using the anthrone-sulfuric acid colorimetric method36.

Analysis of volatile compounds by SPME-GC-MS

The tea powder (1 g) was dissolved in 50 mL of boiling deionized water, then extracted for 10 min in a water bath at 70 °C. After cooling, it was centrifuged at 4000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. The supernatant (10 mL) was transferred to a 20 mL glass vial, with dry NaCl (2 g) and ethyl decanoate (internal standard, 10 µL, 10 mg/L) added sequentially, and the vial was immediately sealed.

The vial was equilibrated and extracted in a 40 °C water bath for 60 min using a 50/30 µm DVB/CAR/PDMS Stableflex fiber (Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA). These conditions were optimized based on preliminary experiments to ensure adsorption saturation. Subsequently, the SPME fiber was inserted into the GC-MS injection port for desorption at 250 °C for 3.5 min in splitless mode. Chromatographic separation was performed using an SH-Rxi-5Sil MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm) with high-purity helium (>99.999%) as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The GC oven temperature program was set as follows: initially held at 40 °C for 3.5 min, increased to 70 °C at a rate of 4 °C/min and held for 1 min, then raised to 140 °C at 2 °C/min and held for 1 min, and finally ramped to 250 °C at 15 °C/min and held for 3 min. Mass spectrometry was conducted in electron impact (EI) mode at 70 eV. The ion source and interface temperatures were maintained at 230 °C and 250 °C, respectively. Data acquisition was performed in full scan mode covering a mass range of 20–500 amu, with a scan speed of 1250 amu/s and a scan interval of 0.3 s. All volatile compounds were compared with the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) mass spectrometry database, using a similarity threshold of ≥75. Then, the volatile compounds were identified by the published retention index (RI). The RI of each volatile compound was calculated using a homologous series of alkanes ranging from C5 to C33. The C5–C8 standards were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), and the C9–C33 standards were obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). To ensure experimental consistency, the alkane standard mixture was pipetted into a sealed headspace vial. The standards were then subjected to headspace solid-phase microextraction at 40 °C for 1 h. Following extraction, the fiber was inserted into the GC injection port for desorption and analysis under the same chromatographic conditions as the tea samples. Based on the concentration of the standard and the percentage of peak area, the relative content of aroma compounds was calculated37.

The OAV for each volatile compound was calculated using the equation: OAV = C/OT, where C represents the concentration of the volatile compound in the sample, and OT represents the odor threshold of the compound in water. Compounds with an OAV ≥ 1 were considered to contribute significantly to the overall aroma profile. The aroma characteristics were identified based on the Perflavory (https://www.perflavory.com/) and Flavornet (https://www.flavornet.org/flavornet.html) databases.

Microstructure observation

The Osmanthus samples at 8 h of scenting represented the mid-stage of dehydration, where morphological variations among groups were most pronounced. Consequently, these samples were subjected to vacuum freeze-drying and then observed under a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Zeiss Gemini 560, Carl Zeiss, Germany).

Statistical analysis

All the experiments were repeated three times. Histograms, violin plots, stacked bar, and line graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism 10.0. Radar plots were created using Origin 2019, while heatmaps were constructed with TBtools. Multivariate analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA), OPLS-DA, and hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA), were conducted in SIMCA 14.1. Image quantification was performed in ImageJ. One-way ANOVA was executed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.
