Analysis of Langevin midpoint methods using an anticipative Girsanov theorem
Matthew S. Zhang

Abstract
We introduce a new method for analyzing midpoint discretizations of stochastic differential equations (SDEs), which are frequently used in Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods for sampling from a target measure π ∝ exp ⁡ ( − V ) \pi\propto\exp(-V) . Borrowing techniques from Malliavin calculus, we compute estimates for the Radon–Nikodym derivative for processes on L 2 ​ ( [ 0 , T ) , ℝ d ) L^{2}([0,T);\mathbb{R}^{d}) which may anticipate the Brownian motion, in the sense that they may not be adapted to the filtration at the same time. Applying these to various popular midpoint discretizations, we are able to improve the regularity and cross-regularity results in the literature on sampling methods. We also obtain a query complexity bound of O ~ ​ ( κ 5 / 4 ​ d 1 / 4 ε 1 / 2 ) \widetilde{O}(\frac{\kappa^{5/4}d^{1/4}}{\varepsilon^{1/2}}) for obtaining a ε 2 \varepsilon^{2} -accurate sample in 𝖪𝖫 \mathsf{KL} divergence, under log-concavity and strong smoothness assumptions for ∇ 2 V \nabla^{2}V .



1
Introduction

At their core, many algorithms for sampling over distributions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
boil down to approximating Itô SDEs:

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=b(t,X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(t,X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}\,,

where
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a Brownian process, and
b:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\sigma:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are suitably regular coefficients.

A model example arises with the Langevin diffusion
[
Smo18
]
, given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-\nabla V(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}\,,

(LD)

for a continuously differentiable
V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. (
LD
) is the canonical algorithm for sampling from
\pi
.
A generalization
[
Lan+08
]
of this diffusion, which incorporates a momentum component, is given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}X_{t}&=P_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t\,,\\
\mathrm{d}P_{t}&=\bigl\{-\gamma P_{t}-\nabla V(X_{t})\bigr\}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\gamma}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}\,,\end{aligned}

(ULD)

where
\gamma>0
is a friction coefficient. Under mild conditions, this converges to
\boldsymbol{\pi}(x,p)\propto\exp(-V(x)-\frac{\lVert p\rVert^{2}}{2})
.

In this work, we consider the simulation of such equations. Generally, one hopes to quantify the number of queries made by the algorithm to
\nabla V
or
V
that are needed to guarantee that the
\mathsf{KL}(\operatorname{law}(X_{T})\mathbin{\|}\pi)\leq\varepsilon^{2}
, with particular focus on asymptotic dependence on quantities as the ambient dimension
d
or the desired error
\varepsilon
. Exact simulation of the equation is generally impossible due to the non-linearity
\nabla V
in the dynamics. Instead, one adopts methods for approximating the dynamics on small enough timescales. The simplest such method is the Euler–Maruyama discretization, which fixes a finite set of interpolant times
0\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq\ldots\leq t_{N}
. On each of these intervals, we replace the nonlinearity
\nabla V(X_{t})
by
\nabla V(X_{t_{k}})
, which then gives a sequence of linear SDEs whose laws are easily simulated.
Error estimates for such schemes have been extensively studied in both the SDE and sampling literature; within sampling, the monograph
[
Che24
]
is an excellent reference.

We can expect that a more refined scheme may outperform this simple interpolation. By analogy, to simulate an ordinary differential equation, the gradient estimator
\nabla V(X_{t_{k}})
may be replaced with an expression which involves midpoints such as
\frac{1}{2}(X_{t_{k}}+X_{t_{k+1/2}})
, or other more complex schemes, which gives rise to a rich family of midpoint or Runge–Kutta schemes.

In this spirit, the work of
[
SL19
]
introduced the randomized midpoint scheme for simulating (
ULD
), which introduced an additional random interpolant timestep in order to debias the gradient estimate. This was extended to (
LD
) in
[
HBE20
,
YKD23
]
. To be precise, let us just look at this scheme on a single discretized step
[0,h]
, with
h>0
a step-size. Fix some interpolant time
\tau
, and write

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} X^{+}&=X_{0}-\tau\nabla V(X_{0})+\sqrt{2}B_{\tau}\\
X_{h}&=X_{0}-h\nabla V(X^{+})+\sqrt{2}B_{h}\,.\end{aligned}

(1.1)

After repeating this process
N
times,
X_{Nh}
is taken to be the approximate sample from
\pi
.
As shown in
[
SL19
]
, this scheme enjoys better error guarantees compared to the standard discretization, although the analysis in it and subsequent works
[
HBE20
]
are conducted solely in Wasserstein distance using coupling techniques. See also the works
[
KN24
,
SN25
]
.
Building on these approaches, the separate line of work
[
AC24a
,
ACZ25
]
proposed a method which was able to strengthen these guarantees to bounds on the Kullback–Leibler (
\mathsf{KL}
) divergence between the terminal marginal
\operatorname{law}(X_{T})
and
\pi
. While these works have led to significant improvements in our understanding of these methods, they are unable to obtain process-level bounds between (
LD
) and (
1.1
) directly.

We resolve this question in the current work. We highlight an existing framework for Girsanov analysis of non-adapted processes. In general, consider some possibly anticipating transformation of the Brownian motion,
\mathsf{T}:C_{0}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})\to C_{0}([0,T);\mathbb{R}^{d})
(where
C_{0}
are the set of continuous paths
\omega:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\omega_{0}=0
),

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{t}(\omega)=\omega_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\bar{\mathsf{u}}_{s}(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}s\,,

(1.2)

for a process
(\bar{\mathsf{u}}_{s})_{s\in[0,T]}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which may anticipate the Brownian motion, in the sense that
\bar{\mathsf{u}}_{s}
is not necessarily a measurable random variable with respect to the filtration
\mathscr{F}_{s}
at time
s
, and
\omega=(\omega_{t})_{t\in[0,T)}\in C_{0}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
corresponds to a particular realization of the Brownian motion trajectory. Given a measure
\mathbf{P}
over
\Omega\equiv C_{0}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
, one then produces
\mathbf{Q}=\mathsf{T}_{\#}\mathbf{P}
. It is reasonable to ask if
\mathbf{Q}\ll\mathbf{P}
, or to inquire about the magnitude of
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Q}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{P}}
.

Although such processes
(\mathsf{T}_{t}(\omega))_{t\in[0,T)}
lose many of the beneficial features of the Itô calculus, it is indeed still possible
[
Nua95
]
to quantify the Radon–Nikodym derivative between
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}
under sufficient regularity conditions of the transform
\mathsf{T}
, using techniques from Malliavin calculus. By closely investigating these results, it is possible to leverage them to obtain guarantees for such processes in information theoretic divergences such as
\mathsf{KL}
or Rényi.

1.1
Related work

Sampling and midpoint methods.

The theory of sampling methods has seen extensive development in recent years. The continuous-time dynamics in (
LD
) and (
ULD
) have an elegant interpretation as gradient flows of
\mathsf{KL}(\cdot\mathbin{\|}\pi)

[
JKO98
,
Vil+08
,
AGS08
]
. More relevant to us is the history of SDE discretizations, in particular “unadjusted” schemes which do not add a Metropolis–Hastings filter
[
Has70
]
. The Euler–Maruyama discretization was first introduced for the simulation of SDEs in
[
Mar55
]
. Numerous refinements of this method have since been analyzed in the literature, which would be impossible to fully survey. See
[
KPS12
]
for an introduction.

Analysis within the sampling literature is concerned with guarantees for the law of the terminal iterate
X_{T}
, typically in a metric such as total variation or Wasserstein distance. In this context the Euler–Maruyama discretization of (
LD
) was first analyzed in
[
DT12
]
, which showed the first non-asymptotic guarantees for this algorithm in
W_{2}^{2}
distance. Numerous refinements have since been made, holding in “metrics” as strong as the
q
-Rényi divergence
[
Dal17
,
DMM19
,
Che+24
]
.

The randomized midpoint scheme was first introduced by
[
SL19
]
, and its analysis was refined by
[
HBE20
,
CLW20
,
YKD23
]
. Other Runge–Kutta type schemes have also been considered in the literature
[
Li+19
,
WCW24
]
. We comment on
[
SZ21
,
PW24
]
, which introduces a similar midpoint method as (
DM–ULMC
) (defined in §
4.2
) but uses only a singular midpoint. All of the aforementioned works only obtain guarantees in
W_{2}^{2}
, and do not provide techniques for obtaining guarantees in
\mathsf{KL}
or
\mathsf{R}_{q}
, as the resultant process will be non-adapted. While the analyses of
[
SL19
,
SZ21
]
were sufficient to achieve better complexity bounds for single midpoint discretizations of (
ULD
) in the
W_{2}^{2}
metric, it is not possible at the moment to show similar guarantees in
\mathsf{KL}
with only a single midpoint. Instead, we generalize their analysis to a scheme with two midpoints, which recovers their rates.
Finally, although not considered in this work, we also mention the existence of implicit discretizations of Langevin diffusions, which can also be seen as anticipating processes
[
Wib19
,
SKR19
,
HSR21
]
.

We also mention that there exists an army of methods for discretizing (randomized) Hamiltonian Monte Carlo dynamics
[
Mon21
,
BE23
,
BS23
,
BK25
]
. These methods also provide guarantees in terms of closeness of the final iterate to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, but cannot be seen as discretizations of (
ULD
) in the same way as the methods considered in this work.

Shifted composition.

The parallel line of work
[
AC24
,
AC23
,
AC24a
,
ACZ25
]
proposes a different method for analyzing midpoint schemes, known as the
shifted composition
rule. Unlike the present work, the shifted composition method is able to obtain sharper marginal guarantees by differentiating between the
weak
and
strong
errors of the algorithm. The former measures
\lVert\E X^{\mathsf{alg}}-\E X\rVert
, while the latter measures
\lVert X^{\mathsf{alg}}-X\rVert_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}}
, where
X^{\mathsf{alg}},X
are obtained from the algorithm and true process started at a single point. Secondly, the conditions in shifted composition are more user-friendly, as there is no need to compute the determinant of a Malliavin derivative, which introduces significant technical challenges in our work.

In light of this, we should justify why anticipating Girsanov theorems are necessary at all. Firstly, the
\mathsf{KL}
bounds that we obtain are genuinely process-valued, which allows us to obtain stronger guarantees if one is interested in functionals of the entire stochastic process (for instance, auto-correlations). Secondly, the key assumption needed in
[
AC24a
,
ACZ25
]
is
cross-regularity
, which measures
\mathsf{KL}
(or
\mathsf{R}_{q}
) between the laws of
X^{\mathsf{alg}}
and
Y
, where
X^{\mathsf{alg}}
is obtained from an algorithm starting at
x
and
Y
is obtained from the reference process started at
y
. This requires a bound on
\mathsf{KL}(\operatorname{law}(X^{\mathsf{alg}})\mathbin{\|}\operatorname{law}(Y))
in terms of
\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}
and other quantities. The shifted composition framework is unable to provide tools for directly analyzing this quantity, and the bounds obtained thus far have been largely on a case-by-case basis. On the other hand, the anticipating Girsanov theorem presented in this work can be used to obtain such bounds directly, and thus provides a complementary contribution to the literature.

Anticipating stochastic calculus.

We would be remiss not to touch upon the history of anticipating stochastic calculus. The study of non-adapted processes has nearly as long a history as the standard Itô calculus, and the same techniques used to define the anticipating Girsanov theorem were also used to place the Stratonovich calculus on a rigorous mathematical foundation
[
OP89
]
. The differential and integral formulations of Malliavin calculus were developed independently by Malliavin
[
Mal78
]
and Skorohod
[
Sko76
]
respectively. The application to non-adapted processes was developed in
[
NP88
,
Buc91
,
Oba06
]
, among many other works. See
[
Par06
]
or the bibliography in
[
Nua95
]
for a more comprehensive survey.

The original, non-anticipating Girsanov theorem is from
[
Gir60
]
. Its anticipating variant is due to
[
Buc94
]
, based on other generalizations of Girsanov’s theorem
[
Ram74
,
Kus74
]
. It has been further generalized to other settings
[
ÜZ92
,
ES93
,
ÜZ13
]
, and has also seen applications in a large body of recent scholarship
[
KPS13
,
KPS13a
,
HKS19
]
.

1.2
Approach

We briefly summarize the technical approach which leads us to our bounds. Broadly speaking, if we approximate our process
\bar{\mathsf{u}}_{s}
by an elementary process
\sum_{k=0}^{T/\eta-1}f_{k}((B_{[j\eta,(j+1)\eta)})_{j=0,1,\dotsc,T/\eta-1})\one_{[k\eta,(k+1)\eta)}
(which is constant on intervals
[k\eta,(k+1)\eta)
for
\eta
sufficiently small, and depends on
B_{[0,T)}
only through the finite collection of increments
(B_{[k\eta,(k+1)\eta)})_{k\in[T/\eta]}
), then it becomes simple to write a change of measure so long as (
1.2
) is invertible. The techniques of Malliavin calculus allow us to discuss the validity of this limit as we improve the granularity of the approximation. Given
\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{P}
which correspond to the laws of the “paths”
X_{[0,T)}
following (
LD
) and a suitable interpolation of (
1.1
), we can use this result to obtain bounds on the
\mathsf{KL}(\mathbf{P}\mathbin{\|}\mathbf{Q})
, or
\mathsf{R}_{q}
as desired.

The key to this computation lies in bounding the determinant of the additional quantity
\mathsf{D}_{s}\mathsf{u}_{t}
, which for elementary processes is simply the matrix of derivatives of
f_{k}
with respect to the
j
-th Brownian increment. To do this, we take the following procedure: (1) approximate all continuous processes with a suitably fine, piecewise constant interpolation; (2) expand the log-determinant in terms of traces of matrix powers using Lemma
33
, resolving only the lowest-order terms. These contribute negligible factors to our bounds. We make these notions precise in the following sections.

In isolation, these Girsanov bounds can lead to some guarantees, by combining with the weak triangle inequality (Lemma
29
) and the convergence of (
LD
) or (
ULD
) in Rényi divergence (§
A.1
). However, our bounds work best when complimenting the results in
[
AC24a
,
ACZ25
]
, which can witness the weak error of the various numeric schemes but requires an additional
cross-regularity
ingredient which cannot be proved using their framework alone. We go into more detail in the subsequent section.

1.3
Contributions

We establish direct
\mathsf{KL}
and
\mathsf{R}_{q}
bounds between various midpoint interpolations. We summarize our primary contributions as follows.

•

We state a variant of Girsanov’s theorem for anticipating processes (Theorem
6
), and show how the quantities within this theorem can be effectively computed for discretizations of interest. While this result is not new, it has not thus far been effectively utilized within the sampling community, and we hope that our exposition will serve as an interpretable blueprint for future works aiming to analyze similar methods.

•

We introduce a deterministic double midpoint method for simulating (
ULD
) called (
DM–ULMC
), new to this work. In our analysis, we show that under a strong Hessian smoothness assumption (Assumption
2
), we can obtain
\mathsf{KL}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{Nh}\mathbin{\|}\boldsymbol{\pi})\leq\varepsilon^{2}
for some computationally tractable measure
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{Nh}
, with no more than
\widetilde{O}\Bigl(\frac{d^{1/4}}{\varepsilon^{1/2}}\Bigr)
queries. See Theorem
21
for a more precise statement. This is the first such result in
\mathsf{KL}
divergence.

•

We apply Theorem
6
for several midpoint methods used in sampling. Namely, we compute the Radon–Nikodym derivative between
\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q}
for the single midpoint method for the overdamped Langevin (
M-LMC
), as well as the double midpoint method for the underdamped Langevin (
DM–ULMC
). These can be used to derive cross-regularity for these schemes, as we discuss in §
5
.

In
[
AC24a
, Lemma 6.2]
, cross-regularity was proven for (
M-LMC
) in an ad-hoc way, leading to an extra factor of
\log\frac{1}{\beta h}
. On the other hand,
[
ACZ25
]
was unable to prove sufficiently strong cross-regularity bounds for (
DM–ULMC
), and so could only state guarantees if the exponential Euler discretization was used in the final step. We dispense with both of these caveats in this work through Corollary
12
and Lemma
15
respectively. We also note that our bounds hold in
q
-Rényi divergence.

•

For (
DM–ULMC
), the approaches used in
[
AC24a
,
ACZ25
]
are insufficient for a
\widetilde{O}(d^{1/4})
query complexity bound. We emphasize that the present work is neither derivative of nor detracts from the results in
[
ACZ25
]
. Instead, we see our approach as complementary; we can provide genuine
\mathsf{KL}
bounds on the process laws, which can be combined with the weak error through the framework of
[
ACZ25
]
.
The combination of both approaches
is necessary to obtain our final guarantees.

Organization

In §

, we provide some general preliminary notions. In §

2
Preliminaries

Notation.

In the sequel, we will consider the triple
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbf{P})
, induced by a Wiener measure
\mathbf{P}
(most naturally thought of as the measure which induces the “path law” of Brownian motions). As a result, we will let
L^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the set of functions
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\E_{\mathbf{P}}\lVert f\rVert^{2}<\infty
. As a special case,
L^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbb{R})
corresponds to real-valued functions
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\E_{\mathbf{P}}\lvert f\rvert^{2}<\infty
.

Likewise, we will use
L^{2}([0,T);\mathbb{R}^{d})
to denote functions
\mathsf{h}:[0,T)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\mathsf{h}(t)\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty
.
(B_{t})_{t\in[0,T)}
will be the standard
\mathbf{P}
-Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
; where necessary, we will denote
(\mathscr{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T)}
as its corresponding filtration. Almost surely,
(B_{t})_{t\in[0,T)}
takes values on
C_{0}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which denotes the set of random processes
(\mathsf{u}_{t})_{t\in[0,T)}:[0,T)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\mathsf{u}_{0}=0
almost surely.

L^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}([0,T);\mathbb{R}^{d})
will denote random processes
(\mathsf{u}_{t})_{t\in[0,T)}
with
\E[\lVert\mathsf{u}\rVert_{{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}}^{2}]<\infty
. A fortiori,
\mathsf{u}_{t}\in L^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for almost every
t\in[0,T)
.

We say that a process
(\mathsf{u}_{t})_{t\in[0,T)}
is
\mathcal{F}
-adapted if
\mathsf{u}_{t}
is measurable with respect to
\mathscr{F}_{t}
(the filtration corresponding to the
\mathbf{P}
-Brownian motion) for all
t\in[0,T)
.
For some simple examples of processes which are not adapted, one can consider the constant-in-time functions
t\mapsto B_{\tau}
for
\tau\in(0,T]
.

If a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{(d_{1}+d_{2})\times(d_{1}+d_{2})}
can be partitioned into blocks
\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{1}},\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}},\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}\times d_{1}},\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}\times d_{2}}
, we refer to these as
A_{[1,1]}
,
A_{[1,2]}
, etc. respectively. This is not to be confused with the notation
A_{i,j}
, which indicates the
(i,j)
-th entry of
A
. For instance,
(A_{[2,2]})_{i,j}=A_{d_{1}+i,d_{1}+j}
. We use the notation
\lesssim,\gtrsim,\asymp
to denote bounds up to an implied absolute constant, and these are equivalent to
O(\cdot),\Omega(\cdot),\Theta(\cdot)
.
a=\widetilde{O}(b)
denotes
a=O(b\operatorname{polylog}b)
, and likewise for
\widetilde{\Theta},\widetilde{\Omega}
.

General notions

To measure closeness between measures on arbitrary domains, we introduce the following information divergences and metrics between probability measures.

Definition 1
.

Let
\mu\ll\nu
be two distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, we can define the Kullback–Leibler (
\mathsf{KL}
) divergence between then as

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)=\E_{\mu}\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\,.

Likewise, we can define the
q
-Rényi divergence for
q>1
as

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)=\frac{1}{q-1}\log\int\Big\lvert\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\Big\rvert^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,,

where these definitions are to be interpreted as equalling
+\infty
when
\mu\not\ll\nu
.
The above generalizes to two measures on an infinite dimensional space such as
\Omega
, if we consider
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}
as the Radon–Nikodym derivative of
\mu
with respect to
\nu
. We note that the
q
-Rényi divergence is monotonically increasing in
q
and recovers the
\mathsf{KL}
-divergence when
q\to 1
.
\mathsf{KL}
divergence is also sufficient to upper bound the squared total variation distance via Pinsker’s inequality, with

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)\geq 2\lVert\mu-\nu\rVert_{\mathsf{TV}}^{2}\coloneqq 2\sup_{B\in\mathscr{F}}\lvert\mu(B)-\nu(B)\rvert^{2}\,.

Finally, we introduce the Wasserstein distance as, for
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\{\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2d}):\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(x,\mathrm{d}y)=\mu(x),\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(\mathrm{d}x,y)=\nu(y)\}
the set of couplings with marginals
\mu,\nu
respectively,

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\inf_{\rho\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\E_{(x,y)\sim\rho}\Bigl[\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}\Bigr]\,.

It is known that exponential convergence of (
LD
) in the divergences mentioned above can be obtained if we assume that the target measure satisfies a relevant functional inequality.

Definition 2

(Log-Sobolev inequality)
.

A measure
\pi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality with parameter
1/\alpha
if for all smooth and compactly supported functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\ent_{\pi}f\coloneqq\E_{\pi}\Bigl[f\log\frac{f}{\E_{\pi}f}\Bigr]\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha}\E_{\pi}[\lVert\nabla f\rVert^{2}]\,.

(LSI)

Notationally, for (
ULD
), consider
\boldsymbol{\pi}(x,p)\propto\exp(-V(x)-\frac{\lVert p\rVert^{2}}{2})
and an initial measure
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
. We will consider the following triplet of processes:
(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t},\boldsymbol{\pi})_{t\in[0,T)}
, which correspond respectively to the evolutions of
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}
along the algorithm,
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}
along (
ULD
), and
\boldsymbol{\pi}
under (
ULD
) (noting that
\boldsymbol{\pi}
is stationary). Similarly, we let
(\mu_{t},\tilde{\mu}_{t},\pi)
be the analogous versions along (
LD
).

3
Malliavin calculus

3.1
Warm-up: overdamped midpoint discretization

We now proceed with a brief warm-up. Before going into details, we state an important assumption which will be used throughout our work.

Assumption 1

(Regularity)
.

Let
V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be twice continuously differentiable with
-\beta I_{d}\preceq\nabla^{2}V\preceq\beta I_{d}
. Moreover, let
\nabla^{2}V
be uniformly continuous.

Remark.

The uniform continuity assumption is only needed for convergence of the certain approximation schemes when obtaining expressions for the Radon–Nikodym, and does not feature quantitatively in any of our results on
\mathsf{KL}
or
\mathsf{R}_{q}
. As a result, it can be effectively ignored using the standard technique of weakly convergent approximation combined with lower semi-continuity of information divergences.

We consider the overdamped midpoint discretization, which was introduced at the start of this paper. Here, we do not consider a random interpolant point; instead, suppose that the choice of interpolation times
\tau
is fixed and inhabiting
[0,h)
.

\displaystyle X_{t}=X_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\nabla V(X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s+\sqrt{2}\tilde{B}_{t}\,,

and

\displaystyle X_{t}=X_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\nabla V(X^{+})\,\mathrm{d}s+\sqrt{2}B_{t}\,,

(M–LMC)

where
(B_{t})_{t\in[0,h)},(\tilde{B}_{t})_{t\in[0,h)}
are
\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q}
Brownian motions respectively, and we define the interpolant

\displaystyle X^{+}=X_{0}-\tau\nabla V(X_{0})+\sqrt{2}B_{\tau}\,.

The reason behind this formalism is that given
X_{0}
, we can essentially think of
X_{t}
as deterministic functions of either
(B_{t})_{t\in[0,h)}
or
(\tilde{B}_{t})_{t\in[0,h)}
, and if
(B_{t})_{t\in[0,h)}
has the distribution of a Brownian motion, then
X_{h}
will now exactly have the marginal law of a single iteration of the midpoint discretization (see
[
SL19
]
). On the other hand, if
(\tilde{B}_{t})_{t\in[0,h)}
has a Brownian law, then
X_{h}
will instead be the marginal distribution of (
LD
). As a result, if we can compute the Radon–Nikodym and
\mathsf{KL}
divergence of the laws of
(B_{t})_{t\in[0,h)},(\tilde{B}_{t})_{t\in[0,h)}
, then we can immediately upper bound the
\mathsf{KL}
divergence between the marginals of the two sampling processes.

Part (A): Discretization.

To formulate this change of measure principle, it is instructive to first introduce a coarse discretization. Consider two sequences of standard normal random variables
\boldsymbol{\xi}=[\xi_{1},\dotsc,\xi_{h/\eta}]
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=[\tilde{\xi}_{1},\dotsc,\tilde{\xi}_{h/\eta}]
for
\eta\leq h
evenly dividing
h
, each inhabiting
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
\sqrt{\eta}\xi_{i}
,
\sqrt{\eta}\tilde{\xi}_{i}
representing the
i
-th Brownian increment
B_{i\eta}-B_{(i-1)\eta}
and
\tilde{B}_{i\eta}-\tilde{B}_{(i-1)\eta}
respectively. Now, define the sequence of marginals by, for
k\in[h/\eta]

\displaystyle\hat{X}_{k\eta}^{\eta}

\displaystyle=\hat{X}_{(k-1)\eta}^{\eta}-\eta\nabla V(\hat{X}_{(k-1)\eta}^{\eta})+\sqrt{2\eta}\tilde{\xi}_{k}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{X}_{k\eta}^{\eta}

\displaystyle=\hat{X}_{(k-1)\eta}^{\eta}-\eta\nabla V(X^{+})+\sqrt{2\eta}\xi_{k}\,,

and as
\eta
will be fixed for the moment, we omit it from the superscript. The definition of
X^{+}
does not change so long as
\eta
also subdivides
\tau
, which we can assume without much loss of generality, and is given by

\displaystyle X^{+}=X_{0}-\tau\nabla V(X_{0})+\sqrt{2\eta}\sum_{i=1}^{\tau/\eta}\xi_{k}\,.

We call the processes above “elementary” as they depend only on a finite number of Brownian increments. Now, consider the sequence

\displaystyle\tilde{\xi}_{i}=\xi_{i}+\sqrt{\frac{\eta}{2}}\Bigl(\nabla V(\hat{X}_{(i-1)\eta})-\nabla V(X^{+})\Bigr)\,,

(3.1)

and define
\mathsf{T}(\xi_{1},\dotsc,\xi_{h/\eta})=(\tilde{\xi}_{1},\dotsc,\tilde{\xi}_{h/\eta})
.
Then, (noting that both
X^{+}
,
\hat{X}^{\eta}_{k\eta}
are explicit functions of
(\xi_{i})_{i\in[h/\eta]}
), this gives a deterministic function which defines each
\tilde{\xi}_{i}
in terms of the entire sequence
(\xi_{i})_{i\in[h/\eta]}
.

Now, let us compute the Radon–Nikodym between the two sequences
(\xi_{i})_{i\in[h/\eta]}
,
(\tilde{\xi}_{i})_{i\in[h/\eta]}
. Define the functions

\displaystyle\mathsf{u}_{i}(\xi_{1},\dotsc,\xi_{h/\eta})=\sqrt{\frac{\eta}{2}}\Bigl(\nabla V(\hat{X}_{(i-1)\eta})-\nabla V(X^{+})\Bigr)\,.

(3.2)

Consider the “matrix”
\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u}
which has entries
(\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u})_{i,j}=\partial_{\xi_{j}}\mathsf{u}_{i}
. To disambiguate the gradient in
\xi
from the standard gradient e.g., in
\nabla V
, we write
\mathsf{D}
to denote the former. Note that each entry of
\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u}
is really matrix-valued, since both
\xi_{j}
and
\mathsf{u}_{i}
are vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, so that
\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times(h/\eta)\times(h/\eta)}
; however, we will typically view it as a matrix of size
(h/\eta)\times(h/\eta)
(temporal indices) with entries
d\times d
(spatial indices).

Change of measure.

Now, define the random factor

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}\coloneqq\lvert\det(I+\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u})\rvert\exp\Bigl(-\sum_{i=1}^{h/\eta}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\mathsf{u}_{i},\xi_{i}}}\right\rangle}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{h/\eta}\lVert\mathsf{u}_{i}\rVert^{2}\Bigr)\,,

omitting the dependence of
\mathsf{u}_{i}
on
\boldsymbol{\xi}
. Here, let
\lVert\mathsf{u}\rVert^{2}
be the
2
-norm (Hilbert–Schmidt norm) on
\mathbb{R}^{d\times(h/\eta)}
. As shall be seen later, it is instead more convenient to consider the quantities:

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}\coloneqq\Bigl\{\lvert\det(I_{d}+\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u})\rvert\exp(-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u})\Bigr\}\exp\Bigl(-\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{h/\eta}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\mathsf{u}_{i},\xi_{i}}}\right\rangle}-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u}\bigr)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{h/\eta}\lVert\mathsf{u}_{i}\rVert^{2}\Bigr)\,,

Here, the trace is both temporal and spatial, i.e.,
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u}=\sum_{i=1}^{h/\eta}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}((\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u})_{i,i})
.
\sum_{i\in[h/\eta]}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\mathsf{u}_{i},\xi_{i}}}\right\rangle}-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u}
has the pleasing interpretation that it is mean zero under
\mathbf{P}
, via a Gaussian integration by parts formula. Now, consider a non-negative random variable of the form
Z=f(\boldsymbol{\xi})
, and
f\in C_{b}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{h/\eta};\mathbb{R}_{+})
. We have another random variable

\displaystyle Z\circ\mathsf{T}=f(\xi_{1}+\mathsf{u}_{1},\,\dotsc,\,\xi_{h/\eta}+\mathsf{u}_{h/\eta})\,.

(3.3)

Now we have

\displaystyle\E_{\mathbf{P}}\Bigl[(Z\circ\mathsf{T})\cdot\mathbf{M}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathbf{P}}\biggl[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{h/\eta}}\lvert\det(I_{d}+\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u}(\mathbf{x}))\rvert\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{h/\eta}(x_{j}+\mathsf{u}_{j}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\cdot f\Bigl(x_{1}+\mathsf{u}_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\dotsc,x_{h/\eta}+\mathsf{u}_{1}(\mathbf{x})\Bigr)\cdot(2\pi)^{-\frac{h/\eta}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\biggr]\,,

with
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{h/\eta})
. Here, the extra factor of
-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{h/\eta}x_{j}^{2}
in the exponent arises from
\boldsymbol{\xi}
being Gaussian under
\mathbf{P}
. Applying the change-of-variables formula and as
\mathsf{T}
is one-to-one,

\displaystyle\E_{\mathbf{P}}\Bigl[(Z\circ\mathsf{T})\mathbf{M}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathbf{P}}\biggl[\int_{\operatorname{Im}_{\operatorname{id}+\mathsf{u}}}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{y}\rVert^{2}\Bigr)f(y_{1},\dotsc,y_{h/\eta})\cdot(2\pi)^{-\frac{h/\eta}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\biggr]\,,

Here,
\operatorname{Im}(\operatorname{id}+\mathsf{u})
is the image of
\mathbb{R}^{h/\eta}
under the mapping
\mathbf{x}\mapsto\mathbf{x}+\mathsf{u}(\mathbf{x})
. Replacing this with the integral over the whole space gives

\displaystyle\E_{\mathbf{P}}\Bigl[(Z\circ\mathsf{T})\cdot\mathbf{M}\Bigr]\leq\E_{\mathbf{P}}[Z]\,,

(3.4)

with equality when
\operatorname{id}+\mathsf{u}
is onto, e.g. when
\mathsf{T}
is invertible (rather than merely locally invertible).

Part (B): Computation of relevant quantities.

We note that
\mathsf{D}\nabla V(X^{+})
is

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{i}\{\nabla V(X^{+})\}=\nabla^{2}V(X^{+})\cdot\sqrt{2\eta}\one_{i\leq\tau/\eta}\,.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathsf{D}_{i}\nabla V(\hat{X}_{k\eta})&=\nabla^{2}V(\hat{X}_{k\eta})\cdot\mathsf{D}_{i}\Bigl(X_{0}-k\eta\nabla V(X^{+})+\sqrt{2\eta}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\xi_{k}\Bigr)\\
&=\nabla^{2}V(\hat{X}_{k\eta})\cdot\Bigl(k\eta\nabla^{2}V(X^{+})\cdot\sqrt{2\eta}\one_{i\leq\tau/\eta}+\sqrt{2\eta}I_{d}\one_{i\leq k}\Bigr)\,,\end{aligned}

(3.5)

using the formula for
\mathsf{D}_{i}(\nabla V(X^{+}))
. Now, we can compute the determinant as needed. For later convenience, we will consider the determinant involving
q\mathsf{u}
for some
q>0
. The coefficients can be written in the following form:

\displaystyle I+q\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u}=\begin{bmatrix}I+(q\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u})_{[1,1]}&0\\
(q\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u})_{[2,1]}&I+(q\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u})_{[2,2]}\end{bmatrix}\,,

where the block
(q\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u})_{[1,1]}
has size
(\tau/\eta)\times(\tau/\eta)
(with each entry a block matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, so that
\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u}
is really a
4
-tensor), and
I
is an identity matrix of appropriate size. As
(q\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u})_{[2,2]}
involves only the components that are strictly below the diagonal, we have
\det(I+(q\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u})_{[2,2]})=1
. We can also neglect
(q\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u})_{[2,1]}
as the matrix is block upper triangular. It remains to consider the determinant of the top left.

By abuse of notation, all our subsequent calculations will be
restricted to coordinates in this block
. We will continue to use the same notation for our matrices, noting that these are really the restrictions of the matrices to the
[1,1]
block. Now, we make some simplifications. First, we have the expansion

\displaystyle\lVert\sqrt{\eta}\,\mathsf{D}(\nabla V(\hat{X}))\rVert\lesssim\lVert\mathsf{A}\rVert+O(\beta^{2}h^{2})\,,\qquad\mathsf{A}_{i,j}=\sqrt{2}\eta\nabla^{2}V(\hat{X}_{(j-1)\eta})\one_{i\leq j-1}\,.

The reason for the offset
j-1
in the index arises from our definition of
\mathsf{u}_{i}
in (
3.2
).
We note that the terms arising from differentiating
\nabla V(X^{+})
within
\nabla V(\hat{X})
are of higher order and so can be ignored, which can be seen from (
3.5
) (multiplying both sides by
\sqrt{\eta}
), wherein the first term has eigenvalues of order
O(\beta^{2}h^{2})
, whereas the second gives rise to
\mathsf{A}
and has eigenvalues of order
O(\beta h)
. Define
\mathbbm{v}=[1,\dotsc,1]\in\mathbb{R}^{h/\eta}
. Appealing to Lemma
33
, we have for
h\lesssim\frac{1}{\beta q}
,

\displaystyle\log\lvert\det(I+q\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u})\rvert-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}\bigl(q\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u})\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad=-\frac{q^{2}}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}\Bigl((\mathsf{D}\{\nabla V(X^{+})\}\mathbbm{v}^{\top}-\mathsf{A})^{2}\Bigr)+O(\beta^{3}dh^{3}q^{3})

\displaystyle\qquad=-\frac{q^{2}}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}\Bigl(\mathsf{A}^{2}-2\mathsf{D}\{\nabla V(X^{+})\}\mathbbm{v}^{\top}\cdot\mathsf{A}+(\mathsf{D}\{\nabla V(X^{+})\mathbbm{v}^{\top})^{2}\Bigr)+O(\beta^{3}dh^{3}q^{3})\,.

Validity of the discretization.

Finally, we can verify the invertibility from our Hessian computations involving
\mathsf{T}
. We can see that
q\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u}
has operator norm bounded by
O(\beta hq)
. So long as
h\lesssim\frac{1}{\beta q}
for a sufficiently small implied constant, then invertibility follows if we can show properness of the transformation
\mathsf{T}
. Properness of
\mathsf{T}:\boldsymbol{\xi}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\xi}+q\mathsf{u}
follows as, from (
3.1
) and Lipschitzness of
\nabla V
,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \lVert\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\rVert^{2}&\geq\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert^{2}-q\cdot O\Bigl(\eta\lVert\nabla V(\hat{X})\rVert^{2}+h\lVert\nabla V(X^{+})\rVert^{2}\Bigr)\\
&\geq\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert^{2}-q^{2}\cdot O\Bigl(h\lVert\nabla V(X_{0})\rVert^{2}+(h+\beta^{2}h^{3})\lVert\nabla V(X^{+})\rVert^{2}+\beta^{2}h^{2}\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert^{2}\Bigr)\\
&\geq\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert^{2}-q^{2}\cdot O\Bigl((h+\beta^{2}h^{3}+\beta^{4}h^{5})\lVert\nabla V(X_{0})\rVert^{2}+(\beta^{2}h^{2}+\beta^{4}h^{4})\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert^{2}\Bigr)\,.\end{aligned}

(3.6)

and thus taking
\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert\to\infty
, we can ensure that
\lVert\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\rVert\to\infty
for
h\lesssim\frac{1}{\beta q}
. This implies (
3.4
) is indeed an equality, and the factor
\mathbf{M}
is a true Radon–Nikodym derivative.

3.2
Details of Malliavin calculus

It turns out that the computations of the preceding section can be considerably generalized, as the relevant quantities are stable in the limit as we take
\eta\to 0
. As we consider now arbitrary endpoints, we consider processes defined on
[0,T)
.
1
1

1

We exclude the right endpoint of
[0,T)
for simplicity, although it can be included via a slight (but tedious) modification of the notation.

In particular, we introduce the following definitions. Use the familiar shorthand
\xi_{i}^{\eta}=(B_{i\eta}-B_{(i-1)\eta})
,
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\eta}=[\xi_{1}^{\eta},\dotsc,\xi_{T/\eta}^{\eta}]
. Now, we define the Malliavin derivative of functions of
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\eta}
.

Definition 3

(Malliavin derivative)
.

The
Malliavin derivatives
of functions of
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times T/\eta}
,
\mathbf{F}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times T/\eta}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be defined component-wise as

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{i}\mathbf{F}=\partial_{i}\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\eta})\,.

Remark.

Intuitively, functions
\mathbf{F}
can be considered a function of the entire Brownian path. Due to technical subtleties, we would prefer that these functions depend only on a finite number of nice statistics relating to the path; for instance, on a finite number of Itô integrals against nice functions. Then, the Malliavin derivative gives a way of differentiating the function
\mathbf{F}
with respect to the underlying Brownian motion, such that
\mathbf{F}
satisfies nice continuity properties with respect to the topology of
\Omega
(the product topology). The monographs
[
Nua95
,
Eld16
,
Hai21
]
contain more detailed treatments of these issues. Furthermore, the Malliavin derivative is typically not defined as a linear form, but rather as a process on
L^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This definition is consistent with the definition above up to some factors of
\eta
; while our definition bears greater resemblance to elementary calculus, it is somewhat less elegant when taking the limit
\eta\searrow 0
.

Definition 4

(Skorohod adjoint operator)
.

We denote by
\updelta
the Skorohod adjoint operator to the Malliavin derivative. For any function
\mathbf{F}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times T/\eta}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathsf{u}\in L^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
a random process which is piecewise constant on each of the intervals
[(i-1)\eta,i\eta)
,
i\in[T/\eta]
, we have

\displaystyle\E_{\mathbf{P}}[\mathbf{F}\updelta\mathsf{u}]=\E_{\mathbf{P}}\Bigl[\sum_{i=1}^{T/\tau}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\mathsf{D}_{i}\mathbf{F},\mathsf{u}_{i}}}\right\rangle}\Bigr]\,,

and one can verify that this gives

\displaystyle\updelta\mathsf{u}=\sum_{i=1}^{T/\eta}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\mathsf{u}_{i},\xi_{i}}}\right\rangle}-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u}\,.

Remark.

This is sometimes referred to as the “Skorohod integral” of the element
\mathsf{u}
, since (i) it agrees with the Itô integral when
\mathsf{u}
is adapted to the filtration of the standard Brownian motion, (ii) aesthetically, it has the effect of “integrating the Malliavin derivative by parts”. However, it is not clear how much this represents a bona fide stochastic integral in the non-adapted case.

The following definition will be useful for defining determinants of kernels over general spaces, which appears in the anticipating Girsanov theorem in a vital way.

Definition 5

(Carleman–Fredholm determinant)
.

For finite kernels
K=\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times(T/\eta)\times(T/\eta)}
, the
regularized Carleman–Fredholm determinant
is defined as

\displaystyle{\det}_{\mathsf{CF}}(I+K)\coloneqq\det(I+K)\exp(-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}K)\,.

Now, we can accurately state the anticipating Girsanov theorem. In the formulation of (
1.2
), we have
\mathsf{T}:B_{[0,T]}\mapsto B_{[0,T]}+(\int_{0}^{\cdot}\bar{\mathsf{u}}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s)_{[0,T]}
. If we write in terms of standard Gaussians, and suppose that
\bar{\mathsf{u}}
is piecewise constant over each interval the intervals
[(k-1)\eta,k\eta)
,
k\in[T/\eta]
, then this coincides with our definition in (
3.3
) if, for
t\in[(k-1)\eta,k\eta)
,

\displaystyle\bar{\mathsf{u}}_{t}^{\eta}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta}}\mathsf{u}_{k}^{\eta}\,.

(3.7)

Then, we write, using the same notation

\displaystyle\log{\det}_{\mathsf{CF}}(I+\mathsf{D}\bar{\mathsf{u}}^{\eta})\coloneqq\log{\det}_{\mathsf{CF}}(I+\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u}^{\eta})\,,\qquad\updelta\bar{\mathsf{u}}^{\eta}\coloneqq\updelta\mathsf{u}\,.

Furthermore, for processes which are not piecewise constant, we can define

\displaystyle\log{\det}_{\mathsf{CF}}(I+\mathsf{D}\bar{\mathsf{u}})\coloneqq\lim_{\eta\searrow 0}\log{\det}_{\mathsf{CF}}(I+\mathsf{D}\bar{\mathsf{u}}^{\eta})\,,\qquad\updelta\bar{\mathsf{u}}\coloneqq\lim_{\eta\searrow 0}\updelta\bar{\mathsf{u}}^{\eta}\,.

for some approximation
\bar{\mathsf{u}}^{\eta}
to
\bar{\mathsf{u}}
. Note that
I+\mathsf{D}\bar{\mathsf{u}}
is not itself a well-defined object in our notation, although the
\log{\det}_{\mathsf{CF}}
is well-defined from the expressions above. It turns out
[
Nua95
]
that these definitions are invariant with respect to the specific choice of approximation, as long as the limiting quantities are well-defined for the sequence of piecewise constant approximations, and the convergence
\bar{\mathsf{u}}^{\eta}\to\bar{\mathsf{u}}
holds in
L^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Under this convention, it turns out that the preceding computations give rise to a generalization of Girsanov’s theorem.

Theorem 6

(Anticipating Girsanov theorem, adapted from
[
Nua95
, Section 4.1.4]
)
.

Suppose that in (
1.2
),
\mathsf{T}
is bijective. Then, let
\mathbf{Q}
be the measure under which
\mathsf{T}((B_{t})_{t\in[0,T)})
is Brownian, and
\mathbf{P}
the measure under which
(B_{t})_{t\in[0,T)}
is Brownian. The Radon–Nikodym derivative is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Q}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{P}}(\omega)=\lvert{\det}_{\mathsf{CF}}(I+\mathsf{D}\bar{\mathsf{u}}(\omega))\rvert\exp\Bigl(-(\updelta\bar{\mathsf{u}})(\omega)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\bar{\mathsf{u}}_{t}(\omega)\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigr)\,,

where
\bar{\mathsf{u}}(\omega)
refers to the value of
\bar{\mathsf{u}}=(\bar{\mathsf{u}}_{t})_{t\in[0,T)}
given
(B_{t})_{t\in[0,T)}=\omega\in C_{0}([0,T);\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where
\bar{\mathsf{u}}(\omega)
is deterministic by assumption.
(\mathsf{D}\bar{\mathsf{u}})(\omega),\ \updelta\bar{\mathsf{u}}(\omega)
are similarly interpreted.

This lemma was already proven for elementary processes in the preceding section. Whereas the extension to arbitrary processes can be formally done using some heavy analytic machinery
[
Nua95
]
, this will not be necessary for computing
\mathsf{R}_{q}
between the processes, by using the weak convergence of the elementary approximations as well as the lower semicontinuity of
\mathsf{R}_{q}
in both arguments. See
[
Nua95
, Section 4.1]
for a complete proof.

For reference, we provide the original Girsanov theorem below. It will be easier to utilize than the anticipating Girsanov equation when dealing with transformations given by an SDE, i.e., without any anticipation in the drift.

Theorem 7

(Non-anticipating Girsanov; adapted from
[
Øks13
, Theorem 8.6.8]
)
.

Suppose that in (
1.2
),
\mathsf{u}_{s}\in L^{2}_{\mathbf{P},\operatorname{ad}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
is adapted. Then, if
\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{P}
are again the measures induced by
\mathsf{T}
and the Wiener measure respectively as in Theorem
6
, the Radon–Nikodym derivative is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Q}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{P}}(\omega)=\exp\Bigl(-\int_{0}^{T}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\bar{\mathsf{u}}_{t},\mathrm{d}B_{t}}}\right\rangle}(\omega)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\bar{\mathsf{u}}_{t}(\omega)\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigr)\,,

where again
\int_{0}^{T}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\bar{\mathsf{u}}_{t},\mathrm{d}B_{t}}}\right\rangle}(\omega)
denotes the Itô integral of
\mathsf{u}_{t}
(evaluated at the Brownian path realization
(B_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}=\omega
). This requires Novikov’s condition to be satisfied:

\displaystyle\E_{\mathbf{P}}\Bigl[\exp\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\bar{\mathsf{u}}_{t}(\omega)\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigr)\Bigr]<\infty\,.

(3.8)

Theorem
7
can be recovered as a special case of the anticipating Girsanov theorem. In this case,
\mathsf{D}\bar{\mathsf{u}}
is a quasi-nilpotent kernel (all its eigenvalues are zero),
{\det}_{\mathsf{CF}}(I+\mathsf{D}\bar{\mathsf{u}})=1
. Secondly, the Skorohod adjoint reduces to the Itô integral, which then gives the theorem as desired.

User’s guide to the anticipating Girsanov theorem

The following procedure provides a step-by-step guide to the machinery developed in the sections above. Here, we suppose we want to compare two discretizations of the forms

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} X_{t}&=X_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathsf{G}_{s}(X_{[0,T]})\,\mathrm{d}s+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{B}_{t}\\
X_{t}&=X_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathsf{F}_{s}(X_{[0,T]},B_{[0,T]})\,\mathrm{d}s+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}\,,\end{aligned}

(3.9)

where for all time,
\mathsf{F},\mathsf{G}
depend on
X_{[0,T]}
only through finitely many statistics of the form
\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}f(t)X_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t
for
t_{0}<t_{1}
and some function
f
, and likewise depend only on finitely many statistics of
B_{[0,T]}
. Furthermore,
\mathsf{F},\mathsf{G}
should be suitably continuous in time. Certain generalizations of this set-up may also be permitted, but we do not cover such generality in this text.

(A)

Discretize the two formulations above using piecewise constant schemes (which resemble the “Euler–Maruyama” schemes), defined for
t\in[0,T]
, with
\xi_{k}
a sequence of i.i.d. standard
\mathbf{P}
-Gaussians which correspond to the increments
B_{k\eta}-B_{(k-1)\eta}
, and with
\tilde{\xi}_{k}
defined as

\displaystyle\hat{X}_{k\eta}^{\eta}

\displaystyle=\hat{X}_{(k-1)\eta}^{\eta}-\eta\mathsf{G}_{(k-1)\eta}(\hat{X}^{\eta}_{[0,T]})+\sqrt{2\eta}\tilde{\xi}_{k}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{X}_{k\eta}^{\eta}

\displaystyle=\hat{X}_{(k-1)\eta}^{\eta}-\eta\mathsf{F}_{(k-1)\eta}(\hat{X}^{\eta}_{[0,T]},B_{[0,T]})+\sqrt{2\eta}\xi_{k}\,.

Note that the scheme above is possibly implicit, in that the vector
\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}^{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times T/\eta}
solves an equation like
\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}^{\eta}=\boldsymbol{\psi}(\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}^{\eta},\boldsymbol{\xi})
for some non-linear function
\boldsymbol{\psi}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times T/\eta}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times T/\eta}
. Where necessary, one needs to ensure that this implicit equation has a unique and well-defined solution for
\eta
small enough, and that it converges to the solution of (
3.9
) in
L^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
. These properties are verified using standard analysis techniques, which is usually best done at the end (as it requires computations from the successive parts).

(B)

One then considers

\displaystyle\mathsf{u}^{\eta}_{k}=\sqrt{\frac{\eta}{2}}\Bigl(\mathsf{G}_{(k-1)\eta}(\hat{X}^{\eta}_{[0,T]})-\mathsf{F}_{(k-1)\eta}(\hat{X}^{\eta}_{[0,T]},B_{[0,T]})\Bigr)\,,

and computes the relevant Malliavin objects
\log{\det}_{\mathsf{CF}}(I+\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u}^{\eta})
,
\updelta\mathsf{u}^{\eta}
using standard finite-dimensional calculus.

(C)

Then, assuming that the expressions
\log{\det}_{\mathsf{CF}}(I+\mathsf{D}\mathsf{u}^{\eta})
,
\updelta\mathsf{u}^{\eta}
remain almost surely finite in the limit as
\eta\searrow 0
, one formally assigns these limits as the values of
\log{\det}_{\mathsf{CF}}(I+\mathsf{D}\bar{\mathsf{u}})
,
\updelta\bar{\mathsf{u}}
respectively.

(D)

Substitute these expressions into Theorem
6
in order to obtain a simple expression for the Radon–Nikodym derivative. Use this to compute the
\mathsf{KL}
divergence between path measures, or whatever other statistic that may be desired. Here, some extra work is usually needed to control
\lVert\bar{\mathsf{u}}\rVert_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}
, which is somewhat orthogonal to the anticipating Girsanov machinery; instead, it is usually similar to existing discretization analyses in sampling, see
[
Che24
]
.

Remark.

Note that it does not matter precisely how one discretizes (
3.9
), as long as the discretization scheme is well-defined and converges in
L^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

Part (D): Computation of the
\mathsf{R}_{q}

At last, we can consider the
\mathsf{R}_{q}

Lemma 11
.

If
\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{P}
are the laws of
(\tilde{B}_{t})_{t\in[0,Nh)}
,
(B_{t})_{t\in[0,Nh)}
respectively, under Assumption
1
, for
q\geq 2
,
h\lesssim\frac{1}{\beta q}
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{q}(\mathbf{P}\mathbin{\|}\mathbf{Q})

\displaystyle\lesssim\beta^{2}dh^{2}qN+\frac{1}{q}\max_{k\in[N]}\log\E_{\mathbf{P}}\exp\Bigl(\beta^{2}q^{2}\int_{(k-1)h}^{kh}\lVert X_{t}-X_{k}^{+}\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigr)\,.

Proof.

Using Theorem
6
and Lemma
10
, with our step-size condition being necessary for existence, and applying Cauchy–Schwarz,

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{q}(\mathbf{P}\mathbin{\|}\mathbf{Q})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q-1}\log\E_{\mathbf{Q}}\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{P}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Q}}\Bigr)^{q}=\frac{1}{q-1}\log\E_{\mathbf{P}}\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{P}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Q}}\Bigr)^{q-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{q-1}\log\E_{\bf P}\Bigl[\Bigl\lvert{\det}_{\mathsf{CF}}\Bigl(I+\mathsf{D}\bar{\mathsf{u}}\Bigr)\Bigr\rvert^{-(q-1)}\exp\Bigl(q-1)\updelta\bar{\mathsf{u}}+\frac{(q-1)^{2}}{2}\lVert\bar{\mathsf{u}}\rVert^{2}_{{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}}\Bigr)\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{q-1}\log\E_{\bf P}\Bigl[\Bigl\lvert{\det}_{\mathsf{CF}}\Bigl(I-2(q-1)\mathsf{D}\bar{\mathsf{u}}\Bigr)\Bigr\rvert\exp\Bigl(2(q-1)\updelta\bar{\mathsf{u}}-2(q-1)^{2}\lVert\bar{\mathsf{u}}\rVert^{2}_{{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}}\Bigr)\Bigr]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{q-1}\log\E_{\bf P}\biggl[\exp\Bigl(\bigl((q-1)+2(q-1)^{2}\bigr)\lVert\bar{\mathsf{u}}\rVert^{2}_{{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}}\Bigr)\frac{\lvert{\det}_{\mathsf{CF}}(I+\mathsf{D}\bar{\mathsf{u}})\rvert^{-2(q-1)}}{\lvert{\det}_{\mathsf{CF}}(I-2(q-1)\mathsf{D}\bar{\mathsf{u}})\rvert}\biggr]\,.

Via Corollary
25
, the first term has unit expectation if we consider the process
-2(q-1)\bar{\mathsf{u}}
. Such a process is still invertible for
h\lesssim\frac{1}{\beta q}
sufficiently small as its determinant can be found from Lemma
10
.
Now, we note that we had bounded

\displaystyle\log\lvert{\det}_{\mathsf{CF}}(I+r\mathsf{D}\bar{\mathsf{u}})\rvert

\displaystyle\lesssim-\sqrt{2}r^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{(k-1)h}^{(k-1)h+\tau_{k}}t\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}(\nabla^{2}V(X_{t})\nabla^{2}V(X_{k}^{+}))\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{\tau_{k}^{2}r^{2}}{\sqrt{2}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}((\nabla^{2}V(X^{+}_{k}))^{2})+O(\beta^{3}dh^{3}\lvert r\rvert^{3}N)\lesssim\beta^{2}dh^{2}r^{2}N\,,

almost surely, for
h\lesssim\frac{1}{\beta q}
, and furthermore this holds for all
r\in\mathbb{R}
. If we consider the cases where
r=1
and
-(q-1)
respectively, then we can bound the determinant by

\displaystyle\log\Bigl[\frac{\lvert{\det}_{\mathsf{CF}}(I-2(q-1)\mathsf{D}\bar{\mathsf{u}})\rvert^{-1}}{\lvert{\det}_{\mathsf{CF}}(I+\mathsf{D}\bar{\mathsf{u}})\rvert^{2(q-1)}}\Bigr]\lesssim\beta^{2}dh^{2}q^{2}N\,.

almost surely.
Now, almost surely we can write

\displaystyle\lim_{\eta\searrow 0}\lVert\bar{\mathsf{u}}\rVert_{{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}}^{2}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{(k-1)h}^{kh}\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\lVert X_{t}-X_{k}^{+}\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,.

It follows that as
\tau_{k}\leq h
, and for

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{q}(\mathbf{P}\mathbin{\|}\mathbf{Q})

\displaystyle\lesssim\beta^{2}dh^{2}qN+\frac{1}{q-1}\log\E_{\mathbf{P}}\exp\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\beta^{2}q^{2}\int_{(k-1)h}^{kh}\lVert X_{t}-X_{k}^{+}\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigr)

\displaystyle\lesssim\beta^{2}dh^{2}qN+\frac{1}{q}\max_{k\in[N]}\log\E_{\mathbf{P}}\exp\Bigl(\beta^{2}q^{2}N\int_{(k-1)h}^{kh}\lVert X_{t}-X_{k}^{+}\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigr)\,.

∎

Corollary 12
.

If
\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{P}
are the laws under which

follows (

) and (

) respectively, it follows that if the initial distribution is

, under Assumption

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{q}(\mathbf{P}\mathbin{\|}\mathbf{Q})\lesssim\beta^{2}dh^{2}qN+\frac{1}{q}\sup_{k\leq N}\log\E_{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl[\exp\Bigl(\beta^{2}h^{3}q^{2}N\lVert\nabla V(X_{(k-1)h})\rVert^{2}\Bigr)\Bigr]\,.

In particular,

and

The assumption

is reasonable in light of

). Indeed, one will find that the corresponding path measure is mutually singular with respect to

, the measure of

. Instead, we seek a process with the correct marginal law, but which minimizes the error which would arise through Girsanov’s theorem. This has the form

\displaystyle\operatorname{objective}(\mathsf{G}_{[0,h)})\propto\E_{\mathbf{P}}[\lVert\mathsf{G}_{[0,h)}-\nabla V(X)_{[0,h)}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}]\,.

In particular, let
\mathsf{G}^{\mathsf{opt}}_{[0,h)}
be the process in (

)
subject to the two constraints at time

given by (

). As our constraints must hold for every fixed realization of the Brownian path

, our objective is also most naturally formulated pointwise with respect to this path.

Although

itself depends implicitly on

, we can reasonably expect that this dependence will generally be lower order (on the scale of
h^{2}
); furthermore, incorporating this dependence in the optimization leads to a solution that is difficult to express in closed form. Thus, when optimizing, we ignore the differential of

with respect to

\displaystyle\mathsf{G}_{t}^{\mathsf{opt}}(X_{[0,h)})=\nabla V(X_{t})-\mathcal{E}_{1}(t,h)\lambda_{1}(X_{[0,h)})-\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,h)\lambda_{2}(X_{[0,h)})\,,

where the coefficients

Now, consider the marginal effective velocities
\mathsf{G}^{\mathsf{p}},\mathsf{G}^{\mathsf{x}}
which gives (up to a scaling constant) the amount of gradient we accumulate in the momentum and position respectively at

.
Evidently, this causes

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{h}\mathcal{E}_{1}(t,h)\mathsf{G}_{t}^{\mathsf{opt}}(X_{[0,h)})\,\mathrm{d}t=\mathsf{G}^{\mathsf{p}}\,,\qquad\int_{0}^{h}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{E}_{1}(s,t)\mathsf{G}_{s}^{\mathsf{opt}}(X_{[0,h)})\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t=\mathsf{G}^{\mathsf{x}}\,,

as we can view the matrix
\Sigma
with coefficients

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{h}\mathcal{E}_{1}(t,h)\mathsf{G}_{t}^{\mathsf{opt}}(X_{[0,h)})\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{h}\mathcal{E}_{1}(t,h)\nabla V(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t-\Bigl[\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}\operatorname{diff}\Bigr]_{1}\,,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{h}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{E}_{1}(s,h)\mathsf{G}_{s}^{\mathsf{opt}}(X_{[0,h)})\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{h}\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,h)\nabla V(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t-\Bigl[\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}\operatorname{diff}\Bigr]_{2}\,,

where
\operatorname{diff}^{\mathsf{p}}=\int_{0}^{h}\mathcal{E}_{1}(t,h)\nabla V(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t-\mathsf{G}^{\mathsf{p}}
and
\operatorname{diff}^{\mathsf{x}}=\int_{0}^{h}\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,h)\nabla V(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t-\mathsf{G}^{\mathsf{x}}

\operatorname{diff}=[\operatorname{diff}^{\mathsf{p}},\operatorname{diff}^{\mathsf{x}}]^{\top}

can easily be seen to satisfy both constraints, corresponding to

Note that the solution for

\mathsf{G}_{t}^{\mathsf{opt}}

4.2.1
Deterministic double midpoint

Now, it remains to obtain a refined bound through a higher-order expansion. Assume that
\tau_{k}^{-}=\frac{h}{3}
and
\tau_{k}^{+}=\frac{h}{2}
identically for all
k\in[N]
. To take advantage of the improved properties of the discretization, we impose the following assumption. First, for a
3
-tensor
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times d}
, we define its
\{1,2\},\{3\}
norm as follows:

\displaystyle\lVert A\rVert_{\{1,2\},\{3\}}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Bigl\{A_{ijk}\,x_{ij}\,y_{k};\quad\lVert x\rVert_{F}\leq 1,\,\lVert y\rVert\leq 1\Bigr\}\,.

We can also define its
\{1,2,3\}
norm, or Frobenius norm, with

\displaystyle\lVert A\rVert_{\{1,2,3\}}=\sqrt{\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}A_{i,j,k}^{2}}\,.

The following assumption may seen somewhat technical at a glance, but it is crucial for our results.

Assumption 2
.

V
is thrice differentiable and its third derivative tensor has the property that, for
\xi
a standard Gaussian and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
deterministic and arbitrary, for all
\delta\in(0,1/c)
for an absolute constant
c
, with probability
1-\delta
, and for some parameters
\beta_{\mathsf{eff}},\beta_{H}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
\beta_{H}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lVert\nabla^{3}V(x)\rVert_{\{1,2\},\{3\}}
,

\displaystyle\lVert\nabla^{3}V(x)[\xi,\xi]\rVert^{2}\lesssim\beta_{H}^{2}d+\beta_{\mathsf{eff}}^{2}d^{1/2}\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\beta_{\mathsf{eff}}^{2}\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}\,.

The assumption of thrice-differentiability is not strictly necessary; twice-differentiability combined with boundedness of the subdifferential of
\nabla^{2}V
is enough, although we do not work with such an assumption for simplicity. Assumption
2
is somewhat non-standard, compared to the assumption
\lVert\nabla^{3}V\rVert_{\{1,2\},\{3\}}\lesssim\beta_{H}
which has appeared in
[
PW24
,
CG23
]
. Nonetheless, we demonstrate that Assumption
2
holds with dimension-independent
\beta_{\mathsf{eff}}
in two key settings.

Example 16

(Product measures)
.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{\pi}
is a product measure, so that
(\nabla^{3}V)_{i,j,k}=0
unless
i=j=k
. Then, this reduces to bounding

\displaystyle\Bigl\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bigl\lvert\partial_{i}^{3}V(x_{i})[\xi_{i},\xi_{i}]\bigr\rvert^{2}-\E\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bigl\lvert\partial_{i}^{3}V(x_{i})[\xi_{i},\xi_{i}]\bigr\rvert^{2}\Bigr\rvert\lesssim\lVert\nabla^{3}V\rVert_{\{1,2\},\{3\}}^{2}\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{d}\xi_{i}^{4}-\E\sum_{i=1}^{d}\xi_{i}^{4}\rvert\,.

noting that
\lVert\nabla^{3}V\rVert_{\{1,2\},\{3\}}=\max_{i\in[d]}\lvert\partial_{i}^{3}V\rvert
, and thus that
\beta_{H}^{2}
is dimension-free.

Now, this is an order-
4
diagonal Gaussian chaos. Consider the vector
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d/2}
with
X_{j}=\xi_{2j}^{2}+\xi_{2j+1}^{2}
, for

(assuming

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{\lvert\lVert X\rVert-\E\lVert X\rVert\rvert\geq t\}\leq 2\exp\Bigl(-\frac{t}{8}\Bigr)\,,

or alternatively, if

, then with probability

\displaystyle\lVert X\rVert\lesssim\E[\lVert X\rVert]+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\,.

It follows by Jensen’s inequality and Young’s inequality that

\displaystyle\lVert X\rVert^{2}\lesssim\E[\lVert X\rVert^{2}]+\E[\lVert X\rVert]\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}\,.

As
\E[\lVert X\rVert^{2}]\lesssim d
, in this case we have (multiplying by

with

.

Example 17

(Dimension-free
\beta_{T}
)
.

When

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigl\{\Bigl\lvert

\displaystyle\lVert\nabla^{3}V(x)[\xi,\xi]\rVert^{2}-\E\lVert\nabla^{3}V(x)[\xi,\xi]\rVert^{2}\Bigr\rvert\geq t\Bigr\}

5.1
Improved cross-regularity guarantees for M–LMC and DM–ULMC

Both algorithms (
M-LMC
) and (
DM–ULMC
) can be viewed as deterministic equivalents of the over- and underdamped randomized midpoint algorithms.

In
[
AC24a
,
ACZ25
]
, a key ingredient in these proofs is the cross-regularity of the diffusion. For (
M-LMC
), this was proven in a somewhat circuitous way in
[
AC24a
, Lemma 6.2]
, conditioning on the probability that the interpolant time satisfies
\tau_{k}\leq(1-\delta)h
for small
\delta
and then integrating over this distribution. This causes an additional
\log\frac{1}{\beta h}
to appear in their cross-regularity bounds. Instead, we note that Corollary
12
directly provides a bound on the cross-regularity with

\displaystyle\E_{x\sim\boldsymbol{\mu}_{(N-1)h}}[\mathsf{R}_{q}(\delta_{x}\boldsymbol{P}^{\mathsf{alg}}\mathbin{\|}\delta_{y}\boldsymbol{P})]\lesssim\frac{\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}}{h}+\beta^{2}dh^{2}q\,,

and furthermore that this bound has been improved to the
\mathsf{R}_{q}
divergence.

For (
DM–ULMC
), the work
[
ACZ25
]
considers a modified algorithm in which all but the final step uses DM–ULMC. They are unable to complete the proof using DM–ULMC alone; instead, one analyzes the composition of this algorithm with a single step of the standard, exponential Euler discretization (ULMC). On the other hand, Lemma
15
shows that cross-regularity in Lemma
19
holds with

\displaystyle(\bar{b})^{2}\lesssim\frac{\beta^{2}h^{3}}{\gamma}\Bigl(d+\mathsf{R}_{3}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{(N-1)h}\mathbin{\|}\boldsymbol{\pi})\Bigr)\,.

As generally
\mathsf{R}_{3}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{(N-1)h}\mathbin{\|}\boldsymbol{\pi})\lesssim d\log\kappa
for a reasonable initialization in our setting, then this gives a bound of

\displaystyle(\bar{b})^{2}\lesssim\frac{\beta^{2}dh^{3}}{\gamma}\log\kappa\,,

which is sufficient for the
O(d^{1/3})
bounds obtained in that paper. We again note that our cross-regularity is obtained in Rényi divergence rather than
\mathsf{KL}
. Since changing the algorithm in the last step is arguably unnatural, this result fills an important gap in their work.

Now, via Cauchy–Schwarz, if we consider the paths up to iteration

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}\log\E_{\bf P}

\displaystyle\exp\Bigl(N\int_{(k-1){h}}^{k{h}}\beta^{2}q\lVert X_{t}-X_{k}^{+}\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigr)

using the standard bounds on the supremum of a Gaussian process.

\displaystyle\mathrm{II}

\displaystyle\to-\frac{\sigma_{21}}{\Delta_{\sigma}}\biggl[\int_{0}^{h}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathcal{E}_{1}(s,h)\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,h)\mathcal{E}_{2}(s,t)}\nabla^{2}V(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t

Proof of Lemma

For simplicity, it suffices to take
k=0
and work with a single step. For this, we need only bound, with
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\coloneqq(\lambda_{0,1},\lambda_{0,2})
, and
\Lambda^{2}\coloneqq\lVert\lambda_{1}\rVert^{2}+{h}^{2}\lVert\lambda_{2}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{h}\lVert\mathcal{E}_{1}(t,{h})\lambda_{1}+\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,{h})\lambda_{2}\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\lesssim h\Lambda^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Bigl(\frac{{h}\sigma_{22}^{2}}{\Delta_{\sigma}^{2}}+\frac{{h}^{3}\sigma_{12}^{2}}{\Delta_{\sigma}^{2}}\Bigr)\lVert\int_{0}^{h}\mathcal{E}_{1}(t,{h})\nabla V(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t-\mathsf{G}^{\mathsf{p}}_{0}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\Bigl(\frac{{h}\sigma_{21}^{2}}{\Delta_{\sigma}^{2}}+\frac{{h}^{3}\sigma_{11}^{2}}{\Delta_{\sigma}^{2}}\Bigr)\lVert\int_{0}^{h}\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,{h})\nabla V(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t-\mathsf{G}^{\mathsf{x}}_{0}\rVert^{2}\,,

noting that these inequalities hold almost surely. For
{h}\lesssim 1
, it follows that
\sigma_{22}\lesssim{h}^{3}
,
\sigma_{12}=\sigma_{21}\lesssim{h}^{2}
,
\sigma_{11}\lesssim{h}
and
\Delta_{\sigma}\gtrsim{h}^{4}
, so that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{h}\lVert\mathcal{E}_{1}(t,{h})\lambda_{1}+\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,{h})\lambda_{2}\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{{h}}\lVert\int_{0}^{h}\mathcal{E}_{1}(t,{h})\nabla V(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t-\mathsf{G}^{\mathsf{p}}_{0}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{{h}^{3}}\lVert\int_{0}^{h}\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,{h})\nabla V(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t-\mathsf{G}^{\mathsf{x}}_{0}\rVert^{2}\,.

We call the first term the momentum error and the second term the position error.

Crude analysis:

We begin with the weaker bound which does not leverage higher order smoothness. Conditional on our initial point
(X_{0},P_{0})
,

\displaystyle\lVert\int_{0}^{h}\mathcal{E}_{1}(t,{h})\nabla V(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t-\mathsf{G}^{\mathsf{p}}_{0}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\beta^{2}{h}^{2}\Bigl\{\sup_{t\in[0,{h})}\lVert X_{t}-X_{\tau_{1}^{+}}\rVert^{2}+\lVert X_{\tau_{1}^{+}}-X^{+}\rVert^{2}\Bigr\}\,.

For the first term, we use
\sup_{t\in[0,h)}\lVert\mathsf{G}_{t}^{\mathsf{opt}}\rVert^{2}\lesssim\sup_{t\in[0,h)}\lVert\nabla V(X_{t})\rVert^{2}+\Lambda^{2}
,

\displaystyle\beta^{2}

\displaystyle{h}^{2}\sup_{t\in[0,{h})}\lVert X_{t}-X_{\tau_{1}^{+}}\rVert^{2}\lesssim{\beta^{2}{h}^{6}}\sup_{t\in[0,{h})}\lVert\mathsf{G}_{t}^{\mathsf{opt}}\rVert^{2}+{\beta^{2}{h}^{4}}\lVert P_{0}\rVert^{2}+{\beta^{2}\gamma{h}^{4}}\sup_{t\in[0,{h})}\lVert B_{t}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim{\beta^{2}{h}^{6}}\sup_{t\in[0,{h})}\lVert\nabla V(X_{t})\rVert^{2}+{\beta^{2}{h}^{6}\Lambda^{2}}+{\beta^{2}{h}^{4}}\lVert P_{0}\rVert^{2}+{\beta^{2}\gamma{h}^{4}}\sup_{t\in[0,{h})}\lVert B_{t}\rVert^{2}\,.

For the gradient term, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\nabla V(X_{t})\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lVert\nabla V(X_{0})\rVert^{2}+\beta^{2}\lVert X_{t}-X_{0}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lVert\nabla V(X_{0})\rVert^{2}+{\beta^{2}{h}^{4}}\sup_{t\in[0,{h})}\lVert\nabla V(X_{t})\rVert^{2}+{\beta^{2}{h}^{4}\Lambda^{2}}+{\beta^{2}{h}^{2}}\lVert P_{0}\rVert^{2}+{\beta^{2}\gamma{h}^{2}}\sup_{t\in[0,{h})}\lVert B_{t}\rVert^{2}\,.

Taking supremum over
t\in[0,{h})
, this shows after rearranging, if
{h}\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta}}
, that we have

\displaystyle\beta^{2}

\displaystyle{h}^{2}\sup_{s\in[0,{h})}\lVert X_{t}-X_{\tau_{1}^{+}}\rVert^{2}\lesssim{\beta^{2}{h}^{6}}\lVert\nabla V(X_{0})\rVert^{2}+{\beta^{2}{h}^{6}\Lambda^{2}}+{\beta^{2}{h}^{4}}\lVert P_{0}\rVert^{2}+{\beta^{2}\gamma{h}^{4}}\sup_{t\in[0,{h})}\lVert B_{t}\rVert^{2}\,.

For the other term, we have

\displaystyle\beta^{2}{h}^{2}\lVert X_{\tau_{1}^{+}}-X^{+}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim{\beta^{2}{h}^{2}}\lVert\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}^{+}}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{E}_{1}(s,t)\,\mathsf{G}_{s}^{\mathsf{opt}}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t-\mathcal{E}_{3}(0,\tau_{1}^{+})\nabla V(X_{0})\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim{\beta^{2}{h}^{6}\Lambda^{2}}+{\beta^{2}{h}^{2}}\lVert\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}^{+}}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{E}_{1}(s,t)\{\nabla V(X_{s})-\nabla V(X_{0})\}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\rVert^{2}\,.

We can then bound

\displaystyle\lVert\int_{0}^{{h}/2}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{E}_{1}(s,t)\{\nabla V(X_{s})-\nabla V(X_{0})\}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\beta^{2}{h}^{4}\sup_{t\in[0,{h})}\lVert X_{s}-X_{0}\rVert^{2}\lesssim{\beta^{2}{h}^{8}}\sup_{t\in[0,{h})}\lVert\mathsf{G}_{t}^{\mathsf{opt}}\rVert^{2}+\beta^{2}{h}^{6}\lVert P_{0}\rVert^{2}+\beta^{2}\gamma{h}^{6}\sup_{t\in[0,{h})}\lVert B_{t}\rVert^{2}\,.

Combining this with our earlier bounds on
\lVert\mathsf{G}_{t}^{\mathsf{opt}}\rVert^{2}
, we have for

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \beta^{2}{h}^{2}\lVert X_{\tau_{1}^{+}}-X^{+}\rVert^{2}\lesssim{\beta^{2}{h}^{6}\Lambda^{2}}+{\beta^{4}{h}^{10}}\lVert\nabla V(X_{0})\rVert^{2}+{\beta^{4}{h}^{8}}\lVert P_{0}\rVert^{2}+{\beta^{4}\gamma{h}^{8}}\sup_{t\in[0,{h})}\lVert B_{t}\rVert^{2}\,.\end{aligned}

(A.3)

We see that the bound on
\lVert X_{\tau_{1}^{+}}-X^{+}\rVert^{2}
will yield only higher order terms (in
{h}
) relative to the bounds we obtained for

\displaystyle\lVert\int_{0}^{h}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}(t,{h})\nabla V(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t-\mathsf{G}^{\mathsf{p}}_{0}\rVert^{2}\lesssim{\beta^{2}{h}^{4}}\Bigl\{{{h}^{2}\lVert\nabla V(X_{0})\rVert^{2}}+{{h}^{2}\Lambda^{2}}+{\lVert P_{0}\rVert^{2}}+\gamma\sup_{t\in[0,{h})}\lVert B_{t}\rVert^{2}\Bigr\}\,.

The position error is handled in almost the exact same way, but gains two orders of

due to

being present in place of

\displaystyle\lVert\int_{0}^{h}\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,{h})\nabla V(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t-\mathsf{G}^{\mathsf{x}}_{0}\rVert^{2}

and both of these terms are handled in the exact same way.
Thus, we can bound

\displaystyle\Lambda^{2}\lesssim{\beta^{2}{h}^{4}}\lVert\nabla V(X_{0})\rVert^{2}+{\beta^{2}{h}^{2}}\lVert P_{0}\rVert^{2}+{\beta^{2}\gamma{h}^{2}}\sup_{t\in[0,{h})}\lVert B_{t}\rVert^{2}\,.

Substituting this into our Girsanov bound and using the standard supremum bounds on the Brownian motion (Lemma

) tells us that, using AM–GM (or equivalent Cauchy–Schwarz), for some absolute constant

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \log&\exp\Bigl(\int_{0}^{h}\frac{q^{2}}{\gamma}\lVert\mathcal{E}_{1}(t,{h})\lambda_{1}+\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,{h})\lambda_{2}\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigr)\\
&\lesssim\log\E_{\mathbf{P}}\exp\Bigl(\frac{C\beta^{2}{h}^{5}q^{2}}{\gamma}\lVert\nabla V(X_{0})\rVert^{2}\Bigr)+\log\E_{\mathbf{P}}\exp\Bigl(\frac{C\beta^{2}{h}^{3}q^{2}}{\gamma}\lVert P_{0}\rVert^{2}\Bigr)+{\beta^{2}d{h}^{4}q^{2}}\,.\end{aligned}

(A.4)

Proof of Lemma

Throughout the present proof, we assume that
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{c})
for some absolute constant

. Recall that if

is obtained from (

) after time

, and

\displaystyle\lesssim\lVert\int_{0}^{h}\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,{h})\{\nabla V(X^{-})-\nabla V(X_{t})\}\,\mathrm{d}t\rVert^{2}\,,

\displaystyle\lVert P_{h}-P_{h}^{\mathsf{alg}}\rVert^{2}

We can bound, noting that

\displaystyle\lVert\int_{0}^{h}\int_{{h}/2}^{t}(\mathcal{E}_{1}(t,{h})-1)\nabla^{2}V(X_{s})P_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\rVert^{2}

With probability
1-\delta

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}

By symmetrizing the integral, we find for the other term,

\displaystyle\lVert\int_{0}^{h}\int_{{h}/2}^{t}\nabla^{2}V(X_{s})P_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert\int_{{h}/2}^{h}\int_{{h}/2}^{t}\{\nabla^{2}V(X_{s})P_{s}-\nabla^{2}V(X_{{h}-s})P_{{h}-s}\}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert\int_{{h}/2}^{h}\int_{{h}/2}^{t}\int_{{h}-s}^{s}\,\mathrm{d}\{\nabla^{2}V(X_{r})P_{r}\}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim{h}^{2}\int_{{h}/2}^{h}\int_{{h}/2}^{t}\lVert\int_{{h}-s}^{s}\,\mathrm{d}\{\nabla^{2}V(X_{r})P_{r}\}\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\,.

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{II}+\mathrm{III}+\mathrm{IV}\,.

, where

(A.7)

An Itô–Taylor expansion gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{h}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,{h})\nabla V(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{{h}}\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,{h})\nabla V(X_{{h}/3})\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{h}\int_{{h}/3}^{t}\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,{h})\nabla^{2}V(X_{s})P_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\,.

Now, noting that
\lvert\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,{h})-({h}-t)\rvert\lesssim\gamma{h}^{2}
, and
\lVert\nabla^{2}V\rVert\lesssim\beta
, then we have by Cauchy–Schwarz,

\displaystyle\lVert\int_{0}^{h}

\displaystyle\int_{{h}/3}^{t}\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,{h})\nabla^{2}V(X_{s})P_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lVert\int_{0}^{h}\int_{{h}/3}^{t}({h}-t)\nabla^{2}V(X_{s})P_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\rVert^{2}+\lVert\int_{0}^{h}\int_{{h}/3}^{t}(\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,{h})-({h}-t))\nabla^{2}V(X_{s})P_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\rVert^{2}\,.

We can bound

\displaystyle\lVert\int_{0}^{h}

\displaystyle\int_{{h}/3}^{t}(\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,{h})-({h}-t))\nabla^{2}V(X_{s})P_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim{h}^{2}\int_{0}^{h}\int_{{h}/3}^{t}\lVert(\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,{h})-({h}-t))\nabla^{2}V(X_{s})P_{s}\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\lesssim\beta^{2}\gamma^{2}{h}^{6}\int_{0}^{h}\int_{{h}/3}^{t}\lVert P_{s}\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\,.

With probability
1-\delta
, we know that if
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}
has
\mathsf{R}_{q}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}\mathbin{\|}\boldsymbol{\pi})\lesssim d^{1/2}
for
q\geq 2
, then under
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t}
for all
t\geq 0
, we have with probability
1-\delta
via Lemma
31
,

\displaystyle\beta^{2}\gamma^{2}{h}^{6}\int_{0}^{h}\int_{{h}/3}^{t}\lVert P_{s}\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\lesssim\beta^{2}\gamma^{2}d{h}^{8}+\beta^{2}\gamma^{2}{h}^{8}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\,.

By symmetrizing the integral, we find, noting that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{h}\int_{{h}/3}^{t}({h}-t)\nabla^{2}V(X_{h/3})P_{h/3}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t=0\,,

then we have, expanding
\nabla^{2}V(X_{s})P_{s}
around
{h}/3
,

\displaystyle\lVert\int_{0}^{h}\int_{{h}/3}^{t}({h}-t)\nabla^{2}V(X_{s})P_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert\int_{0}^{h}\int_{{h}/3}^{t}\int_{{h}/3}^{s}({h}-t)\,\mathrm{d}[\nabla^{2}V(X_{r})P_{r}]\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim{h}^{4}\int_{0}^{h}\int_{{h}/3}^{t}\lVert\int_{{h}/3}^{s}\,\mathrm{d}[\nabla^{2}V(X_{r})P_{r}]\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\,.

We now again apply Itô’s lemma and change expectations to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
. All the terms are handled in a similar way to momentum error, gaining an extra two powers of
{h}
as we have
\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,{h})
in place of
\mathcal{E}_{1}(t,{h})
. We omit the calculations as they are straightforward. However, we note that we obtain the following intermediate result,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \lVert\int_{0}^{h}\int_{{h}/3}^{t}\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,{h})\nabla^{2}V(X_{s})P_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\rVert^{2}&\lesssim\beta^{2}\gamma d{h}^{7}+{(\beta_{H}^{2}+\beta_{\mathsf{eff}}^{2})d{h}^{8}}\\
&\qquad+\Bigl({\beta_{\mathsf{eff}}^{2}d^{1/2}{h}^{8}}+\beta^{2}\gamma{h}^{7}\Bigr)\log\frac{1}{\delta}+{\beta_{\mathsf{eff}}^{2}{h}^{8}}\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}\,.\end{aligned}

(A.9)

Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\int_{0}^{{h}}\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,{h})\nabla V(X_{{h}/3})\,\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{{h}}\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,{h})\nabla V(X^{-})\,\mathrm{d}t\rVert^{2}\lesssim\beta^{2}{h}^{4}\lVert X_{{h}/3}-X^{-}\rVert^{2}\,.

This is bounded entirely analogous to the momentum errors. Thus, we find for the position strong errors

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \lVert\int_{0}^{h}\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,{h})\{\nabla V(X^{-})-\nabla V(X_{t})\}\,\mathrm{d}t\rVert^{2}&\lesssim\beta^{2}\gamma h^{7}\Bigl(d+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\Bigr)+{(\beta_{H}^{2}+\beta_{\mathsf{eff}}^{2})d{h}^{8}}\\
&\qquad+{\beta_{\mathsf{eff}}^{2}d^{1/2}{h}^{8}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}+{\beta_{\mathsf{eff}}^{2}{h}^{8}}\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}\,.\end{aligned}

(A.10)

The position weak errors are similarly handled, giving

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \lVert\int_{0}^{h}\mathcal{E}_{2}(t,{h})\{\E\nabla V(X^{-})-\E\nabla V(X_{t})\}\,\mathrm{d}t\rVert^{2}&\lesssim{\beta^{3}{h}^{8}}\Bigl(d+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\Bigr)+{(\beta_{H}^{2}+\beta_{\mathsf{eff}}^{2})d{h}^{8}}\\
&\qquad+{\beta_{\mathsf{eff}}^{2}d^{1/2}{h}^{8}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}+{\beta_{\mathsf{eff}}^{2}{h}^{8}}\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}\,.\end{aligned}

(A.11)

∎

\displaystyle\lVert\int_{0}^{h}\int_{{h}/2}^{t}\nabla^{2}V(X_{s})P_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\rVert^{2}

A.4
Existence and approximation for DM–ULMC

We state an existence result for our application of interest.

Lemma 24
.

Consider the implicit equation which gives
X_{[0,h]}\in L^{2}_{\bf P}([0,h];\mathbb{R}^{d})
in terms of
B_{[0,h]}
through (
DM–ULMC
) with
N=1
,
k=0
.
For the discrete approximation (
A.1
), the transform
\mathsf{T}
is invertible if
h\lesssim\frac{1}{\beta^{1/2}}
for a sufficiently small absolute constant, and the approximate solution converges to a solution
X_{t}
of (
DM–ULMC
) in
L^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}([0,h];\mathbb{R}^{d})
, in the sense that
\lVert X-\hat{X}^{\eta}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}([0,h];\mathbb{R}^{d})}\to 0
. Furthermore, a solution to (
DM–ULMC
) exists and is unique.

Proof.

We will keep the realizations of
X_{0}=x_{0}
and
B_{[0,h]}
fixed throughout this argument. First, we show that (
DM–ULMC
) has a unique solution using generic principles. Define
F(X_{[0,h]})
to be the right side of (
DM–ULMC
), or more precisely, let
F(X_{[0,h]})\in{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}
with

\displaystyle F_{t}(X_{[0,h]})=X_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{2}(0,t)P_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{E}_{2}(s,t)\mathsf{G}_{s}^{\mathsf{opt}}(X_{[0,h]})\,\mathrm{d}s+\sqrt{2\gamma}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{E}_{2}(s,t)\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}\,.

Indeed, if
h
is sufficiently small, consider two candidate solutions
X_{[0,h]}
,
\bar{X}_{[0,h]}
for the fixed point equation with the same initial starting point,
X_{0}=\bar{X}_{0}
. Letting
P_{[0,h]},\bar{P}_{[0,h]}
be the corresponding momentum processes, recall that
P
should solve its own fixed point equation (viewing
P_{[0,h]}
as deterministic given
X_{[0,h]}
and
B_{[0,h]}
),

\displaystyle P_{t}=\mathcal{E}_{1}(0,t)P_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{E}_{1}(s,t)\mathsf{G}_{s}^{\mathsf{opt}}(X_{[0,h]})\,\mathrm{d}s+\sqrt{2\gamma}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{E}_{1}(s,t)\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}\,.

we have, using that

\displaystyle\lVert\mathsf{G}_{[0,h]}^{\mathsf{opt}}(X_{[0,h]})-\mathsf{G}_{[0,h]}^{\mathsf{opt}}(\bar{X}_{[0,h]})\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\lVert\nabla V(X)_{[0,h]}-\nabla V(\bar{X})_{[0,h]}\rVert^{2}_{{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}}\,,

from which we get

\displaystyle\lVert P_{[0,h]}-\bar{P}_{[0,h]}\rVert^{2}_{{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}}

\displaystyle\lesssim{h^{2}}\lVert\nabla V(X)_{[0,h]}-\nabla V(\bar{X})_{[0,h]}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\lesssim{\beta^{2}h^{2}}\lVert X_{[0,h]}-\bar{X}_{[0,h]}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\lVert X_{[0,h]}-\bar{X}_{[0,h]}\rVert^{2}_{{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}}

\displaystyle\lesssim{h^{2}}\lVert P_{[0,h]}-\bar{P}_{[0,h]}\rVert^{2}_{{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}}\,.

Putting these together says that if
h\lesssim\frac{1}{\beta^{1/2}}
for a small enough implied constant, then
F
is a contraction on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with constant
c<1/2
, and furthermore, via a fixed point theorem, that the solution must be unique.
As a result, denote by
(X^{*}_{[0,h]},P^{*}_{[0,h]})
the solution to (
DM–ULMC
).

We also show the following stability result: for
X_{[0,T]}\in L^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}([0,h];\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
X_{0}=x_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert F(X_{[0,h]})-X_{[0,h]}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\lVert X_{[0,h]}-X_{[0,h]}^{*}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}-\lVert F(X_{[0,h]})-F(X_{[0,h]}^{*})\rVert^{2}_{{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\lVert X_{[0,h]}-X_{[0,h]}^{*}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}-c\lVert X_{[0,h]}-X_{[0,h]}^{*}\rVert^{2}_{{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}}\,,

where
c<1/2
is the implied contraction constant from earlier. As a result, we obtain

\displaystyle\lVert F(X_{[0,h]})-X_{[0,h]}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\lVert X_{[0,h]}-X_{[0,h]}^{*}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,.

For the second part of the claim, we proceed by a direct analysis. Define
F_{\eta}(X_{[0,h]})
to be the right side of the approximation in (
A.1
). For processes
\hat{X}_{[0,h]}^{\eta}
which are constants on intervals
[k\eta,(k+1)\eta)
, by comparison of (
A.1
) and (

), we get

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \lVert F(\hat{X}^{\eta}_{[0,h]}&)-F_{\eta}(\hat{X}^{\eta}_{[0,h]})\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
&\lesssim{\gamma h^{3}}\sup_{k\in[h/\eta]}\sup_{s\in[0,\eta]}\lVert B_{k\eta+s}-B_{k\eta}\rVert^{2}+{h^{2}}\cdot\eta^{2}\lVert P_{0}\rVert^{2}+{h^{3}}\cdot\eta^{2}\lVert\nabla V(\hat{X}^{\eta})_{[0,h]}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,.\end{aligned}

(A.12)

It remains to ensure that

is bounded as
\eta\to 0
.

We now prove this bound.

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{X}_{[0,h]}^{\eta}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim h\lVert X_{0}\rVert^{2}+{h^{3}}\lVert P_{0}\rVert^{2}+{h^{4}}\lVert\hat{\mathsf{G}}_{[0,h]}^{\mathsf{opt}}(\hat{X}_{[0,h]}^{\eta})\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\gamma h^{3}\sup_{t\in[0,h]}\lVert B_{t}\rVert^{2}\,.

(A.13)

Furthermore, we can bound very crudely (along the lines of the proof of Lemma
13

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{\mathsf{G}}_{[0,h]}^{\mathsf{opt}}(\hat{X}_{[0,h]}^{\eta})\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lVert\nabla V(\hat{X}^{\eta})_{[0,h]}\rVert^{2}_{{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lVert\nabla V(\hat{X}^{\eta})_{[0,h]}-\nabla V(0)_{[0,h]}\rVert^{2}_{{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}}+\lVert\nabla V(0)_{[0,h]}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}

into the left side of (

Thus, we have, using stability and invariance of

under

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{X}^{\eta}_{[0,h]}-X^{*}_{[0,h]}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lVert F(\hat{X}^{\eta}_{[0,h]})-\hat{X}^{\eta}_{[0,h]}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}

The following can be found as an intermediate bound in the proof of

Lemma 26

(Moments of a Gaussian chaos)
.

Suppose
H
is a 3-tensor with operator norm bounded by, for
\beta_{H},\beta_{T}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\displaystyle\lVert H\rVert_{\{1,2\},\{3\}}\leq\beta_{H}\,,\qquad\lVert H\rVert_{\{1,2,3\}}\coloneqq\sqrt{\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}H_{i,j,k}^{2}}\leq\beta_{T}

Then, if
\xi
is a standard Gaussian,
Z\coloneqq\lVert H[\xi,\xi]\rVert_{2}^{2}
, then we have

\displaystyle\E[\lvert Z-\E Z\rvert^{p}]^{\frac{1}{p}}\lesssim\beta_{T}^{2}p^{2}+(\beta_{T}^{2}+\beta_{H}^{2}d^{1/2})p\,,

where the implicit constant does not depend on
p,\beta_{H},\beta_{T},d
.

We upgrade this into a bound on the tail probabilities of the Gaussian chaos.

Lemma 27

(Concentration of a Gaussian chaos)
.

Suppose
H
is a 3-tensor with norms bounded as

\displaystyle\lVert H\rVert_{\{1,2\},\{3\}}\leq\beta_{H}\,,\qquad\lVert H\rVert_{\{1,2,3\}}\leq\beta_{T}\,.

Then, if
\xi\sim N(0,I_{d})
is a standard Gaussian, we have for an absolute constant
c>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{\lvert\lVert H[\xi,\xi]\rVert^{2}-\E\lVert H[\xi,\xi]\rVert^{2}\rvert\geq t\}\leq 2\exp\Bigl(-c\min\Bigl\{\sqrt{\frac{t}{\beta_{T}^{2}}},\frac{t}{\beta_{H}^{2}d^{1/2}+\beta_{T}^{2}}\Bigr\}\Bigr)\,.

Proof.

Let
Z=\lVert H[\xi,\xi]\rVert^{2}
. Using Chebyshev’s inequality and Lemma
26
, we find that,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{\lvert Z-\E Z\rvert\geq t\}\leq\frac{(C\max\{(\beta_{H}^{2}d^{1/2}+\beta_{T}^{2})p,\beta_{T}^{2}p^{2}\})^{p}}{t^{p}}\,.

Here,
C
is an absolute constant which we can assume
\geq 1
without loss of generality.
When
t\leq\frac{2C(\beta_{T}^{2}+\beta_{H}^{2}\sqrt{d})^{2}}{\beta_{T}^{2}}
, take
p=\frac{t}{2C(\beta_{T}^{2}+\beta_{H}^{2}\sqrt{d})}
and use the first term in the maximum to find the bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{\lvert Z-\E Z\rvert\geq t\}\leq\exp\Bigl(-\frac{ct}{\beta_{H}^{2}d^{1/2}+\beta_{T}^{2}}\Bigr)\,.

Otherwise, if
t>\frac{2C(\beta_{T}^{2}+\beta_{H}^{2}\sqrt{d})^{2}}{\beta_{T}^{2}}
, setting
p=\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\sqrt{2C}\beta_{T}}
we use the second term in the maximum to find

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{\lvert Z-\E Z\rvert\geq t\}\leq\exp\Bigl(-\frac{c\sqrt{t}}{\beta_{T}}\Bigr)\,.

Putting these two together yields the bound.
∎

\lambda<\frac{1}{2t}

\displaystyle\E\exp\Bigl(\lambda\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\lVert B_{s}\rVert^{2}\Bigr)\leq\Bigl(\frac{1+2\lambda t}{1-2\lambda t}\Bigr)^{d}\,.

Lemma 29

(Rényi weak triangle inequality, adapted from
[
Che24
, Lemma 6.2.5]
)
.

For
q>1
,
\lambda\in(0,1)
, and measures
\mu,\nu,\pi
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)\leq\frac{q-\lambda}{q-1}\mathsf{R}_{q/\lambda}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)+\mathsf{R}_{(q-\lambda)/(1-\lambda)}(\nu\mathbin{\|}\pi)\,,

and in particular we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)\leq\frac{q-1/2}{q-1}\mathsf{R}_{2q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)+\mathsf{R}_{2q-1}(\nu\mathbin{\|}\pi)\,.

For
\mathsf{KL}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)\leq 2\mathsf{KL}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)+\log\bigl(1+\chi^{2}(\nu\mathbin{\|}\pi)\bigr)\,.

Lemma 30

(Score concentration, adapted from
[
Che24
, Lemma 6.2.7]
)
.

Let
\pi\propto\exp(-V)
and assume that
\nabla V
is
\beta
-Lipschitz. Then
\nabla V
is
\sqrt{\beta}
-subgaussian under
\pi
, such that for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\E_{\pi}\exp{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\nabla V,v}}\right\rangle}\leq\exp\Bigl(\frac{\beta\lVert v\rVert^{2}}{2}\Bigr)\,.

In particular, for
0<\delta<1/2
, with probability at least
1-\delta
under
\pi
,

\displaystyle\lVert\nabla V\rVert^{2}\lesssim\beta d+\beta\log\frac{1}{\delta}\,.

Lemma 31

(Rényi change of measure, adapted from
[
Che24
, Lemma 6.2.8]
)
.

Suppose that under two measures
\mu,\pi
, and a test function
\phi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\pi\{\phi\geq{h}\}\leq\psi({h})\,,

for some growth function
\psi
. Then,

\displaystyle\mu\{\phi\geq{h}\}\leq\psi({h})^{(q-1)/q}\exp\Bigl(\frac{q-1}{q}\mathsf{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi)\Bigr)\,.

We will make repeated use of the following helper lemma.

Lemma 32

(Sub-Weibull change of measure)
.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}
satisfies
\mathsf{R}_{q}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}\mathbin{\|}\boldsymbol{\pi})\lesssim d^{1/2}
for
q\geq 2
. Then, if under
(X,P)\sim\boldsymbol{\pi}
, we have for some measurable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}
with probability
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle f(X,P)\lesssim\mathsf{C}_{1}d+\mathsf{C}_{2}d^{1/2}\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\mathsf{C}_{3}\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}\,,

Then under
(X,P)\sim\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t}
following (
ULD
) starting from
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}
with target
\boldsymbol{\pi}
for all
t\leq{h}
, we have with the same probability

\displaystyle f(X,P)\lesssim\max\{\mathsf{C}_{1},\mathsf{C}_{2},\mathsf{C}_{3}\}d+\mathsf{C}_{2}d^{1/2}\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\mathsf{C}_{3}\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}\,.

Proof.

This is straightforward from Lemma
31
, noting that
\mathsf{R}_{q}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t}\mathbin{\|}\boldsymbol{\pi})\leq\mathsf{R}_{q}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}\mathbin{\|}\boldsymbol{\pi})
via the data-processing inequality, as both arguments evolve along (
ULD
) and
\boldsymbol{\pi}
is stationary under (
ULD
).
∎

Lemma 33

(Linearization of
\log\det
)
.

Consider
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with
\lVert M\rVert_{\operatorname{op}}<1
. Then, we have the expansion

\displaystyle\log\det(I_{d}+M)=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}(M)-\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}(M^{2})+O(\lVert M\rVert^{3}_{\operatorname{op}}\,d)\,.

Proof.

This can be obtained by Taylor expansion of
t\mapsto\log\det(I_{d}+tM)
, which is the logarithm of a polynomial in
t
and hence analytic.
∎
