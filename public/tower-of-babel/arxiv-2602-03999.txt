Functional Stochastic Localization
Anming Gu, Bobby Shi, Kevin Tian

Abstract
Eldan’s stochastic localization is a probabilistic construction that has proved instrumental to modern breakthroughs in high-dimensional geometry and the design of sampling algorithms. Motivated by sampling under non-Euclidean geometries and the mirror descent algorithm in optimization, we develop a functional generalization of Eldan’s process that replaces Gaussian regularization with regularization by any positive integer multiple of a log-Laplace transform. We further give a mixing time bound on the Markov chain induced by our localization process, which holds if our target distribution satisfies a functional Poincaré inequality. Finally, we apply our framework to differentially private convex optimization in ℓ p \ell_{p} norms for p ∈ [ 1 , 2 ) p\in[1,2) , where we improve state-of-the-art query complexities in a zeroth-order model.

Functional stochastic localization.

A key feature of stochastic localization is that it primarily lends itself to tools tailored to the Euclidean geometry. This is unsurprising: Gaussians are induced by a (Euclidean) quadratic potential

As discussed previously, the ideas underpinning localization schemes were first introduced as Eldan’s stochastic localization

Renormalization.

Renormalization is yet another perspective on the derivation of functional inequalities, developed in the physics literature
[
92
,
82
]
; for a recent survey, see
[
10
]
. The formalism associates with a measure a flow of measures in terms of a renormalized potential; this structure can be used to prove functional inequalities under a multiscale generalization of the Bakry-Émery criterion. In fact, this perspective is dual to stochastic localization: at a high level, stochastic localization focuses on the stochastic evolution of a random measure, while renormalization focuses on a Markov process and its associated renormalized potential. This framework has been successively employed to derive log-Sobolev inequalities in discrete systems, including in Euclidean field theory
[
67
,
83
]
and in Ising models
[
11
,
12
]
. We draw upon this literature in Section

\operatorname{Var}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Var}[Y\mid X]]+\operatorname{Var}[\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]].

Log-Laplace transforms.

A central object in our development is the log-Laplace transform.

Definition 1
.

The
log-Laplace transform (LLT)
of
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as

\varphi^{\sharp}(\mathbf{x}):=\log\left(\int\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right\rangle-\varphi(\mathbf{y})\right)\textup{d}\mathbf{y}\right).

We will typically use
\psi:=\varphi^{\sharp}
as shorthand when
\varphi
is fixed.

For a measure
\nu
, define the tilted probability density at
\mathbf{a}
as

\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{a}}\nu(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{\exp(\left\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x}\right\rangle)\nu(\mathbf{x})}{\int\exp(\left\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{z}\right\rangle)\nu(\mathbf{z})\,\textup{d}\mathbf{z}}.

(4)

In this way,

\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{x}}\exp(-\varphi(\mathbf{y}))=\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right\rangle-\varphi(\mathbf{y})-\psi(\mathbf{x})\right),

(5)

which is indeed a probability density as the normalization constant is exactly given by
\exp(-\psi)
. More generally, if
f
is any integrable nonnegative function over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we let
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{x}}f
be the density that is
\propto\exp(\left\langle\mathbf{x},\cdot\right\rangle)f
, where we use

to indicate proportionality.

Lemma 1

\mu=\exp(-\varphi)

This is a straightforward calculation: for all
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\chi^{\sharp}(\mathbf{w})

\displaystyle=\log\left(\int\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}\right\rangle-\chi(\mathbf{y})\right)\textup{d}\mathbf{y}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\int\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}\right\rangle\right)\pi(\mathbf{y})\textup{d}\mathbf{y}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\int\left(\int\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z}\right\rangle\right)\nu(\mathbf{z})\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{z}\right\rangle\right)\mu(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{z})\textup{d}\mathbf{z}\right)\textup{d}\mathbf{y}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\int\left(\int\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{z}\right\rangle\right)\mu(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{z})\textup{d}\mathbf{y}\right)\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z}\right\rangle\right)\nu(\mathbf{z})\textup{d}\mathbf{z}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\int\left(\int\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}\right\rangle\right)\mu(\mathbf{y})\textup{d}\mathbf{y}\right)\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z}\right\rangle\right)\nu(\mathbf{z})\textup{d}\mathbf{z}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\int\left(\int\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}\right\rangle-\phi(\mathbf{y})\right)\textup{d}\mathbf{y}\right)\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z}\right\rangle-\varphi(\mathbf{z})\right)\textup{d}\mathbf{z}\right)=\phi^{\sharp}(\mathbf{w})+\varphi^{\sharp}(\mathbf{w}).

The first two lines are definitions, the third expands
\pi=\mu\ast\nu
, the fourth applies Fubini’s theorem, the fifth performs a change of variables, and the last again applies definitions.
∎

Lemma
1
will prove particularly useful to us when convolving multiple copies of a density with itself. For
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\pi=\exp(-\varphi)
is a density, and
\tau\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
\varphi^{\ast\tau}
to be such that
\exp(-\varphi^{\ast\tau})=\pi\ast\ldots\ast\pi
,
\tau
times in total. Then, repeatedly applying Lemma
1
gives

\left(\varphi^{\ast\tau}\right)^{\sharp}=\tau\psi.

Our framework in Section
3
only relies on Lemma
1
through the above fact, so we always assume that
\int\exp(-\varphi(\mathbf{x}))\textup{d}\mathbf{x}=1
without loss of generality, so that we need not carry around normalizing factor adjustments. This can always be achieved by shifting
\varphi
by a constant, which does not change any of the densities used by our Markov chains (e.g., Algorithm
2
), nor the starting assumptions of any of our main results (e.g., Theorem
1
and Corollary
2
).

Localization processes.

We introduce localization processes, primarily following the expositions of
[
23
,
84
]
.

Definition 2
.

A
localization process
is a measure-valued stochastic process
\left\{\pi_{t}\right\}_{t\geq 0}
on a state space
\Omega
, which satisfies the following properties:

(1)

\pi_{t}
is a probability measure of
\Omega
for all
t
, almost surely.

(2)

For all
A\subseteq\Omega
,
t\mapsto\pi_{t}(A)
is a martingale.

(3)

For all
A\subseteq\Omega
,
\lim_{t\to\infty}\pi_{t}(A)\in\left\{0,1\right\}
, almost surely.

A
localization scheme
maps
\pi
, a measure over
\Omega
, to a localization process
\left\{\pi_{t}\right\}_{t\geq 0}
with initial condition
\pi_{0}=\pi
. Further, the
localization dynamics
associated with the localization process and some fixed time
T>0
is the Markov chain with transition kernel defined by

\mathsf{p}_{T}^{\pi}(A\mid\omega)=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\pi_{T}(\omega)\pi_{T}(A)}{\pi(\omega)}\right],\quad for\,all\,\omega\in\Omega,\,A\subseteq\Omega.

(6)

A discrete-time localization process indexed by time
\tau
can be viewed as a continuous time localization process that is constant on
[\tau,\tau+1)
.

Fact 3
.

For any
T>0
,
\pi
is reversible and stationary for (
6
).

Proof.

By property (2) of Definition
2
, it holds
\mathbb{E}[\pi_{T}]=\pi
, which implies for
\pi
-almost every
\omega\in\Omega
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\pi_{T}(\omega)\pi_{T}(\Omega)}{\pi(\omega)}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\pi_{T}(\omega)}{\pi(\omega)}\right]=1.

Thus,
\mathsf{p}_{T}^{\pi}(\cdot\mid\omega)
is a probability density. For every
A,B\subseteq\Omega
, by Fubini’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\int_{B}\mathsf{p}_{T}^{\pi}(A\mid\omega)\pi(\textup{d}\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{B}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\pi_{T}(\omega)\pi_{T}(A)}{\pi(\omega)}\right]\pi(\textup{d}\omega)=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{B}\frac{\pi_{T}(\omega)\pi_{T}(A)}{\pi(\omega)}\pi(\textup{d}\omega)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\pi_{T}(A)\pi_{T}(B)\right]=\int_{A}\mathsf{p}_{T}^{\pi}(B\mid\omega)\pi(\textup{d}\omega),

so that the Markov chain is reversible and stationary.
∎

This immediately implies that every localization scheme induces an unbiased sampling algorithm.

Isoperimetric and covariance inequalities.

We require a few additional definitions, centered around isoperimetric inequalities in a metric defined locally by a convex function
\varphi
.

Definition 3

(
\varphi
-PI)
.

Let
\pi\propto\exp(-V)
for
V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, and let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be convex. We say that
\pi
satisfies a
\varphi
-Poincaré inequality with constant
\alpha
(abbreviated as
\alpha
-
\varphi
-PI) if, for all smooth functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\textup{Var}_{\pi}[f]\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\left\lVert\nabla f\right\rVert_{(\nabla^{2}\varphi)^{-1}}^{2}\right].

This is also called a mirror Poincaré inequality in the literature if
\varphi
is a mirror map. If
\varphi=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
(so that
\nabla^{2}\varphi
is the identity), this recovers the standard PI.

Fact 4

(Brascamp-Lieb)
.

If
\pi
is
\alpha
-strongly log-concave with respect to
\varphi
, i.e.,
-\log\pi\succeq\alpha\varphi
, then
\pi
satisfies
\alpha
-
\varphi
-PI.

Similar to
\alpha
-
\varphi
-PI, we also respectively abbreviate
\alpha
-
\varphi
-strongly log-concave.

Fact 5

(Cramér-Rao)
.

If
\pi\propto\exp(-V)
for
V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbf{Cov}_{X\sim\pi}(X)\succeq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\pi}[\nabla^{2}V(X)]^{-1}
.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose
\pi=\pi_{1}*\pi_{2}
, where
\pi_{i}
satisfies an
\alpha_{i}
-
\varphi
-PI,
i=1,2
. Then
\pi
satisfies an
\varphi
-PI with constant

\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}^{-1}+\alpha_{2}^{-1}}.

Proof.

Let
X\sim\pi_{1}
,
Y\sim\pi_{2}
be independent. Then by subadditivity of the variance,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}[f]=\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}[f(X+Y)]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{1}}[f(X+Y)\mid X]+\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{2}}[f(X+Y)\mid Y]

\displaystyle\leq(\alpha_{1}^{-1}+\alpha_{2}^{-1})\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\nabla f(X+Y)\right\rVert^{2}_{(\nabla^{2}\varphi)^{-1}}\right],

which concludes the proof.
∎

Joint distribution induced by the LLT.

Using Definition
1
and Lemma
1
, we obtain a joint density corresponding to a target distribution
\pi
. Fix
\tau\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, and define
\tilde{\pi}_{(\tau)}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})
as

\tilde{\pi}_{(\tau)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right\rangle-\varphi^{*\tau}(\mathbf{y})-\tau\psi(\mathbf{x})\right)\pi(\mathbf{x}).

(7)

It is immediate that
\pi=\tilde{\pi}_{(\tau)}^{X}
.

The joint density
\tilde{\pi}_{(\tau)}
will be our primary object of study. In Section
3
, we show that the process obtained from taking
\tilde{\pi}_{(\tau)}^{X\mid Y=\mathbf{y}_{\tau}}
with
\mathbf{y}_{\tau}\sim\tilde{\pi}_{(\tau)}^{Y}
is a discrete-time localization process, where
\tau
indexes time. In Section
4
, we prove general statements about two-state Gibbs sampling and compare these to the localization-based results from
[
23
]
. In Section
5
, we combine the two perspectives to prove rapid mixing for the proximal sampler induced by
\tilde{\pi}_{(\tau)}
.

3
Functional Stochastic Localization

Write
\pi_{\tau}^{\mathbf{y}}:=\tilde{\pi}_{(\tau)}^{X\mid Y=\mathbf{y}}
. We construct a discrete-time stochastic localization process via the following:

\mathbf{x}\sim\pi,\quad\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{\tau}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{x}}\exp(-\varphi),\quad\mathbf{y}_{\tau}:=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mathbf{a}_{i},\quad\pi_{\tau}:=\pi_{\tau}^{\mathbf{y}_{\tau}}=\tilde{\pi}_{(\tau)}^{X\mid Y=\mathbf{y}_{\tau}},

(8)

with
\mathbf{y}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
, so that
\mathbf{y}_{\tau}
is a noisy observation of
\mathbf{x}
. Intuitively, as
\tau\to\infty
, we receive more signal about
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{x}}\exp(-\varphi)
, so (
8
) has a natural interpretation as denoising a sample
\mathbf{x}\sim\pi
. Indeed, taking
\varphi=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
, the process (
8
) exactly reduces to the noisy Gaussian channel studied by
[
3
]
.

Note that
\mathbf{a}_{i}\mid\mathbf{x}\sim\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{x}\right\rangle-\varphi(\mathbf{a}_{i})-\psi(\mathbf{x})\right)
, so by Lemma
1
,

\mathbf{y}_{\tau}\mid\mathbf{x}\sim\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{y}_{\tau},\mathbf{x}\right\rangle-\varphi^{*\tau}(\mathbf{y}_{\tau})-\tau\psi(\mathbf{x})\right).

Then

\tilde{\pi}_{(\tau)}^{Y}(\mathbf{y})=\int\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}\right\rangle-\varphi^{*\tau}(\mathbf{y})-\tau\psi(\mathbf{x})\right)\pi(\textup{d}\mathbf{x}).

As stated, (
8
) produces
\mathbf{y}_{\tau}
via
\tau
draws from a “shared sample”
\mathbf{x}
, which appears to be a history-dependent process. We give a convenient reformulation of (
8
) that shows it is Markov.

Lemma 3
.

The process
\{\mathbf{y}_{\tau}\}_{\tau\geq 0}
is Markov.

Proof.

Given
\mathbf{y}_{\tau}\sim\tilde{\pi}_{(\tau)}^{Y}
, we claim that we can generate
\mathbf{y}_{\tau+1}
with marginal density
\tilde{\pi}_{(\tau+1)}^{Y}
as follows: draw
\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{y}_{\tau}}\exp(-\tau\psi)\pi
and
\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{z}}\exp(-\varphi)
, and set
\mathbf{y}_{\tau+1}=\mathbf{y}_{\tau}+\mathbf{w}
(Algorithm
1
).

This process is clearly Markov. It remains to show
\mathbf{y}_{\tau+1}\sim\tilde{\pi}_{(\tau+1)}^{Y}
. Indeed, consider the joint density over
(\mathbf{y}_{\tau},\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w})
: this is proportional to

\displaystyle\left(\int\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{y}_{\tau},\mathbf{x}\right\rangle-\varphi^{*\tau}(\mathbf{y}_{\tau})-\tau\psi(\mathbf{x})\right)\pi(\textup{d}\mathbf{x})\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\frac{\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{y}_{\tau},\mathbf{z}\right\rangle-\tau\psi(\mathbf{z})\right)\pi(\mathbf{z})}{\int\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{y}_{\tau},\mathbf{z}\right\rangle-\tau\psi(\mathbf{z})\right)\pi(\textup{d}\mathbf{z})}\cdot\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w}\right\rangle-\varphi(\mathbf{w})-\psi(\mathbf{z})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{y}_{\tau}+\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z}\right\rangle-\varphi^{*\tau}(\mathbf{y}_{\tau})-\varphi(\mathbf{w})-(\tau+1)\psi(\mathbf{z})\right)\pi(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle\qquad=\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{y}_{\tau+1},\mathbf{z}\right\rangle-\varphi^{*\tau}(\mathbf{y}_{\tau})-\varphi(\mathbf{y}_{\tau+1}-\mathbf{y}_{\tau})-(\tau+1)\psi(\mathbf{z})\right)\pi(\mathbf{z}),

where the last expression is proportional to the joint density over
(\mathbf{y}_{\tau},\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}_{\tau+1})
via a change of variables. Integrating over
\mathbf{y}_{\tau}
, we notice that this is in the form of a convolution, so
(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}_{\tau+1})
has joint density equal to

\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{y}_{\tau+1},\mathbf{z}\right\rangle-\varphi^{*(\tau+1)}(\mathbf{y}_{\tau+1})-(\tau+1)\psi(\mathbf{z})\right)\pi(\mathbf{z}).

Finally, marginalizing over
\mathbf{z}
, we immediately have that
\mathbf{y}_{\tau+1}\sim\tilde{\pi}_{(\tau+1)}^{Y}
.
∎

3.1
Renormalization

It is instructive to demonstrate the evolution of
\mathbf{y}_{\tau}
as a Markov process by explicitly giving the Markov operator. In the remainder of this section, we will use
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
to denote another time index. Define for all
0\leq\tau\leq\lambda
:

\displaystyle V_{\tau}(\mathbf{a})

\displaystyle:=-\log\int\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{z}\right\rangle-\tau\psi(\mathbf{z})\right)\pi(\textup{d}\mathbf{z}),

(9)

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{\tau,\lambda}f(\mathbf{a})

\displaystyle:=\exp(V_{\tau}(\mathbf{a}))\int f(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{w})\exp(-V_{\lambda}(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{w})-\varphi^{*(\lambda-\tau)}(\mathbf{w}))\,\textup{d}\mathbf{w}.

We can think of
V_{\tau}
as “renormalized potentials” and
\mathsf{P}_{\tau,\lambda}
as the “Polchinski operator” in the framework of
[
10
]
but reversed with respect to time; see also
[
84
]
.

Moreover, we can identify
V_{\tau}
with
-(\tau\psi-\log\pi)^{\sharp}
, that is, the negative of the log-Laplace transform of
\tau\psi-\log\pi
. Note that
V_{0}(\mathbf{a})=-\log\int\exp(\left\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{z}\right\rangle)\pi(\textup{d}\mathbf{z})
, so in particular
V_{0}(\mathbf{0})=0
.

Proposition 1
.

\mathsf{P}_{\tau,\lambda}
defines a discrete-time Markov semigroup. Moreover,
\mathsf{P}_{0,\tau}f(\mathbf{0})=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}_{(\tau)}^{Y}}[f]
and

\tilde{\pi}_{(\tau)}^{Y}(\mathbf{y})=\exp\left(-V_{\tau}(\mathbf{y})-\varphi^{*\tau}(\mathbf{y})\right).

Proof.

Using the fact that
\exp\left(-\varphi^{*0}\right)=\delta_{\mathbf{0}}
with respect to test functions,

\mathsf{P}_{\tau,\tau}f(\mathbf{a})=\exp(V_{\tau}(\mathbf{a}))\int f(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{w})\exp\left(-V_{\tau}(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{w})-\varphi^{*0}(\mathbf{w})\right)\,\textup{d}\mathbf{w}=f(\mathbf{a}).

Let
\tau\leq\lambda\leq\sigma
. First, define

g(\mathbf{a}):=\mathsf{P}_{\lambda,\sigma}f(\mathbf{a})=\exp\left(V_{\lambda}(\mathbf{a})\right)\int f(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{w})\exp\left(-V_{\sigma}(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{w})-\varphi^{*(\sigma-\lambda)}(\mathbf{w})\right)\,\textup{d}\mathbf{w}.

Then,

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{\tau,\lambda}g(\mathbf{a})

\displaystyle=\exp\left(V_{\tau}(\mathbf{a})\right)\int g(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{y})\exp\left(-V_{\lambda}(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{y})-\varphi^{*(\lambda-\tau)}(\mathbf{y})\right)\,\textup{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(V_{\tau}(\mathbf{a})\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\int\left[\exp\left(V_{\lambda}(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{y})\right)\int f(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{w})\exp\left(-V_{\sigma}(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{w})-\varphi^{*(\sigma-\lambda)}(\mathbf{w})\right)\,\textup{d}\mathbf{w}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\exp\left(-V_{\lambda}(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{y})-\varphi^{*(\lambda-\tau)}(\mathbf{y})\right)\,\textup{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(V_{\tau}(\mathbf{a})\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\int\int f(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{w})\exp\left(-V_{\sigma}(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{w})-\varphi^{*(\sigma-\lambda)}(\mathbf{w})-\varphi^{*(\lambda-\tau)}(\mathbf{y})\right)\,\textup{d}\mathbf{w}\,\textup{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(V_{\tau}(\mathbf{a})\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\int\int f(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{z})\exp\left(-V_{\sigma}(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{z})-\varphi^{*(\sigma-\lambda)}(\mathbf{w})-\varphi^{*(\lambda-\tau)}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{w})\right)\,\textup{d}\mathbf{w}\,\textup{d}\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(V_{\tau}(\mathbf{a})\right)\int f(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{z})\exp\left(-V_{\sigma}(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{z})-\varphi^{*(\sigma-\tau)}(\mathbf{z})\right)\,\textup{d}\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{P}_{\tau,\sigma}f(\mathbf{a}),

which proves the first statement. For the second statement,

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{0,\tau}f(\mathbf{0})

\displaystyle=\exp(V_{0}(\mathbf{0}))\int f(\mathbf{w})\exp\left(-V_{\tau}(\mathbf{w})-\varphi^{*\tau}(\mathbf{w})\right)\,\textup{d}\mathbf{w}

(10)

\displaystyle=\int\int f(\mathbf{w})\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z}\right\rangle-\varphi^{*\tau}(\mathbf{w})-\tau\psi(\mathbf{z})\right)\pi(\textup{d}\mathbf{z})\,\textup{d}\mathbf{w}

\displaystyle=\int f(\mathbf{w})\tilde{\pi}_{(\tau)}^{Y}(\textup{d}\mathbf{w}),

as claimed. The explicit formula for
\tilde{\pi}_{(\tau)}^{Y}
can be seen from the first line of (
10
) as the definition of integrating functions
f
with respect to
\tilde{\pi}_{(\tau)}^{Y}
.
∎

The next lemma describes the density of
\mathbf{w}
conditioned on
\mathbf{y}_{\tau}
when
\mathbf{w}
is generated as in Lemma
3
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\mathbf{w}
be generated as follows: for fixed
\mathbf{y}
,
\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{y}}\exp(-\tau\psi)\pi
, and
\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{z}}\exp(-\varphi)
. Then the density
\nu^{\mathbf{y}}_{\tau}
of
\mathbf{w}
is

\nu^{\mathbf{y}}_{\tau}(\mathbf{w})=\exp(-\varphi(\mathbf{w})-V_{\tau+1}(\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{w})+V_{\tau}(\mathbf{y})).

Proof.

The density of
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{y}}\exp(-\tau\psi)\pi
is
\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\right\rangle-\tau\psi(\mathbf{z})+V_{\tau}(\mathbf{y})\right)\pi(\mathbf{z})
, and the density of
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{z}}\exp(-\varphi)
is
\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w}\right\rangle-\varphi(\mathbf{w})-\psi(\mathbf{z})\right)
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\nu^{\mathbf{y}}_{\tau}(\mathbf{w})

\displaystyle=\int\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\right\rangle-\tau\psi(\mathbf{z})+V_{\tau}(\mathbf{y})\right)\pi(\mathbf{z})\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w}\right\rangle-\varphi(\mathbf{w})-\psi(\mathbf{z})\right)\,\textup{d}\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle=\int\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z}\right\rangle-(\tau+1)\psi(\mathbf{z})-\varphi(\mathbf{w})+V_{\tau}(\mathbf{y})\right)\pi(\textup{d}\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\exp(-\varphi(\mathbf{w})-V_{\tau+1}(\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{w})+V_{\tau}(\mathbf{y})),

as desired.
∎

The noise
\mathbf{w}
added to
\mathbf{y}_{\tau}
at time
\tau
thus depends on both
\mathbf{y}
and
\tau
via the renormalized potentials.

Lemma 5
.

The gradient and Hessian of
V_{\tau}
satisfy

\displaystyle\nabla V_{\tau}(\mathbf{a})

\displaystyle=-\nabla(\tau\psi(\mathbf{a})-\log\pi(\mathbf{a}))^{\sharp}=-\mu(\pi_{\tau}^{\mathbf{a}}),

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}V_{\tau}(\mathbf{a})

\displaystyle=-\nabla^{2}(\tau\psi(\mathbf{a})-\log\pi(\mathbf{a}))^{\sharp}=-\mathbf{Cov}(\pi_{\tau}^{\mathbf{a}}).

Proof.

These are direct calculations similar to Fact
1
.
∎

3.2
A localization process

Using our constructions from Section
3.1
, we demonstrate that
\pi_{\tau}
is a localization process.

Proposition 2
.

The measure-valued random process
\{\pi_{\tau}\}_{\tau\geq 0}
defined in (
8
) is a localization process (Definition
2
).

Proof.

We check the conditions of Definition
2
. First, (1) holds by definition. To check (2), one possibility is to use Definition 3.1,
[
79
]
; we have that
\pi_{\tau}
is the posterior density of
X
given
\mathbf{y}_{\tau}
, which by the Markov property, is equal to the posterior density of
X
given the entire filtration up to
\tau
, i.e.,
\pi_{\tau}
is a Doob measure-valued martingale. Alternatively, to demonstrate (2) directly, first define
\mu_{\tau}
to be the density of
\mathbf{w}
given
\mathbf{y}_{\tau}
in the described Markov chain. Consider the density of
\pi_{\tau+1}
conditioned on realizations of
\mathbf{y}_{\tau}
and
\mathbf{w}
. Then

\displaystyle\pi_{\tau+1}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{y}_{\tau+1},\mathbf{x}\right\rangle-(\tau+1)\psi(\mathbf{x})+V_{\tau+1}(\mathbf{y}_{\tau+1})\right)\pi(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{y}_{\tau}+\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\right\rangle-(\tau+1)\psi(\mathbf{x})+V_{\tau+1}(\mathbf{y}_{\tau}+\mathbf{w})\right)\pi(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{y}_{\tau},\mathbf{x}\right\rangle-\tau\psi(\mathbf{x})+V_{\tau}(\mathbf{y}_{\tau})\right)\pi(\mathbf{x})\cdot\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\right\rangle-\psi(\mathbf{x})-V_{\tau}(\mathbf{y}_{\tau})+V_{\tau+1}(\mathbf{y}_{\tau}+\mathbf{w})\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\right\rangle-\psi(\mathbf{x})-V_{\tau}(\mathbf{y}_{\tau})+V_{\tau+1}(\mathbf{y}_{\tau}+\mathbf{w})\right)\pi_{\tau}(\mathbf{x}).

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\tau+1}}[f\mid\pi_{\tau}]=\int\int f(\mathbf{x})\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\right\rangle-\psi(\mathbf{x})-V_{\tau}(\mathbf{y}_{\tau})+V_{\tau+1}(\mathbf{y}_{\tau}+\mathbf{w})\right)\pi_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})\nu^{\mathbf{y}_{\tau}}_{\tau}(\mathbf{w})\,\textup{d}\mathbf{w}\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}.

From Lemma
4
, we have

\nu^{\mathbf{y}_{\tau}}_{\tau}(\mathbf{w})=\exp(-\varphi(\mathbf{w})-V_{\tau+1}(\mathbf{y}_{\tau}+\mathbf{w})+V_{\tau}(\mathbf{y}_{\tau})),

and so

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\tau+1}}[f\mid\pi_{\tau}]

\displaystyle=\int\int f(\mathbf{x})\pi_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})\exp\left(\left\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\right\rangle-\varphi(\mathbf{w})-\psi(\mathbf{x})\right)\,\textup{d}\mathbf{w}\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\tau}}[f],

using the definition of the LLT.

To check (3), first fix
\mathbf{x}\sim\pi
. Then by the strong law of large numbers and Fact
1
,

\frac{\mathbf{y}_{\tau}}{\tau}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mathbf{a}_{i}}{\tau}\xrightarrow[\tau\to\infty]{}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{a}_{1}\sim\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{x}}\exp(-\varphi)}[\mathbf{a}_{1}]=\nabla\psi(\mathbf{x}).

In other words,
\mathbf{y}_{\tau}
asymptotically gives perfect information about
\mathbf{x}
(since
\left(\nabla\psi\right)^{-1}=\nabla\psi^{*}
, where
\psi^{*}
is the convex conjugate of
\psi
; see
[
18
]
), and
\pi_{\tau}
thus converges to
\delta_{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}\sim\pi
, as we have realized
\pi_{\tau}
as a posterior density.
∎

4
Gibbs Sampling

In this section, we revisit general two-state Gibbs sampling, and provide several structural facts relating the variance contraction of the “primal process” that samples from one of two state spaces, to the variance contraction of the corresponding “dual process” on the other state space. These facts will be used to analyze Algorithm
1
in Section
5
.

For readers more familiar with discrete probability, we provide an intuitive explanation of our main characterizations in Lemmas
6
and
9
when both spaces are discrete in Appendix
A
.

In this section, we work with two spaces
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
, equipped with
\sigma
-algebras
\Sigma_{\mathcal{X}},\Sigma_{\mathcal{Y}}
, respectively. For intuition, the reader may assume
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
equipped with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra.
X,Y
denote random variables on
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
, respectively. Let
\Omega=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
be equipped with the product
\sigma
-algebra, and as stated in Section
2
, let
\tilde{\pi}
be a joint
probability measure on
\Omega
with marginal densities
\tilde{\pi}^{X}
,
\tilde{\pi}^{Y}
and conditional densities
\tilde{\pi}^{Y\mid X}
,
\tilde{\pi}^{X\mid Y}
.

Consider the Hilbert spaces
L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{X}),L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{Y})
, and define the operator
\mathsf{K}:L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{Y})\to L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{X})
as

[\mathsf{K}g](\mathbf{x}):=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}g(\mathbf{y})\tilde{\pi}^{Y\mid X=\mathbf{x}}(\textup{d}\mathbf{y})=\mathbb{E}[g(Y)\mid X=\mathbf{x}].

(11)

We can derive the adjoint
\mathsf{K}^{\dagger}:L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{X})\to L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{Y})
as follows

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathsf{K}g,f\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{X})}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(\mathbf{x})[\mathsf{K}g](\mathbf{x})\tilde{\pi}^{X}(\textup{d}\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}f(\mathbf{x})g(\mathbf{y})\tilde{\pi}^{Y\mid X=\mathbf{x}}(\textup{d}\mathbf{y})\tilde{\pi}^{X}(\textup{d}\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}f(\mathbf{x})g(\mathbf{y})\tilde{\pi}(\textup{d}\mathbf{x},\textup{d}\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}f(\mathbf{x})g(\mathbf{y})\tilde{\pi}^{X\mid Y=\mathbf{y}}(\textup{d}\mathbf{x})\tilde{\pi}^{Y}(\textup{d}\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=:\left\langle g,\mathsf{K}^{\dagger}f\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{Y})},

which is simply Bayes’ rule. Then,

[\mathsf{K}^{\dagger}f](\mathbf{y}):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(\mathbf{x})\tilde{\pi}^{X\mid Y=\mathbf{y}}(\textup{d}\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid Y=\mathbf{y}].

(12)

A single iteration of Gibbs sampling starts from
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, samples
\mathbf{y}\sim\tilde{\pi}^{Y\mid X=\mathbf{x}}
, and finally samples

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\sim\tilde{\pi}^{X\mid Y=\mathbf{y}}
. Symmetrically, there are analogous dynamics on
\mathcal{Y}
. These dynamics reflectively define Markov operators
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}:=\mathsf{K}\mathsf{K}^{\dagger},\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{Y}}:=\mathsf{K}^{\dagger}\mathsf{K}
.

It is immediate that
\tilde{\pi}^{X}
is stationary for
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}f]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}[\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}f](\mathbf{x})\tilde{\pi}^{X}(\textup{d}\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}[\mathsf{K}\mathsf{K}^{\dagger}f](\mathbf{x})\tilde{\pi}^{X}(\textup{d}\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\tilde{\pi}(\mathbf{x},\textup{d}\mathbf{y})\tilde{\pi}(\textup{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y})\tilde{\pi}^{X}(\textup{d}\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\tilde{\pi}(\textup{d}\mathbf{x},\textup{d}\mathbf{y})\tilde{\pi}(\textup{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\tilde{\pi}(\textup{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y})\tilde{\pi}^{Y}(\textup{d}\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\tilde{\pi}^{X}(\textup{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[f],

and a similar calculation holds for
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{Y}}
. Moreover,
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}},\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{Y}}
are self-adjoint on
L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{X}),L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{Y})
:

\left\langle\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}f_{1},f_{2}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{X})}=\left\langle\mathsf{K}\mathsf{K}^{\dagger}f_{1},f_{2}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{X})}=\left\langle\mathsf{K}^{\dagger}f_{1},\mathsf{K}^{\dagger}f_{2}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{Y})}=\left\langle f_{1},\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}f_{2}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{X})},

(13)

and a similar calculation holds for
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{Y}}
.

4.1
Variance contraction via spectral gap

Now, we interlude with a discussion on the mixing rate for Markov chains associated to
general
localization processes by completing the argument in Proposition 19,
[
23
]
. That is, Proposition 19,
[
23
]
gives a generic way of converting a variance conservation bound in a localization scheme to a spectral gap on the associated Markov chain. We provide a more explicit variant of this calculation that in fact holds for generic Markov chains, not just Gibbs samplers.

Lemma 6
.

For all functions
f\in L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{X})
,

\frac{\textup{Var}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}f]}{\textup{Var}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[f]}\leq\lambda_{2}\left(\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}f]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[(\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}f-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}f])^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[(\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}f)^{2}]-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[f]^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[f^{2}]-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[f^{2}]+\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[(\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}f)^{2}]-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[f]^{2}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[f]-\left(\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[f^{2}]-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[(\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}f)^{2}]\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[f]-\left(\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}^{2}-\left\langle\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}f,\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}f\right\rangle_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[f]-\left(\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}^{2}-\left\langle\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}f,f\right\rangle_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}\right)

by reversibility. This object in the parentheses is the Dirichlet form
\mathcal{E}(f,f)
associated with
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}
. Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[f]-\left(\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}^{2}-\left\langle\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}f,f\right\rangle_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\mathcal{E}(f,f)}{\operatorname{Var}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[f]}\right)\operatorname{Var}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[f]

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{2}(\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}^{2})\operatorname{Var}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[f]

\displaystyle=\lambda_{2}(\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}})^{2}\operatorname{Var}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[f],

obtaining the squared rate.
∎

We apply Lemma
6
to prove a convergence rate on Gibbs sampling from the LLT-induced joint density (
7
) in Section
5
. Interestingly, even the specialization of our result to Euclidean stochastic localization yields a rate that is twice as fast as derived in existing applications of localization schemes machinery (e.g., Theorem 58,
[
23
]
), which owes to our sharper characterization in Lemma
6
.

4.2
Contraction rate for Gibbs sampling

We now give a more precise characterization of this squared rate in Gibbs sampling due to contraction of both forward and backward channels (at the same rate). In particular, we only require proving a bound on the contraction factor of one of the two channels, as contraction of the other channel follows from the fact that the operators on the two channels are adjoint.

We start with the following two lemmas.

Lemma 7
.

The operators
\mathsf{K},\mathsf{K}^{\dagger}
map mean-zero functions to mean-zero functions, i.e., for
g\in L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{Y})
with
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}^{Y}}[g]=0
and
f\in L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{X})
with
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[f]=0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[\mathsf{K}g]=0,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}^{Y}}[\mathsf{K}^{\dagger}f]=0.

Proof.

This is a simple calculation in iterated expectation. We have

\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[\mathsf{K}g]=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[\mathbb{E}[g(Y)\mid X=\mathbf{x}]]=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}^{Y}}[g]=0.

A similar calculation proves the statement for
\mathsf{K}^{\dagger}
.
∎

Lemma 8
.

Write
\mathsf{K}_{\mid\mathbf{1}^{\perp}},\mathsf{K}^{\dagger}_{\mid\mathbf{1}^{\perp}}
to be the restriction of the operators
\mathsf{K},\mathsf{K}^{\dagger}
to mean-zero functions on
L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{Y}),L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{X})
, respectively. Then
\lVert\mathsf{K}_{\mid\mathbf{1}^{\perp}}\rVert_{L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{Y})}=\lVert\mathsf{K}^{\dagger}_{\mid\mathbf{1}^{\perp}}\rVert_{L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{X})}
.

Proof.

It suffices to show
\mathsf{K}^{\dagger}_{\mid\mathbf{1}^{\perp}}=(\mathsf{K}_{\mid\mathbf{1}^{\perp}})^{\dagger}
. The result then follows as the norms of a bounded operator and its adjoint are equal. We have, for
f,g
mean-zero in
L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{X}),L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{Y})
, respectively,

\displaystyle\left\langle g,\left(\left(\mathsf{K}_{\mid\mathbf{1}^{\perp}}\right)^{\dagger}-\mathsf{K}^{\dagger}_{\mid\mathbf{1}^{\perp}}\right)f\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{Y})}

\displaystyle=\left\langle g,\left(\mathsf{K}_{\mid\mathbf{1}^{\perp}}\right)^{\dagger}f\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{Y})}-\left\langle g,\mathsf{K}^{\dagger}_{\mid\mathbf{1}^{\perp}}f\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{Y})}

\displaystyle=\left\langle g,\left(\mathsf{K}_{\mid\mathbf{1}^{\perp}}\right)^{\dagger}f\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{Y})}-\left\langle g,\mathsf{K}^{\dagger}f\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{Y})}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathsf{K}_{\mid\mathbf{1}^{\perp}}g,f\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{X})}-\left\langle\mathsf{K}g,f\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{X})}

\displaystyle=0.

By non-degeneracy of the inner product, we have the claim
(\mathsf{K}_{\mid\mathbf{1}^{\perp}})^{\dagger}=\mathsf{K}^{\dagger}_{\mid\mathbf{1}^{\perp}}
.
∎

Our interpretation of variance contraction for Gibbs sampling is captured by the following lemma.

Lemma 9
.

We have

\sup_{\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}^{Y}}[g]=0}\frac{\textup{Var}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[\mathsf{K}g]}{\textup{Var}_{\tilde{\pi}^{Y}}[g]}=\sup_{\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[f]=0}\frac{\textup{Var}_{\tilde{\pi}^{Y}}[\mathsf{K}^{\dagger}f]}{\textup{Var}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[f]}.

(14)

Moreover, if both quantities in (
14
) are
\beta<1
, then for all mean-zero
f
in
L^{2}(\tilde{\pi}^{X})
,

\frac{\operatorname{Var}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}f]}{\operatorname{Var}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[f]}\leq\beta^{2}.

Proof.

The first statement is a consequence of Lemmas
7
and
8
. To see the second, for any
f
with
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[f]=0
, we have

\displaystyle\textup{Var}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}f]=\textup{Var}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[\mathsf{K}\mathsf{K}^{\dagger}f]\leq\beta\textup{Var}_{\tilde{\pi}^{Y}}[\mathsf{K}^{\dagger}f]\leq\beta^{2}\textup{Var}_{\tilde{\pi}^{X}}[f],

as long as we ensure
\mathsf{K}^{\dagger}f
is mean-zero; but this is Lemma
7
.
∎

Lemma
9
is a somewhat more explicit variant of Lemma
6
, which shows that controlling just one of the two quantities in (
14
) is sufficient to obtain the squared contraction rate for the overall Gibbs sampler. This is convenient in Section
5
, as one of the relevant bounds in (
14
) is much more straightforward to obtain in our particular application of LLT proximal sampling.

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{x})

V\succeq\alpha\psi
. Then Theorem

holds for
\pi
.

Proof.

The statement is an application of Brascamp-Lieb (Fact

).
∎

For completeness, we prove a variant of Theorem

6
Zeroth-Order Private Convex Optimization

In this section, we apply our framework to problems in differentially private convex optimization. To keep our presentation self-contained, we first define differential privacy
[
30
,
31
]
. Let
\mathcal{S}
be an abstract domain, and let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We say that a mechanism (randomized algorithm)
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{S}^{n}\to\Omega
that takes size-
n
datasets in
\mathcal{S}^{n}
to outcomes in
\Omega
satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy (DP) if for any event
S\subseteq\Omega
, and any two datasets
\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}
that differ in exactly one element,

\Pr\left[\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D})\in S\right]\leq\exp(\varepsilon)\Pr\left[\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\in S\right]+\delta.

We now define the main pair of problems we study.

Problem 1

(DP-ERM and DP-SCO)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
,
D,G\geq 0
, and let
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be compact and convex with diameter at most
D
in a norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
. Let
\mathcal{P}
be a distribution over a set
\mathcal{S}
such that for any
\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{S}
, there is a
f(\cdot;\mathbf{s}):\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
which is convex and
G
-Lipschitz in
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
. Let
\mathcal{D}:=\{\mathbf{s}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
consist of
n
independent draws from
\mathcal{P}
, and let
f_{i}:=f(\cdot;\mathbf{s}_{i})
for all
i\in[n]
.

In the
differentially private empirical risk minimization (DP-ERM)
problem, we receive
\mathcal{D}
and wish to design a mechanism
\mathcal{M}
which satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP and approximately minimizes

F_{\textup{erm}}(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}f_{i}(\mathbf{x}).

In the
differentially private stochastic convex optimization (DP-SCO)
problem, we receive
\mathcal{D}
and wish to design a mechanism
\mathcal{M}
which satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP and approximately minimizes

F_{\textup{sco}}(\mathbf{x}):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}\sim\mathcal{P}}\left[f(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{s})\right].

Our goal is to obtain improved algorithms for Problem
1
, when the norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
used to measure the regularity of the instance is an
\ell_{p}
norm, for
p\in[1,2)
. We focus on solving Problem
1
under a
zeroth-order (value) oracle
access model, where we can query the value of
f_{i}(\mathbf{x})
for any
i\in[n]
and
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
. While Problem
1
has also been studied under first-order oracle access
[
7
,
9
]
, all first-order methods currently lose logarithmic factors (or worse) in their excess risk bounds. To address this gap,
[
44
,
42
]
introduced a sampling approach that leverages zeroth-order oracles within a regularized exponential mechanism to obtain improved tradeoffs.

While these previous sampling-based approaches
[
42
,
43
]
establish state-of-the-art tradeoffs between the sample size
n
and the excess risk, the resulting algorithms are inefficient from the perspective of the number of oracle queries used. Specifically,
[
42
]
used the proximal sampler of
[
69
]
, which was catered to Euclidean geometry and thus lost dimension-dependent factors in its oracle query complexity for
p<2
. On the other hand,
[
43
]
developed a basic non-Euclidean proximal sampler using the theory of LLTs, that yielded a suboptimal convergence analysis compared to Euclidean counterparts. This gave a quadratic overhead in the oracle query complexity of the
[
43
]
algorithm, even when granted a warm start. Our main application uses our functional stochastic localization framework to remove the quadratic overhead of the
[
43
]
algorithm, while retaining its state-of-the-art excess risk bounds.

As in prior results, our main workhorse is the following structural fact, showing that sampling from an appropriately-regularized distribution provides the stability required for DP to hold.

Proposition 4

(Theorem 3, Theorem 4,
[
42
]
, Theorem 6.9,
[
44
]
)
.

In the setting of Problem
1
, let
k\geq 0
, and let
r:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be
1
-strongly convex with respect to
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
, with additive range
\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}r(\mathbf{x})-\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}r(\mathbf{x})
at most
\Theta
. Let
\pi
be the density on
\mathcal{X}
satisfying
\textup{d}\pi(\mathbf{x})\propto\exp(-k(F_{\textup{erm}}(\mathbf{x})+\mu r(\mathbf{x})))\textup{d}\mathbf{x}
. Then the algorithm which returns a sample from
\pi
for

k=\frac{\sqrt{d}n\varepsilon}{G\sqrt{2\Theta\log\frac{1}{2\delta}}},\;\mu=\frac{2G^{2}k\log\frac{1}{2\delta}}{n^{2}\varepsilon^{2}},

(20)

satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP, and guarantees

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\pi}\left[F_{\textup{erm}}(\mathbf{x})\right]-\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}F_{\textup{erm}}(\mathbf{x})\leq O\left(G\sqrt{\Theta}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{d\log\frac{1}{\delta}}}{n\varepsilon}\right).

Further, the algorithm which returns a sample from
\pi
for

k=\frac{1}{G\sqrt{\Theta}}\cdot\sqrt{\left(\frac{d\log\frac{1}{2\delta}}{\varepsilon^{2}n^{2}}+\frac{1}{n}\right)}\cdot\min\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}n^{2}}{\log\frac{1}{2\delta}},nd\right),\;\mu=G^{2}k\cdot\max\left(\frac{\log\frac{1}{2\delta}}{n^{2}\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{1}{nd}\right)

(21)

satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP, and guarantees

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\pi}\left[F_{\textup{sco}}(\mathbf{x})\right]-\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}F_{\textup{sco}}(\mathbf{x})\leq O\left(G\sqrt{\Theta}\cdot\left(\frac{\sqrt{d\log\frac{1}{\delta}}}{n\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right).

Proposition
4
gives a straightforward way to attack Problem
1
. Our goal is to design a regularizer
r:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
that is
1
-strongly convex in a norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
with small additive range
\Theta
, and such that

\textup{d}\pi(\mathbf{x})\propto\exp\left(-kF_{\textup{erm}}(\mathbf{x})-k\mu r(\mathbf{x})\right)\textup{d}\mathbf{x}

(22)

admits an efficient sampler from the perspective of zeroth-order query complexity, for the choices of parameters
k,\mu
in (
20
), (
21
). Our strategy is to directly choose
r
to be a multiple of an appropriate LLT, and to apply our framework to sample from the density (
22
) efficiently.

7
Conclusion

Our work significantly expands upon the theory and potential applications of proximal sampling via the log-Laplace transform by constructing a novel localization process and connecting it to two-state Gibbs sampling. There are several important open directions, which we believe will be of interest to the community.

Faster mixing rate.

While we improve upon the quadratic dependence on the relative convexity parameter from
[
43
]
and further weaken this assumption to a Poincaré inequality, our mixing rate still scales linearly in
\log\beta
, where
\beta
is the warm-start parameter, which may incur dimension-dependent factors, e.g., see the discussion before Corollary
3
. This is avoided in the Euclidean setting by using the stronger condition of a log-Sobolev inequality and conducting the analysis in KL divergence, which achieves a runtime scaling in
\log\log\beta
.
Although we take an initial step towards this result in Appendix
C
under Assumption
2
, it remains to prove the result in broader generality. Specifically, it is interesting to understand whether a weaker condition than Assumption
2
suffices, as well as the types of regularizers
\psi:=\varphi^{\sharp}
that satisfy such conditions.

Samplers for explicit functions.

Our work primarily focuses on establishing the theory of functional stochastic localization and its connection to the non-Euclidean proximal sampler from
[
43
]
, leaving sharper, practical, algorithmic guarantees of sampling of the conditional distributions for future work. We briefly mention that
[
43
]
provides some algorithms for
\ell_{p}
geometries, e.g., via rejection sampling and the inverse Laplace transform, as discussed in Section
6
. However, even these applications require generic log-concave sampling algorithms (in Proposition
5
), which do not exploit problem structure. This could potentially be avoided for specific
\varphi
of interest.

Additional applications.

Our main application in Section
6
is for sampling in continuous spaces. We are hopeful that our framework will have applications for sampling in discrete spaces as well; see for instance
[
5
]
, where the theory of LLTs was used in a discrete sampling problem.

Appendix A
Gibbs Sampling for Discrete Spaces

In this section, we perform a derivation that clarifies the spectral gap of the Gibbs sampler
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}
of Section
4
in the discrete setting, where
\mathcal{X}=[n]
and
\mathcal{Y}=[m]
.

Let
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n\times m}
be the matrix corresponding to the joint probabilities of
\tilde{\pi}
, so that
\mathbf{P}_{xy}=\tilde{\pi}(x,y)
. Then
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{1}_{m},\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{n}
describe the marginal probabilities, and form the matrices
\mathbf{R}=\textbf{{diag}}{\left(\mathbf{r}\right)},\mathbf{C}=\textbf{{diag}}{\left(\mathbf{c}\right)}
. A direct calculation shows that the conditional probabilities are:

\tilde{\pi}^{Y|X=x}(y)=\frac{\mathbf{P}_{xy}}{\mathbf{r}_{x}},\qquad\tilde{\pi}^{X|Y=y}(x)=\frac{\mathbf{P}_{xy}}{\mathbf{c}_{y}}.

Gibbs sampling as described in Section
4
thus yields the transition matrices

\mathsf{K}=\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{P},\quad\mathsf{K}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{P}^{\top},\quad\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}=\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{P}^{\top}.

We proceed by deriving the spectral gap of
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}
. We have

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,x^{\prime}}\mathbf{r}_{x}(\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}})_{xx^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{x}-\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{x^{\prime}})^{2}\\
=\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\top}\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\varphi}-\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{P}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\varphi}.

Then

\displaystyle\mathsf{gap}(\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\sum_{x,x^{\prime}}\mathbf{r}_{x}(\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}})_{xx^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{x}-\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{x^{\prime}})^{2}}{2\operatorname{Var}_{\tilde{\pi}^{\mathcal{X}}}[\boldsymbol{\varphi}]}

-\nabla^{2}\varphi(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})&\nabla^{2}\varphi(\mathbf{y})+\nabla^{2}\varphi(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\end{pmatrix}.

For
-\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
to be positive semidefinite, it is necessary that both diagonal blocks, i.e.,
\nabla^{2}\varphi(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})-\gamma\nabla^{2}\varphi(\mathbf{x})
and
\nabla^{2}\varphi(\mathbf{y})+\nabla^{2}\varphi(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})
are positive semidefinite. There are one-dimensional counterexamples to this (take
\varphi(x)=x^{4}
and
x=y
), and indeed, in general, counterexamples to
\nabla^{2}\varphi^{*\tau}\succeq\frac{1}{\tau}\nabla^{2}\varphi
.
