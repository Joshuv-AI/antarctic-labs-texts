Gut microbiota and mycobiota change with feeding duration in mice on a high-fat and high-fructose diet
Authors: Ruoyi Zheng, Xingwei Xiang, Ying Shi, Junyan Xie, Lin Xing, Tao Zhang, Zhijun Zhou, Dongmei Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), formerly known as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), is becoming the most common chronic liver disease. The gut microbiome is regarded to play a crucial role in MAFLD, but the specific changes of gut microbiome, especially fungi, in different stages of MAFLD are not well understood. This study aimed to observe the longitudinal changes of colon bacteria and fungi of mice at different feeding duration of a high-fat and high-fructose diet (HFHFD), and explore the association between the changes and the progression of MAFLD.MethodsTwenty-eight male C57BL6J mice were randomly assigned to the normal diet (ND) group and HFHFD group. At the 8th and 16th weeks, mice were sacrificed to compare the diversity, composition, and co-abundance network of bacteria and fungi in colon contents among groups.ResultsHFHFD-8W mice exhibited increases in Candida and Dorea, and decreases in Oscillospira and Prevotella in comparison to ND-8W mice, HFHFD-16W mice had increases in Bacteroides, Candida, Desulfovibrio, Dorea, Lactobacillus, and Rhodotorula, and decreases in Akkermansia, Aspergillus, Sterigmatomyces, and Vishniacozyma in comparison to ND-16W mice. And compared to HFHFD-8W mice, HFHFD-16W mice had increases in Desulfovibrio, Lactobacillus, Penicillium, and Rhodotorula, and decreases in Talaromyces and Wallemia. Spearman and GEE correlation analysis revealed that Bacteroides, Candida, Desulfovibrio, and Lactobacillus positively correlated with NAFLD activity score (NAS).ConclusionGut microbiota and mycobiota undergo diverse changes at different stages of MAFLD.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-024-03663-0.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), formerly known as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), is becoming the most common chronic liver disease, with a reported global prevalence as high as 25% [1–3]. The spectrum of MAFLD covers from isolated steatosis with a relatively benign nonprogressive clinical course to the serious condition of non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), characterized by a state of hepatocellular injury, inflammation, and fibrosis, with a progressive course that may lead to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [4, 5]. During the past two decades, studies suggest that gut microbiome may contribute to the metabolic health of the human host and, when aberrant, to the pathogenesis and progression of MAFLD [6, 7]. Microbiome-targeted interventions (e.g. probiotics, prebiotics, synbiotics, fecal microbiome transplantation, engineered bacteria, et al.) have been proposed as new approaches for the treatment of MAFLD [8].

However, changes in gut microbiome in MAFLD are not consistently observed among studies. High-fat diet (HFD) is the most commonly used method for establishing animal models of MAFLD [9, 10]. Some studies reported a decrease in α diversity of gut bacteria community and an increase in Firmicutes (Bacillota) to Bacteroidetes (Bacteroidota) ratio (F/B ratio) in HFD-fed animals [11–13], while some did not [14–16]. Differences in HFD-feeding duration may partially explain these inconsistencies. Moreover, different feeding durations lead to varying severity and stages of MAFLD. For instance, C57Bl/6 mice on HFD for 8 weeks manifest primarily as isolated steatosis, whereas 12 ~ 16 weeks’ feeding shows characteristics of NASH [17–19]. These findings suggest that the gut microbiome changes during the development of MAFLD, and that these changes may differ at different stages of the disease. However, the gut microbiome at different stages of MAFLD have not yet been fully described.

More importantly, in contrast to intensive research on gut bacteria, the roles of other components of gut microbiome, such as gut fungi, are underappreciated. Gut commensal fungi, collectively referred to as the mycobiota, comprise a very small portion of gut microbiome, but interact with the host and gut bacteria [20], exerting a crucial influence on the structure of the gut microbial community and playing a pivotal role in maintaining the stability of gut bacteria and mucosal immune responses [21, 22]. Changes in fecal fungi have been described in several pathological conditions, and the contributions of the gut fungi to liver diseases have been recently recognized. Demir et al.. reported that non-obese MAFLD patients with advanced disease stages have a distinct composition of fecal fungi and antifungals alleviate liver damage in mice that received fecal microbial transplants from patients with NASH [23]. Mbaye et al.. found higher occurrences of fungi Pichia kudriavzevii, Candida glabrata, Candida albicans, and Galactomyces geotrichum in NASH patients, and further analysis revealed that these fungi are involved in endogenous production of ethanol and triglyceride [24]. These findings highlight the pathogenic roles of the gut mycobiota in the progression of MAFLD; nevertheless, research on the gut mycobiota is still in its infancy, the potential of fungal biomarkers and therapeutic targets requires further exploration.

The present study employed a high-fat and high-fructose diet (HFHFD) to mimic unhealthy dietary habits in humans to establish a diet-induced MAFLD model. Previous studies have indicated that mice fed a HFHFD for 8 weeks primarily exhibit isolated steatosis, while those fed for 16 weeks manifest NASH [18, 19]. We hypothesized that gut microbiota and mycobiota changes with HFHFD-feeding duration and severity and stages of MAFLD. Here, we examined colon fungi and bacteria in mice fed a HFHFD for 8 weeks and 16 weeks, and analyzed the correlation of these changes with pathological features of MAFLD.

Materials and methods

Animal study

A total of twenty-eight male C57BL6J mice, aged 5 weeks, were acquired from Hunan Slac-Jingda Laboratory Animal Co. (Changsha, China). After one week of adaptation, the mice were randomly assigned to two groups (n = 14 per group) as follows: (1) mice fed a standard chow diet (ND, containing 12% fat, 66% carbohydrates, 22% protein, and providing 3.50 kcal/g), and (2) mice fed a high-fat and high-fructose diet (HFHFD). The HFHFD consisted of a high-fat diet (Research Diets-USA, D12492, containing 60% fat, 20% carbohydrates, 20% protein, and providing 5.40 kcal/g), along with 10% g/v high-fructose in drinking water. The HFHFD group mice received fructose water as the only drinking water. All mice, housed three to four per cage, were maintained in one room and provided with ad libitum access to water and food. Food intake and body weight were recorded weekly during the 16-week experiment. At the end of the 8th week of the experiment, six mice were randomly selected and sacrificed from both groups. The remaining mice were sacrificed at the end of the 16th week of the experiment. All mice were sacrificed from 2 PM to 6 PM after a 6-hour fast. Euthanasia was performed under inhalation anesthesia with 1.5–3.0% isoflurane (RWD Life Science Co., China).

Animal protocols were approved by the Animal Use and Care Committee of Central South University and were conducted according to the regulations set by Central South University (No.2020sydw0989).

Intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT)

The IPGTT was conducted in the 8th and 16th week of the experiment from 3:00 PM to 5:30 PM after a 5-hour fast. The mice were weighed, placed in separate cages, and received an injection of 50% D-glucose (2.0 g/kg) intraperitoneally. The blood glucose levels were measured from the tail tip at 0, 15, 30, 60, and 120 min following the glucose injection using a portable glucose monitor (ACCU- CHEK; Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany).

Body composition measurements

Dual Energy X-ray Absorptiometry (DXA, GE Lunar Corp., United States) was employed to assess body fat rate using specialized small animal software (GE Medical Systems Lunar, Madison, WI, United States).

Sample collection and liver pathology

Liver tissues and epididymal fat pads (visceral fat) were collected and weighed. The collected liver tissues were subsequently fixed with a 4% paraformaldehyde solution. Colon content samples were carefully gathered under a sterile fume and then frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80℃.

Paraffin-embedded liver slices with 4 μm thickness were stained by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Images were captured by a light microscope (Zeiss, Germany). NAFLD activity score (NAS) was evaluated in a single-blind manner by an experienced physiologist using a modified semiquantitative Brunt score [25]. Specifically, the indices of steatosis, lobular inflammation, and hepatocellular ballooning were evaluated semi-quantitatively: steatosis (0–3): 5–33% (1), 34–66% (2), more than 66% (3); lobular inflammation (0–3): none (0), mild (1), moderate (2), and many (3); and hepatocellular ballooning (0–2): none (0), few balloon cells (1), and prominent ballooning (2). The NAS is the sum of these three indices.

Triglyceride and total cholesterol in liver tissues

Intrahepatic triglyceride (IHTG) and intrahepatic total cholesterol (IHTC) were measured using commercial kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol [26].

Gut bacteria and fungi analysis

The total genome DNA from colon content samples was extracted using the CTAB method and its concentration and purity were checked on 1% agarose gels. The DNA was then diluted to a concentration of 1 ng/µL using sterile water. Specific primers [16S: 341F(5’-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3’), 806R(5’-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3’), ITS: ITS5-1737 F (5’-GGAAGTAAAAGTCGTAACAAGG-3’), ITS2-2043R (5’-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3’)] were used for amplification of 16S rRNA genes of distinct regions (16 S V3-V4) and ITS rRNA genes of distinct regions (ITS 1–5 F) [27–29]. PCR reactions were carried out using Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs), and after amplification, products were mixed in equidensity ratios. The mixture was purified using Qiagen Gel Extraction Kit, and sequencing libraries were generated using TruSeq® DNA PCR-Free Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA). Index codes were added to the library, which was quality-checked using Qubit@ 2.0 Fluorometer. Finally, the Illumina NovaSeq platform was used for sequencing and 250 bp paired-end reads were generated.

QIIME software (v.2022.2) was used for demultiplexing and quality filtering to obtain the feature table of amplicon sequence variant (ASV), with sequencing depths of 64,956 (16S) and 56,611 (ITS) after quality control. The pre-trained GREENGENES 13_8 99% database (16S) and UNITE version 8.3 (ITS) was utilized with QIIME2 feature-classifier plugin to generate the taxonomy table. Sequence data analyses were mainly performed using QIIME and R (v.4.2.1) [30]. Diversity metrics were calculated using the core-diversity plugin within QIIME2. Alpha diversity indices, including Chao1, Shannon, Simpson, and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity were used to estimate microbial diversity in samples. Beta diversity distance measurement - Bray Curtis was performed to investigate the structural variation of microbial communities across samples and then visualized via principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) was performed to identify taxonomic biomarkers that distinguished between groups. An ITS/16S diversity ratio was calculated based on the α diversity indices of fungi and bacteria [31, 32]. Spearman correlation analysis and generalized estimated equation (GEE) correlation analysis revealed associations between microbial communities and environmental factors. Co-abundance analysis between gut bacteria and fungi was calculated using the R package “pheatmap [33]”, and Gephi v0.9.2 was used for visualization. The Benjamini and Hochberg procedure corrected p values to control for false discovery rate.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0; IBM, Armonk, United States) and GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, California, USA). For comparison between the two groups, an unpaired t-test was used. For comparisons among more than two groups, a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Effects of HFHFD on body weight, visceral fat, and glucose metabolism

Body weight of the HFHFD group significantly increased from the first week of HFHFD feeding in comparison with the ND group (Fig. 1A-C). At the 8th and 16th weeks, the HFHFD mice exhibited significant increases in visceral fat weight, relative visceral fat weight, body fat rate, and blood glucose in comparison with the age-matched ND mice (Fig. 1D-H). Compared to the HFHFD-8W mice, HFHFD-16W mice had a significant increase in visceral fat weight and body fat rate (Fig. 1D, F), but no significant difference was found in relative visceral fat weight (Fig. 1E).

Liver pathology in HFHFD mice

Pathologically, the liver of HFHFD-8W mice displayed hepatic steatosis without inflammatory cell infiltration or ballooning, while HFHFD-16W mice showed hepatocyte ballooning, inflammatory cell infiltration, and increased extent of hepatocellular steatosis vs. HFHFD-8W mice. Compared with HFHFD-8W mice, HFHFD-16W mice had significantly increased NAS (Fig. 2A, B).

Compared with the ND-16W group and the HFHFD-8W group, liver weight in the HFHFD-16W group was significantly increased (Fig. 2C). The HFHFD-8W and HFHFD-16W mice had higher levels of IHTG than their age-matched ND mice (Fig. 2D). There were no significant differences in IHTC among the groups (Fig. 2E).

Changes in colon bacteria (Microbiota) in HFHFD mice

Compared to the ND-8W group, the ND-16W group had decreased α diversity, as indicated by Chao1, Shannon, and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity indices. Compared to the HFHFD-8W group, the HFHFD-16W group exhibited significant decreases in Chao1, Shannon, and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity indices. At the 8th and 16th weeks, there was no significant difference in α diversity between the ND group and the HFHFD group (Fig. 3A). PCoA revealed a significantly different bacterial composition among the HFHFD-8W group, HFHFD-16W group and their age-matched ND groups (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Fig. 1A).

Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria (Pseudomonadota), and Verrucomicrobia were the dominant phyla in each group (Fig. 3C). Compared to ND-8W mice, ND-16W mice had decreased Firmicutes and increased Verrucomicrobia. No significant differences were observed in abundances of major phyla between the ND-8W group and the HFHFD-8W group. However, compared to the ND-16W group, the HFHFD-16W group showed significant increases in abundances of Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and the F/B ratio, accompanied by significant decreases in Bacteroidetes and Verrucomicrobia (Fig. 3D, E).

Differences in bacterial composition across groups were noted at the genus level (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. 2A). Compared to the ND-8W group, the abundances of Allobaculum and Akkermansia significantly increased, while the abundance of Prevotella and Oscillospira significantly decreased in the ND-16W group. In HFHFD-fed mice, there were significant increases in Lactobacillus and Desulfovibrio in the HFHFD-16W group vs. the HFHFD-8W group. The abundances of Dorea significantly increased, while the abundances of Prevotella and Oscillospira significantly decreased in the HFHFD-8W group vs. the ND-8W group. Compared to the ND-16W group, the HFHFD-16W group exhibited significant increases in Bacteroides, Lactobacillus, Desulfovibrio, and Dorea, while Akkermansia significantly decreased (Fig. 4B).

Analysis with the LEfSe method revealed that the ND-16W group exhibited significant increases in Sutterella, Desulfovibrio, Lactobacillus, Allobaculum, and Akkermansia, and significant decreases in Oscillospira, Coprococcus, Prevotella, and Ruminococcus vs. the ND-8W group. Compared to the HFHFD-8W group, the HFHFD-16W group had significant increases in Klebsiella, Lactobacillus, Allobaculum, and Desulfovibrio. Compared to the ND-8W group, the HFHFD-8W group exhibited decreases in Oscillospira, Coprococcus, Prevotella, and Ruminococcus, and an increase in Bacteroides. Compared to the ND-16W group, the HFHFD-16W group displayed significant decreases in Akkermansia and Sutterella, and significant increases in Klebsiella, Lactobacillus, Desulfovibrio, and Bacteroides (Fig. 4C).

Changes in colon fungi (mycobiota) in HFHFD mice

In ND-fed mice, no significant differences were observed between the ND-8W group and the ND-16W group in the α diversity of fungi, but PCoA showed significant differences in the fungal composition between the ND-8W group and the ND-16W group. In HFHFD-fed mice, there were no significant differences in the α diversity and fungal composition between the HFHFD-8W group and HFHFD-16W group (Fig. 5A-B, Supplementary Fig. 1B).

There were no significant differences in the α diversity between the ND-8W group and HFHFD-8W group, and PCoA revealed no distinct clustering of the gut fungal composition between the two groups. There were no significant differences in the α diversity of the gut fungal community between the ND-16W group and HFHFD-16W group, but PCoA revealed a distinct clustering of gut fungal community between the two groups (Fig. 5A, B).

Ascomycota and Basidiomycota were the dominant phyla in each group (Fig. 5C). No significant differences were observed in the relative abundance of Ascomycota and Basidiomycota between the ND-8W group and the HFHFD-8W group, as well as between the ND-8W group and the ND-16W group. Compared to the ND-16W group and the HFHFD-8W group, the HFHFD-16W group exhibited a decrease in Basidiomycota (Fig. 5D).

At the genus level, the global composition in the fungal community was determined in groups (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Fig. 2B). Sterigmatomyces significantly increased in the ND-16W group vs. the ND-8W group. Compared to the ND-8W group, the HFHFD-8W group exhibited a significant increase in Candida. Compared to the ND-16W group, the HFHFD-16W group had increases in Rhodotorula and Candida, and decreases in Aspergillus, Sterigmatomyces, and Vishniacozyma. Compared to the HFHFD-8W group, Wallemia and Talaromyces significantly decreased, Penicillium and Rhodotorula significantly increased in the HFHFD-16W group (Fig. 6B).

Compared to the HFHFD-8W group, the HFHFD-16W group exhibited significant increases in Tremella and Rhodotorula, and significant decreases in Wallemia and Talaromyces (Fig. 6C).

Relationship between gut bacteria, fungi and metabolic traits

We performed generalized estimating equations (GEE) correlation analysis to examine the relationship between the major genus and NAS (Figs. 4B and 6B). The results showed that Bacteroides, Lactobacillus, Desulfovibrio, and Candida were positively correlated with NAS (p < 0.05) (Fig. 7A). We then conducted Spearman correlation analysis at the genus level to investigate the relationships among all detected gut fungi, bacteria, and metabolic parameters. Among gut bacteria, Prevotella exhibited negative correlations with body weight, visceral fat weight, relative visceral fat weight, body fat rate, and IHTG, while Enterococcaceae-Enterococcus, Erysipelotrichaceae-Clostridium, AF12, Anaerotruncus, and Streptococcus showed positive correlations with body weight, visceral fat weight, relative visceral fat weight, body fat rate, and IHTG. As for NAS, Inhella, Opitutus, and Prevotella showed negative correlations, while Mucispirillum, Enterococcaceae-Enterococcus, Lactobacillus, Allobaculum, Klebsiella, Lactococcus, Streptococcus, and Desulfovibrio exhibited positive correlations. Dorea and Desulfovibrio exhibited positive correlations with blood glucose. Among gut fungi, Vishniacozyma negatively correlated with body weight, visceral fat weight, relative visceral fat weight, and body fat rate, while Rhodotorula positively correlated with body weight, visceral fat weight, relative visceral fat weight, and body fat rate. Leucosporidium exhibited positive correlations with NAS and IHTG. Byssochlamys, Rhodotorula exhibited positive correlations with NAS (Fig. 7B).

Fungal-bacterial interactions in HFHFD mice

To explore the equilibrium between fungi and bacteria diversity in the gut, we determined the fungi-to-bacteria diversity ratio. The ratios of Chao1 and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity significantly increased in ND-16W mice vs. ND-8W mice (Fig. 7C). Compared to HFHFD-8W mice, the Shannon and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity ratios of ITS/16S significantly increased in HFHFD-16W mice (Fig. 7C). There were no significant differences in the ratios of α diversity-related indices between HFHFD-fed mice and age-matched ND-fed mice (Fig. 7C).

Also, a co-abundance analysis of gut bacteria and fungi in mice was conducted (Fig. 7D, E). Compared to the ND-8W group, a significant reduction in co-abundance network density between gut bacteria and fungi was observed in the ND-16W group, primarily characterized by significant decreases in the number of neighbors and relative connection. The HFHFD-16W group exhibited a significant decrease in co-abundance network density compared to the HFHFD-8W group. Interestingly, compared to the age-matched ND group, we observed a significant increase in the co-abundance network density between bacteria and fungi in both the HFHFD-8W and HFHFD-16W groups.

Discussion

Recent studies have demonstrated that gut microbial dysbiosis plays an important role in MAFLD pathophysiology, and gut microbiome holds strong promise as novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets for MAFLD [7, 8]. However, few studies explored the longitudinal changes of gut microbiome in different stages of MAFLD. In addition, roles of gut mycobiota in MAFLD are recently recognized and insufficiently studied. Our study here showed that the composition of colon bacteria and fungi varies with feeding duration in both HFHFD-fed mice and ND-fed mice in a controlled environment. More importantly, we found that the composition of gut bacteria and fungi changes with the progression of MAFLD, and several compositional alterations are associated with the phenotype of the disease. To be best of our knowledge, this is the first report to explore the longitudinal changes of gut mycobiota in MAFLD.

In ND-fed mice, we found that ND-16W mice had decreased alpha diversity of gut bacteria than ND-8W mice (Fig. 3A), which agrees with findings in clinical studies related to aging and longevity [34, 35]. It is commonly observed that low alpha diversity is suggestive of a dysbiotic gut bacteria, and clinically is related to obesity, inflammation, and poor cognition [36], indicating that gut microbiota is involved in aging-related conditions. Additionally, the composition of gut bacteria in ND-16W mice was significantly different from ND-8W mice, especially in the dominant bacterial phyla Firmicutes and Verrucomicrobia (Fig. 3B, D). As for gut fungi composition, a significant difference between ND-16W mice and ND-8W mice was also observed (Fig. 5B). These findings confirmed that compositions of gut bacteria and fungi vary with age in mice, and age matching should be considered when studying the relationship between diseases and gut microbiome.

Consistent with previous observations [18, 19], HFHFD-fed mice in our experiment also exhibited isolated steatosis by 8 weeks of feeding, and NASH by 16 weeks of feeding (Fig. 2A-B). MAFLD is characterized by excess accumulation of triglyceride in the liver [37]. Among gut bacteria, Enterococcus, Mucispirillium, Desulfovibrio, and Streptococcus have been discovered to be positively correlated with hepatic triglyceride [38, 39], and Prevotella is negatively correlated with hepatic triglyceride [40]. The results of our study agree with these findings (Fig. 7B). It has been proposed that insulin resistance (IR) results in steatosis. Prevotella and Oscillospira have been identified to be negatively associated with IR [41], and Dorea positively correlated with IR [42, 43]. Here, the HFHFD-8W mice exhibited increases in Dorea, and decreases in Prevotella and Oscillospira vs. ND-8W mice (Fig. 4B). Impaired gut-liver axis characterized by increased gut permeability exacerbates the penetration of bacterial-derived endotoxins, i.e. lipopolysaccharide (LPS), into the systemic circulation and promotes the development of NASH. We found that HFHFD-16W mice had increases in Lactobacillus, Desulfovibrio, and Dorea, and decreases in Akkermansia vs. ND-16W mice, and compared to HFHFD-8W mice, HFHFD-16W mice showed increases in Lactobacillus and Desulfovibrio (Fig. 4B). Among them, Akkermansia is known to be involved in short chain fatty acids (SCFAs) production and gut barrier integrity [44]. Desulfovibrio is recognized as LPS-producing bacterium [45, 46], and induces increased intestinal permeability, and contributes to a more severe MAFLD phenotype [47, 48]. Our data highlight the roles of the gut microbiota in the pathogenesis of MAFLD, and specific bacterial signatures are related to hepatic steatosis and NASH.

Few studies focused on the dynamics of gut fungal communities during the progression of MAFLD. In the present study, the PCoA showed that there were no significant differences in gut fungal structure between HFHFD-8W mice and ND-8W mice, but a significantly different fungal structure was noted between HFHFD-16W mice and ND-16W mice (Fig. 5B). These findings may suggest that gut fungi are resistant to a short-term dietary intervention, and tend to keep a relatively stable structure.

The analysis of major fungal abundance showed that the abundance of Candida in the HFHFD group was significantly higher than the age-matched ND groups at both 8th week and 16th week (Fig. 6B). It has been reported that Candida induces epithelial barrier disruption and has abilities to produce endogenous ethanol and triglycerides in vitro experiments [24, 49]. Candida albicans, a species of the Candida genus, is believed to be a resident pathogenic commensal in the gut, and can translocate into the mesenteric lymph nodes or systemic circulation when gut permeability increases [50]. Consistently, Demir et al. observed that plasma antibodies specific to Candida albicans are increased in patients with MAFLD and advanced fibrosis [23]. The GEE correlation analysis in our study also revealed a positive correlation between Candida and NAS (Fig. 7A). Recently, Wang X et al. reported that Candida albicans colonization caused increases in hepatic cholesterol and triglyceride in Apoe−/− mice [51]. Lipid accumulation has been suggested to be the ‘‘first hit’’ for MAFLD [52, 53]. These results indicated that Candida may be involved in the occurrence and development of MAFLD. More studies are needed to dissect the roles of Candida in MAFLD progression.

Fungi and bacteria coexist and interact with each other [54, 55]. Antibiotics treatment leads to major fungi expansions that are then reduced following antibiotic cessation, suggesting a balance between fungi and bacteria [56]. Our study found that the ITS/16S diversity ratio increases with age in both ND mice and HFHFD mice (Fig. 7C). An increased ITS/16S diversity ratio has been suggested to be associated with a weak interaction between fungi and bacteria [57]. In line with it, we found the density of co-abundance network, which reflects intensity of interaction between fungi and bacteria decreased with age in both ND mice and HFHFD mice. However, the co-abundance network density in HFHFD mice was consistently higher than that in age-matched ND mice (Fig. 7D-E). It may suggest that abnormal interaction between fungi and bacteria participates in the development of MAFLD. A recent study in colorectal cancer patients found that the gut interkingdom co-abundance network was more complex than the control group, and also proposed that intra- and interkingdom interactions in gut microbiome may play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of colorectal cancer [58]. Nevertheless, the significance of interactions between gut fungi and bacteria remains incompletely understood, and further research is necessary to explore the specific mechanisms of these interactions in host health and disease. In addition, considering that fungal taxonomy is much less well established than bacterial taxonomy, interpreting ITS/16S diversity ratios as validated markers should be approached with caution.

While our study has revealed some significant findings, there are certain limitations to be acknowledged. First, the time span of the study is relatively short, with limited time points, leading to the inability to capture the microbial characteristics of disease progression in the later stages. Second, we did not measure microbial metabolites, which may help us better understand the roles of these bacterial and fungal changes in MAFLD progression. Third, we carried out the experiment only in male mice. Sex is considered an important factor in the composition of gut microbiome [59] and the incidence of metabolic diseases [60, 61]. Characteristics of the gut microbiome during different disease stages in female mice need to be described. Last, this study is observational and descriptive. Further studies, including functional studies, fecal microbiome transplantation, and single-strain colonization experiments, et al., are needed to explore the potential causal relationships between the gut microbiome and MAFLD. Therefore, more studies are needed to explore the role of gut microbiome in the progression of metabolic disorders.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study investigated longitudinal alterations in gut microbiota and mycobiota in different stages of MAFLD in a controlled environment, and explored the correlation of these alterations with pathological features of MAFLD. The findings reveal specific changes in colon microbiota and mycobiota at different stages of MAFLD, which are crucial for a deeper understanding of the impact of gut microbiota and mycobiota on MAFLD. Our results support the idea that microbiome-targeted interventions are promising for diagnosis and treatment of MAFLD. Additionally, our study emphasizes the importance of considering age and disease stage matching in scientific research and clinical practice.
