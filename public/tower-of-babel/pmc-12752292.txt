Prevalence and drug-resistance patterns of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and extended-spectrum β-lactamase producing Enterobacteriaceae isolated from raw vegetables in Aroge Gebeya, Hawassa, Ethiopia
Authors: Duressa Shafi Ahmed, Alemitu Beyene Gebre, Hadbacho Tsegaye Erancho, Habtamu Teshale Shinato, Moges Desta Ormago
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntimicrobial resistance presents a major threat to global public health. Vegetables contaminated with antibiotic-resistant bacteria can cause foodborne illnesses. Recently, multi-drug resistant (MDR) pathogens, such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and extended-spectrum β-lactamase producing Enterobacteriaceae (ESBL-PE), have become increasingly significant in public health. In this context, the present study aimed to determine the prevalence and drug-resistance patterns of MRSA and ESBL-PE isolated from raw vegetables in Aroge Gebeya, Hawassa, Ethiopia.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in Aroge Gebeya Market, Hawassa City (March-May 2024). Six types of raw vegetables (216 samples in total, with 36 samples each of red onion, lettuce, tomato, carrot, kale and potato) were collected. Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and ESBL producing Enterobacteriaceae were identified using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method. Data on sociodemographic variables and hygiene practices were collected using semi-structured questionnaires on Kobo Toolbox Software and analyzed with SPSS Software v29.0.1.ResultsThe overall prevalence of MRSA and/or ESBL-PE isolated from vegetables was 18.1% (39/216). Among the 160 isolates, 56.9% were MDR, 29.2% were MRSA, and 24.1% were ESBL-PE. The most frequently detected isolates were Staphylococcus aureus (30.0%), Enterobacter species (15.6%), Citrobacter species (15.0%) and Escherichia coli (10.6%). The highest frequency of bacterial isolates was found in tomatoes (21.9%), kale (18.1%), and lettuce (16.9%). Vegetables bought from vendors with untrimmed nails had a significantly higher prevalence of MRSA and/or ESBL-PE (AOR = 4.123; 95% CI: 1.681–10.109, p = 0.002).ConclusionThis study revealed a high prevalence of MRSA and ESBL-PE in vegetables, with strong resistance to commonly used antibiotics in Ethiopia. The isolation of MRSA from vegetables poses a significant public health risk. Additionally, many bacterial strains were resistant to multiple drugs and extended-spectrum β-lactamase, highlighting poor handling and safety practices by vendors as risk factors. Local health authorities should develop and implement effective control strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04645-6.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a major global public health and development threat, occurring when microorganisms evolve to withstand antimicrobial treatments [1]. By 2050, AMR could cause 10 million deaths annually [1]. The issue extends beyond human-linked habitats, as animals also contribute to the spread and maintenance of antimicrobial resistance [2]. Soil and water bodies further play a role in sustaining antimicrobial resistance [3]. Antibiotic resistance, a key aspect of AMR, often results from the misuse and overuse of antibiotics, creating selective pressure on bacterial pathogens [4]. Recently, antibiotic-resistant bacteria in food, including vegetables, have become a public health concern [5].

Vegetables are widely consumed but lack a terminal heating process before consumption, making them susceptible to microbial contamination and food-borne pathogen outbreaks [6]. Poor handling practices and inadequate food safety regulations exacerbate this risk, leading to rising food-borne illnesses linked to vegetables in countries like Ethiopia [7].

Multi-drug resistance (MDR), the phenomenon where microbes become resistant to several drugs, is now common among many bacterial pathogens [8]. In the last few decades, several epidemiologically significant MDR bacterial pathogens have emerged [9]. These include methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and extended-spectrum β-lactamase producing Enterobacteriaceae (ESBL-PE) [8, 9].

Staphylococcus aureus species are non-spore-forming Gram-positive bacteria that appear as cocci arranged in grape-like clusters; they are non-mobile, mesophilic, biofilm-forming, and facultative anaerobes that produce enterotoxins [10]. Staphylococcus aureus can be transmitted through the consumption of contaminated food, and its transmission is mainly due to the poor handling of food during processing [11]. It can release a variety of heat-stable staphylococcal enterotoxins (SEs) into food [12]. The consumption of food contaminated with these toxins produced by S. aureus can lead to staphylococcal food poisoning, which may cause severe gastroenteritis, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and abdominal pain within 1 to 6 h after the consumption of contaminated food [13]. This has been recognized as one of the most prominent culprits in food poisoning outbreaks worldwide [14]. Staphylococcus aureus can also lead to other diseases; some of them are severe, such as sepsis, endocarditis and necrotizing pneumonia [15].

Currently, this microorganism’s ability to develop resistance to antibiotics is notorious [15]. The resistance is usually acquired by horizontal gene transfer, although mutation and selection are also important [16]. Infections caused by resistant strains are common in epidemic waves by one or more clones; MRSA is prominent in epidemic waves, being historically associated with hospitals and health units (healthcare-associated MRSA (HA-MRSA)) [11, 16]. Nowadays, it has emerged as a cause of community-acquired infections (CA-MRSA), spreading rapidly among healthy individuals and its presence is a cause of concern due to resistance to various antibiotics, limiting treatment [11].

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) strains were first described in 1961 [17]. They were distinguished by the presence of an altered penicillin-binding protein (PBP2a), which has reduced affinity for methicillin and allows peptidoglycan synthesis to continue in the presence of the drug [18]. Resistance can arise through the acquisition of determinants by horizontal gene transfer of mobile genetic elements (MGEs) [19]. Transposons and the staphylococcal cassette chromosome element are particularly important contributors to this process [20]. In addition, chromosomal mutations that alter drug binding sites and increased expression of endogenous efflux pumps further enhance resistance development [21].

Another epidemiologically significant MDR bacterial pathogen is ESBL-PE [22]. This pathogen has emerged as a serious problem not only in nosocomial infections but also in community settings and obtained intensive attention in recent years [6]. Raw vegetables can be contaminated by ESBL-PE through various sources, such as contaminated biological fertilizer or water, poor hygiene practices of the workers involved in food production, ineffective food safety management and unsanitary vegetable packaging or display [23].

β-Lactamases are bacterial enzymes that inactivate β-lactam antibiotics by hydrolysis, rendering them ineffective [24]. The primary factor contributing to β-lactam resistance in Gram-negative bacteria is β-lactamase production, which evolves due to selection pressure from various β-lactam agents [25]. Extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBLs) confer resistance to oxyimino-cephalosporins and monobactams but not to cephamycin or carbapenems, and they can be inhibited by classical β-lactamase inhibitors such as clavulanic acid, sulbactam, and tazobactam [26].

Recently, antibiotic resistance of bacteria contained in foods such as vegetables has become a public health problem [5]. In many countries, including Ethiopia, the frequency of food-borne illnesses related to vegetables is rising as well. Contamination can occur in all vegetables sold in public markets, supermarkets, and even on the sides of the road, causing significant health problems for consumers [7]. In developing countries like Ethiopia, the burden is higher due to poverty and lack of public health awareness. Globally, food-borne diseases cause an estimated 1.9 million fatalities per year [5].

In Ethiopia, vegetables are widely cultivated by rural farmers and supplied to local markets [27]. Poor storage and lack of hygienic pre- and post-harvest practices contribute significantly to contamination risks [28]. Inappropriate transportation methods further increase the likelihood of microbial contamination in fresh produce [29]. Previous studies have reported a high prevalence of MRSA in Ethiopia, with Jimma showing 42.8% MRSA prevalence [30]. In Fiche town, 11% of vegetable samples were contaminated with Staphylococcus aureus [27]. Debre Berhan reported a 66.1% contamination rate, including 64.8% multidrug-resistant isolates and 18.5% ESBL producers [5]. These findings highlight the urgent need for surveillance beyond healthcare settings.

Both MRSA and ESBL-PE are critical public health concerns due to their resistance to commonly used antibiotics. Their presence in vegetables raises serious concerns about transmission to the general population. Research on foodborne pathogens and their antibiotic resistance patterns remains limited in low-income countries, and Ethiopia currently lacks market-level microbiological quality standards for vegetables. To help address these gaps, this study aimed to present baseline data on the prevalence of MRSA and ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae in raw vegetables sold at Aroge Gebeya market in Hawassa, Ethiopia. Such data are essential for local food safety, national antimicrobial resistance surveillance, and global scientific efforts within a One Health framework. In addition, the findings will inform health interventions and raise awareness about the risks associated with consuming contaminated vegetables.

Methods

Study design and study area

A cross-sectional study was conducted from March to May 2024 in Aroge Gebeya, a prominent market in Hawassa, the capital city of Sidama regional state. Aroge Gebeya is the primary food supplier for Hawassa’s residents and is characterized by its large size, accessibility, and economic significance. The market’s unique blend of permanent and temporary stalls, daily operation, and high foot traffic make it an ideal location for research.

Ethical approval and consent to participate

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval of the study was obtained from the institutional review board (IRB) of the College of Medicine and Health Sciences at Hawassa University (Ref. No: IRB/165/16). All participants were made aware of the study’s goal and each vegetable handler was provided with written informed consent. For minors, both consent and assent were obtained. The recruitment period for this study began on 25/03/2024 and ended on 23/05/2024. All methods were performed following the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Because the EP was less than 10%, the margin of error (d) was set to half of the EP (0.5 × EP = 0.03335) [31]. Thus, the minimum required sample size was 216 vegetable samples. The total was proportionally divided among six vegetable types, resulting in 36 samples per type.

Data and sample collection

Data on socio-demographic characteristics and sanitary risks were collected using semi-structured questionnaires on Kobo Toolbox software. The questionnaire used to collect data on vegetable handling practices was developed for this study and is provided as Supplementary File 1. A total of 216 vegetable samples, comprising 36 each of tomatoes, potatoes, carrots, red onions, kale, and lettuce, were purchased from Aroge Gebeya market. Half a kilogram each of carrots, red onions, tomatoes, and potatoes was bought, while kale and lettuce were purchased in bunches. The market was divided into distinct segments, and samples were selected using stratified random sampling with a computerized random number generator. Vendors were instructed to randomly select vegetables as they usually sell them to customers and were monitored to ensure random selection. The vegetables were placed in labeled, clean plastic bags and transported in a cold box to the Microbiology Laboratory of Hawassa University College of Medicine and Health Sciences (HUCMHS).

Laboratory testing method

Sample preparation

Vegetable samples were prepared for analysis by aseptically opening the packaging bags with a sterile knife. From each half kilogram of packed vegetables or bunches, one sample was randomly selected using a computer random number generator. The selected vegetables were then sliced into small pieces using sterilized equipment while wearing latex gloves.

To create the test samples, 25 g of each vegetable was taken from the sliced pieces and enriched in 225 ml of Buffered Peptone Water (BPW; Oxoid Ltd., Basingstoke, Hampshire, U.K.). The mixture was homogenized with a Stomacher device (Seward Ltd., U.K.) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, following FDA-approved Bacteriological Analytical Manual (BAM) guidelines for accurate bacterial identification [32].

Isolation and identification of bacteria

Following homogenization and incubation, a loopful (approximately 10 µl) of each sample was inoculated onto solidified MacConkey Agar (Oxoid Ltd., Basingstoke, Hampshire, UK) and Mannitol Salt Agar (Central Drug House (P) Ltd.). Pure colonies were obtained and key characteristics were recorded. Enterobacteriaceae were identified based on colonial morphology and pigmentation, carbohydrate fermentation, H2S production, citrate utilization, motility, indole formation, lysine decarboxylase, and urea hydrolysis. Staphylococcus aureus was differentiated by colonial characteristics, as well as catalase, coagulase, and oxidase tests [32].

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was performed using the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method, following Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. Pure culture colonies of 24-hour growth were suspended in 4 ml of physiological saline to achieve bacterial inoculums equivalent to 0.5 McFarland turbidity standards. A sterile cotton swab was dipped into the suspension, rotated to avoid excess fluid, and evenly inoculated onto Muller-Hinton agar (MHA) (Biomark Laboratories). Antibiotic disks (Oxoid Ltd., Basingstoke, Hampshire, UK) were then placed on the MHA plates [33].

For S. aureus isolates, the antibiotics used were ciprofloxacin (CIP, 5 µg), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (SXT, 25 µg), gentamicin (GEN, 10 µg), clindamycin (CC, 2 µg), cefoxitin (FOX, 30 µg), and erythromycin (ERY, 15 µg). For Enterobacteriaceae isolates, the antibiotics included trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (SXT, 25 µg), ciprofloxacin (CIP, 5 µg), meropenem (MER, 10 µg), tetracycline (TET, 30 µg), ceftriaxone (CTR, 30 µg), cefotaxime (CTX, 30 µg), and ceftazidime (CAZ, 30 µg). The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The diameters of the inhibition zones around the disks were measured with a ruler and interpreted based on CLSI criteria as sensitive, intermediate, or resistant [33].

Screening for MRSA

All isolates of S. aureus were screened for methicillin resistance using cefoxitin discs (30 µg) with the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion technique. Isolates with a zone of inhibition ≤ 21 mm around the cefoxitin disc were identified as methicillin-resistant [33].

Inducible clindamycin resistance (ICR)

Inducible Clindamycin Resistance (ICR) was identified using a D-test, which involves the application of erythromycin (ERY, 15 µg) and clindamycin (CC, 2 µg) discs spaced 15–26 mm apart on MHA, as per CLSI guidelines. Flattening of the zone of inhibition adjacent to the erythromycin disc and cloudy growth within the zone of inhibition around clindamycin indicate ICR [33].

Screening of ESBL-PE

Each Enterobacteriaceae isolate with reduced susceptibility to cefotaxime and/or ceftazidime was included as a potential ESBL producer. Isolates with an inhibition zone size of ≤ 22 mm for ceftazidime (30 µg) and/or ≤ 27 mm for cefotaxime (30 µg) were considered potential ESBL producers. To screen for ESBL production, discs of ceftazidime (30 µg) and cefotaxime (30 µg) were placed 25 mm apart on MHA plates prepared with a bacterial suspension adjusted to 0.5 McFarland turbidity standards [33].

Confirmation of ESBL production

In addition to the individual antibiotic discs, combination discs containing ceftazidime/clavulanic acid (30/10µg) and cefotaxime/clavulanic acid (30/10µg) were placed on the agar plates. The plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C for 18–24 h. Bacterial isolates were confirmed as ESBL producers if there was an increased zone of inhibition diameter of ≥ 5 mm for the combination discs compared to ceftazidime or cefotaxime alone, following CLSI guidelines [33].

Data analysis and interpretation

Data were imported into SPSS Statistics for Windows, v29.0.1 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Frequency distributions and prevalence were calculated and displayed in statistical tables. Socio-demographic and sanitary risk variables for the overall prevalence of MRSA and/or ESBL-PE isolated from vegetables were assessed using bivariate logistic regression analysis. Factors with a p-value of ≤ 0.25 were further investigated using multivariable logistic regression. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to measure the strength of associations, and statistical significance was determined at a p-value ≤ 0.05.

Frequency and distribution of bacterial isolates among vegetables

Among the 160 bacterial isolates, the most frequently detected were S. aureus 30.0% (48/160), Enterobacter spp. 15.6% (25/160), Citrobacter spp. 15.0% (24/160), Escherichia coli 10.6% (17/160), Salmonella spp. 10.0% (16/160), and Klebsiella spp. 8.1% (13/160). The highest frequency of bacterial isolates was found in tomatoes 21.9% (35/160), kale 18.1% (29/160), and lettuce 16.9% (27/160). Staphylococcus aureus was the most prevalent isolate in tomatoes 22.9% (11/48), kale 20.8% (10/48), and red onions 18.8% (9/48). Among the Enterobacteriaceae isolates, E. coli was most frequently detected in kale 47.1% (8/17). Enterobacter spp. were predominantly isolated from potatoes 28.0% (7/25) and carrots 20.0% (5/25). Citrobacter spp. were mainly found in tomatoes 25.0% (6/24) and carrots 20.8% (5/24) (Table 2).

Prevalence of MDR, MRSA and ESBL-PE

The overall prevalence of MRSA and/or ESBL-PE isolated from vegetables was found to be 18.1% (39/216). Only two vegetable samples (one red onion and one lettuce) contained both MRSA and ESBL-PE simultaneously; the remainder contained either MRSA, ESBL-PE, or neither. Of the 160 bacterial isolates, 56.9% (91/160) were MDR, 29.2% (14/48) were MRSA, 98.2% (110/112) were screened for ESBL production, and 24.1% (27/112) were confirmed as ESBL producers. Among the isolates, Salmonella spp. (81.3%; 13/16), Citrobacter spp. (79.2%; 19/24), and Klebsiella spp. (69.2%; 9/13) showed the highest rates of MDR. Klebsiella spp. (46.2%; 6/13), E. coli (35.2%; 6/17), and Salmonella spp. (25.0%; 4/16) were among the isolates showing the highest rates of confirmed ESBL production (Table 3). The highest number of confirmed ESBL producers was found in tomatoes (22.2%; 8/36), followed by red onions (13.9%; 5/36). Additionally, the highest number of MRSA isolates was found in tomatoes (11.1%; 4/36) and red onions (11.1%; 4/36) (Table 4).

Antimicrobial resistance of bacterial isolates

The highest level of resistance was observed by Enterobacteriaceae with cefotaxime (50.0%; 56/112), ceftazidime (46.4%; 52/112) and ceftriaxone (34.8%; 39/112). Staphylococcus aureus showed the highest resistance to cefoxitin at (29.2%; 14/48) while showing no resistance to ciprofloxacin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and gentamycin. However, Enterobacteriaceae isolates showed lower resistance to ciprofloxacin (14.2%; 16/112) and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (21.4%; 24/112) (Table 5). Of the bacteria isolates, (5.0%; 8/160) were resistant to five or more antibiotics, while (30.6%; 49/160) isolates were not resistant to any of the antibiotics tested. There were also resistant bacteria for one (23.8%; 38/160), two (15.6%; 25/160), three (13.8%; 22/160) and four (11.3%; 18/160) antibiotics (Table 6). Out of the total 14 MRSA isolates, 14 (100%), 12 (85.7%), and 10 (71.4%) were susceptible to gentamycin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and ciprofloxacin, respectively. Conversely, 5 (35.7%) and 2 (14.3%) of MRSA isolates were resistant to clindamycin and erythromycin, respectively (Fig. 1). Among the total MRSA isolates, 2 out of 14 (14.3%) showed inducible clindamycin resistance (ICR), whereas none of the methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA) isolates showed ICR (Table 7). Of the 27 ESBL-PE isolates, 19 (70.4%) and 13 (48.1%) were susceptible to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and ceftriaxone, respectively. On the other hand, 20 (74.1%), 16 (59.3%), and 11 (40.7%) of ESBL-PE isolates were resistant to cefotaxime, ceftazidime, and ceftriaxone, respectively (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study found a high prevalence of S. aureus (30.0%; 48/160) among bacterial isolates in raw vegetables, indicating significant contamination. This finding suggests that raw vegetables can be a substantial source of S. aureus contamination, posing several public health risks. Staphylococcus aureus contamination can lead to foodborne illnesses due to the production of enterotoxins, causing symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and abdominal cramps. Similar studies in Ethiopia, as well as in Nigeria, Benin, Algeria and Korea, reveal variations in prevalence rates, attributed to factors like sampling methods, types of vegetables, local hygiene practices, and agricultural methods [5, 7, 34–37]. Vegetables can become contaminated through handling by farmers, vendors, and consumers, as well as from contaminated soil, irrigation water, and animal manure [5].

In this study, 70.0% (112/160) were Enterobacteriaceae isolates. Although the scope of the studies varied, other similar studies conducted in Ethiopia, Nigeria, Israel, Malaysia, Spain and Romania have also identified various enteric bacteria from raw vegetables [5, 23, 38–41] These differences could be due to the bacterial load in the environment, the culture techniques used, and the data collection period. Generally, the prevalence of enteric bacteria can be linked to coliform bacteria, which are typically found in feces and are common in areas with open defecation. Additionally, the use of human and animal manure as natural fertilizer by farmers contributes to the contamination of vegetables grown on these farms [5, 38].

The study found the highest frequency of bacterial isolates in tomatoes (21.9%; 35/160), kale (18.1%; 29/160), and lettuce (16.9%; 27/160). This higher prevalence could be attributed to several factors, including the larger surface area and higher moisture content of these vegetables, which provide a conducive environment for bacterial growth. Additionally, these vegetables are often consumed raw, which means they are less likely to undergo processes that kill bacteria. These findings align with other studies that have shown leafy vegetables and tomatoes to be common sources of bacterial contamination [5, 42].

In this study, the overall prevalence of MRSA and/or ESBL-PE isolated from vegetables was 18.1% (39/216). Several factors might contribute to this prevalence, including poor hygiene practices during handling and processing and environmental contamination [38, 39]. To our knowledge, this is the first study to report the overall prevalence of MRSA and/or ESBL-PE in vegetables.

In this study, the prevalence of MRSA was 29.2% (14/48) among S. aureus isolates and 6.5% (14/216) among vegetable samples. This is lower compared to a study conducted in Debre Berhan, which found a prevalence of 36.4% (12/33) among S. aureus isolates and a nearly similar prevalence of 6.7% (12/180) among vegetable samples. This finding indicates the presence of S. aureus in Ethiopian vegetables, which harbor non-intrinsic resistance determinants, such as methicillin resistance [43]. The vegetables analyzed in this study are typically consumed raw or slightly cooked, suggesting that consumers may be at risk of S. aureus infections.

The prevalence of ESBL-PE in this study was found to be 24.1% (27/112) among Enterobacteriaceae isolates and 12.5% (27/216) among vegetable samples, suggesting that raw vegetables could be a reservoir for resistance genes. This prevalence is higher than that reported in Debre Berhan, which was 18.5% (23/124) among Enterobacteriaceae isolates and a nearly similar prevalence of 12.8% (23/180) among vegetable samples [5]. Recent research indicates that vegetables may act as potential conduits for the dissemination of resistance genes within the population. The overuse and unregulated use of beta-lactam antibiotics may have contributed to the emergence of ESBL producers.

In this study, 56.9% (91/160) of the pathogens were found to be MDR. Both S. aureus and Enterobacteriaceae exhibited a significant frequency of MDR. Similar findings have been reported in other investigations, with high MDR frequencies observed in Ethiopia (64.8%), Nigeria (65.12%), Romania (46.1%) and Spain (33.5%) [5, 38, 41, 44]. These studies indicate that vegetables can act as vectors for a variety of MDR pathogens. The variation in bacterial-strain resistance rates across studies may be attributed to differences in geographical locations, the characteristics of fresh vegetable samples, and the selection of antibiotics and breakpoints.

This study reveals significant antibiotic resistance patterns among MRSA and ESBL-PE isolates in Ethiopia. Out of the total 14 MRSA isolates, 5(35.7%) were resistant to clindamycin and 2(14.3%) were resistant to erythromycin. Additionally, 2(14.3%) MRSA isolates showed ICR. Among the 27 ESBL-PE isolates, 20(74.1%), 16(59.3%), and 11(40.7%) of ESBL-PE isolates were resistant to cefotaxime, ceftazidime, and ceftriaxone, respectively. The implications of these findings are significant for the treatment of infections, highlighting the need for alternative therapeutic options such as newer antibiotics or combination therapies.

The antibiotics tested in this study are widely used in both community and hospital settings. For Staphylococcus aureus isolates, ciprofloxacin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, gentamicin, clindamycin, cefoxitin, and erythromycin were included, reflecting drugs frequently prescribed for skin, soft tissue, and systemic infections. Cefoxitin was used as a surrogate marker for methicillin resistance [33]. For Enterobacteriaceae isolates, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, ciprofloxacin, meropenem, tetracycline, and third-generation cephalosporins (ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, ceftazidime) were tested, representing key therapeutic options for severe infections such as septicemia, urinary tract infections, and pneumonia. Encouragingly, no resistance was observed to ciprofloxacin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, or gentamicin, indicating that these agents remain effective options. However, resistance to clindamycin and erythromycin among S. aureus and to third-generation cephalosporins among Enterobacteriaceae is concerning, as these drugs are widely relied upon in clinical practice. Meropenem, a carbapenem antibiotic, was included as a last-line option, underscoring its critical role in managing multidrug-resistant infections. The resistance patterns observed in this study restrict therapeutic choices and mirror recent reports from Ethiopia and globally, where cephalosporin resistance among Enterobacteriaceae and macrolide resistance in S. aureus are steadily increasing [45, 46]. These findings highlight the urgent need for antimicrobial stewardship and strengthened surveillance systems to safeguard the effectiveness of existing antibiotics.

Vegetables purchased from vendors with untrimmed fingernails exhibited a significantly higher overall prevalence of MRSA and/or ESBL-PE. Specifically, the odds of these vegetables having these bacteria were more than four times higher (AOR = 4.123) than those bought from vendors with trimmed nails. This finding was statistically significant (95% CI: 1.681–10.109, p = 0.002), highlighting a robust correlation. The implication here is profound. The condition of a vendor’s fingernails can be a key indicator of hygiene practices, and untrimmed nails can harbor harmful bacteria, which can easily transfer to the vegetables during handling. This emphasizes the critical role of vegetable handlers in maintaining hygiene standards to prevent cross-contamination and the spread of antibiotic-resistant bacteria. It underscores the importance of regular hand hygiene and proper nail care among vendors to protect public health.

These findings highlight the interconnectedness of human, environmental and food sources of antimicrobial resistance, aligning with the One Health perspective. Poor hygiene practices by vendors facilitate bacterial transfer from humans to food, while the use of animal manure and contaminated irrigation water introduces resistant bacteria from agricultural environments into vegetables [5, 38]. Although our study did not stratify resistance patterns by sex or age of vendors, previous research suggests that demographic and behavioral factors can influence hygiene practices and contamination risks vegetables [5, 27] Addressing these issues requires integrated interventions that combine public health education, agricultural best practices, and food safety regulations to reduce the spread of resistant pathogens across human, animal, and environmental interfaces.

This study also aligns with global health priorities outlined in the Sustainable Development Goals. Specifically, it contributes to SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-Being) by addressing the burden of antimicrobial resistance and its impact on food safety and public health. It further supports SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production) by emphasizing the importance of hygienic vegetable handling, responsible antibiotic use, and improved food safety practices. By providing baseline data on MRSA and ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae in Ethiopian vegetables, our findings strengthen national and international efforts to reduce antimicrobial resistance within a One Health framework.

Limitations of the study

This study’s limitations include the lack of molecular confirmation, which restricts understanding of gene-based virulence and antibiotic resistance, and the failure to analyze common resistant antibiotic combinations. Limited antibiotic discs further restricted susceptibility testing.

Despite these limitations, the study has notable strengths. Systematic sampling was conducted across multiple vegetable types commonly consumed in Ethiopia, ensuring representative coverage. Robust laboratory methods were applied for bacterial isolation and antimicrobial susceptibility testing, following CLSI standards. Importantly, the findings provide relevant baseline data for food safety and antimicrobial resistance surveillance in Ethiopia, contributing to both national and global One Health efforts.

Conclusion

This study revealed a high prevalence of MRSA and ESBL-PE in vegetables, with strong resistance to commonly used antibiotics in Ethiopia. The isolation of MRSA from vegetables poses a significant public health risk. Additionally, many bacterial strains were resistant to multiple drugs and extended-spectrum β-lactamase, highlighting poor handling and safety practices by vendors as risk factors. These findings contribute to the field of microbiology by providing critical insights into antimicrobial resistance patterns in foodborne pathogens, which can guide future surveillance, prevention strategies, and policy development.

Recommendations

Strict hygiene standards in markets and safe transportation, handling, and continuous screening of vegetables are crucial. The public health sector should monitor the use of treated animal manure to prevent antimicrobial-resistant bacteria. Regular surveillance of antibiotic susceptibility is necessary to track resistance patterns.
