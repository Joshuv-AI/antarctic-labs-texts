An alternating sum of the floor function of square roots Thanks: Second author’s research supported in part by the Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council of Canada, Grant # 145628481
Marc Chamberland, Karl Dilcher

Abstract
. We show that the alternating sum of the floor function of j ​ n \sqrt{jn} , with j j ranging from 1 to n n , has an easy evaluation for all odd integers n ≥ 1 n\geq 1 . This is in contrast to known non-alternating sums of the same type which hold only for a class of primes. The proof is elementary and was suggested by an AI model. To put this result in perspective, we also prove an asymptotic expression for the analogous sum without the floor function.

1.
Introduction

The famous two-volume “Problems and Theorems in Analysis” by Pólya and
Szegő contains the following curious identity:
if
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
is a prime, then

(1.1)

\sum_{j=1}^{\frac{p-1}{4}}\left\lfloor{\sqrt{jp}}\right\rfloor=\frac{p^{2}-1}{12},

where the
floor function
of
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
is the integer

\lfloor{x}\rfloor
defined by
\lfloor{x}\rfloor\leq x<\lfloor{x}\rfloor+1
.
Further remarks on this identity can be found in Section
4
below.

It is a natural question to ask whether there are any similar results for

alternating
sums of the type (
1.1
). While no such identity seems
to exist for the summation limit
(p-1)/4
, numerical experiments led us to
the following identity.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any odd integer
n\geq 1
we have

(1.2)

\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{j+1}\left\lfloor{\sqrt{jn}}\right\rfloor=\frac{n+1}{2}.

This result is surprising for at least two reasons.
First, it holds for
all
odd
n\geq 1
, and not just
for primes of a certain form, as is the case in (
1.1
).
Second, using standard methods from analytic number theory, we can prove
the following estimate, valid for any odd integer
n\geq 1
:

(1.3)

\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{j+1}\sqrt{jn}=\frac{n}{2}+C\sqrt{n}+\frac{1}{8}+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\qquad(n\rightarrow\infty),

where
C\simeq 0.3801
is a constant that can be explicitly expressed as an
infinite series.

Comparing (
1.3
) with (
1.2
),
we see that by putting
\sqrt{jn}
between the floor brackets, the error term

C\sqrt{n}+1/8+O(1/n)
turns into the surprisingly neat constant
1/2
.

It is the main purpose of this paper to prove the identity (
1.2
); this is
done in Section
2
. In Section
3
we prove the estimate
(
1.3
), and we conclude this paper with a few remarks in
Section
4
.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

The following proof was suggested by the AI model
Gemini

[
5
]
. It
was then simplified and rewritten by us.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We rewrite the positive integer
\lfloor\sqrt{jn}\rfloor
as a sum and then
change the order of summation, obtaining

(2.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{j+1}\left\lfloor{\sqrt{jn}}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{j+1}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor{\sqrt{jn}}\rfloor}1

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{j=\lceil k^{2}/n\rceil}^{n}(-1)^{j+1},

where we have used the fact that
k\leq\sqrt{jn}
is equivalent to

j\geq k^{2}/n
. Next, when
\lceil k^{2}/n\rceil
is odd, then the first
summand in the inner sum on the right of (
2.1
) is 1, followed by an equal
number of summands
-1
and 1. When
\lceil k^{2}/n\rceil
is even, the summands
all cancel each other, and therefore the inner sum is 1 when

\lceil k^{2}/n\rceil
is odd and is 0 otherwise. Hence we have with
(
2.1
),

(2.2)

\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{j+1}\left\lfloor{\sqrt{jn}}\right\rfloor=\#\{1\leq k\leq n\mid\lceil k^{2}/n\rceil\;\hbox{is odd}\}.

where as usual
\#A
denotes the cardinality of a finite set
A
.
When
k=n
, then
\lceil k^{2}/n\rceil=n
is odd. Next, we note that

\left\lceil\frac{(n-k)^{2}}{n}\right\rceil=\left\lceil n-2k+\frac{k^{2}}{n}\right\rceil=n-2k+\left\lceil\frac{k^{2}}{n}\right\rceil.

Since
n
is odd,
\lceil(n-k)^{2}/n\rceil
and
\lceil k^{2}/n\rceil

have opposite parities, and for
1\leq k\leq(n-1)/2
, they are exactly all
the remaining elements of the set in (
2.2
). So finally, by (
2.2
)
we have

\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{j+1}\left\lfloor{\sqrt{jn}}\right\rfloor=1+\frac{n-1}{2}=\frac{n+1}{2},

which was to be shown.
∎

1.1

(2.4)

Conjecture 2.1
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an odd integer, and suppose that
\delta:=d_{n}(\lambda)\geq 2

for some
\lambda\geq 1

\lambda

\delta-1

3.
Proof of (
1.3
)

Along with proving the estimate (
1.3
), we are going to show that the
constant
C
is given by

(3.1)

C=1+\sqrt{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{(2k-1)8^{k}}\binom{2k}{k}\zeta(k-\tfrac{1}{2}),

where
\zeta(s)
is the Riemann zeta function.
We begin by rewriting

(3.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{j+1}\sqrt{jn}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n}-\sqrt{n}\sum_{m=1}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\left(\sqrt{2m}-\sqrt{2m+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n}-\sqrt{n}\sum_{m=1}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\sqrt{2m}\left(1-\sqrt{1+(2m)^{-1}}\right).

We now use the binomial theorem, which in the special case of power 1/2 can be
written as

(3.3)

\sqrt{1-x}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{2k}{k}\frac{x^{k}}{(1-2k)4^{k}},\qquad|x|<1;

see, e.g.,
[
6
, (1.104)]
. With this identity we get

(3.4)

1-\sqrt{1+(2m)^{-1}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2k-1}\binom{2k}{k}\left(\frac{-1}{8m}\right)^{k},

and after changing the order of summation we have

(3.5)

\sum_{m=1}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\sqrt{2m}\left(1-\sqrt{1+(2m)^{-1}}\right)=\sqrt{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(2k-1)8^{k}}\binom{2k}{k}\sum_{m=1}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\frac{1}{m^{k-\frac{1}{2}}}.

We now use the following well-known estimate for the partial sums of
\zeta(s)
:

(3.6)

\sum_{m=1}^{N}\frac{1}{m^{s}}=\zeta(s)+\frac{N^{1-s}}{1-s}+\frac{1}{2N^{s}}+O\left(\frac{1}{N^{s+1}}\right);

see, e.g.,
[
4
, pp. 114f]
. We use (
3.6
) with
N=\frac{n-1}{2}
and

s=k-\frac{1}{2}
. Then with (
3.1
) and (
3.5
) the first term on the
right of (
3.6
) immediately gives
1-C
.

To deal with the second term on the right of (
3.6
), we separate the
summands for
k=1
and
k=2
in (
3.5
), obtaining

(3.7)

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(2k-1)8^{k}}\binom{2k}{k}\cdot\frac{\left(\tfrac{n-1}{2}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}-k}}{\tfrac{3}{2}-k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\cdot\frac{-2}{8}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\tfrac{n-1}{2}}}{1/2}+\sqrt{2}\cdot\frac{6}{3\cdot 8^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{-\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\tfrac{n-1}{2}}}+O\left(n^{-3/2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{n-1}-\frac{1}{8}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-1}}+O\left(n^{-3/2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{n}+\frac{1}{4\sqrt{n}}-\frac{1}{8\sqrt{n}}+O\left(n^{-3/2}\right),

where in the last line we have used the facts that

\sqrt{n-1}=\sqrt{n}\left(1-\tfrac{1}{2n}\right)+O\left(n^{-3/2}\right),\quad\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-1}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}+O\left(n^{-3/2}\right).

Similarly, the third term on the right of (
3.6
), together with
(
3.5
), gives

(3.8)

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(2k-1)8^{k}}\binom{2k}{k}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\cdot\frac{-2}{1\cdot 8}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2\sqrt{n-1}}+O\left(n^{-3/2}\right)=-\frac{1}{4\sqrt{n}}+O\left(n^{-3/2}\right).

Finally, the
O
-term in (
3.6
) is easily seen to also be

O(n^{-3/2})
. Altogether, (
3.5
) with (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) then
give

\sum_{m=1}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\sqrt{2m}\left(1-\sqrt{1+(2m)^{-1}}\right)=1-C-\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}-\frac{1}{8\sqrt{n}}+O\left(n^{-3/2}\right).

Substituting this identity into (
3.2
), we obtain the desired estimate
(
1.3
).

4.
Further Remarks

1.
Pólya and Szegő attribute the identity (
1.1
) to
Bouniakowski
[
1
]
,
whose name is better known in its English transliteration Bunyakovsky.
In
[
7
]
the identity (
1.1
) is the last of several exercises that
use the technique of counting lattice points (Part 8, Problem 20). A more
recent alternative proof was given by Shirali
[
8
]
.

While the identity (
1.1
) does not hold for primes

p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, there is in fact an evaluation in this case which involves
the class number of the imaginary quadratic field
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-p})
.
This has been proved in the forthcoming paper
[
2
]
, along with several
other related results, all extending (
1.1
).

2.
Since the summands in the identity (
1.2
) have little or no
arithmetic structure, we cannot expect any classical inversion formula, such as
Möbius inversion, to apply here. However, the curious identity

(4.1)

\left\lfloor\sqrt{k}\right\rfloor=\sum_{d=1}^{k}\lambda(d)\left\lfloor\frac{k}{d}\right\rfloor,

which appears in
[
3
, p. 50]
, could be considered an inversion of the
identity (
1.2
). Here
\lambda(n)
is the well-known
Liouville function, defined by

\lambda(n)=(-1)^{\Omega(n)},

where
\Omega(n)
is the number of all prime divisors of
n
, counting
multiplicities. We have not been able to use the identity (
4.1
) in the
study of sums such as (
1.2
).
