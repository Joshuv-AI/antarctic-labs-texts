Kernel Treatment Effects with Adaptively Collected Data
Houssam Zenati, Bariscan Bozkurt, Arthur Gretton

Abstract
Adaptive experiments improve efficiency by adjusting treatment assignments based on past outcomes, but this adaptivity breaks the i.i.d. assumptions that underpins classical asymptotics. At the same time, many questions of interest are distributional, extending beyond average effects. Kernel treatment effects (KTE) provide a flexible framework by representing counterfactual outcome distributions in an RKHS and comparing them via kernel distances. We present the first kernel-based framework for distributional inference under adaptive data collection. Our method combines doubly robust scores with variance stabilization to ensure asymptotic normality via a Hilbert-space martingale CLT, and introduces a sample-fitted stabilized test with valid type-I error. Experiments show it is well calibrated and effective for both mean shifts and higher-moment differences, outperforming adaptive baselines limited to scalar effects.



Keywords—
causal inference, adaptive experiments, kernel mean embeddings, kernel two sample tests

Introduction

Data in modern experiments are increasingly collected
adaptively
, with treatment assignments chosen sequentially in response to past outcomes, as in multi-armed and contextual bandits
[
27
]
, adaptive clinical trials
[
8
]
, and dynamic pricing strategies in economics
[
38
,
1
]
. Adaptivity improves participant welfare and accelerates learning during data collection, but it fundamentally alters the statistical properties of the data: allocation proportions and effective sample sizes become random and history-dependent
[
3
]
. This breaks the classical i.i.d. assumptions that underlie standard asymptotic theory
[
47
]
, and as a consequence, estimators that are asymptotically normal under fixed designs may converge to non-normal limits or exhibit inflated variances
[
3
]
.

Simultaneously, reliance on the average effects is often insufficient, as many scientific and practical questions are inherently
distributional
. In medicine, clinicians care not only about mean efficacy but also about the distribution of side effects across patients
[
40
]
; in finance and operations, decision-makers evaluate policies using tail-sensitive criteria such as conditional value-at-risk (CVaR)
[
39
]
; and in reinforcement learning, distributional approaches explicitly target higher moments or quantiles of return distributions
[
10
]
. Existing statistical methods often rely on cumulative distribution functions
[
7
,
24
]
, which become difficult to extend to high-dimensional or structured outcomes.

Kernel methods provide a powerful alternative. Counterfactual mean embeddings (CME) represent outcome distributions as elements of a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
[
33
,
2
,
15
]
, enabling nonparametric comparison of distributions via kernel distances and supporting inference on complex outcomes such as images, sequences, or graphs
[
14
]
. This framework has been used to define distributional kernel treatment effects
[
35
]
, to design kernel-based hypothesis tests
[
41
,
31
,
12
]
, and to extend efficiency theory to Hilbert-space parameters
[
30
]
. However, all existing KTE methods assume i.i.d. data, and it remains unknown how to conduct distributional causal inference when outcomes are observed under adaptive, history-dependent policies.

In this paper, we develop the first framework for
kernel treatment effect inference under adaptive data collection
. Our contributions are as follows: i) we construct a doubly robust estimator that incorporates per-round variance stabilization using only past data, ensuring stable fluctuations under adaptivity. We show that the resulting procedure admits a Hilbert-space martingale CLT, delivering
\sqrt{T}
-asymptotic normality, where
T
is the total sample size. ii) We develop a reweighted plug-in estimator of the conditional variance estimator and we prove its pathwise consistency. iii) Subsequently, we extend it to a sample-split stabilized test that yields valid Gaussian limits under the null. iv) Finally, we provide numerical simulations to validate our findings. Conceptually, our work unites two lines of research—kernel-based distributional causal inference and inference with adaptivity—closing the gap between how distributional kernel treatment effects are modeled and how modern experiments are actually run.

3
Problem statement

We formalize the estimation of
kernel treatment effects
(KTE) when data are collected via an
adaptive experiment
(e.g., contextual bandit algorithm). This setting departs from classical i.i.d. assumptions, and requires rethinking identification and estimation under adaptively chosen actions and possibly adaptive stopping times.

3.1
Adaptive data collection setting

We consider a contextual decision-making system operating over
T
rounds. At each round
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
, the agent observes a context
X_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
, sampled independently from an unknown distribution
P_{X}
, i.e.,
X_{t}\sim P_{X}
. Given
X_{t}
, the agent selects an action
A_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
according to a possibly adaptive policy
\pi_{t}\in\Pi
, such that
A_{t}\sim\pi_{t}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},X_{t})
, where
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}:=\sigma(X_{1},A_{1},Y_{1},\ldots,X_{t-1},A_{t-1},Y_{t-1})
denotes the filtration up to time
t-1
. The outcome
Y_{t}\in\mathcal{Y}
is then generated according to a fixed, unknown outcome model
Y_{t}\sim P_{Y\mid X,A}(\cdot\mid X_{t},A_{t})
, depending only on the current context and action. We assume that the action space
\mathcal{A}
is discrete and the outcome space
\mathcal{Y}
may be either discrete or continuous, and that each policy
\pi_{t}
admits a density with respect to a base measure
\mu_{\mathcal{A}}
. The sequence of policies
\{\pi_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
may depend on past observations, rendering the overall data-generating process adaptive rather than i.i.d. The observed dataset consists of the trajectory
\mathcal{D}_{T}=\{(X_{t},A_{t},Y_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}
. We assume the existence of a potential outcome function
a\mapsto Y_{t}(a)
such that
Y_{t}=Y_{t}(A_{t})
, and that the collection
\{Y_{t}(a)\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
is conditionally independent of
A_{t}
given
X_{t}
, i.e., conditional ignorability holds.

Target Parameter

Let
k_{\mathcal{Y}}
be a positive definite kernel on the outcome space
\mathcal{Y}
with associated RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
and feature map
\phi_{\mathcal{Y}}(y)=k_{\mathcal{Y}}(\cdot,y)

of the counterfactual outcome distribution of

\eta(a):=\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}\times P_{Y\mid X,A}}\left[\phi_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y(a))\right].

(1)

Then the generalized

(KTE) can be expressed as the MMD of the two counterfactual mean embeddings

and

\displaystyle\Psi(a,a^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=\eta(a)-\eta(a^{\prime}),

(2)

\displaystyle\tau(a,a^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=\|\Psi(a,a^{\prime})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}

(3)

This expression reduces to the binary-treatment KTE of
Martinez Taboada et al. 2023
when
a=1
and
a^{\prime}=0
, and naturally extends the kernel two-sample idea to nonparametric treatment comparisons.

Now, define the following conditional mean embedding
[

,

of the distribution

\mu_{Y\mid A,X}(a,x):=\mathbb{E}_{P_{Y\mid X,A}}[\phi_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y)\mid A=a,X=x].

(4)

Under the following assumption, we will be able to identify the (CME) from observable data.

Assumption 3.1

Assume i) Consistency:
Y=Y(a)
when

, ii) Conditional exchangeability :
{Y(a)}\perp A\mid X
.
iii) Strong positivity: there exists

\operatorname*{ess\,inf}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\ \pi_{t}(a\mid x)\ \geq\ c,\quad\forall a\in\mathcal{A},\ \forall t\geq 1
,
where the essential infimum is with respect to

Under Assumption

, the counterfactual mean embedding
[

,

can be written as:

\eta(a)=\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}\left[\mu_{Y\mid A,X}(a,x)\right].

(5)

Canonical Gradient.
To construct variance-stabilized estimators under adaptive data collection, we define canonical gradient mapping into the RKHS

\displaystyle D^{\prime}(\pi,\bar{\mu},a)(X,A,Y):=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{A=a\}}{\pi(a\mid X)}\left(\phi_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y)-\bar{\mu}(A,X)\right)

(6)

\displaystyle+\bar{\mu}(a,X).

for discrete

(see

for extensions to continuous treatments). This term is directly linked to the influence function of Hilbert-valued counterfactual mean embedding

, with

a model of

3.3
Failure of standard asymptotic normality under adaptivity

Let
(\widehat{\mu}_{Y\mid A,X}^{(t)})_{t\geq 1}
denote a sequence of estimators for the conditional mean embedding
\mu_{Y\mid A,X}
, where each
\widehat{\mu}_{Y\mid A,X}^{(t)}:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
is trained using data up to round
t
, i.e., is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable.

In i.i.d. settings, canonical-gradient-based estimators are asymptotically linear with
an influence function in the outcome RKHS
[
30
]
, and asymptotic
normality follows from a standard i.i.d. CLT in Hilbert spaces

[
5
]
. Under adaptive collection, however, the summands are no longer
i.i.d. or even stationary; realized propensities depend on the past, and in this case
martingale arguments are needed to recover Gaussian limits. Formally, define the canonical-gradient difference

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{t}:=

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{t}(a,a^{\prime},\pi_{t}):=\ D^{\prime}(\pi_{t},\widehat{\mu}_{Y\mid A,X}^{(t-1)},a)(X_{t},A_{t},Y_{t})

(7)

\displaystyle-D^{\prime}(\pi_{t},\widehat{\mu}_{Y\mid A,X}^{(t-1)},a^{\prime})(X_{t},A_{t},Y_{t}).

The following
S_{T}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\phi}_{t}
,
is the
\sqrt{T}
-scaled estimator of the effect difference
\Psi(a,a^{\prime})
. In the i.i.d. case,

(\hat{\phi}_{t})
are independent, centered, and identically distributed in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
,
so
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E[\hat{\phi}_{t}\otimes\hat{\phi}_{t}]\;\to\;\Gamma,

for some deterministic covariance operator
\Gamma
, and Bosq’s Hilbert-space CLT
applies directly (see Theorem
10.10
in Appendix
10
). In the adaptive case, however, the policy
\pi_{t}
depends on the past filtration

\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, so the conditional covariance
\Cov(\hat{\phi}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})

is random and path-dependent. Consequently, the predictable quadratic variation of the normalized sum

\Gamma_{T}\;:=\;\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E[\hat{\phi}_{t}\otimes\hat{\phi}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}],

may fail to converge, or converge only along subsequences.
However, a martingale CLT requires
\Gamma_{T}\to\Gamma
in Hilbert–Schmidt norm for some deterministic, trace-class operator
\Gamma
. This is the quadratic-variation convergence criterion (see condition (B2) of Theorem
10.10
, Appendix
10
): the accumulated conditional covariance of the summands must stabilize to a fixed limit. All in all, this explains why naïve i.i.d. estimators
become miscalibrated in adaptive regimes
[
3
]
, as illustrated in Example
3.2
below.

Figure 1
:
Histogram of the miscalibrated DR-xKTE statistic over
500
runs
(
T=700
,
d=5
,
t_{0}=15
,
\varepsilon=10^{-3}
) with true adaptive
propensities
\pi_{t}(1\mid X_{t})
.

Example 3.2

(Contextual extension of
[
3
]
, Section 3.1.1)
.

Let
X_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and

Y_{t}(0)=f(X_{t})+\varepsilon_{t}
,
Y_{t}(1)=f(X_{t})+\Delta(X_{t})+\varepsilon_{t}
, where
\Delta=Y_{t}(1)-Y_{t}(0)
is the outcome shift.
The policy explores uniformly for
t\leq t_{0}
and then commits to the empirically better arm with
\varepsilon
-randomization:

\pi_{t}(1\mid X_{t})=\begin{cases}0.5,&t\leq t_{0},\\
1-\varepsilon,&t>t_{0}\text{ and arm }1\text{ selected},\\
\varepsilon,&t>t_{0}\text{ and arm }0\text{ selected}.\end{cases}

Since the committed arm is history-dependent, the design is adaptive. Under
H_{0}\!:\Delta\equiv 0
,
Bibaut and Kallus 2025
show that the ATE has a non-Gaussian mixture limit. Figure
1
shows the DR-xKTE statistic of
Martinez Taboada et al. 2023
is similarly miscalibrated.

To restore asymptotic normality, one must enforce stabilization so that the quadratic
variation converges deterministically. In particular, when realized propensities are path-dependent, a standard
approach
[
18
,
4
]
is to normalize each increment
\hat{\phi}_{t}
by an
estimate of its conditional standard deviation; in our RKHS case
\omega_{t}^{-2}
(to be given explicitly in the next section), ensuring and forcing

\Tr(\Cov(\omega_{t}\hat{\phi}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}))=1
. In the next section, our estimator follows
this strategy: by rescaling canonical gradients to have predictable unit variance, we
restore convergence of the quadratic variation and ensure validity of the martingale CLT
in the RKHS setting.

4
Variance-stabilized estimator

We now present a generic construction of a variance-stabilized estimator for the counterfactual mean embedding differences
\Psi(a,a^{\prime})=\eta(a)-\eta(a^{\prime})
in the contextual and adaptive data collection setting. The estimator leverages sequential plug-in estimators of the conditional mean embedding and of the conditional standard deviation of the canonical gradient, adapted to the RKHS-valued structure of the problem. We also state conditions under which the resulting estimator is asymptotically normal.

4.1
Stabilized estimator with plug-in weights

Recall the definition of the canonical-gradient difference
\hat{\phi}_{t}
in Equation (
7
) for estimating
\Psi(a,a^{\prime})
, and define the conditional standard deviation of the influence function as below:

\displaystyle\omega_{t}^{-2}:=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left\|\hat{\phi}_{t}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\phi}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right].

(8)

Let
(\widehat{\omega}_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be a given sequence of estimators of the conditional standard deviation
\omega_{t}
, where each
\widehat{\omega}_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-measurable.

The stabilized estimator of the counterfactual mean embedding difference
\Psi(a,a^{\prime})
rescales the empirical influence–function average by

\Lambda_{T}:=\Big(\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\widehat{\omega}_{t-1}\Big)^{-1}
:

\widehat{\Psi}_{T}(a,a^{\prime})\;=\;\Lambda_{T}\,\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\widehat{\omega}_{t-1}\,\hat{\phi}_{t}(a,a^{\prime},\pi_{t}).

(9)

4.2
Asymptotic normality guarantees

We now characterize the asymptotic distribution of the stabilized estimator
\widehat{\Psi}_{T}(a,a^{\prime})
under regularity conditions. As for
[
4
]
, we exclude degenerate scenarios and we introduce the following assumption.

Assumption 4.1

(Non-degenerate efficiency bound)
.

For
a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
, define
\phi(a,a^{\prime},\pi)\;:=\;D^{\prime}(\pi,\mu_{Y\mid A,X},a)\;-\;D^{\prime}(\pi,\mu_{Y\mid A,X},a^{\prime})
and assume

\inf_{\pi\in\Pi}\ \E_{P_{X}\times P_{Y\mid X,A}}\!\left[\big\|\phi(a,a^{\prime},\pi)-\E[\phi(a,a^{\prime},\pi)]\big\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}\right]\ >\ 0.

This assumption rules out degenerate settings where the difference
\eta(a)-\eta(a^{\prime})
can be exactly recovered from a single observation under some fixed logging policy
\pi
. A simple sufficient condition for Assumption
4.1
is that the conditional mean embedding
\mu_{Y\mid A,X}(a,X)
is non-degenerate in
X
, i.e.,

\E\!\left[\big\|\mu_{Y\mid A,X}(a,X)-\E[\mu_{Y\mid A,X}(a,X)]\big\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}\right]>0.

We next introduce standard assumptions on the RKHS and the associated kernel
[
29
,
43
]
.

Assumption 4.2

(Bounded outcome kernel)
.

The outcome kernel
k_{\mathcal{Y}}
is bounded: there exists
\kappa<\infty
such that
k_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,y)\leq\kappa
for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. Consequently,
\|\phi_{\mathcal{Y}}(y)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}\leq\sqrt{\kappa}
.
Moreover,

is assumed to be characteristic, ensuring the injectivity of the distribution embeddings in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}

[
46
]
.

Next, we require the following convergence conditions on the conditional mean embedding (similar to Assumption 4 in
[
4
]
) and the propensities
\pi_{t}
.

Assumption 4.3

(Nuisance parameters convergence)
.

Assume there exists
\mu_{\infty}
, and
\pi_{\infty}
such that:
i)
\widehat{\mu}^{(t-1)}_{Y\mid A,X}\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{L_{2}(P_{X}\times\mu_{\mathcal{A}})\ \text{a.s.}}\ \mu_{\infty}.
ii)
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{X}}\!\left[\|\pi_{t}(\cdot\mid X)-\pi_{\infty}(\cdot\mid X)\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\right]\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{\ \mathrm{a.s.}\ }0.

where we use
\|q-p\|_{\mathrm{TV}}:=\tfrac{1}{2}\int|q-p|\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathcal{A}}
for conditional distributions on
\mathcal{A}
with base measure
\mu_{\mathcal{A}}
.

We next state a condition on the convergence of
\widehat{\omega}_{t}
, to be proved in Section
5

Condition 4.4

(Consistent standard deviation estimators)
.

Let
(\widehat{\omega}_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be a sequence of estimators for the conditional standard deviation weights
\omega_{t}
defined above. We assume: i) Ratio consistency:

\widehat{\omega}_{t}/\omega_{t}\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{\text{a.s.}}1
, ii) Uniform boundedness:
\sup_{t\geq 1}\widehat{\omega}_{t}<\infty

We are now in position to state one of our main asymptotic normality results, starting with

Theorem 4.5

(Asymptotic normality of the stabilized RKHS estimator)
.

Under Assumptions
3.1
,
4.1
,

4.4
,
4.2
, and
4.3
,

\sqrt{T}\,\big(\widehat{\Psi}_{T}(a,a^{\prime})-\Psi(a,a^{\prime})\big)\xRightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\Gamma)\quad\text{in }\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}},

where
\mathcal{N}(0,\Gamma)
is the centered Gaussian measure on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
with covariance
\Gamma
(see Appendix
10
, Theorem.
10.10
) and
\Gamma=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E[D_{t}\otimes D_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]
is a positive, trace-class operator, where

D_{t}~:=~\omega_{t-1}~\left(~\hat{\phi}_{t}(a,a^{\prime})~-~\mathbb{E}~[\hat{\phi}_{t}(a,a^{\prime})~\mid~\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\right).

Sketch of proof.

Define the stabilized, centered increments
Z_{t}=\widehat{\omega}_{t-1}\big(\hat{\phi}_{t}-\E[\hat{\phi}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\big)

\E[Z_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=0

\sqrt{T}(\widehat{\Psi}_{T}-\Psi)=\Lambda_{T}\,T^{-1/2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}Z_{t}

holds almost surely in Hilbert–Schmidt norm (Lemma

).
The no–big–jump condition (B1) follows from uniform envelopes implied by bounded kernels and strong positivity, while the Lindeberg/tightness condition (B3) follows from nuisance regularity and variance consistency.
Thus Bosq’s Hilbert-space MCLT (Thm.

T^{-1/2}\sum_{t}Z_{t}\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}(0,\Gamma)

Conditional Variance Estimation

We now present plug-in variance weights

needed for our stabilized KTE estimator. These weights approximate the conditional standard deviation of the canonical–gradient difference

and are computed sequentially from past data only. Our construction extends the importance–weighted variance estimator of

Importance weighted empirical moments.
Our goal is to estimate
\omega_{t}
defined in Equation (

Fix

. Define, for each past round

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{s,t}(a,a^{\prime},\pi_{t}):=

\displaystyle D^{\prime}(\pi_{t},\widehat{\mu}_{Y\mid A,X}^{(t-1)},a)(X_{s},A_{s},Y_{s})

\displaystyle-D^{\prime}(\pi_{t},\widehat{\mu}_{Y\mid A,X}^{(t-1)},a^{\prime})(X_{s},A_{s},Y_{s}).

To correct the mismatch between the logging policy
\pi_{s}
at time

w_{s,t}\ :=\ \frac{\pi_{t}(A_{s}\mid X_{s})}{\pi_{s}(A_{s}\mid X_{s})}.

(\pi_{t},\widehat{\mu}^{(t-1)})
is fixed conditioned on

is drawn from the data-generating law. Define

M_{2,t}:~=~\E\!\big[\|\hat{\phi}_{s,t}(a,a^{\prime},\pi_{t})\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]

\omega_{t}^{-2}~=~M_{2,t}~-\|M_{1,t}\|^{2}
.

The corresponding importance-weighted empirical moments (based on the history up to
t-1

\displaystyle\widehat{M}_{1,t}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{t-1}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}w_{s,t}\,\hat{\phi}_{s,t}(a,a^{\prime},\pi_{t}),

(12)

\displaystyle\widehat{M}_{2,t}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{t-1}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}w_{s,t}\,\|\hat{\phi}_{s,t}(a,a^{\prime},\pi_{t})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}.

(13)

Hence, we estimate the conditional variance by

\widehat{\omega}_{t}^{-2}\ :=\ \widehat{M}_{2,t}-\big\|\widehat{M}_{1,t}\big\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}.

(14)

All terms in (
12
)–(
14
) are
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-measurable, and importance weighting accounts for policy adaptivity. We can then state the following Proposition that assesses the consistency of the plug-in conditional variance weights.

Proposition 5.1

(Pathwise consistency of the plug-in conditional variance weights)
.

Assume
3.1
,
4.2
, and
4.3
.
Let any predictable policy sequence
(\pi_{t})_{t\geq 1}
with
\pi_{t}\in\Pi
a.s. for all

. Then, along the realized data path,

\frac{\widehat{\omega}_{t}}{\omega_{t}}\ \xrightarrow[\ t\to\infty\ ]{\ \mathrm{a.s.}\ }1.

6
KTE Estimation: A Sample-Split Test

Our target is
\mathrm{KTE}(a,a^{\prime}):=\|\Psi(a,a^{\prime})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}
, where

\Psi(a,a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
is the difference of counterfactual mean embeddings.
Sections
4
–
5
provide a doubly robust, variance–stabilized
estimator of
\Psi
and a ratio–consistent estimator of its predictable variance. Consequently, a natural KTE point estimator is obtained by
taking the RKHS inner product of the CME difference with itself:

\widehat{\mathrm{KTE}}^{\,2}\ :=\ \big\langle\widehat{\Psi}_{T},\widehat{\Psi}_{T}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}},

and hence, by the continuous mapping theorem,
\widehat{\mathrm{KTE}}:=\|\widehat{\Psi}_{T}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}\xrightarrow{p}\|\Psi(a,a^{\prime})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}
. Nevertheless, testing
H_{0}:\Psi(a,a^{\prime})=0
via this direct plug–in of a single stabilized RKHS sum leads to a non–Gaussian, typically infinite
\chi^{2}
–mixture under
H_{0}
as MMD is a degenerate statistic
[
26
]
.
To recover valid type–I error, our method mirrors cross–
U
statistic in i.i.d
[
26
]
: construct
two
stabilized linear statistics on disjoint folds and take their
inner product
. In our case, because each foldwise sum is asymptotically Gaussian by a Hilbert–space martingale CLT and the disjointness of the folds yields martingale orthogonality, we can recover the same effects as in the i.i.d. case.

Test construction.

Let
T=2n
and split
\{1,\dots,T\}
into two folds
\mathcal{I}_{1},\mathcal{I}_{2}
of size
n
(folds need to be chronological). For
r\in\{1,2\}
, fit the nuisance
\hat{\mu}^{(r)}
on fold
\mathcal{I}_{3-r}
. For
t\in\mathcal{I}_{r}
define the stabilized score difference in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}^{(r)}_{t}(a,a^{\prime},\pi_{t}):=

\displaystyle D^{\prime}(\pi_{t},\hat{\mu}^{(r)};a)(X_{t},A_{t},Y_{t})

(15)

\displaystyle-D^{\prime}(\pi_{t},\hat{\mu}^{(r)};a^{\prime})(X_{t},A_{t},Y_{t}).

and let
\widehat{\omega}_{t}^{(r)}
be the foldwise variance–stabilizing weights (Section
5
),
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
–measurable. Set

\psi_{t}^{(r)}:=\widehat{\omega}_{t}^{(r)}\,\hat{\phi}^{(r)}_{t},\qquad\tau_{r}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{I}_{r}}\psi_{t}^{(r)}.

(16)

Define the cross inner product and its variance proxy

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{h}^{\dagger}:=

\displaystyle\big\langle\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}},\kern 5.0ptS_{h}^{\dagger}:=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}\big\langle\psi_{i}^{(1)},\psi_{j}^{(2)}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}.

(17)

and the studentized statistic

Sketch of proof.

We first reduce to an oracle setting. Sample splitting fixes the nuisance
\hat{\mu}^{(r)}
within each evaluation fold, so Lipschitz continuity and bounded stabilizers give

\tau_{r}=\tau_{r,\infty}+o_{\Pr}(1)
and
\langle\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\rangle=\langle\tau_{1,\infty},\tau_{2,\infty}\rangle+o_{\Pr}(1)
,
where
\tau_{r,\infty}
is the oracle stabilized sum. Each
\tau_{r,\infty}
is a Hilbert–space martingale with predictable covariance converging to
\Gamma
, and by Theorem
4.5
we obtain
\tau_{r,\infty}\ \Rightarrow\ \mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}(0,\Gamma).

Since
\mathcal{I}_{1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{2}
are disjoint, martingale orthogonality yields asymptotic independence, hence

\langle\tau_{1,\infty},\tau_{2,\infty}\rangle\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,\Tr(\Gamma^{2}))
.

For the denominator,
\widehat{\psi}_{\mathrm{cross}}
splits into a predictable part, which converges to
\Tr(\Gamma^{2})
by quadratic-variation limits, and a centered part, which vanishes by a martingale SLLN. Thus
\widehat{\psi}_{\mathrm{cross}}\xrightarrow{p}\Tr(\Gamma^{2})
. By Slutsky’s theorem,
T_{\mathrm{cross}}^{\omega}(a,a^{\prime})\ \Rightarrow\ \mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

In short, stabilization guarantees unit-variance growth for each foldwise sum, while disjoint folds give independence so that the cross inner product behaves like a Gaussian quadratic form. Full technical details appear in Appendix

Numerical Simulations

In this section, we study the empirical calibration and power of our proposed test

Adaptive data collection.

Actions follow an
\varepsilon
-greedy contextual bandit with per–arm online ridge.
At time

\hat{\theta}_{a}=S_{a}^{\dagger}b_{a}
,

.
The propensity is

\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{t},&q_{1}(t)<q_{0}(t),\\
\tfrac{1}{2},&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

8
Discussion

We introduced
VS-DR-KTE
, the first kernel test for distributional treatment effects with adaptively collected data. By pairing doubly robust scores with predictable variance stabilization, it attains Gaussian limits under history-dependent policies, yielding a well-calibrated and powerful test for both mean and higher-moment shifts. This extends adaptive inference beyond scalar ATEs to full outcome distributions. Future directions include conditional effects, richer embedding regressors, and weaker causal assumptions. More broadly, arguments based on variance-stabilized martingale of distribution embeddings appear to be a general recipe for distributional inference under adaptivity.

10.1
Review of Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces

A
positive definite kernel
on a set
\mathcal{F}
is a function
k:\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, any
w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}\in\mathcal{F}
and any
c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}c_{i}c_{j}\,k(w_{i},w_{j})\geq 0
.
By the Moore–Aronszajn theorem,
k
induces a unique Hilbert space of functions
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{F}}
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{F}}}
such that (i)
k(\cdot,w)\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{F}}
for every
w\in\mathcal{F}
, and (ii) the
reproducing property
holds:

f(w)\;=\;\langle f,\ k(\cdot,w)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{F}}}\qquad\forall f\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{F}},\ \forall w\in\mathcal{F}.

We write the associated canonical feature map as
\phi_{\mathcal{F}}(w):=k(\cdot,w)\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{F}}
. Typical choices in applications include Gaussian and Matérn kernels; when
k
is
characteristic
, mean embeddings (discussed below) are injective
[
17
, e.g.,]
.

Kernel mean embeddings (KME).

Let
W\sim P
be a random element in
\mathcal{F}
with
\E\!\big[\sqrt{k(W,W)}\big]<\infty
.
The
kernel mean embedding

[
44
]
of
P
into
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{F}}
is defined by

\mu_{P}\ :=\ \E\big[\phi_{\mathcal{F}}(W)\big]\ \in\ \mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{F}}.

The embedding vector
\mu_{P}
represents the probability distribution
P
within the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{F}}
. Given samples
(w_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
, the empirical embedding is naturally defined as the sample mean:
\hat{\mu}_{P}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{\mathcal{F}}(w_{i})
.

Conditional mean embeddings (CME).

Let
X\in\mathcal{X}
,
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
be random vatriables, with corresponding RKHSs
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{X}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
and feature maps
\phi_{\mathcal{X}},\phi_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
Define the (uncentered) covariance operators

C_{YX}\ :=\ \E\big[\phi_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y)\otimes\phi_{\mathcal{X}}(X)\big],\qquad C_{XX}\ :=\ \E\big[\phi_{\mathcal{X}}(X)\otimes\phi_{\mathcal{X}}(X)\big].

When
C_{XX}
is injective, the
conditional mean operator

\,\mathcal{C}_{Y\mid X}:\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}\,
is given by

\mathcal{C}_{Y\mid X}\ :=\ C_{YX}\,C_{XX}^{-1},\quad\text{so that}\quad\mu_{Y\mid X}(\cdot)\ =\ \mathcal{C}_{Y\mid X}\,\phi_{\mathcal{X}}(\cdot)\ =\ \E\!\big[\phi_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y)\mid X=\cdot\big]\ \in\ \mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}.

Given data
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and Gram matrix
K_{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
over
n
samples
\{x_{i}\}
, i.e.,
[K_{X}]_{i,j}=\langle\phi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{i}),\phi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{j})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{X}}}
, an
\ell_{2}
–regularized estimator is given by

\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{Y\mid X}\ =\ \Phi_{Y}\,(K_{X}+\lambda I_{n})^{-1}\,\Phi_{X}^{\top},\qquad\widehat{\mu}_{Y\mid X}(x)\ =\ \widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{Y\mid X}\,\phi_{\mathcal{X}}(x),

where
\Phi_{X}=[\phi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{1}),\ldots,\phi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{n})]
and
\Phi_{Y}=[\phi_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{1}),\ldots,\phi_{\mathcal{Y}}(y_{n})]
collect the feature maps in their columns (operator-valued notation as in §
10
),
I_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the identity matrix, and
\lambda
is the regularizer. See
Song et al. 2009
,
Li et al. 2022
.

Maximum mean discrepancy (MMD).

For distributions
P,Q
on
\mathcal{F}
, the
MMD
is the RKHS distance between their embeddings:

\mathrm{MMD}(P,Q)\ :=\ \|\mu_{P}-\mu_{Q}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{F}}}.

If
k
is characteristic,
\mathrm{MMD}(P,Q)=0
if and only if
P=Q

[
17
]
.
Given independent samples
\{w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
from
P
and
\{w^{\prime}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
from
Q
, the usual unbiased
U
–statistic estimator of
\mathrm{MMD}^{2}
is degenerate under
P=Q
. Recent
cross

U
–statistics avoid permutation calibration and yield asymptotically normal tests by sample splitting
[
26
]
. This perspective is what we leverage in our cross–fitted KTE test (Section
6
).

Embeddings for counterfactuals.

In our paper,
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{Y}
and the corresponding canonical feature map is
\phi_{\mathcal{Y}}
. The (counterfactual)
mean embedding

[
33
]
under action
a
is

\eta(a)\ :=\ \E_{P_{X}}\!\big[\,\mu_{Y\mid A,X}(a,X)\,\big]\ \in\ \mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}},

and the kernel treatment effect between
a
and
a^{\prime}
is quantified by the MMD:
\|\eta(a)-\eta(a^{\prime})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}
(see Section
3
). Estimation procedure leverages the CME
\mu_{Y\mid A,X}
and the RKHS framework discussed above.

Finite-sample operators and HS norms.

Given any
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
–valued random element
Z
with
\E\|Z\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}<\infty
, the covariance operator
\Cov(Z)=\E[(Z-\E Z)\otimes(Z-\E Z)]
is self-adjoint, positive, and trace-class, with
\Tr(\Cov(Z))=\E\|Z-\E Z\|^{2}
(cf. Eq. (
18
)).
We use Hilbert–Schmidt (HS) norms to control deviations of operator-valued quantities (Appendix
10
); empirical versions are constructed with the same
\Phi_{X},\Phi_{Y}
ingredients as above.

10.2
Review of Hilbert Schmidt Operators

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable Hilbert space and
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
the space of bounded linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
.
An operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
is called
Hilbert–Schmidt
(HS) if

\|T\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}:=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\|Te_{j}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}<\infty,

for some orthonormal basis (ONB)
(e_{j})_{j\geq 1}
of
\mathcal{H}
.
The value of
\|T\|_{\mathrm{HS}}
does not depend on the chosen ONB, and satisfies

\|T\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=\Tr(T^{\ast}T)
, where
T^{\ast}
is the adjoint of
T

[
42
]
.
The collection
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
of all HS operators forms a Hilbert space with inner product

\langle S,T\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}:=\Tr(T^{\ast}S),\qquad\|T\|_{\mathrm{HS}}=\sqrt{\langle T,T\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}}.

In finite dimensions,
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{HS}}
coincides with the Frobenius norm, and HS operators correspond to square–integrable matrices
[
42
]
.

Geometric intuition.

Hilbert–Schmidt operators can be viewed as the infinite-dimensional analogue of random matrices with finite second moment.
Each
T\in\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
acts as a “square–integrable linear map” whose columns (or images of an ONB) are
\ell_{2}
–summable in
\mathcal{H}
.
Thus, the HS norm measures the total energy of an operator in
\mathcal{H}
, and covariance operators—expectations of random rank-one tensors—are canonical examples of trace-class (and hence Hilbert–Schmidt) operators.

Spectral and ideal properties.

If
(\lambda_{j})_{j\geq 1}
denote the singular values of
T
, then

\|T\|_{\mathrm{HS}}=(\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}^{2})^{1/2}
, and
T
is HS if and only if
(\lambda_{j})_{j\geq 1}\in\ell_{2}
.
Hilbert–Schmidt operators form a two–sided ideal in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
[
42
]
:
for
A,C\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
and
B\in\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
,

\|A\,B\,C\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,\|B\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\,\|C\|_{\mathrm{op}},\qquad\|T\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\|T\|_{\mathrm{HS}}.

They satisfy
\mathcal{L}_{1}(\mathcal{H})\subset\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{H})\subset\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H})

(trace-class
\subset
HS
\subset
compact operators), and
\|T\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq\|T\|_{1}
for
T\in\mathcal{L}_{1}(\mathcal{H})

[
42
]
.

More generally, for separable Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{G}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{F}}
, the space

\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{G}},\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{F}})
consists of all bounded linear operators

T:\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{G}}\!\to\!\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{F}}
such that

\|T\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\|Te_{j}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{F}}}^{2}<\infty
for some
ONB
(e_{j})
of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{G}}
; it forms a Hilbert space with inner product

\langle S,T\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=\Tr(T^{\ast}S)
.
When
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{F}}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{G}}=\mathcal{H}
, we simply write
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
.

Rank-one and tensor operators.

For
f\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{F}}
and
g\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{G}}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{F}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{G}}
are Hilbert spaces, the tensor product operator
f\otimes g
is defined as the rank-one operator from
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{G}}
to
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{F}}
that acts on any
h\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{G}}
as

(f\otimes g)(h):=\langle g,h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{G}}}\,f,\qquad h\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{F}}.

This operator is Hilbert–Schmidt and its norm satisfies

\|f\otimes g\|_{\mathrm{HS}}=\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{F}}}\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{G}}}
. Furthermore, the inner product of such two operators is given by
\langle f\otimes g,\ f^{\prime}\otimes g^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=\langle f,f^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{F}}}\langle g,g^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{G}}}
.
These elementary tensors generate
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{G}},\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{F}})
by completion and provide the building blocks of covariance operators
[
42
]
.

Trace class operators.

A bounded, self-adjoint, positive operator
T
on a separable Hilbert space is
trace-class
if

\Tr(T):=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\langle Te_{j},e_{j}\rangle<\infty
, independently of the chosen orthonormal basis.
Equivalently, its eigenvalues are absolutely summable.

Covariance operators.

Let
W
be a square–integrable
\mathcal{H}
–valued random element (i.e.
\E\|W\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}<\infty
).
The
covariance operator
of
W
is the expected tensor product
[
23
]

\Cov(W):=\E\big[(W-\E W)\otimes(W-\E W)\big],

which is self-adjoint, positive, and trace-class.
Its trace equals the total variance:

\Tr(\Cov(W))=\E\|W-\E W\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}.

(18)

When conditioning on a
\sigma
–field
\mathcal{G}
, the predictable conditional covariance

\Cov(W\mid\mathcal{G}):=\E[(W-\E[W\mid\mathcal{G}])\otimes(W-\E[W\mid\mathcal{G}])\mid\mathcal{G}]

shares these properties almost surely.

Total variation for conditional laws.

For conditional densities
p,q
on
\mathcal{A}
(pointwise in
x
) with respect to a base measure
\mu_{\mathcal{A}}
, we use the total variation distance
[
11
]

\|q-p\|_{\mathrm{TV}}:=\tfrac{1}{2}\int|q-p|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathcal{A}}.

(19)

Perturbation inequality for conditional covariances.

The following inequality provides a useful continuity property of the covariance operator in the HS norm.

Lemma 10.1

(Covariance perturbation inequality)
.

Let
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
be a separable Hilbert space,
\mathcal{G}
a
\sigma
–field, and

U,V
be
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
–valued random elements with
\E\|U\|^{2},\E\|V\|^{2}<\infty
.
Then, almost surely,

\big\|\Cov(U\mid\mathcal{G})-\Cov(V\mid\mathcal{G})\big\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\;\leq\;\Big(\big(\E\|U\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}\big)^{1/2}+\big(\E\|V\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}\big)^{1/2}\Big)\,\big(\E\|U-V\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}\big)^{1/2}.

Proof.

Let
U_{0}:=U-\E[U\mid\mathcal{G}]
and
V_{0}:=V-\E[V\mid\mathcal{G}]
. Then

\Cov(U\mid\mathcal{G})-\Cov(V\mid\mathcal{G})=\E\big[\,U_{0}\!\otimes U_{0}-V_{0}\!\otimes V_{0}\,\big|\,\mathcal{G}\big].

Using
x\!\otimes x-y\!\otimes y=(x-y)\!\otimes x+y\!\otimes(x-y)
and

\|a\!\otimes b\|_{\mathrm{HS}}=\|a\|\,\|b\|
,

\|U_{0}\!\otimes U_{0}-V_{0}\!\otimes V_{0}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq(\|U_{0}\|+\|V_{0}\|)\,\|U_{0}-V_{0}\|.

Taking conditional expectations and applying conditional Cauchy–Schwarz to each term,

\big\|\Cov(U\mid\mathcal{G})-\Cov(V\mid\mathcal{G})\big\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq\Big(\big(\E\|U_{0}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}\big)^{1/2}+\big(\E\|V_{0}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}\big)^{1/2}\Big)\,\big(\E\|U_{0}-V_{0}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}\big)^{1/2}.

Finally,
\E\|U_{0}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}\leq\E\|U\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}
, similarly for
V
, and

\E\|U_{0}-V_{0}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}\leq\E\|U-V\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}
, yielding the stated bound.
∎

10.3
Review of Martingale Difference Sequences

Let
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a filtration and let
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be square–integrable
\mathcal{H}
–valued martingale differences:

\E[Z_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=0
and
Z_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
–measurable.
We write
\E_{t-1}[\cdot]:=\E[\cdot\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]
.

Then the sequence is
L_{2}
–orthogonal:

\E\langle Z_{s},Z_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\;=\;0\qquad\text{for all }s\neq t.

(MO)

We refer to (
MO
) as
martingale orthogonality
. It is weaker than independence but suffices to cancel cross terms in second–moment calculations.

Proof.

Assume
s<t
. Since
Z_{s}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
–measurable,

\E\langle Z_{s},Z_{t}\rangle=\E\!\big[\E(\langle Z_{s},Z_{t}\rangle\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\big]=\E\!\big[\langle Z_{s},\E(Z_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\rangle\big]=\E\langle Z_{s},0\rangle=0,

and the case
s>t
is symmetric.

Remark 10.2

(Variance identity)
.

If
(Z_{t})
is an
\mathcal{H}
–valued MDS with
\sum_{t}\E\|Z_{t}\|^{2}<\infty
, then

\E\Big\|\sum_{t=1}^{n}Z_{t}\Big\|^{2}\;=\;\sum_{t=1}^{n}\E\|Z_{t}\|^{2},

by (
MO
). In particular, martingale orthogonality ensures that cross terms vanish in second–moment expansions.

Theorem 10.3

(Strong law for martingale sums
[
19
, Thm. 2.18, p. 35]
)
.

Let
\{S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},\ \mathcal{F}_{n},\ n\geq 1\}
be a martingale and
\{U_{n},\ n\geq 1\}
a nondecreasing sequence of positive random variables such that
U_{n}
is
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
–measurable for each
n
. If
1\leq p\leq 2
then

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}U_{i}^{-1}X_{i}\ \text{converges a.s. on the set }\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}U_{i}^{-p}\,\E\!\big(|X_{i}|^{p}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\big)<\infty\Big\},

and

\lim_{n\to\infty}U_{n}^{-1}S_{n}\;=\;0\quad\text{a.s. on the set }\Big\{\lim_{n\to\infty}U_{n}=\infty,\ \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}U_{i}^{-p}\,\E\!\big(|X_{i}|^{p}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\big)<\infty\Big\}.

If
2<p<\infty
, then both conclusions hold on the set

\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}U_{i}^{-1}<\infty,\ \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}U_{i}^{\,1-p/2}\,\E\!\big(|X_{i}|^{p}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\big)<\infty\Big\}.

Remark 10.4

(How we use Theorem
10.3
)
.

Taking
p=2
and
U_{n}=n
yields

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\xrightarrow{\textup{a.s.}}0\quad\text{if}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}i^{-2}\,\E\!\big[X_{i}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\big]<\infty.

In our proofs we apply this entrywise to scalar martingale differences
X_{i}=\langle M_{i},\ e_{j}\otimes e_{k}\rangle
, for which

\E[X_{i}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]\leq\E[\|M_{i}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]
; uniform second–moment bounds then ensure
\sum_{i}i^{-2}\E[X_{i}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]<\infty
.

Definition 10.5

(2-smooth Banach space)
.

A Banach space
(X,\|\cdot\|)
is
(2,D)
–smooth
(often simply “2–smooth”) if there exists
D>0
such that for all
x,y\in X
,

\|x+y\|^{2}+\|x-y\|^{2}\ \leq\ 2\|x\|^{2}+2D^{2}\|y\|^{2}.

(20)

Equivalently, the modulus of smoothness satisfies
\rho_{X}(\tau)\leq\tfrac{D^{2}}{2}\,\tau^{2}
for all
\tau\geq 0
.

Remark 10.6

(Our setting is 2–smooth)
.

The outcome space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
is an RKHS, hence a real separable
Hilbert
space.
For Hilbert spaces the parallelogram identity gives

\|x+y\|^{2}+\|x-y\|^{2}=2\|x\|^{2}+2\|y\|^{2},

so (
20
) holds with
D=1
. Therefore all Pinelis-type martingale inequalities that require 2–smoothness apply to our analysis with the best constant
D=1
(no extra geometric assumption needed).

Theorem 10.7

(Pinelis’ martingale inequality in 2-smooth spaces
[
37
, Thm. 3.5]
)
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\|\cdot\|)
be a separable Banach space that is
(2,D)
–smooth, and let

f=(f_{j})_{j\geq 1}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X})
be a zero-mean martingale with differences

d_{j}:=f_{j}-f_{j-1}
adapted to
(\mathcal{F}_{j})_{j\geq 0}
.
Assume that for some
b_{\ast}>0
,

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\bigl\|\,\E_{j-1}\|d_{j}\|^{2}\,\bigr\|_{\infty}\;\leq\;b_{\ast}^{2}.

Then, for all
r\geq 0
,

\Pr\!\big(f^{\ast}\geq r\big)\ \leq\ 2\exp\!\left\{-\,\frac{r^{2}}{\,2D^{2}b_{\ast}^{2}\,}\right\},\qquad\text{where }\ f^{\ast}:=\sup_{j\geq 1}\|f_{j}\|.

In particular, when
\mathcal{X}
is a Hilbert space (so
D=1
), the same bound holds with
D=1
.

Remark 10.8

(How we apply Theorem
10.7
in Step 2)
.

Fix
t
and work on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
Define the martingale (in the index
u\leq t-1
)

f_{u}^{(t)}\ :=\ \sum_{s=1}^{u}\,\xi^{(t)}_{s,1},\qquad d_{s}^{(t)}:=\xi^{(t)}_{s,1},

adapted to
\mathcal{G}^{(t)}_{u}:=\sigma(\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\vee\mathcal{F}_{u})
. By construction

\E\!\big[\xi^{(t)}_{s,1}\mid\mathcal{G}^{(t)}_{s-1}\big]=0
, so
(f_{u}^{(t)})_{u\leq t-1}
is a
zero-mean
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
–valued martingale.
Step 1 gives the uniform envelope
\|\xi^{(t)}_{s,1}\|\leq 2B/c
, hence

\E\!\big[\|d_{s}^{(t)}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}^{(t)}_{s-1}\big]\ \leq\ (2B/c)^{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\E\!\big[\|d_{s}^{(t)}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}^{(t)}_{s-1}\big]\ \leq\ (t-1)\,(2B/c)^{2}.

Applying Theorem
10.7
with
D=1
and
b_{\ast}^{2}=(t-1)(2B/c)^{2}
yields, for all
r\geq 0
,

\Pr\!\Big(\max_{u\leq t-1}\big\|f_{u}^{(t)}\big\|\geq r\Big)\ \leq\ 2\exp\!\Big\{-\,\frac{r^{2}}{2(t-1)(2B/c)^{2}}\Big\}.

Choosing
r=\varepsilon\,(t-1)
and summing over
t
shows by Borel–Cantelli that

\frac{1}{t-1}\max_{u\leq t-1}\|f_{u}^{(t)}\|\to 0
almost surely; in particular

\frac{1}{t-1}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\xi^{(t)}_{s,1}\to 0
a.s. The scalar case

i=2
is identical (work in
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}
), giving the Step 2 averages
\to 0
a.s.

Definition 10.9

(Gaussian measure on a Hilbert space)
.

Let
(\mathcal{H},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
be a real separable Hilbert space.
A random element
\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{H}
is said to be
Gaussian
if, for every
u\in\mathcal{H}
,
the real-valued random variable
\langle\mathcal{G},u\rangle
follows a Gaussian distribution.
It is
centered
when
\E[\mathcal{G}]=0
.
If its covariance operator
\Gamma:=\E[\mathcal{G}\otimes\mathcal{G}]
is self-adjoint, positive,
and trace-class, we write

\mathcal{G}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{H}}(0,\Gamma).

Equivalently, for all
u\in\mathcal{H}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the moment generating function satisfies

\E\!\left[\exp\{t\,\langle u,\mathcal{G}\rangle\}\right]=\exp\!\left(\tfrac{t^{2}}{2}\,\langle\Gamma u,u\rangle\right).

Theorem 10.10

(Hilbert-space martingale CLT
[
5
, Thm. 2.16]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a real separable Hilbert space and let
(Z_{t},\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be square-integrable
\mathcal{H}
–valued martingale differences
(
\E[Z_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=0
). Let
(e_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be a fixed ONB of
\mathcal{H}
.
Assume, as
T\to\infty
:

(B1)

T^{-1/2}\,\E\!\big[\max_{1\leq t\leq T}\|Z_{t}\|\big]\to 0
.

(B2)

For all
j,k\geq 1
,

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle Z_{t},e_{j}\rangle\,\langle Z_{t},e_{k}\rangle\ \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\ \psi_{jk}
, for some real
(\psi_{jk})
.

(B3)

With
r_{N}^{2}(x):=\sum_{j=N}^{\infty}\langle x,e_{j}\rangle^{2}
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\ \limsup_{T\to\infty}\ \Pr\!\Big(r_{N}^{2}\!\big(T^{-1/2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}Z_{t}\big)>\varepsilon\Big)=0
for all
\varepsilon>0
.

Then
T^{-1/2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}Z_{t}\ \Rightarrow\ \mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{H}}(0,\Gamma)
, where

\langle\Gamma e_{j},e_{k}\rangle=\psi_{jk}
, and
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{H}}(0,\Gamma)
is the centered Gaussian measure
on
\mathcal{H}
with covariance operator
\Gamma
.

Remark 10.11
.

(B1) is a negligibility condition (no big jumps). (B2) is convergence of the empirical quadratic variation,
asymptotically equivalent (under a martingale LLN) to convergence of the predictable covariance

\Gamma_{T}:=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}\E[Z_{t}\otimes Z_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]
. (B3) is a tightness condition controlling
the “tail” in infinite dimensions; it is automatic in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A convenient sufficient route is:

\Gamma_{T}\to\Gamma
in trace or HS norm and
\Tr((I-P_{N})\Gamma)\to 0
, which yields

\E\,r_{N}^{2}(T^{-1/2}\sum_{t}Z_{t})=\Tr((I-P_{N})\,\E\widehat{\Gamma}_{T})\to 0
by Markov.

We now analyze the asymptotic behavior of our estimator of the kernel treatment effect (KTE) using tools from the theory of weak convergence in Hilbert spaces. We restate the main theorem for convenience.

Theorem
4.5

(Asymptotic normality of the stabilized RKHS estimator)

Under Assumptions

,
4.1
,
4.2
,

(26)

We now state the first quadratic-variation convergence below.

Lemma 11.1

(Quadratic-variation convergence)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
4.2
,
3.1
,
4.3
,
4.4
,
4.1
,
4.3
hold.
Then the predictable covariance

\Gamma_{T}:=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E[Z_{t}\otimes Z_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]

converges almost surely in Hilbert–Schmidt norm to a positive trace-class operator

\Gamma\in\mathcal{L}_{1}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}})
.

Proof.

Let
\mu_{\infty}
be the
L_{2}
–limit of the nuisance
\hat{\mu}_{Y|A,X}^{(t)}
and
\pi_{\infty}
the limit of
\pi_{t}
. Our goal is to show

\Gamma_{T}:=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E[Z_{t}\otimes Z_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\to\widetilde{\Sigma}(\pi_{\infty},\mu_{\infty})

a.s. in HS norm.
The proof proceeds in three steps, each controlling one term in the decomposition

\underbrace{\Gamma_{T}-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t}}_{\text{Step 1}}\;+\;\underbrace{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big(\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t}-\widetilde{\Sigma}(\pi_{t},\mu_{\infty})\big)}_{\text{Step 2}}\;+\;\underbrace{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big(\widetilde{\Sigma}(\pi_{t},\mu_{\infty})-\widetilde{\Sigma}(\pi_{\infty},\mu_{\infty})\big)}_{\text{Step 3}}.

Step 1:

We start by writing

\Gamma_{T}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\widehat{\omega}_{t-1}^{\,2}\,\Sigma_{t}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Big(\tfrac{\widehat{\omega}_{t-1}}{\omega_{t-1}}\Big)^{\!2}\,\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t}.

Set
r_{t}:=(\widehat{\omega}_{t-1}/\omega_{t-1})^{2}
. Then

\Big\|\Gamma_{T}-\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t}\Big\|_{\mathrm{HS}}=\Big\|\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(r_{t}-1)\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t}\Big\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}|r_{t}-1|,

since
\|\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq 1
. By Assumption
4.4
(i),

r_{t}\to 1
a.s., and by (ii) plus strong positivity and the efficiency bound,
(r_{t})
is a.s. bounded. Hence,
by the Cesàro/Toeplitz lemma,
\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}|r_{t}-1|\to 0
a.s., proving the following:

\Big\|\Gamma_{T}-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t}\Big\|_{\rm HS}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\ 0.

(27)

Step 2:

Next, under Assumptions
4.2
and
3.1
, the IPW factors
1/\pi_{t}
and the feature norms
\|\phi_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y)\|
are uniformly bounded. Let

\Delta_{t}(b,x):=\widehat{\mu}^{(t-1)}_{Y\mid A,X}(b,x)-\mu_{\infty}(b,x).

For any fixed
b\in\mathcal{A}
,

D^{\prime}(\pi_{t},\widehat{\mu}^{(t-1)};b)-D^{\prime}(\pi_{t},\mu_{\infty};b)=-\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{A=b\}}{\pi_{t}(b\mid X)}\,\Delta_{t}(A,X)\;+\;\Delta_{t}(b,X).

Hence, using
\|u+v\|^{2}\leq 2\|u\|^{2}+2\|v\|^{2}
and strong positivity (Assumption
3.1
),

\displaystyle\E\!\big[\|D^{\prime}(\pi_{t},\widehat{\mu}^{(t-1)};b)-D^{\prime}(\pi_{t},\mu_{\infty};b)\|^{2}\,\big|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\E\!\Big[\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{A=b\}}{\pi_{t}(b\mid X)^{2}}\,\|\Delta_{t}(A,X)\|^{2}\ \Big|\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\Big]+2\,\E\!\big[\|\Delta_{t}(b,X)\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]

\displaystyle=2\,\E\!\Big[\frac{1}{\pi_{t}(b\mid X)}\,\|\Delta_{t}(b,X)\|^{2}\ \Big|\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\Big]+2\,\E\!\big[\|\Delta_{t}(b,X)\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]

\displaystyle\leq 2\Big(\tfrac{1}{c}+1\Big)\,\E\!\big[\|\Delta_{t}(b,X)\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big].

Applying this for
b=a
and
b=a^{\prime}
and using the fact
\|u-v\|^{2}\leq 2\|u\|^{2}+2\|v\|^{2}
gives

\displaystyle\E\!\big[\|\hat{\phi}_{t}-\phi(\pi_{t},\mu_{\infty})\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\;C(c)\,\Big(\E\!\big[\|\Delta_{t}(a,X)\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]+\E\!\big[\|\Delta_{t}(a^{\prime},X)\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]\Big)

(28)

\displaystyle\leq\;C(c)\,\big\|\widehat{\mu}^{(t-1)}_{Y\mid A,X}-\mu_{\infty}\big\|_{L_{2}(P_{X}\times\mu_{\mathcal{A}})}^{2}.

Indeed, in the finite-action case (with
\mu_{\mathcal{A}}
the counting measure),

\big\|\widehat{\mu}^{(t-1)}_{Y\mid A,X}-\mu_{\infty}\big\|_{L_{2}(P_{X}\times\mu_{\mathcal{A}})}^{2}=\E_{X}\!\Big[\sum_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\|\Delta_{t}(b,X)\|^{2}\Big]\ \geq\ \E\|\Delta_{t}(a,X)\|^{2}+\E\|\Delta_{t}(a^{\prime},X)\|^{2},

and the same inequality holds conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
. Next, by Lemma
10.1
(applied conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
) with
U:=\hat{\phi}_{t}
and
V:=\phi(\pi_{t},\mu_{\infty})

we obtain

\|\Sigma_{t}-\Sigma(\pi_{t},\mu_{\infty})\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\;\leq\;\Big(\big(\E\|\hat{\phi}_{t}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big)^{1/2}+\big(\E\|\phi(\pi_{t},\mu_{\infty})\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big)^{1/2}\Big)\,\big(\E\|\hat{\phi}_{t}-\phi(\pi_{t},\mu_{\infty})\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big)^{1/2}.

Under Assumptions
4.2
and
3.1
, the first (sum) factor is uniformly bounded in
t
, and by (
28
) above, the second factor is controlled by the
L_{2}(P_{X}\times\mu_{\mathcal{A}})
error of
\widehat{\mu}^{(t-1)}
. Therefore,

\|\Sigma_{t}-\Sigma(\pi_{t},\mu_{\infty})\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\ \lesssim\ \big\|\widehat{\mu}^{(t-1)}_{Y\mid A,X}-\mu_{\infty}\big\|_{L_{2}(P_{X}\times\mu_{\mathcal{A}})}\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{a.s.}\ }0.

Finally, by continuity of the trace,
\Tr(\Sigma_{t})\to\Tr(\Sigma(\pi_{t},\mu_{\infty}))
, and therefore

\|\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t}-\widetilde{\Sigma}(\pi_{t},\mu_{\infty})\|_{\rm HS}\ \lesssim\ \|\Sigma_{t}-\Sigma(\pi_{t},\mu_{\infty})\|_{\rm HS}+\big|\Tr(\Sigma_{t})-\Tr(\Sigma(\pi_{t},\mu_{\infty}))\big|\ \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}{}0.

(29)

Step 3:

Our goal is to control the sensitivity of
\Sigma(\pi,\mu_{\infty})
to changes in
\pi
.
For any bounded measurable operator-valued
h:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}})
with

\|h\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{x,a}\|h(x,a)\|_{\rm HS}<\infty
, define

H(\pi)\ :=\ \E_{X\sim P_{X}}\Big[\int h(X,a)\,\pi(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}a\mid X)\Big].

Then, for any conditional laws
\pi(\cdot\mid x),\pi^{\prime}(\cdot\mid x)
,

\displaystyle\big\|H(\pi)-H(\pi^{\prime})\big\|_{\rm HS}

\displaystyle\leq\int\Big\|\int h(x,a)\,\big(\pi-\pi^{\prime}\big)(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}a\mid x)\Big\|_{\rm HS}\,P_{X}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)

(30)

\displaystyle\leq\int\Big(\int\|h(x,a)\|_{\rm HS}\,\big|\pi-\pi^{\prime}\big|(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}a\mid x)\Big)\,P_{X}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\|h\|_{\infty}\int\|\pi(\cdot\mid x)-\pi^{\prime}(\cdot\mid x)\|_{\rm TV}\,P_{X}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x),

using the Bochner triangle inequality and
\int|q-p|\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=2\|q-p\|_{\rm TV}
.

Next, under strong positivity (Assumption
3.1
) and bounded kernel
(Assumption
4.2
), the maps

(x,a)\ \mapsto\ \E\!\big[\phi(\pi,\mu_{\infty})\mid X\!=\!x,A\!=\!a\big],\qquad(x,a)\ \mapsto\ \E\!\big[\phi(\pi,\mu_{\infty})\otimes\phi(\pi,\mu_{\infty})\mid X\!=\!x,A\!=\!a\big]

are uniformly bounded in HS norm, and—when
\mathcal{A}
is finite—are pointwise Lipschitz in
\pi
since

\big|1/\pi(b\mid x)-1/\pi^{\prime}(b\mid x)\big|\leq c^{-2}\,|\pi(b\mid x)-\pi^{\prime}(b\mid x)|.

Define

m(\pi):=\E_{P_{X},\pi}[\phi(\pi,\mu_{\infty})],\qquad Q(\pi):=\E_{P_{X},\pi}[\phi(\pi,\mu_{\infty})\otimes\phi(\pi,\mu_{\infty})],\qquad\Sigma(\pi,\mu_{\infty})=Q(\pi)-m(\pi)\otimes m(\pi).

Then

\|\Sigma(\pi_{t},\mu_{\infty})-\Sigma(\pi_{\infty},\mu_{\infty})\|_{\rm HS}\ \leq\ \|Q(\pi_{t})-Q(\pi_{\infty})\|_{\rm HS}+\|m(\pi_{t})\otimes m(\pi_{t})-m(\pi_{\infty})\otimes m(\pi_{\infty})\|_{\rm HS}.

(31)

(i) Second moment.
Let

g_{\pi}(x,a):=\E[\phi(\pi,\mu_{\infty})\otimes\phi(\pi,\mu_{\infty})\mid X\!=\!x,A\!=\!a]
.
We split

Q(\pi_{t})-Q(\pi_{\infty})=\E_{X}\!\Big[\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\big(g_{\pi_{t}}-g_{\pi_{\infty}}\big)(X,a)\,\pi_{t}(a\mid X)\Big]+\E_{X}\!\Big[\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}g_{\pi_{\infty}}(X,a)\,\big(\pi_{t}-\pi_{\infty}\big)(a\mid X)\Big].

(32)

We begin by bounding the first term in (
32
).
Conditioning on the covariates and the treatment
X=x,A=a
and using
\mu=\mu_{\infty}
, we obtain

\phi(\pi,\mu)=\Big(\tfrac{\phi_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y)-\mu(a,x)}{\pi(a\mid x)}+\mu(a,x)\Big)-\mu(a^{\prime},x),

so

\phi(\pi,\mu)-\phi(\pi^{\prime},\mu)=\Big(\tfrac{1}{\pi(a\mid x)}-\tfrac{1}{\pi^{\prime}(a\mid x)}\Big)\big(\phi_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y)-\mu(a,x)\big).

By strong positivity and bounded kernel,

\|\phi_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y)-\mu(a,x)\|\leq C
and

\big|1/\pi(a\mid x)-1/\pi^{\prime}(a\mid x)\big|\leq c^{-2}\,|\pi(a\mid x)-\pi^{\prime}(a\mid x)|
.
Using
\|u\otimes u-v\otimes v\|_{\rm HS}\leq(\|u\|+\|v\|)\|u-v\|
and taking the conditional expectation,

\|g_{\pi}(x,a)-g_{\pi^{\prime}}(x,a)\|_{\rm HS}\;\leq\;L_{g}\,|\pi(a\mid x)-\pi^{\prime}(a\mid x)|\;\leq\;2L_{g}\,\|\pi(\cdot\mid x)-\pi^{\prime}(\cdot\mid x)\|_{\rm TV}.

Hence,

\Big\|\E_{X}\!\Big[\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\big(g_{\pi_{t}}-g_{\pi_{\infty}}\big)(X,a)\,\pi_{t}(a\mid X)\Big]\Big\|_{\rm HS}\;\leq\;L_{g}\int\|\pi_{t}(\cdot\mid x)-\pi_{\infty}(\cdot\mid x)\|_{\rm TV}\,P_{X}(dx).

We now turn to bounding the second term in (
32
). We
apply (
30
) with
h(x,a)=g_{\pi_{\infty}}(x,a)
(note that
\|h\|_{\infty}<\infty
by bounded kernel and positivity):

\Big\|\E_{X}\!\Big[\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}g_{\pi_{\infty}}(X,a)\,(\pi_{t}-\pi_{\infty})(a\mid X)\Big]\Big\|_{\rm HS}\;\leq\;2\|g_{\pi_{\infty}}\|_{\infty}\int\|\pi_{t}(\cdot\mid x)-\pi_{\infty}(\cdot\mid x)\|_{\rm TV}\,P_{X}(dx).

Combining the two bounds,

\|Q(\pi_{t})-Q(\pi_{\infty})\|_{\rm HS}\;\leq\;(L_{g}+2\|g_{\pi_{\infty}}\|_{\infty})\int\|\pi_{t}(\cdot\mid x)-\pi_{\infty}(\cdot\mid x)\|_{\rm TV}\,P_{X}(dx).

(ii) Mean outer product.
Let
h_{\pi}(x,a):=\E[\phi(\pi,\mu_{\infty})\mid X\!=\!x,A\!=\!a]
.
By the same reasoning as above and using Equation (
30
), we have

\|m(\pi_{t})-m(\pi_{\infty})\|\ \leq\ C\!\int\!\|\pi_{t}(\cdot\mid x)-\pi_{\infty}(\cdot\mid x)\|_{\rm TV}\,P_{X}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x).

Hence, using the vector-operator norm inequality for tensor products
\|u\otimes u-v\otimes v\|_{\rm HS}\leq(\|u\|+\|v\|)\|u-v\|
together with the uniform
L_{2}
bounds on
m(\pi)
, we obtain

\|m(\pi_{t})\otimes m(\pi_{t})-m(\pi_{\infty})\otimes m(\pi_{\infty})\|_{\rm HS}\ \leq\ C\int\|\pi_{t}(\cdot\mid x)-\pi_{\infty}(\cdot\mid x)\|_{\rm TV}\,P_{X}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x).

Combining this result with Equation (
31
), we establish the bound on the difference of the covariance operators

\big\|\Sigma(\pi_{t},\mu_{\infty})-\Sigma(\pi_{\infty},\mu_{\infty})\big\|_{\rm HS}\ \leq\ C\int\|\pi_{t}(\cdot\mid x)-\pi_{\infty}(\cdot\mid x)\|_{\rm TV}\,P_{X}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x),

where
C
depends only on
c
,
\kappa
, and the uniform bound on
\mu_{\infty}
. Finally, Assumption
4.1
ensures that there is a uniform trace lower bound. This implies that the normalization

\Sigma\mapsto\widetilde{\Sigma}:=\Sigma/\Tr(\Sigma)
is Lipschitz on the relevant set. Hence

\big\|\widetilde{\Sigma}(\pi_{t},\mu_{\infty})-\widetilde{\Sigma}(\pi_{\infty},\mu_{\infty})\big\|_{\rm HS}\ \leq\ C^{\prime}\int\|\pi_{t}(\cdot\mid x)-\pi_{\infty}(\cdot\mid x)\|_{\rm TV}\,P_{X}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x).

Taking Cesàro averages and invoking Assumption
4.3
yields

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\widetilde{\Sigma}(\pi_{t},\mu_{\infty})-\widetilde{\Sigma}(\pi_{\infty},\mu_{\infty})\|_{\rm HS}\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{a.s.}\ }\ 0.

(33)

Conclusion

: Therefore, combining Equations (
29
) and (
33
), we obtain

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t}\ \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}{}\ \widetilde{\Sigma}(\pi_{\infty},\mu_{\infty}).

Equation (
27
) then yields
\Gamma_{T}\to\widetilde{\Sigma}(\pi_{\infty},\mu_{\infty})
in HS a.s.
Since the operator

is continuous in

Remark 11.2

(iii), all inverse propensities are uniformly bounded, so neither weighted nuisance control nor explicit exploration–decay rates

]

We now provide an additional lemma on the convergence of the inverse of the average of conditional variance estimators.

Lemma 11.3

(Average stabilizer)
.

Let
\widehat{\omega}_{t}
be estimators with ratio consistency
\widehat{\omega}_{t}/\omega_{t}\to 1
a.s.
Suppose Assumptions
3.1
,
4.2
,
4.3
,
4.3
,
4.1
hold. Then,

\Lambda_{T}\ :=\ \Big(\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\widehat{\omega}_{t-1}\Big)^{-1}\ \xrightarrow[\ T\to\infty\ ]{\ \mathrm{a.s.}\ }\ \lambda_{\star}^{-1}\ \in(0,\infty),

for some
\lambda_{\star}\in(0,\infty)
.

Proof.

Set

z\ :=\ \Tr\!\big(\Sigma(\pi_{\infty},\mu_{\infty})\big)\ \in(0,\infty),\qquad\lambda_{\star}\ :=\ z^{-1/2}.

Each
\hat{\omega}_{t}
approximates
\omega_{t}
, which is the inverse conditional standard deviation, i.e.

\omega_{t}^{-2}=\Tr(\Sigma_{t})
by (

). So it is enough to
control the Cesàro average of
\Tr(\Sigma_{t})
.
(A) We show
\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Tr(\Sigma_{t})\to c
using the same

, in particular the TV–Lipschitz bound
(

(B) We pass from traces to
\omega_{t}
via the continuous map

on a positive bounded interval.
(C) We replace
\omega_{t}
by
\widehat{\omega}_{t-1}
using ratio consistency
(Assumption
4.4
(i)).
Together these give
\Lambda_{T}\to\lambda_{\star}^{-1}
.

Step 1:

In this part, we will control the Cesàro convergence of traces. We first show

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big|\Tr(\Sigma_{t})-z\big|\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{a.s.}\ }\ 0.

(34)

Decompose

\big|\Tr(\Sigma_{t})-z\big|\ \leq\ \underbrace{\big|\Tr(\Sigma_{t})-\Tr(\Sigma(\pi_{t},\mu_{\infty}))\big|}_{\text{(A1)}}\ +\ \underbrace{\big|\Tr(\Sigma(\pi_{t},\mu_{\infty}))-\Tr(\Sigma(\pi_{\infty},\mu_{\infty}))\big|}_{\text{(A2)}}.

(A1)
By Step 2 of Lemma
11.1
, we have

\|\Sigma_{t}-\Sigma(\pi_{t},\mu_{\infty})\|_{\rm HS}\to 0
a.s.
Under our boundedness assumptions (Assumptions
4.2
,
3.1
),
all these covariance operators are uniformly trace-class with uniformly bounded second moments.
Since the trace is continuous along Hilbert–Schmidt convergent sequences in this uniformly bounded set,

\big|\Tr(\Sigma_{t})-\Tr(\Sigma(\pi_{t},\mu_{\infty}))\big|\to 0
a.s., hence its Cesàro average vanishes.

(A2)
Write

We write

. This centers the stabilized sum and puts us in the setting of a Hilbert-space martingale CLT.

We now verify (B1)–(B3) of Theorem

for

(B1) Negligibility.

Recall

(B2) Covariance (quadratic-variation) convergence.

Let

\Gamma_{T}:=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E[Z_{t}\otimes Z_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\widehat{\omega}_{t-1}^{\,2}\ \Sigma_{t},\qquad\Sigma_{t}:=\Cov(\hat{\phi}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}).

By Lemma

\Gamma_{T}\to\Gamma

\Gamma
.

Uniform fourth–moment bound for
Z_{t}
.

Write
Z_{t}=\widehat{\omega}_{t-1}\big(\phi_{t}-\E[\hat{\phi}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\big)
.
By leveraging the assumptions of a bounded kernel and strong positivity (Assumptions
4.2
,
3.1
), and incorporating the uniform

bound on
\widehat{\mu}^{(t-1)}
(Assumption
4.3
), there exists a finite constant

\|D^{\prime}(\pi_{t},\widehat{\mu}^{(t-1)};b)\|\leq B\quad(b\in\mathcal{A}),\qquad\|\hat{\phi}_{t}\|\leq 2B,\qquad\big\|\hat{\phi}_{t}-\E[\hat{\phi}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\big\|\leq 4B.

Let
\Sigma_{t}=\Cov(\hat{\phi}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})
and
\omega_{t}^{-2}=\Tr(\Sigma_{t})
. As explained in the Step 2 of the proof of Lemma
11.3
, by strong positivity, bounded kernel, and the efficiency bound, there exist
0<\eta\leq M_{\Sigma}<\infty
and
T_{0}
such that

\eta\leq\Tr(\Sigma_{t})\leq M_{\Sigma}
for all

; ratio consistency (Assumption

) then implies
\widehat{\omega}_{t-1}\leq C_{\omega}
eventually, and the finitely many initial terms have finite fourth moments. Hence, we have
\sup_{t}\E[\widehat{\omega}_{t-1}^{4}]<\infty
and

\sup_{t}\E\|Z_{t}\|^{4}=\sup_{t}\E\!\Big[\widehat{\omega}_{t-1}^{4}\,\big\|\phi_{t}-\E(\phi_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\big\|^{4}\Big]\ \leq\ (4B)^{4}\,\sup_{t}\E[\widehat{\omega}_{t-1}^{4}]\ <\ \infty.

L_{2}
control of
\widehat{\Gamma}_{T}-\Gamma_{T}
.

Set
\widehat{\Gamma}_{T}:=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}Z_{t}\otimes Z_{t}
and

\E\|M_{t}\|_{\rm HS}^{2}=\E\big[\Var(Z_{t}\otimes Z_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\big]\ \leq\ \E\|Z_{t}\otimes Z_{t}\|_{\rm HS}^{2}=\E\|Z_{t}\|^{4}\ \leq\ C,

where we use the facts that

yields

\E\Big\|\widehat{\Gamma}_{T}-\Gamma_{T}\Big\|_{\rm HS}^{2}=\E\Big\|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}M_{t}\Big\|_{\rm HS}^{2}=\frac{1}{T^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E\|M_{t}\|_{\rm HS}^{2}\ \leq\ \frac{C}{T}\ \to\ 0.

Consequently, we conclude that
\widehat{\Gamma}_{T}-\Gamma_{T}\to 0
in

which implies convergence in probability.

Entrywise convergence.

Fix an ONB
(e_{j})_{j\geq 1}
of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
and define the scalar martingale differences

From

Therefore,

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle Z_{t},e_{j}\rangle\,\langle Z_{t},e_{k}\rangle=\big\langle\widehat{\Gamma}_{T}e_{j},e_{k}\big\rangle=\big\langle\Gamma_{T}e_{j},e_{k}\big\rangle+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}m_{t,jk}\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{a.s.}\ }\ \big\langle\Gamma e_{j},e_{k}\big\rangle,

since
\Gamma_{T}\to\Gamma
a.s. in HS (hence entrywise). This verifies exactly (B2).

(B3) Tail/tightness in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
.

Let
S_{T}:=T^{-1/2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}Z_{t}
and
P_{>N}
be the orthogonal projection onto

(e_{j})
.
For any

and bounded
B

\Tr\!\big(B(w\otimes w)\big)=\langle Bw,w\rangle
. With
B=P_{>N}
(note
P_{>N}=P_{>N}^{\ast}=P_{>N}^{2}
),

\mathbb{E}\|P_{>N}S_{T}\|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\langle P_{>N}S_{T},P_{>N}S_{T}\rangle=\mathbb{E}\langle S_{T},P_{>N}S_{T}\rangle=\Tr\!\big(P_{>N}\,\mathbb{E}[S_{T}\!\otimes S_{T}]\,P_{>N}\big).

Since
S_{T}=T^{-1/2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}Z_{t}
and
(Z_{t})
are martingale differences, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[S_{T}\!\otimes S_{T}]=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{s,t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[Z_{s}\!\otimes Z_{t}]=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[Z_{t}\!\otimes Z_{t}]=\mathbb{E}\,\Gamma_{T}.

Hence,

\mathbb{E}\|P_{>N}S_{T}\|^{2}=\Tr\!\big(P_{>N}\,\mathbb{E}\,\Gamma_{T}\,P_{>N}\big)=\mathbb{E}\,\Tr\!\big(P_{>N}\Gamma_{T}P_{>N}\big)=\mathbb{E}\,\Tr(\Gamma_{T}P_{>N}).

Here we used linearity of
\Tr
and
\mathbb{E}
(Fubini/Tonelli is valid since

is uniformly bounded), and the cyclicity rule for traces with a trace–class
A
and bounded

\Tr(PAP)=\Tr(AP^{2})=\Tr(AP),

since

.

Each

is positive trace–class with

\Tr(\mathbb{E}[Z_{t}\otimes Z_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}])=\mathbb{E}[\|Z_{t}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]

\Gamma_{T}
and

are positive trace–class and the traces above are well-defined. By Lemma

,
\Gamma_{T}\to\Gamma
a.s. in HS. Passing to coordinates in the ONB and using dominated convergence (the traces are uniformly bounded) yields

\mathbb{E}\|P_{>N}S_{T}\|^{2}\ \longrightarrow\ \Tr(P_{>N}\Gamma)\qquad(T\to\infty).

Since
\Gamma
is trace–class,
\Tr(P_{>N}\Gamma)\to 0
as

\limsup_{T\to\infty}\Pr\big(\|P_{>N}S_{T}\|>\varepsilon\big)\leq\varepsilon^{-2}\,\Tr(P_{>N}\Gamma)\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}0,

which verifies (B3).

By (B1)–(B3), Bosq’s Hilbert-space MCLT (Theorem

T^{-1/2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}Z_{t}\xRightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}(0,\Gamma)

By the average-stabilizer Lemma

Applying Slutsky’s lemma to (

\sqrt{T}\big(\widehat{\Psi}_{T}-\Psi\big)\ \xRightarrow{d}\ \mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}\big(0,\ \lambda_{\star}^{2}\Gamma\big).

Renaming

\Gamma

∎

12
Analysis of the Conditional-Variance Estimator

We now prove pathwise (a.s.) consistency of the plug-in conditional variance/covariance estimators. In brief, we rewrite the targets as importance–weighted moments under the
evaluation
policy at time
t
, apply a martingale SLLN uniformly over predictable policies (enabled by strong positivity and the bounded kernel), and then transfer these uniform LLNs to consistency by continuity.

Proposition
5.1

(Consistency of the adaptive variance weights)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
,
4.2
, and
4.3
hold.
Let any predictable policy sequence
(\pi_{t})_{t\geq 1}
with
\pi_{t}\in\Pi
a.s. for all
t
. Then, along the realized data path, , the estimated inverse variance weight converges to its true value almost surely:

\widehat{\omega}_{t}^{-2}\ \xrightarrow[\ t\to\infty\ ]{\ \mathrm{a.s.}\ }\ \omega_{t}^{-2},\qquad\text{hence}\qquad\frac{\widehat{\omega}_{t}}{\omega_{t}}\ \xrightarrow[\ t\to\infty\ ]{\ \mathrm{a.s.}\ }1.

(This final ratio convergence relies on the fact that, due to the strong positivity assumption and the efficiency bound,
\omega_{t}
is eventually bounded away from
0
and
\infty

Proof.

We recall the main text definitions for clarity. For each
t
and
s<t
,

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{s,t}:=\hat{\phi}_{s,t}(a,a^{\prime},\pi_{t})

\displaystyle:=D^{\prime}(\pi_{t},\widehat{\mu}_{Y\mid A,X}^{(t-1)};a)(X_{s},A_{s},Y_{s})-D^{\prime}(\pi_{t},\widehat{\mu}_{Y\mid A,X}^{(t-1)};a^{\prime})(X_{s},A_{s},Y_{s}),

\displaystyle w_{s,t}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\pi_{t}(A_{s}\mid X_{s})}{\pi_{s}(A_{s}\mid X_{s})},

\displaystyle\widehat{M}_{1,t}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{t-1}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}w_{s,t}\,\hat{\phi}_{s,t}(a,a^{\prime},\pi_{t}),\qquad\widehat{M}_{2,t}:=\frac{1}{t-1}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}w_{s,t}\,\|\hat{\phi}_{s,t}(a,a^{\prime},\pi_{t})\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}},

\displaystyle\widehat{\omega}_{t}^{-2}

\displaystyle:=\widehat{M}_{2,t}-\|\widehat{M}_{1,t}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}.

The one–step (conditional) targets under the
evaluation
policy
\pi_{t}
are

M_{1,t}:=\E\!\big[\hat{\phi}_{t,t}(a,a^{\prime},\pi_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big],\qquad M_{2,t}:=\E\!\big[\|\hat{\phi}_{t,t}(a,a^{\prime},\pi_{t})\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big],

so
\omega_{t}^{-2}=M_{2,t}-\|M_{1,t}\|^{2}
.

The proof essentially boils down to establishing a pathwise uniform law of large number for the quantities
M_{1,t},M_{2,t}
. Specifically, we will demonstrate the following almost sure convergence results:

\widehat{M}_{1,t}-M_{1,t}\ \longrightarrow\ 0\quad\text{a.s. in }\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}},\qquad\widehat{M}_{2,t}-M_{2,t}\ \longrightarrow\ 0\quad\text{a.s.}

Step 1 (uniform envelope).

By strong positivity (Assumption
3.1
) and the bounded kernel
(Assumption
4.2
), set

M:=\sup_{t\geq 1}\ \sup_{b\in\mathcal{A},x\in\mathcal{X}}\ \big\|\widehat{\mu}^{(t-1)}_{Y\mid A,X}(b,x)\big\|<\infty,\qquad\|\phi_{\mathcal{Y}}(y)\|\leq\sqrt{\kappa},\qquad\pi_{t}(b\mid x)\geq c.

Then, for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
y\in\mathcal{Y}
,
b\in\mathcal{A}
,

\big\|D^{\prime}(\pi_{t},\widehat{\mu}^{(t-1)};b)(x,b,y)\big\|\leq\frac{1}{c}\big(\sqrt{\kappa}+M\big)+M.

Hence, for all
t\geq 2
, all
s<t
, all
a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
, and
P
-a.s. in
(X_{s},A_{s},Y_{s})
,

\big\|\hat{\phi}_{s,t}(a,a^{\prime},\pi_{t})\big\|\leq B,\qquad 0\leq w_{s,t}=\frac{\pi_{t}(A_{s}\mid X_{s})}{\pi_{s}(A_{s}\mid X_{s})}\leq\frac{1}{c}\ \Rightarrow\ \begin{cases}\ \big\|w_{s,t}\,\hat{\phi}_{s,t}(a,a^{\prime},\pi_{t})\big\|\leq\dfrac{B}{c},\\[6.0pt]
\ 0\leq w_{s,t}\,\big\|\hat{\phi}_{s,t}(a,a^{\prime},\pi_{t})\big\|^{2}\leq\dfrac{B^{2}}{c},\end{cases}

(36)

where one can take
B:=\dfrac{\sqrt{\kappa}+M}{c}+2M
.
The constant

is deterministic and depends only on
(c,\kappa,M)
; in particular, it does
not

depend on
s,t
, on the realized data, nor on the particular predictable policy sequence
(\pi_{t})
.

Step 2 (martingale decomposition and a.s. convergence via Pinelis).

Fix
t
and set
\mathcal{G}^{(t)}_{s}
the
\sigma
–field that contains the information up to time
s

and the information frozen at time

.
Because
\mathcal{F}_{t}
is a filtration we have:

\mathcal{G}^{(t)}_{s}:=\sigma\!\big(\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\vee\mathcal{F}_{s}\big)=\mathcal{F}_{t-1},\qquad u\leq t-1.

Define the (vector/scalar) martingale differences

\displaystyle\xi^{(t)}_{s,1}:

\displaystyle=w_{s,t}\,\hat{\phi}_{s,t}(a,a^{\prime},\pi_{t})-\E\!\big[w_{s,t}\,\hat{\phi}_{s,t}(a,a^{\prime},\pi_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}},

\displaystyle\xi^{(t)}_{s,2}:

\displaystyle=w_{s,t}\,\|\hat{\phi}_{s,t}(a,a^{\prime},\pi_{t})\|^{2}-\E\!\big[w_{s,t}\,\|\hat{\phi}_{s,t}(a,a^{\prime},\pi_{t})\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]\in\mathbb{R},

From the envelope in Equation (
36

\|\xi^{(t)}_{s,1}\|\leq b_{1}:=\frac{2B}{c},\qquad|\xi^{(t)}_{s,2}|\leq b_{2}:=\frac{2B^{2}}{c}\qquad\text{a.s. for all }s<t.

Let
S^{(t)}_{u,1}:=\sum_{s=1}^{u}\xi^{(t)}_{s,1}
and
S^{(t)}_{u,2}:=\sum_{s=1}^{u}\xi^{(t)}_{s,2}
.
Since
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}

and, in the scalar case,

\Pr\!\Big(\big|S^{(t)}_{t-1,2}\big|\geq r\Big)\ \leq\ 2\exp\!\left(-\,\frac{r^{2}}{2(t-1)b_{2}^{2}}\right).

Taking

\Pr\!\Bigg(\Big\|\frac{1}{t-1}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\xi^{(t)}_{s,1}\Big\|>\varepsilon\Bigg)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\,\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2b_{1}^{2}}\,(t-1)\right),\qquad\Pr\!\Bigg(\Big|\frac{1}{t-1}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\xi^{(t)}_{s,2}\Big|>\varepsilon\Bigg)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\,\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2b_{2}^{2}}\,(t-1)\right).

\Pr\big(\bigcap_{t=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{m=t}^{\infty}E^{(i)}_{m}(\varepsilon)\big)=0\quad(i=1,2),

so with probability

\Big\|\tfrac{1}{t-1}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\xi^{(t)}_{s,1}\Big\|\leq\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad\Big|\tfrac{1}{t-1}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\xi^{(t)}_{s,2}\Big|\leq\varepsilon\qquad\text{for all }t\geq T_{\varepsilon}.

Since this holds for every

\frac{1}{t-1}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\xi^{(t)}_{s,1}\ \xrightarrow{\ \mathrm{a.s.}\ }0\ \text{in }\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}},\qquad\frac{1}{t-1}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\xi^{(t)}_{s,2}\ \xrightarrow{\ \mathrm{a.s.}\ }0.

Step 3 (identify the conditional targets).

Conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, both

are fixed, so any integrand

(X_{s},A_{s},Y_{s})
only.
Using a one-step change-of-measure with importance sampling,

\displaystyle\E\!\big[w_{s,t}\,h(X_{s},A_{s},Y_{s})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]

\displaystyle=\E\!\Big[\,\E\!\big[w_{s,t}\,h(X_{s},A_{s},Y_{s})\mid X_{s},\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]\,\Big]

\displaystyle=\E\!\Big[\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{s}(a\mid X_{s})\,\frac{\pi_{t}(a\mid X_{s})}{\pi_{s}(a\mid X_{s})}\,\E\!\big[h(X_{s},a,Y)\mid X_{s},A\!=\!a\big]\Big]

\displaystyle=\E_{X\sim P_{X}}\!\Big[\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{t}(a\mid X)\,\E\!\big[h(X,a,Y)\mid X,A\!=\!a\big]\Big].

Choosing
h=\hat{\phi}_{s,t}(a,a^{\prime},\pi_{t})
and
h=\|\hat{\phi}_{s,t}(a,a^{\prime},\pi_{t})\|^{2}
gives

\E\!\big[w_{s,t}\,\hat{\phi}_{s,t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]=M_{1,t},\qquad\E\!\big[w_{s,t}\,\|\hat{\phi}_{s,t}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]=M_{2,t},

and these equalities do not depend on
s
(only on
t
via
\pi_{t}
and
\widehat{\mu}^{(t-1)}
). Therefore,

\frac{1}{t-1}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\E\!\big[w_{s,t}\,g_{i}(\hat{\phi}_{s,t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]=M_{i,t}\quad(i=1,2).

Combining with Step 2,

\widehat{M}_{i,t}-M_{i,t}=\frac{1}{t-1}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\xi^{(t)}_{s,i}\ \xrightarrow{\ \mathrm{a.s.}\ }0\quad(i=1,2).

Conclusion:

We just have shown that
\widehat{M}_{1,t}\to M_{1,t}
in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
and
\widehat{M}_{2,t}\to M_{2,t}
a.s. Since the map

(m_{2},m_{1})\mapsto m_{2}-\|m_{1}\|^{2}
is continuous, we notice that

\widehat{\omega}_{t}^{-2}=\widehat{M}_{2,t}-\|\widehat{M}_{1,t}\|^{2}\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{a.s.}\ }\ M_{2,t}-\|M_{1,t}\|^{2}=\omega_{t}^{-2}.

As
\omega_{t}>0
a.s. (Assumption
4.1
), continuity of
x\mapsto x^{-1/2}
on
(0,\infty)
yields
\widehat{\omega}_{t}\to\omega_{t}
a.s.
∎

Proof of Theorem

We work with a general (non-adaptive) split

We will show the following steps.

3.1

4.4

so
\tau_{r}=\tau_{r,\infty}+o_{\Pr}(1)=O_{\Pr}(1)

We now work under the augmented filtration

since
\widehat{\omega}_{t}^{(r)}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}^{\ast}

–valued MDS and

\tau_{r,\infty}\ :=\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{I}_{r}}\psi_{t,\infty}^{(r)}\ \in\ \mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}},\qquad r=1,2.

Hence, by Corollary
13.1

\tau_{r,\infty}\ \Rightarrow\ G_{r}\ \sim\ \mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}(0,\Gamma),\qquad r=1,2.

Next, if

Eventually, the bilinearity and continuity of

Step 3: Variance consistency and Slutsky.

Decompose

\widehat{\psi}_{\mathrm{cross}}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}\big\langle\psi_{i,\infty}^{(1)},\psi_{j,\infty}^{(2)}\big\rangle^{2}\;=\;C_{n}+P_{n},

where

and

P_{n}:=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}\E\!\big[\big\langle\psi_{i,\infty}^{(1)},\psi_{j,\infty}^{(2)}\big\rangle^{2}\,\big|\,\mathcal{F}^{\ast}_{\max(i,j)-1}\big].

Centered part
C_{n}
.
Fix

. As a function of

Hence, by the martingale SLLN (Theorem

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}(\cdot)=o_{\Pr}(1)
for each fixed

.

Predictable part
P_{n}
.
Set

\Sigma_{i}^{(1)}:=\E\!\big[\psi_{i,\infty}^{(1)}\!\otimes\!\psi_{i,\infty}^{(1)}\mid\mathcal{F}^{\ast}_{i-1}\big],\qquad\Sigma_{j}^{(2)}:=\E\!\big[\psi_{j,\infty}^{(2)}\!\otimes\!\psi_{j,\infty}^{(2)}\mid\mathcal{F}^{\ast}_{j-1}\big].

Split the predictable term as

\displaystyle P_{n}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\!\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}\E\!\big[\langle\psi_{i,\infty}^{(1)},\psi_{j,\infty}^{(2)}\rangle^{2}\,\big|\,\mathcal{F}^{\ast}_{\max(i,j)-1}\big]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{n^{2}}\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{I}_{1},\,j\in\mathcal{I}_{2}\\
i<j\end{subarray}}\Big\langle\psi_{i,\infty}^{(1)}\!\otimes\!\psi_{i,\infty}^{(1)},\,\Sigma_{j}^{(2)}\Big\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}}_{=:P_{1,n}}\;+\;\underbrace{\frac{1}{n^{2}}\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{I}_{1},\,j\in\mathcal{I}_{2}\\
j<i\end{subarray}}\Big\langle\Sigma_{i}^{(1)},\,\psi_{j,\infty}^{(2)}\!\otimes\!\psi_{j,\infty}^{(2)}\Big\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}}_{=:P_{2,n}}.

Replace the empirical tensors by their predictable counterparts in each piece and control the remainders.
For
P_{1,n}
,

\displaystyle P_{1,n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\!\sum_{i<j}\!\Big\langle\Sigma_{i}^{(1)},\,\Sigma_{j}^{(2)}\Big\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}\;+\;\underbrace{\frac{1}{n^{2}}\!\sum_{i<j}\!\Big\langle\psi_{i,\infty}^{(1)}\!\otimes\!\psi_{i,\infty}^{(1)}-\Sigma_{i}^{(1)},\,\Sigma_{j}^{(2)}\Big\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}}_{=:\delta_{1,n}}.

|\delta_{1,n}|\;\leq\;\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}\big\|\psi_{i,\infty}^{(1)}\!\otimes\!\psi_{i,\infty}^{(1)}-\Sigma_{i}^{(1)}\big\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\Big)\,\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}\big\|\Sigma_{j}^{(2)}\big\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\Big)\;=\;o_{\Pr}(1)\cdot O_{\Pr}(1)\;=\;o_{\Pr}(1),

since the HS–martingale LLN yields

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}(\psi_{i,\infty}^{(1)}\!\otimes\!\psi_{i,\infty}^{(1)}-\Sigma_{i}^{(1)})\to 0
in HS (foldwise),
and the

An identical argument gives

P_{2,n}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\!\sum_{j<i}\!\Big\langle\Sigma_{i}^{(1)},\,\Sigma_{j}^{(2)}\Big\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}+o_{\Pr}(1).

As a result,

P_{n}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\!\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}\Big\langle\Sigma_{i}^{(1)},\,\Sigma_{j}^{(2)}\Big\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}\;+\;o_{\Pr}(1)=\Big\langle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}\Sigma_{i}^{(1)},\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}\Sigma_{j}^{(2)}\Big\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}\;+\;o_{\Pr}(1).

By the quadratic–variation convergence (Lemma

, applied on each fold),

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}\Sigma_{i}^{(1)}\to\Gamma
and

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}\Sigma_{j}^{(2)}\to\Gamma
almost surely in HS, hence

P_{n}\ \xrightarrow{\ \Pr\ }\ \langle\Gamma,\Gamma\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}\;=\;\Tr(\Gamma^{2}).

Therefore,

\widehat{\psi}_{\mathrm{cross}}=C_{n}+P_{n}\ \xrightarrow{\ \Pr\ }\ \Tr(\Gamma^{2}).

From Steps 1–2,
\langle\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\rangle\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}\!\big(0,\Tr(\Gamma^{2})\big)

∎

Remark 13.4

(Why variance stabilization?)
.

In benign (nearly stationary) designs one can obtain a CLT for the unscaled martingale increments, but in adaptive bandits the conditional covariance
\Sigma_{t}:=\Cov(\hat{\phi}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})
typically drifts with
\pi_{t}
, so the raw predictable average
\Gamma_{T}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\leq T}\Sigma_{t}
may fail to converge (or lead to mixed–normal limits) and variance identification becomes delicate. Our stabilization chooses weights
\omega_{t-1}^{-2}=\Tr(\Sigma_{t})
and works with

Z_{t}\ :=\ \widehat{\omega}_{t-1}\big(\hat{\phi}_{t}-\E[\hat{\phi}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\big),\qquad\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t}\ :=\ \omega_{t-1}^{2}\,\Sigma_{t}=\frac{\Sigma_{t}}{\Tr(\Sigma_{t})}\quad(\Tr(\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t})=1).

Under mild Cesàro stabilization of the logging policy, bounded kernel, and strong positivity, the normalized predictable covariance satisfies

\Gamma_{T}^{\omega}\ :=\ \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\leq T}\E[Z_{t}\otimes Z_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\ \xrightarrow[\ \text{(HS)}\ ]{\ \text{a.s.}\ }\ \Gamma,

a positive trace–class,
unit–trace
limit (Lemma
11.1
). This unit–variance scale unlocks two key advantages: (i)
robust asymptotics
—Bosq’s Hilbert–space martingale CLT applies with weak, verifiable conditions (no stationarity of
\pi_{t}
), and self–normalized inequalities (Pinelis) control plug–in remainders uniformly; (ii)
clean variance identification
—in the cross statistic, the two folds share the same limit
\Gamma
, so the variance of the Gaussian limit is
\Tr(\Gamma^{2})
and is consistently estimated by

15.1
Kernel

In our experiments, we employed the Gaussian kernel (also known as the Radial Basis Function or RBF kernel), defined for all
h_{i},h_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{H}}}
as:

\displaystyle k_{\mathcal{H}}(h_{i},h_{j})=\exp\left(-\frac{\|h_{i}-h_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\right).

The parameter
\gamma
is the length-scale of the kernel, which controls the smoothness of the resulting function space. The Gaussian kernel is widely used in practice and satisfies the crucial properties of boundedness, continuity, and characteristicity
[
46
]
. For both the covariate space
\mathcal{X}
and the outcome space
\mathcal{Y}
, we utilized the Gaussian kernel, setting the length-scales based on the median of the pairwise Euclidean distances from the given data. Specifically, for a dataset
\{h_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T}
, the median pairwise distance is given by

\displaystyle\gamma_{\text{median}}=\text{median}\{\|h_{i}-h_{j}\|_{2}\mid 1\leq i<j\leq T\}.

In particular, we chose the length-scale for the covariate kernel (
k_{\mathcal{X}}
) to be equal to the median pairwise distance, and for the outcome kernel (
k_{\mathcal{Y}}
), we set the length-scale to be one half of the calculated median distance.

|\mathcal{I}_{r}|\times|\mathcal{I}_{r}|
propensity matrices per fold (with the same greedy/non-greedy/tie rule as above). These matrices, together with the realized
w_{t}
, are passed to the test procedures.

Kernels and run lengths.

Outcome similarities use an RBF kernel with bandwidth set as
\gamma=1/\sigma^{2}
(i.e.,
\gamma=2.0
when
\sigma^{2}=0.5
), unless otherwise stated. Each experiment uses a trajectory length
T=1000
and we run
200
Monte-Carlo replications per configuration. All other defaults follow the description above.

15.3.2
IHDP data

To evaluate our proposed method on a real-world benchmark, we generate a semi-synthetic dataset based on the Infant Health and Development Program (IHDP) data
[
20
]
. The original IHDP data originates from a randomized experiment on the effects of specialist home visits on cognitive test scores.

Following the preprocessing steps used in
[
31
]
, we retain
908
samples with
18
covariates (
9
continuous,
9
categorical), resulting in
X_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{18}
for all
t
. We synthesize the adaptive policies
\pi_{t}
with two arms, using an
\epsilon
-greedy with online ridge regression. This policy structure is identical to the one discussed in the preceding section, and it results in binary treatments,
A_{t}\in\{0,1\}
.

The potential outcomes are generated according to the following equations:

\displaystyle Y_{t}(0)

\displaystyle=\text{cos}(\beta^{\top}X_{t})+\epsilon_{t},\quad Y_{t}(1)=\text{cos}(\beta^{\top}X_{t})+\delta_{t}+\epsilon_{t}.

Here, the term
\delta_{t}
is used to control the treatment effect, defining four different experimental scenarios. The noise term
\epsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.5)
is an i.i.d. Gaussian random variable with zero mean and variance
0.5
, i.e.,
\epsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.5)
.

Scenarios.

We utilize the same four scenarios, that we adapted for the synthetic data experiments from
Martinez Taboada et al. 2023
, by defining the treatment effect term
\delta_{t}
: (i) Scenario I (Null): The treatment has no effect, defined by
\delta_{t}=0
; (ii) Scenario II (Mean Shift): The treatment introduces a constant positive shift, defined by
\delta_{t}=2
; (iii) Scenario III (Symmetric Mixture): The treatment effect is a symmetric mixture, defined by
\delta_{t}=2\,S_{t}
with
S_{t}\in\{-1,+1\}
Rademacher
(0.5)
; (iv) Scenario IV (Random Scale): The treatment effect is randomly scaled, defined by
\delta_{t}\sim\mathrm{Uniform}[-4,4]
.

Evaluation protocol.

We evaluated our method’s performance across varying sample sizes. This was done by running experiments on the IHDP dataset using subsampling without replacement, where the subset size was varied uniformly within the set

\{100,150,200,\ldots,850,900,908\}
, with
908
representing the full available dataset. We utilized the non-adaptive alternating fold splitting protocol, consistent with our synthetic dataset experiments, and ran each distinct experiment over
200
Monte-Carlo replications.

For the Gaussian kernels used, we followed a median heuristic: the length-scale for the covariate kernel was set equal to the median pairwise distance, while the length-scale for the outcome kernel was set to one half of that median distance. The regularization parameter
\lambda
was set to
10^{-2}
.

The true positive rates for Scenarios II-IV, utilizing the full available dataset, are presented in Table

. A separate discussion detailing additional results that incorporate varying data sizes is provided in Section
15.4
.

dSprite dataset

We adapt the structured image benchmark of
Xu and Gretton 2023
and adapt it to the two-scenario setting of our adaptive kernel test. Each outcome
Y\in[0,1]^{64\times 64}
is a grayscale image of a heart shape on a black background, rendered from latent coordinates
(\mathrm{posX},\mathrm{posY})\in[0,1]^{2}
. Contexts
X_{t}=(x_{t}^{(1)},x_{t}^{(2)})
are sampled uniformly from
\mathrm{Unif}([0,1]^{2})
, and images are generated through a deterministic renderer

Y_{t}(a)=g(X_{t},a)\in[0,1]^{64\times 64},

where
a\in\{0,1\}
indexes the treatment and
g
draws a white heart centered at position
(x_{t}^{(1)}+\Delta_{a}^{(1)},\,x_{t}^{(2)}+\Delta_{a}^{(2)})
with fixed scale and rotation. The offsets
(\Delta_{a}^{(1)},\Delta_{a}^{(2)})
define the two experimental regimes:

\displaystyle\text{Scenario I (null):}

\displaystyle(\Delta_{0}^{(1)},\Delta_{0}^{(2)})=(0,0),\quad(\Delta_{1}^{(1)},\Delta_{1}^{(2)})=(0,0);

\displaystyle\text{Scenario IV (shift):}

\displaystyle(\Delta_{0}^{(1)},\Delta_{0}^{(2)})=(0,0),\quad(\Delta_{1}^{(1)},\Delta_{1}^{(2)})=(\delta,0),

where
\delta=0.15
induces a rightward translation of the heart under
A=1
while preserving mean pixel intensity. Gaussian pixel noise
\mathcal{N}(0,0.01)
is added to each image. Hence, the marginal intensity distributions of
Y(0)
and
Y(1)
coincide, but their spatial structure differs. Figure

shows observational samples generated under Scenario IV, where the adaptive policy produces trajectories with spatially translated outcomes.
Figure
5
depicts corresponding counterfactual image pairs
(Y(0),Y(1))
, confirming that the treatment
A=1
only shifts the heart horizontally without altering overall brightness or shape.

Adaptive data collection.

Logged trajectories
\{(X_{t},A_{t},Y_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}
are generated by an
\varepsilon
-greedy contextual policy with two arms and per-arm online ridge regression, identical to the adaptive linear setting in §
15.3.1
. Each arm
a\in\{0,1\}
maintains the sufficient statistics

S_{a}=\mathrm{diag}(0,\lambda,\ldots,\lambda),\qquad b_{a}=0,

with
\lambda=10^{-2}
and features
x_{t}^{\mathrm{aug}}=(1,X_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
At each round
t
, the arm parameters
\theta_{a}=S_{a}^{-1}b_{a}
yield predictions
q_{a}(t)=\langle\theta_{a},x_{t}^{\mathrm{aug}}\rangle
. The exploration rate follows

\varepsilon_{t}=\max(\varepsilon_{\min},\,\varepsilon_{0}/(t+1)^{p}),\qquad\varepsilon_{0}=0.2,\ \varepsilon_{\min}=0.05,\ p=0.99.

Actions are sampled according to

\pi_{t}(1|X_{t})=\begin{cases}1-\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{t},&q_{1}(t)>q_{0}(t),\\[3.0pt]
\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{t},&q_{1}(t)<q_{0}(t),\\[3.0pt]
0.5,&q_{1}(t)=q_{0}(t),\end{cases}\qquad\pi_{t}(0|X_{t})=1-\pi_{t}(1|X_{t}).

After observing
(X_{t},A_{t},Y_{t})
, only the chosen arm is updated:

S_{A_{t}}\leftarrow S_{A_{t}}+x_{t}^{\mathrm{aug}}(x_{t}^{\mathrm{aug}})^{\top},\qquad b_{A_{t}}\leftarrow b_{A_{t}}+x_{t}^{\mathrm{aug}}Y_{t}.

The sequence
\{\pi_{t}(1|X_{t})\}
is stored to compute the stabilized kernel test statistics.

Foldwise evaluation.

To enable cross-fold variance stabilization, we use an
alternating
split
(\mathcal{I}_{0},\mathcal{I}_{1})
and record fold-specific propensity matrices
\Pi_{r\leftarrow r}
computed from the parameter snapshots
\{\theta_{a}^{(t)}\}_{t\in\mathcal{I}_{r}}
.
Each matrix encodes, for every evaluation time
t
in a fold, the propensities
\pi_{t}(A_{s}|X_{s})
for all contexts
s
within the same fold.

Evaluation protocol.

Each experiment runs for
T=1000
adaptive rounds and is repeated over
200
Monte-Carlo replications. For each test, empirical Type-I error is the proportion of rejections at level
0.05
under Scenario I, and empirical power is the proportion of rejections under Scenario IV.
All tests use a Gaussian RBF kernel on outcomes with bandwidth chosen by the median heuristic and
\lambda=10^{-2}
regularization.
VS-DR-KTE
operates directly on flattened images
Y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{4096}
, while baseline methods (
CADR
,
AW-AIPW
) are restricted to the mean pixel intensity as scalar outcome.
