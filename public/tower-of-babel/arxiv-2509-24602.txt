Kinetic energy instability of pipe flow in finite domains Journal: Nuclear Physics B
Chiara Giraudo, Ingeborg G. Gjerde, Miroslav Kuchta, L. Ridgway Scott

Abstract
The instability of pipe flow has been a subject of extensive research, yet a significant gap remains between experimental observations and theoretical predictions. This study revisits the classical problem of kinetic energy instability of pipe flow using contemporary computational methods. We focus on finite domains to address the limitations of previous infinite pipe analyses. We analyze two different cases by imposing homogeneous Dirichlet and periodic boundary conditions. Our investigation reveals that the critical Reynolds number for instability approaches values established by Joseph and Carmi ( 1969 ) (1969) as the length of the pipe increases. Specifically, when homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions are applied, the critical Reynolds number converges monotonically to a value of Re c ≤ 81.62 {\mathrm{Re}}_{c}\leq 81.62 . In contrast, for the periodic case, the critical Reynolds number varies periodically with the length of the pipe, exhibiting local minima at Re c = 81.58 {\mathrm{Re}}_{c}=81.58 . We further characterize the shape of the perturbations by computing their magnitude and vorticity for several local minima and maxima associated with the periodic problem. Focusing on the perturbations obtained by imposing the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, we compute their temporal evolution. As expected, the L 2 L^{2} -norm of the perturbation decreases from the initial time when solving for the critical Reynolds number, while it initially increases and then eventually decreases for Reynolds numbers exceeding the critical value.



1
Introduction

In this study we investigate the critical Reynolds number at which pipe flow becomes unstable. Despite extensive research over many years, a significant gap still exists between experimental and theoretical estimates of this critical value. Linear stability analysis erroneously suggests that pipe flow remains stable for all Reynolds numbers, which is believed to hold true for both axisymmetric and non-axisymmetric disturbances
[
1
]

[
2
, Section 31.2]
. Experimentally, the onset of instability in pipe flow is identified around Reynolds number
{\mathrm{Re}}\approx 2000

[
3
]
.

The seminal work of Orr
[
4
]
, which focused on kinetic energy instability, showed that pipe flow is unstable, but proposed a much lower stability limit at
{\mathrm{Re}}=180
for infinitely long pipes. Later, Joseph and Carmi
[
5
]
analytically established the existence of an even lower mode at
{\mathrm{Re}}=82.88
for perturbations without streamwise variation in infinitely long pipes. In the same paper, they numerically identified a lower stability limit of
{\mathrm{Re}}=81.49
, associated with a spiral mode. They argued that Orr’s mistake was assuming that the most unstable mode is always of a streamwise nature. In their study, the critical Reynolds numbers were obtained by maximizing a functional ratio derived from the Reynolds-Orr energy identity.

Focusing on plane Poiseuille flow, Orszag
[
6
]
showed that it becomes linearly unstable for
{\mathrm{Re}}>5772
. Recent work by
[
7
]
supports Orr’s assumptions while raising questions about some of Joseph and Carmi’s findings
[
5
]
with respect to plane Poiseuille flow. In
[
7
]
the authors examined two types of perturbations in the planar domain
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times[-1,1]
: longitudinal perturbations, independent of
x
(the streamwise direction), and transverse perturbations, independent of
y
and with a zero second component in the
y
direction. Using their notation, Orr assumed that only transverse perturbations were admissible, while Joseph and Carmi also considered longitudinal perturbations when determining the critical Reynolds number. In
[
7
]
, it was proven that longitudinal perturbations are globally kinematically energy stable for any Reynolds number. This global stability implies that while these perturbations may initially grow, they will eventually decay. Furthermore, the authors conjectured that the maximum should be found on “a subspace of the space of kinematically
admissible perturbations”, and under this assumption, they established that longitudinal perturbations are kinematically energy stable for all Reynolds numbers. This conjecture was validated for plane Couette and Poiseuille flows in
[
8
]
, suggesting that “the critical nonlinear Reynolds numbers are obtained along two-dimensional perturbations, the transverse perturbations”. Although these results hold true for the plane Poiseuille flow, it remains unclear whether and how they can be extended to pipe flow. In
[
9
]
, the Reynolds-Orr instability in planar Couette flow was studied, and while exponential growth was observed for Reynolds numbers up to 100,000, the perturbed flow field ultimately decayed.

In the context of transient energy growth at subcritical Reynolds number, previous studies
[
10
,
11
]
for plane Poiseuille flow, along with
[
12
]
for pipe flow, show that longitudinal perturbations characterize the initial conditions that lead to the greatest increase in energy over a specified period of time. This temporary increase in energy is attributed to the non-normal nature of the linear stability operators, where their eigenfunctions are not orthogonal. Furthermore, experimental and simulation studies for Poiseuille flow in cylindrical pipes
[
3
,
13
]
support the radial and azimuthal nature of pipe instability. These studies suggest that instability initially appears in localized structures known as puffs and slugs. Puffs occur for
2000<{\mathrm{Re}}<2700
and are turbulent regions with a defined upstream boundary and a diffuse downstream boundary, maintaining a roughly constant size. In contrast, slugs are observed at
{\mathrm{Re}}>3200
and tend to grow spatially over time. Kerswell
[
14
]
further characterized initial disturbances that can extract energy from Hagen-Poiseuille flow as helical and predominantly streamwise dependent. Recent efforts have aimed to identify alternative solutions to the Navier-Stokes equations to explain the abrupt transition from laminar to turbulent flow in pipes. These solutions, which occur at
{\mathrm{Re}}>773

[
15
]
, are thought to facilitate the transition to turbulence. They are characterized by “wavy streaks with staggered quasistreamwise vortices”
[
15
]
offering a new perspective on pipe flow instability.

Joseph and Carmi
[
5
]
employed a Fourier-like transform of the solutions for three-dimensional
(3D)
Poiseuille flow, based on spiral modes characterized by wave number
\alpha
and spiral number
N
. They determined the critical Reynolds number by maximizing the functional ratio
[
5
, p.575]
, derived from the Reynolds-Orr equation
[
16
]
, with respect to
\alpha
and
N
. Their work suggested that the critical mode could be either periodic or of infinite extend. For this reason, we consider both periodic and non-periodic boundary conditions (BCs) in our study. However, as they remark, the modes obtained from the maximization may not necessarily satisfy the associated Euler-Lagrange equations
[
5
, p.576]
. Our method is based on solving the Euler-Lagrange equations numerically, while weakly enforcing the zero divergence condition.

With the objective of identifying the most unstable modes within pipe flows across finite domains, this study revisits the Euler-Lagrange equations and develops a finite element method to address the associated eigenvalue problem. Differing from the semi-analytical approach of Joseph and Carmi, which was tailored to pipes, our approach can be applied to arbitrary domains. Notably, our findings show that the critical Reynolds numbers converge toward those previously established by Joseph and Carmi as the length of the pipes increases. Specifically, we identify
{\mathrm{Re}}_{c}=81.58
as the critical limit for periodic boundary conditions and
{\mathrm{Re}}_{c}\leq 81.62
for homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions.

The paper is organized as follows: In
Section

2
, we state the base flow equations
and derive the eigenvalue problem associated with the kinetic energy instability of pipe flow. In
Section

3
, we detail the discretization and numerical methods used to solve the eigenvalue problem and the dynamical problem of the perturbed solution. We discuss our findings in
Section

4
. Finally, we summarize our results in
Section

5
.

2
Kinetic Energy Instability

In this section we report the Navier-Stokes equations that describe the base flow
and derive the eigenvalue problem associated with the kinetic energy stability approach.
Finally we link the eigenvalues and the critical Reynolds number.

2.1
Base flow equations

Let
\Omega
denote a pipe with radius
R
and length
L
, defined as

\Omega=\{{\mathbf{x}}=(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:x^{2}+y^{2}<R,\,0<z<L\},

with the boundaries denoted as
\Gamma_{\text{wall}}=\{{\mathbf{x}}\in\partial\Omega:x^{2}+y^{2}=R,0\leq z\leq L\}
,

\Gamma_{\text{in}}=\left\{{\mathbf{x}}\in\partial\Omega\,:\,z=0\right\}
and

\Gamma_{\text{out}}=\left\{{\mathbf{x}}\in\partial\Omega\,:\,z=L\right\}
.
Suppose that the velocity-pressure pair
({\mathbf{u}},p)
,
{\mathbf{u}}:\Omega_{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
p:\Omega_{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
\Omega_{T}=[0,T]\times\Omega
,
solves the dimensionless Navier-Stokes equations in this domain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{{\mathbf{u}}}}{\partial t}-\frac{1}{{\mathrm{Re}}}\Delta{\mathbf{u}}+\,{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla{\mathbf{u}}+\nabla p

\displaystyle={\mathbf{0}}\text{ in }\Omega_{T},

(1)

\displaystyle{{\nabla\cdot}\,}{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle=0\text{ in }\Omega_{T},

together with the initial condition

{\mathbf{u}}(0,{\mathbf{x}})={\mathbf{u}}_{0}({\mathbf{x}})\quad\forall{\mathbf{x}}\in\Omega.

(2)

We will consider two sets of boundary conditions. In the Dirichlet
case we let

\displaystyle{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{0}}\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{\text{wall},T}=[0,T]\times\Gamma_{\text{wall}},

(3)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{g}}\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{\text{in},T},

\displaystyle{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{g}}\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{\text{out},T},

while in case of periodic boundary conditions we prescribe

\displaystyle{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{0}}\text{ on }\Gamma_{\text{wall},T},

(4)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{u}}(t,{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{in}})

\displaystyle={\mathbf{u}}(t,{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{in}}+(0,0,L))\quad\forall(t,{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{in}})\in\Gamma_{\text{in},T}.

We note that with the latter boundary conditions we aim to model flow in infinitely long pipes
under the constraint that the solution will be period.

The dimensionless equations are obtained by scaling the spatial coordinates with respect to the radius
R
,
and the velocity with respect to the maximum velocity
U_{0}
of the flow. The coefficient
{\mathrm{Re}}
is the Reynolds number,
defined for this flow as

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Re}}=\frac{U_{0}R}{\nu},

(5)

where
\nu
is the kinematic viscosity. At low enough Reynolds numbers, pipe flow admits the well-known
steady state solution

\mathbf{u}_{\text{HP}}=(0,0,u_{\text{HP}})=\left(0,0,U_{0}\left(1-\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{R^{2}}\right)\right),

(6)

referred to as Hagen-Poiseuille (HP) flow (e.g.
[
17
]
). Notably, since the HP flow does not depend
on the
z
-component, it also satisfies the periodic boundary conditions (
4
). We next
define the notion of stability of the HP flow.

2.2
Kinetic energy instability

To define the concept of kinetic energy instability and derive the corresponding eigenvalue problem, we follow the
methodology outlined in
[
9
, Sections 2-5]
. Let
(\mathbf{u},p)
be the laminar solution of the Navier-Stokes equations
(
1
), (
2
) and (
3
), with the initial condition given by
the Hagen-Poiseuille flow (
6
). We remark that the following derivation equally applies
also to the period boundary conditions (
4
).

Let
({\mathbf{w}},q)
be a solution of the Navier-Stokes equations with a different
initial condition than
{\mathbf{u}}_{0}
(used for
(\mathbf{u},p)
). That is
({\mathbf{w}},q)

satisfies

{\mathbf{w}}_{0}={\mathbf{w}}(0,{\mathbf{x}})\not={\mathbf{u}}_{0}({\mathbf{x}})\text{ on }\Omega,

(7)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\mathbf{w}}}{\partial t}-\frac{1}{{\mathrm{Re}}}\Delta{\mathbf{w}}+\,{\mathbf{w}}\cdot\nabla{\mathbf{w}}+\nabla q

\displaystyle={\mathbf{0}}\text{ in }\Omega_{T},

(8)

\displaystyle{{\nabla\cdot}\,}{\mathbf{w}}

\displaystyle=0\text{ in }\Omega_{T},

\displaystyle{\mathbf{w}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{0}}\text{ on }\Gamma_{\text{wall},T},

\displaystyle{\mathbf{w}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{g}}\text{ on }\Gamma_{\text{in},T}\cup\Gamma_{\text{out},T}.

For their difference
{\mathbf{v}}={\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{w}}
in
\Omega_{T}
with
{\mathbf{v}}_{0}({\mathbf{x}})={\mathbf{v}}(0,{\mathbf{x}})\not=0
we now consider its
kinetic energy

{\mathcal{E}}(t)=\int_{\Omega}|{\mathbf{u}}(t,{\mathbf{x}})-{\mathbf{w}}(t,{\mathbf{x}})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}.

(9)

Following
[
18
, Definition 2.1]
, we say that the flow
{\mathbf{u}}
is
kinetic energy unstable
at
t=0

due to the perturbation
{\mathbf{v}}_{0}
for Reynolds number
{\mathrm{Re}}
if

\frac{d\phantom{t}}{dt}{\mathcal{E}}(0)>0.

(10)

If
{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}(0)\leq 0
for all
{\mathbf{v}}_{0}\in V
, then we say that the flow
{\mathbf{u}}
is
kinetic energy stable
at
t=0
. Throughout this paper, when we refer to stable or unstable, we mean
kinetic energy stable
or
kinetic energy unstable
, respectively.

2.3
The kinetic energy eigenvalue problem

To derive the eigenvalue problem related to the kinetic energy stability let us first note
that
{\mathbf{v}}
satisfies the equations

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\mathbf{v}}}{\partial t}-\frac{1}{{\mathrm{Re}}}\Delta{\mathbf{v}}+\big({\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{w}}\cdot\nabla{\mathbf{w}}\big)+\nabla o

\displaystyle={\mathbf{0}}\text{ in }\Omega_{T},

(11)

\displaystyle{{\nabla\cdot}\,}{\mathbf{v}}

\displaystyle=0\text{ in }\Omega_{T},

\displaystyle{\mathbf{v}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{0}}\;\text{ on }\Gamma_{\text{wall},T}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{v}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{0}}\text{ on }\Gamma_{\text{in},T}\cup\Gamma_{\text{out},T}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{v}}(0,{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle={\mathbf{v}}_{0}({\mathbf{x}})\text{ in }\Omega,

where
o=p-q
.
Before formulating the variational problem for equations (
11
), we rewrite
the nonlinear term in the Navier-Stokes equation as follows, using the definition
{\mathbf{v}}={\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{w}}
,

{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{w}}\cdot\nabla{\mathbf{w}}={\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla{\mathbf{v}}+{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\nabla{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\nabla{\mathbf{v}},

we introduce the symmetric gradient of
{\mathbf{v}}
,

{\cal D}({\mathbf{v}})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla{\mathbf{v}}+\nabla{\mathbf{v}}^{T}\right)
,
the trilinear form

e({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{w}})=\int_{\Omega}({\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla{\mathbf{v}})\cdot{\mathbf{w}}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}

(12)

and the divergence-free subspace

V_{\text{div}}=\left\{\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in(H^{1}(\Omega))^{3}\,:\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}=0\text{ in }\Omega,\,\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\mathbf{0}\text{ on }\partial\Omega\right\}.

By choosing
{\mathbf{v}}\in V_{\text{div}}
as a test function we obtain from (
11
) that

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}|{\mathbf{v}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}+\frac{2}{Re}\int_{\Omega}|{\cal D}({\mathbf{v}})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}+\big(e({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{v}})+e({\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})-e({\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{v}})\big)=0.

(13)

Exploiting the well-known result from
[
19
, Section 20.1.2]

e({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{w}})=-e({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{v}})+\oint_{\partial\Omega}{\mathbf{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}\,({\mathbf{v}}\cdot{\mathbf{w}})\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}

(14)

and noting that
{\mathbf{v}}\in V_{\text{div}}
, we find that
e({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{v}})=0
,
and
e({\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{v}})=0
since
{\mathbf{u}}={\mathbf{0}}
on
\Gamma_{\text{wall}}
and
{\mathbf{v}}={\mathbf{0}}
on
{\partial\Omega}\backslash\Gamma_{\text{wall}}
.
Moreover,

e({\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})=\int_{\Omega}({\mathbf{v}}\cdot\nabla{\mathbf{u}})\cdot{\mathbf{v}}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}=\int_{\Omega}{\mathbf{v}}\cdot(\nabla{\mathbf{u}}){\mathbf{v}}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}=\int_{\Omega}{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\mathcal{D}({\mathbf{u}}){\mathbf{v}}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}.

Thus, (
13
) can be rewritten as

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}|{\mathbf{v}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}=-\frac{2}{Re}\int_{\Omega}|{\cal D}({\mathbf{v}})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}-\int_{\Omega}{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\mathcal{D}({\mathbf{u}}){\mathbf{v}}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}},

(15)

which corresponds to the Reynolds-Orr equation, as noted in

[
16
]
and
[
20
, Section 5.6.1]
.

We note that the term
\int_{\Omega}|{\cal D}({\mathbf{v}})|^{2}\,d{\mathbf{x}}
in (
15
) is always positive.
However, the sign of the second term is not known a priori. In particular, for a given base
flow there may exist an initial perturbation
{\mathbf{v}}_{0}
for which the right-hand side in
(
15
) is positive. Specifically, from the definition of kinetic energy instability characterized by equation (
10
), this means that the flow
{\mathbf{u}}
is unstable at
t=0
due to the perturbation
{\mathbf{v}}
.

As shown in
[
9
, Section 5.2]
the question of existence of such a perturbation to
the HP flow can be related to an eigenvalue problem

b_{{\mathbf{u}}}({\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{z}})=\lambda a({\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{z}})\quad\forall{\mathbf{z}}\in V_{\text{div}}.

(16)

Here the bilinear forms
a,b_{\mathbf{u}}
are defined for any
{\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{z}}\in V_{\text{div}}
as

\displaystyle a({\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{z}})=2\int_{\Omega}{\cal D}({\mathbf{v}}):{\cal D}({\mathbf{z}})\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}},\quad b_{{\mathbf{u}}}({\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{z}})=\int_{\Omega}{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\mathcal{D}({\mathbf{u}}_{0}){\mathbf{z}}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}.

(17)

We recall that
\mathbf{u}_{0}=\mathbf{u_{\text{HP}}}/U_{0}
so that

\mathcal{D}({\mathbf{u}}_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\mathbf{u}_{0}+\nabla\mathbf{u}_{0}^{T}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&-x\\
0&0&-y\\
-x&-y&0\end{pmatrix}.

(18)

In particular, if there exists a negative
\lambda_{c}
such that

\lambda_{c}=\min_{{\mathbf{0}}\neq{\mathbf{v}}\in V_{\text{div}}}\frac{b_{{\mathbf{u}}}({\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{v}})}{a({\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{v}})},

(19)

we can equivalently state that the flow
{\mathbf{u}}
is kinematic energy unstable at
t=0
if

{\mathrm{Re}}>-\lambda_{c}^{-1}.

(20)

In turn, the critical Reynolds number
{\mathrm{Re}}_{c}
is defined as the Reynolds number at which the flow becomes unstable,
specifically the smallest Reynolds number for which there exists a perturbation
{\mathbf{v}}_{0}\in V_{\text{div}}
that
satisfies (
20
) with equality instead of inequality. Thus, with
\lambda
being
the eigenvalues of (
16
), the critical Reynolds number can be expressed as

{\mathrm{Re}}_{c}=\frac{1}{\max_{\lambda<0}|\lambda|}.

(21)

The corresponding eigenvector of (
16
) is then the critical perturbation.

3
Numerical Methods

In this section, we state an alternative form of the eigenvalue problem
(
16
) which allows for practical computations with the
finite element method. We then describe discretization of dynamical problem
employed to study the evolution of the perturbed flow.

3.1
Computational solution of the eigenproblem

Solving the eigenproblem (
16
) for
{\mathbf{v}}_{\lambda}\in V_{\text{div}}
can be
computationally challenging as the basis of
V_{\text{div}}
is not readily available for
general domain
\Omega
. In the following we shall therefore seek for
{\mathbf{v}}_{\lambda}
in a larger
space
V
and enforce the divergence-free constraint in a Lagrange multiplier space
Q
. Specifically
we consider the spaces

V=(H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))^{3},\,Q_{0}=L_{0}^{2}(\Omega)=\left\{p\in L^{2}(\Omega)\,:\,\int_{\Omega}p\,d{\mathbf{x}}=0\right\}.

(22)

and define the Lagrangian functional

\mathcal{L}({\mathbf{z}},q)=\frac{1}{2}\left(a({\mathbf{z}},{\mathbf{z}})-\frac{1}{\lambda}b_{{\mathbf{u}}}({\mathbf{z}},{\mathbf{z}})\right)+c({\mathbf{z}},q)\,,\quad{\mathbf{z}}\in V\,,\,q\in Q_{0}.

(23)

where

c({\mathbf{z}},q)=\int_{\Omega}q{\nabla\cdot\,}{\mathbf{z}}\,d\mathbf{x}\qquad\forall{\mathbf{z}}\in V,\,\forall q\in Q_{0}.

(24)

Characterizing the saddle point of
\mathcal{L}
, the problem of solving
the eigenvalue problem (
16
) is equivalent to
finding
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
({\mathbf{v}}_{\lambda},p)\in V\times Q_{0}
such that

\displaystyle a({\mathbf{v}}_{\lambda},{\mathbf{z}})-\frac{1}{\lambda}\,b_{{\mathbf{u}}}({\mathbf{v}}_{\lambda},{\mathbf{z}})+c({\mathbf{z}},p)=0\qquad

\displaystyle\forall{\mathbf{z}}\in V,

(25)

\displaystyle c({\mathbf{v}}_{\lambda},q)=0\qquad

\displaystyle\forall q\in Q_{0}.

In particular, in the continuous case, due to the choice of spaces, it holds that

{\nabla\cdot\,}V\subset Q_{0}
, meaning
{\nabla\cdot\,}{\mathbf{v}}_{\lambda}\in Q_{0}
. From the second equation in
(
25
), this implies that
{\nabla\cdot\,}{\mathbf{v}}_{\lambda}
is orthogonal
to all functions in
Q_{0}
, leading to the conclusion that
{\nabla\cdot\,}{\mathbf{v}}_{\lambda}=0
almost
everywhere
[
21
]
.
We note that in the space
Q_{0}
, the constraint of zero average over the domain
\Omega

is necessary to ensure uniqueness in the pressure, since we did not prescribe
any boundary conditions for the pressure. For further details about the well-posedness
of (
25
) we refer to
[
22
]
.

The eigenvalue problem (
25
) avoids the issue
with
V_{\text{div}}
space, however, the space
Q_{0}
remains impractical for implementation.
Introducing again a larger space
Q=L^{2}
and a scalar Lagrange multiplier associated with
a zero mean constraint leads to the final form of the eigenvalue problem:

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
({\mathbf{v}}_{\lambda},p,l)\in V\times Q\times R
such that

\displaystyle a({\mathbf{v}}_{\lambda},{\mathbf{z}})+c({\mathbf{z}},p)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\,b_{{\mathbf{u}}}({\mathbf{v}}_{\lambda},{\mathbf{z}})\qquad

\displaystyle\forall{\mathbf{z}}\in V,

(26)

\displaystyle c({\mathbf{v}}_{\lambda},q)+d(q,l)=0\qquad

\displaystyle\forall q\in Q,

\displaystyle d(p,m)=0\qquad

\displaystyle\forall m\in\mathbb{R},

with
d(\cdot,\cdot)
denoting the
L^{2}
-inner product. Introducing operators

\displaystyle A:\,V\rightarrow V^{\prime},\quad\big\langle A{\mathbf{v}}_{\lambda},{\mathbf{z}}\big\rangle=2\int_{\Omega}{\cal D}({\mathbf{v}}_{\lambda}):{\cal D}({\mathbf{z}})\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}},

(27)

\displaystyle B_{{\mathbf{u}}}:\,V\rightarrow V^{\prime},\quad\big\langle B_{{\mathbf{u}}}{\mathbf{v}}_{\lambda},{\mathbf{z}}\big\rangle=\int_{\Omega}(\mathcal{D}({\mathbf{u}}){\mathbf{v}}_{\lambda})\cdot{\mathbf{z}}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}},

\displaystyle C:\,V\rightarrow Q^{\prime},\quad\big\langle C{\mathbf{v}}_{\lambda},p\big\rangle=\int_{\Omega}p{\nabla\cdot\,}{\mathbf{v}}_{\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}},

\displaystyle D:\,Q\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad\big\langle Dp,l\big\rangle=l\int_{\Omega}p\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}.

we rewrite the eigenvalue problem (
25
) in the operator form
as: find
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbf{x}=({\mathbf{v}}_{\lambda},p,l)\in V\times Q\times\mathbb{R}
such that

\mathcal{A}\mathbf{x}=\lambda^{-1}\mathcal{B}\mathbf{x},

(28)

where

\mathcal{A}=\begin{bmatrix}A&\quad C^{T}&0\\
C&0&D^{T}\\
0&D&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathcal{B}=\begin{bmatrix}B_{{\mathbf{u}}}&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}.

(29)

3.1.1
Discrete eigenvalue problem

To discretize (
28
) we consider triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{h}
of
domain
\Omega
with characteristic mesh size
h>0
and utilize the lowest order Taylor-Hood
elements, i.e.

\displaystyle V_{h}

\displaystyle=\left\{{\mathbf{v}}_{h}\in(C^{0}(\Omega))^{3}:\,{\mathbf{v}}_{h}|_{K}\in({P}_{2}(K))^{3}\,\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\right\}

(30)

\displaystyle Q_{h}

\displaystyle=\left\{q_{h}\in C^{0}(\Omega):\,q_{h}|_{K}\in P_{1}(K)\,\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\right\}.

We note that the Taylor-Hood spaces are conforming,
V_{h}\subset V
and
Q_{h}\subset Q
, and
crucially satisfy the inf-sup condition. However,
{\nabla\cdot\,}V_{h}\not\subseteq Q_{h}

and so the divergence-free constraint is not satisfied pointwise.

Denoting
\mathcal{A}_{h}
,
\mathcal{B}_{h}
the matrices due to discretization of
(
29
) on space (
30
) the discrete version of the eigenproblem
reads: find
\lambda_{h}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbf{x}_{h}\in V_{h}\times Q_{h}\times\mathbb{R}
such that

\mathcal{A}_{h}\mathbf{x}_{h}=\lambda_{h}^{-1}\mathcal{B}_{h}\mathbf{x}_{h}.

(31)

Here, owing to the inf-sup condition the matrix
\mathcal{A}_{h}
is invertible.
We remark that both matrices are symmetric.

In the following we apply Gmsh
[
23
]
to generate tetrahedral
meshes of the computational domain and use FEniCS
[
24
]
for
the finite element discretization. The eigenvalue problem (
31
)
is solved with SLEPc
[
25
]
. Specifically, since the matrices

\mathcal{A}_{h}
and
\mathcal{B}_{h}
in (
31
) are symmetric but indefinite,
with the left matrix being invertible, the eigenvalue problem is formulated as a (right)
generalized non-Hermitian eigenvalue problem (GNHEP) in SLEPc. To solve it we apply the
shift-and-invert
(SI) spectral transformation, as detailed in the PETSc manual

[
26
]
. Given that the matrix
\mathcal{A}_{h}
is non-singular, the scalar
parameter in the SI algorithm is fixed to zero, and we solve the equivalent eigenproblem

\mathcal{A}_{h}^{-1}\mathcal{B}_{h}\mathbf{x}_{h}=\lambda_{h}\mathbf{x}_{h}.

(32)

Here the matrix
\mathcal{A}_{h}
is first iteratively inverted using GMRES method, with LU preconditioner.
The discrete eigenproblem (
32
) is then solved using the Krylov-Schur method

[
27
]
, which computes the largest eigenvalues in magnitude. Because we employ the spectral method SI,
we require the solver to compute the true residual for the convergence test. During each iteration
\ \kappa
of the numerical
method, the relative residual error of the eigenproblem (
31
),

r_{\kappa}=\frac{|\mathcal{A}_{h}{\mathbf{v}}_{\kappa}-\lambda_{h,\kappa}^{-1}\mathcal{B}_{h}{\mathbf{v}}_{\kappa}|_{2}}{|\lambda_{h,\kappa}^{-1}{\mathbf{v}}_{\kappa}|_{2}},

(33)

is computed. When this residual is smaller than a predetermined tolerance, the approximating eigenvalue is considered converged. In our simulations, we set this tolerance to be the default value
10^{-8}
.
Since we are mainly interested in the critical Reynolds number, as defined in (
21
),
we compute only the first few with the largest magnitude. In our experiments, computing 4 extremal eigenvalues was
sufficient to ensure that at least one of them is negative.

3.2.1
Discretization

Numerical solution of the system of equations defined by (
8
),
(
38
) requires both temporal and spatial discretizations.
To discretize the system in time we consider a uniform partition of
the interval
[0,T]
and denote by
\Delta t
the time step size.
To achieve second-order convergence in time, we apply the Crank-Nicholson (CN) method
which solves the system of equations at time
t_{n+1/2}
. The time derivative of the velocity at
t_{n+1/2}
is approximated using
a second-order central scheme, while the velocity at this time is expressed as the average of the known velocity at
time
t_{n}
and the unknown velocity at time
t_{n+1}
. Following
[
24
, Chap. 22]
the convective term

({\mathbf{w}}_{n+1/2}\cdot\nabla){\mathbf{w}}_{n+1/2}
can be
approximated either implicitly or semi-implicit. In the implicit case, both velocities in the term are evaluated based on the
approximation of
{\mathbf{w}}_{n+1/2}={\mathbf{w}}(t_{n+1/2},{\mathbf{x}})
, as discussed previously. This leads to the classical Crank-Nicholson
formulation. In the semi-implicit case, the convective term is linearized using Adams-Bashforth projection, where the
convecting velocity is explicitly expressed as a linear combination of the known velocities
{\mathbf{w}}_{n}
and
{\mathbf{w}}_{n-1}
.
This represents the Crank-Nicholson Adams-Bashforth formulation. Both formulations yield a second-order discretization
in time and solve for the velocity at time
t_{n+1}
and pressure at time
t_{n+1/2}
. The pressure at the subsequent time step

n+1
can be derived through post-processing as
q_{n+1}=2q_{n+1/2}-q_{n}
. While the initial velocity is known, the
initial pressure is unknown and it set to zero. To improve the pressure approximation, we perform one step of the
Backward Euler method when using the implicit discretization, and two steps of the Backward Euler method when using the semi-implicit discretization.

Following temporal discretization of (
8
) and
(
38
) we consider a monolithic scheme where the velocity and pressure
are solved for simultaneously. We use Taylor-Hood elements (
30
) for spatial discretization
such that the scheme is second-order accurate in space. Then, at each discrete time step, the nonlinear
problem is solved computationally through Newton’s method with line research, utilizing the SNES library
of PETSc
[
30
]
. The linear solver employed within Newton’s method is set to MUMPS
[
31
]
,
allowing the code to be run also in parallel.

4.3
Dynamics of the perturbation over time

To investigate the temporal evolution of perturbations in pipe flow, we numerically
compute the perturbed flow by solving the discrete form of the governing temporal-spatial
equations, as defined in equation (
8
), with initial conditions
defined in (
38
) where the scaling factor for the perturbation
is fixed to
1/4
. In order to achieve a good balance between accuracy and computational cost,
we solve the dynamical problem in a pipe of length
L=4
. To ensure that the perturbation reaches
the end of the pipe, we run the simulations until
T=10
. Since equations (
8
) solve for the perturbed flow
{\mathbf{w}}
, and the steady-state solution
\mathbf{u}_{\text{HP}}
, defined in (
6
), does not depend on time, the evolution on time of the perturbation
{\mathbf{v}}
is post-computed as

{\mathbf{v}}(t,{\mathbf{x}})={\mathbf{w}}(t,{\mathbf{x}})-\mathbf{u}_{\text{HP}}({\mathbf{x}})\quad\forall t\in[0,T]\text{ and }\forall{\mathbf{x}}\in\Omega.

(41)

We shall solve the dynamical problem only using Dirichlet boundary conditions for two primary reasons. First,
dynamical behavior of the perturbation does not necessarily stay periodic. Second, imposing periodic
boundary conditions does not allow for energy to enter
the system, leading the perturbation and the flow to decay due to viscous dissipation.

Since our analysis is based on the kinetic energy theory from Reynolds and Orr
[
16
]
, we
compute the
L^{2}-
norm of the perturbation and its time derivative, to observe their evolution over time.
We shall also separately consider the streamwise- (
\mathbf{e}_{z}{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{z}
) and spanwise-
(
{\mathbf{v}}-({\mathbf{v}}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{z})\cdot\mathbf{e}_{z}
) components and evaluate their respective
L^{2}
norms.

In the following we first investigate the role of the mesh size on the evolution in time of the perturbation, by solving the dynamical problem (
8
) for different mesh sizes and their relative critical Reynolds numbers.
Then, having established a mesh size with a good accuracy, we examine the behavior of the perturbation at Reynolds
numbers equal to and exceeding the critical value.

5
Conclusion

In this study we numerically investigated the kinetic energy instability
[
4
,
16
]

of pipe flow. We considered finite pipe lengths, with the longest being
L=32
. To address the eigenvalue problem, we imposed either periodic boundary conditions or homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. In the case of periodic boundary conditions, the critical Reynolds number varies periodically with the pipe length, reaching local minima at
{\mathrm{Re}}_{c}=81.58
. Conversely, with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, the critical Reynolds number decreases as the pipe length increases, suggesting a lower bound smaller than
{\mathrm{Re}}=81.62
. Nonetheless, in both scenarios, the critical Reynolds numbers converge to similar values, which are also comparable to those found by Joseph and Carmi
[
5
]
(
{\mathrm{Re}}_{c}=81.49
for periodic perturbations). Unlike other studies
[
5
,
32
]
, our approach did not impose assumptions regarding the nature of the perturbations (streamwise or spanwise). The periodic perturbations depend on all the variables, but show a greater dependence on the spanwise (
x
and
y
) directions.

We then computed the temporal evolution of the most unstable mode, focusing on the eigenmode with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. As expected from the theory, when analyzing the perturbed flow at the critical Reynolds number, the
L^{2}-
norm of the perturbation decreases from the initial value. In contrast, when solving for Reynolds numbers exceeding the critical value, the
L^{2}-
norm of the perturbation initially increases before eventually decaying. The perturbations slightly change shape over time as they propagate down the pipe until they dissipate. Additionally, the perturbations exhibit a stronger streamwise component during the initial phase of their evolution.

Our analysis concentrated on Reynolds numbers around
{\mathrm{Re}}\approx 100
, and as a result, we did not observe
the formation of puffs or slugs
[
3
]
. Analysis of regimes with higher Reynolds number
is a potentially interesting topic for future research.
