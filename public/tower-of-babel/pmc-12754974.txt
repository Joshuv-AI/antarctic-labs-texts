Intuitive eating, maternal attachment, and weight status in postpartum women: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Begüm Çiler Ayvaz, Pelin Akin, Gözde Ede İleri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe postpartum period is characterized by significant changes in eating behaviors, emotional regulation and body weight. Intuitive eating and maternal attachment may play a key role in regulating women’s weight status during early postpartum.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted between February and May 2022 in Turkey, involving 264 healthy postpartum women (1–4 months) aged 18–40 years. Sociodemographic information, dietary habits, and anthropometric measurements were collected. Intuitive Eating Scale-2 (IES-2) and Maternal Attachment Scale (MAS) was administered. We classified BMI levels below 25 as “lower BMI”” and above 25 as “higher BMI”.ResultsOf the participants, 45.8% reported eating intuitively. Body mass index (BMI) was significantly higher in those with low levels of intuitive eating behaviour (p = < 0.001). A positive correlation was found between maternal attachment and intuitive eating (r = 0.129, p = 0.037). Higher intuitive eating was associated with lower BMI (β = -0.269, p = < 0.001). Skipping meals and night eating were associated with lower intuitive eating (p = 0.023). In logistic regression analysis, low intuitive eating (< 3.4 points) was associated with a high BMI during the early postpartum (OR = 2.63; 95% CI 1.57–4.40, p = < 0.001).ConclusionsIntuitive eating behaviour is positively associated with maternal attachment. However, higher intuitive eating is associated with lower BMI levels, night eating, and skipping meals. The results suggest that public health counselling promoting intuitive eating may be beneficial for early postpartum women who are at risk of excessive weight gain.Keywords: Intuitive eating, Maternal attachment, Postpartum, Obesity, Weight status

Introduction

Postpartum period is a concept that combines emotional, physical, hormonal, biological, cultural, and sociological factors. Becoming a mother changes a woman’s social, physical, and emotional world and leads to changes in relationships with those around her. Furthermore, the postpartum period is a time when motherhood is learned along with new responsibilities. This period also includes a physical change process [1]. During the postpartum period, it is particularly important for women to adopt healthy lifestyle behaviors to support optimal maternal health in both the short and long term. In this context, intuitive eating has emerged as a promising strategy to promote a healthier relationship with food [2].

The purpose of intuitive eating is to enable postpartum women to enjoy eating while also being able to recognize the signals that indicate the body has reached physical fullness. Research has determined that intuitive eating is related to weight status, and higher levels of intuitive eating are associated with a lower BMI [3].

The term intuitive eating was first defined by Tribole and Resch [4] as the ability to rely on internal cues to eat without experiencing guilt or ethical dilemma. Intuitive eating is a behavior that includes three main characteristics: reliance on internal hunger and satiety cues to determine the timing of starting and ending eating, non-restrictive eating, and eating for physical reasons rather than emotional reasons. In this way, postpartum women can regulate their eating behaviors by controlling emotional eating and dietary restriction. Intuitive eating habits aim to enable the individual to recognize bodily signals that indicate physical satiety and enjoy eating [3]. Intuitive eating involves experiencing the pleasure of eating by following hunger and fullness signals rather than being guided by a program that includes rigid dietary rules and an individual’s emotions. For this reason, people who eat intuitively do not classify foods as “good” or “bad” and pay attention to their hunger signals. Therefore, individuals choose the foods they like and decide the time and amount of eating according to hunger and satiety signals [5].

Intuitive eating behavior has come to play a crucial role in eating disorders and body weight control. This behavior, which focuses on hunger and satiety signals in the body, is inversely related to body mass index (BMI). Therefore, it has beneficial effects in the prevention and treatment of obesity. A high level of intuitive eating behavior reduces consuming food and portion sizes. Individuals with a lower BMI may feel confident in their body’s ability to regulate eating according to hunger and satiety signals [6, 7]. Intuitive eating is a dynamic structure that involves both personality traits and situational components. While dimensions such as trusting your body reflect more enduring tendencies, eating behaviors based on emotional or external reasons may vary depending on the situation. Mindfulness or self-compassion-based interventions have been shown to improve intuitive eating skills [8].

Women in the postpartum period have a crucial role in early life behaviors such as breastfeeding, sleep, and physical activity that affect the health of newborns. Maternal attachment refers to a mother’s emotions, thoughts, and beliefs about herself as a parent and her newborn [9]. This process develops over time and provides the basis for establishing the newborn’s relationships with the external world and its psychological development [10]. The postpartum physiological process is a multidimensional concept and has subdimensions such as the mother’s self-care, learning about baby care, social support, the mother’s psychological state, adaptation to the new process of motherhood, and attachment to the baby [11, 12].

Several studies have been conducted in the literature to evaluate the effect of intuitive eating on gestational diabetes and postpartum weight loss. Quansah et al. [13] showed that intuitive eating in the postpartum period is associated with better diet quality and metabolic health in women with a history of gestational diabetes. Leahy et al. [7] reported that women with high levels of intuitive eating experienced greater postpartum weight loss and that this behavior supported healthy weight management. Bijlholt et al. [14] systematic review emphasized that eating behaviors during pregnancy are decisive for both weight gain during pregnancy and postpartum weight retention, suggesting that approaches based on internal awareness, such as intuitive eating, may prevent excessive weight gain. Giacone et al. [15] showed that individuals with high levels of intuitive eating had healthier eating habits and lower self-reported weight. Gao et al. [16] reported that intuitive eating in women with gestational diabetes was significantly associated not only with pregnancy weight and glucose levels but also with depression and anxiety levels. Further research examining how maternal attachment may affect intuitive eating and early postpartum BMI is still needed.

Night eating is defined as eating after dinner or upon waking from sleep, characterized by recurrent nighttime eating episodes. This behaviour is characterized by a loss of appetite in the morning and a strong desire to eat between dinner and bedtime and/or during the night [17, 18]. Meal skipping is defined as skipping or not consuming one or more of the main meals (breakfast, lunch, or dinner). Meal skipping has been identified as one of the factors affecting body weight and dietary quality [19, 20].

Maternal bonding refers to the maternal emotions and thoughts toward one’s infant. It is thought to develop during pregnancy or immediately after birth, and to evolve over the first months of the infant’s life. Bonding includes maternal behaviors such as proximity seeking, touching, contact behavior, gazing, baby talk, positive expression, cuddling, smiling, and adaptation to cues expressed by the infant [21, 22].

The tendency and need to establish emotional bonds refers to the attachment system that is necessary and developmentally functional for babies to survive. By maintaining strong physical closeness between babies and their caregivers, the attachment system provides both protection against environmental threats and the necessary conditions for exploring their environment. Therefore, maintaining closeness with mothers is a primary goal within the attachment system, providing babies with a safe environment in which to develop. For a secure attachment to develop, the baby needs a mother who is consistently responsive, sensitive, and always available. If the mother is insensitive, overly intrusive, and unpredictable, the baby will develop an anxious/ambivalent attachment pattern. A mother’s loving bond with her baby is one of the most important elements that promotes the child’s healthy growth and development [23, 24].

Maternal attachment, the loving bond between a mother and her baby, is one of the most important elements that promotes the healthy growth and development of the child. Maternal attachment is a unique and special relationship that develops over time [11]. The mother-infant bond develops throughout pregnancy, birth, and the postpartum period and is supported by mother-infant interaction during the postpartum period. The stages of maternal love and attachment vary according to the Maternal Role Adaptation Theory [22]. According to this, the first phase occurs approximately one month after birth and is a physical recovery phase. During this phase, the mother is preoccupied with her own care and the care of her baby. During this phase, the mother adapts to motherhood socially and psychologically. The second phase is the achievement phase, which is completed around four or five months after birth and involves the acquisition of a maternal identity. In a study investigating intuitive eating practices during the postpartum period, it was noted that maternal nutrition interventions could be enhanced by incorporating this approach and that it supports mothers in making healthy food choices [2]. However, there is no research evaluating the relationship between intuitive eating and maternal attachment in the postpartum period to the best of our knowledge. Therefore, we aimed to determine the relationship between intuitive eating and maternal attachment levels and the effect of BMI on intuitive eating. We hypothesized that higher intuitive eating would be associated with lower BMI and greater maternal attachment. Furthermore, we hypothesized that intuitive eating would be associated with meal skipping and night eating behaviors.

Methods

Study design and population

We recruited women during the early postpartum period through announcement on social media in this cross-sectional study. Inclusion criteria were singleton pregnancy, aged 18–40 years, 1–4 months postpartum, currently breastfeeding and having healthy and full-term newborns. The inclusion criteria for the study were evaluated through the women’s self-report responses in the questionnaire during the data collection phase. We excluded women with psychological illnesses, those following restrictive diets, not breastfeeding, and those whose newborns had birth weights outside the normal range (i.e., < 2500 g or > 4000 g), or any birth weight-related complications. We selected the study sample accordingly because the MAS is valid and reliable for use with mothers with babies between 1 and 4 months postpartum in Türkiye [25].

The sample size was calculated using the PASS program prior to the study. A minimum of 106 women was needed to achieve 80.0% power at a 5.0% Type I error level, ensuring detection of a clinically significant difference.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by Ethics Committee of Istanbul Okan University (protocol code 149 and 9 February 2022). Additionally, the work was reported in accordance with the STROBE checklist.

Data collection

Data were collected between February and May 2022 using an online questionnaire created with Google Forms. The online questionnaire form was delivered to the participants free of charge via a link in social media applications (Facebook, Instagram, e-mail, WhatsApp) and groups created on these platforms. On the first page of the online questionnaire form, detailed information about the study aims and the researchers was given. Women who volunteered for the study selected ‘I agree’ to the consent question on the first page and answered the questions.

The online questionnaire form has four sections. In the first part of the questionnaire form, we recorded the maternal age, educational level, and employment status. The second part of the questionnaire included questions about early postpartum period such as first breastfeeding time and breastfeeding frequency. The third part the questionnaire included body weight and height measurements during the early postpartum period. The fourth part of the questionnaire included dietary habits, IES-2, and MAS.

Level of education was measured by asking women to indicate the following school from which they most recently graduated and received their diploma: junior high school or less, high school, university, and post graduate. Women were also categorized by the time of their first breastfeeding after postnatal period and their breastfeeding frequency. Occupational status was classified as employed or unemployed. Night eating and meal skipping were measured by asking women to indicate their level of involvement.

Anthropometry

Body weight and height were obtained based on data measured and recorded by healthcare personnel during a routine postnatal medical examination between 4 and 6 weeks postpartum in the gynecology clinic. We used the weight (kg)/height (m2) formula to calculate BMI and evaluated its levels based on the World Health Organization (WHO) classification. Accordingly, we considered those with a BMI 18.5 to < 25.0 kg/m² as normal weight and with a BMI 25.0–29.9 kg/m² as overweight, and with a BMI 30.0–35.0 kg/m² as obesity class I [26].

Intuitive eating scale-2

The IES-2, developed by Tylka and Kroon Van Diest [27] and validated by Akırmak et al. [28] was used to assess intuitive eating. The scale contains 23 items and categorized into four subscales: eating for unconditional permission to eat (UPE, allowing to eat when hungry and refusing label certain foods as forbidden), eating for physical rather than emotional reasons (EPR, eating due to physically hungry rather than to cope with emotional distress), relying on hunger and satiety cues (RHSC, eating by trusting internal hunger and satiety signals), and body-food choice congruence (BFCC, matching food choices with bodies’ needs.). Each item was measured using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). The total IES-2 score was calculated by averaging the scores of the 23 items, while the subscale scores were calculated by averaging the scores of the associated items. Total IES-2 scores range from 1 to 5, with higher scores indicating greater intuitive eating. The total scale (Cronbach’s α = 0.84) and subscales (UPE: Cronbach’s α = 0.39, EPR: Cronbach’s α = 0.82, RHSC: Cronbach’s α = 0.96, and BFCC: Cronbach’s α = 0.94) showed good internal consistency in our sample.

Maternal attachment scale

The 26-item MAS, developed by Müller [29] and validated by Kavlak et al. [25] was used to assess women’s attachment levels to their infants. Each item of the one-dimensional scale was evaluated with a 4-point Likert scale (1 = never to 5 = always), reflecting the degree of emotions women feel for their infants. The total MAS score was by averaging the scores of the 26 items. Total scores range from 26 to 104 points, with higher scores indicating stronger maternal attachment. The Turkish version of the scale demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.87) in our study population.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS version 25.0, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. The normality of the variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, histograms, and probability plots. Differences in BMI levels among subgroups for education level (junior high school or less, high school, university, post graduate), occupation status (employed vs. employed), time of first breastfeeding (in 0–1, 2–4, 5–12, 12–24 h), frequency of breastfeeding (each cry, 8–9 times, 6–7 times, < 4–5 times), skipping meal (yes or no), and night eating (yes or no) were analyzed Pearson Chi-Square tests. Student’s t-test was used to determine the difference in maternal age and total IES-2 scores among groups classified according to BMI levels (normal weight vs. with overweight/ obesity). Differences in total MAS scores among groups (normal weight vs. with overweight/ obesity) were analyzed using Mann-Whitney U test. Pearson correlation test was used to determine correlations between total intuitive eating and subgroup scores with maternal attachment and early postpartum BMI. The cut-off value of 3.4 for intuitive eating was determined by ROC curve analysis according to early postpartum BMI categories (normal vs. with overweight/obesity) [27]. Multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to examine the association between total IES-2 scores and potential factors such as early postpartum BMI, skipping meals, night eating, and maternal attachment. Maternal age, employment status, time to first postpartum breastfeeding, breastfeeding frequency, night eating, and skipping meals were included as covariates in the model. The final model constructed according to the variables most strongly associated with intuitive eating. The assumptions of the regression model were also tested. Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests were applied to examine whether the residuals were normally distributed. The Shapiro–Wilk test indicated normal distribution (W = 0.994, p = 0.330), and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was also nonsignificant (D = 0.028, p = 0.200). Standardized residuals ranged between − 2.21 and 2.48, remaining within the ± 3 range, and no outliers were detected. Taken together, these results indicate that the regression model is valid and reliable. The independent variables significantly predict intuitive eating levels, and the assumptions required for linear regression have been satisfied. The model assessing the association between intuitive eating and early BMI levels did not include age as a covariate. All models were tested using the total IES-2 score and subscale scores as dependent variables. Logistic regression analysis was used to assess the impact of independent variables (BMI categories, night eating, skipping meal, and maternal attachment score) associated with intuitive eating. Women were divided into two groups based on their early postpartum BMI: normal weight (18.5 ≤ BMI < 25.0 kg/m2) and with overweight and obesity (25.0 ≤ BMI < 30.0 kg/m2). The normal BMI category served as the reference group for comparing the women with overweight/obesity categories in predicting intuitive eating styles. Model fit was assessed using appropriate residual and goodness-of-fit statistics.

Six of these women were excluded because they had multiple pregnancies, five were more than five months postpartum, and four had low birth weight infants. Overall, 264 women were included in the final analysis. Discussion

The hypothesis of the present study were to: (1) higher intuitive eating would be associated with lower BMI and greater maternal attachment, (2) intuitive eating would be associated with meal skipping and night eating behaviors. This study examined the relationship between intuitive eating, maternal attachment, and early postpartum BMI, addressing a less explored area of research. In the early postpartum period, women with higher intuitive eating behaviour tended to have lower BMI compared to those who had fewer intuitive eating behaviour. Specifically, women who ate for physical reasons and relied on hunger and satiety cues had lower BMI levels. These findings are consistent with the research conducted by Sire et al. [30], which identified a negative correlation between intuitive eating and BMI in women. Intuitive eaters may avoid excessive energy intake compared to restrictive dieters, who experience episodes of overeating and binge eating. A possible mechanism explaining this association is that intuitive eating may be a protective factor against excessive weight gain and resulting obesity by regulating food intake. Previous research has predominantly focused on the connection between intuitive eating, body perception, and eating disorders in the general women population [31–34], while studies in the postpartum period have largely addressed its relationship with weight loss, weight retention, and body image satisfaction [7, 14, 35–38]. By addressing these gaps, our research provides a more comprehensive understanding of the early postpartum period, emphasizing the multifaceted benefits of intuitive eating.

The early postpartum period is a time marked by substantial physical and emotional transitions. In this context, women may face challenges such as disrupted sleep, fatigue, body image dissatisfaction, and emotional stress—all of which can affect eating behaviors. Conventional approaches to weight management that emphasize restriction or external dietary rules may be difficult to maintain during this period and may even exacerbate psychological distress. In contrast, intuitive eating promotes a more compassionate and self-regulated approach to food, which may be particularly well-suited for the postpartum experience. In our study, 61.9% of women were identified as having intuitive eating habits, which was higher than the 32.0% reported in a cross-sectional study of 419 postpartum women [37]. The lower prevalence in that study was attributed to unhealthy weight control behaviors, including binge eating and self-starvation, during the postpartum period. The mean intuitive eating score in our study (3.3 ± 0.65) was similar to previous findings in postpartum populations [37, 39]. Women with overweight or obesity had significantly lower scores on the subscales of eating for physical reasons and reliance on hunger and satiety cues compared to women with normal BMI. Higher body weight may impair hunger and satiety perception, causing individuals to disregard physiological signals [40]. In a longitudinal observational study involving adults showed that women with higher scores of IES were more likely to maintain their weights within ± 2 kg than those with low IE scores. Women with low IE scores (< 3.7) were more likely to gain weight (> 2 kg) than those with high IE scores [15].

These results indicate that intuitive eating associated with lower weight gain and BMI during the early postpartum period. Lower frequencies of night eating and meal skipping as well as higher maternal attachment scores predicted greater adherence to intuitive eating. These findings highlight the role of maternal attachment and BMI in shaping intuitive eating habits during the postpartum period.

Intuitive eating may positively influence postpartum health by reducing eating-related discomfort and enhancing attention to hunger and satiety signals [7]. In our study, night eating and skipping meals were negative predictors of IES-2 scores, while higher maternal attachment scores predicted greater adherence to intuitive eating at four months postpartum. These findings emphasize the benefits of intuitive eating on the nutritional habits and health outcomes of breastfeeding women. Considering the significance of eating behaviors during the early postpartum period, our results highlight the importance of implementing targeted strategies to promote intuitive eating in the postpartum period. Consistent with existing evidence, our findings confirm that intuitive eating supports healthy eating habits without emphasizing weight loss.

Eating habits in the postpartum period are essential for both maternal and fetal health [41]. Modifying eating behaviors is critical for achieving the goals of lifestyle interventions aimed at preventing excessive weight gain and obesity [36, 42]. In our study, there were no significant differences in skipping meals or night eating among the groups. However, both behaviors were inversely associated with intuitive eating. These findings are consistent with study showing that postpartum women practicing intuitive eating report healthier eating behaviors [37]. Additionally, Quansah et al. [13] found a similar association between intuitive eating and healthy eating patterns. These results indicated that intuitive eating may contribute positively to nutritional regulation during the postpartum period. Our study aligns with previous research showing that postpartum women with a normal BMI tend to have higher intuitive eating scores than those with overweight or obesity [7, 37, 38]. Intuitive eating, which focuses on listening to the body’s signals and avoiding restrictive eating, may help manage hunger and fullness cues, thereby preventing excess weight gain and lowering BMI [40, 43, 44]. The findings from our study suggest that intuitive eating could serve as an important approach in managing postpartum weight and preventing obesity-related health issues.

Maternal attachment plays a significant role in a woman’s adaptation to the postpartum period, with breastfeeding being associated with higher attachment levels [21]. In our study, we found a positive correlation between the IES-2 and MAS scores (β = 0.19, p = 0.037), and higher MAS scores predicted greater adherence to intuitive eating. Women who better adapt to the postpartum period tend to score higher in intuitive eating, with improved body satisfaction and self-confidence. Intuitive eating, by promoting a positive relationship with food, can help eliminate maladaptive eating behaviors often triggered by dietary interventions [45]. Intuitive eating behaviour is positively associated with increased optimism, self-esteem and life satisfaction, and negatively associated with eating disorder symptoms such as body mass index (BMI), dieting and binge eating. Among adult women, intuitive eating behaviour has been found to be linked with a lower incidence of eating disorders, improved emotional functioning, and various other psychosocial benefits [46]. This is probably because inconsistent eating patterns are more common when dieting. Intuitive eating may help women with overweight or obesity to maintain their weight in the long term, especially those who have not succeeded with calorie-restrictive diet. The relationship between maternal attachment and intuitive eating can be explained by emotional regulation and body awareness mechanisms. Strong maternal attachment is generally associated with lower stress levels, improved emotional balance, and higher self-compassion; this can facilitate the individual’s ability to respond more mindfully to internal hunger and fullness cues. Additionally, mothers with secure attachment tend to develop higher acceptance and confidence in their bodies, which may contribute to a reduction in restrictive or emotional eating behaviors. The combination of these factors may positively influence both nutrition and psychological well-being in the postpartum period by supporting instinctive eating behaviors [47]. While no prior research has explored the link between maternal attachment and intuitive eating, our study offers new insights into how maternal attachment and eating behaviors can improve postpartum women’s nutritional habits and metabolic health. Maternal attachment, which refers to the affectional attachment a mother feels for her infant and the process by which she protects her infant, is critical to the health of both the infant and the mother [48]. Moreover, increased maternal attachment has been shown to reduce negative emotional states such as anxiety and depression, suggesting that practices aimed at enhancing attachment may also help regulate eating behaviors by fostering emotional well-being in the postpartum period [49, 50]. Since maternal mental health is affected by stress levels, increasing maternal bonding is an important factor in reducing this level [51, 52]. Given that eating habits can also be affected by stress, increasing maternal bonding to promote intuitive eating habits may be an effective intervention method in early postpartum women.

Supporting intuitive eating behaviors in the early postpartum period can help mothers develop healthier eating habits and strengthen their emotional well-being in clinical and public health area. In this context, dieticians who care for maternal health should organize nutrition education programs to encourage women to practice intuitive eating. Additionally, community health professionals should implement an integrative and long-term treatment approach to body weight management by integrating nutritional interventions focused on intuitive eating into traditional weight management strategies.

Conclusions for practice

The present study was the first to examine the relationship between intuitive eating, maternal attachment, and BMI status in the early postpartum period, to the best of our knowledge. Our findings indicate that intuitive eating is associated with lower BMI. This study highlights that maternal attachment is associated with positive outcomes. Promoting intuitive eating could be an effective strategy for improving eating habits and managing body weight, particularly among early postpartum women with overweight or obesity. It is crucial for women to receive breastfeeding education and psychological support when needed, alongside expert nutrition education during early postpartum period. These programs should also emphasize the importance of maintaining normal body weight. Future research should investigate maternal energy intake, dietary patterns, and infant growth parameters to deepen understanding of factors relevant to intuitive eating and maternal and infant health.

We are conscious of likely limitations in the present survey. The present study should be considered in light of its limitations. The study was cross-sectional, and our results cannot establish directionality of these associations. Our sample was drawn from a specific geographic region and included only women in the early postpartum period, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Therefore, further research is needed in other populations to determine whether these results can be generalized to the broader postpartum women. In our future studies, we plan to evaluate the effects of body composition and psychological illness on intuitive eating of women during postpartum period. Another limitation of the study is that recruitment through social media and online platforms increases the participation of postpartum women who are more active in digital environments. Nutrition interventions can be enhanced by incorporating intuitive eating approaches, encouraging the development of self-regulation in eating and a mind-body connection, thereby supporting healthier and more sustainable food choices throughout the perinatal period. Additional research is needed to ensure that women practice intuitive eating behaviors to promote healthy eating habits in the early postpartum period. We planned to measure the maternal body fat and muscle levels, as well as whether they had an eating disorder.
