Regions of symmetry in images of natural scenes and artworks
Authors: Lisa Koßmann, Gonzalo Muradás Odriozola, Johan Wagemans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile established as an important contributor to shape or pattern “goodness,” mirror symmetry has yet to be extensively studied in complex images more closely resembling our everyday visual surroundings. With this work, we provide insights into symmetry detection and its underlying processes using a novel bounding-box method on 200 images of natural scenes and 200 images of artworks. In a large online study, 901 participants were asked to indicate regions of symmetry within each image by placing a rectangular bounding box on the image, adjusting it to the region of symmetry they perceived, and marking one of the two axes of the rectangle as the main symmetry axis. They could do this for as many boxes as they saw fit, and they could also use the same bounding box to mark both orthogonal axes in case of double symmetries. For every box, participants also rated the saliency of the region as well as the perceived strength of the symmetry. Next to these behavioral tasks, we asked participants in a separate block to rate the aesthetic appeal of the images. Our new method replicates the previously established dominance of vertical axes of symmetry in dot-patterns and geometric shapes in real-world images. Moreover, we noted a decrease in salience, symmetry strength, and size with the number of boxes within an image. We present the results in relation to our hypotheses and discuss their contribution to the broader literature on symmetry and aesthetics, including a comparison between human and machine vision.Keywords: symmetry, perception, natural images, natural scenes, art images, perceptual organization, bounding box, aesthetics

Introduction

Symmetry is abundant in our everyday visual reality. We can think of symmetry as a property that allows for certain transformations (i.e., reflection, rotation, and translation) without changing the appearance of a pattern, shape, or object (i.e., invariance) (Gorini, 2023, Chapters 2–3; Stewart, 2013). The most commonly associated type of transformation is reflectional symmetry, which is present if a line of symmetry placed upon a shape results in two identical (mirror) halves of that shape. In rotational symmetry, rotating a shape to any degree other than 360° will still result in the shape's original appearance. Finally, translational symmetry is not located within the shape itself but occurs if every point in the object is shifted (translated) the same distance in the same direction without changing the shape. Translational symmetries also occur when a particular pattern is repeated multiple times in a single direction (i.e., frieze patterns) or in two or more different directions (i.e., wallpaper patterns).

However, symmetries that might not be mathematically perfect can still be perceived as symmetrical. In fact, perceivers tend to be biased toward symmetry, even when displays are slightly asymmetric (King et al., 1976). Such imperfections can include distorted symmetry, symmetries with certain degrees of noise, or skewed symmetry. In distorted symmetry, symmetry is changed in some way, but the new structure still retains some symmetry, while symmetry with variable degrees of noise enables studying thresholds for tolerance to noise and/or sensitivity to symmetry in more systematic ways (e.g., van der Helm, 2010). Skewed symmetry occurs when the plane of symmetry is slanted away from being orthogonal to the line of sight (i.e., viewing the symmetry from an oblique point of view) (Saunders & Knill, 2001; Sawada & Pizlo, 2008; Wagemans, 1993; Wagemans et al., 1991).

Symmetry plays an important role in perceptual organization (Bertamini et al., 1997; Wagemans et al., 2012a; Wagemans et al., 2012b), shape detection and figure-ground segmentation (Bertamini, 2010; Kimchi et al., 2007; Machilsen et al., 2009), and aesthetic appreciation (Bertamini et al., 2019; Damiano et al., 2023; Pecchinenda et al., 2014; Pombo et al., 2023). In addition, the literature on human detection and perception of symmetry and the factors influencing the underlying processes and putative neural mechanisms is quite extensive (for reviews, see Bertamini et al., 2018; Treder, 2010; Wagemans, 1995, 1997). Symmetry has been mostly investigated in artificial dot patterns and shapes. According to these studies, symmetry detection seems to be scale-invariant (Dakin & Herbert, 1998) and task-independent (Höfel & Jacobsen, 2007; Makin et al., 2020, 2024). Only recently have attempts been made to move away from highly controlled artificial stimuli to real-world images.

Common behavioral methods to investigate symmetry detection are forced-choice tasks, categorization tasks, discrimination tasks, and arrangement tasks. In forced-choice tasks, participants are presented with stimuli simultaneously or sequentially and are asked to choose the stimulus that contains symmetry. This paradigm can be used, for example, to distinguish between symmetric and asymmetric planar figures in perspective and orthographic views (Sawada & Pizlo, 2008), or to investigate the role of symmetry in perceptual grouping (Machilsen et al., 2009). Categorization tasks include participants organizing stimuli into different categories (e.g., symmetric versus asymmetric). Pecchinenda and colleagues used this paradigm to compare affective responses to random and symmetric dot-patterns (Pecchinenda et al., 2014). Discrimination tasks require participants to discriminate between stimuli based on a specific property (e.g., which face is more symmetric) (Tjan & Liu, 2005). In the case of simpler stimuli, arrangement tasks can be very useful to investigate symmetry and its impact on aesthetic appreciation, as demonstrated by Hübner and Thömmes (2019), who replicated and extended the work of Ethel Puffer (Puffer, 1903).

Most of the preceding work was limited to simple dot patterns and shapes. While these are well controlled, they do not carry any meaning and are oftentimes presented in isolation and not embedded in a background or scene. This poses the question of how well these findings would generalize to stimuli more closely resembling our visual reality. Would we find similar results when we present participants with rich natural scenes containing meaningful objects, patterns, and shapes and their multidimensional feature variation? In those stimuli, symmetries would likely be harder to find and would often only be present in imperfect form. The perception of symmetry might also not only rely on bottom-up feature extraction and pairwise element matching in these cases but might also depend on familiarity with and knowledge about the objects in the rich scene.

Unfortunately, the traditional methods to investigate symmetry detection described above do not easily transfer to rich images. For example, forced-choice tasks are difficult to realize as there is almost always some kind of symmetry present in rich real-world images. Similarly, categorization tasks do not work well for rich scenes. While images could be categorized into more symmetric or less symmetric, the specific symmetries picked up by the participant would not be recorded. In summary, many traditional methods are difficult to apply to more complex stimuli like real-world images and artworks, where there is less control over the number and types of symmetries present and where symmetries might be imperfect.

Some of the distinction between previously employed simple stimuli and natural images 1 relates to the distinction between global and local symmetry. Global symmetry encompasses the whole object or image, while local symmetry only consists of parts of an object or image within the whole. Their interplay is complex, with evidence that global symmetry facilitates the perception of symmetry as a whole, but impairs the detection of local (a)symmetries once the global percept is established (Locher & Wagemans, 1993), global (vertical) symmetry is detected faster than local symmetry in certain stimuli and tasks (Nucci & Wagemans, 2007), and (vertical) mirror symmetry aids in figure-ground segmentation and shape detection (Machilsen et al., 2009). Different combinations of local and global symmetries (e.g., high vertical symmetry in combination with local symmetries vs. high global horizontal symmetry) influence viewers’ complexity, pleasure, and interest judgments (Damiano et al., 2023). The dichotomy between global symmetry, encompassing the whole image, and local symmetry, encompassing smaller parts of objects, does not seem to leave room for possible medium-sized regions of symmetry encompassing an object with its local symmetries and possibly part of its background. Real environments (or large-scale images thereof) are rarely (if ever) globally symmetric, and detecting or perceiving local symmetries in isolated patterns or shapes against an empty, uniform background may constitute a rather trivial task compared to detecting regions of symmetry in real environments.

The present study focuses on the detection of symmetric regions in images of rich natural scenes and images of artworks. Leveraging a novel symmetry detection task, using a bounding-box method, we enable our participants to indicate as many regions of symmetry as they perceive within an image. Additionally, we are steering away from traditional approaches that focused on global or local symmetry exclusively and instead are allowing participants to indicate regions of variable size, which we postulate to be more in alignment with the human visual system's natural process of localizing coherent image regions with Gestalt properties such as symmetry and closure as potential objects of interest. With multiple bounding boxes, it can be illustrated that the same region can be part of multiple, even nested symmetries spanning local areas and the global image. It also allows us to study the interactions between grouping, segmentation, figure-ground organization, and shape or object detection in more realistic input images.

The primary goal of this research is to introduce and evaluate a new method to investigate human symmetry detection in complex real-world images. Second, we will report insights into this kind of symmetry detection, in relation to previous work on human symmetry perception, and compare it with symmetry detection performed by a neural network. Finally, we create an extensive image dataset with over 39,000 human symmetry annotations for images of natural scenes and artworks, which can be used as a dataset and benchmark for future studies.

Methods

Participants

The study was preregistered on the Open Science Framework platform https://osf.io/c5q4f. Through the platform Prolific, 901 participants (310 female, 578 male, 13 other) were recruited. Before the start of the experiment, we collected basic demographic data from our participants, such as gender, birth date, native language, colorblindness, education level, art education, and art-history education. Participant age ranged from 18 to 79 years (M = 34.6, SD = 11.75, Median = 31). Nine participants reported implausible birth dates (e.g., dates implying ages below 18, which was an exclusion criterion, or widely above 100 years). We recoded those ages outside the 18–100 range to “NA.” Most participants reported being native speakers of English (49.2%), and a small proportion reported color vision deficiencies (0.9%). Regarding educational background, 58.5% had completed university higher education, and 17.4% had some form of art education. No participants were excluded based on demographic characteristics. All participants gave informed consent. All procedures were approved by the Social and Societal Ethics Committee of the KU Leuven (G-2023-6369-R2). Materials, Data, Analysis, and Code are available at https://osf.io/9tf4e/

Stimuli

Images were selected and prescreened for regions of symmetry from established image datasets by the authors. Two hundred images of natural scenes were chosen from the following databases: AADB (Kong et al., 2016), BSD (Martin et al., 2001), CAT2000 (Borji & Itti, 2015), COCO (Lin et al., 2014), EVA (Kang et al., 2020), FLICKR-AES (Ren et al., 2017), Oxford 102 Flower (Nilsback & Zisserman, 2008), MemCat (Goetschalckx & Wagemans, 2019), MIRFLICKR25 (Huiskes & Lew, 2008), VIU (Koehler et al., 2014). Two hundred artworks were taken from LAPIS (Maerten et al., 2025). From these datasets, images were selected based on the presence of at least some visible symmetrical structure or regions exhibiting approximate symmetry. The selection process was guided by visual inspection rather than automated criteria, with the goal of including examples that varied naturally in both the number and degree of symmetric regions and aesthetic quality. Variation in aesthetic quality here refers to the natural images ranging from mere depictions of everyday scenes to more artistically showcasing objects and scenes and varying subjective image complexity. For the artworks, which arguably all contain a certain aesthetic quality, the first 70 images were selected by going through the LAPIS dataset alphabetically, looking for symmetries. The remaining 130 images were selected by ordering the images along the aesthetic scores provided by the LAPIS authors and selecting 64 images from the highest-rated 25% and 66 images from the lowest-rated 25% containing symmetry. The selection was iterative, and the result was agreed upon by all authors.

The resulting stimulus set therefore includes a mixture of images that appear visually appealing and others that are more neutral in content and composition. This variability allows us to examine responses to symmetry across a range of aesthetic contexts while maintaining a focus on images with discernible symmetrical features.

To fit within the viewing window for the stimulus presentation in the online experiment, images were rescaled to a maximum width or height of 512 pixels for the largest dimension, thus maintaining aspect ratio.

Procedure

During an online study, participants were asked to indicate regions of symmetry and report on their aesthetic preference for either 20 artworks or 25 natural scenes (varied between participants). We chose to reduce the number of artworks presented to an individual participant because we noticed (in a pilot study) that participants need more time for the tasks when presented with an artwork than with a natural image. The experiment consisted of two blocks (see Figure 1), which were counterbalanced between participants to avoid possible sequential effects. For each individual participant, the images in both blocks were the same, but they were presented in different random orders.

Experimental design.

Note. The order of the two blocks was counterbalanced between participants. Paintings left to right: Petrov-Vodkin, K. (1930). Apple and Lemon [Oil on canvas], Sisley, A. (1876). Grapes and walnuts on a table [Oil on canvas].

In the symmetry detection block, the participants were presented with an image that contained some symmetry and asked to indicate one or more regions of symmetry within the image (one by one, sequentially). To do this, they could superimpose a rectangle on top of the region they wished to indicate (Figure 1). The rectangle was adjustable in width and height and could be rotated. We also instructed them to indicate the symmetry axis of the region by selecting either the short or long axis of the rectangle. (Note that the same region could be indicated twice, with the two different axes, in case of double symmetries.) In a next step, we asked our participants to rate the region they had just selected on salience (i.e., “How obvious or salient is the region? We say it is ‘salient’ when it pops out from the surround.”) on a five-point scale (from “very low” to “very high”) and to rate the strength of symmetry of the region they had selected (i.e., “How strong is the symmetry? We say it is ‘strong’ when the region is almost 100% symmetric.”) on a five-point scale with the items ranging from “very weak” to “very strong”). Salience is supposed to reflect how easy it is to find the region, which may depend on additional figure-ground and segmentation cues, and not just on the degree of symmetry. Strength is supposed to reflect the degree of symmetry (perfect or variable degrees of less than perfect), once the region has been delineated. Both can be interrelated, but this is not necessarily the case. Our participants then had the option to select another region within the same image or move on to the next image. They could select as many regions as they saw fit, but they had to indicate a minimum of one box per image before they could go on to the next image.

In the aesthetic preference block, our participants were asked to rate images on their aesthetic appeal on a five-point scale (e.g., “How aesthetically pleasant would you rate this image?”) with the items ranging from “very unpleasant” to “very pleasant.”

Counterbalanced blocks between participants allowed us to verify the possibility that the aesthetic preference could be larger when it was judged second compared to when it was judged first. If so, this could constitute evidence for some kind of visual literacy effect, in the sense that participants who have first paid attention to the possible symmetries in the image have looked more carefully before giving their aesthetic preference rating. This could also indicate a more direct relationship between symmetry and aesthetics.

The average completion time was 30.36 min (SD = 15.4; median = 27.75, IQR = 18.78; range = 6.85–217.41 min). All durations were retained as long as participants finished the experiment because participants could complete the task at their own pace.

Results

The dataset included 400 images with 39,253 boxes drawn by 901 participants in total, with an average of 98.1 boxes per image. One annotation from the Art condition was excluded because its height was recorded as zero. The total number of boxes drawn per image ranged from 20 to 128. Participants drew an average of 1.99 boxes per image (SD = 0.87). BoxSizes showed substantial variability, with a mean of 28,557 px2 (pixels squared) (SD = 38,732 px2) and a median of 18,055 pixels, indicating a right-skewed distribution, and a range from 377 to 446,420 px2. These extremes illustrate the variability in participant annotations, from very small selections to very large ones.

Box counts and sizes differed between the two experimental conditions. In the Art condition, participants drew up to 27 boxes per image, with individual BoxSizes ranging from 180 to 446,420 px2. In the Natural Scenes condition, box counts per image peaked at 22 boxes per image, and BoxSizes spanned 377 to 289,738 px2. Bayesian independent samples t-test on box counts per person per image (BF10 = 2.23 × 10126) and mean BoxSize per person per image (BF10 = 2.1 × 1029) provided statistical evidence for a difference between the two experimental conditions. These results indicate that both the number of annotations and the sizes of selected regions varied between conditions. This could reflect differences in participant behavior depending on stimulus type but could also be due to the different stimuli in both conditions containing more or less symmetric regions. This is hard to confirm as no ground truth for these images exists (Muradás Odriozola et al., 2026b).

We also collected aesthetic ratings for all images by our participants. Natural Scenes received higher aesthetic ratings than Artworks. The median rating for Natural Scenes was 4 (M = 3.54, n = 17,062), compared with a median of 3 for Artworks (M = 3.32, n = 22,124). A Wilcoxon rank-sum test indicated that this difference was statistically significant (W = 44,212,790, p < .001). The effect size, measured as rank-biserial correlation, was small (r = 0.096), reflecting substantial overlap between rating distributions (Figure 2).

Hypothesis 1: Vertical axes of symmetry will be indicated most often, in comparison with horizontal and diagonal axes of symmetry

Within our paradigm, participants had the ability to rotate the bounding box to indicate the bounds of the region of symmetry they perceived within the image as precisely as possible. To submit any region, they also had to indicate the axis of symmetry.

The orientation of the symmetry axis was categorized into four classes. Angles in the intervals [−22.5°, 22.5°] and [157.5°, 202.5°] were labeled as vertical, and those in the intervals [67.5°, 112.5°] and [247.5°, 292.5°] were designated as horizontal. The angles in the intervals [22.5°, 67.5°] and [202.5°, 247.5°] were labeled as left diagonal, and those in [112.5°, 157.5°] and [292.5°, 337.5°] were labeled as right diagonal.

Examining the proportions of axes of symmetry indicated, we find that vertical axes of symmetry are most frequently indicated for Natural Scenes and Artworks alike, followed by horizontal and diagonal axes (see Table 1 and Figure 3). A chi-square goodness-of-fit test showed that the distribution of symmetry-axis orientations differed significantly from uniformity, for Natural Scenes, χ2(2, N = 17,062) = 12,295, p < .001, and for Artworks, χ2(2, N = 22,191) = 17,016, p < .001. These findings confirm our Hypothesis 1.

Hypothesis 2: Symmetry ratings and salience ratings will be positively correlated

We expected ratings of symmetry strength and salience to be positively correlated. This holds true for Natural scenes and Artworks alike with respective Spearman's rho of ρ = 0.532, N = 17062, p < .001 and ρ = 0.513, N = 22191, p < .001, indicating that regions rated as more salient were also rated as stronger in symmetry.

Hypothesis 3: The size of the indicated region of symmetry will decrease with the number of indicated symmetries per image. (Larger regions of perceived symmetry are indicated first.)

BoxSize values were highly variable in both Natural Scenes and Art images (see Figure 4). Natural Scene images had generally larger BoxSizes than Art images, but both datasets included a wide range of values and occasionally strong outliers. BoxSize was positively skewed in both Natural and Art images, with Art images showing the more extreme skew. We used the fitdistrplus (Delignette-Muller & Dutang, 2015) package to determine the distribution of our dependent variable, BoxSize, in both datasets. Based on the goodness-of-fit tests, the log-normal distribution provides the best fit for the data. Conceptually, log-normal is a good choice for modelling BoxSize, as it models positive values (BoxSize cannot be negative) and can accommodate strong outliers. To test our hypothesis, we therefore fit a Bayesian multilevel regression model with a lognormal likelihood to predict BoxSize from BoxNumber, including varying intercepts for participant and image (see Equation 1). Four chains were run for 4,000 iterations each (1,000 warmup), yielding 12,000 post-warmup draws.

Artworks

Model Fit and Performance Analogous to the Natural Scenes condition described above, we fit a lognormal mixed-effects model. The coefficient for BoxNumber was estimated at −0.14 (95% CI: −0.14 to −0.13), with the entire credible interval lying below zero, indicating that BoxSize decreases systematically as BoxNumber increases on the log scale (see Table 2). Random-effects estimates showed meaningful variability across both participant (SD = 0.43) and image (SD = 0.65).

Together, these results support the hypothesis that BoxNumber is negatively associated with BoxSize, although it explains only a small portion of the total variability. Despite the strong statistical evidence for the effect of BoxNumber, the low marginal R2 suggests that additional unaccounted factors contribute substantially to variation in BoxSize.

Conclusion

Comparing the results for Natural Scenes and Artworks, we can conclude that the effect of BoxNumber on BoxSize is weaker for Artworks and the model explains less of the variance for Artworks, but the direction of the effect is the same (boxes indicated earlier are larger).

In addition, BoxNumber probably also contributes to the between-participants variance, as demonstrated by the variability we report in the introduction of this section. Participants might be applying different strategies throughout the experiment, become fatigued, and/or choose to report only one box per image.

Hypothesis 4: Within each image, the salience ratings of the region will decrease with the number of indicated symmetries per image. (Regions of perceived symmetry that are indicated first are rated higher on salience.)

We hypothesized that the regions of symmetry indicated first would be rated higher in salience than regions indicated later. This was supported by exploratory rank correlations, which were negative in both conditions (Natural Scenes: ρ = −0.12; Artworks: ρ = −0.08, both p < .001). We fit a Bayesian Ordinal Logistic regression model with the saliency rating as the outcome variable and the BoxNumber as the predictor, including varying intercepts for participant and image (see Equation 2).

Artworks

The coefficient for BoxNumber was −0.22 (95% CI [−0.23, −0.20]), suggesting a negative association with the odds of being in higher salience-rating categories (see Table 3). As for Natural Scenes, predicted posterior probabilities reveal that saliency ratings decrease as BoxNumber increases (see Figure 5). Boxes appearing early in the sequence (BoxNumber = 1–5) are more frequently assigned higher or intermediate ratings, whereas later boxes (BoxNumber ≥ 15) are predominantly rated the lowest. The probability of a middle rating reaches its maximum around BoxNumber = 5 before declining, and highest possible salience ratings are almost entirely absent beyond BoxNumber = 10. Credible intervals are broader for higher ratings, reflecting greater uncertainty for these less common outcomes. These findings indicate a systematic shift toward lower saliency ratings for later boxes, consistent with a negative association between BoxNumber and perceived salience.

Conclusion

Across both datasets, the ordinal regression models consistently indicated a negative association between BoxNumber and salience ratings. In each case, higher BoxNumber values were linked to lower probabilities of responses falling in higher salience-rating categories. The estimated effect was slightly stronger for Natural Scenes (β = −0.32) than for Artworks (β = −0.22), but comparable participant-level variability across conditions (SD = 1.12 for Natural Scenes; 1.18 for Artworks) suggests broadly similar response patterns. Image-level variability was marginally lower for Natural Scenes (SD = 0.81) than for Artworks (SD = 1.03), indicating slightly more homogeneous salience judgments. Taken together, the results provide support for the hypothesis that BoxNumber is negatively related to perceived salience in both Natural Scene images and Art images.

Hypothesis 5: The predictions of a machine learning model trained to detect symmetry will correspond better with the human data for regions with higher saliency and strength ratings

We employed a machine learning model, a U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015), to investigate the generalizability of the data collected with our bounding box method to other images and to compare deep neural network symmetry detection to that of our human participants. We are opting for a more complex machine learning model instead of traditional computer vision algorithms, because human symmetry perception is more flexible, graded, and complex than the strict mathematical definition of symmetry on which such algorithms rely. To take the “human factor” of perception into account, we chose to employ machine learning in combination with human data.

U-Net was developed for image segmentation tasks and is especially suitable for smaller training sets. Image segmentation and symmetry are codependent. Image segmentation is relevant for our symmetry task because it defines geometric boundaries of regions within which mirror reflections can be found. This helps differentiate regions or objects with and without symmetry. Symmetry, on the other hand, is a known cue for segmentation.

The U-Net's U-shaped architecture consists of a contracting path, which captures contextual information by progressively down-sampling the image, and an expansive path, which restores spatial resolution for precise localization. In the contracting path, repeated convolutions and pooling operations extract key features, while in the expansive path, up-sampling and concatenation with features from the contracting path refine the segmentation. The network's layers work together to analyze, simplify, and reconstruct the image. In the final layer, a 1 × 1 convolution maps each feature vector to the desired number of classes, assigning image regions to categories. The description is based on the original paper by Ronneberger et al. (2015); more details can be found in their publication.

We chose U-Net because it is a well-established and widely used convolutional neural network for image segmentation. Another advantage is that U-Net can produce full-resolution pixel-wise predictions efficiently without requiring excessively large or computationally expensive models.

For our purpose of symmetry detection, we first trained the model on a procedurally generated dataset (Muradás Odriozola et al., 2026a). This dataset was created using the MNIST (Deng, 2012) dataset (see Supplemental Material for more information), and its fully synthetic nature forces the model to “learn” to distinguish between symmetries and asymmetries.

To test our hypothesis, we investigated how accurately the model could classify symmetries in relation to human ratings on the strength and salience of the symmetric areas. To evaluate the model's accuracy on the salient and symmetric regions rated by our participants, we tested each symmetry axis individually as reported by participants. For each axis, we generated a mask consisting of a 10-pixel-wide segment (matching the thickness used during model training) to delineate the symmetry axis. We then performed inference on the image, obtained the model's prediction, and calculated accuracy by dividing the intersection of the prediction and ground truth (participant-labeled data) by the ground truth. Following this, we classified the results into five categories for both symmetry and saliency, based on the reported strength of each feature.

The general trend (see Figure 6) reveals that the model more accurately detects symmetries of high salience and strong symmetry rating.

F1 scores (see Equation 3) are commonly used for comparing classification models by taking precision (P) and recall (R) into account. Precision is the accuracy of positive predictions and recall the sensitivity or true positive rate (see Equations 3 and 4, respectively) (Goodfellow et al., 2016). Both terms use hits and misses, borrowing terms from Signal Detection Theory (SDT) but do not use them in the same way as the typical SDT metrics for sensitivity and bias. True positives are hits, false negatives are misses, false positives are false alarms, and true negatives are correct rejections. While hits contribute to both Precision and Recall, false alarms only affect Precision and misses only affect Recall. Although Precision and Recall are therefore not identical to sensitivity and bias, adjusting the decision threshold in a machine learning model is analogous to adjusting the bias in SDT. When comparing models with unbalanced numbers of classes, the Weighted-F1-score (see Equation 6) is employed, because it calculates the F1 score for each class and then weighs it by the number of instances in that class.

Even though our model's performance has increased drastically through fine-tuning with human data, its performance is better for natural scenes compared to artworks. One reason for this may be that there seems to be more variability in the human data for artworks as well, illustrated by the smaller model estimates and larger random effects for image and participants in our predictive models above (e.g., H5, H6). Hypothesis 7: Aesthetic appreciation will be higher on average if the ratings are done after the symmetry detection task, compared to the ratings being done before the symmetry detection task.

Our counterbalanced design of the symmetry task in one block and the aesthetic ratings in the other enables us to make some inferences on the influence of prior exposure to the images for their aesthetic appeal. To investigate differences between the groups of participants who performed the symmetry task first and those who performed the aesthetic rating task first, we employ a Bayesian alternative to a t-test, using the ttestBF function from the BayesFactor (Morey et al., 2015) package. It evaluates whether two groups differ in their mean values by comparing the predictive performance of two models: one in which the group means are allowed to differ (the alternative hypothesis) and one in which the means are assumed equal (the null hypothesis). The Bayes factor quantifies how much more (or less) the observed data supports one model over the other (Figure 8).

Aesthetic ratings of the counterbalanced blocks.

Natural Scenes

The Bayes factor comparing the alternative hypothesis to the null was BF10 = 0.120. This indicates that the data were approximately 8.3 times more likely under the null hypothesis (BF01 ≈ 1 / 0.120 = 8.3) than under the alternative, providing substantial evidence for no difference between the groups. We consider this strong evidence in favor of the null hypothesis.

Artworks

For our Artworks condition, the Bayesian independent-samples t-test result reports a Bayes Factor of BF10 ≈ 0.016, which means the data is about 62.5 times more likely under the null hypothesis than under the alternative hypothesis. As with the Natural Scenes before, the order of experimental blocks seems to make no difference for the aesthetic rating. We therefore reject the alternative Hypothesis 7. For this analysis, one participant had to be excluded as the order of blocks was not recorded due to technical issues.

Further Investigation Into Symmetry and Appreciation

In addition to this preregistered version of H7, we have also investigated another, perhaps somewhat more direct way to test the relationship between symmetry and appreciation.

We aggregated the data by participant and image, and computed the sum of symmetry boxes. We then fit a Bayesian cumulative ordinal mixed-effects model to examine the effect of the number of symmetry boxes drawn on each image on aesthetic ratings across all participants. The model included random intercepts for participants and images to account for individual baseline differences and variation across images.

To investigate individual differences, we then fit a hierarchical model including random slopes for participants, allowing participants to differ in both baseline rating tendencies and sensitivity to the number of boxes. Specifically, we analyzed how the number of symmetry boxes drawn by each participant on each image related to their aesthetic rating of that image. This reflects the assumption that participants might vary in their baseline aesthetic rating (e.g., give generally higher or lower ratings overall) and/or their sensitivity to the number of symmetries present within an image and that these two variations might be correlated.

Natural Scenes

Across images, there was a non-significant positive population-level effect of symmetry boxes on aesthetic ratings (β = 0.05, 95% CI = [−0.01, 0.09] (Table 5)). Random intercepts revealed substantial variability across participants (SD = 0.76 (95% CI [0.69, 0.83]) and images (SD = 1.24 (95% CI [1.11, 1.38]).

This implies that participants who generally give high aesthetic ratings to the artworks tend to respond negatively as the number of symmetries increases, whereas participants who generally give lower ratings tend to respond more positively (see Figure 9).

Conclusion

Together, these results indicate that while the number of symmetry boxes shows a small overall effect on image ratings, individuals might vary in how they incorporate the number of boxes into their judgments. Notably, each participant rated a subset of images with varying numbers of boxes; the estimated participant-specific slopes reflect sensitivity within the observed data, with partial pooling stabilizing estimates for participants with fewer or less variable observations.

The very weak negative relationship between symmetric regions and appreciation should not be taken as evidence that other aspects of composition, in addition to the presence of symmetries as marked by the bounding box method, play no role at all. In another study, we are investigating more sophisticated notions of composition and spatial layout in relation to aesthetic appreciation (Koßmann & Wagemans, 2023).

Discussion

A New Method for Symmetry Detection in Rich Images

The purpose of this study was to investigate human symmetry detection in rich images of Natural Scenes and Artworks, using a novel bounding box technique. The bounding box method enables us to deal with the inherent presence of multiple different symmetries within a complex stimulus, while still gathering data on the strength and salience of each individual symmetry. Participants are not confined to experimenters’ preselection and can indicate as many symmetries as they are perceiving, tailoring the bounding box to the desired size and orientation. The strong contribution of random effects, meaning the effects of the different images and participants, in our analyses also highlights a practical advantage of the bounding box method: by collecting multiple regions per participant and per image, we capture a richer set of observations that reflects both individual differences and stimulus-specific variability.

A possible limitation of our method is that, currently, the box is constrained to a rectangular shape. The consequence is the negative space at the edges, meaning that the box is usually larger than the boundary of the object, if one wanted to just indicate that. Supplementing the rectangular bounding box with other options (e.g., ellipses, circles, and freeform polygons) could result in more refined annotations. We are working on incorporating those features and making the code available for other researchers to use.

Symmetry Detection With the Bounding Box Method

For Natural Scenes as well as Artworks, vertical symmetries are indicated most often, replicating findings already made in simpler stimuli (for reviews, see Treder, 2010; Wagemans, 1995, 1997). The size of the bounding boxes also decreases from those indicated first to later boxes, with some notable outliers for images of artworks. However, a Bayesian log-normal regression model to quantify this relationship only explains a small amount of variance. Next to participant preferences and image characteristics, another reason for this could be that participants do not necessarily select the largest possible symmetric regions, but that they are instead strongly influenced by object characteristics. We investigate this in a manuscript in preparation (Muradás Odriozola et al., 2026b) which focuses on figure-ground segmentation and utilizes segmentation and object detection algorithms as additional factors.

Our measures of symmetry and salience were highly correlated in Natural Scenes and Artworks. The strength and saliency of a symmetric region was determined by asking our participants to rate how salient the region was (i.e., “How obvious or salient is the region? We say it is ‘salient’ when it pops out from the surround.”) and how strong the symmetry was that they perceived (i.e., “How strong is the symmetry? We say it is ‘strong’ when the region is almost 100% symmetric.”). Previous work found that the salience of symmetry was found to depend on context, even in mirror symmetric patterns (Cohen & Zaidi, 2013), and that bilateral symmetry leads to stronger figure-ground percepts in Kanizsa configurations (Dresp-Langley, 2019). Therefore, the correlation we observe could result from embedding the symmetry in more complex backgrounds in our study, as well as the sequential nature in which participants indicated boxes and rated the symmetries. We confirmed these intuitions by fitting a Bayesian Ordinal Logistic regression, with the rating of salience as the dependent variable and size of the bounding box as the predictor.

When compared to human symmetry detection, we see that a U-Net trained on synthetic symmetries and fine-tuned on our dataset corresponds more closely to human performance in Natural Scenes and Artworks than a model that was not fine-tuned on our dataset. As a general trend, model performance and human ratings are higher in Natural Scenes than Artworks. Additionally, the model's accuracy is higher in regions that human participants labeled as high in salience and/or strongly symmetric. The implications of our rich human data for successful model training and symmetry detection are discussed further in more detail in a more technical paper (Muradás Odriozola et al., 2026a).

Aesthetic Preference

For our stimuli set, we used 200 images of everyday scenes from various datasets and 200 images of artworks. These natural images closely resemble our visual reality and vary in complexity. All images were selected to contain symmetry but were not specifically sampled to be aesthetically pleasing. In previous work on patterns and designs, symmetry has been found to be an important factor for preference (Jacobsen & Höfel, 2002; Weichselbaum et al., 2018; Westphal-Fitch et al., 2012). However, as the complexity of the stimulus increases, for patterns and designs (Jacobsen & Höfel, 2002; Weichselbaum et al., 2018) as well as (art) photographs (Vissers et al., 2020), the content rather than image features drives the aesthetic ratings. In our study, images are rich overall and always contain meaningful content (even in abstract art, artists do not paint or draw lines and shapes randomly). The median beauty rating across all images in our study was 4 for Natural Scenes and 3 for Artworks on a 5-point scale, with 5 being the most aesthetically pleasing. Although natural scenes received statistically higher aesthetic ratings than artworks overall (owing to the large sample size), the magnitude of this difference was small. Ratings for the two categories showed substantial overlap, as reflected in a small effect size (rank-biserial r ≈ .10). This suggests that while natural scenes are, on average, perceived as slightly more aesthetically pleasing, both stimulus types elicit broadly similar aesthetic responses.

We also find that a participant's aesthetic response is not well predicted by the number of symmetries indicated in an image by all participants or the participant themselves. Our data shows large individual differences in the form of variability of their baseline aesthetic rating and their sensitivity to symmetry.

We were also interested in examining whether a detailed investigation of an image, as necessitated by our symmetry task, would lead to higher aesthetic appreciation than present in a conventional rating task. Our counterbalanced order of blocks, symmetry detection first or aesthetic rating first, enabled this investigation.

Bayesian t-tests indicate that the order of stimulus presentation had little to no effect on participants’ ratings. As before, this might be due to participants relying on image content and other high-level features to make their aesthetic judgement, more than on symmetry or other image regularities that require more careful visual inspection.

General Conclusion

This work introduces a new behavioral task for symmetry detection and extends findings from experiments using simplistic stimuli to more ecologically valid complex images of Natural Scenes and Artworks. The results derived from this method are compatible with traditional findings of symmetry detection. We have also gained some new insights into the relation of symmetry and salience in rich images as well as the process of symmetry detection in more ecologically valid stimuli. Flexible enough to accommodate real-world images, the bounding box method is a useful tool to investigate human regional symmetry detection.
