Obesity does not influence SARS-CoV-2 humoral vaccine immunogenicity
Authors: Mathieu D’Souza, Alexa Keeshan, Christopher A. Gravel, Marc-André Langlois, Curtis L. Cooper
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Obesity is a recognized factor influencing immune function and infectious disease outcomes. Characterization of the influence of obesity on SARS-CoV-2 humoral vaccine immunogenicity is required to properly tailor vaccine type (mRNA, viral-vector, protein subunit vaccines) and dosing schedule. Data from a prospective cohort study collected over 34 months was used to evaluate the slope of antibody production and decay and neutralizing capacity following SARS-CoV-2 vaccination in individuals with and without obesity at baseline. Most participants were female (65.4%), white (92.4%), and received mRNA vaccines. 210 were obese and 697 non-obese. Sex and infection-acquired immunity were identified as effect modifiers for the relationship between obesity and COVID-19 vaccine humoral immunogenicity. No consistent influence of obesity on peak titres, titre retention, antibody isotype (IgG, IgM, IgA), or neutralization was identified when controlling for other key variables. It may not be necessary to consider this variable when developing SARS-CoV-2 vaccine dosing strategies.

Introduction

The severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) emerging in Wuhan China in December 2019, quickly spread worldwide due to its high level of transmissibility causing millions of deaths and hospitalizations1. In response to the novel pandemic, vaccines were developed to reduce dissemination, morbidity and mortality resulting from SARS-CoV-2 virus infection. Vaccine immunogenicity is influenced by host factors including age, gender, gut microbiota, and comorbidities,2,3 viral variants (i.e., Omicron sublineages), as well as external elements pertaining to vaccine administration, type, number of doses, and dosing interval3,4. Across a wide range of other infectious diseases including influenza, hepatitis B, tetanus,5 and middle eastern respiratory virus (MERS), obesity has been recognized as a variable influencing vaccine immunogenicity6–8. The influence of obesity on SARS-COV-2 vaccine immunogenicity has been evaluated but not been fully elucidated9,10. Projections from the World Obesity Federation estimate that by 2035, the international prevalence of obese and overweight individuals will rise from 38% (in 2020) to over 50%11. The escalating obesity epidemic,12 and its multifaceted impacts on public health, emphasizes the importance of gaining a more comprehensive understanding of its influence on both acute and long-term humoral vaccine efficacy.

Individuals living with obesity are at higher risk for severe outcome with COVID-19 infection including increased hospitalization and ICU admission9,13,14. Prior studies have suggested lower antibody titres6–8 accelerated antibody waning, and attrition of neutralizing capacity with obesity9. However, the literature is not consistent. In fact, some prior publications report that obese patients exhibited higher peak antibody titres9, including neutralizing antibody tires10.

The Stop the Spread Ottawa (SSO) cohort study provides insight into the dynamics of the immune response to natural infection and vaccination in a diverse, urban-based population followed over the entirety of the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic. Our robust serial monitoring of participants allows for an in-depth assessment of vaccine immunogenicity in obese and non-obese individuals. To this end, our group assessed quantification of key antibody isotypes against the full trimeric spike protein and receptor binding domain as a function of obesity. We hypothesize that obese individuals will exhibit a reduced rate of antibody production and faster rate of antibody decay following vaccination.

Results

Study participant characteristics

Of 907 eligible participants, 210 were classified as obese and 697 as non-obese (Table 1). Of 121 participants with self-reported obesity, 72 had BMI data available. The mean BMI for these participants was 37.1 (standard deviation: 5.8; range: 30.0–55.1). Compared to the non-obese group, those classified as obese were older (mean age; 48.2 vs 44.9 years), had a greater proportion of men (39.1% vs 33.3%) and comorbidities including hypertension (21.0% vs 7.6%) and diabetes (9.0% vs 3.2%). Non-obese individuals consumed more alcohol (mean average number of standard drinks per week; 3.3 vs 2.4). Almost 90% of participants received an mRNA vaccine for their initial dose and 98% plus received a mRNA vaccine for subsequent doses (Supplemental Table 1). The crude incidence of SARS-CoV-2 infection (46.2% vs 38.2%) and incidence of repeat infection (8.1% vs 4.9%) was higher in obese SSO participants. Univariable models comparing obese and overweight participants to normal and underweight were generated for anti-S and anti-RBD IgG to explore the impact of different comparison groups on vaccine immunogenicity. This grouping attenuated the effect of obesity and overweight participants were not grouped with obese participants for all study analyses. Sensitivity analyses were conducted with univariable models to investigate BMI cut-offs of 35 and 40 (except a BMI of 40 for males with no prior infection due to limited sample size). The results did not indicate that an effect by obesity on vaccine immunogenicity was present at higher BMI values.

Modelling results for some measures and timepoints differed according to sex (Fig. 2a–c). The slope of anti-S IgG, anti-RBD IgG and anti-S IgA antibody production was reduced for obese males with no prior SARS-CoV-2 infection after the first vaccine dose but not with subsequent doses or for IgM antibodies. These observations contrast those of female participants with no prior infection, for which no difference in the slope of titre production was observed following the first vaccine dose. Obese female participants with a history of SARS-CoV-2 infection experienced a less steep increase in neutralizing capacity following dose 1 compared to non-obese females. This was not observed in obese males. Therefore, sex was identified as a potential modifier in obesity’s influence over vaccine-elicited titres.

Preexisting infection-acquired immunity against SARS-CoV-2 was also identified as a variable that may modify the relationship between obesity and vaccine humoral response. Obese females who had a prior SARS-CoV-2 infection when the first vaccine dose was administered demonstrated a less steep increase in neutralization activity following vaccination compared to non-obese females, but no difference was seen for obese females without prior SARS-CoV-2 at the time of vaccination.

No reduction in neutralization activity was observed for obese males without history of SARS-CoV-2 infection, suggesting that the observed reduction in the slope of titre production for anti-S IgG, anti-RBD IgG and anti-S IgA may not be clinically meaningful (Supplementary Table 1). Trends in neutralization activity were similar for obese and non-obese females after two vaccination doses, regardless of SARS-CoV-2 history. Altogether, obesity did not predict the humoral response to SARS-CoV-2 vaccines. We did not observe consistent fluctuations in the slope of anti-S or anti-RBD IgG, IgM, and IgA titres or anti-S IgG neutralizing capacity increase and decay as a function of obesity following SARS-CoV-2 vaccination after two doses and one booster dose.

Discussion

Overall, our analysis illustrates a lack of consistent influence of obesity on humoral SARS-CoV-2 vaccine immunogenicity assessed by antibody titre production, antibody titre decay rates, and neutralizing capacity. This is in contrast to several other publications which suggest a negative influence of obesity on SARS-CoV-2 immunogenicity6,7,9,10,15. However, many of these studies were cross-sectional and only analysed crude IgG titres against either the S or RBD antigen16–20. Small sample sizes (ranging from n = 63–252), which reduced power and/or precluded subgroup analysis by sex, and short follow-up (e.g., 1 month after the second vaccine dose) are other limiting factors in the literature16–20. For example, Phumisantiphong et al. reported lower titres against the RBD after 2 doses of the Sinovac vaccine for obese participants (BMI > 30) compared to lower BMI categories in a cohort of 473 healthcare workers21. Although the sample size was large, the cross-sectional comparison was limited to 4-5 weeks after dose 2 and no adjustment for any confounders such as sex or age was completed. The vaccine type also differed compared to our study. mRNA based SARS-CoV-2 vaccines tend to be more immunogenic than non-mRNA based vaccines22–25. It is plausible that the use of mRNA SARS-CoV-2 vaccines may overwhelm any influence of obesity on vaccine immunogenicity.

Several studies support our findings and did not report an impact by obesity on SARS-CoV-2 vaccine immunogenicity26–42. Papaioannidou et al. conducted multivariable analysis of anti-S IgG titres 2–4 weeks after two doses of the Pfizer-BioNTech (BNT162b2) vaccine in a cohort of healthcare workers (n = 290)41. Before and after adjusting for sex and age, obesity and BMI did not predict titres. Additionally, according to Bayart et al. BMI did not predict anti-S/RBD IgG up to 42 days following complete vaccination with BNT162b2 after adjusting for sex, age and blood type, regardless of SARS-CoV-2 infection history (n = 231)30. Similar results were obtained for the AstraZeneca/Oxford vaccine (ChAdOx1) vaccine in multivariable regression analyses of anti-S/RDB and anti-S IgG titres performed by Lee et al. and Singh et al.36,42. Some studies have obtained variable results regarding the effect of obesity on SARS-CoV-2 vaccine humoral immunogenicity depending on previous SARS-CoV-2 infection status, the measure of obesity or humoral immunogenicity assessed, sex, age group and time point of assessment7,9,16,30,43–45. Many of these reports were also limited by the same biases that impact the studies that suggest that obesity negatively impacts vaccine immunogenicity.

We believe that our analysis is more robust as we considered key confounders, accounted for multiple antibody isotypes, prospectively assessed the durability of immunogenicity longitudinally over an extended follow-up period and evaluated neutralization as well. While evaluation of antibody titre trends provided a quantitative measure of the humoral immune response, additional investigation into neutralizing capacity allowed for a greater representation of functional efficacy leading to a more comprehensive assessment. We adjusted for important factors which reportedly influence antibody titre decline in our analyses, including aging, immune compromised status, vaccine type, and the interval between first and second doses7,46,47. Among the research conducted, few have reviewed the long-term effects of obesity spanning up to three doses while also employing a large, heterogeneous population.

Our analysis suggests that sex is a key modifier of the influence of obesity on the slope of IgG titre production following vaccination. This effect was predominantly in male participants leading to a blunted humoral response with the initial doses of vaccine which was not observed with subsequent boosters. Prior COVID-19 studies highlight decreased neutralization capacity and lower antibody titres in male participants10,47. Yamamoto et al. also demonstrated effect modification by sex and BMI on anti-S IgG titres after two doses of the BNT162b2 vaccine (n = 2435)48. These results are consistent with our findings. The observed difference may be the results of the underlying physiological difference that exist between males and females. Adipose tissue distribution in men and women differ (e.g., obese males have more visceral fat which leads to higher levels of chronic inflammation) and higher oestrogen levels in women are associated with increased T and B cell activity, while testosterone has been linked to diminished antibody production20,48,49.

Distinct humoral responses to the initial vaccine dose were observed in this study for obese and non-obese participants according to preexisting infection-acquired immunity against SARS-CoV-2. Differences in the immune response to natural infection and vaccination have been noted for SARS-CoV-2 in previous studies and the characterization of hybrid immunity and its complexities continues to be an important area of research26,50. Kara et al. similarly identified infection-acquired immunity as a variable influencing the relationship between obesity and vaccine humoral immunogenicity7: obesity was associated with lower anti-S IgG titres after vaccination for those without a prior infection but not those with a history of COVID-19 (n = 290). Together with our results, these data suggest that hybrid immunity may also modify the relationship between obesity and vaccine humoral response and consequently should be considered in future work.

Although slower production of some antibody isotypes was observed after dose 1 for obese males without a prior SARS-CoV-2 infection, there was no corresponding reduction in antibody activity in neutralization assays. These results emphasize the importance of investigating antibody activity in addition to antibody concentration to fully characterize the humoral immune response. While often correlated51,52, discrepancies may occur between antibody levels and neutralization capacity53,54. Neutralization activity for males and females after 2 vaccine doses was similar among obese and non-obese participants up to 3 doses, whether they had vaccine-derived immunity or hybrid immunity. Higher normalized neutralization titres are strongly associated with improved protection against COVID-1955–57, and indeed incidences of breakthrough infections during follow-up were similar for obese and non-obese participants. These findings demonstrate the effectiveness of vaccination against COVID-19 in obese individuals.

Optimizing vaccine type and adjuvants as well as dosing schedule and mode of delivery in vulnerable populations is crucial to reduce COVID-19 morbidity and mortality. Vaccination schedule is a key factor in immunization as antibody waning leaves individuals vulnerable the longer they wait for a booster dose. This phenomenon is exemplified in obese individuals who reportedly possess increased antibody decay rates leaving them more vulnerable to breakthrough infection due to poor long-term immunity/sustained antibody response45,58. In our study, we accounted for the time interval between doses and no correlation was found between obesity and poorer antibody waning. However, obese individuals had a higher overall risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection and reinfection. Evidence in the literature indicates that obese individuals are at a higher risk of severe COVID-1959. SARS-CoV-2 vaccination is an effective means of improving clinical outcomes60,61 and we demonstrated that humoral vaccine response is comparable in obese and non-obese individuals. SARS-CoV-2 vaccination and boosting remains a powerful tool for developing and maintaining protection against COVID-19 in obese individuals, especially as immunity wanes and novel variants emerge over time56.

Several limitations are acknowledged. Cases of COVID-19 infection and vaccination details were obtained either partly or completely by participant self-report. We believe that our data is of high quality given the standardization of data collection protocols, prospective data collection and verification of vaccination and infection values by use of serology data generated from our participants. We were unable to assess vaccine immunogenicity beyond dose three given sparsity of data. Obesity status was ascertained at baseline by self-report and/or BMI calculation. We did not reassess for weight change over the course of the study which could have influenced our findings in the later months of SSO participation. BMI, a common and easy way of tracking body fat percentage, is not a perfect measure of obesity and can vary due to bone density, and muscle mass. We did not assess other anthropomorphic measures such as waist circumference or body fat percentage which could have potentially increased the reliability of the results. Diet, exercise, and genetic predispositions could influence the immune system but were not considered in this analysis. A large proportion of the cohort is white and this may limit generalizability. We assessed an adult cohort and cannot comment on the role of obesity on vaccine immunogenicity in childhood.

We did not assess mucosal humoral immunity or measures of cellular immune response. Further research into these domains of immune response in obese individuals following SARS-CoV-2 may provide additional insights as the impact of obesity on vaccine-elicited humoral and cell-mediated immunity may differ. Few studies have been conducted in which both the humoral and cell-mediated immune response to COVID-19 vaccination were analysed62. Animal studies provide some evidence that both classes of adaptive immunity are impaired in obese mice. O’Meara et al. and Chen et al. reported that serum antibody titres and T cell activation following vaccination with the BNT162b2 vaccine were reduced in mice fed a high-fat diet compared to a normal diet63,64. Evidence in humans is mixed. In an analysis of a cohort (n = 115) in which 67% of participants received two doses of the ChAdOx1 vaccine, BMI did not predict vaccine-elicited anti-S antibody titres but higher BMI was associated with a reduced frequency of TNF+CD4+, TNF+CD8+, and IL-2+CD4+ SARS-CoV-2 specific T cells65. However, participants with a BMI above 30 were poorly represented (n = 13) which may bias these results, and fewer individuals received an mRNA vaccine compared to the SSO cohort, which limits comparability with our study. Another study by Choi et al. reported a negative association between BMI and T cell response but not humoral immunogenicity; however, similarly only four individuals were obese according to their BMI and all 80 received the ChAdOx1 vaccine66. Contrasting results were obtained by Herzberg et al. in a longitudinal cohort study of 184 individuals vaccinated with BNT162b267. Obesity was associated with reduced anti-S IgG titres and neutralizing antibodies but not with SARS-CoV-2 specific T cell response. Zhu et al. reported that inactivated vaccines (BBIBP-CorV or CoronaVac) elicited lower anti-RBD IgG titres, neutralizing antibody titres, and SARS-CoV-2 specific TNF-α+ T cell activity in those classified as obese or overweight68. The literature is limited and conflicted.

Various studies indicate that mRNA vaccines are more immunogenic and effective than alternative types such as ChAdOx165,69. mRNA vaccines may be sufficiently immunogenic as to generate comparable humoral and cell-mediated immune responses in obese and non-obese individuals while other vaccine types may fail to elicit the same response and may impact T cell and B cell functioning differently. In this study, the majority of doses administered were mRNA vaccines. Therefore, a comparison of immunogenicity between mRNA and non-mRNA vaccines was not possible. Humoral and cell-mediated immune responses in obese individuals could not be compared according to vaccine type. Additionally, the findings of this study may not apply to other vaccine types.

In conclusion, our analysis of a large urban-based North American cohort suggests that obesity does not consistently affect SARS-CoV-2 anti-S and anti-R IgG, IgM, and IgA following vaccination. Similarly, obesity does not influence neutralizing antibodies. As such, it may not be necessary to consider this variable when developing SARS-CoV-2 vaccine dosing strategies.

Methods

Study design

The Stop the Spread Ottawa (SSO) cohort study was implemented to assess SARS-CoV-2 humoral and cellular immune responses following infection and vaccination across multiple at-risk groups. SSO is a prospective study which has collected longitudinal data on individuals residing in the Ottawa, Canada region, 18 years of age or older, and who are at high risk of exposure to SARS-CoV-2 (due to health conditions or occupation), experiencing severe SARS-CoV-2 infection outcomes, and/or have a previous documented SARS-CoV-2 infection (confirmed by quantitative reverse transcription polymerases chain reaction test (RT-PCR) or serology analyses)46,70. Recruitment occurred from September 2020 until February 2022. The initial 1006 participants were followed for 10 months and underwent serial blood draws and completed serial questionnaires. Three hundred of these participants completed an additional 24-month extension phase.

Individuals meeting SSO eligibility criteria (n = 1006) were recruited and included in a convalescence cohort comprised of individuals with a history of COVID-19 infection (confirmed by RT-PCR or serology analyses) and a surveillance cohort comprised of individuals at high risk of exposure to SARS-CoV-2. Surveillance cohort participants with a confirmed case (by RT-PCR or rapid antigen test) of COVID-19 infection were voluntarily transferred to the convalescent cohort. Specific to our obesity analysis, participants were included with data regarding obesity status, serum antibody results, and vaccination dates. Participants who became pregnant during follow-up were excluded. Participants were classified as obese at baseline if they had a BMI of 30 or above71 or if they self-identified as being obese in the baseline questionnaire. Variables including age, sex, ethnicity, comorbidities, immune compromised status, time between vaccine doses, and time from last COVID-19 infection were adjusted for in the analysis. Immune compromising conditions were identified and classified based on expert opinion and evidence in the literature regarding strength of immunosuppressive effects. Cancer, organ/tissue transplant, primary immunodeficiencies, rheumatological conditions (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, ankylosing spondylitis), multiple sclerosis, myasthenia gravis, active HIV (detectable viral load ≥20 IU/mL), and serious liver disorder (e.g., severe cirrhosis) were considered high-level immune compromising. Respiratory conditions requiring medication (i.e. asthma), autoimmune diseases (e.g., celiac disease, autoimmune hepatitis), fully suppressed HIV (viral load <20 IU/mL), less severe liver disorder (e.g., mild cirrhosis) and kidney disorders (i.e. chronic kidney disease) as well as excessive alcohol consumption were considered low-level immune compromising. Use of immunosuppressants and inflammatory bowel disease were also included as immune compromising states, but the level was dependent on the strength of immunosuppression caused by the specific medications.

SARS-CoV-2 vaccination and infection data

SARS-CoV-2 IgG, IgA and IgM antibodies were quantified using a high-throughput direct chemiluminescent ELISA performed on participant serum samples. IgG results were calibrated according to the World Health Organization (WHO) international reference standard and expressed in international binding antibody units (BAU) per mL. IgA and IgM results were expressed in micrograms (μg) per mL. ELISA is subject to a limited range of detectability often causing variability among assays. On account of this, serum, and plasma dilutions of 1:1000 of 1:100 were used to ensure that antibody titres fell within a quantifiable range thus providing accurate results72. A surrogate neutralization ELISA was performed to ascertain neutralizing capacity (%) for anti-S IgG. Anti-N IgG antibodies were used to identify incident SARS-CoV-2 infections for which a positive test result was not reported. Individuals positive for anti-N IgG in addition to either anti-S or anti-RBD were considered to have acquired immunity through natural infection. Vaccination status at time of serum sample collection was determined using self-reported COVID-19 vaccination dates, which were verified using medical records when available. Participants with hybrid immunity were identified according to date of vaccination (self-reported and verified) and infection (self-reported or identified by anti-N IgG titres). If an infection occurred within 14 days after vaccination, this infection was considered as prior to vaccination to account the 2-week delay in vaccine-elicited immunity73.

Statistics and reproducibility

The data had a non-normal and skewed distribution and was linearized through the log10-transformation. One unit was added to all unadjusted values to eliminate undefined values that would have otherwise been produced by log-transforming 0 values. Outliers and some skewing were still observed for the log-adjusted data. Two-sided non-parametric or parametric statistical tests with an alpha level of 0.05 were used where appropriate (as determined by histograms and Q-Q plots). Given the high number of models generated for different doses and antibody isotypes, statistical significance was interpreted conservatively.

To determine the impact of obesity on humoral immunity following COVID-19 vaccination, the slope of antibody production and decay after each vaccine dose was assessed by univariable and multivariable segmented Bayesian robust linear regression using the Student’s t-distribution for the outcome. Both absolute antibody titres and neutralizing capacity for anti-S IgG following SARS-CoV-2 vaccination were assessed. Baseline titres and the slopes of titre production and decay for obese participants were compared to overweight, normal weight, and underweight participants. A linear spline was placed at the titre peak to generate separate slope estimates for the regions of antibody production and decay and the x-axis was restricted to remove data from prior doses and exclude sparse data at extended follow-up. Random effects for intercepts (and slopes where sufficient data was available) were incorporated to address autocorrelation inherent to longitudinal data. The inclusion of an interaction term between obesity and time allowed slopes to vary according to obesity. Data was stratified by antibody isotype (IgG, IgM, IgA) and anti-S/RBD antibody specificity, vaccine dose number, SARS-CoV-2 infection history, and sex due to evidence in the literature indicating that the slope of antibody decay may vary by sex48,74. Sensitivity analyses exploring different BMI groupings were conducted with univariable models.

For some models which failed to converge with the Student’s t distribution, the “skew-normal” family was used75. Bayesian models used default non-informative priors, 4 chains and at least 7000 iterations. Model convergence and chain mixing was verified with Rhat (<1.05) and Bulk-ESS (>400). Statistical tests comparing demographics and vaccination and infection characteristics between obese and non-obese participants were conducted using Statistical Analysis System (SAS) version 9.4. RStudio version 2023.06.1 was used to complete regression analyses (package brms) and generate figures (package ggplot2).
