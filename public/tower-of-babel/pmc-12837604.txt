The preventive effect of emergency psychological intervention on post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in patients with acute spinal cord injury: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Ting Li, Hua Xu, Chenchen Jiang, Xiao Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAcute spinal cord injury (ASCI) often leads to severe disability and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), yet evidence on early psychological prevention during the emergency stage is limited.AimThis study aimed to evaluate whether emergency psychological intervention can effectively prevent PTSD in ASCI patients and improve rehabilitation outcomes, addressing a key gap in current trauma management research.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted among 191 ASCI patients admitted to the emergency department of the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University from June 2023 to December 2024. Patients were divided by injury severity (ISS ≥ 16 or ISS < 16) and exposure to emergency psychological intervention. Primary outcome was PTSD incidence; secondary outcomes included exercise compliance, muscle strength recovery, emergency stay time, readmission rate, and medical costs.ResultsThe incidence of PTSD was significantly lower in the severe intervention group than in controls (26.7% vs. 51.8%, P = 0.024). Exercise compliance and muscle strength recovery were higher in intervention groups, and average medical expenses per patient decreased by ¥3,953 in the severe group.ConclusionEarly emergency psychological intervention can effectively prevent PTSD and enhance rehabilitation outcomes in ASCI patients, while reducing healthcare costs and improving efficiency in emergency care. These findings support integrating a “physiological–psychological” management model into routine trauma practice.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Acute Spinal Cord Injury (ASCI) is a profoundly disabling condition, most commonly resulting from traumatic events such as traffic accidents and falls. It is associated not only with severe physical impairment but also with significant psychological trauma [1]. Globally, the annual incidence of ASCI ranges from 40 to 80 cases per million population, with a rising trend observed in recent years [2]. Beyond physical challenges such as limb paralysis and sensory loss, patients also exhibit a high incidence of Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD)—reported in 30% to 40% of cases—manifesting as recurrent flashbacks, hypervigilance, and emotional numbness. These psychological symptoms severely impede the rehabilitation process and diminish quality of life [3].

Current emergency management of ASCI primarily focuses on hemodynamic stabilization, surgical decompression, and prevention of secondary injury, yet psychological distress is often inadequately addressed. Research indicates that the emergency phase represents a critical window for the development of psychological trauma [4, 5]. Intense pain, unfamiliar environments, and anxiety about long-term outcomes can exacerbate stress responses, potentially triggering PTSD. However, most existing studies have concentrated on the relationship between physiological markers—such as inflammatory factors and coagulation profiles—and neurological outcomes, with limited systematic investigation into preventive psychological strategies. Although some clinical settings have begun incorporating psychological support, such efforts remain poorly standardized and lack stratified intervention protocols tailored to ASCI patients [6].

In recent years, psychological interventions have gained increasing recognition in trauma care. Modalities such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) [7] and crisis intervention [8] have demonstrated efficacy in alleviating post-traumatic anxiety and improving treatment adherence. Nevertheless, most studies on spinal cord injury have focused on psychosocial sequelae and PTSD prevalence during rehabilitation, rather than on early prevention. Established interventions—including prolonged exposure therapy and psychological first aid—typically target patients who have already developed PTSD symptoms during later stages of recovery. Their preventive utility during the emergency phase remains unclear [9, 10].

Furthermore, evidence from broader trauma populations suggests that the initial hours post-injury constitute a critical period for psychological adaptation—a phase that remains understudied in ASCI. Thus, a significant knowledge gap exists regarding whether early psychological intervention in the emergency setting can mitigate PTSD onset and enhance rehabilitation outcomes in this population.

Given the novelty of the emergency psychological intervention program at our institution, along with ethical and logistical constraints that precluded randomization, a retrospective cohort design was adopted. This approach enabled a comparison between patients who received emergency psychological support and those who did not under real-world clinical conditions, providing ecologically valid preliminary evidence to inform future prospective and randomized trials.

This study therefore aims to address a critical gap in the current literature by evaluating whether early psychological intervention during the emergency phase can effectively prevent PTSD and improve rehabilitation outcomes in patients with ASCI. While psychological outcomes following spinal cord injury have been extensively studied in rehabilitation contexts, few investigations have examined preventive psychological care in the emergency setting. Through retrospective analysis, we assessed the impact of emergency psychological intervention on PTSD incidence and functional recovery. Additionally, we explored the feasibility of an integrated “physiological–psychological” management model that links acute medical care with psychological support—offering a novel approach to trauma care. In addition to core clinical outcomes such as PTSD incidence, this study incorporates health economic indicators including medical costs and emergency department length of stay, thereby contributing empirical evidence to support optimized resource allocation in emergency settings.

Clinical data

Participants

A retrospective analysis was conducted on ACSI patients admitted to the emergency department of the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University from June 2023 to December 2024 as the research subjects. According to their injury severity score (ISS) [11], they were divided into the severe group (ISS ≥ 16 points, n = 86) and the mild group (ISS < 16 points, n = 105). Of the enrolled patients, those in the severe group met the criterion of an Injury Severity Score (ISS) ≥ 16 plus at least one of the following conditions: (1) Severity of spinal cord injury, classified by the American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) as Grade A (complete injury) or Grade B (incomplete injury with sensory but no motor function preserved); (2) Multi-system trauma, defined as associated injuries to the chest, abdomen, or pelvis with an Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) score ≥ 3 in at least one of these regions; or (3) Risk of complications, including respiratory failure (PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio < 300), neurogenic shock, or requirement of mechanical ventilation upon emergency admission.

Patients in the mild group were defined as having an ISS < 16 and meeting all the following criteria: (1) Spinal cord injury classified as ASIA Grade C (incomplete injury with partial motor function preserved) or Grade D (incomplete injury with muscle strength ≥ Grade 3); (2) Single-system injury, primarily spinal, with AIS scores ≤ 2 in other body regions; and (3) Hemodynamic stability, with no requirement for advanced life support and absence of major complications.

Within each severity stratum, patients were further categorized based on receipt of emergency psychological intervention: Intervention Group 1 (severe patients who received intervention), Control Group 1 (severe patients who did not receive intervention), Intervention Group 2 (mild patients who received intervention), and Control Group 2 (mild patients who did not receive intervention). Detailed baseline characteristics of each group are presented in Table 1. No statistically significant differences were observed across groups in terms of demographic data, injury mechanisms, associated injuries, vital signs, or laboratory parameters (all P > 0.05). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to its retrospective nature and the use of fully anonymized data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee to ensure privacy protection.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) admission to our hospital’s emergency department due to traumatic spinal cord injury; (2) age between 18 and 65 years, regardless of gender; (3) compliance with national emergency triage standards; (4) presentation to the emergency room within 24 h post-injury; and (5) conscious state and readiness to receive psychological care.

Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) documented history of psychiatric disorders (including prior diagnosis of depression, anxiety disorders, or post-traumatic stress disorder); (2) coma, impaired consciousness, or communication barriers; (3) non-traumatic spinal cord injury (e.g., due to spinal tumors, vascular malformations, or infectious diseases); (4) pregnancy or lactation; or (5) concomitant life-threatening injuries, including severe traumatic brain injury (AIS score ≥ 4) or multiple organ failure (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment [SOFA] score ≥ 8) [12, 13].

Ethical approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University (Approval No.: XYFY2024-KL479-01; Date of Approval: October 18, 2024). Owing to the retrospective design and the use of fully anonymized patient data, the requirement for informed consent was waived to ensure privacy protection.

Research design and grouping basis

This study was a retrospective cohort study. All data were derived from the hospital’s electronic medical record system and follow-up records. Patients were not randomly assigned to receive emergency psychological intervention. Instead, allocation to the intervention or control group was determined by the availability of the psychological intervention team, the clinical urgency of the case and the patient’s willingness to participate. Since our hospital has gradually launched the emergency psychological intervention project since June 2023, some patients have not received psychological intervention for the following reasons: (1) The psychological intervention team was insufficient in the early stage of the project to cover all patients; (2) A very small number of patients refused to participate due to personal will or communication barriers; (3) For some critically ill patients, psychological intervention could not be carried out temporarily due to the priority treatment of physical complications.

Method

Control groups (Control group 1 & 2)

Patients in Control Group 1 (severe) and Control Group 2 (mild) received standard emergency and subsequent care. This included routine psychological guidance utilizing standardized scripts for basic emotional support during daily rounds, passive distribution of health education materials, and non-proactive telephone follow-ups after discharge, without implementing active psychological intervention strategies. Additional components comprised complication prevention and scheduled post-discharge follow-ups.

Intervention groups (Intervention group 1 & 2)

In addition to standard care, Intervention Group 1 (severe) and Intervention Group 2 (mild) received structured psychological interventions initiated upon arrival in the emergency resuscitation room. The specific protocol included the following components:

Multidisciplinary intervention team establishment

Multidisciplinary intervention team establishment

Multidisciplinary intervention team establishment

A specialized emergency psychological intervention team was established, consisting of one attending spinal surgeon, one licensed psychologist (holding a Level-2 psychological counseling certificate), one specialty department head nurse, and three additional senior specialty nurses. All members completed standardized training in supportive psychological intervention protocols and demonstrated proficiency in procedural steps and key intervention elements.

(2)Comprehensive psychological assessment

(2)Comprehensive psychological assessment

Comprehensive psychological assessment

Upon admission, the intervention team conducted an initial psychological evaluation for each patient, establishing individualized psychological profiles. The Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) and Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) were administered at admission to both groups. Based on assessment results, personalized psychological intervention plans were formulated.

(3)Disease-specific psychoeducation

(3)Disease-specific psychoeducation

Disease-specific psychoeducation

For conscious patients, the team provided structured education about spinal cord injury using patient-friendly language, simultaneously engaging family members. This educational component aimed to enhance disease understanding and mitigate negative emotional responses.

(4)Structured listening and psychological counseling

(4)Structured listening and psychological counseling

Structured listening and psychological counseling

Recognizing the sudden onset of spinal cord injury and associated anxiety, the team conducted one-on-one sessions (30–60 min each) once patients’ vital signs stabilized. Through active listening, therapists identified patient needs, assessed psychological status, and encouraged expression of psychological stress responses. Patients were reassured about the normality of these reactions and received guidance in emotion and stress management techniques.

(5)Injury grade-specific psychological care

Injury grade-specific psychological care

ASIA Grade A (Complete Injury): Implemented crisis intervention centered on “VR Immersive Relaxation Therapy” to alleviate acute traumatic stress. Utilizing natural environment simulations (e.g., forests, beaches) combined with breathing guidance and mindfulness training, this approach aimed to redirect attention from physical pain and reduce anxiety. Collaborating with rehabilitation therapists, “Bedside Mirror Therapy” was implemented, using mirror reflections to stimulate the motor cortex while delivering psychological suggestion (e.g., “Your body is gradually regaining control”) to promote integrated recovery.ASIA Grades D-E (Near-Normal Function): Focused on preventive psychoeducation and long-term adaptation. The “AI Psychological Assistant for Spinal Cord Injury” delivered personalized rehabilitation knowledge and stress management skills (e.g., cognitive restructuring training) tailored to injury severity and personal characteristics. An “Emergency-Community Linkage Mechanism” connected patients with matched peer supporters through online platforms before discharge to reduce PTSD risk.

(6)Empowerment-based education

(6)Empowerment-based education

Empowerment-based education

With family participation, the team conducted psychological empowerment education covering stress disorder manifestations, consequences, contributing factors, and preventive strategies. Using theoretical frameworks of psychological empowerment, therapists identified specific patient issues, encouraged emotional expression, and developed individualized heart rate variability-informed intervention plans with continuous evaluation.

(7)Group psychoeducation

(7)Group psychoeducation

Group psychoeducation

Following clinical stabilization, patients were grouped by age, education level, and condition for stress disorder knowledge sessions (60 min each). Post-lecture, the team developed personalized psychological intervention guidance based on knowledge acquisition assessment.

(8)Predictive psychological support and follow-up

(8)Predictive psychological support and follow-up

Predictive psychological support and follow-up

The team provided anticipatory guidance regarding psychological stress responses, collaborating with families to develop coping strategies. At discharge, a dedicated WeChat group was established for proactive psychological support, including psychological relaxation training and mood regulation techniques to promote holistic recovery.

Definition of exercise compliance rate

During the inpatient rehabilitation period, patients were required to complete a daily personalized exercise plan developed by the rehabilitation department, comprising muscle strength training and joint range of motion exercises. Compliance was categorized as follows: complete compliance was defined as completion of ≥ 90% of the daily training volume; partial compliance as completion of 60%–89% of the daily training volume; and non-compliance as completion of less than 60% of the daily training volume.

Definition of excellent rate of muscle strength recovery

Muscle strength recovery at discharge was evaluated according to the ASIA Spinal Cord Injury Neurological Function Grading Standard, with emphasis on motor function of the upper and lower limbs. Recovery was graded as excellent (muscle strength ≥ grade 4, indicating ability to move against gravity and partial resistance), good (muscle strength grade 3, indicating movement against gravity but not resistance), or poor (muscle strength ≤ grade 2, indicating movement only in a gravity-eliminated plane). The excellent rate was calculated as the proportion of patients with excellent recovery relative to the total number of cases × 100%.

Secondary outcome measures

Secondary outcomes included length of stay in the emergency department (measured in hours), readmission rate, and total medical expenses.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 21.0 statistical software. The measurement data were tested for normality and homogeneity of variance and conformed to a normal distribution. Result

Baseline characteristics

No statistically significant differences were observed between Intervention Group 1 and Control Group 1 within the severe cohort regarding demographic characteristics such as gender, age, type of health insurance, marital status, or place of residence (all P > 0.05). Furthermore, comparisons of clinical and injury-related variables—including injury mechanisms (e.g., traffic accident, fall from height, other), patterns of associated injuries (involving organs, brain, or limbs), fracture types, severity of traumatic brain injury (as assessed by AIS score), treatment interventions, length of hospital stay, and functional status at discharge (based on ASIA score)—also showed no statistically significant differences (all P > 0.05). These findings indicate that the two groups were well-balanced in terms of baseline demographic, injury, treatment, and early rehabilitation characteristics. Comparison of clinical characteristics in the mild case group

In the mild case cohort, no statistically significant differences were observed between Intervention Group 2 and Control Group 2 regarding demographic characteristics such as gender, age, medical insurance type, marital status, or place of residence (all P > 0.05), indicating balanced baseline demographic profiles. Similarly, comparisons of injury-related and clinical management variables—including injury mechanisms (e.g., traffic accident, fall from height, other), distribution of concomitant injuries (involving organs, brain, or limbs), fracture types, severity of traumatic brain injury (as measured by AIS score), treatment approaches, duration of hospitalization, and functional status at discharge (assessed by ASIA score)—revealed no statistically significant intergroup differences (all P > 0.05). These findings suggest that the two groups were comparable in terms of injury patterns, treatment regimens, and rehabilitation status at baseline (Table 2).

Collectively, the results demonstrate that both in the severe and mild case groups, the intervention and control groups were well-matched in demographic characteristics, injury mechanisms, associated injuries, and laboratory indicators, confirming adequate baseline comparability across all study cohorts.

Comparison of PTSD incidence, exercise compliance, and muscle strength recovery

During the 3-month post-discharge follow-up, the incidence of PTSD in the severe intervention group (Intervention Group 1) was 26.67%, significantly lower than the 51.79% observed in the severe control group (Control Group 1, P = 0.024), indicating a substantial preventive effect of psychological intervention on PTSD development in severe cases. Furthermore, Intervention Group 1 demonstrated significantly higher rates of exercise compliance (76.67% vs. 48.21%, P = 0.011) and excellent/good muscle strength recovery (66.67% vs. 39.29%, P = 0.015) compared to Control Group 1, suggesting that psychological intervention indirectly enhances functional recovery by improving treatment adherence.

In the mild case cohort, the PTSD incidence in Intervention Group 2 (9.09%) was numerically lower than that in Control Group 2 (24.59%), though this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.152), potentially due to sample size limitations or inherently milder stress responses among mild-injury patients. Nevertheless, both exercise compliance (86.36% vs. 57.38%, P < 0.001) and the excellent rate of muscle strength recovery (65.91% vs. 49.18%, P < 0.001) remained significantly higher in Intervention Group 2 compared to its control counterpart, further supporting the broad rehabilitative benefits of psychological intervention across injury severity levels (Figs. 2 and 3).

Discussion

Spinal cord injury typically presents with severe clinical manifestations, frequently accompanied by limb paralysis and bladder dysfunction, substantially compromising patients’ quality of life [14]. The sudden and traumatic nature of such injuries often precipitates intense psychological distress, including fear, anxiety, and depression, which further exacerbates the overall disease burden [15, 16]. This study systematically evaluated the preventive efficacy of emergency psychological intervention against PTSD in patients with ASCI, and its role in promoting rehabilitation outcomes. Notably, we developed a novel tiered psychological intervention system based on neurological impairment classification, offering an innovative approach to psychological management in the emergency care of ASCI patients.

Our findings demonstrate that the incidence of PTSD was lower in both severe and mild injury groups receiving emergency psychological intervention compared to their respective controls. In the severe cohort, the PTSD incidence in Intervention Group 1 was 26.67%, significantly lower than the 51.79% observed in Control Group 1 (P = 0.024), indicating a substantial preventive effect of psychological intervention in critically affected patients. Although the mild intervention group (Intervention Group 2) also showed a numerically lower PTSD incidence (9.09%) compared to its control (24.59%), this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.152), potentially attributable to limited sample size or inherently milder psychological stress responses in less severely injured patients.

The more pronounced intervention effect in severe cases may be explained by several mechanisms. First, the ASIA impairment scale correlates with psychological stress intensity; patients with ASIA Grade A (complete injury) demonstrate significantly higher PTSD risk due to comprehensive motor function loss compared to Grades D-E, underscoring the necessity of intensified crisis intervention in severe cases. Second, the VR immersive relaxation therapy implemented in this study may reconstruct a sense of safety through multi-sensory stimulation and interfere with traumatic memory consolidation. Neuroimaging evidence confirms that VR interventions can reduce amygdala activation while enhancing functional connectivity between the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex [17]—mechanisms directly relevant to PTSD pathophysiology. Furthermore, the family–medical collaboration platform introduced in the severe group enhances patients’ perceived social support, a recognized protective factor against PTSD development [18].

This study provides novel clinical evidence to the emerging field of early psychological intervention following spinal cord injury. Compared with previous research, our work contributes to the subject area in several important aspects. First, while most existing SCI literature has focused on PTSD prevalence, risk factors, and psychosocial consequences [19, 20], few studies have evaluated structured psychological interventions in this population. Existing intervention trials primarily target established PTSD during rehabilitation using prolonged exposure or other trauma-focused psychotherapies [10, 21], rather than emphasizing primary prevention in the immediate post-injury phase. In contrast, our study evaluates a comprehensive emergency-department-based psychological intervention specifically designed for acute SCI patients, targeting PTSD prevention during the critical early window. Second, our research extends the broader early-intervention literature, where psychological first aid and other brief interventions in general trauma populations have shown mixed results in preventing PTSD diagnosis despite short-term distress reduction [9, 22]. By integrating early psychological support, graded goal-setting, and virtual reality–assisted relaxation within a severity-adjusted stepped program, our study provides disease-specific evidence for acute SCI—a population largely overlooked in previous prevention trials. Third, this study establishes connections between psychological outcomes and functional recovery alongside health service indicators—such as rehabilitation adherence and emergency care costs—which are rarely jointly analyzed in SCI psychosocial research or economic evaluations of PTSD interventions [19]. Collectively, these findings suggest that integrating structured emergency psychological care into routine acute SCI management may not only mitigate PTSD risk but also enhance rehabilitation engagement and optimize acute care resource utilization.

Consistent with findings in other trauma populations, our results confirm the transdiagnostic relevance of early psychological support. However, given the unique challenges faced by ASCI patients—including prolonged immobilization and extended rehabilitation—their psychological needs are particularly complex. Future intervention strategies should be individualized, accounting for injury severity, physiological status, and dynamic treatment environment factors to maximize efficacy across this heterogeneous patient population.

It is noteworthy that emergency psychological intervention significantly enhanced both exercise compliance and excellent muscle strength recovery rates across mild to severe spinal cord injury cases. This suggests that cognitive restructuring and empowerment education may promote more active patient engagement in therapeutic activities by reinforcing self-efficacy during rehabilitation [23]. Specifically, the visual progress feedback incorporated in the stepwise goal-oriented intervention may have activated the ventral striatal dopamine reward pathway—a mechanism consistent with findings by Sutor et al. regarding the neural basis of motor rehabilitation [24].

From a health economic perspective, the cost reduction associated with psychological intervention carries substantial policy relevance. In this study, the average medical cost per severe-case patient was reduced by approximately 3,953 yuan. Based on an estimated annual volume of 21,000 ASCI patients treated in tertiary hospitals across China, widespread implementation of this intervention could yield annual savings of around 83.01 million yuan in medical expenditures [25–27]. Additionally, the significantly shorter emergency department length of stay observed in both severity groups not only improves patient flow efficiency but also offers a practical strategy for alleviating emergency department overcrowding—a pressing issue in current clinical practice.

Several methodological limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the retrospective, single-center, non-randomized design precludes complete exclusion of selection bias or residual confounding. Allocation to psychological intervention was influenced by team availability, patient preference, and clinical urgency; thus, the intervention and control groups may have differed in unmeasured characteristics such as pre-injury mental health status, baseline PTSD symptoms, pain severity, social support, medication use (e.g., sedatives or analgesics), and rehabilitation intensity. Second, the relatively small sample size, particularly in the severe intervention group, may have limited statistical power to detect between-group differences in certain outcomes, such as PTSD incidence in the mild injury cohort. Third, PTSD assessment was conducted solely at 3 months using self-report scales, without longer-term follow-up or structured diagnostic interviews, which restricts conclusions regarding the durability of intervention effects. Future research should incorporate several methodological refinements. A prospective, multicenter randomized controlled trial with stratification by injury severity (e.g., ISS and ASIA grade) would help minimize selection bias. The inclusion of an active control group—matched for time and attention but devoid of specific psychological techniques—would help disentangle nonspecific support effects from intervention-specific benefits. Blinded outcome assessment and the use of clinician-administered PTSD interviews alongside self-report measures would further enhance validity. Analytically, adjusting for potential confounders such as baseline anxiety/depression, pain levels, medication use, and rehabilitation adherence via multivariable regression or propensity score methods would provide more robust causal inference regarding the effects of emergency psychological intervention on PTSD prevention and functional recovery after ASCI.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that early psychological intervention in the emergency setting can effectively reduce PTSD incidence and enhance functional recovery in patients with acute spinal cord injury. The intervention group exhibited significantly lower PTSD rates, higher exercise adherence, and improved muscle strength recovery relative to controls. Furthermore, the intervention was associated with reduced medical costs and shorter emergency department stays, underscoring its dual clinical and economic value. These findings affirm the utility of an integrated physiological–psychological management model in acute trauma care and support its broader implementation to improve patient outcomes and healthcare efficiency.
