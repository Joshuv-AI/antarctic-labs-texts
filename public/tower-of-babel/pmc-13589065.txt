Structural optimization and freeze-thaw stabilization of carnauba wax and beta-sitosterol bigels via peanut protein and sodium alginate complexation
Authors: Chun Hu, Chunyu Li, Weinong Zhang, Junbo He, Yanpeng Zhang, Lijuan Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the influence of peanut protein/sodium alginate (PP/SA) complex and oleogel-to-hydrogel ratio on the structure, mechanical properties and stability of carnauba wax/β-sitosterol-based bigels. The results indicated adding SA increased PP mean particle size from 88.1 to 395.2 and 729.6 nm, and absolute zeta-potential from 38.5 to 53.0 and 60.5 mV, and improved its surface hydrophobicity. Additionally, at 40% and 50% oleogel, SA addition was shown to improve the performance of PP bigels in water holding capacity, elastic modulus (G′), viscous modulus (G″), textural properties (hardness, springiness, chewiness and gumminess), as well as physical and freeze-thaw stability. Notably, the bigels with 50% oleogel exhibited the most compact structure and optimal functional properties. However, at 60% oleogel, the PP bigels declined in texture parameters, rheological properties and stability, making the positive effects of SA negligible. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for utilizing PP/SA bigels as potential spreadable sauce substitutes

Introduction

In recent years, bigels have attracted considerable attention as an emerging system due to their unique thermodynamic stability, viscoelasticity and controlled release capability (Zhu et al., 2021). Bigels are hybrid gel systems created through the combination or emulsification of hydrogels and oleogels (Han et al., 2025). Specifically, hydrogels consist of a continuous aqueous phase in a cross-linked network of natural or synthetic polymers (such as sodium alginate, gelatin and chitosan), exhibiting high water content and biocompatibility (Chao et al., 2024). In contrast, oleogels use oil as the continuous phase, in which gelling agents (such as beeswax, oryzanol and plant sterols) self-assemble to a three-dimensional network capable of immobilizing the liquid oil to inhibit flow through intermolecular forces such as hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces (Chao et al., 2024). By integrating the characteristics of both systems, bigels overcome the limitations of individual components and offer synergistic properties (Han et al., 2025). Consequently, these systems hold extensive application potential in fields of food, medicine and biomaterials.

In the past decade, significant attention has been centered on the fabrication and physicochemical characteristics of bigels. It is well established that the characteristics of bigels can be tailored by varying oleogel-to-hydrogel ratio, concentration of gelators and their intrinsic nature (Tian et al., 2025). For instance, Zhu et al. (2021) have found that in bigels based on monoglyceride-beeswax and high-acyl gellan gum, a higher oleogel content not only improved mechanical strength (storage modulus and firmness), but also enhanced thermal stability. In the study of Jiang, Chen, et al. (2024), they observed that in Chlorella pyrenoidosa protein (CPP)/xanthan gum-based systems, increasing the oleogel content could shift the microstructure from oleogel-in-hydrogel (O/W) to bicontinuous and finally W/O structure.

Additionally, the properties of hydrogel phase and the interactions between biopolymers play a pivotal role in modulating bigel performance. Goncalves et al. (2024) reported that increasing protein concentration in potato protein-based bigels improved hardness and storage modulus (G′), while altering the oleogel fraction modified droplet distribution and consistency. Moreover, Qiao et al. (2025) found sodium caseinate/sodium alginate complexes significantly increased the storage and loss modulus of gels, yielding composite systems with excellent resistance to pH and heat variations. Besides, whey protein isolate (WPI) single-network oleogels have been reported to display predominantly viscous behavior and shear-thinning characteristics, while WPI - sodium alginate composite oleogels exhibited plastic behavior, irreversible shear thinning, and enhanced oxidative and oil-holding stability (Li, Zheng, et al., 2025). Furthermore, compared with soy protein bigels, soy protein/beet pectin bigels at a 6:4 ratio exhibited optimal mechanical strength and better water-holding capacity (Teng et al., 2026). Despite these advances, commonly used proteins such as potato, whey or soy protein often present limitations in bigel applications, mainly due to their susceptibility to thermal aggregation during processing or their inherent strong beany flavors, which can compromise product quality. To address these challenges, peanut protein was preferred due to its unique thermal stability and mild flavor profile.

Aside from its thermal advantages, peanut protein (PP), as a widely utilized plant-based protein, is also favored for its high nutritional value, balanced amino acid profile, and excellent functional properties, including good solubility and emulsifying capacity (Yu & Eghbali, 2025). Sodium alginate (SA), a natural anionic polysaccharide, is renowned for its exceptional gelation and thickening capabilities, enabling the formation of robust three-dimensional networks in aqueous media. Consequently, it is frequently used to modulate rheological properties and stabilize emulsified systems (Zhang et al., 2020). The incorporation of SA into peanut protein systems has been shown to significantly enhance gelling strength and emulsifying stability through synergistic interactions, while also imparting a desirable thickening effect (Yang et al., 2024). Additionally, β-sitosterol, as a natural plant sterol, has a unique molecular structure and hydroxyl groups, enabling it to self-assemble with fatty acid esters to form a stable three-dimensional network, thereby enhancing the mechanical properties and thermal stability of gels (Yang et al., 2018). Carnauba wax, a natural plant wax composed of fatty acid esters and fatty alcohols, possesses excellent surface activity and lipophilicity, thus enabling it to work in synergy with β-sitosterol to construct a stable and high-performance complex oil gel network (Habibzadeh et al., 2026). Based on the complementary functions of the two in their respective systems, the use of PP/SA and carnauba wax/β-sitosterol as hydrogel and oil gel agents respectively can not only achieve efficient synergy and structural integration of water and oil phases, but also endow the composite gel with excellent mechanical properties and diverse application potentials. In recent years, protein-polysaccharide composite bigels constructed with plant-based component have received increasing attention for the development of healthy fat-based food products (Chao et al., 2024). While a number of recent studies have explored the basic formulation of protein-polysaccharide bigels for general food applications, few have targeted their structure-function matching to the specific requirements of spreadable sauce products. In particular, the approach of tuning the mechanical properties of bigel systems through structural design of PP/SA hydrogels and oleogels has not been systematically investigated. For spreadable condiments, well-defined viscoelasticity, appropriate mechanical strength and uniform texture are critical to meet the demands of practical application (Esmaeili et al., 2025), which are still rarely addressed in existing bigel research.

Building on the well-documented interfacial stabilization effect of protein-polysaccharide complexes, we hypothesize that incorporating PP/SA complexes can simultaneously reinforce the hydrogel network and stabilize the oil-water interface of bigels, overcoming the common limitations of conventional bigels including weak mechanical strength and poor interfacial adhesion between oil and water phases. Additionally, we hypothesize that tuning the oleogel-to-hydrogel ratio allows targeted regulation of bigel viscoelasticity and freeze-thaw stability, enabling the plant-based bigel system to meet the practical application requirements of spreadable condiments.

To test this hypothesis, against this background, this study aimed to clarify the influence mechanism of peanut protein/sodium alginate (PP/SA) complex and oleogel-to-hydrogel ratio on the structure, mechanical properties and stability of bigels. Specifically, the impacts of SA concentration on the structural characteristics of PP/SA complex were first analyzed in terms of particle size, zeta-potential, surface hydrophobicity, Fourier transform infrared spectra (FTIR) and X-ray diffraction (XRD). Subsequently, the effects of PP/SA complex and oleogel-to-hydrogel ratio on bigel structure were evaluated by microstructure observation, FTIR, XRD and thermodynamic analysis. Finally, the functional properties of bigels were investigated through rheological properties, texture and freeze-thaw stability. The results demonstrated that the incorporation of PP/SA complex acted as an interfacial stabilizer and hydrophase reinforcer, effectively overcoming the limitations of weak interfacial adhesion and inferior mechanical strength typical of conventional bigels. Furthermore, the study systematically elucidated the regulatory effect of the PP/SA complex and oleogel-to-hydrogel ratio on the microstructure and freeze-thaw stability of the system. These findings provide useful information for developing new strategies of creating potential spreadable sauce substitutes, particularly targeting products such as mayonnaise and other soft condiments.

Materials and methods

Materials

In this study, the materials used were phytosterols (purity ≥95%, Xian Minglang Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China), carnauba wax (purity ≥99%, Shanghai Lexiang Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China), Golden dragon sunflower oil (Yihai Kerry Grain and Oil Industry Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China), sodium alginate (Henan Shuangchen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China), Nile Red (purity ≥99%, Shanghai Maclean Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China), Fluorescein isothiocyanate (purity ≥99%, Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), ethanol, potassium bromide (KBr), 8-aniline-1-naphthalene sulfonic acid (ANS), and sodium hydroxide of analytical grade (Sinopharm Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Preparation of PP/SA complexes

Peanut protein (PP) (1 g) was dissolved in 100 mL of distilled water and stirred at 600 rpm for 4 h. Subsequently, PP solution was mixed with sodium alginate (SA) (0.4 and 0.8 g) and stirred at 600 rpm for 2 h to prepare PP/SA complexes at the mass ratio of 1:0.4 and 1:0.8, respectively, based on the method of He et al. (2024). Finally, all solutions (SA, PP and PP/SA complexes) were adjusted to pH 7, and then stored at 4 °C for further analysis.

Preparation of oleogels

The oleogels were prepared as described by Yang et al. (2018) with a small adjustment. Briefly, sunflower oil was weighed and a gelator mixture of phytosterol and carnauba wax (at a mass ratio of 1:4) was incorporated at a total concentration of 8 wt% (based on the total oleogel weight). Next, the resulting mixture was heated to 90 °C under continuous stirring for 30 min to ensure complete melting of gelling agents. Finally, the samples were kept at 35 °C in a thermostatically controlled water bath.

Preparation of bigels

The hydrogel phase (SA, PP and PP/SA complexes) and the oleogel phase were kept separately at 35 °C in a water bath. Subsequently, the oleogel was blended with the hydrogel with a constant total sample mass of 40 g at the oleogel-to-hydrogel mass ratio of 40:60 (BG40), 50:50 (BG50) and 60:40 (BG60), respectively. Next, the blends were homogenized at 12,000 rpm for 3 min using a high-speed homogenizer to ensure uniform dispersion. Finally, all the samples were stored overnight at 4 °C to facilitate the formation of stable bigels.

Characterization of PP/SA complexes

Microstructure observation of PP/SA complexes

The surface structures of SA, PP and PP/SA complexes were observed following the method of Li, Hu, Zhang, Han, et al. (2025) with small adjustments. Briefly, the samples were freeze-dried and fixed on copper stubs using conductive tape. Subsequently, the samples were coated with a thin layer of gold using a sputter coater. Finally, the microstructures of all samples were observed using a scanning electron microscope (Tescan Mira4, Tescan, China) at 20 kV acceleration voltage, with images acquired at 500×.

Particle size and zeta-potential measurement of PP/SA complexes

The particle size and zeta-potential of SA, PP and PP/SA complexes were measured using a Malvern Nano Laser particle size analyzer (Malvern Instruments, UK) as reported by Li, Hu, Zhang, Han, et al. (2025). Each sample was diluted to 0.1 mg/mL with distilled water (pH 7). The refractive index and absorption of the dispersed material were set to 1.45 and 0.001, respectively. Each sample was independently determined three times. The zeta-potential (ζ) was computed by the formula:UE=2εζfka3ηwhere, UE is the electrophoretic mobility (m2.v−1.s−1); ε, the dielectric constant of solvent (F/m); f(ka), Henry constant; η, medium viscosity (Pa.s).

Surface hydrophobicity determination of PP/SA complexes

The surface hydrophobicity of SA, PP and PP/SA complexes was determined by a fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-4600, Hitachi, Japan) using the method of Li et al. (2019) with a minor adjustment. Briefly, SA, PP and PP/SA complex solutions were prepared with deionized water in the 0.05–0.25 mg/mL concentration range and an aliquot of 20 μL of ANS (8 mM) was added to 4 mL of each sample. The fluorescence intensity was determined at excitation and emission wavelengths of 390 nm and 470 nm, respectively. The surface hydrophobicity (H0) was computed based on the initial slope of the fluorescence intensity curve plotted against the sample concentration.

FTIR measurement of PP/SA complexes

The FTIR of SA, PP and PP/SA complexes were determined by a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Nexus 670, Nicolet Instruments, USA) using the method of Hu and Xiong (2022). Briefly, all sample solutions were adjusted to pH 7 before freeze-drying for 48 h. Next, each freeze-dried sample was mixed with KBr and pressed into transparent flakes. Finally, the spectra were obtained by scanning the samples 32 times at 4 cm−1 resolution in the 4000–400 cm−1 wavelength range.

XRD determination of PP/SA complexes

The crystalline structures of SA, PP and PP/SA complexes were determined by an X-ray diffractometer (Empyrean, PANalytical, Netherlands) with the method of Hu and Xiong (2022). Briefly, each frozen-dried sample was placed on a sample holder and leveled to obtain a flat surface. XRD patterns were recorded at 25 °C using Cu Kα radiation at 40 kV operating voltage and 40 mA current. The scanning range (2θ) was set from 5° to 60° with the 0.168°/s scanning speed in a continuous scanning mode.

Micromorphology observation of bigels

The micromorphology of bigels was observed by a Laser confocal scanning microscope (FV1200, Japan) as reported by Liu et al. (2025). Briefly, the prepared bigels were mixed with Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) (0.1% w/v) for the aqueous phase and Nile Red (0.01% w/v) for the oil phase. Subsequently, the stained sample was placed on a slide and gently covered with a coverslip. Finally, observations were performed at 20× magnification using 488 nm and 543 nm excitation wavelength for FITC and Nile Red, respectively.

Structure of bigels

Attenuated total reflectance (ATR) - FTIR determination of bigels

The changes in functional groups of the bigels were determined using an FTIR spectrometer (Nexus 670, Nicolet Instruments, USA) in an ATR mode, as previously described (Kaimal & Singhal, 2023). A small quantity of sample was placed on the ATR crystal surface, with air serving as the background. The scanning range was 4000–400 cm−1, with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and 64 scans per spectrum.

XRD pattern measurement of bigels

The XRD patterns of bigels were measured by an X-ray diffractometer (Empyrean, PANalytical, Netherlands) as outlined by Xie et al. (2023) with a small adjustment. The sample was loaded on a sample holder and maintained at 25 °C during the measurement. Diffraction pattern was recorded in a scanning angle (2θ) range of 5–60° at 0.168°/s scanning speed, 40 kV operating voltage and 40 mA current.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) determination of bigels

The thermodynamic properties of bigels were determined by a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC Q2000, USA) with the method of Goncalves et al. (2024). The samples (5–10 mg) were accurately weighed in aluminum pans and hermetically sealed. DSC was performed as follows: heating from 25 to 90 °C at 5 °C/min, followed by cooling to 25 °C at the same rate, under a nitrogen flow of 20 mL/min. The peak temperature (Tp) and enthalpy of melting (ΔH) were analyzed using TA universal analysis software.

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance and relaxation time of bigels

The Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) imaging and relaxation time of bigels were observed by a magnetic resonance imaging analyzer (NMI20–040 V-1, China) as described by Liu et al. (2025). Bigel samples were transferred to a 40 mm diameter glass tube and placed in the NMR probe for scanning.

Imaging was conducted using a Spin Echo (SE) pulse sequence with field of view (FOV) = 50 mm × 50 mm, average = 8, slice = 3, and slice thickness = 3 mm. The resulting images were pseudo-colored using MRI image processing software.

Subsequently, the transverse relaxation time (T2) was measured using a Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill (CPMG) pulse sequence at 20 MHz resonance frequency. The acquisition parameters were set as repetition time (TR) = 2000 ms, echo time (TE) = 0.35 ms, echoe number = 12,000, and scan number = 8.

Rheological properties measurement of bigels

The rheological properties of bigels were measured by a multifunctional rheometer (Kinexus Pro+, UK) with a 40 mm aluminum plate as previously described (Kaimal and Singhal, 2023, Li et al., 2025). First, a strain sweep was performed to determine the linear viscoelastic region (LVR) in the 0.01–100% strain range at 1 Hz and 25 °C, with a gap of 1 mm. During this test, the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were recorded as a function of strain. Subsequently, a frequency sweep was performed from 0.1 to 100 Hz at a constant strain of 0.05% to monitor the changes in G′ and G″. Finally, the apparent viscosity was measured as a function of shear rate in the 0.1–100 s−1 range.

Texture characteristics determination of bigels

The textural properties of bigels, including hardness, springiness, chewiness, gumminess and cohesiveness, were measured using a texture analyzer (TMS-Pro, FTC Instruments, USA) as previously reported (Kaimal & Singhal, 2023) with a small adjustment. The measurement was performed using a P10 gel probe with pre-test, test, and post-test speed set to 1, 1, and 5 mm/s, respectively. Samples were compressed to 50% deformation with a trigger force of 5 g.

Stability measurement of bigels

Physical stability determination of bigels

The bigel stability was measured by the Turbiscan Tower stability analyzer (Turbiscan Lab, France) using the method of He et al. (2024) with minor modifications. The samples were loaded in a glass cylindrical measurement cell and placed in the instrument. Measurements were performed in multi-scan mode at 25 °C for 1 h.

The freeze-thaw stability measurement of bigels

The bigel freeze-thaw stability was measured using the method described by Yang et al. (2022) with a small adjustment. Specifically, 5 g of each bigel sample was subjected to a freeze-thaw cycle consisting of freezing at −20 °C for 22 h followed by thawing at 30 °C for 2 h. After thawing, the samples were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min to separate the released oil. The supernatant (released oil) was collected, and the oil-holding capacity (OHC) was determined. This procedure was repeated for three consecutive cycles. The OHC was calculated by the formula:OHC%=1−m1−m2m1−m0×oil content%×100%where, m0 is the mass of empty centrifuge tube; m1, the total mass of sample and centrifuge tube before centrifugation; m2, the mass of the oil separated after centrifugation.

Statistical analysis

The experiment data were analyzed with SPSS 17.0 by performing a one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan's multiple comparison test. Each experiment was conducted three times using three independently prepared samples and the data were expressed as average value ± standard deviation (n = 3). Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Particle size and zeta-potential analysis of PP/SA complexes

The average particle diameter and zeta-potential of SA, PP and PP/SA complexes are presented in Fig. 1B, with an average grain size of 883.50 nm for SA, while a significantly smaller size of 88.10 nm for PP. After adding SA to PP, the mean particle size of the PP/SA complexes significantly increased to 395.17 nm and 729.57 nm for 0.4% and 0.8%SA/PP complexes, respectively (p < 0.05). This was likely attributed to the adsorption of SA onto the surface of PP, acting as a bridge to connect small PP particles into larger aggregates (Li, Hu, Zhang, Han, et al., 2025). Meanwhile, the absolute zeta-potential was 72.50 and 38.50 mV for SA and PP, respectively. After adding 0.4% and 0.8% SA to PP solution, the absolute zeta-potential significantly increased to 53.00 and 60.53 mV for the two PP/SA complexes, respectively (p < 0.05). The result indicated that the addition of SA significantly improved the colloidal stability of PP/SA complex systems. This was likely because highly negatively charged SA molecules adsorbed onto the surface of PP by hydrogen bonding, increasing the surface negative charge density of the complexes, enhancing electrostatic repulsion between PP particles, and thus improving the system stability (Dong et al., 2021).

Surface hydrophobicity analysis of PP/SA complexes

Fig. 1C presents the surface hydrophobicity of SA, PP and PP/SA complexes, with a significantly different (p < 0.05) value of 0.55 and 13.41 for SA and PP, respectively. After adding 0.4% SA to PP, the surface hydrophobicity value increased to 13.67 for the PP/SA complex. This was probably attributed to the combination of SA and PP, where SA induced the rearrangement of the PP molecular structure, resulting in the exposure of more hydrophobic regions (Li, Hu, Zhang, Han, et al., 2025). When the SA amount increased to 0.8%, the surface hydrophobicity value dropped from 13.67 to 10.36. A possible explanation is that the entanglement and bridging effects between SA chains have become the dominant forces, which can pull together the particles covered by SA on the hydrophilic surface to form larger aggregates (He et al., 2024). This result can be supported by observation of particle size (Fig. 1B).

The XRD patterns of SA, PP and PP/SA complexes are shown in Fig. 1H, where SA is seen to have a relatively wide peak at 22.1°, indicating its semi-crystalline nature with low crystallinity and a significant proportion of amorphous regions (Xun et al., 2023). Meanwhile, PP showed a relatively wide peak at 21.0°, suggesting its semi-crystalline or amorphous structural characteristics, typically associated with the presence of disordered polypeptide chains. This broad diffraction pattern was common in plant-based proteins, resulting from heterogeneous composition, flexible chain conformation, and the influence of hydration or processing procedure, collectively contributing to reduced crystallinity (Li, Hu, Zhang, He, et al., 2025). Additionally, when adding 0.4% and 0.8% SA to PP, the PP/SA complexes exhibited a relatively wide peaks at 21.3° and 20.6°, respectively, bearing a strong resemblance to the diffraction pattern of SA. The results suggested that SA incorporation can significantly influence the PP structure. This was likely attributed to the enhancement of PP-SA molecular interactions (such as hydrogen bonding or electrostatic forces), disrupting the native ordering of PP while reinforcing the SA-dominated amorphous character (Xun et al., 2023).

Microstructure analysis of bigels

To gain further insight into the phase behavior of bigels, their microstructures were observed using a laser confocal scanning microscope. Fig. 2 presents the impacts of PP/SA complex and oleogel-to-hydrogel ratio on the morphological structure of bigels, with red for the oleogel, green for the hydrogel, and a mixed color for the bigel in the microscopy image. As shown in Fig. 2A–C, for bigels (BG) at 40% oleogel, the oleogels dispersed as irregular droplets in a continuous hydrogel matrix (SA, PP and PP/SA complexes), forming oil-in-water (O/W) bigels. Meanwhile, the oleogel in PP BG40 exhibited a network structure, with oleogel more uniformly dispersed in a continuous hydrogel. A possible explanation is that the PP component had altered the interface properties of oleogel, causing it not only to exist as individual droplets in the system, but also to form a connected network (Wang, Li, et al., 2024). When the oleogel proportion was raised to 50% (Fig. 2D–F), the oleogel transformed from irregular droplets to a continuous phase (red area), forming bi-continuous bigels. This was probably because 50% oleogel fraction induced a phase inversion, driving droplet coalescence into a continuous network and forming a bi-continuous structure (Qiu et al., 2024). With the oleogel proportion further increased to 60% (Fig. 2G–I), the irregular hydrogel droplets (SA, PP and PP/SA complexes) dispersed in oleogels, forming water-in-oil (W/O) bigels. This was attributed to phase inversion caused by the dominance of the oleogel phase, encapsulating the hydrogel as a dispersed phase (Qiu et al., 2024). Structure analysis of bigels

ATR-FTIR analysis of bigels

FTIR can be used to identify the specific functional groups and analyze their interactions in the gel samples (Liu et al., 2025). The impacts of PP/SA complex and oleogel-to-hydrogel ratio on FTIR of bigels are shown in Fig. 3A–C. No new peaks were observed in the bigel samples with varying fractions of oleogel (40%, 50% and 60%), suggesting that the combination of SA, PP, PP/SA complexes and oleogel was purely physical without any chemical interactions (Xie et al., 2023). At 40% oleogel, the absorption bands observed at 2928.8 and 2857.4 cm−1 represent the antisymmetric and symmetric CH₂ stretching vibrations in SA BG40, while the characteristic peaks at 1741.1, 1465.2 and 724.8 cm−1 were ascribed to the CO stretching vibration, C—H bending of CH₃, and (CH₂)n bending of the alkyl chain (Xie et al., 2023). Meanwhile, SA, PP, 0.4% and 0.8%SA/PP BG40 exhibited no significant differences in FTIR, suggesting that no chemical reaction occurred between SA and PP. As the oleogel ratio increased from 40% to 50% and 60%, the absorption bands of symmetric and antisymmetric CH2 stretching vibrations shifted to 2924.4 cm−1, 2855.2 cm−1 and 2845.1 cm−1, respectively. This may be attributed to the denser structure of gel network and enhanced intermolecular interactions (Liu et al., 2025). XRD analysis of bigels

XRD was used to characterize the crystal structure of bigels to better understand their formation mechanism. The effects of PP/SA complex and oleogel-to-hydrogel ratio on the crystal structure of bigels are shown in Fig. 3D–F. At 40% oleogel, all the bigel samples showed a broad peak at 19.5°, indicating the presence of liquid triglycerides due to physical immobilization of liquid oil in the gel matrix (Zheng et al., 2020). Meanwhile, all the bigel samples exhibited two peaks at 21.5° and 23.9° in the diffractograms, corresponding to the spacings of 4.13 and 3.72 Å, respectively, according to the Bragg's law (2d sinθ = nλ), suggesting the presence of well-ordered crystalline structure from the saturated fatty acids. These sharp diffraction peaks confirmed the successful crystallization of the oleogelator, which acted as the structural backbone to immobilize the liquid oil (Liu et al., 2025). Meanwhile, regardless of the increase of oleogel content and changes in hydrogel components, the crystalline peak position in the diffraction pattern showed no great change. This result suggested that the oleogel crystalline structure was primarily determined by its internal gelator and remained highly independent, confirming that the system was a physical blend without strong chemical interactions between the two phases. Additionally, with the increase of oleogel content from 40% to 50% and 60%, the peak intensity of bigels increased at 19.50°, 21.5° and 23.9°, respectively. This may be attributed to the rise in crystalline fat concentration following the increase of oleogel fraction, leading to a greater number of ordered structures and intensified diffraction peaks (Xie et al., 2023).

Thermodynamic properties analysis of bigels

DSC was used to investigate the melting behavior of bigels by measuring the heat flow during temperature variations. The thermodynamic properties of bigels under the influence of PP/SA complex and oleogel-to-hydrogel ratio are shown in Fig. 3G–I. Specifically, SA BG40 exhibited a melting peak at 77.5 °C with an enthalpy of 1.395 J/g, while PP BG40 showed a melting peak at 79.4 °C with an enthalpy of 1.331 J/g. Adding 0.4% and 0.8% SA to PP led to no great change in the melting peak of the PP/SA BG40 samples, but the enthalpy decreased from 1.331 to 1.254 and 0.9658 J/g, respectively (Fig. 3G). This decrease might be induced by SA addition, which increased the system viscosity and created spatial steric hindrance at the oil-water interface, hindering the diffusion, arrangement, and lattice growth of gel agent molecules (β-sitosterol and carnauba wax) during cooling, reducing the number of formed crystals and thus a decrease in the total heat released during melting (Zhu et al., 2021). Additionally, with oleogel increased from 40% to 50%, the melting temperature showed no great change in SA and PP BG50 samples and their enthalpies showed a slight increase relative to BG40 (Fig. 3H). A possible explanation is that at 50% oleogel, the crystal structure in the bigels became more compact or orderly (Zhu et al., 2021). Adding 0.4% and 0.8% SA to PP caused no great change in the melting temperature and enthalpy of PP BG50. This indicated that SA merely constructed a physical framework, but did not fundamentally activate the crystallization core of the oil. When the oleogel content further increased to 60%, the melting temperature of PP BG sample decreased from 79.0 to 77.2 °C (Fig. 3H and I). This indicated that the network structure formed between PP and oleogel was unstable at a high oleogel content. Moreover, when adding 0.4% and 0.8% SA to PP, the melting temperature of PP BG60 decreased from 77.2 to 76.8 and 76.4 °C, while the enthalpy value increased from 1.985 to 2.684 and 2.345 J/g (Fig. 3I). This change was probably attributed to the high-oil-content PP BG60 system, where SA acted as a crucial strengthening agent, slightly lowering the melting point, significantly enhancing the intermolecular forces to increase enthalpy, thereby improving the structural strength of bigels (Jiang, Chen, et al., 2024).

LF-NMR imaging and moisture distribution analysis of bigels

Fig. 4A presents the influence of PP/SA complex and oleogel-to-hydrogel ratio on the LF-NMR imaging in bigels. SA and PP BG40 samples exhibited little red color, suggesting a low water content in the bigels. When adding 0.4% and 0.8% SA to PP, the red color intensity increased significantly in the corresponding PP/SA BG40 samples (Fig. 4A-c and d), suggesting that SA introduction significantly enhanced the WHC of the bigel system. Fig. 4B shows the influence of PP/SA complex and oleogel-to-hydrogel ratio on the transverse relaxation time (T2) in bigels. It is generally believed that the relaxation time of bound water (T21) ranges from 0 to 10 ms, immobile water (T22) from 10 to 600 ms, and free water (T23) exceeding 600 ms. As presented in Fig. 4B-a, SA BG40 showed three distinct peaks at 1.32, 151.99 and 1417.47 ms in the relaxation spectrum. Similarly, PP BG40 displayed three peaks at 1.15, 147.74 and 1417.47 ms in the relaxation spectrum. Upon the addition of 0.4% and 0.8% SA to PP, the 0.4% and 0.8%SA/PP BG40 samples displayed three distinct peaks at 1.52, 151.99, 1232.85 ms, and 1.00, 151.99, 1232.85 ms in the relaxation spectra, respectively. The T23 peak shift from 1417.47 to 1232.85 ms upon SA addition indicated a reduction in the mobility of free water in PP BG40. This result can be ascribed to the strong hydrophilicity of SA, which introduced numerous hydrophilic groups (hydroxyl and carboxyl) to bind water molecules by hydrogen bonds (Liu et al., 2021).

Additionally, with oleogel content raised from 40% to 50%, the SA, PP, 0.4% and 0.8% SA/PP BG50 samples showed three distinct peaks at 1.15, 86.98, 1232.85 ms, 1.32, 132.19, 1232.85 ms, 1.52, 114.98, 1072.27 ms, and 1.32, 114.98, 932.60 ms in the relaxation spectra. Meanwhile, the T22 relaxation time of all BG40 samples showed a decrease with oleogel content increased from 40% to 50% and 60%, suggesting a significant alteration in the microstructure of bigels. One possible explanation is that the higher volume of hydrophobic oleogel likely occupied the pores in the hydrogel network, exerting a squeezing effect on the water phases, resulting in a more crowded environment that physically restricted molecular mobility (Xie et al., 2023). Moreover, with oleogel content increased from 40% to 50% and 60%, all BG samples showed a significantly increase in the peak area proportion of T22, while a significantly decrease of T23, suggesting the migration of water from a free state to an immobilized state. This was possibly attributed to the filling effect of oleogel, which occupied free volume and restricted water mobility, and the increase of oil content also enlarged the interfacial area, promoting water binding to biopolymers and network, thereby enhancing water-holding capacity (Jiang, Chen, et al., 2024).

Rheological properties analysis of bigels

The viscoelastic properties of bigels were evaluated by oscillatory rheological tests (amplitude sweep and frequency sweep). The amplitude stress sweep was conducted to estimate the linear-viscoelastic region (LVR) of bigels (Fig. 5A–C). At the shear strain below 0.15%, the elastic modulus (G′) and viscous modulus (G″) of all BG40 samples remained stable. At the shear strain above 0.15%, both G′ and G″ decreased rapidly in PP and 0.4%SA/PP BG40 samples, but decreased slowly in SA and 0.8%SA/PP BG40 samples. These results suggested that pure PP BG40 was structurally fragile and susceptible to external forces, whereas SA incorporation (particularly at 0.8% high concentration) significantly reinforced the network, thereby enhancing its shear resistance and structural stability (Ye et al., 2025). Besides, with the oleogel content increased from 40% to 50% and 60%, the LVR region reduced in all bigels and their G′ and G″ increased. This result suggested that increasing the oleogel content can enhance the bigel hardness and strength, but sacrificed its tolerance to external force deformation. All the bigel samples showed an increase in both G′ and G″ with increasing scanning frequency, indicating their strong frequency dependence. A possible explanation is that the pure PP network was relatively loose with large pores that hindered the oleogel tight encapsulation, while SA introduction increased the viscosity and compactness of the continuous phase to enhance spatial obstruction and binding of the dispersed phase, thereby significantly improving the modulus (Kaimal & Singhal, 2023).

The effects of PP/SA complex and oleogel-to-hydrogel ratio on the apparent viscosity of bigels are shown in Fig. 5G–I. The apparent viscosity of all bigel samples showed a down trend as the shear rate increased, indicating their pseudoplastic fluid characteristics. At 40% oleogel, the apparent viscosity was less for PP BG than 0.4% and 0.8%SA/PP BG samples. This result suggested that SA addition can effectively form a stronger and denser gel network, thereby increasing the system apparent viscosity (Jiang, Chen, et al., 2024). With oleogel content increased from 40% to 50% and 60%, the apparent viscosity exhibited an increase in all bigels, but with no great differences among SA, PP and SA/PP BG60 samples in apparent viscosity. This indicated that the bigel apparent viscosity was highly sensitive to the oleogel content (with higher oleogel content leading to greater viscosity). However, at 60% oleogel, the type of gelator (SA, PP and their mixture) had a negligible effect on apparent viscosity, and the system entered a rheological state dominated by oil phase content (Jiang, Wang, et al., 2024).

Texture characteristics analysis of bigels

This was probably attributed to the relatively weak adsorption capacity of single emulsifier (SA or PP) at the oil-water interface, causing the oil droplets to float upwards easily (Jiang, Chen, et al., 2024). After adding 0.4% SA to PP, the PP/SA BG40 exhibited a small TSI value and had a small increase at 0–5500 s. This result indicated that 0.4% SA addition significantly improved the physical stability of the bigel system, probably due to its contribution to the formation of a denser three-dimensional network structure, thus better retaining the oil droplets (Jiang, Chen, et al., 2024). However, after adding 0.8% SA to PP, the PP/SA BG40 sample exhibited a significant increase in the TSI value at 0–5500 s. This might be due to excessive SA addition, which led to phase separation or weakened network structure, causing the bigel system to become unstable (He et al., 2024). Additionally, with oleogel increased from 40% to 50% and 60%, the TSI value reduced in SA, PP and 0.8%SA/PP BG samples and exhibited minimal fluctuations during the 0–5500 s period, in contrast to an increase for 0.4%SA/PP BG. Moreover, at 50% and 60% oleogel, the TSI value showed no significant differences among SA, PP, 0.4% and 0.8%SA/PP BG samples. These observations indicated that the bigel stability was modulated by oleogel content. At a low oleogel content (40%), the hydrogel microstructure (0.4%SA/PP network) played a decisive role in the bigel stability. At a high oleogel content (50%–60%), the stability was dominated by the dispersed phase dense packing, which minimized the performance difference among the formulations.

The freeze-thaw stability analysis of bigels

The influence of PP/SA complex and oleogel-to-hydrogel ratio on the freeze-thaw stability of bigels was explored by subjecting each sample to three freeze-thaw cycles.

In Fig. 7A, B and C, it is shown that with the increase of oleogel content from 40% to 50% and 60%, bigels exhibited no great change in appearance, indicating that all bigels were relatively stable. At 40% oleogel (Fig. 7D), the SA BG sample showed a significant decrease in OHC with the freeze-thaw cycle number increased from 0 to 3 (p < 0.05). A possible explanation is that pure SA hydrogel was brittle and lacked elasticity, and during freezing, the expansion of ice crystals caused shear forces to directly destroy the surrounding gel matrix (Wang, Li, et al., 2024). However, PP, 0.4% and 0.8%SA/PP BG40 samples showed a small change in OHC, probably due to the formation of a stronger and more stable gel network, which can resist freeze-thaw damage and maintain the oil retention ability (Wang, Wu, et al., 2024). With oleogel increased from 40% to 50% (Fig. 7E), all bigels showed an increase in OHC, possibly because the oil phase became continuous, trapping liquid oil in the crystal network rather than merely encapsulating it, thereby significantly enhancing binding capacity (Yang et al., 2022). Besides, the SA and PP BG50 samples exhibited a significant decrease in OHC after the first freeze-thaw cycle (p < 0.05), but showed no great change in OHC with the freeze-thaw cycle increased from 1 to 3. This result indicated that the damage to SA and PP BG50 was the most severe in the first freeze-thaw process. This can be attributed to the initial freezing, where ice crystal growth exerted mechanical stress to disrupt the gel scaffold and denature interfacial proteins, thus compromising emulsion stability and causing significant oil loss (Yang et al., 2022). However, 0.4% and 0.8%SA/PP BG50 samples showed no great change in OHC with the increase of freeze-thaw cycles from 0 to 3, suggesting that adding SA to PP can effectively and significantly improve the freeze-thaw stability of the bigel system at 50% oleogel. This might be ascribed to the interaction between PP and SA, forming a viscoelastic interface film, and effectively suppressing the destructive force of ice crystals (Jiang, Chen, et al., 2024). Additionally, at 60% oleogel (Fig. 7F), all bigel samples (SA, PP, 0.4% and 0.8%SA/PP BG60) exhibited an increase in OHC, but with a significant decrease in OHC with the increase of freeze-thaw cycles from 0 to 3 (p < 0.05). This was likely attributed to the dilution of hydrogel network and the increase of interfacial burden at 60% oil phase, inducing structural fragility and impairing the system freeze-thaw resistance (Zhou et al., 2024). Conclusions

This study confirmed the initial hypothesis that complexing peanut protein (PP) with sodium alginate (SA) offered an effective strategy for enhancing the structural and functional properties of bigels. The results revealed that both PP/SA complex and oleogel-to-hydrogel ratio can significantly influence the structure, mechanical properties and stability of bigels. Specifically, at the oleogel proportion from 40% to 60%, the bigels transitioned from O/W to bi-continuous and W/O phases. Additionally, the combination of SA, PP, PP/SA complex and oleogel was shown to be a physical process, and appropriately increasing the oleogel proportion could enhance intermolecular interactions and stabilize the crystalline structure. Moreover, at 50% oleogel, the bigels exhibited a more compact structure and SA addition was shown to improve their water holding capacity, rheology, texture and stability. However, at 60% oleogel, all performance metrics declined, rendering the positive impact of SA negligible. This study has laid a theoretical foundation for utilizing PP/SA bigels as a potential spreadable sauce substitute.
