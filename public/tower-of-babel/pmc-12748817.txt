Investigating the effect of runoff characteristics on fatigue cracking in HMA modified with nano-hydrated lime using mechanical and thermodynamic approaches
Authors: Zahra Rasouli, Mehdi Mehdinazar, Gholam Hossein Hamedi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Moisture from surface runoff significantly accelerates fatigue cracking in asphalt by damaging bitumen cohesion and its bond with aggregates. This effect is worsened when surface contaminants like dust and soot change the pH of the runoff water. Accordingly, the influence of pH on fatigue cracking in asphalt mixtures was investigated using two aggregate types, two performance grade bitumen types, and nano-hydrated lime (NHL) at two dosage levels as a bitumen modifier. For this purpose, mechanical evaluations were conducted at intermediate temperatures, including Dynamic Shear Rheometer (DSR) tests on bitumen and Semi-Circular Bending (SCB) tests on HMA samples. Furthermore, surface free energy (SFE) tests, such as Wilhelmy plate (WP) and Universal Sorption Device (USD), were conducted to determine the thermodynamic interactions between bitumen, aggregate, modifier, and acidic/basic runoff. Results showed that applying environmental conditions, particularly acidic conditions, lowered the debonding energy of bitumen-aggregate samples by an average of 10%. This phenomenon was more evident in the mixture’s performance, which showed an average 20% reduction in fracture toughness (FT) under acidic/basic wet conditions. This was attributed to diminished bitumen-aggregate adhesion, which reduces resistance to crack growth in the presence of moisture. Consequently, the fracture energy (FE) of the HMAs under acidic/basic moisture conditions decreased by an average of 17%. In contrast, the application of NHL was found to increase the basic elements while reducing the acidic elements of bitumen’s SFE. According to SFE theory, this resulted in stronger intrinsic bitumen-aggregate adhesion, which was reflected in the mixture as a 24% increase in FT. NHL also improved the nonpolar elements of SFE, positively affecting both cohesive and adhesive strength. The influence of this factor on the performance of the bitumen and mixture also included a reduction in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{G}^{*}\times\:\mathrm{sin}\delta\:$$\end{document} and an increase in FE. For instance, the PG 58 − 22 bitumen modified with 0.4% NHL exhibited a fatigue parameter of 1078 kPa under acidic conditions (pH = 5), which was even lower than that of the base bitumen. Overall, the combination of PG 58 − 22 modified with 0.4% NHL and limestone demonstrated the greatest resistance to fatigue under acidic/basic moisture conditions.

Introduction

Advancements in transportation infrastructure have created a growing demand for innovative asphalt technologies. Simultaneously, intensified socio-economic development has resulted in heavier traffic loads and increased vehicle weights, highlighting the critical need to improve the performance characteristics of asphalt mixtures1–3. A fundamental approach to improving asphalt performance involves incorporating specialized additives. These materials enhance crack resistance, increase flexibility, and improve thermal and moisture stability, thereby strengthening pavement durability under high traffic loads. Widely used additives—such as polymers, fibers, and waterproofing agents—are selected based on the unique requirements of each project. Using different additives to enhance various properties of the hot-mix asphalt (HMA) increases its resistance to different types of distress4,5.

One of the most prevalent types of distress observed in HMA is the phenomenon of fatigue cracking. When a vehicle wheel is placed at a certain point on the pavement surface, a deformation occurs in the HMA layer, which generates tensile stress beneath that point. As the vehicle passes that point, the HMA layer tends to return to its original state. With the repetition of this process, even if the tensile stress beneath the HMA layer does not exceed its permissible tensile stress, fatigue cracks develop in the HMA layer6. The intrusion of water and air into existing fatigue cracks not only exacerbates their severity but also leads to the development of other forms of damage on the pavement surface. Moisture and air entering the HMA can cause stripping and accelerate the aging process, respectively7–9. Water infiltration undermines the longevity of HMA by penetrating either the bitumen film or the interface between bitumen and aggregate. A reduction in bitumen strength or in the bonding integrity between bitumen and aggregate heightens the risk of fatigue cracking—occurring either as cohesive failure within the mastic or as adhesive failure at the bitumen–aggregate10,11. The intensity of moisture’s effect also depends on the properties of HMA ingredients, environmental conditions, design and construction methods, and traffic volume. The properties of moisture infiltrating an HMA pavement can be altered by various environmental conditions, which in turn changes its effects on the mixture. Surface runoff is recognized as a primary source of this moisture ingress. The pH level of runoff is influenced by the dissolution of surface pollutants such as dust and soot on the pavement, leading to acidic or basic conditions. Additionally, air pollution contributes to fluctuations in the pH of incoming runoff12,13.

The chemical and mechanical mechanisms investigated, across micro and macro scales, revealed that acidic/alkaline moisture primarily degrades the bitumen-aggregate adhesion and the cohesive strength of the bitumen. The weakening of these two properties causes cracks to propagate either through the bitumen phase (cohesive failure) or along the bitumen-aggregate interface (adhesive failure) Given that fatigue in pavements manifests as cracking, it is evident that the adhesive and cohesive properties of the mixture significantly govern its fatigue performance. In conclusion, assessing the fatigue performance of HMAs under acidic and alkaline moisture conditions provides a highly realistic perspective on mixture behavior in the field. This understanding is a crucial prerequisite for developing optimal mix designs or effective mitigation strategies against these detrimental effects12,14.

Literature review

The literature reviewed in this section is divided into two main parts. The first part examines studies on the impact of impure water on HMA performance. The second part evaluates previous research on the use of HL and its derived nanomaterial in pavement applications.

SFE studies

Hughes et al.15 showed that an increase in water acidity levels weakens the adhesion between the bitumen-aggregate. Yoon and Tarrer16 evaluated the effect of physical, chemical, and electrochemical attributes of aggregates on the moisture sensitivity of HMAs. They concluded that water’s contact with the aggregate changes the pH of the water and decreases the bitumen’s coverage on the aggregate by dissolving functional groups. The stripping level of HMA increased at higher pH levels. This was attributed to the sensitivity of various bonds formed between the bitumen-aggregate to pH fluctuations. Furthermore, the high percentage of CaO in limestone formed potent bonds with the acidic elements of bitumen, such as carboxylic acids, which resisted decomposition in water with varying acidity levels, thereby enhancing resistance to moisture damage. Pang et al.17 showed that the content of saturates, aromatics, and resins in the bitumen decreased after exposing to aqueous solutions while the asphaltene content increased. In other words, the salts present in the water contributed to the oxidation-induced aging of bitumen, thereby increasing its potential for stripping. Based on surface free energy (SFE) concepts, Arabani et al.18 revealed that surface pollutants—specifically dust and soot—impact the acidity of runoff water and increase the vulnerability of HMA to moisture damage. In a separate study, Arabani et al.19 showed that incorporating zeolite into bitumen enhanced the tensile performance of HMA when subjected to water with different acidity or alkalinity levels. Zeolite modification contributed positively to both the cohesion free energy (CFE) of bitumen and the adhesion free energy (AFE) between bitumen and aggregates. Hamedi et al.21 assessed the impact of graphene nano-oxide to mitigate the negative effects of pH changes in runoff. According to SFE method, Graphene nano-oxide reduced the debonding energy (DE) in HMAs. Besides, the DE variations were minimal in neutral environments but became more pronounced as the environmental conditions polarized, especially under basic conditions.

Lamothe et al.22 indicated that wet samples exhibited reduced fatigue strength compared to dry samples. Additionally, the HMA stiffness was inversely related to the duration of sample saturation. Yang et al.23 revealed that following the ionization or dissolution of specific polar and ionic groups within bitumen, the aqueous solutions (acidic, alkaline, and saline) increased the elastic component, thereby enhancing the stiffness and rutting performance. Conversely, this increase in stiffness due to aging impaired the fatigue cracking performance by reducing the number of cycles to failure. Moreover, Fakhri et al.24 examined the impact of moisture content on the fracture toughness (FT) across four failure modes. The FT significantly was lowered with a raising number of freeze-thaw cycles. Mehdinazar et al.14 found that acidic and basic conditions weaken AFE, increasing the risk of fatigue cracking. To counter this, they added calcined dolomite to bitumen, which significantly improved both AFE and mechanical performance under moisture exposure. Zarrinfam et al.25 examined the effects of acidic and alkaline runoff on the fatigue performance of HMA and proposed the use of carbon nanotubes (CNTs) and nano-graphene oxide (NGO) as modifiers. Their study demonstrated that moisture with altered pH significantly deteriorates the rheological and mechanical properties of bitumen and asphalt mixtures. However, incorporating CNTs and NGO—particularly NGO at 0.6% by bitumen weight—substantially improved fracture energy, toughness, and fatigue resistance under various moisture conditions, highlighting their potential as effective additives for enhancing HMA durability against moisture-induced cracking. Zou et al.20 indicated that the acidic solution with pH = 3 had the most significant effect on the bitumen’s morphology, ultimately leading to the formation of cracks. A study by Azarhoosh et al.26 found that acidic and alkaline moisture conditions severely degrade HMA performance by reducing fatigue life and weakening the bitumen-aggregate DE. The most significant loss of AFE and CFE was reported at a pH of 6, with acidic environments causing the most severe fatigue damage. According to Mehdinazar et al.27, the fracture energy (FE), FT, and fatigue parameter (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{G}^{*}\times\:\mathrm{sin}\delta\:$$\end{document}) of HMAs, as well as the AFE of the bitumen-aggregate bond, are all compromised by acidic and alkaline waters at intermediate temperatures. The detrimental impact of extreme pH levels on asphalt materials was highlighted in work by Asadi et al.12,28. Their SFE testing demonstrated that acidic and alkaline waters aggressively reduce the DE of bitumen-aggregate system, leading to premature cracking. At low temperatures, this weakness promotes failure either within the mastic or along the aggregate interface. Moreover, these aggressive environments were found to inflict significantly greater damage on fracture-related parameters (FE and FT) and rheological properties than exposure to distilled water. A subsequent study by Mehdinazar et al.29 found that acidic/alkaline exposure raises the low-temperature cracking potential of HMAs by up to 32%. This increased susceptibility is a direct consequence of a 12% loss in DE, which facilitates the stripping of bitumen from aggregate surfaces.

In summary, the review of this section demonstrates that altering water pH in laboratory conditions and studying its effect through moisture application on mixture and material samples leads to a severe decline in bitumen-aggregate adhesion and bitumen cohesion. These two factors significantly increase the likelihood of various cracking types, including fatigue cracking, regardless of the failure mechanism—whether it propagates through the bitumen itself or along the bitumen-aggregate interface.

NHL in asphalt mixture

Recent findings across various studies indicate that incorporating nanomaterials leads to considerable improvements in the physical, microstructural, and rheological properties of bitumen30–32. Also, nanoparticles are able to enhance the adhesive attributes of bitumen-aggregates system30,33. Numerous studies have explored the beneficial effects of nano-hydrate lime (NHL) on minimizing damage in HMA mixtures. NHL has been shown to improve the durability and performance of HMA by addressing some of its critical weaknesses, particularly under environmental and loading stresses. The inclusion of NHL has been found to enhance the moisture sensitivity of HMAs by improving the adhesion of the bitumen-aggregate, thereby reducing the likelihood of stripping and water-induced damage. Furthermore, the use of NHL contributes to mitigating oxidative aging in bitumen, which can otherwise result in increased stiffness and reduced flexibility, leading to premature cracking34,35. Behbahani et al.36 studied the influence of NHL on HMA’s moisture sensitivity, focusing on its mechanism of action on the aggregate-bitumen adhesion. The use of NHL resulted in an increase in the tensile strength ratio (TSR). Mohammad et al.37 also evaluated the rheological and adhesive characteristics of bitumens with different percentages of NHL. Adding 4% NHL effectively improved the properties of bitumen. In a comprehensive study, Amjad et al.38 demonstrated a significant enhancement in bitumen stiffness, particularly at 6% NHL. Dynamic shear rheometer (DSR) testing revealed that NHL provided greater stability at high temperatures and substantial resistance to rutting. Additionally, the elastic modulus of the HMA increased through adding NHL to the bitumen. Moisture sensitivity testing further showed that NHL significantly enhanced moisture resistance. Yarahmadi et al.39 evaluated the synergistic effects of Parafiber, Micro-silica, and Hydrated Lime in HMA to identify the most effective composite. Laboratory testing—including Marshall stability, indirect tensile strength (ITS), and semi-circular bending (SCB)—revealed that the sample incorporating all three additives consistently outperformed others, showing peak resistance to moisture-induced damage, low-temperature cracking, and plastic flow.

A review of the literature in this section indicates that NHL is an effective bitumen additive, significantly enhancing mechanical properties and, in particular, improving bitumen-aggregate adhesion. This capability has established NHL as a popular material for mitigating moisture-induced damage.

Problems and objectives

Moisture and changes in its acidity are significant factors in the occurrence and intensification of various HMA failures. One material that has demonstrated excellent performance in the presence of moisture in prior studies is hydrated lime. Due to its strong hydrophobic properties, it has established itself as an effective anti-stripping agent. A review of the existing literature indicated that NHL performance as a modifier for bitumen’s fatigue-related properties under acidic/alkaline moisture conditions had not yet been investigated. Therefore, this study investigated the effect of acidic, basic, and distilled water moisture conditions on the fatigue properties of bitumen and HMA modified with NHL using mechanical and SFE methods. The SFE method has been widely and successfully used to evaluate various failure modes related to cohesive and adhesive breakdown based on the fundamental properties of materials. Furthermore, this approach can compensate for a key weakness of mechanical methods: their inability to adequately describe the chemical interactions between materials based on their fundamental properties. Therefore, the SFE method was utilized as a powerful analytical tool specifically for validation and for providing a better explanation of the underlying mechanisms. Experimental design

In Fig. 1, the experimental program design utilized in this study is illustrated. In this study, materials including aggregates such as siliceous and limestone, bitumens such as PG 58 − 22 and PG 64 − 16, and NHL at dosages of 0.2% and 0.4% by the weight of bitumen were selected and prepared. The gradation of aggregates was determined, and the bitumen was modified by incorporating the NHL. Subsequently, various samples were prepared: HMA specimens for SCB test, bitumen samples for DSR test, aqueous solutions with NaOH and HCl to simulate acidic runoff conditions, and samples for SFE analysis, comprising bitumens, aggregates, and solutions. The experimental phase included mechanical and thermodynamic testing. The mechanical evaluations involved SCB tests conducted at intermediate temperatures to measure the fatigue performance of HMA under different moisture conditions and DSR tests to evaluate the fatigue characteristics of modified bitumen under varying moisture exposure. Thermodynamic tests were conducted to measure the SFE elements of the solutions and to determine the SFE properties of bitumen and aggregates, for which the wilhelmy plate (WP) method and the universal sorption device (USD) were employed.

The first set of tests utilized the WP to determine the SFE elements of bitumens. A total of 72 samples were prepared, considering three probe liquids and four test repetitions for each condition.

In another series of tests, the WP was used to evaluate the SFE of solutions at 5 different pH levels. A total of 48 samples were tested, including three probe liquids and four repetitions. Marshall mix design tests were performed using siliceous and limestone aggregates combined with PG 64 − 16 and PG 58 − 22 bitumens to determine the optimal bitumen content (OBC) of HMA. This test aimed to determine FE and FT, with three test repetitions for each condition. A total of 216 samples were analyzed.

Finally, the DSR test was conducted on both control and modified bitumens, under dry and moisture-conditioned states at 5 different pH levels. This test assessed rheological properties, including phase angle, complex modulus, and fatigue parameter, with three test repetitions, resulting in a total of 108 samples.

Bitumen

In bitumen, temperature has a direct impact on its viscosity. Higher temperatures reduce viscosity, which makes the bitumen more fluid and easier to mix with aggregates and compact during construction. In contrast, lower temperatures increase viscosity, causing bitumen to become stiffer, which can lead to cracking under mechanical stress. Additionally, prolonged exposure to high temperatures during production or storage accelerates the aging of bitumen, making it less flexible and durable over time. The rheological properties of bitumen, including the phase angle and complex modulus, are also temperature-dependent, affecting its resistance to deformation and fatigue. In HMA, temperature plays a critical role in compaction and performance. Elevated temperatures enhance the workability of the mix, facilitating better compaction and reducing air voids. However, excessively high temperatures can cause segregation or oxidation of the mix. At high service temperatures, HMA may soften, resulting in rutting under traffic loads, whereas at low service temperatures, it becomes brittle, increasing the risk of thermal cracking. Furthermore, temperature fluctuations exacerbate moisture damage in HMA, as they influence the adhesion between bitumen and aggregates, making the mix more susceptible to stripping and weakening over time44,45. Accordingly, the selection of the appropriate bitumen type is highly effective in the performance of bitumen and, consequently, the asphalt mixture.

In this study, two bitumens, PG 64 − 16 and PG 58 − 22, were chosen, both commonly used in temperate and cold climates. The two bitumen types, PG 58 − 22 and PG 64 − 16, exhibit differences in their performance properties tailored to specific temperature and environmental conditions. PG 64 − 16, designed for higher temperature ranges, demonstrates better resistance to deformation at elevated temperatures compared to PG 58 − 22. This indicates its suitability for regions with hotter climates or high traffic loads. However, PG 58 − 22, with its lower temperature grade, offers improved flexibility and resistance to cracking at colder temperatures, making it more appropriate for areas experiencing lower temperatures. PG 64 − 16, with its higher resistance to aging under heat, might offer better durability, especially in hotter environments. In general, PG 64 − 16 demonstrates greater resistance to high temperatures, making it less prone to softening or rutting under extreme heat and heavy loads. Conversely, PG 58 − 22 is engineered to maintain flexibility in colder temperatures, reducing the likelihood of thermal cracking. From an economic perspective, bitumens such as the two types used in this study, which are designed for moderate climatic conditions, are typically produced without the need for polymer additives or specialized bitumen modification processes. This simplicity in production not only reduces manufacturing complexities but also contributes to lower costs compared to bitumens formulated for extreme climatic conditions.

Table 4 lists the performance characteristics of these two bitumens. Both bitumen types meet the ASTM standards for flash point, viscosity, and performance under aging conditions, including tests with the rolling film oven test (RTFO) and pressure aging vessel (PAV). While both demonstrate excellent thermal and aging resistance, minor differences in weight loss and rheological properties reflect their respective design for different climatic conditions. Additionally, both types exhibit acceptable creep stiffness and m-values, indicating their compliance with cold temperature performance requirements, although each is optimized for distinct temperature thresholds.

Bitumen modification

A high-shear mixer, capable of rotating up to 14,000 rpm, was used to mix the bitumens and NHL. The maximum rotational capacity of the mixer was utilized. NHL was progressively mixed into the bitumen once it reached 160 °C. Two amounts of NHL (0.2% and 0.4% by weight of the bitumen) were used. The mixing continued for 20 min to ensure the homogeneous distribution of NHL particles within the bitumen. SFE elements of materials.

CFE of bitumen

CFE is a fundamental measure of the energy required to disrupt the internal molecular or structural bonds within the bitumen. This energy reflects the intrinsic cohesion within the bitumen and its ability to maintain integrity under external stresses. A higher CFE indicates stronger intermolecular interactions, meaning the material has a greater capacity to resist the initiation and growth of cracks when subjected to mechanical loads, and environmental variations. Based on Table 7, adding NHL at 0.2% by weight of the bitumen and increasing its percentage to 0.4% elevated the bitumen’s total SFE and, consequently, its CFE. NHL raised the energy needed to create a crack with a unit area in the bitumen. Accordingly, modifying bitumen using NHL raised its strength to cohesive failure. In addition, PG 58 − 22 exhibited greater CFE than PG 64 − 16; that is a higher amount of energy was required to initiate fracture in the mastic composed of PG 58 − 22. NHL particles are highly polar and basic. They have a strong affinity for the acidic, polar components in the bitumen (like asphaltenes). By preventing asphaltene flocculation, NHL maintains a more stable and homogeneous colloidal structure. This stabilized system has a higher resistance to internal degradation, which is measured as an increase in CFE.

Bitumen-aggregate AFE

AFE is vital for understanding how well the bitumen adheres to the aggregates, particularly in the presence of moisture or other environmental stressors. High AFE indicates strong interfacial adhesion, reducing the risk of debonding and enhancing the mixture’s durability. The AFE values are presented in Fig. 5. The AFE of mixtures made with siliceous was meaningly higher than in those made with limestone. This difference can primarily be related to the higher non-polar SFE element of siliceous than limestone. Conversely, non-modified bitumens had acidic properties and exhibited better adhesion to limestone, which had basic properties.

PG 58 − 22 exhibited superior adhesion to aggregates compared to PG 64 − 16 due to its higher content of both polar and non-polar SFE elements. The inclusion of NHL further improved the bonding between bitumen and aggregates. As presented in Table 8, NHL enhanced the non-polar SFE attributes in both bitumen grades, reinforcing non-polar interactions. Additionally, NHL helped neutralize bitumen’s acidity (by increasing the alkaline element), enhancing its bonding capability with acidic aggregates.

DE

From a thermodynamic perspective, DE is closely linked to the fundamental principles of energy balance and interfacial interactions in asphalt mixtures. It represents the work done to overcome cohesive and adhesive forces within the mixture’s elements, such as the bitumen, aggregates, and their interfaces. This energy quantifies the amount of external energy required to disrupt these bonds, causing a separation or fracture under applied loads. DE is influenced by the Gibbs free energy changes within the system. The Gibbs free energy, which combines the effects of enthalpy (bonding energy) and entropy (disorder), governs the stability of bonds in the mixture. When separation occurs, the system undergoes a transition from a bonded state to a separated state. A lower Gibbs free energy (more negative DE) change during separation suggests that the material requires less work to fail, indicating weaker interfacial and cohesive bonds. Conversely, a higher DE (close to zero) implies stronger and more thermodynamically stable bonds that resist cracking and failure. Figures 6 and 7 illustrate the DE values under neutral, acidic, and basic conditions. The incorporation of NHL increased DE in the modified samples, resulting in enhanced bitumen–aggregate stability and a reduced tendency for debonding. This suggests that NHL mitigated adhesive failure by strengthening the interfacial adhesion between bitumen and aggregates. Furthermore, adding 0.2% NHL significantly increased the DE, while using 0.4% NHL had no considerable effect on increasing the DE. Previous research has observed that various additives—including CNTs, NGO, Zeolite, and calcined dolomite—improve bitumen-aggregate adhesion in the presence of acidic/alkaline runoff12,14,21,27. However, a comparison of the improvement trends indicates that NHL outperformed these additives.

The maximum DE values were observed under wet conditions with neutral pH (7). A reduction in pH below this level led to a marked decrease in DE. Exposing the system to acidic environments elevated the energy released, thereby increasing the probability of cracking. On the other hand, increasing pH also decreased DE, but the rate of this reduction was less pronounced than decreasing pH. The acidity or alkalinity of water reduced the adhesion. Limestone exhibited higher DE values compared to siliceous aggregates, indicating a stronger adhesive interaction. Consequently, mixtures containing siliceous aggregates demonstrated a greater propensity for bitumen detachment. This trend suggests that HMAs incorporating acidic aggregates are more vulnerable to bitumen–aggregate separation under moisture exposure across different pH conditions. This increased the probability of HMA cracking, which is attributed to the adhesive properties of bitumen-aggregates. Arabani et al.18 demonstrated that acidic moisture conditions have the most significant impact on reducing DE. Consequently, the stress field around the bitumen-aggregate interface will be more severe under acidic moisture conditions. The reduction in DE under both acidic and alkaline moisture conditions has been confirmed in the studies by Asadi et al.12 and Hamedi et al.21.

A t-test statistically determines whether there is a considerable difference between the two comparing groups. The t-test assists in hypothesis testing by comparing the sample mean to a specified value (one-sample t-test) or by comparing the means of two samples (independent or paired t-test). The null hypothesis in the t-test indicates that there is no considerable difference between the means. In other words, the null hypothesis assumes that any observed difference between the group means is due to chance and not the real effect of the studied variable. The t-test is used to determine whether the sample data provide strong statistical grounds to challenge the null hypothesis. A rejection of the null hypothesis implies a meaningful gap between the group means, potentially attributable to the influence of the independent variable. However, if the null hypothesis is retained, any apparent difference in group means is likely minor and could be attributed to random fluctuations within the experimental framework.

Table 8 presents the results of t-tests conducted to examine the impact of NHL on the DE values. In the case of limestone, for both PG 58 − 22 and PG 64 − 16, 0.2% NHL significantly lowered the DE compared to the control group (without NHL). The t-values were high, and the p-values (sig.) were less than 0.001, indicating a statistically significant difference. Comparing 0.2% with 0.4% NHL shows that the mean difference was not statistically significant for PG 58 − 22 (p = 0.166), suggesting that increasing the percentage of NHL did not significantly reduce the DE. For PG 64 − 16, the difference between 0.2% and 0.4% NHL was also statistically significant (p < 0.001), indicating that increasing NHL from 0.2% to 0.4% led to a measurable change in DE.

Similar trends were observed with siliceous. Adding 0.2% NHL to both PG 58 − 22 and PG 64 − 16 significantly decreased DE compared to the control sample (p < 0.001). Comparing 0.2% and 0.4% NHL demonstrated significant differences for both bitumen types, indicating that the higher NHL content continued to influence DE positively.

In summary, the results in Table 8 indicate that adding NHL significantly lowered the DE for both types of aggregates and bitumens, suggesting stronger bonding and possibly better performance of HMA under moisture conditions that could lead to debonding. Increasing the amount of NHL maintained or improved the adhesion between bitumen and aggregates under the influence of waters with various pH levels, although this impact might vary based on the specific bitumen type used.

Figures 8 and 9 depict the G* and δ values ​​of the non-modified and modified bitumens, respectively. In bitumens with higher G*, the bitumen was stiffer, and deformation occurred with the accumulation of large stresses. Evidently, applying acidic and basic moisture conditions increased the G* and δ values ​​in all base and modified bitumen samples. Since the work wasted in each loading cycle rose with increasing G* and δ, it can be concluded that non-neutral conditions weakened the bitumen’s intermediate-temperature performance under loading. Therefore, the pH of water is known as a factor which increases the HMA’s potential for fatigue cracking.

Moreover, according to Figs. 8 and 9, it is observed that using NHL reduced G* and δ. NHL lowered the stiffness of bitumen, and the bitumen could deform without accumulating large stresses. Also, NHL raised the elastic property of bitumen by reducing δ and caused the modified bitumen to recover the deformations more quickly and regain its original conditions faster. Accordingly, lowering G* and δ due to the addition of NHL improved the bitumen’s performance against cracking at intermediate temperatures. Based on Figs. 5 and 6, the bitumens containing 0.4% NHL demonstrated the best performance under intermediate-temperature cracking and, consequently, the best resistance to fatigue.

Comparative analysis revealed PG 58 − 22 exhibited reduced G* and δ values relative to PG 64 − 16, reflecting its lower stiffness characteristics. These rheological properties suggest superior performance of PG 58 − 22 at intermediate temperature ranges because it could deform without generating high cumulative stresses under repeated loading cycles. Moreover, it retained its elastic properties at intermediate temperatures and recovered the deformations faster. Accordingly, PG 58 − 22 was more resistant to fatigue cracking.

FE

FE is the energy required to change a unit surface of a material from its initial state to a failure state. This definition demonstrates that CFE and AFE influence the value of FE under dry conditions, while DE significantly affects FE under wet conditions. Figures 11 and 12 depict the FE of HMAs made with limestone and siliceous. Applying moisture conditions on HMA samples significantly reduced FE. This means that the samples failed in less time and absorbed less energy until failure. When HMA samples were placed under moisture conditions with pH = 7, they had higher FE compared to other samples. The pavement surface contaminants that change the chemical properties of runoff have a greater destructive effect than clean pavement surfaces. Based on the discussion in the section “Results of the DSR test”, the bitumen mastic (bitumen + fine aggregates) is weaker and a compromised matrix when exposed to acidic/alkaline moisture condition. The mixture loses its ability to deform plastically and absorb energy. It behaves in a more brittle manner, failing with little deformation.

Using NHL increased FE by decreasing the stiffness of the bitumen and improving its ability to absorb energy from loading to failure. Dry samples containing PG 58 − 22 exhibited higher FE than those with PG 64 − 16, mainly due to the reduced stiffness of the HMA and enhanced energy absorption capacity under load. NHL particles are extremely small (nano-scale), giving them a very high surface area. They act as a superior filler, densely packing into the micro-voids and cavities within the bitumen mastic. This creates a more homogeneous, continuous, and denser matrix. When a load is applied, stress is distributed more evenly throughout this reinforced matrix. Instead of stress concentrating and creating a crack, the energy is absorbed by the system, requiring more force to initiate and propagate a crack. FT

Figures 13 and 14 show the results of FT in different mixtures. Placing HMA samples under moisture conditions caused their resistance to decrease at the critical moment of failure. Various moisture conditions increased the tendency of bitumen to separate from the aggregate surface, and the greater this tendency, the lower the resistance at the critical moment to prevent crack growth. Among the wet samples, FT was higher for samples placed in distilled water. On the other hand, acidic or basic moisture conditions lowered the FT. The bitumen itself is a complex hydrocarbon with polar components. Extreme pH values trigger chemical reactions that break it down. The high pH (OH⁻ ions) reacts with acidic compounds in the bitumen (like carboxylic acids) to form soap-like compounds. This reaction breaks down the larger, structurally important molecules (asphaltenes) that give bitumen its stiffness and strength12,16. The bitumen becomes softer, less cohesive, and loses its glue-like properties. These observations were evident in the reduction of the DE. As the results from the section “Thermodynamic analysis”, indicated, acidic and alkaline waters disrupted the balance of the polar and non-polar components governing bitumen-aggregate adhesion, leading to a lower DE within the system. The H⁺ ions in acidic water can attack and break certain chemical bonds (e.g., ester or sulfide linkages) in a process called hydrolysis. This is often accompanied by accelerated oxidation. This breaks the long polymer-like chains in the bitumen, leading to a loss of flexibility and an increase in brittleness28. The bitumen cannot stretch or deform as much before breaking (cohesive failure). FT indicates a mixture’s resistance to crack propagation. Consequently, when a mixture has higher adhesive and cohesive strength, incipient cracks cannot easily find a path to grow through the bitumen phase or along the bitumen-aggregate interface, meeting greater resistance. In summary, the loss of adhesion and cohesion due to the presence of acidic/alkaline water causes cracks to propagate through the bitumen or along the interface more rapidly than under dry conditions.

HMA’s performance was better in modified samples. This is due to the use of NHL, which raised the non-polar bonds of bitumen and aggregate. The addition of NHL enhanced the alkaline characteristics while diminishing the acidic components in bitumen, consequently decreasing its reactivity with various aqueous media. The well-dispersed, hard NHL particles act as physical obstacles within the bitumen matrix. When a micro-crack starts to propagate, it encounters these nanoparticles. The particles force the crack to change direction, pin its tip, or require additional energy to bypass them. This process absorbs energy and slows down crack growth. By resisting crack propagation, the binder maintains its integrity and ability to relax stress, thereby preventing a decrease in FT. This simple mechanism has also proven effective in preserving the mixture’s adhesion and cohesion. NHL creates a stronger mastic and a superior bond with the aggregate. As a result, micro-cracks propagating through the bitumen are forced to contend with the resistance offered by the NHL at the adhesive/cohesive interface, which significantly impedes crack propagation.

The combination of PG 58 − 22 and limestone provided the highest FT. Accordingly, this combination had the highest resistance to cracking at intermediate temperatures and, consequently, the best fatigue performance.

Conclusions

Understanding the influence of environmental factors and material modifications on HMA behavior is essential for improving pavement durability and ensuring reliable performance under variable field conditions. This study investigated the influence of environmental factors and bitumen modification with nano-NHL on the mechanical and thermodynamic behavior of HMA at intermediate temperatures. Through a series of rheological, thermodynamic, and moisture-sensitivity evaluations, critical insights were obtained regarding the role of pH variation, aggregate and bitumen type, and the use of NHL as a performance-enhancing additive. Future research directions

NHL modification offers a life-cycle economic benefit for asphalt pavements in corrosive environments. By enhancing resistance to adhesive and cohesive failure, NHL reduces damage, leading to less frequent maintenance and significant long-term cost savings. This study found that 0.2% and 0.4% NHL significantly improve key performance parameters (e.g., AFE, CFE, FE, FT, G^*×sin⁡δ) under acidic/alkaline conditions, thereby reducing fatigue cracking—a common failure requiring costly mitigation. While the initial cost of NHL and plant modifications are economic barriers, a full Life Cycle Cost Analysis (LCCA) would likely reveal net savings from reduced maintenance. Combining LCCA with a Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) is recommended for future research to holistically evaluate economic and environmental impacts. Furthermore, while a PG 58 − 22 and limestone blend showed superior performance, modifying weaker mixes (e.g., PG 64 − 16 with siliceous aggregate) with 0.4% NHL brought their fatigue performance close to that of higher-quality mixes, justifying its use where premium materials are unavailable. This demonstrates that by developing mathematical models based on a sufficient volume of experimental data, mathematical representations of various pavement distresses could be created that account for moisture conditions, different additives, and base material combinations - ultimately enabling the development of optimized mix designs for specific environmental conditions.
