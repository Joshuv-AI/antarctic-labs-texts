Students’ perceptions of school climate factors supporting school belonging in faith-based schools: a qualitative study
Authors: Beyza Nur Turgut, Hasan Batmaz, Eyüp Çelik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose & relevanceThis study aimed to investigate the factors that foster school belonging, students' levels of belonging, and the role of school climate in faith-based educational institutions. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for developing inclusive educational environments that support students' psychological and social integration.Type of research & populationAdopting a qualitative research design to provide in-depth insights into students',perceptions, and interpretations, the study was conducted with a study group of 78 students (Mean age = 15.74, SD = 1.09; 48 girls and 30 boys).InstrumentsData were collected through a semi-structured interview form designed to explore various dimensions of the school perception.Main resultsThe findings revealed that positive peer relationships and sincere teacher-student bonds form the core of school belonging. Voluntary enrollment significantly accelerates the development of belonging, whereas family pressure acts as a barrier, although some students adapt over time. Negative factors such as bullying, exclusion, and cultural or linguistic differences were found to hinder adaptation. Furthermore, while the school's religious structure and academic support positively influence belonging, inadequate sports and social activity facilities, high academic pressure, and negative teacher attitudes weaken this bond. Perceptions of disciplinary practices varied: some students favored flexibility, while others associated strict discipline with a stronger sense of belonging. Some participants reported experiencing religious practices under duress and perceiving the spiritual atmosphere as oppressive.ConclusionsTo enhance students' sense of belonging, religious-based schools should prioritize strengthening social relationships, improving teacher communication, and upgrading physical and social infrastructure. Additionally, targeted interventions are essential to mitigate the negative impacts of cultural differences and exclusionary social behaviors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05307-6.

Introduction

Educational institutions are structures that support personality development through cultural norms and values and educate individuals academically. However, it is known that religious education within formal education plays a supporting role in achieving learning missions [52]. As a matter of fact, Fahmi et al. [30] emphasize the necessity of integrating academic life with moral and spiritual values ​​and religious education. In this context, faith-based schools, which occupy an important place among educational institutions, offer holistic learning environments that support students academically and spiritually [7, 26]. Kaya [42] stated that faith-based schools play an important role in teaching religious and moral values, adopting moral responsibilities, adapting to social norms, and carrying out the education process through knowledge transferWhen looking at the roles that faith-based schools play in education, it is stated that they are pioneers in strengthening students' belief systems and commitment to their traditions compared to other types of schools (e.g., unlike secular schools, other private schools, public schools, etc.) [53]. Integrating religious education into the curriculum in belief-based schools allows students to reinforce their religious beliefs [40]. It can be argued that individuals demonstrate multifaceted development regarding religious knowledge, cultural values, and social norms. The reason for choosing faith-based schools is that individuals who attend them can apply their education to their daily lives and develop a deeper understanding of ethical and religious values ​​ [40]. In other types of schools, educational content is based on academic performance criteria, standardized test scores, and curriculum [78]. In addition to this educational content, it can be argued that purposeful activities aimed at imparting religious education in religiously based schools are among the contexts that influence the academic mission of these activities. In this context, social norms, traditions, values, ceremonies, and symbols in faith-based schools are dynamic factors that shape the school climate by fostering social relations [29].

School climate is evaluated as a holistic construct encompassing the school's social atmosphere, academic performance, teacher-student relationships, and emotional processes [13, 22]. A positive school climate is defined as a commitment to teaching environments, trust among peers, alignment between expectations and values, cooperation among administrators, teachers, students, and parents, and respectful expression of ideas [13, 16]. However, school climate is interpreted differently depending on individuals' perceptions of the school and the school's own reality [64]. However, research agrees that a positive school climate is the foundation of students' engagement, motivation, and school success [24, 49, 82]. A study found that revealing students' capacities in faith-based schools and increasing their success can be achieved through education grounded in religious values and cultural norms [63]. However, it has been noted that the positive school climate in such schools is essential for fostering a sense of belonging and creating a positive learning environment [58]. A positive school climate that supports effective continuing education can be considered a factor that enhances students' sense of belonging.

School belonging is defined as the commitment students feel towards the school they attend [23]. The orderly progression of positive processes in educational institutions improves school belonging and enables quicker access to the school's missions [29]. School belonging is influenced by academic motivation, emotional stability, personal characteristics, parental support, peer support, teacher support, gender, race and ethnicity, extracurricular activities, and environmental/school safety [2]. Law et al. [49] stated that a positive school climate and a sense of belonging can be achieved by eliminating inequalities, ensuring equal opportunities, and supporting participation in learning. The support offered to students, the school's disciplinary methods, and justice within the school contribute to creating a positive school climate by shaping students' sense of belonging [27, 69]. How students perceive their schools, the sense of security, the relationships at school, and the teaching and learning process constitute the elements of the school climate and predict school belonging [62]. A study by Vang & Nishina [74] found that school belonging is associated with students' health, well-being, and a positive school climate. Perceived school climate and school belonging are reported to be influenced by peer support, teacher support, and a sense of autonomy [18]. Similarly, Çimen [21] found in his study with Anatolian Imam Hatip High School students that teacher qualifications regarding the communication process affect students' sense of belonging to the school. A study by Ooi & Cortina [56] examined 2018 OECD-PISA data from 76 countries to determine the degree of sense of belonging in schools. The study found that perceived cooperation was positively correlated with school climate across all countries. Furthermore, school climate explained 18.8% of the variance in school belonging across countries. A study conducted with students attending imam hatip high schools found that school climate accounted for 57% of character development. Furthermore, a positive relationship was found between character development and school climate [26]. In a study conducted at the Faculty of Islamic Sciences in Bilecik, Karacan [13] found that students had a positive learning environment. Furthermore, students' academic motivation and perceptions of school climate were positively correlated. Similarly, [46, 47] found a positive relationship between imam hatip middle school students' perceptions of school climate and their commitment to the school.

Previous studies have also explored school belonging from students’ perspectives. For example, research has shown that students’ perceptions of teacher support, peer relationships, and school climate play a significant role in shaping their sense of belonging [2, 8, 20, 66]. In research examining teachers' perceptions of the school climate and student belonging in Imam Hatip high schools, Kalkan [41] surveyed teachers' perceptions of the advantages and disadvantages of working in project Imam Hatip high schools and found that working in Imam Hatip high schools is more advantageous than other schools in terms of a positive school climate. In a study examining guidance counselors' findings at Imam Hatip High School regarding students' expectations and problems, it was noted that school belonging is one of the most important problem areas in schools [9]. Looking at relevant research, it appears that school climate and sense of belonging in Imam Hatip high schools vary across schools and depend on teachers' perceptions. Most studies examining school climate and sense of belonging in faith-based schools rely on administrators' and teachers' opinions [28, 36, 46, 47]. The lack of research examining students' opinions in studies of faith-based schools represents a gap in this field. The current study aims to address this gap by presenting student perceptions. St-Amand et al. [69] also reported that insufficient research has examined the link between school climate and sense of belonging. This research is believed to offer significant contributions to the field and practice by examining the school climate that fosters school belonging in faith-based schools through student opinions.

However, Imam Hatip high schools were selected as the context of this study because they represent a faith-based educational setting within the Turkish education system, where religious, moral, and spiritual values are integrated with academic education [9]. This educational context may influence students’ sense of belonging through perceptions of academic, social, and value-related aspects. Therefore, examining school climate and school belonging in Imam Hatip schools may provide insights into how different dimensions of school climate contribute to students’ perceptions of belonging within faith-based educational settings.

In faith-based schools, the combined provision of academic courses and religious values ​​education can be crucial for students' developing a sense of belonging, fostering academic success, and internalizing cultural and individual values. Studies have shown that these schools should integrate religious values ​​and the educational process (Muthmainnah et al., 2021); [40, 41]. However, research exploring how school climate is interpreted in such schools and examining its impact on the sense of belonging is limited. In particular, examining students' perceptions and expectations regarding the school atmosphere and sense of belonging can fill a gap in this area. Religious schools harbor diverse dynamics that can influence school belonging, and understanding these dynamics will guide administrators and educational processes.

In summary, the current research can be considered important in determining the elements of the school climate that develop school belonging in faith-based schools, determining the dynamics that affect school climate and belonging, and examining the effect of school climate on the educational process and student belonging. However, it will support educational institutions and administrators in determining the situations that schools will prioritize to support student educational and spiritual development. The data obtained through this research will contribute to the determination of the dynamics that improve cooperation in the school, to the development of a quality relationship that satisfies all individuals, and to the continuation of educational processes more efficiently.

Method

The primary aim of this study is to determine students’ opinions regarding the school climate that fosters a sense of belonging in faith-based schools. To achieve this aim, the study was designed as a qualitative research employing content analysis, which allows for the systematic identification, organization, and interpretation of patterns within qualitative data. Qualitative research provides in-depth information, perceptions, and interpretations about a phenomenon or event [19]. In line with the aim of the study, the following research questions were formulated:

Study group

Following permissions from the Ministry of National Education, three schools were granted approval to conduct student interviews. Two of these schools were selected for data collection. The study group consisted of 78 students (Mean age = 15.74, SD = 1.09; 48 girls and 30 boys) attending two Anatolian Imam Hatip high schools in Sakarya. The study group was selected using a purposive sampling method to examine students' perceptions across different levels in detail, which aligned with the study's purpose.

Data collection tool

The interview questions were developed based on the study’s research objectives and questions and were reviewed by three experts to ensure clarity and content validity. The interview protocol consisted of three open-ended, participant-oriented questions designed to explore students’ perceptions in depth and flexibly. The interview questions were formulated to support an exploratory qualitative inquiry process and to capture students’ experiences and perceptions related to the research objectives. The third question, which adopted a solution-oriented structure, was included to elicit students’ perceptions of possible improvements in school climate and to capture their reflective views rather than to impose predefined expectations. Before the interviews, the concepts of “school climate” and “school belonging” were explained to participants using simple, age-appropriate, and neutral language to facilitate their understanding while preserving the openness of their responses. A purposive sampling strategy was used to select participants who could provide rich and relevant information about the phenomenon under investigation. The selection criteria included being currently enrolled in the participating schools and voluntarily agreeing to participate in the study. Students from different grade levels were included to ensure a range of perspectives. Although permission was obtained from three schools, data collection was completed in two schools due to scheduling constraints and the inability to establish appropriate interview conditions in the third school during the data collection period. All interviews were conducted face-to-face in quiet and suitable environments provided by the school administrations. Each interview lasted approximately 30 min and was audio-recorded with participants’ consent. The recordings were transcribed verbatim and used solely for research purposes. Ethical approval was obtained from Sakarya University and the Ministry of National Education, and written parental consent was obtained for all participants. During the interviews, no additional structured questions were used. However, the researchers occasionally used brief, spontaneous clarifying prompts to ensure participants’ responses were understood. These prompts were not part of the interview guide and did not constitute a separate set of questions.

Participant recruitment and data collection were carried out in May 2025. Audio-recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim shortly after data collection, with transcription beginning on 9 May 2025.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using content analysis [19], which enables the systematic identification, organization, and interpretation of patterns in qualitative data. Initially, all interview transcripts were read repeatedly to ensure familiarity with the data. Meaningful units were then identified and coded through an open coding process. These initial codes were compared for similarities and differences and grouped into categories. In the next stage, related categories were organized into broader themes, and sub-themes were developed where necessary to more accurately represent the structure of the data. To enhance the reliability of the analysis, two researchers independently coded the data. The coding results were then compared, and discrepancies were discussed until consensus was reached. When necessary, a third expert was consulted to ensure consistency and methodological rigor in coding decisions. To strengthen the credibility of the findings, direct quotations from participants were included in the results section, ensuring that the analysis remained grounded in participants’ own expressions.

Trustworthiness

To ensure the study's trustworthiness, several strategies were employed. Credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement with the data and the use of direct quotations from participants to support the findings. In addition, the coding process was conducted to accurately represent participants’ perspectives. Transferability was supported by providing detailed descriptions of the research context, participants, and data collection procedures, enabling readers to assess the applicability of the findings to other settings. Dependability was ensured by clearly documenting the research process, including data collection and analysis procedures. The coding and categorization steps were systematically followed and described in detail. Confirmability was established by grounding the findings in the data and maintaining transparency in the analytical process. Interpretations were grounded in participants’ responses to minimize the influence of researcher bias.

Researcher’s role

The researchers were actively involved in data collection and analysis. During analysis, careful attention was paid to minimizing potential bias. The researchers aimed to remain objective by consistently referring back to the data and following a systematic coding procedure. The researchers are academics holding doctoral degrees and have prior experience in qualitative research. No prior relationship was established between the researchers and the participants before the study. Participants were informed of the research's purpose prior to the interviews.

When Table 2 is examined, most students indicated that friendships and positive bonds with teachers were influential in fostering a sense of belonging to school. Teachers' warm attitudes and trust-building communication were particularly prominent factors. K7 emphasized this by saying, "The teachers' interest in us is very nice, which makes me feel like I belong." However, E20 stated that "when students get low grades on exams, teachers adopt a supportive attitude by saying 'you'll get high grades next time,' which positively affects their credibility." Furthermore, the peer circle's warmth significantly impacts students' positive feelings about school. E9 said, "My friendships contribute to my sense of belonging."

This finding suggests that school belonging is strongly shaped by relational factors within the school environment, particularly the quality of teacher–student and peer relationships. Supportive teacher behaviors, such as providing encouragement after poor academic performance, appear to enhance students’ perceptions of being valued and understood within the school context. This indicates that emotional support from teachers contributes not only to academic motivation but also to students’ psychological attachment to school. Similarly, positive peer relationships reinforce students’ social integration, which is a key component of school belonging.

Students who come to school voluntarily appear to have a more developed sense of belonging. E25 said, " I belong to this school because I wanted to." On K24, she said, "I came to this school willingly and I don't regret it. I can express myself comfortably at school, and this makes me feel like I belong." It is also observed that this sense develops later in students who come at their family's request. K17 explained this situation by saying, "I did not come voluntarily. I came at my family's request and do not feel like I belong."

This finding indicates that students’ sense of school belonging may begin to form even before they actively engage in school life, depending on the degree of autonomy in school selection. Students who voluntarily choose their school appear to develop a stronger psychological ownership of the institution, which facilitates early emotional attachment and identification with the school. In contrast, students who attend the school due to external pressures, such as family decisions, may initially perceive a weaker emotional connection and delayed development of belonging. This suggests that perceived autonomy in school choice plays a significant role in shaping students’ initial attitudes toward school and their subsequent sense of belonging.

Another factor affecting a sense of belonging is the school's structural characteristics. Some participants stated that the disciplinary rules were either too strict or inadequate. E24 explained, "Teachers should put pressure on students for their lessons; they should be more disciplined." K46 stated, “Teachers have high expectations, but there is no classroom environment that supports these expectations. Lessons should be more structured and more intensive.” Furthermore, the school's imam hatip school structure appears to increase the sense of belonging for students with religious sensitivities. E2 stated, “The school’s Imam Hatip background meets my expectations; sharing a common mindset makes me feel a sense of belonging.” Similarly K38 said, "Because it is an imam hatip school, most of us have the same thoughts at this school. We have much common ground."

These findings suggest that students’ sense of belonging is not only shaped by interpersonal relationships but also by the structural and institutional characteristics of the school. Disciplinary expectations and academic organization appear to influence how students perceive the effectiveness and seriousness of the school environment, which, in turn, shapes their emotional connection to the institution. While some students see stricter discipline and higher academic demands as necessary for effective learning, others view shared values and a common worldview within the school context as key sources of belonging. In particular, alignment between students’ personal values and the school’s institutional identity, as in the case of Imam Hatip schools, appears to strengthen students’ identification with the school community.

In addition to the factors that support the development of belonging, several factors that hinder this process were also identified. These factors, while not directly contributing to the formation of belonging, play a significant role in weakening or obstructing it. Negative social interactions, such as bullying, cliques, and exclusion, were found to lead students to withdraw from the school environment. For instance, K8 expressed this by stating, "I do not feel like I belong. There are cliques. " Similarly, teacher-related factors may negatively influence students’ sense of belonging. As K10 noted, "Some teachers are racist.” E26 stated, “Some teachers take a solution-oriented approach to students’ requests. Teachers and administrators need to understand everyone and meet their expectations. "

Supportive and challenging aspects of a school climate that develops school belonging

When Table 3 is examined, the theme most frequently mentioned by students is their relationship with teachers. For example, K1 stated, "We get along with the teachers; they take us more seriously," indicating the positive impact of the teacher's approach. Similarly, E18 expressed the positive contribution of teachers to a sense of belonging by saying, "The teachers are sincere, they tell us what to do, there are very good teachers." On the other hand, K10 touched on the challenging effect of teacher indifference with the sentence, "When you tell some teachers something, they do not even want to listen."

These statements indicate that teacher–student relationships play a central role in shaping students’ sense of school belonging through both emotional and communicative dimensions. When teachers are perceived as approachable, respectful, and attentive, students are more likely to feel valued within the school environment, which strengthens their emotional attachment to the institution. Conversely, perceived teacher indifference or lack of responsiveness may weaken students’ sense of recognition and inclusion, thereby negatively affecting their belonging. This suggests that the quality of interaction, rather than merely the presence of teachers, is a critical factor in fostering a supportive school climate.

Perceiving a supportive and sharing circle of friends increases students' positive outlook on school. For example, K8 said, "I can easily explain things to my friends, and they find solutions. Similarly E20 stated, "Everything in this school is valuable to me. In particular, the sincerity and support of my friends positively affect my sense of belonging." However, K9 expressed the negative impact of exclusion on their sense of belonging by saying, "I was excluded from my group of friends, and that made me very upset."

These findings highlight that peer relationships constitute a key social foundation of school belonging. Positive peer interactions, characterized by support, trust, and mutual assistance, contribute to students’ feelings of acceptance and emotional security within the school environment. Such relationships foster a sense of inclusion, which strengthens students’ overall attachment to school. In contrast, perceptions of exclusion or social rejection can undermine this sense of belonging, leading to feelings of isolation and emotional discomfort.This suggests that peer acceptance and group integration are critical determinants of students’ school-related well-being.

Students who can express themselves freely and voice their perceptions feel that the school environment is safer and more supportive. K13 touched upon the positive impact of free expression on sense of belonging by saying, "I can speak for myself, I can express my perception." In contrast, K39 clearly stated the negative impact of obstacles on the expression of sense of belonging by saying, "When I express my thoughts, the response is bad… I want this school to end. Similarly, "E30 stated, "I do not share my thoughts with the administrators. Administrators who listen to students and understand their situation increase the sense of belonging to the school."

These findings suggest that opportunities for self-expression play a crucial role in shaping students’ perceptions of psychological safety within the school environment. When students feel that their opinions are heard and valued, they are more likely to perceive the school as an open and supportive space, which enhances their sense of belonging. In contrast, negative or dismissive responses from school authorities may discourage students from expressing themselves, leading to feelings of exclusion and emotional detachment. This indicates that communicative openness within the school context is a key determinant of students’ relational integration into the school community.

Activities play an important role in developing a sense of belonging. K5 said, "Conferences are held before Ramadan to raise our awareness." Similarly, E13 stated that the activities increased the sense of unity, saying, "We hold competitions every day, and everyone congratulated us when we won." However, K22 complained about the lack of variety of activities, saying, "There are always religious conferences; it would be better if there were motivational activities." E29 stated, "Basketball courts and tournaments can improve relationships with friends and teachers, and activities should be diversified to increase the sense of belonging."

These findings indicate that school-based activities contribute to students’ sense of belonging by fostering opportunities for shared perceptions and collective participation. Such activities appear to strengthen social cohesion by bringing students together around common goals and perceptions, thereby enhancing their sense of unity within the school community. In this sense, extracurricular and institutional activities function as a bridge between students and the school environment. However, limited diversity in activities may restrict students’ engagement and reduce their emotional connection to the school, suggesting that variety and inclusiveness in school activities are important factors in sustaining students’ sense of belonging.

Being in an atmosphere where common religious values ​​are shared is emphasized as a supportive element for many students. E4 stated, "Being part of the Imam Hatip culture helps us come together on a common ground." K26, "As a result of studying at the imam hatip school, I developed my religious knowledge, which made me very happy," she said, citing the school's religious structure as a supportive element. On the other hand, K38 stated that there are situations where the spiritual atmosphere is perceived as coercive, saying, “There are always Quran lessons, it feels like there is pressure, this is not right.”

These findings suggest that shared religious values within the school environment can serve as a unifying factor, strengthening students’ sense of belonging by fostering a common identity and value system. When students perceive alignment between their personal beliefs and the institutional culture, this shared framework may enhance their sense of integration and emotional attachment to the school. However, the perception of religious practices as overly imposed or coercive may have the opposite effect, potentially weakening students’ sense of autonomy and reducing their emotional comfort within the school. This indicates that the impact of religious atmosphere on school belonging is closely related to students’ perceptions of voluntariness and inclusivity within that environment.

While some students view a calm and orderly school environment positively, a lack of equipment and inadequate social spaces can lead to feelings of loneliness and distance from the school. E20 described the physical environment as supportive: "It is a very quiet school; there is no noise." On the other hand, E27 touched on the challenging aspects of the physical environment, saying, "The heaters were not turned on in the cold, and the classroom facilities were not good."

These findings indicate that the physical environment of the school contributes to students’ sense of belonging by shaping their daily comfort, safety, and overall school perception. A calm and orderly environment may foster concentration and emotional comfort, thereby supporting students’ positive perceptions of the school. In contrast, inadequate physical conditions, such as insufficient heating or lack of facilities, may negatively affect students’ well-being and create feelings of discomfort and neglect. This suggests that the quality of the physical learning environment plays a supportive role in students’ emotional attachment to school, alongside social and relational factors.

Looking back, those activities are all that matters.” Similarly, E3 mentioned, “Tournaments like chess and mangala can be organized. The number of trips can be increased.” K23 also suggested, “Research on the Quran can be conducted. Groups can be formed and a more intimate environment can be created.”

These statements indicate that school belonging is constructed through active participation and shared perceptions, rather than the mere existence of activities. This suggests that students develop emotional attachment to school when they are given opportunities to interact, collaborate, and take part in collective activities. In this sense, school belonging appears to function as a socially constructed experience grounded in engagement and interaction.

Many students also criticized the school's physical structure. K18 stated, “There is no gym. They could make the garden more useful,” while E19 noted, “There is no basketball hoop; it is a waste of time.”

These findings suggest that students interpret physical facilities as an indicator of how much value the school places on student well-being. The lack of adequate physical spaces may reduce students’ perception of school as an engaging and supportive environment, thereby weakening their sense of belonging. Conversely, well-designed and functional facilities may strengthen students’ emotional connection to the school by increasing opportunities for participation and social interaction.

Students expressed that teachers' attitudes significantly influence their sense of belonging. E1 stated, “Our teachers are very supportive of us. Thanks to this, I can spend my time effectively at school.” E13 added, “Teachers have a great influence on my sense of belonging because they are very sincere, and I can express myself without hesitation.” K33 emphasized the need for a more caring and understanding teacher approach.

These findings suggest that teacher-student relationships function as a primary emotional support mechanism within the school environment. Supportive and approachable teacher behaviors appear to reduce students’ communication barriers, thereby facilitating greater classroom engagement and self-expression. This indicates that belonging is not only shaped by instructional practices but also by the quality of interpersonal relationships in the classroom context.

Students also criticized administrative attitudes. K39 stated, “Our principal acts as if she were our boss. I wish they were more lenient.”

These findings suggest that administrative behavior contributes to students’ sense of belonging through its impact on perceived power distance and institutional accessibility. Authoritative or distant leadership styles may create psychological distance between students and the school, whereas more supportive leadership may enhance students’ sense of inclusion and institutional trust. This highlights that school belonging is also shaped by macro-level leadership practices, not only classroom interactions.

Intense academic expectations can make it difficult for students to form emotional bonds with the school. K38 stated, “There is pressure that everyone in this school must be successful.” Similarly, K18 noted, “We memorize the Quran in class out of fear. It would be better if the pressure were reduced.” In contrast, E29 emphasized the positive role of teacher support by saying, “The interest of the teachers increases my desire to study.”

These findings suggest that academic expectations shape students’ sense of belonging through their influence on how they perceive the school as either supportive or pressure-driven. Excessive academic pressure may weaken students’ emotional attachment to school by creating an environment perceived as performance-oriented rather than relational. In contrast, perceived academic support and guidance enhance students’ sense of being valued members of the school community, thereby strengthening their identification with the school. This indicates that school belonging is closely linked to how academic demands are balanced with supportive educational relationships.

Discussion

The primary purpose of this research is to determine students' views on school climates that develop school belonging in religious-based schools. Within the scope of this main purpose, the research examines the factors that are effective in the formation of students' sense of belonging to school, the supportive and compelling elements for the formation of a school climate that develops school belonging, and what can be done to create a school climate that develops school belonging in religious-based schools under 3 main questions. Using a content analysis, this study provides valuable insights into the factors that affect students' sense of belonging to school. The findings obtained from the research are examined under three headings.

First question: " What are students’ perceptions of the factors associated with their sense of belonging in faith-based schools?” Four main themes emerged: Social Relationships, Adaptation to School, Institutional Characteristics of the School, and Inhibitory Factors (see Fig. 1). Under the Social Relationships theme, there are categories such as positive relationships with teachers and friends. Uslu and Gizir [73] revealed that teacher-student relationships, peer relationships, and family involvement significantly affect adolescents' sense of belonging to school. Furthermore, G. Johnson et al. [33] argue that cultural values provide an important context for shaping perceptions of belonging. This perspective is particularly relevant to the present findings, as students described their relationships within an Imam Hatip school characterised by shared values and a common institutional identity. The "teacher-student relationship" occupies a central position in the construction of belonging and the mitigation of risks. While İbrahim and El Zaatari [38] state that supportive interactions promote emotional attachment, Raufelder and Kulakow [60] have shown that strong social belonging and teacher support are the most important factors preventing the development of learned helplessness in adolescents. Gowing [35] argues that peer relationships are a fundamental mechanism for building school belonging. According to the study's results, positive peer interactions build an emotional bridge between the student and the school, increasing institutional commitment, while a lack of social support triggers the risk of alienation from school. In the present study, however, students' accounts suggest that these supportive relationships were experienced within a faith-based institutional environment. Rather than describing friendships and teacher support solely as positive interpersonal experiences, participants also emphasised a shared understanding and common values, indicating that social relationships were reinforced by the school's institutional religious culture. The research shows that the effect of positive peer support on school belonging strengthens further with age. Saggers et al. [65] state that positive peer interactions are not only a social need but also a fundamental mechanism that enables a student to participate in the school community and feel valued fully. While these findings are consistent with the broader literature, the present findings suggest that supportive teacher and peer relationships in this study were experienced within the institutional context of an Imam Hatip school. This interpretation is supported by participants' descriptions that the Imam Hatip background of the school "meets my expectations" and that students "have the same thoughts," suggesting that interpersonal relationships were strengthened by shared values embedded in the school's institutional religious culture, rather than reflecting only a positive school climate.

Allen et al. [1] concluded that the quality of student–teacher relationships strengthens students' sense of belonging to school and psychological well-being. Fan and Bellmore [31] found that high-quality friendships among high school students are an important factor in increasing the sense of belonging to school. Similarly, Ibrahim and El Zaatari [38] emphasized that trust-based teacher-student relationships strengthen students' emotional attachment to school. A systematic review revealed that the most consistent and powerful variables influencing school belonging in teacher-student relationships are teachers' interest, trust, and high expectations, which directly shape students' emotional connection to the school and their perception of institutional belonging [54]. Under the main theme of social relationships, supportive interactions with teachers and peers emerged as important contributors to school belonging. However, participant accounts indicate that these relationships gained additional meaning within the Imam Hatip school context, where students described a shared worldview and common values. This suggests that social relationships did not operate solely as universal dimensions of positive school climate but were embedded within the school's institutional religious culture. Consequently, students' sense of belonging appears to have been strengthened not only through interpersonal support but also through identification with the school's faith-based institutional identity.

Under the theme of Adaptation to School, the categories of coming to school voluntarily and under family pressure are included. One of the important determinants of school belonging is school adjustment, as students who experience successful adaptation processes are more likely to develop stronger connections with their schools. Liu et al. [51] found that perceived social support improves adolescents' school adaptation by increasing their sense of belonging to school,physical activity also plays a regulatory role in this process. Family relationships also emerged as an important factor in students' adjustment and belonging. Deniz et al. [25] showed that healthy family communication contributes positively to adolescents' psychological well-being and school belonging. Another finding is that students who are willing to find meaning in school and set goals there develop a sense of belonging to the school. In the present study, this finding is particularly important because students' willingness to attend the school appeared to be related not only to academic expectations but also to their perceptions of the school's identity and values. Previous studies have similarly demonstrated that a strong sense of belonging supports adolescents' psychosocial and academic adjustment [61, 80], while supportive family relationships provide an important foundation for developing attachment to school [59]. In line with these findings, the present study suggests that school adaptation is shaped by the interaction between students' personal motivations, family expectations, and their perceptions of the school's institutional identity. For students attending a faith-based school, adjustment may involve not only becoming familiar with the school environment but also developing a sense of compatibility with the values and expectations represented by the institution.

Under the theme of the Institutional Features of the School, the Impact of the Imam Hatip structure and the categories of discipline/rules (positive/negative) have been developed. Koç [46, 47], bstates that students who voluntarily choose their schools and have clear educational goals develop stronger institutional belonging. This finding is consistent with the present study, as students' willingness to attend Imam Hatip schools appeared to be associated with their expectations and perceptions of the school's identity. Arini [3] stated that implementing local religious practices in schools strengthens students' sense of belonging. Gencer [34] noted that Imam Hatip High School students have a high level of school belonging, and that belonging is significantly related to students' reasons for choosing the school and their future goals, rather than gender or academic achievement. The findings of the present study suggest that the institutional characteristics of Imam Hatip schools contribute to students' sense of belonging through more than general school climate factors. Participants' references to the school's structure, discipline, and shared values indicate that belonging was shaped by students' perceptions of compatibility between their own expectations and the institutional identity of the school. Therefore, the Imam Hatip context appears to function not only as an educational setting but also as a value-based institutional environment in which students develop a sense of connection and identification.

Students' views on discipline were closely related to their perceptions of fairness, trust, and belonging within the school community. Kim et al. [44] showed that disciplinary practices at the primary school level significantly weaken students' sense of school belonging and that these punitive actions have long-term negative effects on the bond between the student and the institution. Bora et al. [14], in their study based on TIMSS data, found that school principals' perceptions of discipline and teachers' perceptions of safety are decisive factors in students' sense of school belonging. In the present study, students' perceptions of discipline appeared to be linked not only to rule enforcement but also to the sense of order, trust, and shared expectations within the Imam Hatip school environment. Thus, disciplinary practices may contribute to belonging when they are perceived as consistent with the institutional values and expectations of the school community.

Within the research's theme of "Inhibitory Factors," bullying and exclusion stand out as key risk factors that impair school belonging (see Fig. 1). A review of the literature reveals that peer bullying directly targets not only individual well-being but also institutional ties. Li et al. [50] and Xu and Fang [79] found that exposure to bullying negatively affects students' subjective well-being and academic commitment, while school belonging serves as a mediating and protective factor, buffering these negative effects. Similarly, Arslan [4] emphasized that school belonging is a critical mechanism that mitigates internalization and externalization problems stemming from bullying in young people. Especially in vulnerable groups such as international students, school belonging constitutes the fundamental dynamic of psychological and academic adjustment [81]. Overall, the present findings indicate that bullying and exclusion threaten students' sense of belonging by weakening their connection with the school community. In a faith-based educational context, creating an inclusive environment that reflects the school's values may be particularly important for preventing feelings of alienation and strengthening students' identification with the institution.

The second question, six main themes were identified based on the responses to the question " How do students perceive the school climate factors that contribute to or challenge their sense of belonging in faith-based schools?", and the sub-categories of Teacher-Student Relationship, Friendship Relationships, Freedom of Expression, Events and Social Activities, Religious Atmosphere, Physical Environment, and Facilities were created (see Fig. 2). It is evident that these main themes overlap with those derived from the answers to the third question, " What are students’ views regarding practices and conditions that may strengthen their sense of belonging in faith-based school settings?" Therefore, the themes of the second and third questions were considered together. The students were asked the third question, and four subcategories were identified: Social Events, Teacher and Administrative Attitudes, Physical Facilities, Academic Climate, and Courses. While some themes, including teacher–student relationships, friendship relationships, and physical environment and facilities, overlap with the broader dimensions of school belonging discussed in the first part of the discussion, the present section focuses on the aspects that provide more specific insight into the faith-based school context. In particular, students’ experiences regarding religious atmosphere, freedom of expression, participation in school activities, and academic climate are further considered to understand how institutional culture and daily school experiences shape belonging in faith-based settings.

Based on participants' responses to the second question (the Events and Social Activities subcategory) and to the third question (the Social Events subcategory), increasing the frequency of social events and improving physical facilities are critical supporting mechanisms for strengthening school belonging. For example, Bouchard et al. [15] found that such activities strengthen peer bonding and increase students’ sense of belonging by creating opportunities for social interaction and participation. Similarly, O’Donnell et al. [55] emphasized that extracurricular activities can support students’ emotional adjustment, particularly among adolescents with weaker feelings of belonging. Not only organized club activities but also unstructured time periods play an important role in building belonging. Whiting et al. [77] found that social opportunities during leisure time, such as lunchtime, directly foster students' institutional commitment by reducing social isolation. In faith-based school contexts, these activities may also provide spaces where students experience shared values and develop a sense of community. However, participants’ emphasis on the need for more diverse activities suggests that school belonging is strengthened not only through activities reflecting the institution’s religious identity but also through inclusive opportunities that allow students with different interests to participate actively. The findings from the "Freedom of Expression" subcategory of the research show that students' ability to freely express their feelings and thoughts to teachers or the school administration has a significant positive effect on school belonging. The literature considers the perception of freedom of expression not only as a form of communication but also as a cornerstone of institutional identity and student commitment. For example, King-Kostelac [45] states that freedom of expression is closely associated with school belonging, emphasizing that a school climate where students feel heard and respected encourages institutional commitment and active participation. In conclusion, creating a free environment for expression within the school, free from hierarchical barriers, makes students feel valued and accepted, acting as a democratic lever in building institutional belonging.

Participants indicated that institutional dynamics, such as teacher attitudes, guidance services, management approach, and academic climate, are fundamental supporting mechanisms for building school belonging and minimizing potential hindering factors (see Table 4). The findings, shaped within this framework, parallel relational and managerial theories in the literature. Elements such as sincerity, understanding, and supportive roles, highlighted under the main theme of Teacher Attitudes and Communication, determine the quality of student–teacher interaction. Allen et al. [1] emphasized that the quality of this interaction strengthens school belonging, which is directly related to academic achievement and psychological well-being. Similarly, Virat et al. [75] revealed that teachers’ compassionate and respectful approaches not only increase belonging but also positively shape students’ perceptions of justice, which is particularly important in school contexts where interpersonal relationships reflect broader institutional values. On the other hand, students’ perceptions of unsupportive or restrictive attitudes may create barriers to developing a strong sense of belonging. Guidance and management mechanisms constitute the institutional guarantee of belonging. Shi and Watkinson [67] state that guidance services serve as a critical bridge in building a sense of belonging, especially among disadvantaged groups (foreign language learners, etc.),while Tuxbury and Gibson [72] found that supportive interventions by guidance units regarding academic processes reinforce students' self-efficacy perceptions and institutional commitment. At the administrative level, the perception of "shared leadership" emphasized by Jang and Lee [39] proves that a participatory school environment provides a democratic ground that fosters students' sense of belonging. Overall, school belonging appears to emerge through the interaction of teacher support, accessible guidance services, and participatory administrative practices.

Faith-based schools differ from secular educational settings in that teachers’ roles often extend beyond academic instruction to include pastoral care, spiritual guidance, and character formation. Within these contexts, teachers may also be perceived as individuals who represent the religious values and moral principles underlying the institution’s mission [17, 37]. As a result, teacher–student relationships may take on a deeper relational and value-based dimension, fostering trust, emotional security, and a shared sense of purpose. Research has consistently shown that supportive relationships with teachers are among the strongest predictors of students’ sense of belonging, engagement, and well-being [1, 2, 57]. In faith-based contexts, these effects may be particularly pronounced because students experience teacher support not only as academic assistance but also as holistic mentorship aligned with the school’s religious ethos. Therefore, the strong association between teacher support and school belonging observed in our study suggests that students may derive a heightened sense of acceptance, security, and community from relationships with teachers who embody and reinforce shared values and beliefs.

The positive impact of peer relationships on school belonging can be assessed within the context of shared institutional culture and social identity processes. Faith-based schools my provide provide students with a shared framework of beliefs, values, and practices that extends beyond academic life and shapes daily social interactions. Participation in activities and practices reflecting the school's religious culture may strengthen interpersonal bonds and reinforce a shared sense of community [6, 68]. From a sociological perspective, such environments can function as a “sacred canopy” that provides meaning, harmony, and social integration, thereby fostering stronger peer bonds and a greater sense of community [11]. Furthermore, social identity theory suggests that belonging to a group characterized by shared values and norms can enhance feelings of acceptance, inclusion, and emotional attachment [70]. Consequently, the significant relationship observed in our study between peer support and school belonging may reflect the extent to which faith-based school environments foster cohesive peer networks grounded in shared moral and spiritual commitments. These value-based social bonds can provide students with vital emotional support and foster a more inclusive, supportive school environment.

While strict discipline is often associated with reduced autonomy and may sometimes undermine students’ sense of belonging in general school settings, our findings may suggest a somewhat different pattern within faith-based schools. This finding may reflect the distinctive way in which discipline is framed and experienced in religious educational environments. In faith-based schools, behavioral expectations are frequently embedded within broader moral and spiritual frameworks that emphasize character formation, self-regulation, responsibility, and concern for others rather than mere compliance with authority [5, 12]. As a result, school rules may be interpreted by students as expressions of a shared ethical commitment and collective responsibility rather than as punitive restrictions. Research on school climate further indicates that students are more likely to develop a sense of belonging when disciplinary practices are perceived as fair, supportive, and consistent, fostering trust and psychological safety within the school community [71, 76]. Moreover, educational environments characterized by clear norms and shared values can strengthen social cohesion and reinforce students’ identification with the school community [10]. Consequently, the positive association between discipline and school belonging observed in our study may suggest that students in faith-based schools perceive discipline as a mechanism that promotes order, security, and communal well-being. In such settings, discipline may foster school belonging by reinforcing a predictable environment grounded in shared moral expectations and mutual respect. The findings regarding the religious atmosphere suggest that the role of institutional culture in shaping belonging is not uniform but depends on how students experience and interpret the school's religious identity. Some participants perceived the religious environment as a source of shared meaning, connection, and community, indicating that alignment between students’ values and the school culture may strengthen institutional belonging. However, other participants described certain religious practices as restrictive, suggesting that belonging may weaken when institutional values are experienced as imposed rather than personally meaningful. Therefore, the contribution of religious ethos to school belonging appears to depend not only on the presence of religious practices but also on students’ perceptions of inclusion, voluntariness, and personal identification with the school culture.

Conclusion

To create a school climate that enhances school belonging in faith-based schools, attention should be given to how students experience the school’s religious culture, shared values, and interpersonal relationships. Felice [32] examined the sense of belonging, spirituality, and general perceptions of students from different ethnic backgrounds in religious-based schools,she revealed that common spiritual values ​​and inclusive religious practices strengthen students' sense of belonging to the school, and the integration of cultural identity and religious support systems contributes to students feeling more valued. Khan and Imam [43] found that students' sense of belonging in full-time Islamic schools in the USA was strengthened by the sharing of religious values, teacher relationships, and a supportive school environment, and that this sense positively affected academic and social development. Krug [48], in his study investigating cooperation and sense of belonging in an Islamic school, emphasized that school personnel played a critical role in increasing students' and employees' sense of belonging. As a result, the development of belonging in religious-based schools is a complex process that depends on the quality of social relationships and on the school's cultural and structural characteristics. This study reveals that to increase a sense of belonging to the school, it is necessary to strengthen teachers' sincere and supportive attitudes, encourage student participation, diversify social activities, and create an inclusive school climate that is sensitive to student differences.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that while the core pillars of school belonging (such as teacher and peer support) remain universal, their operational mechanisms in faith-based schools are deeply intertwined with the institution's religious ethos and shared moral values. Future policies in faith-based education should focus on sustaining these unique mentorship styles and community practices rather than relying solely on generic school climate interventions.

Limitations

This research employed a qualitative design and has limitations related to the study group's characteristics and the sampling method. First, the appropriate sampling method was used in the research. Since this method is based on the researcher selecting the individuals he can most easily reach and include in the data collection process, the study group's ability to represent the research universe is limited. This situation reduces the generalizability of the findings and allows inferences specific to the sample group only. The study includes only students from two schools that provide religious education in the province of Sakarya, limiting the study's spatial and institutional diversity. The fact that most participants were female students also created an imbalance in the gender distribution, making it challenging to make gender-based comparisons. In addition, only the perceptions of high school students were consulted, thereby excluding the perspectives of other age groups (e.g., middle school or university students).

Although the principle of data saturation, which is frequently used in qualitative research, was followed in the data collection process, because its application is subjective, there may be criticisms of data adequacy. The lack of details regarding the interview process (e.g., interview duration, level of structure) may raise questions about the depth and reliability of the data obtained. Since the participants are studying in religious schools, this may affect their value judgments and forms of expression; there are limitations to the generalizability of the findings across different types of schools. In addition, since the researcher's interpretive role is decisive in qualitative data analysis, the risk that the researcher's biases and assumptions are reflected in the interpretation of the data should be considered. Providing brief, neutral explanations of key concepts before the interviews may have slightly shaped participants’ initial understanding of the topics; however, care was taken to avoid directing responses or providing examples. Another limitation affecting data validity is that students may give insincere answers during the interview due to a desire for social acceptance or because they feel pressured by the school administration's and teachers' indirect influence. All these elements indicate that caution should be exercised when interpreting research findings.

The findings of this study should be interpreted in light of its contextual characteristics. The data were collected from a relatively small sample of students from two schools within a specific context. While this is consistent with qualitative research design, it may limit the transferability of the findings to other settings. The characteristics of the participating schools and students may have influenced the results. Therefore, caution should be exercised when applying these findings to different educational contexts with varying demographic, institutional, or cultural features. To support transferability, detailed descriptions of the research context and participants have been provided, allowing readers to determine the extent to which the findings may be applicable to their own settings. Future research should employ mixed-methods approaches or use faith-based, context-specific scales to capture the depth of the religious ethos's interaction with student belonging.
