Feasibility, agreement, and time savings of PHANTOM open-field binocular integrated refraction versus conventional clinical refraction in healthy young adults
Authors: Yo Iwata, Rie Hoshikawa, Takushi Kawamorita, Tomoya Handa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPHANTOM is a novel open-field binocular integrated refraction system that uses device-specific binocular target presentation and lensless refractive simulation to perform objective refraction, subjective refraction, and visual acuity testing in one device. However, evidence is limited regarding how corrective refraction derived from such systems compares with prescriptions obtained using conventional subjective refraction combined with binocular balancing. We aimed to compare refractive correction determined using the conventional method with that obtained using PHANTOM and evaluate its potential role as an adjunctive tool for spectacle prescription.MethodsWe compared PHANTOM with a conventional clinical refraction workflow. Twenty healthy young adults (20.3 ± 0.9 years) were subjected to both conditions in a randomized order, with examinations performed by one experienced orthoptist; right eye data were analyzed. Conventional refraction comprised monocular autorefraction plus subjective refraction with prism-dissociated duochrome binocular balancing. Agreement (PHANTOM–conventional) was assessed using the Bland–Altman analysis.ResultsObjective spherical equivalent (SE) was − 3.09 ± 2.25 D vs. −3.10 ± 2.24 D (bias 0.02 D; 95% limits of agreement [LoA] − 0.55 to 0.58). Subjective SE was − 2.78 ± 2.41 D vs. −2.79 ± 2.39 D (bias 0.01 D; LoA − 0.56 to 0.58) with no proportional bias. Proportional bias was detected in objective J45 (p = 0.01) and subjective J0 (p = 0.006). Visual acuity was − 0.17 ± 0.02 vs. −0.16 ± 0.04 logMAR (bias − 0.01; LoA − 0.10 to 0.08). Examination time was 199.7 ± 33.9 vs. 527.6 ± 70.0 s (61.6% shorter; p < 0.0001).ConclusionsIn healthy young adults under controlled study conditions, participants completed the integrated PHANTOM protocol and achieved visual-acuity endpoints comparable to those obtained with conventional refraction. Under these conditions, SE agreement was generally good and the examination time was substantially reduced, whereas astigmatism components warrant further investigation. Further validation in broader clinical populations is required before these findings can be generalized.

Background

Visual acuity and refraction testing are among the most frequently performed examinations in ophthalmic practice [1, 2]. In addition to visual acuity assessment, objective and subjective refractions are essential for determining spectacle and contact lens prescriptions. Because inappropriate refractive correction may cause blurred vision, asthenopia, reduced visual performance, and lower patient satisfaction [3–10], accurate and reproducible prescription decisions are crucial. However, final prescription determination in subjective refraction depends on examiner skill and experience and requires a certain level of expertise [11, 12]. Systems automating part or the entire refraction process have been developed to address this issue, and their effectiveness has been reported [13–15].

Conventionally, subjective refraction is performed monocularly, with the fellow eye occluded to minimize binocular interaction and the influence of fusional convergence. However, monocular occlusion can alter accommodation and shift refraction toward myopia in both objective [16, 17] and subjective [18] refraction. Because spectacles and contact lenses are worn under binocular viewing, assessment under binocular open-view conditions is physiologically preferable. In routine clinical practice, the conventional workflow therefore includes binocular balancing after monocular subjective refraction to equalize the accommodative state between the two eyes under binocular viewing [19, 20]. Thus, it is clinically important to consider not only isolated refraction steps but also agreement with the full conventional prescription workflow, including binocular balancing, when evaluating a new binocular open-view system.

There is an increasing interest in all-in-one devices that can sequentially perform objective refraction, subjective refinement, and visual acuity testing under binocular open-view conditions [15, 21]. Recently, PHANTOM (NIDEK), a novel binocular open-view device, was developed. Following objective refraction under binocular open viewing, PHANTOM enables integrated subjective refraction and visual acuity testing while maintaining binocular viewing within the instrument. By combining binocular target presentation, a fogging mechanism to reduce accommodative involvement, and an in-device subjective refraction module, PHANTOM is designed to obtain clinically relevant refractive information within a single workflow.

Although previous studies have reported the usefulness of binocular open-field refraction and automated refraction systems, it remains unclear whether the refractive correction obtained with an integrated binocular open-view system agrees with the final prescription derived from the full conventional clinical workflow, rather than from isolated refraction steps alone. In addition, because PHANTOM integrates multiple examination steps within one device, its effect on examination time is also clinically relevant. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the agreement in the refractive outcomes between PHANTOM and the conventional refraction workflow, including binocular balancing, and assess whether PHANTOM can improve examination time efficiency in a clinical workflow, thereby clarifying its potential role as a tool to support spectacle prescription.

Methods

The participants were 20 healthy young adults (20.3 ± 0.9 years, 19–22 years) with no ocular disease other than refractive error. Routine ophthalmic examination was performed before enrollment. We excluded individuals with structural abnormalities of the cornea, lens, retina, and other ocular structures; those with strabismus; or those who did not achieve 40 s of arc on the Titmus stereo test. The ocular deviation measured using the alternate prism cover test was − 2.20 ± 2.21 (− 8 to + 1) prism diopters (PD).

The primary outcome for sample-size planning was the agreement in subjective spherical equivalent (SE) between PHANTOM and the conventional workflow. Agreement was assessed using the Bland–Altman method, with the 95% limits of agreement (LoA) defined as the mean paired difference ± 1.96 × the standard deviation (SD) of the paired differences. The sample size was determined on the basis of the precision of the estimated LoA boundaries rather than on a null-hypothesis significance test. Assuming an SD of the paired differences of 0.30 D, based on the most comparable previous study available at the study-planning stage [15], we calculated the minimum number of independent paired measurements required such that the half-width of the two-sided 95% confidence interval for each LoA boundary would be ≤ 0.25 D, using the standard Bland–Altman approximation for the confidence interval of the LoA boundary. This yielded a minimum required sample size of 20 independent pairs. As measurements from the two eyes of the same participant are not independent, one prespecified eye (the right eye) was used for the primary analysis.

For each participant, a spectacle prescription assuming full-distance correction was determined under the following two conditions:

Condition 1 (refraction using PHANTOM)

Objective and subjective refraction under binocular open-view conditions using PHANTOM.

Condition 2 (conventional refraction)

conventional objective and subjective refraction (including binocular balancing).

For both conditions, the examinations were performed by an orthoptist with > 10 years of clinical experience. To avoid confounding device comparisons with inter-examiner differences, the same examiner conducted both tests. Because subjective refraction includes examiner-dependent judgments and participant guidance, examiner experience may influence both the refractive endpoint and examination time. Therefore, although the use of a single experienced orthoptist reduced inter-examiner variability in this device comparison, it also limited the generalizability of the findings to less-experienced examiners and other clinical settings. To minimize the order and learning effects, the order of the two conditions was randomized for each participant using Excel-generated random numbers. In addition, the recording forms were separated so that the measurements from each condition were not cross-referenced at the time of testing, thereby reducing the possibility of arbitrary adjustments based on the results. Because PHANTOM and the conventional workflow used different devices and examination procedures, masking of the examiner to the test condition was not feasible. In addition, although the recording forms were separated to reduce direct cross-reference, the same examiner necessarily remained aware of the study objectives and of having performed the alternative procedure in the same participant. Therefore, examiner-dependent bias affecting subjective decision thresholds and examination time could not be completely excluded. A 5-min rest period was provided between conditions to minimize carryover effects on perception. The room illuminance was maintained at 600–800 lx.

Condition 1: Examination procedure using PHANTOM

Under Condition 1, objective refraction, subjective refraction, and visual acuity testing were performed sequentially under binocular open-view conditions using PHANTOM. The PHANTOM is an open-field refraction system that measures objective and subjective refraction while maintaining binocular viewing without an occluder in front of the eyes. Its external appearance is shown in Fig. 1. The device comprises two measurement units, one for each eye (Fig. 2a). Each unit integrates an objective-measurement subsystem and a subjective-refraction/presentation subsystem, and the measurement and presentation beams are relayed to the examined eye via a concave mirror (Fig. 2b). This configuration enables refractive measurements under fully binocular, open-field conditions without any obstruction in front of the eyes. To compensate for ocular movements during measurement, each unit includes an anterior-segment observation camera. Eye position is detected from the anterior-segment image, and a three-axis tracking mechanism moves the measurement unit so that it follows the examined eye. Objective refraction is based on the same ring-image principle used in conventional autorefractors. The measurement beam emitted from the illumination source is reflected by the ocular fundus, and the returning beam passes through a ring lens in the detection optics to form a ring image on a two-dimensional sensor. Spherical power, cylindrical power, and cylinder axis are calculated from the ring-image size, the degree of distortion, and the distortion angle [22]. Representative ring-image patterns corresponding to different refractive states are shown in Fig. 2c. The fixation target for objective refraction is projected from both units and is perceived by the participant as a single binocular target. Binocular fogging can also be applied, and objective refraction can be measured simultaneously in both eyes. During objective refraction, pupil diameter is measured automatically from the anterior-segment images, and interpupillary distance is calculated from the tracking positions of the two units. Subjective refraction is performed using LCD displays and dedicated presentation optics in each unit. The chart-presentation optics include a movable stage and a cylindrical-lens unit. Spherical power is adjusted by translating the stage, whereas cylindrical power and axis are adjusted by the cylindrical-lens unit. In this way, the refractive state corresponding to a corrective lens can be optically simulated without physically placing trial lenses in front of the eye. The objective and subjective optical paths are combined coaxially by a dichroic mirror (Fig. 2b) and guided to the examined eye. By presenting the test and fusion targets to the examined eye and only the target background and fusion target to the fellow eye, the device enables monocular testing while maintaining binocular fusion (Fig. 3).

First, the objective refraction was measured simultaneously in both eyes. The obtained values were then applied with an additional + 0.50 D of fogging, a red–green target was presented, and subjective refraction was performed one eye at a time while maintaining binocular viewing. This initial + 0.50 D fogging step was incorporated because objective refraction obtained with autorefractors is generally more myopic than subjective refraction, particularly in young participants, owing to accommodative involvement. Therefore, subjective refinement was started from a slightly fogged state to reduce accommodative influence and avoid over-minus responses. During subjective refraction, the optical viewing distance was 5 m. The distance between the participant’s eye and the concave mirror was approximately 60 cm. Because participants were positioned using a forehead rest, large variations in this distance were unlikely during measurement. However, the sensitivity of the measurements to small variations in this distance was not independently quantified in the present study. To avoid overcorrection (excessive minus addition) and to minimize unnecessary accommodative involvement in young participants, we repeatedly instructed participants verbally during the examination to “not try hard to see, but respond while naturally fixating on the distance target.” This instruction was intended to standardize participant responses and suppress voluntary accommodative effort as much as possible. Although its quantitative importance was not independently evaluated in the present study, it was considered important for reducing over-minus responses during subjective refinement. For red–green judgment, participants were instructed to respond based on which side the black optotype appeared darker/clearer rather than the brightness of the background color, in accordance with a previous report [23]. Next, for astigmatic correction, an additional + 0.50 D of fogging was applied, and an astigmatism chart was presented to correct the astigmatism. This additional fogging step was used as part of the astigmatism-testing procedure to bring the posterior focal line closer to the retina, thereby enhancing awareness of astigmatic blur. Finally, a Landolt ring was presented, and visual acuity was measured. At this stage, the refraction was repeated to obtain the weakest correction that achieved the best visual acuity. The entire series of tests was performed under binocular open-view conditions. The subjective refraction for the PHANTOM condition was determined based on the spherical power, cylindrical power, and axis obtained using these procedures (Fig. 4). The subjective-refraction sequence under PHANTOM was performed according to the device-specific integrated protocol under binocular open-view conditions. Accordingly, astigmatism was refined using the in-device astigmatism chart after a fixed + 0.50 D fogging step, rather than by reproducing the conventional cross-cylinder procedure. This approach was chosen to evaluate PHANTOM as used in its intended clinical workflow, not to isolate individual refraction substeps under matched procedural conditions. Therefore, differences in the astigmatism-determination procedure and fogging strategy between PHANTOM and the conventional workflow were prespecified features of the study design and should be considered when interpreting agreement, particularly for J0 and J45.

Condition 2: Conventional objective and subjective refraction (Conventional)

Under Condition 2, assuming a standard clinical workflow, objective refraction was first measured in one eye at a time using an autorefractor (ARK-1s, NIDEK). Pupil diameter was measured simultaneously with objective refraction using the ARK-1s. Subsequently, subjective refraction was performed using a visual acuity chart presenting Landolt rings (VC-60, TAKAGI), with objective refraction values used as a reference, and the prescription assuming full-distance correction was determined (test distance: 5 m). The cross-cylinder method was used to determine the cylindrical power. Binocular balancing was performed using the prism-dissociated duochrome technique. The specific procedure for the prism-dissociated duochrome technique has been described previously [20]. The examiner was instructed to perform the procedure by assuming a spectacle-prescription workflow.

Binocular fogging with plus lenses

Plus lenses were added in + 0.25-D increments until the participant reported that the letters on the red background appeared clearer.

Prism dissociation

A 3Δ base-down prism was added to the right eye and a 3Δ base-up prism to the left eye, and a vertically dissociated red–green chart was presented. The upper and lower images corresponded to the right and left eyes, respectively.

Adjustment of the right-eye image (upper image)

With the participant fixating on the upper image (right eye) and starting from a condition in which the red side appeared clearer, spherical power was reduced in − 0.25-D steps. The first point at which the participant reported equal clarity of the red and green letters was defined as the right-eye endpoint.

Adjustment of the left-eye image (lower image)

Using the same procedure, the lower image (left eye) was adjusted, and the point at which the red and green letters appeared equally clear was defined as the left-eye endpoint.

Prism removal and binocular fine-tuning

After prism removal, + 1.00 D of fogging was added to both eyes. With both eyes open, the plus lenses were then reduced simultaneously in 0.25-D steps until the maximum binocular visual acuity was achieved; this point was defined as the binocular endpoint for determining the final prescription.

Using the above procedure, the final spherical power, cylindrical power, and axis for Condition 2 were determined, and visual acuity was measured.

Statistical analysis

As the primary outcome, the agreement in SE obtained via PHANTOM and the conventional method (standard examinations performed in real-world conditions) was evaluated. For each comparison (PHANTOM−conventional), the mean difference (bias) and 95% LoA were calculated using the Bland–Altman analysis. For the primary SE analysis, approximate normality of the paired differences was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The proportional bias was assessed by regressing the difference (PHANTOM−conventional) on the mean of the two methods and testing whether the regression coefficient (slope) differed from zero using a t-test for the regression coefficient. For the astigmatic components, the cylindrical power (C) and axis (A) were converted into power vectors (J0 and J45) [24]. For J0 and J45, the mean difference (bias) and 95% LoA were calculated using the Bland–Altman analysis. Proportional bias was assessed by regressing ΔJ0 (or ΔJ45) on mean J0 (or mean J45) and evaluating the slope with a t-test. Furthermore, visual acuity (logMAR) and pupil diameter were evaluated for inter-device agreement (bias and 95% LoA) using the Bland–Altman analysis. The examination time (from seating on the first device until the completion of all tests) was compared between PHANTOM and the conventional method using a paired t-test (two-sided). For hypothesis-based analyses (paired t-test for examination time and regression tests for proportional bias), the significance level was set at p < 0.05 (two-sided). This significance threshold was not used for the sample size calculation, which was precision-based. All statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel (Social Survey Research Information Co., Ltd.).

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the School of Allied Health Sciences, Kitasato University (approval number: 2025-029; approval date: November 13, 2025). All procedures were performed in accordance with the approved guidelines, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before the study.

Results

The results of this study are summarized in Table 1. The objective SE was − 3.09 ± 2.25 D with PHANTOM and − 3.10 ± 2.24 D with the conventional method. The Bland–Altman analysis for objective SE showed a mean difference (bias) of 0.02 D (95% CI − 0.12 to 0.15), which indicates a small average difference. The 95% LoA were − 0.55 to 0.58 D (Fig. 5a). When the difference was regressed on the mean as a test for proportional bias, the regression coefficient (slope) was 0.001 (95% CI − 0.06 to 0.07) and did not differ significantly from zero (p = 0.97), which indicated no proportional bias.

The subjective SE was − 2.78 ± 2.41 D with PHANTOM and − 2.79 ± 2.39 D with the conventional method. The Bland–Altman analysis for subjective SE showed a mean difference (bias) of 0.01 D (95% CI − 0.13 to 0.14), which indicated a small average difference that was similar to that of the objective SE. The 95% LoA were − 0.56 to 0.58 D (Fig. 5b). In the test for proportional bias, the regression coefficient (slope) was 0.01 (95% CI − 0.05 to 0.07) and did not differ significantly from zero (p = 0.71), which indicated no proportional bias. The paired differences in subjective SE demonstrated a slight deviation from normality (Shapiro–Wilk W = 0.897, p = 0.036).

Astigmatism was evaluated after conversion to a power vector (J0/J45). For objective astigmatism, J0 was 0.27 ± 0.37 D with PHANTOM and 0.28 ± 0.35 D with the conventional method. For objective J0, the mean difference (bias) was − 0.01 D (95% CI − 0.08 to 0.07), indicating a small mean difference. The 95% LoA were − 0.33 to 0.31 D (Fig. 6a). In the test for proportional bias, the slope was 0.05 (95% CI − 0.18 to 0.28) and did not differ significantly from zero (p = 0.65). For objective astigmatism, J45 was − 0.04 ± 0.24 D with PHANTOM and − 0.10 ± 0.20 D with the conventional method. For J45, the mean difference (bias) was 0.06 D (95% CI 0.02 to 0.09), and the 95% LoA were − 0.10 to 0.21 D (Fig. 6b). In the test for proportional bias, the slope was 0.19 (95% CI 0.04 to 0.34) and was statistically significant (p = 0.01), suggesting proportional bias for the J45 component. Although this proportional bias was statistically significant, the absolute mean bias was small (0.06 D). Therefore, the finding should be interpreted with reference not only to the p value but also to the magnitude of the bias and the 95% LoA. For subjective astigmatism, J0 was 0.26 ± 0.37 D with PHANTOM and 0.26 ± 0.27 D with the conventional method. For the subjective J0, the mean difference (bias) was 0.01 D (95% CI − 0.08 to 0.09), whereas the 95% LoA were − 0.35 to 0.36 D (Fig. 6c). In the test for proportional bias, the slope was 0.34 (95% CI 0.11–0.57) and differed significantly from zero (p = 0.006), which suggested proportional bias wherein the difference varies with the amount of astigmatism. However, the mean bias itself was close to zero (0.01 D), whereas the 95% LoA (− 0.35 to 0.36 D) indicated participant-level variability. Thus, the statistical significance of the proportional bias should be distinguished from the clinical interpretation of the average between-method difference. For subjective astigmatism, J45 was − 0.05 ± 0.23 D with PHANTOM and − 0.05 ± 0.19 D with the conventional method. For J45, the mean difference (bias) was 0.002 D (95% CI − 0.06 to 0.07), and the 95% LoA were − 0.27 to 0.28 D (Fig. 6d). In the test for proportional bias, the slope was 0.20 (95% CI − 0.13 to 0.52) and was not significant (p = 0.22), which indicated broadly comparable agreement for the J45 component.

The examination times were compared using a paired t-test (two-sided). The examination time was 199.7 ± 33.9 s with PHANTOM and 527.6 ± 70.0 s with the conventional method (mean ± SD). The mean difference (PHANTOM−conventional) was − 327.9 s (95% CI − 362.7 to − 293.0), and the examination time was significantly shorter with PHANTOM (p < 0.0001). The mean reduction rate was 61.6% (95% CI 57.9–65.3%), which indicated a substantial time-saving effect. At the individual level, examination time was shorter with PHANTOM in all participants. The within-participant time difference (PHANTOM−conventional) ranged from − 469 to − 201 s, with a median of − 316.5 s (interquartile range, − 395.5 to − 285.25 s). The reduction rate ranged from 43.0% to 73.1%, with a median of 62.0% (interquartile range, 56.6% to 68.3%).

Discussion

In this study, the primary outcome, SE, showed small mean differences and no proportional bias for both objective and subjective measurements, indicating good agreement between the two methods. Traditionally, refraction is measured when the non-measured eyes are occluded. However, spectacles and contact lenses are worn during binocular viewing, and there is an inherent discrepancy between the testing condition (monocular occlusion) and the use condition (binocular viewing). One aspect of this discrepancy is that pupil diameters differ substantially between binocular viewing and monocular occlusion [25]. In the present study, the pupil diameter was evidently smaller with PHANTOM than with the conventional method. Moreover, although fusion is maintained under binocular viewing, the ocular alignment under monocular occlusion reflects a dissociated position. Therefore, binocular conditions and the contributions of convergence and accommodation differ. In conventional workflows, binocular balancing has been positioned to compensate for potential interocular accommodative imbalances that originate from such differences in the conditions [19, 20], whereas PHANTOM adopts a different approach in that the entire measurement process proceeds under binocular open-view conditions.

The finding that the mean difference in the subjective SE obtained with PHANTOM was small relative to the final SE determined using the conventional method, including binocular balancing, suggests that PHANTOM may provide broadly comparable information for the SE under the present study conditions. However, the Bland–Altman LoA for subjective SE (− 0.56 to 0.58 D) suggests participant-level variability in the between-method differences, which may reflect inter-individual factors and/or measurement variability. Moreover, as the paired differences in subjective SE demonstrated slight deviation from normality, the parametric confidence intervals around the LoA should be interpreted with some caution. Although the average agreement was good, the between-method difference in individual participants was not negligible, and the clinical usefulness of PHANTOM should be interpreted in relation to the practical tolerance limits for spectacle prescription, such as ± 0.50 D. Accordingly, in clinical practice, especially for patients with anisometropia or strabismus, confirmation using conventional methods or supplementary examinations should be considered when needed. Importantly, despite PHANTOM’s device-specific binocular target presentation and lensless refractive simulation, participants were able to complete the integrated subjective protocol and achieve endpoints broadly comparable to those obtained with the conventional workflow. These findings suggest that PHANTOM may be useful as a support tool within the clinical workflow, rather than as a standalone substitute for the conventional final prescription process.

Differences were observed in the astigmatic components between PHANTOM and the conventional method. These differences should be interpreted primarily in the context of methodological differences between the two workflows, rather than as evidence of an intrinsic device-related effect alone. First, the procedure used to determine astigmatism differed between PHANTOM and the conventional method. The conventional method used the cross-cylinder technique, whereas PHANTOM used an astigmatism chart; thus, the target presentation and endpoint criteria were not identical. Second, PHANTOM transitioned to subjective testing under uniform + 0.50 D fogging from the objective value, whereas the conventional workflow used a different fogging sequence and binocular fine-tuning. Because the fogging amount can influence the astigmatic endpoint and the participant’s perception of blur, these procedural differences are likely to have contributed, at least in part, to the observed proportional bias in objective J45 and subjective J0. In general, the fogging amount is frequently set to approximately one-half of the expected residual astigmatism by positioning the posterior focal line on the retina to maximize awareness of astigmatism [26]. Therefore, a fixed amount of fogging may not be equivalent across participants with different astigmatism magnitudes and axes. At the same time, PHANTOM also differs from the conventional workflow in its device-specific binocular target presentation and lensless refractive simulation; therefore, a contribution of intrinsic device-related effects cannot be excluded. However, because the present study was designed to compare overall clinical workflows rather than to isolate each component, the relative contributions of methodological differences and device-related effects cannot be determined from the current data. In the power-vector analysis, proportional bias was detected in the objective J45 component and subjective J0 component. In young adults, with-the-rule astigmatism is common; therefore, the distribution of astigmatic axes may be biased, which can cause information to concentrate on J0 in the power-vector representation. Consequently, differences may be detected more readily at J0 than at J45 in subjective measurements, particularly when the dataset contains relatively few eyes with large oblique components. Future work should include comparisons in which the target, procedure, and fogging strategy for astigmatism assessment are matched as closely as possible between methods, as well as within-PHANTOM comparisons in which the logic for determining the astigmatic endpoint is modified, to clarify which factors most strongly drive the observed proportional bias in objective J45 and subjective J0.

Regarding repeatability, as this study was designed to focus on the agreement between PHANTOM and the conventional method, we did not evaluate test–retest variability based on repeated measurements in the same participants. However, agreement with a conventional method does not necessarily imply measurement reliability. For clinical implementation, it is essential to quantify the variability of repeated measurements obtained under the same conditions, because prescription support requires not only agreement on average but also stable within-device performance. Therefore, the absence of repeatability data is a major limitation of the present study and constrains the interpretation of PHANTOM’s clinical applicability. Previous reports have indicated that the measurement environment and device design can affect reproducibility. For example, a study comparing a binocular open-field wavefront autorefractor with a monocular closed-field wavefront autorefractor and calculating the intersession repeatability reported that the binocular open-field system showed better repeatability [16]. In this context, future work should systematically evaluate the reproducibility of PHANTOM, including test–retest repeatability, within-session repeatability and inter-operator reproducibility, and assess the reliability of SE and astigmatic components.

From an efficiency standpoint, PHANTOM significantly reduced the examination time compared with that in the conventional method, with an average reduction of approximately 62%. This is likely because objective refraction, subjective refraction, and visual acuity assessment can be performed sequentially using a single device, reducing ancillary tasks such as moving between examination rooms, device setup, lens exchange, and transcription of results. In addition, PHANTOM provides guided protocols within the device, which may contribute to the standardization of procedures and reduce examiner workload. In clinical environments with limited human resources and settings with high throughput, such as health checkups and screening, the ability to perform refraction and visual acuity assessments in an integrated, short time may be advantageous. As a practical consideration, unlike conventional autorefractors, PHANTOM does not provide a corneal mire image that the examiner can use to visually judge tear-film stability during objective refraction. Therefore, in individuals with tear-film instability or ocular-surface dryness, additional attention (encouraging blinking and repeating measurements as needed) may be required, although this cue is less directly observable with PHANTOM. However, in this study, measurements were performed by an orthoptist with > 10 years of clinical experience, who provided verbal instructions to suppress accommodative interventions. Thus, it remains unclear whether comparable measurement accuracy and workflow advantages can be obtained by less-experienced examiners and non-specialists. Future studies should evaluate the effects of examiner experience and develop training and education protocols.

This study had some limitations. First, test–retest repeatability of PHANTOM was not evaluated. Because agreement with a conventional method does not necessarily establish measurement reliability, the absence of repeatability data is a major limitation when interpreting the clinical applicability of PHANTOM. Future studies should assess within-session repeatability, test–retest repeatability, and inter-operator reproducibility. Second, the participants were limited to healthy young adults, and the external validity in older populations or those with ocular diseases was limited. In addition, the refractive-error distribution in this cohort was strongly skewed toward myopia, with very few hyperopic participants. This likely reflects, at least in part, the fact that the study population consisted of healthy young Japanese adults. At the same time, objective refraction obtained with autorefractors is generally known to be more myopic than subjective refraction because of accommodative involvement, particularly in young participants. Therefore, the absence of positive objective SE values in this study may have reflected both the population characteristics and accommodative influences during objective measurement. However, the present study was not designed to determine the relative contribution of population characteristics, accommodative tendency, and instrument-related measurement effects, and these possibilities should therefore be interpreted cautiously. The additional fogging step should not be interpreted as a consequence of the low frequency of hyperopia in the sample itself. Rather, it was incorporated as part of the protocol to reduce accommodative involvement and avoid over-minus responses in young participants. Because the present study included only healthy young adults with relatively uncomplicated refractive profiles, it remains unclear whether a similar magnitude of examination-time reduction would be observed in broader clinical populations, such as older adults, patients with presbyopia, ocular pathology, or reduced visual acuity. The pupil diameter is known to decrease with age [27], and accommodative amplitude decreases with age [28]. Therefore, the effects of differences in pupil size and accommodative states between binocular viewing and monocular occlusion may differ according to age, and studies stratified by age, including presbyopic groups, are warranted. In addition, none of the participants exhibited a large ocular deviation. In particular, when exophoria is large, accommodation may be influenced by fusion and convergence, and refractive power may differ between binocular viewing and monocular occlusion; therefore, further investigation is required. Third, the present comparison assessed the overall clinical workflow using PHANTOM versus the conventional method and was not designed to isolate the pure effect of “binocular open-view vs. monocular occlusion” alone. Future studies could directly evaluate the influence of binocular viewing on refraction and astigmatic endpoints by comparing binocular open-view and monocular occlusion conditions within PHANTOM. Fourth, because the sample size was designed based on the SE, the distribution of refractive status may not have been fully represented. The assumed SD was derived from the most comparable prior study available at the planning stage; however, that study involved a different population and refractive-error distribution, which limits the transportability of this assumption. Stratified analyses based on clinically acceptable tolerances and validation across a wide refractive range are required. Furthermore, although using the same examiner for both conditions avoided confounding introduced by inter-examiner differences, the examiner could not be masked to the device condition or study purpose. In addition, complete masking to prior measurements within the same participant was not feasible, even though the recording forms were separated. Therefore, examiner expectations, relative familiarity with each technique, and learning effects may have influenced subjective decision thresholds and examination time. To further reduce these effects, future studies should consider designs in which multiple examiners perform both conditions, or use separate evaluators and masking procedures where feasible.

Conclusions

This study showed that in healthy young adults under controlled study conditions, despite PHANTOM’s device-specific binocular target presentation and lensless refractive simulation, participants were able to complete the integrated subjective protocol and achieve visual-acuity endpoints comparable to those obtained with the conventional workflow. Compared with the conventional method, PHANTOM demonstrated generally good agreement in SE and substantially reduced examination time. However, differences remained in the astigmatic components, which may be attributable to differences in measurement conditions and procedures. Repeatability was not evaluated in this study. Future work should examine astigmatism assessment under controlled measurement conditions and systematically evaluate PHANTOM’s repeatability and reproducibility in broader clinical populations to further clarify its clinical validity and applicability as a device to support spectacle prescriptions.
