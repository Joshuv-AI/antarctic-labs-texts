Small GTPase Rac promotes hyphal formation and microconidiogenesis in Trichophyton rubrum
Authors: Masaki Ishii, Shinya Ohata
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMorphogenesis plays a pivotal role in the infection process of Trichophyton rubrum, a primary aetiological agent of dermatophytosis that inhabits superficial human tissues. T. rubrum proliferates by extending filamentous structures, or hyphae, which are composed of highly polarized cells. In response to environmental stimuli, T. rubrum also produces asexual spores called microconidia, consisting of individual cells. Although these dynamic morphological changes are critical for T. rubrum proliferation and environmental adaptation, the molecular mechanisms underlying these processes remain poorly understood. In previous research, we demonstrated that repressing Cdc24, a guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) for the small GTPases Rac and Cdc42, disrupts fungal cell polarity and impairs hyphal formation in T. rubrum. In this study, we show that Rac deficiency in the Δrac strain minimally affects hyphal formation, as indicated by the cell polarity index (the ratio of a cell’s long to short diameter in hyphae). However, simultaneous Rac deficiency and Cdc42 repression in the Δrac/Pctr4 cdc42 strain significantly disrupted cell polarity, suggesting that Rac and Cdc42 perform overlapping functions in hyphal morphogenesis. Interestingly, Rac deficiency inhibited microconidia formation, whereas cdc42 repression had no detectable impact. Furthermore, adding cysteine, a radical scavenger abundant in keratins, to the growth medium reduced microconidia production in the wild-type strain but not in the Δrac strain. These findings suggest that cysteine in host tissues inhibits Rac-mediated microconidia formation. Overall, this study identifies Rac as a key regulator of T. rubrum morphogenesis, with specific roles in both hyphal development and microconidia formation.

Introduction

Trichophyton rubrum, the most common fungal cause of dermatophytosis, including athlete’s foot, predominantly resides in human superficial tissues [1]. The morphogenesis of fungal cells is essential for infection and is influenced by various cellular functions, including nutrient responses, cytoskeletal dynamics, and intracellular signalling [2]. In response to environmental changes, T. rubrum undergoes structural transitions into two distinct cellular morphologies: hyphae and microconidia. Understanding the molecular mechanisms controlling fungal cell morphogenesis is crucial not only for studying the physiological functions of primitive eukaryotic cells but also for developing novel drugs to inhibit morphogenesis associated with fungal infections.

The growth of T. rubrum occurs alongside the elongation of filamentous cell structures called hyphae. While hyphal elongation is critical for T. rubrum to invade and colonize host tissues [3,4], the precise molecular mechanisms remain unclear. Recently, our research demonstrated that Cdc24, a guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) for the small GTPases Rac and Cdc42, promotes hyphal cell morphology in T. rubrum [5]. Inhibiting cdc24 expression not only impairs hyphal morphogenesis but also inhibits fungal growth on culture media [5]. Furthermore, under Rac-deficient conditions, repression of cdc42 expression causes similar growth inhibition on culture media, comparable to the effects observed when cdc24 expression is suppressed [5]. These findings suggest that Rac and Cdc42 may regulate hyphal cell morphogenesis in T. rubrum. However, direct evidence for the involvement of Rac and Cdc42 in promoting hyphal cell morphogenesis is still lacking.

Microconidia are spherical or droplet-shaped cells with a single nucleus and serve as the primary asexual reproductive structures of T. rubrum on culture media. Various roles for Rac and Cdc42 in fungal morphogenesis have been reported. In the endophytic fungus Epichloë festucae, Cdc42 and Rac are involved in hyphal fusion and fungal cell structure within host tissues [6]. In Neurospora crassa, RAC-1 is essential for cell fusion and the formation of conidial anastomosis tubes (CAT), which are hyphal structures extending opposite the germ tube on the conidia. CDC-42 has been shown to be both necessary and sufficient for normal germ tube development in Neurospora crassa [7]. These studies indicate that Rac and Cdc42 participate in diverse processes of fungal cell morphogenesis. However, the role of Cdc42 and Rac in microconidiogenesis in T. rubrum remains unknown.

The morphogenesis of T. rubrum is influenced by environmental factors, and microconidia formation is inhibited in the host environment [8,9]. The primary components of skin, nail, and hair are keratin and keratin-associated proteins, which are rich in cysteine. For instance, the cysteine concentration in human nails is approximately 15% [10], and hair protein extract contains 11.2 mol % cysteine [11]. Proteinases (keratinases), major virulence factors in dermatophytes, degrade these proteins in superficial tissues, releasing large amounts of cysteine. It has been demonstrated that T. rubrum takes up the released cysteine and uses it to facilitate keratin degradation [12]. However, whether cysteine plays a role in inhibiting microconidia formation remains uncertain.

In this study, we investigated the roles of CDC42 and Rac in cell morphogenesis in T. rubrum, including hyphal and microconidial formation. We also found that cysteine inhibits Rac-mediated microconidial formation. Our study identifies both a T. rubrum gene and a host factor that regulate cellular morphogenesis in this dermatophyte fungus.

Materials and methods

Fungal strains and culture conditions

Trichophyton rubrum CBS118892, a clinical isolate from a patient’s nail sample, was obtained from Westerdijk Fungal Biodiversity Institute and used as the wild-type (WT) strain [13]. All mutant strains used in this study were generated previously [5]. These strains were cultured at 28°C on Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA; 1% Bacto peptone, 4% glucose, 1.5% agar, pH unadjusted).

Observation of cell morphology by fluorescent microscope

Cell morphological analysis was performed according to the previous study [5]. Briefly, fungal cells were stained with 0.2 mm calcofluor white (Fluorescent Brightener 28; Merck, Germany). The stained cells were observed under a fluorescent microscope Olympus B×53(Japan). The length of the cell diameter was measured using ImageJ software. The polarity index was calculated as the length, which was measured at the longest diameter of a cell, divided by the width, measured at the midpoint of the length and perpendicular to the longest diameter.

Microconidia formation assay

Microconidia of T. rubrum were prepared as described previously [14,15]. Briefly, 2.0 × 106 conidia were spread onto 1/10 Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) (0.1% [weight/volume, wt/vol] Bacto peptone, 0.2% [wt/vol] glucose, 0.1% [wt/vol] KH2PO4, and 0.1% [wt/vol] MgSO4 · 7 h2O) supplemented with 10 μM CuSO4 or 20 μM bathocuproinedisulfonic acid (BCS). To assess the effect of cysteine, 1 mm cysteine was added. After 14 days of incubation at 28°C, the microconidia were scraped from each agar medium using 20 ml of 0.05% Tween 80/0.90% NaCl and a conlage stick. The resulting suspension was filtered through a 40 µm cell strainer to collect the microconidia, which were then counted using a haemocytometer.

Statistical analysis

The means of the two groups were compared using Student’s t-test. For three or more groups with a single variable, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test was conducted. For comparisons involving three or more groups with two variables, two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test was performed. Statistical analyses were conducted using Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software, Boston), and statistical significance was defined as a p-value of < 0.05.

Use of generative AI

We used DeepL (https://www.deepl.com) and Claude (https://claude.ai) for language improvement.

Results

Compatibility of Cdc42 and Rac in hyphal morphogenesis

We previously generated a T. rubrum strain in which the copper ion transporter promoter, Pctr4, was inserted upstream of the cdc24 open reading frame (hereafter referred to as Pctr4 cdc24) [5]. In this strain, cdc24 is expressed in the absence of copper ions and repressed in their presence [5]. When copper ions are present (causing cdc24 repression), the cells of the Pctr4 cdc24 strain exhibit an elliptical form rather than a filamentous form as observed in WT (CBS118892 strain) [5]. To investigate the potential roles of Cdc42 and Rac in regulating cell shape, we compared hyphal morphologies under rac deficiency in the Δrac strain and cdc42 repression in the Pctr4 cdc42 strain with those of the WT and Pctr4 cdc24 strains. As previously reported [5], the WT exhibited elongated hyphae, whereas the Pctr4 cdc24 strain cultured with copper ions showed pronounced hyphal swelling (Figure 1a). This disruption in hyphal morphology was further quantified by calculating the polarity index (major axis length/minor axis length), revealing a significant deviation from the WT (Figure 1b). Notably, neither rac deficiency nor cdc42 repression alone caused substantial changes in hyphal morphology or the polarity index (Figure 1a, b), despite both being activated by Cdc24 during guanine nucleotide exchange reactions [5]. To explore whether rac and cdc42 complement each other, we examined hyphal morphology in the Δrac/Pctr4 cdc42 strain cultured with copper ions (causing cdc42 repression). Interestingly, the combination of rac deficiency and cdc42 repression led to a pronounced disruption in hyphal morphology and a significant reduction in the polarity index (Figure 1a, b). When cdc24 or cdc42 expression was induced in the Pctr4 cdc24, Pctr4 cdc42, and Δrac/Pctr4 cdc42 strains by adding BCS (a copper chelator), polar growth and hyphal formation were restored (Figure 1c). Combined with our previously reported biochemical finding that Cdc24 promotes the guanine nucleotide exchange reaction of Rac and Cdc42, the present results suggest that Rac and Cdc42 have overlapping roles downstream of Cdc24 in promoting hyphal morphogenesis. Promotion of microconidiogenesis by Rac

To investigate whether Cdc42 and Rac are involved in cell morphogenesis beyond hyphal formation in T. rubrum, we compared the number of microconidia among the WT, Δrac, Pctr4 cdc42, Pctr4 cdc24, and Δrac/Pctr4 cdc42 strains (Figure 2a-c). The number of microconidia was significantly reduced in the Δrac strain compared to the WT strain (Figure 2a). No significant differences in microconidia formation were observed among the strains after cultivation with BCS, except for the Δrac/Pctr4 cdc42 strain, which lacks rac (Figure 2b). Furthermore, there was no difference in microconidia formation between the WT and Pctr4 cdc42 strains in the presence of copper ions (causing cdc42 repression in Pctr4 cdc42 strain) (Figure 2c). We also attempted to measure conidiogenesis in Pctr4 cdc24, and Δrac/Pctr4 cdc42 strains. However, under the suppression of cdc24 or cdc42 gene expression with copper ions in the agar medium, sufficient growth could not be observed, and microconidia formation was not observed. These findings suggest that Rac plays a critical role in microconidia formation. Cysteine inhibits microconidia formation in Trichophyton rubrum

Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase regulator NoxR, a fungal homolog of p67phox that enhances reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, is one of the most well-characterized effectors downstream of Rac [16,17]. ROS generation by NADPH oxidases NoxA and NoxB has been implicated as a crucial factor in promoting conidia formation in the phytopathogenic fungus Alternaria alternata. [18] Cysteine, a common biological ROS scavenger, is a major amino acid component of keratins and keratin-associated proteins, which make up mammalian superficial tissues. To examine the effect of cysteine on microconidiogenesis, we assessed microconidia formation in media supplemented with cysteine. In the WT strain, the number of microconidia decreased significantly with higher cysteine content in the medium (Figure 3a). In contrast, the Δrac mutant strain did not exhibit a cysteine-dependent reduction in microconidia formation (Figure 3b). These results suggest that cysteine suppresses microconidia formation by inhibiting the Rac-mediated pathway. Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that T. rubrum Rac promotes both hyphal formation and microconidiogenesis. Furthermore, we showed that cdc42 and rac in T. rubrum have functionally compatible roles in hyphal morphogenesis (Figure 3c). The cooperative regulation of hyphal cell morphogenesis by Rac and Cdc42, which is essential for dermatophyte growth, explains previous observations indicating that both Rac and Cdc42 are required for mycelial growth (Figure 3c) [5]. Conversely, microconidia formation was inhibited in the Δrac strain but not in the Pctr4 cdc42 strain cultured with copper ions. This finding raises the possibility that Cdc42 is not involved in microconidia formation. However, even when cdc42 expression was repressed in the Pctr4 cdc42 strain, cdc42 mRNA expression was detectable by RT-PCR [5]. Therefore, it is also possible that minimal cdc42 expression is sufficient for microconidia formation. Further analysis is needed to determine the precise role of Cdc42 in microconidia development.

Identifying the effectors of Rac and Cdc42 is an important future research direction because these small GTPases regulate morphogenesis by activating downstream effectors. In hyphal morphologies, the p21-activated kinase (PAK), a downstream effector shared by Cdc42 and Rac, has been implicated as a critical regulator across various fungal species [19]. We recently reported that the p21-activated kinase Cla4 interacts with Rac and promotes mycelial growth and hyphal branching [20]. The mycelial growth inhibition observed in the Δcla4 strain is significantly less severe compared to the Δrac/Pctr4 cdc42 or Pctr4 cdc24 strains cultured with copper ions [5,20]. Moreover, the hyphal cell morphology of the Δcla4 strain is comparable to that of the WT strain, without the pronounced morphological defects seen in the Δrac/Pctr4 cdc42 and Pctr4 cdc24 strains [5,20]. These observations suggest that, in addition to Cla4, other effectors essential for hyphal morphogenesis contribute significantly to the regulation of mycelial growth. Another candidate downstream factor of Rac in T. rubrum morphogenesis is NoxR, which promotes ROS production [6]. Fungal Rac-deficient or suppressed strains exhibit reduced ROS production [21,22]. Adding cysteine, a ROS scavenger abundantly present in keratinized host tissues, to the culture medium partially inhibited microconidia formation in T. rubrum (Figure 3a). This inhibition was not observed in Rac mutant strains (Figure 3b). Thus, ROS generation may explain the role of Rac in microconidia formation in T. rubrum. Further investigation is required to clarify this mechanism.

Hyphae represent the predominant cell morphology of T. rubrum in both culture media and host tissues. In contrast, microconidia, the characteristic microstructures of T. rubrum, readily form on culture media but not within host tissues [8,9]. Compared to arthroconidia, which form in the host environment, microconidia are more susceptible to chemical stress [23]. This study demonstrates that cysteine, a component of proteins in superficial tissues such as keratin, inhibits microconidiogenesis [10,11]. Proteinases (keratinases), the primary virulence factors of T. rubrum [24], are believed to degrade cysteine-rich proteins, releasing substantial amounts of cysteine. These findings offer insight into the physiological responses of pathogens in host environments and contribute to understanding fungal pathogenesis mechanisms. However, the inhibitory effect of cysteine on microconidiogenesis observed in this study is relatively modest. The microconidia formation medium used contains Bacto peptone, which includes peptides and amino acids, such as cysteine, potentially confounding the results. Further research using a synthetic medium devoid of cysteine is needed to clarify the impact of cysteine on microconidiogenesis.

Our findings on the roles of Rac and Cdc42 in T. rubrum morphogenesis align with and expand upon observations in other fungal species [25–27], while also revealing unique functional attributes. In filamentous fungi such as Aspergillus nidulans and N. crassa, Cdc42 and Rac have been shown to cooperatively regulate polarized growth and hyphal development, though with distinct emphases on particular morphological processes [25,26]. Our observation that Rac and Cdc42 function redundantly in T. rubrum hyphal formation represents an integrated functional relationship between Rac and Cdc42 in T. rubrum hyphal elongation. Furthermore, the Rac-specific regulation of microconidiogenesis in T. rubrum contrasts with findings in Aspergillus niger, where both Cdc42 (CftA in A. niger) and RacA contribute to asexual sporulation [27]. The cysteine-mediated inhibition of Rac-dependent microconidiation suggests an environmental sensing mechanism that may be particularly relevant to dermatophytes adapted to keratin-rich host environments [10,11]. These comparative insights indicate that while the fundamental roles of Rho-family GTPases in regulating fungal morphogenesis are conserved across species, their specific functional allocations and regulatory mechanisms may have evolved to suit the ecological niches and life cycles of individual fungal pathogens.

Our findings establish Rac as a key regulator of morphogenesis in T. rubrum, functioning as a molecular switch that orchestrates both hyphal development (through overlapping pathways with Cdc42) and microconidiogenesis (potentially through ROS-dependent mechanisms). The discovery that host-derived cysteine inhibits Rac-mediated microconidia formation provides a compelling explanation for the observed absence of microconidia in infected tissues and demonstrates how this pathogenic fungus adapts to the host environment. These insights enhance our understanding of the molecular mechanisms governing fungal morphological transitions during infection and identify the Rac signalling pathway as a potential target for novel antifungal therapies. Future studies should focus on characterizing specific downstream effectors of Rac in T. rubrum and their interactions with host-derived molecules to further elucidate the signalling networks that regulate morphological plasticity in dermatophytes.
