Whispers of Many Shores: Cultural Alignment through Collaborative Cultural Expertise
Shuai Feng, Wei-Chuang Chan, Srishti Chouhan, Junior Francisco Garcia Ayala, Srujananjali Medicherla, Kyle Clark, Mingwei Shi

Abstract
The integration of large language models (LLMs) into global applications necessitates effective cultural alignment for meaningful and culturally-sensitive interactions. Current LLMs often lack the nuanced understanding required for diverse cultural contexts, and adapting them typically involves costly full fine-tuning. To address this, we introduce a novel soft prompt fine-tuning framework that enables efficient and modular cultural alignment. Our method utilizes vectorized prompt tuning to dynamically route queries to a committee of culturally specialized ’expert’ LLM configurations, created by optimizing soft prompt embeddings without altering the base model’s parameters. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our framework significantly enhances cultural sensitivity and adaptability, improving alignment scores from 0.208 to 0.820 (cf.Table 1 ), offering a robust solution for culturally-aware LLM deployment. This research paves the way for subsequent investigations into enhanced cultural coverage and dynamic expert adaptation, crucial for realizing autonomous AI with deeply nuanced understanding in a globally interconnected world.

Introduction

Against the backdrop of globalization and technological advancement—particularly with the rise of large language models (LLMs)
—communication and interaction have been profoundly transformed, not only in terms of understanding mutual language expressions but also in capturing the subtle emotional nuances embedded within specific language use. Yet, as various LLMs are being increasingly integrated into routine applications in personal and professional scenarios, alignment of generated content with cultural norms is increasingly scrutinized due to broad audience impact. Hence, how to guarantee the content generated that aligns with cultural criteria presents a critical issue.

Despite remarkable capabilities in generating instantaneous modality, such as text and images, current state-of-the-art LLMs frequently reflect cultural biases inherent in their predominantly Western-centric training datasets
(
Seo et al. 2025
)
, limiting their effectiveness in multicultural interactions and potentially causing misunderstandings or cultural insensitivity. Public access to low-resource data remains a daunting challenge. Under such circumstances, large-scale human annotation—especially for different cultural contexts—is nearly impossible. Another pain point in cultural alignment lies in tailoring large language models to diverse cultural contexts. The challenge extends beyond conforming to singular cultural norms, values, and expressions; it also involves navigating cross-cultural and hybrid cultural dynamics.

2
Related Works

2.1
Prompts enhancement

Recent advancements in culturally-aware AI emphasize the critical importance of aligning large language models (LLMs) with nuanced cultural contexts. Prompt fine-tuning, a popular category within LLM alignment methods, offers a flexible and resource-efficient alternative to traditional fine-tuning approaches. Prompt fine-tuning can be further categorized into hard prompt tuning, soft prompt tuning, and reinforcement-based prompt tuning.

Hard prompt tuning involves optimizing discrete prompts that guide the behavior of pre-trained models without modifying their parameters. Conversely, soft prompt tuning optimizes continuous prompt embeddings, enabling more nuanced control over model outputs.
Masoud et al. 2025
introduce an innovative cultural alignment approach using soft prompt tuning combined with Differential Evolution (DE). Their method addresses the non-differentiable nature of cultural alignment objectives derived from complex survey data, a significant limitation of traditional supervised or reinforcement learning methods. By freezing core model parameters and optimizing only prompt embeddings, this approach significantly enhances alignment efficiency and reduces overfitting.

Wang et al. 2025
critically examine limitations in LLMs used for simulating human behaviors, highlighting challenges such as the absence of intrinsic psychological states, insufficient diversity in training data, and inherent biases. Despite these challenges, they emphasize LLMs’ scalability and cost-efficiency, advocating for refined cultural representations to enhance realism in culturally diverse applications.

Reinforcement-based prompt tuning methods integrate reinforcement learning principles to dynamically adapt prompt tuning strategies based on performance feedback. Liu
Liu 2025
presents the Reinforcement Learning Free Agent (RLFA) algorithm within a mixture-of-experts framework, dynamically replacing underperforming agents to maintain high adaptability and performance. Inspired by professional sports dynamics, this method fosters continuous system improvement, crucial for maintaining cultural sensitivity in evolving contexts.

Furthermore,
Guan et al. 2025
propose the Deliberative Alignment method, which employs explicit chain-of-thought reasoning combined with supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning. This explicit reasoning approach substantially enhances models’ adherence to safety and ethical guidelines, improving interpretability and robustness, essential qualities for culturally sensitive interactions.

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Stiennon et al. 2022
)
is another significant alignment strategy, involving labeling responses based on human preference to train a reward model guiding agent behavior. More recently, Reinforcement Fine Tuning (ReFT) introduced by OpenAI and Bytedance
(
Luong et al. 2024
)
, replaces the reward model with a verifier, enhancing alignment through explicit verification and reasoning capabilities, thereby supporting more coherent chain-of-thought outputs.

Compared to reinforcement learning-based alignment methods, soft prompt fine-tuning offers greater flexibility, reduced computational costs, and easier implementation, making it highly suitable for efficient and adaptive cultural alignment applications.

2.2
Sensitivity Content generated by LLM

As LLMs are trained on vast and diverse datasets, they may inadvertently reproduce or amplify cultural stereotypes, biases, or inaccuracies, particularly when the training data includes underrepresented or misrepresented communities. The generation of culturally sensitive content by large language models (LLMs) has prompted increasing scrutiny, particularly in cross-cultural deployments. Studies such as
Blodgett et al. 2020
have highlighted how NLP systems, including LLMs, often perpetuate dominant cultural ideologies, marginalizing underrepresented linguistic and cultural groups. Similarly,
Fleisig et al. 2024
demonstrated that LLMs exhibit biases, particularly along cultural lines, revealing risks in global deployment. The scarcity of culturally diverse training data—especially in low-resource settings—has been flagged by
Joshi et al. 2020
, who emphasize the limitations in language coverage and the resulting skewed cultural representation. To address alignment challenges,
Zhou et al. 2022
introduced modular architectures that decouple language understanding from cultural reasoning, enabling fine-grained adaptation. Furthermore,
Ganguli et al. 2023
explored cultural value misalignment in LLMs and the importance of personalization in generative outputs. In terms of sensitivity detection,
Madaan et al. 2022
and
Carlini and Terzis 2021
have demonstrated the utility of contrastive and unsupervised methods for identifying sensitive or harmful content, suggesting viable paths beyond rule-based moderation. These findings collectively motivate approaches that support scalable, interpretable, and culturally grounded sensitivity control in LLM systems.

2.3
Cutural-Awareness Agent

Designing an agent capable of creating responses that fit multiple cultures requires an understanding of pluralistic alignment methods proposed by the research community. Pluralistic alignment refers to developing AI systems capable of representing diverse human values, even if such values sometimes diverge
(
Sorensen et al. 2024
)
. This necessitates alignment techniques beyond RLHF that suppress individuality by averaging. LLMs fail to do this out of the box as they tend to reason about individualistic human preference with about 60% accuracy
(
Jiang et al. 2024
)
. Multiple research efforts have been directed to solve this issue, making LLMs more adept at reasoning about individual preferences: both PERSONA
(
Castricato et al. 2024
)
and ValuePrism
(
Sorensen et al. 2024
)
generate synthetic personas based on US census data to improve pluralistic alignment of language models. Of more interest to this work are research efforts that occur at the agent orchestration layers, such as Modular Pluralism
(
Feng et al. 2024
)
or Plurals
(
Ashkinaze et al. 2025
)
, where LLM agents are made to represent the values of a group to improve group-decision making as a whole. We derive inspiration from these methods when designing our multi-agent system to produce responses that adapt to the cultures of the user who requests it.

Culturally-Aware Automatic Workflow

This section presents our culturally adaptive response generation framework(cf.figure.
1
), offering a comprehensive account of the system’s architecture, algorithmic design, and evaluation methodology. The proposed system operationalizes the hypothesis that personalized cultural representations, when integrated with a modular agent-based architecture, significantly enhance the contextual relevance and cultural sensitivity of generative outputs.

3.1
Workflow Overview

Our system aims to generate culturally-aligned responses by processing user profiles and queries through a multi-agent architecture, which detects sensitivities, identifies cultural context, selects experts, and synthesizes final outputs.
The framework is structured as a multi-agent pipeline that incorporates user profiles, topic understanding, cultural sensitivity detection, expert reasoning, and response synthesis. User data—including responses to the World Value Survey
(
AlKhamissi et al. 2024
)
and persona metadata
(
Castricato et al. 2024
)
—is embedded using the
mxbai-embed-large
model
(
Lee et al. 2024
;
Li and Li 2023
)
, selected for its efficacy in producing semantically rich and linguistically coherent representations across diverse cultural contexts. This embedding serves as the foundation for downstream reasoning.

The workflow consists of the following components:

1.

Input Embedding:
Converts user profiles, questionaire, and options variables into dense vectors capturing dimensions such as values, beliefs, region, age, and social class. This step ensures that user identity is preserved in subsequent semantic computations.

2.

Sentopic Agent (Sensitivity Detection):
We implement a cultural sensitivity detection agent using an LLM-as-judge prompt, achieving 95% accuracy in testing .

3.

Topic Extraction Agent:
To extract cultural context for expert routing, utilizes a LLM to extract potentially insensitive topics from user input. It identifies core subject areas prone to cultural misunderstanding, stereotypes, or biases (e.g., Religious Attire, Gender Roles, Racial Bias). The agent is guided by a prompt that provides rich context and examples, returning a set of topics separated by slashes (”/”). These topics form a high-level abstraction of sensitive dimensions in the input and support downstream routing and evaluation.

4.

Planner Agent:
Integrates semantic features from user embeddings and extracted cultural topics, constructing a unified context vector. This vector is passed to the router for expert matching and later supports consistency control during expert response composition.

5.

Router-based Cultural Expert Activation:

A dedicated routing module computes a merged representation of the user profile and the topical context to identify the most culturally aligned expert agents. This fusion is evaluated against static embeddings of expert personas, which encode culturally grounded traits such as values, norms, and communicative styles. Top-k experts are selected based on alignment in the embedding space, and fallback strategies ensure robust selection under uncertainty. The final set of experts is softmax-weighted and the selected experts are then used to generate culturally aligned responses. This unified routing and activation mechanism ensures accurate expert matching and stylistically coherent response generation grounded in cultural context.

6.

Composer Agent:

This module synthesizes culturally diverse expert responses into a single coherent final output. Expert responses are generated using the top-k culturally aligned experts identified by the router module. Rather than merging raw model outputs, the Composer aggregates expert-generated subresponses into a structured prompt, which is then passed to a language model for generation. This prompt includes the original query, user demographic preferences, and the top-
k
expert outputs, annotated by cultural source. The generation process is guided to remain under a fixed length constraint (e.g., 200 words) and optimized for stylistic fluency, coherence, and cultural appropriateness. While the current implementation uses a single LLM to compose the final output, the architecture can be extended to support weighted response fusion or LLM-based meta-aggregation for higher-fidelity expert integration.

The modular design facilitates clear attribution of responsibility to each agent, while supporting extensibility, interpretability, and dynamic cultural adaptation.

3.3
Evaluation Metrics for Cultural Alignment

To assess the performance of the proposed system, we introduce a comprehensive suite of evaluation metrics that reflect the underlying modular architecture and the dynamic state captured in the
GraphState
schema. These metrics are designed to provide interpretable, quantitative insights into the quality, diversity, cultural alignment, and sensitivity-awareness of the generated responses. Each metric corresponds to specific components in the generative pipeline, enabling a principled analysis of system behavior.

•

Cultural Alignment Score (CAS):

\displaystyle\text{CAS}=\frac{|\{c_{i}\in C\mid c_{i}\in R\}|}{|C|}

This metric quantifies the proportion of expert responses whose associated cultural identity
c_{i}
appears within the set of culturally relevant regions
R
, as determined by the
Planner Agent
. A high alignment score indicates that the
Router Agent
successfully activates experts corresponding to culturally appropriate contexts.

•

Cultural Alignment Variance (CAV):

\displaystyle\text{CAV}=\mathrm{Var}\left([f_{r}(c)]_{c\in R}\right)

This measures the variance in the distribution of expert responses across the relevant cultures. Lower variance suggests more balanced cultural representation, whereas high variance may imply cultural overrepresentation or bias within the routing mechanism.

•

Cultural Diversity (Shannon Entropy):

\displaystyle H(C)=-\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\log_{2}p_{i},\quad p_{i}=\frac{\text{count}(c_{i})}{|C|}

Entropy is computed over the set of cultural identities
C
associated with all expert responses. This metric captures the richness of cultural perspectives in the output, independent of predefined alignment criteria. Higher entropy values indicate greater diversity in cultural contributions.

•

Sensitive Topic Mention Rate (STMRate):

\displaystyle\text{STMRate}=\frac{|\{r_{i}\mid\exists t\in T,\ t\subseteq r_{i}\}|}{|E|}

This metric represents the proportion of expert responses
r_{i}\in E
that explicitly reference at least one sensitive topic
t\in T
, as flagged by the
Sentopic Agent
. It serves as an indicator of sensitivity awareness and responsiveness to flagged content areas.

•

Sensitive Topic Coverage:

\displaystyle\text{Coverage}=\frac{|\{t\in T\mid\exists r_{i},\ t\subseteq r_{i}\}|}{|T|}

Sensitive topic coverage measures the extent to which the set of identified sensitive topics
T
is addressed across the generated responses. It reflects the breadth of attention given to culturally delicate issues within the dialogue.

•

Response Completeness:

\displaystyle\text{Completeness}=\frac{|\{r_{i}\in E\mid\text{complete}(r_{i})\}|}{|E|}

This metric evaluates the structural integrity of responses, using a predicate
complete
(
r_{i}
) that returns true if all required semantic markers (e.g., ’a’, ’b’, ’c’, ’d’) are present. This reflects the ability of the
Composer Agent
to generate coherent and well-structured outputs.

•

Response Length Statistics:

–

Average Response Length:
Reflects the general verbosity and informativeness of the generated outputs.

–

Standard Deviation of Response Lengths:
Captures the stylistic variability among expert responses. Large deviations may signal inconsistency in prompt interpretation or expert behavior.

•

Unique Cultural Representations:

\displaystyle\text{UniqueCultures}=|\{c_{i}\}|,\quad c_{i}\in\texttt{responses}

This metric measures the number of distinct cultures represented across expert responses. While related to entropy, it provides a more coarse-grained indication of the breadth of cultural engagement.

The above metrics are tightly coupled with the key modules in the LangGraph architecture. The
Planner Agent
generates topic and cultural relevance metadata; the
Router Agent
determines expert activation paths; the
Expert Agents
produce culture-specific responses; the
Composer Agent
synthesizes final outputs; and the
Sentopic Agent
flags sensitive content for downstream emphasis. By aligning evaluation directly with these structural components, our framework supports modular debugging, targeted ablation studies, and iterative system refinement.

4.2
Qualitative Case Studies

We analyze three sample prompts (Appendix
A.4
) to illustrate how our system balances cultural sensitivity, routing efficiency, and response diversity.

Case 1: Drug Addiction — Strong Sensitivity, Deep Divergence

The prompt “Are drug-addicted neighbors a concern?” activated five culturally diverse full experts (U.S., Vietnam, India, Thailand, China), reflecting a strong sensitivity score (9/10) and high response entropy (2.322). Western responses centered on harm reduction and public health, while Asian responses prioritized social cohesion and stigma avoidance. This fine-grained divergence exemplifies the model’s capacity for cultural mapping across moral frameworks. In contrast, both the direct LLM and baseline essays generalized the topic, failing to surface cultural framing—despite longer outputs (1,822 and 2,315 characters vs. 1,313 for ours). The system’s shorter but denser response reflects effective cultural compression, affirming its alignment-focused synthesis (Appendix
A.3
§2).

Case 2: Trust in Television — Low Sensitivity, Targeted Routing

For the prompt “Do you trust television?”, only Germany was activated as a full expert, with brief inputs from the U.S., Spain, India, and Indonesia. Despite a low sensitivity score (2/10), the system selectively highlighted regional broadcasting attitudes (e.g., German trust in public media) and achieved moderate entropy via cross-cultural sketching. This case demonstrates the model’s dynamic economy: it adjusts routing depth proportionally to cultural salience. Compared to our system’s efficient 1,269-character output, the direct LLM (2,451 chars) focused on cognitive media effects, while the baseline (2,284 chars) delivered a structured but culture-neutral essay. Appendix
A.2
confirms that low-sensitivity prompts elicit minimal expert engagement without compromising representational grounding.

Case 3: Housewife Fulfillment — Normative Debate, Cultural Nuance

The prompt “Can being a housewife be fulfilling?” generated full responses from five countries (U.S., India, Japan, China, Philippines), yielding the shortest response (977 characters) among all prompts with high alignment (8/10). Despite the brevity, the answer weaves together divergent views: U.S. autonomy, Indian familial values, Japan’s traditional-modern balance, Chinese modernization tensions, and Philippine contextuality. The cultural alignment model accomplishes efficient normative framing through expert convergence—avoiding generic subjectivism seen in the LLM and avoiding abstract essayism in the baseline (1,585 and 2,318 chars respectively). This supports the hypothesis that alignment and entropy can be achieved with minimal token usage through focused expert synthesis (Appendix
A.3
).

5
Discussion

The proposed framework demonstrates clear improvements in cultural alignment, diversity, and awareness of sensitivity across a range of evaluation metrics. The system achieves a significantly higher Cultural Alignment Score (0.82 vs. 0.21) and Diversity Entropy (1.66 vs. 0.44) compared to the baseline, confirming the effectiveness of the router in selecting culturally appropriate experts. On average, responses integrate perspectives from over three distinct cultural profiles, validating the intended behavior of the expert activation module.

Further, the system exhibits higher sensitivity coverage and a stronger response to flagged content, indicating practical propagation of sensitive topic signals from the Sentopic Agent through to final generation. Importantly, structural integrity is maintained across all outputs, with 100% response completeness.

While the latency is considerably higher due to multi-stage expert activation and composition, this reflects a trade-off favoring depth and cultural contextualization over speed. The observed variability in response lengths suggests stylistic adaptability rather than inconsistency, aligning output verbosity with query complexity and cultural nuance.

This work pushes interesting new directions. For example, although we demonstrated that our proposed system performs better than a baseline LLM with no multi-agent architecture, comparing the system to other multi-agent architectures, such as Plurals
Ashkinaze et al. 2025
, will further demonstrate that our system indeed pushes the state of the art in achieving cultural alignment during inference beyond baseline LLMs. Furthermore, our pipeline operates completely autonomously with no human intervention. Investigating the potential of human assistance in a multi-agent architecture based on symbolic signals
Kambhampati et al. 2024
is an interesting direction for increasing performance. Finally, we relied on prompt engineering to design our cultural agents. A qualitative analysis of how these agents behave in consultation with experts from the cultures we aim to represent can further enhance the performance of our system. Although our work performs cultural alignment in a controlled question-and-answer task, we believe that battle-testing a multi-agent system in such a setting can have considerable implications and set the groundwork for more complex environments, especially as LLM agents become increasingly independent.

5.1
Limitations

Despite the encouraging results, several limitations remain:

•

Latency Overhead:
The multi-agent pipeline introduces non-trivial latency, which may impact real-time usability.

•

Static Expert Embeddings:
Expert personas are fixed at runtime and do not adapt to evolving cultural signals or domain shifts.

•

Limited Cultural Coverage:
The current set of 20 cultural profiles does not account for intra-cultural variation or intersectionality.

•

Single Inference Backbone:
Both expert and final responses are generated using a single LLM, which may introduce residual biases.

•

Overrepresentation Risk:
High frequency of certain topics may inflate sensitivity metrics without indicating deeper coverage.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we present a multi-agent architecture that embeds a user profile and a culturally complex question as input, determines sensitive topics, creates a plan to invoke experts to answer the question based on our novel top-k cultural routing algorithm, and composes a culturally aligned response. We demonstrate that our multi-agent system performs better than an LLM with no multi-agent capabilities, particularly in achieving cultural alignment and diversity entropy. Based on these insights, we elucidate limitations and propose future work directions.
