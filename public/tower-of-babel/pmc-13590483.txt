FFEM-DB 2.0: an updated and extended spatio-temporal dataset of flood fatalities occurred from 1980 to 2024 in the Euro-Mediterranean region
Authors: O Petrucci, K Papagiannaki, M Diakakis, A Badoux, A-I Bănescu, R Brázdil, T M N Carvalho, K Chromá, M M de Brito, G Ioana-Toroimac, A Kahraman, Ö Kılıç, A Krahn, H Kreibich, K Liechti, M C Llasat, M Llasat-Botija, N Macdonald, G A Moroșanu-Mitoșeriu, C Orieschnig, S Pereira, J Řehoř, J Rossello Geli, M Sima, A H Thieken, F Vinet, L Zaharia, M -L Zenker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis data descriptor paper presents version 2.0 of the Database of Flood Fatalities in the Euro-Mediterranean Region (FFEM-DB 2.0), a multinational dataset documenting 3,737 flood fatalities that occurred from 1980 to 2024 across 16 territories (12 of which correspond to entire countries). FFEM-DB 2.0 substantially expands the spatial and temporal coverage of the previous release (FFEM-DB) by updating records through December 31, 2024, incorporating four additional study areas, and introducing a new event identifier that allows event-based analyses and comparison with international disaster databases. The database provides anonymized information on victims’ profiles, accident locations, and fatality circumstances, enabling systematic investigation of demographic, behavioral, situational, and environmental factors associated with flood mortality at the scale of LAU (Local Administrative Units). Standardized data collection criteria and a harmonized classification methodology ensure consistency and comparability across regions beyond administrative boundaries, while also highlighting variations in data completeness across the study areas. Due to its open and scalable structure, FFEM-DB 2.0 enables continuous spatial expansion and temporal updates, providing a basis for monitoring and interpreting current trends in flood-related mortality across Europe and the Euro-Mediterranean region.

Background & Summary

Understanding how and under which circumstances loss of life occurs during flood events requires information at the level of individual fatalities, including basic demographic characteristics, where people were exposed, what they were doing at the time of the accident, and how the event unfolded1. Such information can be systematically coded and used to anticipate high-risk scenarios and target prevention2–4), it is also essential for supporting disaster preparedness and response strategies, and becomes increasingly relevant under climate change, which is projected to affect flood hazard across most parts of Europe and the Mediterranean region3.

Vulnerability to fatal flooding is shaped by a combination of socio-demographic4, geographic, cultural, age-related, and behavioral factors5,6. As highlighted by scientific literature5,7, flood-related mortality between 2010 and 2020 differed markedly across local contexts, with death rates increasing by up to 15 times in highly vulnerable regions. Accordingly, flood-fatality data with national and sub-national resolutions are essential for locally appropriate, inclusive, and gender-responsive risk communication and disaster risk reduction (DRR) actions (https://wrd.unwomen.org/practice/topics/early-warning). Continuous and systematic disaster loss data collection also supports the evaluation of DRR strategies over time6. Finally, knowledge of the circumstances that caused the victims is necessary to improve early warning systems, designed to be comprehensive and end-to-end, responding to the “Early Warnings for All” initiative launched by the United Nations, with support from the World Meteorological Organization (https://wmo.int/activities/early-warnings-all/wmo-and-early-warnings-all-initiative).

Despite this recognized need, detailed information on the circumstances of flood fatalities remains fragmented and inconsistent, often scattered across multilingual media archives and institutional reports that are not easily accessible or systematically compiled, reported or analyzed. This has resulted in few studies examining or considering the multiple variables influencing flood vulnerability, which has limited the utility and application of evidence in supporting consistent evidence for decision-makers or public health planners.

Existing international databases, such as EM-DAT (https://www.emdat.be), provide valuable overviews of catastrophic disasters but typically lack fatality-level information on the circumstances of death. They often exclude events with one or a few fatalities (e.g. EM-DAT requires ten deaths (dead or missing) for inclusion), and may therefore distort flood mortality patterns in regions where frequent events produce limited death tolls, while the multi-hazard character of hydrometeorological disasters further complicates the attribution of loss of life to flooding alone1.

To address these limitations, an international research effort initiated in 2018 led to the progressive development of a database of flood fatalities, evolving through earlier datasets (MEFF, EUFF, cfr. Table 1) towards the Flood Fatalities from the Euro-Mediterranean Database (FFEM-DB), while maintaining consistent standards for collecting, reporting, and controlling data6. The FFEM-DB was released as an open-access dataset in a dedicated research data repository to ensure long-term availability, version control, and reusability. Selected versions were also made accessible through interactive maps within the Risk Data Hub (https://drmkc.jrc.ec.europa.eu/risk-data-hub#/damages/FFEM), a web platform developed by the Disaster Risk Management Knowledge Centre.

Building on the first release of the FFEM-DB6, this data paper presents version 2.0 of the database. FFEM-DB 2.0 extends the temporal coverage of the previous release to 31 December 2024 and expands its spatial scope by including four additional study areas, increasing the total coverage to 16 territories, 12 of which correspond to entire countries. Beyond this expansion, the updated release strengthens the database’s role as a stable and comparable reference resource for flood-mortality research, enabling more robust cross-regional and cross-temporal analyses, and supporting its use as a benchmarking dataset in studies of flood risk, impacts, and disaster risk reduction across Europe. Moreover, the newly introduced event identifier further allows event-based analyses and comparisons with international disaster databases.

By design, the database supports evidence-based decision-making, disaster risk education, and comparative research, contributing to the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction goal of reducing disaster mortality by 2030 (https://www.preventionweb.net/). In addition, it provides evidence relevant to disaster-related indicators under the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG), particularly SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities) and SDG 13 (Climate Action), thereby contributing to the goal of substantially reducing disaster mortality by 20308,9.

Methods

FFEM-DB 2.0 is a curated dataset of flood fatalities compiled following the methodological framework described in6. The database documents multiple characteristics of flood-related deaths, including demographic attributes of victims (such as age and gender), the activity of the victim at the time of the accident, the location and dynamics of the fatal event, the reported cause of death, and the presence of protective or hazardous behaviors.

Information for each fatality is organized using a consistent set of predefined fields and categories, allowing comparable documentation of flood-related deaths across different study areas and periods.

The FFEM dataset has been released through successive development stages, evolving from earlier datasets (EUFF) to the consolidated FFEM-DB framework and the present FFEM-DB 2.0 release. The acronyms, development stages and the main characteristics of each public release are summarized in Table 1.

The current version integrates all previously covered study areas and includes four additional ones: Albania, Austria, Switzerland (1980–2024), and Romania (2000–2024). It maintains the same compilation, verification, and classification principles applied in earlier releases. Data sources

The data collection and source verification procedures applied in FFEM-DB 2.0 follow the methodological framework established in the database’s first release6. The same types of documentary sources were used for the updated entries, ensuring consistency with previous releases.

Information on flood fatalities was collected from documentary sources, including national and regional newspapers (printed and online), as well as reports produced by institutions involved in disaster and risk management, such as police forces, fire brigades, and civil protection authorities. These sources were used to identify flood events with reported fatalities and to extract information on individual fatality circumstances.

As an example of coding procedure, a newspaper report stating: “A 63-year-old man was swept away while traveling with his car on a road closed by police near the village of X during the heavy rainfall of 12 October 1996” would be coded as follows (excluding the sections allowing localization of the accident and unknown fields):

A fatality was included in the database only when confirmed by more than one independent source. When available, complementary information from institutional reports was used to improve the reliability and completeness of records.

Data Records

In contrast to the previous version, FFEM-DB 2.0 aims to make the data simpler and improve its usability. For this reason, the database consists of a single spreadsheet containing 3,737 rows, each corresponding to a flood fatality, and 37 columns describing the fatality’s spatial location, as well as the victim’s characteristics and the accident’s circumstances. Column names are intentionally explicit and, where needed, redundant (e.g., victim age, victim gender, victim activity), so that each variable can be understood independently. This choice facilitates both data filtering and analysis by non-specialist users. In general, the fields follow the same nomenclature and content used in previous versions of the database. Three new fields are introduced in this release (Event code, Fatality code, and Behavior/actions) and are described below.

The 37 variables included in the database are clustered into three main classes:

Spatial localization of flood fatalities using NUTS (https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/nuts), and their temporal occurrence (Table 4). All FFs are georeferenced to the smallest available administrative unit, the Local Administrative Unit (LAU), except in Albania and Switzerland, where LAUs are not in use. Since the 1st of January 2021, UK has been using ITL (International Territorial Levels), with three tiers, comparable to the NUTS system (https://www.ons.gov.uk/methodology/geography/ukgeographies/eurostat).

This class includes the newly introduced field named the “event code”, which allows grouping fatalities reported as belonging to the same flood occurrence within a given region and time period. It is created by combining the NUTS 2 code, the year, the month, and the day of the fatal accident, followed by a progressive number identifying fatalities associated with the same reported event (e.g., AT12-19820818-004 for a fatality that occurred in Austria on 8 August 1982). Technical Validation

Data validation is performed before data entry, following the procedures described in6. Each flood fatality is identified from multiple independent documentary sources, and the information used for database compilation is extracted through a meticulous process that involves the joint analysis of all available sources to gather as many details as possible from each.

Chronicle-based sources often provide detailed accounts of fatal events. Cross-checking information across different outlets, including reports published in the days following the event, helps ensure that preliminary or inaccurate information reported immediately after floods is corrected in the final dataset. The reporting of victims’ names in many sources also helps prevent duplicate counting of the same fatality, however fatality names are not reported within the database.

A comparison with international disaster databases is not repeated here, as this analysis was already conducted in11. That study showed that FFEM-DB differs substantially from widely used disaster databases, such as EM-DAT12 or HANZE-E13, in terms of scope and structure. While these databases primarily focus on disaster events and typically report aggregated fatality numbers, FFEM-DB records individual flood fatalities and documents the circumstances of each fatality, independently of the event’s overall societal impact. Differences in hazard attribution and administrative resolution further limit direct comparability across datasets; however, FFEM-DB 2.0 provides the highest spatial and temporal resolution available enabling more detailed geospatial analysis.

It is important to note that for Albania—whose dataset was compiled exclusively from international sources—the time series may be incomplete.

Usage Notes

FFEM-DB 2.0 database is openly accessible through the 4TU Centre for Research Data10 and can be directly used for analysis. This updated release extends both the temporal coverage and the spatial scope of the database, enabling analyses that include recent flood events and newly covered study areas within the Euro-Mediterranean region. Further updates are planned over the next four years and will include the addition of new study areas.

The expanded coverage and continued use of consistent variable definitions across versions support comparative analyses across time periods and study areas, as well as the replication and extension of previous studies based on earlier releases of FFEM-DB. The availability of individual-level demographic, spatial, temporal, and contextual information enables analyses at multiple spatial scales, from individual fatalities to larger multi-fatality events occurring within NUTS/ITL administrative units.

FFEM-DB 2.0 can be readily integrated into broader analytical frameworks addressing disaster impacts and risk reduction. In particular, the updated dataset facilitates cross-regional and cross-temporal investigations of flood-related mortality patterns, including comparisons across different geographic settings, levels of urbanization, and policy contexts, and supports the assessment of temporal changes in fatality patterns over time in relation to evolving risk management practices.
