Assessing community pharmacists’ practices and strategies to minimize medication dispensing errors: a Jordanian study
Authors: Mohanned S. Jallad, Karem H. Alzoubi, Faten Odeh, Fahmi Y. Al-Ashwal, Rana K. Abu-Farha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectives:The purpose of this study was to examine community pharmacists’ adherence to best medication dispensing practices, their awareness of medication dispensing errors, perceived risk factors, and strategies for minimising errors.Methods:A cross-sectional, survey-based study was conducted over six weeks (March–April 2024) among Jordanian community pharmacists. A validated, self-administered questionnaire assessed sociodemographic characteristics, adherence to dispensing practices (17-item Likert scale), awareness of medication dispensing errors, perceived risk factors, and preventive strategies. Data were analysed using SPSS.Result:Of the 214 pharmacist participants, high levels of adherence were reported for verifying contraindications (77.1%), counselling on proper dosage (78.5%), and timing of medication administration (79.0%); however, low levels of adherence were found for counselling on missed doses (34.6%), adverse drug reactions (40.7%), and documenting pharmacist interventions (36.9%). Major perceived risk factors for medication dispensing errors included inadequate education (67.3%), vague prescriptions (66.8%), similar medication names (61.7%), and excessive workload (54.2%). Additionally, attending a medication dispensing errors workshop was positively correlated with adherence to the best dispensing practices amongst community pharmacies in Jordan (β = 0.183, p = 0.007).Conclusion:Jordanian community pharmacists demonstrate strong adherence to essential dispensing practices and substantial awareness of common medication errors. However, gaps remain in counselling, documentation, and recognition of less obvious errors. Targeted continuing education, structured workflows, and system-level interventions – such as electronic prescribing and optimised staffing – are essential to further reduce dispensing errors and enhance patient safety in community pharmacy settings.

Introduction

Community pharmacists are the first point of contact for access to medications worldwide, having dispensed over 4.38 billion prescriptions in the United States alone in 2019 (Santos et al., 2022). Community pharmacists have an essential role in optimising patients’ medication therapy, as well as in providing education and support for health maintenance and disease prevention by utilising their knowledge derived from training, clinical experience, or professional judgment as experts in drug therapy(Peterson et al., 1999). Pharmacists also conduct evaluations of prescriptions to verify that medications are safe and/or appropriate for patients based on an evaluation of factors including the efficacy, safety and costs of the medication and any available alternative (ACCP, 2008).

Pharmacists routinely evaluate prescriptions for possible errors including but not limited to inappropriate dosing, drug–drug interactions, contraindications and therapeutic duplication, especially when multiple prescribers are involved in the care of a patient (Goode et al., 2019; Messerli et al., 2016). Pharmacists also can assist with monitoring of controlled substances and detecting any potential misuse, abuse or adverse drug interactions with this type of substance (Doong et al., 2016; Upton et al., 2020). When the dispensing of medications is performed correctly, it has a tremendous impact on the overall success of the medication being used safely and effectively adding to the patients overall wellbeing (Al-Arifi, 2014). However, in many locations, dispensing procedures are not adequately documented (Alaqeel & Abanmy, 2015; Ibrahim et al., 2016).

As per the United States National Coordinating Council for Medication Error Reporting and Prevention, medication errors are ‘any preventable events that may cause an inappropriate medication to be administered or may cause harm to the patient’ (American Society of Health-System Pharmacists [ASHP], 2021). They may involve incorrect medications, doses, frequencies, or routes of administration (Luchen, 2021). Studies conducted in Jordan and the United Arab Emirates indicate that most medication errors are minor to moderate in severity, while a smaller proportion are classified as serious (Abdel-Qader et al., 2021; Patel et al., 2007). Medication errors can have a negative impact on healthcare and may lead to issues such as patient noncompliance; adverse drug reactions; failure to receive appropriate treatment; increased use of health-care services; and significant financial consequences. Some estimates indicate that medication errors account for as much as 6–7 percent of all hospital admissions in some countries (Masotti et al., 2010; Patel et al., 2007).

One of the most commonly reported types of medication error is called a medication dispensing errors, which is defined as a difference between medication prescribed and what was dispensed (Cheung et al., 2009). Various types of dispensing errors occur, including: providing the wrong drug/strength; incorrectly filled dosage forms or quantities; and dispenser labelling mistakes (e.g. wrong patient name, quantity or instructions) (Abdel-Qader et al., 2021; Ibrahim et al., 2020; James et al., 2010; Soubra & Karout, 2021; Szeinbach et al., 2007). Contributing factors that lead to errors include: look-alike and sound-alike drugs, illegible handwriting and high workload, inadequate staff levels or frequent interruptions during dispensing (Al-Worafi et al., 2018).

In Jordan, prior research has primarily focused on determining the frequency of medication dispensing errors, with limited research focusing on pharmacists’ perceptions of medication dispensing errors (Abdel-Qader et al., 2021). The purpose of this study was to examine community pharmacists’ adherence to best medication dispensing practices, their awareness of medication dispensing errors, perceived risk factors, and strategies for minimising errors. The findings will be used to support development of national standardised protocols to promote patient safety and prevent medication dispensing errors.

Methods

Data collection

The study is survey-based cross-sectional study that was conducted over a six-week period from March 8 through April 13, 2024, to examine community pharmacists’ adherence to best medication dispensing practices, their awareness of medication dispensing errors, perceived risk factors, and strategies for minimising errors. All Jordanian community pharmacists who actively dispense medications were eligible for recruitment into the study, while pharmacy interns and students were excluded. Participants were recruited using a non-probability convenience sampling approach, whereby pharmacists who were accessible and willing to participate were invited to complete the survey. This approach was adopted due to the lack of a comprehensive sampling frame and feasibility considerations.

Questionnaire development and validation

The instrument was developed based on an extensive search of the academic literature using electronic databases such as Google Scholar and PubMed (Al-Arifi, 2014; Aldhwaihi et al., 2016; Cheung et al., 2009; Goode et al., 2019; Ibrahim et al., 2020; Maharaj et al., 2020; Santos et al., 2022; Soubra & Karout, 2021). The survey instrument was initially drafted and reviewed by a panel of three clinical pharmacy academics to ensure content validity, confirming that the items were clear, relevant, and comprehensive. A pilot study with five community pharmacists was then conducted to evaluate clarity, structure, length, and overall comprehensibility, supporting face validity. Completion time was recorded, and minor adjustments were made based on feedback. Data from the pilot study were excluded from the final analysis. Internal consistency of each questionnaire section was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, with the following results: Section Two = 0.907; Section Three = 0.888; Section Four = 0.959; and Section Five = 0.983, indicating excellent reliability across all sections.

The questionnaire was translated from English to Arabic using a forward–backward translation process as suggested by Beaton et al. (2000) cross-cultural adaptation guidelines (Beaton et al., 2000). An academic who is fluent in English and Arabic initially translated the document from English to Arabic (forward). A second independent bilingual academic (who is also a pharmacy expert) translated the Arabic back into English (backward) with no prior knowledge of the original English document's contents. The original and back-translated documents were compared, and there were no discrepancies between the two, demonstrating their equivalence. The final version of the questionnaire was uploaded to Google Forms so that it could be used to collect responses, after being shared on various social media platforms (Facebook, WhatsApp, Instagram, and X).

The final questionnaire was designed for completion in about 10 min and consisted of 5 major parts. The sociodemographic part gathered information from the community pharmacists who participated in the study; the second part measured pharmacist's compliance with best medication dispensing practice through the use of 17 statements assessed on a 5-point Likert scale (5 = always, 4 = often, 3 = sometimes, 2 = rarely, and 1 = never); the third part measured pharmacists’ awareness of the different types of medication dispensing errors; the fourth part assessed pharmacists’ perceptions about the risk factors leading to medication dispensing errors through use of a 5-point Likert scale (5 = strongly agree, 4 = agree, 3 = neither agree nor disagree, 2 = disagree, 1 = strongly disagree); the fifth part evaluated pharmacists’ perceptions of strategies to prevent or reduce medication dispensing error by way of using a 5-point Likert scale (5 = strongly agree, 4 = agree, 3 = neither agree nor disagree, 2 = disagree, 1 = strongly disagree). An overall adherence score for each pharmacist was calculated for the second section based on their responses. The minimum and maximum scores were between 17 and 85, respectively (i.e. with a higher number indicating better adherence to best practices for dispensing medication).

Sample size calculations

Using the Raosoft formula n = P × (1- P) × z2/d2, the sample size was calculated. Given a conservative estimated proportion (P) of 50%, a margin of error of 10%, and a confidence level of 0.95, the minimum sample size that was required was found to be 96 (Raosoft, 2004).

Ethical considerations

The World Medical Association's Declaration of Helsinki was followed during the completion of this study. The Research and Ethics Committee of Applied Science Private University approved the study (Approval No. 2024-PHA-18). Study Participants were made aware of the purpose of the study and that their participation was voluntary before they had access to the questionnaire. Electronic informed consent was obtained from all the participants. Each participant was informed they had the right to withdraw from the study without penalty at any time. There were no personal identifiers collected in order to protect the anonymity and confidentiality of the participants.

Statistical analysis

Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 22 was used to analyse all quantitative data collected for this study. Categorical data are presented using frequencies or percentages while continuous data are summarised using mean (Standard deviations (SD)) or median (interquartile range (IQR)) based on variable normality. Data normality was assessed by both Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and histogram visual inspection. A p-value ≤ 0.05 from the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test indicates that the data significantly deviate from a normal distribution. Linear regression analyses were conducted to identify independent factors associated with pharmacists’ prescription dispensing practices. Variables with a p-value < 0.250 in the univariate analysis were included in the multiple regression model, following the purposeful selection strategy recommended by Bursac et al. (2008). This approach helps ensure that potentially important predictors are not prematurely excluded from the multivariate model, even if they do not reach conventional significance levels in univariate testing. Multi-collinearity of independent variables was determined using a Pearson’s correlation coefficient; if the coefficient was < 0.90, then the non-independent variables were acceptable. A P-value of≤0.05 was considered statistically significant. The reliability of the questionnaire was assessed using the Cronbach’s alpha; values ≥ 0.70 indicate that the measure possesses acceptable internal consistency (Taber, 2018).

Results

In this study, 214 community pharmacists agreed to participate in the study. Most pharmacists held a Bachelor of Science in Pharmacy degree (n = 147, 68.7%), and the majority were employed as staff pharmacists (n = 183, 85.5%). In terms of professional experience, nearly one-third of respondents had seven or more years of practice (n = 66, 30.8%). Most participants worked in independent community pharmacies (n = 162, 75.7%), and almost half were located in the central region of Jordan (n = 100, 46.7%). Regarding workload, 94 participants (43.9%) reported serving fewer than 50 patients per day, while 150 participants (70.1%) dispensed fewer than 50 prescriptions per day. Pharmacists identified a range of factors they perceive as contributing to medication dispensing errors (Table 4). The most frequently recognised risk factors, based on combined ‘strongly agree’ and ‘agree’ responses, were similarity of medication names (n = 132, 61.7%), lack of adequate training for correct medication dispensing practice (n = 144, 67.3%), unclear handwriting on prescriptions (n = 143, 66.8%), inexperienced staff (n = 130, 60.8%), and high workload, including a large number of patients (n = 116, 54.2%). These findings suggest that both human factors, such as training and staff experience, and system-related factors, including workload and prescription clarity, are considered major contributors to dispensing errors. On the other hand, factors perceived as less influential included job dissatisfaction (n = 63, 29.4%), issues with computer software (n = 67, 31.3%), and dispensing a different medicine as a replacement for an out-of-stock one (n = 77, 36.0%).

Pharmacists reported several measures they believe could minimise the risk of medication dispensing errors (Table 5). The highest perceived measures, based on combined agreement responses, were using electronic prescriptions (n = 167, 78.0%), following the six rights of medication administration (n = 167, 78.0%), collaboration with physicians to confirm prescribed drugs (n = 162, 75.7%), reducing avoidable interruptions during dispensing (n = 161, 75.3%), and improving prescription handwriting (n = 163, 76.2%). Discussion

This study examined community pharmacists’ adherence to best medication dispensing practices, their awareness of medication dispensing errors, perceived risk factors, and strategies for minimising errors. Accurate dispensing of medications is key to patient safety and the rational use of medications, thus leading to effective therapy and better patient outcomes (Al-Arifi, 2014). The process of dispensing prescription medications consists of a number of different phases; therefore to determine where gaps exist and improve service quality, it is critical to understand each of these phases (Ha et al., 2018; Ibrahim et al., 2016).

The results revealed a high level of adherence among pharmacists to core dispensing practices. Many respondents reported routinely verifying patient data, assessing for contraindications, and counselling patients regarding how to dose and take their medicines while taking into account timing as well as safety. These results also coincide with data reported in Brazilian studies where community pharmacists showed high rates of adherence with respect to transcribing and informing practices (Santos et al., 2022). However, this high level of adherence should be interpreted with caution, as self-reported practices may overestimate actual performance due to social desirability bias.

Despite this overall positive trend, variability existed in counselling on drug interactions, non-pharmacological therapies, storage, adverse drug reactions, and missed doses, highlighting areas where standardised protocols could improve patient education consistency (ASHP, 1997; Mesquita et al., 2013). This variability may reflect underlying system-level constraints such as limited time, high workload, and the absence of standardised counselling protocols in community pharmacy settings, rather than solely individual-level differences in knowledge or skills.

Monitoring patient health and documenting interventions were less consistent, reflecting challenges seen in low- and middle-income countries, such as limited staff and time constraints (Cerqueira Santos et al., 2016). However, a large number of pharmacists indicated that they regularly referred their patients to other healthcare providers (62.1%) and consulted with physicians when they were uncertain about what was prescribed (65.0%) thus, demonstrating their aptitude for collaboration within the healthcare system. More specifically, the findings of this study suggest that the pharmacists in this sample were more engaged in the safety of their patients than other studies (29.6%) and therefore proved their ability to be proactive participants in ensuring patient safety (Santos et al., 2022).

Awareness of medication dispensing errors was generally high. Pharmacists most frequently recognised dispensing wrong doses, wrong medications, and wrong types of drugs, followed by errors in dispensing with wrong dosing instructions. However, lower levels of awareness were observed for dispensing expired products, giving incomplete instructions, and issuing the drug to the wrong patient. This pattern suggests that pharmacists may be more attuned to clinically obvious errors while underrecognizing operational or system-related errors, indicating potential gaps in training emphasis. Expanding educational programmes to include less apparent but equally critical errors could enhance overall medication safety (Asmelashe Gelayee & Binega Mekonnen, 2017; Karout et al., 2022).

According to regression analysis, attendance at medication dispensing error workshops helped to increase adherence to best practice standards. However, this association should not be interpreted as causal due to the cross-sectional design of the study. This finding nevertheless highlights the potential importance of continuing professional development (CPD) as a contributing factor to improved dispensing practices. In contrast, demographics, and workload factors did not appear to impact adherence to best practice standards. This finding reinforces that CPD plays a vital role as enablers of safe medication dispensing practices (Aldosari et al., 2020; Halila et al., 2015; Saade et al., 2018). The introduction of annual credits for CPD in Jordan should therefore provide a framework for pharmacists to participate in structured continuing professional development (CPD) that will enhance their skills and improve patient safety (Al-Rusasi et al., 2023).

Community pharmacists reported various factors leading to medication dispensing errors, including workload, insufficient training, complicated prescriptions, look-alike/sound-alike drugs, interruptions, exhaustion, and environmental conditions. These findings reinforce the multifactorial nature of dispensing errors, where both human and system-related factors interact to influence practice. Similar factors have been reported in previous studies, emphasising the importance of addressing both individual competencies and workplace conditions (Abdel-Qader et al., 2021; de las Mercedes Martínez Sánchez, 2013; Ibrahim et al., 2020; Maharaj et al., 2020).

Preventative measures mentioned by participants included: using e-prescribing, double-checking prescriptions, following the ‘six rights,’ working with doctors, having a structured workflow, keeping alike medications separated, and educating patients when there are limited interruptions and privacy. As such, this group's preventative strategies are consistent with the widely adopted international guidelines for the use of technology, training and/or staff development, optimising workflow, and quality assurance processes (Karthik et al., 2023; Maharaj et al., 2020; Marinković et al., 2019; Santos et al., 2022; WHO, 2016).

The study has several limitations which needs to be recognised. The cross-sectional study design does not allow for the establishment of causal links between variables identified and compliance with best practices for dispensing medications. The data obtained were based on self-reported information, which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Additionally, as the survey was conducted online, no specific measures were implemented to prevent duplicate responses or verify respondent identity, which may affect data integrity. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with these limitations in mind. As such, compliance and awareness levels may be lower than the actual values. The researchers assessed pharmacists’ perceived behaviours related to safe dispensing practices, however actual errors during dispensing were not directly measured, therefore the study cannot be a complete reflection of reality. Moreover, the use of a non-probability convenience sampling method may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution, as the sample may not be fully representative of all community pharmacists in Jordan. Finally, while the model explains a small proportion of the variance in pharmacists’ adherence to best medication dispensing practice, it highlights significant associations, such as the positive effect of attending a workshop on adherence.

Conclusion

Community pharmacists in Jordan demonstrate good adherence to safe dispensing practices and awareness of errors, though variability exists in counselling and documentation. Workshop attendance was linked to better adherence, but causal relationships cannot be established.

Workload, training gaps, and environmental factors were identified as contributors to errors. Strategies such as continuing education, structured workflows, electronic prescriptions, and collaboration with physicians are recommended to enhance patient safety.
