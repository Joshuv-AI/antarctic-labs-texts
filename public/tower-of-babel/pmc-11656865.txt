Collection and sharing of health information in mental health and related systems in Australia: perspectives of people who access mental health services
Authors: Anne Honey, Nicola Hancock, Helen Glover, Justin Newton Scanlan, Yidan Cao, Andrew Povolny, Mark Orr, Grenville Rose, Sumathi Govindasamy, Lorraine Smith, Naseem Ahmadapour
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInformation sharing and information privacy are important issues in mental health services. Yet the perspectives of Australians who access mental health services about these issues are poorly understood. This article addresses the research question: What are the concerns of people who use mental health services about the collection and use of their health information in mental health and related systems in Australia?MethodsParticipants were 16 people who had accessed mental health services and were involved in a series of co-design workshops as part of a larger study. Focused group activities were conducted in which participants were asked to discuss and create a visual map to describe their collective experiences of sharing information relating to mental health and recovery. The data were analysed using qualitative content analysis and the coding techniques of constant comparative analysis.ResultsParticipants expressed a sense of unknowability about the information held and accessed by various organisations. They described providing information to a wide variety of entities, though not always in a free or informed way. They believed other information held about them had been generated and/or shared by people other than themselves, often without their knowledge or consent. Participants reported that both the collection and storage of this information involved risks for them, and they sometimes restricted the information they provided. To improve their comfort with the use of their information, participants recommended customised and transparent information collection; individuals’ access and input to their own information; and use of information only for their benefit.ConclusionsTrust is key to positive experiences of information provision and information sharing. Central to establishing trust are transparent practices that facilitate greater choice and control.

Background

The twin issues of health information privacy and health information sharing have become increasingly prominent in health services generally and in mental health services in particular in recent years. According to Australia’s Privacy Act (1988), health information encompasses all personal information collected in the course of providing a health service. This includes, for example, personal details and all information or opinion relating to a person’s health status or health services provided, planned or desired. Digital technology has provided the capacity to easily collect, store and share multitudinous and diverse health information via platforms such as electronic health/medical records [1].

There are a range of acknowledged benefits to information sharing in health services. First, it is seen as beneficial to individuals’ health care. For, example, the use of electronic medical records to record and share comprehensive health information appears to contribute to higher quality, better integrated and safer care through staff having timely access to information [2] and has been associated with reduced psychiatric readmission rates [3]. Health service users have acknowledged benefits of these technologies including prevention of errors and reduced burden on their memories (e.g., [4]).

Second, information sharing can be used to benefit health systems and the wider community. Analysis of large volumes of health information allows monitoring of outcomes and identification of treatment and prevention strategies most likely to be effective for people with specific characteristics, environments and lifestyles, facilitating what has been referred to as precision medicine [1, 5]. Further, it is thought to enhance health system planning [6]. Many health service users support the use of their information for ‘public good’ and to improve service delivery and care for other people, especially if the information used is deidentified (e.g., [7, 8]). Australia’s National Mental Health Strategy has emphasised the need for both more information collection and greater access to or sharing of that information across the service system to enhance system integration and improve outcome evaluation and system improvement efforts [9] ,

Alongside recognition of the potential benefits of information sharing, however, has been increasing concern with the protection of information privacy, not just in health care but broadly, fuelled by the rampant sharing of personal information by private companies and well publicised data breaches [1]. Privacy in a health context is about people wishing to have “greater control over the collection and dissemination of their personal health information by healthcare organizations and technology vendors” ([4], p. 1016). Recent research has focused on health information privacy concerns held by health service users because their concerns are thought to result in behaviours that are considered undesirable from a data collection and service provision stance, such as refusing to consent to information sharing between organisations, avoiding treatment seeking, withholding information, or failing to comply with health recommendations that are seen as compromising privacy (e.g., [10–13]). Privacy concerns include unauthorised secondary use of information, improper access (both within an organisation and by third parties), errors in stored and shared information, technology break downs and lack of control over use and access to health information [4, 14].

The privacy of information relating to mental health is often seen as being particularly important due to the stigmatised nature of mental health issues and the inclusion in records of historical information that may be traumatic or further stigmatising [15]. It is therefore regarded as being particularly sensitive information. In the UK, people using mental health services reported being more concerned about their mental health information being shared than their physical health information. They described fear of a range of consequences such as breaches of data security, reduced quality of care due to diagnostic overshadowing, discrimination in physical health care and institutional discrimination [7]. Yet in countries like Australia, where mental health services are fragmented, information sharing between health and social services for people with mental health conditions is seen by many as particularly important for integrated care [16].

Australia’s national electronic health record system, called “My Health Record”, was introduced in 2019 as an opt-out framework after poor uptake when previously introduced as an opt-in system [17]. Additional safeguards were made available on an opt-in basis to make this record more secure, for example, the ability to permanently delete the record or institute access codes to limit access to particular providers or particular documents. However, while 90% of eligible Australians now have the record, less than 40,000 people have put in place the advanced access controls. It has been noted that some people using mental health services are likely to be part of a vulnerable group least likely to be “sufficiently informed, motivated and able to take steps to opt out” if they wish to, and, indeed, to opt into and execute the additional privacy features ([17], p.38).

Scholars have noted that the perspectives and experiences of people who use mental health services around information sharing and information privacy are not currently well understood [15] and a recent scoping review noted the dearth of service user perspectives on electronic health records [2]. This is even more notable in an Australian context, where no studies examining concerns held by people who use mental health services about the use and sharing of their health information could be located. Understanding these perspectives is crucial for developing processes and structures for collecting, sharing, and protecting their information that are beneficial for them.

This article aims to contribute to addressing this gap by answering the following research question: What are the experiences and perspectives of people who use mental health services regarding the collection and use of their health information in mental health and related systems in Australia? This study differs from most previous studies, not only by using Australian data, but by focusing on people’s views about the clinical and proximal sharing and use of their individual information rather than about the use of their deidentified information for research and administrative purposes (e.g., [18–20]) or discussing both together (e.g., [7, 8]).

Method

A qualitative content analysis design was used to explore and describe participants’ experience and opinions around information sharing in a mental health context. This method involves systematic identification and description of both manifest and latent content of narrative data [21]. Ethical approval for the project was granted by The University of Sydney Human Research Ethics Committee (Protocol #2023/141). Researchers with lived experience of mental health challenges were involved in the conceptualisation, data collection, write up and analysis of this study.

Context

The project team included backgrounds in occupational therapy (3), psychology (3), social work (1), and digital design (2). Five of the team had expertise in co-design and implementation research, three team members had direct lived experience of mental health service use and all but two of the team had experience in mental health service delivery.

The data for this paper were collected as part of a larger study, the purpose of which was to generate ideas, design and test an app to facilitate information sharing and collaboration between mental health workers and people using mental health services. This larger project will be described in detail in upcoming publications. The first stage of this project was a series of nine human-centred co-design labs in Sydney, Australia. The labs were facilitated by HG with both lived experience and human centred co-design expertise. Over eight months during 2023–2024, co-design participants worked iteratively to define the challenge, generate ideas to solve the problem, and prototype and test ‘end user’ desired app features. As part of the first design lab, group members participated in a focused group activity designed to explore and understand their experiences with providing and sharing health information with mental health workers and mental health and related organisations. Participants in the focused group activity used simple generative tools specifically designed by the team to map and explore their experiences and perspectives [22]. This paper reports the analysis of the data collected from people with lived experience of mental health service use in that activity.

Sampling and recruitment

Participants in the larger study were people with experience of using and/or providing mental health services. This was the only eligibility criterion apart from an interest in participating in the design of an app to facilitate collaborative recovery planning. Information, including a Participant Information Statement and a link to an expression of interest form, was distributed via the researchers’ networks and community managed mental health organisations operating in and around Sydney. Those who volunteered were provided with an opportunity to ask questions prior to providing their informed consent, including for data generated during the design labs to be recorded and analysed for research purposes. Participants who were not supported by an employer to attend were compensated for their time. Recruitment continued until 20 participants had been recruited for the design labs. One participant subsequently withdrew for personal reasons, one was unable to attend the first design lab, and two were mental health workers without their own lived experience of mental health challenges. Although they contributed to the discussion, their inputs were not included in this analysis, which focused on lived experience perspectives. The results are therefore drawn from 16 people with lived experience of mental health challenges.

Data collection

The design lab participants were divided into 4 groups, ensuring a spread of lived and work experience across groups. Each group was facilitated by one or two members of the research team and lasted 105 min. Participants were provided with generative tools in the form of large pieces of paper and wooden cut-outs to represent people, places and services, thoughts and feelings, and information collection tools. They were asked to discuss and create a visual map to describe their collective experiences of sharing information relating to mental health and recovery. Tracing paper was used to flag aspects of information sharing that were less transparent to participants. Lines drawn and explanatory notes written by participants showed connections and additional thoughts. Throughout the activity participants were asked to talk aloud. Facilitators asked probing questions (e.g., “why do you think that information is needed” or “how do you feel about giving that information?”) and sought clarifications and reflections from the group. After the focused group activity, the larger group reassembled and each group reported the themes and issues that stood out from their groups, giving other participants the opportunity to ask questions and comment on the ideas expressed by each group. This discussion lasted approximately 30 min. With participants’ permission, the conversations during the focused group activity and full group discussion were audio-recorded and transcribed. Photographs of the visual maps and a recording or the reflections of the researchers after the activity were taken to assist with interpreting the transcripts. Data analysis

Data from the 16 participants who had lived experience of mental health service use were analysed using constant comparative analysis, a well-established technique often associated with grounded theory studies [23], but equally useful for qualitative content analysis. Authors 1–6 engaged in detailed discussion of the data and its meanings. Coding was primarily conducted by Author 1, with checking and input from Author 2. Codes were inductively developed by identifying the underlying concept in each chunk of data (such as a sentence or phrase) and assigning a code name. For example, one of the initial codes assigned to the comment “I got copies of the psychiatric report …a lot of it was incorrect” was “incorrect information from experts”. Subsequent chunks of data were compared to existing codes to ascertain whether they revealed similar concepts. If not, new codes were generated. As coding progressed, codes were compared to each other. Similar codes were merged or grouped into higher level categories. For example, the initial code “incorrect information from experts” was grouped with other codes into the higher-level category “risks of misuse of the information held”. The higher-level codes were compared to each other to ascertain their relationships and code names adjusted to best reflect these and establish the final categories. For example “risks of misuse of the information held” became the category “Information held about me can be misused”, a subcategory of the category “The risks”. This detailed process helped to ensure that the final categories represent participants’ responses as closely as possible rather than the researchers’ pre-conceived ideas. NVivo 12 software was used to manage the data and coding.

Findings

Of the 16 people with lived experience of mental health issues, five also worked in the mental health sector as peer workers. Three worked in the non-government sector and two in government services. People’s experience of accessing services straddled the mental health sector including the use of government, non-government and private services. Throughout the discussion, all participants drew not only on their own personal experiences, but on their understandings of the experiences of people they knew or had worked with. Ten participants were male, and six were female.

Throughout these findings we use the term “health information” to refer to all personal information that is collected or held by health services, even if that information was initially collected by or is also held by non-health entities. Participants described health information about them being held by many different entities. Some of this was information they had provided, though not always with full knowledge and permission. Other information was generated and/or shared by other parties. Participants discussed the risks they saw with the collection and holding of this information, how they responded to that risk, and the various changes they would like to see in how mental health and related services collect and manage their information. Quotes are identified by participant numbers.

My health information

My health information is everywhere

Participants discussed a wide range of service providers and organisations who held health information about them. Some people expressed that the amount of information held about them by different entities was immense and unknowable. P3, for example, said that they had “signed so many forms and been to so many government departments that they probably know everything about my life by now”. Similarly P1 characterised the organisations that held their health information as “literally anywhere… it’s never ending”.

Health services that participants described as holding their health information included psychiatrists, GPs, allied health professionals, pharmacists, support workers, peer workers, hospitals, NGOs, community mental health services and emergency services. Non-health organisations identified as holding participants’ health information included both government and private entities such as income support, health, child protection, immigration and taxation departments, disability services, educational institutions, workplaces, schools/childcare, employment services, housing services, immigration, insurance companies, police and the legal system.

Providing information is a varied experience

People shared a variety of health information with various entities, however it was not always given with full knowledge and willingness. Rather it was provided for a variety of reasons, not all of which implied willingness and meaningful consent.

Sharing freely

In the best circumstances, information was given because the person wanted to provide it. For example, the person trusted a health worker and believed the information would be used to benefit them or would allow the health worker to better understand, help or support them. P8 described providing information “when you’re empowered and you – and I - go ‘yeah, and I trust you, look what I’ve done and look where I’ve come from’”.

Transactional sharing

Other information was provided in a far less optimal way. In some cases it was seen as a required exchange; it was given in order to get the support services needed, for example, for service intake or applications for housing or government disability funding. P6 summarised this idea, saying: “You give them the information and they give you services.” P12 was even more frank: “you’re in a tough spot but you’ve got to do it – you don’t want to do it but you have to do it”. P8 gave the example of people providing any information requested simply “because I just want to be in a safe bed tonight”. Other information was given because of a perception of legal obligation; examples included information required by workplaces or entities in the legal system, such as probation officers.

Sharing for others’ agendas

Sometimes people felt pushed or obliged to provide information that were not actually required for service, as P7 described: “they really coerce you to do [the assessment, even though] it’s not mandatory.” In other instances, people simply provided information because they were expected or asked to. They often did so without knowing what would be done with the information, why it was needed or the implications of providing or not providing it. As P6 reported “It’s just, okay, form done, tick the box, and then I’ll do it in a few months’ time.” People often suspected that the information was collected less for their direct support and more as standard procedure or for the organisation’s purposes, such as “head count for funding” (P15).

Assumed knowledge

A few participants also talked about accidentally sharing information about themselves because of circumstances or misunderstanding. P4 “mentioned I had a mental illness” to [a disability support] worker because “I thought that she knew anyway. She had a blank look, I’m like, ‘ooh no, I just overshared something she didn’t even know.’”.

A lot of information is ‘about me’ not ‘from me’

Much of the health information that participants reported being held about them had not been directly provided by themselves. Rather, it was information that service providers had generated about them, or that had been shared between organisations.

Some information held about them represented the views, perspectives and assessments of people with whom they had interacted, such as “the paramedic that happened to notice something” (P8). This information included observations of their behaviour and professionals’ assessments of their physical or mental status or history. These were documented in case notes, incident reports, “functioning reports, OT [occupational therapy] reports” (P7), clinician rated assessments, scores and interpretations from participant-completed assessments, and summary reports.

People also reported that a considerable proportion of the health information that various entities held about them had been obtained from other organisations. This included both information the person had provided to another organisations and information about them that another organisation had generated. This information sharing was sometimes done in their presence, for example at case conferences, or with their knowledge and permission. However, at other times it appeared to occur behind the scenes and was poorly understood. As P6 summarised “there’s also the information that they have that you didn’t give them that they somehow got from [other services]”. The general feeling was that a great deal of information was shared and that this sharing was often invisible to the person themselves. P3 and others described times when “people seemed to know more about me than what I told them” and that they “didn’t realise at the time that (the worker) had full access to my medical files”. Feelings about this varied, with different participants describing or exhibiting responses including acceptance, apathy, powerlessness, frustration, anger and even feeling “paranoid, with all the information being shared” (P4).

Inappropriate sharing could even occur within organisations, when people had access to information that they had no legitimate reason to know. Three peer workers, for example, talked about inadvertently hearing private information when telehealth consultations were conducted in an open office. Another participant emphasised that privacy of health information depends on every individual person who has access to it.

P5: I guess it’s like the individual holds the trust and does the right thing… the individual, how they interpret the data and what they think they can do with it or not do with it. So it takes everybody [to] play a part in trusting. You can only have systems in place so much but the individual still needs to back it up or support it, I guess.

The risks

Participants believed that their health information could be used by organisations to benefit them, for example, to facilitate their recovery, decide the correct treatment, or to protect or help them. Nevertheless, they also saw and had experienced a number of problems and risks. Some related to the collection of information from them. Other risks related to the retention, storage and use of that information.

Providing information can be distressing

Participants talked about how providing information was not always a positive or neutral experience. It could often be a frustrating, annoying and even distressing experience. They expressed frustration, annoyance and a sense of depersonalisation about situations where they had been asked to provide information that seemed excessive or unnecessary, or when they had to repeat the same information and stories, especially when they were or should have been on record. This was common, as exemplified in the following exchange:

Facilitator: So where in this do you feel like you tell the story more often than you should have to?

P15: Christ, everywhere.

P16: Gov[ernment] just always asking for information that they’ve already got… I think with hospitals, they have all that information; they just want to hear you say it.

Providing information was also uncomfortable and distressing when people were required to disclose sensitive and even traumatic information. P7 described having to describe their trauma to a stranger who would not be providing any service, but had the power to assess their eligibility for service. They “felt angry. But I just thought, maybe I’m being too – maybe I should just deal with it, or take it on the chin. But I felt, yeah, I didn’t feel comfortable with it.” What distressed people could also be very individual. P8 gave the example of people feeling uncomfortable even being asked their address if “some of the trauma, or the problem is coming from [there], or you don’t have an address and there’s shame around that”.

Participants described having to phrase information in negative and pessimistic ways in order to get some services, and especially for disability funding under Australia’s National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS). Some wondered about how the negative picture they were forced to paint might influence people’s recovery:

P11: If you ask all those horrible questions about yourself, you might internalise that negativity on your life….

Worker: Yeah, it's like self-prophesising in a sense…

P12: It might have a subtle impact on your subconscious or something, yeah.

Information held about me can be misused

Whether health information had been provided by them, shared by other organisations, or generated by other people, most participants were concerned with the risk of this information being misused, as shown in this exchange:

P14: Will they use it against me?….

P16: Yeah, I worry about that as well…

P15: How will they use it against me is often how I have to measure it.

Fear was caused by the power that some of these entities held. P11, for example, wondered if his concerns about two government departments might be excessive but concluded that “I’m not paranoid, I’m legitimately frightened of them.” Perceptions of risk around misuse of information were amplified when the information that organisations held and relied upon was inaccurate. P15 described “several reports where there’s incorrect information that has been reported to be fact that then is weighed and judged upon me”.

Participants believed that their health information could be, and sometimes had been used to stigmatise, stereotype, discriminate or make inappropriate decisions about them. Several had experienced their information being used to deny them the services and supports that they needed. P12 illustrated this saying: “My psychologist found out I was using [drugs] and he just dropped me.” A peer worker described how records of “offences that have been done in the past”, which were often simplistic and misleading, were misused: “so they’re just like, ‘you used drugs in the past, we’re going to put you into this, this, this’ as opposed to understanding the whole story as to why.” Similarly, P17 talked about how people they knew “worried if you were schizophrenic that you’re going to have your kids taken off you”.

Addressing the risks

Minimising the risks for myself

While some participants felt powerless in the face of the mass of health information held about them, others reported taking active steps to address the risks described above. This action, however, was necessarily limited to restricting the information that they provided.

Some suggested that it was important to be assertive and intentional in knowledge sharing, and to question service providers requests for information that seemed inappropriate.

P5: You have to accept responsibility for yourself and finding out as much as you can, sort of thing. So not just hand it over to the people in the know or think they’re in the know….

P1 Yeah, do some research.

P5: Yeah.

P1 Actually have a look into things yourself.

Others at times had refused to answer questions, shared only selected information, or provided information that was not necessarily accurate so that the box could be ticked or they could get the outcome they wanted. As P8 said: “We become very skilled in going, ‘no, I’ll tell you what you want to hear’”.

In some cases, people described simply forgoing the services they needed or opportunities they wanted because of requirements for the provision of excessive and/or sensitive information. This was particularly emphasised in regard to disability funding applications. P10, a peer worker, felt that the extensive amount of information required “almost discourages [the people I work with] to apply for it… like ‘okay, actually screw it, I don’t want to do it in the end’”. P12 gave up the opportunity to do an education course because “they asked you your diagnosis and they wanted a written confirmation from your treating doctor and psychiatrist… I thought, ‘I’m not going to do that.”

How systems and services can minimise risks

Participants detailed a number of factors that they would like to see changed in order to reduce the risks involved with collection, storage and use of their health information by mental health and related services.

Customising information collection

Rather than everybody having to do the same assessments in the same way (“a one-formula questionnaire” P10) some participants thought that the way information is collected should take into account the preferences and needs of service users. This included preferences for what tools were used, when they were completed, and whether the assessment was completed with a worker or independently. P11 provided the example of wanting to be able to say to a worker “’I don’t want you to do the mini mental state on me, I want to do a recovery tool’ … Even just to say, ‘can you understand I’m not feeling well today, and I just don’t want another form to fill out’.

Collecting information transparently

Participants also wanted service providers to be clear, when they asked for information, about the implications of providing that information: “where the information is going… how it’s going to be used and specifically for what” (P10). They also wanted to know the implications of not sharing the information. For example P18 wanted workers to be explicit about any service implications of refusing to provide answers, while P5 wanted to know “is there things that you are protected with, or don’t have to answer?”

Giving me access to my information

Participants wanted to be able to access the health information that organisations held about them. A number described a sense currently that they did not know what information was held and that information generated by others existed that they had not been told about. P14, for example “found out years later that there was like a diagnosis that I never even knew about because I’d never seen the notes”. Several others talked about never being told the results of assessments that they had completed. P8 summarised the concern:

P8: I think often it’s things that we’re not even aware of. So, I think it’s information that sits out there that does impact us… So, there’s information that some gatekeeper’s holding over there, and they’re making judgements, and all this is impacting us here.

People felt that this information should be relatively easy for them to access and that both the possibility and process of accessing it should be clear. Again, this was not seen as currently being the case. A couple of people expressed that people who used mental health services were not encouraged to access their health information and that, consequently, many were not aware that they could do so. Occasionally participants attributed negative motives to this lack of openness. For example, P17 suggested that workers “look down upon people with mental health thinking, ‘you wouldn’t be able to understand that’”, while P16 suspected that workers discouraged people from viewing their information because “if they make a mistake they’re less likely to get caught out if no one’s looking at it”.

Even when people knew that they had a right to the information held about them, most reported that it was not easy to obtain. Instead it was “a whole big process” (P11) involving applying, waiting, and sometimes being “deemed that I’m not allowed to get it” (P8).

Giving opportunities to influence the information held and shared about me

A number of participants said that they not only wanted to be able to see the information held about them, they wanted the opportunity to influence that information. Most described a sense that once an organisation had collected information about them, this information was then out of their hands and out of their control; they had no ability to question, remove, correct, or add nuance or feedback to that information and little ability to control who it was shared with. P16 summarised their group’s discussion about this.

P16: I think that’s the nature of the conflict is who is the ultimate owner of the information? Us, in our group think that individuals should be. It seems that for whatever reason society has given that over to hospitals and clinics or whatever because they’re the ones that control it.

Participants were particularly concerned about their inability to change information generated by health professionals that was not accurate. As P15 questioned: “Where do I get to go and say, ‘that’s an incorrect piece of information’?” But some participants also wanted the opportunity to include their own perspectives in the information generated by others them, for example, by reading, correcting and adding comments to reports to “complement their record-keeping with more information” (P15), or simply by adding their own perspective. P1 suggested having 10–15 min at the end of each conversation to discuss what the person would like added to their health information: “that’s when you can draft your blurb about how you feel like you were going or where you feel like you’re improving, or just the whole experience.”

Whether true or untrue, the inability for people to revoke or correct information or to influence its interpretation, was thought to make some information inappropriately ‘sticky’. Sticky information was seen as being applied to a person even when it was simplistic or no longer appropriate and followed people around between organisations. P5 provided the example of disclosing a heroin addiction he saw as purely historical information after not having used drugs for 15 years, and finding this included on all his subsequent referrals “because maybe they think, well, if he’s a drug addict, he’s always going to behave in a certain way.” Other information, such as the person’s strengths and positive qualities, was often far less sticky, quickly falling out of a person’s history and being forgotten.

Participants also often wanted to choose who to share information with and, importantly, what specific information to share. While they were often asked to consent for their information to be shared, several people felt that this consent was too broad, and wanted options to make more specific choices. P18, for example, wanted to consent to “each transaction as opposed to a broad consent”, P15 thought that broad consent did not cover “what you share and when and how? That’s what comes down to, well, ‘hang on, how much do I get to control the content?’”.

Participants were not only concerned about preventing the inappropriate sharing of information between organisations, but also about ensuring that information is appropriately, and with their permission, shared in a way “that is meaningful to other people who are going to treat you to use for whatever is going to help you in your recovery” (P15) and to prevent them from having to repeat their story multiple times unnecessarily.

Using information for my benefit

Finally and unsurprisingly, people wanted the information they provided and that was held about them to be used only for their benefit rather than to deprive them of services or stigmatise them, as discussed above. Essentially they wanted their health information to be used “To protect people and not to abuse power” (P12).

Discussion

This study is the first, to the authors’ knowledge, to examine health information sharing from the perspectives of Australians who use mental health services. Participants believed that their information was held by a wide variety of entities, and that the nature, extent and location of the information held was often opaque. They had provided some of this information themselves, though not always with full understanding, intention and willingness. Other information, however, had been generated by other people or obtained from other entities, often without the person’s knowledge or permission. People perceived risks to themselves in both the provision and the potential uses of their information, and some responded by restricting the information they provided. Participants cited a number of changes they would like to see in the ways that mental health and related services collected and managed their information. These were: customised and transparent information collection; allowing people to not only access their own information, but have input into what information was held and shared, and for the information about them to only be used for their benefit.

A thread clearly woven through these findings is one of choice. It was clear that participants wanted to exercise choice and control over the information about them that was collected, stored and shared but currently felt that they had very little choice or control over this. Of all the information held about them, they usually only had choices about a small subset - the information that they directly provided to organisations - and even provision of this information was not always a free choice. This is particularly important because it occurs in a broader historical, and arguably ongoing context of the choices and autonomy of people with mental health challenges being removed or ignored [24]. Choice and control within the context of health systems have been recognised internationally as fundamental human rights [25, 26] and are inherent to the international disability activist motto and agenda of “nothing about us without us” [27]. Further, mental health systems internationally are required by policy to shift from a more controlling orientation towards a greater recovery-orientation [28]. Core to this orientation is greater service user self-determination or choice and control [29]. Whilst there may be significant systemic benefits of information sharing, it is clear from participants that both information collection and information sharing processes may inadvertently risk eroding personal recovery outcomes such as people’s sense of personal agency, meaning making, choice and control [30]. Collection and sharing of information practices risk working in opposition to principles and policies espousing greater recovery-orientation in service delivery (e.g., [31]).

The findings highlight that the act of providing health information is not always neutral. For example, people who use mental health services have previously expressed their dislike of repeating their stories (e.g.,[7]). Some described feeling frustrated and worried by repeated questioning, took it to indicate that staff had not looked at the existing notes or were trying to catch them out, and believed that it could result in delayed treatment [32]. A more recent synthesis of mental health service user experiences of information collection highlighted the importance of adopting trauma-informed approaches that included humanising the process and providing information about what would happen [33]. Clearly it would be beneficial to the relationships between mental health service users and providers for information to be collected only when there is a clear reason to do so, with explicit information provided about what information is already held, why the additional information is needed, what will be done with it, and what the implications of refusing to provide the information are. “Co-Created Notes”, and accompanying reflections, developed by survivor advocate Indigo Daya [34] is an excellent resource to support a shift in data collection practice towards one that facilitates collaboration, transparency and recovery-orientation.

Our participants’ wishes to access their records and correct or dispute inaccurate information echo perspectives expressed by people who use mental health and other health services internationally [7, 8, 35]. The ability to contribute to health records, alongside control over sharing, may help reduce the “stickiness” of stigmatising or negative information and improve the “stickiness” of positive or nuanced information shared by people themselves. From a systemic perspective, greater service user involvement in the development of information collection plans to ensure that information collected and shared is relevant and fit for purpose is a key recommendation within the recent Australian National Mental Health and Suicide Prevention Plan (2019).

While some researchers have expressed concerns about the potential emotional impacts of people being able to see their own health information (e.g., [36]), this does not, except in unusual circumstances, justify withholding information. Rather people should be supported through the process of accessing their information if they wish to undertake it. In a mental health context, some measure of the concerns about distress can be addressed at the front end, by clinicians writing their reports and notes in a positive, strengths focused and recovery-oriented way. Such records are likely to be far less distressing than those replete with pejorative and deficit focused statements. It was apparent from the comments of some of our participants that a lack of transparency can result in people distrusting the motives of organisations. Having a mechanism to at least register disagreement with, or document their perspective on information that people perceive to be inaccurate or biased should also be an inbuilt component of Electronic Health Records.

Similar to research conducted in other countries, our research indicates that people often had little to no knowledge about how and with whom their health information was shared [35, 37] and felt that they had little control over it. They wanted the opportunity to consent or refuse to share particular pieces of information with particular people. “Patient-controlled granular information sharing” is where people decide which information to share with whom and for what purpose [38]. Research indicates that people prefer this approach and want to choose the privacy levels they are comfortable with in specific situations, especially around sharing of sensitive information such as mental health status [35, 39–41]. This not only includes sharing between organisations, but deciding who is able to access information within a practice, with information only being accessible on a “need to know” basis [35]. Some scholars and clinicians also support this position, believing that, despite technological capabilities, maintaining privacy is critical to maintaining service user-provider trust and people’s willingness to both present for care and disclose sensitive information (e.g., [42]).

However, many researchers and health care providers have also voiced concerns that offering service users control over the sharing of health care information would interrupt providers’ ability to access accurate information in a timely manner, compromising care, relationships between health service users and providers, and service user safety (e.g., [42, 43]). A major concern expressed by providers is that health service users generally, and people with mental health challenges specifically, lack an understanding of what information is relevant and needs to be shared [38, 42]. In one study, when service users at a public hospital were given the option to restrict access to their information for some staff members, 5% chose to allow no access to any information in their health records for any of the staff, while 26% refused access by all doctors or all nurses in the practice [41]. While this may appear troubling, participants’ rationales for restricting access were not collected, making this difficult to interpret. Health professionals working with people with behavioural and mental health challenges have reported being concerned about service users limiting access to ‘need to know’ information due to their lack of understanding of the information relevancy for others within the service or system ([38], p.11). This may reflect a view that a service user’s choice to withhold information is an irrational one based on ignorance. Yet it is unwise to assume that health workers’ interpretations are necessarily the correct ones. Shared information that is perceived as reasonable and intended to help and protect by workers may be seen as discriminatory and carrying a risk of negative consequences by people who use mental health services as described by participants in our study.

Some health service users agree that they are not the best judge of when access to their information is necessary. In one study 20% of participants expressed these views [35]. Other research with vulnerable health service users has indicated that, while they wanted granular control, they were aware of the risk to their care of withholding information, and were therefore often willing to give access to all information (e.g., [37, 41]). It is clear that wanting the ability to dictate who can and cannot access one’s health information does not necessarily mean that access is restricted. Participants in our study wanted meaningful sharing of information that was relevant to a particular clinician, would promote appropriate or enhanced care, and could avoid the need for repetition. These wishes are not contradictory to the desire for granular control. Rather, they demonstrate the importance of informed choice, even where the choice may ultimately be unlimited sharing. Many participants had experienced or witnessed too many instances of inappropriate use of health information to trust others to make decisions about sharing their information. Some appeared to experience the worst of both worlds: they felt that their information was everywhere, yet they still had to repeat their stories. As some authors have noted, health professionals in the age of information sharing technologies may experience a “data deluge,” where they have access to so much information about each service user that they cannot process it all [42, 44].

Limitations and future research

This study should be interpreted with consideration for several limitations. Participants were part of a larger project that involved designing a recovery focused app. They may, therefore, have been more advocacy focused and aware of and interested in issues around mental health service provision. While this made them information rich participants, we do not claim that they are representative of all people with lived experience of mental health challenges. Further, a group format for data collection always involves a risk that the views of more reticent participants or those with views contradicting the majority may be overshadowed or overlooked. However, anonymous feedback on the design workshops indicated that people felt that saw the context as a safe environment and felt able to share, suggesting that this risk may not be too large. These findings indicate that much future research is needed. Just two important areas of inquiry include first, gaining a greater understanding of the current varying levels of service user access and influence over their personal data, and second, examining the impact of this variance on therapeutic alliance and trust as well as personal recovery including sense of agency and self-determination. Evaluating the implementation of lived experience developed (e.g., [34]) or co-designed resources to support greater service user control over their data collection experiences would support practice change initiatives.

Conclusion

In terms of information collection and sharing, mental health service users and health professionals alike agree that trust is key [7, 20]. Trust may be eroded when services ask for information that seems unnecessary or when true consent is compromised because the price of refusal is losing access to needed services or benefits. By not being open to having service users viewing, contributing to, and having a say in who is able to access their information, health workers risk losing trust as service users may assume the organisation has something to hide. When distrust results in people making use of the only control they have – withholding the direct and honest provision of relevant information – this is likely to be detrimental. Ultimately services need people to provide their information willingly, because they understand and agree with the purpose and what will happen to it. Concerns about people not allowing access to all relevant information that is held about them can be largely alleviated by the cultivation of trust through transparency, the education of service users about the necessary uses of information, and direct discussion where gaps in information are apparent [38, 41, 45].
