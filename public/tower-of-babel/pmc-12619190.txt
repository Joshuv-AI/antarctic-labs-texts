Development and evaluation of a systems-based point-of-care ultrasound curriculum for a master of science in athletic training program
Authors: Joshua I Johnson, Christopher M Ludwig, Russell T Baker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs an imaging modality, point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) holds substantial value to health professionals who are adequately trained in its use. The athletic training profession is an allied health profession with significant potential to adopt POCUS in training and practice. However, published research on POCUS curricula for graduate entry-level programs in athletic training is lacking. Furthermore, there are no published studies of learning outcomes from POCUS courses for graduate entry-level athletic training students.MethodsWe developed a POCUS curriculum tailored for a graduate athletic training program. Following updated Commission on Accreditation of Athletic Training Education standards, we described POCUS evaluation of select organ systems in the abdomen and thorax, as well as the extended focused assessment with sonography in trauma (eFAST) protocol. Course efficacy was assessed with a prospective observational study using a convenience sample of entry-level graduate athletic training students enrolled in our elective course. All (n = 15) students were starting the second year in a Master of Science in Athletic Training (MSAT) program. Pre-and post-course tests were administered to assess cognitive learning outcomes, and an imaging practical exam was developed to gauge students’ psychomotor proficiency. In addition, students’ experiences were evaluated with a post course survey in the following self-perceived domains: (1) confidence in their future education and clinical practice; (2) satisfaction with the course; and (3) confidence in their POCUS proficiency.ResultsStudents demonstrated significant learning in cognitive domains (pre-test average = 48%, post-test average = 73%, P < 0.001), displayed proficiency in congruence with our course objectives in a practical evaluation, reported satisfaction with a variety of course features, and gained confidence in POCUS skills. Notably, students were dissatisfied with the brevity of the course and displayed heterogeneous proficiency across applications.ConclusionsStudents in a professional MSAT program can develop meaningful knowledge and proficiency in the use of POCUS across organ systems following a brief elective. Additional training is needed to facilitate clinical implementation across the profession. Positive perceptions and self-confidence among students following POCUS training may help support POCUS adoption by athletic trainers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08178-4.Keywords: Curriculum evaluation, Allied health education, Athletic training, Interprofessional education, EFAST

Introduction

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) refers to the use of diagnostic ultrasound by a practitioner to answer focused clinical questions at the bedside [1]. Health professionals have adopted POCUS as a modality because equipment is now portable, affordable, and capable of producing dynamic high-quality imaging without radiation [1]. POCUS implementation, however, is operator dependent and its use requires adequate training to develop requisite knowledge and skills. Therefore, POCUS courses developed for health professionals should measure performance across cognitive and psychomotor domains [2–4]. Significant barriers to POCUS education development and implementation remain common, including a lack of, course standards, equipment, faculty, and curricular time [5].

Nevertheless, the value of POCUS to healthcare professionals in sports medicine and physiatry has been recognized for decades [6–10]. Moreover, allied health professions such as paramedics [11, 12], physicians assistants [13, 14], physical therapists [15, 16], and chiropractors [17] are moving to adopt POCUS. Despite these developments, ultrasound education in athletic training remains scarce. Bliven et al., [18, 19] describe a phased approach to integrate ultrasound across existing athletic training curricula, and Schroeder et al., [20] described a six-month continuing education course; however, content in these approaches was focused on musculoskeletal (MSK) applications, which are highly technical [21]. Athletic trainers can benefit from POCUS applications beyond MSK, both as novice learners (i.e., starting with applications easier to learn), and in clinical practice [8, 22, 23]. Moreover, athletic training practice has expanded to cover a range of patient populations, practice settings, and organ systems [22, 23]. While a mature POCUS curriculum in athletic training should include MSK-specific content, organ-system based coursework provides an accessible starting point while addressing the broadening scope of the profession. For example, accreditation standards require that athletic training students learn to evaluate and manage medical conditions affecting the cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, genitourinary, and respiratory systems, as well as to perform or obtain diagnostic results including from imaging studies [22]. Educators must curate appropriate curricula in response [24].

The increased use of POCUS in contemporary sports medicine and general allied health care coincides with the ongoing efforts to establish a broader scope and higher standard of care within athletic training [24–27]. As a result, athletic training is well positioned to benefit from increased exposure to POCUS education across a range of applications. However, to date, no published curricula exist detailing a broad organ-system based approach to POCUS curricula in athletic training education. Additionally, no authors have reported student learning outcomes achieved in athletic training POCUS courses. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to address these gaps in the literature through the description of a POCUS course in a Master of Science in Athletic Training (MSAT) program and assessment of relevant student outcomes. The curriculum introduced POCUS through an organ-system approach in the abdomen and thorax following an established conceptual model [28]. In addition, outcomes from written and practical assessments to measure students’ knowledge and proficiency development are reported. Finally, a post course survey captured insights into student perceptions and experiences. Together, these efforts provide preliminary evidence for a novel approach to POCUS education in the athletic training profession and generate transferrable insights to institutions hoping to pursue similar efforts.

Methods

Research design

A prospective observational study design was used and certified exempt by the University of Idaho Institutional Review Board (Protocol 22–105).

Course design

The course began with an introduction to principles and instrumentation of sonography essential to the operation and implementation of POCUS [19, 29, 30]. Subsequent content introduced applications of POCUS through the indications, acquisition, interpretation, medical decision making (I-AIM; Fig. 1) model of ultrasound which delineated steps necessary for scanning in all clinical scenarios [28]. For example, health practitioners apply interventions based on appropriate clinical indications. Once an indication for POCUS has been identified (e.g., flank pain, hematuria), the operator must acquire the indicated sonographic images (e.g., scan kidneys and bladder as indicated by flank pain), interpret the findings (e.g., evaluate images for optimal quality, then determine the presence or absence of pathology such as nephrolithiasis or hydronephrosis), and incorporate the data into ensuing decisions (e.g., diagnosis, referral, treatment plan). To ensure accuracy and relevancy, clinical content was sourced from published POCUS guidelines [31], then tailored to athletic training practice [23] and standards for accreditation of athletic training education [22].

The I-AIM model. Each step of the I-AIM model, a description of the concept, and operationalized decision tree. Our curriculum stops short of training students to make medical decisions based on clinical findings as this is more appropriate for advanced courses in clinical settings

Didactic course meetings applied the I-AIM model to POCUS in the renal/urinary, hepatobiliary, cardiac, and pulmonary systems, as well as examination of the aorta and inferior vena cava. The penultimate course meeting and imaging practical assessment were dedicated to the extended focused assessment with sonography in trauma (eFAST) protocol. We included the eFAST for three reasons. First, it is indicated in patients with blunt or penetrating trauma, particularly when clinical status worsens or decompensation occurs [8]. For this reason, the protocol is a cornerstone of emergency medicine and is frequently introduced to prehospital and allied health professionals [8, 11–14]. Second, the eFAST builds on scanning techniques from earlier sessions, reinforcing prior learning while extending students’ system-based review. Third, the eFAST is linked to a broad set of skills that are directly applicable to trauma assessment and clinical decision-making, while also offering a natural framework to assess imaging proficiency across multiple domains.

To deliver didactic content, students were provided short asynchronous videos (e.g., www.5minutesono.com), and 30-minute lectures were delivered at the outset of each 90-minute class. Didactic content reinforced relevant gross anatomy, then outlined the process of image acquisition and interpretation as indicated by various systemic findings. Standard images were defined and described with attention to important anatomical landmarks and conventional image orientation (e.g., probe marker orientation, tomographic axis). Normal and pathological sonographic findings were contrasted in lecture materials. To facilitate access to clinical images, materials were sourced from the POCUS atlas (www.POCUSatlas.com), a free open access resource library of sonograms organized by diagnosis (including normal) and organ system. The POCUS atlas was particularly valuable in developing didactic content without an in-house source of clinical POCUS images. Following each lecture, the remaining class time (~ 60 min) was dedicated to hands-on scanning practice.

Students scanned in pairs, alternating between sonographer and (if willing) scanning model. In the case of incidental findings in students who served as scanning models, the policy was to refer students for follow up with their primary care provider. However, no abnormal findings were encountered. Each pair of students were provided with an all-in-one Butterfly IQ + POCUS probe (Butterfly Inc., Burlington MA), succinct scanning guides to model image acquisition, and multiple scanning instructors were present to provide real-time feedback.

Course development and instruction was led by (JIJ), an anatomist with POCUS teaching experience in medical education [32]. A second faculty (CL) was an athletic trainer and clinical faculty in the MSAT program. Each contributed to curriculum design by ensuring congruence of POCUS content and learning objectives with the MSAT program objectives; further, each participated in faculty development (e.g., CORE ultrasound fundamentals course, Point-of-Care Ultrasound Academy Fundamentals Certification) to learn and teach POCUS. To increase the available instructors, first-year medical students with POCUS experience were invited to support student learning; three medical students volunteered and were selected for these roles. The medical student instructors were in the final weeks of their first year in the medical school curriculum at our campus and had completed a six-month POCUS elective course described previously [32].

The athletic training POCUS course consisted of six 90-minute sessions scheduled each Tuesday and Thursday evening across the first three weeks of the MSAT program summer semester in 2022. Table 1 outlines the topics and learning objectives for each class. In total, students received 2.5 h of didactic input and participated in 5 h of supported scanning practice. Students submitted sonograms at each class meeting to document attendance and participation.

Participants

A convenience sample of students who voluntarily enrolled in the POCUS elective course during the summer semester of 2022 was used for the study. Fifteen of 23 students in the cohort enrolled in the POCUS course; participation in the associated research component was voluntary and students could withdraw at any time without consequence. Informed consent was obtained in writing from all participants prior to completing any research activities.

Knowledge assessment

An overarching goal of the course was for students to develop an understanding of theoretical didactic components undergirding POCUS use. Therefore, to measure cognitive learning outcomes, we used a previously reported [32] written pre- post course test. However, the original test consisted of 30 questions that covered content areas excluded from the current MSAT curriculum, such as obstetrics and ultrasound guided procedures. Therefore, we modified the test for the scope of this course by removing extraneous items on topics omitted from the current curricula. The modified test covered all content areas of the curriculum (e.g., principles and instrumentation of sonography, image interpretation across a range of applications), included 15 multiple choice questions, three true/false questions, and two fill in the blank questions. One fill in the blank was a four-part question, bringing the total point value of the test to 23. The test was administered prior to commencement of the first teaching session; an identical test was administered at the conclusion of the course. Tests were distributed on paper in class and students were not able to see or review testing materials outside of the two exam delivery windows.

Imaging practical assessment

The final course meeting was dedicated to assessment. After the written post-test was administered, students performed a simulated eFAST exam to evaluate their psychomotor proficiency across a range of image acquisitions. The eFAST exam was developed to rapidly assess trauma patients for the presence of pneumothorax and free fluid within the peritoneal, pericardial, or pleural cavities [8]. To achieve these goals, practitioners scan standard locations with high sensitivity for collecting free fluid or gas in the respective cavities (Table 2; Fig. 2). The imaging practical required students to acquire, optimize, record, and submit brief video clips (sonograms) for each of the eFAST views.

Students were instructed on important aspects of performing the eFAST and spent part of each class period practicing relevant image acquisition. However, to set clear expectations, faculty provided a live demonstration of the eFAST based on the criteria in the rubrics below (Tables 2 and 3) immediately prior to the practical assessment. Rubrics were based on published criteria underlying conventional eFAST image acquisition and optimization [31] which were described throughout our curriculum and narratively reiterated in the demonstration. Finally, during the assessment, students were provided with imaging guides identical to those developed for the didactic eFAST class, which included anatomical landmarks, conventional image orientation, landmarks for probe placement, patient positioning, and examples of exemplary sonograms for each view. For scoring purposes, we developed an image rating system similar to existing tools [2] using a three-point (0–2) Likert scale across two domains: anatomy and knobology (i.e., the art of adjusting image settings using various knobs on cart-based diagnostic ultrasound machines).

Scoring rubric for image submissions

In setting scoring criteria for anatomy, we first cataloged anatomical landmarks in each view, then assigned them status as major landmarks or detailed landmarks in the domain of anatomy (Table 2). We defined major landmarks as those required to identify the image submission without annotations. For example, a view of the liver, kidney, and hepatorenal recess is generally sufficient to identify the right upper quadrant (Fig. 2). Submissions lacking this level of detail (i.e., images that could not be recognized) were scored as 0-Indecipherable, while those that met this standard were scored as 1-Acceptable. A score of 2 required visualization of major and detailed landmarks, which are essential clinically to ensure thorough evaluation and demand more refined image acquisition skills. For example, when scanning the right upper quadrant in the eFAST, in addition to the major landmarks described, a provider must visualize the costophrenic recess, subphrenic space, caudal tip of the liver, and inferior pole of the kidney, searching these areas thoroughly (e.g., scan through the entire parenchyma of the kidney from anterior to posterior) to rule out free fluid in the area [31]. The spatial volume separating these structures generally requires multi-axis probe manipulations to visualize each component, demanding a higher level of imaging proficiency. Submissions that approached this standard (i.e., contained all major and minor landmarks) were scored as 2-Exceptional. Finally, we considered aspects of image orientation and probe handling skills evident in clips (e.g., skin contact, conventional probe orientation) under anatomy (Table 2). For example, an image containing all anatomical landmarks may be reduced by a point if conventional orientation was reversed, or the image was disrupted by inadequate coupling gel.

Limitations with the scoring system and faculty reviewers were considered within the study design. Specifically, we scored on the presence of anatomical features, image orientation, and image clarity, and not the diagnostic accuracy of the sonograms due to a lack of faculty with necessary clinical training in POCUS to make diagnoses, a pre-clinical student population which lacked access to pathological populations for practice, and a simulated setting that precluded the possibility of positive clinical findings during testing. Furthermore, lacking faculty resources to evaluate students while scanning, results are based on clip review after the conclusion of the assessment.

The Butterfly IQ + system operates in conjunction with a variety of touch-screen mobile devices and tablets resulting in a user-friendly knobology interface. In the pursuit of convenience, some imaging parameters (e.g., sound beam frequency, focal zone, probe type) are automated within each imaging preset. The primary settings for the Butterfly IQ + are depth, gain, and preset. Therefore, knobology was scored based on these three parameters (Table 3). Gain and depth were considered adequate when adjacent tissues had good contrast, and structures of interest were appropriately centered, respectively. Presets were considered adequate if the automated parameters were appropriate for the scan. For example, the FAST preset is acceptable across all views. Additionally, students could choose to toggle between the abdominal, bladder (pelvic), cardiac, and lung-specific presets for acquisition of the homonymous views. However, scanning the abdomen using the lung preset, for example, is not acceptable.

All image submissions were reviewed and scored by the course lead (JIJ), and the duration of students’ exams was calculated from the timestamps on the first and last clip submitted.

Survey

To collect feedback on students’ course experience, we used a survey tool that we previously developed for a POCUS course with medical students [32]. Tables 4 and 5 contain the full text of each question presented to participants in the study reported here. Modifications to the previously used survey included minor alterations to the wording or content of survey items to ensure relevance to the athletic training profession and the scope of the curriculum described here. Surveys were administered via Qualtrics (Qualtrics, Seattle, WA). A statement of informed consent was presented with the surveys, and responses were collected anonymously. Likert questions included 6-point response scales (Domain 1: Confidence in future education/practice 1 = much worse, 6 = much better; Domain 2 and 3: course satisfaction, and POCUS confidence, respectively: 1 = Strongly disagree, 6 = strongly agree) across three domains (Table 4). Open-ended questions (Table 5) captured additional feedback in these domains and asked students about their perceptions and experiences [33]. Students were notified of the survey in class, and a link to complete the survey was delivered through email at the conclusion of the course.

Data analysis

Data analyses were performed in Microsoft Excel (Version 2301). Changes in pre-post knowledge tests were assessed with a two-tailed T-test. Alpha was set at p ≤ 0.05, and Cohen’s d was calculated to determine the effect size. Effect size was defined as small (0.2), medium (0.5), large (0.8) or very large (1.3) [34]. Review of student eFAST submissions took place in the Butterfly Network database. Scores for each clip were recorded in Excel, then used to generate descriptive statistics. Likert responses were reported as mean ± standard deviation, and open-ended responses were summarized to provide greater context for student feedback.

Results

Participants

A total of 15 students (4 Male, 11 Female) signed up for the elective and gave consent to participate in the study. All participants were starting their second year in the MSAT program.

Knowledge test

Thirteen students completed the pre class test with a mean score of 11.5/23 (48.5%; SD ± 13%, High = 16/23 [69.5%], Low = 5/23 [21.7%]) and 15 completed the post course knowledge tests with a mean score of 16.9/23 (73.4%; SD ± 9.6%, High = 19/23 [82.6%], Low = 13/23 [56.5%]). The increase in test scores was statistically significant (p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 2.1).

Imaging practical

All 15 students completed the imaging practical in an average of 4.4 min (Longest = 8 min, Shortest = 2 min, SD ± 2.06 min). Students should have submitted 105 sonograms total (15 students, seven sonograms each). However, six students failed to include a sagittal view of the pelvis, one failed to submit a transverse pelvic view, and one submitted a single lung view. As the assessment stipulated the submission of these views, we scored omissions as zero in both domains. To avoid overestimating students’ imaging skills, we included all omissions in our analysis.

In the domain of anatomy, 26% (28/105) of submissions scored zero, 50% (52/105) scored one, and 24% (25/105) scored two points. Overall, 73% (77/105) of the sonograms submitted by students captured major anatomical landmarks. Students were more successful acquiring sonograms from the abdomen and lung views than cardiac and pelvic views. By process of elimination, we determined that 71% (20/28) of indecipherable sonograms were attempts at pelvic (seven by omission) or cardiac views. By contrast, lung scans comprised 76% (19/25) of exceptional images. Students displayed greater proficiency in knobology with 80% (84/105) of sonograms receiving a score of one or more. Moreover, 60/105 (57%) received a score of two. Interestingly, pelvic and cardiac sonograms also trended lower on knobology scores, with 40% (18/45) scoring zero, and comprising 86% (18/21) of submissions scoring zero in knobology. Survey results

Six of 15 students (40%) completed the post course survey. Responses to Likert-style questions were summarized in Table 4. Responses to open-ended questions were summarized in Table 5. Not all respondents left open-ended feedback.

Overall, the Likert responses (Table 4) suggest students felt more confident in their future education and clinical skills following the POCUS course. Student confidence toward board exams received the smallest improvement. Course satisfaction was high, particularly regarding the instructors and equipment. Course integration and duration scored lowest in the course satisfaction domain. Students felt more confident in some cognitive domains of POCUS than their image acquisition and interpretation skills. Moderate confidence (Table 4) appeared to be appropriately aligned with results from the imaging practical (Fig. 3).

Most respondents included brief feedback on one or more open-ended questions (Table 5). Overall, students were motivated to enroll in the elective because they perceived POCUS to be a unique skill among athletic trainers that could benefit their future practices. Students valued the hands-on portions of the course, access to instructors for feedback while scanning, and exposure to the eFAST protocol. Suggestions for improvements revolved around course scheduling and duration. More specifically, students reported insufficient mental capacity to maximize their POCUS course experience as it followed a full day of challenging required classes. Moreover, several students reported a lack of sufficient time in each course meeting (Table 5), which was corroborated in Likert questions that revealed lower satisfaction with the integration and duration of the course relative to other items in the domain of course satisfaction (Table 4).

Discussion

The integration of POCUS into athletic training practice holds significant potential to improve practice and patient outcomes [6, 8], provided practitioners are availed of appropriate and comprehensive education. Despite the evident benefits, there is a scarcity of published accounts on POCUS in athletic training education, and our program did not have a POCUS course offering. To address the need for POCUS education, we designed a novel course tailored for MSAT students. Our curriculum diverges notably from previous reports [18–20], providing valuable insights into alternative didactic content, curricular focus, assessment methods, and strategies to overcome implementation barriers such as limited faculty, time constraints, and resource availability.

Knowledge and proficiency outcomes

Students’ post-course test scores were significantly higher than pre-test scores, indicating a meaningful increase in their content knowledge of POCUS had occurred across domains such as image interpretation, and the principles and instrumentation of ultrasound. In the practical exam, student proficiency was notably higher in the domain of knobology compared to anatomy (Fig. 3). Moreover, proficiency scores were inversely related to the technical demands of image acquisition and interpretation, illustrating the unique demands on operator skill across applications. For example, cardiac images, a difficult application of POCUS, scored lowest; the left upper quadrant scored slightly lower than the right upper quadrant (the spleen is often harder to visualize than the liver); and lungs views scored highest. These results are largely in alignment with prior reports of ultrasound learning curves [21, 35, 36], with two notable deviations: lung and pelvic views.

Breunig et. al., [36] demonstrated that physician’s assistant students rapidly achieved excellent pelvic image acquisition. In contrast, our students exhibited subpar performance in pelvic imaging. The disparity is largely attributable to a substantial number of missing pelvic submissions which were scored as zero. Because no other views were similarly impacted, this may suggest that our expectations for pelvic views in both sagittal and transverse planes were not adequately conveyed to students. Further, “dirty shadowing,” an imaging artifact indicating the presence of air or gas [31], obscured anatomy in several pelvic submissions. Novices often neglect to place their probe adjacent to the pubic symphysis, resulting in a scan of the small intestines (gas). In addition to clarifying expectations, strategies to promote student hydration and modesty such as drapes, privacy curtains, and water bottles during exams could result in improved student performance on pelvic image acquisition.

The reason for our student’s excellence in lung ultrasound when compared to the results from Breunig et al., which found equivalent learning curves across lung and abdominal scans, is less clear [36]. However, the variable scoring methods and scanning procedures employed across studies may have contributed to the disparity. For example, we developed our own scoring methods tailored to non-clinical faculty image reviewers, and anatomical landmarks required to carry out the eFAST protocol. By contrast, Breunig et al. used POCUS trained clinical faculty and a modified clinical scoring tool applied to more comprehensive lung imaging protocols.

These procedural differences highlight the heterogeneity across image evaluation metrics and POCUS applications [2, 37]. For example, in the eFAST, lung views are captured at the anterior upper lung fields, which are easy to locate and access. Moreover, the pleural line is generally shallow, and readily visualized as a hyperechoic (i.e., bright) “shimmering” line, making image acquisition relatively straightforward [31]. By contrast, comprehensive lung ultrasound, which poses additional and unique cognitive and psychomotor demands, requires views in multiple locations at greater depths to evaluate the lung parenchyma for a variety of clinical findings [31, 38]. Interpretation of our results is therefore limited to sonograms optimized for the detection of pneumothorax in the eFAST protocol and cannot be extended to lung ultrasound more generally.

Finally, while there are no standards for POCUS in athletic training education, proposed landmarks for medical education suggest that appropriate goals for pre-clinical students include understanding the rationale for (i.e., indications), and adequate image acquisition of, POCUS studies in simulated settings [39]. Our results indicate positive outcomes appropriate to preclinical students’ knowledge and proficiency. However, there is a clear need for additional POCUS training prior to effective clinical use. Several reports on learning curves for diagnostic ultrasound suggest image acquisition skills continue to improve with 15–35 examinations in each domain (i.e., acquiring images of the heart, lungs, kidneys, etc., of 15–35 unique individuals). Therefore, increasing the time dedicated to scanning practice with mandatory image portfolios assembled from multiple scanning models could improve proficiency outcomes in introductory POCUS courses.

However, researchers have also found that POCUS learning outcomes are difficult to predict based on contact hours or repetitions [35], and no accepted minimum duration has been put forth [40]. Therefore, the conditions necessary to engender proficiency are uncertain, and likely vary across users [21, 35, 36]. A possible solution to this is the use of competency-based assessments to continually assess proficiency for individual learners across POCUS applications [2, 37]. Ultimately, the adoption of POCUS by athletic trainers will benefit from the development of a multifaceted training infrastructure including introductory courses such as that reported here, reinforced by supervised POCUS use in clinical training, and robust access to continuing education opportunities. At present, clinical education in POCUS is underdeveloped within the athletic training profession. Faculty development in POCUS therefore represents a crucial next step if efforts to establish POCUS are to succeed.

Self-reported confidence

Practitioners use POCUS to gather real-time information about a patient’s condition, often in emergency or time-sensitive situations. Misinterpretations may lead to incorrect diagnoses or delayed interventions, affecting patient outcomes [31]. Therefore, it is critical that sonologists have an accurate appraisal of their skills to avoid mistakes resulting from an overabundance or lack of adequate self-confidence.

Participants in our study reported increased confidence in their knowledge and various scanning abilities over our three-week curriculum. By contrast, a recent six-month continuing education course for athletic trainers yielded no improvement in participants’ confidence in image interpretation while scanning, despite other positive outcomes (e.g., demonstrated improvements to image acquisition proficiency, confidence in image interpretation while observing experts scanning) [20]. The variation in self-reported outcomes between our studies may be attributed to differences in the population sampled. For example, it is possible that more experienced athletic trainers or those who have regularly observed trained provider-performed POCUS in their careers have better context with which to benchmark their skills.

The students who participated in our study, having only the experience provided in our course, may fall prey to the Dunning-Kruger effect, where individuals report inflated confidence following introduction to new topics that do not sufficiently explore the nuance and intricacy of the subject [41]. For example, our participants have inflated self-confidence in certain domains, such as a rating of 4.67 on their ability to capture images for clinical interpretation (Table 4), while results from the practical exam show significant need for continued skill development. However, while these findings highlight students’ over-confidence, most images were scored as adequate on the imaging practical exam, highlighting the development of basic skills in POCUS use among participants. Future research could better delineate the progression of students’ confidence and skill level by measuring confidence at multiple intervals following an introduction to POCUS, such as early in clinical implementation. However, the alignment between moderate proficiency demonstrated in our imaging practical exam (Fig. 3) and moderate self-reported confidence on the post-course survey (Table 4) suggests reasonable congruence between our objective assessment and participants’ subjective perceptions of skill levels.

Finally, our institutionally developed evaluations revolve around image quality rather than diagnostic accuracy and were applied to images captured in a simulated setting. Therefore, interpretation of student confidence and proficiency for our study should remain limited to an academic, rather than clinical context. Further research is needed to investigate students’ knowledge, proficiency, diagnostic accuracy, and confidence in POCUS as they matriculate into practice and use POCUS with pathologic populations.

Limitations

Our study is not without limitations, and several aspects of the study design limit the generalizability of the results. For example, we studied a single cohort of students who self-selected by voluntary course enrollment, institutionally developed surveys and knowledge tests were not independently validated, and post-course surveys captured a subset of course participants and may not be representative of all students’ experiences (e.g., non-response or acquiescence bias). However, findings support the efficacy of our local pre-clinical curriculum efforts. Nevertheless, additional sources of feedback such as semi-structured interviews, more robust survey data from future cohorts, or national surveys of athletic trainers would benefit our understanding of the perceptions and state of integration surrounding POCUS within the discipline. Finally, our image rating system was developed and implemented without validation by clinical faculty with POCUS expertise.

Future POCUS courses could be enhanced by incorporating expanded curricula, including topics such as MSK or ocular evaluations, longitudinal asynchronous didactic content, and refined and validated ultrasound competency assessment tools. Given that the core of athletic training practice remains focused on sports medicine, a mature POCUS curriculum for students entering the profession should include a robust MSK-specific element. The large scope of MSK-specific ultrasound content would benefit from integration across the MSAT program (e.g., coursework on anatomy, evaluation, rehab, etc.); however, such integration requires significant investment into faculty and preceptor development, and reform of existing curriculum beyond the scope of our introductory elective curriculum.

Multiple image evaluators together with live scanning observations could improve proficiency assessments in future courses. Additionally, there is a need for research to measure long-term outcomes, including the durability of POCUS knowledge and proficiency, the prevalence of POCUS among practicing athletic trainers, and the impact of POCUS on patients evaluated and managed by athletic trainers. These data are essential for understanding the clinical use of POCUS by athletic trainers and shaping POCUS content and assessment guidelines for educators in athletic training.

Conclusions

Athletic training practice can benefit from POCUS only if adequate education is available to students and practitioners. Currently, athletic training suffers from a paucity of resources in this regard. With this study, we evaluated an elective POCUS curriculum aiming to teach the basic principles and instrumentation of sonography, and describe the indications, acquisition, and interpretation of POCUS images using an organ-system approach. The course was well received and, with only 7.5 contact hours, engendered positive learning outcomes, perceptions of POCUS, and basic skills in image acquisition across a range of applications. This research adds to the literature on diagnostic ultrasound in athletic training education to help guide others hoping to pursue similar projects.
