Early Carotid Structural and Functional Changes Associated With Elevated Blood Pressure in Children: A Multimodal Ultrasound Study
Authors: Hui Wang, Yuanyuan Sheng, Xueyuan Lin, Ying Guo, Miaoru Yu, Youping Wang, Jie You, Jinfeng Xu, Yingying Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChildhood hypertension is closely associated with cardiovascular disease in adulthood, and the carotid artery is one of the principal target organs affected by elevated blood pressure (BP). The purpose of this study is to assess the association between carotid ultrasound‐derived indices and elevated BP in children and to identify early vascular changes using multimodal ultrasound imaging. This case‐control observational study included 118 children aged 10–11 years, of whom 55 had elevated BP and 63 had normal BP. Carotid ultrasound measurements included carotid diameter, conventional carotid intima‐media thickness (CIMT), ultrahigh frequency CIMT (uhf‐CIMT), carotid intima thickness, carotid media thickness (CMT), intima‐to‐media ratio (I/M), and radiofrequency‐derived pulse wave velocity (rf‐PWV). Associations between ultrasound parameters and BP were evaluated using multivariate linear regression models adjusted for age, sex, and body mass index. Children with elevated BP had higher values of uhf‐CIMT, CMT, rf‐PWV and lower values of I/M compared with children with normal BP (all p < 0.05). Systolic BP with the strongest association observed for CMT (r = 0.45). After adjustment for potential confounders, elevated BP remained independently associated with CMT, uhf‐CIMT, I/M, and rf‐PWV (all p < 0.05). Rf‐PWV was significantly associated with carotid structural indices, particularly the I/M ratio (p < 0.05). Elevated BP in children is associated with early carotid structural remodeling and increased arterial stiffness. Ultrahigh frequency ultrasound may facilitate the detection of subtle vascular changes in pediatric with elevated BP.

Background

Hypertension is increasingly prevalent worldwide and is now recognized as a major contributor to global morbidity and mortality [1]. Accumulating evidence suggests that elevated blood pressure (BP) during childhood and adolescence is not a benign condition but is strongly associated with the early development of cardiovascular abnormalities and an increased risk of heart failure and atherosclerosis later in life [2, 3]. Notably, emerging evidence indicates that timely detection and early intervention for elevated BP in childhood can partially or even fully reverse early cardiovascular damage, underscoring the importance of early risk identification and management [4]. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the impact of BP on cardiovascular structure and arterial stiffness in children and adolescents, is essential for improving cardiovascular risk stratification and preventing the development of cardiovascular disease in adulthood.

The carotid artery is key target organs in hypertension. Carotid intima‐media thickness (CIMT), measured via two‐dimensional B‐mode ultrasound along a longitudinal section of the common carotid artery, serves as a primary indicator of vascular structural damage and a predictor of cardiovascular events [5]. Furthermore, increased arterial stiffness, indicated by pulse wave velocity (PWV), is a strong predictor of cardiovascular events and mortality [6]. It is traditionally measured by determining the speed of pulse propagation between carotid and femoral vessels, termed carotid femoral PWV [7]. This global assessment of PWV has notable limitations, relying on imprecise measurements that are impacted by nonuniform vessel behavior and wave reflections.

Given these limitations, two recent novel imaging modalities, ultrahigh frequency ultrasound (UHFUS) and radiofrequency ultrasound imaging, offer improved techniques to evaluate CIMT and PWV that may help identify earlier signs of pathologic changes in the vasculature of young children with elevated BP [8]. CIMT measured by UHFUS (uhf‐CIMT) allows for noninvasive, high spatial resolution imaging of blood vessel structure. It can further measure carotid intima thickness (CIT) and carotid media thickness (CMT), and its clinical relevance has been demonstrated in adults [9, 10, 11, 12], though its use in children remains less [13, 14]. Radiofrequency ultrasound technology is an efficient, accurate, and repeatable tool for assessing PWV [15, 16]. The employment of UHFUS and radiofrequency ultrasound to assess the local structure and local stiffness of the vasculature at the same location (i.e., the carotid artery) can further allow us to understand the relationship between these two measures of vascular function, and how they may be impacted by clinical variables.

In this pilot study, we evaluated uhf‐CIMT and radiofrequency ultrasound PWV (rf‐PWV) of the carotid artery in children with elevated BP. We hypothesized that a larger uhf‐CIMT and higher local carotid rf‐PWV would be observed in elevated BP in relation to age and sex‐matched normal BP controls that would be impacted by BP and CVD risk factors. Furthermore, we aimed to assess whether uhf‐CIMT and rf‐PWV, as measures of vascular structure and stiffness, respectively, are independently associated with elevated BP state, given the known early pathogenesis of CVD in elevated BP.

Patient and Methods

Sample Size Estimation

A total of 44 participants (22 per group) would provide 80% power (two‐sided α = 0.05) to detect a between‐group difference of 0.04 mm in CIMT, assuming mean CIMT values of 0.45 ± 0.05 mm in the elevated BP group and 0.41 ± 0.04 mm in the normal BP group, based on prior literature [17]. After accounting for an anticipated 20% dropout rate, the target sample size was 56 participants (28 per group).

Patients

We conducted a case‐control observational study involving 55 children diagnosed with elevated BP, forming the elevated BP group. A control group was established, consisting of 63 children with normal BP (Figure 1).

Study population.

Considering the significant variations in children's physiological indicators due to growth and development, the study exclusively included subjects aged 10–11 years. This age group was selected based on research indicating that over one‐third of children within this age bracket are overweight or obese [18]. Notably, overweight and obesity are strongly correlated with the incidence of essential hypertension in young children [19, 20]. All participants underwent physical measurements, routine blood and biochemical tests, and ultrasound assessments of target organs. BP measurements adhered to the 2017 American Academy of Pediatrics hypertension guidelines: normal (<90th percentile), elevated BP (≥90th to <95th percentile), stage 1 HTN (≥95th percentile to <95th percentile + 12 mm Hg), and stage 2 HTN (≥95th percentile + 12 mm Hg), on the basis of age, sex, and height percentiles [21].

Exclusion factors included hypertension, congenital heart disease, images cannot be analyzed, BMI below normal range (BMI below established age‐ and gender‐specific cutoff points [22]).

The study received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Shenzhen People's Hospital (reference number: LL‐KY‐2024157‐01). Written informed consent was obtained from each participant or their parents (or legal guardians) prior to enrollment.

Basic Clinical Information and Family History

Data on age, sex, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), BP, and resting heart rate were collected from children in the elevated BP group and control group. In our study, height, weight, and BP were measured using calibrated and validated devices. Shoes and hats were removed and light clothes were worn during measurement, height was accurate to 0.1 cm, weight was accurate to 0.1 kg, and the average value of two measurements was taken to calculate BMI = weight/height (kg/m2). The same researcher (X.L) measured the BP of all participants on the left upper arm after they had rested for 15 min in the supine position, using an oscillometric device (HEM 7012Omron, Osaka, Japan). The mean of two measurements, 1 min apart, was used in the analysis.

Conventional Ultrasound Measurements of Carotid Artery

All measurements were performed by one of two investigators (H.W. and Y.S), who were blinded to the diagnosis of elevated BP, using a Reasona A20 pro (Mindray, Shenzhen, China) ultrasound machine equipped with a LM 18‐5WU probe.

CIMT and carotid arterial internal diameter measurements were performed in a longitudinal section, strictly perpendicular to the ultrasound beam, with both arterial walls clearly visualized. A quality standard B‐mode image was acquired along a section of the left common carotid artery at 1.0 to 1.5 cm proximal to the carotid bifurcation to measuring the CIMT (Figure 2A).

Biochemical Analysis

Blood samples were collected from both study and control groups after overnight fasting and analyzed for glucose, insulin, total cholesterol, triglycerides, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL), creatinine, uric acid, and recombinant human growth hormone (RHGH) following the standard laboratory protocols of the Hospital for Children.

Statistical Analysis

Data following a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). To assess the statistical significance of differences between children with elevated BP and controls across various parameters, independent t‐tests were utilized. Measures not normally distributed are expressed as median and interquartile range [M (P25, P75)]. Comparisons between these groups were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Univariate linear regression analyses identified associations between vascular (dependent variables) and BP (independent variables). Multivariate enter linear regression analyses adjusted for a limited set of potential confounders. These confounders were selected based on previously published studies that demonstrated correlations with the outcome variables and potential differences between children with elevated and normal BP (a priori selection). Both Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated to examine the relationships between vascular parameters, along with their statistical significance. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 23 (IBM SPSS Statistics, IBM Corp), and a two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All sample size calculations were performed using PASS 2021 (NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, UT, USA).

Correlations Between Ultrasonic Measurements and Blood Pressure

This study utilized univariate and multivariate regression analyses to examine the correlations between BP and carotid ultrasound parameters in children from both the study group with elevated BP and the control group (Figure S1). Notably, rf‐PWV and HC were excluded from the statistical analysis due to their derivation from blood pressure inputs (Table S1–S2).

Carotid ultrasound parameters and SBP: Univariate regression analysis indicated significant correlations between CIT, CMT, I/M, uhf‐CIMT, CIMT, diameter with SBP (p < 0.05). After adjusting for age, sex, and BMI, the correlations for CMT, I/M, uhf‐CIMT remained significant, whereas those for CIT, CIMT and diameter were no longer significant (p > 0.05).

Carotid ultrasound parameters and DBP: Univariate regression analysis showed significant correlations for CIT, CMT, and uhf‐CIMT with DBP (p < 0.05). After multivariate adjustment for age, sex, and BMI, the correlations for CMT and uhf‐CIMT remained significant, while the correlation for CIT was no longer significant (p > 0.05).

We evaluated the impact of PWV on the relationship between CIT, CMT, uhf‐CIMT, CIMT, I/M. Univariate linear regression analyses identified associations between carotid artery structure index (dependent variables) and rf‐PWV (independent variables). Univariate regression analysis indicated significant correlations between I/M and rf‐PWV (p < 0.05). Discussion

In this pilot study, we assessed the carotid artery in children aged 10–11 years who exhibit elevated BP, utilizing UHFUS and RF techniques. We observed the carotid arteries undergo structural and vascular stiffness changes. UHFUS can detect changes in the structure of carotid artery in children with elevated BP earlier. The measurement indices obtained through this method have a stronger correlation with blood pressure, providing a more accurate reflection of the impact of increased blood pressure on carotid artery structure.

In terms of vascular structure, there was no significant difference in diameter and CIMT as measured by conventional ultrasound between children with elevated BP and the control group. However, using UHFUS revealed significant differences in the detailed structure of the carotid artery. Specifically, the CMT, uhf‐CIMT of children were higher in the elevated BP group, I/M values were lower, and the differences between groups were statistically significant. The higher CMT, lower I/M suggests that in children with elevated BP, the primary structural change in the carotid arteries is CMT, with less significant CIT. This is consistent with previous research indicating that these changes are due to the proliferation and hypertrophy of mesangial vascular smooth muscle, along with varying degrees of collagen and elastic fiber proliferation [23]. Additionally, this study found that uhf‐CIMT measurements obtained by UHFUS, when compared with CIMT values measured by conventional ultrasound, indicated that conventional ultrasound tends to overestimate carotid CIMT by approximately 0.107 ± 0.096 mm, aligning with findings from prior studies [14]. The measurement parameters of UHFUS are independently associated with elevated blood pressure states and offer a clear advantage by enabling earlier and more precise detection of structural changes in the carotid intima and media. This provides more relevant and valuable information for clinical practice.

After controlling for confounding variables, the differences in rf‐PWV between groups were no longer statistically significant. rf‐PWV, a measurement indicative of large artery stiffness, serves as a sensitive marker for vascular function. This outcome suggests that in the studied pediatric population, alterations in arterial stiffness linked with elevated blood pressure may be partially mediated by common factors, including age, sex, and obesity, rather than solely by elevated blood pressure. BMI a significant correlate of elevated blood pressure and metabolic disturbances in children, may concurrently impact blood pressure and arterial elasticity through mechanisms such as inflammation and endothelial dysfunction. Consequently, variations in rf‐PWV may more accurately represent the broader effects of obesity and associated metabolic disorders on vascular function [24].

Elevated blood pressure in childhood significantly impacts vascular structural changes. Univariate regression analysis demonstrated that several carotid ultrasound parameters are significantly correlated with SBP, indicating a strong relationship between elevated SBP and carotid wall thickening, as well as alterations in the intima‐media ratio. This finding aligns with previous studies, underscoring that SBP, as a crucial indicator of large artery load, is closely associated with vascular structural changes [25, 26]. After adjusting for age, gender, and BMI, the correlations between CMT, I/M, uhf‐CIMT and SBP remained significant, independent of physical development and obesity factors.

Conversely, CIT and vascular diameter are more influenced by body size and growth, and their association with SBP diminishes after adjusting for confounding factors, indicating their relatively limited specificity. Regarding DBP, both univariate and multivariate regression analyses revealed that only CMT and uhf‐CIMT remained significantly correlated with DBP, emphasizing the potential role of media layer structural changes in elevated DBP in children. Notably, CMT and uhf‐CIMT maintained significant correlations in multivariable analyses with both systolic and DBP, demonstrating their high sensitivity in detecting early, subtle changes in vascular wall structure. Compared to traditional CIMT, ultrahigh frequency ultrasound can more precisely differentiate the layers of the vascular wall, potentially making it more suitable for assessing early subclinical vascular damage in children and adolescents.

Previous research suggests that essential hypertension primarily increases carotid artery stiffness due to elevated BP [27]. However, recent studies indicate that rf‐PWV is directly proportional to arterial elasticity (Young's modulus) and thickness, and inversely proportional to arterial diameter [28]. Furthermore, rf‐PWV has been shown to be positively associated with CIMTthe correlation between rf‐PWV and CIMT persists even after adjustments for age and BP [29]. In our study, we observed significant correlations between I/M and rf‐PWV (p < 0.05). The advantage of I/M measurement using UHFUS is its ability to amplify differential structural changes in the carotid artery in response to increased BP, thereby providing a more accurate reflection of medial thickening. Historically, numerous studies have utilized I/M as an indicator of vascular aging and subclinical atherosclerosis [30, 31, 32]. In this study, a significant negative correlation was observed between I/M and rf‐PWV, indicating a relationship between alterations in carotid artery structure and changes in arterial stiffness during periods of elevated BP in children. I/M has shown superior performance compared to other carotid artery structural indicators. However, research on this metric has been limited due to constraints related to instruments and equipment. In conclusion, I/M is a promising metric that warrants further comprehensive investigation.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross sectional design precludes causal inference between elevated blood pressure and carotid structural or functional changes. Our research group is conducting a longitudinal study to determine whether these vascular changes progress with time or predict future cardiovascular outcomes. Second, the sample size was relatively modest and derived from a single center, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Finally, the absence of central arterial blood pressure and ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) data is a limitation of this study. Future studies should incorporate these measurements to provide a more comprehensive assessment of hemodynamic load. Despite these limitations, the homogenous age range and comprehensive multimodal ultrasound assessment strengthen the validity of our findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, elevated BP in children is independently associated with early carotid structural remodeling and increased arterial stiffness, with medial thickening representing the predominant vascular change. UHFUS enhances the detection of these subtle alterations and demonstrates stronger associations with blood pressure than conventional measurements. The observed relationship between carotid structural indices and arterial stiffness suggests a close link between early vascular remodeling and functional impairment in pediatric populations. These findings underscore the importance of early BP control and support the potential role of advanced vascular imaging in the early cardiovascular risk assessment of children.
