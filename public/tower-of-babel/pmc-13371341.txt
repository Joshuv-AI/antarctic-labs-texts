A modified FLIT-B model of trigeminal neuralgia reveals anterior cingulate cortex involvement in anxiety-like behavior in mice
Authors: Xiaoqin Lian, Taoxia Xie, Chen Chen, Jing Deng, Sijing Zhao, Tongtong Gao, Mei Fu, Weiyi Ye, Kunxin Yang, Na Zhou, Lei Tang, Dihan Lu, Xia Feng, Shiqian Shen, Qian Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTrigeminal neuralgia (TN) is a debilitating orofacial pain disorder characterized by paroxysmal, lancinating pain and prominent affective comorbidities. Increasing evidence suggests that chronic TN involves abnormal plasticity in supraspinal pain circuits. However, the lack of animal models that faithfully recapitulate key clinical features of TN has limited mechanistic investigations of these central processes. Here, building upon our previously established foramen lacerum impingement of the trigeminal nerve root (FLIT) model, we developed an improved glass bead-based version (FLIT-B) that enables reproducible and controlled compression of the trigeminal nerve root and facilitates investigation of supraspinal mechanisms in TN.MethodsIn the FLIT-B model, calibrated glass beads were implanted through the foramen lacerum to compress the trigeminal nerve root. Behavioral assays were used to assess sensory and affective phenotypes, while histological analyses evaluated trigeminal nerve pathology. To examine supraspinal circuit alterations, neuronal activation in the primary somatosensory barrel field (S1BF), anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), amygdala, and hippocampus was assessed using c-Fos immunostaining. Structural and functional changes in excitatory neurons, particularly in the ACC, were further analyzed using AAV-mediated labeling, in vivo fiber photometry, and two-photon calcium imaging in both sham and FLIT-B mice.ResultsFLIT-B mice exhibited spontaneous pain-like behaviors, including asymmetric facial grimacing and peri-orbital hair loss associated with excessive grooming, suggesting persistent orofacial discomfort. Histological analyses confirmed consistent trigeminal nerve root demyelination. c-Fos staining and calcium imaging revealed widespread activation of the S1BF, ACC, amygdala, and hippocampus, with the ACC showing the highest degree of activation, particularly in spontaneous activity patterns. Morphological analyses demonstrated significant dendritic remodeling in excitatory neurons of the ACC and S1BF. Chemogenetic inhibition of ACC excitatory neurons significantly alleviated both pain-like and anxiety-like behaviors.ConclusionsThe FLIT-B model provides a reproducible and clinically relevant rodent model of TN by compression of the trigeminal nerve root at an anatomically relevant site. Background

Trigeminal neuralgia (TN) is characterized by paroxysmal, lancinating facial pain and is widely regarded as one of the most debilitating pain conditions in humans [1–4]. Beyond its sensory severity, TN is frequently accompanied by profound affective comorbidities, including anxiety and depression, which substantially exacerbate disease burden and impair quality of life [4, 5]. Increasing evidence indicates that chronic TN involves extensive plasticity within supraspinal circuits beyond the primary nerve injury site. Neuroimaging studies in patients with TN have reported abnormal activity and structural alterations in multiple brain regions implicated in pain processing and affective regulation, including thalamocortical circuits, the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and the amygdala [6, 7]. Consistent with these findings, both clinical and experimental studies suggest that TN is not solely a peripheral disorder but involves maladaptive central plasticity that contributes to persistent pain and affective dysfunction [8–11]. However, how focal peripheral insult translates into widespread circuit-level alterations remains incompletely understood.

Progress in this area has been limited in part by the availability of animal models that adequately capture the pathophysiological features of TN. Several peripheral nerve injury models have been widely used to study trigeminal neuropathic pain, particularly the infraorbital nerve chronic constriction injury model (IoN-CCI) [8, 12–14]. These models are well established, technically accessible, and highly reproducible, and have been shown to recapitulate key sensory hypersensitivity as well as, in some cases, affective disturbances associated with chronic pain. However, because they primarily target distal branches of the trigeminal nerve, they more closely model peripheral trigeminal neuropathic pain rather than key features of TN, which is most commonly associated with compression of the trigeminal nerve root at its brainstem entry zone. As a result, certain hallmark features of TN, including paroxysmal pain attacks, are not consistently reproduced in these models. To address this gap, we previously developed the Foramen Lacerum Impingement of the Trigeminal nerve root (FLIT) model [15, 16], which leverages a natural skull-base foramen to access and compress the trigeminal nerve root at an anatomically relevant site. This approach more closely recapitulates the clinical etiology of TN, in which neurovascular compression occurs near the root entry zone, and enables the study of both sensory and affective dimensions of the disorder within a pathophysiologically relevant framework.

Despite its translational relevance, the original FLIT procedure requires precise microsurgical manipulation to deliver gelatin sponge through the foramen lacerum, a process associated with a relatively steep learning curve and potential operator-dependent variability [15, 16]. To improve procedural consistency, we developed a modified version of this model, termed the Foramen Lacerum Impingement of the Trigeminal Nerve Root using Glass Beads (FLIT-B). In this approach, gelatin sponge is replaced with calibrated glass beads, allowing more standardized and anatomically constrained compression of the trigeminal nerve root. Using this model, the present study was designed to determine how trigeminal nerve root compression engages supraspinal brain regions involved in the sensory and affective dimensions of TN-like pain. Guided by prior clinical and experimental evidence implicating distributed pain-processing networks, we focused on key cortical and limbic regions, including the primary somatosensory barrel field (S1BF), ACC, hippocampus, and amygdala [6–10, 17–23]. We hypothesized that persistent trigeminal pain would induce coordinated functional and structural plasticity within these regions, particularly within the ACC, a region strongly implicated in affective pain processing.

Results

The FLIT-B model induces reproducible trigeminal nerve demyelination with persistent pain-like and anxiety-like behaviors

Building on the original FLIT model [12, 15], we refined the procedure by introducing a glass bead-based compression strategy to improve reproducibility and reduce operator-dependent variability. As shown in Figure S1A, the original FLIT approach requires precise delivery of gelatin sponge at certain insertion angle and depth, a process associated with a relatively steep learning curve and variable compression outcomes. To address these limitations, we adopted calibrated glass beads as a uniform compression material and optimized the implantation trajectory. Guided by key anatomical landmarks of the mouse skull base, including the foramen lacerum and tympanic bulla, we determined an implantation trajectory for the glass beads (indicated by forceps in Fig. 1A) and the target site for trigeminal nerve compression (dashed circle in Fig. 1A) with mice in the supine position. Based on the measurements of the foramen lacerum in C57BL/6 mice from our previous work (length: 1.97 ± 0.05 mm; width: 0.52 ± 0.06 mm) [16], 0.6 mm glass beads were selected (Fig. 1B-D). Implantation of two beads resulted in clear compression of the ipsilateral trigeminal nerve root, while the contralateral side remained unaffected. Removal of the nerve root further confirmed accurate and reproducible bead placement within the foramen lacerum (Fig. 1E-G). A detailed surgical procedure is provided in Supplementary Video 1. In addition, comparison of surgical outcomes between trained novices performing FLIT and FLIT-B procedures showed a higher rate of successful implantation and more consistent behavioral phenotypes in the FLIT-B model (Figure S1C).

FLIT-B mice developed characteristic spontaneous phenotypes following surgery, including facial asymmetry or grimacing (observed in 50% of mice) and periorbital hair loss (observed in 36% of mice, likely reflecting excessive grooming) (Fig. 1H-I). These features are consistent with persistent orofacial discomfort. Notably, facial mechanical thresholds were comparable across mice exhibiting different postoperative phenotypes (Figure S1B), and bead positioning was highly consistent (Figure S2), supporting there producibility and stability of the refined procedure. To determine whether these phenotypes were associated with structural alterations of the trigeminal nerve, histological analyses were performed at post-operative day 7. S100β immunofluorescence revealed an increased number of Schwann cells in the ipsilateral trigeminal nerve of FLIT-B mice (Fig. 1J-K) [24], while myelin basic protein (MBP) levels were significantly reduced (Sham: 3.03 ± 0.36; FLIT-B: 1.84 ± 0.09), indicating demyelination (Fig. 1L-M).

We next evaluated pain-related and affective behaviors in Sham, FLIT, and FLIT-B mice (Fig. 2A-B). Different bead diameters were tested to optimize compression strength. Implantation of two 0.3 mm beads induced only transient mechanical hypersensitivity, whereas two 0.5 mm, 0.6 mm, or 0.8 mm beads produced sustained facial mechanical allodynia. Among these, the 2 × 0.6 mm configuration yielded the most stable and reproducible behavioral phenotype and was therefore used for subsequent experiments (Fig. 2C). Both FLIT and FLIT-B models exhibited significant mechanical allodynia; however, hypersensitivity in FLIT-B mice persisted for up to 42 days, indicating improved long-term stability compared with the original FLIT model (Fig. 2D and Figure S3A).

In addition to persistent pain-like behavior, both models exhibited anxiety-like phenotypes. At 14 days post-surgery, mice showed reduced center exploration in the open-field test (OFT; Fig. 2E-G) and decreased open-arm exploration in the elevated plus maze (EPM; Fig. 2H-J), with no significant differences between FLIT and FLIT-B groups. To further assess pharmacological responsiveness, we tested the effect of carbamazepine, a first-line treatment for TN [25]. Carbamazepine administration significantly reduced facial mechanical allodynia in FLIT-B mice (Figure S4), supporting the translational relevance of the model. Importantly, similar patterns of mechanical hypersensitivity and anxiety-like behaviors were observed in adult female mice subjected to the FLIT-B procedure, including reduced facial mechanical thresholds, decreased center exploration in the OFT, and reduced open-arm exploration in the EPM (Figure S3A-G), supporting the robustness of the model across sexes.

Together, these results indicate that the FLIT-B model produces reproducible trigeminal nerve root compression, associated demyelination, and persistent pain- and anxiety-like phenotypes.

FLIT-B is associated with acute and persistent activation of pain-related brain regions

The cerebral cortex and associated limbic structures are involved in the sensory and affective processing of pain [17, 26]. To determine whether trigeminal nerve root compression in the FLIT-B model is associated with activation of these regions, we first examined neuronal activity using c-Fos immunofluorescence at 2 h after surgery (Fig. 3A). Compared with Sham controls, FLIT-B mice exhibited robust increases in c-Fos-positive neurons across multiple pain-related regions, including the S1BF, ACC, amygdala (AMY), and hippocampus (HIP) (Fig. 3B-D). Quantitatively, the number of c-Fos-positive cells in S1BF (Sham: 62.67 ± 7.42, FLIT-B: 352.00 ± 6.43, counted per 1.90 mm²), ACC (Sham: 85.00 ± 14.18, FLIT-B: 285.30 ± 38.75, per 1.40 mm²), AMY (Sham: 73.00 ± 4.93, FLIT-B: 192.30 ± 38.32, per 0.80 mm²), and HIP (Sham: 35.00 ± 3.79, FLIT-B: 123.00 ± 16.86, per 0.80 mm²) were all significantly elevated, indicating that FLIT-B is associated with widespread acute activation of cortical and subcortical structures (Fig. 3D). We next performed laminar analysis of cortical c-Fos expression. Cortical layer boundaries were defined based on standard anatomical references from a mouse brain atlas to ensure accurate laminar segmentation. At 2 h after FLIT-B surgery, c-Fos-positive neurons were significantly increased across layers 2–6 of the S1BF, whereas in the ACC the increase was predominantly confined to layers 2/3 (Figure S5A-D). These findings indicate that FLIT-B induces region-specific laminar activation during the acute phase.

To determine whether this activation persisted beyond the acute postoperative period, we further quantified endogenous c-Fos expression at postoperative days 7 and 14. At postoperative day 7, c-Fos expression remained elevated in S1BF, mainly in layers 2/3 and 6, and also remained increased in the ACC, where activation was predominantly confined to layers 2/3 (Figure S6). By postoperative day 14, c-Fos activation in the S1BF returned to baseline levels across layers. In contrast, ACC activation remained significantly elevated, particularly in superficial layers 1 and 2/3 (Figure S7), suggesting a transition from transient sensory cortical activation to more sustained ACC involvement during the TN-like state.

To further characterize neuronal ensembles engaged during the subacute phase, we used Fos-TRAP2 mice crossed with a Cre-dependent tdTomato reporter mouse line to label neurons activated within a defined time window [27]. Tamoxifen was administered at postoperative day 7, and labeled neurons were analyzed at postoperative day 14. This approach captures neuronal populations activated within a limited time window spanning several hours around tamoxifen administration, providing a time-locked snapshot of circuit engagement during the subacute phase rather than continuous or fluctuating activity across the chronic period. Compared with control animals, FLIT-B mice exhibited significantly increased tdTomato-labeled neurons in the S1BF, ACC, AMY, and HIP (Fig. 3E-I). Laminar analysis showed that tdTomato-positive neurons were broadly distributed across layers 2–6 in the S1BF, whereas in the ACC they were predominantly confined to layers 2/3 (Figure S5E-H). The broader laminar distribution of tdTomato-labeled neurons in the S1BF, compared with endogenous c-Fos expression at postoperative day 7, likely reflects the temporal integration property of Fos-TRAP2 labeling, which captures neurons activated during the tamoxifen-dependent recombination window rather than only at the time of tissue collection.

Together, these results reveal distinct spatiotemporal and region-specific patterns of neuronal activation in the FLIT-B model, characterized by widespread acute activation, transient sensory cortical activation in the S1BF, and more persistent, superficial-layer-enriched activation in the ACC.

FLIT-B is associated with increased dendritic complexity in S1BF and ACC neurons

Chronic pain has been associated with structural remodeling in neuronal circuits [28]. To examine whether trigeminal nerve root compression in the FLIT-B model is accompanied by morphological changes in pain-related brain regions, we performed three-dimensional reconstruction of sparsely labeled excitatory neurons in the S1BF, ACC, and HIP. On day 7 post FLIT-B, mice received intravenous injections of AAV-PHP.eB-CaMKIIα-EGFP-P2A-EGFPf for sparse neuronal labeling, using a method previously established in our laboratory for reconstructing individual cortical neurons [29]. Neuronal morphology was analyzed after 21 days of viral expression (day 28 post FLIT-B) (Fig. 4A-B).

In S1BF neurons, Sholl analysis revealed significantly increased dendritic complexity in FLIT-B mice compared with Sham controls. Both basal and apical dendrites exhibited higher area under the curve (AUC) values (basal: Sham 1634 ± 132.5, FLIT-B 2137 ± 123.6; apical: Sham 853.9 ± 55.14, FLIT-B 1132 ± 113.7), increased maximum intersections (basal: Sham 25.90 ± 2.35, FLIT-B 33.89 ± 2.61; apical: Sham 9.40 ± 0.62, FLIT-B 13.89 ± 1.34), and longer total dendritic length (basal: Sham 1913 ± 155.6 μm, FLIT-B 2530 ± 154.4 μm; apical: Sham 1113 ± 86.03 μm, FLIT-B 1551 ± 158.5 μm) (Fig. 4C-H). Similarly, ACC neurons in FLIT-B mice showed marked increases in dendritic complexity. Both basal and apical compartments exhibited higher AUC (basal: Sham 1595 ± 153.5, FLIT-B 2821 ± 199.4; apical: Sham 1212 ± 114.7, FLIT-B 1952 ± 203.8), increased maximum intersections (basal: Sham 22.00 ± 2.27, FLIT-B 31.56 ± 1.96; apical: Sham 9.69 ± 0.84, FLIT-B 12.89 ± 1.30), and longer total dendritic length (basal: Sham 1744 ± 159.8 μm, FLIT-B 3101 ± 256.1 μm; apical: Sham 1640 ± 155.7 μm, FLIT-B 3141 ± 275.4 μm) (Fig. 4I-N), indicating enhanced dendritic arborization in cortical regions critical for pain processing. In contrast, three-dimensional reconstruction of neurons in hippocampal subregions, including Cornu Ammonis 1 (CA1), dorsal dentate gyrus (dDG), and ventral dentate gyrus (vDG), revealed no significant differences in dendritic length or branching complexity between FLIT-B and Sham mice (Figure S8).

Together, these results indicate that FLIT-B is associated with increased dendritic complexity in cortical regions, including the S1BF and ACC, while no significant structural changes were observed in the hippocampus.

ACC excitatory neurons exhibit enhanced spontaneous activity and contribute to pain-like behaviors in the FLIT-B model

To investigate whether neuronal activity changes are associated with the observed structural remodeling, we performed in vivo fiber photometry to monitor calcium dynamics of CaMKIIα-positive excitatory neurons in the contralateral S1BF, ACC, amygdala, and hippocampus in FLIT-B mice (Fig. 5A-C). Baseline recordings obtained prior to FLIT-B surgery confirmed stable signal acquisition under the recording conditions, following habituation to the setup (Figure S9A-B). On day 14 post FLIT-B, spontaneous calcium transients were observed across all examined regions. Notably, analysis of spontaneous activity revealed that calcium transient episodes were more prominent in the ACC compared with S1BF, AMY, and HIP (Figure S9C). Quantification of calcium signals further showed that the magnitude of activity was higher in the ACC relative to other regions (S1BF: 17.87 ± 1.44%; ACC: 33.07 ± 5.37%; AMY: 15.53 ± 3.46%; HIP: 7.84 ± 0.58%) (Fig. 5C-E), indicating region-specific differences in spontaneous neuronal activity when pain-like and anxiety-like behaviors are established.

To further examine neuronal activity at the cellular level, we performed in vivo two-photon calcium imaging of CaMKIIα-positive excitatory neurons in the ACC through a cranial window (Fig. 5F-I). AAV-PHP.eB-CaMKIIα-GCaMP6f was injected into the ACC, and robust expression was confirmed in coronal brain sections (Fig. 5G). Two-photon imaging revealed clear GCaMP6f fluorescence in layer 2/3 excitatory neurons (Fig. 5H-I). Longitudinal imaging demonstrated that spontaneous neuronal activity was increased on both day 7 and day 14 post FLIT-B compared with baseline (Fig. 5J). Quantitative analysis of pooled data across hemispheres showed significant increases in calcium event amplitude, frequency, and AUC, while the duration of individual events remained unchanged (Fig. 5K-N). To address potential hemispheric differences, we analyzed neuronal activity in the ipsilateral and contralateral ACC relative to the surgical side (Figure S10). We found that calcium event amplitude exhibited lateralized changes, with higher amplitudes observed in the contralateral ACC compared with the ipsilateral side. In contrast, event frequency and AUC were increased bilaterally without significant hemispheric differences, and event duration remained unchanged across conditions. These findings indicate sustained hyperactivity of ACC excitatory neurons following FLIT-B.

Given the pronounced activation of the ACC, we next tested whether suppressing excitatory neuronal activity in the ACC could alleviate FLIT-B-induced pain-like behaviors (Fig. 6A-C). Chemogenetic inhibition of ACC CaMKIIα-positive neurons was achieved via AAV2/9-mCaMKIIα-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry, with viral transduction was largely confined to the ACC and minimal spread to adjacent regions (Figure S11). Administration of clozapine N-oxide (CNO) [30] reduced c-Fos expression in ACC neurons in FLIT-B mice (Fig. 6D-E), confirming effective inhibition. Behaviorally, inhibition of ACC excitatory neurons significantly increased facial mechanical pain thresholds (Fig. 6F) and reduced both the number of spontaneous grooming bouts (Sham+Saline: 3.60 ± 0.24/10 min; FLIT-B + Gi+Saline: 6.00 ± 0.68/10 min; FLIT-B + Gi+CNO: 2.71 ± 0.18/10 min) and grooming duration (Sham+Saline: 73.33 ± 15.16 s; FLIT-B + Gi+Saline: 164.0 ± 35.94 s; FLIT-B + Gi+CNO: 55.17 ± 15.31 s) (Fig. 6G-I).

In addition, ACC excitatory neuron inhibition improved anxiety-like behaviors. Compared with FLIT-B + Gi+Saline mice, CNO-treated mice showed increased center exploration in the OFT on day 14 post FLIT-B, reflected by changes in center distance (Center/total distance ratio: Sham+Saline: 0.17 ± 0.01; FLIT-B + Gi+Saline: 0.12 ± 0.01; FLIT-B + Gi+CNO: 0.17 ± 0.01) and center time (Sham+Saline: 1.33 ± 0.19 min; FLIT-B + Gi+Saline: 0.62 ± 0.10 min; FLIT-B + Gi+CNO: 1.25 ± 0.22 min) (Fig. 6J-L). Similarly, EPM testing revealed increased open-arm exploration in CNO-treated mice, reflected by both distance (Moving open/close arm ratio: Sham+Saline: 0.19 ± 0.02; FLIT-B + Gi+Saline: 0.07 ± 0.02; FLIT-B + Gi+CNO: 0.18 ± 0.02) and time spent in open arms (Staying open/close arm ratio: Sham+Saline: 0.17 ± 0.02; FLIT-B + Gi+Saline: 0.05 ± 0.01; FLIT-B + Gi+CNO: 0.15 ± 0.02) (Fig. 6M-O).

Together, these results indicate that ACC excitatory neurons exhibit sustained and enhanced spontaneous activity and contribute to pain-like and anxiety-like behaviors in the FLIT-B model.

Discussion

TN is a debilitating orofacial pain disorder frequently accompanied by affective disturbances, yet the supraspinal mechanisms linking persistent trigeminal pain to these comorbidities remain incompletely understood [4, 31, 32]. In the present study, we used a modified trigeminal nerve root compression model (FLIT-B) to investigate how chronic trigeminal injury engages central circuits. We found that trigeminal root compression is associated with persistent pain-like and anxiety-like behaviors, sustained spontaneous hyperactivity of ACC excitatory neurons, and cortical excitatory neuronal dendritic remodeling. Chemogenetic inhibition of ACC excitatory neurons alleviated both pain-like and anxiety-like behaviors. Together, these findings support the ACC as an important supraspinal node within a broader trigeminal pain-affect circuit.

A major challenge in TN research has been the development of animal models that capture clinically relevant features while remaining experimentally tractable [12, 15, 16, 33–36]. The widely used IoN-CCI model has provided important insights into trigeminal neuropathic pain and reliably produces mechanical hypersensitivity following distal trigeminal nerve injury [8, 12–14]. Prior studies using IoN-CCI and related trigeminal neuropathic pain models have also demonstrated altered neuronal activity in somatosensory cortex and medial prefrontal cortical regions, supporting the involvement of supraspinal plasticity in trigeminal pain [8, 10]. However, because IoN-CCI primarily targets distal branches of the trigeminal nerve, it more closely models peripheral trigeminal neuropathic pain than the proximal nerve root compression that is frequently implicated in classical TN. By contrast, FLIT and FLIT-B target the trigeminal nerve root region and therefore provide a complementary approach for studying proximal trigeminal injury and its associated central adaptations [15, 16, 33, 35, 36]. The primary advantage of FLIT-B is not technical simplicity compared with IoN-CCI, but the combination of an anatomically relevant compression site and improved reproducibility through standardized glass bead-mediated compression.

The behavioral phenotype observed in FLIT-B mice should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Classical paroxysmal TN is characterized by brief, recurrent, stimulus-evoked attacks with pain-free intervals [4, 31]. In contrast, FLIT-B produces a persistent pain-related state, including facial mechanical hypersensitivity and spontaneous face-directed behaviors. This persistent phenotype does not fully recapitulate purely paroxysmal TN. However, contemporary clinical frameworks recognize that TN can include concomitant continuous or near-continuous pain between attacks [4, 31, 37, 38]. Thus, FLIT-B is best described as a trigeminal root compression-associated TN-like or trigeminal neuropathic pain model rather than a complete model of all clinical features of TN.

Using this model, we observed activation of a distributed supraspinal network, including S1BF, ACC, amygdala, and hippocampus. This finding is consistent with clinical neuroimaging studies showing structural and functional alterations in cortical and subcortical regions in TN patients [6, 7, 19, 21]. It is also consistent with preclinical trigeminal neuropathic pain studies showing cortical reorganization and altered activity in somatosensory, prefrontal, and insular regions after trigeminal nerve injury [8, 10, 18, 20, 23]. The S1BF is closely linked to sensory-discriminative processing of orofacial nociceptive input, whereas the ACC is strongly implicated in affective-motivational aspects of pain and cognitive-emotional modulation of pain experience [8, 10, 17]. The amygdala and hippocampus may contribute to emotional salience, aversion, and contextual memory associated with persistent pain [17]. Therefore, the widespread activation observed after FLIT-B supports the concept that trigeminal pain involves distributed supraspinal plasticity rather than isolated peripheral pathology. Among the examined regions, the ACC showed particularly prominent and persistent changes. This result aligns with prior evidence implicating medial prefrontal and cingulate cortical regions in trigeminal neuropathic pain and chronic pain-associated affective dysfunction [10, 17, 22]. In the present study, fiber photometry and two-photon calcium imaging demonstrated enhanced spontaneous activity of ACC excitatory neurons after FLIT-B. Because spontaneous ongoing activity may more closely reflect persistent pain and affective burden than purely stimulus-evoked responses, these findings suggest that ACC hyperactivity may contribute to the maintenance of the FLIT-B phenotype.

In addition to functional activation, FLIT-B was associated with increased dendritic complexity in S1BF and ACC excitatory neurons. Structural remodeling has been recognized as an important feature of chronic pain states [28], and previous trigeminal neuropathic pain studies have reported cortical and myelin-related changes in supraspinal regions, including the ACC [22]. Our findings extend these observations by showing region-specific dendritic remodeling in cortical regions implicated in trigeminal sensory and affective processing. However, these morphological changes should be interpreted as associative rather than causal. Although increased dendritic complexity may provide a structural substrate for altered excitability or synaptic integration [39, 40], the present study does not establish that dendritic remodeling is required for pain-like or anxiety-like behavior. Future studies using longitudinal imaging, synaptic physiology, and interventions that specifically modify structural plasticity will be needed to determine whether dendritic remodeling drives, stabilizes, or merely accompanies chronic trigeminal pain-related circuit dysfunction.

Several limitations should be considered. First, FLIT-B produces relatively stable mechanical compression, whereas human TN may involve dynamic neurovascular compression, pulsatile irritation, and intermittent attacks [4, 31]. Second, most mechanistic experiments were performed in young adult male mice, whereas TN is more common in middle-aged and older adults and shows female predominance in clinical populations [4, 31]. Although we added behavioral data from female mice, future studies in aged animals of both sexes will be important. Third, early c-Fos activation may reflect acute surgical and inflammatory responses, not only the established chronic pain state. The later c-Fos and Fos-TRAP analyses help address this issue, but Fos-TRAP labeling captures activity during a defined recombination window and may include stress- or handling-related activation [27]. Future studies with improved temporal and spatial resolution for c-Fos mapping might provide additional insight. Fourth, neuronal activity was recorded under head-fixed conditions, which may influence ACC activity despite habituation. In addition, calcium imaging was performed under spontaneous conditions without external stimulation, which may not fully capture stimulus-evoked responses. Future studies combining freely moving recordings, electrophysiology, sensory stimulation, and pathway-specific manipulations will be needed to determine how ACC activity encodes spontaneous pain, evoked facial hypersensitivity, and anxiety-like behavior. Finally, although chemogenetic inhibition of ACC excitatory neurons attenuated both pain-like and anxiety-like behaviors, these findings should be interpreted as evidence that ACC excitatory neurons contribute functionally to the FLIT-B phenotype rather than as proof that local ACC activity alone is sufficient to account for these behaviors. Future studies using projection-specific chemogenetic or optogenetic manipulations, terminal-specific inhibition, and pathway-resolved calcium imaging will be needed to determine which ACC output circuits mediate the sensory and affective components of the FLIT-B phenotype.

In summary, the present study establishes FLIT-B as a reproducible trigeminal root compression model for investigating neural mechanisms relevant to TN-like pain. This model recapitulates a clinically important anatomical feature of the trigeminal nerve root and identifies ACC excitatory neuron hyperactivity and cortical structural plasticity as supraspinal features associated with chronic trigeminal pain and anxiety-like behaviors. These findings suggest that ACC-centered cortical circuits may represent a mechanistically informed target for future therapeutic studies. In particular, pharmacological approaches that restore cortical excitatory-inhibitory balance, or neuromodulatory strategies designed to modulate ACC-related pain-affect circuits, may provide useful directions for treating trigeminal pain states accompanied by affective disturbances.

Conclusions

The present study establishes FLIT-B as a reproducible and clinically relevant model for investigating the neural mechanisms of TN. This model identifies ACC excitatory neuronal plasticity as a key supraspinal substrate associated with chronic trigeminal pain and affective disturbances.

Methods

Animals and drug administration

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Zhongshan Institute for Drug Discovery (IACUC-P2-2026-03-CQ-01). Male and female C57BL/6 wild-type mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Guangdong Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. Fos2A-iCreERT2 mice (Fos-TRAP2; JAX #030323) were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory. To enable activity-dependent neuronal labeling, Fos-TRAP2 mice were crossed with a Cre-dependent tdTomato reporter line (RCL-tdTomato, Catalog C001742; Cyagen Biosciences). This reporter line carries a loxP-flanked STOP cassette upstream of the tdTomato gene and is functionally comparable to the widely used Ai14 reporter mouse line. All mice were housed in SPF conditions at the Zhongshan Institute for Drug Discovery, maintained on a 12 h light/dark cycle with controlled temperature (22 ± 2 °C) and humidity (50 ± 5%), and had ad libitum access to food and water. Carbamazepine (MedChemExpress, HY-B0246) was administered by oral gavage at 60 mg/kg to mimic the clinical route of administration in patients with TN [15]. The drug was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and further diluted in corn oil to yield a final vehicle containing 10% DMSO. Tamoxifen (Sigma, H6278) was administered via intraperitoneal injection at 50 mg/kg dissolved in corn oil. Clozapine-N-oxide (CNO; MedChemExpress, HY-17366) was administered via intraperitoneal injection at 2 mg/kg dissolved in saline. Control groups received equivalent volumes of the corresponding vehicle. Behavioral experiments were performed in both male and female mice. Mechanistic experiments, including fiber photometry, two-photon imaging, and chemogenetic manipulation, were primarily conducted in male mice to maintain consistency across datasets. Sample sizes were estimated based on prior experience and commonly used standards in the field; no statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes. All animal experiments and data analyses were performed with experimenters blinded to genotype and treatment conditions.

To capture the temporal progression from peripheral injury to central circuit adaptation, we adopted a stage-based experimental design. Early time points (e.g., 2 h post-surgery) were used to assess acute neuronal activation, intermediate time points (e.g., day 7) to examine subacute circuit engagement, and later time points (e.g., days 14–28) to investigate the maintenance phase of chronic pain, including stable behavioral phenotypes, sustained circuit activity, structural remodeling and chemogenetic manipulation.

FLIT and FLIT-B surgical procedures.

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (3% induction, 1.5-2% maintenance) and oxygen (1–2 L/min), placed in the supine position with limbs secured, and the neck region was shaved and disinfected. A 1–1.5 cm midline cervical incision was made, and superficial tissues, including the salivary glands, were gently separated. A mini retractor was used to expose the anterior and posterior digastric muscles.

FLIT procedure

The FLIT procedure was performed as previously described, with minor modifications [15, 16]. Under a surgical microscope (30 × magnification), branches of the left submental vein were sequentially cauterized and used as anatomical landmarks to guide blunt dissection of the masseter and digastric muscles. The tympanic bulla and otic capsule served as reference structures to locate the foramen lacerum. A 1.0 mg gelatin sponge, halved and compacted, was gently inserted into the foramen lacerum at an angle of 45–60° relative to the trachea, to a depth of approximately 2 mm, to compress the trigeminal nerve root.

FLIT-B procedure: Surgical exposure and positioning were identical to the FLIT procedure. Key modifications included: [1] reduction of cauterization sites from three to two, with submental vein branch 1 for dissection while branch 2 served as a anatomical landmark (Fig. 1B); [2] replacement of the gelatin sponge with calibrated glass beads, which were gradually inserted into the foramen lacerum using a customized bead pusher and positioned between the trigeminal nerve root and the auditory vesicle. Bead diameter could be adjusted to modulate the degree of nerve compression and pain phenotype. Total procedure time was ~ 10–15 min. A representative FLIT-B surgery is shown in Supplementary Video 1.

Sham mice underwent identical exposure and dissection without insertion of compression materials. Minor bleeding was controlled with gelatin sponge as needed.

Facial mechanical threshold (von Frey test)

Facial mechanical sensitivity was assessed using a series of calibrated von Frey filaments (Aesthesio, USA) as previously described [16]. Mice were habituated to a restraining device that allowed free movement of the head for 30 min daily for three consecutive days. On the testing day, following a 30 min acclimation period, the whisker pad area innervated by the V2 branch of the trigeminal nerve on the surgical side was stimulated laterally with von Frey filaments of increasing force. Each filament was applied for 1 s with an inter-stimulus interval of 10 s. A brisk head withdrawal was considered a positive response. Testing began with a 0.4 g filament and followed the up-down method for five trials. The lowest force eliciting a positive response was defined as the mechanical withdrawal threshold. Baseline measurements were obtained one day prior to surgery, and postoperative assessments were performed at designated time points.

Facial grooming behavior

Facial grooming was assessed as previously described [41]. Mice were habituated to a transparent acrylic chamber (12 × 12 × 16 cm) equipped with an overhead camera for 30 min daily over three days before the test. On the test day, following 30 min of acclimation, mice were recorded for 10 min. A grooming episode was defined as a sequence in which both forepaws left the floor and made contact with the mouse’s face, terminating when the forepaws returned to the floor. Episodes were required to last at least 3 s and include distinct face-directed movements. Grooming bouts separated by less than 6 s were considered a single episode. The number and total duration of facial grooming episodes within 10 min were quantified by observers blinded to treatment.

Open field test (OFT)

Anxiety-like behaviors were evaluated in an open field arena (40 × 40 × 40 cm acrylic box) as previously described [15, 42]. The central zone was defined as a 20 × 20 cm square located at the center of the arena. Mice were habituated to the testing room for 30 min prior to testing, without exposure to the apparatus, and were then placed in the center of the arena. Locomotor activity was recorded for 10 min using an automated video tracking system. Total distance traveled, distance and time spent in the central zone, and the ratio of central distance to total distance were calculated.

Elevated plus maze (EPM)

The EPM apparatus consisted of two open arms (65 × 5 cm) and two closed arms (65 × 5 × 15 cm) elevated 53 cm above the floor as previously described [42]. On the test day, mice were habituated to the testing room for 30 min and then placed at the center of the maze facing an open arm. Behavior was recorded for 10 min using an automated video tracking system under minimal external disturbance. Time spent and distance traveled in open and closed arms were quantified automatically. Anxiety-like behavior was expressed as the ratio of open-to-closed arm time and distance.

In vivo calcium imaging

Fiber photometry recording

A multichannel fiber photometry system (FPS-SS-MC-OG, ThinkerTech Nanjing Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) was used to record GCaMP6f fluorescence signals. AAV-PHP.eB-CaMKIIα-GCaMP6f (9.1 × 10⁸ vg per brain region) was stereotaxically injected into the following regions: primary somatosensory barrel field (S1BF; AP: 0 mm, ML: +3.15 mm, DV: −2 mm), anterior cingulate cortex (ACC; AP: +0.73 mm, ML: +0.28 mm, DV: −1.75 mm), amygdala (AMY; AP: −2.15 mm, ML: +4.68 mm, DV: −4.83 mm; 15° lateral angle), and hippocampus (HIP; AP: −1.67 mm, ML: +0.4 mm, DV: −1.8 mm). Optical fibers (200 μm core diameter, 0.5 numerical aperture; R-FOC-BL200C-50NA, RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China) were implanted above each region. Fiber lengths were 4 mm for S1BF, ACC, and HIP, and 6 mm for AMY. Fibers were secured with dental acrylic, and mice were singly housed for recovery and viral expression for at least 3 weeks prior to recording. Before recording, mice were habituated to the recording setup for 3 consecutive days (30 min/day) with the patch cord connected to minimize stress and movement-related artifacts. Fiber photometry recordings were performed at baseline and during the chronic phase (14 days post-surgery), with time points matched to behavioral assessments. Fluorescence signals were expressed as ΔF/F = (F − F₀)/F₀, where F₀ was defined as the mean baseline fluorescence calculated over a 100 s period of stable activity. Background offset was measured and subtracted prior to normalization.

The 470-nm laser beam was reflected by a dichroic mirror and focused through an objective lens to excite GCaMP6f. The 405-nm laser was used as an isosbestic control channel to account for motion artifacts and photo-bleaching. The calcium signal was detected using a photomultiplier tube and converted into electrical signals via customized acquisition software written in NI LabVIEW (National Instruments). A stable 4-minute recording segment was selected for each mouse in a blinded manner, and the maximum calcium signal amplitude from each of the four brain regions was analyzed using custom MATLAB (MathWorks) scripts based on MAT files generated by Thinker Biotech [43].

Craniotomy and virus injection

The surgical and imaging procedures were performed as previously described, with minor modifications [43, 44]. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (3% for induction, 1.5–2% for maintenance). Meloxicam (1 mg/kg, intraperitoneal) was administered pre-operatively and every 24 h for 3 days post-surgery to reduce pain and inflammation. The head was shaved from the nasal septum to the occipital bone, and ophthalmic ointment was applied to prevent corneal drying. Mice were then secured in a stereotaxic frame with a gas anesthesia mask. The surgical area was disinfected with alternating applications of 2% iodine and 70% ethanol. The scalp overlying the dorsal skull was removed, and the periosteum was carefully cleared. A 3 × 3 mm craniotomy was made above the ACC, with the dura left intact and continuously moistened with sterile saline. To express GCaMP6f in ACC pyramidal neurons, AAV-PHP.eB-CaMKIIα-GCaMP6f (1 × 10¹² vg/mL) was injected at a depth of approximately 300 μm into the ACC at 5–6 sites using a microinjector (RWD, China). For cranial window implantation, two coverslips were bonded using ultraviolet-curable optical adhesive (NOR-138, Norland). A smaller coverslip (3 mm diameter) was fitted into the craniotomy, and a larger coverslip (5 mm diameter) was affixed to the surrounding skull. A custom-designed head plate was secured over the cranial window using dental cement (Metabond, Parkell).

Two-photon imaging and data analysis

Imaging was performed 3–4 weeks after viral injection to allow sufficient expression. Prior to imaging, mice were habituated to head fixation in the two-photon microscope room for 30 min per day for at least three sessions. In vivo imaging was performed in awake, head-fixed mice using a two-photon microscope (FluoView FVMPE-RS, Olympus, Japan) equipped with a Mai Tai DeepSee Ti:sapphire laser (Spectra-Physics, USA). The laser was tuned to 910 nm, and the average power at the sample was maintained at approximately 20–30 mW. Imaging was performed using a 25×, 1.05 NA water-immersion objective (Olympus). Images were acquired at depths of 300–500 μm below the pial surface at a frame rate of 10 Hz using resonant scanning with Olympus acquisition software. Each field of view (FOV) was 509 × 509 μm at a resolution of 512 × 512 pixels.

Imaging data were analyzed using custom MATLAB scripts (MathWorks, USA), as previously described in our previous study [44]. For calcium imaging analysis, 3,000 frames were acquired per mouse at 10 Hz to assess spontaneous neuronal activity. Motion artifacts, primarily arising from small movements in the x–y plane, were corrected using rigid-body image registration to align imaging frames. Regions of interest (ROIs) corresponding to individual neurons were manually identified based on neuronal morphology and fluorescence dynamics by an experimenter blinded to group identity. Binary masks were generated for each ROI, and fluorescence signals were extracted for subsequent analysis. Calcium activity was quantified as ΔF/F, where F0 was defined as the baseline fluorescence calculated as the median fluorescence over the recording period. To characterize calcium dynamics in individual neurons, four parameters were quantified: event duration, event amplitude, event frequency, and AUC. Calcium events were detected using a threshold based on the median absolute deviation (MAD), defined as 2 × (MAD/0.6745), which provides a robust estimate of the standard deviation under Gaussian noise assumptions. A calcium event was defined as a period during which the ΔF/F signal exceeded the detection threshold for at least 1 s. Event onset was defined as the first frame in which ΔF/F exceeded the median baseline value, and event offset was defined as the frame at which the signal returned below this level. Event duration was calculated as the time between onset and offset, and event amplitude was defined as the peak ΔF/F during the event. Event frequency was calculated as the number of detected events per second (Hz), and all parameters were computed on a per-cell basis. AUC was calculated as the integral of the ΔF/F signal above baseline (y = 0) and normalized to the recording duration.

In vivo chemogenetic manipulation

The surgical procedures were performed as previously described with minor modifications [45]. AAV2/9-mCaMKIIα-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry (1.80 × 10¹² vg/mL; Taitool Bioscience, Cat# S0141-9, China) was bilaterally injected into the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC; AP: +0.73 mm, ML: ±0.28 mm, DV: −1.75 mm) at a volume of 240 nL per side. Viral injections were delivered at a rate of 30 nL/min, and the injection needle was left in place for 5 min after infusion to minimize backflow. After 21 days of viral expression, mice underwent pain model surgery. On postoperative day 14, CNO (2 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally, and behavioral testing was performed 30 min after injection.

Immunofluorescence staining

Mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane (RWD Co., China) and transcardially perfused with ice-cold 1× PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 1× PBS. Brains were dissected, post-fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C overnight and sectioned at 50 μm thickness using a vibratome (VT1000S, Leica). Brain sections were permeabilized in PBS containing 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min and then blocked in blocking buffer (PBS containing 15% goat serum and 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA)) for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were incubated with primary antibody (rabbit anti-c-Fos, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 2250S, 1:200) in blocking buffer at 4 °C overnight. After primary incubation, sections were washed three times (10 min each) in PBS containing 0.2% Triton X-100 and then incubated with secondary antibody (donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# A-21206, 1:1000) for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were subsequently washed twice (10 min each) and counterstained with Hoechst (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# H3570) in PBS.

Quantification of c-Fos-positive neurons

To minimize potential confounding effects, all handling and experimental procedures were standardized to reduce external stress and variability. Mice were subjected to pain model surgery, and c-Fos-positive neurons were quantified at 2 h postoperation, postoperative day 7, and day 14. All imaging and histological analyses were performed at anatomically matched stereotaxic coordinates across animals, with consistent sampling of the same coronal levels, to minimize variability and enable reliable comparisons between groups. For the S1BF and ACC regions, images were acquired using an Olympus FV3000 microscope with a 10× objective. Brain regions and cortical layers were identified by referencing a standard brain atlas in ImageJ, and laminar quantification was performed. Cell counts across layers were summed to obtain the total number of c-Fos-positive neurons for each region. For the AMY and HIP regions, images were acquired using a 20× objective and quantified directly in ImageJ. For each region, c-Fos-positive neurons were quantified by combining counts from both hemispheres, providing an overall measure of regional activation.

To assess the effect of hM4Di-mediated inhibition on c-Fos expression, two neuronal populations were analyzed: c-Fos single-positive neurons (not infected with AAV2/9-mCaMKIIα-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry) and c-Fos/mCherry double-positive neurons. The fluorescence intensity of c-Fos in double-positive neurons was normalized to mCherry negative neurons prior to statistical analysis.

Anti-S100β staining

Mice were transcardially perfused with 4% PFA in 1× PBS. Trigeminal nerve roots were rapidly dissected, post-fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C overnight, and cryoprotected in 10%, 20%, and 30% sucrose solutions sequentially. Tissues were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound and sectioned into 10 μm-thick cryosections. Sections were air-dried at 56 °C for 10 min to improve adhesion and then washed three times with PBS (5 min each). Sections were permeabilized in PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 for 20 min at room temperature, followed by blocking in PBS containing 15% sheep serum and 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were then incubated with primary antibody (mouse anti-S100β, Proteintech, Cat# 66616-1-Ig, 1:500) diluted in blocking solution containing 0.3% Triton X-100 at 4 °C overnight. After primary incubation, sections were washed three times with PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 (5 min each). Sections were subsequently incubated with secondary antibody (donkey anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 555, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# A-31570, 1:1000) diluted in blocking solution containing 0.3% Triton X-100 for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# H3570). Finally, sections were washed twice with PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 and once with PBS (5 min each) prior to mounting.

Sparse neuronal labeling and morphological analysis

Sparse neuronal labeling was performed with reference to previously published methods, with minor modifications [29, 46]. To achieve sparse labeling of excitatory neurons across different brain regions, AAV-PHP.eB-CaMKIIα-EGFP-P2A-EGFPf (6.6 × 10⁸ vg/mouse) was administered via tail vein injection. After 28 days of viral expression, mice were transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS followed by 4% PFA in PBS. Brains were sectioned into 200-μm-thick coronal slices using a vibratome (VT1000S, Leica). Sections were rinsed in PBS and permeabilized in PBS containing 0.5% Triton X-100 for 2 h at room temperature. After rinsing, sections were blocked in PBS containing 15% normal goat serum, 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA), and 0.2% Triton X-100 for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were incubated with primary antibody (rabbit anti-GFP, Abcam, Cat# ab6556, 1:1000) at 4 °C for 48 h, followed by five washes in PBS (15 min each). Secondary antibody incubation (donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# A-21206, 1:1000) was performed at 4 °C for 12 h. Sections were then counterstained with Hoechst (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# H3570) for 15 min at room temperature. After washing five times in PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 and once in PBS alone (15 min each), sections were mounted using Fluoromount-G (Southern Biotech, Cat# 0100-01). All incubation and washing steps were performed on an orbital shaker (150 rpm).

Whole-brain images were acquired using a VS200 wide-field microscope (Olympus, Japan) with a 10× objective. EGFP-labeled neurons were imaged using a confocal microscope (FV3000, Olympus, Japan). To preserve dendritic integrity, neurons located near the mid-depth of sections were selected for imaging using 20× (NA 0.8) or 40× (NA 0.95) objectives with a Z-step size of 1.5 μm. Image quality was enhanced using built-in signal optimization functions. Individual neurons were manually reconstructed in 3D using the Filament module in Imaris software (v10.0.1, Oxford Instruments, USA). Background noise was reduced using the Surface module. Morphological parameters, including total dendritic length and Sholl intersections, were quantified. Sholl analysis was performed, and AUC and peak intersection values were calculated using customized MATLAB scripts (MathWorks, USA). Total dendritic length was defined as the sum of all dendritic segments traced from a single neuron.

Western blot

Mice were euthanized 7 days after surgery, and trigeminal nerve tissues were collected for protein extraction. Tissues were homogenized in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, Cat# P0010) and equalized across samples. Equal amounts of protein (20 µg per sample) were separated by SDS-PAGE using 4–12% polyacrylamide gels (ACE Biotechnology, Cat# F00001GelS) and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against myelin basic protein (rat anti-MBP, Sigma, Cat# MAB386, 1:1000) and β-tubulin (mouse anti-β-tubulin, Yeasen, Cat# 30301ES40, 1:10,000). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-rat, Yeasen, Cat# 33301ES60, 1:10,000; goat anti-mouse, Yeasen, Cat# 33201ES60, 1:10,000) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (ChemiDoc MP, Bio-Rad) and quantified using ImageJ software. Band intensities were normalized to β-tubulin as a loading control.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v10.0). Data was first assessed for normality and homogeneity of variance. For normally distributed data with equal variances, comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests, and comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way or two-way ANOVA, as appropriate. For data that did not meet normality assumptions, the Mann-Whitney U test was used for two-group comparisons. All experiments and data analysis were performed in a blinded manner. Statistical significance was defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

One-way ANOVA within the FLIT-B group further revealed significant regional differences; post-hoc tests indicated that compared with the ACC, signal amplitudes were lower in S1BF, AMY, and HIP. (J) Left: heatmaps of calcium activity at baseline, postoperative day 7 (D7), and postoperative day 14 (D14). Right: representative ΔF/F traces from five neurons showing progressive increases in activity following FLIT-B surgery. (K–N) Quantitative analysis of individual calcium events.
