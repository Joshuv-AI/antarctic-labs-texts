Inverse problem for semidiscrete stochastic parabolic operators in arbitrary dimensions
Rodrigo Lecaros, Ariel A. Pérez, Manuel F. Prado

Abstract
. In this paper, we study two types of inverse problems for space semidiscrete stochastic parabolic equations in arbitrary dimensions. The first problem concerns a semidiscrete inverse source problem, which involves determining the random source term of the white noise in the stochastic parabolic equation using observation data of the solution at the terminal time and the trace of the discrete spatial derivative of the solution on a subdomain at the boundary of the space. The second problem addresses a semidiscrete Cauchy inverse problem, which involves determining the solution of the stochastic parabolic equation in a subdomain of space-time, from the data of the solution and the trace of the discrete spatial derivative on a subdomain of the boundary of space and time. To address these problems, we establish two Carleman estimates for a semidiscrete stochastic parabolic operator in arbitrary dimensions: one for the homogeneous boundary condition and the other for the nonhomogeneous boundary condition. Applying these Carleman estimates, we obtain Lipschitz and Hölder stability for the first and second inverse problems, respectively.

We also define the trace operator

We consider the following

\begin{cases}\mathcal{A}_{h}w=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{1i}A_{i}D_{i}(w)+a_{2}w\right)dt+gdB(t),&\text{in }Q\triangleq\mathcal{M}\times(0,T),\\

Semidiscrete Cauchy Problem.
We now consider the same semi-discretization of (

(1.8)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{A}_{h}w=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{1i}A_{i}D_{i}(w)+a_{2}w\right)dt+a_{3}w\,dB(t),&\text{in }Q\triangleq\mathcal{M}\times(0,T),\\

The size of the observation set

The two inverse problems under consideration are associated with stochastic parabolic equations. Stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) have broad applications across multiple disciplines. They model phenomena such as particle diffusion and heat propagation in physics, option pricing in finance, epidemic spread and population dynamics in biology, systems with uncertain parameters in engineering, and pollutant dispersion in environmental sciences. These equations incorporate randomness and uncertainty, making them particularly suitable for modeling inherently unpredictable phenomena. Consequently, stochastic PDEs often provide more realistic representations than their deterministic counterparts. Nevertheless, research on inverse problems for stochastic PDEs remains limited. For further details, see
(
lu2021mathematical
, Chapter 5)
and the references therein.

For the inverse source problem related to stochastic parabolic equations,
ref27
establishes uniqueness in determining the source function
f
in the drift term. The inverse source problem of simultaneously determining two types of sources,
f
(drift term) and
g
(diffusion term), in a stochastic parabolic equation was studied in
ref37
using observations at the final time and on the lateral boundary. A unique result for this inverse problem was obtained using a global Carleman estimate for the continuous stochastic parabolic equation. In addition,
ref1
and
ref2
discuss the application of regularization techniques within numerical methods to inverse random source problems.

The Cauchy problem aims to recover the solution using the observed data on the lateral boundary. In
Yamamoto_2009
, conditional stability was established for the Cauchy problem of deterministic parabolic equations. The conditional stability and the convergence rate of the Tikhonov regularization method for the Cauchy problem of stochastic parabolic equations were obtained in
ref13
. It is worth mentioning that
ref28
provides a detailed review of inverse problems for stochastic PDEs. However, all of these results were obtained within a continuous framework. For semidiscrete inverse problems related to stochastic differential equations, see
WWW:2024
, which studies the one-dimensional case of both issues, including a semidiscrete inverse random source problem and a semidiscrete Cauchy problem.

This article builds upon the results of
WWW:2024

provides integrability conditions for the coefficient

that depend on the spatial dimension of the problem. Furthermore, Theorem

2.
Semidiscrete inverse random source problem

In this section, we establish the stability of the discrete inverse random source problem (Theorem

The key tool for proving this result is a semidiscrete Carleman estimate, whose proof is deferred to

Assume
\gamma_{i}>0

\mbox{reg}(\gamma)\triangleq\text{ess}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in G\\
i=1,\cdots,n\end{subarray}}\left(\gamma_{i}+\frac{1}{\gamma_{i}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\partial_{i}\gamma_{i}|^{2}\right)<\infty.

, define the weight functions

\displaystyle\psi(x)

\displaystyle=|x-x^{*}|^{2},

(2.2)

and for

Given

Remark 2.1
.

Compared to
EstimateCarlemanStochasticControl
and other works on parabolic operators, the weight function here has been modified, particularly in its temporal component; however, the structure

is preserved. Moreover, the functions satisfy

and

for some constant

and for all

are obtained, and most of the cross-product terms can be treated in a manner analogous to

3.
Semidiscrete Cauchy problem.

In this section, we establish the conditional stability result for the semidiscrete Cauchy problem, as stated in Theorem
1.3
. The section is structured as follows: First, we derive an auxiliary estimate for the solution of a semidiscrete stochastic parabolic equation with non-homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. This estimate plays a crucial role in adapting the Carleman estimate (
1.6
) to this setting. Second, we present a modified Carleman estimate adapted to the semidiscrete stochastic parabolic operator with nonhomogeneous boundary data, and provide its proof. Finally, we prove Theorem
1.3
by combining the Carleman estimate obtained in the second step with geometric arguments inspired by
Yamamoto_2009

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\xi\in H_{\mathcal{F}}^{1}(0,T;H_{h}^{1/2}(\partial\mathcal{M})\cap H_{h}^{1}(\partial\mathcal{M}))
with
\xi(0)=0
. Then, for a.e.
\omega\in\Omega
, the solution
u\in L^{2}(0,T;H_{h}^{2}(\mathcal{M}))\cap C(0,T;L_{h}^{2}(\mathcal{M}))
of the system:

(3.1)

\begin{cases}du-\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}(\gamma_{i}D_{i}u)\,dt=0,&\text{in }\mathcal{M}\times(0,T),\\
u=\xi,&\text{on }\partial\mathcal{M}\times(0,T),\\
u(0)=0,&\text{in }\mathcal{M},\end{cases}

satisfies the following stability estimate for any constant
C>0
:

(3.2)

\|u\|_{L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}(0,T;H_{h}^{2}(\mathcal{M}))}+\|u\|_{L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega;C([0,T];L_{h}^{2}(\mathcal{M})))}\leq C\|\xi\|_{H^{1}_{\mathcal{F}}(0,T;H_{h}^{1/2}(\partial\mathcal{M})\cap H_{h}^{1}(\partial\mathcal{M}))}.

Moreover, the following boundary estimate holds for
t_{r}^{i}(D_{i}u)
on
\Gamma^{+}
:

(3.3)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma^{+}}t_{r}^{i}(|D_{i}u|^{2})\,dt\leq C\|\xi\|_{H^{1}_{\mathcal{F}}(0,T;H_{h}^{1/2}(\partial\mathcal{M})\cap H_{h}^{1}(\partial\mathcal{M}))}.

Proof.

Noting that for a.e.
\omega\in\Omega
and
t\in[0,T]
,
\xi\in H_{h}^{1/2}(\partial\mathcal{M})
, there exists
v\in H_{h}^{1}(\mathcal{M})
such that for a.e.
\omega\in\Omega
and
t\in[0,T]
,
v=\xi
on
\partial\mathcal{M}
and

(3.4)

\|v\|_{H_{h}^{1}(\mathcal{M})}=\|\xi\|_{H_{h}^{1/2}(\partial\mathcal{M})}.

Let
\tilde{u}=u-v
. Since
v(0)=0
by (
3.4
) and
\xi(0)=0
, it follows that
\tilde{u}(0)=u(0)-v(0)=0
. Moreover, on
\partial\mathcal{M}\times(0,T)
we have
\tilde{u}=\xi-\xi=0
. Therefore,
\tilde{u}
satisfies

(3.5)

\begin{cases}d\tilde{u}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}(\gamma_{i}D_{i}\tilde{u})\,dt=\left(\partial_{t}v-\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}(\gamma_{i}D_{i}v)\right)\,dt,&\text{in }\mathcal{M}\times(0,T),\\
\tilde{u}=0,&\text{on }\partial\mathcal{M}\times(0,T),\\
\tilde{u}(0)=0,&\text{in }\mathcal{M}.\end{cases}

Multiplying the first equation of (
3.5
) by
\tilde{u}
and integrating over
\mathcal{M}
, we get

\int_{\mathcal{M}}\tilde{u}\,d\tilde{u}-\int_{\mathcal{M}}\tilde{u}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}(\gamma_{i}D_{i}\tilde{u})\,dt=\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}\tilde{u}\,\partial_{t}v-\int_{\mathcal{M}}\tilde{u}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}(\gamma_{i}D_{i}v)\right)\,dt.

Applying integration by parts to the second integral on both sides, we obtain

\int_{\mathcal{M}}\tilde{u}\,d\tilde{u}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\gamma_{i}|D_{i}\tilde{u}|^{2}\,dt=\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}\tilde{u}\,\partial_{t}v+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\gamma_{i}D_{i}v\,D_{i}\tilde{u}\right)\,dt,

where we used
\tilde{u}=0
on
\partial\mathcal{M}
. Observing that
\tilde{u}\,d\tilde{u}=\frac{1}{2}\,d(\tilde{u}^{2})
and applying Young’s inequality to the right-hand side of the previous inequality, we get

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\,d(\tilde{u}^{2})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\gamma_{i}|D_{i}\tilde{u}|^{2}\,dt\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\tilde{u}|^{2}+\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\partial_{t}v|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\gamma_{i}|D_{i}v|^{2}\right)\,dt.

Now, adding
\frac{1}{2}\|\tilde{u}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}
and using (
3.4
) in the above inequality, we obtain

(3.6)

\frac{1}{2}\,d\left(\|\tilde{u}\|^{2}_{L_{h}^{2}(\mathcal{M})}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\|\tilde{u}\|^{2}_{H_{h}^{1}(\mathcal{M})}\,dt\leq\left(\|\tilde{u}\|^{2}_{L_{h}^{2}(\mathcal{M})}+C\|\xi\|_{H^{1}(0,T;H_{h}^{1/2}(\partial\mathcal{M}))}\right)\,dt.

Letting
E(t)=\|\tilde{u}\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{h}(\mathcal{M})}
and
\mathcal{C}_{1}=C\|\xi\|_{H^{1}(0,T;H_{h}^{1/2}(\partial\mathcal{M}))}
in (
3.6
), we have

\frac{1}{2}\,dE(t)\leq(E(t)+\mathcal{C}_{1})\,dt.

Multiplying by
e^{-2t}
and using Itô’s lemma, we obtain

\begin{split}e^{-2t}\,dE(t)-e^{-2t}E(t)\,dt\leq&e^{-2t}\mathcal{C}_{1}\,dt,\\
d\left(e^{-2t}E(t)\right)\leq&e^{-2t}\mathcal{C}_{1}\,dt.\end{split}

Since
E(0)=0
, integrating over
(0,t)
for
t\in[0,T]
, we have

(3.7)

\|\tilde{u}\|^{2}_{L_{h}^{2}(\mathcal{M})}=E(t)\leq\int_{0}^{t}e^{2(t-s)}\mathcal{C}_{1}\,ds=\frac{-1+e^{2t}}{2}\mathcal{C}_{1},

for all
t\in[0,T]
. Recalling that
u=\tilde{u}+v
, and using (
3.4
) and (
3.7
), we get

\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{h}(\mathcal{M})}\leq\|\tilde{u}\|^{2}_{L_{h}^{2}(\mathcal{M})}+\|v\|^{2}_{L_{h}^{2}(\mathcal{M})}\leq\frac{e^{2t}-1}{2}\mathcal{C}_{1}+C\|\xi\|_{H^{1}(0,T;H_{h}^{1/2}(\partial\mathcal{M}))}.

Therefore, taking the supremum over
[0,T]
in the above inequality, we conclude that

\|u\|_{C(0,T;L_{h}^{2}(\mathcal{M}))}\leq C\|\xi\|_{H^{1}(0,T;H_{h}^{1/2}(\partial\mathcal{M}))}.

Furthermore, using (
3.6
), (

) and integrating over
(0,T)
, we have

\|u\|_{L_{h}^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathcal{M}))}\leq C\|\xi\|_{H_{h}^{1}(0,T;H^{1/2}(\partial\mathcal{M}))}.

Combining the last two inequalities and taking the expectation, we derive the first estimate (
3.2
).

In turn, we note that multiplying by
x_{j}A_{j}D_{j}u
, where
x_{j}:=e_{j}\cdot x
, the main equation from (

) we obtain

First, let us focus on the right-hand side above. Thanks to (
1.13
) we write

(3.9)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}(\gamma_{i}D_{i}u)x_{j}A_{j}D_{j}udt=

\displaystyle x_{j}D_{j}\gamma_{j}|A_{j}D_{j}u|^{2}+x_{j}A_{j}\gamma_{j}D_{j}^{2}u\,D_{j}A_{j}u

\displaystyle+\sum_{i\neq j}x_{j}D_{i}(\gamma_{i}D_{i}u)\,D_{j}A_{j}u.

Then, writing

, and applying an integration by parts it follows

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{M}^{\ast}_{j}}D_{j}(A_{j}\gamma_{j}x_{j})|D_{j}u|^{2}

Hence, integrating over

\displaystyle\int_{G}\,x_{j}A_{j}D_{j}u\,du=

The proof is divided into three steps. First, in Section
