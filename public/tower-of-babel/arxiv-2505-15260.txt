How thin does random interlacement have to be so that a random walk can see through it?
Nicolas Bouchot

Abstract
The random interlacements ℐ ⁡ ( u ) \mathscr{I}(u) at level u u has been introduced by Sznitman, as a Poissonian collection of independent simple random walk trajectories on ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} , d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 , with intensity u > 0 u>0 . Since then, several works investigated the properties of the random interlacements intersected with large sets of ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} . In this paper, we study the asymptotic behavior of the capacity of ℐ ⁡ ( u ) ∩ D N \mathscr{I}(u)\cap D_{N} , where D N D_{N} is the blow up of a compact set D D , with typical size N N . We determine the correct window ( u N ) N ≥ 1 (u_{N})_{N\geq 1} of the intensity parameter for which the capacity cap ⁡ ( ℐ ⁡ ( u N ) ∩ D N ) \mathrm{cap}(\mathscr{I}(u_{N})\cap D_{N}) starts to become negligible compared to cap ⁡ ( D N ) \mathrm{cap}(D_{N}) ; this roughly means that a random walk starting from far away starts to see through ℐ ⁡ ( u N ) ∩ D N \mathscr{I}(u_{N})\cap D_{N} . In the same spirit, we investigate the capacity of the simple random walk conditioned to stay in a large Euclidean ball up to time t N t_{N} , and find similar asymptotics by taking t N = u N ​ N d t_{N}=u_{N}N^{d} . Keywords: capacity, random interlacement, conditioned walk 2020 Mathematics subject classification: Primary 60G50, 60K35.



1
Introduction

The random interlacement at level
u>0
is a random subset
\mathscr{I}(u)\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
d\geq 3
, which was introduced by Sznitman in
[
26
]
, in order to study the trace of the simple random walk on the torus
\mathbb{T}_{n}\vcentcolon=\mathbb{Z}^{d}/n\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
It can be thought as a Poissonian collection of bi-infinite simple random walk trajectories on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
(that are transient since
d\geq 3
), with an intensity parameter
u>0
.
We refer to
[
15
]
for a detailed introduction and overview.

In the following, we denote by
\mathbf{P}_{x}
the law of a simple random walk
(S_{n})_{n\geq 0}
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, with starting point
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. We also simply write
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{P}_{0}
.
We also denote
K\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
to say that
K
is a finite subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Then, a characterization of the law
\mathbb{P}
of the random interlacement
(\mathscr{I}(u))_{u>0}
is given by the following relation

\forall K\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}\,,\quad\mathbb{P}\big(\mathscr{I}(u)\cap K=\varnothing\big)=\exp\big(-u\,\mathrm{cap}\left(K\right)\big)\,.

(1.1)

Here,
\mathrm{cap}\left(K\right)
denotes the capacity of the set
K
in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, defined as

\mathrm{cap}\left(K\right)\vcentcolon=\sum_{x\in K}\mathbf{P}_{x}\big(H_{K}=+\infty\big)\,,

(1.2)

with
H_{A}\vcentcolon=\min\{i\geq 1,S_{i}\in A\}
the first return time of the set
A
; by convention
\min\varnothing=+\infty
.
The capacity is a non-decreasing function which characterizes the size of a subset seen by a (simple) random walk coming from far away. In this paper, we are interested in how the capacity of a set compares with the capacity of the trace of random walk(s) on this set.

1.1
Our main question

We consider a bounded open set
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and we let
D_{N}\vcentcolon=(N\cdot D)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be its (discrete) blow up of size
N
.
We are interested in
\mathscr{I}(u)\cap D_{N}
the trace of the random interlacement inside
D_{N}
,
and more precisely in the ratio of their capacities

\varsigma^{\mathrm{RI}}_{N,u}=\varsigma^{\mathrm{RI}}_{N,u}(D)\vcentcolon=\frac{\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathscr{I}(u)\cap D_{N}\right)}{\mathrm{cap}\left(D_{N}\right)}\,,

(1.3)

in the regime
n\to\infty
and
u\downarrow 0
.
Notice that since
\mathscr{I}(u)\cap D_{N}\subseteq D_{N}
, we have that
\varsigma_{n,u}\in[0,1]
.
In particular, we are looking for a condition on the sequence
(u_{N})_{n\geq 0}
for the ratio
\varsigma_{n}
to converge either to
1
or
0
, in
\mathbb{P}
-probability.

Let us define

\Theta_{N}\vcentcolon=\begin{cases}N&\text{ if }d=3\,,\\
N^{2}/\log N&\text{ if }d=4\,,\\
N^{2}&\text{ if }d\geq 5\,.\end{cases}

(1.4)

The motivation behind this definition (
1.4
) of
\Theta_{N}
is so that
\Theta_{N}
and
\mathbf{E}\big[\mathrm{cap}(\mathcal{R}_{N^{2}})\big]
are of the same order (see Theorem
2.2
below).
We then have the following criterion.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
d\geq 3
and let
(u_{N})_{N\geq 1}
be a sequence of positive numbers.
For any compact regular set
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have the following convergences in
\mathbb{P}
-probability (and in
L^{1}
):

\varsigma^{\mathrm{RI}}_{N,u_{N}}=\varsigma_{N,u_{N}}^{\mathrm{RI}}(D)\vcentcolon=\frac{\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathscr{I}(u_{N})\cap D_{N}\right)}{\mathrm{cap}\left(D_{N}\right)}\xrightarrow[N\to+\infty]{}\begin{cases}0&\quad\text{ if }\ \lim_{N\to\infty}u_{N}\Theta_{N}=0\,,\\
1&\quad\text{ if }\ \lim_{N\to\infty}u_{N}\Theta_{N}=+\infty\,.\end{cases}

(1.5)

Moreover, if
0<\liminf_{N\to\infty}u_{N}\Theta_{N}\leq\limsup_{N\to\infty}u_{N}\Theta_{N}<+\infty
, then

0<\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\varsigma^{\mathrm{RI}}_{N,u_{N}}]\leq\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\varsigma^{\mathrm{RI}}_{N,u_{N}}]<1\,.

(1.6)

Remark 1.2
.

In Theorem
1.1
, the assumption that
D
is regular means that
\mathrm{cap}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(D\right)=\mathrm{cap}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\mathrm{Int}(D)\right)
, where
\mathrm{cap}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\,\cdot\,\right)
denotes the Newtonian capacity on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(see
[
24
]
for the necessary definitions). It is required to compare
\mathrm{cap}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(D\right)
with
\mathrm{cap}\left(D_{N}\right)
(see Proposition
2.3
below).

Remark 1.3
.

In the case where
\lim_{N\to\infty}u_{N}\Theta_{N}=a\in(0,+\infty)
, we expect that the ratio
\varsigma_{N,u_{N}}
converges in distribution to a random variable
\varsigma_{a}\in(0,1)
.
A first step towards this would be to prove the following tightness-type result:
for any
\varepsilon>0
there is some
\eta>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\big(\eta<\varsigma^{\mathrm{RI}}_{N,u_{N}}<1-\eta\big)\geq 1-\varepsilon\,.

(1.7)

The bounds (
1.6
) only shows that for sufficiently small
\eta>0
, the probability
\mathbb{P}\big(\eta<\varsigma_{N}<1-\eta\big)
remains bounded away from
0
.

Remark 1.4
.

Let us notice that the parameter
u
is related to the density (with respect to the volume) of random interlacements in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Indeed, for any fixed
u
, we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{|\mathscr{I}(u)\cap D_{N}|}{|D_{N}|}=1-e^{-u/g_{0}}=\vcentcolon\vartheta(u)\,,

(1.8)

with
g_{0}\vcentcolon=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}_{0}(S_{n}=0)
.
In particular, when
u_{N}\downarrow 0
, the random interlacement
\mathscr{I}(u_{N})
is very sparse and has large holes, but the capacity
\mathrm{cap}(\mathscr{I}(u_{N})\cap D_{N})
may remains equivalent to
\mathrm{cap}(D_{N})
, meaning that the holes are not “visible” for a random walk arriving from far away.
In that sense, Theorem
2.2
gives a threshold on
(u_{N})_{n\geq 1}
for these holes to become “visible”.

About the capacity of a random walk conditioned to stay in a ball

Random interlacements can be interpreted as an infinite volume measure for the simple random walk on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}_{N}=(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^{d}
. The link between these two objects has been a powerful tool to tackle problems involving this random walk on the torus (see
[
29
,
4
,
25
]
) as well as large-deviation events for the simple random walk on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
(see
[
21
,
10
]
).

The coupling between the two objects presented in
[
8
]
(and previous works) relies on a heuristical argument, which roughly explains that given a subset
K
of the torus, the random walk may visit
K
and then stay in
\mathbb{T}^{d}_{N}\setminus K
a time larger than its mixing time. The eventual return to
K
would then be independent from the last times the random walk was in
K
, and the number of such returns is well-approximated by a Poisson random variable.

Consider the discrete Euclidean ball
B_{N}
with radius
N\geq 1
and a sequence
(t_{N})_{N\geq 1}
, and write
\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}=\left\{S_{0},\,\dots,S_{t_{N}}\right\}
the range up to time
t_{N}
of the simple random walk on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Under
\mathbf{P}_{0}(\cdot\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})
, the random walk behaves in a way such that the heuristical argument still has some truth (see
[
7
]
for a more rigorous statement). By comparison to random interlacements, the range under
\mathbf{P}_{0}(\cdot\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})
can be expected to resemble a random interlacement with parameter
u_{N}\sim ct_{N}N^{-d}
. Therefore, we can conjecture an analogue to Theorem
1.1
for
\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}
under
\mathbf{P}_{0}(\cdot\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})
, by plugging
u_{N}=t_{N}N^{-d}
. We prove this result in Section
3
, although our method does not rely on any coupling.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
d\geq 3
and let
(t_{N})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of positive numbers, under
\mathbf{P}(\cdot\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})
we have the following convergences in
\mathbb{P}
-probability and in
L^{1}
:

\varsigma_{N,t_{N}}^{\mathrm{RW}}\vcentcolon=\frac{\mathrm{cap}(\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}})}{\mathrm{cap}(B_{N})}\xrightarrow[N\to+\infty]{}\begin{cases}0&\quad\text{ if }\ \lim_{N\to\infty}t_{N}\Theta_{N}/N^{d}=0\,,\\
1&\quad\text{ if }\ \lim_{N\to\infty}t_{N}\Theta_{N}/N^{d}=+\infty\,.\end{cases}

(1.9)

Remark 1.6
.

We stated the result in the case of the ball
B_{N}
, but Theorem
1.5
remains valid for « reasonable » subsets
D_{N}
in dimension
d\geq 4
(see the hypothesis on
D
in the next chapter). However, we are unable to get the full statement for dimension
d=3
. Indeed, properly relaxing the random walk takes a time
N^{2}\log N
(to dwarf the next terms in the spectral decomposition, see Lemma
3.7
), thus we are not able to handle the case where
N^{2}\ll t_{N}\leq C\log N
for some constant
C>0
, which corresponds to
1\ll t_{N}\Theta_{N}N^{-d}\leq C\log N
.

Remark 1.7
.

With our proofs, it is easy to see that an analogue to (
1.6
) also holds for the random walk under
\mathbf{P}(\cdot\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})
. However, getting the full statement would require a bit more control on the constants appearing in the proofs, which is not the focus of this paper. We can however prove that there exist
\alpha_{-},\alpha_{+}\in(0,+\infty)
such that

\varlimsup\limits_{N\to+\infty}\frac{t_{N}\Theta_{N}}{N^{d}}\leq\alpha_{-}
implies
\varlimsup\limits_{N\to+\infty}\mathbf{E}\left[\varsigma_{N,t_{N}}^{\mathrm{RW}}\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}\right]<1
; and similarly if
\varliminf\limits_{N\to+\infty}t_{N}\Theta_{N}N^{-d}\geq\alpha_{+}
, then
\varliminf\limits_{N\to+\infty}\mathbf{E}[\varsigma_{N,t_{N}}^{\mathrm{RW}}\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}]>0
.

Comparison with the volume of the random walk.

Instead of the capacity, it is natural to study the volume of the random walk conditioned to stay in
B_{N}
. It is relatively easy to see that in dimension
d\geq 3
, the relative volume of the simple random walk undergoes a phase transition according to the following criterion: under
\mathbf{P}(\cdot\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})
,

v_{N,t_{N}}^{\mathrm{RW}}\vcentcolon=\frac{|\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}|}{|B_{N}|}\xrightarrow[N\to+\infty]{\mathbb{P}}\begin{cases}0&\quad\text{ if }\ \lim_{N\to\infty}t_{N}N^{-d}=0\,,\\
1&\quad\text{ if }\ \lim_{N\to\infty}t_{N}N^{-d}=+\infty\,.\end{cases}

(1.10)

One way to understand this phase transition is through the coupling between the SRW on the torus and random interlacements, or more closely related the coupling presented in
[
7
]
.

One can observe that for
d\geq 5
, the ratio
\varsigma_{N,t_{N}}^{\mathrm{RW}}
undergoes a phase transition at
t_{N}
of order
N^{d-2}
, while the ratio
v_{N,t_{N}}^{\mathrm{RW}}
undergoes a phase transition at
t_{N}
of order
N^{d}
. One can then make two observations.

First, we stress the existence of a phase
N^{d-2}\ll t_{N}\ll N^{d}
where the walk has negligible volume but full capacity, the same way as random interlacements.

Second, Theorem
1.5
is part of the large collection of results about the capacity of the simple random walk in dimension
d
that are analogs of results about its volume in dimension
d-2
. One can see for example the remarks below
[
3
, Corollary 1.5, Prop 1.6]
as well as the works
[
16
,
11
,
17
]
.

Comparing with independent Bernoulli random variables.

Let us compare Theorem
1.1
to the case where the random interlacement
\mathscr{I}(u)
is replaced by the set
\mathscr{B}(p)\vcentcolon=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},\omega_{x}=1\}
, where
(\omega_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
is a collection of i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables with parameter
p=1-e^{-u}>0
with law also denoted by
\mathbb{P}
.
In the spirit of Remark
1.4
, the question is to understand whether the “holes” are more visible for the random interlacement than for the Bernoulli random variables.

Adapting the proof of Theorem
1.1
gives the following result.

Proposition 1.8
.

Let
d\geq 3
and let
(p_{N})_{N\geq 1}
be a sequence of positive numbers and write
p_{N}=1-e^{-u_{N}}
.
For any compact regular set
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have the following convergences in
\mathbb{P}
-probability:

\kappa_{N,p_{N}}=\kappa_{N,p_{N}}(D)\vcentcolon=\frac{\mathrm{cap}(\mathscr{B}(p_{N})\cap D_{N})}{\mathrm{cap}(D_{N})}\xrightarrow[N\to+\infty]{}\begin{cases}0&\quad\text{ if }\ \lim_{N\to\infty}u_{N}N^{2}=0\,,\\
1&\quad\text{ if }\ \lim_{N\to\infty}u_{N}N^{2}=+\infty\,.\end{cases}

(1.11)

Moreover, if
0<\liminf\limits_{N\to\infty}u_{N}N^{2}\leq\limsup\limits_{N\to\infty}u_{N}N^{2}<+\infty
, then

0<\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\kappa_{N,p_{N}}]\leq\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\kappa_{N,p_{N}}]<1\,.

(1.12)

We therefore notice that in dimension
d=3
and
d=4
, the threshold on
(p_{N})_{N\geq 1}
for the ratio
\kappa_{N,p_{N}}
to vanish is much smaller than for
\varsigma^{\mathrm{RI}}_{N,p_{N}}
. This means that the “holes” of the random interlacement make the capacity
\mathrm{cap}(\mathscr{I}(p)\cap D_{N})
(much) smaller than
\mathrm{cap}(\mathscr{B}(p)\cap D_{N})
in dimension
d=3,4
.
The threshold is the same in dimension
d\geq 5
, but an interesting question would be to determine whether in general we have
\mathbb{E}[\varsigma^{\mathrm{RI}}_{N,u_{N}}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\kappa_{N,p_{N}}]
, or if
\mathbb{E}[\varsigma^{\mathrm{RI}}_{N,u_{N}}]
is much smaller than
\mathbb{E}[\kappa_{N,p_{N}}]
in the regime
u_{N}N^{2}\to 0
.

1.2
A motivation from the folding of random walks

One of our main motivations is the study of folding phenomena for random walks; this arises for instance when considering a random walk among obstacles.
More precisely, consider a random walk
(S_{n})_{n\geq 0}
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, which evolves in a (random) set of obstacles
\mathscr{O}
, with law denoted
\mathbb{P}
. The random walk is killed when hitting
\mathscr{O}
, so the survival time is
\tau\vcentcolon=\min\{i\geq 0,S_{i}\in\mathscr{O}\}
.
One can then study the probability of surviving for a long time and the properties of the random walk when conditioned on surviving for a long time: one may either consider a
quenched
setting, that is fixing a realization of
\mathscr{O}
, or an
annealed
setting, that is averaging over realizations of
\mathscr{O}
.
The quenched survival probability is
\mathbf{P}(\tau>n)=\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{O}\cap\mathcal{R}_{n}=\varnothing)
, where
\mathcal{R}_{n}=\{S_{0},\ldots,S_{n}\}
is the range of the simple random walk up to time
n
; the annealed probability is
\mathbb{P}\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{O}\cap\mathcal{R}_{n}=\varnothing)
.

There are (at least) two natural sets of obstacles that one could consider:
\mathscr{O}=\mathscr{B}(p)
, a set of independent Bernoulli obstacles of parameter
p\vcentcolon=1-e^{-u}
, or
\mathscr{O}=\mathscr{I}(u)
, a random interlacment of parameter
u>0
.
Then, the annealed probabilities of survival are respectively

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{B}(p)\cap\mathcal{R}_{n}=\varnothing)\big]=\mathbf{E}\Big[e^{-u|\mathcal{R}_{n}|}\Big]\,,\qquad\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{I}(u)\cap\mathcal{R}_{n}=\varnothing)\big]=\mathbf{E}\Big[e^{-u\mathrm{cap}(\mathcal{R}_{n})}\Big]\,,

(1.13)

using (
1.1
) in the case of interlacements.
Both survival probabilities can also be interpreted as partition functions of a Gibbs measure penalizing a random walk
(S_{i})_{0\leq i\leq n}
by its volume, resp. by its capacity.
Studying these annealed probabilities then amounts to obtaining large deviation principles for the volume, resp. the capacity, of the range
\mathcal{R}_{n}
of a random walk.

Random walk penalized by its volume.

The first model of (
1.13
),
i.e.
the random walk penalized by the size of its range, is by now well-understood, starting from the seminal work of Donsker and Varadhan
[
14
]
.
It is now known that the random walk folds into a ball of radius
\rho_{n}\vcentcolon=c_{d}(n/\beta)^{1/(d+2)}
and fills it, see
[
12
,
5
]
for precise statements.

Let us briefly present the heuristic for determining the radius
\rho_{n}
.
The probability that a random walk remains up to time
n
in a ball of radius
r
is approximately
\exp(-\lambda_{d}n/r^{2})
(with
\lambda_{d}
the principal eigenvalue of the Laplacian in the unit ball). On the other hand, the volume of that ball is
\pi_{d}r^{d}
(with
\pi_{d}
the volume of the unit ball).
Optimizing over the entropic cost
\lambda_{d}n/r^{2}
and the volume penalty
\beta\pi_{d}r^{d}
gives the correct radius
\rho_{n}\vcentcolon=c_{d}(n/\beta)^{1/(d+2)}
of the ball that the random walk effectively folds into.

Let us mention that the literature is mostly concerned with the case of a constant parameter
\beta>0
, but Donsker and Varadhan’s argument should also be valid if
\beta=\beta_{n}\downarrow 0
, as long as
\rho_{n}\ll n^{1/d}
,
i.e.

\beta_{n}\gg n^{-2/d}
.
Indeed, a random walk constrained to remain in a ball of radius
\rho_{n}
will fill almost completely this ball as long as its volume is much smaller than
n
, that is as long as
\rho_{n}\ll n^{1/d}
.
The case when
\beta_{n}
is of order
n^{1/d}
actually falls into the large deviation regime studied in
[
31
]
, known as the
Swiss cheese
picture: the random walk will stay on a scale
n^{1/d}
, leaving large holes in its range.

Random walk penalized by its capacity.

As far as the second model in (
1.13
) is concerned,
i.e.
the random walk penalized by the capacity of its range, we are not aware of any result.
The large deviations results obtained in
[
1
]
for
\mathrm{cap}(\mathcal{R}_{n})
do not give a sharp large deviation principle and actually do not treat the scale needed to estimate the right-hand side of (
1.13
).

It is however believed that also in that case, the random walk should fold into a ball of radius
\rho_{n}
.
One can repeat the heuristic argument from the above section, replacing the volume of the ball of radius
r
by its capacity
\varpi_{d}r^{d-2}
(with
\varpi_{d}
the capacity of the unit ball).
Optimizing over the entropic cost
\lambda_{d}n/r^{2}
and the capacity penalty
u\varpi_{d}r^{d-2}
gives the correct radius
\rho_{n}\vcentcolon=c^{\prime}_{d}(n/u)^{1/d}
of the ball that the random walk should effectively fold into.

Let us stress that here, for a fixed
u
, the walk constrained to stay in a ball of radius
n^{1/d}
should not fill completely that ball, leaving some holes; however its capacity should still be very close to the capacity of the ball.
In the same spirit as in the previous paragraph, the heuristic argument should hold as long as the capacity of a random walk constrained to stay in a ball of radius
\rho_{n}
is equivalent to the capacity of the ball. According to Theorem
1.5
this should hold as long as
\rho_{n}\ll\varrho_{n}
with

\varrho_{n}\vcentcolon=\begin{cases}N&\text{ if }d=3\,,\\
(n/\log n)^{1/2}&\text{ if }d=4\,,\\
n^{1/(d-2)}&\text{ if }d\geq 5\,.\end{cases}

(1.14)

The argument breaks down when
\rho_{n}
is of order
\varrho_{n}
,
i.e.
when holes appears in the range of the random walk (from the point of view of the capacity, see Remark
1.4
).
The corresponding large deviation results for
\mathrm{cap}\big(\mathcal{R}_{n}\big)
falls into the scope of the work (in progress)
[
30
]
, which describes an
Italian spaghetti
picture: in dimension
d\geq 5
, the random walk should stay on a scale
n^{1/(d-2)}
, leaving “holes for the capacity”.

1.3
Some other related works

The intersection
\mathscr{I}(u)\cap D_{N}
of the interlacement with a blow-up of a compact has already been studied in the literature, see
[
22
,
28
]
.
Let us mention in particular that, for a fixed parameter
u>0
, large deviation have been obtained for its volume
|\mathscr{I}(u)\cap D_{N}|
and of its capacity
\mathrm{cap}(\mathscr{I}(u)\cap D_{N})
, in particular in order to study probabilities of disconnection events by the random interlacement, see for instance
[
27
]
.

As far as the volume
|\mathscr{I}(u)\cap D_{N}|
is concerned,
[
28
]
obtains the following large deviation result for the covering ratio

\nu_{N,u}\vcentcolon=\frac{|\mathscr{I}(u)\cap D_{N}|}{|D_{N}|}\,.

Recall that
\vartheta(u)\vcentcolon=1-e^{-u/g_{0}}
is defined in (
1.8
). Then for any
1>\nu>\vartheta(u)
,
[
28
, Theorem 6.1]
obtains

\lim_{N\to+\infty}\frac{1}{N^{d-2}}\mathbb{P}\left(\nu_{N,u}>\nu\right)=-\inf\left\{\frac{1}{2d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}\,:\,\varphi\in\mathscr{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\int_{D}\vartheta\big((\sqrt{u}+\varphi(z))^{2}\big)\,\mathrm{d}z>\nu|D|\right\}\,.

For the capacity
\mathrm{cap}(\mathscr{I}(u)\cap D_{N})
,
[
23
]
obtained a large deviation principle for the ratio
\varsigma^{\mathrm{RI}}_{N,u}
defined in (
1.3
), in the case where
D
is the unit ball of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
: for any
u>0
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
,

\lim_{N\to+\infty}\frac{1}{N^{d-2}}\mathbb{P}\left(\varsigma^{\mathrm{RI}}_{N,u}<\lambda\right)=-\frac{u}{d}\inf_{A\in\Sigma_{\lambda}}\mathrm{cap}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(D\setminus A\right)\,,

where
\Sigma_{\lambda}\vcentcolon=\{A\subset D\,:\,\mathrm{cap}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(A\right)\leq\lambda\mathrm{cap}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(D\right),\text{ $A$ is a finite union of boxes}\}
.
In particular, it shows that for a fixed
u>0
, the ratio of the capacities
\varsigma^{\mathrm{RI}}_{N,u}
goes to
1
in
\mathbb{P}
-probability. In the present paper (that is Theorem
1.1
) we tackle a somehow different question, asking how small
u
can be to still have a ratio of order
1
.

2
Random interlacements: Proof of Theorem
1.1

2.1
Preliminaries on capacities

Let us start by rewriting the ratio
\varsigma_{n,u}
defined in (
1.3
).
Recalling the definition (
1.2
), we define for
K\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
the equilibrium measure of
K
as

e_{K}(x)\vcentcolon=\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{x\in K\right\}}\mathbf{P}_{x}(H_{K}=+\infty)\,,

so in particular
\mathrm{cap}(K)=e_{K}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})=e_{K}(\partial K)
.
Define also the normalized equilibrium measure of
K
, or harmonic measure with respect to
K
, as
\bar{e}_{K}\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{\mathrm{cap}(K)}e_{K}
, which is supported on the (interior) boundary of
K
.

If we denote
g(x,y)\vcentcolon=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\mathbf{P}_{x}\left(S_{k}=y\right)
the Green function of the random walk, the equilibrium measure (and the capacity) is linked to
g(x,y)
through the last exit decomposition (see e.g.
[
15
, Lem. 2.12]
):

\mathbf{P}_{z}\left(H_{K}<+\infty\right)=\sum_{y\in K}g(z,x)e_{K}(x)\,.

(2.1)

In turn, we also have another characterization of the capacity of a set
K
, as the properly normalized probability that
K
is hit by a simple random walk starting from far away.
The harmonic measure
\bar{e}_{K}
can then be interpreted as the distribution of the hitting point
S_{H_{K}}
for a simple random walk starting from far away, conditioned to hit
K
. More precisely, we have

\mathrm{cap}(K)=\lim_{|z|\to\infty}\frac{1}{g(0,z)}\mathbf{P}_{z}\big(H_{K}<+\infty\big)\,,\quad\bar{e}_{K}(x)=\lim_{|z|\to\infty}\mathbf{P}_{z}\big(S_{H_{K}}=x\mid H_{K}<+\infty\big)\,.

(2.2)

One can find the proof of these results in
[
20
, Section 6.5]
.

Our starting point for the rest of the proof will be the following formula for the difference of two capacities: if
A\subseteq B\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
\mathrm{cap}(B)>0
, then we have

\mathrm{cap}\left(B\right)-\mathrm{cap}\left(A\right)=\mathrm{cap}\left(B\right)\sum_{x\in B}\bar{e}_{B}(x)\mathbf{P}_{x}\big(H_{A}=+\infty\big)\,.

(2.3)

Indeed, starting from (
2.2
), we get that

\begin{split}\mathrm{cap}\left(B\right)-\mathrm{cap}\left(A\right)&=\lim_{|z|\to\infty}\frac{1}{g(0,z)}\big(\mathbf{P}_{z}(H_{B}<+\infty)-\mathbf{P}_{z}(H_{A}<+\infty)\big)\\
&=\lim_{|z|\to\infty}\frac{1}{g(0,z)}\mathbf{P}_{z}(H_{B}<+\infty)\mathbf{P}_{z}(H_{A}=+\infty\mid H_{B}<+\infty)\,,\end{split}

where we have used that
A\subseteq B
to get that
\mathbf{P}_{y}(H_{B}<+\infty)-\mathbf{P}_{y}(H_{A}<+\infty)=\mathbf{P}_{y}(H_{B}<+\infty,H_{A}=+\infty)
.
Decomposing the last probability as

\mathbf{P}_{z}(H_{A}=+\infty\mid H_{B}<+\infty)=\sum_{x\in B}\mathbf{P}_{z}(S_{H_{B}}=x\mid H_{B}<+\infty)\mathbf{P}_{x}(H_{A}=+\infty)\,,

we deduce (
2.3
) from (
2.2
).

2.2
Main argument and key proposition

Let us write
\bar{e}_{N}=\bar{e}_{D_{N}}
for simplicity, then (
2.3
) yields

1-\varsigma^{\mathrm{RI}}_{N,u_{N}}=\sum_{x\in D_{N}}\bar{e}_{N}(x)\mathbf{P}_{x}\big(H_{\mathscr{I}(u_{N})\cap D_{N}}=+\infty\big)=\sum_{x\in D_{N}}\bar{e}_{N}(x)\mathbf{P}_{x}\big(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap\mathscr{I}(u_{N})\cap D_{N}=\varnothing\big)\,,

where
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}=\{S_{0},S_{1},S_{2},\ldots\}
is the range of the random walk
(S_{n})_{n\geq 0}
in infinite time-horizon.
Since
\varsigma^{\mathrm{RI}}_{N,u_{N}}\in[0,1]
, in order to prove (
1.5
), we only need to control

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[1-\varsigma^{\mathrm{RI}}_{N,u_{N}}\right]&=\sum_{x\in D_{N}}\bar{e}_{N}(x)\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{P}_{x}\big(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap\mathscr{I}(u_{N})\cap D_{N}=\varnothing\big)\right]\\
&=\sum_{x\in D_{N}}\bar{e}_{N}(x)\mathbf{E}_{x}\left[e^{-u_{N}\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap D_{N}\right)}\right]\,,\end{split}

(2.4)

where we have used the characterization (
1.1
) of the law of the random interlacement.

Then, we use the following key result, which estimates the capacity
\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap D_{N}\right)
when the random walk starts from a point drawn from
\bar{e}_{N}
.
Recall the definition (
1.4
) of
\Theta_{N}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For any
\eta>0
, there is some
\delta(\eta)
with
\lim_{\eta\downarrow 0}\delta(\eta)=0
such that

\limsup_{N\to+\infty}\sum_{x\in D_{N}}\bar{e}_{N}(x)\mathbf{P}_{x}\left(\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap D_{N}\right)\not\in[\eta,\tfrac{1}{\eta}]\Theta_{N}\right)\leq\delta(\eta)\,.

This proposition allows us to conclude the proof of Theorem
2.2
.
Indeed, we have the following bound:

\mathbf{E}_{x}\left[e^{-u_{N}\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap D_{N}\right)}\right]\geq e^{-\frac{1}{\eta}u_{N}\Theta_{N}}\mathbf{P}_{x}\big(\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap D_{N}\right)<\tfrac{1}{\eta}\Theta_{N}\big)\,.

If we let
\ell_{1}\vcentcolon=\limsup_{n\to\infty}u_{N}\Theta_{N}
then thanks to Proposition
2.1
,
we get that for any
\eta>0
,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[1-\varsigma^{\mathrm{RI}}_{N,u_{N}}\right]\geq e^{-\frac{1}{\eta}\ell_{1}}(1-\delta(\eta))\,,

or in other words

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\varsigma^{\mathrm{RI}}_{N,u_{N}}\right]\leq 1-e^{-\frac{1}{\eta}\ell_{1}}(1-\delta(\eta))\,.

In particular, if
\ell_{1}=\lim_{N\to\infty}u_{N}\Theta_{N}=0
, then since
\delta(\eta)
can be made arbitrarily small, this shows that
\mathbb{E}\left[\varsigma^{\mathrm{RI}}_{N,u_{N}}\right]
goes to
0
as
N\to\infty
,
i.e.
the second line in (
1.5
).
Otherwise, it proves the upper bound in (
1.6
).

For the other bound, we use a similar idea:

\begin{split}\mathbf{E}_{x}\left[e^{-u_{N}\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap D_{N}\right)}\right]&\leq e^{-\eta u_{N}\Theta_{N}}\mathbf{P}_{x}\big(\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap D_{N}\right)>\eta\Theta_{N}\big)+\mathbf{P}_{x}\big(\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap D_{N}\right)\leq\eta\Theta_{N}\big)\\
&=1-\mathbf{P}_{x}\big(\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap D_{N}\right)>\eta\Theta_{N}\big)(1-e^{-\eta u_{N}\Theta_{N}})\,.\end{split}

If we let
\ell_{2}\vcentcolon=\liminf_{N\to\infty}u_{N}\Theta_{N}
, then thanks to Lemma
2.1
we get that for any
\eta>0
,

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[1-\varsigma^{\mathrm{RI}}_{N,u_{N}}\right]\leq 1-(1-\delta(\eta))(1-e^{-\eta\ell_{2}})\,,

or put otherwise

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\varsigma^{\mathrm{RI}}_{N,u_{N}}\right]\geq(1-\delta(\eta))(1-e^{-\eta\ell_{2}})\,.

In particular, if
\ell_{2}=\lim_{N\to\infty}u_{N}\Theta_{N}=+\infty
, then since
\delta(\eta)
is arbitrarily small we obtain that
\mathbb{E}\left[\varsigma^{\mathrm{RI}}_{N,u_{N}}\right]
goes to
1
, which proves the first line in (
1.5
).
Otherwise, it proves the lower bound in (
1.6
).
∎

2.3
Estimates on
\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap D_{N}\right)
: proof of Proposition
2.1

Before we prove Proposition
2.1
, let us collect useful results on the capacity of the range of the simple random walk up to time
n
, that we denote
\mathcal{R}_{n}\vcentcolon=\{S_{0},S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}\}
.
In particular, the law of large numbers for
\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{n}\right)
has been proved in dimension
d\geq 5
in
[
18
]
(see also
[
2
]
for central limit theorems) and in dimension
d=4
by
[
3
]
; in dimension
d=3
, the convergence in distribution of the rescaled
\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{n}\right)
has been proven in
[
9
]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

In dimension
d\geq 4
, we have the following law of large numbers: there is
\alpha_{d}>0
such that
\mathbf{P}
-almost surely

\begin{split}\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{n}\right)&\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}\alpha_{d}\quad\text{ if }d\geq 5\,,\\
\frac{\log n}{n}\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{n}\right)&\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}\quad\text{ if }d=4\,.\end{split}

(2.5)

In dimension
d=3
, we have the following convergence in distribution:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{n}\right)\xrightarrow[t\to+\infty]{(d)}\frac{1}{3\sqrt{3}}\,\mathrm{cap}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(W_{[0,1]}\right)=\vcentcolon\mathscr{C}_{W}\,,

(2.6)

where
W_{[0,1]}
is the trace of a three-dimensional Brownian motion up to time
1
.

2.3.1
Probability that
\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap D_{N}\right)
is large

We now prove the first part of Proposition
2.3
.
First of all, since
D
is compact, we can find some
r>0
such that
D_{N}\leq B_{rN}
, where
B_{rN}
is a ball of radius
rN
in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
; only to simplify notation, let us assume that
r=1
.

Therefore, we have that
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap D_{N}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap B_{N}
, and we will bound

\sup_{x\in B_{N}}\mathbf{P}_{x}\big(\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap B_{N}\right)\geq\tfrac{1}{\eta}\Theta_{N}\big)\,.

By rotational invariance, we only need to teat the case of a generic
x\in B_{N}
, without worrying about the supremum.
For this, we use a last exit decomposition: letting
L_{N}\vcentcolon=\sup\{i\geq 0,S_{i}\in B_{N}\}
the time of last visit to the ball
B_{N}
, using that
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap B_{N}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{L_{N}}
, we have

\mathbf{P}_{x}\big(\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap B_{N}\right)\geq\tfrac{1}{\eta}\Theta_{N}\big)\leq\mathbf{P}_{x}\big(\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{AN^{2}}\right)\geq\tfrac{1}{\eta}\Theta_{N}\big)+\mathbf{P}_{x}\big(L_{N}>AN^{2}\big)\,,

where
A=A(\eta)>1
is some fixed parameter.

For the first term, by the law of large numbers for the capacity, recalling the definition (
1.4
) of
\Theta_{N}
, we have that:

—

In dimension
d\geq 4
, if
A=c_{d}/\eta
with
c_{d}>1/\alpha_{d}
if
d\geq 5
and
c_{d}>16/\pi^{2}
if
d=4
, then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbf{P}_{x}\big(\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{AN^{2}}\right)\geq\tfrac{1}{\eta}\Theta_{N}\big)=0\,.

—

In dimension
d=3
, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbf{P}_{x}\big(\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{AN^{2}}\right)\geq\tfrac{1}{\eta}\Theta_{N}\big)=\mathbf{P}\big(\mathscr{C}_{W}\geq\tfrac{1}{\eta\sqrt{A}}\big)\,.

It remains to control the other term. By Donsker’s invariance principle, we have that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbf{P}_{x}\big(L_{N}>AN^{2}\big)\leq\mathbf{P}_{0}\Big(\inf_{t>\sqrt{A}}\|W_{t}\|\leq\sqrt{d}\Big)\,,

where
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion (the factor
\sqrt{d}
comes from the variance of the simple random walk);
\mathbf{P}_{x}
also denotes it law, with starting point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
In the above, we have also used that the probability
\mathbf{P}_{x}(L_{N}>AN^{2})
is maximized at
x=0
.
Now, if we let
\tau_{r}\vcentcolon=\inf\{s>0,\|W_{s}\|=r\}
be the hitting time of the sphere of radius
r
, we get that for any
r>1

\mathbf{P}_{0}\Big(\inf_{t>\sqrt{A}}\|W_{t}\|\leq\sqrt{d}\Big)\leq\mathbf{P}_{0}\big(\tau_{r}<\sqrt{A}/2\big)\sup_{|z|=r}\mathbf{P}_{z}\big(\tau_{\sqrt{d}}<+\infty\big)+\mathbf{P}_{0}\big(\tau_{r}>\sqrt{A}/2\big)\,.

Now, by standard results on Brownian motions, for
|z|=r
we have that
\mathbf{P}_{z}\big(\tau_{1}<+\infty\big)=(r/\sqrt{d})^{2-d}
in dimension
d\geq 3
.
Using that the hitting time of
1
is larger for the one-dimensional Brownian, we get thanks to the reflection principle that

\mathbf{P}_{0}\big(\tau_{r}\geq\sqrt{A}/2\big)=\mathbf{P}_{0}\big(\tau_{1}\geq\sqrt{A}/(2\sqrt{r})\big)\leq 2\mathbf{P}\big(Z\geq\sqrt{A}/(2\sqrt{r})\big)\,,

where
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
All together, we obtain that

\mathbf{P}_{0}\Big(\inf_{t>\sqrt{A}}\|W_{t}\|\leq\sqrt{d}\Big)\leq\inf_{r>1}\Big\{(r/\sqrt{d})^{2-d}+2e^{-\frac{A}{r}}\Big\}\leq C(A/\log A)^{2-d}\,.

We have therefore shown the following.
In dimension
d\geq 4
, choosing
A=c_{d}/\eta
with a sufficiently large constant
c_{d}
,

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sup_{x\in B_{N}}\mathbf{P}_{x}\big(\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap B_{N}\right)\geq\tfrac{1}{\eta}\Theta_{N}\big)\leq C^{\prime}\big(\eta\log\tfrac{1}{\eta}\big)^{d-2}\,.

In dimension
d=3
, using that
\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{C}_{W}>\frac{1}{\eta\sqrt{A}})\leq CA\eta^{2}
since
\mathscr{C}_{W}
has a finite second moment (see
[
9
, Rem. 4.1]
), we get that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sup_{x\in B_{N}}\mathbf{P}_{x}\big(\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap B_{N}\right)\geq\tfrac{1}{\eta}\Theta_{N}\big)\leq C\inf_{A>0}\Big\{A\eta^{2}+A^{-1}\log A\Big\}\leq C^{\prime}\eta\log\tfrac{1}{\eta}\,,

the optimal being
A\approx\tfrac{1}{\eta}|\log\eta|^{1/2}
, thus concluding the proof of the first part of Proposition
2.1
.

2.3.2
Probability that
\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap D_{N}\right)
is small

For
\varepsilon>0
, let us introduce
D^{\varepsilon}\vcentcolon=\{x\in D,d(x,D^{c})\geq\varepsilon\}
and
D_{N}^{\varepsilon}\vcentcolon=(N\cdot D^{\varepsilon})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Now, if we write
H_{N}^{\varepsilon}\vcentcolon=H_{D_{N}^{\varepsilon}}=\min\{i\,:\,S_{i}\in D_{N}^{\varepsilon}\}
, we have that

\mathbf{P}_{x}\big(\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap D_{N}\right)\leq\eta\Theta_{N}\big)\leq\mathbf{P}_{x}\big(H_{N}^{\varepsilon}=+\infty\big)+\mathbf{E}_{x}\Big[\mathbf{P}_{S_{H_{N}^{\varepsilon}}}\big(\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap D_{N}\right)\leq\eta\Theta_{N}\big)\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{H_{N}^{\varepsilon}<+\infty\right\}}\Big]\,.

Now, since the ball centered at
S_{H_{N}^{\varepsilon}}
and of radius
\varepsilon N
is included in
D_{N}
, we get that

\begin{split}\mathbf{P}_{S_{H_{N}^{\varepsilon}}}\big(\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap D_{N}\right)\leq\eta\Theta_{N}\big)&\leq\mathbf{P}_{0}\big(\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\tau_{\varepsilon N}}\right)\leq\eta\Theta_{N}\big)\\
&\leq\mathbf{P}_{0}\big(\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon^{3}N^{2}}\right)\leq\eta\Theta_{N}\big)+\mathbf{P}_{0}(\tau_{\varepsilon N}\leq\varepsilon^{3}N)\,,\end{split}

where
\tau_{r}\vcentcolon=\min\{i\geq 0,\|S_{i}\|\geq r\}
is the exit time of the ball of radius
r
.

Now, by Donsker’s invariance principle, we have that
\mathbf{P}_{0}(\tau_{r}\leq\varepsilon r^{2})\to\mathbf{P}_{0}(\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\|W_{s}\|\geq\sqrt{d/\varepsilon})
as
r\to\infty
, with
(W_{s})_{s\geq 0}
a standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion.
By the reflection principle, we get as above that this probability is bounded by
2e^{-\varepsilon/2d}
.

Now, using Theorem
2.2
and recalling the definition (
1.4
) of
\Theta_{N}
, we also get that:

—

In dimension
d\geq 4
, if
\varepsilon^{3}=c_{d}\eta
with
c_{d}>1/\alpha_{d}
if
d\geq 5
and
c_{d}>16/\pi^{2}
if
d=4
, then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbf{P}_{0}\big(\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon^{3}N^{2}}\right)\leq\eta\Theta_{N}\big)=0\,.

—

In dimension
d=3
, taking
\varepsilon=\eta^{1/3}
, we get that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbf{P}_{0}\big(\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon^{3}N^{2}}\right)\leq\eta\Theta_{N}\big)=\mathbf{P}\big(\mathscr{C}_{W}\leq\eta^{1/2}\big)\,.

All together, we obtain that for any
\eta>0
, letting
\varepsilon\vcentcolon=c_{d}\eta^{1/3}
for some appropriate constant,

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbf{E}_{x}\Big[\mathbf{P}_{S_{H_{N}^{\varepsilon}}}\big(\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap D_{N}\right)\leq\eta\Theta_{N}\big)\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{H_{N}^{\varepsilon}<+\infty\right\}}\Big]\leq 2e^{-c_{d}^{\prime}\eta^{1/3}}+\mathbf{P}\big(\mathscr{C}_{W}\leq\eta^{1/2}\big)\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{d=3\right\}}\,.

Notice that
\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{C}_{W}>0)=1
, so we get that
\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{C}_{W}\leq\eta^{1/2})
goes to
0
as
\eta
goes to
0
.

It therefore remains to bound

\sum_{x\in D_{N}}\bar{e}_{N}(x)\mathbf{P}_{x}\big(H_{N}^{\varepsilon}=+\infty\big)=\frac{\mathrm{cap}\left(D_{N}\right)-\mathrm{cap}\left(D_{N}^{\varepsilon}\right)}{\mathrm{cap}\left(D_{N}\right)}\,,

where we have used the identity (
2.3
).
Now, we can use that the capacity of the blow-up
D_{N}=(N\cdot D)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is related with the Newtonian capacity of
D
via the following well-known result (see
[
6
, Lem. 2.2]
or
[
21
, Prop. 2.4]
).

Proposition 2.3
.

For any compact regular set
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\lim_{N\to+\infty}N^{2-d}\mathrm{cap}\left(D_{N}\right)=\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{cap}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(D\right)\,.

Therefore, we obtain that choosing
\varepsilon=c_{d}\eta^{1/3}
for some appropriate
c_{d}
,

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbf{P}_{x}\big(\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\cap D_{N}\right)\leq\eta\Theta_{N}\big)\leq\frac{\mathrm{cap}\left(D\right)-\mathrm{cap}\left(D^{\varepsilon}\right)}{\mathrm{cap}\left(D\right)}+2e^{-c_{d}^{\prime}\eta^{1/3}}+\mathbf{P}\big(\mathscr{C}_{W}\leq\eta^{1/2}\big)\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{d=3\right\}}\,.

Since
\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\mathrm{cap}\left(D^{\varepsilon}\right)=\mathrm{cap}\left(D\right)
(see
[
24
, Prop 1.13]
), we get that the right-hand side goes to
0
as
\eta\downarrow 0
, which concludes the proof.
∎

3
Constrained random walk: Proof of Theorem
1.5

3.1
Some preliminaries on confining probabilities

Consider the transition kernel
P_{A}
of the random walk killed on the boundary of a finite connected subset
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. We introduce
(\lambda_{A},\Phi_{A})
the first
\ell^{1}
-normalized eigencouple of
P_{A}
, that is

P_{A}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2d}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{x\sim y;x,y\in A\right\}}\,,\quad P_{A}\Phi_{A}=\lambda_{A}\Phi_{A}\,,\quad\|\Phi_{A}\|=\sum_{z\in A}\Phi_{A}(z)=1\,.

(3.1)

For
j\geq 2
we also write
(\lambda_{A,j},\Phi_{A,j})
for the other ordered eigencouples, meaning that
(\lambda_{A,j})_{j\geq 2}
is non-increasing and
1>\lambda_{A}\geq\lambda_{A,2}
. Note that
[
13
, Lemma A.1]
proves the following bound on the maximal value of
\Phi_{A}
:

\text{There is a universal constant $\kappa>0$ such that}\quad|\Phi_{A}|_{\infty}\leq\kappa(1-\lambda_{A})^{d/2}.

(3.2)

When
A=B_{N}
the discrete Euclidean ball of radius
N
, we will simply write
\lambda_{N},\Phi_{N}
. Let us mention that it is known (see
e.g.

[
32
, (3.27),(6.11)]
) that
\lambda_{N}
satisfies

\lambda_{N}=1-\frac{\lambda}{2d}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\big(1+\bar{\mathcal{O}}(\tfrac{1}{N})\big)\,,

(3.3)

where
\lambda
is the first eigenvalue of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on the unit ball of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

In the proof, we will use the fact that the first eigencouple of the transition matrix restricted to
A
is the main contributor to the probability for the random walk to stay inside
A
for a long time. We will use the following result, which is can be proven similarly to Lemma 3.10 in
[
13
]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a connected open set with smooth boundary, and write
D_{N}=(N\cdot D)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for
N\geq 1
large enough. Then, if
T\geq cN^{2}\log N
with
c
large enough, for all
x\in D_{N}
we have

\mathbf{P}_{x}(\mathcal{R}_{T}\subseteq D_{N})=(\lambda_{D_{N}})^{T}\frac{\Phi_{D_{N}}(x)}{\|\Phi_{D_{N}}^{2}\|}(1+\bar{\mathcal{O}}(N^{-2}))\,.

(3.4)

with
\bar{\mathcal{O}}(N^{-2})
uniform in
x\in D_{N}
and
T\geq cN^{2}\log N
.

We only give a sketch of the proof, as we will not use this exact result: we will restrict ourselves to the case where
D_{N}
is the ball
B_{N}
.

Sketch of proof.

The eigenvalues of the random walk absorbed on
\partial D_{N}
satisfy an expansion similar to (
3.3
). This implies a spectral gap of order
N^{-2}
. By writing the eigen-decomposition of the transition matrix, we use the spectral gap to show that the main contribution to the probability comes from
(\lambda_{D_{N}},\Phi_{D_{N}})
. Note that the assumption
t_{N}\gg N^{2}\log N
is used to absorb the secondary terms in the eigen-decomposition.
∎

In the case where the domain is the Euclidean ball, Lemma
3.1
can be extended to all
T\geq N^{2}
at the cost of a constant.

Lemma 3.2

(
[
20
, Corollary 6.9.6]
)
.

There exist a universal constant
c>0
such that for all
N
large enough and all
T\geq N^{2}
,

\frac{1}{c}\lambda_{N}^{T}\leq\sup_{z\in B_{N}}\mathbf{P}_{z}(\mathcal{R}_{T}\subseteq B_{N})=\mathbf{P}_{0}(\mathcal{R}_{T}\subseteq B_{N})\leq c\lambda_{N}^{T}\,.

(3.5)

In the following, we will also study the case where the domain is an annulus, in which case using the local limit theorem yields a similar upper bound. More precisely, for any
K>0
, there are positive constants
c_{K}>0
and
C>0
such that, for all
T\geq 1
, for all sequences
a_{N}>b_{N}\to+\infty
such that
\limsup\limits_{N\to+\infty}\tfrac{a_{N}}{b_{N}}\leq K<+\infty
, provided
N
large enough we have

\sup_{z\in{B_{a_{N}}\setminus B_{b_{N}}}}\mathbf{P}_{z}\left(\mathcal{R}_{T}\subseteq B_{a_{N}}\setminus B_{b_{N}}\right)\leq C\exp\Big(-c_{K}\frac{T}{(a_{N}-b_{N})^{2}}\Big)\,.

(3.6)

To prove Theorem
1.5
, we will first prove a direct upper bound that goes to zero in the regime
t_{N}\Theta_{N}N^{-d}\to 0
, meaning
t_{N}
“small”. Then, for the lower bound, we use the same strategy as in Section
2.2
by considering the probability for the random walk conditioned to stay in
B_{N}
of avoiding an infinite range. Fixing an
\varepsilon>0
small enough, we get that with high probability, this infinite range leaves a trace
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}^{\varepsilon}
in
B_{N}
of reasonnable size. In the regime
t_{N}\Theta_{N}N^{-d}\to+\infty
, meaning
t_{N}
is “large”, we prove that avoiding
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}^{\varepsilon}
is too costly for the walk under
\mathbf{P}(\cdot\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})
.

3.2
Upper bound on the ratio

In this section, we prove that
\varsigma_{N,t_{N}}^{\mathrm{RW}}\to 0
when
t_{N}N^{-d}\Theta_{N}\to 0
. The strategy is a direct upper bound on the capacity of the range
\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}
conditioned to stay in the ball. This upper bound relies on the fact that in this regime, time
t_{N}
is sufficiently small, and thus the capacity of the walk does not have the time to get big enough.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
d\geq 3
, there are constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that for all
N
large enough we have

\mathbf{E}[\mathrm{cap}(\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}})\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}]\leq c_{1}\frac{t_{N}}{N^{2}}\Theta_{N}+c_{2}\Theta_{N\wedge\sqrt{t_{N}}}\,.

(3.7)

Using Proposition
3.3
, since
\mathrm{cap}\left(B_{N}\right)\geq c_{d}N^{d-2}
we have

\limsup_{N\to+\infty}\mathbf{E}\left[\varsigma_{N,t_{N}}^{\mathrm{RW}}\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}\right]\leq\limsup_{N\to+\infty}\bigg(c_{1}\frac{t_{N}}{N^{d}}\Theta_{N}+c_{2}^{\prime}\frac{\Theta_{N\wedge\sqrt{t_{N}}}}{N^{d-2}}\bigg)\,.

(3.8)

By the assumption
t_{N}\Theta_{N}/N^{d}\to 0
, the first term goes to
0
. For the second term, in dimension
d\geq 4
we use
\Theta_{N\wedge\sqrt{t_{N}}}\leq\Theta_{N}\leq N
which implies
\Theta_{N\wedge\sqrt{t_{N}}}N^{2-d}\to 0
. For
d=3
, the assumption
t_{N}\Theta_{N}N^{-d}\to 0
can be rewritten as
t_{N}N^{-2}\to 0
. In particular we have
\Theta_{N\wedge\sqrt{t_{N}}}N^{2-d}=\Theta_{\sqrt{t_{N}}}N^{-1}=\sqrt{t_{N}}/N\to 0
.
Therefore, in both cases, we get the first part of Theorem
1.5
for
t_{N}\Theta_{N}N^{-d}\to 0
.

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

Provided
t_{N}\geq N^{2}
, we split the range into bits of length
N^{2}
and for
i\in\left\{1,\dots,\lfloor t_{N}N^{-2}\rfloor\right\}
, we write
\mathcal{R}^{(i)}\vcentcolon=\big\{S_{(i-1)N^{2}+1},\dots,S_{iN^{2}}\big\}
. Writing
\bar{t}_{N}\vcentcolon=\lfloor t_{N}/N^{2}\rfloor N^{2}
, by subbadditivity of the capacity,

\mathbf{E}\big[\mathrm{cap}(\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}})\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}\big]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor t_{N}/N^{2}\rfloor}\mathbf{E}\big[\mathrm{cap}\mathcal{R}^{(i)}\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}\big]+\mathbf{E}\big[\mathrm{cap}\mathcal{R}([\bar{t}_{N},t_{N}])\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}\big]\,.

(3.9)

Note that if
t_{N}<N^{2}
, we can write (
3.9
) with an empty sum and we are left to bound
\mathbf{E}\big[\mathrm{cap}\mathcal{R}([\bar{t}_{N},t_{N}])\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}\big]
. Therefore, bounding (
3.9
) also covers the case where
t_{N}<N^{2}
.

We first prove that
\mathbf{E}[\mathrm{cap}\mathcal{R}^{(i)}\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}]\leq c\Theta_{N}
for some
c>0
uniform in
i
. To do so, use the Markov property at times
(i-1)N^{2}
and
iN^{2}
:

\begin{split}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{cap}\big(\mathcal{R}^{(i)}\big)&\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}\right\}}\Big]\\
&=\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\mathcal{R}_{iN^{2}}\subseteq D_{N}\right\}}\mathbf{E}_{S_{iN^{2}}}\big[\mathrm{cap}\big(\mathcal{R}_{N^{2}}\big)\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\mathcal{R}_{N^{2}}\subseteq B_{N}\right\}}\mathbf{P}_{S_{(i+1)N^{2}}}(\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}-iN^{2}}\subseteq B_{N})\big]\Big].\end{split}

(3.10)

Using Lemma
3.2
, we can bound the probability appearing in (
3.10
): there is a
c>0
such that

\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{cap}\big(\mathcal{R}^{(i)}\big)\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}\right\}}\Big]\leq\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\mathcal{R}_{iN^{2}}\subseteq B_{N}\right\}}\mathbf{E}_{S_{iN^{2}}}[\mathrm{cap}\big(\mathcal{R}_{N^{2}}\big)]\Big]c\lambda_{N}^{t_{N}-(i+1)N^{2}}\,,

(3.11)

where we also bounded the second indicator function by
1
.
Since
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathrm{cap}
are invariant by translation,
\mathbf{E}_{S_{iN^{2}}}[\mathrm{cap}\mathcal{R}_{N^{2}}]=\mathbf{E}[\mathrm{cap}\mathcal{R}_{N^{2}}]\leq c\Theta_{N}
by definition of
\Theta_{N}
and Theorem
2.2
. Again with Lemma
3.2
, we finally get

\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{cap}\big(\mathcal{R}^{(i)}\big)\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}\right\}}\Big]\leq c\Theta_{N}\lambda_{N}^{t_{N}-(i+1)N^{2}}\,,

(3.12)

Therefore, we deduce that

\mathbf{E}[\mathrm{cap}\big(\mathcal{R}^{(i)}\big)\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}]\leq c\frac{\lambda_{N}^{t_{N}-N^{2}}\Theta_{N}}{\mathbf{P}_{0}(\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})}\leq c^{\prime}\Theta_{N}\ ,

(3.13)

where we used that
\lambda_{N}^{-N^{2}}
is bounded from above by a constant for
N
large enough (recall (
3.3
)).

Let us now turn to the last term in (
3.9
), on which we use the Markov property in similar fashion as in (
3.10
) to get

\begin{split}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{cap}\big(\mathcal{R}([\bar{t}_{N},t_{N}])\big)\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}\right\}}\Big]&\leq\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\mathcal{R}_{\bar{t}_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}\right\}}\mathbf{E}_{S_{\bar{t}_{N}}}[\mathrm{cap}\big(\mathcal{R}([\bar{t}_{N},t_{N}])\big)]\Big]\\
&\leq c\Theta_{(t_{N}-\bar{t}_{N})^{1/2}}\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{R}_{\bar{t}_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})\,.\end{split}

(3.14)

Lemma
3.2
implies that
\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{R}_{\bar{t}_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})\leq c^{\prime\prime}\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})
for some
c^{\prime\prime}>0
.
In particular, we get
\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{cap}\big(\mathcal{R}([\bar{t}_{N},t_{N}])\big)\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}\right\}}\Big]\leq c^{\prime\prime}\Theta_{(t_{N}-\bar{t}_{N})^{1/2}}
.
We have the immediate bound
\Theta_{(t_{N}-\bar{t}_{N})^{1/2}}\leq\Theta_{N\wedge\sqrt{t_{N}}}
. Combining with (
3.13
) and injecting in (
3.9
), we get the announced result.
∎

3.3
Lower bound for Theorem
1.5

In this section we assume
t_{N}
to be « large », that is
t_{N}N^{-d}\Theta_{N}\to+\infty
, and prove that the constrained walk fills the ball
B_{N}
in the sense of capacity. Similarly to the proof of Section
2.2
, our starting point is identity (
2.3
) applied to
B=B_{N}
and
A
the range up to time
t_{N}
of the confined random walk.

To discriminate between the confined random walk and the infinite range involved in (
2.3
), we write
\mathbf{P}^{1}_{x_{1}}
,
\mathbf{P}^{2}_{x_{2}}
for the laws of two independents random walks on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
starting at points
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. The corresponding ranges
\mathcal{R}^{1},\mathcal{R}^{2}
respectively correspond to the (finite) range of the CRW, and the unconstrained (infinite) range.
Applying identity (
2.3
) with this notation yields

\mathbf{E}^{1}_{0}[1-\varsigma_{t_{N}}^{\mathrm{RW}}\,|\,\mathcal{R}^{1}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}]=\sum_{z\in B_{N}}\bar{e}_{B_{N}}(z)\mathbf{E}^{1}_{0}[\mathbf{P}^{2}_{z}(\mathcal{R}^{2}_{\infty}\cap\mathcal{R}^{1}_{t_{N}}=\varnothing)\,|\,\mathcal{R}^{1}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}]\,.

(3.15)

In order to obtain a lower bound on
\mathbf{E}^{1}_{0}\big[\varsigma_{t_{N}}^{\mathrm{RW}}\,|\,\mathcal{R}^{1}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}\big]
, we therefore seek an upper bound on the expectation in the right-hand side of (
3.15
), that is uniform in
z\in\partial B_{N}
.

We will use the same strategy as for the random interlacement by changing our point of view: note that

\mathbf{E}^{1}_{0}[\mathbf{P}^{2}_{z}(\mathcal{R}^{2}_{\infty}\cap\mathcal{R}^{1}_{t_{N}}=\varnothing)\,|\,\mathcal{R}^{1}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}]=\mathbf{E}^{2}_{z}[\mathbf{P}^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{R}^{2}_{\infty}\cap\mathcal{R}^{1}_{t_{N}}=\varnothing\,|\,\mathcal{R}^{1}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})]\,.

(3.16)

Therefore, instead of evaluating the probability for the infinite range to avoid the constrained walk, we pick an infinite range and evaluate the probability for the contrained walk to avoid this range.

We start by proving that with high
\mathbf{P}^{2}_{z}
-probability,
\mathcal{R}^{2}_{\infty}\cap B_{N}
has a capacity that is at least of order
\Theta_{N}
. This will allow us to consider only the ranges
\mathcal{R}^{2}_{\infty}
which are « easy » to hit for the constrained walk. Afterwards, for such « nice » configurations of
\mathcal{R}^{2}_{\infty}
, we prove it is too costly for the constrained walk to avoid
\mathcal{R}^{2}_{\infty}
for such large time
t_{N}
.

Let us start by restricting ourselves to these « nice » configurations of
\mathcal{R}^{2}_{\infty}
. For
r\in(0,1)
, we write
B_{N}^{r}\vcentcolon=B(0,rN)=\{x\in B_{N},d(x,B_{N}^{c})\geq(1-r)N\}
, and for
\varepsilon>0
we define the stopping time
\tau_{\varepsilon}\vcentcolon=H_{B_{N}^{1-3\varepsilon}}\wedge H_{\partial B_{N}^{1-\varepsilon}}
. Note that when starting from
z\in\partial B_{N}^{1-2\varepsilon}
, the time
\tau_{\varepsilon}
is the exit time of the annulus
B_{N}^{1-\varepsilon}\setminus B_{N}^{1-3\varepsilon}
. Let
\varepsilon,\eta>0
, we then define the event

\mathcal{A}_{N,2}^{\varepsilon,\eta}\vcentcolon=\left\{H^{2}_{B^{1-2\varepsilon}_{N}}<+\infty,\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}^{2}\big([H_{B_{N}^{1-2\varepsilon}},H_{B_{N}^{1-2\varepsilon}}+\tau_{\varepsilon}]\big)\right)\geq\eta\Theta_{\varepsilon N}\right\}\,.

(3.17)

On the event
\mathcal{A}_{N,2}^{\varepsilon,\eta}
, the infinite range
\mathcal{R}^{2}_{\infty}
starting at
\partial B_{N}
reaches
B_{N}^{1-2\varepsilon}
, and its range before exiting the annulus
B_{N}^{1-\varepsilon}\setminus B_{N}^{1-3\varepsilon}
has capacity at least
\eta\Theta_{\varepsilon N}
. On the event
\big\{H^{2}_{B^{1-2\varepsilon}_{N}}<+\infty\big\}
, we write
\mathcal{R}^{2,\varepsilon}_{\infty}\vcentcolon=\mathcal{R}^{2}\big([H^{2}_{B^{1-2\varepsilon}_{N}},H^{2}_{B^{1-2\varepsilon}_{N}}+\tau_{\varepsilon}]\big)
this part of the range.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
d\geq 3
and fix
\varepsilon\in(0,\tfrac{1}{4})
. Then, for any
\eta>0
sufficiently small, there is a
\delta(\varepsilon,\eta)
that goes to
0
as
\eta
and
\varepsilon
go to
0
such that

\limsup_{N\to+\infty}\sum_{z\in B_{N}}\bar{e}_{B_{N}}(z)\mathbf{P}^{2}_{z}\big((\mathcal{A}_{N,2}^{\varepsilon,\eta})^{c}\big)\leq\delta(\varepsilon,\eta)\,.

(3.18)

Proof.

We first get a bound on the probability for
\mathcal{R}^{2}_{\infty}
to avoid
B^{1-2\varepsilon}_{N}
: using the identity (
2.3
),

\sum_{z\in B_{N}}\bar{e}_{B_{N}}(z)\mathbf{P}^{2}_{z}(H^{2}_{B^{1-2\varepsilon}_{N}}=+\infty)=1-\frac{\mathrm{cap}\big(B^{1-2\varepsilon}_{N}\big)}{\mathrm{cap}(B_{N})}\leq c_{1}\varepsilon\,,

(3.19)

for some
c_{1}>0
that is uniform in
N
. Note that on the event
\left\{\tau_{\varepsilon}>\varepsilon^{3}N^{2}\right\}
, we have
\mathrm{cap}(\mathcal{R}^{2}_{\tau_{\varepsilon}})\geq\mathrm{cap}(\mathcal{R}^{2}_{\varepsilon^{3}N^{2}})
. Therefore, for any
w\in\partial B_{N}^{1-2\varepsilon}
, we have

\mathbf{P}^{2}_{w}\Big(\mathrm{cap}(\mathcal{R}^{2}_{\tau_{\varepsilon}})\leq\eta\Theta_{\varepsilon N}\Big)\leq\mathbf{P}^{2}_{w}\Big(\mathrm{cap}(\mathcal{R}^{2}_{\varepsilon^{3}N^{2}})\leq\eta\Theta_{\varepsilon N}\Big)+\mathbf{P}^{2}_{w}(\tau_{\varepsilon}\leq\varepsilon^{3}N^{2})\,.

(3.20)

Define
\tilde{A}^{\varepsilon}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,1-3\varepsilon\leq|y|\leq 1-\varepsilon\}
and write
\tilde{\tau}_{\varepsilon}
for the exit time of
\tilde{A}^{\varepsilon}
for the Brownian motion. With the same arguments as above Proposition
2.3
, we have the following upper bound:

\varlimsup_{N\to+\infty}\sup_{z\in B_{N}}\mathbf{P}^{2}_{z}\Big(\frac{\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}^{2,\varepsilon}_{\infty}\right)}{\Theta_{\varepsilon N}}\leq\eta,H_{B_{N}^{1-2\varepsilon}}<+\infty\Big)\leq\kappa_{d}(\varepsilon\eta)+\sup_{|x|=1-2\varepsilon}\mathbf{P}_{x}(\tilde{\tau}_{\varepsilon}\leq\varepsilon^{3})\,,

(3.21)

with
\kappa_{d}(\eta)\vcentcolon=\mathbf{P}\big(\mathscr{C}_{W}\leq\eta^{1/2}\big)
if
d=3
(with
\mathscr{C}_{W}
as defined in (
2.6
)), and
\kappa_{d}(\eta)=0
if
d\geq 4
. The right-hand side of (
3.21
) goes to
0
as
\eta
and
\varepsilon
go to
0
for the same reasons as in the second part of Section
2.3
. Since

\mathbf{P}^{2}_{z}\big((\mathcal{A}_{N,2}^{\varepsilon,\eta})^{c}\big)\leq\mathbf{P}^{2}_{z}(H^{2}_{B^{1-2\varepsilon}_{N}}=+\infty)+\mathbf{P}^{2}_{z}\Big(\frac{\mathrm{cap}\left(\mathcal{R}^{2}_{\tau_{\varepsilon}}\right)}{\Theta_{\varepsilon N}}\leq\eta,H_{B_{N}^{1-2\varepsilon}}<+\infty\Big)\,,

assembling (
3.19
) and (
3.21
) yields the lemma.
∎

To prove Theorem
1.5
in the case
t_{N}\Theta_{N}/N^{d}\to+\infty
, we will prove the following result.

Proposition 3.5
.

For
d\geq 3
, and any
\varepsilon,\eta>0
, assuming
t_{N}\Theta_{N}/N^{d}\to+\infty
we have

\limsup_{N\to+\infty}\sum_{z\in B_{N}}\bar{e}_{B_{N}}(z)\mathbf{E}^{2}_{z}\Big[\mathbf{P}^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{R}^{2}_{\infty}\cap\mathcal{R}^{1}_{t_{N}}=\varnothing\,|\,\mathcal{R}^{1}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{A}_{N,2}^{\varepsilon,\eta}}\Big]=0\,.

(3.22)

Indeed, assuming Proposition
3.5
, we choose
\varepsilon>0
and
\eta>0
, and recall (
3.15
). Then, combining Lemma
3.4
and Proposition
3.5
, we have

\liminf_{N\to+\infty}\mathbf{E}^{1}_{0}[\varsigma_{t_{N}}^{\mathrm{RW}}\,|\,\mathcal{R}^{1}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}]\geq 1-\delta(\varepsilon,\eta)\,.

(3.23)

Taking
\eta
and
\varepsilon
to zero then yields Theorem
1.5
in the case
t_{N}\Theta_{N}/N^{d}\to+\infty
.

A way to prove Proposition
3.5
is to relate the probability in the expectation to eigenvalues of the transition matrix of the random walk killed when hitting
\partial B_{N}\cup\mathcal{R}^{2}_{\infty}
. However, we are only able to prove this connection in the case where
t_{N}
is at least
CN^{2}\log N
for a constant
C>0
large enough, which does not cover the full regime
t_{N}/N^{2}\to+\infty
if
d=3
.
Therefore, we will need to adopt another strategy for dimension three, which will fully use the fact that for
d\leq 3
, the random walk can easily hit another independent random walk (see
[
19
, Chapter 5]
for example).

3.3.1
Dimension four and higher

On the event
\big\{H^{2}_{B^{1-2\varepsilon}_{N}}<+\infty\big\}
, recall the definition of
\mathcal{R}^{2,\varepsilon}_{\infty}
just above Lemma
3.4
, and write
K=K_{N}^{\varepsilon}
for the connected component of
B_{N}\setminus\mathcal{R}^{2,\varepsilon}_{\infty}
that contains the origin. More simply put,
K_{N}^{\varepsilon}
contains all the points of
B_{N}\setminus\mathcal{R}^{2,\varepsilon}_{\infty}
that are accessible for the random walk started at
0
.
As explained previously, in dimension
d\geq 4
we are interested in the case
t_{N}\gg N^{2}\log N
. This means that the
\mathbf{P}^{1}_{0}(\cdot\,|\,\mathcal{R}^{1}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})
-probability for
\mathcal{R}^{1}_{t_{N}}
to avoid
\mathcal{R}^{2,\varepsilon}_{\infty}
is given by the first eigenvalue of the domain
K_{N}^{\varepsilon}
.

In the following, we will write
\lambda(\Lambda)
to designate the first eigenvalue of the transition matrix
P_{\Lambda}
of the simple random walk killed when exiting
\Lambda
.

Lemma 3.6
.

There is a constant
c>0
such that for
N
large enough and
T\geq c^{\prime}N^{2}\log N
with
c^{\prime}
large enough,

\mathbf{P}_{0}(\mathcal{R}_{T}\subseteq K_{N}^{\varepsilon})\leq c\lambda(K_{N}^{\varepsilon})^{T}\,.

(3.24)

Moreover, there are positive constants
b,b_{\varepsilon},\mu
such that for all
N
large enough,

1-\frac{b_{\varepsilon}}{N^{2}}\leq\lambda(B_{0}^{1-\varepsilon})\leq\lambda(K_{N}^{\varepsilon})\leq\lambda_{N}\leq 1-\frac{b}{N^{2}}\quad,\qquad|\Phi_{K_{N}^{\varepsilon}}|_{\infty}\leq\mu N^{-d}\,.

(3.25)

Proof.

We first prove (
3.25
). The first part follows from
B_{N}^{1-3\varepsilon}\subseteq K_{N}^{\varepsilon}\subseteq B_{N}
and
[
13
, Lemma A.2]
, which is an application of the minmax theorem for eigenvalues and asymptotic expansion for the eigenvalue of a discrete blowup. Combining this with (
3.2
) gives the second part.
Recall that
\Phi_{K}
stands for the first eigenvector of the transition matrix of simple random walk starting from
0
, killed on
\partial K
.
The first part of the lemma is a consequence of the following claim: assuming
T\geq N^{2}\log N
and
N
large enough, we have

\Big|\mathbf{P}_{0}(\mathcal{R}_{T}\subseteq K_{N}^{\varepsilon})-\lambda(K_{N}^{\varepsilon})^{T}\frac{\Phi_{K_{N}^{\varepsilon}}(0)}{\|\Phi_{K_{N}^{\varepsilon}}\|_{2}^{2}}\Big|\leq\lambda(K_{N}^{\varepsilon})^{T}|K_{N}^{\varepsilon}|e^{-c\frac{T}{N^{2}}}\,.

(3.26)

This claim follows from the same proof as in
[
13
, Lemma 3.10]
thanks to (
3.25
) that implies a spectral gap of order
N^{-2}
for
K_{N}^{\varepsilon}
(see
[
13
, Lemma A.2]
).

Notice that combining
|\Phi_{K_{N}^{\varepsilon}}|_{\infty}\leq\mu N^{-d}
with
\|\Phi_{K_{N}^{\varepsilon}}\|=1
implies that there are at least
c_{1}N^{d}
points
z
in
K_{N}^{\varepsilon}
such that
\Phi_{K_{N}^{\varepsilon}}(z)\geq c_{2}N^{-d}
, where
c_{1},c_{2}
are some small constants, independent from
N
large enough. In particular, for any configuration of
K_{N}^{\varepsilon}
,

\frac{\Phi_{K_{N}^{\varepsilon}}(0)}{\|\Phi_{K_{N}^{\varepsilon}}\|_{2}^{2}}\leq\mu N^{-d}\bigg[\sum_{z\in K_{N}^{\varepsilon}}\Phi_{K_{N}^{\varepsilon}}^{2}(z)\mathbbm{1}_{\big\{\Phi_{K_{N}^{\varepsilon}}(z)\geq c_{2}N^{-d}\big\}}\bigg]^{-1}\leq\frac{\mu N^{-d}}{c_{2}^{2}N^{-2d}c_{1}N^{d}}=\frac{\mu}{c_{1}c_{2}^{2}}\,.

Assuming
T\geq cN^{2}\log N
with
c
and
N
large enough to have
|K_{N}^{\varepsilon}|e^{-c\frac{T}{N^{2}}}\leq 1
, (
3.24
) implies

\mathbf{P}_{0}(\mathcal{R}_{T}\subseteq K_{N}^{\varepsilon})\leq 2\lambda(K_{N}^{\varepsilon})^{T}\frac{\Phi_{K_{N}^{\varepsilon}}(0)}{\|\Phi_{K_{N}^{\varepsilon}}^{2}\|}\leq\frac{2\mu}{c_{1}c_{2}^{2}}\lambda(K_{N}^{\varepsilon})^{T}\,,

thus proving (
3.24
) which concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

For any
\varepsilon,\eta
small enough, there exist positive constants
c,C
such that for all
N
large enough, all
z\in\partial B_{N}
,

\mathbf{E}^{2}_{z}[\mathbf{P}^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq K_{N}^{\varepsilon}\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{A}_{N,2}^{\varepsilon,\eta}}]\leq C\mathbf{E}^{2}_{z}\left[\exp\big(-ct_{N}(\lambda_{N}-\lambda(K_{N}^{\varepsilon}))\big)\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{A}_{N,2}^{\varepsilon,\eta}}\right]\,.

(3.27)

Proof.

Note that using Lemmas
3.2
&
3.6
(recall
t_{N}\gg N^{2}\log N
), we get that for
n
large enough,

\mathbf{P}^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq K_{N}^{\varepsilon}\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})=\frac{\mathbf{P}^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq K_{N}^{\varepsilon})}{\mathbf{P}^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})}\leq c_{1}\bigg(\frac{\lambda(K_{N}^{\varepsilon})}{\lambda_{N}}\bigg)^{t_{N}}\,.

(3.28)

Using (
3.25
), we know that both
1-\lambda_{N}
and
1-\lambda(K_{N}^{\varepsilon})
are of order
\asymp N^{-2}
. Therefore, a Taylor expansion of the logarithm implies that

\bigg(\frac{\lambda(K_{N}^{\varepsilon})}{\lambda_{N}}\bigg)^{t_{N}}=\exp\left(t_{N}[\log\lambda_{N}-\log\lambda(K_{N}^{\varepsilon})]\right)\leq\exp\left(-ct_{N}(\lambda_{N}-\lambda(K_{N}^{\varepsilon}))\right)\,,

for some constant
c>0
that is uniform in
N
large enough. Since this holds true for any configuration of the range
\mathcal{R}^{2}_{\infty}
, we can take the expectation
\mathbf{E}^{2}_{z}\big[\,(\,\cdot\,)\,\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{A}_{N,2}^{\varepsilon,\eta}}\big]
on both sides.
∎

3.3.2
Dimension three

As mentionned previously, in dimension three we want to take
t_{N}/N^{2}\to+\infty
thus we are not able to use Lemma
3.7
. What we gain instead is that hitting a given set is easier than in higher dimensions. Fixing
\delta\in(0,\varepsilon)
, the strategy of the proof consists in considering excursions of the conditioned walk from
B_{N}^{1-3\varepsilon}
to
B_{N}^{1-\delta}
. We prove in Lemma
3.9
that each of these excursions has a positive probability of hitting
\mathcal{R}^{2,\varepsilon}_{\infty}
. By the Markov property, these excursions are independent and therefore the probability for
\mathcal{R}^{1}_{t_{N}}
to avoid
\mathcal{R}^{2,\varepsilon}_{\infty}
is bounded by the product of probabilities. Lemma
3.10
then proves that provided
t_{N}\gg N^{2}
, this product goes to
0
as
N\to+\infty
, hence proving the theorem.

In this section, on the event
\mathcal{A}_{N,2}^{\varepsilon,\eta}
, we fix a realization of the range
\mathcal{R}^{2,\varepsilon}_{\infty}
, which is thus treated as any other subsets of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with capacity at least
\Theta_{\varepsilon N}
. In particular, hitting times refer to the other walk (with range
\mathcal{R}^{1}_{t_{N}}
). To lighten notation, we drop the «
1
» for the conditioned walk.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
d=3
and recall that
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}^{2,\varepsilon}\subseteq B_{N}^{1-\varepsilon}\setminus B_{N}^{1-3\varepsilon}
. Consider
\delta\in(0,\varepsilon)
. There is a constant
c_{0}=c_{0}(\varepsilon,\eta,\delta)>0
such that uniformly in
N
large enough, on the event
\mathcal{A}_{N,2}^{\varepsilon,\eta}
we have

\inf_{x\in B_{N}^{1-3\varepsilon}}\mathbf{P}_{x}\Big(H_{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}^{2,\varepsilon}}<H_{\partial B_{N}^{1-\delta}}\Big)\geq c_{0}\,.

(3.38)

Proof.

For
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, write
G_{A}
for the Green’s function of the simple random walk absorbed on the interior boundary
\partial A
, that is
G_{A}(x,y)=\mathbf{E}_{x}\big[\sum_{i=0}^{H_{\partial A}}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{S_{i}=y\right\}}\big]
for
x,y\in A
. We use a last exit decomposition for the random walk killed on
\partial B_{N}^{1-\varepsilon}
:

\mathbf{P}_{x}\Big(H_{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}^{2,\varepsilon}}<H_{\partial B_{N}^{1-\delta}}\Big)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{R}_{\infty}^{2,\varepsilon}}G_{B_{N}^{1-\delta}}(x,y)\mathbf{P}_{y}(H_{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}^{2,\varepsilon}}>H_{\partial B_{N}^{1-\delta}})\,.

(3.39)

Now, we claim that there is a constant
c_{\varepsilon}>0
such that for all
x\in B_{N}^{1-3\varepsilon}
, all
y\in\mathcal{R}_{\infty}^{2,\varepsilon}
, we have
G_{B_{N}^{1-\delta}}(x,y)\geq c_{\delta}/N
for some
c_{\delta}>0
uniform in
N
large enough. The proof can be found in the Appendix, see Lemma
3.14
.

Since avoiding
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}^{2,\varepsilon}
before exiting
B_{N}^{1-\delta}
can be done by avoiding it forever, we deduce that

\mathbf{P}_{x}\Big(H_{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}^{2,\varepsilon}}<H_{\partial B_{N}^{1-\delta}}\Big)\geq\frac{c_{\delta}}{N}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{R}_{\infty}^{2,\varepsilon}}\mathbf{P}_{y}(H_{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}^{2,\varepsilon}}=+\infty)=\frac{c_{\delta}}{N}\mathrm{cap}\big(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}^{2,\varepsilon}\big)\,.

(3.40)

On the event
\mathcal{A}_{N,2}^{\varepsilon,\eta}
, we have
\mathrm{cap}\big(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}^{2,\varepsilon}\big)\geq\eta\Theta_{\varepsilon N}=\eta\varepsilon N
, and the lemma follows.
∎

Let
(\tau_{i}^{\mathrm{in}})_{i\geq 0}
and
(\tau_{i}^{\mathrm{out}})_{i\geq 0}
denote the successive returns to
B_{N}^{1-3\varepsilon}
after exiting
B_{N}^{1-\delta}
, that is
\tau^{\mathrm{out}}_{0}=0
and for
i\geq 0
,

\tau^{\mathrm{in}}_{i+1}=\inf\big\{t>\tau^{\mathrm{out}}_{i}\,:\,X_{t}\in B_{N}^{1-3\varepsilon}\big\}\quad,\quad\tau^{\mathrm{out}}_{i+1}=\inf\big\{t>\tau^{\mathrm{in}}_{i+1}\,:\,X_{t}\not\in B_{N}^{1-2\delta}\big\}\,.

(3.41)

Let us write
\mathcal{N}(t_{N})\vcentcolon=\sup\left\{i\,:\,\tau_{i}^{\mathrm{out}}\leq t_{N}\right\}
, we then have

\mathbf{P}_{x}(\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\infty}^{2,\varepsilon}=\varnothing\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})\leq\mathbf{P}_{x}(\forall i\in\left\{1,\dots,\mathcal{N}(t_{N})\right\},\mathcal{R}_{[\tau^{\mathrm{in}}_{i},\tau^{\mathrm{out}}_{i}]}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\infty}^{2,\varepsilon}=\varnothing\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})\,.

(3.42)

Note that by definition of the times
(\tau^{\mathrm{in}}_{i})_{i\geq 1}
and
(\tau^{\mathrm{out}}_{i})_{i\geq 1}
, we have the equality between events:

\left\{\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}\right\}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{\mathcal{N}(t_{N})}\left\{\mathcal{R}([\tau^{\mathrm{out}}_{i-1},\tau^{\mathrm{in}}_{i}])\subset B_{N}\right\}\cap\left\{\mathcal{R}([\tau^{\mathrm{out}}_{\mathcal{N}(t_{N})},t_{N}])\subset B_{N}\right\}\,.

(3.43)

With Lemma
3.9
, we get that for all
i\geq 1
,
\mathbf{P}_{x}(\mathcal{R}([\tau^{\mathrm{in}}_{i},\tau^{\mathrm{out}}_{i}])\cap\mathcal{R}_{\infty}^{2,\varepsilon}=\varnothing)\leq 1-c_{0}
. Therefore, using the Markov property at times
(\tau^{\mathrm{in}}_{i})_{i\geq 1}
and
(\tau^{\mathrm{out}}_{i})_{i\geq 1}
, we get the upper bound

\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\infty}^{2,\varepsilon}=\varnothing\,,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})\leq\mathbf{E}_{x}\left[(1-c_{0})^{\mathcal{N}(t_{N})}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}\right\}}\right]\,.

(3.44)

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be small enough. For any sequence
(t_{N})_{N\geq 1}
which satisfies
t_{N}/N^{2}\to+\infty
, we have the convergence

\lim_{N\to+\infty}\mathbf{E}\left[(1-c_{0})^{\mathcal{N}(t_{N})}\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}\right]=0\,.

(3.45)

Proof.

First write for any
K>1
,

\lim_{N\to+\infty}\mathbf{E}_{x}\left[(1-c_{0})^{\mathcal{N}(t_{N})}\,\big|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}\right]\leq(1-c_{0})^{K}+\lim_{N\to+\infty}\mathbf{P}_{x}(\mathcal{N}(t_{N})\leq K\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})\,.

(3.46)

Hence, it suffices to prove that for any
K>1
, the probability on the right-hand side of (
3.46
) goes to
0
as
N\to+\infty
. Recall that
\mathcal{N}(t_{N})
counts the number of excursions from
B_{N}^{1-3\varepsilon}
to
B_{N}^{1-\delta}
achieved before time
t_{N}
. We can expect an excursion to take a time
\asymp N^{2}
and thus have
\asymp t_{N}/N^{2}
excursions. If
\mathcal{N}(t_{N})
is smaller then expected, this means that the walk spent too much time on one excursion.

Note that
\mathcal{N}(t_{N})\leq K
implies that at least one of these excursions has length at least
t_{N}/K
, meaning that there exists a
i\in\llbracket 1,K\rrbracket
such that
\tau^{\mathrm{in}}_{i}-\tau^{\mathrm{in}}_{i-1}\geq t_{N}/K
. For such
i
, this also implies that
\tau^{\mathrm{out}}_{i+1}-\tau^{\mathrm{in}}_{i}
or
\tau^{\mathrm{in}}_{i+1}-\tau^{\mathrm{out}}_{i+1}
is greater than
t_{N}/2K
. Finally, observe that writing
I_{k,K}^{N}\vcentcolon=\llbracket kt_{N}/4K,(k+1)t_{N}/4K\rrbracket
, all of the above imply that there is an interval of time
I_{k}^{K,(N)}
such that
\mathcal{R}(I_{k}^{K,(N)})\subseteq B_{N}^{1-\delta}
or
\mathcal{R}(I_{k,K}^{N})\subseteq B_{N}\setminus B_{N}^{1-\delta}
.

If we summarize the previous paragraph, using two consecutive union bounds we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{x}

\displaystyle(\mathcal{N}(t_{N})\leq K\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{P}_{x}(\exists k\in\llbracket 0,4K\rrbracket,\mathcal{R}(I_{k,K}^{N})\subseteq B_{N}^{1-\delta}\text{ or }\mathcal{R}(I_{k,K}^{N})\subseteq B_{N}\setminus B_{N}^{1-\delta}\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})

(3.47)

\displaystyle\leq 2K\sup_{k\in\llbracket 0,4K\rrbracket}\Big(\mathbf{P}\big(\mathcal{R}(I_{k,K}^{N})\subseteq B_{N}^{1-\delta}\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}\big)+\mathbf{P}\big(\mathcal{R}(I_{k,K}^{N})\subseteq B_{N}\setminus B_{N}^{1-\delta}\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N}\big)\Big)\,.

Using the same trick as in the proof of Proposition
3.3
, we use the Markov property at the start and the end of
I_{k,K}^{N}
to control both of the conditional probabilities. For the first term, we get the upper bound

\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{R}(I_{k,K}^{N})\subseteq B_{N}^{1-2\delta}\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})\leq c_{1}\lambda_{N}^{-t_{N}/4K}\sup_{z\in B_{N}^{1-\delta}}\mathbf{P}_{z}(\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}/4K}\subseteq B_{N}^{1-\delta})\,,

(3.48)

for which a use of Lemma
3.2
yields

\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{R}(I_{k,K}^{N})\subseteq B_{N}^{1-\delta}\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})\leq c_{1}^{\prime}\lambda_{N}^{(\frac{1}{(1-\delta)^{2}}-1)t_{N}/4K}\leq c_{1}^{\prime\prime}\exp\Big(-c_{b}2\delta^{2}\frac{t_{N}}{4KN^{2}}\Big)\,.

(3.49)

On the other hand, with the same reasoning and a use of (
3.6
), we get

\mathbf{P}\Big(\mathcal{R}(I_{k,K}^{N})\subseteq B_{N}\setminus B_{N}^{1-\delta}\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})\leq c_{2}^{\prime\prime}\exp\left(-(\tfrac{c_{1}}{\varepsilon^{2}}-c_{b})\frac{t_{N}}{4KN^{2}}\right)\,.

(3.50)

Therefore, assuming that we took
\varepsilon>0
small enough, injecting (
3.49
) and (
3.50
) in (
3.3.2
) finally yields the bound

\mathbf{P}_{x}(\mathcal{N}(t_{N})\leq K\,|\,\mathcal{R}_{t_{N}}\subseteq B_{N})\leq c\exp\bigg(-\frac{c^{\prime}}{4K}\frac{t_{N}}{N^{2}}\bigg)

(3.51)

for some constants
c,c^{\prime}>0
that do not depend on
N
or
K
. In particular, we get the Lemma by using that
t_{N}N^{-d}\Theta_{N}\to+\infty
means that in dimension three
t_{N}/N^{2}\to+\infty
.
∎

Appendix

A classical result for the simple random walk states that the exit point of a ball is roughly uniform provided that the starting point is not too close to the boundary. Recall that
\tau_{R}
is the exit time of the discrete Euclidean ball
B_{R}=B(0,R)
.

Proposition 3.12

(
[
20
, Lemma 6.3.7]
)
.

There is a constant
c_{3}>0
such that, for any
R
large enough,

\frac{1}{c_{3}R^{d-1}}\leq\inf_{u\in\bar{B}_{R/4}}\inf_{z\in\partial B_{R}}\mathbf{P}_{u}\big(X_{\tau_{R}}=z\big)\leq\sup_{u\in\bar{B}_{R/4}}\sup_{z\in\partial B_{R}}\mathbf{P}_{u}\big(X_{\tau_{R}}=z\big)\leq\frac{c_{3}}{R^{d-1}}\,.

(3.52)

The proof relies on a use of a last exit decomposition on the discrete sphere
\partial B_{R/2}
and time reversal of the last excursion.

An important consequence of Proposition
3.12
is that given two points
x,y
at typical distance
R
, the random walk has a positive probability of going from
x
to a
\eta R
-neighborhood of
y
while staying in a straight narrow corridor of width
\delta R
.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
\eta,\delta>0
, and for
\ell>1
write
\mathscr{R}_{\ell}(x,y)\vcentcolon=[x,y]+B(0,\ell)
for the
\ell
neighborhood of the segment
[x,y]
. Then, there is a constant
c_{\eta,\delta}>0
such that for any
R
large enough,

\sup_{x,y;|x-y|\leq R}\mathbf{P}_{x}\Big(H_{B(y,\eta R)}<H_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\mathscr{R}_{\delta R}(x,y)}\Big)\geq c_{\eta,\delta}\,.

(3.53)

Sketch of proof.

Fix
x,y
. There is some
I=I(x,y)\in\mathbb{N}
such that there exists a chain
(z_{i})_{i=0}^{I}
which satisfies
z_{0}=x
,
z_{I}=y
, and for all
i\leq I-1
, we have
|z_{i+1}-z_{i}|=\delta R
and
B(z_{i},\delta R)\subset\mathscr{R}_{\delta R}(x,y)
. Then, there is a
c_{\delta}>0
such that for all
i
we have
|\partial B(z_{i},\delta R)\cap B(z_{i+1},\tfrac{1}{4}\delta R)|\geq c_{\delta}R^{d-1}
. In particular, using Proposition
3.12
, there is a
c_{\delta}^{\prime}>0
such that for all
i
,

\mathbf{P}_{z_{i}}(X_{H_{\partial B(z_{i},\delta R)}}\in B(z_{i+1},\delta R/4))\geq c_{\delta}^{\prime}\,.

Therefore, by the Markov property,
\mathbf{P}_{x}(H_{B_{R}^{y,\delta}}<H_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\mathscr{R}_{\delta R}(x,y)})\geq(c_{\delta}^{\prime})^{I}
. Note that we can bound from above the length
I(x,y)
of the chain uniformly in
x
and
y
, by some
I_{0}=I_{0}(\delta)
. Therefore, we deduce that
\mathbf{P}_{x}(H_{B(y,\delta R)}<H_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\mathscr{R}_{\delta R}(x,y)})\geq(c_{\delta}^{\prime})^{I_{0}}=c(\delta)>0
. Note that with such “chain” event, the SRW stays at distance at most
\delta R
from the segment
[x,y]
, thus concluding the proof.
∎

With such estimate, we can get a lower bound on the Green function of the SRW killed on the boundary of a large ball
B_{R}
. The following lemma states that, restricting ourselves to the bulk of the ball,
G_{B_{R}}
is comparable to
G
.

Lemma 3.14
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. There is a constant
c_{\varepsilon}>0
such that for all
R
large enough, we have

\inf_{x,y\in B_{(1-\varepsilon)R}}G_{B_{R}}(x,y)\geq c_{\varepsilon}R^{2-d}\,.

(3.54)

Note that we trivially have
G_{B_{R}}(x,y)\leq G(x,y)\leq C_{d}R^{2-d}
.

Proof.

Fix
\delta\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon)
and consider the ball
B_{\delta R}^{y}
centered at
y
with radius
\delta R
. We have the lower bound

\begin{split}G_{B_{R}}(x,y)&\geq\mathbf{P}_{x}(H_{B_{\delta R}^{y}}<H_{\partial B_{R}})\inf_{z\in\partial B_{\delta R}^{y}}G_{B_{R}}(z,y)\\
&\geq\mathbf{P}_{x}\big(H_{B_{\delta R}^{y}}<H_{\partial B_{R}}\big)\inf_{z\in\partial B_{\delta R}}G_{B_{2\delta R}}(z,0)\,,\end{split}

(3.55)

where we have used the monoticity of
G_{A}
with respect to the domain
A
(since
B_{2\delta R}^{y}\subseteq B_{R}
by assumption on
\delta
) and the invariance of
\mathbf{P}
with respect to translations.
Now, using
[
20
, Proposition 6.3.5]
, we have for
R
large enough:

G_{B_{\delta R}}(z,0)=a_{d}\big(|z|^{2-d}-(\delta R)^{2-d}\big)+\bar{\mathcal{O}}(R^{1-d})\geq\frac{a_{d}}{4\delta^{d-2}}\frac{1}{R^{d-2}}\,.

According to Lemma
3.13
and since
\delta<\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon
, the probability appearing in (
3.55
) can be bounded from below by a constant
c=c(\delta)
that is uniform in
x,y\in B_{(1-\varepsilon)R}
, thus proving the lemma.
