Comparison of preformed orthodontic archwires with modified dental arch forms: A cross-sectional study

Background

Preservation of a patient’s original arch form is a cornerstone of orthodontic stability and periodontal safety. Recent evidence emphasizes that arch dimensions and shapes vary by sex and ancestry and are influenced by genetic and environmental factors [1–5]. Preformed NiTi archwires—engineered for favorable load‑deflection properties—are widely employed during alignment and leveling, yet their standardized shapes do not necessarily reflect the diversity of natural arches [6–10]. Multiple studies since 2020 have shown inconsistency between commercial archwire forms and clinical arch forms at key landmarks, especially at the canine level where standardized wires are commonly wider. [6, 8, 9, 11, 12] Moreover, the increasing adoption of digital workflows has improved the precision of arch form measurement and setup through three‑dimensional dental models [13–19].

NiTi metallurgy has continued to evolve, including heat‑activated and copper‑NiTi variants; nevertheless, randomized trials demonstrate similar early alignment efficiency across many NiTi types, shifting clinical emphasis toward wire form rather than solely alloy subtype [7, 20–24]. Customized CAD/CAM appliances and robotic wire bending provide promising avenues for individualized archwire design, but preformed labial archwires remain dominant in daily practice [25–29]. For clinicians operating in under‑represented populations, such as Yemeni adults, local normative data remain essential to reduce unnecessary transverse expansion, minimize periodontal risk, and support long‑term stability. [3, 4] This study reports population‑specific arch dimensions and predominant arch forms and benchmarks the fit of commonly used preformed NiTi archwires against a bracket‑point–based reference graph.

Methods

Study design and setting

Design and setting: Cross-sectional study of pretreatment dental casts collected from two university orthodontic clinics and three private practices in Sana’a, Yemen. Screening and inclusion are summarized in Fig. 2. Reporting followed the STROBE guideline for cross-sectional studies [30].

Flow diagram of record screening, eligibility assessment, and final sample inclusion

Sampling frame and sample size

Sampling frame and size: Records from two university clinics and three private orthodontic practices were screened (n = 3,000). After applying eligibility criteria, 222 subjects were included in the final analyses (444 casts: maxillary and mandibular). A priori, we targeted ≥ 200 subjects to estimate mean transverse widths within ± 0.5 mm at 95% confidence, assuming SD ≈ 3 mm based on pilot data and contemporary literature. This also provided ≥ 80% power to detect sex‑related differences ≥ 1.5 mm in Wc and Wm using two‑sided α = 0.05. Eligibility criteria mirrored everyday case mix to enhance external validity while excluding conditions that distort landmarks (e.g., large restorations encroaching on CBPs).

Participants and eligibility

Eligibility criteria: Yemeni adults aged 16–28 years with permanent dentitions (excluding third molars), pretreatment casts of adequate quality, and Angle Class I or II malocclusion. Exclusion criteria included prior orthodontic treatment, craniofacial anomalies, missing teeth (except third molars), extensive restorations affecting landmark identification, and casts with defects that precluded reliable digitization.

Rationale for including Class I and Class II: The study aimed to generate population‑level arch‑dimension data and evaluate the fit of commonly used preformed archwires in the typical orthodontic case‑mix. Because sagittal malocclusion subtype may influence transverse dimensions, we performed sensitivity analyses restricted to Class I and stratified analyses by sex to assess robustness.

Model digitization and quality control

Model digitization and quality control: Alginate impressions were poured within 30 min using vacuum-mixed ISO Type 3 dental stone. Casts were scanned on a laboratory scanner with manufacturer-specified trueness ≤ 20 μm; STL files were inspected for holes and artifacts. The occlusal plane was standardized by three-point orientation (incisal midpoint and mesiobuccal cusp tips of first molars) to minimize projection bias. Landmarks were placed on CBPs identified as the midpoint of the bracket footprint per crown, consistent with current digital measurement guidance [13–17, 31, 32].

Landmarks and measurements

Clinical bracket points (CBPs) were identified on each crown as the midpoint of the estimated bracket footprint on the facial axis of the clinical crown. From CBPs, transverse widths were computed as chord lengths at the canine level (L3; intercanine width, Wc) and first‑molar level (L6; intermolar width, Wm). Arch depths were measured as orthogonal distances from the midline to the corresponding chord lines at L3 (Dc) and L6 (Dm). Measurement definitions and the reference‑arch schematic are shown in Fig. 1.

Reference grid and archwire evaluation

Reference grid construction: The CBP reference grid comprised a sixth‑order polynomial representation of the clinical arch path. To approximate the position of the working archwire relative to the enamel surface, bracket‑base thickness offsets were added to the CBPs (central incisor: 1.34 mm; canine: 0.75 mm; first molar: 0.73 mm), consistent with published bracket‑base dimensions used in reference‑arch methods. Widths at L3 and L6 were computed as chord lengths between left/right CBPs; depths were orthogonal distances from the midline to the chord lines. Proportional indices (WDc, WDm) were recorded to capture anterior vs. posterior fullness independent of absolute size.

Archwire inventory and tracing: Twenty‑seven preformed rectangular 0.019 × 0.025‑inch NiTi archwires (maxillary and mandibular forms) from 11 commercial manufacturers commonly available in the local market were evaluated. Each archwire was scanned and digitally traced onto the CBP reference grid, and wire widths were recorded at the canine (L3) and first‑molar (L6) levels defined in Fig. 1.

Arch form categorization

Arch form categorization: Following contemporary practice, arches were categorized into tapered, ovoid, or square based on an automated best‑fit to canonical templates using root‑mean‑square (RMS) error minimization, then visually verified by two calibrated examiners. Disagreements were resolved by consensus; Cohen’s κ was computed for interrater agreement on form categories.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were archwire–arch width differences at L3 and L6 for maxillary and mandibular arches; secondary outcomes included arch‑form distribution and sex differences in transverse dimensions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical plan: Normality was assessed by Shapiro–Wilk and homoscedasticity by Levene tests. Sex comparisons used independent‑samples t‑tests or Mann–Whitney U as indicated; arch form distribution by sex used χ² tests. For wire‑to‑arch comparisons, we summarized mean differences and their 95% CIs at L3 and L6 for each archwire model; one‑sample tests evaluated whether the mean difference deviated from 0 mm. Equivalence analyses used two one‑sided tests (TOST) with prespecified equivalence margins of ± 1.0 mm at L3 and ± 1.5 mm at L6, aligned with clinically acceptable bounds informed by contemporary orthodontic guidance [7, 33]. Multiplicity was addressed by Benjamini–Hochberg FDR control (q = 0.10) given the benchmarking nature of the analysis.

Reliability and repeatability

Repeatability and reliability: 10% of casts were re‑landmarked and re‑measured after a two‑week washout period by the same examiner and by an independent examiner. We report ICC(2,1) for absolute agreement with 95% CIs for Wc, Wm, Dc, and Dm; Bland–Altman plots confirmed absence of systematic bias [33]. Category agreement for arch form used quadratic‑weighted κ and percent agreement; pre‑specified thresholds for acceptable reliability were ICC ≥ 0.90 and κ ≥ 0.80.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses: (1) Restriction to Class I subjects; (2) Stratification by sex; (3) Substitution of enamel‑based landmarks (FA points) for CBPs to evaluate robustness of width estimates. Results were directionally stable across sensitivity analyses (details available on request).

Data-derived archwire selection guidance

Based on the observed mismatch pattern, a practical selection sequence can be derived directly from the data. First, obtain calibrated digital models and measure Wc/Wm and Dc/Dm at CBPs; then determine whether the arch is tapered, ovoid, or square. Next, shortlist preformed archwires whose anterior width approximates the patient’s Wc, because the largest mismatch in this dataset was concentrated at L3. Posterior fit should then be checked at L6, with preference given to models that minimize combined mismatch across both arches. In this cohort, narrower tapered or narrower ovoid forms generally performed better than broader square forms, whereas markedly broad anterior forms were more likely to over-expand canine width. Discussion

Findings align with recent literature reporting mismatch between commercial archwire geometries and clinical arch forms, notably at the canine level [6, 8, 9, 11, 12, 34, 35]. Given comparable alignment efficiency among many NiTi subtypes, clinicians should prioritize wire form to preserve native arches, supplementing with individualized bends as needed [7, 21–23]. Digital measurement reliability is high, and customized appliances can reproduce target forms with good accuracy, although posterior overcorrection may still be necessary in some systems [13–19, 25, 36]. For expansion therapies, predictability diminishes posteriorly and skeletal gains are limited; maintaining pretreatment intercanine width therefore remains important for stability [29, 37–40]. Strengths of the present study include the sample size, CBP-based 3D measurements, and explicit benchmarking of multiple commercially available forms.

Compared with studies from East Asian and European cohorts, the Yemeni sample showed a similar qualitative distribution of arch forms, with ovoid predominance, but smaller absolute intercanine widths, particularly in the mandible. This pattern suggests that wire catalogs configured around broader North American or European averages may require additional narrow tapered options for Middle Eastern populations to reduce unintended transverse expansion.

The Yemeni adult cohort reflects a typical university/private-practice orthodontic case mix in an urban setting. Although the results may generalize to neighboring populations with shared ancestry and environmental exposures, clinicians should still consider local anthropometric data wherever possible. Arch development is shaped by both genetic and environmental influences, and the present values should therefore be used as population guidance to support individualized selection rather than as rigid treatment targets [2–4].

We incorporated several safeguards to strengthen data quality: laboratory scanning with high trueness, a standardized landmarking protocol, blinded repeat measurements, and pre-specified equivalence bounds to discourage post hoc thresholding. Although residual imprecision may arise from scanner stitching near interproximal contacts, CBP estimation on abraded enamel, and variation in bracket-base thickness between products, the magnitude of the observed wire-arch mismatches, especially at L3, was substantially larger than plausible measurement error.

Taken together, these results support a conservative clinical strategy: start from the patient’s pretreatment form, favor the narrowest suitable anterior dimension, and individualize finishing adjustments rather than defaulting to broader stock forms. In settings with access to CAD/CAM or robotic wire bending, customized geometries derived from CBPs may further improve fit and biologically appropriate loading [15, 24–28, 36].

Limitations and future research

The benchmarking approach used mean arch values, which may obscure subject-level mismatches; future work should therefore incorporate case-matched fitting analyses and prospective periodontal and stability outcomes. Bracket prescription and slot size can alter the effective archwire path; although bracket-base offsets were modeled, true in vivo trajectories may vary with adhesive thickness and torque play. The wire portfolio reflected products commonly available in the study region, so inclusion of additional global brands may broaden generalizability. Finally, although prior imaging literature suggests caution with buccal expansion in thin biotypes, direct CBCT assessment of alveolar housing was beyond the scope of this study.

Conclusions

Preformed NiTi archwires frequently over‑expand canine widths in Yemeni adults. Selecting narrower preformed forms at L3 and slightly narrower at L6 can better preserve individual arch forms. No single product matched all metrics; individualized adjustments remain necessary.
