Morphological description and molecular characterization of Leucocytozoon australis sp. nov. (Apicomplexa: Haemosporida: Leucocytozoidae) infecting the Austral parakeet (Enicognathus ferrugineus), an endemic parrot from the southern Andes
Authors: Valeria Ojeda, Laura M Casalins, Juan F Masello, Gala Ortiz, Silvina Ippi, Rodrigo A Vásquez, Javier Martínez, Santiago Merino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBirds of the order Psittaciformes are rarely infected by haemosporidian blood parasites in the wild, and the few records of Leucocytozoon in parrots correspond to host-generalist lineages detected in captive birds. Here we describe a new species, Leucocytozoon australis sp. nov., infecting the Austral parakeet (Enicognathus ferrugineus) in the southern Andes. We examined 18 individuals — 15 sampled for blood (preserved on FTA cards and as stained blood films) and 3 sampled for liver — collected between 2004 and 2021 at two regions separated by ∼1500 km (Navarino Island, Chile, and the Bariloche area, Argentina). Infection was assessed by microscopy of stained blood films and by PCR and sequencing of a cytochrome b fragment that fully encompasses the standard HaemFL–HaemR2L barcode region. Leucocytozoon was detected in 8 of 18 birds by PCR and in 5 of 8 blood films examined. The morphological description is based on films from Navarino Island infected exclusively with a single lineage. Gametocytes are roundish but usually irregular and develop in roundish host cells with a markedly irregular nucleus; infected cells are typically distorted. Two closely related cytochrome b lineages were recovered, ENIFER01 and ENIFER02 (GenBank PZ371351 and PZ371352), differing by seven substitutions (0.93%); neither matched any lineage in the MalAvi database, confirming both as previously unreported. Both fall within a well-supported subclade of Leucocytozoon from South American passerines, with which they share 97.8–98.3% (ENIFER01) and 98.8–99% (ENIFER02) identity. To our knowledge, L. australis sp. nov. is the first Leucocytozoon species to be formally described, morphologically and molecularly, from a Psittaciformes host. The omnivorous diet of Austral parakeets, a trait previously associated with haemosporidian infection in Psittaciformes, may facilitate exposure.Keywords: Austral temperate forest, Patagonia, Psittaciformes, Omnivorous diet, Haemoparasites

Introduction

Many avian haemosporidian parasites (phylum Apicomplexa) were described morphologically long before the use of molecular tools for detection of avian blood parasites (Valkiūnas, 2005). The subsequent rise of PCR-based screening and the identification of mitochondrial cytochrome b lineages have since revealed thousands of lineages across the three main avian genera, Plasmodium, Haemoproteus and Leucocytozoon (Bensch et al., 2009; Duc et al., 2025). This has created a growing mismatch between the ∼300 morphologically described species and the far greater molecular diversity now catalogued (5369 lineages in the MalAvi database; http://130.235.244.92/Malavi/): many morphospecies still lack genetic characterization, while most molecular lineages have no formal morphological description (Nilsson et al., 2016; Valkiūnas and Iezhova, 2018, 2022). Linking a lineage to a described species requires sampling parasites from the original host, and preferably near the type locality (Bensch et al., 2004; Santiago-Alarcon et al., 2012; Outlaw and Ricklefs, 2014) — a logistical constraint that has slowed taxonomic progress.

This gap is particularly wide in Leucocytozoon. These parasites cause avian leucocytozoonosis in poultry and wild birds across most avian orders and are distributed worldwide, from warm lowlands to cold high mountains (Valkiūnas, 2005; Forrester and Greiner, 2008). Yet, although the MalAvi database lists over 1500 Leucocytozoon lineages, fewer than 2% are reliably linked to a described species (Valkiūnas and Iezhova, 2023), and their genomes and life cycles remain poorly known. This uncertainty at the species level hampers the integration of molecular, morphological and ecological data (Valkiūnas and Iezhova, 2023). With a single exception, Leucocytozoon spp. are transmitted by blackflies (Simuliidae), whose larvae require cold, oxygen-rich running waters; this dependence underlies the inverse latitudinal gradient reported for the genus in the Americas, where infection increases towards higher, colder latitudes (Merino et al., 2008; Lotta et al., 2016; Fecchio et al., 2020). Blackfly habitat is abundant in the Andean forests of southern South America (Patagonia), where Leucocytozoon is relatively common in birds, contrasting with the mosquito-borne Plasmodium (Culicidae) and the Haemoproteus transmitted mainly by biting midges (Ceratopogonidae) and hippoboscid flies (Hippoboscidae), which require warmer conditions (Merino et al., 2008; Doussang et al., 2019; Mozaffer et al., 2022).

Parrots (Psittaciformes) are a conspicuous exception to this widespread occurrence: haemosporidian infections are rare in wild psittaciforms, and the few reports of Leucocytozoon in parrots come from captive birds (Masello et al., 2018). Where infections do occur, they have been associated with an omnivorous diet, which may increase exposure to vectors or infective stages (Masello et al., 2018). Consequently, records from wild parrots are scarce, and no Leucocytozoon species has ever been formally described from a Psittaciformes host, leaving the wild parrot–Leucocytozoon association almost entirely unexplored.

Among the endemic birds of southern Andean forests is the Austral parakeet (Enicognathus ferrugineus Müller, 1776), a medium-sized (28–36 cm), sexually size-monomorphic parrot with the southernmost distribution of any living parrot (to 54°S), occurring from sea level to 2000 m a.s.l. (Darrieu, 1982). Little is known about the health of its wild populations (Valdebenito et al., 2015), and information on its haemoparasites was largely unavailable until Masello et al. (2018), who, while screening wild Psittaciformes globally, detected a single, undescribed Leucocytozoon lineage in Austral parakeets from two regions ∼1500 km apart — southernmost Chilean Patagonia (Navarino Island) and north-western Argentine Patagonia (near Bariloche) — but did not characterize it further.

That Leucocytozoon has since remained uncharacterized: its cytochrome b lineage was not fully resolved, its morphology was never described, and its phylogenetic affinities were unknown. Here we address this gap by (i) providing the complete molecular characterization of the Leucocytozoon lineages infecting E. ferrugineus, (ii) describing the parasite morphologically and erecting it as a new species, and (iii) assessing its phylogenetic position within the genus. In doing so, we report the first Leucocytozoon species described from a wild Psittaciformes host.

Materials and methods

Study areas and sampling

Samples were collected at different times and locations between 2004 and 2021 (Table 1). On Navarino Island (southernmost Chilean Patagonia, 54°55.8′ S, 67°36.0′ W), two adult Austral parakeets were captured by mist-netting in January 2013. In the northern Patagonian Andes near San Carlos de Bariloche, Argentina (hereafter "the Bariloche area"; ∼41°16′ S, 71°17′ W), nesting adults were captured at cavity nests within Nahuel Huapi National Park —specifically in the Challhuaco Valley (∼15 km south-east of Bariloche)— in March 2015 and during the 2020–2021 breeding season (December–January). These were analysed together with three birds found dead in the area between 2004 and 2014 and four birds sampled opportunistically between August 2015 and May 2016 from injured individuals in rehabilitation prior to release, both from sites within the Bariloche area but outside the park. Throughout, "the Bariloche area" denotes this whole northern region, in contrast to the southern locality on Navarino Island (∼1500 km to the south). Molecular detection of blood parasites in the samples collected before April 2015 was reported by Masello et al. (2018), but those parasites were neither described morphologically nor linked to molecular data; the 2015–2016 and 2020–2021 samples are analysed here for the first time.

Navarino Island has a subpolar oceanic climate with little seasonal temperature variation (∼1.9 to −9.6 °C) and is covered by a mosaic of evergreen and deciduous Nothofagus forests and Drimys winteri, reaching 1118 m a.s.l. (Pisano, 1977; Rozzi et al., 2006). The northern Patagonian Andes within Nahuel Huapi National Park comprise lakes, glacial valleys and rugged mountains dominated by Nothofagus spp., with mean seasonal temperatures of 2.3 °C (winter) and 16.4 °C (summer), and a marked west–east precipitation gradient (∼2000 to ∼500 mm), falling mostly as snow in winter (SMN, 2023). Captures in this region were conducted in the Challhuaco Valley, ∼15 km south-east of Bariloche, in mature (>200-year-old) deciduous N. pumilio forest. Austral parakeets are resident, gregarious birds that form large flocks year-round and roost communally in tree cavities (Forshaw, 1989; Díaz, 2012). Although this connectivity suggests that birds sampled in 2020–2021 belong to the same population sampled in 2015, samples were treated as independent, as most individuals came from different cavity nests and the species shows strong nest fidelity (Díaz and Ojeda, unpublished data).

Bird capture and blood sampling

On Navarino Island, birds were captured by mist-netting. In Nahuel Huapi, adults were captured at nests in 2015 (chick-rearing) and 2020–2021 (incubation and early brood-rearing), when adults — mostly incubating females — remain inside the cavity. Cavities <8 m high were first inspected with a telescopic pole fitted with a video probe; adults were then captured at cavities 2.6–7 m above ground using a noose pole and torch, gently drawing the bird to the entrance and immobilising it by hand. Where both members of a pair were captured, the male and female were taken a few days apart to minimise disturbance. Captured birds were placed in a clean cloth bag for descent; nests were rechecked at least once afterwards, and no adverse effects on nestlings were observed. In total, blood was obtained from 13 adults at 11 cavities.

Blood was collected by puncture of the cutaneous ulnar vein immediately after capture, using a sterilized 26- or 27-gauge needle and heparinized capillary tubes (70 μl); the maximum volume taken was well below 1% of body weight (Campbell, 1995). Haemostasis was achieved with light pressure, and birds were released on site. Because heparin prevented coagulation and in excess could produce alteration of blood cells, capillaries were used to transfer immediately blood to a slide to prepare thin blood films in the field. Blood smears were air-dried and, when sufficient blood was available (Table 1), a fraction was also stored on FTA classic cards (Whatman, UK).

Blood-film staining and microscopy

Thin blood films, were prepared from non-coagulated blood in the field, immediately after extraction and air-dried, were fixed in absolute methanol shortly afterwards to preserve them until staining, which was carried out later in the laboratory. Thus, blood was not stored in any container containing anticoagulants and capillaries were only used to transfer blood to the microscopic slide. Morphological alterations of blood cells due to an excess of heparin like crenation of erythrocytes or nuclear degeneration of white blood cells were not observed in any case.

Films were stained by one of two Romanowsky (azure–eosin) methods, depending on the sampling campaign. Films from Navarino Island — the type material — were stained with 10% (v/v) Giemsa in pH 7.2 buffer (40 min), following the protocol commonly used for the morphological study of avian haemosporidian morphospecies (Valkiūnas, 2005). Films from the Bariloche area were stained by the May–Grünwald–Giemsa (Pappenheim) method, a two-step procedure in which the methanolic May–Grünwald solution (working solution, 3 min) is followed, after a rinse in pH 7.2 buffer, by commercial Giemsa diluted to 10% (v/v) in the same buffer (30 min); this is a standard Romanowsky-type polychromatic method whose Giemsa phase shares the same azure–eosin chemistry (Piaton et al., 2016), rendering the diagnostic characters comparable to those in the Giemsa-stained type films. In both cases films were then rinsed in buffered water and air-dried.

Stained films were examined under a Leica DM750 microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany), first at ×100 (10 min) and then at ×1000 (∼1 h per slide); digital images were captured with an Olympus SC30 camera. Morphometric measurements were taken during the preparation of this manuscript.

Infection intensity was estimated as the number of parasites per 10,000 erythrocytes by scanning 100 fields at ×1000 in monolayer areas. Length, width, perimeter and area of the parasite, host cell and host-cell nucleus were measured on calibrated images following Matta et al. (2014).

Molecular analyses

DNA was extracted from FTA-stored samples following Martínez et al. (2009) and purified with the NZYGelpure kit (NZYTech, Lisbon, Portugal). A fragment of the Leucocytozoon cytochrome b gene was amplified with primers L180/L970 (Table 2; García-del-Río et al., 2020) in a final volume of 20 μl containing Supreme NZYTaq 2× Green Master Mix (NZYTech), 250 nM of each primer, 20–100 ng of template DNA, and water to volume, on a Veriti thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems) under the conditions in Table 2. This fragment fully encompasses the standard HaemFL–HaemR2L barcode region (Hellgren et al., 2004), allowing lineage assignment against the MalAvi database. Each batch included a negative control (water), in which no amplicon was ever obtained, and a positive control (Leucocytozoon sp. haplotype LeuA, GenBank KJ415278). Amplicons were purified from agarose gels and sequenced in both directions on an ABI 3730XL sequencer (Applied Biosystems). DNA extraction and PCR set-up were performed in separate laminar-flow cabinets. Mixed infections were identified as double peaks, confirmed in both directions, at the positions distinguishing the two lineages.

Phylogenetic analyses

To build a phylogenetic tree of Leucocytozoon, we selected cytochrome b sequences after performing a BLAST search, retaining only those with at least 98% query coverage and removing duplicated (identical) sequences. The final alignment comprised 98 sequences and spanned 745 bp, including the sequences obtained in the present study and sequences of the genera Haemoproteus, Parahaemoproteus and Plasmodium used as outgroup (all sequences, hosts, geographic origins and GenBank accession numbers are listed in Tables S1 and S2). The amplified fragment fully encompasses the standard HaemFL–HaemR2L barcode region (Hellgren et al., 2004), allowing lineage assignment by BLAST against the complete MalAvi database. The alignment was performed with the ClustalW algorithm implemented in BioEdit 7.0.5.3 (Hall, 1999). Phylogenetic reconstruction followed two complementary approaches.

Maximum Likelihood (ML) analysis was conducted in IQ-TREE v3 (Nguyen et al., 2015; Wong et al., 2026) using ModelFinder (Kalyaanamoorthy et al., 2017) for automatic model selection under the Bayesian Information Criterion, which selected GTR + F + I + R3 as the best-fit model. Node support was assessed with the Ultrafast Bootstrap approximation (1000 replicates; Hoang et al., 2018).

Bayesian Inference (BI) was performed in MrBayes v3.2.7a (Ronquist et al., 2012), approximating as closely as possible the model selected by IQ-TREE. As the FreeRate model (+R) is not implemented in MrBayes, we used a GTR substitution model (nst = 6) with a non-parametric rate-mixture model (rates = kmixture) and four rate categories (nmixtcat = 4) to accommodate the fraction of invariable sites together with variable-rate classes, and character-state frequencies fixed to their empirical proportions (statefreqpr = fixed(empirical)). The Markov Chain Monte Carlo process was run for 1,275,000 generations (samplefreq = 100); the consensus tree was built from 19,108 trees sampled after discarding the first 25% as burn-in. The final average standard deviation of split frequencies was below 0.01. Convergence and adequate sampling were confirmed in Tracer (Rambaut et al., 2018): trace plots reached stationarity and Effective Sample Size (ESS) values for the log-likelihood and model parameters exceeded 200. The resulting tree was visualised and edited in Figtree v1.4.3 software.

Statistical analyses

Given the small sample, infection was summarised as infection rates (infected/examined) with exact 95% Clopper–Pearson confidence intervals, reported separately by method (microscopy, molecular) and sample type (blood, liver). No hypothesis testing was undertaken. Morphometric data are given as range (mean ± SD). Analyses were performed in Statistica v12.

Results

Infection rates

Leucocytozoon infection was assessed in 18 Austral parakeets by PCR (15 blood samples on FTA cards and three liver samples) and, in the subset for which blood films were available (n = 8; two from Navarino Island and six from Nahuel Huapi, 2020–2021), also by microscopy. By PCR, 8 of 18 birds were infected (44.4%; 95% CI 21.5–69.2), and by microscopy 5 of 8 films examined were positive (62.5%; 95% CI 24.5–91.5). The two methods were not fully concordant: two birds from Nahuel Huapi (2020–2021) were positive on blood films but negative by PCR, whereas five PCR-positive birds had no blood film available; considering both methods jointly, 10 of 18 birds were infected (55.6%; 95% CI 30.8–78.5; Table 3). Infection was detected in both regions and across all sampling periods: both birds from Navarino Island and 6 of 16 from the Bariloche area were PCR-positive, including one of three liver samples.

Lineages and their distribution

Sequencing of the eight PCR-positive samples yielded two cytochrome b haplotypes, designated by their MalAvi lineage names ENIFER01 (haplotype A; GenBank PZ371351) and ENIFER02 (haplotype B; PZ371352). BLAST comparison against the complete MalAvi database and GenBank returned no identical match for either sequence, confirming both as previously unreported lineages. The two lineages differed at seven nucleotide positions (0.93%) along the ∼750-bp fragment, six of them synonymous and one resulting in a conservative isoleucine-to-valine replacement, so that the cytochrome b reading frame was intact in both. Single infections predominated: ENIFER01 was recovered alone in five birds and ENIFER02 alone in one, whereas both lineages co-occurred in two birds; overall, ENIFER01 was present in seven birds and ENIFER02 in three. Mixed infections were identified as double peaks, confirmed in both sequencing directions, at the seven positions distinguishing the two lineages. The two lineages showed a partially segregated distribution (Table 4): the two southernmost birds (Navarino Island) carried ENIFER01 exclusively, whereas ENIFER02 and both co-infections were restricted to the northern locality.

Phylogenetic analyses

Leucocytozoon was recovered as monophyletic (Fig. 1). One strongly supported clade (Clade 1, collapsed in Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S1) comprised 48 sequences from Passeriformes. The remaining Leucocytozoon sequences did not form a well-supported group (ML/BI = 78/59) and are therefore not treated as a formal clade; they included sequences from Passeriformes, Strigiformes, one Coraciiformes, one Columbiformes, one Apodiformes, and the two from Austral parakeets. Both parakeet lineages fell within a strongly supported subclade (ML/BI = 100/99) together with five sequences from South American passerines (Scytalopus sp., Ochthoeca rufipectoralis, O. pulchella, Syndactyla rufosuperciliata and Drymophila caudata). Within this subclade, ENIFER01 and ENIFER02 were recovered as sisters, although with moderate ML and low BI support (86/53), consistent with their minimal divergence. Over the complete fragment, ENIFER01 shared 97.8–98.3% identity with these South American passerine sequences and ENIFER02 shared 98.8–99%. The close affinity of both lineages to Leucocytozoon of Neotropical passerines, their absence from any database, and the fact that they infect a wild psittaciform — an order from which no Leucocytozoon has been described — support the recognition of a new species, described below.

Macrogametocytes (Fig. 2A–F). Roundish but usually irregular in outline, developing in roundish host cells whose nucleus is markedly irregular and does not cover half of the gametocyte circumference. Remarks

Leucocytozoon australis develops in roundish host cells, with volutin granules and only some clearly discernible vacuoles in the parasite cytoplasm (Fig. 2). The presence of host-cell cytoplasm around the parasite and the occasional enucleation of the host cell resemble L. bennetti (Valkiūnas, 1993); however, in L. australis the host cell and its nucleus are more irregular in outline and the gametocytes are larger (14.4 ± 1.2 for L. bennetti macrogametocytes and 13.6 ± 1.4 for microgametocytes (Valkiūnas, 2005)) as well as the nucleus of the microgametocyte (8.0 ± 0.4 and almost round for L. bennetti while usually elongated in L. australis (Fig. 2G–I)) distinguishing the two species. In addition, the nucleolus is not prominent and well seen as in the case of L. bennetti. Unfortunately, to our knowledge, no genetic data are available for L. bennetti, preventing phylogenetic comparison. No signs of disease were observed in any infected bird. As far as we are aware, this is the first Leucocytozoon species described from a wild psittaciform host.

Intensity of parasitaemia (hapantotype): Less than one parasite per 10,000 erythrocytes.

Etymology: the specific epithet australis (Latin, "southern") refers to the southern distribution of the host and of the parasite — the southernmost so far reported for Leucocytozoon in a Psittaciformes host.

Discussion

Morphological differences among Leucocytozoon species are often subtle and manifest more as distortion of the host cell than as distinctive features of the parasite itself. In blood films, Leucocytozoon australis sp. nov. develops in roundish host cells whose nucleus is markedly irregular, the infected cells being typically distorted and adherent to neighbouring blood cells; volutin granules and occasional clear vacuoles are present in the parasite cytoplasm, and enucleation of the host cell occurs in a few cases. The presence of host-cell cytoplasm and occasional enucleation recall L. bennetti Valkiūnas, 1993, but in L. australis the host cell and its nucleus are more irregular in outline and the gametocytes are larger. In addition, the nucleus of the microgametocyte is usually elongated in L. australis and not round like in L. bennetti and the nucleolus is not prominent and well seen as in the case of L. bennetti. All these characteristics allow to distinguish between the two species.

Several lines of evidence support the recognition of L. australis as a distinct species rather than merely a new lineage. Although the description of Leucocytozoon species based solely on host identity is not accepted, as some parasites infect several host species, Valkiūnas (2005) noted that Leucocytozoon species tend to segregate among avian orders; the occurrence of a distinct, host-associated parasite in a wild parrot — an order from which no Leucocytozoon species has previously been described — is consistent with this pattern. Moreover, the two cytochrome b lineages recovered here, ENIFER01 and ENIFER02, had no identical match in either the MalAvi database or GenBank and are therefore previously unreported. Together, the novel host order, the novel lineages and the morphological characters distinguishing the parasite from L. bennetti provide a coherent case for a new species; this novelty strengthens the case but does not by itself replace the formal morphological criteria, which rest on the type material described above: Giemsa-stained films from a single-lineage (ENIFER01) infection at the type locality.

Leucocytozoon infections have only exceptionally been reported from parrots, and the existing records correspond to captive birds. Early surveys documented Leucocytozoon in parrots held in zoos and aviaries (Bennett et al., 1982; Bishop and Bennett, 1992), but these infections were attributed to transmission from other birds sharing captivity and were not regarded as natural (Masello et al., 2018). Likewise, the two Leucocytozoon lineages so far recorded from Psittacidae in the molecular literature are host-generalist parasites of other bird orders detected in captive birds: CHRKLA02, originally described from the Klaas's cuckoo (Chrysococcyx klaas; Chaisi et al., 2019), and CIAE02, a widely distributed lineage of raptors, the former reported from captive Poicephalus parrots (Gaorekwe et al., 2026) and the latter from a captive Brotogeris cyanoptera (Himmel et al., 2019). By contrast, L. australis was recovered from wild, free-ranging Austral parakeets across their native range and is embedded within a clade of Leucocytozoon from sympatric Neotropical passerines, indicating a genuine, locally transmitted parasite rather than an incidental captive record. To our knowledge, L. australis is therefore the first Leucocytozoon species described from a wild parrot host.

Two closely related lineages, ENIFER01 and ENIFER02, differing by only seven nucleotide substitutions (0.93%), were recovered from Austral parakeets. Their minimal divergence, their placement together in a strongly supported subclade of Neotropical Leucocytozoon, and the absence of any morphological differentiation between infections indicate that they are most parsimoniously regarded as two lineages of a single species. The co-occurrence of both lineages within some hosts is best explained by intraspecific co-infection rather than by mitochondrial heteroplasmy: single-lineage infections predominated (six of eight infected birds), and both lineages were also recovered on their own, demonstrating that either can establish independently in a host — an observation incompatible with strict heteroplasmy, under which every infection would carry both haplotypes. The two lineages showed a partially segregated distribution, ENIFER01 being the only lineage detected at the southernmost locality and ENIFER02, together with all co-infections, being restricted to the northern locality; this pattern, however, must be interpreted cautiously given the small southern sample (n = 2), which precludes inferring true absence of ENIFER02 from the southern range. A single mitochondrial marker cannot formally exclude the alternative that the two lineages represent distinct, co-infecting cryptic species, since these can differ by only a few cytochrome b substitutions in this genus (Galen et al., 2018); the data required to resolve this question are outlined below.

The ecology of the Austral parakeet and its environment are consistent with local transmission of Leucocytozoon. With a single exception, Leucocytozoon spp. are transmitted by blackflies (Simuliidae; Valkiūnas, 2005), whose larvae develop in the cold, oxygen-rich running waters that are abundant in the Andean–Patagonian Nothofagus forests inhabited by this parrot (Merino et al., 2008; Hernández et al., 2009). This is congruent with the inverse latitudinal gradient reported for Leucocytozoon in South American birds, in which infection increases towards higher, colder latitudes (Merino et al., 2008; Chapa-Vargas et al., 2020; Fecchio et al., 2020), and with our detection of infection across the whole latitudinal range sampled. Host behaviour may further favour transmission: Austral parakeets are gregarious, forming large flocks and roosting or nesting communally in tree cavities (Forshaw, 1989; Díaz, 2012), which could increase exposure to nocturnal blackfly biting. Notably, several of the Neotropical passerines sharing this Leucocytozoon subclade with L. australis — particularly Scytalopus tapaculos, cavity-nesting birds belonging to old Neotropical lineages largely endemic to Andean montane forests (Cadena et al., 2020) — occur in sympatry with the Austral parakeet, so that shared cavity-nesting habits and Andean forest habitat may expose phylogenetically related parasites to the same blackfly vectors. Because Leucocytozoon infections are typically chronic and may persist for the life of the host, this combination of suitable vector habitat and aggregative behaviour could account for the relatively frequent infection detected here. Many blackfly species occur in Patagonia (Hernández et al., 2009), but their associations with Leucocytozoon remain unknown, and the specific vector(s) of L. australis await identification.

Host feeding ecology may also influence haemosporidian infection. Masello et al. (2018) reported a positive association between haemosporidian infection and omnivorous diets in Psittaciformes. Austral parakeets are more insectivorous than previously assumed, supplementing pollen and plant material with animal items, including insect larvae, during the pre- and post-reproductive seasons (Díaz and Kitzberger, 2006; Díaz and Peris, 2011), and have even been observed feeding on animal carcasses (Supplementary Material 1 and 2). The seasonal food shortages they experience are thought to have driven this broader dietary niche relative to other parrots (Díaz and Kitzberger, 2006); the same study also reported that forest-dwelling birds were more frequently infected than those from open habitats, consistent with the forest ecology of E. ferrugineus.

Although no clinical signs were detected in any infected parakeet, this does not necessarily imply that infection is harmless. Haemosporidian infections can reduce reproductive success and survival in wild birds, but these effects are often subtle and become apparent mainly after experimental manipulation (Merino et al., 2000; Martínez-de la Puente et al., 2010); moreover, Leucocytozoon can be severely pathogenic in some avian groups, including waterfowl, pigeons, gallinaceous birds, raptors and ostriches (Valkiūnas, 2005; Forrester and Greiner, 2008). The chronic and relatively frequent infections detected here therefore leave the fitness consequences for E. ferrugineus an open question.

Our results underline the value of combining microscopy and molecular tools for the study of avian haemosporidians in this host: neither method alone captured all infections — two birds were positive on blood films but negative by PCR, whereas several PCR-positive birds lacked a corresponding blood film — so the two approaches proved complementary rather than redundant (Valkiūnas and Iezhova, 2023). Applying both systematically in future surveys, with lineage typing across the standard HaemFL–HaemR2L barcode region (Hellgren et al., 2004) and additional Giemsa-stained films from single-lineage infections, would extend the morphological description of L. australis and help resolve the status of the second lineage, ENIFER02 — whether an intraspecific variant or a distinct, co-occurring species — which will ultimately require an independent nuclear marker. Denser sampling across the host's range, extended to the sister species, the Choroy (E. leptorhynchus), and to other Andean forest birds, together with identification of the blackfly vectors and assessment of potential fitness effects, would further clarify the ecological role and evolutionary dynamics of Leucocytozoon in these southern forest ecosystems.

Conclusions

We describe Leucocytozoon australis sp. nov., the first Leucocytozoon species described from a wild Psittaciformes host, infecting the Austral parakeet across ∼1500 km of its range in the southern Andes. The parasite comprises two closely related, previously unreported cytochrome b lineages (ENIFER01, ENIFER02) that most parsimoniously represent intraspecific variation and co-infect some hosts. Clarifying the status of the second lineage, identifying the vectors, and evaluating the effects of infection on this endemic parrot remain priorities for future work.
