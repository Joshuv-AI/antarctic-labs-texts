High prevalence and multifactorial risks of sarcopenia in knee osteoarthritis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Tian-Xin Chen, Tong-Jie Yang, Sheng Cheng, Zhi-Long Zhang, Tao Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the prevalence and risk factors of sarcopenia in patients with knee osteoarthritis (KOA) through a systematic review and meta-analysis.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Scopus, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, and WanFang databases for studies published between January 2000 and June 2025. Eligible studies were screened according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Data were extracted, study quality was assessed, and meta-analyses were performed using Stata 17.0.ResultsA total of 35 studies involving 13,528 KOA patients were included, of whom 3597 were diagnosed with sarcopenia. The pooled prevalence of sarcopenia and sarcopenic obesity was 25.07% (95% CI 18.85–31.29%) and 12.06% (95% CI 8.24–15.87%), respectively. Subgroup analyses revealed that sarcopenia prevalence increased with disease severity, reaching 26.01% (95% CI 16.27–35.75%) in patients with Kellgren–Lawrence grade 4 KOA. Prevalence was 25.65% (95% CI 20.30–31.00%) among female and 25.85% (95% CI 18.64–33.06%) among Asian populations. Among patients with osteoporosis and diabetes mellitus, prevalence rates were 21.68% (95% CI 8.84–34.53%) and 28.36% (95% CI 17.43–39.29%), respectively. Additionally, sarcopenia was more common among alcohol consumers (30.72%, 95% CI 15.58–45.86%) and smokers (29.82%, 95% CI 18.87–40.77%), with the prevalence being notably higher at 35.20% (95% CI 28.09–42.31%) among patients with irregular physical activity.ConclusionSarcopenia and sarcopenic obesity are highly prevalent in patients with KOA and are significantly associated with disease severity, gender, geographic region, comorbidities, and lifestyle factors. These findings highlight the clinical importance of assessing sarcopenia in KOA management. Early screening and timely interventions may improve patient outcomes and quality of life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06391-x.Keywords: Knee osteoarthritis, Sarcopenia, Prevalence, Risk factors, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a prevalent chronic disorder characterized by progressive cartilage degradation, osteophyte formation, and synovial inflammation within the knee joint [1]. Epidemiological studies indicate that the prevalence of KOA has increased substantially in parallel with global population aging [2]. Recent studies report that the prevalence of KOA exceeds 10% among individuals aged 60 years and older, establishing it as a leading cause of disability in older adults [3]. KOA causes knee pain, stiffness, and restricted mobility, and in severe cases, can lead to loss of ambulation. This profoundly impairs quality of life and places a substantial burden on patients and their families. Moreover, KOA is frequently accompanied by complications such as periarticular muscle atrophy and osteoporosis. These complications further accelerate disease progression, thereby creating a vicious cycle that impedes patient recovery [4].

Sarcopenia is a geriatric syndrome characterized by the progressive and generalized loss of skeletal muscle mass with aging, frequently accompanied by declines in muscle strength and physical performance [5, 6]. Sarcopenia not only diminishes muscle strength and compromises knee joint stability but also increases mechanical loading on the joint, thereby accelerating degeneration of the articular cartilage. Furthermore, sarcopenia is strongly associated with adverse outcomes, including impaired balance, increased risk of falls, and reduced capacity for activities of daily living [7].

However, existing evidence regarding the prevalence and risk factors of sarcopenia in patients with KOA remains inconsistent [8]. Since the development and progression of both conditions are shaped by a complex interplay of inflammatory, metabolic, and mechanical factors, it is essential to investigate the contribution of major comorbidities (e.g., osteoporosis, diabetes) and lifestyle behaviors (e.g., smoking, alcohol consumption), which may serve as shared risk factors or aggravating conditions. Therefore, this study sought to comprehensively evaluate the prevalence and risk factors of sarcopenia in patients with KOA by conducting a systematic review and meta-analysis.

Materials and methods

Literature search

This study was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [9] and registered in the PROSPERO database (registration number: CRD420251068187). A comprehensive literature search was performed using the keywords “Sarcopenia” and “Knee Osteoarthritis” across the Web of Science, Cochrane Library, PubMed, EMBASE, CNKI (China National Knowledge Infrastructure), and WanFang databases. The search queries for each database are detailed in Supplementary Tables S1–S6. The search was restricted to studies published between January 1, 2000, and June 1, 2025. Eligible studies included journal articles and dissertations published in Chinese or English. In addition, the reference lists of systematic reviews published within the past three years were manually screened, and further relevant studies were identified through citation tracking.

Inclusion criteria

① Study population: Patients aged ≥ 50 years diagnosed with KOA [10]. ② Study design: Cross-sectional studies reporting the prevalence or number of individuals with sarcopenia, either directly or indirectly. ③ Diagnostic criteria: KOA was diagnosed according to the American Rheumatism Association criteria [11] or a Kellgren–Lawrence grade ≥ 2 [12], whereas sarcopenia was diagnosed according to the criteria established by the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) [13], the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP) [14], the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health (FNIH) [15], or other comparable criteria. ④ Outcomes: Prevalence of sarcopenia and sarcopenic obesity, noting that the diagnostic criteria for obesity varied by region (e.g., BMI ≥ 25 in Asia and ≥ 30 in Europe) among patients with KOA.

Exclusion criteria

① Animal studies. ② Studies in which sarcopenia prevalence data could not be obtained. ③ Duplicate publications. ④ Reviews, case reports, conference abstracts, and similar non-original research.

Literature screening

Two researchers independently deduplicated the records. Subsequently, they screened titles, abstracts, and keywords against the inclusion and exclusion criteria, followed by a full-text review to make the final inclusion decisions. Any discrepancies were resolved by a third researcher through discussion. Data on the first author, year of publication, sample size, and number of sarcopenia cases were extracted and recorded in an Excel spreadsheet.

Quality assessment

Cross–sectional studies were assessed according to the criteria developed by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) [16]. This tool comprised 11 items, each scored 1 for “yes” and 0 for “no” or “unclear.” Scores of 8–11 indicated high quality, 4–7 indicated moderate quality, and 0–3 indicated low quality. Only studies rated as moderate or high quality were included in the quantitative analysis.

Statistical analysis

A meta-analysis was performed using Stata 17.0 to estimate the pooled prevalence of sarcopenia among patients with KOA, along with the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, the P-value, and prediction intervals. A fixed-effects model was applied when I2 < 50% and P > 0.1 [17]; otherwise, a random-effects model was employed. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to gender, comorbidities (e.g., osteoporosis, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia), diagnostic criteria, Kellgren–Lawrence grade, geographic region, and lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking, alcohol consumption) to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of the findings. Publication bias was assessed using the Begg–Mazumdar and Egger’s tests, with P-values > 0.05 indicating the absence of significant bias.

Results

Search results

A total of 1352 records were retrieved from databases and other sources, comprising 382 articles in Chinese and 970 in English. After removing duplicates, screening titles and abstracts, and reviewing the full texts, 35 studies were ultimately included. The study selection process is presented in Fig. 1.

Basic characteristics and quality assessment of the literature

A total of 35 studies were included [18–52], consisting of 31 English-language and 4 Chinese-language articles, and spanning 11 countries, including China, Korea, and Turkey. Collectively, these studies encompassed 13,528 patients with KOA, among whom 3597 were diagnosed with sarcopenia. The sample sizes of the included studies ranged from 18 to 1,865, with quality scores between 5 and 9, indicating that all were of moderate to high methodological quality. Meta-analysis results

Prevalence of sarcopenia

A total of 35 studies comprising 13,528 patients with KOA were included in the meta-analysis. Given the substantial heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 99.1%, P < 0.1), a random-effects model was employed for the meta-analysis. The pooled prevalence of sarcopenia among patients with KOA was estimated to be 25.07% (95% CI 18.85–31.29%) in the meta-analysis, as shown in Fig. 2. Additionally, the prediction interval for the prevalence of sarcopenia was calculated to be [0%, 74.01%], indicating that future studies could observe a prevalence within this range.

Meta-analysis of the prevalence of sarcopenia in patients with KOA

Prevalence of sarcopenic obesity

A total of 14 studies reported data on sarcopenic obesity, comprising 3565 patients with KOA, among whom 378 were diagnosed with sarcopenic obesity. The heterogeneity analysis revealed substantial variation among studies (I2 = 95.5%, P < 0.1), prompting the use of a random-effects model. The pooled prevalence of sarcopenic obesity among patients with KOA was estimated at 12.06% (95% CI 8.24–15.87%) in the meta-analysis, as shown in Fig. 3. Additionally, the prediction interval for the prevalence of sarcopenia was calculated to be [0%, 67.78%], indicating that future studies could observe a prevalence within this range.

Subgroup analysis

Disease characteristics

Regarding the Kellgren–Lawrence classification, the prevalence of sarcopenia among patients with KOA was 26.01% (95% CI 16.27–35.75%) in grade 4, higher than 19.74% (95% CI 11.53–27.95%) in grade 3 and 12.98% (95% CI 1.58–24.38%) in grade 2, as shown in Supplementary Figure S1. Among patients with KOA awaiting arthroplasty, the prevalence of sarcopenia was 25.36% (95% CI 19.17–31.65%), marginally higher than the 24.44% (95% CI 14.78–34.10%) observed in those not undergoing arthroplasty (Supplementary Figure S2). With respect to diagnostic criteria, sarcopenia defined according to AWGS 2019 showed the highest prevalence at 24.99% (95% CI 18.75–31.22%), compared with 19.60% (95% CI 12.46–26.74%) under the EWGSOP definition (Supplementary Figure S3). Subgroup analysis of the prevalence of sarcopenia in patients with KOA

Demographic information

With respect to gender, the prevalence of sarcopenia among female patients with KOA was 25.65% (95% CI 20.30–31.00%), higher than the 22.22% (95% CI 16.09–28.34%) observed in male patients (Supplementary Figure S4). By geographic region, the prevalence of sarcopenia was highest among patients with KOA in Asia (25.85%, 95% CI 18.64–33.06%), followed by Europe and North America (20.20%, 95% CI 12.26–28.14%), and lowest in Africa (8.22%, 95% CI 3.08–17.04%), as shown in Supplementary Figure S5. Detailed results of the subgroup analyses are summarized in Table 2.

Comorbidities

Among patients with KOA and osteoporosis, the prevalence of sarcopenia was 21.68% (95% CI 8.84–34.53%), compared with 14.46% (95% CI 9.71–19.21%) in those without osteoporosis (Supplementary Figure S6). In patients with KOA and diabetes mellitus, the prevalence of sarcopenia was 28.36% (95% CI 17.43–39.29%), compared with 24.29% (95% CI 12.67–35.91%) in non-diabetic patients (Supplementary Figure S7). Among patients with KOA and hyperlipidemia, the prevalence of sarcopenia was 12.54% (95% CI 8.87–16.21%), compared with 31.92% (95% CI 7.88–55.96%) in those without hyperlipidemia (Supplementary Figure S8). In patients with KOA and hypertension, the prevalence of sarcopenia was 24.89% (95% CI 16.95–32.84%), compared with 27.14% (95% CI 13.59–40.70%) in those without hypertension (Supplementary Figure S9). The results of these comorbidity-based subgroup analyses are summarized in Table 2.

Lifestyle factors

Among patients with KOA who consumed alcohol, the prevalence of sarcopenia was 30.72% (95% CI 15.58–45.86%), compared with 27.63% (95% CI 17.57–37.69%) in non-drinkers (Supplementary Figure S10). Regarding smoking status, the prevalence of sarcopenia was 29.82% (95% CI 18.87–40.77%) among smokers, compared with 25.01% (95% CI 15.71–34.31%) in non-smokers (Supplementary Figure S11). In terms of physical activity, the prevalence of sarcopenia was 22.86% (95% CI 9.97–35.96%) among patients with regular physical activity, whereas it was notably higher at 35.20% (95% CI 28.09–42.31%) in those with irregular physical activity (Supplementary Figure S12). The results of the lifestyle-related subgroup analyses are summarized in Table 2.

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

Sensitivity analyses and publication bias assessments were conducted using the overall prevalence of sarcopenia as the primary indicator. The sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the findings of this study were robust, as exclusion of any single study did not substantially affect the overall results (Fig. 4). Egger’s test yielded P = 0.23, and Begg’s test yielded P = 0.08, indicating no significant evidence of publication bias.

Discussion

KOA and sarcopenia are highly prevalent geriatric syndromes that substantially compromise quality of life and physical function in older adults. In recent years, a growing body of evidence has underscored the association between these two conditions. However, discrepancies and uncertainties remain regarding the reported prevalence of sarcopenia and its associated risk factors among patients with KOA [4]. In this study, we performed a comprehensive meta-analysis to estimate the prevalence of sarcopenia and its subtype, sarcopenic obesity, in patients with KOA, and to identify related risk factors. The results showed that the overall prevalence of sarcopenia among patients with KOA was 25.07%, whereas the prevalence of sarcopenic obesity was 12.06%.

The subgroup analyses in this study revealed that disease-related factors—such as KOA severity and preoperative status for arthroplasty—substantially influence the prevalence of sarcopenia. The Kellgren–Lawrence grading system is a standardized radiographic method for assessing KOA severity, categorizing disease progression from grade 0 (normal) to grade 4 (severe). This system facilitates the evaluation of joint degeneration and guides individualized treatment planning [12]. As KOA severity increases, the phase angle decreases, reflecting reduced quadriceps strength [53], likely attributable to joint pain, limited mobility, systemic inflammation, and metabolic dysfunction in patients with KOA [54]. Our findings align with prior research [18, 26, 36], demonstrating a clear trend of increasing sarcopenia prevalence with higher Kellgren–Lawrence grades. Arthroplasty is the primary treatment for end-stage KOA, the utilization of which is rising with an aging global population. This study observed a higher prevalence of sarcopenia among patients with KOA awaiting arthroplasty compared with those not scheduled for surgery, potentially attributable to chronic pain, reduced mobility, systemic inflammation, metabolic disorders, and obesity [55]. Moreover, sarcopenia may represent a risk factor for suboptimal postoperative functional recovery [56–58]. Therefore, preoperative assessment of sarcopenia-related characteristics is essential for designing individualized rehabilitation strategies [59, 60]. Furthermore, the diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia vary. Liao et al. [61] reported a higher prevalence of sarcopenia when applying the AWGS criteria compared with the EWGSOP standards in the same population. Similarly, our study demonstrated that the prevalence of sarcopenia was higher when defined by the AWGS 2019 criteria than by the EWGSOP criteria. The AWGS 2019 criteria incorporate a more comprehensive evaluation of muscle mass, strength, and physical performance, specifically tailored to Asian populations. By contrast, the EWGSOP criteria are derived from European data. Therefore, the choice of diagnostic criteria should be tailored to the specific study population and research objectives [62].

This study identified both gender- and region-related variations in the prevalence of sarcopenia among patients with KOA, with higher rates observed in females and in Asian populations. With respect to gender, both males and females exhibited markedly reduced lower limb muscle strength across all stages of sarcopenia. However, females showed greater declines in hip abductor strength at the low physical function stage and more pronounced impairments in overall lower limb strength and balance. These differences may be attributable to lower muscle mass, hormonal influences such as estrogen levels, and variations in muscle activation patterns during physical activity [63]. Nonetheless, a meta-analysis of 68 studies reported inconsistent findings regarding the association between gender and sarcopenia [64]. Although our study observed gender-related differences, further research is warranted. Geographic variation was also evident, with the prevalence of sarcopenia highest in Asia, followed by Europe and the United States, and lowest in Africa. These disparities may be attributed to several factors, including differences in diagnostic criteria, environmental conditions, lifestyle, and ethnicity [65].

Patients with KOA frequently present with chronic comorbidities such as osteoporosis, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. This study demonstrated that patients with KOA and osteoporosis or diabetes mellitus exhibited a higher prevalence of sarcopenia, whereas those with hypertension or hyperlipidemia showed comparatively lower prevalence rates. Osteoporosis, KOA, and sarcopenia commonly coexist in older adults and have increasingly become a focus of geriatric research [66]. Basic research has shown that osteoporosis may exacerbate KOA progression by enhancing osteoclast activity in the subchondral bone [67]. In addition, population-based studies in Korea have reported a correlation between osteoporosis and KOA severity [68]. Moreover, a meta-analysis of 17 studies revealed that the risk of sarcopenia among individuals with osteoporosis was 2.63-fold higher than among those without, underscoring the need for proactive screening and prevention in this population [69]. Diabetes mellitus, a chronic metabolic disorder, has been positively associated with greater pain severity in patients with KOA [70]. When coexisting with KOA, diabetes further increases both the prevalence and severity of sarcopenia, often manifesting as reduced muscle strength (e.g., diminished handgrip strength), decreased physical activity, and reduced quality of life [71]. Hypertension, a cardiovascular disorder, has been significantly associated with the development of osteoarthritis, particularly of the knee joint. This association may be attributable to impaired joint perfusion combined with local mechanical stressors [72]. However, the relationship between hypertension and sarcopenia remains complex and not fully understood. While many studies have identified sarcopenia as a potential risk factor for hypertension [73, 74], the reverse association is less well understood. Endo et al. reported a negative correlation between hypertension and sarcopenia risk, possibly attributable to lifestyle modifications, such as diet and exercise, commonly adopted by hypertensive patients [75]. Hyperlipidemia, a lipid metabolism disorder, has been linked to increased knee pain and more severe KOA-related synovial inflammation [76, 77]. However, evidence directly addressing its association with sarcopenia remains limited. Our findings are consistent with those of a large meta-analysis on sarcopenia risk factors [64], suggesting that osteoporosis and diabetes mellitus may increase sarcopenia risk, whereas hypertension and hyperlipidemia appear to have no clear association.

Lifestyle factors play a pivotal role in the prevention and management of KOA and sarcopenia. In this study, patients with KOA who consumed alcohol or smoked exhibited a higher prevalence of sarcopenia [78, 79]. Alcohol consumption and smoking are widely recognized as harmful lifestyle behaviors and established risk factors for multiple diseases. The impact of alcohol consumption on KOA remains controversial. A meta-analysis of 29 observational studies identified an inverse association between frequent alcohol intake and osteoarthritis risk; however, this relationship lost significance after adjustment for confounding variables [80]. While the association between alcohol consumption and KOA remains uncertain, its detrimental impact on sarcopenia is increasingly well-established. Excessive alcohol intake may induce gut microbiota dysbiosis and autophagy-related hyperammonemia, thereby promoting muscle protein breakdown and inhibiting protein synthesis [81]. Conversely, another observational study reported no significant association between alcohol intake and sarcopenia risk, but highlighted the potential influence of smoking and dietary habits [82]. Although some studies have suggested that smoking may exert a protective effect against KOA [83, 84], a meta-analysis challenged this conclusion, attributing the observed effect to potential selection bias [85]. While smoking may appear to confer a protective effect on KOA, it has consistently been identified as a significant predictor of sarcopenia [86]. Smoking substantially increases the risk of sarcopenia in older adults, particularly among long-term smokers. By contrast, smoking cessation has been shown to mitigate this risk by alleviating mechanisms such as oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and capillary degeneration, which contribute to muscle aging and atrophy [87, 88]. Regarding physical activity, our findings indicate that patients with KOA who engage in regular physical activity exhibit a lower prevalence of sarcopenia compared to those with irregular physical activity. These results are consistent with the existing literature, which underscores the critical role of physical activity in preserving muscle mass and function [89, 90]. Regular physical activity, especially resistance training and weight-bearing exercises, has been shown to mitigate muscle wasting associated with sarcopenia by promoting muscle protein synthesis, enhancing muscle strength, and reducing systemic inflammation [91, 92]. In contrast, a sedentary lifestyle or irregular physical activity increases the risk of sarcopenia, as it contributes to muscle disuse atrophy, diminished mobility, and exacerbating systemic inflammation. Physical inactivity is also linked to a decline in muscle mass and function, which accelerates frailty and disability, particularly in older adults with KOA [50].

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this meta-analysis exhibited a high degree of heterogeneity. Such heterogeneity is not uncommon in prevalence meta-analyses, with a median I2 of 96.9% reported across 134 studies [93]. This high heterogeneity often reflects real-world clinical and methodological diversity rather than methodological flaws. To mitigate this heterogeneity and enhance the reliability of the findings, we performed both subgroup and sensitivity analyses. Although subgroup analyses, such as those focusing on osteoporosis, demonstrated a partial reduction in heterogeneity, significant variation remained within these subgroups. This is particularly evident in factors such as differences in diagnostic criteria, sample selection, and regional disparities among studies. These factors are likely key contributors to the observed heterogeneity. In addition, prediction intervals were employed to better capture and explore the uncertainty arising from substantial heterogeneity. Second, limitations in the available literature and data precluded a comprehensive evaluation of all potential risk factors. Moreover, the diagnostic criteria for certain variables (e.g., obesity, alcohol consumption) varied across studies, thereby limiting standardization. Furthermore, reliance on cross-sectional and case–control studies limited the ability to infer causality. Future validation through high-quality cohort studies and randomized controlled trials is warranted.

Conclusions

In summary, sarcopenia and sarcopenic obesity are highly prevalent among patients with KOA and are closely associated with disease severity, gender, geographic region, comorbidities, and lifestyle factors. These findings highlight the clinical significance of sarcopenia in KOA management. They emphasize the need for clinicians to evaluate sarcopenia risk and implement early detection and intervention strategies to improve patient outcomes and quality of life.
