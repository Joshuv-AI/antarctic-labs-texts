Reverse-BSDE Monte Carlo
Jairon H. N. Batista, Yuri F. Saporito, Flávio B. Gonçalves, Rodrigo S. Targino

Abstract
Recently, there has been a growing interest in generative models based on diffusions driven by the empirical robustness of these methods in generating high-dimensional photorealistic images and the possibility of using the vast existing toolbox of stochastic differential equations. In this work, we offer a novel perspective on the approach introduced in [ 15 ] , shifting the focus from a “learning” problem to a “sampling” problem. To achieve this, we reformulate the equations governing diffusion-based generative models as a Forward-Backward Stochastic Differential Equation (FBSDE), which avoids the well-known issue of pre-estimating the gradient of the log target density. The solution of this FBSDE is proved to be unique using non-standard techniques. Additionally, we propose a numerical solution to this problem, leveraging on Deep Learning techniques. This reformulation opens new pathways for sampling multidimensional distributions with densities known up to a normalization constant, a problem frequently encountered in Bayesian statistics.



1
Introduction

Generative models have emerged as powerful tools for learning complex data distributions, with applications spanning from image synthesis, natural language processing, to scientific modeling (
[
15
]
,
[
3
]
). Among the diverse approaches, diffusion-based generative models stand out due to their ability to generate high-dimensional, photorealistic data with remarkable fidelity. These models rely on stochastic processes to transform simple noise distributions into structured data, effectively capturing multimodal distributions that are challenging to represent with traditional methods.

A foundational work in this domain is
[
15
]
, which established a framework for training diffusion models by solving stochastic differential equations (SDEs) to approximate the underlying data distribution. This approach reframes the problem as one of learning a “score function” – the gradient of the data distribution’s log density – using the reverse SDE studied in
[
1
]
. While highly successful, most research inspired by this work has focused on improving the learning process, leaving the potential of these models for tackling sampling problems underexplored.

In this work, we take a different perspective by shifting the focus from “learning” the data distribution to “sampling” from it. Specifically, we reinterpret the generative process as a Forward-Backward Stochastic Differential Equation (FBSDE), a framework commonly used in fields like stochastic control and financial mathematics
[
16
]
. This reformulation allows us to directly address the problem of sampling from high-dimensional distributions where the density is only known up to a normalization constant—a scenario frequently encountered in Bayesian statistics and other probabilistic domains.

To make this approach practical, we propose solving the FBSDE numerically using Deep Learning
[
6
]
. By parameterizing the forward and backward components of the equation with neural networks, we develop a method that efficiently approximates the sampling process for complex, multidimensional distributions. This approach not only broadens the applicability of diffusion-based generative models but also bridges the gap between generative modeling and statistical sampling techniques.

There are a few works exploring the use of this framework for sampling, as, for instance,
[
10
]
and
[
13
]
. In both studies, the reverse SDE is employed to sample from a distribution known up to a normalization constant. The main challenge lies in estimating the score function. In
[
10
]
, the authors reformulate the score function as an expectation with respect to an appropriate distribution and then use Monte Carlo methods (such as Importance Sampling and Langevin Dynamics) to approximate this expectation. Meanwhile, in
[
13
]
, the author estimates the score function by solving the log-Fokker-Planck equation that the score satisfies, leveraging numerical methods for PDEs based on deep learning
[
12
]
.

Our contributions can be summarized as providing a novel perspective on diffusion-based generative models by re-framing the generative process as an FBSDE for the sampling problem. Additionally, we investigate numerical methods based on Deep Learning to solve the FBSDE, and apply this method in some experiments.

2
Reverse-BSDE Monte Carlo method

2.1
Literature review

In this section we review the methodology of
[
15
]
, which is based on the well-known result of Anderson
[
1
]
, when applied to the problem of sampling from
p_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})=\pi(\boldsymbol{x})/c
, when
c
is an unknown constant, but
\pi
is computable at any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Under suitable regularity conditions on
p_{0}
, the authors in
[
9
]
, stated that if
\beta:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is a continuous function and
(\mathcal{X}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the following diffusion process,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}d\mathcal{X}_{t}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\beta^{2}(t)\mathcal{X}_{t}dt+\beta(t)dW_{t},\\
\mathcal{X}_{0}\sim p_{0},\end{cases}

(1)

where
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a
n
-dimensional Brownian motion independent of
\mathcal{X}_{0}
, then the reverse process
\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{t}=\mathcal{X}_{T-t}
is also a diffusion process, and satisfies

\displaystyle d\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{t}=(\tfrac{1}{2}\bar{\beta}^{2}(t)\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{t}+\bar{\beta}^{2}(t)\nabla\log p(T-t,\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{t}))dt+\bar{\beta}(t)d\overline{W}_{t},

(2)

where
\bar{\beta}(t)=\beta(T-t)
,
p(t,\cdot)
is the density of
X_{t}
,
(\overline{W}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is another
n
-dimensional Brownian motion, and
\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
is independent of
(\overline{W}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. More complex dynamics could be considered, as state in Appendix
B
.

Based on this result, the idea of the sampling algorithm in
[
15
]
is that, if we sample
\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
from a standard Gaussian, then
\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{T}
will be approximately distributed as
p_{0}
, since the limiting distribution of
\mathcal{X}
is standard Gaussian. The main challenge in applying a numerical method in (
2
) is to estimate the value of the score function
\nabla\log p
. In
[
15
]
, under a different situation where one has iid samples from
p_{0}
, the authors rewrite the
L^{2}
-loss of estimating the score with a function
s(t,x)
so only samples of
p_{0}
are needed.

For our setting, when
p_{0}
is computable up to a normalizing constant, one needs to consider a different approach. Note that, under regularity conditions,
p
is the unique smooth solution of the Fokker-Planck PDE:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p(t,\boldsymbol{x})-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}(t)\left[\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\nabla p(t,\boldsymbol{x})+n\,p(t,\boldsymbol{x})+\Delta p(t,\boldsymbol{x})\right]=0,

(3)

with
p(0,\boldsymbol{x})=p_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})
. One could pursue a numerical solution of this Fokker-Planck PDE to estimate the score
\nabla\log p
. However, this could be challenging since we do not have access to
p_{0}
because of the unknown normalizing constant. Hence, we consider the following auxiliary function

u(t,\boldsymbol{x})=\log p(T-t,\boldsymbol{x})-\frac{n}{2}\int_{0}^{T-t}\beta^{2}(s)ds+\log c.

(4)

One can directly verify that
u
solves the following
semi-linear
PDE:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(t,\boldsymbol{x})+

\displaystyle\bar{\beta}^{2}(t)\left[\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}+\nabla u(t,\boldsymbol{x})\right]\cdot\nabla u(t,\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\bar{\beta}^{2}(t)\Delta u(t,\boldsymbol{x})-\frac{1}{2}\|\bar{\beta}(t)\nabla u(t,\boldsymbol{x})\|^{2}=0,

(5)

with
u(T,\boldsymbol{x})=\log\pi(\boldsymbol{x})
. Numerically solving this PDE is an interesting approach to compute the score since the final condition is known. However, exploring this equation in high-dimensions can be hard, which suggests the use of methods based on deep learning, as in
[
14
]
and
[
12
]
. This precise strategy was followed in
[
13
]
.

Another way to represent the value of the score was given in
[
10
]
. Assuming
\beta^{2}\equiv 2
for simpler exposition:

\displaystyle\nabla\log p(t,\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\sim\rho_{t}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{x})}\left[\pi(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\right]}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\sim\rho_{t}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{x})}\left[\frac{e^{-t}\boldsymbol{x}_{0}-\boldsymbol{x}}{(1-e^{-2t})}\cdot\pi(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\right],

where

\displaystyle\rho_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}|\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(-\frac{\left\|\boldsymbol{x}-e^{-t}\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right\|^{2}}{2\left(1-e^{-2t}\right)}\right).

Then
[
10
]
uses Langevin Dynamics to estimate the first expected value or use self-normalized importance sampling to estimate the ratio of expected value in the second equality.

2.2
Our method

Differently from the aforementioned references, we propose here a novel approach to explore the PDE in (
2.1
) by recasting it as a
forward-backward stochastic differential equation (FBSDE)
; see Appendix
A
for a primer on BSDEs.

Indeed, if we denote
(\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{t},u(t,\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{t}),\bar{\beta}(t)\nabla u(t,\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{t}))
by
(X_{t},Y_{t},Z_{t})
, then using Itô’s formula, we can directly verify that

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dX_{t}=(\tfrac{1}{2}\bar{\beta}^{2}(t)X_{t}+\bar{\beta}(t)Z_{t})dt+\bar{\beta}(t)d\overline{W}_{t}\\
-dY_{t}=-\frac{1}{2}\|Z_{t}\|^{2}dt-Z_{t}d\overline{W}_{t}\\
Y_{T}=\log\pi(X_{T}),\mbox{ and }X_{0}=\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{0}=\mathcal{X}_{T},\end{cases}

(6)

which relates the reverse SDE (
2
) to the FBSDE in (
6
).

The advantage of equation (
6
) is that it eliminates the need to pre-estimate the value of the score
\nabla\log p
. Consequently, to sample from
p_{0}
, it suffices to numerically solve this FBSDE.

Main Results

We now present the results that supports the use of the FBSDE in (

) to generate samples from
p_{0}

p_{0}
based a numerical approximation that uses a neural network to estimate the score function. Finally, a result that provides an upper bound for the approximation error is presented.

We now make the connection between the reverse SDE (

.

(A.1)

(Integrability of

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{p_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})^{2}}{(1+|\boldsymbol{x}|^{2})^{\lambda}}d\boldsymbol{x}<+\infty.

(7)

(A.2)

(Smoothness of

C^{1,2}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{n})

(Novikov’s condition):

.

Assumption (A.1) holds if

Lemma 1
.

Suppose Assumption (A.1) holds, and let

be any continuous functions. If

is defined as in Equation (

.

Suppose that assumptions (A1)-(A3) hold. Then, the FBSDE in (

(X,Y,Z)
that is adapted with respect to filtration of
(\overline{W})_{t\geq 0}
, such that

satisfies the Novikov’s condition. Furthermore, the unique solution is given by

(\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{t},u(t,\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{t}),\bar{\beta}(t)\nabla u(t,\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{t})).

In particular,
X_{T}\sim p_{0}
.

The idea of the proof is to verify that
(\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{t},u(t,\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{t}),\bar{\beta}(t)\nabla u(t,\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{t}))
is a solution of the FBSDE in (

is smooth and well-defined, allowing us to apply Itô’s formula to conclude that

(\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{t},u(t,\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{t}),\bar{\beta}(t)\nabla u(t,\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{t}))

The most challenging part of the proof is establishing the uniqueness of the solution. Notably, this is not straightforward under the standard theory of BSDEs, as the generator function exhibits quadratic growth and the terminal condition is unbounded. For further non-standard results on uniqueness of BSDEs, see

5

4
The Algorithm

Notice that, by Theorem
1
,
X_{T}=\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{T}=\mathcal{X}_{0}\sim p_{0}
. Therefore, to explore our FBSDE formulation of the reverse SDE given in Eq. (
6
), we need to discuss the simulation of fully-coupled FBSDEs. There are several methods to choose from, see for instance the survey in
[
4
]
.

Here, we present the Deep BSDE method proposed in
[
6
]
. Let
0=t_{0}<\cdots<t_{N}=T
be a partition of
[0,T]
with
\Delta t=t_{i}-t_{i-1}
. The algorithm is based on the Euler-Maruyama discretization of the FBSDE:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}X_{i+1}^{(N)}=X_{i}^{(N)}+(\tfrac{1}{2}\bar{\beta}^{2}_{t_{i}}X_{i}^{(N)}+\bar{\beta}_{t_{i}}Z_{i}^{(N)})\Delta t+\bar{\beta}_{t_{i}}\Delta W_{t_{i}},\\
Y_{i+1}^{(N)}=Y_{i}^{(N)}+\tfrac{1}{2}\|Z_{i}^{(N)}\|^{2}\Delta t+Z_{i}^{(N)}\Delta W_{t_{i}},\\
Z_{i}^{(N)}=\tfrac{T-t_{i}}{T}\mathbb{NN}(t_{i},X_{i}^{(N)};\theta_{Z})+\tfrac{t_{i}}{T}\bar{\beta}_{t_{i}}\nabla g(X_{i}^{(N)}),\\
Y_{0}^{(N)}=\mathbb{NN}(t_{0},X_{0}^{(N)};\theta_{Y}),\end{cases}

(8)

where
\mathbb{NN}(\cdot;\theta)
is a neural network with parameters
\theta
,
\Delta W_{t_{i}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}N(0,\Delta t)
and
\bar{\beta}_{t_{i}}=\beta(T-t_{i})
. The parameterizing choice for
Z^{(N)}
guarantees
Z_{N}^{(N)}=\bar{\beta}(T)\nabla g(X_{N}^{(N)})
, as the exact
Z
also satisfies.

For a fixed simulation size
M
and given
\theta_{Y}
and
\theta_{Z}
, we run (
8
), from
i=0
to
N-1
, compute the loss function

\displaystyle L(\theta_{Y},\theta_{Z})=\frac{1}{M}\|Y_{N}^{(N)}-\log\pi(X_{N}^{(N)})\|^{2},

(9)

where
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidian norm in
\mathbb{R}^{M}
(i.e.
L
is the mean-squared loss over the simulated values), and perform one step of gradient descent:

\displaystyle\theta_{Y}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\theta_{Y}^{(k)}-\eta_{k}\nabla_{\theta}L(\theta_{Y}^{(k)},\theta_{Z}^{(k)}),

(10)

\displaystyle\theta_{Z}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\theta_{Z}^{(k)}-\eta_{k}\nabla_{\theta}L(\theta_{Y}^{(k)},\theta_{Z}^{(k)}).

(11)

We then repeat the procedure above
K
times.

This algorithm is theoretically supported by the following theorem:

Theorem 2

(from
[
8
]
)
.

Under the same assumptions as
[
8
]
, for any given
(Y^{0}_{t},Z_{t}^{0})_{t\in[0,T]}
, there exists
C>0
, independent of
\Delta t
and
n
, such that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[|X_{t}-X_{t}^{(N)}|^{2}+|Y_{t}-Y_{t}^{(N)}|^{2}\right]+\mathds{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}|Z_{t}-Z_{t}^{(N)}|^{2}dt\right]

(12)

\displaystyle\leq C(\Delta t+\mathds{E}[|\log\pi(X_{T}^{(N)})-Y_{T}^{(N)}|^{2}],

(13)

where
(X,Y,Z)
is the unique adapted solution of equation (
6
) and
(X^{(N)},Y^{(N)},Z^{(N)})
is the constant-by-parts version of the discrete time approximation in (
8
).

This result tells us that minimizing the loss function (
9
) is meaningful, as the discretization error of the entire path of
X,Y
and
Z
vanishes as
\Delta t
goes to zero along with the approximation error of the final condition. The norm of the left-hand side of the inequality is the standard practice in the BSDE literature.

Appendix A
On Backward Stochastic Differential Equations

A Backward Stochastic Differential Equation (BSDE) is a stochastic differential equation where the final value is known (albeit random). Its study started during the 1970s in the stochastic optimal control literature. There are several textbooks on the subject, as for instance,
[
16
]
.

Naively, one could think that integration of the usual SDE from
t
to
T
would give a first guess of what a BSDE would be:

X_{t}=X_{T}-\int_{t}^{T}\mu(s,X_{s})ds-\int_{t}^{T}\sigma(s,X_{s})dW_{s}.

The problem is that the right-hand side is
\cF_{T}
-measurable and the left-hand side must be
\cF_{t}
-measurable, where
(\cF_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is the Brownian filtration. Hence, we cannot choose
\sigma
arbitrarily. In fact, it is necessary to allow the volatility process to be part of the solution. More precisely, we must consider

\displaystyle Y_{t}=\xi+\int_{t}^{T}f(s,Y_{s},Z_{s})ds-\int_{t}^{T}Z_{s}dW_{s},

(14)

where
\xi\in L^{2}(\cF_{T})
. Notice that
\xi
is the final condition for
Y
:
Y_{T}=\xi
. The function
f
is called the generator of the BSDE. A solution for this BSDE is a pair
(Y_{t},Z_{t})
,
adapted to

\cF
, and that satisfies the equation above.

It is important to notice two things: being adapted to
(\cF_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is the most important requirement. If we would drop it, the BSDE would have trivial solutions like
Y_{t}=\xi
and
Z_{t}=0
(in the case of
f(s,0,0)\equiv 0
). Moreover, if
\xi\in\cF_{0}
, this previous argument would hold and the trivial solution would be valid. Therefore, it is paramount to have
\xi
measurable with respect to
\cF_{T}
and the pair
(Y,Z)
adapted to
(\cF_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
.

Notice as well that BSDE (
14
) can also be written as an SDE (with unknown initial value):

\displaystyle Y_{t}=Y_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}f(s,Y_{s},Z_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}Z_{s}dW_{s},

(15)

or

dY_{t}=-f(t,Y_{t},Z_{t})dt+Z_{t}dW_{t}.

A
Forward
-BSDE (FBSDE) adds the dynamics of a SDE to specify the dynamics of the final condition
\xi
and the driver
f
:

\displaystyle X_{t}=x+\int_{0}^{t}\mu(s,X_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s,X_{s})dW_{s},

(16)

\displaystyle Y_{t}=g(X_{T})+\int_{t}^{T}f(s,X_{s},Y_{s},Z_{s})ds-\int_{t}^{T}Z_{s}dW_{s}.

(17)

Additionally, one could also consider that
(Y,Z)
impacts the drift and volatility of the forward process. This would be called fully-coupled FBSDE.

Appendix B
Reverse SDE

The existence of the reverse SDE first proposed in
[
1
]
was also studied in
[
9
]
with simpler assumptions that are easier to verify. We state them here under the more general case:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}d\mathcal{X}_{t}=b(t,\mathcal{X}_{t})dt+\sigma(t)dW_{t},\\
\mathcal{X}_{0}\sim p_{0}.\end{cases}

In this case, the reverse SDE holds under the following assumptions on
b
and
\sigma
:

(a)

There exists
K>0
such that for all
t\in[0,T]
and
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\R^{n}

\|b(t,\boldsymbol{x})-b(t,\boldsymbol{y})\|\leq K\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|

and

\|b(t,\boldsymbol{x})\|+|\sigma(t)|\leq K(1+\|\boldsymbol{x}\|).

(b)

There exists
\alpha>0
such that
\sigma(t)^{2}\geq\alpha
for all
t\in\R
and
\boldsymbol{x}\in\R^{n}
.

(c)

Assumption (A.1) holds.

The main theorem in
[
9
]
states that, under these assumptions, the reverse process
\overline{\mathcal{X}}
satisfies

d\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{t}=\left(-b(T-t,\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{t})+\sigma^{2}(T-t)\nabla\log p(T-t,\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{t})\right)dt+\sigma(T-t)d\overline{W}_{t}.

Proof of Lemma
1
.

In our case,
b(t,\boldsymbol{x})=-\frac{1}{2}\beta(t)^{2}\boldsymbol{x}
, and
\sigma(t)=\beta(t)
. Since
\beta(t)
is a continuous positive function defined in a compact, there is
A>0,\alpha>0
such that
0<\alpha\leq\beta(t)\leq A
. Hence, the condition
(a)
holds since:

\|b(t,\boldsymbol{x})-b(t,\boldsymbol{y})\|=\frac{1}{2}\beta(t)^{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|\leq\frac{A^{2}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|,

\|b(t,\boldsymbol{x})\|+|\sigma(t)|\leq\frac{1}{2}A^{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|+A,

and condition
(b)
holds because
\sigma(t)^{2}\geq\alpha^{2}
. Obviously, condition
(c)
holds under our assumptions. This concludes the proof.
