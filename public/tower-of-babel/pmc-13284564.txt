Gastrointestinal-Predominant ALK-Negative Systemic Anaplastic Large Cell Lymphoma Presenting as Fever of Unknown Origin: Case Report
Authors: Chang Hyun Kim, Sang Hyub Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALCL) is a CD30-positive mature T-cell lymphoma. ALK-negative systemic ALCL typically affects older adults, with aggressive clinical behavior. Predominant gastrointestinal involvement is rare and may present with nonspecific systemic symptoms, posing diagnostic challenges, particularly when manifesting as fever of unknown origin. We report a case of a patient with ALK-negative systemic ALCL and predominant gastrointestinal involvement. The patient had a history of indolent cutaneous extranodal marginal zone B-cell lymphoma. The patient presented with a prolonged, intermittent fever and constitutional symptoms. The initial evaluations, including computed tomography, 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography, and bone marrow examination, were not diagnostic. The persistent fever, which was accompanied by newly developed gastrointestinal symptoms and progressive hypoalbuminemia, prompted an endoscopic evaluation that revealed multiple ulcerofungating gastric lesions. Histopathological examination demonstrated the diffuse proliferation of large atypical lymphoid cells with high CD30 expression, no ALK expression, a high Ki-67 proliferation index, and clonal T-cell receptor gene rearrangement. These findings were consistent with ALK-negative systemic ALCL. Staging then revealed multifocal gastrointestinal involvement. The patient was treated with brentuximab vedotin plus cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and prednisone, following which the febrile episodes improved. This case illustrates an uncommon gastrointestinal-predominant presentation of ALK-negative systemic ALCL presenting as fever of unknown origin. This case underscores the importance of continued diagnostic reassessment and symptom-guided tissue acquisition when conventional staging studies are not initially diagnostic, particularly in patients with a history of indolent lymphoma.Keywords: Anaplastic large cell lymphoma, Anaplastic lymphoma kinase, Gastrointestinal diseases, Fever of unknown origin

INTRODUCTION

Anaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALCL) is a distinct subtype of mature T-cell lymphoma characterized by uniform expression of CD30 and variable clinical behavior [1,2]. According to the 5th edition of the World Health Organization classification, systemic ALCL is subdivided into ALK-positive and ALK-negative entities, with ALK-negative disease typically affecting older adults and showing a more aggressive clinical course and poorer prognosis [2-4]. ALK-negative systemic ALCL frequently presents with advanced-stage disease and constitutional B symptoms; however, primary or predominant gastrointestinal involvement is uncommon, and initial diagnostic evaluations may be unrevealing [1,5,6]. In addition, ALK-negative ALCL represents a genetically heterogeneous entity, with recurrent alterations involving genes such as TP63, DUSP22, and epigenetic regulators, contributing to variable clinical outcomes and diagnostic complexity [2,4]. Fever of unknown origin (FUO) remains a challenging clinical presentation, particularly in patients with a history of indolent lymphoma, in whom recurrent or transformed disease must be carefully distinguished from infectious, inflammatory, or unrelated malignant causes [7,8]. Delayed or atypical extranodal manifestations may further obscure the diagnosis, especially when initial imaging and bone marrow evaluations are negative [1,6,7]. Here, we report a rare case of ALK-negative systemic ALCL with predominant gastrointestinal involvement, initially presenting as prolonged FUO in a patient with a prior history of indolent cutaneous extranodal marginal zone B-cell lymphoma. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges posed by atypical clinical presentation, the limitations of conventional staging studies in early disease, and the importance of appropriate histologic evaluation guided by newly emerging clinical clues in patients with persistent FUO.

CASE REPORT

The patient was a 69-year-old woman with a history of extranodal marginal zone B-cell lymphoma of the skin, diagnosed three years earlier in 2022 after undergoing a wide excisional biopsy of a right flank skin lesion. Histopathologic examination demonstrated a CD20-positive B-cell lymphoma with a low proliferative index (Ki-67 approximately 10%). The patient was considered to be in complete remission without additional antilymphoma therapy and was followed with observation alone. The patient later presented with a 4-month history of intermittent fever. The fever reached up to 39.5°C and was accompanied by night sweats, showing a waxing and waning pattern at intervals of one to two weeks. Dyspnea on exertion and generalized fatigue were also noted. During this period, the patient reported intermittent, nonspecific abdominal discomfort without other prominent gastrointestinal symptoms. Laboratory evaluation revealed leukocytosis (14630/μL), anemia (hemoglobin 9.7 g/dL), and a platelet count of 337×103/μL. Inflammatory markers showed an elevated high-sensitivity C-reactive protein level of 1.78 mg/dL. Hypoalbuminemia (2.6 g/dL) and mildly elevated lactate dehydrogenase (220 IU/L) were also observed. Chest and abdominal computed tomography demonstrated no remarkable findings, and blood cultures were negative. Extensive infectious and rheumatologic evaluations were unrevealing, except for a low-titer antinuclear antibody (1:80). The patient had undergone esophagogastroduodenoscopy three years prior to the onset of FUO, which revealed no significant abnormalities other than a duodenal ulcer scar. Given the prior history of lymphoma, further evaluation for FUO was performed. 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) showed no significant hypermetabolic lesions. Bone marrow biopsy revealed normoto hypercellular marrow with a cellularity of 60%–80%, without abnormal lymphoid aggregates and with preserved normal hematopoiesis. Based on these findings, lymphoma-related B symptoms were considered unlikely, and the patient was managed with symptomatic treatment and close observation. Approximately four months later, intermittent fever persisted, and new gastrointestinal symptoms developed, including abdominal pain and diarrhea. Laboratory testing demonstrated worsening hypoalbuminemia (2.5 g/dL), raising suspicion for protein-losing enteropathy. Upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopic evaluations were therefore performed. Colonoscopy revealed no abnormal findings. Esophagogastroduodenoscopy revealed multiple ulcerofungating mass lesions measuring approximately 20 mm and 8 mm in the gastric antrum, and forceps biopsy were obtained from ulcerofungating lesions on the posterior wall and the greater curvature of the gastric antrum (Fig. 1). Histopathologic examination of both biopsy specimens demonstrated diffuse proliferation of large atypical lymphoid cells. Immunohistochemical staining showed focal positivity for CD3, negativity for CD20, positivity for CD4, negativity for CD8, strong positivity for CD30, and absence of ALK expression. The Ki-67 proliferation index was markedly elevated at approximately 90%. Granzyme B showed focal positivity, while CD56, CD103, and FOXP3 were negative. Epstein– Barr virus in situ hybridization was negative. TCR βF1 was positive, TCR δ was negative, and TCRγ gene rearrangement demonstrated monoclonality (Fig. 2). Next-generation sequencing identified mutations in DNMT3A, TET2, and TP53. These findings were consistent with ALK-negative systemic ALCL. Initial PET/CT showed no significant hypermetabolic lesions; however, subsequent PET/CT performed for baseline staging demonstrated multiple intense hypermetabolic lesions involving the gastric antrum, small bowel, and large bowel, consistent with lymphoma involvement (Fig. 3). Abdominal computed tomography revealed wall thickening of the gastric antrum, further supporting gastrointestinal involvement by lymphoma. Based on multifocal gastrointestinal involvement, the disease was classified as stage IV. The patient subsequently initiated systemic chemotherapy with brentuximab vedotin, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and prednisone (A-CHP) [9]. The patient is currently undergoing treatment, and fever improved after the first cycle of therapy.

DISCUSSION

This case demonstrates a rare clinical presentation of ALK-negative systemic ALCL with predominant gastrointestinal involvement, in which the diagnosis was established through endoscopic biopsy. ALK-negative systemic ALCL is an aggressive mature T-cell lymphoma that typically presents with advanced nodal disease and constitutional B symptoms [2-4]. Although extranodal involvement has been reported, gastrointestinal tract involvement as the predominant site of disease remains exceedingly uncommon [1,5,6]. In previously reported cases, gastrointestinal ALCL often manifested with nonspecific symptoms or was detected at a late stage of the disease course, frequently after repeated negative diagnostic evaluations [1,6]. Similarly, despite persistent FUO and systemic symptoms, repeated imaging studies, including computed tomography, PET/CT, and bone marrow examination, were initially non-diagnostic in the present case [6,7,10]. The definitive diagnosis was achieved through histologic evaluation of nonspecific ulcerative lesions identified on upper gastrointestinal endoscopy [1,5]. The endoscopic appearance was indistinguishable from more common gastrointestinal malignancies or inflammatory conditions; however, histopathologic examination and comprehensive immunophenotypic analysis revealed strong CD30 expression, absence of ALK expression, a high proliferative index, and T-cell clonality, supporting the diagnosis of ALK-negative systemic ALCL [2,3]. This case underscores that, even when conventional staging studies are negative, endoscopic evaluation with tissue acquisition can be critical when clinical suspicion persists. The diagnostic process in this case was further complicated by the patient’s prior history of indolent extranodal marginal zone B-cell lymphoma of the skin. In patients with a history of lymphoma, prolonged fever is often initially attributed to disease relapse, transformation, or treatment-related complications [7,8]. Such assumptions, however, may contribute to diagnostic anchoring and delay consideration of a biologically distinct secondary lymphoma [6]. In this patient, the absence of radiologic evidence of recurrence, lack of bone marrow involvement, and negative PET/CT findings initially suggested a low likelihood of lymphoma-related fever, thereby delaying diagnosis [6,7,10]. The absence of abnormal findings on initial computed tomography and PET/CT, despite the presence of febrile symptoms, may be attributed to a limited disease burden at that time, as gastrointestinal involvement by ALK-negative systemic ALCL can initially present as subtle lesions without overt mass formation or significant metabolic activity. With disease progression, expansion of the involved areas and an increased tumor burden likely contributed to the detection of hypermetabolic lesions on follow-up imaging. The subsequent development of gastrointestinal symptoms and hypoalbuminemia ultimately prompted endoscopic evaluation, which proved decisive [1,5]. Next-generation sequencing revealed mutations in DNMT3A and TET2, which are commonly observed in clonal hematopoiesis and have also been reported in subsets of T-cell lymphomas [2,4]. At present, there is insufficient evidence to establish a direct biological relationship between the prior indolent B-cell lymphoma and the subsequent development of ALK-negative ALCL. Rather, the observed molecular findings may reflect nonspecific background genetic alterations, the clinical significance of which warrants further investigation.

This case highlights a rare presentation of gastrointestinal-predominant ALK-negative systemic ALCL diagnosed by endoscopy and illustrates how a prior history of indolent lymphoma can complicate the diagnostic evaluation of persistent FUO. In patients with a history of lymphoma, persistent FUO should prompt ongoing reassessment and consideration of repeat or alternative tissue sampling based on evolving clinical features, rather than reliance solely on initial negative staging studies.
