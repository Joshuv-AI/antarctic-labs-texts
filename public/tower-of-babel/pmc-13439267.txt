Pilot-scale remediation, resource utilization, and long-term stabilization of cadmium, lead and arsenic co-contaminated soil
Authors: Jing Liu, Nian Liu, Yexun Li, Chengfeng Yang, Long Liang, Jianyu Gao, Yeqin Xu, Xiaoyong Zuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Soils contaminated with multiple heavy metals pose significant environmental and health risks due to their toxicity and persistence. Herein, the remediation efficiency of soil contaminated with complex mixtures of cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb), and arsenic (As) was investigated, along with the feasibility of resource utilization of the soil post-remediation through batch experiments, pilot-scale trials, and engineering applications. In the batch experiments, heavy metals (HMs) were effectively immobilized by the remediation agent, which consisted of a mixture of iron oxides and aluminum oxides. The remediation efficiency was positively linear correlated with the dose of the remediation agent. Considering the economy, a mass ratio of 10% of the remediation agent to soil was found to be the optimal solidification and stabilization of HMs. The leaching concentrations of Cd, Pb and As, were reduced from initial concentration of 517.9 µg/L, 1123.4 µg/L, and 73.7 µg/L to 85.7 µg/L, 427.5 µg/L, and 26.7 µg/L after solidification treatment. The stabilized soil was subsequently utilized for brick fabrication. At a 10% dose and a calcination temperature of 900 °C, the resulting bricks exhibited low heavy metal leachability, with Cd and Pb concentrations of only 2.5 µg/L and 3.6 µg/L, and As was not detected. X-ray diffractometer (XRD) analysis indicated that Pb was effectively incorporated into the feldspar structure, an common and environmental friendly mineral phase in the earth’s crust. In the pilot-scale trials, results confirmed the effective of solidification of heavy metals, with leaching concentrations of Cd, Pb, and As all below 0.1 mg/L in the soil remediation process. Leachability assessments of the bricks confirmed that HMs were effectively immobilized within mineral crystal structures during high-temperature sintering, while compressive strengths remained consistently high, ranging between 30.6 MPa and 32.4 MPa, exceeding the standard requirement of 10 MPa. In the engineering application, long-term verification was conducted after treatment. Even 2.5 years after treatment, the concentrations of Cd, Pb and As, in the remediated soil remained significantly below the remediation target values. Therefore, brick fabricate not only achieved the solidification and stabilization of HMs in the soil but also realized the resource utilization of the remediated soil, having certain economic benefits and significant environmental benefits.

Introduction

Soils contaminated with heavy metal(loid)s have become a widespread environmental issue that threatens infrastructure, food safety and human health1–5. In China, 82.8% of contaminated sites were significantly affected by heavy metal(loid)s (HMs), including mercury, cadmium, lead, chromium, arsenic, copper, nickel, and zinc, which was published by Ministry of Ecological Environment of P.R.C and Ministry of Land and Resources of P.R.C. in 20146–10. Previous studies have indicated that industrial activities such as mining, smelting, electroplating, battery manufacturing, and chemical production are major contributors to soil heavy metal pollution11,12. Different from organic contaminants, HMs cannot be biologically degraded and remain in soils, though their chemical form can change under different environmental conditions, affecting their toxicity and bioavailability13–15. Therefore, heavy metal contamination is characterized by concealment, long-term effects, and irreversibility. When HMs accumulate to a certain extent, it not only leads to a decrease in crop yield and quality but also contaminates surface water and groundwater through runoff and leaching, deteriorating the hydrological environment. Furthermore, it can directly poison plants or pose a threat to human health through the food chain16.

Given the severity and universality of heavy metal contaminated sites, some remediation technologies have been developed, including solidification/stabilization, soil thermal desorption, soil washing, phytoremediation, bioremediation, electrokinetic remediation, and so on17–22. Those techniques have several limitations and advantages of in terms of achieved degree of remediation, working mechanisms, costeffectiveness, practicality, and eco-friendliness17,23. Among these, solidification/stabilization technology has been widely promoted over the past decade due to the high treatment efficiency, widely available materials, low costs, and simple operation, making it one of the most extensively applied technologies for the remediation of heavy metal-contaminated sites globally. Moreover, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) has recognized Solidification/stabilization (S/S) as one of the best demonstrated available technologies for the treatment of 57 types of hazardous wastes24–26.

Solidification/stabilization involves the use of physical and chemical processes to stabilize and immobilize heavy metals within the soil, thereby reducing their mobility and making it difficult for the contaminants to leach out into the environment24,25. Recent studies have investigated the effects of different remediation agents, dosages, soil types, and environmental conditions on HMs immobilization, demonstrating significant reductions in leaching concentration. However, these studies remain at laboratory scale and focus on short-term outcomes, lack pilot-scale validation, and rarely evaluate the feasibility of resource utilization of the stabilized soils16,27,28. And few studies have addressed long-term leaching behavior and large-scale engineering applicability24. Despite its widespread use, the effective application of this remediation technique necessitates a thorough understanding of the specific characteristics of soil heavy metal contamination, identification of key influencing factors, and the selection of appropriate remediation agents. Additionally, the development of practical remediation methods to stabilize and immobilize heavy metals remains imperative.

However, the goal of remediating contaminated soil is not merely to comply with national standards; it should also consider how the soil can be sustainably utilized in the future12. The utilization of remediated soil that cannot be backfilled has gained attention from researchers. Currently, research is ongoing to explore further reuse options to reduce landfilling, remediated heavy metal-contaminated soil has been used for valuable metal recovery, greening soil, roadbed filler, and manufacturing lightweight aggregate materials29,30. Therefore, the utilization of remediated soil is a feasible way to solve the problem of soil pollution, which can effectively reduce environmental pollution risks and provide possibilities for the sustainable use of soil. Although the contaminated soil has been reutilized, the complex and variable physical properties of the soil, high heavy metal content, complex production processes, and low social acceptability result in a low comprehensive utilization rate and persistent issues of secondary pollution. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct in-depth research on the transformation and diffusion of heavy metals during the resource utilization process.

Herein, this study focused on industrial site contaminated with Cd, Pb, and As, and aimed to prove an efficient and operationally facile method for stabilization and immobilization of HMs in soil, and utilized the remediated soil. The real contaminated soil was collected as the study object. A detailed analysis of the speciation and concentrations of different heavy metals in soil was provided because forms significantly impacted the subsequent immobilization of heavy metals. The effect of different dose of remediation agents was investigated by laboratory-scale research to select effective remediation agents and ratios. The pilot was also operated to further demonstrate the results of batch-mode lab experiments. Through brick-making processes, the remediated soil was utilized for resource reuse, achieving sustainable soil utilization. The existence form, leaching concentration of HMs and brick compressive strength were measured to assess the migration and diffusion risks of heavy metals in the process of resource utilization, further reduce the secondary pollution. Compared with previous studies, this work provides new insights by combining lab-scale optimization, pilot-scale validation, and practical engineering application with resource recovery, offering a comprehensive evaluation of heavy metal stabilization and sustainable utilization for HMs contaminated soil.

Materials and methods

Project overview

The project was located in Zhuzhou City, Hunan Province, which was characterized by a subtropical monsoon humid climate. The average annual temperature ranged from 17.1 °C to 18.9 °C, and the average annual precipitation was 1447 mm. Ex situ solidification / stabilization and resource utilization of bricks technology were applied to treat 45,902 m3 of contaminated soil to a depth of 9 m. The experimental soil was sourced from this contaminated site. Soil sampling was conducted in 10 layers, with depths ranging from 0 to 9 m. Sampling points were arranged in a 20 m × 20 m grid for the suspected contamination area and in a 40 m × 40 m grid for the area of concern. For the batch experiment, pilot-scale trial, and total concentration analysis during long-term monitoring, Subsamples from each depth were mixed to form a composite sample. For depth-profile long-term monitoring, subsamples from the same depth were mixed separately to represent each soil layer. The main heavy metal pollutants present in the contaminated soil were Cd, Pb and As, with initial concentrations of 125.55 mg/kg, 1835.41 mg/kg, and 256.99 mg/kg, respectively, and an initial pH of 6.89. The collected samples were naturally air-dried, pestled through a 60-mesh sieve, and then stored in a light-protected and low-temperature (4 °C) environment for further using. Figure 1 showed the chronology of the experiments and analyses conducted in this study.

Experimental materials

The main compositions of the remediation agent used to treat the polluted soil were high iron oxide content red soil, alumina oxide, silver powder, quartz sand, and silica dioxide. And the contents of iron oxide and aluminum oxide were each approximately 30–45%. The shale and coal gangue used in the fabricate brick process were obtained from a local brick factory. During the soil remediation process, the remediation agents were added to the contaminated soil in different proportions. After a curing period of 8 days, the stabilized soil was used for fabricate brick by mixing with shale and coal gangue.

Soil remediation in batch experiment

The batch experiment was conducted in 1000 mL glass beaker equipped with the mechanical rotating paddle (200 r/min). All reactions were initiated by mixing1 kg of soil with remediation agent by stirring, adding 35% deionized water for thin film coverage, and curing for 8 days at room temperature. During the reaction, no pH adjustment was made. The impact of different remediation agent dosages (mass ratios of 2%, 5%, 8%, 10%, and 15% between the agents and soil) was investigated. After curing, the soil was air-dried at room temperature and sieved through a 60-mesh sieve before samples were taking for leaching tests31. The batch experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Pilot-scale and engineering remediation process

The pilot-scale remediation trial was conducted using engineering equipment and procedures in a on-site contaminated soil treatment center. Based on the optimal remediation agent dose of 10% determined from batch experiments, the contaminated soil was formed into treatment units measuring 10 m × 4 m × 2.5 m (≈ 100 m³). A total of 16 tons of remediation agent was applied in two stages (30% initially, followed by 70%), with mixing, crushing, and sieving performed using excavators. The treated soil was then transferred to a curing area for 5–7 days of stabilization. After sampling confirmed compliance with remediation standards, the soil was transported to a brick manufacturing plant for high-temperature recycling into construction bricks.

Based on the pilot-scale results, 45,902 m3 of contaminated soil was treated in the real engineering application, and the remediation agent dose was 10%.

Resource utilization of fabricate bricks

The remediated soil, shale, and coal gangue mixed in a ratio of 7:2:1 by mass. The mixtures were dosed into a ball mill with a certain amount of water to form a slurry, with stirring for 18 h. After drying, the mixture was pressed in a press machine with a pressure of 650 MPa to form cylindrical bodies with a diameter of 20 mm and a height of 5 mm. The cylindrical bodies were burned for 3 h in the temperature of 800–1000 ℃, then naturally cooled to room temperature. Some of the sintered cylindrical bodies were pestle in an agate mortar to a particle size smaller than 10 μm for subsequent characterization analysis and toxicity leaching tests.

A similar method was used for the engineering-scale brick production, where the bricks produced were larger and the equipment used was different.

Analysis and characterization methods

The soil samples were digested with HCL-HNO3-HF microwave airtight digestion technology to analysis the concentration of HMs32. And the leaching test in remediated soil was evaluated following the horizontal shaking leachate method30. Air-dried, homogenized, and sieved samples (100 g) were mixed with deionized water at a 10:1 L/kg ratio, sealed in 2 L inert extraction bottles, and shaken at 110 ± 10 cycles/min for 8 h at room temperature, followed by 16 h of static settlement. The leachate was then separated through 0.45 μm filtration and acidified for metal analysis.

According to the standard of “Solid waste-extraction procedure for leaching toxicity-sulphuric acid & nitric acid method” (HJ/T299-2007), the leaching toxicity of heavy metals for bricks was assessed for resource utilization. Samples were digested with a mixed H2SO4/HNO3 solution to simulate acidic leaching under landfill-like conditions, and the extracts were collected and subsequent analysed.

The chemical speciation of heavy metals in soil samples was determined using the BCR sequential extraction procedure30,33,34. Samples were dried, ground, and sieved. 1 g of sample was used for each extraction. The water-soluble fraction was extracted with 20 mL deionized water for 16 h, followed by centrifugation for 30 min; the weak acid-soluble fraction with 40 mL 0.11 mol/L acetic acid; the reducible fraction with 40 mL 0.5 mol/L hydroxylamine hydrochloride; and the oxidizable fraction with H2O2 and 50 mL 1 mol/L ammonium acetate under the same conditions. The residual fraction was obtained by complete digestion with HCl, HNO3, HF, and HClO4.

The total soluble Cd, Pb and As were determined by the method of inductively coupled plasma (ICP, Agilent 720 ES, USA) and acidifying with 4% ultrahigh purity HNO3.

XRD (D8 Advance X, Bruker) analyses were conducted with Cu Kα operated at 40 kV and 40 mA, with continuous scanning ranging from 10° to 90°, scan speed was 0.2 s/step, and scan step was 0.01°/step.

The compressive strength of the bricks was tested using a micro electromechanical servo pressure testing machine (Model: DYE-3000 S, Manufacturer: Zhongjiao Jianyi Corp.). The bricks were loaded at a rate of 0.1–0.2 MPa/s until failure occurred. The machine recorded the maximum load at failure to determine the compressive strength of the bricks.

Results and discussion

HMs content and speciation in contaminated soil

The concentrations of Cd, Pb, and As were analyzed, which were 125.55 mg/kg, 1835.41 mg/kg and 256.99 mg/kg, respectively, even exceeding the Class II land standards for construction land use in China (Table 1)35, where the permissible limits are 65 mg/kg, 800 mg/kg, and 60 mg/kg.

The existence speciation of HMs in soil are correlated with the pH of the environmental medium36,37. The original pH is 6.89. And the existence forms of HMs in soil are water-soluble form, exchangeable form, carbonate-bound form, iron-manganese-bound form, hydrochloric acid-extractable form, and residual form (Fig. 2). Among the five forms of HMs in the soil, the exchangeable form is the most active and easily released into the environment, with the highest mobility and bioavailability30. The carbonate-bound form is unstable under acidic conditions and is easily released into the environment for absorption and utilization. These two forms have great mobility and bioavailability due to weak metal-soil binding and are considered as the key forms of concern. Iron-manganese-bound HMs are soluble and released under reducing conditions. Organic-bound HMs are released under oxidizing conditions. Iron-manganese oxide-bound and organic-bound states are relatively stable and less mobile but still had some bioavailability under changing redox conditions. The residual form represented insoluble heavy metal elements that are generally unavailable for biological utilization.

According to the analysis using the sequential extraction method, residual is the main form for Cd, Pb and As, and follows by the form of iron-manganese oxide-bound form for Pb and As. The lowest fraction is the exchangeable form for Cd and Pb, and carbonate for As. Overall, although the HMs in the soil existed primarily in the residual state, the non-residual fractions still accounted for a considerable proportion, indicating that the HMs may still have potential mobility and transformation risks. Therefore, further treatment was required to ensure soil safety.

Therefore, the exchangeable and carbonate-bound forms of Cd, Pb, and As are not predominant but still account for a certain proportion in the soil. These relatively labile fractions indicate that part of the heavy metals may have potential mobility and bioavailability, thus posing environmental risks under changing soil conditions1,4,5. Thus, further stabilization treatment is still necessary to reduce the mobility and environmental risk of heavy metals in the contaminated soil.

Remediation of HMs-contaminated soil in batch experiment

The high concentration of HMs in the soli required further treatment. In this study, the remediation agent was added into soil to achieve the effect treatment for HMs. The toxicity leaching concentration of the soil after treatment was analysis (Fig. 3a-c). Results showed that when the mass ratio was below 8%, the leaching concentrations were 347.2 µg/L (2%), 275.2 µg/L (5%), and 185.7 µg/L (8%) for Cd. When the mass ratio was 15%, the leaching concentrations of Cd, Pb and As were 85.7 µg/L, 427.5 µg/L, and 26.7 µg/L, respectively, which were comparable to or lower than those reported in previous studies21, with Pb removal efficiency even exceeding that observed in certain cases38.

In addition, when the dose of remediation agent was less than 10%, the leaching concentrations of HMs were negatively linear correlated with the agent dose (Fig. 3d). That was, as the agent dose increased, the leaching concentrations decreased. But the leaching concentration tended to stabilize after the remediation agent dose reaches 15%. Thus, considering the remediation effect and the economic of agent, the optimal dose of remediation agent was 10%.

The observed decrease in leaching concentrations with increasing remediation agent dose demonstrates the efficacy of iron and aluminum oxides in adsorbing and immobilizing Cd, Pb, and As30,32,39,40. Notably, agent dosages above 10% result in HMs leaching concentrations within acceptable limits, suggesting that further addition of stabilizer yields minimal gains and would not be cost-effective. The results are comparable to or better than those in previous studies21,38, particularly for Pb immobilization, highlighting the practical effectiveness of the selected agent and optimal dose.

Resource utilization of soil remediation

Fabricate brick could effectively achieve the resource utilization of the remediated soil. Figure 4 shown the detail process of the fabricate brick42. The remediated soil, shale, and coal gangue mixed in a ratio of 7:2:1 by mass. The mixtures were pestled, mixed, molded, and calcined to form bricks. And XRD, compressive strength test and toxic leaching concentration are used to characterize the brick.

The crystalline structure and composition of the brick that sintered at different temperatures was revealed by XRD (Fig. 5). It could be observed that with the increase of the calcination temperature, the diffraction peaks intensity became sharper, indicating that the brick calcined at high temperature had a larger scattering domain size and higher crystallinity. The crystal phase and composition also transformed at different calcination temperatures. The main phases were α-PbO and β-PbO for 500 °C and 600 °C. When the temperature increased to 700–900 °C, a lead feldspar structure appears, mainly existing as lead feldspar structure. And the proportion of the feldspar gradually increased with the increase of temperature in 700–900 °C. When the temperature reached to 950 °C, the composition completely transformed to lead feldspar. What’s more, lead feldspar was widely and commonly distributed in the earth, and stable and environmental friendly43.

The compressive strength test of the brick calcined at 800 °C was shown in Fig. 6. The compressive strength was 32.80 Mpa and 35.01 Mpa for the remediation agent ration of 10% and 15%, meeting the strength standards of brick of 10 MPa44. Furthermore, the compressive strength was positively correlated with the dose of the remediation agent. And the larger dose of the agent, the bigger compressive strength.

The leaching of Cd and Pb in the bricks that calcined at 800 °C and 900 °C was analyzed in Fig. 7. Results showed that the immobilization effect for Cd and Pb at 900 °C was better than 800 °C. And with the increase of the dose of remediation agent, the leaching concentrations were gradually reduced, and the immobilization was improved. For As, the leaching concentration was not detected under the two calcination temperatures. When the calcination temperature was 800 °C, the leaching concentrations of Cd in the brick were 4.5 µg/L for reagent dosage of 10% and 4.1 µg/L of 15%, concentrations of Pb were 5.9 µg/L of 10% and 5.7 µg/L of 15%. When the calcination temperature was 900 °C, the leaching concentrations of Cd in the brick were 2.5 µg/L for reagent dosage of 10% and 2.4 µg/L of 15%, concentrations of Pb were 3.6 µg/L of 10% and 3.3 µg/L of 15%. Therefore, the optimal remediation agent addition ratio was 10% at a calcination temperature of 900 °C. This conclusion was consistent with the results of the batch soil remediation experiments, where a 10% remediation agent dosage was found to be the most effective for stabilizing HMs.

The transformation of Pb into a lead feldspar structure during high-temperature calcination provides not only chemical stabilization but also environmental compatibility. The compressive strength indicates that the bricks made from remediated soil meet engineering standards and have low leaching concentrations 41,44. This dual functionality demonstrates the potential of converting contaminated soil into a construction material. By combining stabilization and resource recovery, our work reduces pollution and enhances environmental outcomes.

Pilot-scale performance in soil remediation

The pilot study implemented on the heavy metal-contaminated soil from the Qing Shuitang Old Industrial Zone in Zhuzhou City, Hunan Province. The soil was contaminated with Cd, Pb, and As, with initial concentrations of 125.55 mg/kg, 1835.41 mg/kg, and 256.99 mg/kg, respectively, posing significant environmental risks. Based on the results of the batch experiments, the optimal dose of iron and aluminum oxides composite remediation agents was determined to be 10% of the soil mass. A total of 1,387 t of the stabilizer was applied to treat approximately 43,000 m3 of contaminated soil. The remediation process involved excavation, spreading the soil into approximately 100 m3 units, followed by a two-stage application of stabilizer (30% in the first stage, 70% in the second) to ensure more sufficient and homogeneous mixing with the contaminated soil, followed by mechanical mixing, screening, and forced homogenization (Fig. 8a-Figure 8f). This procedure helped improve the contact between the remediation agent and soil, which was beneficial for the stabilization process. The treated soil was then naturally reacted for 8 d to promote the stabilization of heavy metals in iron and aluminum oxides.

The pilot-scale results confirm that the stabilizer dosage optimized in the laboratory is effective when applied at a larger scale, with leaching concentrations of Cd, Pb, and As remaining well below the limits value. This indicates that the solidification/stabilization approach can be applied at scale and reliably reduce environmental risks in field applications, supporting the feasibility for further engineering implementation.

Long-term monitoring of remediated soil

The long-term monitoring was conducted to evaluate whether the remediated soil continued to meet the remediation target required for the engineering project. Figure 10a showed that even after 2.5 years post-remediation in the pilot site, the total concentration of Cd, Pb and As was measured at 0.71 mg/kg, 31.2 mg/kg, and 31.1 mg/kg in soil, well below the remediation target concentration of Class II Land Standards (Table 1). Figure 10b illustrated that the distribution of Cd, Pb and As across various soil layers after 2.5 years post-remediation was relatively uniform, with no significant fluctuations observing. The total concentrations of Cd, Pb and As at all depths remained at low levels, which were 4.45–6.17 mg/kg, 24.26–55.13 mg/kg, and 3.61–8.76 mg/kg, significantly below the remediation target values35.

Heavy metals are non-biodegradable, and the decrease in total metal concentrations should be attributed to a dilution effect by adding remediation agent, soil heterogeneity, excavation, mixing, and redistribution during engineering treatment. Therefore, the long-term monitoring results of total concentrations confirm that the remediated soil meet the engineering remediation target values after solidification treatment. And the applied solidification process could effectively control the environmental risk of heavy metals in the engineering application. Nevertheless, leached metal concentrations and/or bioavailable fractions should be further included in future long-term monitoring to more accurately evaluate the long-term stability and risk control performance of the remediated soil.

Mechanisms of HMs removal by remediation agents

The main compositions of the remediation agent used to treat the Cd, Pb and As polluted soil were iron oxide, aluminum oxide, quartz sand, and silica dioxide. Thus, the selection of iron oxide and aluminum oxide was primarily based on the fact that various forms of these compounds were essential constituents of soil and were non-toxic, thus minimizing the risk of secondary pollution. And iron oxide and aluminum oxide were well known sorption agent for heavy metals, which effectively removed the Cd, Pb and As from the polluted soil. Once iron oxide and aluminum oxide dosed into the soil, the heavy metals would transfer to the surface of the iron oxide and aluminum oxide39,40,45,46. During the adsorption process, the hydrogen atoms in the hydroxyl groups on the surface of iron oxide and aluminum oxide were replaced by Cd, Pb and As ions, forming stable coordination bonds. The conceptual model of the solidification of Cd, Pb and As by iron oxide and aluminum oxide was shown in Fig. 11.

The arsenic removal was primarily due to coordination adsorption and metal bridging46–50. Arsenic existed as arsenate or arsenite, which are negatively charged51. Iron oxide and aluminum oxide surfaces, both rich in hydroxyl groups (-OH), adsorb arsenic through ligand exchange, where arsenate anions replace the surface hydroxyl groups, forming stable complexes46–48,52. Additionally, arsenic can be co-precipitated during the iron oxide or aluminum oxide formation process (such as Fe3+ or Al3+ hydrolysis)48,49, where arsenate ions may be encapsulated or incorporated into the mineral structure, forming solid solutions or amorphous co-precipitates, leading to irreversible removal. Besides, Cd and Pb present in the soil may also contribute to arsenic adsorption through metal bridging50, where positively charged metal ions (cations) act as intermediaries, linking the negatively charged arsenic ions to the negatively charged surfaces of both iron and aluminum oxides.

The Cd and Pb ions removal processes included sorption and co-precipitation for iron oxides53. Iron oxide and aluminum oxide captured Cd and Pb by forming form surface complexes with hydroxyl groups on the surface of oxides, and fixed Cd and Pb ions on the surface through coordination bonds. Otherwise, the efficiency of Cd and Pb removal could also be contributed aided by the co-precipitation effects for iron oxide. And Cd and Pb ions also could react with hydroxide ions to form a trace of insoluble hydroxide precipitates in the iron oxide.

Conclusions

After immobilization and stabilization treatment, the HMs in the contaminated soil are effectively immobilized and can be used for brick production, achieving both remediation and resource utilization. During the soil remediation process, the optimal mass ratio of the stabilizing agent is found to be 10%. Following treatment, Cd, Pb, and As are effectively immobilized. During long-term monitoring, the total concentrations of Cd, Pb, and As remained below the regulatory limits of the Class II Land Standards after 2.5 years, indicating that the remediated site continued to meet the requirements for construction land use. The remediated soil is successfully utilized for resource-based brick production, with leaching concentrations of HMs and compressive strength meeting relevant standards. The crystalline phase in the bricks is lead feldspar, a stable and environmentally friendly phase.

These results show that the stabilization/solidification technology is effective in reducing the environmental risks of heavy metals and highlights the potential to use remediated soil in construction, supporting the circular economy and sustainable development. Future studies should focus on optimizing large-scale applications, including long-term monitoring of leached concentrations and/or bioavailable fractions, and addressing potential secondary pollution risks to promote the broader use of remediated soil.
