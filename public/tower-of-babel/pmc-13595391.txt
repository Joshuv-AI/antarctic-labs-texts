Effect of Skin Types and Microenvironment of Dermal Exposure to Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbon Derivatives and Heavy Metals
Authors: Siying Liu, Xinxin Li, Wanting Liang, Yinzuo Liu, Ting Wang, Hongjun Mao, Lei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dermal exposure is one of the main ways for the human body to adsorb atmospheric pollutants, and different skin types significantly affect the adsorption of pollutants. This study established a simulation device using polyethylene balloons coated with different doses of glycerol trioleate to simulate oily skin, neutral skin, and dry skin types and investigated the adsorption of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and their derivatives (NPAHs and OPAHs) and metal elements on different skin types. The results show that (1) the adsorption of NPAHs and OPAHs with different ring numbers conforms to the first-order kinetic model. The adsorption concentration of NPAHs and OPAHs by balloon-simulated skin is the highest on the oily surface, followed by the neutral surface, and the lowest on the dry surface. (2) In the four scenarios analyzed in this study, the adsorption of NPAHs and OPAHs by the skin showed that the adsorption amount was the highest in dusty weather and the lowest in the indoor area of the campus. In the indoor and outdoor environments of the campus, the adsorption amount of NPAHs was greater than that of OPAHs. (3) Among the 11 heavy metals detected, Cu was detected only in sand–blowing weather, while As, Zn, V, and Ti were detected in four cases, and their adsorption capacity was accorded with the order of V > Zn > As > Ti. Therefore, the health risks associated with skin contact with pollutants on oily skin and during sandy weather should be given more attention, and appropriate protective measures should be implemented.

Introduction

The pathways of human exposure to pollutants mainly include inhalation, ingestion (dust intake and dietary intake), and dermal exposure. , Although inhalation and ingestion are commonly considered to be the dominant routes of human exposure to pollutants, dermal exposure has gained increasing attention in recent years. Dermal exposure of the skin, the largest organ in the human body, makes an important contribution to personal exposure to atmospheric contaminants. However, the skin areas directly exposed to ambient air are typically limited to the face, neck, and hands. Nevertheless, dermal exposure remains an important route for pollutant uptake, especially for lipophilic organic compounds and particulate-bound substances that can adhere to or penetrate the skin surface. The concentrations of urinary metabolite induced by dermal exposure to low-molecular-weight phthalates and 2–butoxyethanol are approximately equal to or even greater than those of inhalation exposure. , Unlike respiratory exposurewhich involves continuous inhalation and rapid absorption across the alveolar epitheliumdermal exposure is characterized by slower but sustained uptake, particularly in occupational settings or highly polluted environments. Moreover, certain pollutants, such as nitrogen- and oxygen-containing polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (NPAHs and OPAHs), have been shown to have comparable or even higher dermal absorption capacity than inhalation. A toxicological risk assessment of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) in soil from a typical industrial park in Ningxia reported an incremental lifetime cancer risk (ILCR) of 4.5 × 10–6 for adults, with inhalation and dermal exposure identified as the primary pathways contributing to potential carcinogenic risk. Similarly, compared with other exposure routes, the contribution of daily clothing to dermal exposure dose cannot be ignored. These findings highlight the necessity of incorporating the dermal route in comprehensive health risk assessments. Therefore, understanding the relative contribution of dermal exposure under different environmental conditions and skin types is crucial for accurately assessing individual exposure and related health risks.

Among the diverse array of airborne pollutants, NPAHs and OPAHs have emerged as significant hazardous organic contaminants. NPAHs and OPAHs are important derivatives of PAHs that contain two or more functional groups. , OPAHs are derivatives of parent PAHs containing carbonyl functional groups, and NPAHs are formed by nitro substitution of parent PAHs, both recognized as hazardous organic pollutants. Toxicological studies have shown that certain NPAHs and OPAHs have more direct carcinogenic and mutagenic effects than their parent PAHs, posing a serious threat to human health. , NPAHs originate from the incomplete combustion of fossil fuels and can also be produced through photochemical reactions involving NO x . In the environment, NPAHs exist at concentrations that are 1 to 2 orders of magnitude lower than those of their parent PAHs, but their carcinogenic and direct mutagenic effects are 10 and 105 times higher than those of parent PAHs, respectively. Moreover, after being transformed into nitrohydroxy derivatives, they exhibit mutagenic and carcinogenic properties. In 2012, NPAHs were classified as Group 2 or Group 3 carcinogens by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC). OPAHs can be directly emitted along with PAHs through the combustion of fossil fuels, or they can be secondarily generated by the photolysis of PAHs or through chemical reactions with atmospheric oxidants (HO·, O3, NO3 –, and NO2). OPAHs are affected by many ″dead-end″ biological and chemical degradation pathways, are relatively stable and more polar, and thus persist in the environment for a longer time. In addition, metal elements are also significant air pollutants. Metal pollutants are a type of chemical substance with high toxicity and environmental persistence. Their accumulation and spread in the environment pose a serious threat to ecosystems and human health. Metal pollutants mainly originate from industrial emissions, traffic pollution, agricultural activities, and natural processes. Skin contact with metals in the human body may pose potential harmful risks. The main reason for the harmful effects of environmental heavy metals is the generation of oxidative stress, and oxidative stress is an important factor in skin aging. , In addition, heavy metals, especially nickel (Ni), can induce excessive expression of collagenase, leading to the attenuation of the extracellular matrix and thus resulting in the loss of skin health and elasticity. In addition to the above-mentioned toxicity, metals may also induce contact allergies and other serious skin diseases. Metals in the environment accumulate in the human body after passing through human tissues, leading to many metabolic imbalances and promoting the development of tumors. These three types of pollutants cover a variety of characteristics related to skin adsorption. NPAHs and OPAHs are hydrophobic organic compounds, mainly distributed in lipid-rich media such as sebum; while heavy metals mainly exist in ionic or particle-bound states, which may exhibit different deposition and adsorption mechanisms. Studying these different types of pollutants simultaneously helps to comprehensively understand the combined health risks of complex pollutant mixtures through dermal exposure in real environments. Convincing evidence indicates that exposure of human skin to heavy metals may have potentially harmful effects. The primary reason for the harmful effects of environmental heavy metals (cadmium and nickel) is the production of oxidative stress, which is an important factor in skin aging. Some studies have shown that heavy metals have potentially harmful effects on human skin. For example, contact with heavy metals will destroy the skin barrier, accompanied by apoptosis, DNA damage, and lipid oxidation. A study assessing dermal exposure to heavy metals among recycling facility workers found an increased cancer risk among those exposed to arsenic and chromium. Studies of metal workers have shown that long-term exposure to heavy metals can affect multiple organs, including the brain and peripheral nerves.

The estimated dose of dermal exposure was commonly calculated using models based on the concentrations of pollutants in ambient air. In addition, various methods have been employed to assess dermal exposure to atmospheric pollutants, including human skin slice cultures, hand wiping, tape-stripping, and soft polypropylene exposure pads. Although these methods provide valuable insights, each has its limitations. Skin slice culture is complex and challenging in field studies, while hand wipes measure pollutants already present on or in the skin, making it difficult to control for skin type. The retention capacity of exposure pad materials is higher than that of real skin, which may overestimate the exposure levels. Additionally, existing studies rarely consider the impact of the skin type on pollutant exposure levels. Human skin can be divided into three general types based on sebum content, including dry skin, neutral skin, and oily skin, whereas the ratio of sebum content in oily skin to dry skin ranges from 1.6 to 2.1 times. , Theoretically, there might be significant differences in the skin adsorption capacity among different skin types for lipophilic pollutants. To date, no study has systematically investigated the health risks of dermal exposure to pollutants under varying skin types and environmental conditions. The influence of different environmental conditions on pollutants is multifaceted. Environmental factors such as temperature, humidity, pH, and airflow can substantially affect the migration and transformation of pollutants across multi-interface environments, further complicating exposure assessment. In this study, to simulate the adsorption of NPAHs and OPAHs of PAHs and metal elements by different skin types, a simulation device suitable for human dermal pollutant exposure assessment was established, which used polyethylene balloons coated with different doses of glycerol trioleate (C57H104O6) to simulate oily skin, neutral skin, and dry skin type, reveals the health risks of dermal exposure to OPAHs, NPAHs and metals, and the device was applied to the assessment of dermal exposure to atmospheric pollutants in different scenarios.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals

Polyethylene balloons were purchased from a local retailer in Tianjin. Glycerol trioleate (100%, purity) was obtained from Myriad (Tianjin, China). Sixteen NPAH standards were purchased from Chiron AS (Trondheim, Norway). Four OPAHs9-fluorenone (9-FO), 9,10-anthraquinone (ATQ), benzanthrone (BZO), and benzo­[a]­anthracene-7,12-quinone (BaAQ)were obtained from TCI (Portland, USA) and AccuStandard (New Haven, USA), respectively. The full list of target NPAHs and OPAHs is provided in the Supporting Information (shown in Tables S1 and S2). Two surrogate standards, 6-nitro-d11 and 9-nitroanthracene-d9, were purchased from CDN isotopes (Quebec, Canada). Eleven heavy metal standards (Mg, Ti, V, Mn, Cu, Zn, As, Mo, Cd, Sb, and Pb) were obtained from Tanmo Quality Inspection (TMRM, Beijing, China). All organic solvents (methanol, acetone, hexane, and dichloromethane) were of chromatographic grade and were used for sample extraction and cleanup; they were purchased from Concord (Tianjin, China). Concentrated hydrochloric acid and nitric acid (GR grade, mass fraction of about 37%) were obtained from Tianjin Chemical Reagent No. 5 Factory (Tianjin, China) and used for metal digestion. Detailed information on these metals is available in the Supporting Information (shown in Tables S3).

Sample Collection

Atmospheric samples were collected using a flow sampler in high-load particulate matter (HY–100WS, Qingdao Hengyuan Technology Development Co., Ltd., Qingdao, China) at a flow rate of 100 L/min. A quartz fiber inorganic filter membrane with a diameter of 90 mm was placed in the filter support to collect samples of PAHs in the form of particles; for gaseous compounds, polyurethane foam was used.

The indoor campus environment represents a public indoor space with potential indoor sources, located in a naturally ventilated university dormitory on campus, representing residential air. Potential indoor sources include cleaning products, electronic devices, and human activities. The outdoor campus (calm wind) serves as a baseline for general urban ambient air, being an open area at least 50 m away from major roads with minimal local interference. A busy traffic artery was selected to represent a microenvironment severely affected by fresh vehicle emissions, located at a roadside site with heavy traffic flow, where fresh emissions are the main source of PAH derivatives. Sandstorm weather (during a sandstorm event on campus outdoors) was chosen as an extreme natural event that can resuspend particulate matter and significantly increase atmospheric particulate matter concentrations, with sampling conducted during a severe sandstorm event on the same campus. This gradient enables us to assess the changes in skin adsorption from a clean indoor environment to severe outdoor pollution, thereby gaining insights into exposure under both daily and extreme (Table ).

Pretreatment

Tables S1–S3 explain the target analytes of this study. For NPAHs and OPAHs analysis, 100 μL of a 500 mg/L surrogate standard solution was spiked onto the balloon surface prior to extraction. The balloon was then cut into smaller circular fragments using ceramic scissors, then subjected to sonication for 20 min in 15 mL of hexane and dichloromethane mixture (1:2, v: (v), and cycled three times. The extracts were combined and concentrated to approximately 1 mL with a nitrogen blower. The extract was centrifuged at 15,000 r/min for 20 min and passed through a 0.22 μm filter membrane. The concentrated extract was purified and fractionated with a SPE column (Si: Al = 2:1, h/h, 3 mL).

The balloon was cut into smaller circular fragments using ceramic scissors and then put into a 125 mL brown wide-mouth bottle. The balloon was eluted in a brown bottle with 10 mL of concentrated nitric acid and concentrated hydrochloric acid mixture (v: v, 1:3). It was then placed in an ultrasonic water bath at 25 °C for 20 min, cooled, transferred to a prepared 50 mL volumetric flask, and circulated three times. After the extraction solution was transferred, the brown bottle was washed with a small amount of nitric acid solution at least 3 times. The washing solution was filtered and collected in a volumetric flask, and the volume was fixed to 50 mL with ultrapure water. A 2 mL aliquot of the final solution was filtered through a 0.22 μm nylon membrane, diluted 10-fold with ultrapure water, and analyzed by ICP-MS.

Analytical Methods

For NPAHs and OPAHs, all extracts were analyzed by GC–MS with a DB–5MS capillary column (the same as the parent), and the temperature of the injection port was 290 °C. The ramp-up procedure was as follows: constant temperature at 60 °C for 2 min, rise to 150 °C at the rate of 45 °C/min, constant temperature for 5 min, then rise to 300 °C at the rate of 5 °C/min, and maintain it at 300 °C for 2 min. The carrier gas is high-purity helium, and the reaction gas is high-purity methane. The mass spectrum conditions used for NPAHs and OPAHs were a negative chemical ionization source at 150 °C, and the quadrupole temperature is 150 °C. Heavy metals were tested using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP–MS).

Quality Assurance and Quality Control (QA/QC)

For NPAHs and OPAHs, all experimental vessels were rinsed twice with Milli-Q water and chromatographic grade acetone, dried in a laboratory fume hood, and then stored wrapped in aluminum foil. In the GC–MS analysis, a calibration standard was injected after every 10 samples to check the sensitivity of the instrument to drift, and pure solvent was injected to check the carryover of the target chemicals from sample to sample. A laboratory blank and a matrix-spiked sample (100 μL of 2 μg/L mixed standard and 100 μL of 500 mg/L mixed standard of NPAHs and OPAHs) were analyzed for each batch of balloons with different amounts of glycerol trioleate coating. The detected concentrations after the background concentration is deducted are reported as the real concentration of the samples. Also, the amounts of all 16 NPAHs and 4 OPAHs were below the detection limits, indicating minimal contamination during the experiments. Surrogate standards were added to each sample, and the recoveries of the substituted standards for each sample species ranged from 75 to 120% (Figure a). Considering the effect of glycerol trioleate content on the recovery of NPAHs and OPAHs in samples, the matrix-spiked recovery was determined using balloon-simulated skin coated with the highest amount of glycerol trioleate. The recoveries of all 18 PAHs detected in the matrix-spiked sample were between 80 and 120%, 16 NPAHs and 4 OPAHs detected in the matrix-spiked sample were between 65 and 120%.

Establishment of Simulation Experimental Methods

Polyethylene is a nonpolar, hydrophobic material with a chemically inert surface, minimizing background interactions with the target nonpolar pollutants (NPAHs, OPAHs) and ensuring that adsorption is mainly controlled by the triolein coating, which is a major component of human skin sebum. Polyethylene balloons coated with different amounts of triolein were used to simulate the human skin epidermis and sebum (Figure ). The size of the balloon can be adjusted according to the requirement. The face width and length of a Chinese adult man are 143.0 ± 3.9 and 223.0 ± 10.7 mm, respectively, while the face width and length of a Chinese adult woman are 136.0 ± 3.7 and 216.0 ± 9.8 mm, respectively. Therefore, the facial skin area (SAface) of Chinese adults is calculated to be 272.8–341.8 cm2. In this study, the diameter of the balloon was controlled at 5.00 cm using vernier calipers, accounting for an exposed area of 314.0 cm2 to simulate the SAface of Chinese adults.

Schematic diagram of the skin adsorption simulation sampling device.

Based on the Baumann skin type indicator and the sebum excretion levels measured by the skin tester (RBX–916, Real Bubee Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., China), the facial skin types are classified into three categories, i.e. dry skin (sebum secretion of 250 μg/cm2), neutral skin (sebum secretion of 500 μg/cm2) and oily skin (sebum secretion of 900 μg/cm2), according to the different concentrations of facial sebum secretion. The content of glycerol trioleate was multiplied per unit area by the facial surface area so as to accurately measure different amounts of glycerol trioleate, i.e., 0.1 mL for dry skin, 0.2 mL for neutral skin, and 0.3 mL for oily skin. Then they were dissolved in MeOH, applied evenly to the surface of balloons with a wool brush, and then were precleaned with acetone and dried in a fume hood before use. After the MeOH on the surface of the balloon was dried, use the skin tester to detect the sebum content of each balloon, then the balloons were carefully suspended at the sampling sites. The sampling height was set as 164.0 cm from the ground to simulate the average body height of Chinese adults, as the average height of men and women aged 18–44 in China is 169.7 and 158.0 cm. The adsorption kinetics of OPAHs and NPAHs by dry balloons-simulated skin, neutral balloons-simulated skin, and oily balloons-simulated skin at 2, 4, 6, and 8 h were analyzed. An 8 h experimental duration was chosen as the end point to simulate a typical daily exposure period (such as about 8 h of exposure on a working or school day), thereby evaluating the cumulative skin adsorption over a realistic time scale. This duration is also sufficient to observe the change in the adsorption process from initial rapid uptake to approaching the steady state. The advantages of this device include the following: (1) the dosage of glycerol trioleate can be adjusted to simulate different skin types; (2) it can be conveniently applied to field simulation investigations under different sampling periods and environmental conditions; (3) the device can reflect the influence of different environmental conditions on the skin adsorption of pollutants; (4) it can provide a standardized method to compare the dermal exposure risks of pollutants under different conditions.

To estimate the potential uncertainty brought by the static lipid coating, we compared the initial lipid load used in the experiment (250–900 μg/cm2) with the sebum excretion rate of human facial skin (0.1–2.0 μg/(cm2·min)). Over an 8 h exposure period, the cumulative sebum secretion concentration could range from 48 to 960 μg/cm2. For dry skin, this could significantly increase the available lipid phase and potentially enhance adsorption. For oily skin, the additional secretion is comparable to the initial secretion. However, factors such as deep skin absorption, evaporation, and oxidative degradation of sebum complicate direct inferences. Based on the possible range of lipid mass changes, we estimate that the absolute adsorption value uncertainty caused by static lipids may be around ± 30–50%.

Results and Discussion

Adsorption Kinetics of NPAHs and OPAHs on the Balloon-Simulated Skin

The adsorption rate of NPAHs and OPAHs increases rapidly in 0–4 h and slowly in 4–6 h, and reaches a steady state in 6–8 h (Figure ). The 8 h experimental duration was sufficient to capture the process of adsorption approaching equilibrium. The adsorption of NPAHs and OPAHs with different ring numbers conformed to the first-order kinetic model (R 2 > 0.94, Figure and Table ), further confirming that the adsorption process follows predictable kinetic laws and approaches equilibrium within the 8 h exposure period. For OPAHs, the k value on the oily surface was 0.771, which was 1.6 times that on the dry surface (k = 0.473). Similarly, the k of NPAHs with 3–4 aromatic rings (NPAH3–4 rings) on the oily surface (k = 0.528) was 2.2 times that on the dry surface (k = 0.241). In general, the adsorption concentrations of NPAHs and OPAHs on the balloon-simulated skin were in the order of oily surface > neutral surface > dry surface (Figure ). As hydrophobic compounds, NPAHs and OPAHs have a higher affinity for nonpolar or hydrophobic surfaces, such as oily skin. As oily surfaces are typically nonpolar, they have a higher adsorption rate on oily surfaces than on dry ones.

Among NPAHs and OPAHs, the k value of OPAHs (0.473 < k < 0.800) is higher than that of NPAH3–4 rings (0.241 < k < 0.528). This difference can be attributed to the combined effects of multiple physicochemical properties. In addition to hydrophobicity, volatility is one of the key factors. The saturated vapor pressure of a compound determines its partition ratio between the gas phase and the particle phase, which in turn affects its mass transfer rate to the skin surface. Some OPAHs (9-fluorenone and anthraquinone) have lower volatility than their corresponding NPAHs, making them more likely to accumulate in the particle phase or directly settle on the skin surface, thereby increasing the adsorption rate. Second, molecular size and stereostructure also play a role. The fused ring structure of OPAHs makes their molecules more planar and polar, which may be more conducive to forming van der Waals forces and π–π stacking interactions with lipid molecules in sebum, thereby enhancing their retention in the lipid layer. Although the simulation device in this study lacks a real stratum corneum barrier, it can be inferred that in real skin, the molecular size and polarity of compounds will also affect their ability to penetrate the stratum corneumsmaller and more hydrophobic molecules may be more easily absorbed, while larger and more polar molecules may remain more on the skin surface. This suggests that there is a complex trade-off relationship between the initial adsorption and subsequent penetration of OPAHs and NPAHs on the skin surface, which is worth further exploration in future studies using more complex skin models.

Adsorption of NPAHs and OPAHs on Skin under Different Scenarios

Skin adsorption experiments of NPAHs and OPAHs were conducted using balloon-simulated skin in an indoor environment and three outdoor scenarios (calm wind, sand-blowing, and traffic artery). The results indicate that dermal adsorption of NPAHs and OPAHs was highest under sand-blowing conditions and near the traffic artery, followed by the outdoor calm wind scenario, with the lowest adsorption observed in the indoor environment (Figure ). In all four scenarios, the adsorption sequence of NPAHs and OPAHs was oily surface > neutral surface > dry surface (Figure ). The air pollution of NPAHs and OPAHs in the indoor environment of the campus is generally lower than that in the outdoor environment. Therefore, the adsorption amount of total NPAHs and OPAHs on the skin outdoors was higher than that indoors. In the traffic artery, the total adsorption capacity of NPAHs and OPAHs is significantly higher than that in the indoor environment (Figure ), mainly due to the fact that the main sources of NPAHs and OPAHs are motor vehicles, including gasoline and diesel vehicle exhaust emissions. The ambient concentrations of total NPAHs and OPAHs were the highest in sand-blowing weather, indicating that the dermal exposure risk to NPAHs and OPAHs was greatest under outdoor sand-blowing conditions. This is likely due to the increased particle load during sandstorms, as high winds resuspend large quantities of dust from the ground, substantially elevating atmospheric particulate matter concentrations. These particles act as carriers for semivolatile organic compounds, including NPAHs and OPAHs, which adsorb onto the particle surface during transport. Dust particles have a rough surface and large surface area, which can retain more pollutants and facilitate their transfer when in contact with the lipid-coated skin surface. Moreover, the high wind speed during sandstorms enhances the impact and turbulent deposition of particles on the skin surface, accelerating the mass transfer. These combined effects result in significantly higher dermal exposure during sandstorm weather compared to other scenarios. In the outdoor calm wind and indoor environments, the adsorption contribution of NPAHs with 3–4 rings was higher, which may be related to the physicochemical properties of NPAHs, the physiological characteristics of the skin, and environmental conditions.

The differences in the adsorption of NPAHs and OPAHs in different scenarios reflect fundamental differences in their dominant sources and atmospheric behavior. In traffic and sandstorm conditions, the adsorption rate of NPAHs is significantly higher than that of OPAHs (Figure ). This is consistent with the emission characteristics of the primary combustion sources. Motor vehicles, especially diesel engines, directly release large amounts of NPAHs through incomplete combustion, with 2-nitrofluorene and 9-nitroanthracene being typical markers of traffic emissions. During sandstorms, the resuspension of road dust, which has accumulated NPAHs from traffic over time, further amplifies the NPAH content in the air.

In the outdoor sand-blowing environment and traffic arteries, the adsorption concentrations of 9-NPhe and 3-NPhe were significantly higher than those in other scenarios (Figure ). It is worth noting that the indoor adsorption concentrations of some OPAHs (such as BZO and BaAQ) are higher than those in outdoor still air scenarios (Figure ). This phenomenon may be related to indoor-specific pollution sources. Studies have shown that coal combustion is an important emission source of PAHs and their oxygenated derivatives, especially during the winter heating period; printers and copiers release various PAH derivatives in the form of toner and volatile organic compounds during operation. Environmental tobacco smoke has been confirmed as an important source of indoor OPAHs, and high-temperature cooking (such as frying and grilling) also generates OPAHs. Additionally, the relatively closed indoor environment and low air exchange rate make it difficult for pollutants to disperse and dilute, potentially leading to the accumulation of semivolatile OPAHs in indoor air and enhancing their adsorption on the skin surface. This result suggests that the pollution contribution of the indoor microenvironment should not be overlooked when assessing the dermal exposure risk of the population, especially for rooms and offices, where people spend a long time.

Adsorption Kinetics of Heavy Metals

The adsorption rates of V (vanadium) and As (arsenic) increased rapidly in 0–2 h and reached a stable state in 2–8 h. For Ti (Titanium) and Zn (Zinc), their growth was relatively stable in 8 h, showing a slow growth trend (Figure ). The adsorption rates of V and As increased rapidly within 0–2 h and reached a stable state within 2–8 h, while Ti and Zn showed a relatively gentle continuous growth trend within 8 h, without a clear plateau phase (Figure ). This indicates that for some metals (especially Ti and Zn), an exposure duration of 8 h may not be sufficient to achieve complete adsorption equilibrium, which may be related to their different physicochemical forms and slower sedimentation kinetics.

Model fitting for the adsorption of heavy metals on balloon-simulated skin for different skin types (dry, neutral, and oily surface) by the first-order kinetic model.

The adsorption of metals conformed to the first-order kinetic model (R 2 > 0.517, Figure and Table ). In general, the adsorption rate constant of metals on the balloon-simulated skin is not the same as that of OPAHs and NPAHs. The adsorption of metals is not significantly related to the concentration of glycerol trioleate on the balloon-simulated skin surface. For example, for V, the k value is not much different on the oily surface, neutral surface, and dry surface, which may be due to its high chemical activity and ability to form strong chemical bonds with various surface functional groups. For Zn and As, the k value on a dry surface is greater than that on an oily surface. Among the 11 metals, only 4 metals were detected in the four scenarios sampled in this study. The k value of As was the highest (1.781 < k < 3.532), followed by Zn (0.373 < k < 2.675) and V (1.574 < k < 1.601), while that of Ti was negative (−0.044 < k < −9.233). Negative k values have no physical significance for the adsorption process, indicating that under these conditions a simple first-order model is not suitable for Ti. There could be multiple factors contributing to this situation. First, the concentration of Ti in the atmosphere might be very low, approaching the analytical detection limit, leading to high data variability and poor model convergence. Second, Ti often exists in the earth’s crust as stable oxides such as TiO2, and surface interactions might be different from those of more soluble or reactive metals. Its adsorption might be mainly dominated by the deposition of fine titanium-containing particles rather than a process that is not suitable for a first-order model. Finally, the apparent negative adsorption might reflect net desorption or experimental noise, masking the minimal adsorption signal. This highlights the limitations of the model for elements with extremely low environmental concentrations or distinct physicochemical behaviors.

Adsorption of Metals on Skin in Different Scenarios

Skin adsorption experiments of heavy metals were conducted using balloons in an indoor environment and three outdoor scenarios (calm wind, sand-blowing, and traffic road). The highest total metal adsorption was observed in the ″sand-blowing″ condition and in the outdoor ″traffic artery″ scenario, followed by the indoor environment, with the lowest adsorption in the outdoor ″calm wind″ scenario (Figure ). Unlike hydrophobic organic compounds such as NPAHs and OPAHs, the adsorption of heavy metals has no significant correlation with the concentration of triolein on the balloon surface. This is due to the fundamentally different adsorption mechanisms between the two. Heavy metals in the atmosphere mainly exist in two forms: ionic and particle-bound. Among them, the particle-bound form is the dominant form of atmospheric heavy metals, and heavy metals can be combined with carriers such as mineral dust and carbonaceous particles through adsorption, encapsulation, and other means. Under the field exposure conditions of this study, the adsorption of heavy metals on the balloon-simulated skin surface is likely mainly achieved through the dry deposition mechanism of particulate matter rather than the partitioning process of ionic heavy metals into the lipid layer. This explains why the adsorption amount of heavy metals is not related to the concentration of triolein: the deposition behavior of particulate matter is mainly influenced by particle size, aerodynamic characteristics, and the properties of the collection surface rather than the regulation of surface lipid content. This mechanism is also supported by the following observation: the adsorption amounts of V, Zn, As, and Ti on dry, neutral, and oily surfaces show no significant differences (Figure ), indicating that the lipid layer has a limited trapping effect on metals.

In all four scenarios, there was no significant difference in metal adsorption on the balloon-simulated skin surfaces with different concentrations of glycerol trioleate. Among the 11 metals detected, As, Zn, V, and Ti were detected in all four scenarios, while Cu was only detected during sandstorm weather, which is a scenario-specific detection. The four metals followed the same concentration order: V > Zn > As > Ti (Figure ). V consistently demonstrated the highest adsorption capacity across all scenarios and skin types, with adsorption levels approximately 2 to 5 times that of the next most abundant metal (Zn). In the atmosphere, V mainly exists in an oxidized form, such as the vanadyl ion (VO2+), which is highly chemically reactive and can form stable coordination complexes with organic ligands. This property may enhance its retention on the surface of triglyceride acid coatings more effectively than less reactive metals like titanium. In the sampling area (Tianjin, a highly industrialized city), significant emissions of V from coal combustion, oil refining, and steel manufacturing have led to higher environmental V concentrations compared to other metals such as arsenic and titanium. The adsorption amounts on the skin surface must reflect these potential concentration gradients in the environment. Moreover, some studies have shown that vanadium may have a higher affinity for organic coatings than other metals, which might be due to its ability to participate in ligand exchange reactions with carboxyl and hydroxyl groups on the oxidized organic surface. Unlike NPAHs and OPAHs, the metal adsorption on the skin in the indoor environment was higher than that in the outdoor calm wind environment (Figures and ). This might be due to the higher presence of metal substances in the indoor environment, the high content of As, Zn, etc. in indoor dust, and the existence of various pollution sources, such as printers and instruments, in the indoor environment. In the traffic artery, the adsorption concentration of Ti was significantly higher than that in other sites, and the total metal content was also relatively high (Figures and ). The sampling concentrations of total heavy metals were the highest in sand-blowing weather, and Cu was only detected in the outdoor sand-blowing environment, indicating that the risk of metal exposure on the skin was the greatest under the outdoor sand-blowing condition (Figures and ). These can be explained by the fact that dust resuspended from the road surface by moving vehicles and dust resuspended from other surfaces by wind are the main sources of atmospheric heavy metal pollution. ,

Summary

This study developed a simulation device using polyethylene balloons coated with different doses of glycerol trioleate to simulate the adsorption of PAH derivatives (NPAHs and OPAHs) and metals by different skin types. Polyethylene balloons coated with different doses of glycerol trioleate were used to simulate oily skin, normal skin, and dry skin types. The device was applied to the assessment of dermal exposure to atmospheric pollutants in different scenarios. Through on-site simulation experiments, it was found that the adsorption of NPAHs and OPAHs with different ring numbers conforms to the first-order kinetic model. The increase in skin oil content led to an increase in the skin adsorption capacity of NPAHs and OPAHs. The adsorption concentration sequence was oily surface > neutral surface > dry surface. In 8 h, the adsorption amount of OPAHs on the oily surface was approximately 1.2–1.6 times that on the dry surface. For NPAHs3–4 rings, the adsorption amount on the oily surface is approximately 1.3–1.7 times that on the dry surface. This is primarily attributed to the hydrophobic interactions and van der Waals forces between OPAHs, NPAHs, and the oily surface, whereas the adsorption of metals shows little correlation with the concentration of triolein on the balloon-simulated skin surface. In diverse exposure scenarios, the adsorption of NPAHs and OPAHs on the skin revealed that the adsorption quantity is the greatest in sand-blowing weather, followed by the outdoor in the campus and the traffic artery, and the least in the indoor area of the campus. The adsorption amount of NPAHs in the outdoor sand-blowing scenario was approximately 20 times that in the indoor scenario. Consequently, the adsorption amount of total NPAHs and OPAHs on the outdoor skin was higher than that on the indoor skin, mainly due to the exhaust emissions from gasoline and diesel vehicles outdoors. In sand-blowing weather and the traffic artery, the adsorption amount of NPAHs exceeds that of OPAHs, while in the indoor and outdoor environments of the campus, the adsorption amount of OPAHs was larger than that of NPAHs, and the adsorption contribution of 3–4-ring NPAHs was higher. Among the 11 metals in the four scenarios, only four metals (As, Zn, V, and Ti) were detected. The adsorption amounts conformed to the sequence V > Zn > As > Ti, which was in line with the chemical reactivity sequences of these four metals. The highest total metal adsorption amount occurred under sand-blowing weather and near the traffic artery, followed by the indoor environment, and the lowest was in the outdoor calm wind environment. The total metal adsorption amounts in the outdoor sand-blowing environment, indoor environment, and near the traffic artery were 44, 18, and 14% higher than those in the outdoor calm wind environment, respectively. This was because there were fewer pollution sources and the wind force was relatively weak in the outdoor calm wind environment, leading to a weaker suspension and diffusion capacity of particulate matter. Therefore, greater attention should be given to the health risks of dermal exposure to pollutantsparticularly NPAHs, OPAHs, and metalsfor individuals with oily skin and during sandstorm events. However, polyethylene balloons coated with triolein cannot fully replicate the complexity of human skin, including the stratum corneum barrier, the presence of sweat (which can alter the solubility and pH of contaminants), the dynamic microbiome, and active biological processes, such as metabolism or desquamation. Moreover, in real-life scenarios, the surface of human skin is often covered with various skin care products, such as moisturizers, sunscreens, and foundations. These products contain multiple functional ingredients that can significantly alter the physicochemical properties of the skin surface, including hydrophilicity, charge state, and surface roughness, thereby influencing the adsorption behavior of contaminants. For instance, inorganic nanoparticles (TiO2, ZnO) in sunscreens may directly participate in the coadsorption of contaminants; hydrophilic components in moisturizers may change the skin surface’s affinity for hydrophobic organic contaminants. This study did not consider the influence of skin care products, so the results mainly reflect the adsorption behavior under ″bare skin″ conditions. Future research could incorporate common skin care product ingredients into the balloon coating to systematically assess their regulatory effects on the adsorption of different types of contaminants. Therefore, our model is the most suitable for simulating the initial adsorption stage on the skin surface. The actual transdermal absorption and subsequent health impacts are regulated by these additional biological factors, which are beyond the scope of this study but provide important directions for future research using more complex in vitro or in vivo models. Additionally, the current study may focus more on short-term dermal exposure, while the effects of long-term exposure to contaminants on the skin and their potential health consequences still require further investigation.
