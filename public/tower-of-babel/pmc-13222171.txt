Evaluating Cost-Effectiveness in Relation to the Supporting Clinical Evidence Across the Type 2 Diabetes Continuum: A Review of Metformin and SGLT2is
Authors: Ian W. Campbell, Kerstin M. G. Brand, Ulrike Gottwald-Hostalek, Julian Dettenbach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metformin is a safe and effective glucose-lowering agent, widely regarded as the first-line treatment option for type 2 diabetes (T2D), and available at very low cost. Recent updates to the American Diabetes Association (ADA) Standards of Care guidance demonstrate a shift towards the earlier use of newer, more expensive, anti-diabetic agents for the treatment of T2D, based on concurrent conditions such as cardiovascular and/or renal complication reduction, and significant weight loss. As of 2026, this has culminated in sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2is) being recommended for patients with T2D and established atherosclerosis cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) or indicators of high ASCVD, heart failure or chronic kidney disease, independently of the use of metformin. The main evidence base supporting the use of SGLT2is in these patient populations come from cardiovascular and renal outcome trials, which recruited patients usually with long-standing T2D and high levels of cardiovascular and/or renal comorbidities. Whether the cardiovascular and renal benefits shown during these trials are generalisable to the early T2D or even pre-diabetic population may be debated. Ultimately, this leaves the question as to whether patients with early T2D and no cardiovascular and/or renal complications should receive treatment with SGLT2is, at a higher cost, where clinical evidence of superiority to established therapies like metformin are scarce. As T2D is a chronic, progressive disease, often advancing through pre-diabetes, early T2D and eventually treatment intensification, the cost-effectiveness of treatments at each stage of the disease must also be considered to understand the long-term economic burden. This review explores the available cost-effectiveness evidence, supported by clinical data, for the use of metformin and SGLT2is across the complete T2D continuum.

Introduction

The American Diabetes Association (ADA) has been involved in the development and dissemination of diabetes clinical care guidelines for over 35 years and recently published the 2026 update to their “living” guidance document, Standards of Care in Diabetes [1]. Metformin, termed the “cornerstone therapy” for T2D [2], is the most common glucose-lowering agent used daily by over 200 million people and, with more than 60 years of clinical development, has a well-understood benefit–risk balance [3]. Sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2is) have been indicated for patients with T2D following initial FDA approval of canagliflozin in 2013 [4]. Due to the demonstration of cardiovascular and renal benefits as addition to standard of care (SoC) in pivotal trials [5–8], recent ADA guidelines have seen a gradual shift towards recommendation of SGLT2is in patients with T2D and cardiovascular disease (CVD), CVD risk factors and/or renal complications [9–12].

Despite a longstanding link between T2D and CVD and/or renal complications [13], the increased focus on these outcomes assessed in the SGLT2i trials cannot be completely attributed to ‘exploratory interest’. The historical basis relates to a proposed link between a specific class of T2D therapies, thiazolidinediones, and an increase in cardiovascular risk and mortality by one class member, rosiglitazone [14]. Approved in the US and Europe by 2000, use of rosiglitazone peaked in 2006 until a meta-analysis in 2007 suggested treatment was associated with a 43% and 64% increased risk of myocardial infarction (MI) and death by cardiovascular causes, respectively [15]. This ultimately led to the drug being suspended from European markets by 2010 [16]. This newly identified risk also encouraged the United States (US) Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to release specific guidance in 2008 regarding cardiovascular safety requirements for new drugs developed for the management of T2D [17]. Recommendations included the adjudication of independent cardiovascular endpoints during all phase II and III trials, with cardiovascular mortality, MI and stroke considered as mandatory endpoints. This guidance informed the CANVAS programme, a combination of two studies designed to evaluate the effects of canagliflozin on the risk of CVD and to assess safety and tolerability in patients with inadequately controlled T2D and increased cardiovascular risk [5, 18]. In addition to demonstrating the requested absence of cardiovascular risk, CANVAS was able to demonstrate a lower risk of cardiovascular events in the assessed patient group versus placebo [5] and set the precedence for a ‘class effect’ in CVD and/or renal benefit for SGLT2is [6–8]. Notably, this requirement was not installed for metformin, which had been marketed for more than a decade in the US and more than four decades in Europe at that time. Additionally, metformin had already demonstrated cardiovascular benefit in the United Kingdom Prospective Diabetes Study (UKPDS) trial in 1998 [19] and was then considered the first line treatment for T2D in the joint treatment recommendation by ADA and The European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD) [20]. Therefore, despite a well-documented clinical history displaying long-term safety and efficacy [3], the recent evidence of metformin’s cardiorenal benefit is based on meta-analyses and real-world evidence [21–27] and not reflected in the genericised US metformin labels.

Current Clinical Guidance

Pre-diabetes

The term pre-diabetes refers to individuals with blood glucose concentrations that are higher than normal, but do not yet meet diabetes criteria [28]. Pre-diabetes is categorised into impaired fasting glucose or impaired glucose tolerance (IGT), with both significant risk factors for progression to diabetes [29]. Various countries have adopted programmes to reduce the conversion rate to T2D, generally focussing on intensive lifestyle change (ILC) as the main intervention [30–32]. While ILC programmes have shown improvements versus conventional dietary and exercise advice in the controlled clinical setting [30], evidence of their efficacy in less restricted settings is sparse [33]. One meta-analysis found that ILC interventions are most likely to benefit “high risk individuals who have the motivation and social support to achieve sustained lifestyle change”, representing a limited proportion of the pre-diabetes population [34].

Currently, the ADA recommends that metformin should be considered in adults at high risk of T2D, especially those aged 25–59 years, with BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2, higher fasting plasma glucose [e.g. ≥ 110 mg/dL (≥ 6 mmol/L)], and higher glycosylated haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) [e.g. ≥ 6.0% (≥ 42 mmol/mol)], and in individuals with prior gestational diabetes mellitus [29]. Despite this recommendation and approval in numerous countries for the prevention of diabetes, metformin remains unapproved in pre-diabetes in the US. In 2017, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) updated their United Kingdom (UK) guideline with metformin recommended to be used, based on clinical judgement, in people with deteriorating blood glucose test results despite of, or being unable to participate in, intensive lifestyle programmes [35]. The Chinese Society of Endocrinology also recommend metformin in patients with pre-diabetes with poor blood glucose control or worsening hyperglycaemia, despite 6 months participation in ILC [36]. Overall, there is meanwhile broad global acceptance of metformin for use in pre-diabetes, as also exemplified in the 2025 International Diabetes Federation (IDF) Global Practice Recommendation, which suggests to consider metformin as the only additional drug intervention in the prevention of diabetes [37].

Metformin and SGLT2is in Early T2D

Until 2021, the ADA installed metformin as the initial pharmacologic agent for all patients with T2D (if tolerated and not contra-indicated) [10]. This changed in 2022 with a shift towards the assessment of cardiorenal comorbidities and risk factors to help determine treatment initiation, but still generally including the use of metformin [11]. More recently, treatment approaches for T2D management have been further segmented and are now tailored to cardiovascular and renal complication and risk reduction, weight management, and glycaemic control [29]. Metformin (or other agents) are now recommended to reach effective glycaemic control [29]. Metformin is assessed as moderately lowering weight, benefits patients safety by not causing hypoglycaemia [38, 39], and is recommended where cost-related barriers may exist [1].

Some data also support initial combination therapy in patients with drug-naïve T2D particularly in the case of high HbA1C levels, due to the rapid attainment of glycaemic control [40, 41]. NICE stipulates that, even in combination therapy, metformin (preferably in modified release form) must be initiated first and titrated to the maximum tolerated dose prior to initiation of the combination partner [42]. However, the choice to use a non-metformin option for the first-line treatment of T2D, especially in patients without metformin contraindications, has only very recently been propagated. In 2024, the ADA recommended the use of SGLT2is for patients with T2D and established atherosclerosis CVD (ASCVD) or indicators of high ASCVD, HF or CKD, independently of the use of metformin [43], which is echoed in the 2025 and 2026 updates [1, 29]. The EASD have also recommended SGLT2is independently of metformin in patients with established, or at high risk of, CVD, HF or CKD [44].

Treatment Intensification

The ADA recommends that treatment intensification or modification is not delayed in patients with T2D who are not meeting their individualised treatment goals [1]. Data from comparative effectiveness meta-analyses suggest that each additional class of oral non-insulin agents added to a metformin lowers HbA1C by approximately 0.7–1.0% (8–11 mmol/mol). Traditionally, progressive treatment with more intensive glucose lowering agents has been recommended in a stepwise process with the metformin “backbone” maintained, allowing physicians a clear assessment of positive and negative impacts of a new treatment as well as reducing potential side effects and unnecessary expense [45]. Treatment plans should be continuously monitored to ensure efficacy and limit side effects [1].

Aim of the Review

The recent changes in the ADA guidelines raise the question of prioritisation during T2D treatment decision-making. SGLT2is have demonstrated their cardiovascular benefit predominantly in patients with established disease, and their primary prevention effect may not outweigh the benefit of tight glycaemic control, especially in patients with newly diagnosed T2D [46]. The global prevalence of CVD among those with T2D varies across countries, with a weighted average in 2019 of 34.8% [47]. Those at low cardiovascular and/or renal risk may not require more intensive diabetic agents with benefits in the management of later stage complications. Diabetes represents a significant global economic burden, with overall costs in the US and Europe estimated at USD$327 billion in 2017 [48] and EUR€142bn in 2019 [49], respectively. Newer treatments, such as SGLT2is, are often accompanied by higher prices compared to established treatments such as metformin. The overall value and economic impact following the wider implementation of these treatments is yet to be demonstrated and carefully determining which patient groups benefit most will be important to ensure cost-effectiveness. This review aims to understand the current cost-effectiveness evidence, supported by clinical data, for the use of metformin and SGLT2is across the T2D continuum.

Methods

A literature search was conducted using Medline via the PubMed database. Search criteria included articles published between 2019 and 2024 to capture the most current literature. Search strings were developed that included the phrases “type 2 diabetes”, “metformin”, “SGLT2 inhibitors”, “comorbidities” and “cost-effectiveness analysis” in order to focus on the clinical and economic evidence for metformin and SGLT2is in patients with T2D. A total of 187 articles were retrieved and assessed for suitability of inclusion, leading to analysis of a collection of 70. Clinically relevant publications, as decided by the authors, were also included. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Results

Pre-diabetes

Metformin for Reducing or Delaying the Development of T2D

A review on metformin for the prevention or delay of T2D and its associated complications in patients at increased risk for the development of T2D included 20 RCTs comparing metformin with any pharmacological glucose-lowering intervention, behaviour-changing intervention, placebo or standard care [50]. Overall, metformin, when compared with either placebo or diet and exercise, was found to reduce the risk of T2D in high-risk individuals by up to 50% [50]. Although metformin did not reduce or delay the risk of T2D versus ILC alone [50], the proportion of patients with pre-diabetes expected to benefit from ILC alone in the real world is limited [34]. Therefore, metformin still represents an important clinical tool for managing pre-diabetes. More recently, metformin plus ILC has been shown to further reduce the risk of developing T2D than ILC alone in the Chinese population [51]. In the US, long-term follow-up of the Diabetes Prevention Program now spans 21 years and has shown that the initial intervention effects during the 3-year trial period in reducing diabetes incidence with metformin and ILC continue to be sustained versus the placebo cohort [52]. The greatest reduction in incidence with metformin was seen in younger patients aged 25–44 years at baseline [52].

An existing ADA recommendation and IDF global guideline for the use of metformin in patients with pre-diabetes [37, 53], alongside the NICE recommendation for the use of metformin to prevent or delay the onset of T2D, represents an important shift in the validity of metformin for management of pre-diabetes. Further to this, by 2019, at least 20 RCTs ranging from 1978 to 2018 had been conducted evaluating metformin in the pre-diabetic population [50]. This represents the most thoroughly studied anti-diabetic drug, when looking specifically at the benefit–risk balance in the reduction of overt diabetes.

SGLT2is in Patients with Pre-diabetes

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to summarise the effects of SGLT2is in preventing T2D in patients with pre-diabetes and HF or CKD [54]. The analysis found that, for adults with pre-diabetes and HF or CKD, treatment with SGLT2is was associated with a 20% reduction in the risk of developing T2D compared to placebo (32% in dapagliflozin and 18% in empagliflozin) [54]. Such data could expand the use of SGLT2is in this patient population, furthering their applicability in patients with cardiovascular and/or renal complications, if approved for this indication.

Economic Evidence for Metformin in Pre-diabetes

A study in Belgium assessed the cost-effectiveness and budget impact of nationwide implementation for both ILC and pharmacotherapy with metformin using a semi-Markov model to simulate prevalence and costs related to pre-diabetes, specifically IGT, and T2D [55]. For both ILC and metformin, the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) from a healthcare perspective fell below the cost-effectiveness threshold [€40,000 per quality adjusted life-year (QALY)] at the 8-year timepoint post-programme initiation [55]. From this point onwards, ICERs became increasingly cost-effective and, by the 20-year timepoint, ILC and metformin programs cost just €7770 and €8600 per QALY gained, respectively. From a societal perspective, ICERs indicated that both interventions became cost-effective at 6 years post-implementation and cost-saving at the 8-year timepoint [55]. ILC and metformin programs were found to be approximately €26,600 and €31,000 per QALY, respectively, by year 20. Early Type 2 Diabetes

The Legacy Effect of Metformin Use in Early T2D

Read-outs from the UK Prospective Diabetes Study (UKPDS), namely after the intervention program in 1998 [57], upon 10-year open-label follow-up in 2008 [58] and most recently in 2024, have contributed to a 42-year follow-up assessing the potential legacy effect of metformin following early intensive glycaemic control in patients with newly diagnosed T2D [59]. Both the 20-year prospective controlled UKPDS trial and 10-year post-trial follow-up had previously shown major clinical benefit for patients with newly diagnosed T2D treated with metformin compared to conventional therapy (diet only), with an enduring and emerging metformin legacy treatment effect [57, 58]. Confirmation that this treatment effect does not wane for up to 42 years has now been shown through the extended 14-year follow-up period [59]. This supports the long-term benefit of metformin use directly after T2D diagnosis, compared to dietary restriction, even despite the uncontrolled treatment regimen after the controlled study phase and the interview-based character of the long-term follow-up.

Cardiovascular and/or Renal Benefit Following Treatment with Metformin in Early T2D

Data from the UKPDS 42-year follow-up also provide insights into the effect of early glycaemic control with metformin on long-term cardiovascular risk reduction [59], demonstrating the overall relative risk reduction from baseline in the metformin group was 31% (12–46%; p = 0.003) for MI. This shows that establishing and maintaining near normoglycaemia from the time of T2D diagnosis with metformin not only reduces the risk of dying but also impacts cardiovascular comorbidities associated with the disease. As the data now span up to 42 years in a single patient, with a minimum enrollment age of 25 years, the results demonstrate the near-lifelong legacy effects of metformin in minimizing later risk of diabetes-related complications [59].

A population-based cohort study in Taiwan assessed the link between metformin adherence in patients with T2D and the risk of CVD [60]. In the metformin-adherent group, stroke hospitalisation occurred 48% less often than in the non-adherent group, MI hospitalisation risk was halved, and haemorrhagic stroke and ischemic stroke were reduced by 45% and 51%, respectively. Analysis of additional outcomes also showed a significantly lower risk of hospitalisation due to heart failure (− 74%), hospitalisation due to lactic acidosis (− 82%) and hospitalisation for lack of glycaemic control (− 77%) in the metformin-adherent group. Overall, the results showed that patients with T2D who adhered to metformin had a significantly lower risk of CVD events, including stroke and MI, independent of concurrent treatment with insulin, sulfonylurea (SU) or dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors (DPP-4is) [60].

A retrospective, observational study assessed the association between death and cardiovascular comorbidities in patients with T2D depending on whether patients were initially treated with metformin monotherapy or non-metformin monotherapy [61]. The cumulative incidence rate for all-cause death, cardiovascular death, HF and HF hospitalisation were lower in the metformin monotherapy group across all time points of the 5-year follow-up period (Fig. 2). The cumulative incidence for all-cause death was significantly lower in the metformin monotherapy group at 7.29% versus 11.91% in the non-metformin monotherapy group (p < 0.0001). The difference in the 5-year cumulative incidence of HF was also found to be significant between study arms in favour of metformin monotherapy at 7.55% versus 11.61% (p < 0.0001). A real-world cohort study assessed the impact of metformin, in combination with an SGLT2i, as a first-line therapy for T2D, on kidney disease progression and mortality [27]. Compared to first-line SGLT2i monotherapy, patients receiving combination therapy with metformin demonstrated a significantly lower risk of all-cause mortality and incidence of kidney disease progression, suggesting a renal protective and increased survival benefit in maintaining the metformin backbone for patients receiving an SGLT2i in early T2D [27].

Cost-Effectiveness of Metformin versus Newer Treatments in Early T2D

Although there are limited data directly comparing the cost-effectiveness of newer treatment options in the first-line treatment setting with metformin, one study has assessed SGLT2is and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RAs) versus metformin in patients with drug-naïve T2D in the US [62]. First-line use of SGLT2is or GLP-1 RAs increased undiscounted life expectancy by just 3.0 months and 3.4 months, respectively, versus first-line metformin (Fig. 3). First-line treatment with metformin had the lowest average per-person lifetime cost, with 60–70% of the cost difference compared with SGLT2is and GLP-1 RAs accounted for by the newer medicine’s higher medication costs. Use of SGLT2is in Patients with Early T2D

SGLT2is in Combination Therapy for Early T2D

Five papers focused on the cost-effectiveness of empagliflozin as an add-on to metformin or SoC in patients with T2D and established CVD [63–67]. Despite higher treatment costs associated with the addition of empagliflozin, the predicted increase in QALYs associated with the treatment in these patients led to an overall decrease in the ICER versus metformin or SoC alone across all studies [63–67]. Empagliflozin as an add-on to metformin or SoC was also found to be cost-effective versus metformin or SoC alone below each of the studies’ WTP threshold [63–67]. Although the modelled time horizon varied from a minimum of 5 years [65] to a maximum of 50 years [63] or even lifetime [66], the results continued to show cost-effectiveness for the addition of empagliflozin to metformin or SoC alone in patients with T2D and established CVD [63–67].

One study was identified that evaluated the first-line use of empagliflozin in addition to SoC versus SoC alone in patients with T2D and CKD from the perspective of US commercial insurers and Medicare [68]. Addition of empagliflozin to SoC in patients with T2D and CKD is cost-effective when considering a WTP threshold of USD$150,000 per QALY gained [68].

Three studies were identified assessing the cost-effectiveness of dapagliflozin as an add-on to metformin or SoC in patients with T2D and either established, high risk, or some prevalence of CVD [69–71], from the perspective of healthcare systems in Spain [69], Australia [70] and China [71], respectively. The modelled population of two studies was based on data from the DECLARE-TIMI 58 trial [69, 71], while the population of the remaining study was based on data from the CVD-Real Nordic trial [70]. It should be noted that the baseline patient characteristics of CVD-Real Nordic had a prevalence of CVD of only 25% [72], far lower than other pivotal trials assessing SGLT2is in early T2D (40.6% to > 99%) [5, 6, 8]. As an add-on to SoC, dapagliflozin was found to decrease costs versus placebo [69] and increase costs versus SoC [71]. However, as dapagliflozin plus SoC was also associated with an increase in QALYs, both studies found that the addition of dapagliflozin in patients with T2D and established or high-risk CVD led to a decrease in the overall ICER per QALY gained versus either placebo or SoC alone, respectively [69, 71]. This led to dapagliflozin plus SoC being considered cost-effective by the assumed Spanish and Chinese WTP threshold under EUR€20,000 and CHN\242,928, respectively [69, 71].

In the case of the patients modelled from the CVD-Real Nordic study, comparing first-line combination metformin with dapagliflozin versus metformin monotherapy and gradual dapagliflozin addition [70], the study assumes that all patients included will eventually progress onto combination therapy, despite a CVD prevalence of only 25% at baseline. As this is one of the only studies identified assessing SGLT2is in early T2D with low CVD prevalence, it is important that this distinction is clear. The study found that, compared to initial metformin shortly followed by the gradual addition of dapagliflozin, first-line use of combination dapagliflozin and metformin was likely to be a cost-effective approach to the management of Australians with T2D under a WTP of AUD$50,000 per QALY [70].

One study was identified assessing the cost-effectiveness of both dapagliflozin and canagliflozin as an add-on to SoC in patients with T2D and CKD [73]. Baseline demographics were taken from the CREDENCE trial for canagliflozin on renal outcomes in T2D and neuropathy [74], and modelled from a Canadian healthcare perspective [73]. Both SGLT2is were found to be cost-effective as an add-on to SoC versus SoC alone under a WTP threshold of CDN$50,000 per QALY in patients with T2D and CKD, primarily due to their efficacy in delaying dialysis [73].

SGLT2is as Monotherapy in Early T2D

Three studies were identified assessing SGLT2is as monotherapy in the first-line treatment setting in patients with T2D and cardiovascular and/or renal complications [75–77]. From a Dutch healthcare perspective, empagliflozin, dapagliflozin and canagliflozin were associated with a predicted ICER of €5440 per QALY in the “reimbursed population” compared with “care-as-usual”, which was cost-effective from a WTP threshold of €20,000 per QALY [75]. At the time, the reimbursed population for SGLT2is in the Netherlands included patients with T2D and previously proven CVD or CKD [78]. Another study assessed dapagliflozin versus placebo from a UK perspective, demonstrating that treatment with dapagliflozin was estimated to reduce lifetime costs by £2552 with the greatest benefit seen in the prior HF patient subgroup where lifetime costs were reduced by £4150 [76]. The final study assessing SGLT2i monotherapy in early T2D compared the use of empagliflozin with dapagliflozin in patients with T2D and established CVD in Greece [77]. Empagliflozin was estimated to be cost-effective versus dapagliflozin in patients with T2D and established CVD, based on a WTP threshold of €36,000 per QALY gained in Greece [77].

One further retrospective study of health plan enrollees in the US compared healthcare resource utilisation and costs in patients with T2D after initiating dapagliflozin versus sitagliptin, a DPP-4i [79]. When assessing the entire patient population, all-cause costs and diabetes-related costs were found to be similar between the dapagliflozin and sitagliptin cohorts. However, in the subgroup of patients receiving oral anti-diabetic (OAD) monotherapy at baseline, all-cause costs were found to be 17.0% lower in those that initiated dapagliflozin than sitagliptin ($14,884 vs. $12,353; P = 0.026) [79]. This suggests that previous OAD monotherapy, the vast majority prescribed as metformin, leads to lower costs in patients that are intensified onto SGLT2is versus DPP-4is [79].

Economic Evidence Supporting Treatment Intensification with SGLT2is

Ten studies were identified assessing the use of SGLT2is as later-line pharmacotherapy options in patients who were uncontrolled by metformin as monotherapy or in combination with an additional anti-diabetic treatment [80–89]. Six studies included treatments as an add-on to metformin monotherapy [80–85] and four studies included treatments as an add-on to metformin and DPP-4is [86–88] or metformin and SUs [89], respectively.

Of the studies assessing SGLT2is as an add-on to metformin monotherapy, the comparator varied between DPP-4is [80, 84], SUs [82] and GLP-1RAs [81, 83, 85]. However, regardless of the national healthcare perspective considered or the comparator, both dapagliflozin and empagliflozin were consistently cost-effective when used in combination with metformin in patients with T2D who are not controlled on metformin monotherapy [80, 82–85].

In the first study assessing treatment intensification for patients with T2D not controlled with metformin and a DPP-4i, SGLT2is were assessed as a class from the perspective of the UK healthcare system [86]. For patients uncontrolled on metformin and a DPP-4i, intensification with an SGLT2i prior to insulin was cost-neutral or cost-effective compared with immediate insulin intensification. In the remaining two studies, stepwise treatment intensification by adding an SGLT2i to existing metformin and DPP-4i combination therapy was compared with switching to metformin and GLP-1 RA combination therapy [87, 88]. Both studies assessed this from a US payer perspective, with one study further splitting results between private and public settings [87]. From a private perspective, addition of an SGLT2i versus switching to a GLP-1 RA resulted in an ICER of $42,125 per patient, whereas, from a public perspective, an ICER of $103,829 was generated. In the second study, intensification to triple combination with metformin, DPP-4is and SGLT2is were found to be the dominant treatment strategy and resulted in a $9511 lifetime cost saving versus switching to a GLP-1 RA [88].

The final study compared dapagliflozin with DPP-4is as an add-on to metformin and SUs from the perspective of the Dutch healthcare system [89]. Dapagliflozin was found to be a cost-saving alternative compared to DPP-4is when added to metformin and SUs. Overall, of the studies identified analysing the cost-effectiveness of treatment intensification with an SGLT2i, all explored SLGT2is as an add-on to the metformin backbone [80–89]. Only one study suggested that, from a US public payer perspective, switching to metformin and GLP-1 RA combination therapy versus stepwise treatment intensification on top of the metformin backbone may be a cost-effective option [87].

Conclusions

Across the T2D continuum, the level of cost-effectiveness evidence based on the known clinical benefits of metformin and SGLT2i varies greatly at the different stages of disease. Metformin, as a low-cost, easily accessible and approved treatment option dominates the pre-diabetes space and is largely considered the only pharmacotherapy option for this patient group. Despite some clinical evidence to support SGLT2is in patients with both pre-diabetes and cardiovascular and/or renal risk [90–94], the cost-effectiveness of this treatment strategy has not been established. Therefore, metformin remains a cost-effective and cost-saving treatment option for many patients with pre-diabetes [55], particularly due to the low level of adherence to ILC [56]. The ongoing cardiovascular outcome trial (VA-IMPACT), exploring metformin’s effect on ASCVD outcomes in patients with pre-diabetes, may inform further benefits for metformin in this patient group [95].

As patients progress to early T2D, the skew in available data shifts towards SGLT2i, with a plethora of recent studies exploring SGLT2is, mainly in addition to metformin or SoC, focused on the cost-effectiveness in patients with T2D and cardiovascular and/or renal complications. However, direct comparison of SGLT2is versus metformin in the broader, drug-naïve early T2D population found that SGLT2is were not cost-effective versus metformin [62]. Metformin also remains the only pharmacotherapy agent for the glycaemic management of patients with robust data for early T2D in the form of the UKPDS trial, which specifically recruited patients with newly diagnosed T2D [46]. The long-term evidence generated for metformin now demonstrates benefits which include cardiovascular risk reduction [59]. It is important to note that further data should soon be available through the read-out of the SMARTEST study, a registry-based randomised trial comparing first-line metformin versus dapagliflozin, which will also include a cost-effectiveness analysis [96].

Due to the progressive nature of the disease, many patients require intensification from metformin monotherapy or dual combination. Prior metformin use has been shown to improve clinical outcomes in later-line patients [6] and lead to economic savings versus prior use with other agents [79]. Cost-effectiveness of treatment intensification with SGLT2is was exclusively found to be assessed while maintaining the metformin backbone [80–89].

Overall, in patients with T2D who are at low risk of cardiovascular and/or renal complications and who are able to achieve glycaemic goals with metformin alone, there is no economic evidence to support initiation of other, more costly, anti-diabetes agents. Conversely, this review demonstrates the wealth of literature supporting the clinical benefit and cost-effectiveness of maintaining the metformin backbone across the T2D continuum. This is further supported through the very recent IDF guidelines that, despite the ADA updates, continue to prioritise metformin as a highly effective first-line treatment with a well-established safety profile [37, 46]. Ultimately, metformin’s low cost counters growing global concerns over resource use, further establishing the requirement for it to remain the cornerstone to be utilised across the T2D continuum.
