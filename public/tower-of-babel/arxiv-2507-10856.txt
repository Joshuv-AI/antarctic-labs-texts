Higher spin Richardson-Gaudin model with time-dependent coupling: Exact dynamics
Suvendu Barik, Lieuwe Bakker, Vladimir Gritsev, Jiří Minář, Emil A. Yuzbashyan

Abstract
We determine the exact asymptotic many-body wavefunction of a spin- s s Richardson-Gaudin model with a coupling inversely proportional to time, for time evolution starting from the ground state at t = 0 + t=0^{+} and for arbitrary s s . Contrary to common belief, the resulting wavefunction cannot be derived from the spin- 1 / 2 1/2 case by merging spins, but instead requires independent treatment for each spin size. The steady state is non-thermal and, in contrast to the spin- 1 / 2 1/2 case, does not conform to a natural Generalized Gibbs Ensemble. We show that mean-field theory is exact for any product of a finite number of spin operators on different sites. We discuss how these findings can be probed in cavity QED and trapped ion experiments.



I
Introduction

Explicit time-dependent Hamiltonians are ubiquitous across all domains of quantum physics. The time dependence may take various forms, such as a quantum quench—a step-like change in parameters
Fan and García García 2024
—or a continuous, often monotonic variation, as encountered in Landau-Zener (LZ) problems
Landau 1932
;
Zener and Fowler 1997
, atom-atom and atom-ion collisions
Delos 1981
, and nuclear dynamics
Nakatsukasa et al. 2016
. Alternatively, it can be periodic, as in Floquet protocols
Bai et al. 2021
;
Claeys et al. 2019
;
Mizuta and Fujii 2023
, which are used, for example, in studies of time crystals
Wilczek 2012
;
Yao and Nayak 2018
;
Khemani et al. 2019
, the generation of gauge potentials
Goldman and Dalibard 2014
, and spin physics
Geier et al. 2021
in cold atom experiments, as well as in studies of integrable models
Gritsev and Polkovnikov 2017
;
Fan et al. 2020
. The study of such systems remains central to quantum simulation, sensing, and computation, and is closely related to quantum optimal control
d’Alessandro 2021
. Here, the goal is to evolve an easily prepared initial state of the system into a desired target state. Designing efficient protocols for such state preparation is an active area of research
Yuan et al. 2019
;
Cerezo et al. 2021
, with the two standard paradigms being discrete (gate-based)
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
;
Georgescu et al. 2014
;
Fauseweh 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2022
and continuous time evolution
Albash and Lidar 2018
;
Monroe et al. 2021
.

In most relevant situations, time-dependent many-body Hamiltonians are not amenable to exact treatment. This necessitates further simplifications, such as mean-field analysis
Senese et al. 2024
or numerical perturbation theory
Puzzuoli et al. 2023
, which often limit these models to weak couplings, small Hilbert spaces, or short-time evolutions. As a result, they remain notoriously difficult to solve analytically. In this context,
exactly solvable models
remain an important recourse, serving as reference points for numerical and approximate methods, and offering clear analytical guidance in the design of quantum state preparation protocols. A well-studied class of such models is periodically driven systems, where, by exploiting their underlying Lie-algebraic structure, exact solutions have been constructed in both closed
Gritsev and Polkovnikov 2017
;
Fan et al. 2020
;
Oka and Kitamura 2019
and open
Scopa et al. 2018
;
Scopa et al. 2019
;
Bakker et al. 2020
settings. More generally, it has been conjectured
Patra and Yuzbashyan 2015
that a
necessary
condition for the exact solvability of a time-dependent quantum Hamiltonian is its quantum integrability, in the sense of possessing nontrivial parameter-dependent commuting partners. A corresponding
sufficient
condition was later formulated
Sinitsyn et al. 2018
as the existence of a non-Abelian gauge field with zero curvature in the space of system parameters. This framework has unified existing solvable models under the broader umbrella of
integrable time-dependent models

Yuzbashyan 2018
;
Sun et al. 2019
;
Chernyak et al. 2020
;
Chernyak and Sinitsyn 2021
;
Malla et al. 2022
;
Zabalo et al. 2022
;
Barik et al. 2025
;
Suzuki et al. 2025
.

An important class of such solvable models comprises Gaudin magnets and their generalizations, such as the Richardson-Gaudin (RG), inhomogeneous Dicke (also known as Tavis-Cummings), and various central spin Hamiltonians. The celebrated BCS theory maps onto the spin-
1/2
RG model when expressed in terms of Anderson pseudospins—bilinear combinations of fermionic creation and annihilation operators
Anderson 1958
;
Coleman 2015
. In the time-independent case, this model has been solved using a classical version of the Bethe ansatz
Richardson and Sherman 1964a
;
Richardson 1965
;
Gaudin and Caux 2014
;
Sklyanin 1989
. Introducing time dependence in the Hamiltonian parameters generally breaks integrability. However, for certain special choices—in particular, when the interaction strength is inversely proportional to time—the model remains integrable
Sinitsyn et al. 2018
;
Yuzbashyan 2018
;
Zabalo et al. 2022
. Closely related integrable models include generalized bosonic and fermionic Tavis-Cummings Hamiltonians with linear time dependence
Sun et al. 2019
;
Malla et al. 2022
;
Suzuki et al. 2025
.

Time-dependent RG models also give rise to a variety of exactly solvable multilevel LZ problems that have attracted considerable attention over the past decades
Child 1971
;
Bandrauk 1972
;
Osherov and Ushakov 1996
;
Ostrovsky 2003
;
Sinitsyn 2014
;
Barik et al. 2025
, with applications ranging from ion-atom collisions
Nikitin 1970
and transitions between vibrational modes
Mies 1964
to Rydberg transitions
Nascimento et al. 2009
;
Baumgartner and Helm 2010
;
Feynman et al. 2015
and molecular collisions
Tantawi et al. 2000
.

The dominant focus so far has been on spin-
1/2
RG models. The prevailing assumption is that the physics of higher-spin models can be obtained from the spin-
1/2
case by appropriately combining spins and projecting onto the relevant subspace. For example, an effective spin-1 Hilbert space can be constructed by combining two spin-
1/2
particles and projecting onto the triplet subspace, as relevant for the spin-1 BCS problem (see also Sec.
V.3
). This approach is closely analogous to the construction of the paradigmatic AKLT model
Affleck et al. 1987
, or to numerical studies of the Haldane gap in alternating-bond Heisenberg spin chains
Hung and Gong 2005
. Gaudin employed this strategy to derive the exact solution of higher-spin Gaudin magnets from the spin-
1/2
case
Gaudin and Caux 2014
: “It is sufficient to bring the
\varepsilon
together in packets to obtain operators
\ldots
the size of each spin being arbitrary.” What is meant here is that the solution to the higher-spin Gaudin model is obtained from the spin-
1/2
problem by merging several spin-
1/2
degrees of freedom, as described above. While Gaudin’s assertion is entirely correct, we note that he considered only time-independent Hamiltonians.

In this work, we show that, in striking contrast to the time-independent case, for time-dependent RG Hamiltonians one
cannot
obtain higher-spin solutions from the spin-
1/2
ones by following a similar procedure. Consequently, it becomes necessary to analyze spin-
s
RG models on a case-by-case basis for different spin magnitudes
s
, without expecting solutions for lower spins to recover their higher-spin counterparts. Moreover, we find that the late-time asymptotic wavefunctions of higher-spin RG models do not conform to the natural emergent generalized Gibbs ensemble (GGE), unlike in the spin-
1/2
case
Zabalo et al. 2022
.

We begin by presenting a simple example of two spins, illustrating the fundamental difference between the time-dependent and time-independent cases, followed by a brief introduction to the Richardson-Gaudin Hamiltonian.

The first major result of this paper is the derivation of the exact long-time dynamics of the many-body wavefunctions for spin-1 and spin-
3/2
systems
(Sec.
II
and Sec.
III
, respectively). The novelty of this analysis lies in the incorporation of the exact solution for a two-site spin-
s
time-dependent RG Hamiltonian, recently obtained in
Barik et al. 2025
. By comparing the asymptotic form of the two-site wavefunction with results from a saddle-point analysis, we extract the full
t\rightarrow\infty
asymptotic wavefunction of the corresponding
N
-body RG Hamiltonian. These results extend the limited set of known exact solutions for time-dependent many-body Hamiltonians.

The second major result is a partial solution to the general time-dependent spin-
s
RG Hamiltonian, presented in Sec.
III
. This solution is complete up to a corrective term. We propose a conjectural form, based on previous results and supported by numerical evidence, which may also solve the spin-2 RG Hamiltonian.

In Sec.
IV
, we compute the thermodynamic limit of local observables for the spin-1 model and, in Sec.
V
, investigate the corresponding steady states for the spin-1 and spin-
3/2
models. The third major result is the finding that these steady states conform to an ensemble
different from
the standard GGE. This contrasts with the spin-
1/2
case studied in
Sinitsyn et al. 2018
;
Zabalo et al. 2022
, which does converge to a GGE distribution. We also briefly comment on the general spin-
s
case.

Finally, in Sec.
VI
, we discuss possible experimental realizations of the theory developed in this work. Higher-spin representations of RG Hamiltonians arise naturally in systems with twofold degeneracies, such as in graphene
Andrei and MacDonald 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2025
, superconducting ladders
Dagotto et al. 1992
;
Takahashi et al. 2015
;
Jiang et al. 2018
, and double-layer structures in the strong co-tunneling regime
Zhou et al. 2021
;
Guo et al. 2025
. Motivated by the high degree of control in current cold atom and ion experiments, we identify experimental platforms that feature the two main ingredients of our model: spin-1 (or more generally, spin-
s
) degrees of freedom and all-to-all spin connectivity. These conditions are naturally realized in cavity QED
Mivehvar et al. 2021
and trapped ion setups
Monroe et al. 2021
;
Schneider et al. 2012
, and we argue that the physics studied here can be probed using existing experiments, such as those in
Davis et al. 2019
;
Kotibhaskar et al. 2024
, as detailed in Sec.
VI
.

Non-analytic behavior

We begin by illustrating the non-analytic behavior with respect to merging spin degrees of freedom with a simple example.
Consider a two-site spin-
1/2
BCS Hamiltonian with an interaction strength inversely proportional to time:

\hat{H}_{\alpha}(t)=2\varepsilon_{1}\hat{s}_{1}^{z}+2\varepsilon_{2}\hat{s}_{2}^{z}-\frac{1}{\nu t^{\alpha}}(\hat{s}_{1}^{+}+\hat{s}_{2}^{+})(\hat{s}_{1}^{-}+\hat{s}_{2}^{-})

(1)

Here,
\varepsilon_{1,2}
are the on-site Zeeman fields,
\nu>0
is the ramp rate,
\hat{s}^{(x,\,y,\,z)}
are spin-
1/2
operators, and
\hat{s}^{\pm}=\hat{s}^{x}\pm i\hat{s}^{y}
. The exponent
\alpha
is a positive real number. The
4\times 4
Hamiltonian in Eq. (
1
) is block-diagonalized according to the eigenvalues of the total magnetization
\hat{J}^{z}=\hat{s}_{1}^{z}+\hat{s}_{2}^{z}
. This yields three blocks, from which we focus on the largest sub-block corresponding to
J^{z}=0
. This sub-block is identified as a
2\times 2
hyperbolic Landau-Zener problem
Barik et al. 2025
. In the singlet-triplet basis

\begin{split}\ket{\sigma_{z}=-1}&=\ket{1,0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}+\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}\right),\\
\ket{\sigma_{z}=+1}&=\ket{0,0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}-\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}\right),\end{split}

(2)

this sub-block takes the form:

\hat{H}^{0}_{\alpha}(t)=\frac{1}{\nu t^{\alpha}}\sigma^{z}-\Delta\sigma^{x},

(3)

where
\Delta=\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}
,
\sigma^{x,\,z}
are the Pauli matrices, and we have omitted a multiple of the
2\times 2
identity matrix, as it does not affect the transition probabilities.

It is also helpful to introduce a dimensionless
time variable
\tau=|\Delta|t
. The non-stationary Schrödinger equation becomes

i\frac{d\ket{\Psi(\tau)}}{d\tau}=\hat{H}^{0}_{\alpha}(\tau)\ket{\Psi(\tau)},

(4)

where

\hat{H}^{0}_{\alpha}(\tau)=\frac{|\Delta|^{\alpha-1}}{\nu\tau^{\alpha}}\sigma^{z}-\text{sgn}(\Delta)\sigma^{x}.

(5)

Note that we define the sign function so that
\text{sgn}(0)=0
.

It is straightforward to see that
\alpha=1
exhibits special behavior in the limit
\Delta\to 0
, which corresponds to merging the two spin-
1/2
degrees of freedom, as proposed by Gaudin and discussed above. Let us initialize the system in the triplet state
\ket{1,0}
at
\tau=0^{+}
and consider the transition probability
P_{\ket{1,0}\rightarrow\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}}
at
\tau\gg 1
. The curves in Fig
1
display a sharp discontinuity at
\Delta=0
for
\alpha=1\,
—a feature absent when
\alpha\neq 1
. We note that
\alpha=1
corresponds to the integrable model
Sinitsyn et al. 2018
;
Yuzbashyan 2018
;
Zabalo et al. 2022
;
Barik et al. 2025
. Thus, the observed non-analytic behavior in this finite-size system is a signature of integrability and persists for any
\nu>0
. To see this explicitly, consider two scenarios:

(A)

Evolve the system and then take the limit
\Delta\to 0
.

(B)

Set
\Delta=0
first and then evolve the system.

In scenario (B), the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
3
) is proportional to
\sigma^{z}
. Since the initial state is an eigenstate of
\sigma^{z}
, the system remains in that state throughout the evolution from any given initial time, yielding
P_{\ket{1,0}\rightarrow\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}}=\frac{1}{2}
.

In scenario (A) we initialize the system in the triplet state at a small
\tau_{\mathrm{in}}
and evolve it to a large
\tau_{\mathrm{fn}}
. For
\alpha=1
, the transition probabilities in scenario (A) are known explicitly for any
\tau_{\mathrm{in}}
and
\tau_{\mathrm{fn}}

Barik et al. 2025
. These depend only on the sign of
\Delta
[as already evident from Eqs. (
5
) and (
4
)], and
P_{\ket{1,0}\rightarrow\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}}
is greater than
1/2
for any
\Delta>0
and less than
1/2
for any
\Delta<0
, at any finite
\nu
. Therefore, there is a discontinuity at
\Delta=0
for
\alpha=1
.

Now consider scenario (A) for
\alpha\neq 1
. At small
|\Delta|
, the first term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
5
) dominates for
\alpha>1
and becomes negligible for
\alpha<1
, so the system effectively evolves with a Hamiltonian proportional to
\sigma^{z}
or
\sigma^{x}
, respectively. In either case,
P_{\ket{1,0}\rightarrow\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}}
tends to
1/2
in the limit
\Delta\to 0
. Hence, the transition probability is a continuous function of
\Delta
for all
\alpha\neq 1
.

II
The Spin-1 Model

We begin with the spin-1 time-dependent Richardson-Gaudin Hamiltonian

\hat{H}(t)=2\sum_{j=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{j}\hat{s}_{j}^{z}-g(t)\sum_{j,k=1}^{N}\hat{s}_{j}^{+}\hat{s}_{k}^{-},

(6)

where the time-dependent coupling,

g(t)=\frac{1}{\nu t}

(7)

is taken to be identical to that in the spin-
1/2
case studied in
Zabalo et al. 2022
, and
N
is the number of spins. The operators
\hat{s}^{z}
,
\hat{s}^{+}
, and
\hat{s}^{-}
are spin-1 operators that satisfy the standard
\mathfrak{su}(2)
algebra, with each single-site Hilbert space spanned by the eigenstates of
\hat{s}^{z}
:
{\ket{-1},\ket{0},\ket{1}}
.

Since Eq.(
6
) conserves the
z
-component of the total spin,

\boldsymbol{\hat{J}}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}_{k},

(8)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}_{k}=(\hat{s}^{x}_{k},\hat{s}^{y}_{k},\hat{s}^{z}_{k})
and
[\hat{H},\hat{J}^{z}]=0
, the Hamiltonian can be block-diagonalized into sectors labeled by different values of
J^{z}
. In the remainder of the text, we refer to the first (second) term in Eq. (
6
) as the Zeeman (interaction) term, respectively.

II.1
Ground state of the model

At the initial time
t=0^{+}
, up to a divergent multiplicative constant, the Hamiltonian (
6
) becomes
\hat{H}_{\text{int}}\propto\boldsymbol{\hat{J}}^{+}\boldsymbol{\hat{J}}^{-}
. After fixing the total magnetization
J^{z}
, the lowest energy state in a given
N_{+}=N+J^{z}
sector is a symmetric superposition of all states obtained by applying
N_{+}
raising operations to the pseudo-vacuum
\ket{\odot}\equiv\ket{-1}^{\otimes N}
. This state is given, up to normalization, by

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi^{\scalebox{0.55}{$(N_{+})$}}\scalebox{0.85}{$(0)$}}\propto

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{\hat{J}}^{+}\right)^{\!N_{+}}\ket{\odot}

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left|\{\alpha\}\right|+2\left|\{\beta\}\right|\\
=N_{+}\end{subarray}}2^{\frac{N_{1}}{2}}\ket{\{\alpha\}}\ket{\{\beta\}}\ket{\oslash},

where
\ket{\{\alpha\}}
and
\ket{\{\beta\}}
are product states composed of
\ket{0}
at positions
\{\alpha\}\equiv(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{N_{1}})
, and
\ket{+1}
at positions
\{\beta\}\equiv(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{N_{2}})
, while
\ket{\oslash}
is a product of
\ket{-1}
over all remaining sites. Here,
\alpha_{1}<\ldots<\alpha_{N_{1}}
and
\beta_{1}<\ldots<\beta_{N_{2}}
, and we refer to the
\alpha
(
\beta
) sites as singly (doubly) raised. The sum includes
all possible ordered sets

\{\alpha\}
and
\{\beta\}
satisfying
N_{+}=\left|\{\alpha\}\right|+2\left|\{\beta\}\right|
.

Alternatively, one may express
\ket{\Psi^{\scalebox{0.55}{$(N_{+})$}}\scalebox{0.85}{$(0)$}}
in Eq. (
9
) as a projected spin-1 BCS state:

\ket{\Psi^{\scalebox{0.55}{$(N_{+})$}}\scalebox{0.85}{$(0)$}}\propto P_{N_{+}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}\left(\ket{-1}+\sqrt{2}\ket{0}+\ket{1}\right),

(10)

where
P_{N_{+}}
is the projector onto states with fixed
J^{z}
.

In what follows, we refer to this lowest energy state (with fixed
N_{+}
) simply as the ground state.

The formal solution of the wavefunction

In this work, we use the solution of the non-stationary Schrödinger equation for the Hamiltonian (
6
), derived in
Yuzbashyan 2018
;

by means of the off-shell Bethe ansatz method. This method was pioneered in

and further developed in
Sedrakyan and Galitski 2010
;
Fioretto et al. 2014
. The general solution
\ket{\Psi^{\scalebox{0.55}{$(N_{+})$}}\scalebox{0.85}{$(t)$}}

is given by an
N_{+}
– fold contour integral over variables
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N_{+}}

\ket{\Psi^{\scalebox{0.55}{$(N_{+})$}}\scalebox{0.85}{$(t)$}}=\oint_{\chi}d\boldsymbol{\lambda}\exp\left(-\frac{i\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\varepsilon,t)}{\nu}\right)\Xi(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\varepsilon),

(11)

where

\Xi(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\varepsilon)=\prod_{\lambda_{r}\in\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\hat{L}^{+}\left(\lambda_{r}\right)\ket{\odot},\;\hat{L}^{+}(\lambda_{r})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\hat{s}_{j}^{+}}{\lambda_{r}-\varepsilon_{j}}.

(12)

The quantity
\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\varepsilon,t)
, known as the Yang-Yang action, reads

\begin{split}\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\varepsilon,t)=2\nu t&\sum_{\alpha}\lambda_{\alpha}+2s\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{\alpha}\ln\left(\varepsilon_{j}-\lambda_{\alpha}\right)\\
&-\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}\ln\left(\lambda_{\beta}-\lambda_{\alpha}\right).\end{split}

(13)

For the remainder of this section, we set

. Higher spins are considered in Sec.
III
. The choice of the contour
\chi

11

\ket{\Psi^{\scalebox{0.55}{$(N_{+})$}}\scalebox{0.85}{$(t)$}}

\ket{\Psi^{\scalebox{0.55}{$(N_{+})$}}\scalebox{0.85}{$(0)$}}
in (
9

II.3
Saddle point analysis

We use the formal solution (
11
) of the model to determine the long-time asymptotic wavefunction
\ket{\Psi_{\scalebox{0.75}{$\infty$}}^{\scalebox{0.55}{$(N_{+})$}}\scalebox{0.85}{$(t)$}}
.

Specifically, we apply the saddle point method
Fedoryuk 1989
to evaluate the integral (
11
) in the limit
t\rightarrow\infty^{+}
. In this regime, the integral localizes around the stationary points of the Yang-Yang action, determined by the equations
\partial\mathcal{S}/\partial\lambda_{p}=0
, which take the form

\nu t+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\lambda_{p}-\varepsilon_{j}}=\sum_{j\neq p}\frac{1}{\lambda_{p}-\lambda_{j}},\;p=1,\dots,N_{+}.

(14)

These equations are known as the Richardson equations
Richardson 1963
;
Richardson and Sherman 1964b
;
Richardson 1977
, which play a central role in computing the exact spectrum of time-independent RG Hamiltonians. In our context, they determine the instantaneous spectrum at time
t
. We also note that at
t=0^{+}
, all
\lambda_{p}
associated with the ground state diverge as
(\nu t)^{-1}

Yuzbashyan et al. 2003
. This behavior implies that the contour
\chi
must be deformable toward infinity in order to enclose all
\varepsilon_{j}
without encountering any essential singularities.

As
t\to\infty
, each
\lambda_{p}
tends toward one of the
\varepsilon_{p}
. Moreover, the
\frac{1}{\lambda_{p}-\varepsilon_{p}}
term in (
14
) must compensate the divergent
\nu t
term. This suggests the following ansatz for the solution:

\lambda_{p}=\varepsilon_{p}+\frac{z_{p}}{t},

(15)

which we substitute into Eq. (
14
) and solve for
z_{p}
.

In this limit, the raising operator
L^{+}(\lambda_{p})\to\frac{\hat{s}_{p}^{+}}{\lambda_{p}-\varepsilon_{p}}
.
In the spin-1 problem, two raising operations are allowed per site—namely,
\ket{-1}\rightarrow\ket{0}
and
\ket{0}\rightarrow\ket{1}
. Therefore, at most two
\lambda_{p}
can converge to the same
\varepsilon_{p}
. By contrast, in the spin-
1/2
case, only a single raising operation (
\ket{\downarrow}\rightarrow\ket{\uparrow}
) is permitted, and hence each
\lambda_{p}
approaches a unique
\varepsilon_{p}
.
Thus, for the spin-1 problem, we first identify the pairings
(\lambda_{q}^{+},\lambda_{q}^{-})
that converge to the same
\varepsilon_{q}
, while the remaining
\lambda_{p}^{\circ}
each converge to distinct
\varepsilon_{p}
.

(39b)

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{j}

(39c)

IV.2
Classical BCS dynamics

To compare local observables with the mean-field predictions, we require the mean-field wavefunction from Eq. (
6
). In the mean-field picture, each quantum spin evolves independently under an effective magnetic field. Each spin is in a coherent state and remains so at all times. The wavefunction is then written similarly to a BCS product state, in terms of coherent states at each site:

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi_{\text{mf}}(t)}

\displaystyle=\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\left(u_{k}\ket{-1}+v_{k}\ket{0}+w_{k}\ket{1}\right),

(41a)

\displaystyle u_{k}

\displaystyle=e^{i\phi_{k}(t)}\sin\left(\frac{\theta_{k}(t)}{2}\right)^{2},

(41b)

\displaystyle v_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sin(\theta_{k}(t))\,,

(41c)

\displaystyle w_{k}

\displaystyle=e^{-i\phi_{k}(t)}\cos\left(\frac{\theta_{k}(t)}{2}\right)^{2}.

(41d)

The angles
\phi_{k}(t),\theta_{k}(t)
are time-dependent. For the normalization,

|u_{k}|^{2}+|v_{k}|^{2}+|w_{k}|^{2}=1
. The remaining task is to determine the angles from the mean-field average of
\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}_{k}
for
s=1
as calculated in
Zabalo et al. 2022
:

\displaystyle\braket{\hat{o}_{k}}_{\text{mf}}\equiv\bra{\Psi_{\text{mf}}(t)}\hat{o}_{k}\ket{\Psi_{\text{mf}}(t)},

(42a)

\displaystyle\;\zeta_{k}\equiv\pi(k-\mu)/\nu,

(42b)

\displaystyle\braket{\hat{s}_{k}^{z}}_{\text{mf}}=\cos(\theta_{k}(t))=-\tanh\left(2\zeta_{k}\right),

(42c)

\displaystyle\braket{\hat{s}_{k}^{-}}_{\text{mf}}=e^{-i\phi_{k}(t)}\sin(\theta_{k}(t))=\frac{e^{-2i\varepsilon_{k}t+i\varphi_{k}}}{\cosh(2\zeta_{k})},

(42d)

\displaystyle\braket{\hat{s}_{k}^{+}}_{\text{mf}}=\braket{\hat{s}_{k}^{-}}_{\text{mf}}^{*},

(42e)

\displaystyle\;\varphi_{k}=\frac{2}{\nu}\sum_{j\neq k}\braket{\hat{s}_{j}^{z}}_{\text{mf}}\ln|\varepsilon_{j}-\varepsilon_{k}|.

(42f)

The chemical potential
\mu
is determined from the below constraint that

-\sum_{k=1}^{N}\tanh\left(\frac{2\pi(k-\mu)}{\nu}\right)=J^{z}

(43)

for some fixed total spin

. Finally we provide the expressions for the angles
\theta_{k}(t)
and
\phi_{k}(t)
that complete the expression of Eq. (

\displaystyle\theta_{k}(t)=\pi-\cos^{-1}\left(\tanh(2\zeta_{k}\right)),

(44a)

\displaystyle\phi_{k}(t)=2\varepsilon_{k}t-\varphi_{k}.

(44b)

IV.3.1
Primary considerations

In the thermodynamic limit, the Zeeman term in the Hamiltonian (
6

N
, while the interaction term
\sum_{j,k}\hat{s}^{+}_{j}\hat{s}^{-}_{k}
scales as
N^{2}

g(t)=\frac{1}{\nu t}
must be scaled to offset the

(45)

where

In order to obtain properly normalized quantities, we evaluate
\braket{\Psi_{\scalebox{0.75}{$\infty$}}^{\scalebox{0.55}{$(N_{+})$}}\scalebox{0.85}{$(t)$}|\Psi_{\scalebox{0.75}{$\infty$}}^{\scalebox{0.55}{$(N_{+})$}}\scalebox{0.85}{$(t)$}}
. Since the operators
\hat{U}_{k},\hat{V}_{k}
and
\hat{W}_{k}
all commute with each other for arbitrary sites and

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{U}_{k}=

\displaystyle\mathbf{I},

(46)

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{V}_{k}=

\displaystyle 2\cosh\left(\frac{\pi}{\nu}\right)e^{-\frac{4\pi k}{\nu}}\mathbf{I},

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{W}_{k}=

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{8\pi k}{\nu}}\mathbf{I},

we find
2
2

2

note the change of variables
\xi=\phi-\phi^{\prime}
where
\phi^{\prime}
comes from
\bra{\Psi_{\scalebox{0.75}{$\infty$}}^{\scalebox{0.55}{$(N_{+})$}}\scalebox{0.85}{$(t)$}}

\braket{\Psi_{\scalebox{0.75}{$\infty$}}^{\scalebox{0.55}{$(N_{+})$}}\scalebox{0.85}{$(t)$}|\Psi_{\scalebox{0.75}{$\infty$}}^{\scalebox{0.55}{$(N_{+})$}}\scalebox{0.85}{$(t)$}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{2\pi}_{-2\pi}d\xi\exp\left[NG(\xi)\right],

(47)

where

\begin{split}&G(\xi)=i\xi\frac{N_{+}}{N}+\\
&\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\ln\left[1+e^{-i\xi}\left(e^{\frac{\pi}{\nu}}+e^{-\frac{\pi}{\nu}}\right)e^{-\frac{4\pi j}{\nu}}+e^{-2i\xi}e^{-\frac{8\pi j}{\nu}}\right].\end{split}

This integral further simplifies into a summation:

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N_{1}+2N_{2}\\

or smaller

\xi_{0}^{(1)}=i\ln\left[\frac{1-\exp\left(\frac{2\pi N_{+}}{\nu}\right)}{\exp\left(-\frac{2\pi(2N-N_{+})}{\nu}\right)-1}\right].

(50)

IV.3.2
Multi-site operators

Consider the following notation for an arbitrary (bi-linear) single-site operator:

\hat{s}^{p}_{i}\hat{s}^{q}_{i},\quad p,q\in\{0,+,-,z\},\quad\hat{s}^{0}=\mathbf{I}_{3\times 3}.

(51)

Using this, we define an
n
-local operator
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
for some fixed positive integer
n
as

\hat{\mathcal{B}}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(\hat{s}^{p_{i}}_{i}\hat{s}^{q_{i}}_{i}\right),\quad p_{i},q_{i}\in\{0,+,-,z\}.

(52)

It turns out that the specific choice of sites
i
used to construct
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
is immaterial. Without loss of generality, we may choose the ordered set
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
for convenience.

We are interested in evaluating the normalized non-zero matrix elements of
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
between the states given in Eq. (
38
) for different
N_{+}

\mathcal{N}_{\Delta N_{+}}\bra{\Psi_{\scalebox{0.75}{$\infty$}}^{\scalebox{0.55}{$(N_{+}\!+\!\Delta N_{+})$}}\scalebox{0.85}{$(t)$}}\hat{\mathcal{B}}\ket{\Psi_{\scalebox{0.75}{$\infty$}}^{\scalebox{0.55}{$(N_{+})$}}\scalebox{0.85}{$(t)$}}

(53)

in the limit
N\to\infty
.
\Delta N_{+}
is defined as the difference between the number of raising operations and lowering operations involved within
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
. The
\mathcal{N}_{\Delta N_{+}}
term accounts for the normalization of the bra/ket states used in (
53
). Since the noncommutativity of
\hat{\varphi}_{j}
with
\hat{s}_{i}^{+,-}
contributes only at order
\mathcal{O}(1/N)
, we are justified in replacing the operators
\hat{s}_{i}^{z}
and
(\hat{s}^{z}_{i})^{2}
in the exponents of (
39

\hat{s}_{i}^{z}\rightarrow\braket{\hat{s}_{i}^{z}},\quad(\hat{s}^{z}_{i})^{2}\rightarrow\braket{(\hat{s}^{z}_{i})^{2}}.

(54)

These substitutions effectively replace the
\hat{s}^{z}
operators and their powers in the wavefunction (
38
) with scalars, thereby eliminating the operator ordering issues that arise due to the noncommutativity with
\hat{\varphi}
.

To calculate the non-zero matrix element of any single-site operator
\hat{s}_{j}^{p}\hat{s}_{j}^{q}
, we first evaluate the below (unnormalized) matrix element

\begin{split}&\braket{\Psi_{\scalebox{0.75}{$\infty$}}^{\scalebox{0.55}{$(N_{+}\!+\!\Delta{(p,q)})$}}(t)|\hat{s}_{j}^{p}\hat{s}_{j}^{q}|\Psi_{\scalebox{0.75}{$\infty$}}^{\scalebox{0.55}{$(N_{+})$}}(t)}=\\
&\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-2\pi}^{2\pi}d\xi\,\exp\left[NG(\xi)\right]\,g^{(p,q)}_{j}(\xi).\end{split}

(55)

We refer to
g^{(p,q)}_{j}(\xi)
as a
g
-function
and denote by
\Delta{(p,q)}
the difference between the number of raising (
+
) and lowering (
-
) symbols in
\{p,q\}
. These
g
-functions depend on the specific local operator under consideration. The unnormalized matrix element for the operator
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
defined in Eq. (
53
), without
\mathcal{N}
, is written analogously to (
55
), except that the
g

g(\xi)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}^{(p_{i},q_{i})}(\xi).

(56)

Note that equations (
47
) and (
55
) share the same stationary point
\xi_{0}^{(1)}
given in (
50
). This allows us to immediately evaluate Eq. (
53
) to order
\mathcal{O}(1/N)
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\Delta N_{+}}\bra{\Psi_{\scalebox{0.75}{$\infty$}}^{\scalebox{0.55}{$(N_{+}\!+\!\Delta N_{+})$}}\scalebox{0.85}{$(t)$}}\hat{\mathcal{B}}\ket{\Psi_{\scalebox{0.75}{$\infty$}}^{\scalebox{0.55}{$(N_{+})$}}\scalebox{0.85}{$(t)$}}

(57)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{i}{2}\xi_{0}^{(1)}\Delta N_{+}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}^{(p_{i},q_{i})}(\xi_{0}^{(1)})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}e^{-\frac{i}{2}\xi_{0}^{(1)}\Delta(p_{i},q_{i})}g_{i}^{(p_{i},q_{i})}(\xi_{0}^{(1)})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{N}_{\Delta(p_{i},q_{i})}\bra{\Psi_{\scalebox{0.75}{$\infty$}}^{\scalebox{0.55}{$(N_{+}\!+\!\Delta{(p_{i},q_{i})})$}}\scalebox{0.85}{$(t)$}}\hat{s}_{i}^{p_{i}}\hat{s}_{i}^{q_{i}}\ket{\Psi_{\scalebox{0.75}{$\infty$}}^{\scalebox{0.55}{$(N_{+})$}}\scalebox{0.85}{$(t)$}}.

Thus, the non-zero matrix element of any
n
-local operator from Eq. (
52

or smaller, and therefore vanish in the thermodynamic limit

Returning to the general

V
Steady State

Having addressed the thermodynamic limit of
n
-local observables, we now turn to the long-time behavior of Eq. (
6
) for spin-1, spin-3/2, and spin-2 systems. We investigate the steady-state properties of the RG model in the
N\to\infty

V.1
Probability distribution

At large times (
t\gg 1
) the interaction term in (
6
) becomes suppressed, which only leaves the Zeeman term,
2\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}\hat{s}_{i}^{z}
. The spin-
s
steady state of interest is the asymptotic state given in Eq. (
34
), in which each basis state can be rewritten as follows:

\bigotimes_{\{\alpha^{(j)}\}\in\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\ket{\{\alpha^{(j)}\}}\otimes\ket{\oslash}=\ket{\{s^{z}\}},\;\;\ket{\{s^{z}\}}\equiv\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\ket{s^{z}_{i}}.

(75)

The
configuration
of on-site magnetization
\{s^{z}\}\equiv\{s^{z}_{1},\dots,s^{z}_{N}\}
, for each
\ket{\{s^{z}\}}
is determined as follows: for all
m=1,\dots,2s
and for every site
i\in\{\alpha^{(m)}\}
,
s^{z}_{i}=-s+m
; for the remaining sites
k
not included in any
\{\alpha^{(m)}\}
,
s^{z}_{k}=-s
. With this, the probability distribution
P_{s}(\{s^{z}\})
for the system having configuration
\{s^{z}\}
is easily determined from the asymptotic wavefunction in Eq. (
34
). The general form of
P_{s}(\{s^{z}\})
is

P_{s}(\{s^{z}\})=\frac{1}{Z}e^{-\sum_{j}\left(\frac{4s\pi js_{j}^{z}}{\nu}+\varrho_{j}^{(s)}(\nu)\right)}\!\delta\!\left(J^{z}-\sum_{k}s_{k}^{z}\right).

(76)

Here,
Z
is the partition function and
\varrho_{j}^{(s)}
are the additional terms that are determined from the expression of
\gamma
in Eq. (
35d
). We provide
\varrho_{j}^{(s)}(\nu)
for different spin magnitudes
s
from
1/2
to
2
as follows:

\varrho_{j}^{(1/2)}(\nu)=0,

(77a)

\varrho_{j}^{(1)}(\nu)=(s_{j}^{z})^{2}\ln\left(2\cosh\frac{\pi}{\nu}\right),

(77b)

\varrho_{j}^{(3/2)}(\nu)=\frac{1}{2}(s_{j}^{z})^{2}\ln\left[2\cosh\left(\frac{2\pi}{\nu}\right)+1\right],

(77c)

\begin{split}\varrho_{j}^{(2)}(\nu)=&\left[\frac{7}{12}(s_{j}^{z})^{2}-\frac{1}{12}(s_{j}^{z})^{4}\right]\log\left(\frac{3^{\frac{5}{2}}}{4^{\frac{5}{2}}}\frac{\sinh\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}\pi}{\sqrt{8}\nu}\right)}{\sqrt{2}\sinh\left(\frac{\pi}{4\nu}\right)}\right)+\\
&\frac{1}{2}(s_{j}^{z})^{2}\ln\!\left[1+\frac{\sinh\left(\frac{5\pi}{\nu}\right)}{\sinh\left(\frac{3\pi}{\nu}\right)}\right].\end{split}

(77d)

For
s=1/2
, we have used the asymptotic wavefunction from
Zabalo et al. 2022
. For spin-
1
and spin-
3/2
we have utilized (
28
) and (

), respectively. Finally, for
s=2
, we used our conjectured form of the unknown function
h(\nu)
in Eq. (
109
), together with Eq. (
108
). The partition function
Z
is determined from the constraint that the sum of the probabilities over all possible spin configurations must equal unity. The delta function appears due to conservation of the total spin of the system. In the mean-field picture, where one directly deals with a product state, the probability is given by the same function, but without the delta function.

In the thermodynamic limit, however, the probability distribution corresponding to both the asymptotic solution and the mean-field solution become identical up to
\mathcal{O}(1/N)
corrections, as demonstrated in Figs.
8
and
9
. Focusing specifically on the case
s=1
, the distribution becomes

P_{1}(\{s^{z}\})\approx C_{p}\exp\left[-\sum_{j}\left(\frac{4\pi(j-\mu_{(1)})s_{j}^{z}}{\nu}+\varrho_{j}^{(1)}(\nu)\right)\right],

(78)

where
C_{p}
is a normalization constant.

V.2
Non-GGE predictions

At
t\rightarrow\infty
, the eigenstate of the Hamiltonian (
6
) is given by

\ket{\psi(t)}=\sum_{\{s^{z}\}}C_{\{s^{z}\}}e^{-iE_{\{s^{z}\}}t}\ket{\{s^{z}\}},

(79)

where
E_{\{s^{z}\}}
is the eigenvalue of the Zeeman term corresponding to the eigenvector
\ket{\{s^{z}\}}
.
The expectation value of any observable
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
is written as

\braket{\hat{\mathcal{B}}}=\sum_{\{s^{z}\}}\left|C_{\{s^{z}\}}\right|^{2}\braket{\{s^{z}\}|\hat{\mathcal{B}}|\{s^{z}\}},

(80)

where the diagonal ensemble
\left|C_{\{s^{z}\}}\right|^{2}
is given by
P_{s}({\{s^{z}\}})
from Eq. (
76
). Hence,
(2s+1)^{N}
coefficients are required to fully determine the observable. At this point, we construct a statistical ensemble
\hat{\rho}_{\text{s.t.}}^{(s)}
as a density matrix, that the system conforms to as
t\rightarrow\infty
. This ensemble incorporates a finite set of integrals of motion of the system, together with
their integer powers
. Focusing on the
s=1
case, these are
s_{k}^{z}
and
(s_{k}^{z})^{2}
for every site index
k
. The construction of the ensemble is given below:

\hat{\rho}_{\text{s.t.}}^{(1)}\propto\exp\left\{-\sum_{k}[\vartheta_{k,1}\hat{s}^{z}_{k}+\vartheta_{k,2}(\hat{s}^{z}_{k})^{2}]\right\},

(81)

where
\vartheta_{k,1},\vartheta_{k,2}\in\mathrm{C}
. In its diagonal basis, which are the eigenstates
\ket{\{s^{z}\}}
, the diagonal elements of (
81
) are

\rho_{\text{s.t.}}^{(1)}(\{s^{z}\})=C_{\rho}e^{-\sum_{k}(\vartheta_{k,1}s^{z}_{k}+\vartheta_{k,2}(s^{z}_{k})^{2})}

(82)

where
C_{\rho}
is a normalization constant. Here we require at most
2N
coefficients
\vartheta
to determine the distribution, against the
(2\times 1+1)^{N}
coefficients
\left|C_{\{s^{z}\}}\right|^{2}
in Eq. (
80
). Comparing (
82
) with (
78
), we find

\displaystyle\vartheta_{k,1}=

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi(k-\mu_{(1)})}{\nu},

(83)

\displaystyle\vartheta_{k,2}=

\displaystyle\ln\left(2\cosh\frac{\pi}{\nu}\right).

This result is different from the case of
s=1/2
described in
Zabalo et al. 2022
where it was found that

\hat{\rho}_{\text{s.t.}}^{(1/2)}\propto\exp\left(-\sum_{k}\frac{2\pi(k-\mu_{(1/2)})}{\nu}\hat{s}^{z}_{k}\right).

(84)

which is equivalent to the Generalized Gibbs Ensemble
\hat{\rho}_{\text{GGE}}
for a integrable Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
, which is

\begin{split}&\hat{\rho}_{\text{GGE}}\propto\exp\left(-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}\hat{I}_{k}\right)\\
&[\hat{H},\hat{I}_{k}]=0,\;\;[\hat{I}_{j},\hat{I}_{k}]=0,\;\;\lambda_{k}\in\mathrm{C}.\end{split}

(85)

In the spin-
1/2
problem, the conserved quantities are
\hat{I}_{k}=\hat{s}^{z}_{k}
for all
k
and
\hat{H}
is given by Eq. (
6
) with the spin-
1/2
operators. The authors in
Zabalo et al. 2022
identify the distribution (
84
) for a system that starts at
t=0^{+}
in its lowest energy state, which is Eq. (
33
) with
s=1/2
, and evolves for a long time thereafter. Accordingly, they refer to this distribution as an “emergent GGE” to emphasize the dynamical context in which it arises. A key distinction in the spin-
1
case is the presence the quadratic terms in Eq. (
82
), which means the system
no longer conforms
to the form of the distribution given in Eq. (
85
). The same deviation holds for the
s=3/2
and
s=2
cases.

V.3
Discrepancy from the spin 1/2 problem

With the available expressions for the probability distributions
P_{s}({s^{z}})
at different spin magnitudes
s
, we now comment on the deviations observed between the solutions of the spin-
1/2
RG Hamiltonian and those for Hamiltonians with
s>1/2
. To illustrate this, we revisit the example mentioned in the Introduction. Consider (
6
) for an arbitrary spin magnitude
s
, but now with a
time-independent
coupling
g
. Starting with
s=1/2
, after we introduce the same Zeeman magnetic field
\varepsilon_{i}
for the site
i
and its neighboring site
i+1
in a pairwise manner, they can be combined into a single larger-spin using the standard Clebsch-Gordon decomposition. An example of this procedure is given below, where a pair of spin-
1/2

\hat{s}^{z}
operators is transformed into a spin-
1

\hat{s}^{z}
via a local unitary transformation
U

\begin{split}&U\left(\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1&\cdot\\
\cdot&-1\end{bmatrix}\otimes\mathrm{I}_{2\times 2}+\mathrm{I}_{2\times 2}\otimes\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1&\cdot\\
\cdot&-1\end{bmatrix}\right)U^{-1}=\\
&\hphantom{=}\begin{bmatrix}1&\cdot&\cdot\\
\cdot&0&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&-1\end{bmatrix}\oplus\mathbf{0}_{1\times 1};\;\;U=\begin{bmatrix}\,1&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\,\\
\cdot&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&1\\
\cdot&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&\frac{-1}{\sqrt{2}}&\cdot\end{bmatrix}.\end{split}

(86)

Here
\mathbf{0}_{1\times 1}
denotes the spin-
0
(singlet) sector. The same procedure applies to
\hat{s}^{\pm}
operators. By making disjoint pairings of all the sites (provided they are also even in number to begin with), we obtain the spin-
1
Hamiltonian after applying local unitary transformations to every pair. We neglect the spin-

components of the model, as the initial spin-
1/2
state comprises only of triplet states which do not couple with singlet sectors. We explicitly consider an initial condition where all
\varepsilon_{j}
are distinct, so that no degeneracies are present. However, one can imagine slowly tuning two adjacent Zeeman fields
\varepsilon_{j}
and
\varepsilon_{j+1}
to become equal, as illustrated schematically in Fig.

In this section we sketch possible pathways towards an experimental realization of the Hamiltonian central to this work. For convenience we repeat the definition of the Hamiltonian below:

\displaystyle\hat{H}(t)

\displaystyle=\hat{H}_{z}+\hat{H}_{\rm int}

(88a)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{z}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{j=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{j}\hat{s}_{j}^{z}

(88b)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\rm int}

\displaystyle=-g(t)\sum_{j,k=1}^{N}\hat{s}_{j}^{+}\hat{s}_{k}^{-},\;\;g(t)=\frac{1}{\nu t}.

(88c)

One possibility for simulating the time-dependent Hamiltonian (
88
) is on a quantum computer. To this end, we note a recent experiment with superconducting circuits, in which ground states of the RG model were prepared
O’Brien et al. 2023
. Given the all-to-all nature of the spin-flip interaction, other candidate platforms include cold atoms coupled to a cavity or spin Hamiltonians engineered in trapped-ion systems.

VI.1
General considerations

First, we address the question of the relevant energy and time scales. The energy scale of the spin-flip interaction term in the Hamiltonian is given by
g(t)
. To characterize the energy scale of the Zeeman term, we consider the standard deviation of the set of Zeeman fields
\{\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{N}\}

\Delta\varepsilon=\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\varepsilon_{i}-\bar{\varepsilon}\right)^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}},

(89)

where
\bar{\varepsilon}
is the mean energy. For the linear ramping of the Zeeman fields,
\varepsilon_{i}=i/N
, as considered in this article, we obtain

\Delta\varepsilon^{2}=\frac{1}{12}\frac{(N+1)N(N-1)}{N^{3}},

(90)

which in the large
N
limit sets
\Delta\varepsilon^{2}\approx 1/12
. With these definitions at hand we define a scaled (dimensionless) time as

\tilde{t}=\frac{\Delta\varepsilon}{g(t)}=\Delta\varepsilon\nu t.

(91)

In our numerical simulation (not shown), we observe that the scaled time
\tilde{t}_{\rm ss}
, after which the steady state is reached to a reasonable accuracy, only depends weakly on the values of
\Delta\varepsilon
and
\nu
such that
\tilde{t}_{\rm ss}\approx{\rm const.}
for a given
N
. Specifically, for
N\lesssim 10
we find that
\tilde{t}_{\rm ss}\sim O(100)
and that it increases with
N
. This is consistent with the results of Ref.
Zabalo et al. 2022
, which analyzed the spin-
1/2
model in large-
N
and mean-field limits, finding that for
\tilde{t}\sim N^{2}
one achieves steady state predictions for
\braket{\hat{s}^{z}_{j}}
with considerable accuracy.

Based on these estimates, we find that the times required to reach the steady state (or even to observe non-trivial transient dynamics) are prohibitive in the cavity setting, which typically involves a large number of atoms (
10^{5}
). We refer the reader to Appendix
E
for further details. There, we also briefly discuss the possibility of using the cavity setup to probe a macroscopic spin limit of the model (
88
). For the remainder of this section, we therefore focus on trapped-ion setups, which permit controlled manipulation of between 1 and
\sim 100
ions.

VI.2
Trapped ions

One of the approaches to implement (
88
) is using trapped ions. To do this, three key ingredients are needed, all of which have been demonstrated experimentally. These are

(i)

all-to-all
equal
interactions,

(ii)

operate spin-1 qudits instead of qubits,

(iii)

being able to simulate the evolution under the full Hamiltonian (
88
) which contains both the spin-exchange interaction (
\propto\hat{s}^{+}\hat{s}^{-}
) and the transverse field (
\propto\hat{s}^{z}
).

The common starting point for deriving effective spin Hamiltonians in
trapped ion experiments is the native Hamiltonian that describes the interaction of a system of
N
trapped ion qubits with a laser light of frequency
\omega_{L}
and
k
-vector
k_{L}

Monroe et al. 2021
;
Schneider et al. 2012
;
Porras and Cirac 2004
(In this Section we use “qubits” for the spin-1/2 systems and “qudits” for higher spin systems. The usual Pauli matrices
\sigma_{x,y,z}
used below pertain to the qubit basis.). Setting
\hbar=1
, we have

\hat{H}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Omega_{i}\cos(\mu t+\varphi_{i})(\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}_{i})\,\sigma^{i}_{\theta_{i}},

(92)

where
\Omega_{i}
is the Rabi frequency coupling the two qubit levels of the
i
-th ion,
\mu=\omega_{L}-\omega_{0}
with
\omega_{0}
the qubit transition frequency,
\varphi_{i}
is an arbitrary offset phase,

\sigma^{i}_{\theta_{i}}=\sigma^{x}_{i}\cos(\theta_{i})+\sigma^{y}_{i}\sin(\theta_{i})

(93)

and

\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}_{j}=\sum_{m}\eta_{jm}\left(a^{\dagger}_{m}{\rm e}^{i\omega_{m}t}+a_{m}{\rm e}^{-i\omega_{m}t}\right).

(94)

Here

\eta_{im}=|\vec{k}_{L}|b_{im}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2M\omega_{m}}}

(95)

is the so-called Lamb-Dicke parameter corresponding to the coupling of the
m
-th motional eigenmode of frequency
\omega_{m}
of the ion crystal to the motion of the
i
-th ion
\vec{x}_{i}
, and
M
is the ion mass. A Magnus expansion is used to derive the unitary evolution operator
U(t)={\cal T}{\rm exp}(-\int^{t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}\,H(t^{\prime}))
corresponding to the (explicitly time-dependent) Hamiltonian (
92
). After what are by now standard derivations and approximations, in particular keeping only the secular terms in
U(t)
, in case of (
92
) this leads to
Kotibhaskar et al. 2024

\hat{H}_{\rm eff}=\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}\sigma^{i}_{\theta_{i}}\sigma^{j}_{\theta_{j}},

(96)

with

J_{ij}=k^{2}_{L}\frac{\Omega_{i}\Omega_{j}}{2M}\sum_{m}\frac{b_{im}b_{jm}}{\mu^{2}-\omega_{m}^{2}}.

(97)

We are in the position to discuss the ingredients (i)–(iii) of the implementation.

(i)

It is well known that the range of effective interactions
J_{ij}
follows a power-law decay, i.e.,
J_{ij}\propto 1/|i-j|^{\alpha}
, where the exponent
\alpha
lies within the range
0\leq\alpha\leq 3
and can be tuned via the laser de-tuning
\mu
. In particular, all-to-all equal couplings, corresponding to
\alpha=0
, can be realized by tuning
\mu\approx\omega_{\text{COM}}
, close to the so-called center-of-mass (COM) mode, as demonstrated experimentally, e.g., in
Britton et al. 2012
.

(ii)

In principle, the above equations (
92
)–(
97
) can be readily generalized to qudits, and specifically to spin-1. To this end, there is ongoing work to extend the ion-based quantum simulation toolbox to qudits, including the experimental demonstration of a universal gate set for qudits
Ringbauer et al. 2022
, as well as further theoretical developments of qudit gates for ions
Low et al. 2020
.

(iii)

A more subtle point concerns the engineering of an analogue version of the Hamiltonian (
88
). While the Magnus expansion-based derivation of
H_{\rm eff}
closes for spin-spin interaction Hamiltonians of the form (
96
), it includes higher-order contributions if an effective magnetic field

\hat{H}_{B}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}B_{i}\sigma^{z}_{i}

(98)

is also included in (
92
). Here,
B_{i}
can be achieved by additional lasers resonant with the qubit transition
Porras and Cirac 2004
;
Schneider et al. 2012
. This would then lead to a correction term
Wang and Freericks 2012
,
\hat{H}_{\text{Error}}
to Eq. (
88
), the effect of which on the time dynamics should be carefully examined and evaluated.

For this reason, a popular scheme is to implement time evolution using the Trotter-Suzuki expansion of the time-evolution operator. This approach has the advantage that one applies a sequence of spin-exchange Hamiltonians
\sum_{ij}\hat{s}^{+}_{j}\hat{s}^{-}_{i}
interleaved with the transverse field ones
\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}\hat{s}^{z}_{i}
, which individually do not suffer from the additional errors
H_{\text{Error}}
. Experimental demonstrations of such a discretised time evolution include, for instance, the implementation of the transverse-field Ising model
Lanyon et al. 2011
and variational quantum simulations in quantum chemistry
Nam et al. 2020
.

J_{x}=J_{y}=J\approx 2\pi\times 100,{\rm Hz}

\ket{\Psi(t)}=\prod_{j=1}^{N_{\rm steps}}{\rm e}^{-i\hat{H}_{z}(t_{j})\Delta t}{\rm e}^{-i\hat{H}_{\rm int}(t_{j})\Delta t}\ket{\Psi(0)},

(99)

where
\ket{\Psi(0)}
is the initial BCS state given in Eq. (

VII
Conclusion and Discussion

In this work, we have investigated the late-time dynamics of a time-dependent Richardson-Gaudin model with interaction strength inversely proportional to time, for arbitrary spin
s>1
. Starting from the off-shell Bethe ansatz solution of the model in terms of a multiple contour integral, we applied the saddle point method to obtain the asymptotic wavefunction and subsequently identified a correction term, which we verified numerically.

We explicitly derived the full many-body asymptotic wavefunctions for spin-
1
and spin-
3/2
in terms of elementary functions and the gamma function
\Gamma(x)
. For the general spin-
s
case, we obtained the solution up to a time-independent correction term. Determining this term requires solving the two-site Richardson-Gaudin model for spin-
s
. For spin-
2
, we proposed a candidate correction term in Appendix
B
that appears to be numerically exact, while obtaining an explicit expression for the correction at
s>2
remains an open problem.

We used the asymptotic wavefunction to derive several results and make a range of observations. One of the central findings is that, in contrast to the time-independent case, higher-spin solutions of time-dependent Richardson-Gaudin Hamiltonians cannot be generated by fusing lower-spin degrees of freedom. For instance, the asymptotic probability distribution of the local magnetization,
P_{s}\left(\{s_{j}^{z}\}\right)
, includes quadratic terms
(s_{j}^{z})^{2}
for
s=1
and
s=3/2
, and a quartic term
(s_{j}^{z})^{4}
for
s=2
, whereas in the spin-
1/2
case the distribution is linear in
s_{j}^{z}
. These nonlinear contributions cannot be reproduced by combining neighboring spin-
1/2
sites. This nonanalytic behavior reflects the intrinsic complexity of the time-dependent problem and emphasizes the necessity of analyzing each spin-
s
model independently, rather than expecting higher-spin dynamics to emerge from lower-spin cases.

The validity of the mean-field approximation depends on the type of observable under consideration. A distinction must be made between local and nonlocal observables. Local observables are defined as sums of products of spin operators in which the number of operators per term remains finite in the thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
, where
N
is the total number of spins. We have shown that all such local observables agree exactly with the corresponding mean-field predictions to leading order in
1/N
. In contrast, the mean-field approximation fails for nonlocal observables, such as the Loschmidt echo and bipartite entanglement entropy.

At large times, the interaction term vanishes and the
z
-components of the spins,
\hat{s}_{j}^{z}
, emerge as natural integrals of motion. In the spin-
1/2
case, it has been previously shown that the steady state is described by a generalized Gibbs ensemble (GGE) constructed from these emergent conserved quantities
Zabalo et al. 2022
. However, we explicitly demonstrated that this description breaks down for
s>1/2
, where the steady-state density matrix contains higher powers of
\hat{s}_{j}^{z}
, indicating a more intricate structure that lies beyond the standard GGE framework.

Finally, we discussed possible avenues for experimentally realizing the spin-
s
Richardson-Gaudin model. While our analysis of these implementations is rudimentary at best, we are confident that the predictions made in this work can be tested using one or more of the proposed experimental platforms. Given the scarcity of exact solutions for interacting, time-dependent quantum models, the model we present offers an interesting testing ground for exploring time-dependent many-body quantum mechanics.

(123)

where

\displaystyle=\chi_{ij}^{+}+\chi_{ij}^{-},

\displaystyle h_{i}

(125c)

\displaystyle\bar{n}(\delta_{c})

(125d)

A complete analysis of the experiment in Ref.
Davis et al. 2019
, including effects such as the transverse and longitudinal distribution of atoms and inhomogeneities in the couplings, lies beyond the scope of this work. In particular, we note that both
h_{i}
and

125

i
and
j

\Omega_{i}=\Omega(x_{i})

\chi_{ij}=\text{const}

)]. In what follows, to estimate the range of experimentally accessible regimes, we assume constant couplings that are representative of the experiment, namely

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle=2\pi\times 200\,{\rm kHz},

(126a)

\displaystyle\Omega_{i}

\displaystyle=\Omega\approx 2\pi\times 20\,{\rm Hz}

(126b)

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=2\pi\times 95\;{\rm MHz}.

(126c)

h

\chi
