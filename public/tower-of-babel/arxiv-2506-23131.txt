Conductance Estimation in Digraphs: Submodular Transformation, Lovász Extension and Dinkelbach Iteration Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Sihong Shao, Chuan Yang, Xinyang Ye

Abstract
Conventional spectral digraph partitioning methods typically symmetrize the adjacency matrix, thereby transforming the directed graph partitioning problem into an undirected one, where bipartitioning is commonly linked to minimizing graph conductance. However, such symmetrization approaches disregard the directional dependencies of edges in digraphs, failing to capture the inherent imbalance crucial to directed network modeling. Building on the parallels between digraph conductance and conductance under submodular transformations, we develop a generalized framework to derive their continuous formulations. By leveraging properties of the Lovász extension, this framework addresses the fundamental asymmetry problem in digraph partitioning. We then formulate an equivalent fractional programming problem, relax it via a three-step Dinkelbach iteration procedure, and design the Directed Simple Iterative ( 𝐃𝐒𝐈 \mathbf{DSI} ) algorithm for estimating digraph conductance. The subproblem within 𝐃𝐒𝐈 \mathbf{DSI} is analytically solvable, and the algorithm is guaranteed to converge provably to a binary local optimum. Extensive experiments on synthetic and real-world networks demonstrate that our 𝐃𝐒𝐈 \mathbf{DSI} algorithm significantly outperforms several state-of-the-art methods in digraph conductance minimization.

A General Framework for Seeking Equivalent Continuous Formulas

The submodular transformation was proposed to unify and generalize the Cheeger inequalities for undirected graphs, directed graphs, and hypergraphs
[

]

Under this framework, we derive continuous formulations of (

We first give the definition of submodular transformation in Section

.
Then, we introduce an important tool, Lovász extension in Section

.
Finally, we show how to find equivalent objective functions on multiple graph models in Section

2.1
Conductance under Submodular Transformations

Let
V
be a set of size
n
and
P(V)=\{S:S\subseteq V\}.

f:P(V)\rightarrow\mathbb{R},
is called
submodular
if for every
S,T\subseteq V
, we have
f(S)+f(T)\geq f(S\cap T)+f(S\cup T)
.
Note that the
cut function

\mathrm{cut}_{G}:\{0,1\}^{V}\to\mathbb{R}
associated with an undirected graph (a digraph)
G
is submodular, where
\mathrm{cut}_{G}(S)
for a vertex set
S\subseteq V
represents the sum of weights of edges (arcs) leaving
S
and entering
V\setminus S
.
The submodular transformation was proposed by Yoshida
[
27
]
to give a general framework for graph conductance.

Definition 1
(Submodular transformation
[
27
]
).

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph,
V
and
E
are its vertex set and edge set, respectively. Let
P(V)=\{S:S\subseteq V\},
and
F:P(V)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{E}
is called
submodular transformation
if for each
e\in E
, the induced component function
F_{e}:P(V)\to\mathbb{R}
is submodular, where for any
S\subset V,

F_{e}(S)
is the corresponding component of
F(S)
related to
e
.

Example 2.1
(Digraph).

Let
G=(V,A)
be a digraph. A submodular transformation
F:\{0,1\}^{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{A}
relating to cut value can be obtained as follows. For each arc
e\in A
, let the corresponding component
F_{e}:P(V)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
denote the cut function of the digraph with the single arc
e
, where
F_{e}(S)=1
if
e\in\mathrm{cut}^{+}(S)
and
F_{e}(S)=0
otherwise.

Remark 2
.

Note that the cut function
\mathrm{cut}_{G}(S)=\min\{\mathrm{cut}^{+}(S),\mathrm{cut}^{-}(S)\}
is not submodular.

For a partition
(S,\bar{S})
of
V
and a submodular transformation
F,
the conductance of
S
is defined as:

\varphi_{F}(S)=\frac{\min\{\mathrm{cut}_{F}(S),\mathrm{cut}_{F}(V\backslash S)\}}{\min\{\mathrm{Vol}(S),\mathrm{Vol}(V\backslash S)\}},

(8)

where
\mathrm{cut}_{F}(S)=\sum\limits_{e\in E}F_{e}(S),

and
\varphi_{F}(G)=\min\limits_{\emptyset\neq S\subsetneq V}\varphi_{F}(S).

\displaystyle\mathrm{cut}^{+L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\to j\in A}w_{ij}\int^{\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq n}x_{i}}_{\min\limits_{1\leq i\leq n}x_{i}}\chi_{x_{j}\leq t<x_{i}}dt

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\to j\in A}w_{ij}\max\{0,x_{i}-x_{j}\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\to j\in A}\frac{w_{ij}}{2}(x_{i}-x_{j}+|x_{i}-x_{j}|).

Similarly, for
\mathrm{cut}^{-}({S})
, its Lovász extension is

\mathrm{cut}^{-L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})=\sum_{i\to j\in A}\frac{w_{ij}}{2}(x_{j}-x_{i}+|x_{j}-x_{i}|).

For
\mathrm{cut}(S)=\min\{\mathrm{cut}^{+}(S),\mathrm{cut}^{-}({S})\}
, its Lovász extension
\mathrm{cut}^{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})
is not linear for each arc and is not additive. Instead of directly calculating
\mathrm{cut}^{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})
, we notice it can be upper bounded by
\mathrm{cut}^{+L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})
and
\mathrm{cut}^{-L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}).
Under condition (
11
), the upper bound function will not violate the equivalent condition.
From Lemma
5
, for all
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{cut}^{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})

\displaystyle\leq\min\{\mathrm{cut}^{+L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}),\mathrm{cut}^{-L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})\}=\frac{1}{2}(I(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})-J(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})):=G(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}),

(12)

\displaystyle G(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{Vol}(V)\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\|_{\infty}-I^{+}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})-J(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})\right):=F(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}),

(13)

where
I(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})=\sum\limits_{i\to j\in A}{w_{ij}}|x_{i}-x_{j}|.

It’s easy to check that for any set
S\subset V
,
F(\mathbf{1}_{S})=\min\{\mathrm{cut}^{+}(S),\mathrm{cut}^{-}(S)\}
,
thus according to Corollary
8
, we have

\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{t\boldsymbol{\mathrm{1}}\},t\in R}\tilde{r}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})=\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{t\boldsymbol{\mathrm{1}}\},t\in R}r(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})=\min\limits_{\emptyset\neq S\subsetneq V}\varphi_{D}(S),

where
r(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})=\frac{F(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})}{N(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})}
,
\tilde{r}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})=\frac{G(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})}{N(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})}
, and now
finishes the proof of (
3
).

Single Directed Conductance

Given a digraph
G=(V,A),
the equivalent continuous formulations for the out-conductance
\varphi^{+}(G)
mentioned in Section
1
are

\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{t\boldsymbol{\mathrm{1}}\},t\in R}\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(V)\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\|_{\infty}-I^{+}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})-J_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})}{2N(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})}=\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{t\boldsymbol{\mathrm{1}}\},t\in R}\frac{I(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})-J_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})}{2N(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})}=\varphi^{+}(G),

where
J_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})=\sum\limits_{i\to j\in A}w_{ij}(x_{i}-x_{j})=\sum_{i}d_{i}^{\delta}x_{i}
. For
\varphi^{-}(G),
it is nearly the same.

Undirected Conductance
[
21
,
8
]

Given an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, let
\{i,j\}\in E
denote the edge connecting
i\in V
and
j\in V
. For any nonempty set
S\subsetneq V
,
let
\mathrm{cut}(S)
denote the sum of the weights of all edges crossing the partition
(S,\bar{S})
. The conductance of

is defined as

\varphi(G)=\min\limits_{\emptyset\neq S\subsetneq V}\varphi(S),\quad\varphi(S)=\frac{\mathrm{cut}(S)}{\min\{\mathrm{Vol}(S),\mathrm{Vol}(V\backslash S)\}}.

(14)

By Corollary

, the two equivalent continuous formulations of
\varphi(G)
are

\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{t\boldsymbol{\mathrm{1}}\},t\in R}\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(V)\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\|_{\infty}-\sum\limits_{\{i,j\}\in E}{w_{ij}}|x_{i}+x_{j}|}{N(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})}=\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{t\boldsymbol{\mathrm{1}}\},t\in R}\frac{\sum\limits_{\{i,j\}\in E}{w_{ij}}|x_{i}-x_{j}|}{N(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})}.

(15)

Given the non-uniqueness of equivalent continuous objective functions in

critically impacts computational efficiency. As demonstrated in the numerical experiments of

, iterative algorithms based on the first equivalent formulation of (

(

) for solving digraph conductance

.

3
A Simple Iterative Algorithm

In this section, we elaborate on the
\mathbf{DSI}
algorithm, which employs a continuous optimization approach to solve the discrete optimization problem.
Notably, this strategy has been applied to various graph cut problems, including Max Cut
[
23
]
, AntiCheeger Cut
[
22
]
, and Cheeger Cut
[
21
]
in undirected graphs.
From (
3
), we know that

\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{t\boldsymbol{\mathrm{1}}\},t\in R}{r}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})=\min\limits_{\emptyset\neq S\subsetneq V}\varphi_{D}(S),

where
r(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})
is defined in (
4
).
Dinkelbach iterative algorithm
[
12
]
was designed to solve fractional programming and here we use it to solve
\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{t\boldsymbol{\mathrm{1}}\},t\in R}r(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}).

For notational convenience, we define

\Omega_{p}^{1}=\{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\max\limits_{i}x_{i}+\min\limits_{i}x_{i}=0,\,\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\|_{p}=1\}
and
\Omega_{p}^{2}=\{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\min\limits_{i}|x_{i}|=0,\,\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\|_{p}=1\}.

Set

Q_{r}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})=\frac{I^{+}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})+J(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})+2rN(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})}{\mathrm{Vol}(V)}.

(16)

Note that
\Omega_{p}^{1}\cup\Omega_{p}^{2}
is a compact set, and
from Dinkelbach’s algorithm, the sequence
\{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k}\}
generated by the iterative steps

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}\limits_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\Omega_{p}^{1}\cup\Omega_{p}^{2}}\{\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\|_{\infty}-Q_{r^{k}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})\},~p\in[1,\boldsymbol{\infty}],

(17a)

\displaystyle r^{k+1}=r(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1}).

(17b)

will converge to the optimum of
r(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}),
whose value is denoted as
r_{\min}.

Theorem 10
.

The sequence
\{r^{k}\}
generated by (
17
) will converge to the global optimum
r_{\min}.

Proof.

It indicates
r_{\min}=\varphi_{D}(G)
. The definition of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1}
in (
17a
) implies

0=\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k}\|_{\infty}-Q_{r^{k}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k})\geq\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1}\|_{\infty}-Q_{r^{k}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1}),

which means

r^{k}\geq\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(V)\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1}\|_{\infty}-I(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1})-J(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1})}{2N(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1})}=r^{k+1}\geq r_{\min},\ \forall\,k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}.

Thus we have

\exists\,r^{*}\in[r_{\min},r^{1}]\text{ s.t. }\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}r^{k}=r^{*}.

It suffices to show that
r_{\min}\geq r^{*}
.
We denote

f(r)=\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\Omega_{p}^{1}\cup\Omega_{p}^{2}}(\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\|_{\infty}-Q_{r}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})),

which must be continuous on
\mathbb{R}
by the compactness of
\Omega_{p}^{1}\cup\Omega_{p}^{2}
. Note that

\displaystyle f(r^{k})

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1}\|_{\infty}-Q_{r^{k}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1})

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1}\|_{\infty}-\frac{I^{+}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1})+J(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1})+2r^{k}N(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1})}{\mathrm{Vol}(V)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2N(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1})}{\mathrm{Vol}(V)}\left(\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(V)\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1}\|_{\infty}-I^{+}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1})-J(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1})}{2N(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1})}-r^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2N(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1})}{\mathrm{Vol}(V)}\cdot(r^{k+1}-r^{k})\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad k\to+\infty,

we have

f(r^{*})=\lim_{k\to+\infty}f(r^{k})=0,

and thus

\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\|_{\infty}-Q_{r^{*}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})\geq 0,\,\forall\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\Omega_{p}^{1}\cup\Omega_{p}^{2}.

Hence,
r_{\min}\geq r^{*},
which completes the proof.

Note that
Q_{r}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})
is convex, so we can introduce the subgradient method to make a relaxation.
Let
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function, and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a point in the domain
\text{dom}(g),
a vector
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called a
subgradient
of
g
at
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}
if it satisfies

g(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}})\geq g(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})+\langle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\rangle,\,\,\forall\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\in\text{dom}(g),

(18)

where
\langle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}\rangle
is the inner product of vector
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{v}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}.
The subdifferential of
g
at
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}
is a set consisting of all the vectors
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}
satisfying inequality (
18
), denoted as
\partial g
.
Since
Q_{r}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})
is convex and homogeneous of degree one, then for all
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

\langle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}\rangle\leq Q_{r}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}),\,\,\forall\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}\in\partial Q_{r}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}).

(19)

Plugging the relaxation (
19
) into the subproblem (
17a
), we get a relaxed version of (
17a
). The domain
\Omega_{p}^{1}\cup\Omega_{p}^{2}
can be relaxed to
\{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}:\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\|_{p}=1\}.

This modifies the two-step Dinkelbach iterative scheme into the following three-step one:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}\limits_{\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\|_{p}=1}\{\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\|_{\infty}-\langle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\rangle\},~p\in[1,\boldsymbol{\infty}],

(20a)

\displaystyle r^{k+1}=r(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1}),

(20b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}^{k+1}\in\partial Q_{r^{k+1}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1}).

(20c)

It has been pointed out that the subproblem (
20a
)
admits an analytical solution
[
23
]
, with
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1}
remaining non-constant (see Algorithm
3
and Remark
15
).
Moreover, we are able to prove that such an iterative scheme still keeps the monotonicity (see Theorem
11
) and has local convergence (see Theorem
14
).

.

Proof.

Given the definitions of

\displaystyle r^{k+1}-r^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(V)\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1}\|_{\infty}-I^{+}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1})-J(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1})-2r^{k}N(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1})}{2N(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k+1})}

Since

Lemma 12
(Lemma 3.1 in
[

Suppose
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{k}

(1)

\end{array}\right.\\

(21)

(2)
\|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\|_{1}>1

Conclusion and Outlook

In this paper, we present an algorithm for estimating digraph conductance that addresses the fundamental asymmetry challenge inherent in directed networks. To overcome this challenge, we leverage properties of the Lovász extension to derive equivalent continuous formulations for both digraph conductance and conductance under submodular transformations, thereby bridging the discrete-continuous optimization gap.
Building on this novel continuous formulation, we introduce the Directed Simple Iterative (
\mathbf{DSI}
) algorithm for digraphs with the Dinkelbach iterative framework. The outputs of
\mathbf{DSI}
are rigorously proven to converge to binary local optima.
Comprehensive evaluations on synthetic and real-world datasets demonstrate that
\mathbf{DSI}
significantly outperforms benchmark methods including Sweepcut and Gurobi.
This work breaks through the limitations of symmetric Laplacian-based approaches for digraphs, opening new avenues for research in the analysis of directed complex networks. Notably, our framework demonstrates the potential for generalization to estimate hypergraph conductance and other related combinatorial metrics, highlighting its broad applicability.
