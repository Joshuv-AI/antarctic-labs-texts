Geometric decomposition of information flow: New insights into information thermodynamics
Yoh Maekawa, Ryuna Nagayama, Kohei Yoshimura, Sosuke Ito

Abstract
We propose a decomposition of information flow into housekeeping and excess components for autonomous bipartite systems subject to Markov jump processes. We introduce this decomposition by using the geometric structure of probability currents and the conjugate thermodynamic forces. The housekeeping component arises from the cyclic modes caused by the detailed balance violations and maintains the correlations between the two subsystems. In contrast, the excess component, is a contribution of conservative forces that alters the mutual information between the two subsystems. With this decomposition, we generalize previous results, such as the second law of information thermodynamics, the cyclic decomposition, and the information-thermodynamic extensions of thermodynamic trade-off relations.

Introduction

Information thermodynamics is the field that studies the conversion of information into energy, and vice versa.
This framework can be traced back to Maxwell’s demon

;

;
Goold et al. 2016
.
Information-energy conversion can now be analyzed in a wide variety of systems, including quantum systems

;
Ptaszyński and Esposito 2019
;
Yada et al. 2022
and biological processes
Barato et al. 2014
;
Sartori et al. 2014
;

. Several experiments

;

;

;

;

;

;

;
Amano et al. 2022
;
Yada et al. 2025
;
Oikawa et al. 2025
can demonstrate these concepts, such as information erasure and information-energy conversion.

In the context of information thermodynamics for autonomous information processing, information flow quantifies the information exchanged between two subsystems
Ito and Sagawa 2013
;
Hartich et al. 2014
;
Horowitz and Esposito 2014
;
Horowitz and Sandberg 2014
;
Shiraishi and Sagawa 2015
;

;

;

;

;

;

;

;

;
Tojo et al. 2025
. When the system is out of equilibrium, the entropy change rate associated with the subsystems can be negative, apparently violating the second law of thermodynamics. However, taking into account information flow resolves this apparent violation, resulting in a modified inequality known as the second law of information thermodynamics
Parrondo et al. 2015
. Information flow has been actively studied especially in the context of Markov jump processes for bipartite systems
Hartich et al. 2014
;
Horowitz and Esposito 2014
, where the two subsystems do not change simultaneously. It has been analyzed in various contexts, including biological processes such as sensory adaptation

;

;

;
Amano et al. 2022
;
Leighton et al. 2024
.

Information flow stems from the nonequilibrium nature of a system and is closely related to its entropy production rate (EPR). The EPR is considered a measure of irreversibility, and its nonnegativity is considered the second law of thermodynamics
Seifert 2025
. In information thermodynamics, the partial EPR, which is the contribution of the EPR in the subsystem, provides a measure of irreversibility in subsystems, and its nonnegativity is considered the second law of information thermodynamics
Parrondo et al. 2015
;
Horowitz and Sandberg 2014
. Information flow is a contribution of the interaction between the subsystems in the partial EPR, and it also becomes non-zero when a subsystem is irreversible.

There are two distinct aspects of the nonequilibrium nature of Markov jump processes: nonconservativeness and nonstationarity. One way to determine whether irreversibility stems from nonconservativeness or nonstationarity is by decomposing the EPR. A geometric decomposition of the EPR into the excess and housekeeping EPRs
Maes and Netočnỳ 2014
;
Dechant et al. 2022a
;
Dechant et al. 2022b
;
Yoshimura et al. 2023
;
Ito 2024
is a way to decompose the EPR into contributions of nonconservativeness and nonstationarity. Unlike other EPR decompositions such as the Hatano–Sasa decomposition
Hatano and Sasa 2001
or the adiabatic-nonadiabatic decomposition
Esposito and Van den Broeck 2010
, the excess EPR provides the tighter bounds on dynamic speeds, such as the speed limits
Nakazato and Ito 2021
;
Aurell et al. 2012
;
Ito 2024
;
Yoshimura et al. 2023
and thermodynamic uncertainty relations (TURs)
Dechant et al. 2022b
;
Yoshimura et al. 2023
. The housekeeping EPR, on the other hand, can be expressed through the cyclic decomposition
Yoshimura et al. 2023
or the oscillatory mode decomposition
Sekizawa et al. 2024
, meaning it accounts for oscillatory contributions of the EPR due to detailed balance violations. This geometric decomposition of the EPR is universal and can be generalized to various systems, including deterministic chemical reactions, reaction-diffusion systems, open quantum systems, and fluid systems
Nagayama et al. 2025a
;
Yoshimura and Ito 2024
;
Yoshimura et al. 2025
. However, it is unclear whether this universal decomposition applies to the partial EPR in information thermodynamics or to information flow.

In this paper, we show that, similarly to the EPR decomposition, information flow can be decomposed into two nonequilibrium contributions for bipartite systems described by Markov jump processes. The excess information flow, which quantifies nonstationarity, induces temporal changes in the correlation between the two subsystems. By contrast, the housekeeping information flow, which is caused by nonconservativeness, enables information to be transferred between the subsystems without altering their correlations.
The former has been partially discussed in terms of the non-autonomous information processing by Maxwell’s demon via a measurement-feedback scheme
Sagawa and Ueda 2010
;
Parrondo et al. 2015
.
We can also find concepts similar to the latter one in information thermodynamics for Markov jump processes in the steady state
Horowitz and Esposito 2014
;
Yamamoto et al. 2016
. Therefore, our new decomposition of information flow appropriately separates the two concepts of information flow in information thermodynamics.

Furthermore, our results provide a decomposition of the partial EPR into excess and housekeeping components. Their non-negative properties respectively provide two generalizations of the second law of information thermodynamics, offering a novel perspective in information thermodynamics where the contributions of both housekeeping and excess information flows appear to violate the second law of thermodynamics. Moreover, by considering a geometrical interpretation of the excess partial EPR, we can derive generalizations of TURs and speed limits for subsystems in information thermodynamics. We note that TURs for the partial excess EPR are regarded as generalizations of TURs for the EPR
Barato and Seifert 2015
;
Horowitz and Gingrich 2020
;
Otsubo et al. 2020
and
for the partial EPR
Otsubo et al. 2020
;
Wolpert 2020
;
Tanogami et al. 2023
.
These speed limits correspond to the information-thermodynamic speed limits
Nakazato and Ito 2021
;
Kamijima et al. 2025a
based on optimal transport theory
Villani et al. 2008
in Langevin systems. It is obtained by newly extending the 2-Wasserstein distance
Maas 2011
;
Yoshimura et al. 2023
in Markov jump processes for subsystems. Our speed limits, which are based on the 2-Wasserstein distance, are directly related to the natural decomposition of information flows. This differs from speed limits based on the 1-Wasserstein distance
Dechant 2022
;
Van Vu and Saito 2023a
;
Van Vu and Saito 2023b
;
Kolchinsky et al. 2024
;
Nagayama et al. 2025b
and its applications to information thermodynamics
Nagase and Sagawa 2024
;
Kamijima et al. 2025b
;
Nagase and Sagawa 2025
.
In contrast, the housekeeping partial EPR and housekeeping information flow can be decomposed into cycles, as in information thermodynamics at the steady state
Horowitz and Esposito 2014
;
Yamamoto et al. 2016
. Two information flows in each subsystem satisfy an antisymmetric relation for a cycle, meaning that they are identical except for their signs. We illustrate these results numerically using a simple four-state model.

II
Review of geometric approach to stochastic thermodynamics

In this section, we briefly review a geometric approach to stochastic thermodynamics. Throughout this paper, the vector space of real column vectors of dimension
d
is denoted by
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the space of real matrices of size
d_{1}\times d_{2}
is denoted by
\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
. For a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
, its kernel and image are defined as
\ker A=\{\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\mid A\bm{v}=\bm{0}\}
and
\mathop{\mathrm{im}}A=\{A\bm{v}\mid\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\}
, respectively.

II.1
Master equation

We consider a Markov jump process in the system
Z
, and the state of the system
Z
is represented by
z
. The set of states of
Z
is denoted by
\mathcal{Z}=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{|\mathcal{Z}|}\}
.
We assume that
z
does not contain any odd variables, such as velocity, where the odd variable changes sign in the time-reversal operation.
The probability that the system
Z
is in state
z\in\mathcal{Z}
at time
t
is denoted by
p(z;t)
.
The system is in contact with several baths, and a bath is denoted by the symbol
\nu
. Each transition between two states is associated with one of these baths. The time evolution of
p(z;t)
is governed by the following master equation:

\displaystyle d_{t}p(z;t)=\sum_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z},\nu}R^{(\nu)}(z|z^{\prime})p(z^{\prime};t).

(1)

Here,
R^{(\nu)}(z|z^{\prime})
is the transition rate from
z^{\prime}
to
z
associated with the bath
\nu
. For
z\neq z^{\prime}
, we require
R^{(\nu)}(z|z^{\prime})\geq 0
, and the diagonal elements satisfy
R^{(\nu)}(z|z)=-\sum_{z^{\prime}:z^{\prime}\neq z}R^{(\nu)}(z^{\prime}|z)
. In the following, we can omit the time symbol
t
for the sake of simplicity. For convenience, we define the probability vector
\bm{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Z}|}
by giving its components as
[\bm{p}]_{z}=p(z)
.

We introduce a graph-theoretic description of the master equation. We write the transition from
z
to
z^{\prime}
associated with the bath
\nu
as
(\nu,z\to z^{\prime})
and interpret it as a directed edge with source
z
and target
z^{\prime}
. The set of all such edges is denoted by
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{all}}=\{e=(\nu,z\to z^{\prime})\mid R^{(\nu)}(z^{\prime}|z)>0\}
. We now impose a reversibility condition: for every
e=(\nu,z\to z^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{all}}
, the reverse edge
-e\coloneqq(\nu,z^{\prime}\to z)
is also in
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{all}}
. Let
\mathcal{E}=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{|\mathcal{E}|}\}
be a set of edges
containing only either
e
or
-e
for each pair
(e,-e)
. In this way, we define a loopless directed graph
G=(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{E})
. For convenience, we label each edge
e=(\nu(e),\mathrm{s}(e)\to\mathrm{t}(e))
by its associated bath
\nu(e)
, source state
\mathrm{s}(e)
, and target state
\mathrm{t}(e)
, and write the corresponding transition rate as
R^{(\nu(e))}(\mathrm{t}(e)|\mathrm{s}(e))=R_{e}
. The forward flux of edge
e
is defined as
J_{e}^{+}\coloneqq R_{e}p(\mathrm{s}(e))
, and the reverse flux is
J_{e}^{-}\coloneqq R_{-e}p(\mathrm{t}(e))
. Therefore, the reverse flux of the edge
e
is equivalent to the forward flux of the reverse edge
-e
, i.e.,
J_{e}^{-}=J_{-e}^{+}
. The net probability flux of the edge
e
, given by
J_{e}\coloneqq J_{e}^{+}-J_{e}^{-}
, is called the current of
e
.
We define the current vector
\bm{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{E}|}
by
[\bm{J}]_{e}=J_{e}
. If we emphasize the distribution dependence of
\bm{J}
, we may write
\bm{J}(\bm{p})
.

Next we introduce a matrix
\nabla\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{E}|\times|\mathcal{Z}|}
by
[\nabla]_{e,z}=\delta_{z,\mathrm{t}(e)}-\delta_{z,\mathrm{s}(e)}
, where
\delta_{x,y}
is the Kronecker delta.
This matrix can be regarded as the discrete gradient operator, since
[\nabla\bm{\phi}]_{e}=\phi(\mathrm{t}(e))-\phi(\mathrm{s}(e))
is equal to the change of
\bm{\phi}
in the transition corresponding to the edge
e
.
We can then rewrite the master equation (
1
) as

\displaystyle d_{t}\bm{p}=\nabla^{\top}\bm{J}(\bm{p}),

(2)

where
\nabla^{\top}
is the transpose of
\nabla
, also known as the incidence matrix of the directed graph
G
in graph theory
Bondy and Murty 2008
(see also Fig.
2
for an example). Because
\nabla^{\top}
is considered to be the adjoint of the discrete gradient, namely, the discrete divergence, Eq. (
1
) can be interpreted as a discrete version of the continuity equation, where
\nabla^{\top}
multiplied by the current
\bm{J}
is equal to
d_{t}\bm{p}
. Note that the divergence of the current disappears in the steady state
\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}}
, i.e,

\displaystyle\nabla^{\top}\bm{J}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})=\bm{0}.

(3)

For simplicity, we assume that the directed graph
G
is connected. Here,
G
is said to be connected if, for any two states
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}
, there exists a path connecting them when the direction of the edges of
G
is ignored. Physically, this assumption limits our attention to a single physical system. If
G
had multiple components, each would correspond to an independent system. In this paper, we do not consider such cases.

A graph and its incidence matrix (transposed, giving the divergence). The graph
G
consists of the nodes
\mathcal{Z}=\{1,2,3,4\}
and the edges
\mathcal{E}=\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},e_{4},e_{5},e_{6}\}
. Each node represents a state of the system, and the edges indicate reversible transitions between two different states.
We will refer to this graph repeatedly in figures.

II.2
Entropy production rate

We discuss the local detailed balance and the detailed balance conditions. In the setup of Sec.
II.1
, we assumed the reversibility of the transitions:
R_{e}>0\Rightarrow R_{-e}>0
for all
e\in\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{all}}
. As a first step to introduce thermodynamics, we impose the local detailed balance condition:

\displaystyle\frac{R_{e}}{R_{-e}}=e^{s_{e}}.

(4)

The quantity
s_{e}\in\mathbb{R}
represents the entropy change of the bath
\nu(e)
in the transition corresponding to the edge
e
, with the Boltzmann constant is set to unity. Note that
s_{e}
satisfies the antisymmetric relation
s_{e}=-s_{-e}
.

Next, we introduce the equilibrium distribution,
\bm{p}^{\mathrm{eq}}
. It is defined as the distribution that satisfies the following detailed balance condition:

\displaystyle J_{e}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{eq}})=R_{e}[\bm{p}^{\mathrm{eq}}]_{\mathrm{s}(e)}-R_{-e}[\bm{p}^{\mathrm{eq}}]_{\mathrm{t}(e)}=0

(5)

for any
e\in\mathcal{E}
. This condition can be written compactly as
\bm{J}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{eq}})=\bm{0}
, and thus the equilibrium distribution is a stationary distribution.

\bm{p}^{\mathrm{eq}}
exists if and only if there exists an observable
\bm{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Z}|}
, with
[\bm{\epsilon}]_{z}=\epsilon_{z}
, such that
s_{e}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{t}(e)}-\epsilon_{\mathrm{s}(e)}
holds for all
e\in\mathcal{E}
.
In this case, the equilibrium distribution is expressed as
\bm{p}^{\mathrm{eq}}=e^{-\bm{\epsilon}}/Z(\bm{\epsilon})
, where the normalization factor is given by
Z(\bm{\epsilon})=\sum_{z}e^{-\epsilon_{z}}
and we use the notation
[e^{-\bm{\epsilon}}]_{z}=e^{-\epsilon_{z}}
. By explicitly separating the inverse temperature
\beta>0
and the energy level
\bm{E}
as
\bm{\epsilon}=\beta\bm{E}
, the equilibrium distribution takes the form of a canonical distribution
\bm{p}^{\mathrm{eq}}=e^{-\beta\bm{E}}/Z
.

Based on
s_{e}
introduced by the local detailed balance condition [Eq. (
4
)], we can define the entropy production rate (EPR) as

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}(\bm{p})=d_{t}S^{\mathrm{sys}}(\bm{p})+\dot{S}^{\mathrm{env}}(\bm{p})

(6)

with the system entropy
S^{\mathrm{sys}}
and the environmental entropy change rate
\dot{S}^{\mathrm{env}}
defined as

\displaystyle S^{\mathrm{sys}}(\bm{p})=-\sum_{z}p(z)\ln p(z),\;\dot{S}^{\mathrm{env}}(\bm{p})=\sum_{e}s_{e}J_{e}(\bm{p}).

(7)

If we define the thermodynamic force
\bm{F}(\bm{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{E}|}
as

\displaystyle F_{e}(\bm{p})=s_{e}-[\ln p(\mathrm{t}(e))-\ln p(\mathrm{s}(e))],

(8)

the EPR is rewritten into the insightful form
Schnakenberg 1976

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}(\bm{p})=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}J_{e}(\bm{p})F_{e}(\bm{p})=\langle\bm{J}(\bm{p}),\bm{F}(\bm{p})\rangle.

(9)

Here,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the notation for the inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{E}|}
.
The thermodynamic force is deeply associated with the current because we have

\displaystyle F_{e}(\bm{p})=\ln J_{e}^{+}(\bm{p})-\ln J_{e}^{-}(\bm{p})

(10)

due to the local detailed balance.
The EPR is often regarded as a quantitative measure of the irreversibility of thermodynamic processes in a system and its environment.
This is formalized by the second law of thermodynamics, which states that the EPR is non-negative:
\dot{\Sigma}\geq 0
.
The expression in Eq. (
10
) proves this inequality because it shows the fact that
J_{e}(\bm{p})
and
F_{e}(\bm{p})
always have the same sign.

The thermodynamic force is divided into a thermal part and an informational part as

\displaystyle\bm{F}(\bm{p})=\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}+\bm{F}^{\mathrm{info}}(\bm{p}),

(11)

where
[\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}]_{e}=\ln(R_{e}/R_{-e})=s_{e}
and
[\bm{F}^{\mathrm{info}}(\bm{p})]_{e}=\ln[p(\mathrm{s}(e))/p(\mathrm{t}(e))]
.
Note that these terms correspond to
\dot{S}^{\mathrm{env}}
and
d_{t}S^{\mathrm{sys}}
in the EPR, respectively.
The informational component quantifies the Shannon entropy change, which becomes clearer if we define
\bm{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Z}|}
as
[\bm{h}]_{z}=-\ln p(z)
; then, it is rewritten as
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{info}}(\bm{p})=\nabla\bm{h}
.
On the other hand, the thermal part is given as a gradient only if the system is detailed balanced.
When
s_{e}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{t}(e)}-\epsilon_{\mathrm{s}(e)}
holds for all
e\in\mathcal{E}
, the thermal force reads
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}=-\nabla\bm{\epsilon}
, and we obtain

\displaystyle\bm{F}(\bm{p})=-\nabla(\bm{\epsilon}-\bm{h}).

(12)

As discussed in the next section, we define a thermodynamic force as
conservative
if it is the gradient of a potential (i.e., a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Z}|}
); the above discussion shows that the thermodynamic force is conservative if and only if the system is detailed balanced.

We now introduce the geometric expression for the EPR.
First, we define the edgewise Onsager coefficient
l_{e}
by the ratio of
J_{e}
to
F_{e}
as

\displaystyle l_{e}(\bm{p})\coloneqq\frac{J_{e}^{+}(\bm{p})-J_{e}^{-}(\bm{p})}{\ln J_{e}^{+}(\bm{p})-\ln J_{e}^{-}(\bm{p})}=\frac{J_{e}(\bm{p})}{F_{e}(\bm{p})}>0.

(13)

The relation
F_{e}=l_{e}J_{e}
can be interpreted as a microscopic analog of the Onsager relation in linear response theory
Onsager 1931a
;
Onsager 1931b
, and
l_{e}
is a discrete analog of the diffusion coefficient in Langevin systems
Van Vu and Hasegawa 2021
.
Introducing the diagonal matrix of the edgewise Onsager coefficients
L(\bm{p})=\mathrm{diag}(l_{1}(\bm{p}),l_{2}(\bm{p}),\dots,l_{|\mathcal{E}|}(\bm{p}))\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{E}|\times|\mathcal{E}|}
, we obtain the compact expression
\bm{J}(\bm{p})=L(\bm{p})\bm{F}(\bm{p})
.
We call a pair of vectors
(\bm{J},\bm{F})
a conjugate pair if they are connected by
L(\bm{p})
as
\bm{J}=L(\bm{p})\bm{F}
.
Since
L(\bm{p})
is positive definite, we can define an inner product and norm with the metric
L(\bm{p})
for vectors
\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{E}|}
as
\langle\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime}\rangle_{L(\bm{p})}=\bm{v}^{\top}{L(\bm{p})}\bm{v}^{\prime}
and
\|\bm{v}\|_{L(\bm{p})}=\sqrt{\langle\bm{v},\bm{v}\rangle_{L(\bm{p})}}
, respectively. The EPR is thus expressed as the squared norm of the thermodynamic force in the vector space of forces, and is therefore non-negative,

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}(\bm{p})=\|\bm{F}(\bm{p})\|^{2}_{L(\bm{p})}\geq 0.

(14)

The inequality on the right-hand side is nothing more than the second law of thermodynamics.
In the following sections, we sometimes make
\bm{p}
dependence implicit for simplicity when clear from the context.

II.3
Geometric housekeeping-excess decomposition of EPR

We briefly review the geometric housekeeping-excess decomposition of the EPR. The content of this section follows Ref.
Yoshimura et al. 2023
. Important consequences of this decomposition are summarized in Secs.
II.3.1
and
II.3.2
.

We begin by introducing the conservativeness of thermodynamic forces, which plays a central role in geometric decomposition.
A force
\bm{F}
is called a conservative force if there exists a potential
\bm{\phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Z}|}
such that
\bm{F}=-\nabla\bm{\phi}
.
Furthermore, we write the set of all conservative forces as
\mathop{\mathrm{im}}\nabla

and call this set the conservative subspace (or the image of
\nabla
).
If and only if the system is detailed balanced, the force is always expressed as
\bm{F}(\bm{p})=-\nabla(\bm{\epsilon}-\bm{h})
, and thus is conservative.
In other words, the important thing is that the non-conservativeness characterizes the violation of the detailed balance.

Although the thermodynamic force
\bm{F}
is not necessarily conservative, any dynamics can be reproduced by a conservative force. Let us explain this fact concretely; for any
\bm{p}
, the following linear equation for
\bm{J}^{\prime}
has a unique solution:

\displaystyle\nabla^{\top}\bm{J}(\bm{p})=\nabla^{\top}\bm{J}^{\prime},

(15)

under the condition

\displaystyle\bm{J}^{\prime}=L(\bm{p})\bm{F}^{\prime}\;\text{and}\;\bm{F}^{\prime}\in\mathop{\mathrm{im}}\nabla.

(16)

We write the solution as
\bm{J}^{*}
and the conjugate force as
\bm{F}^{*}=L^{-1}\bm{J}^{*}
. Let us explain the physical meaning of the solution. We can rewrite the continuity equation (
2
) as

\displaystyle d_{t}\bm{p}=\nabla^{\top}\bm{J}^{*},

(17)

so we can interpret
\bm{J}^{*}
as the current that effectively drives the dynamics at each moment.
Furthermore, considering that
\bm{J}^{*}
is conjugate to
\bm{F}^{*}
, we obtain the picture that any time evolution
d_{t}\bm{p}
can be interpreted as the dynamics driven by a virtual conservative force
\bm{F}^{*}
. Note that
\bm{J}^{*}
satisfying only Eq. (
15
) may not be uniquely determined because
\ker\nabla^{\top}
can contain non-trivial elements. The addition of the condition (
16
) to Eq. (
15
) establishes the existence and uniqueness of the solution, as explained in the next paragraph.

We now interpret the solution
\bm{J}^{*}
from a geometric perspective by focusing on the conjugate force
\bm{F}^{*}
. Formally decomposing the force as
\bm{F}=\bm{F}^{*}+(\bm{F}-\bm{F}^{*})
, we find that
\bm{F}^{*}\in\mathop{\mathrm{im}}\nabla
and
\bm{F}-\bm{F}^{*}\in\ker\nabla^{\top}L
, since
\nabla^{\top}L(\bm{F}-\bm{F}^{*})=\nabla^{\top}\bm{J}-\nabla^{\top}\bm{J}^{*}=\bm{0}
holds. Note that
\nabla^{\top}L
is the adjoint of
\nabla
with respect to the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{L}
. Thus, we find that the decomposition
\bm{F}=\bm{F}^{*}+(\bm{F}-\bm{F}^{*})
is the orthogonal decomposition of
\bm{F}
into
\mathop{\mathrm{im}}\nabla
and
\ker\nabla^{\top}L
, with respective components
\bm{F}^{*}
and
\bm{F}-\bm{F}^{*}
. Therefore,
\bm{F}^{*}
is given by the orthogonal projection of
\bm{F}
onto
\mathop{\mathrm{im}}\nabla
with respect to the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{L}
. We can express
\bm{F}^{*}
through the following two equivalent minimization problems:

\displaystyle\bm{F}^{*}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\underset{\bm{F}^{\prime}:\bm{F}(\bm{p})-\bm{F}^{\prime}\in\ker\nabla^{\top}L(\bm{p})}{\arg\min}\|\bm{F}^{\prime}\|_{L(\bm{p})}^{2}

(18)

\displaystyle=\underset{\bm{F}^{\prime}\in\mathop{\mathrm{im}}\nabla}{\arg\min}\|\bm{F}(\bm{p})-\bm{F}^{\prime}\|^{2}_{L(\bm{p})}.

(19)

The fact that the projection is uniquely determined justifies the existence and uniqueness of the solution to Eq. (
15
) with the constraints (
16
).

Let us now introduce the geometric housekeeping-excess decomposition of the EPR. Take a potential
\bm{\phi}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Z}|}
such that
\bm{F}^{*}=-\nabla\bm{\phi}^{*}
. Note that
\bm{\phi}^{*}
is not unique;

\bm{F}^{*}
is invariant under the transformation
\bm{\phi}^{*}\to\bm{\phi}^{*}+r(1,1,\ldots,1)^{\top}
for any scalar
r
, and there is no other freedom
1
1

1

Since
\ker\nabla=\{r(1,1,\ldots,1)^{\top}\mid r\in\mathbb{R}\}
. This fact can be shown as follows. For a potential
\bm{\psi}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Z}|}
satisfying
\nabla\bm{\psi}=\bm{0}
,
[\bm{\psi}]_{\mathrm{s}(e)}-[\bm{\psi}]_{\mathrm{t}(e)}=0
holds for all
e\in\mathcal{E}
. Since
G
is connected there exists a path connecting any two states
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Z}|}
, so we obtain
[\bm{\psi}]_{1}=[\bm{\psi}]_{2}=\cdots=[\bm{\psi}]_{\mathcal{Z}}
.
.
If the system is detailed balanced, we have
\bm{F}=-\nabla(\bm{\epsilon}-\bm{h})
, hence we can set
\bm{\phi}^{*}=\bm{\epsilon}-\bm{h}\eqqcolon\bm{\phi}^{\mathrm{eq}}
. As a property of the projection,
\bm{F}^{*}
and
\bm{F}-\bm{F}^{*}
are always orthogonal with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{L}
, or we can directly show
\langle\bm{F}^{*},\bm{F}-\bm{F}^{*}\rangle_{L}=\langle-\nabla\bm{\phi}^{*},\bm{J}-\bm{J}^{*}\rangle=-(\bm{\phi}^{*})^{\top}\nabla^{\top}(\bm{J}-\bm{J}^{*})=0
. Using this orthogonality, we can decompose the EPR into two non-negative terms:

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}(\bm{p})=\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})+\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\bm{p}),

(20)

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})\coloneqq\|\bm{F}(\bm{p})-\bm{F}^{*}(\bm{p})\|^{2}_{L(\bm{p})},

(21)

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\bm{p})\coloneqq\|\bm{F}^{*}(\bm{p})\|^{2}_{L(\bm{p})}.

(22)

Here,
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}
and
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}
are referred to as the housekeeping EPR and the excess EPR, respectively
Yoshimura et al. 2023
. Let us briefly characterize these two types of EPRs through the following two situations.
The first one is the steady state, where
\nabla^{\top}\bm{J}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})=\nabla^{\top}L(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})\bm{F}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})=\bm{0}
, i.e.,
\bm{F}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})\in\ker\nabla^{\top}L(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})
holds. Focusing on the minimization problem (
18
), we find that
\bm{F}^{*}(\bm{p})=\bm{0}
is the minimizer. Therefore, the following two equalities hold:

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})=\dot{\Sigma}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}}),~\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})=0.

(23)

Second, assume the system is detailed balanced; i.e.,
\bm{F}(\bm{p})\in\mathop{\mathrm{im}}\nabla
holds. Focusing on the second minimization (
19
), we find that then
\bm{F}^{*}=\bm{F}(\bm{p})\in\mathop{\mathrm{im}}\nabla
is the minimizer, which implies

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})=0,~\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\bm{p})=\dot{\Sigma}(\bm{p}).

(24)

The physical meaning of the decomposition can also be clarified by rewriting the decomposed components in the following form:

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\bm{p})=d_{t}S^{\mathrm{sys}}(\bm{p})+\dot{S}^{\mathrm{ex,\,env}}(\bm{p}),

(25)

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})=\langle\bm{J}(\bm{p})-\bm{J}^{*}(\bm{p}),\bm{F}^{\mathrm{nc}}(\bm{p})\rangle,

(26)

where
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{nc}}(\bm{p})\coloneqq\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}-\bm{F}^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{p})=\bm{F}(\bm{p})-\bm{F}^{*}(\bm{p})
with the conservative component
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{p})\coloneqq\bm{F}^{*}(\bm{p})-\bm{F}^{\rm info}(\bm{p})
is the nonconservative component of the thermal force and
\dot{S}^{\mathrm{ex,\,env}}(\bm{p})
is the excess entropy change rate in the environment. To define
\dot{S}^{\mathrm{ex,\,env}}(\bm{p})
, we introduce the pseudo-energy

\displaystyle\bm{\epsilon}^{*}(\bm{p})=\bm{\phi}^{*}(\bm{p})+\bm{h}(\bm{p}),

(27)

analogous to
\bm{\epsilon}=\bm{\phi}^{\mathrm{eq}}+\bm{h}
in detailed balanced systems.
The excess entropy change rate in the environment is then defined as

\displaystyle\dot{S}^{\mathrm{ex,\,env}}(\bm{p})\coloneqq-\langle\bm{J}^{*}(\bm{p}),\nabla\bm{\epsilon}^{*}(\bm{p})\rangle.

(28)

Because
\bm{F}^{*}=-\nabla\bm{\phi}^{*}=\nabla(\bm{h}-\bm{\epsilon}^{*})
, we obtain Eq. (
25
) as follows,

\displaystyle d_{t}S^{\mathrm{sys}}+\dot{S}^{\mathrm{ex,\,env}}

\displaystyle=(\nabla^{\top}\bm{J}^{*})^{\top}\bm{h}-\langle\bm{J}^{*},\nabla\bm{\epsilon}^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{J}^{*},\nabla(\bm{h}-\bm{\epsilon}^{*})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{J}^{*},\bm{F}^{*}\rangle=\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}.

(29)

We discuss when these quantities vanish based on the above expressions.
Since the excess EPR is also given by
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\bm{p})=-(d_{t}\bm{p})^{\top}\bm{\phi}^{*}
, it vanishes when the system is in the steady state,
d_{t}\bm{p}=\bm{0}
. Therefore, the excess EPR quantifies dissipation caused by nonstationarity.
On the other hand, the housekeeping EPR vanishes when
\bm{F}^{\rm nc}(\bm{p})=\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}-\bm{F}^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{p})=\bm{0}
. If the system is detailed balanced, then
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}
becomes conservative and the condition
\bm{F}^{\rm nc}(\bm{p})=\bm{0}
satisfies.
Therefore, the housekeeping EPR quantifies dissipation caused by nonconservativeness.

Besides, we can find an alternative expression of the excess entropy change rate in terms of the excess heat flow. If the system is detailed balanced, we obtain
\dot{S}^{\mathrm{ex,\,env}}(\bm{p})=\dot{S}^{\mathrm{env}}(\bm{p})
. The pseudo-energy level
\bm{\epsilon}^{*}
is also equal to
\bm{\epsilon}
, and it could be the product of the inverse temperature
\beta
and energy level
\bm{E}
, i.e.,
\bm{\epsilon}^{*}=\bm{\epsilon}=\beta\bm{E}
. The heat flow
\dot{Q}
is introduced as
\dot{Q}\coloneqq\langle\bm{J}(\bm{p}),\nabla\bm{E}\rangle=(d_{t}\bm{p})^{\top}\bm{E}

and we can obtain the relation
\dot{S}^{\mathrm{ex,\,env}}=\dot{S}^{\mathrm{env}}=-\beta\dot{Q}
. Analogously to the case of the detailed balance condition, we may define the excess heat flow
\dot{Q}^{\rm ex}
as
\dot{Q}^{\rm ex}\coloneqq\beta^{-1}\langle\bm{J}^{*},\nabla\bm{\epsilon}^{*}\rangle=(d_{t}\bm{p})^{\top}\beta^{-1}\bm{\epsilon}^{*}
if the system is attached to a single bath with inverse temperature
\beta
.
From this definition, we obtain
\dot{S}^{\mathrm{ex,\,env}}=-\beta\dot{Q}^{\mathrm{ex}}
. When multiple baths are present, the excess heat flow becomes ambiguous, and only the excess entropy change rate
\dot{S}^{\mathrm{ex,\,env}}
may be well defined in general.

II.3.1
Cyclic decomposition

In this section, we review an expression for the housekeeping EPR
Yoshimura et al. 2023
that generalizes a well-known formula for the steady-state EPR provided by Schnakenberg in Ref.
Schnakenberg 1976
.
To this end, we define a cycle
C
in
G
as a nonempty subset of
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{all}}
satisfying the following two properties: (1)
C
is a closed path in
G
. When we express
C
explicitly as
C=\{e_{i(1)},\ldots,e_{i(|C|)}\}
,
\mathrm{t}(e_{i(|C|)})=\mathrm{s}(e_{i(1)})
and
\mathrm{t}(e_{i(k)})=\mathrm{s}(e_{i(k+1)})
hold for
k=1,\ldots,|C|-1
.
(2)
C
does not self-intersect; that is, there is at most one
e\in C
satisfying
\mathrm{s}(e)=z
for any
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, and there is at most one
e\in C
satisfying
\mathrm{t}(e)=z
for any
z\in\mathcal{Z}
.

We can deal with cycles concisely in a linear-algebraic manner.
We define a vector
\bm{\mathcal{S}}(C)\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{E}|}
corresponding to a cycle
C
by

\displaystyle[\bm{\mathcal{S}}(C)]_{e}=\begin{cases}1&e\in C,\\
-1&-e\in C,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(30)

From a direct computation, we can find
\nabla^{\top}\bm{\mathcal{S}}(C)=\bm{0}
for any cycle
C
.
Thus, every vector
\bm{\mathcal{S}}(C)
generated from a cycle belongs to
\ker\nabla^{\top}
.
Furthermore, it is known that by appropriately choosing cycles, we can create a basis of
\ker\nabla^{\top}
from such vectors.
We label cycles whose corresponding vectors form a basis by a label
\mu\in\mathcal{M}=\{1,2,\dots,\dim\ker\nabla^{\top}\}
; that is, we can provide a basis by
\{\bm{\mathcal{S}}(C^{\mu})\}_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}}
.
For simplicity, we write
\bm{\mathcal{S}}(C^{\mu})
as
\bm{\mathcal{S}}^{\mu}
.
We call the set
\{\bm{\mathcal{S}}^{\mu}\}_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}}
the cycle basis of
G

(see also Fig.
3
for an example).

In his seminal work
Schnakenberg 1976
, Schnakenberg gave an expression for the steady-state EPR in terms of cycles.
Note that since
\nabla^{\top}\bm{J}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})=\bm{0}
, the steady-state current
\bm{J}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})
is in
{\rm ker}\nabla^{\top}
, and can be expanded in terms of the cycle basis as

\displaystyle\bm{J}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{J}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}};C^{\mu})\bm{\mathcal{S}}^{\mu}.

(31)

Here, the expansion coefficients
\mathcal{J}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}};C^{\mu})
represent the cyclic current that flows along the cycle
C^{\mu}
. Thus, the steady-state current can be interpreted as a superposition of such cyclic currents.
We note that the set of the coefficients
\{\mathcal{J}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}};C^{\mu})\}_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}}
is uniquely determined by
\bm{J}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})
because
\mathrm{Span}\{\bm{\mathcal{S}}^{\mu}\}_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}}=\ker\nabla^{\top}
.
The cycle affinity
\mathcal{F}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}};C^{\mu})
conjugate to
\mathcal{J}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}};C^{\mu})
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}};C^{\mu})\coloneqq\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}[\bm{\mathcal{S}}^{\mu}]_{e}[\bm{F}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})]_{e}=\langle\bm{\mathcal{S}}^{\mu},\bm{F}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})\rangle.

(32)

The cyclic currents and conjugate affinities lead to the decomposition of the steady-state EPR into contributions from individual cycles
Schnakenberg 1976
:

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})

\displaystyle=\bigg\langle\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{J}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}};C^{\mu})\bm{\mathcal{S}}^{\mu},\bm{F}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})\bigg\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{J}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}};C^{\mu})\langle\bm{\mathcal{S}}^{\mu},\bm{F}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{J}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}};C^{\mu})\mathcal{F}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}};C^{\mu}).

(33)

Ref.
Yoshimura et al. 2023
generalized this formula to nonstationary situations, giving a similar expression for the housekeeping EPR. For convenience, we write the housekeeping current and the housekeeping force as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})=\bm{J}(\bm{p})-\bm{J}^{*}(\bm{p}),\;\bm{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})=\bm{F}(\bm{p})-\bm{F}^{*}(\bm{p}).

(34)

Since
\nabla^{\top}\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})=\bm{0}
and
\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})\in{\rm ker}\nabla^{\top}
, a similar decomposition as in Eq. (
31
) is obtained for the housekeeping current:

\displaystyle\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})\bm{\mathcal{S}}^{\mu}

(35)

with coefficients
\{\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})\}_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}}
. The cycle affinity
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})
conjugate to
\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})\coloneqq\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}[\bm{\mathcal{S}}^{\mu}]_{e}[\bm{F}(\bm{p})]_{e}=\langle\bm{\mathcal{S}}^{\mu},\bm{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})\rangle.

(36)

Therefore,
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}
can always be decomposed into contributions from each cycle, even in a nonstationary regime
Yoshimura et al. 2023
:

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p};C^{\mu}).

(37)

This expression is a generalization of the expression Eq. (
33
) in Schnakenberg network theory.

II.3.2
Wasserstein geometry

This section briefly reviews the connection between the Wasserstein geometry and the geometric housekeeping-excess decomposition.

The Wasserstein distance was originally invented to quantify the cost to transport one distribution to another in a continuous space
Villani et al. 2008
. Extensions compatible with dynamics with discrete degrees of freedom have been accomplished in recent works
Maas 2011
;
Yoshimura et al. 2023
.
The extension, which we simply call the
2
-Wasserstein distance, is provided through the following minimization problem: for two probability distributions
\bm{p}^{(0)}
and
\bm{p}^{(1)}
, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(\bm{p}^{(0)},\bm{p}^{(1)})=\min_{\bm{p},\bm{F}^{\prime}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\|\bm{F}^{\prime}(t)\|^{2}_{L(\bm{p}(t))}dt\right)^{1/2},

(38)

subject to

\displaystyle d_{t}\bm{p}=\nabla^{\top}L(\bm{p})\bm{F}^{\prime},~\bm{p}(0)=\bm{p}^{(0)},~\bm{p}(1)=\bm{p}^{(1)}.

(39)

If there is no pair of
\bm{p}
and
\bm{F}^{\prime}
that satisfies Eq. (
39
) for a given
(\bm{p}^{(0)},\bm{p}^{(1)})
, we may let
\mathcal{W}(\bm{p}^{(0)},\bm{p}^{(1)})=\infty
.
We can equivalently express this
2
-Wasserstein distance with the integration interval
[0,\tau]
by performing the change of variables
t\to t/\tau
for arbitrary positive values of
\tau
. The minimization problem is rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(\bm{p}^{(0)},\bm{p}^{(1)})=\min_{\bm{p},\bm{F}^{\prime}}\left(\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}\|\bm{F}^{\prime}(t)\|^{2}_{L(\bm{p}(t))}dt\right)^{1/2},

(40)

subject to

\displaystyle d_{t}\bm{p}=\nabla^{\top}L(\bm{p})\bm{F}^{\prime},~\bm{p}(0)=\bm{p}^{(0)},~\bm{p}(\tau)=\bm{p}^{(1)}.

(41)

The connection between the Wasserstein distance and the geometric housekeeping-excess decomposition is discussed in terms of the instantaneous speed in the space of the
2
-Wasserstein distance. The instantaneous speed is defined as

\displaystyle v(\bm{p}(t))\coloneqq\lim_{\Delta t\to+0}\frac{\mathcal{W}(\bm{p}(t),\bm{p}(t+\Delta t))}{\Delta t}.

(42)

The paper
Yoshimura et al. 2023
shows that the square of the instantaneous speed is equivalent to the excess EPR, i.e.,
v(\bm{p}(t))^{2}=\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\bm{p}(t))
. Therefore, the instantaneous speed
v(\bm{p}(t))
is always well-defined. The relation
v(\bm{p}(t))^{2}=\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\bm{p}(t))
is useful to obtain the thermodynamic speed limit
Yoshimura et al. 2023
;
Van Vu and Hasegawa 2021
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{W}(\bm{p}(0),\bm{p}(\tau))^{2}}{\tau}\leq\int_{0}^{\tau}\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\bm{p}(t))dt.

(43)

This inequality indicates that the lower bound on the integrated excess EPR (i.e., the excess EP) comes from squaring the distance between the initial and final distributions and dividing by the time interval. We can interpret the speed limit as follows: The speed required to change the system’s state is limited by dissipation due to nonstationarity.

III
Geometric approach in information thermodynamics

In this section, we offer a geometric interpretation of the conventional theory of information thermodynamics for bipartite systems. This is more than just a mathematical rewrite. It refines the conventional formulation and establishes a foundation for a deeper understanding of information thermodynamics, which will be illustrated by the main results.

Let us introduce the setup and notation. We restrict our focus to a situation where the system
Z
consists of two subsystems
X
and
Y
so that we can discuss information theory. The states of
X
and
Y
are denoted by
x
and
y
, respectively, so we write the state of
Z
as
z=(x,y)
. Let
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{|\mathcal{X}|}\}
and
\mathcal{Y}=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{|\mathcal{Y}|}\}
be the sets of states of
X
and
Y
, respectively.
Then the set of states of
Z
is given by
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
. The joint probability of
(x,y)
is denoted by
p(x,y)
, and we define a vector
\bm{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}|}
by
[\bm{p}]_{z=(x,y)}=p(x,y)
.
The marginal distributions are given by
p_{X}(x)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}p(x,y)
and
p_{Y}(y)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p(x,y)
. Here, we define the marginalization matrices
\Pi_{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}|}
and
\Pi_{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Y}|\times|\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}|}
as
[\Pi_{X}]_{x^{\prime},z=(x,y)}=\delta_{x^{\prime},x}
and
[\Pi_{Y}]_{y^{\prime},z=(x,y)}=\delta_{y^{\prime},y}
, respectively. Using these matrices, we can express the marginal distributions as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{p}_{X}=\Pi_{X}\bm{p},\quad\bm{p}_{Y}=\Pi_{Y}\bm{p},

(44)

where
[\bm{p}_{X}]_{x}=p_{X}(x)
and
[\bm{p}_{Y}]_{y}=p_{Y}(y)
. We use the index
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
to denote either
X
or
Y
. For example, the above equations can be written as
\bm{p}_{\alpha}=\Pi_{\alpha}\bm{p}
.
We also write the other as
{\alpha}^{\rm c}\in\{X,Y\}
(thus,
X^{\rm c}=Y
and
Y^{\rm c}=X
). We call
{\alpha}^{\rm c}
the complementary system of
\alpha
.

III.1
Bipartite systems

We discuss bipartite systems
Barato et al. 2013
;
Hartich et al. 2014
;
Horowitz and Esposito 2014
, which are the simplest setup for studying information thermodynamics based on Markov jump processes. Bipartite systems are introduced through the following condition of transition rates:

\displaystyle R^{(\nu)}((x,y)|(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))=0

(45)

if
x\neq x^{\prime}
and
y\neq y^{\prime}
. That is, simultaneous transitions in subsystems
X
and
Y
are prohibited in the dynamics of bipartite systems.
In other words, jumps can induce a change in either
\mathcal{X}
or
\mathcal{Y}
. Thus, when the bipartite system is designated by a directed graph
G=(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{E})
, the set of edges
\mathcal{E}
can be partitioned into two sets
\mathcal{E}_{X}
and
\mathcal{E}_{Y}
as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{E}_{X}=\left\{\left(\nu,(x,y)\to(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\right)\in\mathcal{E}\mid x\neq x^{\prime},y=y^{\prime}\right\},\\
\mathcal{E}_{Y}=\left\{\left(\nu,(x,y)\to(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\right)\in\mathcal{E}\mid x=x^{\prime},y\neq y^{\prime}\right\},\end{split}

(46)

which satisfy
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{X}\cup\mathcal{E}_{Y}
and
\mathcal{E}_{X}\cap\mathcal{E}_{Y}=\emptyset
.
We also split the set of all the edges
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{all}}
into
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{all}}_{\alpha}=\bigcup_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}}\{e,-e\}
with
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
(i.e.,
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{all}}_{X}\cup\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{all}}_{Y}=\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{all}}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{all}}_{X}\cap\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{all}}_{Y}=\emptyset
).

For the sake of simplicity, we label edges by an index
i\in\{1,\dots,|\mathcal{E}_{X}|+|\mathcal{E}_{Y}|\}
as
e_{i}\in\mathcal{E}
such that
\mathcal{E}_{X}=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{|\mathcal{E}_{X}|}\}
and
\mathcal{E}_{Y}=\{e_{|\mathcal{E}_{X}|+1},\ldots,e_{|\mathcal{E}_{X}|+|\mathcal{E}_{Y}|}\}
without loss of generality.
We also assume that the
i
th row of a matrix (possibly a vector) with
|\mathcal{E}|
rows corresponds to
e_{i}
.
Then, it is split vertically, where the upper components correspond to
\mathcal{E}_{X}
and the lower to
\mathcal{E}_{Y}
(examples follow soon).
Similarly, assuming the correspondence in matrices with
|\mathcal{E}|
columns, we partition them horizontally, where a left component corresponds to
\mathcal{E}_{X}
and a right component corresponds to
\mathcal{E}_{Y}
.

We can find a special form of the discrete continuity equation (
2
) in bipartite systems. Note that the decomposition
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{X}\cup\mathcal{E}_{Y}
leads to the two graphs
G_{X}=(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{E}_{X})
and
G_{Y}=(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{E}_{Y})
(see also Fig.
4
(a) for an example). It further provides us the corresponding incidence matrices
\nabla^{\top}_{G_{X}}
and
\nabla^{\top}_{G_{Y}}
to
G_{X}
and
G_{Y}
, respectively. The incidence matrix of the original graph
G
can be reconstructed as

\displaystyle\nabla^{\top}=(\nabla^{\top}_{G_{X}}~\nabla^{\top}_{G_{Y}}),

(47)

because
[\nabla^{\top}]_{z,e}
is equal to
[\nabla^{\top}_{G_{\alpha}}]_{z,e^{\prime}}
when the edge corresponding to
e^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
is the same as the edge corresponding to the index
e\in\mathcal{E}
(this is an example of the “horizontal partition”). The flux
\bm{J}(\bm{p})
is also decomposed into
\bm{J}_{X}(\bm{p})
and
\bm{J}_{Y}(\bm{p})

where the vector
\bm{J}_{X}(\bm{p})=(J_{e_{1}}(\bm{p}),\ldots,J_{e_{|\mathcal{E}_{X}|}}(\bm{p}))^{\top}
is the set of fluxes for transitions corresponding to
\mathcal{E}_{X}
, and the vector
\bm{J}_{Y}(\bm{p})=(J_{e_{|\mathcal{E}_{X}|+1}}(\bm{p}),\ldots,J_{e_{|\mathcal{E}_{X}|+|\mathcal{E}_{Y}|}}(\bm{p}))^{\top}
is the set of fluxes for transitions corresponding to
\mathcal{E}_{Y}
(the “vertical partition”).
In particular, we use a symbol
\oplus
to express the recomposition of partitioned vectors; for example, we express the flux
\bm{J}(\bm{p})
as
\bm{J}(\bm{p})=\bm{J}_{X}(\bm{p})\oplus\bm{J}_{Y}(\bm{p})
. Generally, the symbol
\oplus
represents the operation of creating a column vector
(v_{e_{1}},\dots,v_{e_{|\mathcal{E}_{X}|+|\mathcal{E}_{Y}|}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{E}_{X}|+|\mathcal{E}_{Y}|}
from the column vectors
(v_{e_{1}},\dots,v_{e_{|\mathcal{E}_{X}|}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{E}_{X}|}
and
(v_{e_{|\mathcal{E}_{X}|+1}},\dots,v_{e_{|\mathcal{E}_{X}|+|\mathcal{E}_{Y}|}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{E}_{Y}|}
.
With the partition of
\nabla^{\top}
and
\bm{J}(\bm{p})
, the time evolution of the joint probability distribution
\bm{p}
governed by Eq. (
2
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle d_{t}\bm{p}

\displaystyle=\nabla^{\top}\bm{J}(\bm{p})=(\nabla^{\top}_{G_{X}}~\nabla^{\top}_{G_{Y}})\begin{pmatrix}\bm{J}_{X}(\bm{p})\\
\bm{J}_{Y}(\bm{p})\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\nabla^{\top}_{G_{X}}\bm{J}_{X}(\bm{p})+\nabla^{\top}_{G_{Y}}\bm{J}_{Y}(\bm{p}).

(48)

This equation can be regarded as a discrete version of the two-dimensional continuity equation.

The marginal distributions also obey a characteristic equation of motion. For an edge
e=(\nu(e),(x,y)\to(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))
, we introduce the notations
\mathrm{s}_{X}(e)=x
,
\mathrm{t}_{X}(e)=x^{\prime}
,
\mathrm{s}_{Y}(e)=y
, and
\mathrm{t}_{Y}(e)=y^{\prime}
. Using these notations, the
(x,e)
-component of
\Pi_{X}\nabla^{\top}_{G_{Y}}
for
e\in\mathcal{E}_{Y}
is calculated as

\displaystyle[\Pi_{X}\nabla^{\top}_{G_{Y}}]_{x,e}=\sum_{(x^{\prime},y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}[\Pi_{X}]_{x,(x^{\prime},y)}[\nabla^{\top}_{G_{Y}}]_{(x^{\prime},y),e}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x^{\prime},y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\delta_{x,x^{\prime}}(\delta_{(x^{\prime},y),\mathrm{t}(e)}-\delta_{(x^{\prime},y),\mathrm{s}(e)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x^{\prime},y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\delta_{x,x^{\prime}}(\delta_{x^{\prime},\mathrm{t}_{X}(e)}\delta_{y,\mathrm{t}_{Y}(e)}-\delta_{x^{\prime},\mathrm{s}_{X}(e)}\delta_{y,\mathrm{s}_{Y}(e)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}(\delta_{x,\mathrm{t}_{X}(e)}\delta_{y,\mathrm{t}_{Y}(e)}-\delta_{x,\mathrm{s}_{X}(e)}\delta_{y,\mathrm{s}_{Y}(e)})

\displaystyle=\delta_{x,\mathrm{t}_{X}(e)}-\delta_{x,\mathrm{s}_{X}(e)}.

(49)

For the third equality, we used the identities
\delta_{(x^{\prime},y),\mathrm{s}(e)}=\delta_{x,\mathrm{s}_{X}(e)}\delta_{y,\mathrm{s}_{Y}(e)}
and
\delta_{(x,y),\mathrm{t}(e)}=\delta_{x,\mathrm{t}_{X}(e)}\delta_{y,\mathrm{t}_{Y}(e)}
. Because
\mathrm{t}_{X}(e)=\mathrm{s}_{X}(e)
for
e\in\mathcal{E}_{Y}
, we obtain
\delta_{x,\mathrm{t}_{X}(e)}=\delta_{x,\mathrm{s}_{X}(e)}
and
[\Pi_{X}\nabla^{\top}_{G_{Y}}]_{x,e}=0
. Therefore, we find
\Pi_{X}\nabla^{\top}_{G_{Y}}=O
, where
O
indicates the zero matrix. Due to symmetry, we also have
\Pi_{Y}\nabla^{\top}_{G_{X}}=O
and thus
\Pi_{\alpha}\nabla^{\top}_{G_{\alpha^{\rm c}}}=O
for
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
. Hence, the time evolution of the marginal distribution
\bm{p}_{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
is given by

\displaystyle d_{t}\bm{p}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\alpha}\nabla^{\top}_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{J}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})+\Pi_{\alpha}\nabla^{\top}_{G_{\alpha^{\rm c}}}\bm{J}_{\alpha^{\rm c}}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\alpha}\nabla^{\top}_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{J}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}).

(50)

This equation can also be considered a discrete version of the continuity equation.

Let us see what happens in the steady state of a bipartite system. Because the current satisfies
d_{t}\bm{p}^{\rm st}=\nabla^{\top}_{G_{X}}\bm{J}_{X}(\bm{p}^{\rm st})+\nabla^{\top}_{G_{Y}}\bm{J}_{Y}(\bm{p}^{\rm st})=\bm{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla^{\top}_{G_{X}}\bm{J}_{X}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})=-\nabla^{\top}_{G_{Y}}\bm{J}_{Y}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}}).

(51)

This equation shows that, in the steady state, the divergence of the
X
-component of the current is canceled by that of the
Y
-component globally.
By using
\Pi_{\alpha}\nabla^{\top}_{G_{\alpha^{\rm c}}}=O
in Eq. (
51
), we also obtain

\displaystyle\Pi_{\alpha}\nabla^{\top}_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{J}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})=\bm{0},

(52)

for
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
.
It shows that the inflow and outflow of current are balanced in each subsystem.

.

III.2
Information flow

In this section, we introduce a thermodynamic measure of continuous information flow for bipartite systems called
information flow

Parrondo et al. 2015
. First, we define the Kullback–Leibler divergence between probability distributions
\bm{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{|\mathcal{Z}|})^{\top}
and
\bm{q}=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{|\mathcal{Z}|})^{\top}
as
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bm{p}\|\bm{q})=\sum_{z}p_{z}\ln(p_z/q_z)
.
Using the Kullback–Leibler divergence, we can introduce mutual information, which is a measure of dependence between two random variables. Mutual information between the random variable for
X
at time
s
, denoted by
\hat{X}_{s}
, and the random variable for
Y
at time
t
, denoted by
\hat{Y}_{t}
, is defined as

\displaystyle I(\hat{X}_{s};\hat{Y}_{t})=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bm{P}(s,t)\|\bm{p}_{X}(s)\otimes\bm{p}_{Y}(t)).

(53)

Here,
[\bm{P}(s,t)]_{(x,y)}\coloneqq P(x;s,y;t)
is the joint probability of the state
x
at time
s
in the system
X
and the state
y
at time
t
in the system
Y
, and
[\bm{p}_{X}(t)\otimes\bm{p}_{Y}(s)]_{(x,y)}\coloneqq p_{X}(x;s)p_{Y}(y;t)
is given by the marginal distributions
p_{X}(x;s)=\sum_{y}P(x;s,y;t)
and
p_{Y}(y;t)=\sum_{x}P(x;s,y;t)
. Let the distribution
\bm{p}(t)
be
\bm{p}(t)=\bm{P}(t,t)
.
Finally, information flow from system
Y
to
X
and that from
X
to
Y
are defined as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{I}_{X}(\bm{p}(t))=\lim_{\Delta t\to+0}\frac{I(\hat{X}_{t+\Delta t};\hat{Y}_{t})-I(\hat{X}_{t};\hat{Y}_{t})}{\Delta t},\\
\dot{I}_{Y}(\bm{p}(t))=\lim_{\Delta t\to+0}\frac{I(\hat{X}_{t};\hat{Y}_{t+\Delta t})-I(\hat{X}_{t};\hat{Y}_{t})}{\Delta t},\end{split}

(54)

respectively
Allahverdyan et al. 2009
;
Horowitz and Esposito 2014
.
Intuitively, information flow is expressed in terms of the partial derivative of mutual information. The quantity
\dot{I}_{\alpha}
measures the rate of change of mutual information due to transitions in the subsystem
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
.
Alternatively, it can be interpreted as the rate at which the subsystem
\alpha
learns about the other system
{\alpha}^{\rm c}
, and is thus also referred to as the learning rate
Hartich et al. 2014
or merely the change in mutual information between the two subsystems
Sagawa and Ueda 2012a
;
Still et al. 2012
;
Ito and Sagawa 2013
.

We next introduce the explicit form of information flow for bipartite systems. We define the vectors of stochastic Shannon entropy
\bm{h}(\bm{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}|}
,
\bm{h}_{X}(\bm{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|}
and
\bm{h}_{Y}(\bm{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Y}|}
as

[\bm{h}(\bm{p})]_{z=(x,y)}=-\ln p(x,y)
,
[\bm{h}_{X}(\bm{p})]_{x}=-\ln p_{X}(x)
, and
[\bm{h}_{Y}(\bm{p})]_{y}=-\ln p_{Y}(y)
, respectively. Using the stochastic Shannon entropy, the vector of stochastic mutual information
\bm{i}(\bm{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Z}|}
is defined as

\displaystyle\bm{i}(\bm{p})=-\bm{h}(\bm{p})+\Pi_{X}^{\top}\bm{h}_{X}(\bm{p})+\Pi_{Y}^{\top}\bm{h}_{Y}(\bm{p}).

(55)

The
(x,y)
-component of
\bm{i}(\bm{p}(t))
is given by

\displaystyle[\bm{i}(\bm{p}(t))]_{(x,y)}=\ln\frac{p(x,y;t)}{p_{X}(x;t)p_{Y}(y;t)},

(56)

and hence its expectation value gives the mutual information when
t=s
:

\displaystyle(\bm{p}(t))^{\top}\bm{i}(\bm{p}(t))

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}p(x,y;t)\ln\frac{p(x,y;t)}{p_{X}(x;t)p_{Y}(y;t)}

\displaystyle=I(\hat{X}_{t};\hat{Y}_{t}).

(57)

Using
\bm{i}(\bm{p}(t))
, we can express information flow as the inner product of the current and the gradient of stochastic mutual information, with respect to the subsystem
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
(see Appendix
A
for the derivation):

\displaystyle\dot{I}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}(t))=\langle\bm{J}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}(t)),\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{i}(\bm{p}(t))\rangle.

(58)

An expression corresponding to Eq. (
58
) in continuous systems has also been found in two-dimensional overdamped Langevin systems
Allahverdyan et al. 2009
;
Horowitz and Sandberg 2014
;
Rosinberg and Horowitz 2016
;
Nakazato and Ito 2021
where two-dimensional overdamped Langevin systems with independent noises can be regarded as bipartite systems.

The condition for bipartite systems enables us to split the time derivative of the mutual information into the sum of information flows:

\displaystyle d_{t}I(\hat{X}_{t};\hat{Y}_{t})=\dot{I}_{X}(\bm{p}(t))+\dot{I}_{Y}(\bm{p}(t)).

(59)

To derive Eq. (
59
) from Eq. (
58
), we focus on the following identity

\displaystyle\bm{p}^{\top}d_{t}\bm{i}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)d_{t}(\ln p(x,y)-\ln p_{X}(x)-\ln p_{Y}(y))

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}d_{t}p(x,y)-\sum_{x}d_{t}p_{X}(x)-\sum_{y}d_{t}p_{Y}(y)

\displaystyle=0,

(60)

where we used
d_{t}\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)=0
and
d_{t}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}p_{\alpha}(a)=0
for
(\alpha,\mathcal{A})\in\{(X,\mathcal{X}),(Y,\mathcal{Y})\}
. Using this identity, we can obtain Eq. (
59
) as follows:

\displaystyle d_{t}I(\hat{X}_{t};\hat{Y}_{t})

\displaystyle=(d_{t}\bm{p}(t))^{\top}\bm{i}(\bm{p}(t))+(\bm{p}(t))^{\top}d_{t}\bm{i}(\bm{p}(t))

\displaystyle=[\nabla^{\top}_{G_{X}}\bm{J}_{X}(\bm{p}(t))+\nabla^{\top}_{G_{Y}}\bm{J}_{Y}(\bm{p}(t))]^{\top}\bm{i}(\bm{p}(t))

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{J}_{X}(\bm{p}(t)),\nabla_{G_{X}}\bm{i}(\bm{p}(t))\rangle+\langle\bm{J}_{Y}(\bm{p}(t)),\nabla_{G_{Y}}\bm{i}(\bm{p}(t))\rangle

\displaystyle=\dot{I}_{X}(\bm{p}(t))+\dot{I}_{Y}(\bm{p}(t)).

(61)

We discuss information flow in the steady state. In this case,
\left.d_{t}I(\hat{X}_{t};\hat{Y}_{t})\right|_{\bm{p}(t)=\bm{p}^{\rm st}}=0
holds because the steady state condition
d_{t}\bm{p}^{\rm st}=0
leads to
\left.d_{t}I(\hat{X}_{t};\hat{Y}_{t})\right|_{\bm{p}(t)=\bm{p}^{\rm st}}=(d_{t}\bm{p}^{\rm st})^{\top}\bm{i}(\bm{p}^{\rm st})=0
.
Therefore, Eq. (
59
) implies that information flow in the steady state satisfies the relation

\displaystyle\dot{I}_{X}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})=-\dot{I}_{Y}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}}).

(62)

This relation means that information flow can be nonzero in the steady state, where the time derivative of mutual information is zero. Therefore, information flow between
X
and
Y
can still exist in the steady state.

III.3
The second law of information thermodynamics

This section discusses the second law of information thermodynamics for the subsystems, which is a generalization of the second law of thermodynamics. First, we introduce the entropy change rate associated with a subsystem
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
, which we write as
\sigma_{\alpha}
.
It is given by the sum of the entropy change rate in the subsystem, that is
d_{t}S^{\mathrm{sys}}_{\alpha}
, and that in the environment due to transitions in
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
, that is
\dot{S}^{\mathrm{env}}_{\alpha}
, as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha}=d_{t}S^{\mathrm{sys}}_{\alpha}+\dot{S}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{env}},

(63)

where
S^{\mathrm{sys}}_{\alpha}
and
\dot{S}^{\mathrm{env}}_{\alpha}
are defined as

\displaystyle S^{\mathrm{sys}}_{\alpha}=-\sum_{a}{p}_{\alpha}(a)\ln p_{\alpha}(a),

(64)

\displaystyle\dot{S}^{\mathrm{env}}_{\alpha}=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}}s_{e}(J^{+}_{e}-J^{-}_{e}),

(65)

respectively. If the complementary system
{\alpha}^{\rm c}
does not exist, then
\sigma_{\alpha}
corresponds to the EPR and is nonnegative. However, due to the influence of the complementary system
{\alpha}^{\rm c}
,
\sigma_{\alpha}
can generally take negative values. This negativity appears to contradict the second law of thermodynamics, in which case the complementary system acts as Maxwell’s demon. Nevertheless, the total system’s EPR
\dot{\Sigma}(\bm{p})
remains nonnegative, and the second law of thermodynamics is not violated.

Accounting for information flow
\dot{I}_{\alpha}
from
{\alpha}^{\rm c}
to
\alpha
, the thermodynamic irreversibility in the subsystem
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
can be properly quantified. Similar to the second law of thermodynamics, the following inequalities can be proven for the subsystems:

\displaystyle\sigma_{X}\geq\dot{I}_{X}\;\text{and}\;\sigma_{Y}\geq\dot{I}_{Y}.

(66)

This type of inequality in a subsystem is generally referred to as the second law of information thermodynamics
Parrondo et al. 2015
. Since
\dot{I}_{\alpha}
can take negative values,
\sigma_{\alpha}
can also be negative. In this case, we can interpret that the complementary system acts as Maxwell’s demon, and information flow compensates for the negative entropy changes in the subsystem
\alpha
and its environment. Based on the second law of information thermodynamics, we can define the partial EPR
\dot{\Sigma}_{\alpha}
, which quantifies the thermodynamic irreversibility associated with subsystem
\alpha
, as

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}_{\alpha}=\sigma_{\alpha}-\dot{I}_{\alpha}\geq 0.

(67)

Its non-negativity is due to the second law of information thermodynamics [Eq. (
66
)].

We briefly outline the derivation of Eq. (
66
). First, we decompose the thermodynamic force
\bm{F}(\bm{p})
as follows,

\displaystyle\bm{F}(\bm{p})=\bm{F}_{X}(\bm{p})\oplus\bm{F}_{Y}(\bm{p}),

(68)

\displaystyle\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}=\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}_{X}\oplus\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}_{Y},~\bm{F}^{\mathrm{info}}(\bm{p})=\bm{F}^{\mathrm{info}}_{X}(\bm{p})\oplus\bm{F}^{\mathrm{info}}_{Y}(\bm{p}),

(69)

where
\bm{F}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
means the force corresponding to
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
in the system
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
and
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}_{\alpha}=\bm{F}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})-\bm{F}^{\mathrm{info}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
with
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{info}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})=\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{h}
. Using the notation and Eq. (
50
), we obtain
d_{t}S^{\mathrm{sys}}_{\alpha}=(d_{t}\bm{p}_{\alpha})^{\top}\bm{h}_{\alpha}=(\Pi_{\alpha}\nabla^{\top}_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{J}_{\alpha})^{\top}\bm{h}_{\alpha}=\langle\bm{J}_{\alpha},\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\Pi^{\top}_{\alpha}\bm{h}_{\alpha}\rangle
and
\dot{S}^{\mathrm{env}}_{\alpha}=\langle\bm{J}_{\alpha},\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}_{\alpha}\rangle
. Then, we can show the non-negativity of the EPR:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sigma_{\alpha}-\dot{I}_{\alpha}&=\langle\bm{J}_{\alpha},\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\Pi^{\top}_{\alpha}\bm{h}_{\alpha}+\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}_{\alpha}\rangle\\
&\quad-\langle\bm{J}_{\alpha},\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}(-\bm{h}+\Pi^{\top}_{X}\bm{h}_{X}+\Pi^{\top}_{Y}\bm{h}_{Y})\rangle\end{split}

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{J}_{\alpha},\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}_{\alpha}+\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{h}\rangle=\bm{F}_{\alpha}^{\top}L_{\alpha}\bm{F}_{\alpha}\geq 0,

(70)

where we define the positive-definite diagonal matrix
L_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}|\times|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}|}
as
[L_{\alpha}]_{e^{\prime},e}=l_{e}\delta_{e,e^{\prime}}
and used
\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\Pi^{\top}_{{\alpha}^{\rm c}}=O
,
\bm{F}_{\alpha}=\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}_{\alpha}+\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{h}
and the relation
\bm{J}_{\alpha}=L_{\alpha}\bm{F}_{\alpha}
. Similarly to Eq. (
14
), we can also express the partial EPR geometrically. Here, we define the inner product and norm with respect to
L_{\alpha}
as follows:
\langle\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime}\rangle_{L_{\alpha}}=\bm{v}^{\top}L_{\alpha}\bm{v}^{\prime}
and
\|\bm{v}\|_{L_{\alpha}}=\sqrt{\langle\bm{v},\bm{v}\rangle_{L_{\alpha}}}
for
\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}|}
. Using this norm, we obtain

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}_{\alpha}=\langle\bm{J}_{\alpha},\bm{F}_{\alpha}\rangle=\|\bm{F}_{\alpha}\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}}\geq 0.

(71)

From this expression, we find that
\dot{\Sigma}_{\alpha}=0
is achieved if and only if
\bm{F}_{\alpha}=\bm{0}
, and thus the equality condition of the second law of information thermodynamics (
66
) is also given by
\bm{F}_{\alpha}=\bm{0}
. When this equality holds, the relation
\bm{J}_{\alpha}=L_{\alpha}\bm{F}_{\alpha}=\bm{0}
provides
\sigma_{\alpha}=0
and
\dot{I}_{\alpha}=0
. Thus, the equality condition is only achieved when information flow does not exist. We note that this fact does not prevent finite information transfer with zero partial entropy production. This situation can be viewed as an optimal information-thermodynamic engine, such as the Szilard engine
Szilard 1929
;
Sagawa 2014
. If we consider the situation where
\dot{I}_{\alpha}=O(1/\tau)
and
\dot{\Sigma}_{\alpha}=O(1/\tau^{2})
for a time interval
\tau
, then its time integral
\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\dot{I}_{\alpha}
could be finite but the partial entropy production
\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\dot{\Sigma}_{\alpha}
can become zero in the limit
\tau\to\infty
.

IV
Main results

In this section, we present the main results, which are the decomposition of information flow and its consequences.

IV.1
Geometric housekeeping-excess decomposition of information flow

We will decompose information flow into housekeeping and excess parts based on the decomposition of currents.
When the system is bipartite, currents that appeared in the EPR decomposition are partitioned into
X
and
Y
components.
Recall that
\bm{J}^{*}(\bm{p})
and
\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})
were defined respectively as the current that reproduces the dynamics as in Eq. (
15
) and given by a conservative force as in Eq. (
16
) and as the remainder
\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})=\bm{J}(\bm{p})-\bm{J}^{*}(\bm{p})
, which consists of cyclic contributions.
Then, they are decomposed as
\bm{J}^{*}(\bm{p})=\bm{J}^{*}_{X}(\bm{p})\oplus\bm{J}^{*}_{Y}(\bm{p})
and
\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})=\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{X}(\bm{p})\oplus\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{Y}(\bm{p})
in bipartite systems.
Given these decompositions, the continuity equation (
48
) is rewritten as

\displaystyle d_{t}\bm{p}=\nabla^{\top}_{G_{X}}\bm{J}_{X}^{*}(\bm{p})+\nabla^{\top}_{G_{Y}}\bm{J}_{Y}^{*}(\bm{p}).

(72)

Because of the condition
\Pi_{\alpha}\nabla^{\top}_{G_{{\alpha}^{\rm c}}}=O
, the time evolution of the marginal probability distribution in Eq. (
50
) can also be rewritten as

\displaystyle d_{t}\bm{p}_{\alpha}=\Pi_{\alpha}\nabla^{\top}_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{J}^{*}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}),

(73)

for
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
.
Since
\nabla^{\top}\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})=\bm{0}
, the decomposition
\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})=\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{X}(\bm{p})\oplus\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{Y}(\bm{p})
leads to the antisymmetric relation

\displaystyle\nabla^{\top}_{G_{X}}\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{X}(\bm{p})=-\nabla^{\top}_{G_{Y}}\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{Y}(\bm{p}).

(74)

Then, housekeeping and excess information flow are defined by

\displaystyle\dot{I}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}),\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{i}(\bm{p})\rangle,

(75)

\displaystyle\dot{I}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{J}^{*}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}),\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{i}(\bm{p})\rangle,

(76)

for
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
. Due to the relation
\bm{J}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})=\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})+\bm{J}^{*}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
and the explicit formula (
58
), we find that they satisfy
\dot{I}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})=\dot{I}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})+\dot{I}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
.
Besides, from Eq. (
74
) we find that the housekeeping information flow satisfies the antisymmetric relation

\displaystyle\dot{I}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{X}(\bm{p})=-\dot{I}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{Y}(\bm{p}),

(77)

reproducing the relation in Eq. (
62
) fulfilled by the total information flow in the steady state.
Combining this property with Eq. (
59
) further implies that the time derivative of the mutual information is solely determined by the excess information flow (compare with Eq. (
59
))

\displaystyle d_{t}I(\hat{X}_{t};\hat{Y}_{t})=\dot{I}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{X}(\bm{p}(t))+\dot{I}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{Y}(\bm{p}(t)).

(78)

When the system is in the steady state, since we have
\bm{J}^{*}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})=\bm{0}
, we can show that

\displaystyle\dot{I}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})=\dot{I}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}}),~\dot{I}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})=0,

(79)

for
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
; i.e., the housekeeping information flow coincides with the total information flow in the steady state.
If the system is detailed balanced, we generally have
\bm{J}^{*}(\bm{p})=\bm{J}(\bm{p})
and we obtain

\displaystyle\dot{I}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})=0,~\dot{I}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})=\dot{I}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}),

(80)

for
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
and any
\bm{p}
, which means that the housekeeping information flow is absent if the system is detailed balanced.

We interpret the physical meanings of the housekeeping information flow
\dot{I}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
and the excess information flow
\dot{I}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
.
Originally, information flow
\dot{I}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
in Eq. (
54
) represents the change in mutual information caused by the time evolution in subsystem
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
. From Eq. (
80
), we see that it only consists of the excess contribution if the detailed balance condition is satisfied; if not,
\dot{I}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
potentially consists of two distinct modes.
Specifically,
\dot{I}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}
will arise with
\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})
not vanishing.
As already explained,
\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})
represents the cyclic contribution in the whole dynamics, given as a sum of cyclic currents; therefore, we can interpret
\dot{I}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}
as quantifying the “cyclic” mode in the information flow.
Since the cyclic current leaves the total distribution unchanged, the housekeeping information flow does not change the mutual information [Eq. (
78
)].
Nevertheless,
\dot{I}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{X}
and
\dot{I}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{Y}
have non-zero values, which are balanced as in Eq. (
77
).
Reproducing the antisymmetric relation for the total information flow in the steady state, this relation reveals that the housekeeping information flow provides the information flow required to maintain the nonequilibrium steady state.
On the other hand,
\dot{I}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
can be interpreted as the “conservative” mode, since it arises from
\bm{J}^{*}(\bm{p})
, which is conjugate to a conservative force. Because
\bm{J}^{*}(\bm{p})
gives the time evolution of
\bm{p}
in the continuity equation [Eq. (
17
)], this mode substantially changes the mutual information, and thus, Eq. (
78
) holds. In the steady state, the mutual information does not change over time. In fact,
\dot{I}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}
also vanishes in the steady state.

IV.2
Generalization of the second law of information thermodynamics

In this section, we derive a generalization of the second law of information thermodynamics. We show that the second law of information thermodynamics (
66
) can be expressed as two inequalities, one corresponding to the housekeeping contribution and the other to the excess contribution.

First, we discuss how to define the housekeeping and excess entropy change rates associated with a subsystem
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
.
For this purpose, we refer to the expressions of the housekeeping and excess EPRs given in Eqs. (
25
) and (
26
).
In Eq. (
25
), the excess EPR is defined as the sum of the time derivative of the Shannon entropy
d_{t}S^{\mathrm{sys}}(\bm{p})
and the excess environmental entropy change
\dot{S}^{\mathrm{ex,env}}(\bm{p})
.
Therefore, we can define the excess entropy change rate associated with
\alpha
,
\sigma_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\bm{p})
, by replacing the Shannon entropy of
\bm{p}
,
\bm{J}^{*}(\bm{p})
, and
\nabla
with that of
\bm{p}_{\alpha}
,
\bm{J}_{\alpha}^{*}(\bm{p})
, and
\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}
, respectively. Thus, we define
\sigma_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\bm{p})
as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=d_{t}S^{\mathrm{sys}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})+\dot{S}^{\mathrm{ex,\,env}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}),

(81)

where
\dot{S}^{\mathrm{ex,\,env}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
is defined as

\displaystyle\dot{S}^{\mathrm{ex,\,env}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})=-\langle\bm{J}^{*}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}),\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{\epsilon}^{*}(\bm{p})\rangle.

(82)

Using the definition of
d_{t}S^{\mathrm{sys}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
,
\sigma_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\bm{p})
is calculated as
\sigma_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\bm{p})=\langle\bm{J}^{*}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}),\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}(\Pi^{\top}_{\alpha}\bm{h}_{\alpha}-\bm{\epsilon}^{*})\rangle
.
On the other hand, Eq. (
26
) characterizes the housekeeping EPR as the inner product between the housekeeping current
\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})=\bm{J}(\bm{p})-\bm{J}^{*}(\bm{p})
and the nonconservative part of thermal force
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th,\,nc}}(\bm{p})=\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}-(\bm{F}^{*}(\bm{p})-\nabla\bm{h}(\bm{p}))
. Similarly, we define the housekeeping entropy change rate of subsystem
\alpha
, denoted by
\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
. We decompose the forces as
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}=\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}_{X}\oplus\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}_{Y}
and
\bm{F}^{*}=\bm{F}^{*}_{X}\oplus\bm{F}^{*}_{Y}
, and define the nonconservative thermal force associated with
\alpha
as
\bm{f}^{\mathrm{nc}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})=\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}_{\alpha}-(\bm{F}^{*}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})-\nabla_{g_{\alpha}}\bm{h}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}))
. Here,
\nabla_{g_{\alpha}}\coloneqq\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\Pi^{\top}_{\alpha}
is the gradient operator associated with a graph
g_{\alpha}
that can be obtained using the
graph contraction method
, which is explained in detail in Appendix
B
. Thus,
\nabla_{g_{\alpha}}\bm{h}_{\alpha}
is the gradient of the stochastic Shannon entropy for the marginal distribution
\bm{p}_{\alpha}
.
Then, we define
\sigma_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})
as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}),\bm{f}^{\mathrm{nc}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})\rangle.

(83)

Here, we have an equality
\langle\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}),\nabla_{g_{\alpha}}\bm{h}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})\rangle=0
because
\langle\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}),\nabla_{g_{\alpha}}\bm{h}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})\rangle=(\Pi_{\alpha}\nabla^{\top}_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}))^{\top}\bm{h}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})=(-\Pi_{\alpha}\nabla^{\top}_{G_{{\alpha}^{\rm c}}}\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{{\alpha}^{\rm c}}(\bm{p}))^{\top}\bm{h}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})=0
, where we used Eq. (
74
) and
\Pi_{\alpha}\nabla^{\top}_{G_{{\alpha}^{\rm c}}}=O
. Therefore,
\sigma_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})
can be rewritten as
\sigma_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})=\langle\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}),\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}_{\alpha}-\bm{F}^{*}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})\rangle
.

Let us see basic properties of
\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
and
\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
.
If
\alpha
represents the entire system and
{\alpha}^{\rm c}
does not exist,
\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
and
\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
reproduce
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\bm{p})(\geq 0)
and
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})(\geq 0)
, respectively, because
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}=\mathcal{E}
and
\bm{p}_{\alpha}=\bm{p}
.
However, in the presence of
{\alpha}^{\rm c}
, the positivity of excess and housekeeping entropy change rates
\sigma_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\bm{p})
and
\sigma_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})
is no longer guaranteed. In fact, they can generally be negative, as demonstrated by numerical calculations in Sec.
V
.
If the system is detailed balanced, we have
\bm{J}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})=\bm{J}^{*}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
and
\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{\epsilon}^{*}=\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{\epsilon}=\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}_{\alpha}
, so that

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})=0,\;\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})=\sigma_{\alpha}(\bm{p}).

(84)

When the system is in the steady state
\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})=\sigma_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}}),\;\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})=0

(85)

because
\bm{J}^{*}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})=\bm{0}
and
\bm{F}^{*}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})=\bm{0}
are satisfied.

We note that
\sigma_{\alpha}(\bm{p})\neq\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})+\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
in general. The following calculation confirms this fact:

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}+\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{J}_{\alpha}-\bm{J}^{*}_{\alpha},\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}_{\alpha}-\bm{F}^{*}_{\alpha}\rangle+(d_{t}\bm{p}_{\alpha})^{\top}\bm{h}_{\alpha}-\langle\bm{J}^{*}_{\alpha},\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{\epsilon}^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=(d_{t}\bm{p}_{\alpha})^{\top}\bm{h}_{\alpha}+\langle\bm{J}_{\alpha},\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}_{\alpha}\rangle\\
&\quad-\langle\bm{J}_{\alpha},\bm{F}^{*}_{\alpha}\rangle+\langle\bm{J}^{*}_{\alpha},\bm{F}^{*}_{\alpha}-\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}_{\alpha}-\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{\epsilon}^{*}\rangle\end{split}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\alpha}-\langle\bm{J}^{*}_{\alpha},\bm{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}+\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}_{\alpha}+\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{\epsilon}^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\alpha}-2\langle\bm{J}^{*}_{\alpha},\bm{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}\rangle,

(86)

where we used
\langle\bm{J}_{\alpha},\bm{F}^{*}_{\alpha}\rangle=\langle\bm{J}_{\alpha}^{*},\bm{F}_{\alpha}\rangle
,
\sigma_{\alpha}=(d_{t}\bm{p}_{\alpha})^{\top}\bm{h}_{\alpha}+\langle\bm{J}_{\alpha},\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}_{\alpha}\rangle
, and
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}_{\alpha}+\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{\epsilon}^{*}=\bm{F}_{\alpha}+\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}(\bm{\epsilon}^{*}-\bm{h})=\bm{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}
.
Due to the presence of the system
{\alpha}^{\rm c}
,
\langle\bm{J}^{*}_{\alpha},\bm{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}\rangle
cannot vanish in general, whereas the sum with
\langle\bm{J}^{*}_{{\alpha}^{\rm c}},\bm{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{{\alpha}^{\rm c}}\rangle
is always zero as

\displaystyle\langle\bm{J}^{*}_{\alpha},\bm{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}\rangle+\langle\bm{J}^{*}_{{\alpha}^{\rm c}},\bm{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{{\alpha}^{\rm c}}\rangle=\langle\bm{J}^{*},\bm{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}\rangle=0.

(87)

Given
\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}
and
\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
, we can generalize the second law of information thermodynamics. Since both
\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}
and
\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}
can take negative values, they do not quantify irreversibility of thermodynamic processes as
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}
and
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}
do. On the other hand, combining
\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}
with
\dot{I}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}
and
\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}
with
\dot{I}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}
results in the following two inequalities:

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})\geq\dot{I}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}),

(88)

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})\geq\dot{I}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}).

(89)

These inequalities can be interpreted as generalizations of the second law of information thermodynamics [Eq. (
66
)].

These two generalizations of the second law of information thermodynamics offer a fresh perspective on existing discussions in information thermodynamics. According to the conventional second law of information thermodynamics [Eq. (
66
)], the entropy change of a subsystem is guaranteed by information flow. The existence of Maxwell’s demon is assumed when the entropy change of the subsystem is negative. On the other hand, according to our results, information flow has two components: housekeeping and excess information flows. These components impose different bounds [Eqs. (
88
) and (
89
)]. Therefore, whether the quantities
\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
and
\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
can become negative depends on the signs of
\dot{I}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
and
\dot{I}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
, respectively. Therefore, we can discuss the existence of Maxwell’s demon with regard to housekeeping and excess dissipation separately, based on the negativity of the respective quantities
\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
and
\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
. Indeed, we present a numerical model in which Maxwell’s demon exists with respect to housekeeping dissipation, but not with respect to excess dissipation in Sec
V
.

We provide the proofs for Eqs. (
88
) and (
89
). First, recalling the definitions of
\bm{i}
[Eq. (
55
)] and
\dot{I}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}
[Eq. (
75
)], we can perform the following calculation:

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}-\dot{I}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha},\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}_{\alpha}-\bm{F}^{*}_{\alpha}+\nabla_{g_{\alpha}}\bm{h}_{\alpha}\rangle-\langle\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha},\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha},\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}_{\alpha}-\bm{F}^{*}_{\alpha}+\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{h}-\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\Pi^{\top}_{{\alpha}^{\rm c}}\bm{h}_{{\alpha}^{\rm c}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha},\bm{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}\rangle,

(90)

where we used
\bm{F}_{\alpha}=\bm{F}^{\mathrm{th}}_{\alpha}+\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{h}
,
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}=\bm{F}_{\alpha}-\bm{F}^{*}_{\alpha}
, and
\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\Pi^{\top}_{{\alpha}^{\rm c}}=O
. From the linear relation
\bm{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}=L_{\alpha}\bm{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}
, we obtain
\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}-\dot{I}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}=(\bm{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha})^{\top}L_{\alpha}\bm{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}=\|\bm{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}}\geq 0
, which is the desired bound [Eq. (
88
)]. Next, using the definitions of
\bm{i}
[Eq. (
55
)] and
\dot{I}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}
[Eq. (
76
)], we obtain

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}-\dot{I}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{J}^{*}_{\alpha},\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}(\Pi^{\top}_{\alpha}\bm{h}_{\alpha}-\bm{\epsilon}^{*}-\bm{i})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{J}^{*}_{\alpha},\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}(\bm{h}-\bm{\epsilon}^{*})-\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\Pi^{\top}_{{\alpha}^{\rm c}}\bm{h}_{{\alpha}^{\rm c}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{J}^{*}_{\alpha},\bm{F}^{*}_{\alpha}\rangle=\|\bm{F}^{*}_{\alpha}\|_{L_{\alpha}}^{2}\geq 0,

(91)

where we used
\bm{F}^{*}_{\alpha}=\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}(\bm{h}-\bm{\epsilon}^{*})
,
\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\Pi^{\top}_{{\alpha}^{\rm c}}=O
and
\bm{J}^{*}_{\alpha}=L_{\alpha}\bm{F}^{*}_{\alpha}
.
This inequality is equivalent to the desired bound [Eq. (
89
)].

Based on the inequalities [Eqs. (
88
) and (
89
)], we define the partial housekeeping EPR
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
and the partial excess EPR
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
as

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})\coloneqq\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})-\dot{I}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})=\|\bm{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})\|_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p})}^{2},

(92)

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})\coloneqq\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})-\dot{I}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})=\|\bm{F}^{*}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})\|_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p})}^{2},

(93)

respectively. Since these quantities are nonnegative, the quantities
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
and
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
can be considered measures of dissipation.
Here,
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}
quantifies the irreversibility due to nonconservative external driving forces that prevent the subsystem
\alpha
from reaching equilibrium, and
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}
quantifies the irreversibility arising from the nonstationarity in the subsystem
\alpha
. Because
[L_{\alpha}]_{e,e^{\prime}}=l_{e}\delta_{e,e^{\prime}}
and

L(\bm{p})=\mathrm{diag}(l_{1}(\bm{p}),l_{2}(\bm{p}),\dots,l_{|\mathcal{E}|}(\bm{p}))
, we obtain

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{X}(\bm{p})+\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{Y}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\|\bm{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})\|_{L(\bm{p})}^{2}=\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p}),

(94)

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{X}(\bm{p})+\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{Y}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\|\bm{F}^{*}(\bm{p})\|_{L(\bm{p})}^{2}=\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}(\bm{p}).

(95)

Therefore, the housekeeping and excess EPRs in the total system are decomposed into the partial components in the subsystems.
On the other hand, the partial EPRs are not reconstructed from the partial housekeeping and excess EPRs; i.e.,
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})+\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})\neq\dot{\Sigma}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
for
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
in general because
\sigma^{\rm hk}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})+\sigma^{\rm ex}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})\neq\sigma_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
while
\dot{I}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})=\dot{I}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})+\dot{I}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
.
Instead, we can provide a geometric decomposition of the partial EPRs based on the concept of local conservativeness, which will be discussed in Sec.
IV.5
.

IV.3
Generalization of cyclic decomposition

Here, we present the cyclic decomposition of the partial housekeeping EPR and the housekeeping information flow.
This result generalizes the cyclic decomposition of the housekeeping EPR [Eq. (
37
)] for subsystems.
Moreover, it extends other information-thermodynamic results to nonstationary situations; the partial EPR
Yamamoto et al. 2016
and the information flow
Horowitz and Esposito 2014
are known to be expressed in the form of the cyclic decomposition in the steady state, as is the total EPR. We will provide a generalization of these results to non-steady states, with a focus on the housekeeping term introduced in preceding sections.

As a preliminary step to the cyclic decomposition, we categorize the cycles
\{C^{\mu}\}_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}}

as either local or global.
A cycle
C^{\mu}=\{e_{i(1)},\ldots,e_{i(|C^{\mu}|)}\}
(
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
) is defined to be
\alpha
-local (
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
) if
C^{\mu}\subset\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{all}}_{\alpha}
holds. Due to the definition, the edges in
C^{\mu}
satisfy

\displaystyle\mathrm{s}_{{\alpha}^{\rm c}}(e_{i(k)})=\mathrm{s}_{{\alpha}^{\rm c}}(e_{i(k+1)})

(96)

for any
k\in\{1,2,\dots,|C^{\mu}|\}
, in addition to the cyclic condition
\mathrm{t}(e_{i(k)})=\mathrm{s}(e_{i(k+1)})
.
That is, the state in
{\alpha}^{\rm c}
never changes in an
\alpha
-local cycle.
We write the set of indices of
\alpha
-local cycles as
\mathcal{M}^{\alpha}\subset\mathcal{M}
(
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
).
Global cycles are defined as cycles neither
X
-local nor
Y
-local, whose set of indices is denoted by
\mathcal{M}^{XY}
; thus,
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}^{X}\cup\mathcal{M}^{Y}\cup\mathcal{M}^{XY}
.

To demonstrate the cyclic decomposition, let us define the partial housekeeping cycle affinity of
C^{\mu}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})\coloneqq\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}}[\bm{\mathcal{S}}^{\mu}]_{e}[\bm{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p})]_{e},

(97)

for
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
.
The quantity
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})
resembles the housekeeping cycle affinity (
36
), but is not identical because the sum range is restricted by
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
, resulting in a different value.
They coincide only if the cycle is
\alpha
-local; i.e., we have
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})=\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})
only if
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{\alpha}
.
In the other cases,
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{{\alpha}^{\rm c}}
or
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{XY}
,
we find
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})\neq\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})
in general. In particular,
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})=0
for
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{{\alpha}^{\rm c}}
because then
[\bm{\mathcal{S}}^{\mu}]_{e}=0
for all
e\in\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
.

Using the partial housekeeping cycle affinity
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})
and the housekeeping cyclic current
\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})
[Eq. (
35
)], we can decompose the partial housekeeping EPR of the subsystem
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
into the contributions from individual cycles:

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{\alpha}\cup\mathcal{M}^{XY}}\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p};C^{\mu}).

(98)

This expression is a generalization of Eq. (
37
) for
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}
, and a generalization of the expression for the partial EPR in the steady state discussed in Ref.
Yamamoto et al. 2016
.

It is noteworthy that Eq. (
98
) can be considered as a cyclic decomposition for the contracted graph
g_{\alpha}
. Precisely,
\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})
and
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})
can be interpreted as the cyclic current and cycle affinity associated with a cycle in
g_{\alpha}
. The cycles in
g_{\alpha}
are called
contracted cycles
. A concrete explanation of the concept of a contracted cycle is provided in Appendix
C
, and the proof of Eq. (
98
) is provided in Appendix
D
.

Then, we discuss the cyclic decomposition of the housekeeping information flow. To this end, we formally expand the concept of
information affinity
, which was originally introduced by Horowitz and Esposito for the steady state
Horowitz and Esposito 2014
, to include the non-steady state. More specifically, the information affinity along cycle
C^{\mu}
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{info}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}}[\bm{\mathcal{S}}^{\mu}]_{e}([\bm{i}(\bm{p})]_{\mathrm{t}(e)}-[\bm{i}(\bm{p})]_{\mathrm{s}(e)})

(99)

for
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
.
Note that
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{info}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}};C^{\mu})
coincides with the conventional definition.
We can interpret
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{info}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})
as describing the change in mutual information caused by the changes specified by the edges in
C^{\mu}\cap\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{all}}_{\alpha}
.
Although we formally defined
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{info}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})
for all the cycles in
G
, this quantity is zero for any local cycle:
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{info}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})=0
holds for
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{X}\cup\mathcal{M}^{Y}
(see Appendix
E
). Therefore, information affinity can only be non-zero within a global cycle during which information is exchanged between two subsystems.

The next step is to use the information affinity to derive the cyclic decomposition of the housekeeping information flow. Here, let us simply state the result (see also Appendix
E
for the proof):

\displaystyle\dot{I}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{XY}}\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{info}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p};C^{\mu}).

(100)

Therefore,
\dot{I}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
is expressed solely by the contributions from global cycles
\{C^{\mu}\}_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{XY}}
.
For the steady state, the representation of information flow by information affinity and its decomposition into global cycles was discovered by Horowitz and Esposito
Horowitz and Esposito 2014
, and further analyzed in detail by Yamamoto et al.
Yamamoto et al. 2016
. Therefore, Eq. (
100
) is regarded as a generalization of that result for the housekeeping information flow in a non-steady state.

To further characterize Eq. (
100
), we define the housekeeping information flow along the global cycle
C^{\mu}
(
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{XY}
) as
\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})=\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{info}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})
, which leads to the decomposition
\dot{I}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{XY}}\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})
. We point out that
\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})
satisfies the antisymmetric relation at the level of individual cycles:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{X}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})=-\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{Y}(\bm{p};C^{\mu}).

(101)

The antisymmetric relation can be verified as follows:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{X}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})+\dot{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{Y}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{hk}}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{info}}_{X}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})+\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{info}}_{Y}(\bm{p};C^{\mu}))=0,

(102)

because

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{info}}_{X}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})+\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{info}}_{Y}(\bm{p};C^{\mu})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}[\bm{\mathcal{S}}^{\mu}]_{e}([\bm{i}(\bm{p})]_{\mathrm{t}(e)}-[\bm{i}(\bm{p})]_{\mathrm{s}(e)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}[\bm{\mathcal{S}}^{\mu}]_{e}\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}[\nabla]_{e,(x,y)}[\bm{i}(\bm{p})]_{(x,y)}

\displaystyle=(\nabla^{\top}\bm{\mathcal{S}}^{\mu})^{\top}\bm{i}(\bm{p})=0,

(103)

where the last equality follows from
\nabla^{\top}\bm{\mathcal{S}}^{\mu}=\bm{0}
.

IV.4
Short-time thermodynamic uncertainty relation

We discuss a short-time thermodynamic uncertainty relation (TUR) for the partial excess EPR, which is a generalization of the short-time TUR for the excess EPR in Ref.
Yoshimura et al. 2023
.
We use the geometric representation of the partial excess EPR [Eq. (
93
)] to obtain a TUR.

To this end, we introduce the time-independent observable
\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}\in\mathop{\mathrm{im}}\Pi^{\top}_{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
. Note that
[\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}]_{(x,y)}
depends on only the state of the subsystem
\alpha
. This fact can be confirmed as follows. By introducing
\bm{\psi}_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{A}|}
(
(\alpha,\mathcal{A})\in\{(X,\mathcal{X}),(Y,\mathcal{Y})\}
) such that
\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}=\Pi^{\top}_{\alpha}\bm{\psi}_{\alpha}
, we have
[\bm{\Psi}_{X}]_{(x,y)}=[\bm{\psi}_{X}]_{x}
and
[\bm{\Psi}_{Y}]_{(x,y)}=[\bm{\psi}_{Y}]_{y}
.

To obtain a TUR, we consider the following Cauchy–Schwarz inequality with respect to the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p})}
:

\displaystyle(\langle\bm{F}^{*}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}),\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}\rangle_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p})})^{2}\leq\|\bm{F}^{*}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p})}\|\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p})}

(104)

for any time-independent observable
\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}\in\mathop{\mathrm{im}}\Pi^{\top}_{\alpha}
. Because
d_{t}\bm{p}_{\alpha}=\Pi_{\alpha}\nabla^{\top}_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{J}^{*}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})=\nabla^{\top}_{g_{\alpha}}\bm{J}^{*}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
holds, we can rewrite
\langle\bm{F}^{*}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}),\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}\rangle_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p})}
as

\displaystyle\langle\bm{F}^{*}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}),\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}\rangle_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p})}

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{J}^{*}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}),\nabla_{g_{\alpha}}\bm{\psi}_{\alpha}\rangle

\displaystyle=(d_{t}\bm{p}_{\alpha})^{\top}\bm{\psi}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=d_{t}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{\top}\bm{\psi}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=d_{t}(\bm{p}^{\top}\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}).

(105)

From the geometric representation
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}=\|\bm{F}^{*}_{\alpha}\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}}
and Eqs. (
104
) and (
105
), we obtain the following TUR for the partial excess EPR:

\displaystyle\frac{(d_{t}(\bm{p}^{\top}\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}))^{2}}{\|\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}\|_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p})}^{2}}\leq\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}).

(106)

This inequality implies that
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}
is bounded by the time derivative of the expected value
\bm{p}^{\top}\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}
, which quantifies the speed of the subsystem
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
measured by the observable
\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}
.

Additionally, Eq. (
106
) may be applicable for inferring
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}
if
L_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
is known. Although we cannot know
L_{\alpha}(\bm{p})
, we can still evaluate the denominator
\|\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}\|_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p})}^{2}
by considering its upper bound given by the diffusivity of
\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}
. To clarify this notion, we introduce a stochastic change in the value of an arbitrary time-independent observable
\bm{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}|}
over a sufficiently small
\Delta t(>0)
as
\delta\bm{\Phi}\coloneqq[\bm{\Phi}]_{(x(t+\Delta t),y(t+\Delta t))}-[\bm{\Phi}]_{({x}(t),{y}(t))}
, where
x(t)
and
y(t)
denote the states of
X
and
Y
at time
t
, respectively. The diffusivity of
\bm{\Phi}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\bm{\Phi})\coloneqq\lim_{\Delta t\to+0}\frac{\mathrm{Var}[\delta\bm{\Phi}]}{\Delta t},

(107)

where
\mathrm{Var}[\delta\bm{\Phi}]
is the variance of
\delta\bm{\Phi}
.
The upper bound is obtained as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{D}(\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha})}{2}\geq\|\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}}.

(108)

The proof of Eq. (
108
) is provided in Appendix
F
.
The equality holds if and only if
J_{e}^{+}=J_{e}^{-}
for any
e\in\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{all}}_{\alpha}
, where we trivially have
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}=0
. Thus, we expect the values on both sides of Eq. (
108
) to be close to each other near equilibrium.

Finally, combining Eqs. (
105
), and (
108
) with Eq. (
106
) yields the following looser TUR:

\displaystyle\frac{2(d_{t}(\bm{p}^{\top}\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}))^{2}}{\mathcal{D}(\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha})}\leq\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}.

(109)

Although this inequality is looser than Eq. (
106
), it may be more useful for inferring the partial excess EPR. Calculating the lower bound only requires knowledge of how the expected value changes and the diffusivity of the focusing observable. Both of them are experimentally accessible. This result is an information-thermodynamic extension of the TUR for excess EPR in Ref.
Yoshimura et al. 2023
.

Wasserstein geometry for subsystem

We present a formulation of Wasserstein geometry for subsystems in Sec.

IV.5.1
Wasserstein distance for marginal distributions

We here propose a formalization of the
2

(110)

subject to

\displaystyle\Pi_{\alpha}\bm{p}(t)=\bm{p}_{\alpha}(t),

(111)

\displaystyle d_{t}\bm{p}_{\alpha}=\nabla^{\top}_{g_{\alpha}}L_{\alpha}(\bm{p})\bm{f}_{\alpha},

(112)

\displaystyle\bm{p}_{\alpha}(0)=\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(0)},~\bm{p}_{\alpha}(\tau)=\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(1)}.

(113)

We refer to
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\cdot,\cdot)
as the
2
-Wasserstein distance for the subsystem. Note that we can choose the time
\tau>0
arbitrarily
2
2

2

For any
\tau^{\prime}>0

Let us discuss a property of the minimizer of Eq. (

2

Yoshimura et al. 2023

Maas 2011

g_{\alpha}

\alpha
is defined as
\mathop{\mathrm{im}}\nabla_{g_{\alpha}}
. The elements of
\mathop{\mathrm{im}}\nabla_{g_{\alpha}}
are called local conservative forces.
Then, we can demonstrate that the optimal force for the minimization problem in Eq. (
110

\{\bm{f}^{*}_{\alpha}(t)\}_{t\in[0,\tau]}

In other words, there is a potential
\{\bm{\varphi}^{*}_{\alpha}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{A}|}\}_{t\in[0,\tau]}
(

This fact enables us to rewrite the
2

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(0)},\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(1)})

\displaystyle=\min_{\bm{p},\bm{\varphi}_{\alpha}}\left(\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}\|\nabla_{g_{\alpha}}\bm{\varphi}_{\alpha}(t)\|_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}(t))}^{2}dt\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(114)

We emphasize that this definition of the
2

Yoshimura et al. 2023

\bm{p}_{\alpha}(t)

\bm{p}(t)

of the subsystem
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}

Here, we show that

satisfies the axioms of a metric. By definition, we confirm that
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfies the axioms of the non-negativity and the non-degeneracy, i.e.,
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\cdot,\cdot)\geq 0

H

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(0)},\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(2)})\leq\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(0)},\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(1)})+\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(1)},\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(2)}),

\displaystyle v_{\alpha}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}(t))=\min_{\bm{p},\bm{f}_{\alpha}}\left(\|\bm{f}_{\alpha}\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p})}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(0)}=\bm{p}_{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}(t),\bm{p}_{\alpha}(t+\Delta t))

(120)

IV.5.2
Geometric decomposition of partial EPR

We discuss the geometric decomposition of the partial EPR
\dot{\Sigma}_{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
.
We consider a solution to the master equation
\{\bm{p}(t)\}_{t\in[0,\tau]}
(i.e.,
d_{t}\bm{p}=\nabla^{\top}\bm{J}(\bm{p})=\nabla^{\top}L(\bm{p})\bm{F}(\bm{p})
) and the corresponding marginal distribution
\{\bm{p}_{\alpha}(t)=\Pi_{\alpha}\bm{p}(t)\}_{t\in[0,\tau]}
. To perform the geometric decomposition, we focus on the fact that the following linear equation for
\bm{f}_{\alpha}^{\prime}
has a unique solution:

\displaystyle\nabla_{g_{\alpha}}^{\top}\bm{J}_{\alpha}(\bm{p})=\nabla_{g_{\alpha}}^{\top}L_{\alpha}(\bm{p})\bm{f}_{\alpha}^{\prime},

(123)

under the condition
\bm{f}_{\alpha}^{\prime}\in\mathop{\mathrm{im}}\nabla_{g_{\alpha}}
.
We write the solution to this linear equation as
\bm{f}^{\ast}_{\alpha}=-\nabla_{g_{\alpha}}\bm{\psi}_{\alpha}
.
Then we find
\langle\bm{f}^{*}_{\alpha},\bm{F}_{\alpha}-\bm{f}^{*}_{\alpha}\rangle_{L_{\alpha}}=-\langle\nabla_{g_{\alpha}}\bm{\psi}_{\alpha},\bm{F}_{\alpha}+\nabla_{g_{\alpha}}\bm{\psi}_{\alpha}\rangle_{L_{\alpha}}=-(\bm{\psi}_{\alpha})^{\top}\nabla^{\top}_{g_{\alpha}}L_{\alpha}(\bm{F}_{\alpha}+\nabla_{g_{\alpha}}\bm{\psi}_{\alpha})=0
because
\nabla^{\top}_{g_{\alpha}}L_{\alpha}\bm{F}_{\alpha}=\nabla^{\top}_{g_{\alpha}}\bm{J}_{\alpha}=-\nabla^{\top}_{g_{\alpha}}L_{\alpha}\nabla_{g_{\alpha}}\bm{\psi}_{\alpha}
. Thus,
\bm{f}^{*}_{\alpha}
is given by the projection of
\bm{F}_{\alpha}
onto the local conservative subspace
\mathop{\mathrm{im}}\nabla_{g_{\alpha}}
, with respect to the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{L_{\alpha}}
. Inspired by the analogy between this projection and the projection of
\bm{F}
discussed in Sec.
II.3
, we define the local housekeeping EPR and the local excess EPR of the subsystem
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
as
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk,\;loc}}_{\alpha}\coloneqq\|\bm{F}_{\alpha}-\bm{f}^{*}_{\alpha}\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}}
and
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex,\;loc}}_{\alpha}\coloneqq\|\bm{f}^{*}_{\alpha}\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}}
, respectively. Then, the orthogonality leads to the following decomposition:

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}_{\alpha}=\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk,\;loc}}_{\alpha}+\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex,\;loc}}_{\alpha}.

(124)

This equality is verified as follows:
\dot{\Sigma}_{\alpha}=\|\bm{F}_{\alpha}\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}}=\|(\bm{F}_{\alpha}-\bm{f}^{*}_{\alpha})+\bm{f}^{*}_{\alpha}\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}}=\|\bm{F}_{\alpha}-\bm{f}^{*}_{\alpha}\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}}+\|\bm{f}^{*}_{\alpha}\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}}=\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk,\;loc}}_{\alpha}+\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex,\;loc}}_{\alpha}
because
\langle\bm{f}^{*}_{\alpha},\bm{F}_{\alpha}-\bm{f}^{*}_{\alpha}\rangle_{L_{\alpha}}=0
.

We briefly discuss the connection between the local excess EPR
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex,\;loc}}_{\alpha}
and the
2
-Wasserstein distance for the subsystem. Specifically, the speed
v_{\alpha}
provides a lower bound of
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex,\;loc}}_{\alpha}
as

\displaystyle v_{\alpha}^{2}\leq\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex,\;loc}}_{\alpha}.

(125)

We note that while this result corresponds to an equality relationship between the excess EPR and the 2-Wasserstein distance in the total system
Yoshimura et al. 2023
, this becomes an inequality when focusing on the subsystem.
This inequality is shown as follows. The pair
\{\bm{p}(t),\bm{f}^{*}_{\alpha}\}
is a candidate of the minimization problem [Eq. (
117
)] because the pair satisfies
\Pi_{\alpha}\bm{p}(t)=\bm{p}_{\alpha}(t)
and
d_{t}\bm{p}_{\alpha}(t)=\nabla^{\top}_{g_{\alpha}}L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}(t))\bm{f}^{\ast}_{\alpha}
. Thus, we obtain
v_{\alpha}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}(t))\leq\|\bm{f}^{\ast}_{\alpha}\|_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}(t))}=\sqrt{\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex,\;loc}}_{\alpha}}
, whose square yields Eq. (
125
). This inequality arises because
\bm{p}(t)
is not necessarily equivalent to the optimal distribution for Eq. (
118
). From Eq. (
122
), we can see that equality holds when the dynamics are given by the geodesic in the space of the
2
-Wasserstein distance for the subsystem.

The local excess and housekeeping EPRs are different from the partial housekeeping and excess EPRs. For example, we find
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk,\;loc}}_{X}+\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk,\;loc}}_{Y}\neq\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}
and
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex,\;loc}}_{X}+\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex,\;loc}}_{Y}\neq\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}
in general, while Eqs. (
94
) and (
95
) hold. This difference arises from the order in which the EPRs are decomposed geometrically. The partial housekeeping and excess EPRs
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}
and
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}_{\alpha}
are obtained by decomposing
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}
and
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}
into contributions from the subsystem
\alpha
, where the housekeeping and excess EPRs
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk}}
and
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}
are obtained by decomposing the total system’s EPR
\dot{\Sigma}
based on the conservativeness described by
\nabla
.
On the other hand,
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{hk,\;loc}}_{\alpha}
and
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex,\;loc}}_{\alpha}
are obtained by decomposing the partial EPR
\dot{\Sigma}_{\alpha}
based on the local conservativeness described by
\nabla_{g_{\alpha}}
.

Although the local and partial excess EPRs,
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex,\;loc}}_{\alpha}
and
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}
, are fundamentally different quantities, they are linked by the following inequality (see Appendix
K
for the proof):

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex,\;loc}}_{\alpha}\leq\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}.

(126)

We can intuitively understand the inequality in the following way.
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex,\;loc}}_{\alpha}
is the minimum dissipation required to change (at least) the subsystem
\alpha
, while
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}
is the contribution of subsystem
\alpha
to the minimum dissipation required to change the total system. Therefore,
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex,\;loc}}_{\alpha}
is never greater than
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}
. We can also obtain the hierarchy
v_{\alpha}^{2}\leq\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex,\;loc}}_{\alpha}\leq\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}
by combining Eqs. (
125
) and (
126
).

IV.5.3
Speed limit

We now introduce the thermodynamic speed limit. We consider a solution to the master equation and the corresponding marginal distribution:
\{\bm{p}(t)\}_{t\in[0,\tau]}
and
\{\bm{p}_{\alpha}(t)\}_{[t\in[0,\tau]}=\{\Pi_{\alpha}\bm{p}(t)\}_{t\in[0,\tau]}
for
\alpha\in\{X,Y\}
. We write the initial and final distributions as
\bm{p}^{(0)}=\bm{p}(0)
,
\bm{p}^{(1)}=\bm{p}(\tau)
,
\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(0)}=\bm{p}_{\alpha}(0)
, and
\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(1)}=\bm{p}_{\alpha}(\tau)
.

First, the triangle inequality yields
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}(0),\bm{p}_{\alpha}(\tau))\leq\int_{0}^{\tau}v_{\alpha}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}(t))dt
.
From the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}(0),\bm{p}_{\alpha}(\tau))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}v_{\alpha}dt\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}v_{\alpha}^{2}dt

\displaystyle\leq\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex,\;loc}}_{\alpha}dt

\displaystyle\leq\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}dt,

(127)

where we used Eq. (
125
) and Eq. (
126
). We here introduce the quantities
\tilde{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}dt
and
\Delta_{\alpha}I_{XY}(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\tau}\dot{I}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}dt
, which satisfy

\tilde{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\tau)-\Delta_{\alpha}I_{XY}(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\tau}\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}dt=:\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\tau)
.
Therefore, Eq. (
127
) is rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}(0),\bm{p}_{\alpha}(\tau))^{2}}{\tau}\leq\tilde{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\tau)-\Delta_{\alpha}I_{XY}(\tau),

(128)

which is called information-thermodynamic speed limit.

This inequality can be understood in three different contexts.
Firstly, Eq. (
128
) corresponds to the thermodynamic speed limit [Eq. (
43
)] in the information thermodynamic scheme for bipartite systems. The apparent difference between these two inequalities is that the right-hand side of the former comprises both an entropic quantity (
\tilde{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}
) and an informational quantity (
\Delta_{\alpha}I_{XY}
). Secondly, Eq. (
128
) provides a tighter bound than the generalization of the second law of information thermodynamics Eq. (
89
), which can be rewritten as
\tilde{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(\tau)\geq\Delta_{\alpha}I_{XY}(\tau)
for a finite time. Thirdly, Eq. (
128
) corresponds to the information-thermodynamic speed limit, which is originally proposed by Nakazato and Ito for overdamped Langevin systems
Nakazato and Ito 2021
.

We briefly characterize the quantity
\Delta_{\alpha}I_{XY}(\tau)
. This quantity
\Delta_{\alpha}I_{XY}(\tau)
implies the change in mutual information due to subsystem
\alpha
. Specifically, according to Eq. (
78
), we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{X}I_{XY}(\tau)+\Delta_{Y}I_{XY}(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\tau}d_{t}I(\hat{X}_{t};\hat{Y}_{t})dt

\displaystyle=I(\hat{X}_{\tau};\hat{Y}_{\tau})-I(\hat{X}_{0};\hat{Y}_{0}).

(129)

We note that
\Delta_{\alpha}I_{XY}(\tau)
does not diverge for stationary systems in the limit
\lim_{\tau\to\infty}|\Delta_{\alpha}I_{XY}(\tau)|<\infty
, even when the detailed balance condition is violated and
\dot{I}_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{st}})\neq 0
is satisfied. On the other hand, the integral of
\dot{I}_{\alpha}
, defined as
\tilde{\Delta}_{\alpha}I_{XY}(\tau)\coloneqq\int^{\tau}_{0}\dot{I}_{\alpha}dt
, formally satisfies the relation equivalent to Eq. (
129
):
\tilde{\Delta}_{X}I_{XY}(\tau)+\tilde{\Delta}_{Y}I_{XY}(\tau)=I(\hat{X}_{\tau};\hat{Y}_{\tau})-I(\hat{X}_{0};\hat{Y}_{0})
. However,
\tilde{\Delta}_{\alpha}I_{XY}(\tau)
may diverge in the limit
\lim_{\tau\to\infty}|\tilde{\Delta}_{\alpha}I_{XY}(\tau)|=\infty
since the housekeeping contribution of the information flow exists. Therefore, the quantity
\Delta_{\alpha}I_{XY}(\tau)
has a physically sound property in this sense.

Finally, we derive a tighter inequality by defining the thermodynamic length as
l_{\alpha}(t)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}v_{\alpha}dt
. From Eq. (
127
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{l^{2}_{\alpha}(t)}{t}\leq\tilde{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha}(t)-\Delta_{\alpha}I_{XY}(t)

(130)

for
t\in[0,\tau]
.
While
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}(0),\bm{p}_{\alpha}(\tau))
corresponds to the geodesic,
l_{\alpha}
quantifies the length of the path
\{\bm{p}_{\alpha}(t)\}_{t\in[0,\tau]}
. Therefore, unlike the 2-Wasserstein distance for subsystems, this quantity
l_{\alpha}(t)
requires information not only about the initial and final states
\bm{p}_{\alpha}(0)
and
\bm{p}_{\alpha}(\tau)
, but also about the path
\{\bm{p}_{\alpha}(t)\}_{t\in[0,\tau]}
.

VI
Discussion

In this paper, we propose a geometric housekeeping-excess decomposition of information flow for bipartite systems described by Markov jump processes. Our decomposition is based on the orthogonal decomposition of a thermodynamic force into its conservative and non-conservative components. The housekeeping information flow, driven by the non-conservative force, satisfies the antisymmetric relation [Eq. (
77
)]. Consequently, it does not contribute to the time derivative of mutual information between the two subsystems.
Conversely, the excess information flow, driven by the conservative force, substantially changes the mutual information (see Eq. (
78
)).

Our decomposition yields four main consequences. The first is the generalization of the second law of information thermodynamics. We derived the two inequalities, Eqs. (
88
) and (
89
), by combining the housekeeping/excess entropy change rates with the housekeeping/excess information flows. These two inequalities are formally equivalent to Eq. (
66
). The second consequence is the generalization of cyclic decomposition. By extending partial cycle affinity to non-steady states, we decomposed partial housekeeping EPR into contributions related to each cycle [Eq. (
98
)]. In Appendix
D
, we also demonstrate that this decomposition can be described by cycle affinities and cyclic currents for cycles within the contracted graph (i.e., contracted cycles). Furthermore, by formally defining the information affinity for non-steady states, we demonstrate that the housekeeping information flow can be expressed solely in terms of contributions from global cycles [Eq. (
100
)]. The third consequence is the short-time TUR [Eq. (
106
)]. Although Eq. (
109
) is weaker than Eq. (
106
), it enables us to obtain a lower bound for partial excess EPR using the only quantities that are more easily accessible: the rate of change of the expected value and the diffusivity of an observable. Thus, Eq. (
109
) may be useful for inferring the partial excess EPR. The fourth is the information-thermodynamic speed limit [Eq. (
128
)], which is based on optimal transport theory. We also proposed a geometric decomposition of partial EPR and discussed its connection to the Wasserstein distance for subsystems.

We discuss the application of optimal transport theory to information thermodynamics. This theory is employed to analyze the optimality of information processing in information thermodynamicss
Nakazato and Ito 2021
;
Fujimoto and Ito 2024
;
Nagase and Sagawa 2024
;
Kamijima et al. 2025a
;
Kamijima et al. 2025b
and to derive the finite-time Landauer principle
Nakazato and Ito 2021
;
Van Vu and Saito 2023a
. While the
2
-Wasserstein distance is typically employed in information thermodynamics for Langevin systems
Nakazato and Ito 2021
;
Kamijima et al. 2025a
, the 1-Wasserstein distance is mainly used for one of Markov jump processes
Kamijima et al. 2025b
;
Nagase and Sagawa 2024
;
Nagase and Sagawa 2025
. Although this use of the 1-Wasserstein distance simplifies the analysis, it has some drawbacks. The 1-Wasserstein distance does not clearly relate to housekeeping and excess EPRs, and it requires the treatment of activity, which is a kinetic quantity. Our extension of the
2
-Wasserstein distance to the subsystems of Markov jump processes will enable us to derive thermodynamic trade-off relations and analyze the optimality of the information heat engine as in the Langevin systems. It also naturally connects to the housekeeping-excess decomposition in information flow. It would be interesting to compare our generalization of the
2
-Wasserstein distance with a variant of the 1-Wasserstein distance that focuses on subsystems
Kamijima et al. 2025b
;
Nagase and Sagawa 2024
;
Nagase and Sagawa 2025
.

We note that there may be alternative approaches to decomposing information flow. In this study, we decompose information flow using the geometric decomposition of the EPR, which is based on our definition of the inner product with the edgewise Onsager coefficients. This method is directly related to optimal transport theory and enables us to derive thermodynamic trade-off relations. For example, there are other options for the housekeeping/excess decomposition of the EPR in Markov jump processes: the traditional Hatano–Sasa decomposition
Hatano and Sasa 2001
;
Esposito and Van den Broeck 2010
, the decomposition based on Hessian geometry induced by the Legendre duality between force and current
Kobayashi et al. 2022
, and the decomposition based on information geometry of unidirectional fluxes
Kolchinsky et al. 2024
. Although it is unclear how to decompose information flow using these options, it may also be possible to do so based on these other options. Even if the decomposition succeeds, it is unlikely that alternative approaches will possess natural properties, such as Eqs. (
77
) and (
78
), in the decomposition of our information flow. However, since information-geometric decomposition has advantages when decomposing the total EPR into partial EPRs in non-bipartite systems
Ito et al. 2020
, it may have other desirable properties when an information-geometric decomposition is successful.

Finally, we discuss future perspectives. Information thermodynamics is studied in settings broader than just bipartite systems described by Markov jump processes. These settings include non-bipartite systems
Ito and Sagawa 2013
;
Ito 2016
;
Chetrite et al. 2019
;
Wolpert 2020
, overdamped Langevin systems
Allahverdyan et al. 2009
;
Sagawa and Ueda 2012b
;
Ito and Sagawa 2013
;
Horowitz and Sandberg 2014
;
Ito and Sagawa 2015
;
Nakazato and Ito 2021
, underdamped Langevin systems
Ito and Sano 2011
;
Horowitz and Sandberg 2014
;
Kumasaki et al. 2025
;
Dechant et al. 2025
, deterministic chemical reaction networks
Amano et al. 2022
;
Penocchio et al. 2022
, and open quantum systems
Sagawa and Ueda 2008
;
Ptaszyński and Esposito 2019
;
Yada et al. 2022
. The geometric housekeeping/excess decomposition of the EPR is possible in these systems
Yoshimura et al. 2023
;
Dechant et al. 2022a
;
Dechant et al. 2022b
;
Yoshimura et al. 2025
. Thus, the information exchange in these systems could also be decomposed into housekeeping and excess parts. In linear Langevin systems, the housekeeping EPR can also be decomposed into each oscillatory mode
Sekizawa et al. 2024
. Therefore, we may be able to decompose the housekeeping information flow into oscillatory modes. This notion is analogous to decomposing the housekeeping information flow in the cycle basis in this study. It would also be interesting to clarify how the housekeeping and the excess information flows behave in concrete systems, such as the macroscopic limit of electric circuits
Freitas and Esposito 2022
;
Freitas and Esposito 2023
, information-driven heat engines
Leighton et al. 2024
, and biological processes such as membrane transport
Yoshida et al. 2022
.

Appendix A
Derivation of the explicit form of information flow

We derive the explicit form of information flow given by Eq. (
58
). Due to the symmetry of information flow, it is sufficient to consider
\dot{I}_{X}
.
Here, we write the probability of the transition from
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
to
(x,y)
occurring within the time interval
[t,t+dt]
as
P(x,y;t+dt|x^{\prime},y^{\prime};t)
. As
X
and
Y
cannot change simultaneously in a bipartite system,
P(x,y;t+dt|x^{\prime},y^{\prime};t)=o(dt)
holds if
x\neq x^{\prime}
and
y\neq y^{\prime}
, where
dt
is an infinitesimal time interval and
o(dt)
is the Landau symbol.

The joint probability
P(x;t+dt,y;t)
, which is needed to compute the mutual information
I(\hat{X}_{t+dt};\hat{Y}_{t})
, is given with the transition probability as

\displaystyle P(x;t+dt,y;t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}P(x,y^{\prime};t+dt|x^{\prime},y;t)p(x^{\prime},y;t).

(134)

Because

\displaystyle\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}P(x,y^{\prime};t+dt|x^{\prime},y;t)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}P(x,y;t+dt|x^{\prime},y;t)+o(dt)&(x\neq x^{\prime})\\
\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\cal Y}P(x,y^{\prime};t+dt|x,y;t)&(x=x^{\prime}),\end{cases}

(135)

it can be further rewritten as

\displaystyle P(x;t+dt,y;t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\prime}(\neq x)}P(x,y;t+dt|x^{\prime},y;t)p(x^{\prime},y;t)

\displaystyle+\sum_{y^{\prime}}P(x,y^{\prime};t+dt|x,y;t)p(x,y;t)+o(dt).

(136)

In the short-time limit, the transition probability is given by the transition rates as

\displaystyle P(x,y^{\prime};t+dt|x^{\prime},y;t)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}R(x,y|x^{\prime},y)\delta_{y,y^{\prime}}dt+o(dt)&(x\neq x^{\prime})\\
R(x,y^{\prime}|x,y)\delta_{x,x^{\prime}}dt+o(dt)&(y\neq y^{\prime})\\
1-\sum_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\Delta_{x,y}}R(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}|x,y)dt+o(dt)&(\text{otherwise}),\end{cases}

(137)

where
R(\cdot|\cdot)=\sum_{\nu}R^{(\nu)}(\cdot|\cdot)
and we defined

\displaystyle\Delta_{x,y}=\{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\mid(x^{\prime}=x\,\&\,y^{\prime}\neq y)\;\text{or}\;(x^{\prime}\neq x\,\&\,y^{\prime}=y)\}.

(138)

Therefore, we find

\displaystyle P(x;t+dt,y;t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\prime}(\neq x)}R(x,y|x^{\prime},y)p(x^{\prime},y;t)dt

\displaystyle+\sum_{y^{\prime}(\neq y)}R(x,y^{\prime}|x,y)p(x,y;t)dt

\displaystyle+p(x,y;t)-\sum_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\Delta_{x,y}}R(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}|x,y)p(x,y)dt+o(dt).

(139)

\displaystyle=p(x,y;t)+\sum_{x^{\prime}(\neq x)}R(x,y|x^{\prime},y)p(x^{\prime},y;t)dt

\displaystyle-\sum_{x^{\prime}(\neq x)}R(x^{\prime},y|x,y)p(x,y)dt+o(dt).

(140)

For fixed
y
, each choice of
x^{\prime}(\neq x)
and
\nu
corresponds to an element in
\mathcal{E}_{X}
as
e=(\nu,(x,y)\to(x^{\prime},y))
or
(\nu,(x^{\prime},y)\to(x,y))
. Thus, we can easily see

\displaystyle\sum_{x^{\prime}(\neq x)}\big(R(x,y|x^{\prime},y)p(x^{\prime},y;t)-R(x^{\prime},y|x,y)p(x,y)\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{X}}(\delta_{(x,y),\mathrm{t}(e)}-\delta_{(x,y),\mathrm{s}(e)})J_{e}(\bm{p}(t)).

(141)

Because the coefficient on the right-hand side coincides with the incidence matrix as

[\nabla^{\top}_{G_{X}}]_{(x,y),e}=\delta_{(x,y),\mathrm{t}(e)}-\delta_{(x,y),\mathrm{s}(e)}
, we obtain

\displaystyle P(x;t+dt,y;t)=p(x,y;t)+[\nabla^{\top}_{G_{X}}\bm{J}_{X}]_{(x,y)}dt+o(dt).

(142)

By multiplying by
\Pi_{X}
, we can also find

\displaystyle p_{X}(x;t+dt)=p_{X}(x;t)+[\Pi_{X}\nabla^{\top}_{G_{X}}\bm{J}_{X}]_{x}dt+o(dt)

(143)

because
[\Pi_{X}]_{x^{\prime},(x,y)}=\delta_{x^{\prime},x}
and
\sum_{y}P(x;t+dt,y;t)=p(x,t+dt)
.

We derive Eq. (
58
) from Eqs. (
142
) and (
143
).
Recall that the mutual information
I(\hat{X}_{t+dt};\hat{Y}_{t})
is defined as

\displaystyle I(\hat{X}_{t+dt};\hat{Y}_{t})

\displaystyle=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bm{P}(t+dt,t)\|\bm{p}_{X}\otimes\bm{p}_{Y}(t+dt,t))

(144)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}P(x;t+dt,y;t)\ln\frac{P(x;t+dt,y;t)}{p_{X}(x;t+dt)p_{Y}(y;t)}.

(145)

Through straightforward computation, those relations lead to

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{P(x;t+dt,y;t)}{p_{X}(x;t+dt)p_{Y}(y;t)}\right)=\ln\left(\frac{p(x,y;t)}{p_{X}(x;t)p_{Y}(y;t)}\right)

\displaystyle+o(dt).

for the conditional probability of

given

for sufficiently small

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(z^{\prime};t+\Delta t,z;t)=P(z^{\prime};t+\Delta t|z;t)p(z;t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu}R^{(\nu)}(z^{\prime}|z)p(z;t)\Delta t+o(\Delta t)

\displaystyle=\Delta t\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{all}}}J^{+}_{e}(\bm{p}(t))\delta_{z^{\prime},\mathrm{t}(e)}\delta_{z,\mathrm{s}(e)}+o(\Delta t).

(167)

To obtain the last equality, we used the fact that for

R^{(\nu)}(z^{\prime}|z)>0\iff(\nu,z\to z^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{all}}
. Therefore, the ensemble average of

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\delta\bm{\Phi}]=\sum_{(z,z^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}}([\bm{\Phi}]_{z^{\prime}}-[\bm{\Phi}]_{z})\mathbb{P}(z^{\prime};t+\Delta t,z;t)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\Delta t\sum_{(z,z^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}}\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{all}}}([\bm{\Phi}]_{z^{\prime}}-[\bm{\Phi}]_{z})J^{+}_{e}(\bm{p}(t))\delta_{z^{\prime},\mathrm{t}(e)}\delta_{z,\mathrm{s}(e)}\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+o(\Delta t)\end{split}

\displaystyle=\Delta t\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{all}}}J^{+}_{e}(\bm{p}(t))([\bm{\Phi}]_{\mathrm{t}(e)}-[\bm{\Phi}]_{\mathrm{s}(e)})+o(\Delta t).

(168)

In the same way, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\delta\bm{\Phi})^{2}]=\sum_{(z,z^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}}([\bm{\Phi}]_{z^{\prime}}-[\bm{\Phi}]_{z})^{2}\mathbb{P}(z^{\prime};t+\Delta t,z;t)

(169)

\displaystyle=\Delta t\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{all}}}J^{+}_{e}([\bm{\Phi}]_{\mathrm{t}(e)}-[\bm{\Phi}]_{\mathrm{s}(e)})^{2}+o(\Delta t)

\displaystyle=\Delta t\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\sum_{e^{\prime}\in\{e,-e\}}J^{+}_{e^{\prime}}([\bm{\Phi}]_{\mathrm{t}(e^{\prime})}-[\bm{\Phi}]_{\mathrm{s}(e^{\prime})})^{2}+o(\Delta t)

\displaystyle=\Delta t\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}(J^{+}_{e}+J^{-}_{e})([\bm{\Phi}]_{\mathrm{t}(e)}-[\bm{\Phi}]_{\mathrm{s}(e)})^{2}+o(\Delta t).

(170)

Here, we used
J^{+}_{-e}=J^{-}_{e}
and
\mathrm{t}(-e)=\mathrm{s}(e)
to obtain the last equality. Combining the above calculations with Eq. (
166
) yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[\delta\bm{\Phi}]

\displaystyle=\Delta t\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}(J^{+}_{e}+J^{-}_{e})([\bm{\Phi}]_{\mathrm{t}(e)}-[\bm{\Phi}]_{\mathrm{s}(e)})^{2}+o(\Delta t),

(171)

which leads to the following formula:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\bm{\Phi})

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Delta t\to+0}\frac{\mathrm{Var}[\delta\bm{\Phi}]}{\Delta t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}(J^{+}_{e}+J^{-}_{e})([\bm{\Phi}]_{\mathrm{t}(e)}-[\bm{\Phi}]_{\mathrm{s}(e)})^{2}.

(172)

We now prove Eq. (
108
) by applying Eq. (
172
) to
\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}=\Pi^{\top}_{\alpha}\bm{\psi}_{\alpha}
. To this end, we focus on the fact that for
e\in\mathcal{E}_{{\alpha}^{\rm c}}
,
[\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}]_{\mathrm{t}(e)}-[\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}]_{\mathrm{s}(e)}=0
. It can be confirmed by
[\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}]_{\mathrm{t}(e)}=[\bm{\psi}_{\alpha}]_{\mathrm{t}_{\alpha}(e)}
,
[\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}]_{\mathrm{s}(e)}=[\bm{\psi}_{\alpha}]_{\mathrm{s}_{\alpha}(e)}
, and
\mathrm{t}_{\alpha}(e)=\mathrm{s}_{\alpha}(e)
for
e\in\mathcal{E}_{{\alpha}^{\rm c}}
. Therefore, we can rewrite
\mathcal{D}(\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha})
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}(J^{+}_{e}+J^{-}_{e})([\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}]_{\mathrm{t}(e)}-[\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}]_{\mathrm{s}(e)})^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}}(J^{+}_{e}+J^{-}_{e})([\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}]_{\mathrm{t}(e)}-[\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}]_{\mathrm{s}(e)})^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}}(J^{+}_{e}+J^{-}_{e})[\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}]^{2}_{e}.

(173)

Here, we used
[\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}]_{e}=[\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}]_{\mathrm{t}(e)}-[\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}]_{\mathrm{s}(e)}
to obtain the last equality. Finally, by using the inequality
J^{+}_{e}+J^{-}_{e}\geq 2(J^{+}_{e}-J^{-}_{e})/(\ln J^{+}_{e}-\ln J^{-}_{e})=2l_{e}
, we arrive at

\displaystyle 2\|\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{(e,e^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\times\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}}[\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}]_{e}[L_{\alpha}]_{e,e^{\prime}}[\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}]_{e^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{(e,e^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\times\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}}[\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}]_{e}l_{e}\delta_{e,e^{\prime}}[\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}]_{e^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}}2l_{e}[\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}]^{2}_{e}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}}(J^{+}_{e}+J^{-}_{e})[\nabla_{G_{\alpha}}\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}]^{2}_{e}=\mathcal{D}(\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha}).

(174)

Appendix G
The conservativeness of the minimizer

We prove the formula (
114
), which implies the conservativeness of the minimizer
\bm{f}^{*}_{\alpha}(t)=-\nabla_{g_{\alpha}}\bm{\varphi}^{*}_{\alpha}(t)

&\quad\quad+(\bm{\varphi}_{\alpha}(t))^{\top}[d_{t}\Pi_{\alpha}\bm{p}(t)+\nabla^{\top}_{g_{\alpha}}L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}(t))\bm{f}_{\alpha}(t)]\biggr)dt\end{split}.

(175)

The Lagrange multiplier method corresponding to Eq. (
110

111

112

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\tau}\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(0)},\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(1)})^{2}=\min_{\bm{p},\bm{f}_{\alpha}}\max_{\bm{\varphi}_{\alpha}}\mathbb{I}[\bm{p},\bm{f}_{\alpha},\bm{\varphi}_{\alpha}],

(176)

subject to

The functional derivative of

.
Therefore, we find that the optimal force

\displaystyle\bm{f}^{*}_{\alpha}(t)=-\nabla_{g_{\alpha}}\bm{\varphi}^{*}_{\alpha}(t).

(179)

Appendix H
Proof of the symmetry

We prove the symmetry
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(0)},\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(1)})=\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(1)},\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(0)})
.
Let
\{(\bm{p}^{*}(t),\bm{f}^{*}_{\alpha}(t))\}_{t\in[0,\tau]}
be the minimizer of Eq. (
110
). Then, the time-reversal pair
\{(\bm{p}^{\dagger}(t),\bm{f}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(t))\}_{t\in[0,\tau]}\coloneqq\{(\bm{p}^{*}(\tau-t),-\bm{f}_{\alpha}^{*}(\tau-t))\}_{t\in[0,\tau]}
satisfies conditions Eqs. (
111
), (
112
), and (
113
) for the optimization problem [Eq. (
110
)], where
\bm{p}^{(0)}
and
\bm{p}^{(1)}
are exchanged. Therefore, the following inequality

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\|\bm{f}^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(t)\|_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{\dagger}(t))}^{2}dt\right)^{1/2}\geq\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(1)},\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(0)}),

(180)

holds. Combining

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(0)},\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(1)})

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\|\bm{f}^{*}_{\alpha}(t)\|_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{*}(t))}^{2}dt\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\|\bm{f}^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(t)\|_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{\dagger}(t))}^{2}dt\right)^{1/2},

(181)

with the inequality Eq. (
180
) yields

\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(0)},\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(1)})=\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(1)},\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(0)})
.

Appendix I
Proof of the triangle inequality

Here, we prove the triangle inequality of the
2
-Wasserstein distance for the subsystem. Since a geodesic travels at a constant speed, i.e.,
\|\bm{f}_{\alpha}(t)\|_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}(t))}={\rm const.}
(see also Appendix
J
), the definition of the
2
-Wasserstein distance for the subsystem can be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(0)},\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(1)})

\displaystyle=\min_{\bm{p},\bm{f}_{\alpha}}\left(\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}\|\bm{f}_{\alpha}(t)\|_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}(t))}^{2}dt\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\min_{\bm{p},\bm{f}_{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{\tau}\|\bm{f}_{\alpha}(t)\|_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}(t))}dt,

(182)

under the constraints [Eqs. (
111
), (
112
), and (
113
)].
This definition has the same minimizer as the one in Eq. (
110
). For simplicity, we set
\tau=1
. Let
(\bm{p}^{(10)},\bm{f}_{\alpha}^{(10)})
and
(\bm{p}^{(21)},\bm{f}_{\alpha}^{(21)})
be the minimizers that give
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(0)},\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(1)})
and
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(1)},\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(2)})
, respectively. Then, we define the pair
(\bm{p}^{\prime},\bm{f}^{\prime}_{\alpha})
as

\displaystyle\bm{p}^{\prime}(t)=\begin{cases}\bm{p}^{(10)}(2t)&\text{if $t\in[0,1/2]$},\\
\bm{p}^{(21)}(2t-1)&\text{if $t\in(1/2,1]$},\end{cases}

(183)

\displaystyle\bm{f}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(t)=\begin{cases}\bm{f}_{\alpha}^{(10)}(2t)&\text{if $t\in[0,1/2]$},\\
\bm{f}_{\alpha}^{(21)}(2t-1)&\text{if $t\in(1/2,1]$}.\end{cases}

(184)

Note that the pair
(\bm{p}^{\prime},\bm{f}^{\prime}_{\alpha})
satisfies the constraints, Eq. (
112
),
\Pi_{\alpha}\bm{p}^{\prime}(0)=\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(0)}
and
\Pi_{\alpha}\bm{p}^{\prime}(1)=\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(2)}
. Therefore, we can derive the triangle inequality as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(0)},\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(2)})\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|\bm{f}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(t)\|_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{\prime}(t))}dt

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\int_{0}^{1/2}\|\bm{f}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(2t)\|_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{\prime}(2t))}dt\\
&\quad+\int_{1/2}^{1}\|\bm{f}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(2t-1)\|_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{\prime}(2t-1))}dt\end{split}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(0)},\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(1)})+\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(1)},\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(2)}),

(185)

where we used
\int_{0}^{1/2}\|\bm{f}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(2t)\|_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{\prime}(2t))}dt=\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(0)},\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(1)})

.

Appendix J
The constant speed property of a geodesic

We here show that a geodesic has a constant speed. Firstly, using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}\|\bm{f}_{\alpha(t)}\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}(t))}dt\geq\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\|\bm{f}_{\alpha}(t)\|_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}(t))}dt\right)^{2},

(186)

for any pair
(\bm{p},\bm{f}_{\alpha})
. Secondly, as we will show later, the minimizer
(\bm{p}^{*},\bm{f}^{*}_{\alpha}=-\nabla_{g_{\alpha}}\bm{\varphi}^{*}_{\alpha})
of Eq. (
110
) satisfies the following inverse inequality:

\displaystyle\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}\|\bm{f}^{*}_{\alpha}(t)\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{*}(t))}dt\leq\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\|\bm{f}^{*}_{\alpha}(t)\|_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{*}(t))}dt\right)^{2}.

(187)

Combining Eqs. (
186
) and (
187
) yields

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\|\bm{f}^{*}_{\alpha}(t)\|_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{*}(t))}dt\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}\|\bm{f}^{*}_{\alpha}(t)\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{*}(t))}dt

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(0)},\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(1)})^{2}.

(188)

Since the equality in the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality holds if and only if
\|\bm{f}^{*}_{\alpha}(t)\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}(t))}=\mathrm{const.}
, we conclude that the geodesic has constant speed and satisfies

\displaystyle\|\bm{f}^{*}_{\alpha}(t)\|_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{*}(t))}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(0)},\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(1)})}{\tau}.

(189)

To complete the proof, we next show Eq. (
187
). Let
(\bm{p}^{\prime},\bm{f}^{\prime}_{\alpha})
be a pair satisfying the constraints [Eqs. (
111
), (
112
), and (
113
)]. We define the pan-functional of the pair
(\bm{p}^{\prime},\bm{f}^{\prime}_{\alpha})
parametrized by
\eta>0
as

\displaystyle\tilde{s}_{\eta}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\left(\eta+\|\bm{f}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(t^{\prime})\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{\prime}(t^{\prime}))}\right)^{1/2}dt^{\prime}.

(190)

Since
d_{t}\tilde{s}_{\eta}(t)>0
, the inverse function
\tilde{t}_{\eta}\coloneqq\tilde{s}_{\eta}^{-1}
is well defined. Using
\tilde{t}_{\eta}
, we introduce the following quantities,

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{p}}(s)=\bm{p}^{\prime}(\tilde{t}_{\eta}(s)),~\tilde{\bm{p}}_{\alpha}(s)=\Pi_{\alpha}\tilde{\bm{p}}(s),

(191)

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{f}}_{\alpha}(s)=(d_{s}\tilde{t}_{\eta}(s))\bm{f}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\tilde{t}_{\eta}(s)).

(192)

The pair
(\tilde{\bm{p}},\tilde{\bm{f}}_{\alpha})
also satisfies the constraints [Eqs. (
111
), (
112
), and (
113
)] on the time interval
s\in[0,\tilde{s}_{\eta}(\tau)]
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{p}}_{\alpha}(0)=\Pi_{\alpha}\bm{p}^{\prime}(0)=\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(0)},\;\tilde{\bm{p}}_{\alpha}(\tilde{s}_{\eta}(\tau))=\Pi_{\alpha}\bm{p}^{\prime}(\tau)=\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(1)},

(193)

and

\displaystyle d_{s}\tilde{\bm{p}}_{\alpha}(s)

\displaystyle=\left.(d_{s}\tilde{t}_{\eta}(s))d_{t}\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)\right|_{t=\tilde{t}_{\eta}(s)}

\displaystyle=(d_{s}\tilde{t}_{\eta}(s))\left.[\nabla^{\top}_{g_{\alpha}}L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{\prime}(t))\bm{f}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(t)]\right|_{t=\tilde{t}_{\eta}(s)}

\displaystyle=\nabla^{\top}_{g_{\alpha}}L_{\alpha}(\tilde{\bm{p}}(s))\tilde{\bm{f}}_{\alpha}(s).

(194)

Therefore, we obtain the following inequality:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(0)},\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(1)})^{2}\leq\tilde{s}_{\eta}(\tau)\int_{0}^{\tilde{s}_{\eta}(\tau)}\|\tilde{\bm{f}}_{\alpha}(s)\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}(\tilde{\bm{p}}(s))}ds

\displaystyle=\tilde{s}_{\eta}(\tau)\int_{0}^{\tau}(\left.d_{s}\tilde{t}_{\eta}(s)\right|_{s=\tilde{s}_{\eta}(t)})^{2}\|\bm{f}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(t)\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{\prime}(t))}d_{t}\tilde{s}_{\eta}(t)dt

\displaystyle=\tilde{s}_{\eta}(\tau)\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{\|\bm{f}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(t)\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{\prime}(t))}}{\left(\eta+\|\bm{f}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(t)\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{\prime}(t))}\right)^{1/2}}dt\leq\tilde{s}_{\eta}(\tau)^{2},

(195)

where we performed the variable transformation
t=\tilde{t}_{\eta}(s)
and used the following relation,

\displaystyle\left.d_{s}\tilde{t}_{\eta}(s)\right|_{s=\tilde{s}_{\eta}(t)}

\displaystyle=\left(d_{t}\tilde{s}_{\eta}(t)\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\eta+\|\bm{f}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(t)\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{*}(t))}\right)^{-1/2}.

(196)

Taking the limit
\eta\to 0
yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(0)},\bm{p}_{\alpha}^{(1)})\leq\int_{0}^{\tau}\|\bm{f}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(t)\|_{L_{\alpha}(\bm{p}^{\prime}(t))}dt,

(197)

which is an equivalent restatement of Eq. (
187
).

Appendix K
Proof of Eq. (
126

We prove Eq. (

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ex,\;loc}}_{\alpha}=\min_{\tilde{\bm{f}}_{\alpha}}\|\bm{F}_{\alpha}-\tilde{\bm{f}}_{\alpha}\|^{2}_{L_{\alpha}},

(198)
