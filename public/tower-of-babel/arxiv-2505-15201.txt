Pass@K Policy Optimization: Solving Harder Reinforcement Learning Problems
Christian Walder & Deep Karkhanis

Abstract
Reinforcement Learning algorithms commonly sample multiple ( n > 1 n>1 ) solution attempts for each problem and reward them independently. This optimizes for pass ​ @ ​ 1 \mathrm{pass@1} performance and prioritizes the strength of isolated samples at the expense of the diversity and collective utility of sets of samples. Such algorithms under-utilize the sampling capacity, limiting exploration and eventual improvement on harder examples. As a fix, we propose Pass-at- k k Policy Optimization (PKPO), a transformation on the final rewards which leads to direct optimization of pass ​ @ ​ k \mathrm{pass@k} performance, thus optimizing for sets of samples that feature a large maximum reward when considered jointly. Our primary contribution is to derive novel low variance unbiased estimators for the pass ​ @ ​ k \mathrm{pass@k} and its gradient, in both the binary and continuous reward settings. We show that the optimization with these estimators reduces to standard reinforcement learning with (mini-batches of) rewards that have been jointly transformed by a function that is stable and efficient to compute. While previous efforts propose transformations for k = n k=n , our transformations are the first to enable robust optimization of the pass ​ @ ​ k \mathrm{pass@k} for any arbitrary k ≤ n k\leq n . Rather than simply trading off pass ​ @ ​ 1 \mathrm{pass@1} performance for pass ​ @ ​ k \mathrm{pass@k} gains, our method allows annealing k k during training, optimizing both metrics and often achieving strong pass ​ @ ​ 1 \mathrm{pass@1} performance alongside significant pass ​ @ ​ k \mathrm{pass@k} gains. We validate our reward transformations on illustrative toy experiments, which reveal the variance reducing properties of our formulations. We also include real-world examples using the open-source model Gemma-2 . We find that our transformation effectively optimizes for the target k k . Furthermore, higher k k values enable solving more and harder problems, while annealing k k boosts both the pass ​ @ ​ 1 \mathrm{pass@1} and pass ​ @ ​ k \mathrm{pass@k} . Crucially, for challenging task sets where conventional pass ​ @ ​ 1 \mathrm{pass@1} optimization stalls, our pass ​ @ ​ k \mathrm{pass@k} approach unblocks learning, likely due to better exploration by prioritizing joint utility over the utility of individual samples.

2.1
An Unbiased
\mathrm{pass@k}
Estimator

An estimator for the
\mathrm{pass@k}
was given in
[
10
]
: given
n\geq k
i.i.d. samples of which

\displaystyle\rho(n,c,k)\equiv 1-\frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}.

(3)

The following was proven in
[
10
]

Theorem 1
.

\rho(n,c,k)
is an unbiased estimator of the
\mathrm{pass@k}
.

Proof.

Let
x_{1},x_{2},\dotsm,x_{n}\sim p(x|\theta)
,
f_{i}=f(x_{i})
, and
\mathcal{I}
be a set of
k
elements sampled uniformly without replacement from
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{pass@k}=\mathbb{E}_{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{I}}\left[1-\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}}(1-f_{i})\right].

(4)

Averaging over all subsets of size
k
recovers
\rho
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\mathcal{I}|=k\\
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,n\}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}}(1-f_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\mathcal{I}|=k\\
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,n\}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}}(1-f_{i})

(5)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}

(6)

\displaystyle\equiv\rho(n,c,k),

(7)

where (
6
) holds because the sum on the r.h.s. of (
5
) is the number of subsets of size
k
of the
(n-c)
incorrect elements. Since averaging in this way retains unbiasedness, this completes the proof. ∎

2.2
An Unbiased
\mathrm{pass@k}
Gradient Estimator

Given a mini-batch of

\displaystyle\widehat{\nabla}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\nabla_{\theta}\log p(x_{i}|\theta),~~~\textrm{where}~~~r_{i}=\begin{cases}\frac{k}{n}&\mathrm{if~}f_{i}=1\\

\end{cases}

(8)

The following well-known results will be used to show that (

Lemma 1

(Policy Gradients)
.

For any absolutely continuous distribution

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p(x|\theta)}\left[r(x)\nabla_{\theta}\log p(x|\theta)\right]=\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p(x|\theta)}\left[r(x)\right].

(9)

Corollary 1
.

If
c
is constant w.r.t. both
\theta
and

then

\mathbb{E}_{p(x|\theta)}\left[c\nabla_{\theta}\log p(x|\theta)\right]=0.

Proof.

By
Lemma

1

\mathbb{E}_{p(x|\theta)}\left[c\nabla_{\theta}\log p(x|\theta)\right]=\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}\left[c\right]=\nabla_{\theta}c=0

Theorem 2
.

\widehat{\nabla}
is an unbiased estimator of the gradient of the
\mathrm{pass@k}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}\sim p(x|\theta)}\left[\widehat{\nabla}\right]=\nabla_{\theta}\mathrm{pass@k}.

(10)

6
Conclusions and Outlook

In RL training with multiple independent samples per task, optimizing the
\mathrm{pass@k}
maximizes the expectation of the
best
reward in the set of samples, rather than the
average
one. This retains model output diversity leading to the eventual solution of more problems, and so to stronger policies. We provide drop-in replacements for more traditional RL reward transformations that robustly and efficiently optimize the
\mathrm{pass@k}
. This work can be extended in various ways, such as to other inference-time search algorithms, and to more sophisticated baseline techniques.
