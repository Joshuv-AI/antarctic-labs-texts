Is blood benign?—current evidence on perioperative pediatric transfusion
Authors: Whitman B Wiggins, Heather Liebe, Ana R Do, Janice A Taylor, Shawn D Larson, Izabela Oluwole, Reema Kashif, Steven L Raymond
Published: 2026

The study by Goobie and Faraoni provides a timely and expansive description of red blood cell transfusion exposure and associated outcomes in pediatric noncardiac surgery (1). Beyond describing transfusion trends, the study raises an important clinical question: how should perioperative transfusion be understood—as a marker of illness severity, a clinician-directed exposure that may be optimized, or both?

Using the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program Pediatric (NSQIP Pediatric) database, Goobie and Faraoni examined more than 1.3 million children undergoing noncardiac surgery and found that 6.3% received red blood cell transfusion during the intraoperative period or within 72 hours after surgery (1). Notably, transfusion exposure varied over the study period, peaking at 7.0% in 2021, and was associated with substantially higher 30-day mortality and postoperative morbidity (1). The association persisted after adjustment for multiple clinical factors, with an adjusted odds ratio for 30-day mortality of 2.11 [95% confidence interval (CI), 1.95–2.27] (1). Neonates represented a particularly vulnerable subgroup, with a transfusion rate of 13.3% and a 30-day mortality rate of 14.9% among those transfused, compared with 1.8% among nontransfused neonates (1). Although these findings may reflect confounding by indication, as transfused children are often among the most clinically complex surgical patients, the association has recurred across pediatric perioperative cohorts spanning different eras, populations, institutions, and analytic approaches (1-7). This consistency does not establish causality, but it makes transfusion exposure difficult to dismiss as merely a marker of measured illness severity and supports closer examination of perioperative transfusion practices.

These observations highlight an important clinical reality: although red blood cell transfusion is often necessary and can be lifesaving, it remains a serious intervention whose potential deleterious consequences may be underestimated. The findings by Goobie and Faraoni (1) should prompt clinicians to more critically assess the risks, indications, and downstream consequences of perioperative transfusion. While their findings do not prove that transfusion causes mortality or complications, they demonstrate that transfusion is a powerful risk marker in pediatric perioperative care, with the strongest association observed among neonates. When considered alongside prior neonatal and pediatric surgical studies, these findings support the view that perioperative transfusion is not simply a marker of illness severity, but a clinician-directed exposure independently associated with worse outcomes whose indication, threshold, timing, and dose warrant closer scrutiny (1-4,6,7).

The primary strength of this study is its scale. By analyzing more than a decade of multicenter data, Goobie and Faraoni provide one of the most comprehensive descriptions to date of transfusion exposure in North American pediatric noncardiac surgery (1). The use of NSQIP Pediatric data also allows for standardized capture of 30-day postoperative outcomes, which is particularly valuable in children, in whom adverse events may be infrequent and difficult to characterize outside large datasets. The use of multivariable models further strengthens the analysis by demonstrating that the association between transfusion and adverse outcomes persisted after adjustment for multiple measured patient, operative, and acuity-related factors (1). The authors’ stratification by age and transfusion volume further strengthens the study, because pediatric patients are not a physiologically homogeneous population (1). A neonate undergoing laparotomy and an adolescent undergoing orthopedic surgery may both be pediatric surgical patients, but their transfusion risks, circulating blood volume, oxygen delivery reserve, and postoperative vulnerability are fundamentally different.

The temporal pattern in transfusion exposure is one of the more provocative descriptive findings. Transfusion rates varied across the study period, reaching a low of 4.7% in 2016, peaking at 7.0% in 2021, and remaining above the overall average at 6.8% in 2023 (1). Importantly, Goobie and Faraoni noted that surgical complexity remained stable across the study period, with a constant median relative value unit (RVU) of 10, suggesting that the temporal increase in transfusion exposure is less likely to be explained by increasing operative complexity alone and may instead reflect persistent variability in perioperative transfusion practice (1). This pattern is notable because pediatric patient blood management (PBM) has become increasingly visible over the same period, with emphasis on preoperative anemia diagnosis and treatment, blood conservation, restrictive transfusion thresholds, antifibrinolytics, cell salvage, standardized transfusion algorithms, and point-of-care viscoelastic testing (8,9). The persistence of substantial transfusion exposure despite this broader PBM framework suggests that implementation, adherence, and procedure-specific tailoring remain important targets for improvement.

The current study extends prior NSQIP Pediatric work by evaluating transfusion exposure in a larger, more contemporary cohort across an 11-year period (1). In 2016, Goobie et al. reported a volume-outcome relationship among children undergoing noncardiac surgery, with higher weight-based transfusion volumes associated with increased 30-day mortality and postoperative infection (6). The present study broadens that earlier work by adding temporal trends, age-specific risk, and adjusted outcome associations across more than 1.3 million pediatric surgical patients (1). Importantly, the implications of these findings extend beyond neonates. Although neonates had the highest absolute mortality and complication rates, Goobie and Faraoni found that transfusion was associated with higher mortality and morbidity across pediatric age groups (1). This broader signal strengthens the relevance of the study beyond neonatal surgery alone and suggests that perioperative transfusion should be treated as a meaningful risk marker throughout pediatric surgical care. The clinical challenge is therefore not limited to one age group, but rather extends to developing age-appropriate, physiology-specific, and dose-conscious transfusion practices across the pediatric surgical population.

Among the age-specific findings, the neonatal results deserve particular attention. Compared with older children, neonates have limited circulating blood volume, developmental differences in oxygen delivery and consumption, immature immune responses, and a narrow margin between anemia and transfusion-associated harm (8,10). The worse outcomes observed among transfused neonates are consistent with several recent neonatal surgical studies (2-4). Fazzone et al. analyzed 19,105 neonates from the NSQIP Pediatric database from 2021 to 2022, of whom 2,687 (14%) received perioperative red blood cell transfusion, and found that transfusion was associated with increased 30-day mortality and morbidity after propensity matching (4). Similarly, Mehl et al. reported a dose-dependent association between transfusion volume and postoperative complications in neonatal surgery, with an inflection point for 30-day mortality around 30–35 mL/kg (3). Dukleska et al. found that preoperative transfusion within 48 hours of neonatal surgery was independently associated with increased postoperative morbidity and mortality, even after propensity matching (2). Importantly, Fazzone et al. also found that preoperative anemia alone did not fully account for these outcomes; among transfused neonates, 30-day mortality increased at higher preoperative hematocrit levels, particularly at values above 33% (4). These findings raise an important clinical possibility: transfusion given to “optimize” a neonate with a relatively acceptable hematocrit may not be benign.

Despite this consistency, interpretation must remain cautious. Observational transfusion studies are intrinsically vulnerable to confounding by indication: children receive blood because they are bleeding, anemic, unstable, premature, septic, ventilated, or undergoing higher-risk operations. Importantly, available datasets cannot reliably distinguish transfusions administered for active hemorrhage, physiologic instability, chronic anemia, or perioperative optimization—factors that likely carry different risks and benefits. In the study by Goobie and Faraoni, transfusion was defined as red blood cell transfusion during the intraoperative period or within 72 hours after surgery, a pragmatic but clinically heterogeneous exposure window that combines hemorrhage resuscitation, treatment of anemia, and postoperative transfusion into a single variable (1). The primary analysis also did not adjust for preoperative anemia as a distinct covariate, despite prior work identifying preoperative anemia as an independent predictor of mortality in children and neonates undergoing noncardiac surgery (1,11,12). In addition, preoperative hematocrit was differentially available, raising the possibility of residual confounding related to which patients had laboratory data captured (1). Even careful risk adjustment cannot fully separate the effect of transfusion from the condition that prompted transfusion. This limitation is not unique to the present study; it is a central challenge of transfusion outcomes research. In clinical practice, transfusion is often administered at the intersection of multiple risks, many of which are incompletely captured in retrospective datasets, including blood loss, hemodynamic trajectory, lactate, vasopressor requirement, tissue oxygenation, and clinician concern. Therefore, the association between transfusion and mortality should not be simplified into the claim that transfusion caused mortality.

However, confounding by indication should not be used to dismiss the association. The current study and related surgical studies attempted to account for baseline illness severity, surgical complexity, and preoperative acuity, and transfusion remained independently associated with worse outcomes (1-4,6). Transfusion should therefore be recognized as a clinically significant intervention whose indication, dose, and physiologic effect require reassessment. The clinical challenge is to distinguish scenarios in which transfusion is lifesaving, such as active bleeding or severe anemia, from those in which the expected benefit is less certain, such as stable mild anemia or preoperative optimization (8,9).

Preoperative anemia represents another critical part of this discussion, although it does not fully explain the neonatal transfusion-outcome relationship. In neonates, Goobie et al. previously identified preoperative anemia, defined as a hematocrit <40%, as an independent risk factor for postoperative mortality (12). In older children undergoing noncardiac surgery, preoperative anemia has also been associated with increased in-hospital mortality, even after adjustment for transfusion and other confounders (11). More recently, McCormack et al. evaluated the relationship between preoperative anemia and transfusion across an 11-year period and found that anemia, transfusion, and especially the combination were associated with increased 30-day mortality and postoperative complications (7). However, Fazzone et al. added important nuance in the neonatal surgical population: in their matched analysis, neonates with similar perioperative transfusion rates had comparable morbidity and mortality regardless of whether preoperative hematocrit was <35% or >40%, whereas the hematocrit-mortality gradient was observed specifically among transfused neonates (4). This finding suggests that relative preoperative anemia alone may not explain postoperative morbidity and mortality, and that transfusion for preoperative “optimization”, particularly at higher hematocrit levels, should not be assumed to be benign. Together, these studies demonstrate the clinical dilemma: preoperative anemia may identify limited physiologic reserve, but transfusion to correct or “optimize” anemia does not necessarily eliminate that risk and may introduce additional risk. Pediatric PBM must therefore address both sides of the pathway by identifying and treating anemia when possible, minimizing blood loss, and reserving transfusion for situations in which the expected physiologic benefit outweighs potential risk.

The goal of pediatric PBM is not simply to reduce transfusion rates. A narrow “less blood is better” approach risks undertreating children who need transfusion. Importantly, transfusion thresholds may appropriately differ in selected populations, including children with sickle cell disease, cyanotic heart disease, extracorporeal support, or other conditions in which oxygen delivery or disease-specific considerations alter the risk-benefit profile. Instead, pediatric PBM aims to optimize hemoglobin, minimize blood loss, support hemostasis, and to reserve transfusion for situations likely to improve oxygen delivery or prevent physiologic deterioration (8,9).

The randomized neonatal transfusion literature provides important context for these observational surgical findings. In the TOP and ETTNO trials (13,14), liberal hemoglobin thresholds did not improve survival or neurodevelopmental outcomes compared with restrictive thresholds in extremely low birth weight infants, and a subsequent meta-analysis of six randomized trials similarly found that restrictive thresholds did not increase mortality or the composite of death or neurodevelopmental impairment (15). These findings support restrictive transfusion strategies in the neonatal intensive care unit, but they do not address whether perioperative transfusion exposure, timing, and dose are modifiable contributors to postoperative risk in surgical neonates. These trials compared higher versus lower transfusion thresholds, not transfusion versus no transfusion; in ETTNO, 60.5% of infants in the restrictive group still received transfusion (14). The NECTARINE cohort demonstrated substantial variability in perioperative transfusion triggers among neonates and infants, but its findings are also notable for the magnitude of adverse outcomes among transfused patients: perioperative transfusion was associated with up to a 5-fold higher mortality, with a mortality of 13.9% (95% CI, 10.3–18.0%) among transfused patients younger than 1 year (5). This closely parallels the 14.9% 30-day mortality among transfused neonates reported by Goobie and Faraoni, reinforcing that the association between transfusion exposure and adverse outcomes is not isolated to a single dataset or health system (1). Taken together, the randomized threshold trials and perioperative observational studies support the same practical conclusion: transfusion can be lifesaving when clinically necessary, but more liberal transfusion has not been shown to improve outcomes, and transfusion exposure should not be assumed to be benign.

The findings across these studies also have biologic support. Transfusion-related immunomodulation provides a potential mechanism linking red blood cell exposure with adverse outcomes, particularly infection, inflammation, and organ dysfunction. Red blood cell products contain residual leukocyte-derived mediators, free hemoglobin, iron, extracellular vesicles, storage-related metabolites, and other factors that may alter the recipient’s innate and adaptive immune responses (16). In neonates, transfusion has been associated with changes in inflammatory cytokines and endothelial activation, and these effects may vary by sex, gestational age, transfusion burden, and underlying illness (10). Furthermore, the impact of blood product characteristics, including storage duration, leukoreduction status, irradiation, and donor exposure, remains incompletely understood and is not captured within most large surgical databases. These mechanisms do not prove causality in surgical outcomes, but they reinforce that transfusion is not physiologically inert. In the perioperative setting, where tissue injury, inflammation, anesthesia exposure, and immune stress are already present, transfusion may plausibly amplify or modify these responses in selected patients (10,16).

The practical implication is that perioperative transfusion should be measured, justified, and reviewed as a clinically significant intervention rather than treated as routine supportive care. Institutions should know their transfusion rates by age, procedure, urgency, and transfusion volume, and transfusion decisions should be paired with clear documentation of indication, including active bleeding, anticipated blood loss, hemodynamic instability, impaired oxygen delivery, severe anemia, or institutional threshold. These data would allow pediatric PBM programs to identify patterns of potentially avoidable exposure and inform age-, procedure-, and physiology-specific protocols. The World Health Organization (WHO) guidance on implementing PBM provides a practical framework for this work, including healthcare organization (HCO)-specific PBM structures, data systems, institutional processes, and a neonatal and pediatric PBM toolkit in Annex 9 (17). Future work should build on the well-described association between transfusion and adverse outcomes by focusing on the clinical contexts in which transfusion decisions remain most variable and consequential, including elective optimization, stable anemia, trauma, burns, emergency neonatal surgery, and other high-risk pediatric conditions. Future investigations should incorporate physiologic markers of oxygen delivery and tissue perfusion rather than relying solely on hemoglobin or hematocrit thresholds. Prospective and, where ethically feasible, randomized studies are needed to determine when transfusion improves outcomes, when it represents avoidable exposure, and how thresholds should differ by age, procedure, physiology, and acuity.

In conclusion, Goobie and Faraoni provide an important contemporary analysis of transfusion exposure and outcomes in pediatric noncardiac surgery (1). Their findings should not be read as an argument against necessary transfusion, but as a call for transfusion precision. Perioperative transfusion should be understood as more than a marker of illness severity; it is a clinician-directed exposure whose indication, threshold, timing, and dose should be deliberately optimized. The goal should be to preserve the lifesaving role of transfusion while reducing avoidable exposure.
