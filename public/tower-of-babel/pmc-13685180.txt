Geographic differences in outcomes after emergency surgery in nonagenarians and centenarians admitted to intensive care in Australia: a population-based study
Authors: Je Min Suh, Jacob Zand, Carl Reyneke, Nattaya Raykateeraroj, David Pilcher, Dong-Kyu Lee, Laurence Weinberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionNonagenarian and centenarian patients increasingly undergo emergency surgery with subsequent intensive care unit (ICU) admission, but it remains unclear whether geographic disparities in outcomes arise during the index hospitalization or emerge after discharge in universal health systems.MethodsWe conducted a national, population-based retrospective cohort study using linked data from the Australian and New Zealand Intensive Care Society (ANZICS) Adult Patient Database and the Australian National Death Index. We included all nonagenarian and centenarian patients admitted to Australian ICUs after emergency surgery between January 1, 2018, and December 31, 2024. Geographic residence on ICU admission was classified with the Modified Monash Model as metropolitan (MMM1) or regional/remote (MMM2–7). Outcomes were ICU mortality, in-hospital mortality, and all-cause post-discharge mortality at 5 years among patients discharged alive. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to examine associations between geographic residence and post-discharge mortality, with adjustment for age, sex, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) III score, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, major chronic comorbidities, and hospital type.ResultsAmong 4,887 patients admitted to ICUs after emergency surgery, ICU and hospital mortality did not differ meaningfully by geographic residence. In contrast, among patients discharged alive, regional or remote residence was independently associated with higher post-discharge mortality at 5 years (adjusted HR, 1.133, 95% CI 1.027–1.250, p = 0.013) compared with metropolitan residence. Survival curves diverged after hospital discharge and remained separated throughout follow-up. In a secondary cohort of 7,896 patients undergoing elective surgery, geographic residence was not associated with ICU, in-hospital, or post-discharge mortality.ConclusionAmong nonagenarian and centenarian patients admitted to ICUs after emergency surgery, geographic residence was not associated with mortality during the index hospitalization but was independently associated with higher long-term mortality after discharge. While no differences were observed during the acute care episode, patients residing in regional or remote areas experienced lower post-discharge survival compared with those living in metropolitan areas. These findings highlight potential geographic variation in long-term outcomes and support further investigation into factors influencing post-discharge care and survival among the oldest surgical patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13054-026-06215-2.

Introduction

Recent improvements in healthcare delivery, living conditions, and chronic disease management have extended life expectancy and led to rapid growth in global nonagenarian and centenarian populations [1, 2]. In 2020, the number of individuals 90 years or older reached 21.5 million worldwide and is projected to increase to 30.9 million by 2030 [3], with consequent implications for acute and critical care systems.

Emergency surgery in patients of very advanced age is associated with substantial perioperative risk, reflecting limited physiological reserve, multimorbidity, and vulnerability to acute stress [4]. Contemporary series report in‑hospital mortality approaching or exceeding one‑third and postoperative complication rates of 60% or higher in nonagenarian surgical patients, particularly when procedures are emergent [5]. These patients frequently require intensive care unit (ICU) admission for hemodynamic support, organ failure management, and postoperative surveillance, making timely access to high‑quality critical care a central determinant of survival and recovery [4].

As in many countries, ICU services in Australia are heavily concentrated in metropolitan centers, with large geographic catchment areas served by relatively few facilities. Approximately 28% of the Australian population resides in rural or remote regions that encompass 98% of the land mass [6, 7], and these communities experience higher burdens of chronic disease, greater rates of acute illness, and shorter life expectancy than metropolitan populations. Rural and remote residents also face structural barriers, including longer transport times, constrained specialist availability, and workforce shortages, that may delay access to definitive surgical and critical care [8]. These well‑described geographic inequities suggest that place of residence may modify outcomes for very elderly patients undergoing emergency surgery and admitted to ICU [9].

Evidence regarding how geography influences outcomes in this population remains limited and inconsistent [10]. Studies of trauma and vascular emergencies in Australia have shown higher mortality in rural patients before hospital arrival, with broadly similar ICU and in‑hospital outcomes once patients reach specialist centers [11, 12]. Conversely, work in older adults discharged from acute care has demonstrated that rural residence is associated with higher post‑discharge mortality [13], particularly among those receiving institutional post‑acute care, despite similar rates of referral to such services [9, 14]. Emerging literature on nonagenarian ICU patients indicates that, although ICU admission of the oldest old is increasing, prognostic data are sparse and focused largely on short‑term outcomes, with little attention to the interplay between geography, emergency surgery, and long‑term survival [15].

Importantly, geographic disparities in healthcare access are recognized worldwide and are commonly characterized using country-specific rurality or healthcare accessibility classifications [16]. Regardless of the classification system used, regional, rural, and remote populations frequently experience reduced access to specialist services, rehabilitation, primary care, and post-discharge support compared with metropolitan populations [17]. Consequently, understanding whether geographic access to healthcare influences long-term recovery following critical illness has important implications beyond individual healthcare systems.

Within Australia, residential location is routinely classified using the Modified Monash Model (MMM), a nationally endorsed framework that integrates population size and remoteness to distinguish metropolitan (MMM = 1) from regional, rural, and remote (MMM = 2–7) areas [18]. This classification underpins funding and workforce policies and offers a policy‑relevant exposure with which to examine geographic disparities in care and outcomes among very elderly ICU patients.

We therefore conducted a national, population-based cohort study of nonagenarian and centenarian patients admitted to Australian ICUs after emergency surgery to evaluate the association between geographic residence and mortality across the continuum of care. We hypothesized that geographic residence would not be associated with mortality during the index ICU or hospital admission but would be independently associated with higher mortality after hospital discharge.

By distinguishing in-hospital from post-discharge outcomes and examining both 1- and 5-year mortality, this study aimed to identify the phase of care at which geographic differences in outcomes become apparent. These findings may help inform future strategies to improve transitions of care and long-term outcomes for this rapidly growing and clinically vulnerable population.

Methods

Ethics approval

Ethical approval was granted by the Alfred Hospital Human Research Ethics Committee (project number 253/24). Given the retrospective nature of the study and the use of de-identified data, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived. All analyses were performed after ethics approval. The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement and the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected Data (RECORD) guidelines.

Data sources, study population, and variables

De-identified patient-level data were obtained from the Australian and New Zealand Intensive Care Society (ANZICS) Adult Patient Database, a large clinical registry capturing demographic, clinical, physiological, and outcome data from approximately 98% of ICUs in Australia. Data collection, quality assurance, and governance are overseen by the ANZICS Centre for Outcome and Resource Evaluation (CORE) [19].

This population-based cohort study included nonagenarian and centenarian patients (aged ≥ 90 years at ICU admission) admitted to ICUs in Australia following emergency surgical procedures between January 1, 2018, and December 31, 2024. In the ANZICS Adult Patient Database, emergency surgery is defined as surgery that cannot be delayed for more than 24 hours, whereas elective surgery refers to procedures that can be delayed beyond 24 hours. Geographical residence was classified with the MMM, as defined by the Australian Government Department of Health and Aged Care, according to patients’ residence at the time of admission. MMM 1 was defined as metropolitan residence, whereas MMM 2–7 were grouped and defined as regional or remote residence.

Hospital type was included to account for differences in case mix and care delivery across institutions. Hospital type is centrally maintained by the ANZICS CORE and is self-assigned by each submitting institution at the commencement of data submission. Four hospital types are recognized, according to management structure and demographic characteristics: tertiary (large teaching hospitals), metropolitan (non-tertiary hospitals in large cities or towns), rural or regional (small non-teaching hospitals located outside large cities or towns), and private hospitals.

The extracted variables included age at ICU admission, sex, surgical admission type, hospital type, ICU and hospital outcomes, discharge destination, and dates of ICU and hospital admission and discharge. Acute illness severity was assessed at ICU admission with the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) III score and the derived Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score. The captured pre-existing comorbidities included chronic cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, and hepatic disease. All deaths were ascertained through linkage with the Australian National Death Index, thus enabling complete follow-up for post-discharge mortality.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was all-cause post-discharge mortality at 5 years among patients discharged alive. 1 year post-discharge mortality was also reported descriptively to characterize early mortality after hospital discharge.

Secondary outcomes included ICU mortality, in-hospital mortality during the index admission, and ICU and hospital lengths of stay, measured in hours.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted in R software, version 4.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were assessed by visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots, with consideration of distributional symmetry and the presence of outliers. Variables with approximately symmetric distributions are presented as means with standard deviations (SDs) and were compared using t-test, whereas skewed variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies with percentages and were compared with Pearson’s χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

The primary exposure was geographic residence, classified with the MMM and dichotomized into metropolitan versus regional or remote. Time to death following hospital discharge was analysed using Cox proportional hazards regression models. For post-discharge analyses, only patients discharged alive were included. Time zero was defined as the date of hospital discharge. Follow-up continued until death or administrative censoring at 5 years, whichever occurred first. Patients who remained alive at 5 years were censored at that time. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported.

Multivariable Cox models were prespecified, and adjusted for age, sex, APACHE III score, SOFA score, chronic comorbidities (including cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, or hepatic disease), and hospital type (tertiary, metropolitan, rural/regional, or private). Age, APACHE III score, and SOFA score were modeled as continuous variables. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed with Schoenfeld residuals. Sensitivity analyses were performed using alternative models including either APACHE III score alone or SOFA score alone, in place of the combined severity adjustment, to evaluate the robustness of the findings and the potential influence of conceptual overlap between the two severity scores. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated to illustrate post-discharge survival, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. In addition, adjusted survival curves were derived from the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model using the same covariates as the primary analysis.

As a prespecified contextual analysis, patients undergoing elective surgery were analyzed separately using the same multivariable Cox regression modelling approach to determine whether the association between geographic residence and post-discharge mortality was also observed in this population.

In addition, ICU and in-hospital mortality were analyzed as binary outcomes using logistic regression models to estimate odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs. Both unadjusted and multivariable-adjusted models were constructed. The adjusted models included age, sex, APACHE III score, SOFA score, chronic comorbidities (cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, and hepatic disease), and hospital type. These analyses evaluated the association between geographic residence and mortality during the index hospitalization, whereas Cox regression was used for the primary time-to-event outcome of post-discharge mortality.

ICU and hospital lengths of stay were analysed with log-transformed linear regression models to account for right-skewed distributions. The models were adjusted for the same covariates as the time-to-event analyses, including hospital type. Model coefficients were back-transformed to the original scale and are presented as length-of-stay ratios with 95% CIs. Regression model assumptions were assessed with residual diagnostics. Multicollinearity was evaluated with variance inflation factors. To account for the competing risk of in-hospital death, additional Fine–Gray competing risks regression analyses were performed with ICU discharge and hospital discharge as the events of interest, respectively, and death before discharge as the competing event.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. No adjustment was made for multiple comparisons. Missing data were minimal (< 5% for all included variables) and were handled with complete-case analysis.

Patients in metropolitan areas had higher illness severity at ICU admission, with higher APACHE III scores (66.00 [IQR, 57.00–78.00] vs. 65.00 [56.00–75.00]; p < 0.001) and higher SOFA scores (4.00 [2.00–6.00] vs. 4.00 [2.00–5.00]; p = 0.005). Metropolitan patients also had a shorter ICU length of stay (38.67 h [20.20–67.62] vs. 44.15 h [22.57–82.93]; p < 0.001) but a longer hospital length of stay (287.51 h [171.12–497.01] vs. 254.10 h [145.93–415.60]; p < 0.001). The sex distribution was similar between groups (female, 57.8% vs. 56.6%; p = 0.473). There were no significant differences in ICU outcomes (p = 0.086), including ICU mortality (5.5% vs. 5.8%). However, in-hospital mortality differed between groups (14.3% vs. 12.5% ). Discharge patterns also varied, with metropolitan patients more frequently discharged to rehabilitation (21.0% vs. 12.3%), whereas regional or remote patients were more commonly transferred to other acute hospitals (25.6% vs. 12.1%).

Chronic cardiovascular disease was more prevalent among regional or remote patients (22.3% vs. 18.6%; p = 0.006), while other comorbidities, including chronic respiratory (8.3% vs. 8.5%; p = 0.850), liver (0.2% vs. 0.3%; p = 0.789), and renal disease (5.5% vs. 6.0%; p = 0.521), were similar between groups.

Mortality outcomes after emergency surgery

Mortality outcomes after emergency surgery varied by geographic residence. The differences were most evident in the post-discharge period (Table 2). Among 4,887 nonagenarian and centenarian patients admitted to intensive care, the ICU mortality was 0%–7.7%, and the hospital mortality was 0%–100% across residence categories. Metropolitan patients had an ICU mortality of 5.5% and a hospital mortality of 14.3%, and comparable ranges were also observed in patients from regional or remote areas. Among patients in the most remote group, no ICU deaths occurred, and no patients survived to hospital discharge.

Across models, greater age was consistently associated with higher mortality at 5 years after discharge. Male sex was independently associated with mortality after adjustment. Higher APACHE III scores were associated with greater post-discharge mortality in both the unadjusted and adjusted analyses, whereas an association between SOFA score and mortality was observed only in the unadjusted models but did not remain significant after multivariable adjustment. Variance inflation factors for all covariates were below 2, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity among the variables included in the multivariable models (Supplementary Table 1).

Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of these findings by modelling illness severity using either APACHE III score or SOFA score alone. The results are demonstrated in Supplementary Table 2. Regional residence was associated with increased post-discharge mortality after adjustment in both models. The effect remained consistent whether adjusting for SOFA score (adjusted HR 1.12, 95% CI 1.02–1.24, p = 0.022) or APACHE III score (adjusted HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.03–1.25, p = 0.013).

Results for ICU and in-hospital mortality are presented in Supplementary Table 3. In both unadjusted and adjusted analyses, geographic residence was not associated with ICU or hospital mortality, whereas acute illness severity (APACHE III and SOFA scores) was the strongest predictor of short-term outcomes; chronic liver disease was independently associated with hospital mortality.

Elective surgery analyses

Among 7,896 patients undergoing elective surgery, geographic residence was not associated with post-discharge mortality at 5 years in both the unadjusted and adjusted analyses (Supplementary Table 4).

Lengths of stay

Lengths of stay after emergency surgery differed by geographic residence (Table 4). In adjusted analyses, patients from regional or remote areas had longer ICU stays than those from metropolitan areas (adjusted time ratio, 1.08; 95% CI, 1.01–1.15). Greater age was associated with shorter ICU lengths of stay, whereas male sex, higher APACHE III scores, and higher SOFA scores were independently associated with longer ICU stays. Chronic comorbidities were not independently associated with ICU length of stay after adjustment.

Discussion

Key findings

In this national, population-based study in nonagenarian and centenarian patients undergoing emergency surgery with subsequent intensive care admission, geographic residence was not associated with mortality during the index ICU or hospital admission but was independently associated with higher mortality after hospital discharge. After adjustment for age, sex, acute illness severity, major comorbidities, and hospital type, residence in regional or remote areas was associated with increased risk of death at 5 years after discharge compared with metropolitan residence. Importantly, short‑term outcomes, including ICU and in‑hospital mortality, were broadly similar across geographic categories, whereas survival curves diverged after discharge and remained separated over extended follow‑up, indicating that determinants of excess mortality operate predominantly beyond the hospital setting.

The association between non‑metropolitan residence and elevated post‑discharge mortality was specific to emergency surgery. Among patients undergoing elective surgery, geographic residence was not associated with ICU, hospital, or post-discharge mortality after multivariable adjustment. This differential pattern suggests that geographic disparities in long‑term outcomes are most consequential when very elderly patients are exposed to unplanned surgical insults and complex, often fragile recovery trajectories, rather than when surgery is elective and care pathways can be anticipated and structured.

Importantly, we believe the principal contribution of this study extends beyond the Australian healthcare system. Although geographic residence was classified using the Australian Modified Monash Model, the key finding was not simply the presence of geographic differences in long-term mortality, but that these differences emerged only after hospital discharge despite comparable ICU and in-hospital mortality. This suggests that improving long-term outcomes in the oldest critically ill surgical patients may depend as much on post-discharge recovery and healthcare accessibility as on acute hospital care, a concept that is directly relevant to healthcare systems worldwide.

Relationship of the findings to the literature

Our study findings extend and integrate several strands of existing evidence. First, they add to the growing literature describing outcomes among nonagenarian and centenarian ICU patients, a population in whom demand for critical care is increasing but robust prognostic data remain scarce. Previous studies have described variable ICU and hospital mortality among the oldest old, with outcomes influenced by admission diagnosis, treatment intensity, and local practice patterns, but have seldom examined long‑term survival or the role of geography. By leveraging a national registry with long‑term mortality linkage, our analysis demonstrates that, in the context of a universal healthcare system and near‑complete ICU coverage, geographic residence was not associated with in‑hospital survival for very elderly emergency surgical patients but is an important determinant of long‑term survival after discharge.

Second, the observed pattern aligns with work in trauma and other time‑critical conditions showing that rural disadvantage is often concentrated in the pre‑hospital phase, with higher rates of pre‑admission death but convergence of ICU and in‑hospital mortality among patients who reach specialist care [11, 12]. In Australian trauma and abdominal aortic aneurysm cohorts, rural and remote patients have exhibited higher early mortality yet similar ICU outcomes to metropolitan patients [11, 12], suggesting that once definitive care is accessed, the quality of acute management is comparable across settings. Our findings suggest that, among very elderly surgical ICU patients, a parallel phenomenon may operate at the post‑acute end of the care continuum: once patients survive to discharge, the subsequent phase of care becomes the locus of geographic disparity.

Third, our findings are consistent with a broader body of literature demonstrating that rural–urban differences often emerge or widen after hospital discharge and are closely linked to post‑acute care trajectories. Large cohort studies of Medicare beneficiaries have shown that, although rural and urban patients are similarly likely to be discharged to formal post‑acute care, patients from rural counties experience higher post‑discharge mortality, particularly when discharged to institutional settings, and are less likely to transition back to the community or access home health services. Our findings mirror this pattern in a distinct clinical context: non‑metropolitan nonagenarian and centenarian patients who survive emergency surgery and ICU admission face persistently higher long‑term mortality risk, despite comparable in‑hospital outcomes to their metropolitan counterparts.

Finally, we situate these observations within the Australian context of the Modified Monash Model, which classifies residential areas according to remoteness and town size and underpins healthcare workforce and funding policies. While MMM effectively captures gradients in access and remoteness at a national level, our results suggest that for very elderly surgical ICU survivors, residence in MMM2–7 areas is a marker of exposure to post‑discharge environments where access to rehabilitation, geriatric expertise, primary care continuity, and community‑based support may be constrained.

Potential mechanisms

The dissociation between in-hospital and post-discharge outcomes suggests that the association between non-metropolitan residence and survival may primarily reflect factors operating after hospital discharge rather than differences in perioperative or ICU care. For nonagenarian and centenarian patients, recovery from emergency surgery frequently extends well beyond the index hospitalization and depends on a series of interlinked processes: structured rehabilitation, ongoing medical review, medication optimization, timely detection of clinical deterioration, and alignment of care with evolving goals and preferences. Patients returning to regional or remote settings may face multiple structural barriers to these processes, including limited availability of inpatient rehabilitation and geriatric services, fewer allied health resources, workforce shortages in primary care, and longer travel distances for follow‑up. These organisational factors may also influence discharge destination and length of stay, independent of differences in patient illness severity.

In this context, geographic residence likely functions as a proxy for a complex constellation of unmeasured factors. These include functional status, frailty, cognitive impairment, social support, socio‑economic disadvantage, and the density and configuration of community services, none of which were captured in the registry. For example, very elderly patients with limited informal support in remote communities may have fewer options for community‑based rehabilitation or home care, increasing reliance on emergency departments and acute hospital readmissions and reducing opportunities for proactive management of deconditioning, falls, and polypharmacy [9, 20, 21].

The absence of a geographic gradient in elective surgical patients provides additional insight. Elective procedures are embedded within planned pathways that allow preoperative optimization, anticipatory discharge planning, and pre‑arranged postoperative follow‑up, which may partially buffer the impact of geographic disadvantage. In contrast, emergency surgery often occurs in the setting of acute physiological decompensation and limited time for shared decision‑making or care coordination. For nonagenarian and centenarian patients residing in non‑metropolitan areas, this may magnify the consequences of constrained post‑acute infrastructure, leading to higher long‑term mortality despite successful completion of the index hospitalization [9, 22].

Clinical and policy implications

These findings have several implications for clinical practice and health policy. First, they underscore the importance of extending risk assessment for very elderly surgical patients beyond the perioperative period to encompass recovery after hospital discharge. Our findings suggest that, despite comparable ICU and hospital outcomes, long-term recovery may be influenced by factors beyond the acute episode of critical illness. Accordingly, risk assessment should consider not only perioperative illness severity but also factors that may influence post-discharge recovery and access to ongoing healthcare.

Second, our findings highlight the importance of optimizing transitions of care for very elderly ICU survivors. Although healthcare systems differ internationally, many countries face similar challenges in providing equitable access to rehabilitation, specialist follow-up, primary care, and community-based support for geographically dispersed populations. Improving coordination between tertiary hospitals and community healthcare providers, together with structured discharge planning and post-discharge follow-up, may represent important opportunities to improve long-term outcomes.

Finally, our findings have broader implications for healthcare systems caring for rapidly ageing populations. As increasing numbers of nonagenarian and centenarian patients survive emergency surgery and critical illness, healthcare planning should extend beyond the delivery of acute hospital care to encompass long-term recovery and survivorship. While the specific strategies adopted will differ between countries, our findings support greater emphasis on post-acute care pathways, rehabilitation, and equitable access to healthcare services as potential targets for improving long-term outcomes among the oldest critically ill surgical patients.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several important strengths. First, it leveraged a large population-based national registry capturing approximately 98% of ICU admissions in Australia, permitting near‑complete inclusion of nonagenarian and centenarian patients undergoing emergency surgery across metropolitan, regional, and remote settings and minimizing selection bias at the institutional level. The use of a validated, policy-relevant geographic classification (MMM) based on residential postcode enhanced the external validity and facilitates translation to health‑system planning. Long-term mortality ascertainment through linkage through the Australian National Death Index provided virtually complete follow‑up for post‑discharge mortality at both 1 and 5 years, an uncommon strength in studies of the oldest old. Adjustment for detailed physiological severity and major chronic comorbidities and hospital type further strengthened the internal validity of the findings.

Several limitations warrant careful consideration. As with any observational study, residual confounding cannot be excluded. The registry lacks key geriatric constructs, including frailty, baseline functional status, cognitive impairment, and social support, as well as measures of socio‑economic position and health‑system characteristics at the community level. These unmeasured factors are likely to be unequally distributed across MMM categories and may mediate part of the observed association between geography and post‑discharge mortality. Second, the MMM classifies geographic areas rather than individual‑level access to services, and its application has been criticized for not fully capturing heterogeneity within regions and for inconsistently reflecting true service availability. Patients living in larger regional centers classified as MMM2–3 may have substantially better access to rehabilitation and outpatient services than those in very remote communities, yet both are grouped as “regional or remote” in our primary analyses. Small sample sizes in the most remote categories limited precision for those strata and precluded more granular modeling of the geographic gradient.

Our analysis was restricted to patients admitted to ICU after emergency surgery and therefore does not capture very elderly individuals deemed unsuitable for surgery or ICU admission, nor those managed in non‑ICU settings. Thresholds for offering emergency surgery and critical care may vary by geography, potentially leading to selection of more resilient non‑metropolitan patients into ICU, which would be expected to attenuate, rather than exaggerate, observed mortality differences; nevertheless, selection bias cannot be excluded. Furthermore, although missing data were minimal (< 5% for all variables), complete-case analysis assumes that missingness did not materially influence the results, an assumption that cannot be fully verified in an observational registry study. Finally, information on cause‑specific mortality and post‑discharge healthcare utilization (including readmissions, rehabilitation intensity, primary care follow‑up, and transitions to residential aged care) was unavailable, precluding direct evaluation of the mechanisms linking geographic residence to long‑term outcomes. Although comprehensive death registry linkage reduces loss to follow‑up, migration after discharge, including relocation to metropolitan areas or long‑term care facilities, could not be fully accounted for.

Despite these limitations, the consistency of the association between non‑metropolitan residence and post‑discharge mortality across adjusted models and time horizons, in contrast to the absence of an association with in‑hospital mortality and the null findings in elective surgical patients, supports the robustness of the central observation. However, the observed effect size was modest and should be interpreted in the context of its absolute magnitude and the observational nature of the study. Overall, these findings suggest that, for nonagenarian and centenarian emergency surgical ICU survivors, geography may represent an important marker of long-term survival risk that appears to exert its influence predominantly after hospital discharge.

Future directions

Future research should focus on elucidating the mechanisms underlying the observed excess post-discharge mortality among patients of very advanced age residing outside metropolitan areas. Linkage of ICU registries to datasets capturing functional status, frailty, cognitive impairment, social determinants of health, and detailed post‑acute care utilization would enable more precise characterization of vulnerability after discharge and identify modifiable gaps in care. Prospective studies incorporating standardized geriatric assessments at ICU admission and discharge could clarify how geography interacts with physiological reserve and social context to shape recovery trajectories.

Interventional studies targeting the transition from hospital to community are warranted, particularly for regional or remote populations. These interventions might include structured discharge planning, enhanced rehabilitation pathways, and telehealth-supported follow-up tailored to the oldest surgical patients. Finally, incorporation of geographic risk into perioperative decision-making and post-discharge risk stratification frameworks might support more equitable allocation of resources and improve long-term outcomes in this rapidly growing population.

Conclusion

In this national population-based study of nonagenarian and centenarian patients admitted to intensive care after emergency surgery, geographic residence was not associated with mortality during ICU or hospital admission, but was independently associated with mortality after hospital discharge. These findings indicate that differences in survival among surgical patients of very advanced age emerge predominantly after discharge rather than during in-hospital care. As populations continue to age globally, understanding how healthcare accessibility influences recovery after critical illness may represent an important opportunity to improve survivorship among very elderly ICU patients across diverse healthcare systems.
