Insights into the heterogeneous muscle lipidome of dysferlin-deficient mice: effects of age, muscle type, and sex
Authors: Stacey N Keenan, Jackie Bayliss, Olivia Lee, Erin M Lloyd, Peter J Meikle, Miranda D Grounds, Matthew J Watt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDysferlinopathy is an age-dependent muscular dystrophy caused by loss of the membrane-associated protein dysferlin. Disease severity increases with age and selectively affects specific muscle groups, yet the molecular basis for this vulnerability remains unclear. Since lipid remodeling is a hallmark of dysferlinopathy and aging, we investigated how age, sex, and muscle fiber type interact to shape the muscle lipidome in dysferlin-deficient mice. We performed omics-scale lipid profiling across 738 lipid species and 30 lipid classes in quadriceps and gastrocnemius (fast-twitch muscles exhibiting pronounced pathology), soleus (slow-twitch) and extensor digitorum longus (EDL; fast-twitch, relatively spared) muscles from male and female dysferlin-deficient (BLA/J) and wildtype C57BL/6J (WT) mice aged 3, 10, and 26 months. Normal aging was associated with broad lipid remodeling, however, in the absence of dysferlin markedly amplified this remodeling, leading to elevations in specific triglycerides, diglycerides, cholesterol esters, gangliosides, ceramides, and sphingomyelin compared to WT muscle. Interestingly, we observed minimal sex differences between dysferlin-deficient muscles. Fast-twitch muscles, particularly quadriceps and gastrocnemius, exhibited the most extensive lipid alterations, whereas the slow-twitch soleus muscle showed relative lipid stability even at advanced age. Thus, fast-glycolytic muscles are more susceptible to age- and dysferlin-dependent lipid dysregulation than slow oxidative muscle. The preferential vulnerability of fast-twitch muscles to muscle wasting in dysferlinopathy suggests that fiber-type-dependent lipid handling contributes to selective muscle degeneration. This work defines a comprehensive lipidomic signature of disease progression and provides a framework for understanding how aging, sex, and muscle phenotype interact in muscular dystrophy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13395-026-00427-4.Keywords: Dysferlinopathy, Dysferlin, Lipidomics, Lipid metabolism, Skeletal muscle

Background

Dysferlin is a 237 kDa membrane-associated protein, that is highly expressed in the sarcolemma of skeletal muscles [1]. Dysferlin is a member of the Ferlin family of transmembrane proteins, which have roles in protein vesicle fusion and trafficking [2]. Dysferlin is an important regulator of membrane repair in vitro, due to roles in vesicle trafficking, endocytosis, membrane turnover, regeneration and T-tubule formation [3].

Dysferlinopathy is a rare autosomal recessive muscular dystrophy caused by loss-of-function mutations in the dysferlin gene. The absence of dysferlin leads to the progressive loss of muscle tissue and weakness, inflammation, altered lipid metabolism and marked accumulation of lipid droplets [4–6]. Clinical symptoms arise in young adults and include high levels of serum creatine kinase and distal limb girdle muscle weakness [7], as well as histological changes including adipocyte infiltration between myofibers and inflammatory cell infiltrates [8–15]. While the intramyocellular lipid accumulation and the apparent ‘replacement’ of skeletal muscle by adipocytes are key pathological features [16], there is limited understanding of the factors that lead to these lipid disturbances.

Lipids are involved in a myriad of cellular processes and play an important role in modulating skeletal muscle mass, function and repair [17]. In recent years, it has been found that muscle injury is accompanied by increased fat infiltration [18–20]. Our recent metabolic and lipidomic analyses of dysferlin-deficient mice revealed striking changes in muscle lipids, with elevated levels of membrane-associated lipids such as sphingolipids, phospholipids, glycerolipids and cholesterol in male dysferlin-deficient BLA/J mice aged 4 months. This was an unexpected finding, as these lipidomic changes were detected prior to the emergence of any observable lipid droplet accumulation [21]. Other studies have shown lipidomic perturbations in mice with dysferlinopathy [16, 22], in particular, elevated triglycerides, trihexosylceramides, phosphatidylserines, sulfatides and sphingomyelin. There are multiple factors that underpin disease heterogeneity in dysferlinopathy; mainly age, muscle type and sex. The pattern of muscle involvement can be considered characteristic for dysferlinopathy, whereby increased fat replacement correlated positively with disease duration and functional impairment [23]. Some muscles are more susceptible to damage or impaired function compared with others; for example, quadriceps are highly susceptible, while levator scapulae are refractory to damage [24]. Additionally, histological and functional features are known to worsen with age, with more limb muscles exhibiting pathology over time [25].

Although dysferlinopathy affects both men and women, human MRI and murine lipidomic studies have shown that females have a higher degree of muscle fat than males in some muscles [16, 23]. It remains unclear whether these clinical parameters are associated with altered muscle lipids in patients with dysferlinopathy, necessitating a deeper examination of the muscle lipidome across different muscle types, ages, and sexes.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to examine the lipidome in different muscle types of dysferlin-deficient and wildtype mice and determine whether these changes are exacerbated with age and influenced by sex. The results from this study provide a comprehensive profile of the changes in the muscle lipidome in dysferlinopathy and will serve as an important resource for future studies.

Materials and methods

Dysferlin-deficient mouse model

Control C57Bl/6J (WT) and B6.A-dysf[prmd]/GeneJ (BLA/J) male and female mice were obtained from the Animal Resources Centre, Murdoch, Western Australia. Mice were housed individually at the Preclinical Animal Facility (University of Western Australia) on a 12-h light/dark cycle at ambient temperature of 20–22 ºC, with free access to a standard rodent chow diet (SF00-100, 11% total digestible energy from lipids in mixed vegetable oils and canola oils, 14.0 MJ/kg; Barastoc Irradiated Mice Cubes, Ridley Agri Products) and drinking water. Experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Ethics and Experimentation Committee of the University of Western Australia (RA/3/100/1436) and conformed to the National Health & Medical Research Council of Australia code of practice. Mice were euthanised via cervical dislocation under anaesthetic (2% v/v Attane isoflurane) and muscles were dissected from 3-, 10-, and 26-month-old mice, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C.

Lipidomic analysis

Mixed quadriceps (containing mixed slow and fast myofibers), gastrocnemius (mixed myofibers), soleus (predominately slow myofibers), and extensor digitorum longus (EDL, mainly fast myofibers) (~ 30 mg) were homogenized in 900 µL soap-free Radio-Immunoprecipitation Assay (RIPA) buffer, and total protein was determined by the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Pierce 660 nm Protein Assay; Thermo Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA, #22,662). A 10 µL aliquot of muscle homogenate was combined with 10 µL of an internal standard mix containing between 10 and 10,000 pmol each of 36 non-physiological or stable isotope-labelled lipid standards (Supplementary Table 1), then 200 µL chloroform:methanol (2:1) was added and each sample vortexed. Quality control samples were created for each muscle type and multiple blank and NIST 1950 plasma samples were also included. Samples were mixed (rotary mixer, 10 min), sonicated at room temperature (RT) for 30 min, then allowed to stand at RT for 20 min. Samples were centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min at RT. The supernatant was transferred to wells of a 96-well plate (0.5 mL polypropylene) and dried under nitrogen stream on a 40 °C heat block. Samples were reconstituted in 50 µL of water saturated butanol and sonicated for 10 min at RT, then 50 µL of 10 mM ammonium formate in methanol was added to each sample. Extracts were centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was transferred into 0.2 mL micro-inserts in 1.5 mL glass vials with teflon insert caps. Lipid analysis was performed by liquid chromatography electrospray ionisation-tandem mass spectrometry using an Agilent 1290 HPLC coupled to an Agilent 6495 C triple quadrupole mass spectrometer using settings in Supplementary Table 2 [26]. Liquid chromatography was performed on a Zorbax Eclipse Plus C18, 1.8 µm, 100 × 2.1 mm column (Agilent Technologies) using chromatographic conditions listed in Supplementary Table 3. The first 0.8 min of each analytical run was diverted to waste. Quantification of individual lipid species was performed using dynamic multiple reaction monitoring where data was collected for a retention time window specific to each lipid species. Results from the chromatographic data were analysed using Mass Hunter Quant B9.0 where relative lipid abundances were calculated by relating each area under the chromatogram for each lipid species to the corresponding internal standard. Correction factors were applied to adjust for different response factors, where these were known. Total lipid concentration of each class was calculated by summing the individual lipid species. Data presented have been normalised to total phosphatidylcholine (PC), but results were similar when data were normalised to initial tissue weight or to total PC.

Analysis of gene expression

Mixed quadriceps muscle was homogenized in TRI reagent (RNA Isolation Reagent, Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, USA, T9424). Total RNA was extracted, and 1 µg mRNA was reverse-transcribed (iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit, Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Gene products were measured by quantitative real-time PCR (CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System, Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) using the QuantiNova SYBR Green PCR Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany, #208,056). Skeletal actin (Acta1) was used as a reference gene and did not vary between groups. The relative quantification was calculated using the ∆∆Ct method, and values were normalized to WT. Statistical analyses

Data are reported as mean ± standard error of the mean. To examine differences between BLA/J and WT mice, data were analyzed with unpaired two-tailed t-tests or two-way analysis of variance with Benjamini–Hochberg post hoc testing where appropriate. Statistical significance was determined a priori at P < 0.05.

To account for the non-independence of measurements arising from collection of four muscles from each mouse, a mouse-level identifier was generated to link muscle samples originating from the same animal. Briefly, the sample identifier string (formatted as Age_Sex_Genotype_Muscle) was parsed and a within-group index (_1, _2, …) was appended to distinguish biological replicates within each Age × Sex × Genotype × Muscle combination. This index was then used to construct a MouseID (Age_Sex_Genotype_index), allowing the four muscles with the same index to be treated as repeated measures from the same mouse. Age cohorts were analysed as independent groups (no pairing across ages was assumed). For each lipid class, genotype, muscle type, and age effects were tested using linear mixed-effects models (lme4). Models were fit by maximum likelihood with a random intercept for mouse to capture within-mouse correlation across muscle samples: Abundance ∼ Genotype + Muscle type + Age + (1∣MouseID).

Statistical significance of each fixed effect was assessed using likelihood ratio tests, comparing the full model to reduced models with the term of interest removed (Genotype, Muscle type, or Age). Where multiple lipid classes were evaluated, false discovery rate control (Benjamini–Hochberg) was applied across lipid classes separately for each effect family (Genotype, Muscle type, and Age), and adjusted q-values were used to define significance where indicated.

Results

Dysferlin-deficient muscles have a heterogeneous lipidomic profile

We assessed the lipidome of quadriceps, gastrocnemius, soleus and EDL muscles from BLA/J and WT mice (Fig. 1A). These muscles were selected based on their variable disease involvement and the age at which histopathology of lipid droplets is evident, i.e. quadriceps are affected at 10 months, gastrocnemius at 24 months, and soleus and EDL show minimal overt histopathology in mice [5, 6, 9, 10, 16, 27–29]. Using electrospray ionisation-tandem mass spectrometry, we detected five lipid categories: fatty acyls, glycolipids, glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids and sterols across 30 lipid classes and 738 lipid species (Fig. 1B).

Circle outlines indicate clusters corresponding to different muscle groups in the PCA plot (blue – quadriceps, grey – gastrocnemius, purple – soleus and pink – EDL)

To compare the lipidomic profiles of different muscle types in both healthy and dysferlin-deficient mice, we calculated the abundance of each lipid class normalised to the total lipid and performed a principal component analysis (PCA) (Fig. 1C). Not all muscles formed separate clusters, indicating similar lipid profiles between quadriceps and gastrocnemius across all ages. A notable difference was observed for the lipid profiles of soleus and EDL muscles, which showed more distinct clustering from all other muscle types. However, no distinct clustering was observed when comparing healthy and dysferlin-deficient mice for any of the four muscle types.

Dysferlinopathy is characterised by the accumulation of lipids within muscle. Therefore, we wanted to assess the overall abundance of all lipids in WT and dysferlin-deficient mice in the four muscle types with increasing age. The overall abundance of lipids varied across muscle types (Fig. 2A). During early pathology (3 months of age), the soleus contained the highest amount of total lipid relative to total PC, followed by the gastrocnemius, quadriceps and EDL. Compared to their younger counterparts, aging induced a dramatic increase in total lipids within quadriceps and gastrocnemius at 26 months of age, which was driven by glycerolipids, predominantly triglycerides (Fig. 2A-B).

Linear mixed-effects modelling revealed that muscle type was the dominant determinant of lipid class abundance (Table 2), with highly significant effects observed across nearly all lipid classes (q < 10⁻12 to < 10⁻⁸⁰). Genotype effects were more selective, primarily affecting neutral lipids, sphingolipids, and membrane phospholipids associated with membrane structure and remodeling. Age-dependent changes were observed for a subset of lipid classes, particularly ether-linked phospholipids and anionic phospholipids, indicating progressive remodeling of membrane lipid composition with aging.

Aged dysferlin-deficient mice display greater changes in the lipidome

To explore the dynamic changes in the lipidome over time, we examined the aging trajectory across mice aged 3, 10 and 26 months (Fig. 2A-B). At 3 months of age, there were minimal changes between genotypes, highlighting the lack of pronounced pathology in young mice (Fig. 2B). Greater changes were evident at 10 months of age, whereby, 15 lipid types were increased in the quadriceps (diglycerides (DG), cholesterol esters (CE), hexosylceramides (HexCer), gangliosides (GM), sphingomyelin (SM), phospholipids (PL), lysophospholipids (LPC)), followed by 11 in the gastrocnemius, 11 in the EDL and 4 in the soleus. Interestingly, LPC(O) was the only lipid to be consistently increased in all aged muscles (1–2.5 fold increase), which likely contributes indirectly to muscle membrane stability through its role in ether-phospholipid remodeling. Ether-linked phospholipids are enriched in tissues with high mechanical and metabolic demand, including skeletal muscle, because ether bonds confer increased membrane rigidity and oxidative resilience. The accumulation of LPC(O) in dysferlin-deficient muscle may therefore indicate ongoing membrane turnover, phospholipase activation, or altered ether-lipid homeostasis [30]. Given that LPCs can influence membrane curvature, promote membrane repair signalling, or destabilize bilayers depending on concentration [31, 32], elevated LPC and LPC(O) could sensitize dysferlin-deficient fibers to membrane fragility or aberrant calcium influx. Excess intracellular calcium activates calpain proteases, promotes mitochondrial dysfunction, and drives myofibre degeneration [33–35]. Therefore, lipid species capable of increasing membrane permeability, such as LPC, may exacerbate calcium-dependent pathology in dysferlin-deficient muscle.

Dysferlin deficiency is associated with increased intramyocellular lipid accumulation and adipocyte infiltration in affected skeletal muscle of patients [36, 37]. Skeletal muscle lipid droplets primarily consist of triglycerides and cholesterol esters, featuring a hydrophobic core of neutral lipids. The largest changes in these lipid species were found in the quadriceps and gastrocnemius, specifically the dysferlin-deficient quadriceps contained the highest level of triglyceride relative to PC (quadriceps: 66.0%, gastrocnemius: 65.4%, soleus: 45.7%, EDL: 22.6%, of total lipid) at 26 months of age (Fig. 3). Of the 131 triglyceride lipid species assessed (Fig. 4), all were significantly increased within the BLA/J quadriceps, ranging from 3–19-fold increase, with the greatest increase being in unsaturated lipid species compared to WT mice. Comparatively, only one triglyceride species was increased in the gastrocnemius of BLA/J compared with WT mice, while 12 were decreased in the soleus and no differences were observed in the EDL (Fig. 4). These findings indicate a clear difference in the subcellular distribution of lipid droplets within different muscle types.

Skeletal muscle triglycerides can be hydrolysed into free fatty acid (FFA), and excessive accumulation of FFAs can lead to lipotoxicity that is linked to muscle damage and impaired regeneration [38]. While FFA content varied across muscle types in both strains, the BLA/J quadriceps was the only muscle to have increased FFA content at 26 months (1.5-fold increase compared to WT) (Fig. 3). Given that FFAs act as bioactive lipid intermediates capable of modulating inflammatory and stress-related signalling pathways, a ~ 50% increase is likely to represent a biologically meaningful shift in lipid homeostasis, although lipidomic measurements alone cannot determine the downstream functional consequences. This indicates that dysferlin-deficient quadriceps is most likely a valuable tissue to examine the deleterious effects of lipotoxicity during disease progression. However, while altered lipid composition may imply changes in fatty acid utilization or mitochondrial function, lipidomic profiles alone cannot distinguish impaired oxidation from adaptive shifts in lipid synthesis and remodelling. Consistent with this limitation, we previously demonstrated using radiometric tracer studies that dysferlin-deficient soleus muscle exhibits increased incorporation of fatty acids into triglyceride stores without a corresponding change in fatty acid oxidation [21]. Together, these findings suggest that lipid accumulation in dysferlin-deficient muscle may reflect altered lipid partitioning toward storage rather than impaired oxidative capacity.

Lipotoxicity is also linked to the accumulation of certain lipid intermediates, particularly diglycerides and ceramides. Coinciding with our previous findings [21], we observed a significant increase in almost all diglyceride species (23/24 (95.8%)) in BLA/J mice at 10 months of age (Fig. 3), followed by a significant increase in all diglyceride species at 26 months of age. In these same mice, 29/45 (64.4%) ceramide species were significantly increased in BLA/J compared with WT at 26 months of age. Together, BLA/J mice displayed lipotoxic remodelling in aged mice.

It is still unclear how dysferlin deficiency initially results in the severe pathology of only specific muscles. As previously reported, the soleus and EDL show minimal overt histopathology, even in older mice [16, 26]. Of interest, muscles composed of slow-twitch oxidative fibers (predominately in the soleus) contain more total lipid than glycolytic fibers (predominately in the EDL) [34, 35]. Indeed, the slow-twitch oxidative soleus muscle had higher overall lipid content in 16 lipid species compared to the glycolytic EDL muscle in both BLA/J and WT mice (Fig. 3A-B). However, only three lipid classes were increased in BLA/J soleus, compared to WT mice (TG, PG, LPC(O)). This indicates that while lipid content is higher in the dysferlin-deficient soleus, it undergoes less lipid remodelling than in the EDL.

Molecular remodelling of BLA/J quadriceps

We further interrogated our lipid species analysis in all muscle types at 26 months of age (Fig. 4). Quadriceps muscle had the greatest remodelling of lipid species, followed by the gastrocnemius, soleus and EDL. Skeletal muscle membrane fluidity is an important factor in membrane function and is determined by the phospholipid species and fatty acyl saturation [39]. Interestingly, the quadriceps was predominantly remodelled by unsaturated lipid species: TG (50:1, 50:2, 50:3, 50:4, 52:2, 52:3, 52:5, 54:2, 54:3, 54:4, 54:5), DG (18:1/18:1), CE(18:1), Cer (18:1/16:0), SM (42:3), HexCer (18:1/24:0), Hex2Cer (18:1/18:0), PC (15:1/18:2), PE (P-18:1/18:2), PE (O-34:1, 36:3), PG (34:1), PS (36:1) and LPE (22:6) (Fig. 4). It is possible that the increased fluidity by unsaturated phospholipids (PC, PE, PG, PS, LPE) could influence sarcolemmal stability and potentially contribute to membrane damage in dysferlin-deficient muscles.

Since the most extensive changes occurred in the quadriceps muscle, we assessed the expression of genes encoding regulatory proteins of lipid metabolism (Fig. 5A). Across both genotypes, no differences were found in the quadriceps from young mice (3 months of age). However, there was a clear age-related increase in genes associated with triglyceride metabolism and fatty acid oxidation, which were exacerbated in BLA/J mice. Of interest, genes encoding Cd36, a fatty acid uptake transporter, and Dgat2, an enzyme regulating the terminal step in triglyceride synthesis, were upregulated in BLA/J mice aged 10 and 26 months compared with age-matched WT mice (Fig. 5A). This may explain the increases observed in triglycerides in aged mice. Noteworthy increases also occurred in genes encoding enzymes of triglyceride hydrolysis, including Pnpla2 (encoding ATGL) and Lipe (encoding HSL), other proteins that regulate triglyceride metabolism at the surface of lipid droplets (e.g., Plin2 and Plin5), and oxidative genes Cpt1 and Pdk4. Additionally, we examined genes encoding sphingolipid and glycerolphospholipid metabolism. Age-related increases were only observed in BLA/J mice, specifically Cerk, which is a crucial lipid-modifying enzyme that phosphorylates ceramide into ceramide-1-phosphate, was significantly increased. In addition, we observed an increase in Ptssd1, which catalyses the conversion of phosphatidylserine to phosphatidylethanolamine. Together, this indicates that BLA/J mice have age-specific increases in genes regulating triglyceride, sphingolipid and glycerophospholipid metabolism (Fig. 5B).

To place the observed lipidomic changes into a broader biological context, we performed lipid ontology and pathway enrichment analyses on significantly altered lipid species (Fig. 5C). Lipid ontology enrichment revealed a strong over-representation of neutral glycerolipids, including triacylglycerols and diacylglycerols, as well as terms associated with lipid storage and lipid droplets, indicating coordinated remodeling of lipid storage pathways (Fig. 5C). In addition, enrichment of glycerolipid headgroups with neutral charge suggests a shift toward lipid classes that influence membrane physicochemical properties. Several sphingolipid-related categories were also enriched, including hexosylceramides and ceramide phosphocholines (sphingomyelins), consistent with altered sphingolipid metabolism.

Pathway-level analysis further supported these findings, identifying significant enrichment of sphingolipid metabolism, phospholipid remodeling (Lands’ cycle), and fatty acid desaturation and elongation pathways (Fig. 5D). Directionality analysis showed that these pathways were predominantly decreased in BLA/J mice, indicating an overall reduction in lipid species involved in membrane remodeling and long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acid metabolism. Notably, pathways related to polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) enrichment (AA-like pools) and fatty acid elongation exhibited the largest magnitude shifts, suggesting altered acyl chain composition and membrane lipid remodeling. Consistent with these findings, stratification of lipid species by total double-bond content demonstrated a shift toward lower unsaturation, reflecting reduced abundance of highly unsaturated lipid species (Fig. 5E). This concordant decrease in acyl chain unsaturation supports the pathway-level inference of diminished PUFA enrichment and associated elongation/desaturation processes. Consistent with this, stratification of PC species by total double-bond content revealed a shift toward lower unsaturation, indicating a relative reduction in highly unsaturated PC species (Fig. 5E). These findings suggest that dysferlin-deficiency is associated with altered PC acyl chain composition, potentially reflecting modified phospholipid remodeling or fatty acid desaturation processes. Collectively, these ontology- and pathway-based analyses provide biological context for the lipidomic alterations and point to changes in membrane phospholipid composition in dysferlin-deficient muscle.

Lipid content is comparable between male and female dysferlin-deficient mice

Previous murine studies using Oil red O staining of neutral lipids indicate that female BLA/J mice have more severe lipid accumulation compared with male BLA/J mice [16], indicating a more severe muscle pathology. We repeated our in-depth lipidomic analysis in a smaller subset of female muscle samples, consisting of quadriceps from mice aged 3 and 10 months of age (note that muscle was not available from female mice aged 26 months). PCA showed little clustering at 3 months of age (Fig. 6A), with greater separation of genotypes and sex at 10 months of age. In contrast to previous findings [16], total lipid content was not different between female and male BLA/J mice (Fig. 6B), with only two lipid types significantly different, methyl-CE and AC at 3 and 10 months respectively (Fig. 6C). While our results show minimal sex differences, sex-specific divergence of the lipidome may have occurred in aged mice. Overall, our dysferlin-deficient aging lipidome atlas clearly demonstrates lipid diversity and its unique profiles across muscle types and sex.

Discussion

In this study, we provide an extensive lipidomic catalogue of dysferlin-deficient muscles, across three ages and four muscle types. Our observations broadly demonstrate that dysferlin deficiency perturbs lipid composition and contributes to the histopathological changes seen in steatotic muscles. MRI studies in human dysferlinopathy patients show that muscle fat fraction increases significantly with time after symptomatic onset and even more so in non-ambulant patients [37]. Cross-sectional muscle MRI studies revealed a characteristic pattern of muscle-type involvement and asymmetry in the severity of muscle pathology, where all symptomatic patients had at least one affected posterior lower leg muscle [23]. While disease duration correlated with the degree of fat replacement in all muscles, the earliest and most affected lower-leg muscles were the gastrocnemius and soleus (quadriceps analysed independently with upper-leg muscles), warranting further analysis of muscle-type asymmetry seen in dysferlinopathy.

Human skeletal muscle consists of heterogeneous muscle fibers, which are typically classified into three fiber types: I, IIa, and IIx (four fiber types in mice including type IIb) [40]. In patients with advanced-stage dysferlinopathy, a predominance of Type I fibers has been observed, suggesting a selective loss of Type II fibers, or a process of conversions of Type II fibers into Type I [12]. Previous functional studies in middle aged BLA/J mice (aged 10 months), when the pathology has manifested, showed a differential impact of dysferlin deficiency on slow soleus muscles (relying more on oxidative lipid metabolism), compared with fast EDL muscles (primarily using glucose as a fuel via glycolytic metabolism) [41], with different calcium and metabolic signalling between strains [42]. These functional myofiber type-dependent differences between the predominantly slow soleus and fast EDL dysferlin-deficient BLA/J muscles provided a strong rationale to examine lipidomic changes for these specific muscles.

Consistent with these functional distinctions, a notable separation was observed in the lipidomic clustering of soleus and EDL muscles compared with the other muscle types, highlighting intrinsic metabolic and compositional differences between slow-oxidative and fast-glycolytic muscles. Although the soleus had fewer alterations in lipid species, it had more than double the number of triglycerides than the EDL, suggesting that fat accumulation can decrease functional capacity. Indeed, many studies have demonstrated that fatty infiltration (i.e. the presence of many adipocytes that can be sparse or in large clumps) is associated with reduced force production, likely due to the reduced contractile tissue within the muscle volume [43–46]. Likewise, at the individual myofiber level, intramyocellular lipid droplets (steatosis) also results in impaired functions of individual myofibers, likely due to the relatively reduced proportion of contractile filaments in affected myofibers [47].

The soleus’ primary function is postural support and is engaged in more sustained low-intensity contractions, compared to more rapid and powerful movements like the quadriceps and gastrocnemius. It could be suggested that lower intensity contractions in the soleus might lead to less membrane damage and lipid alterations than other muscles. Indeed, the quadriceps had the highest amount of lipid content, followed by the gastrocnemius, soleus and EDL. This may coincide with dysferlin expression levels, where it is highly expressed in the quadriceps and gastrocnemius [16], followed by the soleus (EDL was not measured in this study). Interestingly, other studies have shown that the quadriceps and gastrocnemius exhibit higher levels of lipid peroxidation, protein carbonyls and superoxide dismutase [48], which coincides with our observations of increased oxidised lipid species.

Previous MRI studies conducted in BLA/J mice demonstrate increased fat infiltration and reduced muscle volume by 9 months of age, followed by progressive motor decline by 12 months [6]. However, the mechanisms underlying lipid accumulation in dysferlin-deficient muscle remain unclear. In the present study, we identified significant age-related alterations in the muscle lipidome. Notably, although prior work has reported that calcium handling abnormalities and impaired Ca2⁺-dependent membrane repair in dysferlin-deficient muscle as early as 3 months of age [49], we did not detect major changes in the global lipidome at this early timepoint. These findings suggest that large-scale lipid remodeling may not represent a primary initiating event in dysferlinopathy. Rather, early pathology may be driven predominantly by defective membrane repair and calcium dysregulation, with lipidomic alterations emerging as a secondary consequence of cumulative membrane instability, inflammation, or progressive degeneration. Alternatively, localized lipid perturbations at the sarcolemma or within membrane repair vesicles may not be detectable using whole muscle lipidomics approaches.

Consistent with this possibility, alterations in membrane lipid composition can influence muscle structure and viscoelastic properties [50]. While no differences in mechanical properties were observed in BLA/J quadriceps at 3 months (including the male 3- and 10-month samples analyzed here, which were also included in [50]), age-related exacerbation of lipid remodeling and associated changes in membrane fluidity may contribute to the mechanical alterations observed in BLA/J quadriceps by 24 months [50]. Together, these findings refine the temporal sequence of pathological events in dysferlinopathy and support a model in which lipid dysregulation contributes to disease progression rather than disease initiation.

Previous studies in patients with a range of Limb Girdle Muscular Dystrophies including dysferlinopathy (i.e. LGMDR2 dysferlin-related), have shown that males have higher myofiber atrophy [51]. This has been attributed to the possibility that women have a smaller initial muscle size associated with endocrine factors, which could attenuate sex-specific muscle loss. It is well known that testosterone and estrogens influence skeletal muscle homeostasis and metabolism. Sex differences in muscle damage induced by eccentric exercise have been reported in humans and animals, where the female hormones (17-β-oestradiol) were suggested to have a positive effect on muscle force generation by reducing muscle membrane damage [51]. MRI scans of dysferlinopathy patients show that females exhibit a higher pattern of fat replacement, which may be a protective mechanism against muscle damage [23]. However, in our study, we did not observe a clear sex difference in fat accumulation in BLA/J mice at the ages analysed, likely reflecting species-specific (mouse and human) differences and the timing of disease progression. In this mouse model, the onset and course of dysferlinopathy may mask early sex-specific differences in lipid accumulation. While aged dysferlin-deficient mice have shown greater lipid accumulation in female gastrocnemius and quadriceps compared with males, these effects are most pronounced at advanced ages (~ 95 weeks) rather than in young adults, indicating that sex differences may emerge later in disease progression [16]. Hormonal regulation of fat accumulation after muscle injury further underscores the influence of sex steroids on muscle lipid dynamics, but also highlights that such effects are context-dependent. Collectively, these observations suggest that, although sex differences are evident in human patients, they may not manifest under the age ranges examined here.

In summary, we found distinct changes in the lipidome of dysferlin-deficient mice, highlighting a drastic increase in lipids in aging and heterogeneous lipid profile across muscle types. Thus, our results serve as an important resource, providing new insights into the potential link between lipid metabolism and aging. The biological importance of lipids described in this study will be further investigated, and other layers of omics data, such as proteomics, are required to deepen our understanding of the pathological changes in dysferlinopathy.
