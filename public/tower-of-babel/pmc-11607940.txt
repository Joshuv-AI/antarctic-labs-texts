Clinical efficacy on the reconstruction of bone defects using modular hemipelvic prosthesis in patients with pelvic tumors
Authors: Yuanrui Luo, Hongtao Sheng, Yong Zhou, Li Min, Chongqi Tu, Yi Luo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the clinical efficacy of modular hemipelvic prosthesis replacement in the treatment of pelvic tumors and the relationship between postoperative functional recovery and the position deviation of the hip rotation center.MethodsA retrospective research was conducted involving 56 patients who underwent surgical resection of pelvic tumors followed by modular hemipelvic prosthesis reconstruction between 2010 and 2016. Comprehensive data encompassing preoperative conditions, intraoperative and postoperative follow-up information were systematically collected. Limb function was evaluated at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively using the Musculoskeletal Tumor Society Scores (MSTS). The positional deviation of the hip joint’s rotational center was quantified. Statistical analyses were performed utilizing SPSS 26.0 software.ResultsAll surgical interventions were successful, with an average operation time of 268.82 ± 42.17 min and mean intraoperative blood loss of 1086.64 ± 538.28 ml. Postoperative complications were noted in 26.79% of patients, predominantly comprising incision-related issues. Hip dislocation was documented in 5.4% of cases, while seven patients experienced prosthesis loosening or fracture during follow-up. A statistically significant improvement in MSTS scores was observed, increasing from an average of 14.3 at 1 month to 21.1 at 12 months postoperatively. The recorded minor deviations in the position of the prosthetic hip joint’s rotational center did not exhibit a significant correlation with functional outcomes. The observed survival rates were 94.64%, 83.93%, 73.21%, and 48.21% at 1, 2, 3, and 5 years, respectively.ConclusionsThe modular hemipelvic prosthesis serves as an effective reconstructive solution for bone defects resulting from pelvic tumor resection, facilitating early functional rehabilitation. The prosthesis demonstrates commendable adaptability and mechanical properties, with a low incidence of complications such as hip dislocation and wound-related issues, thereby contributing to favorable functional recovery of the affected limb.Keywords: Modular hemipelvic prosthesis, Pelvic tumors, Limb function, Hip joint rotational center, Postoperative complications

Introduction

The pelvis is primarily composed of cancellous bone [1], making it susceptible to malignant tumors that can easily breach the cortical bone and invade surrounding soft tissues. Similarly, soft tissue tumors can readily erode the pelvic bone. Generally, pelvic tumors present insidiously, with non-specific symptoms, making early diagnosis challenging. By the time of detection, these tumors are often large, involving adjacent blood vessels, pelvic organs, and nerves, complicating surgical treatment and increasing postoperative complications. Approximately 10–15% of primary malignant bone tumors occur in the pelvis, with common sarcomas including chondrosarcoma, osteosarcoma, and Ewing’s sarcoma, which often invade critical anatomical structures, making surgical resection extremely challenging [2].

For patients with pelvic malignancies, the overall goal of treatment is not only to achieve oncological control but also to restore pelvic stability and function while minimizing complications and preventing tumor recurrence. With advancements in chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and material manufacturing research, surgical treatment for malignant pelvic tumors has evolved from hemipelvectomy to limb-sparing approaches. Function-preserving reconstruction following the resection of malignant tumors involving the acetabulum has become a primary treatment method, and the objectives of reconstruction are precise to preserve acetabular function, maintain pelvic ring integrity(which is essential for proper load distribution, overall biomechanical function, optimizing functional outcomes and improving the patient’s quality of life following extensive surgical intervention), and restore limb length.

Standard limb-sparing methods include femoral head ostectomy, autologous tumor bone inactivation and reimplantation, allogeneic hemipelvic transplantation, saddle prosthesis replacement, ice cone prosthesis replacement, customized hemipelvic prosthesis replacement, and modular hemipelvic prosthesis replacement [3–8]. Different limb-sparing methods yield varying postoperative outcomes. Tumor bone inactivation replantation is characterized by suitable shape, moderate size, good intraoperative matching, compatibility, and low infection rate [9, 10]. However, due to the requirement that inactivated bone has a certain bone strength to play a supporting role, it is limited to osteogenic or mixed tumors, and the local recurrence rate may be high due to insufficient inactivation of tumor cells, leading to its clinical application being significantly limited [11, 12]. Although the local recurrence rate of using allograft bone to reconstruct the bone defects after hemipelvectomy is low [5, 13, 14], it has a higher incidence of postoperative complications, including wound infection, delayed wound healing, and, particularly in late stages, wound exudation, deep infection, bone resorption, and rejection reactions. Fractures may also occur, and some patients may develop sinus tract formation or non-healing wounds. Additionally, due to its rarity, its clinical application remains limited [15–17]. Saddle prosthesis is the first artificial hemipelvic prosthesis and is used primarily in foreign countries, but it requires a residual part of the iliac crest as the base for its fixation, so it cannot be used for the reconstruction of malignant pelvic tumors invading zone IV. Moreover, its obvious cutting effect on the iliac crest can cause iliac crest fracture and sacroiliac joint subluxation. Ice cone prosthesis offers the advantage of ease of use; however, they necessitate the retention of a large amount of ilium during surgery for secure fixation, which limits their application primarily to pelvic tumors surrounding the acetabulum, making them less versatile.

Currently, the most commonly used reconstruction methods for hemipelvic bone defects are customized hemipelvic prosthesis replacement and modular hemipelvic prosthesis replacement. The customized prosthesis is designed and manufactured based on personalized specifications; it aligns with the anatomical shape of the pelvis, ensuring a good fit and firm early fixation. Following the preoperative plan saves operative time, reduces intraoperative bleeding, and results in good postoperative function. However, due to an excessive emphasis on making the prosthesis resemble a healthy pelvis, the prosthesis may become too large, leading to excessive soft tissue destruction. Postoperatively, there is often less soft tissue around the prosthesis, resulting in insufficient prosthesis coverage and a higher wound infection rate. Additionally, discrepancies between the actual surgical resection and the preoperative plan can lead to prosthesis mismatching and surgical failure; also, careful preoperative planning and preparation of customized prostheses may delay patient treatment and impose a greater financial burden.

The flexibility of modular hemipelvic prosthesis allows for intraoperative assembly even in cases when extensive iliac resection is required [18, 19]. Its comparatively smaller size enables greater soft tissue coverage during surgery, eliminating dead space and permitting stronger muscle reconstruction, which promotes hip joint function rehabilitation and presumably minimizes the incidence of postoperative wound infections. After resecting pelvic tumors, varying degrees of pelvic ring defects occur, making intraoperative positioning of the acetabulum challenging. Different degrees of iliac bone defects can lead to varying positional deviation, resulting in a shift of the hip joint’s rotational center. Although some studies have reported cases of using modular hemipelvic prostheses to reconstruct bone defects following the resection of pelvic tumors(Table 1), there has been no research on the extent of rotational center deviation of the prosthetic hip joint after modular hemipelvic prosthesis replacement and the relationship between this deviation and postoperative function.

This retrospective study reviews cases from 2010 to 2016 at our hospital, whose pelvic tumor was resected and modular hemipelvic prosthesis reconstruction was performed. We analyzed tumor characteristics, postoperative complications, functional outcomes, tumor prognosis, and limb function at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after surgery in all patients. Furthermore, we investigated the position of the rotational center of the prosthetic hip joint and its correlation with postoperative function.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

This retrospective study included patients who underwent surgical resection for pelvic tumors at our hospital between 2010 and 2016.

Inclusion criteria

(1) Postoperative pathological diagnosis was confirmed; (2) Patients with tumors originating in the pelvic region who underwent modular hemipelvic prosthesis reconstruction; (3) Complete records, including perioperative auxiliary examinations and surgical records; (4) Standardized postoperative care was followed; (5) Follow-up data was available, with complete records of limb function at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Patients who underwent modular hemipelvic prosthesis reconstruction combined with other reconstruction methods or those who underwent other reconstruction methods alone; (2) Patients lacking postoperative anteroposterior pelvic X-rays or three-dimensional CT scans; (3) Patients who died within 6 months postoperatively.

Tumor classification and staging

Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 60 patients were initially collected, with 56 meeting the study criteria. Among them were 33 males and 23 females, aged 12 to 73 years, with an average age of 39.68. The follow-up period ranged from 12 to 86 months, with an average follow-up of 36.36 ± 20.40 months. The body mass index (BMI) ranged from 17.6 to 25.9, with an average BMI of 23.50.

The distribution of tumor pathology types was as follows: 19 cases of osteosarcoma, 12 cases of chondrosarcoma (3 of which were recurrences), 8 cases of malignant giant cell tumor of bone (all were recurrences), 4 cases of plasmacytoma, 3 cases of leiomyosarcoma, 1 case of renal clear cell carcinoma, 1 case of malignant paraganglioma, 1 case of metastatic squamous cell carcinoma, 1 case of malignant melanoma, 1 case of synovial sarcoma, 1 case of angiosarcoma, 1 case of chondroblastoma, 1 case of aggressive osteoblastoma with aneurysmal bone cyst, 1 case of epithelioid sarcoma, and 1 case of malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor.

According to Enneking’s staging system, there were 15 cases in stage IB, 4 in stage IIA, and 37 in stage IIB. Based on Enneking’s pelvic tumor location classification, 21 cases were in Zones II + I, 16 in Zone II, 9 in Zones II + I + III, and 10 in Zones II + III (Table 2).

Surgical procedure

All surgeries were conducted by the same senior orthopedic surgeon, with patients positioned in the lateral decubitus position. Utilizing the Kocher-Langenbeck and Smith-Petersen approaches, optionally extended inguinally [23], emphasis was placed on preserving the hip joint muscles and their attachment points. Osteotomy was performed to ensure sufficient tumor margins, taking into account the specific clinical scenario. The prosthesis was implanted and secured with multiple screws according to the preoperative plan. A suitable CS fixator was selected, with screws affixed to the remaining sacroiliac bones. Following pelvic reconstruction, the polyethylene liner angle within the acetabulum was adjusted appropriately to 5°-10° and fixed with bone cement. The acetabulum position was verified using C-arm fluoroscopy, with an abduction angle of 45° and an anteversion angle of 15°-25°, making necessary adjustments to the polyethylene cup angle. In cases where the tumor involved Zone III of the pelvis, an appropriate length of the pubic plate was used for reconstruction. Finally, the femoral head and neck prostheses were installed, and hip joint reduction was performed. The remaining muscles around the hip joint and their attachment points were subsequently sutured onto the prosthesis (Figs. 1 and 2). Intraoperative blood loss was directly estimated by recording the volume of blood collected by suction devices and measuring the blood absorbed by sponges and gauze.

Postoperative management and functional exercise

To minimize hematoma and deep tissue infection risks, drainage tubes were removed if the drainage volume was less than 50 ml within 24 h. Anti-thrombotic stockings and calf compression devices were routinely employed to prevent deep vein thrombosis, with chemical thromboprophylaxis regimens determined by postoperative coagulation function test. Patients received third-generation cephalosporin for 1 to 3 days. They were advised to maintain bed rest for 8 to 10 weeks to facilitate the healing of soft tissue trauma.

Follow-up and observation indicators

Follow-up examinations included pelvic radiographs, chest radiographs, three-dimensional pelvic CT, chest CT, and PET/CT as needed. Standard chemotherapy was initiated one month after surgery. During the first six months, patients were reexamined monthly to monitor for postoperative complications, guide functional exercises, and provide information on adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy. From six to twelve months, follow-up was every 2 to 3 months to assess functional recovery and gait correction and to monitor for tumor recurrence, metastasis, and prosthesis-related issues. After one year, follow-up was conducted every 3–6 months, depending on the patient’s condition. Patients unable to attend outpatient visits were followed up by phone or mail.

Measurement of pelvic anteroposterior X-ray data

A standard anteroposterior pelvic X-ray of the patient was selected, and relevant anatomical data were measured (Fig. 3). Specific measurement steps: (1) Determine the circle’s center. The femoral head is ideal for a sphere, and the “double line method” is used to determine the rotation center; 2 lines are selected around the femoral head arc to make the middle vertical line. The circle’s center is the intersection point of the two middle vertical lines (O1, O2) (2). Determine the bilateral axis of symmetry of the pelvis. Make the tangential line through the bilateral ischium (either through the bilateral obturator foramen, or the upper margin of the bilateral ischium magnum, etc., L1 in the figure), and make the vertical line through the symphysis pubis or the center of the sacrum (L2 in the figure) (3). Femoral Offset (FO) was measured. FO refers to the vertical distance from the femoral head’s center to the femoral shaft’s midline (F1O1, F2O2) (4). Abductor Lever Arm (ALA): Perpendicular line distance from the center of the femoral head to the tangent of the greater trochanter (O1A1, O2A2) (5). Rotation Center Offset (RCO), which refers to the shifting distance of the circular center of the prosthetic femoral head relative to the healthy side in the upward, downward, inward, and outward directions (O1B1, O2B2).

Schematic diagram of the measurement of pelvic anteroposterior X-ray data

In this study, hip rotation center deviation within 10 mm of ideal anatomic position after hemipelvic prosthesis replacement was considered as standard reconstruction. According to the horizontal deviation of the hip rotation center, they were divided into inward-shifting group (the center of hip rotation of the prosthesis was shifted inwards > 10 mm), normal group (the center of hip rotation of the prosthesis was shifted inwards or outwards ≤ 10 mm) and outward-shifting group (the center of rotation of the prosthesis was shifted outwards > 10 mm). Similarly, in the vertical direction of this study, they were divided into an upward-shifting group (the center of hip rotation of the prosthesis was shifted upwards > 10 mm), a normal group (the center of hip rotation of the prosthesis was shifted upwards or downwards ≤ 10 mm) and a downward-shifting group (the center of hip rotation of the prosthesis was shifted downwards > 10 mm). According to the groups, in the horizontal direction, 16 cases were divided into the inward-shifting group, 36 cases in the normal group, and 4 cases in the outward-shifting group. In the vertical direction, there were 13 cases in the upward-shifting group, 37 in the normal group, and 4 in the downward-shifting group.

Data collection and statistical analysis

SPSS software (version 26.0, Chicago, IL, USA) was used, measurement data were expressed as “mean ± standard deviation,” independent sample t-test or one-way ANOVA was used to compare the differences between groups, and the Kaplan-Meier method was used for survival analysis. Inspection level: P < 0.05, the difference was statistically significant.

Results

The operations were successful for all patients, and their postoperative vital signs remained stable. The operation duration was (179.0-348.0) minutes, with an average of (268.82 ± 42.17) minutes. The intraoperative blood loss was (420.0-3370.0) ml, with an average of (1086.64 ± 538.28) ml. The postoperative blood transfusion was (0.0-2800.0) ml. The average length of hospital stay was (15.18 ± 3.75) days, ranging from 10.0 to 26.0 days (Table 3).

Postoperative complication

Short-term postoperative complications occurred in 15 cases (26.78%), and incision-related complications occurred in 12 cases (21.42%). Cutaneous margin necrosis occurred in 7 cases, with obvious wound exudation, and the wounds healed after dressing changes. Five cases experienced wound dehiscence with marginal necrosis and serosanguinous discharge; the wounds healed after debridement, negative pressure wound therapy, and prophylactic antibiotic treatment.

In this group, a total of 3 patients had hip dislocation on the affected side after the operation (Fig. 4), and the incidence was 5.4%. Among them, 2 cases occurred within 1 month after the operation and were successfully reduced by manipulation. One case underwent open reduction because the dislocation occurred 2 ~ 3 months after surgery, the soft tissue around the prosthesis was tightly wrapped, and manual reduction failed. During long-term follow-up, seven patients were found to have aseptic loosening or fracture of the prosthesis, all of whom had undergone revision surgery using 3D-printed customized hemipelvic endoprostheses; the median follow-up duration between primary and revision surgery was 36 months (range 16–73 months) (Table 4).

Postoperative function evaluation

Hip joint motion on the affected side was mostly measured 6 to 12 months after surgery. Hip flexion in this group was 70 to 101°, with an average of (84.21 ± 5.39)°. Posterior extension: 5 ~ 31°, average (18.92 ± 6.85)°; Abduction: 19 ~ 61°, average (38.96 ± 10.21)°. Fifteen patients (26.79%) walked without support, 33 patients (58.93%) walked with crutches or single crutches, and eight patients (14.29%) walked with double crutches or walkers. In addition to 8 patients, the other 48 patients had different degrees of abnormal gait. Among the 56 patients, the MSTS score 1 month after surgery was 11 ~ 17, with an average of 14.3, and no patients with excellent or good function. MSTS score 3 months after operation: 17–25, average 20.1, whose excellent and good rate reached 100%; MSTS score at 6 months after surgery: 15–28, average 20.8, whose excellent and good rate reached 97.6%, one of the patients had a local recurrence 5 months after surgery, whose pain in the affected limb increased, and the MSTS score was 15; MSTS score 12 months after operation: 19 ~ 28, average 21.1, whose excellent and good rate reached 100%. The function of the affected limb gradually recovered at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months after surgery, and the MSTS score increased. There were significant differences in MSTS functional scores at 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months after operation (P < 0.05). The functional improvement was not significant between 6 months and 12 months after surgery, and the MSTS score was nearly the same (P > 0.05) (Table 5).

Position of hip joint rotation center and postoperative function

In this group, the anteroposterior X-ray data of the patients showed that the FO of the healthy side was 20.1 ~ 54.7 mm, with an average of 38.4 mm. In contrast, the FO of the affected side was 15.5 ~ 47.7 mm, with an average of 30.1 mm, and the affected side was 8.3 mm shorter than the healthy side. The ALA of the healthy side was 35.7 ~ 76.5 mm (mean 56.1 mm), the affected side was 41.5 ~ 71.7 mm (mean 57.8 mm), and the affected side was 1.7 mm longer than the healthy side. In the vertical direction, the rotation center of the femoral head shifted from 19.0 mm downward to 20.1 mm upward, with an average upward deviation of 2.83 mm. In the horizontal direction, the deviation ranges from 33.3 mm inward to 14.3 mm outward, with an average inward deviation of 5.2 mm.

According to the horizontal shifting of the hip joint rotation center of the affected side relative to the healthy side, the patients were divided into the interward-shifting, normal, and outward-shifting groups. The MSTS scores at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months post-surgery showed no statistically significant variance among the three groups (P > 0.05). Similarly, patients were divided into upward-shifting, normal, and downward-shifting groups based on the vertical shifting of the hip joint rotation center on the affected side relative to the healthy side; compared among the three groups, there was no statistical difference in MSTS scores at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after surgery (P > 0.05) (Table 5).

Oncology prognosis

The follow-up period for the 56 patients ranged from 12 to 86 months, with an average of 36.36 ± 20.40 months. The 1-year survival rate was 94.64%, 2-year survival rate was 83.93%, 3-year survival rate was 73.21%, and 5-year survival rate was 48.21%. After surgery, two cases of giant cell tumor of bone with bilateral lung metastasis (without local recurrence), three cases of osteosarcoma, and one case of chondrosarcoma with lung metastasis were treated with adjuvant radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Additionally, one case of local recurrence of chondrosarcoma received local radiotherapy. By the end of follow-up, a total of 30 patients had died, all due to distant metastasis after tumor surgery (Fig. 5).

Discussion

One of the significant advantages of modular hemi-pelvic prosthesis is that it does not require preoperative customization, and its main bearing components are thickened and reinforced. It primarily consists of an internal spinal fixator, an acetabular cup, a pubic plate, and an ischiatic branch. Each component has various types of accessories, making it easy to adjust and assemble during surgery, thus offering strong operability and ease of installation. The proximal end is fixed to the remaining iliac bone, the lower margin of the sacroiliac joint, or the auricular plane of the sacrum, which increases compressive stress on the prosthesis while reducing shear force and eccentric moments, resulting in a firm fixation and reducing the likelihood of postoperative loosening and fracture. The finite element analysis of previous research demonstrated the modular hemipelvic prosthesis’ good adaptability and mechanical conductivity by comparing stress distributions between an implanted pelvis and a healthy pelvis under simulated bilateral standing loads: the stress patterns in critical regions, such as the sacroiliac joints, iliac crest, and prosthesis connection points, were similar in both models of the research, indicating that the prosthesis maintained the biomechanical integrity of the pelvis. Even with deviations of the hip joint’s rotational center of less than 10 mm, the stress distribution remained stable, confirming the prosthesis’ compatibility and effective mechanical load transfer, thus reducing the risk of mechanical failure [26].

Additionally, the prosthesis is of moderate size, ensuring sufficient soft tissue and muscle coverage during surgery, leading to a low postoperative infection rate and minimizing the occurrence of skin necrosis and wound non-union. In modular hemipelvic prosthesis replacement, some studies have reported that the complications can be as high as 50 ~ 60% [27]. Early complications for patients primarily include wound bleeding, wound necrosis, wound infection, delayed wound healing, neurovascular injury, urinary system injury, venous embolism of the lower extremities, and hip dislocation [8, 21]. Late complications are mainly related to the loosening and fracture of the prosthesis. In this research, selective intraoperative low abdominal aortic balloon occlusion was applied to control bleeding. Intraoperative blood loss ranged from 420.0 to 3370.0 ml, averaging 1086.64 ml. Postoperative blood transfusion ranged from 0.0 to 2800.0 ml, averaging 542.40 ml. This approach significantly reduced intraoperative bleeding and greatly improved surgical safety; as a result, the surgical field can be fully exposed, and the operation time can be reduced, which is beneficial to tumor resection and reconstruction [28]. In our research, incision complications occurred in 12 cases (21.42%), and cutaneous margin necrosis and exudation were evident in 7 cases whose wounds healed after the dressing change. Five cases experienced wound dehiscence with marginal necrosis and serosanguinous discharge; the wounds healed after debridement, negative pressure wound therapy, and prophylactic antibiotic treatment.

Hip dislocation is prone to occur in the early stages following hemipelvic prosthesis replacement, typically within the first three months post-surgery. This is due to early-stage laxity of surrounding hip muscles and fascia, with no pseudocapsule encapsulation. Consequently, the joint cannot maintain normal tension, making hip dislocation more likely even under minimal stress. Therefore, attention should be paid to protecting the lower limb position in the early stages, and a hip saddle brace should be used when necessary. According to the study by Guo et al. [29], the incidence rate of hip dislocation was about 8.3% within six months post-operation. In our group, three patients experienced dislocation of the affected hip after surgery, with an incidence rate of 5.4%. Among these, 2 cases were successfully managed with manual reduction, while 1 case required open reduction due to the dislocation occurring 2 to 3 months post-surgery, where the soft tissue around the prosthesis was tightly wrapped, increasing tension, and manual reduction failed. Therefore, patients must learn to get out of bed, turn over, and perform functional exercises before discharge. Emphasis should be placed on maintaining proper posture, specifically keeping the hip in an abduction-neutral position. The hip joint should be securely stabilized with a brace, even during rest periods, until scar tissue forms around the hip and surrounding muscle strength is restored.

During long-term follow-up, seven patients were found to develop prosthesis loosening or fracture, all of whom had undergone revision surgery; the median follow-up duration between primary and revision surgery was 36 months (range 16 months–73 months). Thus, we emphasize that limiting the range of motion in all directions is crucial for a short time after surgery to maintain hip stability. Preserving the psoas major and gluteus maximus during surgery is also essential for maintaining the stability of the reconstructed hip. While short-term stability of the hemipelvic prosthesis can be achieved with screws, implant loosening is inevitable in the long term; however, the long-term stability of the implant can be maintained by the surrounding scar tissue.

Under normal conditions, patients are encouraged to begin functional exercises starting from the first day after surgery, mainly involving bed rest, quadriceps, and other muscle relaxation and contraction exercises. If the patient is unable to perform active exercises, a designated caregiver should assist with the exercises to prevent restrictions in hip joint activity due to adhesion of the perihip muscles. In this study, the range of motion of the hip joint of all patients was well maintained, and the functional recovery of the affected limb was satisfactory. The average MSTS score at 6 months post-surgery was 20.8, with an excellent and good rate of 97.6%. Twelve months after surgery, there was no significant change in limb function, with an average score of 21.1 and an excellent and good rate of 100%, which was not statistically significant compared to the 6-month results. Statistical comparison of MSTS scores at 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months post-surgery showed significant differences, indicating that within the first 6 months, functional recovery of the affected limb improved with overall recovery and increased functional exercise. From 6 months to 12 months post-surgery, functional recovery of the affected limb stabilized, with no significant change, highlighting the importance of early functional exercise.

Due to varying degrees of ilium bone defects, accurately positioning the prosthesis while constructing the pelvis with a modular hemipelvic prosthesis can be challenging. Currently, no related research has been reported on this issue. In this group of cases, the FO on the affected side was, on average, 8.3 mm shorter than on the healthy side. The ALA on the affected side was, on average, 1.7 mm longer than on the healthy side. To further analyze the relationship between the center of rotation and postoperative function, patients were categorized based on the horizontal deviation of the femoral head’s center of rotation relative to the ideal position: into the inward, normal, and outward shifting group; the shift ranged from 33.3 mm inward to 14.3 mm outward, with an average inward deviation of 5.2 mm. The MSTS scores at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively were compared across these three groups. No statistically significant differences were found between the groups (P > 0.05), suggesting that variations within this inward or outward deviation range did not significantly affect postoperative function. Similarly, in the vertical direction, patients were divided into upward, normal, and downward-shifting groups based on the deviation of the hip joint rotation center; the hip joint’s rotational center shifted upward by an average of 2.83 mm, with the displacement ranging from 19.0 mm downward to 20.1 mm upward. There were no significant differences in MSTS scores at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after surgery among the three groups (P > 0.05), indicating that vertical deviation within this range, whether upward or downward, had no significant impact on postoperative function. Furthermore, these positional deviations did not influence the incidence of postoperative complications or oncological outcomes.

Finally, the nature of the tumor and the extent of surgical resection directly influence the recurrence and metastasis of the tumor after surgery. Local recurrence rates following complete resection and reconstruction of pelvic tumors vary across studies, ranging from approximately 7.4–33%, with mortality rates between 40% and 75% [21, 30–33]. Many postoperative deaths are primarily attributed to tumor recurrence and metastasis. In this study, 56 patients were followed up for 12 to 86 months, with an average follow-up of 36.36 ± 20.40 months. By the end of the follow-up period, 30 patients had died, all due to distant metastasis after tumor surgery. Survival analysis revealed a 1-year survival rate of 94.64%, a 2-year survival rate of 83.93%, a 3-year survival rate of 73.21%, and a 5-year survival rate of 48.21%. This research supports Pring ME et al. [34] findings, which emphasize that extensive resection, when possible, is crucial for controlling local tumor recurrence. A multidisciplinary approach is essential to reduce complication rates and improve patients’ quality of life and overall survival. This approach includes collaboration between orthopedic surgeons, radiotherapists, medical oncologists, rehabilitation physicians, and other specialists, ensuring the most effective treatment plan is implemented. A recent study has shown that a multidisciplinary approach and navigation guidance can significantly reduce complication rates and improve surgical outcomes [35]. In particular, the use of navigation guidance in pelvic tumor surgeries has been demonstrated to lower the incidence of surgical errors, leading to fewer complications without compromising survival or recurrence rates. Furthermore, chemoradiotherapy and other comprehensive treatments, alongside meticulous surgical planning, have been shown to reduce complication rates, enhance functional outcomes, and lower the risk of recurrence and metastasis [36, 37].

The study presents several limitations that warrant careful consideration. Firstly, the relatively small sample size of 56 patients may compromise the generalizability of our findings and limit the statistical power necessary to draw robust conclusions regarding the efficacy of the modular hemipelvic prosthesis; future research should include more patients and consider separating sarcomas from other malignancies to understand better their specific prognostic factors, complications, and treatment outcomes. The absence of control groups also prevents a comparative analysis that could highlight this prosthetic design’s relative benefits or drawbacks against other treatment options. Furthermore, due to the nature of hemipelvic prosthesis replacement surgery, even though efforts are made to position the pelvis correctly during pelvic X-ray examinations, some degree of deviation may still occur, affecting the accuracy of measurements.

Conclusion

The modular semi-pelvic prosthesis provides stable fixation for reconstructing bone defects after tumor resection in the pelvis, allowing for early functional exercises. Due to the comprehensive coverage of perihip muscles, the incidence of postoperative complications is low, and functional recovery of the affected limb is favorable. Additionally, the prosthesis demonstrates excellent adaptability, effective mechanical integration with the reconstructed pelvis, and a reduced incidence of complications such as prosthesis loosening and fracture.
