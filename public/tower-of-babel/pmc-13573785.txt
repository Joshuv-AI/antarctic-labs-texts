Methodological and Statistical Concerns Regarding “Nanofiber Matrix Dressing for Split-Thickness Skin Graft Donor Sites” [Response To Letter]
Authors: Maciej Biskupski, Sergey Antonov, Zofia Małgorzata Górecka, Rafał Wójcik, Katarzyna Żuchowska, Marek Majewski, Kamil Torres
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We thank Dr He1 for his interest in our article and for the constructive comments regarding the methodology and statistical interpretation of our randomized comparative study.2 We appreciate the opportunity to clarify several aspects of the analysis and interpretation. We agree that studies should be interpreted carefully, particularly when sample sizes are modest and patient-reported outcomes are included. At the same time, we believe that several of the concerns raised can be addressed by clarifying the analytical approach, the nature of the outcomes, and the scope of the conclusions drawn from our data.

First, regarding the analysis of pain scores, we agree that 0–10 numeric rating scale data require careful handling because they are bounded and ordinal in nature.3 However, we would like to clarify that Student’s t-test was not used for the between-group comparisons of pain scores in our study.4 As stated in the Methods section, the choice of statistical test was based on the type of variable and distributional assessment, including evaluation of normality with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed continuous variables, whereas non-normally distributed or ordinal variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Accordingly, the pain-score comparisons were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U-test, not a parametric t-test. Therefore, our interpretation of the early pain results does not depend on assuming that pain scores were normally distributed interval-level data. Pain was also presented using distribution-oriented descriptive statistics in the tables, including medians, minimum and maximum values, and standard deviations. The relevant finding was a consistent and clinically coherent between-group difference in early donor-site pain, most evident on postoperative days 1, 2, and 5. We agree that reporting additional distributional details, such as interquartile ranges or frequency distributions, could further improve transparency in future studies, but the available summary data already support the conclusion that early pain intensity was lower in the nanofiber matrix group than in the conventional dressing group.

Second, Dr He questioned the postoperative day 14 pain pattern, particularly the value of 0.00 ± 0.00 in the control group. We acknowledge that complete absence of variability can appear unusual in some clinical datasets; however, in this specific context it is clinically plausible.5 Donor-site pain after split-thickness skin graft harvest is typically most relevant in the early postoperative period and often declines substantially by the second postoperative week, especially when epithelialization is advanced. In our dataset, all patients in the control group reported no donor-site pain on day 14, while the study group showed minimal residual pain overall. Importantly, day 14 pain was not the endpoint driving our conclusions: the between-group difference at this time point was not statistically significant, and our conclusions focused on the clearly observed reduction in early postoperative pain and shorter healing time. Thus, the day 14 result should be interpreted as a floor effect occurring late in the early recovery period rather than as evidence of reporting bias.

Third, we agree that missing data must be handled transparently. In our study, missing data affected the late scar assessment, not the main early postoperative pain and healing-time outcomes. Most patients left the hospital within the first few days after surgery, and subsequent follow-up was performed either during outpatient visits or, when appropriate, by telephone or e-mail. Late scar data could not be obtained from all enrolled participants because some patients did not attend outpatient follow-up and/or could not be reached by telephone or e-mail after discharge. Accordingly, the late scar analysis was performed in a reduced subset of participants, and this was explicitly indicated in the table caption. We recognize that baseline comparisons between completers and non-completers, as well as prespecified missing-data strategies, would strengthen a larger trial.6 However, because the late scar outcomes were secondary and exploratory, and because the amount of missingness was limited, we did not perform multiple imputation. We also avoided making strong claims regarding late scar superiority. On the contrary, our manuscript reported no significant between-group differences in late hyperpigmentation, scar elevation, or late pruritus, and we acknowledged incomplete late follow-up as a limitation. Therefore, the primary clinical message of the study remains based on the early postoperative and healing outcomes rather than on late scar endpoints.

Fourth, regarding exudate, we agree that this finding should be interpreted cautiously. Mild exudate occurred in only three patients in the nanofiber matrix group, and no statistically significant between-group difference was observed. We did not interpret this finding as evidence of a causal adverse effect of the nanofiber matrix. Similarly, the association between exudate and longer healing time was based on very few cases and should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating only.7 Our safety conclusion was therefore intentionally conservative: we reported that no other local or systemic infection-related complications were identified and that short-term safety appeared broadly comparable between groups. We agree that larger studies are needed to define rare safety outcomes and wound-exudate patterns more precisely.

Finally, we acknowledge that our study was a single-center randomized comparative study with a relatively small sample size. We therefore did not intend to present the findings as definitive practice-changing evidence. Rather, the study was designed to provide additional clinical data on a translational wound-care technology in routine reconstructive practice. The results suggest that nanofiber matrix dressing may reduce early postoperative pain and shorten donor-site healing time compared with the conventional dressing regimen used in our center. These conclusions are consistent with the observed data, but they should be interpreted within the limitations of the study design. Importantly, this study also served as an early clinical evaluation of nanofiber-based donor-site management in our institutional setting and helped us identify methodological, logistical, and follow-up-related challenges that should be addressed in future protocols. We agree with Dr He that future research should include larger, ideally multicenter randomized trials, more detailed reporting of pain-score distributions, prespecified handling of missing data, formal sample-size justification, and broader outcomes such as analgesic use, dressing burden, patient satisfaction, and cost-effectiveness.

In summary, we appreciate Dr He’s comments and agree that further methodological rigor is needed in future investigations of nanofiber-based donor-site management. Nevertheless, we believe that the main findings of our study remain valid when interpreted appropriately: nanofiber matrix dressing was associated with lower early postoperative donor-site pain and shorter healing time, while late scar outcomes and short-term safety did not show clear between-group differences. We hope that, as further studies emerge, this initial clinical experience will be viewed as a useful contribution to the development of more rigorous and clinically relevant evaluations of electrospun nanofiber technologies in reconstructive wound care.

Yours sincerely,

Maciej Biskupski
