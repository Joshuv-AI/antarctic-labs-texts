On Conformal Machine Unlearning
Yahya Alkhatib, Wee Peng Tay

Abstract
The increasing demand for data privacy—driven by regulations such as GDPR and CCPA—has made machine unlearning (MU) essential for removing the influence of specific training samples from machine learning models while preserving performance on retained data. However, most existing MU methods lack rigorous statistical guarantees, rely on heuristic metrics, and often require computationally expensive retraining baselines. To overcome these limitations, we introduce a new definition for MU based on conformal prediction (CP) , providing statistically sound, uncertainty-aware guarantees without the need for the concept of naive retraining. We formalize conformal criteria that quantify how often forgotten samples are excluded from CP sets, and propose empirical metrics—the Efficiently Covered Frequency (ECF at c c ) and its complement, the Efficiently Uncovered Frequency (EuCF at d d ) —to measure the effectiveness of unlearning. We further present a practical unlearning method designed to optimize these conformal metrics. Extensive experiments across diverse forgetting scenarios, datasets and models demonstrate the efficacy of our approach in removing targeted data.

Introduction

The accelerating deployment of data-driven technologies has sharpened public and regulatory scrutiny of how models remember—or forget—individual data points.
High-profile frameworks such as the
General Data Protection Regulation
(GDPR) and the
California Consumer Privacy Act
(CCPA) enshrine a “right to be forgotten,” making

.

Existing approaches span (i)

.
Certified variants frequently draw inspiration from differential privacy (DP) or influence-function theory to bound residual leakage
[

Compared with brute-force retraining,

promises faster response times and lower carbon cost, while offering a defence against membership-inference and model-inversion attacks
[

,
5
]
.

Nevertheless, two limitations persist:

S1

Certifiability dependence on retrained baselines
—most guarantees hinge on comparisons with a model trained from scratch without the forget set, an expensive requirement at scale
[
2
,
6
]
. That is, the ultimate very aim of unlearning is approximating such a retrained model.

S2

Heuristic evaluation metrics
—accuracy on the forget/retain/test splits fails to quantify uncertainty and again presupposes access to a fully retrained reference model. This stems from the fact that it might not always be easy to predict how the accuracy on the forgot data or the retained data will be affected by some unlearning operations, necessitating the reference to the retrained model.

S3

Unrealistic evaluation via random instance forgetting.
Randomly deleting
independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.)
data points, a common benchmarking shortcut, fails to model any real unlearning request. In practice, erasures are
targeted
: either all records linked to an individual (privacy compliance) or a specific set of known errors (label correction). Such structured deletions can be correlated and class-specific, whereas random removal only perturbs the training distribution within sampling noise and rarely changes the learned decision boundary in a statistically meaningful way (see
appendix

H
for a formal discussion).

We ask the following question:

Can we devise a statistically rigorous, uncertainty-aware notion of machine unlearning that eliminates the retrain-from-scratch dependency and re-envisions the unlearning objective differently?
We base our unlearning scheme on two observations.

•

G1 — Baseline fragility:
Reliance on a single retrained baseline obscures the stochastic variability inherent to training.

•

G2 — Uncertainty blindness:
Current metrics provide point estimates but no coverage guarantees for how completely a sample’s influence has been removed, lacking any statistical rigour in quantifying the unlearning operation.

These gaps translate into prohibitive compute budgets for large models, hinder timely compliance with takedown requests, and leave practitioners without defensible statistical evidence that sensitive information is truly gone.

To tackle
G1–G2
, we leverage
conformal prediction (CP)

to endow
MU
with finite-sample coverage guarantees that are independent of any retrained baseline. Specifically:

•

Novel MU definition.
We introduce a conformal-probability objective that quantifies forgetting via
CP
, dispensing with retrain-from-scratch comparisons.

•

Theory & metrics.
We prove
(\alpha,\beta)
-conformal guarantees and propose practical empirical scores—
ECF
at
c
and
EuCF
at
d
—to measure unlearning quality.

•

Empirical Conformal MU method.
We design a scalable algorithm aligned with the new framework, demonstrating robust privacy protection without sacrificing retain-set performance.

Our approach marks the first departure from DP-inspired definitions toward a conformal-probability view of unlearning, offering practitioners a rigorous yet computationally viable path to privacy compliance

,
22
,

,

In auxiliary unlearning, we assume the forgotten dataset exhibits shared characteristics (e.g., in the feature or label space) represented by the random variable

For a trained model

\displaystyle\pi_{\theta}(X,Y)\triangleq 1-p_{\theta}(Y|X),

(1)

where

is the softmax probability from the model

.
For regression tasks, other non-conformity score functions could be used
[
24
]
. In this paper, we avoid discussing this straightforward extension.

In split

{\lvert{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{calib}}}\rvert}=n

\lceil(1-\alpha)(n+1)\rceil/n

{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}}

{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{unlearn}}}

\displaystyle C({X_{\mathrm{test}}})=C({X_{\mathrm{test}}};\theta)=\left\{y:\pi_{\theta}({X_{\mathrm{test}}},y)\leq\hat{q}_{\alpha}\right\}.

(2)

Under the condition that
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{calib}}}
and
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}}
are exchangeable, split

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\pi_{\theta}({X_{\mathrm{test}}},{Y_{\mathrm{test}}})\leq\hat{q}_{\alpha}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left({Y_{\mathrm{test}}}\in C({X_{\mathrm{test}}};\theta)\right)\geq 1-\alpha,

(3)

where the probability is due to the randomness in the calibration set and the test point. In our experiments, split

Our

Conformal Machine Unlearning

In this section, we introduce a new notion of conformal unlearning grounded in
CP

In this framework,
\alpha

On the other hand,
\beta

Therefore,

is the primary parameter of focus for evaluating unlearning. Our framework leverages the standard

This framework offers a new paradigm for machine unlearning. Rather than being limited to approximating a retrained model, we propose a more flexible, statistically-grounded objective. This empowers users to define their unlearning goals in a way that is tailored to their specific needs and risk tolerance.

3.2
Empirical Conformal Unlearning Metrics

In this subsection, we introduce new empirical metrics that help practitioners evaluate their
MU

without the need for the naively-retrained baseline

In
[
7
]
,

of a prediction set

for a dataset

is defined as:

\displaystyle coverage_{\mathcal{D}}(C)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum\limits_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}}{\bf 1}\left\{y\in C(x)\right\},

(12)

where
{\bf 1}\{\cdot\}
is the indicator function.

Following
1

4
and
5

From

is concerned, large prediction sets become quickly uninformative. Therefore, large prediction sets also indicate low confidence of the model in classifying those points. Hence, we introduce a relaxed version of the coverage metric in this work. As a result, we want to maximize the

of

and

.

For empirical studies, we approximate probabilities by frequencies. Hence, for a retain set

and positive integer

, we define the

of a prediction set

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}_{{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}}\left(c\right)=\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}_{c}|}\sum_{(x,y)\in{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}_{c}}{\bf 1}\left\{y\in C(x)\right\},

(13)

where
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}_{c}=\left\{(x,y)\in{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}\nonscript\,:\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}|C(x)|\leq c\right\}
.

On the other hand, for a forget dataset
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}
and positive integer
d
, we define the
efficiently uncovered frequency (EuCF) at
d
of a prediction set
C(\cdot)
as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{N}_{{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}}\left(d\right)=\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}_{d}|}\sum_{(x,y)\in{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}_{d}}{\bf 1}\left\{y\notin C(x)\right\},

(14)

where
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}_{d}=\left\{(x,y)\in{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}\nonscript\,:\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}|C(x)|\leq d\right\}
.

Note that
C(\cdot)
is the same in the whole framework.
In our experiments, we test different values of
c
and
d
(see
section

G.1
). The final objective is to maximize both
eq.

13
and
eq.

14
for
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}
. Note that although
\mathfrak{C}
and
\mathfrak{N}
are mathematically simple extensions of the coverage, they correspond to significant statistical interpretation as illustrated after
2
.

3.3
Conformal Unlearning-Based Framework

As can be seen in
eq.

3
, the position of the quantile score relative to the scores of the forget set points is what determines whether the point is covered or not. Therefore, to prevent the forget points from being covered by
CP
, the scores of forgotten data points must be shifted to be larger than the quantile. The opposite should be maintained for retained data points. Hence, a natural extension of this discussion is to introduce a framework to solve this problem. Our empirical aim in conformal
MU
is to maximize the metrics in
eq.

13
and
eq.

14
. Therefore, we resort to minimising the difference between the non-conformity scores of the forgotten class data points and the quantile score. Hence, we define (see
appendix

E
for the derivation) the unlearning loss function of this problem as

\displaystyle\widetilde{L}\left(\theta_{u};{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}},{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{f}(\theta_{u})-\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{r}(\theta_{u}),

(15)

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{r}(\theta_{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}|}\sum_{({X_{u}},{Y_{u}})\in{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}}\ell\left((1+\delta)p_{\theta_{u}}(Y_{q}\mid X_{q})-p_{\theta_{u}}({Y_{u}}\mid{X_{u}})\right),

(16)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{f}(\theta_{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}|}\sum_{({X_{u}},{Y_{u}})\in{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}}\ell\left((1+\delta)p_{\theta_{u}}({Y_{u}}\mid{X_{u}})-p_{\theta_{u}}(Y_{q}\mid X_{q})\right),

(17)

and
(X_{q},Y_{q})\in{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{calib}}}
represents the calibration point whose conformity score is closest to
\hat{q}_{\alpha}
and its label, and
p_{\theta_{u}}(\cdot)
is the softmax probability. The function
\ell(\cdot)
is the sigmoid surrogate to the indicator function with steepness factor
\gamma
(not shown explicitly above), and
\delta
is a margin buffer that we choose to be
\delta=0.0001
in all our experiments.

The objective is to minimize
eq.

15
. Since we typically initialize from the original trained model, we incorporate this by adding a regularization term that penalizes deviation from the original weights. This helps prevent significant drops in performance on the retained classes and mitigates overfitting to the specific forget and retain sets. The final optimization objective is therefore

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{o\rightarrow\mathfrak{U}}=\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta}\left\{\widetilde{L}+\lambda\left\lVert\theta-\widehat{\theta}_{o}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right\}.

(18)

The additional hyperparameters in this framework are the regularization constant
\lambda
, the margin buffer
\delta
and the surrogate steepness factor
\gamma
. The norm in the regularization term can be modified. We stick to the Euclidean norm for differentiability. The margin buffer delta is not really needed per se. It is generally used for the stability of the scores. That is, we push the scores relative to the quantile slightly more than necessary to avoid hard thresholding. There are other works which do the same thing (e.g.
[
7
]
). Note that we do not fine-tune
\delta
. We choose it to be small (0.0001) for all our experiments. Hence, our method does not create an overkill in terms of the number of hyperparameters (only two additional hyperparameters to fine-tune beyond the learning rate and the number of epochs).

We call the above framework Conformal Predication MU (CPMU). It does not require access to the training dataset,
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}}
. It only requires access to
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{calib}}}
,
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}
, and some retained data points in
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}
, such that
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}\cup{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}
are exchangeable with
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{calib}}}
. This makes our framework more feasible when the original training dataset is not available, especially if the model is being deployed by third parties that do not have access or knowledge of the original set
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}}
.
The summary of CPMU can be found in
algorithm

1
in
section

A.2
.

Numerical Experiments

We evaluate unlearning performance on CIFAR-100

]
(see

) using a ResNet-18 model trained from scratch for 50 epochs with SGD (initial learning rate 0.1, linearly decayed to 0.0001, momentum 0.9, weight decay

We also include in the appendices results over Imagenet-100 with Resnet18, Ag-News with BertaDistill and 20-NewsGroups with BertaDistill.

6
Conclusion

We have presented a new perspective on
MU
by grounding it in the theory of
CP
, enabling rigorous unlearning without reliance on naively retrained baselines. By introducing a conformal
MU
notion and empirical metrics—ECF at
c
and EuCF at
d
—we provide a principled way to quantify unlearning performance through the exclusion of forgotten data and the coverage of retained data. This framework delivers statistical reliability for forgetting while maintaining accuracy on retained data.
Furthermore, the conformal approach is flexible and can be naturally extended to regression, graph neural networks, and natural language models.

Future directions include developing tighter theoretical guarantees, adaptive methods for various model architectures, and broader metrics to capture different aspects of data influence. We hope this work gives both practitioners and regulators greater confidence that
MU
can be certifiable, practical, and ready for real-world deployment.

Appendix B
Overview of Conformal Unlearning in the Field

B.1
How is our Framework Different?

Although we share with
[
7
]
the high-level motivation of integrating
CP
into machine unlearning, our approach pursues a
distinct
theoretical and practical direction. First, we introduce a formal
MU
notion via “
\alpha
-conformal” MU, providing definitions and theorems (§
3
) that rigorously characterize when a model’s prediction sets can be guaranteed to exclude forgotten data with a certain probability. In addition, our
Efficiently Covered Frequency (ECF)
metric is designed to capture subtle differences in how
CP
sets change
relative to the original model
(i.e., how much less likely the forgotten label is to appear). By contrast,
[
7
]
measures coverage and set size in absolute terms, without providing a corresponding formalism for
\alpha
-conformal guarantees. Moreover, we introduce a novel practical unlearning method whose design aligns with our conformal framework.

Our framework aims to sidestep naive retraining baselines. While many methods compare an “unlearned model” to a fully retrained counterpart, we establish theoretical conditions (
1
and
2
in our work) that do
not
require such a baseline. This feature makes our approach more amenable to large-scale or time-sensitive deployments where repeated full retraining is impractical. Overall, our contribution goes beyond the previous
CP
-motivated method by offering a unified, rigorously defined notion of when forgetting has truly occurred, along with concrete metrics and proofs that guarantee the statistical soundness of the unlearning process.

B.2
How Can Our Framework Be Generalized?

Given the length constraint of the paper, in this work we focus on
MU
in classification tasks and the results are more on class-wise forgetting. However, our work paves the way for a new paradigm of
MU
based on the rigorous quantification of
CP
uncertainty. This paradigm can be straightforwardly extended to regression tasks by using proper non-conformity score functions as shown in classical works on
CP
(cf.
[
24
,
22
]
). Moreover, our paradigm could be extended to any field where
CP
has been explored with proper handling of its notions. For example, multiple works have explored
CP
in graph neural networks
[
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
]
. Other works have introduced
CP
for natural language processing
[
34
,
35
]
. This makes our paradigm directly relevant to current research in
MU
since the recent focus within the community has been on
MU
for large language models
[
36
,
37
]
. The extension of our paradigm to these fields is an interesting topic of future research.

Conformal prediction (

3

non-exchangeable

. Let

{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{calib}}}=\{Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}\}
. Then, the coverage is guaranteed with a new bound such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({Y_{u}}\in C({X_{u}};\theta)\right)\geq 1-\alpha-\sum\limits_{1\leq i\leq n}\widetilde{\omega}_{i}\cdot d_{TV}\left(\pi(\bm{Z}),\pi(\bm{Z^{i}})\right),

(27)

where
\widetilde{\omega}_{i}=\frac{\omega_{i}}{\omega_{1}+\dots+\omega_{n}+1}
for some predefined weights
\{\omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
d_{TV}(P,Q)
is the total variation divergence between
P
and
Q
,
\pi(\cdot)
is the (non)-conformity score function,
\bm{Z}=\{Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n},{Z_{u}}\}
, and
\bm{Z^{i}}
is the set
\bm{Z}
when the
i^{th}
entry is exchanged with
{Z_{u}}
such that
\bm{Z^{i}}=\{Z_{1},\dots,Z_{i-1},{Z_{u}},Z_{i+1},\dots Z_{n},Z_{i}\}
. It is logical to assign the weights such that a data sample
Z_{i}
has a higher weight
\omega_{i}
if it is more similar to the test sample
{Z_{u}}
. We will refrain from exploring this form of conformal prediction more here and instead refer to the original paper
[
25
]
for more information.

What matters for us here is the effect of this gap correction on our results, mainly in
1
. Let us define the correction gap for a test sample
{Z_{u}}
as
g({Z_{u}})
. Under non-exchangeability, we have (as stated before)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({Y_{u}}\in C({X_{u}};\theta)\right)\geq 1-\alpha-g({Z_{u}}).

(28)

It follows that for
1
to hold, we have to include the coverage gap into
eq.

4
and
eq.

5
. That is,

Proposition D.1
.

In the auxiliary forgetting scenario, suppose
\mathbb{P}\left(({X_{u}},{Y_{u}})\in{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}\right)>0
. Moreover, suppose
g({Z_{u}})>0
(otherwise
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{unlearn}}}
and
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{calib}}}
will be exchangeable). Then,
eq.

5
implies
eq.

4
if
\beta^{\prime}\geq\alpha^{\prime}\geq\alpha+g({Z_{u}})
, where
\alpha^{\prime}
and
\beta^{\prime}
are to replace
\alpha
and
\beta
in
eq.

4
and
eq.

5
, respectively.

Proof.

From
eq.

28
, we have that
\mathbb{P}\left({Y_{u}}\in C({X_{u}};\theta_{u})\right)\geq 1-\alpha-g({Z_{u}})
. From
eq.

5
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({Y_{u}}\in C({X_{u}};\theta_{u})\nonscript\,\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}({X_{u}},{Y_{u}})\in{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 1-\beta^{\prime}\leq 1-\alpha^{\prime}.

Suppose
eq.

4
does not hold (i.e.
\mathbb{P}\left({Y_{u}}\in C({X_{u}};\theta_{u})\nonscript\,\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}({X_{u}},{Y_{u}})\in{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}\right)<1-\alpha^{\prime}
). Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({Y_{u}}\in C({X_{u}};\theta_{u})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left({Y_{u}}\in C({X_{u}};\theta_{u})\nonscript\,\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}({X_{u}},{Y_{u}})\in{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(({X_{u}},{Y_{u}})\in{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}\right)

\displaystyle+\,\mathbb{P}\left({Y_{u}}\in C({X_{u}};\theta_{u})\nonscript\,\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}({X_{u}},{Y_{u}})\in{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(({X_{u}},{Y_{u}})\in{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}\right)

\displaystyle<1-\alpha^{\prime},

a contradiction if
\alpha^{\prime}\geq\alpha+g({Z_{u}})
. Since also
\beta^{\prime}\geq\alpha^{\prime}
by definition, the proposition holds.
∎

We can see that even if exchangeability is hard to assume in some applications where there might be some dependencies between samples (e.g. time-series), conformal unlearning is still a valid framework with minor corrections involved in the coverage guarantees.

Appendix E
Machine Unlearning Framework Derivation

Corresponding to the case where
c=d=\lvert\mathcal{Y}\rvert
in
eqs.

8
and
9
, we maximize the following optimization objective:

\displaystyle J(\theta_{u})\triangleq\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}|}\sum\limits_{{X_{u}}\in{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}}{\bf 1}\left\{{Y_{u}}\notin C({X_{u}};\theta_{u})\right\}+\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}|}\sum\limits_{{X_{u}}\in{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}}{\bf 1}\left\{{Y_{u}}\in C({X_{u}};\theta_{u})\right\},

where
\pi_{\theta_{u}}({X_{u}},{Y_{u}})=1-\mathbb{P}\left(f_{\theta_{u}}({X_{u}})={Y_{u}}\right)
with the probability being the softmax probability of the model
f_{\theta_{u}}
.

Smooth surrogate for the indicators.

Because the indicator
I\{\hat{q}_{\alpha}<\pi\}
is non-differentiable, we adopt the
sigmoid surrogate

\displaystyle\ell(u)\triangleq\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\gamma u)},\qquad\gamma>0,

and replace the empirical rates by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{r}(\theta)

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}|}\sum_{{X_{u}}\in{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}}\ell\left(\hat{q}_{\alpha}-\pi_{\theta}({X_{u}},{Y_{u}})\right),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{f}(\theta)

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}|}\sum_{{X_{u}}\in{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}}\ell\left(\pi_{\theta}({X_{u}},{Y_{u}})-\hat{q}_{\alpha}\right).

Accordingly,

\displaystyle\tilde{J}(\theta)\triangleq\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{f}(\theta)+\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{r}(\theta).

This objective is what we want to optimize. However, the quantile score
\hat{q}_{\alpha}
is found using the quantile function which is non-differentiable. Hence, we find the softmax probability of the calibration point in
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{calib}}}
whose conformity score is the closest to
\hat{q}_{\alpha}
and use that softmax probability in our loss as follows.

Explicit soft-max expansion of the penalized objective.

Define the soft-max probability under the unlearned model

f_{\theta_{u}}
by

\displaystyle p_{\theta_{u}}({Y_{u}}\mid{X_{u}})\triangleq\frac{\exp\left(f_{\theta_{u}}({X_{u}})_{{Y_{u}}}\right)}{\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp\left(f_{\theta_{u}}({X_{u}})_{y^{\prime}}\right)}.

Replacing
\pi_{\theta}({X_{u}},{Y_{u}})=1-p_{\theta_{u}}({Y_{u}}\mid{X_{u}})
inside the
sigmoid surrogate yields

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{r}(\theta_{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}|}\sum_{({X_{u}},{Y_{u}})\in{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}}\ell\left(p_{\theta_{u}}(Y_{q}\mid X_{q})-p_{\theta_{u}}({Y_{u}}\mid{X_{u}})\right),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{f}(\theta_{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}|}\sum_{({X_{u}},{Y_{u}})\in{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}}\ell\left(p_{\theta_{u}}({Y_{u}}\mid{X_{u}})-p_{\theta_{u}}(Y_{q}\mid X_{q})\right),

where
(X_{q},Y_{q})\in{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{calib}}}
represents the point whose conformity score is closest to
\hat{q}_{\alpha}
and its label.

Substituting these in the penalty form gives the trainable loss

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}_{\text{pen}}(\theta_{u})

\displaystyle=-\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{f}(\theta_{u})-\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{r}(\theta_{u})

and the updated parameters are obtained via minimising this loss such that

\displaystyle\theta_{u}=\min_{\theta}\mathsf{L}_{\text{pen}}(\theta).

Appendix F
More On CIFAR100 and The Baselines

F.1
CIFAR100

CIFAR-100 is a carefully curated, labeled subset of the 80 Million Tiny Images dataset developed by Alex Krizhevsky, Vinod Nair, and Geoffrey Hinton . It comprises 60 000 color images of size 32×32 pixels, evenly distributed across 100 distinct object classes . Each class contains exactly 600 images, which are split into 500 samples for training and 100 for testing. These 100 classes are further organized into 20 higher-level “superclasses,” enabling both fine-grained and coarse-grained classification experiments. Every image carries two annotations: a fine label denoting its specific class and a coarse label indicating its superclass. The small 32×32 resolution makes CIFAR-100 computationally efficient for prototyping convolutional networks and other vision models. Its perfectly balanced class distribution and hierarchical labelling have established CIFAR-100 as a standard benchmark in the computer-vision community. The dataset shares its file-format conventions (Python “pickled” batches, MATLAB files, or binary versions) with CIFAR-10, where each batch bundles image data and labels together
[
27
]
. CIFAR-100 is also natively supported in major ML libraries like TensorFlow Datasets and PyTorch’s torchvision for seamless integration into research pipelines. In our experiments, we load CIFAR100 using torchvision’s datasets library.

F.2
On The Baselines

Here we summarize the objectives of each of the baselines and how they achieve unlearning. This section is not meant to be comprehensive but to give a clearer idea about the methods we compare with.

\nabla\tau

[
15
]
introduces a new loss objective that focuses on pushing the loss of the data meant to be forgotten from the training data to become larger than the loss of some validation data (carrying the same unlearned labels). They merge it with the original objective of minimizing the loss on the retained data. The new loss is then given by

\displaystyle L=\alpha\left(\mathrm{ReLU}(L_{\mathcal{D}_{v}}-L_{\mathcal{D}_{f}})\right)+(1-\alpha)L_{\mathcal{D}_{r}},

where
\alpha
controls how much emphases should be given to the retained versus forgot data. In our framework, however, non of the subsets
\mathcal{D}_{v}
,
\mathcal{D}_{f}
, and
\mathcal{D}_{r}
is seen during training. We assume that this causes the method to fail.

SCRUB
[
14
]
builds the loss function on the KL-divergence of the unlearning model to a teacher that was trained on the full training data (both
\mathcal{D}_{r}
and
\mathcal{D}_{f}
). This is nothing but the original base model trained on
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}}
. They add one more regular loss term to be minimized over the retained data to maintain performance on those points. The final loss that should be minimized becomes

\displaystyle L=\frac{\alpha}{N_{r}}\sum\limits_{x_{r}\in\mathcal{D}_{r}}d_{KL}(x_{r};\omega^{u})+\frac{\gamma}{N_{r}}\sum\limits_{(x_{r},y_{r})\in\mathcal{D}_{r}}l(f(x_{r};\omega^{u}),y_{r})-\frac{1}{N_{f}}\sum\limits_{x_{f}\in\mathcal{D}_{f}}d_{KL}(x_{f};\omega^{u}),

where
N_{r}
is the number of data points to retain,
N_{f}
the number of data points to forget, and
\alpha
and
\gamma
control the importance of the terms of retaining. Notice that SCRUB tries to make the distributions of the unlearning model and the base model converge to each other on the retained data and diverge from each other on the forget data. Hence, when given unseen data, it actually starts learning about the retained data and its accuracy increases on those more than a naively retrained model as shown in the results (e.g.
table

6
,
table

7
). More emphasis seems to be put on the retained data compared to the forget data by adding the regular loss term on
\mathcal{D}_{r}
. That is why it increases the performance on
\mathcal{D}_{r}
but does not cause the performance to drop on
\mathcal{D}_{f}
.

SSD
[
16
]
uses synaptic dampening of the parameters (weights) of the model which are ”specialized” for
\mathcal{D}_{f}
. SSD compares the ”importances” of the weights using the first-order derivative property of the Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) and decides whether to dampen a weight if it is more specialized for
\mathcal{D}_{f}
than for other training data, as follows.

\displaystyle[]_{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[-\left.\frac{\delta^{2}\ln p(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta)}{\delta\theta^{2}}\right|_{\theta^{*}_{D}}\right],

\displaystyle[]_{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[(\frac{\delta\ln p(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta)}{\delta\theta})(\frac{\delta\ln p(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta)}{\delta\theta})^{T}|_{\theta^{*}_{D}}\right].

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=\min(\lambda\frac{[]_{\mathcal{D},i}}{[]_{\mathcal{D}_{f,i}}},1),

\displaystyle\theta_{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\beta\,\theta_{i},&\text{if }[]_{\mathcal{D}_{f,i}}>\alpha\,[]_{\mathcal{D},i},\\
\theta_{i},&\text{if }[]_{\mathcal{D}_{f,i}}\leq\alpha\,[]_{\mathcal{D},i},\end{cases}\quad\forall\,\,i\in[0,\lvert\theta\rvert].

Generally, they assume that the training data importances can be calculated before training and then the importances of the parameters will be compared between
\mathcal{D}_{f}
and
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}}
. In our framework,
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}
is not part of
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}}
. Hence, it is not straightforward to argue for a stable relation between the importances. That seems to be the reason why SSD sometimes demolishes the performance of the model, especially if the number of forgotten classes is small (see
table

6
and
table

1
).

AMN
[
9
]
randomly relabels the data to be forgotten. It replaces the classes to be forgotten with new random labels over the whole training set and then retrains the model for a few iterations over the newly labeled data. However, in our case we feed
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}
and
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}
to the unlearning algorithm rather than the full training set. Since
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}
in our framework is not used for training and is just a smaller subset compared to the fraction of forget data in the training set, we expected AMN to perform badly on those points. However, the results reported in the main body (see
table

1
) and in the appendices, especially for group-wise forgetting (see
table

12
and
table

13
) suggest that AMN can actually perform well even if the unlearned data is unseen during training.

BADT
[
11
]
introduces a bad teacher initialized with random noise which induces forgetting by minimizing the KL-divergence between its distribution and that of the unlearned model (student) on the forget data. On the other hand, BADT minimizes the divergence between the distribution of the base model and that of the student on the retained data. The objective of BADT is given below.

\displaystyle L(x,l_{u})=(1-l_{u})\mathcal{K}\mathcal{L}(T_{s}(x)||S(x))+l_{u}\mathcal{K}\mathcal{L}(T_{d}(x)||S(x)),

where
l_{u}
is the label to be forgotten,
x
is a sample point,
T_{s}
is the base model,
T_{d}
is the bad teacher model, and
\mathcal{K}\mathcal{L}(P,Q)
is the KL-divergence between the
P
and
Q
.
Note that BADT is suitable for label-wise forgetting since it depends on the label to induce unlearning. That is why we do not use it for instance-wise forgetting. For demonstration purposes, we still include it for auxiliary group-wise unlearning but we cannot expect it to perform well.

UNSIR
[
28
]
constructs noisy data by maximizing the loss on the noisy samples that carry the label to be forgotten. Then, it feeds the loss-maximizing noise to the model along with some retrained data in an impair-repair fashion. Similar to BADT, UNSIR depends on the label to be forgotten to construct the noise. Hence, it is not suitable for auxiliary group-wise or instance-wise forgetting. That is why we do not include its results in those scenarios.

G.2
Limitations of CPMU

The limitations of CPMU mainly come from the limitations of
CP
. We outline them here.

•

In split
CP
, we have to leave out a subset of data that is not used for training. This means that we deprive the base model from data that could boost its performance.

•

Also, unlearning is not known beforehand, sometimes it is hard to leave out data at the training stage. This leaves the assumptions of exchangeability under question. One could apply the extension in
appendix

D
but then there will be a coverage correction gap and the weights must be defined properly.

•

In CPMU, we actually use two calibration sets as outlined in
section

4
. This creates even a larger problem in data splitting and leaving out during training.

Appendix H
Why Random Instance Forgetting Is Not a Meaningful Unlearning Scenario

In this section, we contend that
random instance forgetting
—the deletion of arbitrarily selected training points—does not reflect meaningful or practical machine unlearning scenarios. Our reasoning is as follows:

1.

Absence of Practical Relevance.

Randomly removing samples does not align with real-world motivations for unlearning. In practice, data is deleted to:

•

Comply with individual privacy requests (e.g., the GDPR “right to be forgotten”), or

•

Correct specific errors, such as mislabeled or outdated records (e.g., removing obsolete financial entries).

In both cases, data points are selected based on identity or error, not at random.

2.

Targeted Privacy vs. Randomness.

Fulfilling a data subject’s privacy request requires removing
all
data linked to that individual. For instance, if user
u
requests erasure, we delete
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\mid\text{owner}(x_{i})=u\}
. This process eliminates a structured, potentially correlated subset of the data, rather than a random
i.i.d.
sample.

3.

Correcting Known Errors.

Similarly, correcting mislabeled samples requires the targeted removal or modification of specific data points. For example, if sample
j
is identified as mislabeled, we remove or relabel
(x_{j},y_{j})
accordingly. Random deletion, on the other hand, may inadvertently eliminate correctly labeled data or fail to address the actual errors.

4.

Statistical Insignificance under
i.i.d.
Sampling.

Assume the training set
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
is drawn
i.i.d.
from some distribution
\mathcal{P}
. Deleting
k
points chosen uniformly at random yields a new empirical distribution

\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{-S}(x,y)\;=\;\frac{1}{n-k}\sum_{i\notin S}\delta_{(x_{i},y_{i})},

which converges to the same
\mathcal{P}
as
n\to\infty
. Thus, random forgetting only perturbs the finite-sample approximation and does not meaningfully alter the learned model’s decision boundary beyond sampling noise.

5.

Indistinguishability under Conformal and Accuracy Metrics.

Metrics based on conformal prediction assume exchangeability between the calibration set and the points to be evaluated. However, when the model is trained on the full dataset—including the points to be “forgotten”—this assumption is violated. Even disregarding this, generalization implies that other samples from the same class will occupy similar regions in feature space. As a result, both conformal and accuracy-based metrics are unlikely to detect any meaningful “unlearning effect” when points are removed at random.

6.

Resource Expenditure Without Practical Value.

Assessing unlearning algorithms by evaluating their performance on randomly selected subsets
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{D}
yields little meaningful information about their effectiveness in real-world scenarios. Specifically, such evaluations do not test the algorithm’s ability to:

•

Remove sensitive or personally identifiable information (
targeted forgetting
),

•

Correct specific, known errors (
label unlearning
), or

•

Handle realistic unlearning requests, which typically involve structured or contiguous data subsets.

As a result, benchmarking on random deletions expends computational resources without providing actionable insights into the algorithm’s practical utility.

Illustrative Example.

Consider a facial-recognition system trained on images from many users. If user Alice requests erasure, we remove all images tagged as
Alice_1.jpg
,
Alice_2.jpg
, etc. Randomly deleting 5% of images (perhaps including some of Alice’s) neither guarantees Alice’s privacy nor emulates any compliance procedure. Moreover, evaluating unlearning by how often the model still recognizes
Alice_3.jpg
is ill-posed: the model may rely on correlated features learned from other users’ faces with similar attributes.

In general,
random instance forgetting
is a strawman: it neither represents the data deletion demands of privacy legislation nor the controlled label correction. Instead, unlearning evaluations should focus on
targeted
forgetting tasks that reflect true operational needs. In a sense, all forgetting scenarios are constrained in the label space
\mathcal{Y}
or the feature space
\mathcal{X}
and their extensions. Hence, in their most general forms, the class- and group-wise forgetting scenarios cover all meaningful unlearning requests.

H.1
Instance-wise Group Forgetting Results

We provide additional results to illustrate the performance of CPMU in the instance-wise group forgetting scenario. Unlike the auxiliary group-wise forgetting setting, here the
MU
algorithms operate directly on the training data. We apply the same PCA-based method, but select samples from the original training set
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}}
. Specifically, we identify all points to forget whose principal component falls among those designated for forgetting, and select an equal number of retained points to match the number of forgotten points, rather than using the entire training set.

This scenario poses a greater challenge for CPMU because the exchangeability condition is violated between the calibration set and both the forgotten and retained sets, as the latter two subsets were used to train the base model. To address this, CPMU constructs the unlearning calibration set as a mixture of trained and potentially unseen data, so that their conformity scores better approximate those of the training data. However, the testing calibration set remains completely unseen during training and unlearning. All experiments were conducted using random seeds 42, 43, and 44. We further fine-tuned our model; for example, when
\alpha=0.20
, we set
\lambda=0.0003
, number of epochs to 9, learning rate to 0.003, and
\gamma=461
, and when
\alpha=0.05
, we just change the learning rate to 0.01. Note that further testing with
\nabla\tau
revealed that lowering the learning rate prevents it from collapsing. We set its learning rate to 0.00001.

I.1
The Effect of
\alpha
on
\mathfrak{C}_{{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}}
,
\mathfrak{N}_{{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}}
, and
H(CE)

The parameter
\alpha
in split
CP
plays a pivotal role, as it directly controls the tolerated miscoverage level according to user requirements. A smaller
\alpha
yields higher coverage, meaning a greater proportion of samples are included in the prediction sets. This relationship is evident in
fig.

4
and
fig.

5
, where increasing
\alpha
leads to a decrease in coverage
\mathfrak{C}_{{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}}
across different
c
values, causing the harmonic mean
H(CE)
to decline as well.

Within our conformal unlearning framework, increasing
\alpha
necessitates a corresponding increase in
\beta
(since
\beta\geq\alpha
). Initially, this results in a rising trend. However, as
\alpha
becomes large (between
0.25
and
0.30
),
fig.

6
demonstrates that the (anti)-coverage
\mathfrak{N}_{{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}}
drops sharply for all
c
values. Although our paper does not establish a theoretical upper bound for
\beta
, these results indicate that, in practice,
\beta
cannot be increased arbitrarily. This limitation explains the observed decline in
H(CE)
in
fig.

4
as
\alpha
approaches
0.30
.

I.2
The Effect of
c
on
\mathfrak{C}_{{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}}
,
\mathfrak{N}_{{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}}
, and
H(CE)

We now examine how varying the parameter
c
affects our metrics. Recall that evaluating performance across different
c
values provides a more comprehensive assessment of a
MU
algorithm. Unlike other
MU
methods, which may fail or become unstable for small
c
(see Section 6 in the main text), CPMU demonstrates robustness across the entire range of
c
values.

As illustrated in
fig.

7
,
H(CE)
exhibits a slight increase for small
c
(from
c=1
to
c=10
), after which the trend either stabilizes (for larger
\alpha
) or decreases smoothly (for
\alpha=0.05
). For small
\alpha
, the quantile value increases, resulting in larger prediction sets, which explains the non-constant behavior in
fig.

8
and
fig.

9
. As
\alpha
increases, the quantile value decreases, causing most points in
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}
to have very small prediction sets—likely because the model is highly confident in misclassifying them—while prediction set sizes over
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}
increase, as is typical in
CP
with high
\alpha
. This dynamic leads to the observed jump in
fig.

8
for
\alpha=0.30
as
c
approaches
100
.

Notice that high
\alpha
induces more variability in the results so does high
c
(as shown in
fig.

4
,
fig.

5
, and
fig.

6
). That is because for high values of
\alpha
and
c
, different initializations of the subsets result in increasing different results, since it becomes harder for more points to be covered. Beware that larger
\alpha
makes it harder for points to be covered by easier to achieve the
CP
validity over the whole test set.

We also investigated how the choice of regularization norm—
\ell_{1}

I.5
Approximate Memory Requirement

CPMU requires approximately 12,414 MB of GPU memory when unlearning 10 classes on CIFAR100, compared to 4,068 MB for NABLA, 3,228 MB for SCRUB, 3,556 MB for SSD, and 2,136 MB for AMN.
As noted in the main appendix, CPMU generally consumes more memory—about four times that of the next most demanding method. This increased memory usage stems from CPMU’s approach: it computes quantile scores and simultaneously processes batches from
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{forget}}}
,
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{retain}}}
, and
{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{calib}}}
during unlearning. Loading all three subsets into GPU memory at once leads to this overhead. Future code optimizations may help reduce the memory footprint.
