Plasma fibrinogen level is independent risk factor associated with the incidence of pulmonary infection in patients with spinal cord injury: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jinlong Zhang, Cheng Wang, Chengqi He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with spinal cord injury (SCI) are at higher risk of developing pulmonary infection (PI), and plasma fibrinogen level may be an independent risk factor for PI. However, the relationship between fibrinogen level and PI incidence in the SCI population remains unclear. This study aimed to elucidate the association between plasma fibrinogen level and the occurrence of PI among SCI patients.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of 576 SCI patients admitted to the Rehabilitation Medicine Department between January 1, 2017, and December 31, 2021. Following exclusions, 491 patients were included in the final analysis, with 139 PI cases identified.ResultsSurgery, level of injury and chest comorbidities were covariates in the relationship between fibrinogen level and PI incidence. Other identified potential risk factors for PI included age, D-dimer level, urinary tract infections (UTI), deep vein thrombosis (DVT), anticoagulant therapy, injury mechanism, and the American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale (AIS) grades. After adjusting for these factors, we found that for every 1 g/L increase in fibrinogen level, the risk of developing PI increased by 18% (HR = 1.18, P = 0.011), and indicating a positive linear relationship between fibrinogen level and PI incidence.ConclusionPlasma fibrinogen was an independent risk factor for PI in patients with SCI, especially for AIS-B and C grades. Proactive management of fibrinogen level after admission to rehabilitation medicine department could be crucial in reducing the incidence of PI in this vulnerable population.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-024-03332-y.

Introduction

Patients with spinal cord injuries (SCI) faced a significantly increased risk of pulmonary infections (PI) [1], a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in this population [2]. Such infections were particularly life-threatening for SCI patients [3], partly due to respiratory myasthenia [4], which affects lung function and the ability to cough, common after SCI. Infections were the main cause of death and were a rehabilitation confounder associated with impaired recovery for SCI [5]. For those with cervical SCI, pneumonia was a common and serious complication [6], respiratory dysfunction emerged as one of the most severe and potentially fatal complications [7]. If patients with traumatic cervical SCI have a complication of swallowing disorder [8], other more serious complications could occur, such as aspiration pneumonia and mechanical airway obstruction increasing mortality and morbidity [9]. At discharge, most traumatic SCI developed sacral ulcer and hospital-acquired pneumonia [10]. SCI patients may take polypharmacy for lung infections, which increases the risk of emergency room visits within two years and increases overall medical costs four fold in the year after the injury [11]. Hence, the prevention of PI and the provision of intensive care for those with severe SCI were of paramount importance [12, 13].

A systematic review highlighted that fibrinogen is linked to mortality in children with various stages of sepsis, including septic shock [14]. In patients with and without sepsis, mean plasma fibrinogen level was higher in the former group [15], suggesting its utility as a cost-effective and convenient biomarker for conditions such as peri-prosthetic joint infection [7, 16]. Additionally, patients with periprosthetic joint infection exhibited significantly elevated fibrinogen level compared to those without, and fibrinogen level can been used as serological marker for screening periprosthetic joint infection [17]. In middle-aged and elderly SCI patients, increased plasma fibrinogen level was positive correlated with the severity of depressive symptoms [18]. Yet, the specific relationship between plasma fibrinogen level and the incidence of PI in the SCI population remains unclear. Addressing this gap, our clinical observational study aims to elucidate the connection between plasma fibrinogen level and PI incidence among SCI patients, with the goal of informing prevention strategies.

Methods

Study design

We employed an observational, retrospective cohort study design.

Setting and participants

The study included 576 SCI patients admitted to the Rehabilitation Medicine Department of The First Affiliated Hospital of USTC, Division of Life Sciences and Medicine, University of Science and Technology of China, from January 1, 2017, to December 31, 2021.

Procedure

After excluding 85 patients due to non-compliance with inclusion or exclusion criteria, 491 patients were analyzed. PI were primary identified through computed tomography (CT) scans, utilizing a predefined set of radiological criteria to ensure consistency and accuracy in diagnosis. The criteria for identifying PI on CT scans included the following: Parenchymal Opacities: The presence of focal, multifocal, or diffuse opacities within the lung parenchyma, which may present as consolidation, ground-glass opacity, or a mixed pattern. Consolidation: Defined as an area of increased lung density with air bronchogram, obscuring the underlying blood vessels and bronchi. Ground-Glass Opacity: Characterized by hazy increased lung opacity through which the underlying vascular and bronchial structures remain visible. Nodules: Rounded opacities with a diameter of 3 cm or less, with or without features suggestive of infection such as the ‘halo sign’ or surrounding ground-glass opacity. Cavitation: The presence of a thick-walled or thin-walled lucency within the area of opacity, indicative of necrosis. Lung Abscess: A localized collection of pus within the lung parenchyma, typically with a thick-walled cavity and air-fluid levels. Pleural Effusion: An abnormal collection of fluid in the pleural space, which may be associated with infection. Lymphadenopathy: Enlarged lymph nodes, particularly in the hilar or mediastinal regions, which may be reactive or indicative of an infectious process. Centrilobular Nodules: Small nodules centered within secondary pulmonary lobules, which can be a sign of early infection. Tree-in-Bud Pattern: Characterized by small centrilobular nodules connected to branching linear opacities, indicative of bronchiolar inflammation or infection.

The CT scans were interpreted by the radiologists who were blinded to the clinical data, and a consensus was reached for cases with divergent interpretations. The radiologists utilized a standardized reporting form to record the presence, location, extent, and pattern of opacities, as well as any associated features. In addition to the radiological assessment, the final diagnosis of PI was confirmed by a combination of clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and, where available, microbiological data [19]. Finally, PI was identified in 139 patients through computed tomography. The criteria for inclusion and exclusion are detailed in Fig. 1.

Data collection

For this retrospective study, we established a rigorous data collection process to ensure the integrity and reliability of the information obtained from existing records.

Data collector blinding

To minimize bias and maintain objectivity, data collectors were blinded to the study objectives. Data collectors were trained research assistants who were not involved in the formulation of the research hypotheses. They were provided with a standardized data extraction form and instructed to extract data without any knowledge of the specific study aims or the outcomes of interest. This approach ensured that the data collection process was conducted in a neutral and systematic manner, reducing the potential for selective reporting or unconscious bias.

Handling of missing data

The issue of missing data was addressed through a series of predefined steps. Initially, we conducted a thorough search to identify all available records relevant to our study objectives. Where data were missing, we attempted to trace and retrieve the information from alternative sources, such as supplementary documents or databases, to ensure the completeness of our dataset. In cases where data remained missing despite these efforts, we employed statistical techniques suitable for handling missing data. We reported the proportion of missing data for key variables and conducted additional analyses to explore the potential impact of missing data on the study results. The methods used to handle missing data were selected based on their suitability for the data distribution and the nature of the missingness. Throughout the data collection and analysis process, we adhered to a predefined protocol to ensure consistency and transparency.

Variables

Collected data encompassed demographics, coagulation test results (D-dimer and fibrinogen level), comorbidities (type 2 diabetes mellitus [T2D] and high blood pressure [HBP]), injury specifics(American Spinal Cord Injury Association impairment scale [AIS] grades, mode of injury, level of injury), smoking history, surgical treatment post-SCI, anticoagulant therapy before rehabilitation, average length of stay (ALOS), and complications (deep vein thromobosis[DVT], urinary tract infection[UTI], PI and chest comorbidities ) during rehabilitation (Fig. 1). All clinical data were collected on the second day after admission to the rehabilitation department. PI was identified through computed tomography during hospitalization for rehabilitation.

Anticoagulant therapy

In the event of venous thromboembolism (VTE) or a hypercoagulable state upon hospital admission, the subjectives may initiation of anticoagulant therapy. The selection of anticoagulants is primarily guided by clinical evidence and patient-specific factors, with the following agents being commonly utilized: Low Molecular Weight Heparin (LMWH): Administered subcutaneously in doses ranging from 4,000 to 6,000 IU every 8 to 12 h. Warfarin: Initiated at a dose of 0.25 to 5 mg, taken once to three times daily. Rivaroxaban: Given at a daily dose of 5 to 10 mg. The duration of anticoagulant therapy varies among patients, typically ranging from 1 to 8 weeks, depending on the presence of VTE, the resolution of the hypercoagulable state, and the individual’s clinical response to treatment.

Statistical methods

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) (Gaussian distribution) or median (minimum, maximum) (skewed distribution), and categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages (N%).The Pearson’s Chi-squared test(χ2) or Fisher’s precise test(theoretical frequency < 10) test for categorical variables, and student’s T-test(normal distribution) or Welch two sample T-test(skewed distribution) for continuous variables were used to analyze the differences between PI-negative and PI-positive groups(Table 1).

For accounting for the missing data (D-dimer level), we used multiple imputation based on a mean method in the R MI procedure [20, 21], name “variable(imputed)”and transform them to a categorical variable named “variable (indicator)”. Dummy variables were used to indicate the missing covariate values for “variable (indicator)”, if covariate values were missing, we denote it as “not recorded” [22, 23].

The Cox proportional hazards model (Cox model) is a widely used method for analyzing survival data. It is a semi-parametric model that allows for the analysis of the time to event data, where the primary interest is in the time until a particular event occurs. The model is particularly useful in the context of censored data, where not all individuals in the study experience the event of interest by the end of the study period. The Cox model assumes that the hazard ratio, which is the ratio of the hazard rates for two groups, is constant over time. The Cox model is given by the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\lambda\:\left(t|{x}_{1},{x}_{2},\cdots\:,{x}_{p}\right)={\lambda\:}_{0}\left(t\right)exp\left({\beta\:}_{1}{x}_{1}+{\beta\:}_{2}{x}_{2}\cdots\:{\beta\:}_{p}{x}_{p}\right)$$\end{document}λt|x1,x2,⋯,xp=λ0texpβ1x1+β2x2⋯βpxp

Where λ(t|x1​,x2​,…,xp​) is the hazard function, representing the instantaneous rate of occurrence of the event of interest at time t given the values of the covariates x1​,x2​,…,xp​. λ0​(t) is the baseline hazard function, which is an unspecified function representing the hazard for an individual with all covariate values equal to zero. It is a function of time but does not depend on the covariates. exp(β1​x1​+β2​x2​+… + βp​xp​) is the hazard ratio, which describes how the hazard for an individual with covariate values x1, x2, …, xp compares to the baseline hazard. β1​,β2​,…,βp​ are the regression coefficients corresponding to the covariates x1​,x2​,…,xp​, respectively. The model allows for the estimation of the regression coefficients β using partial likelihood methods, which do not require specification of the baseline hazard function. This makes the Cox model robust to the choice of the baseline hazard and flexible for analyzing data with various types of censoring.

The univariate binary logistic regression (Cox model, time: ALOS) was used to analyze the possible associations of variables (including fibrinogen level) and the incidence of PI (Table 2). The multivariate binary logistic regression method (Cox model, time: ALOS) were used to analyze the association of fibrinogen level and the incidence of PI with three models (Table 3). Model 1 was the non-adjusted model with no covariates adjusted. We used a multivariate logistic regression analysis to screen out the covariates of fibrinogen level and the incidence of PI; the variables were excluded if the variance inflation factor(VIF) was greater than ten [24, 25]. Subsequently, potential confounders were selected if they changed the estimates of incidence of PI by at least 10% in the final models [26, 27] (Table S1). Model 2 was the minimally adjusted model with the covariate (presented in Table S1). Model 3 was the fully adjusted model adjusted for covariate presented in Table S1 and probable risk factors for PI presented in Table 2.

Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) are an extension of linear models that provide a framework for analyzing data where the relationship between the response variable and the predictors is not necessarily linear. GAMs achieve this by replacing the linear components of the predictors with smooth functions, which can be thought of as a series of basis functions that capture the nonlinear trends in the data. The use of smooth functions allows for the estimation of complex, nonparametric relationships while maintaining the model’s interpretability. GAMs are particularly advantageous when dealing with continuous predictors that exhibit a nonlinear pattern or when the data suggests that a linear model may not adequately capture the underlying structure. The flexibility of GAMs lies in their ability to automatically select the appropriate level of smoothing for each predictor, thus reducing the need for subjective decisions about the form of the relationship. The two-piece binary logistic regression model is an extension of the standard logistic regression model that allows for different regression equations above and below a certain threshold. This model is designed to capture situations where the relationship between the predictor variables and the log-odds of the outcome is not constant across the range of the predictors. It is particularly relevant when there is a clear breakpoint or threshold in the data, beyond which the dynamics of the relationship change. In a two-piece model, the logistic regression is divided into two segments, with a knot at a specified value of the predictor variable. The knot serves as the point of transition between the two pieces of the model. The two segments are estimated separately, and the model includes parameters that describe the change in the slope at the knot. The layered or stratified binary logistic regression model is a valuable analytical tool for examining the relationship between predictor variables and a binary outcome while accounting for heterogeneity in the baseline risk across distinct subgroups. This approach involves estimating separate logistic regression models within each stratum and is akin to a series of subgroup analyses that share parameters across strata for certain covariates. Stratification is often employed when there is a suspected effect modifier, such as gender, age group, or disease subtype that could potentially influence the relationship between the predictors and the outcome. By stratifying the analysis, the model can more accurately estimate the unique effects of predictors within each subgroup, thereby providing a more nuanced understanding of the data. Results

Characteristics of the study population

Significant differences were observed between the PI-positive and PI-negative groups across several parameters: age, fibrinogen level, D-dimer level, smoking history, UTI, DVT, anticoagulant therapy, mode of injury, AIS grades, level of injury and chest comorbidities (all P < 0.001), indicating a marked distinction between the groups. However, ALOS, sex, surgical history, T2D, and HBP showed no significant differences (P > 0.05). Notably, the imputation of missing D-dimer data did not alter these outcomes, confirming the robustness of our results (Table 1).

Univariate binary logistic regression analysis of fibrinogen level and incidence of PI

Univariate logistic regression analysis identified multiple factors significantly associated with PI incidence. Positively correlated factors (HR > 1) included age (HR = 1.02), fibrinogen level (HR = 1.23), D-dimer level (HR = 1.04), UTI (HR = 2.02), DVT (HR = 2.06), anticoagulant therapy (HR = 2.18), and chest comorbidities(chest or lung injury[HR = 2.37] and combined injury[HR = 5.33] versus No) all demonstrating statistical significance(P < 0.05). In contrast, factors negatively correlated with PI incidence (HR < 1) included ALOS, mode of injury, AIS-D grade, and levels of injury (thoracic and lumbar versus cervical), again showing significant associations (P < 0.05) (Table 2).

Multivariate binary logistic regression analysis of fibrinogen level and incidence of PI

After accounting for multicollinearity, highlighted the surgery, level of injury and chest comorbidities as significant covariates(Table S1). Model 1, without adjustments, showed each 1 g/L increase in fibrinogen level raised the PI incidence by 23% (HR = 1.23, P < 0.001). Model 2, adjusting for the covariates (surgery, level of injury and chest comorbidities), showed a 18% increase (HR = 1.18, P = 0.010), and Model 3, further adjusting for the covariates (surgery, level of injury and chest comorbidities) and identified possible risk factors presented in Table 2(age, D-dimer level[imputed], UTI, DVT, anticoagulant therapy, mode of injury, AIS grades, level of injury and chest comorbidities), also showed a 18% increase (HR = 1.18, P = 0.011), underscoring fibrinogen’s impact on PI incidence (Table 3). In the context of clinical practice, our findings indicate that for each 1 g/L increment in fibrinogen levels, there is a clinically meaningful increase in the risk of PI by 18%. This elevation in risk underscores the potential for fibrinogen to serve as a prognostic indicator in SCI patients, guiding clinicians to consider more vigilant monitoring or preemptive interventions.

Curvilinear fitting analysis of fibrinogen level and incidence of PI

Once we adjusted for confounding variables (age, D-dimer level[imputed], UTI, DVT, anticoagulant therapy, mode of injury, AIS grades, surgery, level of injury and chest comorbidities), we observed a positive linear association between fibrinogen levels and the incidence of PI in patients with SCI (Fig. 2).

Sensitive analysis of fibrinogen level and incidence of PI

Sensitivity analyses, including stratified and interaction analyses, confirmed the robustness of the fibrinogen-PI incidence association. Notably, the significant P-values (P < 0.05) indicate that plasma fibrinogen levels were more strongly related to PI risk in certain subgroups, particularly among younger and older age groups, lower D-dimer level subgroup, male, surgical patients, no smoking, UTI, DVT, non-T2D, HBP individuals, and patients with traumatic injuries or AIS-B and C grades, cervical or thoracic level of injury. These results suggest that fibrinogen may be a significant factor in predicting PI risk in these specific populations (Fig. 3). Interaction analyses indicated surgery as a potential effect modifier, but this was not sustained after adjusting for confounders, suggesting no significant interaction effects among the analyzed confounders (Table S2-S6).

Discussion

This study substantiated the role of surgery, injury level and chest comorbidities as pivotal covariates in the relationship between fibrinogen level and the incidence of PI among SCI patients. Notably, age, D-dimer levels, UTI, DVT, anticoagulant therapy, injury mechanism, AIS grades, injury level and chest comorbidities were identified as potential risk factors for PI. Crucially, our findings revealed that for each 1 g/L increase in fibrinogen levels, the incidence of PI escalates by 18%, underscoring a significant positive linear correlation.

Pneumonia, a primary cause of mortality post-acute SCI [28], parallels findings in elderly patients with acute stroke and post-operative hip fractures [29, 30], where age and comorbid conditions like hypertension and diabetes have been flagged as PI risk factors [31]. And a study showed among children with plastic bronchitis, 67.44% (29/43) had elevated D-dimer and fibrinogen levels [32]. A total of 1853 pediatric patients who sustained either cervical or thoracic SCI, ventilator-associated pneumonia was a most frequently encountered adverse events [33]. Nevertheless, the relationship between age, D-dimer and PI in patients with SCI has not been reported. Our study found that age and D-dimer level may associated with the incidence of PI in this population.

Patients with SCI had impaired immune function, which was clinically influenced by the phase of injury, completeness of injury, sympathetic innervation impairment, age, and sex [34]. SCI grades, particularly AIS-C and D, drastically alters the relationship between level of injury and SCI-induced immune deficiency syndrome, which was also known as “immune paralysis” [35]. This immune deficiency can lead to clinically relevant infections, including PI, because SCI disrupts neural and humoral control of immune cells, culminating with the development of “SCI-induced immune deficiency syndrome“ [36]. Incomplete lesions (AIS-C and D grade) were predictive of lower odds for infections (including PI) for patients with SCI [4]. Additionally, a study showed that patients with motor-complete traumatic SCI (AIS-A or B grade) had a higher incidence of secondary health conditions, such as PI and UTI, compared to patients with motor-incomplete traumatic SCI (AIS-A or B grade) [37]. And another study reported individuals with traumatic or AIS A, B, or C tetraplegia were at highest risk for pneumonia [38].Further, a machine learning study found that age, AIS grade and tracheotomy were the independent risk factors of pneumonia in SCI [39]. Our research corroborated lower infection odds in patients with incomplete lesions (AIS-C and D), diverging from the heightened secondary health condition risks seen in patients with motor-complete traumatic SCI (AIS-A or B).

Higher D-dimer level was a risk factor of DVT, and mode of injury-trauma and lower limb motor weakness should be considered when analyzing D-dimer levels [40]. Patients who experience traumatic spine injuries, had a higher incidence of DVT, UTI and pneumonia for patients with SCI [41]. D-dimer and fibrinogen level were risk factors for venous thromboembolism of spinal fractures, especially for SCI [42]. Anticoagulant therapy could decrease incidence of DVT for SCI [43]. However, there have been no reports on the relationship between anticoagulant therapy, DVT and pneumonia in patients with SCI. In our study, we found AIS grades, level of injury and UTI may associated with PI in patients with SCI, and found DVT in lower extremities, anticoagulant therapy and mode of injury maybe risk factors for PI. The level of injury, identified as a significant covariate, suggests that the clinical context, particularly the severity and location of the SCI, plays a pivotal role in the manifestation of PI. This insight is crucial for clinicians when assessing the need for targeted preventative strategies, such as early mobilization or respiratory therapy, in patients with different levels of SCI.

Plasma fibrinogen could be used to measure the severity of acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [44] and could be considered an indicator of the body’s inflammatory state [45]. A research showed that plasma fibrinogen was a marker of lung infection in patients with cystic fibrosis and could be used to predict prognosis [46]. It could also be a useful indicator for early prediction of infected nonunion [47]. A vitro study showed drug-resistant pathogenic Staphylococcus aureus can bind to fibrinogen and increase biofilm formation and the attaching of Staphylococcal protein A to the cell wall, which may induce lethal pneumonia [48]. Activation of fibrinogen expression results in an increase in inflammatory reactions [49]. Another study found that a high fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio was an independent potential risk factor for stroke-associated pneumonia [50]. Furthermore, plasma fibrinogen level was considered as a prognostic factor for sepsis in a study [51]. Anticoagulants, such as heparin and warfarin, affect the coagulation process through different mechanisms. The use of anticoagulants can reduce the plasm fibrinogen levels [52, 53].Although, up to now, there has been no literature found on the effects of anticoagulant therapy on PIs. However, as mentioned above, fibrinogen levels may increase after PI, and anticoagulant therapy can reduce fibrinogen levels, so anticoagulant therapy may have some effect on PIs. Of course, this requires further research. Fibrinogen levels can fluctuate due to various factors including inflammation, infection, and the progression of disease. Dynamic monitoring over time can provide insights into these changes and help in the timely adjustment of treatment strategies. Longitudinal data on fibrinogen levels may have predictive value for patient outcomes, and this was a direction for future research. After adjusting for confounders, our study showed plasma fibrinogen was an independent potential risk factor and had a positive linear association with the incidence of PI in patients with SCI. Given the independent association between plasma fibrinogen levels and the risk of PI, clinicians should consider incorporating fibrinogen level assessments into routine care for SCI patients. This could facilitate timely interventions, potentially reducing the incidence of PI and improving patient outcomes.

One of the primary limitations of this retrospective study is its inability to establish causality. Unlike experimental studies, which can manipulate variables and control for confounding factors, our observational design is constrained to analyzing data as it was collected, without the capacity to influence the sequence of events or the context in which data were generated. The temporal sequence of events, a critical component for inferring causality, cannot be definitively ascertained from retrospective data. While we have identified associations between variables of interest, we cannot conclude that one variable causes the other. It is plausible that the observed relationships are due to confounding factors, reverse causality, or other unmeasured variables that were not accounted for in our analysis. Despite these limitations, our study provides valuable insights into association of plasma fibrinogen level and incidence of PI. The findings contribute to the existing body of literature and can inform future research, including prospective studies designed to explore causality more directly. It is recommended that the results of this study be interpreted with caution and used to generate hypotheses rather than to draw definitive conclusions about causal relationships. Future research employing prospective cohort or randomized controlled trials, to validate the findings and establish causality and randomized controlled trials will be essential to confirm our findings and to explore the causal mechanisms underlying the observed associations.

We acknowledge the small sample size of this study may limit the generalizability of the findings, as it may not be representative of the broader population. Future research should aim to replicate these findings in larger, multicenter studies to validate the results and explore their applicability across different populations and healthcare systems, and to enhance the generalizability and impact of the findings.

We also acknowledge the limitation of our study stemming from the lack of external validation, which is crucial for establishing the generalizability and robustness of our findings. The relationship between plasma fibrinogen levels and the incidence of PI in SCI patients, as observed in our study, needs to be confirmed in independent cohorts to ensure that the results are not specific to our patient population or setting.

The importance of external validation in confirming the reliability and applicability of our findings cannot be ignored. To address this limitation, we propose that future research should focus on conducting external validation studies in diverse and distinct populations. These studies could involve multicenter trials or collaborations with other rehabilitation centers to include a broader range of SCI patients with varying demographics, injury severities, and comorbidities. By doing so, researchers can assess the consistency of the association between fibrinogen levels and PI risk across different settings and patient groups. This process is crucial for establishing the robustness of the observed relationship between fibrinogen levels and PI risk, and for determining whether the results can be confidently applied in clinical practice.

In light of these considerations, we recommend that future research prioritize external validation of our findings. By doing so, we can enhance the credibility of our results and their implications for clinical practice, ultimately contributing to improved patient care and outcomes. Despite these constraints, our findings offer a foundation for subsequent research, advocating for more robust clinical trials and cohort studies to explore the long-term interplay between fibrinogen level and PI in SCI populations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, plasma fibrinogen level emerged as an independent predictor of PI risk in SCI patients, especially for AIS-B and C grades. Proactive fibrinogen level management post-admission could be instrumental in curtailing PI incidence among this vulnerable cohort, emphasizing the need for targeted interventions in the rehabilitation medicine department.
