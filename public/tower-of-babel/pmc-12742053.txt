Improving access to inpatient palliative care for patients with end-stage liver disease: a quality improvement project
Authors: Elvina Ward, Georgina Hanbury, Lucia Possamai, Thomas Rassam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEnd-stage liver disease (ESLD) is a terminal diagnosis with a poor prognosis and an accelerating mortality rate in the UK. Limited research suggests that patients with ESLD have unmet palliative care (PC) needs, likely due to various factors, including difficulty predicting prognosis and lack of clear specialist palliative care (SPC) referral criteria.This quality improvement (QI) project aimed to assess and improve access to PC for inpatients with ESLD and unmet PC needs over a 6-month period using a novel intervention incorporating the Supportive and Palliative Care Indicators Tool for Liver Disease (SPICT).MethodsThe project was conducted at a tertiary centre between 2020 and 2022 with three data cycles: one baseline measurement and two Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles. PDSA cycle 1 involved regular screening of inpatients using the SPICT to trigger SPC referral. PDSA cycle 2 also involved screening using the SPICT, but instead to prompt multidisciplinary meeting (MDM) discussion between hepatology and SPC teams. Outcome measures reflecting holistic aspects of PC were assessed across all cycles.ResultsPDSA cycle 1 demonstrated a significant reduction in patients receiving no PC measures (67% to 26%, p: <0.001) and increased SPC input (32% to 52%, p: 0.04). Community PC referrals also rose significantly (13% to 39%, p: 0.01).PDSA cycle 2 failed to improve measures compared to baseline. Only 42% of eligible patients were discussed in the MDM. Most agreed plans were implemented, but only a minority included SPC review, community PC referral or advanced care planning.ConclusionPatients with ESLD continued to have unmet PC needs despite implementing an intervention based on expert guidance. PDSA cycle 1 demonstrated some significant, positive impacts, supporting the clinical utility of the SPICT, but within the context of a small, single-centre QI project with methodological limitations.Keywords: Palliative Care; Healthcare quality improvement; Decision support, clinical; PDSA; Patient-centred care

Introduction

Problem description

End-stage liver disease (ESLD) is a terminal diagnosis with a poor prognosis and significant morbidity.1,4 However, there remains a lack of national and local palliative care (PC) guidelines for this patient population, and limited data suggest that patients with ESLD have unmet PC needs.12 5,7

Available knowledge

The leading causes of mortality from liver disease include alcohol-related liver disease (ARLD), non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and viral hepatitis.8 They share a pathophysiology of progressive liver inflammation and eventual cirrhosis.9 75% of cirrhosis diagnoses occur at a late-stage crisis point.10 Patients with ESLD experience a significant symptom burden similar to cancer.2 The median survival for compensated disease is 12 years, compared with 2 years for ESLD.3 Only a minority of patients qualify for liver transplantation, and up to 11% die while waiting.9 11

Mortality from liver disease is rising in the UK; the rate has increased by 400% since 1970, reaching 10,858 deaths in 2023.1012,14 Most deaths are secondary to ARLD (55% in England in 2023).14 50% of deaths from liver disease in the UK occur between ages 45 and 64.15 In 2023, men accounted for 64% of premature liver deaths.14 16 Between 70% and 75% of patients with ESLD in England die in hospital, approximately 25% higher than the all-cause death average.17 18

The National Health Service defines PC as holistic physical, psychological, social and spiritual support for patients with life-limiting illnesses and their caregivers. PC transitions into end-of-life care once a patient reaches the final stage of illness.19

Only 11% of patients with cirrhosis receive specialist palliative care (SPC) or hospice referral, often late in their disease course.20,24 Small retrospective studies have highlighted low rates of do-not-attempt-cardiopulmonary-resuscitation and advanced care planning (ACP) documentation.20 25 Patients report poor information-sharing regarding disease progression, symptom control and support services.6 7 26 27 Caregivers of patients with ESLD rate the overall quality of care as poorer than those caring for patients with other advanced diseases, with research highlighting high caregiver burden and reduced quality of life.28 29

Rationale

The barriers to PC are likely multifactorial, including lack of primary palliative care (PPC) training for hepatologists and absence of evidence-based SPC referral criteria.5 The British Association for the Study of the Liver Palliative Hepatology Special Interest Group (BASL PHSIG) regards prognostic uncertainty as a major barrier.1 A US national survey found that 86% of hepatologists recognised patients with ESLD benefitted from PC early in their disease course, and 84% agreed that they were the best clinicians to broach ACP. However, they did not initiate this themselves or involve SPC clinicians partly because of prognostic uncertainty.30

Both the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) and BASL PHSIG recommend PC to include symptom control, ceiling of care (COC) discussions, ACP and caregiver support. However, the PC implementation framework remains absent for patients with ESLD.1 5 Evidence suggests that inpatient SPC involvement with community follow-up can align patient and clinician goals, increase comfort-focused care and decrease hospital readmissions.23 Outpatient PC may be associated with improved symptom burden, ACP and care coordination.31 32 A prospective cohort study of inpatients with ESLD demonstrated that 43% would prefer to die rather than receive care in a nursing home.4

PC is often confused with end-of-life care and wrongly associated with the cessation of active treatment.33 However, PC can be provided at any stage alongside active treatment, a strategy termed ‘parallel planning’.5 Both active treatment and PC are provided simultaneously; medical management continues while preparing the patient and their caregivers for deterioration and encouraging ACP. The balance can be adjusted based on the patient’s current needs.1 33 A growing body of evidence supports parallel planning for patients with ESLD and is recommended by AASLD and BASL PHSIG.1 5

There is a lack of local and national PC guidelines and quality measures designed for patients with ESLD.1 5 To address this, AASLD recently published practical guidance recommending two strategies to improve access to PC for patients with ESLD: automating SPC involvement using unspecified trigger criteria; and collaboration between hepatology and SPC clinicians.5 Both AASLD and BASL PHSIG advise that while hepatology clinicians require more PPC training, SPC teams should be involved to support the more complex needs of patients with ESLD.1 5 Numerous researchers suggest that collaboration between hepatology and SPC teams could significantly benefit this patient population.1 9 33 Despite this, SPC involvement remains uncommon.34

Prognostication of ESLD is challenging. In 2019, BASL PHSIG recommended using the Supportive and Palliative Care Indicators Tool for Liver Disease (SPICT) or the Bristol Prognostic Screening Tool (BPST) to more accurately identify those with a poor prognosis.1 In a 2021 study, a panel of SPC clinicians compared their prognostic value for patients with ESLD by assessing their ability to identify inpatients with PC needs benefitting from multidisciplinary discussion between hepatology and SPC teams.35 The SPICT showed excellent sensitivity and positive predictive value (PPV) for identifying SPC needs (96% and 93%, respectively), outperforming the BPST (58% and 79%). For details of the SPICT, see online supplemental table S1. No research has been published since to evaluate the clinical application of the SPICT specifically for patients with ESLD.

Specific aims

This quality improvement (QI) project aimed to assess and improve access to PC for inpatients with ESLD and unmet PC needs over a 6-month period using a novel intervention incorporating the SPICT, with an aspirational target of 80% compliance across all PC measures.

Methods

Context

The project was conducted in the inpatient hepatology unit of a UK tertiary centre between November 2020 and April 2022.

Intervention

Our intervention was designed to reflect the AASLD strategies to improve PC for patients with ESLD.1 We hypothesised integrating the SPICT into clinical practice would clarify which patients had unmet PC needs, and therefore serve as an effective trigger to automate SPC involvement and enhance collaboration between the two teams.

We conducted two Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles to test our hypothesis and refine our approach (see online supplemental figure S1 for a pictorial overview of the cycles).36

PDSA cycle 1 involved regular screening of the inpatient list by hepatology clinicians using the SPICT. Patients who met the criteria were identified as ‘having unmet PC needs’, triggering SPC referral and the implementation of holistic PC measures. The aim was for the two teams to provide a parallel planning approach for patients, including ACP and COC discussions, caregiver involvement and outpatient PC referral on discharge. We anticipated that achieving this level of collaboration might be challenging without formal case discussions.

PDSA cycle 2 was based on the hypothesis that formal case discussions between the specialties would establish a clearer ‘direction of travel’ for parallel planning and create ownership of the different PC measures. Therefore, we created a fortnightly Enhanced Supportive Care multidisciplinary meeting (ESC MDM), attended by both hepatology and SPC clinicians, to formally discuss and plan holistic PC measures. Screening with the SPICT continued as in cycle 1, but instead, patients were referred to the MDM. The MDM discussions regarding COC and ACP were intended as a prelude to direct discussions between clinicians and patients, to ensure coherent and personalised advice was provided, rather than to be decided paternalistically.

Before implementing the intervention, we provided education to the hepatology team regarding the QI project aim, the role and application of the SPICT, and the holistic aspects of PC (the outcome measures) that should be provided to patients who met the criteria with the help of the SPC team. A ‘SPICT’ column was added to the electronic inpatient list to prompt its use in clinical practice.

The project team consisted of junior doctors working in the hepatology department, supported by a consultant hepatologist as the project supervisor. Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct or dissemination of this QI project.

Study of the intervention

Cycle 1 was implemented over a 3-month period from April to July 2021. The dataset was collected immediately at the end of the cycle.

Cycle 2 was implemented over a 3-month period from July to October 2021. The dataset was not collected until April 2022 to allow time for the multidisciplinary team to implement the agreed MDM plans.

Patients were identified retrospectively from historical electronic hepatology inpatient lists. The inclusion criteria consisted of inpatients who had a chronic liver disease diagnosis and met the SPICT criteria. This was deemed sufficient to define a patient as having ESLD, as formally diagnosing liver cirrhosis via liver biopsy or elastography scan is often not clinically necessary or achievable. Patients were excluded if they: did not have an underlying liver parenchymal disease resulting in cirrhosis (eg, liver metastases); were admitted due to an index presentation of ESLD; were electively admitted for a procedure or were reviewed by the hepatology team but remained under the care of another specialty. Patients admitted during the first wave of the COVID-19 pandemic (March–June 2020) were also excluded. The same inclusion and exclusion criteria were used for both PDSA cycles.

Data were collected via the electronic health record software, Cerner. Microsoft Excel was used for all data collection and analysis, using a standardised data collection proforma.

Measures

To address our aim, we developed five outcome measures (see table 1) that reflected the core components of optimal PC for patients with ESLD, as defined by the AASLD and BASL PHSIG.1 5

QI project outcome measures

QI, quality improvement.

The compliance rate of these five domains was used to quantitatively assess improvements in access to PC. Initially, we set an aspirational target of 80% compliance for each outcome measure. However, we realised that this was infeasible after collecting the baseline data. We instead aimed to significantly increase the compliance rate of each outcome measure.

‘SPC input’ included SPC involvement for either advice or direct patient review.

‘Ward-based ceiling of care’ (WBCOC) is defined as restricting a patient’s medical treatment to what can be safely provided on general hospital wards, excluding escalation to critical care settings of high dependency units or intensive care units (Level 2 or 3 care).37

‘ACP’ included planning with patients regarding future care, such as preferred place of care or death, advanced directives or parallel planning regarding the balance between active treatment and PC.

Symptom control was not assessed as an outcome measure due to the limitations of our data collection methodology and because it has been more extensively studied than the holistic measures we used.1

We collected the data at baseline and at the end of each PDSA cycle. The data were collected by two authors with a 100% agreement rate from a subset of data.

Analysis

We compared the compliance rate of the outcome measures between PDSA cycles and with the baseline population.

Demographic data were collected for the cycles and compared with that of the baseline population and national demographic data.

The two-proportion z-test was used to assess the statistical significance of the comparisons. The paired t-test was used to compare mean ages. A two-tailed hypothesis was used for demographic data, and a one-tailed hypothesis was used to assess intervention efficacy. A significance level of p: 0.05 was used.

We created run charts to visually track the compliance rates of each outcome measure over time; assess for any patterns or trends and identify any early signs of sustainability in the post-intervention period. Analysis of the charts was conducted with caution due to the lack of longer-term data collection.

We observed a significant improvement in SPC input, increased community PC referrals, shorter time to SPC referral and younger age at referral. The proportion of patients receiving no PC measures dropped significantly from 67% to 26% (31/46 to 6/23, z: 3.24, p: <0.001).

No significant improvements were seen in the other outcome measures. Interestingly, WBCOC documentation significantly decreased, with a significant increase in patients with no documented COC.

PDSA cycle 2

From July to October 2021, 78 patients were admitted under the hepatology team. After applying the exclusion criteria, 64% of patients (50/78) had ESLD and met the SPICT criteria. However, the hepatology team only correctly identified 42% (21/50) of these for MDM discussion. The average age of the identified patients was 56 years (t: 0.86, p: 0.39) and 76% were men (z: −0.61, p: 0.54). The majority were caused by ARLD (76%, 16/21, z: −0.98, p: 0.33), followed by NAFLD (24%, 5/21, z: −1.50, p: 0.13).

The proportion of patients who received SPC input (42%), via referral to the MDM, increased compared with baseline (32%). However, this was not statistically significant (z: −1.07, p: 0.14) and was lower compared with cycle 1 (52%, z: 0.83, p: 0.41).

Among patients discussed in the MDM, plans were fully implemented in 71% of cases (15/21), partially in 14% (3/21) and not implemented in 14% (3/21).

Only 48% of plans (10/21) included SPC review, community PC referral or ACP discussion. SPC involvement followed in 38% of cases (8/21). Nearly one-third of patients discussed (29%, 6/21) were deemed inappropriate for SPC involvement, despite meeting the SPICT criteria and two of these patients dying during the same admission. One patient was deemed to have no PC needs but was later reviewed by the SPC team.

COC was discussed in 62% of cases (13/21), although only one was documented as ward-based. ACP occurred in just 19% of cases (4/21), which was lower than in both the baseline and cycle 1 cohorts. Caregiver involvement remained unchanged from baseline, occurring in 43% of cases (9/21). A small, insignificant increase in community PC referrals was observed compared with baseline (13% (6/46) vs 19% (4/21), z: −0.64, p: 0.26), which was lower than that of cycle 1.

Discussion

Summary and interpretation

This QI project aimed to assess and improve access to PC for inpatients with ESLD and unmet PC needs over a 6-month period using a novel intervention incorporating the SPICT.

At baseline, only a minority of patients with ESLD who met the SPICT criteria received holistic PC measures.

PDSA cycle 1 demonstrated some significant, positive impacts using the parallel planning model: a reduction in patients receiving no PC measures; an increase in timely SPC input and an increase in community PC referrals. Community PC referral, the least implemented domain at baseline, showed the greatest improvement. The run charts also highlight the positive impact of cycle 1, with an immediate and clear increase in both SPC input and community PC referrals compared with the median line (see online supplemental figures S4 and S8). The mean age of patients referred to SPC also significantly decreased. Unlike the baseline population, most patients receiving PC measures did not die later during the same admission. These findings suggest that the SPICT helped hepatologists objectively identify patients with ESLD and unmet PC needs, addressing prognostic uncertainty and lack of established SPC referral criteria as raised by AASLD and BASL PHSIG.1 5

However, the other PC measures showed no improvement in cycle 1. The significant decrease in WBCOC documentation may have been an unexpected consequence of the intervention, shifting the focus of clinicians from these domains to those that demonstrated improvement.

The run chart for caregiver updates (online supplemental figure S7) suggests that both intervention cycles reduced variability in the compliance rate, resulting in a process with greater stability.

In PDSA cycle 2, the ESC MDM ensured consistent SPC review for patient cases once referred. However, the hepatology team only referred a minority of patients meeting the SPICT, and no significant improvements in PC measures were seen compared with baseline. The run charts visually emphasise the failures of the cycle 2 intervention. For example, the compliance rate of ACP documentation quickly fell below the baseline median, indicating a loss of process control following MDM implementation (see online supplemental figure S6). These findings highlight major flaws within cycle 2.

We assumed an MDM could improve PC measures without a standardised proforma to guide discussions. This was omitted to allow clinicians to adapt discussions to different patient cases but may have caused a lack of direction, inconsistency and avoidance of contentious topics. Despite the lack of PC measures, most agreed management plans were fully implemented, suggesting misdirected efforts or a focus on care already routinised.

The education provided by the project team to the clinicians regarding the MDM may have been insufficient, resulting in a lack of ownership and buy-in. Collecting the dataset shortly after the MDM was established may have introduced bias as clinicians could still have been familiarising themselves with the new system.

Team engagement factors also likely played a significant role, such as inter-specialty discordance, insufficient PPC training among hepatologists, competing clinical priorities or SPC under-representation. The formal setting of the MDM may have created role clarity issues. The hepatologists may have felt once a patient was discussed in the MDM, the responsibility for all PC measures was delegated to the SPC team, while the SPC team may have viewed their role as more advisory. Compared with cycle 1, the structured MDM may have created perceived rigidity, causing hepatologists to feel restricted in their clinical autonomy. Clinicians may have also perceived the structured MDM referral system as adding administrative burden to their workload.

A key strength of this QI project is its robust methodology, with a clear aim, outcome measures based on AASLD and BASL PHSIG guidance, appropriate analysis and transparent acknowledgement of the project’s limitations.1 5 The project was well-designed and executed within its resource and time constraints.

We aimed to improve patient outcomes and experiences by shifting care to a holistic, patient-centred approach embodying a value-based initiative, using AASLD guidance and an evidence-based screening tool.5 35 We aimed to develop an efficient intervention by leveraging the existing SPICT tool and SPC referral system to enhance multidisciplinary care coordination.

While a formal cost analysis was not performed, the project holds potential for financial benefits, including more efficient use of SPC services, reduced caregiver burden and decreased hospital readmissions, length of stay and critical care usage.

Limitations

This was a single-centre study with a small sample size due to limited researchers and inpatient turnover. Unaccounted variables, including shift-to-shift and month-to-month changes in clinicians, may have impacted the internal validity of our results.38

A key opportunity cost was the lack of longitudinal and qualitative data. Time constraints precluded the collection of more longitudinal data, preventing the assessment of longer-term variations in data and the sustainability of positive results. For example, given the sensitive nature of PC discussions, incremental PC involvement may have been evident over time. The lack of more longitudinal data also prevented a longer timeframe for continuous improvement.

We confined the data to binary measures for quantitative comparison of access to PC. However, qualitative data could have highlighted patient, clinician or service-related barriers to effective PC. These could have been addressed, aiding continuous improvement of our intervention. Clinician feedback could have explained why the MDM referral rate using the SPICT was low, while the SPICT appeared to improve some PC measures in cycle 1. It could have also provided explanations as to why most agreed management plans in the MDMs lacked PC measures.

Patient and caregiver involvement was absent due to resource constraints. Their input could have been invaluable to shift our process-driven model further towards a patient-centred, value-based initiative. This could have aligned our outcome measures and intervention more closely with the principles of patient autonomy and shared decision-making. Direct patient input would have also ensured the intervention was user-validated and practical within the patient’s real-world context, thus improving its sustainability and generalisability.

Although the intervention may have been theoretically sound in its design, patient and caregiver feedback could have unearthed issues with PC delivery, including patient-perceived gaps and preferences. For example, patients may have found our cycle 1 intervention more patient-centred as it involved more direct SPC communication. However, they may have found their overall care disjointed by the hepatology and SPC teams reviewing them separately. Patient-reported outcome measures could have been used to measure the quality of PC provided.

Clinicians knew they were being assessed, which may have caused the Hawthorne effect, potentially amplifying the positive changes observed.

We excluded patients admitted during the first COVID-19 pandemic wave between March and June 2020 to minimise its potential confounding effect.

Our demographic data reflects that of patients with ESLD in England, supporting the generalisability of our findings to other UK hepatology centres. The SPICT is transferable, having demonstrated excellent sensitivity and PPV in a UK hepatology centre similar to ours.35 We recommend its implementation given its user-friendly, generic, non-site-specific criteria, with few barriers to clinical integration.

However, the generalisability of our intervention is limited by many contextual factors.

Its effectiveness was resource-dependent, relying heavily on the availability and time commitment of both specialties, which may not be feasible in smaller or under-resourced settings. Direct replication would also be difficult for centres without dedicated hepatology units. The intervention may also be limited to centres with higher volumes of hepatology inpatients to ensure efficient utilisation of resources. Efficacy of the intervention would likely vary depending on hepatologists’ baseline PPC knowledge and training, and the quality of the local working relationship between the two specialties. Finally, our data collection methodology would be more arduous and error-prone for centres without electronic health record systems.

Conclusions

Our QI project provides an up-to-date perspective on the significant pitfalls in PC for patients with ESLD within a UK inpatient hepatology unit. Despite struggling to demonstrate significant improvement across all PC domains, we achieved some positive impacts, and our findings support the clinical utility of the SPICT. We have provided clinical evidence of barriers to effective PC: collaboration difficulties between hepatology and SPC clinicians; patient trajectory uncertainty; lack of hepatology PPC training and absence of clinically established SPC referral criteria.1 5

The intervention has since been sustained as a standard operating procedure; the ESC MDM continues to run and has increased in frequency from fortnightly to weekly. Its maintenance highlights the ability to effectively integrate this model of care into clinical practice and suggests positive clinician opinion regarding its potential benefits for the PC of this patient population. We aimed to design a sustainable intervention by integrating it within existing clinical practice, using a pre-existing SPC referral service, using an evidence-based screening tool, engaging key knowledge users and allowing for adaptations as needed.35

The run chart for community PC referral (see online supplemental figure S8) demonstrated a shift, with all six data points of the intervention cycles consistently above the median line. This provides early evidence of potential sustainability of this positive change. The other run charts did not demonstrate sustained improvements. However, we could not collect more longitudinal data beyond October 2021 to assess longer-term sustainability and variations in our results.

Our project provides actionable insights to accelerate best practices for patients with ESLD. We presented our project findings and recommendations for future work at the 2022 BASL Annual Meeting.39 We hope our work will be used in future practice and policy by raising awareness of the critical gap in PC for patients with ESLD and by providing a replicable model of care for other hepatology units. We also hope it will drive further research, including exploring adaptations of our intervention. For example, a combination of hepatology PPC training and our intervention may have a synergistic effect. Greater impact could be achieved through multicentre involvement, a larger patient cohort and the inclusion of longitudinal and qualitative data.
