Serum proteome analysis detects early molecular signatures of disease progression in IgA nephropathy
Authors: Tony Jialiang Chen, Eleni Skandalou, Katarina Fritz-Wallace, Terje Apeland, Thomas Knoop, Hans-Peter Marti, Øystein Eikrem, Jessica Furriol
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmunoglobulin A nephropathy (IgAN) is a common cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and identifying early molecular signatures is crucial for IgAN risk stratification and timely intervention. In this study we used serum proteomics to investigate molecular differences between IgAN patients who progressed and those who remained stable before separation in kidney function became apparent.MethodsSerum samples from 40 adults with biopsy-proven IgAN were analysed and classified as progressors (PRs; n = 13) or non-progressors (NPs; n = 27) based on the estimated glomerular filtration rate slope over a 5- to 6-year follow-up. Samples underwent protein depletion, SP3 digestion, tandem mass tag labelling and liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry analysis. Differentially expressed proteins were identified using Welch’s t-test. Enrichment analysis (Gene Ontology, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) was performed with ShinyGO and Cytoscape. Random forest machine learning was applied for classification modelling.ResultsFifty-two proteins showed a significant differential abundance between PRs and NPs. Enrichment analysis revealed dysregulation in complement cascade, extracellular matrix signalling and renin–angiotensin system in PRs compared with NPs. Finally, the random forest model on this protein set achieved an accuracy of 85.0% and identified NCAM1, F7, ARG1, CLU and ANXA5 among the most important predictors.ConclusionsSerum proteomic profiling identified early molecular differences between PR and NP IgAN patients. These findings support the utility of serum proteomics for early risk stratification in IgAN.Keywords: cell adhesion, complement-coagulation system, disease progression prediction, IgA nephropathy, proteomics

INTRODUCTION

Immunoglobulin A nephropathy (IgAN) is one of the most prevalent forms of primary glomerulonephritis (GN) worldwide, characterized by IgA deposition in glomerular mesangium [1]. Patients often present with haematuria and diagnosis is confirmed by kidney biopsies.

The disease progression varies significantly; while ≈50% achieve complete remission, others develop end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) [2]. This variability highlights the critical need for reliable methods to distinguish IgAN clinical subgroups and effectively tailor treatment strategies.

Various prognostic tools have been developed to assess IgAN progression. The Oxford MEST-C (mesangial hypercellularity, endocapillary hypercellularity, segmental glomerulosclerosis, interstitial fibrosis/tubular atrophy and crescents) score uses five histological lesions to predict renal outcomes [3]. However, repeat biopsies pose significant risks [4] and MEST-C’s predictive consistency remains debated [5–7]. The International IgAN Prediction Tool estimates 5-year risk of a 50% glomerular filtration rate (GFR) decline based on the clinical parameters and MEST score but lacks urinary or serum biomarkers, which could offer a simpler and potentially more accessible means of risk assessment [8].

In this pilot study, we performed serum proteomic profiling in an IgAN cohort from a previous prospective study on inflammatory biomarkers [9]. Samples were collected at the baseline, before divergence in estimated GFR (eGFR) after an average follow-up of 5–6 years. We aimed to identify early molecular markers of progression and applied supervised machine learning to identify potential predictive molecular features.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

We analysed serum samples from adult patients (age >18 years) with biopsy-confirmed IgAN (n = 40, M = 36). These individuals were part of a previously published longitudinal study of inflammatory biomarkers in IgAN [9]. Briefly, 40 patients with biopsy-verified IgAN and chronic kidney disease (CKD) stages 1–4 were enrolled. Inclusion required an eGFR >15 ml/min/1.73 m2; patients with diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular ischaemic disease or other significant comorbidities were excluded. Clinical data and serum samples were collected at baseline and patients were followed for an average of 5–6 years. Patients were categorized as progressor (PR) or non-progressor (NP), based on the eGFR slope calculated from the eGFR change divided by the years of follow-up. GFR was calculated by using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration formula [10]. The Oxford MEST-C score was not available.

Clinical data included 43 different variables consisting of standard laboratory blood work, urine measurements and physical traits such as sex, blood pressure and body mass index. The sample size (n = 40) was based on availability and the exploratory aim of identifying preliminary proteomic signals of disease progression. The study was approved by the Regional Ethics Committee Western Norway (2023/609670/REK vest).

Samples preparation

Serum samples (10 µl) were thawed on ice. Protein depletion was performed using Protein Depletion Mini Spin Columns (#A36369; Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). To minimize bias, sample handling and liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry analysis were standardized and batch order was randomized. Group assignment was based on objective clinical endpoints assessed independent of proteomic profiling.

Protein digestion and SP3 processing

Protein digestion followed the SP3 protocol (Thermo Fisher). Samples were treated with protease inhibitors (Roche cOmplete; Roche, Basel, Switzerland), 100 µM dithiothreitol, vortexed, sonicated (30 s) and heated (95°C, 7 min). Proteins were alkylated with 200 µM iodoacetamide (1 h, dark) and concentrations measured by bicinchoninic acid assay. SP3 beads (300 µg) were added for protein isolation. Digestion used trypsin (1:25 ratio) in ammonium bicarbonate with calcium chloride, followed by 18 h incubation at 37°C at 1000 rpm. After centrifugation (13 000 rpm, 3 min), overnatant was collected. After, 0.5 M sodium chloride was added to the beads and a second centrifugation and overnatant collection was performed.

Peptide desalting and tandem mass tag (TMT) labelling

Peptides were desalted using OASIS C18 columns. For TMT labelling (Thermo Fisher, A44520), peptides were resuspended in 50 mM HEPES (pH 8). TMT reagents (5 µg/µl in acetonitrile) were added at a 10:1 ratio and incubated (75 min, 1500 rpm). Labelling was repeated to ensure completeness. Reactions were quenched with hydroxylamine (10%, 5 µl, 15 min), freeze-dried and desalted using C18 columns prior to MS analysis.

MS analysis

The labelled peptides were analysed using an Orbitrap Eclipse Tribrid mass spectrometer with field asymmetric ion mobility spectrometry (Thermo Fischer) for improved data-dependent acquisition. MS spectra were acquired at 60 000 resolution (m/z 200), with an automatic gain control target of 4e5 and maximum injection time of 50 ms. Fragmentation was performed using higher-energy collision dissociation (HCD) at 32% collision energy and fragments were detected at 50 000 resolutions. Dynamic exclusion was set to 30 s with isotope exclusion enabled. The ion spray voltage was set to 2000 V with a capillary temperature of 275°C and no sheath or auxiliary gas flow.

Proteome data analysis

Processing of raw mass spectrometric data was performed using Proteome Discover Software (Thermo Fisher). Raw mass spectrometry data were searched against the human UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot database (release 2022_05, https://www.uniprot.org).

Raw data were normalized using total peptide amount using the Proteome Discover Software, then batch effect corrected using LIMMA normalized data and transferred to Perseus version 1.5.5.3 for statistical analysis [11]. Significance was assessed using Welch’s t-test and P-values ≤.05 were considered significant. Gene Ontology (GO) relevant terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis were retrieved from ShinyGO (http://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/) [12, 13]. GO and KEGG terms with a false discovery rate (FDR) ≤0.05 were considered significantly enriched. Protein-pathway heatmaps and cross-study pathway plots were generated using RStudio (version 4.3.2; Posit, Boston, MA, USA) and Cytoscape version 3.10.3 (Cytoscape Consortium, San Diego, CA, USA) [14] using the apps ReactomeFI and stringApp [15, 16].

The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited in the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository [17] with the dataset identifier PXD065216.

Machine learning classification

To assess the robustness of our classification model, we performed a sensitivity analysis using the random forest algorithm. A supervised random forest algorithm was implemented to classify patients based on differentially expressed proteins [18]. For each tree, two-thirds of the original dataset was randomly selected for each tree. Seven variables were randomly chosen from the total set of differential proteins (n = 52) to construct decision trees, and the process was repeated for 300 times. The remaining one-third [out-of-bag (OOB) samples] was used to evaluate the model’s performance, in which the accuracy of the model is calculated by majority vote of all trees. Although the cohort was imbalanced (PR = 13, NP = 27), random forest algorithms are generally robust to moderate class imbalance, and this limitation was considered in the interpretation of results.

This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cohort studies. A completed STROBE checklist is available in Supplementary File 1.

Proteomic profiling and identification of differentially abundant proteins

A total of 845 serum proteins with at least one unique peptide were identified. Proteome analysis showed that 55 serum proteins were differentially abundant in PRs compared with NPs. Three heavy immunoglobulins could not be mapped and were excluded from enrichment analysis.

A two-dimensional principal component analysis (PCA) followed by volcano plots were performed between PRs and NPs (Fig. 1). The PCA revealed some overlap, indicating similarities in the proteomic profiles of the two groups. Notable proteins upregulated in PRs were NRCAM and DNAJ3, while ARG1, F7, CLU and IHH were downregulated.

The top 10 most significant proteins are annotated.

The most significantly enriched BPs were cell adhesion, followed by proteolysis, external encapsulating structure organization and cell-substrate adhesion (Fig. 2A). Supervised random forest algorithm

To assess predictive potential, a random forest classifier was trained using the 52 differentially expressed proteins. Fig. 5 illustrates the relationship between the number of trees and the error rate for IgA NPs, IgA PRs and OOB. The error rate stabilizes well after 250 trees, with a 0.04% error rate for IgAN and 38.4% for IgA PRs. The OOB sample error rate was 15.0%, for a model accuracy of 85.0%.

Random forest classifier performance for predicting disease progression in IgAN. Graph shows the error rate of PRs, NPs and OOB samples versus the number of tree generated in the random forest model. The error rate stabilizes at 38.4%, 0.04% and 15.0%, respectively, when the number of trees is 300. The confusion matrix shows statistical parameters of IgA group prediction using 52 differentially regulated proteins. Model accuracy is 85.0%; true positive is 61.6% and true negative is 99.6%.

To identify the proteins that contribute most significantly to the model’s predictive performance, the mean decrease accuracy (MDA) was calculated through permutation of proteins. Fig. 6 presents the top 30 proteins, ranked by decreasing MDA values. The five with the highest importance are NCAM1, F7, ARG1, CLU and ANXA5. We did not observe significant correlations between the abundance of the proteins of interest and baseline clinical parameters, including serum creatinine, eGFR or proteinuria (Supplementary Table S2).

Correlation of candidate proteins with longitudinal eGFR slope

To further explore the clinical relevance of the classifier’s top features, we examined their association with the rate of renal function decline over the follow-up. As shown in Fig. 7, several of the top-ranked proteins correlated significantly with eGFR slope, including NCAM1 (R = 0.43, P = .0057), F7 (R = −0.39, P = .013), ARG1 (R = −0.35, P = .029), ANXA5 (R = −0.36, P = .021) and CCN2 (R = −0.50, P = .0011). CLU showed a similar but non-significant trend (R = −0.28, P = .082). These findings strengthen the biological plausibility of the random forest predictors and demonstrate that several proteins identified at baseline correlate with renal decline.

DISCUSSION

Clinical and molecular features of progression in IgAN

We profiled serum proteomes from IgAN patients with long-term clinical follow-up and identified molecular differences between PRs and NPs prior to any divergence in routine clinical parameters. As expected, PRs displayed higher baseline proteinuria and steeper eGFR decline. The partial overlap observed in the PCA analysis suggests that serum proteomic differences are subtle at this early stage, consistent with the concept that molecular perturbations precede measurable clinical changes. Consistent with this, correlation analyses with eGFR slope demonstrated that several of the key serum proteins, including NCAM1, F7, ARG1, ANXA5 and CCN2, tracked significantly with the rate of renal function decline over the follow-up.

Although our cohort was predominantly male, which limits sex representation, this reflects the higher prevalence of IgAN in males and is consistent with previous studies [21]. This demographic skew should be considered when generalizing results. Unfortunately, detailed information on immunosuppressive therapy was not available but treatment is expected to have followed contemporary clinical guidelines, including the reported supportive care with ACEi/ARB [85% overall, similarly distributed between PRs (100%) and NPs (74.1)] and immunosuppression when clinically indicated. Nonetheless, these findings highlight key aspects of IgAN progression and may offer prognostic value.

Protein turnover and implication in ECM remodelling

Our proteomic data highlights several BPs are related to ECM structure and organization, reflecting the interconnected nature of ECM dysregulation, a key hallmark of CKD progression [22]. Our study identified several ECM signalling molecules and components in the serum, indicating a profibrotic profile in PRs. For instance, VCAM1, a key mediator in angiogenesis, is upregulated in parietal and tubular cells in IgAN and may promote myofibroblast formation [23, 24]. Another example is AXL, a tyrosine kinase receptor that is linked to epithelial–mesenchymal transition and fibrosis [25]. Selective inhibition of AXL has been shown to improve renal outcome in experimental models [26].

However, some findings were unexpected. While CCN2’s messenger RNA is known to be upregulated in mesangial proliferative lesions and correlates with tubulointerstitial injury [27], we observed lower CCN2 levels in our study. Similarly, COL5A1, that protects renal function and reduces scarring in mice [28], was high in PRs. It is possible that these proteins undergo context-dependent regulation, where their circulating abundance does not directly mirror intrarenal expression or activity. Serum levels may also reflect compensatory or feedback mechanisms rather than simple linear associations with fibrosis. Interestingly, despite these opposite expression patterns, ECM-related pathways emerged consistently across serum, glomerular and tubular datasets in our study and in prior reports, supporting ECM dysregulation as a pan-compartmental hallmark of IgAN progression [29]. Several urinary proteomics studies reinforce this concept, as different classifiers have captured fibrotic remodelling and predicted rapid disease progression with greater accuracy than clinical parameters alone, e.g. for interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy in CKD [30], or specifically for IgAN [31], and even later in the disease course [32].

Future work using network-based approaches and upstream regulatory analyses will be critical to determine whether proteins such as CCN2 and COL5A1 participate in alternative signalling circuits or represent systemic adaptations to progressive injury, thereby clarifying their contribution to the pathophysiology of IgAN progression.

Finally, we identified several proteins that function as immune modulators, such as ARG1 and SIGLEC5. ARG1 is an enzyme that hydrolyses L-arginine to L-ornithine and urea, which minimizes the L-arginine substrate for nitric oxide production, thus the lower levels of ARG1 in PR group might reflect more inflammatory activity and oxidative stress [33]. SIGLEC5, on the other hand, functions as a natural immune checkpoint inhibitor in T cells, and its higher levels might reflect an attempt at modulation of the inflammation [34].

Together, these findings suggest altered ECM and immune signalling between the groups, which may contribute to the progression of IgAN.

Complement cascades

Pathway analysis revealed strong activation of the complement and coagulation cascade in PRs. This is consistent with previous findings: Fibrinogen, the final product of FGA, plays an essential role in inflammation and fibrosis and has been associated with poor renal outcomes in IgAN [35, 36]. Similarly, CFHR3, a plasma protein that activates C3 by competitively inhibiting FH, has been implicated in various nephropathies along with IgAN [37]. Notably, CFHR3 deletion has been shown to increase mesangial deposits of C3, and C3 deposition may correlate with worse renal outcomes [38, 39]. Our findings of high FGA and CFHR3 supports higher inflammation and complement cascade activation in PRs. Although no significant differences of C3 was detected, we found lower CLU levels in PRs, which may indicate complement-mediated reduced protection [40–42]. Interestingly, we detected lower levels of F7 in PRs. Little is known regarding the role of F7 in IgAN and it may have contributed to the heavy proteinuria experienced in this group.

Several trials are ongoing utilizing different strategies in targeting complement-mediated systems, including the C5a receptor inhibitor avacopan [43] and selective factor B inhibitor iptacopan [44], which may be particularly relevant for IgAN patients with early complement activation.

RAS dysregulation

ACEi/ARB therapy remains a cornerstone of supportive treatment in IgAN [45], but IgAN patients are less likely to respond to treatment with severe clinical or histological features, such as low eGFR, hypoalbuminaemia and T1/T2 and E1 histological lesions on the MEST-C score [46]. In our cohort, the use of ACEi/ARB therapy was comparable between groups (100% in PRs and 74% in NPs), indicating that treatment exposure alone is unlikely to explain the observed proteomic differences. We found elevated levels of AGT in PRs compared with NPs, despite ongoing ACEi/ARB therapy, indicating persistent RAS activation and potential treatment resistance. This observation underscores the need to identify adjunctive therapeutic targets beyond conventional RAS blockade for high-risk IgAN cases.

Candidate biomarkers and machine learning

Using a supervised random forest model, we achieved 85% accuracy in distinguishing PRs from NPs based on serum proteome signatures. The top five molecules with the greatest importance were NCAM1, F7, ARG1, CLU and ANXA5. These proteins are involved in fibrosis, cellular adhesion and inflammatory signalling and may represent early markers of systemic disease activity. While no significant correlations with baseline eGFR or proteinuria were observed, this likely reflects the preclinical sampling stage, supporting their potential role as early indicators rather than concurrent disease severity markers. While direct reports linking ARG1, CLU, NCAM1, F7 and ANXA5 specifically to IgAN are limited, each of these proteins has established roles in kidney injury, inflammation or coagulation that make them biologically plausible contributors to IgAN progression. F7 and ARG1 have been previously discussed [47, 48].

CLU is a widely recognized urinary biomarker for tubular injury and has been associated with renal outcome in other contexts such as lupus, nephrotoxicity, delayed graft function and proteinuria progression in IgAN patients treated with budesonide [49–52]. Most recently, a study showed that elevated levels of urinary CLU are correlated with senescent renal epithelial cells and are able to predict CKD progression [53]. Low serum CLU levels in PR may reflect similar pathophysiology in IgAN. NCAM1 has been associated with membranous nephropathy [47] and implicated in interstitial fibrosis [48]. Although not previously linked to IgAN, its upregulation in PRs is consistent with ECM dysregulation. Finally, ANXA5 participates in apoptotic and membrane repair pathways relevant to nephron injury [54]. These mechanistic connections provide biological plausibility for our proteomic observations, while underscoring the need for direct validation in larger IgAN cohorts.

Integration with tissue-based studies

Our findings align with prior proteomic and transcriptomic results from glomerular and tubulointerstitial compartments in IgAN in our group [19, 20, 55]. While few individual proteins overlapped, shared enrichment in ECM and immune pathways was observed across serum, glomerular and tubular datasets, as shown in the cross-study plot.

For instance, Paunas et al. [20] reported significant increases in ECM proteins such as periostin, fibronectin and matrix metalloproteinases, particularly in patients with progressive disease. Similarly, our analysis highlighted enrichment of ECM-related pathways and specific proteins such as VCAM1, AXL and NCAM1.

In the transcriptomic study by Rivedal et al. [55], APOL5 and ZXDC were linked to inflammation and major histocompatibility complex activity, paralleling the role of immune complex formation in IgAN. Similarly, we found impaired complement system and proteolysis that could reflect inadequate regulation of immune responses and tissue homeostasis. These alterations could contribute to chronic inflammation and fibrosis, in agreement with the transcriptomic findings.

Notably, our classification model includes molecular features involved in complement and coagulation cascades as well as markers of inflammation and fibrosis. The overlapping molecular pathways and the observed prognostic power of proteomic and transcriptomic markers suggest that multi-omic integration could improve prediction of IgAN progression. Future studies would benefit from combining proteomic, transcriptomic and urinary markers in larger, multicentre cohorts to build a comprehensive IgAN prognostic tool.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. The small sample size (n = 40) may limit statistical power and generalizability. Nonetheless, the unique longitudinal design, with serum collected years before clinical divergence, provides a rare window into early systemic alterations. This study is exploratory, and findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating pending external validation.

The absence of Oxford MEST-C scores prevents direct comparison with histology. Nonetheless, according to the most recent 2025 KDIGO guidelines for IgAN, only proteinuria and eGFR are validated prognostic markers. Although serum samples were prospectively collected, the retrospective proteomic analysis may be affected by batch effects, despite consistent handling and analysis pipelines. The male-dominant, single-centre cohort may also limit applicability to females and other populations. Progression was defined by eGFR slope alone, which may not fully capture disease trajectories compared with composite endpoints including proteinuria or histology. Information on immunosuppressive treatment was not systematically documented, which precludes adjustment for therapy effects, although it is expected that treatment followed contemporary guidelines. While random forest modelling was applied to mitigate overfitting in a small dataset, this does not replace independent validation or allow for formal regression modelling of individual proteins. Finally, the internal cross-validation applied here cannot substitute for external validation.

Despite the small pilot cohort, proteomic pathways linked to IgAN progressions were identified and suggest broader relevance. Random forest classification with internal cross-validation is consistent with prior findings, strengthening the biological interpretation of the proteomic signatures.

CONCLUSION

Our study demonstrates that patients with IgAN who eventually progress to severe disease show early proteomic changes related to complement and coagulation cascades and ECM signalling. Machine learning identified several potential serum biomarkers, including NCAM1, F7, ARG1, CLU and ANXA5. These molecular signatures could serve as early indicators of disease progression, enabling more personalized treatment strategies for high-risk patients.
