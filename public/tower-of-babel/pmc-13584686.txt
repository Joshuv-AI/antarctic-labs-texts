Development of a Multi‐Organ Microphysiological (MPS) Model to Study Age‐Related Comorbidities
Authors: Gaurav Srivastava, Aakash Patel, Alice Rodriguez‐Fuguet, Akanksh Jambagi, Himanshi Jangir, Ahmad Nawaz, Afeef Mahmud, Camden Dwelle, Xiufang Guo, James Hickman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTComorbidities, the concurrent presence of two or more medical conditions, present significant challenges for diagnosis and treatment. Skeletal muscle sarcopenia and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) are two distinct yet interrelated conditions, often co‐existing in elderly individuals and exacerbating disease progression. Mechanistic understanding of sarcopenia‐HCM comorbidity is limited, largely based on clinical correlation analyses and a few available mouse models. To facilitate pathophysiological research and accelerate therapeutic development, a more efficient human‐relevant in vitro model is needed. This study presents a human‐based sarcopenia‐HCM comorbidity model in a multi‐organ microphysiological system (MPS) that incorporates primary human hepatocytes, human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSC)‐derived skeletal muscle and cardiomyocytes. The phenotypes for sarcopenia and HCM were first induced and characterized in single‐organ systems, then assembled and analyzed in a multi‐organ MPS, maintained in circulated serum‐free medium. The functional phenotypes of skeletal muscle, cardiomyocytes, and hepatocytes, as well as inter‐organ interactions in this organ‐chip system align with clinical reports. This MPS comorbidity platform will enable pathophysiological investigation of sarcopenia, HCM, their comorbidity, and testing of therapeutic efficacy, toxicity, and pharmacokinetics. This study highlights how multi‐organ MPS can revolutionize preclinical research as a human‐relevant, cost‐effective, and ethical alternative for studying comorbidities.

Introduction

Comorbidity is defined as the incidence of two or more diseases/disorders in the same individual. These disorders may interact with each other, which might affect the prognosis, symptoms, and treatment of each condition. It may also contribute to abnormal drug interactions for efficacy and toxicity, as well as affect the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of a therapeutic. Comorbidities are becoming increasingly prevalent among aging populations, where the occurrence of multiple concurrent conditions complicates clinical management. Sarcopenia, characterized by the progressive loss of skeletal muscle function and mass, and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), a condition involving pathological thickening of the heart's myocardium, are two major comorbidities that frequently manifest with aging. Both conditions share several common risk factors, including chronic inflammation, metabolic dysfunction, and physical inactivity, and have a significant impact on morbidity and mortality.

Skeletal muscle sarcopenia, a progressive and degenerative condition, is characterized by the gradual loss of muscle mass, strength, and function, which leads to diminished physical performance and independence [1, 2]. The pathogenesis of sarcopenia is multifactorial, with key contributors including aging‐related changes in muscle innervation, hormonal imbalances (e.g., decreased levels of anabolic hormones), reduced physical activity, and decreased availability of amino acids [3]. Additionally, the phenomenon of “inflamm‐aging,” which is associated with chronic low‐grade inflammation in elderly individuals, aggravates muscle degradation by upregulating pro‐inflammatory cytokines, such as Tumor Necrosis Factor‐alpha (TNF‐α) and interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), which interfere with muscle protein synthesis and accelerate catabolism [4]. Despite its high prevalence, diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia remain variable. Currently, sarcopenia is diagnosed through a combination of physical performance tests, imaging modalities, and biomarkers, but no universally accepted standard exists [5]. Treatment strategies for sarcopenia include resistance training, nutritional supplementation (e.g., protein and vitamin D), and pharmacological interventions targeting muscle metabolism and inflammation. However, the treatments are inconsistent, and more robust and targeted therapeutic approaches are necessary.

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is characterized by the thickening of the myocardial walls, particularly in the left ventricle [6, 7]. This pathological remodeling often leads to chronic hypertension, which increases the workload on the heart and, in turn, triggers compensatory hypertrophic mechanisms [8, 9]. HCM is also influenced by other risk factors, such as diabetes, obesity, and prior cardiovascular events, which further add to the progression of the disease. With the aging global population, the incidence of HCM has risen, with the average age of diagnosis currently at 51 years [10]. The clinical manifestations of HCM are variable, ranging from asymptomatic to severe heart failure, arrhythmias, and sudden cardiac death. Current management strategies focus on alleviating symptoms, controlling arrhythmias, and preventing heart failure, but these treatments are not curative. The role of genetics, imaging, and advanced diagnostic tools has improved the identification of individuals at risk, yet therapeutic approaches remain primarily mitigative.

Sarcopenia and HCM share common etiological factors, including chronic inflammation, metabolic dysfunction, and physical inactivity. This contributes to a cycle of mutual exacerbation. Sarcopenia often worsens cardiovascular outcomes by reducing exercise tolerance, impairing mobility, and promoting a sedentary lifestyle, all of which can accelerate HCM progression. Conversely, the cardiac dysfunction associated with HCM can impair blood flow and oxygen delivery to skeletal muscle, accelerating muscle atrophy and weakness. This bidirectional relationship between sarcopenia and HCM significantly impacts the quality of life and increases the burden of morbidity and mortality in affected individuals [11, 12]. A substantial healthcare burden is associated with sarcopenia and HCM, especially when combined, contributing to significant morbidity, disability, and healthcare costs. Both conditions are associated with loss of independence, reduced mobility, and poor quality of life in aging populations [13, 14]. Comorbidities complicate treatment strategies, as treatments/drugs developed for one condition may inadvertently aggravate the other. For example, beta‐blockers, commonly used to manage HCM, may induce muscle fatigue and worsen sarcopenia, while anabolic agents aimed at treating sarcopenia may further worsen cardiac remodeling. Therefore, there is an urgent need for integrated, individualized approaches to diagnosis and treatment.

Despite the complications in the pathophysiology of sarcopenia and HCM comorbidity, and the challenges it brings to therapeutic development, current available platforms to study this comorbidity are very limited. Our understanding of this comorbidity has primarily come from clinical correlation analysis with limited studies in mouse models. Clinical data analysis indicated a high correlation between skeletal muscle decline and cardiovascular aging and/or diseases [15, 16, 17]. A mouse model has been developed to study the cellular/molecular mechanism of HCM in sarcopenic induced by obesity [18]. Another study in mice revealed skeletal muscle‐derived musclin protects the heart during pathological overload [19]. Due to the ethical limitations of clinical investigation and the species gap concern about animal models, a more efficient and human‐based model is needed to study the inter‐organ interactions under physiological and pathological conditions, as well as to test the efficacy and toxicity of potential therapeutics.

To better understand the pathophysiology of diseases and identify potential therapeutic strategies, advanced in vitro models such as multi‐organ microphysiological systems (MPS) are becoming increasingly important [20, 21, 22]. These systems enable the modeling of human diseases in a controlled environment, providing valuable insights into disease mechanisms and potential interventions [23, 24, 25]. These MPS models either primarily address function, as demonstrated by clinically relevant functional models [22, 26, 27], or anatomy, which is exquisitely represented by organoids and spheroids [28, 29]. There are also a few platforms that address aspects of both [21, 30, 31]. We have previously published a single‐organ functional sarcopenia model [32], a human cardiomyocyte model [27], and a functional human liver model [33]. This study will develop a human‐based skeletal muscle‐heart‐liver MPS model to reproduce the comorbidity of HCM and sarcopenia in the presence of liver.

The multi‐organ MPS‐based comorbidity model reported here is a promising approach for studying the phenotypic characteristics of sarcopenic skeletal muscle and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy in the same recirculating serum‐free medium, as it can replicate certain in vivo cellular behaviors and organ‐specific interactions with high fidelity. The model provides clinically relevant functional readouts for mechano‐transduction through cantilevers (beat frequency and contraction force) and electrical activity through microelectrode array systems (MEAs) (beat frequency, QT interval, and conduction velocity). The model is a serum‐free, gravity‐driven interconnected system [22, 24, 26, 34] that incorporates healthy primary hepatocytes, hiPSC‐derived skeletal muscle, and hiPSC‐derived cardiomyocytes. The reported model holds promise for improving diagnosis, drug development processes, treatment, and management of age‐related comorbidity conditions and contributes to better health care outcomes.

Methods

Cantilever Fabrication and Surface Modification

Cantilevers were prepared as described in a previous publication [27]. Each cantilever chip has 32 cantilevers (2 rows × 16 cantilevers). For skeletal muscle, the cantilevers are 100 µm wide and 737 µm long, while the cantilevers for cardiac cells are wider at 150 µm. Before plating the cantilevers, the chips were plasma treated and coated with (3‐Trimethoxysilylpropyl) diethylenetriamine silane (DETA), introducing a triamine substrate for better protein and cell attachment.

Microelectrode Array Fabrication and Surface Modification

The microelectrode array chips (MEA) for cardiac cells were fabricated, and the surfaces were modified as described previously by Oleaga et al. [26]. The MEA comprises 10 electrodes (200 µm diameter) and two ground electrodes (2000 µm diameter). The wires connecting these electrodes were insulated with 150 nm thick layers of silicon dioxide, silicon nitride, and silicon oxide using plasma‐enhanced chemical vapor deposition. The surfaces were etched by reactive ion etching (a mixture of CHF3 and O2). The MEA chips were further modified before cell plating with PEG, and a U‐pattern was laser ablated (193 nm ArF excimer laser (Lambda Physik, CA) using a quartz photomask across the 10 electrodes.

Cell Culture

Primary Human Hepatocytes

All cell culture procedures were similar to a previous publication [22]. Primary human hepatocytes purchased from Novabiosis were plated 7 days before system assembly. The cells were plated on 15 mm coverslips, which were previously cleaned with an O2 plasma (Harrick Plasma cleaner) and protein‐coated. Gibco Rat tail collagen I was used at 60 µg/mL to coat the coverslips before plating cells. The cells were seeded on the coverslips at 1400 cells/mm2 cell density in the vendor‐provided plating medium (Novabiosis, NHPM). The cells were incubated for 2 h in an incubator (37°C, 5% CO2). After incubation, the medium was changed to fresh plating medium. The cells were again incubated for 2 h. At the end of the second incubation period, the plating medium was aspirated, and the wells were filled with 1 mL hepatocyte maintenance medium (Novabiosis, NHMM 7111). A complete medium change was done the next day, and the cells were maintained in the same medium with media changes every 3–4 days. The hepatocytes were sandwiched 24 h before system assembly using a collagen hydrogel layer. The medium was aspirated, and 90 µL of hydrogel solution (775 µL 1 × DPBS, 225 µL 3 mg/mL collagen, and 1N NaOH) was applied on top of the hepatocytes and incubated for 1 h. At the end of the incubation period, hepatocyte maintenance medium was added to each coverslip.

Human iPSC Cardiac cells

Cardiomyocytes were obtained from Cellular Dynamics and plated on prepared MEAs and cantilevers 7 days before system assembly. MEAs and the polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) barriers were sterilized in 70% ethanol. The MEAs were placed in wells in a 6‐well plate. The barriers were placed on the MEAs, exposing the area with the electrodes. The barriers help to restrict the cells to the U‐pattern formed by laser ablation on the electrodes.

The cantilevers were O2 plasma cleaned and sterilized using ethanol. The cantilevers were placed on top of the sterile 22 mm coverslips, which were previously coated with 1,1,2,2‐perfluorooctyl trichlorosilane (13F), in a 12‐well plate. The barriers were then placed on the cantilever chip while exposing the cantilevers.

MEAs and cantilevers were coated with fibronectin solution at 10 and 50 µg/mL, respectively. After a 1‐h incubation at 37°C, the fibronectin solution was aspirated. Cardiomyocytes (Cellular Dynamics, CMC‐100‐010‐001) were seeded at a density of 200 000 cells/200 µL for cantilever chips and 50 000 cells/100 µL for MEAs in vendor‐provided medium (CDI, CMM‐100‐110‐005). These seeded chips were incubated for 2 h, and later, the barriers were topped off with a 50:50 mix of plating medium: UCF cardiac medium [22]. The next day, 1.5 mL UCF cardiac medium was added to the wells, and the barriers were removed. Two days later, the cantilever chips were moved off the 13F coverslips, and a complete medium change was performed every 2 days.

Human iPSC Skeletal Muscle Cells

DETA‐coated cantilevers and 13F coverslips were sterilized using 70% ethanol. Once dry, the 13F coverslips were placed in the 12‐well plates with cantilever chips on top. The cantilevers were bubble‐coated with collagen I (Gibco) (5 mL of PBS, 100 µL rat tail collagen I, 6 µL of acetic acid) and incubated for 2 h. After incubation, iPSC Human skeletal muscle cells were bubble‐plated at a density of 200 cells/mm2. The plates were again incubated for 2 h, and later, each well was filled with 2 mL of plating/proliferation medium. The cantilevers were monitored for about 80% confluency (2–3 days); once confluent, the cantilevers were transferred to a new plate and supplemented with differentiation medium (NbA4+, Brainbits LLC). The cells were maintained with a half‐medium change every 2 days.

Phenotype Induction

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy was induced using phenylephrine HCl (PE) (Sigma–Aldrich). Half of the plated cardiac MEAs and cantilevers were treated with 20µM PE on day 4 after plating. Once induction was complete, the cultures (healthy and induced) were transferred to the common medium [22, 24, 26, 35] (PE absent) for maintenance and testing. The other half served as healthy controls [36, 37, 38].

Skeletal muscle sarcopenia was induced using TNF‐α (Sigma–Aldrich). Day 4 post‐plating, cell proliferation medium (DMEM + Myocult) was removed, and 3 nM TNF‐α mixed with differentiation medium (NbA4+, Brainbits LLC) was fed to the cantilevers [32]. Once induction was complete, the cultures (healthy and induced) were transferred to the common medium (TNF‐α‐ absent) [22, 24, 26, 35] for maintenance and testing. The cultures were moved to common medium, as it serves as a common medium for multiple cell types, and all cell types were maintained in common medium for the multi‐organ setup.

System Fabrication and Assembly

A human‐on‐a‐chip system was designed as described in previous publications [22, 26, 35]. The system was fabricated using laser‐cut acrylic and polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) sheets. The system consists of 5 chambers that house 3 organs, liver (coverslip), heart (cantilever and MEA chip), skeletal muscle (cantilever), and an empty slot (can incorporate an extra organ as needed), each in a different chamber. The complete system is designed to be pump‐free, and the medium recirculates through a microfluidic network formed by the PDMS gaskets sandwiched between the two acrylic housings. Medium recirculation and uniform medium flow and mixing were ensured by rocking the systems on a lab rocker (Figure S2). The system has two ports for medium sampling and cell feeding. All cell types were plated 7 days before the system assembly, allowing the cells to mature and phenotype establishment.

Prior to assembly, the acrylic housings and the PDMS sheets were sterilized by soaking in 70% ethanol for 1 h. Top and bottom gaskets were aligned on the respective housings and allowed to dry overnight in a sterile environment. The next day, the top and bottom housings with gaskets were passivated using 3% Bovine serum albumin (BSA) solution and left overnight at room temperature. The microfluidic path was carefully filled with BSA solution on both the top and bottom housings. This passivation process renders the surface hydrophilic, therefore helping in smooth medium flow and avoiding the formation of any bubbles in the systems.

On the day of assembly, all the cell types were imaged under a phase microscope to ascertain the quality of the cells. Before assembly, the BSA solution was aspirated, and the fluid path on the bottom housing was filled with 1800 µL of in‐house, multi‐organ, defined, serum‐free medium [22, 24, 26, 35]. The organ coverslip/chips were carefully placed in their respective chambers, and the top housing was then screwed to complete the system. The systems were then transferred to an incubator (37°C and 5% CO2) and placed on a rocker with a 1‐degree tilt and a speed of 1 rock per minute for proper medium flow. The current multi‐organ system offers an advantage over the conventional organ‐on‐a‐chip platform due to its gravity‐driven medium flow, which does not require any pumps or tubing, has a small footprint, and is economical to fabricate and operate [39, 40]. One‐third of the medium change was performed every day to maintain the systems.

A single‐organ system was designed similarly, with two acrylic housings sandwiched between PDMS gaskets. The cantilever chip rests on the bottom housing, while the top housing has a window for medium. One‐third of the medium was changed every day.

Functional Testing

The systems were maintained for a week before functional testing. This allowed the cells to acclimatize to the new environment and establish themselves in the system with all other cell types.

Contractile Force Measurement

The muscle contractile force was measured using a cantilever deflection system as described in previous literature [22]. The muscle plated on the cantilever contracts and exerts a strain on the cantilever. This strain causes the free end of the cantilever to bend vertically, thus converting the horizontal contraction of the muscle into a vertical displacement. The cantilever's vertical displacement (bending) is proportional to the contractile force. This deflection was measured by a laser and a photodetector (Thorlabs). An automated peak detection program measured the laser deflection to determine the average maximum amplitude of the resulting peaks and contraction frequency. The contractile force was obtained by computational analysis using a modified Stoney's equation, the measured displacement, and the compression parameters of the cantilever. The cardiac cells beat spontaneously, while the skeletal muscle was stimulated using a 5‐volt DC pulse at a frequency of 1 Hz [41].

MEA Measurement

MEAs were prepared and seeded with cardiac cells as described above, and a U‐pattern of connected cells was created, connecting all electrodes to conduct the electrical signals. The printed circuit board (PCB) and the MEAs were connected using flexible elastomeric connectors (zebra connectors, FUJIPOLY) inserted into the respective slots on the housings. This connection allows stimulation of the cells and recording from the MEAs. The spontaneous activity of the cardiac cells was recorded for 30 s. The recording was followed by stimulation of cells using 800mV rectangular pulses in 0.25 Hz increments (0.5‐3.0 Hz) for 100 s.

Hepatocyte Functional Testing (Urea and Albumin)

Medium samples were collected from the systems on each testing day. These samples were tested for urea and albumin production to evaluate the hepatocyte functionality. QuantiChrom Urea Assay Kit and QuantiChrom BCG Albumin Assay Kit from Bioassay Systems were used for urea and albumin analysis, respectively.

Endpoint Assays

Hepatocyte Activity

Cyp3A4 activity: After the last day of testing, the systems were disassembled in a sterile environment. The coverslips and the cantilevers were used for endpoint assays and were transferred to 12‐well plates. The hepatocyte coverslips were used for the CYP3A4 assay (Promega P450‐Glo, V9002) to determine the hepatocytes’ activity, which was further normalized with an MTT viability assay [22]. The coverslips were washed 3× with 1× phosphate‐buffered saline solution (PBS) (Gibco, 14190‐144). After the third wash, Luciferin‐IPA reagent [200 µL Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM)/F‐12 (Gibco, 21041‐025) + 0.8 µL Luciferin‐IPA] was administered to the coverslips. The plates were incubated for an hour, and samples were collected for spectrophotometry. The collected samples were mixed with luciferin‐detecting reagent, and readings were taken on a plate reader.

Cell Viability

MTT assay: After the CYP assay, hepatocyte coverslips were administered with 3‐(4,5‐Dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)‐2,5‐diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) reagent, 1.1 mM MTT (Thermo Fisher Scientific, M6494), and 500 µL of hepatocyte maintenance medium (Novabiosis, 7111). The plates were incubated for an hour at 37°C. The coverslips were further treated with a solubilization solution for 10 min to dissolve the purple crystals formed during the reaction. The solubilization solution consists of 9 parts dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Sigma–Aldrich, D4540) + 1 part 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) (Sigma–Aldrich, 151‐21‐3) + 5.26µL/mL glacial acetic acid (Sigma–Aldrich, A6283). The absorbance of the dissolved formazan crystals was measured at 550nm.

Alamar blue: The viability of the cardiomyocytes and skeletal muscle was measured using the Alamar blue assay. Alamar blue reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, DAL1100) and common multi‐organ medium were added to each well in a 1:10 ratio. The plates were incubated for 8 h, and fluorescence was measured using a Synergy HT plate reader and Gen5 software at 560 nm (excitation)/590 nm (emission).

Results

The multi‐organ systems used for the current study consisted of 3 cell types: hepatocytes, skeletal muscle, and cardiomyocytes. Comorbidity conditions included hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and skeletal muscle sarcopenia in the same MPS system, while healthy hepatocytes were used for both healthy and comorbid diseased conditions. The HCM and sarcopenia models were induced individually by acute dosing, then assembled into the 3‐organ MPS that did not include the inducing factor. The hepatocytes were the first component of the system, closest to the medium inlet reservoir; the cantilevers were next to the hepatocyte chamber, followed by MEAs and the medium outlet reservoir. Figure 1A shows the schematics of the multi‐organ system used and the representative phase images (Figure 1E) of the different cell types and their respective positions in the system. After assembly, the cells were allowed to establish and acclimate to the new environment. Functional testing for the MEAs and cantilevers was performed on days 5, 7, 9, and 14 after assembly. Skeletal muscle was tested using cantilevers, while cardiomyocytes were tested using both cantilevers and MEA systems (Representative traces: Figure S1). Endpoint assays were performed after testing and disassembly on day 14. Prior to testing the diseased phenotypes in a multi‐organ system, both sarcopenia and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy disease phenotypes were established and tested in single‐organ systems. All experimental data were normalized to the control systems from the same batch. This approach accounts for inherent batch‐to‐batch variations in cell viability and a metabolic baseline, ensuring that observed effects are attributable solely to the experimental conditions. Further, Student's t‐test was performed, and all data presented are represented as Normalized Mean ± Standard error, and * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, **** p < 0.0001.

Phenotype Development on Single Organ Systems

Sarcopenic Phenotype

The sarcopenic phenotype is primarily characterized by loss of muscle mass, strength, and function. Sarcopenia has also been linked with increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) [42]. The loss of muscle mass was determined by measuring the myotube width from the phase images using ImageJ (NIH) [32, 43]. The myotube width for the healthy skeletal muscle was greater than that of sarcopenic skeletal muscle (Figure 2B,C). The cells were maintained in culture in common medium [22, 24, 26, 35] for 28 days, and the myotube width was maintained for both healthy and sarcopenic skeletal muscle. Dichlorodihydrofluorescein Diacetate, a cell‐permeable non‐fluorescent precursor of Dichlorodihydrofluorescein, is widely used to determine the ROS state of a cell [44]. The phosphorescence values for sarcopenic skeletal muscle were significantly higher than those for healthy skeletal muscle, showing higher ROS generation in sarcopenic muscle (Figure 2E). This was further confirmed with confocal images. The images were analyzed using ImageJ software, which indicated higher ROS generation in sarcopenic muscle (Figure 2D).

There was a significant decrease in the sarcopenic muscle contractile force (Figure 2F) for all four testing days for the control.

Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy Phenotype

The hypertrophic cardiomyopathy phenotype was induced using phenylephrine (PE) and maintained in common medium [22, 24, 26, 35]. HCM is characterized by thicker cardiac muscle, irregular heartbeat (arrhythmia), and increased ejection fraction [45], which translates to an increased contractile force. The thickness of the cardiac cells was evaluated by measuring the width of individual cells using ImageJ (NIH) [43]. There was a significant increase in the width of HCM cardiac cells compared to healthy cardiomyocytes (Figure 3C). The cells were stained for cardiac troponin and analyzed in ImageJ for troponin expression levels, which were significantly higher in the HCM condition (Figure 3B). The contractile force and beat frequency measurements were performed similarly to the skeletal muscle contractile force measurement using a cantilever deflection system. There was no significant change in the beat frequency, while the contractile force for the HCM condition was significantly greater than the healthy cardiomyocytes, confirming the HCM phenotype (Figure 3D,E).

Multi‐Organ System Maintenance, Functional Analysis, and Endpoint Assays

Once the diseased phenotypes were established, all experiments were performed in the multi‐organ system. The multi‐organ system also included a hepatocyte component to better represent physiological conditions. Hepatocytes (coverslips), skeletal muscle (cantilevers), and cardiac cells (cantilevers and MEAs) were plated on respective coverslips, cantilevers, and MEA chips and assembled in the multi‐organ system on the seventh day after plating. A common serum‐free medium [22, 24, 26, 35] was used to maintain cell types within a single multi‐organ system, enhancing physiological relevance and minimizing the effects of serum‐based variables. It is essential for mimicking systemic circulation, enabling communication between cell types and exchange of signaling molecules, which is key for inter‐organ crosstalk and systemic homeostasis. Our previous work validated this approach, showing that shared medium supports the viability and proper physiological functions of different organs for several weeks without confounding variables from organ‐specific media [22, 26, 35]. These systems were maintained for another 5 days for acclimatization and establishment in the system. The functional testing was carried out on days 5, 7, 9, and 14 after assembly. Medium samples were collected on each day of testing to track hepatocyte function by measuring urea and albumin levels. At the end of day 14 testing, the systems were disassembled, and endpoint assays for CYP3A4 for liver activity, MTT for liver viability, and Alamar blue for cardiac and skeletal muscle cell viability were performed.

The cantilever deflection system allowed the collection of contractile force amplitude and beat frequency data for the cardiac cells. The contractile force for skeletal muscle cells was measured using the cantilever system by electrically stimulating the skeletal muscle on the cantilevers (Raw values: Table S1). The force generated by sarcopenic skeletal muscle cells was significantly lower than that of healthy skeletal muscle. A significant decrease was observed on all four days: 5, 7, 9, and 14, with p‐values of 1.3058e(‐09), 9.1154e(‐06), 0.00014, and 3.46e(‐06), respectively (Figure 4B). A significant increase in cardiac force for HCM cells across all four days, with day‐wise p‐values recorded at * p = 0.0316 (Day 5), ** p = 0.0032 (Day 7), * p = 0.0204 (Day 9), and *** p = 0.0004 (Day 14), indicated a robust response. In contrast, the cardiac beat frequency showed loss of significance with time, with p‐values of 0.0407, 0.5434, 0.0836, and 0.1183 for each of days 5, 7, 9, and 14, respectively (Figure 5A,B). This indicates that phenotypes were maintained in the multi‐organ setup throughout the experimental timeline.

The patterned cardiac MEA modules allowed the measurement of the conduction velocity over the electrodes on the patterned cardiomyocytes. The minimum interspike interval (mISI), an analogue to the QT interval, was also evaluated (Raw values: Table S1). An upward trend in mISI was documented, with significant increases on days 5 and 14 (p‐values of 0.0210, 0.6203, 0.3148, and 0.0012 for days 5, 7, 9, and 14, respectively), indicating a prolonged QT interval compared to the healthy cardiomyocytes (Figure 5D). The conduction velocity was initially higher for earlier testing time points (Figure 5C).

The medium samples collected on each testing day were used for albumin and urea analysis to enable hepatocyte functional assessment (Figure 6C,D, respectively). After day 14 functional testing, the systems were disassembled, and enzymatic activity of the hepatocytes was determined by a CYP3A4 assay, indicating a decrease in liver function in terms of CYP3A4 activity for diseased conditions (Figure 6B). The MTT assay was used to determine hepatocyte viability (Figure 7A), while Alamar blue was used for cardiac (Figure 7B) and skeletal muscle (Figure 7C) viability. There was no significant difference in cell viability between the two conditions (Figure 7), indicating that the phenotypes observed in the multi‐organ systems were not due to a change in cell viability.

Discussion

The rise in patient comorbidity poses a significant challenge to healthcare systems worldwide. The disease management for comorbid individuals is becoming increasingly important in healthcare. In the United States, approximately 80% of medicare spending is allocated to individuals with four or more chronic conditions, with healthcare costs increasing dramatically as the number of chronic conditions increases [46, 47, 48]. This has led to an increase in interest among both practitioners and researchers to study the impact of comorbidity, including mortality, health‐related quality of life, functional capacity, and overall healthcare quality [49, 50, 51, 52]. Terms such as multimorbidity, burden of disease, and frailty are often used interchangeably, creating ambiguity in the literature, due to which there can be a lack of direction in research on the subject [53]. A unified international framework for the study of comorbidity is urgently needed to advance research in this area [54, 55]. Globally, the number of individuals with multiple chronic conditions (MCCs) is high, with about one‐third of the population being affected by two or more chronic diseases. MCC prevalence varies: 16%–58% (depending on the specific study, age group, and the number of chronic conditions) in the UK, 26% in the US, and 9.4% in urban South Asia. Understanding these comorbid conditions is crucial for improving healthcare and enabling targeted interventions that enhance treatments and reduce costs [56]. Despite advancements in diagnostics and therapeutics, managing these comorbidities remains challenging due to their complex interplay, which often renders single‐disease interventions insufficient or counterproductive.

With the growing adoption of microphysiological systems (MPS) as accurate and reliable models of human physiology, researchers are developing new models and platforms such as Malaria‐on‐a‐chip [24] and drug overdose models [22]. The Malaria‐on‐a‐Chip digital twin model includes human liver, spleen, and endothelium with P. falciparum‐infected blood, and the overdose model features liver, cardiac, skeletal muscle, and neurons in a four‐organ MPS to investigate systemic drug overdose toxicity. These platforms integrate organ‐specific interactions, allowing for the capture of disease‐specific and systemic responses under physiologically relevant conditions. Such platforms provide an opportunity to evaluate pharmacological efficacy and off‐target toxicity simultaneously, bridging the translational gap between preclinical models and human outcomes. For this study, a functional MPS model that integrates sarcopenic skeletal muscle and hypertrophic cardiomyocyte has been developed. The current MPS comorbidity model aims to replicate in vivo cellular behavior and organ‐specific interactions, offering a robust platform for investigating the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms and testing therapeutic interventions. The model not only provides biomarker‐related information (CYP, urea, and albumin) but also, more importantly, provides clinically relevant functional readouts for electrical activity from MEAs (conduction velocity, beat frequency, QT interval) and mechanotransduction through cantilevers (contraction force and beat frequency). The serum‐free, pumpless, interconnected system incorporates healthy primary hepatocytes, hiPSC‐derived cardiomyocytes, and hiPSC‐derived skeletal muscle cells with disease phenotypes.

To accurately model the systemic nature of age‐related comorbidities, the current platform utilized a dynamically recirculating common medium connecting spatially separated tissues on distinct coverslips. The human body does not operate in isolated, tissue‐specific conditioned medium; rather, diverse organ systems are continuously coupled via the blood. Culturing the skeletal muscle and cardiac tissues in a shared fluidic circuit mimics this physiological reality. This approach is supported by years of prior microphysiological systems research demonstrating that common medium formulations are essential and viable for evaluating systemic multi‐organ interactions [22, 24, 26, 35].

Sarcopenia is the loss of muscle function and mass, leading to reduced mobility. TNF‐α, a polypeptide cytokine, is known for causing muscle wasting and loss of muscle function [32, 57]. The skeletal muscle cells were treated with TNF‐α for 4 days to induce muscle sarcopenia before assembly in the multi‐organ systems. A significant decrease in skeletal muscle contraction force and muscle width was observed, which are the most significant features of sarcopenia. Disease progression and tissue degradation were studied, and muscle was tested for the generation of ROS [42], which was also higher than the healthy group, suggesting the sarcopenic phenotype. The sarcopenic phenotype was maintained for 14 days until disassembly.

As indicated by previous literature, phenylephrine is known to cause inflammation and has been shown to elicit hypertrophic cardiac pathology in vitro in rodent cardiomyocytes, with modification of cellular metabolism as a significant mechanism [36, 37, 38, 58]. The cardiac cells were treated for 4 days with phenylephrine to induce hypertrophic cardiomyopathy before assembly in the system. The induced cells were tested on the single‐organ cantilever systems for 14 days, and a higher contractile force was observed. The cultures were also tested for cardiac troponin levels, which are a marker for HCM [59]. The troponin levels were found to be significantly higher in HCM cardiomyocytes. Phenylephrine induces inflammation, which leads to an increase in cell size. Phenylephrine (PE) is responsible for cardiac inflammation by activating α1‐adrenoceptors, which in turn stimulate the NLRP3 inflammasome. This process is linked to the downregulation of Kir2.1, which is a cardioprotective and anti‐inflammatory factor [60]. Thicker cardiomyocytes are characteristic of HCM, and the induced cells were significantly thicker than the healthy cells.

Cardiomyocytes were tested for electrical readouts (MEA) for conduction velocity and mISI (QT analogue), and mechanotransduction readouts (cantilever) for contraction force and beat frequency. As discussed earlier, thicker cardiomyocytes, higher ejection fraction, arrhythmias, and longer QT intervals characterize HCM. The MEA readout suggests altered beat frequency and conduction velocity spread across different days. The irregularity in these two parameters suggests the presence of arrhythmia, while mISI/QT shows higher values for diseased systems. Higher mISI indicated a longer QT interval, confirming the HCM phenotype. The cardiac force measured on the cantilever was higher for diseased cells. The higher contractile force for the cardiomyocytes suggests a higher ejection fraction, reconfirming the HCM phenotype. The results indicate that the HCM phenotype is maintained till system disassembly (day 14) without any significant cell death.

Previous research suggests that a diseased liver can significantly worsen the symptoms of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, causing further complications such as impaired systolic and diastolic function and abnormal electrical activity in the heart. Essentially, a compromised liver can further stress the already hypertrophied heart muscle. This suggests that a healthy liver is essential for cardiac function and vice versa through the cardiohepatic axis [61]. The significance of the cardiac force amplitude potentiation in HCM is slightly lower in multi‐organ systems compared to single‐organ systems (Figure 5A vs. Figure 3D), suggesting that the presence of a healthy liver could be ameliorating the effects of HCM. However, the phenotype of cardiac force increases on day 14 as the hepatocytes are in culture longer and are under the burden of disease. This decrease in significance during the early testing days and worsened HCM later (day 14) indicated that the multi‐organ system could be similar to physiological conditions and should serve as a model for further studies. While a healthy liver in the multi‐organ system helped improve HCM, sarcopenia was not improved. Literature suggests that while a healthy liver contributes to maintaining muscle health, it does not directly improve sarcopenia [62]. Researchers have shown that hypertrophic cardiomyopathy can also contribute to sarcopenia through various mechanisms, including increased oxidative stress and inflammation. These factors can lead to muscle wasting and weakness, exacerbating sarcopenia [12]. The results of the present study indicate a substantial increase in the sarcopenic phenotype in the multi‐organ system compared to single‐organ sarcopenia systems (Figure 4B vs. Figure 2F). These findings demonstrate that the present comorbidity model closely resembles the pathological aspects of the comorbidity condition, making it more relevant for comorbidity studies.

In this model, skeletal muscle and cardiac tissues were not cultured separately but shared a dynamic medium, allowing continuous exchange of soluble factors, metabolites, and stress signals. It is important to recognize that in vivo sarcopenia–cardiac comorbidity is driven not only by direct signaling pathways but also by systemic metabolic and inflammatory interactions, often mediated by circulating factors rather than clear‐cut pathways. Therefore, the system's ability to simulate shared pathological traits through functional analysis, in a common medium environment, offers a meaningful approximation of early inter‐organ communication, even without fully elucidated signaling hierarchies. This will aid in future studies of therapeutic development.

Urea and albumin are well‐established parameters for determining liver function. No significant change in both urea and albumin production was noted, indicating that the hepatocytes in both healthy and diseased systems are healthy and functioned well. Cell viability for hepatocytes indicated no significant loss. CYP3A4 enzyme activity was normalized to cell viability. Previous literature suggests that hepatocyte enzyme activity decreases in comorbid individuals due to the burden of disease [63]. The hepatocyte enzyme activity for the diseased systems appeared to be slightly lower than that of the healthy systems; however, no significant difference was observed. Cell viability assays showed no considerable cell death in diseased systems compared to healthy systems, demonstrating a robust model for comorbidity studies.

By creating a dynamic fluidic connection through a common medium, the system acts as a physiologically relevant model for phenotypic comorbidity. Since skeletal muscle and cardiac tissues share this recirculating environment, they indicate similar pathological signatures, which are clearly detected by different functional readouts, such as mechanotransduction and electrical activity changes. This shared environment provides a useful approximation of early inter‐organ communication, but it is important to distinguish this systemic coupling from the specific mechanistic crosstalk. Although the platform replicates the combined phenotypic effects of the sarcopenia‐HCM comorbidity, identifying the precise bidirectional signaling pathways, such as circulating cytokines responsible for the decline, was outside the study's scope. Additionally, while the hepatic module offers a necessary baseline systemic context, its exact functional role in modulating this comorbidity remains to be fully understood. Ultimately, this platform establishes a baseline for systemic disease, facilitating future research into targeted biological controls that can clarify signaling pathways and distinguish hepatic metabolic clearance from active, disease‐related cross‐talk.

Conclusion

Sarcopenia and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy are prevalent comorbidities in aging populations. They share common risk factors and pathophysiological mechanisms that complicate diagnosis and treatment. The advancement of new and sophisticated in vitro models, including multi‐organ microphysiological systems, offers a novel way to investigate these diseases in a more human‐relevant context. Unlike traditional animal models that often fail to predict clinical outcomes, the MPS model uses human‐derived cells to study tissue‐specific and systemic interactions under physiological conditions. We have developed a three‐organ microphysiological system that successfully models the systemic comorbidity of sarcopenia and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Skeletal muscle, cardiac tissues, and hepatocytes were co‐cultured in a common serum‐free medium, enhancing physiological relevance while minimizing serum‐based culture variabilities. The platform successfully maintained the disease phenotypes in sarcopenia and HCM over 14 days and captured the modifications of cardiac phenotypes in comorbidity compared to a single‐organ condition. This comorbidity model provides a platform for mechanistic studies about comorbidity, as well as for evaluating drug effects on muscle and cardiac tissues and testing off‐target effects and optimizing efficacy. Additionally, it overcomes animal model limitations, including high costs and low clinical trial success rates. By offering a more accurate and cost‐effective alternative, it can expedite drug discovery and enhance preclinical testing efficiency. This model presents a promising approach to develop more personalized and effective treatments for patients with these complex, comorbid conditions, with the aim to enhance outcomes and quality of life while reducing healthcare costs.
