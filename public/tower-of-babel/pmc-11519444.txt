Utilizing machine learning to predict participant response to follow-up health surveys in the Millennium Cohort Study
Authors: Wisam Barkho, Nathan C Carnes, Claire A Kolaja, Xin M Tu, Satbir K Boparai, Sheila F Castañeda, Beverly D Sheppard, Jennifer L Walstrom, Jennifer N Belding, Rudolph P Rull, the Millennium Cohort Study Team
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Millennium Cohort Study is a longitudinal study which collects self-reported data from surveys to examine the long-term effects of military service. Participant nonresponse to follow-up surveys presents a potential threat to the validity and generalizability of study findings. In recent years, predictive analytics has emerged as a promising tool to identify predictors of nonresponse. Here, we develop a high-skill classifier using machine learning techniques to predict participant response to follow-up surveys of the Millennium Cohort Study. Six supervised algorithms were employed to predict response to the 2021 follow-up survey. Using latent class analysis (LCA), we classified participants based on historical survey response and compared prediction performance with and without this variable. Feature analysis was subsequently conducted on the best-performing model. Including the LCA variable in the machine learning analysis, all six algorithms performed comparably. Without the LCA variable, random forest outperformed the benchmark regression model, however overall prediction performance decreased. Feature analysis showed the LCA variable as the most important predictor. Our findings highlight the importance of historical response to improve prediction performance of participant response to follow-up surveys. Machine learning algorithms can be especially valuable when historical data are not available. Implementing these methods in longitudinal studies can enhance outreach efforts by strategically targeting participants, ultimately boosting survey response rates and mitigating nonresponse.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-77563-8.Keywords: Longitudinal studies, Survey nonresponse, Survey response, Machine learning, Latent class analysis, Survey outreach effortsSubject terms: Epidemiology, Epidemiology

Introduction

Longitudinal studies that use surveys to follow participants over time are indispensable research tools for studying changes in health and quality of life and for examining associations between exposures and subsequent outcomes1. However, the success of these studies hinges on sustained engagement from participants. Participant nonresponse, which refers to the phenomenon in which participants who initially enroll in a study and subsequently do not respond to one or more follow-up survey waves, may pose a risk to the validity of the research2. Associations between exposures and/or outcomes of interest and survey nonresponse may lead to spurious findings and potentially limit the generalizability to the larger source population. To mitigate any potential impact, studies aim to minimize nonresponse by using strategies such as maintaining regular contact with participants, providing incentives for survey completion, and minimizing the burden on participants3,4.

In recent years, predictive analytics has emerged as a promising tool5 to identify patterns in survey data and predict participant nonresponse, which may then be utilized to inform outreach, recruitment, and retention strategies in cohort studies6. Data-driven models also adapt and learn from new data. These models can be trained on a subset of the data and then be used to make predictions on new data as they become available, allowing researchers to continuously tune their models and make more accurate predictions over time.

The effectiveness of machine learning in predicting survey response remains a subject of ongoing debate within the literature. Kern et al.7,8 employed machine learning algorithms to predict nonresponse in two separate longitudinal studies, both sampled German civilian populations and employed web and paper surveys. Publications on both studies used a temporal prequential approach, either sliding or growing window as described by Cerqueira et al.9, and the second publication incorporated indicators of historical survey response, such as completion of the last survey and how many of the previous three waves were completed, as predictors. Although both studies achieved high prediction performance with predicted probabilities ≥ 80%, the likelihood of correctly predicting that a participant will not respond was relatively low, with a predicted probability ≤ 50%, indicating a bias in the model for predicting response.

Another method employed in research on survey response behavior is latent class analysis (LCA) used to classify patterns of historical response. LCA classifies individuals into meaningful but unmeasured groups by identifying underlying patterns across a set of indicator variables using a probability model10. Cernat and Sakshaug11 used LCA to classify participants enrolled in the Understanding Society Innovation Panel based on survey response over five waves and examined the relationship between the historical survey response with later survey response to Wave 10. In logistic regression models, the LCA variable was strongly associated with both response and response mode.

In contrast, predictive models have been criticized for their “black box” nature. Jankowsky and Schroeders12 examined prediction models among two additional longitudinal cohorts, one based in the U.S. and the other in Germany, and found that the more complex generalized boosted regression models did not substantially improve prediction performance when compared with logistic regression. The authors concluded that since machine learning models are more complex to interpret than more traditional models such as logistic regression, they may not be optimally suited for predicting survey nonresponse.

Considering the mixed results in the literature surrounding the effectiveness of predictive analytics, we sought to determine the utility of predictive analytics for survey response in the Millennium Cohort Study (hereafter the Study), the largest and longest running research effort of U.S. service members and veterans that aims to investigate the long-term physical, psychological, and behavioral health impacts of military service13. The first panel of participants (Panel 1) of the Study includes over 77,000 participants with diverse demographics, a larger sample size than that of the aforementioned studies which enrolled only 3,000 to 15,000 participants. Furthermore, our advantage lies in the rich historical data of the Study that enabled us to incorporate past survey responses as predictors in machine learning models. In this analysis, we used data from the Study to evaluate six supervised machine learning algorithms, both with and without LCA classification of historical response patterns and developed a high-skill classifier that estimated the likelihood of response to the 2021 follow-up survey among Panel 1 of the Study.

Methods

Participants

The Study is a longitudinal cohort study that collects self-reported data using web and paper-based surveys administered every 3–5 years. Panel 1 comprised military personnel from all branches and components who were randomly selected and invited to participate in the study and subsequently enrolled between 2001 and 2003 by providing informed consent and completing a baseline survey. Participants were then invited to follow-up surveys conducted in 2004–2006, 2007–2008, 2011–2013, 2014–2016, and, most recently, 2019–2021 (hereafter referred to by the year that the survey wave concluded)14.

The Millennium Cohort Study recently published a study that examined participant response rates for the first 15 years of follow-up15. This study found that the average response rate was 60% (range 43–71%) for 3–15 years of follow-up, which is slightly lower than other established cohort studies (74%)16. These observed differences may be due to differences in survey methods or population characteristics, such as being a younger, highly mobile, and an extensively surveyed military population15.

Response variable

The binary response variable was defined as survey nonresponse (0) or response (1) to the 2021 follow-up survey. Response was defined as initiating a web-based survey or returning a paper survey. Participants were ineligible for these analyses if they were deceased or withdrawn from the study by the start of each follow-up survey cycle. Furthermore, those who opted to complete a short (two page) paper survey for the 2016 survey cycle were also excluded because it constituted a unique outcome for that specific survey cycle. These exclusion criteria resulted in 70,117 eligible Panel 1 participants for the 2021 survey cycle.

Latent class analysis

The analysis was performed in two phases: (1) Latent Class Analysis (LCA) and (2) machine learning predictions, using two distinct datasets with no overlapping variables. In the first phase, LCA was applied to identify patterns in survey responses based solely on previous participation. The dataset for the LCA comprised prior survey responses from the 2003, 2006, 2008, 2013, and 2016 survey cycles. These responses were categorized as non-response (0), web response (1), or paper response (2) to construct a series of LCA models. These models, which ranged from 2 to 11 classes, aimed to identify distinct patterns in historical survey responses across different groups of individuals. The optimal number of classes was determined by evaluating statistical criteria such as the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and substantive criteria like entropy. The BIC assessed model fit, with lower values indicating a better fit, while entropy measured the classification accuracy of the models. Once the optimal number of classes was identified, Sankey diagrams (included in Section A of the Supplemental Material) were manually inspected to visualize the latent patterns of survey responses across the different classes. By comparing these patterns, we characterized and labeled each class based on distinct response behaviors. Section B of the Supplemental Material contain the R code for the LCA.

Subsequently, descriptive statistics were calculated to provide insight into the characteristics and distribution of the LCA classes. Baseline military and demographic variables, including birth year, sex, race, ethnicity, educational attainment, marital status, Gulf War veteran status, service branch, pay grade, component, and occupation, were examined. Additionally, time-varying factors such as military separation were included in the analysis. Chi-square tests were conducted to assess the significance of these variables within the identified LCA classes, and Cramer’s V statistic was calculated to determine effect size for each variable.

Predictor variables

In the second phase, the dataset for machine learning predictions included demographic, military, and self-reported variables. This analysis also incorporated the LCA-derived variable representing prior survey response patterns, while excluding actual response variables to prevent data leakage. Data elements from the first phase were not used in the second phase of analysis, except for the LCA-derived variable.

Demographic predictor variables were obtained from the Defense Manpower Data Center (DMDC) at baseline, including birth year, sex, race, and ethnicity, with additional self-reported information on marital status and educational attainment from study surveys. Participants’ residential locations at the time of survey completion were classified based on the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention National Center for Health Statistics classification scheme. Military predictor variables, such as service branch, component, pay grade, military occupation, length of service, and deployment status, were also obtained from DMDC. Self-reported variables were consistently assessed in the study’s surveys from 2003 to 2016 and included life stressors, mental health, unhealthy behaviors, and physical health conditions. Further details regarding the selection and construction of predictor variables can be found in the Section C of the Supplemental Material.

Correlation analysis

Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) was used to determine correlation of predictor and response variables. When correlation between pairs of variables was exhibited (r > 0.7), only one variable was included in the analysis.

Supervised machine learning

We modeled the training and test schematic with a real-world focus by implementing a sliding window prequential approach9 in order to predict follow-up survey response based on data from the prior survey cycle. That is, machine learning algorithms were trained using predictor variables from the 2013 survey cycle to predict response in the 2016 survey, and then tested using predictor variables from the 2016 survey cycle to predict response in the 2021 survey cycle (Fig. 1). Using test data helps avoid overfitting by providing an independent dataset that the model has not seen during training, ensuring that performance metrics reflect the model’s ability to generalize to new, unseen data rather than just its ability to memorize the training data. This sequential, temporal-based strategy used 50% of the available data for training and the remaining 50% for testing, offering a more rigorous evaluation of the model than the traditional 80%/20% split as it reduces the amount of data available for training. We chose to utilize a sliding window prequential approach, as opposed to a growing window strategy, because it prioritizes the most recent data and is better suited to adapt to changes in health trends, policies, or treatments17,18.

Temporal prequential approach, using a sliding window schematic, to train and test supervised machine learning models using Panel 1 data from the Millennium Cohort Study. Survey response was predicted using predictor variables from the prior survey cycle in both the train and test data.

When selecting machine learning algorithms, we chose to take an empirically driven approach, running various algorithms to let the data determine which would perform best. This method allowed us to avoid preconceived biases about the suitability of any specific algorithm. By leveraging the strengths of different algorithms, we aimed to identify the most effective predictive model based on actual performance metrics rather than theoretical assumptions. This methodology ensured that the best-performing model was identified based on actual performance. The selected algorithms were (1) penalized logistic regression, using a penalty based on the number of coefficients in the model (served as the benchmark model); (2) random forest, a bagging ensemble method that builds multiple decision tree classifiers; (3) interaction forest, which is similar to random forest except decision trees split on pairs of variables and the model tests for interactions; (4) extreme gradient boosting or XGBoost, a boosting ensemble method that uses a gradient descent to minimize the loss function; (5) neural networks, with a single hidden layer that processes data by means of an activation function (logistic sigmoid function); and (6) SuperLearner, which estimates the performance of multiple machine learning models. Where available, we used grid search to optimize hyperparameter settings as outlined in Table 1. For algorithms not available for grid search (interaction forest and SuperLearner), a manual optimization of the hyperparameter settings was conducted. The Supplemental Material provide a more detailed explanation of the model architecture, hyperparameters used, and overfitting prevention strategies (Section D), as well as the corresponding R code (Section E).

Prediction performance of the models was evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve to estimate the likelihood of predicting participant response. Additionally, prediction performance of the positive class (response) was measured using the AUC of the precision-recall curve to estimate the likelihood of correctly predicting that a participant will respond (e.g., true positive). Model performance was assessed using the 2016–2021 test data. Each algorithm was run with and without the LCA variable to compare prediction performance with and without historical survey response, respectively. Furthermore, predictions on the 2013–2016 training data were utilized to assess model fit and can be found in Section F of the Supplemental Materials.

Feature analysis

Predictor variables of the best-preforming model were ranked by importance using information gain when predicting response. The methods for calculating variable importance can vary depending on the type of model used. Logistic regression uses the absolute magnitude of the coefficients, similar to neural networks, which uses the magnitude of weights. Ensemble methods, on the other hand, calculate the difference in entropy before and after the split for each variable.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study protocol was approved by the Naval Health Research Center Institutional Review Board in compliance with all applicable federal regulations governing the protection of human subjects. Research data were derived from approved Naval Health Research Center Institutional Review Board protocol number NHRC.2000.0007. All participants provided informed consent at enrollment.

Results

Response rates

Among 70,117 Panel 1 participants eligible for these analyses, 54.1% (n = 37,934) responded to the 2021 follow-up survey, with 89.5% (n = 33,931) of surveys completed by web.

As commonly done when classes comprise less than 5% of the study population or when theoretically sensible19, three classes were collapsed with their counterparts that represented similar response patterns. That is, classes for mode switchers (web to paper and paper to web), temporary nonresponders (web and paper), and late nonresponders (web and paper) were combined to make three distinct groups. However, a previous analysis for the Study observed differences in characteristics between web and paper-based survey responders20, thus underscoring the need to retain response mode among consistent responders. Moreover, Cernat et al.11 reported demographic differences between classes of response mode, providing further justification for using historical responses based on modality. Therefore, the LCA variable for historical response patterns among participants consisted of six distinct classes.

All military and demographic characteristics appeared to be statistically significant with the six LCA classes in bivariate comparisons (chi-square P < 0.0001). Table 3 lists military and demographic characteristics with at least a medium effect size on the LCA variable as determined by Cramer’s V21. Older individuals (pre-1960) tend to respond more consistently, with a significant portion (46.8%) being web responders. Conversely, younger individuals (1980 +) show a higher tendency toward nonresponse, particularly early nonresponse (31.4%). Educational attainment also influences response patterns, with those holding a master’s degree or higher more likely to be responders (56.5% responding via web), while individuals with a high school education exhibit higher rates of early nonresponse (22.3%). Separation periods also affect response tendencies, as individuals who did not separate from the military have the highest response rates (49.8% responding via web), whereas those separated before 2005 are more prone to early nonresponse (19.1%). Gulf War veterans, especially those on active duty, show higher response rates (44.3% responding via web), while non-Gulf War veterans display higher nonresponse rates (20.2% early nonresponse). Pay grade further differentiates response behaviors, with commissioned officers more likely to respond (56.0% responding via web) and enlisted personnel showing a greater tendency towards early nonresponse (15.6%). Similar patterns for demographic factors such as educational attainment, sex, age, race and ethnicity, have also been reported in other cohort studies22–25. These differences highlight the importance of tailoring engagement strategies based on responder profiles26.

Correlation analysis

Correlation was found among unhealthy behaviors and mental health conditions (r = 0.829). Indicators for unhealthy behaviors were excluded from our analysis. All other variable pairings showed no correlation (r < 0.7).

Supervised machine learning

Models were assessed by comparing performance as measured by AUC of the ROC and precision-recall curves, both with and without LCA, utilizing the 2016–2021 test data. Results reported in Table 4 suggest a substantial improvement in prediction performance by including the LCA variable in a logistic regression model, with an improvement of 11.1%. In the absence of LCA variable, predictive models such as random forest outperformed logistic regression by 9.2%. Plots from the ROC and precision-recall curves of the six algorithms under both conditions are displayed in Fig. 2. In the absence of LCA, random forest and interaction forest methods had higher AUCs than the remaining curves, while in the models with LCA, all six algorithms exhibited relatively uniform curves.

When the LCA variable was included in the model, all six algorithms performed comparably to one another, with differences in AUC of < 2.5%. The ensemble methods and SuperLearner, which incorporates multiple ensemble methods, had the highest prediction performance, with random forest outperforming all other algorithms (ROC AUC = 0.853 and precision-recall AUC = 0.840). In contrast, random forest outperformed the benchmark penalized regression model by 2.3% for AUC of ROC and 1.6% for AUC of precision-recall. Additionally, interaction forest performed on par with random forest (a difference of 0.3% or less in both ROC and precision-recall AUC), implying that interactions have little effect in this model. Further investigation of the interaction forest found that none of the top 19 highest ranking interactions were statistically significant (logistic regression, P > 0.05).

When the LCA variable was not included in the models, random forest again outperformed all other algorithms (ROC AUC = 0.800 and precision-recall AUC = 0.795). Notably, random forest outperformed the benchmark penalized regression model by 9.3% for AUC of ROC and 7.7% for AUC of precision-recall. Moreover, excluding the LCA variable resulted in a decrease of 10% or more in AUC of ROC for all but two (random forest and interaction forest) algorithms.

Feature analysis

Feature importance of the predictor variables in the random forest model with and without the LCA variable is plotted in Fig. 3. The LCA variable (e.g., pattern in historical survey response) was more important than all other variables and accounted for 53.1% of overall importance. Other variables that were highly important in the random forest model were birth year (5.7%), mirroring the findings from the descriptive statistics with the LCA variable (Table 3), and length of service (5.1%). Each of the remaining predictor variables individually accounted for < 5% of overall importance. Excluding the LCA variable from the model resulted in a change in the importance of factors that influence predicting survey response, suggesting that the model identified different patterns in the data to make predictions. Most notably, educational attainment played a more prominent role in prediction performance, with its overall importance increasing by 19.6%. In addition, birth year also played a prominent role in variable importance, increasing by 10.6%. These observations also reinforce the findings presented in Table 3. Each of the remaining predictor variables individually accounted for < 8% of overall importance.

Discussion

The present study aimed to investigate the effectiveness of predictive analytics in identifying key factors of response to follow-up surveys from the Study. We evaluated six supervised machine learning algorithms with and without the inclusion of LCA classification of historical response patterns to predict the likelihood of response to the 2021 follow-up survey among Panel 1. Implementing LCA allowed us to classify participants into six distinct patterns based on historical response, including consistent web responders, consistent paper responders, mode switchers, temporary nonresponders, late nonresponders, and early nonresponders. These response patterns were found to be statistically significant across various military and demographic characteristics.

When comparing the use of the LCA variable, we observed that inclusion of the LCA variable in the model resulted in comparable prediction performance across all six algorithms, with random forest slightly outperforming other algorithms. This suggests that historical survey response patterns, as captured by the LCA variable, play a critical role in predicting survey response. Interestingly, interactions between predictors did not significantly impact the prediction models. Feature analysis revealed that the LCA variable accounted for the majority of predictor importance (53.1%), indicating its central role in the prediction of response likelihood. Other important predictors included birth year and length of service, further supporting the impact of demographic and military characteristics on response behavior.

Excluding the LCA variable from the model led to a notable decrease in prediction performance, emphasizing the importance of patterns in historical response in improving predictive analytics. In the absence of historical data, predictive models, particularly random forest, exhibit significantly improved prediction performance compared with logistic regression. The absence of historical data necessitates reliance on other characteristics, such as participant educational attainment and birth year in the case of the Study. The flexibility and adaptability of predictive models enable the identification of complex relationships and facilitate predictions even in the absence of historical data. These findings underscore the value of predictive analytics as a tool for addressing participant nonresponse in longitudinal cohort studies as well as the use of available historical data when choosing appropriate modeling approaches.

When comparing supervised machine learning algorithms, the superior performance of random forest and other ensemble methods over logistic regression, single layer neural networks, and XGBoost can be attributed to several key factors. Random forest, an ensemble learning method, combines multiple decision trees to reduce overfitting and improve generalization by averaging the predictions from different trees27,28. This is particularly beneficial for analyzing historical survey response patterns, which can be complex and exhibit non-linear relationships. Ensemble methods like random forest can capture these intricate patterns more effectively than simpler models like logistic regression, which assumes a linear relationship between predictors and the outcome. Additionally, random forest’s ability to handle many input variables without requiring significant tuning makes it well-suited for high-dimensional data sets27,29,30, such as those with numerous demographic and historical variables. On the other hand, while single layer neural networks can model non-linear relationships, they may not perform as well with limited data and can be prone to overfitting without sufficient regularization31–33. XGBoost, though powerful, often requires careful hyperparameter tuning34, making it unstable in certain scenarios. Therefore, the robustness, ease of use, and ability to capture complex patterns make random forest and ensemble methods more suitable for predicting survey response behaviors from historical data.

Comparing AUCs of ROC with those of the precision-recall curves illustrates the quality of the model. The AUC of precision-recall curves indicates how well the model performs with the positive class, whereas the AUC of the ROC considers both the negative and positive classes. The fact that we obtained high prediction performance in both classes, as demonstrated by AUCs greater than 80% for both sets of curves, indicates that our models are not biased towards either class. This balanced performance underscores the robustness of our predictive model.

Overall, this analysis demonstrates the potential of using machine learning in predicting participant response in longitudinal cohort studies. We were able to identify patterns in historical survey response that significantly contributed to predicting response to follow-up surveys. Our results demonstrate that while historical survey response has a significant impact on prediction, machine learning algorithms outperform traditional approaches when historical data is not available. Furthermore, our findings show the promise of predictive analytics in context and encourage further exploration of these techniques in diverse research conditions.

In comparison, Kern’s7,8 work predicted survey response but reported relatively low AUC precision-recall results (≤ 50%), indicating a bias in the model for predicting response. Our study, on the other hand, achieved high AUC of ROC and AUC of precision-recall (> 80%), illustrating the value of using a probabilistic method, such as LCA, to capture historical survey response behavior. Cernat and Sakshaug11 employed LCA in conjunction with a multinomial regression model to look for associations. In our work, we used LCA methods as a predictor in machine learning models. We illustrated the value of historical data as a strong predictor of future survey behavior and demonstrated that regression models can predict future survey behavior on par with machine learning models when robust historical data is available.

On the other hand, Jankowsky and Schroeders12 compared logistic regression and gradient boosting machines, only to find minor improvement in prediction performance. Given the small difference and complexity of interpreting machine learning algorithms, they question the utility and generalizability of machine learning in predicting survey response. However, those findings can be attributed to using an 80%/20% train-test split in combination with a temporal approach, which is ideal for predicting baseline rather than follow-up survey response. In contrast, our study focused on predicting follow-up survey responses using machine learning algorithms, which showed that in the absence of historical data machine learning models outperform traditional regression models.

In our work, we demonstrate when machine learning is appropriate and how it can be effectively implemented to predict future survey behavior. Although ensemble methods such as random forest outperformed other algorithms with our data and model, we do not endorse any one algorithm. Instead, we encourage investigators to take an empirically driven approach by comparing multiple algorithms with their own data. This approach ensures that the best model is selected based on the specific context and data characteristics.

Looking into future directions, a follow-up study is planned to incorporate data from the currently ongoing survey cycle scheduled to launch in 2024, to evaluate whether outreach efforts based on our findings have improved response rates. We also plan to update our models to include the upcoming survey cycle as predictors of response to future survey cycles, as the Study is expected to continue for another 45 years. Additionally, developing a method to predict baseline survey responses from individuals for whom we do not have prior data is a promising area for future research. This would involve creating robust algorithms capable of accurately forecasting survey participation without relying on historical response behavior, thereby broadening the applicability of predictive analytics in survey research.

The strengths of our study are the large and diverse population of U.S. military personnel included in the Study, and the comprehensive longitudinal data that allowed us to incorporate historical survey response as a key predictor. However, there are some limitations. One challenge in survey research is the change of residence of enrolled study participants and the potential unavailability of current residential addresses35. From our survey data, we observed consistent increases in response frequency shortly after paper outreach was sent to participants36. However, data confirming the accuracy of participant mailing addresses was not available for use in this machine learning analysis.

In addition, when applying the LCA method to panels with less than five time points, this method did not appear to be effective given that it could not distinguish all groups such as mode switchers or individuals who temporarily drop off from participation. In such instances when historical data are not available, the use of machine learning algorithms becomes even more critical. These algorithms have the capacity to identify complex patterns and relationships within the available data, leveraging advanced techniques to make accurate predictions. By utilizing features such as demographic information, current behavioral data, and other relevant variables, machine learning models can compensate for the lack of historical context, thereby providing valuable insights and maintaining high predictive accuracy.

Lastly, future research should involve testing this method on additional datasets to validate its generalizability and ensure broader applicability. By applying this method to diverse populations and varying types of survey data, researchers can assess the robustness and adaptability of the algorithms across different contexts. Additionally, exploring a wider range of datasets can provide insights into how different demographic, geographic, or temporal factors influence predictive performance, ultimately leading to more reliable and universally applicable predictive analytics methods in survey research.

The application of predictive analytics is a promising approach for improving prediction of longitudinal survey response, particularly in the absence of historical response data. By addressing participant nonresponse, researchers can enhance the validity and generalizability of study findings. This method contributes to the advancement of predictive analytics in longitudinal survey research to inform future data collection strategies, potentially improving participant engagement, and advancing our understanding of the health effects of military service.
