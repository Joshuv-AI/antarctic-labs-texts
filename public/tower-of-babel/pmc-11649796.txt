Designing a sector-coupled European energy system robust to 60 years of historical weather data
Authors: Ebbe Kyhl Gøtske, Gorm Bruun Andresen, Fabian Neumann, Marta Victoria
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs energy systems transform to rely on renewable energy and electrification to mitigate climate change, they encounter stronger year-to-year variability in energy supply and demand. Yet, most infrastructure planning relies on a single weather year, risking a potential lack of robustness. In this paper, we optimize capacity layouts for a European energy system under net-zero CO2 emissions for 62 different weather years. Subsequently, we fix the layouts and optimize their operation in every other weather year to assess resource adequacy and CO2 emissions. Our analysis shows a variation of ± 10% in total system costs across weather years. Layouts designed for years with compound weather events prove more robust, achieving resource adequacy of 99.9% and net-negative CO2 emissions of −0.5% per year relative to 1990 levels. CO2-emitting backup generation regulated by a CO2 tax offers a cost-effective measure to enhance robustness. It increases emissions only marginally, keeping average emissions below 1% of 1990 levels over all layouts. Our findings underscore the need for policymakers and energy stakeholders to account for interannual weather variability in future infrastructure planning.Subject terms: Photovoltaics, Atmospheric dynamics, Energy grids and networks, Wind energy

Introduction

Decarbonizing power systems by rebuilding them around wind and solar photovoltaic (PV) generation has proven to be a cost-effective strategy to reduce greenhouse gas emissions in Europe1,2 and globally3,4. This strategy can also help decarbonize other sectors through direct electrification (via heat pumps or electric vehicles), and indirect electrification (using electrolytic hydrogen and other synthetic fuels)1,2. Future energy systems with high wind and solar penetration might be more sensitive to interannual weather variations and extreme weather events, which should be considered in the planning of infrastructure for the energy transition. However, considering decades of weather data in a single capacity expansion optimization is computationally expensive at the high resolution that infrastructure planning requires. Hence, most previous studies use only a single weather year in their analysis.

Recent efforts to investigate interannual weather variability impacts on energy systems include modeling system dispatch5,6 or estimating optimal capacities7–11 for several weather years, estimating the storage capacity required to deal with Dunkelflauten, i.e., periods of low wind and solar generation12, determining the overlap of near-optimal solution spaces calculated for different weather years11, or attempting to classify the weather regimes that are particularly challenging for the European power system6,13,14. Assessments have also been conducted on the impact of extreme weather events on conventional infrastructure, including investigations of persistent cold spells in Texas15 and heat waves in North America16,17. However, previous research either considers one country in isolation7,9,12,13,18 (omitting the impact of cross-border energy flows, transmission bottlenecks and regional variability), includes only the power system5–8,10–12,19 (omitting the synergetic benefits from sector-coupling1), or considers only interannual variability of wind and solar capacity factors, and electricity demand5,7,8,10,12,19 (disregarding the impact of large interannual variation of hydropower inflow20,21). Furthermore, interannual weather variability not only impacts energy supply but also has a large impact on demand22–24. Resource adequacy is often assessed in a probabilistic way, quantified with the loss of load expectation (LOLE). Today, most European countries rely on the standard of LOLE less than 3 hours per year, while others have more relaxed requirements, e.g., 8 hours per year defined for Ireland25. In this study, we use 62 years of historical weather data (1960-2021) to investigate the impacts of different solar PV, onshore and offshore wind capacity factors, hydropower inflow, heating demand, and coefficient of performance (COP) of heat pumps time series. Our analysis employs PyPSA-Eur, an open networked model of the European sector-coupled energy system2,26 resolved with high spatio-temporal granularity and including the topology of today’s electricity transmission network, allowing us to study the impact of weather variability with high level of details. The model performs regional capacity expansion planning of energy infrastructure, covering the energy demands and CO2 emissions in the electricity, heating, land transport, shipping, and aviation, and industry (including industrial feedstock) sectors with comprehensive carbon management. Using this framework, our assessment includes impacts of interannual weather variability across sectors while considering a broader range of factors than previous research. We first perform a greenfield joint capacity and dispatch optimization for a European system constrained to net-zero CO2 emissions for every weather year to determine 62 alternative capacity layouts. Throughout this paper, we use the term design year to refer to the weather year used as input in the joint capacity and dispatch optimization. Subsequently, we keep the capacities fixed and analyze their robustness to interannual weather variations by optimizing the dispatch in each of the remaining 61 weather years different from the design year. Here, we refer to the weather years in the dispatch optimization as operational years. To accommodate extreme events, we allow the activation of CO2-emitting backup generation, which is subjected to a CO2 tax determined from the shadow price of the design year. Focusing on two key criteria, avoiding unserved energy and mitigating CO2 emissions, we address how interannual weather variations impact a highly renewable European energy system. Furthermore, we shed light on the characteristics that a robust energy system exhibits.

Results

Capacity optimization for different weather years

The optimization obtained with all design years (1960–2021) in Fig. 1a yields an average system cost of 803 billion euro (EUR) per year with a variation of ±10% in the most expensive and the cheapest capacity layouts. The distribution of system costs exhibits long tails (Fig. 1g), suggesting that rare extreme weather years drive the range of system costs. In searching for the main causes of system cost variation, Fig. 1b–d presents the annual available renewable resources from wind energy (aggregate of offshore and onshore), solar PV (utility and rooftop), and hydro inflow (reservoir and run-of-river) that can be utilized for electricity generation in every design year, plotted against total system cost. Wind and solar PV resources are shown as normalized power output (annual capacity factors; see Supplementary Fig. 18, which shows that the annual Europe-aggregated wind and solar PV resources are insensitive to the capacity layout). Stronger wind speeds result in larger capacity factors, hence fewer wind turbines are needed to produce the same amount of electricity. For this reason, we see a strong anticorrelation between annual wind resources and total system cost (r = −0.80). The same effect applies to solar PV but with a weaker correlation coefficient (r = − 0.37). Conversely, while the relative interannual variation is highest for hydro inflow on a country level (see Supplementary Note 1), its impact on the capacity optimization is small (correlation coefficient is not significant) because the volume of wind and solar PV generation is much larger. Figure 1e, f shows the heating demand and the COP of heat pumps. Persistent cold weather leads to high heating demand requiring more generation capacity for the provision of heating. For this reason, a strong correlation appears between annual heating demand and total system cost (r = 0.82). Simultaneously, cold weather leads to low COP for the heat pumps converting electricity to heat which has a similar impact on the total system cost (r = −0.60).

As all capacity optimizations fulfill the net-zero CO2 emissions constraint, the predominant investments are consistently placed in wind and solar PV electricity generation capacity, accounting for approximately 50% of total system costs. Consequently, they comprise on average 93% of the electricity generation. Including hydropower, renewable electricity accounts for 98% (Supplementary Fig. 20). In addition to the investments in renewables, additional costs are allocated for balancing, distributing, and storing electricity. The balancing is provided by firm generation from combined heat and power (CHP) plants fueled with solid biomass and incorporating point-source carbon capture (operated on average at 3864 full load hours per year), as well as flexible generation from gas CHP (611 full load hours per year) and open cycle gas turbine (OCGT; 152 full load hours per year). A small fraction of firm generation is supplied by nuclear power, which operates at base load (8293 full load hours per year). In certain design years, reserve capacity is deployed, with synthetic oil-fired power plants activated for only a few hours (40 full load hours per year). The operational pattern of the flexible generation sources is much different in the modeled scenarios compared to today, primarily to cover the extreme hours (see Supplementary Table 1 for a comparison with historical load hours derived from the ENTSO-E Transparency Platform27). Here, the oil is produced synthetically in Fischer-Tropsch plants combining electrolytic H2 and captured CO2, also covering the demand for oil in industry and aviation. The gas usage in OCGT and CHP plants can be of fossil origin (the system relies on annual production or imports of 121 TWh per year), from biogas (336 TWh per year), and, in some design years, produced synthetically with Sabatier or Helmeth28 reaction (see Supplementary Figs. 65-66).

Design years with less favorable renewable resources compensate by increasing their dependence on firm generation, in this case nuclear, which operates at almost full capacity. As a consequence, during the subsequent dispatch optimization for every capacity layout across 61 operational years, the firm generation may not alleviate challenging events since it cannot further increase its power production (see Supplementary Fig. 21).

Decarbonization of other sectors is obtained via direct electrification (heat pumps and electric vehicles) and indirect electrification (production of H2 and synthetic fuels) that are used in land transport, shipping, aviation, and industry (see Supplementary Figs. 53-67 for the deployed capacities in different sectors). Lastly, reaching net-zero emissions requires investment into point-source and air capture of CO2, as well as in underground CO2 sequestration. All design years fully utilize the sequestration limit of 200 MtCO2 per year assumed in our analysis. See previous works for detailed discussions on the interplay of different technologies1,2,26,29.

Unserved energy happens in winter periods

The capacity layouts were determined while assuming no precautionary robustness measure, such as a safety reserve capacity margin targeted at interannual weather variation. Consequently, during operational years, instances may arise where not all energy demands can be met. While imposing the CO2 tax resulting from the design year in the dispatch optimization, shortages in supply can be alleviated by gas and oil-fired backup capacities if built in the design year. In more severe cases, where available capacities do not suffice to bridge the gap between supply and demand, the system resorts to load shedding in either heating or electricity demand. We use loss of load or unserved energy interchangeably to describe this phenomenon. See Table 1 for definitions of resource adequacy metrics. Figure 2a shows the loss of load aggregated for contiguous hours with unserved energy (for the disaggregated loss of load in every three-hour time step, we refer to Supplementary Fig. 23). In Fig. 2a, unserved energy is most frequently observed during winter, agreeing with previous studies1,6,13,14. The highest sequential unserved energy is encountered in December 1968, December 1996, and January 1966, ordered by magnitude. The longest period with unserved energy occurs in January during the 1972 operational year.

Extreme operational years drive loss of load and emissions

As a next step, we explore all the combinations of operational years based on different capacity layouts (design years). We focus on evaluating the peak loss of load (i.e., the three-hour period with the largest unserved energy) and the annual net CO2 emissions obtained for different combinations of design and operational years. Figure 2b reveals that, in most cases, the highest peak loss of load is driven by operational years with extreme weather conditions (such as 1966 and 1968), as indicated by the long tail of the distributions. Consequently, when simulating the capacity layouts obtained with those years as design years, the system demonstrates greater robustness as they are capable of withstanding all operational years with loss of load lower than 25 GWh (2% of the average hourly electricity demand of Europe; see Fig. 3 for the loss of load duration curves in each design year). In investigating whether the most expensive year in the capacity optimization yield the most effective measure for mitigating loss of load events, we notice in Fig. 2b some relation between total system cost and reducing loss of load. However, the most expensive design year (2010) does not show the lowest peak loss of load. Vice versa, the lowest system cost (2020) does not result in the highest peak loss of load. In Fig. 3, eight capacity layouts exhibit higher robustness than 2010, of which the 1968 design year stands out. Although designed for only one weather year, the 1968 layout achieves a resource adequacy of 99.9% (LOLE of 10 hours per year), matching almost the most relaxed standard currently applied in Europe, which corresponds to Ireland25. Conversely, the 2020 design year reaches the poorest robustness with a resource adequacy of only 90%.

For the CO2 emissions (Fig. 2c), we observe a similar phenomenon to that of the peak loss of load, but in this case, we see consistently the same operational year (2010) causing the highest CO2 emissions regardless of the design year. In the extreme case, an overshoot of 7.6% relative to 1990 emissions levels is observed, while the best case (2020 operational year) shows an undershoot of −1.5%. Net CO2 emissions (accumulated over all operational years) across capacity layouts vary from −0.9% to 3.2%. The overshoot observed during the 2010 operational years can be largely attributed to the low annual wind generation and high annual heating demand, and it is therefore more tied to the total system cost. In capacity optimization, this drives more investments in heating generation and storage capacities, electricity generation capacity, and direct air capture (DAC) (Supplementary Figs. 39-42). As a result, the system becomes the most prepared for mitigating CO2 emissions. Using this year as an operational year for all other capacity layouts, the weather requires the system to compensate for the second highest annual heating demand and lowest annual wind generation (Fig. 1). As a response (Fig. 4), the system increases the heating generation from the fixed capacity of gas boilers, electricity generation from OCGT, co-generation from gas CHP plants, and, in a few cases, synthetic oil-fired power plants, which leads to higher CO2 emissions. In some cases, the system is forced to shut off the DAC, thus, preventing the offset of the increased emissions, which consequently leads to a higher net CO2 emission balance. On average for the different capacity layouts, the operational years create net CO2 emissions of 0.8% relative to 1990 historical levels, i.e., a slight increase compared to the net-zero design requirement.

Compound weather events trigger periods with unserved energy

In our analysis, we identify distinct compound weather events triggering loss of load events. The 1968 operational year entails the highest peak loss of load (Fig. 2b) and, on average, the highest sequential unserved energy (Fig. 2a). While we observe low hydro inflow and high heating demand (both on the border of the 5th and 95th percentiles), the loss of load is mostly driven by a pronounced scarcity of wind resources (below the 5th percentile) in early December (Fig. 5 top). Here, wind lulls are observed in countries such as Germany, Netherlands, Poland, and Denmark, lasting approximately one week (see Supplementary Fig. 35). Simultaneously, the system encounters lost load lasting, on average, 4.7 days with 30.9 GWh unserved energy per hour (approximately 3% of the hourly electricity demand; Fig. 5 bottom). Previous analyses have made similar observations on the high impacts on energy systems of wind energy droughts1,6,13,14. The 1996 operational year encounters a similar wind energy drop causing the second-highest sequential unserved energy. However, the latter coincides with average heating demand and a period with abundant hydropower resources, which suggests that the unserved energy is caused solely by the scarcity of wind energy (see Supplementary Fig. 32).

While the unserved energy of the 1968 operational year also shows some correlation with other phenomena besides the scarcity of wind resources, we find that other years face more clear evidence of compound weather events driving loss of load. The second-highest peak loss of load across all years is encountered in the 1966 operational year. This is caused by a widespread wind scarcity and a concurrent abnormal heating demand due to low temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 29), both transcending the 5th and 95th percentiles. By closer inspection, the 1966 European heating demand is highly driven by unusually high demands in France and Germany during that period (Supplementary Fig. 38). This coincides with a significant drop in wind resources, reaching a stagnation point in those countries for two days (from January 16 to 18; Supplementary Fig. 34). The longest loss of load event occurs in the 1972 operational year and lasts, on average, 7 days (with 7.4 GWh unserved energy per hour, corresponding to less than 1% of the hourly electricity demand). The event coincides with a prolonged hydro reservoir drought throughout January, which is uniformly distributed over Europe and caused by a seasonal shift (from winter to summer) in the hydropower inflow. Additionally, in the same period, Europe also encounters a scarcity of wind energy (lasting four days) and solar PV resources (lasting two days), see Supplementary Fig. 31, 37. Solar PV resources are generally less variable from year to year, and for this reason, we do not find similar cases with solar PV droughts causing significant load shedding. The 2010 operational year has the lowest annual wind resources and the second highest annual heating demand, but it does not cause a similarly large impact as the above-mentioned conditions. This is because the wind lulls are less severe and do not coincide with other weather impacts (Supplementary Fig. 33).

The figure indicates how well the capacity layout prevents load shedding in each country, and how the impacts of the compound weather events are generally distributed among countries. For the latter, we observe a shift between Northeast (NE) and Southwest (SW) encountering high levels of unserved energy in both regions, varying for different operational years. See Supplementary Figs. 27 and 28 for the geospatial distribution of unserved energy in every operational year averaged for all capacity layouts and vice versa. Most design years show load shedding in either NE or SW but is more commonly observed in the NE of Europe. For the dispatch optimization of the most expensive capacity layout (2010) and the two capacity layouts obtaining the lowest peak loss of load on a European level (1966 and 1968), we notice that energy deficits are also concentrated in either NE or SW. However, the 1968 capacity layout shows almost zero unserved energy for all operational years.

Strategies to increase robustness

As a last step, we investigate which properties are needed for the energy system to be robust. First, we describe the characteristics of robust capacity layouts. In Fig. 7, the most robust systems, in terms of avoiding loss of load (1966 and 1968), have more flexible generation (OCGT and gas CHP) in France and Italy, and reserve capacity (synthetic oil-fired power plants) in countries dependent on wind (GB, France, Germany, Netherlands, and Denmark), indicating that it compensates for the observed wind droughts. Spain, which has a solar-dominated electricity generation, does not install backup generation but increases the capacity of solar PV and batteries to be compatible with the 1968 operational year.

Regarding H2 pipelines, we observe distinct topology layouts: The 1968 design year features a more distributed network in the Southwest, whereas the 1966 layout exhibits more reinforcements in the Northeast. This pattern aligns well with the regional distribution of the loss of load for the 1966 and 1968 capacity layouts (Fig. 6), wherein the loss of load occurs in the region that lacks reinforcements.

The system’s preference for synthetic oil-fueled power plants over OCGT in some regions is explained by the intended operation scheme. The synthetic oil-fired power plant has lower energy efficiency and higher running costs but is associated with lower capacity costs. I.e., it is built to run only for a few hours per year, targeting periods with severe wind droughts. This is more pronounced for the 1966 design year, and in combination with a higher reliance on gas boilers in the heating supply (Supplementary Fig. 46), this design year suffers higher CO2 emissions (on average 0.1% net CO2 emissions; Fig. 2). Conversely, the 1968 design year relies more on renewable electrified technologies, with additional deployment of heat pumps and resistive heating, and larger capacities of H2 production, storage, and fuel cells, compared to the average. For this reason, the 1968 design year emits less CO2 (on average, it becomes net negative with a −0.5% undershoot) and deploys less DAC (Supplementary Fig. 42).

Second, we estimate the safety reserve capacity margin in every design year necessary for achieving a fully robust system. The regional distribution of peak loss of load is different across operational years. For this reason, we determine the reserve capacity margin from the sum of the peak loss of load in each country across all operational years as a conservative estimate (see Supplementary Note 2). For the 1968 design year, which has the most promising tradeoff of avoiding unserved energy and mitigating CO2 emissions, our approach leads to a European backup capacity requirement of 42 GW (0.6% of the total electricity generation capacity), distributed over Great Britain, Sweden, and Italy (see Supplementary Fig. 26). Furthermore, it would need to be operated approximately 20 hours per year (0.27% of the time), see Supplementary Fig. 4.

Sensitivity study

In this paper, we assume a fixed electricity transmission equivalent to today’s capacity. In Fig. 7, we showed reinforcements in the H2 pipelines for robust design years, indicating a beneficial impact from a larger volume of transmission, in line with the study by Grochowicz et al.11. In Supplementary Note 3, we assess the sensitivity to expanding electricity transmission. We show an improved robustness from electricity transmission for an average design year. However, since transmission expansion entails more wind generation, the sensitivity to wind variability increases. Concurrently, due to a smaller fleet of backup generation, we observe higher peak loss of load during compound weather events in the capacity layouts with electricity transmission expansion.

To avoid an unrealistic operation of hydropower reservoirs, conflicting with environmental aspects and water accessibility, we included a seasonal constraint on the reservoir filling level according to historical observations. From a sensitivity study (Supplementary Note 3), we show that the reservoir operation constraint has a negligible impact on total system cost. It furthermore leads to a small increase in unserved energy, but this is negligible compared to the variation across all operational years.

Discussion

In this paper, we show that challenging weather conditions affecting the energy system predominantly occur during winter. However, capacity layouts with increased backup generation, energy storage and a more distributed H2 network are capable of alleviating many of the instances. In this section, we discuss the identified compound weather events in the context of previous literature and their impact on energy system reliability. Subsequently, we examine the effectiveness of backup generation in mitigating unserved energy. Finally, we address the implications for CO2 emissions and the robustness of highly renewable energy systems against extreme weather events.

In our study, wind droughts were identified as the predominant driver of unserved energy, which is reinforced by cold spells increasing heating demand. These compound events have also been identified in previous literature1,11,13,14,30, explained by the existence of certain weather regimes across Europe. In particular, European blocking and Greenland blocking weather regimes are known to create high pressure over central Europe causing a period of prolonged low wind and low ambient temperature11,13,14. In our study, the impacts of cold spells are captured better since they entail higher heating demand and lower coefficient of performance of heat pumps, reducing the available electric heating supply. Moreover, we identified an additional type of compound event resulting in load shedding, caused by a combination of wind drought and low inflow in hydropower reservoirs.

The literature underscores the importance of examining longer-lasting energy droughts6,12, as they often exert greater system impacts than isolated extreme hours. Our findings align with this perspective, as the high-impact operational years we identified (1966 and 1968) are characterized by compound events lasting multiple days. Additionally, our study emphasizes the pitfalls of relying solely on average renewable resources and demand of a certain year to assess system reliability. The identified high-impact operational years do not show extreme values for the annual wind resources, hydro inflow, and heating demand. In fact, they show average annual hydro inflow and heating demand, while the annual wind energy is higher than that of eight other operational years.

Across all design years examined, we observe a variation in total system costs of ± 10% relative to the mean, largely determined by the annual wind resources and the demand for heating. Grochowicz et al.11 observed a comparable variation in total system costs using 40 design years, and Schlachtberger et al.8 showed similar correlations between annual wind resources and system costs using four design years. Both studies investigate only the power system. It is relevant to notice that interannual system cost variability remains similar in our case where the power system is coupled with heating, transport, and industry. This can be explained by the consistency of necessary investments in wind and solar PV across all design years, which constitute the predominant part of the investments.

One of the robustness measures to counter compound weather events lies within the power sector, specifically through the deployment of additional backup generation from gas OCGT and synthetic oil-fired power plants. In some countries, oil power plants are preferred over OCGT since they are only used for a few hours per year. Although our assessment identifies design years that are almost compatible with all operational years, we still observe deficits in the electricity supply. For the most robust capacity layout (1968 as the design year), we show that an additional reserve capacity of 42 GW, operated for 20 hours per year, is needed to fully avoid unserved energy in every operational year when accounting for bottlenecks in the electricity transmission network. Assuming this capacity is provided by OCGT power plants fueled with fossil gas, this adds annualized capacity costs of 777 million EUR and fuel costs of 3 million EUR (assuming a gas price of 20 EUR MWh−1), corresponding to a cost increase of 0.1% relative to the average of all design years. Furthermore, this adds emissions of 26 ktCO2 per year, corresponding to an overshoot of less than 0.001% relative to 1990 historical levels. Previous literature has shown the benefit of integrating green methanol into backup generation since methanol storage has a low capacity cost and can be stored and dispatched with interannual fluctuations31. Choosing OCGT fueled with green methanol (with a market price of 100 EUR MWh−1 31) instead changes the picture marginally, resulting in an increase of 13 million EUR in fuel costs (assuming unchanged capital costs).

Our study also addresses the interannual variation of CO2 emissions. We show how years with persistent low wind energy and high heating demand (e.g. 2010) cause the highest net CO2 emissions, while other operational years are capable of reaching net negative. Over the six decades of weather data, the capacity layouts reach average net CO2 emissions of 0.8% relative to 1990, equivalent to 34 MtCO2 per year. This could be offset by installing DAC with a capacity of 4 ktCO2 per hour, which is almost equivalent to the capacity deployed in the design year with the largest DAC installments. This would add annualized capital costs of 3 million EUR for the DAC but would require an additional 17% of the assumed underground sequestration potential to be exploited.

Despite the insights gained from our analysis, it is important to acknowledge several limitations, discussed in the following paragraphs. Deploying additional backup generation capacity is the conservative option to avoid load shedding. In reality, fluctuating electricity prices drive the incentive for demand-side management which would shift some energy services to a later instance when electricity prices are lower. While charging electric vehicles and power-to-X technologies operate flexibly, the remaining residential electricity demand is assumed to be perfectly inelastic. For this reason, the system does not represent consumer behavior as would be expected in reality, which could further prevent instances of unserved energy. Demand elasticities could also be utilized by reducing demand with appropriate monetary compensation. Another aspect is that the model is constrained by its assumption of Europe being autarkic as we do not allow imports of electricity and green fuels from outside of Europe. In reality, intercontinental energy trades could also increase the robustness under compound events, e.g., by storing imported green fuels as a strategic long-term reserve.

Our assessment relies on joint capacity and dispatch optimization, considering the operation throughout a calendar year, repeated for different design years. The model assumes long-term market equilibrium, and for this reason, assets are only deployed if they fully recover their costs. The cost recovery is, however, only guaranteed for the considered design year. For instance, the increased share of backup generation in robust capacity layouts is a result of a difficult design year, but in more favorable design years, less backup generation is needed. This might challenge the financial aspect of the investments associated with the proposed backup infrastructure in the robust capacity layouts, assuming this investment happens on a free market. In the model, this could be addressed by extending the horizon, and thus the perfect foresight, from one year to multiple years, similar to previous research6,9,12,31,32 while including one of the extreme weather years identified in our work. This could potentially replace some of the backup generation with interannual energy storage31,32. This approach is, however, much more computationally expensive, and has not yet been done with the level of spatial, temporal, and technological resolution used in our analysis. An alternative approach for future research could be to consider whether using an ensemble of few weather years in the design stage could lead to a fully robust system, and if so, how this ensemble should be made.

In our work, we assume that the 62 weather reanalysis years encapsulate the realm of likely weather events that may interfere with a future fully decarbonized energy system. Already, from the weather reanalysis, we were able to see a long-term trend in the heating demand and hydro inflow. The aim of our work was, however, to analyze the impact of the natural variability in a steady-state climate. In the future, climate change signals might be even more pronounced and could have more complex implications impacting not only the annual inflow and heating demand. Increasing ambient temperatures leads to a negative trend in the heating demand, while for cooling demand, it is the opposite33. This necessitates the deployment of air-conditioning units in more locations, imposing more stress on the electricity grid during heat waves34. Concurrently, droughts in hydro reservoirs are expected to be more severe in Southern regions of Europe21. Moreover, wind energy35 and solar PV36 resources might also be impacted. Future studies could address this in a similar assessment, but with a greater focus on the climate change signal instead of the natural variability of renewable resources and energy demand as highlighted in our work37.

In conclusion, capacity layouts designed under different weather years show diverse resource adequacy (from 90% to 99.9%) and net CO2 emissions (from −0.9% to 3.2%, relative to 1990 levels). This highlights that analyses for European and national policies should not rely on just a single weather year but on a broader range.

Despite a noticeable total system cost variation ( ± 10%) from interannual weather variability, we show that high penetration of wind and solar is cost-competitive in all cases. Moreover, a European energy system with high renewable penetration is robust to historical weather years if backup generation is built to compensate for week-long wind droughts. The additional cost of getting robustness for a system with high wind and solar penetration is bearable. Additionally, demand-side management, especially in the heating sector, is important to bridge resource droughts during winter and should be incentivized by policy.

Lastly, the perfect zero-fossil system should not be the enemy of the good ones. While in many of our scenarios, CO2 emissions are not completely eliminated, our results indicate that retaining some conventional power plants as reserve capacities can support the system’s robustness against multi-year weather variability at limited environmental and economic cost. Moreover, there is potential for substituting fossil fuels with synthetic green fuels (e.g. methane or methanol) to fully eliminate emissions at a relatively low cost, given the small energy volumes needed for backup. Therefore, governments across Europe need to plan for strategic reserves (fossil or green) to mitigate persistent resource droughts and other disruptive events, ensuring a resilient energy supply.

Methods

We investigate 62 alternative future European energy systems, determined for different weather years, using the open sector-coupled energy system model PyPSA-Eur. The model performs capacity expansion and dispatch optimization of generation, transmission, storage, and conversion technologies. The model assumes an idealized market with perfect competition, and it has perfect operational foresight with some limitations for hydropower explained below. All energy storage (including hydropower reservoirs) is modeled assuming a cyclic operation, ensuring that the storage filling level at the end of the year is equivalent to the initial filling level. Our investigation is split into two steps: 1) joint capacity and dispatch optimization to determine 62 alternative capacity layouts for different weather years (design years), and 2) dispatch optimization of the 62 capacity layouts in every other weather year (operational year) different from the design year, leading to a total of 3844 optimization runs. In the first step, we impose the constraint of net-zero CO2 emissions for Europe and ensure that all energy demands are met in every hour and location.

Joint capacity and dispatch optimization

The capacity layouts are determined from an overnight optimization (with key assumptions summarized in Table 2) based on a network resolution of 37 nodes, covering 33 ENTSO-E member countries, and are solved with a three-hourly resolution to reduce computation time. Previous research8,10,26 shows the negligible impact of downsampling from hourly to three-hourly resolution for long-term planning studies, in particular for studies including multiple weather years. The model includes energy and non-energy feedstock demand and CO2 emissions (energy-related and from industrial processes) in the electricity, heating, land transport, shipping, aviation, and industry sectors and energy demand in agriculture. For heating, we assume district heating is expanded to cover 60% of urban demand. For land transport, we assume 85% of the heavy- and light-duty vehicle fleet to be battery electric vehicles, while the remaining heavy-duty transport uses H2 fuel cells. The shipping sector is fueled with H2. For steel manufacturing, 30% is assumed to originate from the primary route with H2-based Direct Reduced Iron, while the remaining share is from the secondary route (i.e., from recycling). For aluminum, we assume 20% is produced via the primary route and 80% is recycled, while we assume no recycling of plastics and high-value chemicals. The overnight optimization relies on a greenfield investment approach, disregarding existing capacities except for hydropower and electricity transmission. For the electricity transmission, we assume the capacity of today (disregarding planned lines in the Ten-Year Network Development Plan (TYNDP 2018) by ENTSO-E38; see Supplementary Fig. 14). We fix the hydropower capacity as we assume that the cost-effective potential in Europe has already been fully exploited, while we assume a fixed electricity transmission to represent rising network expansion costs in recent years, due to supplier scarcity, and social acceptance constraints. For the electricity transmission assumption, we provide a sensitivity analysis in Supplementary Note 3. Within Europe, countries can import and export electricity and H2 from neighboring countries depending on the available transmission capacity. Meanwhile, the model does not account for intercontinental trading of electricity and green fuels. We include interannual variability for wind energy, solar PV, hydropower inflow, heating demand, and coefficient of performance (COP) of heat pumps. We include the historical national electricity demand from ENTSO-E (deducting the estimated share for heating) as an inflexible electricity demand, assuming zero price elasticity and no interannual variability. Additional electricity demand from electrification in the heating sector determined endogenously by the model can have interannual variation due to the included temperature dependence of the heating demand and COP. Today’s cooling demand is included in the inflexible electricity load and is kept constant in all design and operational years, and it is therefore not dependent on interannual weather variability. For the mathematical formulation, we refer to Supplementary Method 1.

Key assumptions in the capacity optimization

Dispatch optimization under different weather inputs

Following the capacity optimization, we fix the capacity layout and perform a dispatch optimization for every other operational year than the design year. We allow the system to shed electricity and heat load, thus violating the energy balance imposed in the capacity optimization (supplementary equation (2) in Supplementary Method 1). This should be considered a last resort since it is a very expensive solution. We assume a social cost of unserved energy of 100,000 EUR MWh−1, similar to the social cost (87,000$ MWh−1) estimated for the 2021 blackout in Texas15. To evaluate the loss of load events, we identify the metrics summarized in Table 1. Similarly, while the capacity optimization was subject to a net-zero CO2 emissions constraint, the dispatch optimization allows the model to deviate from this constraint as a measure to avoid load shedding. This requires the payment of a CO2 tax, derived from the CO2 shadow price μCO2 in the capacity optimization (supplementary equation (3) in Supplementary Method 1). See Supplementary Fig. 22 for the derived CO2 tax in each design year. We assume a fixed level of CO2 emissions from industrial processes (153 MtCO2 per year), which can be offset by underground CO2 sequestration (assumed potential of 200 MtCO2 per year).

Conversion of weather reanalysis data

Using Atlite39, we convert weather reanalysis from ERA540, acquired for the years 1960 - 2021, to estimate the available renewable time series for wind energy, solar PV, and hydropower (see Section S3.2 in ref. 2 for a detailed description of the wind and solar PV time series, and potential estimation in every region). From the same source, we also estimate the heating demand and the coefficient of performance (COP) of heat pumps. For the wind energy and solar PV resources (Supplementary Fig. 18) and the COP (Supplementary Fig. 19), this is based on the available data for all weather years, without further data processing. Hydro inflow is estimated with Atlite based on the surface runoff data from ERA5, data on hydropower plant locations41 and upstream basins42, and is calibrated with historical annual production from IRENA43. Despite being a conventional electricity generation technology, the historical hydropower capacity has increased from 1960 forward (either by exploring new sites or expanding the capacity at an existing site). This has led to a positive trend in the historical generation. This is accounted for by normalizing the historical generation data by the installed capacity for the corresponding year. The heating demand is estimated based on the heating degree days (HDD) computed from the ERA5 temperature and fitted with historical intraday profiles from BDEW44. From the JRC IDEES45, this is calibrated with the 2015 reported annual data. For the hydro inflow and heating demand, previous work has shown a historical long-term trend in hydro resources46 and heating demand47. For this reason, we apply a method to detrend the data to omit the historical climate change signal. We impose a cutoff at the 1990 weather year, dividing the dataset into two 30-year climate periods. The first 30 years are then scaled to match the average of the subsequent 30-year period while retaining the subannual variation according to the ERA5 data. See Supplementary Figs. 15-17. The average scaling factors between 1960-1989 and 1990-2021 are 1.39 and 1.15 for annual hydro inflow and heating demand. When comparing the annual renewable resources (average capacity factors) and heating demand for every weather year, we aggregate on both technological and geospatial levels. Solar PV resources include utility-scale and rooftop panels, while wind energy includes onshore and offshore turbines. The geospatial aggregation to obtain a European-wide value is capacity-weighted, based on the optimal capacity layout of the capacity optimization. The sensitivity of the European aggregation of wind and solar PV resources to the capacity layout was tested in Supplementary Fig. 18, which shows negligible impact.

Constraint on seasonal hydropower reservoir operation

To emulate a water-energy nexus model that accounts for access to water for other purposes than energy, we consider historical data to delineate the range of realistic operations of hydropower. This is important for the cost-optimal operation of hydropower to harmonize with other natural and human requirements. Previous work has shown that without this, the model tends to operate hydro reservoir more aggressively compared to the historical seasonal trends, mostly driven by the perfect foresight assumption21. In our model, we also observe how countries such as Spain and Greece exert substantial deviation from the historical data when we do not impose any operational constraint on the reservoir filling level (Fig. 8). This could cause unintended problems in water accessibility and river flow continuity. Here, we apply the ENTSO-E data on historical reservoir filling levels to impose a lower bound of the modeled weekly average state-of-charge (SOC). See Supplementary Method 1 for the mathematical formulation of the constraint. The resulting SOC is found in Fig. 8 which shows that this is a conservative approach.
