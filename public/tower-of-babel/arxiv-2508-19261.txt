Floor sensors are cheap and easy to use! A Nihon Buyo Case Study
Miho Imai

Abstract
. As floor-sensing technologies gain traction in movement research, questions remain about their usability and effectiveness for non-expert users. This study presents a case study evaluating Flexel, a modular, low-cost, high-resolution pressure-sensing floor interface, in the context of Nihon Buyo, a traditional Japanese dance. The system was installed, calibrated, and used by a first-time, non-technical user to track weight distribution patterns of a teacher and learner over nine weeks. Live pressure data was synchronized with video recordings, and custom software was developed to process and analyze the signal. Despite expectations that the learner’s weight distribution would converge toward the teacher’s over time, quantitative analyses revealed that the learner developed a consistent yet distinct movement profile. These findings suggest that even within rigid pedagogical structures, individual movement signatures can emerge. More importantly, the study demonstrates that Flexel can be deployed and operated effectively by non-expert users, highlighting its potential for broader adoption in education, performance, and embodied research.



1.
Introduction

As floor-sensing technologies gain traction in movement research, questions remain about their usability and effectiveness for non-expert users. We present a case study
of high school students using
Flexel

(
yoshida2022flexel
)
, a modular, low-cost, high-resolution pressure-sensing floor interface. The students used Flexel to understand the movement and progress of novice dancers in
Nihon Buyo
classes—a traditional Japanese dance. The students installed the system with minimal help, calibrated it, and used it as first-time non-technical users to track the weight distribution patterns of a teacher and a learner over a period of nine weeks. The students used Flexel to obtain pressure data in a simple CSV format, which they then synchronized to video recordings using external software.
This allowed them to understand their results.

Students were surprised: they had an initial hypothesis that a novice dancer’s weight distribution would, over time, align more
closely to that of a professional instructor. But with the quantitative results that they obtained by using Flexel, they were able to discard this theory. Instead, they found that the novice dancer developed a consistent yet distinct movement profile. These findings suggest that even within rigid pedagogical structures, individual movement signatures can emerge. More importantly, this simple case study demonstrates that Flexel can be deployed and operated effectively by non-expert users,
highlighting its potential for broader adoption in education, performance, and embodied research.

2.
Background

Below we provide some background on Flexel and Nihon Buyo.

2.1.
Flexel

Figure 1
.
Nihon Buyo professional dancer

(
yoshida2022flexel

.

Flexel
(
yoshida2022flexel
)
is a floor system that can track where people are standing, walking, or moving. Instead of using cameras or wearables, Flexel uses special tiles with built-in pressure sensors that feel how much weight is on them and where it is coming from.

Each tile, covering
0.25m^{2}
, is made up of 9 sensing units and can be easily placed next to other tiles to cover any size room. Flexel can maintain high sensing accuracy while reducing sensor density, achieving comparable performance with only 1/81 the number of the density of sensors typically required by commercial pressure mats. This makes it cheaper and easier to set up.

2.2.
Nihon Buyo

Nihon Buyo
(
malm1977legacy
)
is a classical Japanese dance form that is structured and requires precise, controlled movements. Dancers are traditionally trained to follow the steps of a master, spending years as apprentices to learn precise moves. Because of the strict nature of the dance, one might wonder whether dancers end up moving the same way, or if they eventually develop their own unique style over time.

3.
Objective

In this project we report on the experience of high school students who used Flexel
to explore a burning question they had: in Nihon Buyo, does the weight distribution of novice dancers start to replicate the weight distribution of an expert instructor over time? By collecting and analyzing data using Flexel, the students were able to collect evidence for and against this hypothesis.

4.
Methodology

Figure 3
.
Screenshot of the software that analyzes the data collected by Flexel. The software displays the pressure and movement in a variety of visual formats that are easy for users to see and understand.

Measurement process.

The students measured the weight pressure distribution of dancers with Flexel. Flexel can sample the pressure distribution at 80Hz. In every square meter of floorboard, there are 144 load cells installed. The students used 4 of these floorboards to capture the weight pressure distributions of the dancers, creating an area of four square meters for each dancer. The students then connected Flexel onto a PC and opened up its collection software (“Unisession for Floor”). At this point, Flexel would stream measurement data and the software would report the pressure distribution and display a pressure image for the students to see and analyze. During this process, Flexel automatically applied a calibration algorithm to ensure accurate and reliable measurements. At the end of the measurement, the software produced a CSV file with the data.

Collecting dance data.

To create the teacher’s dance dataset, the students recorded a professional dancer performing a dance called “Seven Lucky Gods” 3 different times. They then asked a novice dancer to practice the same dance for a period of 9 weeks. The novice dancer had access to a video recording of the teacher’s dance during this timeframe. Each week,
the students measured the novice dancer’s movements using Flexel 3 times.
The students stopped collection after 9 weeks, because the instructor deemed that
the novice dancer had fully learned the dance.

Processing the data

The students processed the data using a Jupyter Notebook. They first determined the x and y center of mass of the dancer by finding the average of the x-axis and y-axis and locating a point with those x and y values recorded on the floorboards.
Since the students did not have background in statistics or data science, an expert provided them with an equation to calculate the center of pressure (COP) in both the x and y directions, which they did on the Jupyter Notebook.
The equations were as follows:

(1)

x_{COP}=\frac{\sum_{i}x_{i}F_{i}}{\sum_{i}F_{i}}

(2)

y_{COP}=\frac{\sum_{i}y_{i}F_{i}}{\sum_{i}F_{i}}

The COP represents the average location of all forces acting on the surface, essentially indicating where the body’s weight is concentrated. In the equations,
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
are the positions where force is applied, while
F_{i}
represents the amount of force at each point. The total force,
F_{total}
, is the sum of all these individual forces. By taking the weighted average of these positions, the COP helps analyze balance and weight distribution, which is crucial in studying movement control. In the figures, the COP sensor data is represented in pixel units of floor sensor. To convert the pixel values into centimeters, they should be multiplied by 8.3, as there are six sensor units per 50 cm (
\frac{50}{6}=8.3
).

The students next removed any outliers: data points where the center of mass differed from the previous frame by over 8 cm in either the horizontal or vertical
direction. Finally, to align data recordings across sessions, the students used the video recordings as a guide to determine when the dancer steps onto the Flexel board.

4.1.
Experiment results

After collecting and analyzing the Flexel data, the students found that the results contradicted their original hypothesis. The students had expected the novice’s weight distribution to gradually become more similar to the expert’s over time. However, the data showed that the overall weight distribution had consistently differed between the two dancers after several weeks of practice.

We repeated the previous analysis, evaluating the cross-correlation and cosine similarity of the dancers for sections of the dance where the dancers performed specific Nihon Buyo movements: hopping on one foot, preparing
the bow, hitting the drums, and standing up and rotating on one foot.
In each of these dance movements, the learner’s weight distribution profile did not become more similar to the teacher’s as they practiced. In contrast, the similarity between the learner’s dance in week one and week nine showed a converging pattern, corroborating our previous findings.

This led the students to reconsider their initial assumption. The students now believed that individual factors such as differences in height, body weight, natural gait, and muscle memory, may play a bigger role in shaping how each person moves, even when performing a tightly regimented choreography. Moreover, the increasing differences between the novice and the professional’s movements over time may also suggest the development of individuality.

5.
Related work

Two areas relate to our work: Nihon Buyo and the use of technology, which is similar to our application domain; and the use of floor sensors to study movement, particularly in dance.

For Nihon Buyo, several systems
(
marumo2009evaluating
;
sakata2011quantifying
)
have been proposed to classify dance styles, analyze the relationship between the center of gravity and performance, and develop instructional tools. However, a key difference from our work is that these approaches depend on expensive hardware and software, often requiring professional installation and operation. This restricts their accessibility to well-funded research teams or professional environments, making them impractical for widespread educational or amateur use.

For floor sensing used in dance, the field has seen a progression from early systems like PodoBoard
(
johnstone1991midi
)
, which required dancers to wear metal plates to detect foot pressure, to sophisticated platforms like Magic Carpet
(
paradiso1997magic
)
, which used a grid of wires covered with piezoelectric material and included Doppler radars to detect foot pressure. More recent innovations include modular systems like LiteFoot
(
griffith1998litefoot
)
, which utilizes optical proximity sensors to detect the location of foot contact on the floor, and Z-Tiles
(
richardson2004z
)
, which employ piezoelectric sensing to register footfalls. Other contemporary systems such as Sensemble
(
aylward2006sensemble
)
use wearable wireless sensors for expressive movement tracking, but are not floor-based and do not provide direct pressure data.

While these systems have expanded the possibilities for non-intrusive dance monitoring, they also illustrate a persistent trade-off: high-end systems like Magic Carpet, LiteFoot, and Z-Tiles are prohibitively expensive and require specialized installation and maintenance. On the other hand, affordable solutions—such as basic capacitive or optical touch floors
(
jeremy2016how
)
—lack critical features such as high-resolution pressure sensing.

Flexel addresses this gap by offering a cost-effective, easy-to-use solution that retains the ability to sense both the location and intensity of foot pressure
(
yoshida2022flexel
)
. This balance is important for the study of Nihon Buyo, where subtle shifts in weight and precise footwork are central to the art form. By making detailed movement analysis more accessible, Flexel opens new opportunities for research, education, and the preservation of traditional dance.

6.
Conclusion

This study demonstrated that Flexel is a practical tool for analyzing center of pressure changes during Nihon Buyo dance practice. While no formal usability assessment was conducted, the students themselves reported that Flexel was easy to use, particularly noting the straightforward processes for recording and importing data through Unisession for Floor, an in-house software interface. This user feedback suggests that Flexel can be readily adopted for student-led research projects.

Flexel’s functionality enabled the successful collection and analysis of data reflecting the nuanced postural shifts and movements characteristic of Nihon Buyo. The ability to quantitatively compare the novice’s and instructor’s movements provided valuable insights into motor learning and performance in traditional dance.

The students are excited to continue their research with a focus on investigating how individual physical differences influence dance movements, particularly in Nihon Buyo, over time.
