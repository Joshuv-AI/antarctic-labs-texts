CircEPHB4 binds to YBX1 to upregulate MRPS16 and promotes glioma progression
Authors: Yuxiang Liao, Bo Liu, Zhiping Zhang, Qian Zhang, Mingyong Xiang, Chen Jin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Glioma is the most frequently diagnosed brain tumor in adults worldwide, and it accounts for approximately 30% of primary brain tumors [1]. Despite the advances in surgery, radio- and chemotherapies, the survival time and prognosis of most patients with glioma remain unfavorable [2]. The median survival of high-grade glioma is ~ 15–36 month [3]. Even after surgical resection, the residual glioma cells contribute to local recurrence and metastasis of glioma [4]. It is of clinical significance to desipher the mechanisms underlying glioma tumor progression.

In recent years, non-coding RNAs have emerged as crucial regulators in glioma pathogenesis. Among these, circular RNAs (circRNAs) represent a novel class of endogenous non-coding RNAs characterized by covalently closed continuous loops. Unlike linear RNAs, circRNAs are generated through back-splicing events and exhibit high stability due to their resistance to exonuclease-mediated degradation [5]. These molecules have emerged as key regulators in various cancers, functioning as microRNA sponges, protein decoys, and transcriptional regulators [6]. Ephrin receptor B4 (EPHB4), a member of EPHB family, is a tyrosine kinase receptor. It is well-established that EPHB4 is implicated in various physiological and pathological processes, including neurooncological diseases [7]. While EPHB4 is known to be elevated and function as an oncogene in glioma [8], and contributes to anti-angiogenic therapy resistance through modulation of vascular morphogenesis and cell survival [9], recent evidence has revealed that its derived circRNA, circEPHB4, may play distinct roles in tumorigenesis. Interestingly, circEPHB4 demonstrates context-dependent functions, acting as either a tumor suppressor or oncogene in different cancer types [10, 11]. In glioma, circEPHB4 is highly expressed [12] and m6A-modified circEPHB4 facilitates the stemness, proliferation and metastasis in glioma [11]. However, the mechanism by which circEPHB4 exerts its oncogenic effect in glioma remains largely undefined.

Mitochondrial ribosome is comprised of the small 28 S subunit (mt-SSU) and the large 39 S subunits (mt-LSU), and it plays an essential role in mRNA translation [13]. Mitochondrial ribosomal protein S16 (MRPS16), a member of small MRP genes, is implicated in the critical step in mt-SSU assembly [14]. It is also a DNase that introduces nicks into supercoiled DNAs [15], and the truncated MRPS16 leads to the decrease of the mitochondrial content of 12 S rRNA [16]. Notably, MRPS16 is highly expressed and correlated with unfavorable prognosis in glioma. Mechanistically, MRPS16 promotes glioma cell growth, migration and invasion through PI3K/Akt/Snail signaling [17]. Similar oncogenic roles have been observed in ovarian cancer and lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), where MRPS16 facilitates LUAD progression via the PI3K/Akt/Frataxin axis [18, 19]. In accordance with previous finding, GEPIA database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/) predicted the induction of MRPS16 in glioma. However, if circEPHB4 regulated MRPS16 to promote glioma progression remains uninvestigated.

The emerging role of RNA modifications in cancer biology provides another layer of regulation. 5-methylcytosine (m5C) modification plays essential roles in various biological processes by modulating protein translation and the stability of mRNA and non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), such as transfer RNA (tRNA), ribosomal RNA (rRNA) or other ncRNAs [20, 21]. m5C modification is catalyzed by three types of regulators, including “writer” methyltransferase, “erasers” demethylases and “reader” binding proteins [20]. Our preliminary analysis predicted several m5C-modified sites in MRPS16 mRNA, as well as the putative interaction between m5C reader Y-box binding protein 1 (YBX1) and MRPS16 mRNA. More importantly, YBX1 is elevated in glioma and promotes the progression of glioma [22, 23]. Intriguingly, a direct binding between circEPHB4 and YBX1 was also predicted by starBase database (https://rnasysu.com/encori/). We thus hypothesized that circEPHB4 might interact with YBX1 to enhance YBX1-mediated recognition and binding to the m5C sites on MRPS16 mRNA, thereby increasing MRPS16 mRNA stability and expression to promote malignant progression of glioma.

Ubiquitination is a tightly regulated post-translational modification which is mediated by a series enzyme, including E1 ubiquitin-activating enzyme, E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme and E3 ubiquitin ligase [23]. An early study has illustrated that retinoblastoma-binding protein 6 (RBBP6) is an E3 ligase responsible for the degradation of YBX1 [24]. Accumulating evidence supports that circRNAs are involved in regulating protein homeostasis via ubiquitin-proteasome pathway [25–27]. Given the predicted interaction between circEPHB4 and YBX1, it is speculated that circEPHB4 might compete with RBBP6 to bind YBX1 to inhibit the ubiquitin-proteasomal degradation of YBX1, thus modulating MRPS16 mRNA stability.

In this study, we demonstrated that circEPHB4 facilitated glioma cell proliferation, migration, and invasion through inducing MRPS16. Mechanistically, circEPHB4 enhanced MRPS16 mRNA stability through YBX1-mediated m5C modification. Additionally, circEPHB4 regulated YBX1 protein stability via RBBP6-mediated ubiquitination. Functional studies illustrated that circEPHB4 interacted with YBX1 to promote malignant progression of glioma via inducing MRPS16 in vitro and in vivo. A novel regulatory axis circEPHB4/YBX1/MRPS16 was identified, and this study provided growing silver linings for glioma treatment.

Materials and methods

Clinical specimen analysis

A total of 15 fresh-frozen glioma tissue specimens were obtained from patients undergoing surgical resection at the Xiangya Hospital of Central South University. The study was conducted with approval from the Institutional Review Board (Approval No: 2023020025), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Total RNA was extracted and subjected to qRT-PCR analysis to determine the expression levels of circEPHB4, YBX1, and MRPS16. For circEPHB4 detection, RNA samples were treated with RNase R to remove linear RNA prior to cDNA synthesis. Gene expression levels were quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Statistical correlations among the three factors were assessed using Pearson correlation analysis in SPSS22.0 software.

Cell culture, transfection, and treatment

The glial cell line SVGP12 and human glioma cell lines SHG44, A172, LN229, U373, BT325 were from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA). Cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) at 37 °C/5% CO2. The scramble shRNA (shNC), shcircEPHB4-1, shcircEPHB4-2, shMRPS16-1, shMRPS16-2, shYBX1 and shRBBP6 were from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). MRPS16 or YBX1 was cloned into pcDNA3.1 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The mutants of circEPHB4 were from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). SHG44 and U373 cells were transfected with shRNA using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). For the treatment, SHG44 and U373 cells were treated with MG132 (20 µM, proteasome inhibitor) for 6 h, chloroquine (10 µM, CQ, autophagy inhibitor) for 48 h or 10 µM cycloheximide (CHX, protein synthesis inhibitor) for 0, 15, 30, 60, 120 and 240 min.

Subcellular fractionation

Subcellular fractionation was performed using PARIS kit (AM1921, Invitrogen). SHG44 and U373 cells were lysed in Cell Fractionation Buffer. After centrifugation, the cytoplasmic fraction was collected, and nuclear fraction was extracted with Cell Disruption Buffer. RNA was then isolated from cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions and examined by qRT-PCR.

qRT-PCR and RNA stability assay

Total RNA was extract from glioma cells or tissues using Trizol (Invitrogen), and reversed transcribed using SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen). qRT-PCR was conducted using SYBR Green Master Mix (ABI, Foster City, CA, USA) and quantified with 2−ΔΔCT method. For RNase R treatment, RNA (1 µg) was incubated with RNase R (3U, RiboBio, Guangzhou, China) at 37 °C for 1 h. For RNA stability assay, glioma cells were treated with Actinomycin D (2 µg/mL, ActD, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA).

RNA FISH/immunofluorescence (IF)

SHG44 and U373 cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and prehybridized with 0.3% Triton X-100. Glioma cells were then hybridized with Cy3-labeled circEPHB4 probe (RiboBio) in hybridization buffer at 37℃ overnight. For IF staining, glioma cells were then incubated with anti-YBX1 (1:100, PA5-83493, Invitrogen) at 4℃ overnight, followed by the incubation of Alexa Fluor 488-goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:500, A11008, Invitrogen). Images were acquired using confocal microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Tissue sections from mice implanted with SHG44 cells expressing vector, wild-type circEPHB4 (circEPHB4-WT), or binding site mutant circEPHB4 (circEPHB4-Mut1) were subjected to RNA FISH/IF analysis. Briefly, sections were fixed, permeabilized, and hybridized with Cy3-labeled circEPHB4 probe (RiboBio). Following hybridization, samples were incubated with anti-YBX1 antibody (1:100, PA5-83493, Invitrogen) overnight at 4 ℃, followed by Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (1:500, A11008, Invitrogen). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

CCK-8 assay

SHG44 and U373 cells were seeded in 96-well plates 24 h prior to transfection. Cells were incubated with CCK-8 solution (10 µL/well, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at 37 °C for 1 h at 0, 24, 48 and 72 h post-transfection. A450 was measured using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad).

EdU incorporation assay

DNA incorporation was assessed using Click-iT EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (C10337, Invitrogen). Transfected SHG44 and U373 cells were incubated with EdU. After fixation and permeabilization, cells were incubated with Click-iT reaction mixture at room temperature for 30 min. The fluorescence intensity of the cells was observed under a fluorescence microscope, and five random fields were selected for imaging and the percentage of EdU-positive cells was quantified by counting fluorescently labeled nuclei using ImageJ software. The EdU positive rate (%) = the number of positive cells/total cells × 100%.

Transwell assay

SHG44 and U373 cells (3 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in the Matrigel (Corning, Corning, NY, USA)-coated upper chamber of Transwell insert (Corning). The lower chamber was filled with complete DMEM medium. After 24 h, the remaining cells in the upper chamber were wiped. The invasive or migratory cells were stained with crystal violet and photographed under a microscope (Nikon). The migration assay was conducted without Matrigel coating. The number of invasive or migratory cells was quantified by counting the crystal violet-stained cells from five random fields under a microscope (100× magnification) using ImageJ software.

Animal study

All animal studies were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (approval number:2023020025). Male BALB/c nude mice (4-week-old, n = 24 per group) were from Hunan Slake Jingda Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd.

In vivo tumorigenesis assay was performed as described [28]. Briefly, SHG44 or U373 cells were infected with adeno-associated virus (AAV)-negative control shRNA (sh-NC), AAV-shcircEPHB4, AAV-shMRPS16 or/and AAV-YBX1 luciferase construct (1 × 108 TU/mL). For intracranial implantation, mice were anesthetized and fixed in a stereotactic apparatus. A horizontal incision on the skin at the connection point of the bilateral eyelids on the top of the head was made, exposing the anterior fontanelle skull landmark. At 1 mm anterior to the midpoint of the anterior fontanelle and 2 mm lateral to the sagittal suture, drill a bone hole with a dental drill. A total of 2 × 105 luciferase-labeled SHG44 or U373 cells in 5 µL of PBS were injected by a 10 µL micro syringe through the bone hole to the bottom of the skull 3.7 mm below, retreat 2 mm, and inject the cell suspension completely within 20 min. Bioluminescence imaging was conducted post-inoculation at day 28. Prior to imaging, mice were intraperitoneally injected with D-luciferin and anesthetized with isoflurane. Image acquisition was conducted 10 min after luciferin administration with an exposure time of 1–60 s. Quantitative analysis was performed using Living Image software (PerkinElmer), where a standardized region of interest (ROI) was drawn to encompass the cranial bioluminescent signal for each mouse. The total flux (photons/second) within each ROI was automatically calculated by the software and recorded. On day 28, 6 mice in each group were sacrificed and the brain tissues were dissected for subsequent analysis. The mice in each group (n = 18 per group) were housed for survival analysis.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The deparaffinized and rehydrated slides were then permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100. Following antigen retrieval, the sections were blocked with 1% BSA and incubated with anti-Ki67 (1:100, ab15580, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-MRPS16 (1:100, PA5-99095, Invitrogen) or anti-YBX1 (1:100, PA5-83493, Invitrogen) antibody at 4 °C overnight. This was followed by the secondary antibody incubation (31460, Invitrogen), and signal was detected using DAB substrate (Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

Western blot

SHG44 and U373 cells were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime), and quantified using BCA protein assay kit (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA). After gel electrophoresis and transfer, the membrane was blocked with 5% silk milk. The blots were then incubated with anti-MRPS16 (1:1000, PA5-99095, Invitrogen), anti-YBX1 (1:2000, A303-230 A, Bethy Laboratories), anti-ALYREF (1:1000, H00010189-M03, Abnova), anti-ubiquitin (1:1000, ab140601, Abcam), anti-RBBP6 (1:500, ab237514, Abcam), anti-Lamin B1(1:1000, ab8982, Abcam), or anti-β-actin antibody (1:2000, ab8226, Abcam) at 4 °C overnight. This was followed by secondary antibody incubation, and signal was detected using ECL substrate (Beyotime).

RNA pull-down assay

RNA pull-down assay was carried out using Pierce RNA pull-down kit (20164, Pierce). Briefly, biotinylated MRPS16 mRNA/circEPHB4 or negative control probe was conjugated with Streptavidin beads, followed by the incubation the cell lysates. The enriched Protein-RNA complexes were then eluted and analyzed by western blot.

RNA Immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay

RIP assay was performed using Magna RIP Kit (17–700, Millipore, Billerica, USA). SHG44 and U373 cells were lysed with RIP Lysis Buffer. Anti-YBX1 antibody (2 µg, A303-230 A, Bethy Laboratories) or normal rabbit IgG (10500 C, Invitrogen) was conjugated to Protein A/G beads, and RNA-protein complexes were then immunoprecipitated by antibody-conjugated beads. After RNA purification, the mRNA level of MRPS16 was analyzed by qRT-PCR.

Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP)

The ubiquitination of YBX1 and the interaction between YBX1 and RBBP6, were assessed by co-IP. Briefly, SHG44 and U373 cells were transfected with shNC/shcircEPHB4 and HA-tagged ubiquitin. Glioma cells were lysed with IP lysis buffer, and incubated with anti-YBX1 antibody (2 µg, A303-230 A, Bethy Laboratories) or normal rabbit IgG (10500 C, Invitrogen) at 4 °C overnight. The protein complexes were then enriched using Protein A/G magnetic beads (88802, Pierce). Proteins were eluted and analyze by western blot. Whole cell lysates were used as an input control.

IF staining

SHG44 and U373 cells were fixed with 4% PFA and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100. Glioma cells were blocked with 1% BSA and incubated with anti-YBX1 (1:100, PA5-83493, Invitrogen) and anti-RBBP6 antibody (1:100, H00005930-B01P, Abnova) at 4 °C overnight. Cells were then incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:500, A11008, Invitrogen) and Alexa Fluor 594-goat anti-mouse IgG (1:500, A11005, Invitrogen). Nuclei were visualized with DAPI, and images were acquired using confocal microscope (Nikon).

Dual-Luciferase reporter assay

The wild-type (WT) MRPS16 3’-untranslated region (3’UTR) segment containing predicted m5C motifs (5’-CGGG-3’) at positions 1154–1160, 1494–1500, and 2181–2187 was cloned downstream of the firefly luciferase gene in the pmirGLO vector (Promega). A mutant (Mut) version of the 3’UTR, in which all three m5C motifs were disrupted by site-directed mutagenesis, was similarly constructed. SHG44 cells were co-transfected with either the WT or Mut reporter plasmid, along with control or gene-specific shRNAs, using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). Forty-eight hours post-transfection, firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured sequentially using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity for each sample, and results are presented as relative luciferase activity from three independent experiments.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± SD. Statistical differences between multi-groups or two-groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA or two-tailed Student’s t test by SPSS22.0 software, respectively. Survival analysis was determined by the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Knockdown of circEPHB4 inhibits malignant progression of glioma in vitro and in vivo

The characterization of circEPHB4 was conducted in glioma cells. qRT-PCR showed remarkable elevation of circEPHB4 in glioma cell lines A172, SHG44, LN229, U373 and BT325 cells, compared with glial cell line SVGP12 cells (Fig. S1A). As presented in Fig. S1B, Sanger sequencing revealed that circEPHB4 was produced through back-splicing of exons 5–17 derived from chr7:100400186–100417918. To validate the back-splice junction of circEPHB4, cDNA and gDNA were amplified. The back-splicing region of circEPHB4 was amplified using divergent primers, and this product cannot be detected in gDNA samples (Fig. S1C). Additionally, RNase R treatment and RNA stability assay showed that circEPHB4 was more stable than EPHB4 mRNA (Fig. S1D-E). Furthermore, the cytoplasmic localization of circEPHB4 in SHG44 and U373 cells were detected by subcellular fractionation and RNA FISH (Fig. S1F-G). To investigate the function of circEPHB4 in glioma, we performed knockdown using two distinct shRNAs (shcircEPHB4-1 and shcircEPHB4-2) to minimize potential off-target effects. As anticipated, transfection of shcircEPHB4-1 or shcircEPHB4-2 markedly decreased circEPHB4 level in SHG44 and U373 cells. shcircEPHB4-2 exhibited higher knockdown efficacy in glioma cells (Fig. 1A). CCK-8 assay and EdU incorporation assay showed that lack of circEPHB4 inhibited the viability and proliferation of SHG44 and U373 cells (Fig. 1B-C). Similarly, the migratory and invasive capacities of SHG44 and U373 cells were also inhibited by circEPHB4 knockdown as detected by Transwell assay (Fig. 1D). Moreover, bioluminescence imaging revealed that silencing of circEPHB4 remarkably decreased the luminescence intensity of xenograft tumors (Fig. 1E). This was accompanied with improved survival rate of mice in shcircEPHB4-1 and shcircEPHB4-2 groups, compared with that of control mice (Fig. 1F). Furthermore, successful knockdown of circEPHB4 in xenograft tumors were confirmed by qRT-PCR (Fig. 1G), along with the reduction of the proliferation marker Ki67 (Fig. 1H). Collectively, these data indicate that circEPHB4 is implicated in the regulation of glioma progression.

Silencing of MRPS16 inhibits glioma progression in vitro and in vivo

Bioinformatics analysis based on GEPIA database predicted that MRPS16 was elevated in glioma (Fig. 2A). In line with this finding, MRPS16 was markedly upregulated in glioma cell lines A172, SHG44, LN229, U373 and BT325 cells, in comparison with SVGP12 cells (Fig. 2B). As presented in Fig. 2C, the mRNA and protein levels of MRPS16 were greatly decreased by shMRPS16-1 or shMRPS16-2 in SHG44 and U373 cells. Similar with circEPHB4, silencing of MRPS16 inhibited cell viability and proliferation in SHG44 and U373 cells (Fig. 2D-E). In addition, impaired migratory and invasive capacities of MRPS16-knockdown SHG44 and U373 cells were also observed by Transwell assay (Fig. 2F). In vivo bioluminescence imaging further revealed that lack of MRPS16 reduced the luminescence intensity of xenograft tumors (Fig. 2G). Compared with control mice, mice in MRPS16-knockdown groups also exhibited better survival rate (Fig. 2H). IHC staining confirmed the successful knockdown of MRPS16 in xenograft tumors, along with the reduction of Ki67 (Fig. 2I). These findings indicate that MRPS16 serves as an oncogene in glioma.

CircEPHB4 promotes glioma cell proliferation, migration, and invasion through inducing MRPS16

To study the role of circEPHB4/MRPS16 axis, a series of functional experiments were carried out. Intriguingly, circEPHB4 knockdown reduced MRPS16 expression in SHG44 and U373 cells (Fig. 3A). shcircEPHB4-2 with higher knockdown efficacy was selected for the subsequent experiments. Additionally, MRPS16 overexpression induced MRPS16 mRNA and protein levels without any effect on circEPHB4 level, and it also compensated for shcircEPHB4-suppressed MRPS16 expression (Fig. 3B-C). CCK-8 and EdU incorporation assays showed that MRPS16 overexpression enhanced the viability and proliferation of SHG44 and U373 cells, and counteracted the impairments caused by shcircEPHB4 on cell viability and growth in both glioma cells (Fig. 3D-E). Moreover, similar tendencies of cell migration and invasion were found by Transwell assay. MRPS16 overexpression alone also facilitated cell migration and invasion in SHG44 and U373 cells, and shcircEPHB4-inhibited migration and invasion were rescued by MRPS16 overexpression (Fig. 3F). These findings suggest that MRPS16 acts as a downstream effector of circEPHB4 in glioma cells.

YBX1 enhances MRPS16 mRNA stability via m5C modification

Database from starBase and RM2Target predicted the putative binding proteins of circEPHB4 and MRPS16. Among the 16 candidates, the m5C-related proteins YBX1 and ALYREF were selected for the subsequent analysis (Fig. 4A-B). Interestingly, GEPIA database predicted that both YBX1 and ALYREF were upregulated in glioma (Fig. 4C). In accordance with the bioinformatics analysis, qRT-PCR and western blot confirmed the elevations of YBX1 and ALYREF in glioma cells (Fig. 4D). RNA pull-down assay further revealed that biotinylated MRPS16 mRNA probe successfully pulled down YBX1, but not ALYREF in SHG44 and U373 cells (Fig. 4E). To investigate the functional role of YBX1 in glioma cells, we performed knockdown experiments using two distinct shRNAs. As expected, both shYBX1-1 and shYBX1-2 effectively reduced YBX1 protein levels in SHG44 and U373 cells, with shYBX1-2 demonstrating superior knockdown efficiency (Fig. 4F). Based on these results, shYBX1-2 was selected for all subsequent functional studies. RIP assay showed that loss of YBX1 reduced the association between YBX1 and MRPS16 mRNA in SHG44 and U373 cells (Fig. 4G). This was accompanied with increased MRPS16 mRNA decay in YBX1-knockdown SHG44 and U373 cells upon ActD treatment (Fig. 4H). These data suggest that YBX1 is required for maintaining the mRNA stability of MRPS16 in glioma cells.

CircEPHB4 enhances MRPS16 mRNA stability through binding to YBX1

We next sought to unravel the mechanism by which circEPHB4 regulated MRPS16 expression in glioma cells. Database from starBase predicted the potential interacting sites between circEPHB4 and YBX1 (Fig. 5A). According to the starBase database prediction, a positive correlation was observed between YBX1 and MRPS16 in brain lower-grade glioma (LGG) (Fig. S2A). Moreover, analysis of 15 clinical glioma specimens confirmed positive correlations between circEPHB4 and MRPS16, and between YBX1 and MRPS16 (Fig. S2B). Consistently, RNA pull-down and RIP assays unequivocally validated the direct association between circEPHB4 and YBX1 in SHG44 and U373 cells (Fig. 5B-C). RNA FISH/IF further showed the co-localization of circEPHB4 and YBX1 in the cytoplasm of SHG44 and U373 cells (Fig. 5D). More specifically, the putative YBX1 binding sites located from nt 307–312 and 1304–1309 of circEPHB4. Two mutants, namely Mut1 (Mut 307–312) and Mut2 (Mut 1304–1309), were constructed (Fig. 5E). RNA pull-down assay revealed that Mut1 abolished the binding between YBX1 and circEPHB4, while Mut2 had no effect on circEPHB4-YBX1 interaction (Fig. 5E), suggesting that nt 307–312 was responsible for YBX1 binding. To substantiate this interaction under more physiologically relevant conditions, we performed RNA FISH/IF co-localization analysis in xenograft tumor tissues. The results demonstrated robust co-localization of circEPHB4 and YBX1 in tumors formed by cells expressing wild-type circEPHB4, whereas this co-localization was markedly attenuated in tumors derived from cells carrying the binding site mutant circEPHB4-Mut1 (Fig. S2C). This finding confirmed that the identified binding site was essential for the circEPHB4-YBX1 interaction within the tumor models in vivo. To investigate whether the circEPHB4/YBX1 axis regulates MRPS16 via m5C modification, we performed dual-luciferase reporter assays. Bioinformatic analysis using the RM2Target database identified three conserved m5C motifs (5’-CGGG-3’) within the MRPS16 3’UTR (positions 1154–1160, 1494–1500, and 2181–2187). A schematic diagram of the constructed dual-luciferase reporters containing either the wild-type or m5C-site-mutated MRPS16 3’UTR is presented in Fig. S3A. Knockdown of either YBX1 or circEPHB4 significantly reduced the luciferase activity of a wild-type MRPS16 3’UTR reporter, but not that of a mutant reporter with disrupted m5C sites (Fig. S3B-C). These results indicated that circEPHB4 promoted MRPS16 expression through YBX1 in an m5C-dependent manner. In addition, knockdown of circEPHB4 reduced circEPHB4, YBX1 and MRPS16 expression, whereas YBX1 overexpression not only upregulated YBX1 level, but also led to an induction of MRPS16 in glioma cells (Fig. 5F-G). Furthermore, YBX1 overexpression alone slowed down the turnover of MRPS16 mRNA in glioma cells, and shcircEPHB4-accelerated MRPS16 mRNA decay was also rescued by YBX1 overexpression (Fig. 5H). These findings indicate that circEPHB4 enhances MRPS16 mRNA stability through recruiting YBX1.

CircEPHB4 regulates YBX1 protein stability through RBBP6-mediated ubiquitination

We further delineated the regulatory mechanism underlying circEPHB4-modulated YBX1 expression. As presented in Fig. 6A, knockdown of circEPHB4 had no remarkable effect on YBX1 mRNA level, but dramatically downregulated its protein level in glioma cells. Moreover, it was shown that knockdown of circEPHB4 led to a decrease in YBX1 protein levels in both the cytoplasm and nucleus, with a more pronounced reduction in the cytoplasm (Fig. S3D). The proteasome inhibitor MG132, but not autophagy inhibitor CQ, counteracted shcircEPHB4-decreased YBX1 protein expression in both SHG44 and U373 cells (Fig. 6B), suggesting that ubiquitin-proteasomal degradation might be implicated in shcircEPHB4-mediated downregulation of YBX1. Additionally, silencing of circEPHB4 also accelerated the protein degradation of YBX1 in the presence of CHX (protein synthesis inhibitor) (Fig. 6C). Co-IP further revealed that circEPHB4 knockdown induced the ubiquitination of YBX1 in glioma cells (Fig. 6D). Hitpredict and Ubibrowser database predicted two potential binding proteins of YBX1, namely RBBP6 and BARD1 (Fig. 6E). Subsequent co-IP confirmed the direct association between RBBP6 and YBX1, but not between BARD1 and YBX1 (Fig. 6F). In line with co-IP result, IF staining showed the co-localization of YBX1 and RBBP6 in the cytoplasm and nucleus of glioma cells (Fig. 6G). More importantly, lack of circEPHB4 enhanced the interaction between RBBP6 and YBX1 (Fig. 6H). As shown in Fig. S4A, silencing of RBBP6 successfully reduced RBBP6 expression, along with the induction of YBX1 in glioma cells. Moreover, RBBP6 knockdown suppressed the protein degradation of YBX1 (Fig. S4B), and inhibited its ubiquitination as detected by co-IP (Fig. S4C). RBBP6 expression wasn’t affected by shcircEPHB4. RBBP6 knockdown had no effect on circEPHB4 level, but reduced RBBP6 expression (Fig. S4D-E). While loss of RBBP6 also upregulated YBX1 and MRPS16 protein levels which was reversed by shcircEPHB4 in glioma cells (Fig. S4E). It is worth noting that RBBP6 knockdown inhibited MRPS16 mRNA decay, and attenuated shcircEPHB4-impaired MRPS16 mRNA stability (Fig. S4F). These data suggest that RBBP6-mediated ubiquitination is involved in circEPHB4-regulated YBX1 expression in glioma cells, thereby contributing to the regulation of MRPS16 mRNA stability.

CircEPHB4 interacts with YBX1 to promote glioma cell growth via inducing MRPS16 in vitro and in vivo

The biological functions of circEPHB4/YBX1/MRPS16 axis were next validated in the glioma models. Consistently, silencing of circEPHB4 impaired the cell viability, proliferation, migration, and invasion in SHG44 and U373 cells. By contrast, YBX1 overexpression exhibited opposite effects, and it also abrogated shcircEPHB4-induced impairments on cell growth, migration, and invasion in glioma cells (Fig. 7A-C). Furthermore, in vivo bioluminescence imaging revealed that circEPHB4 knockdown significantly attenuated the total flux from xenograft tumors compared to the shNC group (Fig. 7D). This suppression of tumor growth conferred a significant survival advantage, with the median survival of mice in the circEPHB4-knockdown group was prolonged (Fig. 7E). circEPHB4 knockdown downregulated circEPHB4, Ki67, YBX1 and MRPS16 expression in xenograft tumors, whereas overexpression of YBX1 upregulated Ki67, YBX1 and MRPS16 in vivo. YBX1 overexpression also reversed shcircEPHB4-decreased Ki67, YBX1 and MRPS16 in xenograft tumors (Fig. 7F-G). These findings suggest that circEPHB4 facilitates glioma progression through upreglating YBX1 to increase MRPS16 expression in vitro and in vivo.

Discussion

Glioma is a most common brain tumor with high mortality and morbidity worldwide. Therapeutic resistance remains the major challenges for glioma treatment. Emerging evidence supports that a variety of signaling pathways, genes and ncRNAs are involved in glioma pathogenesis [29]. In this study, we reported that circEPHB4 stabilized YBX1 protein and increased its expression in a RBBP6-dependent manner, thereby enhancing MRPS16 mRNA stability through m5C modification, thus facilitating the progression of glioma. These findings provide novel insights into targeted therapeutic strategy for glioma.

In accordance with previous report, the characterization of circEPHB4 showed that circEPHB4 was derived from the reverse cleavage of exon 5 and 17 of EPHB4 transcript [11]. The upregulation of circEPHB4 is positively associated with glioma stemness, proliferation and metastasis [11, 12]. METTL3/YTHDC1 is responsible for the m6A modification and cytoplasmic localization of circEPHB4 in glioma cells, and m6A-modified circEPHB4 facilitates glioma growth, stemness and metastasis via SOX2/PHLDB2 axis [11]. In the present study, knockdown studies in vitro also revealed the oncogenic role of circEPHB4 in glioma in which silencing of circEPHB4 impaired the viability and proliferation, as well as the migratory and invasive capacities of glioma cells. Consistently, mice in circEPHB4-knockdown group showed improved survival rate. Moreover, circEPHB4 positively regulated MRPS16 expression in glioma cells, and MRPS16 overexpression rescued shcircEHPB4-imparied cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in glioma cells in vitro, indicating that MRPS16 acts as a critical downstream effector of circEPHB4 in glioma. Consistent with previous finding which has reported that MRPS16 promotes glioma progression through PI3K/Akt/Snail pathway [17], we found that MRPS16 functions as an oncogene in glioma. Collectively, our data suggest that circEPHB4 promotes the progression of glioma via inducing MRPS16 expression, possibly through the activation of downstream PI3K/Akt/Snail axis.

Compelling evidence shows that dysregulation of RNA binding proteins (RBPs) is associated with the pathological processes in different cancers [30, 31]. YBX1 is developmentally regulated in brain tissues [32], and the elevation of YBX1 is correlated with increased glioma cell growth, metastasis and chemoresistance [33–35]. Consistently, the induction of YBX1 was observed in glioma cells in the current study. A recent study has illustrated that KPNB1 promotes glioma progression via YBX1-mediated transcriptional regulation of Neuroligin-3 [23]. YBX1 also facilitates CCT4 translation and mLST8 folding to activate mTOR signaling, thereby promoting glioma growth [22]. In this study, YBX1 was identified as a crucial mediator between circEPHB4 and MRPS16 mRNA in glioma cells. YBX1 facilitates esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) progression by stabilizing SMOX mRNA in an m5C-dependent manner [36]. In breast cancer, NSUN2/YBX1 promotes cancer progression by enhancing the mRNA stability of HGH1 through m5C modification [37]. Similar with these reports, knockdown experiments revealed that YBX1 acted as a key player in the maintenance of MRPS16 mRNA stability in glioma cells. It is worth noting that and shcircEPHB4-accelerated mRNA decay of MRPS16 was counteracted by YBX1 overexpression, suggesting that circEPHB4 is an upstream interacting partner of YBX1 in the regulation of MRPS16 mRNA stability. However, the mechanism by which YBX1 mediated the m5C methylation of MRPS16 mRNA merits further investigation in the future study.

Recently, RBBP6 is identified as a novel regulator of glioblastoma stem cells via CPSF3-dependent alternative polyadenylation [38]. Moreover, RBBP6 is an interacting partner of YBX1 through the RING finger domain, and responsible for the ubiquitin-proteasomal degradation of YBX1 [24]. In line with this research, we reported that RBBP6-mediated ubiquitination of YBX1 was involved in circEPHB4-regulated YBX1 expression. CircEPHB4 competed with RBBP6 to bind YBX1, and suppressed the ubiquitin-proteasomal degradation of YBX1, thus modulating MRPS16 mRNA stability. Notably, the regulation of YBX1 stability by circRNAs exhibits remarkable context-dependence. While circNEIL3 promoted NEDD4L-mediated YBX1 degradation [39], our study revealed that circEPHB4 functioned as a cytoplasmic stabilizer of YBX1 by competitively binding to RBBP6, thereby preventing ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation. This protective mechanism was functionally analogous to circRNA-SORE, which stabilized YBX1 by interfering with PRP19-mediated degradation [40], though through distinct molecular partners. Our subcellular fractionation experiments further demonstrated that circEPHB4 depletion preferentially reduced cytoplasmic YBX1 levels, subsequently diminishing nuclear YBX1 through pool equilibrium, without altering its fundamental nucleo-cytoplasmic distribution. These findings collectively suggested that YBX1 existed in functionally specialized pools regulated by specific circRNA-E3 ligase pairs, highlighting an intricate post-translational regulatory network that fine-tunes YBX1 homeostasis in glioma pathogenesis.

In conclusion, circEPHB4 competitively binds to YBX1, shielding it from RBBP6-mediated ubiquitination and degradation, thereby stabilizing YBX1 protein. The stabilized YBX1 then binds to and enhances the stability of MRPS16 mRNA, ultimately driving glioma tumorigenesis, with this entire axis being dependent on the identified m5C modification sites (Fig. 8). It demonstrated that a novel circEPHB4/RBBP6/YBX1/MRPS16 axis was identified in glioma, and these findings shed light on the targeted therapy for glioma treatment. The most direct strategy would involve developing RNA-based therapeutics, such as antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) or small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), specifically designed to knockdown circEPHB4 in glioma cells. Furthermore, the identification of the critical oncogenic role of the YBX1/MRPS16 downstream axis provides an alternative target; inhibiting this interaction or the downstream metabolic functions of MRPS16 could also disrupt this pro-tumorigenic pathway. Collectively, this work establishes a conceptual foundation for developing novel targeted therapies against this newly identified vulnerability in glioma. Finally, it must be acknowledged that this study has certain limitations. Although we analyzed 15 clinical glioma samples, which revealed positive correlations among these molecules and were further supported by bioinformatic data from larger cohorts, the sample size remains relatively modest. Future studies involving larger, well-annotated clinical cohorts are necessary to further validate the clinical relevance and prognostic value of this axis.
