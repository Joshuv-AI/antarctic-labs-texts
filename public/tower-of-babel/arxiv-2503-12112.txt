Quantifying Irreversibility via Bayesian Subjectivity for Classical & Quantum Linear Maps
Lizhuo Liu, Clive Cenxin Aw

Abstract
In both classical and quantum physics, irreversible processes are described by maps that contract the space of states. The change in volume has often been taken as a natural quantifier of the amount of irreversibility. In Bayesian inference, loss of information results in the retrodiction for the initial state becoming increasingly influenced by the choice of reference prior. In this paper, we import this latter perspective into physics, by quantifying the irreversibility of any process with its Bayesian subjectivity—that is, the sensitivity of its retrodiction to one’s prior. From this perspective, we review analytical and numerical results that highlight both intuitive and subtle insights that this measure sheds on irreversible processes.

A natural question then arises: can this relationship be formalized through a measure of irreversibility that quantifies such prior dependence for arbitrary linear maps? This leads us to this work’s central construct.

IV
Bayesian Subjectivity

We quantify the dependence of a map’s Bayesian inversion on its prior, as an irreversibility measure, through the
Bayesian Subjectivity

\mathrm{I^{\mathsf{s}}}
of that map. For the classical case, we formalize this as:

\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{c}}(\varphi)=\int||\hat{\varphi}_{\gamma_{1}}-\hat{\varphi}_{\gamma_{2}}||_{\lambda}\;d\gamma_{1}d\gamma_{2},

(18)

where
||\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}||_{\lambda}=\sqrt{\lambda_{\text{max}}\big((S^{\varphi_{1}}-S^{\varphi_{2}})^{\text{{T}}}(S^{\varphi_{1}}-S^{\varphi_{2}})\big)}
is largest singular value of the matrix
S^{\varphi_{1}}-S^{\varphi_{2}}
(with
\lambda_{\text{max}}(S)
denoting the largest eigenvalue of some matrix

), which is simply a distance measure between the matrices
S^{\varphi_{1}}
and
S^{\varphi_{2}}
. In this way,
||\hat{\varphi}_{\gamma_{1}}-\hat{\varphi}_{\gamma_{2}}||_{\lambda}
formalizes the retrodictive “disagreement” that emerges from the difference between the two postulated priors
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
.
\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{c}}(\varphi)
then is summation over all such disagreement elements, over across all pairs of all possible priors. Through this construction, the measure captures the sensitivity of map’s retrodiction to the choice of priors. We illustrate these notions through a cartoon in Figure

For any given choice of dimension, erasures for the state space of that dimension always share the same values of Bayesian subjectivity:

\displaystyle\forall(\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{c}}(\Psi_{1})=\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{c}}(\Psi_{2})

\displaystyle\forall(\mathcal{W}_{1},\mathcal{W}_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{d}

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{q}}(\mathcal{W}_{1})=\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{q}}(\mathcal{W}_{2})

These are instantiated in the numerics later.

Since Theorem
4
holds, we normalize the measure for each dimensional context: for a given
d
, for all
\varphi,\Psi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{W}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{c}}(\varphi)}{\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{c}}(\Psi)}\mapsto{\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{c}}(\varphi)},\quad\frac{\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{q}}(\mathcal{F})}{\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{q}}(\mathcal{W})}\mapsto{\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{q}}(\mathcal{F})}.

(20)

This gives a clearer yardstick of irreversibility for any
d
-dimension. We also conjecture that, for any choice of
d
, erasures channels maximizes subjectivity. If this holds, then
\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{c}}(\varphi),\,\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{q}}(\mathcal{F})\in[0,1]
always, with bijections and erasures at the lower and upper bounds respectively.

IV.2
Concatenations & Data Processing Inequality

Sound irreversibility measures ought to obey some expression of the data processing inequality:
Markovian concatenations should never decrease a measure of irreversibility
. Now, we have numerically verified that this indeed holds for all cases we have sampled thus far (order of
10^{3}
samples for arbitrary dimensions and channels). Specifically, we have numerically verified that Markovian concatenations both classical (
\psi\,\circ\,\varphi
) and quantum (
\mathcal{G}\circ\mathcal{F}
) obey the following:

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}_{\mathsf{c}}^{\mathsf{s}}(\psi\circ\varphi)\;

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\;\mathrm{I}_{\mathsf{c}}^{\mathsf{s}}(\varphi)

(21)

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}_{\mathsf{q}}^{\mathsf{s}}(\mathcal{G}\circ\mathcal{F})\;

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\;\mathrm{I}_{\mathsf{q}}^{\mathsf{s}}(\mathcal{F})

(22)

Analytical proof for this, however, remains elusive. For instance, it is not possible to prove this by showing that
disagreement elements
never decrease over concatenations. There are tuples of priors and channels that
decrease
disagreement over concatenations. Formally,
\exists(\varphi,\Omega=\psi\circ\varphi,\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}):

||\hat{\Omega}_{\gamma_{1}}-\hat{\Omega}_{\gamma_{2}}||_{\lambda}<||\hat{\varphi}_{\gamma_{1}}-\hat{\varphi}_{\gamma_{2}}||_{\lambda}

(23)

This also occurs in quantum scenarios. That said, we emphasize that while this occurs for some pair of priors (that is,
element-wise
), integration over
all
priors afterward prevents subjectivity as a whole never decreases (that is, on the level of the
measure
). Therefore, Bayesian subjectivity’s adherence to the data processing inequality is an
aggregate
property.

Now, from the composability of Bayesian inverses
[
34
]
,

\displaystyle\forall(\varphi,\Omega=\psi\circ\varphi)

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle\hat{\Omega}_{\gamma}={\hat{\varphi}_{\gamma}}\circ\hat{\psi}_{\varphi[\gamma]}

(24)

\displaystyle\forall(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{G}\circ\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{\gamma}={\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\gamma}}\circ\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathcal{F}[\gamma]}

(25)

alongside Lemmas
3
,
4
and Theorem
4
, we have:

Theorem 5
.

Bijections (or unitaries) always preserve Bayesian subjectivity, and the Bayesian subjectivity of an erasure channel can never be increased.

\displaystyle\forall(\Phi,\varphi)

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{c}}(\Phi\circ\varphi)=\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{c}}(\varphi)

(26)

\displaystyle\forall(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{q}}(\mathcal{U}\circ\mathcal{F})=\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{q}}(\mathcal{F})

(27)

\displaystyle\forall(\Psi,\varphi)

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{c}}(\varphi\circ\Psi)=\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{c}}(\Psi)

(28)

\displaystyle\forall(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{q}}(\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{W})=\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{q}}(\mathcal{W})

(29)

For detailed proofs of these relations, see Appendix
B.2
. From these we get the expected features of reversible process always making no difference to any original irreversibility value, and the irreversibility value of any maximally irreversible process being impossible to increase by the action of any other process.

and
\mathsf{D_{c}}(\varphi)

\mathsf{F_{c}}(\varphi)
is simply the distance of the fixed point of
\varphi

Joint monotonicity with

quasistaticity & equilibrium entropy

In the case of bit channels, the joint monotonicity of
\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{c}}(\varphi)

\mathsf{D_{c}}(\varphi)

\mathsf{F_{c}}(\varphi)

\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{c}}(\varphi)

\mathsf{D_{c}}(\varphi)

Now, since one may see

\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\mathsf{c}}(\varphi)
properly captures two physical intuitions from thermodynamics: (1) the more approximately quasistatic a process is, the less irreversible it is and (2) the less entropy producing a process is, the less irreversible it is as well.

is also met.

We also compare subjectivity to another measure which automatically satisfies the data processing inequality. We introduce this “control” or “foil” irreversibility measure here for both classical and quantum maps, calling it

\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{d}}
. It is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{d}}_{\mathsf{c}}(\varphi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathsf{div_{c}}(p||\gamma)-\mathsf{div_{c}}(\varphi[p]||\varphi[\gamma])\,dp\,d\gamma

(30)

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{d}}_{\mathsf{q}}(\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathsf{div_{q}}(\rho||\gamma)-\mathsf{div_{q}}(\mathcal{F}[\rho]||\mathcal{F}[\gamma])\,d\rho\,d\gamma,

(31)

where
\mathsf{div_{c}}(p||\gamma)
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence between distributions
p
and
\gamma
and
\mathsf{div_{q}}(\rho||\gamma)=\Tr[\rho\log\rho-\rho\log\gamma]
is the Umegaki quantum relative entropy between density operators
\rho
and
\gamma
. The integrand describes irreversible entropy production when the evolution is a linear map (see e.g.
[
45
]
). In the classical case at least,
\gamma
is indeed the Bayesian prior used to define the reverse process, if the entropy is defined as
\ln(P_F/P_R)
; the formal extension to the quantum case is discussed in Ref.
[
46

One can verify that

VI
Conclusions

Having reviewed analytical and numerical results, we draw this study on the irreversibility of classical and quantum maps to a close. Theorems
1
and
2
set the stage for this work’s central research question: can we quantify the irreversibility of a linear transformation in terms of its Bayesian inversion’s dependence on priors? We answered this question with the measure of Bayesian subjectivity
\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}
given by (
18
) and (
19
).

Analytical results in Theorems
3
,
4
and
5
show that the measure adheres, at every dimensionality, to physical intuitions for the extremes of reversibility and irreversibility. We have also discussed numerical evidence that Bayesian subjectivity (1) obeys the data processing equality, (2) detects physically intuitive properties like quasistaticity and entropy production (doing so in both the classical and quantum regimes) and (3) is sensitive to subtler sources of reversibility, such as those that resulted in the ridge-like features in the case of trits, seen vividly in Figure
6a
.

We have also made comparisons between our subjectivity measure
\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}
and the average change in divergence
\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{d}}
, defined in (
30
) and (
31
)—which obeys the data processing inequality by construction. Our results show that subjectivity and this comparator measure are not trivially equivalent, but also feature sufficient similarities (such as the same properties for extreme maps for both the classical and quantum regime, the joint monotonicties for the bit channel case, and the detection of the ridges for the trits) so as to bolster the viability of
\mathrm{I}^{\mathsf{s}}
.

Together, we have seen strong evidence that the subjectivity of a map that is inherent to its retrodiction is a viable irreversibility, for both classically and quantum-theoretically modeled transformations. We emphasize that while Bayesian retrodiction is in general subjective (retrodiction depends on the choice of prior), the
subjectivity
of a map is not itself subjective. As we have defined it, due to the integration over the space of all possible priors, the measure is finally prior-independent: Bayesian subjectivity is a function of the linear map alone. The measure
objectively quantifies the subjectivity
inherent to Bayesian inference on each transformation.

With this, we conclude by making some remarks on avenues for future work. The most obvious extension would be to analytically prove the data processing inequality for Bayesian subjectivity, as in (
21
) and (
22
). If this can be done, it would imply that
decreases in Bayesian subjectivity
over concatenations would constitute a non-Markovianity witness
[
49
,
50
]
. This has a nice interpretation:
objective memory
from “backflow” decreases Bayesian
subjectivity
. Ongoing numerical simulations have been promising in this regard, but the keystone of an analytic proof for the data processing inequality remains difficult to capture. Besides concerns about the inequality, one can also consider investigating what physically insightful quantum-theoretic parameters constitute joint monotonicity to quantum Bayesian subjectivity in the qubit regime and beyond.

Appendix A
Two Geometric Variables of Classical & Quantum Linear Maps

As they become pertinent in our notes on how Bayesian subjectivity, as an irreversibility measure, satisfies intuitions about quasistaticity, information dissipation and entropy production, we explore two relevant geometric parameters that characterize every linear map. The first is the absolute determinant, that captures the preservation of the input state space, denoted by
\mathsf{D_{c}}(\varphi)
and
\mathsf{D_{q}}(\varphi)
. The second is the fixed centroid displacement denoted by
\mathsf{F_{c}}(\varphi)
and
\mathsf{F_{q}}(\mathcal{F})
.

A.1
Preservation of the State Space

As illustrated in Figure
1
, one can see how maps transform the state space that they act on. The relation between irreversibility and state space preservation is well studied according to several natively appropriate quantifiers of changes in state space volume
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
. In the channel-theoretic framework we employ here, the most straightforward measure is the target map’s determinant magnitude
[
56
,
18
]
or the
absolute determinant
of some matrix representing its the contractive action on the input state space.

For classical maps, the absolute determinant
\mathsf{D_{c}}(\varphi)
is easily obtained since the map can be described by its stochastic matrix. For this, see (
10
) in the main text. This quantity gives the
ratio
of the output space to the input space. For quantum maps, we first note that any channel
\mathcal{F}
acting on
d
-dimensional quantum systems corresponds, through affine transformation, to what is sometimes referred to as the channel’s
transfer matrix

[
57
,
58
,
59
,
60
,
19
]
. The transfer matrix of such
\mathcal{F}
has entries:

(S^{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{P}})_{ij}=\frac{1}{d}\text{{Tr}}\!\left[\mathcal{P}_{i}\mathcal{F}[\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\dagger}]\right],\quad i,j\in\{1,2\dots d^{2}\},

(33)

where, for
k<d^{2}
, the
\mathcal{P}_{k}
operators are Hilbert-Schmidt orthogonal
\Tr[\mathcal{P}_{i}\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\dagger}]\propto\delta_{ij}
and form some operator basis that can be used to define the generalized Bloch vector of any quantum state in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. For
k=d^{2}
, we have the corresponding identity operator. As an affine representation, we get the expected
(S^{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{P}})_{d^{2}d^{2}}=1
, while all other
(S^{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{P}})_{d^{2}j}=0
. Likewise, translation of the state space’s centroid is given by the translative component of
S^{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{P}}
, which is the vector
\mathsf{p^{\mathcal{F}}_{q}}
(we drop
\mathcal{P}
from the notation to avoid encumbrance) with entries:

\left(\mathsf{p^{\mathcal{F}}_{q}}\right)_{i}=(S^{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{P}})_{i,d^{2}}=\text{{Tr}}\!\left[\mathcal{P}_{i}\mathcal{F}[\mathbb{1}/d]\right],

(34)

for
i\in\{1,2\dots d^{2}-1\}
.
As an aside, when
d=2^{n}
, a canonical and orthonormalized choice would be the generalized Pauli basis:

\mathcal{P}_{k}=\bigotimes_{s=1}^{n}\sigma_{k_{s}},\quad\forall s:\;k_{s}\in\{1,2,3,4\},

(35)

with
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}
corresponding to the Pauli matrices, and
\sigma_{4}=\mathbb{1}
. Hence, for qubits, this reduces to

(S^{\mathcal{F}}_{\sigma})_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\text{{Tr}}\!\left[\sigma_{i}\mathcal{F}[\sigma_{j}]\right],\quad i,j\in\{1,2\dots 4\},

(36)

and
\mathsf{p^{\mathcal{F}}_{q}}
becomes the qubit channel’s output given a maximally mixed state.

With this, we have the preservation of the state space for quantum channels:

\mathsf{D_{q}}(\mathcal{F})=\frac{|\text{Det}(S^{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{P}})|}{|\text{Det}{S^{\mathbb{1}}_{\mathcal{P}}}|}.

(37)

The denominator is simply a normalization which goes to
1
for orthonormal choices of operator basis
\{\mathcal{P}_{k}\}
. As with
\mathsf{D_{c}}(\varphi)
is, the state space is totally preserved if and only if
\mathsf{D_{q}}(\mathcal{F})=1
and, sends to
\mathsf{D_{q}}(\mathcal{F})=0
if and only if
\mathcal{F}
sends all states in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
to some lower operator basis.

A.1.1
Relation to Relaxation Time & Quasistaticity

The extent to which the state space is preserved by some map can be related to the quench or
relaxation time
of its corresponding physical process. This is the time it takes for a process to send input states to equilibrium. This may be described in terms of how many iterations some map needs to be applied before all input states become constrained into a small state volume (or subspace) containing fixed points. With this, we may state the relaxation time
\mathsf{t}_{\varphi,z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
of some map
\varphi
, defined for some precision
z\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, as:

\mathsf{t}_{\varphi,z}:\;\underset{t}{\arg\!\min}\;\mathsf{D_{c}}(\varphi^{t})\;\leq\;10^{-z}

(38)

This object’s relationship with
\mathsf{D_{c}}(\varphi)
may be determined in the following way. Noting that for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}:\,|x^{y}|=|x|^{y}
and that
\text{Det}(A^{y})=\text{Det}(A)^{y}
, (
38
) goes to

\displaystyle\mathsf{t}_{\varphi,z}:\;\;

\displaystyle\underset{t}{\arg\!\min}

\displaystyle\;\;\mathsf{D_{c}}(\varphi)^{t}\;\leq 10^{-z}

(39)

\displaystyle\underset{t}{\arg\!\min}

\displaystyle\;\;t\log\mathsf{D_{c}}(\varphi)\;\leq-z

(40)

\displaystyle\underset{t}{\min}

\displaystyle\,t\,\geq\,-\frac{z}{\log\mathsf{D_{c}}(\varphi)}

(41)

We are dealing with stochastic maps, so
0\leq\mathsf{D_{c}}(\varphi)\leq 1
. Since
t
is a positive, non-zero integer, this gives us:

\displaystyle\mathsf{t}_{\varphi,z}\,=\,\left\lceil-\frac{z}{\log\mathsf{D_{c}}(\varphi)}\right\rceil_{>0}

(42)

\mathsf{t}_{\varphi,z}
is thus a positive monotone of
\mathsf{D_{c}}(\varphi)
. As
\mathsf{D_{c}}(\varphi)
decreases from
1

0
,
\mathsf{t}_{\varphi,z}
monotonically decreases from infinity (which corresponds to genuine quasistaticity) to its lower bound in one (which corresponds to instantaneous quenching).
\mathsf{D_{c}}(\varphi)\mapsto\mathsf{D_{q}}(\mathcal{F})
gives the same principle, but for quantum channels.

In this way, these objects quantifying information-theoretic spatial preservation can be understood as an expression of how long it takes for a physical process to bring the input states to equilibrium. In thermodynamics, this captures deviations from quasistaticity, resulting in irreversible entropy production.

Fixed Centroid Displacement

While
\mathsf{D_{c}}(\varphi)
and
\mathsf{D_{q}}(\mathcal{F})
capture how rapidly maps send physical systems to equilibrium, we now consider geometric ways of describing the equilibrium itself. It is well-known that any stochastic map
\varphi

\mathcal{F}
) has at least one fixed point
p_{\tau}
(
\rho_{\tau}
)

7

The bounds on the absolute determinant of these maps show that, unless

\varphi

For the quantum case, the fixed centroid for

A.2.1
Relation to Steady State & Purity

Technicalities about the multiplicity of fixed points aside, it is true for the overwhelming majority of physical maps that
\mathsf{F_{c}}(\varphi)
and
\mathsf{F_{q}}(\mathcal{F})
correspond to the distance between the state space’s center and the map’s equilibrium or, more generally, its steady state. Physically, this captures how
purifying
a process is. When
\mathsf{F_{c}}(\varphi)
(or
\mathsf{F_{q}}(\mathcal{F})
) is closer to unity, the more well-defined the steady state is. For a thermodynamic analogy, lower values of
\mathsf{F_{c}}(\varphi)
(or
\mathsf{F_{q}}(\mathcal{F})
) correspond to states for which the target system has increased in entropy, due to interactions with a hot environment for instance.

A.2.2
A Technical Note for Non-Stabilizing Maps

While (
47

\varphi\text{ s.t. }\forall\mathsf{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}:\varphi^{\mathsf{t}}\not\simeq\varphi^{\mathsf{t}+1}

For the purposes for our investigations however, (

\mathsf{p^{\varphi^{t+s}}_{c}}
is the same as the unique eigenstates of those maps
[
53

55

54

E.1

We first define what we mean by absorbing maps.

•

Classical absorbing maps

\displaystyle\text{Rank}(S^{\bar{\varphi}})=n<d\quad\wedge\quad\left(\textstyle{\bigvee_{ij}^{d}}\;S^{\varphi}_{ij}=1\right)\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\varphi\text{ is classically absorbing},

(56)

•

Quantum absorbing maps
\mathcal{A}_{d,n}
are those where states in the span of a basis in a

(i.e. some generalized Bloch hypersphere) become reduced, over an arbitrarily large number of iterations, to a subspace spanned by new basis with cardinality

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{F}}:\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C}^{d})\mapsto\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C}^{n<d})\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\mathcal{F}\text{ is quantum absorbing},

(57)

where
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C}^{x})
is the space of bounded linear operators on the Hilbert space
\mathbb{C}^{x}
.

One can consider Figure
7

n
-dimension spaces are called the

. The exclusion of the original state space and absorbing space is the

of dimension

. In this way, absorbing maps go to at least partial erasure channels after many iterations.

On Quantum Absorbing Maps

Quantum absorbing maps
\mathcal{A}_{d,n}
are maps that send all states from
\mathbb{C}_{d}
to
\mathbb{C}_{1\leq n<d}
. For our investigation, we simply take special note of quantum deterministic absorbers for qubits
\mathcal{A}_{2,1}
, which is defined for any qubit unitary
U_{2}
and transition weight

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{2,1}[\rho]=\sum_{x=0}^{1}K_{x}\rho K_{x}^{\dagger},\quad

\displaystyle K_{0}=U_{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\sqrt{1-s}\end{pmatrix},\quad K_{1}=U_{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt{s}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(63)

For
U_{2}=\mathbb{1}_{2}
,
\mathcal{A}_{2,1}
models a damping or spontaneous emission, sending all states to
\outerproduct{0}{0}
. In any case, we have for generalized deterministic quantum absorbing maps,

\mathsf{F_{q}}(\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{d,1})=\max_{\mathcal{F}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}}\,\mathsf{F_{q}}(\mathcal{F})=1,

(64)

as they send every state to some pure state, which is as far as possible from the maximally mixed state, for that space of states.

Physical Analogy for Absorbing Maps

Absorbing maps can be seen as an information-theoretic expression of chemically catalyzed processes
[
64
,
65
]
(not to be confused with the kind of catalysis defined for quantum resource-theoretic thermodynamics). The absorbing space are all states for which the product has been totally yielded. The transient space is where there still exist reagents.
T_{\circlearrowleft}
contains stochastic transitions, enabled by the catalyst, modeling how the reagents are gradually processed into the product.
T_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\leftarrow}}
contain the transitions for which the catalytic reaction is complete.
\Phi_{n}
, as in (
59
), are any deterministic transitions within the completed space. These correspond to so-called work steps—processes with no thermal component.

Catalysts aid both forward and reversed arrows of a physical process by lowering the activation energy regardless of the directionality. This might be seen as a physical analogue to Corollary
3
.
