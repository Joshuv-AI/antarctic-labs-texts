Reliability of methods in assessing proximal femur bone quality for hip prosthesis selection
Authors: Marcos Del Carmen-Rodríguez, Aleix Agelet-Pueyo, Andreu Triguero-Espinosa, Daniel Rodriguez-Pérez, Jose Luis Agulló-Ferré, Carmen Gómez-Vaquero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAssessment of proximal femoral bone quality is essential for implant selection in total hip arthroplasty (THA). The Dorr classification, Canal-to-Calcar Ratio (CCR), and Cortical Thickness Index (CTI) are commonly used radiographic tools, but their reproducibility and associations with densitometric parameters remain unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the reproducibility of these three methods and their associations with DXA- and 3D-DXA–derived bone mineral density (BMD), to identify the most reliable tool for preoperative bone quality assessment in THA. A prospective cohort of 203 patients with hip osteoarthritis was assessed using standard radiographs, DXA-derived BMD, and 3D-DXA-derived cortical and trabecular bone parameters. Three independent observers assessed radiographs in two sessions separated by one year. Intra- and inter-observer reliability was tested with kappa, ICC, and Bland–Altman analysis. Correlations with BMD were assessed by ANOVA and Spearman correlation analysis. Subgroup analyses were performed according to sex and BMI. The Dorr classification showed only intra- and interobserver fair-to-moderate reproducibility, while CCR and CTI demonstrated intra- and interobserver moderate-to-excellent reliability. CTI was positively associated with total femoral BMD, whereas CCR showed a negative association; the association was stronger for CTI. Total femoral BMD and cortical sBMD differed across Dorr types. No other associations were found between morphometric indices and 3D-DXA-derived cortical or trabecular BMD. CTI showed the highest intra- and inter-observer reproducibility and the strongest association with DXA-derived BMD. Within this dataset, CTI appeared to be the most reproducible radiographic surrogate among the evaluated indices for preoperative bone quality assessment in THA.Keywords: Total hip arthroplasty, Bone quality assessment, Dorr classification, Cortical thickness index, Canal-to-calcar ratio, Inter-observer agreementSubject terms: Anatomy, Diseases, Medical research, Rheumatology

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) remains one of the most effective interventions for managing advanced hip osteoarthritis, proximal femoral fractures, and other degenerative hip conditions. The choice between cemented and uncemented fixation is critical, as it directly affects initial stability, long-term fixation and postoperative outcomes1. This decision depends largely on proximal femoral bone quality of the proximal femoral bone, which plays a pivotal role in implant integration and load transfer2.

Uncemented implants are generally preferred in younger patients with good bone quality3, but their use has extended to older populations4, and they currently predominate in many countries, even among patients > 70 years5. In contrast, cemented fixation has demonstrated favorable long-term outcomes, particularly in individuals with reduced bone quality6.

Several radiographic methods have been proposed to assess proximal femoral bone quality to guide implant selection. Among them, the Dorr classification remains the most widely used for its simplicity and accessibility7(Fig. 1), categorizing femurs into three types (A, B, C) according to cortical thickness and canal shape. However, its subjectivity and limited reproducibility reduce its reliability, especially in type B femurs8–10.

Dorr classification of proximal femoral morphology. Type A indicates thick cortical walls and a narrow intramedullary canal; Type B shows moderate cortical thickness with a wider canal; and Type C is characterized by thin cortices and a wide, stove-pipe–shaped canal.

Quantitative indices such as the Canal-to-Calcar Ratio (CCR) and Cortical Thickness Index (CTI) provide objective measures of canal width and cortical support11,12(Fig. 2). Despite their promise, their correlation with bone quality parameters and clinical utility remain uncertain. Furthermore, their application requires digital radiological software for precise measurements and subsequent calculations, which may make the process more time-consuming and technically demanding.

Measurement of the Canal-to-Calcar Ratio (CCR) and Cortical Thickness Index (CTI) on anteroposterior hip radiographs. CCR is calculated as the ratio of the canal width at the level of the lesser trochanter (B) to the canal width measured 100 mm distal (A), such that CCR = B/A. CTI is determined 100 mm below the lesser trochanter as the difference between the outer cortical diameter (C) and the medullary canal diameter (B), divided by the total outer diameter, expressed as CTI = (C – B)/C.

Advanced techniques such as quantitative computed tomography (QCT) and three-dimensional dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (3D-DXA) allow compartment-specific analysis of cortical and trabecular bone13–18.

While prior studies have evaluated the relationship between Dorr classification and bone mineral density (BMD) or fracture risk19,20, results remain inconsistent. Similarly, the predictive role of CCR and CTI is unclear21–23, and little evidence exists on their association with 3D-DXA-derived cortical and trabecular bone density.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the intra- and inter-observer reproducibility of the Dorr classification, CCR, and CTI, and to examine their correlation with DXA-derived BMD. We also assessed, for the first time, their association with cortical and trabecular BMD obtained from 3D-DXA. Our objective was to determine which radiographic method offered the highest reproducibility and the strongest correlation with densitometric parameters, in order to guide the assessment of proximal femoral bone quality prior to THA.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

A prospective cohort study was conducted, including 203 patients diagnosed with hip osteoarthritis scheduled for primary THA between 2019 and 2022. All participants underwent standard anteroposterior (AP) and lateral hip radiographs, DXA-derived proximal femoral BMD measured with a Horizon Wi densitometer (Hologic Inc., Bedford, MA, USA), and cortical surface BMD (sBMD, mg/cm²) and trabecular volumetric BMD (vBMD, mg/cm³) of the proximal femur were obtained using 3D-Shaper Research software v.2.14 (3D-Shaper Medical, Barcelona, Spain). Patients with prior hip surgery, metabolic bone disorders other than osteoporosis, or incomplete imaging data were excluded. These densitometric parameters were obtained from standard proximal femur regions and did not anatomically correspond to the fixed levels used for the radiographic methods evaluated in this study.

Radiographic assessment

Three independent observers (with four (observer 1), seven (observer 2) and twelve (observer 3) years of experience in orthopaedic surgery, respectively (the third observer was a hip specialist surgeon, reviewed the radiographs and applied three established methods for evaluating proximal femoral bone quality. The methods used to evaluate proximal femur were the Dorr’s classification (types A, B, and C) (Fig. 1), the CCR and the CTI (Fig. 2). All observers received standardized training using fifteen anteroposterior pelvic radiographs prior to initiating the study. Each observer performed the measurements twice, one year apart, to assess intra-observer reproducibility. All measurements were performed on DICOM calibrated in mm using the Pixel Spacing parameter of the PACS system.

Statistical analysis

The reproducibility of the Dorr classification was assessed using Cohen’s kappa (κ) for both intra- and inter-observer agreement24. κ values were interpreted according to Landis and Koch25: values < 0 indicated poor agreement, 0–0.20 slight, 0.21–0.40 fair, 0.41–0.60 moderate, 0.61–0.80 substantial, and > 0.80 almost perfect.

For CCR and CTI, reliability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) with 95% confidence intervals. Intra-observer reliability was evaluated by comparing the first and second measurements of the same observer using average-measure ICCs from a two-way mixed-effects, absolute-agreement model [ICC(3,2)]. Inter-observer reliability across the three observers was evaluated using average-measure ICCs from a two-way mixed-effects, absolute-agreement model [ICC(3,3)]26,27. ICC values were interpreted as: < 0.50 poor, 0.50–0.75 moderate, 0.75–0.90 good, and > 0.90 excellent reliability28.

Pairwise comparisons were additionally explored using Bland–Altman analysis to visualize agreement patterns between observer pairs29. For each comparison, the mean of the two measurements was plotted on the x-axis and their difference on the y-axis. Systematic bias was reported as the mean difference, together with the 95% limits of agreement (LOA). Proportional bias was evaluated by fitting a regression line of the differences against the means and reporting the regression slope (b) and its p-value.

To examine the relationship between femoral morphology and BMD, a final baseline Dorr category was derived from the classifications of the three observers using a majority-rule (modal) approach. When at least two observers assigned the same Dorr type, that category was used as the final classification for analysis. This approach does not imply complete unanimity across observers, as non-unanimous 2:1 classifications were still assigned according to the modal category. Only one of the 203 baseline cases showed complete three-way disagreement (i.e., one observer assigned type A, one type B, and one type C), and because no unique modal category existed, this case was excluded from the Dorr-based BMD analysis. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of data distributions. Group comparisons were performed with one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and post hoc Tukey tests where appropriate. Spearman correlation coefficients were used to evaluate the association between CCR and CTI values and BMD parameters, including total femur BMD and 3D-DXA–derived cortical and trabecular bone densities. To assess the potential influence of sex and BMI on these associations, partial correlation analyses adjusting for sex and BMI were conducted for continuous variables. For categorical comparisons, factorial ANOVA models including Dorr type and sex, as well as complementary models including BMI, were used to evaluate whether these variables modified the relationship between femoral morphology and bone parameters.

All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Hospital Universitari de Bellvitge (21 November 2019, code: PR217/19). All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Results

Inter and intra-observer reproducibility

A total of 203 AP radiographs were analyzed by the observers, including 84 males and 119 women with a mean age of 67.78 ± 8.36 and 70.65 ± 8.36 years, respectively. The mean BMI of the study population was 29.24 ± 4.20 kg/m².

Intra-observer agreement for the Dorr classification ranged from fair to substantial across observers (Table 1). For continuous radiographic indices, CCR showed moderate-to-good reliability, whereas CTI demonstrated moderate-to-excellent reliability, with the highest ICC values overall (Table 1). Bland–Altman analysis showed small mean differences for both CCR and CTI (Table 1), with proportional bias slopes close to zero across observers, indicating no relevant proportional bias. Correlations between proximal femur morphology and bone mineral density

Significant correlations were observed between morphometric indices and total femoral BMD. A wider canal relative to the calcar (higher CCR) was associated with lower total femur BMD (ρ = − 0.248, p < 0.001), whereas greater cortical thickness (higher CTI) correlated positively with total femur BMD (ρ = 0.441, p < 0.001) (Fig. 5).

Scatterplots showing the association of Canal-to-Calcar ratio (CCR) (A) and Cortical Thickness Index (CTI) (B) with DXA-derived total femur BMD. The solid line represents the fitted trend line. Associations were assessed using Spearman correlation coefficients.

No significant correlations were found between CTI or CCR and cortical sBMD or trabecular vBMD. Additional analyses controlling for sex and BMI did not materially alter these findings.

Discussion

Assessment of proximal femoral bone quality remains central to preoperative planning for THA, particularly when deciding between cemented and uncemented fixation. This study evaluated the reproducibility and potential clinical relevance of three radiographic methods—Dorr classification, CCR, CTI—and their correlation with DXA and 3D-DXA-derived BMD, with the aim of identifying the most clinically informative index.

As expected, the Dorr classification showed only fair-to-moderate intra-observer agreement and limited inter-observer reliability, consistent with previous studies9–11,30. Its lower reproducibility may reflect its subjective and categorical nature. In our study, the highest intra-observer agreement was observed in the most experienced observer, although the study was not designed to formally assess the effect of observer experience. Although reproducibility tended to improve with experience, overall agreement remained suboptimal, particularly in type B femurs, supporting the limited reliability of Dorr morphology for consistent clinical decision-making.

In contrast, both quantitative indices, CCR and CTI, demonstrated higher reproducibility, with ICC values indicating moderate-to-good reliability for CCR and good-to-excellent reliability for CTI. The use of standardized radiographic landmarks likely contributed to this improved agreement31, supporting their application in clinical practice10,23.

The relationship between femoral morphology and bone density has been explored via DXA BMD measurements. The Dorr classification has been reported to correlate with total femur BMD, with a graded decline observed across categories consistent with progressive cortical thinning from type A to type C femurs8,10,32.

CTI correlated directly and CCR inversely with total femur BMD, in line with studies indicating that increased cortical thickness is associated with higher bone mass, and that a wider CCR reflects cortical thinning10,23. Several authors have reported a moderate correlation of CTI—but not CCR—with BMD23,31. The strong inverse correlation observed between CCR and CTI in our study reinforces their shared morphometric basis and suggests potential redundancy when used together. However, CTI appears to better represent diaphyseal cortical support, an essential factor for uncemented fixation32. Recent geriatric data further support the clinical relevance of CTI and CCR, showing lower values in fractured than in non-fractured femora, with good-to-excellent inter-observer reliability33. Therefore, CTI appears to be the most robust radiographic surrogate among the evaluated indices in this dataset.

Neither index was associated with cortical sBMD or trabecular vBMD derived from 3D-DXA. This lack of association may partly reflect the anatomical mismatch between both approaches, which do not directly correspond to the same discrete radiographic levels. Similarly, DXA-derived total femur BMD is calculated in a standard proximal femur region different from the anatomical levels used for CCR and CTI measurements. Therefore, these comparisons should be interpreted as indirect associations between radiographic morphology and proximal femoral bone quality, rather than direct anatomical correspondence. Accordingly, DXA and 3D-DXA should be considered densitometric reference measures of proximal femoral bone status in this study, rather than anatomical gold standards for the same radiographic region.

These findings suggest that although CTI may serve as a practical surrogate of overall bone quality, it does not capture compartment-specific changes critical for cementless THA planning22,23, or the microstructural variation revealed by volumetric 3D-DXA. Although 3D-DXA-derived measurements have shown acceptable validation against QCT and short-term precision34,35, the cortical and trabecular outputs provided by 3D-DXA remain model-derived estimates rather than direct compartment-specific measurements, and this should be considered when interpreting the absence of significant correlations in the present study36,37. Greater cortical thickness does not necessarily imply higher cortical density. Prior studies using QCT and 3D-DXA have also shown that radiographic indices often fail to detect early trabecular deterioration and may overlook subtle cortical changes, particularly cortical thinning, that are relevant to implant fixation and fracture risk. These limitations warrant caution when relying solely on radiographic indices, especially in elderly or osteoporotic patients9,10,18,23. This may explain the significant but inconsistent differences in cortical sBMD observed among Dorr types.

Our results align with prior reports identifying Dorr type C femurs as being at higher risk for periprosthetic fracture after uncemented THA34,35. These femurs are typically characterized by cortical thinning, reduced diaphyseal taper, and altered geometry—features also observed in our sample. High CCR and distal Canal Bone Ratio (CBR) values have been associated with fracture risk38,39, while cortical indices such as AP cortical thickness and FRAX major score independently predicted fractures, whereas CCR and CFI did not40. These findings emphasize the importance of cortical measurements over canal-based ratios, which in our study was best represented by CTI.

Future developments in this field may include the use of artificial intelligence (AI) to improve radiographic assessment of proximal femoral bone quality. AI-based tools could help automate the recognition of proximal femoral morphology, reduce inter- and intra-observer variability, and facilitate opportunistic identification of patients with potentially lower bone quality from standard radiographs41. Deep-learning automation of preoperative radiographic femoral parameters may also support risk stratification in THA38 and contribute to preoperative planning workflows42. However, such systems may still be affected by bias, as their performance depends on the quality of the training data, the reference labels used for supervision, and the external validity of the algorithm. Therefore, AI should be viewed as a promising support tool rather than as a substitute for clinical judgment.

Intraoperative physician assessment (IPA) also deserves consideration. It has been shown to correlate with T-scores and FRAX fracture risk, but not with Dorr or radiographic cortical indices43. Although useful in the absence of densitometry, IPA remains a subjective method—similar to the Dorr classification—and should be interpreted with caution. Implant selection should rely not only on patient age or conventional radiographic morphology, but also on individualized assessment of compartmental bone quality, physiological reserve, fracture risk, and clinical judgement44.

The strengths of this study include its prospective design, the relatively large and homogeneous cohort, and the independent evaluation by three observers with different levels of experience. The use of both DXA and 3D-DXA provided a comprehensive assessment of BMD, including cortical and trabecular compartments, which is rarely reported in this context. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. Radiographic analyses were based on two-dimensional AP views, which do not fully capture the three-dimensional morphology of the proximal femur and are susceptible to positioning errors. In addition, the fixed radiographic levels used to calculate the Dorr classification, CCR and CTI did not directly correspond to the compartment-specific regions assessed by the 3D-DXA software, which may have attenuated potential correlations between both approaches. Finally, the study population was restricted to patients with hip osteoarthritis undergoing THA, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. These limitations reflect common challenges in orthopedic imaging research and should be considered when interpreting the results.

Conclusion

These findings support a pragmatic approach to preoperative bone assessment in THA. The Dorr classification showed lower observer agreement and should not be recommended as a standalone tool for routine practice. Both CCR and CTI were associated with DXA-derived BMD, although the association was consistently stronger for CTI. In this dataset, CTI was the most reproducible radiographic measure among the evaluated indices. However, because no clinical endpoints were assessed, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that CTI is definitively the most suitable standalone tool for preoperative bone quality assessment. Finally, cortical and trabecular compartment measures from 3D-DXA should be interpreted with caution, as they capture different structural domains and may not mirror DXA or radiographic indices. Future prospective studies should determine whether a CTI-guided assessment strategy translates into clinically relevant outcomes.
