Prevalence and persistence of high-risk HPV genotypes in unvaccinated asymptomatic women: a six-year study in Peru
Authors: Caddie Laberiano-Fernandez, Juana del Valle-Mendoza, Miguel Angel Aguilar-Luis, Javier Arias-Stella, Denisse Champin, Graciela Risco de Domiguez, Francisco Perona-Fajardo, Fernando Jeancarlos Imán-Izquierdo, Luis J del Valle, Wilmer Silva-Caso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study was to determine the frequency of HPV-hr and describe the distribution by categories (HPV16, HPV18, HPV-hr non16/18) among unvaccinated asymptomatic women in Peru over a 6-year period (2013–2018). In addition, the disease clearance rate is assessed in a percentage of cases.ResultsOf the 6,665 samples, 2,240 (33.6%) tested positive for HPV. Among the positive cases, single-genotype infections were caused by HPV-16 (14.8%), followed by HPV-18 (2.2%) and clustered high-risk HPV (HR-HPV) genotypes (66.7%). Multiple HPV-16 infections were more common than HPV-18 infections. In addition, 22 cases (1%) presented with infection with HPV-16, HPV-18, and HR-HPV genotypes. A subset of 116 samples from patients with 1 to 2 years of follow-up was analyzed further. Clearance rates were high, at 68.8% for HPV-16 infections and 67.5% for HR-HPV infections. However, the elimination rate of multiple infections with HPV-16 and HR-HPV genotypes was slightly lower, at 55.6%.Keywords: Human Papillomavirus viruses, Papillomavirus infections, Uterine cervical neo-plasms, Genotype, Sexually transmitted diseases

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) infection is the most significant risk factor in the pathogenesis of cervical cancer and precancerous cervical lesions. Molecular and epidemiological studies increasingly provide stronger evidence of the association between high-risk genital HPV strains and cervical squamous cell carcinoma specifically [1]. HPV refers to a heterogeneous group of double-stranded DNA viruses. To date, over 170 HPV genotypes have been identified, isolated, and characterized, with approximately 40 types present in the female genital tract. These are considered high-risk because their integration into the host genome is essential for malignant cell transformation during carcinogenesis [2, 3]. Regarding the prevalence of HPV in asymptomatic women, a systematic review encompassing 32 studies and a population of 224,320 women reported a general prevalence of 11%. The review also highlighted that HPV prevalence was 10% in urban areas and 11% in rural areas, suggesting higher infection rates among rural women with limited access to cancer screening and healthcare services. This provides a reliable estimate of the burden of HPV infection among asymptomatic women across developed and developing countries [4]. Research indicates that high-risk HPV genotypes account for approximately 90% of cervical cancer cases, with HPV-16 and HPV-18 contributing to 70% of cases worldwide. 17 HPV genotypes were considered causative of cervical cancer, with ORs ranging widely from 48.3 (95% CI 45.7–50.9) for HPV16 to 1.4 (1.2–1.7) for HPV51 [5, 6, 7].

The distribution of HPV genotypes varies geographically in women with normal cytology results. findings. HPV-16 is the most prevalent and carcinogenic genotype in the world, followed by HPV-18, which is most prevalent in Asia, North America, Latin America, and the Caribbean [8]. In Brazil, the second most frequently isolated genotypes are HPV-31 and HPV-33, while in Africa, HPV-52 is the second most prevalent genotype [8, 9].

The average time from initial infection with a high-risk HPV genotype and the development of cervical cancer is roughly 25 to 30 years [10]. During this time, cervical intraepithelial lesions (CIN) are classified as CIN1, CIN2 and CIN3; in general, the higher the grade, the greater the likelihood of persistence and progression, while CIN1 and a relevant proportion of CIN2 may regress spontaneously, especially in young women [10–12]. Studies carried out in Asia revealed the predominance of HPV-52, HPV-58, and HPV-16 genotypes in patients with intraepithelial lesions [13, 14]. Thus, infection with high-risk genotypes can be present and persist in women with normal cervical cytology, high grade CINs, or cancer. In Peru, cervical cancer is the second leading cause of cancer related death in women [15]. About 20.5% of cervical cells with normal morphology determined by Papanicolaou test were infected by HPV, and up to 20 different genotypes were identified, with HPV-16 the most common [16]. In addition to this, a study carried out in Peru reports that HPV infections with high-risk genotypes can present coinfections with bacterial pathogens related to sexually transmitted diseases such as Ureaplasma urealyticum, Chlamydia trachomatis and Mycoplasma genitalium. Without investigating whether these simultaneous events have any implications in the development of cervical neoplasms or persistence of viral infection [17].

The National Plan for the Prevention and Control of Cervical Cancer in Peru (Ministerial Resolution No. 440–2017/MINSA) recommends HPV testing in women aged 30–49 years, with a 5-year screening interval when the result is negative; in the event of a positive result, referral may be made to colposcopy or Visual Inspection with Acetic Acid (VIA), depending on local availability. For women aged 50–64 years, cytology is recommended every 3 years after two consecutive negative results. In addition, since mid-2021, the Ministry of Health has initiated the regional implementation of HPV testing in several regions, including Lima, with the intention of scaling up nationwide, although without specifying which molecular platform should be adopted [18, 19].

We therefore evaluated the frequency of high-risk HPV genotypes among unvaccinated asymptomatic women in Peru over a 6-year period (2013–2018). In addition, the disease clearance rate is assessed in a percentage of cases.

Materials and methods

Patients and sample

The results of the present study were obtained retrospectively. The database was created from the results obtained in the HPV evaluation performed on unvaccinated asymptomatic women who attended their routine gynecological check-ups.

The samples were collected between January 2013 and December 2018 in a health facility located in Lima. All patients who met the inclusion criteria were selected. The inclusion criteria included asymptomatic women, not vaccinated against HPV, over 18 years of age, who did not present clinical signs of active infection or a history of previous cervical lesions. Those who presented active sexually transmitted infections in the routine examination or who had reported being vaccinated against HPV were excluded. It is recognized that the lack of representation of women from rural areas could limit the generalization of the results at a national level.

Sample analysis

The analyses included 6665 samples. Endocervical specimens were collected using sterile cytobrushes and immediately preserved in PreservCyt (Hologic) (5 mL), or SurePath (Becton Dickinson) (1 mL) viral DNA transport media. All specimens were subjected to the COBAS 4800 HPV Test (Roche Molecular Diagnostics) which was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This test is FDA cleared for the simultaneous detection and genotyping of HPV in cervical specimens. This automated system for the COBAS 4800 test includes a sample preparation robot and a real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analyzer. It identifies HPV-16 and HPV-18 genotypes individually and 12 other high-risk genotypes (HPV-HR) (this groups the genotypes: HPV-31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66 and 68). Samples were stored appropriately at 4 °C according to the manufacturer’s instructions until use within 24 h of collection.

Ethics statement

The study protocol was approved by the Medical and Research Committee of the Arias-Stella Institute of Molecular Pathology and Biology, located in Lima-Peru. For this retrospective study data was utilized from the samples collected through the Department of Pathology. In accordance with the authorization letter number 011218 − 2018. The patients signed an informed consent as part of the clinic care protocol.

Statistical analysis

The cases were counted as frequencies in percentages. Fisher exact test and chi2-test were used to compare frequencies and to analyze the frequencies distribution. ANOVA and Tukey test were used to analyze the age variable. Differences were considered significant when p < 0.05. Statistical analysis was performed with Minitab® 18 statistical software (Minitab Inc., USA) and the graphical representation was prepared with OriginPro 2018 software (OriginLab Corporation, MA, USA).

Results

Of a total of 6,665 samples analyzed, 4,425 (66.4%) were negative for the presence of any HPV type evaluated, while 2,240 (33.6%) were positive for the tested viral genotypes. HPV-16 and HPV-18, had frequencies of 5.0% and 0.7%, respectively, based on the total number of samples analyzed. The 12 pooled high-risk HPV genotypes (HPV-HR)—including HPV-31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, and 68—accounted for 22.4% of all samples. More than one genotype was identified in 386 cases (5.8%). HPV-positive cases were classified as: HPV16 only, HPV18 only, HPV-HR non16/18, HPV16 + HPV18, HPV16 + HPV-HR, HPV18 + HPV-HR, and HPV16/18 + HPV-HR. The distribution of HPV-positive cases is illustrated in Fig. 1. HPV-16 was present in 14.8% of cases, followed by HPV-18 at 2.2%, and the high-risk genotypes accounted for 66.7%. The presence of HPV16 + HPV-HR was 12.8%, and HPV18 + HPV-HR was 2.1%. It is noteworthy that 44.15% of all positive results occurred in patients aged 26 to 35 years, making this age group the most affected across all categories presented (Table 1).

When analyzing the study period, the distribution of genotypes remained consistent with the overall findings (Fig. 2a; Table 2), with no significant year-on-year variations in the rates or types of positive cocategories (Fig. 2b). These results suggest that the frequency of HPV genotypes remained stable in the community throughout the study period. The large sample size allowed for further exploration of the age distribution of women with HPV-positive results. Figure 3 shows that the mean age of women with any HPV infection was 32 years, significantly lower (p < 0.05) than the mean age of HPV-negative women (36.5 years) and of the overall study population (35.2 years). The median age of women with HPV infections was narrowly distributed between 30 and 31 years (Fig. 3).

Finally, 116 women were followed for 1 to 2 years. Patients who had a negative molecular test during this follow-up period were considered to have cleared the infection. On the other hand, the criterion used to determine persistent infection was two or more samples obtained from the same patient during the follow-up period that showed HPV positivity on the COBAS 4800 HPV test. Figure 4 shows the infection clearance rates. During follow-up visits, the patients remained free of gynecological and other complaints. Clearance rates of 68.8% and 67.5% were observed for HPV-16-only and non-16/18 high-risk HPV (HRHPV) positive cases, respectively. For HPV-18-only cases, the clearance rate was 50%. The clearance rate for HPV-16 and non-16/18 HRHPV cases was 55.6%, with clearance of both viral genotypes in 33.4% of cases and partial clearance (clearance of HPV-16 or non-16/18 HRHPV) in 22.2% of cases. Women aged 26–35 years represented the age group with the highest clearance rate, at 26.7%, followed by women aged 18–25 years at 22.0%. The age group most affected by the persistence of the infection was found among women aged 18 to 25 years, with 16.0% of the total follow-ups carried out (Table 3).

Discussion

Our evaluation of the frequency of high-risk HPV infections over a six-year period in women from Lima, Peru, revealed that the circulating high-risk HPV genotypes in this region are highly heterogeneous. Previous studies have shown that the distribution of HPV genotypes can vary with patient age. For example, So et al. [20] found that HPV-16, HPV-52, and HPV-58 were the most prevalent across all age groups, with HPV-16 and other high-risk types being most frequent in patients younger than 30 years. However, several large studies, including the ATHENA trial (which enrolled 40,901 women) [21], a study of 47,617 women in New Mexico [22], and a study by Wolday et al. in Ethiopia [23], identified HPV-16 as the most prevalent genotype across all age groups, followed by other high-risk genotypes and HPV-18. Other research has reported similar trends [24–27].

Large datasets on the prevalence and distribution of HPV genotypes in Peru are still limited. A study of 397 women from northern Peru identified 77 patients infected with high-risk HPV genotypes, of which 21 (5.3%) had HPV-31 infections, 12 (3.0%) had HPV-16, and only 1 patient had HPV-18 [28]. Previous research in a similar population from the same region identified HPV-16 as the most frequent high-risk genotype [15]. Additionally, Sullcahuaman et al. [29] analyzed samples from women in Lima between 2012 and 2014 and found that 7.7% (n = 36) of cases involved HPV-16, 2.6% (n = 12) had HPV-31, and 1.1% (n = 5) were linked to HPV-18 infections. In our study, we also observed a high prevalence of HPV-16 infections, but a substantial proportion of cases were caused by pooled high-risk genotypes, which were not individually characterized. These findings highlight the heterogeneity of circulating high-risk HPV genotypes within the studied population. The variability in genotype distribution emphasizes the need for regional data to better understand HPV dynamics and tailor public health strategies accordingly.

In our clinical series of unvaccinated, asymptomatic women screened for high-risk HPV using partial genotyping, the overall positivity rate was 33.6% (2,240/6,665), with the highest concentration of positive results observed in the 26–35 age group (44.15% of all positives). In contrast, the ICO/IARC HPV Information Centre summarizes the prevalence of HPV DNA in women with normal cervical cytology based on systematic reviews/meta-analyses and shows a different age pattern for Peru, with the lowest value in the 25–34 age group and a subsequent increase with age, reaching its highest level in women ≥ 65 years. Furthermore, the same report shows high heterogeneity among Peruvian studies (prevalences in normal cytology ranging from 3.2% to 36.0% depending on the study and region), suggesting that the differences between our age curve and that of the report can be explained, at least in part, by the urban clinical sample vs. synthesis of studies with normal cytology, the geographical variation of included populations, and differences in methods (our focus on HPV-AR vs. HPV DNA estimates derived from multiple trials) [30].

Persistent HPV infection is a necessary but not sufficient cause of cervical cancer. While most infections are transient, long-term high-risk infections that progress through premalignant stages are more likely to culminate in cancer [31]. Therefore, HPV detection or high-/low-risk differentiation alone may be insufficient; persistence typically requires genotyping and confirmation of the same genotype on repeat testing. Combining HPV genotyping with the Papanicolaou (Pap) test is more effective for identifying persistent infections with abnormal cytology and improves clinical management [32]; because transient infections are especially common in women < 30 years, pairing cytology with viral detection may be more efficient in those > 30 years [33]. Persistence is strongly linked to CIN2/CIN3, key steps toward carcinoma [1, 2, 34]. In young women, most infections clear within 1–2 years [35]; Rosa et al. reported 52–69% clearance within 1 year and 81% within 19 months [3], whereas HPV-16 persistence for 1–2 years markedly increases subsequent risk of CIN3 or worse [23, 36, 37].

Persistence rates vary widely (10–68% at 12 months; 63–97% at 24 months), with HPV-16 most likely to persist and progress [3]. A meta-analysis of 1,790 CIN patients found HPV-16 was the genotype most likely to persist after treatment [38]. In our study, multiple infections (≥ 2 genotypes), particularly those involving HPV-16, were more likely to persist than single-genotype infections; Woong-Cho et al. similarly reported HPV-16 as the most persistent genotype (although HPV-53 was most prevalent), and other studies also implicate HPV-16, 31, 33, and 52 as highly persistent types [39, 1]. Only 5–10% persist in adolescents/young women [2], and several studies report higher persistence in women > 30 years [40, 41], although others report lower persistence at older ages [1, 8]. Given that most cervical cancers are attributed to HPV-16/18, the primary vaccine targets [1, 42], Latin America should prioritize HPV-based primary screening: cytology programs have underperformed due to coverage/quality gaps, and WHO recommends HPV molecular testing as the preferred primary test [42–44].

In this study, specimens were deposited in lower volumes of liquid medium than those commonly used in standard liquid-based cytology workflows, which may have resulted in more concentrated samples and, consequently, enhanced the analytical detection of viral DNA. However, because all specimens were processed in the same laboratory, using the same cobas 4800 platform and under a uniform workflow throughout the study period, this factor is more likely to have influenced the overall positivity rate than the relative distribution of HPV genotypic categories. This interpretation is consistent with studies showing that the type of preservation medium and pre-analytical handling can affect the performance of HPV assays, including analytical sensitivity and the detection of viral DNA in samples processed with SurePath or PreservCyt. Accordingly, our prevalence estimates should be interpreted within the context of this specific collection protocol and should not be assumed to be directly interchangeable with studies conducted under different pre-analytical conditions [45, 46].

In addition to geographic variation, another important factor that may explain differences in HPV prevalence across Peruvian studies is the type of assay used for viral detection. In our study, the cobas 4800 HPV test was used, which individually identifies HPV16 and HPV18 but reports the remaining 12 high-risk genotypes as a pooled category; this partial genotyping design contributed to the high proportion of non-16/18 results and limits direct comparability with studies using extended genotyping platforms. Indeed, assay comparison studies have shown that different HPV tests do not detect exactly the same infections and that relevant discrepancies exist in overall positivity rates and in the detection of specific genotypes when platforms such as cobas, Aptima, HC2, and others are compared. Therefore, part of the variability between our findings and those of other Peruvian series may be attributable to methodological differences rather than exclusively to epidemiological differences between populations. This issue is particularly important for the non-16/18 high-risk HPV category, as pooled reporting may obscure the relative contribution of individual genotypes whose frequency may vary according to the setting. In this regard, future multicenter studies in the country should ideally harmonize the diagnostic platform or use assays with extended genotyping to improve comparability and to define more precisely the epidemiological contribution of each genotype [47–49].

To conclude, although HPV-16 confers the greatest overall risk, HPV-18 confers a significant risk for glandular lesions but less risk for squamous lesions than some other HPV types. Thus, reporting for HPV-16 and HPV-18 separately is probably warranted in the future. In addition, it may be important to carry out additional studies with techniques that allow other high-risk HPV types to be separated, thereby yielding a better picture of the prevalence of HPV subtypes nationwide.

The 2013–2018 data collection period was appropriate because it allowed the evaluation of the frequency of high-risk HPV genotypes over six consecutive years and, in some cases, the assessment of viral clearance after 1 to 2 years of follow-up. This time frame made it possible to identify the annual stability of genotype distribution, as well as the greater persistence observed in multiple infections and among women aged 18 to 25 years. Although the data are not recent, these findings remain relevant because they provide a baseline for comparison with current patterns of genotype circulation, persistence, and viral clearance in more recent studies.

Conclusions

High-risk HPV genotypes were frequently present in the studied population. In single-genotype infections, HPV-16 was more common than HPV-18. Genotypes comprising 12 high-risk HPV types were also frequently present, making it necessary to evaluate them individually using diagnostic platforms to report their true impact. No significant annual variations in genotype distribution were observed, indicating that they are stable in the community. Viral clearance after 1 to 2 years of follow-up was higher for single-genotype infections compared to multiple infections. The age group most affected by viral persistence was women aged 18 to 25, representing 16.0% of all follow-ups.

Limitations

Some limitations of this study are the access to clinical data, final report of cytology and their correlation with the result of HPV test. Although it was not the purpose of this study, it is important to study additional factors that are established co-factors of persistent HPV infection, such as high parity, smoking, long-duration oral contraceptive use, sexual behavior, genetic factors, alcohol consumption, previous Papanicolaou testing, and coinfection with other pathogens such as Herpes or Chlamydia [3, 8, 9]. Despite these co-factors, the reasons for infection persistence are not fully understood [8]. Since cytology/histology was not available, it is not possible to stratify the prevalence by cervical status (normal vs. abnormalities), which limits comparative interpretation with studies based on normal cytology.
