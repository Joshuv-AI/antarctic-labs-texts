Near Optimal Hardness of Approximating k k -CSP
Dor Minzer, Kai Zhe Zheng

Abstract
We show that for every k ∈ ℕ k\in\mathbb{N} and ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 , for large enough alphabet R R , given a k k -CSP with alphabet size R R , it is NP-hard to distinguish between the case that there is an assignment satisfying at least 1 − ε 1-\varepsilon fraction of the constraints, and the case no assignment satisfies more than 1 / R k − 1 − ε 1/R^{k-1-\varepsilon} of the constraints. This result improves upon prior work of [Chan, Journal of the ACM 2016], who showed the same result with weaker soundness of O ⁡ ( k / R k − 2 ) O(k/R^{k-2}) , and nearly matches the trivial approximation algorithm that finds an assignment satisfying at least 1 / R k − 1 1/R^{k-1} fraction of the constraints. Our proof follows the approach of a recent work by the authors, wherein the above result is proved for k = 2 k=2 . Our main new ingredient is a counting lemma for hyperedges between pseudo-random sets in the Grassmann graphs, which may be of independent interest.

Introduction

The PCP theorem
[
FGL+96
,
AS98
,
ALM+98
]
is a cornerstone result in the theory of computation
with applications and connections to hardness of approximation, cryptography, pseudorandomness, analysis and more. It has many equivalent formulations, and for this paper it will be convenient to state it in the language of constraints satisfaction problems (CSPs in short).

Definition 1.1
.

For an integer
k
, an instance
\Psi
of
k
-CSP consists of a
k
-uniform hypergraph
G=(V,E)
, an alphabet
\Sigma
, and a set of constraints
\{\Phi_{e}\}_{e\in E}
. Each constraint is a map
\Phi_{e}:\Sigma^{k}\to\{0,1\}
, where the set of tuples
\Phi_{e}^{-1}(1)

is thought of as assignments to the vertices of
e
that satisfy the constraints on
e
.

The arity parameter
k
and the alphabet size parameter
|\Sigma|
will be important for
the purposes of this paper. Given a
k
-CSP instance
\Psi
we define its value as

\val(\Psi)=\max_{A:V\to\Sigma}\frac{|\{e=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\;|\;\Phi_{e}(A(v_{1}),\ldots,A(v_{k}))=1\}|}{|E|}.

Using the language of CSPs, the basic PCP theorem
[
FGL+96
,
AS98
,
ALM+98
]
combined with
Raz’s parallel repetition theorem
[
Raz98
]

gives the following result:

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists
\gamma>0
such that for sufficiently large
R
, given an instance
\Psi
of
2
-CSP with alphabet size
R
, it is NP-hard to distinguish the following two cases:

•

YES case:
{\sf val}(\Psi)=1
.

•

NO case:
{\sf val}(\Psi)\leqslant\frac{1}{R^{\gamma}}
.

Let
k>2
. Since any
2
-CSP can be phrased as a

k
-CSP, it is clear that the above hardness result
applies to
k
-CSPs as well. However, one expects a
stronger hardness result to hold. Indeed, if
\Psi
is a
2
-CSP, then taking all subsets of
k/2
constraints from
\Psi
and for each one putting the
k
-ary constraint
corresponding to their “and” yields a
k
-CSP whose value is
\val(\Psi)^{k/2}
. Thus,
1.2
implies:

Theorem 1.3
.

There exists
\gamma>0
such that for sufficiently large
R
, for any even integer
k
, given an instance
\Psi
of
k
-CSP with alphabet size
R
, it is NP-hard to distinguish the following two cases:

•

YES case:
{\sf val}(\Psi)=1
.

•

NO case:
{\sf val}(\Psi)\leqslant\frac{1}{R^{\gamma(k-1)}}
.

For many hardness of approximation applications, one only needs the soundness to be a vanishing function of the alphabet size, in which case results such as
1.2
and
1.3
are sufficient. For certain cases, however, one additionally requires the soundness to be small
relative to
the alphabet size
[
Lae14
,
KS13
,
LM24
]
.
Optimizing the alphabet-soundness tradeoff for
k
-query PCPs is the focus of this paper and quantitatively, one can think of improving this tradeoff as increasing the value of
\gamma
in
1.3
. For this task, one should think of
k
as a fixed constant, and the goal is to obtain larger, concrete values of
\gamma
for
1.3
with this arity
k
.

It is clear that the best we can hope in
1.3
is
\gamma=1-o(1)
. Indeed,
any

k
-CSP of alphabet size
R
has value at least
\frac{1}{R^{k-1}}
, as obtained by a randomly chosen assignment. On the other hand,
1.3
only achieves a small but absolute value
\gamma>0
, leaving a large gap between its assertion and what is potentially possible. Strictly speaking, this implicit value of
\gamma
is still the best known
result to date.

If one is willing to relax the completeness parameter slightly, namely require the value in the YES case to only be close to
1
(as opposed to exactly
1
), then significantly better values for
\gamma
in
1.2
are known. Khot and Safra
[
KS13
]
showed that one may take

\gamma=1/6-o(1)
, and this was subsequently improved by Chan
[
Cha16
]
, who showed that one may take

\gamma=1/2-o(1)
. This result was further improved recently in
[
MZ24
]
, who achieved the near optimal
\gamma=1-o(1)
.

In the same fashion, for
k>2
stronger forms of
1.3
are known to hold, again with imperfect completeness. In this regime, Chan
[
Cha16
]
obtains soundness on the orders of
\frac{k}{R^{k-2}}
if
3\leqslant k<R
(which is the most relevant regime for us, as we think of
k
as a small constant and
R
as being a large constant) and
\frac{k}{R^{k-1}}
for
R\leqslant k
. In terms of
\gamma
, this roughly translates to
\gamma=\frac{k-2}{k-1}
, which approaches
1
for sufficiently large
k
, but is still bounded away from the potentially best possible when
k
is thought of as constant. This gap is even more pronounced for small constant values of
k
such as
3
or
4
. In terms of the best approximation ratio possible for
k
-CSP, it is known that approximating the value of
k
-CSP within factor
O_{k}(\log R/R^{k-1})
is NP-hard
[
MNT22
,
KS15
]
. The downside of this result is that, when stated as a gap problem, the completeness parameter (the value in the YES case) is small and is a vanishing function of the alphabet size
R
.
Our main result is a hardness result that achieves a slightly weaker approximation ratio than the result of
[
MNT22
,
KS15
]
, but has completeness close to
1
.

Theorem 1.4
.

For all
\varepsilon,\delta>0
and arity
k
, and sufficiently large alphabet size
R
, it is NP-hard to distinguish between the following two cases given a
k
-CSP
\Psi
with alphabet size
R
:

•

YES case:
\val(\Psi)\geqslant 1-\delta
,

•

NO case:
\val(\Psi)\leqslant\frac{1}{R^{(1-\varepsilon)(k-1)}}
.

The fact that the value in the YES case is close to
1
and that the alphabet-soundness tradeoff is nearly optimal leads to improved hardness results for
k
-Dimensional Matching and related problems.

Application to
k
-Dimensional Matching

By plugging our improved

-CSP hardness result into the reduction from

-Dimensional matching

.

A

For any constant

As a corollary, we also get hardness of approximation for a number of other problems. We refer the reader to
[

.

For every constant
\varepsilon>0
, there is a sufficiently large

-approximation algorithm for any of the problems

-Set Packing,

-Matchoid,

The prior best hardness of approximation factor for all of these problems was

, also due to

1.1
PCP Construction Overview

Our reduction from an NP-hard problem to a CSP in
1.4
closely follows the reduction of
[
MZ24
]
. For technical purposes, it is convenient to set the arity of the CSP to
k+1
, rather than
k
. Furthermore,
it will be helpful to view our reduction to
(k+1)
-CSP as a
(k+1)
-query PCP construction, and we use the language of PCPs henceforth. The completeness of the PCP becomes the value on the YES case of
1.4
and the soundness becomes the value in the NO case of
1.4
.

Our
(k+1)
-query PCP is constructed by composing an outer PCP and an inner PCP.
The outer PCP is exactly the same as that of
[
KMS17
,
DKK+18
,
DKK+21
,
KMS23
,
MZ24
]
. We give a brief overview of this construction below and refer the reader to
[
MZ24
]
for a
more detailed discussion.
Our inner PCP is based on a higher query generalization of Grassmann consistency test of
[
MZ24
]
, and our test uses
(k+1)
-queries rather than
2
(and in return gains a power
k
improvement in the exponent soundness). The design and analysis of this test is the main new ingredient in our construction. Once we have the outer PCP and the inner we compose them in similar way to
[
MZ24
]
.

1.1.1
Outer PCP

The outer PCP is best described using the following
2
-Prover-
1
-Round Game (see
[
MZ24
]
for a discussion about
2
-Prover-
1
-Round Game and their equivalence to
2
-CSPs). It is based on the
{\sf Gap3Lin}
problem. An instance of this problem is a system of linear equations
{\sf Eq}
over a set of variables
X
, each equation depending on
3
-variables in
X
, and the goal is to assign the variables values from
\mathbb{F}_{2}
so as to satisfy as many of the equations as possible. By Håstad’s result
[
Hås01
]
, for all
\eta>0
it is NP-hard to distinguish between the case there is an assignment satisfying
(1-\eta)
-fraction of the constraints, and the case every assignment satisfies at most
(1/2+\eta)
-fraction of the equations.

Given a
{\sf Gap3Lin}
instance
(X,{\sf Eq})
, in our outer PCP the verifier first chooses
J
equations,
e_{1},\ldots,e_{J}\in{\sf Eq}
uniformly at random. The verifier then sends all
3J
-variables involved in these equations to the first prover. As per the second prover, for each equation
e_{i}
independently, the verifier picks all three of its variables with probability
1-\beta
, and otherwise picks one of them randomly. The verifier sends this collection of variables to the second prover.
The two provers are expected to give consistent assignments to all variables they are sent, and furthermore the assignment of the first prover has to satisfy the equations
e_{1},\ldots,e_{J}
.
This game is known as the smooth parallel repetition of the variables-versus-equation game, and in
[
KMS17
,
MZ24
]
it
was shown to have near perfect completeness in the YES case, and
soundness
2^{-\Theta(\beta J)}
in
the NO case. We refer the reader to
[
MZ24
, Section 1.2.4]
for more details.
1
1

1

Strictly speaking, to be able to compose the outer PCP with an inner PCP we also require a feature known as “advice”. The advice for the second prover can be thought of as a list of uniformly random vectors in the space
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{V}
, where
V
is the set of variables sent to the second prover. Letting
U
be the set of variables sent to the first prover, the advice for the first prover can be thought of as a list of vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{U}
, which are the result of filling
0
’s in coordinates of
U\setminus V
of the second prover’s advice vectors.

1.1.2
Inner PCP

Our inner PCP is based on a local test, called the
(k+1)
-query Grassmann consistency test.
It broadly falls in the category of “subspace versus subspace tests”, which are ubiquitous in PCP constructions, going back all the way to the original proof of the PCP theorem. These tests include the Raz-Safra Plane versus Point test
[
RS97
]
and its variants in other dimensions
[
AS98
,
MR10
,
KS13
,
BDN17
,
MZ23
,
HKSS24
]
. Despite all of these prior works, obtaining
1.4
requires us to consider a new setting wherein the number of queries made is greater than
2
. Furthermore, due to the tight alphabet-soundness tradeoff we are hoping for, we must make these extra queries in a non-trivial manner. At a high level, each extra query needs to nearly beget the power of a fully independent
2
-query test.

The setting of the Grassmann consistency is as follows.
Think of
n
as large and let
{\sf Grass}(n,r)
denote the set of subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
of dimension
r
. Suppose that a linear function
f:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
is encoded via tables
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
as follows:
T_{1}
assigns to each
L\in{\sf Grass}(n,2\ell)
the linear function
T_{1}[L]=f|_{L}:L\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
, and
T_{2}
assigns to each
R\in{\sf Grass}(n,2(1-\delta)\ell)
the linear function
T_{2}[R]=f|_{R}:R\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Given two tables
T_{1},T_{2}
which assign linear functions to subspaces (not necessarily as above), how can one test if they truly come from a global linear function
f
as above? An obvious
2
-query tester proceeds as follows:

•

Choose
R
of dimension
2(1-\delta)\ell
uniformly and
L\supseteq R
of dimension
2\ell
uniformly,

•

Accept if and only if
T_{1}[L]|_{R}\equiv T_{2}[R]
.

It is clear that the test has completeness
1
: if the two tables truly come from the same global function, then the test always accepts. As for the soundness, it is shown in
[
MZ24
]
to be roughly
2^{-2(1-1000\delta)\ell}
, albeit under a non-traditional sense of soundness. Roughly
speaking, their result
states that there are subspaces
Q,W
of constant dimension and codimension respectively, such that
T_{1},T_{2}
agree with a global linear function on non-trivial fraction of subspaces
L
such that
Q\subseteq L\subseteq W
.
2
2

2

It can be shown that the more standard notion of soundness fails, making this non-standard version necessary. See
[
MZ24
]
.

Since the tables have alphabet size
2^{2\ell}
(coming from the number of linear functions over a
2\ell
dimension subspace over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
), these soundness and alphabet size parameters match the nearly optimal tradeoff of
1.4
in the
2
-query case.

Getting a
(k+1)
-query test with near optimal soundness requires some more thought. Using the analysis of
[
MZ24
]
in a black-box way seems insufficient, as it only allows one to repeat the above tester
(k+1)/2
times, and thus amplifies the soundness to just
2^{-2(1-1000\delta)\ell\cdot(k+1)/2}
. This is quadratically off from the soundness in
1.4
. Therefore, to achieve the desired soundness for
1.4
, we need to make the queries in a more dependent manner while not compromising the soundness. Specifically, we are hoping to match the soundness of
k
independent trials of the
2
-query test while making only half the number of queries. We achieve this via the following test:

•

Choose
R\in{\sf Grass}(n,2(1-\delta)\ell)
uniformly and
L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}\in{\sf Grass}(n,2\ell)
such that
R\subseteq L_{i}
for all
i\in[k]
uniformly and independently.

•

Accept if and only if
T_{1}[L_{i}]|_{R}\equiv T_{2}[R]
for all
i\in[k]
.

At a high level, one can think of the test as performing
k
repetitions of the
2
-query test, but reusing the subspace
R
across each trial. By reusing
R
, we are able to save on the number of queries, and furthermore, we show that this change does not sacrifice soundness. Specifically we show that if the above test has soundness
2^{-2(1-1000\delta)\cdot k\ell}
, under the same non-traditional sense of soundness from
[
MZ24
]
. Our analysis involves Fourier analysis and makes use of recent global hypercontractivity results over the bilinear scheme
[
EKL24
]
.

1.1.3
Our Main Analytical Statement

At the heart of our analysis lies an upper bound on the number of hyperedges
between two
pseudo-random sets
in
Grassmann graphs, which may be of independent interest.

Definition 1.8
.

We say that a set
S\subseteq{\sf Grass}(n,\ell)
is
(r,\varepsilon)
-pseudo-random if for all subspaces
Q\subseteq W\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
satisfying
\dim(Q)+\codim(W)=r
, we have
\Pr_{L\in{\sf Grass}(n,\ell)}[L\in S~|~Q\subseteq L\subseteq W]\leqslant\varepsilon
.

With this definition we have the following result:

Lemma 1.9

(Informal version of
Lemma

4.1
)
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{L}\subseteq{\sf Grass}(n,2\ell)
is
(r,\varepsilon)
-pseudo-random
and
\mathcal{R}\subseteq{\sf Grass}(n,2(1-\delta)\ell)
is a set with density
\beta
. Then

\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in{\sf Grass}(n,2(1-\delta)\ell)\\
{\sf Grass}(n,2\ell)\ni L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}\supseteq R\end{subarray}}[R\in\mathcal{R},L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}\in\mathcal{L}]\leqslant 2^{O_{r,k}(1)}\beta^{1-o(1)}\varepsilon^{k(1-o(1))}+2^{-r\delta\ell+1}.

In other words, taking
r
large so that the
2^{-r\delta\ell+1}
term is negligible, the lemma says that the probability that
R
and
L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}
sampled as above are in
\mathcal{R}
and

\mathcal{L}
respectively is not
much more than in a random
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R}
of densities

\varepsilon,\beta
, which would be
\beta\varepsilon^{k}
.
We remark that there is some analogy between the above lemma and
[
MNT22
, Lemma 3.1]
, which makes a similar in spirit assertion for the noisy hypercube. Their
result however requires a large noise rate, which is ultimately the reason their result has small completeness.

Preliminaries

2.1
The Grassmann Domain

A key component of our result is Fourier analysis over graphs related to the Grassmann domains, which we overview here. Throughout this section, we fix parameters
n
and
\ell
with
1\ll\ell\ll n
.

2.1.1
Basic Definitions

We use
{\sf Grass}(n,\ell)
to denote the set of
\ell
-dimensional subspaces
L\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. At times we will have a different vector space
V
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
used as the ambient space, in which case
{\sf Grass}(V,\ell)
denotes the set of
\ell
-dimensional subspaces of
V
.
The number of
\ell
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is denoted by the Gaussian binomial coefficient
\begin{bmatrix}{n}\\
{\ell}\end{bmatrix}_{2}=\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\frac{2^{n}-2^{i}}{2^{\ell}-2^{i}}
. Abusing notation, we denote
\begin{bmatrix}{V}\\
{\ell}\end{bmatrix}_{2}={\sf Grass}(V,\ell)
.

Bipartite Inclusion Graphs.

We will often consider the bipartite inclusion graph between
{\sf Grass}(n,\ell)
and
{\sf Grass}(n,\ell^{\prime})
for
\ell^{\prime}<\ell
. This graph has an edge between every
L\subseteq{\sf Grass}(n,\ell)
and
L^{\prime}\in{\sf Grass}(n,\ell^{\prime})
such that
L\supseteq L^{\prime}
. We refer to the family of such graphs as
Grassmann graphs
. The edges of these are the motivation for the test used in our inner PCP. While we never refer to this graph explicitly, it will be helpful to have it in mind — particularly during
Section

4
.

Zoom-ins and Zoom-outs.

A feature of the Grassmann domain is that they contain many copies of lower dimensional Grassmann sub-domains. We refer to these sub-domains as zoom-ins and zoom-outs, and they play a large part in the analysis of our inner PCP and final PCP. For subspaces
Q\subseteq W\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, let

{\sf Zoom}[Q,W]=\{L\in{\sf Grass}(n,\ell)\;|\;Q\subseteq L\subseteq W\}.

We refer to
Q
as a zoom-in and
W
as a zoom-out. When
W=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
,
{\sf Zoom}[Q,W]
is the zoom-in on
Q
, and when
Q=\{0\}
,
{\sf Zoom}[Q,W]
is the zoom-out on
W
.

2.1.2
Pseudo-randomness over the Grassmann graph

The notion
(r,\varepsilon)
-pseudo-randomness, which captures how much the measure of a set
\mathcal{L}
can increase by a zoom-in/zoom-out restrictions of “size
r
”, will be important for us. Denote
\mu(\mathcal{L})=\Pr_{L\in{\sf Grass}(n,\ell)}[L\in\mathcal{L}]
, and for subspaces
Q\subseteq W\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, denote

\mu_{Q,W}(\mathcal{L})=\Pr_{L\in{\sf Zoom}[Q,W]}[L\in\mathcal{L}]
.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that a set
L\subseteq{\sf Grass}(n,\ell)
is
(r,\varepsilon)
-pseudo-random if for all
Q\subseteq W\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
satisfying
\dim(Q)+\codim(W)=r
, we have
\mu_{Q,W}(\mathcal{L})\leqslant\varepsilon
.

2.2
The Bilinear Scheme

The bilinear scheme is a domain closely related to the Grassmann domain: instead of considering
\ell
-dimensional subspaces, one considers
n\times\ell
matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. To move from the Grassmann domain to the Bilinear scheme we will associate a matrix
M
with its column span.
Thus, the matrices of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times\ell}
roughly correspond to the subspaces in
{\sf Grass}(n,\ell)
and likewise the matrices of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times\ell^{\prime}}
and subspaces of
{\sf Grass}(n,\ell^{\prime})
. This will be important for us later when we define the analogs of the Grassmann inclusion graphs in the Bilinear scheme.
The main advantage of the bilinear scheme is that it is a Cayley graph and therefore Fourier analysis is more easily applicable, and in the rest of
this section we give some background on it.

The Bilinear Scheme:

We denote by
L_{2}\left(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2\ell}\right)
the space of complex valued functions
F:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2\ell}\to\mathbb{C}
equipped with the inner product
\langle F,G\rangle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{M\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2\ell}}[F(M)\overline{G(M)}]
,
where the distribution taken over
M
is uniform.
For
s\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
define
\chi_{s}\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\{-1,1\}
by
\chi_{s}(x)=(-1)^{s\cdot x}
. Then, an orthonormal basis of
L_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2\ell})
is given by the characters,
\chi_{S}:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2\ell}\xrightarrow{}\{-1,1\}
over all
S=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{2\ell})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2\ell}
, where

\chi_{S}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2\ell})=\prod_{i=1}^{2\ell}\chi_{s_{i}}(x_{i})=(-1)^{\sum_{i=1}^{2\ell}s_{i}\cdot x_{i}}.

Thus, any
F\in L_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2\ell})
can be expressed as
F=\sum_{S\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2\ell}}\widehat{F}(S)\chi_{S}
,
where
\widehat{F}(S)=\langle F,\chi_{S}\rangle
. The level
d
component of
F
is given by

F^{=d}=\sum_{S:\;\rank(S)=d}\widehat{F}(S)\chi_{S}.

(1)

If a function
F
only consists of components up to level
d
, i.e.
\widehat{F}(S)=0
for all
\rank(S)>d
, then we say
F
is of degree
d
.

We now describe the analogues of zoom-ins and zoom-outs on the bilinear scheme over
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2\ell}
, as well as an analogous notion of
(r,\varepsilon)
-pseudo-randomness for Boolean functions over
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2\ell}
.

Definition 2.2
.

A zoom-in of dimension
d
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2\ell}
is given by
d
-pairs of vectors
(u_{1},v_{1}),\ldots,(u_{r},v_{r})
where each

and each

. Let

and

denote the matrices whose

th columns are

and
v_{i}
respectively. Then the zoom-in on
(U,V)
is the set of
M\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2\ell}
such that
MU=V
, or equivalently,
Mu_{i}=v_{i}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant d
.

Definition 2.3
.

A zoom-out of dimension
d
is defined similarly, except by multiplication on the left. Given
X\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d\times n}
and
Y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d\times 2\ell}
, whose rows are given by

and
y_{i}
respectively, the zoom-out

is the set of
M\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2\ell}
such that

, or equivalently,

for

.

Let
{\sf Zoom}[(U,V),(X,Y)]
denote the intersections of the zoom-in on
(U,V)
and the zoom-out on
(X,Y)
. The codimension of
{\sf Zoom}[(U,V),(X,Y)]
is the sum of the number of columns of
U
and the number of rows of
X
, which we will denote by
\dim(U)
and
\codim(X)
. For a zoom-in and zoom-out pair and a Boolean function

, we define

to be the restriction of

which is given as

F_{(U,V),(X,Y)}(M)=F(M)\quad\text{for}\quad M\in{\sf Zoom}[(U,V),(X,Y)].

When
\dim(U)+\codim(X)=d
, we say that the restriction is of size
d
. We define
(d,\varepsilon)
-pseudo-randomness in terms of the

-norms of restrictions of
F
of size
d
.
Here and throughout, when we consider
restricted functions, the underlying
measure is the uniform measure
over the corresponding zoom-in and zoom-out
set
{\sf Zoom}[(U,V),(X,Y)]
.

Definition 2.4
.

We say that an indicator function

is
(d,\varepsilon)
-pseudo-random
if for all zoom-in zoom-out combinations
{\sf Zoom}[(U,V),(X,Y)]
such that
\dim(U)+\codim(X)=d
, we have

\left\lVert F_{(U,V),(X,Y)}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon.

We note that for Boolean functions

,

\left\lVert F_{(U,V),(X,Y)}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{M\in{\sf Zoom}[(U,V),(X,Y)]}[F(M)]

definition above generalizes the definition we have for Boolean

3.1
Hardness of 3LIN

As a starting point, we use a classical result of Håstad on the hardness of approximately solving systems of linear equations. To state this result, let us start by formally defining the problem
{\sf 3Lin}

Definition 3.1
.

An instance of
{\sf 3Lin}

{\sf Eq}
over

Each equation in

The goal in

{\sf Eq}

For

of

promised to either have value at least

and the goal is to distinguish between these two cases.

The conditions that two equations in

{\sf Eq}
share at most a single variable and the bound on the number of occurrences of each variable are often not included

{\sf 3Lin}

However, combining the classical result of Håstad
[
Hås01
]
with elementary reductions (see
[
Min22
,
MZ24
]

Theorem 3.1

There exists

{\sf NP}
-hard.

Viewing the advice as a subspace.

3.3.3
The Constraints

To define the constraints, we first need the following relation on the vertices in

(L\oplus H_{U})\sim(L^{\prime}\oplus H_{U^{\prime}})

Lemma 3.3
.

The relation

Proof.

See
[
MZ24

.
∎

One feature of the cliques

Lemma 3.4

Suppose

In words,

f^{\prime}

Proof.

Note that there is only one way to extend

to be

.
∎

.

Suppose that tables

We use an argument of

. Choose a linear function

,

(2)

Now we let

and
S_{R,f}

&\leqslant 2^{O_{r^{\star},t,k}(1)}\beta^{(t-1)/t}\varepsilon^{\prime(kt-2)/t}+2^{-r^{\star}\delta\ell+2}\\

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{O_{r^{\star},t,k}(1)}2^{2k\delta\ell}\beta^{-1/t}\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime(kt-2)/t}}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=2^{O_{r^{\star},t,k}(1)}2^{2k\delta\ell}\cdot\beta^{-1/t}\cdot\varepsilon^{\prime-2/t}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime k}}{\varepsilon}

and

4.2
Proving

In this section we prove

4.1
. To do so, we will express the probability of interest there in terms of an inner product of functions over the bilinear scheme, and then bound it using Fourier analysis. At a high level, we do the following steps:

1.

Define Boolean functions
F:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2\ell}\to\{0,1\}
and
G:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2(1-\delta)\ell}\to\{0,1\}
which act as indicator functions for
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
respectively.

2.

Write the probability from
Lemma

4.1
as an expression involving
F
and
G
.

3.

Show that
\mathcal{L}
being pseudo-random implies that
F
is pseudo-random.

4.

Bound the expression from step 2 using global hypercontractivity.

Fix
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
as in
Lemma

4.1
and towards the first step define the functions
F:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2\ell}\to\{0,1\}
and
G:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2(1-\delta)\ell}\to\{0,1\}
as:

F(M^{\prime})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\im(M^{\prime})\in\mathcal{L},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad\qquad G(M)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\im(M)\in\mathcal{R},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In the above,
\im(\cdot)
is the usual definition of matrix image and refers to the span of the columns of the matrix. Implicit in the definitions is the fact that
F
evaluates to
0
if the columns of
M^{\prime}
are not linearly independent, as in this case their span is not even a dimension
2\ell
subspace, and likewise
G
evaluates to
0
if the columns of
M
are not linearly independent. We also note that
F
and
G
both satisfy a property called
basis invariance
, which is needed to apply results from
[
MZ24
]
. A function
H\in L_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2\ell})
is called basis invariant if
H(M)=H(MA)
for any full rank
A\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2\ell\times 2\ell}
.

To write down the expression for the probability from
Lemma

4.1
, we use an adjacency operator
\mathcal{T}:L_{2}\left(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2\ell}\right)\xrightarrow{}L_{2}\left(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2(1-\delta)\ell}\right)
, which corresponds to a bipartite graph between
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2\ell}
to
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2(1-\delta)\ell}
. This graph morally corresponds to the bipartite graph between
2\ell
-dimensional subspaces and
2(1-\delta)\ell
-dimensional subspaces whose edges are
(L,R)
satisfying
L\supseteq R
. Formally, for any
H\in L_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2\ell})
, the function

\mathcal{T}H\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2(1-\delta)\ell}\to\mathbb{C}
is given by

\mathcal{T}F(M)=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{v_{1},\ldots,v_{2\delta\ell}}[F\left([M,v_{1},\ldots,v_{2\delta\ell}]\right)].

Here,
[M,v_{1},\ldots,v_{2\delta\ell}]
refers to the matrix obtained by adding the columns
v_{i}
to
M
on the right, for all
i\in[2\delta\ell]
. In words, the operator
\mathcal{T}
averages over extensions of the matrix

M
to an
n\times 2\ell
matrix by adding to it
2\delta\ell
random columns. To see how the induced edges of
\mathcal{T}
relate to subspaces
R\subseteq L
, observe that for
M\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2(1-\delta)\ell}
and
M^{\prime}=[M,v_{1},\ldots,v_{2\delta\ell}]
, we indeed have
\im(M)\subseteq\im(M^{\prime})
. Using the adjacency operator
\mathcal{T}
, we can now relate the probability from
Lemma

4.1
to an inner product of functions over the bilinear scheme.

Lemma 4.4
.

For
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R},F,G
as above we have
\Pr_{R;L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}\supseteq R}[R\in\mathcal{R},L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}\in\mathcal{L}]\leqslant 2\langle{(\mathcal{T}F)^{k}},{G}\rangle
.

Proof.

For simplicity, we let
p_{k}
denote the probability on the left hand side in the lemma statement. We expand the inner product on the right hand side:

\displaystyle\langle{(\mathcal{T}F)^{k}},{G}\rangle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{M}\left[G(M)\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{M^{\prime}}[F(M^{\prime})]\right)^{k}\right]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{M,M^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,M^{\prime}_{k}}[G(M)\cdot F(M^{\prime}_{1})\cdots F(M^{\prime}_{k})],

where in the above expectations
M
is uniformly random in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2(1-\delta)\ell}
and
M^{\prime}
as well as each
M^{\prime}_{i}
are uniformly random in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2\ell}
conditioned on the first
2(1-\delta)\ell
columns equaling
M
. Now let
{\sf A}
be the event each one of
M,M^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,M^{\prime}_{k}
in the last expectation has linearly independent columns. Note that conditioned on
{\sf A}
,
\im(M)
is a uniformly random subspace in
{\sf Grass}(n,2(1-\delta)\ell)
, and each
\im(M^{\prime}_{i})
is a uniformly random subspace in
{\sf Grass}(n,2\ell)
containing
\im(M)
. Unpacking the definitions of
G
and
F
, we get that,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{M,M^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,M^{\prime}_{k}}[G(M)\cdot F(M^{\prime}_{1})\cdots F(M^{\prime}_{k})\;|\;{\sf A}]=p_{k},

Using the fact that the quantity in the expectation always has magnitude at most
1
, we get

\langle{(\mathcal{T}F)^{k}},{G}\rangle\geqslant\frac{1}{1-\Pr[{\sf A}]}\cdot p_{k}.

To conclude, we use the following simple bound on
\Pr[{\sf A}]
:

\Pr[{\sf A}]\leqslant(2\ell+2\delta k\ell)\cdot\frac{2^{2\ell}}{2^{n}}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}.

To obtain this bound note that choosing
M_{1},M^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,M^{\prime}_{k}
can be thought of as choosing
2\ell+2\delta\ell k
vectors from
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, so we can union bound over the probability that any vector creates a linear dependence in its respective matrix. Putting everything together, we get
p_{k}\leqslant 2\langle{(\mathcal{T}F)^{k}},{G}\rangle
.
∎

With
Lemma

4.4
we have reduced showing
Lemma

4.1
to bounding the analytical quantity
\langle{(\mathcal{T}F)^{k}},{G}\rangle
. Moreover, we know that
F
comes from a pseudo-random set of subspaces. Intuition from Fourier analysis over the bilinear scheme says that if the function
F
is pseudo-random (in the sense of

is a randomly chosen indicator function. Using global hypercontractivity, we will show that this is indeed the case.

The function

is

-pseudo-random.

Proof.

See

Lemma 4.7

Proof.

In Appendix A of

an operator

\rank(C)=2\delta\ell\end{subarray}}\left[G(M+BC)\right].

In Lemma A.12 of

\mathcal{G}\mathcal{T}F=\Phi F.

(4)

By Lemma A.10 of
[

\left\lVert\mathcal{T}F^{=d}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}=\langle{\mathcal{T}F^{=d}},{\mathcal{T}F^{=d}}\rangle=\langle{F^{=d}},{\mathcal{G}\mathcal{T}F^{=d}}\rangle=\langle{F^{=d}},{\Phi F^{=d}}\rangle.

(5)

The first equality is by definition of
2
-norm and the last equality uses (
4

Now we use the fact that

Because the

(6)

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
F\in L_{2}\left(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2\ell}\right)
and
G\in L_{2}\left(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times 2(1-\delta)\ell}\right)
be basis invariant indicator functions where

is
(r,\varepsilon)
pseudo-random and

has expectation

that are powers of

\langle{(\mathcal{T}F)^{k}},{G}\rangle\leqslant 2^{O_{r,t,k}(1)}\beta^{(t-1)/t}\varepsilon^{(kt-2)/t}+2^{-r\delta\ell+1}.

Proof.

Using the degree decomposition, we can write
\mathcal{T}F=\sum_{i=0}^{2\ell}\mathcal{T}F^{=i}
. We partition the terms of the summation into
L=\sum_{i=0}^{r}\mathcal{T}F^{=i}
and
H=\sum_{i=r+1}^{2\ell}\mathcal{T}F^{=i}
, so that altogether
L+H=\mathcal{T}F
. We will want to start by bounding the probability that
H
is large, and to this end we first calculate its norm. Note that for
i>j

we have that

\langle{\mathcal{T}F^{=i}},{\mathcal{T}F^{=j}}\rangle=\langle{F^{=i}},{\mathcal{G}\mathcal{T}F^{=j}}\rangle=\langle{F^{=i}},{\Phi F^{=j}}\rangle=0.

(7)

The first transition is by combining Lemmas A.10 and A.11 of
[
MZ24
]
,
the second transition is by Lemma A.12 of
[
MZ24
]
, and the last lemma is because by Lemma A.13 of
[
MZ24
]
, the operator
\Phi

preserves the space of degree at most
j
functions. Combining with
Lemma

4.7
it follows that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{M}[|H(M)|^{2}]=\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=r+1}^{2\ell}\left\lVert\mathcal{T}F^{=i}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leqslant\left(2^{-r(2\delta\ell-1)}+3\cdot 2^{r-n}\right)\sum_{i=r+1}^{2\ell}\left\lVert F^{=d}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leqslant 2^{-r(2\delta\ell-1)}.

Letting
E
be the event that
|H(M)|\leqslant 2^{-0.9r\delta\ell}
, we get by Markov’s inequality that
\Pr[\bar{E}]\leqslant 2^{-r\delta\ell}
. We have:

\displaystyle\langle{(\mathcal{T}F)^{k}},{G}\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{M}[(\mathcal{T}F)(M)^{k}\cdot G(M)\cdot\mathbbm{1}(E)]+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{M}[(\mathcal{T}F)(M)^{k}\cdot G(M)\cdot\mathbbm{1}(\bar{E})]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{M}[G(M)\cdot(L(M)+H(M))^{k}\cdot\mathbbm{1}(E)]+\Pr[\bar{E}]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{k}\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{M}[G(M)\cdot|L(M)|^{k}\cdot\mathbbm{1}(E)]+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{M}[G(M)\cdot|H(M)|^{k}\cdot\mathbbm{1}(E)]\right)+2^{-r\delta\ell}.

In the second transition we used the fact that
\mathcal{T}F
and
G
are
1
-bounded, and in the third transition we used
|a+b|^{k}\leqslant 2^{k}(|a|^{k}+|b|^{k})
.
Next, the second term on the right hand side is upper bounded as:

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{M}[G(M)\cdot|H(M)|^{k}\cdot\mathbbm{1}(E)]\leqslant 2^{-0.9r\delta\ell k}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{M}[G(M)]\leqslant 2^{-0.9r\delta\ell k}.

For the first term above, we first use Hölder’s inequality to get

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{M}[G(M)\cdot|L(M)|^{k}\cdot\mathbbm{1}(E)]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{M}[G(M)\cdot|L(M)|^{k}]\leqslant\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{t/(t-1)}\left\lVert L^{k}\right\rVert_{t}=\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{t/(t-1)}\left\lVert L\right\rVert^{k}_{kt}.

By Booleanity of
G
,

\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{t/(t-1)}=\beta^{(t-1)/t}
. To bound
\left\lVert L\right\rVert^{k}_{kt}
we use the triangle inequality along with the fact that
\left\lVert\mathcal{T}F^{=i}\right\rVert_{kt}\leqslant\left\lVert F^{=i}\right\rVert_{kt}
by Jensen’s inequality and
4.6
as follows:

\displaystyle\left\lVert L\right\rVert^{k}_{kt}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\sum_{i=0}^{r}\left\lVert\mathcal{T}F^{=i}\right\rVert_{kt}\right)^{k}\leqslant\left(\sum_{i=0}^{r}\left\lVert F^{=i}\right\rVert_{kt}\right)^{k}\leqslant\left(2^{O_{r,t,k}(1)}\varepsilon^{\frac{kt-2}{kt}}\right)^{k}\leqslant 2^{O_{r,t,k}(1)}\varepsilon^{(kt-2)/t}.

Putting everything together gives

\langle{(\mathcal{T}F)^{k}},{G}\rangle\leqslant\beta^{(t-1)/t}2^{O_{r,t,k}(1)}\varepsilon^{(kt-2)/t}+2^{k}\cdot 2^{-0.9r\delta\ell k}+2^{-r\delta\ell},

where the second term is negligible compared to the third term.
∎

4.1
.

.

,

A Bound on the Number of Decoded Functions

In
4.2
, we showed a decoding theorem that says if the inner test passes with sufficient probability, then for a significant fraction of low dimension zoom-ins
Q
, there is a low codimension zoom-out
W
such that
T_{1}
agrees non-trivially with a linear function inside
{\sf Zoom}[Q,W]
. Roughly speaking, showing soundness for the composed PCP requires the provers to agree on the same linear function with non-trivial property, and this in turn requires them to agree on the same zoom-in
Q
and same zoom-out
W
.
Since the decoding theorem holds for a significant fraction of low dimension zoom-ins
Q
, the provers can agree on a shared zoom-in with reasonable probability using their advice. The same cannot be said for zoom-outs however, and to circumvent this issue we need another tool.

In this section, we present the notion of
maximal
zoom-outs and the result from
[
MZ24
]
which shows that the number of maximal zoom-outs relative to any fixed zoom-in is bounded.

Definition 5.4
.

Given a table
T
on
{\sf Grass}(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{V},2\ell)
, a subspace
Q\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{V}
, a zoom-out
W\supseteq Q
, and a linear function
f:W\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
, we say that the zoom-out, function pair
(W,g_{W})
is
(C,s)
-maximal with respect to
T
on
Q
if the following holds.

•

\Pr_{L\in{\sf Zoom}[Q,W]}[g_{W}|_{L}\equiv T[L]]\geqslant C
.

•

There does not exist another zoom-out function pair,
(W^{\prime},g_{W^{\prime}})
such that
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{V}\supseteq W^{\prime}\supsetneq W
,
g_{W^{\prime}}:W^{\prime}\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{F}_{2}
,
g_{W^{\prime}}|_{W}\equiv g|_{W}
and

\Pr_{L\in{\sf Zoom}[Q,W^{\prime}]}[g_{W^{\prime}}|_{L}\equiv T[L]]\geqslant sC
.

In the case that
Q=\{0\}
, we simply say that
(W,g_{W})
is
(C,s)
-maximal with respect to
T
. We say that the codimension of the zoom-out, function pair
(W,g_{W})
is
\codim(W)
.

In the above statement,
C
should be thought of as small and

s
should be thought of as an absolute constant.
With this in mind, a zoom-out
W
and a linear function on it

is called maximal if there is no zoom-out
W^{\prime}

that strictly contains
W
, and an extension of
g_{W}

to
g_{W^{\prime}}
, that has the same agreement
with
T
as
g_{W}
(up to constant factors). Intuitively, a zoom-out, function pair is “maximal” if its agreement with the table
T
is not explained by any larger zoom-out function pair. In
[
MZ24
]
, it is shown that the number of maximal zoom-out function pairs is bounded with respect to every zoom-out.

Theorem 5.5
.

For any
\delta>0
there is sufficiently large

such that the following holds. For any table

[

.

For all

-CSP

Fix

-CSP with size

-partite

First suppose

Now suppose

We first argue that
\val(\Psi^{\prime})\geqslant\val(\Psi)
. Let

Since in each CSP all of the vertices in

have the same degree, this shows that

also satisfies
\val(\Psi)
of the constraints in

\val(\Psi^{\prime})\geqslant\val(\Psi)

To show

note that we can partition

into edge sets of the form
E^{\prime}_{a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{k},b_{k}}=\{((v_{1},a_{1},b_{1}),\ldots,(v_{k},a_{k},b_{k}))\;|\;(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\in E\}
. Furthermore, for a fixed

\Psi^{\prime}
to only the edges

\eta

\val(\Psi^{\prime})\leqslant\val(\Psi)
.
∎
