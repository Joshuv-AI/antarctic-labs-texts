Surgical Site Infections in Major Lower Limb Amputation (SIMBA): international multicentre study
Authors: The SIMBA Collaborative Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSurgical site infection (SSI) after major lower limb amputation (MLLA) significantly affects function, mobility, morbidity, and mortality, alongside broader impacts on healthcare services. Limited data exist on the incidence, prevention, and management of SSI after MLLA. Improving outcomes and wound healing have been identified as research priorities for patients following MLLA.MethodsSurgical Site Infection in Major Lower Limb Amputation (SIMBA) is an international, prospective, collaborative observational study. Data were collected for consecutive patients undergoing MLLA over 8 months, with 30-day follow-up. Outcomes included the incidence of SSI (defined using Centers for Disease Control and Prevention criteria), wound dehiscence (defined as any partial or complete separation of the incision or flap, with or without infection), further intervention, mortality, adjunct use, and SSI predictors.ResultsValidated data for 1314 MLLAs from 46 centres in the UK (34), Europe (9), Australasia (2), and Asia (1) were collected, comprising 643 (48.9%) transfemoral, 41 (3.1%) through-knee, and 628 (47.8%) transtibial amputations. The most common indications were ischaemia (56.6%, 742) and uncontrolled infection (22.3%, 293). The incidences of SSI and wound breakdown were 10.9% (135 of 1238) and 16.7% (204 of 1217), respectively. Low glomerular filtration rate, cardiac disease, dirty/infected wound class, and transtibial amputations were independent predictors of SSI. SSI was associated with increased postoperative morbidity, wound breakdown, further intervention, and delays in fitness for discharge. The 30-day mortality rate was 7.0% (92 of 1314), with no significant association with SSI.ConclusionSSI and wound breakdown are common following MLLA, and are associated with increased morbidity, delayed recovery, and revision surgery. These findings identify patient- and procedure-specific factors associated with elevated SSI risk, and a lack of procedure-specific evidence, revealing critical gaps in SSI prevention strategies. Addressing these are essential to improve patient outcomes, reduce healthcare costs, and guide future research.Keywords: postoperative complications, risk factors, chronic limb-threatening ischaemia, wound breakdown

Introduction

Surgical site infection (SSI) is a leading cause of in-hospital morbidity and mortality1. However, data regarding the incidence of SSI following major lower limb amputation (MLLA) are limited. A recent systematic review reported a 7.2% incidence of SSI2, with single-centre studies reporting variable incidences of up to 27%. SSI following MLLA increases the risk of wound dehiscence and revision surgery2–7, potentially impacting mobility and function, and placing additional burden on caregivers, community services, and hospital systems. Furthermore, SSI carries substantial healthcare costs8. With an estimated international incidence of 17.0 per 100 000 patients9, and rates projected to rise10–12, MLLA represents a growing healthcare burden. The importance of this issue has been acknowledged by both patients and multidisciplinary clinicians. The priority setting partnership with the James Lind Alliance has outlined improving wound healing and clinical outcomes as two of the top research priorities following amputation surgery13, and SSI and wound healing and SSI are core outcomes for MLLA trial reporting14.

The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence provide guidelines for the prevention and management of SSI1, whereas the Global Vascular Guidelines15, Society for Vascular Surgery16, and the Vascular Society of Great Britain and Ireland17 provide recommendations to optimize patient care following MLLA. More recently, an international enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) consensus specific to MLLA has been published18, with evidence demonstrating that implementation of ERAS protocols is associated with improved outcomes, including reduced complications and better functional recovery19–21, further emphasizing the importance of optimized perioperative pathways18. However, adherence is unknown. Analyses using the Vascular Quality Initiative have also described outcomes following MLLA22; however, despite these important insights, procedure-specific evidence describing the incidence, risk factors, and clinical consequences of SSI remains limited. Adjunct therapies, such as antimicrobial dressings and negative pressure wound therapy, have also become increasingly available. However, beyond the administration of a prolonged 5-day course of prophylactic antibiotics following MLLA23, the evidence supporting specific interventions is limited.

The aim of the Surgical Site Infection in Major Lower Limb Amputation (SIMBA) study was to determine the 30-day incidence of SSI after MLLA. Secondary objectives included assessing the incidence of wound dehiscence and reintervention rates, identifying risk factors for SSI development, and evaluating clinical outcomes. The findings from SIMBA will inform the design of a large platform trial evaluating SSI prevention strategies, in which MLLA is included as a target procedure group24.

Methods

A comprehensive protocol for SIMBA has been published previously25, with an abridged version provided to participating centres (Supplementary material).

Organization and setting

SIMBA was an international, multicentre, prospective, collaborative observational cohort study designed to determine incidence of SSI following MLLA and evaluate current practice. This report was compiled using the STROBE checklist26 and SAMPL guidelines27.

SIMBA was funded by the ROSSINI-platform Accelerator Award (Award ID: NIHR156728)28. The study was sponsored by the Birmingham Centre for Observational and Prospective Studies and supported by the Vascular and Endovascular Research Network (VERN; https://vascular-research.net/), a multidisciplinary trainee-led vascular research collaborative29, with expertise in running national and international studies30,31.

Invitations were disseminated through VERN social media outlets and via the email of individuals engaging in recent VERN studies30–32. Eligible hospitals included those performing emergency or elective MLLA by vascular, orthopaedic, and other appropriate specialties.

Site enrolment opened on 1 October 2023 and closed on 1 January 2024. Data collection commenced once local approvals were obtained, with new patient identification concluding on 1 April 2024 and final data collection concluding on 1 May 2024, allowing for 30-day patient follow-up.

Identification and eligibility

Patients were identified at each individual SIMBA centre via local information technology systems, including theatre and ward-based lists. Patients were eligible if they were aged > 18 years, undergoing primary MLLA for any indication, including but not limited to dysvascular limbs, infection, trauma or malignancy, revision, or re-amputation. Patients undergoing MLLA with complex reconstruction, osseous integration, or staged amputations were excluded. Bilateral MLLAs were included as separate case records, provided both limbs met the eligibility criteria. Revisions or re-amputations occurring within 30 days were captured within the original record. Data for subsequent amputations were collected in full, including indication and outcomes, and each amputation was analysed as a separate event to examine SSI, wound complications, and other outcomes.

Team organization

Each site appointed a local team, including a principal investigator. Teams were responsible for gaining local approvals, identifying suitable patients, and data capture. The study was overseen by the study management group, which convened on a bimonthly basis to review conduct and progress.

Data collection, management, and validation

A clinical research form was developed by the study management group to capture risk factors and outcomes related to SSI. Data were collected prospectively from clinical and electronic records. Variables were objectively defined, except for preoperative perfusion status, which was based on clinical judgment and classified into three categories: good perfusion; the presence of peripheral arterial disease (PAD) not expected to impact wound healing (for example, tibial disease in a patient undergoing transfemoral amputation); or PAD likely to impair healing. Postoperative morbidity was reported using the Clavien–Dindo classification33. Certain variables were grouped where the number of observations was low, or where grouping facilitated clarity of analysis.

Data were pseudonymized and uploaded to the secure online REDCap database. Patient identifiers were retained at local centres in a secure, on-site file, in compliance with local guidelines, to enable linking of preoperative and postoperative data. No patient-identifiable information was transferred to REDCap.

At conclusion of the data-capture period, each site reviewed theatre logs to ensure the inclusion of all eligible patients, retrospectively adding any missing patients. ‘Unknown’ entries were accepted as complete data. Records with < 95% completeness were returned for completion; if not possible, the records were excluded, as per previous VERN projects30. Each centre validated 5% of their data, comparing original with data recaptured by an independent extractor. Additional validation was performed if accuracy fell below 95%. All centres met the 95% accuracy threshold after review; therefore, no exclusions were required.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the 30-day incidence of SSI following MLLA, defined as per the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention criteria8. Only SSIs apparent to the secondary care clinicians were included.

Secondary outcomes included the incidence of wound dehiscence; surgical and radiological reintervention used to manage SSI; revision and re-amputation surgery; early complications related to SSI; additional dressings used to manage SSI; and the type and duration of antibiotics used to manage SSI. Secondary outcomes also included identifying patient and surgical risk factors associated with SSI after MLLA and wound dehiscence after MLLA1,15–17.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis was used to summarize the data. Categorical data are presented as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables were reported as the median with interquartile range (i.q.r.) because of the skewed distribution of the data. Percentages were calculated using the total number of amputations as a denominator to account for bilateral amputees. Percentages for each variable were calculated using only records with available data as the denominator; missing data were omitted from the denominator.

Univariate analysis was performed using the χ2 test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables. Variables with P < 0.1 were considered for inclusion in multivariate analysis. Multivarite logistic regression analysis was conducted to determine variables associated with SSI. Variables were included based on previous literature34–36, clinical relevance, and results of the univariate analysis. Variables with > 15% missing data were excluded from multivariate analyses. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

The same methods were used to assess associations with wound breakdown. Analyses were performed using Stata®/SE version 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). The incidence of SSI from individual centres was presented as a funnel plot using a Microsoft® Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) macro37.

Local audit and ethics approval

Due to the nature of the project, SIMBA did not require National Health Service (NHS) Research and Ethics Committee approval according to the Health Research Authority Decision tool (https://hra-decisiontools.org.uk/research/). Each centre registered SIMBA locally before data collection (audit and service provision registration at NHS sites). Centres outside the UK were compliant with local regulations.

Results

Forty-six centres participated in SIMBA: 34 from the UK, nine from Europe, two from Australasia, and one from Asia. All centres achieved > 95% data validation accuracy. Eleven records were excluded due to unacceptable levels of missing data (> 5%). In all, 1314 amputations were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Of these, 1248 were the first amputation recorded on a given limb during the study period. These may not represent primary amputations, because initial procedures may have occurred before the data collection period. Sixty-five of the 1314 amputations were revisions or re-amputations within 30 days of the procedure on the same limb. One patient underwent a third amputation of the same limb.

Of the amputations, transfemoral amputations accounted for 48.9% (643 of 1314), transtibial amputations (TTA) accounted for 47.8% (628 of 1314), most of which (91.4%, 574 of 628) used the long posterior flap technique and the remainder (8.6%, 54 of 628) using the skew flap technique. Through-knee amputations accounted for 3.1% (41 of 1314) of amputations, including removal of part of the femur (Gritti-Stokes; 43.9%, 18 of 41) or without femoral bone removal (56.1%, 23 of 41). Only 0.15% (2 of 1314) hip disarticulations were recorded.

The primary indication for MLLA was limb ischaemia (56.7%, 745 of 1314); of these, 44.8% (334 of 745) had no revascularization options, 42.7% (318 of 745) had failed revascularization, and 12.4% (93 of 745) were unsuitable for revascularization. Acute limb ischaemia accounted for 26.0% (194 of 745) of indications for MLLA. Other indications included uncontrolled infection (22.4%, 293 of 1311), excessive tissue loss (12.0%, 158 of 1311), previous amputation site breakdown (6.4%, 84 of 1311), and others (2.6%, 34 of 1131). Wound classification at the site of surgical incision was clean in 1167 of 1313 amputations (88.9%), clean–contaminated in 75 of 1313 amputations (5.7%), contaminated in 38 of 1313 amputations (2.9%), and dirty or infected in 33 of 1313 amputations (2.5%). Where reported, 12.4% (155 of 1246) of patients had good preoperative perfusion, 58.6% (730 of 1246) had PAD that was not expected to impair healing, and 29.0% (361 of 1246) had PAD that could impair healing.

Alcoholic chlorhexidine was the most common skin preparation (50.7%, 558 of 1101). Perineural catheters were placed in 75.3% (968 of 1286) of patients and drains were placed in 60.0% (788 of 1281). Closure was with sutures in 80.5% (1051 of 1287) of amputations and with skin clips in 18.1% (236 of 1287). Complete skin closure was achieved in 91.8% (1152 of 1252) of amputations. Initial dressings were typically absorbent adhesive (83.28%, 976 of 1172), with antimicrobial or diakylcarbamoyl chloride used in 7.42% (87 of 1172) of patients and negative pressure systems (open and closed incision) used in 2.73% (32 of 1172). Postoperative prophylactic antibiotics were given to 70.8%(837 of 1182) of patients, with 68.9% (577 of 837) receiving a course of at least 5 days. Postoperative outcomes

SSI development was reported in 10.9% (135 of 1238) of MLLAs, with incidence varying from 0% to 40% between individual centres. This variation is illustrated in Fig. 2, which plots SSI rate against procedural volume. Over 15% (16.8%, 204 of 1217) of amputations had wound breakdown, and further intervention was required in 10.8% (140 of 1298), of which 4.3% (56/1298) were re-amputations.

Of the patients with SSI, 90.8% (118 of 130) received antibiotics. Microbiological samples were obtained from 57.6% (76 of 132) of patients, with pathogens susceptible to prescribed therapy identified in 43.9% (25 of 57; Table S1). Additional dressings were required in 85.6% (95 of 111) of cases: 38.7% (43 of 111) using antimicrobial or dialkylcarbamoyl chloride dressings, and 29.7% (33 of 111) using negative pressure systems. SSI led to sepsis in 29.5% (38 of 129) of cases and unplanned critical care admissions in 13.6% (18 of 132). Further surgical or radiological intervention was required in 50.7% (68 of 134) of cases, including re-amputation in 22.4% (30 of 134).

A comparison of adverse 30-day postoperative outcomes between patients who did and did not develop SSI is presented in Table 2. In all, 7.0% (92 of 1314) of patients died within 30 days of MLLA. Mortality did not differ significantly between those with (7.4%, 10 of 135) and without (6.7%, 74 of 1103) SSI. However, SSI was associated with increased wound breakdown (76.4% (97 of 127) versus 9.1% (98 of 1078); P < 0.001), greater morbidity (higher Clavien–Dindo grades; P < 0.001), a requirement for further intervention (50.8% versus 6.0%; P < 0.001), increased time to medical fitness for discharge (defined as the point at which a patient no longer required acute inpatient medical care; 8 versus 15 days; P < 0.001), and a reduced complete wound healing at 30 days (13.8% versus 82.3%; P < 0.001).

Discussion

This international multicentre cohort study identified a 10.9% incidence of SSI 30 days after MLLA. Patients who developed SSI experienced poorer short-term outcomes, including delayed recovery, a reduced likelihood of being medically fit for discharge, and a higher incidence of wound breakdown. The observed SSI incidence is higher than the 7.2% reported in a recent systematic review2 and in the 2024 National Vascular Registry report38 (4.7% for TTA, 2.8% for transfemoral amputation), but slightly lower than the 12.8% reported in the PERCIEVE study31, underscoring the burden of SSI in this population. SSI incidence varied widely between centres (0–40%), with no association with procedural volume, indicating risk is influenced by factors beyond case numbers. Centre-level differences in case mix, such as the ratio of below-the-knee to above-the-knee amputations, were not analysed in this study.

SSI was associated with considerable postoperative morbidity. Over half of those developing an SSI required further intervention, with 22.2% undergoing proximal re-amputation. Although 30-day mortality did not differ significantly between those with and without SSI, proximal re-amputation is known to be associated with increased mortality39, reduced quality of life40, and higher metabolic demands during ambulation41. This significantly hinders rehabilitation, often limiting or precluding prosthetic use, particularly in frail or co-morbid patients42, leading to wheelchair dependence, institutional care, mental health impacts, and community rehabilitation costs.

Wound breakdown is strongly associated with SSI; however, dehiscence can occur in the absence of SSI, particularly in ischaemic tissue. Clinically, sterile breakdown and SSI can appear similar, and both contribute to prolonged hospitalization, delayed rehabilitation, and an increased need for further intervention. Thus, wound breakdown remains a significant endpoint, warranting focused preventive strategies. This analysis identified independent risk factors for wound breakdown distinct from those for SSI. Female patients comprised a minority of the cohort (26.9%) and although no association was found between sex and SSI, female sex was associated with increased wound breakdown. Although amputation for PAD is generally more common in men, women may present with more advanced or atypical disease43,44, which could influence postoperative outcomes. These findings underscore the importance of considering sex-specific factors in perioperative risk stratification.

SSI contributes substantially to healthcare expenditure, with prolonged hospital stays, increased primary care interactions, antimicrobial prescribing, and re-admissions45. SSI is recognized as the costliest healthcare-associated infection8, with one study estimating that the presence of SSI after MLLA contributes an additional £6103 in costs and increases the length of hospital stay by 21 days46. In the present study, patients with SSI were less likely to be medically fit for discharge at 30 days (35.5% versus 77.8%) and had almost double the time to medical fitness (15 versus 8 days) than those without SSI. Although the present study did not directly measure costs, these findings highlight how increased morbidity and poorer wound healing contribute directly and indirectly to healthcare resource utilization, including environmental cost47, substantial single-use supply waste48, and ongoing community health and social care input.

Reduced eGFR was strongly associated with the development of SSI, consistent with the known immunomodulatory dysfunction and delayed healing seen in renal impairment49,50. Cardiac disease, encompassing both ischaemic disease and cardiac failure, also demonstrated an association with the development of SSI, and has been linked to postoperative morbidity and infection across other specialties51,52. ASA grade > III was not significantly associated with SSI in the present cohort, in contrast with previous reports53,54. However, 93.1% of patients in the present cohort had an ASA grade > III, limiting the discriminative power of this metric. Although many current strategies for SSI prevention focus on limiting wound contamination, these findings underscore the importance of optimizing host factors with patient-related risk stratification.

TTA (long posterior or skew flap) was associated with the development of SSI. Skew flap TTA showed numerically greater rates of SSI (18.9% versus 11.0%; P = 0.095) and wound breakdown (26.9% versus 18.8%; P = 0.161) than long posterior TTA, although the differences were not statistically significant (Table S5), consistent with evidence from a single-centre retrospective study55 and a historical Cochrane review56. However, skew flap TTAs were associated with significantly higher rates of further intervention (24.0% versus 12.0%; P = 0.012) and lower 30-day complete healing (51.2% versus 72.8%; P = 0.004), suggesting technique may influence early healing.

Wound classification significantly influences infection risk, ranging from a risk of 1–2% in clean wounds to 40% in dirty wounds34. Amputations are generally considered clean; however, certain indications, for example uncontrolled infection, may be contaminated or dirty. Moreover, even clean amputations frequently involve devitalized tissue, likely contributing to SSI rates being closer to that of the clean–contaminated spectrum. The National Institute for Heath and Care Excellence guidelines1 do not routinely recommend either preoperative or postoperative antibiotic prophylaxis for clean wounds, but this guidance and the wound classification itself do not account for the higher SSI incidence in MLLA. A meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials supports preoperative antibiotic prophylaxis35, also recommended by the Society for Vascular Surgery16. In the present study, 97.5% of patients with clean MLLAs received induction antibiotics, reflecting adoption of evidence beyond existing guidelines. Emerging evidence also supports the role of postoperative antibiotics, with a recent randomized clinical trial demonstrating that a 5-day course of antibiotics after MLLA significantly reduces SSI rates36. In line with this, 69.4% of patients in the present study received postoperative prophylactic antibiotics.

Evidence supporting recommended interventions for MLLA is limited and often extrapolated from other surgical settings, for example methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus screening and skin preparation. In the present study, 50.7% of centres used alcoholic chlorhexidine, compared with aqueous povidone–iodine being most common in 20144. This shift reflects the impact of the literature and guidelines on practice, despite a lack of conclusive evidence supporting the intervention57,58. Importantly, substantial variation in perioperative practice was observed across centres. Although these differences were not independently associated with SSI in the present analysis, mapping this heterogeneity is valuable because it identifies logical targets for future standardization and evaluation. This study highlights a broader structural limitation with the current literature: a lack of robust, procedure-specific evidence to guide SSI prevention in MLLA. Ongoing work, including the ROSSINI-Platform randomized clinical trial24,59, which includes MLLA as a target procedure group, offers a promising step towards assessing this gap. The multi-arm, multistage design, and multispecialty platform structure of ROSSINI-Platform allow for efficient evaluation of multiple SSI prevention strategies, holding potential to generate higher-quality, procedure-specific evidence. Similarly, the forthcoming clinical practice guidelines on MLLA from the European Society for Vascular Surgery60, due in 2027, will be critical in developing standardized, evidence-based care.

The present study provides prospective data on a large cohort across the UK and international centres. To the authors’ knowledge, this represents the largest prospective study specifically investigating SSI following MLLA. However, limitations must be acknowledged. Only SSIs presenting to the index vascular centre were recorded, potentially underestimating true incidence. It is likely that more minor SSI were managed in the community. Data were self-reported by participating centres and, although internally validated, may be subject to reporting bias. High levels of missing data were observed for some variables that are known to influence SSI, for example body mass index. In addition, the 30-day follow-up period, although consistent with Centers for Disease Control and Prevention criteria for defining SSI, does not capture the full course of postoperative healing after MLLA, and later complications may therefore be underrepresented. A planned 1-year follow-up study on this cohort will provide more comprehensive long-term outcome data. By excluding staged procedures, this study focused on outcomes after primary definitive MLLA. Thus, patients undergoing planned multistage (that is, guillotine) amputations were excluded. Postoperative and early therapeutic antibiotics, which may be influenced by pre-existing infection, were not differentiated, potentially limiting interpretation of antibiotic use. Furthermore, due to the observational study design, causality cannot be inferred. Nonetheless, the findings offer valuable insights into real-world practice and identify key targets for future research, providing an important, although preliminary, foundation for improving the care of MLLA patients.

This large prospective multicentre study found a 10.9% 30-day incidence of SSI after MLLA, associated with increased morbidity, wound breakdown, delayed recovery, and revision surgery. This study also highlights patient- and procedure-specific factors associated with elevated SSI risk, and a lack of procedure-specific evidence revealing critical gaps in SSI prevention strategies for MLLA. The SIMBA study provides valuable real-world data on current practice and outcomes, offering a necessary foundation to inform future research, guideline development, and quality improvement initiatives aimed at reducing infection-related harm and improving MLLA outcomes.
