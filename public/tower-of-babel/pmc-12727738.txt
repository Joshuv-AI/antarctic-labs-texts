Flexible monolithic 3D complementary circuits based on 2D semiconductor inks
Authors: Taoyu Zou, Seongmin Heo, Youjin Reo, Gi-Seong Ryu, Jaeyun Lee, Mingyu Kim, Soonhyo Kim, Gwon Byeon, Ji-Sang Park, Wentao Xu, Yong-Young Noh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Emerging flexible and wearable electronic systems require high-density, low-power circuits that can seamlessly integrate with soft substrates. Monolithic 3D (M3D) integration using 2D semiconductors offers a compelling route in fulfilling these requirements by enabling vertical stacking without compromising mechanical compliance. However, current M3D approaches utilizing 2D semiconductors often require high-temperature processing or transfer steps that hinder their scalability on flexible substrates. Herein, we present a low-temperature M3D integration strategy based on 2D semiconductor inks, which is enabled by a tailored anion-cation doping approach for precise carrier control in n- and p-type devices. Our methodology yields vertically assembled complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor circuits – including inverters, logic, and photosensor-integrated gates, and ring oscillators (ROs) – fabricated entirely at ≤150 °C. Notably, the inverters exhibit a voltage gain up to 462 at a supply voltage of 4 V, and the 5-stage ROs can operate at a maximum oscillation frequency of 13.5 kHz. Beyond their electrical performances, the circuits display robust mechanical stabilities, conforming to curved surfaces, and excellent skin compatibilities. This study reports a scalable, low-temperature platform for the fabrication of M3D electronics in wearable low-power neuromorphic computing systems and bio-integrated electronics.

Introduction

The rapid evolution of flexible and wearable electronics propels the development of miniaturized, highly integrated, energy-efficient systems for application in health monitoring, human-machine interfaces, and soft robotics1–3. To satisfy the demands of these next-generation devices, realizing high-density multilayer/3D integration, minimal power consumption, and a robust mechanical durability under deformation is imperative4,5. Monolithic 3D (M3D) integration using 2D materials has emerged as a promising alternative in realizing such dense 3D architectures. This is due to the ultrathin nature, superior levels of electrostatic control, and inherent scalability of 2D materials that enable the aggressive downscaling of the device dimensions6–9. Early demonstrations of M3D integration predominantly employed mechanical exfoliation and transfer techniques10,11, which, although instrumental in establishing proof-of-concept stacked transistor architectures, encountered significant scalability limitations. Subsequent approaches utilizing chemical vapor deposition (CVD) combined with layer transfer improved the film uniformity12–14, but they introduced complexities related to transfer-induced contamination and alignment inaccuracies. More recently, the direct CVD growth of 2D materials on prefabricated layers was advanced, offering an in-situ fabrication strategy that mitigated process-induced damage15. Nevertheless, these conventional methods still require high processing temperatures, thus constraining their applicability to flexible substrates and underscoring the need for alternative, low-temperature fabrication strategies.

Solution-processed 2D material inks provide a scalable, cost-effective route in preparing large-area electronics by enabling the direct deposition of atomically thin semiconductors without complex transfer steps16–18. Unlike vapor-phase techniques, solution processing supports low-temperature fabrication, flexible substrate integration, and precise control over the film thickness19,20. Recent advances in 2D monolayer inks and nanoscale films enhanced their levels of charge transport while preserving their intrinsic properties21,22. Moreover, establishing broad-area plane-to-plane van der Waals (vdWs) contacts within these films endows them with exceptional levels of mechanical flexibility and stretchability, rendering them particularly well-suited for flexible and wearable electronic applications23. Nevertheless, precisely controlling the carrier concentration remains a significant challenge16,24, particularly those of p-type transistor materials, such as WSe2, where robust solution-processable doping techniques are still lacking. Although various molecular approaches have been explored to modulate the carrier density of n-type MoS2 at low temperatures25,26, the limited understanding of their underlying mechanisms hinders the reliable tuning of the electrical performance. While the M3D integration of 2D semiconductors has been demonstrated via CVD or exfoliation methods, a solution-processed M3D integration strategy has yet to be realized, highlighting the need for innovative fabrication approaches.

In this study, we establish a solution-phase anion-cation co-doping strategy to precisely modulate the carrier concentrations within solution-processed n-type MoS2 and p-type WSe2 thin-film transistors, which is a critical prerequisite for high-performance M3D complementary logic integration. The carriers within both materials are modulated via tailored dopant chemistry, as supported by density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Anions introduce acceptor-like states close to the valence band, enabling electron depletion or hole accumulation, whereas cations preferentially occupy defect sites, effectively suppressing hysteresis and enhancing stability. Thus, we demonstrate the solution-processed M3D integration of complementary 2D semiconductor circuits, including inverters, NOR and NAND gates, photosensor-in-logic circuits, and a ring oscillator (RO), entirely at ≤150 °C, without high-temperature or transfer steps. The M3D inverter realizes a voltage gain of 462 at 4 V, whereas the 5-stage RO reaches a maximum frequency of 13.5 kHz. The flexible circuits maintain stable performance under deformation, highlighting their suitability in wearable skin-compatible electronics.

Electrical properties of n-type MoS2 and p-type WSe2 transistors

Precise carrier concentration control is crucial in preparing stable, low-temperature, high-performance complementary logic circuits. We developed an in-solution strategy by incorporating anion-cation pairs directly into the 2D semiconductor inks prior to deposition. Conventional post-treatment methods involve doping after film formation and often restrict dopant interaction to only the exposed surface of the nanoflakes, requiring elevated temperatures or multistep processing27. In contrast, our in-solution doping approach enables uniform carrier modulation across both the surface and buried layers of the nanoflakes during film formation. This incorporation not only yields superior device metrics, including reduced hysteresis and low subthreshold swing (SS), owing to the maximum treatment across the entire semiconductor film, but also enhances compatibility with ultra-flexible or wearable substrates and facilitates scalable large-area processing.

The electrical performances of the solution-processed transistors are characterized from the transfer characteristics of the n-type MoS2 and p-type WSe2 devices (Fig. 1g–i). respectively. The median SS for n-type MoS2 and p-type WSe2 were 133 mV/dec and 193 mV/dec, respectively. The MoS2 transistor achieved an on/off ratio of >108 and a minimum SS of 88 mV/dec at VDS = 1 V, while the WSe2 transistor exhibited an SS of 119 mV/dec at VDS = –1 V with an on/off ratio of >108. These values are comparable to those of CVD-grown 2D transistors14,28–38 (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 1). Further improvements in the dielectric engineering and the film quality could reduce the SS toward the thermionic limit of 60 mV/dec, underscoring the potential of solution processing for high-performance logic applications. The consistent electrical characteristics across the wafer confirm the reproducibility and scalability of our low-temperature, solution-processed transistors (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Carrier concentration modulation

The proposed doping mechanism is schematically shown in Fig. 2a, b. Electron-withdrawing anions, such as bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (TFSI−) or trifluoromethanesulfonate (OTf−), reduce the excess carriers within intrinsically highly doped n-type 2D materials or induce effective p-doping to increase their hole concentrations (nh). Meanwhile, small cations (e.g., H+ or Li+) may passivate the defect states and suppress trap-mediated hysteresis. To evaluate the individual roles of anions and cations in tuning transistor performance, we systematically investigated MoS2 transistors treated with various TFSI− and OTf− salts. Applying those anion species significantly alters the electron concentration (ne) from 1.8 × 1013 to 5 × 1012 cm−2 and enhances the on/off current ratio from 10 to >107 (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 6). Hence, electron-withdrawing anions are primarily responsible for carrier suppression. To isolate the role of cations, we further examined devices treated with Li⁺-containing salts. The transfer characteristics result in a marked reduction in hysteresis (10 mV), and an almost overlapping forward and reverse sweeps (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8), which is comparable to those reported for MoS2 transistors fabricated using CVD or exfoliated flakes37,39. This improvement is attributed to cation-induced surface passivation, which mitigates charge trapping. We further evaluated the p-doping behaviors of solution-processed WSe2 thin films to enable robust p-type transistor operation. As shown in Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 9, treatment with the TFSI− and OTf− salts significantly increases the nh from 3 × 1011 to 8 × 1011 cm−2 and improves the on/off current ratio from 103 to >107. Hence, the anions are the dominant contributors to effective p-type doping. Beyond the carrier concentration tunability, the doped p-WSe2 and n-MoS2 transistors exhibit strong thermal stabilities, maintaining their electrical performances after annealing at 150 °C (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 10), and thus, they are compatible with downstream processes, such as the atomic layer deposition (ALD) of a dielectric.

Moreover, both the p-WSe2 and n-MoS2 transistors exhibit excellent air stability, showing minimal degradation after one month of ambient storage under controlled low humidity (relative humidity ≈5%, Supplementary Fig. 11), without requiring encapsulation. The observed air stability can be attributed to two key factors: the intrinsic oxygen inertness of dopants such as TFSI−, which remain unaffected even under aggressive oxidative conditions40, and the buried dopant formed during film formation, where overlapping nanoflakes encapsulate a portion of these dopants beneath the surface, thereby sustaining doping efficacy and mitigating ambient degradation.

To elucidate the mechanism of doping within the solution-processed 2D semiconductor, we performed DFT calculations using TFSI as a representative anion alongside various cationic alkali dopants in MoS2 and WSe2 (Fig. 2f). Density of states (DOS) analysis indicates that TFSI induces doping by introducing molecular states close to the valence band edge, which serve as charge-accepting sites. This facilitates electron transfer from the 2D semiconductor to the acceptor levels of TFSI, effectively lowering the ne within MoS2 or enhancing the hole density within WSe2. On the other hand, in MoS2, hydrogen preferentially occupies sulfur vacancies within MoS2 and acts as an acceptor under S-poor conditions with excess electrons, while Li⁺, though a donor in interstitial form, passivating traps and reducing electron concentration (Supplementary Fig. 12a). Similarly, Na⁺ and K⁺ tend to form substitutional deep acceptors rather than interstitial donors under n-type conditions, further weakening the n-type behavior. In WSe2, hydrogen (from HTFSI and HOTf) forms interstitial defects, which have a limited doping efficiency due to the deep transition level, supporting p-type doping via anion (Fig. 2i). In contrast, Li⁺, Na⁺, and K⁺ act as shallow donors in the form of interstitials, partially compensating for anion-induced p-doping and causing a lower nh and on-state current (Supplementary Fig. 12b). These results confirm that tunable carrier modulation arises from the synergistic effects of anion-induced doping and cation-mediated defect passivation. Importantly, this molecular doping framework – based on tailored anion-cation combinations – can be generalized to a broad class of ionic dopants and 2D semiconductors.

Fabricated M3D complementary circuits

With the established control over the electrical performances of the solution-processed pMOS and nMOS transistors, we have constructed M3D logic functions on a rigid SiO2/Si substrate. For circuit fabrication, a 60 nm ALD-grown Al2O3 dielectric was employed on both rigid and flexible substrates to ensure low leakage current, mechanical robustness, and high uniformity during multistep stacking and peel-off processes, thereby preserving the dielectric against processing-induced damage. Figure 3a shows the M3D-stacked inverter fabricated using the solution-processed MoS2 (n-type, bottom) and WSe2 (p-type, top) thin films. The voltage transfer characteristics at different supply voltages (VDD, Fig. 3b) exhibit sharp transitions, switching with a voltage gain exceeding 462 at VDD = 4 V. Meanwhile, the inverter remains functional at an ultra-low VDD = 0.1 V, confirming its potential in ultra-low-power electronics. At VDD = 3 V, it realizes a noise margin of 90.3% (Supplementary Fig. 13), ensuring robust logic operation. The device operates with sub-nanowatt power consumption, with a static power of ~18.5 pW at VDD = 0.3 V corresponding to a normalized power density of ~18.5fW/µm (Supplementary Fig. 14), which is comparable to state-of-the-art values41. Such ultra-low-power operation is attributed to the steep subthreshold characteristics of the MoS2 and WSe2 transistors, enabling sharp transitions. Figure 3c benchmarks the voltage gain of our solution-processed M3D inverter against those of CVD-grown and exfoliated 2D inverters11–15,30,42–48, indicating a comparable or superior performance, even at 0.1 V. Compared to those of the Si-on-insulator-MoS2 stacking inverter46, our inverter realizes a higher gain, with the peak power consumption decreased almost 10-fold, underscoring its energy efficiency in low-power applications. M3D integration enables the realization of multifunctional logic circuits, such as a NOR gate with an embedded photosensing capability (Fig. 3d). The optical image (left) and schematic (right) depict the vertically stacked MoS2/WSe2 CMOS structure, where the top-tier p-type transistor is exposed to light, enabling direct light-modulated logic operations. The input-output characteristics (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 16) confirm the NOR logic functionality, with distinct output voltage levels for all input combinations. Figure 3f shows photosensor-in-logic operation, where the output voltage dynamically responds to on/off light cycles. When exposed to light, the top WSe2 transistor shifts its threshold voltage, altering the photoresponse and enabling light-controllable logic functions. The successful integration of logic and sensing within a single M3D circuit eliminates the need for separate photodetectors, reducing system complexity, which is particularly promising for compact optoelectronic applications. This photosensor-in-logic architecture provides a platform for reconfigurable and stimulus-selective logic arrays, with potential applications in neuromorphic vision. With further spectral tuning and heterostructure design, it may enable multi-channel optical logic photocomputing.

To further validate the potential of solution-processed M3D integration in dynamic logic applications, a 5-stage RO was fabricated (Fig. 3g). The optical image (top) and circuit diagram (bottom) display the vertically stacked structure. The output waveforms of the 5-stage ROs under different VDD values are presented in Fig. 3h, exhibiting stable oscillations with increasing frequency as VDD is increased. The oscillation frequency can be adjusted from 5.1 to 13.5 kHz as VDD increases from 5 to 12 V (Fig. 3i). The frequency increases with supply voltage before gradually saturating beyond ~12 V, which we attribute to intrinsic delays in the circuit. The limited oscillation frequency and high operation voltage primarily arise from a large delay per stage (τ), where the τ = Reff ⋅ Cload reflects the effective resistance (Reff) and total capacitive load (Cload). The large gate area of the transistors leads to high gate capacitance, while gate–electrode overlap and the interlayer dielectric between stacked metal layers introduce significant parasitic capacitance both within and between tiers. Furthermore, the moderate mobility of solution-processed MoS2 and WSe2 could contribute to high Reff. These factors collectively limit switching speed, though future optimization in device geometry, dielectric design, and mobility could substantially improve frequency performance34,35.

Flexible M3D integrated circuits

The mechanical adaptability of solution-processed M3D circuits leverages the vdW interaction between the stacked 2D layers, enabling strain accommodation without compromising their performance. The bond-free interfaces within vdW thin films enable flexibility, stretchability, and structural integrity, rendering them ideal in bio-integrated electronics23. The mechanical robustness of the solution-processed M3D inverter was demonstrated (Fig. 4b–d). Figure 4b shows the voltage transfer characteristics and voltage gain, with the maximum voltage gain reaching 61 at a VDD of 9 V. Figure 4c further shows the transfer characteristics under bending and release states with a bending radius of 9.5 mm, revealing minimal shifts in the threshold voltage, ensuring a consistent logic functionality. This result indicates that the device maintains its switching behavior and amplification capability under strain. Figure 4d assesses the long-term mechanical stability upon subjecting the inverter to 100 bending cycles, with the voltage transfer characteristics and voltage gain remaining nearly unchanged.

The mechanical robustness of flexible M3D logic circuits extends to the NOR and NAND gates and ROs. Figure 4e, f shows the stable input-output characteristics of the NOR and NAND gates, respectively, before and after bending, confirming reliable logic operation under strain. Figure 4g presents an optical image of a flexible M3D-stacked 3-stage RO with via-hole interconnections. Figure 4h demonstrates the stable output waveforms of the RO at different VDD values, with a maximum frequency of 6 kHz at 12 V (Supplementary Fig. 17). We evaluated five independent flexible 3-stage RO (15 M3D-stacked inverters), all exhibiting stable output with frequencies ranging from 1.31 kHz to 2.37 kHz (Supplementary Fig. 18), confirming the reproducibility and uniformity of the integrated logic devices. The flexible 3-stage Ro retained stable output before and after bending (Supplementary Fig. 19), implying mechanical robustness and suitability for wearable applications. We note that flexible devices exhibit reduced inverter gain and oscillation frequency compared to their rigid counterparts, primarily due to mechanical challenges introduced during the peel-and-transfer process. Although a 60 nm ALD-grown Al2O3 layer is deposited on the 2 µm-thick parylene film to reduce surface roughness, the flexible stack remains prone to strain and deformation during transfer and thermal release (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 20). Nevertheless, these results distinguish solution-processed flexible M3D circuits from previously reported circuits, establishing them as promising candidates in wearable and low-power electronics (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

We demonstrated the solution-processed M3D integration of complementary 2D semiconductor circuits, achieving high performance, picowatt-level power consumption, and mechanical flexibility. Using the tailored anion-cation doping strategy, we precisely modulated the carrier concentrations in MoS2 (n-type) and WSe2 (p-type), enabling the preparation of vertically stacked CMOS inverters, logic gates, photosensor-in-logic circuits, and ROs – fabricated at ≤150 °C without transfer steps. The inverter exhibited a voltage gain of 462 at 4 V, and the flexible RO reached a frequency of 6 kHz at 12 V. The circuits maintained stable operation under repeated bending and conformed to curved surfaces, demonstrating excellent skin compatibility. These results establish a scalable, low-temperature M3D platform for future wearable, bio-integrated, and multifunctional electronics.

Methods

Ink preparation

Intercalation was conducted in a two-electrode system with tetraheptylammonium bromide (98%, Tokyo Chemical Industry, Tokyo, Japan) as the electrolyte. A MoS2 or WSe2 crystal was attached to a Cu plate with conductive Cu tape as the cathode, while a graphite rod served as the anode. As the electrolyte, tetraheptylammonium bromide was dissolved in acetonitrile at a concentration of 5 mg/mL. During intercalation, a voltage of approximately 15 V was applied to the cathode for 1 h. Subsequently, the expanded transition metal dichalcogenide (TMD) crystals were sonicated in 40 mL of a polyvinylpyrrolidone/dimethylformamide solution at a concentration of 8 mg/mL for 30 min. We employed iterative centrifugation cascades to remove the intercalated molecules and large flakes. The dispersion was centrifuged at 110, 440, and 990 × g for 30 min each (Centrifuge 1736R, LABOGENE), and the precipitates were discarded. The sediment was collected at a centrifugation speed of 2750 × g and dispersed in isopropyl alcohol (IPA). TFSI− or OTf−-based molecules were dissolved in IPA at a concentration of 10 mg/mL and mixed with the 2D semiconductor ink in a volumetric ratio of 1:10. The relative and absolute concentrations of the 2D semiconductor inks and TFSI may be modified and optimized for best results. WSe2 crystals for electrochemical intercalation were purchased from 2D Semiconductors, USA, and MoS2 crystals obtained from SPI Supplies were used as received.

Thin-film transistor fabrication

First, the SU-8 photoresist was prepared on an Al2O3 (18 nm)/Si substrate using conventional photolithography to form the desired patterns, and the Al2O3 dielectric layer was deposited via ALD. The treated 2D semiconductor inks were drop-cast onto the patterned photoresist to confine the formation of the 2D thin film. The substrate was covered with a Petri dish to slow the solvent evaporation and obtain a well-stacked thin film. After the solvent evaporated, the photoresist was removed with chloroform to yield highly aligned 2D TMD thin-film arrays. The substrate with the 2D thin-film arrays was then annealed on a hot plate in a N2-filled glovebox at 150 °C for 1 h. The Ni (40 nm) electrodes were thermally evaporated and patterned via photolithography. To realize a p-type WSe2 transistor with the optimal performance, the WSe2 device was further annealed in a HTFSI or HOTf solution at 120 °C for 15 min.

DFT calculations

We performed first-principles density functional theory calculations to investigate the doping properties of MoS2 and WSe2. We used the generalized gradient approximation parameterized by Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof49 and the projector-augmented wave method50, implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package code51. For a better description of the dispersion interaction, the DFT-D3 method with Becke-Johnson damping function was used52. The correction scheme suggested by Freysoldt, Neugebauer, and Van de Walle was used for charged defect calculations53. The plane waves were expanded up to 340 eV. The chemical potential of the Mo-rich and S-poor (W-poor and Se-rich) condition was determined by body-centered cubic (BCC) Mo (orthorhombic Se consisting of Se8 rings). The chemical potential of H, Li, Na, and K was obtained from the H2 molecule, BCC Li, BCC Na, and BCC K, respectively.

M3D-stacked CMOS fabrication

Fabricating the M3D-stacked CMOS structure begins with substrate preparation on a Si wafer coated with a sacrificial layer. The sacrificial layer used in flexible device fabrication was a 2 wt% aqueous solution of Micro-90, spin-coated at 1000 rpm for 60 s and baked at 90 °C for 5 min. A 2 μm-thick parylene film was subsequently deposited onto the sacrificial layer using a parylene coating system (OBT-PC300, Obang Technology) via standard chemical vapor deposition. A parylene film (2 μm) was used as the flexible substrate, followed by the ALD of Al2O3 (60 nm), which functioned as a buffering layer to enhance adhesion and structural stability. Fabricating the first-tier transistor followed a standard photolithography and deposition sequence. Ni was patterned as the gate electrode, and then Al2O3 was deposited as a dielectric. The n-type MoS2 active layer was defined via a lift-off process, and the Ni electrode contacts were formed on top to complete the transistor. To facilitate vertical integration, an SU-8 photoresist layer was spin-coated as the interlayer dielectric and crosslinked via ultraviolet exposure, followed by the ALD of an additional Al2O3 buffering layer to prepare for the deposition of the second tier. Fabricating the second-tier p-type transistor followed an identical process to that used in preparing the first-tier n-type transistor. Once both transistor layers were fabricated, thermal release tape was applied to the entire structure, and the wafer was immersed in deionized H2O to dissolve the sacrificial layer, enabling the flexible device to detach. The device, now attached to the tape, was transferred onto the target substrate and annealed on a hot plate to release the tape and complete integration. Vertical electrical interconnections between the two stacked layers were established by via-hole formation at each pixel using pulsed laser ablation, followed by filling with Ag ink to ensure interconnection. All fabrication processes were conducted at ≤150 °C, rendering the protocol compatible with flexible and stretchable substrates.

Characterizations

A sample for use in cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy was prepared using a focused ion beam (Helios Nanolab 650, FEI) and measured using a JEOL JEM-2100F (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). The electrical characteristics of the transistors and M3D circuits were measured using a Keithley 4200 parameter analyzer in an N2-filled glovebox. ne and nh were extracted54,55 using n = (IDSL)/(qWVDSμ), where L is the channel length, W is the channel width, μ is the field-effect mobility, and q is the electron charge. The carrier concentration was calculated at VGS = 0 V.

Flexible demonstration experiments

All wearable demonstration procedures involving human participation complied with the institutional ethical guidelines of POSTECH (Pohang University of Science and Technology). A healthy adult volunteer (female, age 34) participated in the experiments shown in Figs. 1d and 4a to demonstrate the conformal adhesion and flexibility of the fabricated devices. The devices were only attached to the skin surface without any electrical stimulation, sensing, or physiological measurement, and thus posed no physical or psychological risk. The participant provided informed consent for participation.
