Jiaqi Yao, Ding Liu*

Abstract
Matrix multiplication is a fundamental classical computing operation whose efficiency becomes a major challenge at scale, especially for machine learning applications. Quantum computing, with its inherent parallelism and exponential storage capacity, offers a potential solution to these limitations. This work presents a quantum kernel-based matrix multiplication algorithm (QKMM) that achieves an asymptotically optimal computational complexity of O ⁡ ( N 2 ​ log 2 ​ N ) O(N^{2}\log_{2}N) , outperforming the classical optimal complexity of O ⁡ ( N 2.371552 ) O(N^{2.371552}) , where N N denotes the matrix dimension. Through noiseless and noisy quantum simulation experiments, we demonstrate that the proposed algorithm not only exhibits superior theoretical efficiency but also shows practical advantages in runtime performance and stability.

2.1
QKMM Achieves a Level of Asymptotic Optimality and its Comparison with Classic Matrix Multiplication

In classical matrix multiplication, computational complexity is conventionally measured by counting the number of multiplication operations. In this framework, the known upper bound for complexity is
O(N^{3})
, while the theoretical lower bound is
O(N^{2})
. Standard matrix multiplication attains the upper bound
O(N^{3})
, and despite extensive efforts—as surveyed in the Introduction—no classical algorithm has reached the lower bound
O(N^{2})
. However, for quantum matrix multiplication, the numerical multiplication operation is no longer a suitable metric as such discrete operations are not clearly separable within quantum circuits. Instead, we adopt basic quantum gates—the elementary operations in quantum algorithms—as the fundamental unit for complexity analysis. These gates correspond to classical logic gates in computational systems, offering a natural and consistent basis for comparison. Moreover, quantum gates operate at a finer computational granularity than classical multiplication, reflecting a more fundamental level of resource accounting in quantum information processing.

Within this framework, our quantum matrix multiplication algorithm achieves a complexity of
O(N^{2}log_{2}N)
. The principal theoretical advance of this result is the demonstration of asymptotic optimality: as the matrix dimension grows, the algorithm approaches
O(N^{2})
scaling—a bound that remains unattained by the best-known classical counterparts. In Fig.
1
, we compare the complexity of our algorithm against several prominent classical methods and furnish a concise proof of its asymptotic optimality.

For any
N>1
, there exists a function
x=\frac{\ln\log_{2}N}{\ln N}
such that

\log N=N^{x}

(1)

When
N\to\infty
, applying L’Hôpital’s rule yields

\displaystyle\underset{N\rightarrow\infty}{\lim}x

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\ln\log_{2}N}{\ln N}

(2)

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\frac{1}{\log_{2}N}\cdot\frac{1}{\ln 2\cdot N}}{\frac{1}{N}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\ln 2\cdot\log_{2}N}

\displaystyle=0

Consequently, for the complexity
O(N^{2}\log N)
, there exists an asymptotically equivalent representation:

N^{2}\log_{2}N=N^{2+x}=n^{2+o(1)}

(3)

Thus, we rigorously prove that the quantum kernel-based matrix multiplication algorithm with complexity
O(N^{2}\log_{2}N)
can asymptotically approach the theoretical lower bound of
O(N^{2})
.

As the current quantum hardware remains insufficient to execute full-scale matrix multiplication, we performed large-scale simulations under both noiseless and noisy conditions on classical computers. While these simulations cannot capture the theoretical runtime advantage of quantum over classical algorithms, they serve to validate the intrinsic parallel structure and scalability of our QKMM design relative to other quantum approaches. 2.3
Simulation with Noisy model

Despite the potential of quantum computing to transform key tasks in numerical linear algebra, its practical realization in the current Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era is significantly hampered by quantum noise. To rigorously evaluate the operational feasibility and robustness of the QKMM framework, we performed comprehensive simulation experiments that incorporated a realistic spectrum of hardware-level noise parameters. All parameters—including the energy relaxation time (
T_{1}
), the dephasing time (
T_{2}
), and the gate error rates—were constrained to the ranges reported for a representative superconducting quantum processor from Origin Quantum Computing Technology Co., Ltd. We quantify computational errors introduced by noise using fidelity and mean error as correctness metrics.

Our simulations were executed using the noise simulator within Origin Quantum Lab, configured with the following physical parameters: average
T_{1}=50\mu s
, average
T_{2}=30\mu s
, average single-qubit gate fidelity of
99.8\%
, and average two-qubit ECR (Echoed Cross-Resonance) gate fidelity of
97.5\%
.

Noise Effects on Vector Inner Product (V
2
V).
The Hadamard Test and Swap Test serve as benchmark algorithms to evaluate multidimensional inner product operation fidelity and mean error across noise conditions, with detailed results presented in Fig.
3
. Standardized input datasets comprise 500 dimension-matched sets of randomly sampled normalized vectors for robust statistical analysis. Quantum circuit implementations performed 10,000 independent measurement shots, maintaining fidelity errors within ±0.01 bounds.

Experimental results reveal that in noiseless conditions, the quantum kernel-based inner product algorithm maintains consistently high fidelity across increasing dimensions with negligible decay, demonstrating superior computational reliability. Comparative analysis shows both Hadamard and Swap Test algorithms operate at relatively high fidelity levels, yet consistently underperform the quantum kernel approach. These conventional methods exhibit progressive performance degradation with increasing dimensionality. Under single-source noise conditions, all three algorithms show dimension-dependent fidelity reduction, though the quantum kernel method displays more gradual attenuation and slower error growth, indicating stronger noise resilience. In combined noise environments with complex interference patterns, all algorithms suffer significant performance deterioration. Each exhibits rapid fidelity collapse with increasing dimensionality, underscoring the critical impact of compounded noise on quantum computational accuracy.

In summary, the quantum kernel-based algorithm demonstrates superior fidelity preservation and slower error accumulation when simulating inner product operations under combined T1, T2, and gate noise, particularly during high-dimensional scaling. These results indicate its stronger potential for practical implementation in noisy quantum systems and high-dimensional applications. Further research should explore its universal optimization capabilities across diverse quantum hardware noise models.

Noise Effects on Matrix Multiplication.
The QMM algorithm’s correctness and effectiveness were validated through fidelity and mean error analysis of matrix multiplication under noise for dimensions 2, 4 and 8, with detailed results
presented in Fig.
4
. The study employed 500 randomly generated row-normalized matrices of varying dimensions as standardized inputs. All quantum circuits executed 1,000 independent measurement shots, with computation accuracy requiring each resulting matrix element to maintain strict ±0.1 error bounds.

Our results indicate that at low matrix dimensions (2 to 4), the QKMM algorithm shows strong robustness. Even with multiple concurrent noise sources, it retains high fidelity (
>0.95
) while maintaining a low average error, supporting its practical viability for small-scale quantum tasks. However, when scaled to dimension 8, the influence of different noise sources diverges significantly. The environmental noise, such as
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
relaxation, is negligible, with the fidelity staying above 0.95. In contrast, quantum gate noise becomes the dominant factor in initial performance loss, reducing the fidelity to 0.88. This suggests that in deeper circuits arising from higher dimensions, operational inaccuracy from logic gate errors limits algorithmic fidelity earlier than environmental decoherence does.

The most pronounced challenge occurs under combined noise, where fidelity drops sharply to 0.41 at dimension 8—substantially lower than under any individual noise source. This marked decline demonstrates that in complex circuits, multiple types of noise interact nonlinearly rather than additively, inducing coherent error effects that cause computational accuracy to deteriorate rapidly beyond a certain scale.

Our analysis reveals that the performance degradation at higher dimensions stems mainly from the rapid growth in circuit depth and gate count, which is a direct consequence of the resource-intensive amplitude encoding scheme employed in QKMM to map high-dimensional data. Future efforts should therefore focus on designing more efficient data encoding algorithms that preserve the advantages of QKMM while reducing physical gate overhead—a crucial step toward enabling reliable, large-scale quantum matrix operations on near-term NISQ hardware.

3
Discussion

This work proposes a quantum algorithm for matrix multiplication that achieves an asymptotically optimal complexity of
O(N^{2}\log_{2}N)
, representing a significant reduction compared to the current classical optimum of
O(N^{2.371552})
. Rigorous simulations covering both noiseless conditions and realistic noise models demonstrate the operational reliability of the algorithm. This study explicitly defines the complexity of quantum matrix multiplication in terms of the basic quantum gate complexity. This approach unifies the currently ambiguous and inconsistent complexity measurement benchmarks in the field, facilitating the establishment of a unified evaluation standard. Based on this benchmark, future research can further explore more optimal asymptotic algorithms beyond
O(N^{2}\log_{2}N)
and even investigate whether the theoretical lower bound of the complexity of quantum matrix multiplication can achieve a breakthrough beyond
O(N^{2})
.

Furthermore, matrix multiplication is a typical compute-intensive, low-level operation. It is not only the most critical and computationally demanding module in current AI models but also a fundamental operation underlying nearly all scientific computing tasks. Consequently, quantum matrix multiplication not only offers a novel technological pathway to accelerate the training of AI models but also fundamentally provides quantum acceleration solutions for a vast array of scientific computing problems. Further, building upon quantum matrix multiplication to explore quantum acceleration for compute-intensive problems holds broader significance – a domain still largely uncharted, which we term ”quantum-intensive computing”. Amidst the enormous demands of today’s massive AI model training and scientific computations, quantum computing needs to demonstrate clear prospects for acceleration in compute-intensive tasks, this will fundamentally bolster confidence in the future of quantum artificial intelligence.

4.1
Vect to Vect(V
2
V)

The Vect to Vect (V
2
V) is designed to compute the inner product modulus of two vectors, with its core algorithm rooted in the quantum kernel-based inner product modulus method
9
. Classical kernel functions are designed to measure the similarity between two input vectors
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
in the feature space. Specifically, this similarity is defined as the inner product of the vectors
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
in the feature space:

\kappa\left(x,y\right)=\left<\phi\left(x\right),\phi\left(y\right)\right>

Analogously, the quantum kernel method defines the kernel function via the inner product of quantum states. For instance, given any two normalized vectors
|\mathbf{a}\rangle,|\mathbf{b}\rangle\subseteq R^{N}
, amplitude encoding is used to map these normalized vectors into quantum basis states
|0\rangle^{\otimes n}
. The encoded quantum states can be expressed as:

|\mathbf{a}\rangle=U_{a}|0\rangle^{\otimes n},\quad|\mathbf{b}\rangle=V_{b}|0\rangle^{\otimes n}

(4)

where
n=\lceil\log N\rceil
,
U_{a}
and
V_{b}
are the unitary transformations corresponding to the amplitude encoding of
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
, respectively. The quantum circuit for this process is illustrated in Fig.
5
(a), and the evolution of the circuit can be described as follows:

\displaystyle|\varphi_{1}\rangle=U_{a}|0\rangle^{\otimes n}

(5)

\displaystyle|\varphi_{2}\rangle={V_{b}}^{-1}|\varphi_{1}\rangle={V_{b}}^{-1}U_{a}|0\rangle^{\otimes n}

(6)

then the inner product is given by:

\begin{split}\left<\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{b}\right>&=\left<\mathbf{a}|{V_{b}}{V_{b}}^{-1}|\mathbf{b}\right>=\left<\mathbf{a}|{V_{b}}|0\right>^{\otimes n}\\
&=\left<\varphi_{2}\right.\left|{V_{b}}^{\dagger}V_{b}\left|\left.0\right>^{\otimes n}\right.\right.=\left<\varphi_{2}\right.\left|\left.0\right>^{\otimes n}\right.=c_{0}\end{split}

(7)

Here,
c_{0}
represents the coefficient of the superposition state
|\varphi_{2}\rangle
in the ground state
|0\rangle^{\otimes n}
. Therefore, when we perform a measurement on the entire circuit, the obtained probability is
|c_{0}|^{2}
.

Vect to Matrix(V
2
M)

The Vector to Matrix (V
2
M) is an extension of the V
2
V algorithm, designed to compute matrix-vector multiplication. The core idea of V
2
M involves the use of an index register, where each row of the matrix is treated as an individual vector and encoded into a unitary matrix. These encoded unitary matrices are then connected to the index register via multiple control qubits, facilitating the storage of the transformation matrix. Following this, the inverse matrix encoding of an arbitrary vector is applied, enabling the computation of matrix-vector multiplication. V2M leverages the principles of quantum computation and quantum kernels to achieve efficient linear transformations in high-dimensional spaces.

Let
A
be an
N\times N
matrix and
\mathbf{x}
be an
N
-dimensional column vector. Their product results in an
N
-dimensional column vector
\mathbf{b}
, defined as:

\mathbf{b}=A\mathbf{x},

(8)

where the
i
-th element of
\mathbf{b}
, denoted as
b_{i}
, is the dot product of the
i
-th row of matrix
A
and the vector
\mathbf{x}
. Mathematically, this is expressed as
b_{i}=\left<A_{i\cdot}\right.\left|\left.x\right>\right.
.

Since the matrix inversion operation involved in modulus-based inner product calculations consumes significant time and computational resources, we have designed a quantum circuit, as illustrated in Fig.
5
(b), to utilize resources more efficiently. This circuit decomposes the transformation matrix row-wise, treating each row as a single vector. By doing so, we only need to add
\lceil\log N\rceil
index auxiliary qubits and
N
multi-controlled amplitude encoding operations to compute the matrix-vector multiplication result within a single quantum circuit. The specific evolution process of the circuit is as follows:

\displaystyle\left|\left.\varphi_{0}\right>=\right.\left|\left.0\right>\right.^{\otimes n}\left|\left.0\right>\right.^{\otimes n}

(9)

\displaystyle\left.|\varphi_{1}\right>=\left(\small{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\small{\frac{n}{2}}}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}{\left|\left.i\right>\right.}\left|\left.0\right>\right.^{\otimes n}

(10)

\displaystyle\left.|\varphi_{2}\right>=\left(\small{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\small{\frac{n}{2}}}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}{\left|\left.i\right>\otimes U^{-1}_{x}\right.}\left|\left.0\right>\right.^{\otimes n}

(11)

\displaystyle\left.|\varphi_{3}\right>=\left(\small{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\small{\frac{n}{2}}}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}{\left|\left.i\right>\otimes\right.}V_{A_{i}}U^{-1}_{x}\left|\left.0\right>\right.^{\otimes n}

(12)

Here,
n=\lceil\log N\rceil
and
U_{x}
and
V_{A_{i}}
represent the unitary matrices for data encoding, denoted as
\left|\left.x\right>=U_{x}\right.\left|\left.0\right>\right.^{\otimes n}
and
\left|\left.A_{i\cdot}\right>=V_{A_{i}}\right.\left|\left.0\right>\right.^{\otimes n}
, respectively. Following the same logic as in Eq

, the inner product
\left<A_{i\cdot}\right.\left|\left.x\right>\right.

|i\rangle|0\rangle^{\otimes n}

Matrix to Matrix(M

The Matrix to Matrix (M
2
M) represents an advancement of the V
2
M algorithm, aiming to achieve a quantum implementation of matrix multiplication.
Classical matrix multiplication is a fundamental operation in linear algebra, where each element of a row in the first matrix is multiplied by the corresponding element of a column in the second matrix, and the results are summed. Let A and B be N × N square matrices, and the product of these matrices, denoted as

C=A\times B

(13)

each element
c_{ij}
of the resulting matrix
C
is calculated as the dot product of the
i
-th row of matrix
A
and the
j
-th column of matrix
B
, that is,
c_{ij}=\left<A_{i\cdot}\right.\left|\left.B_{\cdot j}\right>\right.

By reviewing the latest advancements in quantum matrix multiplication, reference
21
proposed a method for implementing matrix multiplication by stacking Hadamard test-based linear mapping circuits. This approach unifies matrix multiplication computation within a single circuit. However, it has several drawbacks, such as the excessive consumption of qubits and quantum gates due to circuit stacking, particularly the redundancy in data encoding, which hinders the full utilization of quantum circuit parallelism. Therefore, to better leverage the parallel advantages of quantum circuits and minimize quantum resource consumption, we propose the V
2
M algorithm. The specific method involves introducing two index registers, treating each row/column of the matrix as a single vector, and encoding them into unitary matrices. Each encoded unitary matrix is then linked to the corresponding index register through multi-controlled qubits, achieving the goal of matrix multiplication.

Specifically, matrix
A
is decomposed row-wise into individual rows, with each row treated as a single vector, while matrix
B
is decomposed column-wise into individual columns, with each column treated as a single vector. By doing so, only
2\lceil\log N\rceil
additional index auxiliary qubits and
2N
multi-controlled amplitude encoding operations are required to compute the matrix multiplication result within a single quantum circuit. The quantum circuit is illustrated in Fig.
5
(c), and its specific evolution process is as follows:
