CalWildFire: A high-resolution in situ dataset for wildfire analysis and modeling in the Mediterranean region
Authors: Alessio De Rango, Fabrizio Lo Scudo, Luca Furnari, Alfonso Senatore, Donato D’Ambrosio, Giuseppe Mendicino, Gianluigi Greco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWildfires pose a persistent threat to ecosystems and human safety, particularly in Mediterranean regions where rising temperatures and prolonged droughts exacerbate ignition and spread conditions. Effective risk assessment and forecasting require datasets that integrate meteorological, environmental, and anthropogenic factors with high spatial and temporal fidelity. The CalWildFire dataset contributes to addressing this need by providing a comprehensive collection of wildfire and meteorological data for the Calabria region (southern Italy), derived from in situ ground-based observations managed by the regional environmental agency (ARPACAL). Unlike existing datasets that rely primarily on reanalysis or satellite-derived products, CalWildFire includes 11 years (2008–2018) of daily validated direct meteorological measurements and field-based burned area records, enabling precise analyses of fire occurrence and behavior. The dataset also provides fire weather indices, recalculated from ground data following the Canadian Fire Weather Index methodology. CalWildFire represents a unique, high-quality resource for advancing wildfire prediction, and risk mapping in Mediterranean and similarly fire-prone environments.

Background & Summary

Fires have been a defining feature of natural ecosystems since well before the appearance of Homo sapiens and have been recognized as fundamental ecosystem services1. The main ecological benefits of wildfires include: contribution to nutrient cycling and soil enrichment2; reduction of hazardous fuels, as practiced in prescribed burns3; pest and disease control4; habitat improvement and increased biodiversity by contributing to the creation of heterogeneous landscapes5, and to species regeneration6. On the other hand, inherent wildfire risks, closely linked to poor or inadequate forest and natural landscape management, pose numerous challenges to human and environmental well-being. These include the loss of biodiversity and ecosystem degradation resulting from the destruction of flora, fauna, and soil7; the exacerbation of hydrogeological hazards due to soil impermeabilization and decreased slope stability8; and the increased release of toxic substances that pose significant risks to human health9; and, in a vicious cycle, further increase and acceleration of global warming by contributing to the total emissions of greenhouse gases10.

Indeed, climate change dramatically contributes to an increased likelihood of the occurrence of wildfires, particularly in regions where rising temperatures are accompanied by prolonged periods of drought, such as the Mediterranean basin11,12. In such conditions, desiccated vegetation serves as a fuel source, thereby facilitating both the ignition and the rapid spread of fires. Effective risk management strategies can mitigate the adverse impacts associated with increased wildfire hazards. For example, in the European area of the Mediterranean region, the overall trend in the incidence of forest fires has been declining in recent years13,14.

However, the European Commission has warned that the projected intensification of summer weather conditions can render wildfire protection programs and mitigation activities, originally designed for ’normal’ climatic conditions, ineffective15. This concern is particularly acute for regions such as Calabria, located in the southern part of the Italian peninsula, which are highly susceptible to prolonged droughts16 and the broader impacts of climate change17. Moreover, unlike other Mediterranean areas that exhibit a declining trend in wildfire activity, Calabria does not follow this pattern18,19, and contributes significantly to national greenhouse gas emissions originating from forested land.

Enhanced wildfire risk management fundamentally depends on accurate forecasting of both the likelihood of fire occurrence and its potential for propagation. Given the critical role of meteorological factors, fire weather indices, which are metrics derived solely from atmospheric variables, are widely employed. Among these, the Canadian Fire Weather Index (FWI)20 is the most widely recognized21 and operationally implemented. Since 2007, the FWI has been adopted as the standard metric for fire danger assessment within the European Forest Fire Information System (EFFIS). Currently, forecasting systems based on the FWI are operational on various spatial scales, from continental-level assessments (e.g., for Europe22) to regional applications (e.g., for Calabria23, where it is referred to under the variable name Pericolosità Incendi - Wildfire Hazard).

Although fire weather indices have repeatedly demonstrated their effectiveness in identifying potentially hazardous conditions, they exhibit inherent limitations in quantitatively forecasting burned areas. This limitation arises from their partial consideration of predisposing factors and their general exclusion of triggering factors. Among predisposing factors, not only land cover characteristics, but also geomorphological features contribute to an area’s susceptibility to wildfires. Identification and forecasting of trigger factors pose an even greater challenge, as these are predominantly associated with human behavior. Natural ignition sources, such as lightning, are relatively rare in many fire-prone regions, making anthropogenic activities the primary drivers of fire ignition. However, such human-induced factors cannot be adequately captured using conventional physical modeling approaches, which are generally designed to simulate natural processes.

To address this gap, several publicly available wildfire datasets have been developed that incorporate anthropogenic influences alongside environmental variables. For instance, Kondylatos et al.24 compiled the FireCube dataset comprising 90 variables related to the occurrence and spread of wildfires, at a spatial resolution of 1 km × 1 km, spanning the period from 2009 to 2021. The study area covers approximately 1,253 km × 983 km, encompassing parts of the Balkan Peninsula, western Turkey, and Greece. Anthropogenic factors in this dataset include variables such as distance to roads, proximity to waterways, and annual population density. Furthermore, the dataset integrates daily meteorological data from ERA5-Land, satellite-derived variables from MODIS, soil moisture indices from the European Drought Observatory, topographic information (elevation and slope) from Copernicus EU-DEM, and ten fractional land cover variables derived from the Copernicus CORINE Land Cover inventory.

In continuity with recent advancements in wildfire modeling, the dataset proposed in this work adopts a proven methodological framework but distinguishes itself through the integration of high-resolution, in situ meteorological data. Specifically, CalWildFire builds upon the approach developed by Kondylatos et al.24, while introducing a crucial difference in the source of meteorological and wildfire inputs.

Regarding weather and climate data, whereas the FireCube dataset incorporates satellite-derived data and reanalysis outputs from ERA5-Land25, the CalWildFire dataset uses ground-based observations from meteorological stations. This refinement is particularly significant for applications that demand high spatial accuracy and empirical validation, as in situ data provide direct measurements of environmental conditions at precise geographic locations. ERA5-Land is a reanalysis product derived from the broader ERA5 dataset26, generated by a numerical weather prediction (NWP) system. Specifically, ERA5-Land applies a land surface model (H-TESSEL)27 to simulate soil-vegetation-atmosphere interactions at enhanced spatial resolution. This model assimilates a wide range of observational data to produce a physically consistent and spatiotemporally continuous representation of past atmospheric conditions. However, as with all reanalysis products, the outputs are model-derived estimates rather than direct environmental measurements28. In contrast, observed meteorological data—or in situ data—are obtained from electronic sensors deployed at specific geographic locations. These measurements (e.g., temperature, precipitation, soil moisture) provide precise and temporally accurate records of environmental conditions at the point of observation. As such, they are considered the “gold standard” for accuracy and are often used to validate model-based products and satellite observations. However, despite their high fidelity, in situ measurements present challenges related to spatial representation. As point-based records, their spatial representativeness varies with several parameters and is not constant over time, making interpolation into spatially continuous rasters non-trivial. What introduces a trade-off between the superior accuracy of in situ data and the spatial coverage offered by model-based or satellite-derived datasets is the density and spatial distribution of the measurement stations, which in our study area was proven to be sufficiently high for different hydrometeorological applications28–30.

Another remarkable difference characterizing the CalWildFire dataset compared to Firecube and other existing datasets is the fact that geographical and other relevant information on the indexed wildfires come from direct surveys. CalWildFire contains 8,609 wildfires detected in the field by wildfire prevention and suppression agencies, namely the Carabinieri Forestali Calabria. The majority of these wildfires are not detectable by remote sensing techniques (e.g., the MODIS burned area product) because their limited spatial extent falls below the sensor’s detection threshold. This represents a significant added value of CalWildFire, as it captures small-scale fire events that are systematically underrepresented in satellite-derived datasets, thereby providing a more comprehensive and spatially resolved characterization of wildfire occurrence.

Meteorological data for the CalWildFire dataset were provided by the Centro Funzionale Multirischi, a devoted division of the Calabrian Regional Agency for the Protection of the Environment (ARPACAL), which is responsible for managing and maintaining the comprehensive network of weather stations dedicated to civil protection monitoring and alert systems throughout the region. The point-based observed meteorological data were first subjected to a quality control procedure designed to detect and exclude unrealistic or erroneous measurements, including out-of-range values and sensor malfunctions, and were subsequently spatially interpolated onto a regular 100 m × 100 m grid encompassing the entire Calabrian region. Regarding wildfire-burned area data, it has been crucial to systematize and harmonize this information, which was retrieved and recorded using notably heterogeneous and inconsistent procedures for most of the analyzed period (i.e., 2008–2015), when standardized protocols were not yet firmly established.

This study represents a collaborative effort among multiple institutions, including academia (University of Calabria), regional forestry management authorities (Calabria Region, Forestry Department), wildfire prevention and suppression agencies (Carabinieri Forestali Calabria), and environmental monitoring entities (Centro Funzionale Multirischi, ARPACAL). Strictly related to the test case addressed, it constitutes a step toward enhancing the resilience of Calabria’s forest ecosystems in the context of climate change and its adverse impacts on natural resources and human activities. More generally, this comprehensive dataset, which integrates high-accuracy, spatially distributed, and geolocated ground observations of both wildfires and the weather conditions that predispose their ignition, aims to complement and enhance existing research datasets by providing the scientific community with a new resource for understanding the dynamics and mechanisms that favor fire ignition. In particular, due to its structure, the dataset is tailored for developing classification models for wildfire risk forecasting and can therefore support all researchers working at the forefront of AI and environmental issues. For practitioners and managers, in addition to the indirect benefits of supporting wildfire forecasting and prevention research, the dataset represents a versatile resource that offers a practical example of how to store and organize a range of available wildfire-related information, making it immediately usable for environmental informatics.

To sum up, the CalWildFire dataset distinguishes itself from typical wildfire datasets31,32 through four key features. First, it uses in situ meteorological observations from ground stations rather than satellite or reanalysis products, ensuring higher spatiotemporal accuracy. Second, burned areas are based on field observations by regional authorities instead of satellite algorithms, providing more reliable ground-truth data. Third, FWI components were recalculated from ground-based data following the official Canadian Forest Fire Danger Rating System methodology. Fourth, its structure and flexibility allow it to be easily enlarged, incorporating dynamically recent observation data. To this end, an automated pipeline for acquisition, validation, spatial interpolation, and construction of the required multidimensional data structure, allowing the dataset to be updated with the most recent years of data. The dataset is available at33.

Methods

The CalWildFire dataset covers the Calabria region in southern Italy, a peninsula of approximately 15,000 km2 extending predominantly along the north-south axis and bordered by approximately 800 km of coastline. The region is characterized by complex and steep orography (Fig. 1a), with flat terrain accounting for only 9% of the total area (Fig. 1b). This topographic variability, combined with Calabria’s geographic position, leads to a heterogeneous precipitation regime, with annual rainfall ranging from 600 mm to 1,500 mm34. Summers are typically dry, with high temperatures contributing to increased wildfire occurrence35.

Forests cover approximately 40.6% of the territory (612,932 ha), making Calabria one of the most densely forested regions in Italy. The area includes three national parks and one regional park, and the forest composition is highly heterogeneous36. The dominant tree species include beech (12.6%), pine (12.2%), chestnut (11.3%), oak (14.6%), holm oak (7.1%), and other deciduous species (5.9%)37. For the purposes of this work, the Calabrian area was discretized into a regular grid of 100 × 100 m2 cells, resulting in a total of 1, 403, 027 terrestrial cells potentially subject to wildfire occurrence. The temporal horizon of the dataset spans 11 consecutive fire seasons, from 2008 to 2018, with each fire season defined as the period between April 1 and October 31.

The land cover classification in CalWildFire is based on the official regional dataset CUT - Carta Usi del Territorio (Land Use Map, Fig. 1d), developed by the Calabria Regional Authority, which follows the methodology of the CORINE Land Cover (CLC) project38. The CUT classification provides additional granularity by extending the CLC hierarchy to levels IV and V. It comprises 114 classes derived from multiple sources. The primary source is photo interpretation of aerial orthophotos, supplemented by field surveys, planning instruments and regional and national databases (e.g., landslide inventories, quarry registers, airport records). The original data were provided in vector format and were rasterized onto the adopted grid using the gdal_rasterize utility from the GDAL library (https://gdal.org/en/stable/programs/gdal_rasterize.html).

Wildfire occurrence data were obtained from the Comando Unità Forestali, Ambientali e Agroalimentari dei Carabinieri (Carabinieri Command of Forestry, Environmental and Agri-food Units), the national authority responsible for wildfire monitoring (https://forestazione.regione.calabria.it/gis/ and https://geoportale.regione.calabria.it/dataset). All wildfire data were collected through field surveys. The assigned date corresponds to the detection date of each fire event, regardless of its duration. This represents an inherent limitation stemming from the data collection protocol adopted by the reporting authority. During 2008-2015, only the report date was recorded; although intervention and suppression dates were subsequently introduced, they were not systematically compiled in later records. Therefore, the detection date was adopted as the sole reference date across the entire dataset. Between 2008 and 2018, 9,076 wildfire events were recorded, corresponding to a cumulative burned area of 120,431 ha (Fig. 2)39. The year with the highest impact was 2017, during which approximately 32,055 ha were affected across nearly 1,500 distinct ignition events19,40. The CalWildFire dataset, archiving only wildfires occurring in the April-October fire season, includes 8,609 fires (95% of the total number), with a total burned area of 116,880 ha (97.1% of the total). During the 2017 fire season, the overall burned area was 31,577 ha (98.5% of the total area burned that year), resulting from 1,394 fire events (about 93.6% of the total). As with the CUT, the wildfire data has been collected in vector format and rasterized onto the adopted grid using the gdal_rasterize utility from the GDAL library .

Annual burned areas from 2008 to 2018, as recorded in the CalWildFire dataset, visualized on a multi-colored map, with each year represented by a different color.

The variables included in the dataset are summarized in Table 1 and are categorized into static variables, those invariant over time, and dynamic variables, which vary throughout the study period at a daily scale. For static variables, the Digital Terrain Model (DTM) used in this work was provided by the Italian Military Geographic Institute (IGM) and originally featured a spatial resolution of 20 m. It was resampled to the 100 m target grid (Fig. 1a) using the gdalwarp utility from the GDAL library, with the average resampling method (-r average), which computes each output cell value as the mean of all contributing source pixels. The topographic derivatives, specifically aspect and slope, were calculated from the DTM using standard GDAL algorithms (https://gdal.org/en/stable/programs/gdaldem.html, Fig. 1b,c).

The following paragraphs provide detailed descriptions of each methodological step.

The meteorological monitoring network operated by ARPACAL comprises 159 precipitation gauges, 104 air temperature stations, 30 relative humidity sensors, and 29 wind speed sensors (Fig. 3a). Before interpolating, the collected data were validated by excluding unrealistic values, with limits set on each variable (e.g., no negative precipitation, temperature in the range −10 to 50 °C, RH between 0% and 100%, wind speed in the range 0–50 m/s). Afterwards, a linear relationship with the nearest station for the corresponding variable was used to reconstruct the data gaps. Overall, less than 0.5% of the total data series required reconstruction. Regarding spatial interpolation of the variables, precipitation was first calculated as the cumulative total over the preceding 24 hours and then interpolated onto the 100 m grid using inverse distance weighting (IDW) with a power coefficient of 2. The high network density (11.33 stations per 1,000 km2) supports the suitability of this interpolation method for the study area41. From precipitation maps, a further variable was derived representing the number of consecutive antecedent dry days (i.e., days with zero precipitation) per cell throughout the fire season, to capture the influence of previous dry conditions. The air temperature at 2 m above ground, sampled at noon, was interpolated using a hybrid method that combined cubic spline interpolation with elevation-dependent linear regression, yielding a daily average coefficient of determination R2 of 0.84, thereby enabling topographic correction. Relative humidity was processed using a similar approach, with the regression model incorporating temperature dependency to better represent diurnal variability, yielding a daily average R2 of 0.91. Finally, the wind speed at 10 m height was also interpolated employing cubic spline interpolation. Examples of daily spatially distributed outputs are shown in Fig. 3b–e, with the related stations’ locations overlaid.

As a further contribution, the CalWildFire dataset incorporates a set of large-scale Climate Pattern indices known to influence wildfire dynamics42. Specifically, four teleconnection indices were included: the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO43), computed as the normalized sea-level pressure difference between Gibraltar and Reykjavik (https://crudata.uea.ac.uk/cru/data/nao/nao.dat); the Mediterranean Oscillation Index (MOI44), defined as the normalized pressure difference between Algiers and Cairo (https://crudata.uea.ac.uk/cru/data/moi/moi1.output.dat); the Niño 3.4 index45, computed as the area-averaged sea surface temperature (SST) anomalies over the region 5°N–5°S, 170°–120 °W, using the 1981–2010 climatology from the HadISST1.1 dataset (https://psl.noaa.gov/data/timeseries/month/data/nino34.long.anom.data); and the Western Mediterranean Oscillation Index (WeMOi46), defined as the standardized pressure difference between Padua (Italy) and San Fernando, Cádiz (Spain) (https://crudata.uea.ac.uk/cru/data/moi/Web_WeMOi-2020.txt). All indices are spatially uniform across the study area and vary at the monthly timescale, except for the MOI, which is provided at daily resolution.

The Fire Weather Index (FWI) and its associated components were computed on the same 100 m resolution grid used for the other dynamic variables. Following the standard FWI methodology, the input variables were aggregated at the daily scale under a worst-case scenario assumption, whereby environmental conditions are represented by their noon values. The FWI system and its intermediate indices were calculated using the cffdrs R package (Canadian Forest Fire Danger Rating System, version 1.8.2047, available at https://www.rdocumentation.org/packages/cffdrs/versions/1.8.20), which produces six key indices at the same horizontal resolution as the meteorological inputs: Fine Fuel Moisture Code (FFMC), Duff Moisture Code (DMC), Drought Code (DC), Initial Spread Index (ISI), Buildup Index (BUI), Fire Weather Index (FWI). The first three indices (FFMC, DMC, DC) estimate moisture content in fine surface fuels, loosely compacted organic layers, and deep compact organic matter, respectively. The remaining indices (ISI, BUI, and FWI) are relative (unitless) indicators of the potential of fire behavior, which captures the rate of spread (ISI), the amount of fuel available for combustion (BUI), and fire intensity (FWI). Especially for some variables, namely DMC and BUI, they highlight that the influence of the fuel model derived from the land use classification can provide sharp transitions in the values. The Calabria grid was subdivided into non-overlapping square patches, each composed of 25 × 25 cells (equivalent to 2.5 × 2.5 km2). A total of 2,479 patches were identified: 1,828 of these contained at least one fire event during the observation period, while 651 patches showed no fire activity. Patch selection was guided by a single criterion: at least 30% of the patch surface had to consist of land cells. Importantly, no constraint was placed on the position of burned cells within each patch; that is, a potential fire cell may occur anywhere within the patch. Therefore, compared with a dataset in which the target event consistently occurs at the center of the patch, any statistical model will face a more challenging task when using CalWildFire.

Following the methodology outlined by48,49, this work adopts a spatio-temporal data model structured as a cube. This model features a spatial resolution of 2.5 by 2.5 km2 and spans a temporal range of 10 days. For each cube, all static and dynamic variables are gathered daily and stored in two distinct files.

Due to the nature of wildfire occurrence, the distribution of burned versus unburned cells in the gridded data is inherently imbalanced, with fire-affected cells representing only a small fraction of the total. Imbalance in this context is a well-known challenge in data-driven modeling24,48,49, as it can lead learning algorithms to favor the majority class, unburned areas, while failing to accurately detect the minority class, which is of primary importance. To mitigate this problem and improve the model’s ability to learn meaningful patterns associated with fire occurrence, a sampling strategy was adopted to build a more informative dataset.

Across the full spatio-temporal extent of the dataset, a total of 21,922 data cubes, considering both static and dynamic features, were identified as containing at least one fire occurrence, out of 5,808,297 possible spatio-temporal samples, covering a period of 2,343 days. To reduce the volume of negative samples—while preserving the spatial and temporal variability necessary for effective model training—a targeted sampling strategy was implemented. We began by categorizing the data into two classes: burned and unburned-patches. The burned class includes all patches in which at least one fire event was recorded during the study period, while the unburned class consists of patches that did not experience any documented fire activity throughout the entire temporal window. For each of the 651 unburned-patches, a single date was randomly selected from the set of days in which at least one fire occurred elsewhere in the region. This selection was repeated sixteen times per unburned-patch, resulting in a total of 20,832 negative data cubes.

To sample negative instances from the set of burned patches, a similar strategy was employed. For each year of observation, a single day was randomly selected per patch under the constraint that no fire events occurred on that day or the day immediately following. This approach should guarantee that samples are drawn from meteorologically and seasonally diverse periods, increasing the likelihood that they contain informative patterns, while avoiding excessive redundancy in the dominant class. This procedure resulted in a total of 40,216 additional negative samples.

In addition, a third sampling procedure was implemented to ensure the inclusion of hard-to-predict negative instances, that is, those corresponding to the day immediately preceding a recorded fire event. Thus, for each fire event in the dataset, the data cube corresponding to the same patch on the day prior to the ignition was extracted, provided that no fire occurred on that day. This approach captures pre-ignition conditions that are often difficult for predictive models to distinguish from actual fire days, thereby enhancing the dataset’s value for training robust classifiers. The procedure yielded a total of 21,912 negative samples.

After establishing the sampling strategy for the dataset patches, we can examine the feature distribution. We retained the original values for all variables expressed in physically based units (e.g., m asl for the DTM or degrees for slope and aspect). Land use, however, is indicated through a categorical variable spanning a wide range of discontinuous values. A naive conversion of these values into a compact continuous representation would likely obscure the semantic distinctions among categories. Therefore, a dedicated mapping strategy is needed to facilitate downstream machine-learning tasks. To this end, we aggregated the original categories into 10 classes following the reclassification scheme reported in the supplementary table in the dataset repository, which is also implemented in the Python code distributed alongside CalWildFire.

The distribution of static environmental variables for the sets of burned and never-burned patches (Fig. 6) reveals clear contrasts in their topographic and anthropogenic characteristics. Burned patches tend to occur on steeper terrain (Fig. 6c) and in closer proximity to human-related features, including road networks (Fig. 6e) and urban centers (Fig. 6f). These patterns are consistent with the known influence of human accessibility and landscape gradients on the likelihood of ignition and the dynamics of fire spread. Conversely, never-burned patches are more frequently located at higher elevations, as reflected by the third quartile and whiskers of the Digital Terrain Model (Fig. 6a) boxplot distribution. The aspect (Fig. 6b) exhibits comparable variability across both patch sets, indicating that the aspect of the landscape has a less discriminative influence in this context. Never-burned patches are more frequent with high values of Land Use classes, corresponding to forests (Fig. 6d). Conversely, classes more devoted to agricultural purposes, such as permanent crops or arable land, are more prone to being burned. Overall, these distributions highlight the central role of topography, human proximity, and land use in shaping spatial wildfire patterns across the study area.

The analysis of the distribution of dynamic environmental variables is carried out across the four patch sets: burned, never-burned, burned negatives, and patches one day prior to burning, and illustrated in Fig. 7. Distinct patterns emerge among these groups, reflecting varying meteorological and fuel conditions associated with fire occurrence. Burned patches and those recorded one day before ignition exhibit systematically higher air temperature (Fig. 7a) and lower relative humidity (Fig. 7d) values relative to the other categories, indicating thermal conditions favorable to ignition and fire propagation. Concomitantly, precipitation (Fig. 7c) amounts tend to be lower in these same groups, although not uniformly zero. Similarly, patterns of days without rain (Fig. 7e) mirror those of precipitation, but with opposite implications. Burned and burned one day before patches record the highest number of consecutive precipitation-zero days, resulting in reduced moisture across the vegetation layers that fuel wildfires. This residual variability is likely due to the spatial aggregation of meteorological data at the patch level, which integrates multiple grid cells within each patch. Analysis of the Fire Weather Index (FWI) and its sub-components (i.e., Fine Fuel Moisture Code, Drought Code, Duff Moisture Code, Buildup Index, and Initial Spread Index, (Fig. 7j–o)) further supports this trend. Both burned and one-day-before-burned patches display elevated median values across these indices, suggesting the prevalence of hot, dry, and windy conditions favorable to wildfire ignition and spread. The same analysis, performed at the monthly scale (Fig. 4f–j), further confirms this behaviour for all months, with a general increase in all sub-component values during the hottest and driest months of the fire season. Climate indices reveal distinct patterns for burned and one day before burned patches: these classes are associated with higher MOI values (Fig. 7h), lower NAO index (Fig. 7f), and a slightly elevated WeMOI (Fig. 7i) compared to the other patch classes. The Niño index (Fig. 7g) shows no clear trend in absolute values, but depicts higher distributional variability in burned and pre-burned patches. Technical Validation

Dataset comparison

This subsection presents a multi-level comparison between CalWildFire and several widely used datasets commonly employed in wildfire research. The comparison is structured along three distinct levels, each targeting a different aspect of the database, i.e., meteorological variables, wildfire detection, and FWI calculation, respectively.

Meteorological variables

The first level of analysis focuses on meteorological variables and compares the daily spatially distributed output of CalWildFire against ERA526. ERA5 is a state-of-the-art atmospheric reanalysis produced by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF), designed to provide globally consistent, model-based reconstructions of key climatic variables at a relatively high spatial resolution of 0.25° × 0.25° (approximately 27 km at the latitude of the study area). ERA5 serves as the meteorological backbone of the CEMS - Copernicus Emergency Management Service50,51, which operationally computes the Fire Weather Index (FWI) to support wildfire risk assessment across Europe and beyond.

Figure 8 presents scatterplots comparing CalWildFire and ERA5 meteorological variables at the daily scale, spatially aggregated over the Calabria region. for each of the four variables analyzed (namely, temperature, precipitation, wind speed and relative humidity), both the spatial means (μ), and the spatial standard deviations (σ) are represented. The comparison of spatial means across the four variables reveals strong overall agreement, with Pearson correlation coefficients (R) consistently exceeding 0.83. These results show that CalWildFire is well aligned with the ERA5 reanalysis at the regional scale, supporting the reliability of the meteorological fields underpinning the database. However, the analysis of spatial standard deviations highlights that CalWildFire consistently exhibits greater spatial variability than the coarser ERA5 dataset. Specifically, the time-averaged spatial standard deviation for temperature is 2.88 °C for CalWildFire compared to 1.60 °C for ERA5, for wind speed 3.36 m/s compared to 1.32 m/s, for precipitation 2.11 mm compared to 1.70 mm, and for relative humidity 9.03% compared to 8.27%. This enhanced variability is expected, given the higher spatial resolution of CalWildFire, which better resolves local gradients driven by the complex orography and land-surface heterogeneity of the Calabria region. A further analysis based on the coefficient of variation (CV, defined as the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean) confirms this pattern, particularly for temperature, for which the daily CV of CalWildFire exceeds that of ERA5 in 100% of the days analysed.

Wildfire detection

The second level of analysis addresses wildfire detection, and consists in comparing both the number of fire events and the extent of burned areas provided by the dataset with external, widely used sources. To this end, we employed the satellite-derived MODIS Collection 6.1 burned-area product52, which is among the most extensively adopted datasets in wildfire-related studies53–55. This product provides daily burned-area maps at a horizontal resolution of 500 m × 500 m, offering a spatially explicit record of fire occurrence that serves as a reliable benchmark for evaluating the fire detection capabilities of CalWildFire.

Figure 9a shows the spatial distribution of MODIS-detected burned areas over the period 2008-2018. A qualitative comparison with Fig. 2 immediately reveals that MODIS detects substantially fewer wildfires and a smaller total burned area than CalWildFire. This discrepancy is quantified in Fig. 9b) and Fig. 9c), which compare the total number of fire events and the cumulative burned area, respectively, between CalWildFire and the MODIS satellite-derived product. The comparison highlights significant underdetection by the satellite sensor: over the entire period, more than 35% of the total burned area recorded in CalWildFire is not captured by MODIS, corresponding to 7,122 fire events that remain undetected. The year 2014 represents a particularly striking case: MODIS detected only 2 wildfires for a total burned area of 206 Ha, whereas CalWildFire reported 494 events covering 3,227 Ha. These discrepancies are largely attributable to the well-known limitations of satellite-based fire detection, including the coarse spatial resolution of the MODIS sensor, its fixed overpass times, and the difficulty in detecting small or short-lived fires, especially in regions characterised by complex topography and fragmented land cover such as Calabria.

FWI output

The third and last level of analysis concerns the FWI output and directly compares the index values stored in CalWildFire with those produced by the CEMS system. As noted above, the CEMS-FWI is derived from ERA5 global meteorological fields and is widely used by researchers and operational agencies to assess how fire-prone conditions evolve across both temporal and spatial scales56,57. By comparing the FWI computed from the high-resolution, locally tailored meteorological inputs of CalWildFire with the coarser CEMS-based estimates, the potential added value of finer-scale atmospheric data for characterizing wildfire danger at the regional level is highlighted.

Following the same approach adopted for the meteorological variables, Fig. 10a presents the daily comparison between the spatially averaged FWI over the Calabria region as computed by CalWildFire and by the CEMS system. The two datasets exhibit excellent agreement, with a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.96, confirming that CalWildFire produces FWI estimates that are highly consistent with the operationally established CEMS product at the regional scale. Notably, however, the added value of CalWildFire lies in its ability to capture a greater degree of spatial variability compared to the coarser CEMS product. This is evident in Fig. 10b, which displays the daily spatial standard deviation of the FWI computed over the study area. In 84.6% of the days considered, CalWildFire yields a higher spatial standard deviation than CEMS, indicating that the finer-resolution meteorological inputs employed in CalWildFire allow for a more detailed representation of the spatial heterogeneity in fire danger conditions across the region.

Classification Benchmark

A standardized evaluation protocol has been developed to assess the effectiveness of different classification models on the CalWildFire dataset. The benchmark targets a concrete operational task: given a short spatio-temporal sequence of CalWildFire patches (defined by the dataset’s static and dynamic features), the model must predict whether the patch should be assigned the Wildfire label at the prediction time. This corresponds to a supervised spatio-temporal classification problem in which we learn a mapping from multivariate patch descriptors to a binary decision supporting short-horizon wildfire risk assessment.

We consider two model families to examine which architectural priors are most beneficial in this setting and to quantify the accuracy–efficiency trade-offs that arise in practice. Specifically, we compare generic transformer-based backbones with a recent Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) architecture developed for wildfire-risk prediction. This comparison isolates the role of inductive bias, namely the prior assumptions embedded in a model’s architecture that make some input–output relationships easier to learn than others, independently of the training data.

The two transformer baselines are TimeSformer58 and Video Swin Transformer 3D59. Both rely on self-attention to model spatio-temporal dependencies. TimeSformer employs divided space–time attention to capture long-range interactions efficiently, whereas Video Swin Transformer adopts a hierarchical, window-based attention mechanism that supports multi-scale representation learning. By contrast, CNNs, which have been extensively investigated in prior wildfire studies60–63, encode stronger inductive biases through convolution and pooling operations. Convolutions promote locality, since features are extracted from small neighborhoods, while weight sharing induces translation equivariance and, after pooling, approximate translation invariance. These design choices also imply a degree of spatial stationarity, i.e., the assumption that the same local pattern is informative regardless of its absolute position. Although such biases are often beneficial in vision tasks and can improve sample efficiency, they may be restrictive for (satellite-based) wildfire prediction, where relevant evidence can be spatially dispersed and evolve through time. Because they are not constrained by fixed local receptive fields, transformers can model long-range spatial interactions and temporal dependencies more directly through attention, potentially improving generalization across heterogeneous landscapes and acquisition conditions. More broadly, attention-based architectures impose fewer hand-crafted structural assumptions than CNNs, particularly regarding locality and stationarity. These baselines therefore allow us to test whether a lower-bias, higher-capacity modeling approach is better suited to the heterogeneous, long-range spatio-temporal processes associated with wildfire occurrence, albeit often at greater computational cost. In our implementations, the CNN model described below has approximately 414k parameters, whereas TimeSformer has about 1.16M and Video Swin Transformer about 1.8M.

As a domain-informed CNN baseline, we include LOAN64, a dual 2D–3D CNN framework for wildfire-risk prediction. The 2D branch processes static features (e.g., aspect, slope, road proximity, population density, and proximity to water bodies), while the 3D branch processes dynamic drivers (e.g., temperature, diurnal land surface temperature, soil moisture, relative humidity, wind velocity, 2-meter air temperature, NDVI, atmospheric pressure, 2-meter dewpoint temperature, and precipitation). Two adaptive normalization blocks condition the dynamic stream on static context, allowing local, position-sensitive modulation. LOAN complements the generic transformer baselines by testing whether architectural priors tailored to known wildfire drivers remain advantageous on CalWildFire. Prior evaluations on FireCube65 and NDWS (Next Day Wildfire Spread)66 reported improvements over classical baselines (random forest, XGBoost) and recurrent/conv-recurrent models (LSTM, convLSTM).

We report results on the 2017–2018 test set. These years were selected because they constitute the last two years covered by CalWildFire, enabling a realistic “train-on-past, test-on-future” evaluation aligned with prospective deployment. Moreover, 2017 contains the highest number of wildfire events in CalWildFire, making it a particularly informative stress-test of robustness under high-activity conditions.

Finally, we evaluate two training protocols. The first excludes from training all patches burned one day before the target day, reducing the possibility that models rely on short-term persistence cues and better testing anticipation of new wildfire occurrences or meaningful state changes. The second includes all patches and reflects a more permissive setting where recent burn history is available and can be exploited. Evaluations are performed on both dataset splits, and we report per-class metrics alongside aggregate scores. For interpretability, we treat Wildfire as the positive class and group all other labels into a single Background class.

Experimental settings

In our experiments, we use the implementations of the selected architectures provided by Eddin et al.64, and publicly available at (https://github.com/HakamShams/LOAN). In accordance with the methodology presented by the same authors, we train the models for a total of 40 epochs, maintaining all architectural parameters and hyperparameters consistent with the original study. We employ the same evaluation metrics across the various architectures for the binary classification task. In this context, it is noteworthy that the Intersection over Union (IoU) metric (commonly used in computer vision tasks) is applied here as risk coverage expressed as a semantic layer over the studied region. Furthermore, IoU inherently penalizes both false positives and false negatives more severely than other metrics; with TP, FP, and FN denoting true positives, false positives, and false negatives respectively, IoU is defined as IoU=TPTP+FP+FN. As a result, it tends to yield lower scores compared to the F1 score, particularly in the presence of misclassifications, with F1=2TP2TP+FP+FN. Finally, we also report the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUROC), which summarizes the trade-off between the true positive rate TPR=TPTP+FN and the false positive rate FPR=FPFP+TN as the decision threshold varies. We include AUROC because it is threshold-independent and therefore provides a robust assessment of ranking performance under class imbalance and when operating points (decision thresholds) are not known a priori.

All experiments were carried out using a single node with 96 CPUs, 512 GB RAM, and two NVIDIA V100 GPUs with 16 GB VRAM.

Taken together, these aggregate figures reinforce the intrinsic difficulty of next-day wildfire prediction on CalWildFire. Even with advanced 3D transformer backbones or the specialized dual-branch LOAN architecture, OneDayBefore-inclusive evaluation yields only IoU ≈ 0.42–0.49 and F1 ≈ 0.58–0.64 at the aggregate level (with LOAN peaking at IoU = 0.49 and F1 = 0.64 when also trained on OneDayBefore). The inclusion of OneDayBefore samples appears to make the task more stringent rather than providing immediate benefits: these patches plausibly share meteorological and fuel signatures with ignition days but lack confirmed fire outcomes, thereby increasing label ambiguity and elevating both false alarms and missed detections. This behavior highlights a limitation of treating OneDayBefore as ordinary negatives (or weak positives) without adapting the learning objective or calibration strategy to explicitly model near-miss uncertainty. Finally, LOAN remains consistently competitive and typically top-performing despite its comparatively small parameter budget, suggesting that inductive biases combining dynamic meteorology with static context (and location-aware normalization) can confer a structural advantage over more generic attention mechanisms, particularly under distributional stress introduced by pre-ignition conditions.

A deeper look at the sources of difficulty highlights several intertwined factors. The first concerns label uncertainty and objective mismatch: fire perimeters and patch-based aggregation can misalign with the underlying input grid, injecting noise into both positive and negative labels. This issue becomes particularly salient with OneDayBefore samples, which likely correspond to genuine pre-ignition states and thus blur the boundary between background and wildfire. The empirical signature of this ambiguity is clear in the aggregate results: when OneDayBefore patches are included at evaluation time, AUROC drops by roughly 0.09 and IoU/F1 decrease by about 0.12–0.15 across models, despite otherwise comparable performance on the OneDayBefore-excluded test set. Importantly, training with OneDayBefore only marginally mitigates this gap (e.g., LOAN improves from IoU = 0.46 to 0.49 and from F1 = 0.62 to 0.64 under OneDayBefore-inclusive evaluation), indicating that simply exposing the network to “almost-fire” negatives does not resolve the underlying semantic ambiguity. Relatedly, accuracy remains comparatively stable across evaluation regimes, which is consistent with a heavily imbalanced setting where global correctness can be dominated by true negatives and thus fails to reflect the degradation in overlap- and recall-sensitive metrics.

Temporal confounding likely constitutes an additional source of difficulty. While the preceding analysis points to label ambiguity, next-day ignition, thus OneDayBefore patches, does share many of the same meteorological and environmental signatures as true wildfire patches, yet differ in some finer-scale triggers that determine whether ignition occurs. This makes the distinction between pre-ignition and confirmed-fire states intrinsically difficult, and helps explain why models retain some ranking ability while suffering marked degradation in IoU and F1 when OneDayBefore samples are included in evaluation.

Addressing these limitations will likely require both conceptual and technical advances. A promising direction is to explicitly model pre-ignition dynamics, reframing OneDayBefore not as label noise but as an intermediate state. Multi-state formulations (e.g., background, pre-ignition, ignition) or time-to-event/hazard modeling would better reflect the gradual build-up of risk and could reduce the current objective mismatch induced by a hard binary label. On the optimization side, improved training procedures remain important: cost-sensitive objectives, class-balanced weighting, and calibrated or year-adaptive thresholding could better control the precision-recall trade-off under prevalence shifts and near-miss ambiguity. More broadly, the limited improvements from OneDayBefore-augmented training at the aggregate level suggest that gains will likely require objective-level changes (e.g., uncertainty-aware losses or soft labeling near ambiguous regions) rather than data augmentation alone. Incorporating richer dynamic information may further help disambiguate pre-ignition conditions, for instance via higher-frequency wind/humidity predictors, proxies for human ignition pressure, or more direct fuel-moisture surrogates, potentially coupled with architectures that better encode multi-scale spatial dependencies.

In summary, while AUROC values around 0.83–0.85 (OneDayBefore-excluded) indicate that the models retain meaningful ranking ability, the moderate IoU/F1 (roughly 0.55–0.62 and 0.70–0.75 in aggregate) and their pronounced degradation under OneDayBefore-inclusive evaluation (IoU ≈ 0.42–0.49, F1 ≈ 0.58–0.64) highlight the fundamental difficulty of near next-day wildfire classification on CalWildFire. Future progress will likely depend on reformulating the prediction task to represent pre-ignition states explicitly, improving training and calibration under shifting event prevalence and ambiguous negatives, and enriching spatio-temporal inputs to better reflect the multi-scale processes governing ignition. In parallel, expanding the database to more recent years through an annually scheduled automated pipeline for validation, spatial interpolation, and construction of the required multidimensional data structure would support more robust generalization analyses and facilitate deployment. With specific regard to spatial interpolation of meteorological features, future developments will incorporate a more physically consistent wind-speed spatial interpolation by adopting the principles of mass and momentum conservation, which explicitly account for topographic effects on wind flow67. The overarching objective is an operational setting in which predictions can inform stakeholders (e.g., the Calabria Region and Carabinieri Forestali Calabria) for prevention-oriented decision-making and improved allocation of firefighting resources during the critical season.
