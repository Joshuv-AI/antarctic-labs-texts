Bilateral Multiterritory Ischemic Stroke as an Unusual Presentation of Neurobrucellosis
Authors: Nuray C Usta
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Brucellosis remains endemic in Turkey, with annual incidence varying across different regions. The Eastern Black Sea region, including Trabzon, where our patient resides, is among the areas where brucellosis continues to be reported, reflecting ongoing zoonotic exposure related to livestock breeding. Neurobrucellosis is an uncommon manifestation of brucellosis and typically presents with fever, meningitis, or meningoencephalitis accompanied by cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) abnormalities.[1] Vascular involvement is rare and usually associated with inflammatory CSF findings or single-territory ischemic lesions.[2] Given the patient’s residence in an endemic region and the unusual bilateral multiterritorial infarction pattern without identifiable conventional vascular etiology, infectious causes, including brucellosis, were considered despite the absence of systemic manifestations.

A 57-year-old male from a brucellosis-endemic area presented with acute right-sided weakness and was initially evaluated for ischemic stroke. He had no fever, headache, altered mental status, recent infections, or clear history of animal exposure. Neurological examination revealed mild right hemiparesis and sensory deficits without meningeal signs. Routine laboratory investigations and inflammatory markers were unremarkable, and CSF analysis showed normal protein, glucose, and cell count, with negative Brucella antibodies. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated acute diffusion-restricted lesions in the bilateral anterior cerebral artery–middle cerebral artery watershed regions, along with a focal pontine lesion [Figure 1]. Magnetic resonance angiography showed no evidence of large-vessel stenosis, occlusion, aneurysm, or segmental narrowing. No radiological features suggestive of medium- or large-vessel vasculitis, such as vessel irregularity or beading patterns, were observed. A comprehensive etiological evaluation for ischemic stroke was performed. Carotid and vertebral Doppler ultrasonography revealed no significant stenosis. Twenty-four-hour Holter monitoring did not detect atrial fibrillation or clinically significant arrhythmias. Transthoracic echocardiography showed no intracardiac thrombus, valvular vegetation, or structural cardiac abnormalities. Transesophageal echocardiography was not performed as it was not recommended following the initial cardiological assessment. Screening for hypercoagulable states, including protein C, protein S, antithrombin III levels, factor V Leiden mutation, prothrombin G20210A mutation, and antiphospholipid antibodies, was negative. Malignancy screening was unremarkable.

The atypical multiterritory distribution prompted further evaluation, revealing a markedly elevated Brucella Coombs agglutination titer (1:1280). The patient was treated with doxycycline and rifampicin, resulting in complete neurological recovery.

Although the infarcts were relatively small and located within watershed territories, their bilateral anterior circulation distribution combined with a concomitant pontine lesion was atypical for conventional hypoperfusion or small-vessel disease. The absence of significant carotid stenosis, a cardiac embolic source, or a hypercoagulable condition further raised suspicion for alternative etiologies, including inflammatory or infectious vasculopathy.

Neurobrucellosis-related ischemic stroke is thought to arise from either infectious vasculitis or immune-mediated endothelial dysfunction. In some vascular-predominant cases, CSF findings may remain normal, reflecting immune-mediated microvascular injury rather than active central nervous system infection.[3] Vascular involvement may occur through immune-complex deposition and inflammatory cytokine–mediated endothelial injury, leading to small-vessel vasculopathy without overt systemic inflammatory findings. This mechanism may explain the occurrence of multiterritory ischemic lesions in our patient despite normal CSF findings and the absence of large-vessel abnormalities on angiographic imaging. Multiterritory ischemic involvement affecting both anterior and posterior circulations, as observed in this patient, is rare and may be easily misclassified as a cryptogenic stroke. The favorable response to antimicrobial therapy further supports a reversible immune-mediated mechanism.

CSF examination is not routinely performed in typical cryptogenic strokes. However, in this case, the unusual bilateral multiterritorial infarction pattern and the absence of identifiable conventional vascular etiologies prompted further evaluation to exclude infectious or inflammatory causes. This targeted approach led to the identification of brucellosis despite the absence of systemic manifestations.

This case emphasizes that neurobrucellosis should be considered in the differential diagnosis of cryptogenic multiterritory ischemic stroke, even in the absence of fever or CSF abnormalities, particularly in endemic regions. Early recognition and appropriate antimicrobial treatment can lead to full neurological recovery.
