Sports tourism as a pathway to older adults’ psychological well-being: a dual-pathway model integrating broaden-and-build and social identity perspectives
Authors: Baoxia Chen, Zhenguo Shi, Qian Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explores how sports tourism is associated with older adults’ eudaimonic well-being through dual psychological mechanisms. Drawing on Broaden-and-Build Theory and Social Identity Theory, the research examines an emotion pathway in which positive emotions and sense of physical challenge achievement are linked to psychological resources, and an identity pathway in which sports group identification is linked to positive age identity. Survey data from 467 older Chinese sports tourism participants were analyzed using PLS-SEM and artificial neural network analysis. PLS-SEM results provide support for both pathways, while neural network sensitivity analysis further reveals that sense of physical challenge achievement is the most important predictor of psychological resource accumulation, and psychological resources showed greater importance in predicting well-being than positive age identity. Peer social support showed significant moderating effects on the achievement-to-resources and identification-to-age-identity relationships.Keywords: Sports tourism for older adults, Eudaimonic well-being, PLS-SEM, Artificial neural network, Active agingSubject terms: Psychology, Psychology, Sociology

Introduction

Global population aging, with the number of people aged 60 and above projected to exceed 2.1 billion by 2050, has made older adults’ well-being a central concern. In China, where individuals aged 60 and above represent 18.7% of the population, this urgency is particularly acute. Within this context, tourism has increasingly become an important research focus regarding its contribution to older adults’ well-being1–3. While recent studies have demonstrated that tourism participation can enhance tourists’ positive affect and life satisfaction4,5, the mechanisms linking tourism participation to older adults’ deeper psychological growth remain underexplored.

Three theoretical gaps require attention. First, the literature has predominantly focused on hedonic well-being, leaving the formation mechanisms of eudaimonic well-being, encompassing self-growth, potential fulfillment, and life meaning, insufficiently explained. For older adults, eudaimonic dimensions constitute the psychological foundations of active aging, making this gap particularly consequential6–8. Second, although the positive effects of positive emotions and social interaction on tourism well-being have been separately verified, the psychological resource accumulation pathway centered on positive emotions (grounded in BBT) and the positive age identity pathway centered on group interaction (grounded in SIT) have been studied in isolation. This fragmentation prevents systematic assessment of the relative contributions of these two psychological mechanisms within a unified framework9,10. Third, the predominant reliance on linear SEM assumptions in tourism behavioral research may obscure nonlinear dynamics, such as potential saturation or threshold effects, in the relationships between positive experiences and psychological outcomes among older adults11,12.

Building on these gaps, this study develops a dual-pathway model that integrates the Broaden-and-Build Theory (BBT) and Social Identity Theory (SIT) through the experiential structure of sports tourism, where positive embodied experiences and group-based identity cues are jointly embedded in older adults’ participation. Within this model, the emotion pathway focuses on psychological resource accumulation, whereas the identity pathway focuses on positive age identity, with both pathways converging on eudaimonic well-being. Peer social support is incorporated as a boundary condition across the two pathways, and PLS-SEM is combined with ANN to examine the hypothesized associations and relative variable importance. In doing so, this study clarifies complementary emotion- and identity-related mechanisms, identifies differentiated peer-support boundary conditions, and extends methodological application in tourism well-being research.

Theoretical foundation and research hypotheses

Broaden-and-build theory

The Broaden-and-Build Theory (BBT)9 proposes that positive emotions broaden individuals’ thought-action repertoires and build enduring personal resources—including self-efficacy, psychological resilience, and social capital13,14. While BBT has received empirical support across multiple domains15–17, tourism research has primarily applied it to explain immediate well-being effects4, with the core ‘build’ mechanism remaining underexamined in older adults’ tourism contexts. This study extends BBT by incorporating ‘sense of physical challenge achievement’, grounded in mastery experience18, as a distinct experiential input. Although both positive emotions and challenge achievement belong to positive sports tourism experiences, they reflect different psychological content: the former captures general affective states during participation, whereas the latter captures older adults’ appraisal that they have successfully completed a bodily challenge and confirmed their capability.

Social identity theory

Social Identity Theory (SIT)10 posits that when individuals identify with a group, its positive characteristics become incorporated into their self-concept19,20. In tourism, SIT has been applied to explain community identification effects21 but its application to older adult tourism remains limited. Unlike classic intergroup comparison, the identification process here involves ‘intragroup recategorization’—older adults differentiating themselves from negative age stereotypes by joining positive sports groups. This study conceptualizes the outcome as ‘positive age identity,’ drawing on Kornadt and Rothermund’s22 domain-specific framework.

Theoretical integration: the emotion-identity dual-pathway model

In existing tourism well-being literature, emotional experiences and social identity processes are often discussed separately, with limited dialogue between hedonic and social psychological explanations. This study proposes an “Emotion-Identity Dual-Pathway Model” to examine these two mechanisms within the same sports tourism context. The integration is grounded in the experiential structure of sports tourism: older adults’ participation involves positive affective and mastery-based experiences generated through physical activity, as well as group-based meanings formed through shared participation with other sport tourists. BBT is therefore used to explain the emotion pathway, in which positive emotions and physical challenge achievement are associated with psychological resource accumulation. SIT is used to explain the identity pathway, in which sports group identification is associated with positive age identity. Although these pathways are theoretically distinct, they are contextually connected because both capture psychological meanings that extend beyond momentary enjoyment and converge on eudaimonic well-being.

Accordingly, the model contains two complementary mediation pathways. The emotion pathway links sports tourism positive emotions and sense of physical challenge achievement to psychological well-being through psychological resource accumulation, whereas the identity pathway links sports group identification to psychological well-being through positive age identity. Peer social support further provides a common boundary condition across the two pathways. Supportive companions may validate achievement experiences in the emotion pathway and reinforce group-based meanings in the identity pathway. In this way, the model examines the relative contributions of two distinct but connected psychological mechanisms within a unified sports tourism framework.

Hypothesis development

Positive experiences and psychological resource accumulation (H1, H2)

BBT’s core proposition is that positive emotions help individuals accumulate enduring psychological resources through the “broaden-and-build” mechanism9. Existing research demonstrates significant positive relationships between positive emotions and self-efficacy18, psychological resilience15, and optimistic tendencies23. In tourism contexts, positive emotions similarly enhance psychological resource levels17. In older adults’ sports tourism, which integrates natural environment exposure with physical activity participation, the multidimensionality and intensity of positive emotional experiences may exceed those in everyday contexts. Hence:

H1: Sports tourism positive emotions are positively associated with older adults’ psychological resource accumulation.

Beyond general positive emotional experiences, sense of physical challenge achievement represents a mastery-based appraisal in sports tourism. It may be accompanied by pleasure or pride, but its defining feature is not affective valence itself; rather, it lies in older adults’ evaluation that they have completed a bodily challenge, confirmed their capability, and achieved a personally meaningful standard of mastery. Bandura’s18 self-efficacy theory indicates that mastery experience—defined as individuals’ personal accomplishment of challenging tasks through their own efforts—constitutes the most powerful source of self-efficacy enhancement. From a BBT perspective, this mastery-based appraisal may be more directly related to psychological resource accumulation. Unlike general positive emotions, which may arise from scenery, companionship, or enjoyment, sense of physical challenge achievement is grounded in actual physical performance feedback24. For older adults, successfully completing exercise challenges may help counter the implicit belief that “aging equals capability decline,” providing an embodied positive experience that may be associated with self-confidence and psychological resilience25. Hence:

H2: Sense of physical challenge achievement is positively associated with older adults’ psychological resource accumulation.

Sports group identification and positive age identity (H3)

When individuals identify with a group, the group’s core characteristics (such as values, behavioral norms, and group image) become incorporated into their self-concept through internalization processes19. Sports groups in tourism contexts are often associated with positive, health-oriented, and active group attributes. When older adults identify with sports groups, these positive attributes may be incorporated into their self-concepts, thereby reshaping their perceptions of their own aging status. This study’s conceptualization of positive age identity primarily reflects older adults’ positive perceptions of self-aging in the domains of physical activity and health. This group characteristic internalization process holds particular significance for older populations: role loss and social network contraction following retirement often result in older adults lacking positive social identity references26, making them more susceptible to accepting negative age stereotypes27. Sports groups in sports tourism may provide older adults with a new, positive social identity reference, which may be associated with more positive age-related self-perceptions. Kornadt and Rothermund22 further indicate that this reshaping of age identity may be most pronounced in the domains of physical activity and health. Steffens et al.’s28 meta-analysis confirmed a robust positive relationship between group identification and positive self-perceptions. Hence:

H3: Sports group identification is positively associated with older adults’ positive age identity.

Dual-pathway mediators and outcome: psychological well-being and continued participation intention (H4, H5, H6)

The two pathways converge on psychological well-being through their respective mediators. BBT’s extended logic suggests that psychological resources built through positive emotions possess not only instrumental value but also intrinsic well-being-promoting functions. Luthans et al.’s16 research demonstrates significant positive relationships between psychological capital (confidence, resilience, optimism, and hope) and individuals’ psychological well-being. For older populations, psychological resources constitute a core foundation for coping with life transitions and maintaining positive life states29. Psychological resources accumulated during sports tourism (such as enhanced self-confidence and higher resilience) may provide the psychological foundation for individuals’ pursuit of life purpose, personal growth, and autonomy6,30. Hence:

H4: Psychological resource accumulation is positively associated with older adults’ psychological well-being.

In the identity pathway, the promotional effect of positive age-related self-perceptions on older adults’ well-being has received substantial empirical support. Levy’s27 stereotype embodiment theory indicates that older adults’ positive or negative perceptions of their own aging significantly influence their mental health and physical functioning. Older adults holding positive age perceptions exhibit higher life satisfaction, stronger sense of life meaning, and lower depression levels31,32. In sports tourism contexts, when older adults form more positive age-related self-perceptions within sports groups, these perceptions may correspond to multiple core dimensions in Ryff’s6 psychological well-being model. Huang et al.’s33 empirical research further demonstrates that older adults holding positive age identity exhibit more active engagement across various social activities. Positive age identity may also possess “buffering” functions against negative age stereotypes22. Hence:

H5: Positive age identity is positively associated with older adults’ psychological well-being.

Furthermore, tourism behavior research demonstrates that positive tourism experience outcomes constitute important predictors of subsequent tourism behavior34. Existing research, however, primarily focuses on the influences of satisfaction and hedonic emotions on revisit intention, with relatively insufficient theoretical exploration of relationships between psychological well-being and behavioral intentions. Psychological well-being reflects the deep sense of meaning and growth individuals obtain from tourism30, and such deep psychological gains are expected to be positively associated with more enduring participation motivation. Hence:

H6: Psychological well-being is positively associated with older adults’ continued participation intention.

Moderating effects of peer social support (H7a–H7c)

Peer social support refers to emotional encouragement, practical assistance, and information sharing that older adults receive from accompanying partners during sports tourism35. It is positioned as a moderating variable in this study, examining whether it moderates the three hypothesized experience–outcome relationships.

In the emotion pathway, BBT indicates that the “build” effects of positive emotions may be enhanced under social sharing conditions36. Rimé’s37 social sharing theory further confirms that when positive emotional experiences are shared with others, their positive effects tend to be amplified and prolonged. In sports tourism, companions’ presence provides immediate conditions for such sharing, potentially strengthening the association between positive emotions and psychological resource accumulation. Hence:

H7a: Peer social support positively moderates the relationship between sports tourism positive emotions and psychological resource accumulation.

Similarly, Bandura18 indicates that the building effects of mastery experience on self-efficacy can be strengthened through social validation. When older adults receive recognition from companions after completing physical challenges, such external feedback may increase the psychological salience of achievement experiences and strengthen their association with psychological resource accumulation. Hence:

H7b: Peer social support positively moderates the relationship between sense of physical challenge achievement and psychological resource accumulation.

In the identity pathway, SIT-related research demonstrates that group cohesion is a key factor influencing the efficiency of group characteristic internalization21. Peer social support may strengthen group cohesion and may help individuals develop stronger group belonging and more actively internalize the group’s positive characteristics into their self-perceptions. Hence:

H7c: Peer social support positively moderates the relationship between sports group identification and positive age identity.

Mediation effect hypotheses (H8a–H8c)

Based on the direct effect hypotheses above, mediation effect hypotheses are further proposed to examine the indirect transmission mechanisms within the two pathways. In the emotion pathway, positive emotions and sense of physical challenge achievement are expected to show indirect associations with psychological well-being through psychological resource accumulation9. In the identity pathway, sports group identification is expected to show an indirect association with psychological well-being through positive age identity10,22. Hence:

H8a: Psychological resource accumulation mediates the relationship between sports tourism positive emotions and psychological well-being.

H8b: Psychological resource accumulation mediates the relationship between sense of physical challenge achievement and psychological well-being.

H8c: Positive age identity mediates the relationship between sports group identification and psychological well-being.

Research methodology

Sample and data collection

This study targeted Chinese older adults aged 60 and above who had participated in sports tourism activities (e.g., hiking, cycling, tai chi/qigong) within the past year. Data collection combined convenience and stratified sampling, with paper-based questionnaires distributed at sports tourism groups, sports clubs, and senior universities across multiple cities. Trained surveyors provided on-site guidance. Participants were informed that their responses would remain anonymous and confidential, that participation was voluntary, and that there were no right or wrong answers. To create psychological separation among constructs, predictor-, mediator-, moderator-, and outcome-related items were presented in separate questionnaire sections with distinct instructions. Attention check items were embedded, and invalid questionnaires were deleted. Of 492 collected, 467 were valid (94.9%). G*Power 3.1 confirmed the minimum required sample size was 92 based on a medium effect size criterion (f2 = 0.15, α = 0.05, 1 − β = 0.80)38,39, far exceeded by the 467 valid samples. Variable measurement

Eight constructs were measured using established scales adapted to the older adults’ sports tourism context on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = “strongly disagree” to 7 = “strongly agree”). Chinese versions of the measures were prepared by the research team with reference to the source scales. The wording was checked iteratively against the original construct definitions and refined only where necessary to improve readability for older respondents and contextual fit with sports tourism participation, while preserving the conceptual content of each construct. All items were positively scored with explicit temporal and referential anchors. Specifically, with a sample item translated from the Chinese questionnaire shown for each construct: Sports Tourism Positive Emotions (PE40; “I felt happy and joyful during this sports tourism experience”); Sense of Physical Challenge Achievement (PA24,41; “I accomplished something I had previously believed I could not do”); Sports Group Identification (GI42; “I identify with the atmosphere and values of this sports group”); Psychological Resource Accumulation (PR16; “Since this trip, I have felt more confident in my ability to cope with new challenges”); Positive Age Identity (AI22,27; “My age does not limit my enjoyment of physical activity”); Peer Social Support (SS35; “My companions encouraged me when I was tired or hesitant”); Psychological Well-Being (PWB2,6; “I feel that my life has purpose and direction”); Continued Participation Intention (CI34; “I intend to continue taking part in similar sports tourism activities”). For the two experience-related constructs, PE items focused on respondents’ general affective states during sports tourism participation, whereas PA items focused on post-challenge appraisal.

Analytical methods

A two-stage approach combining PLS-SEM and ANN was employed. Given the study’s predictive orientation and model complexity, the first stage utilized PLS-SEM11,43. The rationale for introducing ANN lies in the theoretical expectation of nonlinear dynamics: BBT’s “upward spiral” hypothesis suggests potential saturation effects, and SIT’s internalization process may exhibit threshold activation—patterns that PLS-SEM’s linear assumptions cannot capture12.

The PLS-SEM stage was completed using SmartPLS 4. Measurement model assessment encompassed indicator reliability (factor loadings > 0.70), internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α > 0.70 and CR > 0.70), convergent validity (AVE > 0.50), and discriminant validity using both the Fornell–Larcker criterion and the heterotrait–monotrait ratio of correlations (HTMT). HTMT inference was evaluated using percentile bootstrap confidence intervals defined by the 5th and 95th percentiles. Structural model assessment encompassed path coefficient significance (bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples), explanatory power (R²), effect size (f²), and predictive relevance (Q²). Mediation effects were examined through bootstrap 95% confidence intervals, and moderating effects employed the product-indicator approach. The ANN stage utilized latent variable scores from significant PLS-SEM paths as inputs, constructing multilayer feedforward neural networks with one hidden layer (neuron numbers at 50%–75% of input variables), sigmoid activation function, and 10-fold cross-validation.

Data analysis proceeded in three steps: SPSS 26 completed descriptive statistics, normality testing, and Harman’s single-factor test; SmartPLS 4 completed measurement model assessment, structural model testing, and Bootstrap testing of mediation and moderation effects; latent variable scores were then imported into R for ANN model construction and sensitivity analysis.

Research results

Common method bias testing

As both the independent and dependent variables in this study were derived from self-reported data collected through a single questionnaire, common method bias (CMB) may be a concern44. To address this, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted as a preliminary diagnostic. All 45 measurement items were entered into an exploratory factor analysis with no rotation and no restriction on the number of factors extracted, using eigenvalues greater than 1 as the extraction criterion. The results yielded eight factors with a cumulative explained variance of 78.103%, with the first factor accounting for 33.442% of the variance, below the commonly used 40% reference value44. These results indicate that the measurement items were not dominated by a single general factor.

Structural model assessment

Having confirmed adequate reliability and validity of the measurement model, this study proceeded to structural model assessment. Following Hair et al.’s43 recommendations, the evaluation was conducted systematically across five aspects: collinearity diagnostics, path coefficient significance, explanatory power (R²), effect size (f²), and predictive relevance (Q²).

Collinearity diagnostics

Variance inflation factor (VIF) values for all structural model paths ranged from 1.000 to 1.197, well below both the conservative threshold of 5.0 and the more stringent threshold of 3.343, indicating the absence of multicollinearity concerns.

Furthermore, this study examined the complete serial mediation effects extending from the experiential front-end to the behavioral intention outcome. The results reveal that the serial indirect effect of PA → PR → PWB → CI was significant (β = 0.039, t = 3.233, p = 0.001), as was that of GI → AI → PWB → CI (β = 0.030, t = 2.614, p = 0.009) and PE → PR → PWB → CI (β = 0.021, t = 2.656, p = 0.008). These findings indicate that both the emotion and identity pathways showed statistically significant serial indirect associations through the variables specified in each pathway, supporting the proposed indirect-effect structure of the dual-pathway model.

To compensate for PLS-SEM’s limitation of detecting only linear relationships, this study further employed artificial neural network (ANN) analysis to capture potential nonlinear relationships among variables and assess the relative importance of independent variables. Following Leong et al.’s12 two-stage analytical framework, latent variable scores from significant PLS-SEM paths served as inputs to construct multilayer feedforward neural networks with one hidden layer and a sigmoid activation function. Integrated comparison of PLS-SEM and ANN results

Synthesizing the above analyses, PLS-SEM and ANN yielded consistent relative-importance patterns for the focal predictors. PLS-SEM showed a stronger association for PA than for PE in predicting PR (β = 0.339 vs. β = 0.186), while ANN produced the same ranking in normalized importance (100% vs. 54.08%). Similarly, PR showed a stronger association with PWB than AI in PLS-SEM (β = 0.331 vs. β = 0.193) and was also assigned greater normalized importance in ANN (100% vs. 28.38%).

ANN additionally provided information on nonlinear predictive performance. Although some ANN models, such as Models C and D, incorporated broader input sets than the corresponding PLS-SEM equations, Model B was directly comparable because GI served as the predictor of AI in both approaches. In this model, ANN yielded an R² of 22.60%, compared with 19.5% in PLS-SEM, suggesting the possible presence of nonlinear components in the GI–AI relationship. Taken together, the consistency in predictor rankings and the additional predictive performance observed in Model B support the complementary use of PLS-SEM and ANN and indicate that the principal predictive patterns were not confined to the linear PLS-SEM specification.

Conclusions, discussion, and implications

Key findings

An emotion–identity dual-pathway model was developed and tested using PLS-SEM and ANN with 467 older Chinese sports tourism participants. Of 12 hypotheses, 11 were supported. PA (β = 0.339) showed a stronger positive association with PR than PE (β = 0.186); GI showed the strongest positive association with AI (β = 0.442, f² = 0.242). Both PR and AI were significantly associated with PWB, and PWB was significantly associated with CI; the serial indirect effects were also significant. Peer social support significantly and positively moderated the PA → PR (β = 0.221) and GI → AI (β = 0.204) relationships but did not significantly moderate the PE → PR relationship (H7a not supported). ANN yielded consistent relative-importance rankings: PA’s normalized importance in predicting PR (100%) exceeded PE’s (54.08%), and PR’s importance in predicting PWB (100%) was approximately 3.5 times that of AI (28.38%).

Discussion

The emotion pathway: the dominant role of physical challenge achievement in psychological resource accumulation

Sense of physical challenge achievement (PA) showed a significantly stronger positive association with psychological resource accumulation than positive emotions (PE), as evidenced by both PLS-SEM (PA: β = 0.339 vs. PE: β = 0.186) and ANN (PA: 100% vs. PE: 54.08%). This pattern suggests that mastery-based appraisal is especially relevant in sports tourism because physical challenge completion may provide older adults with embodied evidence of capability. Compared with general positive emotions, the “I did it” experience may be more closely related to self-efficacy and resilience, which may help explain why PA showed a stronger association with PR than PE. This finding refines the application of BBT in physically active tourism contexts by highlighting the role of mastery appraisal alongside general positive affect.

The identity pathway: group identification and positive age identity

Sports group identification showed the strongest positive association with positive age identity (β = 0.442, f² = 0.242), consistent with Steffens et al.’s28 meta-analysis. This finding highlights a context-specific identity process in which sports group identification is associated with more positive age-related self-perceptions in the physical activity and health domain. Rather than restating the general logic of SIT, the result suggests that participation in active and supportive sport groups may help older adults view ageing through a more capability-oriented lens.

The positive association between AI and PWB (β = 0.193) is consistent with stereotype embodiment theory and Ryff’s eudaimonic well-being perspective. It suggests that positive age-related self-perceptions may be linked to well-being not only through affective enjoyment, but also through a sense of growth and continued capability.

Moderation effects: the “Differentiated Moderation” pattern of social support

The moderating effects of peer social support across the three relationships exhibited a differentiated pattern: peer social support significantly and positively moderated the PA → PR and GI → AI relationships, whereas its moderation of the PE → PR relationship was nonsignificant. This pattern may reflect differences in the relevance of social feedback across the three associations.

For H7b, the positive association between sense of physical challenge achievement and psychological resource accumulation may be particularly responsive to external social feedback. Bandura18 noted that the building effect of mastery experience on self-efficacy depends not only on task completion itself but also on how individuals attribute this success. Companions’ encouragement and recognition may provide older adults with an important external attribution reference, helping them interpret “I completed the challenge” as “I am capable.” For older adults, companions’ recognition may help consolidate achievement experiences as evidence of capability, which is consistent with social sharing theory37.

For H7c, the positive moderation result is consistent with the possibility that peer social support is associated with greater internalization of group characteristics through stronger group cohesion. This pattern differs from H7b’s individual attribution explanation, although both involve socially situated processes. Group cohesion is widely regarded as an important facilitating condition for group-characteristic internalization21. When mutual support is high, members may experience stronger group belonging and may be more willing to incorporate the group’s positive characteristics into their self-perceptions. Supportive peer environments may also make group-based identity internalization less threatening by providing acceptance and belonging during sports tourism participation.

H7a was not supported. One plausible account is offered by the “positivity effect” identified by socioemotional selectivity theory: older adults’ elevated baseline levels of positive affect may leave limited room for peer support to further amplify the association between positive emotions and psychological resource accumulation46. A second possibility is that PE, as a general affective state, is less dependent on external validation than the appraisal-based experience captured by PA, for which companions’ recognition supplies an interpretive reference that emotions alone do not require. These accounts remain interpretive and cannot be adjudicated with the present data.

Collectively, the observed moderation pattern was more pronounced for associations involving achievement validation or group interaction, whereas no significant moderation was observed for the broader PE → PR association.

Complementarity of PLS-SEM and ANN results

The PLS-ANN hybrid approach provided complementary insights beyond linear analysis. In Model B, where input specification matched PLS-SEM exactly, the higher ANN R² (22.60% vs. 19.5%) suggests potential nonlinear dynamics in the group identification–age identity relationship, consistent with SIT’s theoretical expectations regarding threshold-based internalization. Model D’s finding that AI achieved the highest normalized importance in predicting CI is exploratory, as the model included variables without direct theoretical paths to CI. Nevertheless, this exploratory result suggests that positive age identity may have predictive relevance for participation intention beyond well-being—a possibility warranting future testing through competing model comparisons.

Theoretical contributions

This study makes three theoretical contributions. First, it integrates BBT and SIT within a sports tourism context by showing how positive embodied experiences and group-based identity cues are associated with older adults’ eudaimonic well-being through distinct but complementary mechanisms. This responds to the fragmentation between emotion-based and identity-based explanations in tourism well-being research and clarifies how the two perspectives can be examined within one contextually grounded model.

Second, it refines the application of BBT by distinguishing general positive emotions from sense of physical challenge achievement. The stronger role of PA in predicting PR suggests that mastery-based appraisal is an important experiential input in sports tourism, especially when physical participation is central to the tourism experience.

Third, it extends older adult tourism research by positioning positive age identity as a context-specific identity-related pathway component and by identifying peer social support as a differentiated boundary condition. The findings suggest that social support is more relevant to pathways involving achievement validation and group-characteristic internalization than to the broader PE → PR pathway.

Practical implications

The empirical findings of this study carry direct implications for three dimensions of older adults’ sports tourism: product design, group operations, and marketing strategy.

At the product design level, the findings suggest that sports tourism products may place greater emphasis on opportunities for physical challenge achievement rather than hedonic pleasure alone. ANN analysis identified sense of physical challenge achievement as the strongest predictor of psychological resource accumulation. Accordingly, tourism product designers could create multi-tiered, adjustable-difficulty physical challenge components that allow older adults with varying fitness levels to identify appropriate challenge targets, with ceremonial completion markers at each milestone to acknowledge and highlight the mastery experience of “I did it.”

At the group operations level, the finding that sports group identification was the strongest predictor of positive age identity (β = 0.442) suggests that tourism operators may place greater emphasis on building sports communities. Specifically, operators could foster distinctive, belonging-rich sports communities by assigning vibrant group names, designing unified visual identities, and organizing group rituals. Simultaneously, the moderation results showed that peer support was associated with stronger “achievement → psychological resource accumulation” and “group identification → positive age identity” relationships. Peer support components could therefore be embedded within physical challenge and group activity settings, for instance by incorporating icebreaking social interactions and in-depth exchange sessions into group activities, to support achievement validation, group belonging, and positive age-related self-perceptions.

At the marketing strategy level, this study suggests that psychological well-being (rather than hedonic well-being) emerged as an important psychological predictor of older adults’ continued sports tourism participation. This finding suggests a possible shift in tourism marketing from a hedonistic narrative of “making seniors happy” to a self-actualization narrative of “helping seniors grow,” emphasizing themes of “growth,” “breakthrough,” and “discovering a new self.” For example, advertising copy could evolve from “At sixty, enjoy the mountains and rivers” to “At sixty, conquer the mountains and rivers,” positioning participants’ self-transcendence and growth experiences at the heart of brand communication.

Limitations and future directions

First, given the cross-sectional design, the observed paths are interpreted as theory-guided associations, and the temporal ordering between the focal psychological processes and participants’ demographic, health, and participation characteristics cannot be established. Future longitudinal research could further examine the temporal dynamics implied by BBT’s “upward spiral” hypothesis and assess the roles of these characteristics within theoretically prespecified models. Second, as the sample was drawn entirely from Chinese older adults, the collectivist cultural context may have shaped the strength of the group identification pathway. Researchers could perform comparative studies across individualist and collectivist cultural contexts to assess the model’s cross-cultural generalizability. Third, all variables were measured through self-report scales. Future research could introduce objective indicators such as wearable devices to complement subjective measures, and the positive age identity scale, while grounded in established frameworks22,27, would benefit from validation across more diverse populations. Fourth, since the nonsupport of H7a may stem from the heterogeneity of the positive emotions construct, differentiating among types of social support (emotional, instrumental, and informational) and examining their interactions with discrete emotions appears promising for clarifying these moderation boundary conditions.
