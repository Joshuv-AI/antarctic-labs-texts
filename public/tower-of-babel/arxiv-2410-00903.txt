Causal Representation Learning with Generative Artificial Intelligence: Application to Texts as Treatments We thank Christian Fong and Justin Grimmer for kindly sharing their dataset used in Candidate Profile Experiment and Naoki Egami for helpful comments.
Kosuke Imai, Kentaro Nakamura

Abstract
In this paper, we demonstrate how to enhance the validity of causal inference with unstructured high-dimensional treatments like texts, by leveraging the power of generative Artificial Intelligence. Specifically, we propose to use a deep generative model such as large language models (LLMs) to efficiently generate treatments and use their internal representation for subsequent causal effect estimation. We show that the knowledge of this true internal representation helps disentangle the treatment features of interest, such as specific sentiments and certain topics, from other possibly unknown confounding features. Unlike the existing methods, our proposed approach eliminates the need to learn causal representation from the data and hence produces more accurate and efficient estimates. We formally establish the conditions required for the nonparametric identification of the average treatment effect, propose an estimation strategy that avoids the violation of the overlap assumption, and derive the asymptotic properties of the proposed estimator through the application of double machine learning. Finally, using an instrumental variables approach, we extend the proposed methodology to the settings, in which the treatment feature is based on human perception rather than is assumed to be fixed given the treatment object. The proposed methodology is also applicable to text reuse where an LLM is used to regenerate the existing texts. We conduct simulation and empirical studies, using the generated text data from an open-source LLM, Llama 3, to illustrate the advantages of our estimator over the state-of-the-art causal representation learning algorithms.



Key Words:
causal inference, deep generative models, double machine learning, large language models, unstructured treatments

1
Introduction

The emergence of generative artificial intelligence (AI) technology such as large language models (LLMs) has had an enormous impact on a variety of fields, including medicine
(
Thirunavukarasu et al., 2023
)
, education
(
Kasneci et al., 2023
)
, marketing
(
Kshetri et al., 2024
)
, and social sciences
(
Ludwig and Mullainathan, 2024
;
Bisbee et al., 2024
)
. These AI tools come with advanced capabilities to quickly generate realistic texts, images, and videos based on prompts provided by users.

In this paper, we demonstrate that generative AI can be used to enhance the performance of causal representation learning from unstructured data. As a concrete application of the proposed methodology, we focus on the settings where texts are used as treatments. Specifically, we introduce an experimental design (Section
2
) and develop an efficient estimation strategy to infer the causal effects of specific features contained in texts, such as sentiments and topics (Section
3
).

In the proposed experiment, we first generate texts by providing treatment and control prompts to an LLM. If one wishes to use the existing texts as treatments rather than generate new ones to improve external validity
(
Egami and Hartman, 2023
)
, we could ask an LLM to reproduce the texts exactly. We then present each of these generated texts to a randomly selected survey respondent and measure their reactions. Lastly, we directly extract the true internal representation of generated texts from the LLM and analyze it with an machine learning algorithm to disentangle treatment features from other confounding features contained within the same texts. The key insight of our proposed approach is to leverage the true vectorized representation of treatment texts that is available in an open-source deep generative model. This enables us to learn efficiently causal representation of unstructured data even when texts contain strong confounding features.

As part of the proposed estimation approach, we develop a neural network architecture based on TarNet
(
Shalit et al., 2017
)
to separately learn treatment and confounding features. We prove that it is possible to nonparametrically identify a
deconfounder
, which summarizes all confounding features as a lower-dimensional function of the internal representation obtained from a deep generative model. Once a deconfounder is estimated, we use it to model the treatment features and estimate the propensity score. We then apply the double machine learning (DML) methodology to obtain the asymptotically valid confidence intervals
(
Chernozhukov et al., 2018
)
.

Lastly, we extend the proposed methodology to the estimation of the causal effects of
perceived
treatment features, motivated by the fact that respondents may perceive the same treatment features differently (Section
4
). A methodological challenge is that the perceived treatment features may be confounded by possibly unobserved respondent characteristics as well as the confounding features of texts. To address this, we incorporate the instrumental variable approach
(
Imbens and Angrist, 1994
)
into our estimation approach by using the actual treatment features as instruments for their perceived counterparts.

To investigate the empirical performance of the proposed methodology, we design simulation studies based on the candidate profile experiment of
Fong and Grimmer, 2016
(Section
5
). In this experiment, survey respondents are asked to evaluate biographies of different political candidates. Our goal is to infer the causal effects of certain features, such as military background and education levels, contained in these biographies. We use an open-source LLM, Llama 3, to generate a set of new candidate biographies and design simulation studies to compare the performance of the proposed estimator with that of state-of-the-art methods. We also have Llama 3 regenerate the existing biographies to examine the performance of the proposed methodology in the case of text reuse.

We find that the proposed estimator outperforms these competing methods, which estimate the causal representation using the existing embedding
(
Pryzant et al., 2021
;
Gui and Veitch, 2023
)
. Specifically, the proposed estimator has a smaller bias and root mean squared error (RMSE) while its confidence interval retains a proper nominal coverage level. These findings hold up even when the sample size is relatively small and in the case of text reuse. In sum, our simulation studies demonstrate that the AI-generated true internal representation significantly improves causal representation learning.

Finally, we also apply the proposed methodology to the human responses to the candidate profile experiment (Section
6
). Our analysis shows that the previous military experience of candidate does not significantly affect voter evaluation on average.

Related literature.

There exist several works that estimate the causal effects of treatment features of texts. The key difference between the proposed methodology and these previous approaches is the use of generative AI in producing treatment objects. We exploit the fact that the
true
vectorized representation of generated texts can be obtained directly from open-source deep generative models. In contrast, the existing methods learn causal representation from the treatment texts in a variety of ways. For example,
Fong and Grimmer, 2016
;
Ahrens et al., 2021
impose modeling assumptions based on topic models, whereas
Pryzant et al., 2021
and
Gui and Veitch, 2023
estimate the vector representation using the BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) embeddings. We show that the use of true representation not only improves the estimation performance but also significantly increases computational efficiency.

Our work has broader implications for the literature on causal inference and unstructured objects in general
(see
Feder et al., 2022
, for a recent review of causal inference with texts)
. For example, scholars have developed methods that adjust for texts as confounders, but all of these methods estimate a low-dimensional representation of confounding information from texts
(
Veitch et al., 2020
;
Roberts et al., 2020
;
Klaassen et al., 2024
;
Mozer et al., 2020
;
Mozer et al., 2024
)
. Although our paper focuses on the use of texts as treatments rather than confounders, the proposed use of generative AI should be beneficial for these other cases where the existing estimation methods are likely to suffer from estimation error
(
Keith et al., 2020
)
.

Similarly, the proposed approach may also be applicable to causal inference with images and even videos. For example,
Jerzak et al., 2023a
;
Jerzak et al., 2023b
have considered the use of causal inference with images in the observational setting. Given the availability of deep generative models for images, it would be of interest to use generative AI for improved causal inference with images
(
Ramesh et al., 2021
;
Rombach et al., 2022
;
Shrestha and Xie, 2023
;
Ye et al., 2023
, e.g.,)
.

Our work contributes to the literature on representation learning for causal inference. Since unstructured objects like texts are fundamentally high-dimensional, learning a low-dimensional representation is crucial
(
Shi et al., 2019
;
Wang and Jordan, 2022
, e.g.,)
. Some existing works on causal inference with texts learn a low-dimensional representation that predicts both the treatment and outcome well
(
Veitch et al., 2020
;
Gui and Veitch, 2023
, e.g.,)
. While we also use a low-dimensional representation to adjust for confounding features, the proposed methodology disentangles confounding features from treatment features without violating the overlap assumption.

Finally, our methodology differs from that of
Wang and Blei, 2019
though both use the estimated “deconfounder” for confounding adjustment. Most importantly,
Wang and Blei, 2019
adjusts the
unobserved
confounding by estimating the deconfounder as a function of treatments
(
Imai and Jiang, 2019
)
. In contrast, the goal of the proposed methodology is to learn a representation of
observed
confounding by estimating the deconfounder as a function of generative model’s internal representation of treatment objects. Under this setting, we formally establish the nonparametric identification of the average treatment effect.

2
The Experimental Design for Texts as Treatments

While the proposed methodology is general, it is helpful to consider a concrete application. Specifically, we consider the candidate profile experiment conducted and analyzed by
Fong and Grimmer, 2016
which investigates how various features of a political candidate affect their voter evaluation. In the context of this experiment, we describe our alternative approach that uses LLMs to generate treatment texts. In Sections
5
and
6
, we return to this application to empirically evaluate the performance of the proposed methodology relative to the existing state-of-the-art methods.

2.1
Candidate profile experiment

Fong and Grimmer, 2016
gathered a collection of 1,246 candidate biographies (written in texts) from Wikipedia, and then recruited a total of 1,886 voters who were asked to answer an online survey evaluating up to four randomly assigned candidate profiles. Specifically, the survey asked these voters to rate each candidate biography using a feeling thermometer that ranges from 0 (cold) to 100 (warm). The authors
first used a supervised Bayesian model based on the Indian buffet process to discover a total of 10 treatment features and then estimated the marginal association between each treatment feature and the observed feeling thermometer value.

Unlike the original analysis, we consider a setting, in which researchers have a specific treatment feature of interest. Suppose that we are interested in estimating the causal effect of having a military background on voter evaluation. The relevant political science literature suggests that the experience and occupation of candidates play an important role
(
Kirkland and Coppock, 2018
;
Campbell and Cowley, 2014
;
Pedersen et al., 2019
, e.g.,)
. Indeed, the original analysis shows that candidate biographies with military background tend to receive a high feeling thermometer score.

2.2
Using large language models to (re)generate treatment texts

We use an LLM to generate treatment texts. In the current context, we can achieve this in two ways. First, we can provide a prompt to an LLM, asking it to generate a candidate biography from scratch. Alternatively, we can use existing biographies (e.g., those collected by
Fong and Grimmer, 2016
) and then ask an LLM to reproduce the same biographies without any modification.

Both approaches require (1) the coding of the treatment variable (e.g., the existence of military background) and (2) the extraction of the internal representation of LLM used to generate treatment texts. The first requirement may mean that human coders have to read treatment texts unless one is willing to assume that LLM has a good compliance with the instruction in prompts. The second requirement implies that we should use open-source LLMs including GPT (Generative Pre-trained Transformer;
Radford et al., 2018
), PaLM (Pathways Language Model;
Chowdhery et al., 2022
), Llama (Large Language Model Meta AI;
Touvron et al., 2023
), OPT (Open Pre-trained Transformer;
Zhang et al., 2022
). We note that the internal representation is not accessible for OpenAI’s GPT-4, making it impossible to use ChatGPT for our purpose.

We emphasize that the use of LLM in itself does not solve the confounding bias automatically. This is because the LLM learns the associations of words in the real-world texts, and even if manipulate the concepts with instructions, other correlated concepts might be influenced as well, which still causes the confounding bias
(
Hu and Li, 2021
)
. Now, we turn to the proposed methodology that adjusts for confounding features in unstructured treatment objects such as texts to estimate the causal effects of the specific treatment feature of interest.

3
The Proposed Methodology

In this section, we present the proposed methodology. We begin by defining the causal quantity of interest, establishing its nonparametric identification, and develop an estimation strategy.

3.1
Assumptions and causal quantity of interest

Consider a simple random sample of
N
respondents drawn from a population of interest. For each respondent
i=1,2,\ldots,N
, we assign a prompt
\bm{P}_{i}
that is randomly and independently sampled from a set of potential prompts
\mathcal{P}
. In our application, prompts are based on natural language (e.g., “Create a biography of an American politician who has some military experience”). While our framework allows for stratified randomization, for the sake of simplicity, we assume that the random assignment of prompt is done without conditioning on pre-treatment covariates such as respondent characteristics and types of prompts. Given each prompt, we use a deep generative model to generate a
treatment object
, which is denoted by
\bm{X}_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
where
\mathcal{X}
is the support of
\bm{X}_{i}
. These treatment objects are typically high-dimensional and unstructured and, in our application, they are texts.

We use a broad definition of a deep generative model so that it can encompass many LLMs and other types of foundation models. For example, our definition includes a number of models for texts
(
Keskar et al., 2019
;
Yang and Klein, 2021
;
Guo et al., 2022
;
Zhou et al., 2023
;
Chowdhery et al., 2022
;
Radford et al., 2018
;
Touvron et al., 2023
;
Zhang et al., 2022
;
Jiang et al., 2023
, e.g.,)
.

(Deep Generative Model)

A deep generative model is the following probabilistic model that takes prompt
\bm{P}_{i}
as an input and generates the treatment object
\bm{X}_{i}
as an output:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\bm{X}_{i}\mid\bm{h}_{\bm{\gamma}}(\bm{R}_{i}))

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\bm{R}_{i}\mid\bm{P}_{i})

where
\bm{R}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
denotes an observable internal representation of

contained in the model and

is a deterministic function parameterized by

that completely characterizes the conditional distribution of
\bm{X}_{i}
given
\bm{R}_{i}
.

Under this definition of deep generative model,

represents a lower-dimensional representation of treatment object

and is a hidden representation of neural networks. In addition,
\bm{h}_{\bm{\gamma}}(\bm{R})
is the propensity function
(

and is both known and observable in an open-source deep generative model. Thus, under this deep generative model, the treatment object

only depends on
\bm{P}
through

In the proposed experiment, each respondent
i

\bm{X}_{i}

Y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}
where
\mathcal{Y}
is the support of

Y_{i}\ =\ Y_{i}(\bm{X}_{i}).

Prompt is randomly assigned such that the following independence

Y_{i}(\bm{x})\ \mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}\ \bm{P}_{i}.

We are interested in estimating the causal effect of a particular feature that can be part of a treatment object. For simplicity, we consider a binary treatment feature denoted by
T_{i}\in\{0,1\}
for each
i

T_{i}=1

T_{i}=0

\bm{X}

Assumption 3
(Treatment Feature)

There exists a deterministic function
g_{T}:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
that maps a treatment object
\bm{X}_{i}
to a binary treatment feature of interest
T_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, i.e.,

T_{i}\ =\ g_{T}(\bm{X}_{i}).

The function
g_{T}
is called the “codebook function” in the literature
(
Egami et al., 2022
)
. This assumption is violated, for example, if respondents infer different values of the treatment feature from the same treatment object. We address this issue in Section
4

Next, we define the confounding features, which represent all features of
\bm{X}
other than the treatment feature
T
that influence outcome
Y
. These confounding features, denoted by
\bm{U}_{i}
, are based on a vector-valued deterministic function of
\bm{X}
and are denoted by
\bm{U}\in\mathcal{U}
where
\mathcal{U}
denotes their support. The confounding features may be multiple dimensional though we assume its dimensionality is much smaller than that of treatment object. In our application, the confounding features may include other candidate background and characteristics as well as textual features of biographies such as their length. These confounding features are possibly correlated with the treatment feature. Unlike the treatment feature, however, we do not completely observe the confounding features. Formally, the confounding features are defined as follows.

Assumption 4
(Confounding Features)

There exists a unknown deterministic vector-valued function
\bm{g}_{\bm{U}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{U}
that maps an unstructured object
\bm{X}_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
to the confounding features
\bm{U}_{i}\in\mathcal{U}
, i.e.,

\bm{U}_{i}\ =\ \bm{g}_{\bm{U}}(\bm{X}_{i}),

where
\dim(\bm{U}_{i})\ll\dim(\bm{X}_{i})
.

The confounding features are also introduced as a deterministic function of treatment objects in the existing literature on causal inference with texts
(
Fong and Grimmer, 2023
;
Pryzant et al., 2021
)
.

Finally, we assume that the potential outcome is a function of treatment and confounding features and that we can intervene the treatment feature without changing the confounding features. In other words, the treatment feature is not a deterministic function of the confounding features. The confounding features should not be a function of treatment because we only want to control pre-treatment variables
(
Daoud et al., 2022
)
. In the candidate biography application, the assumption essentially implies that researchers need to be able to imagine hypothetical candidate biographies with and without military background while keeping the confounding features constant. We formalize this assumption as follows.

Assumption 5

(Separability of Treatment and Confounding Features)
The potential outcome is a function of the treatment feature of interest
t
and another separate function of the confounding features
\bm{u}
. That is, for any given
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
and all
i

Y_{i}(\bm{x})=Y_{i}(t,\bm{u})=Y_{i}(g_{T}(\bm{x}),\bm{g}_{\bm{U}}(\bm{x})),

where
t=g_{T}(\bm{x})\in\{0,1\}
and
\bm{u}=\bm{g}_{\bm{U}}(\bm{x})\in\mathcal{U}
. In addition,

and

are separable such that there exist no function
\tilde{g}
and

such that

or

\bm{g}_{\bm{U}}(\bm{x})=\tilde{\bm{g}}\circ g_{T}(\bm{x})
for some
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
.

We consider a deep generative model whose decoding is deterministic, meaning that the output text is a deterministic function of the input prompt. Formally, we make the following assumption.

Assumption 6
(Deterministic Decoding)

The output layer of a deep generative model is deterministic. That is, both
\mathbb{P}(\bm{X}_{i}\mid\bm{h}_{\bm{\gamma}}(\bm{R}_{i}))
and
\mathbb{P}(\bm{R}_{i}\mid\bm{P}_{i})
in Definition
1
are degenerate distributions.

Without this assumption, a deep generative model may induce dependency between observations, complicating statistical inference. In addition, if
\mathbb{P}(\bm{X}_{i}\mid\bm{h}_{\bm{\gamma}}(\bm{R}_{i}))
is stochastic, the noise introduced by the deep generative model may confound the treatment in an unknown way. Fortunately, almost all language models satisfy this assumption as they have an option of deterministic decoding including greedy, beam, or contrastive searches. Thus, the assumption can be easily satisfied by changing the hyperparameter of generative model. For example, for greedy decoding, we typically only need to set the temperature parameter to zero, instructing a deep generative model to always produce the same texts
(
Su et al., 2022
, e.g.,)
. Nevertheless, some deep generative models do not satisfy Assumption
6
. They include diffusion and score-based generative models without deterministic sampling, which is used to generate images and videos
(
Ho et al., 2020
;
Song et al., 2020
, e.g.,)
.

Given the above architecture, we estimate the ATE using the double machine learning (DML) framework of

3.4
Practical implementation details

We discuss some important details when implementing the proposed methodology in practice. First, as formally stated above in Assumption
6
, researchers must choose a deep generative model that has an option of deterministic decoding. Aside from appropriately setting hyper-parameters of LLM, the assumption also implies that we should not use batches with LLMs, which may induce unknown correlations across observations. The use of LLMs that have memory should also be avoided.

In addition, the effective implementation of the proposed estimation method requires a careful choice of dimension-reduction strategy for the internal representation as well as the hyperparameter tuning for TarNet. First, the internal representation, which typically corresponds to the last hidden states of a deep generative model, is of high dimension, given by a matrix of size equal to the length of texts
\times
the size of representation for each token. This equals 768 for BERT-base, 1024 for BERT-large and T5-3B, and 4096 for Llama3-8B. As a result, we must apply a pooling operation and obtain a lower dimensional vector.

The type of pooling strategy depends on the architecture of a deep generative model. For example, in BERT, the first special classification token [CLS] contains the entire semantic information
(
Devlin et al., 2019
)
. Thus, one could use the hidden states that correspond to this [CLS] token alone. In BART (Bidirectional and Auto-Regressive Transformers), the special token is added at the end, so researchers might extract the hidden states of the last token
(
Lewis et al., 2020
)
. In contrast, encoder-decoder models like T5 (Text-to-Text Transfer Transformer,
Raffel et al., 2023
) do not have such special tokens, and mean pooling is often applied (e.g.,
Ni et al., 2021
). For decoder-only models, such as Llama
(
Touvron et al., 2023
)
, the autoregressive structure forces all tokens to pay attention only to the past tokens, and hence, we can use the hidden states of the last token.

Second, for TarNet, we must carefully choose hyperparameters such as the size and depth of layers, learning rate, and maximum epoch size. Together, they determine the success of optimization and the quality of deconfounder estimate. The dimension of the deconfounder should be sufficiently large to capture the confounding information but should not be too large to violate the overlap assumption. The learning rate and epoch size are also crucial, as the performance of the proposed methodology depends heavily on the success of the optimization process. As a practical strategy, researchers could try several different values of hyperparameters and select the one that minimizes the loss. If the loss does not decrease within the first few epochs, it is likely that optimization has failed. In such cases, we recommend that researchers try different sets of hyperparameters. This can be done with the advanced automatic hyperparameter optimization methods, such as Optuna
(
Akiba et al., 2019
)
, that search the optimal hyperparameters automatically and efficiently by dynamically constructing the search space.

4
Instrumental Variable Approach to the Perceived Treatment Feature

The methodology described in the previous section enables the estimation of the average causal effect of the treatment feature, which is assumed to be a deterministic function of treatment object. In some cases, however, researchers may be interested in the causal effect of
perceived
treatment feature, which may not necessarily coincide with the treatment feature itself. In addition, the perception of the same treatment feature may vary across respondents. For example, in our application, respondents may disagree as to what constitutes as a military background.

In this section, we extend the proposed methodology to the setting, in which the treatment feature is used as an instrumental variable for the perceived treatment feature. As before, we describe the required assumptions, establish nonparametric identification, and propose estimation and inference strategies.

4.1
Assumptions and causal quantity of interest

We consider the same setting as in Section
3
except that we observe the perceived treatment feature
\widetilde{T}_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, which may not equal the treatment feature itself, i.e.,
\widetilde{T}_{i}\neq T_{i}
for some
i
. We assume that a respondent’s perceived treatment feature is a function of the treatment and confounding features of the assigned treatment object.

Assumption 8
(Perceived Treatment Feature)

The perceived treatment feature
\widetilde{T}_{i}\in\{0,1\}
is a function of treatment and confounding features, i.e.,

\widetilde{T}_{i}\ =\ \widetilde{T}_{i}(T_{i},\bm{U}_{i}),

where
\widetilde{T}_{i}(t,\bm{u})
is the potential value of the perceived treatment feature when the treatment variable
T_{i}
is equal to
t\in\{0,1\}
and the confounding variables
\bm{U}_{i}
equal
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{U}
.

Importantly, under Assumption
8
, different respondents may perceive the same treatment feature differently. In addition, it is also possible for the confounding features to affect the perceived treatment feature. In practice, researchers may measure the perceived treatment feature by directly asking respondents. However, doing so may lead to the so-called
priming bias
, in which the act of asking this question itself draws a respondent’s attention to the treatment feature and confounds the causal effect of interest. To avoid this bias, researchers may measure the perceived treatment feature after the outcome variable is realized. Addressing this methodological issue is beyond the scope of this paper, but interested readers should consult a recent literature on the topic
(
Montgomery et al., 2018
;
Aronow et al., 2019
;
Klar et al., 2020
;
Blackwell et al., 2023
, see e.g.,)
.

To identify the causal effect of perceived treatment feature, we use the treatment feature as an instrumental variable. To do this, we define the potential outcome as a function of the perceived treatment feature, and the treatment and confounding features. Formally, we replace Assumption
5
with the following new separability assumption.

Assumption 9
(Separability with the Perceived Treatment Feature)

The potential outcome is a function of the perceived treatment
\tilde{T}_{i}
, the treatment features of interest
T_{i}
, and the confounding features
\bm{U}_{i}
. That is, for any given
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
and all
i
, we have:

Y_{i}(\bm{x})=Y_{i}(\widetilde{T}_{i}(g_{T}(\bm{x}),\bm{g}_{\bm{U}}(\bm{x})),g_{T}(\bm{x}),\bm{g}_{\bm{U}}(\bm{x}))

where
\widetilde{T}_{i}(g_{T}(\bm{x}),\bm{g}_{\bm{U}}(\bm{x}))\in\{0,1\}
is the perceived treatment feature,
g_{T}(\bm{x})\in\{0,1\}
, and
\bm{g}_{\bm{U}}(\bm{x})\in\mathcal{U}
. In addition,
g_{T}
and
\bm{g}_{\bm{U}}
are separable in a sense that there exist no function
\tilde{g}
such that
g_{T}(\bm{x})=\tilde{g}\circ\bm{g}_{\bm{U}}(\bm{x})
or

\bm{g}_{\bm{U}}(\bm{x})=\tilde{\bm{g}}\circ g_{T}(\bm{x})
for some
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
.

Lastly, we adopt the standard instrumental variable assumptions to the current settings
(
Imbens and Angrist, 1994
)
. First, we assume monotonicity; the existence of treatment feature makes it no less likely for a respondent to perceive it as such. Second, we assume exclusion restriction; the treatment feature only affects the outcome through the perceived treatment feature. Both assumptions are made while holding the confounding features constant. We formally state these assumptions here.

Assumption 10
(Validity of the Instrumental Variable)

We make the following instrumental variable assumptions:

(a)

(Monotonicity) For any
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{U}
, we have:

\widetilde{T}_{i}(1,\bm{u})\geq\widetilde{T}_{i}(0,\bm{u})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{T}_{i}(1,\bm{u})=1)>\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{T}_{i}(0,\bm{u})=1).

(b)

(Exclusion Restriction) For any
\tilde{t}\in\{0,1\}
,
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{U}
, and
i=1,2,\ldots,n
, we have:

Y_{i}(\tilde{t},1,\bm{u})\ =\ Y_{i}(\tilde{t},0,\bm{u})\ =\ Y_{i}(\tilde{t},\bm{u}).

In many practical settings, the monotonicity assumption is reasonable. In our application, for example, if there is no military background in a candidate biography, a respondent should not notice the presence of this treatment feature. Exclusion restriction, however, may not be credible in some cases because it is possible for a respondent to be influenced by the treatment feature without noticing it.

Under this setup, we are interested in estimating the local average treatment effect (LATE) of the perceived treatment feature among the respondents who notice the presence of the treatment feature only when the treatment object actually contains such a feature. We define this LATE as follows:

\displaystyle\beta\ :=\ \mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1,\bm{U}_{i})-Y_{i}(0,\bm{U}_{i})\mid\widetilde{T}_{i}(1,\bm{U}_{i})=1,\widetilde{T}_{i}(0,\bm{U}_{i})=0],

(6)

where the first input of the potential outcome is the perceived treatment feature instead of the treatment feature, i.e.,
Y_{i}(\widetilde{T}_{i}=\tilde{t},\bm{U}_{i}=\bm{u})

5
Simulation Studies

We conduct simulation studies to evaluate the empirical performance of our proposed estimator and compare it to the existing estimators. We use the candidate profile experiment introduced in Section
2
to make the simulation setup realistic.

5.1
Simulation setup

We first generate candidate biographies using an open source LLM, Llama3–8B, that is fine-tuned for instruction-based prompt generations with 8 billion parameters
(
Touvron et al., 2023
)
. We ask the model to create a biography of politicians under a hypothetical name using the system-level and user-level prompts shown in Table
2
. We create hypothetical names by randomly drawing a surname and a first/middle name with replacement from the original corpus of
Fong and Grimmer, 2016
. The total number of biographies in our sample is 4,000.

After generating candidate biographies, we label them based on treatment and confounding features of interest. We consider two scenarios: the first is designed to adhere to the separability assumption (Assumption
5
), while the second is likely to violate it. We focus on the separability assumption because it is the key identifying assumption of the proposed methodology.

For the first scenario, we use a candidate’s military background as the treatment variable. A biography is assigned to the treatment group if it contains at least one of the following keywords: “military,” “war,” “veteran,” or “army.” For confounding features, we first consider a combined topic of politics and education, denoted as
h_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})
where
h_{1}
is a complex function of treatment object. That is, we employ a widely-used embedding-based topic model,
BERTopic

(
Grootendorst, 2022
)
, and then assign
h_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})=1
if a generated biography is classified to a topic whose representative words include “young,” “student,” “college,” “elected,” “senator,” “political,” “graduating,” “education,” and “congressional.” As the second confounding feature, denoted by
h_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})
, we use the sentiment analysis module available in the Python
TextBlob
package, which yields a continuous sentiment polarity score ranging from
-1
to
1
. Since the treatment feature is based on a set of specific keywords that are quite different from the confounding features, the separability assumption is likely to be satisfied under this scenario.

For the second scenario, we use two overlapping topics to define the treatment and confounding features so that the separability assumption is likely to be violated. We again use topics obtained from
BERTopic
, and assign
T_{i}=1
if a generated biography is classified to a topic whose representative words include “college,” “political,” “elected,” “politics,” “student,” “senator,” “education,” “legislative”. For the confounding concept, we set
h_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})=1
if a biography is assigned to a topic whose representative words include “college,” “political,” “senator,” “politics,” “elected,” “student,” “career.” Thus, although the treatment and confounding concepts are coded based on two different topics, they share many representative words, making it likely for the separability assumption to be violated.

Finally, we use the following linear model with an interaction term to generate the outcome variable,

Y_{i}\ =\ \alpha_{1}T_{i}+\alpha_{2}T_{i}h_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})-\alpha_{3}h_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})-\alpha_{4}h_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})+\epsilon_{i},

where
\epsilon_{i}
is the standard normal random variable. Although the model may appear to be a relatively simple function of the treatment and confounding features, these variables themselves are complex functions of texts. Thus, inferring the ATE is not straightforward because it requires learning an accurate representation of confounding features.

To evaluate the performance of each estimator, we assume that researchers do not have access to the confounding features,
h_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})
and
h_{2}(\bm{X}_{i})
. We wish to infer the ATE, which is given by
\tau=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}\mathbb{E}[h_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})]
where we set
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=10
throughout the simulations. We consider the three scenarios: (1) weak confounding
\alpha_{3}=\alpha_{4}=50
, (2) moderate confounding
\alpha_{3}=\alpha_{4}=100
, (3) strong confounding
\alpha_{3}=\alpha_{4}=1000
. Given the computational cost, our evaluation is conditional on a single set of generated biographies and its associated treatment and confounding variables. Therefore,
\mathbb{E}[h_{1}(\bm{X}_{i})]
is set to its sample mean, and randomness comes only from the error term of the outcome model
\epsilon_{i}
.

5.2
Estimators to be compared

Once Llama3 generates all biographies, we extract an internal representation of each biography from the last hidden layer whose dimension is 4096
\times
the number of tokens contained in the biography. For text reuse, we simply ask the LLM to repeat each of the generated texts. To compute the proposed estimator, we follow the discussion presented in Section
3.4
and use the representation of the last token, yielding a 4096 dimensional vector of internal representation for each candidate biography.

In our neural network architecture, we use one linear layer for the deconfounder
\bm{f}(\bm{R}_{i})
whose output dimension is 2048. Similarly, we utilize two consecutive linear layers for the potential outcome models whose output dimensions are 500 and 1. We apply ReLU as an activation function between each layer, and also use a dropout rate of 0.15 to prevent overfitting. We select the neural network architecture, including dropout rates, layer depth, and dimension of each layer, based on additional hyperparameter tuning under the weak confounding setting. While we do not conduct hyperparameter tuning for each setting separately, we find that minor changes in these hyperparameters do not significantly affect the value of the loss function.

We use 40% of our data as a training set, 10% as a validation set, and the remaining 50% is used to perform the downstream causal effect estimation. For optimization, we set the batch size to 32 and train the model for 500 epochs, and use early stopping to prevent overfitting. Specifically, we stop training if the estimated loss does not improve for more than 15 epochs. Since the performance of the methodology can depend on the choice of hyperparameters, especially learning rates, we select the learning rates using an automated hyperparameter tuning package
Optuna
. We do not include the size of the deconfounder as a hyperparameter to be tuned because tuning it for the BERT-based methods is computationally too expensive. We also do not observe any substantial difference in performance when varying the dimensions of the deconfounder for the proposed methodology. Once the outcome model is fitted, we estimate the propensity score via a random forest classifier with the estimated deconfounder, using the
scikit-learn
library.

We evaluate the performance of the proposed estimator in comparison to the following three estimators. First, to establish a baseline, we use the difference-in-means estimator, which does not make any adjustment for confounding. Second, we implement the two existing approaches that estimate vectorized representation of texts using the BERT embedding
\bm{b}(\cdot)
;
Pryzant et al., 2021
estimates nuisance functions with TarNet and calculates the causal effect using a T-learner, while
Gui and Veitch, 2023
uses the same outcome models as
Pryzant et al., 2021
but applies DML with the estimated propensity score. Importantly, both of these approaches are based on the following loss function,

\frac{\lambda}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{Y_{i}-Q_{T_{i}}(\bm{b}(\bm{X}_{i}))\right\}^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\biggl\{T_{i}\log g(\bm{b}(\bm{X}_{i}))+(1-T_{i})\log[1-g(\bm{b}(\bm{X}_{i}))]\biggr\}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}B(\bm{b}_{\mathrm{full}}(\bm{X}_{i}))

(8)

where
Q_{t}(\cdot)
is the outcome model under
T_{i}=t
,
g(\cdot)
is the treatment prediction,
B(\cdot)
is the original BERT masked language loss for estimating vector representations of texts from the embedding
\bm{b}(\cdot)
, and
\alpha,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
are the hyperparameters
(
Veitch et al., 2020
)
. Only the first token’s vector representation
\bm{b}(\cdot)
is used for outcome and treatment predictions because it is the special token called [CLS] token that contains the entire semantic information. On the other hand, for the masked language loss, the entire embedding
\bm{b}_{\mathrm{full}}(\cdot)
is used. The loss function given in Equation (
8
) differs from our loss function (Equation (
3
)) in that in addition to the outcome model, it optimizes the representation learning from texts and the treatment prediction.

After estimating the nuisance functions,
Gui and Veitch, 2023
proposes to estimate the propensity score model by treating
Q_{1}(\bm{b}(\bm{X}_{i}))
and
Q_{0}(\bm{b}(\bm{X}_{i}))
as covariates, i.e.,
\Pr(T_{i}=1\mid\bm{X}_{i})=g(Q_{1}(\bm{b}(\bm{X}_{i})),Q_{0}(\bm{b}(\bm{X}_{i})))
. For propensity score estimation, we use a Gaussian Process, as recommended by
Gui and Veitch, 2023
. We truncate the extreme values of the estimated propensity scores at 0.01 and 0.99 for DR-learner with BERT though such truncation is not necessary for the proposed methodology. For DR-learner with BERT, this truncation occurs even when the separability assumption is satisfied (roughly 5% of time for weak and moderate confounding, and 45% for strong confounding). Without truncation, the bias and RMSE are not computable for these methods. Finally, we follow the default hyperparameter in the original code of the authors and set
\alpha=\lambda=1
while tuning the other hyperparameters regarding the learning rate in the same way as done for our estimator.

7
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we demonstrate that the use of generative AI can significantly enhance the validity of causal inference with unstructured treatments, such as texts and images. The proposed methodology leverages generative AI to both efficiently produce a variety of textual treatments and precisely control confounding bias. By utilizing the true vector representation of generated texts, we avoid need to estimate such representation from texts as done in the previous methods, leading to more efficient and robust causal effect estimation. We formalize the conditions required for nonparametric identification, showing that the separability of treatment and confounding features plays an essential role. We also develop an estimation strategy based on a neural network architecture that mitigate the risk of positivity violation, which commonly occurs to the existing methods. Lastly, we extend the proposed methodology to the settings of perceived treatments, using an instrumental variables approach. Our simulation study shows that the proposed estimator outperforms the existing methods, which estimate the representation of textual treatments from texts themselves.

Although we have focused on texts as treatments in this paper, our proposed approach can be extended to other types of unstructured data, such as images and videos. For images, we expect the proposed methodology to work as it is so long as the dimensionality of internal representation is relatively low. If the dimensionality is high, architectures like convolutional neural networks may be needed to effectively reduce input dimensions. For videos, both treatment and confounding features are likely to exhibit complex relationships due to the combination of audio and images with the additional temporal dimension. Thus, the methodology described here is not readily applicable. Future work should consider how to leverage the internal representation of videos obtained from generative AI.
