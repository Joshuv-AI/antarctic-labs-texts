On the independence problem of Newton’s first law
Ido Yavetz, Ehud Aharoni

Abstract
Newton’s laws of motion pose an apparent problem, sometimes referred to as “the independence problem”: the first law seems to be a simple consequence of the second law, hence it is unclear why it is included as a separate law. Many different possible answers to this question appear in the literature. The main contribution of this paper is to offer two additional answers to this problem. One of which we call “the formal explanation,” since unlike all previous explanations it relies on mathematical formalism, and the other, "the logical explanation", derives the importance of the first law from the fact it states of a more fundamental natural property. A second contribution is a comprehensive review of previous suggested explanations, which we categorize and discuss as well. We provide arguments why we think our novel explanations are the most plausible.



1
Introduction

Newton’s laws of motion contain within them a puzzle: the first law seems redundant. This law, reformulated in modern English, states “Every body not subject to the action of forces continues in its state of rest or uniform motion in a straight line
(
Ellis 1965
, p. 35)
.
The second law can be formulated using the equation
F=ma
: the force equals the product of mass and acceleration. Substituting
F=0
in this formula we obtain that
a=0
, hence the second law implies that without forces a body will have zero acceleration. This statement is equivalent to the first law and therefore the first law is an implication of the second.

These two laws (together with the third one, the law of action and reaction) are listed in Newton’s
Principia
under the title “Laws or axioms.” The question is therefore, why did Newton include as an axiom a statement that appears to be a simple consequence of another axiom?

This problem, sometimes referred to as “the independence problem”
(
Hoek 2023
)
, has received some attention in the literature. Thomas Kuhn writes about it:

“Newton’s first law is a consequence of his second, and Newton’s reason for stating them separately has long been a puzzle.”
(
Kuhn 2000
)

Without a direct answer to this puzzle from Newton himself, we can only offer conjectures based on analysis of the original texts, consideration of the world view and conceptions of Newton and his contemporaries, and logical and philosophical arguments.

Numerous authors addressed this question, and proposed different kinds of answers. Some, for example, suggest that the first law carries with it additional meaning or implications, more than what is apparent from a superficial reading of its text. Exposing this deeper meaning makes it clear the first law is not redundant. Others say that the first law is in fact redundant, but was added for rhetorical or pedagogical reasons. And many more solutions have been offered, as we discuss at length below.

In this paper we propose a novel answer that approaches the issue from a new angle. We suggest that the reason for the existence of the first law is due to a technicality related to the proper use of Euclidean geometry. We call this “the formal explanation.” We further propose a second answer, which we term “the logical explanation,” stating that the first law’s importance is due to capturing a more basic and general property of nature. Such a view is already known in the literature, but we sharpen and extend it. In addition, we summarize and categorize the different existing answers, and discuss their strengths and weaknesses.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
details our novel answers. Section 3 reviews the relevant literature. Section 4 discusses the different approaches, arguing in support of our proposed answers, and Section 5 provides the conclusion of the paper.

2
Novel Explanations

2.1
The formal explanation

In this section we propose a new solution to the independence problem, which relies on an understanding of the mathematical formalism used in the
Principia
.

Newton chose to formulate the
Principia
in terms of Euclidean geometry. One reason for this might have been that at the time number theories did not rest on rigorous mathematical foundations, while Euclidean geometry did. Other authors suggest this reason and other reasons
(
Densmore 2003
;
Maudlin 2012
)
. Regardless of the reason, the fact of the matter is the theorems presented in the
Principia
are phrased using Euclidean concepts and their proofs rely on known geometric theorems, sometimes citing Euclid’s Elements directly.

Due to this choice of mathematical framework, the second law was not presented in the
Principia
as the numerical formula
F=ma
, but stated verbally:

"A change in motion is proportional to the motive force impressed and takes place along the straight line in which that force is impressed."
(
Newton et al. 1999
)

Even though it is expressed in words, it is still a mathematical law, stating that two magnitudes, force and change in motion, are in proportion. We argue that it should be understood in Euclidean terms, as does the rest of the mathematics in the
Principia
. Doing so makes it inevitably clear that the second law is not applicable to the cases of either no force or no acceleration, hence a separate law was added to cover this case.

Book 5 in Euclid’s Elements rigorously defines the concept of proportion. We quote here the three relevant definitions:

“4. (Those) magnitudes are said to have a ratio with respect to one another which, being multiplied, are capable of exceeding one another.

5. Magnitudes are said to be in the same ratio, the first to the second, and the third to the fourth, when equal multiples of the first and the third either both exceed, are both equal to, or are both less than, equal multiples of the second and the fourth, respectively, being taken in corresponding order, according to any kind of multiplication whatever.

6. And let magnitudes having the same ratio be called proportional.”
(
Fitzpatrick 2008
)

Definition 4 states that to have a ratio, magnitudes should be capable of exceeding one another when multiplied. This means that a zero magnitude cannot have a ratio with any other magnitude, zero or non-zero. Definition 5 specifies when the ratios of two pairs of magnitudes are said to be the same, and definition 6 states that in this case they are called proportional. Hence we can conclude that a zero magnitude is not in proportion with any other magnitude.

Since Newton’s second law uses the Euclidean term
proportion
, it is clear it applies only to non-zero force and acceleration. In contrast, in the algebraic formula
F=ma
, where the variables represent numbers or vectors, we can substitute
F=0
and obtain the first law. We cannot do so in the Euclidean formulation of the second law, because the Euclidean definition of proportion becomes inapplicable. Thus, the case of a zero force needs a separate treatment, and this is the role of the first law: to provide this separate treatment.

We further note that the separation into two laws is aligned with the general approach in Euclidean geometry of distinguishing between a magnitude and the absence of a magnitude as two distinct cases. In Euclid’s Elements magnitudes can be of different kinds, such as lines or angles, but they are always taken to be non-zero
(
Grattan-Guinness 1996
, p. 363)
. For example, the definition of an angle in Book 1 reads:

“8. And a plane angle is the inclination of the lines, when two lines in a plane meet one another, and are not laid down straight-on with respect to one another.”
(
Fitzpatrick 2008
)

The last part of this definition rules out a zero angle.
1
1

1

The definition does allow for curvilinear angles, such as the angle between a circle and its tangent, also known as a horn angle. Such angles are mentioned in the Elements, though rarely (Book 3, proposition 16), and are still considered different than zero
(
Grattan-Guinness 1996
)
.

According to this definition, if we place two lines directly one on top of the other, then this is not a special case of an angle whose size is zero. It is a different case, where we can say that an angle is absent. Similarly, we suggest that the two magnitudes Newton’s second law refers to, change in motion and force, are non-zero magnitudes. The case of their absence is handled separately by the first law.

We have demonstrated that the first law does not follow from the second law by mathematical reasoning when the underlying mathematics is Euclidean geometry. However, one might argue that the
Principia
is not a mathematical text, but a text of mathematical-physics. Therefore, we might be asking whether the first law follows from the second not by mathematical reasoning only, but by a combination of mathematical and physical reasoning. An example of this type of reasoning is given by Aristotle
(
Aristotle 2000
, p. 46)
. Aristotle starts from the assumption that the distance that a body endowed with weight moves naturally is proportional to its weight. He goes on to prove that a weightless body will not move at all. His argument is as follows: If the weightless body moves a certain distance AB, and a body endowed with weight moves some other distance AC at the same time, then we can use the proportion assumption to construct another body endowed with weight that moves the distance AB. Aristotle concludes that having both the weightless body and the body endowed with weight moving the same distance is absurd.
2
2

2

This proof from Aristotle’s book is followed by a second argument, that is not directly related to this paper, but still is interesting in relation to Newton’s work. In this segment Aristotle uses a similar style of argument to prove that there cannot be a body that is entirely weightless. Here and in other places we can see Aristotle had some anticipation of Newton’s concept of mass.

This absurdity stems from physical intuition, and this argument is compelling from the physical point of view. A similar argument can be adapted to Newton’s framework: The case of a force that generates the same acceleration as no force at all is physically absurd. Mathematically however, because proportionality does not deal with the case of the absence of one of the magnitudes, the case of no magnitude is mathematically undefined. And we could from the purely formal mathematical point of view associate any arbitrary acceleration with no force. It does not make sense physically, but it does not create a mathematical absurdity. Our argument is that this is precisely the conflict Newton wished to avoid by adding the first law. With the first law explicitly stated, the issue if fully settled: both mathematically and physically the absence of a force requires the absence of acceleration.

In summary, the algebraic formula
F=ma
covers the case
F=a=0
as any other valid satisfying assignment. Describing this case in an additional, separate axiom is superfluous. But when the underlying mathematical formalism is unable to handle the case of zero together with the non-zero magnitudes, this separation into two axioms is the simplest and most natural choice.

2.2
The logical explanation

We now propose a second novel explanation to justify stating the first law as a separate law. According to this explanation, even if the second law is formulated algebraically, in which case our previous explanation does not hold, then still the first law has a role to play. This role is to require a more fundamental natural property. It can continue to to hold even if it turns out the second law is different than the one described in the
Principia
.

The first law states that in the absence of a force a body will preserve its state of motion. If a body is under the influence of forces then its velocity might change in direction or magnitude. But the first law does not specify how it will change, and allows for any relationship between force and acceleration. It is the second law that describes such a specific relationship: proportion.

Thus, we can view the first law as a more general principle. Imagining possible alternative worlds, there can be worlds where the first law holds, but there is a different quantitative relationship between force and acceleration, for example
F=\sqrt{ma}
or
F=ma^{2}
. That is, each such alternative world has a different second law, all of them consistent with the same first law.

In the
Principia
, the role of the second law is to specify this quantitative relationship. In its algebraic form,
F=ma
, the first law becomes redundant in the sense that it adds no information that is not already contained within the second law. But the importance of the first law, we argue, stems from the fact it can remain a valid axiom even if the more specific relationship is refuted. We note that this actually occurred: Relativity changed the second law, but the first law remained the same
(
Taylor 2005
, p. 17)
.

In this view, the fact that the first law can be obtained from the second by substituting
F=0
shows that the quantitative relationship set down by the second law is compatible with the first, but it makes neither redundant.

3
Literature review

In this section we provide an overview of the different solutions to the independence problem offered in the literature.

3.1
Pedagogical and rhetorical reasons

Kuhn suggests that the reason for the first law is pedagogical. The second law uses the terms mass and force (Newton’s original formulation does not use the term mass, but motion, and through definition 2 we understand it means what we call today momentum), which makes it more difficult to understand. Especially so since Newton used these terms with different meanings than his predecessors. The first law was therefore added to help the readers take their first steps in understanding the new theory.

“The answer may well lie in pedagogical strategy. If Newton had permitted the second law to subsume the first, his readers would have had to sort out his use of ‘force’ and of ‘mass’ together, an intrinsically difficult task further complicated by the fact that the terms had previously been different not only in their individual use but in their interrelation. Separating them to the extent possible displayed the nature of the required changes more clearly.”
(
Kuhn 2000
, p. 68)

Other authors suggested that the reason is rhetorical: Newton wanted to emphasize the difference between the new theory and its predecessors, especially the Aristotelian theory:

“Hence, we can undercut the search for an independent message hidden in the first law by viewing it as inaugurating a new ideal of natural order. In other word, the real role of the first law is as a slap in the face of common sense and Aristotle. It is intended to jolt us into a new way of looking at nature.”
(
Sorensen 1992
, p. 10)

Cohen makes a similar statement:

“First, in Newton’s day – as during many preceding centuries – the common belief was that all motion requires a mover, a moving force. The very statement of this law as an axiom was a radical step, a declaration of an important new principle of motion, too important to be a special case of another law.”
(
Cohen 2002
, p. 69)

Cohen’s identification of the first law as an important principle of nature shares some similarities with our logical explanation (Section
2.2
). Though note that Cohen derives its importance from the rejection of previously held beliefs, hence we classified it here as a rhetorical reason, whereas our explanation is future facing. As Newton describes in the
Principia
, all evidence up to that point in time was consistent with both the first and the second laws. Future evidence might either continue to support both, refute both, or refute just the second law and keep supporting the first. Hence we derive the importance of the first law from its potential to endure such future developments.
3
3

3

Additional authors expressing a view similar to Cohen’s are
(
Galili and Tseitlin 2003
)
. They rely on Russian translations of the
Principia
to conclude that the first law not only states that with the absence of a force an object preserves its state, but that it also implies that forces cause a sequence state changes.
They use this extended meaning of the first law to support the view that the first law is more general than the second, similar to our logical explanation. But similarly to Cohen, they state that the importance of the first law comes from expressing a radical conceptual change in the understanding of motion.

3.2
The inertial frame explanation

Many physics textbooks state that the role of the first law is to define what an inertial frame of reference is, to identify it, or to postulate that such a frame exists:
4
4

4

In addition to the three books cited here, see more books cited in
(
Galili and Tseitlin 2003
;
Earman and Friedman 1973
)
.

“The real assertion [of the first law] is, rather: There exists a coordinate system (or coordinate systems) with respect to which all bodies not subjected to forces are unaccelerated”.
(
Bergmann 1976
, p. 8)

“Another thing [the first law] does is give a definition of an inertial frame.”
(
Morin 2004
, p. 52)

“Newton’s first law of motion is the assertion that inertial systems exist”
(
Kleppner and Kolenkow 2013
, p. 51)

Some of these books are vague whether this was the original reason the first law was introduced or whether it is a new modern interpretation of the laws of motion. This vagueness may have contributed to a confusion over this matter, causing a quite widespread misconception that Newton offered this explanation himself, as can be found in some books and web-based publications.

Researchers that address the independence problem clearly separate the discussion between possible modern explanations and Newton’s original reasons. The term “inertial frame” did not exist in Newton’s time, and he used a different concept, “Absolute space.” This concept, as other concepts used by the laws of motion, is explained in the
Principia
in a separate “Definitions” section preceding that of the laws. Hence most scholarly works on this subject reject the plausibility of the inertial frame explanation, arguing that the
Principia
’s text offers no support for it.

Zinkernagel, for example, accepts the inertial frame explanation as a modern explanation, but rejects it as a possible reason for Newton:

“Now, one way to understand Newton’s first law in a non-redundant way is precisely to construct it as an existence claim . . . On this account, Newton’s first law implies that inertial frames . . . exist. Newton himself did not need the first law to secure the existence of an inertial frame as he independently (of the laws) assumed the existence of absolute space.”
(
Zinkernagel 2010
, p. 5)

Earman and Friedman agree:

“In the
Principia
, the statement of Law I, . . . immediately follows the Scholium on Absolute Space. Thus, it is clear that Law I is to be taken to refer to motion with respect to Absolute Space . . . In any case, Newton did not intend nor did he have any need to use the First Law as a definition of inertial frame; because of the assumed existence of Absolute Space, Law I made good sense as it stood.”
(
Earman and Friedman 1973
, p. 338)

We share the views cited above: the inertial frame explanation mismatches the
Principia
’s text and is implausible.

The association between the first law and the definition of inertial frames began in the late 19
th
century, with early definitions of this concept
(
DiSalle 2020
)
. We note that these proposed modern interpretations that justify the independence of the first law are outside the scope of this paper. We focus solely on Newton’s reasons for stating the first law separately.
5
5

5

We did find one scholarly work stating that the inertial frame explanation may have been Newton’s reason for stating the first law separately
(
O’Sullivan 1980
)
, though they do not address the question of how the above-mentioned structure of the
Principia
’s text can be reconciled with such a view.

3.3
Other definitory roles

Some authors suggest that the first law’s role is to define some concepts other than inertial frames.

Zinkernagel
(
Zinkernagel 2010
)
claims the first law’s role is to define absolute time. Here too he may suggest this explanation as a modern interpretation and not as the original reason for this law. Similarly, Arons
(
Arons 1997
)
states the first law provides a qualitative operational definition of a force, again without attributing this explanation directly to Newton.

Earman and Friedman provide a similar explanation, but they do refer to Newton directly:

“Secondly, Law I is psychologically if not logically the source of Law II for Newton since he introduces forces as the cause of deviation from inertial motion.”
(
Earman and Friedman 1973
, p. 346)

Ellis 1965
claims that “force” is not an object that exists in reality, but a convention only, arising from us choosing to treat uniform motion as the natural motion. The first law’s role is to define what a natural motion is, and thus also what a force is.

Maudlin 2012
similarly claims the first law defines the concept of a “state of motion”:

“The Second Law is conceptually parasitic on the First Law: without the definition of a ‘state of motion’ in the First Law, we could make no sense of ‘change in motion’ in the Second”
(
Maudlin 2012
, p. 19)

Koyré
(
Koyré 1965
, p. 66)
also claims that it is the first law that asserts the meaning of a state of motion. Koyré emphasizes the importance of this in context of the previous belief since Aristotle that motion is a process of change and not a state.

All these definitory explanations suffer from a significant weakness. As stated above, the
Principia
has a separate “Definitions” section preceding that of the laws of motion. And the laws are stated as “Axioms.” Hence we think it is unlikely Newton had intended for the laws to play a definitory role, as he clearly separated between definitions and the axioms that rely on these definitions.

We further discuss these definitory explanations in greater depth in Section
4
.

3.4
Historical influence

Another solution to the independence problem is that Newton’s choice to formulate two separate laws is due to influence from his predecessors, specifically Descartes, Galileo, and Huygens
(
Newton et al. 1999
;
Cohen 2002
)
.

Descartes is identified as the source of the first law and the notion that a uniform motion constitutes a state. Huygens, identified as the most important influence, had two hypotheses that are similar to Newton’s first two laws. The first hypothesis stated a body free of air resistance and gravity will move with uniform velocity in a straight line. The second hypothesis described the movement of an object under gravity.

In a statement in the
Principia
, Newton claims that Galileo knew of the two laws of motion and derived his results from them. This may be indicative of influence by previous works, though Newton’s knowledge of Galileo may have come from secondary sources.

One way to understand the historical influence explanation, is that it acknowledges that the first law is redundant. It is just there due to continuity with previous theories. We find this unlikely. Newton chose the word “Axioms” to describe his laws, hence he seemed to think of both of them as important and independent.

Another way to understand this explanation, is that Newton indeed borrowed the separation between the two laws from his predecessors, but also saw a good reason to keep it, and label both these laws axioms. This leaves the historical influence explanation incomplete. The missing – and more interesting - part is: what was this reason?

3.5
Narrowing the scope of the second law

Faced with the problem that the first law is a special case of the second, a possible solution is to narrow the scope of the second law, until it no longer covers the first.

The formal explanation we offered in Section
2.1
belongs to this class. According to this explanation, the first law has its usual, simple meaning, but the second law is narrowed down to apply to non-zero forces only, due to the conventional and proper use of Euclidean geometry.

Koslow 1969
similarly suggests that the second law is only meant to cover the case of non-zero Forces. His reasons are different though: he suggests that Newton thought of forces as external causes and the second law is used when these causes are present. The first law is needed for the case these causes are absent:

“Newton believed that forces which act on a body are external causes whose effect is the body’s motion. . . . Newton’s second law, I suggest, does not state without qualification that the total force on a body equals mass times acceleration. This law has sense only if there are external causes acting upon a body. That is, the second law should be read as follows: if there are forces acting upon a body, then the total of all these forces equals the product of the body’s mass and acceleration . . . A statement of what happens when causes are present does not entail a statement describing what happens when those causes are absent.”
(
Koslow 1969
, p. 551-552)

We note however a weakness of this explanation. The second law is stated as a mathematical law and the physical phenomena it describes are quantified mathematically. The question remains therefore: why cannot zero quantify the case of the absence of causes? Our formal explanation fully answers this question.

Cohen and Whitman offer a different explanation, that in some sense narrows the scope of both laws. They claim that second law deals with impulse forces, whereas the first law, based on the examples Newton offers to it, refers to continually acting forces:

“Thus a possible clue to Newton’s thinking is found in examples used in the discussion of the first law, analyzed below, each of which is (unlike the forces in law 2) a continually acting force. Accordingly, we may conclude that law 1 is not a special case of law 2 since law 1 is concerned with a different kind of force.”
(
Newton et al. 1999
, p. 110)

Cohen and Whitman themselves acknowledge a significant weakness of this explanation, that Newton sometimes applies his second law for continually acting forces as well.
(
Newton et al. 1999
, p. 112)

Hoek 2023
resolves the independence problem in yet a different way, where he both augments the first law and narrows down the second law, hence we list this explanation here. Hoek claims that the usual meaning attributed to the first law is incorrect, and it should be understood in a stronger way.

In the traditional meaning attributed to the first law, which Hoek terms the “weak reading,” the law applies to bodies that no forces act on them, or in other words, it applies to free particles. The “strong reading” he offers is different, stating that any acceleration of a body is caused by a force:

“Every change in a body’s state of uniform rectilinear motion is compelled by the forces impressed on that body.”
(
Hoek 2023
, p. 62)

Hoek justifies this strong reading by various means: analysis of the original text and the examples Newton describes for its use. One further such justification is demonstrating how the strong reading solves the independence problem. For this Hoek offers an interpretation of the second law as well. Instead of reading it as a relationship between the total force and the total acceleration, Hoek suggests it should be read as a relationship between a single force and the acceleration caused by it:

“Accordingly, the ‘change in motion’ that
L_{II}
[the second law] describes is a ‘change in motion due to a particular impressed force’”
(
Hoek 2023
, p. 65)

The extension to multiple forces is handled not by the second law, but by Corollary 2. With this interpretation, the second law does not rule out the possibility of acceleration caused by other causes which are not forces. This is the gap the first law is intended to fill:

“But – and this is the crucial part – it is the First Law, not the Second, which tells us that any additional diversion from the state of motion would have to be due to further impressed forces.”
(
Hoek 2023
, p. 66)

The weak reading of the first law is enough to rule out the Aristotelian world view. For example, it rules out that the natural tendency of an earthly object is towards the center of the Earth. But more generally, if a body is already subjected to forces, then the weak reading cannot rule out that this body has some additional acceleration on top of that caused by the forces. The strong reading closes this option completely, explicitly stating every acceleration is caused by a force.

An obvious weakness of Hoek’s explanation is the need for the “strong reading” for the first law to fully serve its suggested purpose. This reading does not match the literal phrasing of the first law; hence it raises the question why the first law was not phrased in such a way as to more clearly convey this stronger interpretation.

A second weakness is that in Newton’s terminology, what causes acceleration is a force. If there is some natural phenomenon that causes acceleration, Newton describes it using the concept of force. Therefore, the statement that only forces cause acceleration is not so much a law of nature, but more of a declaration of a conceptual framework. In this respect, Hoek’s explanation is similar to the definitory explanations we saw before.
Indeed, the ‘Definitions’ section of the
Principia
mentions only forces as possible causes for acceleration.

4
Discussion

We have seen solutions to the independence problem that address it in different ways. We can group them into three general approaches.

One approach is to accept that the first law is logically redundant in the
Principia
, and that it was stated for other reasons. Such are the rhetorical, pedagogical, and historical influence explanations. This raises the question whether, given these assumptions, Newton’s description of the laws as “axioms” is justified. If Newton’s intention was solely that of introduction of a simple case or emphasis of a certain case, he could have done so using an additional preliminary section or using a corollary. Similarly if he was influenced by past theories: a single axiom and a corollary would seem a better fit. Labeling both laws as ‘axioms’ seems to us as a commitment to something more. Thus, we think Newton did view all the laws as independent and essential in the foundation of his theory, and therefore we believe this approach leads to less likely explanations.

A second approach is to augment the first law with additional content, beyond the mere statement that free particles have zero acceleration. The inertial frame explanation and the other definitory roles use this approach. These explanations claim that the additional content of the first law is implied by its simple statement. This raises a question: if this content is implied by the first law, and the first law is implied by the second law, then again, why do we need the first law? For example, if we accept that the first law is needed to postulate the existence of an inertial frame of reference, we can first derive it from the second law, then use it for this purpose. The answer is that according to these views the first law becomes a prerequisite of the second one. The additional content it implies is a key without which we cannot access the second law. Reiterating Maudlin’s words: “The Second Law is conceptually parasitic on the First Law.”

A full discussion and assessment of this type of argument is beyond the scope of this paper. Here we focus on whether these were Newton’s reasons for stating the first law. As noted previously, we find that these views are not supported by the
Principia
’s text and structure. First, the definitions section seems to be the one providing the prerequisites to the laws. Second, we may ask why was the first law not phrased in a way such that the key aspect of its meaning is more clearly expressed.

One of these explanations does require some additional attention: The suggestion of Maudlin and Koyré that the role of the first law is to define what a natural motion is, and that without proper understanding of this concept we cannot fully understand the second law. This explanation might provide a stronger case, since the
Principia
does not contain an explicit definition of natural motion. However, we provide two reasons that we think weaken this explanation as well.

First, the definitions section does describe natural motion: in both the definition of vis insita (definition 3) and the definition of an impressed force (definition 4). Therefore, once we reach the laws section, we already have an understanding of this concept.

Second, and perhaps more importantly, we argue that the second law can be fully understood by relying solely on the definition of quantity of motion (definition 2), and the properties of a Euclidean space (Euclidean space as a description of space is obvious in the 17
th
century as no other alternatives were conceived). The second law states that a force generates a proportional motion in the same direction as the force. The description that follows it elaborates that this change is added to the existing motion using what we call today vector addition. Hence a tiny force that is close to zero would cause only a tiny change in the object’s quantity of motion and in its direction. If the second law is formulated algebraically, then we can further deduce that a zero force causes zero change in both quantity and direction. This gives us the first law and a definition of a natural motion. In conclusion, the second law does not rely on the first law in any way; it can be fully understood based on explicit prior definitions and the underlying mathematical framework.

A third approach we discussed for solving the independence problem is to narrow the scope of the second law. This approach cleanly removes the overlap between the first two laws, and leaves them as two independent axioms. Among the explanations using this approach, we believe that the formal explanation has some advantages that strengthen the case it is correct.

First, it is very simple. The formal explanation suggests the reason for the separation between the first law and the second law is due to the definition of proportion in Euclidean geometry and more generally due to the convention of treating magnitudes and lack of magnitudes separately. Therefore, the reason for this separation is technical and, in some sense, trivial.

This triviality is a strength of the formal explanation, since it answers another question: Why did Newton not address the independence problem himself? We know that he paid much attention in the
Principia
to the logical foundations of his work. All the other reasons we have reviewed in this paper (except the historical influence one) seem important and interesting enough to be communicated to the
Principia
’s readers, and Newton would have either phrased the laws more clearly, or explained it in a Scholium section. Silence supports the case Newton did not see here any special difficulty that needs to be explained to 17
th
century readers.

Second, there are additional examples in the
Principia
where we see separate treatment of magnitudes and lack of magnitudes. For example, the first law itself describes two separate states: “rest” and “moving uniformly.” It is clear from this separation that “moving” refers to a non-zero velocity.

Another explanation that uses this approach is Koslow’s explanation. Koslow suggests that the separation between the laws is a logical separation between the effects of external causes and the effect of the lack of such causes. This explanation does not contradict the formal explanation, but complements it, and can viewed as relying upon it. The second law is formulated using mathematical tools, and the modern tools we use today can cover both effects with a single formula. As the formal explanation points out, the geometric tools used by Newton cannot do so. The view that the case of an active external cause is altogether different than the case it is absent echoes the view of Euclidean geometry, and contrasts with the view of modern mathematical tools.

The logical explanation we presented in Section
2.2
can be viewed as combining two different approaches. On one hand, it uses the first approach of accepting the redundancy within the
Principia
. In the algebraic formulation of the second law,
F=ma
, that includes the case of zero, the first law from the purely logical point of view is not required as an axiom. Newton’s theory stands for every application because the case of zero is covered as well. On the other hand, it also uses the second approach of augmenting the first law with content. The full extended meaning of the first law is that regardless of how acceleration and force are related, no force means no acceleration.Even if the second law is false, the replacement will still have to ensure that no force means no acceleration.Thus, the first law in its full generality is not derivable from the second law.

A possible weakness of this explanation is that if this was an important consideration then the first law could have been further broken down to two even smaller laws:

Law 1A: In the absence of forces, a body at rest will continue in this state.

Law 1B: In the absence of forces, a body moving with uniform motion will continue in this state.

Here too, law 1A commits to less and is therefore more general. We can imagine a world where law 1A holds, but moving bodies do not preserve their state even in the absence of a force.

Not breaking down the first law in this way can be indicative there was no intention to phrase laws as minimal and as secure as possible. Alternately, we might explain it by claiming Newton did not view rest and uniform motion as two entirely separate states, but as different modes of the same state. Such a view may be supported by the use of the singular form of the word ‘state’ in the first law’s phrasing.

We saw one more explanation that combines two approaches: Hoek narrows the scope of the second law to deal with one force only, leaving room for non-force causes of acceleration, while he augments the first law to read “Only forces cause acceleration.” As we discussed above, we think this approach has similar weaknesses as the other explanations that augment the first law.

5
Conclusion

This paper summarized the various answers given to the independence question. As we have shown, different authors chose to approach this question in different ways.

We further offered novel explanations. One is the formal argument that the case of zero needs to be addressed explicitly and separately in the framework of Euclidean geometry. Second is the identification of the first law as a general principle that may outlast the second law.

As we noted above, the independence problem has caused some confusion, especially revolving around the inertial frame explanation. Many general education texts are unclear whether they explain why Newton himself formulated this law, or whether they present modern views. Furthermore, they present as fact something that is open to multiple interpretations. Even in the broader discussions in the academic literature, papers usually touch upon the independence problem briefly and in passing, presenting an incomplete account of it. We hope that our paper will help remedy these problems.

Lastly, we should always remember that Newton himself never raised the question (let alone answer it) of why the two separate laws are needed. Therefore, any explanation, plausible as it may be, is still historical speculation.
