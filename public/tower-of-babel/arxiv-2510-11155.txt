Abstract

We prove that for every tower 𝒯 \mathcal{T} there are ℵ 1 \aleph_{1} -dense A A and B B so that any “reasonable” forcing notion ℙ \mathbb{P} - an adjective that includes all known ones - for making A A and B B isomorphic will add a pseudointersection for the tower. 1.1.
Preliminaries

The rest of this section is devoted to recalling a few facts and definitions we will need. Our notation is mostly standard, conforming to the monographs
[
9
]
and
[
10
]
. We also refer the reader to
[

]

4
]
for any undefined notions about cardinal characteristics. To begin, we recall the definitions of the cardinal characteristics we will need.

Definition 1.5
.

(1)

Given
f,g\in\omega^{\omega}
we write
f\leq^{*}g
if and only if for all but finitely many
k<\omega
we have
f(k)\leq g(k)
. In this case we say
g

eventually dominates

f
. The
bounding number
, denoted
\mathfrak{b}
is the least size of an unbounded set
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\omega^{\omega}
, i.e. a set so that no single
g\in\omega^{\omega}
eventually dominates every
f\in\mathcal{B}
.

(2)

If
\mathcal{F}
is a family of infinite subsets of natural numbers we say that
\mathcal{F}
has the
strong finite intersection property
if for every finite subset
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
we have that
\bigcap\mathcal{A}
is infinite. If
A\subseteq\omega
is infinite then we say that
A
is a
pseudointersection
of such a family
\mathcal{F}
if
A\subseteq^{*}B
for all
B\in\mathcal{F}
- i.e. for each
B
there is a
k<\omega
with
A\setminus k\subseteq B
. In this case we also say
A
is
almost contained in

B
. The
pseudointersection number

\mathfrak{p}
is the least size of a family with the strong finite intersection property but no pseudointersection.

(3)

A
tower

\mathcal{T}\subseteq[\omega]^{\omega}
is a set that is reverse well ordered by
\subseteq^{*}
. The
tower number
, denoted
\mathfrak{t}
is the least size of a tower with no pseudointersection.

Recall that
\mathfrak{p}\leq\mathfrak{b}
provable in
\mathsf{ZFC}
, see e.g.
[
7
]
. We also need the following celebrated result of Shelah and Malliaris from
[
11
]
.

Fact 1.6
.

It is a
\mathsf{ZFC}
fact that
\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{t}
.

Finally most of the work in proving Main Theorem
1.1
involves translating between the combinatorics of
2^{\omega}
and the topology of
\mathbb{R}
. As part of this we will often associate elements of
P(\omega)
with their characteristic functions i.e. treat them as elements of
2^{\omega}
. In this vein we often abuse notation by saying things like “for
Y\in 2^{\omega}
if
n\in Y
…” which of course really means
Y(n)=1
. Denote by
[x(n)=1]
or
[x(n)=0]
the basic open subsets of
2^{\omega}
consisting of all
Y
so that
Y(n)=1
(or
Y(n)=0
respectively). The key tool for translating combinatorics from
2^{\omega}
to topology on
\mathbb{R}
is the Cantor-Lebesgue map.

Definition 1.7
.

The
Cantor-Lebesgue
map,
\lambda:2^{\omega}\to[0,1]
, is defined by
\lambda(x)=\Sigma_{i\in\omega}\frac{x(n)}{2^{n+1}}
.

This is a well studied object, see e.g.
[
17
]
for a thematically similar application. The following facts are well known and easily verified.

Fact 1.8
.

(1)

\lambda
is continuous (everywhere), onto and one-to-one when restricted to the set of non eventually constant points.

(2)

The set of
d\in[0,1]
so that
|\lambda^{-1}(\{d\})|>1
is simply the set of diadic rationals and for each such
|\lambda^{-1}(\{d\})|=2
. In particular it is two-to-one on a countable dense set and one-to-one outside of that.

(3)

For each
n\in\omega
we have
\lambda``[x(n)=1]=\bigcup_{i\in 2^{n}}[\frac{2i+1}{2^{n+1}},\frac{2i+2}{2^{n+1}}]
.

Let us just remark in English what the final item above says as this is key in the proof of Theorem
1.1
. The image of the set
[x(n)=1]\subseteq 2^{\omega}
under
\lambda
is defined as follows: first divide
[0,1]
into
2^{n+1}
equal pieces (the endpoints will be diadic rationals), then take the union of the even pieces. For instance, the image of
[x(1)=1]
is the set
[\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2}]\cup[\frac{3}{4},1]
i.e. we divide the interval into quarters and take the second and fourth ones.

2.
Adding Pseudointersections with Reasonable Forcing

In this section we prove Main Theorem
1.1
. Modulo some details the main point is the following which we will show the following.

Theorem 2.1
.

For every tower
\mathcal{T}
of size
\aleph_{1}
there are
\aleph_{1}
-dense sets of reals
A_{\mathcal{T}}
and
B_{\mathcal{T}}
so that if
\mathbb{P}
is reasonable for
A_{\mathcal{T}}
and
B_{\mathcal{T}}
then in any generic extension by
\mathbb{P}
there is a pseudointersection to
\mathcal{T}
.

The definition of reasonable is given below. Main Theorem
1.1
follows from this as, given an iteration of reasonable forcing notions which force every pair of
\aleph_{1}
-dense sets to be isomorphic we would cofinally often have to deal with sets of the form
A_{\mathcal{T}}
and
B_{\mathcal{T}}
as in the theorem above and therefore every tower of size
\aleph_{1}
in the extension would have a pseudointersection. Therefore
\mathfrak{t}
and hence
\mathfrak{p}
would necessarily need to be larger than
\aleph_{1}
. Let us now define reasonableness.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
A
and
B
be
\aleph_{1}
-dense sets of reals. A partial order
\mathbb{P}
is called
reasonable
for
A
and
B
if it satisfies the following three conditions.

(1)

(Finite Isomorphism) Every condition
p\in\mathbb{P}
is a finite partial isomorphism from
A
to
B
.

(2)

(Restriction) If
p\in\mathbb{P}
and
q=p\upharpoonright Z
for some finite
Z\subseteq{\rm dom}(p)
then
q\in\mathbb{P}
.

(3)

(Dense Mapping Property) Let
p\in\mathbb{P}
and
x\in A\setminus{\rm dom}(p)
. Suppose
x_{0}<x<x_{1}
so that
x_{0},x_{1}\in{\rm dom}(p)
with
x_{0}
the largest such and
x_{1}
the smallest such. If
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is an open interval contained in
(p(x_{0}),p(x_{1}))
then there is a
q\leq p
so that
x\in{\rm dom}(q)
and
q(x)\in U
.

The dense mapping property is the most important. It states roughly that, within the restrictions given by
p
, for any
x\notin{\rm dom}(p)
there is a dense set of elements of
B
into which we can choose to map
x
by an extension of
p
. By the restriction condition we can take this strengthening to in fact be simply
p
plus a single additional point - namely
\langle x,q(x)\rangle
, a fact we will use implicitly in the proof of Lemma
2.5
below. Note that it is easy to check that if
\mathbb{P}
is reasonable for
A
and
B
then it will generically add a linear order embedding from
A
to
B
since, by the dense mapping property, it is dense to add any particular
a\in A
to the domain. Thus if
G\subseteq\mathbb{P}
is generic then
\bigcup G
will in fact be an embedding from
A
to
B
. The notion of reasonable includes of course very non-proper cases, such as the collection of
all finite partial isomorphisms from

A
to
B
, but also the forcing notions used to produce
\mathsf{BA}
in
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
1
]
and
[
15
, Chapter 15]
. We do not in fact know of a way of producing isomorphisms generically between a given
A
and
B
which are not reasonable (modulo some kind of preparation such as collapsing to force
\mathsf{CH}
such as in
[
6
]
).

Remark 1
.

In this vein we also mention (for the reader in the know) the forcing of
[
15

used to produce an embedding of
B
into
X
for

of size
\aleph_{1}
and

which is
\aleph_{1}
-dense. This forcing notion consists of pairs

where

is a finite partial embedding and

is a finite continuous

Next we prove the following lemma which allows us to add pseudointersections. Fix a tower of size
\aleph_{1}
for the rest of this section, say
\mathcal{T}=\{X_{\alpha}\;|\;\alpha<\omega_{1}\}
where
\alpha<\beta
implies
X_{\beta}\subseteq^{*}X_{\alpha}
. Moreover without loss we assume
X=X_{0}
is infinite, co-infinite. Finally, again without loss we assume that
\mathcal{T}
is
\aleph_{1}
-dense.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
B\subseteq 2^{\omega}
be
\aleph_{1}
-dense consisting of elements which are infinite and almost disjoint from
X
. Let
A=\{\omega\setminus X_{\alpha}\mid\alpha<\omega_{1}\}
. Note that
A
is also
\aleph_{1}
-dense. If
f:A\to B
is a function so that there are infinitely many

for which
f``[x(n)=1]\subseteq\bigcup_{m\in X\setminus n}[x(m)=1]
then
\mathcal{T}
has a pseudointersection.

We remark that no assumption on
f
is made besides it being a function. In particular we do not assume it’s continuous nor need it be defined on all of

. Also, note that the expression

f``[x(n)=1]\subseteq\bigcup_{m\in X\setminus n}[x(m)=1]

means that if
n\in Y
then there is an
m\geq n
so that
m\in X\cap f(Y)
.

Proof.

Let
X_{\infty}=\{n\in X\;|\;f``[n=1]\subseteq\bigcup_{m\in X\setminus n}[m=1]\}
. By assumption this is an infinite set. We want to show that it is in fact a pseudointersection to
\mathcal{T}
. Suppose not and fix

so that
X_{\infty}\nsubseteq^{*}X_{\alpha}
. Let
Y\in B
be such that
f(\omega\setminus X_{\alpha})=Y
. Since

is almost disjoint from

there is a

so that
Y\cap X\subseteq k
. Let
n\in X_{\infty}\setminus X_{\alpha}
be larger than

. Since

we have that
n\in\omega\setminus X_{\alpha}
and hence there is an
m\geq n
in

so that
m\in Y=f(\omega\setminus X_{\alpha})
. But this contradicts the choice of
k
and

.

∎

Proposition 2.4

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)

be an open interval of length

\frac{3}{2^{n+1}}<\epsilon

n
.

Proof.

Fix

,
\epsilon
and

some convex subset of these will cover

. Thus let us pick

so that

. But this means that there is a

so that
[\frac{k}{2^{n+1}},\frac{k+1}{2^{n+1}}]

are both contained in

and one of these is good at

(depending on if

is odd or even).
∎

Suppose

-dense sets containing no diadic rationals so that the following holds:

(1)

For every finitely many

\dot{g}_{A,B}``(\lambda``[x(n)=1])\subseteq\bigcup_{m\in X\setminus n}\lambda``[x(m)=1]

4.
Conclusion

It would be nice to be able to apply the proof strategy from Theorem
2.1
to obtain that
\mathsf{BA}
implies
\mathfrak{p}>\aleph_{1}
. A natural attempt would be to replace the finiteness of the conditions with the fact that every

-dense set under
\mathsf{BA}
(indeed under

) can be covered by countably many closed (even compact) nowhere dense sets and attempt to do some kind of back and forth argument. Part of this is easy as the following lemma shows.

Lemma 4.1
.

Assume
\mathsf{BA}
. Let
A
and
B
be
\aleph_{1}
-dense sets of reals. Let
F\subseteq\mathbb{R}\setminus A
and
G\subseteq\mathbb{R}\setminus B
be closed, nowhere dense sets and let
\varphi:F\to G
be an order isomorphism between them. There is an isomorphism
\Phi:A\cup F\to B\cup G
extending
\varphi
.

Proof.

Fix
F
,
G
,
A
,
B
and
\varphi
as in the statement of the lemma. We call an open interval
(a,b)\subseteq\mathbb{R}\setminus F

maximal
if it is not contained in any properly larger open interval disjoint from
F
. Let us denote by
M(F)
the set of maximal open intervals disjoint from
F
and similarly for
M(G)
etc. The following can be checked easily.

Fact 4.2
.

The following all hold for
F
(and also, for
G
).

(1)

Every
a\in\mathbb{R}\setminus F
is contained in a single

.

(2)

Every

has the form

for some
a<b\in F
.

(3)

If

are distinct then they are disjoint.

The third item immediately implies that if

then we can order them
I<J
if and only if every

is less than every
y\in J
and this is a total order on
M(F)
(or
M(G)
respectively). We will always treat

as a linear order with this ordering moving forward. The point is the following.

Claim 4.3
.

\varphi:F\to G
induces an order isomorphism
\hat{\varphi}:M(F)\cong M(G)
.

Proof of Claim.

If
I\in M(F)
then by item (2) above

for some

so we let
\hat{\varphi}(I)=(\varphi(a),\varphi(b))
. First let us see that this is well defined. If
I\in M(F)
is as above the
\varphi(a)<\varphi(b)\in J

\varphi
is an order isomorphism and

\hat{\varphi}(I)\in M(G)
. Injectivity is immediate so let’s check surjectivity. If

then

. Thus

is a well defined bijection between

and

. Finally, if now

. Consequently

is order preserving.
∎

Now, for each

by

Therefore one can fix a predetermined isomorphism from some closed nowhere dense
