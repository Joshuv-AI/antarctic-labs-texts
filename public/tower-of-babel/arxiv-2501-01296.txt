We say that

Since

Assume that

Following

]

The

Given a positive integer

In
[
17
, p. 185]
, Shimorin asked whether it is true that the
m
-concave and
norm-increasing operators have Wold-type decomposition for
m\geqslant 3.

Here we explain the thought process that could help in answering this question.
In this regard, note that the construction in
[
4
, Example 3.1]
was capitalized on the idea of the weighted
shift on a one-circuit directed graph (see
[
7
, Section 3]
).
The one-dimensional space on which the weighted shift in question fails to be norm-increasing corresponds to the indicator function of an element of the circuit. Thus, to get a counterexample to Shimorin’s question from weighted shifts on directed graphs, it is necessary to confine ourselves to directed graphs without circuits.
This thinking can indeed be strongly based on
[
4
, Theorem 4.3]
, which provide a necessary condition for the failure of the wandering subspace property for analytic norm-increasing
m
-concave operators. Indeed, we have a more general fact:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be an analytic, norm-increasing
m
-concave operator and let
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{H}\ominus[\ker T^{*}]_{T}.

If
T
does not have the wandering subspace property, then for any
\lambda\in\sigma_{ap}(T^{\prime}|_{\mathcal{M}}),
there exists a Weyl sequence in
\mathcal{M}
for both
(\lambda,T)
and
(\lambda,T^{\prime}).
In this case,
\mathcal{M}
is infinite-dimensional.

Theorem
1.1
is not directly related to the objective of this paper, and hence we include its proof in the appendix. In view of Theorem
1.1
, a counterexample to Shimorin’s question can not be built on any one-circuit directed graph (see
[
4
, Corollary 4.4]
). This, combined with the fact that any bounded weighted shift on a rooted directed tree admits Wold-type decomposition (see
[
8
, Proposition 1.3.4]
),
propels us to explore rootless directed graphs without a circuit or more specifically, rootless directed trees. The natural question arises as to which choice of the rootless directed tree would lead to the desired counterexample.
It turns out that a rootless directed tree of finite branching index (see
[
9
, Definition 3]
) does not support analytic weighted shifts. Indeed, by
[
9
, Lemma 6.1]
, the indicator function of the so-called generalized root belongs to the hyper-range of the weighted shift in question. This motivates us to examine rootless directed trees of infinite branching index. A basic example of such directed trees, with which we are primarily concerned, is that of the rootless
quasi-Brownian directed tree of finite valency (see
[
12
, Example 4.4]
). Although we do not have definite answer to Shimorin’s question, the main result of this paper (see Theorem
1.3
) answers the following closely related question

\displaystyle\lambda_{v_{m+1}}g_{m+1,\mathfrak{v}},\quad m\in\mathbb{Z}.

(3.11)

To prove Theorem
3.1
, we need a variant of
[

, Theorem 2.1.1]
and
[
4
, Lemma 2.3]
(see
[

, Remark 45]
). First, let

be as in (

), and
recall from

, Lemma 6.1.1]
that
for any positive integer

\displaystyle S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*m}S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{m}e_{v}=\|S^{n}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}e_{v}\|^{2}e_{v},\quad v\in V,

(3.12)

\displaystyle S^{n}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}e_{u}=\displaystyle\sum_{v\in{\mathsf{Chi}}^{\langle n\rangle}(u)}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(n)}(v)e_{v},\quad u\in V,

(3.13)

\displaystyle\|S^{n}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}e_{u}\|^{2}=\displaystyle\sum_{v\in{\mathsf{Chi}}^{\langle n\rangle}(u)}\big(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(n)}(v)\big)^{2},\quad u\in V.

(3.14)

We now give a simple description of the hyper-range of a left-invertible weighted shift on a rootless directed tree (cf.
[

,
[

[

).

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
be a left-invertible weighted shift on a rootless directed tree
\mathcal{T}=(V,\mathcal{E})
. Then, for any integer
n\geqslant 1,

\displaystyle S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{n}(\ell^{2}(V))=\left\{f\in\ell^{2}(V):\frac{f}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(n)}}\Big|_{{\mathsf{Chi}}^{\langle n\rangle}(v)}\mbox{ is constant for all }v\in V\right\},

(3.15)

where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(n)}
is given by (
1.3
).
Moreover,

\displaystyle S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\infty}(\ell^{2}(V))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigg\{f\in\ell^{2}(V):\frac{f}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(n)}}\Big|_{{\mathsf{Chi}}^{\langle n\rangle}(v)}\mbox{ is constant for each }v\in V

(3.16)

\displaystyle\mbox{ and for every integer }n\geqslant 1\bigg\}.

Proof.

Let
n
be a positive integer. Since
\mathsf{par}(\cdot)
is a function, the inclusion
\subseteq
in (
3.15
) can be obtained by imitating the proof of
[
4
, Lemma 2.3]
. For the remaining inclusion,
let
f\in\ell^{2}(V)
be such that
\frac{f}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(n)}}\Big|_{{\mathsf{Chi}}^{\langle n\rangle}(v)}
is constant for each
v\in V
. Define
g:V\to\mathbb{C}
by

\displaystyle g(v)=\frac{f(u)}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(n)}(u)},\quad v\in V~\mbox{and}~u\in{\mathsf{Chi}}^{\langle n\rangle}(v).

Since
V=\bigsqcup_{v\in V}{\mathsf{Chi}}^{\langle n\rangle}(v)
,
g
is well-defined and

\displaystyle\|f\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{u\in{\mathsf{Chi}}^{\langle n\rangle}(v)}|f(u)|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}|g(v)|^{2}\sum_{u\in{\mathsf{Chi}}^{\langle n\rangle}(v)}\big(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(n)}(u)\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{known-rmk-new-new}}{=}

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}|g(v)|^{2}\|S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{n}e_{v}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\Big(\inf_{v\in V}\|S^{n}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}e_{v}\|\Big)^{2}\sum_{v\in V}|g(v)|^{2}.

This combined with the left-invertibility of
S^{n}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
(since so is
S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
) implies that

and
S^{n}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(g)=f.
Finally, (
3.16
) follows from (
3.15
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

By Lemma

).

Proof of Theorem
1.3

The next step in the proof of Theorem
1.3
provides a Wold-type decomposition theorem for left-invertible, non-analytic, balanced weighted shifts on a rootless directed tree (cf.
[
18
, Theorem 1.1]
).

Theorem 4.1

(Wold-type decomposition)
.

Let
S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
be a left-invertible, non-analytic weighted shift on a rootless directed tree
\mathcal{T}=(V,\mathcal{E})
. Then
S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\infty}(\ell^{2}(V))
reduces
S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
if and only if
S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is balanced. Moreover, if
S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is balanced, then

\ell^{2}(V)=S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\infty}(\ell^{2}(V))\oplus\ker S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}\oplus S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\ker S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*})\oplus\cdots.

We begin with a computational lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

Assume that for some
m\in\mathbb{Z},

h_{m,n}\in\ell^{2}(V),
where

\displaystyle h_{m,n}:=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{u\in\mathsf{Chi}(v_{m-1})}\boldsymbol{\lambda}(u)e_{u}&~\mbox{if~}n=1,\\
\displaystyle\sum_{u\in A(v_{m},n)}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(n)}(u)e_{u}&~\mbox{if~}n\geqslant 2,\end{cases}

Then, for every positive integer
n,

\displaystyle S^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(h_{m,n})=\displaystyle\sum_{w\in A(v_{m-1},n-1)}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(n-1)}(w)\,\|S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}e_{w}\|^{2}\,e_{w}.

Proof.

If
n=1,
then

\displaystyle S^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(h_{m,n})=\sum_{u\in\mathsf{Chi}(v_{m-1})}\lambda^{2}_{u}e_{\mathsf{par}(u)}=\|S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}e_{v_{m-1}}\|^{2}e_{v_{m-1}}.

Assume that
n\geqslant 2.
By Lemma
2.1
(iv),

\displaystyle S^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(h_{m,n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in A(v_{m},n)}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(n)}(u)\,\lambda_{u}\,e_{\mathsf{par}(u)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in A(v_{m},n)}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(n-1)}(\mathsf{par}(u))\,\lambda^{2}_{u}\,e_{\mathsf{par}(u)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{w\in A(v_{m-1},n-1)}\sum_{u\in\mathsf{Chi}(w)}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(n-1)}(w)\,\lambda_{u}^{2}\,e_{w}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{known-rmk-new-new}}{=}

\displaystyle\sum_{w\in A(v_{m-1},n-1)}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(n-1)}(w)\,\|S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}e_{w}\|^{2}\,e_{w}.

This completes the proof.
∎

We also need the following elementary fact.

Lemma 4.3
.

If
\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}
are closed subspaces of a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
such that
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{L},
then

\mathcal{H}\ominus\mathcal{K}=\big(\mathcal{H}\ominus\mathcal{L}\big)\oplus\big(\mathcal{L}\ominus\mathcal{K}\big).

Proof.

Let
h\in\mathcal{H}\ominus\mathcal{K}.
Decompose
h=x+y,
where
x\in\mathcal{H}\ominus\mathcal{L}
and
y\in\mathcal{L}.
Since
h
is orthogonal to
\mathcal{K},
for any
k\in\mathcal{K},

\langle{x+y},\,{k}\rangle=0.
However, since
x
is orthogonal to
\mathcal{L},

\langle{y},\,{k}\rangle=0
for every
k\in\mathcal{K}.
This shows that
y\in\mathcal{L}\ominus\mathcal{K},
and hence

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\ominus\mathcal{K}\subseteq\big(\mathcal{H}\ominus\mathcal{L}\big)\oplus\big(\mathcal{L}\ominus\mathcal{K}\big).

Since
\mathcal{H}\ominus\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{H}\ominus\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{L}\ominus\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{H}\ominus\mathcal{K},
we obtain the other inclusion.
∎

Here is the third lemma needed in the proof of Theorem
4.1
(cf.
[
5
, Theorem 6.4]
).

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
S_{\lambda}
be a left-invertible balanced weighted shift on a rootless directed tree
\mathcal{T}=(V,\mathcal{E})
. Then the following statements are true:

(i)

\|S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{n}e_{v}\|=\|S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{n}e_{u}\|

u
,

S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{n}(\ker S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*})

,

(iii)

,

(iv)

\oplus_{k=0}^{n-1}S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{k}(\ker S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*})=\ell^{2}(V)\ominus S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{n}(\ell^{2}(V)),

.

Let
c(m,v)
denote the constant value of
\|S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{m}e_{w}\|^{2},

(indeed, the proof of
[

[

(ii)
Let
f\in\ker S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}
. In view of
[

for some
v\in V.
For

\displaystyle\langle{S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{m}(f)},\,{S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{n}(g)}\rangle

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{known-rmk-new-new-new}}{=}

\displaystyle c(m,v)\langle{f},\,{S^{n-m}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(g)}\rangle=0.

This yields (ii).

(iiii) Letting

in (

\displaystyle S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{m}(\ker S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*})\subseteq S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{m}(\ell^{2}(V))\ominus S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{m+1}(\ell^{2}(V)).

. Then

that is,

. Consequently,
f\in S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{m}(\ker S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*})
. This together with (

) yields (iii).

(iv) We verify this by mathematical induction on

Clearly, (iv) is obvious for

Assume that (iv) holds for

Thus

\displaystyle\oplus_{k=0}^{n}S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{k}(\ker S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\ell^{2}(V)\ominus S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{n}(\ell^{2}(V))\Big)\oplus S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{n}(\ker S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*})

\displaystyle\overset{(\mathrm{iii})}{=}

\displaystyle\Big(\ell^{2}(V)\ominus S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{n}(\ell^{2}(V))\Big)\oplus\Big(S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{n}(\ell^{2}(V))\ominus S_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{n+1}(\ell^{2}(V))\Big)

where we used Lemma

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

For a bilateral path

Appendix: Invariant subspaces and Weyl sequences

Theorem
1.1
turns out to be a special case of the following general fact
:

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be an analytic norm-increasing operator with spectrum contained in the closed unit disc in
\mathbb{C}.

If
\mathcal{M}
is a nonzero invariant subspace of
T^{\prime},
then for any
\lambda\in\sigma_{ap}(T^{\prime}|_{\mathcal{M}}),
there exists a Weyl sequence in
\mathcal{M}
for both
(\lambda,T)
and
(\lambda,T^{\prime}).

Recall the fact that the fixed points of a contraction and its adjoint are the same (see
[
18
, Proposition 3.1]
). Here is an “approximate” analog of this result.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be a contraction. Assume that there exists a sequence
\{h_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
of unit vectors in
\mathcal{H}
such that
Sh_{n}-h_{n}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty.
Then
S^{*}h_{n}-h_{n}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty.

Proof.

Since
S^{*}
is a contraction and
\|h_{n}\|=1,

\displaystyle\|S^{*}h_{n}-h_{n}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|S^{*}h_{n}\|^{2}+\|h_{n}\|^{2}-2\Re(\langle{S^{*}h_{n}},\,{h_{n}}\rangle)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 2(1-\Re(\langle{h_{n}},\,{Sh_{n}}\rangle)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\Re(\langle{h_{n}},\,{Sh_{n}-h_{n}}\rangle.

However,
|\langle{h_{n}},\,{Sh_{n}-h_{n}}\rangle|\leq\|Sh_{n}-h_{n}\|\rightarrow 0,
and hence

\|S^{*}h_{n}-h_{n}\|\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty.

This completes the proof.
∎

All nontrivial approximate eigenvalues yield Weyl sequences.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\sigma_{p}(S)
be nonzero. Assume that there exists a sequence
\{h_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
of unit vectors in
\mathcal{H}
such that

(S-\lambda)h_{n}\rightarrow 0
,

(5.41)

Then there exists a weakly null subsequence
\{h_{n_{k}}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
of
\{h_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}.

Proof.

Since
\{h_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
is a bounded sequence in a Hilbert space, by Banach’s theorem, there exists a subsequence
\{h_{n_{k}}\}_{k\geqslant 1}
of
\{h_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
weakly convergent to, say,
g_{0}\in\mathcal{H}.
By (
5.41
) or otherwise,
\{Sh_{n_{k}}\}_{k\geqslant 1}
converges weakly to,
Sg_{0}.
By the uniqueness of the weak limit and (
5.41
),
Sg_{0}=\lambda g_{0}.
Since
\lambda\notin\sigma_{p}(S),

g_{0}=0.
This shows that
\{h_{n_{k}}\}_{k\geqslant 1}
is weakly null.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.3
.

Since
T^{\prime}
is a left-invertible, so is
T^{\prime}|_{\mathcal{M}}.
Let
\lambda\in\sigma_{ap}(T^{\prime}|_{\mathcal{M}}).
Then
\lambda\neq 0
and
there exists a sequence
\{h_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
of unit vectors in
\mathcal{M}
such that

T^{\prime}h_{n}-\lambda h_{n}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty.

(5.42)

However,
T^{\prime}
is a contraction (since
T
is an expansion), and hence
|\lambda|\leqslant 1.
As
T^{*}T^{\prime}=I
, we must have
\lambda T^{*}h_{n}-h_{n}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty.

Since the spectrum of
T
is contained in the closed unit disc,
|\lambda|=1
.
In view of (
5.42
), applying Lemma
5.4
to the contractive operator
\overline{\lambda}T^{\prime}
shows that
T^{\prime*}h_{n}-\overline{\lambda}h_{n}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty.
It follows that

T^{\prime*}T^{\prime}h_{n}-h_{n}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty.

Equivalently,

(T^{\prime*}T^{\prime})^{-1}h_{n}-h_{n}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty.
Since
T=(T^{\prime})^{\prime},
it now follows that

\displaystyle(T-\lambda)h_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(T^{\prime}(T^{\prime*}T^{\prime})^{-1}-\lambda)h_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T^{\prime}((T^{\prime*}T^{\prime})^{-1}-I)h_{n}+(T^{\prime}-\lambda)h_{n},

which, by (
5.42
), converges to
0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Thus
\lambda\in\sigma_{ap}(T)\setminus\{0\}.

Since an analytic operator does not have a nonzero eigenvalue, by Lemma
5.5
, there exists a subsequence
\{h_{n_{k}}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
of
\{h_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1},
which is a Weyl sequence for
(\lambda,T).
It is clear from (
5.42
) that
\{h_{n_{k}}\}_{k\geqslant 1}
is also a Weyl sequence for
(\lambda,T^{\prime}).
This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Since the spectrum of a norm-increasing
m
-concave operator is contained in the closed unit disc, one may apply Theorem
