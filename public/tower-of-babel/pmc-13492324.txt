Introduction

Congenital factor XII (FXII) deficiency is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by marked prolongation of activated partial thromboplastin time and activated clotting time (ACT) despite normal in vivo hemostasis. FXII deficiency does not cause increased bleeding diathesis, unlike other factor deficiencies,1 but generates dramatically abnormal contact-pathway–based coagulation assays.

Because standard atrial fibrillation (AF) ablation relies on heparin titrated to ACT, FXII deficiency creates a situation in which ACT is noninterpretable and heparin-based anticoagulation becomes unsafe. Although direct thrombin inhibitors can serve as alternatives, no published cases describe AF ablation using bivalirudin in FXII deficiency or with the Affera Sphere-9 (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) pulsed-field ablation (PFA) system. We report what we believe is the first such case.

Case report

Patient presentation

A woman aged 44 years with hypertension, obesity, gastroesophageal reflux disease, Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, and symptomatic paroxysmal AF presented with episodes that occurred every 3–4 weeks lasting several hours. Antiarrhythmic therapy was limited owing to flecainide intolerance and breakthrough episodes on propafenone 325 mg twice daily. The echocardiogram showed normal left ventricular systolic function with ejection fraction of 60% and grade I diastolic dysfunction. Because of frequent breakthrough on her second antiarrhythmic drug therapy, the patient elected to proceed with catheter ablation for AF. Given a congestive heart failure, hypertension, age ≥75 (doubled), diabetes, stroke (doubled), vascular disease, age 65 to 74, and sex category score of 2, she was not on long-term anticoagulation. Apixaban 5 mg twice daily was initiated 3 weeks before the procedure, with the patient holding only the morning dose on the day of the procedure.

Initial procedure—aborted owing to uninterpretable ACT

On July 2025, before any heparin administration, baseline ACT values were >800 seconds. This was confirmed for 5 measurements using different cartridges and readers. Given concern for an undiagnosed bleeding disorder, and because ACT could not be interpreted and safe anticoagulation could not be achieved, the procedure was aborted. The hematology department subsequently diagnosed FXII deficiency.

Follow-up visit

The patient was examined in clinic in September 2025 and continued to report paroxysms of atrial fibrillation. Hematology evaluation was completed, and baseline hematologic, chemistry, and coagulation values were within normal limits. The hematology department did not make any formal recommendations regarding specific management of antithrombic therapy for the procedure.

Ablation procedure

The ablation procedure occurred in November 2025. Because the patient had FXII deficiency, intraprocedural anticoagulation was achieved using bivalirudin, administered as a 0.75 mg/kg bolus immediately after transseptal puncture, followed by a continuous infusion of 1.75 mg/kg/h, without ACT-based titration. Bivalirudin was chosen owing to its predictable kinetics and the unreliability of ACT in FXII deficiency; importantly, it has a plasma half-life of approximately 25 minutes, with near-complete washout within approximately 1 hour after discontinuation in patients with normal renal function.

Mapping and ablation were performed using the Affera Prism electroanatomical mapping system and the bidirectional Sphere-9 lattice-tip catheter (Medtronic). Because bivalirudin cannot be reversed, radiofrequency energy was intentionally avoided to minimize the risk of perforation due to steam pops, and PFA was used exclusively for all lesion sets.

Wide antral circumferential PFA was applied for pulmonary vein isolation along with carina lines. PFA was then used to create both roof and floor lines across the posterior wall, followed by additional PFA debulking to achieve complete posterior wall isolation. Given the patient’s history of typical atrial flutter, cavotricuspid isthmus (CTI) was ablated using PFA alone, causing bidirectional block with a transisthmus conduction time of 160 ms. Nitroglycerin 2 mg was administered before PFA application on the CTI to minimize risk of coronary vasospasm. Finally, superior vena cava isolation was performed using PFA delivered above at the demarcation line at the level of the inferior border of the right pulmonary artery, to protect the sinus node. Remapping indicated entrance and exit block in all pulmonary veins, and confirmed posterior wall isolation, bidirectional CTI block, and complete superior vena cava isolation. Intracardiac echocardiography revealed no pericardial effusion or thrombus at the conclusion of the case, and hemostasis was achieved using a Perclose device (Abbott, Chiago, IL). The patient was discharged the same day without complications and remained clinically stable on follow-up. Apixaban 5 mg twice daily was continued for 3 months after the ablation.

Discussion

FXII deficiency creates profound challenges for catheter ablation because the ACT values become extremely prolonged and unreliably elevated, despite normal physiologic clotting.1 As a result, alternate procedural anticoagulant strategies are needed that do not require intraprocedural ACT monitoring.

Before the repeat ablation, extensive multidisciplinary planning was undertaken. A discussion was held with the hospital’s pathology department regarding anticoagulation monitoring options. Because ACT values were uninterpretable, one proposed strategy was to use anti‒factor Xa levels to titrate IV heparin. However, anti‒factor Xa was not available as a point-of-care assay, and turnaround time was approximately 1 hour. Given the time requirement, this was not practical to use as a point-of- care test during catheter ablation. Thus, heparin—even if given—could not be safely or responsively monitored.

A strategy that did not require ACT monitoring was needed. Bivalirudin, a direct thrombin inhibitor with predictable dose-response characteristics and FXII-independent mechanism, was the safest option. Although argatroban was also an alternative, given normal renal function in the patient and familiarity of bivalirudin dosing in the cardiac catheterization laboratory, we elected to use bivalirudin over argatroban. Bivalirudin is currently Food and Drug Administration approved for use as an anticoagulant for patients who undergo percutaneous coronary intervention and for the management of patients with heparin-induced thrombocytopenia.2 Bivalirudin offers multiple advantages, with predictable pharmacokinetics with a half-life of approximately 25 minutes, with total body clearance in patients with normal renal function of 3.4 mL/min/kg, with return to baseline coagulation levels in approximately 1 to 2 hours after cessation of infusion.2 Although checking ACT 5 minutes after the initial infusion is recommended, with an additional 0.3 mg/kg bolus to be administered for ACT <225 ms, we elected not to do this because in our case ACT values were uninterpretable, the patient was already on baseline anticoagulation, and an additional bolus dosing may be associated with pericardial effusion, although data are lacking.3,4 Given serial ACT monitoring was not needed, we could safely use this drug in our patient with FXII deficiency.

Because bivalirudin cannot be reversed, a few procedural considerations should be noted. First, we elected to exclusively use PFA as our ablative strategy and avoid radiofrequency, specifically to avoid the risk of steam pops and subsequent perforation. Intracardiac echocardiography guidance was crucial to monitor for any complications.

To the best of our knowledge, this case is the first to describe the anticoagulant management strategy in a patient who underwent catheter ablation with FXII deficiency, in which ACT monitoring is not feasible. This report therefore fills a gap in the literature regarding management of FXII deficiency during left-sided catheter ablations.

Conclusion

In patients with FXII deficiency, ACT cannot be used for anticoagulation monitoring. This case shows that bivalirudin, combined with PFA, provides a safe and feasible approach for left-sided catheter ablation. To our knowledge, this is the first published case using the Medtronic Affera Sphere-9 system with bivalirudin anticoagulation in FXII deficiency.
