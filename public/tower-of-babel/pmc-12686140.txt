Long-term survival in a patient with leptomeningeal metastases from non-small cell lung cancer treated with stereotactic radiotherapy (SRT), programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) inhibitor, and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF): a case report
Authors: Jiale Huan, Chanchan Shan, Huifeng Gu, Yun Li, Weixiang Qi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeptomeningeal metastasis (LM) in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is a highly aggressive malignancy associated with poor prognosis and limited therapeutic efficacy. Currently, effective treatment options remain scarce. Although immunotherapy has revolutionised the treatment of NSCLC, its role in LM remains underexplored, and its efficacy as monotherapy is suboptimal. Preclinical and clinical studies suggest that combining programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) inhibitors with radiotherapy or granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) may enhance antitumor immunity. The combination therapy of stereotactic radiotherapy (SRT), PD-1 inhibitor and GM-CSF is an emerging treatment strategy currently under investigation in various advanced solid tumors, with a growing number of evidence demonstrating promising efficacy and tolerability. This case report offers novel insights and a potential therapeutic strategy for patients with NSCLC and LMs, highlighting the possibility of achieving remarkable and durable response through this combined approach.Case DescriptionWe present the case of a 56-year-old male diagnosed with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)/anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK)-negative NSCLC with concurrent brain parenchymal and LMs. After disease progression on first-line chemotherapy, he received second-line therapy consisting of SRT targeting dominant brain metastases, combined with a PD-1 inhibitor and GM-CSF. Remarkably, all lesions including the LMs, achieved complete resolution, with a progression-free survival (PFS) of 69 months to date.ConclusionsPatients with NSCLC and LMs historically face a devastating prognosis, with median survival often limited to weeks. In this case, however, the patient achieved a remarkable PFS of over 4 years following a multimodal regimen combining SRT, PD-1 inhibition, and GM-CSF. This tri-modality strategy emerges as a promising therapeutic paradigm for NSCLC-associated LM.

Highlight box

Key findings

• A patient with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and leptomeningeal metastases achieved long-term survival following treatment with a novel tri-modality regimen combining stereotactic radiotherapy (SRT), a programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) inhibitor, and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF).

What is known and what is new?

• Leptomeningeal metastasis from NSCLC is associated with an extremely poor prognosis and limited treatment options, with median overall survival typically ranging from 3 to 6 months.

• This case presents unprecedented long-term survival (69 months) using a combination of SRT, PD-1 inhibition, and GM-CSF, suggesting potential synergy and a new therapeutic strategy.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• This case implies that the combined treatment strategy of radiotherapy, GM-CSF and immunotherapy may have a significant effect on NSCLC with meningeal metastasis. A prospective single-arm clinical trial is needed to evaluate the reproducibility of the therapeutic efficacy and safety of this regimen.

Introduction

Lung cancer represents the most prevalent malignancy and the principal cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Central nervous system (CNS) metastases remain a significant clinical challenge in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients, owing to their frequent occurrence and poor prognosis. At initial diagnosis, brain metastases are identified in over 25% of advanced NSCLC cases (1), while leptomeningeal metastasis (LM) develops in 3–5% of patients (2). Notably, one-third of LM cases present with concurrent brain metastases (3). Due to poor performance status (PS), up to one-third of patients with LM receive best supportive care alone (4). Currently, no standard treatment exists for this patient population. Studies and some case reports have shown that radiotherapy, systemic chemotherapy, intrathecal chemotherapy, and targeted therapy may provide clinical benefits to some patients; however, the survival advantages remain limited, and these treatments are frequently associated with severe toxicities, including chemical meningitis (5-9). The median overall survival (OS) for NSCLC patients with LM ranges from 1.4 to 11 months (10).

In recent years, immuno-oncology (I-O) treatments, particularly those targeting the programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and programmed cell death protein ligand 1 (PD-L1) pathway, have emerged as significant therapeutic advances. Immunotherapy has become an essential component of treatment for advanced driver negative NSCLC (11-14). Subgroup analyses from several studies have demonstrated that NSCLC patients with brain metastases may derive benefit from immunotherapy, both as monotherapy and in combination with chemotherapy (11,15,16). However, survival data for NSCLC patients with LMs treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) remain limited. Several small-scale clinical trials have demonstrated generally poor outcomes, with median OS typically below 4 months (17,18). Recent evidence demonstrates that stereotactic radiotherapy (SRT) can potentiate immune-mediated tumor destruction and improve disease control (19). This effect appears synergistic when combined with PD-1 inhibition (20,21). Furthermore, the combination of PD-1 inhibitors with either radiotherapy or granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) may induce abscopal effects, thereby enhancing systemic anti-tumor immunity (22). While the combination of SRT, PD-1 inhibitors, and GM-CSF represents an emerging multimodal strategy currently under investigation in advanced solid tumors, its application in NSCLC patients with LM remains scarcely studied. The potential therapeutic role of this approach—particularly in eliciting a sustained CNS response—has not been adequately demonstrated or mechanistically elucidated. This case report aims to offer valuable insights into the treatment strategy for this patient population, highlighting its potential as a novel therapeutic option. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-1088/rc).

Case presentation

In March 2019, a 56-year-old male, never smoker who denies alcohol consumption was diagnosed with stage IV [American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition] cT4N3M1c NSCLC. His mother deceased due to lung cancer. No other known malignancies in first-degree relatives. He had a PS of 2. He accompanied by brain metastasis and supraclavicular lymph node metastasis. The diagnosis was confirmed through a series of investigations, including chest computed tomography (CT), brain magnetic resonance (MR), positron emission tomography computed tomography (PET-CT), and biopsy of the left supraclavicular lymph node. Notably, the brain metastatic lesion presented as a solitary focus in the right frontal lobe. The tumor marker analysis revealed an elevated serum carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) level of 1,379.24 µg/L (reference range, 0–5.0 µg/L). Pathological examination confirmed a poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma, with immunohistochemical findings as follows: TTF-1+, Napsin A+, CK5/6−, CK7+, chromogranin A (CgA)−, and Ki-67 proliferation index of 60%. Next-generation sequencing (NGS; whole-genome sequencing, Illumina Hiseq) demonstrated: a high tumor mutation burden (TMB) of 43.14 mutation/Mb; no microsatellite instability (MSI); PD-L1 negative expression (assessed via Dako 22C3 assay); and no mutations were detected in key driver genes including epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), ROS1, RET, NTRK, MET exon 14, BRAF V600E, and HER2.

The patient received four cycles of first-line chemotherapy (pemetrexed 500 mg/m2 on day 1, cisplatin 25 mg/m2 on days 1–3) at an external institution. Bevacizumab was withheld due to concurrent lower limb thrombosis and hypertension. The patient refused to undergo radiotherapy for brain metastases. In June 2019, follow-up neck and chest CT demonstrated progression of the primary lung lesion (baseline long-axis diameter: 76 mm) and left supraclavicular lymph node metastasis. Concurrent brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a new metastatic focus in the right frontal lobe, along with associated leptomeningeal enhancement in the cerebellar region. Cerebrospinal fluid cytology subsequently confirmed of LM. The patient developed left-sided limb dysfunction and experienced recurrent seizure activity. Thus, due to the mass effect of the brain metastases, the patient’s PS declined to grade 4. The patient’s body mass index (BMI) was 24.2 kg/m2. Nutritional risk screening (NRS) using the NRS 2002 tool yielded a score of 2. Peripheral blood lymphocyte subset analysis revealed: an absolute CD4+ T-cell count of 370 cells/µL, representing 41.9% of total lymphocytes; CD8+ T-cells accounted for 25.7%; and the CD4+/CD8+ ratio was 1.63.

The patient’s family declined second-line chemotherapy. Consequently, hypofractionated radiotherapy (500 cGy × 6 fractions) was administered to the dominant right frontal lobe lesion. This was combined with immunotherapy comprising: PD-1 inhibitor (camrelizumab 200 mg every 3 weeks) and GM-CSF 200 µg (3 µg/kg) daily for 7 days per 3-week cycle.

In September 2019, follow-up imaging demonstrated complete response of the LMs with ongoing response in the brain metastases, pulmonary lesions and left supraclavicular lymph nodes. Serum CEA levels decreased to 51.98 µg/L. However, by March 2020, serum CEA levels had risen to 120.90 µg/L, and imaging revealed minor progression of pulmonary lesions [stable disease (SD)]. These findings raised suspicion of developing immunotherapy resistance. Consequently, while retaining the original regimen of camrelizumab and GM-CSF, two cycles of nab-paclitaxel (100 mg/m2 on days 1, 8, and 15 per 3-week cycle) were added to enhance antigen release. Following two chemotherapy cycles, serum CEA levels decreased significantly, and CT imaging revealed a marked reduction in the lung lesion. The patient subsequently continued maintenance therapy with camrelizumab in combination with GM-CSF until July 2021. During subsequent follow-up, the patient’s PS score improved to 0 without further epileptic seizures. Serum CEA levels remained within normal range. As of the March 2025 assessment, chest CT indicated resolution of the left supraclavicular lymph node metastasis, while the long-axis diameter of the lung lesion had decreased from 76 to 21 mm. Brain MRI demonstrated a complete response in the LMs, and the irradiated intracranial lesions exhibited further regression (Figure 2). Based on immunotherapy response evaluation criteria in solid tumors (iRECIST) criteria, the patient’s disease status remains a partial response (PR), with a progression-free survival (PFS) of 69 months since radiotherapy to date. Additionally, serum CEA had normalized (Figure 3). Regarding adverse effects, this patient developed only grade 1 reactive cutaneous capillary endothelial proliferation (RCCEP) on the face and back. Other adverse reactions included occasional Grade1 fatigue and ostealgia, which were considered related to GM-CSF. Grading was made in accordance with Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events Version (CTCAE) 5.0. No other treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) were observed, such as pneumonitis, pituitary dysfunction, thyroid dysfunction, myocardial injury, or the elevation of transaminases and creatinine. During the first-line treatment, the patient declined radiotherapy as well as second-line chemotherapy; however, following the initiation of the triple-combination therapy, he demonstrated excellent treatment adherence. We believe this improvement may be attributed to rapid symptomatic improvement, recovery of PS score, and a favorable safety profile, which collectively enabled the patient to gradually resume normal daily activities.

Discussion

Recent advancements in immunotherapy for advanced NSCLC have demonstrated significant clinical progress. Notably, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) reveal that combining radiotherapy synergistically enhances the efficacy of ICIs, suggesting a paradigm shift toward multimodal therapeutic strategies (17).

The prospective randomized controlled clinical trial by Welsh et al., demonstrated that combination radiotherapy with immunotherapy significantly prolongs PFS and increases the out-of-field objective response rate (ORR) in patients with NSCLC, with hypofractionated radiotherapy potentially offering greater benefits than conventional fractionation (23). Furthermore, several studies have investigated the addition of low-dose hypofractionated radiotherapy to high-dose hypofractionated radiotherapy and immunotherapy in advanced NSCLC, showing improved overall response rate and prolonged survival (24). In present case, both non-irradiated LMs and extracranial lesions exhibited sustained regression.

The triple-combination therapy is attracting increasing attention due to its therapeutic potential in advanced solid tumors. Evidence suggests that populations including those with advanced NSCLC may derive clinical benefit from this triple-combination therapy (25,26). Additionally, these studies indicated that the triple-combination therapy was well tolerated, with no grade 4–5 TRAEs observed. Grade 3 TRAEs and immune-related AEs occurred in 11.8% and 5.9% of patients, respectively, a rate comparable to that of anti-PD-1 monotherapy or PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors combined with SBRT (26-28). In the present case, the patient also demonstrated good tolerance to the triplet therapy.

Our case demonstrated comparable therapeutic efficacy to the referenced clinical trial. Notably, while the trial’s eligibility criteria restricted enrolment to patients with asymptomatic brain metastases, the patient case involved symptomatic brain metastases and LMs.

Clinical trials have demonstrated potential efficacy of immunotherapy in the CNS (29,30). However, evidence regarding immunotherapy for LMs remain limited. A phase II trial investigating pembrolizumab in LM (n=20, including one NSCLC patient) reported a median OS of 3.6 months (31). Similarly, a prospective NSCLC cohort (n=19 patients with LM) achieved median OS of 3.7 months from treatment initiation (18). These findings highlight the critical need to optimise combination strategies, such as triplet regimens, for this high-risk patient population.

Traditional perspectives posit that PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies, with molecular weights exceeding 140 kDa, demonstrate limited blood brain barrier (BBB) penetration and primarily exert their effects through peripheral immune cell activation (32). This conventional understanding has historically led to the exclusion of patients with brain or LMs from clinical trials. However, emerging evidence challenges this paradigm through several mechanistic Insights into CNS immune activity. Although the CNS lacks conventional lymphatic drainage, meningeal compartments facilitate immune surveillance via newly identified pathways. T cells infiltration of brain parenchyma may occur through multiple routes: disrupted BBB microvascular endothelium, the porous endothelium of the choroid plexus, or perivascular Virchow-Robin spaces in the subarachnoid region (33). These discoveries provide a compelling rationale for combining radiotherapy with ICIs to overcome CNS immunosuppression.

In the present case, the patient developed LMs following the first-line chemotherapy failure. The combination of SRT, camrelizumab (a PD-1 inhibitor), and GM-CSF yielded an exceptional intracranial and extracranial PFS of 69 months. This survival duration substantially surpasses the median OS of 3–6 months typically reported for NSCLC patients with LM. Notably, although SRT was solely administered to the right frontal lobe lesion, both irradiated and non-irradiated lesions (including both intracranial and extracranial sites) demonstrated sustained regression. The complete resolution of meningeal metastases coupled with significant shrinkage of bulky pulmonary lesions provides compelling evidence for the potential synergistic efficacy of this triple-modality therapeutic approach.

These clinical observations align with existing preclinical and clinical evidence regarding combined radiotherapy, immunotherapy and GM-CSF therapy. SRT facilitates tumor antigen exposure, antigen exposure and activates the cGAS-STING signalling pathway, thereby inducing a systemic anti-tumor immune response. Concurrently, GM-CSF potentiates the functional activation of antigen-presenting cells—particularly dendritic cells—while enhancing antigen presentation efficiency. These synergistic mechanisms likely account for the durable treatment responses observed in this case.

Regarding side effects, after four courses of immunotherapy, the patient developed RCCEP, which primarily was mainly manifested as red, mole-like lesions on the facial skin. This cutaneous adverse reaction gradually subsided by the fourth month of the triple-combination therapy. No other adverse effects—such as immune-related hypothyroidism, hepatic injury, or renal impairment—were observed in the patient.

However, this case report has certain limitations, such as potential biases in treatment selection and follow-up assessments. Therefore, a prospective single-arm clinical trial is needed to systematically evaluate the reproducibility of the therapeutic efficacy and safety of this regimen.

Conclusions

This case highlights the potential of combining SRT, PD-1 inhibitors, and GM-CSF to improve outcomes in NSCLC patients with LMs, a condition historically associated with a median OS of <6 months. The observed sustained regression—notably in non-irradiated extracranial lesions—supports the hypothesis that radiotherapy induces systemic immunomodulatory effects.
