First-in-human, phase 1, open-label study of alomfilimab, an anti-ICOS antibody, as a single agent and in combination with anti-PD-L1 in advanced malignancies
Authors: Aung Naing, Chia-Chi Lin, Manish R Patel, Giuseppe Curigliano, Howard A. Burris, Fiona Thistlethwaite, Filippo De Braud, Anna Minchom, Paolo A Ascierto, Matthew Wake, Mai A Nguyen, Giovanni Abbadessa, Richard C A Sainson, Cintia C Palu, Sonia Quaratino, Cecilia Deantonio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmunosuppression mechanisms mediated by regulatory T cells (Tregs) can lead to poor clinical outcomes in patients undergoing immune-based therapies. Activation markers, such as inducible T-cell co-stimulator (ICOS), are highly expressed on the surface of tumor-infiltrating Tregs and present as relevant targets for targeted depletion of these cells. Here, we present clinical outcomes from a Phase 1 study (NCT03829501) of an anti-ICOS antibody of alomfilimab (SAR445256 or KY1044) as monotherapy and in combination with the anti-programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) antibody, atezolizumab, in patients with advanced solid tumors.MethodsAlomfilimab was administered intravenously once every 3 weeks (Q3W) ±3 days at six dose levels (DLs; 0.8 mg to 240 mg) as monotherapy and five DLs (0.8 mg to 80 mg) in combination with atezolizumab (1,200 mg Q3W ±3 days). Eligible patients must have had advanced metastatic disease as determined by Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors V.1.1 and no viable treatment options according to National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines.ResultsOverall 38 patients were enrolled in the monotherapy cohort, and 102 patients were enrolled in the combination therapy cohort. Alomfilimab had a manageable safety profile and showed a trend toward modest efficacy in tumor growth control when combined with anti-PD-L1 in selected malignancies. At least one treatment-emergent adverse event was reported in 35 patients (89.7%) in the monotherapy cohort, and in 99 (98%) patients in the combination cohort. Objective response was not observed in the monotherapy cohort. In the combination cohort, seven patients had an objective response. Median time to progression-free survival was 2 months for both cohorts. Furthermore, alomfilimab showed evidence of target engagement on T-cell subsets, specifically cluster of differentiation (CD)4+memory cells, in both single-agent and in combination treatment with atezolizumab. This was accompanied by transient elevation of granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor, interferon-γ, and tumor necrosis factor-α levels and dose-dependent reduction of ICOS+Tregs in the tumor microenvironment.ConclusionsAlomfilimab treatment was associated with an acceptable safety profile across both mono and combination approaches, accompanied by decreased ICOS+Tregs populations and enhanced CD4+ and CD8+ effector T cells cell activity. Limited clinical activity was observed despite evidence of biological activity.Trial registration numberNCT03829501.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This is a first-in-human, dose-escalation study (monotherapy and combination therapy with anti-programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)) of alomfilimab, an anti-ICOS monoclonal antibody, in patients with advanced solid tumors. Alomfilimab demonstrated an acceptable safety profile with a dose-dependent reduction of ICOS+Tregs in TME. A trend towards modest efficacy in tumor growth control when combined with atezolizumab was observed in patients with selected advanced tumors. The biological activity in this study highlights the potential for identifying subgroup of patients likely to respond and benefit from anti-ICOS therapy in combination with an anti-PD-L1.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are effective therapeutic strategies that provide long-lasting responses in patients with malignancies such as metastatic melanoma, renal, and bladder cancer.1 2 Real-world application of ICI therapy demonstrated favorable outcomes in terms of antitumor response, safety, and long-term survival in multiple tumor types.3 4 Despite the remarkable benefits observed with targeting immune checkpoints,5 primary and secondary resistance to ICI therapy is common.5 6 Tumor-specific mechanisms for immune evasion include genetic/epigenetic alterations as well as alterations in cellular signaling pathways.7

Recruitment of immunosuppressive cells, such as regulatory T cells (Tregs), within the tumor microenvironment (TME) is a key process that can potentially induce resistance to ICI therapies.8 9 Tregs have potent immunosuppressive functions and reportedly proliferate in the TME following ICI therapy in the presence of local interleukin-2.10

Within tumor tissues, Tregs are highly activated and express activation markers including cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4, programmed cell death protein 1, and inducible T-cell costimulator (ICOS). ICOS is a member of the cluster of differentiation 28 (CD28) superfamily; however, unlike CD28, ICOS cannot be constitutively expressed on resting T cells11 and is rapidly induced following T-cell receptor cross-linking and/or CD28 co-stimulation.12

Tregs express significantly higher levels of ICOS than effector T cells (TEff) and exhibit strong immunosuppressive effects and are associated with tumor progression.9 13 14 High levels of ICOS+ Tregs are reported in several indications, including head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), non-small cell lung cancer, esophageal cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma15 and cervical cancer,16 and are associated with poor outcomes in advanced cancers.9 16

Alomfilimab, a fully human anti-ICOS immunoglobulin G1 (IgG1) kappa monoclonal antibody (mAb), has a dual mode of action, namely depletion of ICOS+ Tregs and co-stimulation of ICOSlow TEff17 (online supplemental figure 1). Sainson et al, demonstrated depletion of ICOS+ T cells and increased intratumoral TEff/Treg ratio by alomfilimab, thereby resulting in an increased secretion of interferon (IFN)-γ and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α by TEff cells.17 This dual mode of action of alomfilimab is expected to overcome resistance mechanisms against anti-programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-(L)1) inhibitors. Hence, alomfilimab alone or in combination with ICIs may benefit patients who are refractory or resistant to ICI therapies.

The Phase 1/2, open-label, multicenter trial (NCT03829501; EudraCT Number: 2018–003172-12) alomfilimab as a single agent or in combination with anti-PD-L1 (atezolizumab) in adult patients with selected advanced malignancies. Here, we report Phase 1 results from this trial.

Methods

Study design

This Phase 1 dose escalation (both monotherapy and combination cohort) study enrolled adult patients with selected advanced/metastatic malignancies. Preferred indications, inclusion/exclusion criteria are listed in online supplemental table 1.

The dose escalation part evaluated six dose levels (DLs; 0.8 mg to 240 mg) of alomfilimab as monotherapy and five DLs (0.8 mg to 80 mg) in combination with atezolizumab (1,200 mg). The combination arm included two enrichment cohorts (DL 2.4 mg and 8 mg) that expanded the DLs to further understand safety, preliminary efficacy, and pharmacokinetic (PK)/pharmacodynamic (PD) relationship (online supplemental figure 2). Each cohort, with a minimum of three patients, had at least two anti-PD-(L)1 naïve patients.

Study treatment

Alomfilimab was administered intravenously once every 3 weeks (Q3W) ±3 days at escalating doses starting at 0.8 mg to 240 mg. Atezolizumab (intravenously) was given at a fixed dose of 1,200 mg Q3W ±3 days. Study treatment was initiated on cycle (C) 1 day (D) 1. Each cycle comprised of 21 days (±3 days) with a treatment period of up to 48 months (approximately 70 cycles). Each patient had a screening period of up to 28 days; safety follow-up was 90 days from the end of treatment. Dose-limiting toxicity (DLT) assessment was performed after the third patient in a given cohort had completed the minimum DLT follow-up period of 21 days. Study completion was defined as the last visit for end of safety follow-up for the last treated patient.

Study outcomes

Primary objective was to assess safety and tolerability (Phase 1) of alomfilimab as monotherapy and in combination with atezolizumab. Key secondary objectives were to assess efficacy, PK, and changes in biomarkers from baseline in tumor tissues as a potential predictor of response to alomfilimab. Key exploratory objectives were to assess the PD effect of monotherapy and combination therapy (tumor tissues and peripheral blood) and target occupancy for monotherapy cohort. Detailed study objectives and endpoints are listed in online supplemental table 2.

Safety

Adverse events (AEs) were classified for severity according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events V.5.0. An AE was defined as related to study treatment when other medications, therapeutic interventions, or underlying conditions did not provide a sufficient explanation for the observed event.

Efficacy assessment

Tumor response was determined according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) V.1.1 and immune-related (i)RECIST every 8 weeks (+7 days) for the first 16 weeks and then every 12 weeks until progression of disease as per iRECIST or patient withdrawal. The progression-free survival (PFS) rate was obtained via Kaplan-Meier estimation using the complementary log–log transformation method at 12 months of treatment.

Additional details are in online supplemental appendix 1.

Blood sample collection

Pharmacokinetics

Serum samples were collected for the determination of alomfilimab concentration using a validated electrochemiluminescent assay. Additional details are in online supplemental appendix 1.

Immune cell profiling and receptor occupancy

Whole blood was collected into sodium heparin cell preparation tubes, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated at clinical sites at four time points (C1D1, C1D8, C2D1, and C2D8) (online supplemental figure 3). These samples were analyzed by chipcytometry (online supplemental appendix 1).

Measurement of plasma cytokine concentration

Plasma was collected at nine time points over the first three cycles, C1D1 (pre-dose), C1D2, C1D8, C2D1 (pre-dose and post-dose), C2D8, C3D1 (pre-dose and post-dose), C3D8 (online supplemental figure 3). Concentrations of granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), IFN-γ and TNF-α were measured at Myriad RBM with a Simoa ultra-high sensitive assay in the first three treatment cycles.

Tumor biopsies

Fresh tumor samples were collected at two different time points, screening and cycle 2 day 8 (C2D8) and processed in the form of a formalin-fixed paraffin embedded block and analyzed by immunohistochemistry (IHC) to assess the relationship between immune cell density (ICOS+ Tregs, CD8+ T cells) and treatment activity as well as the effect of alomfilimab on different immune cells in the TME (online supplemental figure 4A and online supplemental appendix 1).

Statistical analysis

The full analysis set (intent-to-treat population) included all patients allocated to study drug and the safety analysis set (exposed population) consisted of all patients who take at least one dose of study drug. Additional details on clinical and biomarker statistical analysis are in online supplemental appendix 1.

Data availability

Qualified researchers can request access to patient-level data and related study documents, including the clinical study report, study protocol with any amendments, blank case report forms, statistical analysis plan, and dataset specifications. Patient-level data will be anonymized, and study documents will be redacted to protect the privacy of trial patients. Safety

In the monotherapy cohort, 35 patients (89.7%) experienced at least one treatment-emergent adverse event (TEAE), while in the combination cohort, 99 patients (98%) reported TEAEs. The most frequent AE was infusion-related reactions, both for monotherapy (all Grades: 17.9%; Grade ≥3: 2.6%) and combination cohort (all Grades: 27.7%; Grade ≥3: 0). These were observed during C1 when no prophylaxis was allowed (online supplemental table 3).

Grade ≥3 AEs were reported in 13 (33.3%) patients in the monotherapy cohort and in 51 (50.5%) patients in the combination cohort; of these, 2.6% and 6.9% patients had Grade ≥3 AEs related to alomfilimab in monotherapy and combination cohorts, respectively. Treatment-related adverse events leading to study drug discontinuation occurred in one patient in each cohort (online supplemental table 3). All cohorts were completed without DLTs during the first 21 days of treatment. Serious AEs were reported in 12 (30.8%) and 37 (36.6%) patients in the monotherapy and the combination cohort, respectively.

The combination of alomfilimab with atezolizumab may also carry an increased risk of exacerbating immune-related adverse events (irAEs). These irAEs are of particular interest given alomfilimab’s mechanism of action. The most notable was immune-mediated myositis, which occurred in one patient (1.0%) in the combination cohort and infusion-related reactions (2.6% in monotherapy) and were classified as Grade ≥3. Other potential immune-related events included rash (5.1% in monotherapy, 6.9% in combination), rash maculopapular (5.1% in monotherapy, 4.0% in combination), pruritus (2.6% in monotherapy, 7.9% in combination), diarrhea (7.7% in monotherapy, 6.9% in combination), anemia (4% in combination), all of which were Grade <3. No cases of immune-related pneumonitis, colitis, hepatitis, or endocrinopathies were reported (online supplemental table 4).

Efficacy

Response was evaluable in 39 patients in the monotherapy cohort and 101 patients in the combination cohort. Across all DLs, objective response (OR) assessed by RECIST V.1.1 was not observed in any patient in the monotherapy cohort. In the combination cohort, seven patients demonstrated an OR. At the time of analysis in the combination cohort, seven patients achieved OR across diverse tumor histologies, demonstrating broad antitumor activity. One complete response (CR) was observed in a PD-(L)1 treatment-naïve patient with triple-negative breast cancer who received 2.4 mg of alomfilimab. Six patients had partial responses (PR): triple-negative breast cancer (n=1, 2.4 mg), rectal cancer (n=1, 2.4 mg), colorectal cancer (n=1, 2.4 mg), esophageal cancer (n=1, 8 mg), HNSCC (n=1, 8 mg), and penile cancer (n=1, 24 mg). Notably, six of the seven responders were anti-PD-(L)1 treatment-naïve, with only one responder (patient with HNSCC) having received prior anti-PD-(L)1 therapy (online supplemental table 5). Further, 28 patients had stable disease (SD), including 2 unconfirmed PRs; the remaining 52 patients experienced progressive disease (PDy). In the monotherapy cohort, 8 patients had SD, and 24 patients had PDy (online supplemental table 6). Median time to PFS was 2 months for both cohorts. PFS rate and duration of response at 12 months were not calculable for the monotherapy cohort (online supplemental table 7). Median overall survival was 6 months for the monotherapy cohort and 9 months for the combination cohort (online supplemental table 7). Pharmacokinetics

Alomfilimab PK parameters were available from 127 patients (online supplemental table 8). The systemic exposures of alomfilimab appeared generally similar for the monotherapy and the combination therapy. Comparing the exposure of alomfilimab at C3 to C1, there appeared to be no accumulation at the lower doses and only marginal accumulation at doses higher than 24 mg Q3W. The total variability on Cmax (maximum concentration) and the area under the curve from time zero to the time of last quantifiable concentration (AUClast) appeared moderate to high.

A greater than proportional increase in exposure (expressed as AUClast) was noted up to 8 mg. At higher doses, increase in exposure was proportional with dose increase, with a consistent half-life of about 10 days observed in this dose range (8–240 mg). The mean alomfilimab concentration-time profiles are presented in online supplemental figure 5. The lack of an apparent decrease in serum levels beyond C3 suggests negligible impact of anti-drug antibodies on alomfilimab systemic exposure.

ICOS receptor occupancy and alomfilimab target engagement on circulating T-cell subsets at all DLs

Different immune cell populations within PBMCs were assessed for ICOS expression at baseline C1D1 (pre-dose stage; n=94), including ICOS receptor occupancy and target engagement analysis on circulating T-cell subsets (online supplemental figure 6). Both Tregs (CD3+CD4+FOXP3+) and CD4+ memory (CD3+CD4+FOXP3+CD45RA−) were observed to be highly positive for ICOS expression (online supplemental figure 6A). Given the abundance of CD4 memory cells in PBMCs, they were chosen to be monitored for indirect ICOS+ receptor occupancy (RO).

The assay format for indirect assessment of ICOS+ RO by chip cytometry is described in online supplemental figure 6B. DLs ≤8 mg were associated with partial and transient RO, while DLs ≥8 mg were associated with complete RO and prolonged target engagement for monotherapy and combination therapy. The absence of signal post-dosing was attributed to saturated ICOS receptor by alomfilimab, and not due to CD4+ T-cell depletion, which stayed stable throughout assessments (online supplemental figure 6C) suggesting no depletion of cell populations in the periphery even at full target engagement, thereby confirming the observations from the preclinical model.17

Association between DLs (transient/partial RO) and cytokine secretion

Plasma GM-CSF and TNF-α levels were similarly induced across all groups on days 2 and 8 following C1. In C2 and C3, these cytokines were induced on D1 post-dose, with higher induction exhibited by the cohort with partial receptor occupancy that had a lower dose of alomfilimab administered. IFN-γ also increased post-treatment, and patients receiving combination therapy demonstrated higher levels on C1D8 (figure 2). These findings indicate alomfilimab-induced T-cell agonism and cytokine release in the periphery, especially when the target is not saturated.

Levels of ICOS+ Tregs in TME

The cell density of ICOS+ Tregs, defined as cells double positive for ICOS+ and FOXP3+ by IHC at screening and C2D8 were assessed. All data points falling below the diagonal indicate that alomfilimab treatment consistently reduced ICOS+ Tregs at C2D8 compared with baseline across almost all patients and cohorts (figure 3A left panel). Meanwhile, CD8+ cell density was maintained from baseline to C2D8, as observed by the measurements aligning along the diagonal (figure 3A central panel). The cell density and ratio were analyzed in the TME at screening and C2D8 in all available tumor biopsies (114 screening biopsies, 81 C2D8 biopsies). The ratio of CD8+/ICOS+ Tregs increased in most biopsies following treatment driven by the ICOS+ Tregs depletion (figure 3A right panel). When plotting the fold change between baseline and C2D8 against the DL, we observed that there was a mean decrease of ~4, which corresponded to more than 90% reduction of ICOS+ Tregs at C2D8 for DL2 and above for both monotherapy and combination therapy, as well as when categorized by response (figure 3B and C). These results indicate that alomfilimab induces ICOS+ Tregs depletion within the TME.

To further explore the relevance of ICOS+ Tregs, the association between baseline ICOS+ Tregs and response was conducted. The analysis demonstrated that CR, PR group had a significantly higher abundance of ICOS+ Tregs in TME than SD, PDy and not evaluable (NE) at screening in combination therapy (median 309 cells/mm2 in CR+PR, 88 cells/mm2 in SD and 22 cells/mm2 in PDy+NE, p=0.017; online supplemental figure 7A). The abundance of CD8+ cells in the TME was also evaluated and no significant difference was observed in either monotherapy (data not shown) or combination cohorts (online supplemental figure 7B).

PD-L1 expression at screening in the TME

Assessment of PD-L1 expression on tumor cells and immune infiltrate within the TME revealed that the enrolled populations exhibited generally low levels of PD-L1 expression (<25%). Patients with an OR (PR or CR) showed higher levels of CD8+T cells in the TME with low levels of PD-L1 on immune infiltrate (online supplemental figure 8A,B). It should be noted that few patients with cold “non-inflamed” tumors (<25% PD-L1+ immune infiltrate and <227.27 CD8+ cells/mm2 in TME representing the median value) had SD and remained on treatment for at least six cycles (online supplemental figure 8A,6B).

Discussion

In this first-in-human study, alomfilimab exhibited a manageable safety profile and elicited the expected pharmacodynamic responses, including ICOS+Treg depletion and proinflammatory cytokines induction. However, while alomfilimab showed a trend toward modest efficacy in tumor growth control when combined with anti-PD-L1 therapy in selected malignancies, the overall clinical benefit was limited.

Alomfilimab PK characteristics are typical for a monoclonal antibody, with low volume of distribution and a moderate terminal half-life of about 10 days at higher doses (≥8 mg). The evidence of target-mediated drug disposition at lower doses up to 8 mg Q3W is consistent with the observed partial and transient RO in the same dose range.

Our translational analysis showed a reduction in ICOShigh+ Tregs within the TME and T-cell agonism via cytokine secretion, with notable signals at non-saturating doses. These observations suggest a potential dual mechanism for this anti-ICOS antibody in the clinical setting, which appears distinct from other anti-ICOS mAbs that primarily target antitumor responses through CD4+ and CD8+ TEff cells activation. The INDUCE-2 Phase 1/2 trial evaluated feladilimab, an ICOS agonist that enhanced T-cell function by targeting ICOSlow CD4+ and CD8+ TEff cells without depleting ICOS-expressing cells.18 Moreover, the ICONIC first-in-human Phase 1/2 study investigated vopratelimab, a first-in-class, ICOS-binding, humanized IgG1κ agonist mAb, which upregulated ICOS expression on CD4+ T cells.19 However, both feladilimab and vopratelimab demonstrated limited clinical efficacy.18 19 Preliminary data from a monotherapy dose-escalation study of another bispecific antibody showed that XmAb23104 was generally well tolerated and had modest clinical activity in patients with advanced solid tumors.20

Additional aspects lend more robustness to our analyses when compared with previous studies targeting ICOS. While the antitumor efficacy of anti-ICOS monoclonal antibody was previously demonstrated by Amatore et al, their analysis was restricted to primary cells derived from patients with Sézary syndrome.21 A Phase 1 study of anti-ICOS antibody, MEDI-570, showed promising clinical benefit in patients with refractory angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma.22 While MEDI-570 also demonstrated a manageable safety profile, the frequency of ≥Grade 3 TEAEs was considerably higher in this analysis compared with both of our monotherapy and combination therapy cohorts. Further, the number of patients included in their analysis (n=23) was considerably lower compared with our study (n=140).

Further, we demonstrated that alomfilimab could successfully engage with its target on T-cell subsets, namely CD4+ memory cells and ICOS+ Tregs, both as a single agent as well as in combination with atezolizumab. Partial and transient RO was observed up to DL2 (2.4 mg), whereas complete and prolonged RO was confirmed at DL3 (8 mg) and above.

Transient induction in cytokine levels, GM-CSF, IFN-γ, and TNF-α was observed at partial RO (DLs <8 mg). While GM-CSF is observed to enhance antitumor immune response by activation of dendritic cells, IFN-γ and TNF-α are reported to play pleiotropic roles in immune system activation.2325 Hence, secretion of these cytokines may contribute to the antitumor functions of alomfilimab.

Expression of PD-L1 in TME, on tumor cells, and tumor-infiltrating immune cells, is considered a predictive biomarker for response to ICI monotherapies; however, its predictive role reportedly diminishes in the case of combination therapy. Wallis et al also observed that patients with both high and low PD-L1 expression in TME were associated with improved survival over standard treatment.26 27 In our analysis, we observed that patients with a better clinical response (PR or CR) had both low and high PD-L1 expression on tumor cells, which is indicative of the role played by alomfilimab in inducing a clinical response even with low PD-L1 expression. Additional investigation with larger datasets would be needed to confirm this observation. Further, absence and/or low frequency of immune cells within the TME (cold tumors) is indicative of poor antitumor response28; nevertheless, in this trial, two PR outcomes were achieved in patients with low CD8+ T cells at screening (online supplemental table 7).

Our data highlight the potential for identifying patients with solid tumors likely to respond to anti-ICOS therapy. However, it is noteworthy that ICOS+ Tregs within the TME are only a proportion of the entire immunosuppressive Tregs repertoire, and further studies are needed to investigate the correlation between ICOS+ Tregs depletion and clinical efficacy.

In conclusion, alomfilimab presents an acceptable safety and pharmacokinetic profile and therapeutic potential option in combination with anti-PD-L1 in patients with selected advanced malignancies. Notably, it demonstrates consistent dose-dependent ICOS+ Tregs depletion along with evidence of T-cell stimulation.
