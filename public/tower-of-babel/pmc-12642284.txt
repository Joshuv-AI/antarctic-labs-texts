Implementation outcomes of a school-based visual screening program using the peek tool in low-resource settings in Pakistan
Authors: Sarah Aman, Noor-us-Sabah Ahmad, Mohammad Ali A Sadiq, Mohammad Moin, Asad Aslam Khan, Muhammad Ismail Khan, Zahid Awan, Elanor Watts, Sergio Latorre Arteaga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVisual impairment among school-aged children in low-resource settings can significantly impact their quality of life and education due to limited access to comprehensive eye care. This study reports outcomes of a school-based vision screening program using the Peek Capture smartphone app for vision screening and patient referral in two districts of Punjab, Pakistan.MethodsThis was a retrospective, observational study. We analyzed primary programmatic data collected from children undergoing vision screening by School Health and Nutrition Supervisors in the districts of Chakwal and Layyah in Punjab, from January 2022 to September 2024. The Peek Capture platform enabled real-time tracking of program data, including referrals and follow-ups. Primary outcome measures included referral rates and follow-up adherence rates, which were analyzed by age group and gender. Statistical comparisons were made using the z-test for proportions and the Chi-squared test of independence.ResultsAmong 151,456 children screened (54% female), 5.7% (n = 8,682) were referred to Rural Health Centers (RHCs), with higher referral rates in females (6.3% vs. 5.0%, p < 0.001) and older children aged 12–18 (7.9% vs. 4.2%, p < 0.001). Follow-up was completed by 47.1% (n = 4,091), with no significant gender difference but lower rates in older children (45.6% vs. 49.2%, p = 0.001). Among those who followed up, 73.3% (n = 2,999) were confirmed to have an eye condition. Glasses were prescribed in 71.5% of diagnosed cases. Hospital referrals were rare (1.8%) and did not vary by gender.ConclusionThis study demonstrates the feasibility of high-volume, school-based vision screening using a smartphone-based platform in low-resource settings, with strong diagnostic yield among those who followed up. However, the 47.1% follow-up rate highlights a critical implementation challenge. Modest age- and gender-related differences point to areas for targeted programmatic strengthening. Further research is needed to assess the cost-effectiveness and long-term impact of such programs.Keywords: School-based vision screening, Smartphone eye screening, Peek, Pediatric eye health, Follow-up adherence, Low-resource settings

Introduction

Globally, at least 450 million children are affected by an untreated sight condition, with the burden disproportionately concentrated in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) like Pakistan, where access to pediatric eye care remains limited due to constrained resources, low public health awareness, and significant urban–rural disparities in service delivery [1–4]. Uncorrected refractive errors remain the leading cause of preventable vision impairment in Pakistani children [4]. Left unaddressed, childhood vision loss can negatively impact education [5, 6], psychological development [7], and long-term socio-economic outcomes [8].

Pakistan’s healthcare system, particularly in rural districts, faces structural limitations: shortages in trained eye care personnel, low uptake of school-based health services, and fragmented referral pathways [9]. Although school screening programs exist, they often lack standardized protocols and monitoring mechanisms, limiting their effectiveness [10]. Punjab, the country’s most populous province, has initiated school-based screening through its School Health and Nutrition Program, but data on outcomes and scalability remain scarce [11].

Digital health interventions offer a promising avenue to strengthen pediatric eye health delivery in such settings. The Peek Vision platform is a smartphone-based solution developed to support task-shifting, enable real-time referral tracking, automate follow-up reminders via SMS, and facilitate programmatic data capture [12–15]. Initially piloted in Kenya and later implemented in India, the platform has shown improved diagnostic accuracy and program adherence [13, 16]. Its adaptability to the Pakistani context presents a unique opportunity to assess its feasibility at scale.

This study evaluates the implementation of a school-based vision screening program using the Peek tool in two districts of Punjab, Pakistan– Chakwal and Layyah–between January 2022 to September 2024. The evaluation focuses on three process-related outcomes: referral rates, follow-up adherence, and the positive predictive value (PPV) of the screening. These metrics allow for identification of key drop-off points along the care continuum and help assess whether specific subgroups (e.g., by age or gender) may benefit from targeted interventions. The findings aim to inform policy-level decision-making regarding the scale-up of digital vision screening in Pakistan’s school health systems. Referral rates highlight which populations are more likely to be affected by eye conditions; follow-up rates help identify the stage at which patients are not retained in care; and the PPV reflects the screening tool’s accuracy. Together, these metrics help pinpoint both the program’s strengths and areas for improvement.

Methods

Study design and data retrieval

We conducted a retrospective observational study using primary programmatic data collected during routine school-based vision screening conducted with the Peek Capture software and accessed through the Peek Admin web-based platform. The school eye health screening program was implemented at the district level in all government primary and secondary schools in Chakwal and Layyah. In total, data from 1,380 schools were included in the analysis: 940 in Chakwal and 440 in Layyah. Classification of schools as urban or rural was not available within the program’s dataset. Participants were children aged 5 to 18 years who were screened between January 2022 and September 2024.

Visual acuity was measured based on the principles and techniques described in a randomized controlled trial conducted by Rono et al. in Kenya, the pilot site for the software and referral system [12]. Screening was conducted in schools by trained School Health and Nutrition Supervisors (SHNSs) using the Peek Capture mobile application, which includes the clinically validated Peek Visual Acuity (VA) test. Eye health needs requiring referral were identified based on distance VA worse than 6/9 in either eye and/or the presence of other ocular abnormalities requiring further care. Children were screened during routine visits to government primary and secondary schools across both districts. At Rural Health Centers (RHCs), children received comprehensive assessments, including visual acuity measurement, objective and subjective refraction, and anterior and posterior segment evaluation using a slit lamp.

All data were analyzed in aggregate and stratified into two age groups: 6–11 years (middle childhood) and 12–18 years (early adolescence), based on standardized pediatric developmental stages defined by the National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (NICHD). Children aged 5 were excluded from subgroup analysis due to limited representation and their classification under “early childhood” in the NICHD framework [17].

Outcome measures

Key outcome measures recorded and assessed during the analysis included the following: number of children screened, number of children referred to triage at the RHC, number of children referred further to secondary and tertiary healthcare facilities, follow-up rates, true positives (defined as the number of children who failed the initial screening, proceeded to the triage stage, and were confirmed to have a genuine eye condition, whether it be refractive or non-refractive), false positives (defined as the number of children who failed the initial screening, proceeded to the triage stage, but were ultimately found not to have a genuine eye condition, whether it be refractive or non-refractive), and positive predictive value (PPV) of the tool. PPV was defined as true positives divided by all children who underwent confirmatory testing (true positives + false positives), multiplied by 100 (PPV = (True Positives/[True Positives + False Positives]) × 100). Follow-up adherence was defined as attendance at a RHC within a maximum of 3 months from the date of school-based screening, with the majority of follow-ups occurring within the first 2 months.

Data management and analysis

Data was entered into Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) and analyzed using SPSS Statistics (IBM, version 29.02.0). Categorical comparisons between subgroups (by age and gender) were performed using two-proportion z-tests and Chi-square tests of independence. These were primarily descriptive comparisons and do not represent formal interaction testing. Key outcomes assessed included referral rates, follow-up adherence, and the positive predictive value (PPV) of screening. Confidence intervals for proportions were calculated using the Wilson score interval method to estimate the precision of follow-up rates and PPV. Two-proportion z-tests were used for direct comparisons of referral rates between groups, while Chi-square tests of independence were applied to assess associations between categorical variables such as gender, age group, and follow-up or diagnostic outcomes. Due to the use of de-identified aggregate programmatic data, individual-level multivariable analyses were not possible.

Ethical considerations

Data storage, transmission, and retrieval adhered to a Data Protection Agreement (DPA) with local stakeholders, following the guidelines set by the European Union General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). The study was conducted with the approval of the Ethical Review Board of the College of Ophthalmology and Allied Vision Sciences (COAVS), and permissions were obtained from the associated stakeholders to use patient data while ensuring anonymity. Written informed consent from participants’ legal guardians or next of kin was not required, in accordance with national legislation and institutional requirements.

A total of 8,682 children (5.7%) were referred to Rural Health Centers (RHCs) for further evaluation. Referral rates were significantly higher among females compared to males (6.3% vs. 5.0%, p < 0.05, z-test), and higher among children aged 12–18 years compared to those aged 6–11 years (7.9% vs. 4.2%, p < 0.05, z-test).

Of those referred, 47.1% (n = 4,091) attended follow-up at an RHC. Follow-up rates were slightly higher in females than males (47.9% [95% CI: 46.5–49.2%] vs. 46.0% [95% CI: 44.4–47.7%], p = 0.094, Chi-square test), though this was not statistically significant. However, children aged 6–11 years were significantly more likely to follow up than those aged 12–18 (49.2% vs. 45.6%, p = 0.001, Chi-square test).

Among those who attended follow-up, 2,999 children were confirmed to have an eye condition, yielding an overall positive predictive value (PPV) of 73.3%. PPV did not differ significantly by gender (72.1% [95% CI: 69.9–74.2%] in males vs. 74.1% [95% CI: 72.3–75.8%] in females, p = 0.175, Chi-square test). However, PPV was significantly higher in older children compared to younger children (76.4% vs. 69.4%, p < 0.001, Chi-square test).

The majority of confirmed diagnoses were refractive conditions (86.8%), while non-refractive conditions were present in 26.6% of cases. Some children presented with both. Among diagnosed children, 71.5% were prescribed corrective glasses, 12.4% received medications, and 1.8% were referred to higher-level facilities. Only 0.1% were referred to tertiary care hospitals.

Discussion

This study evaluated the implementation outcomes of a large school-based vision screening program in Pakistan using the Peek tool across two districts in Pakistan. Our findings suggest the feasibility of digital screening tools in identifying children with visual impairment while revealing areas for programmatic improvement. By leveraging existing human resources–School Health and Nutrition Supervisors–and digital tools (Peek Capture and Admin), the program achieved widespread coverage, screening over 150,000 children without requiring new personnel or significant new infrastructure.

A key strength of the program was its high PPV among adherents (73.3%), indicating that nearly three out of four referred children were confirmed to have an eye condition upon clinical examination. This is comparable to and higher than the PPV of various similar studies using school-based VA screening initiatives [18]. However, this must be interpreted in the context of relatively low adherence, with only 47.1% of referred children attending RHCs. When viewed at the population level, the diagnostic yield was 2.0% (2,999 diagnosed/151,456 screened), which underscores the risk of attrition in the care cascade and indicates that many children with potential visual needs remain undiagnosed. Moreover, because PPV is influenced by local disease burden, our results may not generalize to populations with differing prevalence.

Our referral rate of 5.7% was lower than the 8–15% range typically reported in similar school screening programs in LMICs, which may reflect more conservative referral thresholds or program-specific screening protocols [18]. While only 47.1% of referred children in our study attended follow-up at the RHC, this is consistent with known barriers to follow-up in school screening programs, including lack of parental awareness, distance to health facilities, and competing household priorities [19]. These findings support the diagnostic performance of the tool among those who adhered to referral, though low follow-up limits its broader programmatic impact.

We found that younger children (6–11 years) had lower referral rates (4.2% vs. 7.9% for older children, p < 0.05) but showed better follow-up adherence (49.2% vs. 45.6%, p = 0.001). Despite this, their PPV was lower (69.4% vs. 76.4%, p < 0.001). While there is limited epidemiologic data from Pakistan, this is consistent with the relatively low prevalence of visual impairment or refractive errors reported in previous small-scale studies. Latif et al. reported a prevalence of 24.4% among high school students in Lahore, with myopia accounting for the majority of cases [10]. Other studies have reported prevalence rates of 19.8% in urban Lahore [20], 20.4% in Kohat [21], and 23% in children aged 11–16 years in Peshawar. This likely reflects the natural history of refractive development, where refractive errors tend to emerge and progress with age, and lower symptom awareness in younger children.

Large population-based studies have shown that the majority of cases of significant visual disorder in childhood are those of refractive error or strabismus, both of which often result in amblyopia [22–26]. Children are less likely to raise visual complaints, and this may be a possible factor for reduced follow-up adherence in this group. Some of the barriers are overcome by the Peek software and data intelligence platform, with SMS reminders in the local language and locally trained screeners, while other barriers require more mobilization efforts in the future. One inevitable limitation of a school screening program, however, is that children who are not enrolled in formal education are missed, and since children with visual impairments are also less likely to be enrolled in formal education, home-based screening may be needed.

Although females had a marginally higher follow-up rate than males (47.9% vs. 46.0%), this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.094). Therefore, our findings suggest no meaningful gender-based disparity in adherence to referral. In contrast, the PPV was significantly higher among females (74.1%) compared to males (72.1%). Of note, however, is that the absolute difference of 2%, despite being statistically significant, is unlikely to carry meaningful clinical or public health implications. This may reflect the relatively equitable access to school-based services in the study setting, where both boys and girls attend government schools under similar conditions. Therefore, gender-specific screening protocols are not warranted based on these findings. Nonetheless, prior literature underscores the broader gender inequities in eye care access, particularly in adult populations, which may become more relevant outside the school setting [9].

While our results offer valuable insights, their generalizability to other regions of Pakistan may be limited. The two districts included, Chakwal and Layyah, differ in socioeconomic status and infrastructure from more urbanized areas or regions with limited school enrollment. Further evaluations across diverse regions are needed to validate these findings at the national level. A key limitation of this study is the inability to conduct multivariable modeling due to the aggregated nature of the available dataset. Although we performed bivariate comparisons using z-tests and chi-squared tests to identify subgroup differences, these methods do not allow adjustment for potential confounders. Future research should leverage individual-level data to support regression models, which would enable more robust estimation of effect sizes and confidence intervals while adjusting for relevant covariates. While we did not apply formal correction for multiple comparisons given the limited number of pre-specified subgroup analyses, we acknowledge the potential for type I error inflation and recommend cautious interpretation of marginally significant findings.

Our findings support the utility of the Peek tool as part of pediatric vision screening efforts, though further evaluation across different populations is needed. Prior studies have demonstrated its sensitivity of 85% and specificity of 91% for detecting visual impairment compared to standard ETDRS charts in field settings [12, 13]. However, its utilization is also subject to several limitations. Challenges related to age and lower cooperation levels in younger children, yielding less reliable results, need to be taken note of during the screening process. In addition, its performance may vary across different settings and populations, necessitating ongoing validation and adjustments. Improving Pakistan’s eye care referral system requires addressing upfront costs for devices, training, and field testing through increased sponsorship and investment. Based on the findings of our study, several strategies can be considered to improve program performance. First, the low follow-up rate among referred children (47.1%) suggests the need for enhanced adherence interventions; implementing more frequent SMS reminders or phone calls directed at parents or caregivers may improve attendance at referral facilities. Second, coordination between school principals and health authorities to facilitate group transportation to RHCs could help overcome logistical barriers, especially in rural settings. Third, digital dashboards used by SHNSs could be configured to generate alerts for children who miss appointments, prompting timely follow-up action. These targeted, system-level enhancements, made possible by the digitized data infrastructure of the program, may help improve continuity of care and reduce patient attrition in school-based vision screening programs. Additional strategies such as discounted glasses, local optical services, and addressing medication costs are also crucial to enhance compliance with prescribed interventions. Future efforts should include validating Peek technology through randomized trials and establishing specialized services closer to residential areas like Chakwal and Layyah. Findings from this school-based screening initiative may help inform Pakistan’s broader efforts toward universal eye health coverage and equitable access, in alignment with Sustainable Development Goals [27].

Conclusion

In conclusion, the Peek tool demonstrated strong diagnostic accuracy, reflected in its high PPV among adherents and validated sensitivity, for vision screening in school-aged children. Its integration into school-based programs enables not only screening and referral, but also real-time program monitoring and identification of critical dropout points in the patient pathway. However, while the tool itself is diagnostically effective, the overall success of such programs depends heavily on improving follow-up adherence and addressing barriers to care. Targeted strategies for specific populations and greater community mobilization are essential to enhance program impact in low-resource settings like Pakistan. Although our study did not assess cost or long-term outcomes, the findings underscore the feasibility of incorporating smartphone-based tools into pediatric eye care delivery in under-resourced areas. Future research should evaluate the cost-effectiveness, sustainability, and broader impact of such programs on child health and education.
