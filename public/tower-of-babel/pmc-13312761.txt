Altered glymphatic-related MRI markers in children with acute lymphoblastic and myeloid leukemia at diagnosis
Authors: Shu Su, Zhou Liu, Shuzhen Huang, Xiang Gao, Chengfen Deng, Weifeng Hou, Long Qian, Libin Huang, Yanlai Tang, Zhiyun Yang, Liping Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile the discovery of the glymphatic system has greatly advanced our understanding of waste clearance and fluid dynamics in central nervous system diseases, the neurofluid dynamics in pediatric acute leukemia remain to be elucidated. This study sought to evaluate MRI markers putatively related to neurofluid dynamics in pediatric acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML), including perivascular space (PVS) burden, free water (FW) fraction, and diffusion tensor imaging along the PVS (DTI-ALPS).MethodsSeventy-two children with acute leukemia and 72 age- and sex-matched typically developing (TD) children (50 ALL and TDs1; 22 AML and TDs2) were included in this prospective study. Group differences in brain volumetric measures and glymphatic-related MRI markers were evaluated. In addition, MRI metrics that differed significantly between groups were further examined for associations with clinical variables and total sleep scale scores using partial correlation analyses.ResultsCompared to TDs, ALL and AML showed no significant differences in intracranial volume. Compared with TDs, decreased brain parenchymal volume and gray matter volume were found in both children with ALL and AML (all FDR-corrected P ≤ 0.04). Compared with TDs, the ALL group exhibited reduced white matter volume and increased cerebrospinal fluid volume (all FDR-corrected P ≤ 0.001), while the AML group showed no significant differences in these measures. For glymphatic-related MRI markers, decreased PVS volume and count, FW value, and DTI-ALPS index were observed in both ALL and AML (all FDR-corrected P ≤ 0.02). In addition, the DTI-ALPS index was negatively correlated with risk stratification and total scale scores in children with ALL (all P ≤ 0.03).ConclusionsThis preliminary, cross-sectional study identified a neuroimaging pattern in pediatric acute leukemia, characterized by reduced brain parenchyma volume and alterations in MRI markers putatively linked to neurofluid dynamics. In patients with ALL, a lower ALPS index was correlated with higher clinical risk stratification and greater sleep disturbance. These observations support future studies to clarify the biological relevance of neurofluid-related imaging features in acute leukemia.Prospectively registered trials numberChiCTR2000031353; registered date: 2020-04.Keywords: Leukemia, Central nervous system, Glymphatic system, Diagnosis, Child

Introduction

Leukemia, the most common malignancy in childhood worldwide, is a malignant disease of abnormal hematopoietic tissue [1], including acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML). With significant advances in therapy, the rates of five-year event-free survival are as high as 80% for ALL and 40–50% for AML [2]. Nevertheless, central nervous system (CNS) involvement of acute leukemia remains one of major clinical challenges and a main reason for disease relapse during the remission period [3]. The current gold standard for evaluating CNS involvement is the detection of leukemic cells in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) through lumbar puncture, but this method has a relatively high false-negative rate [4]. Furthermore, most CNS relapses occur in children initially diagnosed with CNS-negative leukemia, suggesting that CNS involvement may be more common than expected in acute leukemia [5]. Thus, exploring neuroimaging features that may be related to early CNS changes in acute leukemia is important.

Several studies have reported that the blood-cerebrospinal fluid barrier (BCSFB) might be a vital pathway in early-stage CNS involvement of acute leukemia [6, 7]. Leukemia cells entering the CSF could subsequently infiltrate the perivascular spaces (PVS) and brain parenchyma [6, 7]. Considering that the para-arterial CSF influx channels (into the PVS), CSF-interstitial fluid (ISF) exchange channels, and para-venous ISF efflux channels constitute the glymphatic system, early-stage CNS involvement in acute leukemia could affect the glymphatic system [8–10]. Our group’s prior work, utilizing the DTI-ALPS index, was the first to report reduced DTI-ALPS in children with ALL at diagnosis [11]. However, that exploratory study had several constraints: it involved a modest sample size, was restricted to children with ALL, and relied on a single imaging biomarker. The present cohort partially overlaps with that previously published 2023 cohort: 25 of the 50 children with ALL and 20 of the 50 matched TD children were included in the prior study, whereas all 22 children with AML and their 22 matched TD children are newly included in the present analysis. Therefore, the current study extends the prior report by enlarging the ALL cohort, adding an AML cohort, and incorporating multiple MRI markers, including PVS burden and FW fraction in addition to DTI-ALPS.

Hence, we aimed to investigate: (1) alterations in brain volumetric measures and MRI markers putatively related to neurofluid dynamics, including PVS burden, free water (FW), and the DTI-ALPS index, in children with acute leukemia without clinically diagnosed CNS involvement; and (2) the associations between these MRI markers and clinical features, as well as total sleep scale scores in children with acute leukemia.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

This study was prospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2000031353, registered in April 2020). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents of all subjects prior to the study. The study was conducted in accordance with good clinical practice and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Between February 2021 and December 2023, we enrolled children with acute leukemia at the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University if they met the following criteria: (i) newly diagnosed acute leukemia based on the criteria defined by the NCCN guidelines [12, 13]; (ii) age < 18 years; (iii) right-handedness; (iv) no history of therapy; and (v) no CNS infiltration based on conventional diagnostic methods according to the NCCN guidelines [12, 13].

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) any history of neurological disease or brain lesion detected by MRI; (ii) any history of another malignant hematological disease; (iii) any history of cranial radiation, bone marrow transplantation, or chemotherapy; (iv) incomplete clinical information; (v) contraindications to MRI; or (vi) poor image quality.

In addition, TD children were defined as children whose growth and abilities progressed in line with age-expected norms and who had no diagnosed developmental disability or disorder. TDs were enrolled via online advertisements or from the community. Some TDs were recruited after pituitary problems and intracranial lesions had been excluded during screening for short stature (younger than 6 years old, n = 7). Clinical data

Demographic information was collected for all subjects. For children with acute leukemia, white blood cell (WBC) count at diagnosis, subtypes, genetic/molecular markers, CNS status, and risk stratification were acquired. CNS involvement status was clearly delineated as CNS1 (no CSF blasts), CNS2 (< 5 leukocytes/µL with blast cells in CSF and < 10 erythrocytes/µL), and CNS3 (≥ 5 leukocytes/µL with blast cells, or the presence of a cerebral mass or cranial nerve palsy). Risk classification was also evaluated in ALL based on the South China Children Cancer Group (SCCCG) protocol [14]. Specifically, assignment to the high-risk group was based on meeting at least one of the following criteria: (i) age < 1 year; (ii) presence of MLL rearrangement, t (17;19) [TCF3/HLF], or hypodiploidy (< 45 chromosomes); (iii) IKZF1 alteration; (iv) poor response to prednisone or failure to achieve complete remission by the end of induction therapy; (v) minimal residual disease (MRD) ≥ 1% on day 15 or day 33 of induction. Children were classified into the standard-risk group if they met any of the following criteria: (i) age > 10 years; (ii) WBC count > 50 × 10⁹/L; (iii) T-ALL; (iv) TCF3/PBX1 rearrangement; (v) Philadelphia chromosome-positive (Ph+) ALL; (vi) Ph-like ALL; (vii) CNS involvement classified as CNS2 or CNS3; (viii) testicular leukemia at diagnosis; (ix) MRD between 0.1% and 1% on day 15, or between 0.01% and 1% on day 33 of induction. The remaining children with MRD < 0.01% on day 15 and 33 were categorized into the low-risk group. We then analyzed the association between baseline MRI markers and subsequent composite clinical risk classification in children with ALL. Furthermore, sleep issues were assessed in children with acute leukemia using the Sleep Disturbance Scale for Children (SDSC) after the baseline MRI scan and before the initiation of any chemotherapy [15].

Image acquisition

All MRI sequences were performed on a 3.0-T MRI system (SIGNA Pioneer MR, General Electric Healthcare) using a 32-channel head coil. Each subject’s head was placed in the supine position with foam pillows inside the coil to diminish motion artifacts. Conventional T2-weighted imaging and fluid attenuated inversion recovery (T2-FLAIR) was then used to exclude any cranial organic lesions.

The MRI protocol included the following sequences: (1) T1-weighted images were acquired with a 3D fast spoiled gradient-echo sequence. The parameters were repetition time (TR), 8.5 ms, echo time (TE), 3.3 ms, flip angle, 12°, thickness, 1 mm/no gap, pixel size, 1.0 mm × 1.0 mm, field of view (FOV), 25.6 cm, matrix size, 256 × 256, number of excitation (NEX), 1.00, and acceleration factor, 2. (2) T2-weighted images were obtained to quantitatively assess PVS burden. The parameters were TR, 7365 ms, TE, 133.2 ms, slice thickness, 6 mm/no gap, pixel size, 0.5 mm2, FOV, 25.6 cm, and matrix size, 512 × 512. (3) Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) was performed using a single-shot echo planar imaging sequence and was acquired axially parallel to the anterior-posterior commissure to cover the entire brain. The parameters were TR, 10,000 ms, TE, 88.6 ms, FOV, 25.6 cm, matrix size, 128 × 128, voxel size, 2 mm3, NEX, 1.00, number of directions, 32, and b value = 1000 s/mm2. DTI quality control included visual inspection for severe ghosting, dropout, and motion-related distortion, together with eddy-derived motion estimates (mean absolute translation and rotation). Datasets with severe artifacts were excluded.

Image processing and analysis

Brain volume estimation

White matter volume (WMV), gray matter volume (GMV), CSF volume, total choroid plexus volume (CPV), intracranial volume (ICV), and brain parenchymal volume (BPV) were estimated using the FreeSurfer recon-all pipeline (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/, version 6.0). Specifically, GMV and ICV were extracted automatically. WMV was calculated as the sum of “cerebral WM”, “cerebellar WM”, “brainstem”, and “corpus callosum”. Total CPV was obtained by summing the left and right CPV. In addition, BPV was assessed by summing GMV and WMV. Total CSF volume was calculated based on the principle that the intracranial compartment is occupied by BPV and CSF. Accordingly, total CSF Volume = ICV − (GMV + WMV).

PVS burden calculation

T2-weighted images were first evaluated for technical quality prior to segmentation [16–18]. Perivascular spaces in the whole brain white matter were automatically segmented using the uAI Research Portal (Shanghai United Imaging Intelligence Co.Ltd, Shanghai, China). The resulting segmentation masks were then imported into ITK-SNAP and overlaid on the original T2-weighted images for visual inspection. Manual corrections were subsequently performed independently by two trained neuroradiologists, each with 5 years of experience, who were blinded to the clinical and demographic information. Corrections were made to exclude PVS located outside the cerebrum and to ensure that only true PVS were retained. Any discrepancies between the two raters were resolved by consensus. Finally, whole-brain white matter PVS count and volume were calculated.

Diffusivities and DTI-ALPS calculation

The DTI-ALPS index was calculated according to the previous studies [19–22]. DTI preprocessing, including correction for off-resonance effects, eddy current-induced distortions, slice-to-volume motion, overall movement, and outliers, was performed using the Eddy tool in MATLAB R2019b (MathWorks). Specifically, diffusivity maps in the direction of the x-axis (Dxx, right-left), y-axis (Dyy, anterior-posterior), and z-axis (Dzz, inferior-superior) were obtained for each subject. Subsequently, the diffusivity maps in all directions were co-registered to the standard FA map template of the JHU-ICBM-FA-1 mm. A 5-mm-diameter region of interest (ROI) was placed in the area of three contiguous axial slices above the bilateral ventricles in the left hemisphere, since all subjects were right-handed, using ITK-SNAP software. We extracted the diffusivities for each ROI and then calculated the mean value of these diffusivities for all subjects. Two radiologists, blinded to clinical and demographic information with 5 years of work experience, processed the data for all subjects. Finally, each diffusivity parameter measured by the two radiologists was averaged and taken as the final result for each individual. The DTI-ALPS index was calculated as (Dxxproj + Dxxassoc)/ (Dyyproj + Dzzassoc).

FW values calculation

FW maps were constructed from preprocessed diffusion-weighted images using a regularized bi-tensor model with the open-source Diffusion Imaging in Python algorithm [23, 24]. FW across the white matter skeleton of the brain was then analyzed using tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS), and the mean cerebral white matter FW was extracted as the FW values. Statistical analysis

SPSS (version 25.0) was used to perform all statistical analyses. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality. Group differences in age were analyzed using the two-sample t-test, and differences in sex distribution were analyzed using the chi-squared test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The inter-rater reliability for the ALPS index, PVS count, and PVS volume was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs). Group differences in PVS volume, FW, and structural volume were then compared using a GLM controlling for age, sex, and ICV. Group differences in the remaining metrics were performed using a GLM controlling for age and sex. Linear regression analysis was used to evaluate the association between age and the ALPS index in TD children. The Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction method was used for multiple comparisons. FDR-corrected P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Partial correlation analyses were conducted to assess the relationships between clinical information and significant MR parameters, controlling for age and sex. The threshold for statistical significance was set at 0.05.

Results

Demographic characteristics of participants

One hundred and sixty subjects were recruited. Finally, a total of 50 pediatric patients with ALL (age range, 5–14 years; 33 males and 17 females) and 22 with AML (age range, 5–14 years; 15 males and 7 females) were included in this study. To match the acute leukemia group in terms of age and sex, 50 TDs1 with a mean age of 7.98 years (age range, 5–14 years; 33 males and 17 females) and 22 TDs2 with a mean age of 8.54 years (age range, 5–14 years; 15 males and 7 females) were selected. The remaining subjects were excluded for the following reasons: (i) another malignant hematological disease (2 ALL and AML), (ii) incomplete MRI sequences (5 TDs and 3 ALL), and (iii) images with artifacts (2 ALL and AML). As presented in Table 1, there were no significant differences in the distribution of age or sex between the 50 ALL and 50 TDs1 participants or between the 22 AML and 22 TDs2 participants. Among pediatric patients with ALL, there were 11 cases of T-cell ALL and 39 cases of B-cell ALL. Furthermore, three risk groups were identified in children with acute leukemia in this study, including low risk (14 ALL), standard risk (21 ALL), and high risk (15 ALL). Correlations among glymphatic-related MRI markers, sleep scores, and clinical characteristics

In total, 58 valid questionnaires were collected from children with acute leukemia, including 42 with ALL and 16 with AML. The total SDSC score for children with ALL ranged from 30 to 58, with a median of 38 and interquartile range of 33–42, and 15 cases (35.71%) had sleep disorders. Among children with AML, 5 (31.25%) had sleep disorders. Detailed scores are shown in Table 1. No correlation was found between total SDSC scores and PVS or FW values. However, the ALPS index was negatively related to total SDSC scores in children with ALL (r = -0.33, P = 0.03, Fig. 4a). However, there were no significant relationships between total SDSC scores and glymphatic-related MRI metrics in children with AML.

Correlation analyses between ALPS index and total scores of SDSC (a) as well as risk stratification (b) in ALL children. Abbreviations: ALPS: along the perivascular space; SDSC: Sleep Disturbance Scale of Children; ALL: acute lymphoblastic leukemia

We performed correlation analyses using the significant MR metrics (including glymphatic-related MRI metrics and brain volumetric parameters) and clinical characteristics (including risk stratification and WBC at diagnosis) in children with ALL and AML. For children with ALL, a significantly negative correlation was observed between the risk stratification and DTI-ALPS (r = -0.47, P = 0.001, Fig. 4b). Similarly, a significantly positive correlation was identified between risk stratification and Dzzassoc (r = 0.30, P = 0.03). However, no significant correlations were found between other major MR metrics and risk stratification or WBC at diagnosis. For children with AML, only PVS/WMV showed a significantly positive association with WBC count at diagnosis (r = 0.46, P = 0.04), whereas no significant correlations were observed between the other major MR metrics and clinical characteristics.

Discussion

Main findings

This exploratory MRI study assessed putative indicators of neurofluid dynamics in children with acute leukemia without clinically diagnosed CNS involvement. The main findings were as follows: (i) both pediatric ALL and AML groups exhibited smaller BPV than TD children; ii) the ALL group, but not the AML group, showed significantly increased CSF volume compared with its matched TD group; (iii) glymphatic-related MRI markers, including PVS burden, FW values, and the ALPS index, were significantly lower in both pediatric ALL and AML groups than in TDs; and (iv) within the ALL subgroup, the ALPS index was negatively associated with risk stratification and total SDSC scores. Taken together, these cross-sectional findings are consistent with alterations in MRI markers putatively related to neurofluid dynamics in children with acute leukemia, even in the absence of clinically diagnosed CNS involvement; however, they do not directly demonstrate altered neurofluid physiology. The observed correlations suggest that the ALPS index may serve as a hypothesis-generating imaging feature for future longitudinal studies aimed at clarifying subclinical neurological changes in acute leukemia.

Glymphatic-related MRI markers

The cerebral glymphatic system represents a predominant theoretical model for brain waste clearance, which proposes that subarachnoid CSF circulates along PVS, mixes with interstitial fluid, and is subsequently cleared [25]. The MRI metrics used herein are regarded as non-specific, surrogate markers that might relate to different aspects of this proposed physiology: (a) PVS burden is often interpreted as a possible correlate of presumed fluid movement or stasis [9, 26]; (b) CP volume was treated as an exploratory measure that may be associated with CSF production [27]; (c) FW fraction from DTI is thought to possibly reflect interstitial water content, which may be influenced by CSF-ISF exchange [28]; and (d) The ALPS index is designed as an indirect diffusion-based marker related to perivascular water movement [25]. It is critical to emphasize that these metrics provide only inferential and associative data. Their use here is to explore potential physiological correlations, not to assert definitive mechanistic functions.

In our cohort, a very weak association was found between age and the ALPS index (R² = 0.057, p = 0.008). This is consistent with the restricted age range of our sample (5–14 years) and with prior work showing minimal age dependence in younger populations, suggesting that the observed ALPS index alterations are more likely related to pathological or physiological state factors than to normative aging [29].

Possible mechanisms

The mechanisms of CNS involvement in leukemia are complex. The blood-CSF barrier (BCSFB) and the blood-leptomeningeal barrier are considered primary routes for leukemic cell entry [30]. Cells may initially colonize the leptomeninges and CSF, potentially migrating along perivascular spaces [6]. Furthermore, the systemic effects of leukemia may impair glial cell function and induce neuroinflammation, which may consequently affect astrocytic aquaporin-4 (AQP4), a key element of glymphatic function. This could, in theory, impair CSF-ISF exchange and waste clearance [31]. In parallel, reactive astrogliosis, a potential consequence of neuroinflammation, has been shown to reduce glymphatic flow [32].

Our observation of increased CSF volume in patients with ALL might be consistent with a disruption of normal fluid homeostasis, although alternative explanations, such as compensatory mechanisms, cannot be ruled out. The concurrent findings of reduced PVS burden, FW fraction, and ALPS index converge to form an imaging pattern that has been associated with glymphatic dysfunction in other neurological conditions. This raises the hypothesis that altered neurofluid dynamics, potentially involving the glymphatic pathway, may be a feature of pediatric leukemia even in the absence of clinical CNS disease. However, it is critical to emphasize that the present MRI findings are indirect and correlative. They do not establish causality or pinpoint specific disrupted physiological steps. Furthermore, a critical limitation of the ALPS index should be acknowledged. Because this metric is derived from diffusion measurements obtained in regions containing crossing fiber tracts, a reduced ALPS index cannot be interpreted as a specific marker of impaired perivascular clearance and may also be influenced by changes in local white matter microstructure, fiber configuration, or edema. In our cohort, the reduction in the ALPS index was accompanied by decreased white matter volume, making it difficult to distinguish the potential effect of altered white matter architecture from that of putative glymphatic dysfunction.

Emerging studies have described potential anatomical routes for CSF drainage and molecular trafficking between the subarachnoid space and adjacent meningeal structures [33]. Although such findings provide a broader biological context for considering neurofluid-related processes, the present MRI data cannot determine whether these pathways are altered in pediatric leukemia. Therefore, this interpretation remains speculative and should be examined in future mechanistic studies.

Within the ALL subgroup, the ALPS index was the only glymphatic-related MRI metric associated with clinical risk stratification. Because the ALPS index has been proposed as an indirect diffusion-based marker related to perivascular water movement [25], this finding may provide a hypothesis-generating imaging clue for future studies. However, the ALPS index is not a direct measure of glymphatic clearance and may be influenced by white matter microstructure, crossing fibers, edema, and regional diffusion properties. Therefore, its biological and clinical significance in pediatric leukemia should be interpreted cautiously and validated in longitudinal studies.

Neurofluid dynamics, leukemia, and sleep

Sleep disturbances have been reported in children with leukemia following chemotherapy [34]; however, whether leukemia itself contributes to sleep abnormalities remains unclear. Among children with valid SDSC questionnaires, approximately one-third had sleep disturbances (20/58, 34.5%). The association between the ALPS index and sleep disturbance was observed in the ALL cohort, but not in the smaller AML group, which may reflect a true biological difference, insufficient statistical power, or both. Multiple factors are known to influence glymphatic function, including arterial pulsation, respiration, and sleep [35]. Experimental studies in mice have shown that sleep is associated with an increased interstitial volume fraction, which reduces resistance to CSF influx and facilitates glymphatic circulation [36]. Therefore, our findings raise the possibility that neurofluid-related imaging features may be associated with sleep disturbances in leukemia, although the direction of this association cannot be established in this cross-sectional study.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the assessment of PVS was based on conventional 2D T2-weighted images with a slice thickness of 6 mm. This relatively coarse resolution makes quantification susceptible to partial-volume effects and may limit sensitivity for detecting smaller PVS. Therefore, mechanistic interpretations derived from the PVS findings should be made with caution, and our results are better viewed as supporting a correlative association rather than providing definitive evidence of causality. Future studies utilizing high-resolution 3D sequences, such as 3D T2-weighted, are warranted to confirm and refine these observations.

Second, this study analyzed the ALPS index only in the left hemisphere. Although this approach is consistent with that used in most prior studies in the field and facilitates comparison across cohorts, and although our cohort was predominantly right-handed, further research is needed to perform bilateral analyses to determine whether these findings are generalizable. In addition, while adult templates provide a standardized framework, they may not fully capture pediatric white matter anatomy and therefore introduce registration error. The development and application of age-specific templates will represent an important methodological advance in future studies. Likewise, the use of standardized, template-derived ROIs is itself a methodological constraint that may affect the precision of the ALPS index at the individual participant level.

Most importantly, the cross-sectional and correlative nature of our study precludes any causal inference regarding the observed associations among MRI markers, glymphatic function, and clinical measures. The mechanistic interpretations proposed here therefore remain speculative and require validation in longitudinal and interventional studies. In addition, because ALL risk stratification incorporated early treatment response and MRD, it should not be regarded as a purely baseline clinical variable. Accordingly, the observed association should be interpreted as a relationship between baseline MRI findings and subsequent composite clinical risk classification rather than as a purely cross-sectional association with baseline disease severity.

Conclusions

To conclude, this exploratory, cross-sectional MRI study identified associations between pediatric acute leukemia and a neuroimaging profile involving reduced brain volume and alterations in MRI markers putatively related to neurofluid dynamics. Within the ALL subgroup, a lower ALPS index was associated with higher disease risk classification and greater sleep disturbance. Collectively, these findings are hypothesis-generating and support future longitudinal studies to clarify the biological and clinical relevance of neurofluid-related imaging features in acute leukemia.
