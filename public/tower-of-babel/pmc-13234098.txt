Distinguishing Muscle Oxygen Saturation from Tissue Oxygen Saturation: Accuracy, Limitations, and Practical Utility in Muscle Oximetry
Authors: Andri Feldmann, Valentina Quaresima, Stephane Perrey, Marco Ferrari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Muscle near-infrared spectroscopy (mNIRS) is widely used to measure skeletal muscle oxygen saturation, named as muscle-specific oxygen saturation (SmO₂) or tissue oxygen saturation (StO₂). The distinction between SmO₂ and the less specific abbreviation StO₂ is often unclear and frequently conflated. This ambiguity persists in part because these commonly used terms do not explicitly reflect the layered, heterogeneous nature of the tissue beneath the probe, nor do they account for the tissue-optics assumptions required to derive absolute values. Two key factors differentiate SmO₂ from StO₂: the anatomical source of the optical signal and whether the value represents a true oxygen saturation or a derived index. To improve clarity, we propose the term regional SmO₂ (rSmO₂) to emphasize that mNIRS captures a spatially localized, muscle-weighted estimate influenced by instrumental factors, probe geometry, superficial tissues attenuation, and photon migration model, rather than whole-muscle oxygenation. A practical approach for determining whether a device captures a primarily muscle-specific rSmO₂ signal or a more mixed-tissue StO₂ signal is the arterial occlusion method. Muscle-dominant signals display a broad dynamic range, with nadirs of 20% and hyperemic peaks near 80%, whereas signals more strongly influenced by skin pigmentation and adipose tissue thickness show markedly attenuated responses. Although no commercial system can completely isolate muscle tissue, mNIRS remains a valuable tool for evaluating regional muscle oxygenation. Validated instrumentations and detailed protocols should be utilized, always recognizing the potential attenuation from superficial tissues.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40798-026-01033-w.

Introduction

In a 2019 publication, the American physiologist Thomas Jackson Barstow proposed a standard muscle near-infrared spectroscopy (mNIRS) nomenclature [1]. He recognized a range of terms used to identify a set of standard mNIRS variables and correctly pointed out that some were redundant and, at times, obscuring. We applaud this effort and agree that standardizing mNIRS terminology would help expand the use of muscle oximetry. Similar to the recent effort for standardization in functional NIRS terminology [2], an effort could also be made to standardize mNIRS. Publications involving mNIRS are accumulating rapidly, as seen in our recent updated review of the application of mNIRS in sports science [3]. However, standardization of nomenclature alone is insufficient if the underlying physiological and tissue-optics assumptions are not made explicit—most notably the widespread technically applied assumption that tissue is optically homogeneous beneath the mNIRS probe [4]. However, while we advocate using a nomenclature like the one provided by Barstow or a similar common standard, we would like to propose an additional distinction. The term tissue O2 saturation, namely StO2, is traditionally used as an umbrella term for a quantitative measure of tissue oxygenation expressed as a percentage—derived from oxygenated hemoglobin (Hb) and myoglobin (Mb) (oxy[heme]) and deoxygenated Hb and Mb (deoxy[heme]) divided by total Hb and Mb (total[heme]). A list of the measurable parameters (and the corresponding abbreviations) of the commercial mNIRS oximeters is reported in Table S1 of the Supplementary Material of the present study; see a recent review by Perrey et al. [3]. The mNIRS oximeters utilize different related variables like tissue oxygenation index (TOI; Hamamatsu Photonics), tissue saturation index (TSI; Artinis), muscle O2 saturation (SmO2; Fortiori Design, NNOXX Inc., OBELAB, and Train.Red), or, in cerebral oximeters, regional O2 saturation (rSO2; Masimo, and Nonin) and StO2 (ISS Inc.). Confusion surrounding this list and its implementation in research has also been recently raised [5]. None of the above-reported abbreviations is a trademark or unique to the company.

Two important distinctions are present when compiling these variables, which should be clarified, and the terminology should be separated. The first is the site of measurement. Tissue is again an umbrella term for groups of structurally and functionally similar cells that work together to perform a specific motor task. Regarding mNIRS, StO2 relates to the sum of tissues beneath the mNIRS device, which measures only a small volume of tissue. The volume (referred to as the optical field) varies across different mNIRS probes and is about 2–6 cm [6]. This differs significantly from the specification of muscle tissue, which implies that the measurement is taken from a specific tissue source, namely, a portion of muscle tissue. Thus, lumping the generic term “tissue” with “muscle tissue” seems inappropriate. This understanding is also adopted in the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 80601-2-85:202, where it is stated that “measurements are based upon light interacting with all tissue under the probe to determine StO2” [7]. Crucially, “all tissue under the probe” is layered [skin, subcutaneous adipose tissue (AT), muscle] and heterogeneous in both absorption and reduced scattering coefficient (μ′s), and these layers contribute differently as source–detector distance changes [4]. The second distinction is between an index and a saturation. An index is typically a derived value that reflects a biological condition or process on a relative or comparative scale, often combining multiple variables into a single indicator.

In contrast, saturation refers to the extent to which a biological component is wholly occupied or bound, usually expressed as a percentage. While an index is a scaled or composite measure, saturation indicates a direct, absolute level of occupancy or binding, making their purposes and interpretations fundamentally different. These two distinctions are independent of the actual validity of an mNIRS measure and are purely linked to linguistic coherence. Therefore, we recommend separating the StO2 grouping according to the site of measurement (tissue layer investigation) and the quantitative metric used to interpret the signal. In practice, this means explicitly acknowledging which layers dominate the signal for a given probe geometry and algorithm, rather than implying a single, homogeneous “tissue” compartment.

Building on this, we propose introducing the term regional SmO₂ (rSmO₂) to emphasize the spatially localized nature of mNIRS-derived signals. The prefix “r” (for regional) aligns this terminology with the widely used and U.S. Food and Drug Administration-approved rSO₂ abbreviation in cerebral oximetry [8], extending the same logic to muscle by adding “m” to denote muscle. This distinction clarifies that mNIRS does not measure whole-muscle oxygenation, but rather a regional average within the illuminated tissue volume, thereby improving both linguistic and physiological precision. To preserve physiological meaning, rSmO₂ reports should, wherever possible, specify probe geometry (source–detector spacings), whether short-separation regression or measurement was used to account for superficial layers, and any assumed μ′s(λ) model or calibration, because these choices govern the relative layer weighting of the regional signal, and should include any applied corrections or modeling approaches [e.g., multi-distance fitting, two-layer modeling, or AT thickness (ATT) adjustment] that further define how tissue layers were isolated or weighted in the final estimate. Therefore, Barstow’s nomenclature [1], or any attempt at a standard mNIRS nomenclature, should separate the term based on measurement site and quantitative descriptors.

Making these distinctions raises many fundamental questions. What does this mean for understanding mNIRS and its applications? Can rSmO2 be measured, given the requirements stipulated above? A practical corollary is a “do-not-assume-homogeneity” principle: when continuous-wave (CW) spatially resolved spectroscopy (SRS) is used to estimate absolute StO₂/SmO₂, investigators should either (i) mitigate superficial contamination via multiple short- and long-separation channels (and, where available, appropriate multi-layer or time-resolved modeling), or (ii) qualify the metric as a layer-weighted index rather than an absolute saturation of the deeper muscle. Similar depth-sensitivity and layering issues arise, to varying degrees, in frequency-domain (FD) and time-domain (TD) NIRS systems; without targeted methodological strategies to confront these issues, inter-individual variability in adipose thickness, skin pigmentation, or μ′s(λ) can masquerade as physiological differences in muscle oxygenation.

When using mNIRS to measure oxygenation dynamics, a probe is placed on the skin surface, and NIR light is scattered into the tissue via an emitter. Then, the emerging light is measured by a detector spaced apart from the emitter. The interaction between the light and the chromophores is how an oxygenation measurement is performed. Different mNIRS approaches aim to refine this process in various ways [9–11]. SRS NIRS, which operates within the CW framework, uses detectors at multiple distances from the light source to assess how attenuation changes with depth. CW SRS enables the estimation of absolute tissue oxygenation (TOI, TSI, StO2, or SmO2) while reducing reliance on scattering assumptions. Recent analyses of CW SRS further sharpen the point of tissue heterogeneity [4]. First, SRS typically assumes a simple spectral model for μ′s (λ) (often linear/power-law); mis-specifying its wavelength dependence can bias StO2 by ~ ± 10%, especially when μ′s changes are unequal across wavelengths. Second—and more importantly for mNIRS—the presence of a superficial layer produces significant StO2 errors (> 20%) once superficial thickness exceeds ~ 5 mm, regardless of whether the physiological change occurs superficially or in the deeper muscle [4]. These effects are not merely “instrument” problems, but arise from photon migration in layered, heterogeneous media and from the implicit pathlength the model assigns to each layer. Accordingly, many issues commonly framed as “pathlength problems” in CW NIRS are, at their core, layering problems: the effective differential pathlength is layer-weighted and model-dependent. Hence, assuming homogeneity folds anatomical variability directly into the oxygenation estimate [4]. TD NIRS sends short light pulses into tissue and measures the time for photons to travel through the tissue to quantify absorption and scattering separately, thereby improving depth resolution and accuracy [10]. By resolving photon time-of-flight, TD NIRS also attempts to reduce the influence of superficial layers by emphasizing later-arriving, deeper-penetrating photons, thereby improving but not completely isolating deeper tissue signals—similar in principle to the FD approach. FD NIRS utilizes high-frequency modulated light to analyze changes in amplitude and phase, thereby quantifying absorption and scattering separately [11]. The influence of a superficial layer in the quantitative spectroscopic study of strongly scattering media using FD NIRS has been carefully investigated since the 1990 s [12]. Thus, although TD and FD reduce some of the modeling constraints inherent to CW, both remain subject to the same fundamental heterogeneity issue: photon sampling of layered tissue inevitably mixes superficial and deep contributions unless that structure is explicitly modeled or experimentally constrained.

Understanding this mNIRS technology, numerous factors, outlined below, play a role in this process and must be accounted for when applying the suggested terminology correctly. How rSmO2 and StO2 are distinct is the resulting combination of signal contribution and O2 saturation at different levels of analysis. In sum, the conceptual move from undifferentiated “StO2” to regional, layer-aware rSmO2 reframes both nomenclature and methodology: it aligns language with physics, clarifies what is being measured, and highlights that the principal limitation of NIRS in muscles is not just scattering per se, but the layered, heterogeneous nature of biological tissue.

Adipose Tissue Influence

AT, as the primary and most significant factor influencing mNIRS signal accuracy, has been extensively documented [13, 14]. The site of the measurement tissue layering is crucial. For a rSmO₂ measure to be valid, it must distinguish between and separate non-muscle tissue layers, namely skin and AT. In contrast, a generic StO₂ measure may treat the tissue as a homogeneous mass, potentially blending the different layers to varying degrees. ATT should be measured before mNIRS assessments, with A-mode ultrasound recommended as the preferred method, as it can provide accurate and reliable estimates of both adipose and muscle layer thickness [15]. In general, mNIRS should be used with caution on subjects with an ATT higher than 15 mm using CW NIRS [16, 17], 0.8–1 cm using multi-distance FD NIRS [18, 19], and 2.5 cm using TD NIRS [20]. Although Barstow [1] recommended determining ATT to ensure mNIRS can measure muscle oxygenation, many mNIRS studies, unfortunately, omitted reporting ATT [3].

ATT attenuates muscle signal strength, leading to an underestimation of muscle oxygenation, particularly in individuals with higher ATT values. Nasseri et al. [14] demonstrated that when ATT equals the oximeter’s penetration depth, sensitivity drops by ~ 50%. Even the best-performing FD device was affected, leading to overestimation of oxygenation and reduced sensitivity to deoxygenation. Similarly, FD studies in vivo have shown that ATT affects both absolute accuracy and measurement reliability: Celie et al. [18] reported that ATT modulates FD-derived oxygenation values, and Stuer et al. [19] found that thicker ATT reduces signal reliability and attenuates the physiological dynamic range. Using CW SRS, Niemeijer et al. [21] reinforced this with a phantom blood study, showing that as ATT increased, StO₂ readings became increasingly inaccurate, overestimating both oxygenated and deoxygenated states. These findings highlight a fundamental limitation across NIRS modalities: as ATT increases, measurement accuracy declines, regardless of whether CW, FD, or TD techniques are used. While FD and TD systems offer improved depth sensitivity through phase shifts and time-of-flight measurements, respectively, they are still partially influenced by superficial tissue layers [4, 14, 22].TD in particular has demonstrated superior performance in resolving deep tissue signals and isolating hemodynamic changes at specific depths, making it the most promising approach for mitigating AT interference when available [10]. The differential path length factor (DPF), which affects the distance light travels in tissue, is influenced by ATT; however, the direction and magnitude of this effect depend on the combined absorption and scattering of the layered tissues, as well as on the measurement configuration [23].

In vivo, DPF increased with ATT in the vastus lateralis muscle and showed wavelength dependence (lower at wavelengths greater than 800 nm). In contrast, the arm muscle data showed little change over a narrow range of ATT. Overall, the relationships were nonlinear and muscle-specific [24]. Importantly, whether AT uniformly increases light scattering relative to muscle remains unestablished, and there is currently no consensus or validated correction factor to adjust CW NIRS for ATT across individuals or NIRS devices. Moreover, exercise-induced changes can dynamically alter DPF, suggesting the need for personalized, time-dependent adjustments to DPF where possible [25]. Changes in DPF during the arterial occlusion method (AOM) have also been documented [26].

ATT poses many practical challenges, for example, in estimating muscle O₂ consumption (mV̇O₂) and blood flow using mNIRS. Van Beekvelt et al. [27] found a strong negative correlation (r = −0.70) between ATT and mV̇O₂. Subjects with the lowest ATT had mV̇O₂ values twice as high as those with the highest. ATT also showed a weak correlation with muscle blood flow (r = 0.29), which complicates the broader use of mNIRS in exercise settings. ATT further confounds training applications of StO₂ or rSmO₂. Van der Zwaard et al. [28] noted that although CW SRS NIRS is reliable, it is significantly affected by ATT, which determined asymmetric changes in NIRS signals. FD NIRS confirmed that the relationship between ATT and NIRS signal differs across muscles, reinforcing that ATT's influence is muscle-specific and necessitates site-specific calibration [13].

Recent work supports this interpretation. Shoemaker et al. [29] reported that although NIRS variables during reactive hyperemia were generally reliable, reliability markedly decreased in women—a finding the authors attributed to greater ATT. Perlet et al. [30] demonstrated that ATT was the strongest predictor of both desaturation magnitude and desaturation rate during forearm ischemia (r ≈ −0.62 and r ≈ 0.62), outperforming sex, age, and lean mass. Similarly, Chatlaong et al. [31] found that apparent sex differences in reperfusion slope disappeared when ATT (and desaturation rate) were included as covariates, indicating that ATT—not sex—is the primary source of variability in NIRS-derived hyperemia metrics.

Beyond its impact on light scattering and attenuation, AT itself exhibits distinct hemodynamic and metabolic characteristics that meaningfully influence mNIRS-derived signals. Unlike skeletal muscle, subcutaneous AT contains minimal Mb and is therefore dominated by Hb absorption, making Hb the principal chromophore contributing to the optical signal [32, 33]. AT blood flow is closely linked to local metabolic demand—relatively high at rest but markedly reduced during exercise due to sympathetic vasoconstriction and redistribution of blood flow toward active muscle [34]. Consequently, AT often displays dampened or delayed hemodynamic changes compared with muscle, reflecting its lower vascular density and slower oxygen exchange kinetics. Moreover, its high scattering properties can attenuate or mask subtle Hb variations, reducing the apparent sensitivity of mNIRS to rapid metabolic events in deeper layers [33].

Importantly, the contribution of AT to the composite mNIRS signal should not be viewed solely as a confounding factor but as a meaningful physiological component whose relevance depends on the measurement objective. For studies targeting mV̇O₂ or local muscle blood flow, a strong AT signal can obscure muscle-specific dynamics, limiting quantitative accuracy. However, if the research aim includes investigating subcutaneous AT metabolism, perfusion, or microvascular regulation, these same optical contributions become informative rather than problematic. In this sense, the fraction of the signal arising from AT represents not a limitation but a reflection of what the instrument is truly sampling. Recognizing and quantifying this layered contribution—through anatomical assessment or multi-distance NIRS approaches—can thus transform what might appear as “measurement contamination” into an opportunity to study AT physiology in parallel with muscle function.

Skin Tissue Influence

Skin blood flow can affect O2 saturation measurements, particularly under conditions that promote cutaneous vasodilation. Despite no change in muscle blood flow, local and whole-body heating substantially increase NIRS-derived oxygenation signals, indicating a strong superficial signal component. These changes correlate closely with increased skin blood flow, suggesting that cutaneous circulation can heavily influence mNIRS signals [35]. This issue is further complicated by probe geometry. Short source–detector distances (e.g., 2–2.5 cm) heighten sensitivity to superficial tissues, whereas greater distances enable deeper tissue interrogation and reduce the influence of skin tissue signals [36]. At rest, comparative studies of CW SRS and TD NIRS show that superficial skin blood flow significantly alters CW SRS NIRS oxygenation readings more than it does TD NIRS, which is more depth sensitive [37]. Skin blood flow can differentially affect rSmO2 and total Hb volume measures even during exercise. At rest, cutaneous vasodilation increases NIRS-derived oxygenation, but its impact during dynamic leg exercise is smaller and more variable [38]. These findings underscore the importance of accounting for skin perfusion when interpreting mNIRS data, particularly those obtained by CW NIRS.

Myoglobin’s Role

Mb is a protein closely related to Hb, containing a heme prosthetic group that enables reversible O2 binding. Due to its higher O2 affinity than Hb, Mb plays a crucial role in O2 storage, buffering intracellular O2 concentrations, and facilitating O2 diffusion [39]. Its primary function is to modulate O2 delivery during muscle contraction, supporting sustained energy metabolism. In the context of mNIRS, the precise contribution of Mb to the signal remains a topic of debate. Early studies suggested Hb was the dominant source, with Mb accounting for around 10% of the signal [40, 41]. More recent findings, however, measuring 1H NMR, 31P NMR, and NIRS signals in human finger flexor muscles during transitions from rest to muscle contraction, recovery, ischemia, and reperfusion, indicate that the NIRS signal from muscle is predominantly due to Mb [42].

The key to interpreting mNIRS data is to differentiate between the dynamic and static components of the total heme signal. Unlike Hb, which fluctuates with changes in blood flow and volume, Mb concentration remains constant during both rest and exercise. As such, Mb does not dynamically affect the total[heme] signal but contributes meaningfully to the oxygenation signal, particularly under low O2 availability. Mb’s high O2 affinity causes it to desaturate more slowly when O2 is abundant but more rapidly under hypoxic conditions, such as during intense muscle work, thereby increasing its relative contribution to the mNIRS oxygenation signal during these states [43, 44]. During exercise, while the total[heme] signal can rise by up to 30% primarily due to increased microvascular hematocrit, Mb’s relative contribution tends to decrease, from about 70% at rest to 50% at peak exertion [45–47]. The implications of Mb’s contribution are especially relevant in distinguishing between rSmO2 and StO2. Because Mb, localized in muscle tissue, disproportionately affects rSmO₂, whereas StO₂ includes Hb-dominant signals from other tissues such as skin and adipose. Although the exact percentage of Mb’s contribution to the mNIRS signal remains under investigation, accumulating evidence supports its substantial role—exceeding 60%—in shaping oxygenation measurements [42, 45]. This contrasts with earlier interpretations that attributed the mNIRS signal predominantly to Hb, with minimal influence from Mb. Understanding Mb's contribution, using combined NMR and NIRS measurements [42, 48–50], is essential for accurately interpreting mNIRS data, particularly in differentiating rSmO2 and StO2 signals.

Vascular Contribution

Interest in mNIRS largely stems from questions of muscle metabolism. So, the interest is in mV̇O₂ rather than macro hemodynamics, which can be measured effectively via alternative sources (e.g., Doppler ultrasonography). Therefore, knowing at what circulation level the signal is being traced is relevant. What is the contribution of macro and microvasculature from arteries, veins, and capillaries? A consensus has been reached that the mNIRS signal arises almost exclusively from small blood vessels at the mesoscopic scale, specifically those less than 200 µm in diameter, the microvasculature, arterioles, venules, and capillaries [51].

This notion arises from the recognition that the light-absorbing potential in larger vessels with high concentrations of heme groups is so great that all transmitted light is absorbed. It should be noted that contrary positions have been argued; CW SRS NIRS data have been reported, in which, depending on the probe placement, larger vessels can contribute significantly to the signal [52]. When examining the microvasculature, there is a discussion about the proportion of the signal that originates from arterioles, venules, and capillaries. A general approach has been a simple one-third approach for each compartment [53]. Another approach is that the venous compartment constitutes the majority of the mNIRS–visible microvasculature [54, 55]. This proposal originates from the anatomy of cerebral circulation, where cerebral venous volume has been reported to account for 70–80% of the vascular volume [56, 57] and more than 80% of the cerebral oxygenation signal [58]. However, empirical data on the relative contributions of arterioles, capillaries, and venules to the skeletal muscle microvascular network indicate that capillaries comprise approximately 80–90% of the microvascular volume, while arterioles and venules each account for only 5–10% [59]. A recent computational analysis by Koirala et al. [43] revealed a 10% to 80% to 10% split between arterioles, capillaries, and venules at rest.

Skin Pigmentation

Melanin in the skin absorbs NIR light, which can interfere with mNIRS signals by reducing the light reaching deeper tissues such as muscle. This absorption can distort StO2 measurements, particularly in individuals with darker skin tones who have significantly larger and more concentrated melanosomes, raising concerns about the accuracy and equity of NIRS-based monitoring. In a commentary on this issue, Quaresima et al. [60] emphasized that NIRS-based brain oximeters may be biased against individuals with higher melanin content. They called for regulatory bodies to require testing across diverse skin tones and to disclose performance limitations. In a study by Couch et al. [61], muscle StO2 was measured on the lower leg and forearm using various CW NIRS devices in participants with varying levels of skin pigmentation. Results showed a significant (small to medium effect size) negative correlation between skin pigmentation and StO2 in some devices (e.g., r = −0.37, p = 0.008), whereas others showed no correlation. These findings suggest that some mNIRS systems may be affected by skin tone, but the clinical impact is likely specific to the technology and context [61]. Similarly, Wassenaar and Brand [62] found that in a sample of 17 dark-skinned volunteers undergoing isometric leg muscle contraction, the NIRS device failed to record StO2 values in over half the participants. This suggests a real-world functional limitation in some systems when used in individuals with high melanin content.

In contrast, Lacerenza et al. [63] demonstrated that TD NIRS is unaffected mainly by skin pigmentation. In phantom and in vivo pediatric measurements, StO2 values varied less than 2% across all skin tones, with deviations of only ± 1.5% in controlled conditions. Even under dynamic challenges like AOM, skin tone has a negligible influence on measurements, unlike pulse oximetry, where errors of 3–6% are common in individuals with darker skin [64]. While some NIRS devices—especially CW SRS systems—are susceptible to melanin interference, newer technologies like TD NIRS show strong potential to provide accurate, unbiased tissue oxygenation data across various skin tones, thereby supporting more equitable outcomes. To ensure accuracy in mNIRS research, studies should systematically assess and report participants' skin pigmentation. Objective, quantitative measures of skin tone enable a more accurate evaluation of potential melanin-related biases and cross-study comparability. One such approach is the use of the individual topology angle (ITA), which provides a standardized and reproducible measure of skin pigmentation [65]. The ITA can be obtained using devices such as the SkinColorCatch (Delfin, Miami, FL), a validated, noninvasive handheld skin colorimeter compliant with U.S. Food and Drug Administration guidance [66].

Physiological Calibration

To address the difficulties of muscle oxygenation assessment, especially the challenge of quantifying oxygenation using mNIRS, an effective method that has been extensively applied since 1989 is a physiological calibration [5, 55, 67, 68] using AOM followed by the post-occlusive reactive hyperemia (PORH). Using an AOM and a PORH to calibrate the oxy[heme] and deoxy[heme] signals to 0% and 100% effectively normalizes individual mNIRS data when arbitrary units or optical density are used. The same can be done using rSmO2 or StO2 values expressed in %, where you set the minimum rSmO2 or StO2 result to 0% and the maximum result, corresponding to the PORH maximal value, to 100%. However, this prompts the question of the validity of the % scale provided by StO2 and rSmO2 in physiological reality. The calibration would imply that StO2 and/or rSmO2 at their lowest end are truly 0% saturation and at their highest end are 100% saturation. Is this correct? Numerous studies have evaluated physiological responses to prolonged occlusion (≥ 5 min) and conclude that, although the tissue has been substantially deoxygenated, not all the O2 has been consumed by the tissue [69, 70]. The suggestion is that rSmO2 or StO2 should not be expected to reach 0% at the end of an extended but limited occlusion as used for calibration in practice (i.e., 5–6 min [68]). The individualized cuff pressure should be at least 50 mmHg above the systemic blood pressure to ensure a complete arterial occlusion, considering that restrictive cuff pressures should be primarily based on thigh/arm circumference [71]. For the thigh, a recent review suggests a cuff pressure of ≈220 mmHg [72].

Longer occlusions may increase the depth of desaturation and the magnitude of PORH. When applying the AOM, a rapid and complete cuff occlusion is recommended to minimize partial-flow intervals. The subsequent PORH response should be anticipated and considered during analysis. In addition to a physiological calibration, it is also valuable to establish a stable resting baseline—typically after discarding the first 90 s of data—to assess oxygenation relative to steady-state conditions [73]. Such baselines provide a physiologically relevant reference that can complement the AOM.

To further ensure that mNIRS signals reflect proper muscle oxygenation rather than composite tissue effects, a brief muscle function test can be incorporated into the calibration procedure. Such a test—using a transient isometric maximal voluntary contraction, standardized submaximal exercise, performed in an open system or under occlusion conditions—provides a controlled physiological perturbation that should elicit an apparent, time-locked decrease and recovery in rSmO₂ if the probe is sampling muscle tissue [74]. Under occlusion, a localized contraction should accelerate the rSmO₂ decline and delay the post-exercise reoxygenation [17]. These expected kinetics help to differentiate muscle-derived rSmO₂ from mixed-layer StO₂ signals, offering a practical means to verify muscle specificity and regional sensitivity (rSmO₂) across devices and protocols. Recent work has further emphasized that the interpretability of mNIRS signals is intrinsically linked to their behavior under controlled functional perturbation, reinforcing the value of brief muscle activation as a complementary lens through which tissue origin and physiological relevance may be inferred [75]. Including a functional measure, therefore, strengthens the interpretability and reliability of mNIRS calibration and subsequent data analysis. For an mNIRS user applying the straightforward and cost-effective AOM to understand their mNIRS device, what kind of signal response should one expect from StO₂? Or from rSmO₂? To answer this question, we can examine available data on the oxygenation ranges of muscles and tissues in relation to the distinctions outlined above.

Expected mNIRS Signal Response and Muscle Oxygenation

There is a general consensus that trending information tends to align when evaluating physiological calibrations, such as the AOM, both between and within subjects and across devices [53]. Nevertheless, while these temporal patterns are often consistent, the absolute magnitude of change can vary considerably between instruments and measurement techniques. During occlusion tests, some systems exhibit large dynamic ranges, whereas others show only modest variations [53, 76], reflecting differences in device design, algorithms, and tissue sampling depth. In general, the interaction between light and biological tissue behaves as expected. The most straightforward way to assess differences in mNIRS signals remains the evaluation of the nadir and zenith points—the minimum and maximum values reached during the testing procedure. This is then also normalized against 0% and 100%. Therefore, when evaluating the realness of a StO2 or rSmO2 signal, can we generate reasonable expectations for a minimum and maximum value based on the existing literature on O2 transport and metabolism? An effective way of developing a picture for this is following the O2 cascade at rest: the partial pressure of O2 (pO2) decreases stepwise from a very high level in the arteries—~ 100 mmHg—to a lower level in the veins—~ 30 mmHg (see Fig. 1a) [77–87]. Tracking this cascade for each step can provide insight into the changing O2 saturation values that influence the mNIRS signal. This can be done for rest and extended occlusions to determine resting or maximum saturation values and minimum attainable oxygenation values (see Fig. 1b) [77-80; 84, 85; 88-96]. By understanding the O₂ cascade—from microcirculation to the myocyte—and how different components contribute to the mNIRS signal, based on the Hb/Mb dissociation curves, we can better interpret and set realistic expectations for rSmO₂ and StO₂ signals (see Fig. 2) [77-96]. This cascade-based framework also provides a physiological means of differentiating between homogeneous and heterogeneous tissue contributions. The resting O2 cascade across tissue is well documented, as sourced by the citations in Fig. 1a [77-87]. However, the same cannot be said for an O2 cascade influenced by an extensive occlusion state (see Fig. 1b) [77-80; 84, 85; 88-96]. In both cases, we have made assumptions based on existing literature (see Fig. 1a and b) [77-96]. The AT cascade results from the metabolic differentiation between muscle tissue and AT and has a concordant effect on pO2.

At the Mb-relevant level, which, in our estimation, constitutes a considerable portion of the mNIRS signal, obtaining accurate data is challenging. Lanza and colleagues [94] identified a critical pO2 at 0.35 ± 0.2 mmHg, at which point creatine phosphate began to decline. This phenomenon is observed in mNIRS research, where the mNIRS signal reaches its nadir following extensive occlusion [93]. It seems reasonable, therefore, that following an extensive occlusion, pO2 at the Mb-relevant level would be lower than 0.35 mmHg, or potentially much lower, given 31P NMR studies arguing that the entire oxygenated Mb signal is eliminated following occlusion [78, 97]. As mentioned earlier, recent studies have shown that even an extensive occlusion does not completely exhaust the muscle’s O2 supply [69, 70]. Kanick et al. [84], comparing subcutaneous hydrogel-based interstitial O₂ microsensors with a commercial transcutaneous oximetry device, showed that both sensor types detected similarly pronounced drops in subcutaneous tissue oxygenation during arterial occlusion, with pO₂ falling to very low levels consistent with substantial microvascular desaturation. Therefore, based on the current data, we assume that pO2 drops considerably to very low values following an extensive occlusion, but it is unlikely to reach zero in standard calibration protocols.

As discussed above, the sum of these expectations should result from an understanding of the O2 cascade during rest and occlusion, as well as the contributions of different tissue layers and components to the mNIRS signal. Based on the evidence provided in this opinion article, an expectation framework is presented, which will be reviewed and expanded upon. The aim is to address the two distinguishing features of rSmO2 and StO2 and to demonstrate how simple AOM testing, advanced and validated by muscle function tests and baseline evaluations, can inform users whether the device they are using is more or less likely to provide a signal relating to rSmO2 or general StO2.

Together, this conceptual framework underscores that the mNIRS signal—whether expressed as StO₂ or rSmO₂—represents a composite of layered tissue compartments rather than a homogeneous muscle volume. Differences in the relative contributions of muscle, adipose, and skin profoundly influence the measured oxygenation range and the apparent dynamic response during occlusion and reperfusion. By explicitly accounting for tissue heterogeneity, we can begin to interpret why devices or subjects with greater superficial adipose thickness display attenuated desaturation and smaller amplitude changes, even under identical physiological conditions. The proposed framework, therefore, serves as a guide for setting realistic expectation boundaries for mNIRS-derived rSmO₂ during an AOM.

Conclusion and Practical Implications

A measure of rSmO2, under the assumptions laid out in this paper, should exhibit a high dynamic range with low saturation values at the end of an AOM (5–6 min), specifically those at or below 20%. For the hyperemic response, as a normative marker, one should expect a high saturation value near 80%. Moving from this highly muscle-dominated signal to a more general tissue-diluted signal, one would expect the dynamic range to shrink, significantly affecting the minimum (or nadir) response. In the most extreme case, where no signal is measured from the muscle, the range is reduced to marginal changes from rest under the AOM condition.

From a practical standpoint, while FD and TD mNIRS systems offer more accurate and quantitative measurements, they are very expensive, less suited for routine or field applications, and have been used so far in only a few studies [20, 106]. On the other hand, CW NIRS instrumentations are far more accessible due to their low cost, portability, and ease of use. However, they come with limitations in terms of precision and depth resolution. Extreme caution is recommended when using all commercial CW NIRS oximeters with an ATT > 12–20 mm; the critical ATT is strictly dependent on the utilized mNIRS device. Employing a simple AOM can help better understand the practical, yet potentially limited, CW NIRS device. It is essential to recognize that a “pure” rSmO₂ signal—completely isolated from non-muscular tissue contributions—is currently not attainable with any commercial NIRS technology, as CW and even FD/TD systems are not tomographic. The detected light inevitably integrates information from skin, adipose, and muscle tissues, making rSmO₂ a physiologically weighted, muscle-dominant signal rather than an anatomically pure one. However, the absence of perfection should not preclude practical utility. In other words, perfection should not be the enemy of the good. By applying appropriate protocols (e.g., AOM, muscle function tests) and recognizing the expected physiological kinetics, it remains both feasible and scientifically defensible to interpret a signal as rSmO₂. mNIRS continues to provide valuable, functionally relevant insights into regional muscle O2 dynamics in a practical and noninvasive manner.
