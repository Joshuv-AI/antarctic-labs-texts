Hybrid robust beamforming for enhanced multiple moving object detection with phased array scanning radar
Authors: Sheikh Shafaet Islam, Md. Selim Hossain, Shariful Islam, Rakibul Hasan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The deficiencies of conventional radar systems can be addressed by utilizing a phased array scanning radar with robust beamforming capable of detecting and tracking multiple moving objects while rejecting interferences under noisier environments. This paper offers the idea of a 30 × 30 Uniform Rectangular Array (URA)-based radar system with the employment of a hybrid robust beamformer, a combination of Minimum Variance Distortionless Response (MVDR) and Linearly Constrained Minimum Variance (LCMV) beamformer with diagonal loading. Initially, a simulation work with experimental validation of a 1 × 16 Uniform Linear Array (ULA)-based radar system with a conventional beamformer is performed under a well-conditioned scenario, and it shows an overall accuracy of 89.80% for object detection; however, it is not feasible for the noisier condition. In order to enhance radar performance in challenging situations, a new hybrid robust beamforming model on a 30 × 30 URA-based phased array scanning radar system is used. Comparing the proposed radar system to conventional radar systems, the proposed phased array radar shows an overall accuracy of 99.29% for multiple object detection and interference rejection, according to the simulation results.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26071-4.

Introduction

Radar technology has long been an indispensable tool in various applications, ranging from weather monitoring and border security to marine surveillance, air traffic control, and military operations. Its ability to detect and track objects accurately over long distances and in challenging environments makes radar an essential component of modern surveillance systems. Among the many advancements in radar technology, phased array radar systems have emerged as a powerful solution due to their ability to electronically steer the beam without mechanically moving the antenna. This capability allows for improved gain, directivity, and performance in specific directions, making phased array radar particularly well-suited for scenarios requiring rapid and precise detection of multiple moving objects1.

In recent years, the need for robust and efficient radar systems capable of detecting multiple moving objects has become increasingly critical, especially in areas such as national security, environmental monitoring, and disaster response. Traditional radar systems—commonly referred to as non-adaptive, mechanically steered radars—are effective for detecting single targets but often struggle to identify and track multiple targets in environments with high interference due to their limited target discrimination capabilities. These challenges are further exacerbated in environments with high levels of noise and interference, where distinguishing between signals of interest and unwanted signals becomes more complex2,3. To address these challenges, beamforming techniques have been developed and refined to enhance the detection performance of phased array radar systems. It employs an array of antennas to optimize signal reception from specific directions while minimizing noise and interference4. By focusing on the primary lobes and nulls towards the interference signal of no interest (SONI), beamforming can selectively capture signals from desired directions and reject others5. Phased array radar systems present a promising solution for overcoming these challenges by scanning the horizon in an angular pattern without mechanical rotation, using voltage-dependent phase shifts in the antenna elements. While single target detection by radar is well-established, detecting multiple targets remains challenging. Phased array radar, combined with appropriate algorithms, holds potential for addressing this challenge more effectively6,7.

Despite substantial advancements in radar detection performance, there remains considerable scope for improvement. Various methods have been explored to enhance multiple target detection. For instance, the outlier rejection method was introduced in8 to handle multivariable data for target detection but struggled with moving targets and undefined azimuths. Improvements were made with the application of the weighted k-nearest neighbors (KNN) classifier proposed in9, but moving targets remained problematic. The antenna scanning method reported in10 could detect multiple targets in the same range-azimuth but faced limitations due to range azimuth resolution cells. The low-frequency ultra-wideband array-antenna synthetic aperture radar (SAR) was mentioned in11 for imaging both fixed and moving targets. Still, the moving target remained unobtainable to their system. The use of bistatic radar systems for the detection and positioning of several targets was proposed in12,13. Both literature12,13 prioritized multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) radar, whereas literature13 focused on synthetic aperture radar (SAR) that was forward-looking. By successfully detecting and recognizing numerous objects, these methods resolved the spatial synchronization problem in traditional bistatic radars. In literature14, the authors provided a revision of that concept by introducing a bistatic linear array synthetic aperture radar (BLA-SAR) system for imaging and localizing moving targets. However, the detection capabilities for vast amounts of objects degraded as a result of their methodologies, which increased economic costs and processing complexity. The narrowband radar method in15 detected high-speed targets but failed to correct range migration adequately. The ultra-wideband (UWB) antenna method as in16 offered high gain for multiple target detection but was unsuitable without a UWB directional antenna. The low-rank and sparsity-constrained method introduced in17 provided a real beam image for multiple target detection but suffered from poor azimuth resolution due to antenna size limitations. Additionally, techniques requiring multiple radars as suggested in18 were impractical due to their feasibility constraints. MIMO radar reported in19 examined target correlation and detection sensitivity but struggled with new or nearby tracks deviating from assumptions of targets and clutter statistics. Cognitive beamforming techniques in20 for co-located MIMO radars based on Reinforcement Learning (RL) faced performance declines with rising correlation levels. Radar systems with antenna allocation methods in21 and two-element rotating antenna strategies in22 showed potential but had limitations in angle estimation and resolution. In literature23, the Rotman lens beamforming technique was utilized to ascertain the position of an object by analyzing the phasor disparity. However, it was able to differentiate between targets that were equidistant but not at a different angle.

Recent studies have explored diverse strategies to enhance radar performance using intelligent and adaptive technologies. In literature24, reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RIS) were used to improve radar detection, though the analysis was limited to basic setups, lacking multi-target or complex interference considerations. The displaced phase center antenna (DPCA)-based pulse-to-pulse subtraction method proposed in25 effectively suppressed clutter from platform motion, enhancing slow-moving target detection; however, the optimal space-time adaptive processing (STAP)-based techniques required intensive computation and reliable training data, which is scarce in airborne settings. A reduced-dimension STAP framework introduced in26 lowered computational complexity while preserving detection accuracy, though its success hinged on accurate subspace estimation under ideal assumptions. The intelligent reflecting surface (IRS)-assisted MIMO radar proposed in27 showed performance gains in non-line-of-sight conditions, but the semidefinite relaxation and alternating optimization techniques posed real-time implementation challenges. The decomposition-based resource and waveform management scheme in28 achieved efficient multi-node coordination yet relied on perfect synchronization and target state accuracy, which are difficult in practice. In literature29, an intelligent metasurface antenna with reconfigurable beams using hybrid optimization was presented, although its control complexity and circuit demands could hinder scalability for larger systems. The tapered or banded shrinkage covariance (TABASCO) matrix, introduced in30, combined shrinkage towards a scaled identity matrix with tapering to enhance covariance estimation in high-dimensional settings with low sample-to-dimension ratios. Optimal regularization parameter derivation and empirical validation demonstrated its effectiveness, including in STAP for radar systems, though it remains computationally intensive, assumes elliptically symmetric data, and is sensitive to bandwidth selection. In literature31, Constant False Alarm Rate (CFAR) detection methods based on banded sample autocovariance matrices (SACM) achieved superior detection performance and computational efficiency over conventional approaches, with the Maximum Eigenvalue Trimmed (MET) detector offering adaptability without requiring non-target reference data. However, the theoretical complexity of deriving SACM eigenvalue distributions limits practical implementation. A random slow-time coding method based on Signal-to-Interference-Plus-Noise Ratio (SINR) maximization was proposed in32 to mitigate ghost target interference in autonomous driving, demonstrating improved detection but at the cost of a higher noise floor, potentially obscuring weak targets. In literature33, a high-resolution imaging method for high-speed maneuvering targets employed fast sparse Bayesian learning (SBL) and minimum entropy algorithms, leveraging fast Fourier transform (FFT)-based decomposition to reduce matrix inversion costs. While computationally efficient, its performance degraded at low SNRs. Furthermore, literature34 proposed a Frequency Diverse Array–Multiple-Input Multiple-Output (FDA-MIMO) detection technique integrating training data and prior covariance information via Bayesian methods, reducing dependency between transmission snapshots and signal dimensions. This approach, however, relied heavily on accurate prior information and adequate training data. Despite significant progress in this area, existing methods often struggle with issues such as poor resolution, high computational complexity, and limited effectiveness in real-world scenarios involving multiple moving targets.

The blue lines visualize the raw input signal flow from the rectangular array to the weighting network. The orange lines denote the feedback connection to the weight estimator, which is responsible for determining the optimal complex weights. These weights are computed based on the desired signal direction and possibly using adaptive or robust algorithms to maximize target gain while suppressing interference. The red lines indicate the application of computed weights to the received signals. Finally, the green lines represent the summation stage, where all weighted signals are coherently combined to produce the beamformer output. This output maximizes gain in the direction of interest while minimizing contributions from noise and undesired directions.

Beamforming techniques

Beamforming is a signal processing technique used in sensor arrays for directional signal transmission or reception. According to the effectiveness, beamforming techniques can be classified into three parts – conventional beamforming, adaptive beamforming, and robust beamforming35.

Conventional beamformer

In a conventional (or classical) beamformer, also known as a delay-and-sum beamformer, the weight vector is designed to steer the array in the direction of the desired signal without considering interference or noise explicitly. The primary goal is to align the phases of the signals received at different array elements to enhance the desired signal’s direction. For a conventional beamformer, the weight vector is chosen to match the steering vector of the desired signal. This ensures that the array is steered in the direction of the desired signal35.

Adaptive beamformer

An adaptive beamformer is a beamforming approach that dynamically modifies the weights of array elements to focus the beam toward a specified direction while reducing interference from other angles. The adaptation is generally predicated on a cost function, such as minimizing output power or maximizing the SINR37.

Robust beamformer

A robust beamformer is engineered to sustain its performance despite uncertainties or mismatches in the system model, including direction of arrival (DOA) errors, calibration inaccuracies, or variations in signal statistics. The objective of robust beamforming is to mitigate the impact of these faults to attain enhanced reliability in performance relative to conventional adaptive beamformers. Robust beamforming techniques generally adjust or enhance adaptive beamforming algorithms to address these uncertainties42.

The proposed hybrid robust beamformer is constructed by integrating the strengths of the diagonal loading MVDR beamformer and diagonal loading LCMV beamformer. Diagonal loading is incorporated into both methods to address mismatches and enhance resilience against faults, including inaccurate DOA data and array deficiencies. The diagonal loading MVDR is employed to obtain the highest gain at the DOA and the diagonal loading LCMV to obtain the deepest null at the interference. Optimization technique

In order to obtain the optimal weights for both diagonal loading MVDR and diagonal loading LCMV beamformer, we must select the optimal diagonal loading factor by which desired robustness can be achieved. In this research, the condition number-based method is employed for the selection of the diagonal loading factor dynamically.

Modeling of the radar system

The schematic diagram integrating transmission and reception of the radar system is demonstrated in Fig. 6. The system’s phased array radar is set as a monostatic radar so that signals can be transmitted and received via the same antenna array.

Fig. 6Schematic block of the phased array radar system.

Schematic block of the phased array radar system.

The waveform generator generates the radar signal first; next, the power amplifier amplifies it in order to assure that the transmitted signal has the required strength to reach the appropriate detection range and resolution. Once this step is completed, the radar signal gets transmitted to the propagation channel using a phased array antenna (PAA) with the requisite gain capability. Upon reaching the cross-section of the target within the propagation channel, the reflected signal is reabsorbed by the antenna array, thereby being collected by it. Moreover, the duplexer enables the receiver to obtain the reflected signal, facilitating further operations. Subsequently, an analog-to-digital converter is employed to sample the analog signal at discrete intervals, converting these sampled signals into a digital representation that can be further processed. The next step is to steer the beam in a given target azimuth and create nulls for interference by applying optimal beamforming techniques to the signals received by each antenna in the array. Improvements in range resolution, target differentiation, and clutter management are achieved by the utilization of pulse synthesis and matched filtering. To make the radar system practical, the pulse integration technique is employed in the matched filtering section to reduce the transmitted power required. Following the modification of the given pulse, a comparison is made with the signal that is being received. A method known as Doppler processing is applied to moving objects in order to provide the main computer with information regarding their velocity and range. After the detector has precisely counted the number of moving objects within the main computer, the tracking and parameter estimate of objects will come to an end. When this stage is completed, threshold selection is carried out in order to reduce the number of false alarms and improve the detection probability, even in an environment that is noisy. A visual representation of the outcome is created, and it is recorded for use in the future as a radar dataset. Radar signal processing

Radar signal processing involves pulse synthesis, matched filtering, pulse integration, detection threshold selection, and Doppler processing.

Experimental setup

The experimental setup, shown in Fig. 8, is organized according to the system visualization as discussed elaborately in Section ‘Experimentation Process’. The moving target indication (MTI) processor is used here for the detection of moving targets. The phased array antenna controller is employed here for the beam steering control of the PAA. It enables the three scan modes of manual, continuous, and PRF locked (depending on the radar PRF) to be operated with the PAA. In order to facilitate further processing and display, the Dual-channel sampler expands the time of the radar receiver’s I (in-phase) and Q (quadrature) channel baseband signals. Quadrature modulation and demodulation are performed in it. The radar transmitter with complex modulation can generate waveforms utilizing I/Q signals that are visualized on the oscilloscope display. It is also possible to extract the target’s Doppler information from complex data derived from demodulated I/Q signals. The range, azimuth angle, and speed of the multiple targets can be estimated from the radar display.

RCS validation via physical optics approximation

To validate the assumed 1 m² RCS of the half-cylinder target used in this study, a simulation is performed using a Physical Optics (PO)-based approximation in MATLAB53 software. The monostatic RCS is approximated using the PO-based method, mathematically expressed as1,56:99\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{HC}=\left(4\pi\:{r}_{HC}^{2}\bullet\:\frac{1}{{N}_{s}}\sum\:_{i=1}^{{N}_{s}}{\left|\widehat{k}\bullet\:{\widehat{n}}_{i}\right|}^{4}\right)\bullet\:\frac{{h}_{HC}}{\lambda\:}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{N}_{s}$$\end{document} represents the number of surface discretization points along the arc and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{k}$$\end{document} represents the unit vector in the direction of wave incidence. This equation estimates the RCS of a half-cylinder using a simplified PO approximation. The term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\left|\widehat{k}\bullet\:{\widehat{n}}_{i}\right|}^{2}$$\end{document} represents the cosine of the angle between the incident wave and surface normal, which is a common factor in PO-based RCS models and squaring that again (i.e., raising to 4) emphasizes strong scattering directions at broadside.

Fig. 14Angular RCS pattern of the required 1 m2 half-cylinder target at broadside incidence.

Angular RCS pattern of the required 1 m2 half-cylinder target at broadside incidence.

The half-cylinder is modeled with a radius of 0.08 m and height of 1.0 m, illuminated at broadside under monostatic conditions at 10 GHz. The resulting RCS is found to be approximately 1.014 m² (0.06 dBsm), which supports the modeling assumption and is consistent with canonical target behavior reported in1,55. From Fig. 14, one can observe the angular RCS pattern of a half-cylinder target at broadside incidence. The red curve in Fig. 14 represents the angular variation of the RCS of the half-cylinder target as observed from different angles. Specifically, it shows how the RCS changes as the radar beam scans around the target. The plot typically reflects the RCS at broadside incidence (0° or 180°), where the target presents its maximum reflective area to the radar, and the values decrease as the radar moves away from the broadside position. The curve’s peaks and troughs represent the angular distribution of the target’s RCS, with the highest point usually corresponding to the broadside orientation of the half-cylinder.

From Table 7, one can find that the overall accuracy of the simulation and experimentation process is 92.05% and 89.80% respectively. It is obvious that there is no such big deviation between the simulation accuracy and experimentation accuracy, hence, the system performs well in this scenario. However, the maximum unambiguous range for the experimental prototype is very low in terms of real-life application. Thus, when the range of objects is increased, the detection accuracy of the prototype will decline. So, the higher accuracy can be retained in that case if the maximum detectable range and scanning area of the PAA are increased by adding more antenna elements to the array. The experimental prototype is not capable of increasing the object velocity in meter per second range which can be a significant issue for the practical object detection problem. Moreover, the process of interference rejection cannot be performed with this experimental setup and so the overall accuracy is considerably affected with noisier environment. For this reason, a robust algorithm is required for the beamformer used in the radar system so that it can be outperformed the conventional beamformer as well as implemented in practical scenario.

Performance analysis of the proposed hybrid robust beamformer

In order to analyze the performance of the proposed hybrid robust beamformer, a challenging scenario is considered that can reflect the real-life challenges. In this challenging scenario, there are three sources and two interferences and the environment is kept so noisy. So, the SNR in this scenario is set at 0.01 dB, source signal power is 1 dB, and interference signal power is 100 dB. Here, other five beamformers (Conventional, MVDR, LCMV, MVDR with Diagonal Loading, and LCMV with Diagonal Loading) are chosen to make comparison with the proposed hybrid robust beamformer. Apart from that, the whole process is done with three different number of antenna elements and they are 10 × 10 URA (100 elements), 20 × 20 URA (400 elements), and 30 × 30 URA (900 elements) in order to observe the difference in performance of the proposed hybrid robust beamformer. The simulation is performed in the MATLAB53 software environment. For the proposed beamformer, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:$$\end{document} value is chosen \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:0.2$$\end{document} for the better performance in the given scenario. The process for the selection of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:$$\end{document} value in this research is trial-and-error. However, an adaptive technique can be an effective solution for selecting the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:$$\end{document} value rapidly, which may be considered the further improvement of the proposed beamformer.

From Figs. 22, 23, and 24, one can observe the comparison of beam patterns from various beamformers, including Conventional, MVDR, LCMV, MVDR-DL, LCMV-DL, and the proposed hybrid robust beamformer, using a 10 × 10 URA, 20 × 20 URA, and 30 × 30 URA, respectively. Here, the term DL is used as the short form of Diagonal Loading. The beam patterns are normalized with respect to the angle, showing normalized power (dB) as a function of angle in degrees. From Figs. 22, 23, and 24, one can also observe that the conventional beamformer (denoted by the red line) can create main beams to the source directions though there are some look direction errors. However, it cannot create nulls properly at the interference directions. The performance of the simplified MVDR beamformer (denoted by the deep green line) and the simplified LCMV beamformer (denoted by the blue line) are worse than that of the conventional beamformers. These beamformers (MVDR and LCMV) solve an optimization problem that involves inverting the array correlation matrix of the received signals. Due to the lower SNR hence the challenging scenario, the correlation matrix becomes ill-conditioned, meaning that the inversion process introduces large numerical errors, leading to a beam pattern that fails to suppress interference and even steers the beam in the wrong direction. For this reason, these two beamformers cannot provide any appropriate results in the given scenario. The better performance of the conventional beamformer as compared with the MVDR and LCMV beamformers in the challenging scenario is primarily due to its simplicity, which makes its less sensitive to errors in correlation matrix estimation, steering vector mismatches, and other numerical issues.

The ill-condition issue of the correlation matrix is then resolved by adding a regularization factor to the matrix according to the given scenario. As a result, this time, the MVDR-DL beamformer (denoted by the dotted green line) can create main beams to the source signals, however, it cannot create nulls at the interference directions yet due to the challenging scenario. Then, the issue of the MVDR-DL beamformer is mitigated by the LCMV-DL beamformer (denoted by the dotted magenta line), which creates deeper nulls at the interference directions while maintaining its main beams at the source directions. The proposed beamformer (denoted by the black line) can efficiently create its main beam at the signal directions and can also create nulls at the interference directions. From Figs. 22, 23, and 24, it is clear that the LCMV-DL beamformer creates deeper nulls in comparison to the proposed beamformer. However, this advantage of the LCMV-DL beamformer is offset by its higher sidelobe levels, which are more pronounced than those of the proposed beamformer. In contrast, the proposed beamformer exhibits superior sidelobe control, providing better performance in terms of interference suppression while maintaining relatively low sidelobe levels, making it more robust in challenging environments. Overall, the proposed hybrid robust beamformer stands out as a reliable solution, offering a balanced trade-off between null depth and sidelobe control, making it ideal for environments with multiple sources and interference, as highlighted by the performance analysis conducted with three signal sources and two interference sources. Therefore, the proposed beamformer can be implemented to the URA-based phased array scanning radar system to obtain the best possible outcome under noisier environment.

Fig. 25 presents the beam pattern comparison for a 30 × 30 URA under varying SNRs, showing the normalized power as a function of angle. The plot illustrates the beamforming performance across three signal sources and two interference sources at SNR levels ranging from − 10 dB to 10 dB. The beamformer performs well up to 0 dB, with the main beam focusing on the signal sources and creating nulls for interference. However, beyond − 1 dB, performance degrades, with increased sidelobes approaching 0 dB, which is unexpected and inappropriate for beamforming. Although extreme SNR values are presented, Fig. 25 depicts the parametric sweep with intermediate SNRs that offer greater clarity regarding the robustness margin, especially in the − 1 dB to 0 dB range, where the beamformer’s performance begins to deteriorate.

Computational complexity

The condition number-based method is used in this study to obtain the optimal weights for the proposed beamformer. For the grid search method, a grid spacing of 0.5λ is used to ensure adequate resolution for adaptive computations. On the other hand, a maximum iteration limit of 100 is set for the stopping criteria. Besides, a threshold for the change in the objective function (optimal diagonal loading factor) is set. The grid search process is stopped when the difference between successive iterations falls below 10−6. From Fig. 31, one can observe the convergence comparison between Newton-Raphson and grid search (conventional) method. The condition number difference is not converged to zero by the grid search method even after about 100 iterations whereas it is converged to zero by the Newton-Raphson numerical method after only 10 iterations. For this reason, Newton-Raphson numerical method is applied to reduce the computational complexity regarding the optimal weight calculation of the proposed beamformer.

Hardware limitations

Processor: The Intel® Core™ i3-3220, being a 3rd generation processor, has a relatively lower clock speed and fewer cores compared to more modern processors. This results in slower computational speeds for both methods, especially when handling large FLOP counts or multiple iterations.

RAM: The 12 GB RAM is adequate for moderate tasks, but might be limiting for large-scale computations or more complex simulations.

Storage: The HDD of 512 GB and SSD of 128 GB are somewhat outdated for computational-heavy tasks. The slower speed of the HDD could create bottlenecks during disk access, especially if the methods are storing or retrieving large datasets during processing. The smaller SSD capacity may also limit the system’s ability to handle large datasets efficiently.

Core Count: With only 2 cores, the processor’s ability to parallelize computations is limited. Methods like Grid Search, which involve multiple iterations and evaluations, may significantly benefit from a more multi-core processor for faster processing.

Conclusion

In this study, an analysis of the phased array scanning radar system’s performance utilizing the hybrid robust beamformer has been presented relative to that of previous radars. In addition, comprehensive qualitative and quantitative comparative studies have been conducted to examine the capabilities of both existing systems and the proposed model for identifying numerous moving objects using the given requirements. First, a simulation work along with experimental validation of a 1 × 16 ULA-based radar system with a conventional beamformer has been performed under a well-conditioned scenario, and it shows an overall accuracy of 92.05% and 89.80% in terms of the simulation and experimentation process, respectively, for object detection. However, the maximum unambiguous range for the experimental prototype has been very low in terms of real-life application. Thus, when the range of objects has increased, the detection accuracy of the prototype has declined. So, the higher accuracy can be retained in that case if the maximum detectable range and scanning area of the PAA are increased by adding more antenna elements to the array. Moreover, the process of interference rejection cannot be performed with this experimental setup and so the overall accuracy is considerably affected with noisier environment. To enhance the radar performance in challenging situations, the hybrid robust beamforming model on a 30 × 30 URA-based phased array scanning radar system has been employed. In the hybrid robust model, the MVDR and LCMV beamformer with diagonal loading have been combined with a hybrid weighting factor. The condition number-based method has been used to obtain the optimal weights for the proposed beamformer and Newton-Raphson numerical method has been used to reduce computational complexity. Comparing the proposed radar system to conventional radar systems, the proposed phased array radar shows an outstanding overall accuracy of 99.29% for multiple object detection and interference rejection, according to the simulation results. The scanning criteria of the proposed radar is two-dimensional, which can be extended to the three-dimensional scanning by employing additional calibration for elevation channels and more complex steering vector modeling. The performance of the proposed system can be investigated by introducing the non-planar arrays such as conformal or spherical arrays. Moreover, all the targets in this study are non-fluctuating. A future advancement can be done by including Swerling I–IV and micro-Doppler signatures for more realistic diversity.
