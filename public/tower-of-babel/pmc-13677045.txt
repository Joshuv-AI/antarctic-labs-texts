Acinar‐Derived Organoids Reveal Stage‐Specific Control of Acinar Plasticity
Authors: Yongfei Hou, Siyu Li, Chunying Li, Zhifan Lin, Dan He, Li Yang, Liping Fu, Qianyue Zhang, Tianping Zhou, Yao Yao, Xiaolei Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAdult pancreatic acinar cells undergo ordered metaplastic conversion during injury and tumor initiation. Whether individual cells retain redifferentiation capacity after metaplastic expansion, and what signals each transition requires, remains unknown. Here, using organoids derived from single acinar cells, we show that expanded acinar‐lineage organoids can reacquire acinar features under stage‐specific culture conditions. We defined conditions that preserve acinar identity ex vivo and showed that epidermal growth factor (EGF)/EGF receptor (EGFR) signaling initiates conversion from this maintained state. The expansion trajectory recapitulates injury‐ and oncogene‐associated acinar‐to‐ductal metaplasia (ADM) programs in vivo. After expansion, the conditions that preserved acinar identity could not restore it; a staged regimen containing transient insulin‐like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF1R) inhibition and dexamethasone promoted redifferentiation. The KrasG12D;Trp53R172H;Pdx1‐Cre (KPC) genotype reduced EGFR dependence during initiation but destabilized post‐expansion redifferentiation; Kras‐pathway inhibition increased endpoint acinar‐marker expression. These findings demonstrate that acinar plasticity is organized by stage‐specific signals, and that the KPC genotype disrupts this control at specific transition points.Keywords: acinar‐to‐ductal metaplasia, cell plasticity, oncogenic Kras , organoids, redifferentiation

Introduction

Adult pancreatic acinar cells maintain their differentiated state through an active transcriptional program. Ptf1a–Rbpjl, Mist1/Bhlha15 and Nr5a2 sustain digestive‐enzyme expression and constrain metaplastic conversion [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. When injury or oncogenic signaling overrides this program, acinar cells undergo metaplastic conversion, suppressing secretory genes and acquiring ductal or progenitor features. In vivo, this conversion is termed acinar‐to‐ductal metaplasia (ADM) [7, 8, 9, 10, 11] and can resolve after acute injury as acinar identity is restored [12, 13]; under oncogenic Kras, however, metaplasia persists and progresses toward pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) [7, 8, 9, 10, 12]. Sustained Ptf1a expression can counteract Kras‐driven neoplasia in vivo [6, 14], demonstrating that the acinar differentiated state is actively tumor‐suppressive. Whether individual acinar cells retain redifferentiation capacity after sustained metaplastic conversion, and what signals govern each stage of this process, remains unresolved.

Lineage‐tracing and single‐cell transcriptomic studies have mapped ADM as an ordered trajectory through intermediate states [12, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19] and implicated EGFR‐dependent signaling in Kras‐driven metaplasia [20, 21, 22]. In vivo, however, acinar tissue recovery after acute injury is attributed to replication of surviving acinar cells [12, 13], leaving unclear whether metaplastic cells themselves can reacquire acinar identity. Moreover, because identity maintenance, conversion, and potential reversion occur simultaneously, the signals controlling each transition have not been individually isolated.

Ex vivo systems should allow stage‐specific signal control, but acinar cells lose their identity within days of isolation [23, 24, 25, 26, 27], leaving no stable baseline from which to test conversion. Several complementary platforms address parts of this problem: duct‐ or progenitor‐derived organoids expand ductal cells long‐term [28, 29, 30, 31, 32]; pluripotent‐derived organoids generate acinar and ductal fates from developmental precursors [33, 34, 35]; and adult human acinar organoids model cancer onset from sorted acinar cultures [36, 37]. Each captures a specific aspect of acinar plasticity, yet the full trajectory, from a preserved acinar starting state through metaplastic expansion to post‐expansion redifferentiation, has not been achieved within a single system where signals at each transition can be independently controlled.

Here we recapitulate metaplastic conversion and redifferentiation in acinar‐derived organoids and define the signals controlling each transition. We first establish conditions that preserve adult acinar identity ex vivo and show that EGF/EGFR signaling initiates metaplastic conversion from this maintained state. Single acinar cells expand into organoids that traverse an ordered metaplastic trajectory aligned with pancreatitis‐ and Kras‐driven ADM in vivo. After expansion, organoids redifferentiate toward acinar identity when provided with transient inductive signals beyond those that sustain freshly isolated acinar cells. The KPC genotype promotes conversion while destabilizing post‐expansion redifferentiation. Together, these experiments establish a system for examining stage‐specific control of acinar plasticity.

Results

Defined Conditions Preserve Acinar Identity and EGF/EGFR Signaling Initiates Metaplastic Conversion

We first asked whether freshly isolated adult acinar cells could maintain acinar identity under defined ex vivo conditions, as acinar cells typically lose their identity within days of isolation [23, 24, 25, 26]. To track acinar‐cell fate, we used Mist1‐CreERT2; Rosa26‐tdTomato mice, in which tamoxifen‐induced recombination irreversibly labeled Mist1‐positive acinar cells with tdTomato [38]; 99.5% of Amylase‐positive acinar cells were labeled in vivo (Figure 1A,B and Figure S1A). We screened for conditions that could counter this identity loss, testing factors that promote cell survival, suppress ductal conversion, and maintain acinar gene expression. Candidates were selected from an available small‐molecule library to perturb major signaling axes and were advanced when they retained secretory‐enzyme expression without inducing ductal or proliferation genes.

This screen identified a defined maintenance medium (MM) containing nicotinamide, JNK and PDGFR inhibitors, and the Notch inhibitor DAPT (Figure 1C,D and Figure S1B–D). Across secretory‐enzyme, acinar transcription factor, ductal, and proliferation genes, candidates added to the CCJN backbone or to MM produced distinct signatures, including additions that conferred no benefit (Figure 1D). Fresh acinar clusters cultured in MM preserved compact multicellular morphology for 7 d (Figure 1E). Amylase and Cpa1 dropped soon after plating but then stabilized, whereas Krt19 remained low throughout (Figure 1F). Across the same genes, MM‐maintained cells retained higher secretory‐enzyme and acinar transcription factor expression at day 7 than CCJN‐ or EM‐cultured cells (Figure 1G). Immunofluorescence at day 7 confirmed strong Amylase and tdTomato signal with minimal Krt19 staining (Figure 1H). MM‐maintained acini also secreted amylase and responded to ceruletide in a dose‐dependent manner for at least 10 d, whereas neural progenitor cells, included as an unrelated cell type, did not (Figure 1I). MM thus preserved acinar identity for at least 7 d.

Because EGF has been linked to ADM initiation in vivo [20, 22], and MM notably lacks it, we next asked whether EGF addition would be sufficient to disrupt this maintained state. Addition of EGF to MM cultures over 7 d reduced Amylase and Cpa1 and increased Mki67, with higher mean Krt19 and Cftr expression (Figure 2A). Gefitinib co‐treatment opposed these changes (Figure 2A,B). Over 20 d, EGF‐treated cultures showed sustained elevation of ductal and proliferative markers, whereas MM‐maintained cultures preserved stable acinar‐gene expression (Figure S1E). Acinar clusters cultured under 10% FBS or EGF + bFGF (EF) conditions, both of which contain EGF‐family ligands, also underwent rapid ductal conversion within 7 d (Figure S1F,G). These results show that EGF/EGFR signaling is sufficient to convert maintained acinar cultures toward a ductal state, and that acinar identity can be preserved under defined conditions that lack EGF.

Acinar Cells Retain Redifferentiation Competence after Metaplastic Expansion

Single acinar cells did not form organoids under 10% FBS or EF conditions (Figure S2A–C). Since EGF drives metaplastic conversion, we used it as the basis for an expansion medium and screened for additional factors, identifying ECCA: EGF combined with the Wnt agonist CHIR‐99021 for proliferative support, plus CP‐673451 and A83‐01, which improved organoid formation efficiency (Figure 2C–E). Fresh acini were dissociated to single cells and cultured in ECCA for 7 d. Single tdTomato‐positive acinar cells progressed to small spheroids and then to large cystic organoids, with increased organoid size after day 3 and predominantly large cystic organoids by day 7 (Figures 2F and S2D,E). At this point, 89.1% of organoids were tdTomato‐positive, indicating that most arose from lineage‐labeled acinar cells (Figure 2G). Given the near‐complete in vivo labeling, the tdTomato‐negative organoids are unlikely to be explained by incomplete recombination alone, although their origin was not resolved. The expanded organoids expressed Krt19, Pdx1, and Hnf1b with residual Amylase and tdTomato labeling (Figures 2H and S2F). Organoids also formed at seeding densities down to 100 cells per well, a 40‐fold dilution at which individual organoids are widely separated, consistent with formation from single cells rather than by aggregation (Figure S2G). The tdTomato‐negative organoids remained cystic after transfer to DM and redifferentiated far less efficiently than tdTomato‐positive organoids (Figure S2G,H). The same difference was apparent at the protein level: after 8 d in DM, a tdTomato‐positive organoid and an adjacent tdTomato‐negative organoid imaged in the same field showed Amylase staining in the lineage‐labeled organoid and little detectable Amylase in the unlabeled one (Figure S2I). Timed withdrawal of individual components (Figure S3A,B) showed that EGF acted primarily during the initial phase of organoid formation, whereas CHIR‐99021 supported organoid growth across a broader expansion window (Figure S3C–F). CP‐673451 and A83‐01 each increased organoid‐forming efficiency beyond the minimal EGF + CHIR backbone, with the full ECCA combination producing the strongest expansion (Figures 2C–E and S3G). Withdrawal of any component during expansion also weakened subsequent acinar‐marker re‐expression after redifferentiation (Figure S3H,I).

We tested whether expanded organoids could redifferentiate toward acinar identity. After transfer to a differentiation medium (DM) that combined the maintenance medium with transient IGF1R inhibition and dexamethasone, cystic organoids compacted into grape‐like clusters, remained tdTomato‐positive, and showed increased Amylase with reduced Krt19 (Figure 2I,J). Redifferentiated organoids also regained amylase secretion and ceruletide responsiveness, which were absent at the end of expansion (Figure 2K,L). qRT‐PCR during expansion and after redifferentiation showed reciprocal marker changes: Amylase and Cpa1 decreased during expansion and increased after redifferentiation, whereas Krt19, Cftr, and Mki67 changed in the opposite direction; Pdx1 also decreased during redifferentiation (Figures 2M and S2J). After redifferentiation, ECCA‐derived organoids showed higher Amylase and Cpa1 re‐expression, whereas FBS‐ and EF‐derived organoids largely remained cystic (Figure S2K,L), indicating that the expansion conditions influence whether redifferentiation competence is retained.

When intact acinar clusters rather than single cells were used as starting material, cluster‐derived organoids also expanded in ECCA and formed compact grape‐like structures after redifferentiation (Figure S4A,B). At higher magnification, amylase was confined to the cytoplasm and excluded from the nucleus in all three states, appearing as discrete puncta in freshly isolated acini and as a more densely punctate cytoplasmic pattern after maintenance or redifferentiation (Figure S4C). These cultures showed higher Amylase and Cpa1 re‐expression after redifferentiation than single‐cell‐derived cultures (Figure S4D). Acinar‐derived organoids maintained cystic morphology, tdTomato labeling, and cumulative growth through serial passage (Figure S4E,F). Passaged organoids still underwent acinar redifferentiation after transfer to DM, although this response weakened at later passages, with lower acinar‐marker re‐expression and higher residual Krt19 (Figure S4G). Together, these results establish a defined system in which acinar identity can be maintained, converted through metaplastic expansion, and redirected toward acinar identity, although redifferentiation competence diminished with prolonged passage (Figure 2N).

Organoid Expansion Traverses an Ordered Acinar‐to‐Ductal Trajectory

To define how acinar‐derived cells progress during organoid expansion, we profiled freshly isolated acini and ECCA cultures collected at days 1, 3, 5, and 7 by single‐cell RNA sequencing (Figure 3A). Integrated analysis identified acinar, ADM‐like, and Ductal‐like populations as the main epithelial compartments, along with minor non‐epithelial and residual ductal clusters (Figure 3B and Figure S5A–D). Acinar‐lineage cells formed a continuum spanning these three populations (Figure 3C and Figure S5E), progressing from acinar cells at day 0, through an ADM‐like intermediate at day 3, to a predominantly Ductal‐like population by days 5–7 (Figure 3D–F). The ADM‐like population co‐expressed the acinar marker Cpa1 and the duct‐associated marker Krt19 (Figure 3E and Figure S5F). Potency analysis scored freshly isolated acinar cells as terminally differentiated and showed a transient increase in developmental potency in a small fraction of ADM‐like cells. The Ductal‐like endpoint, however, consisted predominantly of unipotent and oligopotent cells (Figure 3G), elevated relative to the acinar starting point without reaching a highly plastic progenitor state. Acinar and duct‐associated markers segregated along this continuum, with Cpa1 confined to the acinar end and Krt19, Muc1, and Cdh1 enriched toward the Ductal‐like terminus (Figure 3H).

Trajectory inference with scFates [39] placed acinar‐lineage cells along a single path from Acinar to Ductal‐like, with pseudotime increasing from day 0 to day 7 (Figure 3I,J and Figure S5G,H). Gene‐expression dynamics along this path showed progressive loss of acinar genes, a transient Reg3b‐high intermediate, and later accumulation of ductal genes including Muc1 and Krt19 (Figure 3K,L). Acinar‐derived organoid expansion thus followed an ordered acinar‐to‐ductal trajectory.

The Metaplastic Trajectory Recapitulates Conserved Injury‐ and Oncogene‐associated Regulatory Programs

To determine whether this trajectory corresponds to ADM programs in vivo, we constructed an integrated reference from two published single‐cell transcriptomic datasets: a pancreatitis ADM atlas (Ma et al.) [17] and an epithelial dataset from Kras G12D mice (Schlesinger et al.) [18]. The Ma et al. dataset contributed a complete acinar‐to‐ADM intermediate continuum, whereas the Schlesinger dataset provided oncogene‐driven metaplastic and ductal terminal states without a corresponding ADM intermediate (Figure 4A,B). On this integrated reference, Acinar, ADM‐like, and Ductal‐like populations mapped to contiguous regions along the acinar‐to‐metaplastic axis, with ADM‐like cells predominantly in the intermediate zone and Ductal‐like cells extending into metaplastic regions shared by both reference datasets (Figure 4C). Time‐resolved projection showed progressive movement from the acinar region into ADM and metaplastic reference regions from day 0 to day 7 (Figure 4D); both acinar and ductal markers retained their established polarity across the projection (Figure 4E).

Label transfer confirmed these assignments: ADM‐like cells corresponded predominantly to acinar/ADM intermediates, whereas Ductal‐like cells distributed across MucinDuctal, Metaplastic, and Cycling_ductal labels (Figure 4F,G). Time‐resolved analysis showed progressive accumulation of ADM, mucinous, metaplastic, and ductal reference identities from day 0 to day 7 (Figure 4H). Parallel analyses on the Ma‐only reference confirmed these findings (Figure S6A–G).

To identify the regulatory programs driving these transitions, we performed regulon analysis, which revealed a temporal cascade: acinar regulons were active early, ADM‐associated regulons peaked at the intermediate phase, and ductal/metaplastic regulons dominated late (Figure 4I,J). An independent screen of transcription factors that changed along pseudotime, without prior annotation, recovered the same ordering in all three systems (Figure 4K), and program‐level activity of the selected regulons followed the same acinar‐to‐ductal progression across the three datasets (Figure S6H). Within the ex vivo data, the Ductal‐like population was not a uniform endpoint: some cells retained a MucinDuctal/Metaplastic regulatory program while others had progressed to a later ductal program, indicating that cells within the expanded population span different stages of the metaplastic trajectory, from the early ADM intermediates in the pancreatitis atlas to the late ductal states in the Kras‐driven dataset (Figure 4I,K). Organoid expansion was thus organized by a temporal cascade of transcription factor programs shared with injury‐ and oncogene‐driven ADM in vivo.

Acinar Redifferentiation Requires Transient Inductive Signals beyond Those That Maintain Acinar Identity

Having confirmed that the ex vivo trajectory recapitulates in vivo ADM programs, we next sought to identify the signals controlling redifferentiation. We first tested whether maintenance conditions were sufficient. MM preserved acinar identity in freshly isolated cells but did not restore acinar features after expansion. To identify candidate inductive signals, we examined pathway and gene‐level changes along the metaplastic trajectory. GSEA identified mTORC1 signaling as the top enriched Hallmark pathway among genes positively correlated with pseudotime, and gene‐level analysis showed that Igf1r and Insr increased toward the ductal‐like terminus whereas GR signaling components Nr3c1 and Fkbp5 peaked at an intermediate position and then declined at the ductal‐like terminus (Figure S7A–C). We therefore tested whether transient IGF1R inhibition with picropodophyllin (PPP) combined with dexamethasone (Dex) [40], added to the MM background, could initiate redifferentiation (Figure 5A). Under DM, expanded organoids compacted into grape‐like structures, activated the Mist1 reporter, and showed progressive Amylase increase with concurrent Krt19 loss over 8 d (Figure 5B,C). Secretory‐enzyme genes and the acinar transcription factors Ptf1a, Rbpjl, Mist1, and Nr5a2 rose progressively while ductal genes declined (Figure 5D,E), and Ptf1a, Gata4, and Mist1 protein became detectable from day 4 onward (Figure 5F), with Amylase increasing and Krt19 declining over the same period (Figure 5G).

Redifferentiation was sensitive to the timing of PPP/Dex exposure. Both staged (first 4 d only) and continuous (8 d) PPP/Dex exposure reduced Krt19 and Mki67 relative to MM, but only the staged regimen produced strong late induction of Amylase and Cpa1 (Figure 5H,I). During the maturation phase, withdrawing dexamethasone or PPP after day 4 raised Amylase and Cpa1, whereas dexamethasone add‐back lowered both genes and PPP add‐back lowered Amylase (Figure S7D). In freshly isolated acinar cells, by contrast, dexamethasone reduced Ptf1a and combined dexamethasone plus PPP reduced Cpa1 (Figure 5J). The cellular response to PPP/Dex thus differed between freshly isolated acinar cells and expanded organoids.

To test whether the effects of the two staged cues were consistent with their proposed pathways, we used complementary pharmacological perturbations. Dexamethasone induced the canonical GR targets Fkbp5, Tsc22d3, and Sgk1, and mean expression was lower with mifepristone co‐treatment; a second agonist, betamethasone, reproduced the effect of dexamethasone on acinar‐gene expression, and mifepristone opposed the agonist response (Figure 5K,L). A structurally unrelated IGF1R inhibitor, linsitinib, reproduced the effect of PPP, and IGF1 addition opposed the PPP response (Figure 5M). Together, these results support roles for glucocorticoid receptor activation and IGF1R‐pathway inhibition within the staged redifferentiation regimen. Component‐withdrawal analysis showed lower mean acinar scores after removal of individual shared components of DM and MM, with nicotinamide removal producing the largest mean reduction during both redifferentiation and maintenance (Figure 5N,O and Figure S7E,F). These results support a common signal foundation for redifferentiation and maintenance, with additional staged cues for redifferentiation.

The KPC Genotype Reduces EGFR Dependence during Initiation and Destabilizes Post‐Expansion Redifferentiation

Having established that EGF acts primarily during the earliest phase of initiation (Figure S3A,C–F,H) and that staged PPP/Dex promotes redifferentiation (Figure 5H,I), we next asked how the KPC genotype alters both processes. We tested whether oncogenic Kras could bypass this EGF requirement, using acini from KPC mice carrying LSL‐Kras G12D/+, Trp53 R172H/+, and Pdx1‐Cre alleles [41] (Figure 6A). In vivo, KPC pancreas contained Krt19‐positive epithelial foci within or adjacent to Amylase‐positive acinar tissue, including cells with overlapping Amylase and Krt19 signal, consistent with spontaneous acinar remodeling (Figure 6B). In vitro, KPC acini formed large cystic organoids that by day 7 showed strong Krt19, Pdx1, Hnf1b, and Ki67 with low residual Amylase (Figure 6C and Figure S8A).

Wild‐type acini formed organoids in ECCA but not in CCA, the same medium without EGF, and gefitinib almost completely blocked organoid formation; by contrast, KPC acini generated cystic organoids in both ECCA and CCA, and gefitinib reduced but did not abolish organoid formation (Figure 6D–G). Within the KPC background, colony‐forming efficiency, organoid size, and gene‐expression profiles were comparable in ECCA and CCA across the 7‐day expansion period (Figure S8B–D). KPC acini also formed organoids under 10% FBS and EF conditions, but these cultures remained largely cystic and showed lower Amylase induction after redifferentiation (Figure S8E,F), reinforcing that the choice of expansion medium shapes subsequent redifferentiation independently of genotype. KPC acini thus formed organoids efficiently without exogenous EGF, with only partial sensitivity to EGFR inhibition. Because the KPC background combines Kras G12D with Trp53 R172H, this result is consistent with genetic evidence that p53 loss is sufficient to bypass EGFR dependence during Kras‐driven pancreatic tumorigenesis [22].

We next asked whether Kras‐pathway inhibition could suppress organoid outgrowth and increase endpoint acinar‐marker expression. During expansion, the Kras G12D‐selective inhibitor MRTX1133 [42] and the pan‐Kras inhibitor BI‐2865 [43] each reduced cystic organoid formation in the KPC cultures, and both were associated with smaller organoid diameters across the three culture models (Figure 6E–G). Consistent with its genotype selectivity, MRTX1133 reduced colony formation in the KPC but not the wild‐type background (Figure 6F). In contrast to the sustained redifferentiation observed in wild‐type cultures, untreated KPC organoids showed only transient Amylase and Cpa1 induction followed by decline, indicating that acinar redifferentiation was unstable in the oncogenic background (Figure 6H). This transient rebound was greater in CCA‐derived than in ECCA‐derived cultures, whereas Krt19, Cftr, Pdx1, and Mki67 fell during early redifferentiation in both conditions (Figure 6H and Figure S8G). Kras‐pathway inhibition, particularly MRTX1133, increased endpoint Amylase and Cpa1 in both ECCA‐ and CCA‐derived KPC cultures (Figure 6H). Redifferentiation in KPC organoids became progressively more unstable with serial passage, with acinar‐gene re‐expression weakening across passages (Figure 6I and Figure S8H,I). Consistent with this unstable redifferentiation, KPC organoids retained more cystic organoids after transfer to DM, whereas wild‐type cultures compacted into grape‐like clusters (Figure 6J). Immunofluorescence of MRTX1133‐treated KPC‐CCA organoids at the differentiation endpoint confirmed stronger Amylase and weaker Krt19 relative to vehicle‐treated controls (Figure 6K). The KPC background was therefore associated with unstable post‐expansion acinar redifferentiation. Kras‐pathway inhibition increased endpoint acinar‐marker expression at both the transcript and protein level, but not to wild‐type levels.

Discussion

Here we show that acinar‐derived organoids undergo metaplastic conversion and redifferentiation under stage‐specific signals. In vivo, ADM can resolve after acute injury [12, 13], although resolved injury leaves durable epigenetic memory that alters subsequent plasticity [44, 45]. This resolution, however, occurs within an intact microenvironment and has not been demonstrated at the single‐cell level. The present findings establish that expanded cells can re‐express acinar markers, although redifferentiation remained partial, possibly constrained by epigenetic changes accumulated during expansion.

The in vitro trajectory aligned with both pancreatitis‐ and Kras‐driven ADM at the level of cell‐state mapping and regulon activity, indicating that the core transcriptional program of ADM can be activated by defined signals ex vivo without the in vivo microenvironment. Critically, by the time cells reach the ductal‐like terminus, acinar transcription factor programs are largely silenced and ductal/metaplastic programs dominate, which may explain why redifferentiation did not fully restore the acinar transcriptional state. In this respect, the present system addresses an aspect not captured by prior acinar organoid cultures, which modeled metaplastic expansion but did not test post‐expansion redifferentiation [36, 37].

During both metaplastic conversion and redifferentiation, each transition separates into an initiation phase and a sustaining phase served by different signals. In the forward direction, EGF/EGFR signaling acts predominantly during the earliest phase of organoid formation, consistent with the established role of EGFR in ADM initiation [20, 22], whereas Wnt activation by CHIR‐99021 supports the subsequent expansion phase. This staged forward conversion, in which acinar cells dismantle secretory architecture, acquire ductal features, and re‐enter the cell cycle, is consistent with paligenosis, a conserved program by which differentiated secretory cells revert to a proliferative state through sequential checkpoints [46, 47]. The present findings identify the specific external signals that govern each step and extend this framework to include post‐expansion redifferentiation toward acinar identity. After expansion, maintenance signals alone do not initiate redifferentiation of expanded organoids. A transient PPP/dexamethasone‐containing first phase supported initiation of the transition, after which maintenance conditions sustained the regained acinar features; staged exposure during the first 4 d only produced stronger redifferentiation than continuous exposure, consistent with a role as an initiation cue rather than a maintenance factor. Notably, the same additions produced different marker responses in freshly isolated acinar cells, indicating context‐dependent responsiveness. Consistent with this context dependence, Nr3c1 and Fkbp5 expression varied along the metaplastic continuum, suggesting that cellular responsiveness to these signals differs between freshly isolated and post‐expansion states. This context‐dependent response raises the possibility that sustained ADM persistence, as seen under oncogenic Kras, reflects not a permanent loss of acinar competence but the absence of conditions that support reacquisition of acinar features after cell‐state change. Both IGF1R and the glucocorticoid receptor are pharmacologically accessible, and whether their modulation can promote ADM resolution in vivo warrants investigation.

The KPC background disrupts this stage‐specific organization at two points. During initiation, KPC acini formed organoids without exogenous EGF, placing EGFR upstream of the oncogenic lesion; because Pdx1‐Cre acts during pancreatic development and the KPC pancreas is already remodeled before culture, this reflects the KPC background as a whole rather than an adult acinar‐cell‐autonomous effect of oncogenic Kras, and the contributions of mutant Kras and mutant Trp53 cannot be separated in this model. These findings may explain why ADM arises spontaneously in Kras‐mutant pancreas without overt injury [7, 8, 9]. At the second point, KPC organoids re‐expressed acinar markers only transiently during redifferentiation, in contrast to the sustained response of wild‐type cultures, and Kras‐pathway inhibition with MRTX1133 increased endpoint acinar‐marker expression without reaching wild‐type levels.

The ex vivo system described here captures the full arc of adult acinar plasticity and allows each transition to be dissected with defined signals. These data establish that expanded acinar‐lineage organoids retain redifferentiation competence under staged differentiation conditions, and that the KPC genotype is associated with facilitated conversion and less stable redifferentiation. This framework enables systematic testing of when and how differentiation‐based interventions can redirect expanded organoids toward acinar recovery.

Limitations

The system is mouse‐based and entirely ex vivo, lacking stromal, immune, vascular, and neural inputs. Redifferentiated organoids did not fully reconstruct the fresh adult acinar state. Secretory function was assessed only by amylase release and ceruletide responsiveness, and polarity and ultrastructure were not examined. Serial passage progressively weakened redifferentiation competence. The culture conditions likely capture only a subset of the metaplastic landscape observed in vivo [12, 17, 18]. Whether human acinar cells share these signal dependencies remains untested. Because viability, apoptosis, pERK/pMEK, and stage‐restricted inhibitor exposure were not assessed, the increased endpoint acinar‐marker signal cannot distinguish direct redifferentiation from altered proliferation or survival of ductal‐like organoids, or from selection or persistence of residual acinar‐like cells.

Materials and Methods

Mouse Strains

Bhlha15(Mist1)‐CreERT2 mice (NM‐KI‐200080; Shanghai Model Organisms Center) were crossed with Rosa26‐tdTomato reporter mice (Jackson Laboratory, #007909) [38], and F1 offspring were used for acinar lineage tracing. KPC mice (NM‐KI‐210096; Shanghai Model Organisms Center) were used as a spontaneous PDAC model [41], with pancreata collected at approximately 8 weeks of age. Mice of both sexes were used. All animal procedures were approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Tongji University (TJAB04720101).

Acinar Cell Isolation

For lineage tracing, 8‐week‐old mice received tamoxifen in corn oil by oral gavage for 3 consecutive days (100 µL per dose; 15 mg mL−1). One week later, the pancreas was collected into ice‐cold DPBS, rinsed, minced, and digested in DMEM containing collagenase (0.15 mg mL−1), DNase I (0.1 mg mL−1), and penicillin‐streptomycin for 30 min at 37°C. The digest was gently pipetted to disperse residual fragments, briefly pelleted, and washed by repeated sedimentation in ice‐cold DPBS to enrich acinar clusters, based on established mouse acinar isolation approaches [48]. Each acinar isolation was prepared from a single mouse and supplied all conditions of a given experiment.

Acinar clusters were further dissociated in TrypLE for 5 minutes at 37°C to generate single cells. Single cells were seeded at 4000 cells per well, and cluster‐based cultures at approximately 800 clusters per well, in Cultrex RGF basement membrane extract (BME) droplets in 48‐well plates. After gel solidification, cultures were overlaid with medium containing 10 µm Y‐27632 and fed on day 4. For all analyses, day 0 denotes freshly isolated acinar cells collected immediately after digestion and purification and before plating, approximately 2 h after dissection.

Acinar Maintenance

For direct acinar maintenance, freshly isolated acinar clusters were embedded in BME and cultured in maintenance medium (MM), defined as the full differentiation base medium plus 10 µm Y‐27632. Medium was changed on day 4. For stepwise maintenance optimization, the reduced CCJ backbone contained CHIR‐99021, CP‐673451, and JNK‐IN‐8 on the basal medium; CCJN denotes CCJ plus nicotinamide. Where indicated, individual candidates were added to the specified backbone condition. Complete medium compositions are provided in Table S1, maintenance‐screen candidates in Table S2, and sources, identifiers, and working concentrations for proteins and small molecules in Table S3.

Organoid Expansion

Basal medium consisted of Advanced DMEM/F12 supplemented with B‐27, 10 mm HEPES, 2 mm GlutaMAX, penicillin‐streptomycin, and 1 mm N‐acetyl‐L‐cysteine. Unless otherwise indicated, expansion medium additionally contained 10% R‐spondin1 conditioned medium and EGF (50 ng mL−1), CHIR‐99021 (4 µm), CP‐673451 (0.25 µm), and A83‐01 (0.5 µm); this condition is referred to as ECCA. CCA denotes the same formulation without EGF. Established organoids were passaged from day 7 onward by dissociation into small clusters and re‐embedding in fresh BME at a 1:6 to 1:8 split ratio every 2 to 3 d.

Direct Differentiation in Organoids

Organoids were differentiated by direct medium switch. The standard two‐step protocol comprised a 4‐day Step 1 medium containing IDE1 (2 µm), CHIR‐99021 (4 µm), DMH1 (2 µm), DAPT (5 µm), dexamethasone (10 µm), picropodophyllin (PPP; 0.2 µm), JNK‐IN‐8 (2 µm), nicotinamide (1 mm), tofacitinib (2 µm), and CP‐673451 (0.25 µm), followed by a 4‐day Step 2 medium identical in composition except that dexamethasone and PPP were omitted. In experiments monitoring gene‐expression changes throughout the process, the differentiation was prolonged to day 10 (4 + 6). Where indicated, 4‐hydroxytamoxifen (4‐OH tamoxifen; 1 µm) was added during differentiation to activate the Mist1‐tdTomato reporter. For reporter‐reactivation experiments, mice were not exposed to tamoxifen before acinar isolation; 4‐OH tamoxifen was added only upon transfer to DM or to the matched EM control. For the maturation‐phase timing experiments (Figure S7D), all groups received picropodophyllin and dexamethasone during days 0–4; during days 4–8, each component was either added to standard Step 2 medium or withdrawn from continuous exposure, as indicated. Where individual components were tested (Figure 5K–M), Step 1 medium was prepared without dexamethasone or without picropodophyllin and single components were added back during days 0–4: dexamethasone (10 µm), betamethasone (5 µm), mifepristone (10 µm), or the indicated combinations to the dexamethasone‐free medium, and picropodophyllin (0.2 µm), linsitinib (1 µm), or picropodophyllin together with IGF1 (100 ng mL−1) to the picropodophyllin‐free medium; glucocorticoid receptor target genes were measured after 4 d and acinar, ductal, and proliferation genes after 8 d.

Immunostaining

Organoids were released from BME with DPBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature, permeabilized in DPBS containing 0.5% Triton X‐100, 0.5% Tween 20, and 2% BSA, and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies in blocking buffer (0.1% BSA and 0.5% Tween 20 in DPBS). After washing, samples were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies (1:1000) for 1 h at room temperature and counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired on an Olympus IX73, an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope (IX83 stand; UPLAPO60XOHR 60×/1.5 NA oil‐immersion objective; 0.138 µm per pixel), or a Zeiss LSM880 microscope and analyzed in ImageJ [49]. For each antibody, acquisition settings were fixed on a reference sample and held constant across all samples within a panel, and images are displayed with identical brightness and contrast scaling. Settings differ between antibodies, so intensities are compared between states within a channel and not across channels or panels. The antibodies used in this study are listed in Table S4.

RNA Extraction and Reverse Transcription Quantitative PCR

Organoids were washed with ice‐cold DPBS and lysed in TRIzol. Total RNA was extracted according to the manufacturer's instructions; up to 1 µg RNA was reverse‐transcribed with NovoScript Plus Reverse Transcriptase, and qPCR was performed with ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix on a CFX384 system. Relative expression was calculated by a ΔCt method [50]. Primer sequences used for qRT‐PCR are provided in Table S5.

For the single‐component dropout screens, expression was reported as the mean within‐mouse log2 fold change relative to the complete formulation. The acinar score was calculated as mean(log2FC of Amylase and Cpa1) minus mean(log2FC of Krt19 and Mki67).

Colony Formation Assay

Single acinar cells were plated at 4,000 cells per well in BME in 48‐well plates. After 7 d, colonies were fixed, stained with DAPI, and counted manually. Colony‐forming efficiency was calculated as colonies/plated single cells × 100%. Redifferentiation efficiency was scored morphologically as the fraction of organoids that compacted from a cystic to a grape‐like morphology after 8 d in DM; where organoids were classified by lineage label, tdTomato‐positive and tdTomato‐negative organoids were scored separately within the same wells.

Amylase Secretion Assay

Cultures were washed and incubated with DMSO, 10 pm ceruletide, or 10 nm ceruletide for 30 min at 37°C, and the supernatant was collected. Amylase activity was measured with an α‐amylase assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Cat#C016‐1‐2) according to the manufacturer's instructions: diluted supernatant was incubated with substrate for 7.5 min, the reaction was stopped with iodine solution, and absorbance was read at 660 nm. Samples comprised freshly isolated acini, acini maintained in MM for 4, 7, or 10 d, acini maintained in CCJN for 10 d, neural progenitor cells as an unrelated control, organoids at expansion day 7, and organoids at differentiation days 4 and 8. Supernatant was pooled from the wells of each condition, two samples were collected per condition, and each sample was assayed in triplicate. Activity is expressed per well, calculated from the measured concentration and the volume of pooled supernatant.

Single‐Cell RNA‐Sequencing Data Processing

Fresh acinar cells (day 0) and ECCA cultures harvested on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 were processed for single‐cell RNA‐seq on the DNBelab C4 platform (BGI). Vendor‐filtered count matrices were imported into R (v4.3.2) and converted to Seurat objects (Seurat v5.3.0) [51, 52]. Cells were quality‐filtered per sample on the basis of UMI counts, detected genes, mitochondrial fraction, nuclear fraction (intronic‐to‐total UMI ratio), and scDblFinder (v1.16.0) singlet classification [53]. Quality‐filtered cells were merged for downstream analysis.

Integrated analysis was performed in Python (v3.10.14) with Scanpy (v1.11.4) and BBKNN (v1.6.0) [54, 55]. Raw counts were normalized to 10 000 per cell and log‐transformed. The 2,000 most variable genes were selected per batch (Seurat v3 method), followed by principal‐component analysis, batch‐balanced nearest‐neighbor graph construction with BBKNN, UMAP embedding, and Leiden clustering at resolution 0.8 [56, 57]. Clusters were annotated as acinar, ADM‐like, residual ductal, ductal‐like, endocrine, stellate, endothelial, or immune on the basis of canonical markers.

For acinar‐lineage‐restricted analyses, tdTomato‐positive cells within the acinar, ADM‐like, and ductal‐like compartments were retained and re‐clustered with the same pipeline. Developmental potency was estimated per sample with CytoTRACE2 (v1.0.0) on raw counts (species = mouse) [58]. Trajectory inference was performed with scFates (v1.2.1) [39]: a principal curve was fit in PCA space, the root was assigned to the tip with the lowest mean sampling‐day index, and pseudotime orientation was validated by correlation with CytoTRACE2 potency scores.

Reference Mapping onto In Vivo ADM Datasets

Two published references were used: the Ma et al. pancreatitis atlas (GSE172380) [17] and the Schlesinger et al. Kras G12D epithelial dataset (GSE141017) [18]. For individual reference mapping, query and reference were restricted to shared genes, and cell‐type labels were transferred with Scanpy ingest. For the integrated reference, the two datasets were combined and batch‐corrected with scVI (scvi‐tools v1.3.1; 30 latent dimensions, negative‐binomial likelihood) [59]; query cells were projected onto the integrated reference space via ingest. For each projected cell, a pancreatitis‐versus‐Kras G12D neighborhood preference was computed as the pancreatitis fraction among its 10 nearest reference neighbors. Mapping confidence was quantified as the Shannon entropy of reference labels among each query cell's 10 nearest neighbors.

Transcription‐Factor Regulon Analysis

Gene regulatory networks were inferred with the SCENIC pipeline [60] using pySCENIC (v0.12.1) [61] and GRNBoost2 [62] on the acinar‐lineage subset (raw counts). Coexpression modules were pruned against two mouse cisTarget v10 motif databases (500 bp upstream / 100 bp downstream, and 10 kb upstream / 10 kb downstream of the TSS), retaining regulons with a normalized enrichment score ≥ 3.0; the regulons assigned to each of the four programs are listed in Table S6; per‐cell regulon activity was scored with AUCell. The same regulon definitions were applied to the Ma and Schlesinger reference datasets to enable cross‐dataset comparison; mean AUCell scores were z‐scored within each dataset before plotting. Dynamic regulons were defined as those whose activity correlated with pseudotime (Spearman |ρ| ≥ 0.2, Benjamini–Hochberg FDR ≤ 0.05), and the cross‐dataset cascade (Figure 4K) retained regulons with concordant ordering across all three datasets.

Statistical Analysis

Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The replicate unit is specified in each figure legend. For mouse‐level analyses, culture wells were averaged within each mouse and condition before comparison. Where n denotes wells, the data are from one isolation and comparisons describe variation within that preparation. Repeated assay measurements in Figures S1G and S7D were averaged within wells. Mouse‐level qPCR comparisons used log2 expression ratios relative to the matched control. Statistical tests and multiple‐comparison corrections are specified in the figure legends. All t tests were two‐sided, and P <.05 was considered statistically significant; ns, not significant. For organoid diameter, comparisons used well medians, except in Figure S2E, which used mouse means.
