Impact of sample processing method and volume on 16 S rRNA profiling of the urobiome
Authors: Sophie C. Ramirez, Zachary J. Lewis, Vanessa L. Hale, Emily L. Coffey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe urinary microbiome (urobiome) plays important roles in both human and animal urogenital tract health. Characterization of these microbial communities presents several technical challenges, largely due to the low microbial biomass of urine. Whereas other low biomass liquid systems, such as aquatic samples, frequently employ small-pore vacuum filtration for microbial DNA concentration, urobiome studies have traditionally relied on centrifugation and pelleting of smaller volumes. Therefore, this study compared the effects of processing method (vacuum filtration versus pelleting) and sample volume on bacterial DNA yield, contaminant burden, and microbial diversity in canine urine.ResultsA total of 50 urine aliquots were obtained across samples. Urine from 15 healthy dogs was pooled into five unique batches and divided into duplicate aliquots at volumes of 1, 3, 10, 30, and 50 mL. One aliquot was pelleted and one filtered (0.2 μm pore filter) prior to DNA extraction of the pellet or filter, respectively, and 16 S rRNA gene (V4) sequencing. Three aliquots ≥ 30 mL could not be filtered due to clogging. Sequence depth, DNA recovery, contaminant abundance, and microbial diversity were similar across urine volumes. Filtered samples contained a higher proportion of reads classified as contaminants (𝑃 = 0.002). Although beta diversity differed between methods (Bray-Curtis PERMANOVA, P = 0.007), the effect size was small (R2 = 0.03) relative to the influence of urine batch (𝑃 = 0.001, R2 = 0.65).ConclusionsThese findings indicate that interindividual variation predominates over methodological effects. Higher urine volumes (≥ 30 mL) were associated with technical challenges in filtered samples, whereas moderate urine volumes (1–10 mL) appear sufficient for urobiome characterization. Similar microbial recovery, increased contaminant signal, and occasional clogging with filtration suggests that pelleting remains an appropriate approach for urobiome characterization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05274-3.

Background

The discovery of unique microbial communities inhabiting the urinary tract, commonly referred to as the urobiome, has reshaped our understanding of urinary tract health and disease [1]. However, investigation of these communities presents unique technical challenges. Urine is a low microbial biomass specimen, making it particularly susceptible to downstream effects of contaminating DNA introduced during sample processing. Limited recovery of authentic microbial DNA can also amplify stochastic effects, complicating interpretation of community structure [2]. Alterations in the urobiome have been associated with a range of disease states, including urolithiasis, neoplasia, urinary incontinence, and urinary tract infection [3–9]. Given both the low biomass nature of urine and the growing links between the urobiome and disease, rigorous optimization of microbial DNA recovery and contaminant control is essential for accurate characterization and meaningful biological interpretation.

Dogs represent a powerful, large-animal model for urobiome research. Canine urinary microbial communities share dominant taxa with those of humans, and dogs naturally develop many of the same urogenital disorders [10–13]. Moreover, disease-associated shifts in the canine urobiome mirror those observed in human patients (e.g., bladder neoplasia, urolithiasis) [10, 14]. As in human studies, urine collection method and DNA extraction approach can influence detected urobiome composition in dogs, though interindividual differences still tend to overwhelm differences introduced by method [1, 15–18]. Recent work in the canine model has identified extraction methods and minimum sample volume thresholds that optimize microbial DNA capture, reduce stochasticity, and limit contamination [15, 19]. Despite growing recognition of the technical factors impacting low biomass samples [20, 21], a standardized approach to urine processing has not been established, and comparative data evaluating alternative microbial concentration strategies remain limited.

Most urobiome studies concentrate bacteria by centrifuging and pelleting urine prior to DNA extraction [1, 18, 22]. In contrast, aquatic microbiome research commonly relies on membrane-based filtration to isolate microbes from larger liquid volumes [23–27]. In some environmental contexts, filtration has even been shown to yield greater DNA recovery than pelleting [26]. Despite the success of filtration in other low biomass liquid samples, its application to urine has been minimally investigated. Additionally, prior work suggests that pelleting may require a minimum urine volume to ensure reliable microbial DNA recovery [19], and human urobiome studies use a wide range of volumes, with some as low as 0.5 mL and others up to 70 mL [28, 29]. In canine studies, volumes above 5 mL have not been systematically evaluated, and collecting large urine samples is not always feasible. Therefore, determining whether alternative concentration strategies or lower volumes can maintain microbial recovery is essential for designing feasible and reproducible urobiome studies.

In this study, we isolated bacteria from healthy canine urine samples using both small-pore vacuum filtration and pelleting across multiple sample volumes to compare the performance of each bacterial isolation method. We aimed to determine if urine filtration, followed by extraction of DNA from the filter, is a comparable or more effective alternative to conventional pelleting for bacterial DNA isolation from urine. We also sought to replicate and extend previous work evaluating the effects of urine volume on microbial DNA recovery and diversity. Improving urinary bacterial DNA isolation will support more robust urobiome profiling and will advance understanding of the urobiome’s role in health and disease.

Materials and methods

Urine specimen collection

Dogs were recruited from the University of Minnesota’s Veterinary Medical Center. Clinically healthy dogs of any age, breed, or sex were eligible for enrollment. Dogs with lower urinary tract signs (e.g., stranguria, hematuria, pollakiuria) were not included. Urine was collected via midstream voiding in a sterile cup and placed in a -80˚ C freezer within 10 min of collection. Urine was obtained from a total of 15 dogs, with volumes ranging from 2 to 90 mL per dog. To achieve sufficient volume for the technical replicates, urine from multiple dogs was combined to create five unique urine batches of at least 188 mL. Written, informed owner consent was obtained before urine collection. Sample collection was approved by the University of Minnesota Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocols 2406–42136 A).

Urine sample processing, DNA extraction, and sequencing

Each urine batch was vortexed for approximately 5–10 s to homogenize samples prior to aliquoting. For each batch, urine was divided into 2 aliquots at each of the following volumes: 1, 3, 10, 30, and 50 mL (Fig. 1). One aliquot at each of the listed volumes was processed via pelleting and the other was processed via filtration. All samples underwent a single freeze-thaw cycle prior to extractions. Samples that were processed via pelleting were centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 min [16, 30, 31], followed by removal of all but approximately 100 µL of supernatant.

Vacuum filtration was performed using a MultiVac 610-MS-T, 6-branch aluminum manifold vacuum filtration system (Sterlitech Corporation, Auburn, WA), in accordance with manufacturer instructions. Urine samples were passed through 0.22 μm filters comprising nitrocellulose mixed ester (MCE) membrane (Sterlitech Corporation, Auburn, WA). Filters were removed from the filtration system using sterile forceps and placed into sterile 50 mL conical tubes for transport prior to extraction. A designated filtration port was used for each urine batch to avoid cross-contamination. The 30 mL and 50 mL volumes of urine clogged the filters and prevented complete vacuum filtration for three of the five urine batches. This resulted in a total of 44 processed samples (25 pelleted and 19 filtered). Three aliquots of sterilized water (1, 3, and 10 mL) were filtered using the same methods, with those filters collected and included as negative controls. Three additional negative controls of only extraction reagents were included at the time of DNA extraction. Two aliquots of 75 µL of ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Community Standard (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA) were each combined with 925 µL of sterile water to create two positive control samples (1 mL each). One positive control preparation was processed via pelleting, and the other was processed via filtration.

DNA was extracted from urine pellets and filters using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, with minor modifications. The volumes of the wash buffers applied to the spin column were increased from 500 µL to 600 µL prior to centrifugation (15,000 x g, 1 min) to improve column saturation and wash efficiency. For filters, sterile forceps were used to transfer rolled filters into the bead-beating tubes and gently fragment them. The remainder of the protocol was performed as described above, except that the initial lysis buffer volume was increased to 900 µL. The concentration and purity of total DNA was evaluated using Quanti-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) and NanoDrop spectrophotometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). DNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing were all performed in a single batch at the University of Minnesota’s Genomics Center. Prior to library preparation, bacterial DNA concentration was quantified by quantitative PCR (qPCR) targeting the 16 S rRNA gene using a standard curve-based approach (reported as molecules/µL). Amplification of the V4 region of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene was then performed using primers 515 F (GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA) and 806 R (GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT) [32]. Amplicon sequencing of DNA from experimental samples and controls was performed using the MiSeq sequencing platform, v3 chemistry, targeting 2 × 300 bp paired-end reads (Illumina, San Diego, CA).

Sequence read processing

Raw sequence reads were processed using QIIME2 (v. 2023.2) and DADA2, including primer removal, quality filtering, and merging of forward and reverse reads [33, 34]. Forward and reverse reads were truncated at 240 and 200 base pairs, respectively, based on inspection of per-base quality score plots. Taxonomic assignment was performed using the Silva reference database (v. 138) [35]. Initial filtering included removal of mitochondria, chimeras, chloroplasts, unassigned amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), and ASVs present in only a single sample or that had fewer than 10 total reads across the dataset, as previously described [16]. ASVs identified in positive control samples that were not components of the expected mock community profile were removed from the dataset [36]. Contaminant taxa were further identified using the prevalence method of the decontam package (v. 1.26.0) with a threshold of 0.5 [37]. Putative contaminants were removed from the dataset prior to downstream analyses (Additional File 1).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were evaluated for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visualized with Q-Q plots. Linear mixed-effect models (lme4 package) with Type III ANOVA were performed to compare post-processing sequence read counts (i.e., after filtration and putative contaminant removal), bacterial DNA concentration (qPCR, molecules/µL), and the proportion of putative contaminants (% of total reads) between the five different volumes (1, 3, 10, 30, and 50 mL) and between the two processing methods (pellet vs. filtration). Urine batch was controlled as a random effect within the model. Comparisons between urine volumes were performed using only pelleted samples, as 30 and 50 mL aliquots could not be filtered for three batches. Comparisons between processing methods and interactions between volume and processing method were assessed using data from the 1, 3, and 10 mL aliquots.

Samples with fewer than 1,000 sequence reads were excluded from analyses evaluating microbial community composition (Additional File 2), and rarefaction curves were used to confirm a plateau in diversity at this sequencing threshold across samples (Additional File 3). ASV tables were normalized using relative abundance transformation. The vegan (v. 2.7.1) and phyloseq (v. 1.50.0) packages were used to assess alpha and beta diversity [38, 39]. Alpha diversity was calculated using the Shannon diversity index, inverse Simpson diversity index, and observed richness. Measures of beta diversity included Bray-Curtis, weighted UniFrac, unweighted UniFrac, and Jaccard. Differences in beta diversity between the different urine volumes and between the two processing methods (pellet vs. filtration) were tested using PERMANOVA with 1,000 permutations (adonis2 function, vegan), while controlling for urine batch as a stratification factor. Differences in dispersion (i.e., within-group variability) between volume and processing methods were evaluated using permutational analysis of multivariate dispersions (PERMDISP) on Bray-Curtis distances performed within each urine batch. Urine volumes were classified as high (≥ 10 mL) or low (1 or 3 mL). Statistical differences in alpha diversity and dispersion between groups and volume were evaluated using linear mixed-effect models and Type III ANOVA, controlling for urine batch. Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to evaluate differences in alpha diversity and dispersion across urine batches. Differentially abundant taxa between processing methods were identified at both the family and genus level using the Analysis of Compositions of Microbiomes with Bias Correction 2 (ANCOM-BC2) method with the ANCOMBC package (v. 2.8.1) [40].

Results

Participant characteristics and sample processing

Midstream, voided urine was collected from 15 clinically healthy dogs, including 9 males (2 intact, 7 neutered) and 6 females (1 intact, 5 spayed). The median age was 6 years (8 months – 14.5 years). Urine samples were combined into unique batches (labelled as batches A through E) to achieve sufficient volume for the desired technical replicates and to capture a wider range of taxa representative of canine urobiome, or urogenital microbiota. Urine from 2 to 5 dogs were included in each batch, and samples from the same dog were not included in more than a single batch. Each urine batch was divided into duplicate aliquots at five volumes (1, 3, 10, 30, and 50 mL), with one aliquot processed via filtration and the other pelleted by centrifugation (Fig. 1). In three of the five batches (batches B, D, and E), the 30 mL and 50 mL samples clogged the filters and were excluded from analyses, resulting in 44 included samples (25 pelleted and 19 filtered).

Effects of volume and processing method on sequence reads, bacterial DNA recovery, and contaminant abundance

A total of 3,180,911 sequence reads (median 60,184 per sample) were recovered from the remaining 44 samples after data filtration and contaminant removal. All bacterial members of the commercial mock community in positive control samples were successfully recovered using both filtered and pelleted techniques. Contaminant removal from samples and controls are summarized in Additional File 1. Urine volume exerted minimal effects on microbial DNA isolation. Differences in sequence reads (P = 0.48), bacterial DNA (P = 0.87), and contaminant abundance (P = 0.12) were not observed between urine volumes across pelleted samples (Fig. 2). When comparing processing methods, differences in sequence reads (P = 0.22) and bacterial DNA (P = 0.36) were not observed between filtered and pelleted samples of the same volume (Fig. 3A-B). However, the proportion of contaminant reads identified relative to total sequence read counts was higher in filtered samples, as compared to the matched pelleted samples (P = 0.002, Fig. 3C). Significant interactions between urine volume and processing method were not observed for sequence reads, bacterial DNA, or contaminant abundance (all P > 0.05). Urine batch was controlled for as a random effect in the linear mixed effect models for comparisons across volume and processing method.

Effects of volume and processing method on alpha diversity, beta diversity, and dispersion

Samples with fewer than 1,000 reads were excluded prior to diversity analyses, leaving 20 pelleted samples and 17 filtered samples (Additional File 2). Bacillota (formerly Firmicutes) was the most abundant phylum in both pelleted and filtered samples, followed by Pseudomonadota (formerly Proteobacteria) and Bacteroidota (formerly Bacteroidetes). The most abundant families observed across pelleted and filtered samples are depicted in Additional File 4.

Results from alpha diversity, beta diversity, and distance-to-centroid testing are summarized in Table 1. All alpha diversity measures varied by urine batch. Shannon diversity index was higher in filtered samples (P < 0.001; Fig. 4A) and showed a significant interaction between processing method and volume (P = 0.02). In contrast, inverse Simpson and observed richness did not statistically differ between processing methods or urine volumes after accounting for urine batch (all P > 0.05, Fig. 4B-C).

Urine batch was the major driver of microbial community composition (Fig. 4D, Additional File 5). Beta diversity did not differ by volume when controlling for batch, but Bray-Curtis, weighted UniFrac, and Jaccard distances differed by processing method. However, urine batch explained the majority of variation, with processing method accounting for < 5% of variation (Table 1). Within-group dispersion testing (PERMDISP; distance-to-centroid based on Bray-Curtis distances) differed by urine batch and tended to be higher in filtered samples, though these differences were not statistically significant (Additional File 6). Dispersion did not differ by volume classification (Additional File 6). Differential abundance testing using ANCOM-BC2 did not identify any taxa at the family or genus level that differed significantly between processing methods after correction for multiple comparisons (FDR-adjusted P values < 0.05; Additional File 7).

Discussion

This study evaluated the effects of processing method (filtration versus pelleting) and sample volume on bacterial DNA recovery and microbial diversity in canine urine. Interindividual variation was the major driver of microbial diversity and community structure, exerting stronger effects than either processing method or urine volume. This finding is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that individuals harbor distinct urinary microbial profiles and that interindividual differences account for the majority of observed variation [16, 18, 19, 41]. Importantly, these results also suggest that both filtration and pelleting, even at lower urine volumes (1–10 mL), are capable of capturing microbial profiles that reflect underlying community composition.

Obtaining large urine volumes is not always feasible, as collection volume is influenced by bladder capacity, voiding behavior, collection method, and other practical constraints. Previous urobiome studies have used a wide range of volumes, from 0.1 mL to 70 mL [19, 29]. In canine studies specifically, volumes are typically lower than those used in human research. One prior study reported that volumes ≥ 3 mL were associated with improved microbial recovery and reduced contaminant abundance, though the maximum volume evaluated was 5 mL [19]. In the present study, volume did not exert major effects on any parameters tested, including sequence read depth, bacterial DNA recovery, contaminant abundance, alpha or beta diversity, or dispersion metrics. Increasing volume beyond 5 mL did not improve microbial detection. These findings align with previous work indicating that urine volume does not strongly correlate with microbial DNA recovery [18]. Interestingly, a subset of 50 mL pelleted urine samples yielded relatively low sequencing depth. While the cause remains unclear, one possible explanation is that increased concentration of PCR inhibitors from the larger volumes interfered with downstream amplification [42]. Additional work evaluating alternative extraction and inhibitor removal approaches would provide more insights on these findings. Collectively, our results suggest that smaller urine volumes of 1–10 mL can yield microbial profiles comparable to those obtained from larger volumes, supporting their use when sample availability is limited.

Another major objective of this study was to compare two microbial concentration methods prior to DNA extraction: vacuum filtration and pelleting. Filtration is commonly used in other low biomass systems, such as aquatic environments, where it has been reported to improve microbial capture relative to pelleting [23–27]. However, those applications often involve substantially larger sample volumes than are feasible in urobiome research. Despite similar technical challenges, urobiome studies have largely relied on pellet-based approaches, with limited direct comparison to filtration. In the present study, filtration and pelleting yielded comparable bacterial DNA quantities and sequencing depth. However, filtered samples contained a higher proportion of reads classified as contaminants. This difference may reflect increased handling during filtration or background DNA introduced from reagents or consumables, a recognized issue in low biomass microbiome research [20, 43]. Although contamination can be mitigated through negative controls and computational filtering, accurate identification and removal of contaminant ASVs remains particularly challenging [2, 20, 37]. Accordingly, when comparable DNA recovery can be achieved, methods associated with lower contaminant burden may be preferable.

Apart from Shannon diversity, alpha diversity metrics did not differ between paired filtered and pelleted samples, indicating that both methods captured comparable within-sample diversity. The isolated difference in Shannon diversity may reflect subtle shifts in evenness or the contribution of rare taxa. However, the absence of differences in inverse Simpson diversity, observed richness, and differential abundance testing suggests that neither method systematically altered the presence or abundance of individual taxa. In contrast, differences in overall community composition were detected across multiple beta diversity distance metrics. Together with the differential abundance results, this pattern suggests that filtration and pelleting do not substantially affect any individual microbe but may introduce small, additive changes across the broader community. Importantly, effect sizes were modest (R2 < 5%) between processing methods, compared to substantially larger influence of individual urine batch (R2 values up to 71%), indicating that biological variation far outweighed methodological effects.

Taken together, these findings suggest that filtration followed by extraction of the filter captures microbial profiles broadly comparable to those obtained from pelleted urine. However, sample processing should be performed consistently within studies, and several technical considerations should be acknowledged. Notably, three samples could not be filtered at higher volumes (30–50 mL) due to filter clogging. This may reflect increased cellularity, crystals, or inflammatory debris in those samples, analogous to turbid aquatic samples that impede filtration. Although strategies such as larger pore-size filters (e.g., 0.45 μm), alternative filter substrates, or pre-filtration steps may reduce clogging, these approaches introduce their own limitations [24, 44]. For instance, larger pore sizes may risk loss of smaller bacterial taxa, and additional processing increases labor and handling complexity, potentially exacerbating contamination risk. In this study, a mixed cellulose ester filter was selected based on previous data supporting consistent microbial detection with this filter type, though other filter types were not tested [24].

The dominant effect of batch in our linear model limits the resolution of comparisons between volume and processing methods and may, in part, reflect cohort heterogeneity. Each urine batch contained samples from multiple dogs varying in age, sex, and breed, suggesting that batch effects are unlikely to be driven solely by demographic composition. Nonetheless, a more uniform cohort overall may have reduced inter-batch variability and allowed for clearer separation of technical effects from underlying biological variation. Batching urine samples from multiple dogs provided logistical advantages but also introduced important limitations when interpreting results. Combining samples enabled sufficient urine volume for technical replicates and expanded the range of taxa represented within each batch. However, this design limited assessment of host-level clinical or biological factors that may influence filtration performance. Concurrent urinalysis and urine culture data would strengthen interpretation of findings, such as filter clogging or shifts in community composition. For example, Batch C was dominated by a single Staphylococcus ASV, raising the possibility that unrecognized host factors, such as pyuria or subclinical bacteriuria, contributed to both microbial community structure and filtration dynamics within that batch. However, all dogs included in the study were clinically healthy, and dogs with lower urinary tract signs were excluded.

Additional limitations should also be considered when interpreting these findings. First, results may not generalize across different extraction kits or laboratory environments, as only a single DNA isolation workflow was evaluated. Second, although a commercial mock community confirmed successful recovery of known taxa, absolute DNA recovery efficiency was not quantified using standardized spike-in controls, limiting direct comparison of recovery performance between methods. Third, these findings in healthy canine urine may not extend to other species or to samples collected during inflammatory or neoplastic disease states. Urine from dogs with urolithiasis, neoplasia, or infection may contain increased host cellular material or debris [45, 46], which could differentially impact filtration performance. Fourth, due to practical limitations in urine collection, we were unable to include technical replicates at each volume and processing condition. Inclusion of replicates would have improved the robustness of comparisons by reducing stochastic variation and increasing power to detect subtle differences. Given that larger sample volumes (≥ 30 mL) were not consistently compatible with filtration, and that smaller volumes performed comparably, future work could prioritize additional technical replicates at lower volumes as a practical approach to improve study design and reliability. Lastly, because small-pore filtration would also capture host cells, high host biomass remains an unresolved technical challenge. While research in aquatic samples has used size fractionation of filtrate to separate eukaryotes, prokaryotes, and free viruses, their feasibility in urine remains uncertain and warrants further study [47].

Conclusions

In conclusion, smaller urine volumes (1–10 mL) of pelleted urine performed comparably to larger volumes, yielding similar sequence depth, bacterial DNA recovery, contaminant burden, and microbial diversity metrics. Although filtration and pelleting produced broadly similar microbial profiles, filtration was associated with increased contaminant signal and occasional clogging at higher volumes (≥ 30 mL), and also did not demonstrate superiority over pelleting for microbial recovery. Importantly, interindividual variation was the dominant driver of community composition, far exceeding methodological effects. Collectively, these findings suggest that urinary microbial profiles primarily reflect underlying biological variation, and that either processing method may be appropriate when paired with rigorous contaminant control. In addition, moderate urine volumes (1–10 mL) appear sufficient for urobiome characterization and may offer practical advantages over higher volumes.
