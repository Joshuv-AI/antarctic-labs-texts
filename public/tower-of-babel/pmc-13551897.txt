Large periampullary mass with vascular contact: Diagnostic and surgical decision-making in a solid pseudopapillary neoplasm
Authors: Imen Ben Ismail, Karim Karray, Mouna Mlika, Marwen Sghaier, Hakim Zenaidi, Saber Rebii
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLarge periampullary masses with indeterminate origin and vascular contact represent a diagnostic and surgical challenge. While solid pseudopapillary neoplasm (SPN) is a rare pancreatic tumor with favorable prognosis, its presentation in the pancreatic head may lead to diagnostic uncertainty and influence operative strategy. A 37-year-old woman presented with a palpable right upper quadrant mass without systemic symptoms. Imaging revealed a large solid–cystic lesion in the cephalic–isthmic pancreas, closely abutting the superior mesenteric vein and exerting a mass effect on the duodenum. The exact origin of the tumor remained uncertain, raising the possibility of a duodenal gastrointestinal stromal tumor. Given this ambiguity, endoscopic ultrasound-guided biopsy was performed and confirmed SPN. The patient underwent pancreaticoduodenectomy. Intraoperatively, the tumor was found to be well encapsulated and separable from the mesenteric vessels, allowing complete resection without vascular reconstruction. The postoperative course was uneventful, and histopathology confirmed SPN with negative margins. This case highlights the limitations of imaging in periampullary masses and underscores the value of preoperative tissue diagnosis when the origin of the lesion is unclear. It also illustrates that vascular contact does not necessarily indicate invasion in SPN and should not preclude surgical resection. In the setting of a large periampullary mass with vascular contact, careful diagnostic assessment, including selective use of endoscopic ultrasound, can guide appropriate surgical management; recognition that vascular contact in SPN frequently reflects displacement rather than true invasion is essential to avoid misclassification and to ensure optimal, appropriately assertive surgical management.Keywords: Solid pseudopapillary neoplasm, Pancreatic head, Frantz tumor, Pancreaticoduodenectomy, Endoscopic ultrasound, Perioperative management

Introduction

Solid pseudopapillary neoplasm (SPN) of the pancreas commonly referred to as Frantz tumor remains an infrequent diagnosis in pancreatic surgery, representing a small fraction of exocrine pancreatic tumors, estimated at 1%-3% in most series [1,2]. Since its original description by Virginia Kneeland Frantz, the lesion has been better delineated as a distinct entity with peculiar clinical behavior, combining indolent growth with a low but definite malignant potential [3].

SPN occurs predominantly in young women in their second to fourth decades of life [1,4], and cumulative molecular data implicate activating CTNNB1 mutations in the Wnt/β-catenin pathway as a consistent feature [5]. Clinical presentation is often nonspecific or entirely incidental [2,6], which contributes to the relatively large tumor size at diagnosis, particularly in the pancreatic head, where early obstructive symptoms are uncommon.

Cross-sectional imaging typically shows a well-demarcated solid-cystic lesion, reflecting intratumoral hemorrhage and degenerative changes [7]. but this appearance is not specific and may overlap with pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms or, less commonly, duodenal gastrointestinal stromal tumors [8]. Endoscopic ultrasound-guided tissue acquisition is a valuable adjunct in this setting, providing preoperative histological confirmation and facilitating surgical planning [9].

Complete surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment and is associated with excellent outcomes, even in locally advanced disease, provided vascular involvement remains resectable [4,10]. Pancreaticoduodenectomy is most often required for tumors of the pancreatic head, with long-term survival exceeding 95% after R0 resection [4,10].

In this context, we report a case of SPN located in the head of the pancreas, with emphasis on the diagnostic pathway and operative management in a setting where preoperative characterization may raise challenging differential diagnoses.

Case presentation

A 37-year-old woman with no significant past medical history presented with a progressively enlarging right upper quadrant abdominal mass. She denied weight loss, anorexia, gastrointestinal bleeding, or changes in bowel habits. There was no history of jaundice or abdominal pain. On physical examination, she appeared in good general condition. Abdominal palpation revealed a firm, nontender mass measuring approximately 10 cm in its largest diameter, located in the right hypochondrium. The mass was fixed to the deep plane, with no evidence of ascites or collateral venous circulation.

Laboratory investigations were unremarkable except for a mild normocytic anemia (hemoglobin 10.35 g/dL); liver function tests, pancreatic enzymes, and tumor markers were within normal limits.

Given the palpable abdominal mass and the absence of jaundice, biliary obstruction, or a pancreatitis-type presentation, contrast-enhanced computed tomography was obtained directly as the first-line cross-sectional imaging study, without preceding abdominal ultrasonography, in order to provide multiplanar characterization of a large mass with anticipated vascular contact. Contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography, including arterial and portal venous phases with axial and coronal reformats, demonstrated a well-defined, heterogeneous solid-cystic mass measuring 11 × 9 × 9 cm, arising from the cephalic-isthmic region of the pancreas (Fig. 1). The lesion abutted the superior mesenteric vein over less than 180° of its circumference, without contact with the superior mesenteric artery or portal vein (Fig. 2), and without a discrete capsule, calcification, or hemorrhagic component on imaging. The main pancreatic duct and common bile duct were of normal caliber, and no metastatic disease was identified. The mass exerted mass effect on the adjacent duodenum, without clear signs of vascular invasion. Given the size of the lesion and its close relationship with the duodenum, the exact origin of the mass remained uncertain on imaging, raising the possibility of a primary duodenal tumor, particularly a duodenal gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST). Features favoring SPN included the predominantly pancreatic epicenter of the mass and its solid-cystic architecture, whereas the intimate contact with the duodenal wall and the absence of an unequivocal pancreatic capsule kept a submucosal duodenal GIST in the differential. Other periampullary and pancreatic lesions considered included pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor, which typically shows more homogeneous hypervascularity and a purely intrapancreatic epicenter without this degree of cystic degeneration; mucinous cystic neoplasm, which is typically septated and centered in the body or tail rather than presenting as a solid-predominant cephalic mass; and pancreatic adenocarcinoma, considered unlikely given the absence of pancreatic duct dilatation, biliary obstruction, or an infiltrative, poorly marginated growth pattern.

In view of this diagnostic uncertainty and the potential implications for surgical strategy, endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) with tissue acquisition was performed. It revealed an approximately 8 cm heterogeneous mass with mixed echogenicity, composed of both hyperechoic and hypoechoic components, and demonstrating low Doppler vascularity of the solid component. The lesion was noted to be in intimate contact with the superior mesenteric vein. EUS-guided fine-needle biopsy was carried out under direct sonographic guidance targeting the solid component of the mass, and histological analysis confirmed the diagnosis of a solid pseudopapillary neoplasm.

The close relationship with the superior mesenteric vein raised the question of possible vascular involvement and resectability. After multidisciplinary discussion, the patient underwent a pancreaticoduodenectomy. Intraoperatively, the tumor was well circumscribed and encapsulated; careful dissection confirmed the absence of true vascular invasion, allowing preservation of the mesenteric venous axis and complete resection without vascular reconstruction (Fig. 3). No peritoneal or hepatic metastases were identified. The postoperative course was uneventful, with a total hospital stay of 10 days and no pancreatic fistula or other major complication.

(A) Intraoperative view demonstrating a well-encapsulated tumor located in the pancreatic head, with a clear dissection plane from the superior mesenteric vein. (B) Surgical specimen following pancreaticoduodenectomy, revealing a well-circumscribed mass with mixed solid and cystic components.

Histopathological examination of the surgical specimen confirmed the diagnosis of solid pseudopapillary neoplasm of the pancreas, measuring 10 cm in greatest dimension, with negative resection margins (closest margin 3 cm), no lymphovascular or perineural invasion, and no lymph node involvement (Fig. 4). Immunohistochemical staining was not performed; the diagnosis was established on the basis of characteristic morphology on both the EUS-guided biopsy and the resection specimen, and the differential diagnoses raised on imaging (duodenal GIST, pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor, mucinous cystic neoplasm, and pancreatic adenocarcinoma) were excluded on morphological grounds. At 8 months of follow-up, the patient remains asymptomatic with no evidence of recurrence.

Discussion

The interest of this case lies less in the pathological diagnosis itself than in the sequence of decisions that led to appropriate surgical management. In the setting of a large periampullary mass, the primary difficulty is often not technical resection, but rather accurate characterization of the lesion and assessment of resectability. In our patient, the combination of a voluminous mass, uncertain origin, and close relationship with the mesenteric vessels created a scenario in which both diagnosis and operative strategy required careful consideration.

Cross-sectional imaging suggested a solid–cystic lesion arising from the cephalic–isthmic pancreas, but did not allow definitive distinction between a pancreatic tumor and a lesion of duodenal origin. This type of ambiguity is not uncommon in large periampullary masses, where anatomical distortion may obscure the site of origin [7]. The initial hypothesis of a gastrointestinal stromal tumor was therefore plausible and would have implied a different surgical approach. In this context, relying solely on imaging could have led to inappropriate operative planning.

The decision to perform endoscopic ultrasound with tissue acquisition proved decisive. While preoperative biopsy is not systematically required for lesions suspected to be resectable pancreatic tumors, it becomes particularly relevant when the diagnosis is uncertain and may influence the surgical strategy [11]. In the present case, confirmation of a solid pseudopapillary neoplasm supported a formal oncologic resection and avoided unnecessary hesitation related to the possibility of a nonpancreatic tumor. More broadly, this highlights the role of endoscopic ultrasound as a problem-solving tool rather than a routine step, to be used selectively in complex presentations.

Another critical aspect was the interpretation of vascular contact. The proximity of the tumor to the superior mesenteric vein raised the question of potential vascular invasion and, by extension, resectability. In daily practice, such findings are often interpreted through the lens of pancreatic adenocarcinoma, where vascular involvement significantly alters management. However, extrapolating these criteria to all pancreatic tumors may be misleading. In SPN, close contact with adjacent vessels frequently reflects displacement rather than true infiltration [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. The intraoperative findings in our patient were consistent with this pattern, as a clear dissection plane was identified, allowing complete resection without vascular reconstruction.

A limitation of this report is that the diagnosis relied on hematoxylin-eosin morphology alone, without confirmatory immunohistochemistry (eg, nuclear β-catenin, CD10, progesterone receptor, vimentin, and Ki-67 index); although the morphological features were considered classic for SPN on both the EUS-guided biopsy and the resection specimen, immunohistochemical confirmation would have strengthened diagnostic certainty and is advisable in similar future cases, particularly when the differential with other periampullary tumors remains close.

This distinction has direct implications for surgical decision-making. Overestimation of vascular invasion may lead to unwarranted exclusion from surgery, whereas a more nuanced interpretation integrating tumor biology and imaging characteristics supports a more assertive approach. The literature, although not specifically focused on cephalic localization, consistently reports low rates of true vascular invasion in SPN, even in large tumors, with excellent outcomes following complete resection [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]].

From a technical standpoint, pancreaticoduodenectomy remains the reference procedure for tumors located in the pancreatic head [7]. In the present case, the indication was straightforward once the pancreatic origin of the tumor had been established. The absence of intraoperative difficulty despite preoperative concerns further illustrates that radiological appearance may overestimate operative complexity in this setting.

When viewed in light of previously published data, this case reinforces several practical points. First, periampullary masses with atypical imaging features should be approached with a broad differential diagnosis, particularly in young patients without systemic symptoms. Second, endoscopic ultrasound-guided biopsy can provide decisive information when imaging is inconclusive. Third, and perhaps most importantly, vascular contact should not be interpreted as a contraindication to surgery in suspected SPN.

Ultimately, the value of this observation lies in its contribution to clinical reasoning. Rather than representing an isolated rare entity, it illustrates a situation frequently encountered in practice: a large abdominal mass of uncertain origin, raising questions about diagnosis, resectability, and operative planning. Recognizing the specific behavior of SPN within this context helps refine decision-making and supports timely surgical management, which remains the main determinant of outcome.
