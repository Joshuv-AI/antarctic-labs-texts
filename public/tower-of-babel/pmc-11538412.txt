Development and validation of a novel AI framework using NLP with LLM integration for relevant clinical data extraction through automated chart review
Authors: Mert Marcel Dagli, Yohannes Ghenbot, Hasan S Ahmad, Daksh Chauhan, Ryan Turlip, Patrick Wang, William C Welch, Ali K Ozturk, Jang W Yoon
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe accurate extraction of surgical data from electronic health records (EHRs), particularly operative notes through manual chart review (MCR), is complex, crucial, and time-intensive, limited by human error due to fatigue and the level of training. This study aimed to develop and validate a novel Natural Language Processing (NLP) algorithm integrated with a Large Language Model (LLM; GPT4-Turbo) to automate the extraction of spinal surgery data from EHRs. The algorithm employed a two-stage approach. Initially, a rule-based NLP framework reviewed and classified candidate segments from the text, preserving their reference segments. These segments were then verified in the second stage through the LLM. The primary outcomes of this study were the accurate extraction of surgical data, including the type of surgery, levels operated, number of disks removed, and presence of intraoperative incidental durotomies. Secondary objectives explored time efficiency, tokenization lengths, and costs. The performance of the algorithm was assessed across two validation databases, analyzing metrics such as accuracy, sensitivity, discrimination, F1-score, and precision, with 95% confidence intervals calculated using percentile-based bootstrapping. The NLP + LLM algorithm markedly outperformed all performance metrics, demonstrating significant improvements in time and cost efficiency. Introduction

The accurate extraction and analysis of data from Electronic Health Records (EHRs) are essential for enhancing clinical decision-making, research, and quality control within healthcare systems1–8. Traditionally, manual chart review (MCR) has been the standard method to systematically review and gather patient information from EHRs. Despite its thoroughness, MCR is notably labor-intensive and time-consuming, frequently leading to significant bottlenecks in the care provision, continuity of care, research discovery, and quality improvement processes9. The human factors involved—including information fatigue due to the overwhelming volume of data, sleep deprivation, variability in performance linked to different stages of training, and the associated costs—are known to potentially adversely affect the quality and accuracy of the extracted data, subsequently affecting the derived analysis and conclusions10–13.

To address these inefficiencies, recent advancements in Artificial Intelligence (AI), specifically in Natural Language Processing (NLP) and Large Language Models (LLMs), have emerged as promising tools to automate and optimize the process of data extraction from EHRs9,14–18. These technologies have the potential to significantly reduce the time and labor associated with MCR, while also enhancing the reliability and accuracy of the data extracted19–21. By reducing human error, including information fatigue and variability in performance, NLP and LLMs could standardize the extraction process across healthcare systems and stages of medical training, ensuring a more uniform quality of data analysis and interpretation.

In this study we aimed to develop and validate a rule-based NLP algorithm with LLM integration for automating the extraction of spine surgery data from EHRs, conducting what is, to our knowledge the first and most comprehensive study on this topic. Secondary outcomes included exploration of time and cost efficiency metrics.

Methods

Guidelines

The design and reporting of this study were supported by the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis + Artificial Intelligence (TRIPOD + AI)22. Although TRIPOD + AI did not consider LLMs at the time of development, many of its principles are applicable and highly relevant in driving transparency in generative AI studies in healthcare. To our knowledge there are no specific reporting standards for the use of LLMs in healthcare yet.

Data source

The present study was reviewed by the University of Pennsylvania Institutional Review Board (IRB), who determined that it met criteria for full review exemption and subject informed consent waiver. All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Our two databases, which were used for validation, were previously established through an integrated data collection approach, involving automated data extraction of pre-specified variables through reports generated by our Data Analytics Center (DAC), complemented by a manual review of Electronic Health Record (EHR) charts.

Patient selection

The inclusion criteria for the first database consisted of a prospectively maintained database of patient records from surgeries performed between August 5, 2019, and July 24, 2023, specifically targeting adult patients undergoing endoscopic diskectomy for decompression23,24. For the second database, records of surgeries conducted from January 1, 2013, to December 13, 2021, were included, focusing on adult patients (age ≥ 18 years old) who underwent elective thoracolumbosacral posterior spinal fusion (PSF) involving six or more vertebrae for the correction of adult spinal deformity (ASD)25. Exclusions criteria were patients under 18 years of age, those with PSF involving fewer than six vertebrae, surgeries including a cervical component, or surgical interventions for indications other than deformity, such as tumors, trauma, or infections.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study were the development and validation of an NLP algorithm with LLM integration to automatically identify and extract information from operative notes, specifically the surgery type, levels operated on, number of disks removed, and presence of intraoperative incidental durotomies. Secondary outcomes included time until classification, tokenization lengths, and cost.

Variables

For each eligible patient in the diskectomy database, the following de-identified variables were extracted: type of surgery, levels operated on, number of disks removed, and operative notes. For each eligible patient in the ASD database, the variables extracted included type of surgery, intraoperative incidental durotomy, and operative notes.

Missing data

There was no missing data for the variables extracted from the diskectomy and ASD databases. All patient records were complete and included in our analysis.

Development

We developed a novel two-stage NLP algorithm with LLM integration. In the first stage, the NLP algorithm, rooted in a rule-based framework, classified candidates and extracted their reference text, followed by confirmation in the second stage using an LLM.

Stage 1 – natural language processing

After standard preprocessing of text, which removed leading and trailing whitespace, the algorithm parsed text-segments sentence for sentence using a fuzzy regex matching function26–28. The fuzzy component with a Levenshtein distance of 1 allowed to handle slight variations in nomenclature of keywords saved in a dictionary, with either one insertion or deletion, for example “diskectmy” = “diskectomyy” = “diskectomy” = “discectmy” is classified as a diskectomy. The regex component handled variations in spinal level pattern, such as “L2-3”=”L23”=”L2to3”=”L2 to L3”=”L2-L3”29–31. Data from matches and reference text segments identified by the fuzzy regex matching function were saved for every record. A merging function then combined levels and text segments from adjacent text segments that detailed different levels of operation, such as in “L2-L3 diskectomy. L34 diskectomy”. Additionally, a spinal order function automatically calculated the number of vertebrae and disks involved, based on the saved information from the fuzzy regex matching function32–34.

Stage 2 – large language model

To address the inherent limitations of a rule-based system in recognizing complex sentence structures and contextual nuances, a transformer-based model, specifically OpenAI’s GPT-4 Turbo (gpt-4-turbo) Application Programming Interface (API), was integrated35,36. Candidate classifications and their reference text segments, along with a few-shot prompt including examples, were used as input, and responses were retrieved through API requests handled concurrently by a multi-threading approach with 20 workers. Rate limits for requests per minute were adhered to through a counting and timeout function. Tokenization lengths of inputs and outputs were recorded using the tiktoken library17,37.

Analysis

Validation of primary outcomes involved the accurate identification and extraction of surgery type, levels operated on, and number of disks removed using the diskectomy database, and detection of intraoperative incidental durotomies using the ASD database. The NLP + LLM pipeline underwent 10-fold repetition (deduplication) to capture variations in performance and potential hallucinations, with results subsequently aggregated35,38. Ground truth for validation was established using a composite of manually extracted information by a resident research fellow for the diskectomy database and by a research year medical student for the ASD database, alongside NLP + LLM classifications, with all discrepancies resolved by a research fellow holding a medical doctorate who had not yet completed postgraduate clinical training39. Performance metrics such as accuracy, sensitivity, Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC-ROC), F1-score, and precision were determined, along with their 95% confidence intervals (CI) using percentile-based bootstrapping with 10,000 resamples. All development, statistical analysis, and the creation of figures were performed using Python, version 3.9 (Python Foundation, Wilmington, DE).

Results

Primary outcomes

We included 162 records from our diskectomy database. After the initial stage, the NLP algorithm correctly identified all procedure types and accurately classified spinal levels with a sensitivity of 0.975 (95% CI 0.950 to 0.994), misclassifying the spinal levels of four records. Following the integration of the LLM in stage two, the pipeline correctly identified and classified all records in eight out of ten replicates. The remaining two replicates each had one hallucination, being off by two levels, and totaled 1618 correct classifications out of 1620 with a sensitivity of 0.999 (95% CI 0.994 to 1.000). The pipeline outperformed a blinded neurosurgery resident in their research year (PGY-5), who achieved a sensitivity of 0.981 (95% CI 0.957 to 1.000), misclassifying the spinal levels of three records.

We included 485 records from our ASD database, among which 43 patients had recorded intraoperative incidental durotomies. In the initial stage, the NLP algorithm identified 73 candidates, achieving a sensitivity of 1.000 (95% CI 1.000 to 1.000) and a precision (PPV) of 0.644 (95% CI 0.537 to 0.750; Table 1). Following the integration of the LLM, the pipeline correctly classified all records in nine out of ten replicates. The remaining replicate featured one hallucination, resulting in one misclassified record (false negative; Fig. 1; Table 2).

Secondary outcomes

The self-reported estimate of the resident’s time until classifications during manual EHR chart review was three minutes per record, totaling 29,160 s. The natural language processing algorithm took 30 s to identify candidates with the LLM taking 9.6 s to correct classifications, totaling 39.6 s.

The self-reported estimate of the medical student’s time until classifications during EHR manual chart review was four minutes per record, totaling 116,400 s. The natural language processing algorithm took 30 s to identify candidates with the LLM taking 4.6 s to correct classifications, totaling 34.6 s.

In our study, we analyzed the tokenization length and costs associated with LLM inputs for diskectomy classification and intraoperative incidental durotomy classification. For the diskectomy classification, the tokenization length of the prompt template was 40,411 tokens for a single replicate, with the extracted text segments totaling 34,860 tokens (Fig. 2). Consequently, the overall input prompt comprised 75,485 tokens. At a cost rate of $10.00 per million tokens using GPT-4-turbo, the expense for a single replicate was approximately $0.75, and $7.55 for ten replicates. The outputs for the inputted candidates amounted to 709 tokens for a single replicate and 7,090 tokens for ten replicates, costing $0.02 and $0.21 respectively. In contrast, using the full lengths of operative notes would have resulted in an input of 385,159 tokens per replicate, escalating the cost to $3.85 for one replicate and $38.52 for ten.

Comparison of mean tokenization lengths of reference text in input prompts per record using natural language processing with large language model integration (NLP + LLM) versus full-length operative notes in input prompts not restricted to candidates (Full Entry) for diskectomy classifications (A) and intraoperative incidental durotomy classifications (B) during validation.

Similarly, for the classification of intraoperative incidental durotomy, the prompt template consisted of 13,724 tokens per replicate. The NLP-extracted text segments used for LLM input amounted to 6,448 tokens, totaling 20,167 tokens for the overall input prompt (Fig. 2). This led to a cost of $0.20 for a single replicate and $2.02 for ten replicates at the same token pricing. Output tokenization was 73 tokens for a single replicate and 730 tokens for ten, incurring costs of $0.002 and $0.02 respectively. Without restricting inputs to candidates identified by NLP, the full operative note lengths would have resulted in 1,820,938 tokens for a single replicate and 18,209,380 for ten replicates, significantly increasing the cost to $18.21 for a single replicate and $182.10 for ten. Correspondingly, the output tokenization would have risen to 485 tokens for a single replicate and 4,850 tokens for ten, totaling $0.01 and $0.15 respectively.

Discussions

To the best of our knowledge, the present investigation was the first study on the development and validation of an NLP algorithm with LLM integration to automate extraction of spinal surgery data from EHR operative notes. Our findings demonstrated that NLP + LLM algorithm accurately identified and classified surgical information, such as intraoperative incidental durotomies. Notably, the integration of OpenAI’s GPT-4 Turbo significantly enhanced the capabilities of the rule-based NLP framework to understand and process complex medical texts, which traditionally pose challenges due to their intricate language, contextual nuances, and specialized terminologies16–18. Additionally, this integration yielded a system that was not only markedly time efficient but also economically viable. While previous studies have reported inconsistent performance in processing varied datasets, our approach suggests that more advanced methods can effectively address these challenges in complex documentation16–18.

In our study, we successfully developed and validated an NLP algorithm with LLM integration (GPT4-Turbo) for automating the extraction of spinal surgery data from EHR operative notes. The primary outcomes included the accurate identification and classification of surgery type, levels operated on, number of disks removed, and detection of intraoperative incidental durotomies. The NLP + LLM algorithm achieved a sensitivity of 0.999 (95% CI 0.994 to 1.000) in the diskectomy database and 0.998 (95% CI 0.984 to 1.000) in the ASD database during decuplicate aggregated validation, notably outperforming medical professionals in training. Additional performance metrics, including accuracy, discrimination, F1-score, and precision demonstrated equally as high results. Several studies have underscored the challenges of MCR in terms of accuracy9,40. These findings suggest that the integration of advanced AI technologies can provide consistent and reliable data extraction capabilities.

The secondary outcomes of our study, which included time until classification, tokenization lengths, and cost-effectiveness of the NLP + LLM algorithm compared to traditional MCR and full-text entry, demonstrated significant improvements in both time efficiency and cost reduction. The average processing time for a single replicate from the ASD database with 485 records was 34.6 s—significantly less than the 116,400 s required by a medical student in their research year, reflecting a reduction factor exceeding 3,000 times. Economically, the NLP + LLM pipeline showed substantial cost-savings over the LLM full-text approach. Specifically, processing the input and output for ten replicates involved in classifying intraoperative incidental durotomies incurred costs of only $2.04 (20,167 tokens input, 730 tokens output), compared to $182.25 for the full-text entry method (1,820,938 tokens input, 730 tokens output). Comparative studies have traditionally underscored the significant labor costs associated with MCR, often identifying them as a major obstacle to scalable healthcare analytics9,41–43.These findings suggest a transformative potential of AI-integrated systems not only to enhance the accuracy of medical data extraction but also to achieve such enhancements with remarkable cost- and time-efficiency, thereby facilitating broader adoption and scalability across healthcare systems.

The findings of this study should be considered within the context of its limitations, despite the innovative approach it introduces to medical data extraction. Although this is the first study on this topic, to the authors’ knowledge, its generalizability may require modifications to adapt to different clinical and technological environments. The system’s framework is deliberately designed to facilitate such adaptations, enhancing its potential applicability across diverse settings. While the algorithm demonstrated exceptional performance within the structured environment of this study, its application in real-world settings might encounter unforeseen challenges not represented in the research environment, necessitating ongoing adjustments and validations to ensure its effectiveness and reliability. Additionally, the evaluation was limited to a single LLM, specifically GPT4-Turbo, which may influence the breadth of the findings and suggests the need for further comparative studies with other models.

Future directions for our NLP + LLM system include scalability to ensure its effective deployment across different surgery types, departments, and healthcare systems, beyond spinal surgery. Review and revision of language variations encoded in the dictionary component, based on stakeholder input and desired outcomes, allow for efficient adjustment to meet local needs. To evaluate and enhance the pipeline’s performance, future validation studies are planned to conduct comparatives analysis comparing different LLMs, context windows, and prompting styles, systematically evaluating hallucinations as we scale the application. Additionally, postoperative billing processes are being investigated to potentially automate them and improve cost-efficiency. Optimization of financial operations, could potentially fund its scalability, cover computations costs, and foster wider adoption, thereby contributing significantly to the future landscape of AI-enhanced medical data management. These efforts will include robust monitoring and correction strategies such as a majority vote mechanism for data extraction tasks, which was an effective option in the present study, and continuous re-evaluation of the system, particularly in areas like postoperative billing where unique challenges may arise. A prospective silent phase might be required to assess utility and monitor performance, especially from a medico-legal perspective. To ensure patient privacy when integrating with GPT-4 Turbo, data underwent de-identification initially, with reference segments extracted by the NLP algorithm excluding any protected health information (PHI). For actual clinical applications, despite the NLP framework’s safeguards to remove PHI, executing a HIPAA-compliant Business Associate Agreement (BAA) with OpenAI or any other entity deploying an LLM is crucial to secure patient privacy and information, as any errors could be catastrophic, and is in process.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed and validated a novel NLP algorithm with LLM integration, specifically GPT-4 Turbo, to automate the extraction of spinal surgery data from EHRs. The algorithm demonstrated exceptional accuracy, sensitivity, discrimination, precision, and efficiency in both time and cost, significantly outperforming traditional MCR. These results suggest the potential for widespread adoption of this technology. Follow-up studies are actively investigating the automation and optimization of postoperative billing to further support the scalability and broader implementation of this system.
