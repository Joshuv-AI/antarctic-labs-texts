Improved laser capture microdissection combined with transcriptome sequencing reveals gene expression reprogramming during rice panicle branch meristems differentiation
Authors: Gui Yan, Tao Song, Lei Xu, Jinbo Li, Binliang Wan, Lei Zhou, Haidan Wu, Yachun Zhang, Jiaojiao Zhao, Liqi Tong, Liushang He, Zhaojian Song, Xianhua Zhang, Wei Wang, Detian Cai, Yuchi He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe number of branches per panicle exhibits a strong positive correlation with the grain number per panicle, which is a key agronomic trait determining rice yield. Efficient and precise identification of genes regulating the development of panicle branch meristems will contribute to high-yield rice breeding.MethodHere, we present an optimized laser capture microdissection (LCM) method capable of extracting high-quality mRNA, along with 3': 5' assay for assessing mRNA integrity in rice LCM samples. Base on this method, we successfully isolated high integrity mRNA from inflorescence meristems, primary branch meristems and secondary branch meristems, which were suitable for RNA-seq.ResultsWe observed extensive transcriptome reprogramming during the differentiation of inflorescence meristems into branch meristems. Numerous phytohormone biosynthesis, transport, and response genes exhibited significant differential expression across the three types of meristems. The results of LCM-qPCR validated the accuracy of the transcriptome sequencing data. We selected three genes from the aforementioned differentially expressed genes for RNA in situ hybridization analysis, the results demonstrated their elevated expression in both primary and secondary panicle branch meristems, suggesting their likely role in regulating the differentiation trajectory from inflorescence meristems to branch meristems.ConclusionWe have established an optimized LCM protocol that enables the isolation of high-quality RNA from young rice panicles, roots, and stems. Using this method, we successfully isolated inflorescence meristems, primary branch meristems, and secondary branch meristems from young rice panicles for transcriptome sequencing. Among the differentially expressed genes identified by transcriptome analysis, a number of key genes known to regulate panicle branch meristems development were detected, demonstrating the feasibility of our established LCM method and its applicability in identifying key genes regulating rice panicle development. Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) stands as one of the world’s most vital food crop [1, 2], sustaining nearly one-third of the global population. Rice yield is collectively determined by three principal agronomic traits: productive panicle number per plant, grain number per panicle, and thousand-grain weight (the weight of 1000 fully filled grains). Among them, grains per panicle is a flexible component that depends on the quantity of primary and secondary branches differentiated [3–5]. Rice panicles exhibit a conical inflorescence structure, during the transition from vegetative to reproductive growth, the shoot apical meristem (SAM), which typically generates foliage leaves and tillers, undergoes a transformation into an inflorescence meristem (IM). Subsequently, the IM sequentially differentiates into primary branch meristems (PBM), secondary branch meristems (SBM), and spikelet meristems (SM) [6–8].

To date, researchers have cloned a series of genes that act as positive or negative regulators of rice panicle branch meristems development, such as: SQUAMOSA PROMOTER BINDING PROTEIN LIKE genes (OsSPL7, OsSPL14, OsSPL17), ABERRANT PANICLE ORGANIZATION1 (OsAPO1), OsAPO2, and GRAIN NUMBER ASSOCIATED (OsGNA), positively regulate branch meristem indeterminacy [9–12]. Loss-of-function mutants of these genes cause the precocious transition to IM fate, resulting in the small panicles with a reduced number of branches. OsSAMS1 encodes S-Adenosyl-L-Methionine Synthetase and negatively regulates the development of panicle branch meristems by modulating ethylene synthesis in the panicle [13]. Compared to wild rice, the promoter region of OsFZP in cultivated rice contains a 4-bp deletion. This deletion impairs binding of the upstream transcription factor OsARF6, leading to reduced OsFZP expression in cultivated rice [14]. Consequently, cultivated rice develops more panicle branches than wild rice. Overexpression of either OsSAMS1 or OsFZP resulted in a significant reduction in panicle branch number compared to the wild-type. Most of the aforementioned genes were identified through map-based cloning or genome-wide association studies (GWAS). However, these approaches typically require the construction of specialized genetic populations or large-scale natural populations, which is time-consuming and labor-intensive [15, 16]. Moreover, number of grains per panicle is a classic quantitative trait that is highly susceptible to environmental influences, often leading to inaccurate genetic mapping. Transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) enables rapid identification of candidate genes regulating panicle branch meristems development by analyzing gene expression differences among distinct meristem types. This knowledge gap persists largely because rice branch meristems develop rapidly and are microscopically small, making it challenging to precisely isolate distinct meristem types for transcriptome sequencing and comparative gene expression analysis [17–20]. In recent years, single-cell RNA sequencing of the young rice panicle has enabled researchers to systematically delineate a dynamic panorama of early inflorescence development and reconstruct the developmental trajectories of diverse cell populations within the rice panicle [21]. Key regulatory genes, such as OsDWT1 and OsAUX1, which govern the transition from the IM to the branch meristem BM, have been identified [22, 23]. However, the absence of specific marker genes currently limits the precise resolution of particular cell subtypes in the panicle—such as primary and secondary branch meristem cells—in single-cell datasets. Moreover, the inherent nature of single-cell sequencing, which involves dissociating cells from their native tissue context, leads to the loss of critical spatial information [24, 25].

Laser capture microdissection (LCM) is a cutting-edge spatial biology technique that enables rapid, precise isolation of target regions from heterogeneous tissues—ranging from single cells to cell populations—with exceptional resolution [26–28]. Consequently, LCM has become a powerful tool for investigating cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying tissue development [29–32]. Therefore, Laser Capture Microdissection (LCM) coupled with transcriptome sequencing is an exceptionally suitable approach for analyzing gene expression differences in rice panicle branch meristem. In the study, we present an optimized LCM-based method for obtaining high-integrity mRNA from rice panicle meristems. By integrating this approach with smart-seq technology, we efficiently identified key candidate genes regulating rice panicle branch meristems development.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The high-yielding super rice cultivar ‘Jigeng88’ (Oryza sativa L. ssp. japonica) served as experimental material throughout this investigation. Seeds were germinated at 28℃ under constant light. After three days, they were transferred to soil. Stems and roots from a subset of seedlings were sampled for 3': 5' assay, The remaining seedlings were transplanted to experimental fields at Hubei University during the rice-growing season in 2024 to collect young panicles at the panicle initiation stage for 3': 5' assay, RNA-seq, LCM-qPCR, and RNA in situ.

To construct overexpression vectors for OsMADS32, RNA from the ZH11 inflorescence at secondary branch development stage was isolated, and complementary DNA (cDNA) generated as previously described. Coding sequence for the gene were amplified (primers in Table S4) and cloned into the overexpression vector pCAMBIA1300 under control of the cauliflower mosaic virus 35S promoter using the In-Fusion cloning Kit. Overexpression constructs were introduced into ZH11 calli using Agrobacterium-mediated transformation and selection by hygromycin B. A total of 18 T1 lines were obtained for 35S::OsMADS32 in ZH11, and three lines were chosen for further analysis based on high levels of transgene expression in young panicle.

Verify that the oligonucleotides do not have the potential to cross-hybridize across different reactions. The three amplified fragments were individually subcloned into the pGEM-T vector (cat. A3600, Promega, Madison, WI, USA) using the In-Fusion cloning kit (cat. 638,948, Takara, Dalian, China) and transformed into electrocompetent E.coli DH5α. Additionally, to remove genomic DNA impurities, which might affect accurate photometric plasmid quantification. The plasmid DNA concentration was measured with PicoGreen on a NanoDrop3300 fluorospectrometer (cat. ND3300, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Thermal cycling conditions comprised: initial denaturation at 95℃ for 20 s, 1cycle; 40 cycles of 95℃ for 1 s and 60℃ for 20 s. Total RNA was isolated from rice tissues, including inflorescence meristems, root stele cells, and stem phloem sieve elements, that were dissected by laser capture microdissection. The Arcturus™ PicoPure® RNA Isolation Kit (cat. KIT0204, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used for RNA isolation. Laser capture microdissection

Samples were fixed in ice-cold CAA solution (chloroform: acetone: acetic acid = 20:60:20, v/v) at 4℃ for 12 h. After fixation, tissues were dehydrated through a graded acetone: ethanol series (25%, 50%, and 75% acetone in ethanol; followed by 100% ethanol) at 4℃ for 1 h per step. Dehydrated samples were then gradually infiltrated with Steedman's wax (low-melting-point wax; Table S1) at 42℃ using a series of ethanol: wax mixtures (25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% wax, v/v), each for 6 h. Finally, tissues were embedded in pure Steedman's wax for sectioning. Tissue blocks were sectioned at 8 µm, mounted on slides covered with Polyethylene Naphthalate (PEN) membrane (cat.11505158, Leica, Richmond, IL, USA), using RNase-free water for tissue section flattening and 37℃ 6 h, ethanol dewaxing. All steps were performed under RNase-free conditions. Inflorescence meristems, primary branch meristems, and secondary branch meristems were microdissected using the LCM system (cat. LMD710, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Laser condition for microdissection: laser Power set at 41, aperture at 9; Cutting Speed at 28; specimen balance at 13; pulse frequency at 3064.

LCM RNA Extraction and LCM RNA-seq

cDNA pre-amplification was performed using the SMARTer™ Ultra Low Input RNA Kit (cat. 634,889, Takara, Dalian, China) with minor modifications: RNA samples underwent first-strand cDNA synthesis followed by 13 cycles of Long Distance (LD) PCR. Amplified cDNA was purified using AMPure® XP beads (cat. A63881, Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, CA, USA), eluted in 15 μL nuclease-free water, and stored at −20 °C for library construction. Libraries were prepared using the TruSeq® RNA Library Prep Kit and clustered on the cBot system with TruSeq® SR Cluster Kit v4 (8 pM pooled libraries; Illumina). Paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp reads) was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 5000 platform. Raw FASTQ files were aligned to the Oryza sativa reference genome (IRGSP 1.0; RAP-DB: https://rapdb.dna.affrc.go.jp) using HISAT2 v2.2.1 [33]. Resultant BAM files were quantified via featureCounts v2.0.0 with IRGSP-1.0 representative transcript annotations (GTF release: 2019–08–29) [34]. Differential expression analysis were performed using DESeq2 (v1.30.1) [35]. Based on the KEGG database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg), the statistical enrichment of differentially expressed genes in the KEGG pathway was tested by ClusterProfiler (3.8.1) software.

Laser capture microdissection-coupled quantitative real-time PCR

In order to verify the gene expression level revealed by transcriptome data, the cDNA samples derived from inflorescence meristems, primary branch meristems, and secondary branch meristems were used as templates for real-time PCR. The internal control gene used for RT- PCR was OsUBQ (LOC_Os03g13170). Quantitative Real time PCR was performed using SYBR Premix Ex Taq™ (cat. CN830S, Takara, Dalian, China) on a XP2300 detection system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Each 25 μL reaction contained: 12.5 μL Premix Ex Taq™ Fast qPCR Mix, 1 μL forward primer (10 μM), 1 μL reverse primer (10 μM), 1 μL cDNA template (100 ng), and nuclease-free water to volume. Thermal cycling conditions comprised: initial denaturation at 95℃ for 30 s; 40 cycles of 95℃ for 5 s and 60℃ for 10 s; followed by melt curve analysis (65℃ to 95℃, 0.5℃ increments). Relative expression levels of genes were quantitated based on three biological replicates via the 2-ΔΔCt method. The primers used for qRT-PCR validation are provided in Table S2.

RNA in situ hybridization

Young rice panicle tissues were collected at approximately 50–60 days after sowing and fixed for 3–5 days at 4 °C in RNase-free FAA solution (formaldehyde-acetic acid-alcohol). Samples underwent graded ethanol dehydration, followed by xylene clearance, and were embedded in Paraplast Plus embedding medium (cat. P3683, Sigma-Aldrich (Merck KGaA), St. Louis, MO, USA) at 60 °C for 4 days. Sections (8 μm thickness) were prepared using rotary microtome. For probe synthesis, Specific fragments of OsMADS32, OsARF2, and OsARF14 were amplified using a forward primer containing the SP6 promoter sequence and a reverse primer containing the T7 promoter sequence, respectively (Table S3). Purified PCR products served as templates for in vitro transcription. Following the manufacturer's protocol of the RNA labeling kit (cat. 10,999,644,001, Roche, Mannheim, Germany), digoxigenin -labeled sense and antisense RNA probes were synthesized using SP6 and T7 RNA polymerases, respectively. The probes were hydrolyzed to an average size of 150 bp using an alkaline buffer solution (0.2 M Na2CO3/NaHCO3, pH10.2). In situ hybridization was performed on longitudinal young panicle sections. Hybridized probes were detected with anti-digoxigenin-AP conjugate and visualized by NBT/BCIP chromogenic reaction.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Fisher’s Least Significant Difference (LSD) post hoc test. Statistical significance was defined as p-value < 0.05.

Analysis of mRNA integrity for rice LCM samples

Conventional RNA integrity assessment relies on electropherogram features from the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, including 18S/28S ribosomal RNA area ratios, 28S peak height, fast area fraction, and marker height, which collectively determine the RNA Integrity Number (RIN) [36, 37]. However, RIN values face increasing contention as they primarily reflect ribosomal subunit integrity rather than mRNA quality [38]. Furthermore, minute RNA concentrations from laser-capture microdissected (LCM) samples (typically picogram-range) preclude reliable RIN determination using standard Agilent systems. Therefore, here, we established a 3':5' assay to evaluate mRNA integrity in rice LCM samples using RT-qPCR (Fig. 1a). We selected the following two genes as reference genes: OsGAPDH (LOC_Os08g03290) and OsAOC (LOC_Os03g32314). OsGAPDH encodes glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase 1 and exhibits constitutive expression as a single-transcript gene, with a full-length mRNA of 1.6 Kb. OsAOC encodes allene oxide cyclase, with a full-length mRNA of 1.3 Kb. It is expressed in rice young panicles, stems, and roots, and also possesses only a single transcript. An optimized LCM method for high-integrity mRNA isolation demonstrates broad applicability across rice tissues

Current laser capture microdissection (LCM) utilizes either frozen or paraffin-embedded sections. While frozen sections preserve mRNA integrity, subzero ice crystal formation compromises cellular architecture, yielding suboptimal morphology that increases non-target cell contamination during microdissection. Paraffin sections maintain superior histology but incur RNA degradation through fixation, dehydration, and embedding protocols. We present an optimized LCM method capable of isolating high-quality mRNA from rice tissues: samples were fixed in ice-cold CAA solution (20% chloroform: 60% acetone: 20% acetic acid, v/v) for 12 h at 4 °C, followed by graded dehydration in acetone: ethanol series (25%, 50%, 75% acetone; 100% ethanol; 1 h per step) at 4 °C. Dehydrated tissues underwent graded Steedman's wax (low-melting-point wax, Table S1) infiltration via ethanol: wax mixtures (25%, 50%, 75%, 100% wax; 6 h per step) at 42 °C before final embedded in 100% Steedman's wax. Comparative analysis demonstrates that our protocol significantly enhances mRNA integrity of rice samples versus conventional LCM protocol (samples were fixed in FAA solution (formaldehyde: glacial acetic acid: 50% ethanol = 1: 1: 18, v/v) or Carnoy's solution (glacial acetic acid: ethanol = 1: 3, v/v), followed by graded dehydration in gradient ethanol and embedded in paraffin. Sections of 8–10 μm thickness were cut using a rotary microtome and mounted on PEN slides for subsequent LCM) (Fig. 2d). Validation across rice young panicle, central cylinder of root and phloem tissue of stem confirmed Our method proves highly effective when applied to key rice tissues such as roots, stems, and young panicles (Fig. 2a-c).

Transcriptome sequencing validated the reliability of the optimized LCM-RNA method

Based on transcriptome sequencing data, we identified significant gene expression differences among IM, PBM, and SBM, indicating extensive transcriptome reprogramming during the developmental transition from IM to both PBM and SBM. Compared with IM, PBM exhibited 251 upregulated and 633 downregulated genes. Relative to IM, SBM showed 193 upregulated and 590 downregulated genes. When comparing PBM and SBM, we observed 163 upregulated and 262 downregulated genes in SBM (Fig. 3a, b). We have labeled 10 key genes previously reported to regulate rice panicle branch meristem development in Fig. 3a MA-plots: OsCKX11 (LOC_Os08g35860), OsARF11 (LOC_Os04g56850), OsMADS5 (LOC_Os06g11330), OsMADS22(LOC_Os02g52340), OsLAX1(LOC_Os01g61480), OsAPO2 (LOC_Os04g51000), OsMADS55 (LOC_Os06g11330), OsGIF1 (LOC_Os03g52320), OsPIN1c (LOC_Os11g04190), and OsPIN1d (LOC_Os12g04000). Among these, OsMADS55 (LOC_Os06g11330) and OsMADS22 (LOC_Os02g52340) exhibited consistently decreased expression during the transition from IM to SBM, while the remaining eight genes showed progressively increased expression during this developmental transition. These results further validate the feasibility of using laser microdissection combined with transcriptome sequencing to identify key genes regulating rice panicle branch meristem development.

In order to classify the functions of DEGs, we used KEGG (Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes classification) pathway annotation to analyze the gene function enrichment. On the basis of KEGG, a large number of differentially DEGs were significantly enriched in the plant hormone signal transduction pathway. Phytohormones are important regulators of branch meristem numbers (Fig. 3c). In this study, we observed significantly elevated expression of OsPIN1c and OsPIN1d in the PBM and SBM compared to the IM, suggesting that auxin accumulation at panicle branch meristem initiation sites may promote meristem differentiation. As auxin-responsive transcription factors, OsARFs are induced by auxin and function as positive or negative regulators of downstream gene expression. We identified five OsARFs exhibiting differential expression across IM, PBM, and SBM: OsARF2, OsARF11, and OsARF14 showed significantly higher expression in PBM/SBM than in IM, whereas OsARF1 and OsARF18 were substantially downregulated in PBM/SBM. OsIAA proteins act as repressors of OsARF activity, accordingly, OsIAA6, OsIAA16, OsIAA21, and OsIAA24 displayed marked expression differences among the three meristem types. Beyond auxin pathway genes, cytokinin- and gibberellin-related genes were differentially expressed, including the cytokinin oxidase/dehydrogenase gene OsCKX11, A-type response regulators (OsRR2, OsRR5), gibberellin 20-oxidases (OsGA20ox6, OsGA20ox7, OsGA20ox8), and GA-stimulated transcripts (OsGASR1, OsGASR9). These findings suggest that cytokinin and gibberellin play critical roles in branch meristems differentiation in rice.

Additionally, we identified differentially expressed MADS-box genes (OsMADS2, OsMADS5, OsMADS32, OsMADS16, OsMADS22, OsMADS55) and multiple OsWRKY family transcription factor genes (OsWRKY68, OsWRKY34, OsWRKY90, OsWRKY102). Notably, OsMADS2, OsMADS5, OsMADS32, and OsWRKY68 were upregulated in branch meristems (PBM/SBM), while OsMADS16, OsMADS22, OsMADS55, OsWRKY34, OsWRKY90, and OsWRKY102 were downregulated relative to IM. Within the aforementioned differentially expressed gene set, OsARF11, OsPIN1c, OsPIN1d and OsGASR9 have established roles in promoting panicle branch meristems differentiation [39, 40], whereas OsCKX11, OsGA2ox6, OsMADS5, OsMADS50, OsMADS22, OsMADS47, OsMADS55, and OsMADS34 function as repressors [41–44]. These findings not only validate the utility of comparative transcriptomics across IM, PBM, and SBM for identifying meristem-regulatory genes but also nominate OsARF2, OsARF14, OsARF18, OsIAA6, OsMADS32, and OsWRKY34 as candidate genes of branch meristems differentiation.

Laser capture microdissection-coupled quantitative real-time PCR (LCM-qPCR) confirmation of DEGs

RNA-seq reveals the expression profiles of thousands of genes. To verify the RNA-Seq results, we selected ten differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from the up-regulated and down-regulated genes among IM, PBM, and SBM for LCM-qPCR (Fig. 4). These genes are involved in basic plant metabolism, hormone signal transduction, gene expression regulation, and other processes. Consistent with the transcriptome data (Table 2), the expression levels of the OsPIN1C and OsPIN1d, as well as the OsARF11, were significantly higher in PBM and SBM than in IM. In contrast, the expression levels of the OsIAA16 and OsIAA24 were significantly lower in PBM and SBM than in IM. The expression levels of MADS-box family genes OsMADS5, OsMADS32, OsMADS55, and OsGRF1 were significantly higher in PBM and SBM than in IM, further confirming the critical roles of the MADS-box and GRF (GROWTH-REGULATING FACTOR-interacting factor) gene families in regulating rice inflorescence development. Overall, the LCM-qPCR results were consistent with the high-throughput sequencing data, indicating the reliability of the LCM/RNA-Seq analysis.

The expression levels of DEGs in RNA-Seq

The spatiotemporal expression patterns of the candidate genes

Three genes exhibiting differential expression across the inflorescence meristem (IM), primary branch meristem (PBM), and secondary branch meristem (SBM), yet previously unreported in the regulation of branch meristems development-OsMADS32, OsARF2, and OsARF14-were selected for RNA in situ hybridization analysis, OsWRKY117—a gene with almost no expression in IM, PBM, and SBM (FPKM < 1,Table 2) according to our transcriptome data—as a negative control. (Fig. 5). Consistent with transcriptomic sequencing and LCM-qPCR data, robust hybridization signals were specifically detected within the primary branch meristems and secondary branch meristems of developing young panicles. This signal intensity was significantly higher than that in the inflorescence meristem. OsMADS32 is specifically expressed in the secondary branch meristems; OsARF2 is highly expressed in both primary and secondary branch meristems; OsARF14 is specifically expressed in the primary branch meristems. As expected, the negative control OsWRKY117 exhibited no obvious signals in the inflorescence meristem, primary branch meristems, or secondary branch meristems. Functional validation of candidate gene

OsMADS32 has been previously characterized as a key regulator of floret development, but its potential role in earlier stages of panicle branch meristem differentiation has not been explored. To validate the function of candidate genes identified through transcriptomic analysis, we generated overexpression lines of OsMADS32 and selected three T1 lines (OsMADS32-OE-2, OsMADS32-OE-6, OsMADS32-OE-10) with high expression levels (Fig. 6c) for agronomic trait evaluation (Fig. 6a, b). Compared with the wild type, these three overexpression lines showed no significant differences in the number of primary branches per panicle or thousand-grain weight. However, the number of secondary branches per panicle was significantly increased in all three lines, resulting in a significant increase in grains per panicle (Fig. 7). These findings indicate that OsMADS32 acts as a positive regulator of panicle secondary branch number in rice when its expression is elevated. Discussion

During cell fate transitions in plants, dramatic transcriptome reprogramming typically occurs, characterized by significant upregulation or downregulation of key regulatory genes [45–48]. Consequently, comparing transcriptomes across distinct cell types enables the precise identification of these critical regulators. Rice panicle development involves the sequential differentiation of the IM into the PBM and SBM, a process determining key yield traits. The identification and utilization of key genes regulating the differentiation of the IM into PBM and SBM hold significant potential for substantially enhancing rice yield potential. However, the specific genes governing the fate determination of the PBM and SBM remain largely unknown, primarily due to substantial technical challenges. The developing rice panicle is encased within the culm, and the critical differentiation events of PBM and SBM are completed at a very early stage, when the young panicle is only 1–2 mm in length. This early developmental timing and physical enclosure make it extremely difficult to precisely determine the onset of PBM and SBM differentiation. Furthermore, the young rice panicle is a complex inflorescence in which the primary and secondary branch meristems are tightly clustered around the inflorescence meristem and are covered by dense trichomes. This intricate morphology presents a major obstacle to the precise isolation of the IM, PBM, and SBM tissues while simultaneously preserving mRNA integrity and preventing degradation.

Laser capture microdissection (LCM) enables precise isolation of tissue samples down to single-cell resolution. This capability makes it particularly suitable for analyzing gene expression differences among various rice inflorescence meristems when combined with SMART-seq. While cryo-sectioning is commonly employed for LCM due to its superior preservation of mRNA integrity, plant tissues present unique challenges. Unlike animal cells, plant cells possess rigid cell walls and large, water-filled vacuoles. Cryopreservation significantly increases tissue brittleness and promotes extensive ice crystal formation [49]. Consequently, sectioning often causes cellular rupture and irregular tissue morphology, hindering precise LCM isolation of specific structures. Furthermore, damaged cells release endogenous RNases, leading to mRNA degradation. Conversely, paraffin sectioning optimally preserves cellular morphology. However, traditional protocols rely on formaldehyde fixation and paraffin embedding. Formaldehyde induces nucleic acid cross-linking, complicating mRNA isolation [50–52], while the high embedding temperatures (> 60℃) typically cause severe mRNA degradation. To address these limitations, we developed an improved fixation solution, designated CAA solution (20% chloroform: 60% acetone: 20% acetic acid, v/v). In this formulation, chloroform and acetone rapidly inhibit endogenous RNase activity without inducing RNA cross-linking [53–56], while acetic acid softens tissues to facilitate sectioning. Subsequent embedding using low-melting-point Steedman's wax at 42 °C effectively preserves mRNA integrity. Given the minute quantities of RNA obtained from LCM-isolated samples, conventional RNA integrity assessment using instruments like the Agilent 2100 Analyzer is often impractical. Therefore, we established a specialized method to evaluate RNA integrity in LCM-derived samples based on the transcript ratio between the 3' ends and 5' ends of the reference gene OsGAPDH. Our analysis confirmed that the CAA solution fixating and Steedman's wax embedding approach maintains mRNA integrity significantly better than traditional FAA solution fixating or Carnoy’s solution fixating coupled with paraffin embedding approach. This method yields high-quality mRNA suitable for downstream comparative transcriptome analysis. Furthermore, we demonstrate its applicability to other rice tissues, including roots and stems, indicating broad utility.

We employed the optimized laser capture microdissection (LCM) method described above to successfully isolate high-quality mRNA from the rice IM, PBM, and SBM for transcriptome sequencing. The sequencing results were validated using LCM-coupled quantitative real-time PCR (LCM-qPCR), confirming the high reliability of the transcriptome data. Transcriptome analysis revealed extensive reprogramming during the differentiation of the IM into PBM and SBM. Numerous genes (OsPINs, OsARFs, OsIAAs, OsRRs, OsGA2oxs) involved in the synthesis, transport, and signaling of auxin, cytokinin, and gibberellin exhibited significant differential expression among these three meristem types, suggesting crucial roles for these phytohormones in regulating panicle branch meristems fate determination [57–59]. Among these differentially expressed genes (DEGs), several have been previously reported to positively or negatively regulate rice panicle branch development, further validating the accuracy of our transcriptome data and demonstrating the feasibility of using LCM combined with transcriptome sequencing for rapid identification of key genes controlling panicle branch meristems differentiation [60, 61]. We selected three uncharacterized DEGs of interest as candidate genes of panicle branch development. mRNA in situ hybridization demonstrated strong expression of these three genes (OsMADS32, OsARF2, OsARF14) specifically within the PBM and SBM regions of the developing rice panicle.

Although OsMADS32 has been reported to function in floret development, with its knockout mutants exhibiting defective palea development and reduced stamen number [62–64], whether this gene regulates panicle branch meristem development in rice remains unclear. To address this, we generated OsMADS32 overexpression transgenic lines. Compared with the wild type, three independent overexpression lines showed a significant increase in the number of secondary branches and grains per panicle, with no significant difference in thousand-grain weight. These findings not only demonstrate that combining laser microdissection with transcriptomic analysis can effectively identify key genes regulating panicle branch meristem development in rice, but also highlight the promising potential of OsMADS32 for application in breeding high-yield rice germplasm. The preferential effect of OsMADS32 on secondary branches may reflect its spatiotemporal expression pattern. Our transcriptome data, LCM-qPCR results, and in situ hybridization assays consistently demonstrated that OsMADS32 expression is significantly higher in SBM than in PBM. This expression bias could explain why the phenotypic effect is more pronounced on secondary branches. Alternatively, OsMADS32 may interact with different partners or regulate distinct downstream targets in PBM versus SBM, leading to branch-specific outcomes. Furthermore, owing to the lack of the OsMADS32 knockout mutant, we were unable to determine conclusively whether knockout of this gene leads to alterations in the number of primary and secondary branches per panicle. In future work, we will construct a OsMADS32 CRISPR/Cas9 knockout vector, generate the corresponding mutant, and further compare the number of primary branches, secondary branches, and grains per panicle between the mutant and wild-type plants.

We isolated IM, PBM, and SBM from rice young panicle sections at the SBM stage for transcriptome sequencing, rather than isolating the corresponding meristems from separate sections at the IM, PBM, and SBM stages. This approach was adopted because a single section at the SBM stage contains late-stage IM, mid-stage PBM, and early-stage SBM, allowing us to capture the dynamic transcriptional changes of key genes regulating the differentiation from IM to SBM across these three meristem types. This strategy is highly advantageous for identifying critical genes controlling rice panicle branch meristem development and, simultaneously, enables maximal resolution of spatial gene expression information. In subsequent studies, we plan to isolate IM, PBM, and SBM individually from sections at their respective specific stages to more accurately capture the dynamic reprogramming trajectory underlying IM-to-BM differentiation.

Conclusions

In this study, we established a laser capture microdissection (LCM) method capable of obtaining high-integrity mRNA from rice tissues and a complementary method for assessing the integrity of trace mRNA in LCM-derived rice samples. Based on this, we precisely isolated rice IM, PBM, SBM and conducted RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). The results demonstrate that a pronounced transcriptome reprogramming occurs during the differentiation of IM into PBM and SBM.
