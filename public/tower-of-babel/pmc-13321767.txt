Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease complicated with brachial plexus neuritis in a young woman: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Yuling Mai, Mingqun Deng, Shengde Li, Yazi Yang, Wei Cao, Yue Guo, Chenglong Liu, Hao Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKikuchi–Fujimoto disease (KFD) is a rare necrotizing lymphadenitis. Brachial plexus neuritis is an exceptionally rare complication of KFD.Case presentationA 31-year-old Asian female was diagnosed with KFD via excisional biopsy of a posterior cervical lymph node. Four days after the biopsy, pain and disability occurred in her left arm and shoulder. While Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) suspected compression of the brachial plexus nerves, the ultrasound indicated inflammatory changes consistent with neuritis. Diagnosis was challenging; however, distinguishing between mechanical compression and inflammation was crucial. Instead of surgery, steroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) were prescribed, and her pain was relieved within 1 week, while her disability did not disappear until 9 months later.ConclusionsWe report a rare case of KFD-associated brachial plexus neuritis emerging shortly after lymph node biopsy. We hypothesize that surgical positioning or traction, superimposed on the KFD inflammatory background, may precipitate this condition. Clinicians should be vigilant for this complication to ensure prompt anti-inflammatory treatment and avoid unnecessary secondary surgeries.Keywords: Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease, Brachial plexus neuritis, Steroid treatment, Case report

Background

Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease (KFD), also known as histiocytic necrotizing lymphadenitis, is an unknown pathology characterized by cervical lymphadenopathy and fever [1]. KFD is a rare disease that is more prevalent among Japanese and other Asian individuals than in other ethnicities. Since it has a low incidence rate and non-specific clinical features, KFD is not well-understood, resulting in significant risks of misdiagnosis and mistreatment [2]. KFD can be misdiagnosed as other disorders, such as lymphoma and lupus [3]. Therefore, a thorough pathologic examination is essential for accurate diagnosis of KFD. However, fine needle aspiration (FNA) often proves to be inadequate and non-specific, and excisional biopsy is needed for diagnosis [4]. Various complications have been reported in KFD patients, among which brachial plexus neuritis is an extremely rare complication. Understanding of brachial plexus neuritis in KFD is very limited. We present the first Chinese case of KFD with brachial plexus neuritis in a young woman and review the literature, hoping to increase clinicians' awareness and recognition of the disease.

Case presentation

(1) Initial presentation and biopsy: A 31-year-old Asian female presented with left neck swelling for 1 month with slight transient pain, but no fever, fatigue, and weight loss. She had slight tender lymphadenopathy in the left post-auricular, posterior cervical, and supraclavicular fossa chains. The rest of the physical examination was unremarkable, including normal vital signs, cardiovascular and abdominal systems. Her leukocyte count was slightly low, while lactate dehydrogenase and erythrocyte sedimentation rates were normal. Tuberculosis, cytomegalovirus, Epstein–Barr virus and autoimmune examinations were all negative. Ultrasound showed multiple enlarged lymph nodes (largest 1.4 cm, distributed in III, IV, and V regions) without central necrosis or abscess formation. The enlarged lymph nodes exhibited uniform cortical thickening, with unclear structures of the lymphatic hilum and blood flow signals consistent with the pattern of the lymphatic hilar. PET–CT indicated multiple metabolically elevated lymph nodes in the left neck and supraclavicular fossa (SUVmax 2.2–32.5) (Fig. 1). An excisional lymph node biopsy of the largest one in the Va region was performed, and tissue samples were sent for a histopathology exam, which confirmed KFD (Fig. 2). During the surgical biopsy, the patient was positioned supine on the operating table with her head rotated to the right at a 90° angle to facilitate exposure of the left side of the neck. The procedure lasted approximately 30 min and the patient did not experience any discomfort during or after the biopsy. (2) Onset of Neurological symptoms: Over the first three postoperative days, there were no abnormalities noted in the patient's movement of her head and shoulders. The procedure was reported to be successful, and no iatrogenic injury was considered. Neither non-steroid anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) nor steroids were prescribed, as she had no fever or severe pain. The patient was discharged from the hospital on the fourth day of the surgical biopsy procedure. However, the patient experienced a sudden onset of pain and weakness in her left shoulder area that day. The symptoms gradually worsened over the following days, resulting in an inability to abduct and shrug her shoulder (Fig. 3). (3) Diagnostic work-up: Upon consultation with the neurology department on day 10, the patient exhibited significant motor deficits. Significant weakness was noted in the left shoulder abductors (Deltoid: Grade 3/5), elbow flexors (Biceps: Grade 4/5), and scapular stabilizers (Rhomboids/Levator scapulae: Grade 3/5), while distal strength (Hand grip) was preserved (Grade 5/5). The left biceps reflex was hypoactive (1 +) compared to the right (2 +), while the triceps reflex was preserved (2 +). Sensation was preserved over the lateral deltoid region. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) of the neck showed a conglomerate of enlarged lymph nodes near the brachial plexus, likely causing local compression of the trunk of the brachial plexus (Fig. 4). Suspicion of misdiagnosis was raised as an unreasonable rapid development of the disease, and its likely invasive feature of compressing surrounding tissues. Surgical excision of the enlarged lymph nodes was under consideration to relieve compression of the brachial plexus and obtain a second pathological examination; and neither steroids nor intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) were prescribed. The electromyography (EMG) detected abnormal spontaneous activities in her left supraspinatus, levator scapulae, and rhomboid muscle. These electrophysiological findings of denervation in C5-innervated muscles correlated strongly with the clinical weakness observed in shoulder abduction and external rotation. Since neurosonography was not available in our hospital, we recommended that she be examined at another medical facility. The ultrasound of the nerves revealed diffuse thickening and hypoechogenicity of the suprascapular nerve consistent with inflammatory neuritis rather than focal entrapment. While MRI findings raised the possibility of brachial plexus trunk compression, the integration of ultrasound and EMG data indicates that widespread inflammatory nerve injury superimposed on a systemic inflammatory background is the more likely etiology. (4) Therapeutic interventions and follow-up: Resulted of the delay visit of the patient and the prolonged diagnosis process, 15 days after the beginning of her pain and weakness, methylprednisolone and IVIG were finally prescribed. Methylprednisolone was initiated at 120 mg/day and was gradually reduced and stopped after 3 months, while IVIG was prescribed at 400 mg/kg/day for 5 days. After 1 week of treatment, the pain in her left shoulder region completely disappeared, and the enlarged lymph nodes returned to normal in 3 months. However, the dystrophy of affected muscles and weakness of the patient's left shoulder did not disappear until 9 months later, which badly affected the patient's normal work and life (Please refer to Table 1 for a detailed timeline of the clinical course).

The patient initially failed to recognize the severity of her symptoms when she experienced sudden onset of shoulder pain and subsequent motor weakness on the fourth day following the biopsy procedure. Following her discharge from the hospital, she chose to recuperate at home. However, the intensity of the pain gradually increased, rendering routine tasks such as dressing and lifting objects unbearable. The subsequent diagnostic phase proved to be a challenging period for the patient, characterized by uncertainty and heightened concern. She faced apprehension regarding the possibility of undergoing another surgical biopsy and the potential consequences of delayed treatment leading to permanent brachial plexus disability. Her relief was palpable upon receiving the neurosonography results, which indicated inflammation rather than compression of the brachial plexus, thus initiating the treatment phase. The prescribed medication initially provided some relief, although the recovery of upper limb functionality necessitated a considerable amount of time. The patient's daily life was significantly impacted by the presence of brachial plexus neuritis. Activities that she had diligently pursued for many years, such as Yoga and swimming, were abruptly halted due to the condition, which frustrated her. The persisting discomfort and ensuing limitations profoundly influenced her overall quality of life. By sharing her perspective, she aimed to contribute to an enhanced understanding of this condition while advocating for improved care and support for individuals facing similar challenges.

Conclusions and discussion

A literature review was conducted using several databases, including PubMed, Google Scholar, and Embase, to identify articles reporting cases of KFD complicated with brachial plexus neuritis. Only three articles were identified that met this criterion. The first article, published in 1998 by Sugiyama et al., reported the case of a 22-year-old woman with KFD who developed right upper limb paresis due to brachial plexus involvement on 7 days of biopsy [5]. In 2016, Rakesh Gupta reported the first defined case of brachial plexus neuritis in a 56-year-old woman with KFD, and brachial plexus neuritis was the unique feature of the case. In this case, compression of the brachial plexus nerve was also indicated in MRI and compression was relieved by surgery, an excision biopsy of the largest supraclavicular lymph node, upon which the left upper limb symptoms of the patient resolved completely [6]. Finally, in 2019, Hussain B reported another case of a KFD patient with brachial plexus neuritis developed within 7 days of biopsy [7]. All three reported cases were female, and in two of them, the development of brachial plexus neuritis occurred approximately 7 days after lymph node biopsy, which is like our case. Steroids were prescribed within days, and symptoms improved quickly [5, 7]. More details about these three cases are in Table S1.

KFD is a rare disease that mainly affects Asian women under 30. The most common clinical presentation of KFD is fever and cervical lymphadenopathy. Diagnosis is typically confirmed by histopathological examination of lymph node biopsy. There is no specific treatment for KFD, and it usually resolves within 1–4 months. Although KFD is generally benign, multiple complications have been reported in KFD patients. One of the rare complications is brachial plexus neuritis, which has been reported in only three cases in the literature. Our case adds to the limited literature on KFD complicated with brachial plexus neuritis.

Brachial plexus neuritis could have a wide range of etiologies, while the most common cause reported in the literature is infection. A critical diagnostic challenge in this case was distinguishing between mechanical compression caused by lymphadenopathy and immune-mediated neuritis. The MRI findings supported a compressive etiology, which would typically necessitate surgical decompression. However, the ultrasound findings of nerve swelling and the favorable response to immunotherapy (steroids/IVIG) strongly support an inflammatory mechanism. The pathophysiology of brachial plexus neuritis is not exactly understood. Viral illnesses and immune causes are two theories mostly known to explain the mechanism of the disease [8]. The pathophysiology of KFD is also not well-known, and a viral or autoimmune cause has been suggested. In other words, the same pathophysiologic process might be shared between KFD and brachial plexus neuritis. However, the patient developed brachial plexus neuritis 4 days after surgical biopsy in our case and about 7 days in the other two reported cases. Is there any relationship between brachial plexus neuritis and biopsy? Direct surgical injury is unlikely, as the biopsy was performed in Level Va, superficial to the prevertebral fascia, whereas the brachial plexus lies deep to this fascia. Furthermore, the patient’s symptoms appeared on day 4. Direct surgical trauma typically causes immediate deficit upon awakening from anesthesia. Idiopathic brachial plexus neuritis, also known as Parsonage–Turner syndrome (PTS) or neuropathic amyotrophy, is a relatively uncommon disorder characterized by a constellation of symptoms that typically affect one side of the body. The condition manifests with a sudden onset of shoulder pain, followed by progressive neurological deficits, motor weakness, sensory disturbances, and numbness in the affected limb [8]. The precise etiology of this syndrome remains unknown; however, it has been documented in various clinical contexts, including postsurgical, post-infectious, post-traumatic, and post-vaccination scenarios. It is noteworthy that the association between PTS and surgical procedures has been extensively reported [9, 10]. In these cases, PTS typically develops during the perioperative period, shortly after the surgical intervention, and sometimes during the rehabilitation phase. There are two primary theories regarding the etiology of postsurgical PTS, which involve traction injury to the brachial plexus resulting from improper positioning and immune-mediated inflammation of the brachial plexus. It is reasonable that PTS may arise from mispositioning, stretching, or pressure-related injuries during operative procedures. The temporal relationship between the lymph node biopsy and the onset of neuritis (4 day post-op) is striking. One hypothesis suggests that brachial plexus neuritis could result from a mechanism similar to postsurgical neuralgic amyotrophy, which has been associated with stretch injuries to the brachial plexus during anesthesia [11]. In our case, the patient was instructed to maintain a right head rotation of 90° for about 30 min, potentially resulting in a stretch injury to the left brachial plexus. Similarly, in the other case of branchial plexus neuritis described by Hussain B [7], the patient underwent an excisional node biopsy that likely involved a specific posture to expose the operative site. In the case reported by Sugiyama in 1998 [5], although no details regarding biopsy were provided, brachial plexus neuritis was observed on the side of the biopsy. Considering that three out of the four known cases of brachial plexus neuritis occurred within a few days of the biopsy, it is reasonable to hypothesize that the biopsy procedure may contribute to the development of the disease in KFD patients, rather than being mere coincidence. Although KFD itself can cause substantial lymph node enlargement and compression of the brachial plexus as reported in one case [6], brachial plexus neuritis may also be associated with surgical biopsies in other cases. We propose that while the enlarged nodes may have created a “crowded” anatomical space, the primary pathology was likely a Parsonage–Turner syndrome-like neuritis triggered by the KFD autoimmune milieu. We hypothesize a “double-hit” mechanism: the patient had an underlying predisposition due to KFD-related systemic inflammation (first hit), and the mechanical stress of patient positioning (neck rotation) or surgical traction during the biopsy (second hit) precipitated the localized neuritis. This suggests that clinicians should be particularly gentle with positioning and traction during cervical biopsies in patients with active inflammatory lymphadenopathy. Although the precise pathological mechanisms remain unclear, it is essential to closely monitor the patient's limb sensation and movement diligently to promptly detect and treat brachial plexus neuritis.

The treatment of KFD complicated with brachial plexus neuritis involves using steroids. All previous reports suggested an excellent prognosis with steroid treatment in these patients. In our case, the patient received 5 days of high-dose steroids along with IVIG, followed by a gradual tapering of the dose over 3 months; while the lymph nodes diminished within days, the patient's weakness and limitation of motion persisted for 9 months, possibly due to the later initiation of treatment. Diagnosing brachial plexus neuritis in KFD patients early is important to prevent unnecessary investigations or surgical explorations [12] and make early initiation of treatment possible.

In conclusion, we reported the fourth case of branchial plexus neuritis in KFD around the world, and our case highlights the importance of considering brachial plexus neuritis as a complication of KFD. Clinicians should be aware of this potential complication, especially in patients who develop weakness and pain in the upper limb after a lymph node surgical biopsy. We first suggested that though the pathophysiology of brachial plexus neuritis in KFD remains unclear, the occurrence of this complication after lymph node biopsy is intriguing, indicating a relation between the process of biopsy and branchial plexus neuritis, but more studies are needed to understand the underlying mechanism. Early diagnosis and prompt treatment with steroids are crucial for a better prognosis and functional recovery.
