Minimal clinically important difference for cognitive scales in people with Parkinson’s disease with cognitive impairment
Authors: Ghorban Taghizadeh, Negin Eissazade, Javier Pagonabarraga, Saul Martinez-Horta, Jaime Kulisevsky, Parvaneh Taghavi Azar Sharabiani, Azar Sharabiani, Pouria Akbarikoli, Seyede Zohreh Jazaeri, Maryam Mehdizadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCognitive impairment (CI) is a significant non-motor symptom of Parkinson’s disease (PD), necessitating effective assessment tools to evaluate treatment efficacy. Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) thresholds provide critical benchmarks for interpreting cognitive intervention outcomes. This study establishes MCID values for PD Cognitive Rating Scale (PD-CRS), Scales for Outcomes in PD-Cognition (SCOPA-COG), and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) in PD-CI. A total of 110 patients with PD-CI were recruited; 105 (mean age 71.96 ± 2.40 years) completed the 12-week Dual-Task Training (DTT) program and were included in the final analyses. Cognitive function was assessed using PD-CRS, SCOPA-COG, and MoCA at baseline and post-intervention, with the Global Rating of Change (GRoC) questionnaire serving as an anchor. The MCID value was determined using anchor-based and distribution-based approaches. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis was employed to evaluate these thresholds. Significant improvements were observed across all cognitive measures post-intervention. The anchor-based MCID values were 7.70 points for PD-CRS, 3.11 points for SCOPA-COG, and 2.05 points for MoCA, respectively. Additionally, ROC curve analysis further determined that MCID cut-off points were > 3 points for the PD-CRS, > 2 points for the SCOPA-COG, and > 1 point for the MoCA. The distribution-based MCID values ranged from 1.48 to 4.10 points for PD-CRS, 1.37 to 5.95 points for SCOPA-COG, and 0.54 to 3.71 points for MoCA. This study established MCID thresholds for PD-CRS, SCOPA-COG, and MoCA in PD-CI, providing essential tools for clinicians and researchers to assess clinically meaningful changes after cognitive intervention.Keywords: Parkinson’s Disease, Cognitive Impairment, Clinical, Distribution-Based, Anchor-BasedSubject terms: Diseases, Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Cognitive impairment (CI) is a prevalent and debilitating non-motor symptom of Parkinson’s disease (PD). It is characterized by considerable heterogeneity in its symptomatology, encompassing deficits mainly in executive and visuospatial functions, verbal fluency, processing speed, complex attention, and memory, significantly diminishing quality of life and survival1–3. Moreover, PD with CI (PD-CI) imposes substantial burdens on the affected individuals, caregivers, and healthcare systems4. A critical barrier to effectively managing PD-CI lies in accurately assessing the efficacy of interventions.

Traditional outcome measures, such as motor function tests and general cognitive assessments, often fail to capture clinically meaningful changes from the patient’s perspective. To address this limitation, minimal clinically important difference (MCID) and substantial clinical benefit (SCB) have emerged as important metrics for evaluating the real-world impact of therapeutic interventions. MCID represents the smallest change in an outcome measure perceived by patients as beneficial, whereas SCB reflects a greater level of improvement associated with meaningful functional gains. These metrics translate statistical changes into clinically meaningful outcomes and are central to decision-making in PD-CI, where even modest improvements in executive function, attention, or memory may enhance daily functioning. They support responder analyses, improve interpretation of treatment effects, and inform both clinical practice and trial design5,6. In PD, MCID thresholds have improved the interpretability of outcome measures, including established motor scales such as the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS), by anchoring change to patient-perceived benefit7,8. However, comparable thresholds for cognitive instruments remain limited, and existing MCID values are often context dependent, varying by intervention type, disease stage, and baseline cognitive severity. This limits interpretation of cognitive intervention outcomes in both research and clinical settings.

Accordingly, this study aimed to establish MCID and SCB thresholds for three widely used cognitive measures in PD-CI: the Parkinson’s Disease Cognitive Rating Scale (PD-CRS)9, Scales for Outcomes in Parkinson’s Disease-Cognition (SCOPA-COG)10, and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA)11. Establishing these benchmarks may improve the interpretability of cognitive outcomes in clinical trials and routine practice by enabling more accurate evaluation of clinically meaningful cognitive change in PD-CI.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

A total of 105 individuals with PD-CI (MoCA score 19–25, consistent with mild-to-moderate CI11) were enrolled from our outpatient clinic in Tehran, Iran. Eligible participants met the United Kingdom Brain Bank criteria for probable PD12, had maintained a stable medication regimen for at least 30 days prior to enrollment, and were able to keep their medication regimen unchanged throughout the 12-week intervention period. No participant met clinical criteria for dementia based on neurological evaluation. Exclusion criteria included the presence of other neurological disorders and/or musculoskeletal or cardiopulmonary conditions.

Interventions and assessments

Participants completed a 12-week Dual-Task Training (DTT) program delivered three times weekly through individualized cognitive training sessions. DTT was selected based on prior evidence supporting its potential to improve cognitive function in PD13. To accommodate individual differences, the intervention included three difficulty levels (easy, intermediate, and advanced), allowing therapists to tailor exercises to each participant’s cognitive abilities. Sessions generally began at the easiest level, with progression contingent on successful completion of the highest difficulty level on two occasions.

The intervention primarily targeted executive function (abstraction, planning, and working memory) and attention (selective and sustained attention), which comprised approximately two-thirds of each session. Tasks included problem-solving under constraints (e.g., budget or dietary restrictions), analogies, similarities and differences, and target identification amid distractors (Table 1). The remaining third focused on memory (e.g., image recall, object placement recall, list memorization/recognition, design replication on a dot matrix, and matching pairs), constructional praxis (puzzles and figure copying), language (synonym and antonym recognition), logical reasoning (pattern and grid completion), and spatial orientation (route planning in virtual home and travel scenarios).

Cognitive tasks and their contents.

All sessions were delivered by two occupational therapists experienced in PD and cognitive rehabilitation. Therapists provided verbal support, feedback, and encouragement to foster an interactive and supportive environment to promote engagement, and adjusted training intensity before and during sessions according to participants’ tolerance and performance. Rest breaks were provided as needed to optimize cognitive engagement while maintaining progressive challenge.

Cognitive assessments were conducted by an occupational therapist blinded to assessment time point and prior test scores. The assessor was not involved in intervention delivery and remained unaware of participants’ attendance or training progress. As this was a single-arm pre-post study, blinding to intervention allocation was not applicable; the absence of a control group should therefore be considered when interpreting changes over time.

All intervention sessions and assessments were performed during the medication ON state, approximately one hour after antiparkinsonian medication intake, corresponding to peak levodopa effect. To minimize diurnal variability, assessments were standardized to the morning (9:00–11:00 AM), and participants maintained stable medication regimens throughout the 12-week study.

Instruments

The Global Rating of Change (GRoC) scale was administered post-intervention to assess participants’ self-perceived change in overall cognitive function relative to baseline. It was initially adapted as a 7-point Likert scale14, consistent with the structure of the Clinical Global Impression (CGI)15, ranging from 1 (“very much worse”) to 7 (“very much improved”). A 7-point format was selected over longer scales to reduce cognitive burden and response confusion in this PD-CI population while maintaining feasibility and reliability4. As per established clinimetric recommendations, patient-anchored measures such as the GRoC improve interpretation of instrument responsiveness and support derivation of clinically meaningful thresholds for cognitive change16,17. To enhance statistical robustness, sparse extreme categories were collapsed by merging “very much worse” with “much worse” and “very much improved” with “much improved,” while retaining “minimally worse,” “no change,” and “minimally improved,” resulting in a final 5-category scale. Before completing the GRoC, participants received standardized verbal instructions to improve discrimination between levels of perceived change. In line with Jaeschke et al. (1989), “minimally improved” was defined as “a change you have noticed that you think is beneficial, even if it is small and does not lead to major improvements in all aspects of your daily life.” “Much improved” and “very much improved” were jointly defined as “a change that is clearly noticeable in your everyday life and makes a meaningful difference in your quality of life” 18.

Three validated cognitive assessment tools were employed to evaluate intervention efficacy:

PD-CRS is a cognitive assessment tool designed to evaluate the cognitive profile of individuals with PD. It assesses both frontal-subcortical (attention, working memory, immediate and delayed verbal memory, phonemic, semantic, alternating, and action verbal fluency, and clock drawing) and posterior cortical (naming and clock copy) functions. A higher score indicates better cognitive function9,19.

Sample size

A PD-CRS MCID threshold of approximately 11 points (standard deviation (SD) = 14.7) was used for sample size estimation22. Based on 80% power and a 5% significance level, a minimum of 28 participants each in the “no change” and “minimally improved” groups, as defined by the GRoC, was required. Recruitment continued until both targets were achieved. The final recruited sample comprised 110 participants distributed across GRoC categories as follows: 4 “very much worse,” 15 “minimally worse,” 28 “no change,” 54 “minimally improved,” and 9 “very much improved.” After excluding 5 participants with incomplete data (3 from “no change” and 2 from “minimally improved”), the final analyzed sample included 105 participants.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, including mean ± SD, and frequencies (percentage %), were used to describe the sample characteristics. Internal consistency of the cognitive assessment tools was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, with values greater than 0.70, 0.80, and 0.90 indicating acceptable, good, and excellent reliability, respectively23. To assess intervention efficacy, a paired t-test was conducted to compare pre- and post-intervention scores across all cognitive measures. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

MCID calculation

MCID values were determined using two complementary methods: (1) Anchor-Based method: Change scores were anchored to patients’ self-reported improvement (GRoC) from pre- to post-intervention. Mean score differences (MDs) between pre- and post-intervention were calculated for participants reporting improvement and compared using independent samples t-tests, with negative t-values indicating greater post-intervention scores relative to baseline. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to identify optimal discriminative thresholds for each cognitive scale using the Youden index, maximizing sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing participants classified as “minimally improved” from those reporting “no change.” An area under the curve (AUC) ≥ 0.70 was considered indicative of acceptable discrimination; (2) Distribution-based method: MCID was calculated using 0.3 × baseline SD, standard error of measurement (SEM) (SD √(1-intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC))), and minimal detectable change (MDC) (SEM × 1.96 × √2)19.

SCB was defined as a substantial clinically meaningful change following intervention. SCB thresholds were determined using anchor-based methods and ROC curve-derived cut-off values distinguishing participants classified as “much improved” from those reporting “minimally improved.”

Results

A total of 105 individuals with PD-CI were included in the study, of whom 78 (74.28%) were male. All participants completed both baseline and post-intervention assessments, and their data were incorporated into the final analysis. The mean age of participants was 71.96 ± 2.40 years, and the mean disease duration was 9.62 ± 4.57 years. Descriptive statistics are summarized in Table 2.

The internal consistency of the cognitive assessment tools was good to excellent, as indicated by Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.92 for PD-CRS, 0.89 for SCOPA-COG, and 0.87 for MoCA.

Following the intervention, significant improvements were observed across all cognitive measures. The PD-CRS total score significantly increased from 78.92 ± 10.51 at baseline to 82.52 ± 12.55 post-intervention (MD = 3.53 ± 9.18, t = − 4.03, p < 0.001). Similarly, the SCOPA-COG total score significantly increased, from 24.01 ± 4.59 at baseline to 25.72 ± 5.01 post-intervention (MD = 1.95 ± 4.13, t = − 4.24, p < 0.001). The MoCA total score also significantly improved, from 22.65 ± 1.80 at baseline to 23.66 ± 2.62 post-intervention (MD = 1.00 ± 2.54, t = − 4.06, p < 0.001).

Correlational analysis between the GRoC and cognitive test score changes further supported the clinical relevance of these findings, with significant associations observed for PD-CRS (r = 0.72, p < 0.001), SCOPA-COG (r = 0.76, p < 0.001), and MoCA (r = 0.69, p < 0.001).

MCID thresholds

The mean difference method yielded MCID values of 7.70 points (95% CI: 5.31, 10.10, p < 0.001) for the PD-CRS, 3.11 points (95% CI: 1.98, 4.23, p < 0.001) for SCOPA-COG, and 2.05 points (95% CI: 1.28, 2.83, p < 0.001) for MoCA (Table 3).

ROC analysis was conducted on the subgroups reporting minimal improvement based on GRoC. The AUC values indicated excellent performance: 0.98 (95% CI: 0.92, 0.99) for PD-CRS, 0.90 (95% CI: 0.81, 0.96) for SCOPA-COG, and 0.84 (95% CI: 0.74, 0.91) for MoCA. Using Youden’s index, the optimal MCID cut-off points were > 3 points for the PD-CRS (sensitivity: 86.54%; specificity: 96.00%), > 2 points for the SCOPA-COG (sensitivity: 71.15%; specificity: 88.00%), and > 1 point for the MoCA (sensitivity: 69.23%; specificity: 84.00%).

Distribution-based MCID values were consistent with these estimates, ranging from 1.48 to 4.10 points (SD = 10.51, 0.3 SD = 3.15, SEM = 1.48, and MDC = 4.10) for PD-CRS, from 1.37 to 5.95 points (SD = 4.59, 0.3 SD = 1.37, SEM = 2.15, and MDC = 5.95) for SCOPA-COG, and from 0.54 to 3.71 points (SD = 1.80, 0.3 SD = 0.54, SEM = 1.34, and MDC = 3.71) for MoCA.

SCB thresholds

The mean difference method yielded SCB values of 19.10 points (95% CI: 16.34, 21.87, p < 0.001) for PD-CRS, 6.32 points (95% CI: 3.87, 8.78, p < 0.001) for SCOPA-COG, and 4.00 points (95% CI: 2.23, 5.76, p < 0.001) for MoCA.

ROC analysis was conducted in the subgroup exhibiting substantial cognitive improvement and further validated these thresholds. SCB cut-off points, derived from Youden’s index, were determined as > 11 points for PD-CRS, > 4 points for SCOPA-COG, and > 3 points for MoCA. The sensitivity and specificity analyses demonstrated excellent diagnostic accuracy. The sensitivity and specificity values were calculated as 92.3% (95% CI: 64.0, 99.8) and 100.0% (95% CI: 88.8, 100.0) for PD-CRS; 88.89% (95% CI: 51.8, 99.7) and 100.0% (95% CI: 86.3, 100.0) for SCOPA-COG; and 66.67% (95% CI: 29.9, 92.5) and 100.0% (95% CI: 88.8, 100.0) for the MoCA. AUC values indicated excellent performance, with AUCs of 0.98 (95% CI: 0.89, 1.000) for PD-CRS, 0.96 (95% CI: 0.83, 0.99) for SCOPA-COG, and 0.94 (95% CI: 0.81, 0.99) for MoCA.

Discussion

This study establishes MCID and SCB thresholds for three widely used cognitive assessment tools in PD: PD-CRS, SCOPA-COG, and MoCA, contributing to a growing body of research that helps clinicians and researchers better assess the clinical significance of interventions in individuals with PD-CI.

Significant improvements were observed across all cognitive measures following the dual-task training (DTT) intervention, indicating potential benefit in PD-CI. Anchor-based MCID values were 7.70 points for PD-CRS, 3.11 for SCOPA-COG, and 2.05 for MoCA, with ROC analysis confirming good discriminative performance (AUC > 0.80) and optimal cut-offs of > 3, > 2, and > 1 point, respectively. SCB thresholds were > 11 (PD-CRS), > 4 (SCOPA-COG), and > 3 (MoCA), while distribution-based estimates for minimal improvement ranged from 1.48 to 4.10, 1.37 to 5.95, and 0.54 to 3.71, respectively. Collectively, these values provide clinically interpretable benchmarks for meaningful cognitive change.

The PD-CRS and SCOPA-COG demonstrated superior and more consistent performance compared with the MoCA (AUC = 0.84), likely reflecting their PD-specific design targeting frontosubcortical deficits central to PD-CI. In contrast, the MoCA is a general cognitive screening tool originally developed for Alzheimer’s disease and vascular populations, which may reduce sensitivity to subtle, disease-specific changes in PD, particularly given the mild ceiling effect observed in this cohort (mean baseline MoCA ~ 22.7). The broader score range and balanced domain structure of the PD-CRS (0-130) may further enhance responsiveness. Accordingly, the proposed MCID and SCB thresholds should be interpreted cautiously and are likely most applicable to PD-CI populations with similar clinical characteristics, limiting direct generalizability to more advanced disease stages, severe motor impairment, or alternative intervention settings. Validation in broader PD populations and across intervention types is warranted.

When interpreting MCID values, the lower mean change in the full PD-CRS sample (3.53 points) compared with the anchor-based MCID (7.70 points) reflects dilution by participants reporting “no change” or worsening. In contrast, the “minimally improved” subgroup (49.5%) showed a mean change (7.26 points) closely aligning with the MCID. This underscores that MCID thresholds are intended for individual or responder-level interpretation rather than full-sample mean change, which may be attenuated by non-responders24.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate MCID and SCB thresholds for PD-CRS, SCOPA-COG, and MoCA in PD-CI. Previous research has primarily focused on establishing MCID values for PD-CRS in an observational study22 and MoCA only in stroke survivors25–27. Furthermore, the methods used to define MCID thresholds have been subject to debate due to methodological variability28. A key strength of our study lies in its rigorous approach to determining MCID and SCB values. The anchor-based method ensured that these thresholds reflect meaningful changes as perceived by patients, while the distribution-based method provided additional statistical validation. The integration of both approaches enhances the reliability of our findings and reinforces their clinical applicability.

Despite these strengths, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The single-arm design and absence of a control group (e.g., waitlist, non-intervention, or non-PD comparator) limits attribution of observed cognitive improvements exclusively to the intervention. Without such comparators, the potential influence of spontaneous cognitive fluctuations and placebo effects cannot be excluded. Moreover, repeated administration of the same cognitive measures over a 12-week interval may have introduced practice effects, although these are generally smaller in individuals with mild-to-moderate CI than in cognitively intact populations29,30. Future randomized controlled studies incorporating no-intervention or waitlist control groups are needed to better isolate treatment effects, quantify practice effects, and refine MCID and SCB estimates. The use of the GRoC scale as the primary anchor introduces potential subjective bias, including recall bias, treatment-related optimism, and mood-related influences. Incorporating complementary objective anchors, such as clinician-rated longitudinal cognitive assessments or comprehensive neuropsychological batteries, may strengthen the validity and generalizability of the derived thresholds. Although the MoCA was used both as an inclusion screening tool and as an outcome measure, MCID estimates were based on change scores (baseline to post-intervention) rather than baseline values, thereby avoiding circular bias. Nevertheless, future studies should consider using independent screening instruments (e.g., Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) or brief neuropsychological batteries) alongside disease-specific measures to further minimize potential overlap between eligibility criteria and outcome assessment. Finally, no informant- or caregiver-reported outcome measures were collected. In PD-CI, caregiver perspectives on daily cognitive functioning may provide an important external anchor to complement patient self-report and reduce recall- or mood-related bias. Future studies should incorporate caregiver-rated measures, such as the Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly or caregiver-based PD-CRS assessments, to further validate MCID thresholds and assess the clinical relevance of cognitive changes from both patient and care partner perspectives.

Conclusions

This study establishes MCID and SCB values for PD-CRS, SCOPA-COG, and MoCA in PD-CI. These findings have important implications for clinical research and treatment decision-making, providing standardized benchmarks for evaluating clinically meaningful changes associated with cognitive interventions in PD-CI.
