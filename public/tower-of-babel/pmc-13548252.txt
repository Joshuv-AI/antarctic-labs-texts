Complementary biomarkers or conflicting signals? DaT SPECT and synuclein skin biopsy in parkinsonian syndromes
Authors: A Enrique Martinez-Nunez, Nur Walker-Pizarro, Aparna Wagle Shukla, Adolfo Ramirez-Zamora, Nikolaus R McFarland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDiagnosing Parkinson’s disease (PD) and related synucleinopathies in patients with early or atypical features remains challenging. Dopamine transporter single-photon emission CT (DaT SPECT) imaging and phosphorylated alpha-synuclein (p-Syn) skin biopsy are increasingly used, but their concordance and combined value in diagnostically uncertain, real-world patients are unknown. We evaluated whether these two biomarkers provide complementary rather than redundant diagnostic information.MethodsWe retrospectively analysed patients evaluated at a tertiary movement disorders centre (January 2022–March 2025) who presented with parkinsonism or tremor not meeting established criteria for PD, multiple system atrophy or dementia with Lewy bodies. 86 patients underwent p-Syn skin biopsy; 52 also underwent DaT SPECT. Using the treating neurologist’s final clinical diagnosis as the reference standard, we calculated sensitivity, specificity, predictive values and concordance for each test and for combined (‘either-positive’) testing and compared clinical features between concordant and discordant groups.ResultsDaT SPECT and p-Syn biopsy showed similar sensitivity (79.0% and 79.0%) and specificity (74.0% and 81.0%) for synucleinopathy. Combined testing increased sensitivity to 95.6% but reduced specificity to 59.9%. Results were discordant in 30.8% of dual-tested patients; +DaT/−p-Syn patients more often reported hyposmia, whereas −DaT/+p Syn patients more often reported REM sleep behaviour disorder. Almost half of discordant cases ultimately met criteria for idiopathic PD.ConclusionsDaT SPECT and p-Syn skin biopsy provide complementary, not redundant, diagnostic information. The high discordance, with eventual PD diagnosis in many cases, is consistent with the dual-origin (brain-first vs body-first) model of alpha-synuclein propagation. Both are best used as rule-in tests in early or diagnostically uncertain disease, where a negative result does not exclude a synucleinopathy; in advanced disease meeting full clinical criteria, they may have greater rule-out value. Combined testing improves early detection at a cost to specificity, with implications for trial enrolment and for interpreting biomarkers alongside symptom evolution.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative condition characterised by progressive motor and nonmotor features.1 The current diagnostic criteria focus primarily on identification of motor features such as resting tremor, rigidity and bradykinesia. When coupled with gradual progression over more than 1 year, the diagnostic accuracy is over 90%–94%.2 The diagnosis can be challenging in patients who present early in the disease course, patients with only one sign of parkinsonism, patients with atypical features or suffer other comorbidities that can affect mobility and confound clinical examination.3 When compared with neuropathology, clinical diagnosis of PD can be inaccurate in 7%–35% of cases,4 and can increase to 74% in patients who respond poorly to dopaminergic drugs or are left untreated.5

The dopamine transporter single-photon emission CT (DaT SPECT) was approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2011 to differentiate patients with essential tremor from those with a parkinsonian disorder. More recently, in 2022, DaT was approved by the FDA for use in patients with suspected dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB). A major limitation of DaT SPECT is that it is unable to differentiate between PD and other disorders with similar nigrostriatal dopamine denervation such as DLB, multiple system atrophy (MSA), corticobasal syndrome (CBS) and progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP).6 7

The phosphorylated synuclein (p-Syn) skin biopsy (Syn-One Test, CND Life Sciences) is a newly available biomarker test that can potentially increase diagnostic certainty for parkinsonian disorders. The p-Syn biopsy relies on immunohistochemical identification of alpha-synuclein deposits in peripheral nerve terminals in at least one of three small punch biopsies of the skin. This test is expected to show p-Syn deposits in patients with synucleinopathy such as PD, DLB, MSA and pure autonomic failure (PAF),8 but not in PSP or CBS, which are associated with abnormal τ accumulation.9 Given that alpha synuclein pathology can distinguish between the synucleinopathies and tauopathies, this test has the potential to clarify the diagnosis in cases of atypical parkinsonism syndromes. Recent data indicate over 92% of those meeting clinical criteria for synucleinopathy disorder have a positive p-Syn skin biopsy with a 3.3% false positive rate in healthy controls.10

Clinical trials and early studies using these tests assessed their performance in selected populations that did not include patients with equivocal, complex or unclear clinical presentations in a real-world practice outside clinical research.10 11 There are currently no data regarding concordance or the utility of combining these tests to guide diagnosis.

The objective of this study was to explore the diagnostic utility of the DaT SPECT and the p-Syn biopsy in patients with clinically unclear parkinsonism in a large, unselected, real-world clinical setting.

Methods

Study design

This is a retrospective analysis of patients evaluated at the University of Florida Norman Fixel Institute for Neurological Diseases from January 2022 to March 2025. We included patients who underwent the Syn-One skin biopsy test (CND Life Sciences) at our centre. The patients included in this study presented with some features of parkinsonism or tremor that did not meet current clinical diagnostic criteria for PD,12 MSA,13 DLB14 or any other more specific diagnosis. We collected demographic and long-term follow-up data on evolution of initial symptoms, occurrence of new symptoms adding to diagnostic certainty, diagnostic impressions along the follow-up period, response to levodopa, neuroimaging (DaT SPECT), and p-Syn biopsy results.

DaT-SPECT protocol

The studies were carried out at the University of Florida and interpreted by an expert neuroradiologist. Briefly, the standard radiological interpretation of this study involves qualitative visualisation for asymmetry between the uptake of (123I) ioflupane in the striatum on either side or decreased uptake in the posterior striatum (comma sign). Each patient was asked to withdraw medication that is known to interfere with the acquisition of this study for a time window specific to each drug class.15 These studies were performed using standard clinical acquisition protocols.16 Because data were abstracted retrospectively from free-text records, adherence to these instructions and each patient’s exact medication status at the time of imaging could not be systematically verified.

Skin biopsy protocol

The biopsies were collected at the University of Florida Norman Fixel Institute and processed as previously described.10 Briefly, three punch biopsies are obtained in the clinic from the posterior cervical region, posterior thigh, and distal leg. These are placed in a fixation medium and sent for analysis to CND Life Sciences (Scottsdale, AZ). A positive result was defined as a positive immunostaining for p-Syn in any of the three sites.8

Clinical diagnosis procedures

Parkinsonism was defined in accordance with the Movement Disorders Society guidelines.17 The guidelines require the presence of bradykinesia with rigidity or rest tremor. Participants included in this study all had parkinsonian features but did not fulfil these criteria. We collected the working diagnosis by the treating physician at our centre before and after each of the tests. We defined the final diagnosis for each patient based on the treating physician’s diagnosis at the last patient visit while following the current clinical guidelines for diagnosis of PD,12 MSA,13 DLB14 and PSP.9

Data collection and statistical analysis

Data were collected by two investigators (AEM-N and NW-P). We collected the data from the medical record written in a free-text format. Missing data was recorded as missing and handled as such during analysis without imputation.

We calculated the proportions of positive test results for each final diagnosis. We classified a case as ‘discordant’ if either of these tests was positive and the other was negative. Patients with a final diagnosis of PSP were excluded from this part of the analysis given that we do not expect alpha-synuclein pathology in this case; therefore, it is expected to have discordant results from the DaT SPECT and p-Syn biopsy.

We used the unified PD rating scale at the time of the first test performed, and whether patients experienced constipation, anosmia, sleep disturbances, tremor, bradykinesia, rigidity, cognitive impairment, depression, anxiety, gait impairment, urinary dysfunction. These data on symptoms were collected from narrative in the clinical notes, and not formally through standardised measuring tools or scales.

We calculated sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value and negative predictive value for each test using the treating neurologist’s final diagnosis at their most recent visit, to define true positives and true negatives, and whether their DaT SPECT and p-Syn biopsy was expected to be positive given their final diagnosis. Then, we calculated the same test performance metrics for combined sequential testing (when one test is performed after the other after the first test results positive) using previously described methods.18 The diagnostic benchmark was the ability of these tests to predict a diagnosis of a synucleinopathy. The target condition (reference-positive) was a synucleinopathy, comprising PD, DLB, MSA and PAF. A test was scored as a true positive when positive in a patient whose final diagnosis was a synucleinopathy in which that biomarker is expected to be abnormal, and as a true negative when negative in a non-synucleinopathy diagnosis (essential tremor, drug-induced parkinsonism or tremor, cervical dystonia, functional neurological disorder, Alzheimer’s disease). Because dopaminergic deficit on DaT SPECT is also expected in the tauopathies PSP and CBS, patients with a final diagnosis of PSP were excluded from the DaT SPECT and combined-testing accuracy analyses. Results

We identified and reviewed data from 86 patients who had a p-Syn biopsy, of which 52 also had a DaT SPECT prior to the biopsy.

Presenting symptoms and final diagnoses

We did not have any data points missing for the symptoms collected in this patient cohort. The initial presenting signs that led to testing were bradykinesia in 57.7%, isolated tremor in 34.6% (kinetic only tremor in two-thirds of patients and rest tremor only in one-third), dysautonomia in 5.8%, and isolated cervical dystonia in 1.9%. The mean follow-up from DaT SPECT to the last clinic visit was 2.91 years (SD 1.55). DaT SPECT always preceded biopsy, by a mean of 7.12 months (SD 2.12; range 6–12 months), and the mean follow-up measured from the date of biopsy was 2.33 years (SD 1.28). Because the two tests, and subsequent follow-up, were separated by these intervals, discordant results should be interpreted with the possibility of interval disease evolution in mind. The most common final diagnoses at the latest time of follow-up were idiopathic PD in 34.6%, essential tremor in 26.9%, Lewy body dementia in 9.6%, drug-induced tremor in 7.7%, drug-induced bradykinesia in 7.7%, progressive supranuclear palsy in 5.7%, cervical dystonia in 3.8%, Alzheimer’s disease in 3.8% and functional tremor in 1.9%. p-Syn test results

The cervical p-Syn biopsy was most commonly positive (73%), followed by the distal leg (44%) and then the posterior thigh (33%). Test results for each final diagnosis are presented in table 1.

Diagnostic performance

We included 86 patients for the p-Syn biopsy performance analysis and 52 for the DaT SPECT analysis, after excluding the three patients with a final clinical diagnosis of PSP (see the ‘Methods’ section). All patients who had a DaT SPECT had it done prior to the skin biopsy, within the range of 6 to 12 months (mean 7.12 months, SD 2.12 months).

Dopamine transporter single-photon emission CT

The sensitivity of the DaT SPECT in our cohort is 79.0% (71.02% to 86.98%) with a negative predictive value of 81.0% (73.31% to 88.69%). The specificity is calculated at 74.0% (65.4% to 82.6%) and a positive predictive value of 85.3% (63.2% to 95.1%). The proportion of patients who were correctly classified with the DaT SPECT is 71.0% (62.11% to 79.89%).

p-Syn biopsy

The sensitivity for the p-Syn biopsy is 79.0% (71.02% to 86.98%) with a negative predictive value of 83.0% (75.64% to 90.36%). The specificity for this cohort is 81.0% (73.31% to 88.69%), with a positive predictive value of 78.0% (69.88% to 86.12%). The proportion of patients who were correctly classified with the p-Syn biopsy is 80.23% (74.01% to 86.45%).

Combined sequential testing

We calculated diagnostic performance for sequential use of both tests, scoring a combined result as positive when either test was positive. Because a positive DaT SPECT is not specific to synucleinopathy (it also occurs in tauopathies), patients with a final diagnosis of PSP were excluded from this analysis, and the either-positive strategy is interpreted as a rule-in for a neurodegenerative parkinsonian disorder rather than for synucleinopathy specifically; synuclein-specific inference rests on the p-Syn biopsy component. For this approach, the sensitivity was 95.59% (90.83% to 100%), with a negative predictive value of 89.08% (76.91% to 100%). The specificity was 59.94% (35.24% to 84.64%), and a positive predictive value of 79.91% (70.82% to 88.99%). The proportion of patients who were correctly classified with combined sequential testing is 92.11% (86.68% to 97.54%).

Test concordance

For this part of the analysis, we included 52 patients who had both a DaT SPECT and a p-Syn biopsy. The DaT SPECT and p-Syn biopsy agreed in 69.3% of cases (see table 3). In the group of patients with discordant results, 10 had a positive DaT SPECT and negative p-Syn biopsy (+DaT/−p-Syn) and 6 had a negative DaT SPECT and a positive p-Syn biopsy (−DaT/+ p Syn).

Double-negative cases

20 of our patients tested negative for both tests, and none of them progressed to a clear clinical diagnosis of a synucleinopathy. The final diagnoses for this group were essential tremor (60%), drug-induced parkinsonism (20%) and cervical dystonia (20%). We found no statistically significant difference in the prevalence of anosmia, REM-sleep behaviour disorder, constipation, tremor, bradykinesia, cognitive impairment, anxiety, depression or psychosis at the time of initial presentation between double negative cases and patients with at least one test positive, supporting that these patients are difficult to tell apart clinically.

Discordant cases

Of our 10+DaT/−p-Syn patients, 5 eventually fulfilled diagnostic criteria for PD, 2 PSP, 1 Alzheimer’s disease, 1 functional neurological disorder (manifesting as tremor, not parkinsonism) and 1 DLB. From our six patients with −DaT/+p Syn, four progressed to satisfy the diagnostic criteria for PD, and two drug-induced tremor (confirmed by reduction of tremor with discontinuation or decrease of their oral tacrolimus).

We found that +DaT/−p-Syn patients had a significantly higher prevalence of self-reported hyposmia compared with −DaT/+p Syn patients (83.3% vs 0%, p=0.032). On the other hand, −DaT/+p Syn patients have a significantly higher rate of self-reported rapid eye movement (REM) sleep behaviour disorder (RBD) compared with+DaT/−p-Syn patients (66.7% vs 0%, p=0.01). Consistent with this, the −DaT/+p Syn group, which included several patients who progressed to PD, reported RBD without hyposmia, mirroring the body-first pattern, whereas the +DaT/−p-Syn group showed the opposite (hyposmia without RBD). There was no significant difference among these groups in the prevalence at presentation of response to levodopa, autonomic symptoms, tremor, bradykinesia, rigidity, symptom asymmetry, anxiety, depression or psychosis.

Discussion

This study highlights the diagnostic complexity of parkinsonian syndromes in a real-world population, particularly when DaT SPECT and p-Syn skin biopsy are discordant. Among the 52 patients with both tests, roughly 30% had discordant results, yet most discordant cases ultimately received a diagnosis of idiopathic PD, underscoring the clinical value of these biomarkers even when imperfectly aligned. Discordance may reflect disease stage (early peripheral alpha-synuclein deposition)19 or the underlying synucleinopathy subtype, for which DaT SPECT performance varies.20

Our findings align with prospective data from Donadio et al,21 who reported high diagnostic accuracy of skin p-Syn in early-stage PD (81% sensitivity, 100% specificity) in patients with recent-onset parkinsonism (<18 months) and showed that p-Syn positivity can identify PD up to 2 years before formal criteria are met, supporting our observation that positive biomarkers may precede clinical certainty. Their reported reproducibility over time (96% between baseline and 18 months) provides reassurance about biomarker stability. Their cohort excluded longer-duration and more complex presentations, which likely explains their higher specificity (100% vs 81% here); real-world application in heterogeneous populations appears to yield modestly lower specificity while retaining clinical utility.

Isaacson et al examined the impact of p-Syn testing on management, reporting a change in diagnosis in 66% and a change in treatment in 55% of patients.22 Whereas their work emphasised clinical utility, our study emphasises diagnostic performance and the interplay between p-Syn and DaT SPECT in uncertain parkinsonism. Together these studies illustrate complementary dimensions of skin biopsy: its management value and its integration with dopaminergic imaging to improve diagnostic resolution in ambiguous or early presentations.

The most diagnostically challenging subset was +DaT/−p-Syn patients. Although this pattern may raise suspicion for non-synuclein parkinsonism such as PSP, these patients more often progressed to clinically established idiopathic PD than to PSP, indicating that a +DaT/−p-Syn pattern does not exclude synucleinopathy and should not prematurely rule out PD. Conversely, two patients with PSP had both a positive skin biopsy and a positive DaT SPECT, showing that a positive biopsy does not exclude a primary tauopathy (false positive) or a comorbid dual proteinopathy.

The contrasting symptom profiles of hyposmia in +DaT/−p-Syn patients and RBD in −DaT/+p Syn patients suggest underlying pathophysiological differences that could help guide clinical suspicion. The alpha-synuclein origin and connectome model proposed by Borghammer23 predicts that brain-first disease presents earlier with olfactory impairment and abnormal DaT SPECT, whereas body-first disease presents earlier with RBD and a positive p-Syn biopsy. Our preliminary results are consistent with this model, although the small subgroup sizes warrant caution.

Some patients initially considered to have drug-induced tremor had positive biomarker testing, raising the possibility of unmasking subclinical PD. These patients improved partially after withdrawing the offending medication but warrant long-term follow-up, as a positive DaT SPECT or p-Syn biopsy may indicate an underlying degenerative process.

Diagnostic performance of the two tests was comparable, with similar sensitivity (~79%) and specificity (~74%–81%). Combining tests substantially increased sensitivity (95.6%) at the expense of specificity (59.9%). This trade-off may suit settings where early identification of possible synucleinopathy is prioritised, such as initiating a dopaminergic trial. For disease-modifying trials, however, the lower specificity of combined testing risks including non-synucleinopathy cases, potentially diluting treatment effects; additional confirmatory criteria or longer observation may be warranted. Both biomarkers reach maximal sensitivity in advanced disease that fully meets clinical criteria and are less sensitive in early or prodromal stages.21 Because our cohort was enriched for early and diagnostically uncertain presentations, the sensitivity we report is expected to be lower than in validation cohorts of established disease; disease stage, which we could not formally stratify given the sample size, should be considered when applying these estimates.

Both tests were originally validated in well-defined populations of established PD versus healthy controls. Our real-world cohort spanned a broad range of parkinsonian and tremor disorders, including PD, PSP, DLB, CBS, drug-induced parkinsonism and functional neurological disorders. In such heterogeneous settings, the positive predictive value of each test declines, and a positive result should broaden, rather than narrow, the differential. Taken together, these findings reinforce that DaT SPECT and p-Syn biopsy are best used as rule-in tests for clinicians who already suspect a neurodegenerative parkinsonian disorder: a positive result supports the diagnosis, can justify a dopaminergic trial, and can identify candidates for disease-modifying trials, but a negative result does not exclude disease, particularly in early or prodromal stages. In advanced disease meeting full clinical criteria a negative result may carry greater weight toward excluding that diagnosis. Discordant results should be interpreted in the context of symptom evolution, ancillary testing (eg, olfaction, RBD) and longitudinal follow-up. We classified Alzheimer’s disease as reference-negative for synucleinopathy; however, Lewy co-pathology is present in a substantial minority of AD cases, so a positive p-Syn biopsy in such a patient could reflect true co-pathology rather than a false positive.24 Both AD patients in our cohort were p-Syn negative, so this did not affect our estimates, but it should be considered when applying the test in cognitively impaired populations.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, the sample is small relative to prior validation studies, reflecting our focus on the minority of movement-disorder patients with genuine diagnostic uncertainty; this also limited the concordance analysis, as not all patients had both tests. Second, the reference standard was expert clinical diagnosis with serial examination rather than neuropathological confirmation. Because the treating neurologist’s final diagnosis was informed by the same DaT SPECT and p-Syn results being evaluated, the estimates of sensitivity and specificity are subject to incorporation bias and likely overestimate true accuracy. Expert clinical diagnosis is accurate in 90%–94% of cases at autopsy2 and dictates management in practice, which we consider a reasonable benchmark. Third, because the cohort comprised only diagnostically uncertain patients, test-performance estimates reflect this spectrum and may not transfer to the selected populations used in validation studies. Fourth, symptoms were abstracted from narrative notes rather than ascertained with standardised instruments, and the subgroup symptom comparisons were based on small numbers and multiple uncorrected tests, so they are hypothesis-generating. Finally, we cannot draw firm conclusions about PSP (only two cases with a positive biopsy, where false positives or concomitant alpha-synucleinopathy cannot be excluded) or about MSA and PAF, in which a positive p-Syn biopsy with a negative DaT SPECT would be expected but could not be assessed in our data. We could not evaluate co-pathology in the absence of neuropathological confirmation.

In conclusion, DaT SPECT and p-Syn skin biopsy play complementary roles in evaluating parkinsonian syndromes, particularly under diagnostic uncertainty. Discordant results were common, yet about half of discordant cases progressed to idiopathic PD, supporting the value of these tests even when misaligned. In early or diagnostically uncertain disease, neither test alone should be used to exclude disease; in advanced disease that fully meets clinical criteria, a negative result may be more informative for exclusion owing to higher sensitivity. In all cases, interpretation must integrate clinical features, symptom evolution, and complementary testing. Combined biomarker testing can aid early diagnosis and therapeutic planning, but prospective study with neuropathological validation remains essential to refine its application.
