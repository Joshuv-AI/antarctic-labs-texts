TNFR1 signaling connects inflammation to impaired fatty acid oxidation to drive intestinal stem cell aging
Authors: Ruixuan Wang, Tahmineh Tabrizian, Donghai Wang, Jeb English, Anita Ayer, Maya Gal, Weng-Lang Yang, Zhiping Wu, Kai Mao, Ardijana Novaj, Xusheng Zhang, Indranil Basu, Nils P Brodin, Wade Koba, Deepak Saxena, Jiahn Choi, Leonard H Augenlicht, Aaron Ericsson, Evripidis Gavathiotis, Chandan Guha, Derek M Huffman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAging is characterized by a decline in function of intestinal stem cells (ISCs), but the extent to which this is shaped by systemic factors is unclear. Here we show that the ISC aging phenotype can be propagated from old to young mice utilizing heterochronic parabiosis, and implicate a role for inflammation in these effects, as anti-inflammatory drugs, including TNF antibodies, restored function. Parabiotic rescue experiments demonstrate that TNFR1 knockout protected young ISCs from the old environment. In young organoids, TNF downregulated crypt budding, while impairing mitochondrial pathways and fatty acid oxidation (FAO). However, aged ISC function was enhanced by boosting mitochondrial fusion, whereas FAO in aged crypts was improved by countering inflammation with salicylate treatment. Thus, these data identify the old environment through the progeronic factor TNF, as a driver of ISC aging phenotypes through intestinal epithelial cell TNF receptor 1 signaling to downregulate FAO, proliferation and regenerative capacity in these cells.Subject terms: Ageing, Research data

Main

Aging is the main risk factor for numerous ailments including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, Alzheimer’s disease and cancer1. The biological underpinnings of the aging process have been attributed to several interconnected hallmarks, including inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, stem cell exhaustion, dysbiosis and cellular senescence2,3. Dysregulation of these processes has been studied extensively in several organs, including skeletal muscle4, liver5,6 and brain7,8. More recently, studies investigating the underlying cause(s) of intestinal decline and its putative role in age-related diseases have emerged. The intestinal epithelium is maintained by Lgr5+ intestinal stem cells (ISCs), which reside in the crypt base alongside Paneth cells (PCs), which provide key niche signals including notch and Wnt9. Lgr5+ ISCs divide daily to give rise to new daughter cells and transit amplifying cells, which migrate along the crypt–villus axis to renew the intestinal epithelium and maintain tissue integrity9–11. However, ISC proliferation rates decline with aging, potentially contributing to impaired intestinal homeostasis12,13.

Although the ISC aging phenotype is now well established, the precise cell autonomous and cell-nonautonomous drivers and hallmarks implicated in this decline remain incompletely defined. Studies investigating these questions in mammals have leveraged a combination of in vivo aging mouse models and complementary ex vivo intestinal organoid cultures14 comprised of isolated crypt cells that, under defined conditions, can proliferate and differentiate into three dimensional (3D) ‘mini-gut’-like structures useful for probing potential regulators of ISC function14. Such studies have identified several putative mechanisms that contribute to ISC aging, including reduced peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR)-δ activity leading to impaired fatty acid oxidation (FAO), which can be rescued by fasting or pharmacologic strategies to boost FAO15. Moreover, aged ISCs have been characterized by a reduction in canonical Wnt signaling16, which was linked more recently to increased secretion of the Wnt inhibitor notum from PCs17. Likewise, senescent cells and their secretory phenotype was shown to impair ISC function in aging through secretion of Ptk7, which induces noncanonical Wnt and YAP signaling18. Similarly, others have linked local shifts in aged ISCs and their microenvironment to dysregulation involving at least in part a proinflammatory imbalance, including interferon gamma (IFNγ)-mediated STAT1 signaling19,20 and aberrant mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) complex 1 (mTORC1) and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) activation in ISCs21. Despite these important mechanistic insights, how these cellular alterations to the ISC niche might be linked to the systemic environment, which, along with the gut microbiome, helps to comprise principal contributors to the aging exposome, is not fully understood.

One well-known strategy that has been leveraged to determine the role of cell-nonautonomous drivers of aging phenotypes is heterochronic parabiosis. Indeed, this surgical technique, which creates a shared circulation between animals of identical (isochronic) or disparate (heterochronic) ages, enables the determination of whether aging phenotypes can be disseminated between younger and older animals, respectively, and the direction, if any, of this change22. Such effects have been observed across a variety of organs and cell types, including brain, liver, skeletal muscle, and the vasculature, as well as at single-cell resolution23,24. Moreover, some of these studies have leveraged parabiosis or related strategies to identify pro- or antigeronic factors such as B2M25, GDF1126–28, CCL11, oxytocin and tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases 2 (TIMP2)29, as potential mediators. However, whether intestinal aging phenotypes are potentially regulated by systemic factors, such as during heterochronic parabiosis, has not been explored.

To determine the extent that ISC aging may be consequential for intestinal and organismal homeostasis, and shaped by the systemic environment, we leveraged a combination of irradiation, pharmacologic, genetic and parabiosis experiments. Beyond confirming reduced ISC function with aging as noted by others, we further establish that this decline is associated with impaired intestinal and ISC resilience following gut-targeted irradiation, which is associated with diminished activation of clusterin. Moreover, utilizing heterochronic parabiosis experiments, we demonstrate that ISC aging phenotypes can be propagated from old to young mice, suggesting that these manifestations are driven, at least in part, by cell nonautonomous factors. Through a series of subsequent key pharmacologic experiments, we identified ‘inflammaging’ as an important mediator in these effects. The administration of several anti-inflammatory agents such as rapamycin, salicylate and aspirin with known systemic effects, but not agents (mesalamine) whose effects are largely confined locally to the gut, boosted ISC function in aged mice, suggesting that targeting whole-body, rather than gut-specific inflammation per se is required for this response. A combination of in vitro screening and parabiotic rescue experiments further link tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-mediated signaling through intestinal epithelial cell TNF receptor 1 (TNFR1) to these effects. We find that TNF triggers downregulation of many FAO-related genes and maximal respiration in intestinal organoids as a likely contributor to impaired ISC proliferation. We show that the fatty acid metabolism transcriptional program is reduced in aged stem and progenitor cells, whereas fatty acid flux is increased in aged crypts following salicylate treatment. Collectively, these data indicate that the age-related decline in ISC function is mediated in part by systemic contributors in the aged environment that instigate overstimulation of the TNFR1 signaling pathway in the intestinal niche to impair mitochondrial function and FAO to impair proliferative and regenerative capacity.

Results

Intestinal aging is characterized by decreased ISC niche function

Ex vivo organoid formation from aged male C57BL/6 small intestinal crypts was reduced ~30% in older mice (Fig. 1a; P < 0.01), whereas a similar decline was observed in aged female mice (Extended Data Fig. 1a; P < 0.01), suggesting this is not sex specific. Reduced ISC proliferation in aged mice was further confirmed by an in vivo 4 h bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) pulse labeling experiment whereby aged animals had significantly fewer BrdU+ cells in the small intestinal crypt than young animals (Fig. 1b; P < 0.01). This was further accompanied by elevated detection of lysosome secretion by PCs in aged crypts (Fig. 1c; P < 0.01). Morphological analysis on the intestinal lumen of C57BL/6 mice revealed significantly decreased villus length in the proximal (mainly duodenum) region of the small intestine in aged mice (P < 0.001), but this difference was not observed in more distal regions (Extended Data Fig. 1b). There were no differences in crypt area or depth measurements between old and young mice (Extended Data Fig. 1c,d), but there was an increase in enterocyte tight junction width in old mice, as measured by electron microscopy (EM) (Extended Data Fig. 1e).

To determine the potential importance of ISC functional decline with age, younger and older mice were subjected to a single dose of 16 Gy abdominally-targeted radiation (AIRg). As expected, bodyweight and temperature were reduced significantly by AIRg in both young and old mice (Fig. 1d,e), although body temperature was lowest by day 4 in old animals (Fig. 1e; P < 0.01). This correlated with markedly lower survival over 14 days in aged mice (~10% survival), compared to 60% survival in young mice (Fig. 1f; P = 0.0197), indicating that aging impaired the ability to renew the intestinal epithelium. Indeed, intestinal permeability at day 3.5 postirradiation to a 40 kDa fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled dextran (FITC-dextran) challenge was compromised by AIRg to a greater extent in older irradiated mice (Fig. 1g; P < 0.01). Concurrently, there was a significant reduction in surviving crypts in the ileum of old versus young animals (Fig. 1h; P < 0.01), but not in the jejunum (Extended Data Fig. 1f). Olfm4+ cells were decreased in both old ileum (Fig. 1i; P < 0.05) and jejunum (Extended Data Fig. 1g; P < 0.05) after irradiation. In contrast, expression of clusterin, a stress-responsive factor that can be triggered in an injury-responsive ISC population, was increased markedly in the intestine of young, irradiated mice, but levels were notably lower in both ileum and jejunum of older animals (Fig. 1j; P < 0.001, Extended Data Fig. 1h; P < 0.01).

Impaired ISC function with aging is propagated from old to young mice

To determine whether aging intestinal phenotypes are shaped by the systemic environment, we next performed heterochronic parabiosis experiments. In agreement with findings in young versus old nonsurgical animals, organoid formation was significantly greater in young–young (YY) versus old–old (OO) isochronic pairs (Fig. 2a; P < 0.001). In contrast, organoid formation in young heterochronic (YO) animals was impaired by exposure to the old environment (Fig. 2a; P < 0.01). Likewise, in vivo BrdU labeling experiments in parabiotic pairs detected fewer BrdU+ cells in OO versus YY isochronic pairs (Fig. 2b; P < 0.01), whereas YO had decreased BrdU+ cells, compared to YY (Fig. 2b; P < 0.05). Meanwhile, OY animals, similar to OO, remained significantly impaired compared to YY, indicating a lack of rejuvenation by the young environment on proliferation rates (Fig. 2b). Moreover, to account for possible cage effects of parabiosis on ISCs, organoid formation was studied in sham-operated cohoused controls, and the age-related difference in organoid budding efficiency between young cohoused and old cohoused mice remained (Fig. 2c; P < 0.05), and was unaffected by cohousing young with old mice (Fig. 2c). Collectively, these data demonstrate that the aged ISC phenotype can be propagated from old to young mice through the systemic environment.

Rapamycin or anti-inflammatory agents can rescue ISC functional decline

As ISC decline with age is modulated at least in part by cell nonautonomous mechanisms, we tested whether the life-extending gerotherapeutic rapamycin30,31 could also restore ISC proliferation in older animals. Indeed, we found in an initial study in which rapamycin was administered by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection for 4 weeks could restore ISC function in old mice (Fig. 3a; P < 0.05) and reduced the levels of several inflammatory markers in plasma (Fig. 3b; P < 0.05). To determine whether these effects were also achieved by oral administration, younger and older mice were fed diets containing either rapamycin encapsulated with Eudragit (eRapa; 42ppm) or a Eudragit-containing control diet for up to 6 weeks (Fig. 3c). Whereas eRapa had no effect on organoid formation in young mice, it partially rescued ISC function in old mice (Fig. 3d; P < 0.05), although BrdU labeling was not different (Fig. 3e); the age-related increase in crypt lysozyme staining (Fig. 3f; P < 0.05) was not affected by eRapa treatment.

Given the known immunomodulating effects of rapamycin, including confirmation of such effects in our cohorts, we hypothesized that an immunomodulatory mechanism may link rapamycin to restored ISC function. To confirm this, young and aged mice, respectively, were treated for four weeks with salicylate—a well-known nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID). Whereas organoid formation of old mice was ~50% lower in this cohort than in young mice, salicylate completely rescued organoid formation, phenocopying the effects of rapamycin (Fig. 3i), supporting the conclusion that the age-related decline in ISC function can be modulated by anti-inflammatory interventions. To verify the extent to which these effects can be modulated by targeting inflammation, an additional cohort of old mice was treated with either 5-aminosalicylic acid (5-ASA), aspirin or salicylate for 4 weeks (n = 6 each group). Consistent with the initial observation, salicylate treatment markedly increased organoid formation in aged mice (Fig. 3j; P < 0.0001). Similarly, aspirin treatment increased organoid formation (Fig. 3j; P < 0.001), but the inflammatory bowel medication 5-ASA, which is confined mainly to the intestinal lumen, had no effect. Moreover, similar to rapamycin, salicylate had no effect on intestinal crypt pS6 levels in these mice (Extended Data Fig. 4b,c).

ISC decline is mediated by TNF and IFNγ

To investigate the potential immunomodulating factor(s) involved, a screen of cytokine effects on organoid growth was performed using a candidate approach32,33, interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-12, IL-17, IFNγ, TNF and IL-23 were evaluated over a dose response, ranging from 5 to 1,000 pg ml−1 for effect on intestinal organoids isolated from young mice. Although IL-12 and IL-23 treatment had no effect, there was potent disruption of organoid formation with IFNγ, followed by IL-1β, TNF and IL-17 at higher doses (Fig. 4a–f; P < 0.05). To determine whether inhibiting these cytokines individually in vivo could rescue organoid formation, old mice were injected with a monoclonal antibody inhibiting either IL-1β, TNF or IFNγ, as well as old isotype controls for 3 weeks, and ISC function was assessed. Remarkably, either TNF (Fig. 4g; P < 0.05) or IFNγ neutralization (Fig. 4g; P < 0.0001) restored ISC function, albeit to different degrees, but anti-IL-1β treatment had no effect (Fig. 4g). Thus, TNF and/or IFNγ are potential mediators of ISC aging proliferative phenotypes.

TNF impairs ISC function through the TNFR1 signaling pathway

Given that systemic pharmacologic neutralization of cytokines with monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) does not offer spatial specificity and is subject to confounding effects, a more targeted approach was used to determine the role of specific signaling pathways on ISC function. To this end, we focused on TNFR1 signaling, considered to have greater proinflammatory effects of the two TNFRs. Therefore, intestinal epithelial cell-specific TNFR1 knockout mice were generated (Extended Data Fig. 5) and used in parabiotic rescue experiments by joining young intestinal TNFR1 knockout (YKO) or control (YCON) mice with young (YNIA) or old mice (ONIA) from the National Institute on Aging (NIA), respectively, thereby exposing YKO and YCON mice to either a young or old environment through parabiosis. Consistent with parabiotic studies in NIA mice, YCON × ONIA animals formed fewer organoids than YCON × YNIA (Fig. 4h; P < 0.05). However, YKO × ONIA mice were protected completely from the old environment, and significantly greater than YCON × ONIA (P < 0.01). However, the age-related difference in YNIA and ONIA organoid formation in adjoining pairs remained, regardless of whether they were attached to YCON or YKO (Fig. 4i; P < 0.01). Thus, these data indicate that the ability of the old environment to impair ISC function in parabionts is reproducible, and requires, at least in part, intestinal TNFR1 signaling.

To further investigate possible cellular consequences of TNF on ISC phenotypes, young organoids were treated with TNF over a range of concentrations. When TNF was added at day 2 before symmetry breaking34, organoid formation declined in a dose-dependent manner, with a maximum decrease of ~30% (Fig. 4j; P < 0.01), and was characterized by formation of pronounced luminal structures by day 9. When TNF was added on day 5 after symmetry breaking, organoid formation also declined (Fig. 4j; P < 0.01), but to a lesser extent than when applied at either day 0 (Fig. 4a) or day 2 treatment. Meanwhile, day 2 or day 5 treatment impaired de novo crypt budding markedly and promoted enlarged luminal-type structure formation at day 9 (Fig. 4k–m; P < 0.01). This included upregulation of several related genes, including lys6a, lys6e and anxa5 (Supplementary Information). Thus, these data indicate that excess TNF can potentially render the intestine less capable of de novo regeneration and repair.

TNF modulates mitochondrial, ribosomal and immune response genes and pathways in young intestinal organoids

To gain greater insight into how TNF signaling might impair function in young ISCs, and the extent to which these changes may resemble old ISCs, organoids were cultured overnight with vehicle or different concentrations of TNF, and then analyzed by bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). PCA separated young and old vehicle-treated organoids, respectively, mainly along PC2 (Fig. 5a). Further, TNF-treated young cells at both 10 ng ml−1 and 50 ng ml−1 were strongly right-shifted away from both young and old control organoids along PC1 in a dose-dependent fashion (Fig. 5a). To determine whether the effects of TNF could be acutely counteracted pharmacologically, oxidative stress was preemptively targeted by treating TNF-challenged organoids at day 5 with either the antioxidant MitoTempo, or a NOX1 inhibitor, as we observed a markedly upregulation in Nox1 expression following TNF treatment. Although TNF treatment led to a dose-dependent decrease in crypt budding, neither compound mitigated this reduction, indicating that oxidative stress is not a primary driver of these effects (Extended Data Fig. 7e–h). We further probed the ability of PPARa, PPARg or PPARd agonists to boost FAO and organoid formation in the presence of TNF. Neither TNF nor PPAR agonists alone or in combination had significant effects on organoid formation (Extended Data Fig. 7i). Moreover, de novo crypt budding was not affected by any of the three agonists in the absence of TNF, although acute PPARα (Extended Data Fig. 7j; P < 0.01), PPARγ (Extended Data Fig. 7k; P < 0.001) or PPARδ (Extended Data Fig. 7l; P < 0.01) agonist conferred partial protection against the reduction in crypt budding by TNF. Although this was insufficient to fully restore budding capacity, these findings are consistent with a role for PPAR-regulated lipid metabolic programs, including FAO, in modulating intestinal organoid resilience to inflammatory stress.

Mitochondrial and inflammatory modulation of FAO and ISC function in aging

Guided by our data linking dysfunctional mitochondria generally, and diminished FAO specifically, through inflammatory signaling, to impaired intestinal crypt function and regeneration capacity with age, we next tested whether pharmacologic modulation of mitochondrial dynamics could act to restore crypt function. Short-term treatment of aged organoids with the mitochondrial fusion activator MASM7 increased budding capacity significantly, but not formation of aged organoids (Fig. 6e–g).

Finally, as chronic in vivo salicylate proved to most potently enhance organoid formation in aged animals, we assessed whether chronic treatment could concurrently enhance FAO flux directly in aged crypts. Freshly isolated crypts from both old control mice and old salicylate-treated mice were incubated for 1 h in vitro with U-13C16-palmitic acid and then submitted for metabolomic analysis. Indeed, both intracellular palmitate enrichment and citrate M + 2 labeling were increased in salicylate-treated aged crypts (Fig. 6i,j; P < 0.05), without a corresponding change in acetylcarnitine enrichment (Fig. 6k). This suggests that anti-inflammatory salicylate treatment enhances fatty acid uptake and routing into the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle in crypt cells, coinciding with improved organoid-forming capacity in aged intestinal organoids from salicylate-treated mice (Fig. 3i). Together, these data support a model in which age-associated inflammatory and mitochondrial alterations converge to limit FAO capacity in intestinal crypts, which can be rescued, at least partially, through targeted interventions.

Discussion

It is now well established that ISC function is dysregulated with aging in several models, including flies and mice16,35. In Drosophila, aberrant ISC function with age leads to impaired intestinal homeostasis and membrane integrity and is often implicated as a principal cause of mortality in flies35,36. The gut-derived exposome also continues to be linked to systemic perturbations and increased risk of age-related ailments at distant sites, including neurodegenerative disorders37,38. Such effects may be linked to a breakdown in intestinal barrier integrity with age, supported by our observation by EM of compromised intestinal tight junctions in old mice. Consistent with this, we further demonstrated that aged animals are less resilient to a gut-targeted irradiation, including a diminished ability to preserve intestinal barrier integrity, ISC survival, and induction of the survival factor clusterin39.

Several cell autonomous and local niche mechanisms and signals have now been characterized to play a role in ISC aging. These include decreased canonical Wnt signaling in aged ISCs16, and more recent studies have identified the Wnt inhibitor notum as a secretory factor increased in aged PCs, capable of interfering with canonical Wnt signaling in ISCs40, with additional sources of Wnt regulators also found to be produced by nearby lymphatics41. Of particular relevance to this study, impaired FAO was reported previously to be an important driver of age-related ISC decline15, and reduced FAO was also observed in a recent study on alternate day fasting and mitochondrial function in aged crypts42. However, whether systemic aging per se plays a causal role in impaired ISC proliferation has not been established clearly. Our study serves as an important extension on these previous studies, providing evidence that cell nonautonomous factor(s) in the aged environment are a main initiator of local dysregulation in homeostasis to the ISC niche.

Using pharmacologic and antibody-mediated interventions, we identified inflammation as a main contributor to the ISC aging phenotype. Treatment of aged mice with rapamycin, salicylate, aspirin or mAb-neutralizing strategies targeting either TNF or INFγ in old mice each improved organoid formation assays in vitro. This effect of rapamycin through inflammation was unexpected as we presumed that inhibition of the mTORC1 pathway would play a central role in its effects. Indeed, aberrant mTOR activation and dysregulation is often observed in aged cells43, and is reported to be increased in old PCs, leading to increased notum production17. However, using intestinal pS6 immunostaining as a surrogate for mTORC1 activity in crypts, there was no evidence supporting a role for this pathway in the regulation of ISC function through rapamycin or salicylate. mTORC1 activity was reported to be reduced in aged ISCs; therefore, the role of mTOR signaling seems complex, cell type specific and may be disparate among different mouse models21,40.

The notion that dysregulation in immune function is a driver of aging phenotypes in solid tissues is now well established44. Likewise, immune-mediated inflammatory processes underlie many bowel syndromes, and targeted therapies, including anti-TNF strategies, are deployed commonly in these patients45. Likewise, evidence here and elsewhere demonstrates that the aged intestine can be defined by a proinflammatory state, including a strong upregulation in major histocompatibility complex class II20, whereas gene set enrichment identified interferon and TNF signaling among the top pathways in aged crypts. Moreover, GO analysis of Lgr5hi ISCs similarly found inflammatory pathways, including TNFR1 signaling, to be enriched in aged cells, with a particular role of IFNγ-mediated signaling identified in impeding the regenerative capacity of the aged epithelium19,20.

In parabiotic rescue experiments, we demonstrated that ISC function was preserved in young mice specifically lacking TNFR1 in the intestinal epithelium when surgically attached to old mice. Moreover, when maturing intestinal organoids (day 5 in culture) from young mice were exposed to TNF overnight, potent downregulation of mitochondrial-related pathways and processes were strongly represented in DEGs and GO analysis, including several key FAO-related genes, such as downregulation of Cpt1a, Fabp1, Fabp2, Pparg and Ppara expression. Importantly, we show that these changes functionally manifest as a preferential impairment in intestinal organoid maximal respiration in vitro by a Palmitate oxidative stress test. Treatment with PPAR agonists partially abrogated the loss in young organoid function by TNF challenge. However, how TNFR1 signaling acts to preferentially downregulate the FAO program is not entirely clear as antioxidant treatment and analysis of canonical Wnt target genes did not support principal roles for these pathways.

Although our data collectively demonstrate that TNFR1-mediated TNF signaling is involved in generating ISC aging-like phenotypes by impairing FAO in these cells, these results also suggest a role for potentially other inflammatory mediators, particularly IFNγ, as previously shown19. In fact, many studies have shown a complex interplay and cooperativity between TNF and IFNγ46. For example, recent transcriptomic analyses found that higher major histocompatibility complex class I and II and IFN-response genes at baseline predicted better responses to the anti-TNF therapy, adalimumab45, underscoring the interconnected nature of these inflammatory pathways. In the context of our findings that the inflammatory phenotype can constrain mitochondrial lipid utilization, it is notable that in vivo salicylate treatment was sufficient to enhance organoid formation and FAO flux in aged crypts. We further corroborated results by observing that the FAO transcriptional program is broadly downregulated in aged ISCs, consistent with other reports of aberrations in metabolic and proinflammatory pathway signatures as defining features of these cells20,47. A previous report from our group found that, at the resolution of single cells, the transcriptional program in aged ISCs and more differentiated cells was altered as to delay or skew proper timing of intestinal cell maturation, which involved perturbations to genes critical for oxidative metabolism along key development branch points47. Notably, these shifts were normalized by either metformin or rapamycin treatment, both of which can modulate metabolic and inflammatory-related pathways, further reinforcing the tight coupling between inflammatory tone, mitochondrial function and ISC competence.

An unexpected observation here was that even though postsurgical animals following parabiosis or sham procedures were fully recovered for weeks before performing organoid assays, lingering effects of surgical stress seemed to result in slightly less efficient organoid formation than typically observed in age-matched nonsurgical animals. Thus, such persistent effects could be attributed to acute and chronic inflammatory responses following surgery, which could have implications for intestinal homeostasis following similar challenges in humans—a possibility that should be tested.

Our results identify several areas for further investigation. Beyond TNF, our data implicate an important role of IFNγ signaling in ISCs. Given the known interplay between TNF and IFNγ, these inflammatory pathways may cooperatively reinforce aging-related ISC decline46. However, interferon signaling in the gut is highly pleiotropic and complex, with roles not only in inflammation, but also regeneration and healing48, suggesting that its effects in the aging intestinal ecosystem probably involve complex multicellular interactions. Although an IFNγ challenge introduced early during organoid development seems to be most toxic to in vitro organoid development, it was not determined whether it is capable of eliciting impairment to de novo crypt generation and enlarged luminal structure formation in maturing organoids, as observed with TNF. Collectively, these results support the central role of TNF-driven metabolic suppression in ISC dysfunction, while allowing for the possibility that closely related inflammatory signals may converge within this same proinflammatory milieu to reinforce the overall inhibitory tone.

An additional interesting observation involved the formation of enlarged luminal-like structures with TNF. The underlying cause(s) of this remains unclear, but could involve activation of YAP1–Notch signaling or YAP1–noncanonical Wnt signaling through TNF, as these factors were reported previously to be involved in altered organoid formation18,34. Serra and colleagues also showed a metabolic shift in these structures consisting of decreased proliferation, lysozyme expression and increased AldolaseB34. STAT1, which can interact with TNFR1, could also play a role, as was shown previously by IFNγ signaling19,20. Finally, TNF induced a sevenfold increase in organoid wnt10b expression, similar to a previous report studying HSP60-deficient crypts, which displayed mitochondrial dysfunction, altered functionality and enhanced release of Wnt10a and Rspo1 (ref. 49).

Although we established a role of TNF through TNFR1 on aging-related ISC decline, it is not clear whether the source of TNF and/or other inflammatory mediators that impair ISC function in normal aging and in young heterochronic parabionts originates from the old systemic milieu or from a more local source provoked by the old environment. We did not observe obvious shifts in immune cell populations in the LP or IEL of the small intestine in young heterochronic parabionts (YO). However, the possibility remains other factors in the old environment may trigger these resident or other surrounding cells, such as senescent cells50, in the gut or lymph to secrete excess key cytokines/chemokines locally or through the lymphatics to drive the ISC phenotypes observed here. Indeed, there is evidence for the latter scenario, including a previous report that proinflammatory cells in the LP were the main source of elevated IFNγ in the aged intestine impacting ISC function19. We also observed that salicylate or aspirin, two systemically acting anti-inflammatory drugs, rescued ISC function, whereas 5-ASA, which is absorbed poorly and confined mainly to the epithelium and luminal side of the intestine, had no effect on organoid formation. This further supports the existence of systemic factor(s) in the aged environment capable of instigating inflammation locally in the ISC niche.

Because parabiosis requires sharing of not only a circulation, but also an external environment, we considered other possible components of the exposome that could mediate these effects. We surveyed the composition of the fecal microbiome, particularly given previous evidence that some features of intestinal function could be restored by fecal transplants from young to old mice51, whereas fecal transplantation from old to young led to impaired intestinal permeability52. We observed a moderate degree of fecal microbiome sharing in cohoused sham-operated animals, but the degree of sharing was profoundly greater in parabionts, although it is unclear if this was due to inherent differences in the surgeries or to some other unique feature of the shared circulation in parabiosis. Nevertheless, as opposed to the propagation of the proliferative ISC phenotype from old to young, the microbiome seemed to spread predominantly from young to old, arguing against broad microbiome shifts as the primary driver of ISC dysfunction. Nevertheless, subtle microbial changes capable of promoting local inflammation cannot be excluded.

Heterochronic parabiosis experiments in aging have been impactful in distinguishing the role of cell nonautonomous mechanisms involved in cellular and tissue aging across different sites24. Most, but not all, reports suggest that aging phenotypes manifest, at least in part, through exposure to progeronic factors, such as CCL11 and b2-microglubin, which were each linked to decreased neurogenesis and cognitive function in mice, respectively25,53. However, some examples of rejuvenation have been observed by antigeronic factors using parabiosis, plasma exchange or ectopic administration such as oxytocin54, TIMP229 and GDF1126,55. In collaborative studies, we also found recently that cerebromicrovascular function is improved56 and the age-dependent increase in senescence and senescence-associated secretory phenotype marker expression were reduced in several tissues from old mice exposed to a young environment57. However, the data presented here demonstrate clearly that the ISC aging proliferative phenotype is a malleable and reversable feature mediated mainly by the aging environment, and identify TNF as a progeronic factor through TNFR1 signaling in these cells, as a driver of ISC aging. Importantly, there are clinical tools available to modulate gut inflammation generally, and TNF specifically. Therefore, there is potential for translation of these findings to improve intestinal homeostasis and rejuvenation capacity in aging humans.

Methods

Animals

Young (4 months) and aged (18–22 months) male and female wild-type C57BL/6 mice were obtained from the NIA aged rodent colony and used for studies. For some dose response experiments and single-cell experiments, C57BL/6J mice were used (Jackson Laboratories; Jax stock no. 000064). To generate Tnfr1 floxed mice, frozen embryos (EM:07099) were obtained from the European Mouse Mutant Archive (EMMA) Consortium under a material transfer agreement with the Alexander Fleming Research Center58, and rederived at the University of Michigan Transgenic Facility. Breeding colonies were established to generate Tnfr1fl/fl offspring, using described genotyping strategies58, and bred with Villin-Cre mice (Jax stock no. 004586) to generate intestinal epithelial-specific TNFR1 knockout mice (TNFR1IECko). All animals were group housed with two to four per cage and provided standard rodent chow (Purina, cat. no. 5001), and water ad libitum, unless otherwise indicated, and maintained under standard temperature and photoperiod as described57,59,60. All experimental procedures were approved by the Einstein Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol nos. 20140916, 20150103, 20171202, 20170314, 00001154 and 00001273). Animals were not randomized formally to experimental groups, but group assignment was performed in a balanced and unbiased manner whenever possible. Littermate controls were used to minimize background and environmental variability, and care was taken to balance groups among ordered batches and litters to avoid bias in assignment. Tissue collection, sample processing and downstream analyses were performed in a mixed order to minimize potential batch effects and reduce experimental bias.

Parabiosis surgery

Parabiotic mouse surgeries were performed by the Einstein Health Span Core as described57,61–64. For standard heterochronic unions, younger (4 months) and older (18 months) male inbred C57BL/6 mice from NIA were joined as YY isochronic, YO heterochronic and OO isochronic pairs. For parabiotic rescue studies, young Tnfr1IECko male mice and control littermates (Tnfr1fl/fl) were paired with either younger or older NIA male mice, respectively. Analgesia was provided initially with Banamine (2 mg kg−1 each) immediately postoperative and twice per day for 3 days and once daily for 4 days, and later transitioned to a meloxicam protocol, whereby animals were offered a bacon-flavored Metacam tablet 1 day before surgery and received subcutaneous injections of metacam (5 mg kg−1 each) on 3 consecutive days and then bacon-flavored Metacam tablets for 2 additional days. For postoperative care, mice were monitored rigorously and received Ringers lactate subcutaneously immediately postoperatively and as needed to prevent dehydration. Animals remained conjoined for approximately 2 months before being euthanized.

In vivo pharmacologic studies

Rapamycin was administered either by i.p. injection (LC Labs) at a dose of 4 mg kg−1 i.p. or an equal volume of vehicle every other day for 4 weeks as described65, or by diet. For dietary studies, young and old mice were treated with 384 ppm Eudragit or encapsulated Rapamycin (42 ppm; Rapa Holdings) incorporated into Purina chow 5001 (LabDiets Inc.) as described30,66, and then euthanized 4–6 weeks later. For salicylate treatment, salicylate (Sigma) was added to the drinking water for up to 28 days, at a dose of 2 mg ml−1. 5-ASA (500 mg kg−1 in diet) and aspirin (21 ppm in diet)67 were incorporated into Purina 5001 chow LabDiets Inc.). For the neutralizing antibody study, aged mice were injected i.p. with 50 µg of IgG1 isotype control (cat. no. MAB005) (n = 3), IFNγ antibody (cat. no. MAB485) (n = 3), TNF antibody (cat. no. MAB4101) (n = 3) or IL-1β antibody (cat. no. MAB401R) (n = 3) twice per week for 3 weeks (300 µg total). At the end of these studies, intestinal crypts were isolated for organoid analysis.

Abdominal irradiation study

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and subjected to gut-targeted irradiation using the SARRP platform (Xstrahl). Computed tomography (CT)-guided targeting was performed before delivery of 16 Gy total abdominal irradiation in two opposing fields. Animals were monitored daily for survival and welfare. A subset of animals (n = 10 group) were implanted with a small transponder (IPTT-300) under the skin for longitudinal body temperature with an IPTT Reader (BMDS, Seaford DE) until day 3.5 postirradiation.

Intestinal permeability assay

Mice were subjected to an in vivo permeability assay similar as described68. Briefly, mice at day 3.5 postirradiation were fasted and were given an oral gavage with 80 μg per 200 μl of FITC-dextran at 40 kDa (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. FD40-100MG). Blood was collected 4 h after gavage by cardiac puncture, centrifuged in a tube and serum was separated. For FITC detection, 50 μl of serum was diluted with PBS at a 1:1 ratio in a 96-well black-walled plate. Diluted serum was analyzed for FITC fluorescence levels using a SpectraMAX spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices) and reported in relative fluorescence units as compared to young nonirradiated controls.

Intestinal morphology

For evaluation of intestinal morphology, the gastrointestinal tract was isolated and divided into four segments: duodenum, ileum, jejunum and colon, and prepared as Swiss rolls as previously described69. Tissues were then cut longitudinally, washed in cold PBS, rolled, fixed overnight in 10% neutral-buffered formalin at 4 °C and transferred to 70% ethanol the next day. Specimens were then processed through a series of alcohols and xylenes, and embedded in paraffin. Images of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained sections (5 μm) were acquired using a PerkinElmer P250 High-Capacity Slide Scanner. Villi length and crypt depth and area were evaluated in 50 random crypts and villi per animal.

Immunohistochemical staining

Immunostaining was performed as described previously70. Briefly, antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer (pH 6) using a pressure cooker with steam for 10 min. After rehydration and blocking endogenous peroxidase by 3.0% H2O2 for 5 min, avidin and biotin blocking (Vector Labs SP-2001) was performed for 15 min each with wash steps. Slides were blocked with goat or rabbit serum (1%) for 20 min followed by incubating with antibodies against BrdU (1:200; cat. no. 5292, Cell Signaling), lysozyme (1:1,000, cat. no. ab108508), pS6 (1:500, cat. no. 5364, Cell Signaling) or clusterin (1:50, cat. no. AF2747, Bio-techne) for 1 h. Slides were then incubated with appropriate biotinylated secondary antibody for 30 min, followed by a streptavidin-HRP detection system (Vector) and application of 3,3′-diaminobenzidine for visualization of the antigen-antibody complex (Scytek). For OLFM4 detection, slides were subjected to antigen retrieval, cooled to room temperature, washed with PBS then peroxidase blocked with BLOXALL (Vector Laboratories, cat. no. SP-6000-100) for 10 min followed by 1 h of blocking with PBS containing 5% horse serum and 0.05% Triton X-100 (ThermoFisher Scientific). Sections were incubated with rabbit monoclonal antibody against olfactomedin-4 (OLFM4) (D6Y5A) (Cell Signaling Technology, cat. no. 39141, 1:400), overnight at 4 °C then rinsed with PBS before incubation with an anti-rabbit secondary antibody conjugated to peroxidase (Vector Laboratories, cat. no. MP-7401). ImmPACT 3,3′-diaminobenzidine EqV (Vector Laboratories cat. no. SK-4103) was used as a chromogen and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin before dehydration and mounting. OLFM4-postive crypts are expressed as positive crypts per 10,000 µm. Slides were scanned using a PerkinElmer P250 High-Capacity Slide Scanner and visualization using CaseViewer and density analysis or cell counting was done using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health).

Electron microscopy

EM was performed (n = 3 per group) for observation of cell–cell tight junctions, and intestinal epithelial subcellular structure. Tissues were processed and analyzed using standard techniques. In brief, tissue pieces were placed immediately in Karnovsky’s KII Solution (2.5% glutaraldehyde, 2.0% paraformaldehyde, 0.025% calcium chloride, in a 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer, pH 7.4), fixed overnight at 4 °C and stored in cold buffer, as described71. Subsequently, tissue samples were postfixed in 2.0% osmium tetroxide, stained en bloc with uranyl acetate, dehydrated in graded ethanol solutions, infiltrated with propylene medium, flat embedded in pure Epon and polymerized overnight at 60 °C. Representative areas were collected with a transmission electron microscope and attached CCD camera in the Einstein Analytical Imaging Facility with assistance from the core facility staff in the preparation and acquisition of EM data. Representative areas were collected and distance was determined across 30 random tight junctions between adjacent enterocytes (n = 3 animals per group).

LP immune cell isolation

For isolating immune cell populations from LP, the Lamina Propria Dissociation Kit (cat. no. 130-097-410, Miltenyi Biotec Inc.) was used as per the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, jejunum was dissected from young and old mice, and placed in ice-cold Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS) without Ca2+ and Mg2+ containing 10 mM HEPES (HBSS W/O). Mesenteric fat was removed and feces were flushed out of the jejunum using HBSS W/O. The small intestine was then cut longitudinally, further rinsed in HBSS W/O and cut into 0.5-cm long pieces. Small intestinal segments were then transferred to a 50-ml tube containing 20 ml predigestion solution (1× HBSS W/O containing 5 mM EDTA, 5% fetal bovine serum, 1 mM dithiothreitol) and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min with continuous gentle rotation. Samples were then vortexed for 10 s and passed through a 100-μm cell strainer (cat. no. 130-110-917, Miltenyi Biotec Inc.) to collect the LP tissue pieces, and this step was then repeated. Samples were then transferred into a gentleMACS C Tube containing the enzyme mix in digestion solution (1× HBSS with Ca2+ and Mg2+ (HBSS W) containing 5% FBS), and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C with continuous rotation. Using a gentleMACS dissociator, tissue was agitated for 1 min, followed by centrifugation at 300g for 5 min and the pellet was collected, resuspended with 5 ml PBS + 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA), passed through a 100-μm cell strainer, and centrifuged at 300g for 5 min. The final pellet containing LP lymphocytes were then resuspended for FACS sorting.

Flow cytometry

IEL and LP lymphocytes were resuspended in FACS buffer (PBS + 2% FBS + 0.2 mM EDTA). Live/dead staining was performed using Live/dead fixable blue dead cell stain kit (cat. no. L34961, Invitrogen) as instructed by the manufacture. Fc-blocking was performed using Fc block (1:100, cat. no. 131301, BioLegend) for 5 min, followed by a 1-h incubation with 1:100 dilution of cell surface primary antibodies against CD45 (cat. no. 109822), CD3 (cat. no. 100204), CD4 (cat. no. 100422), CD8 (cat. no. 100725), F4/80 (cat. no. 123107), CD11c (cat. no. 117327), CD11b (cat. no. 101207) all from Biolegend. An overview of the gating strategy for each cell population can be found in Extended Data Fig. 8. Cells were then fixed and resuspended in PBS (cells were analyzed using Flow Cytometry (BD LSR II flow cytometer, Becton Dickinson Inc.) at Einstein Flow Cytometry and analyzed with FlowJo software (Tree Star).

Overview of gating strategy for immune cell populations in the IEL and LP of the small intestine.

All cells were stained with anti-CD45-Alexa Fluor 700 and fixable live/dead blue stain. a-b. T cells were co-stained with anti-CD3-FITC, anti-CD4-PE/Cy7, and anti-CD8-Pacific Blue. c-d. For macrophage and dendric cells, populations were co-stained with anti-CD11b-PE, anti-F4/80-FITC, and CD11c-PerCP/Cy5.5.

Organoid assays

ISC function was assessed by organoid formation assay, as described71. Approximately 250 isolated crypts were embedded in Matrigel and cultured in advanced DMEM/F12 containing epidermal growth factor, Noggin, R-spondin and supplemental factors. Organoid formation and budding were quantified on days 5–9 and normalized to initial crypt counts. For screening assays, isolated crypts were incubated with increasing concentrations of TNF (cat. no. 410-MT-010/CF), IFNγ (cat. no. 485-MI), IL-1β (cat. no. 401-ML), IL-12 (cat. no. 419-ML), IL-17 (cat. no. 421-ML) and IL-23 (cat. no. 1887-ML), all from R&D Systems, Inc and Y-27632 (ROCK inhibitor) was excluded from the culture medium. Fresh cytokines were added to the medium every 3 days and, at day 9, the number of organoids were counted and normalized to the beginning number of seeded crypts. Organoid formation was evaluated by counting crypt number and enteroid number on day 1 and day 9, respectively, and was calculated by taking the ratio of day 9 over day 1 counts. For TNF studies, isolated crypts were incubated with different concentrations of TNF (315-01A, PeproTech) on day 2 or 5 after crypt isolation, and RNA extraction was done on day 5 treatment after overnight incubation. For PPAR agonist experiments, 10 μM GW1929 (PPARγ agonist, 10 μM GW7647 (PPARα agonist) 1 μM GW501516 (PPARδ agonist) from Medchemexpress or vehicle (medium with 0.1% dimethylsulfoxide to match agonists) were added with or without TNF, on day 5, reapplied on day 6 and counted on day 7. For targeted inhibition studies, either 5 μM MitoTempo or NOX1 inhibitor were added 1 h before adding TNF on day 5, and organoids counted on day 7. For MASM7 studies, compound was provided to old crypts at day 1 in culture at 0.5, 1 or 2 μM as described72.

Inflammatory profiling

For evaluation of plasma inflammatory status, a Bio-Plex MAGPIX Multiplex Reader (Bio-Rad Inc.) was used to measure 23 inflammatory mediators simultaneously in mouse plasma, as described60.

RNA isolation, expression and sequencing

RNA was isolated from organoids using TRIzol Reagent (TRIzol, Life Technologies) and quantified on a Qubit 2.0 fluorimeter was used to determine RNA concentration. Quantitative PCR with reverse transcription (RT–qPCR) reactions were performed with Bio-Rad SsoAdvanced SYBR Green mix on a Bio-Rad CFX384 RT–qPCR thermocycler. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Information. All data were normalized to β-actin using the delta-delta Ct (2−∆∆Ct) method. For bulk RNA-seq analysis of intestinal organoids, samples were sent to Novogene for quality control, library preparation and sequencing. Reads were trimmed, aligned to the mouse mm39 genome using STAR (v2.7.9a), normalized with DESeq2 (v1.34.0) and analyzed for differential expression (FDR < 0.05). GO analysis was conducted in R with the fgsea package (v1.20.0) using GO databases.

Intestinal scRNA-seq

For single-cell analysis, male C57BL/6J mice (Jackson Laboratories) were obtained at 3 weeks of age and maintained on AIN76A purified diet until killed (D10011, Research Diets Inc.). Two biological replicates for each group were isolated and processed as described73. Excised intestines were opened longitudinally, rinsed with cold saline, single-cell suspensions prepared, resuspended in antibody staining buffer, blocked with FcR blocking reagent (Miltenyi Biotec, cat. no. 130-092-575), washed and pelleted, then incubated for 20 min at 4 °C with EpCAM-APC (Miltenyi Biotec, cat. no. 130-102-234) and CD45-PerCP (Miltenyi Biotec, cat. no. 130-102-469). Cells were sorted by FACs on a MoFlo instrument and Epcam positive, CD45 negative total epithelial cells collected. scRNA-seq libraries were constructed by the Albert Einstein Genomics Core using the 3’ kit protocols of 10x Genomics with approximately 10,000 single FACS collected epithelial cells from each mouse processed on a Chromium Chip B microfluidic apparatus (10x Genomics). Library quality was verified by size analysis of transcripts (BioAnalyzer; Agilent) and, after passing quality control assays, several libraries were multiplexed and sequenced by NovaSeqX Plus PE150 using pair-end sequencing, with a readout length of 150 bases (Novogene) and data assigned to individual cells using the sample indexes.

For sequence alignment and subsequent analysis, output Illumina base call files were converted to FASTQ files (bcl2fastq), these from each mouse aligned to the mouse mm10 genome v1.2.0 and converted to count matrices (Cell Ranger software). A range of 4,000–10,000 individual cells were identified for each sample, and unique molecular identifiers were used to remove PCR duplicates. Quality control and downstream analyses were done in R v4.4.3, using Seurat package v4.1.074. Low-quality cells and potential doublets were filtered out by excluding cells with <200 or >6,000 detected genes or with >20% mitochondrial gene content. Cells from different samples were integrated using Seurat FindIntegrationAnchors and IntegrateData functions.

Cell clustering was performed on the integrated dataset using the FindClusters function, which constructs a shared nearest neighbor graph based on PCA and applies the Louvain modularity optimization algorithm to identify clusters. Cell clusters were annotated using established marker genes for intestinal epithelial cell types, and cluster-specific marker genes were identified using Seurat’s FindMarkers function. Module scores representing average expression of fatty acid metabolism-related genesets were calculated using the AddModuleScore function75, using the same gene list employed for KEGG pathway analysis.

Sequencing and analysis of 16S rRNA

Fecal pellets were collected carefully from parabiotic and cohoused control animals, in a clean home cage between 1000 hours and 1200 hours and frozen immediately on dry ice and stored at −80 °C. 16S rRNA amplicon (16S) and analysis was then performed in the University of Missouri Metagenomics Facility. In brief, fecal DNA was extracted using PowerFecal Pro kits (Qiagen), and quantified using quant-iT HS dsDNA reagent kits and Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (ThermoFisher). 16S libraries were then generated using normalized template, and dual-indexed U515F/806R primers targeting the V4 region, followed by sequencing of 2 × 250 paired-end reads on a MiSeq instrument (Illumina). For analysis, Cutadapt76 was used to remove the primer from the 5’ end of the forward read. Read pairs were rejected if one read or the other did not match a 5’ primer, with an allowed error rate of 0.1. A minimal overlap of 3 base pairs with the 3’ end of the primer sequence was required for removal. The QIIME277 DADA278 plugin (version 1.10.0) was used to denoise, de-replicate and count ASVs, incorporating the following parameters: (1) forward and reverse reads are truncated to 150 bases, (2) forward and reverse reads with number of expected errors higher than 2.0 are discarded and (3) Chimeras are detected using the consensus method and removed. R version 3.5.1 and Biom version 2.1.7 were used in QIIME2. Taxonomies were assigned to final sequences using the most current version of the Silva.v13279 database, using the classify-sklearn procedure.

Seahorse respirometry

The Seahorse XF mitochondrial stress test was performed using a Seahorse XF analyzer in Stable Isotope and Metabolomics Core at Albert Einstein College of Medicine. In brief, intestinal organoids were embedded in Matrigel and plated in a Seahorse cell culture microplate for 5 days. Organoids were then treated with/without TNF overnight and washed with Seahorse XF DMEM medium. The Cartridge was hydrated overnight before the test. For the test, organoid medium were replaced with Seahorse medium (Seahorse XF DMEM medium + 10 mM glucose + 2 mM pyruvate + 2 mM glutamine). Port A, B and C were loaded with 40 μM oligomycin, 2.5 μM FCCP (carbonyl cyanide-p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone) and 40 μM rotenone, respectively. Mitochondria stress test settings were determined according to Ludikhuize and colleagues80. For FAO tests, organoids were switched to test medium containing 2 mM glucose, 0.5 mM L-carnitine in Seahorse XF DMEM medium and incubated at 37 °C in a non-CO2 incubator for 70 min. Palmitate/BSA or BSA alone were added according to the manufacturer’s instructions immediately before the test. Data were normalized using organoid DNA concentration.

U-13C16 palmitate flux analysis

Isolated intestinal crypts from aged control and salicylate-treated mice were incubated ex vivo with U-13C16-labeled palmitic acid (100 µM final concentration; BSA-conjugated) for 1 h to assess fatty acid uptake and oxidative metabolism. Labeling medium additionally contained 0.5 mM L-carnitine to support mitochondrial fatty acid transport. Following incubation, crypts were released from Matrigel using StemCell Technologies dissociation reagent with vigorous trituration followed by gentle rotation (10 min; 20 cycles per minute) before pelleting. Crypts were washed three times with ice-cold PBS to remove extracellular tracer, pelleted (600g, 5 min, 4 °C), and metabolites were extracted for targeted LC–MS analysis.

Intracellular carbon metabolism profiling was performed using a high-pH HILIC method, whereas acetylcarnitine was quantified using a low-pH HILIC method, both conducted by the Mount Sinai Metabolomics Core Facility. Intracellular enrichment of palmitic acid was quantified as a measure of tracer uptake and availability. Incorporation of palmitate-derived 13C into citrate (M+2 isotopologue) was used as an index of fatty-acid-derived acetyl-CoA entry into the TCA cycle. In parallel, labeling of acetylcarnitine (M+2) was assessed to evaluate acetyl-CoA buffering and carnitine-dependent shuttling. Isotopologue distributions were corrected for natural isotope abundance, and fractional enrichment was calculated relative to total metabolite pool size.

Statistics and reproducibility

Cross-sectional data were analyzed by either independent sample t-tests, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (age × treatment). When a significant main effect was detected, planned two-group contrasts with either Dunnett’s multiple comparison or Tukey honest significant difference adjustment was applied. Assays involving in vitro organoids derived from the same animals and split for side-by-side comparisons were assessed by paired t-tests. Survival analysis was performed by the Kaplan–Meier method and log-rank tests. Shapiro–Wilk test was used to test for normal distribution. Data not normally distributed were log-transformed to ensure normality of distribution and analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software. Data collection and analysis of assays described were performed unblinded to the conditions of the experiments. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes but our sample sizes are similar to those reported in previous publications15,70,73. All values are reported as means ± s.e. A value of P ≤ 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.
