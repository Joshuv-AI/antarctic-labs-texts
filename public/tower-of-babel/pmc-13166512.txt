Ultrasonographic parameters comparison for pre- and postoperative carpal tunnel syndrome release: A single-center prospective study
Authors: Kai-Xing Alvin Lee, Yu-Fang Chen, Chen-Wei Yeh, Tsung-Yu Ho, Wei-Chih Wang, Dung-Huan Liu, Pei-Ching Wu, Cheng-En Hsu, Yung-Cheng Chiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is a prevalent condition characterized by median nerve compression, impacting a significant portion of the population worldwide. While neurophysiological testing has been traditionally employed for diagnosis, recent advancements in ultrasonography have shown promise in offering a noninvasive alternative. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the mid-term postoperative statistical changes of the median nerve and flexor retinaculum, share our ultrasonographic measurement methodologies for standardized reporting, and suggest a set of ultrasonographic parameters for postoperative tracking. A prospective cohort of 18 patients with 24 CTS-affected hands underwent comprehensive evaluations including ultrasonography, nerve conduction studies, and functional assessments before and 6 months after surgical intervention. Various ultrasonographic parameters such as cross-sectional area (CSA), bowing of the flexor retinaculum, displacement of median nerve, and flattening ratio were measured at inlet and outlet levels of the carpal tunnel. Additionally, functional outcomes including grip strength, pinch strength, disabilities of the arm, shoulder, and hand score (DASH), visual analog scale (VAS), and World Health Organization Quality of Life score (WHOQOL) were assessed. A total of 18 patients (24 wrists) with CTS were included in this study. In this study, there were 15 females and 3 males, with 12 single-sided surgeries and 6 double-sided surgeries. The mean age was 45 years old with a mean duration of symptoms of 26 months. There were no complications and no reoperations during the follow-up period. Improvements in all postoperative parameters were recorded, with statistically significant improvements noted in CSA at outlet level, bowing of the flexor retinaculum at outlet level, displacement of median nerve at inlet and outlet level, flattening ratio at the outlet level and functional scores. The primary contribution of this study lies not in individual ultrasonographic parameters but in the integration of CSA at the outlet, flexor retinaculum bowing, and median nerve displacement into a unified, clinically feasible assessment framework. This streamlined protocol is rapid, reproducible, and suitable for routine postoperative monitoring, offering greater clinical utility than single-parameter evaluation.

1. Introduction

Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is a common disease caused by compression of the median nerve, affecting approximately 10% of the population in their lifetime.[1,2] Symptoms ranged from paresthesia of fingers (except little finger) to thenar muscle weakness and atrophy.[3] Neurophysiological testing is a common tool for diagnosing CTS, and treatment strategies include conservative treatment for mild CTS to surgical interventions for severe CTS.[4–7] Pastare et al conducted a prospective study on 66 patients comparing the diagnostic value of ultrasonography versus neurophysiological testing for CTS screening.[8] From their results, it could be noted that ultrasonography had a positive predictive value of 100% and thus could be used as a screening test for CTS. Numerous trials had since been conducted and a triad of ultrasonographic diagnostic criteria for CTS were proposed and used in clinical settings which includes enlargement of proximal median nerve, flattening of distal median nerve and bowing of the flexor retinaculum.[9]

CTS is a multifactorial disease and is often related with a decrease in carpal tunnel volume and increase in carpal tunnel pressure. The compressed median nerve is further mechanically damaged by transverse sliding in the decreased carpal tunnel space.[6] Improvements in ultrasonography technologies have enabled us to visualize changes in median nerve morphologies, size, and intraneural blood flow. Recently, there has been an increasing number of articles reporting on ultrasonography and CTS due to its availability, convenience and cost effectiveness.[8,10,11] The shape and size of the median nerve were reported to vary along the carpal tunnel, with flattening at the site of compression, proximal enlargement in inlet level and distal flattening in outlet level. A meta-analysis by Torres-Costoso et al reported that cross-sectional area (CSA) of median nerve at the inlet level could be used for CTS diagnosis (odds ratio of 31.11) and that CSA had a strong association with electrophysiological severity.[12] The main limitation of their meta-analysis was that there were variable methodological differences between all the publications due to different measurement sites, different diagnostic criteria and different evaluation techniques. Standardization of ultrasonographic evaluations for CTS are therefore warranted to ensure proper reporting, diagnosis and for monitoring of treatment strategies.

Various diagnostic strategies with ultrasonography were also reported. Horng et al compared ultrasonography with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and reported that ultrasonographic combination of median nerve CSA in rest position with bowing of flexor retinaculum in grasp position had similar diagnostic accuracy as MRI.[1] Similarly, Azami et al reported that median nerve CSA in inlet level was significantly larger (13.31 ± 3.23 mm2 vs 8.57 ± 0.82 mm2) in the CTS hand compared to the healthy hand and that inlet CSA provided optimal diagnostic accuracy with high sensitivity and specificity.[10] The median nerve mobility of healthy individuals and patients with CTS was compared by Yao et al.[2] It was reported that patients with CTS had significantly reduced nerve mobility. In CTS patients, the slope of nerve mobility was 0.0037 mm/degree and 0.0082 mm/degree in controls, indicating a 54.6% decrease in mobility. As a result, Yao et al concluded that median nerve mobility could be used as a diagnostic tool as well as an evaluation tool for treatment effectiveness. Previously, postoperative ultrasound follow-ups have focused mainly on CSA or nerve mobility. Ng et al[9] demonstrated the feasibility of using different ultrasound parameters and it was reported that abnormal results were still noted even after one year of follow-up. There is currently no consensus regarding follow-up ultrasonographic parameters and durations of follow-ups, therefore, further studies are required to better understand changes in ultrasonographic parameters after carpal tunnel release.

In our study, we prospectively recruited 18 patients with a total of 24 CTS hands and conducted a comprehensive preoperative ultrasonography, functional and electrophysiological survey, including inlet and outlet CSA, bowing of flexor retinaculum, displacement, flattening ratio, grip strength, pinch strength, nerve conduction study, disabilities of the arm, shoulder and hand score (DASH), visual analog score (VAS) and World Health Organization Quality of Life score (WHOQOL). All patients underwent surgical intervention of carpal tunnel release and were evaluated 6 months postoperatively. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to evaluate the mi statistical changes of the median nerve and flexor retinaculum, share our ultrasonographic measurement methodologies for standardized reporting, and suggest a set of ultrasonographic parameters for postoperative tracking.

2. Methods

2.1. Patient cohort

This study was approved by the ethics committee of China Medical University Hospital, Taichung, Taiwan and conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration (IRB approval number CMUH109-REC2-184). Written informed consents were obtained from all participants. From January 2024 to December 2024, 18 patients with 24 moderate to severe CTS were assessed for eligibility and prospectively recruited from China Medical University Hospital, Taichung, Taiwan. CTS patients were included based on their clinical symptoms and signs, which were supported by imaging (ultrasonography) and electrophysiological studies (NCV). The inclusion criteria were as follows: nocturnal burning, pins-and-needles sensation, numbness, or weakness in the hands, positive clinical results, including Phalen and Tinel signs, ultrasonographic evaluation and nerve conduction studies. The ultrasonographic criteria included inlet CSA > 9 mm2, inlet and outlet bowing > 2 mm, and flattening of outlet CSA. Exclusion criteria include gout, sarcoidosis, amyloidosis, wrist and forearm traumatic injuries, cervical radiculopathy, rheumatoid arthritis, patients with missing data or incomplete follow-ups. Patients with multiple and severe co-morbidites were also excluded. All patients completed at least 6 months of postoperative follow-ups at our department. CTS severity was evaluated according to the Boston Carpal Tunnel Syndrome Questionnaire.

2.2. Surgical procedures: dual mini-incisions operative techniques for carpal tunnel release

All surgeries were performed by an individual senior hand surgeon using the same technique. Two mini incisions were made in the process of carpal tunnel release. Firstly, a longitudinal incision of approximately 3 cm was created on the distal margin of the transverse carpal ligament, along the radial border of the hypothenar muscle. Secondly, another transverse incision of about 3 cm was made on the proximal margin of the transverse carpal ligament, just above the distal volar wrist crease. The underlying fascial layer was then incised longitudinally, exposing the median nerve with great care. While ensuring the protection of the median nerve, subcutaneous transverse carpal ligament release was performed through these 2 mini-incisions. After achieving adequate decompression of the carpal tunnel, the 2 mini-incisions were closed in layers following meticulous hemostasis and fluid irrigation.

2.3. Post-operative care

Suture removal took place 2 weeks after the operation, and patients were allowed for active finger movement since postoperative day 1. Subsequently, patients were permitted to gradually resume their normal daily activities in the following 2 weeks. They were advised to return to their full activity level without any further restrictions one month postoperatively. Two months after surgery, the surgical wounds had healed with no visible scars.

2.4. Evaluation

Patients were evaluated prior to surgery and at 6 months postoperatively using ultrasonographic examinations, NCV, and functional evaluations including DASH, WHOQOL, VAS and grip strength. Ultrasonography was performed using a 12-MHz linear array transducer (GE LOGIQ 9, General Electric Medical Systems, Milwaukee). The patients were asked to be seated with arm extension and forearm supination on a medical table. All ultrasonographic assessments were performed by a single board-certified specialist using a standardized protocol to minimize variability. The transducer was placed perpendicular to the median nerve, and pressure on the hand was always avoided during the ultrasonography to minimize measurement errors. For CSA, bowing, displacement and flattening ratio, measurements were obtained from transverse ultrasonography of the median nerve at the inlet and outlet levels. The inlet level was determined using the proximal edge of the flexor retinaculum, coupled with identification of bony landmarks of pisiform and scaphoid (Fig. 1). The outlet level was then determined by tracing the median nerve till the distal edge of the flexor retinaculum, coupled with identification of bony landmarks of hook of hamate and trapezium (Fig. 1).

(A) Schematic diagram of cross-sectional area measurement, (B) Schematic diagram of flexor retinaculum bowing measurement, (C) Schematic diagram of median nerve displacement measurement (Grasp-Rest). Note the anatomical structures of pisiform/ scaphoid and hook of hamate/ trapezium at the inlet and outlet levels respectively.

CSA was estimated using the in-built measurement tool to measure the width and height of the median nerve in the cross-sectional image. The perineural hyperechogenic rim was excluded from the measurement. CSA was then estimated using the formula: CSA = π × (width/2) × (height/2) (Fig. 1A). The bowing of the flexor retinaculum was determined by measuring the distance between the outer surface of the retinaculum and a tangential line that connects specific bony landmarks situated at both the inlet and outlet of the retinaculum. At the inlet level, a tangential line was drawn between the scaphoid tubercle and the pisiform bone, whilst at the outlet level, the tangential line was drawn connecting the trapezium tubercle and the hook of hamate. Using the tangential line as a guide, we measured retinacular bowing as a distance perpendicular to this line till the outermost point of the retinaculum (Fig. 1B). Displacement was measured by calculating the differences in distances between the grasp and rest positions, with distance defined as the separation between the medial tip of the hamate or pisiform and the midpoint of the median nerve (Fig. 1C). The flattening ratio was calculated using the ratio of width of median nerve to height of median nerve. The grip strength was measured using a handheld dynamometer. The palmar pinch force of the thumb and index finger was measured using a dynamometer. All measurements were done in triplicates with the average taken. In addition, patients were tasked to complete the WHOQOL-BREF, DASH and VAS scores prior to surgery and at every follow-ups. The WHOQOL-BREF is a streamlined version of the WHOQOL that is inclusive of 26 items across 4 domains: physical health, psychological well-being, social relationships, and environment. Additionally, 2 items are included for assessing life quality and health status. Domain scores originally ranged from 0 to 20, with higher aggregate scores indicating a higher quality of life. The DASH is a 30 items questionnaire with 21 questions concerning function, 6 questions concerning severity and 3 questions concerning psychosocial factors. NCS measurements included the distal median nerve motor latency conduction velocity was measured from the wrist to the thenar eminence.

2.5. Statistical analyses

SPSS software (Version 20) was used for statistical analyses, with a P-value of <.05 considered statistically significant. Paired t-test was used to compare preoperative and postoperative parameters including the CSA, bowing of retinaculum, displacement, flattening ratio, grip and pinch strength, WHOQOL-BREF, DASH, VAS scores and NCV results. Effect sizes were estimated using Cohen d (mean difference divided by pooled standard deviation). This study was designed as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating investigation. No a priori sample size calculation was performed due to the lack of standardized effect size estimates for postoperative ultrasonographic parameters at the time of study conception. Given the exploratory nature of this study and the assessment of multiple correlated ultrasonographic, functional, and electrophysiological outcomes, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to mitigate the risk of type I error across all primary paired comparisons (Supplementary 1, Supplemental Digital Content).

3. Results

A total of 18 patients (24 wrists) with CTS were included in this study. In this study, there were 15 females and 3 males, with 12 single-sided surgeries. A flow chart detailing our patient’s inclusion, diagnosis and exclusion were as shown in Figure 2. The mean age was 45 ± 7 years old with a mean duration of symptoms of 26 ± 5.5 months. 91.7% of the patients experienced median nerve distribution numbness, 45.8% had thenar muscle atrophy, and half of these patients had dysfunction of thumb opposition. Furthermore, almost all the patients experienced positive Tinel sign (95.8%), Phalen sign (91.7%) and Durkan test (95.8%). 50% of the patients were diagnosed with severe CTS. There were no complications and reoperations reported during the follow-up period. All patients had tried conservative treatment such as oral analgesia and localized injection treatments before surgery but to no avail. 4. Discussion

Currently, there are numerous publications reporting on the sensitivity, specificity and low false positive rates of ultrasonographic evaluation for diagnosis of various diseases. There is an increasing trend to use ultrasonography as a diagnostic tool due to its convenience and low costs. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the mid-term postoperative statistical changes of the median nerve and flexor retinaculum, share our ultrasonographic measurement methodologies for standardized reporting, and suggest a set of ultrasonographic parameters for postoperative tracking. In this prospective study, a total of 24 CTS wrists were included and all patients underwent preoperative and postoperative 6 months ultrasonography examinations. There is also no consensus regarding the optimal duration of postoperative follow-ups for CTS. As a result of the possibility of inflammation in the early postoperative stages, we proposed a six-month follow-up period. The decision is consistent with Li et al’s findings,[4] whereby no significant differences were observed between postoperative parameters at 3 days and 2 weeks. They observed significant improvements after 3 months, indicating that longer follow-up periods may offer more meaningful and significant results. Furthermore, in the study by Ng et al, retinaculum bowing was noted only at 12 months of follow-ups. In the initial 1 and 3 months of follow-up, retinaculum bowing was noted to decrease as compared to preoperative data. Ultrasonography and MRI/MR-neurography offer complementary information for CTS.[1] For morphological diagnosis, ultrasonography and MRI provide comparable CSA measurements and grading utility, with ultrasonographic retinacular bowing and MRI nerve signal changes both improving diagnostic confidence.[2] MRI adds value by detecting associated pathologies (e.g., masses, anomalous muscles) when clinical/electrodiagnostic findings are discordant or symptoms persist postoperatively. In contrast, US is dynamic, cost-effective, and repeatable at the bedside, enabling serial assessment of CSA, bowing, and displacement during rehabilitation. In postoperative contexts, both modalities can document decompression and retinacular status; high-resolution MRI visualizes the entire canal, whereas US uniquely captures dynamic nerve/tendon motion. Choice of modality should reflect clinical question, availability, and cost considerations. MR-guided focused ultrasound combines real-time MR guidance and thermometry with focused ultrasound energy for noninvasive ablation, with proven clinical roles in other conditions (e.g., essential tremor). Its technical principles and safety monitoring are well described. To date, there is no clinical evidence supporting MR-guided focused ultrasound as a decompressive therapy for CTS, and it is not recommended as a treatment option for CTS.[13,14] Future translational research may explore whether targeted, image-guided energy delivery has a role in peripheral entrapment neuropathies, but current CTS management remains centered on conservative care, injection, and surgical release.

Our ultrasonographic protocols were relatively simple to execute, with patients seated comfortably having their arms extended and forearm supinated. Images were captured only at the inlet and outlet area without any probe compression, and patients were only asked to grasp twice, once at the inlet and once at the outlet area. To minimize variability, the patients were asked to grasp with only the minimal amount of force. Furthermore, the wrists were kept in a neutral position throughout the scan. The inlet and outlet area were easy to recognize by identifying the proximal and distal ends of the flexor retinaculum and their respective bony landmarks. A triad of palmar bowing (>2 mm), distal flattening of nerve and enlargement of proximal nerve (normal CSA: 9–11 mm2) were generally recommended for diagnosis.[2,13] Azami et al reported that CTS patients had significantly larger CSA at the inlet level as compared to healthy patients and that 9.15 mm2 at the inlet level had the optimal sensitivity and specificity for diagnosis.[10] It was also concluded from their study that CSA at the inlet level could be used as a marker for differentiating mild, moderate and severe CTS. From our results, all our patients included had met the criteria of palmar bowing of >2 mm at both the inlet and outlet levels, had preoperative inlet CSA of 13.58 ± 6.29 mm2 with distal flattening of nerve at the outlet level. Although Azami et al did not include postoperative tracking in their study, CSA measurements at the inlet and outlet levels may be useful for postoperative monitoring. This is because inflammation occurs mainly within the carpal tunnel and could lead to great variability in measurements.[14]

There are 2 anatomical sites of compression in the carpal tunnel, one at proximal tunnel inlet during flexion and one near the outlet due to the presence of the hook of hamate.[15] From our results, it could be noted that preoperative and postoperative outlet CSA and bowing were lower than the inlet. Entrapment of the median nerve caused by increased carpal tunnel pressure was reported to cause ischemic vascular injuries and breakdown in blood-nerve barrier, thereby increasing vascular permeability and accumulation of inflammatory mediators and proteins.[16,17] Histological studies by Liu et al showed that such median nerves had perineural and endoneural micro-vessels thickening with basement membrane replication, Renault’s body formation and fibrotic changes.[17] Therefore, CSA of CTS patients were often noted to be increased as compared to healthy patients. Ng et al reported that postoperative changes in median nerve and retinaculum were most significant at the outlet level, which was similarly observed in our study.[13,16] In their study, it was hypothesized that the outlet was the main area of nerve compression, thus leading to significant improvements after surgery. However, even in patients with postoperative clinical improvements, approximately all parameters were still abnormal 12 months postoperatively. Therefore, our team agreed with Ng et al that the postoperative cutoff range for pathological CTS should not be compared with normal healthy values. According to their study, inlet CSA increased after 1 month, remained stable after 3 months, and decreased 12 months following surgery. Despite these changes, the CSA values were still considered abnormal when compared to healthy controls. Comparatively, our results showed a consistent increase in CSA postoperatively at 6 months, both at the inlet and outlet levels. Furthermore, another study focused on inlet CSA also reported consistent increment of CSA at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively, confirming the trend of an increase in CSA size at the inlet site.[4] According to previous reports, decrease in carpal tunnel pressure due to decompression and decreased inflammatory responses led to expansion of median nerve, which was observed in this study.[18] In addition, our results showed improved nerve conductivity of the median nerve, thus indicating that the increased CSA did not affect nerve conductivity. Although median nerve CSA enlargement is traditionally considered pathological in untreated CTS, postoperative CSA increases should be interpreted differently. At 6 months, CSA enlargement likely reflects a combination of transient intraneural edema, increased vascular permeability, elastic expansion following decompression, and early structural remodeling rather than recurrent compression.[4,16,18] Previous longitudinal studies have demonstrated that CSA may remain enlarged at 6 to 12 months and gradually decrease thereafter, with normalization often occurring beyond 12 months. Therefore, postoperative CSA enlargement at mid-term follow-up should be regarded as a marker of decompression-related adaptation rather than disease persistence.

An increase in pressure and connective tissue remodeling within the carpal tunnel may be associated with preoperative bowing of the flexor retinaculum, with inflammation potentially contributing to this process.[14] Our team hypothesized that the increase in postoperative bowing occurred due to successful release of flexor retinaculum and its fibrotic bands, thus allowing the flexor retinaculum to be more laxed to accommodate for expansion of the median nerve as noted by our increased CSA. This was also supported by MRI and ultrasonographic findings reported by Ng et al where bowing increased significantly at 1 month postoperatively, then progressively decreased over the following months, with approximately 70% of the retinaculum reformed by 12 months.[13,16] While previous studies focused on follow-up periods at 3 and 12 months postoperatively, our study provides additional insights into ultrasonographic data at the 6-month postoperative interval. Although CSA enlargement is often associated with pathology, postoperative increases may represent transient expansion and remodeling of the decompressed nerve. Prior studies have similarly observed CSA enlargement after release, which was not accompanied by deterioration in nerve conduction or clinical outcomes. Therefore, postoperative CSA should be interpreted as a marker of successful decompression rather than disease recurrence, particularly in the mid-term follow-up period. Increased retinaculum bowing was deemed as a satisfactory indicator for CTS decompression surgeries whereby the goal for retinaculum release was to allow it to reform in a more elongated and bowed formation to increase intra-tunnel area.[19,20] Furthermore, incomplete release of retinaculum was further reported to be the most common cause of persistent symptoms after surgeries.[9,15,21] Significant improvements in retinaculum bowing were noted in both inlet and outlet levels at all timepoints except for our results showing no statistically significant improvements at inlet level of postoperative 6 months.

Median nerve displacement also serves as a good marker for diagnosis and postoperative tracking. Yao et al reported that median nerve mobility in the carpal tunnel was significantly reduced in CTS patients as compared to healthy populations.[2,22] No significance was noted in the middle and distal segment due to structural compliance and size of the carpal tunnel, therefore emphasizing our point that ultrasonographic evaluation should be done at the inlet and outlet level. Our results showed that there were significant improvements in postoperative displacement of the median nerve at both inlet and outlet level, thus indicating that our surgeries were successful in relieving carpal tunnel pressure, allowing for increased space for nerve mobility. In the study published by Yao et al, their measurement protocol included measuring nerve mobility from 0 to 90 degrees of wrist flexion at the proximal, middle and distal segments. According to our experience, this could be clinically difficult to execute due to pain, discomfort and possibility of variability of finger flexion on nerve mobility. Furthermore, the variability of grasp strength may affect reliability of ultrasonography for diagnosis. Therefore, our protocol simplified to ask patients to grasp twice with only the minimal amount of force for measurement of postoperative displacement of the median nerve under ultrasonography.

On the other hand, flattening ratio is considered as not a reliable indicator for postoperative follow-ups.[16] Reports regarding flattening ratio were inconsistent and variable. This observation was also reported by postoperative ultrasound follow-ups whereby the median nerve remained flattened even after carpal tunnel release due to fibrosis from chronic compression.[20–22] Most importantly, improvements in the ultrasonographic parameters were observed to have positive correlation with functional outcomes and NCV results, whereby our patients were able to achieve significant pain reduction and improved functional outcomes.[3] However, a limitation of our study was the small sample size involved which would lead to Type II errors. Although no a priori sample size calculation was performed, a post hoc power analysis was conducted based on the observed effect sizes for the primary ultrasonographic and functional outcomes. Effect sizes were estimated using Cohen d (mean difference divided by pooled standard deviation). For CSA at the outlet, bowing at the outlet, and displacement at the inlet, the effect sizes were moderate to large (d = 0.50–0.63), corresponding to a post hoc power of 64 to 85% at α = 0.05. Functional outcomes such as DASH score and VAS demonstrated large effect sizes (d > 1.0) with power exceeding 90%. In contrast, parameters such as CSA at the inlet showed very small effect sizes (d = 0.07) and low power (6%), indicating limited sensitivity for detecting changes in this measure. These results suggest that while the study is adequately powered to detect medium-to-large effects, it is underpowered for small effects. The small sample size may limit the ability to detect subtle changes, as reflected in the low post hoc power for some parameters (e.g., CSA at the inlet). However, the study demonstrated adequate power (>0.80) for clinically relevant outcomes including outlet CSA, bowing, displacement, DASH, and VAS. Nevertheless, larger multicenter studies with a priori power calculations are needed to confirm these findings and evaluate long-term outcomes. FDR correction was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure across all primary paired comparisons. Only VAS and WHOQOL-BREF remained statistically significant after FDR adjustment. All other parameters are interpreted as exploratory trends. Furthermore, these findings represent mid-term postoperative ultrasonographic trends rather than long-term structural normalization.

As there are currently no consensus regarding ultrasonographic evaluations for postoperative follow-ups tracking, our team would like to share our simple, quick and reliable ultrasonographic protocol for follow-up tracking of mid-term postoperative carpal tunnel release. According to our results and with references to recent publications, increase in CSA at the carpal tunnel outlet level, increase bowing of the flexor retinaculum at the carpal tunnel outlet level, improved nerve displacement at both the inlet and outlet levels were observed as useful markers for mid-term postoperative follow-up tracking. In addition to the small sample size, our study is limited by its single-center recruitment, which may restrict generalizability. Ultrasonographic assessments were not blinded, introducing potential observer bias. Follow-up was limited to 6 months, and therefore represents mid-term rather than long-term outcomes. Since structural remodeling may continue up to 12 months or longer, future studies should include extended follow-up intervals to better capture long-term changes. Finally, patients were not stratified by CTS severity despite approximately half of the cohort being classified as severe. Disease severity is known to influence CSA magnitude, nerve mobility, and functional recovery. Pooling moderate and severe cases may have masked severity-specific ultrasonographic trajectories, particularly for inlet-level parameters. Future studies with larger cohorts should stratify analyses by severity to clarify differential postoperative remodeling patterns. While these parameters can provide valuable insight into the success of carpal tunnel release, caution should be exercised when interpreting post-operative retinaculum bowing, as reformation of the retinaculum may introduce bias into the assessment of its clinical significance. However, the ultrasonographic parameters should be considered in conjunction with the functional outcomes after surgery. The novelty of our study lies not in the isolated parameters, which have been previously reported, but in the integration of multiple ultrasonographic measures into a standardized and simplified protocol. This combined approach provides a practical framework for postoperative tracking and could serve as a reference for future consensus development. However, several ultrasonographic parameters, particularly inlet CSA and inlet flattening ratio, demonstrated very small effect sizes with low post hoc power (6–8%), indicating that this study was underpowered to detect subtle morphological changes. Based on observed variance, the minimal detectable effect size for inlet CSA exceeded clinically plausible changes at 6 months. These parameters should therefore be interpreted cautiously and confirmed in larger cohorts. Lastly, inter-rater reliability could not be assessed as all measurements were performed by a single operator. Although standardized acquisition protocols were used, intra-observer repeatability was not formally quantified, which should be addressed in future studies.

The primary contribution of this study lies not in individual ultrasonographic parameters but in the integration of CSA at the outlet, flexor retinaculum bowing, and median nerve displacement into a unified, clinically feasible assessment framework. This streamlined protocol is rapid, reproducible, and suitable for routine postoperative monitoring, offering greater clinical utility than single-parameter evaluation.
