A 24‐year‐old male, a known case of diarrhea‐predominant Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS‐D), presented with asymptomatic multiple symmetrical follicular keratotic papules on the lateral aspect of the thigh for the last 6 months, Figure 1. The patient had episodes of gum bleeding. The patient denied any dietary restriction; however, he had chronic diarrhea on and off for the last 1 year. Systemic examination did not reveal any abnormality. He denied history of personal or familial atopy. Dermoscopic images are shown, Figure 2. Coagulation profile was normal.

Discussion

Patient presented with asymptomatic multiple symmetrical follicular papules on extremities accompanied with gum bleeding and chronic diarrhea. Dermoscopy showed corkscrew hair, perifollicular ecchymotic halo, and follicular plugging, Figure 2. Serum Vitamin C was also found to be significantly low, that is, 1.1 mg/L (Reference range: 4–15 mg/L). Based on clinical, dermoscopic, and laboratory parameters patient was diagnosed to have scurvy. Patient was started on 1 g Vitamin C daily for 2 weeks. Improvement in the skin lesions was observed clinically as well as on dermoscopy, Figure 3.

(A, B) Follicular keratotic papules on thigh healing with hyperpigmentation. (C) Dermoscopy shows subsiding perifollicular ecchymotic halo.

Scurvy is an uncommon multi organ systemic disease caused due to deficiency of Vitamin C [1]. Vitamin C is essential in the formation of collagen. Disruption in the collagen structure leads to loss of integrity of the hair, connective tissue, and blood vessels [2]. Risk factors for scurvy include limited intake of foods high in vitamin C, iron overload, anorexia nervosa, malabsorptive conditions, and dialysis. Chronic diarrhea might have likely contributed to inadequate vitamin C absorption and subsequent development of scurvy in our patient.

Scurvy may present with subtle and easily overlooked signs, often leading to delayed or missed diagnosis [3]. As histopathological examination is invasive, dermoscopy can serve as a valuable non‐invasive tool in the early recognition of scurvy, revealing characteristic features such as perifollicular hemorrhages, corkscrew hairs, and follicular plugging. Diet history and underlying disease should be reviewed in patients with clinical suspicion as well. Supplementation with Vitamin C shows complete resolution of skin lesions within weeks as seen in our patient [2].
