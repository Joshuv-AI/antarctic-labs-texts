Ischemic preconditioning modulates the acute post-exercise inflammatory, angiogenic, and neurotrophic response in endurance runners
Authors: Paulina Brzezińska, Andrzej Kochanowicz, Andżelika Borkowska, Magdalena Kochanowicz, Jędrzej Antosiewicz, Jan Mieszkowski, Błażej Stankiewicz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled trial examined whether a 21-day ischemic preconditioning (IPC) intervention modifies acute inflammatory, angiogenic, and neurotrophic responses to intense endurance exercise in competitive male long-distance runners. Forty runners were allocated to IPC (n = 20) or SHAM (n = 20), and 35 completed the study. Each participant performed a 20 × 400 m interval running test before and after the intervention, with blood collected before exercise, immediately after exercise, and 24 h post-exercise. Before the intervention, the running trial induced significant time-dependent increases in all inflammatory biomarkers and in all angiogenic and neurotrophic markers. After the intervention, IPC was associated with higher post-exercise IL-10 and IL-15 responses than SHAM, with peak between-group differences of 27% and 35%, respectively. Significant group × time interactions were also observed for BDNF and sAPPα; only SHAM showed a significant post-exercise BDNF increase, whereas sAPPα increased more strongly in IPC. In addition, ANG and NGF concentrations were overall 17% and 16% higher, respectively, in IPC than in SHAM. These findings indicate that repeated IPC modulates selected acute molecular responses to intense endurance exercise in a marker-specific manner, although the underlying mechanisms require further investigation.Registry: ClinicalTrials.gov, TRN: NCT05412888, Registration date: 09/06/2022.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52742-x.

Introduction

The interplay between exercise and inflammation is a major focus of contemporary research, particularly in understanding how physical activity modulates inflammatory responses and contributes to post-exercise recovery. Recent studies have increasingly emphasized the inflammatory processes associated with prolonged endurance efforts (e.g., marathons, triathlons, ultramarathons), which are characterized by high energy demands and substantial physiological stress, often resulting in a marked increase in circulating proinflammatory cytokines1–5.

Long-distance running and other forms of prolonged exercise (such as triathlon or long-distance swimming) are exceptionally demanding due to their high metabolic cost and pronounced impact on skeletal muscle integrity. These activities induce a systemic inflammatory response, evidenced by elevated plasma concentrations of cytokines such as monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-1 (IL-1)1,3. Sustained physical exertion has been shown to increase the levels of these inflammatory mediators, which may contribute to the deterioration of both cardiac and skeletal muscle function6,7.

In addition to inflammation, numerous studies have demonstrated that intense and prolonged physical activity stimulates the expression and release of angiogenic and neurotrophic factors, which contribute to vascular remodeling, neuroplasticity, and exercise-induced adaptation. Aerobic endurance exercises such as running, cycling, and swimming have been shown to elevate serum levels of angiogenic proteins, including angiogenin and angiopoietin-1, as well as neurotrophic factors like brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), nerve growth factor (NGF), and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1). These proteins promote endothelial cell proliferation, synaptic plasticity, and neurogenesis, and are considered crucial mediators of the beneficial effects of exercise on both muscular and cognitive performance8,9.

Angiogenesis and neurotrophic signaling are increasingly recognized as essential components of the body’s adaptive response to training. Specifically, BDNF and IGF-1 are key mediators of neural and muscular plasticity, while ANGPT-1 and angiogenin contribute to vascular integrity and capillarization in skeletal muscles. The repeated stimulation of these factors by endurance training suggests that they play a vital role in improving oxygen delivery, recovery, and fatigue resistance. Consequently, understanding how ischemic preconditioning modulates these pathways could provide new insights into optimizing training adaptation and performance.

In light of the aforementioned considerations and the ongoing search for methods to protect the athlete’s body from the adverse effects of intense physical exertion, recent investigations have highlighted the potential of ischemic preconditioning (IPC) as a strategy to enhance recovery and attenuate post-exercise inflammation in physically active individuals. IPC involves repeated short cycles of limb ischemia and reperfusion, typically induced by the inflation and deflation of a pneumatic cuff. This intervention has been shown to exert protective effects on various organs, including the heart, skeletal muscle, liver, and brain10–16.

However, several studies have reported inconsistent outcomes during IPC exposure, suggesting that the efficacy of IPC may depend on individual variability, with participants being classified as either responders or non-responders17,18. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying IPC’s modulation of the post-exercise response is essential for understanding its potential role in exercise-induced adaptation and its implications for athletic performance.

One of the proposed mechanisms underlying the protective effects of ischemic preconditioning involves activation of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), a transcription factor that coordinates cellular adaptation to reduced oxygen availability19–21. Following stabilization under ischemic or hypoxic conditions, HIF-1α translocates to the nucleus, binds to hypoxia response elements (HREs), and promotes the expression of genes involved in angiogenesis and metabolic adaptation21,22. Among the best-characterized downstream targets of HIF-1 are vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1)23,24. Through these responses, HIF-1 signaling may facilitate oxygen delivery and support cellular adaptation to ischemic stress19,21.

These observations suggest that IPC may influence the acute post-exercise response through mechanisms involving not only inflammatory regulation but also angiogenic and neurotrophic signaling. Although previous studies have mainly focused on post-exercise inflammation and oxidative stress, much less is known about whether IPC modulates angiogenic and neurotrophic mediators in endurance-trained athletes. This gap appears particularly relevant for markers such as BDNF, NGF, IGF-1, and sAPPα, which may contribute to vascular plasticity, neuroprotection, and exercise-related adaptation.

Therefore, this study aimed to comprehensively evaluate the influence of a 21-day IPC intervention on acute post-exercise inflammatory, angiogenic, and neurotrophic responses in long-distance runners. We hypothesized that IPC modulates exercise-induced molecular signaling by attenuating inflammatory markers and enhancing the release of angiogenic and neurotrophic factors, thereby promoting more favorable adaptive outcomes in endurance athletes.

Materials and methods

Experimental overview

The overall study protocol, including randomization, blinding, and intervention procedures, closely followed the methodology described by Brzezińska et al.25, with minor adjustments relevant to the present study objectives. Briefly, participants were randomly assigned in a double-blind manner to either the ischemic preconditioning (IPC) group or the sham-control (SHAM) group and completed a 21-day intervention period. During the initial laboratory visit, conducted in the morning before the intervention, baseline anthropometric assessments were performed, including measurements of age, height, and body composition by bioelectrical impedance analysis (InBody 720, Seoul, South Korea), and venous blood samples were collected. On the following day, all participants performed a standardized interval running protocol consisting of 20 × 400 m repetitions with progressively increasing intensity, as specified by the study design. Blood samples were obtained before exercise, immediately after exercise (within 5 min), and 24 h post-exercise. After completion of the 21-day IPC or SHAM intervention, the same running protocol and blood sampling schedule were repeated to assess intervention-related changes in acute post-exercise biomarker responses. Blood samples were centrifuged, aliquoted, and subsequently analyzed for selected inflammatory, angiogenic, and neurotrophic biomarkers. Laboratory analyses were performed at the Gdańsk University of Physical Education and Sport (Gdańsk, Poland), Kazimierz Wielki University (Bydgoszcz, Poland), and the Medical University of Gdańsk (Gdańsk, Poland). Participants

Forty competitive male long-distance runners were enrolled and randomly allocated to either the experimental group (IPC; n = 20) or the placebo-controlled group (SHAM; n = 20) (Fig. 2). During the study period, four participants from the IPC group and one from the SHAM group withdrew from the intervention and were excluded from further analysis, yielding a final cohort of 35 athletes who completed the full study protocol. All participants had a verified history of at least five completed marathon races, with finishing times ranging from 2 h 55 min to 3 h 30 min. Prior to enrollment, all runners underwent a comprehensive medical evaluation, including health screening and physician clearance, to confirm the absence of any known chronic diseases or medication use in the six months preceding the study. Additionally, during the month before the intervention, participants were instructed to abstain from alcohol and any substances known to potentially influence exercise performance or physiological outcomes, such as caffeine, guarana, theine, chocolate, and related products. Moreover, two weeks before the study after dietary consultation, all participants adopted a similar eating pattern calculated individually for their sex, age, work, and physical activity. During the 21-day intervention period, participants were instructed to maintain their usual endurance-training routines and to avoid introducing additional high-intensity sessions, unusual recovery strategies, or substantial changes in overall physical activity. Study protocol was accepted by the Bioethics Committee for Clinical Research at the Regional Medical Chamber in Gdańsk (consent No. KB 29/22) and conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki and registered as a clinical trial NCT05412888 (registration date: 09/06/2022). All participants provided written informed consent to take part in the study and were briefed on the study procedures. However, they were not informed about the underlying rationale or objectives of the study, in order to maintain their naivety regarding the possible effects of ischemic preconditioning procedures.

Remote ischemic preconditioning procedure

The IPC intervention was applied under standardized conditions, following protocols by Brzezińska et al.25. Inflation and deflation of the cuffs were performed manually using a thigh sphygmomanometer (SB45090, Nasco Healthcare, Saugerties, NY, USA). Procedures were conducted in the morning (08:00–10:00) with participants in a supine position. In the IPC group, bilateral thigh cuffs were inflated to 220 mmHg for four cycles of 5-min occlusion followed by 5-min reperfusion. Participants remained supine for 15 min pre- and post-intervention to stabilize cardiovascular parameters. Occlusion efficacy was confirmed via Doppler ultrasonography (Edan DUS 60, SonoTrax Basic, Edan Instruments GmbH, Germany), performed by a certified sonographer in accordance with Polish Ultrasound Society standards. The SHAM group underwent the same timing protocol with unilateral cuff inflation (dominant leg) at 20 mmHg, insufficient to induce arterial occlusion.

Running trial: 20 × 400 m interval protocol

To induce intracellular damage associated with physical exercise and running, an interval training running trial was performed at both the beginning and end of the 21-day experiment consisting of 20 repetitions of 400 m, interspersed with a 200 m active recovery period (jogging break lasting between 1 min 15 s and 1 min 20 s). The running trials started at 8:00 a.m. at Zawisza Stadium in Bydgoszcz, Kuyavian-Pomeranian Voivodeship, Poland, with temperature conditions from 13 to 16 °C. The weather was clear, with no additional wind. The selected running trial represents a traditional training method used in long-distance running and is known to elicit substantial physiological and biochemical responses due to its intensity and duration. All experimental running activities were conducted in April 2024, with consistent weather conditions across both sessions, ensuring no impact on the running outcomes. The 20 × 400 m interval protocol was selected because it represents a demanding and ecologically relevant model commonly used in endurance training, while offering greater experimental standardization than a marathon or prolonged continuous run. It enabled precise control of exercise intensity, recovery intervals, environmental conditions, and blood sampling time points, which was essential for comparing acute biomarker responses before and after the 21-day intervention. Given the varying skill levels of the study participants, a tailored training module was developed. This module was based on the participants’ best 5 km run times, recorded 21 days prior to the main experiment. Prior to the running trial, each participant was provided with a personalized schedule detailing the specific times for each segment. The time constraints for each subsequent running segment were systematically reduced in relation to the previous one, according to Brzezińska et al.25. Three weeks before the main experiment, test–retest reliability of the 20 × 400 m running protocol was assessed in 23 participants. The two assessments were conducted at a one-week interval under comparable measurement conditions. Running pace across consecutive sections of the test demonstrated good to excellent reliability, with ICC(A,1) values ranging from 0.781 to 0.943 and CV values from 2.59 to 5.09% (Supplementary Table S1). These findings support the reproducibility of the protocol as a standardized exercise model for repeated physiological assessment.

Sample collection and biomarker measurements

Blood samples were taken at three different time points: right before, immediately after, and 24 h following a 20 × 400 m running trial, using 5 mL BD Vacutainer Clot Activator Tubes (Becton Dickinson and Company, NJ, USA).

The serum was isolated through centrifugation at 4000×g for 10 min and divided into 500 μL aliquots. The samples were frozen and stored at − 80 °C for a maximum of 6 months until they were analyzed further.

The MAGPIX fluorescence-based detection system (Luminex Corp., Austin, TX, USA) and Luminex assays (Luminex Corp.) were used to measure selected inflammatory, angiogenic, and neurotrophic biomarkers, including interleukins 6, 10, and 15 (IL-6, IL-10, IL-15), angiogenin (ANG), angiopoietin-1 (ANGPT1), growth differentiation factor-15 (GDF-15), brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), nerve growth factor (NGF), insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), and soluble amyloid precursor protein-α (sAPPα).

Blood samples for the determination of basic hematological parameters were analyzed in an accredited laboratory—Synevo (Synevo Laboratory, Bydgoszcz, Poland; PN-EN ISO 15189) using fully automated clinical analyzers.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for all measured variables. Test–retest reliability of running time across four consecutive sections of the 20 × 400 m protocol was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient derived from a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement for single measurements [ICC(A,1)], standard error of measurement (SEM), typical error of measurement (TEM), coefficient of variation (CV), bias, and 95% limits of agreement (LoA). To assess the effects of the 21-day ischemic preconditioning (IPC) intervention on inflammatory, angiogenic, and neurotrophic responses to the 20 × 400 m running trial, a two-way mixed-design ANOVA was used, with group (IPC vs. SHAM) as the between-subject factor and time (pre-exercise, immediately post-exercise, and 24 h post-exercise) as the within-subject factor. Separate analyses were performed for the pre-intervention and post-intervention running trials. Running time across four consecutive sections of the 20 × 400 m test, as well as average 400-m running time, was additionally analyzed using separate two-way mixed-design ANOVAs, with group as the between-subject factor and intervention time (before vs. after the intervention) as the within-subject factor. Resting hematological and biochemical variables measured before and after the 21-day IPC/SHAM intervention were analyzed using the same ANOVA model. When significant main effects or interaction effects were observed, Tukey’s post hoc test was performed. Prior to the ANOVA procedures, normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test, and sphericity was assessed using Mauchly’s test where applicable. For biomarker analyses, the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (BH-FDR) correction was applied separately for each ANOVA effect (group, time, and group × time interaction), separately for the pre-intervention and post-intervention running trials, and separately within the inflammatory, angiogenic, and neurotrophic biomarker domains. For running-time analyses, BH-FDR correction was applied across the five running-time outcomes for each ANOVA effect. Primary interpretation was based on BH-FDR-adjusted q-values. Effect sizes were expressed as eta-squared (η2). All statistical analyses were performed using Statistica 12 software (StatSoft, Tulsa, OK, USA). Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

Baseline hematological parameters before and after the 21-day IPC/SHAM intervention are presented in Supplementary Table S2.

Supplementary Table S3 summarizes running time across the consecutive interval blocks of the 20 × 400 m test before and after the 21-day IPC/SHAM intervention. After BH-FDR correction across the five running-time outcomes, significant main effects of time were found for sections 1–5 (F(1,30) = 6.27, p = 0.018, q = 0.030, η2 = 0.17), sections 11–15 (F(1,30) = 5.64, p = 0.024, q = 0.030, η2 = 0.16), sections 16–20 (F(1,30) = 12.56, p = 0.001, q = 0.007, η2 = 0.30), and average 400-m running time (F(1,30) = 5.65, p = 0.024, q = 0.030, η2 = 0.16), corresponding to reductions of 5.2%, 4.7%, 6.8%, and 4.9%, respectively, from pre- to post-intervention. Running time in sections 6–10 decreased by 3.1%, but this change was not significant after BH-FDR correction (F(1,30) = 1.09, p = 0.305, q = 0.305, η2 = 0.04). No significant main effects of group or group × time interactions were observed for any running-time variable (all q > 0.05), indicating comparable pre- to post-intervention improvement in both groups.

Resting biomarker changes after IPC/SHAM intervention

ANOVA of changes in baseline (pre-exercise) concentrations of inflammatory, angiogenic, and neurotrophic biomarkers following the IPC/SHAM intervention showed, after BH-FDR correction, significant group × time interactions for GDF-15 (F(1, 33) = 13.87, p < 0.01, q < 0.05, η2 = 0.29), IL-6 (F(1, 33) = 5.59, p < .05, q < 0.05, η2 = 0.14), and IL-15 (F(1, 33) = 4.96, p < .05, q < 0.05, η2 = 0.13). Post hoc analysis showed that, following the 21-day IPC intervention, baseline concentrations of GDF-15 and IL-15 increased by 28% (p < .01) and 32% (p < .05), respectively, whereas baseline IL-6 concentrations decreased by 16% (p < .05). For IL-10, significant main effects of group (F(1, 33) = 10.72, p = 0.002, q < 0.05, η2 = 0.25) and time (F(1, 33) = 9.96, p = 0.003, q < 0.05, η2 = 0.23) were found after BH-FDR correction, whereas the group × time interaction was not significant (F(1, 33) = 3.54, p = 0.068, q > 0.05, η2 = 0.10).

Prior to the 21-day IPC/SHAM intervention, all four inflammatory biomarkers, namely GDF-15, IL-6, IL-10, and IL-15, exhibited a significant time-dependent response after BH-FDR correction (all q < 0.01). Post hoc analysis showed a significant increase in cytokine concentrations immediately after the running exercise, followed by a return toward baseline within 24 h. Specifically, GDF-15 increased by 41% (p < .01), IL-6 by 26% (p < .01), IL-10 by 28%, and IL-15 by 33% (p < .01) compared with baseline values. After BH-FDR correction, no significant group effects or group × time interactions were found before the intervention.

Following 21 days of ischemic preconditioning (IPC) or sham (SHAM) procedures, significant time effects were again observed for all inflammatory biomarkers after BH-FDR correction (all q < 0.001). Similar to the pre-intervention findings, post hoc analysis demonstrated significant increases in GDF-15 (35%, p < .01), IL-6 (29%, p < .01), IL-10 (25%, p < .01), and IL-15 (32%, p < .01) immediately after the running exercise, relative to baseline. After BH-FDR correction, significant group effects were found for GDF-15, IL-10, and IL-15, whereas significant group × time interactions were observed only for IL-10 and IL-15. Regardless of the time factor, IL-10, IL-15, and GDF-15 concentrations were 21% (p < .01), 29% (p < .01), and 20% (p < .01) higher in the IPC group compared with SHAM, respectively (Fig. 3). However, the group × time interaction for GDF-15 did not remain significant after BH-FDR correction and should therefore be interpreted with caution. The observed differences in IL-10 and IL-15 concentrations were attributable to an enhanced post-exercise response in the IPC group. Discussion

In the present study, repeated ischemic preconditioning (IPC) was examined as a modulator of acute inflammatory, angiogenic, and neurotrophic responses to intense endurance exercise. The findings showed that IPC altered selected biomarker responses to the 20 × 400 m running trial, indicating a marker-specific effect on the molecular response to strenuous physical activity. These results extend our previous observations that IPC modifies post-exercise serum kynurenine responses and tryptophan metabolism25. However, because the present study was limited to circulating biomarkers, without muscle biopsy or other tissue-level measurements, the mechanistic interpretation of these responses should remain cautious.

The reduction in running time observed after the 21-day period, in the absence of significant group or group × time effects, likely reflects the continuation of regular endurance training during the study rather than a specific ergogenic effect of IPC. This is in line with the study design, as participants were instructed to maintain their usual training routines throughout the intervention period. Therefore, the between-group differences observed in selected biomarker responses are unlikely to be explained simply by differences in running performance.

Before the intervention, all inflammatory biomarkers, including GDF-15, IL-6, IL-10, and IL-15, increased significantly in response to exercise, indicating a robust acute inflammatory response to the running trial.

After the 21-day IPC intervention, IL-6 continued to exhibit a significant time-dependent response to exercise; however, no significant group effect or group × time interaction was observed after BH-FDR correction. It is well established that skeletal muscle is a principal source of IL-6 during exercise. The synthesis of this cytokine is partly triggered by a sustained rise in intracellular calcium, which activates calcineurin signaling pathways and subsequently promotes IL-6 gene expression in muscle fibers26,27. Functionally, IL-6 serves both metabolic and regulatory roles: it facilitates lipolysis and glycogenolysis while also stimulating anti-inflammatory mechanisms28. During exercise, IL-6 upregulates the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-10, IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1ra), and TNF receptors29. At the same time, IL-6 may also act as a pro-inflammatory factor, activating immune responses in various tissues. The absence of more pronounced changes in IL-6 following exercise in our study may reflect the limitation that only systemic (serum) concentrations were measured, rather than local (muscle tissue) production.

Elevated IL-10 concentrations were observed in both groups following the running trial; however, after the 21-day IPC intervention, the magnitude of this response was greater in IPC than in SHAM, with an increase of 40% vs. 29%, respectively, resulting in a 27% higher peak value in the IPC group. This pattern is consistent with the anti-inflammatory profile of IPC, as IL-10, also referred to as cytokine synthesis inhibitory factor (CSIF), suppresses the synthesis of several pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interferon-gamma, IL-2, IL-3, TNF-α, and GM-CSF30.

The exercise-induced increase in serum IL-10 may be accompanied by enhanced skeletal muscle secretion of IL-1531,32. IL-15 is recognized as a potent stimulator of muscle growth and has also been implicated not only in inflammatory signaling, cytotoxic responses, and lymphocyte activation, but also in angiogenesis33. In the present study, post-exercise changes in IL-15 observed before the intervention may reflect exercise-related alterations in blood supply demands and processes associated with neovascularization. After the 21-day IPC intervention, the post-exercise increase in IL-15 was greater in IPC than in SHAM (35% vs. 30%), which is consistent with a stronger adaptive response following IPC. Although this finding does not constitute direct evidence of angiogenesis, it may be consistent with a broader biological role of IPC involving inflammatory and angiogenic signaling.

Another noteworthy finding was that 21-days IPC intervention, overall GDF-15 concentrations were higher in IPC than in SHAM, whereas the acute post-exercise response should be interpreted more cautiously because the group × time interaction was not significant after BH-FDR correction. Growth differentiation factor-15 (GDF-15) is a dimeric cytokine belonging to the transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) superfamily and has been implicated in the regulation of macrophage activity and stress-responsive signaling34. Under non-stressed conditions, its expression generally low, whereas tissue injury, inflammation, and ischemic stimuli markedly increase its expression35,36. Previous studies have shown that GDF-15 may exert protective effects under ischemic conditions, including limitation of tissue injury and apoptosis in the heart35,36. In this context, the higher overall GDF-15 concentrations observed after IPC may reflect a broader adaptive response to repeated ischemia–reperfusion stimuli. However, because the group × time interaction was not significant after BH-FDR correction, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a clearly altered acute post-exercise secretion pattern34.

Despite the documented benefits of IPC, its neurotrophic and angiogenic effects remain incompletely understood. In this context, several preclinical studies have suggested that ischemic preconditioning may promote beneficial vascular responses in ischemic tissues, including mechanisms related to neuroangiogenesis, although current knowledge of the molecular mediators potentially linking IPC with angio- and neurogenesis remains limited37–39. Angiogenesis, vascular remodeling, and neurogenesis are activated early after ischemic insults such as stroke and may remain elevated for several weeks as part of an intrinsic neurorepair response associated with collateral blood flow, neurogenesis, and oligodendrogenesis40–42. Moreover, angiogenesis has also been linked to post-exercise training adaptations43–45. Although numerous angiogenesis-related molecules, including both promoters and inhibitors, have been implicated in ischemic tissue remodeling, much less is known about their regulation in response to repeated IPC combined with intense endurance exercise.

Among the most notable findings of the present study was the association between the 21-day IPC intervention and changes in selected angiogenic and neurotrophic markers, including ANG, ANGPT1, BDNF, IGF-1, NGF, and sAPPα. A significant time-dependent response to the 20 × 400 m running trial was observed for all of these proteins, indicating that intense aerobic exercise exerts a strong stimulatory effect on circulating angiogenic and neurotrophic signaling. However, after BH-FDR correction, IPC-related differences were not uniform across the entire panel. Significant group × time interactions were found for BDNF and sAPPα, whereas significant main effects of group were observed for ANG and NGF. These findings suggest that, although acute exercise broadly stimulated this molecular response, the modulatory effect of IPC was selective rather than generalized. This interpretation is consistent with previous reports showing that aerobic training may enhance circulating angiogenic signaling and that exercise can induce substantial changes in neurotrophic factors, thereby supporting neural adaptation and cognitive function8,46,47.

Angiogenin is a secreted ribonuclease with well-established proangiogenic properties and has also been implicated in neuroprotective and neurorepair-related processes under ischemic conditions48–50. Experimental evidence indicates that angiogenin may support neuronal survival indirectly through astrocyte-mediated antioxidant defense, whereas post-stroke studies have linked this molecule to neurogenic activity within the subventricular zone50,51. In addition to its role in vascular biology, angiogenin has been associated with endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, and altered circulating or tissue expression has been reported in several pathological contexts, including acute cerebral infarction, cancer, Parkinson disease models, and chronic heart failure52–55. In the present study, overall ANG concentrations were higher in the IPC group than in SHAM after the 21-day intervention, while a significant exercise-induced increase was observed in both groups. This pattern may be consistent with a broader adaptive response involving angiogenic and neuroprotective signaling. However, because no significant group × time interaction was found after BH-FDR correction, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a distinctly altered acute ANG response pattern following IPC.

However, the response pattern of angiopoietin-1 differed from that of the other markers. The angiopoietin family, consisting mainly of ANGP1 and angiopoietin-2 (ANGP2), plays an important role in vascular remodeling and maturation through interaction with the Tie2 receptor9,56,57. ANGPT1 is primarily associated with vascular stabilization, endothelial integrity, and reduced vascular permeability57,58. Descriptively, baseline ANGPT1 concentrations after the intervention were approximately 30% higher in IPC than in SHAM, whereas immediately after the running exercise ANGPT1 levels slightly decreased in IPC and increased by about 35% in SHAM. At 24 h post-exercise, ANGPT1 concentrations declined in both groups and reached comparable levels. However, after BH-FDR correction, only the time effect remained significant, whereas neither the group effect nor the group × time interaction was significant. Therefore, although the post-exercise pattern of ANGPT1 differed descriptively between IPC and SHAM, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a clearly modified acute ANGPT1 response following IPC.

A significant group × time interaction was also observed for BDNF and sAPPα, indicating that the IPC and SHAM groups responded differently to exercise. The SHAM group showed a significant increase in BDNF immediately after exercise, whereas no significant post-exercise change was found in the IPC group. BDNF is known to respond to aerobic exercise, and its increase has been associated with beneficial effects of physical activity on hippocampal function and cognitive performance59. In this context, the blunted acute BDNF response observed after IPC indicates that the post-exercise circulating BDNF pattern differed between the IPC and SHAM groups after the intervention. However, in the absence of tissue-level measurements, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of altered neurotrophic signaling or enhanced BDNF uptake or utilization in peripheral tissues.

Alongside BDNF, the present study also assessed soluble amyloid precursor protein-α (sAPPα), a secreted neuroprotective and neurotrophic cleavage product of APP60–62. Previous experimental studies have suggested that exercise may promote non-amyloidogenic APP processing and reduce amyloid-related pathology60,61, whereas more recent human data indicate that sAPPα itself responds to exercise in an intensity-dependent manner62. In the present study, exercise induced changes in sAPPα in both groups; however, after the 21-day IPC intervention, the immediate post-exercise response was more pronounced in IPC than in SHAM, resulting in a 17% higher peak value in the IPC group. This pattern is consistent with the significant group × time interaction observed for sAPPα after BH-FDR correction and indicates that the post-exercise circulating sAPPα response differed between IPC and SHAM after the intervention. However, because no direct assessment of APP processing was performed, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of altered non-amyloidogenic processing or broader neuroprotective effects.

NGF also showed a significant main effect of group after BH-FDR correction, indicating overall higher concentrations in the IPC group than in SHAM. At the same time, the previously noted post-exercise increase in NGF indicates that the release of this protein was directly associated with exercise, which is consistent with the view that aerobic activity may stimulate NGF production and release in various tissues63. In this context, the overall higher NGF concentrations observed after IPC may be consistent with the neurotrophic profile associated with this intervention. In contrast, IGF-1 showed only a significant time effect after BH-FDR correction, indicating responsiveness to exercise, but without a significant IPC-specific group effect or group × time interaction. Therefore, IGF-1 should be interpreted primarily as an exercise-responsive growth factor rather than a marker selectively modulated by IPC64. In summary, the present findings provide new insight into the effects of repeated IPC on acute inflammatory, angiogenic, and neurotrophic responses to intense endurance exercise. The results indicate that IPC modified selected biomarker responses, affecting both baseline values and post-exercise changes in a marker-specific manner. In particular, the clearest IPC-related effects were observed for IL-10, IL-15, BDNF, and sAPPα, whereas ANG and NGF showed overall group-related differences after the intervention. These findings extend current evidence that IPC modulates selected molecular responses to exercise in a marker-specific manner. However, the interpretation of these effects should remain cautious, particularly in the absence of tissue-level measurements and direct mechanistic data.

Perspective and limitations

From a practical perspective, endurance training is frequently associated with repeated high training loads and cumulative fatigue, which may contribute to sustained systemic physiological stress, musculoskeletal discomfort, and an increased risk of overload-related injury65,66. In this context, non-pharmacological strategies capable of modulating selected exercise-induced molecular responses may be of practical interest67. The present findings suggest that repeated IPC may warrant further investigation as a supportive approach for influencing selected inflammatory, angiogenic, and neurotrophic responses to strenuous exercise11,68. However, the current data do not allow conclusions regarding recovery enhancement, pain reduction, injury prevention, or direct application in training practice.

This study has several limitations. First, no muscle biopsy or other tissue-level measurements were performed, which precludes attribution of the observed circulating changes specifically to skeletal muscle and limits mechanistic interpretation of the inflammatory, angiogenic, and neurotrophic responses. Second, because IPC was applied bilaterally, contributions from other tissues and organs cannot be excluded. Third, the functional significance of the observed biomarker changes for performance, recovery, and long-term adaptation remains unresolved. Future studies should combine circulating biomarkers with tissue-level analyses and functional outcomes to clarify the biological relevance of repeated IPC in exercise settings.
