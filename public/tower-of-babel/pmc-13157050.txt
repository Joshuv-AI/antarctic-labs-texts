Protective role of calcitriol in PM2.5-induced apoptosis and inflammation in bronchial epithelial cells in vitro via vitamin D receptor-mediated Nrf2 signaling
Authors: Mutita Pluempreecha, Anyamanee Chatsirisupachai, Tanyapohn Soingam, Tasanee Onkoksoong, Tongchai Payungwong, Siwanon Jirawatnotai, Uraiwan Panich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractCalcitriol protects bronchial epithelial cells from PM2.5-induced apoptosis, oxidative DNA damage, and inflammation through vitamin D receptor (VDR)–mediated activation of Nrf2 redox signaling. Calcitriol decreases apoptotic signaling by reducing p53 phosphorylation and downregulating the mRNA expression of p53 and caspase-3, while also decreasing levels of 8-OHdG, a marker of oxidative DNA damage. Calcitriol exhibits anti-inflammatory effects by suppressing NF-κB activation and downregulating NF-κB p65 expression, along with its downstream pro-inflammatory targets, including IκB-α, TNF-α, and IL-6. Additionally, calcitriol increases VDR protein expression and enhances Nrf2 activity. Through increased VDR binding to the AREs in the promoter regions of Nrf2 target genes, calcitriol upregulates antioxidant genes such as HO-1 and NQO1, thereby providing cytoprotection against PM2.5-induced cellular stress.Unlabelled ImageKeywords: Calcitriol, PM2.5, Human bronchial epithelial cells, Vitamin D receptor (VDR), Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), Inflammation

Introduction

Both acute and long-term exposure to fine particulate matter with a diameter less than 2.5 µm (PM2.5) have been associated with an increased risk of respiratory diseases, primarily through the induction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation, activation of inflammatory signaling pathways, and promotion of apoptosis in bronchial epithelial cells (Li et al., 2018, Pun et al., 2017, Cao et al., 2018). The elevated release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interferons (IFNs), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), plays a critical role in acute lung injury and inflammation of the alveolar epithelial membrane (Wynne et al., 2017). In bronchial epithelial (BEAS-2B) cells, PM2.5 activates the NF-κB signaling pathway, leading to the upregulation of cytokines such as IL‐6 and TNF-α (Zhai et al., 2022). Dysregulated apoptotic signaling has been implicated in the development of interstitial pulmonary fibrosis through injury to alveolar epithelial cells. In the airway epithelium, apoptosis has also been associated with epithelial injury and inflammatory remodeling (Li et al., 2018, Jia et al., 2021). PM2.5 has also been shown to induce cellular stress and apoptosis in bronchial epithelial cells by activating the tumor suppressor protein p53 and caspase-3, a key effector in programmed cell death, and causing oxidative DNA damage (Xu et al., 2020, Chen et al., 2023). Calcitriol, also known as 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3, is the active form of vitamin D. It functions as a ligand for the vitamin D receptor (VDR) and has been shown to exert anti-inflammatory effects, in part by suppressing NF-κB signaling (Huang et al., 2023, An et al., 2010). Impaired VDR function or vitamin D deficiency has been associated with heightened inflammatory responses, and vitamin D/VDR signaling has been shown to modulate NF-κB-linked inflammatory responses in airway epithelial cells (Hansdottir et al., 2010). Additionally, the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2)/antioxidant response element (ARE) signaling pathway plays a critical role in cellular defense against PM2.5-induced oxidative stress and toxicity (Ding et al., 2021, Pardo et al., 2019). Targeting Nrf2-dependent antioxidant expression has been proposed as a promising pharmacological strategy to alleviate PM2.5-induced lung damage (Dong et al., 2023, Sim et al., 2021). Previous studies have indicated that VDR activation may be associated with Nrf2 signaling pathways (Yang et al., 2022). The vitamin D receptor (VDR)–Nrf2 signaling axis is recognized as a key protective mechanism in oxidative stress–related diseases. Activation of Nrf2 promotes the transcription of antioxidant and cytoprotective genes, including NQO1 and HO-1, thereby maintaining redox homeostasis and limiting inflammatory responses. A previous study reported that vitamin D alleviates particulate matter–induced asthma inflammation through activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant pathway in the asthma mouse model and airway smooth muscle cells (Ge et al., 2024). Emerging evidence indicates that vitamin D ameliorates diabetic nephropathy by activating the Nrf2–Keap1 antioxidant pathway and reducing oxidative stress in diabetic rats (Nakai et al., 2014), suggesting a functional interplay between VDR and Nrf2 in regulating oxidative stress–induced tissue injury (Yang et al., 2022, Ge et al., 2024, Nakai et al., 2014). However, the mechanistic crosstalk between VDR and Nrf2 in the context of PM2.5-induced cellular stress and inflammation remains unclear. In this study, we investigated the protective role of calcitriol against PM2.5-induced apoptosis, oxidative DNA damage, and inflammatory responses in human bronchial epithelial BEAS-2B cells, with a specific focus on the modulation of Nrf2-mediated redox signaling via the VDR.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

An immortalized human bronchial epithelial cell line (ATCC, USA, #CRL-3588), BEAS-2B, was cultured in T75 tissue culture flasks (Corning, USA) using Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Invitrogen, #1TFS-1CC-12800017, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Invitrogen, #1TFS-1RS-A5256701, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and antibiotics, including 100 U/mL penicillin (Sigma-Aldrich, #P3032, St. Louis, MO, USA) and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, #S9137, St. Louis, MO, USA). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Once cells reached 80–90% confluence, they were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and trypsinized using 3 mL of 0.25% trypsin-EDTA per flask, followed by incubation at 37 °C for a few minutes to ensure complete detachment. Detached cells were collected by centrifugation at 1200 rpm for 5 min, then resuspended in 200 μL of complete DMEM. Cells were counted, seeded at appropriate densities, and treated with experimental compounds as described below.

For treatment, cells were pre-treated with or without calcitriol (Sigma-Aldrich, #740551, St. Louis, MO, USA) at concentrations of 1, 10, or 100 nM for 24 h followed by PM2.5 exposure at a final concentration of 100 µg/mL. The PM2.5 used in this study was Standard Reference Material (SRM) 2786 (NIST®, Gaithersburg, MD, USA; purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, #NIST2786). SRM 2786 consists of fine atmospheric particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter of < 4 µm, collected from an urban area in the United States. The material was originally collected using high-volume air samplers on quartz fiber filters. Particles were subsequently removed from the filters, homogenized, and extensively characterized by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) for their chemical composition, including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and other constituents. For in vitro experiments, SRM 2786 was suspended in sterile distilled water to prepare a stock solution and sonicated using an ultrasonic bath for 30 min at room temperature to minimize particle aggregation. The suspension was vortexed immediately before dilution in culture medium and was freshly prepared prior to each experiment.

Exposure durations varied from 1 to 48 h depending on the experimental endpoint. Experimental conditions were categorized into three groups: (1) Control-sham group, treated with 0.1% ethanol or 0.5% Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich, #D4540, St. Louis, MO, USA) in fresh medium to assess vehicle toxicity; (2) the PM2.5-only group; and (3) PM2.5 with calcitriol pre-treatment group. After the indicated treatment periods, culture media were removed, and cells were washed with PBS. Cells were then collected and stored at −80 °C for further experiments, including chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay, real-time RT-PCR, and Western blotting.

Morphological assessment and cell proliferation assay

BEAS-2B cells were seeded in 96-well black clear-bottom optical plates (Corning, USA) and pre-treated with or without calcitriol at concentrations of 1, 10, and 100 nM for 24 h, followed by exposure to PM2.5 at 100 µg/mL. After treatment, cell morphology was captured using the bright field mode of the Operetta CLS high-content analysis system (PerkinElmer). For nuclear morphology and proliferation analysis, cells were stained with 1 μg/mL of DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole; Sigma-Aldrich, #D9542, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark, and imaged using the DNA/Nuclei stain mode (Ex/Em: 350/470 nm) under 20 × magnification. Cell numbers were quantified by counting DAPI-stained nuclei, and proliferation rates were calculated based on total nuclear counts.

Apoptotic cell detection by flow cytometry (Annexin V/PI staining)

Apoptosis was evaluated using Annexin V-FITC (BD Biosciences, #556419, San Jose, CA, USA)/propidium iodide (PI) dual staining (Sigma-Aldrich, #P4864, St. Louis, MO, USA). BEAS-2B cells were pre-treated with calcitriol (1, 10, and 100 nM) for 24 h, followed by exposure to PM2.5 (100 or 200 µg/mL). A vehicle control group was treated with 0.1% ethanol. After treatment, cells were washed twice with PBS and detached using trypsin. Cells were resuspended in 500 µl of binding buffer, then incubated with 2.5 μL Annexin V-FITC and 2.5 µL PI (final concentration 50 µg/mL each) for 10 min on ice in the dark. Samples were analyzed using a FACS Calibur flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson, San Jose, CA, USA). Data were analyzed with FlowJo™ v10 Software (Becton Dickinson, San Jose, CA, USA). Cells stained with Annexin V+ and PI- were classified as early apoptotic, while Annexin V+ and PI+ were considered late apoptotic. The percentages of apoptotic cells were calculated from three independent experiments and presented as means ± SD.

Real-time RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the Total RNA Mini Kit (Geneaid, #RB100, Taiwan). cDNA synthesis was performed with iScript Reverse Transcription Supermix (Bio-Rad, #1708841, USA). qPCR was conducted with KAPA SYBR® FAST reagents (Merck, #KK4600, USA) on a QuantStudio™ 5 system using the conditions described above. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S1. GAPDH served as the internal control. Gene expression was quantified using the 2-ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001), and all reactions were performed in triplicate. Results are reported as average fold change ± SD with statistical significance indicated.

Western blot analysis

Cells were washed twice with PBS. Nuclear extracts were prepared using the nuclear extraction kit (Active Motif, #NC9873721, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Whole-cell lysates were obtained by incubating cells in lysis buffer on ice for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 10 min. Protein concentrations were measured by Bradford assay (Bio-Rad, #5000006, Hercules, CA, USA). Equal amounts of protein were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, #1620177, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) containing 0.1% Tween-20 for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-VDR (mouse monoclonal; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc-13133, Dallas, TX, USA; dilution 1:2000), anti-Nrf2 (rabbit polyclonal; Abcam, #ab31163, Cambridge, MA, USA; dilution 1:2000), anti-phospho-p53 (Ser15) (mouse monoclonal; Cell Signaling Technology, #9286, Danvers, MA, USA; dilution 1:1000). After washing, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-Mouse IgG-HRP; Abcam, #ab6721, Cambridge, MA, USA) and visualized using the ECL Western Blotting Detection System (Abcam, #1705061, Cambridge, MA, USA). Membranes were re-probed with anti-β-actin (mouse monoclonal; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc-47778, Dallas, TX, USA; dilution 1:4000) to confirm equal protein loading (Dual Color, #1610374 and Precision Plus Protein™ WesternC™ Standards, #1610376, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA; 5 μL/blot). Band intensities were quantified using ImageLab software (Bio-Rad, USA) and normalized to β-actin. Protein levels were expressed as a percentage of the control (set to 100%).

Immunofluorescence detection

BEAS-2B cells were washed with ice-cold PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (MilliporeSigma, #SIA‑158127‑3KG, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were then permeabilized using a solution containing 0.1 M glycine (Bio-Rad, #1610718, Hercules, CA, USA) and 0.1% Triton X-100 (Bio-Rad, #1610407, Hercules, CA, USA). After washing, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-Nrf2 (rabbit polyclonal; Abcam, #ab31163, Cambridge, MA, USA; dilution 1:200), anti-NF-κB p65 (mouse monoclonal; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc-8008, Dallas, TX, USA; dilution 1:200), and anti-8-OHdG (mouse monoclonal; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc-66036, Dallas, TX, USA; dilution 1:200). Following primary antibody incubation, cells were washed and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with the appropriate secondary antibodies: Alexa Fluor® 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Abcam, #ab150077, Cambridge, MA, USA) and Alexa Fluor® 647-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Abcam, #ab150115, Cambridge, MA, USA). To visualize nuclei, cells were stained with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole), and images were captured using the Operetta CLS high-content analysis system (PerkinElmer) under filter sets for 435/550 nm and 650/760 nm at 20 × magnification. Nuclear translocation of Nrf2 and NF-κB p65 was quantified by calculating the nuclear-to-cytosolic (N/C) fluorescence intensity ratio using Columbus Image Data Storage and Analysis System (PerkinElmer). Values were expressed as fold change ± SD.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

To evaluate VDR binding to antioxidant response element (ARE) regions of Nrf2 target genes, BEAS-2B cells with or without calcitriol pre-treatment (100 nM, 24 h) were subsequently exposed to PM2.5 (100 µg/mL) for 24 h. Sulforaphane (SFN; Sigma-Aldrich, #S6317, St. Louis, MO, USA; 10 µM for 6 h), a known Nrf2 activator, was used as a positive control (Bai et al., 2015, Zhang, 2006). ChIP was performed using the SimpleChIP® Plus Sonication ChIP kit (Cell Signaling Technology, #57976, Danvers, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Sheared chromatin (10 µg) was immunoprecipitated with the following antibodies: anti-Nrf2 (Abcam, #ab62352, Cambridge, MA, USA; 2 µg/IP), anti-VDR (Abcam, #ab109234, Cambridge, MA, USA; 2 µg/IP), normal rabbit IgG (Cell Signaling Technology, #2729), and anti-Histone H3 (Cell Signaling Technology, #4620). Immunoprecipitated DNA was analyzed by quantitative PCR (qPCR) using primers specific for AREs in the NQO1 and HO-1 promoter regions (see Supplementary Table S2). Human RPL30 Exon 3 served as a negative control (Cell Signaling Technology, #7014). qPCR was performed using KAPA SYBR® FAST reagents on a QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems), with thermal cycling conditions: 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 3 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Results were expressed as fold enrichment (2-ΔΔCt) relative to untreated controls.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments (n ≥ 3), each performed on different days with freshly prepared reagents. Statistical significance between non-treated control and PM2.5-treated groups was evaluated using Student’s t-test (#P < 0.05, ##P < 0.01, ###P < 0.001). For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test was used to assess differences between PM2.5-only and PM2.5 + calcitriol-treated groups (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001). All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Calcitriol reverses PM2.5-induced injury in bronchial epithelial BEAS-2B cells

Key hallmarks of apoptosis in nuclei include chromatin condensation and nuclear fragmentation. To assess PM2.5-induced BEAS-2B cell injury and the protective effects of calcitriol, DAPI staining was used to evaluate cell morphology and proliferation. Treatment with PM2.5 (100 µg/mL) induced pronounced morphological alterations in BEAS-2B cells, including cell shrinkage, rounding, and detachment. Pre-treatment with calcitriol at concentrations of 1, 10, and 100 nM alleviated these morphological changes caused by PM2.5 (Fig. 1A). Quantification of DAPI-stained nuclei revealed that PM2.5 exposure reduced cell proliferation to 70.10 ± 9.17% compared to control cells (###P < 0.001; PM2.5 alone vs. control). However, cells pre-treated with calcitriol at 1, 10, and 100 nM exhibited significantly higher cell numbers compared to PM2.5-treated cells without calcitriol as shown in Fig. 1B (**P < 0.01; PM2.5 with calcitriol vs. PM2.5 alone), indicating that calcitriol preserves cell viability and reduces PM2.5-induced cytotoxicity.

Untreated control (Ctrl) and 0.1% ethanol-treated (sham) groups were included as references. Calcitriol protects against PM2.5-induced apoptosis in BEAS-2B cells

Since PM2.5 exposure is known to induce apoptosis in bronchial epithelial cells (Yang et al., 2015, Chen et al., 2018), we employed Annexin V/PI staining to quantify early and late apoptosis. Following exposure to PM2.5 (100 or 200 µg/mL) in a time- and dose-dependent manner, we observed marked reductions in cell viability (Q4) and increases in early (Q3) and late apoptotic cells (Q2) (Supplementary Fig. S1A-C; *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01 and ***P < 0.001). Notably, exposure to 100 µg/mL PM2.5 for 6 h significantly increased early and late apoptosis. Pre-treatment with calcitriol substantially reduced apoptosis in PM2.5-exposed cells (Fig. 2A). The percentage of early apoptotic cells decreased from 15.38% ± 0.35 (PM2.5 alone) to 7.20% ± 1.97, 6.82% ± 2.55, and 6.08% ± 0.91 with 1, 10, and 100 nM calcitriol, respectively (Fig. 2B; ###P < 0.001 and ***P < 0.001). Similarly, late apoptosis decreased from 21.62% ± 0.78 (PM2.5 alone) to 6.05% ± 0.45, 5.09% ± 0.92, and 4.19% ± 0.75 with 1, 10, and 100 nM calcitriol, respectively (Fig. 2C; ###P < 0.001 and ***P < 0.001). These data confirm the anti-apoptotic effects of calcitriol.

Calcitriol mitigates PM2.5-induced apoptotic signaling and oxidative DNA damage in BEAS-2B cells

To investigate the molecular mechanisms by which calcitriol exerts protective effects against PM2.5-induced apoptosis, we first assessed mRNA expression of the apoptosis-related genes p53 and CASP3. PM2.5 exposure significantly upregulated both p53 and CASP3 expression (p53: 2.23 ± 0.26, CASP3: 1.31 ± 0.16 fold change), whereas calcitriol pre-treatment significantly downregulated p53 expression (at 100 nM) and CASP3 expression (at 1, 10, and 100 nM) (Fig. 3A; ###P < 0.001 and ***P < 0.001). Given that previous studies suggest VDR may suppress the p53 pathway (Shang et al., 2024), we further evaluated phosphorylated p53 protein levels. PM2.5 markedly elevated phospho-p53 expression, which was significantly reduced by calcitriol pre-treatment (100 nM) (Fig. 3B; #P < 0.05 and *P < 0.05) (Original blot shown in Supplementary Fig. S4). Calcitriol alone did not alter p53 phosphorylation, suggesting that its protective effects are triggered in response to PM2.5-induced cellular stress. To evaluate oxidative DNA damage, we performed immunofluorescence staining of 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), a well-established marker of oxidative DNA lesions (Valavanidis et al., 2009). PM2.5 exposure significantly increased 8-OHdG accumulation, whereas pre-treatment with calcitriol (10 or 100 nM) significantly reduced 8-OHdG levels (Fig. 3C; ###P < 0.001 and ***P < 0.001). These results collectively indicate that calcitriol protected bronchial epithelial cells from PM2.5-induced damage by inhibiting apoptosis-related signaling (via p53/caspase-3) and reducing oxidative DNA damage.

Calcitriol suppresses PM2.5-induced inflammatory signaling in BEAS-2B cells

To investigate calcitriol’s anti-inflammatory potential (Krishnan et al., 2007, Chen et al., 2020), we measured the mRNA levels of key inflammatory cytokines previously observed in PM2.5-induced lung injury (Zhang et al., 2018). PM2.5 exposure led to significant upregulation of TNF-α expression over 24 h (Supplementary Fig. S2; ###P < 0.001). mRNA expression levels of NF-κB p65, IκB-α, TNF-α, and IL-6 were significantly elevated following PM2.5 treatment, while pre-treatment with calcitriol (1, 10, and 100 nM) significantly downregulated TNF-α and IL-6. NF-κB p65 and IκB-α mRNA expression levels were also reduced by 10 and 100 nM calcitriol (Fig. 4A; ###P < 0.001, *P < 0.05 and ***P < 0.001). Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated that calcitriol significantly reduced the nuclear-to-cytosol ratio of NF-κB p65 (RelA), indicating suppression of NF-κB activity compared to cells treated with PM2.5 alone (Fig. 4B; ##P < 0.01, *P < 0.05 and ***P < 0.001). These results underscore the role of calcitriol in mitigating PM2.5-induced inflammation by downregulating NF-κB activity and the associated inflammatory genes NF-κB p65, IκB-α, TNF-α, and IL-6.

Calcitriol restores PM2.5-induced bronchial cell injury via regulation of VDR and Nrf2 signaling

Previous studies have shown that calcitriol confers cytoprotection against oxidative injury through activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway (Vahidinia et al., 2022, Cui et al., 2021, Dai et al., 2019). Calcitriol is known to bind vitamin D response elements (VDREs), facilitating transcriptional responses that counter oxidative stress (Fernandez-Robredo et al., 2020). We then determined whether calcitriol could mitigate PM2.5-induced dysregulation of VDR and Nrf2. Western blot analysis revealed that pre-treatment with calcitriol (1, 10, and 100 nM) significantly upregulated protein expression of both VDR and Nrf2 in BEAS-2B cells exposed to PM2.5 (100 µg/mL), compared to the PM2.5-only group (Fig. 5A; #P < 0.05, ##P < 0.01, ###P < 0.001 and ***P < 0.001) (Original blot shown in Supplementary Fig. S5 and S6). Additionally, immunofluorescence (IF) analysis showed that calcitriol (10 and 100 nM) significantly increased nuclear translocation of Nrf2, as indicated by an elevated nuclear-to-cytoplasmic (N/C) fluorescence intensity ratio, compared to PM2.5-treated cells without calcitriol (Fig. 5B; ##P < 0.01 and **P < 0.01). These findings suggest that calcitriol enhances both VDR and Nrf2 protein levels and Nrf2 activity under PM2.5-induced stress conditions.

Vitamin D receptor mediates Nrf2-ARE transcriptional activation in response to PM2.5

To investigate the transcriptional mechanisms underlying the antioxidant effects of calcitriol through modulation of the Nrf2 signaling axis, we evaluated VDR binding to ARE regions in the promoters of NQO1 and HO-1. ChIP-qPCR analysis was performed in BEAS-2B cells pre-treated with calcitriol (100 nM) or sulforaphane (SFN, 10 µM), a known Nrf2 activator (Bai et al., 2015, Zhang, 2006). Calcitriol significantly enhanced VDR binding to ARE sequences within the NQO1 and HO-1 promoters, showing 3.87 ± 0.65-fold and 8.88 ± 0.38-fold enrichment, respectively, compared to untreated controls (Fig. 6A, 6B; *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01 and ***P < 0.001). Notably, in PM2.5-treated cells, calcitriol pre-treatment resulted in 2.30 ± 0.46-fold and 2.50 ± 0.08-fold increases in VDR binding to the NQO1 and HO-1 ARE regions, respectively, relative to PM2.5-only treatment, indicating activation of the VDR-ARE axis by calcitriol. To confirm primer specificity and protein-DNA interactions, Nrf2 ChIP-qPCR analysis was performed. Cells treated with SFN as a positive control demonstrated 6.73 ± 1.11-fold and 7.94 ± 0.41-fold enrichment in Nrf2 binding at the ARE regions of the NQO1 and HO-1 promoters compared to the untreated control. The quality of the ChIP assay was verified by gel electrophoresis confirming chromatin fragment sizes of 500–1,000 bp (Supplementary Fig. S3A) and by enrichment of the RPL30 housekeeping gene through histone H3 immunoprecipitation (Supplementary Fig. S3B; ###P < 0.001).

To further validate the role of VDR in regulating ARE-driven antioxidant genes, BEAS-2B cells were pre-treated with calcitriol (10 and 100 nM), resulting in a significant upregulation of NQO1 and HO-1 mRNA expression compared to PM2.5 treatment alone. To determine whether this induction was dependent on Nrf2, cells were co-treated with brusatol (1 µM), a specific Nrf2 inhibitor. Notably, calcitriol still significantly increased the expression of NQO1 (1.87 ± 0.27-fold) and HO-1 (1.85 ± 0.23-fold), even in the presence of brusatol (Fig. 6C, 6D; ##P < 0.01, ###P < 0.001 and ***P < 0.001). These findings suggest that calcitriol activates ARE-driven antioxidant genes through a VDR-dependent mechanism, independently of canonical Nrf2 signaling.

Discussion

PM2.5 has been shown to pose significant risks of both acute and chronic lung injury by inducing inflammation, apoptosis, and oxidative damage in bronchial epithelial cells (Li et al., 2018, Pun et al., 2017, Cao et al., 2018, Zhai et al., 2022, Jia et al., 2021, Zhao et al., 2022, He et al., 2017). 1,25-Dihydroxyvitamin D3, also known as calcitriol, is the active form of vitamin D and has been reported to mitigate PM2.5-induced bronchial epithelial cell injury by inhibiting inflammation and oxidative stress (Chatsirisupachai et al., 2024, Xin et al., 2019). Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the regulatory role of calcitriol in PM2.5-induced inflammatory responses, apoptosis, and oxidative damage, focusing on the crosstalk between VDR and Nrf2 signaling pathways in human bronchial epithelial (BEAS-2B) cells. Exposure of BEAS-2B cells to PM2.5 resulted in activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, upregulating inflammatory genes such as TNF-α and IL-1β (Zhai et al., 2022, Zhou et al., 2023), and enhancing the production of the cytokine IL‐6 (Zhai et al., 2022). Inhalation of PM2.5 also caused elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF‐α and IL‐6 in lung tissues of mice (Jia et al., 2021). Our findings demonstrate that calcitriol exerts protective effects against PM2.5-induced inflammatory responses by downregulating NF-κB signaling, including NF-κB p65 mRNA expression and its downstream target genes (IκB-α, TNF-α, and IL-6) in BEAS-2B cells. In addition, PM2.5 has been implicated in the human bronchial epithelial cell injury via apoptosis and oxidative DNA damage (Zhai et al., 2022, Xu et al., 2020, Chen et al., 2023). Consistent with previous findings, our study demonstrates that calcitriol significantly suppressed PM2.5-induced apoptosis by downregulating apoptosis-related signaling, including reduced phosphorylation of p53 and suppression of p53 and caspase-3 mRNA levels, the key markers of apoptosis and cellular stress response (Gupta et al., 2001, Schuler et al., 2000). Additionally, calcitriol effectively reduced levels of 8-OHdG, a well-established marker of oxidative DNA damage. The protective effects of calcitriol are largely mediated through activation of the VDR. Upon activation, the calcitriol-VDR complex heterodimerizes with the retinoid x receptor (RXR) and binds to vitamin D response elements (VDREs) within the promoter regions of target genes, regulating transcription of genes involved in inflammatory and apoptotic processes (Kim et al., 2020, Zhang et al., 2017, Dominguez et al., 2021, Quesada-Gomez et al., 2020). The calcitriol/VDR axis is also suggested to suppress NF-κB signaling by stabilizing IκB, thereby preventing its degradation, inhibiting NF-κB nuclear translocation and ultimately downregulating the expression of downstream pro-inflammatory genes (Huang et al., 2023). Beyond its anti-inflammatory effects, calcitriol enhances antioxidant defenses via the Nrf2 signaling pathway. Nrf2 is a master transcription factor that binds to ARE and induces expression of cytoprotective genes such as NQO1 and HO-1 (Tonelli et al., 2018, Loboda et al., 2016). Prolonged exposure to water-insoluble PM2.5 particulates in BALB/c mice decreased Nrf2 transcriptional activity and reduced antioxidant gene expression, which was associated with pulmonary fibrosis and enhanced susceptibility to secondary LPS-induced lung injury (Zhao et al., 2022). Clinical evidence also indicates a positive correlation between VDR and Nrf2 levels in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (Xiang et al., 2021). Moreover, calcitriol has been reported to alleviate epithelial inflammation and oxidative stress by activating the Nrf2 pathway (Yang et al., 2022). This effect can be mediated by promoting Nrf2 accumulation through inhibition of its ubiquitin–proteasome-mediated degradation, or indirectly through reducing cellular ROS levels, which subsequently activate Nrf2 signaling (Yang et al., 2022, Cui et al., 2021). Although vitamin D–mediated activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway has been reported in PM2.5-induced airway inflammation (Ge et al., 2024), a comprehensive mechanistic understanding of VDR-dependent modulation of Nrf2 signaling, particularly under defined experimental conditions, remains to be fully elucidated. Our study provides new evidence that calcitriol restores VDR protein expression and enhances Nrf2-mediated redox signaling in PM2.5-exposed BEAS-2B cells. ChIP assays demonstrated that VDR binds to ARE promoter regions of antioxidant genes such as NQO1 and HO-1, suggesting a transcriptional link between VDR and ARE-driven antioxidant gene expression. Notably, calcitriol significantly upregulated Nrf2 target genes (NQO1 and HO-1) even under pharmacological Nrf2 inhibition by brusatol, indicating that the calcitriol/VDR axis could activate ARE-driven gene expression independently of classical Nrf2 activation. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that calcitriol confers protective effects by VDR-mediated activation of Nrf2 redox signaling, highlighting the extended cytoprotective role of the calcitriol/VDR axis in mitigating PM2.5-induced bronchial epithelial cell injury. This protection occurs through VDR-driven transcription of ARE-dependent antioxidant genes, leading to restored redox homeostasis, suppressed inflammation, and reduced apoptotic damage. This study provides insights into the therapeutic potential of calcitriol in protecting against PM2.5-induced lung injury. Further investigation using in vivo models is warranted to validate its efficacy in mitigating lung damage and evaluate its safety in a more complex and physiologically relevant system.

Limitations

This study was conducted in a single immortalized human bronchial epithelial cell line (BEAS-2B), which may not fully recapitulate the structural and functional complexity observed in primary airway epithelium, three-dimensional (3D) airway epithelial cultures, or in vivo pulmonary responses. A defined PM2.5 concentration and selected exposure time points were used to enable mechanistic clarity; broader dose–response and time-course analyses may provide additional insight. The experimental design employed a pre-treatment (preventive) approach rather than a therapeutic post-exposure model. Furthermore, the use of a standardized reference material (NIST SRM 2786) enhances reproducibility but does not capture potential regional or seasonal variability of ambient PM2.5. Future studies in additional experimental models, including primary airway epithelial cells, 3D airway epithelial culture systems, and in vivo models, are warranted to further establish physiological relevance.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that calcitriol exerts protective effects against PM2.5-induced inflammation, apoptosis, and oxidative DNA damage in human bronchial epithelial BEAS-2B cells through upregulation of VDR protein and activation of Nrf2-mediated redox signaling. These findings support a potential transcriptional interplay between VDR and Nrf2 that may mediate the protective effects of calcitriol against PM2.5-induced cellular injury in BEAS-2B cells. This study underscores calcitriol’s potential as a therapeutic candidate for mitigating PM2.5-induced lung injury via VDR-mediated activation of Nrf2 redox signaling.
