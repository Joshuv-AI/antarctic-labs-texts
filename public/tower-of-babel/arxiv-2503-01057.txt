Sparse Randomised Approximation of Normal Cycles
Allen Paul, Neill Campbell, Tony Shardlow

Abstract
We extend our work (2026) for compression of currents and varifolds to a compression algorithm for the embedded normal cycles representation of shape, restricted to the constant normal kernel case, using the Nystrom approximation in Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHS) and Ridge Leverage Score (RLS) sampling. Our method comes with theoretical guarantees on the compression error decay, and the approximations are shown to be effective for downstream tasks such as nonlinear shape registration in the Large Deformation Diffeomorphic Metric Mapping (LDDMM) framework, even for very high compression ratios. The performance of our algorithm is demonstrated on large-scale shape data from modern geometry processing datasets and is shown to accelerate downstream registration tasks significantly.



keywords
normal cycles, compression, Nystrom approximation, randomised approximation, shape registration, LDDMM

1
Introduction

In many real-world geometric learning applications — for example, in computational anatomy — one wishes to build statistical models of shape variation, where the shape data are available as discrete curves and surfaces representing anatomical structures
Younes (2010)
. In order to fit such models, one requires a fidelity metric on shapes in order to compare the data to shapes generated by the model and tune the model parameters. In the majority of such applications, shapes do not have a consistent parametrisation, meaning that simple fidelity metrics relying on known correspondences cannot be used. A large literature has been developed on point-cloud metrics
Dubuisson and Jain (1994)
;
Wu et al. (2021)
such as Chamfer and Hausdorff distance, as well as methods for extracting dense correspondences
van Kaick et al. (2011)
between shapes. However, in general, point-cloud distances are
not
geometric and do not account for the underlying structure of the shapes. Furthermore, dense correspondences may not be well defined or unique, which introduces a bias in subsequent shape learning applications.

In the case where shape data are available as submanifolds of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, one can deal with the lack of parametric correspondences in a more principled manner, using techniques from geometric measure theory
Charon et al. (2020)
. In particular, the so-called currents
Vaillant and Glaunès (2005)
and varifolds
Charon and Trouvé (2013)
representations view shapes as objects that integrate a family of continuous differential forms on the underlying domain, effectively embedding the shapes into the dual space of a space of differential forms. By restricting to differential forms lying in a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
Aronszajn (1950)
, one may use the dual metric on the embeddings in order to compare shapes in terms of their action on forms, independent of their parametrisations. The computation of the dual metric between submanifolds can be written down in closed form in terms of the positive semidefinite kernel function
k\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
associated to the RKHS. One may compute the RKHS embeddings and metrics between discrete shape data in a way that is consistent as the discrete shapes tend to the underlying continuous submanifold. This framework and the resulting shape metrics have been used extensively in the Large Deformation Diffeomorphic Metric Mapping (LDDMM) framework
Charon et al. (2020)
for matching shapes with diffeomorphisms.

While currents and varifolds metrics allow for
geometric
comparison of shapes in a correspondence-less manner, these representations only take into account first-order geometric information, such as tangent and normal vector directions. The structures typically present in real-world applications have regions of high curvature, branching points or distinguished boundaries; in such cases, currents and varifolds metrics are limited as they do not capture ‘higher-order’ properties of the underlying submanifold. Indeed, undesirable features may be generated during diffeomorphic registration when these metrics are used as discrepancy terms for matching complex shapes with the aforementioned features
Charon et al. (2020)
.

A theoretically grounded solution is the normal cycles representation, which originates in geometric measure theory
Federer (1996)
;
Federer (1959)
and has been applied to computational anatomy and LDDMM
Roussillon (2017)
;
Roussillon and Glaunès (2019)
. Whereas the current associated to a surface integrates test differential forms over the surface itself, the normal cycle integrates them over the surface’s
unit normal bundle
, yielding an enriched representation that is sensitive not only to the surface but also to the rate of change of its normal vectors. As a consequence, the normal cycle locally encodes the mean and Gaussian curvatures of the surface
Roussillon (2017)
.

In a similar manner to currents and varifolds, one may compute the distance between two shapes in terms of the dual metric between their normal cycles, embedded into the dual of a suitable RKHS of forms. The advantages of comparing shapes with normal cycles instead of currents or varifolds are evidenced numerically on shapes with high curvature regions, boundaries and branching points in
Roussillon and Glaunès (2019)
. However, the practical computation of the normal cycles metric is costly in terms of both memory and computational complexity. The normal cycles metric complexity scales as
\mathcal{O}(MN)
when comparing triangulated surfaces with
M,N
edges, respectively. While tools such as the KeOps library
Charlier et al. (2021)
exist for large-scale metric (and gradients) computation, it is observed empirically in
Roussillon and Glaunès (2019)
that even with these tools, the normal cycles representation is at least
3
–
5
times more expensive to compute and take gradients of than currents and varifolds and can become very slow for large-scale problems with resolution past
10^{5}
.

1.1
Contribution

In this work, we extend the randomised projection algorithm of
(2026)
from currents and varifolds to normal cycles. Building on
Roussillon and Glaunès (2019)
, we derive an explicit Dirac delta basis form for the discrete embedded normal cycle in the constant normal kernel case (as in
Roussillon and Glaunès (2019)
), which lets us apply the main theorem of
(2026)
and compress a target normal cycle of size
M
to a sparse approximation of size
m\ll M
. Post-compression, the metric between two compressed normal cycles can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(m^{2})
rather than
\mathcal{O}(M^{2})
, with the corresponding gain in gradient cost. To our knowledge this is the first compression algorithm for normal cycles, allowing the metric to scale to problems with resolution
M\sim 10^{5}
. The theoretical guarantees of
(2026)
carry over directly, yielding exponential error-decay bounds (for Gaussian RKHS kernels) of the form

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mu-\hat{\mu}\right\rVert_{W^{*}}^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}(m\exp(-\alpha m^{\frac{1}{d}})),

where
\hat{\mu}
is the approximation of size
m\ll M
to the original normal cycle
\mu\in W^{*}
and
\alpha>0
. The practical benefits are demonstrated in
Section

4
, where we obtain order-of-magnitude acceleration of LDDMM registration on heavily over-sampled real-world surface data; the downstream utility of
Algorithm

1
is further demonstrated in the companion work
Hartshorne et al. (2025b)
, where it is the core matching metric in a learning-based pipeline.

1.2
Existing work

Compression of currents and varifolds has been studied previously in
Durrleman et al. (2009)
;
Hsieh and Charon (2021)
, but the methods of those works are substantially slower than the ridge-leverage-score scheme of
(2026)
on which we build, and do not readily extend to the normal cycles setting. Other work on normal cycles
Roussillon (2017)
;
Roussillon and Glaunès (2019)
;
Cohen-Steiner and Morvan (2003)
has focused on formulation and on applications to surface registration and curvature estimation, not on scaling the metric to large
M
.

Outline

Background

The theory of currents was developed by Federer
Federer (1959)

within the framework of geometric measure theory, originally for the
study of the Plateau problem; the notions of curvature measure,
sets of positive reach, and the additivity property of normal
cycles originate in the same work, see also the
monograph
Federer (1996)
. The normal cycle, which generalises
curvature measures to non-smooth sets, was subsequently developed
by Wintgen
Wintgen (1982)
,
Zähle
Zähle (1986)
, and
Fu
Fu (1989)
, among others. Curvature estimation on
polyhedral surfaces via normal cycles was studied by Cohen-Steiner
and Morvan
Cohen-Steiner and Morvan (2003)
; see
also
Morvan (2008)
for a comprehensive treatment of
generalised curvatures and normal cycles. The application of normal
cycles to computational anatomy and LDDMM was introduced
in
Roussillon (2017)
;
Roussillon and Glaunès (2019)
.

We now define the normal cycle rigorously, using exterior algebra
and differential forms; see
appendix

A
for background. We
follow the notation of
Roussillon (2017)
and begin with the currents
representation, from which the normal cycle is obtained as a
higher-order extension.

2.1
Currents

The core idea of currents is to represent submanifolds as objects that integrate continuous differential forms. Formally, one begins by defining the space of continuous compactly supported differential
m
-forms (for
m\leq d
)

\displaystyle\Omega_{0}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d},(\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}),

equipped with the uniform norm, where
(\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}\simeq\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the dual to the space of
m
-vectors formed through wedge products. See
appendix

A
for background on exterior product spaces. This leads to the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

The space of
m
-currents is defined as
\Omega_{0}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}
, which is the topological dual of
\Omega_{0}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This means that
m
-currents are bounded linear functionals
T\colon\Omega_{0}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
such that the following holds for some
C(T)>0
:

\displaystyle\lvert T(\omega)\rvert\leq C(T)\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{\infty},\qquad\forall\omega\in\Omega_{0}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Given an (oriented)
m
-rectifiable set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the unique current associated with it is defined as
[S]\in\Omega_{0}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}
with action

\displaystyle[S](\omega)\coloneq\int_{S}{(\omega(x)|\tau^{S}(x))\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(x)},\quad\omega\in\Omega_{0}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

(2.1)

where for
\mathcal{H}^{m}
-almost every
x
in
S
, one defines
\tau^{S}(x)\coloneq e_{1}(x)\wedge\dots\wedge e_{m}(x)
, a wedge product of (positively oriented) orthonormal basis vectors of the tangent space at
x
. This is well defined, as the tangent space exists
\mathcal{H}^{m}
-almost everywhere for an
m
-rectifiable set, and the choice of basis vectors does not matter due to invariance of the wedge product under change of orthonormal basis.

From
Eq.

2.1
, we observe that the current associated to
S
integrates a differential
m
-form over
S
with respect to the Hausdorff measure. When
S
is a smooth oriented
m
-dimensional submanifold, this reduces to the standard definition of integration of
m
-forms against
S

Roussillon (2017)
. As shown in
Lemma

A.1
,

\displaystyle\lvert[S](\omega)\rvert\leq\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{\infty}\mathcal{H}^{m}(S),\quad\omega\in\Omega_{0}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

so that
[S]
is indeed a well-defined element of
\Omega_{0}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}
. One can also define a natural push-forward action,
\varphi_{\#}[S]\in\Omega_{0}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}
, on currents under the action of a diffeomorphism
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
on the ambient space. While we do not pursue this definition here, one may prove
Roussillon (2017)
that this action is geometric in the sense that

\varphi_{\#}[S]=[\varphi(S)]
.
This identity makes the diffeomorphic pushforward of a current a straightforward object to handle. This is particularly useful for applications of currents in the LDDMM framework, where shapes are modelled as deformations of template shapes under the action of a diffeomorphism.

2.1.1
Dual space metrics for currents

It was originally suggested in
Vaillant and Glaunès (2005)
that in practical applications, one may compare shapes and submanifolds in a non-parametric, correspondence-less manner, by comparing the associated currents in the dual space metric. The associated distance between the currents associated to shapes may then be measured as

\displaystyle d\bigl([S],[T]\bigr)\coloneq\left\lVert\vphantom{\big|}[S]-[T]\right\rVert_{\Omega_{0}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}}=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega\in\Omega_{0}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\\
\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1\end{subarray}}{\sup}{\big\lvert[S](\omega)-[T](\omega)\big\rvert}.

(2.2)

In
Eq.

2.2
, shapes are compared in terms of how similarly or differently they integrate the same differential forms, and one computes the maximum such discrepancy over all
\omega\in\Omega_{0}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Unfortunately, the uniform norm topology over
\Omega_{0}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is too strong and does not give a useful measure of dissimilarity to compare and register shapes as proven in
Charon and Trouvé (2013)
. Indeed, the distance
Eq.

2.2
simply collapses to the sum of the Hausdorff measure of each shape when
S\neq T
– thus failing to give a useful distance between the shapes.

Instead, as proposed by
Vaillant and Glaunès (2005)
, one embeds currents of interest
[S],[T]
into the
dual
of a smaller space
W\subset\Omega_{0}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of differential forms, equipped with an RKHS structure. The natural dual metric on
W^{*}
induces a weaker topology on currents, and has closed-form expressions which one may use for comparison and optimisation purposes. Following
Vaillant and Glaunès (2005)
, we let
W
be an RKHS of differential forms, generated by a kernel
K\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathcal{L}(\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})
of the form

\displaystyle K(x,y)=K_{p}(x,y)\mathrm{Id}_{\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d}},

where
K_{p}
is a sufficiently smooth positive-definite scalar-valued spatial kernel on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Such a
W
takes the form

\displaystyle W=\bigg\{f=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{K_{p}(\cdot,x_{i})\tau_{i}}\colon\tau_{i}\in\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{W}<\infty\bigg\},

where
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{W}^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{\infty}{K_{p}(x_{i},x_{j})\langle\tau_{i},\tau_{j}\rangle_{\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d}}}
.
Provided that
W
is dense in
\Omega_{0}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
K_{p}
is bounded, this guarantees the dual embedding
\Omega_{0}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}\subset W^{*}
is an injection
Roussillon (2017)
, and that the
W^{*}
metric defines a true metric on the space of currents. A popular choice of kernel function that ensures this is the Gaussian radial basis function (RBF) kernel. The action of
[S],[T]
as elements of
W^{*}
is the same integral action as in
Eq.

2.1
, and they are well-defined elements of
W^{*}
due to the bound

\displaystyle\lvert[S](\omega)\rvert\leq\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{\infty}\mathcal{H}^{m}(S)\leq c\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{W}\mathcal{H}^{m}(S),\qquad\omega\in W,

where we have used the RKHS identity
\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq c\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{W}
for some
c>0
. Using the reproducing property of the RKHS kernel, one may compute
Vaillant and Glaunès (2005)
the dual metric in
W^{*}
between
[S],[T]
explicitly in terms of integration as

\displaystyle d_{W^{*}}([S],[T])^{2}=\left\lVert\vphantom{\big|}[S]-[T]\right\rVert_{W^{*}}^{2}=\bigl\langle[S],[S]\bigr\rangle_{W^{*}}-2\bigl\langle[S],[T]\bigr\rangle_{W^{*}}+\bigl\langle[T],[T]\bigr\rangle_{W^{*}},

(2.3)

where the product terms are defined as

\displaystyle\bigl\langle[S],[T]\bigr\rangle_{W^{*}}=\int_{S}\int_{T}{K_{p}(x,y)\langle\tau^{S}(x),\tau^{T}(y)\rangle_{\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(y)}.

(2.4)

In the discrete case, when we have triangular approximations
\hat{S},\hat{T}
of surfaces
S,T
for example, one forms approximate currents

\displaystyle[\hat{S}]\approx\widehat{[S]}\coloneq\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\delta_{c_{i,\hat{S}}}\tilde{\tau}_{i,\hat{S}}}\in W^{*},\quad[\hat{T}]\approx\widehat{[T]}\coloneq\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\delta_{c_{i,\hat{T}}}\tilde{\tau}_{i,\hat{T}}}\in W^{*},

(2.5)

where
c_{i,\hat{S}},c_{i,\hat{T}}
denote the centre of the
i
th triangle of
\hat{S},\hat{T}
, respectively. The discrete weights are defined as
\tilde{\tau}_{i,\hat{S}}={\tau}^{\hat{S}}(c_{i,\hat{S}})a_{i,S}
where
a_{i,\hat{S}}
is the
i
th triangle area. Applying the reproducing property of the RKHS kernel yields an easily computed dual metric with ‘discrete’ product term

\displaystyle\big\langle\widehat{[S]},\widehat{[T]}\big\rangle_{W^{*}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}{K_{p}(c_{i,\hat{S}},c_{j,\hat{T}})\langle\tilde{\tau}_{i,\hat{S}},\tilde{\tau}_{j,\hat{T}}\rangle_{\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d}}}.

(2.6)

This may be interpreted as the discretisation of
Eq.

2.4
. This yields the discrete currents metric

\displaystyle d_{W^{*}}\bigl(\widehat{[S]},\widehat{[T]}\bigr)^{2}=\bigl\|{\widehat{[S]}-\widehat{[T]}}\bigr\|^{2}_{W^{*}}=\bigl\langle\widehat{[S]},\widehat{[S]}\bigr\rangle_{W^{*}}-2\bigl\langle\widehat{[S]},\widehat{[T]}\bigr\rangle_{W^{*}}+\bigl\langle\widehat{[T]},\widehat{[T]}\bigr\rangle_{W^{*}}.

Such delta approximations as in
Eq.

2.5
are known to be consistent
Charon et al. (2020)
in the sense that

\bigl\|{\widehat{[S]}-[S]}\bigr\|_{W^{*}}\lesssim\tau(h),

where the bounding function satisfies
\tau(h)\to 0
as
h
, the triangulation mesh size, tends to zero.

In order to practically compute the weights in
Eq.

2.6
, one observes
Charon et al. (2020)
the identification
\Lambda^{2}\mathbb{R}^{3}\simeq\Lambda^{1}\mathbb{R}^{3}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{3}
. For surface data, we have
(d,m)=(3,2)
so that we may identify
W
with a space of vector fields on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Under this identification, for triangulated surfaces,
\tau^{S}(x)
corresponds to the unit normal vector at
x\in S
, which is simple to compute. For curves in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
or
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have
(d,m)=(3,1)
or
(d,m)=(2,1)
, respectively, so
W
is again a space of vector fields. In either case, similar Dirac delta decompositions hold, with the weights reducing to the discrete tangent vectors of the discrete curve.

2.2
Normal cycles

The normal cycle of a smooth oriented submanifold
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the current associated to the unit normal bundle of
S
; integrating against the normal bundle captures both position and the rate of change of normals along
S
. We give the precise definition following the notation of
Roussillon and Glaunès (2019)
.
First, we introduce the sets of positive reach following
Federer (1959)
.

Definition 2.2
.

If
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the reach
R\in\mathbb{R}
of
S
is defined as the supremum of
r>0
such that there exists a unique projection onto
S
from
\partial S_{r}\coloneq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:d(x,S)=r\}
, where
d(x,S)\coloneq\inf_{s\in S}\|x-s\|
. A set with reach
R>0
is a set with positive reach.

This definition encompasses all convex sets and smooth submanifolds. For any such
S
and any
r\in(0,R)
, the unit normal bundle is

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{S}\coloneq\{(x,n)\in S\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}:x+rn\in\partial S_{r}\},

(2.7)

which is independent of
r
and of the orientation of
S
, and is a
(d-1)
-dimensional orientable rectifiable subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. For a smooth surface or space curve
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, for example,
\mathcal{N}_{S}
is a
2
-dimensional manifold in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
, visualised as a smooth ‘fattening’ of
S
. Rectifiability and canonical orientation make the associated current well defined; this current is the
normal cycle
of
S
, defined formally below.
Let

\displaystyle\Omega_{0}^{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})=\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1},(\Lambda^{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{*})

for
(d-1)
differential forms on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

Definition 2.3
.

Given a set of positive reach
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the normal cycle associated to
S
is defined as
N(S)\coloneq[\mathcal{N}_{S}]
which is an element of
\Omega_{0}^{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})^{*}
. The action of
N(S)
on
\omega\in\Omega_{0}^{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
is given by

\displaystyle N(S)(\omega)=[\mathcal{N_{S}}](\omega)=\int_{\mathcal{N}_{S}}{\Bigl(\omega(x,\nu)\mid\tau^{\mathcal{N_{S}}}(x,\nu)\Bigr)\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(x,\nu)},

(2.8)

where
\tau^{\mathcal{N_{S}}}(x,\nu)=e_{1}(x,\nu)\wedge\dots\wedge e_{d-1}(x,\nu)
is the wedge product of a positively oriented orthonormal basis of the tangent space at point
(x,\nu)
.

Specific differential forms recover the mean and Gaussian curvatures of
S
locally
Roussillon (2017)
.

In practical geometric learning, we work with discrete shapes in the form of the union of curve segments and triangulated surfaces. The definition of normal cycle given above may also be generalised
Roussillon and Glaunès (2019)
;
Roussillon (2017)
to all finite unions of sets of positive reach. Indeed, if
S=S_{1}\cup S_{2}
is a union of sets of positive reach, such that
S_{1}\cap S_{2}
has positive reach, then
the additivity property holds
Federer (1959)
:

\displaystyle N(S)\coloneq N({S_{1}})+N({S_{2}})-N({S_{1}\cap S_{2}}).

(2.9)

The left and right-hand sides agree if
S
has positive reach. In order to compute the normal cycle
N(\mathcal{T})
of a triangulated surface
\mathcal{T}=\cup_{i=1}^{n_{T}}{T_{i}}
given as a union of closed triangles, one may apply
Eq.

2.9
recursively to obtain a decomposition in terms of the normal cycles of individual triangles and segments. We shall use this decomposition in
Section

3
.

2.2.1
Dual space metric: normal cycles

Similar to the case of currents, the topology on the dual space induced by the uniform norm is too strong to give a meaningful distance between normal cycles. As for currents, fixing an appropriate RKHS
W\subset\Omega_{0}^{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
of test differential forms, one embeds the normal cycle representation into the dual
W^{*}
. As we shall see, this allows one to tractably compute metrics between normal cycles in terms of the kernel function.

One begins by defining a
\mathcal{L}(\Lambda^{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}))
-valued positive-definite kernel on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
as

\displaystyle K((x,u),(y,v))=K_{p}(x,y)K_{s}(u,v)\mathrm{Id}_{\Lambda^{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})},\quad\forall(x,u),(y,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}.

(2.10)

For sufficient regularity of
K_{p}
and
K_{s}
, this induces an RKHS
W\subset\Omega_{0}^{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
of forms
\omega\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\to\Lambda^{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
(see
Roussillon and Glaunès (2019)
;
Roussillon (2017)
). The space
W
takes the form

W=\bigg\{f=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{K_{p}(\cdot,x_{i})K_{s}(\cdot,s_{i})\tau_{i}}\colon\begin{subarray}{l}(x_{i},s_{i},\tau_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\Lambda^{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}),\\
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{W}<\infty\end{subarray}\bigg\}

(2.11)

where

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{W}^{2}\coloneqq\sum_{i,j=1}^{\infty}{K_{p}(x_{i},x_{j})K_{s}(s_{i},s_{j})\langle\tau_{i},\tau_{j}\rangle_{\Lambda^{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}}.

Typical choices for the spherical kernel are constant
K_{s}(u,v)=1
, linear
K_{s}(u,v)=\langle u,v\rangle
, and Gaussian
K_{s}(u,v)=\exp(-\frac{\left\lVert u-v\right\rVert^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}})
. One typically sets
K_{p}
to be the Gaussian RBF kernel.

With the same action as
Eq.

2.8
on elements of
W
, a normal cycle
N(S)
may be embedded as a well-defined element of
W^{*}
, through the inclusion
\Omega_{0}^{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})^{*}\subset W^{*}
. By the reproducing property of the kernel, the dual metric between normal cycles
N(S),N(T)
may be expressed as

\displaystyle\left\lVert N(S)-N(T)\right\rVert_{W^{*}}^{2}=\langle N(S),N(S)\rangle_{{W^{*}}}-2\langle N(S),N(T)\rangle_{{}_{W^{*}}}+\langle N(T),N(T)\rangle_{{W^{*}}},

with product term

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle N(S),N(T)\rangle_{{}_{W^{*}}}\coloneq&\int_{\mathcal{N}_{S}}\int_{\mathcal{N}_{T}}K_{p}(x,y)K_{s}(u,v)\\
&\qquad\qquad\quad\times\langle\tau_{S}(x,u),\tau_{T}(y,v)\rangle\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(x,u)\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(y,v),\end{split}

(2.12)

where
\tau_{S}(x,u),\tau_{T}(x,u)
are defined as in
Eq.

2.8
. Under suitable conditions on the spatial and spherical kernels, the inclusion
\Omega_{0}^{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})^{*}\subset W^{*}
is injective, and the dual metric yields a well-defined distance on
\Omega_{0}^{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})^{*}
. For the
K_{s}=1
case, which is the primary setting for surface matching explored in
Roussillon and Glaunès (2019)
and in this work, the resulting

is not necessarily dense in the space of differential forms, and thus the
W^{*}
metric is only a pseudo-metric on
\Omega_{0}^{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})^{*}
.

For practical applications with discrete shapes, the inner-product in
Eq.

2.12
must be approximated; this is significantly trickier than for currents, as the normal cycle also involves integration over the ‘spherical’ components of the normal bundle. For triangulated surfaces in the case of
K_{s}=1
, this is the content of the following theorem from
Roussillon and Glaunès (2019)
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{T},\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
be two triangulated surfaces. The inner product between the associated discrete normal cycles can be computed in
W^{*}
when
K_{s}=1
as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle&N(\mathcal{T}),N(\mathcal{T}^{\prime})\rangle_{W^{*}}\\
&=4\sum_{i=1}^{n_{e}}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{e}}{K_{p}(c_{i},d_{j})\langle f_{i},g_{j}\rangle}\times\bigg\langle\sum_{\{T\colon f_{i}\in\partial T\}}{n_{T,f_{i}}},\sum_{\{T\colon g_{j}\in\partial T\}}{n_{T,g_{j}}}\bigg\rangle\\
&\quad+\ \pi^{2}\sum_{x_{i}\in\partial\mathcal{T}}\sum_{y_{j}\in\partial\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}{K_{p}(x_{i},y_{j})\langle A_{i},B_{j}\rangle},\end{split}

(2.13)

where
\partial\mathcal{T}
denotes the boundary of the surface
\mathcal{T}
and similarly for
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
. In the above,
n_{e},m_{e}
denote the number of unique edges in each triangulation, and
f_{i},g_{j}
the edges of
\mathcal{T},\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
respectively. The kernel function in the first double sum is evaluated on
c_{i},d_{j}
, which denote centres of the edges
f_{i},g_{j}
, respectively. In the second double sum, the kernel function is evaluated on
x_{i},y_{j}
, which are the vertices of the boundary. Furthermore, the quantity
A_{i}\coloneq\sum_{k}{f_{k}^{i}/\lvert f_{k}^{i}\rvert}
is defined as the sum of normalised edges
f^{i}_{k}
attached to the boundary vertex
x_{i}
, oriented outwards from
x_{i}
. Finally,
n_{T,f_{i}}
denotes the normal to triangle
T
coinciding with edge
f_{i}
, such that
n_{T,f_{i}}\times f_{i}
is oriented inwards for the triangle
T
.

Remark 2.1
.

The constant prefactors
4
and
\pi^{2}
in
Eq.

2.13
differ from the single prefactor
\pi^{2}/4
appearing for both terms in
(
Roussillon and Glaunès, 2019
, Prop. 3)
. Tracing the derivation back to the underlying integrals, this discrepancy originates in the evaluation of the half-circle integral
\int_{C}u\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}(u)
over the semicircle
C=S^{\perp,+}_{f,-f\times n_{T}}
. A self-contained derivation is given in
Lemma

B.1
. The formulae in
Theorems

2.1
and
2.14
use the corrected value, and the derivation of the Dirac delta decomposition in
appendix

B
is consistent with it.

Unlike currents and varifolds, the normal cycle metric has an explicit term for comparing boundaries of shapes, reflecting its sensitivity to boundary geometry. The computational complexity of computing
Eq.

2.13
is much higher than that of currents. The first term is a double loop over edges, of which there are more than the number of triangles, and each term involves more inner product computations than for currents. Furthermore, the added boundary term also contributes an additional cost for shapes with boundary. Even with KeOps, the practical cost of computing the metric and its gradient is around
3
–
5
times higher for normal cycles compared to currents and varifolds
Roussillon and Glaunès (2019)
.

For discrete curves in the case
K_{s}=1
, we have the following representation of the metric as a double sum across edges
Roussillon (2017)
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
C
and
S
be two discrete curves (unions of segments) with

and
M
vertices, respectively. Denoting the spatial kernel as

and fixing constant normal kernel

\displaystyle\langle N(C),N(S)\rangle_{W^{*}}={\pi^{2}}{}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{M}{K_{p}(x_{i},y_{j})}\langle A_{i},B_{j}\rangle},

(2.14)

where

is the sum of normalised edges attached to vertex

, and oriented outwards from

.

This has a similar complexity to the currents metric between discrete curves.

Remark 2.2
.

When

as in the previous theorems, the product kernel reduces to

\displaystyle K((x,u),(y,v))=K_{p}(x,y)\mathrm{Id}_{\Lambda^{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}

3.1.1
Vector-valued embedding

With the Dirac delta decomposition
Eq.

3.2
, we are almost in a position to apply the compression algorithm of
(2026)
to obtain a sparse approximation to

. However, the compression algorithm assumes that

is an RKHS of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
valued functions for some

. To address this, we fix a standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{6}
and
\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})\simeq\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
(of dimension
\binom{6}{2}=15
), and use the resulting basis map to construct an isometric isomorphism of
W
to an RKHS
V
of
\mathbb{R}^{15}
-valued functions.
This type of embedding arises in
Charon et al. (2020)
for currents and varifolds, where
V
is an RKHS of vector fields and real-valued functions.

Concretely, one begins by fixing a choice of isomorphism
B\colon\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})\to\mathbb{R}^{15}
induced by the standard basis
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}=\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{6}
of
\mathbb{R}^{6}
. In particular, given a wedge product
a\wedge b\in\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
, we may expand in terms of
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
to obtain

\displaystyle a\wedge b=\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{6}{a_{i}e_{i}}\bigg)\wedge\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{6}{b_{j}e_{j}}\bigg)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{6}{a_{i}b_{j}(e_{i}\wedge e_{j})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 6}{(a_{i}b_{j}-a_{j}b_{i})(e_{i}\wedge e_{j})}\eqcolon\sum_{k=1}^{15}{c_{k}(a,b)\beta_{k}},

(3.3)

using the bilinearity and antisymmetry properties of the wedge product. In
Eq.

3.3
, we have labelled the corresponding orthonormal wedge basis

\mathcal{B}\coloneq\{\beta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{15}\coloneq\{e_{i}\wedge e_{j}\}_{1\leq i<j\leq 6}

induced by
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
. The coefficients
c_{k}(a,b)
are the antisymmetric coefficients in
Eq.

3.3
of the
k
th wedge basis element
\beta_{k}
. With respect to these fixed standard bases
\mathcal{B},\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
, one may define
B\colon\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})\to\mathbb{R}^{15}
as the linear basis map defined by

B(\beta_{k})=\tilde{e}_{k},\quad k\in\{1,\dots,15\},

where
\tilde{e}_{k}
denote standard basis vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{15}
(distinct from the basis
e_{i}
of
\mathbb{R}^{6}
). This mapping is extended linearly to general wedge products as

\displaystyle B(a\wedge b)=\sum_{k=1}^{15}{c_{k}(a,b)\tilde{e}_{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{15}.

As
B
is a linear map preserving orthonormal bases of the two finite-dimensional inner product spaces, it is an isometric isomorphism.

Having fixed
B
(with respect to choice of standard bases), we may isometrically identify the embedding space
W
in
Eq.

2.15
of
\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
valued mappings, with an RKHS

of
\mathbb{R}^{15}
-valued functions, induced by the kernel

\displaystyle K((x,u),(y,v))=K_{p}(x,y)\mathrm{I}_{{15}},\quad\forall(x,u),(y,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}.

This identification is induced by an isometric isomorphism
F\colon W\to V
with action

F(K_{p}({x},\cdot)\tau)\coloneq K_{p}({x},\cdot)B(\tau),

which extends linearly to general elements of
W
. This in turn induces an isometric isomorphism of dual spaces
\tilde{F}\colon W^{*}\to V^{*}
, which allows one to embed
N(\mathcal{T})
of
Eq.

3.2
into

as

\displaystyle\tilde{F}(N(\mathcal{T}))=2\sum_{i=1}^{n_{e}}{\delta_{c_{i}}B(\gamma_{i})}+{\pi}\sum_{x_{i}\in\partial\mathcal{T}}{\delta_{x_{i}}B(\alpha_{i})}\in V^{*}

(3.4)

with coefficient vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{15}
. Given the weights
\gamma_{i},\alpha_{i}
associated to

in
Eq.

3.2
, each of the weights
B(\gamma_{i}),B(\alpha_{i})
of

3.2
Compression of normal cycles

The decomposition
Eq.

3.2
(and its curve analogue) writes the embedded normal cycle in the generic form

\displaystyle\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\delta_{x_{i}}\alpha_{i}}\in V^{*},\quad(x_{i},\alpha_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k},

with
k=15
for triangulated surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, where

is the RKHS of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
-valued functions induced by the product kernel
K_{p}\,\mathrm{Id}_{k}
. The compression algorithm of
(2026)
produces a sparse approximation
\hat{\mu}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\delta_{c_{i}}\beta_{i}}
with
m\ll n
, as follows.

Algorithm 1

Discrete functional compression with RLS sampling

1:

Fix domain
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
m\ll n
, RKHS
V
, RKHS kernel function
K_{p}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
and target functional

\displaystyle\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\delta_{x_{i}}\alpha_{i}}\in V^{*},\quad(x_{i},\alpha_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}.

2:

Sample
m
distinct control points
\{c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\subset\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
using approximate ridge leverage score (RLS)
Musco and Musco (2017)
;
Cherfaoui et al. (2022)
sampling.

3:

Compute the vectors

\displaystyle y_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{K_{p}(c_{j},x_{i})\alpha_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k},\quad j=1,\dots,m,

and define
y=[y_{1},\dots,y_{m}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
.

4:

Form the control point approximation via orthogonal projection

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\delta_{c_{i}}\beta_{i}}\in V^{*},\quad\beta=K_{CC}^{-1}y\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k},\quad[K_{CC}]_{ij}=K_{p}(c_{i},c_{j})

Control point sampling using approximate RLS sampling algorithms of
Musco and Musco (2017)
;
Cherfaoui et al. (2022)
(with
\mathcal{O}(m^{2}n)
complexity) is fast in practice and yields strong theoretical bounds for decay of the compression error, as proven in
(2026)
.

Theorem 3.2

(
(2026)
)
.

Suppose we have a discrete target functional of the form

\displaystyle\mu_{S}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\delta_{x_{i}}\alpha_{i}}\in V^{*},\quad(x_{i},\alpha_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}

with associated dual vector-valued function

\displaystyle v^{\alpha}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{K_{p}(x,x_{i})\alpha_{i}}.

Subsample
m
distinct control points
\bm{c}=\{c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\subset\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and define the matrix of values

\displaystyle Y_{C}=(v^{\alpha}(c_{1}),\dots,v^{\alpha}(c_{m}))^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k},

which is the evaluation of the dual vector-valued function on the control point locations.
Computing weights

\displaystyle\beta=[\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m}]^{T}=K_{CC}^{-1}Y_{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k},\quad[K_{CC}]_{ij}=K_{p}(c_{i},c_{j})

yields an approximation

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{S}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\delta_{c_{i}}\beta_{i}}

that satisfies

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mu_{S}-\hat{\mu}_{S}\right\rVert_{V^{*}}^{2}\leq C\operatorname{Tr}(K_{XX}-Q_{XX}),\quad Q_{XX}\coloneq K_{XC}K_{CC}^{-1}K_{CX}.

(3.5)

Furthermore, fixing
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{32})
and
S\in\mathbb{N}
, by subsampling control points using the recursive RLS scheme of
Musco and Musco (2017)
, with probability
1-3\delta
we have for a randomized sample size
m\approx S\log(S/\delta)
that

\displaystyle\left\lVert{\mu_{S}-\hat{\mu}_{S}}\right\rVert_{V^{*}}^{2}\leq\frac{Cn}{S}\sum_{i=S+1}^{n}{\lambda_{i}(K_{XX})}.

(3.6)

That is, the randomised compression error decays at least as fast as the eigenvalues of the RKHS kernel matrix, evaluated on the original delta centres. For typical choices of the spatial kernel such as Gaussian RBF, this decay is exponential
(2026)
in
m
, and allows one to compress the target normal cycle to a very small level
m\ll n
, with negligible error in the RKHS norm. We demonstrate this in the following section.

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle\partial_{t}c_{i}^{s}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{P}{k(c_{i}^{s}(t),c_{j}^{s}(t))\alpha_{j}(t)},\qquad c_{i}^{s}(0)=c_{i}^{s},\\

\varphi_{01}^{v^{\alpha}}
deforming the template. It is common practice
Vaillant and Glaunès (2005)
to approximate
\varphi(T)
by computing the pushforward of the
n_{v}
vertices of
T
and forming a new triangulation from the deformed vertices with the
same
mesh connectivity as the original template. This approximation has negligible error for finely sampled shapes. The initial momenta may be optimised with respect to
Eq.

4.1
, so that
\mu_{\varphi^{v^{\bm{\alpha}}}_{01}(T)}\approx\mu_{S}
in the dual metric on the normal cycle embedding.

Computing the objective and its gradients splits into two parts: flow computation and metric computation. At each step of the ODE solver for the flow, one computes a reduction at cost
\mathcal{O}(Pn_{v})
, which may be implemented efficiently using KeOps, and is fast as one takes
P\ll n_{v}
in the control point parametrisation of deformations. The number of vertices
n_{v}
is also much fewer than the number of edges
n_{e}
, and one usually only requires simple first or second-order solvers with few evaluations for the flow. Both in terms of complexity and memory, the metric (and its gradient) computation at
\mathcal{O}(n_{e}^{2})
becomes the dominating cost; for very large
n_{e}
, this computation can become very slow even with KeOps.

We compare this to a modified
compressed
version of the above matching algorithm, which minimises the following objective

\displaystyle E(\bm{\alpha})\coloneq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert v^{\bm{\alpha}}(0)\right\rVert_{V_{k}}^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\mu}_{S}-\hat{\mu}_{\varphi^{v^{\bm{\alpha}}}_{01}(T)}\right\rVert_{V^{*}}^{2},\quad\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{P})\in\mathbb{R}^{Pd},

(4.4)

where the deformation control points and Hamiltonian dynamics
Eq.

4.3
are unchanged between objectives
Eq.

4.1
and
Eq.

4.4
. The cost of flow (and flow gradient) computation remains the same as the deformation mechanics are unchanged. The main difference here is the compressed target normal cycle
\hat{\mu}_{S}
and compressed deformed template normal cycle
\hat{\mu}_{\varphi^{v^{\bm{\alpha}}}_{01}(T)}
in the discrepancy term. The target normal cycle is compressed once, offline (pre-optimisation), and the deformed template normal cycle is compressed at each iteration. We assume a fixed level of compression
m\ll n_{e}
has been chosen. For the deformed template, instead of RLS sampling for compression at each iteration of the registration algorithm, we sample the template normal cycle
once pre-optimisation
, and project the deformed template at each iteration onto the transported RLS samples.

The cost of performing this projection of the deformed template per-iteration is
\mathcal{O}(m^{3}+mn_{e})
, which comes from the linear solve and reduction operation during compression. The reduction operation of cost
\mathcal{O}(mn_{e})
may be implemented efficiently via KeOps and is rapid. The linear systems to be solved at cost
m^{3}
are of size
m\ll n_{e}
, which in practice are small enough to fit comfortably on GPU memory, allowing one to parallelise and significantly accelerate the resulting linear solve. Therefore, compressing the deformed template normal cycle and computing the metric (and its gradients) using the compressed normal cycles is much faster than computation of the full metric and gradient. One may also achieve further speed-up by exploiting the structure of the systems to be solved (e.g., sparsity, symmetry) and using conjugate gradient type schemes, which have been recently combined with the efficient KeOps reduction framework
Charlier et al. (2021)
.

1

The compression scheme of

The ridge-leverage-score sampling step at the heart of

5
Conclusion

In this work, we have extended our algorithm in
(2026)
to compression of normal cycles, allowing us to scale up the normal cycles metric to shapes with large-scale resolution of the order
10^{5}
–
10^{6}
, while keeping the computational cost at a fraction of the full representation. Furthermore, our compression comes with fast error decay and guarantees of small RKHS distance to the normal cycle of the true underlying shape. We have demonstrated the effectiveness of the compression method on real-world shape data in downstream tasks such as nonlinear LDDMM registration. Indeed, our experiments show dramatically reduced run times, with negligible difference in registration quality. We leave as future work the extension of the compression algorithm to linear and Gaussian normal kernel cases, which can help increase the sensitivity of the underlying shape representations to higher-order curvature information.

Background

We briefly introduce definitions required for defining currents and normal cycles in
Section

2
.

A.1
Wedge product and differential forms

Given two vectors
v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the wedge or exterior product is an operation on
(v,w)
that produces an object
v\wedge w
in a way that naturally generalises the properties of the cross product in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, both algebraically and geometrically. In particular,
v\wedge w
contains information about the oriented parallelogram spanned by
v,w
, with modulus equal to the area of the associated parallelogram. This product is characterised by the following properties for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
v,w,z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\wedge
is bilinear:

(v+\alpha w)\wedge z=v\wedge z+\alpha(w\wedge z)
,

\wedge
is alternating:

v\wedge v=0
, and

\wedge
is antisymmetric:

v\wedge w=-w\wedge v
.

The space in which
v\wedge w
lives is denoted
\Lambda^{2}\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the
2
nd exterior power of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which consists of formal linear combinations of elements of the form
v\wedge w
, giving it a vector space structure. One can also define analogously
\Lambda^{3}\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the third exterior power of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
consisting of formal linear combinations of elements
v\wedge w\wedge z
, representing an oriented parallelepiped, spanned by the three vectors. This process can be repeated to obtain the
k
th exterior power of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, denoted
\Lambda^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d}
for all
k<d
.

We now give a formal description of the exterior product spaces following
Sternberg (1983)
;
Morgan (2016)
;
Roussillon (2017)
. We begin by defining the space of alternating multilinear forms on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Definition A.1
.

An alternating multilinear form of degree
k
is a mapping

\displaystyle f:\underbrace{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\dots\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}_{k\ \mathrm{times}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

with the properties that
f
is linear in each entry and
f(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})=0
, whenever
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
are linearly dependent.
Note that these two properties automatically imply

\displaystyle f(v_{\sigma(1)},\dots,v_{\sigma(k)})=\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)f(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})

for all permutations
\sigma
of
\{1,\dots,k\}
. We denote the space of alternating
k
-multilinear forms as
A^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which is a finite-dimensional vector space under pointwise addition and multiplication.

One may subsequently define the
k
th exterior product space
\Lambda^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d}
as follows.

Definition A.2
.

For
0\leq k\leq d
, one defines the
k
th exterior product of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
\Lambda^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d}\coloneq(A^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}
, the dual of the space of alternating
k
-multilinear forms. One further defines the wedge product of vectors
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as the element denoted
v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{k}\in\Lambda^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d}
with action

\displaystyle(v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{k})(f)\coloneq f(v_{1},\dots,v_{k}),\quad f\in A^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(A.1)

It can be shown that products of this form span
\Lambda^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d}
, so that the dual space is made up of linear combinations of such elements. In particular, one has
\mathrm{dim}(\Lambda^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d})=\binom{d}{k}
as a basis of this space is given by
\mathcal{B}=\{e_{i_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{i_{k}}\}_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq d}
. Furthermore, it can be verified that the wedge product of
k
vectors as defined in
Eq.

A.1
is linear in each element, and vanishes when
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
are linearly dependent, thus satisfying the characterising properties.

One may define an inner product on
\Lambda^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d}
by defining it on simple wedges of
k
vectors as

\displaystyle\langle v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{k},w_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge w_{k}\rangle_{(\Lambda^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d})}\coloneq\det((\langle v_{i},w_{j}\rangle)_{1\leq i,j\leq k})

(A.2)

and extending linearly. One may verify that this inner product is a simple Euclidean inner product on components with respect to the basis
\mathcal{B}
. In
Sections

2
,
3
and
B
, we often use the Riesz representation theorem on the resulting inner product space to isometrically identify
(\Lambda^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}\simeq(\Lambda^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

These concepts may be used to define differential forms. For the purposes of defining the current associated to an
m
-dimensional submanifold, one usually considers compactly supported differential
m
-forms, defined as

\Omega_{0}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\coloneq C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d},(\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*})

equipped with the infinity norm

\displaystyle\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\lvert\omega(x)\rvert}_{(\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}}.

A differential
m
form on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a natural object that one can integrate over oriented
m
-dimensional manifolds, in a coordinate-independent manner.

A.2
Hausdorff measure and rectifiable sets

The general setting in which one defines currents is that of rectifiable sets with respect to Hausdorff measure. We briefly define these concepts here.

A.2.1
Hausdorff measure

The
m
-dimensional Hausdorff measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined as follows. First, one defines

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\delta}^{m}(S)\coloneq\inf\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\mathrm{diam}(U_{i})^{m}}\colon U_{i}\ \mathrm{open},\ S\subset\cup_{i=1}^{\infty}{U_{i}},\quad\mathrm{diam}(U_{i})<\delta\bigg\},

which is the effective size of the smallest cover by open sets with diameter bounded by
\delta
. One takes the limit as
\delta\to 0
, to obtain the outer measure

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{m}(S)\coloneq\lim_{\delta\to 0}{\mathcal{H}^{m}_{\delta}}(S),

which can be made into a measure by restricting to measurable sets with respect to
\mathcal{H}^{m}
. The resulting measure is
m
-dimensional Hausdorff measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. When
m=d
, this agrees with
\lambda^{d}
, the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. When
m<d
, this is a natural measure to use for integration and measuring ‘volume’ on
m
-dimensional subsets
S
embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, on which the Lebesgue measure would be zero. The Hausdorff measure may be used to measure sub-dimensional volume for very general subsets, and
\mathcal{H}^{m}(S)
agrees with the classical/intuitive notion of volume for submanifolds
S
of dimension
m
embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. One may also define the Hausdorff dimension of a set as

\displaystyle\mathrm{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}=\inf\{m\geq 0\colon\mathcal{H}^{m}(S)=0\},

which allows for a more general definition of the dimension of a subset
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and agrees with the usual topological definition for smooth manifolds.

A.2.2
Rectifiable sets

Rectifiable sets are a generalisation of smooth manifolds and a central object of study in geometric measure theory, from which one defines currents.

Definition A.3
.

A set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
m
-rectifiable (for
m\leq d
) if it has Hausdorff dimension
m
, and if there exists bounded
U_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
and Lipschitz functions
f_{i}:U_{i}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{m}(S-C)=0,\quad C\coloneq\cup_{i=1}^{\infty}{f_{i}(U_{i})}.

Unlike charts on smooth manifolds, the cover of the set
S
is not necessarily smooth, allowing the above definition to encompass piecewise smooth sets such as triangulated surfaces, as well as much rougher objects such as Cantor sets. Such
m
-rectifiable subsets, despite their general definition, can be shown to have well-defined tangent spaces
\mathcal{H}^{m}
-almost everywhere
Roussillon and Glaunès (2019)
, which allows one to generalise familiar notions of differential geometry on smooth manifolds in a measure-theoretic sense, such as integration of differential forms over rectifiable sets. This leads to the notion of (rectifiable) currents, as defined in
Section

2
.

A.3
Continuity of currents

The following lemma shows the continuity of the current associated to an oriented rectifiable set.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an oriented
m
-rectifiable set with associated current
[S]\in\Omega_{0}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}
defined by

[S](\omega)\coloneqq\int_{S}\bigl(\omega(x)\mid\tau_{S}(x)\bigr)\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(x),\qquad\omega\in\Omega_{0}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

where
\tau_{S}(x)=e_{1}(x)\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{m}(x)
is the wedge product of a positively oriented orthonormal basis of the tangent space at
x
. Then,

\bigl|[S](\omega)\bigr|\leq\|\omega\|_{\infty}\,\mathcal{H}^{m}(S),\qquad\omega\in\Omega_{0}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Proof.

By the standard estimate
\bigl|\int f\bigr|\leq\int|f|
(triangle inequality for integrals), we have

\bigl|[S](\omega)\bigr|=\left|\int_{S}\bigl(\omega(x)\mid\tau_{S}(x)\bigr)\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(x)\right|\leq\int_{S}\bigl|\bigl(\omega(x)\mid\tau_{S}(x)\bigr)\bigr|\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(x).

The pairing
\bigl(\omega(x)\mid\tau_{S}(x)\bigr)
is the duality pairing between
\omega(x)\in(\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}
and
\tau_{S}(x)\in\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By definition of the dual norm on
(\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}
,

\bigl|\bigl(\omega(x)\mid\tau_{S}(x)\bigr)\bigr|\leq|\omega(x)|_{(\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}}\,|\tau_{S}(x)|_{\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d}}.

Since
\{e_{1}(x),\ldots,e_{m}(x)\}
is an orthonormal basis of the tangent space, the inner product on
\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d}
(cf.
Eq.

A.2
) gives

\displaystyle|\tau_{S}(x)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle e_{1}(x)\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{m}(x),\;e_{1}(x)\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{m}(x)\rangle_{\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d}}

\displaystyle=\det\!\bigl(\langle e_{i}(x),e_{j}(x)\rangle\bigr)=\det(I_{m})=1.

Therefore,
|\tau_{S}(x)|_{\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d}}=1
, and the pointwise bound simplifies to

\bigl|\bigl(\omega(x)\mid\tau_{S}(x)\bigr)\bigr|\leq|\omega(x)|_{(\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}}.

Substituting the pointwise estimate into the integral,

\bigl|[S](\omega)\bigr|\leq\int_{S}|\omega(x)|_{(\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(x)\leq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\omega(x)|_{(\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}}\cdot\mathcal{H}^{m}(S)=\|\omega\|_{\infty}\,\mathcal{H}^{m}(S).\qed

Appendix B
Proof of
Lemma

3.1

We now prove
Lemma

3.1
. In the following, we fix an RKHS of forms
W
as in
Eq.

2.15
with spatial kernel
K_{p}
and
K_{s}=1
. The action of the embedded normal cycle
N(\mathcal{T})\in W^{*}
is defined in
Eq.

2.16
.

Given a triangulation
\mathcal{T}=\cup_{i=1}^{n}{T_{i}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
(so
d=3
), a decomposition of
N(\mathcal{T})
into distinct ‘planar’, ‘cylindrical’ and ‘spherical’ components is derived in
Roussillon and Glaunès (2019)
such that

\displaystyle N(\mathcal{T})=N(\mathcal{T})^{\mathrm{pln}}+N(\mathcal{T})^{\mathrm{cyl}}+N(\mathcal{T})^{\mathrm{sph}}\in W^{*},

(B.1)

and the three components are mutually orthogonal in
W^{*}
. Each component may be further decomposed
Roussillon and Glaunès (2019)
into distinct contributions from the triangles and edges of
\mathcal{T}
. We split the proof of
Lemma

3.1
into separate parts for each component, using their decompositions obtained in
Roussillon and Glaunès (2019)
.

B.1
Planar component

In
Roussillon and Glaunès (2019)
,
N(\mathcal{T})^{\mathrm{pln}}
is derived to be the following sum of currents on the normal bundle:

\displaystyle N(\mathcal{T})^{\mathrm{pln}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{T}}{[T_{i}\times[\pm n_{T_{i}}]]}.

In the above,
N_{T}
is the number of triangles of
\mathcal{T}
,
T_{i}
is the
i
th triangle and
n_{T_{i}}
a unit normal to the
i
th triangle face. The individual terms in this sum are of the form,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=[T\times\{\pm n_{T}\}]

for some triangle
T
. This component of the normal cycle has the action

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\omega)=\int_{T}{\omega_{x}(\tau_{x,n_{T}})\,d\mathcal{H}^{2}(x)}+\int_{T}{\omega_{x}(\tau_{x,-n_{T}})\,d\mathcal{H}^{2}(x)},\quad\omega\in W.

Recall that
\tau_{x,u}
is constructed as a wedge product of an orthonormal basis of the tangent space at
(x,u)
of the normal bundle. Indeed, one may show
Roussillon and Glaunès (2019)
that

\displaystyle\tau_{x,u}=\begin{pmatrix}e_{1}(x,u)\\
0\end{pmatrix}\wedge\begin{pmatrix}e_{2}(x,u)\\
0\end{pmatrix}

where
(e_{1}(x,u),e_{2}(x,u),u)
is a positively oriented orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Using the identity
\tau_{x,-u}=-\tau_{x,u}
, the bilinearity and antisymmetry of
\omega_{x}
as a function of its argument, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{T}{(\omega_{x}(\tau_{x,n_{T}})+\omega_{x}(\tau_{x,-n_{T}}))\,d\mathcal{H}^{2}(x)}

\displaystyle=\int_{T}{(\omega_{x}(\tau_{x,n_{T}})+\omega_{x}(-\tau_{x,+n_{T}}))\,d\mathcal{H}^{2}(x)}

\displaystyle=\int_{T}{(\omega_{x}(\tau_{x,n_{T}})-\omega_{x}(\tau_{x,n_{T}}))\,d\mathcal{H}^{2}(x)}=0,

for all
\omega\in W
. As a consequence,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=0\implies N(\mathcal{T})^{\mathrm{pln}}=0.

since
N(\mathcal{T})^{\mathrm{pln}}
is a sum of such terms. The fact that the planar component vanishes for
K_{s}=1
is observed in
Roussillon and Glaunès (2019)
.

B.3
Spherical component

Finally,
N(\mathcal{T})^{\mathrm{sph}}
is shown in
Roussillon and Glaunès (2019)
to be equivalent to the normal cycle of the (possibly empty) boundary curve of the triangulation, so
N(\mathcal{T})^{\mathrm{sph}}=N(\partial\mathcal{T})^{\mathrm{sph}}
,
which is further shown to decompose into

\displaystyle N(\mathcal{T})^{\mathrm{sph}}=N(\partial\mathcal{T})^{\mathrm{sph}}=\sum_{x_{k}\in\partial\mathcal{T}}{[\{x_{k}\}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}]}+\sum_{x_{k}\in\partial\mathcal{T}}\sum_{\{f_{i}\colon e_{k}\in\partial f_{i}\}}{[\{x_{k}\}\times S_{f_{i}/\lvert f_{i}\rvert}^{+}]}.

(B.7)

where
f_{i}
are vector edges of the boundary attached to boundary vertices
x_{k}
, and oriented outwards from the vertex. In
Eq.

B.7
, one denotes

\displaystyle S_{v}^{+}=\{u\in\mathbb{S}^{2}:u\cdot v\geq 0\},

representing oriented half-spheres. Therefore, a generic element of the spherical component of the normal cycle takes the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=[\{x\}\times S],\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

for a full or half sphere
S
. Such terms have the following action

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\omega)=\int_{S}{\omega_{x}(\tau_{x,u})\,d\mathcal{H}^{2}(u)}\quad\omega\in W

On the spherical component
\tau_{x,u}
has the following generic form
Roussillon and Glaunès (2019)

\displaystyle\tau_{x,u}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
e_{1}(x,u)\end{pmatrix}\wedge\begin{pmatrix}0\\
e_{2}(x,u)\end{pmatrix},

for appropriate
e_{1}(x,u),e_{2}(x,u)
, which also satisfy
(e_{1}(x,u),e_{2}(x,u),u)
is a positively oriented orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We may compute an explicit expression for such components by expressing
(e_{1}(x,u),e_{2}(x,u),u)
in the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Indeed for fixed
x,u
one may express (omitting coefficient dependence on
(x,u)
),

\displaystyle e_{1}(x,u)=\sum_{i=1}^{3}{a_{i}e_{i}},\quad e_{2}(x,u)=\sum_{i=1}^{3}{b_{i}e_{i}},\quad u=\sum_{i=1}^{3}{u_{i}e_{i}}.

By bilinearity and antisymmetry of the wedge product, this substitution in coordinates yields

\displaystyle\tau_{x,u}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
e_{1}(x,u)\end{pmatrix}\wedge\begin{pmatrix}0\\
e_{2}(x,u)\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}{a_{i}b_{j}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
e_{i}\end{pmatrix}\wedge\begin{pmatrix}0\\
e_{j}\end{pmatrix}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}{(a_{i}b_{j}-a_{j}b_{i})\begin{pmatrix}0\\
e_{i}\end{pmatrix}\wedge\begin{pmatrix}0\\
e_{j}\end{pmatrix}}

The cyclic coefficients
(a_{i}b_{j}-a_{j}b_{i})
are nothing but the components of the cross product
e_{1}(x,u)\times e_{2}(x,u)
. Since
(e_{1}(x,u),e_{2}(x,u),u)
is a positively oriented orthonormal basis, it implies
u=e_{1}(x,u)\times e_{2}(x,u)
. We may thus write

\displaystyle\tau_{x,u}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}{u_{i}\tilde{b}_{i}}

in terms of the components of
u
, where
\{\tilde{b}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{3}=\bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}0\\
e_{i}\end{pmatrix}\wedge\begin{pmatrix}0\\
e_{j}\end{pmatrix}\bigg\}_{{1\leq i<j\leq 3}}
are orthonormal. This yields

\displaystyle\omega_{x}(\tau_{x,u})=\sum_{j=1}^{3}{u_{j}\omega_{x}(\tilde{b}_{j})}

and thus by linearity of
\omega_{x}
for each
x
,

\displaystyle C(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{S}{\omega_{x}(\tau_{x,u})\,d\mathcal{H}^{2}(u)}=\sum_{j=1}^{3}{\bigg(\int_{S}u_{j}\,d\mathcal{H}^{2}(u))\bigg)\omega_{x}(\tilde{b}_{j})}

\displaystyle={\omega_{x}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{3}\bigg(\int_{S}u_{j}\,d\mathcal{H}^{2}(u)\bigg)\tilde{b}_{j}\bigg)}.

Thus, one may represent elements of the spherical component as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\delta_{x}\alpha,\qquad\alpha\coloneq\sum_{j=1}^{3}\bigg(\int_{S}u_{j}\,d\mathcal{H}^{2}(u)\bigg)\tilde{b}_{j}.

Evaluating the spherical unit normal integral (see
Lemma

B.2
) when
S
is a half-sphere or a full sphere, we see

\displaystyle\int_{S}{u\,d\mathcal{H}^{2}(u)}=\begin{cases}\pi v,&S=S_{v}^{+},\\
0,&S=\mathbb{S}^{2},\end{cases}

so that the full spherical terms vanish due to symmetry, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=[\{x\}\times S]=\begin{cases}\delta_{x}\alpha,\quad\alpha\coloneq\pi\sum_{j=1}^{3}v_{j}\tilde{b}_{j},&S=S_{v}^{+},\\
0,&S=\mathbb{S}^{2}.\end{cases}

Substituting the above expressions into the half-sphere terms of
Eq.

B.7
(where
v
are normalised edges) yields

N(\mathcal{T})^{\mathrm{sph}}=\pi\sum_{x_{k}\in\partial\mathcal{T}}\delta_{x_{k}}\sum_{\{f_{i}\colon x_{k}\in\partial f_{i}\}}\sum_{j=1}^{3}{\frac{f_{ij}}{\lvert f_{i}\rvert}}\tilde{b}_{j},

where
f_{ij}
is the
j
th component of
f_{i}
. Then,

\displaystyle N(\mathcal{T})^{\mathrm{sph}}=\pi\sum_{x_{k}\in\partial\mathcal{T}}\delta_{x_{k}}{\alpha_{k}},

where one defines vertex weights

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}\coloneq\sum_{\{f_{i}\colon x_{k}\in\partial f_{i}\}}\sum_{j=1}^{3}{\frac{f_{ij}}{\lvert f_{i}\rvert}}\tilde{b}_{j}\in\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}),

(B.8)

where we recall
f_{i}
are oriented outward from
x_{k}
.

B.4
Full decomposition and inner product

Combining the expressions derived so far gives the following Dirac delta decomposition of the normal cycle:

\displaystyle N(\mathcal{T})=N(\mathcal{T})^{\mathrm{cyl}}+N(\mathcal{T})^{\mathrm{sph}}=2\sum_{i=1}^{n_{e}}{\delta_{c_{i}}\gamma_{i}}+\pi\sum_{x_{k}\in\partial\mathcal{T}}\delta_{x_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}

for weights defined in
Eqs.

B.8
and
B.6
. The first term is the cylindrical component
N(\mathcal{T})^{\mathrm{cyl}}
, and the second term is the spherical component
N(\mathcal{T})^{\mathrm{sph}}
.

One may verify that this Dirac delta decomposition induces the inner product
Eq.

2.13
between two normal cycles
N(\mathcal{T}),N(\mathcal{T}^{\prime})
. Indeed, denoting
\alpha_{i},\gamma_{i}
weights of
N(\mathcal{T})
, and
\alpha_{i}^{\prime},\gamma_{i}^{\prime}
weights of
N(\mathcal{T}^{\prime})
, one observes

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\langle N(\mathcal{T}),N(\mathcal{T}^{\prime})\rangle_{W^{*}}=\langle N(\mathcal{T})^{\mathrm{cyl}},N(\mathcal{T}^{\prime})^{\mathrm{cyl}}\rangle_{W^{*}}+\langle N(\mathcal{T})^{\mathrm{sph}},N(\mathcal{T}^{\prime})^{\mathrm{sph}}\rangle_{W^{*}}\\
&=4\sum_{i=1}^{n_{e}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{e}^{\prime}}{\langle\delta_{c_{i}}\gamma_{i},\delta_{c_{j}^{\prime}}\gamma_{j}^{\prime}\rangle_{W^{*}}}+\pi^{2}\sum_{x_{k}\in\partial\mathcal{T}}\sum_{y_{l}\in\partial\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}{\langle\delta_{x_{k}}\alpha_{k},\delta_{y_{l}}\alpha_{l}^{\prime}\rangle_{W^{*}}}\\
&=4\sum_{i=1}^{n_{e}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{e}^{\prime}}{K_{p}(c_{i},c_{j}^{\prime})\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}^{\prime}\rangle_{\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})}}+\pi^{2}\sum_{x_{k}\in\partial\mathcal{T}}\sum_{y_{l}\in\partial\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}{K_{p}(x_{k},y_{l})\langle\alpha_{k},\alpha_{l}^{\prime}\rangle_{\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})}},\end{split}

(B.9)

where we use the orthogonality of spherical and cylindrical terms
Roussillon and Glaunès (2019)
in the first line, and the definition of RKHS inner product in the final line. Using the definition in
Eq.

A.2
of the inner product on
\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
, one observes for
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

\displaystyle\bigg\langle\begin{pmatrix}a\\
0\end{pmatrix}\wedge\begin{pmatrix}0\\
b\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}c\\
0\end{pmatrix}\wedge\begin{pmatrix}0\\
d\end{pmatrix}\bigg\rangle_{\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})}

\displaystyle=(a\cdot c)(b\cdot d),

\displaystyle\bigg\langle\begin{pmatrix}0\\
a\end{pmatrix}\wedge\begin{pmatrix}0\\
b\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0\\
c\end{pmatrix}\wedge\begin{pmatrix}0\\
d\end{pmatrix}\bigg\rangle_{\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})}

\displaystyle=(a\cdot c)(b\cdot d)-(b\cdot c)(a\cdot d).

Substituting for the inner product of weights in
Eq.

B.9
, this yields the following for the spherical weights (where we denote
x_{k},y_{l}
boundary vertices of
\mathcal{T},\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, respectively),

\displaystyle\langle\alpha_{k},\alpha_{l}^{\prime}\rangle_{\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})}

\displaystyle=\bigg\langle\sum_{\{f_{i}\colon x_{k}\in\partial f_{i}\}}\sum_{p=1}^{3}{\frac{f_{ip}}{\lvert f_{i}\rvert}}\tilde{b}_{p},\sum_{\{g_{j}\colon y_{l}\in\partial g_{j}\}}\sum_{q=1}^{3}{\frac{g_{jq}}{\lvert g_{j}\rvert}}\tilde{b}_{q}\bigg\rangle_{\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{f_{i}\colon x_{k}\in\partial f_{i}\}}\sum_{\{g_{j}\colon y_{l}\in\partial g_{j}\}}\sum_{r=1}^{3}{\frac{f_{ir}}{\lvert f_{i}\rvert}}{\frac{g_{jr}}{\lvert g_{j}\rvert}}\langle\tilde{b}_{r},\tilde{b}_{r}\rangle_{\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{f_{i}\colon x_{k}\in\partial f_{i}\}}\sum_{\{g_{j}\colon y_{l}\in\partial g_{j}\}}{\bigg\langle\frac{f_{i}}{\lvert f_{i}\rvert},\frac{g_{j}}{\lvert g_{j}\rvert}\bigg\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}

\displaystyle=\bigg\langle\sum_{\{f_{i}\colon x_{k}\in\partial f_{i}\}}{\frac{f_{i}}{\lvert f_{i}\rvert}},\sum_{\{g_{j}\colon y_{l}\in\partial g_{j}\}}{\frac{g_{j}}{\lvert g_{j}\rvert}}\bigg\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{3}},

where we use the orthonormality of
\tilde{b}_{j}\in\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
in the second line. Similarly, the following holds for cylindrical weights (where we denote
f_{i},g_{j}
edges in
\mathcal{T},\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, respectively),

\displaystyle\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}^{\prime}\rangle_{\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})}

\displaystyle=\bigg\langle\begin{pmatrix}f_{i}\\
0\end{pmatrix}\wedge{\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\underset{{\{T\colon f_{i}\in\partial T\}}}{\sum}n_{T,f_{i}}\end{pmatrix}},\begin{pmatrix}g_{j}\\
0\end{pmatrix}\wedge{\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\underset{\{T^{\prime}\colon g_{j}\in\partial T^{\prime}\}}{\sum}n_{T^{\prime},g_{j}}\end{pmatrix}}\bigg\rangle_{\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})}

\displaystyle=\langle f_{i},g_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg\langle\sum_{\{T\colon f_{i}\in\partial T\}}n_{T,f_{i}},\sum_{\{T^{\prime}\colon g_{j}\in\partial T^{\prime}\}}n_{T^{\prime},g_{j}}\bigg\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}.

B.5
Half-circle and hemi-sphere integral

We give two integral calculations for completeness.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
e
be an edge of a triangle
T
with vector edge
f=x_{2}-x_{1}
and unit normal
n_{T}
.
For the half-circle,

C=e\times S^{\perp,+}_{f,\,-f\times n_{T}}\;=\;\bigl\{\,u\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\colon u\cdot f=0,\;u\cdot(-f\times n_{T})\geq 0\,\bigr\},

one has

\int_{C}u\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}(u)\;=\;2\!\left(-\frac{f}{|f|}\times n_{T}\right).

Proof.

Denote the unit vector
\hat{f}={f}/{|f|}
.
Since
n_{T}\perp\hat{f}
, the pair
\{n_{T},\,\hat{f}\times n_{T}\}
is an
orthonormal basis of
f^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Parameterise the great circle
\mathbb{S}^{2}\cap f^{\perp}
by arc length
\theta
so

u(\theta)=\cos\theta\;n_{T}+\sin\theta\;(\hat{f}\times n_{T}),\qquad\theta\in[-\pi,\pi),\qquad d\mathcal{H}^{1}=d\theta.

The half-circle condition
u(\theta)\cdot(-f\times n_{T})\geq 0

reads
-|f|\sin\theta\geq 0
(using
n_{T}\cdot(f\times n_{T})=0
and
(\hat{f}\times n_{T})\cdot(f\times n_{T})=|f|
), which is equivalent to
\theta\in[-\pi,0]
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{C}u\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\pi}^{0}\Bigl(\cos\theta\;n_{T}+\sin\theta\;(\hat{f}\times n_{T})\Bigr)\,d\theta

\displaystyle=n_{T}\int_{-\pi}^{0}\cos\theta\,d\theta\;+\;(\hat{f}\times n_{T})\int_{-\pi}^{0}\sin\theta\,d\theta

\displaystyle=0\cdot n_{T}+(-2)(\hat{f}\times n_{T})

\displaystyle=2\!\left(-\frac{f}{|f|}\times n_{T}\right).\qed

Lemma B.2
.

Let
v\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
be a unit vector and

S^{+}_{v}=\{u\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\colon u\cdot v\geq 0\}

the closed hemisphere oriented by
v
. Then

\int_{S^{+}_{v}}u\,d\mathcal{H}^{2}(u)=\pi v.

Proof.

By rotational symmetry it suffices to take
v=e_{3}=(0,0,1)
.
In spherical coordinates,

u(\theta,\phi)=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\;\sin\theta\sin\phi,\;\cos\theta),\quad\theta\in[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}],\ \phi\in[0,2\pi),

with
d\mathcal{H}^{2}=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi
. The
x
and
y

components of
\int_{S^{+}_{e_{3}}}u\,d\mathcal{H}^{2}
vanish on integrating
out
\phi
, while the
z
component is

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi=\pi
.
Hence
\int_{S^{+}_{e_{3}}}u\,d\mathcal{H}^{2}=\pi e_{3}
, and the general case follows by rotation.
∎
