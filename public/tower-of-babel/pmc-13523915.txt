Overview of adult immunization in Finland: Successes, lessons learned and the way forward
Authors: Jade Pattyn, Sakshi Jindal, Greet Hendrickx, Angela Bechini, Sara Boccalini, Marco Del Riccio, Eric Noehrenberg, Pierre Van Damme, Paolo Bonanni, Hanna Nohynek, Mia Kontio, Asko Järvinen, Heini Salo, Arto A. Palmu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe exchange of knowledge and best practices is essential for improving adult vaccination strategies across the European region. Finland serves as a valuable example, with a centralized, publicly funded National Vaccination Program (NVP) supported by national evaluation for decision-making based on comprehensive population-based registers. These registers enable assessment of disease burden, facilitate the identification of high-risk groups, and enable evaluating vaccination coverage, impact, and safety during the implementation. The NVP allows a centralized tender with lower vaccine prices, centralized vaccine procurement, and uniform processes for vaccine administration by public health care. Despite these strengths, Finland faces challenges in adult vaccination similar to other EU countries, including budgetary constraints, as well as the need for improved implementation, especially for high-risk groups. The complex evaluation, decision, funding, and procurement process causes delays in vaccine introduction into the NVP. This review describes Finland’s adult vaccination system, from policy to implementation, drawing on a structured search of PubMed/MEDLINE, gray literature (2009–2024) and expert input. While there are areas for further improvement, Finland’s commitment to provide cost-effective, equitable, evidence-based vaccination programs with high coverage, including for at-risk subpopulations, ensures continued progress.

Introduction

Adult immunization faces distinct challenges compared to childhood vaccination programs. Unlike pediatric programs, which benefit from established funding, infrastructure and well-defined annual cohorts, adult immunization programs must engage with a large, highly heterogeneous population with diverse health needs and variable access to care. As a result, most European countries currently manage adult immunization through fragmented systems with low success,1 underscoring the importance of cross-border exchange of knowledge and best practices.

Finland serves as a valuable example, with a centralized, publicly funded National Vaccination Program (NVP) supported by national evaluation for decision-making based on comprehensive population-based registers, a framework uncommon in Europe. This review outlines Finland’s adult immunization system, from policy to implementation, highlighting strengths, limitations, and the way forward.

Adult immunization board

The Adult Immunization Board (AIB) is an independent, multidisciplinary group of European experts providing evidence-based guidance on adult immunization at European, national, and sub-national levels.2 The AIB holds a technical meeting and a country meeting annually3–5; this review summarizes the AIB country meeting held in Helsinki, Finland, on December 4–5, 2024, where experts from academia, healthcare, and public policy discussed Finland’s healthcare system, vaccine integration into the NVP, epidemiology, national registers, surveillance, modeling, economic evaluations, public vaccine attitudes, and targeted programs for specific population groups. The detailed presentations are available at http://www.adultimmunizationboard.org/.

Search strategy and selection criteria

References were identified through a structured search of PubMed/MEDLINE and gray literature (2009–November 2024) combining keywords including “Finland,” “vaccination,” “immunization,” and “adult,” supplemented by backward citation chaining and speaker and participant input. Inclusion focused on adult (≥18 y) vaccine-preventable disease and immunization in Finland.

Vaccination within the Finnish health and social care system

Finnish health care and social service reform

Finland, a Nordic welfare state of 5.6 million people, provides universal, largely tax-funded health care. Despite strong outcomes overall, geographical and socioeconomic disparities in access persist. A long-discussed reform was enacted in 2021 and implemented from January 2023, replacing over 300 municipalities with 21 well-being services counties (WBSCs) responsible for health and social services (the City of Helsinki and Åland do not form WBSC, but have similar responsibilities), and shifting healthcare financing from municipalities to the national state budget. Additional funding mechanisms, including occupational healthcare, voluntary health insurance, and out-of-pocket payments, remain in place.6,7 The reform aims to strengthen integration across health and social services and better prepare the system, especially, for an aging population.

The regulatory basis and organization of the vaccination program in Finland

The Communicable Diseases Act (1227/2016) and related decrees provide the legal basis for vaccination policy, with responsibilities divided among THL, the Ministry of Social Affairs and Health (MSAH), the Finnish Medicines Agency (Fimea), and the 21 WBSCs (Figure 1). Private and occupational health services also provide vaccinations outside the NVP, at the cost of the employer or individual. A comprehensive revision of the Act is underway to align with EU regulations, including on Health Technology Assessment (HTAs). The decision-making process of the national vaccination program

New vaccines are evaluated on public health benefit, individual and population-level safety, and cost-effectiveness before budget negotiation with the Ministry of Finance (Figure 2) ensuring evidence-based decisions and fiscal sustainability. Finland has shaped cost-effective vaccination programs for over 20 y,8–11 though newer, costlier adult vaccines are not always judged cost-effective for the general population, targeting high-risk groups is often a more affordable approach, though this often involves more complex implementation strategies. The national vaccination program (NVP)

Finland’s childhood NVP, established in the late 1950s through a network of well-baby clinics with near-universal coverage, is among the most comprehensive in the world. The adult program is more recent but continues to expand, with several vaccines added in recent years for risk-based populations (Table 1). Practical aspects of immunization initiatives in Finland

NVP vaccines are procured via an open EU tender and distributed through a wholesale distributor to WBSCs, which are responsible for implementation. Vaccinations are mainly delivered by public health nurses under physician supervision across health centers, hospitals, care facilities, occupational and military health services, asylum reception centers, and pharmacies. Annual mass campaigns against influenza and COVID-19 also enable co-administration of other vaccines.

Adult vaccination coverage data is publicly available for COVID-19, influenza and Td(ap) on the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare (THL) website. Coverage for these campaign-linked vaccines is high by European standards, but coverage outside campaigns and among high-risk or commercially vaccinated adults remains low (e.g., Td vaccination < 30% at age 25). Against the EU Council’s 75% target for influenza in older adults, Finland reported 59% coverage in persons ≥65 y in 2024/2025 (EU/EEA median: 47%).13 COVID-19 coverage reached 60.3% (60–69 y) and 69.7% (≥80 y), well above the EU/EEA medians of 8.7% and 8.5% respectively.14

A persistent implementation challenge illustrated by county-level experience (e.g., Vantaa and Kerava) is that vaccination remains an individual responsibility: reminders are limited to SMS/portal notices for influenza and COVID-19, built-in electronic record reminders are rare, and pre-NVR records may be incomplete. Self-paid vaccine costs can further contribute to unequal access.

National registers in healthcare and their role in vaccine evaluation and monitoring

All Finnish national registers contain a unique personal identity code, allowing deterministic linkage across registers. This infrastructure underpins disease burden studies, economic evaluations, transmission modeling, coverage and impact assessment, safety monitoring, and risk-group identification.

The national vaccination register (NVR)

Established after the H1N1 pandemic to investigate the post-pandemic narcolepsy signal, the NVR has covered nationwide vaccination records since 2009.15 It was designed to avoid double data entry and double collection, drawing directly from existing primary care registers (Avohilmo) and, since 2022, occupational and private health data. Each vaccination event is defined by personal identity code, vaccine identity, and date; lot-level traceability is maintained in line with EMA requirements, cross-checked against Fimea’s national lot-number lists.

The NVR supports real-time monitoring of coverage trends by population group and region, and by being linked to other health registers, it enables evaluation of vaccine benefit, safety, and the need for program updates.16 Personal-level vaccination data is accessed by citizens and clinicians via the national patient archive (Kanta) rather than the NVR directly.

Other national registers

Several additional registers, each linked via the personal identity code, support infectious disease and comorbidity surveillance, including disease notification systems, primary and secondary care registers,17 intensive care and social care registers, reimbursement and prescription registers, and cause-of-death, population, and cancer registries (Table 2). Future directions: data warehouses and the FinSurveillance project

Finland is undergoing a significant transformation in its registry systems, shifting from traditional static registries to dynamic data warehouses. This transition is guided by a core principle: more frequent data use improves data quality by surfacing errors early and simplifying their correction. It also enables greater flexibility in data access and fosters collaboration between repositories, including cross-border data exchange with other Nordic countries to build larger pools for subgroup analyses and rare adverse-event detection.

The “FinSurveillance” 2025–2028 project will further this ambition by building a comprehensive, resilient information management system for infectious disease surveillance. Key goals include ending manual data entry through automated processing and register linkage, AI-based harvesting of patient records, and a shift toward laboratory notifications and prescription databases (e.g., the Kanta Prescription Center) as primary data sources rather than individual identifiers. Participatory surveillance allowing citizens to self-report home test results is also being explored.

These advances carry important ethical and operational responsibilities. AI-based extraction of patient information requires robust data governance, explicit consent mechanisms, and full GDPR compliance. Finland’s existing data protection framework provides a strong foundation, but continued attention to ethical safeguards is essential to maintaining public trust and ensuring equitable data governance.

Epidemiology, disease burden, and surveillance of vaccine-preventable infections in adults

Discussion of the national surveillance of Herpes zoster (HZ), Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), and pneumococci highlighted registers’ role in estimating disease burden, profiling patient characteristics (such as risk groups), determining the NVP, and assessing the impact of infant vaccination on adult disease.

Herpes zoster (HZ)

An expert group’s 2025 assessment recommended including HZ vaccination for organ and stem cell transplant recipients, patients with hematological malignancies, and age-based vaccination at 75 y, contingent on reasonable pricing.27 This recommendation has been endorsed by the National Advisory Committee for Vaccination and the Ministry of Social Affairs and Health, but no funding decision has been made; price negotiations are ongoing. Register-based analysis (combining ICD-10/ICPC-2 codes and antiviral prescription data) found higher HZ incidence among elderly people living independently than among those in home or institutional care, likely reflecting under-recording when HZ is treated alongside other care.28

RSV

Finland’s national surveillance of RSV was discussed within a broader European context due to the involvement of the Finnish organizations Southwest Finland Wellbeing Services County (VarHa) and THL in the RESCEU (2017–2022) and PROMISE (2021–2024) projects.29 These public-private initiatives generated evidence on the burden of RSV and support preparatory efforts for vaccines and therapeutics.

In Finland, the burden of RSV is estimated from routine electronic health records and national registers, the National Infectious Disease Register (NIDR), Hilmo and Avohilmo. Data quality has significantly improved since 2017, due to enhanced testing practices for adults, driven by the COVID-19 pandemic’s increased monitoring of acute respiratory infections (ARIs), and real-time access to Hilmo.

Further refinement of inpatient and other hospital care definitions will be necessary. In addition, more investigation is needed to understand the effect of different types of healthcare contacts in primary care, including private healthcare and records of sick leaves. Additionally, cost-effectiveness assessments of the interventions are conducted when the optimal strategy is considered for inclusion into NVP.30 An official recommendation is expected by June 2027 by the vaccine-specific working group regarding RSV vaccination for adults.

Pneumococci

A nationwide, population-based follow-up study assessed long-term changes in invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) incidence, mortality, and serotype distribution in adults up to nine years after the infant PCV10 introduction in September 2010.31,32 Adult IPD cases were identified through the NIDR and linked with the population information system to conduct interrupted time-series analysis with age- and serotype-specific rates.

PCV10-serotype IPD declined significantly across all adult age groups. In young and working-age adults, indirect protection reversed the pre-PCV10 upward trend. Among older adults, the case fatality proportion fell; however, after an initial drop, overall IPD incidence resumed rising. Non-vaccine serotype (NVT) replacement, primarily serotypes 3, 19A, 22F, and 6C, was most pronounced in older adults, reducing net vaccine effectiveness. After five years, NVTs stabilized at approximately 90% of IPD cases; introduction of higher-valency vaccines (PCV15, PCV20, or PCV21) is expected to shift this equilibrium.

Pneumococcal disease remains a substantial burden in older adults, and continued serotype surveillance is essential to inform possible inclusion of higher-valency vaccines, on which no official decision has yet been made.33 The study also underscored the broader indirect effects of childhood vaccination on adult disease burden, a theme of growing interest across Europe for RSV and influenza as well.

Immunological surveillance

THL’s serological surveillance during the COVID-19 pandemic showed that by late 2021 immunity was primarily vaccine-derived, after which infection-induced and hybrid immunity became increasingly prevalent, particularly among younger adults, prompting recommendations that equate infection with a vaccine dose for booster planning.34–38 Broader lessons include that immunosenescence and immunocompromise reduce vaccine immunogenicity (supporting adjuvanted, high-dose, or more frequent boosting in older adults), that hybrid immunity can reduce booster need where natural infection contributes durable protection, and that serosurveillance can usefully guide decisions on new vaccine introduction (e.g., RSV) and booster timing (e.g., pertussis).

Modeling and economic evaluations

Modeling

Modeling supports NVP decisions on vaccine inclusion, procurement, and delivery, helping assess vaccine effectiveness, new target groups, and the interaction of program components to identify optimal strategies.

Economic evaluations

An economic evaluation based on national disease burden data is needed to support the decision-making process. A cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) is conducted when a new vaccine is considered for the NVP, using the Incremental Cost–Effectiveness Ratio (ICER, €/QALY) to compare interventions against a willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold. Finland has no explicit, pre-specified WTP threshold below which an intervention is automatically accepted and funded. Infant varicella, pneumococcal, and rotavirus vaccination programs were cost-effective at WTP values of €15,000–25,000 per QALY from a healthcare provider perspective, while childhood influenza and adolescent HPV vaccination were cost-saving.

Vaccines in the NVP are tax-funded and procured by MSAH through public EU tenders every 2–4 y, with THL preparing the tenders. CEA is also used to justify price premiums for more effective products, with the maximum acceptable price difference derived from the ICER at a given WTP threshold. Tender-based procurement keeps Finnish vaccine prices markedly lower than in insurance-based systems, approximately one-fifth of German and one-seventh of Swiss prices.10 Since many adult vaccines are purchased by healthcare providers and reimbursed by insurers, harmonizing procurement approaches across Europe could yield considerable savings and create opportunities for new vaccine introductions.

Finland’s lack of an explicit WTP threshold limits transparency, as budget constraints can influence decisions as much as epidemiological evidence. Narrow healthcare perspectives and short time horizons in CEAs may also underestimate long-term vaccine benefits, and these methodological limitations merit consideration when interpreting cost-effectiveness conclusions.

Finns’ relationship to vaccinations

Effective immunization requires public understanding and acceptance. Finns report higher vaccine confidence than the EU average, with confidence highest among those with more education, higher income, and better perceived health; regional variation exists but is not well explained.39,40 Confidence is also high among Finnish healthcare professionals, particularly public health nurses, though concern about adverse effects remains the main reason for hesitancy in recommending vaccines, especially to pregnant women.41

A modest decline in vaccine confidence has nonetheless been observed: independent surveys reported falling agreement that vaccines are safe and important between 2019 and 2022, even though overall coverage has remained high42–44 raising the question of whether these tools measure vaccine-specific confidence or broader trust in government. It is unclear what the causes of these declines in vaccine confidence are; however, certain events have influenced attitudes toward vaccines. For example, cases of narcolepsy following the H1N1 2009–2010 pandemic flu vaccination led to reduced confidence among Finnish teenagers and young adults.45

To better understand the social and behavioral drivers of vaccination, THL established the Cultural, Behavioral and Media Insights Center (CUBE) in January 2022, in line with WHO guidance to build behavioral and cultural insight (BCI) capacity. CUBE has examined vaccine hesitancy (VAX-TRUST) and HPV vaccine communication among boys, and is now studying hesitancy among fur farmers regarding avian influenza vaccination and broader vaccine-preventable cancers and communicable diseases (JA-SHIELD).

Conclusion and expert opinion

Finland’s adult immunization system demonstrates that centralized, evidence-based vaccination programs can achieve coverage notably higher than many EU peers, supported by population-based registers that inform disease‑burden estimation, cost-effectiveness analysis, coverage monitoring, and safety surveillance. This register-based model, together with Finland’s centralized procurement and tax-funded NVP, offers a template other European countries, many of which lack equivalent infrastructure, could adapt, particularly as Finland moves from static registers toward integrated data warehouses that will further strengthen surveillance and cross-country comparison.

However, governance design alone does not guarantee equitable coverage. Three lessons stand out. First, systematic infrastructure is necessary but not sufficient: implementation barriers, including the absence of systematic reminders and fragmented pre-NVR records, leave vaccination dependent on individual responsibility and must be explicitly addressed, for example, through reminders built into electronic health records, drawing on approaches used elsewhere in the Nordics. Second, evidence-based rigor must be balanced against implementation agility, as illustrated by the gap between avian influenza vaccine authorization (June 2024) and uptake (5% of the target group by November 2024) once initial urgency had waned. Challenges and opportunities discussed during the meeting.

Looking ahead, the global rise in the population aged 65 and over makes lifelong access to vaccination, as called for in the WHO Immunization Agenda 2030, increasingly urgent, even as Finland’s adult population requiring protection is far larger and more heterogeneous than its well-established pediatric cohort. Because newer adult vaccines are not always cost-effective for entire populations, risk-group targeting will remain central, alongside continued investigation of higher-valency pneumococcal vaccines and the pending introduction of herpes zoster and RSV vaccination. Other European countries can draw on Finland’s experience by: (1) establishing population-based registers linked to cost-effectiveness and burden-of-disease assessment; (2) adopting centralized procurement through public tenders; (3) embedding systematic vaccination reminders in electronic records; and (4) ensuring explicit accountability for coverage among occupational and at-risk populations while recognizing that these structural measures must be paired with deliberate cross-sector coordination to achieve equitable uptake.
