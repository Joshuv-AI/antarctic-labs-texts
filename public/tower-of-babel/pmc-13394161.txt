T1 Over Squared Proton Density Ratio to Characterize Multiple Sclerosis Lesions
Authors: Sarah J. Wright, Scotty G. McKay, Jean Ramos Bou, Fen Bao, Vivian B. Truong, Chenyang Li, Li Jiang, Anza B. Memon, E. Mark Haacke, Yulin Ge, Yongsheng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveDifferentiating remyelinated from demyelinated lesions in MS remains challenging without histological confirmation. This study introduces the T1‐to‐PD2 ratio (TPR) imaging approach and evaluates its ability to characterize MS lesions alongside other quantitative MRI (qMRI) metrics.MethodsThirty individuals with MS (mean age: 47.5 ± 11.5 years) underwent 3 T MRI using 3D gradient‐echo qMRI sequences to generate quantitative maps of T1, proton density (PD), T2*, quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM), magnetization transfer ratio (MTR), and MT saturation index (MTsat). TPR images were derived from B1‐corrected T1 and PD maps. White matter hyperintense lesions on T2‐weighted FLAIR were classified as hyper‐, hypo‐, or iso‐intense relative to normal‐appearing white matter on TPR. Lesion‐specific qMRI values were analyzed, and simulated signal intensities based on empirical T1 and PD values were used to illustrate TPR contrast. Statistical analyses included Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests and Spearman correlations (p < 0.05).ResultsWe found that hyperintense lesions on TPR exhibited significantly higher T1 (1566 ms), PD (0.96 a.u.), and T2* (92 ms) values but lower MTR (37.3%) and MTsat (1.57%) compared to hypointense lesions (1085 ms; 0.88 a.u.; 64 ms; 46%; 2.48%, respectively). QSM values varied across lesion types.InterpretationIn summary, TPR imaging visually differentiates MS lesions into hyper‐, hypo‐, and iso‐intense categories, whereas all lesions appear hyperintense on FLAIR. Hyperintense TPR lesions correspond to increased water and reduced myelin content, as confirmed by quantitative T1, PD, and MT metrics, consistent with demyelination. In contrast, hypointense lesions may reflect tissue changes associated with repair processes such as remyelination.

Introduction

MS is a chronic inflammatory demyelinating and neurodegenerative disease of the central nervous system [1]. Its pathology is characterized by lesion evolution, encompassing acute inflammation, demyelination, partial remyelination, and eventual neurodegeneration [1, 2, 3]. During the acute phase, blood–brain barrier disruption allows infiltration of macrophages and T‐cells, triggering early myelin breakdown and axonal injury [4]. Following this inflammatory phase, lesions may continue to demyelinate or initiate repair through remyelination [5, 6], while many ultimately transition to a late inactive stage characterized by axonal loss and gliosis [4]. This dynamic spectrum of lesion evolution highlights the importance of accurately characterizing lesion states for understanding disease mechanisms and guiding clinical management.

Comprehensive lesion characterization is fundamental to advancing our understanding of MS disease pathology and improving clinical outcomes. Histologically, MS lesions are classified into early active, chronic active, inactive, and remyelinated types, each reflecting different degrees of inflammation, tissue damage, and repair potential [7]. Early active lesions are characterized by prominent inflammation and ongoing demyelination. Chronic active lesions exhibit a slowly expanding inflammatory rim, persistent axonal injury, and a limited capacity for remyelination [8, 9]. In contrast, chronic inactive lesions lack active inflammation and are dominated by gliosis. Remyelinated lesions, including shadow plaques, represent a later stage of lesion evolution and are characterized by thin myelin sheath, reflecting structurally limited repair; shadow plaques, by definition, are remyelinated and consistently contain oliogodendrocytes [10, 11, 12].

In vivo, a subset of these histological lesion types can be identified using conventional MRI. Gadolinium‐based contrast agents are routinely used in clinical practice to detect active inflammatory lesions with blood–brain barrier disruption. Paramagnetic rim lesions visible on susceptibility‐based imaging are indicative of chronic active lesions and reflect iron accumulation in active microglia at the lesion edge [13, 14, 15, 16]. However, other lesion types, particularly remyelinated and chronic inactive lesions, remain difficult to distinguish using conventional MRI.

Conventional MRI sequences, including T1‐weighted (T1W) and T2‐weighted (T2W) fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences, are sensitive to lesion detection but lack specificity for underlying pathology, limiting their ability to distinguish demyelinated from remyelinated tissue. Quantitative MRI (qMRI) approaches offer more objective metrics sensitive to tissue microstructure. For example, T1 mapping reflects both water and myelin content, while proton density (PD) mapping reflects water content, both of which vary during myelination status [17, 18, 19, 20]. PD imaging has also been reported to provide a higher lesion contrast‐to‐noise ratio, improving the detection of small lesions [21]. Additional techniques, such as myelin water imaging [22], magnetization transfer imaging [23], multicompartment diffusion models [24], and direct myelin bilayer mapping using ultrashort T2 MRI [25], have also been explored as indirect or direct markers of myelin content and repair. Together, these qMRI methods represent promising non‐invasive strategies in assessing lesion composition and remyelination potential. However, reliably and reproducibly identifying lesion types using qMRI alone remains challenging. Post‐mortem histological studies have demonstrated that qMRI measures, including T1 relaxation time and magnetization transfer (MT) metrics correlate strongly with myelin content and axonal density and can distinguish remyelinated from demyelinated MS lesions [26, 27, 28]. Despite this, translating these findings to in vivo applications remains an unmet challenge.

To overcome these limitations, we propose to use the T1‐to‐PD2 ratio (TPR) image, which is derived from the transmitter and receiver B1 field corrected quantitative T1 and PD maps, as a complementary tool to existing qMRI maps for MS lesion characterization. The motivation for using the TPR image stems from the property that ratio images substantially reduce image inhomogeneity and enhance the visualization of subtle contrast differences. In addition, the ratio image reveals a distinct transition point from bright to dark signal intensity, rather than a linear variation with T1. This behavior is consistent with our initial observation that TPR differentiates MS lesions into bright, isointense, or dark appearances relative to white matter (WM). The objectives of this study are (1) to introduce the TPR imaging, and (2) to interpret the TPR image contrast by lesion‐based analyses using multiparametric qMRI maps and simulations.

Methods

TPR Image Contrast

TPR Minimizes Radiofrequency Inhomogeneities

Pathological changes in MS lesions may alter T1 and PD values of the sampled voxel when the water content and myelin content change along the process of demyelination and remyelination. Quantitative MRI studies have shown that tissue microstructure and water‐related tissue components jointly influence T1 relaxation and PD, resulting in relationships that are not adequately described by simple single‐parameter linear models [29, 30]. In addition, these subtle image contrasts may be difficult to appreciate in a 3D gradient echo (GRE) acquisition, where signal variations introduced by transmitter radiofrequency inhomogeneities (B1 +) and receiver coil sensitivity (B1 −) can obscure the visualization of subtle image contrast between tissues. The signal intensity of a GRE sequence can be written as: (1) S=PD·c·sink·θ·1−e−TR/T11−cosk·θ·e−TR/T1·e−TE/T2* where c is the receiver coil bias or B1 −, k is the transmitter radiofrequency inhomogeneity factor or B1 +; flip angle (FA or θ), repetition time (TR) and echo time (TE) are the given imaging parameters; and PD, T1 and T2* are the MR properties of the voxel. With two or more FAs, the T1 and PD values can be quantitatively estimated by so‐called variable‐flip‐angle (VFA) methods [31, 32]. For a short TE acquisition, the T2* term is negligible in VFA T1 mapping. In addition, pre‐scan normalization is typically used to correct the receiver coil bias c during data acquisition. Therefore, the major obstacle in VFA T1 mapping is the spatially varied transmitter inhomogeneity or k in Equation (1). For a TR much less than the tissue T1, the apparent PD (PDapp) and apparent T1 (T1app) can be estimated by first‐order approximation as previously described [31]: (2) PDapp≅k·PD (3) T1app≅k2·T1

Therefore, the apparent TPR image significantly cancels the k effects by taking the ratio of the two even without B1 + corrections: (4) TPRapp=T1appPDapp2≅T1PD2

When the B1 + corrected T1 and PD maps are available, either by a separate B1 + mapping scan or by fitting‐based B1 + correction [31], the final TPR is therefore free of B1 + variations: (5) TPR=T1PD2

Simulation

To illustrate the B1 + cancellation effect on TPR images, the signal intensities as a function of the empirical B₁+ factor range (k = 0.6 ~ 1.3) for the two FAs of 7° and 30° used for the T1 mapping are plotted for WM, gray matter (GM), and three categories of MS lesions with different T1 values. The simulation used empirical T1 values from the in vivo data described in Section 2.2. These T1 values were 750 ms for WM, 1300 ms for GM, 1600 ms for MS lesions that appeared as hyperintense (denoted as hyper) to adjacent WM on the final TPR image, 950 ms for MS lesions hypointense (hypo) to adjacent WM on the final TPR image, and 1150 ms for MS lesions isointense (iso) to WM on the final TPR image. Since the PD map is semi‐quantitative, instead of using empirical in vivo values in the simulations, PD values for each tissue type were derived using an established physiological model as R 1 = a × β + b, where R 1 = 1/T1, β = 1/PD, a = 1.3, and b = −1.08 [33, 34, 35]. As shown in Figure 1A,B, the signal intensity varied significantly as a function of k on the GRE magnitude images for the two FAs. The effect of the B1 + minimized significantly on TPRapp by taking the ratio of the T1app and squared PDapp, (Equations (2), (3), (4)) as shown in Figure 1C. Finally, the B1 + corrected TPR eliminated the B1 + variations and enhanced the subtle contrast differences among WM, GM, and MS lesions (Figure 1D).

Human Subject Study

Participants

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Wayne State University. Written informed consent was obtained from each subject prior to the scan.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) diagnosis with MS by a neurologist according to the McDonald 2017 diagnostic criteria [36], and (ii) age 18 years or older. Exclusion criteria included presence of other neurological disorders, contraindications to MRI, or pregnancy. Clinical data were recorded including disease duration, and the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS). Disease modifying treatments were not recorded as the present study aims for a lesion‐based analysis.

MRI Data Acquisition

Each subject was scanned on the 3 T MRI (Cima.X, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany), with a 64‐channel head/neck coil. The imaging protocol included a 3D FLAIR sequence for lesion delineation, and a set of 3D GRE sequences for computing the qMRI maps. FLAIR was acquired with a 1 mm isotropic spatial resolution, an inversion time of 1650 ms, a TR of 4800 ms, a TE of 438 ms, and a scan time of 4.5 min for 176 sagittal slices covering the whole head. There were three 3D GRE scans with the following identical parameters: TR of 30 ms, pixel bandwidth of 150 Hz/pixel, slice thickness of 2.0 mm, pixel spacing of 0.67 × 0.67 mm2, given by a field of view of 256 mm × 192 mm and a matrix size of 384 × 288, and scan time of 4 min for 72 axial slices covering the whole brain. The first two GRE scans were using three TEs of 6.5 ms, 15 ms, and 23.5 ms, and FAs of 7o and 30o without MT preparation. The third GRE scan was acquired using a single echo of 6.5 ms, an FA of 7o, and an off‐resonance MT preparation pulse with a duration of 10 ms, a nominal FA of 500o, and a frequency offset of 1200 Hz.

MRI Data Processing

A custom MATLAB (MATHWORKS, version R2017b, Natick, Massachusetts, USA) pipeline was used to automatically compute qMRI maps of T1, PD, T2*, quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM), MT ratio (MTR), and MT saturation index (MTsat). Image co‐registration was performed in the pipeline using SPM12 to align the FLAIR into the axial GRE and to minimize potential motion‐related mismatch between scans [37]. An established method referred to as strategically acquired gradient echo (STAGE) was used to compute the qMRI maps of T1, PD, T2*, and QSM from the first two GRE scans without MT [31, 38, 39]. Briefly, the two first‐echo data were used for T1 and PD mapping with transmitter (B1 +) and receiver (B1 −) radiofrequency inhomogeneity corrections [31], and all the echoes of the two scans were used for the reconstruction of T2* and QSM [38, 40]. The STAGE pipeline also generated susceptibility‐weighted imaging (SWI) and simulated images for double inversion recovery of white matter (sDIR_WM) and gray matter (sDIR_GM) [39]. Then, the first echo of the two 7o GRE scans with (MTon) and without (MToff) MT were used to compute the MTR as MTR = (MToff − MTon)/MToff. The MTsat was calculated using the first echoes of all three GRE scans as described [41]. Finally, the TPR image was computed as Equation (5) using the B1‐corrected T1 and PD maps.

Lesion Analysis

Regions of interest (ROIs) of MS lesions were manually delineated using a two‐stage approach by three raters independently after agreement on training of the delineation criteria (S.J.W., S.G.M., and F.B.; with 2, 3, and 15 years of experience in MS lesion analysis). In the first stage, WM hyperintensity lesions were identified on FLAIR images. As the purpose of this study was to interpret the different lesion appearances on TPR by multiparametric qMRI, large periventricular diffuse lesions were excluded, and lesions were isolated based on their appearance on TPR. Cavitating lesions characterized by well‐defined central black hole with a bright peripheral on FLAIR images were also excluded. Therefore, in the second stage, these preliminary lesional ROIs were refined by visually comparing FLAIR and TPR images side by side. The refinement process focused on identifying distinct hypointense and hyperintense cores visible on TPR and adjusting the ROIs to match lesion boundaries. Lesions that appeared on FLAIR but showed no contrast to WM on TPR were classified as isointense, with those appearing bright on TPR denoted as hyperintense, and those appearing slightly darker than surrounding WM denoted as hypointense. Additionally, reference ROIs were traced in the corpus callosum, representing WM, and in the caudate nucleus, representing GM. Because PD maps are semi‐quantitative and vary across scans and scanners, PD values were normalized on a per‐subject basis to the mean PD within the WM reference ROI, with WM scaled to a reference value of 0.70 [31]. As a result, normalized PD values reflect relative proton density and may exceed unity in tissues with higher water content than WM. This is because the in vivo GRE data acquired with the two flip angles are not optimal for mapping the cerebrospinal fluid regions which require a relatively small flip angle to quantify. Finally, the mean values of each ROI on each qMRI map of T1, PD, T2*, QSM, MTR, and MTsat were computed and tabulated for further analysis.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses and visualizations were conducted using an in‐house MATLAB script. Inter‐rater reliability for lesion type classification was assessed using two complementary metrics: the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and Fleiss' Kappa. ICC was calculated using a two‐way random‐effects model with absolute agreement (ICC (1, 2)), which measures consistency across raters when ratings are treated as ordinal values [42]. Fleiss' Kappa was computed to evaluate categorical agreement beyond chance among multiple raters. Interpretation threshold followed established guidelines. ICC values above 0.75 indicate good agreement, and values above 0.90 indicate excellent agreement. For Fleiss' Kappa, values between 0.61 and 0.80 represent substantial agreement, and values above 0.81 indicate almost perfect agreement [43, 44]. For each lesion and tissue type, in addition to TPR, the mean and standard deviation (SD) were calculated for the six qMRI parameters: T1, PD, QSM, T2*, MTR, and MTsat. Pairwise comparisons between lesion groups were performed using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests. Scatter plots of hyperintense and hypointense lesions were generated to examine relationships between lesion‐level metrics, including T1 versus PD, TPR versus T1, and TPR versus PD. A first‐order polynomial (linear) regression was applied to illustrate the overall relationship between variables. Of note, the above analyses are lesion‐based analyses to provide a practical first step to link TPR appearance with qMRI metrics. In contrast, patient‐level correlations were only examined as exploratory results using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient between the numbers of hyperintense and hypointense lesions and clinical variables such as disease duration and EDSS. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

There were 30 patients with MS enrolled in the human subject study. Demographics and clinical details of the study cohort are summarized in Table 1. Representative images derived from the three GRE scans illustrating various lesion appearances are shown in Figure 3. Across the 30 participants, a total of 886 lesions were manually delineated. Among these lesions, 327 (37%) were classified as hyperintense, 29 (3%) as hypointense, and 530 (60%) as isointense on TPR. Notably, hypointense lesions were identified in 10 of the 30 patients, whereas hyperintense and isointense lesions were observed in all participants. The ICC for absolute agreement among the three raters was 0.887, indicating good agreement. Fleiss' Kappa was 0.875, corresponding to almost perfect agreement beyond chance.

Discussion

This study introduces the T1‐to‐PD2 ratio or TPR as a novel qMRI metric for differentiating MS lesions based on their appearance and underlying tissue properties. Conceptually, TPR is related to previously proposed ratio‐based approaches such as the T1W/T2W or T1W/FLAIR ratio images [45, 46]; however, unlike ratios derived from conventional weighted images, TPR is computed from quantitative T1 and semi‐quantitative PD maps obtained using the STAGE acquisition framework. As a result, TPR provides contrast based on physically interpretable tissue properties rather than sequence‐dependent signal weighting.

Using TPR, MS lesions can be visually classified into hyperintense, hypointense, and isointense groups, revealing distinct neural tissue characteristics that are not evident on conventional weighted images. In contrast, the T1W/T2W ratio images demonstrated a predominantly hyperintense appearance across all MS lesions, limiting visual differentiation between lesion subtypes [45]. Hyperintense and isointense lesions on the TPR images were most prevalent and appeared in all patients, while hypointense lesions were less common and observed in a subset. Quantitative analysis showed that hyperintense lesions had significantly higher T1, PD, and T2* values and significantly lower MTR and MTsat values compared to hypointense lesions, suggesting greater water content, reduced myelin content, and disrupted tissue integrity in hyperintense lesions. Simulation results further demonstrated that TPR contrast enhances lesion differentiation by amplifying subtle differences in T1 and PD while mitigating radiofrequency inhomogeneity effects. Together, these findings highlight the utility of TPR as a sensitive, non‐invasive metric for rapid MS lesion characterization.

Accurate identification of demyelination and remyelination is critical for managing MS, tracking clinical and radiographic disease progression, and evaluating treatment efficacy. Conventional MRI lacks specificity for tissue repair, often failing to distinguish chronic demyelination from remyelinated lesions [13, 47]. The ability to non‐invasively identify remyelination could aid therapeutic decision‐making for individuals with MS, particularly as remyelination‐promoting agents and repair strategies advance into clinical trials. TPR offers a practical solution by integrating biophysical parameters into a single metric, offering a tool complementary to qMRI metrics with the potential for longitudinal monitoring of lesion repair and guiding personalized treatment strategies.

Our results suggest that hyperintense lesions on TPR maps reflect prolonged T1, elevated PD, and reduced MTsat, consistent with increased water and reduced myelin content, characteristics of demyelination. Conversely, hypointense lesions exhibited shorter T1, lower PD, and less MTsat reduction, which may indicate remyelination or partial tissue recovery. The isointense lesions have intermediate water and myelin content levels, suggesting a possible mixed pattern of demyelination and remyelination [26]. These results align with the established knowledge verified through histology that remyelinated lesions have a T1 shorter than that of GM, while demyelinated lesions have a prolonged T1 higher than GM [26], and extend earlier findings on the utility of T1 mapping for assessing myelin integrity [48, 49]. By integrating T1 and PD measurements, TPR captures complementary aspects of tissue microstructure, including water content, myelin density, and potential iron deposition, as highlighted in multiparametric qMRI studies [50, 51]. While this interpretation is supported by histological evidence and ex vivo 7 T MRI work linking T1 mapping to myelin and axonal integrity, confirming these relationships solely in vivo remains challenging and warrants further validation. In addition, the PD maps used to create the TPR image have a slight T2* effect given the short echo time used on the first echoes of the GRE scans, which contributes to the TPR image contrast consistent with the established knowledge on the T1 and PD agreement [33]. The simulation demonstrates a turning point of the TPR signal curve along with the T1 increase, which distinguishes the appearance of MS lesions with different ranges of T1. The signal intensity simulation also confirms that the TPR images minimize the radiofrequency inhomogeneities to enhance the image contrast, although the TPR image contrast between the hypointense lesions and WM is small. We noticed the T1 values used for simulation were slightly lower than those in literature for 3T [31]. As we intended to interpret the TPR signal behavior observed on the in vivo data, we used the empirical T1 values from our in vivo data instead of those in the literature. The underestimated T1 values are likely caused by the overestimated B1 + factors by the fitting‐based B1 + correction methods [52].

In addition to TPR, other qMRI maps provide complementary insights into lesion pathology. Techniques such as QSM and T2* mapping are sensitive to iron deposition and paramagnetic rim lesions associated with chronic lesion activity [47, 51], and have been histologically validated as markers of chronic active lesions. The present study did not focus on paramagnetic rim lesions analysis, given the small cohort of MS patients involved. Subsequent studies may investigate the association between paramagnetic rim lesions and their appearance on TPR images. Moreover, recent MR‐histology studies have further demonstrated that hypo‐intense and iso‐intense lesions on QSM predominantly correspond to remyelinated plaques, suggesting QSM as a promising tool in MS lesion characterization in vivo [53]. However, QSM values did not show statistically significant differences between TPR‐defined lesion groups, and substantial heterogeneity was observed across lesions. This variability likely reflects the combined sensitivity of QSM to both iron‐ and myelin‐related susceptibility sources, as well as the limited sample size of the cohort, which may reduce sensitivity to detect group‐level differences. Nevertheless, future larger studies are warranted to further investigate the relationship between susceptibility‐based imaging and TPR‐derived lesion appearance.

Magnetization transfer imaging and myelin water imaging provide more specific estimates on myelin integrity [53, 54]. Quantitative MT metrics, such as pool size ratio (PSR), provide biophysical specificity to macromolecular content and are less affected by B0 and B1 inhomogeneities compared to conventional MTR [55]. MTsat, as a clinically feasible MT method, further improves upon MTR by correcting for T1 relaxation and radiofrequency field variations [41] and showing stronger correlations with clinical disability and lesion burden in MS [56]. Histological studies confirm that MT parameters, including PSR and MTR, are sensitive to both demyelination and remyelination [57]. In addition to these qMRI assessments, the T1W/T2W ratio given by the weighted images has been proposed as a potential proxy for myelin content with a reduced T1W/T2W ratio indicating more microstructural tissue damage and demyelination [45]. More recently, the T1W/FLAIR ratio has also been used as a non‐specific biomarker of myelin content and microstructural integrity since most of the MS imaging protocols have replaced the T2W with a 3D FLAIR [46]. Despite these advances, myelin‐specific qMRI metrics often require lengthy scans with a complicated modeling of the signals, and ratios of weighted images may suffer from field inhomogeneities. In contrast, the proposed TPR image integrates myelin and water‐related qMRI metrics of T1 and PD into a single metric distinguishing MS lesions into visually different groups.

Several factors should be considered when interpreting these findings. This study did not include postmortem imaging or histological validation, which limits the ability to directly link the observed TPR contrast to specific pathological processes such as demyelination or remyelination. In addition, instead of using advanced myelin‐specific imaging techniques, such as quantitative MT and myelin water fraction, the present study used a more clinically feasible MT technique, MTR and MTsat, to interpret myelin signal in MS lesions. Future studies may further investigate TPR image contrast with advanced myelin‐specific MRI in vivo as well as postmortem imaging for histological validation [20, 53, 58, 59, 60].

The sample size was modest, and although a relatively large number of lesions were identified, conclusions could still be biased by patient‐specific lesion distributions due to the small cohort size. The results therefore require validation in larger, more diverse cohorts. This study introduces TPR using lesion‐based analysis on qMRI maps to interpret the TPR image contrast. However, no correlation was found between lesion classification on TPR and disease duration or severity, likely due to the overall lower EDSS scores in the cohort and insufficient study power for patient‐based correlations. The current findings should be interpreted as lesion‐level associations rather than patient‐level inferences. Future studies with larger and more balanced datasets should investigate associations between TPR imaging findings and clinical assessments using mixed‐effects or other hierarchical models.

In addition, lesion classification on TPR relied on qualitative assessment by the raters, introducing potential subjectivity. A globally normalized PD map may be used in future studies to quantitatively assess the TPR images. Third, imaging was limited to a single time point in the present study, preventing evaluation of lesion evolution over time. Finally, the relatively small number of hypointense lesions in the current cohort reduced statistical power for lesion subgroup analyses. Future studies incorporating longitudinal imaging and larger datasets will be important to confirm these observations and further explore dynamic tissue changes in MS lesions over time.

Conclusion

In summary, TPR derived from the quantitative T1‐to‐PD2 ratio represents a promising non‐invasive metric for visually differentiating MS lesion subtypes. It provides clear visual contrast and quantitative insight into neural tissue properties by integrating water content and myelin density into a single interpretable measure that, if validated, can potentially be used as a neuroimaging biomarker for differentiating remyelination from demyelination. TPR complements existing qMRI maps and may simplify lesion characterization while retaining sensitivity to tissue repair. Future studies should validate TPR in larger, more diverse cohorts, across multiple MRI platforms, and using longitudinal designs to confirm its diagnostic and prognostic utility in MS.
