Plasma-induced flow of de-mineralised water is governed by the aqueous grounding configuration
Authors: Calum T. Ryan, Mariska Schalk, Anton A. Darhuber, Hanneke Gelderblom, Ana Sobota
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Plasma-induced liquid flow is a topic that is receiving accelerated interest in recent years as a possible method to control the distribution of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species through a liquid, thereby improving the efficiency of plasma-liquid interactions. Here we investigate the significance of electro-hydrodynamic forces for the interaction of a monopolar pulsed non-thermal helium atmospheric pressure plasma jet and de-mineralised water through a combination of experimental methods and scaling laws. Our study finds that the flow in electrically floating liquids exhibits behaviour characteristic of gas phase electro-hydrodynamic (EHD) forces being the dominant driving force. We come to this conclusion primarily through linking the flow velocity to gas phase ionisation. In line with this mechanism, we consistently observe that positive jets, which are known to cause stronger gas-phase ionisation, induce stronger liquid flows than negative jets. In electrically grounded liquids, this strong influence of the jet polarity was not observed, which suggests that introducing a grounded electrode into the liquid fundamentally modifies the main driving force of the flow, potentially via interfacial or bulk liquid EHD forces. The results of this study therefore clearly show the important role that the electrical grounding configuration plays in the mechanisms behind plasma-induced liquid flow.

Introduction

Non-thermal atmospheric pressure plasmas are nowadays used in a variety of applications1–4, including medicine, agriculture, material processing and chemical synthesis. In many of these applications, plasma will directly interact with a liquid5,6. Plasma-liquid interactions involve the production of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS). When RONS are deposited into aqueous solutions they produce plasma activated water or plasma treated water7,8. RONS have different desired distributions throughout the liquid depending on the application. Therefore, to improve the efficiency of plasma-liquid interactions it is important to understand and control the transport of RONS through a liquid phase9,10. One possible method of controlling RONS distribution is through the use of plasma-induced liquid flow (PILF)11. While a fundamental part of plasma-liquid interactions, very little is known about the physical mechanisms behind PILF. In recent years, the topic is receiving more and more attention within the field of plasma-liquid research12–18.

There are two flow regimes that have been previously identified within the context of plasma-liquid interactions12,15: the upwards regime (also sometimes called the vortex regime) and the downwards regime. The upwards regime involves axisymmetric vortices that are produced close to the plasma-liquid interaction point below the liquid surface, with upwards liquid flow directly below the plasma13,19,20. Both a visual aid and experimental vector heatmap of this flow regime are given in Fig. 1. These flows resemble those produced when a gas jet impinges on a liquid surface21,22, and so for a plasma, one expects similar driving mechanisms. The primary mechanism that drives these flows are shear stresses, but the presence of plasma makes these non-trivial. It has been previously observed that atmospheric pressure plasma jets (APPJ) induce stronger flows in the gas phase than gas jets of the same flow rate23,24. It is hypothesised that the flow increase is due to plasma-induced mechanisms such as electro-hydrodynamic forces25–27 (EHD), which is also sometimes given the name “ion wind”, and hence, additional shear stress. EHD forces are due to collisions between charged particles, accelerated by an electric field, and the surrounding neutral fluid molecules, exerting collisional momentum transfer and thereby inducing a flow. The normal force exerted by both gas and plasma jets also produce dimples at the liquid interface17, which will become relevant to the flow if the dimple depth is a length scale comparable to the induced flow (in this study, the effects of surface dimpling are negligible - see methods section). The exertion of these additional forces by plasmas, in comparison to gas jets, result in stronger flows in the liquid12. The vortices produced in the upwards regime are expected to improve mixing of RONS, and thereby cause a more homogeneous spreading28, making the upwards regime useful for applications such as the plasma activation of water.

The second flow regime that has been identified is called the downwards regime14,15. In this regime, surface flows move towards the plasma-liquid interaction point before being redirected down into the bulk of the solution. It is therefore expected that these flows would be useful for applications where an efficient transport of RONS across a liquid medium to an underlying layer is preferred, such as in plasma medicine10. The two main processes hypothesised to independently contribute to the downwards regime are surface tension gradient induced Marangoni flows16,29–31 and liquid phase EHD forces12,18. Increasingly, more attention is being brought to both of these directional regimes and how to qualitatively control switching between the two12,15. Less attention has been given to how one can quantitatively control the specific mechanisms behind PILF, to hence modify the intensity of the flow within either the upwards or downwards regimes. Modifying the intensity of the flow within either regime can lead to optimisation and further control over the distribution of RONS, and so in-depth investigations into specific PILF processes are required.

Here, we investigate the effect of modifying the applied voltage and grounding configuration of a plasma-liquid interaction on the PILF induced in de-mineralised water by a monopolar pulsed helium APPJ. The applied voltage is modified through varying several of the voltage parameters; the polarity, voltage amplitude, pulse width and frequency. Particle image velocimetry (PIV) is used to investigate how flow intensities can be modified by tuning the system parameters in the upwards regime. Submerged ground electrode current

The current travelling through the ground electrode submerged at the bottom of the container (and by definition through the DW bulk) is shown in Fig. 7b. During the voltage pulse, a current with the order of magnitude of 10 mA will travel through the DW. This current sharply rises shortly after the start of the voltage pulse, reaching a certain peak value at around 0.25 μs for the positive jet and at around 0.5 μs for the negative jet, after which it steadily decreases in magnitude. The time delay between the start of the applied voltage pulse and the ground current peak could due to the travelling time of the plasma bullet from the jet capillary to the liquid surface. The polarity of these currents match the polarity of the applied voltage, however once the voltage pulse ends and returns to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V = 0\text { kV}$$\end{document}, a sharp spike in opposing polarity current occurs before steadily decreasing to 0 mA over 1 μs. The polarity switch is most likely due to a return stroke of ionisation propagating out of the liquid that has been observed at the same time with respect to the voltage pulse41. The magnitude of the current peak produced by the positively pulsed jet is approximately double the magnitude of the current peak produced by the negatively pulsed jet.

Interpretation

Throughout the study, de-mineralised water was used, which has a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{M}$$\end{document} of approximately 3.6 μs. To assess whether liquid phase EHD can occur we can compare the characteristic interaction time of the plasma and the liquid to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{M}$$\end{document}39. The discharge occurs for approximately 0.2 μs at the start and end of the voltage pulse (see Fig. 9) giving a total \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{on}=0.4\,\,$$\end{document}μs for floating liquids. We see in Fig. 6 that in grounded liquids, lengthening w will drive an increase in P, and so in that case we can assume that there is discharge during the entire voltage pulse. Hence, for grounded liquids \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{on}=1\,\,$$\end{document}μs for the default case of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w = 1\,$$\end{document}μs. Based on these observations, we conclude that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{on}$$\end{document} is smaller than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{M}$$\end{document} in floating liquids and in grounded liquids with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w<3\,$$\end{document}μs, and we therefore assume that liquid phase EHD is negligible in these cases. Furthermore, we assume interfacial Marangoni stresses are negligible as well, because we used only DM water, which is a purified liquid in this study; hence, in our analysis we set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _{\gamma }=0$$\end{document} (see Theory section). Futhermore, since the flow rate of the feed gas was the same in all experiments, we conclude that any modifications to the gas phase flow speed are caused by forces exerted by the plasma.

In the grounded configuration, there is an overlap of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_{z,30s}$$\end{document} irrespective of the polarity of the jet in all experiments. The relation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_{M\, l}\propto |E_{\parallel l}|$$\end{document} in equation (42) shows that the flow is indeed unaffected by the polarity of the jet when Maxwell stresses are the dominant flow mechanism. The overlap of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_{z,30s}$$\end{document} values for both jets in the grounded configuration, combined with the observed transport of charge across the interface and into the bulk of the liquid in Fig. 7b, suggests that Maxwell stresses may contribute to the flow in the grounded configuration. In this scenario, moving the ground closer to the interaction point would increase the electric field strength in the system63, thereby increasing the strength of the different EHD forces, which should result in a faster flow. However, as shown in Fig. 7a, moving the ground electrode closer to the liquid surface reduces the intensity of the liquid flow.

A potential explanation for this flow reduction could come from the boundary layer of the liquid flow moving across the flat plate electrode overlapping with the PILF. Therefore, it is unlikely this flow velocity reduction when the ground electrode is brought closer comes purely from a dampening of the flow due to increased wall effects.

Effect of varying pulse width on plasma

The majority of experiments for different values of w only resulted in substantial changes to both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_{z,30s}$$\end{document} and P in the grounded configuration, where there was a linear trend between w and both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_{z,30s}$$\end{document} and P for higher w values in Figs. 5b and 6. Small w resulted in substantially smaller \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_{z,30s}$$\end{document} and P than the average values in the floating configuration. In the grounded configuration, small w also resulted in smaller \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_{z,30s}$$\end{document} and P values than would be expected from the linear trend seen with higher w. The observed similarities in the effect that reducing w has on both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_{z,30s}$$\end{document} and P further backs the idea that the flow is driven by effects related to the ionisation driven by the plasma, irrespective of the direction or domain the EHD force is present. At low w values there could be shorter time in which the plasma bullet can form; the reduction is observed at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w=0.75\,\,$$\end{document}μs, where the pulse width is approaching the total length of time over which the discharges at the start and end of the pulse occur in floating liquids, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{on}\approx 0.4\,\,$$\end{document}μs. With electrically grounded liquids however, the increase of P and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_{z,30s}$$\end{document} with increased w demonstrates that ionisation occurs during the entire voltage pulse, hence the variation with w. This could lead to the production of bulk liquid phase EHD if w is extended above \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{M}$$\end{document}, where the plasma source injecting current into the liquid creates a system similar to that of an EHD pump. The bulk liquid EHD force produced in this system would be directed in the opposite direction to that of the EHD force in the gas and at the interface. In Fig. 7b we found that moving the ground electrode closer to the liquid surface reduces the flow velocity, which could then strengthen this downward directed bulk liquid EHD force, opposing the forces that drive the upwards regime and reducing the flow velocity. Past experimental results lead us to indeed expect a redirection of current through the liquid bulk51, and we measure current of 10s of mA on the ground electrode in Fig. 7b that would have travelled through the bulk. It is therefore clear that the electrostatic and hydrodynamic processes during the interaction of a plasma and a grounded liquid are currently not understood, and that it would be useful to expand our understanding of how grounding a liquid modifies the plasma-liquid interaction. This greater understanding could lead us to elucidate the possible presence of bulk EHD forces even in DW, and how these bulk forces interact with the EHD forces in the gas and at the interface.

Conclusions

The effect of the applied voltage and grounding configuration on the flow of de-mineralised water induced by a monopolar pulsed plasma jet was investigated, in an attempt to identify the driving mechanisms of liquid flow during plasma-liquid interactions. This investigation was carried out through a combination of experimental methods and scaling analyses. Our experimental results lead us to believe the flows in electrically floating liquids are driven by gas phase electro-hydrodynamic forces, due to the connection of flow velocity to the gas phase ionisation. In electrically grounded liquids, we find that the behaviour of the plasma-driven flow is less clear, and may be driven by concurrent EHD forces in multiple domains of the system. The results of this study therefore show that the mechanisms driving the flow of de-mineralised water is dependent on the electrical grounding configuration of the liquid, where electrically floating liquids exhibit behaviour characteristic of gas-phase EHD dominated flow, but grounding the liquid substantially modifies the driving mechanism.

A schematic of the experimental setup used for the study is shown in Fig. 10. The plasma jet capillary was placed 10 mm above DW with a square open surface with sides of 75 mm, a depth of 56 mm, and was held in a PMMA container. 55 μm diameter polyamide tracer particles (obtained from LaVision) are homogeneously seeded into the DW by mixing with a magnetic stirrer for 5 min. The tracer particles are illuminated by a 1 mW red laser shining through light sheet optics, producing the image to be recorded by a Fastcam Mini AX100 CMOS camera at 60 fps. The square recording window had sides of 1024 px calibrated to 50 mm. The recordings took place for 30 s, and repeated 5 times for each data point. After each experiment, the used liquid was swapped for a new sample to prevent substantial changes to the physical properties of either the DW35 or the tracer particles64. Tracers were filtered out of the DW when being disposed of to prevent micro plastic pollution into the environment. When the solution was described as electrically grounded, a flat galvanised steel electrode with dimensions 65 mm × 65 mm × 2 mm was placed into the bottom of the solution and connected to grounded wiring, over which the current was measured with a Magnelab CT-F1.0-BNC current transformer (separate to the one used for the HV current). The ground electrode was otherwise removed from the setup when the solution was described as electrically floating. Throughout all experiments no surface dimple was observable with respect to the PIV recordings, and so it was assumed that the effect of dimpling on the liquid flow was negligible in the specific plasma-liquid setup used.

The open source MATLAB plugin PIVlab was used for data analysis65,66. A 32 px interrogation window with 50% overlap was used for cross correlation. Once the vector fields for each frame had been obtained, they were time averaged to reduce random noise.
