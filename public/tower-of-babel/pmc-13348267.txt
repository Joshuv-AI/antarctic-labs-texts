Efficacy of topical nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) in the treatment of diabetic macular edema (DME): a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Reem AlShimali, Lojain Jamal, Sara Khorsheed, Zaynab Maqwar, Aseel AlKandari, Alaa AlAli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo assess the efficacy of topical nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) in patients with Diabetic Macular Edema (DME).MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Electronic databases, including MEDLINE, EMBASE, PubMED, CINAHL, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), were comprehensively searched for relevant studies published by December 2023 using key words such as ‘diabetic macular edema’, ‘topical NSAIDS’, and ‘anti-VEGF’. All studies focusing on the treatment diabetic macular edema using NSAIDS versus anti-VEGF were considered, including interventional, observational, pilot, and randomized controlled trials, and the data from the selected studies were pooled for analysis. The primary outcome measured was best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), while secondary outcomes included central macular thickness (CMT), macular volume, and complications.ResultsSix studies, with a total sample size of 446 patients and 892 eyes, were included. The findings showed a statistically significant improvement in BCVA favoring the NSAIDs group (mean difference: 1.82, 95% CI: 0.12 to 3.53) with moderate heterogeneity (I^2 = 60%, P = 0.08). CMT showed a favorable trend towards the NSAIDs group with a mean difference of 51.84 μm (95% CI: -15.23 to 118.91), although this was not statistically significant, with high heterogeneity (I^2 = 91%, P < 0.0001). Macular volume analysis showed no statistically significant difference in macular volume between the groups (mean difference: 0.29, 95% CI: -0.36 to 0.95), with high heterogeneity observed (I^2 = 77%, P = 0.04).ConclusionThis study showed that topical NSAIDs, alone or combined with anti-VEGF therapy significantly improved BCVA; however, the reduction in CMT was not significant. Evidence for macular volume reduction remains insufficient.

Introduction

Background

Diabetes Mellitus (DM) is one of the most common chronic diseases worldwide and poses significant challenges to patient health. In 2021, the worldwide prevalence of diabetes in 20–79 years old adults was reported to be 10.5% (536.6 million people) and is expected to rise to 12.2% (783.2 million people) in 2045 [1].

In patients with uncontrolled diabetes, persistent hyperglycemia causes long-term vascular damage to several organs, resulting in macrovascular and microvascular complications. Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is the most common microvascular complication in patients with diabetes, with an estimated global prevalence of 191 million patients by 2030 [2].

DR is classified into two stages: non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR) and proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR). NPDR is the early stage of DR, in which key features include the formation of microaneurysms, intraretinal hemorrhages, hard exudates, cotton wool spots, venous beading, and intraretinal microvascular abnormalities (IRMAs). PDR is characterized by neovascularization, which can lead to various vision-threatening complications. In addition, patients may develop diabetic macular edema (DME) at any stage of DR [3].

DME is a common complication of diabetes and one of the leading causes of significant vision loss globally. Chronic hyperglycemia and retinal hypoxia stimulate the production of VEGF, resulting in vascular hyperpermeability with intra- and subretinal thickening, which results in the development of DME [4, 5].

Intravitreal anti-VEGF injections are considered the first-line treatment in the management of DME [5]. Some disadvantages of the use of such agents include the need for re-injection, short-term effects, and the risk of complications.

Inflammation also plays an important role in the pathogenesis of DME, resulting in the disruption of the blood-retinal barrier. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) act by inhibiting the enzyme cyclooxygenase (COX), which inhibits prostaglandin production. Thus, topical NSAIDs may be used clinically to treat DME because of their ability to penetrate intraocular tissues [5–7].

New evidence suggests that topical NSAIDs, either alone or in combination with other treatment options such as intravitreal anti-VEGF injections, may be beneficial in the management of DME [6, 8, 9].

Objective

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we aimed to compare the outcomes of using topical NSAIDs in patients with DME, both as stand-alone therapy and as an adjunct to other treatment modalities.

Methods

This systematic review was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) [10]. Studies pertaining to the treatment diabetic macular edema using NSAIDS versus anti-VEGF were reviewed and screened, with those matching the pre-determined eligibility criteria selected and further analyzed.

The primary outcome of this study was improvement in best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA). Secondary outcomes included central macular thickness (CMT), macular volume, and treatment complications.

Eligibility criteria

All clinical trials that compared the use of NSAIDs with other agents in patients with DME were included. Individuals of all ages, sexes, races, ethnicities, and comorbidity statuses were included. The exclusion criteria included studies that were not performed in humans, studies that were predominantly focused on other diabetic ocular pathologies, studies that mainly focused on the use of NSAIDs after cataract surgery, the use of intravitreal NSAIDs, and studies that were conducted on a cohort not representative of the general patient population. After conducting a literature search, 68 published studies were reviewed using this eligibility criteria, with 62 studies excluded, yielding only 6 studies qualifying for inclusion in this review.

Search strategy and selection of studies

The literature search was conducted using several medical databases, to search for relevant articles published on the topic. The search yielded 68 studies to review. The search strategy, performed in December 2023, included all studies published from inception to December 2023. Advanced search techniques available on the websites mentioned earlier, along with the use of synonyms for each of the keywords, were used to maximize the search results and improve accuracy. The search terms used were (‘topical NSAIDs’ OR’ topical non-steroidal anti-inflammatory’) AND (‘diabetic macular edema’ OR ‘diabetic macular oedema’). Only articles written in English were included in this search. Bibliographical lists of relevant articles were also reviewed.

Study selection and data extraction

Two independent researchers reviewed all studies and excluded articles using the pre-set inclusion and exclusion criteria. After thorough search using 5 databases, 68 articles were originally identified. With further examination of the studies using the criteria and removing duplicates, 46 studies were ineligible. Of the remaining 22 studies, 12 were further excluded due to failure of meeting the set criteria. Finally, the remaining 10 studies underwent a thorough review, with a total of 6 studies included for definitive inclusion as they met all pre-requisites set. Any discrepancies between the chosen studies were discussed between the two researchers, and if not resolved, the studies were sent to a third researcher to independently assess and decide if they should be included. The data from each study was then extracted and inputted into a defined Microsoft Excel spreadsheet.

Data synthesis

After collecting the data, the authors assessed whether pooling of the results was considered feasible based on the individual outcomes and if it was reported by more than two studies. The mean difference was used to report continuous data and the odds ratio was used for dichotomous data. Odds ratios were chosen to provide a consistent and easily interpretable effect measure across studies with different baseline risks, which was especially important given the heterogeneity of DME outcomes in this review. Statistical analysis was done by using ‘Review Manager’ and results was presented in forest plots with 95% confidence intervals. When a meta-analysis was not deemed suitable for an outcome, it was pooled qualitatively and presented either in text or tabular format, highlighting the characteristics of each included study.

Methodological quality and risk of bias assessment

The quality and risk of bias of all included articles was assessed using Cochrane’s collaborative tool for randomized controlled trials and the Newcastle Ottawa scale for non-randomized studies. The Cochrane Collaboration tool assesses several domains, including selection, performance, attrition, and reporting biases [11]. The Newcastle Ottawa uses a star-based grading system to assess the methodological quality of observational studies, based on selection, quality, and outcome, with a maximum score of nine stars [12].

Results

Literature search results

The search yielded 68 articles, which were screened by reviewing their titles and abstracts to exclude duplicates and irrelevant articles. The remaining articles were screened more thoroughly according to eligibility criteria. After applying all inclusion and exclusion criteria, six studies were selected for this review based on their overall quality and relevance. Description of studies

A summary of the baseline characteristics of the studies included in this systematic review is shown in the table of Fig. 2. The total sample size was 446 (892 eyes). Most of the studies had the same intervention performed for both eyes of the same patient, apart from one article, Evliyaoglu et al., where in each patient, one eye was treated with the intervention and one with the control [7]. Four of the studies were randomized controlled trials, one was a pilot study and one was a retrospective observational study. The intervention in these studies was the addition of topical NSAIDs, with control groups consisting of either placebo (Friedman et al.), intravitreal anti-VEGF monotherapy (Gabr et al., Nikkhah et al., Mohan et al.), or no intervention (Evliyoglu et al.). The follow up time was varied between the studies. Gabr et al. and Nikkhah et al. reported 6 month follow up outcomes [6, 8]. Evliyoglu et al. had a follow up time of 6 weeks [7]. Whereas, Friedman et al. and Mohan et al. had the longest follow up time at 12 months compared to Pinna et al. with the shortest follow up [9, 13, 14]. Of these studies, three were suitable for meta-analysis with comparable data allowing for further analysis.

Meta-analysis: primary and secondary outcomes

BCVA

The primary outcome of this study was the improvement in best-corrected visual acuity using ETDRS letters as the units. All data displayed in other units such as logMAR, was converted to ETDRS letters. A meta-analysis was performed with three studies and the results are displayed in a forest plot (Fig. 3). This meta-analysis was done using a fixed-effects model. The sample size for this meta-analysis was 245 patients, with 121 patients receiving topical NSAIDs either solely or in addition to the intervention and 124 patients receiving the control. The findings showed a statistically significant difference (P = 0.04) in the mean difference between the two groups, with the results favoring the NSAIDs group. The pooled mean difference was 1.82 with a 95% CI 0.12–3.53. There was moderate heterogeneity (I2:60%, p = 0.08) observed among the studies.

Macular thickness

A meta-analysis of the decrease in macular thickness was done on four studies, and the findings are shown in a forest plot (Fig. 4). A random-effects model was used for this meta-analysis. The total sample size was 245 patients, with 121 patients receiving NSAIDs and 124 receiving controls. There was no statistically significant difference in the mean differences between the group (P = 0.13). However, the results showed a favorable trend towards the use of NSAIDs, with a greater decrease in macular thickness noted in that group. High heterogeneity was noted among studies (I2 = 91%, p < 0.0001). The pooled analysis showed no statistically significant difference between the two groups (mean difference [MD] = 51.84 μm; 95% CI: −15.23 to 118.91; P = 0.13).

Macular volume

Two studies were included in this analysis. The findings of the meta-analysis of the decrease in macular volume are displayed in a forest plot in Fig. 5. A random-effects model was used for this meta-analysis. The meta-analysis included three studies with a total sample size of 175, 86 of which were in the NSAIDs group and 89 in the control group. The pooled analysis showed no statistically significant difference between the groups (mean difference [MD] = 0.29; 95% CI: −0.36 to 0.95; P = 0.38).The studies showed a high heterogeneity (I^2 = 77%, P = 0.04).

Complications

Studies examining the use of NSAIDs in the management of DME have presented varying perspectives on complications associated with their use. Within the included studies, the use of topical NSAIDS in DME generally showed to have a favorable safety profile.

When evaluating bromfenac as an adjunct to intravitreal ranibizumab, mild self-limiting events were reported across both groups with no statistically significant difference and no reported serious eye-threatening complications. Gabr et al. reported in the ranibizumab group two individuals (5.7%) reported self-limited ocular discomfort and three patients (8.6%) reported subconjunctival hemorrhage. Whereas ocular discomfort was observed in five patients (14.2%), and subconjunctival hemorrhage occurred in three patients (8.6%) in the ranibizumab plus bromfenac group [8].

Similarly, Friedman et al. trial with topical nepafenac 0.1% in eyes with noncentral DME reported blurred vision (5 patients in the control group and 4 in the intervention group), floaters (4 patients in the control group, 3 patients in the intervention group), and eye itching in 3 patients from the intervention group [13]. Whereas, Nikkhah et al. and Pinna et al. studies notably reported no side effects with the use of topical Ketorolac [6, 9]. Conversely, Evliyaoglu et al. and Mohan et al. did not explicitly report any complications related to NSAID use [7, 14].

Methodological quality and risk of bias assessment

The results of the bias assessment are displayed in Fig. 6, using Cochranes’ collaborative tool, showed that three studies were determined to have a low risk of bias across all domains, and one study, Evliyaoglu et al., demonstrating a high risk of bias in some aspects. Newcastle Ottawa scale was used for quality assessment, displayed in Fig. 7, showing only one study demonstrated a lower quality due to comparability and outcome limitations. Overall, the included evidence has generally low risk of bias and high methodological quality, with minor concerns from one study.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis reviewed the efficacy and safety of topical NSAID as an adjunct therapy in DME. Within the six studies, including 446 patients (892 eyes), topical NSAIDS were used in conjunction with intravitreal anti-VEGF, and compared with anti-VEGF monotherapy, placebo, or no therapy. Out of the six studies systematically reviewed, three studies contributed data suitable for quantitative synthesis to be included in the meta-analysis.

Outcome 1: BCVA

Primary analysis demonstrated the use of NSAID has a minor, but statistically significant, improvement of BCVA with a pooled mean difference of 1.82 (95% CI 0.12–3.52, p = 0.04). However, this effect is below the standard change deemed clinically meaningful, limiting its practical relevance. Therefore, even though this outcome indicates that the addition of a topical NSAID as part of a combined therapy may confer a small benefit when compared to standard monotherapy caution when interpreting the real-world impact of the treatment on BCVA is required.

The moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 60%) indicates variability across studies, reflecting differences in study design, NSAID selection, baseline characteristics, and other treatments that may have influenced the outcome. The diversity of interventions and outcomes across these studies underscores the complex nature of DME management. Although NSAIDs such as bromfenac, ketorolac, and nepafenac show promise in enhancing visual outcomes when combined with intravitreal therapies, further exploration, larger-scale studies, and individualized treatment approaches are warranted to establish their long-term efficacy and safety profiles.

When applied topically to the eye, NSAIDs penetrate ocular tissues and inhibit the activity of cyclooxygenase enzymes (COX-1 and COX-2), which are crucial in the synthesis of prostaglandins. By blocking prostaglandin production locally within the retina, NSAIDs mitigate inflammation and vascular permeability, two key factors implicated in DME pathogenesis. Additionally, NSAIDs may exert antiangiogenic effects by suppressing the production of VEGF, a potent inducer of abnormal angiogenesis observed in DME. This dual action on inflammation and angiogenesis underscores the potential of topical NSAIDs as adjunctive or standalone therapies in managing DME, offering a targeted approach with potentially fewer systemic side effects than oral NSAIDs [15].

Outcome 2: CMT

Secondary outcome analysis of pooled results, although demonstrating a reduction in CMT favoring the NSAID group, showed no statistical significance for CMT change. The overall pooled mean difference of 51.84 μm (95% CI − 15.23 to 118.91; p = 0.13) may propose that there may have been an anatomical benefit, although not to a standardized significance level. Furthermore, the high heterogeneity (I2 = 91%) limits confidence in this estimated pooled difference. These factors suggest that even though NSAIDS may have effect on CMT change, evidence is insufficient to confidently ascertain the therapy’s true impact.

The variability in outcomes most likely is reflective of the high variability of studies, with differences in protocols, baseline severity, therapy duration, and baseline retinal thickness. Although some studies demonstrated a trend favoring combination therapy due to greater CMT reduction, the findings are inconsistent and unreliable across the pooled dataset. In addition, the high heterogeneity and lack of statistical significance further emphasize the uncertainty of this therapy’s impact on CMT. Overall, this study failed to show any statistically significant advantage of combined therapy over monotherapy of only anti-VEGF.

Outcome 3: Macular volume

On analysis of the impact of topical NSAIDs on macular volume, pooled data showed no statistically significant effect. The pooled data demonstrated a mean difference of 0.29 mm³, 95% CI − 0.36 to 0.95, and a heterogeneity of I² = 77%. Due to the wide confidence interval, and high heterogeneity the conclusion is limited requiring modest interpretation. Despite the fact these findings may suggest there is some effect on macular volume, conclusive evidence is inconsistent, requiring further research for true benefit estimation.

The lack of consistent data regarding macular volume across the included studies limits the ability to ascertain a significant conclusion. While some studies suggest potential benefits of NSAIDs in achieving greater reductions in macular volume when combined with intravitreal therapies, the overall evidence is insufficient owing to the lack of comprehensive data across all studies. Further research is warranted to elucidate the impact of NSAIDs on three-dimensional structural changes in DME.

Outcome 4: Complications

The safety profile of topical NSAIDs in managing DME appears generally favourable, though reports on complications vary. Across all included studies, topical NSAIDs were well tolerated with mostly mild transient events reported. There were no serious complication reports secondary to topical NSAIDS; however, reporting was limited due to the variable relatively short follow-up timings. Mild ocular discomfort and subconjunctival haemorrhage in both the ranibizumab and ranibizumab plus bromfenac groups, with no serious complications such as endophthalmitis or corneal ulceration. Similarly, no adverse effects were observed with bromfenac use. However, some studies did not explicitly report complications, leaving some uncertainty about potential risks.

Overall, while some studies lacked explicit complication reports, others highlighted potential risks. The general safety profile of NSAIDs remains favourable, but individual patient factors, including ocular conditions, should be considered to mitigate complications. Although side effects associated with NSAID use are typically rare, some local irritant effects have been reported. These include conjunctival hyperemia, burning, stinging, and corneal anaesthesia. Some of the more serious complications associated with topical NSAID use include keratitis, indolent corneal ulceration, scleral and full-thickness corneal melt, and perforations [16, 17]. With that being said, the lack of consistent reporting of adverse event between the studies, and the limited sample size of the studies, definitive conclusions cannot be confidently made. Therefore, while the overall safety outlook is promising, clinicians should monitor patients closely, especially those with preexisting ocular conditions.

Several limitation should be considered when interpreting these findings. The overall evidence is constrained due to the small sample size, short study duration, and short follow-up periods. Substantial heterogeneity of the included studies was seen, especially in regards to study design, patient population and intervention strategies. Additionally, not all studies were randomized controlled trials (RCTs); several were retrospective observational studies, which increases the risk of bias. In addition, inconsistencies in accounted confounders and baseline characteristics, such as the type of DME, duration of diabetes, macular thickness, and patient selection, may have potentially influenced the overall results. Methodological limitations, by included both eyes of the same patient, may have created unit-of-analysis bias and overestimate precision. Limitations of the study could be extended further with future research addressing these confounding factors and study design inconsistencies.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the role of topical NSAIDs in management of diabetic macular edema (DME), either as a monotherapy or in combination with intravitreal therapies. Pooled results proved that the use of topical NSAIDs in combination with intravitreal anti-VEGF treatment resulted in a prominent increase in BCVA, although with limited clinical meaningfulness. Additionally, reductions in CMT was observed; however, the combination with NSAIDS did not have a statically significant change on CMT when compared to the group receiving only anti-VEGF. Furthermore, most studies lack evidence regarding macular volume reduction when topical NSAIDs are used alone or combined. Further standardized randomized controlled trials are needed to provide definitive conclusions.
