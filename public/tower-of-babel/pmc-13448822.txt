Predicting optimal CPAP pressure in obstructive sleep apnea among Chinese patients using respiratory endotype derived from polysomnography
Authors: Jichu Zhu, Xia Hu, Xiaoyue Wang, Wenjie Liu, Junjie Song, Ji Chen, Jingchun Luo, Chen Chen, Liang Sun, Cong Fu, Huan Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurately predicting optimal continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) using traditional PSG parameters remains challenging. Respiratory endotypes derived from PSG can reflect the pathophysiological mechanisms of diseases to a certain extent and are expected to provide a novel perspective for individualized pressure prediction. 114 Chinese adults with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) underwent two overnight in-laboratory PSG recordings. Optimal CPAP pressure was defined by standardized manual titration performed on the second night. The analysis incorporated three sets of variables: respiratory endotypes, anthropometric indicators, and PSG respiratory parameters. After initial correlation screening, significant variables were entered into a stepwise multiple linear regression model. The dataset was randomly split into training (80%) and validation (20%) sets to assess predictive performance. A total of 114 participants (15 mild, 38 moderate, 61 severe OSA) were included. The prediction model included loop gain (LG1), ventilation capacity (Vpassive, Vmin), BMI, apnea index (AI), mean respiratory event-related oxygen desaturation (RE-OD-Mean), longest apnea duration (Ap-Dur-Long), and respiratory-related arousal index (RR-ArI): CPAP = 0.708 + 1.804×LG1 + 0.023×Vpassive − 0.015×Vmin + 0.117×BMI + 0.003×AI − 0.074×RE-OD-Mean + 0.019×Ap-Dur-Long + 0.043×RR-ArI. The final model demonstrated acceptable predictive performance in the internal validation set (R² = 0.497; RMSE = 1.507 cmH₂O). Among the retained predictors, LG1 showed the largest effect size, with each 1-unit increase associated with an approximately 1.8 cmH₂O higher predicted CPAP pressure. Integrating respiratory endotypes with anthropometric indicators and PSG respiratory parameters provides a mechanism-based framework for CPAP pressure prediction. Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a common sleep-related breathing disorder characterized by recurrent upper-airway obstruction, intermittent hypoxemia, and sleep fragmentation1. Global estimates suggest that over nine hundred million adults are affected, highlighting its substantial public health impact2. Positive airway pressure (PAP) remains the first-line therapy and significantly improves daytime functioning, cardiovascular outcomes, and quality of life when appropriately titrated3,4. Optimal positive airway pressure is generally established via manual or auto-titration, with auto-titrating positive airway pressure (APAP) recommended only for selected adults with uncomplicated OSA and without significant cardiopulmonary or sleep comorbidities5. Beyond restrictions for specific individuals, the suitability of pressure obtained through auto-titration also depends on the accuracy of the machine’s inherent algorithm. Bench evaluations indicate that commercially available APAP systems differ markedly in their responsiveness to obstructive respiratory events and in pressure modulation, reflecting inherent algorithmic heterogeneity6. Additionally, transitioning patients stably adapted to APAP to fixed CPAP preserves therapeutic efficacy while reducing costs, underscoring that APAP’s long-term clinical advantages are not unequivocally established7. Therefore, whether there exists a more convenient method for obtaining CPAP pressure values has been a longstanding concern. Yet determining the optimal therapeutic pressure requires in-laboratory titration, a process that is resource-intensive, time-consuming, and often limited by laboratory capacity, contributing to delays in treatment initiation8.

To improve accessibility, multiple studies have attempted to predict CPAP pressure using demographic and conventional polysomnographic (PSG) respiratory measures—such as the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI), oxygen desaturation index (ODI), and minimum oxygen saturation (SpO₂-Min)9,10. However, a study validated the currently published prediction formulas and found that most of existing prediction formulas often exhibit weak explanatory power11. This limitation may reflect a fundamental mismatch between what event-based metrics quantify and what actually determines CPAP requirements.

Current evidence indicates that the pathophysiology of obstructive sleep apnea can be conceptualized within the PALM framework, in which pharyngeal airway collapsibility (P), arousal threshold (A), ventilatory control instability or loop gain (L), and upper-airway muscle responsiveness (M) jointly determine disease expression and treatment response12,13. A major methodological advance in this field has come from the work of Scott A. Sands and colleagues, who demonstrated that these traits can be quantified noninvasively from routine PSG using airflow-derived ventilatory patterns and associated oxygenation and arousal signals14. Despite these advances, the clinical application of endotype-derived metrics for predicting fixed CPAP titration pressure among the group of patients diagnosed with OSA remains limited.

The present study aims to examine whether incorporating respiratory endotypes alongside conventional PSG and anthropometric parameters can support the development of a more accurate CPAP pressure prediction model in adults with OSA. By integrating multiple physiological data domains within a unified modeling framework, this study seeks to characterise the contribution of these variables to prediction performance and provide a more robust empirical basis for pressure estimation in routine practice.

Method

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Huashan Hospital (approval number: 2021-811-X1). Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants prior to enrollment.

Participants

Patients diagnosed with untreated OSA were recruited from Huashan Hospital, Fudan University, between January 2022 and August 2025. The diagnosis of OSA was established by a sleep medicine specialist according to the criteria of the International Classification of Sleep Disorders, Third Edition15. All participants underwent two overnight PSG recordings.

Other inclusion criteria included age between 18 and 70 years; completion of standardized overnight PSG and then manual CPAP titration on a separate night; all enrolled patients underwent CPAP titration using a nasal mask interface; and PSG data of sufficient quality to enable respiratory endotype modeling.

Exclusion criteria included significant pulmonary diseases (e.g., chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or pulmonary fibrosis), neuromuscular disorders, other sleep disorders including rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder, narcolepsy or idiopathic hypersomnia, and comorbid insomnia (COMISA). Additionally, patients were excluded if the diagnostic in-laboratory PSG was performed under nonstandard head-of-bed elevation (i.e., obvious upper-body/head elevation documented during the study), because this could alter upper-airway obstruction and make the measured AHI less representative of the patient’s usual obstructive burden under standard flat-bed conditions.

Sample size estimation

From January 2022 to August 2025, a total of 114 patients who met the study criteria were enrolled. An a priori sample size estimation for the planned multiple regression analysis was performed using R (version 4.5.1). At the study design stage, 4 predictors were assumed based on previously published CPAP prediction equations11. With 4 predictors, a medium effect size (f² = 0.15), a significance level of 0.05, and a power of 0.80, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 85. The actual sample size (N = 114) therefore exceeds this requirement, ensuring adequate statistical power and permitting the allocation of a subset of data to a validation set.

Polysomnography

All PSG recordings were obtained using a standard clinical montage, including electroencephalography (EEG) with frontal, central, and occipital derivations, electrooculography (EOG), and submental electromyography (EMG) for sleep staging and arousal detection, as well as electrocardiography (ECG). Respiratory monitoring comprised a nasal pressure transducer and oronasal thermal sensor for airflow measurement, thoracic and abdominal respiratory inductance plethysmography for respiratory effort, and pulse oximetry for arterial oxygen saturation. Body position sensors and anterior tibialis EMG were additionally recorded to assess posture and limb movements. The recordings were scored in accordance with The AASM Manual for the Scoring of Sleep and Associated Events: Rules, Terminology and Technical Specifications, version 3 (AASM Scoring Manual 3). Respiratory events were annotated by technologists possessing more than 10 years of experience in scoring, followed by verification from a senior sleep physician certified as a Registered Polysomnographic Technologist (RPSGT). Apneas were scored as a ≥ 90% reduction in the oronasal thermal sensor signal from pre-event baseline lasting at least 10 s and were classified as obstructive, central, or mixed according to the presence or absence of respiratory effort on thoracoabdominal signals, following AASM criteria. Hypopneas were scored when the nasal pressure signal decreased by ≥ 30% from baseline for at least 10 s and was accompanied by either ≥ 3% oxygen desaturation or an arousal. The apnea index (AI) was calculated as the number of apneas per hour of sleep. The hypopnea index (HI) represented the hourly frequency of these events.

Oxygen desaturation indices included 3% Oxygen Desaturation Index (ODI-3) and 4% Oxygen Desaturation Index (ODI-4), defined as the counts of ≥ 3% and ≥ 4% desaturation drops per hour of sleep, respectively. Minimum oxygen saturation was recorded as the lowest value observed across the entire sleep time. Cumulative Time with SpO₂ < 90% (CT90) was defined as the cumulative duration, in seconds, during which SpO₂ remained below 90% during sleep. The mean oxygen desaturation associated with respiratory events (RE-OD-Mean) was defined as a conventional PSG-derived metric representing the mean magnitude of SpO₂ desaturation associated with all scored apneas and hypopneas. Sleep staging and arousal scoring were performed based on EEG, EOG, and submental EMG signals obtained from PSG, following AASM Version 3.0 criteria. Respiratory-related arousals were scored when an arousal was associated with an apnea or hypopnea, and the respiratory arousal index (RR-ArI) was defined as the number of such events per hour of sleep.

Manual CPAP titration

Manual CPAP titration was performed overnight under technician supervision in the sleep laboratory. The titration goal was to determine the lowest fixed pressure that adequately controlled obstructive respiratory events, including apneas, hypopneas, flow limitation/respiratory effort-related events, and snoring, while maintaining acceptable oxygenation and leak control16. The titration-recommended pressure used in this study was based on the clinical pressure determined during manual titration rather than on an automated algorithm. If a titration study did not provide a clinically interpretable fixed-pressure recommendation because of insufficient informative sleep conditions for reliable pressure confirmation, the patient was not retained for model development.

Respiratory endotype

This study utilized the automated endotype analysis platform using PSG (PUPBeta) to extract respiratory endotypes from the first overnight PSG recordings17,18. The tool is based on nasal pressure airflow signals and integrates manually scored apnea, hypopnea, and arousal events to reconstruct upper airway mechanics and ventilatory control characteristics through model-estimated ventilatory drive. PSG recordings were acquired using the Compumedics clinical polysomnography system. For endotype analysis, all physiological signals were exported in EDF/XML format as system-generated signals, without additional investigator-defined offline filtering before analysis. Respiratory endotype estimation was then performed using the default PUPBeta preprocessing pipeline, including amplitude calibration, quality control, and automatic exclusion of segments with excessive noise or artifacts according to default thresholds. Continuous nasal pressure signals were then segmented using a fixed window length of 7 min, with a sliding window step of 120 s, in accordance with the default PUPBeta implementation, to generate overlapping analysis windows. Each valid analysis window was analyzed independently to estimate respiratory endotypic traits. For each participant, trait estimates from all valid NREM windows were then summarized, and the median NREM value was used as the representative subject-level metric (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were presented as median (interquartile range) for continuous variables, given their non-normal distribution, and as number (percentage) for categorical variables. Missing values were limited to four PUP-derived airflow-related endotype variables (Vpassive, Vactive, Vmin, and Vcomp; 11 participants in total) and were handled using single imputation based on predictive mean matching. The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Group differences were examined using chi-square tests for categorical variables and Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous variables. For significant differences identified in the Kruskal-Wallis tests, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Dunn’s test.

Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to examine the associations between CPAP pressure and anthropometric, respiratory endotype and PSG parameters. Variables showing significant correlations were subsequently entered into a multiple linear regression model to identify independent predictors. A bidirectional stepwise selection procedure was applied using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) as the selection criterion. At each iteration, both forward inclusion and backward elimination were evaluated, and the final model was selected based on the lowest AIC value to balance model fit and parsimony.

The dataset was randomly divided into a training set (80%) and a validation set (20%) to assess model performance. Model fit was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R²), mean squared error (MSE, cmH₂O2), and root mean squared error (RMSE, cmH₂O). To further explore the contribution of respiratory endotypes, a reduced comparison model was additionally fitted using the non-endotype predictors retained in the full multivariable model. As a sensitivity analysis, LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation was performed to assess the robustness of variable selection and model performance from a penalized regression perspective. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1), and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The titration-recommended CPAP pressure rose progressively with disease severity (6.0 (5.5, 7.0) cmH₂O in mild, 7.0 (6.0, 8.0) cmH₂O in moderate, and 8.0 (7.0, 10.0) cmH₂O in severe OSA).

Anthropometric indicators showed a clear upward trend with increasing disease severity. Body Mass Index (BMI) rose significantly from mild to severe OSA (p < 0.05). Respiratory endotypes also differed significantly among the three groups. LG1 and ArThres were higher in patients with more severe OSA (p < 0.001), while the indicators related to ventilation volume including Vpassive, Vactive and Vmin declined gradually (p < 0.001). PSG respiratory parameters showed consistent severity-dependent patterns. AHI, AI, ODI, CT90 and RR-ArI increased markedly from mild to severe groups, whereas SpO₂-Min decreased (p < 0.001). Both mean and longest apnea durations were significantly prolonged in the severe group (p < 0.01).

Correlations between CPAP and anthropometric, endotype and PSG parameters

Spearman correlation analysis revealed significant associations between optimal CPAP pressure and the majority of variables (Fig. 2). Within the respiratory endotypes, CPAP was significantly positive correlated with LG1 (r = 0.47), LGn (r = 0.31) and ArThres (r = 0.41), and significantly negative correlated with Vpassive (r = -0.21) and Vmin (r = -0.54).

Among anthropometric indicators, CPAP was significantly positive correlated with all the physical measurement indicators including BMI (r = 0.45). Regarding PSG respiratory parameters, CPAP showed significant positive correlations with AHI (r = 0.63), AI (r = 0.54), ODI-3 (r = 0.64), RE-OD-Mean (r = 0.46), Longest apnea duration (Ap-Dur-Long) (r = 0.47), CT90 (r = 0.47) and RR-ArI (r = 0.57). The significant negative correlation between CPAP and SpO₂-Min (r = -0.47) was also observed.

Beyond correlations with titration-recommended CPAP, most endotypes demonstrated significant correlations with conventional PSG respiratory parameters. LG1, LGn and ArThres predominantly exhibited positive correlations with PSG metrics, whereas Vpassive, Vactive, Vmin and Vcomp were predominantly found to correlate negatively. In contrast, SpO₂-Min consistently showed inverse associations with endotypes compared with other PSG indices.

The final multiple linear regression model demonstrated a significant fit to the data (F(8, 82) = 16.72, p < 0.001), with an R² of 0.620 for the training set and an R² of 0.497 for the validation set. The mean squared error (MSE) and root mean squared error (RMSE) for the training set were 1.6 cmH₂O2 and 1.265 cmH₂O, respectively, while for the validation set, MSE was 2.272 cmH₂O2 and RMSE was 1.507 cmH₂O. In the final AIC-based stepwise regression model, 8 predictors were retained. Among them, RR-ArI, Vpassive, AI, Ap-Dur-Long, BMI, and LG1 had individually significant regression coefficients (p < 0.05), whereas Vmin and RE-OD-Mean were retained in the final model but did not reach statistical significance in the individual coefficient tests. The residual standard error for the model was 1.332 cmH₂O, with an adjusted R² of 0.583, indicating a reasonable fit of the model to the data. Multicollinearity among predictors retained in the final model was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF) and tolerance values. VIFs ranged from 1.36 to 4.83, with corresponding tolerance values of 0.21 to 0.73, indicating no evidence of severe multicollinearity in the final model. Prediction error was defined as equation-predicted pressure minus titration-recommended pressure, with positive values indicating overestimation and negative values indicating underestimation. Incremental predictive value of endotype variables

To further assess the contribution of respiratory endotypes, we fitted a reduced comparison model using the non-endotype predictors retained in the full multivariable model, namely BMI, AI, Ap-Dur-Long, and RR-ArI. This reduced model showed acceptable predictive performance in the validation set (R² = 0.480; RMSE = 1.525 cmH₂O), although performance was slightly lower than that of the full model including endotype variables (R² = 0.497; RMSE = 1.507 cmH₂O). These findings suggest that respiratory endotypes may provide additional predictive information beyond conventional anthropometric and PSG respiratory measures.

Sensitivity analysis

As a sensitivity analysis, LASSO regression was performed to assess the robustness of variable selection. Based on the optimal lambda (λ_min), 11 variables were retained. Several key predictors identified in the stepwise regression model, including LG1, Vpassive, Vmin, BMI, AI, Ap-Dur-Long, and RR-ArI, were also selected by LASSO. Four additional variables (AC, AHI, AHI-REM, and Hyp-Dur-Long) were uniquely selected by LASSO. Although the retained variables were not completely identical, the predictive performance of the LASSO model was very similar to that of the stepwise model (validation R²: 0.495 vs. 0.497; RMSE: 1.476 cmH₂O vs. 1.507 cmH₂O), providing supportive evidence that the overall model performance was broadly consistent across different variable selection approaches.

Discussion

The present study is the first to systematically incorporate respiratory endotypes into a unified CPAP pressure prediction model spanning mild, moderate, and severe OSA. By integrating endotype parameters with conventional PSG respiratory indices, we developed a model capable of estimating optimal CPAP pressure with improved explanatory performance. Specifically, respiratory endotypes enhanced model performance, yielding an R² of 0.62 in the training set. The consistency between stepwise regression and LASSO results supports the stability of variable selection; however, the decline in performance from the training set to the validation set still indicates that the model may retain a degree of optimism. These findings demonstrate that physiologically informed endotypes provide meaningful incremental information for CPAP pressure prediction.

Several previously published CPAP prediction formulas have reported moderate explanatory performance, with coefficients of determination typically ranging between 0.30 and 0.50. For example, a model developed in Korean patients using anthropometric measures combined with conventional PSG respiratory indices achieved explanatory performance up to R² = 0.4219, while a similar approach in Saudi patients reported R² = 0.3910. These studies were based on in-laboratory PSG data and included patients across the full spectrum of OSA severity, characteristics that are broadly comparable to the population composition of the present study. However, a recent validation review systematically evaluated more than a dozen CPAP prediction equations published over the past two decades and demonstrated heterogeneity in their reproducibility with manual titration pressure11. Notably, some equations—such as the one derived from Saudi patients—showed significant deviations upon external validation, whereas others, including the formula developed in Korean and others from additional populations, did not exhibit statistically significant differences. When these previously externally validated equations were applied to our clinical dataset, nevertheless, their explanatory power was uniformly attenuated, with all models yielding R² values below 0.30.

Such discrepancies are not unexpected. Many historical CPAP prediction formulas were developed under earlier respiratory event scoring frameworks that differ substantially from current AASM standards, thereby limiting their direct applicability to modern PSG datasets20. In addition, several equations were derived from patients enriched with moderate-to-severe OSA21, in which stronger correlations with anthropometric and desaturation-related metrics may inflate apparent model performance. When applied to more heterogeneous clinical populations under contemporary scoring rules, these models may lose considerable power. Collectively, these findings highlight the inherent limitations of traditional CPAP prediction approaches and help explain their reduced transportability to current routine clinical practice (Supplementary Information).

This study is the first to incorporate a comprehensive set of respiratory endotypes into a unified CPAP pressure prediction model, thereby extending prediction beyond a traditional event-based framework toward a more physiology-informed perspective. Respiratory endotypes may provide a more direct and mechanistically relevant characterization of ventilatory control instability and upper-airway mechanical properties. Among the endotypes retained in the final model, LG1 showed a positive association with optimal CPAP pressure, which may indicate that the propensity of the ventilatory control system to amplify disturbances is associated with increased pressure sensitivity and less stable breathing, thereby contributing to higher therapeutic pressure requirements. Meanwhile, Vpassive and Vmin were negatively associated with CPAP pressure, which may be consistent with greater intrinsic upper-airway collapsibility and lower baseline airflow under hypotonic conditions, thus indicating a higher mechanical load that must be overcome to maintain airway patency in the absence of neuromuscular compensation. Furthermore, the positive association between ArThres and CPAP pressure may suggest that patients with a higher ArThres are less susceptible to sleep disruption in response to increasing airway resistance22, which could reduce arousal-mediated termination of obstruction and consequently increase the pressure required to stabilize the airway. Complementary traits such as Vactive and Vcomp may reflect the effectiveness of pharyngeal dilator muscle recruitment during sleep and the capacity for neuromuscular stabilization when the airway is challenged. Collectively, these endotypes may correspond to key physiological determinants of therapeutic pressure, including ventilatory control instability, structural collapsibility and compensatory muscle activation, which are central mechanisms underlying airflow obstruction in OSA23. Nevertheless, the coefficients of individual endotypes should be interpreted cautiously within the context of correlated predictors and overall disease burden.

Traditional respiratory PSG event metrics, including AHI, AI, and HI, were initially considered as candidate predictors because they quantify disease severity and have been central to historical CPAP prediction equations24. Meanwhile, additional PSG-derived measures capturing the severity and consequences of obstructive events, specifically hypoxemia-related indices (ODI-3, ODI-4, SpO₂-Min, and CT90) were also included among the candidate variables. Hypoxic burden has been shown to relate more closely to ventilatory compromise and therapeutic pressure requirements than event counts alone25,26. When jointly evaluated with respiratory endotypes, a subset of these conventional PSG metrics remained independently associated with optimal CPAP pressure. AI was retained in the final model, suggesting that event-based characteristics still contribute to CPAP pressure prediction. Similarly, RR-ArI and Ap-Dur-Long were also retained in the final model, suggesting that sleep fragmentation and event duration provide more insights. RR-ArI likely reflect the sensitivity to sleep disruption and ventilatory instability, and Ap-Dur-Long could indicate sustained airflow limitation, contributing to mechanical and hypoxic stress. Given that both RR-ArI and ArThres are thought to reflect sensitivity to sleep disruption, we hypothesize that RR-ArI is more closely linked to ventilatory instability, as cortical arousals on which RR-ArI is based are more directly and sensitively captured by manually scored respiratory events. This may partly explain why RR-ArI, rather than ArThres, was retained in the final model. The discovery indicates that RR-ArI and Ap-Dur-Long capture downstream effects of airway instability that are not fully accounted for by baseline endotype traits.

A methodological strength of the present study is that the model incorporated patients with mild, moderate, and severe OSA, and demonstrated overall statistical significance. In addition, pressure determination was based on two-night PSG, including an attended titration night, providing pressure is real-world clinical decision-making.

Several limitations should be noted in the present study. Firstly, this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, and model performance was evaluated using only an internal validation set. The decline in performance from the training set to the validation set suggests a degree of model optimism and indicates that external validation is still needed. In addition, the final model was derived using stepwise regression, which may be unstable in relatively small datasets. Although the additional LASSO sensitivity analysis showed broadly similar predictive performance, the present findings should still be interpreted with caution. Generalizability may also be limited because all participants were Chinese patients and because PSG-based PUP endotype estimation depends on high-quality physiological recordings and may be influenced by signal quality, scoring quality, filtering settings, and substantial mouth breathing. Moreover, some predictors in the present model, particularly arousal-related variables and PUP-derived endotypes, may not be readily obtainable in routine Home Sleep Apnea Testing (HSAT). Most HSAT systems do not include EEG for arousal scoring, and although airflow may be available in some devices, the signal configuration and quality may not be sufficient for reliable PUP-based endotype estimation27. Sleep position during in-laboratory titration may not fully reflect patients’ habitual sleep posture at home. Because a greater proportion of supine sleep in the laboratory could increase upper-airway collapsibility and pressure requirement in some individuals, differences between laboratory and home sleep-position patterns should also be recognized as a potential source of variability when interpreting the recommended pressure. Finally, patients with COMISA were not included in the present study, which limits the applicability of the model to the broader OSA population. However, if hypnotic medication is routinely used at home and the same medication is also used during in-laboratory titration, the resulting pressure requirement may partly reflect the patient’s habitual physiological state and real-world treatment needs.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that the integration of respiratory endotypes into CPAP pressure prediction yields improved explanatory performance compared with conventional PSG approaches. By combining mechanistically interpretable physiological traits with PSG indices, the proposed model provides a robust estimation of optimal CPAP pressure across patients with mild, moderate, and severe OSA. These findings support a conceptual shift from purely event-based metrics toward a physiology-oriented framework for CPAP pressure determination. Although the model demonstrated reasonable predictive performance, further studies are needed to evaluate its impact on long-term treatment efficacy and clinical outcomes and to compare its effectiveness and cost-effectiveness with established approaches such as APAP and standard in-laboratory titration, and to confirm the robustness of this physiology-based framework in multicenter cohorts and more diverse populations, as well as to determine whether additional wake-dependent mechanisms, particularly in patients with COMISA, should be considered to further enhance its generalizability and clinical applicability.
