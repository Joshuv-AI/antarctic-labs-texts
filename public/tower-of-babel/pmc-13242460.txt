Cytosolic Ca2+ mediates ATG8i-autophagy activation under osmotic stress in Arabidopsis thaliana
Authors: Luis Castillo-Olamendi, Gladys Jiménez-Nopala, Salvador Barrera-Ortiz, Ausencio Galindo, Elizabeth Cordoba, Paul Rosas-Santiago, Patricia León, Helena Porta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Main conclusionOsmotic stress–induced Ca2⁺ accumulation promotes the formation of specific RFP-ATG8i labeled autophagosome near the hypocotyl-root transition zone, suggesting ATG8i-dependent autophagy or reticulophagy, as a mechanism to alleviate osmotic and endoplasmic reticulum stress in plants.AbstractAutophagy is a crucial and evolutionarily conserved process that breaks down and recycles damaged or unnecessary cytoplasmic components. This process is essential for plant growth and for responding to environmental stresses. In this study, we investigated how autophagy is regulated during osmotic stress in Arabidopsis thaliana, with a focus on the behavior of the RFP-tagged ATG8i protein and the potential role of cytosolic Ca2⁺ in this process.Osmotic stress was induced with sorbitol and Ca2+ accumulation was monitored with a Cameleon transgenic line. Ca2⁺ influx from the apoplast was disrupted using EGTA, and autophagy was analyzed in a transgenic line expressing RFP-ATG8i under its native promoter. We found that osmotic stress triggers Ca2⁺ accumulation, with a pronounced response in the hypocotyl-root transition zone and a weaker response at the root tip. Both osmotic stress and Ca2⁺ signaling promote the accumulation of RFP-ATG8i-labeled autophagosomes in predominantly in hypocotyl-root transition zone. Furthermore, analysis of GFP-HDEL/RFP-ATG8i doubled transgenic line revealed colocalization of RFP-ATG8i with the endoplasmic reticulum marker HDEL, suggesting that ATG8i participates in reticulophagy and may contributed to the ER turnover under stress conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00425-026-05045-4.

Introduction

Autophagy is a highly conserved degradation process in eukaryotic cells that helps break down and recycle dysfunctional cytoplasmic components, including proteins, protein complexes, nucleic acid aggregates, and even entire organelles. In animals, these materials are transported to the lysosome, while in yeast and plants, they are delivered to the vacuole for breakdown and reuse (Cao, et al. 2021).

The genes involved in autophagy, collectively known as ATG (AuTophaGy) genes, were first identified in Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Most ATG genes are conserved across animals and plants (Marshall and Vierstra 2018), indicating that the autophagy machinery functions similarly from yeast to higher eukaryotes (Yang and Bassham 2015). Studies of ATG gene-deficient mutants in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana (A. thaliana) have shown that ATG proteins are essential for proper development (Cao, et al. 2021).

In A. thaliana, the autophagy process generally involves five main stages: induction, nucleation, expansion, maturation, and fusion to improve clarity. Among ATG proteins, ATG8 is widely used to monitor autophagy because it is associated with the inner and outer membranes of the autophagosome during binding, elongation, and fusion with the tonoplast, as well as in forming the autophagic body (Marshall and Vierstra 2018; Lee and Lee 2016).

Several studies on stress responses have indicated that autophagy plays a crucial role in cellular adaptation to osmotic stress. For instance, Liu and coworkers (2009) demonstrated that ATG18a transcript levels increase markedly under osmotic stress, while RNAi-mediated suppression of ATG18a enhances plant sensitivity to this condition. Moreover, the link between autophagy and hydrotropic curvature was demonstrated by the observation that autophagy-deficient mutants (atg2, atg5, atg8b, atg8i, and atg9) failed to develop hydrotropic curvature in response to a water potential gradient induced by sorbitol medium. This finding emphasizes the essential role of autophagy in the plant’s response to osmotic stress (Jimenez-Nopala, et al. 2018).

Calcium is an essential element for plant growth and function. In its ion form, Ca2+, it acts as a key intracellular messenger. Ca2+ can be released from storage sites, including the cell wall/apoplast, the vacuole, and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). At the same time, the nucleus, as well as chloroplasts and mitochondria, can also generate intracellular Ca2+ signals (Stael, et al. 2012). Cytosolic free Ca2⁺ is a key signaling molecule in cellular responses (Xu, et al. 2022). However, because of its high affinity for negatively charged compounds, it can lead to the formation of insoluble calcium phosphate salts, which may impact cellular health. To prevent this, plant cells use specialized pumps to move Ca2⁺ into storage compartments, including the ER, apoplast, vacuole, Golgi apparatus, chloroplast, peroxisome, and nucleus (Pirayesh et al. 2021). Together, these findings suggest a connection between autophagy and Ca2⁺ signaling in mediating root adaptation to osmotic stress.

The ER membrane plays a crucial role at various stages of autophagy (Lamb et al. 2013; Zhuang et al. 2017). In A. thaliana, the ER is the primary membrane source for autophagosome biogenesis. Furthermore, autophagy and the ER stress response are closely linked, enabling the selective degradation of ER regions to maintain cell homeostasis and promote survival under stress conditions (Zhang et al. 2020; Mochida and Nakatogawa 2022). The dynamic interaction between the ER and autophagy highlights their vital role in maintaining cellular balance under both normal and stress conditions.

Our findings indicate that osmotic stress triggers autophagy, a process closely associated with the influx of Ca2+ from the apoplast into the cytosol. This association was confirmed by applying EGTA, a broad-spectrum chelator that binds extracellular Ca2+, preventing its entry from the apoplast into the cytosol and thus stopping autophagy activation in A. thaliana root seedlings. Additionally, colocalization of RFP-ATG8i with the ER marker GFP-HDEL was observed, indicating a potential link with the formation of the phagophore assembly site (PAS). Increased cytosolic Ca2+ levels promote RFP-ATG8i localization to the ER in response to osmotic stress. This Ca2+−dependent process is supported by the reduced accumulation of ER-localized autophagosomes following EGTA treatment. Finally, colocalization of RFP-ATG8i with the ER was seen in autophagic bodies within the vacuolar lumen, suggesting active reticulophagy during DTT-induced ER stress and sorbitol-induced osmotic stress.

Collectively, the findings support a role for Ca2+ in modulating autophagy response to mitigate cellular damage under osmotic stress.

Materials and methods

Plant material

A. thaliana (L.) Heynh Columbia-0 (Col-0) seeds were provided by the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (Ohio State University). The lines used in this study are in the Col-0 background and have been previously described as follows: atg8i (SALK_075713C), atg8b (SALK_039404) a T-DNA insertion mutant (Alonso, et al. 2003), has been previously characterized as a non-hydrotropic mutant (Jimenez-Nopala, et al. 2018, Fig. S1 and S13), and the cyan tonoplast marker vac-ck (CFP fused to the C-terminal of vacuolar membrane protein γ-TIP) (CS16256) (Nelson, et al. 2007), which was obtained from the European Arabidopsis Stock Centre (NASC, Nottingham, UK). Additionally, the transgenic lines GFP-HDEL (Batoko, et al. 2000) and Cameleon (Gilroy, et al. 2014) used in this study have also been previously described.

Generation of proATG8i:RFP-ATG8i construct, complementation of atg8i mutant and generation of GFP-HDEL/RFP-ATG8i and vac-ck/RFP-ATG8i transgenic lines

The proATG8i:RFP-ATG8i construct was synthesized by GenScript (GenScript [Hong Kong] Limited). Specific primers, including a partial region of the att site and the 5’ promoter sequence of ATG8i, were designed to amplify the first round of the proATG8i:RFP-ATG8i construct. In a second round of amplification, primers that complete the att recombination site were used (Table S1). The PCR product was recombined in a BP reaction of the Gateway system with the pDONR207 vector. Positive clones were identified by PCR and plasmid DNA sequencing. For LR recombination, plasmid DNA was digested with ApaI to linearize the pEarlyGate302 vector (Earley, et al. 2006). Plasmid DNA from a positive clone was digested with PvuII to distinguish the empty vector from the clone’s differential band pattern.

To obtain A. thaliana transgenic lines, constructs were transferred into Agrobacterium. tumefaciens (C58C1) mediated by floral dipping (Clough and Bent 1998). Primary transformants were selected based on antibiotic resistance and confirmed by PCR. We examined at least three independent transgenic lines from each construct, including proATG8i:RFP-ATG8i (lines 2.1, 3.1, and 5.1), proATG8i:GUS (lines 2.2, 5.1, and 6.1), GFP-HDEL/RFP-ATG8i (lines 3.1, 4.1, and 7.1), vac-ck/RFP-ATG8i transgenic lines (lines 1.1, 5.1, and 7.1), and atg8i:RFP-ATG8i (lines 2.2, 3.3 and 5.5).

Plant growth conditions, osmotic stress, and drug treatments

A. thaliana seeds were surface sterilized with 95% ethanol for two minutes, then treated with 0.2% (v/v) Triton X-100 and 30% (v/v) bleach solution for 15 min, followed by 4 to 5 washes with sterile water. They were then subjected to cold treatment for four days. The germination medium consisted of solidified 1/2 Murashige-Skoog (MS) medium (Murashige-Skoog vitamin and salt mixture; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA), 0.5% (w/v) sucrose, pH 5.6, and 0.9% (w/v) agar (Beckton Dickinson and Company, Maryland, USA). Seeds were germinated vertically at 21 °C under a 16-h light/8-h dark cycle, with a light intensity of 105 μmol photons m−2 s−1. For sorbitol (0.8 M), chloroquine (100 μM) (Jimenez-Nopala, et al. 2018), ethylene-bis(oxyethylenenitrilo) tetraacetic acid (EGTA; 0.1 mM) (Akita and Miyazawa 2022), and dithiothreitol (DTT; two mM) (Batoko, et al. 2000) treatments, four-day-old seedlings grown on solid MS plates were transferred to a coverslip (24 × 50 mm) fully covered with 1 mL of solid MS medium containing either 0.8 M sorbitol, sorbitol plus chloroquine, or EGTA, along with two mM DTT to induce ER stress, for the indicated times. Non-supplemented MS medium served as a control. The seedlings were covered with a clean coverslip, and fluorescent microscopy images of the roots were captured using confocal microscopy on an inverted confocal Olympus Mphot laser scanning microscope system (FV1000; Olympus), equipped with FV10-ASW software (Olympus). GFP was excited with a 488 nm laser, and emission was collected at 525 nm. RFP was excited at 553 nm and collected at 583 nm. The cyan fluorophore was excited with a 458 nm laser, and emission was collected at 485 nm. Confocal images of live primary roots, spanning 50–100 μM below the hypocotyl-root transition zone, were obtained using a 60 × objective lens. Whole-seeding images of the proATG8i:RFP-ATG8i transgenic line were captured in three sections with a 10 × objective and assembled using the Stitching plugin from ImageJ software (version 2.1.0/154j). Additionally, the quantification of relative fluorescence was analyzed using the Analysis function, followed by the Spot Counter in Fiji (version 2.16). The artwork was created using Adobe Photoshop 2023.0.0 and Fiji.

GUS staining analysis

Seeds from the proATG8i:GUS transgenic line 2.2 were germinated on control medium and grown for four days before being transferred to one of the following media: control medium, sorbitol medium, or sorbitol medium supplemented with EGTA. After 15 min of treatment, seedlings were harvested, incubated with GUS solution (Gallagher 2012) for 12 h, then decolored and observed under an optical microscope with a 20 × objective lens.

Measurements of root growth

Plates containing 30 individuals of four-day-old A. thaliana WT, atg8b, or atg8i mutant seedling were subjected to control or control supplemented 0.1 mM EGTA. Seedlings growth was recorded every 24 h using a conventional scanner (HP Scanjet G3110). Root length was measured with ImageJ software version 2.1.0/1.54j. Relative root length was calculated for each time point by setting the mean root length of WT seedlings grown under control conditions to 1.0 and normalizing all values accordingly.

Immunodetection of ATG8

An anti-ATG8 Western blot was performed using a 15% acrylamide gel, run at 120 V for 2.5 h. The membrane was blocked by incubating for 1 h at room temperature with 5% milk (Bio-Rad, California, USA) diluted in PBS 1X containing 0.5% Triton. Antibodies were diluted in 5% milk, and primary anti-ATG8 (Agrisera, Vannas, Sweden) was used at a 1:1500 dilution. The RFP primary antibodies were rabbit-polyclonal developed in the Animal Facility Unit of the Biotechnology Institute, based in recombinant RFP protein. It was used 1:1000 for 16 h at 4 °C. For the secondary antibody, the blot was incubated with anti-rabbit HRP diluted 1:3000 for one hour at room temperature. Protein detection was performed using the SuperSignal West Femto Luminol Maximum Sensitivity Substrate (Thermo Scientific, Massachusetts, USA).

Prediction of transcriptional factor binding sites in the ATG8i regulation region

A 1000 base pair DNA region upstream of the ATG8i gene’s translation start site was analyzed using the Plant Promoter Database (PPDB; https://ppdb.agr.gifu-u.ac.jp/ppdb/cgi-bin/index.cgi). To find transcription factor (TF) binding sites and co-regulated promoter regions, the Plant Promoter Analysis Navigator 3.0 (PlantPAN; http://PlantPAN.itps.ncku.edu.tw) was used for transcriptional regulatory region (TRR) prediction. TFs were selected based on a similar score, ranging from 0.8 to 1.

Results

Low cytosolic Ca2⁺ levels reduce primary root growth in the atg8i mutant

We focused on ATG8i based on converging evidence from regulatory, genetic, and expression studies (Fig S1). Our previous analysis of A. thaliana ATG genes in a TRR indicated that several members, including ATG8e, ATG8h, and ATG8i, may be transcriptionally regulated by calmodulin-binding transcription activators (CAMTA) factors (Fig. S1e). Genetic evidence further emphasizes the importance of ATG8i: the atg8i mutant, along with other autophagy mutants such as atg2, atg5, atg9, and atg8b, failed to develop hydrotropic curvature (Jimenez-Nopala et al. 2018). Expression studies also show that multiple ATG8 genes (ATG8a, ATG8b, ATG8e, ATG8f, and ATG8i) are active in roots (Slavikova et al., 2005). Notably, we previously observed increased ATG8i expression in roots exposed to the hydrotropic system NM-WSM (Jimenez-Nopala et al. 2018). The complementation of the atg8i mutant with the proATG8i:RFP-ATG8i construct restored WT root growth under control conditions and in the presence of EGTA (Fig. S1b and c). Likewise, expression of the proATG8b:GFP-ATG8b construct in the atg8b mutant restored WT root growth under the same conditions (Fig. S13b and c). Overall, these findings suggest that ATG8i is a promising candidate for understanding how autophagy links osmotic stress and Ca2+ signaling.

To determine whether ATG8i influences root growth responses to apoplastic Ca2⁺ influx, we compared root development in atg8i autophagy mutant and WT plants grown in control medium or in control medium supplemented with EGTA, a chelator of extracellular Ca2⁺. By day seven, EGTA treatment reduced root growth in both genotypes compared to the control; however, this reduction was more significant in atg8b and atg8i (WT: 18.92 ± 0.55 mm; atg8b: 8.5 ± 1.3; atg8i: 9.39 ± 0.61 mm; Fig. 1a and b). These findings suggest that ATG8b and ATG8i independently influence root growth in response to changes in extracellular Ca2⁺ availability.

Osmotic stress reduced the expression of ATG8i in the hypocotyl-root transition zone

To analyze the expression pattern of ATG8i in A. thaliana, we created a transgenic line that expresses β-glucuronidase (GUS), a reporter gene controlled by the ATG8i TRR, which had not been previously characterized (Fig. 2a). The construct includes a 570 bp TRR from the intergenic region between the start codon of the ATG8i gene and the nearby locus AT3G15570. This TRR contains potential binding sites for transcription factors (TFs) involved in water stress response, such as ATHB7 (recognizing: acAATAAtg) and ATBH12 (ggaTGATTga), as well as Ca2⁺ ion signaling proteins CAMTA2 (ACGCGc) and CAMTA3 (both recognizing ACGCGc). The presence of these elements in the TRR suggests that ATG8i and ATG8i-mediated autophagy are likely regulated by osmotic and Ca2⁺ signaling pathways (Materials and Methods; Fig. 2b). In proATG8i:GUS seedlings (line 2.2; Fig. S2), GUS expression was prominently observed in the cotyledons under osmotic stress (Fig. 2c, d, and S3). However, relative expression analysis showed no significant changes in GUS accumulation in response to osmotic stress, EGTA, or their combination compared to the control (Fig. 2c and d). GUS activity was also detectable in the hypocotyl-root transition zone and the nearby root segment (Fig. 2c, e, and S4). In this area, relative expression analysis indicated that GUS accumulation decreased under osmotic stress, EGTA treatment, or combined treatment compared to the control (Fig. 2c and e). Conversely, no GUS expression was observed in the root tip under any of the tested conditions (Fig. 2c and Fig. S5). Similarly, ATG8i expression was absent in the root tip after one and two hours of osmotic treatment (Fig. S6). In summary, ATG8i transcription in both the root and cotyledons appears unaffected by either osmotic stress or the lack of Ca2+ influx from the apoplast. Additionally, ATG8i expression is reduced by both osmotic stress and Ca2+ in the hypocotyl-root transition zone, with no ATG8i expression detected in the root tip. These results provide functional evidence that this regulatory region contains the cis-acting elements to influence the expression of the ATG8i gene in the hypocotyl-root transition zone.

Osmotic stress caused the buildup of cytosolic Ca2+ below the hypocotyl-root transition zone

To assess how osmotic stress affects cytosolic Ca2⁺ levels, A. thaliana seedlings expressing the transgenic Cameleon line with the Ca2⁺ sensor NES-YC3.6 (Gilroy, et al. 2014) were used. Our results showed that under control conditions, the Cameleon seedlings display a low basal level of cytosolic Ca2⁺ mainly in the cotyledons and root tip (Fig. 3a-c). In contrast, osmotic stress led to a significant increase in cytosolic Ca2⁺ levels, especially in the hypocotyl-root transition zone and root tip, with a moderate rise in the cotyledons (Fig. 3d-f). Conversely, chelating Ca2⁺ from the apoplast with 0.1 mM EGTA decreased cytoplasmic Ca2⁺ levels in the hypocotyl-root transition zone and cotyledons during osmotic stress (Fig. 3g-i). Ca2+ accumulation in EGTA-treated control seedlings was similar to that in control seedlings. These findings suggest that osmotic stress triggers the accumulation of cytosolic Ca2⁺. To analyze RFP-ATG8i protein accumulation under osmotic stress, four-day-old seedlings were transferred to either a solid control medium or a medium containing sorbitol, and the fluorescent signal was recorded after 15 min.

Under osmotic stress, RFP-ATG8i shows increased accumulation in all tissues (Fig. 4). ATG8i protein levels rise significantly in the hypocotyl-root transition zone and the nearby region of the root below it (Fig. 4b). A slight increase is also seen at the root tip (Fig. 4b, inset). In contrast, control seedlings do not show noticeable changes in RFP-ATG8i signals along the root during the same period (Fig. S8). Quantification of relative fluorescence indicates a threefold increase after osmotic stress compared to the control. However, no corresponding accumulation of RFP-ATG8i protein is detected by immunoblotting (Fig S10). This discrepancy may reflect changes in the subcellar state of RFP-ATG8i rather that an increase in its abundance. Under control conditions RFP-ATG8i protein is distributed homogenously in the cytosol, whereas osmotic stress rapidly induces its redistribution into speckle dots which likely enhances measured fluorescent signal.

For comparison, the phenotype of proATG8b:GFP-ATG8b (GFP-ATG8b) was included in this analysis. Under osmotic stress, fluoresce signal was observed through the seedling. Notably, GFP-ATG8b accumulated along the entire root, with the strongest signal at the root tip. This accumulation pattern clearly differed from that of RFP-ATG8i, highlighting distinct spatial distribution and suggesting a specific role for these proteins under osmotic stress (Fig. S12). These findings suggest that RFP-ATG8i accumulation is more closely associated with the response of the hypocotyl-root transition zone and the root below it, rather than the root tip.

Osmotic stress is crucial for regulating ATG8i autophagosome buildup

Autophagy is a cellular process activated during stress conditions to recycle cytosolic components and supply nutrients by breaking down the contents of autophagic bodies in vacuoles. To examine the effect of osmotic stress on autophagy, we utilized the transgenic RFP-ATG8i line to track the accumulation of ATG8i autophagosomes specifically. Four-day-old RFP-ATG8i seedlings, initially grown under control conditions, were transferred to osmotic stress medium. Autophagic flux is an important indicator of process completion, reflecting the successful fusion of the outer membrane of the autophagosome with the tonoplast and the subsequent delivery of the autophagic body for degradation. In animal cells, chloroquine (CQ) impairs autophagosome fusion with the lysosomes (Mauthe, et al. 2018). It also raises lysosomal pH, potentially stops autophagic body degradation (Nirk et al. 2020; Ohkuma and Poole 1978). Accordingly, to our observations, CQ is useful to block autophagy flux in plants by both stopping autophagosome-vacuole fusion and the degradation of autophagy bodies within the vacuole. To evaluate autophagic flux during osmotic stress, we treated four-day-old RFP-ATG8i seedlings with sorbitol and 100 µM CQ. After 15 min of treatment, the number of fluorescent dots increased significantly (Fig. 5a and b). Sorbitol alone resulted in 181 dots, while sorbitol combined with CQ increased this number to 488 (Fig. 5a, and b).

Compared to the control, which displayed 64 dots, CQ alone increased the count to 104 dots (Fig. 5a and b). The notable accumulation of fluorescent dots in the presence of CQ confirms the activation of a functional autophagic flux under osmotic stress (Fig. 5a and b). To further support our findings regarding the blockage of autophagic flux in the vacuole with CQ, we generated the transgenic line RFP-ATG8i in the vac-ck background, expressing cyan-tonoplast (Nelson et al. 2007; Fig. 5c). Four-day-old vac-ck/RFP-ATG8i seedlings (line 7.1; Fig. 6 and S9) were treated with sorbitol and then imaged using confocal microscopy. As shown in Fig. 6, the co-localization of red fluorescence with blue fluorescence, most likely at the tonoplast (indicated by the white coloration), is especially clear under control conditions (Fig. 6b). Under osmotic stress, autophagic bodies are visible inside the vacuole lumen (Fig. 6c). These results demonstrate that the autophagic process, involving the ATG8i protein, is activated in response to osmotic stress. The fusion of autophagosomes with the vacuole results in the accumulation of autophagy bodies within the vacuolar lumen. Ca2+ influx from the apoplast to the cytosol regulates RFP-ATG8i-labeled autophagosome formation under osmotic stress

To further investigate whether the decrease in cytosolic Ca2⁺ influences the accumulation of RFP-ATG8i fluorescent dots, four-day-old RFP-ATG8i seedlings were transferred to control and sorbitol-plus-EGTA media (Fig. 5).

The results showed that RFP-ATG8i dots accumulated significantly under osmotic stress caused by sorbitol but decreased when apoplastic Ca2⁺ was chelated with EGTA in the sorbitol-containing medium (Fig. 5a and b). Specifically, the data revealed a drop in RFP-ATG8i autophagosomes from 181 in sorbitol to 130 in sorbitol-EGTA after 15 min, with these differences being statistically significant (Fig. 5a and b). No RFP-ATG8i fluorescent dots are observed in control EGTA (Fig. 5a and b). The reduction in ATG8i autophagosomes observed during osmotic stress with EGTA, compared to osmotic stress alone, indicates that apoplastic Ca2⁺ influx into the apoplast is required to trigger the formation of ATG8i-labeled autophagosomes and their cargo transport towards degradation. However, reducing apoplastic Ca2⁺ influx into the cytosol led to an approximately 27% decrease in RFP-ATG8i accumulation. This partial reduction suggests that RFP-ATG8i regulation involves multiple pathways, consistent with water deprivation-responsive promoter elements (ATHB7 and ATBH12) and Ca2⁺ signaling (CAMTA2 and CAMTA3; Fig. 2b) in its TRR.

To investigate whether reducing cytoplasmic Ca2⁺ regulates ATG8i-mediated reticulophagy, four-day-old seedlings were treated with either EGTA or sorbitol media supplemented with 0.1 mM EGTA, and the colocalization of GFP-HDEL/RFP-ATG8i (yellow signal) was analyzed (Fig. 7a, b, c, and d). Fifteen minutes after treatment with sorbitol supplemented with 0.1 mM EGTA, RFP-ATG8i dots appeared in transgenic plants and colocalized with GFP-HDEL in the cytoplasm and vacuolar lumen. Such structures were rarely seen in EGTA media alone (Fig. 7a, b, c, and d). These results indicate that osmotic stress possible induces reticulophagy. Furthermore, when the apoplastic influx of Ca2⁺ into the cytosol was blocked with Ca2+ chelation (Fig. 3g, h, and i), GFP-HDEL and RFP-ATG8i colocalization decreased, suggesting that Ca2⁺ mediates reticulophagy under osmotic stress.

To investigate the potential interaction between the ER and autophagy under ER stress, four-day-old GFP-HDEL/RFP-ATG8i seedlings were treated with two mM DTT to induce ER stress. During DTT treatment, RFP-ATG8i-labeled autophagosomes colocalize with the ER marker GFP-HDEL (Fig. 7a, b, c, and d under DTT). This GFP/RFP colocalization suggest that reticulophagy may occur through an ATG8i-dependent pathway during ER stress conditions.

To further analyze and quantify the colocalization between GFP-HDEL and RFP-ATG8i signals, fluorescence intensity profiles were measured using ImageJ. Under sorbitol, sorbitol plus EGTA, and DTT treatments, the fluorescence signals showed significant overlap (Fig. 7d). These findings suggest that fragments of the ER marked with GFP-HDEL are incorporated into RFP-ATG8i-labeled autophagosomes, which are then delivered to the vacuole for degradation.

Discussion

Osmotic stress happens when solute levels in the environment increase, creating a physical challenge to cellular balance. To handle this stress, cells activate survival mechanisms. One of these is autophagy (Liu, et al. 2009), a highly conserved process that breaks down and recycles dysfunctional components in the cytoplasm, such as proteins, protein complexes, and organelles. Ca2+ is an essential signaling molecule involved in many developmental and stress responses. An increase in Ca2+ levels caused by biotic and abiotic signals occurs through controlled influx from external sources, such as the apoplast, as well as from internal stores such as the ER (Batistič and Kudla 2012; McAinsh and Pittman 2009).

In this study, we found that osmotic stress and cytosolic Ca2+ levels specifically affect the formation of RFP-ATG8i-labeled autophagosomes, suggesting a possible regulatory link between Ca2+ signaling and autophagy.

Our analyses further suggest that cytosolic Ca2+ levels are essential for root growth, especially when autophagy is impaired. In the atg8i mutant, lowering apoplastic Ca2+ levels with EGTA caused a significant reduction in root growth compared to WT seedlings under the same conditions.

Under control conditions, both atg8b and atg8i mutants exhibited greater primary root length than WT after seven days, reaching 26.94 mm and 24.31 mm, respectively. These data indicate that loss of ATG8b or ATG8i alters root development under basal conditions.EGTA treatment significantly reduced primary root length in both mutants. Apoplastic Ca2⁺ sequestration, likely leading to reduced cytosolic Ca2⁺ availability, limited root growth to 8.2 mm in atg8b and 9.3 mm in atg8i. This pronounced reduction suggests that the absence of ATG8b or ATG8i increases the dependence on extracellular Ca2⁺ to sustain root elongation. The differences in inhibition levels likely reflect the relative contributions of each ATG8b and ATG8i to the observed phenotype, suggesting that these proteins may have distinct functional roles. However, potential redundancy between ATG8b and ATG8i should also be considered, as a double knockout (atg8b atg8i) may display a more pronounced phenotype than either single mutant alone. Rather, the combined effect of impaired autophagy and limited extracellular Ca2⁺ availability imposes a strong constraint on root development. Our findings support a functional interaction between Ca2+ signaling and ATG8i activity during root development, suggesting that the atg8i mutant is particularly vulnerable to fluctuations in Ca2+ homeostasis and, consequently, to environmental stress conditions. Together, these results indicate that apoplastic Ca2⁺ plays a sustained role in supporting primary root growth, particularly under conditions in which autophagy is compromised.

The Cameleon line that expresses the Ca2⁺ sensor NES-YC3.6 (Gilroy, et al. 2014) shows that osmotic stress causes a buildup of cytoplasmic Ca2⁺ in the cotyledon leaves, the root tip, and mainly in the hypocotyl-root transition zone, which extends to the nearby stem and root. The increased Ca2⁺ levels in the hypocotyl-root transition zone observed during osmotic stress may indicate that this ion regulates stress responses in both the root and the shoot, including RFP-ATG8i-mediated autophagy.

Ca2⁺ levels also increase significantly in the cotyledons, potentially affecting stomatal opening and closing in response to osmotic stress. These findings highlight the importance of maintaining Ca2⁺ balance during osmotic stress, which, in turn, regulates autophagy through the specific role of ATG8i in the stress response. To alleviate elevated cytoplasmic Ca2+ concentrations, activation of alternative Ca2+ transport channels, such as the Ca2+ ATPase (e.g., ACA proteins), and mechanosensitive channels (e.g., MSL family members) may be crucial to mobilize Ca2+ into the apoplast or organelles (García Bossi et al. 2020; Yu et al. 2018). These channels have been proposed to transfer Ca2+ from the cytoplasm to the apoplast and organelles, such as the chloroplast and the mitochondria (Hamilton et al. 2015; revised in Castillo-Olamendi et al. 2024). Our unpublished RNA-seq data indicate that MSL3 and ACA8 are upregulated under osmotic stress conditions, further supporting their roles in Ca2+ redistribution. Collectively, these findings suggest that multiple Ca2+ channels contribute to restoring Ca2+ homeostasis during osmotic stress.

The expression pattern of ATG8i in four-day-old was studied under control conditions, sorbitol-induced osmotic stress, and in the presence of both control and osmotic stress with EGTA. Under control conditions, GUS activity driven by the ATG8i promoter was mainly seen in cotyledons, as well as in the hypocotyl-root transition zone. Notably, there was no GUS expression in the root tip (Fig. 2c). Under sorbitol and EGTA treatment, the most significant change occurred in the hypocotyl-root transition zone, where GUS activity was significantly reduced when osmotic stress was combined with EGTA. This indicates that disrupting Ca2+ balance inhibits proATG8i:GUS expression in this region under osmotic stress. This spatial pattern suggests that Ca2+ signaling is essential not only for activating of ATG8i but also for proper organ-specific regulation during stress. Notably, no clear correlation was observed between ATG8i transcript levels and the abundance of the ATG8i protein. Similar discrepancies have been reported for other ATG8 isoforms, supporting the notion that ATG8i is regulated predominantly at the post-translational level as has been previously described (Boyecheva Woltering S, Isono E, 2020).

The expression database shows that all nine ATG8 gene family members are active in the cotyledon and the hypocotyl-root transition zone (TAIR). Our data expand on this, demonstrating that proATG8i:GUS expression decreases in the hypocotyl-root transition zone in response to osmotic stress and lower apoplastic Ca2+ influx. The hypocotyl–root transition zone in dicotyledonous plants connects the embryonic leaves with the developing root system. This region is highly plastic, and its elongation is strongly regulated by environmental factors such as light, gravity, hormones, and temperature (Vandenbussche et al. 2005). Water availability is therefore expected to have a major impact on its growth, with potential consequences for subsequent plant development. Our data contribute to the understanding of Ca2⁺ accumulation in the hypocotyl–root transition zone in response to osmotic stress. Moreover, the activation of autophagy in this context may represent a protective mechanism that helps alleviate cellular stress and maintain tissue integrity.

Osmotic stress and cytosolic Ca2⁺ levels regulate the buildup of RFP-ATG8i autophagosomes below the hypocotyl-root transition zone

We aimed to investigate whether the accumulation of specific RFP-ATG8i autophagosomes contributes to the response to osmotic stress and whether changes in cytosolic Ca2⁺ concentration regulate this process. Our observations suggest that osmotic stress triggers the formation of RFP-ATG8i autophagosomes. These autophagosomes are mainly localized below the hypocotyl-root transition zone. Notably, their accumulation is rarely observed toward the root tip (Fig. 4). However, no proATG8i:GUS was observed in the root tip (Fig. 2, S4, and S6), suggesting mobilization of ATG8i messenger from other parts of the plant, such as the hypocotyl, to the root tip. To date, the systemic movement of autophagy-related mRNAs has not been documented; nonetheless, the long-distance translocation of mobile signals from the shoot to the root is well established (Koenig, and Hoffmann-Benning., 2020).

CQ and HCQ are weak bases that readily diffuse across cellular membranes and accumulate in acidic subcellular compartments such as lysosomes and endosomes, where they become protonated and trapped (Ohkuma & Poole 1978). This accumulation increases the luminal pH (from ~ 4 to ~ 6), leading to inhibition of acidic proteases and other hydrolases within the endolysosomal system (reviewed in Nirk et al. 2020).

Chloroquine has not been extensively validated as an autophagy inhibitor in plants. Nevertheless, as a weak base known to accumulate in acidic compartments and interfere with degradative processes, it can serve as a functional probe. Under our experimental conditions, chloroquine treatment led to a significant accumulation of RFP–ATG8i puncta specifically under sorbitol-induced osmotic stress. This response is consistent with inhibition of the degradative step of autophagy, as described in animal systems (Mauthe et al. 2018; Nirk et al. 2020). Therefore, while we do not make definitive claims regarding its precise mode of action in plants, chloroquine may be considered a functional tool to assess autophagic flux.

Importantly, the significant increase in RFP–ATG8i puncta observed in sorbitol supplemented with chloroquine compared with sorbitol alone indicates that autophagosomes formed under osmotic stress are normally delivered to and processed in the vacuole within 15 min. The additional accumulation detected upon chloroquine treatment is more consistent with active autophagic flux followed by blockade of the degradative step, rather than with a static accumulation of autophagosomes at the vacuolar periphery although this interpretation remains speculative.

Furthermore, autophagic flux was demonstrated using chloroquine to inhibit the degradation of autophagic bodies, along with the transgenic line vac-ck/RFP-ATG8i, which expresses cyan-tonoplast vacuolar protein sorting-associated protein 35 (Fig. 6c). This showed colocalization between the tonoplast and RFP-ATG8i under control conditions (Fig. 6c and d). When autophagosomes were induced by osmotic stress, autophagic bodies labeled with RFP-ATG8i were observed within the vacuolar lumen (Fig. 6c). Our results indicate that sorbitol combined with CQ in the RFP-ATG8i line significantly increased the number of RFP-ATG8i dots (Fig. 5a and b), suggesting that cytoplasmic Ca2⁺ buildup promotes autophagy during osmotic stress. In contrast, adding EGTA to sorbitol reduced the number of RFP-ATG8i dots (Fig. 5a and b), indicating that apoplastic Ca2⁺ is essential for autophagy regulation during osmotic stress. Overall, these results show that the buildup of cytoplasmic Ca2⁺ is a crucial regulator of autophagy, particularly in response to osmotic stress, linking Ca2⁺ signaling to ATG8i-mediated autophagy.

To explore if autophagy degrades ER during osmotic stress, we developed a transgenic line expressing the RFP-ATG8i construct in an HDEL background. HDEL is a C-terminal tetrapeptide sequence (His-Asp-Glu-Leu) that acts as an ER retention signal in plants.

In this transgenic line, RFP-ATG8i colocalized with the ER-HDEL signals (Fig. 7a, b, c, and d), suggesting a close association between ATG8i and the ER under osmotic stress. These findings indicate that structural alterations in the ER may be targeted for degradation through the autophagy pathway. Notably, this degradation was reduced when Ca2⁺ influx from the apoplast was blocked. Together, these findings suggest that Ca2⁺ accumulation in the cytosol in response to osmotic stress may contribute to ER damage, and that autophagy plays a role in ER recycling.

EGTA, a broad-spectrum Ca2⁺ chelator, can affect multiple Ca2⁺-dependent cellular processes. Nevertheless, our results show that osmotic stress induces influx of Ca2⁺ from the apoplast into the cytosol, raising cytosolic Ca2⁺ levels and activating autophagy.

Bioinformatic analysis of the ATG8i promoter region revealed potential binding sites for transcription factors involved in water deprivation (ATHB7, ATHB12) and Ca2⁺ signaling (CAMTA2, CAMTA3), suggesting that these conditions may regulate ATG8i expression. During the process where Ca2⁺ moves from the apoplast to the cytosol under osmotic stress, it likely interacts with Ca2+-dependent protein kinases (CDPKs) or Calmodulin-like proteins (CML), among other sensors. This observation is supported by recent research linking CDPKs to the regulation of autophagy under biotic stress. For example, in wheat, silencing of TaCDPK27 was found to negatively regulate autophagy during infection with powdery mildew (Blumeria graminis f. sp. tritici), indicating that Ca2⁺-regulated CDPKs play a role in autophagy modulation during pathogen defense (Yue, et al 2023). Our RNA-seq analysis shows that osmotic stress increases transcription of CDPK32, CDPK28, CML37-39, suggesting additional potential participants in the Ca2⁺-mediated osmotic stress response. The possible connection between CDPKs, CML and ATG proteins presents an interesting area for further research.

Conclusion

This study aims to enhance our understanding of autophagy regulation, especially its response to osmotic stress. A key focus is to examine the potential role of Ca2⁺ ions in this process. We also seek to determine whether the ATG8i protein is specifically regulated under osmotic stress conditions.

We observed that osmotic stress leads to Ca2+ accumulation in A. thaliana seedlings, particularly in the hypocotyl-root transition zone. This Ca2+ increase mainly comes from the apoplast since it is blocked by EGTA treatment, a Ca2⁺ chelator, suggesting an external source of this ion. Notably, the RFP-ATG8i protein also accumulates in this hypocotyl-root transition zone.

Specialization of ATG8 proteins has been previously reported (Del Chiaro et al 2025). RFP-ATG8i-labeled autophagosomes were predominantly concentrated in the root region immediately distal to the hypocotyl-root transition zone. Notably, they were absent from the root tip, suggesting a potential organ-specific role for ATG8i in the response to osmotic stress. GFP-ATG8b accumulated through the entire root, with the strongest fluorescent detected at the root tip. This distinct accumulation pattern clearly differs from observed for RFP-ATG8i, highlighting their differential spatial distribution and supporting the idea that this ATG8 isoforms may fulfill non redundant functions under osmotic stress. Notably, RFP-ATG8i colocalizes with the ER marker HDEL, indicating a potential role of ATG8i in reticulophagy. This function may help in reducing both osmotic and ER stress.
