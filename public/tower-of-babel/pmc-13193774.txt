Patterns of health-promoting lifestyles among patients with colorectal cancer: A latent profile analysis
Authors: Mengjiao Zhong, Xiaodan Wu, Jingyue Xie, Xinxin Li, Xiaoxuan Wang, Qianqian Du, Chen Yuan, Wenwen Wu, Meifen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify the latent profiles of health-promoting lifestyle and explore the correlates of these profiles among patients with colorectal cancer.MethodsA total of 561 hospitalized patients with colorectal cancer at a tertiary hospital in Guangzhou between July 2023 and June 2024 were enrolled. The Health-Promoting Lifestyle Profile II Revised (HPLP-II R) was used to assess health-promoting behaviors. Latent profile analysis (LPA) was conducted to identify health-promoting lifestyle subgroups. Univariate analysis and multinomial logistic regression were performed to examine the association between profiles and sociodemographic characteristics, physical frailty, and psychosocial factors.ResultsFour distinct health-promoting lifestyle profiles were identified: profile 1 - “the comprehensively positive high health-promotion (8.4%)”, profile 2 - “the nutrition-spiritual growth retained low health-promotion (41.5%)”, profile 3 - “the globally low health-promotion (14.4%)”, and profile 4 - “the balanced moderate health-promotion (35.7%)”. Psychological frailty and friendship isolation were more likely to be associated with Profile 2 (OR= 1.799, P= 0.001; OR= 5.484, P= 0.043) and Profile 3 (OR= 2.249, P < 0.001; OR= 11.449, P= 0.005). Patients with high depression scores (OR= 4.332, P= 0.015) and who are currently smoking (OR= 3.288, P= 0.025) were more likely to be classified into Profile 3. Profile 1 and Profile 4 exhibited a difference in per capita monthly household income.ConclusionsHealth-promoting lifestyle profiles of patients with colorectal cancer has distinct characteristics. These findings provide a basis for nurses to identify high-risk subgroups and implement profile-specific, targeted lifestyle interventions in clinical practice. Target intervention tailored to each profile's characteristics and correlates may modify the lifestyle among patients with colorectal cancer.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Health-promoting lifestyle, Depression, Social isolation, Frailty, Latent profile analysis

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common gastrointestinal malignancies, ranking third in incidence and second in cancer-related mortality worldwide.1 In China, CRC is the third most frequently diagnosed cancer and the fifth leading cause of cancer death.2 With advances in early detection and comprehensive treatment, the global 5-year survival has risen to approximately 65%.3 Consequently, CRC survivors now constitute a large and distinct population experiencing substantial physical and psychological burdens that adversely affect long-term health, prognosis, and quality of life.

Evidence suggests that a healthy lifestyle, including regular physical activity, weight management and a healthy diet, is critically important for improving daily performance, extending overall survival and increasing quality of life.4,5 Despite awareness of the benefits of a healthy life, only about 12% of CRC survivors consistently adhere to the World Cancer Research Fund/American Institute for Cancer Research (WCRF/AICR) recommendation.6 This low adherence may be partly explained by CRC-specific challenges. Unlike many other cancer populations, CRC survivors often face unique treatment-related sequelae, such as bowel dysfunction, ostomy-related challenges, and persistent gastrointestinal symptoms,7,8 which may hinder their ability to maintain healthy lifestyles. Moreover, lifestyle behaviors among CRC survivors are highly heterogeneous. Differences in disease stage, treatment modalities (e.g., chemotherapy, radiotherapy), and functional status contribute to substantial variability. For example, patients with a stoma may face barriers in physical activity and social engagement, while those undergoing chemotherapy may experience fatigue and gastrointestinal toxicity that limit adherence to healthy behaviors. These variations highlight the need for more tailored and individualized lifestyle interventions.

Accurate and comprehensive assessment of colorectal cancer survivors’ lifestyles is the essential foundation for achieving meaningful lifestyle improvement. Lifestyle has various dimensions, including physical and psychological dimensions. Health-Promoting Lifestyle (HPL) refers to a set of behaviors individuals adopt to enhance or maintain their health.9 It is a multidimensional construct encompassing spiritual growth, physical activity, stress management, nutrition, interpersonal relationships, and health responsibility.9 Importantly, these dimensions may not develop uniformly among CRC survivors. For instance, patients may demonstrate adequate health responsibility but insufficient physical activity or poor stress management due to disease-related constraints. Such multidimensional imbalance cannot be fully captured by overall scores alone, highlighting the need to explore underlying patterns of HPL. Previous studies have shown that adopting HPL enhances motivation and self-care capacity, which in turn improves prognosis and quality of life among cancer survivors.10,11 A qualitative study also indicates that adopting HPL improves daily functional performance, reduces treatment-related complications, and improves quality of life among CRC survivors.12

In addition, lifestyle behaviors are not formed in isolation but are influenced by a range of physical, psychological, and social factors. Depression may undermine motivation, reduce self-efficacy, and increase fatigue, thereby limiting engagement in physical activity, healthy eating, and stress management.13 Frailty, characterized by decreased physiological reserve and functional decline, may directly restrict patients’ capacity to perform and sustain health-promoting behaviors.14 Social isolation may further reduce emotional support, access to health information, and external reinforcement, all of which are essential for maintaining healthy routines. Although lifestyle is traditionally viewed as a determinant of frailty, this relationship may be bidirectional, particularly among CRC survivors experiencing treatment-related functional decline.15 Therefore, examining these factors as potential correlates of lifestyle patterns may provide a more comprehensive understanding of behavioral heterogeneity.

However, research on HPL among patients with colorectal cancer remains limited. Most existing studies have predominantly adopted variable-centered approaches (e.g., total score analysis) or cross-sectional qualitative designs, which assume population homogeneity and overlook the presence of distinct behavioral subgroups. As a result, these approaches may fail to identify individuals with moderate overall scores but critically low performance in specific dimensions, limiting the precision of intervention strategies. Latent profile analysis (LPA) is a person-centered approach that identifies latent subgroups with similar characteristics based on multiple observed variables, thereby effectively revealing within-sample heterogeneity.16 Importantly, LPA enables the identification of clinically meaningful subgroups, facilitating targeted and profile-based interventions in nursing practice. Therefore, this study applied LPA to identify distinct profiles of health-promoting lifestyles and their correlates patients with colorectal cancer, aiming to better characterize behavioral heterogeneity and inform more precise, individualized lifestyle interventions to improve long-term health outcomes.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study employed convenience sampling to survey patients with colorectal cancer hospitalized at a tertiary hospital in Guangzhou, China, between July 2023 and June 2024. Inclusion criteria were: 1) Pathologically confirmed primary colon or rectal cancer; 2) Age ≥ 18 years; 3) With clear consciousness and the ability to communicate verbally normally; 4) Being informed of the diagnosis and voluntary participation. Exclusion criteria were: 1) A documented history of severe cognitive impairment or psychiatric disorders; 2) A terminal condition with a life expectancy of fewer than six months (such as multiple organ failure).

The sample size was determined through a dual approach: For the unordered multiclass logistic regression with 20 independent variables, we applied the rule of 10 events per variable (EPV),17 yielding 20 × 10 = 200 cases. To accommodate an estimated 20% dropout rate, the adjusted target was calculated as 200/(1 − 0.2) = 250. For LPA, a minimum of 200 cases is recommended to ensure model stability. The larger value (250) was selected as the target. Ultimately, 561 participants were enrolled after excluding incomplete responses, which was advantageous for improving statistical power, enhancing the stability of latent profile solutions, and ensuring more reliable estimation of subgroup characteristics.

Measures

Demographic and disease-related characteristics

Designed based on the researcher's literature review and consultation with clinical nursing experts, it includes: (1) general demographic characteristics: age, gender, educational level, monthly income; (2) disease-related characteristics: cigarette smoking status, alcohol consumption habits, body mass index (BMI) (kg/m2), tumor node metastasis (TNM) stage, stoma presence, chronic pain, self-perceived health status, self-perceived sleep quality.

Health-promoting lifestyle

The Health Promoting Lifestyle Profile (HPLP) was developed by Walker in 1987 and consists of 48 items.18 This study used the revised version of HPLP, developed by Cao in 2016,19 contains 40 items across six domains (Interpersonal relationships, Nutrition, Health responsibility, Physical activity, Stress management, and Spiritual growth). Each item is rated on a four-point Likert scale (1 = Never to 4 = Always), with total scores ranging from 40 to 160, where higher scores indicate better health-promoting behaviors. The scale has demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach's α = 0.91),19 and is widely applied among cancer patients, individuals with chronic diseases, and older adults in mainland China. This study reported a Cronbach's α of 0.944.

Depression

Zigmond et al.20 developed the Hospital Depression Scale (HDS) in 1983 to assess depressive symptoms over the past month. The scale consists of seven items rated on a four-point Likert scale, with items 3, 4, and 6 reverse-scored. Total scores range from 0 to 21, with 0–7 indicating normal levels (no depression), and scores ≥ 8 suggesting the presence of depressive symptoms. Higher scores indicate greater severity of depression. The HDS has demonstrated good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach's α of 0.879, and is widely applied in clinical research and practice.21 This study reported a Cronbach's α of 0.702.

Frailty

Gobbens et al.22 developed the Tilburg Frailty Indicator (TFI) in 2010 based on the integral conceptual model of frailty, aiming to assess three domains: physical, psychological, and social. The instrument consists of 15 items, with total scores ranging from 0 to 15. A score of ≥ 5 indicates the presence of frailty, with higher scores reflecting greater severity. In 2013, Xi et al.23 translated the TFI into Chinese, retaining the original items and dimensions. The Chinese version demonstrated good reliability and validity in assessing frailty among Chinese populations and has since been widely applied. This study reported a Cronbach's α of 0.670.

Social isolation

Lubben24 revised the Lubben Social Network Scale-6 (LSNS-6) in 2006 from the original social network scale, which comprises 6 items across two domains: family (contacts with relatives by marriage or blood) and friends (interactions with friends or neighbors). It assesses the number of family members and friends with whom an individual has regular contact within the past month and is widely used as an objective measure of social isolation. Each item is rated on a 6-point scale (0 = none to 5 = nine or more), with total scores ranging from 0 to 30. Lower scores indicate a higher risk of social isolation, with a total score < 12 suggesting overall social isolation, and subscale scores < 6 indicating family or friend isolation. The LSNS-6 has been validated in diverse populations. In a study of 2819 older adults, Chang et al.25 reported good reliability and validity, with Cronbach's α coefficients of 0.83 for the total scale and 0.90 and 0.95 for the subscales, respectively. This study reported a Cronbach's α of 0.882.

Statistical analysis

We conducted the analysis using SPSS 26.0 and R version 4.3.2 statistical software. Non-normally distributed quantitative data were presented as medians and interquartile ranges, while categorical data were reported as frequencies and percentages. LPA was conducted to identify potential subgroups based on selected variables. The analysis began with a one-profile model and progressively increased the number of profiles until model fit indices no longer improved. Model selection was guided by the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and adjusted BIC (aBIC), with lower values indicating better model fit. Entropy values (range: 0–1) were used to assess classification accuracy, with values closer to 1 reflecting greater precision. The Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMRT) and the bootstrapped likelihood ratio test (BLRT) were applied to compare adjacent models, with P < 0.05 indicating that a k-class model fit significantly better than a k–1 class model. Moreover, between-group comparisons were conducted using the χ2 test, Fisher's exact test, or Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test, as appropriate. Multinomial logistic regression was further applied to examine the influencing factors of health-promoting lifestyle profiles among patients with colorectal cancer. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Subgroups identified by LPA

A total of 587 questionnaires were distributed, of which 561 were valid and complete, yielding an effective response rate of 95.6%. The data of 561 samples were used for the LPA analysis. Based on the six dimensions of the HPLP-II, LPA models with one to six profiles were estimated (Table 1). Although the five-class and six-class models showed relatively lower AIC, BIC, and aBIC values, and both the LMR and BLRT tests were significant (P < 0.05), their entropy values were below 0.8, indicating reduced classification accuracy. In addition, the additional classes in these models did not represent qualitatively distinct profiles, but rather appeared to be subdivisions of existing classes, with some classes showing relatively small proportions. Considering model fit, classification quality, clinical interpretability, and parsimony, the four-class model was selected as the optimal solution. Fig. 1 shows the distribution of potential characteristics of health-promoting lifestyles in patients with colorectal cancer. The first profile (n = 47; 8.4%) demonstrated consistently high scores across all dimensions, particularly in stress management and physical activity, and was labeled “the comprehensively positive high health-promotion”. The second profile (n = 233; 41.5%) showed generally low scores but performed close to average in nutrition and spiritual growth, which was labeled “the nutrition–spiritual growth retained low health-promotion”. The third profile (n = 81; 14.4%) had low scores across all dimensions, with the poorest performance in interpersonal relationships and spiritual growth, and was labeled “the globally low health-promotion”. The fourth profile (n = 200; 35.7%) scored around the average level across all dimensions, and was labeled “the balanced moderate health-promotion”.

Multinomial logistic regression model of factors influencing latent profiles of health-promoting lifestyles

Latent profile categories of health-promoting lifestyles patients with colorectal cancer were treated as the dependent variable, with Profile 1 selected as the reference category, as it represents the most favorable lifestyle pattern and serves as a clinically meaningful benchmark. Profiles 2, 3, and 4 were compared against Profile 1. Variables with statistical significance in univariate analyses were included as independent variables. As the test of parallel lines yielded P < 0.001, multinomial logistic regression was applied using a stepwise method to select predictors. The results indicated that smoking, depression, psychological frailty, friend isolation, and monthly income were significantly associated with the latent profiles of health-promoting lifestyles (P < 0.05; Table 3). Psychological frailty and friendship isolation were more likely to be associated with Profile 2 and Profile 3. Patients with high depression scores and who are currently smoking were more likely to be classified into Profile 3. Multinomial regression analysis indicated that, compared with the Profile 1, patients in the Profile 4 were significantly less likely to come from households with a per capita monthly income exceeding 9000 CNY.

Discussion

Main findings

The present study revealed that the mean score of health-promoting lifestyles among patients with colorectal cancer was 101.07 ± 18.26, with only 34 patients (6.1%) reaching the “excellent” level (130–160). This finding is comparable to that reported by Park et al.26 in 354 thyroid cancer patients (101.32 ± 15.09), but lower than the results of Wang's11 study on 324 patients with primary liver cancer (124.53 ± 23.08) and Huang et al.’s27 investigation of 1402 community-dwelling Chinese residents (111.19 ± 18.84). These results indicate that the overall level of health-promoting lifestyle among patients with colorectal cancer remains unsatisfactory. Individuals with colorectal cancer face not only the challenges associated with the malignancy and its treatments but also a range of more demanding issues, such as bowel dysfunction,7 body-image concerns,28 stoma care,29 and illness-related stigma.30 These factors can directly affect their physical activity, dietary management, and social engagement, which may partly explain the relatively low level of health-promoting lifestyle. This is consistent with previous evidence showing that colorectal cancer survivors often exhibit suboptimal engagement in health-promoting behaviors across multiple domains.31 Therefore, improving health-promoting lifestyle in patients with colorectal cancer is an urgent priority.

This study employed LPA to explore the heterogeneity of health-promoting lifestyles among patients with colorectal cancer and to identify the associated influencing factors. The analysis revealed four distinct lifestyle profiles: (1) comprehensively positive high health-promotion; (2) nutrition–spiritual growth retained low health-promotion; (3) globally low health-promotion; and (4) balanced moderate health-promotion. These findings not only illuminate the multidimensional nature of health-promoting lifestyles among patients with colorectal cancer but also underscore the existence of substantial intra-group variability. Previous study identified distinct latent classes of health-promoting lifestyles among breast cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy, including subgroups characterized by globally low or imbalanced behavioral patterns. These findings support the notion that health-promoting lifestyles are not homogeneous but cluster into distinct behavioral profiles across different cancer populations.32 Patients in the profile 1 and profile 4 retain the ability to engage in health-promoting behaviors across various dimensions. For these patients, more structured strategies may be beneficial, such as individualized goal-setting, regular self-monitoring (e.g., lifestyle diaries), and nurse-led follow-up or digital health support, which have been shown to improve adherence to health-promoting behaviors in chronic disease populations.33,34 Patients in Profile 4 represented a relatively balanced pattern across all dimensions of health-promoting behaviors. Although no specific domain was particularly deficient, the overall moderate level suggests room for improvement. Interventions targeting this group should emphasize enhancing motivation, self-efficacy, and sustained behavioral engagement to promote incremental improvements across domains. In contrast, patients in Profile 3 demonstrated globally low levels of health-promoting behaviors, particularly in interpersonal relationships and other domains, indicating the need for more intensive and multifaceted interventions, including structured behavioral counseling and psychological support.

Profile 2 (the nutrition-spiritual growth retained low health-promotion) represents the largest subgroup (41.5%) and warrants special attention. Individuals in this subgroup exhibit relatively intact nutrition and spiritual growth, but low levels of physical activity and interpersonal relationships, suggesting a pattern of behavioral imbalance. This pattern may be attributable to symptom burden and treatment-related fatigue, which are highly prevalent among patients with colorectal cancer and can persist long after treatment, thereby limiting participation in physical activity.35 Furthermore, social withdrawal is prevalent in this population, driven in part by the effects of stigma and reduced social motivation, which further constrain interpersonal interactions.36 Under these constraints, patients may turn to introverted coping strategies, such as dietary management and psychological adaptation, which are common adaptive responses following a cancer diagnosis.37 Notably, this group's retained capacity for spiritual growth could serve as a key entry point for intervention. Previous evidence suggests that psychosocial and behavioral interventions can improve lifestyle behaviors among colorectal cancer survivors.35 Therefore, approaches such as motivational interviewing or psychoeducational interventions may help translate these psychological strengths into broader health-promoting behaviors.

Multinomial logistic regression analysis revealed that patients with colorectal cancer who smoked were more likely to be classified into the third profile rather than the first. This finding aligns with previous studies showing that smokers exhibit significantly poorer health-promoting behaviors compared with non-smokers.38 Consistent with Han et al.’s findings among older adults, smoking may be associated with poorer development and maintenance of positive health behaviors.39 Nicotine dependence is strongly associated with negative emotions such as depression and anxiety, evidence suggests that smokers tend to experience higher emotional distress and stress reactivity than non-smokers.40,41 These psychological burdens may be associated with reduced motivation and capacity to engage in healthy behaviors. Moreover, patients with colorectal cancer often face multiple challenges during postoperative recovery and long-term follow-up, including pain, functional restoration, and social reintegration. The coexistence of smoking behaviors may be linked to lower risk perception and reduced willingness to engage in active self-management. Therefore, multilevel strategies combining health education, psychological counseling, and behavioral reinforcement may help patients gradually quit smoking, adopt healthier lifestyle patterns, and ultimately may support improvements in health behaviors.

Multinomial logistic regression analysis revealed that patients with colorectal cancer experiencing depressive symptoms were more likely to be classified into profile 3 characterized by lower levels of health-promoting lifestyles. Psychological distress, such as anxiety and depression, is common among colorectal cancer survivors and is strongly associated with their engagement in health-related behaviors.42 Previous studies have shown that breast cancer survivors with higher depression scores tend to exhibit significantly lower overall health-promoting lifestyle scores.43 Depression may be associated with poorer emotional regulation, lower self-efficacy, and reduced health locus of control, which may in turn relate to lower motivation and engagement in positive health behaviors.44,45 Among patients with colorectal cancer, physical discomfort, postoperative functional impairments, and fear of recurrence are major contributors to depressive symptoms. In such a state, individuals often fall into maladaptive behavioral patterns, such as reduced physical activity, neglect of nutritional balance, and poor self-management, which may contribute to a pattern characterized by “negative emotions–behavioral withdrawal–declining health”. Therefore, clinical nursing practice should prioritize depression screening and psychological intervention, integrating mental health management as a core component of health promotion programs to enhance overall recovery and quality of life.

Our study also found that psychological frailty and friendship isolation were more likely to be associated with Profile 2 and Profile 3. It should be noted that the psychological frailty may partially overlap with depressive symptoms, as both involve aspects of emotional functioning. However, psychological frailty represents a broader construct that includes not only emotional distress but also cognitive functioning and coping capacity. Previous studies have shown a significant negative association between psychological frailty and engagement in health-related behaviors.46 Individuals with higher psychological frailty tend to have lower self-efficacy and a diminished sense of control, which may be associated with reduced ability to adhere to beneficial behaviors such as regular physical activity, healthy dietary patterns, and active self-management.47 In addition, a lack of emotional and informational support from peers or communities may leave patients feeling isolated and helpless when coping with illness and recovery, potentially contributing to a pattern of psychological frailty, social disconnection, and behavioral withdrawal.48 Moreover, friendship isolation, rather than family isolation, showed a stronger association with less favorable lifestyle profiles. This difference may be explained by the distinct functional roles of different sources of social support.49 While family support primarily provides emotional stability and caregiving, peer support is more closely linked to behavioral engagement and social participation, which are directly linked to health-promoting behaviors.50 Therefore, clinical interventions should focus on psychological empowerment and social reconnection.

Implications for nursing practice and research

This study used LPA to identify different subgroups of patients with colorectal cancer with varying health-promoting lifestyle habits, thereby highlighting the need for stratified and tailored care interventions rather than uniform approaches. These findings provide a practical framework for nurses to implement profile-based interventions in clinical settings, with strategies tailored to patients’ specific behavioral patterns and psychosocial characteristics identified in this study. For patients with poor health-promoting behaviours overall, especially those exhibiting psychological frailty and social isolation, interventions should focus primarily on psychological strengthening, social reconnection and behavioural motivation strategies. From a scientific perspective, longitudinal studies are needed to examine the dynamic development of lifestyle categories and their long-term impact on outcomes such as quality of life, recurrence and survival rates. Future multicentre studies with larger and more diverse samples are also needed to validate the stability of latent profiles and develop precise intervention models that integrate psychological and social determinants, thereby advancing individualised health management and continuous recovery for patients with colorectal cancer.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, due to its cross-sectional design, causal inferences among the variables cannot be drawn. Secondly, the study was conducted in a single regional and hospital setting, which may limit the representativeness and generalizability of the findings to other cultural or clinical contexts. Lastly, the identified latent profiles are sample-dependent, meaning that the number and structure of the profiles may vary across different populations or indicator selections. Therefore, future large-scale, multi-center studies are warranted to validate the stability and generalizability of the current classification framework.

Conclusions

This study identified four distinct latent profiles of health-promoting lifestyles among patients with colorectal cancer: comprehensively positive high health-promotion, nutrition–spiritual growth retained low health-promotion, globally low health-promotion and balanced moderate health-promotion. Patients with higher psychological frailty and social isolation were more likely to belong to low levels of health-promoting behaviors. These findings provide empirical evidence to support the development of targeted interventions according to different characteristics and corelletes of health-promoting lifestyles.
