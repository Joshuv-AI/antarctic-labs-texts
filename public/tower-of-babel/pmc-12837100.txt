Safety and efficacy of an extended macular vision intraocular lens (IOL) for age-related macular degeneration (AMD) in real-world settings
Authors: Fritz H Hengerer, Boris Stanzel, Jairo Hoyos-Chacon, Marta Suarez, Sebastian Beckers, Patrizia Busatto, Federico Badalà
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo assess the safety and efficacy of the EyeMax Mono (SharpView Ophthalmology) extended macular vision intraocular lens (IOL), in patients with age-related macular degeneration (AMD) undergoing cataract surgery.MethodsThis retrospective multicentre study analyzed real-world data from 196 AMD eyes (146 patients) across 15 medical centres in Europe. All eyes received the single-piece, hydrophobic acrylic, EyeMax Mono IOL. AMD severity was classified by the centre. Outcomes included intraoperative and postoperative complications and changes in corrected distance and near visual acuity (CDVA and CNVA, respectively).ResultsThe postoperative spherical equivalent was + 1.25 ± 1.32 D. Postoperative LogMAR CDVA improved significantly from a baseline of 0.57 ± 0.38 to 0.38 ± 0.34 (n = 191, p < 0.001), while CNVA improved from 0.49 ± 0.32 at baseline to 0.35 ± 0.27 postoperatively (n = 141, p < 0.001). 63% gained > 1 line of CDVA and 27.5% gained ≥ 3 lines. For CNVA, 41.5% gained > 1 line, and 21.8% gained ≥ 3 lines. The greatest gains occurred in intermediate AMD eyes: CDVA + 0.21 logMAR, CNVA + 0.12 logMAR. Only 2.1% of eyes lost > 1 line of CDVA or CNVA.ConclusionsThe EyeMax Mono intraocular lens (IOL) safely enhances both distant (CDVA) and near (CNVA) visual acuity in patients with age-related macular degeneration, particularly in eyes with intermediate AMD. These findings are consistent with those in the published scientific literature.Keywords: AMD, Extended macular vision, Low-vision aids, Age-related macular degeneration, Cataract, Intraocular lens, Preferred retinal loci, PRL, Eccentric fixation, EyeMax Mono, IOL, Phacoemulsification

Introduction

Age-related macular degeneration is the leading cause of legal blindness, as it affects over 33.6 million people in Europe alone, [1] and it is estimated that it will affect 288 million people worldwide by 2040 [2]. AMD reduces patient independence and increases rates of accidents as well as cognitive decline, depression and mortality [3, 4]. The burden of AMD on patients, their families, and health care systems is growing as life expectancy is prolonged [5].

Current visual rehabilitation options for AMD include low-vision aids such as magnifiers, telescopic lenses, and electronic devices. These aids aim to optimise residual vision for daily activities but are often difficult to use and disrupt image stability and the vestibular ocular response, which coordinates eye movement with head movement [6, 7]. Implantable devices offer benefits by accommodating natural eye movements and preserving the visual field; however, their implementation requires complex surgical skills and is associated with notable complication rates [8].

Individuals with AMD and concurrent cataracts are typically offered standard intraocular lenses (IOLs), which, while safe, these standard IOLs are not tailored to meet the needs of patients with a non-functional fovea [9]. Finding treatments that help AMD patients cope with vision loss, adapt to the disease as it progresses, and make the best use of their remaining vision by supporting natural eye adaptations like preferred retinal loci (PRLs) is an important area of research. Such interventions hold the potential to significantly improve the quality of life for AMD patients.

The EyeMax Mono (Sharpview Ophthalmology, London, UK) is an extended macular vision (EMV) IOL specifically designed to improve image quality at PRLs located within 10° of eccentricity. It helps the patient develop a single or multiple PRLs in healthy areas of the retina [10]. It is a single-piece, soft, hydrophobic acrylic IOL resembling standard monofocal IOLs in implantation technique as well as safety profile except that it features wavefront-optimised optics that are designed to widen the focus and optimise image across a large area of the retina [10, 11].

The main objective of this multicentre study was to evaluate the safety and efficacy of the EyeMax Mono in treating eyes with AMD and to determine its effectiveness for different severities of the condition.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective multicenter study used real-world evidence (RWE) data to evaluate the safety and visual outcomes of patients with AMD who received the EyeMax Mono (SharpView Ophthalmology, London, UK). The study adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, Good Clinical Practice (GCP), and applicable local regulations. All patients were identified by the surgeons from routine clinical records, and no active recruitment was performed: data were collected during the company’s post-market surveillance program, ensuring real-world representativeness. Given the retrospective design of the study and the use of fully anonymised data, the requirement for informed consent was waived, as it was not feasible to obtain consent for secondary research use of the data in some cases. All surgeries were performed between February 2019 and February 2025 at 15 medical centers in Europe: Clínica Suarez Leoz (Spain), Deutsche Augenklinik Mallorca (Spain), Patrizia Busatto private clinic (Italy), Instituto Oftalmológico Hoyos (Spain), Knappschaft Eye Clinic Sulzbach (Germany), Micro Chirurgia Oculare (Italy), and Øjenhospitalet Danmark (Denmark), Clinica Baviera (Spain), Clinica Oftalmologica Dr Olea (Spain), Institut de la Macula (Spain), Klinika Okulistyczna VITA-MED (Poland), Miranza IOA (Spain), Clínicas Mi ES (Spain), Policlinico Gemelli (Italy), and Vithas (Spain).

Patients

The study included patients with AMD who underwent cataract extraction and were implanted with an EyeMax Mono IOL. Eyes with no healthy retina within 10° from the fovea were excluded. Eyes were categorised by the surgeons according to AMD severity into early, intermediate, and late groups based on the classification by Ferris III et al. using fundus photography and OCT images [12]. Patient consent for cataract surgery in the presence of macular disease was obtained following the established protocols. Specifically, patients were informed about the limited benefits in cases of extensive central macular involvement and the potential advantages of image optimization when an established preferred retinal locus existed, or the possibility of developing reliance on one in the future. Patients were also advised on the limitations of optical approaches for progressive disease and informed about the expected gradual visual impairment due to the underlying retinal condition. Additionally, they were notified that IOL implantation would neither prevent the progression of retinal pathology nor cure it, with the primary goal being to optimise existing visual ability.

Data were anonymized and aggregated for analysis. Outcome measures included intraoperative and postoperative complications, postoperative spherical equivalent, preoperative, postoperative, and change in corrected distance, and near visual acuity (CDVA and CNVA, respectively), and intraocular pressure (IOP). Visual acuity was measured using Snellen charts for CDVA and either Jaeger or Radner charts for CNVA, depending on the center. For analysis, all visual acuity was converted to logMAR using the conversion table described by Patel et al. [13]

Safety was assessed by reporting intra- and postoperative complications, and efficacy was evaluated by comparing pre- and post-operative corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA) and corrected near visual acuity (CNVA).

The EyeMax Mono IOL

The EyeMax Mono IOL is a single-piece intraocular lens that received CE mark in 2016. It is designed for implantation in the capsular bag and features wavefront-optimised optics that minimise image blur across all areas of the macula, extending 10° from the foveal center [10]. This provides a retinal area of focus that is 4 times larger than that of standard monofocal IOLs, which have poor image quality outside the central 4 degrees from the foveal center [11].

The dioptric power of the EyeMax Mono IOL affects the curvature of its posterior and anterior surfaces. As the dioptric power increases, the posterior surface becomes more curved, while the anterior surface changes from positive to flatter and then to negative curvature. Detailed descriptions of the extended macular vision IOL are available in the literature [11].

Surgical intervention

​Refractive targets of either emmetropia or modest hypermetropia were determined based on the severity of macular disease and the refractive error in the untreated eye, as decided by the surgeon. For instance, aiming for modest hypermetropia of approximately + 2 to + 3 diopters (D) after surgery resulting in approximately 1.1 to 1.2× magnification when corrected with plus lenses [11]. This strategy leverages spectacle-induced magnification to enhance detail perception, particularly beneficial in patients with central vision loss due to AMD, where larger images can improve functional vision despite compromised foveal function. ​Patients targeted for modest hypermetropia were advised about increased reliance on single-vision distance and near glasses postoperatively.

The power of the intraocular lens (IOL) was calculated using the Haigis formula and an A-constant of 119.2. The implantation procedure was conducted using standard phacoemulsification cataract surgery, and the extended macular vision IOL was inserted into the capsular bag through a 2.2 mm or clear corneal incision, depending on the lens thickness.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was performed using Jamovi version Version 2.3 and Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, USA). Descriptive statistics, in the form of mean ± standard deviation, were used to summarise the changes in CDVA and CNVA. Normality of the data was tested using Shapiro-Wilk test. A paired non-parametric test (Wilcoxon signed-rank test) was used to compare CDVA and CNVA preoperatively versus the last follow-up because the data were not normally distributed. The change in visual acuity was calculated as the difference between preoperative and postoperative logMAR values for each eye, with the mean of these differences reported. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. LogMAR values were converted to ETDRS letters using the formula from the Beck study, [14] where logMAR = 1.7 – (0.02)(letter score).

Results

Data from 196 eyes of 146 patients were analysed, with 50 patients undergoing bilateral EMV IOL implantation. All surgeries were performed between February 2019 and February 2025 by 15 surgeons at 15 European centres. All eyes had dry AMD and were classified into early, intermediate, and late groups based on AMD severity. Patients were followed up ranging from 1 month to 2 years. ​Table 2a shows the changes in visual and refractive outcomes following EyeMax Mono IOL implantation. The mean postoperative SE was + 1.25 ± 1.32 D. CDVA (logMAR) significantly improved from 0.57 ± 0.38 to 0.38 ± 0.34 (p < 0.001), equivalent to a mean improvement of 0.19 ± 0.22 logMAR (9.52 ETDRS letters) with 55% of eyes gaining 2 or more lines (Fig. 1a). Similarly, CNVA (logMAR) showed significant improvement from 0.49 ± 0.32 to 0.35 ± 0.27 (6.76 ETDRS letters) (p < 0.001) with 36% of eyes gaining 2 or more lines (Fig. 1a). ​Additionally, 47% of eyes achieved a postoperative CDVA of 0.2 or better compared to 19% preoperatively (Fig. 2a), and 41% achieved a postoperative CNVA of 0.2 or better compared to 18% preoperatively (Fig. 2b).

Visual acuity changes according to severity

As shown in Table 2b, CDVA and CNVA significantly improved across all severity groups. The mean CDVA gains (logMAR, calculated using the mean of the paired values) in the early, intermediate, and late AMD groups were 0.15, 0.21, and 0.18, respectively (p≤0.003). The mean CNVA gains (logMAR) in the early, intermediate, and late AMD groups were 0.11, 0.12, and 0.08, respectively (p≤0.017). Figure 1b shows a gain of more than 1 CDVA line in 51–58% of eyes. Figure 1c shows that 35–61% of eyes across all severity groups gained one or more lines of CNVA.

Visual acuity gains according to refractive outcome

To evaluate the influence of magnification, the study delineated two distinct subgroups based on postoperative spherical equivalent (SE) values. The first subgroup encompassed 41 eyes with a postoperative SE within the range of ± 0.5 D (termed the emmetropic group), while the second subset comprised 62 eyes with postoperative SE values of + 2 D or higher (designated as the hypermetropic group). 46.3% of the emmetrope group and 45.2% of the hypermetrope group were intermediate AMD eyes – most of the remaining eyes of the emmetrope group did not have AMD stage recorded. CDVA gains were 0.21 ± 0.25 (n = 41) and 0.21 ± 0.24 (n = 62) for the emmetrope and hypermetrope groups, respectively, and CNVA gains were 0.18 ± 0.24 (n = 19) and 0.14 ± 0.19 (n = 52), respectively. There were no statistically significant differences in CDVA and CNVA between the two groups (p = 0.969 and p = 0.474, respectively). Also, postoperative SE was not correlated with CDVA and CNVA gains.

Safety

Only two eyes (1%) experienced mild intraoperative complications: one eye had a slight zonulopathy that caused difficulty in centering the IOL, and in the other case, the IOL was noted to have a small scratch near the edge of the capsulorhexis. No postoperative complications were identified. None of the eyes required IOL exchange, and no patients experienced unexpected levels of post-surgical discomfort. Four eyes (2%) lost more than one line of logMAR CDVA, and three eyes (1.5%) lost more than one line of CNVA (logMAR) (Fig. 1a). Mean intraocular pressure (IOP) was stable, measured at 14.59 ± 2.30 mmHg preoperatively and 13.43 ± 2.61 mmHg postoperatively (p < 0.014) (Table 2).

Discussion

This is the first retrospective multicentre real-world study to assess the safety and efficacy of the EyeMax Mono in patients across early, intermediate, and late stages of AMD. Notably, 55% of the eyes gained two or more CDVA lines, while 36% gained two or more CNVA lines. The largest gains were achieved in the intermediate AMD group with a mean gain of 0.21 logMAR and 0.12 logMAR in CDVA and CNVA, respectively. Furthermore, 55% of eyes achieved a CDVA of 0.3 or better versus 28% preoperatively, and 57% achieved a CNVA of 0.3 or better versus 38% preoperatively.

In the current study, the mean gain of CDVA was 0.19 logMAR (9.52 ETDRS letters), which was less than the gain reported in other EyeMax Mono studies. For instance, Qureshi et al. reported that the mean CDVA gain was 0.35 logMAR [10]. Similarly, a mean CDVA gain of 0.34 logMAR and 0.33 logMAR was reported in Robbie et al. and Gobeka and Erakgün studies, respectively [11, 15]. This disparity may be attributed to the fact that the mean preoperative CDVA was better in the current study (0.57 logMAR) compared to the studies mentioned above (1.06, 0.93, and 1.05 logMAR, respectively), suggesting that the AMD or cataract was more advanced in those studies, making the effect of the IOL or the cataract removal more pronounced.

We reference our results to the AREDS study to illustrate expected outcomes with standard IOLs in the setting of retinal disease [16]. In AREDS, mean CDVA improvements with standard IOLs were 6.13, 3.92, and 1.94 ETDRS letters for early, intermediate, and late AMD, respectively. The current study reported gains of 7.71, 10.64, and 8.58 ETDRS letters, for the same categories (Table 3). It is important to emphasise that the AREDS employed a more rigorous and standardised methodology, including consistent documentation of cataract grade and follow-up duration, which limits the validity of direct comparison. Nevertheless, the visual acuity gains in intermediate and late AMD observed in the present study suggest that the EyeMax Mono IOL’s optical design may confer an additional functional benefit over a standard IOL. While the efficacy of standard IOLs may decline as AMD progresses, extended macular vision IOLs could potentially preserve visual function over a longer period. However, a prospective comparative study is necessary to scientifically confirm this hypothesis.

The mean CNVA gain in this study (0.14 logMAR) was less than the gain in Qureshi et al. (0.48 logMAR) and Robbie et al. (0.32 logMAR) [10, 11]. Similarly, this difference can be attributed to the worse preoperative CNVA in the two studies mentioned (1.36 and 1.05 logMAR respectively compared to 0.49 logMAR in the current study).

Interestingly, CDVA gains in the early AMD group were lower than those in the late AMD group, which may be explained by a ceiling effect [17]. In contrast, CNVA gains were greater in the early AMD group, likely reflecting relatively preserved central retinal function, which supports near vision tasks such as reading. These findings highlight the importance of setting realistic patient expectations based on disease stage and visual function.

Based on empirical experience with standard implantation of the EyeMax Mono, a degree of postoperative hypermetropia is often anticipated to enhance visual acuity through spectacle-induced magnification. In Qureshi et al., the mean postoperative spherical equivalent was + 2.7 D, and in Robbie et al. it was + 2.9 D. [10, 11] This can provide up to 1.2x magnification [10]. However, in the current study, there were no statistically significant differences in CDVA and CNVA gains between emmetropic eyes (with a postoperative spherical equivalent within ± 0.5 D, n = 41) and hypermetropic eyes (with a postoperative spherical equivalent of at least 2 D, n = 62).

The current study focused on evaluating the safety profile of the EyeMax Mono IOL. Only 1.5% of patients encountered mild intraoperative issues and no postoperative complications, comparable to that of a standard IOL. Furthermore, 2.1% of the eyes experienced a drop of more than one line in logMAR CDVA, and an equal percentage (2.1%) experienced a similar decline in CNVA. Notably, the average intraocular pressure remained stable postoperatively. Hence, the safety profile of the EyeMax Mono IOL was found to be satisfactory, which was also reported by Qureshi et al. [10]. This low rate of complications and visual deterioration is noteworthy and encouraging given that all eyes in the cohort were affected by pre-existing retinal disease.

AMD patients typically receive a standard IOL when they need cataract extraction, [9]. which focuses light on the fovea. However, as AMD progresses and the fovea becomes affected, the standard IOL loses its effectiveness [18]. Even initial visual acuity gains from the removal of the cataract are expected to be eroded by the disease progression. Moreover, a standard IOL may worsen visual acuity by focusing the light away from the PRLs that develop to compensate for the diseased fovea [9]. In contrast, an extended macular vision IOL is designed to distribute the focus across the central 10° of the macula—targeting areas of the retina that may still retain function. While relying on PRLs outside the fovea inevitably results in reduced image clarity compared to foveal vision, this trade-off reflects the constraints imposed by the underlying disease.

Moreover, patients with a stationary or progressive macular disease other than AMD (such as Stargardt disease, Macular Telangiectasia Type 2 or acquired vitelliform lesions) are similar to AMD in many aspects, [19] and can be offered an extended macular vision IOL, which can be especially beneficial for younger patients with longer life expectancy.

This study had several limitations. As a retrospective case series with variable follow-up times (ranging from 1 month to 2 years), we could not evaluate the trajectory of visual acuity over time or account for potential disease progression. However, since the primary outcome was defined as the change in visual acuity from preoperative to final postoperative visit — and not as a function of time — the reported improvements reflect the net effect of surgery and IOL implantation, which remains clinically meaningful. To ensure reliable assessment of visual outcomes, only eyes with both preoperative and postoperative CDVA and CNVA measurements were included in the analysis; eyes with missing data at either time point were excluded. While this approach enhances the validity of within-patient comparisons, it may introduce selection bias and limits generalizability. This study is also limited by the lack of randomization, lack of a control group, and its retrospective nature, which contributed to missing data regarding cataract density, but our primary interest was to evaluate the safety of the IOL which is not affected by the grade of cataract. Despite these limitations, this large multicentre real-world dataset provides valuable insights into the safety and efficacy of the EyeMax Mono IOL in AMD patients undergoing cataract surgery. Further work should investigate the performance of the EyeMax Mono IOL in other macular diseases, such as macular holes and the Best disease, and should include prospective comparisons between the EyeMax Mono IOL and standard IOLs in order to establish a stronger evidence base for IOL selection in patients with AMD.

We believe that the main goal of this study is to report an overall safety profile in a variety of settings as well as describe the lens’ use in real-world settings. This study highlights the EyeMax Mono IOL as a safe and effective intervention, demonstrating substantial enhancements in distant (CDVA) and near (CNVA) visual acuity especially in eyes with intermediate AMD. This extended macular vision IOL not only aligns with the safety benchmarks set by traditional IOLs, but also presents promising prospects as a choice for improving and preserving visual function in individuals with AMD undergoing cataract surgery.
