Comprehensive trends in Chronic Kidney Disease prevalence and comorbidities across eGFR and albuminuria categories: a 20-year analysis of NHANES data
Authors: Youssef MK Farag, Zihe Zheng, Aqsa H Dar, Anam Tariq
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic kidney disease (CKD) represents a major public health challenge, contributing to over 1.2 million global deaths annually and ranking as the 12th leading cause of mortality. This study provides a 20-year trend analysis (1999–2018) of CKD prevalence and its associated comorbidities among adults in the United States using the NHANES dataset. Employing the CKD-EPI 2021 equation without the race component, the study revealed a stable CKD prevalence of 20% in adults aged ≥20 years, alongside trends in nine comorbidities. Obesity and diabetes showed significant increases, while hypertension, anemia, hyperkalemia, and cardiovascular disease remained stable. Hypertriglyceridemia and hypercholesterolemia exhibited an initial rise followed by a plateau. The analysis highlights the uneven distribution of CKD comorbidities across KDIGO-defined eGFR and albuminuria subcategories, with G3bA2 and G4A1 patients at heightened risk. These findings underscore the importance of targeted interventions through an individualized care strategy that considers CKD risk category and the comorbidities profiles. Additionally, it offers pivotal epidemiological insights to inform clinical research, policymaking, and precision medicine in nephrology.Keywords: Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD), Estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate (eGFR), Albuminuria, Diabetes Mellitus, Anemia, Hyperkalemia

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a critical public health threat resulting in 1.2 million deaths globally and was ranked the 10th leading cause of mortality in 2021 [1]. In the United States (U.S.) alone, CKD affects over 37 million people [2]. The trend in the adjusted prevalence of CKD in the U.S. population was relatively stable during 2003 and 2014 [3]. However, the damage or loss of kidney function from CKD profoundly impacts multiple organs and increases risks for different comorbidities, including cardiovascular diseases, end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) and all-cause mortality [4,5]. Evaluation of CKD prevalence and related comorbidities by CKD stages with the race-free estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) equation [6,7] and assessing its trends over time are pivotal to the ascertainment and management of CKD progression. Patient segmentation and individualizing evolving therapies to manage CKD and its related comorbidities is critical to provide the best treatments available to the right patients.

There is a lack of consensus regarding the prevalence of clinically relevant comorbidities among people with CKD and whether they varied over time [8,9]. The goal of our study is to characterize the prevalence of common comorbidities in CKD population and to analyze the most recent nationally representative data in the U.S. population to provide important benchmark epidemiological data for current and future CKD research, randomized clinical trials, and inform individualized management of high-risk CKD subgroups. We evaluated the trends of CKD and its comorbidities in NHANES with the cumulative data over two decades, from 1999 to 2018, and described the prevalence of these comorbidities among the various stages of CKD by eGFR and albuminuria.

Methods

Data sources and study population

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is a population-based survey of non-institutionalized U.S. civilians. Conducted by the National Center of Health Statistics (NCHS) of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), NHANES uses stratified, multistage probability sampling to obtain nationally representative health and nutritional information. NHANES was approved by the NCHS Institutional Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Since 1999–2000, NHANES has adopted a 2-year survey cycle. In this analysis, we combined data from the most recent 10 survey cycles (from 1999 to 2000 to 2017–2018) and restricted study population to adults ≥ 20 years old who completed the NHANES interview, examination, and laboratory measurements and didn’t report receiving dialysis. More details can be found in the Supplemental Materials.

CKD status and CKD-related comorbidities

We calculated creatinine-based race-free eGFR using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration Equation (2021) [6]. Measurements of serum creatinine were corrected to a shared reference standard for NHANES cycles 1999–2000, 2005–2006 and 2017–2018 according to analytical guidelines [10–12]. We defined CKD as having a single measure of eGFR < 60 mL/min per 1.73 m2 or urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) ≥ 30 mg/g. We selected and defined nine prevalent and clinically relevant comorbidities related to CKD as binary variables based on NHANES laboratory, examination, and self-reported interview data: hypercholesterolemia, hypertension, obesity, hypertriglyceridemia, gout, diabetes, composite cardiovascular disease (CVD) endpoints (coronary heart disease, angina, heart attack, stroke, or heart failure), anemia, and hyperkalemia. The detailed definition of each comorbidity is listed in supplemental Table S1.

Statistical analysis

The final unweighted analytical sample included 48,153 participants for estimating CKD prevalence and 12,016 individuals for estimating the prevalence of related comorbidities among individuals with CKD. We calculated the 20-year (from 1999 to 2000 to 2017–2018) NHANES examination sample weight with the recommended method [13]. We estimated the prevalence of the different stages of CKD in the general U.S. population by the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) [14]. Within each CKD category, defined by eGFR and albuminuria individually and jointly, we estimated the prevalence of the nine CKD-related comorbidities. To examine the trend and stability of the CKD-related comorbidity prevalence over time, we estimated the prevalence of the nine comorbidities among people with CKD in each of the ten 2-year cycles and displayed the data with a stacked line chart. In this analysis, the 2-year NHANES examination sample weight was used.

In the sensitivity analyses, to test the robustness of our results of the contemporary prevalence estimates, we repeated the estimation for the prevalence of CKD and CKD-related comorbidities using data only from the last three cycles (from 2013 to 2014 to 2017–2018), applying the corresponding 6-year NHANES examination sample weight. To estimate the prevalence of diabetes among people with CKD, we used two alternative definitions that excluded the criteria of random plasma glucose (non-fasting) ≥ 200 g/dL (alternative definition 1) or included fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL (alternative definition 2) (supplemental Table S1). We generated 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for all prevalence estimates. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata 16 (StataCorp).

Results

Using the new race-free eGFR CKD-EPI creatinine equation, the average prevalence of CKD among US adults (≥ 20 years old) was estimated to be 20.0% from 1999 to 2018 (Figure 1a) which plateaued over the past decade (Table S2). Stratified by the KDIGO 2012 eGFR and albuminuria categories, the top three prevalent CKD subcategories in the U.S. from 1999 to 2018 were people with normal or high eGFR and moderately increased albuminuria (4.23%, G1&A2), people with mildly to moderately decreased eGFR and normal to mildly increased albuminuria (2.79%, G3a&A1), and people with mildly decreased eGFR and moderately increased albuminuria (2.58%, G2&A2) (Figure 1a), respectively. Individuals with high risk (2.31%-total percent of the orange regions) or very high risk (1.12%-total percent of the red regions) CKD account for 3.43% of the adult US population. The estimates for the prevalence of CKD subcategory were similar between the 20-year cycle versus the most recent 6-year cycle in NHANES (Figure S1).

In the sensitivity analysis, the estimates for the prevalence of diabetes among individuals with CKD using different definitions yielded similar result patterns for high-risk CKD subcategories (Table S4). Incorporating fasting glucose ≥ 126 g/dL in the sensitivity definition of diabetes increased the estimates for its prevalence (23.2%, 95% CI 21.5–25.0) compared to the main analysis definition (23.3%, 95% CI 21.5–25.0) (Table S4). The prevalence estimates for CKD-associated comorbidities using the most recent NHANES cycles from 2013 to 2018 were similar to the main analysis, except for a higher prevalence of obesity (53.2%, 95% CI 48.9–57.4) among individuals with CKD in more recent years (Table S5).

Discussion

In this study, we found that the prevalence of CKD in the US, estimated from NHANES, in the adult population ≥ 20 years of age remained relatively stable over the past two decades. Using the new race-free eGFR equation [6,7], the prevalence of CKD was 20% among US adults, an estimate 5% higher than using the race-based eGFR equation [15]. The most prevalent CKD subcategories included people with eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min per 1.73 m2 and albuminuria between 30 and 300 mg/g, and individuals with eGFR between 45 and 59 mL/min per 1.73 m2 and albuminuria < 30 mg/g. Our results showed increasing trends for two CKD-related comorbidities (obesity and diabetes), stable trends for five CKD-related comorbidities (gout, hypertension, hyperkalemia, composite CVD and anemia), and an increasing trend followed by a plateau for hypertriglyceridemia and hypercholesterolemia among people with CKD, ascertained by eGFR, albuminuria, and self-report information in NHANES.

Despite the increasing trend of hypertension and diabetes, a recent NHANES study analyzing data from 1999 to 2012 also concluded that the prevalence of CKD at all stages has plateaued [16]. The plateau of CKD prevalence is assumed to be the result of earlier screening, and increased diagnostic and treatment options, including dialysis and transplantation [16]. However, Kibria et al. did not examine the trend or prevalence of CKD-related comorbidities over time or by kidney function subgroups in their study.

CKD poses a global healthcare challenge, which led the United Nations to prioritize reducing the prevalence of CKD within their sustainable development goals [17]. To address the significant challenges to achieve the goal, careful management and monitoring of CKD, along with the key comorbidities among people with CKD, including those examined in this study and beyond, are needed. Most clinical guidelines focus on single diseases, which often leads to conflicting recommendations for different conditions. This can result in multiple medications being prescribed without clear guidance on how to prioritize these recommendations for individuals who may face an overwhelming treatment burden [18]. Our findings provide the nephrology and primary care communities with important benchmark epidemiological context of the prevalence of key comorbidities among people with CKD, which will help them in proactively controlling comorbidities that are common in each stage. For instance, obesity should be proactively controlled at the ealy stages (i.e. G1), reflecting the cardio-kidney-metabolic continuum [19].

Prompt diagnosis and long-term management of CKD will help identify patients at higher risk of developing End Stage Kidney disease (i.e. KDIGO stages G3, G4, G5, A2, A3) who need closer monitoring. Closer monitoring may additionally help lessen the cost burden of treatments, including the long-term costs associated with management of the CKD-associated comorbidities. By focusing on the CKD-related comorbidities (e.g. hypertension, diabetes, gout, obesity, composite CVD), treatment can be tailored and individualized to help decrease the degree and speed of kidney damage. Meanwhile, this nationally representative data has revealed the different distributions of concurrent chronic conditions in various CKD subcategories. The comprehensive prevalence data generated from this analysis could assist the design of future observational studies to obtain real world evidence, inform the participant recruitment and safety evaluation in randomized clinical trials, and guide providers in the clinical decision making for various CKD subgroups.

There are several limitations. First, related to the use of NHANES data, differential nonresponse, particularly lower response among sicker individuals, could have biased the prevalence estimates. This bias turned to be most plausible for the more advanced stages of CKD and thus, the prevalence of CKD stage 4 and 5 may be underestimated among individuals who volunteered to participate in NHANES. Second, the definition of CKD in NHANES was based on single measurement of eGFR and urine albumin instead of repeat measurements within a three-month window. Third, given the limited nature of data collection in NHANES, we were only able to examine selected CKD-related comorbidities. In contrast, the strengths of this analysis are the scope of duration, including survey data from the last two decades and the utilization of the newest race-free CKD-EPI eGFR equation in defining CKD, which generated a more accurate characterization of CKD and its comorbidities for African Americans [6]. Additionally, we also ran several sensitivity analyses with various definitions to test the robustness of our findings.

Conclusion

The prevalence of CKD in the U.S. was stable over the past two decades. Although the major CKD-related comorbidities only slightly increased over time or plateaued in recent years, they had disproportionately impacted various CKD subcategories, defined by eGFR and albuminuria. Our nationally representative estimation of the prevalence of CKD and CKD-related comorbidities could inform future clinical research, drug development, and improvements to the management of patients in primary care and nephrology care settings.
