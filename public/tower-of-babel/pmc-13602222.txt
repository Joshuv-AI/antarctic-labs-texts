Applying ComBat harmonization to ultrasomics enhances machine learning generalizability and performance in multi-center clinical settings
Authors: Shouzhi Lin, Mengyao Cai, Ningni Jiang, Huahui Liu, Shuang Liang, Fengyu Ye, Xinxin Lin, Wei Wang, Erjiao Xu, Hangtong Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryUltrasomics features suffer from multi-source batch effects across centers, including scanning parameters, scanners, and operator variability, limiting the generalizability of machine learning models and impeding their clinical translation. In this study, we systematically validated ComBat harmonization for reducing ultrasomics heterogeneity with a tiered validation framework: phantoms, retrospective clinical thyroid studies, and prospective multi-center human studies. ComBat reduced the proportion of heterogeneous features to below 51%. The concordance correlation coefficients were improved by up to 135%. When models were retrained on harmonized data using features selected pre-harmonization, average AUCs were significantly improved; further gains from optimized feature selection post-harmonization increased average AUCs to up to 0.93, with robust effectiveness across both population screening and high-risk referral settings. Our study suggests that ComBat harmonization can effectively reduce technical heterogeneity in ultrasomics features, serving as a promising tool to enhance the generalizability of machine learning models in ultrasomics research.Keywords: ComBat harmonization, ultrasomics, heterogeneity, machine learning, clinical generalizability and performance

Introduction

Radiomics is a prevalent and crucial strategy that extracts vast arrays of quantitative features from medical images, enabling relationships between image features and disease status to be clarified through a series of analyses.1,2,3 This capability not only enhances diagnostic accuracy but also significantly contributes to personalized medicine by facilitating tailored treatment strategies based on individual imaging biomarkers.

However, a critical challenge in radiomics is the reproducibility of extraction outcomes, also known as heterogeneity.4,5 Specifically, variations in imaging protocols, technical configurations, scanners or institutions can cause significant variations in the distribution of radiomics features across datasets, which are referred to as batch effects. Unlike computed tomography (CT) and MRI, where imaging physics (X-ray attenuation and magnetic resonance signals) and acquisition protocols are highly standardized across vendors and centers, ultrasound imaging is inherently operator dependent and heavily influenced by acoustic wave propagation. Variations in probe pressure, angle, gain, focus, and frequency can dramatically alter speckle patterns, tissue echogenicity, and texture appearance—even when imaging the same anatomical structure. Consequently, ultrasomics, a branch of radiomics that focuses on ultrasound imaging, encounters far more severe feature heterogeneity than CT- or MRI-based radiomics. Our prior research further demonstrates that adjustments in routine ultrasound parameters (e.g., gain, focus, and frequency) and the use of scanners from different manufacturers can significantly affect the reproducibility of ultrasomics features.6 Moreover, unlike the standardized procedures of CT and MRI, the ultrasound (US) image quality is significantly influenced by the operator as well as by patient factors, such as body type, positioning, and breathing. Such challenge impedes its effective application from model discovery to improved diagnostics, patient stratification, and monitoring through ultrasomics-based approaches.7,8

Given these substantial challenges, addressing the issue of heterogeneity in ultrasomics is both urgent and critical. A feature-level harmonization method known as ComBat, originally introduced from genomics, has been widely validated and adopted in the fields of CT, MRI, and positron emission tomography (PET) radiomics.9,10,11,12,13 ComBat harmonization uses a linear model and empirical Bayes shrinkage to decompose data into biological effects, batch effects, and noise. This approach adjusts the additive (location) and multiplicative (scale) batch effects, while preserving the biological signal and accounting for covariates, such as age, gender, and disease status, thereby enhancing the performance of machine learning models.14,15,16 This offered hope for resolving the challenges of heterogeneity.

Although ComBat harmonization has been extensively validated in CT, MRI, and PET radiomics, applying ComBat in ultrasomics may introduce uncertainties due to complex data integration, algorithm adaptation to ultrasound properties, biological interpretation, management of computational resources, and clinical application.17 These distinct sources of heterogeneity mean that prior findings cannot be directly extrapolated to ultrasomics. In this study, the effectiveness of ComBat in reducing heterogeneity arising from varied ultrasound settings, scanners, and institutions was rigorously and systematically verified through phantom models, prospective human studies, and real-world clinical settings, along with an enhancement in classifier performance in clinical scenarios.

ComBat reduced intra-scanner ultrasomics heterogeneity on phantom dataset

For features extracted from ultrasound images captured using the AIXPLORE system, the heterogeneity induced by varying parameter settings was reduced. For gain effect, 279 of 2,560 (10.90%) features were identified as heterogeneous before ComBat harmonization, which was significantly reduced to 18 of 2,560 (0.70%) features after harmonization (Figure 2A). For frequency effect, the number of heterogeneous features decreased from 1,164 of 2,560 (45.47%) to 296 of 2,560 (11.56%) following ComBat harmonization (Figure 2B). The absence of statistically significant differences (p > 0.05) in feature distribution across different batches indicated that the heterogeneity of features was successfully removed. The distributional changes illustrated in Figures 2C and 2D (Using the original first-order Mean feature as an example). Additionally, all features exhibited stable or improved pairwise concordance correlation coefficient (CCC) after ComBat harmonization, with a mean increase of 135% for gain effects and to 10% for frequency effects across all features. Following harmonization, the number of features achieving good-to-excellent reproducibility (CCC >0.9) increased by 720 (28%) for gain effects and by 112 (4%) for frequency effects, as intuitively demonstrated in the scatterplots of CCC changes (Figures 2E and 2F).

ComBat reduced heterogeneity on human dataset across scanners and institutions

A total of 2,400 images from participants without focal liver lesions and 420 images with focal liver lesions were collected from institution A. At institution B, we captured 1,200 2D ultrasound images from cross-sections without focal liver lesions and 420 images from cross-sections of focal liver lesions. After ComBat, the number of heterogeneous features in images without focal liver lesions decreased significantly, as shown in Figure 3 (p < 0.01). For heterogeneity features from different scanners, the reduction was from 1,304 of 2,560 (50.94%) to 632 of 2,560 (24.69%). For heterogeneity features from different institutions, the reduction was from 871 of 2,560 (34.02%) to 247 of 2,560 (9.65%). All features exhibited stable or improved pairwise CCC after ComBat harmonization, with a mean increase of 12% for scanner effect and to 9% for institution effects across all features (Figures 3E and 3F). For participants scanned at both centers, our results showed that the pairwise CCC of these participants was improved by 13% after ComBat harmonization. Similar trends were observed in intra-scanner datasets and images with focal liver lesions (Table S2). These findings confirmed that the ComBat effectively mitigated heterogeneity introduced by batch effects in imaging protocols, scanners, and acquisition institutions, thereby enhancing the reproducibility of ultrasomics in clinical settings.

ComBat enhanced generalizability and performance of machine learning in thyroid nodule classification

3,235 US images of thyroid nodules (1,181 malignant and 1,254 benign from institution B; 400 malignant and 400 benign from institution C) were collected. After applying ComBat to remove heterogeneity caused by batch effects introduced by different institutions with different scanners, the number of heterogeneous features from thyroid nodules decreased from 967 of 2,560 (37.77%) to 631 of 2,560 (24.65%), as shown in Figure 4A (p < 0.01). Using an “Original” feature as an example, Figure 4B showed that ComBat aligned the distributions of ultrasomics features from thyroid nodules, effectively removing heterogeneity from different institutions. All features exhibited stable or improved CCC after ComBat harmonization, with a mean increase of 11%, as shown in Figure 4C. Performance metrics for all machine learning models on the test sets improved significantly after ComBat harmonization, whether using fixed features (selected from raw data) or re-selected features. These results are summarized in Table 3. In population screening scenarios (malignancy rate typically 5%–15%), the AUC increased from 0.80 ± 0.03 to 0.93 ± 0.02 for the support vector machines (SVM) model, from 0.82 ± 0.03 to 0.93 ± 0.02 for logistic regression, and from 0.77 ± 0.03 to 0.87 ± 0.02 for the K-nearest neighbor (KNN) model. In high-risk patients referred for fine-needle aspiration or biopsy (malignancy rate 46%–57%), the models showed comparable improvements: the area under the curve (AUC) rose from 0.79 ± 0.02 to 0.93 ± 0.02 for SVM, from 0.80 ± 0.01 to 0.92 ± 0.03 for logistic regression, and from 0.76 ± 0.01 to 0.87 ± 0.02 for KNN. The improvements of the AUC values of the three models were shown in Figures 5 and 6. The metrics, including specificity, precision, and F1-score, also exhibited varying degrees of improvement, as detailed in Table 3. All improvements in model performance after harmonization were statistically significant (p < 0.01). These results demonstrated the effectiveness of the ComBat method in mitigating ultrasomics heterogeneity, improving the diagnostic performance and risk of overfitting of machine learning models for thyroid nodule classification.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically evaluated ComBat harmonization for reducing ultrasomics heterogeneity from multiple sources, including gains, frequencies, scanners, and clinical institutions, using a comprehensive framework of phantom experiments, prospective multi-center standardized scans, and real-world thyroid nodule cohorts. In phantom and prospective studies, heterogeneous features decreased by 51.53%–100%, ComBat improved CCC by 135% (gain-related noise) and 9%–13% (standardized cross-sections), and real-world models showed average AUC up to 0.93 post-harmonization. Additional analyses confirmed performance gains stemmed from batch effect reduction rather than feature re-selection, and that ComBat preserved clinically relevant signals, with robust performance in both screening and referral settings, supporting its value for improving ultrasomics reliability and generalizability.

Our findings from both rigorously controlled experiments and real-world data demonstrate that ComBat, as a data harmonization method, can effectively remove technical noise and successfully disentangle operator-driven anatomical variability from true biologically relevant anatomical information. By improving data consistency across batches, it thereby enhances the performance of machine learning models. Our CCC analysis revealed substantial improvements under intra-scanner gain conditions in the phantom and prospective studies. In these studies, anatomical textures remained consistent, and inter-batch differences arose solely from technical noise (gain variations). This demonstrated that ComBat effectively removed technical batch effects in the absence of biological differences. CCC enhancements were also observed for batches under the same scanner with different frequencies. Furthermore, although technical noise and biological information were more entangled across different institutions, our analysis included participants scanned in both institutions, for whom ultrasound images were acquired using standardized protocols that yielded highly consistent cross-sections comparable to those in CT or MRI. For these participants, the CCC improved by 13% after harmonization, indicating that the biological information encoded in these features became more consistent, while operator-dependent variability introduced by different sonographers was effectively reduced. The results were further confirmed in the real-world thyroid nodule clinical study. In this study, machine learning models showed performance gains after harmonization. These findings were further corroborated in the real-world thyroid nodule clinical study, in which machine learning models exhibited performance improvements after harmonization. Meanwhile, to directly disentangle the relative contributions of reduced technical heterogeneity and alterations in feature selection to performance improvements following ComBat harmonization, we performed an additional analysis. Specifically, the feature set selected from the raw (non-harmonized) data was fixed and used to retrain the models on the ComBat-harmonized dataset. As shown in Table 3 and Figure 5, models trained on harmonized data with the fixed feature set exhibited consistent improvements across key metrics, including AUC, sensitivity, and F1-score, compared to the raw data benchmarking. These results indicate that the principal source of enhancement is the effective mitigation of batch effects, with additional gains from feature re-selection post-harmonization. Importantly, the preserved or slightly enhanced discriminative power using identical features strongly suggests that ComBat did not inadvertently eliminate clinically relevant anatomical or pathological signals, consisting with reports from other imaging modalities.18,19,20 It is noteworthy that, although the malignant nodule rate is typically only 5%–15%, the proportion of malignant nodules in clinical scenarios requiring precise AI-assisted evaluation is 46%–57%.21,22,23,24,25 Accordingly, to ensure the generalizability of our findings across clinically realistic distributions, we evaluated model performance within both of these clinical scenarios. These results demonstrated that the ComBat harmonization method remained effective and robust in both setting, highlighting ComBat’s applicability for addressing heterogeneity in ultrasomics data in prospective or retrospective studies, making ultrasomics analysis more reliable and generalizable in clinical practice. Overall, these findings demonstrate that ComBat can mitigate technical batch effects in ultrasomics while preserving biological information. This alleviates heterogeneity and enhances machine learning model performance in clinical settings, with a low risk of inadvertently removing authentic anatomical or pathological signals.

ComBat reduced heterogeneity in ultrasomics by aligning data distributions. Machine learning based on ultrasomics is inherently data driven, meaning data quality greatly impacts model performance. Our image data, acquired by experienced experts to ensure high quality, achieved an AUC of 82% in machine learning models even before harmonization. However, uncontrollable batch effects were inevitable. Models are trained to fit the distribution of data for specific medical tasks. Nevertheless, when applied to a test set with a different distribution, the performance may decline due to poor fit, as heterogeneity is a major source of these discrepancies. While prospective studies minimize batch effects by standard imaging protocols, acquiring large samples in a single batch remains challenging and customizing parameters for individuals may limit generalizability.26,27,28,29 It is also impractical for retrospective studies.30 ComBat aligned the distribution of datasets as closely as possible by removing batch effects to reduce heterogeneity. This enabled models to focus on true biological signals instead of technical noise. As a result, significant improvement was observed in machine learning models trained on harmonized data in our study, consistent with findings from other imaging modalities.18,19,31,32 For instance, Panahi et al. demonstrated ComBat’s ability to mitigate variability from gray-level discretization and preprocessing pipelines in Parkinson’s disease cohorts, yielding gains in feature reproducibility and motor subtype classification performance.33,34 Despite ultrasound’s greater inherent heterogeneity from operator dependence and acoustic variability, we achieved comparable reductions in heterogeneous features and classification gains, underscoring ComBat’s robust applicability across modalities with distinct noise profiles.

Deep learning models that integrate radiomics often perform better and hold greater application potential,35,36,37 they continue to confront challenges associated with image heterogeneity. Deep-learning-based techniques, such as domain adaptation, domain generalizability, and disentanglement can mitigate heterogeneity at the raw image level,16,38,39,40,41 but they require extensive computational resources and complex processes. In contrast, operating at the feature level, ComBat requires minimal computational cost and is accessible to researchers without advanced computer backgrounds. Moreover, it does not necessitate image sharing, which benefits the protection of data privacy. In this study, data were harmonized on a laptop without sharing images across institutions, highlighting ComBat’s practicality and security. This ease of implementation, coupled with its demonstrated efficacy in reducing heterogeneity, positions ComBat as a highly practical and scalable solution.

To demonstrate ComBat’s ability to improve classifier performance, Leithne et al.20 reported improved results in the classification of human tissues after applying ComBat, while Orlhac et al.42 found that classifiers struggled to categorize post-ComBat data from different batches, neither of which directly address clinical applications. Our research directly assessed the performance of machine learning models on classification of thyroid nodules, highlighting its clinical value. Besides the classic ComBat approach validated here, several recent refinements have been proposed for specific scenarios or tasks, including CovBat43 and Nested ComBat.44These advances, primarily tested in CT and MRI, highlight the potential for further targeted optimization of ComBat specifically for the unique challenges of ultrasomics.

Limitations of the study

Our study had several limitations. First, ultrasound is inherently limited by inconsistent cross-sections and high operator dependence, which lead to complex entanglement between batch effects and biological variations, which remains an important constraint for future ultrasound radiomics studies. As a linear method, ComBat has inherent difficulty fully disentangling such mixed signals. Nevertheless, our analyses confirm that within our standardized imaging setup, ComBat effectively reduced technical noise while preserving genuine anatomical and pathological information with low risk of over-elimination. Secondly, due to interindividual variability, obtaining a large number of images in one batch with consistent imaging parameters is unrealistic, making it difficult to explore the clinical value of ComBat in eliminating imaging parameter-induced heterogeneity through retrospective studies. Thirdly, our study is lack of external datasets from centers other than the investigated ones, as well as the restriction to only two disease sites. In future research, we will incorporate data from more medical centers and diseases for further validation, so as to provide more robust evidence for our findings. Last but not least, the study was limited to three basic machine learning models, and the efficacy of ComBat with deep learning architectures remains unexplored, warranting future investigation.

In conclusion, within the scope of liver and thyroid imaging examined in this study, ComBat harmonization effectively reduced technical heterogeneity in ultrasomics features, thereby improving the generalizability and performance of machine learning models in multi-center binary classification tasks. These results indicate that ComBat is a promising and practical tool for enhancing the reliability of multicenter ultrasomics studies. Nevertheless, broader validation across diverse organs, disease entities, and clinical scenarios remains necessary before it can be recommended as a routine standardization method.

Experimental model and study participant details

We used three datasets to evaluate the ComBat’s clinical value in ultrasomics step by step. An ABDFAN phantom (Kyoto Kagaku) was used to acquire standard ultrasound images by adjusting gains and frequencies, which were known to vary significantly across scanners and institutions.The prospective participants dataset further validated ComBat’s efficacy on human. Volunteer participants aged 18 years or older were enrolled in this study between August and November 2024. Lastly, a real-world multicenter dataset is used to verify ComBat’s clinical applicability. From January 2024 and November 2025, consecutive adult patients (≥18 years) who underwent ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration or thyroidectomy at either institution were enrolled. Cases were excluded if no preoperative ultrasound images were available at our centers, if final histopathologic or cytopathologic diagnosis was lacking, or if image quality was deemed inadequate (defined as insufficient resolution, obvious motion artifacts, or failure to clearly display the entire nodule, assessed independently by two radiologists with 5 years of thyroid imaging experience; discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion). The inclusion and exclusion of patients and images are documented in Figure 1. All included thyroid nodules were of ACR TI-RADS category 2 or higher, and the spectrum of histological subtypes (e.g., adenoma, adenocarcinoma, papillary carcinoma) is confirmed through pathological diagnosis.

This prospective multicenter study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Eighth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Number: 2024–198–02) and the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University (Number: [2022]061), with written informed consent obtained from all participants. The retrospective multicenter clinical fddstudy was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University (Number: [2022]061), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design and use of anonymized data.

Method details

Phantom study

We first evaluated ComBat’s efficacy in reducing ultrasomics heterogeneity under strictly controlled conditions. The phantom dataset was used to validate the ComBats efficacy in removing heterogeneity induced by technical variations under strictly controlled conditions. The Aixplorer ultrasound system (SuperSonic Imagine S.A.) were utilized for the imaging. Gain levels were tested at four settings (60, 70, 80, and 90 dB), and frequency adjustments included penetration, general, resolution. This variation aimed to encompass the range of conditions typically encountered in clinical practice. All examinations were conducted by Dr. Wang with over 10 years of expertise in ultrasound imaging. Images were captured at the 5th–6th intercostal spaces and below the xiphoid process, targeting the right and left liver lobe of the phantom.

Prospective multicenter participants study

We further validated the efficacy of ComBat in reducing data heterogeneity across different scanners and institutions using human data. At Institution A, two ultrasound systems, Resona 9 (Mindray) and LOGIQ E9 (GE), were used. Institution B also utilized the Resona9 system. For participants without focal liver lesions, four specific liver cross-sections were imaged: the left lobe longitudinal view from the subxiphoid region, the first hepatic hilum, the right anterior lobe, and the right posterior lobe. For those with focal liver lesions, we captured the largest cross-section of the lesions. Fifteen participants without focal liver lesions were scanned at both institutions. The two institutions employed identical imaging protocols to control for specific batch effects. For subjects scanned at both sites, the sonographer referred to the first scan when acquiring the second set of images to ensure consistent cross-sections. Images were acquired with varying gain settings (Resona 9: 70, 75, 80, 85, 90 dB; LOGIQ E9: 50, 55, 60, 65, 70 dB) and frequency settings (Resona 9: 4, 5, 6 MHz; LOGIQ E9: Pen, Gen, Res). Images with poor quality was excluded. Additionally, age and sex of participant were recorded.

Real-world multicenter study

In real-world clinical retrospective settings, variables beyond imaging protocols are difficult to control. This experiment evaluated the effectiveness of ComBat in actual applications. The study population was defined as described previously. A total of 800 thyroid nodules from institution B and 400 from institution C were included. Afterward, we trained and tested four common machine learning models (Support Vector Machine, Logistic Regression and K-nearest Neighbors) on datasets without and with ComBat to distinguish benign from malignant thyroid nodules. To evaluate preservation of clinically relevant biological information, the exact feature sets selected on raw data were fixed and used to train the models on both raw and ComBat-harmonized data. Within each sub-dataset, nodules were randomly divided into a training (70%) and test (30%) set. To ensure robustness and generalizability, 10 sub-datasets were generated through random patient-level sampling, with each training set maintained at a 1:1 malignant-to-benign ratio—a widely accepted and standard practice in machine learning for addressing class imbalance. The corresponding test sets were constructed in two clinically relevant configurations: (i) a 9:1 benign-to-malignant ratio to reflect population screening scenarios, where malignancy rates are typically only 5–15%; and (ii) a 1:1 ratio to mirror high-risk referral populations (malignancy rates 46–57%), the precise clinical context in which machine learning models are intended to support decision-making. Model performance was subsequently benchmarked across these balanced and imbalanced test distributions to evaluate the generalizability of the ComBat method under different prevalence conditions. The training and test sets were harmonized independently, and both sets were randomly partitioned by patient rather than by image to prevent data leakage. Thus, 10 outcome sets of performance of per model were generated.

Theory and process of ComBat harmonization

The method directly applies to the values of ultrasomics feature and estimates the batch effect by matching the statistical distributions of the feature values measured in ROI j for each batch i. ComBat assumes that

α is the average for all feature values, which can be seen as the baseline for feature measurement. X is the design matrix for the covariates of interest, including variables that may influence the measurement such as age or disease stage. β is the vector of regression coefficients corresponding to each covariate, quantifying their impact on the features. γi is an additive batch effect, adjusting for shifts in feature values that consistently appear across all samples processed on the same batch, addressing what may be considered ‘technical deviations. δi is a multiplicative protocol effect affected by an error term (εij). In phantom and perspective human experiments, data were stratified into batches based on gain, frequency settings, ultrasound equipment models, or medical centers. For real-world dataset, batches were defined by equipment models and centers only. We applied the ComBat harmonization method to mitigate these batch effects based on Python software.

Ultrasomics feature extraction and analysis

Ultrasound images were stored in the standard DICOM format. Prior to analysis, all images were anonymized by removing protected health information. For phantom and liver without focal lesions images, the liver contours were manually delineated as regions of interest by Dr. Lin with over four years of experience on ultrasonography. Images containing focal liver lesions and thyroid nodules, the same doctor manually segmented the lesion boundaries. Values of ultrasomics features were extracted by the Ultrasomics Platform (version 2.1, Ultrasomics Artificial Intelligence X-lab). In the thyroid nodules classification experiment, feature selection was performed using a sequential pipeline of univariate analysis (p < 0.05), Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), followed by recursive feature elimination (RFE) with support vector machine. ComBat harmonization were performed on the values. In the phantom and prospective human experiments, gain, frequency, scanner, and institution were treated as batch variables for removal during ComBat harmonization. All the harmonization parameters were implemented using Python (version 3.9). The harmonization workflow followed the procedure provided by Fortin et al.9 (https://github.com/Jfortin1/neuroCombat). The project provided several optional configuration parameters. “batch_cols” was used to specify the name of the variable that encodes batch effects. The parameter “eb” is set to “True”, indicating that Empirical Bayes adjustments were performed. “Parametric” is set to “True”, indicating that parametric adjustments were performed. The parameter “ref_batch” is set to “None”, indicating that no batch is taken as the reference batch. The parameter settings were shown in Table 4. The ultrasomics features were categorized into different groups based on the batch effects. When gain was designated as the batch effect for removal, ultrasomics features were grouped according to different gain levels, ensuring that each group contained consistent frequencies and anatomical cross-sections. Similarly, for frequency effect removal, features were grouped by frequency, with identical gain settings and anatomical cross-sections across groups. When scanner served as the batch effect, each group included features encompassing all gain and frequency variations. For the thyroid nodule dataset, only scanner and institution were designated as batch variables to be eliminated, as gain and frequency could not be fully traced in the retrospective data and impractical for real-world clinical studies to fix entirely static parameters. Additionally, during the execution of ComBat harmonization, empirical Bayes shrinkage and parametric adjustments were performed as per the default program settings to stabilize estimates and correct for both additive and multiplicative batch effects while preserving biological signals. Details of the harmonization was illustrated in the supplementary materials.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Heterogeneity across batches for each feature was statistically tested by normality tests, followed by ANOVA, t-tests, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, or Kruskal-Wallis tests, based on data distribution. A p value below 0.05 indicated significant batch-induced heterogeneity. The number of heterogeneous features before and after ComBat was compared using χ2 tests (p < 0.01 for significance). Concordance correlation coefficients (CCC) were calculated across all batch combinations for each feature. Mean CCC values per feature were derived, with post-ComBat improvements assessed as percentage increases and the proportion of features showing elevation.

We evaluated the performance of the three machine learning models using accuracy, specificity, precision, F1-Score and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Stability across 10 data subsets was assessed using mean values ± standard deviation. Post-harmonization improvements of the models were tested by Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (p < 0.01). The reduction in disparities between the models’ performance on the training and test sets was assessed using paired-sample t-tests.
