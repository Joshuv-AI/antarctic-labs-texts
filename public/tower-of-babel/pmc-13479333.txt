Interrogating the Effects of Technologies of Pregnancy Surveillance on Queer, Trans and Nonbinary Midwifery Service‐Users
Authors: Jennifer M Goldberg, Amaya Perez‐Brumer, Lori E Ross
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTQueer, transgender and nonbinary (QTNB) people access midwifery services, yet little is known about their experiences of midwifery care. Our qualitative study used critical narrative inquiry to examine how QTNB midwifery service‐users in Ontario, Canada, experience cisheteronormativity in healthcare. Drawing on in‐depth interviews with 14 participants, our analysis examines the effects of biomedicalisation on QTNB people who navigate these systems. Our findings show that QTNB people access and participate in technologies of pregnancy surveillance, which deploy information‐seeking practices that collect information about the genetic makeup of the baby, conception and paternity. These ‘lines of inquiry’ present in mundane but insidious ways, embedded within cisheteronormative assumptions about paternity and conception. Stemming from broader biomedical discourse that views sex and gender as one and the same, genetically determined and binary, the discursive effects of lines of inquiry render invisible QTNB identities, family building processes and kinship structures. We explore this tension, showing how lines of inquiry have unequal effects of surveillance across queer family building, kinship and identity, and how QTNB resist dominant discourse around sex, gender and sexuality. Findings can be used by perinatal health professionals to better care for QTNB perinatal service‐users as they navigate technologies of surveillance during pregnancy.

Introduction

Queer, transgender and nonbinary 1 (QTNB) people often avoid accessing medical care because their identities are not reflected in cisheteronormative medical settings (Scheim et al. 2013) or because of previous or anticipated experiences of homo‐, bi‐ and transphobia during medical care. Heteronormativity refers to “localised practices and centralised institutions that legitimate and privilege heterosexuality and heterosexual relationships as fundamental and ‘natural’ within society,” (Cohen 1997, 440) whereas cisnormativity legitimates and privileges cisgenderism—the assumption that a person's sex matches their gender identity. In health care settings, heterosexual and cisgender experiences are normalised and privileged, in part because dominant discourses on sex and gender are shaped by longstanding entrenched beliefs that gender is biologically and genetically determined, binary and conflated with sex. Pervasive cisheteronormativity contributes to how structural exclusion shapes QTNB people's experiences of invisibility, discrimination and isolation in health care settings (Pezaro et al. 2023; Soled et al. 2023).

QTNB people often seek out midwifery services for their prenatal, intrapartum and postnatal care. Globally, midwifery services are regulated nationally, such as in the United Kingdom and New Zealand, or regionally, such as Canadian provincial and territorial levels. Although scope of practice varies across jurisdictions where midwifery is legally recognised, the services are built upon shared values rooted in feminist approaches to care (Burton and Ariss 2014) and includes informed decision‐making, comprehensive postpartum care and choice of birthplace (in hospital, home or birth centre). Midwives are essential primary care providers who are held accountable to regulatory and professional standards of care.

Midwifery's unique value set is also seen in its resistance to biomedical hegemony through its feminist tenets and anti‐oppressive approach to care (Adams and Bourgeault 2004; Burton and Ariss 2014). In recent years, midwifery education programs have been integrating social justice approaches into curricula (Effland et al. 2020; McMaster University 2024). Increased intentionality from midwifery professional and regulatory entities around the work of dismantling oppression and colonialism aims to create midwifery services that are affirming and inclusive for those who experience oppression, such as racism, transphobia and poverty (Association of Ontario Midwives 2024b; Royal College of Midwives 2024). As such, QTNB midwifery service‐users could benefit from affirming and inclusive midwifery approaches to care.

The uniqueness of the midwifery model of care sets it apart from medical models of care, providing QTNB with hope that their experiences will be different from those within the broader biomedical system. Yet, midwifery as a profession is not united in this approach. Recent debates within some global midwifery communities around whether and how to make space for trans and nonbinary people in midwifery care (Pezaro et al. 2024) demonstrate how midwives grapple with cisheteronormative and gender essentialist views. Midwives' assumptions about, and attitudes towards QTNB people shape their negative experiences of cisheteronormativity within midwifery services (Pezaro et al. 2023; Soled et al. 2023; Thomas et al. 2025). In this way, midwifery practice is a situated nexus, anchored by its feminist tenets while interrogating those same norms and innovating to be inclusive.

The profession of midwifery is rooted in a feminist ethos of care (Adams and Bourgeault 2004; Thachuk 2007), effectively resisting biomedicine through its value set (J. Goldberg 2024, 32–34) that includes patient autonomy, choice of birthplace, informed decision‐making and relational care. Midwifery and biomedicine represent distinct social worlds, shaped over time by sociopolitical forces and reflect underlying value systems that result in differences in how care is experienced by QTNB people (J. Goldberg 2024, 32–34). However, despite these non‐normative underpinnings, midwifery is integrated into, and reliant upon, the broader biomedical system it resists to be able to provide comprehensive care and services. This reliance stems from needing to ensure seamless functioning of midwifery within the biomedical system. In this way, midwifery is situated as an anti‐normative value set operating within biomedical norms. Further, there is a limit to what midwives can do, delineated in standards of care that are determined in conjunction with multiple actors, including midwives, professional organisations and local governments. For example, in jurisdictions where prenatal genetic testing and diagnostic ultrasounds are not included in midwives' scope, midwives rely on the biomedical system to be able to offer these services.

In this way, midwives toggle back and forth between providing midwifery services and referring out to biomedical services and rely on commonly available and professionally acquired knowledge and language about biomedical services, tests and technologies (Niles et al. 2021). Standardisation of language used in intake and medical history forms and a host of biomedical technologies that monitor the health of the pregnancy and labour, are utilised by midwives to provide comprehensive and safe care. Midwives are in constant relationship and communication with the broader biomedical system within which they are enfolded.

Our study investigated how QTNB people in Ontario, Canada, experience cisheteronormativity while in midwifery care. Because of midwifery's unique positioning as both anti‐normative and reliant on biomedicine, we explore connections between cisheteronormativity and biomedicalisation in the context of perinatal care for QTNB people. We investigate new linkages between biomedicalisation and cisheteronormativity through midwifery as a site where QTNB people access biomedical technologies of pregnancy surveillance (e.g., ultrasounds, prenatal genetic testing and medical history taking). By situating biomedicalisation in practice, we show how accessing technologies of surveillance has important consequences for QTNB midwifery service‐users. Further, we advance on the notion of stratified biomedicalisation, a systemic force that perpetuates and reinforces social, cultural and ideological infrastructures that sustain existing disparities (Clarke and Shim 2010), by showing how surveillance has unequal effects across queer family building processes, kinship structures and identities.

Biomedicalisation and Queer Reproduction

The effects of biomedicalisation on QTNB people once pregnancy has been achieved have been underexamined in the literature. Considerable feminist scholarship has examined the process of ‘getting to pregnant’; namely, how reproduction, technology and sexuality are constructed through reproductive technologies (Franklin 1997; Mamo 2007; Nordqvist 2008; Rapp 1999), and how lesbians navigate and disrupt these processes (Mamo 2007). This work has been central to understanding how biomedical technologies of reproduction impact queer family building through reification of heterosexuality and the disruption of compulsory heterosexuality by queer family building and kinship (Mamo 2007).

Linkages have been established between biomedicalisation and heteronormativity. Historically, biomedical knowledge has viewed the queer body as disabled or diseased, while defining health norms based on the heterosexual body (Kafer 2013). The organisation of biomedical knowledge around the binary of ‘normal and pathological’ continues to uphold heterosexual norms within health care systems (Spurlin 2023). For example, assisted reproductive technologies continue to focus on infertility of heterosexual couples, thereby effectively limiting queer people's access to family building options and circumventing their needs (Tam 2021). The enforcement of the gender binary regulates sexuality, serving as a powerful tool for reinforcing societal norms through discourse and institutional practices (Butler 2014). Consequently, heterosexual and cisgender experiences are normalised and privileged within health care settings, in part because dominant discourses of sex and gender and their conflation are shaped by longstanding entrenched beliefs that they are biologically determined and binary.

Rooted in heterosexual and cisgender norms, biomedical knowledge has contributed to the historical systematic pathologising of queerness and transness (Spurlin 2023), leaving a residue of harmful discriminatory care by health care providers. Hegemonic normative biomedical discourses of sex and gender continue to be reproduced throughout medical education curricula, thereby producing cisheteronormative language and practices that serve to reify normative sex, gender and sexuality (Annandale and Clark 2000; Butler 1999; Spurlin 2023). Cisheteronormative bias within medical education and healthcare practices often translates into negative, and harmful, health care experiences and outcomes for QTNB people (A. E. Goldberg et al. 2020; Greenfield and Darwin 2020; Hafford‐Letchfield et al. 2019).

Considering how biomedicalisation impacts QTNB experiences of health and constrains queer people's choices around family building (Mamo 2018), the dearth of scholarship on how pregnancy technologies impact queer people is surprising. We are concerned with how the biomedical application of surveillance technologies impacts QTNB gestational parents and their partners and coparents during pregnancy. The central question we explore is: What effects do technologies of surveillance have on QTNB midwifery service‐users?

Theoretical Framework

Our analysis specifically draws on the biomedicalisation scholarship advanced by Adele Clarke. The manipulation of human biological processes marks a shift towards transforming life itself, framing biomedicine as a powerful cultural lens through which bodies and lives are interpreted, understood and modified (Clarke et al. 2003). These processes unfold through a network of technologies, including institutions, practices and sites of knowledge production (Clarke and Shim 2010). The transformation of bodies, lives and futures, takes place through processes of optimisation of life itself (Rose 2001), demanding ‘action in the vital present in the name of vital futures to come’ (Clarke and Shim 2010, 7). The optimisation of life itself is concerned with our increasing ability to control, manage, engineer and reshape the fundamental capacities of humans as living organisms (Rose 2001, 262). In this way, biomedicalisation has developed surveillance technologies that extend into previously unmedicalised aspects of life, including pregnancy.

In our analysis, we foreground how optimisation of life itself occurs through technologies of surveillance deployed to optimise the outcome of pregnancy outcomes. Specifically, we examine the social processes of biomedicalisation of life itself (Clarke et al. 2003). As Clarke contends, ‘the realms and dynamics of the social inside scientific, technological and biomedical domains are too often rendered invisible’ (Clarke et al. 2003, 166). In this way, we seek to uncover the ways cisheteronormativity is hidden and operates within technological domains.

This process occurs through social practices and norms that are actively operating within medical institutions and practices, wherein norms exert disciplinary power over individuals through discourse and practice (Clarke and Shim 2010). Advanced by Foucault, the notion of disciplinary power is a key line of theory we draw from, premised on the idea of knowledge as a regulatory mechanism operating as a nexus of agents, disciplines and technologies that target, and make measurable aspects of individual health and well‐being (Clarke and Shim 2010; McDonnell 2009). Central to the strategy of disciplinary power is how individuals are made visible through processes of observation, examination, and measurement in comparison to established standardised norms (Lupton 1997). Within medical encounters, disciplinary power exerts control over individuals by regulating how they ought to be managing and experiencing their bodies (Lupton 1997).

Surveillance is not merely the act of observing and monitoring individuals; it is the key mechanism through which disciplinary power operates and is a means of exerting power and control over individuals and enforcing obedience (Foucault 1977) to regulate behaviour, instil self‐discipline and maintain social order (Driver 1994). Surveillance is reflected in the monitoring and management of health and illness through biomedical practices, reflecting the pervasive influence of biomedicine and technoscience in shaping individual and collective experiences related to health and well‐being (McDonnell 2009). This includes meticulous observation, recording and training of individuals within medical institutions (Clarke and Shim 2010).

The notion of discipline is evident in how biomedicalisation regulates and normalises bodily functions and behaviours through medical interventions (Clarke and Shim 2010) including surveillance technologies. Therefore, our analysis examines technologies of pregnancy surveillance as combined technologies of disciplinary power. If strategies of disciplinary power make visible the difference of individuals against dominant established norms (Lupton 2012a), then such strategies must have social effects on QTNB people, who diverge from established sex and gender norms. By viewing the data through this framework, we make legible the social effects of power and biomedicalisation on QTNB midwifery service‐users.

Methods

This qualitative study, carried out in Ontario, Canada, was guided by critical narrative inquiry (CNI) to examine narratives within the stories of QTNB midwifery service‐users, and specifically, how biomedicine shapes discourse around sex and gender and regulates reproductive bodies. Midwives have been regulated since 1994 and are funded by the Ministry of Health, caring for almost 20% of births in Ontario (Association of Ontario Midwives 2024a). Clients self‐refer to individual midwifery practices through online booking systems and are assigned a small team of midwives providing continuity during pregnancy, labour and delivery, and the first 6 weeks postpartum. Typically, clients seek out midwives in their local communities and often choose to be assigned a midwife who matches the client's cultural, sexual or gender identity.

Participants were QTNB people who had given birth with midwives in Ontario between 2019–2022, their partners and/or co‐parents. Considering the varied possibilities for how queer families are constituted (Jackson Levin et al. 2020), partner(s) and co‐parent(s) were invited to participate even if the gestational parent did not. REB approval was obtained from the University of Toronto.

Recruitment materials were emailed to all Ontario midwifery practice groups (131 at the time). Potential participants were screened by phone to confirm eligibility and invited to identify whether they were racialised, Indigenous, disabled or had lived/living experiences they felt would contribute to the study, a process that helped us discern individuals we sought to prioritise from those who have been over‐represented in published studies.

There were 14 participants ranging from 25‐ to 41‐years old (Table S1). Eleven self‐identified as queer, and three as lesbian. Half the participants identified as nonbinary and/or transgender, and half as cisgender women. Half the sample identified as Indigenous, Black and/or racialised, and half as White. Eight participants identified having disabilities; at least four participants had previous or current experiences of poverty and at least three lived in semi‐urban or rural settings. Not all participants were asked the poverty and setting screening questions because the PI added them midway through recruitment in response to early findings indicating the relevance of socioeconomic status and geographic location.

Two online (Zoom) interviews were conducted 2–4 weeks apart, ranging from 30 to 120 min per interview. The method of serial interviewing has been described as a two‐interview free association narrative protocol (Hollway and Jefferson 2000) and is congruent with the study's epistemological commitment of relational ethics and deep narrative examination. Serial interviews avoid the constraints of singular interviews, such as running out of time, broad strokes discussions and lack of opportunity for follow‐up questions (Read 2018). The 2–4‐week interval between interviews provided time to tailor the second interview guide based on preliminary analysis of the first interview and allowed participants time to reflect and return fresh to the second interview (Read 2018). Ten participants were interviewed as couples and four individually. Informed verbal consent was obtained. With the exception of one participant who chose not to attend the second interview, all participants completed both interviews.

The first interview was an open storytelling interview, where participants were asked to share their birth stories. The second interview guide aimed to elicit further stories through semi‐structured questions that were selected based on the narratives present in the first interview. Participants were compensated $35 per person per interview via e‐transfer. Interviews were transcribed and verified.

A total of 27 interviews were analysed, the unit of analysis being the narrative within the story. The relationship between narratives and discourse was examined using the critical narrative analysis (CNA), a hybrid analysis combining critical discourse analysis and narrative analysis (Souto‐Manning 2014). CNA enables examination of how dominant discourse about cisheteronormativity shapes narratives and how narratives resist discourse, and how narratives reflect the influence of social forces and how people navigate or negotiate their ‘lifeworlds’ in relation to those forces (Rudman and Aldrich 2017). In this way, we examined how cisheteronormative discourse was recycled in or resisted by the narratives, and what enables or constrains this.

In this approach, the PI wrote about the narratives they noticed in the stories and then conceptualised how these narratives connected to tell an overarching story, essentially to explain ‘what was going on’ with the data (Broom 2021; Stenvoll and Svensson 2011). Following Hollway and Jefferson (2000), this approach consisted of toggling between reading the transcripts and writing about what was noticed in the data, including why some narratives were noticed rather than others, to help explain what might be happening in the data. Narratives that showed how participants experienced cisheteronormativity, and counter‐narratives that showed resistance to dominant norms, were tracked. As the data interpretation progressed, conceptual linkages between narratives and discourse—and between the narratives themselves—began to crystallise and were mapped out onto a singular piece of brown craft paper (24 × 60 inches) to be used as a conceptual framework. This map made visible the inter‐narrative linkages and analytical speculations about what might account for them, while thinking with the theories. The narrative network was contextualised by asking, what are the sociopolitical forces that enable or constrain the narratives within the stories? Our analysis here stems from noticing specific linkages between narratives about queer family building and experiences of invisibilisation within the biomedical domain.

Positionality

The first author is a queer genderqueer midwife, and the co‐authors are queer and cisgender. One co‐author is Latina, two are White and all are settlers. We are guided by reflexivity in our work, recognising how our positionalities shape our perspectives and influence our interactions with participants and data interpretation. We acknowledge the responsibility of intentional anti‐racist and decolonial allyship that comes with the power and privilege we individually hold. We continually applied an anti‐oppressive lens during the study to avoid harm to the participants and to each other.

The PI shared their identities with the participants during the interviews, including their sexual, gender, colonial settler and midwife identities, and their motivations for the investigation. As a queer genderqueer midwife who has provided care to queer families, the PI's knowledge, experience and positionality informed the process of noticing narratives through their ‘creative presence’ (Eakin and Gladstone 2020).

Findings

The central narrative, this is how we make our family, tells how QTNB midwifery service‐users wanted their identities, family building processes and kinship structures to be affirmed by midwives. However, findings show that participants experienced cisheteronormativity when accessing certain procedures and tests such as prenatal genetic screening, ultrasound and history taking. These conventional technologies are deployed as tools of risk mitigation, aiming to optimise pregnancy and birth outcomes through surveillance of the foetus. They function as part of an apparatus that includes practices of gathering medical information, making recommendations and employing tests and procedures, all of which operate simultaneously and in relation to one another (Driver 1994).

We draw from participants' stories to show how procedures and practices of intake questionnaires and medical and genetic history taking deploy lines of inquiry that affect QTNB people. We then show how lines of inquiry are pervasive within biomedical technologies, such as genetic screening tests, prenatal ultrasound and medical history taking, which deploy insidious and quotidian questions and assumptions that have consequences for QTNB people accessing those services. We end by showing how lines of inquiry have unequal surveillance effects across queer family building, kinship and identity, in particular for racialised people.

Lines of Inquiry Within Intake Questionnaires and Clinical Spaces

As part of the broader biomedical system, midwives must play by the rules within that system, which include medicalised practices of collecting information about clients and their families before or during the first in‐person consultation. These intake procedures comprise the routine collection of information about the gestational parent and their family, occurring during the initial midwife visit, by phone or by completing a questionnaire prior to the first visit. It was during intake that participants first encountered lines of inquiry.

It was just assumed when we…had our intake appointment with [the midwives], that I was in a straight relationship, and that I would have a male partner. It took her a little bit on the phone to get used to saying ‘she’ instead of ‘he’. There was a lot of ‘Oh, what does he do?’ and ‘How long have you two been trying?’…I was like, it was very much planned, and it had to be IVF. The process of creating her was just assumed that it was an easy ‘Oh, we just had sex and now we’re pregnant’.

(Maya, White lesbian cisgender female)

Heteronormative assumptions shape how intake questions are asked and what gets left out, leaving no room for any possibility other than Maya being heterosexual and conception occurring via intercourse. Similarly, assumptions are embedded within intake forms sent to participants prior to the first appointment. Alex was looking forward to filling out the medical forms with his partner and gestational parent, Fanny, and getting to contribute as a family member to this process.

I remember being so excited when they were emailed and Fanny and I were going to fill out these questionnaires. I read through it and was like, oh, it’s actually just about Fanny. And that’s fine, I just had the feeling of, wait – this is our family, you know? I was really excited when you got the paperwork. And I read it and was like, it is just your paperwork.

(Alex, White Ashkenazi Jewish queer trans/gender non‐conforming person)

When only information about the gestational parent is solicited, the partner is excluded from the experience they were looking forward to. In cases where information was solicited about the partner, it often included language that excluded queer and trans partners:

[The] forms were still geared towards cisgender couples…when it was about male infertility or sperm and things like that. I can’t fill that section out.

(Rebecca, racialised Asian Filipino lesbian cisgender female)

Intake forms that only make room for ‘mother’ or ‘mother and father’ leave out QTNB people and their non‐normative family structures. Typically, health professionals including midwives make decisions about what to include on the practice's intake forms, and those decisions are put into action by medical administrators who often design and process intake forms. In this way, information and data collection practices are intentional and reflect beliefs about what matters to know and how that information is solicited. Further, information about medical history is typically recorded in the electronic medical record (EMR), a method that standardises the collection of medical information is decided by clinicians, task force members and ministry of health officials. Often, there is no space to record sexual or gender identity in the EMR (Gilmore and Khan 2026), reflecting cisheteronorms. Clarke et al. (2003) contends that standardised formats ‘…make it all but impossible to enter certain kinds of data in the medical record, especially highly individualised information common to medical practice on unique individual bodies’ (Clarke et al. 2003, 174). The misrecognition of QTNB bodies as unique is no accident; lines of inquiry about conception—‘how long have you been trying’ or ‘male infertility’—are rooted in heteronormative assumptions about conception and family building processes. ‘At the same time, these data formats render it all but impossible not to record other kinds of data’ (Clarke et al. 2003, 174), thereby constraining how information appears on forms and who gets to be accounted for.

The pervasiveness of cisheteronormative discourse about who gets to be pregnant extends beyond intake forms, permeating into the physical space of medical and midwifery clinics.

I don’t think that [health care providers] are at this point in time intentionally homophobic or transphobic. It’s these small things where your identity is not recognized, or you don’t feel comfortable bringing [it] up because of the signage or the forms that you fill out. You go into ‘Women and Infant Health’ as a trans person having a baby…and it’s those things that make you feel like you’re not supposed to be in this space.

(Simon, White queer trans‐nonbinary person)

In this example, ‘Women's Health’ signage, ubiquitous to medical spaces, reifies pregnancy as a heterosexual process, thereby inscribing a gender binary onto the process of ‘getting to’ pregnancy. Although not explicitly a line of inquiry, this example foregrounds the pervasiveness of cisheteronormativity deployed through words and signage, which challenges QTNB people's sense of belonging in those spaces.

Lines of Inquiry Within Medical and Genetic History Taking

Midwives routinely collected information about the participants' medical and family history to help identify possible genetic risks. This information guided conversations about options for genetic screening, and informed participants' decisions to access pregnancy and foetal surveillance technologies. During medical history taking, the invisibilizing effects of lines of inquiry were pervasive. For example, a midwife was collecting information about genetic history from Rebecca and Maya, who used reciprocal in vitro fertilisation (IVF) for their family building; Rebecca provided oocytes (eggs) from their ovaries, which were combined with sperm from a donor to create embryos, then transferred into Maya's uterus.

Rebecca (egg donor) : We did reciprocal IVF [and] there was confusion about who to ask…about medical history. A few times they'd be like, oh, what is Maya's medical history? But they were asking in terms of the baby, and the baby's genetically related to me. So there was a lot of confusion around that…

Maya (gestational parent) : They assumed because I was carrying her, that biologically the link was there…We just kept having to remind them…if you're asking about…her genetics and things to look for in her screening…you need to ask Rebecca, not me. If it's about the birth, sure, ask me what family history I have.

Rebecca : And then again, we don't know the donor's medical history. We only know what they've reported…So [we had] to…take on those assumptions and constantly explain and remind people…this is our family‐building process.

In this example, confusion about reciprocal IVF renders the family building process invisible. The parents take on the labour of repeatedly explaining the process of conception to the midwife, otherwise unnecessary for heterosexual cisgender parents. Participants wanted midwives to demonstrate awareness of family building as an intentional time consuming and often expensive journey; have more conversations about family building among partners, coparents and anyone else involved in shaping the family and solicit genetic and medical history accordingly.

Questions and assumptions about genetic history also led to invisibility of transgender and nonbinary partners' gender and/or roles in family building. For example, transgender partners were often asked directly about their genetic history because they were assumed to be cisgender fathers who had made a genetic contribution to the baby:

[In the hospital] there were…random comments like, your partner’s so tall [and] there’s comments about the baby’s size. Or the hearing test and whether there’s any genetic problems with our families and we were like, nope. Irrelevant, but no…Even if we weren’t queer or trans [don’t assume] that everyone who’s involved in the birth is biologically related…people can also be an adoptive parent…

(Simon, White queer trans‐nonbinary person)

The conflation of paternity with genetic contribution reinforces compulsory heterosexuality and heterosexual norms of conception, thereby inscribing the gender binary and invisibilizing the queer family building process. Trans and nonbinary partners being read as cisgender fathers conflates gender with sex. Rooted in cisgenderism, this assumption makes trans and nonbinary partners feel unseen, thereby inscribing compulsory heterosexuality on their roles in the family building process, which are made illegible. In this way, cisheteronormativity reinscribes subjectivity through lines of inquiry about conception, genetic history and paternity, which are interconnected and co‐constituted. These effects are made visible during medical and genetic history taking, during which lines of inquiry deploying questions about the genetics of the baby bring into question paternity (who ‘made’ the baby) and hence conception (how the baby was ‘made’), making each person's identity and role in family building unseen. The effect of this cascade lays bare how compulsory heterosexuality and compulsory gender (Cole and Cate 2008) regulate reproduction via cisheteronormativity.

Genetic Screening and Ultrasound

While in midwifery care, clients accessed genetic screening and ultrasound technologies. Lines of inquiry were deployed routinely during medical consults for foetal surveillance or when accessing ultrasound to monitor foetal anatomical development and growth. Fanny, a queer woman, and Alex, who identifies as trans nonbinary, were at a genetic screening appointment with a physician. The physician was collecting information about genetic history, and in response, Fanny was describing their kinship and family building process:

I’m in a same sex partnership and we used a known donor …I can call the donor if you need any information. And then [the physician] was like, ‘does your partner have a history of one kidney?’ And then [the physician] was like, ‘Oh my God, I’m such an idiot’.

(Fanny, White Ashkenazi Jewish queer femme cisgender woman)

In this example, the physician catches the mistake and addresses it with humour, which the participants appreciated; however, questions pertaining to paternity are deeply rooted in heteronormative assumptions about conception. Even when QTNB people do the labour of describing their family building and conception, heteronormative assumptions are deeply ingrained within lines of inquiry.

Some non‐gestational participants who had donated an egg in reciprocal IVF used their genetic kinship ties in an attempt to assuage their feelings of alienation and missing out on shared experiences of care.

I was feeling left out of the process. And the only thing that really pulled me out of feeling sad or upset about it was realizing that, yes, I did play a role in giving my egg and I went through this extraction piece. I think if that little tiny piece wasn't there…it would have felt a lot worse. Because that was the one thing that kept pulling me back: this baby is likely going to look exactly like me. I played a huge role here.

(Meekah, South Asian colonised queer nonbinary woman)

In this way, having a genetic tie to the foetus/baby makes it possible to see the baby as an extension of oneself and is deployed as a strategy to make the unseen, seen. Genetic ties make legible the connection to the baby, and therefore one's role in family building. Yet not all partners had genetic ties to the baby, such as Alex, who, in response to Fanny's story about the question of her partner having one kidney, shared:

I think that’s what’s so painful. I think that contributes to the isolation piece because I was riddled with anxiety about what that would be like. Will I feel connected? What will my connection be?

(Alex, White Ashkenazi Jewish queer trans/gender non‐conforming person)

The physician's original question about Alex's kidney history, although medically warranted, makes Alex's role in kinship unseen, possibly adding to their feelings of dismissal by the care provider, and their worry about the kind of relationship they will have with the baby. If the family building process is unseen, then the contributing role of each parent remains unseen. And it works in the other direction as well: When the notion of ‘parents’ is constrained to ‘mom and dad’, the complexity and richness of queer families is excluded (Lowik 2015). In this way, lines of inquiry about genetic history invisibilise family building, with consequences for the people involved in that process.

The Insidiousness of Lines of Inquiry

Conflation of paternity with genetic contributions was also seen when gestational parents and their partners attended ultrasound appointments. Partners who identified as cisgender women, or were read that way, were asked ‘who are you?’ or ‘are you a friend?’ by ultrasound technicians. That a same‐gender partner must be a friend is a ubiquitous heteronormative assumption, which immediately invisibilises the intentional non‐heterosexual process of queer family building. This assumption is similarly made when the technician asks about the gestational parent's husband despite having seen the same couple over multiple appointments.

I've just given in to the ultrasound clinic assuming that Meekah is a man. When they're like, ‘OK, we'll invite him in’, I'm like, you're going to be looking for someone who’s not a man, like don't pull in some random man…I had multiple ultrasounds a couple of days apart at the end and saw the same person every single time, and every single time, she said, ‘So is your husband excited?’ And every single time I would say yes, my partner, Meekah, is quite excited.

(Tico, Afro‐Brazilian and White queer nonbinary person)

Tico's response interrupts heterosexuality as a compulsory process of conception, but the onus is on the queer couple to do so. This scenario illustrates how lines of inquiry are insidious in two ways. First, any health provider can operate from these presumptions, from midwives to physicians to nurses to ultrasound technicians. Second, the presumptions are deployed a priori without any consideration for there being an alternative to the heterosexual experience. Patton‐Imani (2020) contends that dominant narratives about heredity and kinship teach people how to understand the roles of mom and a dad, thereby devaluing non‐heteronormative families and kinship structures. Appearing as simple questions, ‘who are you?’ and ‘is your husband excited?’ immediately dispose of the partner's identity and kinship role, and by extension, the family building process.

Work schedules, family and childcare commitments, Covid‐19 restrictions or illness meant partners often were not able to accompany the gestational parent to midwifery or medical appointments. When attending ultrasound appointments alone, some gestational parents were asked, ‘where's the dad?’ when there was not a partner to (potentially) interrupt this heteronormative assumption—surely, there must be a dad out there somewhere! In contrast, some gestational parents presenting alone at ultrasound appointments were not asked about having a partner at all.

Mackenzie: They never had an opportunity to say, ‘Oh, your husband can come in now, or your wife’…because I was by myself.

Cameron: It gives them an opportunity to ignore it and not have to ask.

Mackenzie: Because they never referred to my partner…in the ultrasounds when I'd asked for a second set of pictures…because I wanted to be able to show Cameron. It wasn't significant enough for me…sometimes in situations where I'm never going to see you again…I may or may not correct someone. Especially when I'm half‐naked. But I feel like Covid in a way eliminated…the ability to have an uncomfortable or… transphobic or queerphobic experience because it was like I was the only person there.

(Mackenzie, queer Métis Indigenous cisgender female; Cameron, queer First Nations Indigenous transgender man)

Whether participants were asked where's the dad or not seemed to hinge on the individual technician or whether partners were permitted in the ultrasound room. The norm that holds across technicians' behaviours is cisheteronormativity, thereby structuring ‘correct’ and expected ways of behaving in biomedical spaces by all biomedical actors. That Mackenzie's identity remains unknown to the ultrasound technician is not necessarily experienced in a negative way, as it eliminates the possibility of queerphobia or transphobia. Participants described how identity concealment can make navigating health care easier and safer by avoiding potential queer and transphobic encounters. QTNB people are continually navigating the health care landscape and deciding whether, and when, it is safe to share their sexual and gender identities (Carpenter 2021; Hoffkling et al. 2017). In the above example, there is a literal manifestation of the vulnerability that QTNB people experience in health care contexts when physical examination requires the patient to present ‘half‐naked’. The participant decides to conceal their identity, becausee being half‐naked heightens an already vulnerable state. Often the decision is to remain invisible for one's own safety, despite cisheteronormative assumptions that can invisibilise queerness and ‘just eliminate that part of your life’ (Mackenzie).

Also seen with trans nonbinary partners, Simon described the double‐edged sword of ‘passing’ as a cisgender dad—a privilege, but with the effect of ‘putting you back in the closet you wanted out of’ (Simon). Transness is rendered invisible through cisheteronormative assumptions about who is the dad. Concealment of queer and trans identity, as a way to put up with, or navigate, cisheteronormativity, renders invisible the family building process and kinship structure. Whether being read as father or friend, the partner's sexual and gender identities are rendered invisible, and with them, the family building process.

Lines of Inquiry and Intersecting Oppression

Lines of inquiry are shaped by the biases health providers carry, and racialised participants experienced additional surveillance. In the following passage about reciprocal IVF, the egg donor, Meekah, identifies as South Asian and colonised, and the gestational parent, Tico, identifies as White:

Meekah : [A] question came up around [the baby’s] skin colour…

Tico : …[the baby] came out very dark, like much darker than he is now, which is, I think, uncommon…

Meekah : Our midwives had to navigate the questions of what sperm did you use and whose, and that question wouldn’t come up in a heterosexual [relationship]…or maybe it would if you’re using donor sperm. They had to navigate this conversation with us around, ‘Ok, you used Meekah’s egg and Meekah is brown, but the sperm—where does that land on skin colour and ethnicity, because we need to know.’ [The midwives] approached it really well…it felt like a medical‐ish question.

(Meekah, South Asian colonised queer nonbinary woman; Tico, Afro‐Brazilian and White queer nonbinary person)

In this scenario, the newborn's skin tone was unexpected by the midwife, which prompted ‘medical‐ish’ questions about the donor sperm. The subjective assessment of darker skin tone would not indicate a medical concern per se; however, assessment of the newborn's overall skin colour is included in routine APGAR scoring. The APGAR score is designed to provide an objective basis for assessing a newborn's overall condition at one and 5 min after birth. Newborn skin colour is included among the five criteria, with the optimal colour being pink. The inclusion of skin colour in the APGAR score, designed within a White‐normative framework, reflects and perpetuates systemic racism in these medical assessments. Review studies demonstrate how assessments introduce racial bias into APGAR scoring (Edwards et al. 2023; Grünebaum et al. 2023), because all babies are measured against this White normative assessment, and call for the removal of skin colour in APGAR scoring (Grünebaum et al. 2023). When the standard for normal skin colour is pink, observation of darker skin tone has race‐based surveillance effects on melanated babies and their families, leading to marginalisation and medicalisation. Asking questions about paternity once the baby's skin colour is noticed instead of during medical history taking demonstrates how lines of inquiry have unequal effects of surveillance across queer family building, kinship and identity.

Discussion

During midwifery care, QTNB people participate in technologies of pregnancy surveillance, which function towards the optimisation of the foetus itself and the pregnancy outcome. We argue that lines of inquiry, which are information‐seeking practices that collect information about the baby's genetic makeup and how the baby was ‘made’ are central to how technologies of surveillance operate. Lines of inquiry deploy questions within the purview of biomedical knowledge that is necessary to know yet also present in mundane, insidious assumptions about paternity and conception. Stemming from broader biomedical discourse that conflates sex and gender and views them as genetically determined and binary, the discursive effects of lines of inquiry render invisible QTNB identities, family building processes and kinship structures, thereby illegitimating the affirmation that this is how we make our family.

As a feminist model of anti‐oppression care, midwifery resists biomedicine through its unique value set but also relies on biomedicalisation to function as an integrated health profession. This unique situatedness exposes a tension between distinct value systems (J. Goldberg 2024, 32–35). In accessing technologies of pregnancy surveillance, QTNB people traverse this boundary, experiencing the effects of biomedicalisation despite being in midwifery care, which they view as anti‐normative or queer (J. Goldberg 2024, 32–33). As such, although biomedical integration is essential for midwifery to operate, findings show it affects QTNB midwifery service‐users, resulting in the invisibilisation of their identities, kinship structures and family‐building processes.

Previous research shows the profound impact of reproductive technologies on the reproductive experiences of lesbians, drawing connections between heteronormativity and the marginalisation of lesbian reproductive practices (Nordqvist 2008). Our findings show that this impact persists into the childbearing period, sustained by pregnancy technologies that continue to marginalise reproductive practices by rendering queer family building processes invisible. In contrast, research on heterosexual couples using reproductive technologies suggests their challenges are often framed as identity transitions, particularly from infertility to parenthood (René et al. 2022). Although significant, these experiences are largely contained within cisheteronormative biomedical frameworks that affirm and legitimise cisgender heterosexual parenthood (Tam et al. 2025). By comparison, the identity struggles described by our participants are produced by cisheteronormative assumptions embedded within technologies of surveillance, which impose ideas about who is a parent and what family looks like, effectively invisibilizing queer experiences.

Further work shows how cisheteronormativity within biomedicine overlooks the diverse needs and identities of transgender individuals accessing reproductive services, leading to marginalisation within health systems and limiting access to appropriate reproductive care (Soled et al. 2023). Our analysis expands on this work, showing how effects of technologies follow into pregnancy with similar consequences for QTNB people. Our findings also expand the literature on heteronormativity in midwifery (J. Goldberg and Ross 2022) by showing the impact of cisheteronormativity. Further, research examining childbirth experiences of lesbian, bisexual and queer nonbiological mothers shows how heteronormative language deployed on intake forms and by health care providers (Wojnar and Katzenmeyer 2014) and lack of acknowledgement of their identity and role as a parent (Abelsohn et al. 2013; Howat et al. 2023) contribute to feeling excluded and overlooked during the childbirth process. Our study expands on this literature by showing how trans and nonbinary non‐gestational partners were impacted by cisheteronorms.

Inherent within surveillance technologies is the biomedical discourse of the optimised baby, and what matters to know—genetic history—is sought through the deployment of lines of inquiry. In this way, lines of inquiry hold discursive power and function as part of the process of optimizing pregnancy. Technologies of pregnancy surveillance monitor and surveil the baby and optimise life itself, and in so doing, legitimate the notion that parents are securing the ‘best possible futures’ (Clarke and Shim 2010, 23) for their family. As such, engaging with technologies of pregnancy surveillance is as much about mitigating risk to the foetus as it is about actualizing optimal futures (Clarke and Shim 2010). What has remained less clear are the social effects of foetal optimisation on QTNB people.

Our analysis makes these social effects known by showing how disciplinary power works through risk technologies, aiming to produce docile (Foucault 1977) pregnant subjects who conform to cisheteronormative sex and gender norms. Clarke et al. (2003) argues that biomedical risk technologies are normalizing by establishing standard models against which objects and actions are evaluated. Although we agree risk technologies produce and reproduce standardisation, in the case of a pregnant subject, risk technologies are indeed trying to produce conforming, or docile (Foucault 1977), bodies.

Lupton (2012b) contends that pregnant subjects are disciplined and regulated by biomedical discourse about what matters to know and therefore optimise in pregnancy. If the foetus takes precedent over the gestational parent, then the parent's role is self‐regulation in the interest of optimizing the foetus and foetal future. In this way, gestational parents and their foetuses are inscribed as biomedical subjects by biomedical discourse regulating and defining the meaning ascribed to those subjects (Lupton 2012b). Such ‘reproductive citizenship’ (Lupton 2012b) ensures parents are making the best possible choices for their children's wellbeing. Hence, the docile pregnant body produces the optimised foetus, but our analysis shows how lines of inquiry are deployed by these Trojan horse technologies, exerting their disciplinary power over QTNB people. Lines of inquiry aim to produce docile bodies by holding QTNB subjects to the normative standard of heterosexuality and the gender binary. If the body is constructed through medical discourse and practice, then discourses of sex and gender, and of heterosexuality, are written on the service users; this is how biomedicine sees bodies. In other words, power operates through normativity and provides a place for these technologies to do their work (Jackson 2006), which has consequences for QTNB people.

In the end, biomedical lines of inquiry exert discursive power through the hegemonic standpoint that sex and gender are one and the same, genetically determined and binary. The effect of this is control of gender (who makes the baby) and—through conflation—sex, and control of sexuality (how the baby is made). The designation of ‘male’ or ‘female’ makes legible certain processes that go unchallenged until someone does not ‘fit’ within the system and calls it into question (Currah 2022). QTNB family‐building does just this: it expands upon ‘who and what makes a parent’ (Mamo 2018, 26) by disrupting the gender norm of ‘mother’ and ‘father’ as well the heterosexual norm of how families are constituted (Mamo 2018).

Given the uneven effects of technologies of surveillance via stratified biomedicalisation, racialised bodies are held to White‐normative biomedical standards, as seen in the APGAR scoring. Racialised bodies disrupt the routine practices of measurement against this standard, thereby inscribing the non‐White‐normative body as non‐docile, because it inherently resists measures to shape the foetal future towards these biomedical norms. Within biomedicine, cisheteronormativity operates as a system of power that normalises socially constructed categories of race, class, gender and sexuality, which in turn has the effect of creating multiple and intersecting oppressions (Cohen 1997), thereby reinscribing White normativity on queer and trans racialised bodies. This process results in additional layers of surveillance of racialised bodies with uneven and harmful consequences.

Our study and analysis have several strengths. QTNB people are more likely to resist biomedicine and seek out midwifery as a queer alternative due to previous or anticipated negative or harmful experiences in mainstream biomedical care (Scheim et al. 2013; Soled et al. 2023). Yet, because midwifery functions within and relies upon biomedicine, it serves as an interesting entry point providing insight into how QTNB experience biomedicalised care. In taking up a theoretically informed examination of cisheteronormativity within biomedicine, its implications extend beyond midwifery into perinatal health services. The sample reflected a strong representation of identities; however, being limited to English‐speaking participants, our sample overlooked QTNB non‐English speaking immigrants and minoritised linguistic groups, whose perspectives are crucial to understanding cisheteronormativity.

Recommendations

Participants wanted health professionals to ask about family building and kinship in a way that demonstrated understanding of their choices. For example, health providers can ask questions about family building and kinship that collect necessary medical information and affirm QTNB people by asking those involved in family building who they are to each other, what their roles are in family building and what that process entailed. These approaches can be included within health professional education programs.

The skill and confidence of collecting necessary medical information and affirming identity hones over time with practice. Approaching history taking with openness and avoiding assumptions is a starting point. Awareness of how partners/coparents may be grappling with their roles in family formation should encourage care providers to ensure lines of inquiry are not exclusionary and invisibilizing. This requires a balance between thorough medical history taking and relational aspects of care. When mistakes are made, providers can apologise and continue.

Care providers should be aware of how engaging with ultrasound, prenatal genetic screening and medical history taking, creates additional navigational challenges for QTNB people. Structural awareness is appreciated by QTNB and helps them feel more seen in the struggles they often face within cisheteronormative health systems (J. Goldberg 2024, 89). The unrecognised labour or ‘queer tax’ (Tam et al. 2025) that goes into navigating and negotiating biomedical cisheteronorms occurs when accessing these commonly available and popular technologies of pregnancy surveillance, which include routine lines of inquiry into how the baby was made and by whom. Care providers should also be aware of how certain routine perinatal practices, such as assessment of a newborn's skin tone, are entrenched in White normativity, which impact QTNB service‐users who are racialised and/or Indigenous.

Reproductive health diagnostic professionals (e.g., ultrasound technologists) are key to shaping QTNB people's care experiences. Future work could examine the attitudes, knowledge, skills or behaviours of reproductive health diagnostic professionals towards QTNB people, given the dearth of investigation in this area. Similarly, health care institutions providing technologies of pregnancy surveillance can develop policies for working with QTNB patients, including the use of gender‐inclusive language. Indeed, midwives and perinatal care providers often work in settings that are resource‐constrained; limited time and financial support can restrict opportunities for training and pose challenges to capacity‐building. Further, the perception that QTNB people represent a small proportion of patients can lead to the belief that it is difficult and perhaps not relevant to learn about the needs of QTNB people accessing care (Mikulak et al. 2021). Previous work has shown how midwives often believe that providing the same care to all regardless of sexual orientation or gender identity is good care (J. M. Goldberg et al. 2023). Yet, inclusive and affirming perinatal care require consistency around not making assumptions, and as findings here show, asking all patients about family building, kinship and identity, not just those who ‘seem’ part of the QTNB community. Our recommendation to shift lines of inquiry towards acknowledging the narrative this is how we make our family will make a meaningful difference in the perinatal care experiences of all midwifery and perinatal service‐users.
