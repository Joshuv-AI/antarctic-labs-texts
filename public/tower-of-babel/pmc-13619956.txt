Decoding digital transformation in health professions education: a comparative analysis of knowledge, attitudes, and readiness among faculty and students at KSAU-HS
Authors: Amal I. Khalil, Lamyaa Y. ALyaba, Amnah M. Jambi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDigital transformation is altering educational processes in health professions and needs instructors and students to acquire competencies for its implementation. At the same time, comparative information about readiness for digital transformation for educators and students in Saudi Arabian universities is scarce and the reasons for such readiness are not fully known. This study reviewed the knowledge, attitude and readiness of instructors and learners at King Saud bin Abdulaziz University for Health Sciences.MethodsThe study used a cross-sectional comparative design. Data was collected from the respondents with the help of a specially designed questionnaire, based on the Technology Acceptance Model. The questionnaire measured digital competency, perceived usefulness and ease of use and facilitating conditions.ResultsRespondents displayed a slightly more developed level of digital competence, perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and readiness for digital transformation. As for students, motivation and confidence in one's ability to work with digital technologies turned out to be the best predictors. Structural equation modeling has validated that usefulness and ease of use along with facilitating conditions represent significant factors for predicting the intention to use digital technologies. Hence, psychology and organizational aspects play a bigger role in being ready for the digital transformation than demographic variables.DiscussionThe readiness for digital transformation in health professions education relates to not only the technical aspect but also to individual motivation, confidence, and institutional backing. Therefore, universities should develop specific strategies to motivate and encourage students to learn, along with training faculty, giving sufficient resources, and offering suitable organizational conditions.

Introduction

Digital transformation in education involves the use of new technologies, such as e-learning systems, artificial intelligence (AI), and data analysis, in teaching. This shift is changing how teaching, learning, and assessment happen, especially in colleges and universities. The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the adoption of digital tools and online learning at all levels, prompting universities to move quickly to digital teaching (1, 2). In Saudi Arabia, digital transformation in education supports Vision 2030, which calls for better digital tools and new technology to help the country develop (3).

Universities such as KSAU are increasingly using digital tools to offer blended learning, online classes, and digital materials. These changes make learning and teaching more flexible, personalized, and engaging for both teachers and students (4, 5). However, digital transformation will only work if teachers and students know how to use the new technology and are willing to adopt it.

In these environments, digital literacy is seen as essential knowledge for both educators and students. For faculty, this means being proficient with online teaching tools, such as learning management systems (LMSs), and in creating digital educational materials (6). Al Ghamdi et al. state that a key part of student learning in digital settings is possessing the necessary knowledge, including skills for using digital platforms and online resources (7). Qureshi et al. (8) add that e-learning works well only if instructors have sufficient digital skills; otherwise, targeted training is needed.

On the other hand, the shift towards digital learning in higher education presents numerous obstacles. For instance, many faculty members struggle to incorporate digital tools effectively, often due to inadequate training, limited resources, or insufficient institutional backing. Students, on the other hand, may encounter difficulties accessing and navigating online learning platforms (7, 9). The primary hurdle, however, is educators’ resistance to digital transformation, as they frequently doubt the effectiveness of online learning and fear that digital tools may undermine traditional teaching methods (10). By recognizing these challenges, institutions can better understand the factors that facilitate or hinder digital learning, enabling them to develop solutions that ease the transition process.

The success of digital transformation depends on the attitudes of both educators and students. Positive attitudes facilitate the adoption of new technology, while resistance often stems from fears of becoming outdated or of lacking sufficient support (11, 12). For example, Yan and Pourdavood (9) found that students and faculty with positive views of digital education are more likely to support it. In contrast, those with negative views may see it as a loss of traditional teaching. Al-Asiri (13) says that successful digital transformation requires not only management support but also a culture that encourages innovation. Being prepared means having digital skills, supportive environments, and a willingness to change among faculty and students (14, 15). This also requires access to resources and training, as well as strong institutional support, to overcome challenges (6, 10).

A particularly pertinent context for evaluating digital transformation readiness in KSAU-HS, one of the most distinguished health professions universities in Saudi Arabia, which has progressively integrated digital technologies into academic administration, teaching, and learning. Limited evidence exists regarding the preparedness of faculty members and students to participate in digital transformation initiatives within this context, despite ongoing investments in digital infrastructure and educational technologies. It is crucial to comprehend these viewpoints, as the successful implementation of digital innovations is contingent upon technological resources and the attitudes, competence, and willingness of users to adopt new systems. By examining both faculty and student perspectives within a single health professions institution, this study contributes to the growing body of research on digital health and educational transformation by identifying factors associated with digital transformation readiness and technology acceptance.

Research shows that students at institutions with strong digital support systems are better able to adapt to online learning (8). Faculty at these institutions also have more chances to get training and improve their digital skills (16). Assessing readiness is important for managing finances during digital transformation, as new technologies and training programs require significant investment. Institutions need to ensure these resources are used effectively. For example, Aydin (14) points out that if implementation is not planned, resources can be wasted on unused or ineffective tools. In the end, without sufficient training for faculty and students, these technologies may not yield a good return on investment (16).

Although there is an increasing focus on digital transformation in higher education, significant questions remain about how well faculty and students are equipped to embrace and use digital technologies effectively in learning environments. Earlier research has highlighted various potential obstacles that could affect digital transformation efforts, such as differences in digital skills, availability of technological resources, institutional support, and readiness to embrace new technologies (6, 10, 14, 17). Nonetheless, data on these elements in Saudi health professions education remain scarce, particularly at KSAU-HS. Gaining an understanding of the knowledge, perspectives, and preparedness of faculty and students is crucial for recognizing strengths, tackling obstacles, and guiding specific strategies to facilitate effective digital transformation. This study sought to compare faculty and students in terms of their knowledge, attitudes, and preparedness for digital transformation, and to determine the factors associated with digital transformation readiness at KSAU-HS.

Significance of the study

Digital transformation is now seen as one of the most important parts of the future of higher education, including health professions education. Digital skills affect both learning outcomes and clinical practice, and Education 4.0 expects faculty and students to be ready to use advanced technology in teaching and learning. Many studies show that having enough knowledge, positive attitudes, and readiness to use digital tools are important for both groups (18, 19). Research also shows that digital readiness varies across groups. Student engagement depends on students’ preparedness to use technology, while faculty need strong digital confidence and sufficient institutional support to use technology effectively and teach well (20, 21). Still, there are barriers to digital transformation, such as not enough training and resistance to change (22).

In Saudi Arabia, digital transformation is a national priority, yet little research has examined the preparedness of academic communities in health sciences education for this change. This study aims to assess the readiness of faculty and students, as well as their learning environment. The findings will help institutions improve their support for effective digital transformation in health education.

Theoretical & conceptual framework

Figure 1 summarizes that current research is framed by established theories of technology adoption, namely the technology acceptance model and the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology. These frameworks provide a thorough understanding of how individuals form their perceptions, attitudes, and behavioral intentions towards digital technology.

Theoretical conceptual framework based on the technology acceptance model (TAM) and unified theory of acceptance and Use of technology (UTAUT). The framework illustrates the hypothesized relationships among digital competence, perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, facilitating conditions, attitude toward use, behavioral intention, and digital transformation readiness. The model was developed prior to data collection and guided the study hypotheses and structural equation modeling analyses.

The technology acceptance model (TAM) posits that an individual’s use of a technology is influenced primarily by perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEU), which, in turn, shape attitudes and intentions (23). Digital technology will be used in this research as a foundational determinant of how an individual’s level of digital knowledge influences their perception and behavior. The higher an individual’s digital knowledge, the more likely they are to develop positive attitudes toward digital transformation, as they perceive these technologies as valuable and manageable (24).

The unified theory of acceptance and use of technology builds on previous theories by incorporating additional factors, such as facilitating conditions, social influence, and behavioral intention, into a conceptual framework explaining how one adopts a technology (24). In this framework, attitudinal response refers to an individual’s view of digital transformation. In contrast, readiness to adopt it refers to an individual’s preparedness to use digital systems in an educational institution. As a result, institutional support, training, and access to digital infrastructure are considered enabling factors that increase readiness (25). The framework was developed prior to data collection and served as the theoretical basis for the study’s hypotheses and subsequent structural equation modeling analyses. Study design

This study used a comparative cross-sectional design among faculty members and students at KSAU-HS to assess and compare their knowledge, attitudes, and readiness for digital transformation. This design enabled efficient data collection from a diverse sample and provided a snapshot of participants’ perspectives at a single point in time, allowing comparisons between the two groups (26, 27).

A conceptual framework was developed based on the (TAM)and t (UTA) and (UT) (23, 24). The framework proposed that knowledge influences attitudes toward digital transformation, which in turn affects readiness, while knowledge also exerts a direct effect on readiness. Study area/setting

The study was conducted at (KSAU-HS), a medical university with several campuses across Saudi Arabia. The institution includes four schools: Medicine, Nursing, Applied Medical Sciences, and Health Professions. These campuses support a diverse academic environment and are equipped with digital infrastructure aligned with Vision 2030’s goals for integrating digital technologies into education and healthcare. The university’s focus on innovation and modernization made it an appropriate setting for examining digital transformation practices, perceptions, and effects within its academic community.

Study subjects

The study included students and faculty from all four schools. This diverse group provided perspectives on digital transformation from varied educational and professional backgrounds within healthcare.

Inclusion criteria

Students

Undergraduate and postgraduate learners from the four schools (Medicine, Nursing, Applied Medical Sciences, Health Professions) were included. Their academic and experiential levels help clarify how digital transformation tools are used and accessed in healthcare education.

Faculty members

Professors, lecturers, and instructors play a vital role in adapting to digital changes in teaching. Participants represented a cross-section of the KSAU-HS community, providing a holistic view of the effects, challenges, and successes of digital transformation at this healthcare-focused academic institution.

Sample size

The Roe Soft online program was used to calculate the sample size, targeting 377 students from an estimated population of 20,000 and 169 faculty members from approximately 300 faculty members across three university cities. This calculation used a 95% confidence level (Z-score of 1.96) and a 5% margin of error, balancing accuracy and feasibility. Anticipating a 50% response rate, oversampling was necessary to achieve the desired sample size.

Sampling technique

The study used stratified random sampling and convenience sampling to collect data from the university community. Stratified random sampling involved dividing the population into groups, such as students and faculty, based on university records. Students were then grouped by college—Medicine, Nursing, Applied Medical Sciences, and Health Professions—to reflect their different experiences with digital transformation.

Each group’s sample size was determined by its share of the total population. For instance, if students made up 70% of the university, they also made up 70% of the sample. Each college was included in proportion to its size. Within each group, simple random sampling ensured everyone had an equal chance of being chosen. Combining stratified random sampling with convenience sampling helped ensure the sample represented the university community and made the findings more generalizable.

Data collection methods, instruments used, and measurements: the study’s objectives were met using three main tools

Demographic information was collected, including age, gender, education level, marital status, number of children, area of residence, KSAU college attended, and whether they participated in digital transformation workshops.

The Digital Transformation Readiness Scale for Education and Universities measured readiness in infrastructure, resources, adaptability, perceived benefits, digital skills, and intent to use digital tools. Items adapted from Aroean et al. (28) focused on preparedness in education, digital skills, and institutional support, and the scale also included elements from Beetham and Sharpe’s (2013) Digital Capability Framework (29).

Psychometric properties

The constructs targeted Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.70 or higher, whereas similar studies often reported values above 0.80. Validity was assessed through expert review, factor analysis, and examination of the relationship between readiness scores and technology use. This approach ensured a thorough and reliable assessment.

The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) Scale is a widely used tool that focuses on two main ideas: perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. It helps measure how people accept and use technology. Developed by Davis (25), TAM explains why people adopt and use technology. The updated version also includes Attitude Toward Use and Behavioral Intention to Use. Sample items are: “Digital tools are advantageous for my academic work” (Perceived Usefulness), “My outlook on using digital tools in education is positive” (Attitude Toward Use), “I intend to increase my use of digital tools in educational activities” (Behavioral Intention to Use), and “I find it straightforward to learn new technologies” (Perceived Ease of Use). The original TAM constructs have shown strong internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values typically ranging from 0.70 to 0.95 (23). Later studies, such as Venkatesh and Bala’s (30), have confirmed the model’s reliability and validity across different settings. The adapted TAM Scale is effective for studying technology acceptance in education and for evaluating digital transformation efforts.

The TAM Scale used a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). For sections with four items, scores were summed, yielding a total of 4 (lowest acceptance) to 20 (highest acceptance). Scores of 16–20 indicated positive attitudes and a strong intention to use technology. Scores of 11–15 indicated neutral attitudes, with some willingness but also some doubts. Scores of 4–10 indicated negative attitudes and concerns about technology’s usefulness or ease of use.

The Teachers’ Digital Competence (TDC) Scale, developed by the European Commission’s Joint Research Center as part of the Dig Comp Edu framework, measures teachers’ digital skills and how well they use digital tools in teaching. It assesses skills such as creating digital content, digital communication, using digital teaching strategies, understanding data, keeping online activities safe, conducting assessments, and improving digital skills through professional development.

The TDC Scale has 21 items across seven areas. It measures how ready and able teachers are to use digital resources in education. Each item is rated from 1 (“Not Competent”) to 5 (“Highly Competent”), giving a detailed view of teachers’ digital skills. The TDC Scale has demonstrated robust reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha of 0.80 or higher, indicating strong internal consistency across domains. It also demonstrates construct and content validity, corresponding with established digital competence frameworks, making it a dependable tool for evaluating educators’ digital readiness.

The Students' Digital Competence (SDC) Scale was adapted from DigComp 2.2: The Digital Competence Framework for Citizens, as described by Vuorikari et al. (31). It measures students’ digital skills across education levels, including how they use digital environments, manage digital tools, and follow safe and ethical online practices. It consists of five key areas, each comprising five items, focusing on fundamental digital skills, information literacy, communication, problem-solving, and ethical awareness. It uses a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“Not Competent”) to 5 (“Highly Competent”), to measure students’ self-assessment. This scale had a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.85 or higher, indicating good internal consistency. It aligns with major digital competence frameworks and effectively measures how well students adapt and perform in digital environments.

Reliability for the current study

Cronbach’s alpha was 0.903 for Students’ Digital Competence, 0.896 for Students’ TAM, 0.901 for Students’ Digital Transformation Readiness, and 0.892 for Faculty’s Digital Competence.

Data collection procedure

The data collection approach for the KSAU-HS digital transformation study was systematic and multifaceted. It aimed to secure reliable responses from students and faculty in all four academic colleges. After obtaining approvals from the research unit, King Abdullah International Medical center (KAIMRC), and the (International review Board) IRB, the team created a structured English-language survey in Google Forms. The survey asked about the benefits, challenges, and effects of digital transformation on academic and administrative work. A pilot study tested the survey for clarity, relevance, and reliability.

Once the survey was ready, electronic invitations were sent to students and faculty through official university emails and digital platforms, including the online learning management system. This approach made sure everyone across the campuses had access and encouraged participation. The survey was open for four months, giving everyone enough time to complete it. Weekly reminders were sent to encourage participation and improve response rates.

Participation was tracked during data collection. If response rates were low, follow-up reminders were sent to non-responders, and additional outreach, such as college announcements, was used to increase engagement.

All responses were kept anonymous to protect confidentiality. The data was stored securely in a password-protected database accessible only by the research team.

Data Verification: After the survey closed, the team carefully checked the data for completeness. Any invalid or incomplete responses were removed before analysis. This step helped ensure the final dataset was accurate and representative of the university community.

This organized approach to data collection aimed to create a complete dataset that reflects the different views on digital transformation among students and faculty at KSAU-HS.

Data management and analysis plan

The data analysis was conducted using the latest version of Statistical Package for the Social Sciences. (SPSS). First, descriptive statistics summarized demographic details and responses about knowledge, attitudes, and readiness among faculty and students, using means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages. To compare the two groups, independent-samples t-tests were used, or Mann–Whitney U tests if the data were not normally distributed. To examine relationships within each group, Pearson’s correlation or Spearman’s rho (for nonnormal data) was used to measure the strength and direction of associations. Multiple regression analysis examined how well knowledge and attitudes predicted readiness for digital transformation, while controlling for demographic factors. Chi-square tests were also used to determine whether demographic characteristics were associated with readiness or attitudes. This approach provided a thorough overview of the main variables and helped make recommendations to improve digital transformation readiness.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS for Windows version 20.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL). Continuous data were normally distributed and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical data were presented as numbers and percentages. The chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test when needed, compared categorical variables. The correlation coefficient test assessed the relationship between two continuous variables, and linear regression was used to predict one variable from the other. The internal consistency (reliability) of the questionnaires was also calculated, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.All statistical results reported in the manuscript were verified against the final analytical outputs to ensure consistency in coefficients, significance levels, confidence intervals, and effect size estimates across all tables, figures, and narrative descriptions.

To study how different factors affect digital transformation, we used a covariance-based structural equation modeling (CB-SEM) approach in IBM SPSS AMOS. Figure 8 represents the model which was based on the (TAM) and included psychological readiness (confidence and motivation), behavioral exposure (PC or laptop use), and organizational support (digital transformation training) as factors that influence key TAM elements and the intention to use digital technologies. The results show clear links between socio-demographic and digital-use factors and the main components of TAM. Overall, the model provides moderate support for TAM, with several significant connections aligning with our expectations.

Standardized path coefficients are shown. Significant paths are indicated by *p < 0.05 and **p < 0.01. The overall model demonstrated acceptable fit (χ²/df = 1.28, CFI = 0.999, TLI = 0.997, RMSEA = 0.029, and SRMR = 0.008).

Considering the external factors, the combined set of socio-demographic and usage characteristics influenced parts of TAM in different ways. The biggest impact was on perceived usefulness (β = 0.29, p < 0.01), showing that background and digital readiness strongly shape people’s perception of the value of digital technologies. There was also a smaller but still important effect on ease of use (β = 0.18, p < 0.05), showing that these factors help shape how easy people perceive the technology to be.

On the other hand, the effects on attitude toward use (β = 0.19) and supporting conditions (β = 0.14) were weaker. This means that feelings about using technology and the sense of support from infrastructure are less directly influenced by background and usage factors. It suggests that attitudes are shaped more by thinking and beliefs than by demographics.

Within the TAM model, the results matched the theory’s predictions. Perceived usefulness (β = 0.17, p < 0.05) and ease of use (β = 0.21, p < 0.05) both had clear positive effects on intention to use technology, underscoring their importance for adoption. Support from the organization and technology (β = 0.21, p < 0.05) was also important, highlighting its role in digital engagement.

Although attitude toward use (β = 0.13, p < 0.05) was associated with intention to use technology, the small effect size suggests it accounts for only part of the relationship. Instead, behavioral intention is influenced more by direct thoughts about usefulness and ease of use than by feelings alone. Overall, the model shows that socio-demographic and usage factors affect behavioral intention indirectly through the TAM elements. The effect sizes are small to moderate, indicating that the model performs well in theory. To sharpen its implications, add factors such as digital self-efficacy, organizational culture, or perceived risk.

Because the study aimed to compare faculty members and students, the structural relationships were examined separately for each group. The findings demonstrated that the strength of associations among digital transformation readiness factors and TAM constructs varied between faculty and students, supporting the presence of group-specific patterns in technology acceptance and readiness for digital transformation. For the multi-group analysis, faculty and student groups were examined separately and then compared using a constrained structural-path model. The unconstrained model was compared with a model in which structural paths were constrained to be equal across groups. The chi-square difference test indicated a significant difference between the unconstrained and constrained models, Δχ² (8) = 17.64, p = 0.024, suggesting that some structural paths differed between faculty and students, despite the overall acceptable model fit.

Discussion

This study evaluated five hypotheses based on the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and associated theories of technology adoption. The findings confirmed all five of the hypotheses(H). Digital competence positively impacted attitudes towards digital transformation (H1) and directly improved readiness (H 3). Additionally, attitudes significantly enhanced readiness (H 2). Moreover, attitudes partially mediated the relationship between digital competence and readiness (H 4). The multigroup analysis indicated variations between faculty members and students regarding the strength of these relationships and the factors affecting readiness (H 5), underscoring the need for tailored strategies for different groups to advance digital transformation in health professions education.

This study offers significant insights into digital transformation within health professions education by examining the digital skills, technology acceptance, and preparedness of faculty and students at KSAU-HS. The results suggest that readiness for digital transformation encompasses more than just access to technology; it is also shaped by psychological, technological, and organizational factors, including perceived usefulness, ease of use, facilitating conditions, confidence, and institutional support. These outcomes are consistent with the current literature, which highlights that effective digital transformation in health professions education necessitates both individual competence and organizational preparedness. This is especially crucial as digital technologies are increasingly incorporated into teaching, clinical training, telehealth, artificial intelligence, and data-driven healthcare practices (32–35).

The SEM analysis provided strong evidence for the (TAM), revealing that perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and facilitating conditions significantly impacted the intention to adopt technology. These findings align with earlier research, indicating that both individual and organizational elements influence technology acceptance through TAM constructs. For instance, Mastour et al. identified perceived usefulness and ease of use as key factors in technology acceptance among faculty and students. Similarly, Lee et al. (36) emphasized TAM’s ongoing relevance for understanding technology adoption in health professions education, particularly regarding institutional support and learner characteristics (36, 37). The current results underscore the necessity of developing user-friendly digital environments backed by sufficient institutional resources.

Faculty members exhibited a notably higher level of digital preparedness, perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, positive attitudes, and intention to use technology than students. This disparity might be attributed to their increased interaction with institutional digital platforms, online teaching systems, academic management tools, and professional development opportunities. Similar conclusions have been drawn in research indicating that healthcare educators’ digital readiness is bolstered by their experience, confidence in using technology, and the alignment of technology with their professional duties (21, 36). However, some research has indicated that younger learners often display greater familiarity with technology than faculty members (37). Nevertheless, the current findings imply that being accustomed to everyday digital technologies does not automatically equate to readiness for academic, professional, or clinical contexts. Although students reported frequent use of technology, the considerable variation in their readiness and confidence suggests that mere exposure to technology may not be sufficient to cultivate professional digital competence.

Confidence in adapting to digital environments emerged as a key predictor for both student groups. It played a significant role in forecasting digital competence among faculty and students and in shaping students’ perceptions of usefulness, ease of use, attitudes, and intentions to engage. These results align with growing evidence highlighting digital self-efficacy as a crucial factor in successful digital transformation. Sun and Yoon demonstrated that digital self-efficacy mediates the relationship between digital transformation efforts and educational outcomes, emphasizing that infrastructure alone is insufficient for successful adoption (21). Likewise, research in medical education has underscored the role of digital competence in facilitating self-directed learning, engagement, and effective use of digital information resources (38). Consequently, confidence should be viewed as a changeable outcome that can be enhanced through training, practice, and supportive learning environments.

Motivation was identified as the most significant predictor of students’ digital competence, greatly impacting both facilitating conditions and their preparedness for online learning. This aligns with existing research highlighting the critical roles of engagement, relevance, and self-directed learning in digital education settings (38, 39). According to Kröplin et al., involvement in digital health education activities enhances students’ perception of the importance of digital health and boosts their confidence in using digital technologies (40). Conversely, motivation had a relatively minor effect on faculty readiness, suggesting that organizational expectations, workload, professional duties, and institutional support may play a more substantial role in faculty adoption of AI tools.

The finding that formal digital transformation training improved facilitating conditions and readiness among faculty further highlights the importance of structured professional development (46). Previous European studies have similarly reported fragmented digital health training among healthcare professionals and emphasized the need for systematic educational initiatives to address competency gaps (41, 42). While faculty appear to benefit from dedicated training programs, students may develop digital competence more effectively through curriculum-integrated learning experiences, including simulations, digital case studies, telehealth applications, artificial intelligence tools, and other authentic learning activities, underscoring the value of integrating digital learning throughout the curriculum.

Another significant discovery was the restricted impact of socio-demographic traits on preparedness for digital transformation. In addition to a slight link between marital status and student digital skills, demographic factors were typically poor indicators. This indicates that changeable psychological and organizational factors may have a stronger impact on preparedness for digital transformation than unchangeable personal traits. While international research has highlighted links among age, education, prior digital exposure, and digital skills (43, 44), the relatively uniform academic setting and common technological framework at KSAU-HS might account for the limited demographic influences observed in this study.

Correlation analyses provide further insight into the disparities between faculty and students. Within the faculty, digital competence was primarily linked to perceived ease of use and supportive conditions, indicating that technology adoption is heavily influenced by practical factors such as usability and task efficiency. These results align with the task-technology fit theory, which suggests that technology is more likely to be adopted when it effectively supports users in fulfilling their professional duties and goals (21). Consequently, faculty development programs must focus on discipline-specific uses and practical incorporation into teaching and evaluation practices.

Conversely, students demonstrated more robust connections among TAM constructs, suggesting that technology adoption is more closely associated with attitudes, beliefs, and perceptions. Students who viewed digital technologies as beneficial and user-friendly exhibited more favorable attitudes, stronger behavioral intentions, and greater willingness to undergo digital transformation. Comparable results have been found in blended learning studies, highlighting the significance of digital literacy, perceived usefulness, and engagement in influencing students’ learning experiences (42). Nonetheless, research has indicated that students’ perceptions of digital learning can be affected by factors such as platform quality, workload, digital fatigue, and perceived relevance to clinical practice (20, 32). These results indicate that relying solely on technology use should not be seen as proof of readiness for a digital transformation.

While the suggested model accounted for considerable variation in technology acceptance and readiness, the small effect sizes observed for certain pathways suggest opportunities for further theoretical advancement. Future studies should include more constructs, such as digital self-efficacy, perceived risk, digital stress, organizational culture, data privacy issues, artificial intelligence readiness, and task–technology alignment. Recent findings indicate that modern digital transformation goes beyond conventional TAM assumptions and increasingly includes wider concerns related to ethics, data governance, workflow integration, and digital equity (45, 46). Combining TAM with additional frameworks, such as Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), models of organizational readiness, and task–technology fit theory, could offer a broader perspective on digital transformation in health professions education.

The results have multiple practical implications for education in health professions. Initiatives aimed at faculty must emphasize structured professional growth, practical training, peer mentorship, and ongoing technical support to enable the effective integration of digital tools into teaching, evaluation, simulation, learning analytics, and (AI)-enhanced education. For learners, teaching methods should prioritize boosting motivation, confidence, and involvement while integrating digital health skills throughout the program. Recent evidence shows that structured digital health education enhances students’ confidence, competence, and understanding of digital technologies in future healthcare practices (47, 48).

Strengths of the study

This study stands out for several reasons. First, it offers a thorough comparison of how digital transformation affects employees, using several analytical methods and including faculty and students in health professions education, an area that has not been widely studied.

Second, the study employs several analytical methods, including correlation analysis, multiple linear regression, and CB-SEM. This approach supports the findings with theory and data.

Third, the study is based on the (TAM). By adding components such as confidence, motivation, technology use, training, and support, the model explains the results more fully.

Fourth, the study identifies distinct patterns between faculty and students, highlighting the roles of student motivation and faculty organizational support. This comparative insight represents a key contribution to the literature and provides pragmatic implications for specific interventions.

Fifth, the study’s large, varied sample across several health fields makes the results more reliable and relevant to the institution.

Limitations of the study

While this study has strengths, it also has some limitations. First, because it uses a cross-sectional design, we cannot determine cause-and-effect relationships between variables. The structural equation model shows possible directions, but only long-term studies can confirm cause and timing.

Second, the data were collected via self-report questionnaires, which can introduce response biases, such as social desirability bias or recall inaccuracies.

Third, the study took place at only one university (KSAU-HS), so the results may not generalize to other schools or healthcare education systems with different resources, cultures, or levels of digital technology experience.

Fourth, students were selected using stratified sampling. However, some participants were also chosen for convenience. This could mean that people more interested in digital technology were more likely to participate, potentially introducing selection bias.

Fifth, the SEM model explained only a moderate amount of the results. This means other factors—digital self-confidence, workplace culture, perceived risks, digital stress, or access to technology—were excluded but may help explain digital transformation readiness.

Finally, the study examined TAM factors but did not measure digital skills or technology use. Measuring these would provide a fuller picture of the results of digital transformation.

Theoretical implications

This research contributes to the expanding body of work on digital transformation preparedness in health professions education by extending the use of the (TAM) within higher education in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. In line with TAM, perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness emerged as key factors influencing behavioral intention, reinforcing the ongoing significance of technology acceptance theory in studies on digital transformation.

The results emphasize the significance of psychological and organizational readiness elements in addition to conventional demographic traits. The significant impact of confidence on digital adaptation and motivation indicates that preparedness for digital transformation is shaped by broader self-efficacy and readiness factors that complement the conventional TAM variables. Furthermore, the differing predictors identified between faculty and students suggest that readiness for digital transformation may operate differently across stakeholder groups, underscoring the need for context-aware models of technology adoption in health professions education.

By combining perspectives on readiness, technology acceptance, and organizational support, this study enhances theoretical insight into how digital transformation can be effectively implemented in educational institutions undergoing rapid technological change.

Conclusion

Health professionals in higher education were studied to see how ready they are for using digital technologies and what factors shape their readiness. Results showed that psychological and organizational factors were more influential than demographic characteristics with regard to digital transformational readiness. Adapting to digital technology was the primary predictor of readiness among both faculty members and students, but motivation was particularly important for students. Faculty members needed to feel confident after receiving institution-supplied training and support in order to facilitate their ability to adapt and use digital technologies. Additionally, the results validated some elements of the Technology Acceptance Model, specifically perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and facilitating conditions were all found to contribute to the level of readiness for digital transformation in health professions education. In addition to contributing to the literature on digital transformation, this study has practical implications for successful implementation of digital transformation, based on individual and institutional factors. Therefore, educational organizations are encouraged to develop a holistic digital transformation strategy that includes (a) an appropriate technological infrastructure, (b) on-going technical support, (c) structured opportunities for training, and (d) supportive digital ecosystems. Establishment of monitoring and evaluation mechanisms to evaluate the readiness for digital transformation and the adoption of technology over time, will assist in developing continuous improvement and evidence-based decision-making.

Also, in relation to faculty development, the findings of this study suggest the need for sustained efforts in faculty development that emphasize hands-on training in digital pedagogy and instructional design and integration of technology; and the provision of peer mentoring and communities of practice to facilitate sharing of knowledge and innovation. In relation to students, educational organizations should develop an engaging and motivational learning environment for students that uses interactive, simulation-based, and case-based digital learning approaches. The inclusion of digital health competencies across the curriculum, as well as the provision of experiential learning opportunities (via telehealth simulation, AI applications, and other emerging technologies) will assist in preparing students for future practice in healthcare.

Digital transformation initiatives at the policy level must be aligned with national and institutional digital health strategies and integrated into curriculum planning and accreditation standards by means of establishing digital competence frameworks. Academic institutions are encouraged to collaborate with healthcare organizations to ensure that educational opportunities related to digital competency continue to meet the needs of the evolving clinical practice and workforce requirements.

Longitudinal, experimental, and multi-institutional designs should be used in future research to examine the causal relationships between digital healthcare technologies and evaluate the effectiveness of various interventions designed to improve digital readiness. Future research should examine additional variables such as digital self-efficacy, digital stress, organizational culture, and perceived risk, and should include considerate measures of digital competency and actual technology utilization in addition to cross-national comparative studies. This will improve the generalizability of the findings and allow for a comprehensive understanding of digital transformation readiness in health professions’ education.
