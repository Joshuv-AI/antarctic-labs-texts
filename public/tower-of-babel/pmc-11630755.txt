Espying sarcopenia in gastric cancer: squaring the circle
Authors: Ahmad J Abdulsalam, Murat Kara
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis letter to the editor remarks on the recently published article by Chan et al on the association of sarcopenia with chemotherapy toxicities in patients with gastric cancer.

We read with great interest the recently published article “Association of sarcopenia with severe chemotherapy toxicities and survival in patients with advanced gastric cancer” by Chan et al.1 We would like to congratulate the authors aiming to predict the association of sarcopenia and overall survival of gastric cancer along with the toxicities of chemotherapy. However, we would like to highlight a few points regarding their assessment of sarcopenia.

First, sarcopenia is characterized as the decline in both muscle mass and muscle function.2 In the study mentioned earlier, the researchers solely assessed skeletal muscle mass through computed tomography (CT) without also examining muscle function.2,3 Therefore, the diagnosis of sarcopenia requires the inclusion of muscle function assessment, ideally through tests like the chair stand test and grip strength evaluation. Additionally, longitudinal research has indicated that these assessments can predict significant negative health outcomes, particularly crucial for patients with cancer, such as falls and mortality.4,5

Secondly, they established criteria for sarcopenia using measurements obtained from CT.1 Instead, we suggest measuring the quadriceps muscle, which is affected earlier and more significantly in sarcopenia. This approach shows stronger correlations with physical function tests such as grip strength and gait speed.3 Furthermore, while some studies question the reliability of CT scans for measurement, we also find it impractical for routine clinical use.4

Lastly, recent research highlights the importance of measuring the quadriceps muscle, particularly in patients with cancer. In this regard, a recent study demonstrated a strong link between quadriceps muscle thickness (but not skeletal muscle mass measured by abdominal CT) with complications (ie, neutropenia) among patients with cancer undergoing chemotherapy.6 In summary, we support the assessment of quadriceps’ muscle function, and its thickness by using ultrasound, which could be safer, more appropriate, and convenient for cancer patients in a hospital environment.
