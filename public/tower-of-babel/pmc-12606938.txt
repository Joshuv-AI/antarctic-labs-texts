Unmet need for contraceptives and unintended pregnancies among adolescent girls and young women aged 15 to 24 in Zambia: insights from the Yathu Yathu trial
Authors: Anke Rotsaert, Mwelwa Phiri, Lucheka Sigande, Ab Schaap, Rosemary Zulu-Phiri, Melvin Simuyaba, Louis Mwape, Sian Floyd, Sarah Fidler, Richard Hayes, Musonda Simwinga, Helen Ayles, Bernadette Hensen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn Sub-Saharan Africa, unmet need for contraceptives often remains high among adolescent girls and young women (AGYW), leading to unintended pregnancies. Yathu Yathu, a cluster-randomised trial of a community-based intervention conducted in two communities in Lusaka, Zambia (2019–2021), aimed to provide sexual and reproductive health services, including contraceptive services, to adolescents and young people. The trial showed no impact on pregnancy related outcomes. Hence, this analysis examined the characteristics of AGYW aged 15–24 with an unmet need for modern contraceptives and who experienced an unintended pregnancy. We also explored access to and reach of contraceptive services through Yathu Yathu.MethodsSecondary analysis of Yathu Yathu endline survey data. Using logistic regression, adjusted for cluster, we explored factors associated with unmet need for modern contraceptives and unintended pregnancies among AGYW in the intervention arm and assessed whether these associations differed in the control arm. Using routinely collected service delivery data from the intervention arm, numbers of AGYW accessing hormonal contraceptive services were described by age, community, educational attainment, and marital status.ResultsIn the intervention arm, 395 AGYW ever had sex. Unmet need for modern contraceptives was 40.6% (101/249) among AGYW who had sex in the last 12 months and wished to prevent or space childbearing. Unmet need was higher among younger and single/never married AGYW; in control zones unmet need was additionally higher among those currently in school or with infrequent discussion about sexual health promotion with their sexual partners. Among AGYW ever having had sex, 42.1% (165/392) reported an unintended pregnancy. This was higher among AGYW aged 20–24 years and currently not in school, and additionally among those in control zones who reported food absence. According to the routinely collected data, 12.3% (859/7005) of AGYW in intervention zones initiated hormonal contraceptives; 53.6% (460/859) returned for refills. Uptake was higher among AGYW aged 20–24, residing in community 2, with no/primary education and being married/cohabiting (p < 0.001).ConclusionsThis study highlights persistent challenges in addressing unmet need for modern contraceptives and unintended pregnancies among AGYW, emphasising the need for targeted interventions to improve equitable access among younger and single/never married AGYW.Trial registrationNCT04060420; https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT04060420; and ISRCTN75609016. Introduction

Among the 1.9 billion women aged 15–49 in 2021, 1.1 billion had a need for contraceptive services [1]. An estimated 77% of these women used a modern contraceptive method and 8% a traditional method (e.g. withdrawal) [1]. In sub-Saharan African countries, the proportion of reproductive-aged women who have their need for modern contraceptives met is 56%, one of the lowest percentages globally [1]. Unmet need for contraceptives, defined as the percentage of reproductive-aged women who wish to prevent or space childbearing, but are not using any method of contraception [2], contributes substantially to unintended pregnancies [1]; adolescent girls and young women (AGYW) aged 15–24 are the group most vulnerable to unintended pregnancies [3, 4]. Annually, there are 21 million pregnancies among girls aged 15–19 years in low- and middle-income countries, half of which are unintended [4]. Failing to address unmet contraceptive need impacts women’s lives by increasing the risk of unintended pregnancies and thus maternal mortality and morbidity, unsafe abortions, and fostering poor educational and socioeconomic outcomes [5].

According to the Zambian 2018 Demographic and Health Survey (DHS), unmet need for contraceptives was 20% among married women aged 15–49 [6]. This is despite nearly universal knowledge of at least one modern contraceptive method among married women and men [7], and substantial progress in meeting the demand for modern contraceptive methods between 1990 and 2021 [1]. Additionally, the total fertility rate remains among the highest globally, with 4.2 children per woman, compared to 2.3 globally [8]. Nearly one in three Zambian adolescent girls aged 15 to 19 have given birth, with 6% of these adolescents aged 15 years [7].

Efforts to increase the use of modern contraceptives and improve access to sexual and reproductive health (SRH) services among AGYW in sub-Saharan African countries have predominantly concentrated on making services more youth-friendly, delivering information and education within educational institutions, and implementing mHealth innovations [9–12]. A quasi-experimental study in Malawi showed higher uptake of condoms and hormonal contraceptives among AGYW offered youth-friendly services, including youth-focused spaces, peer educators, and youth-friendly health providers, compared to standard of care [13]. In Tanzania, girl-friendly drug shops experienced a 33-fold increase in contraceptive uptake when providing free contraceptives (i.e. condoms, oral or emergency contraceptives) and free HIV self-testing kits in contrast to drug shops that only offered free HIV self-testing kits and contraceptives at a cost [14].

Yathu Yathu (“For us, by us”), a two-arm cluster-randomised trial (CRT), delivered free comprehensive SRH services through community-based spaces (hubs) with a “loyalty” card system to incentivise service use [15, 16]. Yathu Yathu was co-designed with adolescents and young people (AYP) aged 15–24 in Lusaka, Zambia, and its impact on knowledge of HIV status and secondary outcomes, including met need for modern contraceptives and giving birth in the last 12 months, was evaluated [17]. Yathu Yathu increased knowledge of HIV status among AYP, but there was no evidence of an impact on met need for contraceptives (adjusted prevalence ratio (adjPR) = 1.02, 95%CI 0.79–1.31, p = 0.89), or giving birth in the last 12 months (adjPR = 1.02, 95%CI 0.60–1.72, p = 0.95), among AGYW [17]. In an analysis of routinely collected data, nearly 10% of AGYW in the intervention arm who accepted a loyalty card accessed contraceptive services compared to only 0.5% in the control arm [18].

To better understand the null effect of Yathu Yathu on these two secondary outcomes and who was reached with contraceptive services, this analysis aimed to investigate the characteristics of AGYW who had an unmet need for modern contraceptives and who had reported an unintended pregnancy. The focus was on AGYW in the intervention arm, with a comparative assessment among those in the control arm to evaluate potential differences in associated characteristics. Additionally, we explored which contraceptive services were accessed through Yathu Yathu and by whom. Findings from this analysis have the potential to provide insights to inform the design of future interventions to provide AYP with modern contraceptives [19].

Methods

Study population and location

Yathu Yathu was conducted in two urban communities in Lusaka, Zambia. Each community was subdivided into 10 geographical zones (clusters), each with ~ 2350 AYP residents [15]. As detailed elsewhere [15], random allocation in a 1:1 ratio and stratified by community assigned zones to either the Yathu Yathu intervention or control arm. Before implementing the intervention, all households in the two communities were enumerated. All AYP aged 15–24 were offered a prevention points card (PPC), which aimed to incentivise service use and enabled service use to be monitored [17].

The Yathu Yathu strategy

Yathu Yathu was implemented from September 2019 to September 2021. This implementation period included a pilot phase (September 2019 to January 2020), aimed at informing adaptations to increase coverage of SRH services [20]. In each intervention zone, one centrally located hub (n = 10) was established, which was located away from, but connected to the main health facility. The intervention comprised three key components: (1) delivery of comprehensive SRH services through community-based hubs, (2) implementation of the PPC to incentivise service use, and (3) community engagement to inform AYP and the broader community about the services offered through Yathu Yathu.

The hubs, managed by peer support workers, provided services such as contraceptives (including oral pills and injectables, with referrals to health facilities for implants and intrauterine devices), condom promotion and distribution, as well as other SRH services. AYP could accrue points on their PPC for each service accessed, enabling them to “purchase” health and non-health related rewards, including menstrual pads, soap, and nail polish. AGYW received 250 points for initiating oral or injectable contraceptives and 125 for contraceptive refills.

Data sources

For this analysis, we used two data sources. The first was the endline cross-sectional survey, conducted between April and November 2021, to estimate the impact of Yathu Yathu on the primary and secondary outcomes. The questionnaire included modules on sociodemographics, sexual behaviours, access to and use of contraceptives and condoms, and pregnancy and childbirth. A research assistant administered the questionnaire using an electronic data collection device. However, for questions related to sexual behaviour, AYP had the option to self-complete the questions.

The second was the routinely collected PPC data. These data encompassed information on the services accessed through the hubs or clinic, including date, type, and number of services accessed. For contraceptives, data included the date when AGYW started oral or injectable contraceptives, when they received a refill, collected male or female condoms, and/or obtained emergency contraceptives. For the PPC, we only analysed data from intervention zones.

Outcomes of interest and explanatory variables

We had two outcomes of interest; the first was unmet need for modern contraceptives, defined as the proportion of AGYW who reported not currently using a modern contraceptive method among those who reported having a sexual partner in the previous 12 months and reported either that they did not want (more) children, they wanted to delay having children, or were unsure about when they wanted to have a child (i.e. AGYW with a demand for modern contraceptives). Modern contraceptives included oral contraceptive pills, implants, injectables, diaphragm, female and male condoms, intrauterine device, and emergency contraceptives. Based on the data available, we adapted the DHS definition for unmet need by including single/never married AGYW but excluding pregnant women, and not considering timing of the last pregnancy [21].

The second was the proportion of AGYW who reported an unintended pregnancy among AGYW who ever had sex. Unintended pregnancies were defined as pregnancies (i.e. current or last pregnancy) that were either unwanted (e.g., no/no more children desired) or mistimed (e.g., pregnancies occurring earlier than desired) [22].

We explored factors identified from the literature or hypothesised to influence the outcomes for their association with these outcomes, including age (at time of PPC distribution), educational attainment, current school enrolment, employment status, marital status, wealth index (low/medium/high; using Principal Components Analysis based on household building material, flooring, drinking water, toilet type, energy source and assets), lack of food at household level in the past four weeks due to lack of resources to access food, frequency of communication with sexual partner on HIV or sexually transmitted infections, condom use and contraceptives, and reproductive health gender equitable norms (≥ 15 supporting equitable gender norms/<15 supporting inequitable gender norms, based on reproductive health service use questions of the Gender Equitable Men’s scale) [23].

Using PPC data, we explored use of contraceptive services among all AGYW residing in intervention zones. These services included start and refill of oral and injectable contraceptives, female and male condoms, and emergency contraceptives.

Data analysis

We first described the characteristics of AGYW who completed the endline survey. We estimated the median age for sexual debut and first birth using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. Next, we described our two outcomes.

To explore factors associated with both outcomes, we used logistic regression, with a fixed effect to account for clustering by enumeration zone and adjusting for age as an a priori confounder. The analysis was guided by a conceptual framework, wherein we utilised a hierarchical approach (Fig. 1) [24]. to prevent over-adjusting for variables likely to lie on the causal pathway between another variable and the outcome. As such, variables higher in the framework were considered to influence the variables below them. If a variable was found to be associated with the outcomes, it was only adjusted for variables found to be associated in unadjusted analyses if these variables were higher in the hierarchy (Fig. 1). For example, reproductive health gender norms is not a potential confounder of the association between employment status and the outcomes; as such, employment status was not adjusted for reproductive health gender norms. Variables associated with the outcomes at the p < 0.10-level were included in adjusted analyses. We examined these factors within the Yathu Yathu intervention arm, but repeated the analysis among AGYW residing in the control zones, to contextualise the characteristics of AGYW who continued to have an unmet need for contraceptive services despite the availability of Yathu Yathu.

Subsequently, we described access to contraceptive services, including type, frequency, and timing of services accessed through Yathu Yathu. We described start and refill of oral and injectable contraceptives disaggregated by age (15–17, 18–19, 20–24), community, education and marital status as reported at enumeration. Duplicate services accessed on the same day were excluded.

Results

Description of study population

Overall, 996 AGYW completed the endline survey, with 50.2% (n = 500) of AGYW residing in intervention zones. (Fig. 2) Nearly 80% (79.0%, 395/500) reported ever having had sex, with 78.5% (310/395) reporting sex in the last 12 months. The median age of sexual debut was 18 years [IQR 17–20]. Almost all AGYW who reported sex in the last 12 months (98.4%, 305/310) reported that their last sexual partner was either their husband or a regular/steady partner. Almost two-thirds (62.3%, 245/393) of AGYW who ever had sex had ever given birth, median age at first birth was 20 years [IQR 18–22].

Contraceptive use, unmet need for modern contraceptives, and unintended pregnancies

Among AGYW who reported ever having had sex and who were not currently pregnant, 49.4% (174/352) reported current contraceptive use. The main types of contraceptives used were injectables (53.4%, 93/174), condoms (male/female) (17.8%, 31/174), and oral contraceptives (14.4%, 25/174). Half (52.3%, 91/174) of AGYW reported receiving their contraceptives from a clinic or hospital, 21.8% (38/174) a Yathu Yathu hub, and 8.0% (14/174) a private doctor. The main reasons AGYW reported for not using any contraceptives were being not currently sexually active (46.1%, 82/178) (although 45.1% (37/82) reported having had sex in the last 12 months), being afraid of the side effects (17.4%, 31/178), or planning to get pregnant (9.6%, 17/178). Just over one-third (37.0%, 146/395) were aware of the availability of contraceptives through the hubs.

Among the 249 AGYW with a demand for modern contraceptives, 62.2% (n = 155) were aged 20–24, 81.1% (n = 202) had attained some or completed secondary education, 18.5% (n = 46) were currently in school, 20.9% (n = 52) were employed, and 45.0% (n = 112) were married or cohabiting. Among these AGYW, 40.6% (101/249) had an unmet need for modern contraceptives. (Fig. 2; Table 1) AGYW aged 20–24 were less likely to have an unmet need for modern contraceptives compared to AGYW aged 15–19 (adjOR = 0.57, 95%CI 0.32–0.98, p = 0.04). Unmet need was lower among married/cohabiting AGYW compared to single/never married AGYW (18.8%, 21/112 vs. 58.4%, 80/137; adjOR = 0.17, 95%CI 0.09–0.33, p = 0.001). (Table 1)

In control zones, findings were similar; however, AGYW with a demand for modern contraceptives differed slightly from those in intervention zones, with fewer being enrolled in school (13.6 vs. 18.6%) or employed (15.9% vs. 20.9%), and more experiencing a lack of food (40.7% vs. 34.5%). (Appendix Fig. 1) Similarly, unmet need was lower among AGYW aged 20–24 and those who were married/cohabiting (Appendix Table 1). However, AGYW currently in school reported higher unmet need than those not (77.1%, 27/35 vs. 34.1%, 76/223; respectively, adjOR = 4.15, 95%CI 1.58–11.77, p = 0.004) and AGYW who almost always discussed HIV/STIs, condom use, or contraceptives with their sexual partners were less likely to report an unmet need compared to those with less communication (31.2%, 34/109 vs. 41.7%, 45/108; respectively, adjOR = 0.35, 95%CI 0.14–0.85, p = 0.05). (Appendix Fig. 1) These AGYW differed slightly from those in intervention zones with fewer being enrolled in school (16.8% vs. 21.2%) or employed (18.3% vs. 23.0%), and more experiencing a lack of food (39.7% vs. 35.2%), and often communicating on HIV/STI and pregnancy prevention (40.3% vs. 35.5%). Accessing contraceptive services using prevention points card

According to the PPC data, among the 7005 AGYW who ever used their PPC, 12.3% (859/7005) initiated hormonal contraceptives (Table 3); 80.9% (695/859) accessed injectable and 19.1% (164/859) oral contraceptives. Contraceptive services were accessed almost exclusively at the hubs (99.8%, 857/859). Just over half (53.6%, 460/859) of AGYW initiating contraceptives returned for at least one refill, with 30.8% (265/859) returning for at least two refills. One-third (34.2%, 2399/7005) of AGYW collected male condoms at least once. A minority collected female condoms (5.1%, n = 360) or emergency contraceptives (1.2%, n = 85).

Discussion

We found that an estimated 40% of AGYW with demand for modern contraceptives had an unmet need. Unmet need was more common among younger and single/never married AGYW. Among AGYW who ever had sex, a similar percentage experienced an unintended pregnancy, which was more common among AGYW aged 20–24 and those currently not enrolled in school. Findings were similar in the control arm, where AGYW could access standard-of-care services, including through youth-friendly corners. Based on routinely collected service data, contraceptive uptake among AGYW was low and discontinuation through Yathu Yathu was high. Uptake was higher among AGYW aged 20–24, those residing in community 2, with no/primary education and who were married/cohabiting.

The level of unmet need in this analysis surpasses those documented in the 2018 Zambian DHS (20.0%) [6], and similar studies within Sub-Saharan Africa [25–27]. Comparability of these findings may be affected by socio-demographic variations between study populations, particularly pertaining to marital status and age, as the Zambian DHS study only includes married women aged 15–49 [6]. Furthermore, comparability may be impacted by the operationalisation of the definition of unmet need for modern contraceptives. Our definition, unlike the DHS criteria, excluded pregnant women, did not consider timing of last pregnancy, and extended the period of sexual activity for single/never married AGYW (i.e. 12 months) compared to the DHS standard (i.e. sexual activity within the last 30 days) [28]. Furthermore, our definition included only modern contraceptive methods whereas the DHS encompasses modern and traditional methods [28] and diverging from the broader definition for modern contraceptives provided by the World Health Organization, which also includes other modern methods such as female sterilisation [2]. Our rationale for diverging from the DHS definition was the need to enhance our understanding of unmet need among unmarried AGYW aged 15–24.

Several factors might contribute to the high unmet need for modern contraceptives among AGYW in our analysis and thus, to the limited impact observed in the main trial evaluation [17]. As shown by the PPC data, only 12% of AGYW accessing Yathu Yathu opted to initiate contraceptives via the hub. Limited access may stem from inadequate awareness of contraceptive services within Yathu Yathu hubs, as only one-third of AGYW in the survey who ever had sex reported being aware of the availability of these services through Yathu Yathu. Moreover, a substantial proportion of AGYW discontinued contraceptive use after initiation, with only 50% seeking refills. These implementation challenges highlight the importance of strengthening service visibility, demand-generation, follow-up and user support. There may have been an adverse impact on access due to COVID-19-related restrictions implemented during the study period, which led to closure of the hubs (April 2020 – July 2020) and stock-outs of oral contraceptives [17]. The inconsistent provision of services during this period may have discouraged AGYW from accessing contraceptives services. This low uptake and high contraceptive discontinuation necessitates further investigation to understand the underlying reasons, particularly those that persist after the COVID-19 pandemic, such as poverty and social norms [29]. Furthermore, these disruptions underscore the need for resilience planning and the value of diversified access points, such as digital or mobile outreach, especially during emergencies.

AGYW aged 20–24 were less likely to experience an unmet need compared to those aged 15–19 [26, 27, 30, 31] as were married/cohabiting AGYW relative to single/never married AGYW. These findings are in line with findings from the 2015–2016 Malawian DHS data among (un)married women aged 15–49 [32] and the 2018 Guinean DHS data among AGYW aged 15–24 [31], but contradicting findings from a Tanzanian DHS study, where married AGYW had a higher unmet need compared to unmarried AGYW [27]. In Zambia, sociocultural norms surrounding sexuality, which discourage adolescents (particularly adolescent girls) from engaging in sex and therefore seeking contraceptive services may have influenced uptake [33–35]. The implementation period of the Yathu Yathu intervention, including a community dialogue approach, might have been too short to alter these sociocultural norms surrounding pregnancy and fertility [17]. Additionally, the fear of potential side effects of contraceptives, as reported in our analysis and documented in the literature, might also, in part, explain the high unmet need [36]. These findings underscore the importance of tailoring SRH services to address the needs of different age and marital status groups, considering sociocultural norms and concerns regarding contraceptives. Additionally, addressing and altering norms and misconceptions surrounding contraceptives (e.g. that they cause infertility) must involve engagement at all societal levels.

The substantial unmet need for contraceptives might be linked to the high proportion of unintended pregnancies [37, 38]. Comparison with existing literature shows that the percentage of unintended pregnancies among AGYW in our analysis is higher compared to the pooled prevalence observed in 36 Sub-Saharan African countries, which was 30% among currently pregnant AGYW [37]. However, it is in line with the 45% documented among pregnant women aged 15–49 in Zambia [39], though acknowledging denominator differences (all AGYW ever having had sex, as in our analysis, vs. currently pregnant AGYW). In our analysis, unintended pregnancies were associated with not being enrolled in school. As evidenced by the literature, AGYW with a higher level of education have lower odds of unintended pregnancy [37, 39]; giving support to the protective effect of current school enrolment and educational attainment in preventing pregnancies among AGYW [40]. Despite being less likely to have an unmet need for contraceptives, AGYW aged 20–24 were more likely to report an unintended pregnancy. Among the small number of women who reported a birth in the last 12 months, half were unintended, indicating missed opportunities by Yathu Yathu to reach these AGYW. Unintended pregnancies among AGYW in Zambia remain a public health challenge, despite contraceptive availability and increased attention to addressing social norms [17, 34, 39]. Providing sexual health information outside of school settings could be important in reducing missed opportunities. One way could be through social media, while being cautious about the misinformation circulating there [41], given that media exposure has been found to facilitate AGYWs’ access to SRH information, including pregnancy planning and access to family planning services [39].

As reported elsewhere, Yathu Yathu had no impact on met need for contraceptives or births in the last 12 months [17]. To contextualise the above findings, we repeated our risk factor analysis among AGYW in the control arm. The factors identified as associated with both outcomes were similar across trial arms; however, in the control arm, there was evidence that AGYW currently in school and those who communicated less frequently about sexual health with their sexual partner had a higher unmet need for contraceptives. Similarly, those reporting a lack of food were more likely to have experienced an unintended pregnancy. This suggests that Yathu Yathu may have reached AGYW in school, those with less sexual health promotion communication and those facing a lack of food; however, the small sample size could have led to a lack of power to detect associations. The differences regarding current school attendance and lack of food may be attributed to socioeconomic disparities between arms. Despite random allocation of zones to the two trial arms, more AGYW in intervention zones were currently enrolled in school and fewer experienced a lack of food compared to control zones [42].

We found that contraceptive uptake through Yathu Yathu was higher among AGYW aged 20–24 and those who were married/cohabiting. The difference noted in contraceptive uptake between communities could be attributed to variations in wealth, with community 2 generally having higher socioeconomic position than community 1, confirming findings from other studies [43–45]. In contrast, contraceptive uptake was higher among AGYW with no or primary education compared to secondary education, a population often underserved by SRH services [46, 47]. Notably, differences in refill proportions were less pronounced across socio-demographic groups, indicating the importance of having the ability to initially access contraceptives. As Yathu Yathu failed to sufficiently reach adolescents who also had higher unmet needs, future iterations of similar interventions should consider linkages to schools to promote services or having Yathu Yathu hub staff make periodic visits to local schools.

Our findings can also be interpreted through the socio-ecological model, which highlights how health behaviours of individuals are shaped by a complex interplay of factors within their environments, operating at individual (e.g., age, education), interpersonal (e.g., partner communication), community (e.g., social and gender norms), organisational (e.g., service delivery and organisation), and structural levels (e.g., access to services) [48]. While Yathu Yathu aimed to improve individual-level access, persistent unmet need and high levels of unintended pregnancy suggest that factors beyond the individual continue to limit uptake, including limited awareness of available services, low partner communication, social norms discouraging contraceptive use, and socioeconomic challenges. This underscores the need for further in-depth qualitative research and SRH interventions that address barriers across all levels.

A limitation of this study is the potential influence of social desirability on reporting contraceptive use and sexual behaviour, stemming from social norms that discourage contraceptive use among young unmarried AGYW [49]. This could lead to under- or overestimating unmet need for modern contraceptives. Additionally, it should be noted that services accessed without using a PPC are not captured in the data, which could underestimate contraceptive access by AGYW. As reported in the survey, AGYW also accessed clinics or private doctors to obtain contraceptives. Furthermore, the relatively small sample size, resulting from the exclusion of certain groups (e.g. pregnant or non-sexually active AGYW), may have influenced the power to detect associations between explanatory variables and the outcome. Although this study investigated the null effect of Yathu Yathu on contraceptive use and pregnancy-related secondary outcomes, the analysis presented is valuable in identifying barriers to uptake, informing future SRH intervention design, and contributing to reduce publication bias.

Conclusions

Despite the implementation of a community-based, peer-led SRH service intervention, designed with and for adolescents and young people, this study reveals a high unmet need for modern contraceptives and a high prevalence of unintended pregnancies among AGYW. Challenges related to low uptake of contraceptives services, low awareness of contraceptive service availability, disparities in access among AGYW, and fears towards contraceptives may have contributed to these outcomes. These findings emphasize the importance of improving access and addressing barriers to achieve equitable access to contraceptives, thus meeting the reproductive health needs of all AGYW, including young and single/never married AGYW. Enhancing demand-generation strategies, strengthening community engagement, and integration with schools and digital platforms may be critical for adapting and improving future adolescent-responsive SRH interventions in similar settings.
