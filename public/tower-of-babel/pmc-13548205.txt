Clinicoradiological range dissociation as a clue to spinal dural arteriovenous fistula
Authors: Yui Sanpei, Keita Yasuda, Akira Hanazono
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Spinal dural arteriovenous fistula (sdAVF) is a treatable cause of progressive myelopathy, yet it is frequently overlooked during the initial diagnostic evaluation.1 Even when MRI shows a longitudinally extensive central T2-hyperintense lesion, flow-void findings may be subtle, and inflammatory-appearing cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) may bias the workup towards myelitis.2 3 Selected patients may require repeat angiography before the diagnosis is secured.4 Clinicians therefore need an additional clue that links imaging with bedside examination when the initial workup is unrevealing.

Review of our institutional cases suggested a possible mismatch between MRI lesion extent and sensory deficit range. Prior studies examined associations between MRI or angiographic extent and neurological disability across patients, including preoperative and postoperative disability scores,5 6 but did not address within-patient mismatch between lesion extent and bedside sensory mapping.

We therefore examined eight confirmed sdAVF cases from a consecutive spinal AVF cohort, expressing pretreatment MRI lesion range and sensory deficit range on a common myelomere scale. Our aim was to assess whether range dissociation could prompt diagnostic reassessment, not to establish diagnostic accuracy.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective study at Akita University Hospital between January 2013 and December 2024. Of 10 consecutive patients with spinal AVFs, eight with angiographically or surgically confirmed sdAVF were analysed; two with non-dural fistulas were excluded. MRI lesion range was defined as the craniocaudal extent of central T2 hyperintensity on pretreatment sagittal and axial T2-weighted images and was measured by a single neurologist blinded to clinical data. Sensory deficit range was extracted from neurological examination notes at presentation using the most rostral and caudal dermatomes with definite pinprick impairment. When sensory loss extended to the perianal region, it was considered to reach the S5 myelomere; in case 8, medullary extension was counted as one segment above C1.

For direct comparison, MRI lesion range and sensory deficit range were converted to myelomeres using a published vertebral-myelomere alignment model.7 Length was expressed as the number of involved myelomeres, counted inclusively between the rostral and caudal myelomeres, and the case-level difference was defined as MRI lesion length minus sensory deficit length. Strength in selected bilateral lower limb muscles was summarised as the total Medical Research Council (MRC) score for the iliopsoas, quadriceps and tibialis anterior muscles bilaterally (range 0–30).

Results

MRI lesion length exceeded sensory deficit length in six of eight patients with sdAVF (figure 1). Median MRI lesion length was 12.0 myelomeres (IQR 9.75–15.5), whereas median sensory deficit length was 6.0 myelomeres (IQR 3.5–8.5). The median paired difference, calculated as MRI lesion length minus sensory deficit length, was 7.0 myelomeres (range −4 to 13).

Despite these extensive lesions, weakness in selected bilateral lower limb muscles was absent or mild in most patients; five of eight had total MRC scores of 26/30 or higher across these muscles. This combination of long radiological involvement, shorter bedside sensory mapping and relatively preserved strength is shown in figure 1, together with the case-level total MRC scores. The median time from symptom onset to admission was 8.0 months (IQR 4.75–11.0). As cohort descriptors, the median age was 63.5 years (range 55–77), and seven patients were male.

Two cases did not follow the dominant range-dissociation pattern. Case 7 showed clinical involvement beyond the MRI T2-hyperintense range: a thoracolumbar lesion measuring 9 myelomeres was accompanied by sensory deficits spanning 13 myelomeres and complete paraplegia. Case 8 showed remote-level dissociation: a short high cervical lesion measuring 2 myelomeres was accompanied by lumbosacral sensory deficits spanning 6 myelomeres.

Discussion

In this series, the dominant finding was range dissociation: extensive MRI involvement but a shorter sensory deficit range on bedside examination. This pattern was often accompanied by relatively preserved strength in selected lower limb muscles, so the clinical picture appeared less severe than the MRI lesion would suggest. This mismatch may prompt reconsideration of sdAVF when the initial evaluation remains inconclusive.

Range dissociation may occur because venous congestion extends beyond spinal cord segments that are clinically symptomatic. In sdAVF, the T2 abnormality reflects congestive myelopathy, and reports of asymptomatic or incidentally detected fistulas suggest that radiological cord involvement may precede overt neurological deficits.8–10

Clinicoradiological discrepancy has been reported previously. A four-patient low-flow spinal AVF case series noted poor clinicoradiological correlation; one particularly illustrative case showed a sensory-level remote from a high cervical fistula and craniocervical T2 abnormality, and the authors described this mismatch as a diagnostic clue.11 Our study extends this observation by comparing MRI and sensory ranges within patients on a common myelomere scale.

The two exceptions show the limits of this pattern. Case 7 suggests that range dissociation may be lost when venous congestion progresses to destructive injury, with sensory involvement beyond the MRI lesion and complete paraplegia. Case 8 showed a separate pattern of level dissociation: a short high cervical lesion with a remote lumbosacral sensory deficit. Remote caudal myelopathy from cervical sdAVF has been reported.12 We therefore interpret level dissociation as a separate manifestation rather than the cohort’s main pattern. These observations support venous congestion as an explanation for both patterns while retaining range dissociation as the main cohort-level finding.

Clinically, this pattern is a reason to reconsider sdAVF, not a disease-specific sign. Misdiagnosis and diagnostic delay are common in low-flow spinal AVFs.1 When a long central T2 lesion is accompanied by a narrower sensory map and relatively preserved strength, sdAVF should remain in the differential even when flow voids are subtle. In appropriate cases, the mismatch may inform consideration of repeat angiography after an unrevealing initial evaluation.4 This is particularly relevant when inflammatory-appearing CSF encourages premature attribution to myelitis.3 These observations do not exclude other longitudinally extensive myelopathies; they argue against premature inflammatory attribution.

Limitations include the small, retrospective, single-centre design; chart-based pinprick mapping; anatomical uncertainty in myelomere conversion; and single-rater MRI measurements. The absence of an inflammatory comparator group prevents assessment of disease specificity. These data therefore support a hypothesis-generating clue but cannot establish diagnostic accuracy or generalisability. Future studies should compare sdAVF with inflammatory longitudinally extensive myelopathies, including neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder and myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disease, to determine how often this mismatch pattern prompts reconsideration of sdAVF.
