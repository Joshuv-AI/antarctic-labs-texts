Primary Petit's Triangle Hernia With Bowel Strangulation: Management in a Resource‐Limited Setting
Authors: Biruk Umer Mohammed, Henok Bahru Wodajeneh, Ashenafi Tesfaye Bedada, Frewoyni Gedey Gebregziabher, Dereje Nigatu Elala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLumbar hernias are rare surgical entities that may present asymptomatically or with complications such as obstruction and strangulation. We report a case of a 45‐year‐old woman who presented to the emergency department with a painful, irreducible swelling in the right lumbar region accompanied by vomiting, abdominal pain, and obstipation. Although she had been aware of a painless lump in the same area for months, the acute presentation suggested strangulation. Due to limited access to advanced imaging, an emergency exploration was performed via a direct lumbar incision, revealing a non‐viable segment of small bowel. The affected bowel was resected with primary anastomosis, and the small fascial defect was closed primarily. The patient recovered without complications and showed no recurrence at the 1‐year follow‐up. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges of strangulated lumbar hernias and demonstrates that timely open surgical repair based on clinical assessment alone can be life‐saving, particularly in resource‐constrained settings.Keywords: intestinal obstruction, lumbar mass, open repair, primary petit triangle hernia, strangulation

Introduction

Lumbar hernia is a rare condition. Since its first description by Barbette in 1672, approximately 300 cases have been reported in the literature. One‐fifth of lumbar hernias are congenital; the remaining 80% are acquired and can be divided into primary (spontaneous) or secondary (iatrogenic or traumatic) types [1].

A lumbar hernia may present bilaterally or with other hernia types, mostly alongside inguinal hernias. Due to its rare occurrence, complications such as bowel obstruction from incarceration or strangulation are infrequent. Therefore, there are no specific management guidelines [2]. Here, we present a unilateral strangulated lumbar hernia with no other hernia types. To the best of our knowledge, about 30 cases of strangulated or incarcerated spontaneous lumbar hernias have been reported in the surgical literature [3]. This case aims to highlight the diagnostic challenge and demonstrate that timely open repair without advanced imaging can be life‐saving in resource‐limited settings.

Case History/Examination

A 45‐year‐old woman presented to the emergency department with a painful swelling in the right lumbar region. She presented with vomiting and abdominal pain for 3 days, with associated changes in bowel habits, and inability to pass gas or stool for 2 days. The patient claimed there had been a painless lump on the lower back area to the right side for months, but she did not seek medical attention because it was painless until the current presentation. She does not have any chronic medical illnesses, has not undergone any surgical procedures, and has no history of drug use.

Physical examination revealed that the patient had class one obesity (BMI was 30.1 kg/m2), acutely ill, with a blood pressure of 90/60 mmHg, tachycardic (pulse rate 110 beats per minute), tachypneic (respiratory rate 26 breaths per minute), a temperature of 37.3°C, and an oxygen saturation of 96% on atmospheric air. Abdominal examination revealed a 20 cm irreducible mass in the right lumbar region with localized tenderness (Figure 1).

(A) Preoperative photograph (supine position) demonstrating a bulge in the right flank. (B) Preoperative photograph (right lateral view) showing the 20‐cm bulge in the right flank.

Investigations and Treatment

The differential diagnosis included lipoma, abscess, or other superficial flank masses. Upon investigation, the laboratory tests were normal, except for a mildly elevated WBC count of 11,000 cells/mm3 with a left shift. Plain abdominal radiograph showed a lumbar soft‐tissue bulge and air‐fluid levels. There was marked dilatation of the large bowel loops, the distal rectal gas shadow was absent, there was no evidence of pneumatosis intestinalis, and no pneumoperitoneum (Figure 2). Advanced imaging (CT/MRI) was not available due to resource constraints.

Plain abdominal radiograph showing a soft tissue bulge in the right lumbar region (yellow arrow), air‐fluid levels, and dilated large bowel loops without pneumoperitoneum (the red arrows).

After resuscitation, written informed consent was obtained, and a surgical intervention was decided. Under general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the left lateral decubitus position. Surgical exploration of the mass through a lumbar incision revealed pre‐peritoneal fat and a 5‐cm segment of ischemic small bowel, surrounded by a hernia sac confined within the right Petit triangle with no other organs in the sac. A 5‐cm segment of non‐viable small bowel, located approximately 50 cm from the ileocecal valve, was resected. A hand‐sewn, double‐layered, end‐to‐end primary anastomosis was performed. The fascial defect was then closed primarily with interrupted size‐1 polypropylene sutures, and synthetic mesh was not used (Figures 3 and 4).

Intraoperative photograph showing the ischemic small bowel segment (the blue arrow) strangulated within the hernia defect.

Intraoperative photograph showing the fascial defect after reduction of viable contents, prior to repair.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

Her postoperative course was uneventful. The patient was discharged on the fifth postoperative day. No recurrence was observed at the 1‐year follow‐up.

Discussion

Lumbar hernias are classified by location and etiology, with the two main anatomical types being Grynfeltt (superior triangle) and Petit (inferior triangle) hernias [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Acquired hernias may be primary (spontaneous) or secondary (iatrogenic/traumatic), the latter accounting for 25% of cases [10, 11]. Hernia contents commonly include fat, colon, spleen, or liver [5]. Incarceration rates range from 25% to 30% [9, 10], with an approximately 8% risk of strangulation [12]. In our patient, the sac contained pre‐peritoneal fat and ileum.

Lumbar hernias are a rare clinical condition [4, 13, 14]. Strangulated primary lumbar hernias are even rarer, occurring in less than 10% of lumbar hernia cases [3]. It is usually reported in elderly patients and occurs concurrently with other abdominal wall hernias [3]. However, this patient did not have a concurrent hernia.

The clinical presentation of lumbar hernias is variable, ranging from asymptomatic to severe and life‐threatening. The most common finding is a palpable, often painless, bulge in the lumbar flank. When symptoms occur, they may include localized pain in the back or abdominal tenderness. In rare cases where the hernia becomes incarcerated, symptoms can include severe pain, bowel obstruction, or signs of nerve compression (radiculopathy). Strangulated lumbar hernia with bowel obstruction is a life‐threatening condition; fortunately, it is a rare form of presentation of lumbar hernia [4], similar to this patient's presentation.

During physical examination, the findings are frequently confused with lipoma or other superficial flank masses [1, 2]. A hallmark physical sign is a reducible mass that increases in size with coughing or straining. This classic sign can be absent in cases of small defect size, obesity, or other complicating factors such as incarceration or strangulation [15]. In this patient, the mass was identified by the patient, but she did not seek medical attention. In some cases, the diagnosis is made during diagnostic laparotomy for bowel obstruction, as well as for abdominal wall hernias [3]. X‐rays are used for diagnosis only in cases of obstruction, revealing air‐fluid levels [3]. Other abdominal imaging techniques, such as CT scanning, magnetic resonance imaging, or ultrasonography, are used for the diagnosis of lumbar hernia [3]. Nonetheless, a thorough physical examination remains crucial for its diagnosis, especially when urgent decisions are required [16], as was the case in this patient. A lumbar hernia should be repaired surgically, as it is prone to both obstruction and strangulation. The objectives are to close the defect and restore a durable abdominal wall capable of withstanding physical stress [17, 18]. Management strategies are primarily guided by hernia size and the availability of resources. Various surgical techniques are available, including both laparoscopic and open repair methods. These may involve either tissue repair or mesh repair and can be performed under local or general anesthesia. The open repair remains the most commonly used technique for lumbar hernias [19], which can be performed using a transabdominal or extraperitoneal approach. For small hernias, primary suture closure of the hernia orifice with interrupted tension‐free sutures and reinforcement of the oblique and dorsal muscle groups (Petit surgery) has been effective [5, 20]. For large hernias, repair using nonabsorbable prosthetic material (mesh repair) is recommended [4, 7, 10].

The laparoscopic approach has recently emerged as an expanding management option, offering the advantages of minimally invasive surgery [13, 20, 21, 22]. Studies have demonstrated that laparoscopic repair of lumbar hernia is associated with reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stay, and faster recovery compared with open techniques [23]. The outcomes of the open and laparoscopic approaches demonstrate comparable morbidity and recurrence rates, though open surgery is the best option for diffuse‐type lumbar hernias larger than 15 cm [13, 22].

In cases of strangulated hernias, the use of synthetic mesh for repair has been associated with complications such as infection and bowel obstruction, making its application a subject of debate [4]. Some authors advocate synthetic mesh repair and careful intestinal resection to minimize the risk of infection [2]. A recent meta‐analysis and systematic review concluded that mesh repair for incarcerated and strangulated groin hernias effectively reduces recurrence rates without an associated increase in postoperative complications and should be considered in cases classified as clean. However, in instances where bowel resection is performed, the use of mesh repair may elevate the risk of surgical site infection [24, 25]. In this patient, the surgical team opted for a lumbar approach because there were no indications of perforation or peritonitis. Additionally, the surgical team was unable to perform the laparoscopic procedure because of limited availability and experience. The lumbar approach is preferred for early diagnosis without complications [1].

Conclusion

This case underscores the diagnostic challenge of a strangulated lumbar hernia, particularly in settings where imaging modalities are limited. Careful clinical assessment and early operative decision‐making were critical in achieving a favorable outcome. Open surgical exploration allowed timely resection of nonviable bowel and definitive repair of the defect. In similar low‐resource environments, prompt intervention based on clinical judgment can be lifesaving, even in the absence of advanced diagnostic and minimally invasive options.
