In vitro antibacterial activity of photoactivated flavonoid glycosides against Acinetobacter baumannii
Authors: Maryam Pourhajibagher, Zahra Javanmard, Abbas Bahador
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcinetobacter baumannii’s extensive antibiotic resistance makes its infections difficult to treat, so effective strategies to fight this bacterium are urgently needed. This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of antimicrobial photodynamic therapy (aPDT) mediated by Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin against A. baumannii. Absorbance spectra, fluorescence spectra, and minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin were determined. The intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), cell membrane permeability, expression of ompA and blaOXA−23, anti-biofilm activity, and anti-metabolic activity of Rutin-Gal(III) complex- and Quercetin-mediated aPDT were measured. Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin revealed absorption peaks in the visible spectra. Quercetin and Rutin-Gal(III) complex displayed fluorescence peaks at 524 nm and 540 nm, respectively. MIC values for the Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin were 64 µg/mL and 256 µg/mL, respectively. Quercetin- and Rutin-Gal(III) complex-mediated aPDT significantly reduced the colony forming units/mL (58.4% and 67.5%), EPS synthesis (47.4% and 56.5%), metabolic activity (30.5% and 36.3%), ompA (5.5- and 10.5-fold), and blaOXA−23 (4.1-fold and 7.8-fold) genes expression (respectively; all P < 0.05). Quercetin- and Rutin-Gal(III) complex-mediated aPDT enhanced notable biofilm degradation (36.2% and 40.6%), ROS production (2.55- and 2.90-folds), and membrane permeability (10.8– and 9.6-folds) (respectively; all P < 0.05). The findings indicate that Rutin-Gal(III) complex- and Quercetin-mediated aPDT exhibits antibacterial properties and could serve as a valuable adjunctive strategy to conventional antibiotic treatments for A. baumannii infections. One limitation of this study is that it was conducted solely on the standard strain; testing on clinical isolates would allow for more reliable interpretation of the results.Keywords: Acinetobacter baumannii, Quercetin, Rutin-Gal(III) complex, Antimicrobial photodynamic therapy

Introduction

Acinetobacter baumannii is a hospital-acquired (nosocomial) pathogen that is commonly linked to disease outbreaks in healthcare settings. Multidrug-resistant A. baumannii is an emerging and problematic pathogen that is contributing to difficult-to-treat skin and soft tissue infections, particularly in burn patients, with significant secondary complications such as delayed wound healing, skin graft failure, and sepsis (Farshadzadeh et al. 2018). Due to the rising prevalence of antibiotic resistance in A. baumannii, treatment options using antibiotics to manage the infections are becoming increasingly limited; therefore, there has been a renewed interest in the research and development of new approaches targeting this pathogen (Cavallo et al. 2023; Kyriakidis et al. 2021). It has become a successful and problematic bacterium due to its capacity to thrive in challenging environmental conditions, form biofilms, and display significant antibiotic resistance (Weinberg et al. 2020). Oxacillinases and Outer membrane protein A (OmpA) play significant functions in the pathogenesis of A. baumannii. Understanding the roles of OmpA and oxacillinases is essential for developing effective strategies to combat the pathogenesis of A. baumannii (Nie et al. 2020).

Oxacillinases are a type of beta-lactamase enzyme produced by A. baumannii that confers resistance to oxacillin and other beta-lactam antibiotics, including carbapenems (Evans and Amyes 2014). Specific OXA-type enzymes, including blaOXA−23, blaOXA−24, blaOXA−51, and blaOXA−58, have been identified as significant factors contributing to carbapenem resistance in A. baumannii (Nigro and Hall 2016; Thirapanmethee et al. 2020; Kafshnouchi et al. 2022). Strains of A. baumannii that produce oxacillinase enzymes are frequently linked to nosocomial infections (Medeiros and Lincopan 2013; Neves et al. 2016). The production of oxacillinases is often due to the acquisition of mobile genetic elements, such as transposons and plasmids, which can facilitate the spread of antibiotic-resistance genes between different bacterial species (Yaikhan et al. 2024) and increase the risk of multidrug-resistant infections (Ibrahim et al. 2021; Yaikhan et al. 2024).

OmpA (Outer Membrane Protein A) of A. baumannii is a significant virulence factor associated with the bacterium’s pathogenicity and antibiotic resistance. (Barati et al. 2024). Additionally, OmpA can bind to various host cell receptors, including fibronectin, laminin, and collagen, allowing A. baumannii to attach to and colonize host tissues (Nie et al. 2020; Tram et al. 2021; Yao et al. 2023). OmpA is also involved in biofilm formation, which is a critical factor in the establishment of persistent infections (Barati et al. 2024). OmpA can interact with several host proteins, including complement components and toll-like receptors, which play a crucial role in the recognition and elimination of bacteria by the host immune system (Marion et al. 2019; Nie et al. 2020). OmpA can also inhibit bacterial phagocytosis and establish chronic infections (Chen 2020; Sarshar et al. 2021).

Efforts to control the dissemination of oxacillinase-producing strains, reduce their public health implications, and focus on OmpA represent a potential strategy for creating novel treatments against A. baumannii infections. Antimicrobial Photodynamic Therapy (aPDT) has emerged as a non-invasive treatment option that offers an alternative to conventional antibiotics in combating antibiotic-resistant bacteria (Pourhajibagher and Bahador 2015). aPDT uses a combination of visible light, a photosensitizer, and oxygen, which leads to the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). The generated ROS dramatically increase oxidative stress and cause damage to bacterial cells including lipids, proteins, and DNA, resulting in their death or inactivation (Feng et al. 2021; Gilaberte et al. 2021; Songca and Adjei 2022).

aPDT presents several advantages over traditional antibiotics, making it a promising alternative in the fight against infections, especially those caused by antibiotic-resistant microorganisms. aPDT has high versatility and a broad spectrum of action. It is effective against a wide range of pathogens, including bacteria, fungi, viruses, and protozoa, which allows it to address multiple types of infections simultaneously (Gilaberte et al. 2021; Almenara-Blasco et al. 2024). The antimicrobial effects of aPDT are immediate, unlike antibiotics, which often require hours or days to exhibit their full effects. This rapid action can be crucial in treating acute infections (Gilaberte et al. 2021).

A significant advantage of aPDT is its low likelihood of inducing resistance. Unlike antibiotics, which can lead to the rapid development of resistant strains, repeated use of photosensitizers in aPDT does not typically result in resistance. This is due to the multifaceted mechanism of action that targets various microbial cell components (Liu et al. 2015; Jiang et al. 2024). aPDT is highly site-specific, meaning that it primarily affects the targeted infected area while sparing surrounding healthy tissues. This reduces systemic toxicity and enhances safety during treatment (Liu et al. 2015; Jiang et al. 2024). Studies indicate that aPDT can promote faster healing and better cosmetic outcomes in skin infections compared to traditional antibiotic treatments. This is particularly beneficial for chronic wounds and skin lesions (Liu et al. 2015; Almenara-Blasco et al. 2024).

Flavonoid glycosides are natural photosensitizers that make them a potential candidate for the development of novel aPDT agents (Motallebi et al. 2020). Flavonoid glycosides derived from plants are a group of compounds that have been the subject of thorough research because of their strong biological effects. Some examples of these natural flavonoid glycosides include Quercetin and Rutin (Motallebi et al. 2020). Quercetin has been extensively studied as a photosensitizer in aPDT and has shown potential medicinal properties (Lee et al. 2022). Quercetin (3,4,5,7-tetrahydroxyflavonol) is a flavonoid with a benzopyran core containing multiple hydroxyl groups, which contribute to its biological activity. This compound is known for its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties and is often studied for its potential health benefits in various applications, including antimicrobial and anticancer activities. Quercetin exhibits antibacterial against a wide range of gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria such as Streptococcus mutans, S. sanguis, S. sobrinus, Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Listeria monocytogenes, Aeromonas hydrophila, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, etc., (Aghababaei and Hadidi 2023; Dhanaraj 2023).

Rutin (Quercetin-3-O-rutinoside) is a flavonoid compound that is naturally found in various plant sources, including wheat, apples, and tea, and possesses health benefits for humans. Rutin decreases capillary permeability as well as inhibits platelet aggregation, making the blood thinner and improving circulation. (Motallebi et al. 2020). It possesses various properties, including antibacterial activity (Negahdari et al. 2021). Rutin has been shown to have strong antibacterial activities against S. aureus, A. baumannii, P. aeruginosa (Orhan et al. 2010). These natural photosensitizers were chosen for their potential in aPDT and their availability. Although purified Rutin has the potential to be a natural photosensitizer, but it requires ultraviolet (UV)-A light to work (Vaziri et al. 2023). To make it better suited for aPDT, its ability to absorb visible light needs to be improved. Rutin’s ability to enter cells is important for its effectiveness, and its saccharide groups help it penetrate bacterial cells and fight biofilms. Studies have shown that Rutin can combine with Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved Gallium(III) ions, which shifts its absorption spectrum from UV to visible light, enhancing its safety for medical applications (Vaziri et al. 2023; Pourhajibagher et al. 2024). Gallium(III) has been used to create compounds with other substances, leading to improved photodynamic and chemical properties. These combinations are appealing as photosensitizers because they have high triplet quantum yields and longer triplet lifetimes. Additionally, Gallium(III) prevents the molecules from clumping together and enhances their light-related characteristics (Constantin et al. 2019; Pourhajibagher et al. 2024). It has been revealed that Gallium(III) showed an inhibitory activity on bacterial growth of E. coli, S. aureus, and P. aeruginosa (Best et al. 2020). Vaziri et al. 2023) study revealed that the Rutin-Gallium(III) complex-mediated aPDT showed no toxicity against primary human gingival fibroblast cells. This strategy in a dose dependent manner decreased S. mutans biofilm cell survival and virulence (91.4% and 11.7-fold, respectively).

In this study, the in vitro antimicribial effect of aPDT was assessed by evaluating cell viability and gene expression of ompA and blaOXA−23 on A. baumannii. This study represents the initial investigation to evaluate the potential of natural flavonoid glycosides-mediated aPDT as inhibitors for ompA and blaOXA−23 genes using an in vitro approach. The findings of this study hold promise in providing valuable insights that can contribute to the development of effective therapeutic approaches using aPDT for combatting A. baumannii infections.

Materials and methods

Preparation of photosensitizers

The Rutin-Gallium(III) (Rutin-Ga[III]) complex was synthesized according to our previously published methodology (Pourhajibagher et al. 2024). Briefly, the Rutin-Ga(III) complex was prepared by dissolving Rutin powder (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany in heated ethanol (70 °C). The blend was then cooled and adjusted to the desired concentration using ethanol. The Rutin-Ga(III) complex was formed by combining Gallium(III) nitrate hydrate (GaH2N3O10; Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) with Rutin in methanol as the solvent. The complex was further purified by recrystallization from methanol, resulting in a completely pure final product. Similar to our previous study (Pourhajibagher et al. 2024), the Rutin-Gal(III) complex exhibited its highest absorption peak at 438 nm in the UV-Vis absorption spectrum. Quercetin used in the present study was also purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, Germany. This flavonoid glycoside was dissolved in 0.5% DMSO at a final concentration of 10 mg/mL (Kannan et al. 2022). The absorbance and fluorescence spectra of Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin were then evaluated according to the previous studies (Buchweitz et al. 2016; Vaziri et al. 2023).

Bacterial strain and culture conditions

In this study, the A. baumannii ATCC 19,606 strain was utilized. The strain was cultured and maintained in brain heart infusion (BHI) broth (Merck, Germany), and incubated at 37 °C overnight. the strain was sub-cultured in BHI broth until it achieved an optimal optical density (OD) of 0.08–0.13 at a wavelength of 600 nm, corresponding to 0.5 McFarland standard (1.5 × 108 CFU/mL). Bacterial cells were harvested to determine the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of Quercetin and the Rutin-Gal(III) complex, as well as to investigate the antimicrobial effects of Rutin-Gal(III) complex- and Quercetin-mediated aPDT in this study. This included measurements of intracellular ROS generation, inhibition of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), anti-biofilm activity, anti-metabolic activity, cytoplasmic membrane permeability assays, and gene expression of ompA and blaOXA−23 in A. baumannii.

Determination of the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

According to the protocols of the Clinical & Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) (2019), the antibacterial effects of Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin were evaluated using the MIC determination method. The MIC was determined using the broth microdilution technique in Cation-Adjusted Mueller-Hinton Broth (CAMHB). Briefly, Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin were prepared in dilutions that ranged from 1 to 512 µg/mL, using a two-fold serial dilution method. In each well of a 96-well microtiter plate, 100 µL of bacterial suspension was added after adjusting it to the 0.5 McFarland standard, resulting in a final bacterial concentration of 5 × 105 CFU/mL. The microdilution plate consisted of controls to monitor bacterial growth (CAMHB plus bacteria) and the sterility of the medium (CAMHB). Following this, the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. To determine the MIC, the density of bacterial growth in the wells containing the photosensitizers was compared to the control wells in each set of tests. The lower concentration of Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin where there is no turbidity was documented as the MIC. The optical density was also measured at 600 nm using a spectrometer to validate the detected turbidity and determine the MIC values.

Antibacterial activities

To evaluate the effect of aPDT on the cell viability of A. baumannii in the planktonic phase, 100 µL of A. baumannii suspension (1.5 × 106 CFU/mL) was added to the wells of a 96-well microtiter plate. Subsequently, the bacterial cells were subjected to treatment with the following groups:

A. Quercetin: Treatment was administered to the bacterial cells with 100 µL of Quercetin, diluted to a concentration of 1/2×MIC. The microtiter plate was then kept in darkness for a duration of five min.

A. Quercetin: Treatment was administered to the bacterial cells with 100 µL of Quercetin, diluted to a concentration of 1/2×MIC. The microtiter plate was then kept in darkness for a duration of five min.

B. Rutin-Gal(III) complex: Treatment was administered to the bacterial cells with 100 µL of Rutin-Gal(III), diluted to a concentration of 1/2×MIC. The microtiter plate was then kept in darkness for a duration of five min.

B. Rutin-Gal(III) complex: Treatment was administered to the bacterial cells with 100 µL of Rutin-Gal(III), diluted to a concentration of 1/2×MIC. The microtiter plate was then kept in darkness for a duration of five min.

C. Blue laser: Treatment was administered to the bacterial cells with 100 µL of BHI broth, and subsequently, they were exposed to a blue laser (Laser Diode, ASHA, Iran) emitting light with a wavelength of 405 ± 10 nm for a duration of 60 s.

C. Blue laser: Treatment was administered to the bacterial cells with 100 µL of BHI broth, and subsequently, they were exposed to a blue laser (Laser Diode, ASHA, Iran) emitting light with a wavelength of 405 ± 10 nm for a duration of 60 s.

D. aPDT: Treatment was administered to the bacterial cells with Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin similar to groups A and B, respectively. Afterwards, the bacterial cells from each treatment group were exposed to the blue laser, similar to group C.

D. aPDT: Treatment was administered to the bacterial cells with Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin similar to groups A and B, respectively. Afterwards, the bacterial cells from each treatment group were exposed to the blue laser, similar to group C.

E. Negative control: Treatment was administered to the bacterial cells with 100 µL of normal saline. The microtiter plate was then kept in darkness for a duration of five min.

E. Negative control: Treatment was administered to the bacterial cells with 100 µL of normal saline. The microtiter plate was then kept in darkness for a duration of five min.

After each treatment, the bacterial cells were subjected to serial 5-fold dilution, plated, and incubated at 37 °C for 24. To assess bactericidal activity, the CFU/mL values were calculated based on the results.

Measurement of intracellular ROS generation

Herein, 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) was used to quantify the production of intracellular ROS in A. baumannii cells. The bacterial cells were collected by centrifuging the fresh bacterial suspension at 10,000× g for 15 min, and then washed with PBS (pH 7.4). Subsequently, the bacterial cell pellets were suspended in PBS to achieved a concentration of 108 CFU/mL. These cell suspensions were exposed to various treatment conditions, as described earlier (Antibacterial activities section), while a control group consisting of only bacterial cells remained untreated. After each treatment, the cell suspensions were treated with 200 µL of 10 µM DCFH-DA and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in the absence of light. Subsequently, the fluorescence intensity of DCF was measured using excitation and emission wavelengths of 485 nm and 530 nm, respectively (Pourhajibagher et al. 2022).

Inhibition of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS)

The quantification of EPS was performed using a total polysaccharide quantification assay. A. baumannii strains that underwent different treatment groups, as described earlier, were cultured in 96-well microtiter plate wells containing 1 mL of BHI broth to promote adherence and EPS production. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, the excess BHI broth was removed, and the attached cells were rinsed with PBS to eliminate any unattached planktonic cells. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of 1% phenol and 5 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid were added to the wells and incubated in darkness for one h. Following the incubation period, absorbance of the wells was assessed at a wavelength of 490 nm, and the percentage of inhibition of EPS was determined (Pourhajibagher et al. 2023).

Anti-biofilm activity

Wells of a 96-well microtiter plate were filled with 200 µL of bacterial suspension having a concentration of 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL, and the microtiter plate was then placed in a 37 °C incubator. After a period of 24 h, the bacterial cells were exposed to various treatments, as previously described, the microtiter plates were then washed twice with PBS to eliminate any loosely attached planktonic cells. Afterward, the bacterial cells were stained using a 0.1% v/v solution of crystal violet for 15 min, and the dye was subsequently dissolved by adding 33% v/v acetic acid. Finally, the optical density of the wells was measured at 570 nm.

Anti-metabolic activity

In order to assess the metabolic activity of A. baumannii cells following treatment with the mentioned treatment groups, the XTT (2,3-bis [2-methyloxy-4-nitro-5-sulfophenyl]-2 H-tetrazolium-5-carboxanilide) reduction assay kit from Roche Applied Science in Indianapolis, IN, US, was utilized. According to the protocol described in the 2012 study by Coraça-Hubér et al., the bacterial suspensions that underwent treatment were centrifuged at 2000× g for 10 min. The resulting sedimented bacterial cells were then collected and dissolved in a solution containing XTT-menadione-PBS (200 µL). Following this, the samples were incubated at a temperature of 37 °C for a duration of 3 h. After the incubation period, the solutions were transferred to a new 96-well microtiter plate, and the absorbance at a wavelength of 492 nm was measured.

Cytoplasmic membrane permeability assay

The quantity of nucleic acid released from the bacterial cells was measured as described previously, with some modifications (Saitoet al. 2019). Briefly, the bacterial culture was incubated in BHI broth at 37 °C for 24 h. The bacterial culture was centrifuged at 4000× g for 15 min and washed with PBS before being treated with different groups, as mentioned above. Following a 3-h incubation at 37 °C, the samples underwent centrifugation at a speed of 13,000× g, resulting in the collection of the supernatant. Finally, the quantity of nucleic acid released from the cytoplasm was assessed by measuring the absorbance at a wavelength of 260 nm.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis

To assess the effects of aPDT using flavonoid glycosides on the gene expression of ompA and blaOXA− 23 in A. baumannii, qRT-PCR was conducted. The primers utilized in this process, which were created with Primer3 software version 4.0 (http://bioinfo.ut.ee/primer3/), can be found in Table 1. After treating A. baumannii with 1/2×MIC of Rutin-Gal(III) complex- and Quercetin-mediated aPDT, total RNAs were extracted using the super RNA extraction Kit (AnaCell, Iran). The extracted total RNA underwent DNase I treatment and was then reverse transcribed using the Rever-tAid RT Reverse Transcription Kit from ThermoFisher (Waltham, MA, USA) following the instructions provided by the manufacturer. The amplification step was carried out using SYBR Premix Ex Taq II Kits manufactured by Takara, Japan, and the ABI StepOne™ Real-Time PCR System. The obtained data were normalized against the ribosomal gene 16 S rRNA, which served as an internal control, following the 2−∆∆CT method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001).

Statistical analysis

The experiments were performed in triplicate, and the resulting data was presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was carried out using SPSS 25.0 (IBM, New York, NY, USA) by conducting a one-way ANOVA with Tuckey’s multiple comparisons test to identify any significant differences. A p-value below 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant for all statistical analyses.

Results

Absorbance and fluorescence spectra of flavonoid glycosides

The absorbance and fluorescence spectra of Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin are shown in Fig. 1. UV-visible spectra of Quercetin and Rutin-Gal(III) complex resulted in a broad absorption peak characterized ranging from 240 to 280 nm and 340–440 nm (Fig. 1a) and 400 to 580 nm (Fig. 1b) respectively. Quercetin displayed a fluorescence peak at 524 nm upon excitation at 445 nm (Fig. 1c), whiles Rutin-Gal(III) complex revealed the strong fluorescence emission peak at 540 nm following excitation of 450 nm (Fig. 1d).

The absorbance and fluorescence spectra of flavonoid glycosides; a Absorbance spectra of Quercetin, b Absorbance spectrum of Rutin-Gal(III) complex, c Fluorescence spectra of Quercetin, d Fluorescence spectra of Rutin-Gal(III) complex

MIC of flavonoid glycosides

The antibacterial activity of Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin at different concentrations was tested against A. baumannii. According to broth microdilution test results, the growth turbidity in the broth was observed to evaluate the antibacterial activity. The results showed that the lowest concentrations of Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin that inhibited the visible growth of A. baumannii were 64 µg/mL and 256 µg/mL, respectively. The results obtained from measuring optical density at 600 nm validated the observed turbidity and the MIC values for Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin against A. baumannii.

Effect of aPDT on cell viability

The results in Fig. 2 showed that there was a significant reduction in cell viability of A. baumannii following aPDT groups compared to the control group (P < 0.05). There was a significantly more effective reduction in the CFUs/mL of viable planktonic cells when A. baumannii cells were treated with Quercetin- and Rutin-Gal(III) complex-mediated aPDT, as bacteria mortality was 58.4% and 67.5%, respectively (P < 0.05). Additionally, Quercetin and Rutin-Gal(III) complex groups could statistically reduce the survival of A. baumannii by 27.1% and 32.9%, respectively (P < 0.05). Cell viability reduction (14.2%) was also observed in blue light alone, but this difference was not significant (P > 0.05).

Effect of different treatment groups on cell viability of Acinetobacter baumannii in planktonic form. *Statistically significant different from the control group (no treatment), P < 0.05

Generation of intracellular ROS

The application of Quercetin at 1/2× MIC (128 µg/mL) and the Rutin-Gal(III) complex at 1/2× MIC (32 µg/mL), as well as blue laser irradiation, resulted in a rise in ROS levels compared to the control group (untreated cells) by 1.21-, 1.38-, and 1.15-folds, respectively (Fig. 3). The highest ROS production was detected in the aPDT groups, where the use of 1/2×MIC of Quercetin and the Rutin-Gal(III) complex resulted in 2.55- and 2.90-folds more ROS production, respectively, compared to the control group. This difference was statistically significant (P < 0.05; Fig. 3).

Effect of different treatment groups on the generation of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) in Acinetobacter baumannii. *Statistically significant different from the control group (no treatment), P < 0.05

Effect of aPDT on EPS

The findings indicated that flavonoid glycosides at a concentration of 1/2×MIC (32 µg/mL for Rutin-Gal(III) complex and 128 µg/mL for Quercetin) reduced EPS assembly and synthesis to 22.1% and 35.6%, respectively (P < 0.05; Fig. 4). This effect was also observed in A. baumannii treated with aPDT, with a more impact. aPDT using Quercetin and Rutin-Gal(III) complex resulted in a 47.4% and 56.5% reduction in EPS production, respectively (P < 0.05). However, there was no significant effect on the inhibition of ESP when A. baumannii cells were treated with blue lase alone (P > 0.05).

Effect of different treatment groups on extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) of Acinetobacter baumannii. *Statistically significant different from the control group (no treatment), P < 0.05

Effect of aPDT on biofilm degradation

Based on the data presented in Fig. 5, the untreated A. baumannii biofilm had an optical density (OD) of 2.7, which served as the control group. The biofilm reduction activity of aPDT using Quercetin and Rutin-Gal(III) complex against A. baumannii biofilm was significantly higher than that of the untreated biofilms, with reductions of 36.2% and 40.6%, respectively (P < 0.05). Nevertheless, no notable disparities were observed in the decrease of A. baumannii biofilm when subjected to individual treatments with Quercetin, the Rutin-Gal(III) complex, and blue laser, compared to the control group (all P > 0.05), as demonstrated in Fig. 5.

Effect of different treatment groups on cell viability of Acinetobacter baumannii in biofilm form. *Statistically significant different from the control group (no treatment), P < 0.05

Effect of aPDT on metabolic activity

As Fig. 6 shows, all treatment groups reduced the metabolic activity of A. baumannii cells. Specifically, aPDT using Quercetin reduced the metabolic activity by 30.5%, while a reduction of 36.3% was observed in the aPDT using Rutin-Gal(III) complex (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the application of blue laser irradiation also demonstrated a reduction in metabolic activity in A. baumannii compared to the control group, albeit to a lesser extent than what was observed in the Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin groups.

Effect of different treatment groups on metabolic activity of Acinetobacter baumannii. *Statistically significant different from the control group (no treatment), P < 0.05

Effect of aPDT on cell membrane permeability

According to the results in Fig. 7, a significant statistical difference was observed when comparing the absorbance values of the treatment groups to the control group. Specifically, the absorbance values obtained from aPDT utilizing Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin were higher compared to the absorbance values obtained from the Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin alone. The highest level of cell constituent release was observed when A. baumannii underwent treatment with Rutin-Gal(III) complex-mediated aPDT, showing an absorbance of 0.54, followed by Quercetin-mediated aPDT with an absorbance of 0.48 (Fig. 7; P < 0.05).

Effect of different treatment groups on cell membrane permeability of Acinetobacter baumannii. *Statistically significant different from the control group (no treatment), P < 0.05

Effect of aPDT on gene expression

The ompA and blaOXA−23 genes, which encode oxacillinases (a type of beta-lactamase enzyme) and outer membrane protein A (involved in bacterial adhesion, biofilm formation, and evasion of the host immune system), respectively, were significantly downregulated in response to aPDT, with ompA showing the most notable decrease. We observed that the ompA gene was downregulated by more than 5.5-fold and 10.5-fold in A. baumannii treated with Quercetin- and Rutin-Gal(III) complex-mediated aPDT, respectively in compared to the control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 8). Similarly, the study revealed a significant downregulation of the blaOXA−23 gene following aPDT treatment. The results showed that the aPDT using Quercetin and Rutin-Gal(III) complex could significantly decrease the expression of blaOXA−23 gene by 4.1-fold and 7.8-fold, respectively.

Effect of different treatment groups on gene expression of ompA and blaOXA−23. *Statistically significant different from the control group (no treatment), P < 0.05

Discussion

Infections caused by A. baumannii are a significant concern due to the heightened levels of multidrug resistance and the formation of biofilms, which further complicates treatment (Eze et al. 2018). The bacterium’s increasing antibiotic resistance makes combating infections challenging, with biofilm-associated antibiotic resistance being significantly higher than in planktonic cells, limiting the effectiveness of available antimicrobial therapies (Yang et al. 2019; Sherif et al. 2021). Unlike conventional antibiotics, aPDT does not lead to bacterial resistance as it targets multiple cellular components simultaneously. This makes it challenging for bacteria to develop resistance (Abdelkarim-Elafifi et al. 2021). aPDT can be applied precisely to the affected area, allowing for targeted treatment, minimizing damage to surrounding healthy tissues, and reducing systemic side effects (Algorri et al. 2023).

Photosensitizers play a crucial role in aPDT by acting as light-sensitive agents that generate ROS upon light activation. The type of photosensitizer used is a key factor influencing the outcome of aPDT, with various classes of photosensitizers being utilized, including natural photosensitizers such as flavonoid glycosides like Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin (Abrahamse and Hamblin 2016; Polat and Kang 2021; Pourhajibagher et al. 2021; Lee et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2023; Pourhajibagher et al. 2024). In this work, the enhancement efficiency of flavonoid glycosides-mediated aPDT against planktonic and biofilm cultures of A. baumannii was studied.

This study focused on determining the most effective concentration of Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin when used with a blue laser as photosensitizing agents to improve treatment effectiveness while minimizing potential side effects. Flavonoids are the most abundant and widespread secondary metabolites found in plants, especially in fruits and vegetables. They are the largest and most diverse polyphenolic compounds that have been extensively studied and identified. Flavonoids play a crucial role in determining the fragrance and color of flowers. These compounds offer numerous health benefits and are considered dietary supplements that support overall health and disease prevention. Flavonoids possess potent antioxidant properties and exhibit antiviral, antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and antiallergic activities. Due to their diverse functions and beneficial effects, flavonoids are essential in developing pharmaceutical nutraceuticals, medicinal products, cosmetics, and other applications. Given their antibacterial properties, flavonoids are being used as wound-healing agents. Flavonoids showed great permeability properties through the skin (Chen et al. 2023; Butkeviciute et al. 2022). Previous studies have demonstrated the photophysical properties of flavonoids and their ability to act as photosensitizers in aPDT (Abrahamse and Hamblin 2016; Polat and Kang 2021; Pourhajibagher et al. 2021; Lee et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2023; Pourhajibagher et al. 2024).

It has been revealed that the excitation wavelength for the flavonoid glycosides is 405 nm, which is in the blue light range. Blue light has a relatively shallow penetration depth compared to other visible wavelengths, which could be a limitation for aPDT applications that require deeper tissue penetration. Monte Carlo simulations have shown that the maximum penetration depth using an Intense Pulsed Light (IPL) system is 1 mm for light with a 400 nm wavelength (Badugu et al. 2005). It has shown that the thickness of the human epidermis ranges from 31.2 μm (for the penis) to 596.6 μm (for the plantar aspect of the foot) (Lintzeri et al. 2022). This is also the area where the highest load of microorganisms is found, as stated in the skin and soft tissue infections (Baquero et al. 2021). The active compounds in flavonoid glycosides can penetrate to the depths of the dermis in the skin (Butkeviciute et al. 2022). Irradiation, such as blue light, has the potential to activate these antimicrobial compounds in the epidermis. When exposed to specific wavelengths of light, the compounds generate ROS and dramatically increase oxidative stress enhancing their antimicrobial activity. This suggests that it may be possible to activate these compounds using irradiation in the epidermis, which could potentially help inactivate microorganisms in this area. To inactivate microorganisms in the deeper layers of the skin, specifically the dermis, it is necessary to use IPL with a larger spot size and longer wavelengths of light. Studies have shown that increasing the spot size employed by the IPL device and using longer wavelengths can lead to greater penetration depths in the skin. Using an IPL device with a spot size of at least 10 mm in width leads to a decrease in lateral scattering of the light. This results in greater penetration of the light into the skin. Therefore, using an IPL device with a spot size of at least 10 mm can achieve deeper penetration depths, which may be beneficial for treating cutaneous lesions. Another way to increase the penetration depth of light is to use light with higher wavelengths. When using light with higher wavelengths, it is important to use photosensitizers that absorb light at those specific wavelengths. Studies have shown that by modifying the structure of photosensitizers, they can be activated by light with higher wavelengths (Badugu et al. 2005).

The results revealed that the optimal concentrations of Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin against A. baumannii were 64 µg/mL and 256 µg/mL, respectively. To the best of our knowledge, no prior studies have investigated the impact of aPDT using flavonoid glycosides on A. baumannii. After carefully reviewing the provided sources on aPDT, it is evident that aPDT is effective not only against planktonic cells but also biofilms, making it a promising approach to combat biofilm-associated infections. The therapy’s ability to target a broad spectrum of microorganisms and its non-selective nature with multiple targets make it a valuable tool in overcoming antimicrobial resistance associated with biofilms (de Melo et al. 2013; Martins Antunes de Melo et al. 2021; Li et al. 2023).

At first, photo-eradication of A. baumannii planktonic cultures was performed following aPDT. The results of this study demonstrated that aPDT with Quercetin and Rutin-Gal(III) complex led to a significant decrease in A. baumannii bacterial count by 58.4% and 67.5% respectively. After that, the results demonstrated that the combination of Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin with blue laser light led to a substantial and statistically significant increase in ROS levels compared to the untreated control, indicating the potential antimicrobial efficacy of these aPDT approaches. These findings emphasize that the activation of the photosensitizer and subsequent generation of cytotoxic ROS is the key process underlying aPDT (Kolarova et al. 2008; Ghorbani et al. 2018; Li et al. 2021). In the following, the photo-destruction of A. baumannii biofilm was carried out in the presence of aPDT. The data revealed that aPDT with Quercetin and Rutin-Gal(III) complex remarkably eliminated 36.2% and 40.6% of mature A. baumannii biofilm, respectively.

The effect of aPDT on the EPS of A. baumannii was then investigated. EPS plays a crucial role in A. baumannii biofilm formation and pathogenesis. EPS in A. baumannii is involved in enhancing biofilm formation, aiding in bacterial adherence, contributing to multidrug resistance, and promoting virulence factor expression. The presence of a biofilm matrix surrounding A. baumannii provides the bacteria with the capability to withstand harsh conditions, including antimicrobial treatments. This resilience contributes to the development of chronic and persistent infections, antibiotic resistance, and the bacteria’s ability to survive in hospital environments (Davenport et al. 2014; Deliorman et al. 2019; Gedefie et al. 2021). In this study, it was found that in the presence of aPDT treatment, the EPS was significantly decreased. With decreased EPS, the biofilm becomes more permeable to the ROS produced during aPDT, making the bacteria more susceptible to aPDT treatment, ultimately leading to a reduction in biofilm formation and improved antimicrobial efficacy.

According to previous studies, aPDT causes oxidative stress and metabolic perturbations in microbial cells, leading to a decrease in the number of viable cells (Wanarska et al. 2022). The oxidative stress induced by aPDT results in the generation of ROS within bacterial cells, disrupting cellular functions and metabolic processes. This disruption in metabolic activity can impact various cellular functions, including energy production, protein synthesis, and cell division, ultimately leading to a decrease in bacterial viability. Furthermore, the reduction in metabolic activity following aPDT treatment can affect the biofilm formation capacity of bacteria. This reduction in metabolic activity weakens the biofilm matrix, making the bacteria more susceptible to antimicrobial treatments and reducing their ability to form resilient biofilms (Pourhajibagher et al. 2016; Muehler et al. 2020). The results of this study suggest the potential of aPDT using Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin in reducing the metabolic activity of A. baumannii, which is significant in the context of developing treatments against this pathogen.

The blaOXA−23 and ompA genes in A. baumannii are crucial in biofilm formation, antibiotic resistance, and pathogenicity. These genes contribute to the ability of A. baumannii to form robust biofilms, which are associated with increased resistance to antibiotics and challenges in treating infections caused by this pathogen (Luo et al. 2015; Yang et al. 2019). The role of aPDT in reducing the expression of genes involved in the formation of bacterial biofilms is significant (Abdulrahman et al. 2020; Jordão et al. 2020; Yu et al. 2022). Through the generation of ROS, aPDT has the capacity to disturb the structure of biofilms and suppress the expression of genes accountable for biofilm formation. This disruption impacts proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids present within the biofilm matrix, resulting in cellular damage and the inhibition of biofilm growth (Buchovec et al. 2022). The results of this study demonstrate a significant downregulation of ompA and blaOXA−23 genes in response to aPDT. These findings underscore the capacity of aPDT utilizing Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin not only in disrupting biofilm formation but also in downregulating key genes. The downregulation of the blaOXA−23 and ompA genes may result in reduced resistance to complement-mediated killing and impaired biofilm formation, ultimately impacting the bacterium’s ability to evade host defenses and persist in the host environment. Therefore, by targeting the specific genes, aPDT offers a promising strategy to combat biofilm-associated infections.

The validation of aPDT using flavonoid glycosides against A. baumannii biofilms presents a novel therapeutic strategy with promising outcomes. By targeting biofilm-associated infections, this approach addresses a critical need in healthcare settings where traditional antibiotics often fall short. The use of natural compounds like flavonoid glycosides not only enhances antimicrobial activity but also offers a safer and potentially more sustainable alternative to conventional therapies. It is essential to note that while aPDT shows promise in treating bacterial infections, more research and clinical trials are warranted to optimize its protocols, determine its long-term efficacy, explore the translational potential of this therapy and its applicability in real-world healthcare settings, assess its potential side effects, and investigate its combination with other treatment modalities to maximize its antimicrobial effectiveness against A. baumannii.

It can be concluded that the aPDT promotes the production of ROS as a result of the interaction between a photosensitizer and blue laser light. aPDT approach utilizing Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin resulted in notable reductions in bacterial viability, EPS assembly, biofilm formation, metabolic activity, and downregulating in gene expression of ompA and blaOXA−23. These findings underscore the potential effectiveness of Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin as effective agents in combating A. baumannii infections, offering a multifaceted approach to targeting bacterial cells and biofilms. Moreover, this suggests that aPDT with flavonoid glycosides could be a viable alternative to conventional antibiotic treatments for A. baumannii infections. Nevertheless, further research is necessary to explore the full potential of Rutin-Gal(III) complex and Quercetin as photosensitizers in aPDT against clinical isolates of A. baumannii, as well as to conduct in vivo studies to confirm their effectiveness and ensure their safety profiles for use in clinical settings.
