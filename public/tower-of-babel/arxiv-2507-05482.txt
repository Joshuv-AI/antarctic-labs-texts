Navigating Sparse Molecular Data with Stein Diffusion Guidance
Van Khoa Nguyen, Lionel Blondé, Alexandros Kalousis

Abstract
Stochastic optimal control (SOC) has recently emerged as a principled framework for fine-tuning diffusion models. However, its dependence on computationally intensive simulations makes it impractical for fast sampling. In parallel, a class of training-free approaches has been developed that guides diffusion models using off-the-shelf classifiers on predicted clean samples, bypassing the need to train classifiers on noisy data. These methods can be interpreted as approximate SOC schemes, using Tweedie’s formula to estimate diffusion posteriors. In practice, however, such direct approximations can introduce significant errors, leading to unreliable guidance. In this work, we unify the strengths of both paradigms by proposing a novel training-free diffusion guidance framework based on a surrogate stochastic optimal control objective. We derive a new theoretical bound on the value function that reveals the necessity of correcting the approximate posteriors to remain faithful to the true diffusion posterior. To this end, we connect the problem with Stein variational inference, which seeks the steepest descent direction that minimizes the Kullback-Leibler discrepancy between the two posteriors. Our method, which we refer to as Stein Diffusion Guidance (SDG), introduces a principled correction mechanism and incorporates a novel running cost functional to enable effective guidance in low-density regions. Experiments on challenging molecular generation tasks demonstrate that SDG significantly outperforms standard training-free guidance methods, highlighting its potential for broader applications.

In many scientific disciplines, important discoveries hinge on identifying rare data points hidden within vast, redundant distributions. For instance, in chemistry, there are billions of possible molecules

We hypothesize that these high-interest samples reside in low-density regions, making their discovery challenging and error-prone. As a result, there is growing interest in methods that can accelerate searching for such rare, property-rich samples.
Generative methods, particularly diffusion/score-based models

However, when trained on unlabeled data, diffusion models predominantly sample from high-density data regions, thereby overlooking the low-density areas where high-value samples are likely to reside. This limitation hinders their effectiveness in tasks that require discovery beyond the data majority.
Numerous studies have been proposed to address this challenge. A particularly influential class of methods leverages an auxiliary classifier,

Stochastic optimal control (SOC) methods

Uehara et al. 2024

It thus enables SOC-guided sampling to be more advantageous when leveraging off-the-shelf classifiers directly. However, computing the optimal control value requires backpropagating reward signals through entire neural-SDE sampling trajectories

To circumvent the computational bottleneck, some recent works have proposed directly approximating the diffusion posterior through Tweedie’s formula

Preliminaries

2.1
Diffusion models

Song et al. 2021
proposed a unified diffusion-model framework over continuous-time,
\forall t\in[0,T]
, and continuous-state space,
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, utilizing a pair of forward and backward stochastic differential equations (SDEs). The forward process perturbs clean i.i.d data samples,
\mathbf{x}_{T}\sim p_{T}
, towards a predefined prior,
\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim p_{0}
. Its dynamic is the solution to an Itô SDE:
d\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)dt+\sigma(t)d\mathbf{w}
, where
dt<0
is an infinitesimal timestep,
\mathbf{w}
is the Weiner process, and
\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t),\sigma(t)
denote the drift and diffusion coefficients, respectively. Specific noise schedules
\mathbf{b},\sigma
can recover standard discrete-time diffusion frameworks, termed as variance-exploding
[
40
]
and variance-preserving
[
13
]
SDEs. A backward process gradually denoises samples from the prior
p_{0}
and back to the data distribution
p_{T}
; the reverse dynamic corresponds to another Itô SDE of the form:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}:\;\;\;d\mathbf{x}_{t}=\left(-\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)+\sigma(t)^{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right)dt+\sigma(t)d\mathbf{w}

(1)

where the marginal data score,
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
, can be estimated by the score-matching technique
[
15
]
via a time-dependent score-based network,
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\right)\approx\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
; for simplicity, we omit the explicit
t
-dependence in the score model notation. Unlike
Song et al. 2021
, we employ a positive infinitesimal reverse timestep to facilitate our theoretical development of novel diffusion guidance in subsequent sections.
In sampling, diffusion models can incorporate a classifier
r(\cdot)
to guide samples toward regions with desired properties, a technique known as classifier-based diffusion guidance
[
5
]
. The conditional score can be factorized into
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|y)\propto\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}r(y|\mathbf{x}_{t})
, which requires training the classifier
r(\cdot)
on noisy data of multiple noise levels,
\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{T}),\forall t\in[0,T]
.

2.2
Stein variational inference

Variational inference methods approximate a complex target distribution
p
using a simpler distribution
q\in\mathcal{Q}
, a set of tractable distributions, by solving the KL divergence minimization problem,
\arg\min_{q}D_{KL}\left(q\|p\right)
. Stein variational gradient descent (SVGD)
[
24
,
23
]
is a nonparametric method that finds
q
as a set of interacting particles and deterministically evolves them along a direction
\phi
, which most efficiently decreases the KL divergence.
By leveraging on Stein’s identity and kernelized Stein discrepancy (KSD),
Liu and Wang 2016
proved that the steepest gradient descent direction problem can be solved in the unit ball of a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
\mathcal{H}
; the lemma below summarizes their core result:

Lemma 2.1

-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}D_{KL}\left(q\|p\right)
steepest direction maximizes Stein discrepancy in the RKHS unit ball:

\displaystyle\phi^{*}=\arg\max_{\phi\in\mathcal{H}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q}\left[\text{trace}\left(\mathcal{A}_{p}\phi\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)\right]\;\;\text{st}\;\;\|\phi\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq 1

\displaystyle\text{with}\;\;\mathcal{A}_{p}\phi(\mathbf{\mathbf{x}})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p(\mathbf{x})\phi(\mathbf{\mathbf{x}})+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\phi(\mathbf{\mathbf{x}})

(2)

where
\mathcal{A}_{p}
is the Stein operator,
\phi
is the Stein class of the target density
p
.
Liu et al. 2016
solved the problem (
2.1
) as KSD that yields
\phi^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{i})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}^{j}\sim q(\mathbf{x})}\left[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{j}}\log p(\mathbf{x}^{j})k(\mathbf{x}^{i},\mathbf{x}^{j})+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{j}}k(\mathbf{x}^{i},\mathbf{x}^{j})\right]
, where
k(\mathbf{x}^{i},\mathbf{x}^{j})
is a RKHS kernel, e.g. Radial Basic Function (RBF)
k(\mathbf{x}^{i},\mathbf{x}^{j})=\exp{\left(-\frac{1}{h}\|\mathbf{x}^{i}-\mathbf{x}^{j}\|_{2}^{2}\right)}
. SVGD can approximate the intractable target density
p
by gradually transporting a set of
N
initial particles
\{\mathbf{x}_{0}^{i}\}_{i=0}^{N}\sim q_{0}
along the direction
\phi^{*}
, which relies on the computable score
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p(\mathbf{x})
. The first term of
\phi^{*}
represents a kernel-weighted gradient ascent direction that pushes the particles toward the high-density regions of the target
p(\mathcal{\mathbf{x}})
. The second term denotes the repulsive force that prevents the particles from collapsing into the local models of
p(\mathbf{x})
. In practice, the set of initial particles can be set arbitrarily, and the convergence of particles to their actual density can be guaranteed
\sum_{i=1}^{N}h(\mathbf{x}^{i})/N-\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{x})}[h(\mathbf{x})]=\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N})
for bounded testing functions
h

[
4
]
.

2.3
Stochastic optimal control

In general, the stochastic optimal control problem
[
29
,
7
]
seeks an optimal controller that steers the behavior of a given stochastic system to minimize a pre-specified cost function. For the dynamical system of reverse diffusion process in Equation
1
, we formalize an affine-control problem as below:

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{u}\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\mathbf{u}},t\right)\|^{2}+f\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\mathbf{u}},t\right)\right)dt+g(\mathbf{x}_{T}^{\mathbf{u}})\right]

\displaystyle s.t.\;\;\;\mathbb{P}^{\mathbf{u}}:\;\;\;d\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\mathbf{u}}=\left(-\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\mathbf{u}},t)+\sigma(t)^{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\mathbf{u}}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\mathbf{u}})+\sigma(t)\mathbf{u}\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\mathbf{u}},t\right)\right)dt+\sigma(t)d\mathbf{w}

(3)

where
\mathbf{u}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the feedback control that drives the system dynamics and takes the values from a set of admissible controls
\mathcal{U}
,
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[t,T]\mapsto[0,\infty)
specifies the intermediate state cost,
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto[0,\infty)
is the terminal cost, and
\mathbb{P}^{u}
denotes the controlled probability path measures from
\mathbb{P}
.
The control objective finds an optimal feedback that can minimize the cost functional:
J\left(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x},t\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\mathbf{u}}}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{u}\left(\mathbf{x}_{s}^{\mathbf{u}},s\right)\|^{2}+f\left(\mathbf{x}_{s}^{\mathbf{u}},s\right)\right)ds+g(\mathbf{x}_{T}^{\mathbf{u}})|\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}\right]
. The minimum cost functional defines the value function or optimal cost-to-go
[
9
]
:
V\left(\mathbf{x},t\right)=\inf_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}}J\left(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x},t\right)
.
Moreover, the verification theorem
[
9
,
31
]
proves that the optimal control and value function relate to each other,
\mathbf{u}^{*}=-\sigma\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}V
. The affine stochastic control problem with a quadratic cost on the control can be closely connected to an iterative diffusion optimization (IDO) technique with a relative entropy (or KL divergence) loss
[
33
,
21
,
11
]
, which involves simulating multiple controlled trajectories, computing their cumulative costs, and backpropagating through the trajectories to update a parameterized controller. The value function then satisfies a Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman partial differential equation (HJBPDE)
[
30
]
associated with the uncontrolled reverse SDE (
1
), which can be solved by means of the celebrated Feynman-Kac formula
[
31
]
; a more extensive treatment of SOC problem can be found in
[
29
,
7
]
. Here, we summarize the essential results in the following lemma:

Lemma 2.2

Optimal controller and value function for the control problem in Equation
2.3
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{*}\left(\mathbf{x},t\right)=-\sigma(t)\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}V(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle V(\mathbf{x},t)=-\log\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\exp\left(-\int_{t}^{T}f\left(\mathbf{x}_{s},s\right)ds-g\left(\mathbf{x}_{T}\right)\right)|\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}\right]

(4)

where
\mathbf{x}
refers to the solution of the uncontrolled problem (
1
). As we observe, at each state, we need to integrate through the diffusion trajectories from
\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}
under
\mathbb{P}
to get the value function, and compute the gradients
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}V(\mathbf{x},t)
within the simulation trajectories to output the optimal controller. These operations are very costly and significantly slow for practical applications.
In some cases, the state cost is often being ignored
f(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)=0\;\forall t
, in which
Uehara et al. 2024
established the connection between SOC-based diffusion finetuning and classifier guidance. By using the single sample estimation, the optimal controller can be shown to be equivalent to
\mathbf{u}^{*}=\sigma(t)\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\exp\left(-g\left(\mathbf{x}_{T}\right)\right)|\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}\right]\propto\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[r(\mathbf{x}_{T})|\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}\right]

, wherein
r=-g
, due to the minimization problem, is an off-the-shelf classifier, which was trained from clean data, or a differential reward function.

3
Stein diffusion guidance

We present a surrogate control objective to the original SOC problem. We begin discussing a novel SOC cost functional that enables low-density sampling with guided diffusion models. We then introduce a variational bound on the SOC’s value function, by which we show that standard training-free guidance methods need additional correction steps on the clean diffusion manifold. We propose the Stein correction mechanism that relates to the optimal controller of the surrogate value function. Given the varying terminology in the existing literature, we use the terms classifier and reward function interchangeably throughout the sections below. We provide the detailed proofs in Appendix.

3.1
Low density diffusion sampling as a stochastic control problem

We consider the controlled reverse SDE (
2.3
) and its associated probability path measure
\mathbb{P}^{\mathbf{u}}
. We then propose a novel cost functional
J(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x},t)
that progressively anneals the marginal density
p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
of the uncontrolled diffusion process (
1
) under
\mathbb{P}
to low-density regions. Following Lemma
2.2
, we formulate the low-density diffusion guidance problem as a stochastic optimal control task:

Proposition 3.1

Let the cost functional of the control problem in Equation
2.3
be
J\left(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x},t\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\mathbf{u}}}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{u}\left(\mathbf{x}_{s}^{\mathbf{u}},s\right)\|^{2}+\alpha(s)\log p_{s}\left(\mathbf{x}_{s}^{\mathbf{u}}\right)\delta(s-t)\right)ds-\beta(t)r(\mathbf{x}_{T}^{\mathbf{u}})|\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}\right]
, then the annealed reward-guided marginal density
p_{t}^{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
, the value function
V\left(\mathbf{x},t\right)
, and the optimal control
\mathbf{u}^{*}\left(\mathbf{x},t\right)
corresponding to the controlled-reverse SDE under
\mathbb{P}^{\mathbf{u}}
can be derived as follows:

\displaystyle p_{t}^{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=p_{t}^{1-\alpha(t)}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\exp(\beta(t)r(\mathbf{x}_{T}))

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{*}\left(\mathbf{x},t\right)=\sigma(t)\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\frac{p_{t}^{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x})}{p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}|\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}\right]\;\;\text{and}\;\;V\left(\mathbf{x},t\right)=-\log\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\frac{p_{t}^{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x})}{p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}|\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}\right]

(5)

where
\alpha(t)
and
\beta(t)
denote the annealing schedules controlling low-density weighting and guidance strength, respectively,
r(\cdot)
is an off-the-shelf classifier, which was priorly trained on clean data samples, or a differential reward function. The induced marginal density is the product of the annealed density term
p_{t}^{1-\alpha(t)}
and the guidance term
\exp(\beta(t)r(\mathbf{x}_{T}))
, which can be interpreted as an un-normalized energy density with the energy function
-\beta(t)r(\mathbf{x}_{T})
.

As discussed previously, getting the SOC solutions is computationally expensive as one have to simulate at each reverse diffusion step
\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}
, following the uncontrolled reverse process
\mathbb{P}
, to obtain the endpoint samples
\mathbf{x}_{T}
on the clean diffusion manifold
\mathcal{M}_{T}
, which are then evaluated by the differential classifier/ reward function
r(\mathbf{x}_{T})
. Notably, one also needs to backpropagate through the sampling trajectories to get the optimal control
\mathbf{u}^{*}\left(\mathbf{x},t\right)

[
44
,
47
]
. As a remedy to the expensive SOC simulation, we can approximate the diffusion posterior by a proposal distribution
q_{T|t}(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathbf{x}_{t})\approx p_{T|t}(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathbf{x}_{t})
, whose mean value can be estimated via Tweedie’s formula
\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}+\gamma^{2}(t)\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)}{\eta(t)}
, assuming a forward perturbing kernel
p_{t|T}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{T})=\mathcal{N}(\eta(t)\mathbf{x}_{T},\gamma^{2}(t)I)
.
This one-step posterior approximation has been previously explored in many prior works. However, in this study, we argue that the Tweedie-based proposal distribution is inherently suboptimal. As illustrated in Figure
1
, the approximate solution can significantly deviate from the endpoint sample given by the SOC simulation. In the following sections, we present an upper variational bound on the SOC value function, analyze the sub-optimality of the Tweedie-based approximation, and propose the Stein correction mechanism.

3.2
Value function variational bound

We recall the diffusion posterior
p_{T|t}(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathbf{x}_{t})
, which is the terminal distribution evolving from the state
\mathbf{x}_{t}
under the uncontrolled reverse process
\mathbb{P}
. According to Proposition
3.1
, we can rewrite the low-density guidance value function as
V\left(\mathbf{x},t\right)=-\log\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{T}\sim p_{T|t}(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathbf{x})}\left[\frac{p_{t}^{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x})}{p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}|\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}\right]
, where
p_{t}^{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x})=p_{t}^{1-\alpha(t)}(\mathbf{x})\exp(\beta(t)r(\mathbf{x}_{T}))
. To make optimization more computationally tractable, we introduce a surrogate objective
\widetilde{V}(\mathbf{x},t,q)
with
q\in\mathcal{Q}
, which is the upper bound of the value function:

Proposition 3.2

Given a traceable family of distributions
q\in\mathcal{Q}
, the value function in Proposition
3.1
can be upper bounded:

\displaystyle V(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{V}(\mathbf{x},t,q)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{T}\sim q_{T|t}(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathbf{x})}\left[\log\left(\frac{p_{t}^{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x})}{p_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\right)|\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}\right]+D_{KL}\left(q(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathbf{x}_{t})\|p(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathbf{x}_{t})\right)|_{\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=\alpha(t)\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x})-\beta(t)\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{T}\sim q_{T|t}(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathbf{x})}\left[r(\mathbf{x}_{T})|\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+D_{KL}\left(q(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathbf{x}_{t})\|p(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathbf{x}_{t})\right)|_{\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}}

(6)

The first RHS term corresponds to the part driving samples toward low-density regions. The second term guides samples toward regions with desired properties. When estimating the proposal distribution mean by Tweedie’s formula, we recover exactly the training-free diffusion guidance explored in the previous works
[
3
,
28
,
49
,
27
,
37
,
35
,
17
,
6
]
, indicating no additional classifier training
r(\cdot)
on noisy data inputs
\mathbf{x}_{t}
required as in the standard classifier-based guidance approaches. The last term serves as a KL regularization that minimizes the divergence between the proposal posterior
q(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathbf{x}_{t})
and the true posterior
p(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathbf{x}_{t})
. This formulation shows that using the Tweedie-based approximate posterior alone is suboptimal, as it ignores the KL regularization and thus fails to ensure proximity to the true diffusion posterior. To address this, we propose a novel diffusion correction mechanism that refines the approximate posterior before drawing samples for guidance.

3.3
Stein meets Tweedie for surrogate stochastic optimal control

We consider a set of samples (or particles) residing on a noisy diffusion manifold
\mathcal{M}_{t}
. The optimal control for the surrogate value function
\widetilde{V}(\mathbf{x},t,q)
involves one part of minimizing the KL divergence
D_{KL}\left(q(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathbf{x}_{t})\|p(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathbf{x}_{t})\right)
. Following the result of Lemma
2.1
, this corresponds to evolving the particle set along the KSD’s direction on
\mathcal{M}_{t}
, given as
\phi^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}\sim q_{T|t}(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathbf{x}_{t})}[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t}}\log p(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}|\mathbf{x}_{t}^{j})k(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T},\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T})+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t}}k(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T},\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T})]

\partial\mathbf{x}_{T}^{j}/\partial\mathbf{x}_{t}^{j}
terms. To address this computation burden, we propose
a back-and-forth correction trick
: first, mapping noisy particles backward to the clean diffusion manifold
\mathcal{M}_{T}
, applying the Stein correction step there, and then mapping the corrected particles forward to the noisy manifold
\mathcal{M}_{t}

Sampling novel molecular hits

As shown in Table

As a result, samples drawn from these approximations fail to provide reliable guidance, particularly in low-density regions where score-based models struggle to estimate data scores due to sparse training data. In contrast, SDG substantially enhances guidance by leveraging Stein-corrected samples, leading to improvements of several orders of magnitude. This demonstrates both theoretical and empirical support for our Stein correction mechanism. Moreover, SDG further boosts the performance of the pretrained GDSS model and its classifier-guided variant, MOOD, across different protein targets. Compared to non-diffusion methods, SDG generates more hit compounds for two out of the three target proteins. Figure

We study the problem of approximating ground-truth (genuine) rewards using parametric classifier/reward models. In many applications, genuine rewards are computed by non-differentiable oracle functions, which cannot be directly used in standard diffusion guidance methods. Classifiers are trained to learn these genuine rewards and produce approximate (nominal) rewards, serving as differentiable proxies for classifier-based diffusion. However, due to the finite number of training samples, classifiers tend to provide reliable nominal rewards only within the support of the training data distribution. This limitation becomes more severe in low-density sampling problems.
Uehara et al. 2024
first formulated this issue and proposed an entropy-regularized control approach, which is equivalent to solving a costly stochastic optimal control problem.
In our case study, the absence of regularization leads the reward model to produce highly unreliable estimates of the true reward during the early stages of the sampling process (Figure
5
.b), resulting in the generation of novel molecular structures that lack drug-likeness and are difficult to synthesize (Figure
5
.a and Figure
4
). Additionally, the score models are often misdirected away from the generative manifold (Figure
5
.c and
5
.d). On the generative manifold
\mathcal{M}_{T}
, high-density regions are typically associated with low data scores; in non-low-density settings, the score models for predicted clean (denoised) samples tend to move from high-score to low-score regions during the sampling process.
SDG provides an efficient regularization strategy via the Stein correction mechanism, which improves reward estimation accuracy (Figure
5
.b), promotes the generation of more realistic and synthesizable molecules (Figure
5
.a and Figure
4
), and preserves sampling within the generative manifold (Figure
5
.c and
5
.d), particularly in challenging low-density problems.

5
Conclusion

Tweedie’s formula has emerged as a widely adopted method for estimating diffusion posteriors in many training-free diffusion guidance schemes, as it circumvents the need for training additional classifiers on noisy data. In this work, we prove that this approach is inherently suboptimal through the lens of stochastic optimal control. To address this limitation, we propose a plug-and-play Stein correction mechanism that aligns approximate posteriors with the true posterior before drawing samples for guidance. Moreover, our Stein correction acts as an efficient low-cost regularization technique, ensuring samples remain within generative manifolds and thereby enabling classifier or reward models to yield more reliable estimates of genuine rewards and guidance signals. Our experimental results demonstrate that Stein Diffusion Guidance significantly improves the standard training-free diffusion guidance framework on the challenging low-density molecular sampling problem, highlighting its potential for broader applications in conditional sampling with diffusion guidance that directly leverage off-the-shelf classifiers or reward models across various domains.

Appendix A
Broader impacts and limitations

Our work presents a novel, generic approach to training-free diffusion guidance by connecting with stochastic optimal control theory. The proposed application is for low-density molecular sampling, with the potential to advance drug discovery finding effective drug candidates for cancer treatments. However, in the wrong hands, it could be misused to illicitly design harmful or addictive substances. Ensuring responsible usage is, therefore, critical to maximizing its positive societal impact. The main limitation of our study is that it has only been validated on molecular generation tasks. Nonetheless, we believe that the SDG framework can be extended to other controlled generation settings across different data modalities.

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}\sim q_{T|t}(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathcal{D}_{t})}\left[\left(\mathbf{s}_{\theta}\left(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}\right)-\eta(t)\mathbf{s}_{\theta}\left(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t}\right)\right)k(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T},\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T})+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}}k(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T},\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T})|\mathcal{D}_{t}\right]}_{\textbf{Stein correction on }\mathcal{M}_{T}}

\displaystyle\oplus\quad\underbrace{-\alpha(t)\mathbf{s}_{\theta}\left(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{t}\right)+\beta(t)\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{i}_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T}\sim q^{\epsilon}_{T|t}(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathcal{D}_{t})}\left[r(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T})|\mathbf{x}^{i}_{t}\right]}_{\textbf{low-density guidance on }\mathcal{M}_{t}}

(15)

Proof
: From Lemma
2.2
, we obtain the optimal controller corresponding to the surrogate value function:

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{t},t)}{\sigma(t)}=-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{i}_{t}}\widetilde{V}(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{t},t,q)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{i}_{t}}D_{KL}\left(q(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T}|\mathbf{x}^{i}_{t})\|p(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T}|\mathbf{x}^{i}_{t})\right)}_{\textbf{I}}+\underbrace{-\alpha(t)\mathbf{s}_{\theta}\left(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{t}\right)+\beta(t)\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{i}_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T}\sim q_{T|t}(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathcal{D}_{t})}\left[r(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T})|\mathbf{x}^{i}_{t}\right]}_{\textbf{II}}

(16)

where the first term (
I
) corresponds to the controller component that follows the gradient descent direction of the KL divergence between the approximate and true posteriors with respect to the current noisy state. The second term (
II
) captures low-density sampling and reward/property guidance based on predicted clean samples. Most existing training-free diffusion guidance methods focus solely on the second term while ignoring the first term. It thus makes them inherently suboptimal. Similar to most of standard training-free framework, we initialize the proposal distribution
q_{T|t}(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathbf{x})
with its mean value estimated via Tweedie’s formula
\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}+\gamma^{2}(t)\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)}{\eta(t)}
, assuming a forward perturbing kernel
p_{t|T}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{T})=\mathcal{N}(\eta(t)\mathbf{x}_{T},\gamma^{2}(t)I)
at the
t^{th}
timestep. In this work, we propose a novel training-free guidance method, where the guidance direction is determined by both terms:

The KL divergence term
(
I
):
As the true posterior
p(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T}|\mathbf{x}^{i}_{t})
does not admit a closed-form expression, we can not compute analytically the KL divergence and its gradient. To address this, we leverage the Stein variational inference, a nonparametric approach that identifies the steepest gradient direction to minimize the KL divergence. Assuming a batch of N particle samples at the
t^{th}
diffusion timestep
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\{\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i}\}_{i=0}^{N}
, we apply Lemma
2.1
to obtain the KSD’s direction that minimizes
D_{KL}\left(q(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T}|\mathbf{x}^{i}_{t})\|p(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T}|\mathbf{x}^{i}_{t})\right)
:

\displaystyle\phi^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}\sim q_{T|t}(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathbf{x}_{t})}[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t}}\log p(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}|\mathbf{x}_{t}^{j})k(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T},\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T})+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t}}k(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T},\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T})]

Computing this direction requires numerous Jacobian-vector product evaluations, including
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}}\log p(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}|\mathbf{x}_{t}^{j})\frac{\partial\mathbf{x}_{T}^{j}}{\partial\mathbf{x}_{t}^{j}}
and
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}}k(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T},\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T})\frac{\partial\mathbf{x}_{T}^{j}}{\partial\mathbf{x}_{t}^{j}}
, which is computationally expensive as the number of particles
N
increases. To alleviate this computational burden, we propose
a back-and-forth correction trick
:

•

Apply Tweedie’s formula to map the particle set
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\{\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i}\}_{i=0}^{N}
on
\mathcal{M}_{t}
backward to
\mathcal{D}_{T}\leftarrow\left\{\mathbf{x}_{T}^{i}|\mathbf{x}_{T}^{i}=\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i}+\gamma^{2}(t)\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i})}{\eta(t)},\forall\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{t}\right\}
on
\mathcal{M}_{T}
, which represents the initial proposal distribution
q_{T|t}(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathcal{D}_{t})
.

•

Apply the Stein correction on
\mathcal{D}_{T}
, which results in an updated distribution
q^{\epsilon}_{T|t}(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathcal{D}_{t})

•

Apply the noising kernel
p_{t|T}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{T})=\mathcal{N}(\eta(t)\mathbf{x}_{T},\gamma^{2}(t)I)
to map the Stein-corrected particles on
\mathcal{M}_{T}
forward to
\mathcal{M}_{t}
.

At the second step, the Stein correction applies a particle-based transform on the particle set
\mathcal{D}_{T}
and follows the KSD’s direction, given as:

\displaystyle\phi^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}\sim q_{T|t}(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathcal{D}_{t})}[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}}\log p(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}|\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t})k(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T},\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T})+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}}k(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T},\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T})|\mathcal{D}_{t}]

Our Stein correction only requires the data score
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}}\log p(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}|\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t})
, which is analytically computed:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}}\log p(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}|\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}}\log p(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t}|\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T})+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}}\log p(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T})

\displaystyle=-\eta(t)\frac{\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t}-\eta(t)\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}}{\gamma^{2}(t)}+\mathbf{s}_{\theta}\left(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}\right)

\displaystyle=-\eta(t)\frac{z}{\gamma(t)}+\mathbf{s}_{\theta}\left(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}\right)

\displaystyle=-\eta(t)\frac{z}{\gamma(t)}+\mathbf{s}_{\theta}\left(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}\right)

\displaystyle=-\eta(t)\mathbf{s}_{\theta}\left(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t}\right)+\mathbf{s}_{\theta}\left(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}\right)

The KSD’s direction can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\phi^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}\sim q_{T|t}(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathcal{D}_{t})}[(-\eta(t)\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t})+\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}))k(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T},\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T})+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}}k(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T},\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T})|\mathcal{D}_{t}]

(17)

The particle update is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T}=\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T}+\epsilon(t)*\phi^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T})

The low density reward guidance term
(
II
): We use the Stein-corrected particle-based proposal distribution
q^{\epsilon}_{T|t}(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathcal{D}_{t})
to guide the corresponding particles on
\mathcal{M}_{t}
toward low-density regions with high rewards or desired properties:

\displaystyle-\alpha(t)\mathbf{s}_{\theta}\left(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{t}\right)+\beta(t)\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{i}_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T}\sim q^{\epsilon}_{T|t}(\mathbf{x}_{T}|\mathcal{D}_{t})}\left[r(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T})|\mathbf{x}^{i}_{t}\right]

(18)

where the noisy sample
\mathbf{x}^{i}_{t}
on
\mathcal{M}_{t}
corresponds to its predicted clean version
\mathbf{x}^{i}_{T}
on
\mathcal{M}_{T}
via Tweedie’s formula.

From Equations
17
and
18
, we conclude the proof.

Appendix C
Experimental details

C.1
Metric value normalization

For ease of training and evaluation, we normalize the synthetic accessibility score (
SA
) and the docking score (
DS
) between
0
and
1
as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\textbf{SA}}=\frac{10-\textbf{SA}}{9}\quad

\displaystyle\hat{\textbf{DS}}=\frac{\textbf{DS}}{\min(\textbf{DS}_{train})-0.2}

C.2
Stepsize and annealing schedules

Stein correction stepsize

We utilize an adaptive stepsize schedule
\epsilon(t)
similar to the corrector framework from
Song et al. 2020b
, which is defined as:

\displaystyle\epsilon(t)=2\eta^{2}(t)\left(snr\|\mathbf{z}\|_{2}/\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}\right)^{2}

(19)

where
snr
denotes the signal-to-noise ratio, and
\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{s}_{\theta}\left(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{T}\right)-\eta(t)\mathbf{s}_{\theta}\left(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t}\right)
, assuming a forward noising kernel as
p_{t|T}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{T})=\mathcal{N}(\eta(t)\mathbf{x}_{T},\gamma^{2}(t)I)
.

Annealing schedules

For the low-density sampling, we experiment on two scheduling methods:
constant
and
linear
.

\displaystyle\alpha(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\alpha_{\textit{max}},&\textit{constant}\\
t\times\alpha_{\textit{max}},&\textit{linear}\end{array}\right.

For the guidance strength, we adopt the Polyak stepsize method
[
12
,
37
]
:

\displaystyle\beta(t)=\beta_{\textit{max}}\times\frac{\|s_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\|}{\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}r(\mathbf{x}_{T})\|}

(22)

C.3
Pretrained molecular graph models

Jo et al. 2022
introduced a continuous-time score-based diffusion framework for modeling graph distributions via a system of score-based networks. Given a graph representation
\mathcal{G}=(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{E})
, where
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{E}
denote the node feature matrix and edge matrix, respectively, they define a score-based model for each node and edge component:
s_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}_{t},\mathbf{E}_{t})\approx\nabla_{\mathbf{X}_{t}}\log p(\mathbf{X}_{t},\mathbf{E}_{t})
and
s_{\phi}(\mathbf{X}_{t},\mathbf{E}_{t})\approx\nabla_{\mathbf{E}_{t}}\log p(\mathbf{X}_{t},\mathbf{E}_{t})
. Building on this framework,
Lee et al. 2023
proposed a standard classifier-based diffusion guidance method for conditional molecular sampling in out-of-distribution settings. In our experiments, we use the same pretrained model and hyperparameters as
Lee et al. 2023
.
Specifically, we model the node component using a Variance Preserving SDE (VPSDE) and the edge component using a Variance Exploding SDE (VESDE). In sampling, we adopt the predictor-corrector sampling framework, with the reverse SDE as the predictor and annealed Langevin dynamics as the corrector. As reported in
Jo et al. 2022
(Table 12), the pretrained model tends to generate molecules with very low validity when using either the predictor or corrector framework alone.

C.4
Regressor pretraining on clean data

We pre-train our molecular property predictors on clean (i.e., noise-free) molecular data of ZINC250k, where the target property is defined as the product of the normalized scores:
\hat{\textbf{SA}}\times\hat{\textbf{DS}}
. Since most training molecules satisfy the
QED
hit condition, we thus ignore this target to simplify our multi-objective optimization task. Our regressor architecture is similar to the one from
Lee et al. 2023
with an additional graph convolution layer. We set the learning rate to
0.01
, the number of epochs to
10
, the AdamW optimizer
[
26
]
, and utilize the same architecture hyperparameters for all target proteins.

C.5
Hardware usage

All experiments are conducted on a single NVIDIA Titan RTX GPU with 8 CPU cores, using a Slurm-managed high-performance computing (HPC) system.

C.6.4
Controlled sampling dynamics for fa7 and jak2 proteins

We illustrate the controlled sampling dynamics on the diffusion manifold
\mathcal{M}_{T}
for fa7 in Figure
9
and jak2 in Figure
10

However, the generated molecules are often unrealistic, exhibiting low QED and SA scores. More critically, this reward overestimation can mislead the score models into a false convergence, as shown for jak2 in Figure
10
.d, where the falsely high rewards incorrectly infer that no further improvement is necessary, resulting in flattened gradients. In contrast, SDG introduces a low-cost Stein-based regularization to mitigate reward overestimation. In addition, SDG-regularized models evolve smoothly over the sampling steps, ensuring that sampling remains within generative manifolds, particularly in low-density sampling regimes.
