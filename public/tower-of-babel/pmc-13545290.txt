Age-aware multimetric MRI assessment of patellofemoral dysplasia in a symptomatic nine-year-old girl without documented dislocation: A case report
Authors: Saleck Choumad, Ahmed Ebedda, Aichetou Mohamed El Hacen, Fatma El Mabrouk, Lina Belkouchi, Nazik Allali, Latifa Chat, Siham El Haddad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Patellofemoral symptoms in children require age-aware interpretation because trochlear and patellar morphology change during growth and several commonly used magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) thresholds were derived from adults. We report a 9-year-old girl with anterior knee pain and gait disturbance, without trauma or documented patellar dislocation. MRI showed a shallow trochlear groove (trochlear depth, 1 mm) and a widened sulcus angle (164°), together with Wiberg type II patellar morphology and an Insall–Salvati ratio of 1.3. The latter 2 findings were interpreted as contributory rather than independently diagnostic because of pediatric variability. The educational value of this case lies in integrating multiple concordant measurements, explicitly acknowledging age- and technique-related limitations, and avoiding uncritical transfer of adult cutoffs to a skeletally immature patient. MRI can identify a structural predisposition before a documented dislocation and support an individualized, growth-respecting management strategy.

Introduction

Patellofemoral instability and maltracking are important causes of anterior knee symptoms in children and adolescents. Predisposition is usually multifactorial and may combine trochlear dysplasia, patella alta, lateralized extensor-mechanism alignment, patellar tilt, coronal or rotational malalignment, and insufficiency of medial soft-tissue restraints [1,2]. MRI is particularly useful in children because it evaluates cartilage-dominant morphology, alignment, the medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL), and osteochondral injury without ionizing radiation [2].

Interpretation is nevertheless more difficult in skeletally immature patients than in adults. The cartilaginous and osseous trochlea continue to develop, several measurements vary with age, and measurement reliability depends on the selected axial level and image orientation [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Consequently, an isolated adult cutoff should not be used as a binary diagnostic test in a 9-year-old child. We present a symptomatic child without a documented dislocation in whom concordant trochlear measurements demonstrated a structural predisposition to maltracking. The purpose is not to claim rarity, but to illustrate a reproducible, age-aware multimetric MRI approach and its implications for growth-respecting management.

Case presentation

A 9-year-old girl presented with anterior knee pain and gait disturbance. There was no history of trauma and no clinically documented episode of patellar dislocation. Examination showed peripatellar tenderness and functional limitation. MRI was performed to assess patellofemoral morphology and to exclude an occult osteochondral or soft-tissue injury.

Axial images showed a short, convex medial patellar facet, corresponding to Wiberg type II morphology (Fig. 1A). This configuration was recorded as a morphological feature rather than considered diagnostic of dysplasia in isolation. Trochlear morphology was more clearly abnormal. On the axial image, trochlear depth was calculated as (medial condylar height + lateral condylar height)/2 minus central sulcus height and measured approximately 1 mm (Fig. 1C). The sulcus angle was 164° (Fig. 1D), indicating marked trochlear flattening when compared with the commonly used reference cutoff of approximately 145°. Taken together, the shallow trochlear depth and widened sulcus angle provided concordant evidence of trochlear dysplasia.

Discussion

Patellofemoral stability reflects the interaction of trochlear containment, patellar height and morphology, the MPFL and retinacular restraints, the extensor-mechanism vector, and neuromuscular control. The trochlear groove contributes relatively little constraint in terminal extension but becomes increasingly important as the patella engages during flexion. A shallow groove and a widened sulcus angle therefore reduce osseous containment and can permit lateral maltracking even before a first recognized dislocation [1,2]. In the present case, the 2 concordant trochlear measurements were more persuasive than either the Wiberg type II morphology or the borderline Insall–Salvati ratio alone.

Pediatric measurement requires specific caution. Normal MRI studies demonstrate continued maturation of cartilaginous and osseous trochlear morphology through childhood, with age-related changes in trochlear depth and sulcus angle [3,4]. Other anatomic risk factors also evolve during skeletal growth [5]. In addition, several radiographic and MRI indices used for pediatric surgical decision-making show only variable interobserver reliability unless acquisition level and measurement technique are standardized [6]. MRI measurements and age-aware interpretation.

Static conventional MRI remains the clinical standard, but it captures the knee in a non-weight-bearing position and cannot directly reproduce symptomatic tracking. Dynamic or cine MRI can quantify patellar translation and tilt during motion, whereas weight-bearing MRI may better approximate functional loading. Three-dimensional segmentation and statistical shape modeling can depict the entire trochlear geometry and may improve patient-specific planning [9,10]. These techniques are promising, particularly when static findings and symptoms are discordant, but they are not yet universally available or sufficiently standardized to replace conventional MRI.

Management in a skeletally immature child should be guided by symptoms and documented instability rather than by imaging thresholds alone. With pain or suspected maltracking but no documented dislocation, the initial strategy is generally nonoperative: activity modification, short-term symptom control when needed, and supervised physiotherapy emphasizing quadriceps and hip strength, flexibility, and movement control. Clinical follow-up is required to document recurrent subluxation or dislocation. Persistent or recurrent instability warrants pediatric orthopedic assessment of the complete risk profile, including coronal and rotational alignment. When surgery is necessary, physeal-sparing soft-tissue stabilization is favored while physes remain open; tibial tubercle osteotomy and conventional trochleoplasty are generally deferred until appropriate skeletal maturity because of growth-plate risk [11,12].

This report has limitations. It describes 1 patient, lacks dynamic imaging and longitudinal outcome data, and does not include every alignment metric, such as TT–TG, TT–PCL, or rotational profile. It therefore cannot establish a pediatric threshold or a causal relationship between morphology and symptoms. Its contribution is narrower but clinically relevant: it demonstrates how a marked trochlear abnormality may be recognized in a symptomatic 9-year-old before documented dislocation, while showing why borderline patellar-height and patellar-shape findings should not be overcalled.

Conclusion

MRI can identify a structural predisposition to patellofemoral maltracking in symptomatic children even without a documented dislocation. In this 9-year-old patient, a trochlear depth of 1 mm and a sulcus angle of 164° were concordant markers of trochlear flattening, whereas the Insall–Salvati ratio of 1.3 and Wiberg type II morphology were supportive but not independently diagnostic. Pediatric interpretation should be multimetric, age-aware, and linked to clinical instability and skeletal maturity. Advanced dynamic and 3D methods may refine selected cases, but conventional MRI remains the practical foundation for diagnosis and growth-respecting management planning.
