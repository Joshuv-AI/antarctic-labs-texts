Optimal FOPID controller design for an AVR system using a Chaotic-Enhanced Phototropic Growth Algorithm (C-PGA)
Authors: Pengkai Wen, Xiangyang Liang, Fei Dai, Shaoni Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To address the high-dimensional, non-convex optimization challenge of tuning fractional-order PID (FOPID) controllers for Automatic Voltage Regulator (AVR) systems, we propose a Chaotic-Enhanced Phototropic Growth Algorithm (C-PGA). The standard Phototropic Growth Algorithm (PGA) suffers from premature convergence due to insufficient population diversity; thus, we integrate a Logistic chaotic map for optimized population initialization and dynamic perturbations during stagnation, enhancing global exploration and local exploitation. A weighted multi-objective function incorporating integral time absolute error (ITAE), overshoot, and steady-state error guides the optimization process. MATLAB/Simulink simulations demonstrate that the C-PGA-tuned FOPID controller achieves near-zero overshoot, improved transient responses, satisfactory stability under ± 50% parameter perturbations, and effective disturbance rejection. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests confirm its competitive performance compared to classical (PSO, GA) and modern (RIME, DBO) algorithms, indicating its potential as an effective solution for AVR voltage regulation in a simplified linear benchmark.

Introduction

The quality of electrical energy is a core indicator for modern power systems to meet user demands, with voltage and frequency stability being the key parameters. As a critical component in power generation systems, the stability of the synchronous generator (SG) terminal voltage is vital for the stable operation of the entire grid. The Automatic Voltage Regulator (AVR) system, which controls the excitation voltage of the generator, is the primary unit responsible for maintaining terminal voltage stability1,2. A well-performing AVR system not only ensures voltage stability but also significantly enhances the system’s dynamic response characteristics, mitigates voltage fluctuations caused by load variations, and thus reduces line losses, improving supply reliability.

Despite the advancement of modern control theories, the traditional Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) controller remains dominant in industrial AVR systems due to its simple structure, ease of implementation, and high reliability3. However, traditional PID controllers often lack robustness when dealing with the inherent nonlinearity, time-variability, and various external disturbances in AVR systems. These challenges are becoming more pronounced in modern power systems, including smart grids and high-proportion renewable energy integration4,5. Notably, chaotic optimization algorithms have been proven effective in addressing such complex control problems by enhancing search diversity, making them a promising direction for AVR system improvement6.

The development of fractional calculus theory provides new degrees of freedom for controller design. The fractional-order PID (FOPID) controller, as an extension of the traditional PID, introduces two additional adjustable parameters, the integral order λ and derivative order μ7,8, resulting in five tunable parameters (Kp, Ki, Kd, λ, μ). This enhances the flexibility of frequency-domain response design and offers stronger disturbance rejection and robustness compared to conventional integer-order PID controllers9,10. However, this increases the complexity of parameter tuning, rendering traditional heuristic methods insufficient for optimal control. Recent studies have highlighted the need for more efficient meta-heuristic algorithms to tackle FOPID parameter optimization in AVR systems, as existing solutions often fail to balance convergence speed and robustness11.

Recently, meta-heuristic optimization algorithms have been widely applied to the FOPID parameter tuning problem in AVR systems. Various single-mechanism algorithms have been successfully reported, including Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)12, Genetic Algorithm (GA)13, Artificial Bee Colony (ABC)14, Grey Wolf Optimization (GWO)15, Firefly Algorithm (FA)16, and Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA)17.

To overcome the limitations of single algorithms, such as the tendency to become trapped in local optima and inadequate convergence precision in complex high-dimensional search spaces, recent research has shifted toward hybrid meta-heuristic algorithms. These algorithms combine the advantages of different mechanisms to enhance optimization performance. For example, the hybrid BWOA algorithm, which integrates the Black Hawk Algorithm (BKA) and WOA, has been successfully applied to the FOPID controller design in AVR systems, significantly improving robustness18. The PSO-GWO hybrid algorithm, which improves the global search ability of PSO using the hierarchical mechanism of GWO, effectively addresses transient response optimization in AVR systems19. Additionally, the Harris Hawk Optimization (HHO)-GWO hybrid strategy has been developed to improve convergence speed20, while the Sparrow Search Algorithm (SSA) has shown better dynamic performance in handling nonlinear load disturbances21. Moreover, new efficient algorithms based on physical phenomena, such as the RIME algorithm proposed by Su et al.22, which simulates the soft-hard growth process of hoarfrost in nature, offer a competitive single-algorithm solution for solving complex AVR parameter optimization problems.

However, while hybrid and novel physical algorithms have improved optimization performance to some extent, their structural complexity and high computational costs remain concerns. More importantly, most existing methods (including the aforementioned new algorithms) still rely on random initialization of populations, which fails to fundamentally address the sensitivity to initial conditions23.

Another crucial aspect of the optimization process that warrants particular attention is the selection of the objective function. Existing algorithms introduce various objective functions, typically considering time-domain indicators (such as rise time, settling time, overshoot, and steady-state error) and frequency-domain parameters (such as gain margin and phase margin). One of the most commonly used error-based criteria is the Integral Absolute Error (IAE) or Integral Time Absolute Error (ITAE)24. Another approach is to construct a composite function that minimizes specific time-domain parameters (such as overshoot and settling time). However, one of the most promising strategies in defining the objective function is combining error-integral-based functions with key time-domain parameters4,25. Specifically, this study proposes a weighted objective function that incorporates ITAE, overshoot, settling time, and steady-state error, aiming to comprehensively enhance both the system’s dynamic performance and steady-state accuracy. This multi-dimensional evaluation metric has been shown to effectively prevent excessive control energy in the controller20.

To address these challenges, this paper introduces a Chaotic-Enhanced Phototropism Growth Algorithm (C-PGA) for optimizing the FOPID controller design in AVR systems. The Phototropism Growth Algorithm (PGA), a novel intelligent algorithm inspired by the phototropic growth behavior of plants, has recently been proposed26. To further enhance its performance, we integrate a Logistic chaotic map27, leveraging the ergodicity and aperiodicity of the chaotic sequence to generate high-quality initial populations and apply perturbations during iterations, significantly enhancing the algorithm’s global search ability and preventing premature convergence.

AVR system modeling

The Automatic Voltage Regulator (AVR) is a core component of the synchronous generator excitation system, and its main task is to maintain the generator terminal voltage stable at a given reference value during operation1,2. Influenced by various factors such as load disturbances, grid harmonics, and system nonlinearity, the generator terminal voltage is prone to deviation or oscillation. The AVR compensates for these disturbances by adjusting the excitation voltage, thus ensuring power quality and system stability33.

A typical AVR system consists of five main modules: the controller (PID/FOPID), amplifier, exciter, generator, and sensor. The terminal voltage is measured by the sensor and rectified, then compared with the reference voltage to form an error signal. This signal is processed by the controller to generate a control signal, which is then amplified and applied to the exciter, ultimately adjusting the generator’s magnetic field strength2. Discussion on model linearity and validity

It should be noted that the linearized first-order models adopted in this study, while widely used in benchmark AVR studies , represent a simplification of the actual physical components. Real-world AVR systems exhibit nonlinear characteristics such as saturation, dead zones, and hysteresis, particularly in the exciter and generator. Nevertheless, these linearized models effectively capture the dominant dynamic behaviors of the AVR system and provide a valid foundation for controller design and comparative evaluation. The proposed C-PGA-FOPID framework is inherently extendable to nonlinear models, which will be explored in our future work involving hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) validation.

Theoretical foundations of fractional-order PID control

Fractional calculus and mathematical modeling of the FOPID controller

Owing to its simple structure and clear physical interpretation, the conventional integer-order PID controller has long served as a standard solution in industrial control. Its canonical transfer function, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{\textrm{PID}}(s)=K_p + K_i s^{-1} + K_d s^{1}$$\end{document}, relies on fixed integer-order integral and derivative operators. However, this rigid structural assumption limits its capability to represent complex dynamic systems, particularly AVR plants exhibiting nonlinearity and time-varying characteristics.

The fractional-order PID (FOPID) controller constitutes a natural generalization of the conventional PID within the theoretical framework of fractional calculus. Under the Riemann–Liouville definition, the fractional-order operator \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${}_aD_t^\alpha$$\end{document} is expressed as7:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} _aD_t^\alpha f(t) = \frac{1}{\Gamma (n-\alpha )} \frac{d^n}{dt^n} \int _a^t \frac{f(\tau )}{(t-\tau )^{\alpha -n+1}} d\tau \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where α is an arbitrary real order satisfying n-1<α <n, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Gamma (\cdot )$$\end{document} denotes the Gamma function. Unlike the local nature of integer-order differentiation, the integral term in (7) indicates that fractional-order differentiation is inherently non-local and exhibits a memory effect: the current output depends not only on the present input but also on the entire historical evolution.

On this basis, Podlubny proposed the generalized \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$PI^\lambda D^\mu$$\end{document} controller structure. Its transfer function in the Laplace domain is defined as:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} C_{\textrm{FOPID}}(s) = K_p + \frac{K_i}{s^\lambda } + K_d s^\mu , \quad (\lambda , \mu > 0) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_p$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_i$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_d$$\end{document} are the proportional, integral, and derivative gains, respectively, while λ and μ denote the non-integer orders of integration and differentiation. This formulation introduces two additional real-valued degrees of freedom, enabling the controller to approximate the true dynamic requirements of the plant in a higher-dimensional parameter space.

Key performance advantages: from frequency shaping to robust control

Benefiting from the expanded parameter space, the FOPID controller exhibits three major advantages over conventional controllers in AVR applications, which also constitute the theoretical motivation for adopting C-PGA-based parameter optimization in this study8,34:

1. Flexible frequency-domain sculpting In frequency-domain design, the controller essentially shapes the Bode plot of the open-loop transfer function L(s)=C(s)G(s). For a conventional PID controller, the integral term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s^{-1}$$\end{document} and derivative term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s^{1}$$\end{document} contribute fixed phase shifts of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-90^\circ$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$+90^\circ$$\end{document}, respectively. In contrast, the fractional-order operators \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s^{-\lambda }$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s^\mu$$\end{document} yield phase contributions of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-\lambda \frac{\pi }{2}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu \frac{\pi }{2}$$\end{document}. Consequently, the controller phase can be adjusted continuously and precisely within \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(-\pi ,\pi )$$\end{document}, enabling accurate phase shaping to meet a desired gain crossover frequency \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega _c$$\end{document} and a specified phase margin \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi _m$$\end{document}.

2. Iso-damping property This is arguably the most prominent advantage of FOPID control in AVR systems. In practice, the AVR plant often experiences significant variations in generator gain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_G$$\end{document} due to load fluctuations. With conventional PID control, gain variations typically alter the phase margin and thereby cause pronounced changes in overshoot. By exploiting additional degrees of freedom, the FOPID controller can satisfy the following “flat-phase” constraint:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \left. \frac{d \angle L(j\omega )}{d\omega } \right| _{\omega =\omega _c} = 0 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This condition enforces a locally flat open-loop phase characteristic around the crossover frequency \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega _c$$\end{document}. As a result, even when the plant gain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_G$$\end{document} drifts, the phase margin (and thus the damping ratio) remains approximately invariant. This inherent immunity to parameter perturbations makes the FOPID controller particularly suitable for operating-condition-varying power systems.

3. Decoupling between dynamic response and steady-state accuracy In integer-order PID design, a fundamental trade-off often exists between response rapidity and overshoot suppression. Enhancing speed typically comes at the cost of increased overshoot. As reported by Tang et al.35, the five-dimensional search space of FOPID enables optimization algorithms to decouple these objectives in a higher-dimensional setting. Consequently, an optimized FOPID controller can markedly suppress voltage overshoot (or even achieve zero overshoot) while maintaining short rise and settling times. This performance improvement, however, comes at the expense of increased tuning complexity; classical tuning rules (e.g., Ziegler–Nichols) become ineffective, which motivates the C-PGA-based intelligent optimization strategy adopted in this work.

Engineering realization of fractional-order operators

The mathematical foundations of the fractional-order operator \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s^\alpha$$\end{document} (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha \in \mathbb {R}$$\end{document}) arise from definitions such as Grünwald–Letnikov, Riemann–Liouville, and Caputo. Direct numerical implementation of these definitions is often computationally intensive, potentially ill-conditioned, and difficult to match the desired frequency response. Therefore, frequency-domain approximation methods are commonly employed in control engineering to realize implementable fractional-order operators.

The Oustaloup Recursive Approximation (ORA) is one of the most widely used approaches in practice28. Over a prescribed frequency band \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[\omega _b,\omega _h]$$\end{document}, ORA approximates the fractional differentiation/integration operator using a set of pole–zero pairs:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} s^\alpha \approx K \prod _{k=-N}^{N} \frac{s+\omega _{z,k}}{s+\omega _{p,k}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where the distributions of zeros and poles are given by:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \omega _{z,k} = \omega _b \left( \frac{\omega _h}{\omega _b} \right) ^{\frac{k+N+0.5(1+\alpha )}{2N+1}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \omega _{p,k} = \omega _b \left( \frac{\omega _h}{\omega _b} \right) ^{\frac{k+N+0.5(1-\alpha )}{2N+1}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, N denotes the approximation order; in many AVR applications, N=5 typically provides sufficient accuracy within the relevant frequency band. C-PGA optimization algorithm

Principle of the standard phototropic growth algorithm (Standard PGA)

The Phototropic Growth Algorithm (PGA) is a recently developed biomimetic meta-heuristic proposed by Bohat et al. in 202526. PGA is inspired by Phototropic —a physiological phenomenon in which plants bend toward unilateral light by regulating the spatial distribution of auxin.

In the mathematical abstraction of PGA, the search space of the optimization problem is interpreted as the plant growth environment, and candidate solutions are treated as plant growth nodes. Optimization is achieved by emulating two key growth stages of plants, as detailed below.

Biological inspiration and mapping of light intensity

In nature, the growth direction of plant shoots is guided by light intensity. Accordingly, PGA maps the objective (fitness) value to the light intensity (LI) in a virtual environment. For a minimization problem, the light intensity of the i-th individual is defined as an inverse function of its fitness: 14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} LI_i = \frac{1}{1 + J(X_i)} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J(X_i)$$\end{document} denotes the fitness value of the i-th individual. A higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$LI_i$$\end{document} indicates that the corresponding position is closer to the global optimum (light source), and thus the plant is more likely to grow toward that region.

Global trunk growth mechanism (Exploration)

The trunk represents the plant’s primary growth path, enabling rapid movement toward regions of high light intensity and, therefore, corresponding to the algorithm’s global exploration capability. In this stage, the individual with the highest light intensity (i.e., best fitness) is regarded as the “light source” that guides growth. The trunk update is given by: 15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} X_{new}^{trunk} = X_{old} + \text {Gap} \cdot (X_{best} - X_{old}) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{best}$$\end{document} is the current global best solution and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {Gap}$$\end{document} is a dynamic growth step size that decays with the iteration index, mimicking the reduction in growth rate as the plant becomes taller.

Local branching and leaf growth mechanism (Exploitation)

To alleviate premature convergence caused by a single growth trajectory, PGA further simulates the growth of lateral branches and leaves, which corresponds to local exploitation. Under local light conditions, lateral branches are generated around the trunk in a stochastic manner. The branch position update is typically modeled as a random perturbation around the current location:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} X_{new}^{branch} = X_{old} + \text {rand}() \cdot (X_{best} - X_{old}) + \epsilon \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where ε denotes a small random growth noise.

Although the standard PGA incorporates the above biomimetic mechanisms, it still exhibits structural limitations when solving high-dimensional complex problems, which are analyzed in the next subsection.

Motivation for algorithmic enhancement: from phototropic traps to chaotic ergodicity

Despite the potential demonstrated by the standard PGA26 in conventional numerical optimization tasks through its Phototropic -inspired trunk growth mechanism, its limitations become increasingly pronounced when addressing high-dimensional, non-convex, and multi-modal engineering problems such as FOPID parameter tuning for the AVR system. In this study, these limitations are summarized as two fundamental bottlenecks: rigid trunk trajectories and stagnant branch growth. A chaos-theory-driven enhancement strategy is then developed accordingly.

Phototropic traps and rigid trunk trajectories

The driving force of PGA primarily stems from the trunk’s linear attraction toward the current global best solution (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{best}$$\end{document}). Under the biological metaphor, this resembles the “etiolation” phenomenon under a single dominant light source. From an optimization perspective, the linear interpolation update essentially constitutes a deterministic greedy search.

Because PGA heavily relies on the current best solution to guide the growth direction, the evolutionary trajectory lacks sufficient lateral freedom. When the fitness landscape contains numerous deceptive local optima, once \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{best}$$\end{document} is trapped within a suboptimal basin of attraction, the trunk trajectories of the entire population can be rapidly locked. This rigidity prevents the population from bypassing barriers such as saddle points, forming a so-called phototropic trap and causing an irreversible loss of global exploration capability, ultimately leading to premature convergence.

Limitations of Gaussian perturbations and branch stagnation

Beyond the trunk mechanism, the strategy used by PGA to update inferior individuals in the shadow zone is also challenged by complex landscapes. The original algorithm primarily employs Gaussian perturbations to drive branch update and mutation. However, the Gaussian distribution is statistically characterized by strong concentration near the mean and exponentially decaying tails (i.e., a light-tailed distribution).

As a result, branch mutations are largely confined to a small neighborhood of the current position, making it difficult to produce long-distance jumps. For FOPID parameter tuning problems with deep valleys and high energy barriers, such weak local Gaussian jitters are often insufficient to escape local attractors. Consequently, many inferior individuals may remain stagnant for long periods, wasting computational resources and failing to effectively support exploitation.

Reconstruction strategy based on chaotic ergodicity

To overcome these structural deficiencies, C-PGA introduces chaos theory from nonlinear dynamics to reconstruct the growth mechanisms. First, to mitigate trunk rigidity, a Logistic chaotic map27 is incorporated to emulate environmental wind-induced disturbances. Leveraging the ergodicity and intrinsic pseudo-randomness of chaotic sequences, the trunk is forced to exhibit nonlinear lateral oscillations while maintaining an overall phototropic tendency, thereby enabling dynamic avoidance of saddle points during convergence.

Second, to address branch stagnation, C-PGA designs a chaotic re-germination mechanism based on the global traversal property of chaotic maps. When an individual is detected to be stagnant, chaotic variables provide large-amplitude and unpredictable jump energy, enabling the individual to escape the local basin of attraction and re-seed in unexplored regions. This mechanism converts passive “withering” into an active mathematical reset, realizing an adaptive switching from local exploitation to global exploration.

Core implementation mechanisms of C-PGA

To overcome the aforementioned limitations, the proposed C-PGA introduces substantial modifications in four key procedures: initialization, partitioning, growth updating, and resetting.

Chaotic initialization mechanism

The quality of the initial population distribution is a primary determinant of both convergence speed and global search capability in meta-heuristic optimization. Conventional pseudo-random number generators (PRNGs), typically based on linear congruential methods, may induce a clustering effect in high-dimensional spaces, causing the search to be biased toward certain regions and increasing the likelihood of early trapping. To alleviate this issue, C-PGA employs a Logistic chaotic map to generate the initial population.

The Logistic map is a representative nonlinear dynamical system defined by:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} z_{k+1} = \mu \cdot z_k \cdot (1 - z_k), \quad k=1, 2, \dots , N \times D \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z_k \in (0,1)$$\end{document} is the chaotic variable at iteration k, and μ is the bifurcation parameter. It is well established that μ =4 yields a fully chaotic regime, in which the generated sequence exhibits ergodicity, aperiodicity, and extreme sensitivity to initial conditions. Such ergodicity ensures that the chaotic variable can visit any state in [0, 1] with approximately uniform probability, thereby promoting thorough coverage of the search space.

Mathematical modeling of the FOPID parameter optimization problem

To fully exploit the search capability of the proposed C-PGA, an accurate mathematical formulation is required to describe the FOPID tuning problem for the AVR system. This formulation encompasses the controller realization method, the definition of decision variables and search-space constraints, and the construction of a performance-driven objective function.

Frequency-domain realization via rational approximation of fractional-order operators

The fractional-order PID (FOPID) controller introduces two non-integer orders, λ and μ, and is defined as7,36:25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} C(s) = K_p + \frac{K_i}{s^\lambda } + K_d s^\mu \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_p$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_i$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_d$$\end{document} denote the proportional, integral, and derivative gains, respectively, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda ,\mu \in (0,2)$$\end{document} represent the orders of integration and differentiation9,10.

Since \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s^\alpha$$\end{document} is an irrational function, it cannot be implemented directly in numerical simulation and optimization. In this study, the Oustaloup Recursive Approximation (ORA) is adopted to obtain a rational approximation of the fractional-order operator in the frequency domain28. Over a prescribed frequency band \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[\omega _b,\omega _h]$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s^\alpha$$\end{document} can be approximated by a high-order integer transfer function:26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} s^\alpha \approx K \prod _{k=-N}^{N} \frac{s + \omega _{z,k}}{s + \omega _{p,k}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where N denotes the approximation order (this work uses N=5 to balance accuracy and computational cost35), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K=\omega _h^\alpha$$\end{document} is the gain factor. The zeros \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega _{z,k}$$\end{document} and poles \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega _{p,k}$$\end{document} are determined by the following recursive relations8:27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \omega _{z,k} = \omega _b \left( \frac{\omega _h}{\omega _b} \right) ^{\frac{k + N + 0.5(1 + \alpha )}{2N + 1}}, \quad \omega _{p,k} = \omega _b \left( \frac{\omega _h}{\omega _b} \right) ^{\frac{k + N + 0.5(1 - \alpha )}{2N + 1}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This approximation enables the FOPID controller to be embedded into an integer-order Simulink environment, thereby providing the computational basis for iterative optimization using C-PGA.

Simulation results and performance analysis

To comprehensively validate the effectiveness and superiority of the proposed Chaotic-Enhanced Phototropic Growth Algorithm (C-PGA) for tuning the FOPID controller of the AVR system, extensive multi-dimensional and multi-scenario simulations were conducted on the MATLAB/Simulink platform. The experimental protocol follows a progressive logic: algorithmic capability verification → nominal control performance → robustness and sensitivity analysis → disturbance-rejection evaluation.

To mitigate the intrinsic randomness of meta-heuristic optimizers and to ensure statistical significance, all optimization algorithms were independently executed 30 times under identical hardware conditions and identical parameter bounds. In addition to classical benchmark algorithms (PSO, GA, ABC, and PGA), two recently proposed state-of-the-art physics/biology-inspired optimizers—the Dung Beetle Optimizer (DBO, 2022) and the Rime Optimization Algorithm (RIME, 2023)—were included as strong baselines to reflect the competitiveness of this work. For fairness, all compared algorithms (RIME, DBO, PSO, etc.) used the same general settings, such as population size N and maximum iterations \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{max}$$\end{document}, while algorithm-specific control parameters (e.g., the learning factors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_2$$\end{document} in PSO) strictly followed the default values recommended in the corresponding original references.This practice avoids the introduction of experimenter bias that may arise from manual per-algorithm parameter tuning, and reflects the performance that each algorithm can deliver under its originally proposed configuration.

The key parameter settings of C-PGA are summarized in Table 4. The population size and maximum iterations were set to N=40 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{max}=80$$\end{document}, respectively. The objective function is the weighted composite performance index defined in (30), which aims to balance dynamic response speed, steady-state accuracy, and control energy. These values were not selected arbitrarily but were informed by a preliminary sensitivity analysis. Specifically, when N<30, the population diversity becomes insufficient and the algorithm is prone to local entrapment (i.e., the “phototropic trap”); when N>50, the solution accuracy improves only marginally, whereas the computational burden increases approximately linearly, degrading the performance-to-cost ratio. Therefore, N=40 was chosen as a practical compromise between global exploration and computational efficiency. Ablation study on chaotic enhancement mechanisms

To quantify the individual contribution of each chaotic enhancement mechanism, an ablation study was conducted. Parameter sensitivity and robustness tests

In practical operation, the AVR system faces two major sources of uncertainty: (i) controller parameters may drift due to component aging or discretization errors, and (ii) plant parameters (e.g., generator dynamics) vary with operating conditions such as load. This subsection evaluates robustness from both perspectives.

Conclusions and future work

Conclusions

To address the challenging tuning problem of the fractional-order PID (FOPID) controller for the Automatic Voltage Regulator (AVR) system, this paper proposed a chaos-enhanced Phototropic Growth Algorithm (C-PGA). Through systematic theoretical development and comprehensive simulation studies, the proposed approach was shown to be effective in improving the transient performance, steady-state accuracy, and robustness of the linearized AVR system under the considered operating conditions.

First, from the perspective of algorithmic design and search behavior, C-PGA integrates Logistic-chaos-based initialization, dynamic chaotic perturbations, and a stagnation-triggered branch-withering (reset) strategy. These mechanisms jointly mitigate the susceptibility of standard PGA to premature convergence and loss of population diversity. Convergence analyses indicate that, within the scope of the simplified linear AVR model, C-PGA accelerates the search process while maintaining high solution accuracy. Compared with PGA, PSO, GA, and ABC, C-PGA typically enters the near-optimal region within approximately ten iterations, highlighting its strong capability and computational efficiency for high-dimensional, non-convex optimization problems.

Second, under nominal conditions, the FOPID controller tuned by C-PGA exhibits superior time-domain performance in a simulation environment. The closed-loop system achieves a fast response with virtually zero overshoot (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$OS \approx 0\%$$\end{document}), a reduced settling time of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_s \approx 0.52$$\end{document} s, and a near-zero steady-state error (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_{ss} \approx 0$$\end{document}). Relative to conventional integer-order PID controllers and FOPID controllers optimized by other intelligent algorithms, the proposed C-PGA-FOPID scheme attains the best comprehensive indices, including the integral time absolute error (ITAE), demonstrating a favorable trade-off between transient behavior and steady-state precision.

Finally, robustness evaluations performed on the linearized model further confirm the strong desensitization capability of the proposed controller against simulated plant-parameter perturbations. The results indicate that, even when the time constants of the amplifier, exciter, generator, and sensor vary substantially by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pm 50\%$$\end{document}, the closed-loop system is expected to remain stable with only minor performance degradation. Moreover, under challenging disturbance conditions (control signal perturbations and load disturbances), the C-PGA-FOPID controller exhibits the smallest voltage deviations and the fastest recovery time, further validating its superior disturbance rejection capability in the simulated scenarios.

However, these findings are based on a linearized system model that does not fully capture practical nonlinearities, such as actuator saturation, dead zones, and hysteresis. A comprehensive validation incorporating these physical constraints in a hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) testbed is a necessary next step to confirm these promising results for practical engineering applications.

In summary, the C-PGA-FOPID scheme represents a promising solution for high-performance voltage regulation in AVR systems, subject to further validation under real-world nonlinear and dynamic operating conditions.

Limitations and future work

Despite the promising results obtained for AVR controller optimization, several limitations remain and motivate future research to extend both the theoretical depth and practical scope of this work.

Incorporation of practical nonlinearities and HIL validation

The present study is primarily based on a linearized AVR model, which does not fully capture nonlinear characteristics commonly encountered in real-world excitation systems—such as saturation, dead-zone, and hysteresis. While the control signal saturation analysis in Section Sensitivity analysis of objective function weights confirms that the designed controller operates well within typical actuator limits, a comprehensive validation under a complete set of physical constraints is still necessary.

Accordingly, immediate future work will focus on implementing the C-PGA–FOPID controller in a hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) testbed. However, it should be noted that constructing a full physical HIL platform with a real synchronous generator and exciter is economically prohibitive for most academic laboratories, as such equipment involves significant procurement, maintenance, and safety costs. Consequently, a software-based HIL (real-time digital simulation) approach will be adopted. The controller will be deployed on an embedded platform (e.g., DSP or FPGA), while a detailed nonlinear exciter model incorporating saturation, dead-zone, and hysteresis will run in real time on a digital simulator such as OPAL-RT or RTDS. This emulated HIL configuration can replicate the critical dynamic behaviors of a physical generator while circumventing the hardware constraints. It will enable rigorous evaluation of the controller’s real-time computational efficiency, robustness under actuator constraints, and transient performance during grid faults (e.g., three-phase short circuits and islanding events).

Multi-objective and system-level extensions

In addition to hardware validation, the current optimization objective mainly emphasizes control-performance indices. A multi-objective optimization formulation (e.g., an MO-PGA framework) will be investigated to jointly pursue fast regulation, reduced control energy consumption, and enhanced robustness, thereby providing Pareto-optimal solution sets to support engineering decision-making.

Furthermore, with the increasing penetration of renewable energy, regulating a single AVR may be insufficient to maintain system-wide stability. Therefore, future studies will explore extending C-PGA to the coordinated optimization of AVR with power system stabilizers (PSS) and/or flexible AC transmission systems (FACTS). The proposed framework will also be extended to multi-machine power systems, where coordinated control is essential for damping inter-area oscillations.

Integration with modern grid technologies

The growing share of inverter-based resources (IBRs) introduces new voltage stability challenges. Integrating the C-PGA–FOPID controller with grid-forming converter control strategies represents another promising direction to enhance system-wide voltage support. Finally, data-driven adaptation mechanisms could be incorporated to enable online tuning of the FOPID parameters in response to changing grid conditions, paving the way for intelligent, self-optimizing voltage regulation in future smart grids.
