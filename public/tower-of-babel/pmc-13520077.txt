Enrichment containing myo-inositol in alginate hydrogels with maternal pheromone effects on gestating sow behavior, performance, and physiology
Authors: Lucas Caua Spetic Da Selva, Kiran Niure, Heidi Villalba, John J McGlone, Emerald Julianna Salinas, Allen Jimena Martinez Aguiriano, Brooke Kitting, Faby Hernandez, Marcos David Tovar Alvarado, Gizem Levent, Arlene Garcia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyo-inositol (MYO) in alginate hydrogel beads (AHBs) with maternal pheromone (MP) via enrichment was evaluated using measures of behavior, physiology, and farrowing performance of pregnant sows. Sows (N = 36) were randomly assigned into: a negative control group, without anything (NEG-CON), a positive control with AHBs-MP without MYO via enrichment (POS-AHB), and a treatment group provided AHB-MYO-MP via enrichment (TRT-AHB+). On Day 1 and 8 the POS-AHB group consumed more AHBs than the TRT-AHB+ (p < 0.01). Hematological data showed higher lymphocyte counts in NEG-CON than TRT-AHB+ on Day 8 (p < 0.05) but did not differ from POS-AHB. Monocytes were higher for TRT-AHB+ sows at Day 1 and 8 (p = 0.04 and p = 0.003, respectively) compared to NEG-CON, whereas lymphocytes in TRT-AHB+ were higher than POS-AHB on Day 8 (p < 0.001). On Day 8, platelet distribution width was greater in POS-AHB group compared to NEG-CON (p = 0.04) but did not differ from TRT-AHB+. Total plasma protein was higher in TRT-AHB+ compared with the other groups. Additionally, AST levels were significantly higher in POS-AHB than NEG-CON (p = 0.01). Blood cortisol concentrations and farrowing performance did not differ among groups. Behavior data showed higher active behaviors in TRT-AHB+ during the first and second hour (p < 0.05). Standing and interacting behaviors in TRT-AHB+ were higher during the second hour after providing AHBs (p < 0.05) but did not differ thereafter. In conclusion, AHB-MYO-MP increased enrichment-directed behavior but did not significantly influence physiological signs of stress.Keywords: Hunger, Stress, Satiety, Myo-inositol, Environmental enrichment, Maternal pheromone

Introduction

Modern intensive pig production often relies on housing systems that restrict the expression of natural behaviors and limit opportunities for environmental enrichment, which can compromise animal welfare and increase the risk of stress related behaviors (Bolhuis et al., 2005; Salak-Johnson et al., 2007). Such interactions are subject of ongoing research and debate in the field of animal welfare, particularly concerning their potential to cause frustration and stress in pigs (Martínez-Miró et al., 2016). Additionally, commercial swine production farms implement feed limitations for pregnant sows, providing adequate nutrition to maintain health and reproductive functions while preventing obesity and its associated reproductive and health issues (Read et al., 2020; D’Eath et al., 2018). The feed provided to sows is intended to maintain sow health and reproduction, may fail to satiate them, leading to heightened feeding motivation (referred to in this work as food seeking/appetite foraging), which can result in frustration, boredom, manifestation of stereotypic behaviors, an increase in oral-nasal-facial behaviors (ONF) and welfare issues (Dailey & McGlone, 1997; D’Eath et al., 2018; Vieuille-Thomas et al., 1995).

Stereotypical behaviors are repetitive, invariant behavior patterns with no apparent goal or function and are generally considered indicators of suboptimal environments and compromised welfare (Vieuille-Thomas et al., 1995). Feed-restricted sows were previously documented to show a variety of such behaviors, such as sham chewing, biting bars, increased drinking, and increased activities (Bernardino et al., 2021; Bolhuis et al., 2005; Stolba & Wood-Gush, 1989). The development and expression of stereotypic behaviors are influenced by multiple factors, including housing conditions and feeding management. Individual gestation stalls have been linked to increased stereotypic behaviors, likely as a result of prolonged confinement and limited opportunities to perform natural behaviors (Pan et al., 2002). However, feeding management is also a key determinant of oral stereotypies and feeding-motivated behaviors. Evidence suggests that, under certain experimental conditions, feeding practices may have a stronger influence on the development of these behaviors than the housing system itself (Hulbert & McGlone, 2006). Therefore, the expression of stereotypic behaviors reflects the interaction between housing, feeding management, and other environmental factors that influence sow welfare (McGlone, 2013; Perini et al., 2021).

Under commercial feed restriction, gestating sows are typically fed approximately one-fourth of the amount they would voluntarily consume. Consequently, they consume their daily ration rapidly and may remain unsatiated, continuing to forage and display feed-motivated behaviors. These behaviors are often redirected toward available substrates within the crate, including feeders, bars, drinkers, and other equipment (Søndergaard, 2022).

Various studies have been conducted in the past to mitigate hunger among pregnant sows and most of them included a variety of dietary fibers, to mitigate stress due to feed restriction and its consequence effect on the behavior and health of sows (Huang et al., 2020; Serena et al., 2008). However, the findings of those studies are contradictory and inconclusive due to the differences in the physiochemical properties of those fiber diets; for example, McGlone and Fullwood (2001) found no meaningful change in ONF behaviors when sows were fed a high-fiber diet; however, some sows fed high-fiber diets increased some ONF behaviors. Sodium alginate, seaweed-derived soluble fiber, possesses unique rheological properties that allow it to form stable hydrogel beads capable of increasing gastric distension, prolonging satiety, and serving as a vehicle for encapsulating bioactive compounds (Da Silva et al., 2014; Jensen et al., 2012). Therefore, alginate hydrogel beads (AHBs) were selected in the present study as both an enrichment substrate and a delivery system for MYO. One of our hypotheses was that myo-inositol (MYO; an organic osmolyte) encapsulated in alginate hydrogel beads could potentially help satiate feed limited gestating sows.

MYO is a stereoisomer of inositol that plays a critical role in intracellular signaling pathways, including the phosphatidylinositol/inositol triphosphate (PI/IP3) signaling pathway. Beyond its metabolic functions, MYO participates in neurotransmitter modulation, including serotonergic signaling, and has been associated with the regulation of stress-related response and central nervous system function, providing a biological rationale for investigating its potential influence on sow behavior and welfare (Fijałek et al., 2025).

In livestock, increasing MYO availability through phytase supplementation has been associated with improved growth performance in swine and poultry (Lu et al., 2019; Walk et al., 2018). Recent studies have also demonstrated that dietary MYO supplementation during pregnancy reduces inflammation and oxidative stress in sows while improving maternal metabolic status and litter development, supporting its potential as a nutritional strategy to improve sow physiology during gestation (Sun et al., 2025). Likewise, MYO supplementation has been reported to enhance intestinal epithelial cell function and improve growth performance in waned piglets when included in the diet, suggesting beneficial effects on intestinal health during early life (Ogunribido et al., 2022). Furthermore, MYO has demonstrated neuroprotective properties in preclinical models of diabetic stroke, suggesting broader physiological effects beyond metabolism (Villalba et al., 2018).

Because pigs exhibit mild food neophobia, MYO was encapsulated within alginate hydrogel beads (AHBs) and combined with maternal pheromone (MP). The MP was exclusively used to entice the sows to consume the AHBs. Previous work in weaned piglets demonstrated that this pheromone increased feeding behavior and reduced aggression after weaning, suggesting that it may improve the acceptance of novel feed ingredients (Aviles-Rosa et al., 2020).

The objective of this study was to evaluate the effects of AHBs, with or without MYO coated with MP provided through environmental enrichment (perforated plastic jugs), on the behavior, physiology, and farrowing performance of gestation sows.

Material and methods

Ethics statement

All the procedures of this study were approved by the Texas Tech University (TTU) Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol number 2023–1349). This study was conducted in a commercial swine production facility in the United States. The farm gave permission for the investigators to conduct the work, but it wishes to remain anonymous.

Animals, diet, enrichment

A total of 36 first parity sows in their late gestation (approximately 85 to 100 days of gestation) with an average weight of 200 kg were randomized into treatment groups. This gestational stage was selected based on animal availability at the commercial production facility and because all sows were under the routine feed-restriction program during this period, allowing the enrichment treatments to be evaluated under consistent management conditions. All sows were exclusively housed in stalls under the farm’s routine management. The sows were housed individually in gestation stalls with fully slatted floors. The room temperature was maintained at 18.3 °C. Sows received their feed once a day, with the quantity tailored and dispensed based on their body condition. The sows had ad libitum access to water via nipple drinkers.

Treatment groups were the following: negative control (NEG-CON; n = 12), positive control (POS-AHB; n = 12), and treatment (TRT-AHB+; n = 12). The NEG-CON group sows had no enrichment material, no AHBs, no MYO, and no MP supplement. The POS -AHB group sows were provided with enrichment material and AHBs-MP but no MYO supplement. The TRT-AHB+ group sows were provided with enrichment material and AHBs-MYO-MP supplement. Sows rows were selected to be on opposite sides of the barn, with NEG-CON sows away from POS-AHB and TRT-AHB sows.

Sows were provided with perforated plastic 3.8 L cleaned jugs, one jug for each sow, as enrichment material for seven days. The timeline included eight days (Day 1 to Day 8). The study began on Day 1; the first day of the study (which is equivalent to Baseline) and ended on Day 8; the eighth day of the study.

Enrichment was provided from Day 1 to Day 8. The plastic jugs were hung on the bars of the crate at eye level in front of each sow with metal chains (Fig. 1). A total of 16 holes that were 2 cm in diameter were made on the jugs to facilitate the direct consumption or dropping of AHBs into the sow feeders. This method allowed consumption of AHBs and exploration and playing with the jugs. The plastic jugs were replaced if the researchers were not able to extend them to place AHBs in them. A total of four jugs were replaced in one week. The AHBs were prepared from food-grade sodium alginate powder (Modernist Pantry, LLC, Eliot, ME) according to the standard procedure for making hydrogel beads and myo-inositol (Millipore Sigma) based on the NRC guidelines for the phase of the sows. A total of 1 kg of beads was provided to POS and TRT sows daily for seven days coated with 10 mL of Maternal Pheromone (MP) at a concentration of 100µg/ml for each kg of beads. The MP was synthesized at the Texas Tech University Laboratory of Behavior, Physiology, and Welfare. The amount of AHBs consumed was estimated daily by weighing the AHBs offered, and the remaining AHBs recovered from jug. Daily AHB intake was calculated as the difference between the amount offered and the leftover amount.

Environmental enrichment provided to gestation sows. A perforated 3.8 L plastic jug containing alginate hydrogel beads (AHBs) was suspended from the front of each gestation stall using a metal chain, allowing sows to interact with the jug and consume the AHBs throughout the study.

Blood sample collection and physiological measures

Blood samples were collected twice: on Day 1 (baseline, before the provision of any treatment) and on the morning of Day 8 following completion of the treatment period. Blood sampling was performed between 07:00 and 10:00 am. Sows were restrained in their gestation stalls using a pig snare, and approximately 3–5 mL of blood was collected via jugular venipuncture. Blood collection was completed rapidly using standard handling procedures to minimize handling time and animal disturbance.

The hematological and biochemical parameters were selected to evaluate potential treatment-related changes in immune function, physiological status, metabolic activity, and stress-related response. An HM5® hematology analyzer (Zoetis, US) was used to evaluate hematological parameters, and a Vetscan® VS2 chemistry analyzer (Zoetis, US) was used to analyze blood chemistry to assess the physiological response associated with the experimental treatments. Various blood parameters like white blood cell count (WBC), lymphocyte (LYM), neutrophil (NEU), neutrophil: lymphocyte ratio (NLR), red blood cell (RBC), hemoglobin (HB), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), platelets, total protein (TP), glucose (GLU), creatinine (CRE), potassium (K+), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and cortisol were evaluated.

For blood cortisol analysis, plasma samples were preserved at −80 °C until analysis. Blood samples were spun at 7000 rpm for 10 min to obtain plasma. The samples were analyzed using cortisol Elisa kits (Arbor Assays DetectX® Cortisol Kits, Ann Arbor, MI) in accordance with the protocol provided by Arbor Assays.

Beads consumption data

Each sow in the POS-AHB and TRT-AHB+ groups was given 1 kg of AHBs-MYO or AHBs, provided over one week from Day 1 to Day 7. The daily disappearance/consumption of beads for each sow was determined by subtracting the weight of the remaining beads from the initial weight of the beads provided the previous day (1 kg). The study ended the morning of Day 8 after beads consumption was measured and blood collected for analysis.

Video recording and behavioral measures

Behavior data of the sows following the addition of AHBs-MYO or AHBs in the enrichment jugs was recorded for 24 h utilizing a network video recorder (NVR) to analyze the effect of treatments on different behaviors of the sows. The ethogram for the behaviors of interests is defined in Table 1. Behavior data were observed and recorded by three trained and validated observers, and all the observers were blind to the treatments (although the NEG CON group with no Environmental Enrichment jug could be seen).

Farrowing performance

Following completion of enrichment period, sows remained under routine commercial farm management until farrowing. Farrowing performance data were obtained from farm production records and included the number of piglets born alive, stillborn, and mummified fetus for each sow. These were used to evaluate whether the enrichment treatments administered during late gestation influenced reproductive performance.

Statistical analysis

The data for bead consumption, hematological and blood chemistry were analyzed using statistical software: RStudio (2023.06.0 + 421 version © 2009–2023 Posit Software, PBC). Results were reported as mean± standard errors of mean for normal data. Likewise, blood chemistry hematological data, and blood cortisol were analyzed using a mixed-effects linear regression model. The model included treatment, time and interaction as fixed effects, with sow ID included as a random effect. Bead consumption was analyzed using the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test to compare median intake between the POS-AHB and TRT-AHB+ groups because the data did not meet the assumptions of normality required for parametric analysis (Shapiro-Wilk test p < 0.01). Farrowing performance data were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test to compare differences among treatment groups, as the data did not meet the assumptions of residual normality. Prior to analysis, data were evaluated for normality and homogeneity of variance through visual inspection of residual diagnostic plots. Extreme outliers were identified separately for each response variable using the interquartile range (IQR) method. When identified, only the outlying observation for that specific variable was excluded from the corresponding analysis; the animal remained included in all other analysis. A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant in the study.

Results

The AHBs consumption was measured between the POS-AHB and TRT-AHB+ sows to evaluate the effect of MYO supplement provided to TRT-AHB+ sows compared to the AHBs without MYO supplement in POS-AHB sows. The median AHBs consumption for the TRT-AHB+ group and POS-AHB group was significantly different (p= 0.001). The POS-AHB group had a higher bead consumption (Median = 0.997 kg ± IQR = 0.104 kg) than the TRT-AHB+ group (Median = 0.920 kg ± IQR = 0.216 kg).

Mean NLR values increased from Day 0 to Day 7 in all groups, with the TRT group showing the highest value on Day 7 (1.15 ± 0.113), followed by POS (0.932 ± 0.061) and NEG (0.988 ± 0.119). Sows in the TRT-AHB+ group showed higher standing behavior compared to the NEG-CON and POS-AHB groups during the second hour after the AHBs were provided. There were no significant differences among the treatment groups at any other time for standing behavior (Fig. 5– Panel C).

Farrowing performance did not differ among treatment groups. The median number of piglets born alive was 16, 14.5, and 13.5 for the NEG-CON, POS-AHB, and TRT-AHB+ groups, respectively, with no significant treatment effect (Kruskal-Wallis, P = 0.205). Likewise, the median number of stillborn piglets was 1, 0, and 1 for the NEG-CON, POS-AHB, and TRT-AHB+ groups, respectively, with no significant differences among treatments (p = 0.340). Similarly, the median number of mummified fetuses was 0 for all treatment groups (p = 0.781).

Discussion

The AHBs with or without MYO were provided to TRT-AHB+ and POS-AHB sows. The result of this study shows a higher median bead consumption in POS-AHB sows than in TRT-AHB+ sows. This could be an effect of MYO supplement along with sodium alginate hydrogels on enhanced intake, taste or satiety levels. Sodium alginate hydrogels and other dietary fibers have been reported to increase satiety and decrease abnormal behaviors related to stress due to feed restriction (Sapkota et al., 2016; Da Silva et al., 2014; Jensen et al., 2012). The gelling effect of sodium alginate leading to increased gastric distension, reduced nutrient absorption rate, and colonic fermentation producing Short Chain Fatty Acid (SCFA) has been described as the main function of sodium alginate in satiety (Silva, 2013; Darzi et al., 2011). However, the decreased rate of glucose absorption following AHBs bead consumption may still provoke the sensation of hunger even if the stomach is filled with sodium alginate hydrogel. Blood glucose level is a biomarker of satiety and is considered to induce voluntary feed intake (Al-Zubaidi et al., 2019; Silva, 2013) when there is a transient decrease in blood glucose. However, the effect of reduced bead intake in TRT-HAB+ sows could be explained by their higher level of passiveness or decreased activity. This could be due to the effect of the orientation of the sows. To prevent potential cross-contamination of pheromone, the TRT-AHB+ group was intentionally housed toward the side of the barn, away from NEG-CON group. This spatial separation was necessary due to the use of maternal pheromone in the TRT-AHB+ treatment, which could have influenced behavior in nearby pens. Lighting conditions varied slight between treatment areas, with POS-AHB sows housed in a more central zone in the barn. Although this difference in lighting may have contributed to behavioral variation, the separation was essential to maintain treatment integrity. Lastly, both the TRT-AHB+ and POS-AHB beads were coated with MP, which has previous been shown to increase feed consumption in pigs (Archer et al., 2022). However, the concentration of MP may not have been high enough to mask the smell or taste of MYO. A recent study evaluating the benefits of AHBs in combination with MYO and MP on piglet weaning stress showed similar findings of a slight aversion to AHBs-MYO-MP treatment compared to the AHB-MP treatment (Spetic da Selva, 2023).

Various blood constituents and parameters change due to various external and internal factors such as changes in environment, nutrition, and energy metabolism (Tadesse et al., 2021; Štukelj et al., 2010). Such changes in the blood parameters maintain homeostasis in animals. When the changes in these blood constituents go beyond the normal range, it indicates an abnormal or pathological state. Hence, these hematological parameters are considered vital indicators of physiological, nutritional, and pathological conditions in animals (Štukelj et al., 2010).

Lymphocyte (LYM) counts showed a significant treatment × time interaction in this study and were numerically lower in the TRT-AHB+ group compared to the other treatment groups. Although LYM levels in some sows were close to the lower end of the reference interval for pregnant sows, overall values remained within reported physiological ranges (Boulbria et al., 2021; Bhattarai et al., 2019). Lymphocytes are critical immune cells, and lymphopenia has previously been linked to feed restriction and stress-induced steroid hormone release. For example, Levin et al. (1993) reported lymphopenia in rats subjected to two weeks of feed restriction, which was associated with elevated corticosteroid levels (Levin et al., 1993). In our study, cortisol levels were significantly elevated in the TRT-AHB+ group by Day 7, suggesting an increased physiological or stress-related response. This elevation in cortisol may partially explain the reduced LYM counts observed in this group. However, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), another common indicator of stress in swine (Widowski et al., 1989), did not differ significantly between groups. This mixed evidence highlights the complexity of immune and endocrine interactions and suggests that the observed lymphocyte reduction may result from multiple factors, including the potential immunomodulatory effects of MYO supplementation.

PDWc was significantly higher in POS-AHB sows compared to NEG-CON sows. PDWc, which reflects variability in platelet size, is considered a potential marker of platelet activation in other species. However, its physiological significance in sows remains unclear, and limited literature is available on this parameter in the context of swine health or stress. Future research is needed to better understand the implications of PDWc changes in pigs.

Through blood chemistry analysis, a significant effect of treatment by time interaction was observed for TP. The sows that were given the AHBs-MYO-MP supplement (TRT-AHB+) had significantly higher TP levels as compared to the NEG-CON and POS-AHB. TP has been discussed as an indicator of the variety of conditions associated with liver, kidney, and malnutrition. A high TP level is associated with dehydration (Čobanović et al., 2020; Averos et. al., 2007). This may be related to the high water-absorbing and water-retaining properties of sodium alginate (Wang et al., 2023) which may have had an influence on decreasing hydration levels. In addition, Shi et al. (2021) reported an increase in fecal water content in sows fed with a fiber diet. Similarly, the sodium content in sodium alginate can contribute to the increased osmolality of plasma (Jensen et al., 2012) and reduced water intake may lead to dehydration to some extent, thus showing increased TP in plasma in sows eating AHBs.

The effect of environmental enrichment on serum cortisol concentrations revealed that enrichment influenced the endocrine response of the sows over the study period. On Day 8, sows in the TRT-AHB+ group exhibited the highest cortisol concentrations, followed by the POS-AHB group. The significant treatment x day interaction (p = 0.0031) indicates that cortisol concentrations are different among treatments across experimental periods. However, because serum cortisol was measured only at baseline and Day 8, these results should not interpret as a comprehensive assessment of HPA axis activity or circadian cortisol regulation. Instead, they provide a snapshot of the physiological response associated with the experimental treatments.

Although cortisol is commonly used as an indicator of stress, it is important to interpret elevated cortisol levels in the context of environmental stimulation. Elevated cortisol is not always a sign of distress (Clow et al., 2009). For example, Munsterhjelm et al. (2010) reported that pigs exposed to early-life enrichment exhibited higher basal cortisol levels alongside the development of more defined circadian rhythms, which are often disrupted under chronic stress. While our study did not assess cortisol rhythms due to the use of single-point serum sampling, the elevated cortisol in TRT-AHB+ sows may reflect increased physiological arousal or engagement, rather than harmful stress.

Further support for this interpretation comes from Moyres and Hagger (2023), who found in a meta-analysis that individuals with higher levels of physical activity exhibited healthier cortisol regulation patterns. Although our study did not measure cortisol patterns across days, the increased cortisol in TRT-AHB+ sows could be a result of greater interaction and stimulation, similar to the effect that physical activity has on humans. The enrichment jugs, especially with the addition of MYO, may have triggered greater exploration and activity, thereby stimulating a non-pathological endocrine response. Sows in the POS-AHB group also showed an increase in cortisol on Day 8, although it was not pronounced as in TRT-AHB+ group. This suggests that even basic enrichment can activate the HPA axis, but the presence of MYO may have enhanced the pigs’ motivation to interact, resulting in a more robust physiological response. Future studies incorporating repeated cortisol measurements throughout the day and across multiple days would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of endocrine response to environmental enrichment. Enrichment paired with rewards likely promotes positive physiological engagement. Future studies should combine behavior and hormone data over time to better distinguish stress from stimulation.

Behavioral changes in the gestating sows in our study showed higher active behaviors and lower lying behaviors in sows fed AHBs-MYO-MP via enrichment jugs compared to sows provided AHBs-MP and sows with no provision of these things during the first hour after giving the AHBs. This finding contradicts previous reports of higher lying behaviors in sows fed with various dietary fibers that increased chewing time and decrease feeding motivation linked to increased satiety and reduced abnormal behaviors (Bernardino et al., 2021). However, in our study the sows showed higher activity and lower lying behaviors in TRT-AHB+ sows than NEG-CON and POS-AHB sows. The observed higher levels of active behaviors could be due to the effect of MYO supplement in the TRT-AHB+ sows which motivate the pigs to consume more and hence the sows lying behavior was decreased. Similarly, during the second hour after providing AHBs, the TRT-AHB+ and POS-AHB groups showed higher active behaviors than the NEG-CON group sows. This suggests that the TRT-AHB+ and POS-CON sows irrespective of the availability or unavailability of MYO supplement were more active. We can speculate that the increased activeness in these two groups of sows is due to presence of the MP which may increase feeding motivation due to the availability of the AHBs as the sows are already in a state of hunger due to limited feed provided to them, the availability of something edible could stimulate the sensory mechanism of hunger and elevate the positive feeding motivation and increase the voluntary feed intake (De Silva & Bloom, 2012; Blundell et al., 2010). The nature of active behavior was opposite to the lying behaviors and hence the similar response of the sows regarding the standing behavior, interacting behavior and other behaviors like playing with water and rubbing back against the side walls of crates are higher in POS-AHB and TRT-AHB+ sows which indicates that they performed these behaviors when they stood more to access AHBs, possibly due to increased feed motivation. motivation.

Active behaviors including standing, interacting, and other behaviors showed a decreasing trend compared to lying behaviors in POS-AHB and TRT-AHB+ in the first two hours of post-treatment. In addition to the increased food motivation in TRT-AHB+ and POS-AHB sows, the lower active behaviors after the first few hours of providing AHBs could be explained by the reason, they were more active interacting with the new object in their crate. Pigs are motivated to investigate new material in their environment during the first few hours and this interaction decreased after some time. The interactions with enrichment could have been affected by the decreased AHBs in jugs over time.

Stress during the gestating stage could be detrimental to the health of pregnant animals and their offspring. The MYO supplement used in this present study has been discussed in various studies to have a role in cellular signaling, neurotransmission, insulin and glucose mobilization and reducing oxidative stress and inflammation (Yu et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2022; Bevilacqua & Bizzarri, 2018). Thus, we hypothesized that the use of this MYO supplement could impart a calming effect and some health benefits which could result in better farrowing performances. However, farrowing performance was not affected using MYO supplement in AHBs in pregnant sows. This finding coincides with the findings of Oelke et al., 2018 and Guillemet et al., 2007, who reported no effect of dietary fibers on born alive, still born and mummified fetuses.

This study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. All treatment groups were housed in the same room under commercial farm conditions. Although the MP was used specifically to entire consumption of the AHBs, potential olfactory exposure of the NEG-CON to the maternal pheromone cannot be completely excluded. Finally, serum cortisol was measured only at baseline and Day 8, providing a limited assessment of endocrine response rather than a comprehensive evaluation of HPA axis activity. Future studies should include repeated cortisol measurements and greater spatial separation between treatment groups to further evaluate the physiological effects of enrichment.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the AHBs, particularly when combined with MYO and MP, influenced sow behavior, immune parameters, and physiological activation. Although TRT-AHB+ sows consumed fewer beads, possibly due the MYO’s sensory properties, they exhibited greater active behaviors and reduced lying, indicating increased feeding motivation and engagement. While cortisol levels were elevated in the enriched group, especially in TRT-AHB+ group these changes likely reflect more adaptative HPA axis activation rather than distress. Collectively, these findings suggest that AHB-based enrichment can improve sow welfare by promoting behavioral engagement and potentially mitigating stress associated with feed restriction. This approach specifically addresses feeding motivation and affective states, and offers a refined, welfare-enhancing strategy for managing restricted-fed sows. Further research should explore long-term effects and combine multi-timepoint behavioral and physiological data to distinguish between stress and positive arousal.
