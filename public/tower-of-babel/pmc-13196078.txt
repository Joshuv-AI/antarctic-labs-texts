Climate dominance gives way to litter chemistry during long-term Scots pine needle decomposition along a boreal-to-temperate climate gradient
Authors: Jielin Ge, Björn Berg, Lili Dong, Tao Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The decomposition of needle litter is governed by a complex interplay between climatic conditions and substrate chemical properties, yet how their relative influence shifts during decomposition remains poorly resolved. We synthesized rate-regulating factors for long-term decomposition of Scots pine (Pinus sylvestris) needle litter across a 15-site boreal-to-temperate climatic gradient (mean annual temperature − 0.7 to 6.3 °C). Annual mass loss was analyzed across four decomposition categories, alongside concurrent measurements of acid-unhydrolyzable residue (AUR) and nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and manganese in all samplings. We found that mean annual mass loss declined significantly with increasing decomposition stage, from 30.2% in the early stage to 15.8% in the late stage. Climate, particularly actual evapotranspiration, exerted a strong positive effect during early decomposition but weakened progressively and became negligible in the late stage. In contrast, the influence of litter chemistry increased with decomposition, with stage-specific effects of AUR and nutrients reflecting shifts in substrate quality and availability. The total explanatory variance of measured variables declined from ~ 75% in early stages to ~ 39% in advanced decomposition, indicating increasing complexity and reduced predictability. The relative importance of controlling factors shifted from climate dominance to substrate regulation, with different elements contributing variably across stages. These results provide empirical evidence for stage-dependent controls on litter decomposition and help reconcile contrasting views on the roles of climate and litter chemical traits. Incorporating such temporally explicit dynamics into biogeochemical models will improve predictions of litter-driven carbon and nutrient cycling. Future integration of microbial and enzymatic processes will be essential to better resolve the mechanisms underlying these dynamics and to enhance model realism under changing environmental conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08711-7.

Introduction

Foliar litter decomposition is a key biogeochemical process that breaks down dead plant material and releases carbon and nutrients, supporting microbial activity and plant–soil interactions. This process emerges from interacting physical, chemical, and biological mechanisms mediated by abiotic conditions. Litter decomposition strongly regulates soil carbon availability and contributes substantially to global soil respiration, accounting for approximately half of total emissions [1, 2]. Consequently, decomposition rates represent key parameters in various ecosystem carbon models, as enhanced decomposition can increase nutrient supply while accelerating CO₂ release, thereby influencing long-term ecosystem carbon storage [3, 4]. Understanding how climatic drivers and litter chemical properties jointly regulate decomposition, and how their relative importance shifts during the decomposition process, is therefore essential for improving predictions of ecosystem carbon and nutrient cycling under changing climatic conditions.

Previous studies have demonstrated that early-stage decomposition is tightly linked to climatic variables such as mean annual temperature (MAT) and annual actual evapotranspiration (AET) [5–7]. For example, Kang et al. (2009) analyzed 110 litter species across a semi-global climate gradient (MAT: − 2 to 30 °C) and found strong relationships between first-year litter mass loss, MAT, mean annual precipitation (MAP), and nitrogen (N) concentration [6]. Similarly, Berg et al.. (1993) observed that first-year mass loss of Scots pine (Pinus sylvestris) needle litter was significantly related to AET and MAT across a transatlantic climate gradient (MAT: − 1.7 to 19.3 °C) [8]. These multi- and single-species studies in most terrestrial environments primarily focused on early-stage decomposition, dominated by the loss of readily degradable carbohydrates, and largely ignored the long-term dynamics of litter decomposition, in which more recalcitrant compounds, such as lignin, often analyzed as acid-unhydrolyzed residue (AUR), accumulate and increasingly regulate the final, slowest stages of decomposition rates [3, 9].

As litter decomposition proceeds, the chemical composition of litter changes, altering the factors that regulate subsequent decay, and thus accurate representations of litter quality dynamics could improve our model predictions of mass loss across climatic gradients [10–12]. Acid unhydrolyzable residue has been identified as a key rate-limiting component in later decomposition stages [13, 14]. The breakdown of AUR-dominated litter may depend on elements such as manganese (Mn) and N, which significantly influence microbial activity and lignin degradation [2, 15, 16]. Elemental dynamics in Scots pine litter further underscore these effects: N and phosphorus (P) concentrations tend to increase with accumulated mass loss [17, 18], that of potassium (K) generally rises [19], calcium (Ca) initially increases to a maximum before decreasing [20], and magnesium (Mg) exhibits a decrease followed by an increase [21], whereas Mn concentrations often remain stable [22]. Notably, AUR concentration typically increases linearly with accumulated mass loss, reaching approximately 50% of remaining litter mass in both boreal and temperate biomes [17, 23].

The influence of Mn on decomposition is linked to the formation of manganese peroxidase (MnP) by fungi, which facilitates AUR breakdown [24]. Conversely, high N concentrations may suppress ligninolytic enzyme formation or chemically react with lignin residues, producing more resistant compounds [25–27]. In late-stage decomposition of Scots pine needle litter, Berg et al. (2022) reported that Ca promotes mass loss, while P, K, and Mg exert suppressing effects [17]. Despite these insights, very few studies have systematically examined how the effects of potentially limiting elements of decomposing litter change as decomposition progresses and interact with accumulated mass loss [9, 10], introducing considerable uncertainty in estimating terrestrial carbon and nutrient dynamics. In the long term, the interactions between substrate quality, nutrient availability, and microbial activity become progressively important, shaping the stabilization of organic matter, the retention or release of essential elements, and the contribution of litter decomposition to carbon and nutrient cycling at ecosystem scales [14, 17]. Understanding these late-stage decomposition processes is therefore critical for accurately predicting intricate dynamics of long-term carbon storage, nutrient turnover of forests in response to climate change.

The present study addresses this knowledge gap by investigating whether the controls of annual mass loss in Scots pine litter change in influence throughout the decomposition process. Specifically, we explore how climate factors (AET, MAT, MAP), AUR, and six major elements (N, P, K, Ca, Mg, Mn) regulate decomposition rates across four decomposition stages, as indicated by four categories of accumulated mass loss. To this purpose, we compiled data from 17 plots and 34 field incubations of Scots pine litter along a boreal-to-temperate climate gradient. Annual mass-loss values were analyzed in relation to both climate indices and litter chemistry at the start of each year, allowing assessment of stage-specific effects. We hypothesize that the influence of some factors may either remain constant throughout decomposition or change as litter becomes increasingly recalcitrant. While microbial and enzymatic processes are fundamental drivers of litter decomposition, this current study focuses on quantifying the relative importance of climatic conditions and litter chemistry as integrative controls across a broad spatial and temporal gradient. These drivers reflect ecosystem-level regulation, within which microbial processes are implicitly embedded but not directly measured [7, 9, 28]. Understanding this dynamic is essential for advancing our knowledge of decomposition-rate regulation and its broader implications for carbon and nutrient cycling under changing climatic conditions.

Materials and methods

Our synthesis of decomposition rate vs. litter chemical composition is based on previous long-term studies of local pine needle litter across a boreal-to-temperate climate gradient in Scots pine forests during the 1970–1980 s, see details in Appendix S1. We selected Scots pine as a dominant, iconic evergreen tree in the northern coniferous forest belt due to its significant ecological and commercial importance. Below, we have outlined key aspects of the experimental design, procedure, and data processing.

Experimental sites and design

Decomposition studies were conducted along a north–south climatic gradient in monocultural Scots pine forests using a standardized experimental design across all sites. The gradient extended from 66°22′N (near the Arctic Circle) to 56°26′N (near Copenhagen) and included 17 stands across 15 sites, representing 34 long-term studies of local needle litter decomposition (Appendix S1). Each plot measured at least 30 × 30 m. The inclusion of 17 stands reflects the presence of multiple stands at some sites, capturing local variation in forest structure and site conditions. Mean annual precipitation (MAP) ranged from 412 to 1,067 mm, mean annual temperature (MAT) from − 0.7 to 6.8 °C, and actual evapotranspiration (AET) from 382 to 519 mm. Detailed site information is provided in Appendix S1 for the exact location from where the local needle litter is collected.

Needle litter collection, deployment, and litter mass loss determination

Senesced needle litter was collected in autumn by gently disturbing tree branches over spread tarpaulins, followed by manual removal of any green needles. After air-drying to constant moisture (5–8%), litter was stored at room temperature until further processing. Before weighing, subsamples were oven-dried at 85 °C to determine the exact dry mass.

We used polyester litterbags (8 × 8 cm internal dimensions) with a mesh size of 1.0 × 1.0 mm, each containing 0.6–1.0 g of needles (weighed to ± 0.001 g). Bags were deployed on the litter layer surface at 20–25 randomly selected 1 × 1 m spots per plot. At each such spot, 10–14 litter bags were secured to the ground using stainless steel pegs. Litterbag preparation, handling, and subsequent analyses followed standardized procedures. In short, for each decomposition experiment, 200–400 litterbags were deployed per site or stand. Bags were retrieved 1 to 6 times per year, for up to 5 years, typically at annual intervals. On each retrieval date, one litter bag was collected from each of the 20 or 25 locations.

After collection and drying, the litterbag samples were cleaned by removing ingrown plant residues, such as mosses, grass, shrub materials, and roots. Accumulated mass loss was determined by drying the samples to constant mass at 85 °C and averaged for each set of sampled litterbags (20 or 25 replicate samples).

Analyses of litter chemical components

The replicate samples from a given sampling were combined. All combined litter samples were milled and analyzed for AUR and total N, P, K, Ca, Mg, and Mn, using composite samples. For each site and sampling occasion, litter samples were pooled before chemical analysis to obtain representative values of litter chemistry. All samples were analyzed. For all sites except for Jädraås, total N was determined after wet oxidation in H₂SO₄ using a semi-micro Kjeldahl method. Phosphorus, K, Ca, Mg, and Mn were measured by atomic absorption spectrometry (Perkin-Elmer 603), using acid standards.

At the Jädraås site, total N was determined by combustion (Elemental Analyzer NA 1500; Carlo Erba, Strumentazione, Italy). Phosphorus, K, Ca, Mg, and Mn were analyzed after a two-day digestion in a 2.5:1 (v/v) nitric–perchloric acid mixture using plasma atomic emission spectrometry (ICP-AES; Jobin YVON JY-70 Plus, France). Ash concentration for all sites was determined by combustion at 550 °C for 2 h.

Calculation of annual mass loss and division into different decomposition stages

Each decomposition study included samplings at regular intervals, allowing us to calculate annual mass loss. We restricted the dataset to litter sampled at intervals of 365 ± 14 days. Annual mass loss was calculated using the average remaining litter mass from the start and end of each one-year interval. We subtracted the remaining mass at the end of the year from that at the start, dividing by the starting mass, and multiplying by 100 to obtain a percentage.

To assess how the decomposition stage influences annual mass loss, we grouped observations into four categories based on accumulated mass loss, following established approaches [29–31]. Category A included 0–20% mass loss (n = 33), category B 20–40% (n = 29), category C 40–60% (n = 37), and category D > 60% (n = 20), yielding a total of 119 observations. These categories broadly correspond to recognized stages of litter decomposition, with category A representing early-stage loss of labile compounds, category B a transitional phase with declining substrate quality, and categories C and D progressively later stages dominated by the breakdown of more recalcitrant compounds [2, 32]. These thresholds are operational and may vary depending on litter type and environmental conditions.

Data processing and statistical analyses

We implemented all statistical analyses using R version 4.1.2 (https://www.r-project.org/) [33] with a significance level set at 95% where appropriate. The main packages used included basic packages and “car” for model selection and inference [34], and “ggplot2” for visualization [35]. First, we conducted descriptive statistics to characterize our datasets, including the mean and coefficient of variation for annual mass loss and the corresponding needle litter chemistry variables. Before any statistical analysis, we also examined data normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and conducted natural logarithmic transformation when necessary. Differences in annual mass loss among decomposition categories were assessed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey HSD multiple range test.

Second, we constructed multiple linear regression models using both forward and backward selection to quantify the main temporal drivers of annual mass loss for each decomposition stage; these separate models identified and quantified the direction and strength of relationships between annual mass loss, individual litter chemistry, and climate variables. According to previous studies [10], we selected the most parsimonious models using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), prioritizing those with the highest explanatory power (R²) while minimizing the number of predictors. Models with ΔAIC < 2 were considered equally plausible. We note that while this approach facilitates model simplification, it may be sensitive to collinearity among predictors and can increase the risk of overfitting; therefore, results are interpreted with caution, with emphasis placed on consistent patterns across decomposition stages rather than individual model terms (Appendix S2).

Before performing such analyses for each decomposition stage, we conducted correlation analysis to explore the bivariate relationships among annual mass loss and individual needle litter chemical and climatic variables for each decomposition stage (Appendix S2). We additionally standardized each explanatory variable by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation before any statistical analysis to enable a direct comparison of estimated regression coefficients for each decomposition stage. We also evaluated collinearity among the explanatory variables by calculating the variance inflation factor (VIF) for each decomposition-stage model. (Appendix S2) Finally, we employed variation partition analysis for each decomposition stage to assess the relative contributions of each factor, following a similar procedure as described previously [14, 36]. We note that the dataset comprises 119 observations derived from 34 long-term studies. These observations were treated as independent to facilitate comparison across decomposition stages and sites along the climatic gradient. However, potential non-independence cannot be fully excluded and should be considered when interpreting the results.

According to previous studies [32, 37], we also calculated limit values for litter decomposition, at which the decomposition process either continues at a very low rate or possibly stops. In short, we calculated limit values using the following equation: Lt=m(1-e− kAt/m), in which Lt is the percentage accumulated mass loss, t is time in days, and kA is the decomposition rate at the beginning of the decay (asymptotic k), m represents the asymptotic level (the limit value) that the accumulated mass loss will finally reach [37, 38]. That value is normally not 100% and often considerably less. We used a paired t-test to compare the differences between these limit values for litter decomposition and the actual average accumulated mass loss value in category D.

Results

Temporal dynamics of annual mass loss and litter concentrations of AUR and elements

Annual mass-loss values of decomposing Scots pine needle litter ranged from 2.16% to 43.69%, with an overall mean of 26.23% across the four identified categories. Mean annual mass loss declined significantly with increasing decomposition stage, from 30.16% in category A to 15.76% in category D, although considerable variation was observed within each stage (p < 0.05; Fig. 1).

The concentrations of AUR and the elements N, P, K, Ca, Mg, and Mn varied widely in newly shed litter (Appendix S3). As decomposition progressed, the concentrations of AUR, N, P, and K generally increased, while that of Ca decreased. In contrast, concentrations of Mg and Mn showed no consistent pattern across the four decomposition categories (Appendix S3).

Climate and needle litter chemistry influence annual mass loss

Different factors influenced mean annual mass loss, in both direction and magnitude, with effects of climate and needle litter chemistry varying along the decomposition continuum (Fig. 2). Specifically, the climate variable AET had a significant and positive, but decreasing effect on annual mass loss during the early and mid-decomposition stages (categories A–C, p < 0.05), but no significant effect in the late stage (category D, p > 0.05). Mean annual temperature (MAT) was not retained in the final models after variable selection. Notably, MAT was highly correlated with AET (Pearson’s r = 0.94, p < 0.05; Appendix S2), indicating substantial collinearity between these climatic variables. MAP showed no detectable effect on annual mass loss at any decomposition stage.

The effects of AUR and elements on annual mass loss varied throughout the decomposition process (Fig. 2). AUR had no significant effect during the early stage (category A), but was negatively related to annual mass loss in the mid-to-late stages (categories B and C, starting at 20 and 50% accumulated mass loss, respectively), followed by no significant association with annual mass loss in the late stage (category D). Phosphorus concentrations showed no detectable effect on annual mass loss at any stage. Potassium was negatively correlated with mass loss in the mid-to-late decomposition stage (category C) and showed no significant relationship in any other category. Calcium was positively correlated with annual mass loss in the late stages (categories C and D). Magnesium was positively correlated with mass loss in the early-to-mid stages (categories A and B) but correlated negatively in the late stage (category D, p < 0.05). Manganese was positively associated with mass loss in the early and mid-to-late decomposition stages (categories A, B, and C), but this relationship became insignificant in the late stage (category D, p > 0.05) (Fig. 2).

The relative importance of climate and needle litter chemistry

We found that the total explanatory variance of the combined properties decreased as decomposition progressed, from 74.8 to 74.5% in categories A and B, respectively, to 49.9% for category C and 39.1% for category D. The number of detectable factors increased from three in category A to six in category B and five in each of the categories C and D. The relative importance of climate versus litter properties shifted as decomposition proceeded (Fig. 3). Specifically, the climatic contribution (AET) decreased from 54.9% in category A to 14.1% in category C and had disappeared by category D (Appendix S4). Climate thus had a larger contribution than litter chemistry in the early stage (category A) with AET as the dominant factor, whereas concentrations of Mg and Mn dominated the influence from substrate quality with 22.3 and 22.9%, respectively.

In contrast, the role of needle litter chemistry became increasingly dominant from categories B to D (Fig. 3, Appendix S4), with a variation in the importance of individual elements and compounds across decomposition stages (Fig. 3). Whereas Mn and Mg were the most influential elements in the early stage (category A), AUR, P, and K gained prominence in the mid-decomposition stage (category B). In category C, there were also effects of Ca and K. In the late stage (category D), Ca and Mg were the more influential elements (Fig. 3), but Mn, K, and AUR appeared not to have any significant influence.

Discussion

Our study isolates how the relative contribution of climate and litter chemistry shifts as decomposition proceeds, using four categories of annual mass-loss values for a single coniferous litter type across a boreal–temperate climate gradient. Using one particular tree species—Pinus sylvestris—minimizes confounding effects from interspecific variation in initial litter quality and improves comparability across sites, allowing climate- and substrate-driven controls to be identified with greater precision [12, 39, 40]. The extensive field sampling and much longer incubation periods than most of earlier studies [31, 41, 42], substantially extending well into late decomposition stages, allowed us to rigorously resolve temporal transitions that are rarely achieved in temperate short-term decomposition studies and thereby reduce the risk of yielding misleading findings regarding the relative importance of climate and litter chemistry [14, 27, 41, 43]. Our findings complement and extend those of Berg et al. (2022) by shifting focus from between-species differences in late-stage decomposition to within-species temporal dynamics across all decomposition stages [17]. We found that climate, primarily represented by AET, exerted the strongest influence during the early stage of decomposition, when readily degradable carbohydrates dominate mass loss; however, this climatic influence weakened as decomposition progressed. In contrast, the role of litter chemistry increased steadily, with specific compounds and elements contributing differentially depending on the decomposition stage. This progressive shift from climate-driven to substrate-regulated processes provides new empirical insight into the long-standing debate about the relative importance of climate and litter chemical traits in controlling decomposition among existing decomposition studies of various incubation periods and underlines the urgent need for a temporally explicit framework to understand the mechanisms that govern long-term litter decay across the decomposition continuum.

Climate dominates the early phase of litter decomposition

Climate is regarded as the most important regulator of litter decomposition, but the direction and strength of the relationship between needle litter mass loss and climate varied from early to late decomposition stages. We found that climatic control of decomposition was primarily captured by actual evapotranspiration (AET), which showed the strongest and most consistent influence during early decomposition stages. Although mean annual temperature (MAT) was highly correlated with AET, it was not retained as an independent predictor in the final models, likely due to collinearity between these variables (Appendix S2). Thus, Johansson et al. (1995), analyzing data using single linear regression and the very same local Scots pine needle litter data and the same climate gradient as the present paper, noted that MAT and AET were related to 1st -year mass loss with no effect of the substrate [44]. It appears that the decomposition of local Scots pine litter in the present study mainly follows a pattern with an early stage (category A).

Across the climatic gradient examined, the climate was dominant in category A, starting at an accumulated mass loss ranging from 0 to 20%. With an average annual mass loss of 30.1%, category A thus may cover the proposed early stage plus an interval of accumulated mass loss up to as much as c. 40%. This finding coincides well with previous analyses in tropical regions [43, 45, 46] and global drylands [29], but contrasts with results from transitional forests between tropical and temperate regions [36]. In our study, the influence of climatic variables declined markedly as decomposition advanced, a trend also reported in previous work [32, 45, 47] and has been confirmed by climate warming manipulation experiments [48]. The strong climate sensitivity observed in category A likely reflects the rapid microbial use of readily available compounds and the high responsiveness of early-stage microbial communities to changes in thermal energy supply [49, 50]. Climate, primarily represented by AET, therefore provided the strongest explanatory power across our boreal–temperate climatic gradient, reinforcing the current view that biological processes in these ecosystems remain strongly energy-limited [3, 4, 41].

Placing these findings in a broader context, studies in other forests and litter mixtures often report less distinct or more variable transition points between climate and substrate control. The early-stage dominance of climatic factors (up to ~ 20% mass loss) observed here is broadly consistent with findings from tropical forests, although the duration of this phase may vary with environmental conditions [2, 31, 47]. In contrast, studies on mixed-species litter systems suggest that the relative importance of climate versus litter traits can be more complex and contingent upon the specific range of litter chemical variability and the climate gradient examined [9, 51, 52]. Furthermore, long-term decomposition studies indicate that the influence of climate diminishes in later stages (e.g., after ~ 50% mass loss) [41, 43]. These comparisons suggest that while the general pattern of a shift from climatic to substrate control appears robust, the precise transition point is likely system-dependent, varying with litter type, decomposer community, and environmental conditions. Therefore, extrapolating the patterns observed here to other ecosystems requires careful consideration of this context-dependency. These patterns further indicate that universal climatic drivers alone are insufficient to predict decomposition dynamics, highlighting the need for stage-resolved representations of decomposition processes in biogeochemical models.

Chemical components mediate the mid-to-late and later stages of decomposition

As litter decomposition proceeds into the mid-stages, the influence of climate diminishes, while substrate quality and nutrient interactions become increasingly important (Appendix S4). These results support a mechanistic shift from climate-constrained decomposition to substrate-limited decomposition along the decomposition continuum. These overall findings align well with the initial litter quality hypothesis, which posits that legacy effects of initial litter traits persist into later decomposition stages and that litter chemistry diverges progressively over time [10, 11, 53]. Detailed long-term studies on the organic chemistry during decomposition and the use of N-manipulated litter revealed that the first compounds to be degraded were water-soluble plus cellulose and hemicelluloses that were not lignified, viz., not shielded by lignin. Those compounds were estimated to dominate the accumulated mass loss until c. 30%, giving an early stage, ranging from 0 to 30% accumulated mass loss [17, 54, 55]. In the early stage, the concentration of AUR increases markedly (to 339–368 mg g⁻¹), as non-lignified tissues are consumed, exerting a rate-suppressing effect. When the lignified tissue is dominant in the litter, the lignin level may be related to the decreasing mass loss. Meanwhile, Mn continues to play a positive role through its involvement in MnP-mediated lignin degradation [56, 57].

In category B, the number of significant predictors increased (from three to six) while explanatory power remained similar, indicating a shift from dominance by a few strong drivers to more distributed control among multiple variables. This pattern suggests increasing process complexity, although it should be interpreted with caution due to potential shared variance among predictors and effects of variable selection. During this stage, the influence of climate weakened (e.g., AET explained ~ 33% of variation), coinciding with the onset of nutrient release and AUR breakdown. Phosphorus release began at ~ 32.9% accumulated mass loss (Appendix S5) and was accompanied by K, Ca, Mg, and Mn, indicating relatively high nutrient availability; however, only Mg showed a significant positive effect on mass loss, likely reflecting its role in microbial metabolism and enzyme activity, consistent with the earlier findings [58]. By category C, the rate-retarding effects of N and AUR became more pronounced, possibly due to N incorporation into lignin-derived structures, while the increasing dominance of AUR highlights the growing importance of substrate recalcitrance in regulating decomposition.

At the advanced decomposition stage (beyond 60% accumulated mass loss), the explanatory variance of climate and chemical factors declined sharply to about 39% in category D. This reduction indicates that a smaller proportion of variation in annual mass loss is captured by the measured climatic and chemical variables. Rather than implying weaker controls, this pattern suggests reduced predictability and increasing influence of processes not explicitly represented in the models. These may include microbial community dynamics, microsite heterogeneity, and physical protection of recalcitrant compounds. At advanced stages (> 60% accumulated mass loss), decomposition appears increasingly constrained by internal substrate and biological factors. The observed rate-stimulating effect of Ca and rate-retarding influence of Mg may reflect complex interactions between microbial physiology and element availability [12, 59], while the underlying mechanisms remain somewhat unclear. Moreover, the accumulation of fungal biomass and necromass likely enhances substrate recalcitrance, as lignin-rich residues and microbial by-products reduce carbon availability for further decomposition [13, 60].

Together, these patterns are consistent with a stage-dependent shift from climate-dominated to substrate- and biologically regulated decomposition. However, this interpretation is inferential and should be treated cautiously, as the statistical relationships observed here do not directly demonstrate underlying mechanisms. Although microbial processes and enzyme activities were not explicitly measured, the results align with established frameworks in which early-stage decomposition is strongly climate-sensitive, whereas later stages are increasingly governed by substrate quality and accessibility.

How far can we determine rate-regulating factors during long-term litter decomposition studies?

To further illustrate the control of rate-regulating factors in late-stage decomposition, we compared the observed annual mass loss in category D with the theoretical limit values calculated using an asymptotic function (Fig. 4). The average limit value of accumulated mass loss was 80.84%, whereas the actual average accumulated mass loss value for category D was significantly lower by 10.40% points (p < 0.05). This substantial discrepancy indicates that, even in the late stage, decomposition has not reached its theoretical endpoint, emphasizing that residual litter continues to be influenced by remaining substrate and nutrient availability. In this context, Mn emerges as a particularly important factor. Previous studies have demonstrated a positive association between Mn concentration and the limit value for decomposition in Scots pine litter [56, 61], reflecting its long-term role in AUR degradation through manganese peroxidase activity [16, 57, 62].

The asymptotic behavior observed in later stages suggests the presence of a slowly decomposing residual fraction, consistent with previous descriptions of limit values associated with recalcitrant compounds [32, 37, 43]. However, the extent to which such dynamics represent a general feature of litter decomposition remains unclear. In this study, the observed pattern should therefore be interpreted as ecosystem-specific evidence rather than a universal property. These results nonetheless provide useful insight into late-stage constraints on litter decomposition, while highlighting the urgent need for further evaluation across litter types and environmental conditions. Together, these observations suggest that the long-term progression toward the decomposition limit becomes increasingly constrained, with Mn exerting a dominant but subtle influence on the persistence and stabilization of litter. Therefore, integrating these findings with our asymptotic modeling provides a more comprehensive understanding of how nutrient availability, substrate quality, and enzymatic processes interact to regulate litter mass loss and soil carbon formation over the full decomposition trajectory.

Study limitations and future research directions

One limitation of this study is that microbial community dynamics and enzymatic activities were not directly quantified. These factors play a central role in regulating decomposition and likely mediate the effects of both climate and substrate quality; therefore, the patterns identified here should be interpreted as emergent system-level responses rather than direct mechanistic controls [28, 59]. Future studies should aim to explicitly link the observed stage-dependent patterns to underlying biological processes by integrating measurements of microbial community composition and extracellular enzyme activities with long-term decomposition datasets. In particular, resolving how microbial processes interact with litter chemistry and nutrient availability during advanced decomposition stages will be critical for improving these mechanistic understandings and the representation of late-stage decomposition constraints in biogeochemical models [7, 10, 63]. Incorporating soil physicochemical properties and microsite variability may further explain the reduced predictive power observed in later stages. Extending this framework to other species and mixed-litter systems will help test the generality of these patterns and strengthen their relevance for predicting plant–soil feedbacks and ecosystem nutrient cycling under changing environmental conditions.

Conclusions

This study provides a process-oriented synthesis of long-term Scots pine needle litter decomposition across a boreal–temperate gradient, demonstrating how the relative importance of climate and litter chemistry shifts as decomposition progresses. By tracking annual mass loss through advanced decay stages and coupling these data with high-resolution, multi-element chemical analyses, we extend beyond the early-stage focus and static trait-based approaches of most previous studies. Climate, particularly actual evapotranspiration, dominated early-stage decomposition (up to ~ 20% mass loss), whereas its influence declined with advancing decay, and litter chemistry increasingly regulated later stages. This stage-dependent transition helps explain why models assuming invariant controls may misrepresent late-stage decomposition dynamics. These findings underscore the importance of incorporating stage-specific regulation into biogeochemical models to improve predictions of litter-driven carbon and nutrient cycling, particularly in boreal–temperate forest systems. Future integration of microbial and enzymatic processes will be essential to better resolve the mechanisms underlying these dynamics and to enhance model realism under changing environmental conditions.
