pH-responsive CDs-based nanoplatform for chemo-resistant esophageal cancer treatment via downregulation of HIF-1α related pathway
Authors: Xuewei Wang, Yue Zhang, Hao Wu, Zelei Yang, Sufang Ma, Lihong Li, Lixia Guo, Lili Yan, Boye Zhang, Jianfei Liu, Haipeng Diao, Guodong Ren, Chengwu Zhang, Wen Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEsophageal cancer (EC) is a highly malignant tumor that poses a significant threat to patient survival. Chemotherapy, a mainstay treatment for EC, often yields unsatisfactory outcomes due to chemotherapy resistance, the low efficacy of monotherapy and off-target toxicity. Therefore, it is imperative to develop one novel therapeutic strategy capable of overcoming drug resistance, enabling tumor microenvironment-responsive drug release, and achieving multimodal synergistic therapy. Currently, treatment of chemotherapy-resistant EC (CREC) faces great challenges. To address this, a pH-responsive nanoplatform, CDs-DOX-GOX@PEG (CDG@PEG), composed of iron-doped carbon dots (Fe-CDs), doxorubicin (DOX), glucose oxidase (GOX), and polyethylene glycol (PEG), was fabricated to improve CREC treatment through synergistic and targeted therapy. The physicochemical properties of CDG@PEG were characterized using TEM, XPS, XRD, FTIR and other techniques. Its anti-tumor efficacy was assessed with DOX-resistant KYSE-30 cells and corresponding xenograft mouse models. Moreover, the underlying therapeutic mechanism was further investigated via transcriptomic, immunofluorescence and immunohistochemical analyses. Physicochemical characterization results showed that the designed nanoplatform was successfully fabricated, which was endowed with outstanding pH-responsive behavior and O₂/ROS-generation capabilities. In vitro, CDG@PEG demonstrated excellent cellular uptake and potent tumor cell-killing capacities. In vivo, it displayed superior anti-tumor efficacy with good biocompatibility. Mechanistically, CDG@PEG realized the CREC therapy by down-regulating drug-resistant genes (such as HIF-1α related genes), inducing mitochondrial dysfunction, and triggering cell apoptosis. Introduction

Esophageal cancer (EC) is one of the most aggressive malignancies globally, and poses a significant threat to human health [1]. Chemotherapy, as the first-line treatment modality for EC, achieves primary therapeutic benefit. However, its clinical efficacy is severely limited by drug-resistance and off-target toxicity, leading to poor therapeutic efficacy or even treatment failure [2–4]. Thus, it is imperative to develop novel therapeutic strategies that can achieve anti-resistance and targeted drug-delivery.

A growing body of evidence indicates that the hypoxic tumor microenvironment (TME) is a pivotal driver of chemotherapy-resistant cancer among numerous factors [5, 6]. For example, it was proved that under hypoxic conditions, hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α ) was upregulated, which triggered the upregulation of drug efflux pumps such as P-glycoprotein, enhanced DNA repair pathways, suppressed apoptosis, and promoted the stem-like phenotype [7]. Given the central role of HIF-1α in tumor chemoresistance, it has emerged as a promising therapeutic target for overcoming drug resistance. For example, Yang et al. developed a HIF-1α suppressing micro-nano liposome system, PCAL@TM, which achieved enhanced chemotherapy effect of paclitaxel (PTX) in lung cancer [8]. Similarly, Tang et al. fabricated a thermally deformable ultrasmall ceria system and achieved the excellent anti-tumor effect on 4T1 model by inhibiting HIF-1α expression [9]. These findings underscored the considerable potential of strategies that suppress HIF-1α to improve chemotherapy response. To effectively deliver therapeutic agents to the tumor site and potentiate resistance-overcoming capacity, advanced drug delivery systems are essential. However, traditional delivery strategies for chemotherapeutic drugs are limited by severe side-effects resulting from off-target distribution during systemic administration. To address this issue, various novel approaches have been developed. Among them, PEGylation has been recognized as a well-established strategy to realize targeted drug delivery [10–12]. For example, Wang et al. reported that PEGylated nanomedicine was obviously enriched within the tumor due to enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, thereby minimizing the off-target toxicity to normal cells [13]. Moreover, it was demonstrated that modification by PEG could increase drug solubility, enhance cellular uptake capacity, reduce immunogenicity, and prolong the circulation time of drugs in the bloodstream [14].

Even with targeted drug delivery systems, monotherapy often yields limited clinical outcomes [15–17]. Consequently, the development of synergistic therapeutic strategies is essential. Multifunctional nanoplatforms offer a highly promising approach to achieve this goal [18–20]. Their unique physicochemical properties and design flexibility make them favorable systems for achieving synergistic therapy against chemotherapy-resistant cancers. Such nanoplatforms can be engineered to integrate multiple therapeutic modalities, for example, chemodynamic therapy (CDT) and photothermal therapy (PTT) [21, 22]. Among them, CDT has garnered significant attention in the field of chemotherapy-resistant cancer treatment due to its excellent synergistic effect with conventional chemotherapy. The high levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated during CDT can induce peroxidation of DNA, proteins, and lipids, thereby promoting tumor sensitization to chemotherapeutic drugs [23, 24]. Moreover, ROS can activate multiple cell death signaling pathways, which further complemented or synergized the chemotherapy [25, 26]. For instance, Huang et al. prepared one glutathione (GSH)-responsive nanoplatform for self-augmented chemo/chemodynamic therapy against cisplatin-resistant human non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), demonstrating its excellent antitumor efficacy both in vitro and in vivo [27]. Similarly, Xia et al. fabricated a drug-loaded metal-phenolic network, which effectively inhibited tumor growth and suppressed lung metastases via synergistic chemo-chemodynamic therapy [28]. It should be noted that the efficacy of CDT is often limited by the insufficient concentration of H2O2 within tumor cells [29]. To address this limitation, strategies for in situ H2O2-generation within cancer cells have been developed. Among these approaches, loading of glucose oxidase (GOX) which could induce H2O2 as well as gluconic acid generation by catalyzing the glucose oxidation, was proved to be promising [30–32]. The elevated H2O2 and reduced pH could further enhance the CDT effect [33, 34]. Therefore, developing a nanoplatform that combined chemotherapy, O2/H2O2-generation, and CDT capacities hold great potential for the treatment of chemotherapy-resistant cancers.

With the aforementioned issues in mind, we prepared a multifunctional pH-responsive nanoplatform designated CDs-DOX-GOX@PEG (CDG@PEG). Encapsulation with polyethylene glycol (PEG) endowed CDG@PEG with excellent EPR effect and drug accumulation capacity within tumors. Doxorubicin (DOX) served as the chemotherapeutic agent. Iron-doped carbon dots (Fe-CDs) incorporated into CDG@PEG exhibited outstanding O2 and ·OH generation capabilities, enhancing the chemotherapeutic efficacy of DOX. Meanwhile, GOX catalyzed the oxidation of intratumoral glucose to generate gluconic acid and H2O2, which in turn amplified the catalase-like (CAT) and CDT performance of CDG@PEG. This cascade system enabled spatiotemporal integration of self-augmented chemotherapy and CDT, achieving excellent antitumor efficacy against EC both in vitro and in vivo (Scheme 1).

Results and discussion

Expression profile of HIF-1α in clinical EC samples

It has been well established that drug-resistance was closely related to the hypoxic TME, in which HIF-1α acts as a master regulator [7]. To verify the expression of HIF-1α in EC samples, the bioinformatic assay was carried out based on GEPIA, UALCAN, and STRING databases (Fig. 1A). As shown in Fig. 1B, among the examined cancers, EC had the highest expression level of HIF-1α, suggesting its pivotal role in EC pathogenesis. Meanwhile, HIF-1α expression was found to be up-regulated across different pathological stages of EC, with the trend of increasing expression in more advanced stages (Fig. 1C-D). Moreover, compared with adenocarcinoma, HIF-1α expression was extraordinarily higher expressed in squamous cell carcinoma which accounted for more than 90% of EC cases (Fig. 1E). To further clarify the crucial role of HIF-1α in EC drug-resistance, the relationship between HIF-1α and defined chemo-resistance related genes was analyzed based on STRING database. As shown in Fig. 1F, there was strong interplay between HIF-1α and chemo-resistance associated genes (such as SNAi1, PKM, and SLC2A1). Above results demonstrated that HIF-1α played a pivotal role in EC development and drug-resistance, highlighting its potential as a promising therapeutic target for effective EC intervention.

Characterization of Fe-CDs

Inspired by the bioinformatics results, we proposed and prepared one HIF-1α-suppressing nanoplatform composed of Fe-CDs, DOX, GOX and PEG. Firstly, the multifunctional Fe-CDs were fabricated, and their structural properties were characterized. TEM result revealed that Fe-CDs were quasi-spherical and their diameters were less than 10 nm (Fig. 2A). AFM results confirmed that Fe-CDs had good dispersion without significant aggregation (Fig. 2B). XRD result showed a strong diffraction peak at 2θ = 22.8°, indicating Fe-CDs were predominantly amorphous structure (Fig. 2C). Moreover, FTIR and XPS results showed that there were abundant functional groups on the Fe-CDs surface (Fig. 2D-H). And the high-resolution spectrum of Fe2p confirmed the successful doping of Fe in CDs (Fig. 2I).

Characterization of Fe-CDs. (A) TEM image, (B) AFM results, (C) XRD spectrum, (D) FTIR spectrum, (E) XPS and high-resolution of C 1s (F), N 1s (G), O 1s (H), and Fe 2p (I) spectrum of Fe-CDs. (J) Chemodynamic property of Fe-CDs indicated by TMB. (K) O2-generation capacity of Fe-CDs indicated by RDPP. (L) Schematic illustration of in vitro performance of Fe-CDs

Then the optical and O2/ROS-generation properties of Fe-CDs were further evaluated. As shown in Fig. S1, Fe-CDs exhibited a strong absorption peak at 400 nm, attributable to the n→π* transition of the surface C = O bond [35]. Furthermore, Fe-doping endowed Fe-CDs with prominent peroxidase-like activity, which was assessed using TMB as an indicator. As shown in Fig. 2J, in the presence of H2O2, Fe-CDs exhibited excellent ROS-generation capacity in a concentration-dependent manner. Moreover, Fe-CDs exhibited outstanding CAT-like activity. As shown in Fig. 2K and Fig. S2, after incubating with mixture of Fe-CDs and H2O2, the fluorescence intensity of the RDPP probe gradually decreased with prolonged incubation time, indicating the generation of O2. Above results showed that the Fe-CDs had abundant functional groups and excellent O2/ROS-generation capacities, indicating their great potential for further modification and biomedical applications (Fig. 2L).

Fabrication and characterization of CDG@PEG

To realize satisfactory outcomes for EC therapy, we integrated DOX, GOX, and PEG with as obtained Fe-CDs to get one novel nanoplatform via electrostatic interactions and amide bonds. Firstly, the preparation of CDG@PEG was verified by morphology, absorbance, and Zeta potential analysis. TEM results revealed that CDG@PEG exhibited a quasi-spherical morphology with an average diameter of approximately 86.32 nm (Fig. 3A). The absorption spectrum showed characteristic peaks corresponding to each component, confirming the successful fabrication of the nanoplatform (Fig. 3B). Additionally, Zeta potential qualitative analysis result provided additional evidence for the formation of CDG@PEG (Fig. 3C). Above results confirmed the successful preparation of the CDG@PEG.

(B) Absorbance spectra and (C) Zeta potential of individual components (Fe-CDs, DOX, GOx, CDG and CDG@PEG). (D) Release profile of DOX from CDG@PEG under different physiological conditions (pH 7.4 and pH 6.5) at 37℃. (E) pH value and (F) H2O2 level of CDG@PEG solution after incubating with glucose. (G) The fluorescence spectra of CDG@PEG at different pH solution. (H) O₂-generation and (I) ROS-generation capacity of CDG@PEG. (J) Schematic representation of CDG@PEG’s performance

Subsequently, the drug loading and release behavior of CDG@PEG were investigated. Using the standard curve as reference, the loading efficiencies of Fe-CDs and DOX in CDG@PEG were 21.5% and 16.1%, respectively (Fig. S3A-B). Compared with values reported in the literature, the drug loading capacity achieved in this study was relatively low [36]. This could be attributed to the fact that DOX and Fe-CDs were loaded onto PEG through physical adsorption. And several strategies may be adopted to improve the binding affinity and loading capacity. For instance, functionalizing Fe-CDs with specific functional groups could strengthen π–π stacking and electrostatic interactions with DOX [37]. Moreover, optimizing synthetic conditions, including the DOX-to-carrier feed ratio, pH, ionic strength, and incubation time, could elevate the loading efficiency [38]. Meanwhile, the release profile of DOX from CDG@PEG exhibited a pH dependence. Under acidic conditions (pH 6.5), the cumulative release of DOX reached 60.4%, whereas under physiological conditions (pH 7.4), only 22.7% was released (Fig. 3D).

Moreover, the incorporation of GOX endowed CDG@PEG with GOX-like activity, which catalyzed glucose oxidation to generate gluconic acid and H2O2. As shown in Fig. 3E, in the presence of glucose, the pH of the solution progressively decreased with increasing concentrations of CDG@PEG, a result of gluconic acid generation. This acidification further promoted the release of DOX from the nanoplatform. Meanwhile, the H2O2 concentration gradually increased (Fig. 3F), which could subsequently promote O2/ROS-generation induced by CDG@PEG. Given that pH often affected fluorescence property of fluorescent imaging agents, CDG@PEG’s fluorescence spectrum at different pH environment was evaluated. As shown in Fig. 3G, its fluorescence intensity remained highly stable, indicating CDG@PEG had great potential for tumor imaging. Triggered by the O2/ROS-generation capacities of Fe-CDs, we further examined this performance of CDG@PEG. As shown in Fig. 3H, in the presence of H2O2 and glucose, CDG@PEG induced the most pronounced O2 production, which could be attributed to its synergistic dual-enzyme activity (GOX and CAT). Similarly, CDG@PEG exhibited obvious ROS-generation performance indicated by TMB. And this performance was further enhanced under acidic conditions, highlighting its great potential in tumor therapy (Fig. 3I). Above results demonstrated that CDG@PEG had excellent TME responsiveness, CAT/GOX dual-enzyme activity, fluorescence imaging capability, and potent CDT performance, highlighting its great promise for tumor therapy (Fig. 3J).

In vitro physiochemical performance of CDG@PEG

Triggered by its outstanding physicochemical capacities, we further assessed the performance of CDG@PEG at the cellular level. Firstly, we examined the cellular uptake capacity of CDG@PEG by DOX-resistant KYSE-30 cells. As shown in Fig. 4A-B, CDG@PEG was effectively internalized by DOX-resistant KYSE-30 cells, and uptake reached peak within 4 h. This efficient cellular internalization supported its potential applicability in EC treatment.

The remarkable O2 and ROS generation capabilities of CDG@PEG in vitro prompted us to further investigate its performance at cellular level. As shown in Fig. 4C-D, the fluorescence quenching of RDPP was most pronounced in the CDG@PEG group, indicating the highest O2 level in cells. This performance could be attributed to the synergistic CAT/GOX dual enzymatic activity of CDG@PEG. The CAT-like activity catalyzed the decomposition of H₂O₂ into O₂ within TME. Concurrently, GOX-like activity of CDG@PEG promoted oxidative degradation of glucose in tumor cells, leading to an elevation of H2O2 level and subsequently enhanced O2 generation. Furthermore, fluorescence signal of DCFH-DA revealed that the CDG@PEG group induced the highest level of intracellular ROS compared with other groups (Fig. 4E-F). This could be attributed to the chemodynamic properties of Fe-CDs within CDG@PEG, which were further enhanced by GOX-mediated elevation of H2O2 levels. Above results showed that CDG@PEG had excellent O2/ROS generation performance at the cellular level, indicating its potential in tumor cell killing.

Anti-tumor effect of CDG@PEG in cellular level

Building on its excellent O2/ROS generation performance, we further assessed the anti-tumor efficacy of CDG@PEG against DOX-resistant KYSE-30 cells via MTT assay, Calcein-AM/PI staining, and colony formation experiments. As shown in Fig. 5A, CDG@PEG exhibited a potent tumor cell killing effect, significantly outperforming the DOX only group. The enhanced anti-tumor performance could be attributed to the chemotherapy from DOX, CDT mediated by Fe-CDs, and the catalytic activity of CAT/GOX mimics, which together synergistically induced cell death.

To visually evaluate the anti-tumor effects, Calcein-AM/PI staining and colony formation assays were performed. As illustrated in Fig. 5B and H, the CDG@PEG group showed the best PI signal, representing the highest level of cell death. Consistent results were observed in the colony formation assay, in which CDG@PEG treatment markedly reduced the number of KYSE-30 cell colonies (Fig. 5C and Fig. S4A). Moreover, mitochondrial dysfunction is a key mediator of cell death, so we characterized it by examining mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), and morphology of cristae structure. As shown in Fig. 5D and I, CDG@PEG resulted in a pronounced decrease in MMP of DOX-resistant KYSE-30 cells. Furthermore, TEM imaging revealed significant morphological alterations in DOX-resistant KYSE-30 cells mitochondria after administration of CDG@PEG, especially disappearance of mitochondrial cristae (Fig. 5E). These findings demonstrated that CDG@PEG induced severe mitochondrial damage in DOX-resistant KYSE-30 cells, ultimately triggering cell death. Above results demonstrated the excellent anti-tumor capacity of CDG@PEG.

Metastasis represents a major challenge in the treatment of EC. To investigate the potential of CDG@PEG in inhibiting metastasis, wound healing, invasion, and migration assays were performed. As shown in Fig. 5F and Fig. S4B, CDG@PEG effectively suppressed the wound healing ability of DOX-resistant KYSE-30 cells compared to other groups. Furthermore, CDG@PEG had strong inhibition efficiency on invasion and migration ability of DOX-resistant KYSE-30 cells (Fig. 5G and Fig. S4C-D). These results indicated that CDG@PEG had great potential in metastatic EC therapy.

Biosafety is crucial for the biomedical application of any nanoplatform. Therefore, we assessed the cytotoxic effects of CDG@PEG on human esophageal epithelial cells (HEEC). As shown in Fig. 5J, treatment with CDG@PEG did not significantly impair HEEC viability, indicating negligible cytotoxicity toward normal esophageal cells. Furthermore, hemolysis assay results showed that CDG@PEG hardly induced hemolysis even at concentration of 300 µg/mL (Fig. 5K). These findings proved that CDG@PEG had good biocompatibility, which ensured its potential for further biomedical applications.

Anti-tumor mechanism of CDG@PEG

CDG@PEG exhibited a remarkable anti-tumor effect at the cellular level. To elucidate its underlying molecular mechanisms, transcriptome analysis was carried out. Principal component analysis (PCA) was first conducted to assess the quality of samples. The PCA results showed that there was obvious segregation between the PBS and CDG@PEG-treated groups, while samples within each group clustered closely together, indicating the high quality of the RNA sequencing data (Fig. 6A). Moreover, CDG@PEG treatment induced significant alteration of genes expression, and the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were enriched in pathways related to metal ion binding, apoptotic processes, response to hypoxia, DNA damage response, and glucose metabolism (Fig. 6B-D). Clustering heatmaps of DEGs revealed that CDG@PEG led to the down-regulation of anti-apoptotic genes, while up-regulating pro-apoptotic genes (Fig. 6E). Given that mitochondria serve as energetic hubs closely associated with proliferation, invasion, and migration of tumor cells, we further examined the effect of CDG@PEG on mitochondrial-related pathways. Heatmap demonstrated that CDG@PEG treatment significantly altered mitochondrial functional pathways, indicating their involvement in DOX-resistant KYSE-30 cells death (Fig. 6F). We also investigated the chemotherapy sensitization mechanism of CDG@PEG. As shown in Fig. 6G, after treatment with CDG@PEG, chemo-resistance genes such as HIF-1α, SLC2A1, SNAi1 were significantly down-regulated. Moreover, immunofluorescence staining of HIF-1α and SLC2A1, further confirmed their expression profile (Fig. 6H and Fig. S5). To further prove the key role of HIF-1α in drug-resistant tumor therapy, knockdown experiments were carried out. As shown in Fig. S6, after administration of HIF-1α siRNA, DOX-resistant KYSE-30 cells were more sensitive to DOX, indicating that HIF-1α was a key target mediator of chemo-resistance. Above results suggested that CDG@PEG killed the DOX-resistant KYSE-30 cells by modulating key biological processes including apoptosis, mitochondrial dysfunction and HIF-1α-related drug-resistance.

In vivo performance of CDG@PEG

The promising tumor cell killing capacity of CDG@PEG in vitro inspired us to further assess its in vivo performance. We firstly evaluated its tumor-targeting capability. CDG@PEG was administrated via tail vein injection into KYSE-30 tumor-bearing nude mice, and fluorescence imaging was performed. As shown in Fig. 7A and Fig. S7, CDG@PEG began to accumulate in tumor post-injection, and reached peak 10 h after injection. 24 h later, mice were euthanized, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) along with tumors were harvested for ex vivo imaging. The results revealed obvious accumulation of CDG@PEG in tumor tissue. A noticeable fluorescence signal was also detected in the liver, suggesting hepatic metabolism as a primary clearance pathway for CDG@PEG. These results demonstrated that CDG@PEG efficiently accumulated in the tumor and could be rapidly eliminated from the body, indicating a favorable pharmacokinetic profile for biomedical applications.

After validating the tumor-targeting and imaging properties of CDG@PEG, we further evaluated its anti-tumor efficacy in vivo (Fig. 7B). As shown in Fig. 7C, tumor growth in the CDG@PEG-treated group was significantly suppressed, demonstrating superior anti-tumor effect compared to DOX only and PBS groups. Tumor volume and weight assessment further proved the excellent anti-tumor capacity of CDG@PEG (Fig. 7D-E). Moreover, H&E staining revealed a marked reduction of tumor cells density in the CDG@PEG group, whereas substantial tumor cell remnants were observed in the DOX and PBS group. To investigate the molecular mechanism underlying the anti-tumor effect of CDG@PEG, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining analysis was performed. It showed that there was the weakest Ki67 signal (proliferation marker) and the strongest TUNEL signal (apoptosis marker) in the CDG@PEG treatment group compared with that of DOX and PBS treatment. Additionally, IHC analysis demonstrated that SLC2A1 and HIF-1α, proteins closely related to drug-resistance and tumor cell proliferation, were significantly down-regulated following with CDG@PEG treatment (Fig. 7F). These results showed that CDG@PEG effectively inhibited tumor growth in vivo by down-regulating key proteins involved in tumor drug-resistance and proliferation.

Biosafety of CDG@PEG

Biosafety is critical for the clinical translation of nanoplatforms. Therefore, we further evaluated the in vivo safety of CDG@PEG (Fig. 8A). As shown in Fig. 8B, mice in CDG@PEG group maintained stable body weight. In contrast, mice treated with free DOX exhibited rapid weight loss during the treatment period, which could be attributed to the systemic toxicity of the unencapsulated drug. This result indicated that CDG@PEG significantly reduced the off-target toxicity of DOX.

We further measured the Fe3+ levels, a unique element of the nanoplatform, to assess the metabolic clearance of CDG@PEG. As illustrated in Fig. 8C, Fe3+ concentrations returned to baseline levels within 24 h comparable to those of the control group. Moreover, the pharmacokinetic parameters of DOX and CDG@PEG in the blood were studied according to the reported methods [39, 40]. As shown in Fig. S8, CDG@PEG exhibited longer circulation time compared to free DOX, rendering enhanced CDG@PEG’s accumulation in tumor.

We further evaluated the biosafety of CDG@PEG by monitoring hematological parameters. As shown in Fig. 8D, no significant alteration was observed in liver (ALT, AST) and kidney (BUN, CRE) function markers between the CDG@PEG- and PBS-treated groups. In contrast, the DOX group showed elevated ALT levels, indicating the hepatotoxicity of DOX. Moreover, H&E staining results of major organs showed no structural abnormalities in the CDG@PEG group, compared with that of PBS control. However, liver tissue from the DOX-treated group displayed a distinct deviation from normal hepatic architecture, characterized by disorganized hepatocyte arrangement and structural disruption within the hepatic lobules, indicating severe hepatocellular injury (Fig. 8E). Moreover, we carried out a longer-term repeat-dosing study. Specifically, mice were administered CDG@PEG every other day for a duration of 30 days. Then comprehensive safety assessments were performed, including histopathological analysis of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidney) and complete hematological analysis. As shown in Fig. S9 and Fig. S10, longer-term administration of CDG@PEG did not induce significant alteration in morphology of main organ tissues or obvious change in blood parameters. Above results proved that CDG@PEG had good biocompatibility. CDG@PEG significantly mitigated the toxic side effects of free DOX, indicating its great potential in clinical application.

Conclusion

EC, as a tumor with high incidence and mortality, poses a severe threat to human life. However, its treatments remain a significant challenge. In the present study, we developed one pH-responsive nanoplatform, CDG@PEG, and applied it for EC therapy. The developed nanoplatform exhibited several notable merits: (1) TME-responsive drug release; (2) excellent tumor targeting capability via the EPR effect; (3) outstanding in vivo fluorescence tumor imaging performance; (4) superior anti-tumor efficacy and improved biosafety compared to free DOX; (5) mechanistically inhibiting HIF-1α related drug-resistant pathways. Although CDG@PEG demonstrated promising potential for EC treatment, several aspects warrant further optimization and investigation. For instance, the tumor targeting of CDG@PEG mainly relies on the passive EPR effect, which potentially limits its translational efficacy. Second, drug loading capacity of CDG@PEG was modest, which need further improved. Third, the therapeutic evaluation was conducted only in cellular and xenograft models, which do not fully recapitulate the complexity, heterogeneity, and drug-resistant microenvironment of human EC. Therefore, future work would focus on incorporating active-targeting moieties to improve tumor targeting ability, optimizing the preparation condition to enhance drug loading capacity, and validating the therapeutic efficacy in more clinically relevant models (such as patient-derived xenografts (PDXs) and patient-derived organoids). Overall, this study provided a feasible strategy for improving the treatment of drug-resistant tumors by leveraging the favorable biocompatibility and drug-loading capacity of a rationally designed nanoplatform.

Clinical samples assay

The expression of HIF-1α in EC patients was analyzed using GEPIA, and UALCAN. The correlation of related genes was studied by STRING databases as established methods reported in the literature [41–43].

Preparation of Fe-doped carbon dots (Fe-CDs)

Fe-CDs were synthesized via one-step hydrothermal method. To obtain CDs with both excellent biocompatibility and therapeutic efficacy, hemin and lysine were selected as precursors. Specifically, 20.00 mg of hemin and 200.00 mg of lysine were dissolved in 15.00 mL of deionized water, then the solution was then subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 180 °C for 6 h. Then it was cooled naturally to room temperature. The resulting crude product was centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 10 min), and the supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm aqueous membrane filter. The filtrate was subsequently dialyzed against deionized water for 12 h (MWCO = 1000 Da). Finally, the Fe-CDs powder was obtained by vacuum freeze-drying.

Synthesis of CDG@PEG nanoplatform

CDG@PEG nanoplatform was synthesized by sequentially loading doxorubicin (DOX) and glucose oxidase (GOX) onto the prepared Fe-CDs, followed by surface functionalization with polyethylene glycol (PEG). Specifically, 2.00 mg of Fe-CDs and 5.00 mg of DOX were dissolved in 10.00 mL of deionized water and stirred under dark conditions for 24 h at room temperature. The mixture was then dialyzed (MWCO = 1000 Da) against deionized water for 6 h to remove free DOX. Subsequently, 10.00 mg of GOX, 10.00 mg of EDC and 10.00 mg of NHS were dissolved and stirred for 0.5 h to activate carboxylic acid groups of GOX. Then the above dialyzed solution was added and stirred for another 12 h to form CDs-DOX-GOX (CDG). The CDG solution was then dialyzed (MWCO = 1000 Da) for 12 h to remove unreacted reagents and byproducts. The purified CDG complexes were obtained by freeze-drying. Finally, 2.00 mg of the obtained CDG and 10.00 mg of PEG were dissolved in deionized water and reacted overnight at room temperature. The resulting CDG@PEG product was purified by dialysis (MWCO = 3500 Da) for 12 h, and followed by freeze-drying to get powder.

Characterization of physicochemical properties of Fe-CDs and CDG@PEG

The morphology of Fe-CDs and CDG@PEG nanoplatform was characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). To analyze chemical bonds and functional group, Fe-CDs and CDG@PEG were subjected to X-ray diffraction (XRD), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy examination. Moreover, optical absorption properties and zeta potential of CDG@PEG were also measured.

Drug loading efficiency and release kinetics of CDG@PEG

The loading efficiency of each component was determined using a standard curve method. First, a series of known concentrations (20.00, 40.00, 60.00, 80.00, and 100.00 µg/mL) of Fe-CDs and DOX solutions were prepared. The absorbance values at their respective characteristic absorption wavelengths was measured using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. Standard calibration curves for Fe-CDs and DOX were subsequently plotted. Then according to the curves, the loading efficiency of Fe-CDs and DOX within the CDG@PEG was calculated.

The release kinetics of DOX from CDG@PEG were evaluated under physiological (pH 7.4) and acidic (pH 6.5) conditions using phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) buffers. CDG@PEG solution (10.00 mg/mL) was placed into a dialysis bag and dialyzed against 200.00 mL of the corresponding release medium (PBS, pH 6.5 or 7.4). The external dialysis medium was withdrawn at predetermined time points. Absorbance measurements of the solution were taken at 400 nm and 485 nm. The cumulative release of DOX at each time point was calculated based on the established calibration curves.

Dual-enzyme activity assay of Fe-CDs and CDG@PEG

Fe-doping is a well-established strategy for developing CAT-mimicking nanozymes, offering enhanced stability compared to their biological enzyme counterparts [44, 45]. CAT catalyzes the decomposition of H2O2 into O2. To evaluate the CAT-like activities of Fe-CDs and CDG@PEG, they were incubated with 1.00 mM H2O2 at 37 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, the generation of O2 was measured using the O2 specific probe [Ru(dpp)₃]Cl2 (RDPP).

GOX catalyzes the oxidation of glucose to gluconic acid and H2O2 in the presence of oxygen. Accordingly, the GOX activity of CDG@PEG was evaluated by monitoring changes in both the pH and H2O2 concentration of the reaction solution. The production of H2O2 was measured using a commercial titanium sulfate colorimetric assay kit.

The GOX activity within CDG@PEG consumes glucose to produce H2O2, which is subsequently decomposed into O2 via CAT-like catalysis of Fe-CDs. The generated O2 enhances GOX catalytic efficiency through a feedback mechanism. To investigate this process, 100.00 µg/mL CDG@PEG, 1.00 mM H2O2 and 10.00 mg/mL glucose were mixed, then incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Dissolved oxygen meter was used to measure O2 production.

Chemodynamic properties of Fe-CDs and CDG@PEG

The ROS-generation activities of Fe-CDs and CDG@PEG were assessed using 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) as a colorimetric indicator. First, Fe-CDs solutions at various concentrations (0, 10.00, 20.00, 30.00, 40.00, and 50.00 µg/mL) were prepared. To each solution, H2O2 (1.00 mM) and TMB (0.15 mM) were added followed by incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. Absorption spectra of the mixture were then recorded.

Subsequently, CDG@PEG’s chemodynamic property and pH responsiveness were examined. 100.00 µg/mL CDG@PEG were prepared in PBS buffers at different pH values. H2O2 (1.00 mM ) and TMB (0.15 mM) were subsequently added to each solution. After 30 min, absorption spectra were measured. Control groups containing TMB alone as well as a mixture of TMB and H2O2 were included to evaluate the intrinsic ROS-generating activity of CDG@PEG and its pH-dependent performance enhancement.

Cellular uptake capacity of CDG@PEG

Given that effective nanoplatform internalization is a prerequisite for therapeutic efficacy, cellular uptake efficiency of CDG@PEG was assessed. DOX-resistant esophageal squamous cell carcinoma cells (DOX-resistant KYSE-30) served as cellular model. When DOX-resistant KYSE-30 cells were in logarithmic growth phase, they were seeded in confocal dishes at a density of 1.00 × 105 cells/well. 24 h later, the culture medium was replaced with RPMI-1640 medium containing CDG@PEG (120.00 µg/mL) was added and incubated for different time (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 6 h). Cellular internalization performance was visualized and assessed using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM).

O2 and ROS generation capacities of CDG@PEG at cellular level

The O2 and ROS generation capacities of CDG@PEG at the cellular level were evaluated. DOX-resistant KYSE-30 cells were seeded in confocal dishes at a density of 1.00 × 105 cells/well. After 24 h, cells were treated with CoCl2 (200.00 µM) for 12 h to establish a hypoxia model. Subsequently, Fe-CDs, DOX, or CDG@PEG were added for overnight incubation. Following treatment, the O2 probe RDPP was added to assess the O2-generation capacity of CDG@PEG. Meanwhile, ROS-generation property of CDG@PEG was examined. In detail, DOX-resistant KYSE-30 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at 1.00 × 10⁴ cells/well. After 24 h, cells were treated with Fe-CDs, DOX, or CDG@PEG for another 24 h. The medium was then replaced with DCFH-DA (10.00 µM), and incubated for another 30 min, followed by fluorescence imaging.

Anti-tumor capacity of CDG@PEG at the cellular level

The anti-tumor efficacy of CDG@PEG at the cellular level was evaluated through MTT assay, Calcein-AM/PI staining, colony formation, migration and invasion experiments.

For the MTT assay, DOX-resistant KYSE-30 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 1.00 × 104 cells/well. 24 h later, cells were treated with various concentrations of Fe-CDs, DOX, or CDG@PEG. Subsequently, 100.00 µL of MTT solution (0.50 mg/mL) was added to each well. Following 4 h incubation, 150.00 µL dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added. After 20 min, absorbance at 490 nm of solution was measured.

Calcein-AM/PI staining was performed by seeding DOX-resistant KYSE-30 cells in 24-well plates at 1.00 × 105 cells/well. When cell confluence reached up to 90%, Fe-CDs, DOX, or CDG@PEG were added for 24 h. Then, cells were incubated with Calcein-AM/PI probe for 30 min followed by fluorescence imaging. Moreover, colony formation, migration and invasion assays were also conducted to evaluate the anti-tumor capacity of CDG@PEG as reported in our previous study [46].

The impact on mitochondria of CDG@PEG

Mitochondria are the “energy centers” of tumor cells, so the impact of CDG@PEG on mitochondria was assessed within DOX-resistant KYSE-30 cells. After treating with CDG@PEG, mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) and morphology of the mitochondria were examined using the JC-10 probe and biological transmission electron microscopy (Bio-TEM), respectively.

Anti-tumor mechanism of CDG@PEG

DOX-resistant KYSE-30 cells were seeded at a density of 1.00 × 106 cells/well. Then serum-free RPMI-1640 medium containing CDG@PEG (120.00 µg/mL) was added. 24 h later, cellular RNA was extracted by adding 1.00 mL TRIzol™ reagent, then it was stored at -80 °C for subsequent transcriptome sequencing. A control group was treated with PBS instead. Then, transcriptome analysis was performed.

Establishment of EC xenograft model

To evaluate the in vivo anti-tumor properties of CDG@PEG, a chemotherapy-resistant esophageal cancer (CREC) xenograft model was established. Balb/c nude mice (female, 6-weeks-old) were obtained from HFK Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of The First Hospital of Shanxi Medical University. Mice were housed in individually ventilated cages (IVC). For tumor inoculation, mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and implanted subcutaneously in the right axilla with 1.00 × 107 DOX-resistant KYSE-30 cells. Tumor volume was measured every 48 h using digital calipers, with volume calculated as V = (a × b2)/2 (a was the length and b was width). When tumor volumes reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were randomly assigned to different groups (n = 5). The treatment process was to be terminated if body weight loss reached or exceeded 15% of the initial weight, or if the tumor diameter reached or exceeded 150 mm.

In vivo fluorescence imaging

To evaluate the tumor-targeting capability of CDG@PEG, DOX-resistant KYSE-30 xenograft models were administered 200.00 µL of CDG@PEG (5.00 mg/kg) via tail vein injection. Real-time biodistribution was monitored by detecting the fluorescence of DOX using a multimodal Xtreme in vivo imaging system at predetermined points (2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 24 h post-injection). After 24 h, mice were euthanized and the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) and tumors were harvested for ex vivo fluorescence imaging at identical excitation/emission parameters.

Pharmacokinetic assessment

The mice were randomly divided into two groups, free DOX and CDG@PEG groups. After administration of DOX or CDG@PEG via tail vein injection, 0.20 mL blood was collected at pre-setted time points, and the fluorescence signal derived from DOX was measured.

In vivo anti-tumor capacity of CDG@PEG

When tumor volumes reached approximately 100 mm3, tumor-bearing nude mice were randomly assigned to three groups: (1) PBS, (2) DOX, and (3) CDG@PEG. Treatments were administered once every two days for 14 days. Tumor volumes and mouse body weights were measured and recorded throughout the treatment period.

Upon completion of the treatment regimen, mice were euthanized and tumor tissues were harvested for further evaluation. The therapeutic efficacy and potential molecular mechanisms were subsequently investigated through histological staining of the tumor tissue.

Biocompatibility assessment of CDG@PEG

Following the completion of the treatment protocol, blood samples were collected from the nude mice to assess liver and kidney function. Key related biochemical indicators were measured. Major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were also harvested and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data were analyzed using Origin 8.5 software and are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between groups were determined by a two-tailed Student’s t-test. Statistical significance was defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.
