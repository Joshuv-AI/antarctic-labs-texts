Real‐world implementation and acceptability of HPV self‐sampling in routine cervical screening: Addressing methodological considerations
Authors: Saara Kyllönen, Satu Saloranta, Saara Kares, Anu Mustila, Karolina Louvanto
Published: 2026

Sir,

We thank the authors for their thoughtful commentary 1 regarding our study evaluating HPV self‐sampling within the routine cervical cancer screening program in Pirkanmaa, Finland. 2 We appreciate the opportunity to address their methodological considerations.

We agree that the absence of a contemporaneous randomized control group limits causal inference regarding the comparative effect of self‐sampling on screening participation. However, the FALCON study aimed not to compare invitation strategies or estimate the effectiveness of self‐sampling versus standard care, but rather to evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of introducing self‐sampling within an established population‐based screening program under routine conditions. Previous studies have shown that direct mailing of self‐sample kits is more effective in increasing screening participation. 3 We adopted a real‐world implementation design rather than an experimental design. As noted in the article, participation during the first year was modest and does not allow firm conclusions regarding the impact on screening coverage.

Regarding follow‐up after HPV‐positive self‐sampling, nonattenders received a reminder letter. While three HPV‐positive women ultimately did not undergo follow‐up sampling, this reflects real‐world adherence within a routine screening program rather than loss to follow‐up within a research protocol. It is well known that achieving attendance for follow‐up cervical cytology among women with a positive self‐sample is challenging. Compliance with further follow‐up among hrHPV‐positive women has varied across studies, between 41% and 100%. 3 Furthermore, regarding the six women who subsequently tested HPV‐negative at clinic follow‐up, transient viral clearance during the inter‐test interval and physiological sampling differences between self‐collected vaginal and clinician‐collected cervical specimens are recognized biological occurrences rather than verification bias. 4

Concerning the acceptability survey, the response rate to the follow‐up questionnaire was notably high (260/329, 79%), which minimizes the risk of nonresponder bias. Consistent responses across multiple acceptability measures indicate that the positive participant experiences were not limited to a small subgroup. The purpose of the questionnaire was to assess user experiences after exposure to self‐sampling, rather than to compare perceptions before and after participation. Self‐sampling has been shown to be highly acceptable in numerous studies. 5

We fully agree that operational and economic factors are important when considering large‐scale implementation. In fact, the article explicitly describes the extensive IT modifications, logistical preparations, and laboratory adaptations required before implementation and acknowledges that formal economic evaluation was beyond the scope of the present study. We specifically stated that no conclusions regarding cost‐effectiveness could be drawn from the first year of implementation. Future phases of the program will provide opportunities for more detailed analyses of resource utilization, implementation costs, and scalability.

We appreciate the authors' recognition of the strengths of our study. We share their view that further research is needed and believe that early implementation studies play an important role in informing the gradual integration of HPV self‐sampling into population‐based cervical cancer screening. Our findings provide initial real‐world evidence that self‐sampling can be successfully incorporated into routine screening services and is highly acceptable among participating women.
