Gas Adsorption‐Driven Electronic Modulation in WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 Heterostructure: Mechanistic Origin of Selective Drift Resistance Room‐Temperature Formaldehyde Sensing
Authors: Mohammad Jamir Ahemad, Eunyoung Lee, Chanyoung Kim, Tae‐Wook Kim, Seoung‐Ki Lee, Sang Hyun Lee, Jae‐Hyun Lee, Byung Joon Moon, Sukang Bae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFormaldehyde is a ubiquitous indoor air pollutant whose carcinogenicity, high toxicity, and volatility pose severe health risks even at trace concentrations. Nevertheless, realizing ultra‐low detection limits with high selectivity under ambient‐temperature conditions using a single chemiresistive semiconductor remains an enduring challenge. A heterointerface‐engineered WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 nanocomposite is developed, integrating the high surface reactivity of WO3 with the intrinsic conductivity and ordered π‐conjugated framework of the 2D conductive metal–organic framework (cMOF) Cu3(HHTP)2. Synergistic interfacial orbital hybridization between W 5d (WO3) and O 2p π‐orbitals (cMOF) induces pronounced electronic structure modulation, promoting enhanced charge transport and gas–solid interfacial reaction kinetics. DFT calculations reveal that HCHO preferentially adsorbs at phenolic O sites in the cMOF and O‐vacancy sites on WO3, inducing localized charge redistribution and suppressing mid‐gap defect states. This adsorption‐driven Fermi level shift correlates directly with the experimentally observed resistance drop under HCHO exposure. The WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 sensor exhibits an ultralow detection limit (∼48 ppb), rapid response/recovery, minimal baseline drift, and exceptional selectivity under mixed gas environments at room temperature. These findings establish a clear mechanistic link between interfacial electronic structure tuning and enhanced sensing performance, offering a generalizable strategy for designing next‐generation VOC sensors via surface modification of metal oxides with cMOFs.Keywords: 2D materials, formaldehyde detection, metal–organic frameworks, room temperature, WO3

Introduction

Formaldehyde (HCHO), a colorless and pungent‐smelling gas, is a ubiquitous indoor air pollutant, emitted via off‐gassing from resins in pressed‐wood products, carpets, upholstery, and related manufacturing processes [1, 2]. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classifies HCHO as a Group 1 human carcinogen; both acute and chronic exposures are linked to respiratory irritation, sensitization, and elevated cancer risk [3, 4]. Given its prevalence and toxicity even at trace levels, there is a pressing need for sensitive, selective, and real‐time HCHO monitoring technologies that operate under ambient (room‐temperature) conditions to protect human health and the environment [2].

Among various gas‐sensing materials, semiconducting metal oxides (SMOs) such as SnO2, ZnO, In2O3, and WO3 have been intensively explored for HCHO detection owing to their low cost, scalable synthesis, chemical robustness, and well‐established surface‐chemistry‐driven actuation [5, 6]. To further enhance sensing performance, numerous strategies, including noble‐metal doping, heterojunction engineering, and nanostructuring, have been widely implemented [5]. Nevertheless, state‐of‐the‐art SMO‐based sensors typically require elevated operating temperatures (often >150°C) to initiate surface redox reactions, which results in high energy consumption, shortened device lifetime, and safety concerns for portable/wearable applications [7]. Furthermore, the rigid crystal lattices and limited accessible surface‐active sites of pristine SMOs hinder adsorption–desorption dynamics and interfacial charge transfer, thereby restricting their sensitivity and selectivity toward VOCs under ambient conditions [7]. Despite these limitations, SMOs remain indispensable building blocks due to their intrinsic catalytic activity, oxygen‐vacancy chemistry, and tunable electronic structure. Accordingly, considerable effort has recently been devoted to designing advanced architectures that can overcome these intrinsic drawbacks (i.e., charge transfer, desorption, and catalytic activation) towards the development of high‐performance room‐temperature HCHO gas sensors.

Recently, two‐dimensional conductive metal–organic frameworks (cMOFs), comprising extended π‐conjugated linkers coordinated to transition metal nodes, where ligand‐metal p‐d coupling enables in‐plane through‐bond charge transport, have emerged as compelling gas‐sensing platforms [8, 9]. They provide specific adsorption sites and tunable pore microenvironments, together with intrinsic electrical conductivity, permitting rational control of adsorption energetics and interfacial electron transfer [8]. Functioning simultaneously as molecular receptors and electronic transducers, cMOFs enable deliberate tuning of sensitivity and selectivity via targeted linker functionalization, control of metal‐node identity/valence, pore‐environment engineering, and mesoscale architecture. Nevertheless, selective and sensitive detection in complex mixtures remains challenging due to competitive adsorption, cross‐sensitivity, and complex adsorption‐desorption kinetics that obscure analyte discrimination. These constraints motivate strategies that integrate electronic‐structure modulation with defect chemistry and heterointerface engineering to sharpen molecular recognition, suppress interference, and preserve room‐temperature operation.

Among cMOFs, Cu3(HHTP)2 (HHTP = 2,3,6,7,10,11‐hexahydroxytriphenylene) is a particularly attractive candidate for integration with metal oxide semiconductors because it combines high electrical conductivity, permanent porosity, chemically accessible coordination sites, and structural compatibility with heterogeneous interfaces. Cu3(HHTP)2 exhibits the highest room‐temperature electrical conductivity among the M3(HHTP)2 family (M = Cu, Ni, Co, Zn), originating from efficient π‐d orbital overlap through Cu–O coordination, which enables gas adsorption events to directly modulate carrier transport without thermal activation [10, 11]. Its successful integration with various materials, including ZnO for optoelectronics, MXene for triethylamine detection, and reduced graphene oxide for NH3/H2S sensing, further demonstrates its interfacial adaptability and potential as an active component in sensing architectures [11, 12, 13]. Systematic comparative studies have also shown that the sensing behavior of Cu3(HHTP)2 is governed not simply by adsorption strength, but by a balanced interplay between charge transport and adsorption‐induced electronic modulation [14].

Structurally, the permanent microporosity of Cu3(HHTP)2 provides accessible diffusion pathways for gas molecules, while its layered π–π stacking network supports ordered charge transport and rapid interfacial carrier transfer. Additionally, the phenolic oxygen sites within the HHTP linker can serve as chemically defined recognition sites for polar analytes through Lewis acid‐base interactions [11, 13, 15]. Recent oxide‐cMOF hybrid systems have shown that Cu3(HHTP)2 can form stable interfaces with metal oxides while preserving its porosity and conductivity, and that cMOF‐based heterostructures can promote π‐electron delocalization, directional charge redistribution, and improved charge transport [16, 17, 18, 19].

Nevertheless, constructing such oxide‐cMOF heterojunctions remains fundamentally different from forming conventional oxide‐oxide heterointerfaces. Unlike lattice‐matched oxide interfaces, the interface between a rigid crystalline metal oxide and a soft layered Cu3(HHTP)2 framework is primarily mediated by coordinative and chemical interactions at the metal oxide surface. Therefore, precise synthetic control is required to avoid pore blockage by oxide particles, preserve the ordered π‐stacking essential for MOF conductivity, and optimize MOF coverage so that the interfacial contact area is maximized while maintaining porosity or electrical continuity [14, 16]. In this context, achieving effective interfacial electronic coupling, rather than simple physical mixing, is a key requirement for realizing the full potential of SMO‐cMOF heterostructures in room‐temperature gas sensing [16, 17].

Considering the complementary advantages and limitations of SMOs and 2D cMOFs, their integration constitutes a rational design pathway to enhance chemiresistive sensing. Accordingly, we herein introduce a heterointerface‐engineered WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 architecture that enables selective, room‐temperature HCHO detection with higher sensitivity and operational stability than either component alone. Band alignment at the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 junction generates a built‐in electric field that induces directional charge separation and efficient interfacial transport. Upon HCHO exposure, surface‐adsorbed oxygen species oxidize HCHO, reinjecting electrons into the WO3 conduction band (CB), which contracts the width of the electron depletion layer in WO3 and narrows the space charge region at the p‐n junction interface; the net effect is a decreased resistance and an amplified chemiresistive signal. In parallel, the cMOF layer both offsets the limited conductivity of WO3 via continuous π‐conjugated pathways and provides chemically well‐defined phenolic‐O recognition sites that afford preferential interfacial adsorption of HCHO, while oxygen‐vacancy centers in WO3 serve as complementary catalytically active centers. Acting together, interfacial orbital coupling and defect chemistry accelerate charge transfer and reaction kinetics, enabling rapid, selective room‐temperature sensing. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first demonstration of functionalizing a SMO (WO3) with the 2D cMOF Cu3(HHTP)2 for sensitive and selective formaldehyde detection at room temperature, enabling real‐time detection under ambient conditions.

Results and Discussion

Structure and Characterization

To gain initial insight into the structural characteristics of the materials, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analyses were performed on both the pristine components and the hybrid composite, confirming the successful synthesis and morphological integration of the constituent phases. As shown in Figure S1a, pristine WO3 exhibits a cylindrical morphology with distinctive starfruit‐like surface features, with lengths ranging from ∼0.5 µm to 3.6 µm and widths between 1.0 and 2.5 µm. In contrast, Cu3(HHTP)2 shows a layered, sheet‐like morphology, consistent with its two‐dimensional coordination framework (Figure S1b). Notably, energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDX) mapping (Figure S2a,b) reveals a uniform spatial distribution of W, Cu, C, and O throughout the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 composite, confirming the successful structural integration of both constituents into a coherent composite architecture. Separately, the EDX spectrum (Figure S2c) displays distinct elemental peaks, plotted with X‐ray energy (keV) on the x‐axis and detector counts (arbitrary units, a.u.) on the y‐axis, further validating the presence of these elements.

To further elucidate the nanoscale structural features and verify interfacial integration, conventional and high‐resolution transmission microscopy (HRTEM) analyses were performed (Figure 1). The TEM image of pristine Cu3(HHTP)2 reveals a sheet‐like morphology, characteristic of a two‐dimensional layered structure (Figure S3). Meanwhile, the TEM image of the composite (Figure 1a) exhibits hybrid morphological features originating from both parent components, suggesting intimate interfacial integration. HRTEM images of the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 composite (Figure 1b; Figure S4) reveal well‐defined lattice fringes with a consistently measured interlayer spacing of ∼0.31 nm, as confirmed by lattice fringe intensity profile analysis (∼0.311 nm, Figure S4b), corresponding to the (001) crystallographic plane of Cu3(HHTP)2. This spacing represents the interlayer distance arising from π–π stacking interactions between adjacent HHTP organic linkers in the two‐dimensional MOF framework, consistent with previously reported values [20]. The repeated observation of this spacing in different HRTEM regions indicates that the layered structure of Cu3(HHTP)2 is preserved after composite formation [20]. Complementary EDX mapping (Figure 1c–g) further verifies the uniform elemental distribution, supporting the compositional homogeneity and successful integration of the WO3 and MOF domains within the hybrid framework.

(b) HRTEM image highlighting the Cu3(HHTP)2 domain within WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2. Following the morphological characterization, X‐ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was employed to further examine the crystalline structures of the pristine components and their nanocomposites, as presented in Figure 1h. The XRD pattern of calcined WO3 displays distinct diffraction peaks at 2θ = 23.06°, 23.55°, 24.31°, 26.58°, and 28.7°, corresponding to the (002), (020), (200), (120), and (112) planes, respectively. These peaks are in excellent agreement with the monoclinic phase of WO3 (JCPDS No. 89–4476). Notably, characteristic peaks associated with crystalline hydrated tungsten oxides, such as the (020) reflection of WO3·H2O at 2θ = 16.5° (JCPDS 04‐011‐6930) and the (010) reflection of WO3·2H2O at 2θ = 12.6° (JCPDS 18–1420), are not observed. This result indicates that no detectable crystalline hydrate phase remains after calcination [21, 22, 23]. Pristine Cu3(HHTP)2 exhibits well‐defined reflections at 2θ = 4.73°, 9.60°, 12.68°, 16.58°, and 27.80°, corresponding to the (100), (200), (210), (220), and (002) planes, respectively. These peaks confirm that Cu2+ centers are coordinated with hexadentate HHTP ligands to form extended two‐dimensional (2D) honeycomb layers, which further assemble into a slipped‐parallel AB motif perpendicular to the substrate surface [24, 25]. The XRD pattern of the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 composite contains characteristic reflections from both WO3 and Cu3(HHTP)2, indicating successful hybridization without noticeable phase decomposition (Figure 1h; Figure S5). In the composite, the Cu3(HHTP)2 (200) reflection exhibits a slight shift toward lower 2θ values, suggesting an increase in interplanar spacing. This shift implies that the Cu3(HHTP)2 lattice is structurally perturbed upon interaction with the WO3 component, possibly due to interfacial strain or coordination‐induced lattice distortion. In addition, the WO3 (002) reflection shows measurable peak broadening, with an increased FWHM corresponding to a decrease in crystallite size from 54.22 to 44.11 nm (Table S3) [26]. Such broadening suggests reduced crystallite size and increased structural disorder, which may provide additional surface or interfacial sites relevant to gas adsorption [27]. Similarly, the crystallite size estimated from the Cu3(HHTP)2 (200) reflection decreases from 13.98 to 12.50 nm after composite formation. This decrease suggests that the presence of WO3 influences the growth behavior of Cu3(HHTP)2, likely by promoting heterogeneous nucleation on the oxide surface and limiting excessive grain growth [28]. Overall, the coexistence of both crystalline phases, together with the observed peak shift and broadening, supports the successful formation of a structurally coupled WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 composite. These structural changes are expected to increase the effective interfacial contact area and facilitate charge transfer, thereby contributing to the improved room‐temperature HCHO sensing performance.

To quantitatively assess the porosity and structural evolution induced by hybridization, N2 adsorption–desorption isotherm measurements and corresponding pore size distribution analyses were performed. The isotherms for WO3, Cu3(HHTP)2, and WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 are classified as Type III, Type II, and Type IV, respectively, according to the IUPAC classification (Figure S6a). Notably, the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 nanocomposite exhibits a slight hysteresis loop during the desorption process, indicating the presence of mesoporous features likely arising from structural rearrangement at the MOF‐metal oxide interface. Moreover, the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface areas of WO3, Cu3(HHTP)2, and the composite were determined to be 3.83, 222.35, and 200.46 m2 g−1, respectively, with the corresponding Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) average pore sizes of 23.25, 19.38, and 13.10 nm (Figure S6b). Upon composite formation, the specific surface area moderately decreases from 222.35 to 200.46 m2 g−1, whereas the total pore volume decreases more substantially from 0.7825 to 0.3419 cm3 g−1 (Table S4). Considering that pristine WO3 exhibits a much lower surface area of 3.83 m2 g−1 and a pore volume of 0.0322 cm3/g, this decrease is mainly associated with the incorporation of WO3 domains into the Cu3(HHTP)2‐based porous architecture. This reduction in pore volume is likely related to the partial occupation of larger mesoporous channels and interstitial voids by WO3 during in‐situ formation of the composite, rather than severe blockage of the intrinsic microporous channels of Cu3(HHTP)2. To examine this point in more detail, NLDFT pore size distribution analysis was performed (Figure S6c,d). As shown in Figure S6d, the characteristic micropore features of Cu3(HHTP)2 are largely retained in the composite, indicating that the intrinsic microporous structure, including the ∼1.8 nm channels, remains mostly preserved after hybridization. In contrast, the decrease in pore volume occurs predominantly in the mesopore region (>10 nm) (Figure S6c), which is consistent with the structural integration observed in the HAADF‐STEM image (Figure 1c). These results suggest that, although WO3 incorporation reduces the overall pore volume, a substantial portion of the MOF‐derived microporosity remains accessible for gas transport and sensing operation [29, 30]. In addition, the BJH profile shows that the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 composite has a narrower pore size distribution than pristine Cu3(HHTP)2 (Figure S6b). This more uniform pore structure may be beneficial for controlled molecular diffusion and improved accessibility of gas molecules to the active interfacial sites [31, 32]. Therefore, the retained microporosity and refined mesoporous structure are expected to support efficient gas transport and contribute to the improved sensing performance of the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 nanocomposite [31].

In addition to the structural characterizations, Fourier‐transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to gain molecular‐level insight into the preservation of functional groups and coordination environments following hybridization, thereby confirming the chemical integrity of the resulting WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 framework (Figure 1i). For pristine Cu3(HHTP)2, multiple characteristic vibrational bands are observed, reflecting its π‐conjugated organic backbone and the metal‐ligand coordination environment. Specifically, a broad absorption band in the 3500–3300 cm−1 region is primarily attributed to the asymmetric stretching vibrations of phenolic ─OH groups within the HHTP ligands, as well as residual moisture and solvent molecules confined in the porous structure [33, 34]. The band at 1628 cm−1 corresponds to C═O stretching vibrations, indicative of coordination between carbonyl functionalities and Cu2+ centers [13]. Additional peaks at 1453 and 1305 cm−1 are assigned to C═C stretching and C─H bending modes, respectively, reflecting the presence of aromatic moieties within the conjugated backbone of the HHTP ligands [13]. The C─O stretching mode at 1215 cm−1 further supports the involvement of O atoms in metal‐ligand coordination, while the Cu─O stretching band at 574 cm−1 provides direct spectroscopic evidence of metal‐ligand complexation between Cu2+ ions and the HHTP linker [35]. For pristine WO3, a broad band is also observed in the 3500–3300 cm−1 region, assigned to surface hydroxyl groups and physiosorbed atmospheric moisture on WO3 exposed to ambient conditions, rather than to bulk hydrated tungsten oxide phases. This assignment is consistent with the XRD results, where no characteristic reflections corresponding to crystalline WO3·H2O or WO3·2H2O phases are observed, and also agrees with previous reports on WO3‐based materials in which O─H stretching bands are commonly associated with re‐adsorbed moisture and surface hydroxylation [36, 37, 38, 39]. Moreover, two well‐defined peaks at 756 and 830 cm−1 are assigned to the stretching and bending vibrations of O─W─O and W─O─W bonds, respectively, consistent with the monoclinic WO3 [36].

To further examine the effect of hybridization on the chemical structure and local electronic environment of the composite constituents, Raman spectroscopy was employed in Figure 1j. In the spectrum of WO3, two prominent bands are observed at 718 and 808 cm−1, corresponding to the stretching vibrations of O─W─O linkages [36]. Additional peaks at 273 and 328 cm−1 are ascribed to W─O─W bending modes, further confirming the characteristic vibrational modes of monoclinic WO3 [36]. Meanwhile, the Raman spectrum of Cu3(HHTP)2 is dominated by broad bands in the 1000–1600 cm−1 region, attributed to C═C and C─C stretching vibrations within the π‐conjugated HHTP ligands [34]. This spectral profile reflects not only the coordination environment around the Cu2+ centers but also the extended delocalization of π‐electrons, supporting the formation of a 2D conductive MOF structure [15]. Upon composite formation, the O─W─O stretching bands shift from 718 to 712 cm−1 and from 808 to 800 cm−1, respectively (Figure S7a). The W─O─W bending modes also exhibit downward shifts, although their shift magnitudes are relatively smaller. These redshifts suggest a weakening of the W─O‐related bond force constants, which can be associated with local lattice distortion and charge redistribution induced by the interaction between WO3 and Cu3(HHTP)2 [40]. Additionally, the FWHM broadening of the WO3‐related Raman bands (Figure S7b) indicates increased local structural disorder and coordination asymmetry after hybridization [41]. The relative intensity ratio of the stretching and bending modes also changes after composite formation (Figure S7d), further suggesting modification of the local WO3 vibrational environment. This change is consistent with interfacial perturbation of the W─O bonding network rather than a phase transformation of WO3. In contrast, the main Raman features associated with the Cu3(HHTP)2 framework remain largely preserved after hybridization (Figure S7c), indicating that the π‐conjugated HHTP backbone maintains its structural characteristics within the composite. The moderate change in the ID/IG ratio suggests a slight increase in structural disorder or electronic perturbation within the MOF framework, without severe disruption of its conjugated architecture. Overall, the Raman results indicate that hybridization with Cu3(HHTP)2 modifies the local vibrational environment of WO3 while largely preserving the conjugated framework of the MOF. The observed peak shifts and band broadening support the presence of interfacial structural and electronic interactions between WO3 and Cu3(HHTP)2. These interactions are expected to facilitate charge transfer across the hybrid interface, thereby contributing to the improved room‐temperature gas‐sensing performance of the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 composite [42, 43].

Building upon the structural and electronic modulations revealed by Raman spectroscopy, X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was subsequently employed to gain deeper insight into the elemental composition and chemical state evolution in WO3, Cu3(HHTP)2, and their hybrid WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 composite (Figure 2a–d; Figure S8). In the first instance, the survey spectra (Figure S8a) clearly display characteristic peaks corresponding to Cu 2p, C 1s, O 1s, and W 4f, confirming the successful synthesis of the individual components and their effective integration into the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 composite.

Chemical‐state and optical analyses of WO3, Cu3(HHTP)2, and WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2. (a) O 1s XPS spectra, (b) relative contents of oxygen‐related species, (c) W 4f XPS spectra, (d) Cu 2p XPS spectra, (e) ESR spectra, and (f) UV–vis absorption spectra.

The high‐resolution O 1s spectra for WO3, Cu3(HHTP)2, and WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 were deconvoluted, as shown in Figure 2a. For pristine WO3, three components are observed at 530.31, 531.38, and 532.47 eV, which are assigned to lattice oxygen (OL), oxygen‐vacancy‐related species (OV), and chemisorbed oxygen species (Oads), respectively [44]. In contrast, the O 1s spectrum of Cu3(HHTP)2 can be deconvoluted into four components associated with different oxygen‐containing environments in the framework. The peak at 530.7 eV is attributed to Cu─O bonds formed through coordination between Cu2+ nodes and deprotonated oxygen sites of the HHTP ligand [45, 46, 47, 48]. The peak at 531.78 eV is attributed to C─O bonds arising from phenolic or catecholate‐type oxygen groups in the HHTP linker, which participate in metal‐ligand coordination [45, 46, 47, 48]. The peak at 533.03 eV corresponds to C═O bonds, which are likely associated with partially oxidized HHTP linker units formed during synthesis [45, 46, 47, 48]. The coexistence of C─O and C═O species suggests the presence of redox‐active semiquinone/quinone‐like moieties, consistent with catecholate‐based cMOFs in which the linker can undergo partial redox interconversion among catecholate, semiquinone, and quinone states [45, 46, 47, 48]. The broad component around 536.89 eV is assigned to adsorbed oxygen‐containing species, including surface O─H groups and physiosorbed H2O, which are commonly observed in hygroscopic MOF systems [45, 46, 47, 48]. In the high‐resolution O 1s spectrum of the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 composite, oxygen species attributed to both WO3 and Cu3(HHTP)2 are observed, supporting the successful incorporation of both components within the hybrid structure. The composite spectrum can be fitted with seven oxygen‐related components: four associated with Cu3(HHTP)2 (Cu─O, C─O, C═O, and O─H/H2O) and three associated with WO3 (OL, OV, and Oads), as summarized in Table S5. Notably, the oxygen‐related peaks in the composite exhibit slight negative shifts in binding energy compared with the corresponding pristine materials, indicating an increase in local electron density around oxygen‐containing sites after hybridization [49]. These shifts suggest interfacial charge redistribution between WO3 and Cu3(HHTP)2, supporting the formation of an electronically coupled heterointerface rather than a simple physical mixture [50, 51].

More importantly, quantitative analysis of the O 1s spectra reveals a clear change in the surface oxygen species after hybridization (Figure 2b). Compared with pristine WO3, the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 composite exhibits increased contributions from oxygen‐vacancy‐related species (OV) and chemisorbed oxygen species (Oads). Accordingly, the relative OV/OL and Oads/OL ratios increase substantially in the composite (Figure 2b, right panel), suggesting a higher density of oxygen‐deficient surface sites and improved surface reactivity. This change in the oxygen‐species distribution is likely associated with interfacial electronic interactions between WO3 and the redox‐active Cu3(HHTP)2 framework. The coexistence of C─O and C═O species in Cu3(HHTP)2 indicates the presence of semiquinone‐like moieties, which may participate in interfacial charge redistribution. Such electronic interaction can modify the local oxygen environment of WO3, favoring the formation or stabilization of oxygen‐deficient surface sites and promoting the chemisorption of atmospheric oxygen. The increased OV‐related contribution can provide additional adsorption and activation sites for gaseous oxygen, thereby increasing the amount of surface‐adsorbed oxygen species involved in the sensing reaction [52]. This interfacial modulation of oxygen species is important for the enhanced HCHO sensing performance. The oxygen‐deficient surface sites and enriched chemisorbed oxygen species can facilitate formaldehyde adsorption and oxidation, while the coupled WO3‐Cu3(HHTP)2 interface supports efficient charge transfer during the HCHO oxidation process. Therefore, the modified oxygen‐species distribution likely contributes to the rapid response/recovery behavior and improved sensing performance of the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 composite.

While the O 1s spectra merely substantiate the preservation of the original bonding configurations upon hybridization, further insights into interfacial electronic reconstruction were gained through detailed analysis of the W 4f region. As shown in Figure 2c, the high‐resolution W 4f spectrum of pristine WO3 can be deconvoluted into three distinct peaks at 35.72, 37.86, and 41.32 eV, corresponding to the W 4f 7/2, W 4f 5/2, and W 5p 3/2 states, respectively (Figure 2c), where the first two peaks arise from spin‐orbital splitting of the W6+ oxidation state [36]. Upon hybridization, notably, both the W 4f 7/2, and W 4f 5/2, peaks are shifted to lower binding energies in the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 nanocomposite, indicating an increase in electron density around the W centers [44]. This shift is likely associated with π‐electron transfer from the conjugated HHTP ligands to the vacant d‐orbitals of W atoms, mediated by interfacial orbital hybridization [44]. Such interfacial electronic modulation promotes extended charge delocalization across the hybrid interface and facilitates the formation of continuous charge transport pathways. As a result, the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 nanocomposite exhibits enhanced charge mobility, improved structural integrity, and elevated catalytic activity, key attributes that contribute to its superior performance in gas sensing and gas‐solid interfacial reactions [53, 54].

The Cu 2p spectra (Figure 2d) clearly indicate the coexistence of Cu(II) and Cu(I) oxidation states in both pristine Cu3(HHTP)2 and the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 nanocomposite. Specifically, the characteristic Cu(II) peaks appear at ∼933.7 eV (2p 3/2) and ∼953.6 eV (2p 1/2), accompanied by their shake‐up and satellite features (939.1, 953.8, and 961.8 eV), hallmarks of unpaired 3d9 configurations, while the Cu(I) component is discernible at ∼931.6 eV, consistent with a fully filled 3d10 electronic state [55]. Although Cu(II) remains the dominant species in both materials, the relative proportion of Cu(I) increases notably from ∼2.53% in pristine Cu3(HHTP)2 to ∼6.16% in the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 composite (Table S5). From an interfacial electronic coupling standpoint, this change in the Cu oxidation state distribution is ascribed to interfacial charge transfer at the WO3‐Cu3(HHTP)2 heterojunction [25].

Electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy was further employed to provide complementary evidence for this interfacial electronic interaction (Figure 2e). Pristine WO3 is nearly ESR‐silent, consistent with its diamagnetic d° configuration, whereas pristine Cu3(HHTP)2 displays a broad isotropic ESR signal characteristic of paramagnetic Cu(II) centers (g = 2.12610). After formation of the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 heterostructure, the ESR signal intensity is markedly reduced, indicating a decrease in the population of paramagnetic Cu(II) centers. This signal quenching is consistent with the partial conversion of Cu(II) to ESR‐inactive Cu(I) species through interfacial electron redistribution [25]. Additionally, the g‐value decreases from 2.12610 for pristine Cu3(HHTP)2 to 2.11252 for WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2, reflecting a modified local electronic environment around the Cu‐centers after hybridization. This shift is consistent with increased electron density around Cu sites and agrees with the increased Cu(I) fraction observed in the XPS analysis. Taken together, the Cu 2p XPS and ESR results support the presence of interfacial electronic coupling and charge redistribution in the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 heterojunction [25].

The high‐resolution C 1s spectra for Cu3(HHTP)2 and WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 (Figure S8b) were deconvoluted into three major components corresponding to C─C, C─O, and C═O bonding environments, respectively [56]. Upon composite formation, the C─O component exhibits a positive binding energy shift of +1.10 eV (from 285.80 to 286.90 eV), accompanied by an increase in peak area from 22.25% to 26.28% (Figure S8c,d and Table S5). Such a positive shift reflects a decrease in local electron density around the C─O bonding environment of the HHTP linker, indicating that the oxygen‐containing linker sites are electronically perturbed through interaction with the WO3 component. Similarly, the C═O component also shifts to higher binding energy by +2.28 eV (from 288.15 to 290.43 eV), suggesting reduced electron density around the carbonyl‐related sites of the HHTP ligand. Unlike the C‐O component, however, the C═O peak area slightly decreases from 14.06% to 12.05% after composite formation. These findings indicate that the local chemical environment of the oxygen‐containing functional groups in Cu3(HHTP)2 is modified after hybridization with WO3. The consistent positive shifts of the C─O and C═O components support the presence of interfacial electronic interactions between WO3 and Cu3(HHTP)2, rather than simple physical mixing of the two components. In particular, these changes are consistent with coordinate‐type interactions between the WO3 surface and the oxygen donor sites of the HHTP linker, which can induce localized charge redistribution at the heterointerface. Similar behavior has been reported in semiconductor‐cMOF heterojunction systems, where metal‐oxygen coordination at the interface was shown to play an important role in interfacial charge transfer [18]. Therefore, the modified electronic environment of the HHTP linker provides further evidence for an electronically coupled WO3‐Cu3(HHTP)2 interface, which is expected to facilitate charge transport across the heterostructure and contribute to the enhanced sensing performance of the composite material [57, 58].

In order to further substantiate the occurrence of interfacial electronic interactions revealed by Raman and XPS analyses, the optical absorption behavior of WO3, Cu3(HHTP)2, and their hybrid composite WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 was systematically investigated via UV–vis spectroscopy (Figure 2f; Figure S9). The calcined WO3 nanoparticles display a broad absorption edge in the UV region (Figure S9a), characteristic of monoclinic WO3 [59]. A weak absorption tail extending into the visible region may arise from shallow defect states or residual oxygen vacancies introduced during high‐temperature calcination [60]. On the other hand, both pristine Cu3(HHTP)2, and the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 composite exhibit three distinctive absorption bands (Figure 2f): (i) a sharp peak at ∼270 nm attributed to the HHTP ligand, (ii) a strong π–π* transition band centered at ∼367 nm, and (iii) a broad feature around 658 nm associated with ligand‐to‐metal charge transfer (LMCT) from the oxygenated linker to Cu2+ centers [10]. Compared to pristine Cu3(HHTP)2, the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 composite shows a slight bathochromic shift in the π–π* transition (Figure S9b), which can be ascribed to interfacial charge transfer and orbital hybridization between WO3 and the MOF. These interactions induce a redistribution of π‐electron density across the conjugated backbone and stabilization of the π* orbital, resulting in a narrowed π–π* energy gap and the observed redshift [61, 62]. A similar bathochromic shift in the LMCT band is also observed (Figure S9c), further supporting strong electronic coupling and efficient interfacial charge transfer from the MOF backbone to the WO3 phase [62]. Collectively, these spectral shifts reflect systematic modifications in the electronic structure induced by hybridization between WO3 and Cu3(HHTP)2, promoting enhanced charge delocalization and interfacial electron transport across the composite. Such electronic reconfiguration is expected to play a vital role in the superior gas sensing performance of WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 [63].

Gas Sensing Analysis

The gas sensing performance of pristine WO3, Cu3(HHTP)2 and the composite WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 were systematically evaluated under ambient conditions toward various reducing gases, including HCHO, ammonia (NH3), hydrogen (H2), carbon monoxide (CO), hydrogen sulfide (H2S), ethanol (CH3CH2OH), and acetone (CH3COCH3), at a fixed concentration of 20 ppm (Figure 3a; Figure S10). The selectivity performance toward the target gas over interfering species is a critical requirement for reliable sensor operation in real‐world environments.

As shown in the comparative response profiles in Figure 3b, the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 nanocomposite exhibits markedly enhanced sensitivity and pronounced selectivity toward HCHO relative to its individual constituents, which may be attributed to synergistic interfacial interactions and optimized charge transport pathways within the hybrid architecture. Moreover, to quantitatively evaluate the selective recognition capability, the selectivity coefficient (KS ) was calculated as the ratio of the sensor response to HCHO relative to that of other interfering gases under identical conditions [2]. As illustrated in Figure 3c, the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 nanocomposite demonstrated a significantly higher KS for HCHO than for other tested gas species at 20 ppm, indicating a clear recognition preference for HCHO. This result suggests that the sensor may possess high specificity for HCHO detection, even under ambient or more complex gas environments, although the current evaluation was conducted under single‐gas exposure conditions. This anticipated selectivity likely stems from the effectively suppressed cross‐reactivity of the hybrid material toward nontarget gas species, thereby rendering it a promising candidate for real‐world sensing applications in complex environments. Note that, to further validate this hypothesis, additional experiments under mixed gas conditions will be presented in the following section.

To further assess the sensitivity of the optimized system, the response of the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2‐based sensor was recorded across a range of HCHO concentrations (0.3–20 ppm), as presented in Figure S11. The corresponding dynamic response‐recovery curves reveal excellent reversibility and effective baseline recovery, demonstrating its low signal drift, high signal‐to‐noise ratio, and reliable detection performance over a wide concentration range. Notably, the sensor exhibited clear and reproducible responses even at the lowest experimentally tested concentration of 0.3 ppm (300 ppb), with distinct resistance changes observed in the magnified inset of Figure S11. This result demonstrates experimentally validated sub‐ppm HCHO detection under room‐temperature operation.

Additionally, there was a good linear correlation between response values and HCHO concentrations, as shown in Figure 3d. Notably, even at ppb‐level concentrations, the sensor maintained strong linearity, with a regression coefficient (R2) of 0.996 (inset of Figure 3d), indicating its high sensitivity and reliability. Based on the slope of the calibration curve and the standard deviation of the baseline noise, the theoretical limit of detection (LOD) was calculated to be 48 ppb using the 3σ method (LOD = 3σ/S), which is lower than the WHO indoor air quality guideline for formaldehyde (∼80 ppb). The combination of experimentally confirmed detection at 300 ppb and a calculated LOD of 48 ppb supports the potential applicability of the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 sensor for indoor HCHO monitoring under ambient conditions [34].

In addition to sensitivity, the dynamic sensing behavior was evaluated using the response (tres) and recovery times (trec), which are critical parameters for practical sensor operation. The tres and trec values were measured for WO3, Cu3(HHTP)2, and WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 toward 20 ppm HCHO (Figure 3e). The WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 composite exhibited a tres of 106.35 s and a notably fast trec of 19.38 s. In comparison, pristine WO3 showed much slower kinetics (tres = 138.21 s, trec = 280.63 s), while pristine Cu3(HHTP)2 displayed intermediate behavior (tres = 107.63 s, trec = 126.31 s). The substantially faster recovery of the composite compared with both pristine components indicates a clear synergistic effect arising from the hybrid architecture. This improvement can be attributed to the high surface area, ordered pore structure, and continuous π‐conjugated pathways of the 2D Cu3(HHTP)2 framework, which facilitate gas diffusion and interfacial charge transport [64]. Additionally, the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 heterointerface likely promotes efficient charge transfer during HCHO oxidation by pre‐adsorbed oxygen species, thereby accelerating resistance recovery after gas removal.

Furthermore, as shown in Figure 3f, HCHO exhibited the shortest tres and trec among all tested gases, whereas the interfering gases showed relatively slower dynamic behavior, indicating a kinetic preference of the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 sensor toward HCHO. Pristine WO3 exhibited uniformly slow recovery for all tested gases, reflecting the diffusion‐limited desorption behavior commonly observed on metal oxide surfaces (Figure S12). Similarly, pristine Cu3(HHTP)2 showed moderate and relatively uniform recovery behavior without a clear kinetic preference for a specific analyte (Figure S12). In contrast, the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 composite displayed a distinct kinetic preference toward HCHO, further supporting the role of the heterointerface in enabling both rapid and selective room‐temperature sensing. These results demonstrate that the hybrid sensor is well‐suited for real‐time and continuous HCHO monitoring applications.

The repeatability of the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 sensor was systematically evaluated over 20 consecutive response‐recovery cycles under repeated exposure to 20 ppm HCHO at room temperature (Figure 3g). The coefficient of variation (CV) was determined to be 2.67%, indicating excellent signal stability and reproducibility during continuous operation, which is an essential attribute for reliable real‐time gas monitoring. The long‐term stability of the sensor was examined through repeated measurements performed weekly over an 8‐week period under identical conditions (20 ppm HCHO, room temperature). As shown in Figure S13, the sensor consistently maintained a high response level with only minor signal attenuation, while its dynamic response behavior remained stable throughout the testing period (Figure 3h), exhibiting minimal drift in the response‐recovery characteristics and preserving a signal amplitude comparable to that observed in the first week. To further assess long‐term repeatability and operational stability, an additional test was performed in the ninth week (Figure S14a,b). Although slight signal drift was observed, the response level remained consistent with the average values recorded during the first week. From these findings, we can confirm the excellent long‐term operational stability and durability of the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 sensor, affirming its potential for reliable real‐world gas sensing applications.

The selectivity of WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 towards HCHO was examined through cross‐sensitivity tests performed by exposing the sensor to mixtures of HCHO and potential interfering gases (e.g., C2H5OH, CH3COCH3, NH3, H2, CO, and H2S), each at a concentration of 20 ppm. The transient response‐recovery curves and the corresponding response values are summarized in Figure S14c,d. As illustrated in Figure S14d, the sensor exhibited comparable responses toward pure HCHO and HCHO mixed with other interfering gases, confirming its remarkable selectivity and strong anti‐interference capability in mixed gas environments.

To further evaluate the practical applicability of the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 sensor, its sensing performance toward 20 ppm HCHO was systematically investigated under different relative humidity levels (25–75% RH), as shown in Figure 3i and Figure S15. At low humidity (25% RH), the baseline resistance remained relatively stable, and the sensor exhibited strong and reproducible responses (∼49%), indicating minimal surface perturbation by weakly physiosorbed water molecules [65, 66]. With increasing humidity, the baseline resistance progressively decreased, attributed to the interaction of water molecules with chemisorbed oxygen species and surface defect sites, which partially releases trapped electrons back to the WO3 conduction pathway [67, 68]. The humidity‐dependent response decline is primarily governed by competitive adsorption between water molecules and HCHO at active surface sites, particularly oxygen vacancy sites and coordinatively unsaturated metal centers in WO3 and Cu3(HHTP)2 [69]. This interpretation is further supported by additional DFT adsorption calculations, which show favorable H2O adsorption on both the WO3(001) – 2VO active domain and Cu3(HHTP)2 framework, together with stabilization of activated oxygen species at WO3 vacancy‐related sites (Figure S16). These results indicate that water molecules can perturb both the oxygen‐activation sites and HCHO‐accessible adsorption environments, which is a well‐recognized origin of humidity interference in metal oxide‐based gas sensors [70]. Consistent with this mechanism, the sensor response gradually decreased from ∼49% at 25% RH to ∼30% at 50% RH and further to ∼18% at 75% RH. Nevertheless, at moderate indoor humidity levels (40–60% RH), the sensor retained a substantial fraction of its low‐humidity response, partly attributed to the porous Cu3(HHTP)2 framework facilitating HCHO diffusion and preserving interfacial charge modulation [65]. Future strategies such as hydrophobic surface modification, molecular‐sieve humidity filters, or hydrophobic interfacial layers could be explored to further enhance humidity resistance [67, 70, 71, 72].

Compared with previously reported formaldehyde sensors, the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 sensor exhibits excellent repeatability and competitive room‐temperature sensing performance, as summarized in Figure S17 and Tables S1 and S2. As shown in Figure S17a, although its response magnitude is moderate compared with sensors operated at elevated temperatures (>100°C) or under external photoactivation such as UV/LED illumination, it compares favorably with true ambient‐condition systems that operate without heating or light activation. Notably, the sensor exhibits a rapid recovery time of only 19 s, placing it among the fastest reported for room‐temperature HCHO sensors and highlighting its suitability for real‐time monitoring applications. As shown in Figure S17b, the response time of WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 toward HCHO is also competitive with reported conductive MOF‐based sensors for various target gases. To the best of our knowledge, conductive MOF‐based heterojunction sensors specifically designed for selective room‐temperature HCHO detection have rarely been reported. Therefore, this work provides a meaningful demonstration of a WO3/conductive 2D Cu3(HHTP)2 heterojunction design strategy for room‐temperature HCHO sensing, emphasizing the role of interfacial electronic modulation in achieving selective and drift‐resistant sensor operation.

Gas Sensing Mechanism

As discussed above, the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 nanocomposite exhibits markedly enhanced performance relative to its individual components in terms of sensitivity, selectivity, and dynamic response toward HCHO. This improvement arises from the synergistic integration of the WO3 and Cu3(HHTP)2 components, wherein the high surface area and intrinsic porosity of Cu3(HHTP)2 promote efficient gas diffusion and offer numerous accessible adsorption sites. Concurrently, WO3, known for its surface reactivity and strong affinity toward oxygenated species, is presumed to facilitate favorable interfacial interactions with HCHO. These interactions, in turn, are likely to accelerate interfacial charge transfer processes, thereby amplifying the dynamic gas response of the nanocomposite.

The gas sensing mechanism can be understood based on the well‐established chemiresistive redox process for room‐temperature HCHO detection, in which surface‐adsorbed oxygen species react with HCHO and release electrons back to the semiconductor conduction pathway, thereby modulating the electron depletion layer (EDL) and sensor resistance [2, 12]. In ambient air, oxygen molecules adsorb onto the surface‐defect‐rich WO3 domains and the coordinatively unsaturated Cu‐ligand sites within Cu3(HHTP)2. These adsorbed oxygen species are converted into weakly ionized oxygen species (e.g., O2 − or O−), which extract electrons predominantly from the conduction band of WO3 (Figure 4a). This electron withdrawal significantly expands the electron depletion layer (EDL) at the WO3‐Cu3(HHTP)2 interface and increases the overall baseline resistance (Figure 4a,c). Due to the work‐function difference between Cu3(HHTP)2 (4.48 eV) and WO3 (5.01 eV), the Fermi level of Cu3(HHTP)2 lies higher than that of WO3 relative to the vacuum level. Upon contact, electrons are therefore expected to transfer from Cu3(HHTP)2 to WO3 until Fermi‐level equilibration is reached, generating interfacial charge redistribution and a built‐in electric field at the Cu3(HHTP)2 to WO3 interface (Figure 4c; Figure S18b), which is consistent with the charge density difference (CDD) and electrostatic potential analyses of the simplified WO3/cMOF interfacial model (Figure S19). This internal field further stabilizes and deepens the EDL on the WO3 side, making the system highly sensitive to subsequent surface charge modulation. Upon exposure to HCHO, the sensing process proceeds through two consecutive processes. First, HCHO molecules are preferentially adsorbed on oxygen‐vacancy‐rich WO3 sites and oxygen‐functionalized regions of the Cu3(HHTP)2 framework (Figure 4b). Subsequently, the adsorbed HCHO reacts with pre‐adsorbed ionized oxygen species, producing CO2 and H2O while releasing electrons back to the WO3‐based conduction pathway [12]. This electron release reduces the EDL, lowers the interfacial potential barrier, and consequently decreases the sensor resistance (Figure 4d). These adsorption and reaction steps are mutually connected and together account for the enhanced, selective, and reversible HCHO response of the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 nanocomposites [2, 12, 13]. Additionally, the XPS O 1s analysis reveals that the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 nanocomposite exhibits substantially higher concentrations of oxygen vacancies (OV) and adsorbed oxygen species (Oads) compared to pristine WO3. This observation confirms that incorporation of the cMOF introduces additional defect sites and enhances oxygen chemisorption. These defect‐rich surfaces facilitate the generation of reactive oxygen species and augment the electron‐withdrawing capability at the interface, effectively lowering the local valence‐band energy barrier and improving charge‐transfer efficiency. In addition, the reduced band gap of the nanocomposite (2.36 eV, Figure S18a) suggests more favorable charge‐carrier activation at room temperature. The 0.62 eV valence‐band shift observed in Figure S18c further supports band‐structure modulation and interfacial electronic interaction between WO3 and Cu3(HHTP)2. These features collectively promote efficient charge transfer associated with HCHO oxidation by pre‐adsorbed oxygen species, leading to pronounced EDL modulation, rapid electron return to the conduction pathway, and enhanced room‐temperature sensing response [61].

To deepen the mechanistic understanding of the observed specificity toward HCHO, beyond the macroscale electronic modulation induced by the WO3‐Cu3(HHTP)2 heterojunction, DFT calculations were performed. This theoretical analysis aimed to elucidate the molecular‐level origin of selective HCHO detection by examining how each component interacts electronically with various gas molecules. As an initial step, dipole moments of each gas molecule were calculated to assess their correlation with the sensing trend. However, as shown in Table S6, no clear relationship was found, indicating that molecular polarity alone cannot account for the observed selectivity. Instead, these results indicate that the enhanced response arises from analyte‐specific electronic interactions at the gas–solid interface, particularly involving charge transfer and local electronic structure modulation upon adsorption.

To elucidate the molecular‐level interactions in greater detail, we examined two plausible adsorption configurations for each gas molecule on the Cu3(HHTP)2 framework: one involving coordination to the Cu(II) metal centers and the other involving interaction with the phenolic O‐linker sites (Figure S20). Structural optimization and adsorption energy calculations show that the phenolic O‐linker is the most favorable adsorption site for HCHO (Figure 5a,). This site preference is not governed by direct coordination to the Cu(II) center. Rather, HCHO is stabilized at the O‐linker region through favorable local electrostatic interactions between its polar C═O group and the O‐rich π‐conjugated framework of Cu3(HHTP)2. This adsorption geometry enables HCHO to interact effectively with the framework while remaining spatially separated from the Cu(II) center, indicating that the selective adsorption is mainly driven by linker‐mediated electrostatic stabilization rather than direct metal‐site coordination.

As revealed by the optimized geometry of HCHO adsorption at the O‐linker (Figure S21), the HCHO molecule is located at a nonbonding distance from the Cu(II) center, which rules out the possibility of direct coordination. This spatial separation is corroborated by the CDD map (Figure 5e), which shows minimal electronic redistribution between the O atom of HCHO and the Cu(II) site, indicative of negligible orbital interaction. Consistently, COOP analysis (Figure S26) reveals minimal orbital overlap between Cu(II) and HCHO, thereby ruling out the formation of a coordination bond. Instead, the observed stabilization likely originates from electrostatic dipole‐lone pair interactions or weak hydrogen bonding between the partially positive C─H protons of HCHO and the phenolic O atoms in the linker. As displayed in Figure 5d, this noncovalent binding configuration induces a pronounced downward shift in the Fermi level (E F) of Cu3(HHTP)2, indicative of increased hole concentration within the framework. Such electronic modulation provides a rational explanation for the significant resistance drop observed experimentally upon HCHO exposure.

In contrast, NH3, which exhibited the second‐highest response among the tested gases, shows different adsorption behavior on the Cu3(HHTP)2 framework. As shown in Figures 6a and 5d, while NH3 exhibits comparable binding affinities toward both Cu(II) and O‐linker sites under low‐coverage conditions, its partial coordination to each site gives rise to opposing electronic effects. Notably, unlike HCHO, NH3 shows a nonnegligible orbital overlap with adjacent Cu(II) centers even when adsorbed in proximity to the O‐linker, as revealed by COOP analysis (Figure S26), suggesting residual electron donation pathways. This interaction, in conjunction with its direct coordination to Cu(II) centers, leads to an upward shift in the E F, which counteracts the E F‐lowering effect associated with adsorption at O‐linker sites. This compensation mechanism plausibly accounts for decreased sensing performance of the composite toward NH3 relative to HCHO.

Given that the overall sensing performance of the WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 composite intrinsically depends on the synergistic contributions from both constituents, it is essential to elucidate the gas adsorption behavior at the WO3 component as well. To this end, we investigated the most energetically favorable adsorption configurations of the target analytes on the O‐deficient (001) surface of monoclinic WO3 (WO3‐2VO), as illustrated in Figure S27. Interestingly, the calculated adsorption energy (E ads) of NH3 on the WO3 – 2VO surface is slightly more exothermic (−0.458 eV) than that of HCHO (−0.210 eV), a trend contrasting with experimental observations, wherein HCHO exposure induces a more substantial resistance decrease in both pristine WO3 and the hybrid system.

To reconcile this apparent discrepancy and establish a precise correlation between adsorption energetics and sensor response, we subsequently examined the evolution of the local electronic structure of WO3 upon gas adsorption, focusing in particular on the projected density of states (PDOS) of the W atoms at the adsorption sites. As depicted in Figure 6a and Figure S27, the incorporation of two O‐vacancies into pristine WO3 induces a pronounced reorganization of W‐centered electronic states. Notably, the prominent W 5d states near the conduction band (CB) edge are significantly reduced, accompanied by the emergence of a distinct and localized peak at approximately −2.5 eV within the valence band (VB). This spectral redistribution clearly indicates the formation of vacancy‐induced donor states, arising from lattice distortions and local symmetry breaking at the defect sites. Concurrently, subtle yet noticeable enhancements in the contributions from W 6s and 6p orbitals are observed, consistent with electronic reconfiguration due to the altered local coordination environments.

Upon NH3 adsorption onto the WO3 – 2VO (001) surface, a slightly enhanced contribution of W 5d is observed near the CB edge, accompanied by minor increases in the W 6s and 6p orbitals. These spectral changes indicate partial delocalization and stabilization of vacancy‐induced donor states, arising from electronic interaction between NH3 molecules and the undercoordinated W centers (Figure 5d). In addition, a new localized electronic state appears at approximately −2.08 eV in the DOS of the NH3‐WO3 system (Figure S27d), which can be attributed to orbital hybridization between the lone pair electrons of NH3 and the W 5d orbitals, forming a W‐N σ‐type bonding state within the upper VB. Simultaneously, as shown in Figure 6b and Figure S28, the E F shifts downward from −1.809 to −1.887 eV, reflecting a redistribution of electronic density away from the CB edge. This E F shift, together with the spectral suppression of mid‐gap defect states, indicates a decrease in the conduction electron density, consistent with the experimentally observed increase in resistance upon NH3 exposure.

In contrast, HCHO adsorption induces a more substantial upshift in the E F to −1.602 eV, along with a moderate enhancement of W 5d states near the CB minimum, as illustrated in Figure 6b. Furthermore, a noticeable increase in the density of unoccupied states is observed just above the E F, indicating enhanced availability of thermally accessible conduction band states, which is expected to facilitate carrier delocalization and improve charge transport properties. Notably, the vacancy‐induced localized state previously centered around −2.6 eV is substantially weakened, and the 5d orbital contributions from W1 and W2 in this region are correspondingly diminished. This spectral suppression implies partial passivation or modification of the defect‐related states, likely resulting from electronic coupling between the HCHO adsorbate and the undercoordinated W sites (Figure 5d). Collectively, these spectral features indicate that HCHO adsorption perturbs the defect‐related electronic structure and results in a redistribution of states near the CB edge, consistent with enhanced electronic transport and increased conductivity. Thus, the superior HCHO selectivity is not dictated simply by adsorption strength, but by the more efficient conversion of HCHO adsorption into interfacial charge modulation, enabled by its small molecular size and polar carbonyl functionality. Overall, these results demonstrate that the combined O‐rich adsorption environment of Cu3(HHTP)2, OV‐related active sites of WO3, and interfacial electronic coupling collectively govern the enhanced HCHO sensing performance of the nanocomposite. This work provides critical mechanistic insights that can inform the rational design of future heterostructure‐based sensing platforms for selective and high‐sensitivity gas detection under practical conditions.

Taken together, these findings comprehensively elucidate how the integration of Cu3(HHTP)2 and WO3 within the nanocomposite, through both component‐specific functionalities and interfacial electronic coupling, governs the markedly enhanced gas sensing performance toward HCHO. This work provides critical mechanistic insights that can inform the rational design of future heterostructure‐based sensing platforms for selective and high‐sensitivity gas detection under practical conditions.

Conclusion

In summary, we have developed a WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2 heterostructure‐based chemiresistive sensor that enables ultra‐sensitive and selective detection of HCHO at room temperature. The device exhibits an exceptionally low detection limit of 48 ppb, with rapid response (106.35 s) and recovery (19.38 s) kinetics at 20 ppm. In addition to its high sensitivity, the sensor demonstrates remarkable operational stability, retaining >95% of its initial signal over 8 weeks, and outstanding reproducibility across 20 consecutive cycles (CV = 2.67%). Notably, it maintains high selectivity even in the presence of interfering gas species, reflecting its potential for reliable HCHO monitoring in complex environments. These advanced sensing characteristics originate from the rational integration of WO3 and Cu3(HHTP)2, wherein the high surface area and electronic conductivity of the MOF, combined with the surface reactivity and abundant binding sites of WO3, synergistically amplify gas‐solid interactions. The work‐function difference and interfacial charge redistribution establish a built‐in‐field‐like potential modulation. DFT calculations further reveal that HCHO preferentially interacts with phenolic O‐sites in the MOF and O‐deficient sites on WO3, inducing Fermi level modulation, suppression of mid‐gap defect states, and enhanced carrier delocalization. Collectively, these findings underscore the critical role of interfacial electronic engineering in enabling selective, stable, and high‐performance gas sensing under ambient conditions. Taken together, this study presents a comprehensive design framework for constructing heterostructure‐based gas sensors through interfacial electronic modulation and site‐specific adsorption, offering a viable route toward highly sensitive and selective VOC detection under ambient conditions.

Experimental Section

Chemicals

Tungsten(VI) chloride (WCl6), trimesic acid (BTC, Benzene‐1,3,5‐tricarboxylic acid), formaldehyde solution (∼37%), copper(II) acetate monohydrate (Cu(Ac)2.H2O), N,N‐Dimethylformamide (ACS reagent, ≥ 99.8%), ethanol, and acetone were purchased from Merck. Triphenylene‐2,3,6,7,10,11‐hexaol hydrate (≥ 95%, HHTP) was purchased from TCI chemicals.

Synthesis of WO3

Trimesic acid(263 mg) was dissolved in 40 mL of deionized water (DI) and 20 mL of ethanol with sonication for 30 min, and then 245 mg of WCl6 was added to the above solution under sonication for 30 min. Subsequently, 5 mL of formaldehyde solution was added and stirred for 60 min. After continuous stirring for 60 min, the obtained solution was loaded into Teflon‐lined stainless autoclaves and heated at 180°C for 12 h. The as‐made samples were then collected after washing (1000 rpm) for 5 times by DI water and dried overnight at 60°C. Ultimately, the resulting colloidal powder was transferred to a muffle furnace and calcined at 500°C for 2 h to remove organic matter.

Synthesis of Cu3(HHTP)2 and WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2

Into a glass vial capped with a cap, 19.54 mg of HHTP was added in 20 mL of DI water and 40 mL of DMF with sonication of 30 min. Then, to the above solution, 23.95 mg of Cu(Ac)2.H2O was added with a further ultrasonic bath for 30 min. Subsequently, the vial was transferred to a constant temperature oven set at 85°C for a reaction period of 24 h. After the reaction, the resulting dark blue product was washed several times with both DI water and acetone, followed by drying in a vacuum oven at 40°C for 24 h. For the synthesis of WO3@Cu3(HHTP)2, 30 mg of WO3 powder and 19.54 mg of HHTP was added in 20 mL of DI water and 40 mL of DMF with sonication for 60 min to fully disperse the WO3. After that, the same procedure was followed as for the synthesis of Cu3(HHTP)2 2D cMOF.

Fabrication of the Gas Sensor Device and Measurement

All the dried powder samples were mixed with ethanol to create a homogeneous thick solution, which was dropped onto a SiO2 substrate with Cr/Au interdigital electrodes using a micropipette to form a uniform gas sensing layer. The obtained gas sensor was aged at 25°C for 24 h to improve thermal stability. The devices were then placed in a Nextron 100 cc microprobe station connected to a Keithley 2400 multimeter that controlled the voltage with a temperature controller. The mass flow controller maintained a constant total flow of the gas mixture (100 sccm). A commercially available gas mixture (balanced in N2; Air Korea Co., Ltd., Korea) served as the source gas. To achieve the desired target concentrations, the target gas was further diluted with dry air (synthetic air) using two mass flow controllers (MFCs). One MFC regulated the flow of the source gas, while the other controlled the flow of the diluent air. Characterization

The crystal structures were examined using an X‐ray diffraction (XRD) (Rigaku SmartLab) analyzer with Cu Kαradiation (40 kV, 100 mA) at a scanning speed of 0.5°/min. The average crystallite sizes were estimated from XRD peak broadening using the Scherrer equation. The detailed analysis parameters and calculation methodology are provided in the Supporting Information file (Section S1 and Table S3). The morphologies and microstructures of the samples were investigated using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Tecnai F20, FEI, USA) operated at 200 kV with Aster (NanoMegas, Belgium). High‐resolution TEM (HRTEM) image analysis was performed using a TEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Titan G2 Cube 60–300) at 80 kV. Raman spectroscopy (Horiba) analysis was performed using a 514 nm laser. The absorbance spectra were analyzed by an ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectrophotometer (Jasco, V‐670). BET surface area, BJH, and NLDFT pore size distribution were analyzed using a nitrogen adsorption analyzer (BELSORP MINI X, MicrotracBEL, Japan) at 77 K. X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (K‐alpha, Thermo Scientific) was measured with monochromatic Al Kα (1486.6 eV) X‐rays. The work function was calculated based on Ultraviolet photoelectron spectra (UPS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Escalab 250 Xi, He 21.2 eV).

Computational Methods

Our first‐principles calculations of the structural and electronic properties of the WO3 were performed using the density functional theory (DFT) as implemented in the Cambridge Serial Total Energy Package (CASTEP). The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) of Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) was adopted for the exchange‐correlation functional. The ultrasoft pseudopotentials were employed to describe electron‐ion interactions, and van der Waals interactions were included using the Tkatchenko–Scheffler semiempirical dispersion correction. We set the cutoff energy to 500 eV, the energy tolerance to 10–6 eV, and the maximum force convergence criterion to 0.01 eV Å−1. The Monkhorst–Pack k‐point grid sampling was 10 × 10 × 10 for a primitive WO3 and 3 × 3 × 1 for the surfaces. A vacuum layer of 20 Å along the z‐axis for each slab was added to decouple possible periodic interactions. Geometry optimization and vibrational frequency analyses for various gas molecules were carried out at the B3LYP/6‐311+G(d,2p) and ωb97xd/aug‐cc‐pvtz levels of theory. DFT calculations for Cu3(HHTP)2 and its gas‐adsorbed complexes were performed using the M06‐2X functional with GD3BJ dispersion corrections, employing the 6–311+g(d,p) basis set for C, H, O, and N atoms, and the LANL2DZ basis set for Cu.
