Spanning components and surfaces under minimum vertex degree
Jack Allsop, Ander Lamaison, Richard Lang, Silas Rathke

Abstract
. We study minimum vertex-degree conditions in 3 3 -uniform hypergraphs for (tight) spanning components and (combinatorial) surfaces. Our main results show that a 3 3 -uniform hypergraph G G on n n vertices contains a spanning component if δ 1 ​ ( G ) ≳ 1 2 ​ ( n 2 ) \delta_{1}(G)\gtrsim\tfrac{1}{2}\binom{n}{2} and a spanning copy of any surface if δ 1 ​ ( G ) ≳ 5 9 ​ ( n 2 ) \delta_{1}(G)\gtrsim\tfrac{5}{9}\binom{n}{2} , which in both cases is asymptotically optimal. This extends the work of Georgakopoulos, Haslegrave, Montgomery, and Narayanan who determined the corresponding minimum codegree conditions in this setting.

1.
Introduction

Our starting point is Dirac’s theorem
[
1
]
, which determines optimal minimum degree conditions that guarantee a Hamilton cycle.
We study the generalisation of this problem to hypergraphs focusing on the notions of (tight) connectivity and combinatorial surfaces.

1.1.
Spanning components

We begin with a discussion of connectivity in hypergraphs.
To this end, let us introduce some terminology.
A
k
-uniform hypergraph
(or
k
-graph
for short)
G
consists of a set
V(G)
of vertices and a set
E(G)
of edges, where each edge is a
k
-set in
V(G)
.
We write
e(G)=|E(G)|
.
For
1\leq d<k
, the
minimum
d
-degree
\delta_{d}(G)
of
G
is the maximum integer
m
such that every set of
d
vertices is in at least
m
edges. We refer to
\delta_{1}
simply as the
minimum (vertex) degree
and to
\delta_{k-1}
as the
minimum codegree
.
We remark that minimum
d
-degree conditions are structurally weaker as
d
decreases.
It is therefore usually easier to determine optimal codegree conditions than minimum (vertex) degree conditions.

The
line graph
of a
k
-graph
G
is the
2
-graph on vertex set
E(G)
with an edge
ef
whenever
|e\cap f|=k-1
.
A subgraph of
G
is
(tightly) connected
if it has no isolated vertices and its edges induce a connected subgraph in
L(G)
.
Moreover, we refer to edge-maximal connected subgraphs as
(tight) components
.
A component is
(vertex) spanning
in
G
if it contains all vertices of
G
.

It is a classical and simple fact that all
2
-graphs with minimum degree at least
(n-1)/2
have a spanning component, and that this minimum degree threshold is optimal.
Similarly, it is a simple exercise to show that a codegree of
n/3
in a
3
-graph results in a spanning component
[
3
]
.
However, despite being a fundamental problem, the minimum codegree threshold for the existence of a spanning component is unknown for
k
-graphs with
k\geq 4
(see also
4.3
).
In the setting of minimum vertex degrees, it was conjectured by Illingworth, Lang, Müyesser, Parczyk, and Sgueglia
[
4
]
that a minimum degree of asymptotically
\frac{4}{9}\binom{n}{2}
suffices to force a spanning component in a
3
-graph.
This conjecture turns out to be incorrect as the following lemma shows (see also the construction in
fig.

1
):

X
Y

.

3
-graph without a spanning component. The triangles indicate which
3

Lemma 1.1
.

For every positive integer
n
, there is a
3
-graph
G
on
n
vertices with
\delta_{1}(G)=\frac{1}{2}\binom{n}{2}-O(n)
that does not have a spanning component.

On the positive side, we are able to determine the (asymptotically) best possible minimum degree threshold for the existence of a spanning component in a
3
-graph.
The following is our first main result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Every
3
-graph
G
on
n
vertices with
\delta_{1}(G)\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\binom{n-1}{2}+1
contains a spanning component.

1.2.
Spanning surfaces

Dirac’s famous theorem asserts that any
2
-graph with minimum degree at least
n/2
contains a spanning cycle.
Furthermore, this minimum degree threshold is best possible.
Much work has been done generalising Dirac’s theorem to
k
-graphs for
k\geq 3
.
In the following, we focus on tight Hamiltonicity, which is defined as follows. A
(tight) cycle

C
in
k
-graph
G
is a subgraph whose vertices can be cyclically ordered such that the edge set of
C
consists of all sets of
k
consecutive vertices in this ordering.
Moreover,
C
is
Hamilton
if it contains all vertices of
G
.

A natural way to generalise Dirac’s theorem to
k
-graphs is to consider minimum codegree thresholds for the existence of a Hamilton cycle.
This problem has been asymptotically solved for all values of
k
by Rödl, Ruciński, and Szemerédi
[
12
]
.
The analogous problem for vertex degrees was solved by Reiher, Rödl, Ruciński, Schacht, and Szemerédi
[
11
]
for
k=3
:

Theorem 1.3
.

For every
\epsilon>0
, there is a positive integer
n_{0}
such that every
3
-graph
G
on
n\geq n_{0}
vertices with
\delta_{1}(G)\geq(5/9+\epsilon)\binom{n}{2}
contains a Hamilton cycle.

More recently, Lang, Schacht, and Volec
[
9
]
determined the vertex degree threshold for
k=4
.
We note that there are other notions of Hamiltonicity in hypergraphs and results in the same spirit have been proved for them.
For more details, see, for example, the survey of Simonovits and Szemerédi
[
14
]
.

We now turn to a geometric variant of Hamiltonicity.
A
(k-1)
-dimensional (combinatorial) sphere
in a
k
-graph
G
is a subgraph
H\subseteq G
such that the simplicial complex induced by these edges of
H
is homeomorphic to the
(k-1)
-dimensional sphere
\mathbb{S}^{k-1}
.
We say that
H
is a
(vertex) spanning copy of
\mathbb{S}^{k-1}
in
G
if it contains all vertices of
G
. Now assume that
k=3
and let
\mathcal{S}
be a connected, closed
2
-manifold (or
surface
for short). We say that
G
contains a
spanning copy of
\mathcal{S}
if there is a vertex-spanning subgraph
H\subseteq G
whose edge set induces a simplicial complex that is homeomorphic to
\mathcal{S}
.

The edge set of a cycle in a
2
-graph induces a simplicial complex that is homeomorphic to the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. This observation motivates another generalisation of Dirac’s theorem due to Gowers (see discussion in
[
2
]
): For
1\leq d<k
, what is the minimum
d
-degree threshold for the existence of a spanning copy of a sphere in a
k
-graph?
Georgakopoulos, Haslegrave, Montgomery, and Narayanan
[
2
]
recently considered this problem and gave an asymptotically best possible minimum codegree threshold for when
k=3
. In fact, they found the minimum codegree threshold for the existence of a spanning copy of a given surface
\mathcal{S}
in a
3
-graph.

Theorem 1.4
.

For every surface
\mathcal{S}
and
\epsilon>0
, there is a positive integer
n_{0}
such that any
3
-graph
G
on
n\geq n_{0}
vertices with
\delta_{2}(G)\geq(1/3+\epsilon)n
contains a spanning copy of
\mathcal{S}
.

We remark that a related result in the graph setting was proved by Kühn, Osthus, and Taraz
[
5
]
.
Recently, Illingworth, Lang, Müyesser, Parczyk, and Sgueglia
[
4
]
determined minimum positive codegree thresholds for spanning spheres.

Motivated by
theorem

1.4
, we study minimum vertex degree conditions for containing a spanning copy of a given surface.
Our second main result determines the threshold as follows:

Theorem 1.5
.

For every surface
\mathcal{S}
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a positive integer
n_{0}
such that every
3
-graph
G
on
n\geq n_{0}
vertices with
\delta_{1}(G)\geq(5/9+\varepsilon)\binom{n}{2}
contains a spanning copy of
\mathcal{S}
.

Furthermore, the minimum degree condition in
theorem

1.5
is asymptotically optimal, as the following lemma shows.

Lemma 1.6
.

For every surface
\mathcal{S}
, there is a
3
-graph
G
on
n
vertices with
\delta_{1}(G)\geq\frac{5}{9}\binom{n}{2}-o(n^{2})
that does not contain a spanning copy of
\mathcal{S}
.

1.3.
Spanning spheres

Our last main result bounds the thresholds for spanning spheres by the threshold for Hamilton frameworks.
Let
\delta_{d}^{\hs}(k)
be the infimum over all
\delta\in[0,1]
such that for every
\varepsilon>0
, there is a positive integer
n_{0}
such that every
k
-graph
G
on
n\geq n_{0}
vertices with
\delta_{d}(G)\geq(\delta+\varepsilon)\binom{n-d}{k-d}
contains a spanning copy of the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{k-1}
.
We defer the formal definition of the threshold for Hamilton frameworks, denoted by
\delta_{d}^{\hf}(k)
, to
section

3.1
.
For now, we just remark that
\delta_{d}^{\hf}(k)
is closely related to the threshold for tight Hamiltonicity, and the two currently agree whenever the latter is known.
In particular,
\delta_{1}^{\hf}(3)=5/9
in accord with
theorem

1.3
.

Theorem 1.7
.

For every
1\leq d<k
, we have
\delta_{d}^{\hs}(k)\leq\delta_{d}^{\hf}(k)
.

In

Let

Consider a vertex

So in particular,

Since

For the sake of contradiction, let us assume that

Let

Evidently,

In the following, we distinguish between the cases

Case 1:

Fix a vertex

Let

Since

, the link graph

has at least

\displaystyle e\big(L^{\prime}(x)\big)+e\big(L^{\prime}(y)\big)

\displaystyle>2\cdot\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\binom{n-1}{2}+1-\frac{n}{2}\right)=\binom{n-2}{2}

\displaystyle=e\big(\overline{L^{\prime}(x)}\cap\overline{L^{\prime}(y)}\big)+e\big(L^{\prime}(x)\big)+e\big(L^{\prime}(y)\big)-e\big(L^{\prime}(x)\cap L^{\prime}(y)\big)\,.

We can conclude

(2.1)

e\big(L^{\prime}(x)\cap L^{\prime}(y)\big)>e\big(\overline{L^{\prime}(x)}\cap\overline{L^{\prime}(y)}\big)\,.

We define

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle\coloneqq\big\{v\in V^{\prime}\colon v\not\in V(C_{x}),v\not\in V(C_{y})\big\}\,.

We denote the sizes of the sets

,

, and

, and

, respectively.
Because of

By the assumption on the minimum degree, there is an edge

, that is,

.
We show now that

. Suppose

. Then there is a

, but then we also have that

and

are in

. Thus,

and in the same way one can show that

. Hence,

and, similarly,

. In particular, this implies that for any

. This also implies

Next, consider a pair

\displaystyle\binom{\delta(n-1)}{2}=\delta^{2}\binom{n-1}{2}-\frac{(1-\delta)\delta}{2}(n-1)\,.

Now observe that at most

\displaystyle\geq e(C_{x}^{\prime})+e(C_{y}^{\prime})+e(C_{z}^{\prime})

\displaystyle>n-1+\left(\big(2\alpha-\alpha^{2}\big)+\big(2\beta-\beta^{2}\big)+\big(2\gamma-\gamma^{2}\big)-\frac{3}{2}\right)\binom{n-1}{2}\,.

\displaystyle g(A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\binom{\alpha(n-1)}{2}+\binom{\beta(n-1)}{2}+\binom{\gamma(n-1)}{2}

\displaystyle\quad-\binom{(\alpha+\beta-1)(n-1)}{2}

\displaystyle\quad-\binom{(\beta+\gamma-1)(n-1)}{2}

\displaystyle\quad-\binom{(\gamma+\alpha-1)(n-1)}{2}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{eq:pull out})}{=}\left(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2}-(\alpha+\beta-1)^{2}-(\beta+\gamma-1)^{2}+(\gamma+\alpha-1)^{2}\right)\binom{n-1}{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(-\frac{(1-\alpha)\alpha}{2}-\frac{(1-\beta)\beta}{2}-\frac{(1-\gamma)\gamma}{2}\right)(n-1)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{(2-\alpha-\beta)(\alpha+\beta-1)}{2}(n-1)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{(2-\beta-\gamma)(\beta+\gamma-1)}{2}(n-1)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{(2-\gamma-\alpha)(\gamma+\alpha-1)}{2}(n-1)

\displaystyle=f_{3}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\binom{n-1}{2}+f_{4}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)(n-1)\,,

where

f_{3}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\coloneq\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2}-(\alpha+\beta-1)^{2}-(\beta+\gamma-1)^{2}+(\gamma+\alpha-1)^{2}

and

\displaystyle f_{4}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)

\displaystyle\coloneq-\frac{(1-\alpha)\alpha}{2}-\frac{(1-\beta)\beta}{2}-\frac{(1-\gamma)\gamma}{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{(2-\alpha-\beta)(\alpha+\beta-1)}{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{(2-\beta-\gamma)(\beta+\gamma-1)}{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{(2-\gamma-\alpha)(\gamma+\alpha-1)}{2}\,.

Combining this with the right side of (

\displaystyle f_{1}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\binom{n-1}{2}+f_{2}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)(n-1)<f_{3}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\binom{n-1}{2}+f_{4}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)(n-1)\,.

We will derive a contradiction by showing the following two claims.

Claim 2.1
.

We have
f_{1}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\geq f_{3}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
.

Proof of the claim.

For sake of contradiction, suppose that
f_{1}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)<f_{3}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
.
Rearranging gives

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle>2(\alpha\beta+\beta\gamma+\gamma\alpha)-2(\alpha+\beta+\gamma)+3/2

\displaystyle=2(\alpha-1/2)(\beta-1/2)+2(\beta-1/2)(\gamma-1/2)+2(\alpha-1/2)(\gamma-1/2)\,.

However, this contradicts the fact that
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\gamma
are each at least
1/2
.
∎

Claim 2.2
.

We have
f_{2}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\geq f_{4}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
.

Proof of the claim.

To begin, observe that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq 3-\frac{3}{2}(\alpha+\beta+\gamma)\,,

because
\alpha+\beta+\gamma\leq 2
.
Moreover,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\beta+\alpha\gamma+\beta\gamma-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+\beta+\gamma)\,,

since
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\geq\frac{1}{2}
implies
\alpha\beta\geq\frac{1}{2}\alpha
,
\beta\gamma\geq\frac{1}{2}\beta
, and
\gamma\alpha\geq\frac{1}{2}\gamma
.

Now assume contrariwise that

Simplifying and rearranging gives

0>3+\alpha\beta+\alpha\gamma+\beta\gamma-2(\alpha+\beta+\gamma)\,.

But this contradicts at least one of the above inequalities.

By combining these two claims, we obtain a contradiction to (

Case 3:

Following the same steps as in case 2, we can derive the analogue of (

3.1.
Spanning frameworks

The purpose of this subsection is to state a result that allows us to embed vertex-spanning blow-ups of cycles.

A
(tight) path

k

is a subgraph whose vertices can be (non-cyclically) ordered such that the edge set of

consists of all sets of
k

Let

k

As we shall see, (tight) Hamiltonicity requires three structural features:

Connectivity has been defined in Section

Let

A

.
We say that

. We call

A

between

-graphs

is a function

that maps edges to edges. A

is the image of a homomorphism of a
k
-uniform cycle

. The

of

is the order of

.
We call

if it contains a closed walk whose order is congruent to

It is easy to see that any

-uniform cycle is connected and spacious.
Moreover, in the Dirac-setting, we are typically interested in families

of Hamiltonian

-graphs that contain elements of all orders

.
In this case,

Extremal constructions of such families tend to also satisfy a fourth property, which ensures a form of

Definition 3.1

A family

-vertex subgraph

(1)

is a component, (connectivity)

(2)

(3)

It is not true that every member of a family of

Proof of

Consider a partition with parts

4.

Connectivity in hypergraphs has thus far mainly been studied as a by-product for more complicated substructures such as Hamilton cycles.
However, the notion is interesting enough to warrant investigation in its own right.
