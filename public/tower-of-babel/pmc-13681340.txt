Polymeric Polyaldehyde Approach to Develop Robust Dynamic Covalent Adaptive Polyimine Networks
Authors: Subrata Dolui, Sofia Nieves Casillas‐Popova, Seung Ki Lee, Sun Ju Kim, Hira Rasheed, Zhibin Ye, Minsoo P. Kim, Hyun Wook Jung, Jung Kwon Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPolyimine‐based covalent adaptive networks crosslinked with Schiff bases (PI‐CANs) have been developed as effective building blocks to construct advanced polymeric materials possessing reprocessability, self‐healability, and sustainability. Most approaches to fabricate PI‐CANs involve the synthesis and use of small molecule polyaldehydes. Herein, we report a versatile approach exploring the synthesis of polymeric polyaldehydes (PP‐CHO) with a polyacrylate backbone bearing pendant aldehyde groups to develop self‐healable, reprocessable polyacrylate‐PI hybrid CANs. The approach centers on the synthesis of well‐defined poly(2‐hydroxyethyl acrylate) bearing aldehyde pendants (PHEA‐CHO) by a photo‐indued reversible deactivation radical polymerization,(Photo‐RDRP) followed by post‐polymerization modification. The PHEA‐CHO is highly reactive to an aliphatic diamine, enabling the fabrication of transparent, integrated, dynamic PHEA‐PI hybrid CAN films. They exhibit lower activation energy (Ea = 14.6 kcal/mol) for stress relaxation even at mild temperature (50°C – 80°C) and good self‐healability and reprocessability as retaining their mechanical strengths upon a recycle. Promisingly, they degrade to corresponding aldehyde and amine precursors through acid‐catalyzed hydrolysis of benzoic imine crosslinks, offering potential for sustainability as recyclable dynamic covalent systems. These results suggest that the PP‐CHO approach is versatile for the development of dynamic PI‐CAN materials capable of the built‐in ability for extended operation and enhanced reliability.

Introduction

The development of covalent adaptive networks (CANs) has been explored as a promising platform in the construction of advanced polymeric materials with multifunctionality and sustainability. The common approach to fabricate CAN polymers involves the incorporation of dynamic covalent chemistry into the design of conventional crosslinked thermosets, which allows them to be crosslinked with dynamic covalent bonds [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. These dynamic bonds are capable of reversible exchange (associative mechanism) or dissociation/association (dissociative mechanism) in response to an external stimulus such as heat or light. Hence, CAN polymers overcome the critical drawbacks of conventional thermosets, particularly reprocessing and self‐healing abilities, thus improving their life and service time, as taking the advantages of conventional thermosets like higher mechanical strength, excellent dimensional stability, and good solvent resistance [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11].

Imine, disulfide, boronic ester, and hindered urea as well as Diels‐Alder cycloadduct are typical dynamic covalent bonds [12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. In particular, imine, especially aromatic imine (called Schiff base) bond, is the subject of interest over the years, as this bond can undergo a reversible exchange reaction between primary amine and aldehyde groups through imine metathesis under mild conditions (<80°C) [17, 18, 19, 20]. Owing to unique features, polyimine‐based CANs (PI‐CANs) have been recently used for electronic applications [21] to develop energy harvesting nanogenerators [22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27] and rechargeable batteries [28, 29, 30] requiring self‐healing and dynamic ability. For the development of effective PI‐CANs, most approaches have explored the synthesis and use of small molecule polyaldehydes reacting with polyamines for step‐growth polycondensations through in situ formation of benzoic imine bonds. An approach involves difunctional aldehydes (dialdehydes, functionality = 2), typically including terephthaldehyde and its derivatives [31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38], 2,5‐furandicarboxaldehyde [39, 40], vanillin dimers [41, 42, 43, 44], and aldehyde‐terminated polybutadiene rubber [45], which react with polyamines (functionality > 3). The other approach utilizes trifunctional aldehydes (trialdehyde, functionality = 3) such as 1,3,5‐triformylbenzene [46, 47, 48], perfluoropyridine trimers [49, 50], and furan‐derived trimer [51] as well as tetrafunctional aldehydes (tetraaldehyde, functionality = 4) [52], which are formulated with polyamines typically including diamines. Further to small molecule polyaldehydes, another approach explores polymeric polyaldehydes bearing pendant aldehyde groups. Their mechanical and dynamic properties could be tuned with varying nature of backbones, number of aldehyde pendants, and extent of crosslinking. Naturally‐occurring polymers such as polysaccharides have been randomly oxidized with oxidizing agents such as sodium periodate to convert backbone glucose rings to pendant aldehyde groups; however, their polycondensation have resulted in the fabrication of hydrophilic hydrogel networks [53, 54, 55, 56]. The application with synthetic polymeric polyaldehydes has been rarely studied [57, 58, 59].

Another interesting feature of aromatic imine is its degradability to its corresponding primary amine and aldehyde precursors through acid‐catalyzed hydrolysis [60, 61]. The degradation kinetics in the presence of an acid can be controlled with the choice of substituents attached to imine bonds [62, 63]. These features allow for upcycling monomers and precursors as well as closed‐loop recycling through acid‐catalyzed degradation and depolymerization of PI‐CAN thermosets [64, 65, 66].

Herein, we report our proof‐of‐concept investigation into the polymeric polyaldehyde (PP‐CHO) approach with a polyacrylate backbone to develop self‐healable, reprocessable polyacrylate‐PI hybrid CANs. A well‐defined PP‐CHO based on poly(2‐hydroxyethyl acrylate) (called PHEA‐CHO) was synthesized by photo‐indued reversible deactivation radical polymerization (RDRP), followed by a facile conjugation through the esterification of hydroxyl pendants with 4‐formylbenzoic acid. As illustrated in Scheme 1, the synthesized PHEA‐CHO was examined for step‐growth polycondensation with a diamine, yielding PHEA‐PI hybrid CAN materials. The networks were characterized for optical, thermal, and mechanical properties as well as dynamic properties and mechanisms. Furthermore, their acid‐catalyzed degradation was examined to demonstrate the feasibility of PHEA‐CANs to convert to chemically recycled PI materials. These results suggest the great potential of the PP‐CHO approach with versatility to develop dynamic and sustainable polymeric materials.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis of Well‐Defined PHEA‐CHO

Figure 1a illustrates our approach exploring a post‐polymerization modification with two steps to synthesize well‐defined PHEA‐CHO. First step involves a photo‐induced RDRP through atom transfer radical polymerization (ATRP) of HEA to synthesize well‐defined PHEA. Adapted from the literature [67], RDRP was initiated with ethyl α‐bromoisobutyrate (EBiB) in the presence of a Cu(II)Br2/tris[2‐(dimethylamino)ethyl]amine (Me6TREN) complex in DMSO upon the irradiation of UV light at λ = 370 nm. Under the condition of [EBiB]o/[Cu(II)Br2]o/[Me6TREN]o = 1/0.02/0.12, the initial mole ratio of [HEA]0/[EBiB]0 was set to be 20/1, which corresponds to the targeting degree of polymerization (DP) at complete monomer conversion to be 20. Monomer conversion reached 95% in 40 min. After being purified by precipitation from diethyl ether, the synthesized PHEA was characterized for its structure by 1H NMR spectroscopy and molecular weight by gel permeation chromatography (GPC). 1H NMR in Figure 1b shows the characteristic peaks at 1.50 and 1.62 ppm corresponding to methylene protons adjacent to ester and hydroxyl group, respectively, and 2.30 ppm to methine proton on backbone, presenting PHEA. In addition to those peaks, the peak at 1.00 ppm corresponding to methyl group in the initiating spices appeared. Their integral ratio allowed for the determination of the DP of PHEA to be 20, which is close to theoretically estimated DP = 19 at monomer conversion of 95%. GPC diagram in Figure 1c shows monomodal distribution with molecular weight as number average molecular weight (Mn) = 5400 g/mol and molecular weight distribution as narrow as Đ = 1.07, calibrated with poly(methyl methacrylate) standards. These results confirm the synthesis of well‐defined PHEA20 having 20 OH pendants by a photo‐induced ATRP.

Synthesis of a well‐defined PHEA‐CHO by a post‐polymerization modification approach with two steps including (i) photo‐induced ATRP of HEA and ii) a facile conjugation of PHEA with 4‐FB (a). 1H NMR spectra (b) and GPC diagrams (c) of PHEA precursor and PHEA‐CHO. Condition for photo‐induced ATRP: [HEA]o/[EBiB]o/[Cu(II)Br2]o/[Me6TREN]o = 20/1/0.02/0.12, HEA/DMSO = 1/1 v/v, and [HEA]o = 50 mM.

Given the synthesis and characterization of well‐defined PHEA, the next step involves the conjugation of PHEA with 4‐formylbenzoic acid (4‐FB) through a carbodiimide‐mediated coupling reaction with N‐(3‐dimethylaminopropyl)‐N′‐ethylcarbodiimide (EDC) in dichloromethane, yielding PHEA‐CHO having aldehyde pendants. After our trial‐to‐error experiments, the amounts of 4‐FB and EDC were optimized to be 2.50 and 2.75 mole equivalents to OH groups, respectively. 1H NMR spectrum in Figure 1b shows the characteristic peaks at 10.0 ppm corresponding to aldehyde protons, 4.2–4.5 ppm to two methylene groups adjacent to ester groups. Using their integral ratio, the efficiency of conjugation was estimated to be >98%. GPC diagram in Figure 1c shows that the molecular weight distribution of PHEA was shifted to higher molecular weight region, with no trace of PHEA and its Mn increased to 7500 g/mol with Đ = 1.12. These results confirm the successful synthesis of well‐defined PHEA‐CHO by the post‐polymerization modification.

PHEA‐CHO was further characterized for its thermal properties by differential scanning colorimetry (DSC) analysis (see Figure 3g). The DSC thermogram shows a single transition at 26.5°C, which appears to be greater than that (≈15.2°C) of PHEA reported in literature [68]. The plausible reason could be due to the aromatic ring pendants with aldehyde group to reduce free volume of the PHEA‐CHO chains.

Model Studies for Benzoic Imine Formation

To get an insight into the reactivity of the synthesized PHEA‐CHO toward the formation of benzoic imine bonds, as illustrated in Figure 2a (left scheme), a model study was conducted with hexadecylamine (C16H35NH2) an aliphatic amine, using 1H NMR spectroscopy. They were mixed at 1/1 mole equivalent ratio of CHO/amine in THF for 10 min at room temperature. As seen in Figure S1, 1H NMR spectrum of the mixture shows the peak at 10 ppm that presents unreacted CHO groups and the peaks at 4.0–4.5 ppm presenting two methylene groups. Their integration ratio was used to determine the extent of imine formation to be 98%, suggesting their high reactivity toward the formation of imine bonds.

Fabrication and Film Formation of Dynamic PHEA‐PI CANs

As depicted in Figure 2a (right scheme), the synthesized PHEA‐CHO having 20 pendant CHO groups was examined as a polymeric polyaldehyde for a diamine, allowing for the fabrication of dynamic PHEA‐PI CANs through in situ formation of benzoic imine bonds between pendant CHO and primary amine groups. Priamine 1074, a bio‐based aliphatic diamine manufactured by Cargill, was examined in our proof‐of‐concept investigation where the mole equivalent of CHO/amine was set to be 1/1, which corresponds to 50/50 wt.% ratio of PHEA‐CHO/ Priamine 1074. When PHEA‐CHO was mixed with Priamine 1074 in anhydrous tetrahydrofuran (THF) at 10 wt.%, the reactive mixtures quickly turned to standing gels in vials within 3 min at room temperature (see the digital image in Figure S2). Additionally, rapid gelation was observed even at zero degree or at as low as 5 wt.% of reactive precursors in THF. Such rapid gelation illustrates the high reactivity of aliphatic diamine with pendant benzaldehyde groups, allowing for the immediate formation of PI networks via dynamic benzoic imine crosslinks. The formed gels were dried at 80°C and the resulting gels (e.g., PHEA‐PI) were collected as irregularly shaped granules. Their gel content, as defined with insoluble species in THF, was determined to be >98% (Figure 2b). Their FT‐IR spectrum in Figure 2c and Figure S3 (full scan) shows the appearance of a new band at 1645 cm− 1 corresponding to the stretching vibration of newly formed C = N (imine) groups, along with the band at 1735 cm− 1 corresponding to ester group. The band at 1720 cm− 1 corresponding to the stretching vibration of aldehyde groups significantly decreased and almost disappeared. Note that conventional PI CANs fabricated with small molecule polyaldehydes and polyamines in the stoichiometric balance of CHO/amine = 1/1 contain residual aldehyde groups, predicted by Carothers equation. Such difference could be attributed to the unique design with the PHEA‐CHO having pendant aldehyde groups. These results obtained from gel content measurement and spectroscopic analysis confirm the formation of highly crosslinked gels with dynamic benzoic imine crosslinks.

In a separate experiment, the reactive mixtures were drop‐cast on glass plates, prior to the formation of standing gels, to prepare homogenous, relatively thin films for optical and spectroscopic analysis. They were dried in three different conditions, including at room temperature overnight, and at room temperate for 60 min, followed at 100°C for 60 min. All dried films were transparent with >95% of %transmittance at λ = 650–700 nm, even though films exposed at 100°C were yellow‐colored (see digital images in Figure S4 and UV/vis spectrum with %transmittance in Figure 2d).

In another experiment, the polycondensation of PHEA‐CHO with Priamine 1074 was conducted in methyl ethyl ketone (MEK) and anisole to see how solvent polarity affects the extent of crosslinking and the clarity of dried films of PHEA‐PI networks. Similar to THF, rapid gelation was observed in both anisole and MEK within 3 min. Reactive mixtures were drop‐cast on glass plates and then dried at 100°C for 60 min. Interestingly, films with anisole were transparent, while those with MEK were translucent (see digital images of Figure S5). These results confirm that the choice of solvent is important for the fabrication of transparent PI networks. Specifically, the optical clarity is highly sensitive to the polar solubility parameter (δp) of the solvent; while low‐δp solvents like anisole (δp = 4.1 MPa1/2) and THF (δp = 5.7 MPa1/2) are compatible with the hydrophobic aliphatic chains of Priamine 1074, the significantly higher δp of MEK (9.0 MPa1/2) induces a solubility mismatch that triggers micro‐phase separation and the resulting translucency.

Mechanical, Viscoelastic, and Thermal Properties of PHEA‐PI CANs

The fabricated PHEA‐PI CANs were characterized for their thermal, mechanical, and viscoelastic properties. The dried gels in an irregularly‐shaped granule were subjected to compression molding with a hot press (see digital image in Figure S6). It is critical that compression‐molded films should be integrated with no interfaces of granules as well as homogeneous with no heterogenous phases. We tested the condition for compression molding with various temperatures (80°C –140°C) in different times (20–40 min) to investigate how they influence the quality of compression‐molded films. Three compression‐molded films were prepared with different temperatures/times of 80°C/40 min, 120°C/20 min, and 140°C/20 min under a pressure of 10 MPa. As seen in Figure 3a, all dumbbell‐shaped specimens, including those compression‐molded at 80°C for 40 min, appeared to be well integrated by naked eyes.

For PHEA‐PI CANs, digital images (a), stress‐strain curves (b), and comparison of tensile stress (c), elongation (d), and Young's modulus (e) for compression‐molded films prepared under different conditions of 80°C/40 min, 120°C/ 20 min, and 140°C/20 min. Viscoelastic properties (G′, G″, and tan δ) over temperature of 25°C – 125°C (f), DSC (g) and TGA (h) for compression‐molded films under the condition of 80°C/40 min.

For quantitative analysis, their mechanical properties were measured using a universal tensile tester. Figure 3b shows their stress‐strain curves and Figure 3c–e compares their tensile strength, elongation at break and Young's modulus. The films prepared at 80°C/40 min had their tensile strength of 694.5 ± 7.7 kPa, elongation at break of 35.8% ±4.8%, and Young's modulus of 25.6 ± 2.5 kPa. The films moulded at higher temperatures had the tensile strength of 778.7 ± 11.9 kPa, elongation at break of 43.0% ± 10.0%, and Young's modulus of 23.8 ± 4.0 kPa at 120°C/20 min and 766.0 ± 79.2 kPa, elongation at break of 56.5% ± 2.1%, and Young's modulus of 19.4 ± 2.6 kPa at 140°C/20 min. Overall, with an increasing temperature, tensile strength and elongation tend to increase while Young's modulus to decrease. These results suggest that the tensile characteristics of PI‐CANs are clearly dependent mainly on compression‐moulding temperature. Importantly, the films compression moulded at 80°C for 40 min exhibit competitive tensile characteristics and thus they were used for further characterization.

Given our comprehensive investigation of compression‐moulding condition‐mechanical property relationship, the viscoelastic properties of PHEA‐PI CANs were investigated using dynamic mechanical analyzer (DMA) on a multi‐frequency‐strain mode, with rectangle‐shaped specimens compression‐molded at 80°C for 40 min. Figure 3f shows the evolution of elastic (G′) and storage (G″) moduli as well as tan δ over temperature at 25°C –120 °C. Both moduli underwent thermal transition at 42°C and reached rubbery plateau. Molecular weight between crosslinks (Mc) can be determined by the rubber elasticity‐based equation (Mc = ρRT/G′), where G′ and ρ are rubbery plateau modulus and density of polymer and R and T are gas constant and absolute temperature, respectively. With rubbery plateau modulus at 60°C (13.7 MPa, equivalent to 13.7 J/cm3), the Mc of PHEA‐PI could be estimated to be 252.3 g/mol. These results suggest the maintenance of network integrity and crosslink density.

Next, thermal properties and stability of PHEA‐PI CANs were characterized using DSC and thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA). DSC thermogram in Figure 3g exhibit one single transition at 28.9°C of PHEA‐PI, which is close to that (26.5°C) of its PHEA‐CHO precursor. TGA thermogram in Figure 3h shows that PHEA‐PI remained intact with no significant mass loss until 390°C and then had dramatic mass loss at 400 ‐ 490°C. D1/2 which defines the temperature where mass lost is 50% as a measure of thermal stability was 476°C, which is similar to other PI CANs reported in literature [40, 49]. These results demonstrate that the use of PHEA‐CHO could not impair the thermal resilience of PI CAN materials, thus being suitable for reprocessable vitrimer applications without premature degradation in processing temperatures.

Dynamic Properties of PHEA‐PI CANs

The fabricated PHEA‐PI networks are crosslinked with dynamic benzoic imine crosslinks. As illustrated in Figure 4a, they could undergo dynamic exchange reaction through imine metathesis at mild temperatures (<80°C), retaining PHEA‐PI networks to be self‐healable and reprocessable. To demonstrate the dynamic properties of the PHEA‐PI networks, the thermally‐induced network relaxation was first examined using DMA in compression mode at temperature ranging at 50°C– 80°C, above glass transition temperature of PHEA‐PI. Their stress‐relaxation curves of normalized storage modulus (σ/σo) with Maxwell fit (single mode) are constructed in Figure 4b. As expected, the rate of stress‐relaxation increased with an increasing temperature from 50 to 80°C. The characteristic relaxation time (τ*) corresponding to the time of 1/e of the normalized stress relaxation at temperatures increased at elevated temperatures. For example, the ln(τ) ranged from 4.90 sec at 50°C to 3.04 sec at 80°C. Such rapid stress relaxation of PI‐CAN network in the absence of a catalyst could be attributed to the rapid exchange reactions of dynamic benzoic imine bonds and thus network rearrangement. Temperature‐dependent relaxation follows Arrhenius’ equation as lnτ∗ = Ea/RT—lnA, where Ea is the activation energy, A is the pre‐exponential factor, T is the experimental temperature in Kelvin, and R is the universal gas constant [69]. As constructed in Figure 4c, the Ea was determined to be 14.6 kcal/mol (corresponding to 61 kJ/mol). This value appears to be similar to that (Ea = 64 kJ/mol) for a Schiff‐base CAN based on a small‐molecule tris(4‐formyl‐2‐methoxyphenyl)phosphate (a trialdehyde) and that (Ea = 49–81 kJ/mol for vanillin‐based polyimine vitrimers [70].

Schematic illustration of dynamic exchange of benzoic imine bonds through imine metathesis in associative PHEA‐PI CANs (a); stress relaxation curve with Maxwell fit (b) and plot to fit relaxation time−temperature to Arrhenius equation to determine activation energy for network relaxation and rearrangement (c); scratch depth profiles showing penetration depth and residual depth immediately after scratching (d), along with optical images of the residual depth profiles (e); recovery of scratch profiles on cross‐linked PHEA‐PI films after annealing: recovery rate as a function of annealing time (up to 1 h) at 50°C, 70°C, 90°C, and 110°C (f), evolution of two‐dimensional residual depth profiles at (g) 90°C and (h) 110°C for annealing times of 0, 0.5, and 1 h; and time‐dependent AFM 3D images of the scratch at 110°C (i); optical microscope images to show scratches on the film surface before and after annealing at 110°C (j); and schematic illustration of reprocessability (k) and stress‐strain curves of original and reprocessed films (l).

Next, surface hardness as well as void‐filling and self‐healing performance of the PHEA‐PI networks were characterized using nano‐scratch tester (NST) coupled with atomic force microscopy (AFM). Micrometer‐scale surface damage was introduced via progressive scratching with an indenter, while the normal load was continuously increased from 0.1 to 50 mN. As shown in Figure 4d, the scratch‐depth behavior of the PHEA‐PI crosslinked films was analyzed using two parameters: penetration depth (Pd) and residual depth (Rd). The penetration depth corresponds to the actual depth reached under the applied load during scratching and thus reflects the surface hardness of the film. In contrast, the residual depth represents the remaining depth after unloading and provides information on the elastic recovery of the material. Under a maximum normal load of 50 mN, the Pd profile reached a depth of approximately 11 µm, indicating that the film possesses a relatively soft surface. However, the Rd profile exhibited substantial recovery after deformation, demonstrating that the material retains a high degree of elasticity and can effectively recover from scratch‐induced damage (Refer to the panoramic image of Rd in Figure 4e, obtained over the given normal force range). This pronounced resilience suggests that the PHEA‐PI film is capable of maintaining its structural integrity even after mechanical deformation, which may be advantageous for enhancing its resistance to external mechanical damage.

To further evaluate the healing characteristics of the films, AFM analysis was conducted on scratches produced under a normal load of 40 mN, corresponding to a position 0.8 mm along the scratch path, during annealing at various temperatures (i.e., 50°C, 70°C, 90°C, and 110°C). The recovery rate (R) was calculated from changes in the two‐dimensional cross‐sectional area of the scratch according to the equation, as R = (A0‐Ax)/A0 × 100 (%), where A0 is the initial cross‐sectional area at 0 h, and Ax is the cross‐sectional area after a given annealing time (0.5 and 1 h).

As shown in Figure 4f, under 50°C condition, the scratch profile exhibits negligible change even after 1 h of annealing, indicating minimal healing. At 70 and 90°C, the recovery rate reaches approximately 60% after 1 h. In contrast, at 110°C, the recovery rate increased significantly from 65.4% after 0.5 h to 90.8% after 1 h, implying nearly complete recovery of the scratch. These results demonstrate that the healing process is strongly enhanced at elevated temperatures, which can be attributed to thermally activated reversible exchange reactions in the PHEA‐PI films. Figure 4g,h compare the time‐dependent two‐dimensional evolution of the residual scratch depth at 90 and 110°C. It should be noted that, during scratching, slight variations in crack size were observed even at the same position, leading to differences in the initial residual depth prior to annealing. Despite this variability, the overall healing trends remain consistent. In particular, Figure 4i clearly visualizes the three‐dimensional morphological evolution of the scratch at 110°C. In our separated experiment, the recovery of scratches on the surfaces of PHEA‐PI films when being annealed at 100°C after 30 min was also observed by optical microscopy (Figure 4j).

Given the promising results on dynamic nature and void‐filling, the reprocessability of the dynamic PHEA‐PI networks was evaluated with tensile measurements. This method involves the recovery of tensile properties of materials after being reprocessed via hot‐melt compression (Figure 4k). Initial dumbbell‐shaped tensile films after tensile measurements were cut into small pieces and then compression‐moulded under a pressure of 10 MPa at 120°C for 20 min (Figure S7). The reprocessed films appeared to be integrated and homogeneous with minimal interfaces. Figure 4l compares the stress–strain curves of original and reprocessed PHEA‐PI films. The reprocessed films had their tensile strength of 661.8 kPa, elongation of 47.8 %, and Young's modulus of 17.3 kPa. Compared with those for original films compression‐moulded under the similar condition (120°C/20 min), the data suggest the recovery of tensile properties to be > 85%.

Acid‐Catalyzed Degradability and Chemical Recycling of PHEA‐PI CANs

As illustrated in Figure 5, benzoic imine crosslinks in PHEA‐PI networks could degrade to corresponding aldehyde and primary amine groups through acid‐catalyzed cleavage reaction in the presence of an acid, generating PHEA‐CHO and Priamine 1074 precursors. These precursors could be subjected to chemical recycling to PHEA‐PI networks. To test acid‐catalyzed degradation and chemical recycling of PHEA‐PI networks, we designed a proof‐of‐concept experiment where pieces of PHEA‐PI networks in THF (Figure 5a for digital image) turned into sol (Figure 5b) when incubated with a catalytic amount of HCl (an acid). 1H NMR spectrum of the degraded product shows the characteristic peaks at 10 ppm corresponding to aldehyde group, 7.2–7.6 ppm to aromatic group, and 4.3–4.5 ppm to methylene groups adjacent to ester group (Figure S8). Their integral ratio is quantitative to the number of the protons. These results confirm the cleavage of benzoic imine crosslinks to generate PHEA‐CHO and Priamine 1074 precursors as well as the preservation of CHO functionality of PHEA‐CHO in the presence of a catalytic amount of acid. Next, the degraded products were cast to dried films (Figure 5c), which appeared to not being dissolved in THF (Figure 5d), suggesting the re‐formation of PHEA‐PI networks through benzoic imine crosslinks. These results suggest that PHEA‐PI CAN materials hold great potential for sustainability as recyclable dynamic covalent materials.

Conclusion

We explored the PP‐CHO approach with the synthesis of PHEA‐CHO having pendant CHO groups for the development of dynamic PI‐CAN materials. Well‐defined PHEA‐CHO with predetermined molecular weight and narrow molecular weight distribution was synthesized by a photo‐indued ATRP of HEA for PHEA precursor, followed by its post‐polymerization modification with 4‐FB. The synthesized PHEA‐CHO was formulated as a polymeric polyaldehyde for step‐growth polycondensation with an aliphatic diamine, resulting in quick gelation, which suggests the greater reactivity of pendant benzaldehyde groups to aliphatic amine groups. The resulting network films were transparent with %transmittance of >98% and also integrated with no apparent visible hetero‐domains. They were crosslinked through in situ formation of dynamic benzoic imine crosslinks, confirmed by FT‐IR spectroscopy, and gel content as high as 95% in THF. Benefiting from lower activation energy (Ea = 14.6 kcal/mol) for stress relaxation even at temperature as low as 50°C– 80°C, dynamic PHEA‐PI hybrid CAN materials exhibited good self‐healability and reprocessability as retaining their mechanical strengths upon a recycle. These homogeneous structures and enhanced dynamic properties could arise from associative benzoic imine crosslinks undergoing reversible exchange through imine metathesis. Moreover, PHEA‐PI CANs materials degraded through acid‐catalyzed hydrolysis of benzoic imine crosslinks, offering potential for sustainability as recyclable dynamic covalent systems. These results highlight the design principle of optimizing the reactive functional group ratio in CANs to synthesize effective PI CANs with good mechanical properties, as well as good self‐healing and reprocessability via dynamic exchange‐valuable characteristics for sustainability and recyclability.
