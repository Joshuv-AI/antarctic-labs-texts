Exploring the Association Between Adverse Childhood Experiences and Missed Primary Care Appointments in Adult Patients
Authors: Phillip Anjum, Bernard S Evenhuis, Rebecca Zhang, Clarke Britton, Marshall Fleurant
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMissed medical appointments lead to adverse health outcomes and financial costs. Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are associated with poor health outcomes and may influence health care–seeking behavior in adulthood. This study explored the relationship between ACE scores and missed primary care appointments in a predominantly Black safety-net population.MethodsThe authors conducted a retrospective cohort study of 307 adult patients in a primary care clinic between January and August of 2022. Patients were recruited using a systematic sampling protocol and completed the 10-item Adverse Childhood Experiences Questionnaire. Medicaid insurance status was the strongest predictor of no-show behavior, highlighting the impact of socioeconomic disadvantage on health care engagement.ConclusionSocial determinants such as insurance status may have a stronger influence on appointment adherence than childhood trauma history. Introduction

Missed medical appointments can lead to adverse health outcomes and impose substantial financial costs. 1 No-shows can be defined as missed medical appointments without prior notification or cancellation by the patient, although some studies include appointments rescheduled or canceled belatedly. 2,3

No-show rates in primary care settings vary widely depending on the population, clinic structure, and geographic location, but they typically range from approximately 10% to 30%. 4–6 Factors such as socioeconomic disadvantage, younger age, presence of mental health conditions, lack of transportation, Medicaid insurance coverage, and belonging to marginalized populations frequently correlate with increased likelihood of missing appointments. 7–9

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are associated with poor health outcomes and multiple comorbidities in adulthood, along with disruptions in social relationships that may influence health care–seeking behavior. 10,11 Although the relationship between ACEs and missed appointments has been studied in pediatric populations, less is known about this association in adults. 12 Understanding how ACEs impact health care utilization in adult primary care settings is crucial for improving patient outcomes, particularly among those managing chronic diseases.

OBJECTIVE

This study aimed to explore the relationship between missed primary care appointments and sociodemographic and medical risk factors, including ACE scores, in an adult Black population. The authors hypothesized that patients with higher ACE scores (≥ 4) would be more likely to miss follow-up appointments compared to those with lower ACE scores (< 4).

Methods

The authors conducted a retrospective cohort study of adult patients in a hospital-associated primary care clinic between January and August of 2022. To address both workflow concerns and reduce selection bias, the authors utilized a systematic sampling protocol. Study staff approached every third or fourth patient in the triage area and asked each patient to complete the standard 10-item Adverse Childhood Experiences Questionnaire (Figure 1). The authors excluded those who were unable to complete the survey due to a physical or cognitive disability or those being acutely admitted for a medical or psychiatric reason (Figure 2). Nonrespondants were not tracked; however, study staff reported a refusal rate of approximately 40% (range 30%–70% between interviewers). This nonresponse rate is consistent with recruitment challenges documented in similar safety-net settings. 13

The primary analytic dataset for this study covered the period from January through August of 2022 and included 307 unique patients who completed Adverse Childhood Experiences Questionnaires during this time frame. This dataset served as the basis for all statistical analyses examining the association between ACE scores and missed appointments. Additionally, the authors accessed 2 separate databases: 1) a larger electronic health record (EHR)–derived dataset of the same clinic population covering January 2022 through December 2023; this broader dataset, which included the same patients as the primary analytic sample, was used to provide descriptive context regarding visit frequency patterns in the clinic population but was not used for the primary statistical analyses; 2) SlicerDicer, a self service analytics tool within EPIC’s EHR system where general descriptive analysis was performed on the Grady primary care clinic population (N = 15,134 over 6 months).

This study recruitment period occurred following the stabilization of clinic operations after the initial phases of the COVID-19 pandemic. Internal institutional data suggested that appointment engagement remained resilient during this phase.

The primary outcome was no-show behavior, defined as having at least 1 scheduled appointment that was not attended and not canceled in advance. Demographic and clinical variables were manually extracted from the EHR, including age, sex, race or ethnicity, employment status, insurance type, residential ZIP code, and comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, mood and anxiety disorders, posttraumatic stress disorder, coronary artery disease, and body mass index. The authors conducted sample size calculations to achieve 80% power with an alpha of 0.05, reaching a recruitment goal of at least 80 patients.

For each variable of interest, the authors conducted simple logistic regressions to estimate the probability of a binary outcome of ever having a no-show visit, adjusting for the total number of visits (see Supplementary Material). Variables with P values trending toward significance (P < .10) were selected for inclusion in the final multivariable logistic regression in addition to total number of visits and ACE score as the primary variable of interest.

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc). The authors utilized SlicerDicer by EPIC for clinic level descriptive data analysis. A P value < .05 was considered statistically significant. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Emory University Institutional Review Board (ID: STUDY00002114). The requirement for a signed informed consent form was waived; however, verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Results

In the larger EHR-derived dataset of the same clinic population covering January 2022 through December 2023, patients had a total of 1170 scheduled visits (mean 3.8 visits, standard deviation [SD] 2.3, range 1–12). In this sample, patients were followed for an average of 406.58 days (SD 163.78, range 21–617). These patients missed at least 1 appointment (mean 0.97, SD 1.45) with 135 (43.7%) patients missing at least 1 appointment. The overall no-show rate was 26.2% (Table 1). These figures provide broader context for visit frequency in the clinic population.

In the analysis of the general primary care clinic population (n = 15,134), the authors found the clinic to be 86.8% Black and 43.8% male, and the mean age was 56 (SD 0.12). Common comorbidities included diabetes (39.7%), hypertension (67.7%), and coronary artery disease (11.1%). At least 20.7% were on Medicaid, 21.1% were on Medicare, and 21.2% had commercial insurance. The major psychiatric comorbidities were depression (13.5%), followed by anxiety (17.1%). At least 61.1% of patients were logged as having at least 1 no-show in the record.

The primary analytic dataset had 307 unique patients. This sample was 91.9% Black and 44.0% male, and the mean age was 59 (59.6 [12.8]). Approximately, 22.5% of patients had an ACE score ≥ 4. The average ACE score for patients in the cohort was 2.3 (2.25). Common comorbidities included diabetes (42.9%), hypertension (79.2%), and coronary artery disease (13.4%). At least 20.7% were on Medicaid, 20.1% were on Medicare, 20.7% had commercial insurance, 14.8% were dual insured, and 23.4% were uninsured. The major psychiatric comorbidities were depression (36.4%), followed by anxiety (21.8%) at least 7.8% reported other mood disorders (Table 1).

Overall ACE scores were not associated with missing an appointment (odds ratio [OR], 1.03; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.93–1.14; P = .69) (Table 2). On univariate regression patients on Medicaid had twice the odds of missing at least 1 appointment compared to non-Medicaid patients (OR, 2.04; 95% CI, 1.08–3.85; P = .03). Patients with diagnosed mood disorder also had a higher odds of missing visits (OR, 3.45; 95% CI, 1.22–9.84; P = .02). The authors noted a positive association between missed visits and Hispanic ethnicity (OR, 5.21; 95% CI, 0.90–30.21; P = .06) and posttraumatic stress disorder (OR, 2.55; 95% CI, 0.87–7.49; P = .10). However, it did not reach statistical significance. There was a negative association with hypertension (OR, 0.56; 95% CI, 0.30–1.03) without statistical significance. In multivariate models only Medicaid was significantly predictive of no-show rate (OR, 2.15; 95% CI, 1.07–4.30; P = .03) (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study of adult patients at a safety-net clinic, the authors did not find an association between ACE scores and missed appointments, contrary to the hypothesis. Although ACEs are linked to adverse health outcomes and health care utilization patterns, their impact on short-term clinic attendance may be confounded by other social determinants and comorbidities.

The study population appeared to have a greater burden of disease compared to the general clinic population. Sicker patients may be more engaged, suggested by the lower proportion of missed visits. This phenomenon may contribute to decreased statistical power. The average no-show rate nationally is between 5% and 8%. However, in certain urban safety-net hospital metropolitan areas with high patient volumes, it is as high as 30%. 14 A number of factors clearly contribute to such a wide range of no-show rates nationwide. This clinic population is generally low income, with large proportions on government insurance or self pay, and many have transportation issues.

Medicaid insurance status was the only significant predictor of missed appointments after adjusting for covariates. This finding aligned with previous literature demonstrating that Medicaid insurance status, Medicare insurance status (particularly when lacking Medicaid supplemental coverage), and socioeconomic disadvantage are strong, interrelated drivers of health care nonattendance. 2,15–17 As these prior studies and this study have indicated, individuals with Medicaid are more likely to avoid or delay health care. These associations persist after adjusting for sociodemographic factors, indicating that both insurance type and social risk independently contribute to nonattendance and acute care utilization. 15–17 Neighborhood socioeconomic disadvantage is associated with increased odds of emergency department visits and hospitalizations among Medicaid beneficiaries, suggesting that barriers to outpatient care are more pronounced in these populations. 15,16 Lower income, lower educational attainment, and residence in states with restrictive Medicaid policies, including work requirements and private option programs, further increase the likelihood of forgoing needed care due to cost. 18 These policy and structural factors may further exacerbate challenges related to appointment adherence among vulnerable populations who rely on public insurance programs.

Patients with diagnosed mood disorders had significantly higher odds of missing visits, although this association was attenuated in this multivariate model. Mood disorders are a strong predictor of health care disengagement, with affected individuals demonstrating higher rates of missed appointments and inconsistent attendance in both primary care and behavioral health settings. 17,19,20 The presence of mood disorders compounds the effects of insurance instability and socioeconomic disadvantage, resulting in persistent disruptions in care, especially following Medicaid disenrollment. 19,21 Among Medicare beneficiaries with severe mental illness, dual eligibility with Medicaid is associated with less disruption in care, but disparities remain, particularly for Black individuals and those with disabilities. 20 The intersection of public insurance status, socioeconomic disadvantage, and mood disorders leads to higher no-show rates and lower health care engagement, with the greatest impact observed in populations facing multiple overlapping vulnerabilities.

These findings suggested that structural barriers related to insurance status may mediate the relationship between mental health conditions and appointment adherence, highlighting the complex interplay between these factors. Similarly, hypertension showed borderline significance, but the high prevalence of comorbidities and the predominance of Medicaid patients in the clinic likely introduced substantial variability, potentially obscuring clearer associations. The borderline significance observed for Hispanic ethnicity suggested that cultural and linguistic barriers may also contribute to health care access challenges in this population.

This study had several limitations. These results came from an urban hospital-associated primary care clinic with a predominantly Black population, which may not have been generalizable to populations with different racial compositions or those receiving care in private practice settings. Additionally, although the authors employed a systematic sampling strategy, they did not maintain a comprehensive log of all nonrespondents, preventing a direct analysis of nonresponse bias. Individuals with the highest burden of ACEs and concurrent social instability may have been less likely to present to the clinic or complete the survey during the recruitment period. If these nonrespondents were also those at highest risk for future no-shows, this analysis would exclude the segment of the population where the ACE-appointment relationship is likely strongest. Consequently, this selection bias would drive the estimates toward the null, causing the authors to underestimate the true association between ACE scores and appointment nonadherence. This small study may have been affected by the multiple testing problem. In a prior analysis, with a smaller sample size, the authors found similar findings. The authors lacked detailed measures of income, housing, and employment that could have influenced appointment adherence. Differences between this analytic dataset and the larger EHR-based dataset may also have affected the results. Despite these limitations, this study highlighted the role of present-day structural disadvantage over childhood trauma in predicting appointment adherence in adult primary care.

Conclusion

In this study of adult primary care patients, ACE scores were not significantly associated with missed appointments. This suggested that other social and clinical factors may have a stronger influence on appointment adherence. Medicaid insurance status was a significant predictor of no-show behavior, elucidating the impact of socioeconomic disadvantage on health care engagement. Efforts to improve appointment adherence should therefore focus on reducing barriers related to insurance and access. Future studies with a larger and more diverse population and more detailed socioeconomic data, with use of a validated comorbidity index, could better inform targeted interventions in these populations.
