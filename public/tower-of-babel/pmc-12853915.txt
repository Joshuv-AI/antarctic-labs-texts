Enzyme-assisted nanopore sensor for sensitive and multiplexed detection of microRNAs
Authors: Xiaobin Huang, Yakun Yi, Ke Zhou, Lei Liu, Hai-Chen Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
MicroRNAs (miRNAs) play crucial roles in the pathogenesis of diverse diseases; however, their extremely low abundance in complex biological systems makes highly sensitive and specific multiplexed detection particularly challenging. Here, we report an enzyme-assisted nanopore sensor that enables simultaneous and ultrasensitive detection of multiple miRNAs. Capture DNA (cDNA) immobilized on streptavidin-coated magnetic beads specifically hybridizes with target miRNAs, opening the cDNA hairpin and facilitating the binding of alkaline phosphatase (ALP)-labeled detection DNA to form stable complexes. ALP catalyzes the dephosphorylation of designed phosphorylated peptide probes, inducing conformational and charge changes that yield distinct current blockade signatures in the presence of cucurbit [7]uril within the nanopore. This strategy reduces background interference via magnetic separation and amplifies detection signals through enzymatic reactions, thereby markedly improving sensitivity for low-abundance miRNAs. The sensor achieves a broad dynamic range from 10.0 fM to 10.0 nM across three target miRNAs, with femtomolar-level limits of detection. By integrating molecular separation, enzymatic amplification, and nanopore sensing, this platform provides a robust approach for precise miRNA analysis, offering strong potential for early disease diagnosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03915-9.

Introduction

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are a class of small non-coding RNAs, typically 18–25 nucleotides in length, that play critical roles in diverse biological processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and metabolism [1–3]. Aberrant miRNA expression has been strongly implicated in the pathogenesis of numerous diseases, such as cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and metabolic syndromes [4]. Importantly, the intrinsic stability of miRNAs against degradation renders them promising biomarkers for early disease diagnosis [5, 6]. To date, many approaches have been developed for miRNA detection, including northern blotting, hybridization-based DNA microarrays, quantitative reverse transcription PCR [1], and a variety of electrochemical [7–9] and optical sensors [10–12]. While these techniques provide high sensitivity and accuracy for individual miRNA detection, they often face challenges in multiplexed analysis and reliable application in complex biological samples.

Nanopore technology has attracted increasing attention due to its unique capability for label-free, single-molecule, and real-time analysis. This technique records current fluctuation events as molecules translocate through or interact with a nanopore, enabling qualitative and quantitative assessment of targets based on event amplitude, duration, and frequency [13]. Compared with conventional approaches, nanopore sensing can simultaneously discriminate multiple analytes within a single channel by resolving distinct current signatures. This advantage has led to broad applications in stochastic sensing [14–17], nucleic acid [18–20] and protein sequencing [21–28], physiological mechanism studies [29, 30], and chemical reaction monitoring [31–33]. For RNA detection, both direct nanopore sensing [34, 35] and beacon-assisted strategies [36, 37] have been reported. Nevertheless, nanopore sensing in complex biological samples remains vulnerable to interference from high-molecular-weight contaminants, limiting its utility in early disease diagnostics.

Recently, we reported a nanopore-based enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay for the detection of cancer biomarkers [38]. Building on this foundation, we now extend the method to achieve highly sensitive and specific multiplexed detection of miRNAs. The sensing principle is illustrated in Fig. 1. Biotin-labeled capture DNA (cDNA) is immobilized on streptavidin-modified magnetic beads (MBs) via biotin–streptavidin interaction. Upon hybridization with the target miRNA, the cDNA hairpin structure is opened, exposing its terminus, which then hybridizes with alkaline phosphatase (ALP)-labeled detection DNA (dDNA) to form a stable MB–cDNA–miRNA–dDNA–ALP complex (validation shown in Fig. S1, Fig. S2). Peptide probes FGpYD8, FGpSD8, and FGpTD8 were designed for miRNA-21-5p (miR-21-5p), miRNA-155-5p (miR-155-5p), and miRNA-let-7a-5p (let-7a-5p), respectively, each containing a phosphorylated residue at the third position as the ALP recognition and cleavage site. In the presence of target miRNAs, ALP catalyzes probe dephosphorylation, producing FGYD8, FGSD8, and FGTD8 peptides with significant conformational and charge changes. The phosphorylated and dephosphorylated peptides form distinct host–guest complexes with cucurbit [7]uril (CB [7]), generating different current blockade signatures when translocated through the α-hemolysin (αHL) nanopore. Magnetic separation minimizes background interference from complex matrices, while enzymatic reactions amplify target-specific signals, thereby markedly enhancing sensitivity toward low-abundance miRNAs. By integrating magnetic separation, enzymatic amplification, and nanopore single-molecule sensing, this strategy effectively addresses the challenges of low target abundance and matrix interference, offering a robust and reliable platform for multiplexed miRNA detection with strong potential for early disease diagnosis.

Results

Construction of the peptide probes

To evaluate the signal discrimination between phosphorylated and dephosphorylated peptide probes, phosphorylated sequences (FGpYD8, FGpSD8, and FGpTD8) were enzymatically dephosphorylated by ALP to yield their corresponding counterparts (FGYD8, FGSD8, and FGTD8). Each peptide probe forms a stable host–guest complex with CB [7] through ion–dipole interactions between the N-terminal amino group of the peptide and the carbonyl-lined portals of CB [7, 25, 38, 39]. The translocation behaviors of these complexes were examined using the αHL nanopore. Upon translocation, the complexes produced characteristic current blockade events (Fig. 2A, B, D, E, G, and H). Single-level blockades (level 1) result from the transient trapping and subsequent dissociation of the peptide probe within the nanopore. In contrast, the two-level blockades, comprising levels 1 and 2–2’, arise from two distinct processes: level 1 corresponds to the same transient trapping and dissociation of the peptide probe, whereas level 2–2’ originates from CB [7] oscillation within the vestibule of αHL [40]. Since both types of blockade events contain the same level 1 signal, statistical analysis was performed based on the level 1 signals. Gaussian fitting of the current histograms yielded I/I₀ values of (66.22 ± 0.03)% for FGpYD8⊂CB [7], (60.25 ± 0.05)% for FGYD8⊂CB [7], (65.14 ± 0.07)% for FGpSD8⊂CB [7], (57.64 ± 0.02)% for FGSD8⊂CB [7], (63.99 ± 0.02)% for FGpTD8⊂CB [7], and (58.52 ± 0.02)% for FGTD8⊂CB [7] (Fig. 2B, D, F, H, J, L, and Fig. S3). The significant differences in translocation signals demonstrate the feasibility of employing FGpYD8, FGpSD8, and FGpTD8 as peptide probes for miRNA detection using nanopores.

Detection of MiRNAs through enzyme-assisted nanopore sensing

Under optimized experimental conditions (Fig. S4), the sensing performance of the developed nanopore sensor for multiplexed miRNA detection was systematically evaluated. Phosphorylated peptide probes—FGpYD8, FGpSD8, and FGpTD8—were designed for the detection of miR-21-5p, miR-155-5p, and let-7a-5p, respectively. The MB–cDNA complex specifically captures target miRNAs, forming stable duplexes that subsequently hybridize with ALP-labeled dDNA. The immobilized ALP catalyzes the dephosphorylation of the peptide probes, generating dephosphorylated products (FGYD8, FGSD8, and FGTD8), which produce distinct ionic current blockades during nanopore translocation. These signals are clearly distinguishable from those of the phosphorylated probes (Fig. 3A and C, and 3E). This mechanism effectively converts molecular recognition events into measurable ionic current signatures, thereby establishing a universal and highly sensitive platform for miRNA detection.

As the concentration of miRNA increases, more ALP molecules are immobilized on the MB surface, leading to accelerated enzymatic cleavage and a progressive decrease in phosphorylated probes. Consequently, the frequency of phosphorylated probe translocation events decreases with increasing miRNA concentration (Fig. S5, Fig. S6, Fig. S7 and Fig. S8). Linear regression analysis of event frequency (f) versus the logarithm of miRNA concentration (log C) demonstrated excellent linearity for all three targets over a wide dynamic range (10.0 fM to 10.0 nM). Selectivity and application of nanopore sensor for multiple miRNAs detection

To evaluate the sensing capability of the nanopore sensor in complex biological samples, a series of selectivity assays were performed. MiRNAs such as miR-21-5p, miR-155-5p, let-7a-5p, miR-141-5p, and miR-205-5p commonly coexist in cells; therefore, the latter two were employed as potential interfering species. The concentration of the target miRNA was set at 1.0 nM, while that of the interfering miRNAs was 10.0 nM. In the presence of target miRNA, ALP-mediated dephosphorylation of the phosphorylated peptide probes led to a marked reduction in nanopore current blockade event frequency. By contrast, in the blank and interference groups, the probes remained phosphorylated, producing relatively high event frequencies (Fig. 4A–C). The pronounced differences in current blockade frequencies confirm that the sensor exhibits high selectivity and strong anti-interference capability.

The applicability of the nanopore sensor to biological samples was further assessed by detecting miRNA expression levels in HeLa cell lysates. Lysates containing different numbers of HeLa cells were prepared, incubated with the sensing mixture, and subjected to nanopore translocation measurements. The frequencies of current blockade events correlated well with the corresponding miRNA concentrations. As the cell number increased, production of dephosphorylated peptide probes progressively increased, resulting in a gradual decline in phosphorylated probe event frequency. Among the three miRNAs analyzed, miR-21-5p exhibited the most prominent signal response, followed by miR-155-5p and let-7a-5p. The nanopore detection results were consistent with the RT-qPCR results, confirming the reliability and accuracy of the proposed method. (Fig. 4D–F and Fig. S9). This trend is also consistent with their reported expression levels in HeLa cells [41–43]. These results demonstrate that the nanopore sensor enables direct detection of miRNAs in cell lysates, underscoring its practical potential for bioanalytical applications. Furthermore, the sensor successfully distinguished between various phosphorylated and dephosphorylated peptide probes with high resolution (Fig. 4G). A “separate incubation, mixed detection” strategy was employed. Each target miRNA (miR-21-5p, miR-155-5p, and let-7a-5p) was first incubated individually with its corresponding cDNA to allow specific hybridization and enzymatic reaction. The enzymatic products from the three reactions were subsequently mixed for simultaneous nanopore analysis. Nanopore detection of the mixed sample clearly resolved three pairs of characteristic current blockade signals, enabling accurate identification and discrimination of the different miRNAs (Fig. S10). These results demonstrate the capability of the proposed method for multiplexed detection.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we developed an enzyme-assisted nanopore sensor that converts target recognition into enzymatic dephosphorylation, enabling highly sensitive detection of miR-21-5p, miR-155-5p, and let-7a-5p. By combining magnetic separation to reduce background interference with nanopore technology for high-resolution discrimination of peptide probe–CB [7] complexes, the sensor achieves femtomolar detection limits across a wide dynamic range, providing a reliable and efficient platform for multiplexed miRNA analysis. Further optimization is still required. Integrating nanopore sensing with isothermal DNA amplification methods, such as rolling circle amplification, hybridization chain reaction, or catalytic hairpin assembly, could enable cascaded signal amplification. This synergistic strategy is expected to further improve sensitivity and establish a powerful tool for early disease diagnosis.

Materials and methods

Materials

1,2-Diphytanoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphochline was purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids. All peptide probes (Table S2) were purchased from QYAOBIO and purified by high-performance liquid chromatography. All the peptides were dissolved in deionized water and stored at -20℃. The primer sequences (Table S3), streptavidin-labeled ALP, streptavidin-labeled magnetic beads, and SanPrep Column miRNA Extraction Kit were purchased from Sangon Biotechnology. Biotin was purchased from Innochem. Disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetate (EDTA) was purchased from Aladdin. CB [7], bovine serum albumin (BSA), potassium chloride (KCl), sodium chloride (NaCl), magnesium chloride (MgCl2), and tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane (Tris) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Protein Preparation

Wild type αHL proteins were prepared as described in previous study [29]. The purified heptamer proteins were preserved in protein storage buffer and stored at -80 ℃.

Buffer Preparation

The detection buffer in the cis chamber contained 1.0 M KCl, 10.0 mM citric acid, pH 5.0. The buffer in the trans chamber consisted of 3.6 M KCl, 10.0 mM citric acid, pH 5.0. The asymmetric KCl concentrations between the cis and trans chambers were intentionally applied to establish an ion concentration gradient, which facilitates the generation of electroosmotic flow and enhances the capture efficiency of molecules entering the nanopore [40]. The MB wash buffer consisted of 1.0 M NaCl, 0.50 mM EDTA, and 10.0 mM Tris, pH 7.4. The DNA hybridization buffer comprised 150.0 mM NaCl, 10.0 mM MgCl2, 10.0 mM KCl, and 10.0 mM Tris, pH 7.4. The ALP incubation and digestion buffer contained 150.0 mM NaCl, 20.0 mM Tris, and 1.0 mM MgCl2, pH 9.0. The protein storage buffer contained 10.0 mM Tris and 50.0 mM NaCl, pH 8.0.

Verification of DNA sequences by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis

The designed sequences were verified by 12% polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. The fluorescent dye was prepared by mixing Super Green I with loading buffer at a 1:1 volume ratio. DNA samples were mixed with the fluorescent dye at a 2:1 volume ratio, incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Electrophoresis was performed in 0.5TBE buffer at +110 V for 1 h.

Preparation of MB-cDNA complex

Streptavidin-labeled MB (50.0 µL) was washed with buffer three times. The pretreated streptavidin-labeled MB was then resuspended in DNA hybridization buffer (125.0 µL), followed by the addition of biotin-labeled cDNA (20.0 µL, 10.0 µM). The mixture was gently mixed and incubated at room temperature for 1 h to enable immobilization of the cDNA onto the MB surface via biotin-streptavidin interaction. After incubation, excess free cDNA was removed by magnetic separation to obtain the MB-cDNA complex.

Blocking of the unoccupied streptavidin binding sites on MB-cDNA surface

The remaining streptavidin binding sites on MB-cDNA were blocked with 150.0 µL blocking solution containing 20.0 mg/mL BSA and 100.0 µM biotin. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Excess blocking agents were removed by magnetic separation. The MB-cDNA was resuspended in 250.0 µL DNA hybridization buffer and stored at 4℃ until use.

Preparation of dDNA-ALP complex

To prepare the dDNA-ALP complex, biotin-labeled dDNA (100.0 nM) was mixed with streptavidin-labeled ALP at a molar ratio of 1:2. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 1 h to facilitate complex formation via biotin-streptavidin interaction. The obtained dDNA-ALP complex was stored at 4℃ until use.

Preparation of MB-cDNA-miRNA-dDNA-ALP complex

MB-cDNA (25.0 µL) was mixed with miRNA standard solution (65.0 µL) at varying concentrations and dDNA-ALP (10.0 µL, 100.0 nM). The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 2 h to facilitate specific binding among the miRNA, MB-cDNA, and dDNA-ALP. The unbound components were removed by magnetic separation to obtained the MB-cDNA-miRNA-dDNA-ALP complex was stored at 4℃ until use.

Enzymatic cleavage of peptide probes by ALP

The MB-cDNA-miRNA-dDNA-ALP complex was incubated with peptide probes (5.0 µM) at 37℃ for 1 h to carry out enzymatic cleavage reaction. Peptide sequences were selected according to the target miRNA: FGpYD8 for miR-21-5p, FGpSD8 for miR-155-5p, and FGpTD8 for let-7a-5p. The supernatant containing the cleaved peptide probes were collected by magnetic separation and stored at -20℃ for single-channel recording experiments.

Extraction of MiRNAs in HeLa cells

HeLa cells (human cervical carcinoma cells) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37℃ with 5% CO2. After reaching the desired confluency, the cells were harvested and counted using an automated cell counter. Samples with varying cell numbers were prepared for subsequent analysis. Total miRNAs were extracted from the collected HeLa cells using the SanPrep Column miRNA Extraction Kit. The concentration of miRNAs in the extracted samples was then quantitatively analyzed using a nanopore sensing platform.

Reverse transcription and RT-qPCR analysis

Reverse transcription and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis were performed according to previously reported methods [44]. Briefly, the extracted miRNA was used as a template in a 20.0 µL reverse transcription reaction mixture containing RNase-free water (5.5 µL), 2×miRNA L-RT solution mix (10.0 µL), miRNA L-RT enzyme mix (1.5 µL), miRNA sample (2.0 µL), and stem-loop primer (1.0 µL, 10.0 µM). The reaction was carried out at 16℃ for 30 min, followed by incubation at 37℃ for 30 min, and then at 85℃ for 5 min to inactivate the enzyme. The resulting cDNA was used for subsequent RT-qPCR analysis. RT-qPCR was performed on an Applied Biosystems 7500 RT-qPCR System in a total reaction volume of 20.0 µL, containing RNase-free water (9.0 µL), 2×SGExcel fastSYBR mixture (10.0 µL), forward primer (0.4 µL, 10.0 µM), reverse primer (0.4 µL, 10.0 µM), cDNA template (2.0 µL) (Table S4), and 100×ROX reference dye (0.2 µL). The amplification program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95℃ for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95℃ for 5 s and 60℃ for 30 s. A melting curve analysis was performed at the end of amplification to verify the specificity of the PCR products. The Ct values obtained were converted into the concentrations of miR-21-5p, miR-155-5p, and let-7a-5p using standard curves, thereby enabling quantitative determination of miRNA levels in HeLa cell lysates.

Single-channel current recordings

A planar lipid bilayer composed of 1,2-di-(iso-propionyl)-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine was formed across a circular aperture (100–150 μm in diameter) in a 25 μm-thick polytetrafluoroethylene membrane, physically separating the measurement chamber into cis and trans compartments. The cis chamber was filled with a solution containing 1.0 M KCl, 10.0 mM citric acid, pH 5.0, supplemented with peptide probe (100.0 nM) and CB [7] (25.0 µM). The trans chamber contained 3.6 M KCl, 10.0 mM citric acid, pH 5.0. Ion current was recorded using a pair of Ag/AgCl electrodes under a constant transmembrane potential of +200 mV. The signals were amplified by a patch-clamp amplifier (Axopatch 200B; Axon Instruments), filtered at 10 kHz using a low-pass Bessel filter, and digitized at a sampling frequency of 100 kHz via a Digidata 1550B analogue-to-digital converter (Axon Instruments).

Statistical analysis

Current recording data were analyzed using Clampfit v.11.7 software (Axon Instruments). Events were first automatically detected using the Event Detection feature to identify current events and generate histograms of current amplitude and dwell time. Subsequently, characteristic current events were selected for further statistical analysis. Histogram plotting, curve fitting, and figure preparation were performed using OriginPro 2024 (Microcal) and Clampfit v.11.7. Final figure design was completed with Adobe Illustrator 2023.
