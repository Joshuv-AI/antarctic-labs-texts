Clinical performance of expanded non-invasive prenatal testing for aneuploidies: a real-world experience in Thailand
Authors: Nareenun Chansriniyom, Panyu Panburana, Wasun Chantratita, Insee Sensorn, Duangrurdee Wattanasirichaigoon, Maneerat Prakobpanich, Sthaporn Nimnoo, Chayada Tangshewinsirikul
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundData from high-income countries have shown that expanded non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) is highly reliable for detecting common autosomal trisomies (CATs), whereas its diagnostic accuracy for rare autosomal trisomies (RATs) remains relatively low. Information on the clinical performance of expanded NIPT in middle- and low-income countries is still limited. The objective of this study was to evaluate the clinical validity and utility of an expanded NIPT for CATs, sex chromosome aneuploidies (SCAs), and RATs following its nationwide clinical implementation.MethodsThe results of NIPT were retrospectively reviewed. Those with high-risk test results were followed up via phone and/or by reviewing medical records regarding the downstream prenatal diagnosis and the pregnancy outcomes.ResultsThe NIPT results of 19,714 samples were analyzed. We found screening positive rate of 0.95%; sensitivity at 97.92%, 94.12%, 85.71%, and 90% for T21, T18, T13, and SCAs; and PPV at 87.04%, 69.57%, 50%, and 54.55% for T21, T18, T13, and SCAs respectively. As for RATs, clinical validity included sensitivity at 100% and PPV of 13.3%. Over 95% of the participants with high-risk NIPT results underwent prenatal diagnosis. The majority (86.52%) of those with a confirmed diagnosis chose termination of the pregnancy.ConclusionsThis large-scale study of mixed-risk pregnancies revealed that expanded NIPT had a good sensitivity for CATs (specifically for T21 and T18) and RATs; and fair sensitivity for T13 and SCAs. The PPV is acceptable for CATs and SCAs, but poor for RATs. These data indicated that expanded NIPT results strongly influenced pregnancy outcomes in a real-world experience.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08303-7.

Introduction

Following the discovery of cell-free fetal deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) in maternal plasma in 1997, and with the subsequent advent of massive parallel sequencing, non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) based on cell-free fetal DNA sequencing of fetal common autosomal trisomies (CATs) including trisomy 21 (T21), 18 (T18), and 13 (T13) was established in prenatal settings in 2011 [1–7]. A meta-analysis of the clinical performance of NIPT in detecting fetal CATs has demonstrated that NIPT has high reliability, with ≥ 99% detection rate and a very low false positive rate for CATs [8]. Consequently, several professional societies, including American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) [9], American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) [10], Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RCOG) [11] and, the Royal Thai College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RTCOG) [12], have recommended that NIPT is a powerful tool for detecting CATs and can be offered to all pregnant women during their first antenatal care (ANC) visit, starting at approximately 9–10 weeks of gestation, and not only for those with advanced age [9, 10, 13].

Expanded NIPT is typically described as NIPT testing in addition to CATs, which may include rare autosomal trisomies (RATs), structural anomalies, and/or microdeletion/duplication syndrome. Several studies including a current meta-analysis have indicated that low diagnostic accuracy of expanded NIPT for RATs, partly due to their low prevalence, as such additional study or collective data of expanded NIPT for RATs is essential [14–22].

The majority of published data were from countries with high income levels and advanced education, whereas data on expanded NIPT from middle and low-income countries are restricted, likely due to limited technology development, financial barriers, or cost of testing, and health literacy of the general population.

In Thailand, NIPT was first introduced into clinical practice in 2012 as an alternative prenatal screening option alongside sonographic and maternal serum markers [23, 24]. In 2015, the first next-generation sequencing for NIPT service laboratory in Thailand was developed at Ramathibodi Hospital. Hence, it is called “Thai NIPT” [25]. It had a sensitivity and specificity ≥ 99.9% for T21, T18, T13, and chromosomal X aneuploidies; and positive predictive value (PPV) of 83.30%, 91.40%, 95.10%, and 74.20%, respectively [25]. The NIPT for CATs and sex chromosomal aneuploidies (SCAs) has been commercialized across the country as a university-based service laboratory for prenatal screening in addition to foreign commercial laboratories. Subsequently, NIPT for RATs has been offered as an additional request since 2017, with no standardization guaranteed. Of note, NIPT screening was not covered by the Thai Universal Health Coverage Scheme, only maternal serum screening was covered for all pregnant women (since 2019–2020).

Therefore, the objectives of this study were to evaluate the real-world data on clinical validity of an in-country developed expanded NIPT in detecting fetal CATs, SCAs, and RATs, and its clinical utility in prenatal diagnosis (PND) and pregnancy outcomes following its nationwide commercialization.

Materials and methods

The setting of NIPT services

Thai NIPT is jointly provided by the Division of Maternal-Fetal Medicine, Department of Obstetrics and Gynaecology, and the Center for Medical Genomics, Department of Human Genetics, Faculty of Medicine Ramathibodi Hospital. As for the methods in brief, cell-free fetal DNA was extracted from the maternal plasma, then quality checked and underwent massively parallel sequencing and analyzed according to established protocol [26, 27]. It provides routine screening for CATs and SCAs for all samples submitted, with screening for RATs based on an optional request. The NIPT Service has been commercially offered to any ANC units/hospitals since 2015. A validated Thai language information sheet on cell-free DNA screening is provided as an adjunctive tool for pretest counseling [28], and informed consent is obtained from all pregnant women who choose to undergo NIPT. The requisition form is filled out by the ordering obstetricians. The NIPT results, with a turnaround time of 2 weeks, are reported to the ordering physicians who would perform posttest counseling and downstream actions as appropriate.

During 2015–2019, the NIPT Service did not perform clinical follow-up or provide insurance for false positives (FP) and false negatives (FN) results; therefore, a systematic data collection of FP and FN was not available for quality review after its commercialization. Commencing 2020, the NIPT Service provides insurance and compensation for FP and FN for those with CATs (SCA and RATs not included), these strategies also enhance the reporting of FP and FN cases of CATs. The workflow of the NIPT service and the insurance regimes are detailed in the Supplementary data. In brief, the family was eligible for compensation in the event of FN NIPT for CATs, at 60,000 USD for those confirmed by postnatal diagnosis and 3,000 USD for those diagnosed prenatally. Furthermore, to effectively validate positive cases, the actual direct medical costs, including prenatal diagnostic procedures and the confirmatory tests, were fully covered or reimbursable at 375 USD. The insurance/compensation policy was not offered for SCAs and RATs.

Laboratory and bioinformatic analysis

All laboratory procedures were performed at the Center for Medical Genomics, Faculty of Medicine Ramathibodi Hospital, a medical laboratory accredited by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO 15189:2012). The present study utilizes an updated, independently validated platform, with the specific workflow detailed below.

Sample preparation and Sequencing

Ten milliliters of whole blood samples taken from the pregnant women were stored in Roche cell-free DNA collection tubes at 15°−30 °C. Cell-free fetal DNA was extracted from maternal plasma within 3–5 days of blood collection. Sequencing libraries were then prepared from the extracted cell-free fetal DNA using a fetal aneuploidies detection kit (CapitalBio Corp., China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. This process included end-repair, adapter ligation, and amplification of the cell-free fetal DNA fragments. For DNA sequencing, 20 libraries were pooled and sequenced using the Ion Proton System (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) with semiconductor sequencing technology.

Data processing, Quality control, and Failure management

The sequencer produced raw sequencing reads for each sample with read counts of at least three million and read lengths of approximately 200 bp. Raw data were subsequently filtered and aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner software. Duplicate reads were identified and marked using Picard Tools, and only unique reads were used for subsequent aneuploidy risk assessments.

All processed samples were subjected to stringent quality control (QC) criteria prior to final analysis. The fetal fraction was calculated as a QC measure using a previously described method [26]. The key passing thresholds were as follows: fetal fraction ≥ 4%, usable reads ≥ 3 million, DNA library concentration ≥ 1 nmol, duplicate rate ≤ 20%, GC content between 38 and 45%.

Of note, the average fetal fraction obtained in our laboratory was 12–15%, the average read depth was 3.5 million, and the average GC content was 41%.

A specific protocol was followed for samples that did not initially meet these QC criteria. For failures related to laboratory processes such as cell-free fetal DNA extraction, library construction, or sequencing, a one-time repeat of the procedure was performed on the original sample. Samples with a fetal fraction below 4% were considered ‘initial no-call’ results, in which a new blood sample for a one-time redraw was requested. Samples that failed to pass QC after this repeat or redraw attempt were considered ‘persistent no-call’ results and excluded from the final data analysis.

Fetal CATs (T21, T18, and T13) were detected using a combined GC correction and z-score testing method, as previously described [26]. The z-scores were calculated for each chromosome of interest (13, 18, 21, X, and Y) by comparing the normalized chromosome representation in each test sample to the mean and standard deviation derived from a reference set of euploid control pregnancies. A z-score of >3 was used as the cutoff to determine whether the chromosome representation was increased and to indicate a fetal trisomy for chromosomes 21, 18, or 13. This z-score of 3 represented a chromosome ratio greater than the 99.9th percentile of the reference samples.

To detect sex chromosome aneuploidies, a linear relationship between the X and Y chromosome z-scores was established in female fetuses. Classification criteria based on the X and Y z-scores, as well as their relationship, were used to identify sex chromosome abnormalities such as 45,X; 47,XXX; 47,XXY; and 47,XYY.

Data collection and analysis

This is an observational study. The results of NIPT performed during January 2020 and December 2022 were collected. Inclusion criteria were singleton pregnancy and gestational age > 10 weeks. Demographic data, including maternal and gestational age at blood sampling, gravida, parity, and an indication of NIPT screening, were extracted from the requisition forms. During the study, pregnant women with high-risk results were contacted via telephone at around 3 months from the expected date of delivery and asked about the posttest counseling by their primary obstetricians, fetal ultrasound findings, prenatal confirmatory tests, including their results, and the pregnancy outcomes. In some cases, the data were obtained through the ordering physicians. For women having ANC at Ramathibodi Hospital, their medical records were reviewed to obtain the target data. Detailed information on insurance claims for FPs and FNs was also collected. For low-risk NIPT results, we did not perform a routine clinical follow-up, except for those with insurance claims. Exclusion criteria were inconclusive screening results, incomplete data, and loss of follow-up.

The clinical validity of NIPT was calculated using the results of definitive diagnosis as the gold standard, including karyotyping, chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA), and/or quantitative fluorescence polymerase chain reaction (qfPCR). Clinical validity refers to sensitivity (detection rate), specificity (1-FPR, false positive rate), positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). The clinical utility was determined by the rate of prenatal diagnostic tests performed and the pregnancy outcomes. Additionally, a comparison of NIPT clinical validity between high-risk and low-risk pregnancies was evaluated using a test of proportions.

The study was approved by the Ramathibodi Hospital Institutional Review Board (MURA2022/767), which complied with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using STATA, version 18 (Stata Corp., College Station, TX, USA). Descriptive statistics were reported as mean ± standard deviation, median and range for continuous data, while categorical variables are shown as frequencies and percentages. Statistical significance was assessed using t-tests, chi-squared tests, or Fisher’s exact tests, with a p-value of < 0.05 considered statistically significant. NIPT’s predictive performance indices (sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV) were calculated using the results of prenatal diagnostic tests as the gold standard. The PPV was calculated with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Because of the limited number of cases, especially in the RATs subgroup, the confidence intervals (CIs) for PPV estimates were wide, reflecting increased uncertainty in these estimates.

Results

Demographic data and prenatal diagnostic rate

A total of 19,968 NIPT results were eligible for retrospective review; these specimens were submitted by 143 ANC units (40 hospitals and 103 private clinics), from 51 (out of 73) provinces throughout Thailand (Supplementary figure (Figure S1) in the Supplementary data). Of these results, 41 cases (0.21%) were ‘initial no-call’ results, 214 cases (1.07%) were excluded due to incomplete data (n = 200) and inconclusive test results (n = 14). The inconclusive results were attributed to low fetal fraction, poor DNA quality, or high-risk trisomies involving multiple chromosomes after redraw attempts—collectively referred to as ‘persistent no-call’ results, accounting for 0.07%.

Among 19,754 cases of standard testing for CATs and SCAs, 12,936 (65.48%) cases requested additional analysis for RATs. The mean maternal age was 33.65 ± 4.42 years, and the mean gestational age at blood sampling was 13.53 ± 2.02 weeks. Over half of the cases, 53.20% (10,509/19,754) requested NIPT because of high-risk pregnancy. These included indications consisting of advanced maternal age (51.17%), abnormal maternal serum screening (0.99%), abnormal sonographic markers (0.54%), a prior child with an abnormality (0.27%), or multiple risk factors (0.22%). The remaining 46.80% were considered low-risk (maternal request without a history of increased risk for chromosomal aneuploidies; Table 1). We found 187 out of 19,754 cases with high-risk NIPT results, indicating an overall positive rate of 0.95% for CATs, SCAs, and RATs together (Fig. 1). Data on the downstream actions were available from 147 cases (124 by telephone and 23 by reviewing medical records). We failed to contact the other 40 cases.

Detailed non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) results and pregnancy outcomes

Of note, the distribution of high-risk NIPT results among the total 187 cases was as follows: 73 T21, 32 T18, 14 T13, 49 SCAs, and 19 RATs; whereas the distribution of high-risk NIPT results among the 40 missing cases was as follows: 15 T21, 7 T18, 2 T13, 13 SCAs, and 3 RATs (Fig. 1).

Among the pregnancies eligible for PND, 137 out of 142 (96.48%) cases underwent the procedure (Fig. 1) as follows: 135 amniocenteses and 2 chorionic villous samplings; 127 cases using karyotype analysis with or without qfPCR/CMA and 10 cases by qfPCR only. None reported miscarriage following the procedures.

High-risk NIPT results for CATs and SCAs and pregnancy outcomes

For the standard analyses, we identified 168 out of 187 cases with high risk, including 73 T21, 32 T18, 14 T13, and 49 SCAs, indicating a positive rate of 0.85% (0.60% for CATs and 0.25% for SCAs). Ten cases with high-risk NIPT results did not have a PND done due to spontaneous miscarriage (5 cases) and choosing TOP (3 cases with abnormal fetal ultrasound findings and 2 cases without fetal abnormality detected). Prenatal diagnosis confirmed the following true positive (TP) cases: 47 T21, 16 T18, 6 T13, and 18 SCAs (Fig. 1 and Supplementary tables (Table S1) in the Supplementary data). Among those TP, 100% (69/69) of CATs and 38.89% (7/18) of SCAs opted for termination of pregnancy (TOP) (Table 2). The false positive rate was 0.1% for CATs and 0.08% for SCAs.

High-risk NIPT results for RATs and pregnancy outcomes

There were 16 NIPT results with high risk for RATs, including 3T7, 2T12, 2T15, 2T22, 1T1, 1T2, 1T3, 1T5, 1T14, 1T16, 1T20, indicating a positive rate of 0.12%. Fifteen of 16 cases underwent prenatal confirmation, which showed TP in two cases, of whom one chose TOP and the other went on till delivery (Table 2 and Supplementary tables (Table S2) in the supplementary data). The false positive rate for RATs was 0.1%.

Low-risk NIPT results

Among 19,567 (99.05%) cases with low-risk NIPT results, we observed 5 cases with FN results, including 3 CATs (1 T21, 1 T18, 1 T13) and 2 SCAs, as confirmed by the definitive tests (4 prenatal and 1 postnatal diagnosis). Three cases were ended by TOP following PND performed because of abnormal fetal ultrasound findings (Supplementary tables (Table S3) in the Supplementary data). Placental samples were not available in these cases; therefore, confined fetal mosaicism could not be ruled out.

Clinical validity of NIPT

The incidence of CATs and SCAs was 0.37% and 0.10%, respectively, in the present study. The sensitivity of NIPT in detecting T21, T18, T13, and SCAs was 97.92%, 94.12%, 85.71%, and 90%, respectively, and the specificity was 99.96%, 99.96%, 99.97%, and 99.92%, respectively. The PPV for T21, T18, T13, and SCAs was 87.04% (95% CI: 75.10–94.63), 69.57% (95% CI: 47.08–86.79), 50.00% (95% CI: 21.09–78.91), and 54.55% (95% CI: 36.35–71.89), in respective order. The NPV was 99.99% for all CATs and SCAs (Table 3).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the largest cohort of expanded NIPT studies representing a real-world experience in Thailand. It showed a screening positive rate of 0.95%, high sensitivity for detecting CATs and SCAs, ranging from 85.71 to 97.92%, with excellent specificity and NPV from 99.98 to 100%. The PPV for RATs was low at 13.33%, despite excellent sensitivity, specificity, and NPV. The prenatal diagnostic rate following high-risk NIPT results was high at 96.48%. The FP rate was low, 0.08–0.10%, for CATs, SCAs, and RATs.

Assuming that, without NIPT and all high-risk cases would opt for invasive diagnostic testing, NIPT could potentially reduce the number of invasive procedures by approximately 98% (10,322/10,509). However, in reality, patients may choose other prenatal screening options, and not all high-risk cases would undergo invasive testing. In such scenarios, the actual impact of NIPT in reducing invasive procedures would be lower.

The sensitivity and PPV for CAT’s and SCAs of the present study appear similar to published data [16, 22, 25, 29]. More precisely, the sensitivity for overall CATs, especially T18 and T13, is slightly lower than those reported in other studies [8, 15, 23, 29–32]. Similarly, the RATs sensitivity in this study was 100% and the PPV was 13%. The meta-analysis showed papers ranging from 87.2 to 100% sensitivity and specificity ranging from 90.7 to 99.9% with the pooled PPV of 11.4%. In all, the results of our study are comparable to other published papers and the meta-analysis [15, 16, 20, 22].

For sensitivity analysis, assuming cases with positive screening but declining PND were counted as FP, except for those with spontaneous abortion or elective TOP due to abnormal fetal ultrasound findings, which were counted as TP; there were 2 FP cases and 8 TP cases (cases 1–2 and cases 3–10, respectively; Supplementary tables (Table S1) in the Supplementary data). The sensitivity of NIPT would be recalculated and revealed slightly increased sensitivity at 98.00%, 94.74%, 85.71%, 91.30% for T21, T18, T13, and SCAs in an orderly (Supplementary tables (Table S4) in the Supplementary data).

The number of pregnant women adopting PND following high-risk NIPT results was impressively high in the present study, 96.48%, as compared to the other 66.86–84.26% [15, 16, 23, 30]. The reasons for a high rate of prenatal diagnoses are possibly due to test familiarity among Thai pregnant women and the healthcare providers since NIPT has been around in the country for over 10 years, an informative pretest counseling tool kit in a native language, and the procedure-related risk mitigation and insurance compensation provided by NIPT Service. Moreover, having test insurance and an established clinical follow-up system could potentiate the feedback, report of TP, FP and FN, and the quality assurance of the screening system, which result in a continuing improvement of antenatal screening and care.

Previous studies have reported no-call rates ranging from 0% to 12% [8, 23]. The main reason for a no-call result is a low fetal fraction, which can be influenced by factors such as maternal body mass index, maternal health conditions, and fetal aneuploidy status [5, 8–11]. In our study, the initial no-call rate was 0.21%, which is comparatively low, most likely due to the relatively high fetal fractions (12–15%) in our cohort.

The reasons for discordant NIPT results included placental mosaicism, vanishing twins, fetal mosaicism, maternal chromosome abnormality, the biostorage service, and the analytical laboratory problems [30, 33–38]. Stringent protocol, quality management, and investigation of FP and FN results should be further conducted to improve the clinical performance of the test.

Previous studies have shown that NIPT for T13 and 18 has slightly lower sensitivity than for T21, primarily due to their lower incidence and lower average GC content [39, 40]. Additionally, sequencing depth lower than 5 million reads (depth), a lower average of fetal fraction (acceptable: ≥4%, mean average 10–12%, and higher-than-average: >15%), recommended of GC content of 40–41% close to the average GC content at 40% of the human genome, and the algorithm and the calibration of the bioinformatics pipeline used are also influencing the sensitivities and false positive and false negative rates of the NIPT [8–10, 39–41]. The reasons for the sensitivity for T13 and 18 in the present study being lower than those of prior studies could be due to a wide range of GC content (38–45% instead of 40–41% often recommended), and our lower average sequencing depths (3.5 million). Hence, the sensitivity of our study could be improved through increasing GC content (GC bias correction), increasing sequencing depth, and optimizing the algorithm and the calibration of the software platforms.

From the data on pregnancy outcomes, all cases with confirmed fetal CATs and three-fourths of those with monosomy X culminated in a terminated pregnancy. These findings are consistent with recent studies, which found that genetic anomalies influenced parental decisions to terminate a pregnancy [15, 42]. This screening test (i.e., the NIPT) impacts prenatal management; therefore, the clinical performance of NIPT should be continuously reviewed and enhanced. As for SCAs, 71.43% of pregnancies with TP for monosomy X were terminated by parental choices, whereas the TOP rate was lower at 18.18% for other types of SCAs (XXX, XXY, XYY) (Supplementary tables (Table S5) in the Supplementary data).

Although NIPT has been extended to use for detecting RATs, its clinical applications are heavily debated because of low test performance, specifically PPV [14–16, 20, 22, 43]. Our data support the previous findings of the low test performance of RATs. It has been shown that a significant proportion (65%) of confined placental mosaicism (CPM) (type 3) is associated with FP NIPT results [15, 20, 44, 45]. When considering factors such as fetal mosaicism, uniparental disomy (UPD), and growth restriction caused by CPM, a high-risk RATs result proves to be clinically relevant in approximately 50% of cases—well beyond the 13% indicated by the positive predictive value alone [46]. Low PAPP-A levels have been associated with an increased risk of fetal growth restriction in some RATs cases, especially those with UPD 16 and 20 [46]. Unfortunately, UPD testing for chromosomes 6, 7, 11, 14, 15, and 20 were not performed in the present study.

Interestingly, despite the very low PPV for RATs, the majority (65%) of the women requested this test in the present study. This finding was comparable to 73% of the Netherlands cohort [16]. This might reflect that the pregnant women were not well informed about the low validity of NIPT for RATs screening; in this case, improvement of pretest counseling is essential. Conversely, pregnant women may be willing to accept the low PPV given the low screening positive rate and the high sensitivity of the test and may still choose to proceed with screening because of the potential severity of autosomal chromosome disorders. For the latter, it possibly indicates an unmet need for prenatal RATs screening, and the continuing development of a more robust screening for RATs is warranted to advance the quality of antenatal care.

According to the Royal Thai College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RTCOG), confirmatory testing is recommended for all positive NIPT results prior to any decision regarding pregnancy termination [12]. These include conventional karyotyping, quantitative fluorescence PCR (qfPCR), or chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA). Among these, karyotyping is most widely used nationwide due to its greater availability. In contrast, qfPCR and CMA are generally limited to specialized centers where these techniques are accessible, with CMA most often offered for multiple fetal anomalies detected by ultrasound. In the context of prenatal diagnostic procedures, amniocentesis is widely performed by general obstetricians, whereas chorionic villus sampling (CVS) is typically executed by maternal–fetal medicine specialists.

There were few pregnant women with high-risk NIPT results who chose TOP without fetal ultrasound abnormality or a confirmed diagnosis (2 cases or 1.40%; Supplementary tables (Table S1) in the Supplementary data), representing a critical misuse of the test, which underlines the importance of pretest and posttest counseling [23, 30, 47]. Also, there were a few women with high-risk NIPT results choosing TOP solely based on abnormal ultrasound findings (3 cases or 2.11%). Previous studies showed that significant factors contributing to a decision for interruption of pregnancy included early identification of fetal anomalies, genetic conditions, and presumed lethality [42, 48, 49].

The major strengths of the present study were that it is a large cohort of expanded NIPT as the first-tier screening in almost all the cases (99.01% of participants) and that it represented real-world clinical experience from a middle-income country where socioeconomics and other social health determinants could be different form well developed countries. Data from this study alarms for the need for revalidation of the test performance, proven excellent in the past that its validity could become lower attributed to several reasons, such as – quality assurance problems of the laboratory related issues, the natural target population change, specifically from a test population with a prominent proportion of high-risk pregnancies to a lower proportion owing to the growing use of the test by pregnant women without increased risk, and lower cost over time (Supplementary tables (Table S6) in the Supplementary data) [20, 23, 25]. As a matter of fact, with a relatively increased proportion of low-risk pregnancies among the screened population, lower PPV is predictable, as shown by many studies [9, 16, 47]. Additionally, our data also contributes significant data on RATs, specifically their performance and addressing the unmet need for RATs screening.

There are some limitations to this study. First, there was no regular confirmation of pregnancy outcomes for cases with low-risk NIPT results; therefore, some FN cases could be missing, with the exception of those claiming insurance. Moreover, the sensitivity of NIPT for SCAs and RATs could be less accurate as compared to CATs due to no incentive to report false negative results and the lack of systematic testing of all newborns. Second, the absence of information on maternal chromosomal abnormalities (e.g., maternal mosaicism), which are known confounders that can lead to false-positive results in NIPT. Third, there was a significant percentage of missing data, 21.40% (40/187), due to loss of follow-up among the high-risk NIPT results, which might impact the test performance analysis. However, the effect size is likely small given no statistical differences in patient characteristics and comparable distributions of the individual high-risk NIPT results between the observed and missing cases (Fig. 1 and Table S7 in the Supplementary data). Nevertheless, some potential bias may persist, such as the lost cases were more likely to have normal outcomes, which could lead to underestimating the false positive rate. Fourth, the study is small in numbers with only 16 cases with high-risk result for RATs so the calculated PPVs may have wide confidence intervals. Fifth, UPD testing was not done; therefore, data on this possible disorder could be missing. Lastly, this is a retrospective study so a recall bias could occur.

Conclusion

The study revealed high sensitivity and specificity of the NIPT for T21, T18, T13, and SCAs, although a little lower sensitivity and PPV for T18, T13, and SCAs, but higher PPV for T21, as compared to that standardized 10 years ago. This difference is likely due to the target population change (higher proportion of non-advanced age pregnant women taking the test) and the laboratory-related factors. The test performance indices for RATs were low, close to those observed by other studies. The changing target population screened and the high demand for RATs screening urged for continuing improvement of the screening test for chromosomal abnormalities during pregnancy. Overall, the findings of our study are comparable to published data from Western countries.
