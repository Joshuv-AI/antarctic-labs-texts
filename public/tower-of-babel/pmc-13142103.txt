Protocol for isolation of lipid-laden hepatocytes from high-fat diet-fed and metabolically challenged mice
Authors: Thiruvelselvan Ponnusamy, Kalimuthusamy Natarajaseenivasan, Santhanam Shanmughapriya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe present a protocol to isolate hepatocytes from mice fed a high-fat diet (HFD), modeling features of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and metabolically associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD). We perfuse and enzymatically digest the liver and then purify hepatocytes using a modified Percoll gradient (30%, 25%, and 10%) to retain lipid-laden cells. This protocol enables the isolation of both normal and lipid-filled hepatocytes for culture and downstream studies of drug metabolism, hepatotoxicity, and glucose or lipid metabolism in vitro.

Before you begin

Hepatocytes derived from HFD-fed or metabolically compromised mice exhibit substantial intracellular lipid accumulation,1 which alters their buoyant density and complicates isolation. The isolation of lipid-laden hepatocytes is essential for metabolic research, as lipid droplet sizes vary with disease progression and animal age.2 Lipid droplet size and distribution vary with disease progression, age, and liver zonation,3 necessitating careful optimization of perfusion and purification parameters. Compared with hepatocytes from young, healthy mice, those from steatotic livers are more fragile and require lower perfusion flow rates to maintain viability. Standard hepatocyte isolation methods often fail to recover lipid-rich hepatocytes because these cells are less dense and are lost during high-density Percoll purification. To address this limitation, we describe a protocol in which the Percoll gradients (≤20%–30%) were adjusted based on diet duration and metabolic status to retain both normal and lipid-laden hepatocytes. This approach improves recovery and yields a more representative hepatocyte population for downstream metabolic, imaging, and gene expression analyses.4 We adapted this protocol from published methods with modifications to improve the recovery of lipid-laden hepatocytes.5,6 Filter-sterilize all buffers (0.2 μm) before use. Prepare a 90% Percoll stock solution by mixing 90 mL Percoll with 10 mL 10× PBS.

We used C57BL/6 J mice for hepatocyte isolation. C57BL/6 J mice were housed under standard conditions and fed a high-fat diet (HFD) or a chow diet, as specified in the experimental design. Mice were initially fed a standard chow diet for 3 months. High-fat diet (HFD) feeding was then initiated based on the total age of the experimental group.

Innovation

Conventional hepatocyte isolation protocols using fixed Percoll gradients are optimized for lean, healthy livers and often fail to recover lipid-laden hepatocytes from metabolically compromised models. In HFD-fed mice, hepatocytes accumulate intracellular lipid droplets, reducing their buoyant density and leading to their loss during standard purification steps. This protocol introduces an optimized approach that systematically adjusts Percoll gradient concentrations (10%–30%) based on age, diet, and metabolic status to improve recovery of both normal and lipid-laden hepatocytes. By integrating controlled perfusion flow rates tailored for obese or steatotic livers, the protocol minimizes vascular damage while preserving cell viability. In addition, it incorporates defined criteria for perfusion efficiency and enzymatic digestion to ensure consistent tissue dissociation. A key advancement of this protocol is its ability to retain and isolate hepatocytes with altered density resulting from lipid accumulation, thereby enabling the simultaneous recovery of heterogeneous hepatocyte populations from the same liver. This is particularly important for studying metabolic dysfunction, as conventional methods selectively bias against pathological cells. Overall, this protocol provides a reproducible and adaptable strategy for isolating functionally relevant hepatocytes from HFD-fed mice and can be applied to models of fatty liver disease. It enables downstream applications, including lipid imaging, metabolic assays, and gene expression analyses, thereby improving the physiological relevance of in vitro studies.

Institutional permissions

The isolation of hepatocytes from mice on standard and high-fat diets, and from mice with metabolic disorders, should be performed in accordance with local and institutional regulations and be approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Mice should be kept under standard housing conditions with controlled temperature, humidity, and a 12-h light/dark cycle. Animals fed a high-fat diet require careful monitoring of body weight, liver size, and overall health before hepatocyte isolation. For the experiments described here, only diet-induced obese mice were used; although this protocol can be adapted for NAFLD or NASH models, such models were not employed in the current study.

Timing: 30–40 min

Limitations

Hepatocyte isolation efficiency varies with mouse strain, age, and diet, with high-fat diet (HFD) models presenting enlarged, fragile livers that require careful adjustment of perfusion flow rates. The procedure is sensitive to technical variability, as minor differences in catheterization, flow rate, or perfusion pressure can affect digestion efficiency and cell viability. Enzyme activity, particularly Liberase, may vary between batches, necessitating routine validation. Lipid-laden hepatocytes are more fragile and prone to damage during isolation, resulting in reduced viability compared to cells from lean controls. Strict temperature control (37°C) and rapid processing (within 60–90 min) are essential but limit throughput. Percoll gradient optimization is required for each condition to balance yield and purity, as lower densities may increase contamination with non-parenchymal cells. The protocol is susceptible to microbial contamination and is difficult to scale. Primary hepatocytes also have a limited culture lifespan (3–5 days), and residual Percoll may interfere with downstream assays. Finally, isolation from severely steatotic or fibrotic livers remains challenging due to impaired perfusion. While this protocol improves recovery of lipid-laden hepatocytes from HFD-fed mice, we did not perform systematic quantitative comparisons of cell yield, viability, or metabolic function relative to conventional Percoll gradients. Consequently, the advantages of the modified approach are supported primarily by qualitative assessments, including cell morphology, attachment, and lipid droplet preservation. Future studies incorporating quantitative yield measurements and functional assays will be necessary to rigorously evaluate hepatocyte recovery and metabolic competence.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Catheter puncturing the inferior vena cava during catheterization and while perfusing.

Potential solution

As mentioned earlier, in the Catherization of the inferior vena cava subheading, try to place two sutures around the inferior vena cava, spaced apart, to keep the catheter straight.

Problem 2

Blood remains in the liver after perfusion.

Potential solution

This typically occurs due to incorrect catheter placement or low perfusion flow rate. Ensure the catheter is correctly inserted into the inferior vena cava (Catherization of the inferior vena cava) and that a small amount of blood backflows before starting perfusion. Confirm the portal vein is cut to allow drainage, and maintain a flow rate of 5 mL/min for normal mice. Remove any air bubbles in the tubing to prevent obstruction.

Problem 3

Liver does not blanch or swell during perfusion.

Potential solution

Lack of liver blanching indicates incomplete perfusion, often due to low pressure, blocked tubing, or improper catheter alignment. Check that the perfusion pump is calibrated, the tubing is free of kinks, and the perfusion buffer is prewarmed to 37°C (Perfusion with buffer). Gently reinsert the catheter if needed to reestablish flow.

Problem 4

Liver ruptures or swells excessively during perfusion.

Potential solution

A ruptured or overly swollen liver suggests the perfusion rate is too high, leading to vascular rupture. For older or high-fat diet (HFD) mice, reduce the flow rate to 3 to 4 mL/min (Enzymatic Digestion). Avoid over-tightening sutures and monitor the liver visually for gentle expansion during perfusion.

Problem 5

Reduced single cells in suspension.

Potential solution

Tap the liver tissue gently against the petridish to induce hepatocyte dissociation (Liver removal and cell dissociation) at the single-cell level. Increase the liberase concentration and reduce the flow rate of the digestion buffer to increase the digestion.

Problem 6

Liver remains firm after digestion due to poor enzymatic digestion.

Potential solution

Insufficient digestion results from reduced enzyme activity (Enzymatic digestion). Always prepare the digestion buffer fresh, adding CaCl2 to a final concentration of 1 mM and Liberase to a final concentration of 10 μg/mL immediately before perfusion. Keep Liberase on ice, avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles, and confirm that the perfusion temperature remains at 37°C to optimize enzyme efficiency.

Problem 7

Excessive tissue damage or over-digestion of liver tissue.

Potential solution

Prolonged enzyme perfusion can damage hepatocytes, reducing viability (Liver removal and cell dissociation). Stop perfusion once the liver turns pale and softens. Immediately transfer the liver into the digestion buffer without Liberase, and add plating medium to neutralize residual enzyme activity.

Problem 8

Low hepatocyte yield after centrifugation.

Potential solution

Low yield may result from cell loss during centrifugation or aspiration (Liver removal and cell dissociation). Use gentle centrifugation at 50 × g for 2 min, ensuring the rotor is appropriately balanced. Do not aspirate too close to the pellet. If yield remains low, check digestion efficiency and perfusion completeness.

Problem 9

High proportion of dead or damaged hepatocytes

Potential solution

Cell death often results from the introduction of air bubbles, overdigestion, or temperature fluctuations (setting up perfusion and digestion buffers for perfusion). Prime the perfusion line to remove all air bubbles, maintain buffers at 37°C, and minimize handling time. If viability is below 80%, reduce enzyme concentration or perfusion duration.

Problem 10

Cells floating in the Percoll gradient.

Potential solution

When fewer cells are in the pellet, and more cells are in the supernatant or floating in the Percoll, the Percoll concentration is reduced (Density-based purification). If the cells in the supernatant contain larger or smaller droplets, reduce the Percoll concentration, as collecting from the supernatant may include non-viable cells, which can decrease attachment and affect assay efficiency.

Problem 11

Cell clumping or debris in suspension.

Potential solution

Clumping can occur due to free DNA from lysed cells or incomplete filtration (Liver removal and Cell dissociation). Pass the suspension through a 100 μm cell strainer twice, and include DNase I (10 μg/mL) in the digestion buffer to degrade free DNA. Avoid vigorous pipetting to minimize shear stress.

Problem 12

Poor hepatocyte attachment to collagen-coated plates.

Potential solution

Weak attachment may result from insufficient collagen coating or plating density (Plate preparation and cell culture). Coat plates with 1% collagen for 2 h at 37°C and wash twice with PBS. Adjust plating density for lipid-laden hepatocytes, as they are larger and attach less efficiently. Verify cell viability before plating.

Problem 13

Abnormal hepatocyte morphology after plating.

Potential solution

Cells that remain rounded or fail to spread have likely experienced temperature shock or mechanical stress. Prewarm all media and solutions to 37°C, and handle cells gently during transfer (Buffer set up). Avoid repeated pipetting.

Problem 14

Low recovery of lipid-laden hepatocytes from HFD mice.

Potential solution

Lipid-rich hepatocytes are less dense and can be lost during Percoll centrifugation if the density is too high (Density-based purification). Reduce Percoll concentration to 10%–20% and centrifuge at 200 × g for 20 min with slow acceleration/deceleration at 4°C. Adjust density based on mouse age and lipid accumulation.

Problem 15

Contamination in cultures after plating.

Potential solution

Contamination commonly results from incomplete sterilization of perfusion tubing or instruments (Buffer setup). Clean the entire setup by flushing with 70% ethanol, followed by sterile PBS before perfusion. Perform all post-isolation procedures under a biosafety cabinet (Class II laminar flow hood) using sterile reagents.

Problem 16

Low reproducibility between isolations.

Potential solution

Variability in yield or viability may arise from differences in enzyme potency, mouse age, or metabolic status. Use mice of similar age, weight, and diet, and maintain consistent perfusion temperature and flow rate. Validate each new Liberase batch for activity before use. Consistency in handling, timing, and reagent preparation is key to reproducibility.
