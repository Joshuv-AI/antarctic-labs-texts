Occupational health and safety among tea industry workers in the Ilam district of Nepal: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Rasmita Shrestha, Aditya Shakya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTea industry workers are exposed to various occupational hazards during tea processing. The use of personal protective equipment (PPE) is essential to reduce these risks; however, compliance remains a concern in many developing countries, including Nepal. This study aimed to assess PPE utilisation and its associated factors among tea industry workers in Ilam district, Nepal.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 269 tea workers in Ilam district. Data were collected using a semi-structured questionnaire through face-to-face interviews. Descriptive statistics and inferential analyses were performed to identify factors associated with PPE use.ResultsMost workers reported using at least one type of PPE; that is, 82.5%; however, adherence to the complete set was low (13%). Type of work (adjusted prevalence ratio (aPR)=0.23; 95% CI 0.11 to 0.45) and the provision of complete PPE free of cost by employers (aPR=3.16; 95% CI 1.20 to 8.27) were significantly associated with PPE utilisation among workers.ConclusionDespite the use of individual PPE items by many workers, adherence to the complete set of protective equipment remains low among tea industry workers in Ilam. Strengthening employer responsibility for the provision of complete PPE free of cost, based on the type of work, may improve PPE utilisation and contribute to reducing occupational health risks.

Introduction

Occupational health and safety in agriculture is a growing global concern, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where weak enforcement of safety standards and limited protective measures expose workers to significant risks.1 According to the International Labour Organization (ILO), around 874 million people are employed in the agricultural sector globally, with an estimated 210 000 work-related deaths occurring annually.2 In Nepal, agriculture is one of the most hazardous occupations, with approximately 20 000 occupational accidents and 200 fatalities reported each year.34 Studies indicate that 48.6–69% of Nepalese farmers have experienced work-related injuries, while the use of personal protective equipment (PPE) remains critically low.5–8

Tea production is a cornerstone of the global agricultural economy, employing over 13 million workers worldwide, predominantly in LMICs such as Nepal, India and Kenya.9 In Nepal, the tea industry employs over 15 000 small farmers, and during the harvest/picking season, approximately 350 000 workers are employed, and Ilam district is one of the major hubs for tea production in the country, especially for orthodox tea.10 This sector is characterised by substandard work environments, limited knowledge and understanding of fundamental issues, low productivity and skill levels and insufficient availability of public services.11 Furthermore, informal labour arrangements, gender disparities in wages and healthcare access and systemic underreporting of occupational illnesses challenge the health and safety of workers in this sector.1213 Despite its economic importance, tea cultivation exposes workers to numerous occupational hazards, including prolonged exposure to agrochemicals, ergonomic strain from repetitive motions and heavy loads, as well as chronic pesticide exposure resulting in respiratory symptoms (eg, cough, chest tightness and rhinorrhea), and respiratory diseases such as occupational asthma and allergic conditions and dermatological conditions.14–20 In addition, exposure to high noise levels in tea processing factories, coupled with low use of hearing protection, contributes to an increased risk of occupational noise-induced hearing loss.21 According to a study conducted in Nepal, the most common health problems among tea workers of Nepal were musculoskeletal disorders, gastritis and duodenitis, injuries and anaemia.22 Globally, ILO emphasises the urgent need for context-specific occupational safety interventions in agriculture, particularly in LMICs where informal labour structures and climate vulnerabilities intersect.23 Although Nepal’s current labour legislation identifies tea estates alongside construction, transportation and the hotel sector as key areas of focus, it provides only a brief and broadly defined section on occupational health and safety. This section offers limited direction regarding the protection of workers’ health and overall well-being.2425 Weak enforcement of the Labour Act and fragmented healthcare infrastructure further exacerbate risks to the occupational safety and health (OSH) of the worker.

By identifying health risks, patterns of PPE usage, and influencing factors, the findings of this study will inform targeted workplace interventions and guide local and national policy development. Ultimately, the research aimed to contribute to safer working environments and improved health outcomes for workers.

Materials and methods

Study design

A cross-sectional, institution-based, quantitative research design was used in this study. Tea industry workers employed in selected tea factories of Ilam district were the study population.

Setting for the study

Ilam district was purposively selected for this study due to its significance as one of Nepal’s primary tea-producing regions, especially for orthodox tea. The district is widely recognised as the tea hub of the country, with numerous tea estates and processing factories that employ a substantial portion of the local population. Given the concentration of tea-related industrial activities, Ilam provides an appropriate and relevant setting to study.

Sampling technique

A multistage sampling technique was employed to select the study participants.

Selection of municipalities

Out of the 10 municipalities in Ilam district, 2 rural municipalities and 4 urban municipalities were selected purposively based on the presence and operational status of tea processing factories.

Selection of factories

From each selected municipality, the largest functional tea factory was chosen based on the highest number of employed workers to ensure the inclusion of factories with substantial workforce representation.

Selection of participants

Within each selected factory, all available tea workers present during a scheduled health check-up camp organised at the factory premises were included in the study. This approach facilitates accessibility and ensures the inclusion of workers actively engaged in factory operations (figure 1).

Inclusion criteria for the study

All tea workers who were present in the scheduled health check-up camp organised at the factory premises and willing to participate in the study were included.

Data collection

Data were collected by authors (RS and AS) using a pretested semi-structured questionnaire. The questionnaire for the study was developed referring to related articles and tools that were found pertinent to the study objectives, and it was modified to suit the Nepalese context.578222728 The questionnaire was initially developed in the English language, translated into Nepali and back-translated into the English. It was pretested among tea workers of Suryodaya Municipality. The data were collected from June to July 2025. Data were collected by face-to-face interviews after receiving informed consent from the participants.

Patient and public involvement

The participants were not involved in the development or design of the research question or outcome measures for this study. They were not involved in the recruitment or conduct of the study. However, participants (tea workers) were included as respondents during data collection through face-to-face interviews. The findings of the study will be shared with relevant local health authorities and stakeholders. Efforts will also be made to disseminate the results to participating tea factories and workers through local channels where feasible.

Operational definition

Use of PPE

In this study, participants were from different occupational categories within the tea industry, including leaf pluckers, factory workers, labourers, pesticide sprayers and other support staff. Leaf pluckers were primarily involved in harvesting tea leaves, pesticide sprayers handled agrochemical application, factory workers were engaged in tea processing and machine operation, while labourers performed loading, transportation and other manual tasks. For this study, use of PPE was defined as the use of all worker-specific protective clothing and equipment required to protect against occupational health and safety hazards in the workplace.29 In this study, leaf pluckers and labourers were expected to wear a face mask, gloves, a full-sleeved shirt with long trousers, a hat and boots. Factory workers were expected to wear a face mask, gloves, boots, an apron, safety glasses and a cap/hat. Fertiliser and pesticide sprayers were expected to wear an appropriate face mask, chemical-resistant gloves, safety goggles, a full-sleeved shirt with long trousers, a hat and rubber boots. Drivers and others were expected to wear a face mask and safety shoes. Peons, security guards, cooks and managers were included in the category of others, and the PPE use was considered necessary only when they entered tea fields or factory production areas where occupational hazards were present. Workers were classified as PPE users if they reported using all PPE required for their specific job tasks.

Ethnicity

Ethnicity was classified according to the national classification used by the Government of Nepal.30 For analysis, participants were categorised as Brahmin/Chhetri, Janajati, Dalit, Madhesi and other ethnic groups. Brahmin/Chhetri generally represent traditionally advantaged caste groups, Janajati refers to indigenous nationalities, Dalit comprises historically marginalised caste groups and Madhesi includes people originating from the southern Terai plains of Nepal.

Data management and analysis

Data were entered in EpiData V.3.1, and the coded data were exported to IBM SPSS V.25 for further analysis. Descriptive statistical methods were applied to summarise the data. Categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages. Bivariate analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between the independent variables and the dichotomous outcome variable (use of PPE). Variables that showed a p value ≤0.05 in the bivariate analysis and had an expected cell count greater than five were entered into the multivariable regression model. ORs from logistic regression may overestimate the strength of association; therefore, a modified Poisson regression with robust variance estimation was employed to estimate prevalence ratios, which provide a more direct and interpretable measure of relative risk in cross-sectional studies. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05 with a 95% CI. Adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) with corresponding 95% Wald CIs were reported. The overall model was statistically significant (χ²=40.479, df=7, p<0.001), indicating that the predictors collectively improved the model fit for PPE use. The model showed no evidence of substantial overdispersion (Pearson χ²/df=0.816), supporting the adequacy of the fitted model.

Occupational hazards and PPE usage

Workers reported significant exposure to workplace hazards, particularly physical hazards (84.8%), which include heat (78.1%), cold (56.1%) and noise (53.1%). Chemical hazards (46.1%) mostly involved exposure to fertilisers (74.0%) and pesticides (52.9%). Other major risks include biological hazards (49.4%), which was dominated by insect bites (93.2%) and mechanical hazards (56.9%) which primarily included cuts/injuries (95.4%). Similarly, risk of exposure to psychological hazards was reported by 11.5% of the respondents. In this study, 82.5% of workers used at least one form of PPE, and only 13% used task-specific complete set of PPE. The most common PPE items used were gloves (80.6%) and full-sleeve dresses or aprons (77.5%). Notably, 14% of workers received no PPE from the organisation, and among those who did not use it, the most cited reason was lack of availability (85.1%) (table 3).

Health status and healthcare utilisation

Health concerns were prevalent, with 52.8% of workers reporting health problems. Among those reporting health problems, eye problems (62.1%) and hypertension (27.1%) were the most common conditions. Occupational injuries in the last year were reported by 9.3% of the workforce. Furthermore, 48.7% suffered from musculoskeletal disorders. When seeking care for any health problems, 86.2% visited government health facilities, and 81.4% preferred allopathic medicine. For 25.7% of respondents, there was barrier for health service utilisation, especially economic barrier i.e. 81.1%. For 47.6% of workers, the organisation provided periodic health checkups. Insurance coverage was modest, with only 40.1% of workers having health insurance. Among workers with health insurance, 90.7% were enrolled in the government health insurance scheme. Among enrollees, 58.3% of workers paid the premiums themselves. Regarding safety culture and awareness, 49.8% had received orientation on OSH, and 53.2% were aware of their right to a safe and healthy working environment. Additionally, 61.7% of workers reported that they were aware of the organisation’s policy of providing compensation for occupational accidents or injuries (table 4).

Discussion

PPE represents the final level of protection within the hierarchy of controls, following elimination or substitution of hazards, engineering controls and administrative controls. Despite being the last line of defence, it remains an essential component of occupational health and safety by mitigating residual occupational risks.

This study revealed a substantial gap in occupational health and safety practices among tea industry workers in Ilam district, Nepal. Although a majority of workers (82.5%) reported using at least one form of PPE, only 13% adhered to the complete recommended task-specific set. This finding suggests that partial compliance is common, while comprehensive protection remains limited. Comparable prevalence has been reported in other agricultural sectors in Nepal, where complete PPE usage was reported at 20% and 14.8%.58 Similar trends have also been observed internationally; for instance, a study among agricultural pesticide handlers in Malaysia reported a full PPE utilisation rate of only 17.2%.27 Conversely, a study among farmers in the Bardiya district of Nepal described complete PPE adoption as critically low (2.1%).31 These findings are consistent with global evidence highlighting occupational safety challenges in LMICs.1

More than half of the workers (52.8%) reported at least one health problem, indicating a substantial burden of morbidity among tea industry workers. Eye problems were the most common condition (62.1%), which may be related to prolonged exposure to dust, sunlight, plant materials and agrochemicals during work. A similarly high prevalence of eye problems has been reported among agricultural workers in southern Nigeria where 78.9% had one or more eye disorders.32

Musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) affected nearly half of the workers (48.7%), making them one of the major occupational health concerns identified in this study. This prevalence is comparable to the rate of MSDs reported among garment workers in Nepal (48.5%).33 The high burden of MSDs may be attributed to repetitive movements, prolonged standing, bending, awkward postures and manual handling of loads, which are common in tea plucking and processing activities. These findings highlight the need for ergonomic interventions and regular health screening to reduce work-related musculoskeletal problems.

Factory workers had a significantly lower prevalence of task-specific PPE use than leaf pluckers. This may be explained by the task-specific PPE definition used in this study, whereby factory workers were required to use specialised PPE, including aprons and safety glasses, in addition to masks, gloves, boots and caps. Previous studies have shown that compliance with specialised PPE, such as goggles and aprons, is generally lower than with basic protective clothing because these items may be uncomfortable, interfere with work activities or be inadequately available in the workplace.3435 In the present study, the use of safety glasses was generally low, with only 12.2% of workers reporting their use. Although workers who received complete PPE from the organisation were more likely to use safety glasses than those receiving partial or no PPE, the overall use remained low. Consequently, factory workers were less likely to meet the criteria for complete task-specific PPE use than leaf pluckers.

Among the factors examined, the provision of a complete set of PPE free of charge by the employer emerged as the strongest predictor of complete PPE utilisation. Notably, 85.1% of workers who did not use PPE reported lack of availability as the primary reason, a finding consistent with another Nepalese study.836 This also aligns with broader South Asian evidence, where informal labour structures and economic constraints often shift the burden of safety equipment onto the workers themselves, resulting in low PPE utilisation due to economic constraints.37 Similar findings have been reported in Ethiopia,38 where the utilisation of PPE was seven times higher if provided by the organisation. Collectively, these findings emphasise the critical responsibility of employers to ensure the adequate and appropriate provision of protective equipment tailored to occupational risks. In the absence of institutional support, PPE adoption remains inconsistent and suboptimal. Notably, partial provision of PPE in the study appeared less effective, which may reflect inadequate or inconsistent supply of complete protective equipment necessary for full compliance. In such situations, workers may perceive limited protective benefit from using incomplete gear, leading to reduced motivation for consistent use. Partial provision may also reflect weaker organisational commitment to occupational safety, which can negatively influence safety culture and compliance behaviour. The finding may also be influenced by the small number of workers who reported complete PPE utilisation within this category, resulting in less precise estimates. Therefore, this result should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, the finding suggests that providing a complete set of PPE may be more effective in promoting comprehensive PPE utilisation than providing only selected items.

Although occupational health and safety orientation showed a positive association with PPE use, the relationship was not statistically significant after adjustment. It suggests that training alone may not be sufficient to ensure consistent PPE use in practice. In this setting, structural and contextual factors such as availability of PPE and workplace conditions may play a more decisive role in influencing protective behaviour than awareness alone. However, unlike the present study, some previous studies have reported a positive association between OSH orientation and PPE compliance.38–40 These differences may be attributed to variations in the quality, content and implementation of training programmes, as well as differences in workplace safety culture and reinforcement of safety practices. In some settings, orientation programmes may be more structured and practice-oriented, which could enhance their effectiveness in improving PPE use. Other factors, awareness of OSH rights, and awareness of organisational compensation for occupational accidents and injuries, were not significantly associated with task-specific PPE.

Overall, the findings indicate that complete PPE utilisation among tea industry workers remains low and is strongly influenced by employer provision of protective equipment and the type of work. Strengthening workplace safety policies and ensuring the consistent availability of complete PPE sets may substantially improve worker protection

This study is among the first to assess OSH among tea industry workers in Ilam district, Nepal, addressing an important evidence gap and providing baseline data for future research and policy. It uniquely assessed adherence to the full recommended set of PPE rather than just any PPE use, offering a clearer picture of worker protection. The study also identified key predictors of PPE utilisation, providing practical insights for improving workplace safety interventions. However, this study has some limitations. First, due to the seasonal nature of tea production, data were collected during the summer season, when production and worker attendance are typically higher. Since occupational exposures and health outcomes may vary across seasons because of changes in temperature, humidity, and workload, the findings may not fully represent conditions throughout the entire year. Second, the study relied on self-reported information, which may be subject to recall and reporting bias.

Conclusion

This study identified important gaps in OSH practices among tea industry workers in Ilam district, Nepal. Although most workers used at least one type of PPE, adherence to the full recommended set was low, particularly for the factory workers, indicating inadequate protection against occupational hazards. The findings highlight the importance of ensuring the availability and accessibility of appropriate PPE in promoting its use among workers. To improve occupational safety, the tea industry should prioritise the regular provision of complete PPE sets based on the type of work performed and promote a workplace culture that supports safe work practices. In addition, greater regulatory oversight and enforcement of occupational health and safety standards are needed to enhance PPE compliance and reduce work-related health risks in Nepal’s tea industry.
