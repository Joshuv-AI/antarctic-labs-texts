Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease: pathogenic mechanisms and exercise-induced molecular adaptations
Authors: Senlin Lei, Jiasheng Tao, Xiaoan Chen, Xianhui Li, Songwei Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is a systemic metabolic disorder that spans steatosis, steatohepatitis, fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Although hepatic lipid overload is a central pathological feature, MASLD progression is driven by interconnected disturbances in lipid handling, mitochondrial function, oxidative stress, immune-inflammatory activation, programmed cell death, autophagy–lysosomal homeostasis, and gut–liver communication. This review critically synthesizes current evidence on the pathogenic mechanisms of MASLD and the molecular adaptations induced by exercise. We discuss how impaired lipid storage, oxidation, export, and detoxification interact with organelle stress, macrophage activation, inflammasome and cell-death signaling, defective autophagic clearance, and bidirectional gut–liver inputs to promote hepatocellular injury and fibrogenesis. We further examine how exercise modality, intensity, dose, and timing influence MASLD, and summarize evidence suggesting that exercise may enhance lipid mobilization and fatty acid oxidation, improve mitochondrial and autophagic quality control, restrain inflammatory and programmed cell-death pathways, and reshape gut–liver axis function. Defining disease-stage- and phenotype-specific responses, together with reliable metabolic and molecular biomarkers, will be essential for translating exercise-induced adaptations into individualized MASLD management.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has replaced the exclusion-based concept of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with a framework that identifies metabolic dysfunction as the major driver of steatotic liver disease [1, 2]. The earlier term metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) moved the field toward an inclusion-based diagnosis centered on hepatic steatosis plus obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), or other metabolic abnormalities [1, 3], and the current MASLD nomenclature further aligns steatosis with cardiometabolic risk factors [2]. This conceptual shift is clinically important because MASLD is now recognized as a systemic metabolic disorder rather than a liver-restricted phenotype. Its burden continues to increase worldwide, with an estimated global prevalence of 38.2% and projected prevalence of 55.4% by 2040 [4]. Global Burden of Disease data further indicate that nearly 1.27 billion individuals were affected by MASLD in 2021 [5]. These trends parallel expanding exposure to energy-dense diets, sedentary behavior, obesity, dysglycemia, and other metabolic comorbidities [6].

MASLD encompasses a dynamic disease spectrum from metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver (MASL) to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [2, 7]. The transition from MASL to MASH is not defined by hepatic fat content alone; rather, it occurs when free fatty acid (FFA) overload, lipotoxic intermediates, organelle stress, and inflammatory amplification exceed hepatocellular adaptive capacity [8]. Within this process, hepatocyte injury activates Kupffer cells (KCs) and monocyte-derived macrophages (MoMFs), promotes hepatic stellate cell (HSC)-mediated extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, and drives fibrogenesis [9, 10]. Genetic susceptibility, including patatin-like phospholipase domain-containing protein 3 (PNPLA3) variants, further modifies the threshold for steatosis, MASH, fibrosis, and HCC [11]. Fibrosis stage is the strongest histological predictor of long-term outcomes, and advanced fibrosis is associated with increased all-cause mortality and cardiovascular events [12, 13]. Mechanistically, MASLD progression reflects a mismatch between chronic metabolic input and the hepatic capacity to store, oxidize, export, and detoxify lipids. Persistent FFA overload and adipose tissue dysfunction promote lipotoxicity, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, immune-inflammatory activation, programmed cell death (PCD), autophagy-lysosomal impairment, and gut-liver axis disruption [9, 17]. These processes interact at several levels. Lipotoxic lipids damage mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER); injured hepatocytes and oxidized mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) provide danger signals to immune cells; inflammatory mediators amplify hepatocyte death and HSC activation; and gut-derived lipopolysaccharide (LPS), bile acids, and microbial metabolites further modify hepatic metabolic and inflammatory responses. Thus, steatosis, inflammation, fibrosis, and gut-liver crosstalk should be interpreted as linked pathogenic nodes rather than isolated events. Recent therapeutic advances for adults with noncirrhotic MASH and moderate-to-advanced fibrosis (F2-F3) include the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved agents resmetirom and semaglutide (Wegovy). Resmetirom was approved on the basis of the phase 3 MAESTRO-NASH trial [18], whereas semaglutide received accelerated approval following the phase 3 ESSENCE trial [19]. Other agents, including pegozafermin, tirzepatide, and survodutide, remain under clinical investigation [14]. However, MASLD is a chronic and heterogeneous disorder frequently accompanied by obesity, insulin resistance, dysglycemia, and cardiovascular risk, and a single pharmacological target is unlikely to cover the full disease trajectory. Exercise intervention provides a complementary strategy because it reshapes energy demand, substrate partitioning, and interorgan metabolic communication, thereby influencing hepatic lipid disposal, mitochondrial quality control, inflammatory signaling, autophagy, and gut microbial homeostasis. Therefore, this review summarizes the major pathogenic mechanisms of MASLD and integrates current evidence on exercise-induced molecular adaptations, with particular attention to exercise modality, intensity, dose, timing, disease stage, and metabolic phenotype. We aim to provide a mechanistic and translational framework for moving from generalized lifestyle advice toward phenotype-stratified and biomarker-guided exercise prescriptions.

Pathogenic mechanisms in MASLD

Hepatic lipid overload and lipotoxicity

Hepatic steatosis reflects an imbalance among lipid influx, de novo lipogenesis (DNL), lipid droplet (LD) storage, very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) export, lysosomal lipid degradation, and fatty acid oxidation (FAO). Triglyceride (TG) storage in LDs initially buffers free fatty acid (FFA) toxicity, but this buffering system becomes maladaptive when lipid entry and DNL remain high while LD mobilization, lipophagy, VLDL secretion, and FAO fail to increase accordingly. Under this condition, neutral lipid storage is accompanied by the accumulation of ceramides, diacylglycerols, lysophosphatidylcholines, oxidized phospholipids, and other lipotoxic intermediates that promote endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, mitochondrial injury, inflammatory signaling, and hepatocellular death.

Hepatocellular fatty acid uptake is partly mediated by cluster of differentiation 36 (CD36) at the plasma membrane. In MASLD models, reduced Eva-1 homolog A (EVA1A) activates mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1)–peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma 2 (PPARγ2) signaling and increases CD36 transcription. EVA1A deficiency also enhances CD36 palmitoylation and plasma membrane localization, thereby increasing fatty acid uptake by hepatocytes while reducing the mitochondrial localization of CD36 and fatty acid β-oxidation [20]. When mitochondrial FAO is insufficient, increased CD36-mediated lipid entry enlarges the substrate pool for TG storage and lipotoxic lipid synthesis rather than productive oxidation. DNL is driven by lipogenic transcriptional programs involving sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1c (SREBP1c) and carbohydrate response element-binding protein (ChREBP), but pathological lipogenesis is also shaped by chromatin accessibility and RNA-level control. General control nonderepressible 5 (GCN5) enhances histone H3 acetylation at the SREBP1c promoter and cooperates with liver X receptor alpha (LXRα)/retinoid X receptor alpha (RXRα) to increase lipogenic transcription [21]. T-cell intracellular antigen 1 (TIA1), by contrast, binds the 3′ untranslated region of Srebf1 messenger RNA (mRNA) in stress granules and suppresses SREBP1 translation [22]. When this post-transcriptional restraint is weakened, transcriptional lipogenic signals can be amplified at the protein level and increase downstream lipogenic enzyme expression.

Lipid export and membrane remodeling modify the ability of hepatocytes to dispose of excess lipid. Hepatic VLDL assembly requires apolipoprotein B100 (apoB100) and microsomal triglyceride transfer protein (MTP), which is encoded by the MTTP gene. Within the endoplasmic reticulum, MTP transfers neutral lipids to nascent apoB100 and thereby supports apoB100 lipidation and the initial assembly of apoB-containing lipoproteins; pharmacological inhibition of MTP blocks this early assembly step [23]. Consistent with this role, hepatic MTP overexpression increases VLDL-TG and apoB secretion, indicating that MTP availability can limit hepatic VLDL production [24]. However, VLDL export is not uniformly protective. In individuals with fatty liver, greater hepatic fat content is associated with the overproduction of large, TG-rich VLDL particles, whereas the accompanying increase in VLDL-TG secretion remains insufficient to normalize intrahepatic TG and may increase the circulating burden of TG-rich lipoproteins [25, 26]. Thus, the apoB100–MTP/MTTP axis links hepatic lipid retention to systemic dyslipidemia: insufficient apoB100 lipidation restricts VLDL export and favors hepatic TG accumulation, whereas excessive secretion of TG-rich VLDL increases extrahepatic lipid exposure. Transmembrane 6 superfamily member 2 (TM6SF2) supports VLDL assembly and TG secretion; loss of TM6SF2 function promotes microvesicular LD accumulation, organelle ultrastructural abnormalities, oxidative stress, and metabolic reprogramming [27]. Membrane-bound O-acyltransferase domain-containing 7 (MBOAT7) maintains phosphatidylinositol remodeling by re-esterifying polyunsaturated fatty acids, including arachidonic acid, into phosphatidylinositol. MBOAT7 deficiency reduces 20-carbon polyunsaturated fatty acids in phosphatidylinositol and enhances SREBP1c processing [28]. Altered phospholipid remodeling therefore affects both membrane biology and LD behavior, strengthening the connection between lipid retention and lipogenic activation. LD-associated proteins further shape the transition from adaptive lipid storage to injury. Protein-truncating variants in hydroxysteroid 17β-dehydrogenase 13 (HSD17B13) are associated with a lower risk of chronic liver disease, and hepatic Hsd17b13 knockdown attenuates high-fat diet (HFD)-induced steatosis and liver injury [29, 30]. Patatin-like phospholipase domain-containing protein 3 (PNPLA3) variants, particularly PNPLA3-I148M, impair LD lipid mobilization, alter LD-Golgi interactions, and favor lipid retention in hepatocytes [31, 32]. Mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress

Hepatocytes initially compensate for lipid overload by increasing FAO and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). However, sustained lipid stress progressively compromises respiratory chain efficiency, Ca2⁺ homeostasis, mitochondrial dynamics, and protein quality control, converting ROS accumulation, oxidized mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), and impaired adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production into inflammatory injury signals [33, 34]. Respiratory chain activity and mitochondrial Ca2⁺ uptake are central to hepatic lipid oxidation. Methylation-controlled J protein (MCJ) localizes to the inner mitochondrial membrane and inhibits complex I activity. In MASLD, increased MCJ reduces fatty acid β-oxidation and promotes hepatic steatosis, liver injury, and fibrosis, whereas MCJ silencing enhances complex I activity and FAO without markedly increasing mitochondrial ROS production [35]. Mitochondrial calcium uniporter (MCU) controls Ca2⁺ entry into the mitochondrial matrix and influences tricarboxylic acid cycle enzyme activity, OXPHOS efficiency, and susceptibility to cell death. MCU upregulation is associated with mitochondrial stress in MASH, and MCU reduction can attenuate MASH and fibrosis in some models [36]. Conversely, MCU deficiency may reduce ATP production, deplete mtDNA, and aggravate oxidative stress [37].

Altered mitochondrial dynamics represent another major feature of mitochondrial injury in MASLD. Imbalance between fusion and fission disrupts mitochondrial morphology, membrane potential, cristae organization, and respiratory chain architecture. In MASLD, reduced L-aspartate levels are associated with impaired mitochondrial quality and oxidative capacity. L-aspartate supplementation increases platelet cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP), suppresses platelet activation and ATP release, and thereby limits platelet-derived ATP signaling to hepatocyte purinergic receptor P2X7 (P2X7). This pathway restrains activation of NIMA-related kinase 7 (NEK7) and dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1), reducing excessive mitochondrial fission, lipid accumulation, and hepatocyte death [38]. Excessive DRP1 activation lowers mitochondrial membrane potential, disrupts cristae and respiratory complex organization, increases electron leakage from complexes I and III, and enhances ROS generation. These findings connect platelet–hepatocyte crosstalk with mitochondrial fragmentation, oxidative stress, and hepatocellular injury.

Mitochondrial proteostasis is essential for antioxidant defense and respiratory chain stability. Evolutionarily conserved signaling intermediate in Toll pathways (ECSIT) supports mitochondrial stress adaptation. During MASH progression, reduced mitochondrial ECSIT limits the translocation of ovarian tumor domain-containing deubiquitinase 3 (OTUD3) to mitochondria, weakening OTUD3-mediated deubiquitination and stabilization of sirtuin 3 (SIRT3). This defect promotes oxidative mtDNA damage, ROS generation, and respiratory chain dysfunction [39]. Reduced baculoviral IAP repeat-containing ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme (BRUCE/BIRC6) further impairs mitochondrial lipid oxidation. BRUCE deficiency suppresses mitochondrial FAO, compromises respiratory chain function, and reduces ATP production, thereby limiting mitochondrial lipid clearance and aggravating hepatocellular lipid accumulation. In MASLD mouse models, reduced BRUCE couples protein kinase B (AKT)-related DNL activation with defective mitochondrial lipid clearance and activates signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3)-associated inflammatory and fibrotic signaling [40]. Mitochondrial protein quality control therefore influences fatty acid oxidation, redox homeostasis, and hepatocyte susceptibility to death. Persistent loss of mitochondrial membrane potential and permeability control can sensitize hepatocytes to mitochondria-mediated intrinsic apoptosis [41], whereas oxidized mtDNA and other mitochondrial damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) amplify hepatic immune signaling (Fig. 2B).

Activation of immune-inflammatory responses

Immune-inflammatory activation in MASLD arises from the convergence of hepatocellular injury, gut-derived inputs, and remodeling of the hepatic immune niche. Lipotoxic hepatocytes, damaged mitochondria, and dying cells release DAMPs, whereas intestinal barrier disruption increases portal exposure to pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs). Persistent danger signaling weakens the tolerogenic and efferocytic functions of KCs and strengthens the crosstalk among MoMFs, neutrophils, and HSCs, thereby sustaining intrahepatic inflammation [42].

Amplification of inflammatory signaling cascades

During MASLD progression, DAMPs released from lipotoxic hepatocytes, damaged mitochondria, and dying cells cooperate with gut-derived LPS and PAMPs to activate hepatic innate immune signaling. Beyond canonical inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)–stimulator of interferon genes (STING) axis, the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, and neutrophil extracellular trap (NET)-associated signaling contribute to persistent inflammation, hepatocellular injury, and fibrotic progression.

The cGAS-STING pathway serves as a central signaling pathway for sensing cytosolic double-stranded DNA and initiating innate immune responses. In MASLD, cytosolic mtDNA, nuclear DNA damage, and cell-free DNA released from dying hepatocytes can activate cGAS. cGAS has dual immunometabolic roles in MASLD. On the one hand, cGAS–STING signaling promotes innate immune activation and inflammatory cytokine expression. On the other hand, cGAS can directly bind aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (ALDH2) and inhibit its enzymatic activity, limiting acetaldehyde conversion to acetyl-coenzyme A and reducing histone acetylation-dependent transcription of lipogenic genes, thereby decreasing LD accumulation [43]. Adipose-derived neuregulin-4 (Nrg4) further suppresses hepatocyte cGAS–STING-mediated inflammation and steatosis by activating Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 4 (ErbB4)–AKT signaling, promoting cGAS phosphorylation, and reducing cGAS enzymatic activity [44]. Thus, cGAS-related signaling is not simply pro-inflammatory in MASLD, and its effect depends on the balance between STING-dependent inflammatory pathways, ALDH2-related metabolic regulation and adipose tissue-derived Nrg4 signaling.

The NLRP3 inflammasome is a central amplifier of inflammatory injury during MASLD progression. NLRP3 activation promotes maturation and release of interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-18 (IL-18), and induces heat shock protein 70 (Hsp70) expression and extracellular release. Extracellular Hsp70 acts as a DAMP that binds hepatocyte surface TLR4, activates NF-κB signaling, and promotes the transition from MASLD to MASH; conversely, Nlrp3 deletion blocks Hsp70 release and downstream TLR4 inflammatory signaling, markedly attenuating fatty liver pathology [45]. Oxidative-nitrative stress further enhances NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Peroxynitrite promotes dissociation of thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP) from thioredoxin and facilitates TXNIP binding to NLRP3, thereby driving inflammasome assembly and activation. Inhibition of peroxynitrite reduces TXNIP–NLRP3 colocalization and alleviates MASH pathology [46] (Fig. 2C).

NETs link neutrophil activation to HSC-driven fibrogenesis. NET markers are increased in the livers of patients and mice with MASH. NETs activate TLR4–MyD88–NF-κB signaling in HSCs and promote their transdifferentiation into myofibroblasts; blockade of NET formation or degradation of established NETs attenuates liver fibrosis and inflammation in MASH mice [47]. NET-DNA released by peptidyl arginine deiminase 4 (PAD4)-positive neutrophils can also activate HSCs through the thousand-and-one amino acid kinase 1 (TAOK1)/mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, thereby accelerating MASH fibrosis [48]. Thus, NETs are not merely markers of neutrophil infiltration, but active mediators of neutrophil–HSC communication and inflammatory fibrogenic amplification (Fig. 2D).

Activation of hepatic macrophages

Embryonically derived KCs maintain portal antigen tolerance, efferocytosis, and inflammatory restraint under homeostatic conditions. During MASLD progression, KC dysfunction or death is accompanied by persistent MoMF infiltration, reshaping the hepatic macrophage compartment. In KCs, hypoxia-inducible factor-2α (HIF-2α) enhances mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR)/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK)-dependent inhibitory phosphorylation of transcription factor EB (TFEB), suppresses lysosomal and phagocytic gene programs, impairs clearance of dying hepatocytes, and promotes lysosomal stress-induced KC death. In recruited macrophages, HIF-2α drives ROS production and pro-inflammatory polarization through adenine nucleotide translocase 2 (ANT2) [49]. Myeloid- or KC-specific HIF-2α deficiency, as well as interventions targeting the mTOR–TFEB axis, attenuates experimental MASH inflammation and fibrosis, identifying HIF-2α as a key link between KC loss and pro-inflammatory MoMF activation.

Chemokine signaling is central to MoMF recruitment. Injured hepatocytes and activated KCs secrete C–C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2), which acts through C–C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CCR2) to recruit inflammatory monocytes and sustain MoMF accumulation; the anti-fibrotic signal observed with the CCR2/CCR5 dual antagonist cenicriviroc further supports the relevance of this axis in MASH [50]. Beyond recruitment, pathogenic MoMF differentiation is regulated by Notch–recombination signal binding protein for immunoglobulin kappa J region (RBPJ) signaling. The Notch–RBPJ axis promotes expansion of inflammatory MoMFs, whereas Rbpj deficiency favors the accumulation of Ly6C^lo protective monocytes and enhances CD36-associated lipid uptake and protective interactions with liver sinusoidal endothelial cells [51]. KC survival is also shaped by metabolic homeostasis. Chitinase 3-like 1 (Chi3l1) limits glucose entry into KCs and protects against glucose overload-induced cell death; Chi3l1 deficiency promotes KC depletion, MoMF infiltration, and hepatic inflammation and fibrosis [52]. Thus, macrophage dysregulation in MASH involves not only CCL2–CCR2-mediated monocyte recruitment, but also altered cell fate and metabolic adaptation controlled by Notch–RBPJ and Chi3l1. These fate changes are further shaped by the diseased hepatic niche. In diet-induced steatohepatitis, recruited MoMFs acquire epigenetic, transcriptional, and functional features that partially converge with KC-associated states, whereas resident KCs lose aspects of their homeostatic identity and acquire TREM2- and CD9-associated disease programs [53]. These findings support partial niche-driven convergence rather than complete conversion of TREM2-positive MoMFs into KCs. Within this heterogeneous macrophage compartment, TREM2 sustains MoMF survival through phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT signaling and promotes transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1)-dependent acquisition of inflammation-resolving phenotypes; conversely, myeloid Trem2 deficiency enhances macrophage pyroptosis and aggravates hepatic lipid deposition and inflammatory injury [54].

Macrophage dysfunction also involves impaired anti-inflammatory signaling and defective clearance of dying cells. Dendritic cell-specific intercellular adhesion molecule-3-grabbing non-integrin (DC-SIGN/CD209)-positive macrophages decline with MASLD severity [55]. Restoration of this macrophage subset promotes endocytic degradation of activated TLR4 through direct DC-SIGN–TLR4 interaction, selectively suppresses MyD88-dependent pro-inflammatory signaling, and preserves TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1)/interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3)-related anti-inflammatory signaling, thereby attenuating steatosis, liver injury, and fibrosis. Defective clearance of dying hepatocytes is also linked to the cluster of differentiation 47 (CD47)–signal regulatory protein alpha (SIRPα) axis. In fatty liver, CD47 is upregulated on necrotic hepatocytes and suppresses hepatic macrophage phagocytosis through SIRPα, whereas blockade of CD47–SIRPα enhances necrotic hepatocyte clearance and reduces HSC activation and liver fibrosis [56]. Thus, DC-SIGN–TLR4 and CD47–SIRPα contribute to MASLD immune-inflammatory progression through distinct mechanisms: inflammatory signal bias and impaired efferocytosis.

Lipid accumulation further induces immunometabolic reprogramming. SAM and HD domain-containing deoxynucleoside triphosphate triphosphohydrolase 1 (SAMHD1) is increased in liver tissues from patients with MASLD and in diet-induced MASLD mouse models, and its hepatocyte expression can be induced by palmitate, oleate, cholesterol, and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ). Mechanistically, SAMHD1 promotes SREBP1/2 maturation by upregulating SREBP cleavage-activating protein (SCAP), site-1 protease (S1P), and site-2 protease (S2P), thereby enhancing both fatty acid and cholesterol synthesis. Hepatocyte-specific Samhd1 deletion attenuates Gubra-Amylin MASH (GAN) diet-induced steatosis, liver injury, and macrophage infiltration [57]. Adipose tissue can also shape the hepatic inflammatory microenvironment through secreted factors and extracellular vesicles. In particular, adipose-derived milk fat globule-EGF factor 8 (MFG-E8) enters fatty liver, enhances macrophage–hepatocyte crosstalk, and promotes inflammation and fibrosis [58]. These findings indicate that macrophage activation and immune-inflammatory amplification in MASLD are shaped not only by intrahepatic immune-cell activation, but also by hepatocyte lipogenic programs and adipose tissue-derived signals (Fig. 2D).

PCD amplifies hepatocellular injury

Lipotoxicity, mitochondrial injury, and inflammasome activation reshape the immunological consequences of hepatocyte death. Instead of being removed through relatively ordered clearance, dying hepatocytes may undergo membrane rupture, DAMP release, and inflammatory cytokine maturation. Pyroptosis, apoptosis, necroptosis, and ferroptosis are mechanistically interconnected in MASLD. Their pathological impact depends less on the nominal category of cell death than on whether the death process generates immunogenic signals that amplify inflammation, HSC activation, and matrix deposition.

Pyroptosis

Pyroptosis is a lytic form of PCD driven by inflammasome assembly and caspase activation. In the canonical pathway, the NLRP3 inflammasome activates caspase-1 (CASP1), which cleaves gasdermin D (GSDMD) to generate the pore-forming GSDMD-N fragment and promote IL-1β and IL-18 release. In MASLD, lipotoxic lipids, oxidized mitochondrial DNA (ox-mtDNA), and ATP can activate NLRP3 and initiate the pyroptotic cascade. In a mutant Nlrp3 activation model, sustained NLRP3 activation induced hepatocyte pyroptosis, liver inflammation, neutrophil infiltration, and collagen deposition; systemic NLRP3 activation caused more severe liver injury than myeloid-specific activation [59]. Further studies demonstrated that GSDMD-N was elevated in the liver tissues of patients with fatty liver and in mouse models, while deletion of Gsdmd reduced diet-induced steatohepatitis and inflammatory response [60]. Together, these studies provide causal evidence that the canonical NLRP3–GSDMD pyroptotic pathway contributes to MASLD progression.

Additional regulators extend pyroptotic signaling beyond the canonical NLRP3–GSDMD axis. NUAK1 expression is increased in liver tissue from patients with MASH and in experimental models. Under MASH-related stress, NUAK1 activates CASP6 and promotes pyroptosis and inflammation, whereas CASP6 silencing in hepatocytes attenuates these responses and alters TAK1–RIPK1 interaction and RIPK1 degradation [61]. Mitochondrial dysfunction also provides an upstream trigger for pyroptosis. Abnormal opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) releases ox-mtDNA, which promotes inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptor type 3 (ITPR3)-dependent endoplasmic reticulum Ca2⁺ release and drives NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and pyroptotic execution [62] (Fig. 2C).

Apoptosis

Apoptosis is primarily initiated through death receptor or mitochondrial pathways, resulting in sequential activation of caspase-8, caspase-9, and caspase-3, DNA fragmentation, and ordered hepatocyte clearance. In early simple steatosis, caspase-3/7-mediated apoptosis may remain relatively immunologically silent. As lipotoxic stress increases, persistent activation of the integrated stress response (ISR) elevates phosphorylation of eukaryotic initiation factor 2α (eIF2α). Once this response exceeds a threshold, cell death signaling may shift from C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP)-mediated apoptosis toward an activating transcription factor 3 (ATF3)-dependent transcriptional program. ATF3-induced RIPK3 expression may promote RIPK3–MLKL-dependent necroptosis alongside ongoing apoptotic signaling; however, this mechanism should not be interpreted as a universal linear switch from apoptosis to necroptosis [63]. More broadly, overlapping RIPK1, caspase, RIPK3, and MLKL signaling suggests that several regulated cell-death pathways may operate concurrently during steatohepatitis [64]. This pattern is consistent with PANoptosis-like convergence, although a canonical PANoptosome has not been established as a general mechanism in MASLD.

In animal models of MASLD with T2DM, the isoquinoline alkaloid palmatine suppresses Fas-associated death domain protein (FADD)-mediated extrinsic caspase-8 signaling and caspase-3 activation, reduces gasdermin E (GSDME)-related downstream pyroptotic/apoptotic events, and attenuates PCD, steatosis, inflammatory infiltration, and fibrosis [65]. The immunometabolic molecule cluster of differentiation 1 d (CD1d) is reduced in hepatocytes from patients and mice with MASH and is negatively associated with liver injury severity. Hepatocyte-specific CD1d overexpression recruits and activates Janus kinase 2 (JAK2)/STAT3 signaling through cytoplasmic tail tyrosine phosphorylation, upregulates B-cell lymphoma-extra large (Bcl-xL) and myeloid cell leukemia-1 (Mcl-1), and reduces cleaved caspase-3/7, thereby protecting against palmitic acid-induced lipotoxic apoptosis and concanavalin A-induced immune-mediated hepatocyte apoptosis [66]. These findings identify CD1d as an immunometabolic regulator of hepatocyte apoptosis in steatohepatitis, in addition to lipotoxic stress (Fig. 2C).

Necroptosis

Necroptosis is primarily executed through the RIPK1–RIPK3–MLKL axis. When caspase-8 activity is inhibited, RIPK3 is recruited to the necrosome and phosphorylates MLKL. Phosphorylated MLKL then oligomerizes and translocates to the plasma membrane, where it forms pores that cause cell swelling, membrane rupture, and release of proinflammatory intracellular contents [67]. In liver tissues from patients with MASLD and HFD-induced MASH mice, RIPK3 and MLKL are upregulated and correlate with JNK pathway activation and hepatic inflammation [68]. RIPK3 deficiency reduces liver injury, inflammatory-cell infiltration, and fibrosis, and delays progression from MASH to HCC [69]. RIPK1 Ser166 phosphorylation is also increased in experimental and human steatohepatitis, with prominent activation in hepatic macrophages, supporting crosstalk between regulated cell-death and inflammatory signaling pathways in MASH [64].

Upstream lipotoxic triggers can directly engage necroptotic signaling. Very-long-chain fatty acids induce necroptosis by disrupting plasma membrane integrity [70], whereas ceramides promote RIPK1 oligomerization and membrane pore formation, initiating MLKL phosphorylation-dependent necroptotic signaling [71]. Accordingly, pharmacological targeting of RIPK1/MLKL-related signaling has shown preclinical potential for limiting metabolic liver injury [72]. Several small molecules targeting RIPK1, RIPK3, MLKL, or their combinations have been identified as necroptosis inhibitors [73]. Among them, necrostatin-1 stable (Nec-1 s) has greater metabolic stability and target specificity and is widely used in functional studies of necroptosis [74]. Under impaired antioxidant defense, Nec-1 s attenuates MASLD-associated hepatic inflammation and fibrotic progression [75]. In addition to mediating plasma membrane permeabilization, MLKL exerts non-necroptotic effects on autophagic clearance, indicating that its contribution to MASLD extends beyond canonical RIPK3-dependent necroptosis [76] (Fig. 2C).

Ferroptosis

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent, lipid peroxidation-driven, non-apoptotic form of PCD. Its core mechanisms include expansion of the labile intracellular iron pool, inhibition of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) or glutathione depletion, and lethal peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acid-containing membrane phospholipids [77]. A ferroptosis-like subgroup has been identified among biopsy-confirmed patients with MASLD, characterized by reduced GPX4, enhanced lipid peroxidation, and impaired ferroptosis defense [78]. Human hepatocyte models further connect genetic susceptibility to ferroptotic injury. The PNPLA3-I148M variant promotes abnormal accumulation of lipid intermediates in the endoplasmic reticulum, induces mitochondrial dysfunction and compensatory peroxisomal β-oxidation, and increases ROS and lipid peroxides; ferroptosis inhibition or GPX4 modulation reduces death of PNPLA3-I148M mutant hepatocytes [79].

Ferroptosis is tightly coupled to lipid dysregulation because ferroptotic sensitivity depends on the availability of peroxidizable membrane phospholipid substrates. Oxysterol-binding protein-like 2 (OSBPL2) coordinates iron and fatty acid homeostasis partly through direct binding to heat shock protein 90β (HSP90β). OSBPL2 deficiency alters hepatic fatty acid distribution, increases LD storage, and reduces hepatocyte ferroptotic sensitivity by downregulating acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), ultimately attenuating liver fibrosis in MASH mice [80]. Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα) directly binds the GPX4 promoter. Genetic or diet-induced PPARα loss causes Gpx4 promoter hypermethylation and transcriptional silencing, accompanied by reduced nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) expression, increased malondialdehyde (MDA), enrichment of ferroptosis- and pyroptosis-related gene sets, and enhanced lipid peroxidation injury. These findings establish PPARα as a causal suppressor of ferroptosis in MASLD [81].

Epigenetic regulation further links ferroptosis to hepatic lipid deposition in MASLD. TATA-box binding protein-associated factor 1 C (TAF1C) knockdown or pharmacological inhibition with CeMMEC13 reduces lipid deposition and ferroptosis in steatotic hepatocytes in vitro and in vivo, whereas TAF1C overexpression aggravates these phenotypes. TAF1C recruits the H3K4 methyltransferase SET domain containing 1 A (SETD1A) and remodels H3K4me3/H3K27ac marks at target-gene enhancers, directly increasing ACSL4 expression. ACSL4 then promotes synthesis of polyunsaturated fatty acid-containing membrane phospholipids, providing substrates for lipid peroxidation while accelerating lipid synthesis and LD accumulation. In this context, ACSL4 acts as a functional bridge between ferroptosis and lipogenic metabolism in MASLD [82] (Fig. 2C).

Collectively, PCD in MASLD comprises pyroptosis, apoptosis, necroptosis, and ferroptosis. These death programs are mechanistically interconnected rather than discrete. Their pathological impact depends less on the category of cell death itself than on whether the dying process generates immunogenic signals that propagate inflammation and fibrogenesis in fatty liver.

Autophagy-lysosomal dysfunction

The autophagy–lysosome system maintains hepatocellular homeostasis by clearing excess LDs, damaged mitochondria, and aberrant protein complexes. Lipotoxic stress can also impair autophagic flux through MLKL-dependent signaling, including effects that are partly independent of RIPK3 [76]. In MASLD, defective mitophagy preserves dysfunctional ROS-generating mitochondria, whereas insufficient lipophagy restricts LD turnover and promotes TG retention.

Impaired mitophagy

Mitophagy selectively removes dysfunctional mitochondria and limits persistent accumulation of ROS, mtDNA, and mtDNA-related DAMPs. In MASLD, lipotoxic lipid-induced loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, ROS bursts, and mtDNA leakage would normally trigger damaged mitochondrial clearance. However, mitophagy impairment can occur early in disease progression, allowing abnormal mitochondria to persist. Parkin deficiency accelerates fatty liver-associated lipid deposition, inflammation, and fibrosis [83]. In the HFD-induced MASLD model, suppression of the PINK1/Parkin pathway is accompanied by p62 accumulation and reduced mitochondrial quality, indicating that defective PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1)/Parkin-mediated mitophagy is a key component of mitochondrial quality-control failure in MASLD [84]. In addition to the PINK1/Parkin pathway, receptor-mediated mitophagy contributes to mitochondrial quality control in MASLD. FUN14 domain-containing protein 1 (FUNDC1) is an outer mitochondrial membrane receptor that directly binds microtubule-associated protein 1A/1B-light chain 3 (LC3) through its LC3-interacting region and mediates selective clearance of damaged mitochondria. In liver tissues from patients with MASLD and HFD-fed mice, casein kinase 2α (CK2α) is upregulated, whereas FUNDC1 is reduced. CK2α deletion restores mitophagic flux, attenuates hepatic lipid accumulation and mitochondrial structural injury, and reverses ferroptosis-related changes, including GPX4 reduction and lipid peroxide accumulation [85].

Completion of mitophagy depends not only on recognition of damaged mitochondria, but also on autophagosome–lysosome fusion and lysosomal degradative capacity. Transcription factor EB (TFEB) coordinately regulates autophagosome formation, autophagosome–lysosome fusion, and lysosomal biogenesis. The TFEB agonist desloratadine promotes TFEB nuclear translocation, induces expression of autophagy–lysosome pathway target genes, reduces hepatic TG content, and improves hepatocyte ballooning in HFD-induced MASLD models [86]. GTPase-activating protein-binding protein 1 (G3BP1) is an upstream regulator of autophagy–lysosome function. G3BP1 is reduced in the livers of patients with MASLD and mouse models of MASH, and hepatocyte-specific G3BP1 deletion causes autophagic defects and aggravates hepatic lipid deposition. Mechanistically, G3BP1 directly interacts with STX17 and VAMP8 to promote autophagosome–lysosome fusion. It also facilitates nuclear translocation of transcription factor E3 (TFE3), thereby driving expression of autophagy–lysosome-related genes. Loss of G3BP1 reduces damaged mitochondrial clearance and, together with LD accumulation, amplifies hepatocellular stress and accelerates fatty liver progression [87].

Collectively, impaired mitophagy compromises the clearance of damaged mitochondria, promoting ROS and mtDNA accumulation, lipid peroxidation, and hepatocellular stress and thereby facilitating progression from MASLD to MASH (Fig. 2E).

Impaired lipophagy

Impaired lipophagy contributes to early LD accumulation and lipotoxicity in MASLD. Inhibition of autophagy increases hepatocellular TG content and LD storage, indicating a direct role of autophagy in hepatic lipid mobilization [88]. Enhanced LD–LC3 interaction promotes the delivery of LDs to autophagosomes [89]. LD coat proteins and lysosomal lipases jointly determine whether LDs can be efficiently degraded. Perilipin 2 (PLIN2) stabilizes the surface of hepatocellular LDs and protects them from autophagic degradation. Conversely, PLIN2 deficiency increases LC3-II and reduces sequestosome 1 (p62), facilitating lysosomal degradation of LDs and thereby enhancing lipophagy and lowering hepatic TG burden [90]. AMP-activated protein kinase α (AMPKα) restores lysosomal hydrolysis of LDs through the PLIN2–lysosomal acid lipase (LIPA/LAL) axis, promoting lipophagy and slowing fatty liver progression [91]. In parallel, tripartite motif-containing protein 25 (TRIM25) increases lipogenic pressure by degrading insulin-induced gene 1 (INSIG1) through its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity, thereby releasing sterol regulatory element-binding protein 2 (SREBP2) from endoplasmic reticulum retention and promoting nuclear SREBP2-driven lipogenic transcription. TRIM25 inhibition or deficiency stabilizes INSIG1, reduces SREBP2 activity, and attenuates lipid accumulation and inflammatory phenotypes [92].

Completion of lipophagy also requires efficient autophagosome–lysosome fusion and terminal lysosomal degradation. C terminus of Hsc70-interacting protein (CHIP) promotes K63- and K27-linked ubiquitination of syntaxin 17 (STX17), facilitating assembly of the STX17–synaptosome-associated protein 29 (SNAP29)–vesicle-associated membrane protein 8 (VAMP8) complex and autophagosome–lysosome fusion. This process promotes degradation of PLIN2-associated LDs, whereas CHIP deficiency increases susceptibility to fatty liver under HFD or high-fat/high-fructose feeding [93]. Loss of transmembrane protein 55B (TMEM55B) increases lysosome–LD contacts, but prevents efficient substrate degradation or release, leading to formation of lipolysosomes, blockade of lipophagy, and progression from MASLD to MASH [94]. Beyond macroautophagy-mediated LD degradation, chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) can contribute to fatty liver by impairing lipid export. Mice lacking sphingosine kinase 2 (SphK2) develop hepatic TG accumulation. Mechanistically, SphK2 knockdown reduces phosphorylation of mTOR complex 2 (mTORC2) and aberrantly activates CMA, accelerating degradation of STX17 and VAMP8. This prevents efficient fusion of VLDL transport vesicles with the Golgi apparatus, suppresses VLDL secretion, and disrupts hepatic lipid metabolism [95] (Fig. 2F).

Gut–liver axis injury

The anatomical basis of the gut–liver axis is formed by the portal venous system and the biliary system. The portal vein delivers gut-derived molecules, including absorbed glucose, fatty acids, amino acids, bile acids (BAs), short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), tryptophan derivatives, trimethylamine (TMA), and LPS, to the liver, making the liver the first target organ exposed to intestinal metabolic and immune signals (Fig. 3A). Conversely, the biliary system delivers liver-derived primary BAs, cholesterol, secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA), and antimicrobial molecules into the intestinal lumen, thereby participating in lipid absorption, microbial selection pressure, and maintenance of mucosal immune homeostasis [96, 97] (Fig. 3 B). Gut-derived mechanisms

Functional dysbiosis and barrier disruption

The pathological effects of functional dysbiosis depend on strain-level virulence architecture, metabolite production, and transmissibility, rather than simply on changes in phylum- or genus-level abundance. Human metagenomic studies have shown that Escherichia coli (E. coli), Bacteroides vulgatus, and several Clostridium species are more consistently associated with MASLD/MASH and advanced fibrosis, whereas depletion of Ruminococcus obeum, Eubacterium rectale, and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii is associated with fibrotic progression and reduced SCFA-producing capacity [96, 98, 99]. E. coli-derived LPS [100], flagellin [101], peptidoglycan (PGN) [102], and unmethylated CpG DNA [103] can enter the portal circulation and activate hepatic innate immune responses through TLR4, TLR5, and TLR9, respectively. Accordingly, gut-derived PAMP input can cooperate with hepatocellular lipotoxic stress, Kupffer cell inflammatory polarization, and HSC collagen deposition to promote inflammation and fibrosis in MASH.

Studies on high-alcohol-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae (HiAlc Kpn) demonstrate the direct impact of microbial function on MASLD. HiAlc Kpn can convert dietary carbohydrates into ethanol. Portal delivery of microbiota-derived ethanol increases hepatic cytochrome P450 2E1 (CYP2E1) protein abundance and catalytic activity, thereby enhancing CYP2E1-dependent ethanol oxidation. The resulting increase in CYP2E1 catalytic flux, rather than the basal presence of CYP2E1 in hepatocytes, increases acetaldehyde and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, leading to impaired mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, lipid peroxidation, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and lipid droplet deposition [104, 105]. Colonization with HiAlc Kpn derived from patients with MASLD induces fatty liver-like lesions in mice, whereas phage-mediated depletion of HiAlc Kpn reduces liver injury, inflammatory cytokine expression, and lipogenic gene expression, thereby attenuating steatotic liver-like pathology [106]. Treatment with Bifidobacterium bifidum CIP-01 alleviates HiAlc Kpn-induced MASLD, accompanied by a reduced intestinal ethanol microenvironment, decreased hepatic inflammation, and improved metabolic injury [107]. Bacteroides-associated effects are strain- and metabolic function-dependent. B. vulgatus is associated with advanced fibrosis in MASLD, with potential mechanisms involving BA deconjugation, mucosal inflammation, and barrier disruption [98, 99]. However, specific Bacteroides strains can reduce gut-derived LPS production, improve intestinal permeability, enhance barrier function, increase gut-liver folate and unsaturated fatty acid metabolism, and reduce hepatic LD deposition and lipogenic burden [108, 109]. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) studies in MAFLD mouse models further showed that microbiota remodeling in recipient mice is accompanied by changes in LPS burden, SCFA production, BA transformation, and hepatic inflammatory and lipogenic gene programs [110, 111]. Therefore, the microbiota should be understood as a transferable metabolic and immune regulatory system rather than as a disease phenotype determined by a single genus.

Barrier dysfunction determines the intensity of gut-derived signaling reaching the liver. The intestinal barrier includes epithelial cells, the mucus layer, tight junctions, gut-associated lymphoid tissue, commensal microbiota, sIgA, and the gut vascular barrier (GVB) [112, 113]. Within the GVB, vascular endothelial cadherin (VE-cadherin) contributes to endothelial adherens-junction integrity, whereas increased accessibility or expression of plasmalemma vesicle-associated protein 1 (PV1; encoded by PLVAP) indicates increased endothelial permeability [112, 114]. Patients with MASLD commonly exhibit increased intestinal permeability, small-intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO), tight-junction abnormalities, and elevated serum zonulin [115, 116]. Experimental models indicate that disruption of both the epithelial barrier and GVB can occur early in disease development [112], facilitating the translocation of bacteria and bacterial products into the portal circulation. The farnesoid X receptor (FXR) agonist obeticholic acid reinforces GVB integrity through Wnt/β-catenin signaling, whereas colonization with biliary/intestinal Escherichia coli NF73-1 disrupts the GVB and aggravates HFD-induced fatty liver [117]. Host epithelial factors link barrier injury with specific microbial metabolite influx. Intestinal epithelial TM6SF2 regulates long-chain fatty acid secretion through fatty acid-binding protein 5 (FABP5); Tm6sf2 deficiency alters microbial lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) metabolism and increases LPA levels in feces, portal blood, and liver tissue. Once delivered to the liver, LPA promotes hepatocellular lipid deposition, NF-κB p65 phosphorylation, and collagen deposition. Fecal microbiota transplantation further demonstrated that the TM6SF2 deficiency-associated microbial and metabolic phenotype was transferable to recipient mice [118]. The mucin 1 (MUC1)/neural precursor cell expressed developmentally downregulated 4 (NEDD4) axis maintains the apical mucus-membrane-associated epithelial barrier; MUC1 downregulation allows LPS, bacterial DNA, and peptidoglycan to cross the mucosal barrier and enter the portal vein, aggravating lipogenesis, oxidative stress, and inflammatory injury [119]. Intestinal epithelial hypoxia-inducible factor 1 alpha (HIF-1α) maintains hypoxic adaptation, mucus-layer homeostasis, and tight-junction stability; insufficient HIF-1α adaptation reduces zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1), occludin, and claudin stability, whereas epithelial HIF-1α activation improves Western diet-induced hepatic steatosis and metabolic abnormalities [120].

Abnormal microbial metabolic enzymes and metabolites

After entering the liver through the portal vein, microbial metabolites act on hepatocytes, Kupffer cells, HSCs, and liver sinusoidal endothelial cells through BA receptors, fatty acid receptors, G protein-coupled receptors, aryl hydrocarbon receptor signaling, and oxidative stress pathways. Abnormal microbial BA transformation can alter luminal BA toxicity and portal BA load. Bile salt hydrolase (BSH) deconjugates conjugated BAs (CBAs) into unconjugated BAs (UBAs). In patients with MASLD and advanced fibrosis, fecal BSH activity is increased and is associated with reduced fecal BA levels and exacerbated hepatic histological damage; high-BSH strains increase UBA production, whereas small-molecule BSH inhibitors suppress BSH activity in patient-derived fecal microbial communities and single-strain cultures [121]. Elevated UBAs can weaken the intestinal epithelial barrier and alter FXR/takeda G protein-coupled receptor 5 (TGR5)-mediated feedback controlling hepatic BA synthesis, FAO, and Kupffer cell inflammation. Some Clostridium clusters XI/XIVa harbor 7α-dehydroxylation-related enzymes that promote the conversion of primary BAs into deoxycholic acid (DCA). After entering the liver, DCA induces mitochondrial ROS, DNA damage, and the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) in HSCs, promotes the release of prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) and pro-tumor inflammatory mediators, suppresses antitumor immunity, and contributes to the formation of a MASLD-associated HCC microenvironment [96, 122].

Insufficient secretion of protective microbial metabolites, such as SCFAs and tryptophan metabolites, also weakens gut–liver axis homeostasis. SCFAs influence the intestinal barrier and hepatic lipid metabolism through G protein-coupled receptors and epigenetic regulation, and their levels are markedly reduced in MASLD. Butyrate activates G protein-coupled receptor 43 (GPR43/FFAR2), GPR41, and GPR109A, enhances intestinal epithelial junction stability, and promotes regulatory T cell (Treg) differentiation and reduces macrophage NF-κB activity by inhibiting histone deacetylases (HDACs). In B. ovatus-treated models, increased SCFA production is accompanied by lower serum LPS, IL-1β, TNF-α, and CD163 levels, reduced hepatic Srebp1, Acaca, Scd1, and Fasn expression, and enhanced PPARα-related FAO signaling [109]. Butyrate can also attenuate high-fat/low-fiber diet-induced hepatic steatosis through the GPR41/43–calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII)/HDAC1–cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB) pathway [123]. Recent evidence indicates that gut-derived propionate mediates the beneficial effect of apolipoprotein A-I (ApoA-I) in MASLD through the GPR43–Ca2⁺–CaMKII–adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL) pathway, thereby promoting hepatocellular LD hydrolysis and lipid mobilization [124]. Beyond these direct effects on hepatic lipid metabolism, gut-derived signals may also modulate enteroendocrine communication through microbial metabolites and bile acid–TGR5 signaling. Microbiota depletion markedly impairs the postprandial glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) response in mice, whereas a blunted postprandial glucagon-like peptide-2 (GLP-2) response has been associated with increased intestinal permeability, endotoxemia, and systemic inflammation in patients with biopsy-confirmed MASH [125, 126]. However, direct evidence that trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) specifically suppresses GLP-1 or GLP-2 secretion in MASLD remains insufficient.

Tryptophan metabolites regulate barrier protection and hepatic FAO through aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) signaling. Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) and indole-3-propionic acid (IPA) activate AhR and thereby maintain intestinal epithelial tight junctions, mucosal antimicrobial defense, and IL-22-associated barrier repair. Supplementation with IPA or tryptophan metabolite-producing Limosilactobacillus reuteri restores AhR-mediated protection and attenuates MASLD progression [127]. In addition, the gut mycobiota-associated tryptophan catabolite 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA) directly binds AhR and promotes its nuclear translocation, upregulates transcription of carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A (Cpt1a) and acyl-CoA oxidase 1 (ACOX1), enhances hepatic FAO, and alleviates the MASLD phenotype [128].

In contrast, metabolites such as TMAO and ethanolamine can aggravate gut–liver axis injury. Microbiota-derived TMA enters the liver through the portal vein and is oxidized by flavin-containing monooxygenase 3 (FMO3) to generate TMAO. TMAO suppresses cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase (CYP7A1) and sterol 27-hydroxylase (CYP27A1), alters the BA pool and FXR feedback signaling, and increases oxidative stress and inflammation, thereby jointly increasing hepatocellular lipid deposition and cardiometabolic risk [129]. Multi-omics studies have shown that TMAO promotes MASLD progression while impairing both intestinal and hepatic function [130]. Intestinal interleukin-33 (IL-33) promotes microbiota-derived TMAO synthesis and aggravates MASLD progression through HIF-1α-related signaling [131]. Ethanol produced by HiAlc Kpn enters the liver through the portal vein and induces CYP2E1 expression; CYP2E1-mediated ethanol oxidation generates ROS, which further induces mitochondrial injury, lipid peroxidation, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and inflammatory cytokine expression [104, 106]. Persistent ethanolamine accumulation upregulates microRNA-101a-3p, reduces tight-junction protein mRNA stability, suppresses tight-junction protein translation, and further enhances PAMP entry into the liver and intrahepatic inflammation [132]. Collectively, abnormal microbial metabolic enzymes and metabolites act on the intestinal barrier, hepatic lipid metabolism, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses, thereby aggravating MASLD pathology and progressively driving the transition from metabolic dysregulation to inflammatory injury.

Liver-derived mechanisms

Abnormal BA synthesis and bile output

BAs are the major molecular mediators of liver-derived feedback. Hepatocytes synthesize primary BAs from cholesterol and secrete them into the intestinal lumen through bile canaliculi. In the gut lumen, BAs regulate microbial composition, mucosal barrier function, and intestinal immunity through detergent-like antimicrobial activity and signaling mediated by FXR, Takeda G protein-coupled receptor 5 (TGR5), and fibroblast growth factor 15/19 (FGF15/19). In MASLD, hepatocyte innate immune signaling, inflammatory transcriptional programs, BA re-hydroxylation, and nuclear receptor abnormalities jointly reshape BA pool size, hydrophobicity, the primary-to-secondary BA ratio, and the composition of 12α-hydroxylated BAs, thereby affecting microbial selection pressure, intestinal epithelial receptor signaling, and portal BA return. Hepatocyte innate immune signaling and inflammatory transcriptional programs can influence the gut microbiota and mucosal immunity by regulating BA synthesis. Abnormal MyD88 expression alters the BA profile, gut microbial composition, plasma metabolome, and hepatic metabolome, and is accompanied by glucose intolerance, hepatic inflammation, and hepatic insulin resistance [133]. By affecting BA synthesis and liver-derived metabolic output, MyD88-related signaling can remodel the luminal BA ligand environment, altering BSH-positive bacteria, 7α-dehydroxylating bacteria, and secondary BA-generating capacity. These changes shift the proportions of UBAs, deoxycholic acid (DCA), and lithocholic acid (LCA), thereby affecting intestinal epithelial FXR/TGR5 signaling, mucosal barrier permeability, and portal BA load. In addition, a novel hepatocyte gene program co-regulated by RelA, the core NF-κB subunit, and STAT3 directs the expression of genes involved in the alternative BA synthesis pathway, thereby maintaining high exposure to primary BAs in the liver and intestine. Hepatocyte-specific RelA/Stat3 deletion markedly reduces primary BA exposure and alleviates intestinal inflammation, whereas supplementation with chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA) partially restores the enteritis phenotype [134].

In MASH models, the reduction in secondary 7α-dehydroxylated BAs cannot be fully explained by microbial composition, BSH activity, or 7α-dehydroxylation activity. Enhanced hepatic cytochrome P450 2A12/22 (CYP2A12/22)-mediated BA 7α-rehydroxylation reduces secondary 7α-dehydroxylated BA levels and remodels the BA pool, thereby altering enterohepatic BA composition and exposing the intestine to a different BA signaling environment. This further affects BA-tolerant bacteria, 7α-dehydroxylating microbial populations, mucosal immunity, and intestinal barrier status [135]. Hepatic G protein subunit alpha 13 (Gα13) deletion alters the expression of genes related to BA and cholesterol metabolism, regulates cholic acid (CA) and CDCA levels, and produces region-specific differences in inflammatory cytokine expression between the proximal and distal colon [136]. PPARα regulates the proportion of 12α-hydroxylated BAs through sterol 12α-hydroxylase (CYP8B1) and affects BA transport and homeostasis [137]. When PPARα activity is insufficient, reduced hepatocyte FAO, abnormal BA composition, and altered luminal BA selection pressure may occur simultaneously, promoting microbial metabolic dysfunction and enhanced gut-derived inflammatory input.

After bile enters the intestinal lumen, it provides not only BA-mediated antimicrobial pressure but also sIgA, antimicrobial molecules, and other immunologically active components. Hepatic FXR status can regulate the colonic mucus ecosystem through BA output. Liver-specific Fxr deletion alters the colonic transcriptome, increases the colonic mucus layer, and upregulates antimicrobial and mucus-related genes; its microbial features are also consistent with enhanced mucus barrier function [138]. Because FXR suppresses cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase (CYP7A1) and CYP8B1 expression through the small heterodimer partner (SHP/NR0B2), abnormal hepatic FXR feedback alters luminal BA input and BA composition. This remodels the expression of colonic epithelial MUC2, antimicrobial peptides, Toll-like receptors, and inflammasome-related genes, thereby affecting colonic mucus-layer thickness, the spatial distance between bacteria and the epithelial surface, and the intensity of LPS, bacterial DNA, and PGN entry into the portal vein. BA signaling also has segment-specific immune effects in the intestine. In MASLD, enrichment of secondary BAs enhances TGR5/mTOR/OXPHOS signaling in ileal CD8⁺ T cells and induces CD8⁺ T cell-mediated enteritis. Enteritis further suppresses hepatic FXR activity and promotes MASLD progression, creating a mutually reinforcing loop among hepatic steatosis, BA pool remodeling, ileal immune activation, and hepatic FXR inhibition [139]. Biliary sIgA and the polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (pIgR) participate in hepatobiliary immune defense. Hepatic pIgR mediates IgA transport into the intestinal lumen through bile, thereby limiting bacterial trans-mucosal migration and maintaining spatial segregation of the gut microbiota [140]. Therefore, when bile secretion, BA composition, and biliary immune output are abnormal in MASLD, biliary antimicrobial pressure and sIgA-mediated bacterial coating decline. Bacteria can then approach the epithelial surface more readily, increasing the risk of gut-derived PAMP entry into the liver when the mucus layer and tight junctions are also impaired.

Abnormal hepatocyte metabolic signaling

Hepatocyte metabolic nuclear receptors and liver-derived factors regulate the gut microbiota and barrier through FAO, BA synthesis, cholesterol homeostasis, and endocrine signaling. In MASLD, PPARα, PPARγ, FXR, FGF15/19, fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21), and transmembrane 4 L six family member 5 (TM4SF5) influence gut–liver feedback at different levels. PPARα regulates both hepatic FAO and BA composition. Its activation upregulates genes involved in fatty acid transport and β-oxidation, including CPT1A, acyl-CoA oxidase 1 (ACOX1), and acyl-CoA thioesterase 1 (ACOT1), thereby reducing hepatocellular TG accumulation. PPARα can also directly regulate CYP8B1 expression, increase the proportion of 12α-hydroxylated BAs, and may affect BA negative feedback and transport-related gene expression by competing with FXR for RXRα [137]. When PPARα activity is insufficient in MASLD, reduced FAO, enhanced lipotoxicity, abnormal BA composition, and altered luminal BA exposure may occur simultaneously, thereby affecting microbial selection pressure and gut-derived inflammatory input. Activation of PPARα/PPARγ can also restore microbial diversity and barrier-gene expression, reduce LPS levels, upregulate hepatic β-oxidation-related genes, and decrease hepatic macrophage infiltration and steatosis [141].

FGF15/19 mediates ileal feedback control of BA synthesis, whereas FGF21 conveys liver-derived metabolic stress signals. FGF15/19 is the canonical enterohepatic feedback signal for BA–cholesterol homeostasis. Ileal FXR activation induces FGF15/19 release, and FGF15/19 then acts through the portal circulation on the hepatocyte fibroblast growth factor receptor 4 (FGFR4)/β-Klotho complex to suppress CYP7A1 transcription and limit excessive BA synthesis [142]. FGF15 deficiency leads to systemic BA elevation and cholesterol reduction, indicating that disruption of this feedback can simultaneously alter liver-derived BA output, luminal BA pressure, and BA-metabolizing microbial communities [143]. FGF21 is an important hepatokine under hepatic metabolic stress. In patients with MASLD, increased serum FGF21 is accompanied by alterations in fecal microbiota composition and fecal BA profiles, suggesting that FGF21 changes are associated with gut–liver metabolic disturbance. In a methionine–choline-deficient diet-induced steatohepatitis model, exogenous FGF21 reshapes the gut microbiota and enriches BSH-active genera, including Lactobacillus, Akkermansia and Alistipes, thereby regulating BA metabolism, markedly enhancing hepatic and intestinal FXR/TGR5 signaling, and suppressing hepatic inflammation and fibrosis. Antibiotic-mediated microbiota depletion partially weakens the protective effect of FGF21, whereas feces from FMT-treated recipient mice can restore the therapeutic effect of FGF21, confirming that the anti-steatohepatitic effect of FGF21 depends on microbiota-mediated BA–FXR/TGR5 signaling remodeling [144]. Another study showed that hepatic Fgf21 deficiency attenuates the increase in Dubosiella abundance, whereas supplementation with Dubosiella newyorkensis reduces hepatic lipid deposition and liver injury. Antibiotic treatment and FMT further support the involvement of the gut microbiota in Fgf21-related hepatoprotection [145].

Hepatocyte TM4SF5 overexpression directly promotes hepatocellular lipid metabolic disturbance and, in parallel, remodels the gut microbiota by increasing the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio, reducing beneficial bacteria, enriching SCFA-producing genera, and upregulating SCFA-synthetic enzymes, including phosphate acetyltransferase and butyryl-CoA dehydrogenase. This apparent contrast with the protective effects of butyrate and propionate described above reflects the context-dependent nature of SCFA actions: their net metabolic impact likely varies with SCFA species and dose, dietary substrate, portal exposure, and host metabolic state. Under conditions of high substrate availability, microbiota-derived acetate can reach the liver through the portal vein and be converted into acetyl-CoA by ACSS2, thereby providing substrate for DNL [146]. In the TM4SF5-associated model, this microbial-metabolic remodeling is accompanied by hepatocellular steatosis and increased inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and CCL2. These changes ultimately aggravate hepatic inflammation and MASH progression, whereas TM4SF5 deletion reverses the above dysbiosis and metabolic abnormalities [147]. Collectively, abnormal hepatocyte metabolic signals can affect FAO, BA synthesis, cholesterol homeostasis, microbiota structure and intestinal barrier status, so that liver-derived metabolic abnormalities and gut-derived inflammatory inputs promote each other.

Therapeutic effects and molecular adaptations induced by exercise in MASLD

Exercise prescription-dependent effects in MASLD

Exercise has become a core non-pharmacological strategy for MASLD management, but its therapeutic efficacy is strongly prescription-dependent. Exercise modality, intensity, dose, and timing can differentially influence hepatic lipid metabolism, gut microbial function, mitochondrial quality control, and inflammatory responses. Notably, the effect of exercise timing may further depend on the stage of MASLD progression.

Exercise modality

Multi-omics evidence from HFD-induced MASLD mice indicates that aerobic and resistance exercise both reduce hepatic lipid accumulation and are associated with overlapping but partially distinct gut microbial and hepatic transcriptional responses. Aerobic exercise was associated with enrichment of Candidatus_Sacleftimonas, reductions in selected potentially harmful taxa, and downregulation of predicted steroid-biosynthesis pathways, whereas resistance exercise was associated with enrichment of Christensenellaceae_R-7_group, upregulation of selected lipid-metabolism-related pathways, and reduced hepatic LD accumulation and total cholesterol [148]. But the study did not include microbiota depletion, fecal microbiota transplantation, or strain-specific supplementation, these findings should be interpreted as modality-associated gut–liver signatures rather than evidence that specific microbial changes causally mediate the hepatic response.

Meta-analytic evidence indicates that exercise prescriptions for patients with MASLD typically comprise 2–5 sessions per week, moderate-to-high intensity, and 30–60 min per session, with combined aerobic and resistance training showing favorable overall effects across hepatic and metabolic outcomes [149]. The potential advantage of concurrent training lies in the complementarity between aerobic oxidative metabolism and skeletal muscle metabolic reserve. Aerobic exercise increases fatty acid oxidative flux and reduces hepatic TG storage and visceral fat input, whereas resistance exercise increases skeletal muscle mass, glucose transporter 4-mediated glucose uptake, and glycogen storage, thereby reducing compensatory hepatic uptake of glucose and FFAs [150].

However, the relative effects of different exercise modalities vary according to the physiological outcome assessed. Aerobic and resistance training both reduce hepatic fat, whereas resistance exercise generally imposes lower cardiorespiratory and energy demands and may therefore be more applicable when aerobic capacity is limited [151]. Resistance exercise can also reduce hepatic fat independently of clinically significant weight loss while improving skeletal muscle glucose utilization and functional capacity [152]. However, it requires appropriate technique, progressive loading, and, in some patients, professional supervision. High-intensity interval training (HIIT) provides a time-efficient aerobic stimulus, but its effect on hepatic fat is comparable to, rather than consistently greater than, that of moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT) [153]. For broader implementation, aerobic training has the most established evidence base, whereas resistance and combined training provide complementary adaptations according to cardiorespiratory capacity, muscle function, and metabolic comorbidity [154]. Thus, exercise modalities should be compared according to their relative hepatic, cardiorespiratory, and skeletal muscle effects rather than arranged within a single hierarchy of efficacy.

Exercise intensity and dose

Recent dose–response analyses indicate that reductions in hepatic steatosis are nonlinear and tend to plateau once an effective exercise dose is reached. A dose of 460 metabolic equivalent-minutes per week (MET-min/week) can be considered the minimal clinically important dose, 630 MET-min/week falls within a higher-benefit range, and the benefit appears to peak and plateau around 850 MET-min/week. Concurrent training ranked highest by the surface under the cumulative ranking curve, with approximately 640 MET-min/week associated with favorable hepatic fat reduction, whereas model-based estimates suggested that approximately 130 MET-min/week may already produce a clinically meaningful reduction in liver fat [155]. These dose curves indicate that the therapeutic value of exercise in MASLD is not determined simply by increasing training volume. Rather, appropriate exercise modalities may induce skeletal-muscle substrate clearance, adipose-tissue lipolytic adaptation, hepatocellular FAO, and anti-inflammatory responses. When exercise dose exceeds individual adaptive capacity, mitochondrial respiratory burden, fatigue, and reduced adherence may weaken sustained benefit.

Short-term aerobic exercise studies have shown that different aerobic training doses and intensities can reduce liver fat and visceral adiposity, although between-group differences in liver fat reduction may not be significant. This suggests that, once a sufficient exercise volume is reached, further increases in exercise intensity or dose may not produce proportionally greater reductions in liver fat [156]. Synthesized evidence indicates that HIIT may produce more pronounced improvements in alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), and homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), whereas MICT also produces consistent improvements in ALT, TG, HbA1c, and HOMA-IR [149]. Cardiorespiratory fitness provides a complementary, but not equivalent, indicator of exercise responsiveness. In a recent 24-week randomized trial involving patients with MASLD, HIIT produced a greater improvement in peak oxygen uptake (VO₂peak) than MICT, whereas both protocols achieved comparable reductions in hepatic steatosis. This divergence indicates that improvements in aerobic capacity and hepatic steatosis may occur in parallel but are not necessarily proportional [157]. However, current clinical evidence does not establish that either modality is universally superior for reducing hepatic fat. Animal load-gradient studies provide additional evidence. In HFD-induced MASLD rats, after 8 weeks of model induction, low-, moderate-, and high-load aerobic treadmill training was performed at 20 m/min for 30, 60, or 90 min/day over 8 weeks. All three training loads reduced hepatic TG, TC, ALT, and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and improved hepatic LD deposition. Compared with low-load training, moderate- and high-load training produced greater reductions in hepatic TC and TG, and high-load training further outperformed moderate-load training for some lipid endpoints [158]. These findings support a load-dependent response within the tested range but do not establish that progressively longer or higher-load exercise is universally preferable, particularly when individual tolerance, recovery, and adherence are considered.

In HFD-induced MASLD rats, both HIIT and MICT improved dyslipidemia, glucose regulation, hepatic lipid accumulation, and intestinal barrier-related outcomes, whereas HIIT produced larger changes in selected hepatic markers associated with mitochondrial biogenesis, mitophagy, and mitochondrial dynamics [159]. However, in this model, the short bursts of high-energy stress induced by HIIT may more strongly activate the AMPK–peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α) axis and promote PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy and mitochondrial functional recovery, which may explain the more pronounced effects of HIIT compared with MICT. In addition to these hepatic adaptations, improvements in skeletal muscle insulin sensitivity and metabolic efficiency may enhance peripheral glucose and lipid utilization, redirect postprandial substrates toward muscle glycogen storage, and thereby reduce substrate availability for hepatic de novo lipogenesis [160, 161]. However, these skeletal muscle-related adaptations were not directly assessed in the aforementioned study [159].

Exercise timing and disease stage

The efficacy of exercise is influenced not only by prescription type and dose, but also by circadian timing and disease stage. In MASLD mice, treadmill training was performed at ZT13 or ZT22 for 12 weeks, 5 days per week, and 1 h per session. Zeitgeber time is defined relative to the environmental light–dark cycle, with ZT0 indicating lights on and ZT12 indicating lights off. Only ZT22 training significantly reduced the MASLD score, body weight, fat mass, and hepatic TG while increasing SCFA-associated bacteria, including Akkermansia, Lachnospiraceae, and Rikenella. FMT from ZT22-trained mice also reduced liver weight and plasma TG in recipient mice [162]. The effect of exercise also depends on the disease stage at which intervention is initiated. In patients with MASH, 12 weeks of exercise training reduced hepatic TG, visceral adiposity, and plasma TG, but produced limited changes in circulating inflammatory and fibrotic markers [163]. An AMLN diet-induced MASLD progression model further showed that prolonged dietary exposure increased hepatic lipid deposition and sustained activation of the protein kinase R-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK)–eIF2α–activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) pathway. Genes related to the mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt) and FGF21 secretion increased transiently at week 14, but declined with longer-term excessive lipid deposition. Exercise improved mitochondrial function, remodeled the UPRmt activation pattern, and increased FGF21 secretion, thereby delaying MASLD progression [164]. These findings suggest that exercise initiated at earlier stages may primarily reduce hepatic and visceral fat, whereas in progressive MASLD, lipid lowering alone may be insufficient; restoration of mitochondrial proteostasis and stress-adaptive capacity may be required to modify hepatocellular injury and inflammatory progression.

Disease stage also influences exercise feasibility and implementation. In patients with biopsy-confirmed fatty liver disease, low energy, fatigue, dyspnea, and previous or current injury are common barriers to exercise, while physical activity and perceived fitness decline with increasing fibrosis severity [165]. At the program level, limited time, low motivation, restricted access to facilities or professional supervision, and concerns regarding exercise safety may further reduce long-term adherence. These barriers help explain why efficacy in supervised trials often exceeds the long-term effectiveness observed in routine clinical settings. Precision exercise prescription is further constrained by heterogeneity in training exposure, adherence, dietary control, and endpoint assessment, together with the absence of validated biomarkers that reliably predict modality- or dose-specific responses. Current stratification therefore remains based primarily on disease stage, cardiometabolic comorbidity, cardiorespiratory fitness, muscle function, and exercise tolerance.

Exercise-induced molecular adaptations in MASLD

Exercise promotes LD mobilization and FAO

LDs serve both as storage organelles that buffer FFA toxicity in hepatocytes and as a major substrate pool for exercise-induced intrahepatic lipid mobilization. During HFD-induced MASLD, PLIN2 stabilizes the LD surface and limits its interaction with LC3- and lysosome-associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP1)-positive autophagic–lysosomal compartments, thereby reducing lysosomal TG degradation. Exercise activates AMPK/sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) signaling, increases the LC3-II/I ratio and LAMP1 abundance, enhances colocalization of LC3 and LAMP1 with LDs, and promotes LD recognition, sequestration, and lysosomal delivery, thereby enhancing lipophagy [166]. In parallel, exercise reduces PLIN2-mediated LD stability, increases LIPA-dependent hydrolysis of TG and cholesteryl esters, and shifts stored LDs into a mobilized state [91]. These findings indicate that exercise does not simply remove lipid droplets; rather, it promotes the re-entry of LD-derived TG into fatty acid metabolism by initiating AMPK-mediated lipophagy, reducing PLIN2, and enhancing LIPA-dependent hydrolysis. Fatty acids released from LD hydrolysis may still be re-esterified into TG and contribute to LD re-accumulation if they are not directed toward oxidation or export. HFD increases acetyl-CoA carboxylase alpha (ACCα) activity and malonyl-CoA levels, thereby suppressing CPT1A-mediated mitochondrial transport of long-chain fatty acids. Exercise-induced AMPK phosphorylation reduces ACCα activity, relieves malonyl-CoA-mediated inhibition of CPT1A, upregulates CPT1A expression, and promotes the transport and utilization of LD-derived fatty acids through β-oxidation pathways [166–168]. In parallel, exercise reduces the expression of DNL regulators, including SREBP-1c, ChREBP, and FASN, thereby limiting the supply of newly synthesized fatty acids [159, 168].

Exercise also modifies VLDL kinetics, but current evidence does not support a uniform increase in apoB100–MTP/MTTP-dependent lipid export. In adults with MASLD, 16 weeks of aerobic exercise reduced intrahepatic TG without altering hepatic VLDL-TG or VLDL-apoB100 secretion rates [169]. In a separate randomized trial, exercise increased the fractional catabolic rates of VLDL1-TG and VLDL1-apoB and reduced liver fat, whereas VLDL1-TG production remained unchanged despite an increase in VLDL1-apoB production [170]. Experimental evidence is similarly context dependent. In HFD-fed rats, exercise training reduced hepatic MTP content and VLDL-TG production together with hepatic TG accumulation [171]. These findings indicate that exercise-mediated liver fat reduction is not driven by consistent upregulation of MTTP/MTP or apoB100 secretion. Rather, exercise appears to redirect hepatic lipid flux through reduced fatty acid substrate pressure, enhanced FAO, and improved peripheral clearance of TG-rich VLDL particles.

PPARα is a key nuclear receptor through which exercise redirects fatty acid fate. During HFD-induced hepatic lipid deposition, increased miR-34a suppresses PPARα/SIRT1/AMPK signaling and reduces CPT1A, CPT2, SLC27A1, and SLC27A4, thereby limiting long-chain fatty acid entry into mitochondrial and peroxisomal oxidation pathways. Exercise suppresses miR-34a and restores PPARα, SIRT1, AMPK, and downstream FAO programs [167]. In addition, exercise across different training loads increases PPARα and CPT1A expression and AMPK phosphorylation, while suppressing the LXRα/SREBP-1c/FASN/ACCα pathway, thereby attenuating hepatic lipid deposition [158, 159]. The cysteine dioxygenase type 1 (CDO1)–calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase kinase 2 (CaMKK2)–AMPK axis further defines an upstream mechanism by which exercise activates AMPK. Exercise stimulates Cdo1 transcription through the cAMP–protein kinase A (PKA)–CREB pathway. CDO1 then promotes the interaction between CaMKK2 and AMPK and enhances AMPK phosphorylation, thereby amplifying AMPK-dependent regulation of lipophagy, inhibitory ACC phosphorylation, and mitochondrial biogenesis. Hepatocyte-specific Cdo1 deficiency weakens exercise-induced AMPK activation, FAO enhancement, and hepatic fat reduction [172].

Collectively, current evidence indicates that exercise reduces hepatic lipid deposition by promoting LD hydrolysis and CPT1A-mediated fatty acid transport, restoring PPARα-dependent FAO, and remodeling VLDL-TG/apoB metabolism in a context-dependent manner. However, the actual fate of exercise-mobilized fatty acids remains insufficiently validated. Mitochondrial quality control and attenuation of oxidative stress

Mitochondria are the principal sites of hepatocellular fatty acid β-oxidation and a major source of inflammatory signals under lipotoxic stress. Lipid overload chronically increases the burden on mitochondrial β-oxidation, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, and OXPHOS, leading to increased ROS generation, oxidative mtDNA damage, respiratory chain dysfunction, and impaired proteostasis. Failure to clear damaged mitochondria permits the release of DAMPs, including ox-mtDNA, which activate the NLRP3 inflammasome and JNK-related stress signaling. After activating AMPK, exercise promotes mitochondrial biogenesis by increasing PGC-1α expression and improves the acetylation status of mitochondrial metabolic and antioxidant enzymes through SIRT3, thereby enhancing both FAO capacity and antioxidant defense. Exercise also promotes PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, strengthening recognition and clearance of damaged mitochondria, and regulates the expression of mitofusin 1/2 (MFN1/MFN2) while reducing mitochondrial fission 1 (FIS1), thereby improving mitochondrial fusion–fission dynamics [158, 159, 173]. Therefore, exercise-induced mitochondrial regulation should be understood as a coordinated process involving biogenesis, clearance, and dynamic turnover, rather than simply as an increase in mitochondrial number or biogenesis alone.

HIG1 hypoxia inducible domain family member 1 A (HIGD1A) and UPRmt participate in exercise-mediated repair of mitochondrial injury and proteostatic imbalance, respectively. In DIO mice and FFA-stimulated AML12 cells, reduced Higd1a expression aggravates lipid overload, leading to mitochondrial structural injury, increased ox-mtDNA release, and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and JNK signaling. JNK activation further suppresses FAO-related gene expression, creating a reinforcing cycle among lipid overload, mitochondrial injury, and inflammatory stress. Treadmill exercise upregulates hepatic Higd1a, reduces ox-mtDNA leakage, suppresses NLRP3 inflammasome and JNK signaling, restores the expression of FAO-related genes, including Cpt1a, medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (Acadm), and Ppara, thereby attenuating hepatic steatosis, liver injury, and inflammation [174]. These findings position HIGD1A upstream of the mitochondrial injury–inflammation loop, whereas UPRmt/FGF21 signaling contributes to the restoration of mitochondrial proteostasis after exercise. UPRmt contributes to mitochondrial proteostasis under lipid overload. During MASLD progression, the PERK–eIF2α–ATF4 pathway and FGF21 secretion change according to the degree of mitochondrial stress. When lipid burden remains within the compensatory range, enhanced UPRmt promotes processing of misfolded proteins and mitochondrial repair. However, when lipid deposition continues to increase and exceeds mitochondrial adaptive capacity, UPRmt-related molecules and FGF21 secretion decline, further aggravating mitochondrial proteostatic failure, ROS generation, and hepatocellular injury [164]. Exercise improves mitochondrial function, modulates PERK–eIF2α–ATF4 signaling, enhances antioxidant defense, and promotes FGF21 secretion, thereby restoring mitochondrial protein folding, degradation, and repair capacity [164, 173]. These findings suggest that HIGD1A primarily interrupts lipid overload-induced mitochondrial injury–inflammation amplification, whereas the UPRmt/FGF21-related pathway reflects and participates in exercise-induced restoration of mitochondrial proteostasis (Fig. 4 B).

Suppression of immune-inflammatory responses

Sustained activation of immune-inflammatory responses is a key pathological event in the progression from MASL to MASH. DAMPs released from lipotoxic hepatocytes, damaged mitochondria, and dying cells cooperate with gut-derived PAMPs, such as LPS, to activate hepatic innate immune signaling. This promotes KC dysfunction, MoMF infiltration, NLRP3 inflammasome activation, enhanced cGAS–STING signaling, and amplification of NF-κB-mediated inflammatory transcription. Exercise-induced PPARα activation can reduce lipotoxic substrates, enhance antioxidant defense, and suppress NF-κB inflammatory transcription. In HFD-induced liver injury models, PPARα downregulation is accompanied by reduced HO-1 and SOD1, increased ROS and lipid peroxidation, and enhanced NF-κB inflammatory cascades and macrophage accumulation [175]. Exercise-mediated upregulation of PPARα restores FAO, reduces lipotoxic substrates such as FFAs, oxidized lipids, and ceramides, lowers CYP2E1/ROS burden, strengthens antioxidant defense, and consequently alleviates hepatocyte-derived inflammatory stimulation and macrophage inflammatory responses [158, 159, 175]. PPARγ is regulated by exercise, but its functional consequences are tissue-specific. In steatotic hepatocytes, aberrant PPARγ activation promotes lipid uptake, LD storage, and expression of lipogenesis-related genes. Swimming exercise suppresses hepatic PPARγ and its target-gene transcription, thereby reducing CD36-mediated fatty acid uptake, SCD1-mediated lipid synthesis, and PLIN2-mediated LD stabilization, ultimately attenuating hepatic lipid deposition [176]. In contrast to its hepatic role, PPARγ in adipose tissue can induce the release of neuregulin-4 (Nrg4). Exercise enhances adipose tissue Pparγ-Nrg4 signaling, and circulating NRG4 acts on hepatocyte ERBB4 and activates AKT, promoting inhibitory phosphorylation of cGAS, reducing cGAMP production, and suppressing STING downstream TBK1/IRF3 inflammatory transcription [44]. Thus, exercise may suppress the hepatic PPARγ–CD36/SCD1/PLIN2 lipogenic program while activating an adipose PPARγ–NRG4–ErbB4/AKT signal that inhibits hepatocyte cGAS–STING signaling.

The Krüppel-like factor 10 (KLF10)/fumarate hydratase 1 (FH1) pathway links exercise stimuli with TCA cycle metabolites, epigenetic remodeling, and inflammatory injury. In MASH, lipogenesis, TCA cycle disturbance, cell death, and inflammation reinforce one another. Exercise induces hepatic Klf10 expression through the cAMP/PKA/CREB pathway, and Klf10 directly activates Fh1 transcription. Increased FH1 lowers intracellular fumarate levels, relieving fumarate-mediated competitive inhibition of α-ketoglutarate-dependent histone demethylases. This reduces trimethylation of lysine 4 on histone H3 (H3K4me3) at the promoters of lipogenic genes, including Fasn, Scd1, and Acaca, thereby suppressing SREBP-1c-mediated DNL and hepatocellular lipid accumulation. As a consequence, lipotoxicity-induced production and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and IL-6, are reduced, macrophage activation and HSC fibrogenic responses are inhibited, and hepatic inflammatory infiltration is markedly attenuated. Hepatocyte-specific Klf10 deficiency aggravates MASH pathology and weakens the protective effect of exercise, whereas hepatic Klf10 overexpression enhances exercise-mediated improvement of MASH [177]. These findings suggest that exercise can modulate inflammatory injury through hepatocyte metabolic intermediates and epigenetic remodeling (Fig. 4 C).

Gut microbiota remodeling and improvement of gut–liver axis injury

Gut microbiota remodeling is an important process by which exercise extends its effects in MASLD from intrahepatic metabolic regulation to systemic immunometabolic adaptation. In MASLD, gut dysbiosis, abnormal BA pool composition, insufficient SCFA production, intestinal mucosal barrier disruption, and increased hepatic LPS influx collectively constitute portal inflammatory input. LPS activates inflammatory transcription in KCs and hepatocytes through the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway; BA metabolic disturbance weakens FXR/TGR5-mediated BA synthesis feedback, intestinal barrier protection, and anti-inflammatory signaling; and reduced SCFAs impair GPR41/43- and histone deacetylase (HDAC)-related barrier repair and anti-inflammatory effects [178]. Exercise can reduce portal inflammatory input by remodeling microbial metabolic function. In MASLD mice, exercise performed at ZT22 enriched SCFA-associated bacteria, including Akkermansia, Lachnospiraceae, and Rikenella. FMT from exercise-trained donor mice reduced liver weight and plasma TG in recipient mice and showed trends toward lower MASLD scores and hepatic TG [162]. Another study showed that both aerobic and resistance exercise reversed HFD-induced abnormalities in microbial metabolic function, strengthened the positive association between linoleic acid metabolism and Odoribacter, and was accompanied by changes in hepatic lipid metabolism- and inflammation-related gene responses [148].

The FGF21–Dubosiella axis provides causal evidence that exercise improves MASLD by regulating the gut–liver axis. In HFD-induced MASLD mice, antibiotic-mediated microbiota depletion weakened the protective effect of exercise, whereas FMT from exercise-trained donors transferred part of the hepatic protective phenotype. FGF21 deficiency blunted exercise-induced Dubosiella enrichment and hepatoprotection, whereas supplementation with Dubosiella newyorkensis improved liver function and lipid deposition [145]. In models of fatty liver, the anti-steatohepatitic effect of exogenous FGF21 was partly mediated by the microbiota and BAs; antibiotic treatment weakened its protection, whereas FMT from FGF21-treated donors partially recapitulated hepatic protection [144]. Thus, exercise-induced FGF21 may enhance hepatocyte UPRmt and FAO adaptation on the one hand, while reducing hepatic LPS influx and intrahepatic TLR4/NF-κB inflammatory input through Dubosiella, BA metabolism, and intestinal barrier protection on the other [144, 145]. BAs and SCFAs are key metabolites through which exercise remodels the gut–liver axis. In MASLD, alterations in BA pool size, hydrophobicity, the primary-to-secondary BA ratio, and the composition of 12α-hydroxylated BAs can affect microbial selection pressure, intestinal epithelial receptor signaling, and portal BA return. By improving BA composition, exercise may enable FXR/TGR5 signaling to regulate BA synthesis feedback, intestinal epithelial barrier function, hepatocyte lipid metabolism, and KC inflammatory responses. SCFAs, in turn, strengthen tight junctions and suppress inflammation through GPR41/43- and HDAC-related pathways [179, 180].

A recent fecal microbiota transplantation study provides more direct evidence for an exercise–microbiota–GLP-1 pathway. Transplantation of microbiota from exercise-trained mice into high-fat diet-fed recipients improved insulin sensitivity, glucose homeostasis, circulating lipid and LPS levels, and hepatic steatosis. These effects were accompanied by increased intestinal medium- and long-chain fatty acids (MCFAs/LCFAs) and higher GLP-1 levels in both ileal tissue and serum [181]. Selected fatty acids also stimulated GLP-1 secretion from Caco-2 cells, supporting a transferable microbiota–metabolite–GLP-1 link. Because the study did not include GLP-1 receptor blockade or L-cell-specific manipulation, GLP-1 should be considered a candidate mediator rather than a proven requirement for the hepatic benefits transferred by exercise-remodeled microbiota. Human evidence provides complementary translational support. In obese adults with MASLD, reported as NAFLD in the original study, seven days of supervised vigorous aerobic exercise reduced the elevated fasting GLP-1 concentration but restored the acute GLP-1 response to oral glucose and improved insulin sensitivity [182]. These findings indicate normalization of nutrient-responsive GLP-1 regulation rather than a sustained increase in circulating GLP-1; however, because the study did not assess the gut microbiota or microbial metabolites, microbiota-mediated regulation of this human response remains to be established.

Collectively, exercise improves the gut–liver axis primarily by reducing portal pathological input and by alleviating hepatic metabolic-inflammatory burden through changes in microbial metabolism, barrier function, and liver-derived feedback (Fig. 4 D).

Conclusions and future perspectives

Targeting exercise-induced systemic and local metabolic adaptations represents an important systems-level strategy for managing MASLD and potentially slowing its progression toward MASH and liver fibrosis. Current evidence indicates that exercise may promote LD mobilization and FAO through the AMPK/SIRT1–PPARα axis, improve mitochondrial quality control and redox homeostasis, and limit intrahepatic inflammatory amplification by modulating TLR4/NF-κB, cGAS–STING, NLRP3 inflammasome signaling, and the activation of KCs and MoMFs. In parallel, exercise-mediated regulation of intestinal barrier integrity, portal LPS exposure, SCFAs, and BA–FXR/TGR5 signaling may further reshape hepatic inflammatory thresholds and metabolic responsiveness. Therefore, the therapeutic effect of exercise in MASLD may be best interpreted as a systemic physiological intervention that integrates hepatic metabolic adaptation, mitochondrial remodeling, immune-inflammatory restraint, and regulation of gut-derived inputs.

Although exercise acts at multiple biological levels, the current mechanistic evidence remains dominated by animal and cell-based studies, whereas clinical trials vary substantially in exercise prescription, supervision, adherence, dietary control, and endpoint selection. Improvements in hepatic steatosis also frequently coincide with changes in body weight, adiposity, insulin sensitivity, and dietary behavior, making exercise-specific mechanisms difficult to isolate; similarly, causality remains unproven for many microbiota–metabolite–liver associations. Future trials should therefore standardize exercise and dietary exposure, stratify participants by disease stage and metabolic phenotype, and integrate mechanistic biomarkers with long-term follow-up.

Combining structured exercise with glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1RA) therapy represents a clinically relevant research direction because the two interventions may provide complementary metabolic effects. Semaglutide has improved histological outcomes in patients with MASH, whereas a randomized trial in adults with obesity showed that exercise combined with liraglutide produced greater reductions in body weight and fat mass than exercise alone and improved insulin sensitivity and cardiorespiratory fitness [19, 183]. However, the exercise–liraglutide trial did not enroll patients with biopsy-confirmed MASH or assess liver histology; whether structured exercise provides additional liver-specific benefit during GLP-1RA treatment therefore remains to be directly tested. Modulation of the gut microbiota provides an important entry point for mechanistic research and therapeutic development in MASLD. Probiotics, prebiotics, synbiotics, FMT, and engineered bacteria may influence MASLD by regulating intestinal barrier integrity, BA metabolism, SCFAs, endotoxin burden, and immune-inflammatory status. However, existing studies differ substantially in strain composition, intervention dose, treatment duration, host metabolic background, and endpoint selection [184]. Future studies should determine which microbiota-directed interventions produce reproducible hepatic benefits and whether these effects are mediated by defined microbial taxa, metabolites, or barrier-related pathways.

From a translational perspective, future research on exercise intervention in MASLD should place greater emphasis on long-term adaptive effects and interindividual response heterogeneity. Epigenetic regulation is a promising entry point, because MASLD progression is accompanied by changes in hepatic DNA methylation and chromatin state. Recent evidence suggests that DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1)-mediated methylation can weaken hepatocyte nuclear factor 4 alpha (HNF4α)/PPARα-dependent metabolic transcription [185]. Future studies should test whether long-term exercise reprograms DNA methylation or histone modifications at key metabolic loci, including PPARα- and PGC-1α-related regulatory regions, and whether these changes predict sustained improvement in FAO, inflammatory restraint, and disease recurrence after training cessation. A second priority is to define the interorgan communication networks that determine exercise responsiveness. MASLD is shaped by crosstalk among the liver, skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, and intestine; therefore, exercise mechanisms cannot be fully resolved within a liver-only framework [97]. Tracing technologies, cell-specific knockout models, single-cell RNA sequencing, and spatial transcriptomics may help clarify how exercise-induced myokines, gut-derived metabolites, adipokines, and extracellular vesicles act on hepatocytes, KCs, HSCs, and MoMFs. Recent evidence that exercise-derived exosomes enriched in extracellular nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (eNAMPT) activate intrahepatic SIRT1/nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD +) signaling and improve age-related hepatic steatosis and fibrosis further supports the need to examine extracellular vesicles as mediators of heterogeneous exercise responses [186].

Overall, the future of exercise research in MASLD should shift from proving that exercise is effective to defining which patients benefit, at which disease stage, through which molecular axis, and at what level of biological response. Combined therapeutic strategies, epigenetic regulation, and interorgan communication should ultimately serve one goal: establishing a stratified, monitorable, and adjustable framework for precision exercise intervention in MASLD.
