Efficacy of a Disposable Skin‐Stretch Closure Device Combined With Vacuum Sealing Drainage in the Treatment of Refractory Skin and Soft Tissue Defects: A Single‐Arm Prospective Study
Authors: Hao Guo, Zhengguo Zhu, Jinchang Wang, Zhonghe Wang, Yilin Wang, Jun Zhang, Wei Zhang, Guangping Liu, Zhiguang Chen, Shuangcheng Li, Xingxing Hu, Meijing Dou, Junjun Tang, Peifu Tang, Hua Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThe treatment of refractory skin and soft tissue defects (RSSTDs) has long posed a global challenge for surgeons. Traditional closure methods, such as skin grafting and flap repair, are often associated with significant trauma and a steep learning curve. Skin stretching, which enables the generation of “extra skin,” has emerged as a promising alternative. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy of a disposable skin‐stretch closure (DSSC) device combined with vacuum sealing drainage (VSD) in the treatment of RSSTDs.MethodsA prospective single‐arm clinical study was conducted to analyze the clinical data of 43 patients with RSSTDs who were treated with the DSSC device for wound repair at our hospital between July 2022 and December 2024. Among these patients, 30 were male and 13 were female, with ages ranging from 14 to 71 years (mean: 41.86 ± 16.02 years). The wound areas ranged from 4 to 90 cm2 (mean: 18.56 ± 18.04 cm2). Wound conditions (including closure and healing) and the presence of complications were recorded. Wound closure was defined as the approximation of wound edges by manually compressing the bilateral skin to achieve tension‐free or minimal‐tension coaptation, allowing direct suturing. Closure time and closure rate (wound area/closure time) were recorded. Wound healing was defined as 100% re‐epithelialization of the wound surface, confirmed during two consecutive follow‐up assessments spaced 2 weeks apart, with no exudate, drainage, or dressing requirements. Healing time and healing grade (classified as Grade A, B, or C according to wound healing classification standards) were recorded.ResultsAll 43 patients were followed up, and all wounds achieved primary healing. No complications, including wound edge damage, necrosis, infection, dehiscence, or edema, were reported. Patients did not report pain or discomfort, and no significant scar formation was observed during the follow‐up. The wound closure time ranged from 5 to 17 days (mean: 9.07 ± 2.93 days), which was comparable to the 7.8 ± 3.8 days reported in the literature. The wound closure rate ranged from 0.35 to 7.44 cm2/day (mean: 1.99 ± 1.63 cm2/day). The wound healing time ranged from 17 to 29 days (mean: 22.74 ± 2.80 days). The Visual Analog Scalescores ranged from 1 to 3 (mean: 2.02 ± 0.64), significantly lower than the 7.0 ± 1.0 reported in the literature. One patient experienced complete wound healing within 17 days without the need for suturing.ConclusionThe DSSC device was effective in treating RSSTDs, with short wound closure times, minimal complications, and a simple procedure.

Introduction

The skin is the body's largest organ [1]. It serves as a barrier, regulating immune function and temperature, and supporting metabolism [2]. In recent years, the incidence of high‐force injuries leading to severe soft tissue damage and refractory skin and soft tissue defects (RSSTDs), which cannot be sutured directly, has increased [3, 4, 5]. Treating skin and soft tissue defects remains a persistent challenge for surgeons. In the United States alone, wound treatment and care are estimated to cost up to 25 billion U.S. dollars annually [6].

Although several techniques are used in clinical practice to manage skin defects, including dressing changes, skin grafting, flap repair, and skin stretching, each approach has limitations. Dressing changes often result in prolonged recovery periods, imposing significant psychological burdens on patients [7]. Skin grafting and flap repair can address most types of skin defects; however, both require secondary surgeries that may cause additional trauma. Moreover, flap repair carries risks such as vascular obstruction and flap necrosis, is time‐consuming, and requires advanced surgical expertise, with plantar flap surgery not always yielding optimal results [8, 9]. Mechanical skin‐stretching techniques exploit the biomechanical properties of the skin to generate additional tissue. Various skin‐stretching devices have been introduced in clinical settings [10]; however, their complexity, long treatment duration, and risk of complications limit their widespread adoption [11]. Therefore, continued research and innovation are needed to develop safer, more efficient, and patient‐centered approaches for managing skin defects.

Considering these issues, we developed a disposable skin‐stretch closure (DSSC) device (Chongging Gexin Medical Technology Co. Ltd., Chongqing, China), which has been granted a Chinese National Invention Patent (Patent No.: ZL201610649833.X) and a Medical Device Registration Certificate (Yu Device Registration: 20202020297) (Figure 1). The device is easy to operate, minimally invasive, and widely applicable and can be used in combination with vacuum sealing drainage (VSD). It has demonstrated satisfactory therapeutic outcomes in clinical practice.

The physical diagram of the DSSC device [12].

We analyzed the clinical data of 43 patients with RSSTDs who were admitted to the Senior Department of Orthopedics of the Fourth Medical Center of Chinese PLA General Hospital between July 2022 and December 2024. The aims of this study were: (i) to evaluate the clinical efficacy of the DSSC device combined with VSD in treating RSSTDs; (ii) to discover the advantages of the DSSC device; and (iii) to explore different timing and application methods depending on the presence of skin infection, in order to better promote wound healing.

Materials and Methods

Device Design

The DSSC device consists primarily of a control mechanism and wire clasps. The control mechanism comprises a housing, a base, a ratchet wheel, a rotary plate, a knob, a pull cord, and other components. An external knob is provided, which is connected to the internal rotary plate and ratchet wheel as an integral assembly. The pull cord is wound around the rotary plate. A buckle is arranged on the rear side of the base and is connected to a pawl via a spring. In the normal state, the ratchet wheel and the pawl form a ratchet mechanism that permits rotation in only one direction and prevents reverse rotation, thereby tensioning the pull cord wound on the rotary plate. When the buckle is pushed toward the center, the pawl retracts and disengages from the ratchet wheel, allowing the rotary plate to rotate freely, so that the pull cord can be extended (paid out) for subsequent rewinding (Figure 1) [12].

Material specifications: Control mechanism components: Polyamide 66 (PA66, i.e., polyhexamethylene adipamide). Cord‐winding buckle/clasp: Stainless steel 316 (UNS S31600/1.4401). Pull cord: Carbon fiber.

This device has a ratchet wheel with 25 teeth. It should be noted that the mechanism advances by one gear/click/step per day, rather than by one individual tooth—although functionally these are equivalent.

Sample Size Estimation

With wound closure time as the primary efficacy endpoint, a one‐group target value test was adopted (two‐sided α = 0.05, power 1 − β = 0.80). Based on the preliminary trial standard deviation σ = 3.5 days and a clinically meaningful difference δ = 1.5 days, the sample size was calculated using the formula n = (Z 1₋α/2 + Z 1₋β)2 σ 2/δ 2, yielding a minimum effective sample size of 43 cases. Accounting for a 15% dropout/loss to follow‐up rate, the planned enrollment was 51 cases.

Study Population

After approval by the institutional review committee, we conducted a prospective study (The prospective study registration number: ChiCTR2200057188) to analyze data from all patients with RSSTDs who were treated with the DSSC device combined with VSD in the Orthopedics Department of the Senior Department of Orthopedics of the Fourth Medical Center of Chinese PLA General Hospital between 2022 and 2024 (approval number: S2021‐125‐01). Eligible patients were aged 14–80 years and had wounds located on the limbs, chest, back, or buttocks. Exclusion criteria included severe malnutrition; sepsis; severe hypoproteinemia that could not be corrected (serum albumin < 30 g/L despite infusion); pregnancy or lactation; specific infections such as tuberculosis, tetanus, or fungal infections; wounds caused by tumor invasion or residual malignant tumor cells; skin diseases such as psoriasis elsewhere; peripheral vascular disease; inability to participate due to objective factors; and non‐compliance with postoperative management. A total of 43 patients were ultimately enrolled. All patients completed follow‐up with no dropouts or losses, as the effective sample size (43 cases) met the prespecified minimum requirement. Therefore, enrollment was terminated per protocol, and the final analysis was performed on the full analysis set.

Surgical Technique

Anesthetic methods were selected according to the injury type and surgical requirements. After treating the deep tissues, including bone, nerves, blood vessels, and tendons, the fascia layer was sutured whenever possible in cases of exposed deep structures or internal fixation. The wire clasps were secured to the intact skin using either a skin stapler or sutures, placed at least 0.5 cm from the wound edge, with 2–5 cm spacing between clasps. The pulling wire was then crossed through the clasps, and the knob was rotated to apply appropriate skin tension. The wound was covered with a VSD dressing, with the knob left exposed (Figure 2). Alternatively, the VSD dressing could be applied first, followed by placement of the DSSC device over the VSD surface.

Insertion method of the DSSC device. (A) Placement of wire clasps. (B) Rotation of the knob after winding the pulling wire to tighten the skin.

Postoperative Management

Patients received conventional anti‐infection treatment. They were instructed to rotate the knob once daily, adjusting one gear at a time to avoid excessive tightness or discomfort around the wound. Wound healing was observed daily, and the DSSC device was removed once sufficient closure was achieved, followed by intermittent suturing. Sutures were typically removed after 2 weeks to confirm complete healing. Patients underwent follow‐up evaluations at 1, 3, and 6 months after wound healing, which were independently assessed by two senior physicians.

Data Collection

Operation time, intraoperative blood loss, wound condition (closure and healing), and any complications were recorded. Wound condition was assessed according to the following indicators: (1) Wound closure was defined as achieving skin approximation with minimal or no tension, confirmed by gently compressing the skin on both sides of the wound to allow direct suturing. Closure time and closure speed (wound area/closure time) were recorded. (2) Wound healing was defined as 100% re‐epithelialization of the wound surface, confirmed during two consecutive follow‐up visits 2 weeks apart, without discernible exudate or the need for additional drainage or dressing [13].

Wound healing time and healing grade were also recorded. The healing grade was classified as Grade A, B, or C according to standard wound‐healing criteria. The Vancouver Scar Scale was used to evaluate the severity of the postoperative primary scar. Final scar assessment during the follow‐up was scored on a 15‐point scale, with higher scores indicating greater scar severity.

Statistical Analysis

This study was a single‐arm prospective study. The data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics 26) software. Measurement data were tested for normal distribution using the K‐S test; if the data followed a normal distribution, they were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD); if not, they were represented by M (Q1, Q3).

Results

Patient and Injury Characteristics

A total of 43 patients were included, of whom 30 were male and 13 were female. The patients' age range was 14–71 years, with a mean of 41.86 ± 16.02 years. Wound areas ranged from 4 to 90 cm2, with a mean of 18.56 ± 18.04 cm2. The wound area was calculated using a rhomboid approximation formula, as most wounds were elliptical and relatively similar to a rhombus: S = (a × b)/2, where a and b represent the length and width of the rhombus. The causes of injury included 25 cases of traffic accidents, 12 cases of falls, and 6 cases of falls from heights. The causes of skin and soft tissue defects included 16 cases of open fractures, 12 cases of osteomyelitis, 11 cases of wound infections, 2 cases of degloving injuries, 1 case of skin flap donor‐site necrosis after free grafting, and 1 case of compartment syndrome involving bone and fascia. Injury sites included 23 cases in the lower leg, 9 cases on the lateral ankle, 3 cases in the heel, 3 cases in the sole, 2 cases in the pelvis, 2 cases in the thigh, and 1 case in the forearm. Laterality included 18 cases on the left side and 25 cases on the right side. Detailed baseline data of the patients are presented in Table 1.

Clinical Results

All 43 patients were followed up. All wounds achieved Grade A healing, and no complications, including skin edge damage, wound necrosis, infection, dehiscence, or edema, were observed. None of the patients reported pain or discomfort. No significant scar formation was noted during the follow‐up. Wound closure times ranged from 5 to 17 days, with a mean of 9.07 ± 2.93 days. Wound closure speeds ranged from 0.35 to 7.44 cm2/day, with a mean of 1.99 ± 1.63 cm2/day. Wound healing times ranged from 17 to 29 days, with a mean of 22.74 ± 2.80 days. Visual Analog Scale scores ranged from 1 to 3, with a mean of 2.02 ± 0.64. One patient was transferred to another department due to progression of lung cancer after use of the DSSC device. At the 17‐day postoperative follow‐up, the wound had healed directly without suturing (Table 2). Typical cases are shown in Figures 3, 4, 5.

Discussion

Main Findings of the Study

Medical and societal demands necessitate improved treatment approaches for RSSTDs, with approximately 3.52 million cases requiring clinical intervention annually in the United States alone [14]. In order to resolve the aforementioned issues, our team developed a DSSC device that applies the principle of skin stretching by pulling the skin on both sides of the wound toward the center through a non‐reversible ratchet mechanism, thereby achieving wound closure. In this study, we found that, all wounds of 43 patients achieved primary healing. No complications were reported. The mean wound closure time was 9.07 ± 2.93 days, which was comparable to the 7.8 ± 3.8 days reported in the literature. The mean Visual Analog Scalescores was 2.02 ± 0.64, significantly lower than the 7.0 ± 1.0 reported in the literature. The device offers several advantages: (a) reduced trauma and minimal complications; (b) simple design and procedure with real‐time adjustment of pulling force; (c) effective management of irregular wounds; (d) compatibility with the VSD system; and (e) improved esthetic and functional outcomes when preserving original skin in areas such as the scalp, sole, and other sites (Figure 3).

Current Status of Treatment for Skin and Soft Tissue Defects and Research Advances in Skin‐Stretching Device

The primary clinical methods for managing RSSTDs include dressing changes, skin grafting, flap repair, and skin stretching; however, each has limitations. Dressing changes are time‐consuming and impose a considerable psychological burden on patients [7]. Skin grafting and flap repair can cause secondary trauma, blood supply disturbances, and flap necrosis [8, 9] Furthermore, these procedures are lengthy and require advanced microsurgical expertise with a steep learning curve [8, 9]. Flaps in weight‐bearing areas of the foot lack long‐term durability and are difficult to manage effectively [15]. Recent advances have made skin stretching an increasingly viable option for treating complex skin and soft tissue defects.

Skin stretching gradually expands tissue by harnessing the biomechanical properties of skin, including inherent elasticity, mechanical stretchability, biological stretchability, and stress relaxation. Mechanical stress and hypoxia promote collagen fiber elongation, cellular proliferation, and angiogenesis, resulting in “extra skin” that ultimately enables wound closure [16]. Currently, widely used skin‐stretching devices are typically modified expanders that employ steel needles to stretch soft tissue [17]. However, these devices often cause complications such as penetration of steel needles into skin margins, leading to damage, necrosis, and tearing, particularly in irregular wounds [18]. Topaz et al. [18] designed the TopClosure device, which relies on adhesive fixation; however, the adhesive can withstand only limited tension. Increasing adhesive strength may lead to skin avulsion, and complications such as device failure and skin allergies have been reported [19]. DermaClose employs a clockwork mechanism to automatically release tension and stretch the skin; however, this device pulls the skin continuously and cannot be adjusted to accommodate individual wound characteristics [20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. Moreover, cases of skin necrosis and ischemic injury to muscles and nerves have been reported [25]. In response to these concerns, our team developed a DSSC device that offers significant advantages over existing devices. The DSSC device demonstrated strong therapeutic efficacy with minimal complications. Unlike traditional devices that rely on repeated puncturing of the skin margin with steel needles, the DSSC device employs a skin stapler to secure the wire clasp directly to healthy skin surrounding the wound, thereby avoiding irritation to both the wound and adjacent skin margins. Furthermore, the barbs on the wire clasp are relatively shallow, which minimizes tissue trauma. Pulling and closure forces can also be adjusted in real‐time according to skin tension and the patient's pain tolerance during treatment. In this study, none of the 43 patients developed skin margin damage, tearing, or other complications, and no patients reported pain or discomfort. The resulting scars were relatively small and esthetically favorable.

Advantages of the DSSC Device

The DSSC device has a simple structure and procedure. Traditional skin expanders involve multiple components, including steel needles cut to sizes appropriate for the wound and connected with additional devices before being expanded by methods such as tightening nuts, making the process cumbersome. In contrast, the DSSC device requires only wrapping the pulling wire around the wire clasp in a shoelace‐like fashion under local anesthesia. This technique is quick and easy, involves minimal components, and enables patients to manage their care independently after hospital discharge, thereby reducing hospital stay and lowering medical costs. The approach is convenient for both physicians and patients. Patients can rotate the knob daily according to their pain tolerance; typically, once per day is sufficient, with the goal being adequate tension without excessive tightness or pain.

The DSSC device is particularly effective for irregular wounds, as the wire clasp can be secured around the wound margin to pull surrounding soft tissue toward the center, thereby achieving satisfactory closure. In our study, one patient with an irregular wound on the medial aspect of the calcaneus, who required transfer to another hospital for lung cancer surgery, was lost to follow‐up after 17 days. Subsequent outpatient examination revealed that the wound had healed completely without complications. We speculate that multidirectional stretching of the skin may be more effective than unidirectional stretching.

The DSSC device can be used in combination with the VSD system. For contaminated or infected wounds that often require multiple debridement procedures and VSD negative‐pressure suction—such as incised wounds caused by compartment syndrome due to radial and ulnar shaft fractures or a floating knee—a sponge can be placed first, followed by attachment of the wire clasp on the exterior of the sponge (Figure 5) [26, 27]. The wound film can then be sealed, with the knob separately covered with film. Multiple applications in this study demonstrated a good sealing effect. Alternatively, the VSD system can be applied first, followed by placement of the wire clasp outside the film and connection to the knob, which remains exposed. To avoid friction between the wire and film, a thin layer of gauze can be applied to cover the film surface. In practice, because most patients required trimming of wound edges, we recommend applying slightly greater tension to the skin tissue to prevent insufficient approximation or excessive tension during suturing. For infected patients, infection control is mandatory prior to skin traction. The timing of the DSSC device application depends primarily on the status of infection control and the condition of the wound edges, rather than on the number of debridement procedures. In patients with poor wound edge condition, thorough debridement should be performed first, and skin stretching should only be initiated after the infection has been completely controlled and the wound edges have improved. Alternatively, the DSSC device may be placed away from the wound edges to perform stretching. For patients with good wound edge condition and well‐controlled infection, we recommend using the DSSC device immediately after debridement (Figure 4). However, excessive traction force should be avoided; only minimal mechanical stress should be applied to prevent further contraction of the wound edges [28]. This low level of mechanical tension promotes the regeneration of fibroblasts, collagen fibers, and blood vessels, thereby facilitating the formation of “Extra skin” [29, 30, 31], which is advantageous for rapid wound closure.

The DSSC device can also help generate additional skin through tissue expansion and is advantageous for use at the scalp and sole. Plantar skin is hard, wear‐resistant, and characterized by low sliding friction. Its treatment is controversial, as flap repair is often ineffective and tissue expanders do not yield satisfactory outcomes. In this study, one patient with a soft tissue defect on the sole was treated with the DSSC device and achieved favorable results. Long‐term follow‐up demonstrated satisfactory outcomes, providing valuable insights into the management of scalp defects, diabetic foot, and other conditions.

Limitations and Strengths

However, the DSSC device has some limitations. First, its effectiveness for wounds near joints or in cases with poor skin quality or insufficient elasticity, such as diabetic foot or extensive scarring, remains unclear and requires further investigation. Second, the traction effect may be limited in deep tissues. A second‐generation device is currently under development to achieve stronger fixation in deeper tissues through structural modifications. Third, no studies have yet addressed the optimal intensity or speed of traction. Therefore, further research is needed to determine parameters that minimize distension time while avoiding complications. The limitations of this study include a small sample size and the absence of a control group. Future investigations should include larger patient cohorts, incorporate control groups, and extend follow‐up duration to more comprehensively evaluate the clinical efficacy of this device.

Despite these limitations, the present study has several notable strengths. First, we developed a novel device for the repair of RSSTDs, which features a simple structure and easy operation, offering significant advantages over traditional treatment methods. Second, the prospective clinical study design provides strong evidence for the clinical efficacy of this device. Third, we explored different timing and application methods depending on the presence of skin infection, in order to better promote wound healing.

Indications

This DSSC device is indicated for skin and soft tissue defects that cannot be primarily closed due to various causes, including but not limited to: (1) open wounds and open fractures with skin defects; (2) skin defects secondary to osteomyelitis and wound infection; (3) skin and soft tissue necrosis caused by degloving injuries or skin graft necrosis; and (4) skin defects following fasciotomy for compartment syndrome.

Note: Use is not recommended in patients with periarticular wounds or extremely poor skin conditions (e.g., diabetic foot ulcers, pressure sores, extensive scarring), as its efficacy in these settings requires further investigation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the DSSC device effectively treats RSSTDs, offering short wound closure times, minimal complications, and a simple procedure.
