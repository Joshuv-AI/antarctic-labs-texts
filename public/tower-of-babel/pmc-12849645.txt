Rhizosphere fungal generalists drive plant metabolic adaptability via regulating soil micronutrient availability
Authors: Guozhuang Zhang, Jiaxiang Ma, Gao Xiong, Guangfei Wei, Zhiyi Luo, Lifang Gao, Liping Shi, Lianbin Huang, Ye Liu, Miyi Yang, Jiayu Zheng, Yuqing Zheng, Xiuye Wei, Yanlin Wang, Linlin Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe ecological characteristics of fungal generalists (FG) and specialists (FS) in rhizosphere, as well as their impact on soil micronutrient dynamics and plant metabolic adaptability are largely unknown. Through large-scale sampling of Panax notoginseng (with saponins as the primary specialized metabolite) and ecological analysis, we demonstrated that, compared to FS, the assembly of rhizosphere FG is more strongly governed by deterministic processes and that they play more crucial roles in maintaining network stability. Further, Mantel analysis and multiple machine learning models revealed that FG, rather than FS, are significant contributors to soil micronutrient availability, particularly for iron and zinc. This was substantiated by culture-based approaches, where we confirmed the zinc- and iron-solubilizing capabilities of candidate FG isolates both in vitro and in soil. A driver analysis of plant metabolic variation identified soil micronutrient availability as the predominant factor affecting plant metabolome and saponin accumulation, underscoring the significance of the FG-driven micronutrient availability in shaping plant metabolic adaptability. Among these micronutrients, available zinc exhibited the strongest positive effect on total saponin accumulation (R2 = 0.24, P < 0.001). A zinc-supplement pot experiment further validates the improving effects of zinc on root saponin accumulation, which was correlated to the upregulation of the PnCYP716A47 gene. Collectively, this study clarifies that deterministically assembled rhizosphere FG regulate plant adaptability by influencing soil micronutrient availability. These findings provide new insights for plant–microbe interactions in rhizosphere and are critical for the utilization of functional microbes to enhance plant performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40793-025-00841-z.Keywords: Niche breadth, Soil micronutrient, Saponin, Metabolic adaptability

Introduction

Within complex microbial communities, certain members may exhibit broad environmental adaptability, allowing them to occupy a wide range of niches; these are classified as generalists. Conversely, specialists possess specific environmental adaptations that restrict their existence to narrow habitats [5, 84]. By implicitly accounting for differences in the functional traits of microbial taxa, this conceptual framework is of great significance for understanding the role of microbial community in ecosystem functions and services. In recent years, many studies have explored the ecological characteristics of generalists and specialists—including community assembly mechanisms, co-occurrence patterns, and potential functions—across various habitats, such as farmland soil and marine sediments [39, 43, 66, 72]. However, in the rhizosphere environment, their ecological characteristics, as well as their influence on the soil environment and the resulting effects on plant adaptability, remain unclear. Given the vital role of the rhizosphere microbiome in plant growth and resistance, understanding these dynamics is crucial for the discovery of functional microorganisms capable of enhancing plant performance.

The broad niche occupancy of generalists implies significant environmental adaptability and tolerance, which can be attributed to their metabolic versatility and substrate utilization flexibility [16]. Consequently, the ecological assembly of generalists should be less influenced by environmental filtering—a primary deterministic process—whereas the opposite is expected for specialists [74]. This hypothesis has been supported by studies in free-living habitats such as agricultural soil [39, 84]. However, the rhizosphere is an environment profoundly shaped by the plant host. Since plants colonized land approximately 470 million years ago, they have established intimate interactions and co-evolved with a diverse array of microorganisms in the rhizosphere, a dynamic further modified in recent times by anthropogenic agricultural practices such as fertilization [20, 75]. Therefore, the contemporary rhizosphere represents a landscape shaped by the mutual selection between plants and microbes, as well as by human intervention. From this perspective, generalists may represent the outcome of selection by plants to broaden their own adaptability across environmental gradients. That is, these generalist species may fully occupy rhizosphere niches across different environmental conditions by broadly utilizing diverse plant-derived resources, such as mucilage and exudates [65]. In contrast, rhizosphere specialists are more likely to establish in specific local communities due to certain plant-independent factors like dispersal or random birth–death events [93]. However, our current understanding of the ecological assembly patterns of generalists and specialists within the rhizosphere remains incomplete.

Another significant research gap lies in the unclear impact of generalists versus specialists on the nutrient cycling in rhizosphere soil, and the subsequent implications for plant adaptability. In plant production systems under human intervention, the extensive application of macronutrient fertilizers such as N, P, and K has diminished the regulatory role of microorganisms in soil macronutrient cycling [24, 57]. However, the potential of these microbes to regulate micronutrients remains poorly understood. Micronutrients such as iron (Fe), copper (Cu), and zinc (Zn) exert a non-negligible influence on the life processes. Organisms require metals to perform a variety of essential biochemical functions carried out by metalloproteins, i.e., proteins containing metal ions [36]. However, only a small fraction of metals, specifically water-soluble/exchangeable and organically-complexed types, are organism-available in soil. The rest are fixed as carbonate-bound, oxide/mineral-bound, and residual forms [31]. The conversion of metals from fixed to bioavailable forms is collectively driven by soil conditions, climate, and activities of plant and microorganisms. For instance, microorganisms can solubilize and mobilize essential minerals via the secretion of organic acids and metallophores, consequently impacting the bioavailability of these nutrients [27, 58]. Given that the environmental versatility of generalists likely signifies diverse resource acquisition strategies, and that plants may select for them to enhance their own adaptability, we propose that generalists, not specialists, are important modulators of micronutrient availability in the rhizosphere.

As previously discussed, in the context of long-term co-evolution, plant fitness is expected as the ultimate beneficiary of rhizosphere biological activity [47, 55, 91]. Therefore, we anticipate that the potential influence of rhizosphere generalists on micronutrients has implications for plant fitness. As a key component of plant fitness, metabolic adaptation encompasses the production of a vast and diverse array of plant specialized metabolites (PSMs) [46, 82]. These PSMs, such as flavonoids and saponins, are not essential for plant growth but play crucial roles in plant responses to both biotic and abiotic stresses. For instance, saponins are important chemical weapon that plants deploy against pathogen invasion [25, 45, 54]. For example, protopanaxadiol-type saponins can inhibit the proliferation of Mycocentrospora acerina, a pathogen that causes Panax notoginseng round spot disease [45]. The potential influence of micronutrients on metabolic adaptability is also underpinned by a solid biochemical foundation. For example, cytochrome P450s (CYPs), which are widely involved in the formation of PSM scaffolds, depend on Fe for their activity [14, 35]. Meanwhile, Zn, due to its redox stability, serves as a structural and functional component of numerous transcription factors, further regulating the expression of PSM-related genes [18, 71].

To address the aforementioned gap, we investigated the rhizosphere mycobiome of the medicinal plant Panax notoginseng, which was sampled across a broad environmental gradient in the southwest of China. With a cultivation history of over 400 years, P. notoginseng is now produced almost exclusively through intensive agricultural practices in recent years [30]. P. notoginseng is characterized by dammarane triterpenoid saponins as its main PSMs and bioactive compounds [81]. This study explores the ecological characteristics (including community assembly, potential functions, and ecological interaction roles) of rhizosphere fungal generalists and specialists, their impact on the availability of rhizosphere micronutrients, and the implications of this impact for plant metabolic adaptability. We hypothesized that: (1) compared to specialists, the fungal generalist subcommunity is more strongly influenced by deterministic processes and plays a more significant role in the stability of rhizosphere ecological network; (2) fungal generalists, rather than specialists, have a more substantial effect on soil micronutrient availability; and (3) the micronutrient availability mediated by fungal generalists positively influences the metabolic adaptability of P. notoginseng, as represented by saponin metabolism.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and data acquisition

Sample collection. The sample collection has been detailed described in previous studies [79, 89]. Specifically, a large spatial sampling covering the main production area of P. notoginseng across Yunnan and Guangxi provinces was conducted in October 2017. Three 1.4 × 8.0 m2 plots were randomly selected at each of the 26 sampling sites (Fig. S1). From each plot, ten healthy plants and corresponding rhizosphere soils were harvested as one sample (replicate). At sampling, the plant was dug out using a sterilized spade, and soil loosely adhering to the roots was removed. Then, soil tightly attached to the roots (rhizosphere soil) was collected using sterile resealable bags. The plant roots (medicinal parts) were frozen at − 80 °C for metabolome profiling. The soil samples were sieved (2 mm), mixed well, with one part used for measuring soil physicochemical properties and another part frozen at − 80 °C for DNA extraction. All sampled plants were at the harvest stage, specifically during the root enlargement period in their third year of growth. During the cultivation of P. notoginseng, N, P, and K fertilizers were applied annually before flowering and in winter, respectively. The ratio of compound fertilizers varied depending on the growth year of the plant (Table S1).

Metabolome profiling. After grounded into powder in liquid nitrogen, root samples were extracted using pure methanol. In brief, 0.1 g of the root sample was accurately weighed, and 1 mL of methanol solution containing 0.1% formic acid was added. The mixture was vortexed for 10 s to ensure thorough mixing, followed by ultrasonication for 10 min. Subsequently, the sample was frozen at − 20 °C for 1 h and then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was filtered with 0.22 µm filter membrane and injected into the UPLC-QTOF-MS/MS system (Waters, USA) for metabolite profiling. Mobile phase consisted of water (A) and acetonitrile (B, containing 0.1% formic acid). A full gradient elution was applied (0.4 ml min−1): 0–2 min, 99–80% A; 2–3 min, 80–50% A; 3–7 min, 50–20% A; 7–7.5 min, 20–1% A; 7.5–9 min, 1% A; 9–9.1 min, 1–99% A; 9.1–10 min, 99%A. The temperature of C18 column was 35 °C. The raw data were analyzed using MassLynx (v4.1) and compounds were identified based on the NIST database (https://webbook.nist.gov/chemistry/, srd69). Among the 617 identified compounds, 466 compounds with defined Ontology were retained for downstream analysis (Fig. S1).

Saponin quantification. The methanol extract was quantitively analyzed for saponin contents using a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Agilent, USA) with water (A) and acetonitrile (B) as mobile phase. The elution gradient (1.0 ml min−1) was as follows: 0–12 min, 19% B; 12–60 min, 19–36% B. The temperature of C18 column was 25 °C and the detection wavelength was 203 nm. The contents of notoginsenoside R1 (NR1), ginsenoside Rg1, Re, Rb1, Rd, Rc, and Rb2 were determined based on calibration curves. In which, the Rb1, Rd, Rc, and Rb2 were protopanaxadiol (PPD) type saponins, and the NR1, Rg1, and Re were protopanaxatriol (PPT) type saponins.

Edaphic factors. The physicochemical properties of rhizosphere soil were measured according to previous studies [12, 69, 89]. In specific, soil pH and conductivity (SC) were measured using pH monitor and conductivity monitor, respectively. The content of organic matter (OM) was quantified by potassium dichromate-sulfate colorimetric method. Total nitrogen (TN), total phosphate (TP), and total potassium (TK) were determined by Kjeldahl method, molybdenum blue method, and flame photometry, respectively. Available nitrogen (AN), available phosphate (AP), and available potassium (AK) were measured using the alkali diffusion method, double acid method, and flame photometry, respectively. The available sulfur (AS) was determined using curcumin colorimetry. After ammonium acetate extraction, the exchangeable calcium (ECa) and exchangeable magnesium (EMg) were measured using atomic absorption spectrophotometry. For micronutrients, the available iron (AFe), zinc (AZn), copper (ACu), and manganese (AMn) were analyzed by atomic absorption spectrophotometry following DTPA extraction. The level of available boron was determined using curcumin colorimetry.

Climatic factors. Mean annual temperature (MAT) and mean annual precipitation (MAP) of all sampling sites were obtained from the Worldclim database (www.worldclim.org).

Amplicon sequencing. Genomic DNA was extracted from rhizosphere soil samples using the FastDNA SPIN Kit for soil (MoBio laboratories, Inc., USA). The marker genes of fungi were amplified using the ITS1F/ITS2R (ITS) primer pair [79]. During the DNA extraction and amplification, a no-template control (NTC) consisting of sterile ultrapure water was included to monitor for potential contamination. The resulting PCR products were detected on a 2% agarose gel. The absence of amplification in the NTC confirmed that the system was free of contamination. Subsequently, the target PCR products were purified using a gel extraction kit (Axygen Scientific, Inc., USA), quantified, and pooled in equimolar amounts. Sequencing libraries were then constructed according to the manufacturer’s protocol for the Illumina platform. The libraries were sequenced using the Illumina MiSeq PE250 platform (Shanghai Biozeron Co., Ltd., China). The raw data were quality control and denoised using the QIIME2-DADA2 workflow [8, 11]. The obtained amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were taxonomically annotated using sklearn-based classifiers trained based on UNITE v9.0 databases [48, 60]. ASVs with a total read count of less than 20 were removed to avoid the potential impact of sequencing error on classification [33]. Data analyses were performed on a randomly selected subset of 23,465 sequences (the minimum depth) for each sample to standardize the sequencing effort [51].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses in this study were performed in R (v4.3.1).

Classification of generalist and specialist taxa. The Levin’s niche breadth index was employed to determine the habitat specialization of microbial taxa [23, 43]. Species with high Levin’s index are typically more generalized, while those with low Levin’s index exhibit stronger specialization. To avoid the uncertainty arising from the arbitrary selection of Levin’s index thresholds, we employed permutations (n = 1000) to obtain the random expectation of Levins’ index for each fungal taxon [23]. Specifically, Fungal ASVs with significantly higher or lower Levin’s index than random expectation were classified as generalists or specialists, respectively (two sides, P < 0.05). The calculation of Levin’s niche breadth index and permutation procedure were performed using the EcolUtils and futher.apply packages [6, 64].

Analysis of ecological characteristics of generalist and specialist subcommunities. The primary lifestyle (or functional guild) of fungal taxa was predicted using the FungalTraits database [59]. Richness and Shannon index were calculated using the vegan package to assess the alpha diversity of generalist and specialist subcommunities [51]. To evaluate the influence of neutral processes on subcommunity assembly, we fitted the Sloan’s neutral model to the abundance-frequency pattern of generalist and specialist taxa [70]. Higher model R2 and neutral taxa ratio (NTR) indicate the stronger contribution of neutral processes to community assembly [32, 88]. The null model-based normalized stochasticity ratio (NST) was further calculated using the NST package to estimate the relative contribution of stochastic ecological processes (e.g., dispersal and drift) to subcommunity assembly [49, 93]. Molecular ecological network describing the potential interactions among taxa was constructed based on SparCC correlation. As a log-ratio transformation-based method, SparCC (Sparse Correlations for Compositional data) can estimate correlations in compositional data robustly [26]. Specifically, the SparCC correlations among fungal ASVs were calculated using FastSpar, and the P values were estimated based on 1000 permutations [78]. Based on previous studies, only SparCC correlations with FDR < 0.05 and an absolute value > 0.3 were retained to construct network [37, 73]. Network construction and topological analysis were conducted using the igraph package [21]. Modularity of ecological network was quantified using the fast-greedy algorithm. Four topological features were calculated for each node, including degree centrality (the number of edges linking to a node), betweenness centrality (the number of shortest paths passing through a node), closeness centrality (the number of steps required to access all other nodes from a given node), and eigenvector centrality (the extent to which a node is connected to other highly connected nodes). To assess the contributions of fungal generalists and specialists to ecological network stability, we performed node removal simulations under three scenarios: random removal of arbitrarily nodes, removal of only generalist nodes, and removal of only specialist nodes. Natural connectivity, which was calculated using the pulsar package was used as the metric for network stability [34]. For each removal quantity, the process was repeated 100 times, and the mean natural connectivity was used to represent network stability at that specific removal level. To confirm the robustness of our network analysis, we additionally constructed ecological network based on Spearman correlation and the random matrix theory (RMT), and subsequently performed the same topological analyses [87, 92].

Contributions of abiotic factors and fungal subcommunities to micronutrient availability. The variations in micronutrient availability were represented by the Euclidean distance of the levels of five bioavailable micronutrients (AFe, ACu, AZn, AMn, and AB; scaled transformation). Mantel, Partial Mantel, and multiple regression on distance matrices (MRM) analysis were performed using the ecodist package to estimate the potential contributions of different types of explanatory variables to micronutrient availability variations: Climate (MAT and MAP; Euclidean distance), soil basic property (pH, OM, and SC; Euclidean distance), and biotic factors (fungal generalists and specialists; Bray–Curtis dissimilarity) [28]. Multiple machine learning regression models and performance measures were employed from the mlr3 package suite to evaluate whether incorporating fungal generalists or specialists into abiotic factors would improve model performance [38]. In which, fungal generalists and specialists were represented by the PCoA axes with an explanatory power greater than 5% (the top five axes for both subcommunities). The machine learning regression models used included Cubist (Cubist regression), FNN (Fast Nearest Neighbour), GBM (Gradient Boosting), IBk (K-nearest neighbour), KSVM (Kernlab Support Vector Machine), RF (Random Forest), and SVM (Support vector machine). The R2 and maximum absolute error (MAE) were calculated to estimate the model performance. The Random Forest variable selection was conducted using the rfPermute package (number of permutations = 1000) to estimate the importance and significance of abiotic factors and fungal generalists to the variations in individual micronutrient availability [3]. The structural equation model (SEM) was fitted to the prior model to estimate the model robustness and path coefficients using the lavaan package [61]. Given the importance of PCoA1 and PCoA2 in representing the variations in fungal generalist and their contributions to individual micronutrient availability, these two axes were incorporated to the SEM. To identify fungal generalist taxa that may enhance the bioavailability of iron and zinc, we first filtered genera showing a positive correlation (Spearman ρ > 0) with AFe and AZn, and then evaluated their importance using Random Forest variable selection.

Effects of environmental factors on the metabolic variations of P. notoginseng root. The Euclidean distances of root metabolome, saponins in metabolome, flavones in metabolome, and root saponin content were used as dependent variable. Mantel and Partial Mantel analysis were conducted to estimate the contributions of climate, soil basic properties, soil macronutrients, and micronutrient availability to metabolic variations.

Isolation, identification, and functional study of rhizosphere fungi

Isolation and identification of culturable fungi. The culturable fungi in P. notoginseng rhizosphere were isolated using the strategy combining limiting dilution and multiple culture medium [90, 94]. Specifically, our earlier studies showed that the root inflation period in the second year of growth is when fungal diversity in P. notoginseng rhizosphere reaches its peak [80]. Therefore, we used rhizosphere soil at this developmental stage as the source for isolating strains. Additionally, rhizosphere soil samples from five different cultivation sites in Wenshan Prefecture were combined to maximize the size of species pool. The rhizosphere soil samples were subjected to a tenfold serial dilution using sterile PBS. The dilution step that allowed 100 µL of the diluted supernatant to yield 10–100 colonies on a 1/10 PDA plate (with 1× penicillin and streptomycin solution) was selected. The supernatant (100 μL per plate) from the selected dilution step (10−3) was uniformly spread onto four types of agar plates (1/10 PDA, 1/4 CMA, 1/4 RBA, and 1/4 MEA) containing penicillin (100 U/mL) and streptomycin (100 µg/mL). Colonies with different morphologies were picked and inoculated onto 1/10 PDA plates for purification through two generations. Subsequently, the strains were preserved separately as PDA slant cultures (stored at 4 °C) and in a 30% glycerol solution (stored at − 80 °C). For the identification of fungal strains, the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region was amplified using primer pairs ITS1/ITS4 [94]. Sanger sequencing was performed with the same primers for PCR by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). ITS sequences were searched against the NCBI database using BLAST.

Validation of iron and zinc mobilization capability of candidate fungal strains. Based on the genus-level taxonomic information of the isolates and the significant identity of their ITS sequences with the amplicon sequences of ASVs (Table S2), we selected four potential zinc- and iron-solubilizing fungi from the culturable fungal collection. PDA plates supplemented with 0.2% ZnO (w/v) were used to test the zinc solubilization ability of candidate fungal strains obtained by Random Forest variable selection and culturomics [67]. The formation of a transparent zone at the edge of the colony indicates that the fungus has zinc-dissolving capability. The chrome azurol S (CAS) plate assay was used to assess the siderophore production ability of fungal strains [10]. Fungal strains were further inoculated into culture bottle containing 60Co-treated sterile soil (500 g/bottle), which was collected from a P. notoginseng cultivation site and characterized by low available zinc and iron. The inoculation volume was 5 mL of spore suspension (1 × 107 spores/mL), while the control group (CK) was inoculated with an equal volume of sterile water. Incubation was carried out at 25 °C and 70% RH for 45 days. Every 5 days, the amount of water to be replenished was determined by weighing, and sterile water was added accordingly. The contents of AFe and AZn in the soil were determined before inoculation and after incubation, respectively, using the methods described previously.

Pot experiments for verifying the promoting ability of zinc on saponin accumulation in P. notoginseng roots

Topsoil (0–20 cm) with low available zinc levels (AZn ≈ 2 mg/kg) was collected in Wenshan Prefecture, Yunnan Province (Table S3). After uniformly mixed, soil was filled into pots, with each pot containing 12 kg of soil. Mature P. notoginseng seeds were sown in March, with 20 seeds per pot. After germination, spare seedlings were randomly removed from each pot to ensure a final count of 15 plants per pot. The experiment consisted of two treatments, each replicated across five pots. For the zinc treatment, pots were watered with solutions containing 0.5 g ZnSO4 at three time points: May, July, and September. The control pots (CK) were watered with the same volume of water. Plants were collected in early December to measure fresh weight and plant height. Following the homogenization of plant roots, a subsample was allocated for the determination of zinc and saponin levels, and another subsample was subjected to total RNA extraction for RNA-Seq. The soil remaining in the pots was thoroughly mixed and analyzed for total zinc and available zinc content.

The total RNA was extracted from roots using the TRIzol Plus RNA Purification Kit (Thermo, USA). The Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer was used to estimate RNA quality. The mRNA was enriched using using mRNA Capture Beads (Vazyme), followed by being reverse transcribed into cDNA. The cDNA library was constructed based on Illumina instruction. The raw reads underwent quality control and filtering using fastp to generate clean reads for subsequent analysis [15]. Differential expression genes (DEGs) between zinc and CK groups were identified using the DESeq2 package [44]. Genes with |log2 fold change|> 1 and FDR < 0.05 were identified as DEGs. RT-qPCR analysis of PnCYP716A47 gene was performed with a SLAN-96P Real-time PCR system (SLAN, China) using StarLighter SYBR Green qPCR mix (Forever star, Beijing). The PnACTIN1 gene was used as an internal reference to normalize gene expression [50]. The relative gene expression data of PnCYP716A47 gene was compared between two groups using the 2−ΔΔCt method [42].

The AlphaFold 3 online platform was used to assess the binding potential of saponin biosynthesis-related gene products with Zn2+ [2]. For CYP enzymes, the heme cofactor was also included to help verify the reliability of the model.

For the identification of transcription factor (TF) potentially activate the expression of PnCYP716A47 gene, the previously published RNA-Seq data for P. notoginseng roots from the authentic production area (Wenshan Prefecture) were used to construct a positive regulatory TF network [81]. Only TFs with Spearman correlation > 0.6 and FDR < 0.05 were retained.

Results

Ecological assembly mechanisms and network positions of rhizosphere fungal generalists and specialists

Based on Levin’s niche width, 538 and 1457 fungal ASVs were classified as generalists and specialists, respectively (Fig. 1A). Across all samples, the relative abundance of fungal generalists varied from 12.19 to 88.29%, and for specialists, it varied from 0.74 to 68.70% (Fig. 1A). At the class level, Sordariomycetes, Mortierellomycetes, and Eurotiomycetes contributed to over 80% of the abundance of generalists, whereas Tremellomycetes and Agaricomycetes were specifically abundant among specialists (Fig. 1B). In terms of lifestyle, plant pathogens constituted a higher proportion of the generalists, whereas various types of saprotrophs were more dominant among the specialists (Fig. 1C). Although the total number of taxa was higher for specialists, a comparison of the richness and Shannon indices revealed that generalists exhibited significantly higher biodiversity across samples (P < 0.001 for both indices) (Fig. 1D).

A trend in which taxon distribution increased with abundance implied the nonnegligible role of neutral processes in the assembly of both fungal generalists and specialists (Fig. 1E). However, the results of Sloan’s neutral model fitting indicated that the contribution of neutral processes to the assembly of specialists (R2 = 0.90) was far greater than that for generalists (R2 = 0.22). This finding was further corroborated by the neutral taxa ratio (NTR) (Generalist: 0.24; Specialist: 0.97) (Fig. 1E). The null model-based normalized stochasticity ratio (NST) also suggested that stochastic ecological processes played a more significant role in the assembly of specialists (NST = 0.99) than of generalists (NST = 0.77) (Fig. 1F).

The SparCC correlation-based ecological network displayed a structure with pronounced modularity (greedy optimization, 0.57) (Fig. 1G). Generalists occupied the central positions of the network and formed three major modules (M1, M2, and M3), whereas specialists were located at the periphery (Fig. 1G). Although no significant differences were observed in the closeness centrality between generalist and specialist nodes (P > 0.5) (Fig. 1H), generalist nodes possessed significantly higher degree, betweenness, and eigenvector centrality (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1G and H), indicating their crucial role as mediators within network. Random node removal analysis further confirmed the important role of generalist nodes in network stability (Fig. 1I). Compared to the random removal of arbitrarily nodes, the random removal of generalists led to a more pronounced loss of network stability, whereas the random removal of specialists unexpectedly enhanced network stability (Fig. 1I). The network constructed based on Spearman correlation and random matrix theory (RMT) also exhibited a consistent trend, confirming the robustness of the network analysis results (Fig. S2). In summary, these results confirm our first hypothesis: compared to specialists, the ecological assembly of the generalist subcommunity is more influenced by deterministic processes, and that generalists play a more important role in the rhizosphere ecological network.

Fungal generalists drive the variations in micronutrient availability in rhizosphere of P. notoginseng

To test the second hypothesis, we firstly employed Mantel analysis to assess the correlations of abiotic factors, as well as fungal generalists and specialists, with the overall availability of micronutrients (AFe, ACu, AZn, AMn, and AB) (Fig. 2A). Fungal generalists were significantly correlated with micronutrient availability (r = 0.26, P < 0.001), a relationship not observed for specialists (r = 0.03, P > 0.5) (Figs. 2A and S3). Critically, the correlation between generalists and micronutrient availability remained significant after controlling for all other factors (Partial Mantel r = 0.16, P < 0.05) (Fig. 2A). The multiple regression on distance matrices (MRM) model, which include the four significant variables from the Partial Mantel analysis (pH, OM, MAT, and generalists), further confirmed the unique and significant impact of fungal generalists on micronutrient availability (generalist coefficient = 0.19, P < 0.05) (Fig. 2B). Using micronutrient availability as the dependent variable, various machine learning models showed that adding generalists—but not specialists—to an abiotic-factor-only model led to an obvious increase in R2 and a decrease in MAE (Fig. 2C). Random Forest variable selection for individual micronutrient availability identified that the principal coordinates of fungal generalists exhibited significant importance (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2D).

Drawing on the analyses above and the theory of biotic-abiotic interactions in soil, we developed a priori model to describe the direct effects of fungal generalists on micronutrient availability (Fig. 2E). The model showed a good fit, demonstrating strong robustness (χ2 = 1.35, P = 0.51) (Fig. 2E). Path coefficient estimation confirmed that fungal generalists directly influenced the availability of all micronutrients, exerting the greatest absolute effects on AFe (0.82, P < 0.01) and AZn (0.64, P < 0.01) (Fig. 2E, Table S1). Overall, these results indicate that fungal generalists, not specialists, are the primary drivers of micronutrient availability in rhizosphere soil of P. notoginseng.

Specific fungal generalists can improve the bioavailability of soil iron and zinc

Given the strong and direct effects of fungal generalists on Fe and Zn availability, we used Random Forest variable selection to screen for generalist genera that are potential positive regulators of AFe and AZn (Fig. 3A). Among the fungal genera identified as significantly important in individual model, Linnemannia, Microdochium, and Paecilomyces were significant in both the AFe and AZn models (P < 0.05), and all showed positive correlations with both AFe and AZn (Fig. 3A).

To verify the Fe and Zn solubilizing capabilities of these fungi, we performed a large-scale cultivation and identification of P. notoginseng rhizosphere fungi (Fig. 3B). A total of 628 fungal isolates, representing 69 genera and 9 classes, were obtained. These isolated fungal genera accounted for 3.55–89.09% of the relative abundance (with a mean of 22.44%) in the rhizosphere fungal amplicon data, indicating that the taxa successfully cultured possess good abundance representation within in the total fungal community (Fig. S4A). Compared to the uncultured fungal taxa (at the genus level) in amplicon dataset, the fungal taxa exhibited representations in the culture collection displayed significantly higher abundance and occurrence frequency (P < 0.001) (Fig. S4B). Although specialist taxa were represented by a greater number of genera (n = 380) than generalist taxa (n = 213), among the 69 isolated fungal genera, nearly half were present among the generalists (n = 30); in contrast, only 19 genera were found among the specialists (Fig. S4C). Furthermore, 20.07% of the generalist ASVs were represented in the culture collection (at the genus level), whereas this proportion was only 5.15% for specialist ASVs (Fig. S4D). These results indicated that although the fungal culture collection we established possessed a certain degree of taxonomic diversity, it exhibits a bias towards generalist species.

Notably, four fungal strains from the culture collection belong to the three fungal genera previously identified as potentially having iron- and zinc-solubilizing capabilities, and exhibit significant ITS sequence identity with corresponding fungal ASVs (Figs. 3B and S5; Table S2). Zinc solubilization plate assays confirmed that these four strains could dissolve ZnO, resulting in clear zones around their colonies (Fig. 3C). FRS35S (Paecilomyces) displayed the widest clear zone, indicating its strong zinc-solubilizing capability (Fig. 3C). The chrome azurol S (CAS) plate assay confirmed the siderophore-producing capability of the four fungal strains. The variations in the color and morphology of halo zones implied that the three fungal genera produced different types of siderophores (Fig. 3C). Using P. notoginseng cultivation soil with low available iron and zinc content as the substrate, a 45-day fungi inoculation experiment demonstrated that fungi from all three genera significantly increased the soil AFe and AZn levels (P < 0.01) (Fig. 3D). Overall, these results confirmed that specific fungal generalists in the P. notoginseng rhizosphere could facilitate the transformation of iron and zinc in the soil into their bioavailable forms.

The micronutrient availability in rhizosphere soil serves as the major driver of root metabolome variations and total saponin accumulation

To investigate the impact of micronutrient availability, driven by rhizosphere fungal generalists, on plant metabolic adaptability, we first performed a metabolomic analysis of P. notoginseng root and quantified its saponin contents using HPLC. Based on co-abundance patterns, the metabolites in the root of P. notoginseng were divided into three clusters (Fig. S6A). Module M1 was predominantly composed of flavonoid glycosides, whereas modules M2 and M3 were mainly composed of triterpenoid saponins and triterpenoids (Fig. S6A). According to Mantel analysis, significant correlations were observed between soil basic properties as well as available micronutrients and the variation patterns of the root metabolome and key compounds (saponin and flavone) (Fig. 4A). Among these, the correlation with micronutrient availability was the highest (especially AFe and AZn) and persisted as significant when all other factors were considered (Partial Mantel, P < 0.05) (Figs. 4A and S6B). This metabolome-based pattern was also reflected in the content of total saponin, which is the major component of P. notoginseng metabolic adaptation (Fig. 4B). Among all micronutrients, AZn exhibited the strongest and positive link with root saponin content (R2 = 0.24, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4C). In addition, the significant and positive correlations between soil AZn and root Zn content (P < 0.001), as well as between root Zn content and saponin level (P < 0.001), were also observed, suggesting that the increase in rhizosphere available Zn promoted the absorption of Zn by plant roots, which further facilitated saponin accumulation (Fig. S7).

A Zn addition pot experiment was then conducted to explore the effect of soil AZn on root saponin biosynthesis and the underlying mechanisms (Fig. 5A). The addition of ZnSO4 significantly increased the AZn content (P < 0.001), but not the total zinc content (P > 0.5), in the potting soil, and it also significantly enhanced the zinc concentration in plant roots (P < 0.05) (Figs. S8 and S9). Furthermore, the Zn treatment had no significant effect on the fresh weight and plant height of P. notoginseng (P > 0.05) (Fig. S10). As expected, the zinc treatment elevated the total saponin content in P. notoginseng roots, primarily due to a significant increase in PPD-type ginsenosides (P < 0.05), suggesting that zinc enhances saponin biosynthesis (Fig. 5B). RNA-Seq analysis revealed that zinc treatment induced significant transcriptional changes in P. notoginseng roots, characterized by the upregulation of 1070 genes and the downregulation of 2275 genes (Figs. 5C and S11). Among the 16 experimentally confirmed genes involved in ginsenoside biosynthesis in P. notoginseng, zinc treatment significantly upregulated the expression of only three (Fig. 5C and D). Notably, one of these, PnCYP716A47, encodes an enzyme catalyzing the conversion of dammarenediol II to protopanaxadiol, aligning with the observed increase in PPD-type saponin accumulation. The transcript abundance of PnCYP716A47 also exhibited significant and positive correlations with the contents of total saponins and PPD-type saponins in roots (P < 0.01) (Fig. S12). The upregulation of PnCYP716A47 expression by zinc treatment was further validated by RT-qPCR (Figs. 5C and S13). Furthermore, to investigate if zinc ions affect saponin biosynthesis via pathways unrelated to transcriptional control—specifically, by modulating catalytic activity as an enzyme structural component—the AlphaFold3 was utilized to predict the binding affinity between the proteins encoded by these 16 genes and Zn2+ (Fig. S14). The simulations indicated that none of these enzymes exhibited significant binding capability for Zn2+, suggesting that Zn2+ primarily impacts saponin biosynthesis by enhancing the expression of key gene (PnCYP716A47) (Fig. S14). Utilizing previously published RNA-Seq data of P. notoginseng roots sourced from an authentic production area, we constructed a positive regulatory transcription factor (TF) network for the PnCYP716A47 (Spearman ρ > 0.6, FDR < 0.05) (Fig. S15). This network included several zinc-dependent TFs, such as C2H2, WRKY, and C2C2 family members possessing Zn2+ binding domains, offering potential leads for identifying direct targets of Zn2+ in future research (Fig. S15). In summary, these results confirm the significant impact of rhizosphere soil micronutrient availability on the metabolic adaptation of P. notoginseng, and validate the direct promotional effect of AZn on root saponin accumulation.

Discussion

Based on large-scale sampling, ecological analysis, and experimental validation, this study showed that fungal generalists in the rhizosphere of cultivated P. notoginseng exhibited a more deterministic assembly pattern and played more important roles in maintaining ecological network stability relative to specialists. Additionally, fungal generalists significantly drive the availability of rhizosphere micronutrients, particularly iron and zinc, which is of great importance for plant metabolic adaptability characterized by specialized metabolites. Overall, this study demonstrated that in human-impacted agricultural ecosystems, rhizosphere fungal generalists participate in the establishment of plant adaptability by influencing the form transformation of micronutrients (Fig. 6B). These findings are of great significance for the utilization of functional microbial resources in the rhizosphere and the realization of green agriculture.

Alongside the generalist-specialist paradigm, the core microbiome is another key concept in plant–microbe interactions. Though related, their focuses differ: the core microbiome is a frequency-based concept identifying taxa prevalent across most hosts (the “common core”), whereas the generalist-specialist framework is an ecological strategy paradigm centered on niche breadth and adaptability [85]. Because Panax notoginseng is an undomesticated species grown in heterogeneous soils (compared to crop plants), we argue that an ecological niche perspective is more appropriate for interpreting its rhizosphere fungal community. Ecological niche is defined as a set of environmental conditions occupied by a species [5]. Across rhizosphere soils under different environmental gradients, plant roots can be regarded as a consistently present hyper-dimensional factor that exerts a strong influence on the inhabiting microorganisms [19]. Carbon sources derived from the plant photosynthetic products and released in various forms into the rhizosphere collectively constitute this hyper-dimensional factor [86]. The fungal generalists defined in this study, which include many fungal groups reported to interact closely with plants, are likely to utilize these resources broadly, thereby displaying a more deterministic assembly. For example, fungal classes that are specifically enriched among fungal generalists, such as Sordariomycetes, Mortierellomycetes, and Eurotiomycetes, include many members reported as opportunistic plant pathogens, endophytes, or plant-growth promoting fungi [17, 53]. These include common pathogenic taxa of P. notoginseng, such as Fusarium sp. and Gibberella sp. This inference is also supported by functional prediction based on FungalTraits. However, this finding contrasts with those from the rhizobacterial community of poplar under salt stress, which indicated that bacterial generalists exhibited a more stochastic assembly [23]. Three factors may account for this discrepancy. First, salt stress acts as a strong negative filter, allowing salt-tolerant bacteria to become widely distributed and thus emerge as generalists. Second, salt stress may induce stress intensity-dependent changes in root exudates, enabling plants to recruit distinct microorganisms to enhance their tolerance [13]. Third, there are marked differences in resource utilization between bacteria and fungi: for instance, fungi tend to utilize macromolecules such as mucilage, whereas bacteria prefer readily available compounds like amino acids and phenolic acids [76]. Another key finding regarding ecological characteristics in this study is that fungal generalists tend to act as network connectors, playing a crucial role in maintaining network stability. This suggests that fungal generalists may act as nutrient mediators (e.g., by utilizing macromolecules and producing small-molecule metabolites available to other microorganisms) and/or regulators of the rhizosphere microenvironment, thereby mediating the coexistence and interactions of other taxa [4, 52].

Land plants allocate up to 40% of their photosynthetically fixed carbon into the rhizosphere, fostering niches for diverse microorganisms [56, 65]. This carbon expenditure is evolutionarily significant for sessile plants, which rely on microbes to expand their functional capacities and enhance adaptability [74]. The finding from this study—that rhizosphere fungal generalists can influence micronutrient availability and further enhance plant metabolic adaptability—provides a new example supporting this coevolutionary theory. From the perspective of agricultural application, the bioavailability of micronutrients such as Zn and Fe is strongly influenced by complex soil environment [83]. Consequently, the effectiveness of micronutrient fertilization in boosting plant uptake is highly dependent on soil conditions and may pose environmental pollution risks, making microbial reinforcement of soil micronutrient a promising and eco-friendly alternative. Microorganisms can mobilize metals such as Zn and Fe through the secretion of organic acids and/or producing metal chelators such as siderophore [58]. Among these, fungal secretion of organic acids to enhance metal solubilization has been well-documented [67]. However, reports on fungal production of metallophores in rhizosphere are relatively scarce, especially compared to bacteria [1, 29]. Fungi primarily produce hydroxamate metallophores, which chelate soil metals like zinc, rendering them available for plant uptake [22]. The fungi identified in this study can serve as bio-based soil micro-metal enhancers to improve plant metal utilization.

Moreover, the present study also confirmed the promoting effect of fungal-generalist-driven Zn availability on saponin accumulation in P. notoginseng roots. The influence of micronutrients on adaptability-related specialized metabolism has been well documented across diverse plant species. For instance, Cu2+ and Zn2+ can induce the accumulation of bacoside and lepidine in Bacopa monnieri and Lepidium sativum, respectively [63, 68]. Furthermore, iron is an essential component of all plant redox systems, including heme-containing enzymes [62]. These trace metals may also alter the carbon flux towards specialized metabolism by affecting plant photosynthesis and carbon fixation [36]. Zinc is absorbed by plants in the form of divalent cations (Zn2+) and plays diverse roles in plant physiological processes. Unlike metals such as iron and copper which possess redox-active pairs (Cu+/Cu2+, Fe2+/Fe3+), zinc possesses a fully filled d-shell (10 electrons), existing solely in the Zn2+ state without redox activity. This allows plants to utilize zinc safely, free from concerns about free radical reactions potentially damaging genetic material [7, 77]. Consequently, zinc proteins are extensively employed in organisms for interactions with nucleic acids, serving as regulatory proteins or enzymes [9]. A crucial function of zinc is maintaining the structure and function of transcription factors. For instance, in Arabidopsis thaliana, zinc binds to multiple transcription factors, including FIXATION UNDER NITRATE (FUN), bZIP19, and bZIP23, thereby altering their conformation and transcriptional regulatory activity [40, 41]. Furthermore, zinc’s small ionic radius, high charge density, and strong Lewis acidity (high electron affinity) enable it to readily shift between coordination geometries due to low energy barriers. This flexibility allows Zn to effectively polarize ligands, functioning as an essential cofactor in numerous enzymes like alcohol dehydrogenase and carbonic anhydrase [71]. This study revealed that zinc upregulates the expression of PnCYP716A47 but does not directly bind to enzymes within the saponin biosynthesis pathway. This suggests that zinc enhances saponin accumulation by modulating gene expression through transcription factors. The transcriptional regulatory network constructed for PnCYP716A47 in this study may serve as a valuable resource for identifying zinc-dependent transcription factors influencing saponin-driven plant adaptability.

In summary, this study reveals the ecological characteristics of rhizosphere fungal generalists and specialists in human-managed plant production systems. It highlights the role of rhizosphere fungal generalists in regulating plant metabolic adaptability by influencing soil micronutrient availability, and validates a specific ‘fungi → zinc → aponin’ causal chain. This research provides new insights into plant–microbe interactions in the rhizosphere and holds significant implications for advancing the exploration and utilization of functional microorganisms. However, it should be noted that the validation of this multi-level cascade in our study is largely indirect and qualitative. Future, more rigorous soil-based fungi inoculation experiments are still needed to quantitatively investigate the contribution of fungi-induced zinc mobilization to saponin accumulation of P. notoginseng under physiological conditions.
