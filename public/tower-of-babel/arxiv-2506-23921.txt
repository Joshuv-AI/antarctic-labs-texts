The Trilemma of Truth in Large Language Models
Germans Savcisens, Tina Eliassi-Rad

Abstract
We often attribute human characteristics to large language models (LLMs) and claim that they “know” certain things. LLMs have an internal probabilistic knowledge that represents information retained during training. How can we assess the veracity of this knowledge? We examine two common methods for probing the veracity of LLMs and discover several assumptions that are flawed. To address these flawed assumptions, we introduce sAwMIL (short for Sparse Aware Multiple-Instance Learning), a probing method that utilizes the internal activations of LLMs to separate statements into true , false , and neither . sAwMIL is based on multiple-instance learning and conformal prediction. We evaluate sAwMIL on 5 validity criteria across 16 open-source LLMs, including both default and chat-based variants, as well as on 3 new datasets. Among the insights we provide are: (1) the veracity signal is often concentrated in the third quarter of an LLM’s depth; (2) truth and falsehood signals are not always symmetric; (3) linear probes perform better on chat models than on default models; (4) nonlinear probes may be required to capture veracity signals for some LLMs with reinforcement learning from human feedback or knowledge distillation; and (5) LLMs capture a third type of signal that is distinct from true and false and is neither true nor false. These findings provide a reliable method for verifying what LLMs “know” and how certain they are of their probabilistic internal knowledge.

An LLM,
\mathcal{M}
, has

\mathrm{P}(\neg\phi\mid\mathrm{K}_{\mathcal{M}})\geq\zeta\text{, where }\zeta\in(0.5,\,1]\text{ is a threshold.}

\mathrm{K}_{\mathcal{M}}

\Big[\mathrm{P}(\phi\mid\mathrm{K}_{\mathcal{M}})<\zeta\Big]\text{ and }\Big[\mathrm{P}(\neg\phi\mid\mathrm{K}_{\mathcal{M}})<\zeta\Big]\text{, where }\zeta\in(0.5,\,1]\text{ is a threshold.}

If

should encode the signal associated with the veracity in its intermediate activations.

1.

2.1.1
Flawed Assumption I: Truth and falsehood are bidirectional.

To determine the veracity of a statement
\phi
, an LLM

\mathcal{M}
must develop a mechanism (a.k.a. a probe) to detect

\phi
’s truth or falsehood.
3
3

3

In this example, we assume that the veracity label is
Z\in\{\text{true, false}\}
.

This mechanism must rely on
\mathcal{M}
’s neural activations to find support for
\phi
by using
\mathcal{M}
’s internal probabilistic knowledge
\mathrm{K}_{\mathcal{M}}
.

Existing veracity probes
[
16
,
28
,
21
,
20
]
implicitly assume that truth and falsehood are encoded bidirectionally. That is,

P\left(\phi\mid\mathrm{K}_{\mathcal{M}}\right)=1-P\left(\neg\phi\mid\mathrm{K}_{\mathcal{M}}\right)

(4)

This formulation implies (1) a closed-world assumption, where any statement
\phi
not confirmed as true is considered false, and (2) each decoder symmetrically encodes a signal corresponding to falsehood and truthfulness.

We are not the first to highlight that veracity is embedded via multiple directions. For example,
Bürger et al. 2024
identify a two-dimensional subspace separating true and false statements, while
Marks and Tegmark 2024
show that affirmative and negated statements are encoded along distinct directions.

Valid Assumption I
(Truthfulness and falsehood have distinct directions.)

The representation of truth and falsehood requires more than one direction. This is,

P\left(\phi\mid\mathrm{K}_{\mathcal{M}}\right)\not=1-P\left(\neg\phi\mid\mathrm{K}_{\mathcal{M}}\right).

2.1.2
Flawed Assumption II: LLMs capture and retain everything we know.

To train a probe
g_{i}
, we use
\mathcal{D}_{train}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
.
This data set consists of pairs of factual statements and ground-truth labels
\langle\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i}\rangle
, where (usually)
y\in\big\{\text{true, false}\big\}
.
The labels
y
that
we
assign to the statements in
\mathcal{D}
are based on
our
knowledge (i.e., what we know to be true).
Thus, the veracity labels in
\mathcal{D}
are distributed according to
G_{\mathcal{D}}
.

Our goal, however, is to train a veracity probe
g_{i}
that classifies
what the
LLM
deems to be true, false, or ‘neither’
.
So, the probe
g_{i}
should map the statements to the space of the LLM’s internal probabilistic knowledge
\mathrm{K}_{\mathcal{M}}
. However,
\mathcal{M}
may follow a different distribution
G_{\mathcal{M}}
for the veracity labels. That is,
G_{\mathcal{M}}
may not be equivalent to
G_{\mathcal{D}}
.
For example, we know that “The city of Bissau is in Congo” has a ground-truth label
y=\text{false}
, because we can check maps or official sources. On the other hand, we do not know how
\mathcal{M}
labels it.

Even though models such as
LLaMa-2-Chat
,
LLama-2-13B
, or
OPT-6.7b
are open-source, the precise composition of their training data remains mostly unknown
[
29
,
30
]
.
Even with access to the data, we do not have straightforward methods to verify what has made it into the internal probabilistic knowledge
\mathrm{K}_{\mathcal{M}}
.
Thus, the ground-truth label distribution
G_{\mathcal{D}}
is not necessarily equivalent to the in-model label distribution
G_{\mathcal{M}}
.
Probes like mean-difference classifier
[
16
]
, contrast-consistent search
[
21
,
28
]
, or iterative null space projection
[
22
]
cannot account for the mismatch between the label distributions.
Instead, these probes introduce a systemic bias, where

captures a signal that reflects
our

Valid Assumption II
(LLMs do
not
capture and retain everything we know.)

The distribution of ground-truth labels
G_{\mathcal{D}}
may not be equivalent to the model’s label distribution
G_{\mathcal{M}}
.

2.1.3
Flawed Assumption III: All veracity probes provide calibrated probabilities.

Veracity probes are generally designed to predict discrete labels. That is, they are classification tasks where the probe assigns one of two labels to a given statement:
g_{i}:h_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})\to\big\{\text{true, false}\big\}.

However, as
Herrmann and Levinstein 2025
point out, veracity probes should provide not only discrete labels, but also values that can be interpreted as degrees of belief (or some other alternative that quantifies confidence).
Thus, we need probes that output: “I am
p
% confident that the statement is true”. In this way, a probe can enable an LLM to
abstain
from assigning a veracity label to a statement if its confidence is low.
Although we can ask an LLM to provide some certainty estimate (as a confidence measure) in a zero-shot probe
[
32
]
, representation-based probes with discrete labeling (such as support vector machines) do not provide calibrated probabilities.

Valid Assumption III
(
The probabilities generated by veracity probes are not inherently calibrated.)

The output of veracity probes
g_{i}
may not be calibrated and require additional post-processing to be interpreted as meaningful estimates of confidence.

2.2
Flawed Assumption IV: Every statement is either true or false.

There are cases where the LLM lacks definitive evidence to determine if a statement is true or false. Suppose we have a veracity probe
g_{i}
, which returns a probability of
0.5
for a given statement
\phi
to be true. The question is how we should interpret this probability of
0.5
. There are multiple interpretations:

1.

Aleatoric Uncertainty
:
\mathcal{M}
lacks sufficient knowledge about
\phi
.

2.

Epistemic Uncertainty
: The probe
g_{i}
is under-trained.

3.

Lack of veracity property
: The statement
\phi
does not have a veracity label. For example, “Please, pass a salt!” is neither true nor false.

4.

Veracity has not actualized
: The statement “Boston is in” does not have any veracity value since this fragment does not yet constitute an actualized statement.

Probes like the mean-difference
[
16
]
cannot account for these scenarios.
In Supplementary Sec.
D
, we show an example where the probe assigns high scores to
pre-actualized
and
neither
statements.
For instance, “Yabury is located in the Southern Zbemaku” involves a city and a country that do not exist in the real world and thus should not be classified as either true or false.
We expect that the veracity probe
g_{i}
provides a score associated with low confidence. However, this is not the case, as shown in the examples in Supplementary Fig.
.
At best, the mean-difference probe captures a proxy correlated with veracity that is confounded by other attributes.
So, we need to eliminate irrelevant proxies and narrow down the source of the uncertainty.
To address the issue, we train probes that can account for
neither
cases, so that
g_{i}
can reflect the insufficient evidence in
\mathrm{K}_{\mathcal{M}}
.
Studies with human participants have shown that including options such as “other” or “I do not know” can help with data quality
[
33
]
and uncertainty among the participants
[
34
]
.

Valid Assumption IV
(Some statements are neither true nor false)

A probe
g_{i}
should distinguish between the cases where the model
\mathcal{M}
lacks sufficient support to assess the truthfulness of the statement
\phi
, and the cases where
\phi
lacks a veracity value.

2.3
Flawed Assumption V: We know where the signal for veracity is stored.

The majority of veracity probes are trained on the representation of the last token
[
22
,
16
]
.
For example, if the statement is “Boston is in the US.”, they assume the period alone carries all of the veracity signal.
Such methods assume that any factual signal appearing
n
tokens before the end of the statement will be faithfully preserved until the last token.
A more reasonable approach is to probe at the exact position where the statement is actualized—e.g., immediately after “in the” in the above example—rather than relying on the LLM to
move
that signal all the way to the end of the statement.

Valid Assumption V
(Position of the veracity token is not known a priori)

Probes should include a flexible mechanism for identifying the optimal token positions from which to extract veracity signals, instead of relying on fixed positions such as the final token in the statement.

A probe that directly addresses these flawed assumptions would better reflect the internal knowledge of the LLM and provide a clearer understanding of

1.

the factual information encoded in
\mathcal{M}
,

2.

how
\mathcal{M}
classifies statements as
true
,
false
, or
neither
, and

3.

calibrated measures of
\mathcal{M}
’s confidence in its own probabilistic knowledge.

Researchers have proposed criteria to measure the validity of veracity probes
[
31
,
32
,
22
]
.
We aggregate these into five major categories and provide an overview in Tab.
2
.
We propose to evaluate a probe
g_{i}
along the following criteria:

(i)

Correlation
.
The probe, trained to predict a veracity property
\{\textit{true},\textit{ false}\}\in Z
, should achieve high predictive accuracy on unseen samples that possess this property, i.e., on samples from
\mathcal{D}_{test}
.
When the criterion is satisfied, the

i
-th decoder embeds the information about
Z
to some degree. We cannot rule out the fact that it captures proxies associated with
Z
.

(ii)

Generalization
extends
Correlation
by requiring that the probe generalizes beyond the data set it was trained on.
The probe should have high predictive accuracy on samples that have veracity
Z
, but have different phrasing or come from different domains.
For example, if a probe is trained to identify neural activation patterns associated with veracity on statements related to ecology, this probe should have similar predictive accuracy on statements related to biology.

(iii)

Selectivity

The probe
g_{i}
should avoid classifying statements that are not (or cannot be)
true
or
false
.
Hence, the probe
g_{i}
should abstain from making predictions on the
neither
-valued statements—i.e., statements that the LLM could not have learned from its training data or that inherently lack any truthfulness or falsehood.
Poor selectivity indicates that the probe might capture spurious correlations with unrelated properties.

(iv)

Manipulation
.
We should be able to use the identified direction
\vec{\nu}_{i}
to update
h_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})
and have a predictable change in the distribution of the output tokens
P_{\mathcal{M}}(\tau\mid\boldsymbol{x})
.
Since we focus on
linear
probes, we expect that moving
+c
units along
\vec{\nu}_{i}
should have an opposite effect on
P_{\mathcal{M}}
compared to moving
-c
units.

(v)

Locality

When asking a question such as “Is X true? Answer yes or no,” the manipulation should primarily influence the generation process related to the “yes” or “no” responses. It should minimally affect unrelated tokens.
For example, if a manipulation does not increase the likelihood of the LLM generating “no”, but increases the likelihood of generating tokens such as “elephant”, then the manipulation degrades the LLM’s abilities.

Evaluating a probe according to these criteria allows us to determine how well
g_{i}
captures the signals associated with veracity
Z
and how manipulations (a.k.a. internventions) affect LLM’s output
P_{\mathcal{M}}
. Part of our future work includes adding a new criterion on whether the probe can assess if an LLM can “reason” logically. For example, if
\mathcal{M}
classifies a statement
\phi_{1}
as true and another statement
\phi_{2}
as true, then will
\mathcal{M}
also classify
\phi_{1}\wedge\phi_{2}
as true?

3.1
Sparse Aware Multiple-Instance Learning (sAwMIL)

Algorithms such as logistic regression, support vector machines, and mean-difference classifiers belong to the single-instance learning (SIL) family, where each instance in the data set is individually labeled. In contrast, multiple-instance learning (MIL) is a type of weakly supervised learning that operates on a set of labeled
bags

[
38
]
.
A bag,
\boldsymbol{B}
, is a set of related instances (e.g., patches extracted from the same image or embeddings of individual words in a sentence).
Each bag has an associated binary label,
4
4

4

For simplicity, we assume a binary label.
but the labels for individual instances within the bag remain unknown.
A positive label (
y=1
) indicates that at least one instance in the bag
\boldsymbol{B}
belongs to the positive class.
Thus, an MIL algorithm must identify the most influential instances contributing to the bag’s label.
These algorithms must consider the overall structure of the bag and simultaneously suppress irrelevant instances.

Certain SIL algorithms have MIL counterparts.
For example,
Bunescu and Mooney 2007
introduced sparse balanced MIL (
sbMIL
), an adaptation of linear support vector machines (SVM) .
It is designed for cases where bags are sparse (i.e., only a few instances within a bag are important).

sbMIL
has two training stages. In the first stage, it uses the MIL-modified SVM
[
26
]
, referred to as sparse MIL (
sMIL
, see Fig. 4 in
[
26
]
).
In this stage, the objective is to identify the most important instances within positive bags, pushing all other instances and negative bags toward the opposite side of the separating hyperplane.
Once the initial model is trained, it computes the distribution of scores assigned to each instance in all positive bags.
Then, it computes the
\eta
-quantile.
\eta
is the
balancing hyperparameter
.
Instances scoring above the
\eta
threshold are marked as positive. In the second stage, it switches to the single-instance SVM.
It works with individual samples, disregarding their original grouping into bags, and assigns them the labels determined during the first stage.
We provide the pseudocode for
sbMIL
in the Alg.
1
(Steps 1-5).

We know which tokens come from the actualized part of the statement (e.g., “Latvia”) and which ones come from the non-actualized part of the statement (e.g., “The city of Riga is in”).

Our method,

3.1.3
Benefits of One-vs-all vs. Multiclass
sAwMIL

The benefits of one-vs-all
sAwMIL
are:

1.

It does not assume that truth and falsehood are based on the same mechanism. Thus, it does not assume that “truth and falsehood are bidirectional”.

2.

It excludes training samples with low support. Thus, it does not assume that “LLMs capture and retain everything we know”.

3.

It can choose which tokens have the
largest
veracity signal and is not constrained to making a prediction on the last token. Thus, it does not assume that “we know where the signal for veracity is stored”.

4.

It trains on examples of tokens without any veracity values. Thus, it minimizes the chance of capturing spurious or noisy correlations. That is, it does not assume that “every statement is either true or false”.

The multiclass
sAwMIL
further combines these one-vs-all probes to produce probabilities for
is-true
,
is-false
, and
is-neither
, which sum up to 1. However, the multiclass
sAwMIL
probabilities are not calibrated. That is, they may not accurately reflect the true likelihood that a statement
\boldsymbol{x}
is
true
,
false
, or
neither
.

3.2
Conformal Predictions

Raw outputs from many models, such as Support Vector Machines (SVMs)—specifically, the distance to hyperplane score—are not meaningful as confidence measures.
For example, an SVM might assign a score of
5.2
to “Paris is in France” and
0.1
to “Paris is in Belarus,” yet those numbers alone tell us nothing about how often predictions with these scores are correct.
Simply wrapping them in a sigmoid function to force them into
[0,1]
does
not
create calibrated probabilities.
The same issue applies to one-vs-all
sAwMIL
scores, and even the softmax outputs of the multiclass
sAwMIL
probe.
They can underestimate their true confidence unless they are explicitly calibrated.

Conformal learning is a framework
[
17
,
18
]
that enables us to transform raw scores into prediction sets with
guaranteed coverage
.
Hence, it provides a method to account for uncertainty.
Conformal prediction methods identify intervals within which the probes’ predictions are correct with a probability of
1-\alpha
.

In our work, we focus on “split conformal learning”
[
18
]
, which requires a hold-out (or calibration) data set to compute conformal prediction intervals.
Given a probe
g_{i}
, we use a calibration data set
\mathcal{D}_{calib}
of activation-label pairs
\langle h_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}),y\rangle
to find prediction regions that ensure, for example, that a sample falling within the region is correctly classified
90
% of the time.
If a prediction falls into the overlapping conformal prediction intervals of two (or more) classes, or if it does not fall within any interval, the probe abstains from making any prediction.

Nonconformity score
.
To identify the conformal intervals, we compute a nonconformity score for each sample in
\mathcal{D}_{calib}
.
For the binary cases (such as mean-difference probe and one-vs-all
sAwMIL
), we use the binary nonconformity scoring; see Supplementary Alg.
3
.
It is based on the distance between the prediction and the classifier’s separating hyperplane.
In Eq.
6
,
s
is the signed distance of the sample to the separation hyperplane, and
y
is a ground-truth (or candidate) label:
5
5

5

In this case, labels should be either
-1
or
1
. Thus, all samples with label
0
are assigned label
-1
.

\textit{binaryNC}\,(s,y)=\exp\bigl(-y\,\cdot\,s\bigr),\>y\in\{-1,1\}\text{ and }s\in\mathbb{R}.

(6)

If the sample ends up on the wrong side of the separation hyperplane (e.g.,
s>0
and
y=-1
), then the nonconformity score in Eq.
6
is high and the candidate label is weakly supported by the model.

For the multiclass
sAwMIL
, we use the multiclass nonconformity score
[
39
]
; see Supplementary Alg.
4
.
For a given candidate label
y
, the label is defined in terms of the difference between the predicted probability of the true class and the highest probability among the other classes (with
K
denoting the total number of classes). Formally, for a candidate label
y
with predicted probability
p_{y}
, we calculate the multiclass nonconformity score with the following function:

\textit{multiclassNC}\left(\boldsymbol{p}\right)=\frac{1-(p_{y}-\max_{i\not=y}p_{i})}{2}

(7)

where
\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta^{K-1}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}\mid p_{i}\geq 0,\;\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{i}=1\right\}
and
\Delta^{K-1}
is a simplex.

In both cases, lower scores in Eq.
6
and Eq.
7
indicate that the candidate label
y
is strongly supported by the model.
In our work, we set
\alpha=0.1
.
Thus, if the nonconformity score of a new sample exceeds the
90
th quantile, the probe abstains from prediction (see Supplementary Alg.
4
).
The addition of conformal intervals enables us to distinguish between cases where the statements originate from different distributions, as compared to those in the calibration data set.

\boldsymbol{x}
as input.
These instructions describe the task and explain how the LLM should reply, see Fig.
2
for examples.
The Supplementary Sec.
provides a detailed description and additional results for the zero-shot experiments.

Given the instruction and the statement
\boldsymbol{x}
, a model outputs token-level probabilities over the vocabulary
\mathcal{V}
as

P_{\mathcal{M}}(\tau\mid\text{instruction}\wedge\boldsymbol{x})\text{ with }\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{V}}P_{\mathcal{M}}(\tau\mid\text{instruction}\wedge\boldsymbol{x})=1.

We map these token-level probabilities into the veracity-label probabilities
G_{\mathcal{M}}
as

\displaystyle G_{\mathcal{M}}(\text{true}\mid\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=P_{\mathcal{M}}(\texttt{[1]}\mid\text{instruction}\wedge\boldsymbol{x})

(9)

\displaystyle G_{\mathcal{M}}(\text{false}\mid\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=P_{\mathcal{M}}(\texttt{[2]}\mid\text{instruction}\wedge\boldsymbol{x})

(10)

\displaystyle G_{\mathcal{M}}(\text{neither}\mid\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=P_{\mathcal{M}}(\texttt{[3]}\mid\text{instruction}\wedge\boldsymbol{x})+P_{\mathcal{M}}(\texttt{[4]}\mid\text{instruction}\wedge\boldsymbol{x})

(11)

We include options
[5]
and
[6]
as a sanity-check on the model
\mathcal{M}
.
We assume that the model does not understand the instruction if it chooses these options.
That is we assume that the model abstains if the token-level probability mass for all other tokens in the vocabulary exceeds that of tokens
[1]
,
[2]
,
[3]
, and
[4]
; see Eq.
12
.

G_{\mathcal{M}}(\text{abstain}\mid\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\tau}P_{\mathcal{M}}(\tau\mid\text{instruction}\wedge\boldsymbol{x})\text{, where }\left\{\tau\in\mathcal{V}:\tau\notin\left\{\texttt{[1]},\texttt{[2]},\texttt{[3]},\texttt{[4]}\right\}\right\}

(12)

Note that the zero-shot prompting relies only on the token-level probabilities—i.e.,
\mathcal{M}
’s output.
It does not look at the intermediate hidden representations of
\boldsymbol{x}
.

4.3.2
Mean-difference Probe

The mean-difference probe is a binary classifier with a linear decision boundary
[
16
]
.
We use it to separate true and false statements based on the internal activations
h_{i}
.
For each pair
\langle\boldsymbol{x}_{j},y_{j}\rangle
, we extract the activation of the last token
h_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})_{[L]}
and assemble a set of factually true
\mathcal{X}^{+}=\{h_{i}(\boldsymbol{x_{j}})_{[L]}:y_{j}=\text{true}\}
and a set of false
\mathcal{X}^{-}=\{h_{i}(\boldsymbol{x_{j}})_{[L]}:y_{j}=\text{false}\}
activations. Here,
L
is the index of the last token in
\boldsymbol{x}
.
We then compute the means of each set, denoted
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{+}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{-}
, and compute a direction vector:

\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{+}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{-})\;\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\;(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{+}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{-})^{T}

(13)

In Eq.
13
,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is a pooled covariance matrix. See Alg.
2
in Supplementary Materials for the detailed description.

4.3.3
Conformal Prediction Intervals

We train the mean-difference probe following the methodology outlined in recent literature
[
16
]
.
Since the mean-difference (
MD
) probe is not explicitly trained to account for
neither
cases, we augment it with conformal prediction intervals.
Conformal intervals guarantee to contain a correct prediction
1-\alpha
times.
In our experiments, we set
\alpha=0.1
, which is a widely used value for conformal learning.
If the predicted score falls outside of these intervals, we assume that the sample
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
is too unusual. That is, it is an example of a neither
true
nor
false
statement.
If the mean-difference probe accurately captures the veracity signal, the
neither
statements should fall outside the conformal prediction intervals.
However, if the
MD
probe captures proxies or spurious correlations, the
neither
-type statements might end up within the conformal intervals.

We refer to the mean-difference probe with the conformal prediction intervals as the
MD+CP
probe. To compute the nonconformity scores, we use the function in Eq.
6
.

We do not use conformal prediction intervals in zero-shot prompting.

Kadavath et al. 2022
shows that LLMs provide well-calibrated probabilities when replying to multiple-choice questions.
That said, the conformal predictions can be integrated into zero-shot prompting to abstain from labeling low-support statements.

4.4
Performance and Validity

Here, we describe a pipeline to evaluate our
sAwMIL
probe over the validity criteria specified in Section
2.4
.

Correlation and Selectivity
.
We use the test split of each data set to evaluate the performance of the probe.
We use Matthew’s Correlation Coefficient (MCC) to summarize the statistical accuracy of probes.
The multiclass MCC value is calculated using Eq.
14
.

\operatorname{MCC}=\frac{c\times s-\sum_{k}^{K}p_{k}\times t_{k}}{\sqrt{\left(s^{2}-\sum_{k}^{K}p_{k}^{2}\right)\times\left(s^{2}-\sum_{k}^{K}t_{k}^{2}\right)}},

(14)

where
c
is the number of correct predictions,
s
is the total number of samples,
K
is the total number of classes,
t_{k}
is the number of
k
-class samples in the data set, and
p_{k}
is the number of times
k
-class was predicted.

\operatorname{MCC}=1
indicates that a classifier predicted every instance correctly.
\operatorname{MCC}=0
implies that the predictions are random.

\operatorname{MCC}=-1
indicates that the predictions are inversely correlated with the ground-truth labels.

Zero-shot prompting, one-vs-all
sAwMIL
, and multiclass
sAwMIL
can abstain from making predictions. If a probe abstains too often, it suggests poor performance.
For these cases, we use Weighted-MCC (W-MCC), where the
acceptance rate
serves as the weight (see Eq.
15
).

\operatorname{W-MCC}=\operatorname{MCC}\times\Big(1-\frac{\text{\# abstained}}{\text{\# total predictions}}\Big)

(15)

In contrast to the other probing methods, the
MD+CP
probe cannot abstain. When a statement
\boldsymbol{x}
is too unusual, the probe labels it as
neither
.
For this case, we use the MCC score.

Since
neither
statements are included in the test data set, W-MCC and MCC provide a sense of how well the probe classifies factually
true
or
false
statements and indicate whether the probes can handle
neither
-type cases.

Generalizability
.
To test how well a particular probe
g_{i}
trained on data set
\mathcal{D}_{i}
generalizes, we evaluate its performance using the test split of other data set
\mathcal{D}_{j}
—e.g.,
g_{i}
trained on the city locations data set is evaluated using the test split of the word definitions data set.

4.5
Interventions and Validity

In this experiment, we assess whether perturbing the hidden representation
h_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})
along the veracity direction
\vec{\nu}_{i}
affects the model’s outputs, and whether these interventions satisfy the manipulation and locality criteria defined in Sec.
2.4
.
In other words, we use
\vec{\nu}_{i}
to change the distribution of the output tokens
P_{\mathcal{M}}
and force true or false responses.

The modified representation is then passed to decoder

Further, we compute the conditional probability of the
correct

.
For each statement, we obtain three values: the original conditional probability, the probability after a positive shift
+\vec{\nu}_{i}
, and the probability after a negative shift
-\vec{\nu}_{i}
.
(

\Delta I^{+}_{i}
) and the negative shift (
\Delta I^{-}_{i}
) are of opposite sign, we consider the intervention to have a consistent directional effect and mark it as successful for that statement (see Eq.
20
).
(

) To evaluate the success of the intervention, we also identify the

—i.e., the sign of
\Delta I_{i}^{+}
that appears most frequently across all statements.
The procedures in panels B and C determine the per-statement success (see Eq.
20
).
If more than half of the statements show consistent and directionally aligned changes (see Eq.

),
we consider the overall intervention along
\vec{\nu}_{i}
to be successful.

We look at each factually true statement
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{D}_{test}
—e.g., “The city of Santo Domingo is in the Dominican Republic.”
We split these statements into two segments: a pre-actualized part,
\boldsymbol{x}^{p}
, such as “The city of Santo Domingo is in”;
and second, an actualized part,
\boldsymbol{x}^{a}
such as “the Dominican Republic”.
Given
\boldsymbol{x}^{p}
, we can compute the probability of the actualized part according to Eq.
16
:

P_{\mathcal{M}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{[1:L]}^{a}\mid\boldsymbol{x}^{p})=\prod_{l=1}^{L}P_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{\left[l\right]}\mid\boldsymbol{x}^{a}_{[0:(l-1)]},\,\boldsymbol{x}^{p}\right)\,.

(16)

In Eq.
16
,
L
is the number of tokens in the actualized part
\boldsymbol{x}^{a}
.
8
8

8

The length of the actualized part depends on the tokenization technique the language model uses.
For example,
Llama-3
and
Mistral
models split
Albania
into
[Al] [ban] [ia]
, while
Gemma
models have one reserved token
[Albania]
.

The subscript
[l]
specifies the index of a token in
\boldsymbol{x}^{a}
, and
[1:l]
specifies the range of tokens in
\boldsymbol{x}^{a}
, while
\boldsymbol{x}^{a}_{[0]}
refers to an empty set.
Further, we do not specify a subscript
[1:L]
for brevity unless it is necessary for clarity.

Direction vector

\vec{v}_{i}
.
We train one-vs-all
sAwMIL
probes via dual optimization.
We use the obtained solution to extract the linear direction that points towards the class of interest.
For example, in our
is-true
probe, the class of interest is the true statements.

\vec{v}_{i}=\sum_{j\,\in\,\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{j}\,y_{j}\,h_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\;\text{ with }\mathcal{S}=\{j\mid\alpha_{j}>0\},\;a_{j}\in\mathbb{R},\;y_{j}\in\{-1,1\}.

(17)

In Eq.
17
,
\mathcal{S}
is a set of support vectors,
\alpha_{j}
is the Lagrangian multiplier
[
46
]
,
h_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
is the activation after the
i^{\textrm{th}}
decoder for statement
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
, and
y_{j}
is the class label for
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
.

Interventions
.
Given a pre-actualized part of the statement
\boldsymbol{x}^{p}
, model
\mathcal{M}
, and a hidden representation
h_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}^{p})
, we apply directional interventions by translating the representation of the last token
\boldsymbol{x}^{p}_{[L]}
along
\pm\>\vec{\nu}_{i}
(here,
i
stands for the index of the decoder):

•

Positive directional shift
:

I_{i}^{+}\left(h_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{p}\right)\right)=\left[h_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}^{p})_{[1]},\dots,h_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}^{p})_{[L-1]},h_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}^{p})_{[L]}+\vec{\nu}_{i}\right]

•

Negative directional shift
:

I_{i}^{-}\left(h_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{p}\right)\right)=\left[h_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}^{p})_{[1]},\dots,h_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}^{p})_{[L-1]},h_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}^{p})_{[L]}-\vec{\nu}_{i}\right]

These interventions return modified representations, which we denote as
I_{i}^{+}(\boldsymbol{x}^{p})
and
I_{i}^{-}(\boldsymbol{x}^{p})
, respectively.
Furthermore, we compute the per-sample effect of the directional interventions.
It is defined as a difference between the original probability and the probability we get after the intervention:

\displaystyle\Delta I^{+}_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{a},\boldsymbol{x}^{p}\right)

\displaystyle\leftarrow P_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{a}\mid I_{i}^{+}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{p}\right)\right)-P_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{a}\mid\boldsymbol{x}^{p}\right)

(18)

\displaystyle\Delta I^{-}_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{a},\boldsymbol{x}^{p}\right)

\displaystyle\leftarrow P_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{a}\mid I_{i}^{-}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{p}\right)\right)-P_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{a}\mid\boldsymbol{x}^{p}\right)

(19)

To compare interventions across decoders and models, we look at the success rate of the interventions.
The per-statement intervention is successful if
\Delta I_{i}^{+}
and
\Delta I_{i}^{-}
have opposing effects on the conditional probability
P_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{a}\mid\boldsymbol{x}^{p}\right)
.
In other words, if
\Delta I_{i}^{+}
is positive, then
\Delta I_{i}^{-}
must be negative, and vice-versa.

At the same time, we must ensure that the effect of the intervention is consistent in most statements
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{D}_{test}
.
If half of the statements have positive
\Delta I_{i}^{+}
and another have negative
\Delta I_{i}^{+}
, then our intervention produces a random change in
P_{\mathcal{M}}
.
To ensure that the effect is consistent, we look at the
dominant effect direction
of the intervention,
\overline{d}_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}
.
Here,
\overline{d}_{i}=+1
, if more than half
\Delta I_{i}^{+}
are positive, and
\overline{d}_{i}=-1
if more than half is negative.

In summary, a successful directional intervention is one that produces opposing effects when shifting along
\pm\vec{\nu}_{i}
, and aligns
\Delta I_{i}^{+}
with the dominant direction.
Fig.
3
provides an overview of the workflow to determine the per-statement success of the intervention.
Formally, we define a per-statement success
s
as

s(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbb{I}\Big[\left[\operatorname{sign}\left(\Delta I^{+}_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{a},\boldsymbol{x}^{p}\right)\right)\neq\operatorname{sign}\left(\Delta I^{-}_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}^{a},\boldsymbol{x}^{p})\right)\right]\land\ \left[\operatorname{sign}\left(\Delta I^{+}_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{a},\boldsymbol{x}^{p}\right)\right)=\operatorname{sign}\left(\overline{d}_{i}\right)\right]\Big]

(20)

where

\mathbb{I}\left[\cdot\right]=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if the condition holds},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Why do we only look at the sign?

During our initial experiments, we observed that even when
\vec{\nu}_{i}
was trained to separate true and false statements, the effect of the intervention on
P_{\mathcal{M}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{a}\mid\boldsymbol{x}^{p})
could vary across decoders.
Specifically, in certain decoders, shifting along
\pm\vec{\nu}_{i}
consistently increased the probability of
\boldsymbol{x}^{a}
, while in others it decreased it.
These effects were consistent in the sense that shifting in the opposite direction produced the opposing effect.
This phenomenon can be attributed to the complex interactions within each decoder of the model.
As highlighted by
Heimersheim and Nanda 2024
, activation patching experiments have revealed that interventions can have varying directions of effect depending on the decoder.
Therefore, observing a sign flip in the effect of an intervention does not invalidate the direction
\vec{\nu}_{i}
, as long as it is consistent.

Given a set of true statements from
\mathcal{D}_{test}
, we compute the overall success rate at a decoder
i
as follows:

\omega_{i}\;=\;\frac{1}{N}\sum s\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\right),

(21)

where
N
stands for the number of the true statements in
\mathcal{D}_{test}
and
s\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\right)
is success for the
j^{\textrm{th}}
statement as defined in Eq.
20
.

If the overall success rate at decoder
i
is greater than
50\%
, we claim that the intervention at decoder
i
is successful. Hence, the manipulation criterion is fulfilled.
We use a one-sided binomial test to confirm whether the overall success rate is significant:

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle:\omega_{i}\leq 0.5

(22)

\displaystyle H_{A}

\displaystyle:\omega_{i}>0.5

(23)

For example, the overall success rate of
61\%
with the dominant direction
\overline{d}_{i}=+1
tells us that if we have
100
statements, on average, we increase the probability of the correct answer in
61
statements.
Similarly, a success rate of
98\%
with the dominant direction
\overline{d}_{i}=-1
indicates that shifting along
+\vec{\nu}_{i}
decreases the probability of the correct answer in approximately
98
out of
100
statements.

Locality
.
To further determine the quality of the directional intervention, we assess whether changes in probability are concentrated on the actualized part, rather than being diffused across random tokens in the vocabulary
\mathcal{V}
.
In other words, our intervention should change
P_{\mathcal{M}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{a}\mid\boldsymbol{x}^{p})
and should not change the probability of random tokens
P_{\mathcal{M}}(\boldsymbol{r}\mid\boldsymbol{x}^{p})
.

Specifically, we expect the intervention to primarily affect the likelihood of the correct continuation,

P_{\mathcal{M}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{[1:L]}^{a}\mid\boldsymbol{x}^{p})
,
while leaving the probability of a randomly sampled continuation,

P_{\mathcal{M}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{[1:L]}\mid\boldsymbol{x}^{p})
, mostly unchanged.
Here,
\boldsymbol{r}_{[1:L]}
denotes a random sequence sampled from the vocabulary of the model
\mathcal{M}
.
We quantify these changes as:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{Correct}}

\displaystyle=\left|P_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{a}\mid I_{i}^{+}(\boldsymbol{x}^{p})\right)-P_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{a}\mid I_{i}^{-}(\boldsymbol{x}^{p})\right)\right|

(24)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{Random}}

\displaystyle=\left|P_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\boldsymbol{r}\mid I_{i}^{+}(\boldsymbol{x}^{p})\right)-P_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\boldsymbol{r}\mid I_{i}^{-}(\boldsymbol{x}^{p})\right)\right|

(25)

Further, we say that the intervention satisfies the
locality
criterion if

\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{\text{Correct}}\right]\;>\;\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{\text{Random}}\right].

(26)

That is, the expected change in probability for the correct output,
\boldsymbol{x}^{a}
, exceeds the expected change for a randomly sampled output
\boldsymbol{r}
.

5.4
Recap: Overall Validity

In this section, we established that the multiclass
sAwMIL
probe satisfies the validity criteria. Specifically, we confirmed that it satisfies the correlation and selectivity criteria, outperforming zero-shot prompting and mean-difference probe with conformal prediction intervals. We further demonstrated that multiclass
sAwMIL
satisfies the generalization criterion, indicating that we can successfully apply probes trained on multiclass
sAwMIL
to statements from other domains.
In addition, we showed that the one-vs-all
sAwMIL
probes satisfy the manipulation and locality criteria. In a majority of cases, we can perform interventions that change the probabilities of correct replies. Together, these findings provide strong evidence for the overall validity of
sAwMIL
probes.
Not
all LLMs have a veracity mechanism that has a linear relationship with the output. Exploring this non-linear relationship is part of our future work.

sAwMIL

Using conformal prediction to abstain in ambiguous cases

The multiclass
sAwMIL
places each statement along three dimensions:
is-true
,
is-false
, and
is-neither
.

Thus, if we want to (a) compare probes, (b) evaluate confidence, and (c) enable abstention in ambiguous cases, conformal prediction (or a similar technique) is necessary.

6.4
How to use layer-wise predictions to measure similarity between LLMs?

Figure 11
:
Minimum Spanning Tree: Model similarity based on multiclass
sAwMIL
layer-wise predictions.

We compare models based on how similarly their top
50\%
performing layers classify statements in each data set. We use W-MCC to determine the top layers.
For each model pair, we compute the pairwise Jensen–Shannon distances between their top layers (based on
sAwMIL
output probabilities), and retain the minimum distance as the distance score for that pair.
This process is repeated for each of the three data sets.
The final distance between a pair of models is averaged over the three data sets.
The minimum spanning tree is then constructed from the aggregated distance matrix.
The tree connects all models using the shortest total edge length.
Leaf nodes correspond to models that are most dissimilar from the rest—i.e., their distribution of predictions diverges from the majority.
We observe that default models tend to form leaves, indicating a clear separation from chat-based models.

Qwen
models are among the most distant, appearing at the edges of the tree.

Gemma
and
Mistral
models often fall between or around
Llama
variants, implying their veracity representation aligns more.

The output of the multiclass
sAwMIL
probe allows us to assess how well different LLMs align in their internal representations of veracity. In particular, do LLMs assign veracity labels in similar ways? If not, which models diverge the most in their predictions?

For each statement, the multiclass
sAwMIL
outputs a probability distribution over the three veracity classes:
true
,
false
, and
neither
.
To compare these distributions between models, we compute the pairwise Jensen-Shannon divergence between the corresponding
sAwMIL
outputs.

To reduce noise and focus on informative representations, we restrict this comparison to the top
50\%
of layers in each model (based on W-MCC values). For each pair of models, we compute the Jensen-Shannon divergence between all combinations of their top-performing layers and retain the minimum value. We repeat this procedure across all three data sets and compute the final pairwise distance between each model pair by averaging the minimum Jensen-Shannon divergences across data sets. This gives us a distance matrix of size
\mathbb{R}^{16\times 16}
, where each entry represents the averaged Jensen-Shannon divergence value between LLMs.
Higher values indicate greater disagreement in how two models distribute their veracity scores.

We visualize this distance matrix via the minimum spanning tree in Fig.
11
.
The minimum spanning tree connects all models using the smallest possible total edge weight, highlighting the backbone of model distances.
By retaining only the strongest links, it reveals which models are similar and which ones are misaligned (e.g., models in the distant leaf nodes).

We observe several patterns in Fig.
11
.
First, default models form two separate leaf clusters, indicating that their veracity predictions diverge both from each other and from the rest of the model set.
The default
Qwen
models do not align with the other default models.
In contrast, chat-tuned models are more tightly connected within a central part of the tree, suggesting greater consistency in how they internally represent veracity.
This aligns with our broader finding that alignment techniques such as RLHF and instruction-tuning might improve the representation of facts.

Interestingly, the
Qwen
models – both default and chat – appear at the periphery of the tree.
This family of models is trained on a multilingual corpus, which might lead to a different representation of veracity.
In contrast, the
LLama
family is positioned near the center of the minimum spanning tree, serving as a hub.
Both
Gemma
and
Mistral
models are positioned near the
LLama
models, implying that their veracity signals are more similar, possibly due to architectural or training data overlap.

These results provide new insight into how different models encode factual knowledge and open up a new direction for ecosystem-level analysis
[
25
]
.

7
Conclusions

In this work, we take a critical look at two popular methods for probing the veracity of large language models (LLMs) – namely, zero-shot prompting and the mean-difference probe. The latter is a representation-based probe. We find several flawed assumptions. These include the assumption that (1) truth and falsehood are bidirectional; (2) LLMs capture and retain everything we know; (3) all veracity probes provide calibrated probabilities; (4) every statement is either true or false; and (5) we know
a priori
where the signal for veracity is stored. These popular methods also ignore uncertainty quantification.

To address these flaws, we introduce
sAwMIL
, a multiclass linear probe that combines Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) with Conformal Prediction Intervals (CP).
sAwMIL
uses MIL to capture representations across all tokens instead of focusing only on the last token.
sAwMIL
uses CP to quantify uncertainty and abstain from labeling when its predictions are outside the conformal intervals (i.e., when nonconformity values exceed the calibration threshold).
sAwMIL
explicitly models veracity by using a three-valued logic (
true
,
false
, or
neither
), allowing it to express when it “knows” that a statement is neither
true
nor
false
.

Through extensive validation experiments across sixteen models and three diverse data sets (city locations, medical indications, and word definitions), we show that
sAwMIL
satisfies five validity criteria: correlation, selectivity, generalization, manipulation, and locality.
Thus, we can use this probe to gain insights into how LLMs track and use veracity signals.
Our results show that veracity signals – corresponding to truthfulness, falsehood, and verifiability – are identified within the deeper decoders of LLMs.
In many cases, these signals are not merely representational: directional interventions along learned probe directions can systematically skew the model’s output, suggesting that some LLMs do
use
veracity signals during generation.

We discuss five criteria for evaluating methods that probe the veracity of LLMs (namely, correlation, generalization, selectivity, manipulation, and locality) and provide extensive results on 16 open-source LLMs (including both default and chat models) and 3 newly created data sets. Our results provide several insights into how LLMs encode and use veracity signals:

1.

Veracity is linearly encoded in the deeper decoders, with the strongest signals often concentrated in the third quarter of the model depth.

2.

Truth and falsehood are not always symmetrical:
is-true
and
is-false
signals may appear at different decoders and reflect distinct internal mechanisms.

3.

Linear probes on chat models perform better than on default models.

4.

For some LLMs with RLHF or knowledge distillation (such as
Gemma-2-9B
and
Qwen-2.5-14B
) linearly probing for veracity is not sufficient. One needs to capture the nonlinearity between internal representations and output behavior.

5.

There exists a third type of statement besides
true
and
false
statements. We called these statements
neither
. The signal for these
neither
true nor false statements is a distinct, learnable property. Many models can reliably separate entities or statements they have seen before from those they have not.

Our findings carry broader implications for the field. Linear probes can help distinguish when LLMs are
guessing
,
misleading
, or
conforming
to the user versus when they are recalling and using internalized knowledge, which enables more fine-grained model introspection.
Similarly, directional interventions along veracity axes offer a new avenue for mitigating hallucinations by nudging an LLM’s outputs toward grounded knowledge.

Technical Limitations and Future Work.

While
sAwMIL
advances the state of the art in probing the veracity of LLMs, it has a few limitations. These are part of our future work.

First, our evaluation is limited to three data sets –
City Locations
,
Medical Indications
, and
Word Definitions
– each focusing on factual claims.
These data sets do not cover broad domains such as commonsense reasoning, opinionated content, or temporally shifting facts.
Additionally, all of our experiments are conducted in English, raising open questions about how veracity signals generalize across languages and cultural contexts.
While our experiments suggest that
Qwen-2.5
models exhibit some unique behavior, more evidence is needed to confirm whether this is due to multilingual training or other factors.

Second, we evaluate a curated set of publicly available models ranging from 3B to 14B parameters.
We do not probe very large-scale models such as
LLaMA-2-70B
or
DeepSeek

[
52
]
.
It is unknown whether these larger models maintain linear separability between their internal representations and veracity, or shift toward nonlinear separability.
We also observe that alignment techniques used during fine-tuning can influence how veracity is encoded and used. For instance, we observe that LLMs trained via RLHF or knowledge distillation exhibit different behavior.

Third,
sAwMIL
assumes linear separability: a reasonable but limited approximation.
Since
sAwMIL
is a variant of support vector machines, we will explore using nonlinear kernel functions to capture nonlinearity.
Additionally, our current design treats each sentence as a bag of token embeddings, ignoring word order.
Incorporating attention mechanisms could help capture sequential dependencies and improve signal localization.

B.1
‘Neither’ Statements

Since we do not have access to the training data sets of LLMs, we cannot validate whether LLMs retained information about specific facts or entities.
That is, we do not know the composition of the internal knowledge
\mathrm{K}_{\mathcal{M}}
of an LLM.
Hence, we cannot be certain about what each LLM can (and cannot) verify.
To overcome this issue, we create
neither
statements with
synthetic entities
—i.e., entities that do not exist in the real world or fictional works. The
neither
statements are the ones whose value cannot be determined at present (e.g., due to lack of information).

Generation of ‘Neither’ Statements

We use synthetic names to generate
neither
-type statements.
For example, “The city of
Staakess
is located in
Soldovadago
” mentions a town and a country that do not exist.
From the point of view of an LLM, these statements should be considered
neither-true-nor-false
, as LLMs could not have learned anything about these.

To generate the
neither
statements, we use the Markov-Chain technique
[
42
]
.
Given a set of existing words
[w_{1},w_{2}\dots,w_{n}]\in S
, we break each word
w_{i}
into
n
-grams,
For instance, we break “ability” into the following 2-grams:
[start]a ab bi il li it ty y[end]
.
We then compute a transition matrix
\mathcal{T}_{S}
, which provides the probability of transitioning from the
n
-gram
i
to
n
-gram
j
is given by:

\mathcal{T}_{S}\left(j\mid i\right)=\frac{\operatorname{count}\left(i\rightarrow j\right)}{\sum_{x}\operatorname{count}\left(i\rightarrow x\right)}

In addition, we use
\mathcal{T}_{S}
to sample new synthetic words that follow the
n
-gram distribution of words in
S
.
In our experiments, we use 3-grams for most entities, except for country names, which we generate with 2-grams.
We use the
namemaker
12
12

12

github.com/Rickmsd/namemaker
package that implements a Markov-Chain word generator.
