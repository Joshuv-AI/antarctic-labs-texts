Risk prediction models for venous thromboembolism in the intensive care unit: a systematic review protocol assessing model performance, risk of bias, and clinical applicability
Authors: Furong Zhu, Jiahong Chen, Wenyu Tong, Peng Qiu, Jingjing Mu, Xiaoqun Xu, Qixia Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVenous thromboembolism (VTE) is a prevalent and serious complication in intensive care unit (ICU) patients, often leading to long-term issues, increased healthcare costs, and disrupted care. Given its preventable nature, evaluating the risk of VTE in ICU patients and identifying high-risk groups is crucial. Several VTE risk prediction models for ICU patients have been developed, but their performance, risk of bias, and clinical applicability remain unclear. Therefore, this systematic review protocol aims to evaluate all eligible multivariable prediction models and compare their effectiveness in predicting the risk of VTE in ICU patients, rather than studies that focus on a single risk factor or univariate risk scores.MethodsA literature search will be conducted across multiple databases, including PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, CINAHL, Embase, CNKI, Wanfang Database, VIP, and Sinomed, from inception to April 2025. The language of publications will not be restricted. The search aims to identify studies reporting the development and/or external validation of original multivariable prediction models, including both traditional and machine learning models, to evaluate emerging methods for predicting VTE risk in ICU patients. Two reviewers will independently perform literature screening, data extraction, and assessment of quality and risk of bias, with discrepancies resolved through consensus or adjudication by a third reviewer. Risk of bias and certainty of evidence will be assessed using the Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST) and the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework. If at least five external validation studies are available, a random-effects meta-analysis will be employed to evaluate model performance across these studies. Specifically, the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman method will be used to pool concordance (c)-statistics and observed-to-expected (O:E) ratios, providing a comprehensive assessment of each model’s performance. Subgroup analyses and meta-regression analyses will be performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity where feasible.DiscussionThis study aims to address a significant research gap by comprehensively evaluating existing VTE risk prediction models in ICU patients. The findings will inform clinical decision-making, enabling healthcare professionals to select appropriate risk prediction models and develop individualized VTE prevention strategies. Anticipated challenges in this review include substantial heterogeneity among included studies in terms of population characteristics, outcome definitions, and model methodologies; a limited number of external validation studies for existing prediction models; and potential incomplete or non-transparent reporting of model development and validation processes, which may hinder the assessment of methodological quality and risk of bias.Systematic review registrationPROSPERO CRD420251020721.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13643-026-03259-x.Keywords: Venous thromboembolism, Intensive care units, Prediction model, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), encompassing deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), is a major global health problem and the third leading cause of cardiovascular-related mortality worldwide [1, 2]. Approximately ten million individuals develop VTE each year, and the long-term mortality rate approaches 20% [3, 4]. Patients in the intensive care unit (ICU) are at particularly high risk, with reported incidence ranging from 1 to 45% [5]. This elevated risk is attributed not only to common VTE risk factors such as advanced age, obesity, prior VTE, cancer, stroke, pregnancy, trauma, and recent surgery but also to ICU-specific factors including immobilization, mechanical ventilation, central venous catheterization, and vasopressor therapy [6]. Although thromboprophylaxis is widely recommended, anticoagulant therapy may increase bleeding risk in critically ill patients [7]. Therefore, accurate VTE risk stratification is essential to support individualized thromboprophylaxis decisions and balance the benefits of VTE prevention against the risk of bleeding [8].

Several VTE risk assessment tools, such as the Khorana score [9], the Caprini risk assessment model (Caprini RAM) [10], and the Padua Prediction Score (PPS) [11], are widely used in hospitalized patients. However, these tools may not adequately capture ICU-specific risk factors and often classify a large proportion of critically ill patients as high risk, thereby limiting their ability to support individualized risk stratification and clinical decision-making in ICU settings [12].

In recent years, several multivariable VTE prediction models specifically designed for ICU patients have been developed using both traditional statistical approaches and machine learning methods. For example, Viarasilpa et al. [13] proposed a prediction model incorporating clinical characteristics such as central venous catheterization, prolonged immobilization, and mechanical ventilation to predict symptomatic VTE in ICU patients, whereas Guan et al. [14] developed an interpretable machine learning model using multicenter eICU data. Traditional statistical models remain the foundation of clinical prediction research [15]. However, they may have limited ability to capture nonlinear relationships and complex interactions among predictors; in contrast, machine learning approaches may better identify complex patterns within high-dimensional data but also present challenges related to overfitting, model interpretability, and reporting transparency [16–19].

Despite the increasing number of prediction models for VTE in ICU patients, important limitations remain. Many models have been developed using single-center datasets, lack robust external validation, and may be insufficiently transparent or too complex for routine clinical implementation. Moreover, previous systematic reviews have primarily focused on VTE risk factors or thromboprophylaxis strategies rather than systematically synthesizing and critically appraising ICU-specific multivariable prediction models and their methodological quality [20, 21].

Therefore, this systematic review aims to systematically synthesize and critically appraise multivariable VTE prediction models for ICU patients, including both traditional statistical models and machine learning approaches. By evaluating model performance, calibration, risk of bias, explainability, and clinical applicability, this review seeks to provide evidence-based guidance for selecting appropriate prediction tools and to inform the development and validation of more robust prediction models for individualized thromboprophylaxis in critically ill patients. Methods

Protocol registration and reporting

This systematic review protocol adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines [22] and will report outcomes according to the Transparent Reporting of Multivariable Prediction Models for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (TRIPOD-SRMA) standards [23]. A complete PRISMA-P checklist is provided in Supplementary Material 1. Data extraction and critical appraisal will follow the CHARMS checklist [24] and Debray et al.’s recommendations [25]. This review commenced on March 31, 2025, and is expected to be completed by the end of March 2027. The review protocol was registered with PROSPERO on April 1, 2025 (registration number CRD420251020721). Any substantive revisions will be described in the final report and updated in the PROSPERO record.

Eligibility criteria

This review will include observational studies that develop and/or validate at least one prediction model for VTE in critically ill patients. The PICOTS system [25] was used to construct the research question (Table 1).

Population

Adult ICU patients aged ≥ 18 years will be included. For studies involving both ICU and non-ICU populations, inclusion will be limited to those in which data for the ICU subgroup can be extracted separately or in which ICU patients comprise more than 80% of the total study population [26]. If applicable, the specific type of ICU (e.g., medical, surgical, trauma, cardiac, general, etc.) will be recorded and used for subgroup analysis.

Intervention

Studies evaluating multivariable prognostic prediction models (including at least two predictors) designed to estimate the future risk of VTE in ICU patients will be included. Therefore, this review focuses on prognostic prediction models rather than diagnostic models intended to detect existing or prevalent VTE at the time of assessment. Eligible studies may include model development studies (with or without external validation), external validation studies (with or without model updating), and studies describing model updating strategies, such as recalibration, revision, or extension of existing prediction models. Multivariable statistical models, risk scoring systems, nomograms, and machine learning algorithms will all be considered eligible. Prediction models may report outcome risk as either absolute probability estimates or relative risk scores.

Studies focusing solely on single risk factors, univariable risk scores, diagnostic tools for existing VTE, or association analyses without model development or validation will be excluded.

Outcomes

The primary outcome to be predicted in the included studies will be the occurrence of VTE, including PE and DVT, based on any diagnostic criteria. Secondary outcomes and effect measures will be defined according to the original study authors.

Timing

Included studies need to report on prediction models applied during the period before being diagnosed with VTE.

Setting

Prediction models intended for use by healthcare professionals in the ICU and applicable at any time point during ICU admission will be considered eligible for inclusion.

Study design

All observational studies that develop and/or validate at least one prediction model for VTE in critically ill patients will be included. Reviews, editorials, commentaries, conference abstracts lacking sufficient methodological details, case reports, and animal studies will be excluded. Duplicate publications derived from the same dataset will also be excluded. In addition, studies for which the full text remains inaccessible after two attempts to contact the corresponding authors via email will not be considered eligible.

Model performance

Eligible studies must report at least one measure of model discrimination (e.g., c-statistic or area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUROC]) and at least one measure of model calibration (e.g., calibration plot, observed-to-expected (O:E) ratio, calibration slope, or calibration intercept).

Search strategy

We will conduct a comprehensive search of nine databases, including PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), Embase, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Database, China Science and Technology Journal Database (VIP), and Sinomed, covering literature from inception to April 1, 2025. Including Chinese databases can reduce language and publication bias and enhance global representativeness [26]. Additionally, relevant grey literature will be searched through Google Scholar, Open Grey, medRxiv, and ProQuest Dissertations & Theses on the same day. The language of publications will not be restricted. Studies published in languages other than English or Chinese will be translated using machine translation.

A comprehensive search strategy will be developed using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords related to venous thromboembolism, critically ill patients, and prognostic prediction models. The search terms will include, but not be limited to: (“Venous Thromboembolism” OR VTE OR Thromboembolism OR Thrombosis OR “Venous Thrombosis” OR “Pulmonary Embolism” OR PE OR “deep vein thrombosis” OR DVT) AND (“Intensive Care Units” OR “Critical Care” OR ICU OR “critical* ill*” OR “acute* ill*”) AND (“Risk Assessment” OR “Nomograms” OR “risk model*” OR “risk stratification” OR “risk predict*” OR “risk scor*” OR “predict* model*” OR “predict* scor*” OR “prediction tool*” OR “scor* system*” OR “prognos* predict*” OR “multivaria* predict*” OR algorithm* OR “clinical prediction rule*” OR derivation OR validation OR “risk calculator*” OR “machine learning” OR “deep learning” OR “artificial intelligence” OR “neural network*” OR “random forest” OR XGBoost OR “support vector machine*”). The search strategy will be adapted as appropriate for each database. The references of the eligible literature will be manually screened for potential missing eligible studies. For detailed search strategies for each database, see Supplementary Material 2.

Study selection and screening

EndNote V.21 (Clarivate Analytics) software will be used to integrate and deduplicate the retrieved literature. Based on the study-selection criteria, two researchers (FZ and WT) will independently screen the titles and abstracts of the retrieved literature. After excluding irrelevant literature, full-text articles will be examined to confirm their eligibility. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage will be documented and reported in the PRISMA flow diagram. Inter-rater agreement for screening will be assessed using Cohen’s kappa [27], and any disagreements will be resolved through discussion or arbitration by a third reviewer (QZ). When multiple models are reported within a study, a predefined hierarchy (external validation > internal validation > apparent performance) will be applied to select one “index model” per study for the primary meta-analysis, while all eligible models will be included in the qualitative synthesis.

When studies report multiple performance estimates for the same model under different analytical conditions (e.g., different imputation strategies, outlier handling approaches, or candidate model specifications), the primary analysis defined by the original authors will be preferentially extracted. If no primary analysis is specified, the most conservative estimate will be selected.

If required data (e.g., standard errors for model performance metrics) are not clearly reported, the corresponding authors will be contacted for clarification. If studies report c-statistics or O:E ratios without corresponding standard errors, these will be estimated using the methods proposed by Debray et al. [25], where feasible.

Risk of bias and applicability assessment

The PROBAST will be applied by two researchers (FZ and JC) to assess the risk of bias and applicability of the included studies [30]. Risk of bias and applicability concerns are graded as low, high, or unclear. The risk of bias assessment comprises 20 signaling questions across 4 domains (participants, predictors, outcome, and analysis). Each question is rated as yes (Y), probably yes (PY), no (N), probably no (PN), or no information (NI). A domain is considered low risk if all signaling questions are answered as Y or PY. The overall risk of bias is considered low only when all domains are deemed low risk. Applicability is evaluated using the first three domains (participants, predictors, and outcome), with a similar approach to risk of bias assessment, but without signaling questions. Any discrepancies will be discussed and resolved between the two researchers, with consultation from a third researcher (QZ), if necessary. Development and validation phases will be evaluated separately when both are reported. Inter-rater agreement for the PROBAST assessment will be evaluated using Cohen’s kappa statistic, with kappa values greater than 0.80 considered to indicate excellent agreement between reviewers [27].

Certainty of evidence assessment

Two researchers (FZ and JC) will independently assess the certainty of evidence for the included prediction models using the GRADE framework [25, 30–33], with disagreements resolved through discussion or consultation with a third researcher (QZ). The certainty of evidence will be categorized as high, moderate, low, or very low after evaluating the domains of risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.

Certainty assessments will focus primarily on evidence derived from external validation studies, because predictive performance estimated solely from model development datasets or internal validation procedures may be overly optimistic [25]. Separate certainty assessments may be conducted for the two core dimensions of prediction model performance, namely discrimination and calibration, when sufficient evidence is available [31, 32].

For discrimination, certainty assessment will focus on whether prediction models can reliably distinguish between critically ill patients with different risks of developing VTE and support clinically meaningful risk stratification [32, 33]. Discrimination performance will primarily be evaluated using pooled c-statistics or AUROC. In accordance with recent GRADE guidance for prognostic research, certainty judgments will consider whether discrimination estimates are sufficiently robust to differentiate patient groups with clinically important differences in absolute risk [33]. Inconsistency will be assessed according to variability in discrimination estimates across external validation studies using forest plot inspection, heterogeneity measures, and prediction intervals where appropriate. Indirectness will be considered when substantial differences exist between the included study populations, ICU settings, predictors, outcomes, or thromboprophylaxis practices and the target review question. Imprecision will be evaluated according to the uncertainty surrounding pooled discrimination estimates and whether confidence intervals overlap thresholds considered clinically informative for risk stratification.

For calibration, certainty assessment will focus on the agreement between predicted and observed risks across validation studies. Calibration performance will be evaluated using reported O:E ratios, calibration slopes, calibration intercepts, and calibration plots when available. An O:E ratio close to 1.0 will be considered indicative of satisfactory calibration [31]. Calibration will be considered a particularly important dimension of model performance because models with good discrimination but poor calibration may still produce clinically misleading estimates of absolute risk [32]. Inconsistency in calibration performance across validation studies will therefore receive particular attention. Prediction intervals and heterogeneity measures will be used to evaluate unexplained variability in calibration estimates where feasible. Indirectness will be considered when validation studies are conducted in populations or clinical settings with substantially different baseline VTE risks, case-mix characteristics, or thromboprophylaxis practices. Because calibration measures are inconsistently reported in prediction model studies, certainty assessments for calibration will be interpreted cautiously.

Risk of bias assessments within GRADE will be informed by the PROBAST evaluations of the included studies, particularly concerns related to participant selection, predictor assessment, outcome definition, overfitting, missing data handling, and inadequate validation procedures [30]. Publication bias and small-study effects will be explored when sufficient studies are available. Potential publication bias will be considered when studies reporting favorable model performance are more likely to be published or when selective reporting of discrimination or calibration measures is suspected [31, 32].

If quantitative synthesis is not feasible because of insufficient external validation studies or substantial unexplained heterogeneity, a narrative synthesis of model performance will be conducted, and certainty assessments will focus on the consistency, direction, and clinical applicability of findings across studies.

Statistical analysis

When at least five external validation studies are available for the same type of prediction model, a meta-analysis of model performance metrics will be conducted using R Statistical Software V.4.2.3 (R Core Team, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [34]. This threshold was selected to ensure sufficient data for reliable estimation of pooled performance measures and between-study heterogeneity, as meta-analyses based on very few studies may yield unstable estimates [35]. Studies reporting only model development or internal validation will be included in the review and summarized qualitatively but will not be quantitatively pooled.

To avoid double counting, overlapping cohorts (e.g., identified by institution, timeframe, or registry) will be examined, and the most comprehensive or externally validated report will be preferentially included for quantitative synthesis. When fewer than five external validation studies are identified, or when substantial methodological or clinical heterogeneity precludes meaningful pooling, findings will be summarized narratively rather than quantitatively synthesized.

For quantitative synthesis, random-effects meta-analysis models with the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman (HKSJ) adjustment will be used to pool discrimination measures (e.g., c-statistics/AUROC) and calibration measures (e.g., O:E ratios and calibration slopes), as this approach has been shown to outperform the traditional DerSimonian-Laird method, particularly in the presence of heterogeneity and a limited number of studies [25, 35]. Where feasible, performance estimates will be transformed into comparable formats prior to pooling.

Calibration assessment will be considered a key component of model performance evaluation and clinical applicability, rather than relying solely on discrimination performance. Comparisons across studies will focus on the consistency and magnitude of calibration measures, including O:E ratios and calibration slopes, where available.

Where available, decision-analytic measures, including net benefit and DCA, will be extracted to further evaluate the potential clinical usefulness of prediction models across clinically relevant threshold probabilities. In addition, models lacking sufficient interpretability or transparent reporting will be interpreted cautiously when assessing their potential clinical applicability and implementation.

To quantify uncertainty and heterogeneity, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and 95% prediction intervals (PIs) will be calculated [36]. A wider 95% PI relative to the 95% CI will be interpreted as indicating substantial between-study heterogeneity [37]. When considerable heterogeneity is observed, pooled estimates will be interpreted cautiously, with greater emphasis placed on the range and consistency of findings across studies rather than on the summary estimate alone.

Pre-specified subgroup analyses will be conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity according to study design (cohort, case-control, or cross-sectional), geographic region, ICU type, modeling approach (traditional statistical models vs. machine learning models), and validation type. Additional exploratory subgroup analyses may be performed, where sufficient data are available, based on study population characteristics (e.g., predominant disease category, baseline VTE risk, and thromboprophylaxis use), outcome definitions (e.g., VTE vs. DVT vs. PE; asymptomatic vs. symptomatic VTE), and regional differences in healthcare practices or standards of care. Where feasible, meta-regression analyses may also be conducted using selected study-level and methodological characteristics to further investigate sources of heterogeneity. Meta-regression analyses will only be performed when at least 10 studies are available to avoid overfitting and unstable estimates [26].

Sensitivity analyses will be conducted by excluding studies judged to have a high or unclear risk of bias to assess the robustness of the findings. Consistency between the primary and sensitivity analyses will increase confidence in the pooled estimates, whereas substantial changes will be interpreted as indicating reduced robustness.

If more than 10 studies are included, publication bias will be assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test [38].

Where external validation studies demonstrate poor model performance (e.g., reduced discrimination or unsatisfactory calibration), the potential need for model recalibration or updating will be discussed conceptually, although no recalibration analyses will be performed in this review.

Use of artificial intelligence tools

Studies published in languages other than English or Chinese will be translated using machine-assisted translation tools where necessary. However, no artificial intelligence tools will be used for study selection, data extraction, risk of bias assessment, or statistical analysis.

Discussion

ICU patients have a higher prevalence of VTE. Although various risk prediction models have been developed to assess VTE risk in these patients, their quality and clinical applicability remain uncertain. To our knowledge, this systematic review will be the first to systematically synthesize and critically appraise existing VTE prediction models in ICU patients, providing clinicians with a clear overview of currently available models. By synthesizing evidence on model discrimination and calibration, including c-statistics, calibration slopes, and O:E ratios, this review is expected to help identify models that most reliably stratify VTE risk in critically ill patients. Such information may support clinical decision-making by helping clinicians identify ICU patients at high risk of VTE who may benefit most from thromboprophylaxis, while simultaneously considering the competing risk of bleeding, thereby facilitating more individualized preventive strategies.

However, several potential limitations of this systematic review should be acknowledged. Considerable clinical and methodological heterogeneity is anticipated across studies in terms of study design, geographic region, ICU type, patient characteristics, outcome definitions, thromboprophylaxis practices, and modeling approaches, which may affect the feasibility of quantitative synthesis and limit the generalizability of the findings. If sufficient data are available, subgroup analyses and meta-regression analyses will be conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. In addition, prediction model studies frequently face methodological challenges such as limited event numbers, inappropriate handling of missing data, inadequate adjustment for overfitting, and insufficient external validation, all of which may compromise model stability and transportability. Publication bias may also be present, as studies reporting favorable model performance are more likely to be published. Furthermore, inconsistent reporting and variable adherence to the TRIPOD recommendations may limit the completeness and comparability of extracted model information, particularly for calibration and clinical utility measures. These issues will be carefully considered when interpreting the findings of the review.

Beyond identifying the most promising existing models, the findings of this review may also inform future research on model development, validation, and updating. For example, the synthesis of predictors and modeling approaches across studies may highlight important variables and methodological strategies that could improve future prediction models. Moreover, updating or recalibrating existing models using contemporary multicenter datasets may enhance their transportability and predictive performance in diverse ICU populations.

Direct comparisons between machine learning and traditional statistical models may remain challenging because of differences in predictor handling, model complexity, validation strategies, interpretability, and reporting practices. In addition, some machine learning studies may use “black-box” algorithms without adequate explainability or transparent reporting, which may limit clinical interpretability, reproducibility, and applicability in routine ICU practice. Therefore, explainability methods and model transparency will be considered when interpreting the clinical applicability of machine learning models.

Finally, the clinical implementation of prediction models remains challenging. Even models with good predictive performance may have limited impact if they are difficult to apply in routine practice. Practical considerations such as model simplicity, bedside feasibility, explainability, and integration into electronic health record systems are essential for successful implementation. Therefore, future research should also focus on developing user-friendly tools and embedding validated prediction models into clinical decision support systems to facilitate real-time risk assessment and improve thromboprophylaxis management in critically ill patients.
