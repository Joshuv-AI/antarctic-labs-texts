Revisiting black holes and their thermodynamics in Einstein-Kalb-Ramond gravity
Zhong-Xi Yu, Hong-Da Lyu, Mandula Huhe, Shoulong Li

Abstract
Einstein-Kalb-Ramond gravity is an alternative theory of gravity in which a rank-two antisymmetric tensor field, known as the Kalb-Ramond field, is nonminimally coupled to gravity and can induce spontaneous Lorentz symmetry breaking when it acquires a nonzero vacuum expectation value. In this work, we revisit Einstein-Kalb-Ramond gravity and obtain two classes of exact static black hole solutions with general topological horizons in diverse dimensions within this framework, both with and without a cosmological constant. We further analyze their thermodynamic properties and employ the Wald formalism to compute the Noether mass and entropy, thereby establishing the corresponding first law of black hole thermodynamics. Finally, we discuss the implications of the Noether mass charge for constraining spontaneous Lorentz symmetry breaking within the framework of Einstein-Kalb-Ramond gravity.



I
Introduction

Lorentz symmetry, or Lorentz invariance, is a fundamental postulate underlying our understanding of the universe. Whether it holds exactly or only approximately remains an open question of great interest. Over the past decades, its validity has been extensively examined from various perspectives—within both the Standard Model and gravitational sectors, across high and low energy scales, and from theoretical as well as experimental viewpoints
Mattingly:2005re
;
Liberati:2013xla
;
Will:2014kxa
;
Berti:2015itd
;
Hees:2016lyw
;
Wei:2021vvn
;
Li:2025yvq
. From a theoretical standpoint, one important class of Lorentz-violating mechanisms involves spontaneous breaking, which can occur when certain tensor fields acquire nonvanishing vacuum expectation values (VEVs) at low energies. The well-known Standard Model extension
Kostelecky:1988zi
;
Colladay:1996iz
;
Colladay:1998fq
;
Kostelecky:2003fs
, as a model-independent framework, allows one to systematically incorporate almost all possible spontaneous Lorentz-violating effects in both the Standard Model and general relativity (GR). In the gravitational sector, many important modified gravity theories, particularly those involving coupled matter fields, can be formulated within the framework of the Standard Model extension. For a comprehensive review, we refer the reader to Ref.
Liberati:2013xla
and references therein.

Among Lorentz-violating theories of gravity, Einstein-Kalb-Ramond (EKR) gravity is one of the most notable models
Altschul:2009ae
. It features a rank-two antisymmetric tensor field, known as the Kalb-Ramond (KR) field
Kalb:1974yc
, that is nonminimally coupled to gravity and can trigger spontaneous Lorentz symmetry breaking when it acquires a nonzero VEV. This theory has recently received increasing attention, as choosing a specific nonzero component of the KR field to acquire a VEV enables the construction of exact solutions
Lessa:2019bgi
;
Yang:2023wtu
. These solutions and their associated thermodynamic quantities—particularly the gravitational mass—provide a practical framework for testing spontaneous Lorentz symmetry breaking in the gravitational sector through black hole physics and astrophysical phenomena, where important and stringent constraints have already been obtained in the Solar System
Yang:2023wtu
. These developments have further stimulated interest in the theory. However, because EKR gravity involves nonminimal couplings between the gravitational and matter fields, several subtleties arise that must be handled with particular care.

To discuss these subtleties more concretely, let us recall that the Lagrangian density of EKR gravity takes the form
Altschul:2009ae

L=R-2\Lambda+\gamma_{1}B^{\mu\lambda}B^{\nu}{}_{\lambda}R_{\mu\nu}+\gamma_{2}B_{\mu\nu}B^{\mu\nu}R-\frac{1}{12}H_{\mu\nu\lambda}H^{\mu\nu\lambda}-V(B_{\mu\nu}B^{\mu\nu})\,,

(1)

where
R
and
R_{\mu\nu}
denote the Ricci scalar and the Ricci tensor, respectively, and
\Lambda
is the cosmological constant. The KR field
B_{\mu\nu}
is nonminimally coupled to both the Ricci tensor and Ricci scalar through the coupling constants
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
, while its field strength and potential are denoted by
H_{\mu\nu\lambda}
and
V
, respectively.

A first subtlety concerns the treatment of the nonminimal coupling terms in the Lagrangian. In an initial exploratory work
Lessa:2019bgi
, it was proposed that, when the KR field acquires a nonzero VEV, the term
\gamma_{2}B_{\mu\nu}B^{\mu\nu}R
can be absorbed into the Einstein-Hilbert term via a suitable redefinition. While this argument captures part of the effect, it does not constitute a fully general treatment of the coupled system. Following the typical approach used in the literature to obtain exact solutions, choosing a specific component of the KR field to acquire a nonzero VEV, together with an appropriately selected potential, may render both its field strength
H_{\mu\nu\lambda}
and the potential term
V
vanishing. In this situation, if one further requires the absence of the
\gamma_{1}B^{\mu\lambda}B^{\nu}{}_{\lambda}R_{\mu\nu}
term, the remaining nonvanishing part of the Lagrangian, namely
R+\gamma_{2}B_{\mu\nu}B^{\mu\nu}R
, can effectively be viewed as
\Phi R
. This structure may then be mapped back to GR in vacuum through a redefinition analogous to a conformal transformation. However, for
\gamma_{1}\neq 0
, such a redefinition is not straightforward, and one cannot simply set
\gamma_{2}=0
and discard this term during the solving procedure. Even when
\gamma_{1}=0
, the vacuum equivalence to GR should be examined carefully in contexts involving matter—for instance, in compact star settings—where such redefinitions may not work and lead to physically distinguishable deviations from GR.

A second subtlety arises from the fact that the equation of motion (EOM) of the KR field must be carefully verified during the solving procedure. Evidently, the EOM for the KR field
B_{\mu\nu}
generally involves curvature terms and therefore imposes additional consistency conditions on the spacetime geometry, beyond those arising from the gravitational field equations alone. This contrasts with the situation in Einstein gravity minimally coupled to matter fields. As noted in the previous paragraph, in order to obtain exact solutions, earlier studies often adopted an approach in which certain preferred nonvanishing components—together with explicit functional forms—were assigned to the KR field. However, this practice can lead to configurations where the KR field EOM is not automatically satisfied, even though the gravitational field equations are solved. In addition, in several of those works the KR field EOM was not explicitly checked, which may render the corresponding conclusions incomplete or less robust.

A third subtlety concerns the thermodynamic properties of the black hole, particularly the definition of its gravitational mass, which directly influences any observational tests constructed from the exact black-hole solutions. In modified gravity theories involving nonminimal couplings between gravity and matter fields, such interactions generally introduce additional contributions to black-hole thermodynamic quantities. Similar issues have been observed in gravity theories coupled to other types of fields, including scalars
Feng:2015oea
;
Feng:2015wvb
and vectors
An:2024fzf
;
Chen:2025ypx
;
Li:2025tcd
. Since several previous studies of black-hole thermodynamics in EKR gravity, such as
Yang:2023wtu
, have relied on the standard GR definitions of mass (Komar or Arnowitt-Deser-Misner mass) and entropy (Bekenstein-Hawking entropy), a reassessment of these quantities within a more general and self-consistent framework—such as the Wald formalism
Wald:1993nt
;
Iyer:1994ys
—is therefore warranted. An inappropriate identification of the physical gravitational mass may, in turn, lead to potentially unreliable constraints on the theoretical parameters inferred from astrophysical observations.

In this work, we revisit EKR gravity and derive two classes of exact static black hole solutions in diverse dimensions, both with and without a cosmological constant. We then employ the Wald formalism to compute the Noether conserved mass and entropy, thereby establishing the corresponding first law of black hole thermodynamics and examining the implications of the Noether mass charge for testing spontaneous Lorentz symmetry breaking within the framework of EKR gravity. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Sec.

V

VI

D

VII

IV
Thermodynamics

We now turn to the thermodynamics of the two branches of black hole solutions. The event horizon, located at
r=r_{h}
, is determined by the condition
f(r_{h})=0
, where the timelike Killing vector
\xi=\partial_{t}
becomes null, i.e.,
\xi_{\mu}\xi^{\mu}\big|_{r=r_{h}}=0
. Since the metric function
h
takes the same form in both branches, the corresponding horizon radius
r_{h}
is identical. It is convenient to express the integration constant
m
in terms of

as

m=kr_{h}^{n-1}\,.

(29)

The black hole temperature
T
is defined in terms of the surface gravity
K

\xi

K^{2}=-\frac{\nabla^{\mu}\xi^{\nu}\nabla_{\mu}\xi_{\nu}}{2}\Big|_{r=r_{h}}\,,\quad T=\frac{K}{2\pi}\,.

(30)

Accordingly, the Hawking temperatures for the two branches are

Case 1:

\displaystyle\quad T_{1}=\frac{h^{\prime}(r_{h})}{4\pi\sqrt{1-\ell_{1}}}=\frac{(n-1)k}{4\pi r_{h}\sqrt{1-\ell_{1}}}\,,

(31)

Case 2:

\displaystyle\quad T_{2}=\frac{h^{\prime}(r_{h})}{4\pi}=\frac{(n-1)k}{4\pi r_{h}}\,,

(32)

1
) can be computed using the Wald entropy formula, which generalizes the Bekenstein-Hawking result and possesses notable universality:

(34)

Here,

\displaystyle\quad S_{\textup{W}1}=\frac{2\pi w_{n,k}}{\kappa}(1-\ell_{1})r_{h}^{n}\,,

\displaystyle\quad S_{\textup{W}2}=\frac{2\pi w_{n,k}}{\kappa}(1-\ell_{2})r_{h}^{n}\,.

(D-1)

We next specialize to a variation induced by an infinitesimal diffeomorphism generated by an arbitrary vector field
\xi^{\mu}

(41)

(42)

\mathbf{L}

(43)

where we have used

D

D

(D-1)

The Noether charge can be expressed as

\mathbf{E}_{\psi}=0

(D-1)

V
Implications of the Noether mass charge for observational constraints

After obtaining the Noether mass charge via the Wald formalism, we find that the first branch of metric (
78
) expressed in terms of the Noether mass
M
differs notably from the one written in terms of the Komar mass
Yang:2023wtu
. This naturally raises the question of how observational constraints may vary when different definitions of the mass are adopted. In this section, we illustrate this issue by examining the perihelion precession of Mercury as a representative Solar System test in the first branch of spacetime (
78
), and analyze how the use of the Noether mass charge affects the corresponding observational bounds. Thus, we set
n=2
,
k=1
, and rewrite the coordinate
u=\cos\theta
. The metric can then be rewritten as

ds_{1}^{2}=-\left(1-\frac{2M_{1}}{r\sqrt{1-\ell_{1}}}\right)dt^{2}+\frac{1-\ell_{1}}{1-\frac{2M_{1}}{r\sqrt{1-\ell_{1}}}}dr^{2}+r^{2}(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta d\phi^{2})\,.

(82)

The motion of Mercury around the Sun can be well approximated as that of a test particle along its geodesics in the spacetime (
82
). Generally, the dynamics of a relativistic particle of mass
\mu
is governed by the action

{\cal S}_{1}=\int L_{1}d\tau=\int d\tau\left(-\mu\sqrt{-g_{\rho\sigma}\dot{X}^{\rho}\dot{X}^{\sigma}}\right)\,,

(83)

where
\tau
and
X
represent the proper time and coordinate respectively, and the dot denotes the derivative with respect to
\tau
.
Classically, the action is equivalent to
Li:2020wse

{\cal S}_{2}=\int L_{2}d\tau=\frac{1}{2}\int d\tau\left(\zeta^{-1}g_{\rho\sigma}\dot{X}^{\rho}\dot{X}^{\sigma}-\mu^{2}\zeta\right)\,,

(84)

where
\zeta(\tau)
is an auxiliary field. By varying the action
{\cal S}_{2}
with respect to the
\zeta
gives

\zeta^{2}\mu^{2}+g_{\rho\sigma}\dot{X}^{\rho}\dot{X}^{\sigma}=0\,.

(85)

Solving above equation for
\zeta
and substituting the result back into
{\cal S}_{2}
reproduces the original action
{\cal S}_{1}
. Variation of either
{\cal S}_{1}
or
{\cal S}_{2}
with respect to
X^{\rho}
yields the same geodesic equation,

\ddot{X}^{\lambda}+\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\rho\sigma}\dot{X}^{\rho}\dot{X}^{\sigma}=0\,.

(86)

The conjugate momentum with respect to
X^{\nu}
is given by

P_{\nu}=\frac{\partial L_{2}}{\partial\dot{X}^{\nu}}=\frac{\mu g_{\nu\rho}\dot{X}^{\rho}}{\sqrt{-g_{\rho\sigma}\dot{X}^{\rho}\dot{X}^{\sigma}}}=\mu g_{\nu\rho}\dot{X}^{\rho}\,,

(87)

where we have used the gauge

g_{\rho\sigma}\dot{X}^{\rho}\dot{X}^{\sigma}=-1=g_{tt}\dot{t}^{2}+g_{rr}\dot{r}^{2}+g_{\theta\theta}\dot{\theta}^{2}+g_{\phi\phi}\dot{\phi}^{2}\,.

(88)

The energy
{\cal E}
and angular momentum
{\cal L}
of the test particle with unit mass are given by

\displaystyle{\cal E}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{P_{t}}{\mu}=-g_{tt}\dot{t}\,,

(89)

\displaystyle{\cal L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{P_{\phi}}{\mu}=g_{\phi\phi}\dot{\phi}\,.

(90)

Solving above two equations for
\dot{t}
and
\dot{\phi}
, and substituting the results into Eq. (
88
), the motion of the massive particle is confined to the equatorial plane by imposing the initial conditions
\theta=\pi/2
and
\dot{\theta}=0
, and is then governed by

\dot{r}^{2}+\frac{\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}^{2}}{r^{2}}+1\right)\left(1-\frac{2M_{1}}{r\sqrt{1-\ell_{1}}}\right)}{1-\ell_{1}}-\frac{\mathcal{E}^{2}}{1-\ell_{1}}=0\,.

(91)

We now introduce a dimensionless variable
x=\mathcal{L}^{2}/(M_{1}r)
, for which

\dot{r}=\frac{dr}{d\phi}\dot{\phi}=-\frac{M_{1}}{\mathcal{L}}\frac{dx}{d\phi}\,.

(92)

Differentiating this relation further yields

\frac{d^{2}x}{d\phi^{2}}+\frac{x}{1-\ell_{1}}-\frac{1}{\left(1-\ell_{1}\right)^{3/2}}-\frac{3M_{1}^{2}x^{2}}{\left(1-\ell_{1}\right)^{3/2}\mathcal{L}^{2}}=0\,.

(93)

This procedure automatically eliminates the effect of the integration constant appearing in Eq. (
18
). For Mercury’s orbital motion around the Sun, the last term on the left-hand side is much smaller than the preceding ones.
Next, we decompose
x
as
x=x_{0}+x_{1}
, where
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
correspond to the zeroth- and first-order quantities, respectively, which satisfy the following equations:

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}x_{0}}{d\phi^{2}}+\frac{x_{0}}{1-\ell_{1}}-\frac{1}{\left(1-\ell_{1}\right)^{3/2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\,,

(94)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}x_{1}}{d\phi^{2}}+\frac{x_{1}}{1-\ell_{1}}-\frac{3M_{1}^{2}x_{0}^{2}}{\left(1-\ell_{1}\right)^{3/2}\mathcal{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\,,

(95)

The solution of Eq. (
94
) is given by

x_{0}=(1-\ell_{1})^{-1/2}\left(1+e\cos\left(\frac{\phi}{\sqrt{1-\ell_{1}}}\right)\right)\,,

(96)

where the integration constants are chosen such that the initial angle is
\phi_{0}=0
and the orbit eccentricity is
e
.
Substituting the zeroth-order orbit (
96

95
), the first order solution is given by

x_{1}=\frac{3M_{1}^{2}}{\left(1-\ell_{1}\right)^{3/2}\mathcal{L}^{2}}\left(1+\frac{e^{2}}{2}-\frac{e^{2}}{6}\cos\left(\frac{2\phi}{\sqrt{1-\ell_{1}}}\right)+\frac{e\phi}{\sqrt{1-\ell_{1}}}\sin\left(\frac{\phi}{\sqrt{1-\ell_{1}}}\right)\right)

(97)

It is noted that only the third term in the squared bracket contributes nontrivially to the precession. Thus, the total orbit can be approximately expressed in the form of an elliptical equation as

x\approx(1-\ell_{1})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left[1+e\cos\left(\frac{\phi}{\sqrt{1-\ell_{1}}}\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{(1-\ell_{1})}\right)\right)\right]\,.

(98)

where
\alpha=3M_{1}^{2}/\mathcal{L}^{2}
is a small quantity.
It is worth noting that the total orbit, computed to first order using the Noether mass, differs from the corresponding orbit derived using the Komar mass
Yang:2023wtu
. Evidently, the period
\Phi
of the orbit also differs from previous results, and is then given by

\Phi=\frac{2\pi\sqrt{1-\ell_{1}}}{1-\alpha(1-\ell_{1})^{-1}}\,.

(99)

It is worth noting that, if one directly considers the correction to the orbital period predicted by GR due to the spontaneous breaking of Lorentz invariance, one can perform a Taylor expansion in
\ell_{1}
, as it is necessarily a small quantity, yielding

\Phi=\frac{2\pi}{1-\alpha}-\frac{(1-3\alpha)\pi\ell_{1}}{(1-\alpha)^{2}}+{\cal O}(\ell_{1}^{2})\,.

(100)

It is evident from this expression that an inappropriate definition of mass can lead to significantly different corrections to GR predictions in this theory.

However, in the weak-field regime,
\alpha
is already a small parameter, i.e. GR itself represents a correction to Newtonian gravity. The leading-order correction arising from the spontaneous breaking of Lorentz invariance relative to Newtonian gravity is unaffected by the choice of mass definition, since the influence of the Lorentz-violating parameter on the mass definition is itself suppressed by
\alpha
. To examine this explicitly, one may further perform a Taylor expansion of the above expression in
\alpha
. Thus,

\Phi=2\pi+2\pi\alpha-\ell_{1}\pi\,,

(101)

where the second term corresponds to
\Delta\Phi_{\textup{GR}}
and the third term to
\Delta\Phi_{\ell_{1}}
. This result is consistent with previous findings
Yang:2023wtu
, with differences arising only at higher-order corrections.

VI.1
Exact solutions

We now construct exact static black hole solutions with general topological horizons for the theory (
2

D=2+n

. The metric

and KR field

are assumed to take the same ansatz as in Eqs. (

). In this case, obtaining a exact solution typically requires relaxing the vacuum conditions (
11

V^{\prime}\neq 0
.
This requirement can also be understood directly from the EOMs. As we mentioned in introduction, Eqs. (
4
)–(
5
) may be viewed as two independent constraints on the curvature. When the cosmological constant is turned on, Eq. (
4
) is modified while Eq. (
5
) remains unchanged. Since the KR field is still taken to have the same configuration as in the
\Lambda=0
case, maintaining Eq. (
5

V^{\prime}\neq 0
so that the contribution from the potential cancels the effect of
\Lambda
.

Now, following the approach of Refs.
Bluhm:2007bd
;
Maluf:2020kgf
;
Yang:2023wtu
, we choose the potential in the form
V=\lambda(B_{\mu\nu}B^{\mu\nu}+b^{2})
, which satisfies the vacuum conditions in Eq. (
10

V^{\prime}(x)\equiv dV(x)/dx=\lambda\,.

(102)

The procedure for obtaining the solutions is identical to the case with a vanishing cosmological constant, and again yields two distinct branches:

Case 1:

\displaystyle h=k-\frac{m}{r^{n-1}}-\frac{2\Lambda r^{2}}{n(n+1)}\,,\quad f=\frac{h}{1-\ell_{1}}\,,

(103)

\displaystyle\beta=\frac{b\sqrt{1-\ell_{1}}}{\sqrt{2}}\,,\quad\lambda=\frac{4\Lambda\ell_{1}}{n(1-\ell_{1})b^{2}}\,,

Case 2:

\displaystyle h=k-\frac{m}{r^{n-1}}-\frac{2\Lambda r^{2}}{n(n+1)(1-\ell_{2})}\,,\quad f=h\,,

(104)

\displaystyle\beta=\frac{b}{\sqrt{2}}\,,\quad\lambda=\frac{2(n+2)\Lambda\ell_{2}}{n(1-\ell_{2})b^{2}}\,.

From these results, we observe that for Case 2, unlike the case with a vanishing cosmological constant,
\ell_{2}
explicitly appears in the metric. This behavior is expected, since in the absence of matter, a conformal transformation of the metric can simultaneously rescale the cosmological constant
\Lambda
into an effective value
\Lambda_{e}
, thereby rendering the spacetime equivalent to GR.

\displaystyle V_{1}

\displaystyle\frac{r_{h}^{n+1}\sqrt{1-\ell_{1}}w_{n}}{n+1}\,,

M=\frac{n}{n-1}TS-\frac{2}{n-1}VP\,.

(115)

For the case with

M=\frac{n}{n+1}TS\,.

(116)

VII
Conclusion

In this work, we revisit EKR gravity, carefully addressing several subtleties overlooked in previous studies. Building on these understandings, we obtain two branches of exact static black hole solutions with general topological horizons in arbitrary dimensions, both in the presence and absence of a cosmological constant. One branch coincides with previously known results in four dimensions, while the other is reported here for the first time. Using the Wald formalism, we compute the Noether mass charge and compare it with earlier results, discussing its implications for observational constraints. In particular, although the gravitational mass definition has a negligible impact on observational constraints in the weak-field regime, it becomes crucial for strong-gravity systems such as black holes and neutron stars, or when interpreting tests dominated by relativistic or strong-field effects
Will:2014kxa
;
Berti:2015itd
. A proper definition of the gravitational mass enables more accurate constraints on spontaneous Lorentz symmetry breaking in such contexts. Our results suggest that many existing studies within EKR gravity merit re-examination. The exact solutions and their associated thermodynamic quantities obtained here provide a foundation for future investigations of spontaneous Lorentz symmetry breaking in the EKR framework.
