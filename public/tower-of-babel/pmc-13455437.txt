Use of distal embolic protection devices as a bailout strategy for suboptimal flow reversal during transcarotid artery revascularization: illustrative case
Authors: Andrew B Koo, Sasha Stogniy, Abdelaziz Amllay, Kshiraj V Talati, Daniela Renedo, Joseph Haynes, Joseph Antonios, Nanthiya Sujijantarat, Ryan Hebert, Charles C Matouk

Transcarotid artery revascularization (TCAR) has been established as a safe and effective surgical option for symptomatic carotid disease.1 The ROADSTER (Reverse Flow Used During Carotid Artery Stenting Procedure) and ROADSTER-2 multicenter trials evaluated the use of dynamic flow reversal during carotid artery stenting (CAS) and reported technical success rates > 96% and 30-day stroke rates < 2%.2–4 Overall, TCAR offers comparable perioperative stroke risks compared to transfemoral CAS (TF-CAS) and carotid endarterectomy with shorter procedural and cross-clamp times.5–7

The TCAR procedure uses a transcarotid approach to carotid revascularization via endovascular balloon angioplasty and stenting, using flow reversal as a distal embolic protection (DEP) strategy. A vascular access sheath is established in the common carotid artery and an extracorporeal shunt to the femoral vein is created, allowing blood flow to be reversed away from cerebral circulation while the carotid plaque is crossed. Without a distal basket or filter used in traditional stenting, adequate angiographic flow reversal is critical to minimize the risk of embolic material traveling distally and causing thromboembolic complications.

Preoperative cross-sectional vascular imaging is critical for identifying patients with elevated risk. A subset of patients with certain anatomical variations in the anterior circulation or with a severe degree of carotid stenosis are not able to tolerate reversal of blood flow during TCAR. In previous trials, 1%–2% of patients have been found to be intolerant to flow reversal, although all procedures were successfully completed. Certain conditions such as a contralateral carotid occlusion or an isolated middle cerebral artery (MCA) can reduce compensatory collateral flow and predispose patients to inadequate cerebral perfusion during flow reversal. In these cases, angiographic findings of contrast stasis at the level of stenosis should heighten suspicion that flow reversal alone may be insufficient. When intolerance to flow reversal cannot be mitigated by reducing the shunt flow rate from high to low intraoperatively, an alternate DEP strategy must be considered.

Illustrative Case

A male patient in his 60s presented to our hospital after experiencing bilateral watershed strokes characterized by increased right upper extremity paresthesia and disequilibrium. On arrival, his National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score was 0. CT angiography (CTA) demonstrated complete occlusion of the left cervical and proximal petrous internal carotid artery (ICA) and 99% stenosis of the proximal right cervical ICA by NASCET (North American Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial) criteria (Fig. 1A and B). The anterior circulation was supplied by an intact circle of Willis (Fig. 1C). Follow-up MRI revealed bilateral watershed distribution infarcts, greater on the left than the right, suggestive of a symptomatic right carotid stenosis in the setting of the chronically occluded left ICA (Fig. 1D). Right-sided carotid revascularization via TCAR was recommended for secondary stroke prevention.

Preoperative CT angiograms and MR image of the brain. A: Image showing right ICA high-grade stenosis. B: The left ICA is chronically occluded. C: Maximum intensity projection CTA reconstruction demonstrating the complete circle of Willis. D: Diffusion-weighted MR image of the brain revealing left greater than right watershed infarct pattern.

The carotid dissection and arterial sheath placement were performed as previously described.8 After carotid sheath placement, selective injection of the right common carotid artery confirmed proper positioning without evidence of dissection or injury. The flow line and controller were then prepped and connected to the venous sheath, establishing an extracorporeal arteriovenous shunt with passive flow reversal. On subsequent selective angiography of the right common carotid artery, poor angiographic flow reversal was noted, as evidenced by contrast stasis at the level of the high-grade stenosis (Fig. 2A). Crossing the lesion solely using this flow reversal strategy was therefore deemed unsafe, and the decision was made to proceed with stenting using a DEP device. Under roadmap guidance and passive flow reversal, we deployed the Emboshield NAV6 Embolic Protection System (Abbott) in the distal cervical segment of the ICA (Fig. 2B). Using a standard “pin and pull” technique, we deployed an ENROUTE stent (10 × 40 mm, Boston Scientific) across the lesion, extending into the common carotid artery (Fig. 2C). No poststent angioplasty was performed. Control runs confirmed an excellent angiographic result with resolution of the observed stasis (Fig. 2D). There were no intraoperative changes observed on neuromonitoring.

Intraoperative angiograms depicting the adjunctive use of a DEP device during the TCAR procedure. A: Control run of the right common carotid artery revealing suboptimal flow reversal secondary to significant proximal ICA stenosis (99%). B: The NAV6 DEP device is deployed prior to balloon angioplasty and carotid stenting. This is performed with antegrade flow. C: The DEP is successfully recaptured through the TCAR approach. D: Final angiographic run demonstrating a patent carotid stent and robust antegrade ICA flow.

Postoperatively, the patient had a full clinical recovery and was discharged home on dual antiplatelet therapy. Subsequent carotid ultrasounds demonstrated a durable, patent stent with improved flow velocities. The patient remained stroke free at the 1-year follow-up.

Discussion

In our illustrative case, flow reversal was likely insufficient due to high-grade carotid stenosis and poor collateral flow secondary to a chronically occluded left ICA, despite the presence of an intact circle of Willis. In this case report, we show that the deployment of an adjunctive DEP system (i.e., filter basket) during TCAR can serve as an effective “bailout” strategy in cases with inadequate neuroprotection, helping to prevent thromboembolic complications by capturing embolic debris.

Anatomical Considerations

One important advantage of TCAR over TF-CAS is the ability to bypass a complex aortic arch, characterized by increased vertical distance between the origin of the brachiocephalic artery and the peak of the arch. In patients with complex arch anatomy, some studies have shown that TCAR can decrease the incidence of postoperative stroke and improve neurological outcomes compared to traditional TF-CAS.9 In a large retrospective analysis comparing carotid access types and embolic protection methods, Hamouda et al. retrospectively evaluated the data of 99,030 patients undergoing CAS and demonstrated that TCAR with flow reversal was associated with significantly lower odds of stroke/death compared to both TF-CAS with DEP (OR 1.62, 95% CI 1.45–1.81; p < 0.001) and transcarotid artery stenting with DEP (OR 1.66, 95% CI 1.15–2.39; p = 0.006).10 Interestingly, by isolating access type as a variable, comparing TCAR against transcarotid stenting with DEP, the authors were able to show that flow reversal itself, independent of arch avoidance, confers a meaningful protection advantage. Furthermore, the concept of safe flow reversal can also be similarly applied in the TF-CAS population. For example, Cappuzzo et al. recently demonstrated the feasibility of combining a balloon guide catheter with a DEP filter and intraoperative robotic transcranial Doppler monitoring to assess for sufficient flow reversal during TF-CAS.11 Overall, these results suggest that adjunctive neuroprotection during carotid stenting may have broader applicability across the different carotid revascularization modalities.

The transcarotid approach can also help bypass any additional tortuous vessel anatomy. However, cases with anatomical ­challenges including poor flow reversal may necessitate longer operative times, which has been associated with a greater risk of adverse effects. For example, in a retrospective review of the data of 19,462 TCAR patients, Naazie et al. demonstrated that flow reversal duration > 10 minutes carried a 37% greater risk of stroke (OR 1.37, 95% CI 1.09–1.73; p = 0.006).12 Therefore, a viable bailout strategy such as adjunctive use of a DEP device may help mitigate these risks during the TCAR procedure. Another important anatomical criterion necessary for TCAR flow reversal is the presence of sufficient collateral circulation, defined by the presence of an ipsilateral posterior communicating artery or anterior communicating artery. Without collateral flow, an isolated MCA can significantly increase the stroke risk during TCAR. For example, in a retrospective analysis of 230 asymptomatic patients, Ray et al. demonstrated that an isolated MCA (present in 11.7% of cases) was associated with a significantly increased risk of an immediate neurological event (OR 25.3, 95% CI 2.5–252.4; p = 0.006).13 A DEP device can also be used in cases of poor collateral blood flow after flow reversal has been established. However, absent collateral circulation can usually be identified on preoperative CTA and would be a relative contraindication for the TCAR approach.

Observations

During our case, flow reversal was minimal as evidenced by stagnant contrast, theoretically increasing the risk of a thromboembolic event once antegrade flow was reestablished. Previous researchers have shown that 1%–2% of patients exhibit intolerance to flow reversal during TCAR—even with intra-operative adjustments such as blood pressure augmentation and switching the TCAR controller to the low-flow toggle.7 This intolerance can manifest as inadequate angiographic flow reversal, hemodynamic instability, or neurological changes. Furthermore, in a study of 71 patients with continuous electroencephalographic monitoring during TCAR, Healy et al. demonstrated that 9 (12.7%) patients experienced perioperative neurological changes, and of those, 1 patient experienced a new postoperative neurological deficit.14 During traditional transfemoral and transradial approaches, crossing the carotid lesion is considered to carry the greatest periprocedural embolic risk. Therefore, in our practice, we routinely use this approach to reduce the risk of distal embolization when flow reversal is lost as an alternative neuroprotective strategy.

In this case lesson, there were no perioperative complications deploying or retrieving the DEP device under anterograde flow through the carotid access sheath. Overall, careful screening and assessment of the patient’s anatomical eligibility and tolerance for flow reversal may improve outcomes and help ensure TCAR success.

Lessons

TCAR has emerged as an important surgical option for secondary stroke prevention in carotid stenosis; however, select patients may not tolerate flow reversal. In these instances, adjunctive use of a DEP device through the TCAR approach can help mitigate the risks of distal embolization and periprocedural stroke while maintaining antegrade flow.
