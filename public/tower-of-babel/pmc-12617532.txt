Outcomes of Ertl Osteomyoplasty Transtibial Amputation With Primary Targeted Muscle Reinnervation and Regenerative Peripheral Nerve Interface
Authors: Edgardo R. Rodriguez-Collazo, Shawn Ward, Jose Antonio Delgado, Matthew Hans, Sitong Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Traditional transtibial amputation (TTA) often results in inadequate residual limb stability and increased risks of phantom limb pain and residual limb pain. The Ertl osteomyoplastic TTA technique with primary targeted muscle reinnervation and regenerative peripheral nerve interface may offer solutions to improve structural stability and reduce peripheral nerve pain. We hypothesize that this advanced TTA technique may reduce phantom limb pain and residual limb pain, while improving residual limb stability, contributing to a higher quality of life compared with traditional methods.Methods:A retrospective review was conducted of 31 patients with 33 total limbs who underwent Ertl osteomyoplastic TTA with primary targeted muscle reinnervation and regenerative peripheral nerve interface during a 6-year period. Patient-reported quality of life outcomes were assessed using the EuroQol 5-dimension 5-level instrument and EuroQol visual analog scale.Results:At a mean follow-up time of 49.3 months, results suggested high quality of life outcomes with an average EuroQol 5-dimension 5-level instrument index score of 0.879 ± 0.231 and a visual analog scale score of 89.36 ± 11.26. Patients reported low levels of pain, minimal psychological distress, and mild limitations in mobility, usual activities, and self-care, indicating favorable physical and emotional outcomes.Conclusions:This case series highlighted the value of this more technically complex procedure, which, despite increased operative time and learning curve, is associated with reduced postoperative pain, enhanced limb stability, and improved psychosocial outcomes. These findings support continued adoption and underscore the need for future comparative studies to establish superiority over traditional techniques.

Takeaways

Question: Can Ertl transtibial amputation with primary targeted muscle reinnervation and regenerative peripheral nerve interface enhance postoperative outcomes in lower extremity amputation patients?

Findings: This case series demonstrated low rates of phantom and residual limb pain alongside high health-related quality of life scores, indicating favorable outcomes.

Meaning: This hybrid transtibial amputation approach shows promising improvements in functional and psychosocial outcomes, highlighting the need for future comparative studies against traditional techniques.

INTRODUCTION

A traditional transtibial amputation (TTA) can be the unfortunate outcome of lower extremity trauma, diabetic complications, peripheral vascular disease, neoplasia, or congenital deformities. Approximately 150,000 nontraumatic leg amputations are performed every year in the United States.1 Among individuals with diabetes, there has been a 50% increase in amputation rate from 2009 to 2015.2 Limb amputation can result in severe functional and psychological disabilities for the patient. These issues can be compounded when patients develop phantom limb pain (PLP) or residual limb pain (RLP), with recorded incidence in the literature ranging from 15% to as high as 85%.3–5 Traditional TTA techniques, such as the widely used Burgess method, often leave patients with a residual limb lacking adequate structural stability.6 In 1939, Dr. Janos Ertl introduced the Ertl procedure, an osteomyoplastic TTA technique that created a tibiofibular distal fusion using a bone graft.7 This approach was designed to yield a more stable residual limb. Despite the improved functional benefits provided by the Ertl procedure, patients continued to experience chronic neuropathic pain due to neuroma formation postamputation. To address the neuropathic component of postamputation pain, advances in microsurgery have introduced targeted muscle reinnervation (TMR) and regenerative peripheral nerve interface (RPNI) techniques. TMR involves rerouting transected peripheral nerves to motor nerves of adjacent muscles, promoting organized nerve regeneration, therefore reducing the risk of neuroma formation. RPNI uses free muscle tissue grafts implanted around transected nerve endings. These grafts mitigate nerve irritation and control reinnervation. Both techniques have demonstrated promising results in reducing PLP and RLP.8–13 By combining the Ertl TTA technique with primary TMR and RPNI, this hybrid approach enables surgeons to address both the functional and neuropathic challenges associated with amputation. The purpose of this case series was to explore the incidence of PLP or RLP in patients undergoing transtibial amputations using the Ertl technique with primary TMR and RPNI at the time of amputation. Considering that the Ertl procedure with primary TMR and RPNI is more technically complex compared with traditional TTA techniques, this study aimed to provide data and reasoning to underscore the enhanced physiological and psychological benefits for current and future patients.

METHODS

Thirty-one patients with 33 total limbs (23 men, 8 women) underwent Ertl TTA with primary TMR and RPNI for nonreconstructable pathology. Indications included trauma (n = 4), Charcot neuroarthropathy (n = 9), progressive infection with osteomyelitis (n = 11), and peripheral nerve pathology (n = 11). Two patients required bilateral amputations. The average patient age was 53 years (range: 32–75 y) (Table 1). Of the cohort, 52% (n = 17) had a history of diabetes mellitus, and 9% (n = 3) had a history of smoking (Table 1). Health-related quality of life was assessed using the EuroQol 5-dimension 5-level instrument (EQ-5D-5L). EQ-5D-5L is a standardized patient-reported outcome measure developed by the EuroQol Group. The instrument evaluates 5 dimensions of health status: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. For each dimension, participants selected a severity level based on their symptoms, which corresponded to a numerical score. Severity level options included: no problems (1), slight problems (2), moderate problems (3), severe problems (4), or extreme problems (5). At an average follow-up time of 43.9 months (range: 5–96 mo), surveys were administered to patients through phone interviews using a standardized script. Responses across all 5 dimensions generated a 5-digit health state. Health states can range from 11111 to 55555, indicating best and worst health, respectively.14,15 Obtained health states were then translated to an EQ-5D-5L index value using the US-specific value set. Index scores range from −0.59 to 1. Scores less than 0 correspond to a quality of life considered to be worse than death. Scores closer to 1 indicate ideal health. In addition, patients were asked to rate their overall health on the EQ visual analogue scale (EQ-VAS). This instrument allows patients to self-report their perceived health on a scale ranging from 0 (worst health you can imagine) to 100 (best health you can imagine). EQ-5D-5L and EQ-VAS instruments were selected due to their validation, ease of use, and relevance to amputation patient populations. These instruments assess both physical and psychosocial domains affected by limb loss. Scoring is based on location-specific value sets, ensuring population-appropriate interpretation. The standardized format allows for consistent administration and reliable interpretation across patients. The mean and SD of both EQ-5D-5L index scores and EQ-VAS scores were calculated using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA) to summarize postoperative outcomes of the cohort. Proper licensing for the use of EQ-5D-5L and EQ-VAS was obtained from the EuroQol Research Group.

SURGICAL PROCEDURE

Initial Setup and Dissection

The patient was placed in a supine position with a slight lateral hip bump. A fishmouth incision was planned and outlined, ensuring a long posterior flap. Osteotomy sites were marked 21 cm distal to the tibial tuberosity, where the transtibial and transfibular osteotomies were performed (Fig. 1). The fishmouth incision was made with a no. 10 or no. 15 blade through the subcutaneous tissue. Dissection was performed systematically in a circumferential fashion from anteromedial to lateral aspect of the leg to expose the saphenous, superficial peroneal, deep peroneal, and sural nerve. The anterior, lateral, and posterior compartments of the leg were then exposed. Moving from the lateral face of the tibia, the tibial and fibular osteotomy levels were identified. The anterior and lateral compartment muscles, deep peroneal nerve, superficial peroneal nerve, and vein and arteries were all identified. Posterior dissection of the tibia and fibula was performed to protect the deep compartment neurovascular structures. Using a C-arm and sagittal saw, the transtibial and transfibular osteotomies were performed. Transection of the anterior and lateral compartment muscles was performed. The deep and superficial posterior compartments were dissected away from the distal tibia and fibula, exposing the peroneal artery and the posterior tibial artery, vein, and nerve. These structures are transected or ligated at appropriate lengths along with the deep leg compartment muscles, as well as the soleus and gastrocnemius muscles. The distal limb was then removed.

Ertl Procedure

The distance between the tibia and fibula was measured and fashioned to receive a bone graft. The Ertl graft was taken from the proximal fibula. The typical graft size needed to create this fusion is 1.0–1.5 cm. The Ertl procedure was then performed with C-arm guidance. The fusion hardware was used per surgeon preference. A guide pin was placed from the anterior face of the tibia through the graft and into the fibula (Fig. 2), and then a 4.0-mm cannulated screw was placed (Fig. 3,16 Fig. 4). In this series, a 4.0-mm cannulated screw or flexible suture button was used. Flexible or rigid fixation was used in this series.

TMR/RPNI/Nerve Transfers

The nerve procedures were performed under loupe magnification. Specific nerve approach variability exists due to anatomy and availability. Superficially, the saphenous and sural nerves were identified, and an RPNI procedure was performed (Figs. 5, 617). The superficial peroneal and deep peroneal nerves underwent a TMR procedure performed using the peroneus brevis or longus muscles and the anterior tibial muscle, respectively (Fig. 718) (Table 2). The posterior tibial nerve has several options depending on availability and anatomy. It can be transferred to a nerve allograft to control a stump neuroma. Alternatively, another approach used is to separate the nerve into 2 separate bundles. Each bundle has a TMR procedure performed to the posterior tibial muscle or soleus muscle, respectively.

Myoplasty

The myoplasty and myodesis were performed to provide a stable and balanced distal stump. The peroneal brevis muscle was rotated medially toward the posterior tibial muscle, which was rotated laterally (Fig. 819). The anterior tibial muscle was rotated distally and medially over the distal end of the Ertl fusion. These muscles were sutured appropriately into position. Within the posterior flap, the soleus and fascial layers were mobilized and rotated superiorly to the anterior distal tibia and periosteum. This completes a robust distal stump (Fig. 9). The fishmouth fasciocutaneous flap was then approximated and closed. Layered closure of the incision was performed, and dog ears were excised as needed. A drain was placed at the surgeon’s discretion; however, in our experience, it is not always needed. Postoperatively, care included standard dressings and compressive ACE bandaging. A long leg posterior splint was applied to maintain knee extension and distal stump positioning. All patients were admitted for observation.

RESULTS

During a 6-year period, 33 Ertl osteomyoplastic TTA were performed with primary TMR and RPNI. At a mean follow-up time of 49.3 months (range: 5–96 mo), patient-reported quality of life outcomes were assessed using the EQ-5D-5L and EQ-VAS health-related quality of life instruments. The mean EQ-5D-5L index score was 0.879 ± 0.231, and the mean EQ-VAS score reported was 89.36 ± 11.26 (Table 3). Overall, both scores reflect a high health status among the patient cohort. Across the 5 EQ-5D-5L dimensions, patients reported favorable outcomes. The average score for pain and discomfort was lowest at 1.182, with 91% of patients reporting no pain symptoms (Table 3). Similarly, the average level score for anxiety/depression was 1.212, reflecting minimal to no psychological complications in our cohort. The average scores for self-care were 1.424, mobility 1.515, and usual activities 1.542, which were slightly higher but still well below the threshold for moderate-level impairment (Table 3). Only a small number of patients reported moderate or severe problems in any of the categories, most commonly mobility and usual activities. Results showcase that the combination of Ertl TTA with primary TMR and RPNI not only seems to support physical function but also emotional well-being, contributing to strong long-term outcomes. Postoperative complications were observed in 13 out of a total of 33 patients, representing a 39% complication rate (Table 4). These complications were classified as either minor or major. Minor complications (n = 5) included stump wound dehiscence, and all were managed with local wound care. Major complications (n = 8) consisted of either advancement flap revisions or hardware revisions. All complications observed were successfully treated and did not require a more proximal revisional amputation.

DISCUSSION

With approximately 150,000 patients undergoing a lower extremity amputation every year in the United States, reports of PLP and RLP are projected to dramatically increase.20,21 These pain syndromes not only affect patients from a physiological standpoint but also impair quality of life, daily activity, and prosthetic use.8,22 On this basis, surgical strategies that address both residual limb stability and peripheral nerve complications are needed.

Although the Ertl TTA procedure alone has been associated with increased residual limb stability outcomes, the addition of TMR and RPNI addresses the management of neuroma formation leading to decreased chronic pain. Our study demonstrated that the integration of Ertl TTA with primary TMR and RPNI may provide a comprehensive approach to optimizing patient outcomes. This hybrid method of combining Ertl TTA with TMR and RPNI at the time of amputation can create a highly functional residual limb with minimal to no PLP and RLP.8,23

However, appropriate patient selection is critical when considering this surgical approach. In our experience, it is most appropriate for medically stable patients, patients with adequate soft tissue coverage, preserved vascular supply, and strong functional or prosthetic goals. These anatomical and motivational factors are crucial to ensuring successful healing and long-term benefits.

In traditional methods, such as Burgess TTA without the use of TMR and RPNI, PLP and RLP commonly occur early during the postamputation period due to the trauma that the nerves endure.24 The disrupted neural signals caused by the missing limb eventually begin to form neuromas leading to hyperexcitability and spontaneous discharges causing PLP and RLP symptoms.24 Through TMR and RPNI, transected peripheral nerves are provided with organized reinnervation pathways, preventing disorganized growth that may lead to neuroma formation.9

TMR involves coapting transected nerve endings to nearby motor branches, allowing for organized nerve reinnervation. The technique is best suited for large mixed motor or sensory nerves. An added benefit of TMR is the enhancement of myoelectric prosthesis control. By redirecting larger transected nerves into reinnervated muscle targets, TMR has the potential to create organized, high-amplitude electromyographic signals, improving the signal-to-noise ratio. These robust signals improve prosthesis functionality and intuitiveness.25 RPNI involves the implantation of transected nerve endings onto small grafts of free muscle tissue. Muscle grafts can be harvested from healthy tissue within the amputated limb or from alternative donor sites.26 Over time, these grafts revascularize and reinnervate, acting as a nerve sink for efferent signals preventing neuromas. RPNI is best used on smaller nerve branches or in scenarios where no suitable motor targets are available. Given that TMR is more time-intensive compared with RPNI, its use is best reserved for larger mixed motor or sensory nerves, whereas RPNI remains ideal for smaller nerve branches or when suitable motor targets are unavailable. When used in combination, these techniques allow for comprehensive management of all transected nerve endings in the residual limb. Together, these methods have shown great promise in preventing neuroma formation and limiting PLP and RLP.8–13 In a separate study, Nees et al27 reported outcomes of PLP and RLP of patients requiring TTA. Data used in their study were obtained from the Heidelberg amputation study, which reported that 51% of patients experienced PLP and 49% experienced RLP.27 Results from our case series indicated that only 9% of patients (n = 3) experienced PLP and RLP symptoms, further underscoring the value of our techniques (Table 3).

In addition to improving nerve-related pain outcomes, a study by Wee et al28 demonstrated that the combined use of TMR and RPNI reduced postoperative opioid consumption in amputation patients. These findings not only underscore the efficacy of these techniques but also suggest a role in mitigating long-term dependence on narcotics. Reduced need for postoperative narcotic use after TMR and RPNI supports broader efforts to combat the ongoing opioid crisis.28

In addition to addressing nerve-related pain, the Ertl TTA enhances the stability and function of the residual limb. The tibiofibular fusion supports muscle preservation, distal perfusion, and a stable distal platform. This increased structural stability then has the potential to facilitate better prosthetic integration and function. Previous studies comparing Ertl to traditional Burgess techniques for TTA have found that patients undergoing the Ertl procedure are most commonly classified into more advanced prosthesis classes.29 The K3 prosthetic class typically grants amputation patients the ability to traverse most environmental barriers, maintain employment, exercise, and participate in sports or recreation activities.30 The K4 prosthetic class typically grants amputation patients high levels of mobility and the ability to perform activities that involve high impact, stress, and energy levels.30

In surgical interventions where the Ertl TTA with primary TMR and RPNI is performed, the improved residual limb stability paired with minimal to no PLP or RLP symptoms may significantly enhance quality of life and independence. Our mean EQ-5D-5L index score (0.879 ± 0.231) and mean EQ-VAS score (89.36 ± 11.26) support this statement. Because amputations are commonly associated with negative psychological symptoms such as grief, anxiety, and depression, Ertl with primary TMR and RPNI may hold potential in relieving the psychological drawbacks of amputation.31

Although some surgeons argue that the Ertl procedure increases surgical complexity and operative duration that may not justify its benefits, our findings suggest otherwise. The enhanced physiological and psychological outcomes observed in this case series underscore the procedure’s value. Even without a formal comparison group, the substantial improvements in pain, function, and psychosocial health observed through our results emphasize the value of this hybrid approach. Although our study design is descriptive, the favorable outcomes across the cohort support the potential efficacy of combining Ertl TTA with primary TMR and RPNI. Although external studies offer general reference points, we do not have institution-level data available for patients undergoing traditional TTA techniques, limiting internal comparison. Our results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and provide a foundation for future studies formally evaluating traditional methods and our hybrid approach.

Our cohort demonstrated a total complication rate of 39%. Wound dehiscence complications were classified as minor and represented 38% of the total complications observed. All minor complications were successfully treated with local wound care. The remaining 62% of total complications observed were classified as major, requiring advancement flap revision and/or hardware revision. Although major complications did require secondary surgery, none of the complications required proximal revisional amputations. In a study by Tintle et al,32 comparing the outcomes of Ertl TTA with traditional Burgess TTA techniques in a military population, higher complication rates were reported with the Ertl technique compared with the traditional Burgess technique. Although at first glance the results may seem contradictory, the Ertl group demonstrated improved ambulation and functional outcomes, supporting functional advantages that may justify the complication risks.

Overall, our findings support the Ertl TTA with primary TMR and RPNI as a promising advancement in TTA techniques. This hybrid method addresses both structural and neurological complications, offering enhanced quality of life outcomes. Although our complication rate reflects the technical demands of the approach, it remains within acceptable ranges and resulted in no proximal revisional amputations. Importantly, the favorable patient outcomes observed in our study suggest that the benefits may outweigh surgical complexity. Our findings support the continued adoption of this approach and underscore the importance of future comparative studies that may more definitively establish superiority over traditional TTA techniques.

CONCLUSIONS

This case series demonstrates that the integration of Ertl TTA with primary TMR and RPNI offers advantages in residual limb stability, neuropathic pain reduction, and overall quality of life. Although technically demanding, our findings suggest this approach yields enhanced long-term outcomes for patients. These results serve as a hypothesis-generating platform for future comparative studies evaluating this hybrid technique against traditional methods.
