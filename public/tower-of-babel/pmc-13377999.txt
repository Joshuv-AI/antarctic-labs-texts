Preferences of older Australians for fall prevention exercise program features: a discrete choice experiment
Authors: Alexander Chye, Thomas Lung, Kim Delbaere, Kimberley S. van Schooten, Morag Taylor, Meghan Ambrens, Kirsten Howard, Blake Angell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFalls are a major cause of injury, disability and health service use among older Australians. While exercise-based interventions can reduce falls, participation remains low. Understanding preferences for program features may inform design and implementation of fall prevention exercise programs to maximise uptake, adherence and impact.MethodsAn online discrete choice experiment was conducted with 383 community-dwelling Australians aged ≥ 60 years, independent in daily activities and not currently enrolled in a fall prevention program. Participants completed choice tasks with varying levels of six program attributes: cost per class, delivery mode, class size, personalisation, program duration and access to professional support. Data was analysed using mixed multinomial logit and latent class models to estimate preferences, willingness to pay and preference heterogeneity. Policy simulations estimated predicted uptake under alternative service configurations.ResultsParticipants generally preferred lower-cost, individual programs of short duration (1–3 months) delivered in-person at home with on-demand professional support. There was no significant preference for personalisation. Latent class analysis identified three subclasses: “opt-out” (19%), “cost-sensitive” (37%) and “support-oriented” (44%). Policy simulations showed high uptake in the support-oriented class and low uptake in the opt-out class regardless of configuration and greater responsiveness to cost reductions among the cost-sensitive class.ConclusionsPreferences for fall prevention program features at point of uptake vary across identifiable older Australian subclasses. Offering flexible configurations, including low-cost options for cost-sensitive participants and enhanced features for those willing to pay, may improve program uptake and long-term sustainability. These findings provide actionable evidence to inform program design and policy decisions for scalable fall prevention initiatives.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07602-8.

Background

Falls and fall-related injuries are a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in older people [1]. With an ageing population, preventing falls is a health and economic priority, with treatment of people over 65 years who had fallen costing AUD$3.3 billion (2022-23) [2]. A Cochrane review demonstrated that community-based exercise could prevent 29% of falls in older people [3]. However, exercise program adherence is imperfect, estimated at 21% (home-based) and 69% (group-exercise) [4].

A systematic review of adherence to fall-prevention exercise programs found that location, session duration, exercise type, frequency, intensity, and supervision influenced participation [5]. A systematic review [6] and associated best worst scaling study [7] found that older people participating in physical activity derived the highest utility from: home-based exercise without transport, 60% improvement in ability to do daily tasks at home, no costs, and decreasing chances of falling to 0%. Another study analysing personal preferences of older people for fall prevention programs found that location, exercising alone versus in a group and cost were important [8]. To design a sustainable, motivating, and cost-effective exercise program for older people, it is clearly important to understand what factors influence ongoing adherence and engagement to fall prevention programs.

Equally, it is important to first understand how participants initially choose fall prevention programs, which components they most value and trade-offs they are willing to make.

Discrete choice experiments (DCE) are a stated preference survey technique, which can improve understanding of consumer preferences at the point of choice [9]. A DCE [10] of exercise programs to prevent falls among older people found 82% did not consider exercise program participation worthwhile, even if exercise reduced risk of falling to 0% (the study concluded this was due to insufficient awareness of fall prevention). A DCE of culturally specific fall prevention programs for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islanders [11] found participants preferred culturally specific services (willingness to pay [WTP] AUD$1.76 compared to mainstream sessions) and lower out-of-pocket costs. Transport provision did not significantly influence service choice [11].

Latent class analysis (LCA) extends DCE analysis by identifying heterogeneity in consumer preferences and assumes that distinct ‘classes’ exist within the sample, which may vary by demographic or other characteristics [9]. This can help tailor interventions to differing preferences of specific population subgroups (i.e. different WTP for different attributes), so interventions are not ‘one-size-fits-all’. Previous studies have stratified older people at risk of falls [12] and preferences of older people with chronic disease for models of care [13] and outcomes [14]. However, to our knowledge there has not been previous work studying how preferences for fall prevention programs may vary among older people.

A randomised control trial (RCT) over 2015–2019 aimed to explore whether an e-Health balance and exercise program, StandingTall, could prevent falls in community-living older people [15]. Whilst fall rates were not statistically different between intervention and control groups after 12 months; over 24 months the intervention group had 16% less falls, and 20% less injurious falls, compared to control. Notably, adherence was 40% over six months and 30% over 24 months, outperforming the Cochrane review (21% for home-based programs) [3]. A process evaluation of StandingTall (2019–2022) investigated its implementation in Australia and the UK [16]. StandingTall was acceptable to most older people and after six months, 80% rated StandingTall good or excellent [16]. A qualitative study [17] explored the experiences and preferences of older people using StandingTall. Participants valued flexibility and convenience but were frustrated by regular assessments and exercise repetition [17]. Finally, an economic evaluation found an average cost of $896 per person for StandingTall, with an incremental cost effectiveness of $58,039-$110,698 per quality adjusted life year [18]. Subgroup analysis found improved cost effectiveness for males, people < 80 years, and with a history of falls [18].

Given these findings, we conducted a DCE aiming to identify the most important features of fall prevention programs for community-living older people that influence program uptake and trade-offs they are willing to make between different components. To our knowledge, this is the first DCE including attributes reflecting the option of an e-Health fall prevention program. LCA then enables us to identify the preferences of heterogeneous groups of older people and inform how these preferences influence program uptake. With the economic evaluation, this will improve understanding of design, sustainability and implementation of StandingTall and similar fall prevention programs for older people.

Methods

Participants

A DCE survey was administered by PureProfile (panel provider) to online participants in Australia. Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 60 years, living in the community, independent in activities of daily living and able to walk household distances without walking aids. Exclusion criteria were unstable or acute medical conditions precluding exercise participation, progressive neurological conditions (e.g. Parkinson’s disease) and current participation in fall prevention programs. Inclusion and exclusion criteria matched the original StandingTall study [15]. We aimed to match the demographic distribution from the StandingTall trial for age, gender and living alone [15], with a state distribution to match the Australian population [19]. A sample size of 300 was targeted to match previous DCEs in fall prevention [10] and other areas of healthcare [20], enabling sufficient sample size to explore preference heterogeneity. The original StandingTall trial was registered on ANZCTR (ACTRN12619000540112) on April 4th 2019.

Survey

Each participant completed twelve choice tasks, with one task comprising two unlabelled alternatives for a fall prevention program, A and B; and asking participants to choose between A, B or neither (opt-out alternative). Participants were told prior to choosing that the average annual risk of falling was 40% (approximated from the StandingTall control group [15]), and that a fall prevention program (balance exercises for two-hours/week) may reduce risk of falling by 16% over two years [15].

Attributes and levels

Attributes and levels were chosen after reviewing existing qualitative and quantitative StandingTall analyses [17] and the fall prevention literature [6, 10]. We identified six attributes: cost per class, mode of delivery, class size, personalisation, program duration and access to professional support. Levels were derived through literature review and author consensus. We generated one choice set with twelve tasks and ran a pilot survey with 43 participants to assess attributes, levels and ease of completion. From the pilot, we calculated priors to generate three blocks with twelve tasks each, randomising participants to each block. We completed the final survey with 383 participants. Table 1 presents attributes and levels in the DCE. A sample choice set is in Appendix 1. There was no additional description of the attributes and levels beyond what was supplied in Table 1, consistent with the aim of presenting choices in a concise and realistic manner.

Statistical analysis

A mixed multinomial logit regression modelled choice of fall prevention program as a function of the attributes. Attributes were estimated as random parameters with normal distributions. Cost was included as a continuous variable, and remaining attributes were effects coded as factor variables. Conditional logit model was used to identify any differences between blocks (Appendix 3). Interactions were tested in sensitivity analysis (Appendix 4) with cost and gender; cost and healthcare concession card; cost and fall in the previous year; group and personalised; online and scheduled support; and online and on demand support. Coefficients were converted to odds ratios (OR). An OR greater than 1 indicates a preference for an exercise program with that level (vice-versa). Point estimates for WTP were calculated using the ratio of attribute and cost coefficients.

Subsequently, a latent class logit model was used to identify preference heterogeneity across three classes. Two and four classes were also modelled (Appendix 5); however, we assessed three classes to have the best trade-off between class size, interpretability of results and goodness of fit. Latent class posterior probability was then used to predict class membership for each individual in the dataset. From class membership we could understand the demographic characteristics (age, gender, living in a city, living alone, education, having a healthcare card and falls in the last year) of each class and test differences across classes with one-way ANOVA. For further class validation, a multinomial logistic regression model was used to compare the relative likelihood of class membership based on these demographic characteristics.

Finally, latent class posterior probability prediction was used to illustrate the predicted uptake of different program configurations based on StandingTall for the overall cohort and individual classes. StandingTall is currently a subscription model of $AUD30 per month, and assuming twice per week use for one-hour (two-hours per week recommended), we assume 8–9 classes per month, resulting in an approximate real-world cost of $AUD3.50 per class. Our baseline configuration of StandingTall was online, at home, individual, standardised, medium term (4–6 months) with scheduled support. Modifications to program configurations were chosen using a person-centred approach, considering how to optimise uptake given different preference profiles for each class. Analysis was completed in STATA 18.0.

The ‘cost-sensitive’ class (37%) preferred participation in a fall prevention program rather than none (OR 0.27 [95% CI 0.21–0.35]) and cheaper programs (OR 0.84 [95% CI 0.82–0.86]). They preferred individual over group programs (OR 1.53 [95% CI 1.35–1.75], WTP $2.40) and short-term (1–3 months) relative to long-term (7–12 month) programs (OR 1.23 [95% CI 1.03–1.46], WTP $1.14). They disliked personalisation relative to a standard program (OR 0.86 [95% CI 0.81–1.77], WTP -$0.82) and preferred on-demand support to no support (OR 1.22 [95% CI 1.03–1.43], WTP $1.10).

In contrast, the ‘support-oriented’ class (44%) were less cost sensitive (OR 0.95 [95% CI 0.95–0.96]). This class preferred in-person, at home classes (OR 1.25 [95% CI 1.13–1.39], WTP $4.84) relative to online or outside home. This class also preferred individual relative to group programs (OR 1.14 [95% CI 1.06–1.22], WTP $2.77). Relative to no direct support, this class preferred both scheduled (OR 1.18 [95% CI 1.07–1.29], WTP $3.53) and on-demand support (OR 1.18 [95% CI 1.08–1.28], WTP $3.54).

Analysis of class demographics (Appendix 6) found statistically significant differences between tertiary education (32% for ‘opt-out’, compared to 56% for ‘cost-sensitive’ and 60% for ‘support-oriented’) and experiencing a fall in the previous year (19% of ‘opt-out’, compared to 25% of ‘cost-sensitive’ and 38% of ‘support-oriented’). Class multinomial logistic regression (Appendix 7) supported demographic findings and showed ‘opt-out’ and ‘cost-sensitive’ were more likely to be male than ‘support-oriented’.

Figure 1 illustrates program uptake under different configurations of StandingTall. At baseline, we can see that the ‘support-oriented’ class has the highest predicted participation at 94%, followed by the ‘cost-sensitive’ class at 76%, with the ‘opt-out’ class only at 2%.

From latent class analysis (Table 4), we modified the baseline program configuration (online, at home, individual, standardised, medium term (4–6 months) with scheduled support) to optimise uptake for each class. A ‘Baseline (Subsidised)’ program reducing cost to $0 was targeted at the ‘opt-out’ class, however this did not significantly change their uptake. A short-term ‘No-Frills’ program at lower cost ($1.75) was targeted at the ‘cost-sensitive’ class given their preference for a short duration program and lower cost, which increased participation by 8% compared to baseline. A ‘Support-Oriented’ program at higher cost ($7) with additional features (personalisation, on-demand support) did not significantly change participation of the ‘support-oriented’ class.

Overall, the ‘support-oriented’ class’ program uptake was least sensitive to any change in program features, whilst the ‘cost-sensitive’ class was the most sensitive. A $3.50 change in per class cost changes the overall probability of uptake by +/-5% and has little (< 1%) effect on ‘opt-out’ and ‘support-oriented’ classes but significantly changes the ‘cost-sensitive’ class uptake (+ 10% to 86% uptake if free, -13% to 63% uptake if cost increases to $7.00 per class).

Discussion

Fall prevention programs for older people should be designed to maximise utility and participation given limited resources and need for adherence to improve health outcomes. This DCE has presented the preferences of 383 community-dwelling older Australians for fall prevention programs, showing a general preference for completing a fall prevention program, with lower monetary cost, one-on-one, in person, at home, short-term (1–3 months) and available professional support.

A similar Australian study of community-living older people (≥ 60 years) in a best-worst scaling choice experiment for fall prevention programs [7], found that the highest utility attributes were home-based exercise and no monetary costs, aligning with our findings. A study of 6,949 community-dwelling older Australians (≥ 65 years) investigated participation and economic benefits from 12 weekly low-to moderate-intensity group exercise classes, in person or online during the COVID-19 pandemic [21]. Factors associated with higher class attendance included a free trial class (potentially aligning with our cost findings) and online classes [21]. In contrast, our study found a preference for in person classes at home compared to online classes; however, participants may have interpreted this as supported home-based programs rather than an explicit rejection of digital fall prevention programs. This interpretation aligns with our study’s post-pandemic context, where people increasingly value convenience and flexible delivery. We also note that our study examined preferences at the point of choice rather than ongoing adherence. The digital literacy of older people may also have influenced preferences at the point of uptake; and initial training may also change preferences for online. Subsequent economic analysis [22] noted exercise subsidies are important for older people’s participation, particularly with low incomes (supporting our sensitivity analysis finding that a healthcare concession card [low income] increased cost sensitivity), and may increase group engagement e.g. regional/rural communities, and multiple comorbidities.

Bartmentloo et al. (2020) examined older people’s (≥ 65) preferences for nine different fall prevention programs, finding 49% preferred home-based programs and 43% preferred external locations [8], aligning with our sample’s preference for in-person exercise at-home, particularly the support-oriented class. 46% preferred individual programs and 44% preferred group programs [8], whereas we found a preference for individual programs. They found 51% were willing to pay, but 41% would only participate if free [8]. A systematic review of fall prevention preferences in community-living older people found preferences for group or individual delivery vary, but individual balance/resistance programs were preferred over group programs with high certainty, supporting our findings [23].

A previous systematic review of personally tailored exercises and older people’s physical outcomes found no advantage of personalisation in improving exercise adherence and economic benefits [24]. While earlier qualitative studies of StandingTall users [16] highlighted personalisation with tailored progression as a key feature supporting engagement, our community-based DCE found it was not a significant attribute driving program uptake at the point of choice. This may reflect limited understanding of personalised exercise programs, particularly among older people with less health literacy or familiarity with structured balance training. It may also reflect differences between the surveyed StandingTall users (analysing exercise adherence) and our community sample (without StandingTall experience and analysing program uptake). A Swedish study (n = 67) comparing a self-directed digital fall prevention program (Safestep) with a paper booklet (Otago Home Exercises) found greater satisfaction among digital users, who valued motivational feedback from a virtual physiotherapist [25]. This may support our participants’ preference for on demand support, particularly in our sensitivity analysis which showed that WTP for online classes increased with on-demand support.

Our findings suggest StandingTall may benefit from offering flexible configurations and tiered features to maximise initial program uptake by meeting the needs of different groups, enabling ‘cost-sensitive’ exercisers to participate, and ‘support-oriented’ exercisers to access desired features. Our finding that older people reporting a fall in the last year were more represented as exercisers reflects previous literature suggesting fear of falling or previous falls was associated with greater engagement in fall prevention programs [26]. Bartmentloo et al. (2020) found two subgroups: 27% preferred a program at an external location, in a group, for a fee; and 26% preferred a program at home, one-on-one, no cost [8]. There was not a statistically significant preference for, or against, online programs and so StandingTall may need to provide other features with greater WTP to increase program take-up. Class subgroup analysis (Appendix 6–7) showed that the ‘support-oriented’ class was more likely to be female, tertiary educated, live with others, and report falls in the past year; compared to the ‘cost-sensitive’ and ‘opt-out’ classes. This highlights the importance of demographics when designing exercise programs, to increase appeal for specific groups of older people. These findings suggest that home-based programs offering flexibility, affordability and optional professional support align closely with user preferences and are well positioned for broader implementation. Notably, the small size of the ‘opt-out’ class (19%) suggests most older people were willing to consider participation in fall prevention when programs aligned with their preferences.

Policy simulation showed predicted uptake for the ‘support-oriented’ class remained stable regardless of configuration changes, suggesting strong intrinsic motivation. Similarly, the ‘opt-out’ class showed consistently low participation. In contrast, the ‘cost-sensitive’ class showed greater sensitivity to cost and support features. Recognising the need for person-tailored programs and adaptable delivery models are particularly important for improving participation rates, which can be seen for the ‘cost-sensitive’ class.

Limitations

There were several limitations in generalising our work to StandingTall and the Australian population. Our sample was younger on average (mean 71.6 years) compared to the StandingTall trial (mean 77.4 years), which may reflect our online survey (compared to StandingTall’s community recruitment) [15]. Younger people may participate less in fall prevention programs due to better balance (real or perceived). Nonetheless our sample population rate of at least one fall in the past year (30%) is similar to other Australian studies of older adults [27]. Our work has not considered other demographic characteristics that may influence program uptake, including cultural and ethnic diversity, the social or physical environment, loneliness or other disadvantage, which may be an area of future research. Our WTP estimates imply a linear relationship between cost and utility, which may not be the case. Our study was not completed with previous StandingTall participants; hence results reflect general preferences rather than specifically StandingTall. The proposed reduction in falls (16% over two years) from StandingTall may also not represent older people’s preference for reduction in falls risk. Previous analysis of smallest worthwhile effects of older people’s exercise program participation has estimated absolute falls risk reduction of 16%-35% [10]. For brevity, our DCE did not have in-depth explanations of each attribute. As a result, respondents may have differed in their interpretation of certain attributes, including personalisation, which may have reduced the salience of these attributes at the point of choice and contributed to null findings. Finally, DCEs reflect stated preference under hypothetical conditions at the point of choosing a program, which may differ from real-world program uptake as well as preferences for features that support sustained participation and adherence.

Implications for implementation

The identification of distinct preference profiles highlights the potential value of tailoring fall prevention programs to optimise program uptake across different segments of older people. For example, cost-sensitive participants may benefit from shorter, lower-cost configurations supported by subsidies or integration into existing services, whereas those willing to pay for enhanced features may be offered additional support or in-person delivery. Such flexible program designs could be implemented through modular or tiered approaches, allowing participants to select features that best meet their needs while maintaining sustainable delivery. Implementing such approaches within existing community and digital delivery pathways may facilitate scalable and equitable access. It is also important to consider fall prevention as multifactorial in the wider context of older people’s physical, mental and cognitive health; and understand how other wellbeing programs (e.g. social prescribing, community groups) may synergise with programs such as StandingTall. Apart from fall prevention programs, policy makers should consider the upstream social determinants of falls including the emergence of frailty, to enable a multidisciplinary approach to management of the complications of senescence. Future research (trials and qualitative studies) should evaluate whether stated preferences translate into actual behaviour in real-world settings, examine the cost-effectiveness of different configurations and analyse how programs can support long-term engagement and adherence.

Conclusions

This study provides the first DCE evidence for preferences of fall prevention programs with an e-Health option. In general, surveyed older Australians preferred to participate in low-cost, individual, short-term programs in-person at home, with on-demand professional support. Latent class analysis revealed subclasses with different priorities, and tailoring program configurations to preferences may improve uptake, particularly for a ‘cost-sensitive’ class. By providing detailed estimates of preference patterns and WTP across different subclasses, this study offers practical guidance for design of fall prevention solutions and digital delivery models that align with older people’s needs and expectations to maximise program uptake. Future research should investigate how preferences may change during a fall prevention program to ensure longer term adherence and outcomes.
