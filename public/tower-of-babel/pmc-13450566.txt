Genome-wide identification of GRAS gene family in Camellia oleifera and expression analysis under abiotic stress
Authors: Panpan Zhang, Bingyang Peng, Yue Jiao, Dan Huang, Wenyong Zhan, Shuang Xu, Jiabin Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCamellia oleifera, a member of the family Theaceae and the genus Camellia, is an evergreen shrub or small tree that holds considerable economic value. It is primarily found in the hilly regions of southern China. The GRAS transcription factor family plays a crucial role in various biological processes in plants, including tissue development, fruit maturation, and responses to environmental stresses. Introduction

Transcription factors are a class of DNA-binding proteins that regulate gene transcription by specifically binding to cis-acting elements in the promoter regions of eukaryotic genes, often through interactions with other proteins. The GRAS family represents a significant group of plant transcription factors that play crucial roles in plant growth and development, as well as in responses to abiotic stresses [1]. Previous studies have identified the GRAS gene family exclusively in higher plants [2]. Members of this family possess unique GRAS domains, with some also containing DELLA protein structures. These domains are closely associated with various physiological processes, including plant growth, metabolism, and stress adaptation.

The acronym “GRAS” originates from the characteristic initials of its three initially identified members: GAI (GIBBERELLIN INSENSITIVE), RGA (REPRESSOR OF GAL-3), and SCR (SCARECROW) [3]. Proteins within the GRAS family generally comprise between 400 and 700 amino acid residues. Although the length and sequence of these residues display considerable variation—primarily attributable to the structural diversity of their N-terminal regions—the C-terminal domains remain relatively conserved. The N-terminal domain functions as a critical module in GRAS family proteins. As intrinsically disordered proteins in plants, GRAS members demonstrate versatile roles by specifically interacting with various target proteins, thereby mediating distinct biological functions [4]. The C-terminal GRAS domain is highly conserved and contains five key motifs arranged sequentially as follows: LHRI (leucine heptad repeat I), VHIID, LHRII (leucine heptad repeat II), PFYRE, and SAW [5]. Among these, the VHIID motif constitutes the central core of GRAS transcription factors. It includes a highly conserved structural element, P-N-H-D-Q-L, which is flanked at the carboxyl terminus by the L-R-I-T-G sequence [6]. The central domain is flanked on both sides by two leucine heptad repeat regions, designated as LHRI and LHRII. Within the conserved architecture, the PFYRE subdomain is characterized by three conserved amino acid pairs (P, FY, RE). Similarly, the SAW domain contains three distinct conserved motifs: R-E, W-G, and W-W [7].

The GRAS gene family has been identified in more than 50 plant species to date, such as Arabidopsis thaliana and Oryza sativa [6], Glycine max [8], Capsicum annuum [9], Juglans regia [10], Nicotiana tabacum [11], Malus domestica [12], and Passiflora edulis [13]. The number of genes within this family ranges from 33 in Arabidopsis to 188 in wheat. To further understand the evolutionary relationships among these genes, a phylogenetic analysis was conducted on 581 GRAS proteins from 12 plant species, revealing that GRAS proteins can be classified into 17 subfamilies, including RAM1, NSP1, and NSP2. This analysis demonstrated substantial divergence among GRAS family genes within angiosperms [5]. Based on genomic analyses of A. thaliana and O. sativa, the GRAS family can be phylogenetically classified into nine distinct subfamilies [2]. Each subfamily fulfills specific functional roles in processes such as plant growth, development, and metabolic regulation. Furthermore, members across these subfamilies are involved in diverse biological processes that support growth, development, and adaptation to environmental conditions [14]. Sun summarized the primary biological functions of the ten subfamilies of GRAS transcription factors across different species [4]. Despite their divergence into separate subfamilies, SCR and SHR proteins function synergistically to regulate radial organization in both root and shoot tissues of A. thaliana [15]. Furthermore, members of the PAT1 subfamily (such as CaGRAS30 and CaGRAS34) exhibit higher expression levels in the stems of Capsicum annuum compared to other members of the GRAS gene family [9]. In Passiflora edulis Sims, many PeGRAS members, particularly from the PAT1 subfamily, are significantly involved in stress responses, likely contributing to cold tolerance and drought resistance [13]. Currently, genome-wide identification and bioinformatics analyses of the GRAS gene family have been conducted in multiple species. Collectively, these studies indicate that the GRAS gene family plays crucial roles in plant growth and development, signal transduction, and responses to both biotic and abiotic stress.

C. oleifera, one of the four major woody oil crops worldwide, is an economically important oil-bearing tree species that is widely cultivated in the hilly areas of southern China [16]. It is renowned as the ‘Oriental Olive Oil’ in China [17]. Its seed oil contains bioactive compounds such as polyphenols, saponins, squalene, and flavonoids, which exhibit antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. With an unsaturated fatty acid content exceeding 90%, camellia oil is recognized as a high-quality premium edible oil [18]. Due to its inherent biological traits, it has a prolonged growth cycle and is susceptible to environmental stresses, including extreme temperatures, salinity, and drought. While studies on various gene families in C. oleifera have been published, the GRAS gene family remains poorly characterized. In this study, employing the latest C. oleifera genome assembly, we identified a total of 75 GRAS genes and performed a comprehensive analysis of their phylogenetic relationships, chromosomal locations, promoter regions, conserved domains, cis-acting elements, and collinearity. Expression profiling demonstrated distinct tissue-specific patterns among CoGRAS genes. Furthermore, qRT-PCR analysis of 23 representative genes confirmed their responsive expression under salt, drought, and temperature stress conditions. These findings provide a foundation for the functional characterization of CoGRAS genes in abiotic stress responses and for identifying stress-resistance-related candidates.

Materials and methods

Plant materials, growth conditions, and stress treatments

The experimental material utilized in this study was the C. oleifera variety Changlin No. 53, represented as one-year-old potted seedlings, sourced from Anhui Dechang Seedling Co., Ltd. (It has been commercialized now, no any required permission for its sample collection and use). The growth medium comprised a mixture of peat soil and vermiculite in a 3:1 ratio. After a 60-day acclimation period in controlled-environment growth chambers maintained at 22 ± 1 °C and 70–80% relative humidity, uniformly developed C. oleifera seedlings were selected for differential stress treatment experiments. The seedlings underwent four distinct treatments: 30% PEG-6000, 200 mM NaCl, 4℃, and 40 °C. For gene expression analysis, the seedlings were cultivated over a period of three months, receiving irrigation every three days. In the cases of salinity and drought treatments, seedlings were irrigated with a 200 mM NaCl solution and a 30% PEG-6000 solution, respectively. Temperature treatments were administered by incubating seedlings in growth chambers set at 4℃ and 40 °C. Following each treatment, leaves were harvested at 0, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and subsequently stored at −80 °C for total RNA extraction.

Sequence retrieval and gene identification

The whole genome data of C. oleifera was downloaded from the C. oleifera genome database (https://github.com/Hengfu-Yin/CON_genome_data). A. thaliana GRAS genes were obtained from TAIR (https://www.arabidopsis.org/) [19], O. sativa GRAS sequences were retrieved from the Rice Genome Annotation Project (https://rice.uga.edu/index.shtml), and GRAS genes from C. sinensis were sourced from the tea plant genome database (https://tpia.teaplants.cn) [20]. Using TBtools software (v2.142) [21], we conducted BLASTP alignment searches to identify CoGRAS family members (Additional File 1: Table S1). The protein sequences were validated using NCBI-CDD (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd) and SMART (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/) to confirm the presence of the GRAS domain [22]. Proteins lacking a complete GRAS domain were excluded from further analysis. The protein length, isoelectric point, and molecular weight were analyzed using ExPASy (https://www.expasy.org/) [23]. The subcellular localization of CoGRAS proteins was predicted using the CELLO online program (http://cello.life.nctu.edu.tw/). Detailed information is provided in Additional File 2: Table S2.

Multiple alignment and phylogenetic analysis

Multiple sequence alignments were performed using ClustalW integrated into MEGA 11, incorporating GRAS protein sequences from A. thaliana, O. sativa, C. sinensis and C. oleifera. Phylogenetic analysis was conducted using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) method in MEGA 11, with 1,000 bootstrap replications. The resulting phylogenetic tree was visualized using iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/) [24].

Conserved motifs prediction and gene structure analysis

Conserved motifs of C. oleifera GRAS proteins were analyzed using the MEME online tool (https://memesuite.org/meme/tools/meme), with the maximum number of retrieved motifs set to 8 and other parameters maintained at default settings (Additional File 3: Table S3). Evolutionary relationships among candidate GRAS family members were analyzed using MEGA 11. Conserved motifs and gene structures were visualized using Tbtools [25].

Chromosomal distribution and collinearity analysis

Using gene location information derived from the GFF annotation file, the chromosomal positions of CoGRAS genes were mapped with TBtools. The evolutionary relationships within C. oleifera and with three other species—A. thaliana, O. sativa, C. sinensis—were analyzed utilizing MCScanX. The Advanced Circos function of TBtools was employed to visualize the gene duplication events of CoGRAS. To investigate the evolutionary dynamics of CoGRAS genes, nonsynonymous substitution rates (Ka), synonymous substitution rates (Ks), and their ratio (Ka/Ks) were calculated using the integrated Ka/Ks calculator in Tbtools [14].

Cis-regulatory elements analysis

TBtools was also utilized to extract 2,000 bp upstream sequences of the translation start codon, which were defined as putative promoter regions to examine potential regulatory elements of CoGRAS genes. These sequences were subsequently submitted to the PlantCARE database for analysis and identification of cis-acting element [26].

The expression patterns of GRAS genes in different tissues

To elucidate the potential functions of GRAS genes in C. oleifera, we conducted an analysis of their expression levels across various plant tissues. We retrieved the transcriptome database of C. oleifera (PRJNA973966) from the SRA database available on the NCBI website (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Expression data for GRAS family genes were obtained from buds, leaves, petals, sepals, stamens, pistils, and seeds. Furthermore, we acquired expression data for C. oleifera seeds at different developmental stages from the full-length transcriptome article (PRJNA668531) [27]. These data were subsequently processed using the heatmap tool in TBtools software, resulting in a refined heatmap that illustrates the expression differences of the GRAS gene family across various tissues and fruit developmental stages.

RNA extraction and qRT–PCR analysis

Harvested tissue samples were ground into a fine powder in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted from each sample using the CTAB method [28]. The concentration and integrity of RNA were assessed via electrophoresis and NanoDrop™ One/OneC (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene [27], and the primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. The primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in Additional File 4: Table S4.

For qRT-PCR analysis, reactions were conducted on a LightCycler® 96 Real-Time System (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) utilizing TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNaseH Plus; TaKaRa Biotechnology) in 10 µL reaction volumes [26]. All experiments were performed with three biological replicates to ensure robustness, and relative expression levels of CoGRAS genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method [29]. The PCR cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 94℃ for 120 s, followed by 40 cycles of 94℃ for 5 s and 60℃ for 30 s. Statistical analysis was conducted using lowercase letters to denote significant differences among treatment groups (p < 0.05, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test). Data sharing the same letter are not significantly different. Visualization was performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.4.1).

Results

Identification and characterization of the CoGRAS family

Using BLASTp screening, followed by validation against the CDD database and SMART, we identified 75 members of the CoGRAS gene family. Based on their positions on the chromosomes of C. oleifera, these GRAS members were renamed CoGRAS01 to CoGRAS75. The basic characteristics and properties of the CoGRAS family members are detailed in Additional File 2: Table S2. Their fundamental features, including physical location, number of amino acids, molecular weight, isoelectric point and subcellular localization, were systematically analyzed. Among the 75 CoGRAS proteins, CoGRAS42 was the smallest, comprising 263 amino acids, whereas CoGRAS19 was the largest, consisting of 990 amino acids. The molecular weights of the proteins varied from 29,988.6 Da (CoGRAS42) to 112,729.23 Da (CoGRAS19). The theoretical isoelectric point values ranged from 4.72 (CoGRAS19) to 7.56 (CoGRAS55). Furthermore, subcellular localization analysis indicated that 38 proteins were localized in the nucleus, 23 in the cytoplasm, 11 in the plasma membrane, and only 3 CoGRAS proteins were found in the chloroplast.

Phylogenetic analysis of GRAS family

To investigate the relationship and classification of GRAS family members in C. oleifera, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using 210 protein sequences, which included 33 AtGRASs, 50 OsGRASs, 52 CsGRASs, and 75 CoGRASs, employing the NJ method (Fig. 1, Additional File 1: Table S1). These 210 GRAS proteins were categorized into distinct branches. Employing the subfamily classification system established for A. thaliana, O. Sativa and C. sinensis, the identified GRAS genes were categorized into 13 subfamilies: HAM, PAT1, SCL3, DELLA, OS4, OS43, SCL4/7, LISCL, SHR, SCR, DLT, OS19, and LAS. Based on the subfamily clustering results derived from the phylogenetic tree, we compared the distribution of GRAS members among C. oleifera and two model plants, A. thaliana and O. sativa. In Arabidopsis, 33 GRAS members were identified across the 13 aforementioned subfamilies, whereas rice exhibited a total of 50 GRAS members encompassing all subfamilies.The distribution of 75 GRAS members in C. oleifera across each subfamily exhibited distinct characteristics compared to the model plants. The LISCL subfamily contained 17 members in C. oleifera, in contrast to 7 members each in Arabidopsis and rice. The HAM subfamily included 16 members in C. oleifera, compared to 5 and 7 members in Arabidopsis and rice, respectively. The SHR subfamily comprised 10 members in C. oleifera, versus 3 and 5 members in Arabidopsis and rice, respectively. Additionally, the LAS, OS19, and SCL3 subfamilies exhibited relatively small cluster sizes across all three species, with the OS19 subfamily containing only one member in each species. Furthermore, we compared the subfamily clustering between C. oleifera and its closely related species, C. sinensis, based on the phylogenetic tree. The results indicated that the total number of GRAS members in C. oleifera (75) surpassed that in C. sinensis (52). Regarding subfamily cluster size, the LISCL subfamily in C. oleifera (17 members) was approximately 1.7 times larger than that of C. sinensis (10 members), the HAM subfamily (16 members) was about 1.6 times larger than that of C. sinensis (10 members), and the SHR subfamily (10 members) was twice that of C. sinensis (5 members). In contrast, the SCL3 subfamily contained only one member in C. oleifera, whereas C. sinensis had three members; the LAS subfamily had one and two members in C. oleifera and C. sinensis, respectively. The cluster sizes of the remaining subfamilies, including PAT1, DELLA, SCR, DLT, OS4, OS43, SCL4/7, and OS19, were generally consistent between the two Camellia species.

Gene structure and conserved motifs analysis of GRAS family

The structural features of different CoGRAS members were characterized based on their phylogenetic relationships (Fig. 2A). To gain a more comprehensive understanding of the conserved motifs in CoGRAS genes, MEME analysis was performed, and TBtools was utilized to visualize the conserved motifs of CoGRAS genes (Fig. 2B). Eight conserved motifs were identified among the GRAS proteins. The results indicated that nearly half of the protein sequences in the CoGRAS gene family contained all eight conserved motifs. The number of conserved motifs identified in each protein varied from 3 to 10, among these, motifs 1, 2, 6, 7, and 8 being widespread; these motifs were often located in close proximity across most members. Motifs 1, 2, 4, 6, 7, and 8 were present in 12 subfamilies of the CoGRAS gene family, whereas only the SCL3 subfamily lacked motif 4. Additionally, half of the members in the HAM subfamily commonly contained motifs 1, 2, and 6. The position and order of motifs were conserved within the same subfamily, but distinct arrangements were observed among different subfamilies. Most conserved motifs were localized to the C-terminal domain.

The exon–intron organization represents a significant evolutionary hallmark of genes and provides critical insights into their functional diversification processes [22]. Comparative analysis of exon–intron structures indicated considerable diversity in both the number and arrangement of exons among the 75 CoGRAS genes, with the number of exons varying from 1 to 6 (Fig. 2C). The results indicated that 65.3% (49) of the GRAS genes lacked introns, while CoGRAS36 exhibited the longest introns among the intron-containing CoGRAS genes. Nine CoGRAS genes contained a single intron, whereas CoGRAS15, 38, 39, 42, 45, 50, 53, 64, and 65 possessed two introns. Notably, CoGRAS61 contained as many as five introns.

Chromosomal locations, collinearity and volution analysis of CoGRASs

The 75 CoGRAS genes are unevenly distributed across chromosomes 1 to 15 (Fig. 3), with the number of CoGRAS genes per chromosome ranging from 1 to 12. Chromosome 3 harbors the highest number of CoGRAS genes (12 genes, approximately 16.0%), followed by Chromosome 8 (10 genes, approximately 13.3%) and Chromosome 13 (7 genes, approximately 9.3%). The lowest number is observed on Chromosome 9 (1 gene, approximately 1.3%). Most CoGRAS genes are located on Chromosomes 2, 3, 7, 8, and 10, with additional genes identified on Chromosomes 13 and 15. As illustrated in Fig. 4, Chromosome 7 contains five subfamilies of the CoGRAS gene family, while Chromosomes 10, 13, and 15 each comprise four subfamilies. The remaining chromosomes harbor 1 to 3 subfamilies. In the CoGRAS gene family, five pairs of tandemly duplicated genes were identified on chromosomes 3 and 8 (Additional File 5: Table S5), involving nine members belonging to the HAM and LISCL subfamilies. Notably, chromosome 8 contains four of these pairs, encompassing genes CoGRAS36 to CoGRAS43 (excluding CoGRAS38), all of which belong to the LISCL subfamily. These findings are reminiscent of tandem duplication events previously reported in the GRAS gene family of rye [14] and passion fruit [13], suggesting that subfamilies within the same gene family may undergo similar evolutionary processes.

Collinearity analysis of CoGRAS genes in the C. oleifera genome was conducted using TBtools software (Fig. 4, Additional File 6: Table S6). A total of 29 collinear gene pairs were identified, with two pairs located on Chromosomes 2 and 6, respectively. This observation aligns with the distribution of CoGRAS genes on these chromosomes, suggesting that segmental duplication may have contributed to the expansion of the GRAS gene family in C. oleifera. Several CoGRAS genes exhibited collinear relationships with multiple paralogs. For instance, CoGRAS45 on Chromosome 10 demonstrated collinearity with CoGRAS61 on Chromosome 13, CoGRAS36 on Chromosome 8, and CoGRAS13 on Chromosome 3. Evolutionary analysis revealed that these four genes belong to the LISCL subfamily. Similarly, CoGRAS62 on Chromosome 13 displayed collinear relationships with CoGRAS63 on Chromosome 13 and CoGRAS19 on Chromosome 3, both of which are members of the HAM subfamily. These findings indicate that collinear genes likely originated from chromosomal segmental duplication events and retained functional similarity through subfamily conservation [30]. The substitution rate ratio (Ka/Ks) was utilized to investigate the evolutionary dynamics and selective forces influencing the CoGRAS genes. Analysis revealed that the Ka/Ks ratios for nearly all duplicated gene pairs were below 1, suggesting that these CoGRAS gene pairs underwent purifying selection throughout their evolution (Additional File 7: Table S7). This indicates their essential role in preserving the conserved structural integrity of the GRAS gene family.

To further investigate the evolutionary relationships among the CoGRAS genes, we conducted a collinearity analysis between C. oleifera and three other species (A. thaliana, O. sativa, and C. sinensis) using the MCScanX method (Fig. 5). The analysis revealed that the number of collinear gene pairs between C. oleifera and A. thaliana, O. sativa, C. sinensis was 13, 30, and 105, respectively. Notably, C. oleifera shared a relatively high proportion of GRAS gene orthologs with C. sinensis (81.33%), which included 14 pairs in the LISCL subfamily, 11 pairs in the HAM subfamily, 8 pairs in the SHR subfamily, and fewer pairs in other subfamilies. This finding indicates that C. oleifera has a closer evolutionary relationship with C. sinensis. Additionally, five CoGRAS genes (CoGRAS02, 36, 42, 45, and 60) were identified as having orthologs in the genomes of the other three species, suggesting their significant roles in the evolutionary history of the GRAS gene family. The presence of these orthologous genes further underscores their potential involvement in conserved physiological processes in plants, such as hormone signal transduction, lateral bud development, and stress responses [31]. Based on this evidence, we propose functional hypotheses for specific members: CoGRAS02, an ortholog of Arabidopsis AtHAM1/AtHAM2, may similarly function to maintain organ boundaries and meristem activity by suppressing the premature differentiation of meristematic cells [32]. Furthermore, CoGRAS30, an ortholog of the Arabidopsis DELLA protein AtRGL1, is likely involved in the negative regulation of the GA signaling pathway in C. oleifera, thereby influencing its growth and development [33].

Cis-elements analysis in CoGRAS promoter regions

The identification and characterization of functional cis-acting elements are fundamentally important for understanding the regulatory mechanisms of gene transcription [34]. To further investigate the potential cis-acting elements and their functions in the promoters of CoGRAS family members, TBtools software was employed to extract the sequences located 2000 bp upstream of the initiation sites of CoGRAS genes. Subsequently, using the PlantCARE database, 39 types of cis-regulatory elements (CREs) were identified in the promoter regions of 75 CoGRASs genes, and the cis-acting elements were analyzed.

The results (Fig. 6, Additional File 8: Table S8) demonstrated that these CREs could be classified into three primary categories: (1) CREs responsive to abiotic and biotic stresses (10 types), (2) CREs associated with phytohormone responses (11 types), and (3) CREs involved in plant growth and developmental processes (18 types). The majority of the identified elements responsive to abiotic and biotic stresses were found in the promoter regions of all 75 CoGRAS genes. These elements included MBS (drought-induced response element), LTR (low-temperature response element), ARE (anaerobic-induced response element), DRE core (dehydration-responsive element), W box, TC-rich repeats, and STR (stress response element), among others. This finding suggests that CoGRAS genes may play crucial roles in the growth and development of oil tea. The promoter regions of CoGRAS genes contain transcription activation elements (Sp1) and light-responsive regulatory elements (G-box and Box-4). Additionally, numerous hormone-responsive elements were identified, including auxin-responsive elements (TGA element and AuxRR-core), MeJA-responsive elements (CGTCA motif and TGACG motif), and abscisic acid-responsive elements (ABRE). Among the elements involved in growth and developmental processes, the L-box element, which participates in light and hormone signaling and regulates developmental processes—was identified in the promoters of CoGRAS53 and CoGRAS66. Furthermore, elements regulating gene expression (MRE elements) and circadian rhythm elements, which play a role in the regulation of biological circadian rhythms, were found in the promoters of nine CoGRAS genes. The relatively low frequency of these elements suggests that not all CoGRAS genes are broadly involved in light response, circadian rhythm regulation, or specific developmental pathways, potentially reflecting functional diversification and specificity within this gene family.

To further elucidate the transcriptional regulatory mechanisms of CoGRAS genes in response to stress, we conducted a systematic analysis of their promoter regions. As illustrated in Fig. 7, this study focused on 23 CoGRAS genes that exhibited significant expression differences under stress treatments. By visualizing the types, quantities, and distribution patterns of key stress-responsive cis-acting elements in their promoters, we revealed significant heterogeneity in regulatory elements among different genes. As illustrated in the Fig. 7, ARE element, DRE core element, MBS element, and the binding sites for MYB and MYC are significantly enriched in the promoter regions of the genes CoGRAS02, 20, 21, 24, 25, 44, and 66. The W-box and STRE are more extensively distributed, being present in 15 and 18 genes, respectively, with CoGRAS27 exhibiting the highest number of STREs. The low temperature-responsive element (LTR) is also notably abundant in the genes CoGRAS17, 38, 39, 40, 44, and 70. In contrast, the occurrence frequencies of TC-rich repeats and WUN-motif are relatively low.

The expression patterns of GRAS genes

We analyzed the expression levels of 75 CoGRAS genes in buds, leaves, petals, sepals, stamens, pistils, seeds, and five fruit developmental stages (210 days after pollination (DAP), 235 DAP, 258 DAP, 292 DAP, and 333 DAP).

The results presented in Fig. 8A (Additional File 9: Table S9) indicate that the CoGRAS10, 25, 34, 35, 48, and 50 genes exhibit tissue-specific expression patterns, with each gene being expressed exclusively in a single tissue. Figure 8B further reveals that certain genes are expressed throughout all five stages of fruit development. Specifically, the expression levels of CoGRAS10, 18, 20, 24, 25, 29, 40, 41, 43, 49, and 54 were found to be relatively high at only one developmental stage each. In contrast, CoGRAS03, 04, 23, 26, 39, 42, 59, 74, and 75 genes were not detected in the tested tissues or at various stages of fruit development. These findings suggest that the CoGRAS genes perform diverse biological functions across different tissues or organs of C. oleifera.

Drought treatment

Under drought conditions, nearly all genes responded to the stress. Among these, the expression levels of CoGRAS02, 05, 20, 34, 62, and 63 were significantly upregulated; whereas CoGRAS27, 39, 46, 54, and 56 exhibited downregulated expression; the expression levels of CoGRAS40, 42, 44, and 66 exhibited a pattern characterized by an initial decrease, followed by an increase, and subsequently a decline. Notably, CoGRAS63 displayed a distinct temporal expression profile. During the early to middle stages of drought stress (0–72 h), its transcript levels remained relatively stable, exhibiting no significant fluctuations. However, a marked and specific induction was observed at the 96 h point, where its expression increased approximately fourfold compared to the baseline level. This pattern suggests that CoGRAS63 is specifically activated during the terminal phase of the drought stress response.

Salt treatment

Under salt treatment, three CoGRAS genes (CoGRAS24, 66, and 70) displayed significantly upregulated expression, while six CoGRAS genes (CoGRAS02, 21, 25, 39, 42, and 54) were consistently downregulated throughout the stress treatment process. The remaining genes exhibited fluctuating expression patterns. For instance, CoGRAS17, 23, 27, 31, and 40 demonstrated significant upregulation and downregulation at different stages post-treatment. Among these, CoGRAS42 exhibited a unique biphasic expression pattern characterized by a non-significant decline during the early stage (0–12 h), followed by a stable low level in the mid-phase (24–72 h), and a recovery to the initial level observed at 12 h by the late stage (96 h). This pattern indicates that CoGRAS42 is not a typical immediate defense gene; rather, it serves as a crucial regulatory node involved in late-stage physiological adjustments or recovery preparations. The loss of its function may not impact early stress tolerance; however, it could significantly hinder the plant’s ability to recuperate from prolonged stress, thereby presenting a novel candidate target for breeding crops with enhanced resilience.

Low temperature treatment

Under low Temperature treatment, the majority of genes exhibited significant upregulation. Notably, CoGRAS05, 17, 21, 24, 25, and 31 demonstrated a consistent upward trend. In contrast, CoGRAS42, 44, 46, 56, 62, 63, 66, and 70 displayed fluctuating increases, accompanied by substantial variations throughout the treatment process. Conversely, only two genes, CoGRAS27 and CoGRAS38, exhibited significant downregulation. Furthermore, the expression levels of CoGRAS39, 40, and 54 revealed both significant upregulation and downregulation during the treatment period.

High temperature treatment

Most genes exhibited fluctuating patterns of upregulation and downregulation. The upregulated genes included CoGRAS17, 62, and 70, all of which reached peak expression at 12 h of stress. In contrast, the downregulated genes included CoGRAS02, 20, 24, 40, 42, 44, and 63. Among these, CoGRAS20 and CoGRAS40 demonstrated nearly negligible expression during the stress period, exhibiting exceptionally significant downregulation, while the downregulation of the other genes was less pronounced. The strong and specific suppression of CoGRAS20 and CoGRAS40 suggests that their functions may be detrimental to survival under high temperature conditions, leading plants to rapidly downregulate these genes to optimize resource allocation.

Discussion

In recent years, a substantial number of transcription factors (TFs) have been identified and analyzed across various species using bioinformatics technology. In comparison to other TF families, research progress on the GRAS family has been relatively slow. The GRAS gene family is one of the transcription factor families unique to plants and plays a significant role in plant growth and development, material metabolism, promoting flowering [35], and responses to biotic and abiotic stress [36]. Currently, members of the GRAS family have been identified in numerous plant species, with notable differences in the number of GRAS genes among them. For instance, in A.thaliana [6], O. Sativa [6], G. max [8], C. annuum [9], B. purpurascens [37], and A. sativa [38], a total of 33, 57, 117, 54, 29, and 100 GRAS genes were identified, respectively. The variation in the number of GRAS genes among different species may be attributed to gene duplication, evolutionary processes [39], and interspecific divergence [40].

In this study, a total of 75 GRAS genes (designated as CoGRAS01 to CoGRAS75) were identified in the C. oleifera genome through a combination of BLASTP and hidden Markov model (HMM)-based searches. The newly identified CoGRAS family members were thoroughly analyzed through bioinformatics approaches, including phylogenetic analysis, identification of conserved protein motifs, chromosome localization, gene duplication events, collinearity analysis, and expression pattern analysis.

Based on the phylogenetic tree and sequence structure analysis, the 75 CoGRAS genes were classified into 13 subfamilies, a finding that is consistent with the recent classification of GRAS gene families in rye [14] and passion fruit [13]. Phylogenetic analysis revealed several pairs of closely related orthologous GRAS proteins between C. oleifera and A. thaliana, O. sativa, C. sinensis, including CoGRAS33 and AtSCL15, CoGRAS58 and AtHAM3, CoGRAS09 and CsGRAS39, as well as CoGRAS59 and OsGRAS43. Members of the same subfamily displayed consistent or similar distributions of conserved protein motifs, thereby supporting the findings of the evolutionary analysis and indicating that CoGRAS members within the same subfamily may share similar biological functions.

Analysis of the 75 CoGRAS genes revealed variation in both the composition of conserved motifs and the organization of exon/intron structures among different members. Most gene structures lack introns, allowing for direct protein encoding during transcription and translation without the complexities of RNA splicing. This structural feature facilitates a rapid response to environmental changes, demonstrating higher efficiency and flexibility in addressing environmental stresses [41]. These findings suggest that CoGRAS genes may be transcriptionally regulated under various abiotic stresses. Furthermore, research indicates that gene expression is not strongly influenced by intron density within genes [14]. Eight conserved motifs were identified in CoGRAS proteins, which can aid in predicting the functions of unknown proteins within the same subfamily. For instance, CoGRAS11 contains only one motif, while CoGRAS19 possesses ten motif elements. The motif distribution among the same group of GRAS proteins is relatively consistent, with motifs 1 and 2 present in all CoGRAS proteins, suggesting that these motifs may be crucial for the GRAS gene family’s functional capabilities. Although CoGRAS genes within the same subfamily share similar gene structures, CoGRAS36 displays significant structural differences, likely due to the loss or gain of introns during evolutionary processes.

Through collinearity analysis, identification of duplication events, and assessment of selective pressure on the CoGRAS gene family, this study offers initial insights into its evolutionary dynamics. The research reveals that tandem and segmental duplications are the primary evolutionary forces driving the expansion and diversification of this gene family. A total of five tandem duplication events, involving nine members of the HAM and LISCL subfamilies, and twenty-nine segmental duplication events were identified, collectively contributing to the significant expansion of the family. The evolution of the CoGRAS gene family exhibits a distinctive dynamic balance between the conservation of core functions and innovation in regulatory circuits. On one hand, the strong purifying selection (Ka/Ks < 1) experienced by orthologous genes indicates that the core functional modules of the GRAS family, which regulate fundamental developmental processes, are under stringent constraint in C. oleifera, forming the functional cornerstone of its environmental adaptation [42]. On the other hand, the active duplication events within the C. oleifera lineage, particularly the tandem duplications significantly enriched in the LISCL subfamily, provide crucial genetic raw material for functional innovation. This suggests that this subfamily possesses greater potential for expansion and innovation during evolution [43]. Key evidence emerged from the expression divergence analysis of five tandem duplicate gene pairs (Fig. 8). CoGRAS36/37 exhibited the most significant divergence, with CoGRAS37 showing high specificity to sepals, while CoGRAS36 was broadly expressed. Notably, they displayed complementary expression peaks at different stages of seed development. CoGRAS42/43 also exhibited partial divergence, with a notably enhanced expression of CoGRAS43 in sepals. In contrast, CoGRAS39/40 demonstrated highly similar expression patterns, suggesting functional redundancy. Additionally, CoGRAS40/41 and CoGRAS19/20 may have undergone expression decay [44, 45]. These results indicate that different gene pairs have experienced divergent evolutionary fates following tandem duplication events, including neofunctionalization, partial subfunctionalization, functional redundancy, and expression decay. This provides direct evidence that “tandem duplications drive functional divergence through subfunctionalization” [44]. For the 29 segmental duplicate gene pairs, which are widely distributed across subfamilies such as LISCL, HAM, PAT1, OS4, and SHR, selection pressure analysis revealed that the Ka/Ks ratios for all segmental duplicate gene pairs were less than 1, indicating that they have undergone purifying selection with limited functional divergence. To provide more specific evidence, we selected representative segmental duplicate gene pairs from different subfamilies, such as CoGRAS13/45 from the LISCL subfamily, CoGRAS19/62 from the HAM subfamily, and CoGRAS06/07 from the PAT1 subfamily, for expression pattern analysis (Additional file 9: Table S9). The results indicated differential expression across various tissues, suggesting that segmental duplicate genes may have achieved partial functional specialization through expression pattern divergence. Collectively, these retained tandem and segmental duplicate gene clusters may facilitate more precise regulation of environmental signals via expression pattern divergence. Collectively, these retained tandem and segmental duplicate gene clusters may facilitate more precise regulation of environmental signals through expression pattern divergence [46]. We hypothesize that the tandem duplicate gene clusters enriched in the LISCL subfamily of C. oleifera may enhance its responsiveness and adaptability to local environmental stresses, such as pathogens or seasonal drought, via a similar mechanism.

This study revealed that the promoter regions of CoGRAS genes contain a variety of cis-acting elements associated with hormone and stress responses. Under different stress treatments, the expression levels of 23 CoGRAS genes showed significant variations, and their expression patterns were closely related to the composition of specific stress-responsive elements in their promoters. The W-box element, recognized for its significant role in plant defense and drought resistance responses, has also been shown to be vital for the early senescence of rice flag leaves [47]. The widespread occurrence of this element across multiple candidate genes indicates that these genes may contribute to the regulation of senescence through their functions in drought resistance. Specifically, genes that were strongly and rapidly induced under PEG-simulated drought and NaCl salt stress (e.g., CoGRAS02, 20, 24) exhibited a high-frequency enrichment of drought-responsive elements such as DRE core and MBS in their promoters. This provides a molecular-level explanation for the rapid perception and response of these genes to osmotic stress signals. Genes specifically induced under low temperature stress (4 °C), such as CoGRAS05, 17, and 34, demonstrated significant enrichment of low temperature responsive elements, including LTR, within their promoters. This observation aligns closely with their expression profiles that are specific to low temperatures, suggesting that LTR elements may function as critical regulatory modules facilitating the transcriptional activation of these genes in response to cold stress. Conversely, for genes responsive to high temperature stress (40 °C), such as CoGRAS62 and 70, their expression regulation may depend on broader stress-responsive elements (e.g., STRE) or be mediated through hormone signaling pathways, including ABA. Previous research has shown that numerous members of the GRAS family are involved in stress responses via the ABA signaling pathway. For example, the PAT1 subfamily member CaGRAS1 exhibited significant induction under drought, high salinity, and ABA treatments [48]. In the current study, CoGRAS05, also belonging to the PAT1 subfamily, displayed upregulated expression under both drought and low temperature stress, identifying it as a candidate gene for further investigation. In summary, the expression of the majority of CoGRAS genes is regulated by drought, salt, low temperature, and high temperature stresses. Among these, CoGRAS17, 34, 66, and 70, which belong to the LISCL, OS4, DELLA, and DLT subfamilies, were identified as highly induced genes and are hypothesized to play pivotal roles in the adaptation of C. oleifera to environmental stresses.

Although the GRAS family is conserved in classification and motif structure, significant differences in subfamily sizes exist between C. oleifera and its closely related species, C. sinensis. Specifically, the numbers of members in the LISCL, HAM, and SHR subfamilies are markedly higher in C. oleifera than in C. sinensis, with the SHR subfamily being approximately twice as large as that of C. sinensis. Gene family expansion is generally associated with lineage-specific adaptation [49]. Accordingly, the specific amplification of these three subfamilies in C. oleifera may represent adaptive evolutionary events that occurred after the divergence of C. oleifera and C. sinensis, allowing them to adapt to different ecological niches. Functionally, LISCL is involved in anther development and stress signaling [50], and its expansion aids in meeting the demands for precise regulation of reproductive development in C. oleifera as a perennial woody oil crop. SHR regulates radial root development [15], and its expansion enhances the nutrient uptake potential of C. oleifera under low-phosphorus and drought conditions in the red soil regions of southern China. HAM maintains meristem function [32], and its expansion provides C. oleifera with greater developmental plasticity to adapt to the subtropical monsoon climate. In summary, the specific expansion of LISCL, HAM, and SHR in C. oleifera collectively shapes its species-specific advantages in reproductive development, root adaptation, and developmental plasticity. It should be noted that these speculations are primarily based on bioinformatics analysis, and the specific functions of CoGRAS genes require further experimental validation.

Conclusion

In the present study, a total of 75 CoGRASs were identified in the C. oleifera genome. Phylogenetic classification assigned these genes into 13 distinct subfamilies. Gene duplication events, including both tandem and segmental duplications, were found to be a major driving force for the expansion of the GRAS gene family in this species. Utilizing RNA-Seq data, expression profiling identified 23 candidate GRAS genes that respond to various treatments. Validation through qRT-PCR revealed dynamic expression patterns of these 23 genes under stress at different concentrations and time points. The results indicated that CoGRAS genes regulate multiple stress responses, either positively or negatively. Notably, CoGRAS70 exhibited significant upregulation across all stress conditions, suggesting its potential role as a key gene in the adaptation of C. oleifera to environmental changes. Overall, this study represents the first comprehensive characterization of GRAS genes in C. oleifera. Our results establish a foundation for elucidating the functional mechanisms of GRAS genes in stress tolerance and offer a valuable resource for future functional studies and molecular breeding programs in C. oleifera.
