The radiological impacts of radioactivity in the Crown Mines gold tailings
Authors: Mashinga J Mvelase, Sifiso S Ntshangase, Bonginkosi V Kheswa, Siyabonga N T Majola, Peane P Maleka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMining is the main source of environmental radioactivity in areas where nuclear weapons tests are not conducted. The dispersion of radioactivity in the form of dust is inevitable for residents living near mine tailings. There have been complaints in areas such as Riverlea, in Soweto, Johannesburg, about dust pollution causing skin irritations and severe respiratory problems. The area is very close to the gold mine tailings. Soil samples were randomly collected from the Crown Mines gold tailings and the abandoned mines located west of Johannesburg, behind Soccer City Stadium. The specific activity was assessed using coaxial HPGe -spectroscopy, which measured the -signals in the soil samples, and the comprehensive radiological indices were subsequently evaluated. The results showed that the mean activity concentrations in some samples exceeded the global average, particularly for radium. The average concentrations of Ra, Th, and K were 302.99 ± 6.60, 8.25 ± 1.31, and 113.34 ± 20.80 Bq/kg, respectively. The average radiological indices exceeded their globally recommended values with the values of 1006.34 ± 22.10 Sv/y for AGDE, 323.50 ± 7.04 Bq/kg for Ra, 293.98 ± 6.40 nGy/h for ADR, 5.05 ± 0.11 for ELCR, 1.69 ± 0.05 for H, 1.44 ± 0.03 mSv/y for AEDE, 1.09 ± 0.04 for I and 1.51 ± 0.03 for I. Statistical analyses, including skewness, kurtosis, Pearson correlation coefficients, and ANOVA, were used to characterise radionuclide distributions and relationships. The Pearson correlation matrix revealed a strong positive correlation between Ra and all estimated radiological hazard indices, while Th and K showed a weak but positive correlation with Ra and all indices. These findings suggest that radioactivity from the ambient geology of the mining tailings under survey poses a serious health risk, even if the impacts are not immediate. Therefore, close monitoring and evaluation of these tailings are necessary to ensure that residents’ exposure remains as low as reasonably achievable. Introduction

Natural radioactivity is widespread in the Earth’s environment and has existed in various geological formations, such as the Earth’s crust, rocks, and soils, since the formation of the Earth1–3. Radionuclides found in the environment can be divided into three general categories: primordial, cosmogenic, and anthropogenic. The primordial background radiation in the environment arises from decays in the uranium series (U to Pb), thorium series (Th to Pb), actinium series (U to Pb), and non-series K4,5, which are , , and radiation emitters6–8, with different energies and penetrating powers.

Human activities such as mining, extraction, processing, enrichment, and milling of mineral ores increase natural radioactivity levels in the environment9. Natural radioactivity mainly depends on geological and geographical conditions10 and therefore appears at different levels in soils of various types5. South Africa is endowed with numerous mineral resources, especially metals, coal, and gold. The exploration began in the late 1800s4, and mining started in 188611. Mining activities have caused significant pollution known as mining tailings. Crown Mines, a major contributor to Johannesburg’s economy and the world’s largest gold miner, was founded in 1909 through the merger of seven smaller mining companies. It employed thousands of people and produced over 1.4 million kg of gold. However, the Rand Mines Group closed it in 1977 after operations declined in the 1970s due to rising costs and unstable gold prices.

Previous studies of some tailings around Johannesburg reported that gold mine tailings in the Witwatersrand Basin have high specific activity of U7, and that high specific activity of U is also found in samples away from the gold tailings12. In Soweto, environmental radioactivity primarily comes from gold mining tailings7 near residential areas. During windy and dry seasons, radioactive yellow dust pollution spreads to areas surrounding tailings. Residents of Riverlea, a neighbourhood in Soweto, express concerns about dust from gold tailings entering their homes and exposing them to potential radioactivity. Reported health complaints include asthma, pneumonia, and chronic bronchitis, affecting both nearby and distant residents13–18. The Rn gas, produced in the decay series of U, undergoes radioactive decay, delivering a radiation dose to the respiratory epithelium via its decay products, especially Po and Po. Both Po and Po are alpha emitters, and alpha particles may damage DNA by imparting a high density of ionisations due to their high linear energy transfer (LET)19. A single high LET hit can generate highly reactive oxygen species (ROS) capable of inducing cellular damage through direct reactions with biological molecules20, and nitrogen radicals, which may cause significant DNA damage. High LET radiation is more effective than low LET radiation at inducing cell death, apoptosis, mutation, transformation, carcinogenesis, chromosomal abnormalities, and chromosomal instability21. The highly reactive oxygen species produced by high LET consequently damage various cell components, such as lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, resulting in several chronic and degenerative conditions, including ageing, dementia, kidney, cardiovascular and neurodegenerative diseases, cancer, respiratory disorders, rheumatoid arthritis, and other metabolic disorders22.

Knowledge of the distribution of geogenic and anthropogenic radionuclides in the environment is vital for assessing and managing public health risks and should guide environmental changes23. Most written reports are based primarily on the work of community activists and community-based NGOs opposing mining companies that have left pollution unaddressed. In this changing climate, the gold tailings are even more dangerous, leaving residents caught between the historical mishandling of uranium waste and increasingly severe future storms. This study aims to estimate radioactivity concentration levels and the associated radiological hazards to humans in this area of Johannesburg.

Materials and methods

Geology and geography of the study area

The study site has a subtropical highland climate with a warm, wet summer24. Samples were collected at Crown Mines Outlying (CMO) gold tailings. Most of the land in the research area is used for residential development, with very little used for gold mining24,25. The gold-bearing conglomerates in the sedimentary layers of the West Rand mining area consist of 10–30 phyllosilicates and 70–90 quartz (SiO). Uraninite (UO), brannerite (UOTiO), arsenopyrite (FeAsS), cobaltite (CoAsS), galena (PbS), pyrrhotite (FeS), gersdorfite (NiAsS), chromite (FeCrO), sericite KAl(AlSiO)(OH), and minor minerals such as rutile (TiO), pyrite (FeS), chromite (FeCrO), and uraninite (UO) make up the bulk of the phyllosilicates26,27. The West Rand Group, mainly consisting of quartzite and shale, forms the sedimentary base. The Central Rand Group is characterised by quartzite and conglomerate, particularly gold-bearing conglomerates in its lower section, which are associated with basin-wide unconformities28. The highlands experience long, hot summers, generally from October to March, and short, cold winters from June to August. Most of the annual rainfall, ranging from 600 to 732 mm, occurs during the summer months. Strong storms are frequent from October to March, and the average annual temperature is 16 C27.

Sample collection and preparation

Nineteen soil samples were randomly collected from various locations at the Crown Mines Outlying gold tailings at depths of 5–10 cm below the surface layer29, as dry, fine soils at these depths are prone to being blown away by strong winds. The samples collected here were assigned CMO sample ID codes with corresponding numbers. The samples were dried in an oven at 105 C. After cooling, they were crushed with a mortar and pestle to produce a fine particle size, homogenised, pulverised into powder, and sieved for homogeneity30 using a 2 mm stainless steel mesh to remove leaves and small stones. The samples were then stored in plastic zip bags and labelled accordingly.

The next step was to take the samples to NRF iThemba LABS, a national facility, for further preparation, where they were poured into Marinelli beakers of known empty weight. The gross weights were measured with a calibrated scale, and the difference yielded the mass of the samples. The average mass of the samples was about 250 g. The samples were hermetically sealed, with white silicone applied between the lid and the brim of the Marinelli beakers, and kept for over a month to allow sufficient time for the ingrowth of Ra and its progenies Rn, Pb, and Bi, reaching secular equilibrium before -spectroscopic analysis. Radon leakage must be avoided, as this could destabilise secular equilibrium; therefore, maintaining a tightly sealed Marinelli beaker during the waiting period is essential for obtaining reliable results31.

Analysis of soil samples

Gamma spectroscopic analysis of natural radioactivity (U, Th, and K) in soil was conducted at the Environmental Radiation Laboratory (ERL) of NRF iThemba LABS. The radioactivity in the collected samples was measured using a p-type coaxial Canberra gamma-ray spectrometer HPGe detector, Model No. BE2820 SN 8794, with 45 relative efficiency and a resolution of 2.00 keV (FWHM) at the 1.33 MeV -ray line of Co. An electronic data acquisition system (Canberra DSA-1000 digital signal processing (DSP) system), interfaced with a Multichannel Analyser (MCA) and Canberra Genie 2000 software (version 2.0), was used to acquire the spectrum.

The gamma spectrometry system was calibrated for energy and efficiency using a mixed radionuclide standard covering a wide range of gamma-ray energies (0–2000 keV) in a 500 ml Marinelli beaker. Samples were counted for 25,200 s, with the background count subtracted from the net count. For quality control, calibrations for energy and efficiency were performed to ensure measurement quality.

To assess the specific activity of Ra, the average activity of daughter signals at 295.22 keV and 351.93 keV for Pb and 609.32 keV, 1120.29 keV, and 1764.49 keV for Bi were used29,32–37. For Th, 911.21 keV for Ac and 583.1 keV for Tl were used, while a single 1460 keV signal was used for K35.

The efficiency of the detector and energy calibration curves

When analysing unknown samples, we rely on -spectroscopy; however, before we can use it effectively, we must calibrate the energy scale accurately. To achieve this, we use several well-known radioactive sources that emit gamma rays at specific energies. These include Pb at 45.54 keV, Am at 59.5 keV, Cd at 88 keV, and Co at 122.1 keV, among others. We also use higher-energy sources such as Cs (661.65 keV), Co (which emits at both 1173.2 and 1332.4 keV), and Y (1836.1 keV). The emission probabilities for these radionuclides were obtained from previous research papers38. As there is a direct relationship between channel number and energy, we can convert our channel readings into energy measurements. The radionuclide concentrations were below the National Nuclear Regulator (NNR) of South Africa’s standards of 500 Bq/kg for U, 500 Bq/kg for Th, and 10,000 Bq/kg for K25. The levels are higher than those reported in a Namibian study by Hitila64, where the averages for U, Th, and K were 13.33, 17.73, and 269.6 Bq/kg, respectively, and lower than those reported by Zivuku, who found high levels of U, Th, and K at 52.79 ± 3.40, 71.30 ± 2.40, and 797.36 ± 18.26 Bq/kg, respectively65. The concentrations of U and K are similar to those reported in the study by Manjeru, which found slightly higher thorium levels. Their results for U, Th, and K were 220.95, 32.99, and 276.24 Bq/kg, respectively66. The concentrations are also lower than those found in studies by Kamunda7 and Mashupya25 in South Africa. The specific activity of U is higher than that reported by Olagbaju for farms around Rustenburg, while Th and K have similar values. At Site C, they reported 20.80 ± 1.17, 15.69 ± 2.56, and 86.6 ± 6.36 Bq/kg for U, Th, and K, respectively67. The specific activities for U are of the same order as those reported by Mathuthu, who found 485.3 ± 13.7. The activities for Th and K were 43.9 ± 1.0 and 427.0 ± 13.1, respectively68. The high U levels in this study are due to the presence of uraninite (UO) and brannerite (UOTiO) in rocks mined for gold.

The specific activities (Bq/kg) in decreasing order are: Ra > K > Th. The specific activity of Th was within the acceptable limit; K is intermediate but below the acceptable limit, and the specific activity of Ra dominates all NORM activities in the soil samples from Crown Mines gold tailings.

All the radionuclides exhibited positive skewness values, and this was extended to all radiological parameters. Positive skewness indicates an extended right-side tail in the distribution, meaning the data are right-skewed. These findings suggest potentially elevated values or anomalies at the upper end of the measured primordial radionuclide concentrations.

Notably, K had a negative kurtosis and showed moderate variance among the radionuclides, indicating an uneven distribution of natural radioisotopes in the samples, while both Ra and Th showed positive kurtosis.

It is also noticed that there was high variance in samples for It is also noted that there was high variance in the samples for Ra and K, indicating significant dispersion and heterogeneity, with data points widely spread from the mean.

Notably, the mean values were higher than the corresponding standard deviations, indicating a high degree of uniformity in the distribution across the samples. The data showed positive skewness and kurtosis, indicating that the distribution tails extend towards positive values. The positive skewness in all the radionuclides Ra, Th, and K indicates the asymmetric distribution69.

Positive kurtosis represents a relatively peaked distribution, whereas negative kurtosis indicates a relatively flat distribution. In this study, Ra showed negative kurtosis, indicating relatively flat distributions, while the positive kurtosis of Ra indicated a relatively peaked distribution. Multivariate statistical analysis

To move beyond simple descriptive statistics and uncover the complex interrelationships between the geochemical and radiological variables, multivariate statistical analyses were performed on the complete dataset.

Analysis of Pearson’s correlation coefficient

This analysis is important for understanding the mutual influences and interconnections among the variables, enabling the direction, strength, and statistical significance of the relationships to be determined29. A Pearson correlation matrix was generated in MS Excel using the Data Analysis Toolpak to quantify the linear relationships between the specific activities and the calculated radium equivalent activity. Based on Pearson correlation analysis, the strength of correlation was classified as follows: weak (0.00–0.19), moderate (0.20–0.39), strong (0.40–0.79), and very strong (0.80–1.00)80,81.

Table 4 presents the computed Pearson correlation coefficients for the relationships between the radioactivity concentrations of the naturally occurring radioisotopes Ra, Th, and K, and the estimated radium equivalent activity. Other radiological health parameters were not shown because they’re multiplies of the specific activities calculated which normally makes them to have good positive correlations. Inter-relationships between the naturally occurring radioisotopes Ra, Th, and K showed a weak positive correlation. This is because Ra, Th originate from different naturally occurring radionuclide decay series, while K is a non-decay series radioisotope. However, a strong positive correlation was observed between Ra and the radium equivalent index, and poor positive correlation Th, and K with values of 0.9978, 0.1680, and 0.2809, and suggesting that Ra contributed proportionately higher than the other two radionuclides to the index. This indicates that Ra, a descendant of U, is a significant contributor to the overall radiological risk in the study area. More importantly, it confirms that the U (Ra) series is the primary contributor to the overall radiological hazard. Based on this data, K and Th do not contribute significantly to the radiological risk, and their geological distributions are independent of that of radium in this environment.

Analysis of variance (ANOVA)

In this study, the Data Analysis ToolPak in MS Excel was used to perform and interpret the one-way ANOVA test results. The entire data set was processed and reduced to two tables: one for the summary and one for the ANOVA results.

The one-way ANOVA results revealed statistically significant differences in Ra concentrations across the samples, with a p-value of 4.74 less than (p < 0.05). Furthermore, the F-statistic value of 407, which exceeded the critical F-value of 2.73, provided further support for the finding of significant variations. The sum of squares (SS) values showed that the between-group variation (1 319 808) contributed more to the overall variance than the within-group variation (77 859), supporting the conclusion that there were significant variations in Ra concentrations among the samples. A significant ANOVA result (p < 0.05) indicates that at least one group mean differs from the others, suggesting that mining significantly influenced radioactivity levels.

In this investigation, higher radiological hazard indices were found in gold mining tailings, indicating significant radionuclide concentrations that could pose long-term risks to human health and the environment. Additionally, the relatively high amount of K observed in all samples, although not exceeding the world average value, can be attributed to the abundance of potassium silicate minerals, such as sericite KAl(AlSiO)(OH). Conclusion and future outlook

The findings revealed only geogenic radionuclides in the samples, indicating the absence of anthropogenic radionuclides. Ra (U) exceeded the global average, while Th and K were below their recommended limits. Among the radiological hazard indices, the average Ra and H were below their recommended limits. The average ADR and ADR were higher than their recommended values. The average AEDE and AEDE were also higher than recommended limits. The average ELCR and ELCR exceeded the recommended values. The AGDE was higher than the recommended limit. H, I, and I hazard indices exceeded unity. The high radiological parameters were attributed to elevated Ra, a decay product of U present in uraninite (UO) and brannerite (UOTiO) found in the geological formations of the rocks hosting gold ores.A single radionuclide with higher specific activity than others can account for all the radioactivity in the area, as demonstrated by radium in this study. The elevated radiological parameters suggest that the gold tailings may have negative consequences for ecosystems, wildlife, and human health, although these effects may take a long time to manifest.

The high level of radium implies high levels of radon gas in the area, highlighting the need for alpha spectroscopy in future measurements, although radon can be estimated from radium concentrations. Gamma spectroscopy alone is limited in determining alpha particles; the gamma detector used in the study recorded signals only up to 2000 keV, thus missing significant gamma signals above this range. These findings provide a foundation for future research and support ongoing environmental monitoring to minimise resident exposure in accordance with ALARA principles. The municipality must be strategic in rehabilitating the tailings and conduct continuous environmental monitoring to track radiation levels.
