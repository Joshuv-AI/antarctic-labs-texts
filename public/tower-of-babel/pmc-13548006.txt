Second-Stage Knee Pain is Not Increased After Staged Bilateral Total Knee Arthroplasty: A Retrospective Linear Mixed-Effects Analysis of 72-Hour Postoperative Pain
Authors: Khanin Iamthanaporn, Arnan Wiwatboworn, Pawin Wanasitchaiwat, Peeranut Purngpiputtrakul, Varah Yuenyongviwat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposePain perception following staged bilateral total knee arthroplasty (TKA) remains controversial, with previous studies suggesting increased pain after the second knee attributed to central sensitization. This retrospective observational study aimed to compare postoperative pain between first- and second-stage TKA using a linear mixed-effects model adjusted for opioid consumption and peripheral nerve block type.Patients and MethodsThis retrospective observational study included 155 patients who underwent staged bilateral TKA under spinal anesthesia between 2015 and 2020 at Prince of Songkla University Hospital, Hat Yai, Thailand. Pain was assessed using a verbal numerical rating scale (VNRS) every 4 hours for 72 hours postoperatively. A linear mixed-effects model with a random intercept for each patient was used for the primary analysis, with last observation carried forward (LOCF) as sensitivity analysis.ResultsAmong the 155 patients included, no significant difference in pain was found between the first and second TKAs (β = −0.027; 95% CI, −0.139 to 0.085; P = 0.638). Pain decreased significantly over time (β = −0.073 per 4-hour interval; 95% CI, −0.083 to −0.063; P < 0.001). Femoral nerve block was associated with reduced pain (β = −0.321; 95% CI, −0.509 to −0.133; P = 0.001). Higher opioid consumption was associated with increased pain (β = 0.018; 95% CI, 0.014 to 0.022; P < 0.001). The LOCF sensitivity analysis confirmed no significant difference between stages (β = −0.051; 95% CI, −0.147 to 0.045; P = 0.300).ConclusionWithin the first 72 hours, pain following second-stage TKA was not significantly different from that following first-stage TKA. These findings do not support central sensitization as an inevitable cause of increased second-knee pain and suggest that previously reported increases may have been confounded by uncontrolled opioid exposure. As an observational analysis, these results cannot establish causation.

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is an effective treatment for end-stage knee osteoarthritis (OA), providing substantial pain relief and improving joint function and quality of life. Bilateral symptoms are present in approximately one-third of patients with knee OA,1 and approximately 40% of patients undergoing primary unilateral TKA will eventually require contralateral TKA within 10 years.2 Staged bilateral TKA is widely considered safe and effective, with fewer complications and lower mortality than simultaneous bilateral TKA.3,4

Several studies have reported increased postoperative pain in the second knee following staged bilateral TKA, often attributed to central sensitization—a process by which the nervous system becomes more responsive to nociceptive stimuli following previous surgical exposure.5,6 Kim et al6 and Sun et al7 observed significantly higher pain scores after second-stage TKA, and Koh et al8 found that ketamine, an N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor antagonist, did not significantly reduce second-knee pain in a randomized controlled trial, suggesting the mechanism may be more complex. However, conflicting results exist: Huang et al9 reported less pain in the second knee during simultaneous bilateral TKA, and Lee and Kim10 highlighted that opioid-induced hyperalgesia from fentanyl patient-controlled analgesia may have confounded previous studies. More recent studies have also reported similar or improved outcomes for second-stage procedures.11–14

Beyond nociceptive mechanisms, patient expectations and prior surgical experience may also influence perceived pain. Scott et al15 and Gruenberger et al16 demonstrated that expectations change between the first and second TKA, with dissatisfaction frequently reported after the second side even when clinical outcomes are comparable. Wu et al17 and Schwarzkopf et al18 described a “learning effect”, whereby the first surgical experience influences preparation and outcomes of the subsequent procedure.

Given these inconsistencies, and the limited number of studies that have controlled for analgesic consumption and nerve block technique within their statistical models, the present retrospective study was conducted to compare postoperative pain between first- and second-stage TKA using a linear mixed-effects model adjusted for opioid consumption, peripheral nerve block type, and other covariates. The specific hypothesis was that, after adjustment for these factors, pain levels would not differ significantly between the two stages. The principal novelty of this study lies in its longitudinal repeated-measures modeling of pain across the entire 72-hour period, with simultaneous adjustment for cumulative opioid consumption and peripheral nerve block type—factors that were frequently uncontrolled in earlier studies and that may have inflated previously reported second-knee pain. Secondary outcomes were cumulative opioid consumption and length of hospital stay.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective study was conducted using the electronic medical records of patients who underwent TKA (International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, code: 81.54) between January 2015 and December 2020 at Prince of Songkla University Hospital, Hat Yai, Thailand.

Patient Selection

Patients who underwent staged bilateral TKA under spinal anesthesia, performed by two experienced arthroplasty surgeons, were eligible for inclusion. Both procedures were required to be primary TKA performed for primary osteoarthritis, with complete postoperative pain records available. Patients were excluded if they had secondary osteoarthritis (eg, post-traumatic, inflammatory, or infective arthropathy), previous open knee surgery, or prior knee infection; if either knee required revision surgery during follow-up owing to infection or other complications; or if general anesthesia was used for either procedure.

Surgical Procedure

All surgeries were performed by two experienced joint surgeons. A tourniquet was applied during each surgery. Prophylactic antibiotics and tranexamic acid (750 mg) were intravenously administered. A minimally invasive medial parapatellar approach was employed, followed by a measured resection technique. Cemented posterior-stabilized prostheses were implanted, with either fixed-bearing or mobile-bearing designs, and patellar resurfacing selectively performed based on the severity of patellofemoral joint osteoarthritis, at the surgeon’s discretion.

Anesthesia and Postoperative Care

Spinal anesthesia was administered in all cases. Peripheral nerve block—either femoral nerve block or adductor canal block—was performed at the discretion of the attending anesthesiologist; some patients received no peripheral block. Blocks were given as a single-shot perioperative injection, but the precise timing, drug, and dose of each block were not consistently documented in the medical records and were therefore not analyzed. Dexamethasone and periarticular injections were not administered. Postoperative pain management included scheduled paracetamol (1 g every 6 hours) and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (etoricoxib 60–90 mg daily or celecoxib 200 mg twice daily), with opioids (morphine, fentanyl, and tramadol) used for rescue analgesia as needed. Opioid consumption was recorded and converted to oral morphine-equivalent units for analysis using standard equianalgesic conversion factors.19 Aspirin was administered as prophylaxis for deep vein thrombosis. Patients were encouraged to use a walker the morning after surgery and to follow a standardized rehabilitation program comprising range-of-motion and muscle-strengthening exercises.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was a 10-point verbal numerical rating score (VNRS) for pain at rest, recorded every 4 hours for 72 hours postoperatively. Secondary outcomes included cumulative opioid consumption, conversion to morphine-equivalent units, and length of hospital stay. The discharge criteria were based on achieving 90° knee flexion, the absence of intravenous pain medication, and readiness for independent mobility and rehabilitation.

Data Collection

Demographic and clinical data were collected, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, Charlson comorbidity index, Kellgren–Lawrence classification for osteoarthritis severity,20 femorotibial angle, and peripheral nerve block type.

Ethics Approval and Informed Consent

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee and Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Medicine, Prince of Songkla University (approval No. REC 63-261-11-1). The study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards as laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. The requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective and anonymous nature of the data. Clinical trial number: not applicable. This study was reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines for observational studies.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. McNemar’s chi-square test or the Stuart–Maxwell test was applied for categorical variables, whereas paired t-tests were used for normally distributed continuous variables. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. A linear mixed-effects model with a random intercept for each patient was used to analyze VNRS scores. The model included TKA stage (first vs second) and time (postoperative hours, treated as a continuous variable) as fixed effects, together with peripheral nerve block type (no block as the reference category, femoral nerve block, and adductor canal block), femorotibial angle, Kellgren–Lawrence classification, surgeon, and cumulative opioid consumption as covariates. Including nerve block type as a categorical covariate, with “no block” as the reference category, allowed all patients—including those who received no block—to be analyzed within a single model. The model did not include a stage × time interaction term, because the primary aim was to estimate the overall between-stage difference in pain across the entire postoperative period rather than to compare temporal trajectories; the closely parallel pain curves for the two stages in Figure 1 support this modeling choice. Regression coefficients (β) are reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and model assumptions of residual normality and homoscedasticity were examined and considered acceptable. Patients were stratified by the interval between surgeries (<6 months, 6–12 months, and >12 months), and analysis of variance or Kruskal–Wallis tests were used to compare postoperative VNRS changes between subgroups. Missing Data Handling

Pain scores were recorded by nursing staff every 4 hours, yielding up to 19 scheduled assessments per knee (38 per patient). Missing observations occurred when patients were asleep, in the bathroom, or otherwise unavailable. Of the 5,890 scheduled assessments (155 patients × 2 stages × 19 time points), 4,705 (79.9%) were available for the primary analysis and 1,185 (20.1%) were missing. Missingness was low during the first 48 hours (generally <10%) and increased at later time points as patients approached discharge. The primary analysis used all available observations under a missing-at-random assumption inherent to the linear mixed-effects framework. As a sensitivity analysis, the last observation carried forward (LOCF) method was applied; although LOCF has known limitations in longitudinal pain data, where scores are expected to decline, it was included to assess the robustness of the primary findings.

Sample Size

Following Sun et al,7 a sample size of 72 patients per group was required to achieve 80% statistical power at a 95% confidence level. Given the retrospective design, all eligible patients were included, yielding 155 patients who met the inclusion criteria.

The distribution of peripheral nerve blocks differed significantly between the first and second TKAs, with greater use of adductor canal blocks in the second TKA group (47.1% vs 24.7%, P < 0.001). Other factors, such as age, BMI, Charlson Comorbidity Index, femorotibial angle, and Kellgren–Lawrence classification, were similar between the groups (Table 1). Linear Mixed-Effects Model Analysis

A linear mixed-effects model with a random intercept for each patient, incorporating 4,705 observations from 155 patients, was used to analyze the factors influencing pain. The model was adjusted for time since surgery, peripheral nerve block type, femorotibial angle, Kellgren–Lawrence classification, surgeon, and cumulative opioid consumption.

Fixed-effects analysis showed no significant difference in pain between the first and second TKA stages (β = −0.027; 95% CI, −0.139 to 0.085; P = 0.638). Pain decreased significantly over time (β = −0.073 per 4-hour interval; 95% CI, −0.083 to −0.063; P < 0.001). Femoral nerve block was significantly associated with reduced pain (β = −0.321; 95% CI, −0.509 to −0.133; P = 0.001), whereas adductor canal block was not (β = −0.056; 95% CI, −0.248 to 0.136; P = 0.566). Femorotibial angle (β = 0.012; 95% CI, −0.004 to 0.028; P = 0.144) and Kellgren–Lawrence classification (β = −0.034; 95% CI, −0.324 to 0.256; P = 0.817) were not significantly associated with pain, whereas higher cumulative opioid consumption was associated with higher pain (β = 0.018; 95% CI, 0.014 to 0.022; P < 0.001). The surgeon variable was not significantly associated with pain (β = −0.231; 95% CI, −0.543 to 0.081; P = 0.148).

Sensitivity Analysis

A sensitivity analysis using the LOCF method (5,890 observations) was consistent with the primary model. No significant difference in pain was found between stages (β = −0.051; 95% CI, −0.147 to 0.045; P = 0.300). The decrease in pain over time remained significant (β = −0.068; 95% CI, −0.076 to −0.060; P < 0.001), and femoral nerve block continued to show a significant pain-relieving effect (β = −0.221; 95% CI, −0.384 to −0.058; P = 0.008). Adductor canal block had no significant effect (β = −0.062; 95% CI, −0.229 to 0.105; P = 0.465), and the surgeon effect did not reach significance (β = −0.264; 95% CI, −0.564 to 0.036; P = 0.087). The association between higher opioid consumption and higher pain was confirmed (β = 0.017; 95% CI, 0.015 to 0.019; P < 0.001).

Subgroup Analysis

A subgroup analysis was conducted based on the interval between the first- and second-stage TKAs, dividing the patients into three groups: < 6 months (n = 46), 6–12 months (n = 51), and > 12 months (n = 58). No significant differences in demographic characteristics, including BMI and ASA classification, were observed across the groups, although the “> 12 months” group was slightly younger (P = 0.017) and had a higher proportion of Grade 3 Kellgren–Lawrence classification (P = 0.034). Pain trajectories from the immediate postoperative period to 72 hours after surgery did not differ significantly between the groups, although fluctuations in the VNRS scores were observed over time (Figure 2). The wider confidence intervals at the 72-hour time point, particularly in the <6-month group, reflect the smaller number of patients still hospitalized and assessed at that point. Discussion

This study examined postoperative pain following first- and second-stage bilateral TKA, focusing on temporal changes and the influence of perioperative factors. The primary analysis showed no significant difference in pain between the two stages after adjustment for opioid consumption and peripheral nerve block type. The LOCF sensitivity analysis was consistent, also showing no significant difference (P = 0.300). Together, these findings indicate that, within the first 72 hours, pain following the second TKA is not higher than that following the first procedure.

These findings contrast with earlier studies reporting higher second-knee pain attributed to central sensitization.6,7 Koh et al8 found that ketamine did not reduce second-knee pain, suggesting a more complex mechanism. However, the present results align with Sun et al’s7 subgroup finding that pain differences diminished with longer inter-surgical intervals; the mean interval in this study was 12.34 months, which may partly explain the absence of a significant stage effect. Additionally, Lee and Kim10 noted that opioid-induced hyperalgesia from fentanyl patient-controlled analgesia after first-stage TKA could inflate second-knee pain scores in studies that did not control for analgesic exposure. In the present study, cumulative opioid consumption was included as a covariate; given the observational design, this is best regarded as a plausible contributor to, rather than a proven cause of, the discrepancy with earlier reports.

The finding that femoral nerve block was associated with significantly reduced pain (β = −0.321, P = 0.001), whereas adductor canal block showed no significant effect, should be interpreted with caution. This observational finding may reflect residual confounding, differences in block technique or timing, or the temporal shift from femoral nerve block to adductor canal block during the study period, rather than a true difference in analgesic efficacy. Several randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses have demonstrated broadly similar analgesic outcomes between the two techniques.21–24

Beyond nociceptive mechanisms, patient expectations and prior surgical experience may also play a role. Scott et al15 and Gruenberger et al16 showed that expectations change between the first and second TKA, with dissatisfaction frequently reported after the second side despite comparable outcomes. Wu et al17 and Schwarzkopf et al18 described a “learning effect” whereby the first surgical experience shapes preparation and outcomes of the subsequent procedure. The finding that second-stage pain was not increased may partly reflect better-calibrated expectations. This is consistent with recent studies reporting similar patient-reported outcomes for second-side surgery.11–13

Several factors temper these conclusions. Central sensitization was not directly assessed; quantitative sensory testing, psychological evaluation, and validated central sensitization instruments were unavailable in this retrospective cohort. The present results therefore indicate a failure to detect the increased second-knee pain predicted by the central sensitization hypothesis, rather than a direct refutation of that mechanism. Self-reported central sensitization and related preoperative factors have been shown to predict 1-year postoperative pain after TKA,25 and persistent pain at 1 year is associated with worse joint-specific function and health-related quality of life;26 these longer-term outcomes lay beyond the 72-hour scope of the present analysis and warrant dedicated prospective study.

These data provide useful insights for surgeons counseling patients. The absence of significant pain differences suggests that patients can undergo a second procedure once they have adequately recovered, without concerns about increased pain. Regarding the inter-surgical interval, previous research has reported no significant differences in complication rates or functional outcomes across different intervals,1,27,28 and the subgroup analysis similarly showed no significant differences in pain trajectories. However, this study did not measure complications, long-term function, or patient satisfaction, and any inferences about these outcomes would be speculative. Surgeons should consider individual patient factors and recovery from the first TKA, rather than relying solely on time-based protocols. In day-to-day practice, these findings can reassure patients and surgeons that proceeding with the second knee once recovery from the first is adequate is unlikely to entail greater early postoperative pain.

Overall, these results do not support the prevailing assumption that second-stage TKA is inherently more painful owing to central sensitization. The convergence of the primary and sensitivity analyses strengthens confidence in this null finding. The divergence from some earlier studies likely reflects the relatively long mean inter-surgical interval, the statistical adjustment for opioid consumption and nerve block type, and evolving perioperative care, suggesting that previously reported increases in second-knee pain may have been partly attributable to methodological and confounding factors rather than to an inevitable biological mechanism.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective observational design limits the ability to establish causal relationships and control for all potential confounders. Important pain-related determinants such as preoperative pain severity, psychological factors (eg, pain catastrophizing, anxiety, and depression), pre-existing chronic pain conditions, and measures of central sensitization were not collected. Second, the reliance on subjective pain scores recorded by nursing staff may not fully capture the nuances of postoperative pain. During the study period, there was a transition from femoral nerve block to adductor canal block, potentially influencing pain control, although multiple randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses indicated similar pain relief between the two techniques.21–24 Third, the inclusion of opioid consumption as a covariate in the pain model warrants acknowledgment, as opioid use may lie on the causal pathway between pain and subsequent pain scores; it was included to account for the direct pharmacological effect of analgesics on reported pain levels. Fourth, no data on long-term functional outcomes or patient satisfaction were collected, which limits the clinical generalizability of these findings. Finally, the perioperative analgesic protocols may have evolved during the 2015–2020 study period, and detailed protocol variations over time were not captured. In addition, the precise timing, drug, and dose of the peripheral nerve blocks were not consistently documented and could not be analyzed. The absence of functional outcome scores, patient-reported outcomes, and data on chronic pain is a further limitation; consequently, the findings apply only to acute in-hospital pain within the first 72 hours and should not be extrapolated to longer-term recovery, satisfaction, or persistent pain. Finally, although the final sample exceeded the a priori requirement, the study may have had limited power to detect small, clinically minor differences in pain between stages.

Conclusion

Within the first 72 hours after surgery, pain following second-stage TKA was not significantly different from that following first-stage TKA, and both the primary and sensitivity analyses consistently showed no significant between-stage difference. The interval between surgeries did not appear to significantly influence early pain. By using a linear mixed-effects model that adjusted for cumulative opioid consumption and peripheral nerve block type, this study provides a methodologically more rigorous assessment of early postoperative pain than many earlier reports, and the results do not support central sensitization as an inevitable cause of increased second-knee pain. These findings apply to acute in-hospital pain only; they suggest flexibility in scheduling the second procedure without concern for increased early pain, while individual patient factors should continue to guide timing. Prospective studies incorporating quantitative measures of central sensitization, psychological factors, and patient-reported and longer-term outcomes are needed to fully characterize pain perception in staged bilateral TKA.
