Effectiveness of photobiomodulation therapy for ophthalmic diseases: protocol for a systematic review and meta-analysis of disease-specific and shared physiological outcomes
Authors: Seunghwan Choy, Jin Mi Chun, Ji-Woo Seok, Se-Ra Park, Jung-Dae Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPhotobiomodulation therapy (PBMT)—a noninvasive therapeutic strategy—employs low-level red or near-infrared light to regulate cellular bioenergetics, reduce oxidative stress, and modulate inflammation. It has demonstrated therapeutic potential for several ophthalmic diseases, including age-related macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy, glaucoma, retinitis pigmentosa, and dry eye disease. However, the clinical evidence remains fragmented due to heterogeneity in study designs, treatment parameters, and outcome measures. Moreover, existing reviews are largely confined to single diseases. Robust, prespecified assessments that standardize disease-specific endpoints and only explore shared physiological measures across conditions remain scarce.MethodsThe protocol is registered in the PROSPERO database. This review will follow the PRISMA guidelines; randomized controlled trials and comparative nonrandomized clinical studies involving PBMT interventions for any clinically diagnosed ophthalmic disease will be included. Eligible comparators will include sham treatment, no treatment, or standard care, and outcomes will be primarily analyzed at the disease-specific level, with endpoints including best-corrected visual acuity, drusen volume, intraocular pressure, and diabetic retinopathy severity. Selected shared physiological or functional outcomes (e.g., electroretinogram amplitude, tear cytokine concentrations, and optical coherence tomography measurements of retinal structure) will be examined in a secondary exploratory framework to provide hypothesis-generating insights across conditions. Databases—including PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and Scopus—will be searched for articles published up to July 31, 2025. Two reviewers will independently select studies, extract data, and assess risk of bias. Random-effects models will be used for the meta-analysis, and heterogeneity will be explored through prespecified subgroup (disease type, wavelength, irradiance, fluence, session duration, treatment frequency) and exploratory meta-regression analyses.DiscussionThis review will provide pooled effect estimates for PBMT based on clinical outcomes across ophthalmic diseases. These will be stratified by intervention parameters and outcome types (disease-specific and shared physiological/biomarker-based). Rather than aiming to determine optimal treatment strategies, the findings are intended to provide exploratory insights and generate hypotheses for future clinical research. Background

Ophthalmic diseases include various degenerative and inflammatory conditions that can lead to blindness and significantly impact quality of life [1–3], such as age-related macular degeneration (AMD) [4], diabetic retinopathy (DR) [5], glaucoma [6], retinitis pigmentosa (RP) [7], dry eye disease (DED) [8], uveitis [9], keratitis [10], conjunctivitis [11], optic neuropathy [12], retinal detachment [13], and cataracts [14]. Most of these ophthalmic diseases are chronic and progressive, and in many cases treatment aims at symptom control, rather than cure. Early detection and intervention strategies to delay disease progression are therefore essential; however, existing treatments are often invasive and associated with significant side effects, or inadequate for reversing the underlying pathological mechanisms [15–17]. For instance, antivascular endothelial growth factor injection therapy for AMD can suppress drusen accumulation and macular edema; however, its limitations include low patient compliance, the burden of frequent injections, and limited visual recovery [18, 19].

Photobiomodulation therapy (PBMT) has recently gained attention as a noninvasive, cell-based, biological regulatory approach that can overcome the limitations of existing treatments. It generally uses low-power red or near-infrared light within the 600–1100 nm wavelength range to stimulate mitochondrial cytochrome C oxidase, thereby increasing adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production, reducing oxidative stress (ROS), modulating inflammatory responses, promoting tissue regeneration, and providing neuroprotection [20–23].

These mechanisms are effective for ophthalmic tissues—such as the retina, optic nerve, and cornea—which have high metabolic activity and are highly susceptible to oxidative damage. Various preclinical and early clinical studies have reported that PBMT improves functional visual outcomes, promotes tissue regeneration, regulates inflammation, and protects neural structures in conditions such as AMD, DR, glaucoma, and RP [24–28]. Several recent reviews have explored the potential of PBMT in ophthalmology, highlighting its therapeutic capacity and the methodological limitations of existing clinical studies. For instance, Fantaguzzi et al. [29] systematically reviewed the effects of PBMT on AMD, reporting visual improvements. However, the overall evidence was constrained by the inclusion of only three small-scale randomized controlled trials (RCTs), each with < 60 participants, and limited blinding or allocation concealment procedures.

Rassi et al. [30] conducted a meta-analysis specifically focusing on dry AMD that included three sham-controlled RCTs comprising 247 eyes; follow-up ranged from 9 to 13 months. Their results demonstrated statistically significant but clinically marginal improvements in best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA; mean difference (MD) = + 1.76 ETDRS letters; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.04–3.48), and a modest reduction in drusen volume (DV; MD = − 0.12 mm3; 95% CI: − 0.22 to − 0.02). However, neither outcome met the minimum clinically important difference thresholds of 6.8 letters for BCVA and 0.39 mm3 for DV. Trial sequential analysis further indicated that the pooled sample size was insufficient for reliable conclusions, with a required information size of 555 and 444 eyes for BCVA and DV, respectively. All included trials were judged to have at high risk of bias, especially for outcome measurement and incomplete data due to coronavirus diseases 2019–related disruptions.

A more recent meta-analysis conducted by Chen et al. [31] included six RCTs involving 360 patients (477 eyes); five of these patients were eligible for quantitative synthesis. No significant improvement in BCVA (standardized mean difference (SMD) = − 0.30; 95% CI: − 0.85 to 0.26; p = 0.30) or DV (SMD = − 0.08; 95% CI: − 0.52 to 0.37; p = 0.74) was demonstrated, and high and moderate heterogeneity were observed for visual outcomes (I2 = 83%) and anatomical endpoints (I2 = 48%), respectively. The authors emphasized that short-term improvements in quality of life did not translate into meaningful structural or functional retinal benefits, despite including more studies than previous reviews. The overall risk of bias ranged from low to moderate, and the follow-up durations were too short to assess long-term effects.

These meta-analyses indicate that PBMT may produce significant short-term effects; however, the clinical relevance of these findings remains uncertain. This uncertainty is attributable to three factors: first, the small sample sizes of the studies included in the review resulted in insufficient data for reliable conclusions; second, the heterogeneity of the intervention protocols precluded the establishment of clear guidelines; and third, the quality of the available evidence was found to be low to moderate, which is generally considered the lowest level of evidence that can be used to make reliable and valid conclusions. The substantial variability in treatment parameters—such as wavelength (range, 590–850 nm), energy dosage, treatment frequency, and definitions of sham controls—poses additional challenges to generalizability and hinders the synthesis of consistent conclusions. Ahn et al. [26] conducted a targeted review evaluating the effects of light irradiance and PBMT on glaucoma. Their analysis—incorporating both preclinical models and a limited number of early-phase clinical studies—indicated that PBMT within the 625–635 nm red light spectrum may enhance retinal ganglion cell (RGC) survival, lower intraocular pressure (IOP), and attenuate oxidative stress associated with blue light exposure. However, the authors emphasized that most existing evidence was derived from animal studies and small-scale pilot trials. The absence of adequately powered RCTs and lack of standardized outcome measures were identified as significant limitations that hinder the strength and applicability of current findings within the context of glaucoma management.

Most systematic reviews and meta-analyses were restricted to single diseases (predominantly AMD), and have not evaluated the effects of PBMT across the broader spectrum of ocular diseases such as DR, DED, uveitis, and retinal vein occlusion. Furthermore, no meta-analyses have integrated data for multiple ocular conditions while systematically accounting for treatment variability.

These gaps are compounded by the limitations of individual studies, which include small sample sizes, inadequate randomization, and inconsistent trial design. Moreover, the treatment parameters for PBMT—such as wavelength, irradiance, energy fluence, session duration, and frequency—vary widely across studies, making it challenging to compare results or derive unified clinical guidance. The use of disease-specific clinical endpoints that are not directly comparable—including BCVA and DV for AMD, retinal thickness for DR, and IOP or visual field progression for glaucoma—further exacerbates this heterogeneity.

Considering these substantial limitations, this study will employ meta-regression analyses to explore how variations in PBMT treatment parameters—specifically, wavelength, irradiance, session frequency, and exposure duration—impact therapeutic outcomes. These analyses will be prespecified and primarily exploratory, aiming to generate hypotheses about parameter–outcome relationships, rather than establish definitive treatment windows. While the results may provide methodological insights to inform future trial designs, they will not be used to propose standardized clinical protocols at this stage.

The recognition of PBMT as a general therapeutic approach in ophthalmology depends on its clinical effects within specific ocular diseases, which need to be examined separately. Each disease entity has distinct pathological mechanisms and clinical manifestations. Accordingly, disease-specific comparisons will serve as the primary analyses, while any cross-disease explorations will be clearly designated as secondary and hypothesis-generating.

We will also investigate whether PBMT influences common visual function metrics and physiological biomarkers across these diseases. Standardized outcome measures—such as BCVA, retinal thickness measured using optical coherence tomography (OCT), electroretinogram (ERG) amplitude, tear production tests, and inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6 and IL-8)—allow for quantifiable comparisons across studies. In this protocol, these shared physiological outcomes will be assessed in a secondary exploratory framework to generate insights into potential cross-disease mechanisms, rather than to support claims of PBMT as a universal therapeutic strategy.

This study will systematically evaluate the effectiveness of PBMT for treating various ophthalmic diseases, and quantitatively analyze outcome differences according to treatment parameters, disease types, and key clinical measures through meta-analysis. Unlike previous reviews that focused on a single disease or lacked parameter-level comparisons, this study will integrate findings across diverse ocular conditions while clearly distinguishing between primary disease-specific analyses and exploratory cross-disease evaluations. This may address existing limitations in the literature by applying structured subgroup comparisons and carefully prespecified exploratory meta-regression analyses. Rather than identifying optimal treatment conditions or proposing clinical applicability, this review will emphasize hypothesis generation and methodological guidance for the design of future multicenter clinical trials.

Methods/design

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis will be conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines. The objective of this review will be to comprehensively evaluate the clinical effectiveness of PBMT for treating various ophthalmic diseases and analyze differences in outcomes based on treatment parameters and disease types. The protocol is registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD420251033569). Significant protocol alterations during the review process (e.g., modifications to eligibility criteria or analysis plan) will be recorded in PROSPERO and transparently disclosed in the final publication.

Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome (PICO) question

This review will address the following core research question: “What are the clinical effects of PBMT on various ophthalmic diseases, and how do disease-specific and physiological outcomes vary according to disease type and treatment parameters?” The review will adopt the PICO framework as follows: the population will comprise patients diagnosed with various ophthalmic diseases, including AMD, DR, RP, glaucoma, DED, uveitis, keratitis, conjunctivitis, optic neuropathy, retinal detachment, and cataracts; the intervention will be PBMT or low-level light therapy without restrictions on wavelength, power, or energy density; the comparators will be sham treatment, no treatment, standard care, or active controls, including pharmacologic or surgical interventions; and outcomes will be analyzed with a primary focus on disease-specific endpoints, such as BCVA and DV for AMD, retinal thickness and leakage for DR, IOP and visual field for glaucoma, and tear volume and cytokine concentrations for DED. Additionally, selected shared physiological or functional outcomes (e.g., OCT-based retinal thickness, ERG amplitude, tear production (such as Schirmer’s test), inflammatory markers (such as IL-6 and IL-8), and visual acuity improvements (such as ETDRS letters)) will be examined in a secondary exploratory framework to generate hypothesis-driven insights across conditions.

This approach prioritizes disease-specific comparisons while allowing exploratory analyses of shared outcomes to suggest potential common mechanisms, without implying a unified therapeutic effect across all ocular diseases.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Studies that meet the following criteria will be included: clinical studies involving human participants (including RCTs and nonrandomized comparative studies); studies that applied PBMT as an intervention, including those using light-emitting diode (LED) or laser-based light sources in the red or near-infrared range (600–1100 nm); studies that included a comparator group (such as sham, no treatment, or standard care); studies that reported quantitative clinical outcomes relevant to ophthalmic function (such as BCVA, retinal thickness, ERG amplitude, and IOP); and peer-reviewed articles published with accessible full text.

Exclusion criteria

The following will be excluded: preclinical animal or in vitro studies; reviews, expert opinions, or theoretical papers lacking original data; case reports or studies without control/comparator groups; duplicate publications with overlapping data (the most comprehensive report will be included); protocol-only papers or studies with inaccessible full texts; and conference abstracts without full data.

Search strategy

We will systematically search PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and Scopus for studies published up to July 31, 2025, without any restriction on the start date. Manual searching of reference lists (snowballing) will be conducted to identify additional eligible studies not captured through electronic searches. The complete draft of the PubMed search strategy, including Boolean operators and filters, will be provided in Supplementary Appendix 1 to ensure reproducibility.

Study selection process

Two reviewers will independently screen the titles and abstracts of all retrieved studies to identify potentially eligible articles. The full texts of the selected studies will be reviewed in detail based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and any disagreements between the reviewers will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Data extraction

Data extraction will be independently performed by two reviewers using a standardized form and will include the following items: study characteristics (author, year, country, study design, and ophthalmic disease); participant information (age, sex, diagnostic criteria, and sample size); and intervention details (wavelength, irradiance, energy density, exposure time, and frequency).

The clinical outcomes will be extracted with a primary focus on disease-specific endpoints (e.g., BCVA for AMD, IOP for glaucoma, and OCT thickness for DR). Shared functional or physiological outcomes (e.g., ERG amplitude, tear cytokines, and visual acuity) will also be collected, but only analyzed in a secondary exploratory framework to generate hypotheses about potential cross-disease mechanisms.

In cases where necessary data are absent or presented in different formats, calculations will be conducted to ascertain the missing values (e.g., transforming standard errors into standard deviations). If multiple values are reported at different time points, the final postintervention value will be extracted.

All retrieved records will be organized utilizing reference management software (e.g., EndNote), ensuring that duplicates are eliminated before the screening process. The records for study selection will be monitored utilizing an Excel-based log, and the data obtained from eligible studies will be systematically stored and organized within Microsoft Excel spreadsheets. Consistent backups will be conducted, and access will be limited to the review team. Any disagreements in data extraction will be resolved through discussion or third-party adjudication. The authors of the original studies will be contacted to obtain missing information if needed.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias for each study will be independently assessed by two reviewers. The Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2.0) tool will be used to assess the RCTs, and the ROBINS-I tool will be used to evaluate nonrandomized comparative studies. The results of the risk of bias assessments will be summarized in tabular form, and disagreements will be resolved through consensus or third-party consultation.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative synthesis will be conducted when at least two studies report on the same outcome using comparable definitions and measurement methods, and when sufficient data are available to compute effect sizes. For continuous outcomes, the MD or SMD with 95% CIs will be calculated. For dichotomous outcomes, odds ratios or risk ratios will be reported. All outcome data will be synthesized using a random-effects model (DerSimonian and Laird method) to account for between-study variability. A narrative synthesis will be conducted if quantitative synthesis is inappropriate owing to a lack of data or excessive clinical or methodological heterogeneity.

This review will primarily conduct disease-specific meta-analyses for each ophthalmic condition using its respective primary clinical outcomes (e.g., BCVA for AMD, IOP for glaucoma). Additionally, selected shared physiological or functional outcomes (e.g., OCT-based retinal thickness, ERG amplitude, tear cytokine concentration) will only be examined in a secondary exploratory framework to generate hypotheses about potential cross-disease mechanisms, without implying pooled clinical effects across conditions.

Statistical heterogeneity will be assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I2 statistic, with I2 > 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity. For high heterogeneity, subgroup analyses will be performed according to disease type and PBMT treatment parameters (e.g., wavelength, dose, session duration, and frequency). Meta-regression analyses (prespecified for wavelength, irradiance, fluence, session duration, and treatment frequency) will be conducted to examine how these PBMT variables influence treatment effects on disease-specific outcomes, with exploratory analyses of shared outcomes only included for hypothesis generation. Publication bias will be assessed using funnel plots, Egger’s test, and the trim-and-fill method when applicable. All analyses will be conducted using R software (with packages such as the metafor package) or JASP. p-values < 0.05 will denote statistical significance. The certainty of evidence for key outcomes will be assessed using the GRADE approach, and findings will be explicitly categorized (high, moderate, low, or very low) to ensure transparency regarding the strength of evidence.

Discussion

Rationale and significance of the study

PBMT is a noninvasive, light-based therapeutic approach that has demonstrated promising effects in modulating cellular bioenergetics and ROS, and promoting anti-inflammatory and regenerative pathways [32–36]. These properties suggest that PBMT may have potential relevance for various ophthalmic diseases. Considering the chronic and progressive nature of many ophthalmic conditions, and the limitations of current standard treatments—which are often invasive, costly, or only partially effective—PBMT represents a promising area for further investigation as a complementary and noninvasive strategy [37, 38]. This systematic review and meta-analysis will aim to synthesize the available evidence across multiple ophthalmic disease categories and provide exploratory insights that may inform hypothesis generation and the design of future clinical studies, rather than draw conclusions about immediate clinical adoption.

Biological mechanisms and therapeutic potential of PBMT in ophthalmic diseases

PBMT primarily targets cytochrome C oxidase in the mitochondrial respiratory chain, thereby enhancing ATP synthesis, mitigating ROS, and activating intracellular signaling pathways related to tissue repair and inflammation control [39–42]. These biological effects are relevant to ophthalmic tissues such as the retina, optic nerve, and cornea, which have high metabolic demands and are susceptible to oxidative damage [43, 44]. PBMT may slow photoreceptor degeneration and improve retinal pigment epithelium function in AMD [45–47]. Moreover, it has the potential to preserve RGCs and visual field integrity in glaucoma, independent of IOP modulation [48, 49]. PBMT also supports meibomian gland function and tear film stability in DED, likely through anti-inflammatory pathways [50, 51]. These effects suggest disease- and tissue-specific mechanisms of action, reinforcing the therapeutic potential of PBMT.

Disease-specific effects of PBMT across ophthalmic conditions

PBMT has been investigated for its potential effects in different ophthalmic diseases based on the preclinical and clinical literature. For AMD, studies have reported possible improvements in BCVA, modest reductions in DV, and the stabilization or reduction of retinal thickness (measured by OCT). Additionally, PBMT may mitigate photoreceptor degeneration and support retinal pigment epithelium mitochondrial health. For DR, preliminary findings suggest decreases in vascular leakage, hemorrhage area, and retinal edema; PBMT is believed to modulate the vascular endothelial growth factor pathways and oxidative stress, thereby reducing microvascular damage. For glaucoma, experimental and pilot clinical studies indicate potential neuroprotective effects on RGCs, maintenance of visual field, and structural integrity of the optic nerve; PBMT may enhance axonal transport and reduce excitotoxicity. For RP, small-scale studies suggest possible enhancement of ERG amplitude, preservation of peripheral visual field, and reduction in fundus autofluorescence abnormalities; PBMT may delay photoreceptor loss by supporting mitochondrial bioenergetics. For DED, early clinical evidence has shown increases in tear secretion, improved tear breakup time, and reduced concentrations of inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-6 and IL-8). These benefits are likely mediated by the effects of PBMT on the meibomian glands and ocular surface inflammation. For keratitis and conjunctivitis, preliminary findings indicate faster epithelial healing, improved corneal transparency, and reduced neovascularization or hyperemia; PBMT may accelerate wound healing and suppress inflammatory neovascular signaling. For uveitis and retinal vein occlusion, some studies suggest decreased retinal inflammation and fluid accumulation, with potential improvements in visual acuity, possibly through immune modulation and vascular stabilization. Mechanisms include immune modulation and vascular stabilization. For optic neuropathy and retinal detachment, exploratory evidence indicates retinal layer preservation and partial prevention of functional vision loss; PBMT may promote axonal regeneration and photoreceptor viability. For cataracts, limited exploratory evidence exists suggesting a potential delay in lens opacification through a reduction in oxidative damage to the lens epithelium; however, further investigation is needed.

These heterogeneous findings will be synthesized and compared, primarily at the disease-specific level, with results interpreted cautiously to generate hypotheses and inform directions for future research, rather than to establish definitive therapeutic effects.

Shared functional and physiological outcomes of PBMT

This review will incorporate a secondary exploratory framework focused on shared physiological and functional outcomes across different ophthalmic conditions. These outcomes include retinal thickness (as measured by OCT), ERG amplitude, tear production, inflammatory biomarkers, and visual acuity improvements. While these metrics are valuable for assessing the biological impact of PBMT, their interpretation must be cautious considering the heterogeneity of disease mechanisms.

This exploratory framework will allow hypothesis generation regarding whether PBMT may influence common pathways across conditions, such as oxidative stress reduction, mitochondrial stimulation, and inflammation control. Rather than supporting the immediate generalizability of PBMT as a universal ophthalmologic therapy, these analyses aim to identify potential response patterns and candidate biomarkers that could inform the design of future disease-specific trials.

Addressing parameter heterogeneity and meta-regression strategy

A critical challenge in PBMT research is the significant heterogeneity in treatment parameters. The studies conducted to date vary widely regarding wavelength, irradiance, fluence, exposure duration, treatment frequency, and delivery devices. This variability can profoundly influence treatment outcomes and makes cross-study comparisons difficult.

In this review, heterogeneity will be primarily examined within disease-specific analyses, with subgroup analyses conducted according to prespecified PBMT parameters such as wavelength, fluence, and session frequency. Exploratory meta-regression analyses will also be considered to generate hypotheses about how treatment parameters may relate to clinical and physiological outcomes. These analyses are intended to provide methodological insights for future research, rather than to define optimized or condition-agnostic treatment protocols.

Clinical and translational implications

The noninvasive nature, safety profile, and potential for repeated use suggest that PBMT may hold promise as a complementary approach to conventional therapies for chronic ophthalmic conditions. Although the emergence of commercial PBMT devices—such as the CE-certified Valeda system—and ongoing regulatory reviews (such as by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA)) indicate growing interest, they also highlight the need for more rigorous evidence before widespread clinical use. The compactness of LED-based PBMT systems raises the possibility of future home-based applications, which remain exploratory at this stage.

This review will evaluate both disease-specific and shared outcomes. The findings will be interpreted cautiously and are intended to generate hypotheses regarding potential condition-specific effects and physiological response markers. Rather than informing immediate implementation strategies, the results aim to provide methodological insights to guide the design of future clinical studies.

Limitations and future directions

This protocol addresses several ophthalmic diseases; however, certain limitations should be considered. First, the considerable heterogeneity in study designs and treatment parameters may result in high statistical heterogeneity, which may limit the interpretability of pooled estimates. Second, some diseases—such as retinal detachment and cataracts—have limited available data, which could restrict comparative analyses. Third, the inclusion of studies using combination therapies may confound the ability to isolate PBMT-specific effects. Fourth, nonrandomized studies are expected to carry a high risk of bias; although the ROBINS-I tool will be used to evaluate these studies, the risk of bias will be explicitly acknowledged when interpreting pooled estimates. Additionally, all subgroup and meta-regression analyses will be restricted to prespecified variables (wavelength, irradiance, fluence, session duration, and treatment frequency) to minimize the risk of post hoc data dredging and enhance the reliability of the findings. Finally, publication bias remains a concern, especially considering the predominance of positive results in early-stage intervention studies.

Future research should aim to refine PBMT protocols through disease-specific trials and exploratory evaluations of shared physiological markers. Large-scale, multicenter RCTs should incorporate standardized clinical and physiological outcomes; additional stratification by disease subtype and responsiveness in exploratory markers (such as tear cytokine concentrations and OCT metrics) may help generate hypotheses about therapeutic windows. These steps will be essential for improving the methodological rigor of PBMT research and guiding the design of future clinical investigations.
