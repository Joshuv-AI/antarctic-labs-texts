1.3.
Construction of the Random Weight
w
:

In order to prove Theorem
6

B_{R}
into a collection of finitely overlapping unit balls
\{\alpha_{k}\}
that cover
B_{R}
. Since
\text{Vol}(B_{R})\sim R^{d}

\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k=1}^{CR^{d}}

Our construction of a random weight is quite elementary and informally its description is as follows: at each site
\alpha_{k}
we independently flip a coin with probability of success

\alpha_{k}
then we add the characteristic function of
\alpha_{k}
to our weight

More precisely, let
\{\delta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{CR^{d}}
be an i.i.d. collection of selector random variables where
\mathbb{P}(\delta_{k}=1):=\delta=O(\frac{1}{R})
and
\mathbb{P}(\delta_{k}=0)=1-\delta
, for each
1\leq k\leq CR^{d}
. We set

. From here we see that
\mathbb{E}(\norm{w}_{1})\sim\mathbb{E}(\sum_{k=1}^{CR^{d}}\delta_{k})\sim R^{d-1}
, since each of the
\alpha_{k}

and are finitely overlapping.

From the previous line, it is clear that with high probability the random weight

\norm{w}_{1}\sim R^{d-1}
, so property 3 of Theorem

satisfies property 2 of Theorem

The main technical difficulty lies in establishing that property 1 holds with high probability. We show property 1 is true by establishing the following theorem:

Theorem 9
.

In the setting coming from the discussion above, the following holds:

\mathbb{E}\sup_{\norm{g}_{L^{2}(\Sigma)}\leq 1}\int\absolutevalue{Eg(x)}^{2}w(x)dx\lesssim R^{\epsilon}.

Theorem

\sup_{\norm{g}_{L^{2}(\Sigma)}\leq 1}\int\absolutevalue{Eg(x)}^{2}w(x)dx\lesssim R^{\epsilon},

which in turn implies (by rescaling) that

\int_{B_{R}}\absolutevalue{Eg(x)}^{2}w(x)dx\lesssim_{\epsilon,d}R^{\epsilon}\int_{\Sigma}\absolutevalue{g(\omega)}^{2}

Proposition 10

Let

1.4.
Suprema of Stochastic Processes:

The key inspiration behind the proof of Proposition
10
is to use ideas from the theory of bounding the expected supremum of stochastic processes. We can reframe (
10
) more abstractly as a question that asks for tight bounds for the quantity

(11)

\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in T}X_{t},

for some stochastic process
(X_{t})_{t\in T}
. There is an incredibly rich and interesting theory surrounding this family of problems and we refer the reader to Talagrand’s book
[
21
]
and the references therein for a comprehensive introduction. The study of the expected suprema of Gaussian processes is especially comprehensive; when
(X_{t})_{t\in T}
is a Gaussian process it is known that the quantity (
11
) is completely determined by the metric induced by the
L^{2}
distance between random variables on the index set
T
, given by
d(s,t)=(\mathbb{E}(X_{t}-X_{s})^{2})^{1/2}
for
s,t\in T
. A powerful strategy to used bound the expected supremum of a Gaussian process is called the
chaining
method. An informal description of chaining for Gaussian processes is as follows: assuming the process is centered
5
5

5

That is,
\mathbb{E}X_{t}=0
for all
t\in T
.
we can write

\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in T}X_{t}=\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in T}(X_{t}-X_{t_{0}}),

and then write
(X_{t}-X_{t_{0}})=\sum_{k}(X_{\pi_{k}(t)}-X_{\pi_{k-1}(t)})
as some telescoping sum, where the
\pi_{k}(t)
’s can be thought of as successive approximations to
t
in the metric space
(T,d)
. One can then use measure concentration bounds
6
6

6

In the case of a Gaussian process, one usually uses the Gaussian tail bound:
\mathbb{P}(\absolutevalue{X_s-X_t}>\lambda)\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-\lambda^{2}}{2d(s,t)^{2}}\right)
.
to control the size of the increments
X_{\pi_{k}(t)}-X_{\pi_{k-1}(t)}.
A celebrated application of the chaining method is
Dudley’s inequality
which states in this setting that

\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in T}X_{t}\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log N(T,d,\epsilon)}d\epsilon,

where
N(T,d,\epsilon)
is the
\epsilon

covering number
of
T

\epsilon

(T,d)
required to cover

[
21
]
and
[
22
]
for excellent introductions to the chaining method and applications. Our method for proving Proposition
10
comes from the chaining method, along with some basic tools from restriction theory and is inspired by the important works
[
4
]
,
[
20
]
, and
[
19
]

Remark 11
.

The astute reader may have realized that the quantity

\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in T}X_{t}

may not be well defined if
T
is uncountable (since the random variable
\sup_{t\in T}X_{t}
may not even be measurable). The fix for this is to alter the definition of the expected supremum of a stochastic process so that all the previous arguments make sense by defining

\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in T}X_{t}\coloneqq\sup_{F\subseteq T,\text{ finite}}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in F}X_{t}.

We will proceed with this convention for this paper as well.

A key idea is to gain control of covering numbers of polytopes in Hilbert space. This goes back to work of Maurey, which is often referred to as the
empirical method
. Maurey’s method can be adapted to more general settings, and for our purpose we require the following version.

Theorem 13
.

Let
(\mathcal{H},\norm{\cdot})
be a Hilbert space (over
{\mathbb{R}}
). Consider
y_{1},y_{2},...y_{n}\in\mathcal{H}
and suppose
\norm{y_k}\leq K
for some absolute constant
K
. Let
\mathcal{C}=\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}y_{k}:\sum_{k=1}^{n}\absolutevalue{\alpha_k}\leq 1\}
(that is,
\mathcal{C}
is the balanced convex hull of
\{y_{1},...,y_{n}\}
). Then
\log\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{C},\norm{\cdot},\epsilon)\lesssim\frac{\log n}{\epsilon^{2}}
.

Proof.

Fix
x\in\mathcal{C}
. We may write
x=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}y_{k}
with
\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\absolutevalue{\lambda_k}}\leq 1
. Define the (
\mathcal{H}
valued) random variable
Y
with the following distribution:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y=y_{k})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{k}\quad\text{for
}1\leq k\leq n

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y=0)

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\absolutevalue{\lambda_k}

Clearly,
\mathbb{E}(Y)=x
. Let
Y_{1},...,Y_{k}
be i.i.d. copies of
Y
. We now have the following estimate:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\norm{\frac{1}{k}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k} Y_j \right) - x}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\mathbb{E}\norm{\sum_{j=1}^{k} (Y_j - x)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\mathbb{E}\langle(Y_{i}-x),(Y_{j}-x)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\norm{Y_j-x}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{k}\quad\text{(since $\norm{Y_j- x}\leq 2K$.)}

where the second line follows from the following fact that if
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
are independent
\mathcal{H}
-valued random variables with mean
0
, then
\mathbb{E}\langle Z_{1},Z_{2}\rangle=0
. We refer the reader to Lemma
30
of the appendix.

As a consequence of this estimate we note that there is a
realization
of
(Y_{1},...,Y_{k})
such that

\norm{\frac{1}{k}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k} Y_j \right) - x}\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}},

and thus for
k
with
k\sim\lceil{\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}}\rceil
, then there is a realization of
(Y_{1},...,Y_{k})
such that

(17)

\norm{\frac{1}{k}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k} Y_j \right) - x}\leq\epsilon.

For such
k
, let
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{H}
be the set

\left\{\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}:x_{1},...,x_{k}\subset\{0,y_{1},...,y_{n}\}\right\}.

(
17
) shows that
\mathcal{F}
is an
\epsilon
- net for
\mathcal{C}
. Moreover, a simple counting argument gives us that
\absolutevalue{\mathcal{F}}\leq(n+1)^{k}
. Thus
\log\absolutevalue{\mathcal{F}}\leq k\log(n+1)\lesssim\frac{\log n}{\epsilon^{2}}
. Thus,

\log\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{C},\norm{\cdot},\epsilon)\leq\log\absolutevalue{\mathcal{F}}\lesssim\frac{\log n}{\epsilon^{2}},

which proves the claim.
∎

Next we use a duality result. For optimal concentration of measure, we need control of covering numbers for a ‘dual problem’.

By definition

. Now,

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}\left(\left(\bigcap_{v=1}^{u}Y_{v}\right)^{c}\cup A^{c}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\sum_{v=1}^{u}\mathbb{P}(Y_{v}^{c})-\mathbb{P}(A^{c})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(A)-\sum_{v=1}^{u}\mathbb{P}(Y_{v}^{c})

On the other hand, by (

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{B},\norm{\cdot}_{\sim},2^{-k-1})\exp(-2^{2k+3}t \log\log R)

Putting everything together we see that:

\mathbb{E}\sup_{\norm{g}_{L^{2}(\Sigma,{\mathbb{R}})}\leq 1}\sum_{k=1}^{CR^{d}}\absolutevalue{\int_{\alpha_k} \tilde{E}g(x)dx}^{2}\lesssim(\log R)^{O(1)}.

4.1.
Proof of the Main Theorem (Theorem

We now show that our construction of (random) weights has the property that they have large mass and low tube occupancy, completing the proof of Theorem

Lemma 28
.

Given our definition of
\{\delta_{k}\}
, we define the (random) set
S=\{k:\delta_{k}=1\}
. We define the (random) weight

as
\sum_{k\in S}\delta_{k}\mathbbm{1}_{\alpha_{k}}
. With this, with high probability the following two properties hold:

(1)

\norm{w}_{L^{1}(B_{R})}\sim R^{d-1}

(2)

\sup_{T\in\mathbb{T}}w(T)\lesssim_{\epsilon}R^{\epsilon}

Proof.

Property 1 follows from the fact that
\mathbb{E}(\absolutevalue{S})\sim R^{d-1}
. Focusing on property 2, we first note that there are
\sim R^{2(d-1)}
distinct 1 - tubes covering
B_{R}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and
\sim R
many unit balls
\alpha_{k}\subset T
. We find that given a 1-tube
T
, we can estimate the probability that

exceeds some threshold

[

\mathbb{P}(w(T)>t)=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{\alpha_{k}\subset T}\delta_{k}>t\right)\lesssim\left(\frac{C}{t}\right)^{t},

therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{T\in\mathbb{T}}w(T)>\log R\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{T\in\mathbb{T}}\{w(T)>\log R\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}}\mathbb{P}\left(w(T)>\log R\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim R^{2(d-1)}\left(\frac{C}{\log R}\right)^{\log R}\rightarrow 0.

Thus, with very high probability,
\mathbb{P}(\sup_{T\in\mathbb{T}}w(T)\leq\log R)
which establishes property 2.

are mutually independent for all
k
, and thus the random variables
\mathbbm{1}_{A_{k}}
are i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables with mean
O(\frac{1}{R})
. Now the first term above can be estimated as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{\norm{g}_{L^{2}(\Sigma)\leq 1}}\sum_{k}\overline{w_{k}}\int_{\alpha_{k}}\absolutevalue{Eg(x)}^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq 2d\cdot\mathbb{E}\sup_{\norm{g}_{L^{2}(\Sigma)\leq 1}}\sum_{k}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{k}}\int_{\alpha_{k}}\absolutevalue{Eg(x)}^{2}dx

\displaystyle\sim_{d}\mathbb{E}\sup_{\norm{g}_{L^{2}(\Sigma)\leq 1}}\sum_{k}\delta_{k}\int_{\alpha_{k}}\absolutevalue{Eg(x)}^{2}dx

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\epsilon,d}R^{\epsilon}.

where the last line follows from our main proposition, Proposition
40
. We turn to estimating the second term. By the Agmon–Hörmander trace inequality we have the crude estimate that
\int_{\alpha_{k}}\absolutevalue{Eg(x)}^{2}dx\leq\int_{B_{R}}\absolutevalue{Eg(x)}^{2}dx\lesssim R\norm{g}_{L^{2}(\Sigma)}^{2}
. As a consequence,

\displaystyle\leq R\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{k:w_{k}>2d}(\tilde{w_{k}}-2d)\right)

\displaystyle\leq R\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{k:\tilde{w_{k}}>2d}\tilde{w_{k}}\right).

We turn to estimating the term
\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{k:\tilde{w_{k}}>2d}\tilde{w_{k}}\right)
, which by linearity of expectation can be rewritten as
\sum_{k=1}^{CR^{d}}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{w_{k}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tilde{w_{k}}>2d\}})
. Estimating one of the terms in this sum we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\tilde{w_{k}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tilde{w_{k}}>2d\}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2d+1}^{R^{d-1}}j\mathbb{P}(\tilde{w_{k}}=j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2d+1}^{R^{d-1}}j\binom{R^{d-1}}{j}\left(\frac{1}{CR^{d}}\right)^{j}\left(1-\frac{1}{CR^{d}}\right)^{R^{d-1}-j}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j=2d+1}^{R^{d-1}}j\left(\frac{R^{d-1}}{j}\right)^{j}\left(\frac{1}{CR^{d}}\right)^{j}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j=2d+1}^{R^{d}-1}\frac{1}{j^{j-1}R^{j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{R^{2d}}\sum_{j=2d+1}^{R^{d}-1}\frac{1}{j^{j-1}R^{j-2d}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{R^{2d}}.

Adding all of these terms together we get that

\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{k:\tilde{w_{k}}>2d}w_{k}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{CR^{d}}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{w_{k}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tilde{w_{k}}>2d\}})\lesssim CR^{d}\cdot\frac{1}{R^{2d}}\sim\frac{1}{R^{d}}.

As a consequence,

\mathbb{E}\sup_{\norm{g}_{L^{2}(\Sigma)}\leq 1}\sum_{k:\tilde{w_{k}}>2d}(\tilde{w_{k}}-2d)\int_{\alpha_{k}}\absolutevalue{Eg(x)}^{2}dx\lesssim\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{k:\tilde{w_{k}}>2d}\tilde{w_{k}}\right)\lesssim R\cdot\frac{1}{R^{d}}\lesssim 1.

Combined with our previous result this proves Theorem
43
.
∎

5.1.
Some Useful Probability Results

Lemma 30
.

Let
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
be two independent and identically distributed
\mathcal{H}
- valued random variables that take finitely many values with mean
0
. Then,
\mathbb{E}\langle Z_{1},Z_{2}\rangle=0
.

Proof.

Fix any
w\in\mathcal{H}
. We first show that
\mathbb{E}\langle Z_{1},w\rangle=0
for all
w\in\mathcal{H}
. Since
Z_{1}
has finite support we may assume that
Z_{1}
has the following distribution:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}=x_{k})=\alpha_{k}

for some
x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n}\in\mathcal{H}
and
\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
with
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}=1
. Moreover, since
\mathbb{E}Z_{1}=0
, we have that
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}x_{k}=0
. Now the (
\mathbb{R}
- valued) random variable
\langle Z_{1},w\rangle
has the distribution given by:

\mathbb{P}(\langle Z_{1},w\rangle=\langle x_{k},w\rangle)=\alpha_{k}.

Thus,
\mathbb{E}\langle Z_{1},w\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\langle x_{k},w\rangle\mathbb{P}(\langle Z_{1},w\rangle=\langle x_{k},w\rangle)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}\langle x_{k},w\rangle=\langle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}x_{k},w\rangle=\langle 0,w\rangle=0
.
We now prove the lemma by conditioning on
Z_{2}
by using the tower property for expectations.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\langle Z_{2},Z_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{Z_{2}}(\langle Z_{1},Z_{2}\rangle|Z_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z_{2}}(0)

\displaystyle=0

The first line follows from the tower property of conditional expectations and the independence of
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
. The second line follows from the fact that
\mathbb{E}\langle Z_{1},w\rangle=0
for every
w\in\mathcal{H}
. This proves the claim.
∎

The following result first appeared in
[
20
]
, but we include a short proof of this here for completeness.

Proposition 31

(Variant of Bennett’s inequality
[
20
]
)
.

Consider a random variable
Z
with
\absolutevalue{Z}\leq 1
a.e,
\mathbb{E}Z=0
, and
EZ^{2}\leq\delta
. Let
(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be a sequence of i.i.d. copies of
Z
, and let
a=(a_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be a sequence of real numbers. Then for all
t>0
, we have the following large deviation inequality:

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}Z_{k}\geq t\right)\leq\exp(- \frac{t}{\norm{a}_{\infty}} \log\frac{t\norm{a}_{\infty}}{\delta\norm{a}_2^2}).

Proof.

We have by Chebyshev’s inequality that for all
\lambda>0
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}Z_{k}\geq t\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\exp(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda a_k Z_k)\geq\exp(\lambda t)\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\lambda t}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left(\exp(\lambda a_k Z_k)\right)

Now observe that the inequality

(44)

e^{x}\leq 1+x+\frac{1}{2}x^{2}e^{\absolutevalue{x}}

is true for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
(this becomes clear if you expand both sides as power series).
Thus,

\mathbb{E}(\exp(\lambda a_k Z_k))\leq 1+\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}a_{k}^{2}\delta e^{\lambda\absolutevalue{a_k}}\leq\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}a_{k}^{2}e^{\lambda\absolutevalue{a_k}}\right).

Plugging this into our original estimate gives us that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}Z_{k}\geq t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\lambda t+\frac{\lambda^{2}\delta\norm{a}_{2}^{2}}{2}e^{\lambda\norm{a}_{\infty}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\lambda t+\frac{\lambda\delta\norm{a}_{2}^{2}}{2\norm{a}_{\infty}}e^{2\lambda\norm{a}_{\infty}}\right)

(The last step is justified by noting that
x\leq e^{x}
when
x>0
so that
\frac{e^{\lambda\norm{a}_{\infty}}}{\norm{a}_{\infty}}\geq 1
.)
Setting

\lambda=\frac{1}{2\norm{a}_{\infty}}\log\frac{t\norm{a}_{\infty}}{\delta\norm{a}_{2}^{2}},

proves the claim.
∎

5.2.
Some Useful Results from Fourier Analysis

We begin by collecting some standard ‘local constancy lemmas’ that are used to supplement to proof of Proposition
10
.

Lemma 32

(Convolution Lemma)
.

For all
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
we have
Eg_{i_{1},...i_{k}}*\eta(x_{0})
where
\eta
is a Schwartz function with the property that

(1)

\hat{\eta}\equiv 1
on
B(0,1)\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

(2)

\hat{\eta}\equiv 0
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus B(0,2)

(3)

\eta(x)\leq C_{N}(1+\absolutevalue{x})^{-N}
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

The proof of the first two items is a straightforward consequence of the convolution property for Fourier transforms. Letting
\eta
be a standard bump function that satisfies properties 1 and 2 above, we have

\displaystyle Eg_{i_{1},i_{2}...,i_{k}}*\eta(x_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}Eg(y)\eta(x_{0}-y)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}g(\omega)\omega_{i_{1}}\omega_{i_{2}}...\omega_{i_{k}}\int_{\Sigma}e^{2\pi i\omega\cdot y}\eta(x_{0}-y)dyd\sigma(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R^{d}}}g(\omega)e^{2\pi i\omega\cdot x_{0}}\omega_{i_{1}}\omega_{i_{2}}...\omega_{i_{l}}\int_{\Sigma}e^{-2\pi i\omega\cdot u}\eta(u)dud\sigma(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}g(\omega)e^{2\pi i\omega\cdot x_{0}}\omega_{i_{1}}\omega_{i_{2}}...\omega_{i_{l}}\hat{\eta}(\omega)d\sigma(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}g(\omega)e^{2\pi i\omega\cdot x_{0}}\omega_{i_{1}}\omega_{i_{2}}...\omega_{i_{l}}d\sigma(\omega)

\displaystyle=Eg_{i_{1},i_{2},...i_{l}}(x_{0})

where the third lines follows from Fubini’s theorem, fifth line follows from the fact that
\hat{\eta}(\omega)\equiv 1
for all
\omega\in\Sigma
, since
\Sigma\subseteq B(0,1)
.

The third property follows the fact
\eta
is a Schwartz function (by virtue of being the Fourier transform of a Schwartz function.)
∎

Lemma 33

(Local Constancy Lemma)
.

For any
x_{i}\in B_{R}
we have
\absolutevalue{Eg_{i_1,i_2,...i_l}(x_i)}^{2}\lesssim\norm{Eg_{i_1,i_2,...,i_l}}^{2}_{L^{2}({w_{x_{i}}})}
, where
w_{x_{i}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is a weight with the property that
w_{x_{i}}(y)\leq C_{N}(1+dist(y,x_{i}))^{-N}
for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Using Lemma
32
write

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{Eg_{i_1,i_2,...,i_l}(x_i)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\absolutevalue{Eg_{i_1,i_2,...,i_l} * \eta(x_i)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\absolutevalue{\int_{\R^d}Eg_{i_1,i_2,...,i_l}(y) \eta(x_i-y)dy}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\absolutevalue{Eg_{i_1,...,i_l}(y)}^{2}\absolutevalue{\eta(x_i-y)}dy\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\absolutevalue{\eta(x_i-y)}dy

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\absolutevalue{Eg_{i_1,i_2,...,i_l}(y)}^{2}\absolutevalue{\eta(x_i-y)}dy

where the third line follows by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Setting
w_{x_{i}}(y):=\absolutevalue{\eta(x_i-y)}
, we see that as a consequence of Property 3 of Lemma
32
, that
w_{x_{i}}(y)\leq C_{N}(1+dist(x_{i},y))^{-N}
.
∎

Lemma 34

(Pointwise weight inequalities)
.

Let
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a collection of points in
B_{R}
that are
R^{-\epsilon}
-seperated. Then for any
y\in B_{2R}
, we have the pointwise weight inequality:

\sum_{i\in I}w_{x_{i}}(y)\lesssim_{\epsilon,d}R^{\epsilon d}.

Proof.

Fix any
y\in B_{2R}
. for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
C_{k}=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:2^{k-1}R^{-\epsilon}\leq dist(x,y)<2^{k}R^{-\epsilon}\}
. Note that we have
\mathrm{vol}(C_{k})\sim_{d}2^{kd}R^{-d\epsilon}
. Since
I
is a maximal
R^{-\epsilon}
separated set,
\mathrm{card}({C_{k}\cap I})\lesssim_{d}2^{kd}
. Thus

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}(y)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sum_{i\in C_{k}\cap I}w_{i}(y)

\displaystyle\leq C_{N}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}2^{kd}\cdot(2^{k}R^{-\epsilon})^{-N}

where the above inequality holds for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, by Lemma
33
. Setting
N=2d
completes the proof.
∎

The next lemma will be crucially used in the proof of Proposition
27
.

Lemma 35
.

Let
\{x_{j}\}_{i\in I}
be a
R^{-\epsilon}
- separated subset of
B_{R}
. Then

\sum_{j\in I}\absolutevalue{Eg(x_j)}^{2}\lesssim_{\epsilon}R^{\epsilon d}\int_{B_{2R}}\absolutevalue{Eg(x)}^{2}dx+R^{-10000d}\norm{g}_{L^{2}(\Sigma)}^{2}

Proof.

By Lemma
33
, we have that
\absolutevalue{Eg(x_j)}^{2}\lesssim\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\absolutevalue{Eg(y)}^{2}w_{x_{j}}(y)dy
for each
i\in I
. Thus

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in I}\absolutevalue{Eg(x_j)}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{R^{d}}\absolutevalue{Eg(y)}^{2}\left(\sum_{j\in I}w_{j}(y)\right)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{2R}}\absolutevalue{Eg(y)}^{2}\left(\sum_{j\in I}w_{j}(y)\right)dy+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus B_{2R}}\absolutevalue{Eg(y)}^{2}\left(\sum_{j\in I}w_{j}(y)\right)dy

The pointwise weight inequality given in Lemma
34
shows that the first term is
\lesssim_{\epsilon}R^{\epsilon d}\int_{B_{2R}}\absolutevalue{Eg(x)}^{2}dx
. To estimate the second term, we note that if
\absolutevalue{y}\geq 2R
, then
\sum_{i\in I}w_{x_{i}}(y)\leq C_{N}\mathrm{card}(I)(\absolutevalue{y}-R)^{-N}\lesssim_{d}C_{N}R^{(1+\epsilon)d}(\absolutevalue{y}-R)^{N}
. Using the Hölder and Tomas-Stein inequalities we have:

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus B_{2R}}\absolutevalue{Eg(y)}^{2}\left(\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}(y)\right)dy

\displaystyle\lesssim\norm{Eg}_{\frac{2d+2}{d-1}}^{2}C_{N}\left(\int_{\absolutevalue{y}\geq 2R}(\absolutevalue{y}-R)^{-\frac{N(d+1)}{2}}\right)^{\frac{2}{d+1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\epsilon,d,N}\norm{g}_{L^{2}(\Sigma)}^{2}\left(\int_{\absolutevalue{y}\geq R}u^{-\frac{N(d+1)}{2}}du\right)^{\frac{2}{d+1}}

Choosing
N
to be sufficiently large (e.g.
N=10^{6}d
) will result in the integral above being bounded by (up to an absolute constant depending on
d
)
R^{-10000d}
.
∎

Remark 36
.

One can replace
Eg
with
Eg_{i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{l}}
in the above proof, and an analogous statement is true.

At many stages in the proof we rely on this classical fact that the Mizohata–Takeuchi Conjecture is known to be true when
w
is the characteristic function of
B_{R}
. This is referred to in the literature as the Agmon–Hörmander Trace inequality. The proof is well known and is essentially a consequence of Plancherel’s theorem, but we record it here for completeness.

Proposition 37

(Agmon–Hörmander Trace Inequality, M–T conjecture is true for
w(x)=\mathbbm{1}_{B_{R}}
)
.

\int_{B_{R}}\absolutevalue{Eg(x)}^{2}dx\lesssim R\int_{\Sigma}\absolutevalue{g(\omega)}^{2}d\sigma(\omega).

Since
\Sigma
is a compact hypersurface, we may (locally) parameterize
\Sigma
as
(\omega^{\prime},\Sigma(\omega^{\prime}))
where
\omega^{\prime}=(\omega_{1},...,\omega_{d-1})
with
\omega^{\prime}\in[0,1]^{d-1}
(say). Denote the spatial variable
x
as
(x^{\prime},x_{d})
, where
x^{\prime}=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{d-1})
. We may write

\int_{B_{R}}\absolutevalue{Eg(x)}^{2}dx=\int_{B_{R}}\absolutevalue{\int_{[0,1]^{d-1}}g(w',\Sigma(\omega')) e^{2\pi i \omega' \cdot x'} e^{2\pi i \Sigma(\omega') \cdot x_d }N(\omega') d \omega'}^{2}dx^{\prime}dx_{d}

where
\absolutevalue{N(\omega')}\lesssim_{d}1
for all
\omega^{\prime}\in[0,1]^{d-1}
, with
\norm{g}_{L^{2}(\Sigma)}\sim\norm{\tilde{g}}_{L^{2}[0,1]^{d-1}}
, where
\tilde{g}(\omega^{\prime})\coloneqq g(\omega,\Sigma(\omega))
. By integrating in
x^{\prime}
first and using Plancherel’s theorem (with repsect to the function
g(w^{\prime},\Sigma(\omega^{\prime}))e^{2\pi i\Sigma(\omega^{\prime})\cdot x_{d}}N(\omega^{\prime})
), and then integrating in
x_{d}
, we see that the above expression can by bounded by
\sim R\norm{g}_{{L^{2}(\Sigma)}}^{2}
as desired.

Remark 38
.

A similar statement is true for the operator
Eg_{i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{l}}
