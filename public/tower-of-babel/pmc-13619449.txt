Ultrahigh pressure phase transition in ice
Authors: Israel Osmond, Mikhail A Kuzovnikov, David A Lewis, Hannah A Shuttleworth, Tomás Marqueño, Milo J Dixon, Cerian E A Robertson, Vitan Cherepnalkoski, Bihan Wang, Andreas Hermann, Miriam Peña-Alvarez, Ross T Howie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOne of the most enduring problems in dense H2O ice has been the experimental realization of the pressure-induced symmetry breaking of cubic ice X to an orthorhombic crystal structure. Here, through diamond anvil cell experiments combined with synchrotron X-ray diffraction and Raman spectroscopy techniques, we demonstrate that post-symmetrization, the crystal structure of ice X continuously distorts with pressure. Subjecting ice to pressures in excess of 308(5) GPa, we observe a first-order phase transition to ice XXII, consistent with the long-predicted phase possessing an orthorhombic crystal structure with Pbcm symmetry. We find that ice XXII is stable to pressures of at least 340(5) GPa.Subject terms: Structure of solids and liquids, Phase transitions and critical phenomena, Phase transitions and critical phenomena

Main

Ice exists in the most extreme environments of the universe, from comets travelling through the cold vacuum of space, to the hot, dense interiors of giant planets. Terrestrial experiments continue to discover new forms of ice at high pressure–temperature conditions1–9, such as superionic states, which bridge the stability fields of solid and fluid water5,10. Concurrently, state-of-the-art theoretical works predict a rich landscape of unexplored structural phases of ice, which seemingly are beyond the current technical limitations of high-pressure experimentation11–17.

Despite exhibiting a complex pressure–temperature phase diagram, upon room-temperature compression, the phase sequence of ice is relatively simple, with only three known solid phases (VI, VII, X), all of which have been extensively studied18–21. Ices VII and X share the same body-centred cubic crystal lattice of oxygen atoms and only differ in the arrangement of their hydrogen atoms22. Upon compression, the isolated H2O molecules of phase VII become indistinguishable, transforming to hydrogen-ordered phase X, within which hydrogen atoms are midway between nearest-neighbour oxygen atoms23–28.

For almost 30 years, density functional theory (DFT) calculations have predicted that cubic ice X should transform to an orthorhombic crystal structure (with Pbcm symmetry) at higher pressures12,17,28–30. This orthorhombic modification of crystalline ice is predicted to be stable over a vast pressure regime, exceeding that of any other experimentally observed phase8,12,17. The transition to Pbcm ice has never been experimentally realized, and, up until now, remained one of the most important unresolved problems in the most abundant molecule on Earth.

Despite the importance for planetary science, the studies of H2O at such ultrahigh pressures are limited owing to the experimental challenge. This is due to a combination of constrained small sample volumes, weak Raman scattering cross section and ice inherently being a poor X-ray scatterer. Nevertheless, vast improvements have been made in diagnostic techniques, namely the brilliance of synchrotron sources, combined with the focusing capabilities at X-ray diffraction beamlines31,32.

To investigate the properties of ice at ultrahigh pressure conditions, we have performed a series of static compression experiments, reaching pressures in excess of 330 GPa. Through synchrotron X-ray diffraction and Raman spectroscopy measurements, we demonstrate that post-symmetrization of hydrogen bonds, the crystal lattice of ice X continuously distorts upon compression. Above 308(5) GPa, we observe a first-order phase transition from distorted ice X to ice XXII, which is consistent with the previously predicted Pbcm phase.

Compression of ice X

The molecular structures of all currently known ices obey the so-called H2O ice rules—each oxygen atom is bound to four neighbouring oxygen atoms by hydrogen bonds, and each bond is realized by one hydrogen atom2,5,33–36. At room temperature, fluid H2O crystallizes into ice VI with P42/nmc space group at 1.0 GPa, before transforming to ice VII with Pn3¯m symmetry at 2.2 GPa (refs. 37,38) (see phase diagram in Fig. 1). At pressures above 70 GPa, ice VII continuously transforms to ice X and it is indistinguishable from a supercritical crossover25,26,39. Ices VII and X share the same body-centred cubic crystal lattice of oxygen atoms and only differ in the arrangement of their hydrogen atoms22,40.

While the transition from ice VII to ice X is unidentifiable in X-ray diffraction experiments, the Raman spectra provide clear signatures of the changing crystal symmetry, arising from shifts in hydrogen atomic positions. These changes are not necessarily centred on the phase boundary or Widom line but are nonetheless characteristic of the phase change39. Upon compression, it has been previously shown that the symmetric stretching mode of H2O ice VII is not observed above 60 GPa, while above 80 GPa the translational modes are superseded by a single band in phase X26. According to group theory analysis, there is only one allowed Raman active phonon (T2g) in Pn3¯m H2O ice X, corresponding to the vibrations of oxygen atoms surrounded by stationary hydrogen atoms26,28.

We have conducted high-resolution Raman spectroscopy measurements of ice X up to pressures of 253 GPa (Fig. 2). We observe the appearance of the T2g mode above 80 GPa, which is in agreement with a previous study that tracked the frequency dependency up to a maximum pressure of 128 GPa (ref. 26). However, we find that the T2g mode continues to broaden until it splits into two distinct and resolvable modes above 160 GPa. While both modes harden with pressure, the splitting increases (for example, Δν ≃ 52 cm−1 and 69 cm−1 at 167 GPa and 253 GPa, respectively). This was observed in all experimental runs and the frequency of each mode at a given pressure was consistent between experiments. The splitting of the T2g mode is indicative of a change in the symmetry of the oxygen lattice. Concomitantly, the X-ray diffraction measurements in all experimental runs demonstrate a marked asymmetry in the (110) reflection of ice X at pressures above ~100 GPa (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Figs. 1, 5, 10 and 11), coinciding with the splitting of the T2g Raman mode. We attribute this to a weak tetragonal distortion of the crystal lattice. Unfortunately, within this pressure regime, the (200) and (211) reflections are too weak to discern any asymmetry.

Within calculations, any distorted structures of ice X are mechanically unstable below 300 GPa and relax back to cubic (Pn3¯m) symmetry. In fact, a mechanical instability (c12 > c11) of the Pn3¯m model for ice X only develops around 500 GPa, well within the energetic stability region of the predicted Pbcm phase (see Supplementary Fig. 2 for the calculated elastic stiffness coefficients of ice X). This instability leads to a tetragonal variant of ice X (space group P42/nnm) that has been predicted recently as a metastable phase17. As the only available candidate structure, we have used the P42/nnm structural model in the Rietveld refinements above 100 GPa to account for a possible distortion. While the predicted phase is elongated along c at 500 GPa (c/a ~1.20), our measurements find a small compression of c, which increases with pressure (for example, c/a ~0.99 at 140 GPa to c/a ~0.97 at 300 GPa). We have simulated Raman spectra of different tetragonally distorted variants of ice X at a series of densities. We find good agreement between the experimental and calculated Raman frequencies at the experimentally determined distortions (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3). Very similar behaviour is seen in NH4F, an ionic ice analogue, where NH4F-III (a structural analogue of ice X) develops a tetragonal distortion across a wide pressure range, before transforming to a new high-pressure phase41. Similarly, it would seem that this tetragonal distortion is intrinsic to ice X and not due to strain effects within the sample chamber. An assessment of whether these observations could be attributed to deviatoric stress conditions of the sample is included in the Supplementary Information.

Transition to ice XXII

Upon further compression, we observe distorted ice X up to 308 GPa, above which there are distinct changes in the X-ray diffraction pattern (Fig. 3a). Over a pressure regime of ~10 GPa, we observe further splitting of the first ice X reflections into 3 distinct components, indicative of a transformation to another polymorph, henceforth called ice XXII.

The presence of three distinct components in the main group of peaks suggests that the symmetry of ice XXII is no higher than orthorhombic. The higher-angle peak at 10° is more intense than the lower-angle peaks. This indicates that the orthorhombic distortion is applied to the 1×2×2 supercell of the cubic structure rather than to the original cubic unit cell, which would otherwise result in a splitting into three components with equal intensities. Such a distortion corresponds to an Abmm space group. In some of the ice XXII patterns (such as that shown in Supplementary Fig. 5), we observe a weak feature at 10.5°, which could be indexed as (021). Among the maximal subgroups of Abmm, only Pbcm and Pbmb allow the presence of the (021) reflection. Pbmb can be rejected on the grounds that it should also have an appreciable intensity of the (001) peak at 4.7°, which is not observed experimentally. Thus, the Pbcm space group provides the highest symmetry compatible with the experimentally observed X-ray diffraction patterns of ice XXII.

DFT-based computational quantum chemistry has previously predicted that H2O should transform into an orthorhombic structure with Pbcm space group upon compression above 250–350 GPa (refs. 12,17,28–30). Figure 3c shows a comparison of the crystal structure factor of ice XXII at 338 GPa with the simulated structure factor of the predicted Pbcm model, relaxed with DFT at 400 GPa, with the refined lattice parameters a = 2.279 Å, b = 3.562 Å and c = 3.495 Å. Despite the weak sample signal and strong grain effect, we observe a good agreement regarding the intensity distribution in the main peak group. The intensities of the (100), (110) and (021) reflections of the Pbcm model, expected at 7.3°, 8.7° and 10.5°, respectively, depend on the atomic coordinates of the oxygen atom, O (0.251, −0.054, 1/4), which were taken from the DFT calculation at 400 GPa. These coordinates were fixed in the Rietveld refinement to directly compare the structure factor of the predicted Pbcm model with the experiment. Refining these coordinates does not significantly improve the residual R-factors, which are mostly limited by the grain effect.

There is also good agreement regarding the magnitude of the orthorhombic splitting (right inset in Fig. 3), and the pressure at which the peak splits, which allows us to assign a Pbcm crystal structure model to ice XXII. Structurally, the O–H–O bonds remain the same in Pbcm compared with ice X, but adjacent layers in the ab plane are sheared with respect to each other (inset in Fig. 1). Both the structure and transition pressure are in good agreement with previous predictions12,17,28–30. Upon the transition to ice XXII, we observe a discontinuity in volume of −0.04 Å3 per H2O, which is consistent with our DFT calculated value of −0.02 Å3 per H2O and indicative of a first-order phase transition (Fig. 4). In one experimental run, pressure dropped from 338 GPa to ~39 GPa owing to the failure of one of the diamond anvils, which resulted in a transformation back into ice VII (Supplementary Fig. 7).

To assess possible non-hydrostatic effects, we have compared the X-ray diffraction patterns collected at each pressure from the sample and the gold pressure marker (Supplementary Figs. 4–6 and 8). As gold is surrounded by the sample, the possible non-hydrostatic effects should affect both patterns. Particularly, if the observed peak splitting in the X-ray diffraction patterns of the sample at around 308 GPa is to be associated with non-hydrostaticity, the comparable changes should be additionally observed in the gold pattern. However, no pressure-induced changes in the profile of the gold reflections are observed, which rules out such an interpretation.

In a subsequent experimental run, we were able to reproduce the transition to ice XXII. However, the data quality was much poorer, giving more data scatter in the extracted volumes, with the pressure overestimated owing to the gold calibrant being compressed against the gasket. This data can be found in Supplementary Figs. 9, 10 and 12. Taking this overestimation into account, this experimental run demonstrated ice XXII to be stable to at least 355(10) GPa.

We were unable to measure the Raman spectra of ice XXII owing to the weak Raman scattering of H2O, combined with increased pressure-induced fluorescence engulfing the sample signal in the low frequency regime at pressures above 250 GPa. However, extrapolating the Raman frequencies of the modes observed in distorted ice X, the values are consistent with two of the most intense modes calculated through DFT of Pbcm ice (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Figs. 3 and 14). This further suggests that the distorted ice X is an intermediary state leading to phase XXII. While complementary Raman and IR spectroscopy studies exploring the ice X to XXII transition could provide invaluable information, inherent limitations of current diamond anvil cell experiments would hinder such studies. In the case of Raman studies, all but one of the most intense Raman modes for the Pbcm structure are in a frequency regime that would be obscured by the stressed diamond anvil (grey shaded area in Fig. 2b). Although the IR stretching band of ice X is predicted to split upon the transition to ice XXII, the most intense modes lie at extrapolated frequencies of ice X and would most probably be indiscernible (Supplementary Figs. 3, 15 and 16).

Ice XXII is predicted to be stable over a vast range of pressure–temperature space, with another orthorhombic modification, with Pbca symmetry, expected to emerge at 760 GPa (ref. 11). However, this is currently beyond the experimental limitations of conventional static high-pressure experiments and poses the next generational challenge. Shock studies have shown that at temperatures exceeding 2,000 K and in the pressure regime where we observe phase XXII, ice adopts an fcc structure, ice XVIII3. Bridging the gap between static and dynamic compression experiments to understand the XXII–XVIII transition, as well as how these relate to the superionic states, will be the subject of great future interest.

In summary, we observe a distortion of ice X above pressures of 160 GPa, leading to the formation of ice XXII, at 308(5) GPa; to our knowledge, this is the most dense H2O polymorph ever observed (Fig. 1). Ice XXII possesses an orthorhombic crystal structure, which is in agreement with quantum chemistry calculations predicting a Pbcm phase29. Our study is an advancement towards understanding the internal structure of giant planets, and provides evidence that the state-of-the-art quantum chemistry calculations based on DFT accurately reproduce the behaviour of H2O under such extreme environments.

Methods

Sample preparation and pressure calibration

We conducted a total of 10 independent experimental runs up to a maximal pressure of 355 ± 5 GPa. Two of these experimental runs exceeded pressures required for the transition to phase XXII. Pressure was generated using a custom-designed (piston–cylinder type SH) symmetric diamond anvil cell. These cells were equipped with Boehler-Almax cut diamonds from Almax-easyLab (type IIas for X-ray diffraction, type IIam for Raman spectroscopy), with culet diameters ranging from 15 μm to 50 μm bevelled to a diameter of 200 μm. We used either Al2O3 composite gaskets or rhenium gaskets. The Al2O3 composite gaskets were created by initially indenting 200 μm rhenium foil to a thickness of ~6 μm. The foil was then drilled using a custom-built UV laser micromachining system with a hole diameter slightly larger than the culet diameter. An Al2O3-epoxy powder was then placed in the indent and compressed until a thickness of ~6 μm was achieved. The sample chamber was then drilled using the UV laser micromachining system to a diameter approximately 50% culet size. In other experiments, rhenium foils were indented to a thickness ranging between 5 μm and 10 μm, and sample chambers drilled to a diameter approximately 50% culet size. In all experiments, high-purity Milli-Q H2O was loaded. No reaction was observed between the rhenium or the Al2O3 composite gasket and H2O over the pressure regime studied.

In X-ray diffraction studies, a small amount of gold powder was placed inside the sample chamber, and pressure was determined using the previously established volume (pressure) equation of state of gold43. In Raman spectroscopy measurements, the pressure was determined using the frequency of the stressed edge of the diamond anvil. The first-order diamond Raman band becomes elongated in frequency space with pressure. The frequency of the stressed culet was determined by the frequency (ν) at which dI/dν was minimized (where I is the intensity of the mode). Pressure was calculated using the calibration given in ref. 44.

X-ray diffraction measurements

Angular-dispersive powder X-ray diffraction experiments above pressures of 250 GPa were performed at the Extreme Conditions Beamline (P02.2) at PETRA III, Hamburg, Germany32. Additional data below 250 GPa were collected at the ID15B beamline at the ESRF, Grenoble, France31. Typically, the diamond anvil cell was oscillated around the ω axis by either ±5° or ±10°, and the 2D diffraction image was collected with acquisition times ranging between 60 s and 240 s.

At P02.2, we used an incident X-ray beam with E ≈ 42.4 keV (λ ≈ 0.292 Å), focused to a 2 μm × 2 μm spot using Kirkpatrick–Baez mirrors. Diffraction images were recorded using a PerkinElmer XRD1621 detector with a sample-to-detector distance (SDD) of ~400 mm, as calibrated with a CeO2 standard. At ID15B, an incident beam with E ≈ 30.2 keV (λ ≈ 0.410 Å) was focused to an approximately 1 μm × 1 μm spot. The diffraction patterns were collected with an EIGER2 X 9M detector with SDD ≈ 158 mm. The SDD was calibrated using a Si powder standard.

The SDD, detector orientation and wavelength calibration, primary processing, azimuthal integration and first background subtraction were performed with the DIOPTAS v0.6.1 software45. All detector images collected upon compression from each individual cell were processed with the same mask to enable common background subtraction at a later stage. At pressures of about 350 GPa, the ratio of the maximum diffraction signal to the background owing to Compton scattering from diamonds was between 1/50 and 1/300 depending on the experimental run. Spatial variations of the detector counting efficiency produced additional intensity variations of about 1/200. To correct for these, we manually subtracted a common background from each pattern after proper exposure normalization (Supplementary Fig. 17). Rietveld refinements were done with the POWDERCELL 2.4 programme46 and FULLPROF 7.00. Only the first peak group in the patterns, resulting from the splitting of the (110) reflection from ice X, was clearly visible up to the highest pressure, and for this reason, only the peaks from this group were used for the refinements. For the Pbcm model, only the lattice parameters and, in some cases, the March parameter for the March–Dollase (100) preferred orientation model were refined, whereas the atomic parameters were taken from DFT and fixed to directly compare the structure factor of the predicted Pbcm model with the experiment. Atomic displacement parameters were not refined owing to the narrow useful range of momentum transfers.

Raman spectroscopy measurements

A custom-built Raman spectroscopy setup was used to collect Raman spectra in a confocal backscattering geometry. We used a 514.53 nm Ar-laser (Innova 70C, Coherent), with a typical output power of 20 mW, as the excitation source. An IsoPlane 320 spectrometer (Princeton Instruments) equipped with 1,200 mm−1 and 1,800 mm−1 gratings and a liquid nitrogen cooled PyLoN CCD (Princeton Instruments) was used. Before collecting each spectrum, the spectrometer was calibrated using an Ar–Ne lamp. Typical spectra were collected with acquisition times of 60 s and 10 accumulations.

DFT calculations

We performed DFT calculations with the CASTEP code47 using norm-conserving pseudopotentials. A plane wave cut-off of 1,200 eV and a Monkhorst–Pack48 k-point grid with spacing 0.04 Å−1 was sufficient to give enthalpy differences between ice X and Pbcm, which were converged to better than 1 meV per formula unit. Of the different exchange-correlation functionals tested (BLYP49, PBE50, PBEsol51, revPBE52 and revPBE+D4 (refs. 52–55)), we found that the BLYP functional P(V) equation of state aligns best with the experimental data for ices X and XXII, and was used for all phonon, IR and Raman calculations. For a given pressure, BLYP has the largest cell volume, and revPBE+D4 the smallest. The predicted transition pressure from ice X to Pbcm varies by almost 50 GPa across all functionals considered. The calculated X → XXII transition pressures are approximately 260 GPa, 300 GPa, 320 GPa, 330 GPa and 340 GPa for PBEsol, PBE, revPBE, revPBE+D4 and BLYP, respectively. We further find a ground state transition from the proton-ordered ice VIII to ice X of ~130 GPa with BLYP. Standard DFT calculations are known to overestimate the pressure onset of stability for ice X owing to additional quantum effects that are not accounted for here24,39. Phonon frequencies and IR/Raman intensities were obtained with CASTEP within the density functional perturbation theory framework56. Phonon density of states calculations used a q-point Monkhorst–Pack grid spacing of 0.08 Å−1 and a fine interpolation grid with 0.02 Å−1 spacing. Elastic stiffness coefficients were obtained from linear stress–strain relationships derived from appropriate lattice strains of magnitudes ±0.5% and ±1%, and subsequent relaxation of atomic positions (for example, see refs. 57–59). Raman spectra for tetragonally distorted ice X were obtained for several pressure points, along a series of volume-conserving tetragonal distortions of the ice X unit cells that covered the range c/a ≈ 0.85–1.15.
