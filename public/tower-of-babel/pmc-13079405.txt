Feasibility and Challenges of Telemedicine in Delivering Emergency Healthcare Among Digitally‐Connected Populations in Yemen: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Waheeb Naser, Yaser Aljaro, Mohammed Alzubairy, Yavuz Yigit, Waleed Salem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsYemen's healthcare system is severely weakened by prolonged conflict, limited resources, and workforce shortages, resulting in poor health outcomes. Telemedicine may offer solutions for emergency care provision in such fragile systems, yet its feasibility and adoption in Yemen remain unclear. This study aimed to assess the feasibility of telemedicine for emergency healthcare among digitally connected individuals in Yemen and to identify key barriers and predictors of adoption.MethodsA cross‐sectional survey targeted adults aged 18–80 years with internet access across Yemen, conducted from November 2024 to December 2024. A self‐administered, piloted questionnaire delivered via KoboToolbox collected data on demographics, telemedicine awareness and use, and perceived barriers. Main outcomes were telemedicine awareness, utilization for emergency care, and reported barriers. Descriptive statistics summarized participant characteristics and key measures; logistic regression identified predictors of telemedicine adoption.ResultsAmong 263 respondents (mean age 33.8 years, SD 10.5; 71.1% male), 59.3% reported frequent digital device use and 57.4% had reliable internet. Only 21.3% were aware of telemedicine. Emergency telemedicine use was reported by 18.3%, mainly for pain management (62.5%) and pediatric emergencies (25.0%), while use for stroke, cardiac, trauma, and obstetric emergencies was less common. Adoption was higher among healthcare workers (45.7% vs. 20.9%; OR = 2.14, 95% CI: 1.27–3.61) and those with reliable internet (26.5% vs. 18.3%; OR = 1.85, 95% CI: 1.05–3.24). Major barriers included poor connectivity (73.0%), limited trust or awareness (49.0%), insufficient staff training (43.7%), and weak infrastructure (42.2%).ConclusionTelemedicine is underutilized for emergency care among Yemen's digitally connected population, mainly due to infrastructure, digital literacy, and trust barriers. Addressing these gaps through improved connectivity, provider training, local guidelines, and policy development is essential to advance emergency telemedicine in fragile health systems.Keywords: adoption, conflict settings, digital health, emergency care, telemedicine, Yemen

Introduction

Yemen's healthcare system is among the world's most fragile, characterized by fewer than one physician per 10,000 people, life expectancy well below regional averages, and some of the highest maternal and infant mortality rates globally [1]. Even before the escalation of conflict, systemic weaknesses impeded the delivery of both essential and emergency care [2]. Current health indicators further illustrate this vulnerability: life expectancy remains just 67 years, infant mortality is over 50 per 1000 live births, and maternal mortality stands near 200 per 100,000 live births. Nearly two‐thirds of the population resides in rural areas, compounding barriers to timely emergency care (Table 1).

Years of war have deepened the crisis: destruction of hospitals and clinics, disrupted supply chains, and the collapse of pre‐hospital and in‐hospital emergency services have left millions without access to life‐saving interventions [3]. In this context, innovative solutions are urgently needed to restore and strengthen healthcare delivery. Telemedicine—the use of digital communication technologies for medical services—offers a promising approach to mitigate these systemic gaps [4, 5].

While telemedicine has been widely adopted for routine outpatient visits, chronic disease management, and health education, its deployment in emergency or acute care involves unique and far greater challenges. Emergency telemedicine (“tele‐emergency”) requires rapid, often life‐saving remote assessment, triage, and intervention for acute conditions such as trauma, stroke, cardiac arrest, or pediatric emergencies [6]. Compared to general telehealth, emergency applications demand more robust infrastructure, immediate expert engagement, and the ability to support high‐stakes, real‐time decision‐making under severe constraints.

Experiences from conflict‐affected countries such as Syria and Afghanistan have demonstrated that, when effectively structured, telemedicine can enable trauma management, remote diagnostics, and specialist consultations even in settings with minimal resources [3, 7, 8]. These examples highlight the potential of digital health technologies to preserve access to expert care when in‐person services are unavailable. Local experience in Yemen has also demonstrated the feasibility of telemedicine at the case level, with several reports describing successful remote consultations and interventions despite systemic challenges [9]. However, Yemen's compounding crises—including active conflict, infrastructure collapse, and workforce shortages—present even steeper barriers to implementation [2].

Evaluating whether and how telemedicine can feasibly deliver emergency care in Yemen is therefore an urgent research priority. This study aims to assess the feasibility, adoption, and key challenges of using telemedicine to support emergency healthcare provision among digitally‐connected populations in Yemen, with a specific focus on identifying barriers and predictors of adoption.

Material and Methods

Study Design

A cross‐sectional study was conducted from November 2024 to December 2024 to evaluate the adoption, feasibility, and barriers of telemedicine among digitally connected populations in Yemen. Eligible participants were adults aged 18 to 80 years who owned a digital device and had access to the internet.

Objectives

The primary objective of the study was to evaluate the feasibility of telemedicine for emergency healthcare in Yemen, with emphasis on accessibility, usage patterns, and perceived effectiveness. Secondary objectives included identifying barriers to adoption, such as infrastructure limitations, training needs, and socio‐cultural challenges, as well as exploring the potential integration of telemedicine into Yemen's emergency response system.

Data Collection Procedure

The self‐administered questionnaire consisted of 23 items divided into five sections [1]: demographic information [2], digital access and telemedicine awareness [3], patterns of telemedicine use [4], telemedicine perception and satisfaction, and [5] perceived barriers and challenges. It included multiple‐choice items, 4‐point Likert scales, and open‐ended questions. The questionnaire incorporated skip logic to enhance relevance and minimize respondent burden. Participants were initially asked about their awareness and prior use of telemedicine in Yemen; those reporting no prior use were automatically directed to the section on anticipated challenges and barriers, thereby bypassing items on usage patterns and satisfaction. This design ensured that experienced users provided detailed feedback while still capturing valuable perspectives from those without prior exposure. The instrument was pilot‐tested among 20 participants for clarity and reliability, with minor adjustments made before final distribution. A screenshot of the questionnaire is provided in the Supporting Information S1.

Survey Distribution

Data collection was performed using KoboToolbox, a platform optimized for surveys in remote and conflict‐affected settings. To maximize participation, survey links (in both English and Arabic) were distributed via WhatsApp and other social media groups, allowing respondents to participate securely and conveniently.

Sampling Method and Setting

A convenience sampling strategy was used, as reaching conflict‐affected populations posed substantial logistical challenges. While this approach allowed for broad recruitment via digital platforms, it limits the generalizability of findings, particularly for those without stable internet, as well as residents of remote rural areas and high‐conflict zones. For geographic classification, respondents were categorized according to their place of residence: urban areas included major cities such as Sana'a and Aden; rural areas were defined as districts where more than half of livelihoods were based on agriculture; and combat zones referred to governorates experiencing active conflict during the study period, including parts of Abyan, Dhala, Taiz, Marib, and Hodeidah.

Ethical Considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Conference on Harmonization—Good Clinical Practice guidelines (ICH‐GCP E6). Participation was voluntary, with informed consent obtained electronically through an introductory statement in the survey. Responses were anonymized to protect participant confidentiality. Ethical approval was granted by the Research and Ethics Committee, Faculty of Medicine and Health Sciences, University of Aden (REC‐209‐2024).

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using both descriptive and inferential statistics. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) with ranges, or as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) where distributions were non‐normal. Categorical variables were presented as counts (n) and percentages (%), with both numerators and denominators reported.

Prespecified analyses included χ 2 tests to examine associations between categorical variables (e.g., location and awareness of telemedicine), independent‐samples t‐tests to compare mean scores between two groups (e.g., HCWs vs. NHCWs satisfaction levels), and one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to evaluate differences across multiple groups (e.g., education level and perception of telemedicine effectiveness). Binary logistic regression was applied to identify predictors of telemedicine adoption, with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported. Ordinal logistic regression was used to model levels of trust in telemedicine based on demographic factors. Exploratory analyses were conducted to assess additional associations not originally specified in the study protocol (e.g., education and perceived effectiveness).

All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a significance level of α = 0.05 was applied. Exact p values are reported where relevant, except where p < 0.001. Bonferroni adjustments were applied in cases of multiple comparisons. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). To enhance data interpretation, various visualizations were generated, including bar charts providing a clear representation of key findings. Qualitative data from open‐ended questions were thematically analyzed to identify current practices, challenges encountered, and suggestions for telemedicine adaptation.

Study design and statistical analyses were conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines for observational research, referencing standard methodological works, including Altman's Practical Statistics for Medical Research and Hosmer & Lemeshow's Applied Logistic Regression.

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 263 participants were included, with a mean age of 33.8 years (SD = 10.5, range 15–71). The majority were male: 71.1% (187/263), while females accounted for 28.9% (76/263). Most participants resided in urban areas (74.1%, 195/263), with smaller proportions in rural areas (22.4%, 59/263) and conflict zones (3.4%, 9/263). By occupation, 39.9% (105/263) were healthcare workers (HCWs), and 60.1% (158/263) were non‐healthcare workers (NHCWs) (Table 2).

Access to Technology and Telemedicine Awareness

Frequent digital device use was reported by 59.3% (156/263) of participants. Reliable internet access was available to 57.4% (151/263), while 41.4% (109/263) reported intermittent or unreliable access. Only 21.3% (56/263) indicated prior telemedicine awareness. A χ 2 test showed a significant association between internet reliability and location (χ²(4) = 19.15, p = 0.001), indicating that urban residents were more likely to have stable connectivity than those in rural or conflict zones.

Telemedicine Usage

Among HCWs, 42.9% (27/63) reported delivering healthcare services via telemedicine, with physicians accounting for 58.7% (17/29) in this group. As shown in Figure 1, pain management was the most common service (62.5%, 15/24), followed by other emergencies (45.8%, 11/24) and pediatric emergencies (25.0%, 6/24). Acute emergencies were rarely managed remotely: trauma (18.8%, 3/16), cardiac events (14.6%, 2/16), stroke (8.3%, 1/12), and obstetric emergencies (8.3%, 1/12). These results suggest telemedicine is primarily used for noncritical cases, likely reflecting infrastructural limitations and the need for in‐person care in severe emergencies.

Binary logistic regression identified both occupation and internet reliability as significant predictors of telemedicine adoption. Adoption rates were substantially higher among healthcare workers (HCWs) than non‐healthcare workers (NHCWs): 48/105 (45.7%) of HCWs had adopted telemedicine compared to 33/158 (20.9%) of NHCWs. HCWs had over twice the odds of adopting telemedicine relative to NHCWs (odds ratio [OR] = 2.14, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.27–3.61, p = 0.005).

Internet reliability also played a significant role. Adoption was higher among participants with reliable internet access (40/151, 26.5%) versus those with unreliable or intermittent access (20/109, 18.3%). Reliable internet increased the odds of adoption by 85% (OR = 1.85, 95% CI: 1.05–3.24, p = 0.034). These findings underscore the dual importance of professional role and infrastructure reliability in shaping telemedicine uptake in Yemen.

Perception and Satisfaction With Telemedicine

Overall, 69.9% (184/263) believed that telemedicine could improve healthcare access in Yemen. Satisfaction levels were mixed: 39.2% (103/263) reported satisfaction, 35.7% (94/263) were neutral, and 9.9% (26/263) were dissatisfied. A one‐way ANOVA indicated that higher education was associated with more positive perceptions of telemedicine's effectiveness (F(4,258) = 3.92, p = 0.004). There was no significant difference in satisfaction by gender (t(261) = −0.61, p = 0.543). However, HCWs rated telemedicine as significantly more effective than NHCWs (t(261) = 2.26, p = 0.025), suggesting that professional familiarity fosters acceptance. Ordinal logistic regression was performed to assess how demographic factors relate to the level of trust in medical advice or diagnosis received via telemedicine. Only education level showed a statistically significant relationship (χ² = 21.22, p < 0.001), while gender, age, location, and healthcare job role did not demonstrate significant associations (p > 0.05 for each).

Barriers to Telemedicine Adoption

Participants identified multiple barriers. As shown in Figure 4, poor internet connectivity was the most frequently cited (73.0%, 192/263), followed by lack of trust or awareness (49.0%, 129/263), lack of trained staff (43.7%, 115/263), and insufficient infrastructure (42.2%, 111/263). Additional challenges included unreliable electricity (35.0%, 92/263), privacy concerns (25.9%, 68/263), and limited device access (23.6%, 62/263). These barriers collectively illustrate both systemic infrastructural weaknesses and significant socio‐cultural challenges impeding telemedicine expansion in Yemen.

Recommendations for Enhancing Telemedicine

Participants recommended expanding internet coverage and ensuring stable electricity to strengthen telemedicine infrastructure. Training programs for healthcare providers were suggested to improve digital literacy, and public awareness campaigns were proposed to boost trust, reduce cultural resistance, and educate patients about telemedicine′s benefits.

At the policy level, respondents highlighted the importance of forging partnerships with international organizations for infrastructure investment, developing Yemen‐specific telemedicine protocols tailored to emergency care, and integrating telemedicine into national health strategies. The establishment of regulatory frameworks for data privacy and cross‐border collaboration was also advised to ensure safety, trust, and continuity of care. Collectively, these reforms were viewed as essential for scaling and sustaining telemedicine in Yemen's conflict‐affected, resource‐limited settings.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that, despite its promise, telemedicine remains underutilized for emergency healthcare delivery among digitally connected populations in Yemen. Use for life‐threatening emergencies was low, largely reflecting major deficits in digital infrastructure, integration with emergency services, and clinician confidence. It is important to note that these findings represent internet‐enabled participants and may not generalize to Yemen's broader, often offline, population.

Our findings are consistent with research from other crisis settings, showing telemedicine's feasibility when supported by accessible technologies [10, 11]. In conflict‐affected contexts such as Syria and Afghanistan, low‐cost telemedicine has proven effective for noncritical cases and remote diagnostics when embedded within structured disaster response frameworks [3, 12]. By contrast, Yemen faces more pronounced infrastructure gaps, absence of standardized emergency protocols, and limited provider readiness, hampering application for acute emergencies [11, 13, 14].

In our study, telemedicine was most often used for pain management and pediatric emergencies, but rarely for stroke, cardiac, trauma, or obstetric emergencies Participants reported key barriers including inadequate connectivity, lack of clinical protocols, and provider hesitancy around remote, high‐stakes decision‐making. These findings align with challenges highlighted in prior international reports [11, 15, 16]. As a result, telemedicine in Yemen currently serves more as a supplementary than a primary tool for emergency care. Among the limited proportion (8.5%) of respondents who reported using dedicated telemedicine platforms, locally available services such as Tabibi/طبيبي [17], Tabiby Avicenna [18], Otlob Tabib/اطلب طبيب [19], and Reayt/رعاية [20] —accessible via mobile applications and web platforms—were identified. Tabibi and Tabiby Avicenna provide free, volunteer‐based consultations through chat‐based and advisory services [17, 18], whereas Otlob Tabib and Reayt combine teleconsultation with paid home‐visit and integrated care services [19, 20]. These emerging platforms complement the more widespread informal tools such as WhatsApp and regular audio calls used for daily medical communication, yet their penetration remains limited and their integration into formal emergency response pathways is embryonic.

In contrast with our findings, international studies and authoritative guidelines increasingly support an expanded, integrated use of telemedicine in acute emergency assessment, triage, and care, provided suitable infrastructure and standards are in place. The American College of Emergency Physicians (ACEP) and the World Health Organization (WHO) both endorse telemedicine for emergency and acute care—including triage, diagnosis, and critical care consultation—provided quality, privacy, and provider training requirements are met [21, 22]. In particular, the WHO highlights telemedicine as critical for health system resilience and emergency service continuity, especially during crises and outbreaks [16]. These recommendations underscore Yemen's untapped potential should system‐level barriers be addressed.

Adoption is further shaped by factors outlined in the Diffusion of Innovation Theory, such as observable benefit, peer influence, digital literacy, and regulatory safeguards [13, 14]. In this study, being a healthcare worker and having reliable internet access significantly increased telemedicine uptake, in line with literature underscoring the role of professional capacity and infrastructure [23, 24]. Our finding that healthcare workers were more likely to adopt telemedicine is consistent with recent global evidence. For example, a scoping review of telemedicine in cancer care during the COVID‐19 pandemic found that provider competence, confidence, and readiness are critical drivers of telehealth adoption and satisfaction [25]. This underscores the importance of targeted training and support for healthcare providers to maximize telemedicine uptake in resource‐limited settings.

Cybersecurity and data privacy present additional challenges. Robust legal frameworks addressing data protection and secure communication, as emphasized by Healey [23], have yet to be established in Yemen, raising concerns about patient confidentiality and further limiting telemedicine scale‐up. Nevertheless, a significant regulatory milestone was achieved in 2025 when the Ministry of Public Health and Population (MoPHP) issued Executive Regulation No. 71 on the licensing and organization of telehealth services [26]. Although the MoPHP currently operates no national telemedicine platform, this regulation provides the legal foundation for future national initiatives, setting standards for licensure, quality assurance, and integration with Yemen's digital‐health strategy. The existence of this regulatory framework represents an important enabler for scaling telemedicine services, though its enforcement and operationalization remain critical implementation challenges.

Policy and Implementation Implications

To facilitate sustainable integration, a phased approach is recommended: prioritize low‐bandwidth modalities such as audio calls and SMS, expand infrastructure through public–private and international partnerships, and develop Yemen‐specific protocols for emergency use [11, 15, 16]. Leveraging the newly established regulatory framework under Executive Regulation No. 71 (2025), efforts should focus on translating policy into operational guidelines, including standardized protocols for emergency tele‐triage, credentialing and licensing of telemedicine providers, and clear liability frameworks for remote consultations. Public–private partnerships with existing platforms such as Tabibi, Tabiby Avicenna, Otlob Tabib, and Reayt could accelerate service delivery by formalizing volunteer networks, ensuring quality standards, and expanding coverage to underserved populations. Community engagement and patient feedback systems can improve cultural acceptability and address social resistance, while regular audits and transparent reporting will help sustain public trust.

Given that life‐threatening emergencies such as cardiac arrest, stroke, and trauma were seldom managed remotely in this study, future implementation efforts should include targeted tele‐triage and remote consultation protocols tailored for these conditions. Pilot projects in high‐burden regions could evaluate response time, clinical outcomes, and cost‐effectiveness for these acute cases, informing scalable emergency telemedicine frameworks. Such pilots could build on the infrastructure of existing platforms while ensuring integration with emergency medical services (EMS), pre‐hospital care systems, and hospital emergency departments.

Aligned with the WHO's consolidated telemedicine implementation guidance, further steps should include developing comprehensive, costed investment plans and piloting roll‐outs in regions with the highest readiness [22]. Policy‐makers should institute ongoing monitoring and evaluation with equity‐focused indicators to avoid leaving behind vulnerable populations. Multisectoral collaboration beyond the health sector—including local government and private partners—will be critical for technical, regulatory, and financial sustainability. Regulatory frameworks should be broadened to address licensing, liability, record‐keeping, consent, and cross‐border care, in addition to privacy and data security. Integration with essential health services and early engagement of both users and providers will be essential to build trust, cultural acceptability, and long‐term adoption [22].

Limitations

This study offers important insights into the feasibility of telemedicine for emergency care in Yemen; however, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, recruitment was conducted exclusively through digital channels such as WhatsApp and social media, resulting in a sample representative primarily of Yemen's digitally connected population. Consequently, vulnerable groups—particularly those without reliable internet access in remote, rural, or conflict‐affected areas—were likely underrepresented, and findings should not be generalized to all Yemenis. Nonetheless, this approach enabled broad outreach and rapid data collection amidst significant logistical and security constraints.

Second, the reliance on self‐reported data may introduce recall or social desirability bias. While such measures are standard in telemedicine research and often unavoidable in challenging settings, they may affect the accuracy of reported behaviors and perceptions.

Third, the range of acute emergencies covered in the survey was limited, potentially underestimating telemedicine's broader applicability in emergency care.

Finally, the study did not assess long‐term outcomes, such as cost‐effectiveness, sustainability, or integration into national healthcare policy. Future research should address these gaps through larger, multi‐center studies, mixed‐methods approaches incorporating objective outcome measures, and targeted sampling of digitally disconnected and vulnerable populations.

Conclusion

Telemedicine offers considerable potential to improve access to emergency care in Yemen′s conflict‐affected health system but is currently limited by infrastructure, workforce, and policy barriers. Addressing these constraints through targeted investment, capacity building, regulatory reform, and trust‐building measures will be essential for realizing the full benefits of digital health in this and similar fragile contexts.
