1.
Introduction

An interesting aspect of the coarse geometry of a finitely generated group
\Gamma

is its
growth function

f_{(\Gamma,S)}(n)=\#\bigl\{g\in\Gamma:\|g\|_{S}\leq n\bigr\},

defined with respect to a finite generating set
S
and the associated word length
\|g\|_{S}
.

The subject originated in Milnor’s comparison of Riemannian volume growth with word-metric growth of a fundamental group.
Wolf refined this viewpoint, Gromov proved the landmark theorem that a finitely generated group has polynomial growth precisely when it is virtually nilpotent, and Grigorchuk’s celebrated 1984 example revealed the landscape also contains groups of intermediate (subexponential) growth.

Our focus will be the
exponential growth rate

\operatorname{gr}(\Gamma,S)\ =\ \lim_{n\to\infty}f_{(\Gamma,S)}(n)^{1/n}\ \in\ [1,\infty).

Different generating sets yield comparable—though not identical—values, so we fix a preferred
S
whenever we speak of
the
growth rate.
For a comprehensive treatment of growth rates, see
[
6
]
.

When
\Gamma
is a free group
F_{r}
of rank
r\geq 2
and
S
is the usual free basis,
the Cayley graph is the degree
(2r-1)
tree
T_{2r-1}
.
A direct computation shows
\omega(F_{r},S)=2r-1
.
If
H\leq F_{r}
is an arbitrary subgroup we measure its growth by

\omega_{F_{r}}(H)\;=\;\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{{}_{(H,F_{r},S)}}(n)^{1/n}\ \in\ [1,2r-1],

where
f_{{}_{(H,F_{r},S)}}(n)
counts the number of words in
H
of length
n
(in
T_{2r-1}
).
At the extremes, cyclic subgroups have growth
1

while finite‐index subgroups share the growth of
F_{r}
.
A natural, question is:

Which values are attained by
\omega_{F_{r}}(H)
as
H
ranges over all subgroups?

It was proven in
[
5
]
that the set of growth rates of finitely generated subgroups of
F_{r}
is dense in the interval
[1,2r-1]
.
It is natural to ask if every
\alpha\in[1,2r-1]
arises as the growth rate of some subgroup.
If we restrict to finitely generated subgroups the answer is no, as the growth rate of a finitely generated subgroup is an algebraic number (see Remark
Remark
).

However, if
H\leq F_{r}
is allowed to be infinitely generated, we prove:

Theorem 1.1

(Main Theorem)
.

Let
r\geq 2
and
\alpha\in[1,2r-1]
.
There exists a subgroup
H\leq F_{r}
whose
growth rate within
F_{r}
equals
\alpha
. That is

\omega_{F_{r}}(H)=\alpha.

This is a consequence of the following attractive result, which is of independent interest:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
J\rightarrow B
be an immersion of graphs where
B
is a finite bouquet of circles.
Let
J_{i}
be an increasing sequence of finite connected based subgraphs with
J=\cup_{i=1}^{\infty}J_{i}
.
Let
H=\pi_{1}J
and for each
i
, let
H_{i}=\pi_{1}J_{i}
, and regard these as subgroups of
F=\pi_{1}B
.
Then

\lim_{i\to\infty}\omega_{{}_{F}}(H_{i})\ =\ \omega_{{}_{F}}(H).

1.1

2.
Subgroups of the free group

Our main tool for studying the subgroups of the free group is through graphs and graph immersions, as popularized by Stallings
[
11
]
.

For a locally finite graph
G=(V,E)
and a vertex
v\in V(G)
, denote by
N_{G}(v)
the set of edges with (at least) one endpoint equal to
v
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
A
and
B
be two directed graphs.
An
immersion
of graphs is a function
f:A\rightarrow B
mapping vertices to vertices and edges to edges, which is locally injective:
f_{v}:N_{A}(v)\rightarrow N_{B}(f(v))
is injective for each
v\in V(A)
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
(A,a)
and
(B,b)
be directed based graphs.
Let
f:A\rightarrow B
be a basepoint preserving immersion.
Then
f_{*}:\pi_{1}(A,a)\rightarrow\pi_{1}(B,b)
is injective.

Proof.

This holds since the induced cover between universal covers is an immersion of trees, and hence an injection. See
[
11
, prop 5.3]
.
∎

Let
B_{r}
be a graph with one vertex and
r
directed edges.
Letting the basepoint of
B_{r}
be its vertex, we have
\pi_{1}B_{r}\cong F_{r}
, where
F_{r}
is a free group of rank
r
whose basis corresponds to the directed loops of
B_{r}
.
If
G
is a directed graph with an immersion from
G
to
B_{r}
and
v\in V(G)
is a vertex, then
\pi_{1}(G,v)\leq F_{r}

by Lemma
2.2
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected graph whose degrees are bounded by
2r
.
Then,
G
can be labeled and directed so that there is an immersion from
G
to
B_{r}
if and only if every finite subgraph of
G
can be labeled and directed in such a way.

Remark
.

Theorem
2.3
is more general than what needed for our purposes: we construct an infinite graph by gluing together immersed graphs along a long ray, hence the graph inherits that immersion.

If
G
is a finite, undirected graph whose vertex degrees are
\leq 2r
, then there is an immersion from
G
to
B_{r}
.
See
[
4
, p. 57]
, which we revisit in
[
5
, lem 2.2]
.
Thus we have the following:

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected graph whose degrees are bounded by
2r
.
Then the edges of
G
can be labeled and directed so that there is an immersion from
G
to
B_{r}
.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

The proof is similar to the proof of the de Bruijn–Erdős Theorem
[
2
]
.
Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected graph with maximal degree
2r
.
Labeling (and ordering) the edges of
G
means choosing a function
L:E\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,r\}\times\{\pm 1\}
, assigning to each edge of
G
a letter and a direction.
We use the notation
L(G)
for the directed, labeled graph obtained by applying
L
to
G
.
A function
L
is a
proper labeling
of
G
if there is an immersion
L(G)\rightarrow B_{r}
.

Give
\{1,\ldots,r\}\times\{\pm 1\}
the discrete topology, and define
X
to be the space of all possible labelings (proper or not) of
G
.
Then
X
has the product topology,
X=(\{1,\ldots,r\}\times\{\pm 1\})^{E(G)}
.
By Tychonoff’s theorem,
X
is compact.

For a finite subgraph
F\subset G
, the following set is closed:

X_{F}\ =\ \{L\in X:L\text{ is a proper labeling of }F\}

To see this, observe that a labeling
L
is proper if and only if every vertex
u\in L(G)
has neither two outgoing edges nor two incoming edges labeled with the same letter. Hence the following set of non-proper labelings is open:

X_{F}^{C}\ =\ \cup_{v\in V(F)}\cup_{e_{1}\neq e_{2}\in N(v)\times N(v)}\{L:L(e_{1})=L(e_{2})\}.

Moreover, for finite subgraphs
F_{1},\ldots,F_{k}
in
G
, we have

X_{F_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap X_{F_{k}}\ =\ X_{F_{1}\cup\cdots\cup F_{m}}

and since
X_{F_{1}\cup\cdots\cup F_{m}}\neq\emptyset
by our assumption, the sets
\{X_{F}:F\text{ is a finite subgraph}\}
satisfy the finite intersection property.
The compactness of
X
implies that
\cap_{F}X_{F}\neq\emptyset
, which completes the proof, as any
L\in\cap_{F}X_{F}
is a proper labeling for
G
.
∎

2.1.
Growth rates of graphs

Let
G
be an undirected, connected graph with vertex degrees bounded above by
2r
. Let
L
be a proper labeling of
G
, and let
v\in G
be a vertex.
By the discussion above,
\Gamma:=\pi_{1}(L(G),v)\leq\pi_{1}(B_{r})
.
We are interested in the growth rate of
\Gamma
in
\pi_{1}B_{r}
, with
\pi_{1}B_{r}
generated by the
r
labeled edges of
B_{r}
.

The map
L
induces an embedding of every closed non-backtracking loop based at
v
in
G
in the Cayley graph of
\pi_{1}B_{r}
(freely generated by the
r
edges), hence, the growth rate of
\Gamma
in
F_{r}
equals

\omega(G):=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\text{NB}_{v}(n)^{1/n},

where
\text{NB}_{v}(n)
is the number of
length
n
non-backtracking loops based at
v
.
The growth rate does not depend on the vertex
v
.

We approach the proof of Theorem
1.1
by constructing a graph
G
with the appropriate growth rate, as described Section
3
.

Remark
.

In general, the graph
G
and the subgroup

\Gamma=\pi_{1}G
will not be finitely generated.
Indeed, there are only countably many finite graphs, so only countably many
\alpha
could arise. Moreover,
every finitely generated subgroup
\Gamma\leq F_{r}
is
\pi_{1}
of a finite immersed graph
G
, so its growth rate is the Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue of the non-backtracking matrix of
G
, and is thus an algebraic number.

2.2.
Linear algebra cheat sheet

A major part of our analysis of growth rates of graphs uses Perron-Frobenious Theory for non-negative matrices.
A detailed treatment of this subject can be found in many texts, e.g.
[
10
]
.
We mention the relevant facts for our purposes.

Let
T
be a finite matrix with non-negative entries.

Definition 2.5
.

T
is
irreducible
if for all
i,j
, there exists
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
(T^{k})_{i,j}>0
.

By the Perron-Frobenious Theorem, the leading eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(T)
of an irreducible non-negative matrix
T
is a positive number, with an eigenvector with positive entries.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
T
be an irreducible non-negative matrix.
Then,

(1)

\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}\big((T^{k})_{i,j}\big)^{1/k}=L
for all
i,j
.
(So the limit is independent of
i,j
).

(2)

L=\lambda_{1}(T)
, the Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue of
T
.

Remark
.

If
T
is aperiodic, we can replace the
\limsup
with
\lim
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph.
Denote by
\overrightarrow{E}=\{(u,v):\{u,v\}\in E\}
the set of oriented edges of
G
.

Definition 2.7
.

The
non-backtracking matrix
of
G
is the
|\overrightarrow{E}|\times|\overrightarrow{E}|
matrix
B_{G}
with entries:

\displaystyle\big(B_{G}\big)_{e,f}\ =\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }e=(u,v)\text{ and }f=(z,w)\text{ where }v=z\text{ and }u\neq w\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

If
G
is a finite connected graph with minimal degree
\geq 2
, that is not a cycle, then
B_{G}
is irreducible
[
3
]
.
Thus,
\lambda_{1}(B_{G})=\limsup_{n}(B^{n}_{e,f})^{1/n}=\omega(G)

by Lemma
2.6
.

Our goal is to construct a graph with given growth rate.
As discussed above, the graph will generally be infinite so we need to extend the theory to infinite graphs and matrices.
If
G
is locally finite and
B_{G}
is irreducible, the equality
\limsup_{n}(B^{n}_{e,f})^{1/n}=\omega(G)
remains true:
\omega(G)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\text{NB}_{v}(n)^{1/n}
for
v\in V(G)
, and

(1)

\displaystyle\text{NB}_{v}(n)=\sum_{e=(v,\cdot)}\sum_{f=(\cdot,v)}B^{n}_{e,f}

so
\limsup_{n}\text{NB}_{v}(n)^{1/n}=\max_{e,f}\{\limsup_{n}(B_{e,f}^{n})^{1/n}\}
.
Since
G
is locally finite, the sum in (
1
) is finite, and since
B
is irreducible,
\limsup_{n}(B_{e,f}^{n})^{1/n}
does not depend on
e,f
, which concludes the result.

Let
T
be an infinite (countable) matrix, so that
T^{k}_{i,j}<\infty
for all
i,j,k
.
Definition
2.5
extends naturally to the infinite case, and Lemma
2.6
.(
1
) remains true
[
8
]
.
It follows that if
G
is an infinite, locally finite, connected graph with minimal degree
\geq 2
, and contains a cycle, then
B_{G}
satisfies the condition of Lemma
2.6
and
\omega(G)=\limsup_{k}\{(B^{k}_{e,e^{\prime}})^{1/k}\}
.

The following result will enable us to compute
\omega(G)
using finite approximations.
For an infinite matrix
T
and an integer
n
, denote by
T_{n}
the
\{1,\ldots,n\}\times\{1,\ldots,n\}
submatrix of
T
, and by
\omega(T)=\sup_{k}\{(T^{k}_{i,j})^{1/k}\}
.

Lemma 2.8

(
[
9
, thm 3]
)
.

Let
T
be an infinite, irreducible non-negative matrix, with
T^{k}_{i,j}<\infty
for all
i,j,k
.
Let
\{R_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a subsequence of
{\mathbb{N}}
with
T_{R_{n}}
irreducible for all
R_{n}
.
Then
\omega(T)=\sup_{n}\omega(T_{R_{n}})
.

Definition 2.9

(Core)
.

Let
D
be a based connected graph.
The
core
of
D
is
the smallest connected based subgraph
D^{\prime}\subset D
containing all cycles of
D
.
Note that
D^{\prime}
is either a single vertex,
or
D^{\prime}
contains a cycle and has the property that each vertex has degree
\geq 2
.
Furthermore, the inclusion
D^{\prime}\rightarrow D
induces an isomorphism
\pi_{1}D^{\prime}\rightarrow\pi_{1}D
. See
[
11
]
.

We now have all the ingredients for proving Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
J^{\prime}
be the based core of
J
.
If
J^{\prime}
is finite, the claim holds trivially, so we assume
J^{\prime}
is infinite and locally finite.
The minimal degree in
J^{\prime}
is
2
, and
J^{\prime}
contains a cycle.
The following suffices to prove
B_{J^{\prime}}
is irreducible: Any directed edges
i,j
of
J^{\prime}
, are contained in a finite subgraph
X
of
J^{\prime}
with minimal degree
2
which contains a cycle, but not a cycle itself.
Thus by
[
3
, prop 2.3]
the non-backtracking matrix of
X
is irreducible, hence
B_{X}^{k}(i,j)\neq 0
for some
k
.
This implies
B^{k}_{J^{\prime}}(i,j)\neq 0
.

Let
\{J_{i}\}
be an increasing sequence subgraphs with
J=\cup_{i}J_{i}
.
It suffices to prove the claim for their cores
\{J^{\prime}_{i}\}
as they have the same
\pi_{1}
.
For sufficiently large
i
, each
J^{\prime}_{i}
has a cycle and minimal degree
2
, so
\{B_{J^{\prime}_{i}}\}
is a sequence of irreducible truncations of
B_{J^{\prime}}
, and so we can apply Lemma
2.8
.
Observe that
\omega_{F}(\pi_{1}J^{\prime}_{i})\leq\omega_{F}(\pi_{1}J^{\prime}_{i+1})

since
J^{\prime}_{i}
is a subgraph of
J^{\prime}_{i+1}
, so we can replace
\sup
by
\lim
in Lemma
2.8
.
∎

3.
Constructing a graph with a given growth rate

The goal of this section is to prove:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\alpha\in(1,2r-1)
.
There exists a graph
G
with degrees bounded by
2r
, such that
\omega(G)=\alpha
.

The statement of Theorem
3.1
with
\alpha\in\{1,2r-1\}

is also valid as we can let
G
be an immersed circle or
2r
-regular graph.

The construction is conceptually simple: it was shown in
[
5
]
that given
\alpha
and
\epsilon>0
, there exists a finite graph
H
with
|\omega(H)-\alpha|\leq\epsilon
.

Starting with a sequence of finite graphs
\{H_{n}\}
whose growth rates converge to
\alpha
, one can “glue” them together along an infinite path, as in Figure
1
.

The first obstacle to implementing the idea is that some of the graphs we obtain by the method in
[
5
]
might have a growth rate larger than
\alpha
.
The growth rate of a graph is at least the growth rate of any of its subgraphs, so we ensure
\omega(H_{n})<\alpha
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\alpha\in(1,2r-1)
.
There is a sequence of graphs
\{H_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that:

(1)

For all
n
,
H_{n}
is connected, the degrees of
H_{n}
are in
\{2,\ldots,2r\}
, and there is at least one vertex
v_{n}\in V(H_{n})
such that
\deg(v_{n})<2r
.

(2)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\omega(H_{n})=\alpha
and ,
\omega(H_{n})<\omega(H_{n+1})<\alpha
for all
n
.

Proof.

Let
\beta\in(1,2r-1)
.
By

[
5
, thm 3.2]
, there exists a sequence of graphs
\{T_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that
|\omega(T_{k})-\beta|\leq\frac{C}{\log k}
for some constant
C
.
We construct the sequence
H_{n}
.
To get
H_{1}
, invoke the Theorem with
\beta_{1}=\alpha-\epsilon_{1}
for some
\epsilon_{1}>0
.
We get a sequence
\{T_{k}\}
so that
|\omega(T_{k})-\alpha+\epsilon_{1}|\leq\frac{C}{\log k}
, thus there is some
k
with
T_{k}
satisfying
\omega(T_{k})<\alpha
.
Set
H_{1}
to be this
T_{k}
.
Observe that
[
5
, thm 3.2]
guarantees that the degrees of
T_{k}
are in
\{2,\ldots,2r\}
and that
T_{k}
is connected.
Since
\omega(T_{k})<\alpha<2r-1
, there is a vertex with degree
<2r
.

Continue the construction similarly.
To get
H_{n}
, invoke the Theorem with
\beta_{n}=\alpha-\epsilon_{n}
, where
0<\epsilon_{n}<\alpha-\omega(H_{n-1})
and
\epsilon_{n}\rightarrow 0
.
∎

Another ingredient in the gluing-along-infinite-path construction is gluing.
But gluing where? Lemma
3.3
gives a quantitative answer.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
be two finite graphs, whose minimal degree is at least
2
and where both
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are not cycles.
Create a new graph
G_{3}
by connecting a path of length
k
to
G_{1}
from one end, and to
G_{2}
from the other end.
Then
\omega(G_{3})=\max\{\omega(G_{1}),\omega(G_{2})\}+\epsilon(k)
, where
\epsilon(k)\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
.
More precisely,
\epsilon(k)\leq C/m^{k}
, where
m=\max\{\omega(G_{1}),\omega(G_{2})\}
.

Proof.

The proof uses linear algebra.
By the discussion in
2.2
,
\omega(G)=\lambda_{1}(B_{G})
.

We use the Collatz-Wielandt formula for non-negative matrices (see e.g.
[
1
, p. 31]
):
Let
A
be an irreducible,
n\times n
real matrix such that
A_{i,j}\geq 0
, and let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a vector such that
x_{i}>0
.
Then
\lambda_{1}(A)\leq\max_{i\in[n]}\frac{(Ax)_{i}}{x_{i}}
.

Let
u\in G_{1}
and
v\in G_{2}
be the vertices in
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
which are connected to the
k
-path
u=w_{0},w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}=v
, let
B_{1},B_{2},B_{3}
be the non-backtracking matrices of
G_{1},G_{2},G_{3}
respectively, and let
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
be the Perron–Frobenius eigenvectors of
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
; i.e.
x_{1,i},x_{2,i}>0
and
B_{1}x_{1}=\lambda_{1}(B_{1})x_{1},B_{2}x_{2}=\lambda_{1}(B_{2})x_{2}
.
By
[
3
, prop 2.3]
, both
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are irreducible.

Denote the maximal eigenvalue
m=\max\{\lambda_{1}(B_{1}),\lambda_{1}(B_{2})\}
. Define the vector
\tilde{x}
, indexed by directed edges in
G_{3}
as follows
where we choose
C_{0},C_{1}
later:

\displaystyle\tilde{x}(e)=\begin{cases}x_{1}(e)&\text{ if }e\in G_{1}\\
x_{2}(e)&\text{ if }e\in G_{2}\\
m^{-t-k-1}C_{0}&\text{ if }e=(w_{t-1},w_{t})\\
m^{-t}C_{1}&\text{ if }e=(w_{t},w_{t-1})\end{cases}

Observe that this gives a non-zero value for every directed edge of
G_{3}
.
Our goal now is to bound
\max_{e\in G_{3}}f(e)
with
f(e):=\frac{B_{3}\tilde{x}(e)}{\tilde{x}(e)}
.

Let
e\in G_{1}
such that both endpoints of
e
are different from
u
.
Then by our definition
f(e)=\lambda_{1}(B_{1})
.
Similarly, if
e\in G_{2}
such that both endpoints are different from
v
, then
f(e)=\lambda_{1}(B_{2})
.

Let
e=(x,u)
be an edge entering
u
(not from the added path).
Then

\frac{B_{3}\tilde{x}(e)}{\tilde{x}(e)}\ =\ \frac{\sum_{e^{-1}\neq h=(u,t)}\tilde{x}(h)+\tilde{x}((u,w_{1}))}{\tilde{x}(e)}\ =\ \lambda_{1}(B_{1})+\frac{m^{-k}C_{0}}{\tilde{x}(e)}.

Let
e=(w_{t-1},w_{t})
be an edge on the path with
w_{t+1}\neq v
.
Then

\frac{B_{3}\tilde{x}(e)}{\tilde{x}(e)}\ =\ \frac{m^{-t-k}C_{0}}{m^{-t-k-1}C_{0}}\ =\ m.

Let
e=(w_{k-1},v)
.

(2)

\displaystyle\frac{B_{3}\tilde{x}(e)}{x(e)}\ =\ \frac{\sum_{(v,t)=h\neq e^{-1}}\tilde{x}(h)}{C_{0}m}\text{ ,}

and pick
C_{0}
so that (
2
) is smaller than
