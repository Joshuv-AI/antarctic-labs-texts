Temporary retinal inactivation reverses effects of long-term monocular deprivation in visual cortex by induction of burst mode firing in the thalamus
Authors: Madison Echavarri-Leet, Tushar Chauhan, Teresa LM Cramer, Ming-fai Fong, Mark F Bear
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYMonocular deprivation during an early postnatal critical period causes amblyopia, an impairment of vision in one eye, by altering the development of synapses in the primary visual cortex (V1). Prior studies have shown that these synaptic changes can be rapidly reversed after the critical period by temporarily inactivating the non-deprived (fellow) eye with tetrodotoxin (TTX). Here, we show in mice that the inactivation of one eye causes dorsal lateral geniculate nucleus (dLGN) neurons postsynaptic to the other eye to fire in high-frequency bursts due to recruitment of the low-threshold voltage-gated calcium channels. Elimination of bursting by genetic deletion of Cav3.1 in the dLGN prevents the therapeutic effect of retinal inactivation without affecting another prominent form of experience-dependent synaptic modification. Inactivation of the amblyopic eye alone also triggered full recovery, suggesting that dLGN bursting is both necessary and sufficient to restore synaptic strength in the visual cortex after retinal silencing.

INTRODUCTION

Amblyopia is the loss of vision in one eye caused by poor early-life binocular experience.1,2 The current treatment standard—patching or blurring vision of the non-amblyopic (fellow) eye—is effective only during a critical period of development.1,3,4 Nevertheless, amblyopic vision has been shown to improve spontaneously in adult humans after loss of the fellow eye.5 These clinical observations inspired animal experiments to test the hypothesis that recovery from the effect of long-term monocular deprivation (MD) is enabled by silencing the fellow eye. Studies in mice showed that temporary inactivation of fellow eye retinal ganglion cells via an intravitreal injection of tetrodotoxin (TTX) causes a rapid, complete, and long-lasting reversal of the effect of MD monitored using the visual evoked potential (VEP).6,7 Of particular significance, this recovery occurred in animals at ages when reverse occlusion (modeling patch therapy in humans) failed. Additional analyses in cats showed full reversal of the MD-induced shrinkage of neurons in the visual pathway and no long-term adverse effects in the visual pathway that had been temporarily inactivated.8-10 Non-invasive VEP recordings in cats and Old World monkeys subsequently confirmed that the strength of input to V1 from the amblyopic eye (AE) can be significantly improved by this treatment.11 These findings, corroborated in three evolutionarily distant species using multiple methods, suggest the possibility of developing a new treatment approach for human amblyopia based on fundamental neurobiology research.4,12

As exciting as these results are, the mechanisms by which fellow eye inactivation promotes recovery when patching does not are completely unknown. One proposal was based on findings that the threshold for synaptic strengthening is lowered after prolonged immersion in complete darkness,13,14 thereby enabling the functional recovery from amblyopia in rodents and cats after the critical period.15,16 However, the sliding modification threshold concept17-19 may not apply to the effects of monocular retinal inactivation in mice. For example, based on the anatomy of the visual pathway in mice,20 the brief elimination of activity in the ipsilateral eye is expected to have only a modest effect on overall cortical activity, yet it is sufficient to enable the full recovery of visual responsiveness to the amblyopic contralateral eye.6 Moreover, recordings from awake animals revealed, surprisingly, that retinal silencing does not substantially reduce activity relayed to the cortex via the dorsal lateral geniculate nucleus (dLGN).21 Instead, dLGN neurons postsynaptic to the inactivated retina begin to fire spontaneously in high-frequency bursts, an effect not observed after simple monocular lid closure.

Here, we report tests of the hypothesis that induction of this burst mode firing in the dLGN is essential to the mechanism that enables synaptic enhancement after fellow eye inactivation in mice. We show, first, that inactivation of the ipsilateral (fellow) eye also causes dLGN neurons postsynaptic to the other (amblyopic) eye to fire in bursts; second, that abrogation of this burst firing in the thalamus prevents the therapeutic effect of fellow-eye TTX; and third, that temporary inactivation of the AE alone is sufficient to reverse the effect of chronic MD in V1.

RESULTS

Injecting one eye with TTX drives bursting in dLGN neurons postsynaptic to the uninjected eye

A previous study in awake mice showed that dLGN neurons postsynaptic to a retina silenced by TTX show a prolonged increase in burst firing, operationally defined as two or more spikes with an interspike interval of <4 ms, preceded by a >100 ms period of quiescence.21 We were curious to know if this bursting activity was restricted to the functionally denervated dLGN neurons or if it also occurs in neurons postsynaptic to the seeing eye. To address this question, a multi-channel probe was used to record spontaneous and evoked dLGN spiking activity in head-fixed, awake mice 2 h after injecting the ipsilateral eye with TTX or saline (Figure 1A). Analyses, performed experimenter blind, were restricted to only the single units that were visually responsive to stimulation presented to the unmanipulated contralateral eye. In these neurons, during periods of visual stimulation, we observed a statistically significant decrease in interspike intervals when the ipsilateral eye was inactivated compared to controls (Figure 1B). Using the classical definition of T-type calcium channel bursts described above,21-24 we found that there was a significantly higher percentage of spikes in bursts when the ipsilateral eye was inactivated during periods of visual stimulation (Figures 1C-1E). Increased bursting was also observed in the absence of visual stimulation (unpaired t test with Welch’s correction; p = 0.0465). While many bursts were spike doublets, there was also an enhancement of bursts comprising 3 and 4 spikes (Figure 1F). Consistent with previous findings,21 increased bursting was associated with a significant increase in simultaneous firing of neuron pairs (Figures S1A and S1B). The reduction in interspike intervals was also confirmed 48 h after ipsilateral-eye TTX in mice that had been rendered amblyopic via 3-week contralateral eye deprivation, as was the increased percentage of spikes that are in bursts during periods of visual stimulation (Figure S2). Thus, ipsilateral eye inactivation produces a qualitative change in the firing properties of neurons postsynaptic to the contralateral eye in both neurotypical and amblyopic mice.

Bursting in dLGN is due to the recruitment of T-type calcium channels

Given the extensive literature demonstrating the importance of T-type calcium channels in mediating thalamic bursting,25-27 we used a genetic strategy to reduce or eliminate dLGN burst mode firing. Transgenic mice in which the gene encoding Cav3.1 (Cacna1g), the predominant isoform of the T-type calcium channel in the dLGN,28 was flanked by loxP sites received an injection of herpes simplex virus (HSV)-Cre-GFP to create a thalamic T-channel knockout (TTKO). Wild-type (WT) littermates also were injected with HSV-Cre-GFP and served as controls. One week after injecting HSV, we recorded from dLGN neurons postsynaptic to the contralateral eye 2 h after TTX was injected into the ipsilateral eye (Figure 2A). We discovered that following TTX injection, there were fewer instances of shorter interspike intervals and a decreased percentage of spikes in bursts in TTKO animals compared to WT controls (Figures 2B and 2C). As expected, there was also a decrease in correlated firing in TTKO mice (Figures S1C and S1D). Other than the absence of bursts, spiking in response to visual stimulation of the contralateral eye was unchanged in TTKO compared to WT controls (Figure 2D). In addition, there was no evidence for HSV toxicity in the infected dLGN compared to the uninfected dLGN in the opposite hemisphere (Figure 2E). VEPs measured in the binocular visual cortex (V1b) in response to phase-reversing gratings were also unaffected, as was the normal contralateral-eye bias, in the TTKO (Figure 2F).

dLGN bursting is necessary for the recovery that is enabled by fellow eye inactivation

We next tested whether increased burst mode firing is required for the therapeutic effect of fellow eye inactivation in mice rendered amblyopic by long-term MD. The experimental design was identical to our previous study6 with the exception that all mice received an HSV-Cre injection into the dLGN at postnatal day (P)40 and ocular dominance in the V1b was assayed using single-unit recordings (Figure 3A). Unit recordings enabled measurements of the relative strength of inputs from the two eyes onto binocularly responsive neurons, which was not possible using the VEP method employed previously. For each unit, the maximal evoked responses to each eye were used to calculate an ocular dominance index (ODI; the contralateral-ipsilateral [contra-ipsi] response difference divided by the sum), and the unit scores were averaged to yield an ODI score that represents the state of binocularity for that animal. Neurotypical mice have a positive ODI, reflecting the innate dominance of the contralateral eye. Depriving the contralateral eye of patterned vision during the critical period shifts the ODI to values closer to zero.

We compared three groups of mice that were all injected with HSV-Cre at P40 and monocularly deprived by contralateral eyelid suture from P26 to P47. At the time of eyelid opening, groups of WT and TTKO mice received an intravitreal injection of TTX into the fellow (ipsilateral) eye, while an additional control WT group received intravitreal saline. The assay of ocular dominance was performed 1 week later, at P54. As expected, long-term MD shifted ocular dominance in control animals, rendering the V1b equally responsive to visual stimulation of each eye. Corroborating our previous findings using VEPs,6 we found in monocularly deprived WT mice that a single injection of TTX in the fellow eye was sufficient to significantly reverse the ocular dominance shift in monocularly deprived WT mice, with ODI values favoring the contralateral eye. However, the treatment effect was lost in TTKO mice compared to the WT (Figures 3B and S3A). Consistent with these findings, analysis of the firing rate responses to contralateral (amblyopic) eye stimulation was significantly elevated after FE TTX treatment compared to FE saline, and this effect failed to occur in the TTKO (Figure S3B). Analysis of the orientation selectivity index (OSI) for neurons responsive to the contralateral eye showed no effect of genotype or FE treatment (Figure S3C).29 Thus, the data indicate that the recovery from amblyogenic rearing observed following fellow eye retinal inactivation is prevented in the absence of burst mode firing in the dLGN.

Bursting in the dLGN is not required for all forms of experience-dependent synaptic plasticity

Thus far, the data indicate that thalamic deletion of Cav3.1 abolishes bursting induced by retinal silencing, without otherwise affecting visual responses in the dLGN or V1. After finding that Cav3.1 deletion abrogates the plasticity-promoting effect of intravitreal TTX in monocularly deprived mice, we wondered if other forms of experience-dependent plasticity in V1 would also be disrupted. To address this question, we studied stimulus-selective response plasticity (SRP).30 As an initially novel visual stimulus, such as a grating of a particular orientation, becomes familiar, cortical responses to that stimulus change. Originally identified as an increase in the amplitude of the layer 4 VEP to phase reversals of a familiar stimulus,31 this effect has also been observed at the cellular level using electrophysiology and calcium imaging.32-34 The modifications are stimulus selective because a simple change, such as rotating the grating to a new orientation, reverts cortical responses to the naive state.

To assess SRP, awake, head-fixed TTKO and WT animals were exposed to the same phase-reversing stimulus orientation over 4 days, with daily VEPs recorded from layer 4 via chronically implanted electrodes. On the fifth day, the familiar stimulus was interleaved with a novel grating of a different orientation (Figure 3C). There was no difference in VEP amplitude or SRP between groups (Figure 3D). Therefore, although the plasticity necessary for the recovery from amblyogenic rearing enabled by inactivation of the fellow eye is lost in TTKO animals, this does not reflect a global impairment of cortical plasticity, as at least one other form of cortical plasticity is intact. This also suggests that more fundamental aspects of visual processing (i.e., the ability to see and distinguish between differing visual stimuli) are preserved.

Inactivation of the amblyopic eye can drive a reversal of the ocular dominance shift

Thus far, the data show that TTX injection into the fellow (ipsilateral) eye induces burst firing in dLGN neurons postsynaptic to the contralateral eye, and this bursting appears to be essential for the recovery of amblyopic eye responses in the V1b. The question naturally arises of whether burst activity in dLGN neurons is sufficient to drive recovery. We reasoned that if it were, inactivation of the amblyopic (contralateral) eye alone would also lead to recovery since it similarly causes burst firing in the dLGN.21 To examine this possibility, we again compared 3 groups of mice. One group was normally raised, without MD or treatment, until P54, when ocular dominance in the V1b was assayed by single-unit recordings. The second group underwent contralateral eye MD from P26 to P47, followed by a binocular visual experience, until recordings were made from the V1b at P54. The third group also underwent MD from P26 to P47 but received a single intravitreal injection of TTX into the contralateral, amblyopic eye at the time of eyelid reopening. After the TTX effect subsided, these mice experienced binocular vision until P54, when ocular dominance was assessed (Figure 4A). Note that in all groups, visual experience through the ipsilateral eye remained unaltered.

As expected, normally reared animals showed a bias toward the contralateral eye, while those subjected to 3 weeks of MD without treatment exhibited a significant ocular dominance shift 1 week after eye reopening. Remarkably, inactivation of the amblyopic eye restored ODI values to levels observed in normally reared animals (Figures 4B and 4C). Consistent with these findings, analysis of the firing rate responses to contralateral eye stimulation was significantly reduced by MD and rescued by amblyopic eye TTX (Figure S4). These findings support the hypothesis that induction of burst activity in dLGN neurons postsynaptic to the amblyopic eye may supplement—or substitute for—the visually-driven activity required to strengthen the weak connections serving this eye in the V1b.

DISCUSSION

Although the most common causes of human amblyopia are strabismus and anisometropia, the most severe and therapeutically recalcitrant form of the disorder is caused by MD.4 Indeed, individuals with deprivation amblyopia are typically excluded from clinical trials of emerging therapies due to the condition’s pronounced resistance to treatment.4 Because our animal model most closely resembles this type of amblyopia, we are effectively setting the bar high, allowing us to investigate unconventional treatments with the hope that they will have clinical translations across all types of amblyopia. Previous studies have indicated that intravitreal inactivation of the fellow eye may be a compelling intervention, as it reverses the effects of amblyogenic rearing in evolutionarily distant animal models (mice and cats).6 Recent work in rhesus monkeys has also shown promise, with a low dose of fellow-eye TTX promoting recovery in the amblyopic eye without compromising the function or structure of the injected, fellow eye.11,35 The objective of the current study in mice was to advance understanding of the mechanism.

To briefly summarize the major findings, we demonstrated that inactivation of one retina causes dLGN neurons postsynaptic to the unmanipulated (seeing) eye to transition into burst mode firing. Considering the previous observation that burst mode firing is also induced in neurons postsynaptic to the silenced eye,21 the data indicate that a widespread change in the qualities of dLGN activity patterns is induced by treatment of either eye. Although we did not follow the detailed time course of this change in firing, our previous study suggests that it lasts for the duration of retinal silencing (~48 h after a single injection).21 The activity resembles that observed during slow-wave sleep when T-type calcium channels, de-inactivated by a period of quiescence, initiate brief, spontaneous, high-frequency bursts of action potentials.22,23 Indeed, we found that genetic deletion of Cav3.1, the major thalamic T-channel isoform in the dLGN,28 abolished burst mode firing in the dLGN caused by retinal silencing. Eliminating Cav3.1 also prevented the therapeutic effect of fellow-eye TTX in amblyopic mice, suggesting that bursts are essential to the mechanism for recovery of synaptic strength in V1. Given that TTX injection into either eye triggers burst activity throughout the dLGN, we hypothesized that inactivation of the amblyopic eye could be sufficient to initiate recovery of its input to the V1b. We were astonished to find that a brief silencing of the amblyopic eye alone triggered a complete reversal of the ocular dominance shift caused by long-term MD. This effect was comparable in magnitude to that observed in previous studies using fellow-eye6 and binocular36 TTX treatments. Taken together, the data suggest that retinal inactivation in adulthood enables the visual system to “reboot” by temporarily recreating spontaneous thalamic activity patterns that resemble those that are responsible for the initial establishment of functional connectivity during early development.37-40

These findings raise several interesting questions that will require further study. One is why neurons throughout the dLGN transition to burst mode firing when only one eye is inactivated. Ganglion cells of the ipsilateral retina provide only a small fraction of the retinal inputs to the dLGN,20 and yet the neurons that are visually responsive to the contralateral eye show an increase in bursting when the ipsilateral eye is inactivated. Evidence has emerged recently that, unlike cats and monkeys with cytoarchitecturally laminated lateral geniculate nuclei, a substantial number of dLGN neurons in mice have binocular connectivity.41 Although the majority of relay neurons respond to stimulation of one eye only,42 most inhibitory interneurons receive input from both eyes43 and can exhibit burst firing when released from hyperpolarization.44,45 Loss of tonic excitation from one retina may therefore be sufficient to promote low threshold spiking in these cells, which, in turn, trigger bursts in relay neurons throughout the nucleus. Another possibility is that increased burst mode firing occurs via feedback from the cortex, potentially through the thalamic reticular nucleus (TRN). There is evidence that decreasing V1 activity leads to increased TRN activity,46 and it is well documented that the TRN drives bursting in the dLGN.47,48

Another key question is how burst firing of the dLGN enables reversal of the ocular dominance shift after long-term MD. A simple hypothesis is that bursts induce long-term synaptic potentiation (LTP) of thalamocortical input to the V1b, a phenomenon previously shown to be triggered by high-frequency electrical stimulation of the dLGN in rodents.30,49 According to this line of reasoning, well-correlated burst mode firing in dLGN neurons postsynaptic to the amblyopic eye may temporarily substitute for visual experience, thereby driving the recovery of synaptic strength in the V1b. In normally reared animals, burst mode firing has been shown to be more effective at driving postsynaptic activity than tonic spikes and to enhance the detectability of visual stimuli,50-52 conditions that are expected to facilitate LTP. Alternatively, or in addition, burst firing may alter the configuration of inhibitory networks in the V1b that constrain Hebbian modifications.53 Indeed, diverse global manipulations that alter V1 inhibition mediated by parvalbumin-expressing (PV+) GABAergic neurons have been shown to allow re-expression of plasticity in adult rodents.3,54-57 Reduced PV+ cell activity could result simply from the elimination of stimulus-driven activation of V1 through the inactivated eye; however, an interesting alternative explanation is that the shift in LGN firing mode might preferentially recruit somatostatin-positive (SOM+) interneurons. In L4 of the sensory cortex, PV+ inhibitory neurons are known to be strongly inhibited by SOM+ neurons,58,59 and it has been observed that thalamic bursting increases the probability of firing in principal cells and SOM+ cells but not PV+ interneurons.60 It is noteworthy in this context that other manipulations that enhance the activity of SOM+ cells can restore a juvenile response to MD after the critical period and promote recovery from long-term MD.57

A distinct mechanism by which Cav3.1 deletion might impair recovery is through disruption of “offline” synaptic consolidation during slow-wave sleep, which typically involves thalamic burst mode firing.24 Although sleep itself would not be expected to be altered by a unilateral and highly localized excision of Cav3.1, it is still plausible that bursting in this geniculo-cortical circuit during sleep is key to the mechanism of recovery. We cannot rule this possibility out, but note that SRP, which has been shown to be disrupted by sleep deprivation,61 was unaffected by loss of Cav3.1 in the dLGN.

Taken together, the data support the conclusion that induction of dLGN burst mode firing by monocular retinal inactivation sets in motion changes that enable rapid and complete reversal of the ocular dominance shift caused in the V1b by long-term MD. The recovery of responses driven by inactivation of the amblyopic eye occurs at ages when simply patching the fellow eye has no durable efficacy.6 Although these electrophysiological data are a good predictor of improved vision through the amblyopic eye, they still need to be confirmed with careful behavioral testing. To assess the functional—and clinical—significance of these findings, we believe it is appropriate to employ other species with frontally placed eyes, well-differentiated visual systems, good spatial acuity, and stereoscopic depth perception, such as cats and non-human primates.62,63 So far, the effects of fellow-eye TTX on amblyopic eye evoked responses generalize to both cats and monkeys, and time will tell if amblyopic eye inactivation is equally effective and improves behavioral measures of vision.

We are cautiously optimistic that these findings may lead to a new treatment approach for human amblyopia, particularly given the discovery that silencing the amblyopic eye is effective. In this context, it is noteworthy that some recent studies in humans suggest that “inverse occlusion” (patching only the amblyopic eye) can mildly improve acuity in some adult patients.64,65 Our findings indicate that this mild effect could be substantially improved by temporarily inactivating the amblyopic eye retina. We presume that such treatments would be met with more enthusiasm by patients and caregivers than interventions targeting the normal, fellow eye. Regardless, the current study identifies bursting in dLGN as a key feature that distinguishes simple patch therapy from retinal inactivation. As we learn more about how bursting opens a plasticity gate in the cortex, we can anticipate that other therapeutically relevant insights will emerge.

Limitations of the study

The finding that dLGN neurons postsynaptic to the contralateral eye transition to burst firing mode when the ipsilateral eye alone is inactivated led to the hypothesis that dLGN bursting is needed for the therapeutic effect of fellow-eye TTX treatment in amblyopic mice. This hypothesis is supported in this study by the finding that genetic deletion of Cav3.1, which suppresses burst firing in the dLGN, prevents the effect of treatment. Because it had been shown that burst mode firing also occurs in dLGN neurons postsynaptic to the same eye that is inactivated,21 we were motivated to ask if silencing the amblyopic eye alone could trigger recovery of synaptic strength in V1. The finding that temporary inactivation of the amblyopic eye is indeed sufficient to restore responses in V1 is consistent with the thalamic burst hypothesis, but it must be noted that we have not shown that it is the bursting of neurons postsynaptic to the amblyopic eye, per se, that drives the recovery. This mechanism for recovery after amblyopic eye inactivation would be more firmly established by examining whether it also depends on Cav3.1 in the thalamus or, more definitively, by using optogenetic stimulation to substitute for TTX treatment and directly impose burst firing on dLGN neurons. These are worthwhile experiments in pursuit of further understanding how amblyopic eye TTX enables recovery of responsiveness to this eye. When considered together with the finding that dLGN bursting is necessary for recovery following fellow-eye TTX, it would be surprising if amblyopic eye TTX did not use the same mechanism.

To test the thalamic burst hypothesis, we chose to target Cacna1g. The rationale for deleting this specific gene was that it encodes the most highly expressed T-type calcium channel in the sensory relay neurons of the thalamus, Cav3.1.28 We showed that disrupting expression of this gene alone is sufficient to virtually eliminate burst mode firing in the dLGN after retinal silencing with TTX. However, we make no claim here that Cav3.1 is uniquely involved in thalamic burst mode firing. It is possible that manipulations of Cav3.2 and Cav3.3, channels highly expressed in the TRN, would also interfere with the transition to burst mode firing in the dLGN. Additional experiments are required to parse the requirement for different T-type channels in the therapeutic effect of retinal silencing.

The present study relied on the use of Kilosort 2.5 to sort single units. It is well known that the shape and amplitude of waveforms change within a burst, which adds an additional challenge for spike sorting.66 While all proposed single units were manually curated with Phy, with special care to identify misclassified bursts, it is likely that there were some split errors specific to bursting units. However, these types of errors would have happened across groups and are unlikely to have affected one treatment group differently from the rest. Thus, it is possible we are underestimating the degree of burst mode firing, and if anything, there is a greater difference between groups than what we are reporting in the present work.

STAR★METHODS

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY DETAILS

All procedures adhered to the guidelines of the National Institutes of Health and were approved by the Committee on Animal care at MIT. For experiments involving wildtype mice, male and female C57BL/6J mice were ordered from Jackson Laboratory for experimentation or as breeders for a colony maintained at MIT. Colony offspring were housed in groups of two to five same-sex littermates after weaning at P21-23 and maintained on a 12-h light- 12-h dark cycle. All recordings were conducted during the light cycle. Food and water were available ad libitum. For experiments involving genetic knockout of the T-type calcium channel Cav3.1 isoform in dLGN neurons, we used mice expressing floxed Cacna1g alleles and their wildtype littermates as controls. Our breeding scheme for the fCav line (graciously provided by the Tonegawa lab, MIT69) involved housing male and female heterozygous fl/+ mice together to generate a mix of fl/fl and +/+ offspring for experiments. Heterozygous fl/+ mice were used either as cage mates to avoid single housing of experimental animals or as breeders, depending on experimental needs.

METHOD DETAILS

Experimental design

For experiments involving MD, the procedure was initiated at postnatal day 26 (P26) and continued for three weeks until P47. P26 was selected based on prior evidence indicating that this is the age when V1 is most sensitive to MD,70 the contribution of LGN plasticity is minimized,71 and to allow direct comparison with our previous study.6 Intravitreal injection of TTX/saline occurred at the same time as eye opening, at P47. Animals receiving HSV-Cre into the dLGN were injected at P40 to allow a week of recovery before intravitreal injection occurred. Any recordings done in dLGN were completed at P47, two hours after intravitreal injection, with habituation to head fixation taking place at P45 and P46, and headplate implant occurring between P42-44. Any recordings done in V1b were completed at P54, one week after eye opening and treatment (if any) to assess the degree of ocular dominance shift. Habituation to head fixation was at P52 and P53 and headplate implants were done on P50-P51. Target sample sizes (number of biological replicates) were estimated based on pilot data and previous experiments. In order to be included in the final dataset, mice needed to meet all of the following a priori determined inclusion criteria: mice maintained good health status throughout experiment, eyelid stayed fully closed during MD (if any), pupil dilation was confirmed after TTX (if injected), confirmation of HSV-Cre injection and/or electrode placement (histologically in the case of dLGN and observation of an ipsilateral eye VEP in the case of V1b), cornea, retina, and all parts of the eyeball were healthy and intact at the completion of experiment, and in the case of electrophysiological recordings, both eyes remained open and free of eye discharge for the duration of the recording session. Decisions to exclude animals were made blinded to animal treatment condition and genotype.

Eyelid suture

At P26, animals were anesthetized via inhalation of isoflurane (1-3% in oxygen), and body temperature was maintained on a heated surface at 37°C. Both eyes were moistened with sterile saline. After checking the ocular health of both eyes, the eye being sutured had fur on upper and lower eyelid trimmed, with special care taken to preserve the whiskers. Then, the eyeball surface was rinsed with sterile saline. A thin layer of ophthalmic ointment containing bacitracin, neomycin, and polymyxin was placed on the eye. The eyelid was closed using a single mattress stitch of polypropylene suture (Ethicon, 7-0 Prolene, 8648G), with care taken not to touch the corneal surface, and a square knot was tied on the exterior of the lower lid. Ointment containing lidocaine, bacitracin, and polymyxin was applied to the lower eyelid around the knot. Nails on the fore and hind paw on the same side of body as the eyelid suture were trimmed. To remove sutures (3 weeks later, at P47), mice were anesthetized and maintained as described above. The suture was cut and removed, and the eyelids were gently separated. The corneal surface was rinsed with sterile saline and examined for signs of damage. The same ophthalmic ointment was applied. At this time, the non-deprived eyeball was also observed to assess ocular health. Animals recovered from MD and eye-opening procedures in their home cages.

Intravitreal injections

Mice were anesthetized using isoflurane, maintained at 37°C, and had eyes moistened as described for eyelid sutures. Ocular health of both eyes was assessed. For the injected eye, an ~500 nm incision was made at the temporal corner of the eyelid, and a sterile silk suture thread (Ethicon, 7-0 silk, 7733G) was pulled through the exposed conjunctiva. The suture was pulled toward the nasal aspect to secure the globe and expose the temporal aspect of the eyeball. A 30-gauge needle was used to penetrate the sclera and globe. The eye was rinsed with sterile saline just prior to inserting a glass micropipette into the vitreous chamber. A nanoliter injector was used to deliver 1 μL of either TTX (1 mM in citrate buffer; Abcam ab120055) or sterile saline. The micropipette was removed 1 min after injection. The eye was rinsed with sterile saline and the spot of penetration was coated with ophthalmic ointment containing bacitracin, neomycin, and polymyxin. Animals recovered from intravitreal injection in their home cages. Other surgical procedures were never scheduled within 48 h following intravitreal injection as it has been documented that burst mode firing in the dLGN is reduced under anesthesia.21

HSV-Cre viral injection

At P40, both Cav3.1+/+ and Cav3.1 fL/fL mice were anesthetized (1–3% isoflurane) and maintained at 37°C as previously described. Pre-operative meloxicam (5 mg/kg s.c.) was administered. The fur on the top of the head was shaved and the mice were subsequently placed in stereotaxic ear bars. The exposed scalp was cleaned using a povidone-iodine solution (10% w/v) and ethanol (70% v/v). An incision was made along the midline and connective tissue over lambda, bregma, and the dLGN of interest was cleared. The head was leveled, and a burr hole was drilled over dLGN (For distance lambda to bregma = Xmm; ML: 0.54*X, AP: 0.53*X). A glass capillary tube (model 3-000-203-G, Drummond Scientific Company) was backfilled with mineral oil before being loaded into a Nanoject III injector (model 3-000-207, Drummond) affixed to the stereotaxic arm. The capillary was front loaded with HSV-Cre (Neurotools, RN5, titer: 1 × 109 infection units/mL) and gradually lowered ~2.85mm into the brain and permitted to equilibrate for 5 min. A total of six injection pulses of 13.8nL were performed at depths of 2.8, 2.7, and 2.6mm with 20 s between pulses and 2 min between depths. The pipette was retracted to 2.55mm for 5 min after the final injection and then slowly raised out of the brain. The scalp was then sutured closed with sterile vicryl suture (Ethicon, 6–0, J562G) and tissue adhesive (3M, Vetbond) except in the case of VEP experiments (described below). Animals were removed from anesthesia and transferred to a heated recovery chamber and slow-releasing Buprenorphine (1mg/kg s.c.) was administered. Health was carefully monitored by experimenters and veterinary staff, and supplemental fluids or heat was delivered if needed.

V1b VEP implant surgery

For the SRP experiment, animals received an implant in V1b during the same surgery as HSV-Cre injection into the dLGN. In these animals, the scalp was resected and the connective tissue on the skull was removed. A steel post was affixed to the skull anterior to bregma. A craniotomy was made over the right prefrontal cortex for implanting a silver wire reference electrode on the cortical surface. Craniotomies were made in each hemisphere over binocular V1 (3.1mm lateral of Lambda), and a tungsten microelectrode (300–500 kΩ; FHC 30070) was lowered to L4 (450 μm from the cortical surface). The steel posts and male gold pins coupled to the skull were secured to the skull using a cyanoacrylate glue (Loctite 454). The skull was then covered with dental acrylic (C&B Metabond Quick Adhesive Cement System).

Headplate surgery

Mice were anesthetized (1–3% isoflurane) and maintained at 37°C as previously described. Pre-operative meloxicam (5 mg/kg s.c.) was administered. The fur on the top of the head was shaved and the mice were subsequently placed in stereotaxic ear bars. The exposed scalp was cleaned using a povidone-iodine solution (10% w/v) and ethanol (70% v/v). The scalp was resected and connective tissue on the skull was removed. The head was leveled using bregma and lambda as landmarks. A shallow reference point was drilled into the skull in the location of the future craniotomy (LGN: For distance lambda to bregma = Xmm; ML: 0.54*X, AP: 0.53*X; V1b: 3.1mm lateral of lambda). Then, a stainless-steel headplate was secured to the skull using cyanoacrylate (Loctite 454). A craniotomy was made over the right prefrontal cortex and a silver wire reference electrode coupled to a male gold pin was secured with cyanoacrylate glue on the cortical surface. The skull around the outside of the headplate well was then covered in dental acrylic (C&B Metabond Quick Adhesive Cement System). The well created by the headplate was filled with Kwik-Sil (World Precision Instruments). Animals were removed from anesthesia and transferred to a heated recovery chamber and slow-releasing Buprenorphine (1 mg/kg s.c.) was administered. Health was carefully monitored by experimenters and veterinary staff, and lemental fluids or heat was delivered if needed.

Single unit electrophysiological recordings

Mice were habituated to head fixation and an opaque eye occluder while viewing gray screen in the two days preceding the recording session. On the morning of the acute recording, mice were anesthetized (1–3% isoflurane) and maintained at 37°C. Ocular health was assessed. The Kwik-Sil plug was removed, the skull was thinned, and a small burr hole was created over the previously created reference point. A drop of aCSF was placed over the cranial opening and Kwik-Sil was re-applied to the headplate well. Mice were allowed to recover for >1 h. At the time of recording, mice were head fixed and the Kiwk-Sil plug was removed and replaced with warm aCSF. Apart from the amblyopic eye TTX experiment, all recordings were performed with a 32-channel probe from NeuroNexus (dLGN: A1x32-Poly3-5mm-25s-177-CM32; V1: A1x32-Poly2-5mm-50s-177-CM32) which was slowly lowered either into the dLGN (~2.6–2.9mm perpendicular to the cortical surface) or V1b (~0.8–1.0mm at a 30° angle to the cortical surface). Recordings were conducted in awake, head-fixed animals with full-field visual stimuli presented on an LCD monitor in the binocular visual field at a viewing distance of 20cm. Placement was confirmed by showing either binocular (LGN) or ipsilateral eye (V1b) simulation and observing visually evoked channel rastergrams and/or VEPs. Continuous LFP and spike data were collected, amplified, digitized, and filtered using the Recorder-64 system (Plexon). Recordings were sampled at 25kHz with a 500 Hz high and low pass filter. Electrophysiological recordings in the amblyopic eye TTX experiment were collected using a 64-channel laminar probe (Cambridge NeuroTech) and recordings were amplified, digitized, and filtered using the Intan Technologies RHD Recording System. These recordings were sampled at 25kHz and used a 0.1 Hz high pass and 7.5 kHz low pass filter. This change in protocol was necessitated by a sudden stimulus computer malfunction that required use of the lab’s other unit recording setup.

Visual stimuli for the dLGN recordings consisted of a full field flash of white and black for 0.5 s each with a presentation of gray screen in between each for an equal length of time. There were 75 presentations of white/black presented per block. Each eye was presented with 3 blocks (for a total of 225 exposures of both white and black) with 30 s of gray screen between blocks. V1b recording stimuli consisted of drifting gratings at 100% contrast, 0.2cpd, presented for 1 s each with 1–1.5 s of gray screen between each presentation. There was a >30 s period of gray screen viewing between testing of the two eyes. A total of 8 stimulus orientations were presented (0, 45, 90, 135, 180, 225, 270, 315) and each eye saw each angle 30 times in a random presentation order.

VEP electrophysiological recordings

Recordings were conducted in awake, head-fixed animals with full-field visual stimuli presented on an LCD monitor in the binocular visual field at a viewing distance of 20cm. On the days prior to the initial recording, animals were habituated to head restraint, gray screen viewing, and an opaque occluder that restricted vision to one eye or the other. Other than the visual stimulus, the experimental room was kept dark and free of distractions. For the SRP experiment, LFP data were recorded from layer 4 of binocular V1 during visual stimulation using phase-reversing sine-wave gratings (0.05 cycles per degree, 100% contrast, 2 phase reversals per second). All recordings began with 30 s of dark screen, followed by 30 s of a uniform gray stimulus. For each of the first four days, the mice were shown gratings oriented at 30°. The gratings were presented for 5 blocks of 100 s each (200 phase reversals), separated by a 30 s gray stimulus. The total recording time was 11 min 50 s. On the ‘test’ day, the mice were presented 5 blocks of the familiar 30-degree grating randomly interleaved with 5 blocks of a novel grating (120°). The test recording lasted 22 min and 40 s. Finally, the mice were presented with phase-reversing gratings in each eye to derive a contra/ipsi ratio. To minimize the influence of SRP on this measurement, stimuli with a hitherto unseen spatial frequency (0.2 cycles per degree) and orientation (one of 165, 75 or 105°) were used. The stimuli were programmed using psychopy, and the LFP was recorded using the Plexon Omniplex amplifier at a sampling rate of 1000Hz. The wide-band signal was low-pass filtered at 200Hz, and powerline noise was removed using a bandstop Butterworth filter centered at 60Hz.

Electrophysiological data analysis

Single units: Spike sorting of single units was completed using Kilosort 2.5 and manual correction with Phy. For all dLGN analyses, only units that were found to be responsive to white and/or black full field stimulation presented to the contralateral eye were included in analysis. Visual responsivity in this case was defined as having a maximal average firing rate within the 150ms of stimulus onset that was 1.25-fold the maximal average firing rate in the 150ms of gray screen presentation preceding stimulus onset. Bursting was defined as 2 or more spikes with an interspike interval <4ms with a preceding period of ≥100ms quiescence.21-24 In V1b, units were classified as visually responsive if at least one eye evoked a response at one or more orientations wherein the maximal average firing rate within 150ms of stimulus onset was at least 2-fold the maximal average firing rate in the 150ms of gray screen presentation preceding stimulus onset. Visual responsivity thresholds were empirically derived and differed in dLGN and V1 because the baseline firing rates differed in these two areas as did the magnitude of the visual responses. The ocular dominance index (ODI) was calculated using each eye’s ratio of: (maximal firing rate post stimulus/maximal firing rate pre stimulus) at their preferred orientation using the formula: (Contra−Ipsi)∕(Contra+Ipsi). Similarly, the orientation selectivity index (OSI) was calculated as the difference in response to preferred and non-preferred orientations divided by the sum.

VEPs: The VEP was defined as the phase reversal triggered LFP, averaged across all phase reversals within an individual animal, time point, and viewing condition. VEP magnitude was defined as the peak-to-peak amplitude of each biphasic VEP waveform and analyzed using custom Python functions. The LFPs were epoched at phase-reversals, and each epoch was normalized by subtracting the average voltage recorded in the 5 ms preceding the phase-reversal. The VEP was defined as the average of these epoched waveforms, and its magnitude was defined as the peak-to-peak voltage of the first negative-to-positive cycle in the transient response.

Postmortem analysis

Mice were deeply anesthetized using isoflurane and rapidly decapitated. Both eyeballs were removed for immediate ocular examination and dissection in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The corneas were examined under a microscope for signs of damage, and parts of the eye were dissected for assessment of any abnormalities, particularly the lens or retina. Meanwhile, the brains were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature. Tissue remained in the fixation solution for 24hr and thereafter stored in PBS. Coronal slices of dLGN were made at 50μm using a vibrating microtome. Slices were rinsed with water and phosphate buffer, and then mounted on charged glass slides (Fisher Scientific, 12-550-15). The mounted slices dried at room temperature and ambient humidity for 24hr and then coverslipped with 1.5 glass (VWR, 48393-251). All surgeries involving targeting of the dLGN with a glass pipette (in the case of HSV-Cre) or silicone electrode (electrophysiological recording) included coating these instruments with CM-DiI (Invitrogen) for histological confirmation with DAPI (Vectashield Vibrance Mounting Medium with DAPI) used as a counterstain. TTKO animals used for the SRP experiment instead had a fluorescent Nissl stain (Invitrogen, NeuroTrace 435/455) with mounting medium (Vectashield Vibrance) to enable cell counting. Slides were coverslipped with 1.5 glass (VWR, 48393-251). Slices were imaged on a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus IX81) using 405nm and 559nm excitation. Images were compared to a mouse brain atlas to confirm proper targeting to dLGN. For cell count analysis, z stack images (six optical sections, 1μm step size) were obtained. Images were taken using a 20× objective with an NA of 0.75. All imaging parameters were kept constant across conditions. To minimize variability, the dLGN of both injected and uninjected hemispheres was imaged from three sections per mouse, with data collected from a total 5 mice. Cell density values were averaged across these sections. Cell counting in the dLGN was performed using a custom Python script integrated with the ImageJ image-processing framework. This script, available as a plugin, is openly accessible on GitHub (https://github.com/dcolam/Cluster-Analysis-Plugin). Representative example images were processed using ImageJ.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism (GraphPad). Normality testing was performed using the Shapiro-Wilk, and the outcome was used to determine whether to proceed with parametric or nonparametric tests. In the case of parametric tests, an F-test or Brown-Forsythe test was computed to determine whether to correct for unequal variances. All analyses that included comparing a single value for each animal across treatment groups ended up requiring a parametric test, and therefore a t test (with Welch Correction for unequal variance where applicable) or a One-Way ANOVA with Tukey’s correction for multiple comparisons. Analyses of distributions (i.e., ISI frequencies) were analyzed with a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. A two-way ANOVA with Sidak’s multiple comparisons test was used to compare burst frequency across treatment groups at different numbers of spikes within a burst. For SRP experiments, two-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test was used to compare VEP magnitude across days of familiar stimulus presentation and a two-way ANOVA with Sidak correction was used to compare familiar and novel responses on test day.

To determine if correlated firing existed between pairs of neurons, the Bernoulli correlation coefficient ρ(δ)=p12(δ)−p1p2p1(1−p1)p2(1−p2) between the neurons’ spike trains was calculated.21,72 P1 or P2 is the firing probability per bin of neuron 1 or neuron 2 and P12(δ) is the probability of joint firing at time lag δ. Positive (or negative) departures of ρ from zero indicate synchronous (or anti-synchronous firing). To check the statistical significance of manipulation induced correlation changes, ρ value across each lag was calculated and summed over lags from – 10ms to 10ms for each pair of neurons. These values were then compared using unpaired t-tests.
