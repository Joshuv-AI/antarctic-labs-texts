Optimizing ergonomics during laparoscopic cholecystectomy in situs inversus abdominis with levocardia: a technical note
Authors: Vikram Saini, Parshant Kumar, Saikumar Kumbar, Sourabh Meena, Tulsi Ratilal Patodia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLaparoscopic cholecystectomy in patients with situs inversus abdominis with levocardia is technically challenging because of mirror-image anatomy and unfamiliar ergonomics. We describe technical modifications for safe laparoscopic cholecystectomy in a 38-year-old woman with symptomatic cholelithiasis. Preoperative imaging confirmed left-sided positioning of the liver and gallbladder with a normally positioned heart. A modified four-port configuration with mirrored subcostal ports was used, with the surgeon positioned on the patient’s right side. Hartmann’s pouch was retracted with the dominant right hand, while Calot’s triangle was dissected through the epigastric port with the left hand. After adhesiolysis, an anomalous cystic artery located to the right of the cystic duct was identified. The critical view of safety was achieved before division of the cystic structures. The procedure was completed without complications, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 1. Individualized port placement and surgeon positioning may facilitate safe surgery in patients with situs anomalies.Keywords: Laparoscopic cholecystectomy, Cholelithiasis, Situs inversus, Ergonomics, Anatomy

INTRODUCTION

Situs inversus is a rare congenital condition characterized by abnormal positioning of thoracic or abdominal viscera [1]. Situs inversus is classified into situs inversus totalis, in which both thoracic and abdominal organs are arranged as a complete mirror image of normal anatomy and which is usually associated with dextrocardia, and situs inversus partialis, in which only selected thoracic or abdominal organs are transposed [1,2]. Situs inversus with levocardia is considerably rarer than situs inversus totalis and represents an incomplete form of visceral transposition in which the abdominal viscera are reversed while the heart remains in its normal left-sided position [2]. This technical note describes operative modifications, surgeon positioning, port placement, and ergonomic strategies that facilitated a safe laparoscopic cholecystectomy in a patient with situs inversus abdominis with levocardia.

TECHNIQUE

A 38-year-old woman presented with symptomatic cholelithiasis and was diagnosed preoperatively with situs inversus abdominis with levocardia on ultrasonography and contrast-enhanced computed tomography (Fig. 1). Cardiopulmonary evaluation excluded associated congenital cardiac abnormalities, and an elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy was planned.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the abdomen showing situs inversus. (A, B) Coronal sections demonstrating the gallbladder (white arrows) on the left side of the abdomen and the stomach on the right side. The heart is located on the left side in the mediastinum. (C) Axial section showing the gallbladder (white arrows) on the left side of the abdomen and the stomach on the right side.

The patient was taken to laparoscopic surgery under general anesthesia. A modified four-port configuration was adopted to accommodate the mirror-image abdominal anatomy (Fig. 2A). The surgeon stood on the patient’s right side to improve ergonomic access. A 10-mm umbilical camera port was inserted using the Veress needle. A 10-mm epigastric working port was placed. Diagnostic laparoscopy confirmed left-sided positioning of the liver and gallbladder and demonstrated omental adhesions over the gallbladder (Fig. 2B, C). Two 5-mm left subcostal ports were placed in the midclavicular and anterior axillary lines. The patient was positioned in the reverse Trendelenburg position with the left side tilted up. The assistant used a lateral subcostal port to provide traction on the gallbladder. Hartmann’s pouch was retracted in a lateral, cranial, and inferior direction through the left subcostal midclavicular port using the surgeon’s dominant right hand, while the dissection of Calot’s triangle was performed through the epigastric port with the left hand. During dissection, the cystic artery was identified to the right of the cystic duct (Fig. 2D). The critical view of safety was achieved, and the cystic structures were clipped and divided. The specimen was delivered, and the laparoscopic cholecystectomy was completed successfully without complications.

(A) Operating room setup and port placement. (B) Laparoscopic view showing the stomach (yellow arrow) on the right side. (C) Laparoscopic view showing the gallbladder (yellow arrow) below the falciform ligament, with the fundus directed toward the left side of the abdomen. (D) Laparoscopic view showing the cystic artery (yellow arrow) located to the right of the small cystic duct.

The patient’s postoperative recovery was uneventful. Oral intake resumed 6 hours after surgery, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 1. Histopathology showed chronic calculous cholecystitis without dysplasia or malignancy. She remained asymptomatic at the 3-month follow-up.

DISCUSSION

Situs inversus with levocardia is a rare subtype in which the abdominal organs are reversed while the heart remains left-sided. This anomaly is important to recognize because altered visceral orientation can delay diagnosis, cause atypical symptom localization, and complicate surgery due to unfamiliar anatomy [2]. Preoperative cardiopulmonary evaluation is recommended to exclude associated congenital anomalies and aid perioperative planning.

The position of the gallbladder may vary in patients with situs anomalies. Preoperative cross-sectional imaging is therefore essential for defining organ position and facilitating operative planning. Computed tomography and magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography can help delineate the gallbladder, biliary anatomy, and associated anatomical anomalies [2–4]. In our case, the gallbladder was located in the left epigastric region, while the cystic artery was located to the right and anterior to the cystic duct.

Several operative techniques have been described for laparoscopic cholecystectomy in situs inversus, with no universally accepted ergonomic configuration. The reported variables include surgeon position, number and location of ports, port size, use of an additional assistant port for traction, and allocation of the dominant and nondominant hands for traction and dissection. The surgeon has been described as standing on the patient’s right side and between the patient’s legs in the French position [5,6]. Similarly, mirror-image port (left subcostal) placement has been performed using three ports, with some authors adding a fourth port to allow an assistant to retract the gallbladder [5,6]. The principal working port also varies across reports, with either the left subcostal or epigastric port used as the larger port (10/12 mm) for dissection or clip application [3,5]. Hand allocation represents another important ergonomic variable. Using the right hand for dissection may facilitate dissection for a right-handed surgeon; however, it can result in instrument crossing and loss of optimal triangulation depending on the port configuration. Conversely, performing Calot’s triangle dissection with the nondominant hand can preserve the conventional instrument orientation but may increase technical difficulty, particularly for surgeons with limited proficiency in the nondominant hand [5,6]. Thus, several combinations of surgeon position, port configuration, assistant traction, and hand allocation are possible, and the optimal arrangement may depend on individual surgeon experience and familiarity with the mirror-image anatomy.

In our case, we used a four-port configuration with a left subcostal port, with the surgeon positioned on the patient’s right side. A 10-mm epigastric port was used for dissection and application of clips to the cystic duct and artery. A fourth port was used by the assistant to provide controlled traction of the gallbladder. The surgeon used the dominant right hand for gallbladder traction and the left nondominant hand for dissection of Calot’s triangle. This arrangement maintained satisfactory triangulation while avoiding the excessive instrument crossing that can occur when the dominant hand is used for dissection through a mirror-image working port. The use of the nondominant hand for Calot’s dissection was facilitated by the surgeon’s established proficiency in left-hand laparoscopic dissection. Therefore, the technical novelty of our approach lies not in any individual port or surgeon position, but in the specific combination of right-sided surgeon positioning, four-port mirror-image placement, separate assistant traction and deliberate allocation of the dominant and nondominant hands according to their respective functions. These ergonomic advantages are qualitative observations from a single case and should not be interpreted as evidence that this configuration is universally superior to other reported techniques.

Indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence cholangiography may provide an additional safety adjunct in patients with situs inversus by enabling real-time visualization of the cystic duct and extrahepatic biliary tree in altered anatomy. Case reports have described the feasibility of ICG fluorescence for biliary visualization in patients with situs inversus [7]. However, ICG should complement, rather than replace, meticulous dissection and the attainment of the critical view of safety. In the present case, ICG fluorescence was not used because the biliary anatomy could be adequately delineated by conventional laparoscopic dissection.

To our knowledge, situs inversus with levocardia is exceptionally rare. This case adds to the limited literature by describing an ergonomic strategy that combines right-sided surgeon positioning, individualized left-sided port placement, and deliberate hand allocation for traction and dissection. Although based on a single patient, this configuration may provide a practical option for surgeons performing laparoscopic cholecystectomy in patients with similar rare anatomical variations.
