Genomic phylogenetic characterization of Brucella abortus biovar 1 from a human case in Yunnan Province, China
Authors: Qiuju Yang, Binbin Yu, Xiangdong Yang, Jiao Yang, Su Zhao, Qing Zhang, Fuping Yang, Zhenjun Li, Zhiguo Liu, Peng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Scientific question: Brucellosis has become an endemic disease with persistent transmission in Yunnan Province, China. The first human-derived Brucella abortus strain was isolated from local clinical cases. Characterizing its molecular profiles lays a solid foundation for subsequent epidemiological tracking.•Evidence before this study: Brucellosis has exhibited an expanding epidemic trend across Yunnan. Regional surveillance has detected multiple Brucella species. Brucella melitensis dominates local circulation and causes most human cases, while sporadic infections by Brucella canis have also been reported. Human infections with Brucella abortus are rare, and the genetic diversity of this pathogen remains unclear.•New findings: Phylogenetic analyses using multilocus variable-number tandem repeat analysis (MLVA)-16 and core genome single nucleotide polymorphism (cgSNP) demonstrated that this isolate is genetically more closely related to strains from Brazil and Argentina than to Chinese isolates, suggesting it belongs to a potential newly identified lineage of Brucella abortus.•Significance of the study: These findings expand the regional pathogen spectrum of brucellosis and demonstrate the unique phylogenetic features of the strain. This study advances our understanding of local Brucella diversity and highlights cross-border transmission risks of Brucella abortus linked to animal movement and livestock trade.

Introduction

Brucella abortus (B. abortus) is a common zoonotic pathogen that leads to substantial economic losses in livestock production and poses a serious public health risk due to its potential to cause severe systemic complications in humans [1]. B. abortus was first discovered by Bernhard Bang, and is known to cause undulant fever in human beings and abortions in cattle [2]. The main source of transmission of B. abortus to human is through consumption of unpasteurized dairy products or contact with aborted fetuses, placental membranes and uterine discharges [3]. B. abortus remains non-negligible and serious threat to human health, causing the painful and febrile illness known as undulant fever, which imposes significant physical and mental health burdens on affected individuals [4], [5]. For example, B. abortus was isolated from lumbar disc bulging in a woman who had rheumatoid arthritis and diabetes mellitus as underlying conditions [6].

A meta-analysis showed that overall seroprevalence of brucellosis in dairy cattle herds in China’s mainland was 1.9% from 2008 to 2018, rising from 1.6% in 2008–2012 to 2.6% in 2013–2018; in northern China, the brucellosis seroprevalence was >10%, while in southern China it reached only 5.5% [7]. In China's mainland, most human brucellosis cases were caused by Brucella melitensis [8]; however, the predominant strains differ by province; for example, Brucella suis (B. suis) in Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region and B. abortus in Sichuan Province were the most frequently isolated species, respectively [9]. A review showed that B. abortus biovar 3 was the predominant species (81.9%) and biovar (70.3%) in dairy cattle in China's mainland, live cattle movement or trade was assumed to be the key factor driving brucellosis transmission [10]. Accordingly, human cases of infection with B. abortus have been reported in some regions of China [11], [12]. In addition, the B. abortus has been isolated from farming environments in Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region (China), poses a risk of transmission from environment to animals and subsequently to humans [13]. Notably, this represents the first reported isolation of a B. abortus strain from a patient in Yunnan Province, suggesting an emerging public health concern. To identify the source of infection and characterize the molecular features of the strain, we performed bacteriology, whole-genome sequencing-based single nucleotide polymorphism (WGS-SNP) analysis, in silico multilocus sequence typing (MLST), and multilocus variable-number tandem repeat analysis (MLVA). This study provides critical insights for developing a targeted pathogen surveillance program.

Material and methods

Epidemiological, serology tests, the isolate of strains and species identified

On April 10, 2024, a patient developed symptoms including dizziness, chills, vomiting (approximately 5–6 episodes per day), abdominal pain, back pain, and loss of appetite. On April 18, her condition worsened with increased upper abdominal pain and the onset of fever (peaking at 40.3 °C, displaying an undulant pattern), necessitating hospital admission. To identify the potential source of infection, 5 mL blood samples were aseptically inoculated into culture bottles and incubated using the fully automated BACTEC™ FX blood culture system (Becton Dickinson) for a period of 2–4 weeks. Additionally, a total of 23 blood samples were collected: three from the patient’s family members, and 20 from the patient’s breeding cattle. All samples were tested using the standard rose bengal plate test (RBPT) and the serum agglutination test (SAT) in accordance with established protocols [14]. For rapid species/biovar determination, the strain was first screened by Brucella cell surface protein 31 (BCSP-31) polymerase chain reaction [PCR, 223 base pair (bp) amplicon] [15], and then confirmed using the AMOS-PCR assay [16]. Further the bio-typing of the strain was performed according to standard methods, including tests for growth for CO2 requirement, H2S production, dye sensitivity (basic fuchsin and thionin), agglutination with monospecific sera (anti-A, anti-M), and lysis by phages (Wb, Bk2, and Tb) to determinate the biovar of strain [17].

DNA prepare and whole genome sequence

The genomic deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) of the heat-inactivated Brucella strain (85 °C for 15 min) was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Kit (Qiagen, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Subsequently, a sequencing library was constructed using the Nextera XT Library Preparation Kit (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions, which included tagmentation, PCR amplification, and purification steps. The library was quantified with a Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and its quality was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) was then performed on the MGISEQ-2000 platform (MGI Tech Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China). Raw sequencing data underwent quality control by FastQC v0.11.9 [18]. Low-quality bases and adapters were trimmed using Trimmomatic v0.39 [19]. The obtained clean reads were de novo assembled using SPAdes v3.15.5 with default parameters [20], the resulting genome assembly was subsequently annotated with Prokka v1.14.6 with default settings [21].

Comparative genomic, in silico MLST and MLVA-16 genotyping analyses

For accurate species classification, the average nucleotide identity (ANI) between the strain and five reference genomes (Brucella melitensis, B. suis, B. canis, B. ovis, and B. neotomae) was calculated using the orthologous average nucleotide identity tool (OAT) [22]. The high coverage and similarity to the reference sequence (both >98%) provided sufficient quality for the subsequent identification of virulence-associated and antimicrobial resistance genes using established bioinformatic methods [23], [24]. Collinear analysis between the assembled genome and the B. abortus reference genome (GCA_000369945.1) was performed using Mauve software [25]. Following established MLVA and MLST schemes [26], in silico MLST and MLVA of the strain were conducted as previously described [27]. A MLVA-based comparative analysis of B. abortus strains was performed to elucidate their genetic relationships. Among these, a subset of 29 strains closely related to those identified in the current study was selected and analyzed using Bionumerics 8.0 to visualize the phylogenetic results (Table S1).

Global core genome SNP-based phylogenetic analysis

All 436 B. abortus genome sequences (Table S2) were downloaded from GenBank, and genome completeness and contamination were assessed using CheckM2 [28] (Table S2). Assemblies with completeness ≥98% and contamination ≤2% were included in further analyses. Using B. abortus 544 (GCA_000369945.1) as the reference genome, core genome SNPs (cgSNPs) were identified with Snippy v4.6.0. Recombination-associated SNPs were detected and removed using Gubbins v2.0.0 [29]. A recombination-free SNP alignment was generated with snp-dists v0.8.2. A maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was then constructed from the recombination-free SNP matrix using IQ-TREE v2.2.0 with 1,000 bootstrap replicates [30], and the resulting tree was visualized in iTOL v6.5.7 [31]. Further, a subclade containing 49 strains (1 from this study and 48 from GenBank) was extracted, and the phylogenetic tree was generated using the abovementioned methods to illustrate their phylogenetic relationships.

Results

Epidemiological investigation and serological testing

Epidemiological investigation revealed that the patient, a 62-year-old female, developed symptoms including dizziness, chills, vomiting (approximately 5–6 times per day), abdominal pain, back pain, and loss of appetite on April 10, 2024. Her symptoms worsened by April 16. On April 18, she subsequently presented with upper abdominal pain and fever (peak temperature 40.3 °C, remittent type). On March 24, a blood culture was positive for Brucella spp. Furthermore, the patient was engaged in cattle breeding (herd size = 20) and occasionally participated in grazing activities and farm fecal removal. She reported a history of direct contact with livestock, animal fur, and manure without the use of personal protective equipment such as gloves, masks, or dedicated workwear. Subsequently, serological testing of the patient's serum samples yielded positive results in both the RBPT and the SAT, with an SAT titer of 1:200 (++). Serum test results were negative for the other two family members. In addition, one bull out of the 20 cattle tested yielded a positive result in the Brucella rapid antigen detection test. The exact transmission route from cattle to humans remains insufficiently understood and requires further investigation. An on-site investigation identified a contiguous-pen housing system and divergent cattle sourcing: the index farm maintained a closed herd, while neighboring farms introduced external cattle, creating distinct pathogen introduction risks.

Isolate and species identification of Brucella

The morphological observation revealed that the colonies were round, raised, smooth, and transparent, exhibiting a faint blue luminescence on Brucella agar. The strain was preliminarily identified as the Brucella by BCSP-31 PCR, which produced a specific 223 bp size amplicon. Subsequent AMOS-PCR analysis yielded a 489 bp product (Fig. S1), confirming the strain as B. abortus, which was designated YN24001. Biochemical and phenotypic characterization identified the isolate as B. abortus biovar 1 (Table 1). Genome overview and comparative genomic analysis

The genome size of the B. abortus strain YN24001 was 3,264,269 bp, with 21 scaffolds; N50 value was 367,669 bp, and the N75 as 251,664 bp. The longest scaffold was 518,897 bp, the total length of the coding genes was 2, 828,517 bp, and the GC content was 55.45%. ANI was 99.98% to Brucella abortus 544 (biovar 1) (Fig. 1), confirming the causative agent as B. abortus. The genome of the strain contained peptide antibiotics and defensin resistant mprF gene; a total of 72 virulence association genes were identified, of which 31 were involved in lipopolysaccharide (LPS, VF0367) synthesis (Table 2 and Fig. 2), 14 in T4SS secreted effectors (VF0695), 12 in VirB type IV secretion system, six in Brucebactin (VF0692), two in BvrR-BvrS (VF0368), and one gene in Direct heme uptake system (VF0693), BmaC (VF1341), BigB (VF1345), CG (VF0366), BtpB (VF0522), SP41 (VF0694), and BtaF (VF1343), respectively (Table 3 and Fig. 2). These genes play a vital role in immune modulation, effector delivery systems, nutritional/metabolic factors, and strain adherence. Collinearity analysis revealed that both chromosomes (I and II) of strain YN24001 exhibited a high degree of synteny with the B. abortus reference genome. Phylogenetic analysis of B. abortus biovar 1 strain YN24001

In silico MLST typing showed that the strain belonged to sequence type 1 (ST1), consistent with B. abortus, and nine allele loci (gap/aroA/glk/dnaK/gyrB/trpE/cobQ/int_hyp/omp25) ordered as 2-1-1-2-1-3-1-1-1. In silico MLVA analysis showed that its panel 1 and MLVA-11 codes were 28 and 82, respectively. Based on a global comparison using MLVA-16, this strain was found to be closely related to strains originating from Brazil (Fig. S3). Global cgSNP phylogenetic analysis showed that the strain from the present study was more closely related to B. abortus strains originating from South American countries, including Argentina and Brazil, than to previously reported Chinese strains (Fig. 3). Previously reported Chinese B. abortus were divided into six clades (Chinese Ba I − Ba VI), strain YN24001 from this study formed subclade with strains from Brazil and Argentina (Fig. S4). Discussion

In the present study, bacteriology, bio-typing, MLST, MLVA, and WGS-cgSNP phylogeny analysis were applied to investigate the source and molecular features of a strain. As far as we know, this is the first reported isolation of B. abortus from a patient in Yunnan Province. B. abortus strains have been persistently spreading in livestock and wildlife in northern provinces, including Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region (Inner Mongolia), Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region (Xinjiang), and Qinghai Province [32]. In Inner Mongolia, there are at least three B. abortus biovars (1, 3 and 6), and B. abortus biovar 3 is the predominant one [33]. There were nine B. abortus strains collected from across Xinjiang, China from 2010 to 2015 [34], and two B. abortus biovar 1 strains were isolated from Himalayan Marmot in Qinghai Province, China [35]. These data imply that B. abortus strain has been present in animal populations in Yunnan Province, which poses an important public health risk to the population due to trade and movement of cattle across the region frequently. Meanwhile, isolated B. abortus strains from humans further expanded the pathogen spectrum of brucellosis in Yunnan Province. These finding highlights that strengthening surveillance of animal brucellosis and the management of infected livestock (cattle) is a priority strategy to reduce the infection risk of the human population.

Collinearity analysis demonstrated that strain YN24001 maintains extensive synteny with the B. abortus reference genome, supporting the genomic stability typical of this pathogen. However, the identification of several short fragment deletions on chromosome II may represent lineage-specific genetic variations. Further functional studies are needed to determine whether these genomic alterations contribute to altered pathogenicity, host range expansion, or epidemiological features that could impact public health interventions. MLST analysis showed strain belonging to ST1. This sequence type is widespread in Europe, America, and China over a time span of isolated strains from 1923 to 2020 [36]; indicating that this strain population has maintained a persistent presence. MLVA analysis showed that the strain was closely related to strains from Brazil. Furthermore, global WGS-cgSNP phylogenetic analysis revealed high genetic similarity with strains from Brazil and Argentina. This evidence suggests that the case was potentially caused by a newly identified lineage of B. abortus, though the exact source of infection requires further investigation. Similarly, strain XZ19-1, isolated from a yak in Lhasa, Xizang Autonomous Region, was shown by whole-genome phylogenetic analysis to be more closely related to B. abortus strains originating from European countries than to previously collected Chinese strains [37]. In contrast, phylogenetic analysis showed that strain BD002 from Tianjin Municipality directly under the Central Government of China formed a sub-clade with strains from other Chinese provinces, including Inner Mongolia, Jilin Province, Heilongjiang Province, Hebei Province, and Shanxi Province [38]. Global phylogenomic reconstruction demonstrates that B. abortus originated in Africa, with its subsequent dissemination to the Middle East, Europe, and Asia being probably driven by the historical movement of infected cattle [1]. Whole genome single nucleotide polymorphism (wgSNP) analysis of 46 subclade C1 strains shows clustering with strains from neighboring regions/countries (e.g., North Caucasus, Western Russia, Siberia, China, Mongolia), providing genomic support for the historical introduction of this clade into Kazakhstan via migration and livestock trade from the 1730s to 1970s [39]. The 19th century in Costa Rica was marked by extensive ranching expansion, during which several Bos taurus dairy breeds were introduced into the territory. Consistent with this historical context, phylodynamic analysis reveals that the main B. abortus lineages, likely introduced over a century ago, are now widespread, whereas newer introductions appear geographically restricted [40].

Yunnan Province is committed to building itself into the Bovine Capital of China, aiming to establish a major beef cattle production base and high-end beef supply hub in southern China. In 2021, the province's year-end cattle inventory reached 8.71 million head, representing a 15.0% increase compared to 2012, with an average annual growth rate of 1.6%. Epidemiological survey data indicated that smuggled cattle originated from Myanmar, Laos, Thailand, India, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka and other countries, and were subsequently transported across borders via multiple routes through various countries [41]. Frequent livestock trade, particularly in cattle, likely contributes to the introduction of new lineages. However, the specific scale and implementation details are still being evaluated, and further research is needed to determine the exact origins.

In order to accelerate the development of rural animal husbandry, beef cattle breeding has been introduced as a new industry for local farmers to develop. However, farmers often lack scientific breeding and disease prevention awareness. Consequently, they fail to take protective measures when delivering calves, handling aborted animals and carcasses [42], thereby exposing themselves to secretions and excretions of animals infected with Brucella, which increases the risk of brucellosis infection. Therefore, this study underscores the necessity for strict quarantine measures, particularly for breeders importing livestock. It is imperative to prevent infected animals and their products from entering the market.

This study offers vital insights into the spectrum of Brucella species but acknowledges key constraints. Primarily, the lack of systematic cattle surveillance data and the unavailability of a bovine isolate prevent definitive confirmation of a bovine-to-human transmission route. Furthermore, reliance on a single isolated strain limits a comprehensive evaluation of B. abortus genetic diversity in Yunnan Province. Consequently, we recommend that subsequent research integrate more extensive environmental or animal monitoring, including screening of additional local cattle herds, to furnish new evidence for source tracing.

Conclusions

Based on comprehensive molecular, genomic, and phenotypic profiling, strain YN24001 was definitively identified as B. abortus biovar 1, indicating a potential emerging public health concern in the region. Phylogenetic analysis further revealed that this ST1 strain clusters more closely with South American lineages than with previously documented Chinese isolates, suggesting the emergence of a novel B. abortus lineage. Collectively, this study not only expands the known genetic diversity of B. abortus in the region, but also underscores B. abortus as a growing public health threat and highlights the urgent need for enhanced surveillance systems to monitor its further transmission.
