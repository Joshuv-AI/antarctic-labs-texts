The pediatric ocular surface microbiome in pet-owning households
Authors: Xiangtian Ling, Charlene C Yim, Qihang Sun, Yu Peng, Yuzhou Zhang, Ka Wai Kam, Alvin L Young, Patrick Ip, Stephen Kwok-Wing Tsui, Clement C Tham, Chi Pui Pang, Li Jia Chen, Jason C Yam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Microbial diversity increases on ocular surfaces in children with pet exposure.•Ocular diversity is dose-dependent on daily pet-contact time.•Pet-exposed children harbor more diverse environmental and animal-related bacteria.•Ocular microbiota reveals a novel link between pet exposure and immune regulation.Keywords: Pet exposure, Ocular surface microbiome, Pediatric ophthalmology, Microbial diversity

Introduction

Pet ownership is associated with both beneficial and adverse health outcomes in children, (Friedmann and Son, 2009) including enhanced immune tolerance and reduced risk of allergic diseases, as well as potential exposure to zoonotic pathogens. The ocular surface, a direct interface with the environment, is particularly susceptible to microbial contamination through close contact with animals. Given that children's immune systems are still developing, interactions with pets may influence mucosal immunity, including that of the ocular surface (Gensollen and Blumberg, 2017).

The ocular surface microbiome (OSM), a relatively low-diversity but stable community of bacteria, viruses, and fungi inhabiting the conjunctiva, plays an important role in maintaining ocular surface homeostasis and modulating local immune responses (Zilliox and Bouchard, 2023; Gomes et al., 2020). Dysbiosis of the OSM has been implicated in various ocular conditions, including dry eye disease, blepharitis, keratitis, and allergic conjuntivitis (Song et al., 2024; Wardle et al., 1996; Kang et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2021).

Children represent a unique population for microbiome research due to their dynamic microbial development and heightened environmental interactions. Contact with pets, a modifiable environmental factor, is known to influence gut and skin microbiomes (Kim et al., 201a; Trinh et al., 2018; Lehtimaki et al., 2018). However, its specific impact on the pediatric OSM remains largely unexplored. Understanding this relationship is increasingly important in urban settings, where rising pet ownership and shifting microbial exposures due to modern lifestyles may alter early-life microbiome development (Gensollen and Blumberg, 2017).

In this study, we hypothesize that pet exposure is associated with distinct compositional differences in the OSM of children. By comparing the OSM profiles of children with and without pet exposure, we aim to identify potential microbial signatures associated with pet contact. These signatures may have implications for ocular surface immunity and susceptibility to eye diseases.

Methods

Subject recruitment and sample collection

Children aged 5–15 years were recruited from the CUHK Hong Kong Children Eye Study (Yuan et al., 2019; Yam et al., 2020; Cheung et al., 2018). Exclusion criteria included history of ocular trauma or congenital malformations, inability to complete ocular examinations and use of topical eye drops or contact lenses within one week prior to sampling. All participants underwent comprehensive ophthalmic and physical examinations. A detailed history of household pet exposure over the preceding six months was obtained for each child using standardized questionnaires administered to both the children and their parents together. These questionnaires were designed to capture key aspects of living environment and lifestyle. Specifically, participants were asked to report the average daily time spent in contact with a pet, differentiated across school weekdays, school weekends, and vacation days. This temporal data was subsequently aggregated and analyzed as a leveled measure of exposure intensity. Furthermore, additional demographic and environmental data were collected, including housing type, time spent outdoors, and living space.

The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Chinese University of Hong Kong. Written informed consent was obtained from guardians, with oral assent from children.

Conjunctival sampling

Before sampling, one drop of sterile proxymetacaine hydrochloride 0.5 % (ProVain-POS Eye Drops 0.5 % w/v, Germany) was used in each eye. After 2 min, the inferior conjunctival fornix of both eyes was sampled using separate sterile Puritan flocked swabs (Puritan Medical Products, USA). Swabs were gently rolled from nasal to temporal fornix 3–5 times per eye, avoiding contact with eyelids/lashes. Samples were immediately placed in sterile tubes on ice and transferred to −80 °C storage within 2 h.

DNA extraction and sequencing

DNA was extracted from swabs within two weeks using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (Qiagen, Netherlands) following manufacturer protocols. DNA concentration was quantified via Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher, USA).

The V3–V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 341F (CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG) and 805R (GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC). Amplicons were sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform (2 × 300 bp paired-end; Illumina, USA).

Bioinformatics and statistical analysis

Raw sequences were processed in QIIME2 (v2023.05) (Hall and Beiko, 2018) DADA2 denoising generated amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Chimeric sequences were removed, and ASVs were aligned (Callahan et al., 2016). ASVs were classified taxonomically using a q2-feature-classifier trained on the SILVA 138 database, trimmed to the 341F/805R region (Quast et al., 2013). Potential contaminants were removed using the decontam package with the prevalence method (threshold = 0.5), a strict criterion chosen specifically for the low-biomass nature of ocular surface samples (Davis et al., 2018). ASVs were filtered if they: were singletons (appearing only once) or had total read counts <10 across all samples. Samples with <10,000 available ASVs were considered as low quality and excluded.

Alpha diversity including Shannon index, Simpson index, Observed index was calculated with vegan and compared via Mann–Whitney U tests. Beta diversity were compared via permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) with vegan R package. Microbial signature was identified using Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) via microbiomeMarker package.

Functional prediction was conducted with Picrust2 (Douglas et al., 2020), to process with Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway (Kanehisa and Goto, 2000). Differential abundance analysis of pathways was performed using ggpicrust2 package (Yang et al., 2023).

Results

Demographic data

After quality control, which excluded 11 samples with fewer than 10,000 reads, a final cohort (Table 1) of 347 Chinese children (mean age 11.39 ± 2.07 years) was included in the analysis. Following quality control and contamination removal, totally 13,141,314 ASVs were retained for analysis (mean per sample: 37,871 ± 14,689 ASVs). Among participants, 51 children (67 % male; mean age 11.41 ± 1.97 years) had household pet exposure (mean 37,498 ± 14,134 ASVs), while 296 children (49 % male; mean age 11.38 ± 2.09 years) had no pet exposure (mean 38,080 ± 15,234 ASVs).

Alpha diversity

All alpha diversity indices (Shannon, Simpson, and Observed) were significantly higher in the pet-exposed group (P < 0.05; Fig. 1). We found positive correlations between time spent with pets and all diversity indices: Shannon (r = 0.15, P = 0.0042), Simpson (r = 0.13, P = 0.019), and Observed ASVs (r = 0.12, P = 0.025, Fig. 2).

Bacterial composition and the microbial signatures

Firmicutes, Proteobacteria and Actinobacteriota constituted the dominant phyla across all children. The most abundant genera in both groups (Fig. 3A) were Staphylococcus, Corynebacterium, uncultured Neisseriaceae and Streptococcus. Beta diversity analysis revealed significant compositional differences between groups (Bray-Curtis dissimilarity; PERMANOVA: P = 0.015; Fig. 3B).

Bacterial compositions in pet-exposed and non-exposed groups. A. The top 10 bacterial compositions at genus level in non- and pet- exposed groups. B. Distinct beta-diversities in non- and pet- exposed groups C. LefSe distinguished the signature genera in two groups.

LEfSe analysis identified distinct microbial signatures (Linear Discriminant Analysis, LDA score > 2, P < 0.05): Streptococcus, Actinomyces, Neisseria and more than 10 genera were enriched in the pet-exposed group, while only Lawsonella and Psychrobacter were enriched in the non-exposed group.

Functional prediction

Picrust2 was used to predict the KEGG pathway and compare the two groups. Biosynthesis of 12-, 14- and 16-membered macrolides, Biosynthesis of type II polyketide backbone and Cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway were found to be higher abundant in non-exposed group while the regulation of lipolysis in adipocytes, VEGF signalling pathway and NF-kappa beta signalling pathway were enriched in pet-exposed group (Fig. 4).

Discussion

This is among the first studies to characterize distinct OSM alterations in children with pet exposure. Firstly, we found that pet-exposed children exhibit higher alpha diversity in their OSM, and this diversity positively correlates with the amount of daily time spend with pets. Beyond overall diversity, we identified specific bacterial taxa that were more abundant in the pet-exposed group, including Streptococcus, Actinomyces, Neisseria, and others. These compositional shifts suggest that pet exposure may play a role in shaping the microbial ecology of the ocular surface. Furthermore, functional prediction analysis revealed enrichment of pathways related to NF-κB signaling and VEGF signaling in the pet-exposed group, indicating potential immunological and vascular implications. In contrast, children without pet exposure showed increased activity in macrolide biosynthesis pathways, which may reflect differences in microbial metabolic functions or host-microbiome interactions.

The increase in alpha diversity (both richness and evenness) in pet-exposed children aligns with the "microbial exchange" hypothesis. Dogs exhibit higher ocular microbial diversity than humans (Clougher et al., 2025), likely due to frequent environmental contact and fur-mediated microbial retention. Our observed modest dose-response relationship, where pet contact time positively correlated with diversity metrics, further reinforce the likelihood of direct microbial transmission through physical interaction or shared environments.

This process is consistent with recent studies demonstrating that the indoor microbiome directly influences the microbial communities of residents, including those in the respiratory tract and gut (Zhang et al., 2023a, 2023b). Since microbial transfer from the indoor environment to a child's oral cavity has been documented (Zhang et al., 2023a), it follows that the ocular surface, which is continually exposed to the air, is similarly affected. Microbial transfer has been directly observed from the indoor environment to the childhood oral cavity, establishing a precedent for this pathway. Therefore, pets likely contribute to a more diverse ocular surface microbiome both through direct contact and by enriching the overall microbial environment of the home. This expanded pool of indoor microbes then serves as a source for colonization. This pattern is consistent with previous research on gut and skin microbiomes where pet cohabitation enriches microbial gene pools (Nermes et al., 2015; Song et al., 2013), and now our findings extend this paradigm to the ocular surface.

Compositionally, pet-exposed children harbored distinct bacterial signatures, including Neisseria, Veillonella, Moraxella, uncultured Caulobacteraceae, genera reported in canine conjunctival, oral or fur microbiomes (Clougher et al., 2025; Cusco et al., 2017). While some taxa like Neisseria include opportunistic pathogens linked to keratitis or endophthalmitis (Gomart et al., 2019), their enrichment here may reflect commensal colonization rather than pathogenicity (Kim et al., 2019b). Notably, the dual presence of both protective and pathogenic microbes highlights the "hygiene hypothesis" paradox: controlled microbial exposures may train immunity despite theoretical infection risks (Tabilas et al., 2022; Rook and Bloomfield, 2021).

Functional predictions suggest important host-microbe interactions. Enrichment of NF-κB signaling and VEGF pathways in pet-exposed children implies microbial priming of innate immunity regulation, mechanisms potentially underlying reduced allergy risks (Edwards et al., 2009). Early-life pet exposure is epidemiologically linked to lower rates of rhinoconjunctivitis and atopy (Dharmage et al., 2012); and our findings propose OSM modulation as a novel mechanistic link in this relationship. Conversely, non-exposed children showed elevated macrolide biosynthesis pathways, potentially reflecting antimicrobial defense mechanisms in microbially naive ocular surfaces (Gao et al., 2025).

There are some limitations of this study. First, our data were collected from self-report questionnaires, may introduce recall bias for data collection like pet exposure duration or frequency and the standardized questionnaires lack of data like specific pet types, cleaning habits, and presence of siblings. Future study may benefit from collecting these detailed environmental factors. Second, the cross-sectional design limits the explanation of result and could not reveal any casual effect. Longitudinal studies tracking OSM before and after pet adoption may need. Third, the functional analysis in this paper was predicted with bioinformatic tools. To validate these predicted immune-modulating pathways (e.g., NF-κB and VEGF), future work should employ techniques such as shotgun metagenomics to directly profile functional genes, or metatranscriptomics and metaproteomics to measure microbial gene expression and protein production.

In conclusion, pet exposure influences the pediatric ocular microbiome, increasing diversity, introducing animal-related or environmental taxa, and modulating immune-metabolic pathways. This suggests OSM as a mediator of pet-related immune education, potentially influencing ocular surface health and allergy susceptibility in children.
