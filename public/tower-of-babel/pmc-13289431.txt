Bingo Lab: game-based learning in microbiology
Authors: Doaa M AlEraky, Salah Eldin Kassab, Aswathy Nandakumar, Sherif Aly Sadek, Hesham Marei, Mohamed M Al-Eraky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAssessment in the microbiology laboratories is traditionally structured around spot diagnosis or Objective Structured Practical Examinations (OSPEs) to assess students’ ability to identify the causative agents of infectious diseases. Game-based learning has great potential to engage students and indicate their learning gaps in a non-threatening learning environment.PurposeThe study introduced ‘Bingo Lab’ as an innovative gamification intervention in undergraduate microbiology courses. We plan to pilot Bingo Lab in two programs, Dentistry and Nursing. The study tracks students’ engagement throughout the activity and examines gender differences and their correlation with microbiology performance. Additionally, it explores how Bingo Lab can create opportunities for meaningful learning and gathers students’ feedback on their experience.MethodologyThe intervention was structured in five stages: planning, orientation, Bingo Lab., feedback, and reporting. We shortlisted 25 ‘unknown’ microbiology spots as a mock (revision) exam in a laboratory setup. The spots were randomly mapped on a 5x5 Bingo card. One Bingo is awarded when any student completes five stations, whether horizontally, vertically, or diagonally on the Bingo card. Students’ engagement was measured three times: early in the session, in the middle, and towards the end. We also solicited their feedback anonymously using a template of three sections: I like, I wish, and I wonder. ResultsStudents’ engagement scores significantly increased from the early stage to the middle of the activity (p = .005) and from the beginning to the end of the session (p < .001), but there was no significant difference between the beginning and the end of the activity. There were no significant gender differences in engagement at any time point. Bingo Labs were well received by students and helped them address their learning gaps, but they also shared a couple of concerns about having more time for preparation and receiving instructions in advance. DiscussionBingo in the microbiology lab was well received by students and enabled them to identify their learning gaps and seek feedback and validation from experts. The study supported the effectiveness of the Bingo Lab as a game-based learning approach in improving students’ engagement. In a competitive culture, exam-oriented students need to celebrate their excellence, such as through Bingo, a rewarding experience that boosts their self-esteem. ConclusionsThe use of Bingo in microbiology laboratories has been found to be effective in engaging students and guiding them to indicate their learning gaps in a non-threatening environment. Keywords: Microbiology, Bingo, Laboratory, Assessment, Game-based learning

Background

Microbiology has been integrated into almost all undergraduate programs in health professions education, but it still needs to be tailored to each program’s learning outcomes. For instance, the scope, breadth, depth, and selection of organisms vary in medicine, dentistry, and pharmacy compared with nursing [1, 2]. While it’s fascinating and essential in health professions education, microbiology is often perceived by students as a challenging subject due to the complex classification and nomenclature of microorganisms, their intricate ecosystems, metabolism, genetics, and pathogenesis, along with their constant evolution and adaptation [1]. Students need to apply critical thinking and problem-solving skills to recognize and isolate microorganisms based on their biochemical profiles, behaviors, and microbial structures [3–5].

The identification and characterization of ‘unknown’ organisms are hallmarks of microbiology laboratory training. But preparing the microbiology spots for teaching, review, and assessment requires extensive logistics to provide unknown culture mixes, tubed and plated culture media for a large sections of microbiology labs [6]. The traditional station-to-station lab practical sessions have become less feasible with the growing number of students, and with the integration of microbiology across different programs, e.g. medicine, nursing, pharmacy, and dentistry [7].Logistical constraints exacerbate this issue, including limited contact time, constrained lab resources, and uneven faculty-student ratios [8]. We need to adopt innovative approaches to ensure sustainability and the efficient use of laboratory resources, personnel, time, and space [9]. Some studies reported the effective use of technology, like virtual simulation [10], while others introduced elements of gamification [11]. Game-based learning (GBL) might have great potential to motivate students to memorize complex concepts and apply them under pressure in a non-threatening environment [12].

The flow theory

The theoretical underpinning for using GBL is rooted in the Flow Theory, which describes a psychological state characterised by intense focus, enjoyment, and immersion in an activity [13]. Flow is optimally experienced when an individual’s skills perfectly align with the task’s challenge level [14] The GBL environments are particularly effective at eliciting this state of ‘flow’ because they maintain a careful equilibrium between difficulty and achievability, thus keeping students persistently engaged and motivated to continue learning [15] A learner who enters a flow state during a laboratory game is more likely to assimilate microbiological principles and apply them to diagnostic [16]. When comparing two different student-centered strategies, such as GBL and problem-based learning (PBL), students reported more satisfaction with GBL, as compared with PBL, particularly in clarifying misconceptions and advancing their performance in microbiology [17].

The concept of GBL incorporates gaming principles, such as competition, rewards, real-time feedback, and goal-directed tasks, into educational activities. It creates immersive, interactive learning environments that promote motivation, active engagement, and experiential knowledge acquisition [18]. Unlike passive learning modes, GBL fosters intrinsic motivation by allowing learners to apply knowledge in a dynamic, simulated context [19]. Scholars reported strong evidence on the positive impact of GBL on students’ learning satisfaction, critical thinking, clinical decision-making, and clinical skills [20].

The GBL approach instructs learners (either individually or in teams) to complete a specific task within a limited time frame. For instance, escape rooms were designed for students to solve a puzzle in groups before leaving the room, whether in person or virtually. The literature supports the claim that escape rooms foster problem-solving skills, reasoning, and teamwork [21–25]. Group games might be more effective in societies with low power distance and a low masculinity index, as per Hofstede’s six cultural dimensions [26]. These societies value partnership, consensus, and harmony. This is not the case for Arab societies, which prioritize categorization and ranking over collaboration and teamwork. In academic settings, students are motivated by personal achievement and grades; they tend to fight for each mark. Exam-oriented students need another set of games where they can compete as individuals, not in groups, and celebrate their excellence, such as Bingo [27].

Scholars reported other GBL approaches in microbiology and immunology. For instance, the Immune Battle mimics real-world interactions between immune cells and microbes using board game mechanics. Students act as either pathogens or members of the immune system, trying to defend the host from infection [28]. In an earlier study, we used Immune Café, inspired by the World Café concept, in which student groups were assigned to study one immune cell through a jigsaw collaborative learning approach. Then, a messenger is nominated from each group to explore the interaction among immune cells [29].

This study aims to implement Bingo Lab in microbiology courses across various programs as an alternative to traditional lab practical sessions for teaching and assessment. We plan to measure students’ engagement during the Bingo Lab and report on gender differences in engagement scores and the correlation between engagement and performance. Additionally, students’ feedback will be collected regarding the Bingo Lab and how it provided opportunities for meaningful learning.

Methodology

Study design & context

This cross-sectional study was conducted in the microbiology laboratory across two programs at the Colleges of Dentistry and Nursing at Gulf Medical University (GMU) in the United Arab Emirates (UAE).

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Gulf Medical University (GMU), Ref. no. IRB-COD-FAC-233-June-2025.

Participants

All second-year students enrolled in the 2024–2025 academic year, because this is the year where they study microbiology across different programs in two colleges, namely: the Bachelor of Dental Surgery (BDS program), n = 79 students, and the Bachelor of Science in Nursing (BSN program), n = 71 students. Students’ participation in the study was contingent upon their consent. Students who were absent on the day of the lab sessions, did not demonstrate a willingness to participate, or failed to complete the students’ engagement surveys on the three occasions, were excluded from the study.

Methods used for data collection

After applying Bingo Lab in microbiology, a validated 7-item survey was administered to measure students’ engagement. The survey was adapted from the previously validated short version of the User Experience Questionnaire [30] and from its applications in using 360° videos for Anatomy education [31, 32]. The 7-item survey is presented in Table 1.

The 7-item survey to measure students’ engagement

The 7-item survey measures the state of flow, an intense engagement characterized by distinct features. The survey includes seven items assessing one construct of student engagement as a flow-like state, from different aspects. For instance, immersion is measured using items #1, #2, and #7 (reverse-scored), while diminished awareness of time in items #3 and #4, and enjoyment in items #5 and #6. The seven items measure different aspects of the construct of state flow.

Students’ engagement was measured using QuestionPro [33] three times, early in the Bingo Lab, in the middle, and towards the end of the session, as demonstrated in Fig. 1.

Data collection process

The study was conducted in five stages: Planning, Orientation, Bingo Lab, Feedback, and Reporting, as shown in Fig. 1.

Stage 1: planning

We shortlisted 25 unknown spots as microbiological stations as a mock (revision) exam in a laboratory setup. The spots were carefully selected to present the salient characteristics of the most common microorganisms, including their growth patterns on a plated culture medium, types of hemolysis, biochemical reactions, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing. The spots were randomly mapped on a 5 × 5 Bingo card. Each station has a unique number from 1 to 25, as arranged in Table 2.

Stage 3: Bingo Lab

Students were divided into groups of 25 each, and they were admitted to the Microbiology Lab with their Bingo Cards. They were given 2 min to recognize the spot, move through a sequence, and write their findings at the relevant station number on the Bingo card. Any student can claim Bingo whenever he/she feels they have a series of FIVE stations in a row, in any arrangement, whether horizontal, vertical, or diagonal. Faculty members are moving around to validate the Bingos. Meanwhile, students’ engagement was measured using the 7-item survey (Table 2) throughout the Bingo Lab. early on in the session, in the middle, and towards the end. Gender differences in student engagement scores will be reported along with the correlation between engagement and performance.

Stage 4: feedback

After the Bingo Lab, students were asked to provide their feedback on the overall learning experience using a Padlet template/framework with I like, I wish, and I wonder. [34]. Responses were coded and categorized into themes to provide insights on how to improve the process in future encounters of the Bingo Microbiology Lab. Meanwhile, the model answers for the entire Bingo card were disclosed, and students were asked to validate their answers and identify their knowledge gaps.

Stage 5: reporting

A detailed report was drafted to document the process and the outcome of the Bingo Lab, to identify lessons learned and areas for improvement.

Plan for the analysis and interpretation of students’ responses

Students’ responses on the 7-item survey were scored on a 10-point Likert scale from Strongly Agree (SA) to Strongly Disagree (SD). All items are positively worded, except item #7. For positively worded items, SA = 10 and SD = 1, while it’s reversed in item #7, which was negatively worded. Quantitative analysis used IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 21.0) [35].

Descriptive statistics, including means and standard deviations, were computed for engagement scores at three time points: early, middle, and the end of the educational activity. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to examine differences in engagement scores across the three time points. Where statistically significant differences were found, post hoc comparisons were conducted using the Least Significant Difference (LSD) and Bonferroni corrections to control for Type I error.

To assess gender differences in engagement, independent-samples t-tests were conducted at each time point. Levene’s test was used to evaluate the assumption of homogeneity of variances. Additionally, Pearson product-moment correlation coefficients were calculated to explore the relationships between engagement scores and objective structured practical examination (OSPE) performance. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Reliability analysis

The internal consistency reliability of the 7-item student engagement tool was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. The overall scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.80 (α = 0.797) and an alpha based on standardized items of 0.82, indicating a satisfactory level of homogeneity. Item-level descriptive statistics showed mean scores ranging from 3.05 to 3.98 and standard deviations from 0.96 to 1.30, suggesting adequate variability in student responses and no evidence of restricted range.

These findings indicate that the student engagement tool demonstrates acceptable to good reliability for assessing student engagement during game-based learning activities in Microbiology laboratories.

One-way ANOVA and post-hoc comparisons

A one-way ANOVA was conducted to compare students’ engagement scores at three time points: early on in the session, in the middle, and towards the end of the gamified learning activity. The results indicated a statistically significant difference in engagement across the three groups, F(2, 216) = 9.52, p < .001. Post hoc comparisons using LSD and Bonferroni corrections revealed that engagement scores significantly increased from the beginning to the middle of the activity (p = .005) and from the middle to towards the end of the activity (p < .001). However, no significant difference was found between during and after the activity (Table 5).

In summary, students’ engagement scores increased significantly across the three time points, with ANOVA results indicating a main effect of time. Post hoc tests revealed that engagement was significantly higher in the middle and towards the end of the activity than early on, though no significant difference was found between the middle and the end. Independent samples t-tests showed no significant gender differences in engagement at any time point, nor between dental and nursing students. Although engagement scores were not significantly correlated with OSPE performance, Pearson correlations indicated a strong positive relationship between engagement in the middle and towards the end of the Bingo Lab.

Reporting students’ feedback

This novel intervention of the Bingo Lab was well received by the students, as supported by their feedback on their learning experience. For instance, one student said:

“I liked how I was able to test myself and find the gaps in my learning. It’s such a fun and engaging way to reinforce our knowledge. I love how it challenges us to quickly identify and match the different bingo squares with the corresponding microbes”.

Students’ feedback focused on key concepts of satisfaction, gamification, activity readiness, and motivation.

Discussion

Bingo Labs in microbiology is one of the GBL approaches that introduces fun and competition in a non-threatening environment. The intervention was well received by students and enabled them to identify their learning gaps and seek feedback and validation from experts. The study supported the effectiveness of the Bingo Lab in improving students’ engagement.

Engagement throughout the Bingo Lab

The engagement scores escalated over time throughout the Bingo Lab, with a significant increase from the early stages to the end. This can be interpreted as game-based learning that stimulates a flow-like state, with three components: immersion, enjoyment, and diminished awareness of time. Yeh et al. reported a similar effect of game-based learning in creating a flow experience, which also contributes to mindful learning [36]. Similar studies found that integrating game-based learning improved students’ engagement and performance [37].

Engagement scores and gender difference

The gender gap in education in favour of girls is a widely known phenomenon [38], where male students, on average, have significantly lower scores for planning, task management, and persistence; and higher scores for disengagement [39]. Yet in the current study, no significant difference in students’ engagement was found between males and females. These findings resonate with other studies that do not focus on gender differences but rather invite educators to foster supportive environments that recognize the unique needs of male and female students [40].

Engagement scores and OSPE scores

The main purpose of correlating with OSPE scores was to test the relationship between their academic performance in the domain of activity (microbiology) and their engagement in the labs. No significant difference was found between students’ engagement scores and their OSPE performance. To interpret these findings, we may need to shed light on the complexity of the construct of students’ engagement, with its components, namely behavioral, cognitive, and psychological engagement [41]. Interestingly, performance was positively influenced by behavioral and cognitive engagement, but negatively by psychological engagement [42]. To investigate the relationship with performance, another more comprehensive tool should be applied, such as the University Student Engagement Inventory [31], to measure engagement across the behavioral, emotional, and cognitive dimensions.

Beyond engagement, bingo also contributed to better exam scores on the topics covered in the Bingo activity [23]. Lotakis et al. [45] used another variation of bingo to document the essential procedures and tasks required during the first two months of surgical residency programs [24]. In this case, the bingo cards were filled over weeks, not in a single setting, as reported in our study, and the approach is primarily used to mitigate variations in experience in clinical practice. Likewise, Sannathimmappa et al. [44] reported a statistically significant improvement in students’ scores before and after the bingo laboratory in immunology.

Satisfaction and meaningful learning

Students reported high satisfaction with the Bingo Lab, as evidenced by their quotes and comments about their learning experience. They highlighted key points, providing an opportunity for meaningful learning on how they validated their learning and identified knowledge gaps during the Bingo Lab. The Bingo game was reported as an effective game-based strategy for promoting students’ learning in physiology [43] and immunology [44], and for planning clinical rotations in surgery [45] and nursing [46]. Students can compete as individuals to interpret unknown stations in a special arrangement and seek validation by their instructors before claiming a Bingo. The Bingo Lab provides an embedded mechanism for formative feedback, enabling students to validate their answers and reflect on their performance to identify knowledge gaps in real time. This formative loop enhances self-assessment and calibration, critical components of metacognitive learning [47].

Implications of the study

Faculty members may use Bingo as a GBL approach for three benefits. First, Bingo was well-received and kept students engaged throughout the session. Second, students reported ‘perceived’ learning (understanding) in their qualitative responses. Third, which is more relevant to logistics, Bingo Labs is an efficient solution for capitalizing on limited space and time resources to prepare microbiology laboratories for teaching and assessment to variable numbers of students. With a large number of students, assessors may use multiple bingo cards for different groups. Moreover, Bingo lab can be adjusted based on class size and the availability of spots (unknown items) in any discipline, including microbiology, histology, anatomy, etc.

Strengths & limitations

The study applied Bingo Labs in microbiology courses across different programs, including dental and nursing. The findings highlight the significant potential of GBL to facilitate learning and assessment in a challenging subject like microbiology. The positive feedback from students was encouraging, but careful planning and orientation are essential, particularly for those unfamiliar with the Bingo game dynamics.

Naturally, the study has some limitations. We could have assessed the effectiveness of Bingo Labs on learning by using students’ grades on high-stakes exams. However, the game might lose its fun aspect, and exam anxiety could negatively impact students’ responses to the intervention. Additionally, evaluating learning was beyond the scope of this study, which focused on collecting students’ feedback and engagement. We could also have repeated the intervention with the same student group, but we doubted that the excitement of GBL would last over time. Finally, students’ responses were gathered using a strict template (I like, I wish, I wonder). Although responses were written, they were too abstract to truly express their reactions and feelings. A more systematic approach, such as using focus groups or interviews, could have provided deeper insights into which elements of the game contributed most to their satisfaction and enjoyment, and how to improve the intervention.

There are three points to consider for future research: one technical and two practical areas. The technical point is about using dot plot analysis before conducting the inferential statistics. This analysis will help to identify potential outliers in student engagement scores. Examining these outliers would help to differentiate between actual differences in engagement scores and measurement artifacts. The two practical points concern the implementation of Bingo in laboratories across microbiology and beyond. First, the Bingo Lab can be upgraded to assess higher-order thinking skills by connecting relevant tests to mimic clinical reasoning and improve diagnostic abilities, particularly for senior students and residents. The ‘unknown’ spots can be grouped to shed light on one microorganism. Second, we acknowledged that Bingo Labs are not for all, because students perceived GBL differently. While many students enjoyed the experience, some feel more secure with traditional teaching and learning approaches, such as lectures or tutorials. There’s a need to assess students’ readiness for GBL using psychometric tools and personality traits. Then assign GBL only to students who would like and benefit the most from these non-traditional instructional designs.

Conclusion

Bingo labs in microbiology have been found effective in engaging students and helping them identify their learning gaps in a non-threatening environment. Enriching students’ learning experience in a competitive yet friendly environment can be achieved through effective time management and proactive planning, using the Bingo Lab approach across different courses and contexts, in microbiology and beyond.
