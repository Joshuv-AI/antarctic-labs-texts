Metabolome and transcriptome profiling reveals distinct regulatory networks underlying high-protein and high-oil traits in soybean cultivars Wandou 28 and Zhongdou 63
Authors: Qingnan Hao, Chao Li, Yuanxiao Yang, Shuilian Chen, Hongli Yang, Zhonglu Yang, Zhihui Shan, Haifeng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSoybean is a vital global source of protein and oil. Breeding is constrained by a persistent trade-off between seed protein content, oil content, and yield. Wandou 28 demonstrated a “defense-oriented” strategy, with sustained investment in specialized metabolism and stress responses across leaves, stems, and pods, particularly through the 9S-lipoxygenase-mediated oxylipin pathway, diverting linoleic acid from seed oil synthesis. Background

Soybean (Glycine max (L.) Merr.) is a crucial global dual-purpose crop, serving as a key source of high-quality plant protein and edible oil, and is thus indispensable for ensuring global food security and sustainable agricultural development. Driven by population growth, shifting consumption patterns, and increasing demand for plant-based foods, breeding soybean varieties with synergistically enhanced protein and oil content, coupled with high yield potential, has become a paramount objective in soybean improvement [1]. However, breeding efforts are consistently confronted by a persistent “trade-off” dilemma: significant negative correlations are commonly observed between protein content and oil content, as well as between protein content and yield [2, 3]. This complex trait trade-off indicates the existence of profound physiological constraints and competing molecular regulatory mechanisms in the allocation of finite resources, such as photoassimilates and nitrogen, within the soybean plant. This conflict presents a major bottleneck for the synergistic improvement of both yield and quality [4].

Biologically, the synthesis and accumulation of seed storage proteins and oils represent a spatiotemporally precise and intricate process. It relies not only on the biosynthetic capacity of the seed (the sink) but also on the photosynthetic productivity of source organs (leaves), the transport efficiency of conductive tissues (such as stems), and the coordinated allocation strategy for carbon and nitrogen during reproductive growth [5]. At the molecular level, this process is governed by a multi‑layered network of gene expression and metabolic pathways. The classical “growth–defense trade‑off” theory posits that a plant’s investment in defensive secondary metabolism—including the synthesis of phenylpropanoids and flavonoids—often occurs at the expense of growth and reproductive processes, such as the accumulation of storage reserves [6]. Growing evidence, however, suggests that this trade‑off is not a simple binary opposition, but is dynamically coordinated through a complex regulatory network involving plant hormone signaling (e.g., auxin, jasmonate, abscisic acid). This network integrates environmental cues with endogenous developmental programs to ultimately direct resource partitioning [7, 8]. Therefore, understanding the basis of high‑protein or high‑oil/high‑yield phenotypes requires moving beyond isolated studies of single organs or individual genes. A holistic, system‑level approach is needed to dissect the overarching resource‑allocation strategies and molecular regulatory foundations from the perspective of “source–flow–sink” synergy.

Advances in modern systems biology, particularly the maturation and integrated application of high‑throughput omics technologies, have provided unprecedented opportunities to dissect the molecular mechanisms of complex traits at a systems level [9]. Transcriptomics reveals genome‑wide dynamic changes in gene expression, pinpointing key regulatory genes and signaling pathways associated with trait formation [10]. Metabolomics directly reflects the end products of cellular biochemical processes, offering the most direct chemical evidence for phenotypic differences and enabling the inference of active metabolic routes [11]. By integrating transcriptome and metabolome data across multiple tissues—such as leaves, stems, and pods—and key developmental stages (e.g., flowering and seed filling), spatiotemporal dynamic maps can be constructed. This approach enables the systematic delineation of the complete regulatory chain from gene expression to final phenotype, facilitating the identification of core regulatory modules and metabolic hubs that drive trait differentiation [12]. Such multi‑omics integration strategies have achieved notable success in elucidating complex traits like nutrient‑use efficiency and stress resistance in crops such as maize and rice [13, 14]. However, systematic research aimed at the synergistic improvement of yield and quality in soybean remains relatively limited.

Currently, numerous linkage analyses and genome‑wide association studies (GWAS) have mapped many quantitative trait loci (QTLs) and candidate genes associated with soybean protein and oil content [15, 16]. However, most of these studies focus primarily on the seed itself or a single developmental stage, often lacking a systematic interpretation of the synergistic contributions of source and transport organs during critical growth periods. It remains unclear how high‑protein or high‑oil traits are established through the coordination—or competition—among different plant tissues across development, and what overarching transcriptional regulatory networks and metabolic reprogramming features underlie these processes. In particular, there is a notable lack of integrative research that can directly reveal the fundamental strategic differences, at the whole‑plant “source–flow–sink” system level, between these two trait paradigms: high‑protein versus high‑oil/high‑yield.

To address this gap, we employed a dynamic multi‑omics integration approach. Two representative elite soybean cultivars were selected for systematic profiling of transcriptomes and broad‑target metabolomes across three tissue types—leaf (source), stem (flow), and pod (sink)—at two critical developmental stages: flowering (R2, marking active source‑organ function) and seed filling (R5–R6, representing peak sink demand). This study aimed to: (1) map the spatiotemporal transcriptomic and metabolomic landscapes of high‑protein versus high‑oil/high‑yield genotypes during decisive developmental windows; (2) identify consistently divergent core biological pathways, metabolite accumulation patterns, and their tissue‑specific signatures through comparative analysis; (3) dissect key metabolic and regulatory nodes governing protein‑oil partitioning, with emphasis on systemic differences in nitrogen metabolism, lipid metabolism, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and hormone signaling; (4) utilize weighted gene co‑expression network analysis (WGCNA) to uncover trait‑associated gene modules and screen for potential master regulators; and (5) ultimately, construct systems‑level molecular network models elucidating the formation—and inherent trade‑offs—of high‑protein and high‑oil/high‑yield traits. Beyond advancing the theoretical understanding of yield‑quality determination in soybean, this work provides a critical knowledge base and genetic resources for molecular design breeding, aimed at overcoming conventional trait compromises and developing novel cultivars with synergistically superior quality and yield.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

The test materials used in this study were two major soybean varieties promoted in southern China: Zhongdou 63 and Wandou 28. Zhongdou 63, a high-oil and high-yield cultivar, was provided by the Oil Crops Research Institute of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. Wandou 28, a high-protein cultivar, was supplied by the Crop Research Institute of the Anhui Academy of Agricultural Sciences.

Plants were grown at the Yangluo Experimental Base of the Oil Crops Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, in June 2020. The experimental soil was clay with medium fertility and flat terrain, following a previous crop of rapeseed. Before sowing, a compound fertilizer (N-P-K) was applied at a rate of 375 kg/ha. Other field management practices, including irrigation and pest control, followed local conventional farming standards.

A randomized complete block design was adopted with three independent biological replicates. Seeds were sown using standard agronomic practices. The plants reached physiological maturity in late August and were subsequently harvested manually. Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses were performed on samples collected from the Yangluo location.

Field data measurement methods and statistical analysis

At the maturity stage of soybeans, 10 representative plants were selected from each plot for indoor assessment and quality trait analysis. Agronomic traits investigated included plant height, number of nodes on the main stem, number of effective branches on the main stem, number of effective pods per plant, grain weight per plant, and 100-grain weight. The mean values of these traits were calculated and recorded as the agronomic trait values for the plot. Protein and fat contents were measured using a Zeltex ZX50 handheld grain analyzer (USA), which was calibrated daily using internal standards provided by the manufacturer. Each sample was measured in triplicate, and the mean value was used for analysis. The coefficient of variation between replicate measurements was consistently below 2%. During harvest, yield was measured from the middle three rows of each plot. The data were analyzed using SPSS 17.0 software, and the graphs were plotted using Microsoft Excel 2010. Variance components across replicates and sites were partitioned using a linear mixed model in SPSS to quantify genotype, environment, and genotype × environment interaction effects.

Experimental design and sample collection

Leaf (source), stem(translocation pathway), and pod(sink, including seeds during pod-filling) tissues were collected at two critical developmental stages: flowering stage (R2, source activity) and pod-filling stage (R5-R6, sink strength). Whole pods were collected to capture both maternal (pod wall) and filial (seed) contributions to source-sink interactions. We acknowledge that this approach may dilute seed-specific transcriptional signals, which should be addressed in future studies by dissecting seed and pod wall tissues separately. For each tissue and time point, samples were collected from six plants per biological replicate and pooled to reduce biological noise and focus on genotype-level differences. This pooling strategy, however, masks intra-plot variability, a limitation noted in the Discussion. All samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C until analysis.

Metabolite extraction and UPLC-MS analysis

Weigh 80 mg of the sample, add 20 μL of internal standard (L-2-chlorophenylalanine, 0.3 mg/mL; prepared in methanol) and 1 mL of methanol–water (V:V = 7:3). Place two small steel beads in the mixture, pre-cool at -20 °C for 2 min, and then grind in a grinder (60 Hz, 2 min). Perform ultrasonic extraction in an ice-water bath for 30 min, followed by overnight standing at -20 °C. Centrifuge for 10 min (13,000 rpm, 4 °C), draw 150 μL of the supernatant with a syringe, filter it through a 0.22 μm organic phase pinhole filter, and transfer it to an LC vial. Store at -80 °C until LC–MS analysis. The total quality control sample (QC-ALL) is prepared by mixing equal volumes of extraction solutions from all samples. For each sample type, the respective QC is prepared by mixing equal volumes of extraction solutions from all samples of that type. The volume of QC is the same as that of the samples. All extraction reagents are pre-cooled at -20 °C before use.

Ultra-high-resolution mass spectrometry was performed on an Impact II UHR-QqTOF instrument (Bruker Daltonic GmbH), which was coupled to an UltiMate 3000RS UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher) via an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. The separation was achieved using a Waters ACQUITY BEH C18 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 1.7 μm particle size).

The analysis employed a binary mobile phase system at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min, consisting of solvent A (water with 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid). Following sample injection (5 μL), a 23-min gradient elution was performed as follows: 5% B (0–1 min), 5–40% B (1–15 min), 40–70% B (15–18 min), and 70–5% B (18–23 min). The separation was carried out with the column maintained at 40 °C and the autosampler temperature set to 4 °C.

Each sample was analyzed in two technical replicates using both positive and negative ionization modes. The nebulizer pressure was maintained at 2 bar, with nitrogen serving as the drying gas at a flow rate of 10 L/min and a temperature of 220 °C. The capillary voltages were set at 4,000 V for positive mode and 3,000 V for negative mode, with data acquisition covering a mass range of 50–1,300 m/z.

Data acquisition was performed using HyStar v3.2 (Bruker Daltonic). The generated data underwent noise subtraction, retention time alignment, and peak picking using Expressionist for MS 10.5 (Genedata, Basel, Switzerland). Data processing followed four sequential workflows: first, chemical noise reduction was applied; second, retention time alignment was conducted; third, m/z features were identified using the summed-peak-detection algorithm implemented in Expressionist for MS software; fourth, only peaks detected in at least 10% of mass spectra were subjected to isotope (13C) clustering, with single-member clusters (singletons) excluded from subsequent analysis.

For quality control, pooled QC samples were injected periodically throughout the run. The coefficient of variation (CV) for each metabolite across QC injections was calculated, and metabolites with CV > 30% were excluded from downstream analysis. Principal component analysis of QC samples confirmed minimal signal drift and batch effects, as QC samples clustered tightly together.

Differential metabolite selection combined VIP scores from OPLS-DA (VIP > 1) and p-values from t-tests, with multiple testing correction applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR < 0.05).

Screening of differential metabolites between groups was performed using a combination of multidimensional and single-dimensional analytical approaches. In the OPLS-DA analysis, the Variable Important in Projection (VIP) value was employed to select metabolites with VIP > 1. Model validity was assessed using 200 permutation tests, and Q2 values were calculated to evaluate predictive performance and overfitting. This helped identify biologically significant differential metabolites, which were subsequently verified for statistical significance using t-tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing (FDR < 0.05). Metabolite identification confidence was determined based on MS/MS spectral matching against in-house and public databases. Annotation confidence levels were assigned according to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI): Level 1 (validated by reference standards), Level 2 (putatively annotated based on spectral similarity), and Level 3 (putatively characterized compound classes).

Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the KEGG IDs of differential metabolites to obtain metabolic pathway enrichment results. The hypergeometric test was applied to identify significantly enriched pathway entries among the significantly differentially expressed metabolites compared to the entire background. The p-value in the metabolic pathway indicates the significance of the enrichment. A red line represents a p-value of 0.01, and a blue line represents a p-value of 0.05. When the top of a bar exceeds the blue line, the signaling pathway it represents is considered significant. Detailed annotation information, including spectral match scores, is provided in Table S1.

RNA extraction, library construction, and transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using the mirVana miRNA Isolation Kit (Ambion) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA integrity was evaluated using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The samples with RNA Integrity Number (RIN) ≥ 7 were subjected to the subsequent analysis. The libraries were constructed using TruSeq Stranded mRNA LTSample Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Then these libraries were sequenced on the Illumina sequencing platform (HiSeqTM 2500 or Illumina HiSeq X Ten) and 125 bp/150 bp paired-end reads were generated.

The transcriptome sequencing and analysis were conducted by OE biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Raw data (raw reads) were processed using Trimmomatic [17]. The reads containing ploy-N and the low quality reads were removed to obtain the clean reads. Then the clean reads were mapped to reference genome using hisat2 [18].

FPKM [19] value of each gene was calculated using cufflinks [20], and the read counts of each gene were obtained by htseq-count [21]. FPKM values were used only for PCA visualization and were not employed for differential expression analysis. DEGs were identified using the DESeq2 R package [22]. Genes with an absolute log2 fold change ≥ 1 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg correction) were considered significantly differentially expressed. Hierarchical cluster analysis of DEGs was performed to explore genes expression pattern. GO enrichment and KEGG [23] pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs were respectively performed using R based on the hypergeometric distribution. To account for directionality of expression changes, separate enrichment analyses were performed for up- and down-regulated genes.

If it was transcript-level quantification, FPKM and read counts value of each transcript (protein_coding) was calculated using bowtie2 [24] and eXpress [25]. DEGs were identified using the DESeq functions estimateSizeFactors and nbinomTest. P value < 0.05 and foldChange > 2 or foldChange < 0.5 was set as the threshold for significantly differential expression. Hierarchical cluster analysis of DEGs was performed to explore transcripts expression pattern. GO enrichment and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs were respectively performed using R based on the hypergeometric distribution.

Integrated multi-omics analysis

WGCNA was performed using the WGCNA package in R. Prior to network construction, genes with low expression (FPKM < 1 in more than 80% of samples) were filtered out, resulting in 25,200 genes for analysis. A soft-thresholding power of 14 was selected based on the scale-free topology fit index (R2 > 0.85). Module stability was assessed using module preservation statistics with 100 permutations. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using clusterProfiler. Integration of transcriptome and metabolome data was achieved through nine-quadrant analysis and Pearson correlation analysis. Regulatory networks were visualized using Cytoscape.

Results

Two elite cultivars exhibit complementary trait advantages

Comprehensive phenotyping verified that the two elite soybean cultivars, Wandou 28 and Zhongdou 63, exhibit distinct and complementary agronomic advantages (Table 1). Wandou 28 was distinguished by its high‑protein trait, with seed protein content reaching approximately 49.1% at Yangluo and 48.5% at Jingmen—significantly higher than the ~ 45.7% and 45.6% measured in Zhongdou 63. In contrast, Zhongdou 63 displayed a high‑oil and high‑yield phenotype. Its oil content remained consistently around 22.5–22.6% across both locations (with text records indicating it could reach 23.5%), markedly surpassing the ~ 21.0–21.4% observed in Wandou 28. Moreover, Zhongdou 63 demonstrated strong yield performance, achieving 3554.85 kg·ha-1 in Yangluo and 3406.12 kg·ha-1 in Jingmen, substantially exceeding the respective yields of 1991.92 kg·ha-1 and 2843.72 kg·ha-1 recorded for Wandou 28. Variance component analysis indicated that genotype explained the largest proportion of variance for protein (68%) and oil content (71%), while location and genotype × location interaction effects were not significant (p > 0.05) (Table S2).

Summary of transcriptome analysis

To capture the transcriptional profiles associated with these distinct traits, we conducted RNA‑seq analysis on leaves, stems, and pods collected at both flowering and pod‑filling stages. A total of 36 libraries were sequenced, corresponding to two cultivars, two developmental stages, three tissues, and three biological replicates each. Samples were labeled as follows: L (leaf), P (pod), S (stem); W (Wandou 28) and Z (Zhongdou 63); the suffix “R” indicates the pod‑filling stage, while its absence denotes the flowering stage. After quality control, each library yielded an average of 46.65 million clean reads, with Q30 scores exceeding 93.36% and GC content around 44%, confirming high data quality suitable for further analysis (Table S3). Clean reads were uniquely mapped to the soybean reference genome (Williams 82.a4.v1) with an average mapping efficiency of 96.41% (Table S4).

Principal component analysis (PCA) of gene expression (FPKM values) showed clear separation of samples mainly by tissue type along PC1 (71.25% of variance) and by developmental stage along PC2 (7.74% of variance), highlighting the dominant role of spatiotemporal factors in shaping the transcriptome (Fig. 1A). Notably, during the pod‑filling stage—a critical period for seed‑storage reserve accumulation—pod samples from Wandou 28 and Zhongdou 63 formed distinct, separate clusters, indicating pronounced genotype‑specific transcriptional reprogramming in this primary sink organ. To quantify the relative contributions of these factors, variance partitioning analysis using a linear mixed model was performed. Tissue type explained the largest proportion of variance (68.2%), followed by developmental stage (11.8%) and genotype (7.6%), with the remaining variance attributable to interactions and residuals (Table S5).

To identify genes potentially involved in shaping the contrasting phenotypes, pairwise comparisons were conducted for each tissue at both developmental stages. Using a stringent threshold (|log2FoldChange|> 1, FDR < 0.05), a total of 9,547 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified across all comparisons (Fig. 1B). The most pronounced transcriptional divergence was observed in stems at the filling stage (comparison ZRS_1‑vs‑WRS_1), which yielded 3,715 DEGs (1,437 up‑regulated, 2,278 down‑regulated). This result underscores the intense transcriptional activity and marked divergence in this supportive organ during active seed filling. Stage‑specificity was evident, as illustrated by the dramatic increase in stem DEGs from 276 at the early pod‑setting stage to 3,715 at the filling stage. In the comparisons with the highest DEG counts—encompassing filling‑stage stems, filling‑stage leaves, and early pod‑setting leaves—the number of down‑regulated genes consistently exceeded that of up‑regulated genes. This pattern suggests a potential prevalence of gene‑expression suppression in Wandou 28 within these key tissues. Venn diagram analysis further distinguished shared from unique DEGs across tissues and stages, highlighting both common and tissue‑specific regulatory mechanisms (Fig. 1C).

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis of DEGs

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of DEGs revealed distinct functional profiles underlying the contrasting phenotypes. To account for directionality of expression changes, separate enrichment analyses were performed for up- and down-regulated genes (Table S6). In the high‑protein cultivar Wandou 28, DEGs were significantly enriched for biological processes related to defense responses—such as the plant‑type hypersensitive response and response to chitin—as well as for signal transduction and transmembrane transport activities involving sulfate and amino acids (Fig. 2). In contrast, DEGs in the high‑oil, high‑yield cultivar Zhongdou 63 were predominantly linked to anabolic processes essential for growth and storage, including lipid storage, seed oil‑body biogenesis, cell‑wall organization, and DNA replication. This functional divergence extended to molecular functions: Wandou 28 showed enrichment for ATP‑binding and kinase activities, whereas Zhongdou 63 was enriched for nutrient‑reservoir activity and fatty‑acid binding. Spatially, the most pronounced transcriptional differences were observed in stems at the pod-filling stage. In Zhongdou 63, GO terms associated with cell-wall biogenesis and xyloglucan metabolism were notably up-regulated, highlighting the importance of structural remodeling and carbon partitioning in supporting its higher yield. Together, these results illustrate a fundamental transcriptional trade-off: the high-protein phenotype coincides with substantial investment in defense and signaling, while the high-oil, high-yield phenotype is propelled by coordinated up-regulation of biosynthetic and storage pathways.

Top 30 significantly enriched GO terms in Wandou 28 and Zhongdou 63

KEGG pathway enrichment further clarified the distinct metabolic and regulatory strategies of the two cultivars. In Wandou 28, up‑regulated DEGs were significantly enriched in pathways involved in environmental interaction and stress adaptation—such as plant hormone signal transduction, MAPK signaling, and ABC transporters—as well as in primary metabolic processes including nitrogen metabolism and carbon fixation, reflecting a transcriptome tuned to perceive and respond to external cues (Fig. 3). Conversely, in Zhongdou 63, down‑regulated DEGs frequently pointed to the suppression of resource‑intensive or competing pathways, such as phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, flavonoid biosynthesis, and drug metabolism, potentially redirecting metabolic flux toward storage and growth. Critically, Zhongdou 63 exhibited strong and coordinated up-regulation of core biosynthetic and growth-related pathways. This was especially evident in pod-filling-stage stems (ZRS_1-vs-WRS_1), where up-regulated genes were enriched in starch and sucrose metabolism, carotenoid biosynthesis, and brassinosteroid biosynthesis. Similar patterns in other tissues involved glycerolipid metabolism and fatty-acid degradation. These findings highlight a robust anabolic network that ensures carbon skeleton supply, energy provision, and growth promotion, forming the basis for its synergistic high-oil and high-yield traits.

Overall, the integrated GO and KEGG profiles consistently depict Wandou 28 as allocating transcriptional resources toward defense and signaling, while Zhongdou 63 optimizes its gene expression for efficient carbon allocation into storage compounds and structural growth.

Characterization of differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) in Zhongdou 63 and Wandou 28

To uncover the biochemical basis of the divergent phenotypes, we performed non-targeted metabolomic profiling of leaves, stems, and pods from both cultivars at the two developmental stages. A total of 4,733 DAMs were identified (FDR < 0.05) (Fig. 4), whose spatiotemporal patterns revealed pronounced differences in metabolic investment between the two genotypes. Quality control metrics confirmed the reliability of the metabolomic data. Metabolites with high variability (CV > 30%) across QC injections were removed prior to analysis. PCA of QC samples showed tight clustering, indicating excellent instrument stability and minimal batch effects throughout the run. Metabolite annotation confidence levels and spectral matching scores are provided in Table S1.

Number of differentially accumulated metabolites

In source leaves, the metabolic profiles of the two cultivars diverged markedly (Fig. 5, Tables S7–S8). At flowering, the high‑protein Wandou 28 displayed a defense‑oriented profile, characterized by substantial accumulation of structurally complex specialized metabolites. These included various flavonoid glycosides and isoflavonoid phytoalexins, such as (‑)‑Glyceollin II and 6α-Hydroxyphaseollin, indicating a constitutively active defense state. This was accompanied by elevated levels of stress‑related hormones (abscisic acid, methyl jasmonate) and abundant free amino acids and dipeptides, together pointing to a resource‑intensive defense strategy that likely restricts photoassimilate allocation to developing seeds. In contrast, the high-oil, high-yield Zhongdou 63 exhibited a pattern consistent with efficient resource management. During pod‑filling, its leaves accumulated key intermediates of energy metabolism (e.g., dihydroxyacetone phosphate, phosphoglycolic acid) and several dipeptides (leucyl-leucine, valyl-leucine), suggestive of an effective nitrogen-transport system. Notably, the accumulation of triglyceride TG(16:0/18:0/20:4) indicated strong carbon fixation and storage capacity for lipid precursors. A comparatively simpler profile of specialized metabolites further reflected a resource-conserving strategy in this cultivar.

Top 20 KEGG enrichment of differentially accumulated metabolites

Stem metabolism further highlighted these strategic differences. In Wandou 28, stems consistently accumulated high levels of triterpenoid saponins, complex flavonoids, and eicosanoids across both stages. This persistent, metabolically costly investment effectively turned the stem into a significant metabolic sink, which may have compromised the efficient transport of photoassimilates to developing seeds. Zhongdou 63 stems presented a contrasting picture: accumulation of dipeptides and key metabolic intermediates like loganin, combined with a lower burden of specialized metabolism, collectively established an efficient resource‑transport system that structurally supports higher yield.

Pod metabolism most directly reflected the formation of the final quality traits. In Wandou 28, pod tissues sustained extensive biosynthesis of specialized metabolites throughout development, including diverse saponins, triterpenoids, and complex glycosides. These pathways consumed substantial amounts of key precursors such as acetyl‑CoA and sugar nucleotides, while eicosanoid accumulation further diverted precursors away from lipid synthesis. This created strong competition with the synthesis of storage compounds. Conversely, pods of Zhongdou 63 exhibited classic storage‑organ characteristics, with significant accumulation of sugars (D‑glucose, D‑maltose), TCA‑cycle intermediates (isocitric acid, oxoglutaric acid), and dipeptides, thereby supplying ample substrates for the synthesis of storage proteins and oils. Its restrained investment in specialized metabolism ensured that a greater proportion of resources was directed toward these storage‑substance pathways.

Nitrogen metabolism in leaves: differential strategies of assimilation and partitioning

Integrated analysis of transcriptomic and metabolomic data from leaves at the flowering stage revealed significant co‑enrichment in pathways central to nitrogen assimilation and amino acid metabolism, specifically in Nitrogen metabolism and Alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism. This convergence points to a fundamental divergence in how the high‑protein cultivar Wandou 28 and the high‑oil, high‑yield cultivar Zhongdou 63 regulate nitrogen metabolism at the source level.

The transcriptional profile in Zhongdou 63 leaves was marked by the up‑regulation of genes that facilitate efficient primary nitrogen acquisition and assimilation (Fig. 6). Key enzymes involved in early pathway steps-such as the high‑affinity nitrate transporter 2.5 (Glyma.08G284000), nitrate reductase 2 (Glyma.13G084000), and ferredoxin–nitrite reductase (Glyma.07G212800)-were significantly up‑regulated, indicating enhanced capacity for nitrate uptake and reduction. Concurrent up‑regulation of alpha carbonic anhydrase 7 (Glyma.10G059400) suggests tighter coordination with photorespiration and carbon metabolism. Notably, glutamate decarboxylase 1 (Glyma.08G091300, Glyma.08G091500) was strongly induced, potentially modulating the carbon/nitrogen balance via the GABA shunt under conditions of high nitrogen influx. In contrast, Wandou 28 exhibited higher expression of genes linked to nitrogen reassimilation, specific amino‑acid synthesis, and alternative nitrogen fluxes. These included glutamate synthase (Glyma.06G127400), glutamate dehydrogenase B (Glyma.16G153400), alanine–glyoxylate aminotransferase 2 (Glyma.01G129400), and, crucially, asparagine synthetase 2 (Glyma.11G170300).

Profiles of DEGs in the nitrogen metabolism and alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism pathway of flowering stage leaves

Metabolomic data provided decisive insights. Consistent with the high expression of asparagine synthetase 2, metabolites including L-Asparagine, L-Aspartic acid, and L-Glutamate were significantly more abundant in Wandou 28 leaves. This pattern indicates a substantial investment in synthesizing and accumulating nitrogen-rich amino acids—particularly asparagine, a major nitrogen transport and storage compound in soybean—within source leaves during flowering.

Together, these findings support a model in which Zhongdou 63 prioritizes high-capacity nitrogen uptake and rapid export from leaves, whereas Wandou 28 retains nitrogen in source tissues as amino acids, potentially limiting immediate flux to sinks.

Linoleic acid metabolism

Integrated multi‑omics analysis revealed systemic differences in linoleic acid metabolism, a pathway significantly enriched across multiple developmental contexts—including filling‑stage leaves, pods at both early and filling stages, and flowering‑stage stems—indicating its potential role in the developmental regulatory networks of both cultivars (Fig. 7).

Profiles of DEGs in the linoleic acid metabolism pathways

The two cultivars exhibited distinct expression patterns at the initial step of lipoxygenase catalysis. In filling‑stage leaves, Wandou 28 systematically expressed higher levels of the 9S‑lipoxygenase family (involving 13 gene loci), with Glyma.07G006900 and Glyma.07G034900 showing the most pronounced differences. This pattern persisted in pod tissues, though with reduced intensity. In early pod‑setting pods, Glyma.08G189600 and Glyma.04G105500 maintained higher expression in Wandou 28; in filling‑stage pods, Glyma.07G006900 and Glyma.13G347800 continued to show elevated expression. Flowering‑stage stems displayed a distinct regulatory pattern, where Wandou 28 specifically expressed higher levels of lipoxygenase genes such as Glyma.13G347500 and Glyma.13G347600, suggesting isoform‑specific regulation across tissues.

Metabolomic profiling further uncovered complex spatiotemporal dynamics. In filling‑stage leaves, Wandou 28 specifically accumulated 9,12,13‑TriHOME along with phospholipids PC(18:4/15:0) and PC(18:3/22:4), aligning with its high 9S‑lipoxygenase expression and indicating active flux through the 9‑LOX pathway. Early pod‑setting pods presented an opposite trend: Zhongdou 63 specifically accumulated various oxylipins, including 12,13‑DiHOME, 13S‑HODE, and 9,12,13‑TriHOME, and showed higher abundance of PC(16:0/14:1). In contrast, Wandou 28 only accumulated 9S‑HODE, suggesting differing branch preferences in linoleic acid metabolism during early pod development. By the filling stage, pod‑tissue characteristics shifted again: Wandou 28 accumulated specific oxylipins such as 12(13)‑EpOME, 8‑HpODE, and 9,12,13‑TriHOME, along with PC(16:0/14:1). In flowering‑stage stems, Wandou 28 specifically accumulated 8(R)‑hydroperoxylinoleic acid.

Transcriptomic and metabolomic signatures of phenylpropanoid biosynthesis in early pods

Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic profiling of pods at the early pod‑setting stage revealed that phenylpropanoid biosynthesis constitutes a central pathway distinguishing the two cultivars, characterized by pronounced differences in both gene expression and metabolite accumulation (Fig. 8).

Profiles of DEGs in the linoleic phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathways in early pods

At the transcriptional level, genotype‑specific patterns were evident. Zhongdou 63 exhibited broader and more coordinated transcriptional activity. Several peroxidase (POD)‑encoding genes were significantly up‑regulated, including Peroxidase 3 (Glyma.06G302600), Peroxidase 25 (Glyma.06G221700), and Peroxidase 53 (Glyma.09G109800). These enzymes participate in lignin polymerization and the oxidation of phenylpropanoid derivatives. Concurrently, a key upstream gene, 4‑coumarate–CoA ligase 1 (Glyma.17G064400), which catalyzes the conversion of hydroxycinnamic acids to their CoA esters, was also up‑regulated. Furthermore, a cannabidiolic acid synthase‑like 1 gene (Glyma.05G122000) showed exceptionally high expression in Zhongdou 63, suggesting the potential activation of specific phenolic‑compound biosynthesis. In contrast, Wandou 28 showed higher expression of two beta‑glucosidase genes (Glyma.02G225000, Glyma.04G201600), which hydrolyze phenolic glycosides to release bioactive aglycones. Notably, a spermidine hydroxycinnamoyl transferase gene (Glyma.18G113100), involved in conjugating hydroxycinnamic acids with polyamines to form phenolamides, was expressed at significantly higher levels in Wandou 28.

Metabolomic data aligned with these transcriptional profiles, detecting cultivar-specific accumulation of phenylpropanoids. Several derivatives were significantly enriched in Wandou 28 early pods, including 2-Hydroxycinnamic acid, Coumarin, and Methylisoeugenol. This enrichment corresponds to Wandou 28’s transcriptional signature—marked by high expression of beta-glucosidases and spermidine hydroxycinnamoyl transferase—and suggests active biosynthesis and accumulation of specific hydroxycinnamic-acid derivatives and phenolic compounds.

In summary, during early pod development, the regulatory landscapes of phenylpropanoid biosynthesis differed distinctly between cultivars. Pods of Zhongdou 63 showed broad activation of peroxidase-mediated oxidation and polymerization processes, along with specific phenolic synthases. In contrast, pods of Wandou 28 exhibited enhanced transcription for beta-glucosidase-mediated glycoside hydrolysis and phenolamide synthesis, accompanied by significant accumulation of various hydroxycinnamic acids and derivatives. These findings suggest divergent resource-allocation strategies toward phenylpropanoid metabolism in the reproductive organs at this developmental stage.

Hormonal reprogramming in stems: transcriptional evidence for differential signaling

Transcriptomic profiling of stems during seed filling revealed systematic, genotype‑specific reprogramming of plant hormone signaling pathways between Wandou 28 and Zhongdou 63 (Fig. 9, Table S9). In Zhongdou 63, the auxin signaling network was broadly activated, marked by significant up‑regulation of auxin‑responsive genes (e.g., IAA17), transporters (AUX1‑like genes), and auxin‑amido synthetases (GH3.1, GH3.3). This transcriptional pattern is consistent with enhanced polar auxin transport and signaling conducive to growth and transport efficiency, though direct measurements of auxin levels or transport rates would be required for confirmation. This pattern suggests enhanced polar auxin transport and refined signaling conducive to growth and transport efficiency. In contrast, Wandou 28 showed pronounced up‑regulation of jasmonic acid (JA) and abscisic acid (ABA) pathways. High expression of JAR1 and multiple TIFY transcription factors indicated strengthened JA signaling and defense readiness, while elevated expression of SAPK3 and PP2C8 pointed to altered ABA sensitivity. The cultivars also diverged in cytokinin signaling: Zhongdou 63 exhibited higher expression of type‑A response regulators (ORR22, ORR26), whereas Wandou 28 up‑regulated type‑B regulators (ARR8, ARR9).

Profiles of DEGs in the plant hormone signaling pathways in stems during seed filling

Notably, several cell‑cycle‑related genes, including five cyclin‑D3‑1 genes (e.g., Glyma.06G042900), were systematically up‑regulated in Wandou 28 stems, implying sustained cell‑division activity during reproductive growth. Transcriptional regulators also differed: bZIP factors TGA9 and TGA10 were elevated in Zhongdou 63, potentially integrating immunity with development, while multiple xyloglucan endotransglucosylase/hydrolase genes were up‑regulated in Wandou 28, possibly influencing stem mechanical properties and transport capacity.

Together, these hormonal divergences are associated with the contrasting agronomic strategies of the two cultivars. The auxin-dominated transcriptional network in Zhongdou 63 is correlated with its high-yield, high-oil phenotype, while the JA- and ABA-enhanced profile in Wandou 28 is associated with its defense-oriented, high-protein but lower-yield outcome.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis identifies trait-associated modules

WGCNA was conducted to systematically delineate the complex regulatory networks underlying cultivar differentiation at a systems level, identifying clusters of highly correlated genes (modules) linked to specific tissues, developmental stages, and agronomic traits.

Using a dynamic tree‑cut approach, 17 distinct co‑expression modules were identified from 25,200 expressed genes, ranging in size from 81 to 6,979 genes (Fig. 10A). Module‑trait correlation analysis revealed pronounced spatiotemporal expression patterns across these modules (Fig. 10B). Module stability was confirmed using module preservation statistics with 100 permutations.

A Hierarchical clustering tree of genes based on topological overlap, with different colors representing the 17 identified co-expression modules. Module sizes range from 81 to 6,979 genes. B Module–trait correlation heatmap. Displays the correlation between each module’s eigengene and sample traits (cultivar, tissue, stage), with color intensity indicating the strength of the correlation. C Expression profile of the greenyellow module. D Functional enrichment of the cyan module. E Function of the tan module

The greenyellow module emerged as a central driver of the high‑oil, high‑yield phenotype in Zhongdou 63. It was most active in stems during pod‑filling (ZD_YR_S) (Fig. 10C) and showed strong positive correlations with both seed oil content and yield. Functional enrichment analysis revealed its role as a multifunctional integrator, significantly enriched in pathways that ensure energy sufficiency (oxidative phosphorylation, thermogenesis), macromolecular turnover and precision (proteasome, ribosome, spliceosome), and systemic signaling and homeostasis (retrograde endocannabinoid signaling, RNA polymerase). This transcriptional profile is consistent with enhanced metabolic activity in stems, which may contribute to efficient resource transport and allocation in Zhongdou 63.

The cyan module exhibited strong association with pod tissue of Wandou 28 at the early pod-setting stage. Enrichment analysis indicated enrichment in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, taurine and hypotaurine metabolism, and steroid hormone biosynthesis (Fig. 10D). Enrichment in methane metabolism and the bacterial ‘biofilm formation-Vibrio cholerae’ pathway—though unusual—may reflect the repurposing of specific enzyme families for pod cell-wall metabolism or specialized-compound synthesis. Collectively, the cyan module suggests that during early pod development, Wandou 28 prioritizes a metabolic program that combines energetic preparation with enhanced potential for synthesizing specialized, stress-associated compounds.

The tan module (235 genes) was positively correlated with stems of Zhongdou 63 during pod-filling but negatively correlated with stems of Wandou 28. At the trait level, it correlated positively with seed protein content and negatively with seed oil content, capturing the classic agronomic trade-off (Fig. 10B). Functional enrichment analysis revealed enrichment in ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, RNA transport, spliceosome, RNA degradation, and thyroid hormone signaling (Fig. 10E). This module may represent a conserved regulatory program deployed in distinct organs, potentially contributing to opposing resource-allocation strategies.

Discussion

Through integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses across multiple tissues and developmental stages, this study systematically elucidated the regulatory networks underlying the high-protein and high-oil, high-yield traits in soybean, revealing the deep molecular and metabolic basis of the trade-off between them.

A systemic trade-off: molecular and metabolic divergence between defense investment and yield construction

The core finding of this study suggests that the high‑protein cultivar Wandou 28 and the high‑oil, high‑yield cultivar Zhongdou 63 represent two fundamentally distinct systems‑level strategies of resource allocation. This observation aligns with the classical “growth–defense trade‑off” theory in plant resource economics [26].

Wandou 28 displays a pronounced “defense‑oriented” profile. At the critical flowering stage, its source leaves accumulate substantial levels of flavonoid and isoflavonoid phytoalexins, together with stress‑related hormones such as ABA and JA. This metabolic signature resembles the reprogramming observed when plants shift resources from growth to defense under biotic or abiotic stress, as documented in earlier studies [27, 28]. In addition, stems and pods in Wandou 28 function as active synthesis and storage sites for diverse specialized metabolites, including triterpenoid saponins and phenylpropanoid derivatives [29]. Such a high‑cost investment in chemical defense consumes considerable amounts of carbon skeletons (e.g., acetyl‑CoA), nitrogen, and energy. Consequently, the vascular tissues and reproductive organs themselves become substantial metabolic “sinks” creating strong competition with the synthesis of storage compounds—namely protein and oil [30, 31]. This offers a potential molecular‑level explanation for the commonly observed negative correlation between high seed protein content and yield: the plant channels a greater proportion of its resources into specialized metabolic pathways linked to biotic/abiotic stress responses, rather than into reproductive growth and yield‑related components [7].

In contrast, Zhongdou 63 exemplifies a “yield‑construction” strategy. Both its transcriptomic and metabolomic profiles converge toward efficient central carbon and nitrogen metabolism, robust biosynthetic capacity, and effective resource translocation. In source organs, leaves at the filling stage are enriched with intermediates of energy metabolism and triglyceride precursors, reflecting strong carbon fixation and storage capacity—consistent with reported patterns of efficient photosynthetic export in high‑yielding varieties [32]. In stems, vigorous cell‑wall biosynthesis (e.g., xyloglucan metabolism), active starch and sucrose metabolism, and prominent signaling of growth‑promoting hormones (auxin, brassinosteroids) collectively establish an efficient transport “pipeline” and structural support [33, 34]. Within sink organs (pods), the accumulation of sugars, TCA‑cycle intermediates, and dipeptides supplies ample substrates for the synthesis of storage compounds [35]. Crucially, Zhongdou 63 systemically down-regulates resource-intensive specialized metabolic pathways such as phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis, thereby redirecting metabolic flux predominantly toward growth and storage. This “lean and efficient” metabolic mode appears central to achieving synergistic gains in both oil content and yield-paralleling the observed suppression of “luxury metabolism” documented in certain other high-performing crop varieties.

Reprogramming of nitrogen metabolism: allocation, not total assimilation, is key to high protein

Analysis of nitrogen metabolism challenges the simplistic assumption that high seed protein content necessarily reflects superior total nitrogen assimilation capacity. Notably, in leaves at the flowering stage, the high‑oil, high‑yield cultivar Zhongdou 63 actually up‑regulates genes encoding several high‑affinity nitrate transporters and primary assimilation enzymes (nitrate reductase, nitrite reductase), indicating a strong capacity for nitrogen acquisition and assimilation at the source [36]. However, metabolomic data show that Zhongdou 63 does not accumulate substantial amino acid pools in leaves. This suggests that assimilated nitrogen is rapidly converted into transport forms and efficiently exported to reproductive sinks [37]. Such high nitrogen flux at the source likely contributes to its high‑yield potential [38].

In contrast, the high‑protein cultivar Wandou 28 accumulates significant levels of amino acids—particularly asparagine and glutamate—in its leaves. This pattern is associated with notably high expression of asparagine synthetase, a key enzyme for synthesizing this major nitrogen transport and storage compound [39]. These findings suggest that Wandou 28 employs a “storage–remobilization” strategy, or exhibits stronger nitrogen retention capacity at the source [40]. While this creates a substantial nitrogen reserve that can support later seed protein accumulation, it may also reduce the efficiency of photosynthetic export from flowering to early seed filling, potentially limiting sink capacity establishment and final yield [41].

Therefore, achieving high protein content may depend not merely on nitrogen assimilation capacity, but more critically on the spatiotemporal balance of nitrogen allocation between source and sink organs—a balance that often comes at the expense of immediate photosynthetic export efficiency and yield potential [42, 43]. This insight emphasizes that future breeding for high‑protein soybean should prioritize optimizing nitrogen‑partitioning efficiency, rather than focusing solely on enhancing total nitrogen uptake [44].

Hormone and signaling networks: the core of coordinating growth-defense balance and source-sink strength

Systematic differences in plant hormone signaling pathways represent a further core regulatory layer underlying the phenotypic divergence between the two cultivars. During the filling stage, stems—a critical hub connecting source and sink—displayed distinct hormone profiles. In Zhongdou 63, the auxin signaling network was comprehensively activated, including up‑regulation of response factors, transporters, and GH3 amido synthetases. This pattern aligns with physiological demands for promoting cell elongation, vascular development, and polar auxin transport, all supporting high yield [45]. Concurrent up‑regulation of the brassinosteroid biosynthesis pathway further reinforced its growth‑promoting signals [46].

In contrast, Wandou 28 exhibited enhanced jasmonic acid and abscisic acid signaling. High expression of JAR1 promotes the synthesis of active jasmonoyl‑isoleucine, while multiple TIFY transcription factors were up‑regulated [47]. Specific expression patterns of PP2Cs and SnRK2 kinases within the ABA signaling pathway collectively shaped a state of heightened defense readiness and stress sensitivity [48]. Additionally, elevated expression of multiple Cyclin D3 genes in Wandou 28 stems suggests that its vegetative tissues maintain relatively high cell‑division activity during the reproductive phase, which may delay the full transition to reproduction and affect the preferential allocation of assimilates to seeds [49].

These contrasting hormone network configurations solidify the distinct growth–defense investment strategies of the two cultivars at the systemic signaling level, underscoring the central role of hormones in integrating environmental cues with developmental programs to determine ultimate resource allocation [50].

Linoleic acid metabolism in leaves: divergent oxylipin pathways are associated with source-sink priorities during seed filling

The spatiotemporal regulation of linoleic acid metabolism, particularly through the 9S‑lipoxygenase (9S‑LOX) pathway, serves as a critical node illustrating the divergent resource‑allocation strategies of the two cultivars. Our integrated multi‑omics analysis reveals that the high‑protein cultivar Wandou 28 systemically and persistently up‑regulates the 9S‑LOX‑mediated oxylipin biosynthesis pathway in source leaves during seed filling. This coordinated activation, involving multiple LOX gene family members (e.g., Glyma.07G006900, Glyma.07G034900) and the consequent accumulation of specific oxylipins such as 9,12,13‑TriHOME and related phospholipids (e.g.,PC(18:4/15:0)), is correlated with a physiological state oriented toward enhanced production of signaling molecules and potential stress mediators [51, 52].

Oxylipins, including hydroxy fatty acids like TriHOMEs, are established as key players in plant defense against herbivores and pathogens, in wound signaling, and in modulating developmental processes [53, 54]. Their sustained production in Wandou 28 leaves may indicate a constitutive or environmentally primed level of stress perception. Crucially, this metabolic commitment represents a significant diversion of resources. Linoleic acid (18:2), the substrate for LOX, is also a direct precursor for the synthesis of seed storage triacylglycerols (TAGs) [55]. By channeling linoleic acid into the localized synthesis of volatile or signaling oxylipins within source leaves, Wandou 28 effectively reduces the pool of this fatty acid available for export to developing seeds. This creates a metabolic competition at the source, limiting the flux of lipid precursors to the reproductive sink and contributing to the observed trade‑off between defense metabolism and high oil accumulation [32, 56].

Conversely, the high‑oil, high‑yield cultivar Zhongdou 63 employs a contrasting strategy marked by the relative suppression of 9S‑LOX pathway gene expression and lower accumulation of related oxylipins in source leaves during the same critical period. This pattern indicates a more conservative approach to linoleic acid metabolism in vegetative tissues. By minimizing investment in oxylipin biosynthesis, Zhongdou 63 likely conserves a larger proportion of linoleic acid and its derivatives within the precursor pool for membrane and storage lipid synthesis. This strategy supports more efficient source‑to‑sink partitioning of carbon skeletons. The preserved fatty acid precursors can be efficiently incorporated into phosphatidylcholines for phloem transport or directly utilized for TAG assembly in developing seeds, directly underpinning its high‑oil phenotype [57, 58]. This aligns with its overall “lean metabolism” strategy, which minimizes competing metabolic sinks in source tissues to optimize resource flow toward yield components. In summary, the divergent regulation of linoleic acid metabolism in leaves suggests a potential association with the observed trait trade-off. Wandou 28’s active investment in the 9S-LOX/oxylipin pathway during pod-filling may reflect a priority on in-situ defense signaling and stress-response metabolism, potentially at the expense of precursor supply for seed oil synthesis. In contrast, Zhongdou 63’s down-regulation of this pathway is associated with its strategy for maximizing the export efficiency of photosynthetic and lipid precursors, thereby potentially reinforcing the synergy between high oil content and high yield. This finding suggests that the partitioning of fatty acids between signaling and storage pathways in source organs may be associated with final seed composition, though direct measurements of metabolic flux are needed to confirm this relationship.

Potential implications for breeding

The results of this study suggest potential candidate targets for molecular breeding in soybean, though experimental validation is required before these candidates can be applied in breeding programs. For breeding aimed at increasing oil content and yield, the focus should be on strengthening the efficient source-flow-sink synergy network represented by the greenyellow module, including enhancing the energy and transport capacity of stems (e.g., optimizing oxidative phosphorylation, cell wall synthesis) [59], while moderately suppressing resource-intensive specialized metabolism in vegetative tissues (e.g., phenylpropanoid pathways) to promote the directional flow of photosynthetic products towards oil synthesis [60]. Candidate genes include those related to auxin signaling (e.g., GH3s), brassinosteroid biosynthesis, and key enzymes in lipid metabolism.

For breeding focused on raising protein content, the central challenge lies in decoupling the frequently observed linkage between high protein and low yield. Potential strategies may include optimizing the timing of nitrogen partitioning, by fine‑tuning the spatiotemporal expression of assimilation enzymes such as asparagine synthetase to enhance nitrogen delivery to seeds at later stages without severely compromising photosynthetic output during critical growth phases. Reshaping source–sink relationships is another key approach, which could involve modulating cell‑cycle regulators (e.g., Cyclin D3 genes) and specific hormone signals (e.g., jasmonic acid) to establish stronger sink dominance earlier in development. Furthermore, in certain agro‑ecological environments, selectively retaining stress‑related metabolites from Wandou 28, such as specific isoflavones, could aid in developing varieties that combine stable yield with enhanced quality.

Limitations and future perspectives

We recognize several limitations in this study that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, our analysis focused on only two cultivars. While selecting extreme phenotypes (high-protein versus high-oil/high-yield) was intentional to maximize biological contrast and reveal fundamental regulatory differences, this design necessarily limits the generalizability of our conclusions. The molecular networks we describe may reflect variety-specific regulatory configurations rather than universal mechanisms governing the protein-oil trade-off. A logical next step would be to examine a broader panel of genotypes, including recombinant inbred lines or diverse accessions with intermediate trait combinations, to determine whether the pathways and modules identified here—particularly the 9S-lipoxygenase family, asparagine synthetase, and hub genes within the greenyellow and tan modules—show consistent association with trait variation across genetic backgrounds.

Beyond the limited genetic diversity, our study remains largely descriptive at the physiological level. We infer source-sink dynamics and resource allocation patterns from transcript and metabolite profiles, but direct measurements of phloem sap composition, stem hydraulic conductivity, respiration rates, or hormone concentrations would provide more definitive evidence for the proposed “source-flow-sink” framework. Similarly, while we observed consistent differences in linoleic acid metabolism and proposed that oxylipin production competes with oil synthesis for precursor availability, steady-state metabolite levels alone cannot confirm metabolic flux. Isotope labeling experiments and direct measurements of lipid turnover rates would be required to establish whether this competition indeed represents a causal mechanism underlying the observed trade-off.

Our sampling strategy also involved trade-offs. Pooling multiple plants per biological replicate reduced noise from individual variation but masked within-genotype heterogeneity. Using whole pods rather than dissected seed and pod wall tissues may have diluted seed-specific transcriptional signals, potentially obscuring important filial contributions to storage compound accumulation. Future studies with higher tissue resolution—separating maternal and filial tissues during seed development—would complement our current findings.

Additionally, because multi-omics data were collected from a single location, we could not assess how the identified regulatory networks respond to environmental variation. Genotype × environment interactions are well documented for protein and oil content in soybean, and understanding whether the molecular strategies we observed remain stable across growing conditions would be valuable for breeding applications.

Finally, and perhaps most importantly, this study identifies correlations and generates hypotheses but does not establish causality. The candidate genes and modules we highlight—including specific transcription factors, hormone pathway components, and metabolic enzymes—require functional validation through targeted genetic manipulation. Approaches such as CRISPR/Cas9 editing, overexpression, or mutant analysis in relevant genetic backgrounds would test whether modulating these targets can shift the balance between protein, oil, and yield in predictable directions.

Despite these limitations, we believe the value of this work lies in providing a systems-level framework and a rich resource of candidate genes, metabolic pathways, and co-expression modules for the soybean research community. The hypotheses generated here—particularly around nitrogen partitioning, oxylipin-mediated metabolic competition, and stem-localized regulatory modules—offer concrete starting points for future mechanistic studies. Integrating physiological measurements, flux analysis, and functional genetics across diverse environments and genetic backgrounds will ultimately be needed to translate these findings into practical strategies for soybean improvement.

Conclusion

In summary, this study employed spatiotemporal multi-omics profiling to delineate the dynamic molecular landscape associated with the formation of high-protein versus high-oil, high-yield traits in soybean. More importantly, it systematically revealed distinct systems-level strategies underlying the inherent trade-offs among these traits from multiple integrated perspectives—including whole-plant metabolism, signaling networks, and gene co-expression modules.

Specifically, we demonstrated that:

The high-protein cultivar Wandou 28 prioritizes nitrogen retention in source leaves (accumulating asparagine via elevated asparagine synthetase) and invests in specialized metabolism and defense signaling through the 9S-lipoxygenase-mediated oxylipin pathway, diverting linoleic acid from seed oil synthesis.

The high-protein cultivar Wandou 28 prioritizes nitrogen retention in source leaves (accumulating asparagine via elevated asparagine synthetase) and invests in specialized metabolism and defense signaling through the 9S-lipoxygenase-mediated oxylipin pathway, diverting linoleic acid from seed oil synthesis.

The high-oil/high-yield cultivar Zhongdou 63 suppresses costly secondary metabolism, activates auxin-dominated hormone signaling and oxidative phosphorylation in stems, and maintains efficient nitrogen export from leaves, maximizing resource flow to reproductive sinks.

This work generates testable hypotheses—including the roles of 9S-lipoxygenase, asparagine synthetase, and the hub genes within the identified modules—for future functional validation studies aimed at establishing causal relationships and translating these insights into breeding strategies.
