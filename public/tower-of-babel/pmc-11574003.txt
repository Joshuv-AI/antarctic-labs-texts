Down-regulation of ESRP2 inhibits breast cancer cell proliferation via inhibiting cyclinD1
Authors: Caiping He, Yuting Chen, Ximin Zhang, Huancun Feng, Yuzhen Rao, Tangyang Ji, Wenya Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Epithelial splicing regulatory protein 2 (ESRP2),an important alternative splicing protein of mRNA, is reported to have a dual role in tumors, which can promote or inhibit the occurrence and development of tumors. However, the function and mechanism of ESRP2 in breast cancer (BC) remain unclear. The distribution of ESRP2 expression in breast cancer and the correlation between ESRP2 expression and the overall survival rate were detected by The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. Gene Ontology(GO)analysis, containing biological process, cellular components, and molecular function, was utilized to evaluate the potential mechanism of ESRP2 in breast cancer. The ESRP2 expression in breast cancer cell lines was detected by real-time quantitative PCR analysis (RT-qPCR) and western blotting. Cell clone was performed to examine the proliferation of ESRP2 knockdown in MCF-7 cells. The cell cycle was measured by flow cytometry assays. The role of ESRP2 knockdown in synergistic effect with chemotherapeutic agents was also determined by MTT assay. Bioinformatics analysis demonstrated that the ESRP2 gene was elevated in breast cancer cells and its overexpression was strongly correlated with shorter overall survival. GO analysis revealed that ESRP2 expression was related to cell proliferation. ESRP2 mRNA and protein expression were elevated in breast cancer cell lines, compared to the normal human breast cell line MCF-10 A. Dwon-regulation of ESRP2 inhibited cell proliferation and promoted the sensitivity of chemotherapy drug, Cisplatin(DDP) and Paclitaxel (TAXOL), in MCF-7 cells.Additionally, ESRP2 knockdown obstructed the cell cycle at the G1 phase and caused a decrease in cyclinD1 protein expression. These findings reveal that ESRP2 is highly expressed in breast cancer and is correlated with poor prognosis in breast cancer patients. ESRP2 knockdown can inhibit MCF-7 cell proliferation by arresting the cell cycle at the G1 phase and promoting the sensitivity of chemotherapy drugs (DDP and TAXOL)in MCF-7 cells. ESRP2 may be required for the regulation of breast cancer progression, as well as a critical target for the clinical treatment of breast cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-77980-9.

Introduction

According to recent global cancer data, BC is the greatest threat to women’s health, and in 2020, had the highest incidence rate of all cancers worldwide in 20201,2. In 2022, BC remains the second most common cancer in terms of global incidence rate and has the highest incidence rate of all cancers among women3. Despite the continuous development of treatments4, and even with 5-year survival rates of 99% for noninvasive BC and 85% for invasive BC with lymph node dissemination5, the survival rate decreases to 27% when distant metastasis occurs6. Metastatic breast cancer (MBC) is currently considered fatal because of its high rate of bone metastasis7. Moreover, mutations in specific genes may increase the possibility of progression and the severity of BC8. Therefore, the identification of new therapeutic targets or molecular markers to reduce the mortality of BC and to improve the quality of life of patients is of paramount importance9.

Epithelial splicing regulatory protein 2 (ESRP2)10, a member of the RNA binding protein hnRNP family (including ESRP1 and ESRP2), promotes epithelial-type splicing and interacts with exon or intron splicing inhibitors to promote exon hopping, which affects the posttranscriptional regulation of premRNA gene expression11. Tumors may use alternative splicing to gain growth and survival advantages, which affects tumor occurrence and development12. ESRP2 and its related family members also participate in the regulation of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT). EMT is the process by which epithelial cells transform into migrating and invasive cells13. In addition to changes in transcriptional regulation during EMT, the splicing of various genes generated after transcription can also induce cell migration and invasion14, which are related to the genesis and progression of tumors.

ESRP2 had dual functions across various types of cancer through distinct pathways. Its regulation of variable splicing enhanced its impact on a broad spectrum of cancers: promoting invasion and resistance to therapy driven by androgen receptor antagonists in prostate cancer15, inhibiting Wilms tumor development16and enhancing the expression of mucin, which indirectly affected the prognosis of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma17. ESRP2 influenced the risk of metastasis and overall prognosis in BC18–20and head and neck cancer21through its involvement in the EMT process. ESRP2 also affected the progression of cervical cancer22, laryngeal cancer23, clear cell renal cell carcinoma24, and pituitary adenoma25by interacting with both upstream and downstream molecules. Using differential gene expression analysis, researchers confirmed the role of ESRP2 in the RNA-binding protein prognostic model for prostate cancer patients26, and its reduced expression was linked to a greater likelihood of intrahepatic recurrence in hepatocellular carcinoma27. However, the function of ESRP2 in BC, in addition to its participation in EMT, is still unclear.

In this study, we examined ESRP2 expression in BC and how its downregulation affects BC cell proliferation. Additionally, our study confirmed that the downregulation of ESRP2 can synergically inhibit BC cell proliferation with chemotherapy drugs. We postulate that ESRP2 may be a new target for oncotherapy in BC.

Materials and methods

GEPIA analysis

GEPIA2.0 (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/) is a web-based tool for the analysis of data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the GTEx that provides interactive and user-defined functional analysis of gene expression, including analysis of differential expression, contour map drawing, correlation analysis, and dimension reduction analysis28. Using this database, we determined the expression of ESRP2 in different cancers and normal tissues and the correlation between ESRP2 and cyclin D1 mRNA levels in BC and normal breast tissues.

Analysis of ESRP2 protein expression in normal breast samples and breast cancer samples

The Human Protein Atlas (HPA) (www.proteinatlas.org) summarizes the overall expression of human proteins through immunohistochemistry and immunocytochemistry29. The value of this database lies in its ability to compare the protein expression of ESRP2 between normal breast samples and BC samples.

University of Alabama Cancer database analysis

The UALCAN database (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu) is used for verification of target genes and for screening of tumor subgroup-specific biomarkers, mainly through in-depth analysis of TCGA data, which includes Level 3 RNA-seq and clinical data of 31 cancer types30. The UALCAN database was used to analyze the relationships between the expression of ESRP2 and BC classification, patient race, and disease grade.

Survival analysis

The Kaplan–Meier plotter can be used to analyze the correlation between specific genes and cancer prognosis by integrating gene expression data and clinical data from the TCGA, EGA, and GEO databases31. We determined the expression of ESRP2 and total survival of BC patients through a Kaplan–Meier plotter analysis, and we also obtained a formula for survival analysis of patients according to ESRP2 expression in different subtypes and different grades of BC.

GO analysis

The LinkedOmics database (http://www.linkedomics.org) includes data from 32 cancer types in the TCGA database and 11,158 patients with multiple omics and clinical data and can be used for correlation analysis and cancer gene enrichment analysis32. By performing Gene Ontology (GO) analysis and setting corresponding parameters, the role of ESRP2 in biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions was determined.

Cell culture

The human normal breast epithelial cell line MCF-10 A and the BC cell lines MCF-7, T47D, and MDA-MB-468 were purchased from the Cell Resource Center of the Shanghai Institute of Life Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences. For the composite culture of MCF-10 A cells, DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with insulin (10 µg/mL), cholera toxin (100 ng/mL) (Sigma‒Aldrich, Shanghai, China), EGF (20 ng/mL) (R&D Systems, Wiesbaden, Germany), hydrocortisone (500 ng/mL) and L-glutamine (Procell, Wuhan, China) was used. MCF-7 and T-47D cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS (A31160801, Gibco, Carlsbad, CA). MDA-MB-468 cells were cultured in DMEM/high glucose (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA) supplemented with 10% FBS. All cells were cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2. siRNAs targeting human ESRP2 and the control (siNC) were obtained from Guangzhou RiboBio Co., Ltd. To knockdown ESRP2 in vitro, ESRP2-siRNAs were transfected into MCF-7 cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (11668-019; Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations.

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR analysis

TRIzol (15596-018, Invitrogen, CA, USA) was used to extract total RNA from cultured cells. The RNA quality was assessed via a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, which was used to determine the 260/280 ratio. Gene expression analysis was conducted on samples with ratios ranging from 1.8 to 2.0. A PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (AG11705, Accurate Biology, Hunan, China) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions to reverse transcribe one microgram of mRNA into complementary DNA. Assays for alternative splicing by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) were conducted using Invitrogen’s Platinum II Hot-Start Green PCR Master Mix. SYBR Green (AG11701, Accurate Biology, Hunan, China) was used for real-time quantitative PCR (RT‒qPCR). The 2-ΔΔCt method was used to evaluate the relative expression levels of mRNAs. The following primer sequences were used for RT‒qPCR analysis: ESRP2, forward 5’-TGC CAC AGA GGA TGA CTT TG-3’ and reverse 5’- ATT GAC TGC TGG GCT CTT TG-3’; GAPDH, forward 5’-CGA GAT CCC TCC AAA ATC AA-3’ and reverse 5’- GGC AGA GAT GAT GAC CCT TT-3’.

Western blotting

The cells were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) containing phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) (Beyotime) on ice for 30 min and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. The total protein concentration was measured with a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (P0010, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). The protein lysate was analyzed via SDS‒PAGE, and immunoblotting was performed with the primary antibody and its corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibody, as shown. The antibodies used were as follows: Esrp2 (1:1000, Thermo Fisher, #PA5-21110), CyclinD1 (1:1000, Abcam, ab226977) and GAPDH (1:2000, Proteintech, 60004-1-Ig). The membranes were then incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody (1:5000, Anti-Mouse IgG, Cell Signaling Technology, #14709; 1:5000, Anti-Rabbit IgG, Cell Signaling Technology, #7074s) coupled to horseradish peroxidase (HRP) for 1 h at 37 °C. An electrochemiluminescence (ECL) kit from Tanon, Shanghai, China was used to visualize the immunoreactions, after which the membranes were scanned using a Bio-Rad ChemiDocTM Touch Imaging System. ImageJ software was used to analyze the relative integrated density value (IDV) of the internal control, GAPDH.

Cell colony formation assay

MCF-7 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were digested with 0.25% trypsin/0.02% EDTA solution (SM-2003, Sigma‒Aldrich®) to prepare a single-cell suspension in culture medium. The number of cells in a 10-µL single-cell suspension was counted under an inverted microscope. Cells (approximately 200 cells per well) were added to six-well culture plates containing a sterile glass coverslip in each well. The medium was changed every 3 days until the number of cell clones was measured by crystal violet (C0121-100 ml; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) staining.

Flow cytometry cell cycle assays

The cells were transfected with siESRP2 or siNC for 48 h. After digestion, the cells were collected by centrifugation. After the supernatant was discarded, the cells were washed twice with cold PBS. Cells were fixed in prechilled 70% ethanol at 4 °C. The cells were collected and stained with propidium iodide (PI) (ST511, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) experiments were performed within 5 h to measure the distribution of cells in the cell cycle.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted via SPSS 25.0 software. The data are expressed as the means ± SDs. We evaluated the differences between the two groups using Student’s t test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. GraphPad Prism was used for clinical data analysis of survival rates and related correlations.

High expression of ESRP2 is closely associated with poor prognosis in BC patients

We further evaluated the prognostic value of ESRP2 in BC. The K‒M plotter database analysis revealed that BC patients with high ESRP2 expression had shorter overall survival (HR = 1.19, P < 0.05) (Fig. 4A, B). Analysis of different BC subtypes, especially in the analysis of different grades of BC, revealed that high expression of ESRP2 mRNA was significantly correlated with grade 2 (HR = 1.43, P = 0.0014) BC (Fig. 4G). However, the overexpression of ESRP2 was not significantly correlated with the survival rate of patients with grade 1 (HR = 1.57, P = 0.088) (Fig. 4F) or grade 3 BC (HR = 1.3, P = 0.0059) (Fig. 4H). The sample size of those with grade 1 may have been too small to analyze the difference in survival between the high-expression group and the low-expression group. Among the different BC subtypes, high expression of ESRP2 was significantly associated with shortened overall survival of patients with the luminal A, luminal B, and Her-2 subtypes (with P < 0.05) (Fig. 4C, D, E). Downregulation of ESRP2 inhibits MCF-7 cell proliferation by targeting CyclinD1

The upregulation of the ESRP2 gene in BC suggests a potential association between elevated ESRP2 levels and the development and progression of this disease. We then determined whether the downregulation of ESRP2 could affect cancer cell proliferation. When MCF-7 cells were transiently transfected with siESRP2, the protein level of ESRP2 decreased (Fig. 6A). A colony formation assay revealed that the ability of cells to form clones was significantly lower after transfection with siESRP2 than after transfection with the negative control (siNC), which indicated that interference with si-ESRP2 inhibited the cloning ability of MCF-7 cells (Fig. 6B). We then performed flow cytometry to determine the distribution of MCF-7 cells in specific stages of the cell cycle to evaluate whether the effect of ESRP2 on the proliferation of BC cells is mediated through cell cycle arrest. Compared with the control group, ESRP2-downregulated MCF-7 cells were evidently arrested in G1 phase of the cell cycle (Fig. 6C). Furthermore, GEPIA analysis revealed that ESRP2 was significantly positively correlated with CCND1 (Cyclin D1, a key cell cycle regulator, and a G1 phase marker) (Fig. 6D). In addition, the downregulation of ESRP2 decreased the protein level of Cyclin D1 (Fig. 6E). Downregulation of ESRP2 enhances the DDP- and TAXOL-mediated inhibition of proliferation

Our previous experiment confirmed that the downregulation of ESRP2 can inhibit BC proliferation. We then investigated whether the downregulation of ESRP2 and the combination of cisplatin/paclitaxel (DDP/TAXOL) exerted synergistic effects on cell proliferation after ESRP2 knockdown for 48 h. Compared with the blank control group treated with DDP/TAXOL alone or DDP/TAXOL combined with siNC, the combination of ESRP2 downregulation and DDP/TAXOL treatment reduced cell viability (Fig. 7A, B). In addition, Fig. 7C, D shows the CI plots generated by CompuSyn software (version 1.0; ComboSyn, Paramus, NJ, USA) for downregulated ESRP2 combined with DDP/TAXOL treatment in MCF-7 cells. A CI > 1, CI = 1 and CI < 1 indicate antagonism, additive effects and synergism, respectively. The plots showed a synergistic pattern (CI < 1) and revealed that the synergistic effect increased with increasing doses of chemotherapy drugs. Discussion

BC is a common malignant tumor that threatens the health of women worldwide, and the treatment methods for this disease have gradually moved from traditional treatment to precise and targeted treatment modalities33,34. Effective biomarkers can be used to predict patients’ progress and formulate treatment plans, which is the key to accelerating the research process of precision treatment of BC35. In recent years, interest in the following BC biomarkers and potential treatments has increased: Gelsolin (GSN, a valuable diagnostic marker)36; magnetic field-induced intermittent disruption of F-actin (a promising therapeutic approach)37; the tubulin alpha-1b chain (a potential therapeutic target)38; and cytokine-induced apoptosis inhibitor 1 (a potential diagnostic, prognostic, and immune biomarker for invasive BC)39.

Previous studies have shown that ESRP2 can directly or indirectly participate in the progression of various tumors by affecting splicing variation, the EMT process, or interactions with upstream and downstream molecules22,40–43. Most reports on ESRP2 in BC have focused only on the relationship between ESRP2 and EMT, which affects the occurrence and development of BC, but have not further analyzed the role of ESRP2 expression and the related molecular mechanism19,20,43. To add to these findings, we explored its function in BC.

In recent years, bioinformatics tools have become increasingly important in the advancement of cancer research. The Gene Set Cancer Analysis (GSCA) tool provides a benchmark for assessing the significance and reliability of findings44–47. The utility of TCGA, Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx), Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA), The human protein atlas (HPA), Oncomine, Cancer Second Exchange Alliance, Tumor Immune Single Cell Hub (TISCH), and other databases in bioinformatics research is also highlighted with respect to their cancer diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic prediction value48. In addition, TCGA and GTEx can further highlight the methodology’s validity in exploring various cancers49.

In this study, we fully utilized a combination of bioinformatics tools, including GEPIA, the Kaplan‒Meier plotter and UALCAN, as well as experimental validation to more deeply explore the function of ESRP2 in BC. Horvath A et al43. sequenced the RNA from tissues of the HER2 + BC, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), and non-TBNC subtypes and validated a new set of cancer-related gene mutations in BC, including ESRP2. Coincidentally, our research results are consistent with the results of their study. Here, we found that ESRP2 was highly expressed in BC tissues compared with normal breast tissues. The high expression of ESRP2 in BC is related to BC classification, patient race, and disease grade. By comparing the immunohistochemical maps in the Human Protein Atlas, we also found that the overall expression level of ESRP2 in BC patients was increased. In addition, a survival analysis demonstrated the prognostic value of ESRP2 in BC, as high expression of ESRP2 is associated with poor prognosis in BC patients. We found that ESRP2 was highly expressed in the human BC cell lines MCF-7, T-47D, and MDA-MB-468 at the mRNA and protein levels compared with the human normal breast cell line MCF-10 A. These results suggest that ESRP2 may be a specific oncogene involved in the occurrence and development of BC.

After discovering that ESRP2 is overexpressed in BC, we explored the role and mechanism of ESRP2. GO analysis of the LinkedOmics database revealed that ESRP2 was related to cell proliferation in BC. Interestingly, and importantly, we showed that the downregulation of ESRP2 inhibited MCF-7 cell proliferation. To further investigate the mechanism by which ESRP2 affects BC cell proliferation, we found that knockdown of ESRP2 induced cell cycle arrest in G1 phase. In addition, our results showed that the downregulation of ESRP2 and treatment with chemotherapeutic drugs synergistically inhibited the proliferation of BC cells. In summary, the evidence confirms that ESRP2 knockdown inhibits BC cell proliferation, which suggests that ESRP2 may be a potential therapeutic target in BC.

If additional clinical samples become available and if reproducible experiments are performed to verify the correlation between ESRP2 expression and BC, and if more upstream and downstream protein-related pathways and drug resistance pathways are explored, the findings could provide more meaningful value of ESRP2 in diagnosis and clinical treatment. Future research should aim to determine how ESRP2 interacts with other proteins and how this interaction could influence BC cell migration and invasion.

Conclusions

Our research indicated that ESRP2 is highly expressed in BC and is associated with poor prognosis in BC patients. Knockdown of ESRP2 expression repressed the proliferation of BC cells and exerted synergistic anticancer effects with chemotherapeutic agents. Mechanistically, the downregulation of ESRP2 inhibits BC cell growth by inducing cell cycle arrest in G1 phase and inhibiting cyclin D1. Our research indicates that ESRP2 is crucial for regulating the progression of BC and provides an experimental basis for identifying ESRP2 as a potential biomarker and for expanding its potential clinical value.
