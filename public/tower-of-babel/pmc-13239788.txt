Development of transdisciplinary approach scale for healthcare professionals
Authors: Emi Watanabe, Kiyotaka Nemoto, Hirokazu Tachikawa, Junji Haruta, Tetsuaki Arai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study developed and validated the Transdisciplinary Approach Scale (TDAS), the first instrument specifically designed to assess transdisciplinary collaboration abilities among mental health professionals. While existing scales measure interprofessional collaboration attitudes or general competencies, none explicitly address transdisciplinary‐specific elements such as role release and shared conceptual frameworks.MethodsFollowing a systematic scale development framework, we conducted a literature review, expert content validation, and psychometric testing. Between October 2019 and March 2021, 117 mental health professionals from 14 professions enrolled in the Psychiatric Staff Education Program for Transdisciplinary Approach (PsySEPTA). Baseline data were collected from 96 participants for scale development. Exploratory factor analysis, internal consistency testing, known‐groups validity assessment, and responsiveness evaluation were performed. Pre‐post data from 53 one‐year course participants were used to assess responsiveness to educational intervention.ResultsThe final 10‐item TDAS had a two‐factor structure explaining 57.1% of variance: Transdisciplinary Collaboration (six items, α = 0.881) and Transdisciplinary Practice (four items, α = 0.765). No significant differences were observed across professional groups or genders. Following PsySEPTA participation, significant improvements occurred in both factors (Cohen's d = 0.64–0.73), demonstrating the scale's sensitivity to educational intervention.ConclusionThe TDAS is a psychometrically sound, brief instrument for assessing transdisciplinary collaboration abilities. It captures both collaborative processes and their practical application in patient care, filling an important gap where no validated transdisciplinary assessment previously existed. The scale offers a valuable tool for evaluating team‐based, patient‐centered care initiatives.

INTRODUCTION

Various interprofessional teams have been established in psychiatric healthcare settings. 1 , 2 , 3 McGrath et al. 4 reported that the lifetime prevalence of any mental disorder was 28.6% for male respondents and 29.8% for female respondents, with conditional probabilities of first onset peaking at approximately 15 years of age. Several psychiatric disorders, including schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, are chronic illnesses with heterogeneous courses. 5 , 6 Lifelong neurodevelopmental disorders such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) also exist. 7 , 8 Consequently, to support diverse patients across various age groups, multiple types of interprofessional teams exist in psychiatric care.

Within the discourse on interprofessional teamwork, three conceptual frameworks are frequently employed: multidisciplinary, interdisciplinary, and transdisciplinary approaches. 9 In this paper, we define these terms as follows 10 , 11

‐Multidisciplinary: Professionals from different disciplines work in parallel or sequentially, providing services independently with limited interaction.‐Interdisciplinary: Professionals work collaboratively toward common goals, with each discipline contributing from their distinct expertise while sharing information and participating in joint decision‐making.‐Transdisciplinary: Professionals transcend disciplinary boundaries to develop a shared conceptual framework. Through mutual understanding of each other's roles, they engage in role release to deliver patient‐centered care holistically.

The concept of transdisciplinary in healthcare comprises two essential and interrelated dimensions. 10 , 11 First, transdisciplinary practice requires relational and communicative processes through which team members transcend traditional professional boundaries. These processes include developing shared team ethics, mutual respect for collective decision‐making, active listening to diverse professional perspectives, and constructive resolution of interprofessional conflicts. 10 Second, transdisciplinary practice involves the practical application of shared conceptual frameworks in patient care delivery, operationalized through collaborative needs assessment, integrated care planning, and role release—the deliberate extension of professional practice beyond traditional disciplinary boundaries through mutual agreement and shared accountability. 11

These dimensions are conceptually distinct from interdisciplinary collaboration. While interdisciplinary teams share information and participate in joint decision‐making, professionals maintain their distinct disciplinary roles and boundaries. In contrast, transdisciplinary practice requires active integration of roles and shared ownership of care delivery, enabling more flexible and holistic responses to complex patient needs. 9 , 10 , 11

In the context of interprofessional teamwork, psychiatric care requires healthcare providers capable of transdisciplinary collaboration. Empirical evidence suggests that transdisciplinary approaches may produce superior outcomes in complex health contexts. For example, assertive community treatment (ACT)—a psychiatric care model embodying transdisciplinary principles of role flexibility and shared treatment frameworks—has demonstrated effectiveness in reducing hospitalizations and improving community functioning among individuals with severe mental illness. 2 However, despite the theoretical importance and potential clinical benefits of transdisciplinary collaboration, no validated instrument existed to specifically assess these abilities. To address diverse patient needs effectively, professionals must clearly distinguish which tasks are discipline‐specific and which can be shared across disciplines, enabling flexible and appropriate coordination in response to varying clinical situations.

Several scales have been developed to assess interprofessional collaboration abilities. In Japan, Japanese versions of existing scales such as the Readiness for Interprofessional Learning Scale (RIPLS), Jefferson Scale of Attitudes Toward Interprofessional Collaboration (JeffSATIC), and Assessment of Interprofessional Team Collaboration Scale II (AITCS‐II), 12 , 13 , 14 as well as Japan‐original scales including the Japanese Self‐assessment Scale of Interprofessional Competency (JASSIC) 15 and Team Approach Assessment Scale (TASS) 15 are available. These scales primarily measure attitudes or competencies in interprofessional collaboration among students and/or clinical professionals. However, to our knowledge, no validated scale specifically measures transdisciplinary collaboration. To address this gap and evaluate the effectiveness of our previously developed interprofessional education (IPE) program—the Psychiatric Staff Education Program for Transdisciplinary Approach (PsySEPTA) 16 —we developed the Transdisciplinary Approach Scale (TDAS) in 2019. This study describes the development and psychometric validation of the TDAS.

METHODS

IPE program development

In 2019, we developed an IPE program grounded in transdisciplinary principles for mental health professionals—PsySEPTA. The program design incorporated key elements of transdisciplinary practice, including role release, shared conceptual frameworks, and patient‐centered care. 10 , 11 A detailed description of the program structure and content has been published elsewhere. 16

Scale development

The TDAS was developed in 2019 following a systematic approach to scale construction as outlined by Boateng et al. 17

Domain identification and item generation

In 2019, prior to scale development, we conducted a literature review to confirm the absence of existing transdisciplinary assessment instruments and to inform our conceptual framework. We searched PubMed using the search terms “transdisciplinary” AND “scale” AND “medical” for publications from 2000 to 2019, which identified 48 articles. Review of titles and abstracts revealed that while several scales measured interprofessional or interdisciplinary collaboration, none specifically assessed transdisciplinary collaboration among healthcare professionals.

We conducted a supplementary search using “interprofessional” AND “scale” AND “competency” (2000–2019), which yielded 90 articles. After applying filters for free full‐text, English‐language, and human studies, 22 articles remained. Review of titles and abstracts identified 6 relevant studies that informed our understanding of interprofessional competency assessment frameworks. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 These findings confirmed the need for a dedicated transdisciplinary assessment instrument. A comparison of the six scales identified in the supplementary search with the TDAS developed in the current study is presented in Table 1.

Based on this literature review and guided by theoretical frameworks on transdisciplinary collaboration, 10 , 11 we generated initial items aligned with PsySEPTA's educational objectives. 16 Through discussions with content experts, we established a shared understanding of transdisciplinary collaboration and developed items to capture its essential components: transcending traditional professional boundaries, mutual understanding of professional roles, engagement in role release, and implementation of patient‐centered care through shared conceptual frameworks.

Content validity

To establish content validity, two experts (J.H. and H.T.) independently reviewed all scale items. Each expert assessed items for relevance, clarity, and comprehensiveness. Through iterative discussions, we refined item wording, eliminated redundant items, and ensured adequate coverage of the transdisciplinary domain.

Scale format

The TDAS is a self‐report scale that employs a 5‐point Likert‐type response format ranging from 1 (cannot do at all) to 5 (can do very well), with higher scores indicating greater perceived competence in transdisciplinary collaboration. Following content validation, the initial scale consisted of 16 items.

Participants

Between October 2019 and March 2021, 117 mental health professionals enrolled in PsySEPTA (1‐year course: n = 86; 2‐year course: n = 31). Baseline TDAS data were collected from 96 participants (82.1% response rate) for scale development. For pre‐post evaluation, 53 one‐year course participants who completed both baseline and follow‐up assessments were included (61.6% of enrollees; 73.6% of completers).

Data collection

Baseline TDAS data were collected immediately before program commencement using an online questionnaire platform. For pre‐post evaluation, follow‐up data were collected from 1‐year course participants immediately after program completion (approximately 12 months after baseline). Participants provided informed consent online, or for data collected prior to ethics approval, were given the opportunity to opt out. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Tsukuba Faculty of Medicine (Approval No.1654).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0.2.0 (IBM Corp.). Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, median, interquartile range) were calculated for participant characteristics and TDAS items.

Scale development analyses (exploratory factor analysis [EFA), internal consistency, known‐groups validity) were conducted using baseline data from all available participants (N = 96). Pre‐post comparisons were conducted for 1‐year course participants who completed assessments at both time points (n = 53).

Item reduction and factor extraction

Item‐total correlations and inter‐item correlations were examined to identify items for potential deletion. EFA using principal axis factoring with promax rotation was performed to examine the underlying factor structure. The number of factors to retain was determined based on eigenvalues > 1.0 and scree plot inspection. Factor loadings of approximately 0.40 were considered substantial. An iterative process was used, with items deleted and analyses re‐run until a satisfactory factor structure was achieved.

Tests of reliability

Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach's alpha coefficient, with values ≥ 0.70 considered acceptable.

Test of validity

Known‐groups validity was examined using Kruskal–Wallis tests to compare TDAS factor scores across professional groups and gender. Responsiveness to change was assessed using paired t‐tests to examine changes in TDAS scores from baseline to follow‐up. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two‐tailed).

Scale structure and psychometric properties

Item reduction and factor extraction

The initial 16‐item scale was subjected to EFA using principal axis factoring with Promax rotation. Although eigenvalues > 1.0 suggested a three‐factor solution, scree plot examination indicated that a two‐factor solution was more parsimonious and theoretically interpretable. The iterative item reduction process proceeded over three rounds.

In Round 1, EFA of all 16 items was conducted. Two items were excluded because their factor loadings fell below the criterion of 0.50 on both factors: Q8 (“Coordinate, advise, and summarize discussions”; loadings: Factor 1 = 0.337, Factor 2 = 0.476) and Q14 (“Collaborate in flat relationships”; Factor 1 = 0.272, Factor 2 = 0.478), indicating insufficient specificity to either construct (see Table S1 for the complete pattern matrix).

In Round 2, EFA of the remaining 14 items was conducted. Four additional items were excluded based on conceptual criteria: Q2 (“Trust and respect each other's roles”; Factor 1 = 0.881) was excluded due to conceptual redundancy with Q1 (“Understand each other's professional roles”), as both items address mutual professional recognition without additional construct coverage; Q9 (“Accurately convey necessary information”; Factor 1 = 0.740) was excluded as accurate information transmission is relevant to all levels of interprofessional collaboration and does not capture the transdisciplinary‐specific element of transcending professional boundaries; Q15 (“Demonstrate strong leadership”; Factor 2 = 0.934) was excluded as it reflects general individual leadership ability rather than the shared, boundary‐transcending collaboration characterizing transdisciplinary practice; and Q16 (“Swiftly fulfill roles assigned by superiors”; Factor 1 = 0.507) was excluded as hierarchical role adherence is conceptually opposite to the role release that defines the transdisciplinary approach (see Table S1).

In Round 3, EFA of the final 10 items yielded a clear two‐factor structure, explaining 57.1% of the total variance (Table 4). Factor loadings ranged from 0.396 to 1.039. One item (Q4: “Assess patient and family needs”) showed a loading slightly exceeding 1.0, which is mathematically permissible in oblique rotation (Promax) when factors are moderately correlated (r = 0.649). Variance inflation factors (VIF) for Factor 2 items ranged from 1.12 to 1.39, well below the threshold of concern (VIF < 5.0), indicating no problematic multicollinearity. Internal consistency reliability

The TDAS demonstrated good internal consistency (Table 4). Cronbach's alpha was 0.881 for Factor 1 (transdisciplinary collaboration) and 0.765 for Factor 2 (transdisciplinary practice), both exceeding the acceptable threshold of 0.70.

Known‐groups validity

Kruskal–Wallis tests revealed no significant differences in TDAS factor scores across professional groups (Factor 1: H = 14.52, df = 13, p = 0.338; Factor 2: H = 8.28, df = 13, p = 0.825) or gender (Factor 1: H = 1.44, df = 1, p = 0.230; Factor 2: H = 3.78, df = 1, p = 0.052).

Responsiveness to change

TDAS scores significantly improved following PsySEPTA participation for all 53 participants who completed both assessments (Table 5). DISCUSSION

This study developed and validated the TDAS, a brief 10‐item instrument designed to assess transdisciplinary collaboration abilities among mental health professionals. While several existing scales assess interprofessional collaboration abilities, 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 24 they primarily focus on attitudes or general competencies without explicitly addressing transdisciplinary‐specific elements such as role release and shared conceptual frameworks that characterize transdisciplinary practice.

The TDAS demonstrated a two‐factor structure with good psychometric properties and responsiveness to educational intervention. This structure aligns with theoretical conceptualizations of transdisciplinary approaches. Factor 1 (transdisciplinary collaboration) captures the collaborative processes through which teams transcend disciplinary boundaries—sharing ethics, respecting collective decisions, listening to diverse perspectives, and resolving conflicts. These items reflect the relational and communicative abilities essential for developing shared conceptual frameworks, a defining feature of transdisciplinary practice. 10 , 11 , 25 Factor 2 (transdisciplinary practice) represents the practical application of transdisciplinary approaches in patient care, including role release and patient‐centered planning, which integrate diverse professional perspectives into holistic care delivery.

The moderate correlation between factors (r = 0.649) indicates that while these dimensions are related, they represent distinct aspects of transdisciplinary approaches. This finding aligns with the implementation science literature, which demonstrates that gaps commonly exist between what is known (collaborative attitudes and processes) and what is actually done in practice. 26 The TDAS's two‐factor structure is consistent with this perspective, capturing both the relational foundations of transdisciplinary collaboration and their practical application in patient care delivery.

In addition, the significant improvements in TDAS scores following PsySEPTA participation (effect sizes d = 0.64–0.73, all exceeding Cohen's threshold for medium effects) provide evidence of the scale's sensitivity to educational interventions. These effect sizes compare favorably with those reported for other interprofessional competency assessments, indicating adequate responsiveness to targeted interprofessional education. The similar effect sizes across both factors suggest that PsySEPTA effectively enhanced collaborative abilities and transdisciplinary practice abilities, supporting the scale's utility for program evaluation. The brief 10‐item format makes the TDAS practical for repeated administration, allowing educational institutions and healthcare organizations to assess baseline abilities, evaluate training effectiveness, and monitor development over time.

Unlike profession‐specific competencies, transdisciplinary abilities—such as role release, shared decision‐making, and patient‐centered planning—are learnable skills that are not inherently tied to specific disciplines. The absence of significant differences in TDAS scores across professional groups and genders supports this premise, 10 , 11 , 25 though this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample sizes for some professional groups. Furthermore, the significant improvements observed following PsySEPTA participation suggest that all healthcare professionals, regardless of professional background or gender, can develop transdisciplinary abilities through appropriate education and training.

These findings support the TDAS's utility for evaluating transdisciplinary practice in clinical settings. Healthcare organizations implementing team‐based care models can use the TDAS to identify professional development needs, evaluate the effectiveness of transdisciplinary training initiatives, and support quality improvement efforts focused on collaborative practice. The scale may also be applicable in educational contexts for assessing learners who have clinical experience, such as professionals participating in continuing education programs or advanced students with substantial clinical practice opportunities.

LIMITATIONS

This study followed the systematic scale development framework proposed by Boateng et al. 17 However, several psychometric validation steps remain incomplete. First, confirmatory factor analysis was not conducted to verify the two‐factor structure. Second, convergent and discriminant validity were not assessed through correlations with existing interprofessional scales. Third, test–retest reliability was not examined, though internal consistency was excellent (α = 0.77–0.88). Future studies should address these gaps and extend validation by examining predictive validity, specifically associations between TDAS scores and patient outcomes.

The TDAS items are relatively abstract statements assessed through self‐report, which may be interpreted differently by respondents from different professions or with varying levels of clinical experience. The current 5‐point Likert scale provides general anchors but lacks concrete behavioral examples for each response level, which may affect measurement precision. Future versions of the TDAS should consider providing illustrative behavioral examples for each item and more detailed anchor descriptions for each scale point. Additionally, supplementing self‐report data with supervisor ratings or behavioral observations could provide convergent evidence of transdisciplinary abilities.

Regarding generalizability, several limitations warrant consideration. Our scale development sample was drawn from a single interprofessional education program in Japan, with some professions represented by very small numbers (e.g., dentists n = 2, care managers n = 2). Although no significant differences in TDAS scores were observed across professions, these small sub‑samples limit statistical power, and the absence of differences should not be interpreted as evidence of true equivalence. Larger and more balanced samples are needed to more rigorously examine whether transdisciplinary abilities transcend occupational boundaries. Future research should also validate the TDAS across diverse psychiatric settings—including inpatient units, community mental health centers, and specialized treatment programs—and conduct cross‑cultural validation, as cultural and healthcare system contexts may shape transdisciplinary practice. Establishing psychometric equivalence across countries and systems would further support the scale's broader applicability.

Finally, the TDAS relies on self‐reported abilities, which may not fully reflect actual clinical performance and may be subject to social desirability bias. Future research should examine relationships between TDAS scores and objective measures of transdisciplinary practice, such as behavioral observations or patient outcomes.

CONCLUSIONS

The TDAS is a psychometrically sound, brief 10‐item instrument for assessing transdisciplinary collaboration abilities among mental health professionals. Its two‐factor structure, good reliability, evidence of construct validity, and responsiveness to educational intervention support its use in interprofessional education research and program evaluation. To our knowledge, this is the first validated scale specifically designed to assess transdisciplinary collaboration. As defined in this study, transdisciplinary collaboration involves professionals transcending disciplinary boundaries through role release and shared conceptual frameworks to deliver patient‐centered care holistically. The brief format makes it practical for repeated use in clinical settings. While developed in psychiatric settings, the transdisciplinary elements assessed by the TDAS—role release, shared conceptual frameworks, and patient‐centered collaboration—may be applicable across diverse healthcare contexts where team‐based care is emphasized. Future research should examine the TDAS's utility in other medical specialties.
