Older Patients and Conservative Kidney Management
Authors: Michelle Prong Geiger, Paul R. Duberstein, Rebecca J. Allen, Kevin A. Fiscella, Ronald M. Epstein, Fahad Saeed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ImportanceIn the US, dialysis remains the reflexive treatment option for end-stage kidney disease. Conservative kidney management (CKM), a nondialytic pathway, is infrequently utilized despite the burden dialysis often imposes on patients, caregivers, and health care systems.ObjectiveTo examine why older adults choose CKM, how caregivers influence this choice, and how nephrologists support CKM through advance care planning and palliative care referrals.Design, Setting, and ParticipantsThis qualitative study included secondary analysis of clinical notes and visit data from patients, caregivers, and nephrologists in 2 randomized trials in the US collectively spanning 2019 to 2024. Descriptive and qualitative methods were used to examine decision-making among older adults who chose CKM in CKD-EDU (Chronic Kidney Disease Enhanced Dialysis Education) and DIAL-SDM (Shared Decision Making in Dialysis), based on coaching visit data and nephrology clinic notes collected from January 10, 2019, to July 18, 2024. The secondary analyses for this study were performed May 19, 2024, to February 7, 2025.Main Outcomes and MeasuresMain outcomes were patients’ reasons for choosing CKM; caregiver and nephrologist support for CKM; and frequency of advance care planning and palliative care referrals.ResultsOf the 118 patients enrolled in the 2 trials, 35 chose CKM (mean [SD] age, 82 [5.9] years; 19 female [54.3%]; 8 Black [22.9%], 26 White [74.3%], 1 other [2.8%]). Qualitative analysis revealed the most common reasons for choosing CKM: (1) avoiding anticipated burdens of dialysis to maintain quality of life, (2) current poor health making CKM a logical choice, (3) witnessing the benefits and burdens of dialysis and CKM in loved ones, and (4) avoiding overmedicalization at the end of life. Caregivers often preferred life-prolonging therapies but typically aligned with patients’ wishes once those wishes were known. Nephrologists frequently framed dialysis as the reflexive option, sometimes questioned goal-concordant CKM choices, and rarely initiated advance care planning or palliative care referrals.Conclusions and RelevanceIn this qualitative study of patients, caregivers, and nephrologists, support for CKM was limited, despite patients consistently citing reasons for choosing CKM. Expanding CKM will require nephrologist training, caregiver-inclusive decision-making, institutional CKM pathways, and policies that incentivize its use.

Introduction

In the US, dialysis is commonly the reflexive treatment for patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD),1 and many patients perceive their choice as dialysis or death.2,3 A lack of informed decision-making is associated with dialysis regret,4,5 yet most patients want comprehensive education on their kidney disease and treatment options.4,5,6 Accordingly, nephrology guidelines recommend shared decision-making (SDM), presentation of conservative kidney management (CKM) as a viable option, and engaging in advance care planning (ACP) for patients with ESKD.7,8,9 CKM is a person-centered, nondialytic approach emphasizing slowing CKD progression, enhancing quality of life (QoL), and addressing physical, psychosocial, and spiritual needs.10,11 Decisions to pursue dialysis or CKM are often made in a triangle of patients, caregivers, and nephrologists.12 Studies have shown caregiver involvement can improve SDM by increasing questions during clinical encounters without limiting patient participation,13,14 but may also exert pressure to pursue more aggressive treatments, including dialysis.5,15,16,17

Nephrologists should ideally help patients consider kidney therapy options, including CKM.10 However, nephrologists have varying attitudes toward CKM: some perceive CKM as their failure while others recognize its benefits.1,6,18 Even when nephrologists are eager to support patients choosing CKM, the lack of reimbursement policies means few US nephrologists work in offices with established CKM protocols.19,20,21 Not surprisingly, the uptake of CKM is limited, even among older patients with multiple comorbidities.22

Given that dialysis is commonly the reflexive kidney therapy option in the US and caregivers and nephrologists significantly affect kidney therapy decisions, this study examines: (1) why older adults with ESKD choose CKM, (2) how caregivers influence this choice, and (3) how nephrologists support this choice.

Methods

We used 2 qualitative data sources: (1) deidentified transcripts of coaching visits from 2 intervention trials supporting kidney therapy decision-making for older adults (CKD-EDU [Chronic Kidney Disease Enhanced Dialysis Education]23 and DIAL-SDM [Shared Decision Making in Dialysis]18 trials), and (2) nephrology office notes of all participants choosing CKM. We performed content analyses of these 2 data sources for the present study.24 We reviewed coaching notes for only the intervention groups and nephrology notes for both the control and intervention groups. This study the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) reporting guideline for qualitative studies.

The CKD-EDU23 trial (conducted 2019-2020) was an audio-recorded, palliative care–based intervention where patients and caregivers received a decision aid followed by coaching from palliative care clinicians. The DIAL-SDM18 trial (conducted 2022-2024) was an audio-recorded intervention for patients, caregivers, and nephrologists. Intervention patients and caregivers viewed a video emphasizing goal-concordant care, received a decision aid, and met with a communication coach (1 medical student and 2 master’s-level family therapy students) to practice discussing kidney therapy concerns. For both trials, coaches were not members of nephrology teams and had no prior relationships with participants. Intervention nephrologists received SDM communication coaching. Control patients in both trials received standard CKD care and kidney therapy education.

All participants in both trials were asked about their kidney therapy choices. Response options included CKM, hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, and “unsure.” In the DIAL-SDM trial, participants could also select a “decide not to decide” option.25 The current study includes only participants who chose CKM on their last available assessment in both trials.

We also performed medical record reviews to assess ACP completion (living will, medical orders for life-sustaining treatment [MOLST] form, health care proxy form) and palliative care referrals. Participants provided written informed consent for participation, medical record review, and interviews at enrollment in the CKD-EDU and DIAL-SDM trials. Both trials were approved by the University of Rochester institutional review board.

Multiple qualitative sources captured complementary perspectives on CKM decision-making and care delivery. Coaching transcripts reflected patient and caregiver values and concerns, treatment preferences, and family influences, while nephrology notes documented CKM discussions, ACP documentation, palliative care referrals, and treatment planning. Because the sources differed in purpose, depth, and context, we treated them as complementary rather than interchangeable evidence.

Data Analysis

We used descriptive statistics to summarize participant demographics, including self-reported race and ethnicity information and ACP documentation and palliative care referrals. Race and ethnicity data were collected as variables in this study because, while racial and ethnic disparities in chronic kidney disease outcomes are well-established from prior research, the magnitude of those disparities related to CKM uptake and access is unclear. For study outcomes, we used content analysis with a combined deductive and inductive coding approach.24 Initial coding categories were informed by the study questions, prior research on CKM and SDM,14,26,27,28,29 and the structure of the parent trial interventions. Codes were then refined inductively as coders reviewed transcripts and nephrology notes, identifying additional concepts in the data.30

Two coders independently analyzed the qualitative data using content analysis assisted by MAXQDA version 24 software (VERBI GmbH).24 Coder 1 (M.P.G.) conducted initial coding of qualitative data from 35 patients across the CKD-EDU and DIAL-SDM trials. Data coding from 14 of these 35 patients (40%) was then verified (F.S.). Coder 2 (nonauthor contributor) performed a parallel analysis of qualitative data from 13 patients in the CKD-EDU trial and was similarly verified (F.S.). Verification was used to support coding consistency and occurred through consensus review. We coded the data from May 19, 2024, to February 7, 2025. The coding team met regularly to compare coding decisions, discuss discrepancies, refine definitions, and reach consensus on final themes. Because this was a secondary analysis of existing trial data, we assessed thematic sufficiency rather than prospectively sampling to saturation. During coding, we tracked whether new data generated or substantially changed themes and stopped when additional data only reinforced existing themes.

To support trustworthiness, we maintained an analytic record of codebook revisions, coding decisions, and consensus discussions; used multiple coders with verification by a senior team member (F.S.); compared themes across data sources; and selected illustrative quotations and clinical note excerpts that reflected both common and discrepant data patterns. The multidisciplinary research team brought clinical and qualitative expertise to the analysis, which strengthened interpretation but may have introduced assumptions favoring SDM and goal-concordant CKM. To mitigate this, coding decisions and themes were reviewed collaboratively and checked against both coaching transcripts and nephrology documentation. We report illustrative quotations for each theme in the Results (additional quotations are provided in eTable 1 in Supplement 1).

Results

Of the 118 patients enrolled in the 2 trials, 35 patients chose CKM. Their mean (SD) age was 82.0 (5.9) years; 19 patients (54.3%) were women; 8 patients (22.9%) were Black or African American, 26 (74.3%) were White, and 1 (2.8%) identified as Native Hawaiian and American Indian (Table 1). A combined 16 caregivers and 17 nephrologists participated in the 2 trials.

Factors Cited for Choosing CKM

Burdens of Dialysis and QoL

Many older patients worried about the risks and the physical, emotional, and logistical burdens of dialysis. One participant remarked, “Going into the hospital 3 times a week...it’s painful.” Several patients shared specific apprehensions about complications of dialysis, such as the risk of infections: “I would certainly not choose to have points of infection inserted into me,” and, “If I put another thing in my arm, am I going to get another infection?”

Several patients reflected on the limited utility of dialysis if it were not going to relieve their most bothersome symptoms and possibly make their QoL worse. Said one patient, “If you are going to go through and get dialysis, inconvenience, the pain, lack of energy… the minuses outweigh the pluses in my book because what is a couple of months or a year or 2 compared to what you have to put up with?... I would rather die.”

With or without dialysis, many patients realized their remaining time was short. “I know I’m not going to get back to what I was like a year ago,” observed one patient. “Being 87, I don’t think it’s worth it to consider dialysis. I know it’ll probably get worse, but I don’t think dialysis would make it any better.” Another patient stated, “You know, I got this far...and there’s not much further to go....I don’t want to be laying in bed and not being able to move.”

Current Health Status

Older patients and family members often discussed the weighty burden of current medical comorbidities as reasons for choosing CKM. One patient shared, “I have all these [medical problems] wrong, but they’re not really hurting me right now.…I’m still able to get up, walk in the other room, look at my flowers, and check one of the orchids blossoming.” This patient’s wife shared, “He had COVID 2 years ago and suffered weight loss and breathing problems along with the kidney disease, heart disease, and diabetes.…Then to read about what it would entail to be treated by dialysis, it was just too much.”

Similarly, other patients admitted that their declining functional status influenced their choice for CKM. One patient explained, “I find a lot of things that I can’t do that I could do several months ago, like trying to make my meals.…I get a lot more charley horses in my legs that I didn’t get before.”

Witnessing Dialysis and CKM in Loved Ones

Many patients discussed their loved ones’ difficulties with dialysis, and witnessing these challenges firsthand led them to refuse dialysis themselves. “I know a few people on dialysis, and I see how it interferes with everything I want to do,” said one patient. “You have to make time every 3 days... I would rather try something like some medicines.” Another patient expressed, “My sister had dialysis; she did peritoneal, and [family member] did regular dialysis. I don’t want to take the dialysis…I can’t see it.” When the interviewer suggested CKM as an alternative, the patient’s caregiver affirmed, “That’s what she wants.” Only one participant mentioned family members’ positive experiences with CKM as influential in his decision-making. As their nephrologist wrote, “He did have 2 uncles with advanced CKD, and both refused [kidney] replacement therapy.”

End-of-Life Care Preferences

Many patients opting for CKM expressed a desire for a more dignified, less medicalized end-of-life course. A patient succinctly stated, “[CKM] sounds good....It’s not so much work and a peaceful death.” Another patient shared his experience choosing a less intense care plan when previously diagnosed with esophageal cancer: “I considered quality of life as well as how long I was going to live.…I had esophageal cancer and they wanted me to have [an intense surgery].…I refused to do it, and it turned out to be the right decision.” This patient went on to contrast his hope to minimize end-of-life medical intensity with suffering he witnessed in his friends around their deaths. “I have seen friends suffer for a while, and it is not a pretty sight.” In a visit with a patient and her daughter, a nephrologist documented, “[Patient] and her daughter, who provides a lot of her care, have concerns about dialysis causing increased medicalization at end-of- life. They had questions about managing symptoms associated with CKD.”

Caregivers and CKM Choice

Patients often noted that choosing CKM was influenced by having supportive caregivers who validated their choice and provided necessary care. One patient expressed, “I have my daughter...she will take good care of me. She will know the right thing to do.” Another patient also discussed family support: “There is nothing that scares me about taking that path [CKM] with the support of my family.” During an office visit for another patient, a nephrologist noted, “[Patient] is convinced that she doesn’t want any dialysis....Her son agrees with her wishes.”

There was occasional tension between caregivers and patients about whether CKM was the appropriate choice. After one patient expressed interest in CKM, their spouse remarked, “[All I want is] to keep him going... I would vote yes for [prolonging life], but I would hate to have him go through [dialysis] if he does not want to.” The patient responded, “I don’t want anything more stuck in me...I compromised my life the past 2 years, and now to deliberately make it worse [with dialysis] …if [death] happens, it happens, because this is how I want it.” By including caregivers in decision-making discussions, they ultimately understood patients’ desires for CKM and supported the CKM choice.

Nephrologists and CKM

Most nephrologists’ documentation suggested dialysis was the reflexive therapy option for ESKD by invoking its necessity. Several clinicians’ notes, even for patients who had already chosen CKM, included statements like, “She is aware of possibility of requiring dialysis if her renal function continues to deteriorate,” and, “I anticipate that she will likely require dialysis in the next several months.” There were multiple examples where nephrologists documented repeated vascular access placement referrals, sometimes despite clearly stated preferences for CKM, including statements such as, “[Patient] doesn’t want any dialysis when needed, had declined preparation for vascular access on multiple previous visit[s].”

A few nephrologists’ notes indicated support for patients choosing the CKM pathway by placing palliative care referrals and engaging in ACP when a patient expressed interest in CKM. One nephrologist wrote, “The patient very clearly stated with understanding that he would never want to be on dialysis. Will refer to palliative nephrology.” Another nephrologist wrote, “Patient has decided not to pursue dialysis... will refer to palliative nephrology for symptom management.” A third nephrologist documented, “We again had a discussion about management of [ESKD]....I feel that dialysis would be less likely to offer benefit, perhaps provide a few months of extra length of life, and no clear change in quality of life. She expressed today that she has decided she does not want to pursue dialysis…. Her daughter agrees that no dialysis and a conservative care approach is the best plan.”

In descriptive data on ACP completion and palliative care referrals, 23 patients (65.7%) had a health care proxy, 4 (11.4%) had a living will, and 20 (57.1%) had a MOLST (Table 2). Only 4 patients (11.4%) received a palliative care referral.

Discussion

This study of older adults, their caregivers, and their nephrologists explored patients’ reasons for choosing CKM and the roles of caregiver and nephrologist support for the CKM pathway. We wish to highlight 4 key findings. First, patients’ reasons for choosing CKM augment the existing research.26,31,32 A 2024 review33 found that patients choose CKM to maintain QoL while avoiding caregiver burden, dialysis-related challenges, and interference with daily life. Scherer et al19 identified functional status and comorbidity burden as influential in CKM decisions. Additionally, consistent with our findings, Winterbottom et al34 found that patients’ kidney therapy choices are influenced by others’ experiences with ESKD. However, unlike previous research, our study uses multiple qualitative data sources to show that choosing CKM is a deliberative process shaped by anticipatory concerns about current health and future and end-of-life goals. Our study also highlights CKM as a goal-concordant choice rather than simply declining dialysis.

Second, we found that caregivers’ validation and willingness to provide care facilitated patients’ CKM choice. Furthermore, caregivers who initially preferred dialysis ultimately supported patients’ CKM choice after hearing their preferences. The presence of caregivers during decision-making conversations has been associated with more comprehensive clinical encounters, improved patient support during complex decision-making, and more favorable caregiver attitudes toward CKM.13,14,28,35 Our study advances this research by highlighting the critical role of caregiver support, and that patients would not be abandoned in choosing CKM.

Third, our analysis of nephrology documentation showed reflexivity toward dialysis initiation and inconsistent support for the CKM pathway. Some notes indicated active support for CKM, while others suggested disapproval through repeated questioning or language implying nonadherence to recommendations (refusing dialysis access placement) despite choosing CKM. Prior studies have shown that most nephrologists self-report being comfortable with providing CKM care, but analyses of patient experiences and practice patterns suggest that CKM is inconsistently discussed or rarely framed as a viable choice.9,19,36,37,38,39,40,41 Our results support these findings, although clinical documentation may not fully reflect the nuances of patients’ conversations with clinicians.

Last, one-third of patients who chose CKM lacked a documented health care proxy, and few received palliative care referrals. These findings highlight inconsistencies in the delivery of CKM, as ACP and palliative care are essential components of the CKM pathway.10,42,43,44,45 CKM is an active care pathway that requires delivery of its core components, as emphasized in clinical guidelines.10,46

Our study has several implications. First, it highlights why older adults choose CKM, emphasizing the need to explore patients’ QoL perspectives, self-assessments of health, the experiences of kidney therapy from trusted others, and caregiver support.28,47,48,49 Second, our findings emphasize the need for policy-driven cultural change in ESKD care to promote informed, goal-aligned decisions and train nephrologists in both life-prolonging and QoL-enhancing options.50,51,52 Such changes require consistent clinician training in communication and SDM, as well as reimbursement changes to incentivize CKM.29,51,53 Third, inconsistent ACP completion and palliative care referrals among patients choosing CKM may reflect fragmented care, uncertainty about ACP’s role in CKD care, and unclear responsibility for initiating ACP discussions.54 These deficiencies suggest a need to build clinical infrastructure to consistently deliver high-quality CKM.19,20,35,37,38,55,56 Potential strategies include identifying local CKM champions, automated ACP and palliative care referral reminders, partnerships with primary care and palliative care clinicians, and standardized checklists and workflows.27

To our knowledge, this is one of the first US-based studies of older patients with ESKD that explores reasons for choosing CKM, the influence of caregivers in decision-making conversations, and the variability in nephrologist support on the CKM pathway. Our analysis is grounded in direct patient quotes and clinician documentation, offering an authentic reflection of clinical practice compared with studies relying on nephrologists’ self-reports.19,36,37,57 Importantly, we captured ACP and palliative care referrals—data not well described in prior studies.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. Our study is a secondary analysis of 2 randomized clinical trials rather than a cohort designed to study CKM, limiting the sample to trial participants. The sample was drawn from 1 academic and 2 community-affiliated hospitals in Rochester, New York. Although modest, our sample is among the largest reported on this topic. We included only patients who chose CKM; therefore, our findings may not apply to patients choosing dialysis or to caregiver and nephrologist roles in those decisions. Finally, most participants (31 of 35) had at least a high school education, limiting generalizability to the broader US ESKD population.

Conclusions

In this qualitative study of older patients, their caregivers, and their nephrologists, patients reported many reasons, including avoiding additional health care burdens and witnessing loved ones' experiencing, for choosing CKM. These findings suggest that high-quality CKM requires clinical, research, and policy initiatives that strengthen caregiver support, clinician training, and clinical infrastructure to facilitate goal-concordant, patient-centered care.
