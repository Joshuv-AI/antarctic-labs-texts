Systematic review and meta-analysis of toluene concentration: a comparative assessment of indoor air quality in residential buildings
Authors: Ali Momen, Ali Taherkhani, Kosar Shahabi, Roohollah Rostami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVolatile organic compounds (VOCs), particularly toluene, are major contributors to indoor air quality degradation in residential settings, yet comprehensive assessment of global residential toluene concentrations remains limited. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to: (1) quantify pooled toluene concentrations in residential indoor air, (2) examine temporal trends, and (3) identify factors influencing concentrations.MethodsFollowing PRISMA 2020 guidelines, we systematically searched PubMed, ScienceDirect, and SpringerLink databases through April 13, 2023. Studies quantitatively reporting toluene concentrations in residential indoor air were included. We conducted random-effects meta-analysis, temporal trend analysis, and moderator analyses for geographical region, smoking status, and season. Health risk was assessed using hazard quotient (HQ) calculations.ResultsTwenty-three studies met inclusion criteria (from 444 initially identified). The pooled toluene concentration was 27.13 µg/m3 [95% CI: 20.05 – 34.20 µg/m3], showing a statistically significant decreasing temporal trend (β = -0.034, p = 0.004). Rural areas exhibiting significantly higher levels than urban areas (p < 0.001). Smoking status and seasonal variations showed no significant impact. The non-cancer health risk (HQ) values were low (< 1) for all of the studies (mean HQ = 3.10 × 10⁻4), and publication bias was detected.ConclusionsResidential toluene concentrations have declined over the past two decades but vary significantly by geographical region. Current exposure levels appear to pose minimal acute health risks, though publication bias warrants cautious interpretation. Standardized sampling methodologies and larger sample sizes are needed for improved risk assessment.Graphical AbstractTable of Contents (TOC) Graphic Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-26939-8.

Introduction

Recently, there has been an increasing concern among the public about the threat linked to poor indoor air quality (IAQ) [1]. The impact of indoor air pollution is prolonged and consequential, potentially resulting in various health issues like discomfort, temporary illness, disability, diseases, and, in extreme cases, even death [2–4]. IAQ is influenced by various factors including indoor and outdoor sources, ventilation, and building design [5–7]. Despite the common belief that indoor spaces provide safety against harmful pollutants [8], scientific evidence illustrates that indoor air can be far more polluted than outdoor air in large industrialized cities [6, 8, 9]. In modern lifestyles, where individuals typically spend approximately 90% of their lives indoors, indoor air quality is raised as a critical concern affecting both human health and the environment [10, 11]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), indoor air pollution contributes to over 4 million premature deaths annually, with a significant majority affecting children [2, 12]. Examining indoor pollutants with the most harmful effects on human health is crucial for understanding the correlation between IAQ and well-being [4, 6]. Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are recognized as a major indoor pollutant, with evidence suggesting their global significance [7, 9, 12, 13]. Moreover, research indicates that indoor air serves as the primary pathway for human exposure to VOCs, often hosting higher concentrations than outdoor air [11, 14]. The VOC category, the BTEX group (benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylene) has created specific concerns [9]. Toluene (C6H5CH3), is an aromatic hydrocarbon widely used as a solvent in different products. According to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's Guidelines for Carcinogen Risk Assessment (USEPA), the current data are insufficient to assess the carcinogenic potential of toluene definitively for humans. Furthermore, existing evidence from experimental animal studies indicates a lack of carcinogenicity associated with toluene exposure [15–17]. Indoor sources of toluene include building materials (e.g., solvent- and water-based adhesives, floor coverings, paint, chipboard), consumer and automotive products (e.g., cleaners, polishes, adhesive products, oils, greases, lubricants), and environmental tobacco smoke [9, 11, 18]. Exposure to toluene has been shown to cause eye, nose, and throat irritation as well as headaches, dizziness, and feelings of intoxication. It has also been linked to neurological effects including poorer performances in short-term memory, attention and concentration, visual scanning, perceptual movement speeds, and finger dexterity in completing physical tasks as well as negative effects on color vision and hearing capacity [2, 19]. In controlled exposure studies, healthy adults exposed to toluene for 4.5 to 7 h reported increased eye, nose, and throat irritation as well as headaches, dizziness, or feelings of intoxication at concentrations ranging from 189 to 566 mg/m3 [20].

Environmental tobacco smoke (ETS) is a well-documented source of indoor volatile organic compounds, including toluene [21]. Given that smoking behavior in residential settings may substantially influence indoor toluene concentrations, we designed our search strategy to capture both general residential indoor air studies and those specifically investigating ETS contributions. This comprehensive approach enabled us to: (1) compile comprehensive data on residential toluene concentrations across diverse exposure scenarios, and (2) examine smoking status as a potential moderator of indoor toluene levels through subgroup analysis.

The primary objective of this research was to assess toluene concentrations in residential buildings, with additional aims to analyze variations across different analytical variables including smoking status, seasonal variation, and geographical region (urban/rural/sub-urban) to identify key factors influencing indoor toluene concentrations to inform potential mitigation strategies.

Material and methods

Search strategy and information sources

A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, ScienceDirect, and SpringerLink databases to identify studies published from inception through April 13, 2023. The included studies covered data collected from indoor residential environments over a 21-year period from 2003 to 2023. The search strategy employed combinations of terms including “BTEX”, “Indoor, “Cigarette”, “Waterpipe”, “Shisha”, “Hookah”, “Tobacco”, and “ETS”. Smoking-related terms were included because environmental tobacco smoke is a significant indoor source of toluene and other VOCs, and we sought to comprehensively capture residential toluene measurements regardless of smoking status. This broad strategy enabled subsequent subgroup analysis comparing toluene concentrations in smoking versus non-smoking households.

Detailed search strategies for each database are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [22], ensuring transparency and reproducibility. The research question was formulated using the PICO framework (Supplementary Table S2).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included original, peer-reviewed articles published in English that explicitly quantified toluene concentrations in residential indoor air environments. Studies conducted in industrial zones, adjacent to industrial activities or major roadways, or within settings with potentially elevated toluene exposure (e.g., workplaces, restaurants) were excluded, except for designated smoking areas in residences. Investigations assessing toluene as a biomarker in biological matrices or measuring toluene within tobacco-product emissions (mainstream or sidestream) were also excluded. Abstracts, editorials, conference proceedings, and review articles were not eligible for inclusion.

Study selection

After deduplication, two reviewers (A.T. and K.S.) independently screened titles and abstracts of all identified records against predefined eligibility criteria. Full-text articles were obtained for all records that could not be confidently excluded based on title and abstract alone. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (A.M.). To ensure comprehensive coverage and minimize inadvertent exclusions, articles initially excluded by the first two reviewers underwent reassessment by the third reviewer.

Data extraction

Following the selection process, we meticulously extracted pertinent information regarding the study characteristics, including the region, season, and smoking status. We also systematically collected comprehensive data, particularly focusing on exposure to toluene in indoor air. In instances where seasons were not explicitly mentioned, we categorized autumn and winter as cold seasons, while spring and summer were designated as warm seasons. Finally, we extracted the outcomes of interest (the toluene concentration) for subsequent analysis.

Quality assessments

The methodological rigor of each included study was evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist for analytical cross-sectional studies [23]. This comprehensive instrument comprises eight domains, each addressing a distinct aspect of study validity and reporting transparency. Detailed definitions and decision rules for each domain are provided in Table S3.

In the initial phase of our assessment, we comprehensively evaluated the explicit inclusion criteria and sampling frame (Domain 1) and the detailed description of the study setting and participant characteristics (Domain 2) to assess external validity. Subsequently, we examined the validity and reliability of toluene exposure measurement methods (Domain 3) and ensured that outcomes were determined using objective and standardized criteria (Domain 4). Furthermore, we determined whether potential confounders were identified a priori (Domain 5) and implemented appropriate strategies, such as statistical adjustment or stratification, to mitigate their impact (Domain 6). Finally, we assessed the appropriateness of outcome measurement methods (Domain 7) and the suitability of statistical analyses for the study design and data distribution (Domain 8), thereby completing our domain-level appraisal.

Each domain was evaluated and scored by a single reviewer (R.R.) based on pre-defined criteria. Ratings were categorized as “Yes” if the criterion was clearly met, “No” if not addressed, “Unclear” if insufficiently described, and “Not applicable” where relevant. Following domain-level assessments, an overall appraisal was conducted to determine whether each study should be included, excluded, or flagged for additional information. Where reporting was insufficient to make a confident judgment, studies were marked as “Seek further information,” although no additional contact with study authors was pursued.

Statistical analysis

We conducted the statistical analysis of the data using R software version 4.3.3. Descriptive statistics were utilized to furnish a succinct summary of the data's central tendency and dispersion. Since the toluene concentration of indoor residential buildings did not show normal distribution (Shapiro–Wilk test, p < 0.050), we used the Kruskal–Wallis and Wilcoxon tests.

Temporal trend analysis

Data for this study were collected from indoor environments over a 21-year period spanning from 2003 to 2023. Prior to statistical analysis, indoor toluene concentration data were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, which if it revealed significant deviation from normal distribution (p < 0.050), logarithmic transformation (base 10) was applied.

The core trend analysis was conducted using ordinary least squares (OLS) linear regression with the toluene concentrations as the dependent variable and year as the independent variable. Statistical significance was evaluated at α = 0.050.

To ensure the robustness of findings, particularly given the potential influence of outliers in environmental data, two additional regression approaches were implemented. First, MM-estimator robust regression was performed using the lmrob function, which combines high breakdown point estimation with efficient M-estimation to provide resistance to outliers while maintaining statistical efficiency. Second, Huber M-estimation was conducted using the rlm function, employing a modified loss function that reduces the influence of outliers compared to ordinary least squares regression.

Time series analysis complemented the regression approach by accounting for potential temporal autocorrelation in the data. The toluene concentrations were converted to a time series object with annual frequency, and stationarity was assessed using the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test with a deterministic trend component (p < 0.050). Optimal time series modeling was performed using the auto.arima function, which employs a stepwise approach to select the most appropriate ARIMA (Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average) model based on information criteria.

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.3) with appropriate packages for each analytical technique.

Meta-analysis

We extracted the mean concentration, standard deviation (SD), and sample size (n) of toluene from the included studies to compute the effect size. However, for studies lacking sufficient data (e.g., sample size, dispersion indices), determining the effect size was not feasible. This methodology allowed for the inclusion of studies with incomplete reporting of SD values into the overall analysis, thereby augmenting the comprehensiveness of the study findings. We conducted a thorough statistical analysis of the meta-analysis, assessing the heterogeneity of the studies through the reporting of Cochran's Q test, I2 statistics, and tau2. If the p of heterogeneity, which represents the heterogeneity of the available associations, was higher than 0.1, we applied the fixed effect model. Otherwise, we used the random effect model [24]. Finally, a random-effects meta-analysis was conducted to synthesize the toluene concentration data across studies. The standard error (SE) for each study was calculated using the reported SD and n according to the formula SE = SD/√n. Studies with missing SE values were excluded from the analysis to ensure statistical validity.

The meta-analysis was performed using the "metafor," "metaforest," and “forestplot” packages in R 4.3.3. A random-effects model was fitted using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation method. Forest plots were generated to visualize the effect sizes and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals for each study, as well as the overall pooled estimate.

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

One or more studies with extreme effect sizes that don't quite fit in can also cause between-study heterogeneity. Especially when the quality of these studies is low or the studies are very small, this can distort our pooled effect estimate. Therefore, it's a good idea to reevaluate the pooled effect after removing such outliers from the analysis [25]. Outlier detection as sensitivity analysis was performed using confidence interval overlap criteria. Studies were classified as outliers if their 95% confidence intervals (concentration of toluene ± 1.96 × SE) did not overlap with the pooled estimate's confidence interval. The meta-analysis was then repeated after excluding identified outliers to assess their impact on the overall results.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to examine potential moderating effects of smoking status, season, and geographical region. These variables were treated as categorical moderators in separate mixed-effects meta-regression models. The significance of moderator effects was assessed using omnibus tests of moderators.

Publication bias assessment

Publication bias was evaluated using two complementary approaches: 1) Visual inspection of funnel plots, which displayed study effect sizes against their standard errors. 2) Egger's regression test for funnel plot asymmetry was conducted to formally assess potential publication bias. The significance level was set at α = 0.05 for all statistical tests.

Study selection

After removing 8 duplicates, 436 unique records underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 375 records were excluded as clearly not meeting eligibility criteria (primarily due to wrong outcome measures, non-residential settings, or non-original research). The remaining 61 full-text articles were assessed in detail for eligibility. Of these, 38 articles were excluded for the following reasons: wrong setting/population, insufficient quantitative data, reporting only biological markers, and non-English language. Quality assessment

Quality assessment was conducted by R.R. based on the criteria of the cross-sectional JBI checklist, encompassing eight domains (Fig. 3).Fig. 3The quality assessment of studies in each domain (“Y” stands for “Yes”, “N” represents “No”, and “?” signifies “Unclear”)

The quality assessment of studies in each domain (“Y” stands for “Yes”, “N” represents “No”, and “?” signifies “Unclear”)

Accordingly, the best definitions were related to domains two, three, and seven, with all studies (100.0%) fulfilling them (Figure S1). This indicates that the included studies have been reasonably successful in adequately describing their subject matter and methodological characteristics. The analysis of the JBI checklist domains in the study reveals a mixed landscape of methodological rigor. It's clear that methodological standards are being followed in areas like subject and setting description, exposure measurement validity, and outcome measurement validity. However, it's also clear that there are problems in areas like making inclusion criteria clear and coming up with ways to deal with confounding factors. While these limitations exist in some primary studies, they do not compromise our systematic review's validity because: (1) pooling 23 independent studies through meta-analysis provides more robust estimates that mitigate individual study weaknesses, (2) all studies adequately addressed the critical domains for measuring indoor toluene concentrations, and (3) our subgroup analyses inherently controlled for some confounding factors.

Trending of toluene concentration within residential buildings

Initial analysis of indoor toluene concentrations revealed a non-normal distribution (Shapiro–Wilk test, p < 0.001). This significant deviation from normality necessitated logarithmic transformation (base 10) of the toluene concentration data to enable valid statistical analysis. The transformation corrected the skewness commonly observed in environmental concentration data, aligning with established practices in air quality research [54].

A linear regression model analyzing the correlation between log-transformed toluene concentrations and year revealed a statistically significant decreasing trend (β = −0.034, SE = 0.011, p = 0.004). The 95% confidence interval for the year coefficient [−0.056, −0.011] excludes zero, further confirming the temporal trend’s significance. This finding suggests an approximate 3.4% annual reduction in indoor toluene concentrations over the study period (2003–2023) (Fig. 4). However, the model explains only a modest portion of the total variance (R2 = 0.083, Adjusted R2 = 0.074), indicating that while the decreasing trend is statistically significant, the year alone accounts for less than 10% of the variation in indoor toluene levels. This suggests that other factors not included in the model, such as building characteristics, ventilation systems, indoor activities, or changes in regulatory policies, likely contribute significantly to the observed variability.Fig. 4The trend of logarithmic indoor toluene concentration of residential buildings over years

The trend of logarithmic indoor toluene concentration of residential buildings over years

A time series presents of log-transformed toluene concentrations from 2003 to 2023 revealed substantial year-to-year variability superimposed on an overall decreasing trend. The ADF test yielded a test statistic of −3.97, exceeding the 5% critical value (−3.60) but falling short of the 1% critical value (−4.38). This result suggests that the time series is stationary after accounting for the deterministic trend. The auto.arima function identified an ARIMA(0,0,0) model with a non-zero mean as the optimal fit for the time series. This implies that after accounting for the mean level (1.31), the yearly observations of log-transformed toluene concentrations exhibit essentially white noise behavior without significant autocorrelation structure. The absence of autoregressive or moving average components indicates that past values do not significantly influence future values beyond the overall mean level, underscoring the stochastic nature of year-to-year fluctuations in indoor toluene concentrations. The model’s error metrics (RMSE = 0.30, MAPE = 21.6%) and information criteria (AIC = 13.36, BIC = 15.45) suggest a reasonable fit, despite its simplicity.

In light of the potential influence of outliers in environmental datasets, two robust regression approaches were employed: Least Absolute Deviation (LAD) and Huber M-estimation. The LAD-based robust regression (MM-method) confirmed the significant negative trend in toluene concentrations (β = −8.43 × 10–5, SE = 2.67 × 10–5, p = 0.002). Furthermore, the Huber M-estimation provided comparable results (β = −0.0001, t = −2.78), further validating the observed decreasing trend, even in the presence of potential outliers.

The concentration of toluene within residential buildings

According to the results, the concentration of toluene in indoor residential buildings was 31.00 ± 34.00 µg/m3. Indoor air quality studies have revealed significant variability in VOC concentrations across residential buildings.

From 23 studies (51 observations), 18 studies (44 observations) had sufficient data (mean of toluene, standard deviation of toluene, and sample size) to estimate the pooled concentration of toluene. The pooled concentration of toluene in indoor residential buildings was 27.13 µg/m3 (95% CI: 20.05 to 34.20 µg/m3) (Fig. 5).Fig. 5Forest plot of toluene concentration in indoor residential buildings (µg/m.3)

The high heterogeneity (I2 = 99.99%; τ2 = 530.81, and p-value of Cochran’s Q test < 0.001) underscores the substantial variability among studies. As a sensitivity analysis, we exclude outlier from our analysis. Discussion

Pooled toluene concentrations and comparison with guidelines

This systematic review and meta-analysis revealed a pooled toluene concentration of 27.13 µg/m3 (95% CI: 20.05–34.20 µg/m3) in residential indoor environments. All included studies demonstrated hazard quotient (HQ) values below the permissible limit (HQ < 1), with a mean calculated HQ of 3.10 × 10⁻4 ± 3.40 × 10⁻4.

In comparison to other indoor environments, residential toluene concentrations are substantially lower than those in newly constructed or renovated buildings. During the initial six months following building occupancy, toluene consistently emerges as one of the most prevalent VOCs. Riveron et al. [57] reported peak concentrations of 577 µg/m3 in newly constructed office buildings [57], while Shin and Jo [58] noted 184 µg/m3 during pre-occupancy stages of new apartments [58]. Liang et al. [59] observed levels of 664 µg/m3 after door installations, 634 µg/m3 post-cupboard addition, and 447 µg/m3 following furniture placement [59]. Public spaces in Korea exhibited seasonally elevated concentrations reaching 316.8 µg/m3 during summer [60], while newly renovated offices in Poland ranged from 1.4 to 125.3 µg/m3 [61]. Transportation microenvironments show variable levels, with aircraft cabins ranging from 0 to 180 µg/m3 [62]. Schools present concentrations of 2.14 to 8.25 µg/m3, aligning with the lower end of residential exposure [63].

Geographical variation: urban vs. rural differences

Geographical region emerged as a significant moderator of indoor toluene concentrations (p < 0.001), with rural areas exhibiting higher concentrations compared to urban environments. This counterintuitive finding can be attributed to several factors specific to rural areas.

Rural households predominantly rely on biomass fuels (wood, dung, agricultural waste) for cooking and heating, leading to substantial VOC emissions including toluene [64–66]. Incomplete combustion of solid fuels in traditional stoves contributes significantly to indoor pollution, with research establishing correlations between coal and biomass combustion and elevated VOC concentrations [67, 68]. Agricultural activities such as crop burning and pesticide application also release VOCs that infiltrate indoor spaces [69, 70].

Unlike urban residences where building codes increasingly mandate mechanical ventilation systems that provide reliable, controllable air exchange independent of weather or occupant behavior, rural dwellings predominantly rely on natural ventilation through windows and unsealed envelopes, a passive approach that proves inconsistent and often inadequate for mitigating high pollutant loads from biomass combustion [71, 72]. Mechanical ventilation systems demonstrably outperform natural ventilation in maintaining consistent indoor air quality, with studies showing up to 65.0% greater reduction in fine particle concentrations and more stable pollutant dilution regardless of seasonal constraints or occupant habits [73, 74]. Seasonal variations further influence exposure, as colder temperatures necessitate increased use of wood-burning stoves while simultaneously limiting pollutant dispersion [75, 76].

While overall HQ values remained below 1, several considerations warrant attention regarding rural areas: (1) HQ calculations are based on average exposures and may not reflect peak concentrations during specific activities, (2) reference concentrations may not adequately protect susceptible individuals, and (3) concurrent exposure to multiple pollutants from biomass combustion can produce cumulative effects not captured by single-compound HQ assessments.

Temporal trends and their implications

The consistent decline in indoor toluene concentrations over the past two decades demonstrates the efficacy of air quality enhancement measures. This trend can be explained by established mass transfer models correlating emissions from indoor sources with measured air concentrations [52, 77].

The mass-balance model establishes the relationship between emission rate (E) and indoor concentration:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{dC}{dt}=\frac{E}{V}-(\lambda +k)C$$\end{document}where C is indoor concentration, V is room volume, λ is air exchange rate, and k encompasses removal processes such as surface sorption and chemical reactions [52, 77]. Since emission rate directly governs pollutant load, reducing E through source control using low-emitting materials yields more sustained and energy-efficient reductions than increasing ventilation alone. While enhancing λ can dilute existing pollutants, it cannot fully counteract continuous emissions and often entails higher energy costs. Therefore, emission-reduction strategies have proven more effective in driving observed declines [52, 77].

Wolkoff et al. [78] demonstrated that temperature and humidity dependencies influence long-term concentration trends, with effects varying by building product and VOC type. These models predict that emission-reduction strategies targeting source materials have more sustained effects than environmental controls alone [79].

The observed downward trend aligns with findings from other longitudinal studies. A systematic review of indoor VOCs in China (2000–2021) revealed similar declining trends attributed to enhanced ventilation and reduced emissions from building materials [80]. A 15-year German study observed substantial reductions in aromatic VOCs [53]. However, modest R2 values in our analysis indicate substantial unexplained variance, highlighting the complexity of indoor air quality dynamics and suggesting stochastic year-to-year fluctuations from unaccounted factors.

The decline is attributed to regulatory interventions, technological advancements, and evolving consumer behaviors. Regulatory measures have imposed VOC emission limitations on building materials and consumer products, supported by labeling and certification programs [81]. Indoor air quality management legislation, such as Taiwan's IAQM Act, has enhanced monitoring and regulation [82]. Organizations including the EU and WHO have established comprehensive VOC guidelines, reinforced by national policies like the UK's Clean Air Strategy [83].

Advancements in building practices have substantially contributed to declines. Sorptive building materials that absorb VOCs, combined with optimized ventilation strategies, have effectively reduced toluene levels [84, 85]. Sustainable building practices incorporating low-VOC materials and energy-efficient designs have been promoted through green building certifications [86]. Integration of renewable energy systems and advanced materials has enhanced indoor air quality [87], accelerated by growing consumer awareness driving market preferences toward low-VOC products [88, 89].

Non-significant factors: season and smoking status

Environmental tobacco smoke (ETS) is a recognized indoor source of toluene [21]. Nevertheless, our meta-analysis did not identify a statistically significant association between smoking status and residential toluene concentrations (p = 0.488). This finding should be interpreted cautiously, as fewer than half of the included studies (48%, n = 11) reported smoking-related information, limiting statistical power.

Several contextual and behavioral factors may attenuate the measurable impact of ETS on indoor toluene levels. Households with smokers may adopt compensatory behaviors, such as opening windows, increasing mechanical ventilation, or restricting smoking to specific areas, which can substantially reduce indoor pollutant accumulation [90–92].. Such adaptive behaviors were rarely quantified in the included studies, resulting in potential residual confounding.

Moreover, indoor toluene originates from multiple concurrent sources, including building materials, furnishings, and household activities such as painting, solvent use, and cooking [93, 94]. In environments with substantial non-smoking-related emissions, the relative contribution of ETS may be diluted at the population level. While our pooled analysis suggests no consistent smoking effect across studies, individual investigations continue to document elevated toluene concentrations in smoking households. This discrepancy indicates that the influence of ETS is context-dependent and may vary according to building characteristics, ventilation patterns, and measurement protocols.

Overall, our findings suggest that smoking status is not a universal determinant of residential toluene concentrations, although it may remain an important exposure source in specific household settings.

Our meta-analysis did not detect a statistically significant effect of season on residential toluene concentrations (p = 0.735), indicating relative stability across seasonal cycles. This pattern suggests that indoor sources play a dominant role compared with outdoor influences, consistent with evidence showing that emissions from building materials, furniture, and household products persist year-round [95–97].

The influence of environmental conditions on VOC emissions can be described using temperature–humidity relationships. Xiong et al. [98] proposed a mechanistic emission model [98]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[E={E}_{1}{T}^{0.75}exp\left({E}_{2}RH-\frac{{E}_{3}}{T}\right)]$$\end{document}

Application of this model to Beijing’s climate indicated that the emission-rate ratio between summer and winter ranged from 0.63 to 1.07, corresponding to a modest increase of approximately 7% under high-temperature and high-humidity summer conditions [98]. This limited seasonal contrast is consistent with our meta-analytic findings and supports the use of time-invariant emission factors in climate-controlled residential environments.

The absence of a consistent seasonal effect at the meta-analytic level can be explained by several interacting mechanisms. In colder seasons, reduced ventilation rates intended to conserve heat may elevate indoor pollutant concentrations [99]. However, lower temperatures can simultaneously suppress evaporative emissions from indoor materials [100]. In warmer seasons, higher temperatures promote VOC volatilization, but this effect may be counterbalanced by increased natural ventilation through window opening [101, 102]. The net seasonal pattern therefore reflects a balance between emission and dilution processes, which varies across climates, housing types, and occupant behaviors.

In addition, only 57% (n = 13) of included studies reported seasonal information, further limiting power. Among these studies, the results were inconclusive, with some reporting elevated winter concentrations and others reporting elevated summer concentrations. This heterogeneity implies that seasonal effects are highly context-dependent rather than universally applicable.

Taken together, these findings indicate that season is not a robust predictor of residential toluene concentrations across diverse geographical and climatic settings. Instead, indoor emission sources, building characteristics, and ventilation practices appear to exert stronger and more consistent influences.

Health risk assessment interpretation

Calculated HQ values below 1 indicate that current residential toluene exposure levels pose minimal immediate health risks based on established reference concentrations. However, comprehensive understanding requires consideration of both acute and chronic exposure effects across different populations.

Short-term toluene exposure can cause immediate respiratory and central nervous system effects [103]. Inhalation exposure ranging from 150 to 495 mg/m3 results in headaches, dizziness, degradation of color vision, and impaired neurological and psychomotor functioning [104].

Prolonged exposure entails multiple risks. Chronic inhalation exposure correlates with neurobehavioral manifestations such as diminished cognitive function, memory impairments, and reduced attention span [105–107]. Toluene exposure may manifest reproductive effects [108], with population-based cohort studies indicating heightened risk of preterm birth (OR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.12, 1.63) in mothers exposed to concentrations exceeding 0.36 μg/m3 [109]. Chronic exposure disrupts lipid metabolism, resulting in elevated urinary benzyl mercapturic acid levels associated with increased risk of hypertriglyceridemia (OR = 1.58, 95% CI: 1.13, 2.21) while mitigating risk of high LDL cholesterol (OR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.43, 0.89) [110]. Blood toluene levels associate with elevated cardiovascular disease risk, including congestive heart failure, angina pectoris, and heart attack, with highest quartile of BTEX mixture exposure demonstrating significantly elevated CVD risk (OR = 1.64, 95% CI: 1.23, 2.19) [111]. VOC exposure including toluene has been linked to obesity and abdominal obesity, evidenced by positive correlations between urinary metabolites and BMI/waist circumference [112]. Occupational toluene exposure associates with increased bladder cancer risk (OR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.06, 2.43) [113].

It is important to note that HQ-based assessment is a screening-level approach that provides an initial indication of potential health concern but has several limitations: (1) it relies on chronic exposure assumptions and does not account for acute peak exposures, (2) it uses population-average reference doses that may not protect highly sensitive or vulnerable individuals, (3) it assumes additive rather than synergistic toxicity when multiple pollutants are present, and (4) it does not account for individual variability in exposure patterns, breathing rates, or susceptibility. Therefore, HQ values should be interpreted as conservative screening estimates rather than definitive risk characterizations.

Study limitations and heterogeneity

This study presents several significant limitations. Firstly, its focus on residential buildings restricts applicability to other indoor settings such as industrial environments. Secondly, the correlational nature precludes definitive causal conclusions regarding toluene concentration trends.

Key data gaps include insufficient information on building characteristics, occupancy patterns, and emission sources. Missing variables encompass interior decoration practices, furniture age and composition, renovation activities, and occupant behaviors such as ventilation usage. These factors substantially influence indoor toluene levels through emissions from paints, adhesives, and finishes.

Statistical limitations include potential inadequacy of p-values for small sample sizes and inability to capture seasonal variations due to annual measurement frequency. Furthermore, the white noise pattern identified by ARIMA modeling suggests that significant temporal dynamics occur at shorter time scales than measured.

Implications for policy and future research

The substantial geographical disparities identified emphasize the necessity of targeted interventions, particularly in rural areas. Effective mitigation strategies encompass transitioning to cleaner fuels such as liquefied petroleum gas and electricity. Accelerating the shift from solid biomass and kerosene to LPG has demonstrated substantial reductions in household air pollution exposures, with field trials showing 50.0–70.0% lower personal exposure to fine particulate matter and associated combustion byproducts following sustained LPG adoption [114, 115]. Policy interventions coupled with subsidies and educational campaigns can substantially diminish VOC emissions [64, 116, 117].

Structural modifications and engineering controls can substantially reduce indoor pollutant concentrations. In areas with acceptable outdoor air quality, improved natural ventilation through window installation and usage during high-emission activities can be effective. However, in regions with poor outdoor air quality, source control measures such as kitchen exhaust hoods, portable air cleaners with activated carbon filters, and mechanical ventilation systems with filtration are more appropriate. Additionally, installing chimneys for combustion appliances and maintaining proper stove maintenance can reduce emissions at the source [69, 118, 119].

Raising awareness about health hazards associated with biomass combustion and the significance of proper ventilation can encourage behavioral modifications and promote adoption of cleaner practices [64, 116].

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide a comprehensive assessment of toluene concentrations in residential indoor air globally. Our findings directly address the stated objectives:

First, regarding pooled toluene concentrations, we determined a combined estimate of 27.13 µg/m3 (95% CI: 20.05–34.20 µg/m3) across 23 studies. This concentration is substantially below the WHO guideline value of 260 µg/m3 for weekly exposure, suggesting that acute health risks from toluene in typical residential settings are minimal under current conditions.

Second, temporal trend analysis revealed a statistically significant declining trend in residential toluene concentrations over the 21-year study period (2003–2023), with an approximate 3.4% annual reduction (β = −0.034, p = 0.004). This favorable trend likely reflects improved building standards, reduced VOC emissions from consumer products, and increased environmental awareness. However, the trend explains only 8.3% of total variance, indicating that other factors significantly influence indoor toluene levels.

Third, moderator analysis identified geographical region as the primary determinant of indoor toluene concentrations (p < 0.001), with rural areas exhibiting significantly higher levels than urban settings. This pattern likely reflects differences in building construction, ventilation practices, and heating/cooking fuel sources. In contrast, neither seasonal variation nor smoking status demonstrated significant effects on indoor toluene concentrations in the pooled analysis, though individual studies reported variable patterns.

Health risk assessment using hazard quotient calculations indicated low non-cancer risk across all studies (mean HQ = 3.10 × 10⁻4, all HQ < 1). However, several important limitations warrant consideration. Publication bias was detected, suggesting potential overrepresentation of studies reporting lower concentrations or certain geographical regions. Substantial heterogeneity among studies (I2 > 90%) and methodological limitations in primary studies (inconsistent sampling strategies, limited confounder control) introduce uncertainty in pooled estimates.

Future research should prioritize: (1) standardized sampling protocols and analytical methods to improve comparability, (2) larger sample sizes with stratified sampling across socioeconomic and building types, (3) longitudinal studies to characterize temporal variability and assess intervention effectiveness, (4) comprehensive VOC profiles rather than single-compound analyses, and (5) research in underrepresented regions (Africa, Americas) to improve global coverage.

In conclusion, while current residential toluene exposure levels appear to pose minimal acute health risks based on hazard quotient assessments, significant geographical variation exists that warrants targeted interventions in high-concentration settings. The declining temporal trend is encouraging but should not diminish efforts to further reduce indoor air pollution, particularly in rural areas and developing regions where concentrations remain elevated. Context-specific mitigation strategies that prioritize source control, appropriate ventilation, and air purification can further mitigate exposure and associated health risks, particularly in rural areas.
