Feasibility of computed tomography-derived surgical margin assessment in an ex vivo sublobar lung resection model
Authors: Shinsuke Kitazawa, Nicholas Bernards, Alexander Gregor, Yuki Sata, Yoshihisa Hiraishi, Hiroyuki Ogawa, Takamasa Koga, Tsukasa Ishiwata, Masato Aragaki, Fumi Yokote, Andrew Effat, Kate Kazlovich, Robert Weersink, Michael Cabanero, Yukio Sato, Kazuhiro Yasufuku
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOBJECTIVESComputed tomography (CT) imaging of a sublobar resection specimen may inform intraoperative surgical margin assessment. However, consistency with final pathological margins has not been previously evaluated. In this study, we investigated the concordance between surgical margin measurements by CT versus pathology measurements using an ex vivo sublobar lung resection model.METHODSPig lung wedge samples containing agarose pseudotumours were harvested. CT images were acquired following specimen inflation. The specimen was bisected along the same plane observed by CT for accurate comparison with pathological surgical margin measurement. The bisected samples were then fixed in formalin before preparing haematoxylin & eosin slides. Surgical margin length at four distinct stages (CT, gross pre-formalin fixation, gross post-formalin fixation and pathology) were measured and compared.RESULTSA total of 50 lung specimens were analysed. After specimen processing, Surgical margin length decreased in 94% (47/50) and increased in 6% (3/50) of samples. Mean surgical margin lengths were as follows: CT 14.0 mm (range: 4.5–28.3 mm), gross pre-formalin fixation 13.0 mm (range: 4.0–25.0 mm), gross post-formalin fixation 12.1 mm (range: 2.5–26.0 mm) and pathology 10.9 mm (range: 1.0–23.4 mm). There was an average −23.8% (range: +11 to −82%) change in surgical margin length from CT to final pathology (P < 0.001).CONCLUSIONSWhile CT-based surgical margin measurement is feasible, we observed an average 23.8% discordance when compared to final pathology measurement. Surgeons must be aware that the CT-derived surgical margin generally overestimates the pathology-derived surgical margin.Keywords: lung cancer, sublobar resection, surgical margin, computed tomography

INTRODUCTION

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer deaths worldwide [1]. The high mortality is mainly attributable to most lung cancers being detected at advanced stages of disease. Patients can nonetheless live cancer-free if the diagnosis is made at a curative stage and timely treatment is administered.

The increased prevalence of computed tomography (CT) screening and advances in diagnostic technology, particularly the widespread use of thin-slice CT, have enabled increased detection of early-stage lung cancers [2, 3]. This has renewed interest in sublobar lung resection as an alternative to traditional anatomic lobectomy. The randomized controlled trial JCOG0802/WJOG4607L demonstrated that segmentectomy was superior to lobectomy in patients with stage IA non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) in terms of both overall survival and postoperative lung function [4]. Additionally, the randomized phase III CALGB 140503 trial showed the efficacy and non-inferiority of sublobar resection, including wedge resection, for stage IA NSCLC [5]. Therefore, small-sized lung cancers could be optimal candidates for sublobar resection.

In performing sublobar resection, especially for non-palpable or non-visible tumours, both precise nodule localization and appropriate operative approach are required. Surgical margin (SM), which is defined as the amount of normal lung tissue excised around the tumour, is a key factor impacting loco-regional recurrence [6]. The threshold for an adequate SM in sublobar resection has been defined as either (i) at least 20 mm or (ii) greater than the size of the nodule [7]. If the SM is insufficient, reoperation or adjuvant radiation may be required.

To date, there is no established method for reliably assessing whether a sufficient SM has been obtained during surgery. Although intraoperative touch preparation cytology has been an option to confirm absence of disease at the resection margin, this technique cannot tell how much SM length has been obtained [8]. Intraoperative frozen section for the evaluation of SMs constitutes standard practice in many pathology departments but it seems unjustified due to low rates of positive margins [9].

In breast cancer, CT scanning of surgical specimens enabled confirmation of successful lesion excision and assessment of the SM, both of which are useful for intraoperative decision-making regarding the need for further excision [10]. Immediate CT imaging of a resected lung specimen may similarly enable accurate evaluation of the SM while surgery is ongoing. Should the imaging SM be deemed insufficient, an immediate margin revision would be able to be performed, theoretically eliminating the need for further local therapy and reducing the risk of loco-regional recurrence. There have been two studies that assessed radiological evaluation of fresh lung specimens as a SM guidance tool during surgery [11, 12]. While the SM length measured by intraoperative CT correlated with the macroscopic gross SM, there has been no study evaluating whether intraoperative CT-derived SM length is consistent with the gold-standard postoperative histopathology-derived SM. The relationship between CT-based and pathology-based SM measurement must be understood prior to making any determinations on the feasibility of intraoperative CT for SM assessment. Therefore, we investigated the concordance between SM measurements by CT versus final pathology using an ex vivo sublobar lung resection model.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animal models pseudotumour creation

Animal use approval was waived by the University Health Network Animal Care Committee as all work was performed on recently sacrificed pigs that were dedicated to other experiments. The lungs were obtained from the carcasses of recently sacrificed Yorkshire pigs. 0.5 ml of 3% agarose gel (Agar, Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) mixed with a methylene blue dye (Bioshop, Burlington, Ontario, Canada) was injected into the peripheral lungs randomly, varying in depth and location, using a 23-gauge needle to mimic peripheral lung nodules (Fig. 1A). Lung sublobar resection specimens were harvested using a surgical stapler (Endo GIA Universal Staplers and Reloads, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) (Fig. 1B). To ensure adequate tissue contrast between the pseudotumour and surrounding parenchyma, resected lung samples were inflated by the instillation of air using a 25-gauge needle. Air injection pressure and approximate internal lung pressure were monitored by attaching a pressure transducer (Compass, CT disposable pressure transducer, Cook Medical, Indianapolis, IN, USA) (Fig. 1C). The injection pressure was kept lower than 100 mmHg to prevent alveolar wall destruction. Static pressure was maintained higher than 20 mmHg to achieve consistent expansion. The specimen was then embedded into a silicone gel (Mould star 19 T, Smooth-On, Inc., Macungie, PA, USA) within a 3D printed 10 × 10 × 10 cm container (Fig. 1D). This allowed consistent specimen positioning between CT imaging and specimen processing. The silicone was allowed to solidify over 20 min at room temperature before handling (Fig. 1E).

Computed tomography image acquisition and surgical margin measurement

The specimen was positioned with its staple line perpendicular to the CT axial plane (identified by a landmarking laser emitted by the CT scanner) to facilitate accurate SM length measurement (Fig. 1F). The ex vivo scans of embedded resected lung samples were acquired using a 64-row multidetector CT scanner (Revolution Frontier, GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). CT parameters included tube voltage, 80 kV; rotation time, 0.4 seconds; pitch, 0.984; tube current, 50 mA; and slice thickness 0.625 mm. The SM length, defined as the minimum distance between pseudotumour and innermost staple, was measured using digital callipers (Fig. 2A). After specimen imaging, the bed position was adjusted manually to align the CT scanner axial landmarking laser with the image slice of interest. This line was subsequently traced on the surface of the silicone using a permanent marker to provide alignment between the CT slice and planned specimen bisection.

Margin measurement by gross analysis

Specimen-containing silicone gel blocks were bisected using a 150 mm long pathology blade (CellPath Ltd, Powys, UK) along the pre-marked plane, as described above. Thereafter, the bisected specimen was laid flat and the gross SM length was measured using a metric ruler and/or measuring calliper. This was defined as the gross pre-fixation margin (Fig. 2B). The entire staple line was trimmed off by scissors, sparing adjacent lung tissue, before placing the lung specimen into 10% neutral buffered formalin for fixation. After fixation for 24 hours, specimens were trimmed to 3–4 mm thickness, maintaining the original surface of interest used for prior SM length measurement. The SM length was measured again and documented as the gross post-fixation margin (Fig. 2C). The specimen was then placed back into formalin and submitted for final processing.

Margin measurement by pathological analysis

The sliced specimens were placed in standard tissue cassettes and submitted to the Pathology Research Program at Toronto General Hospital. One histology slice with a thickness of 4 μm was cut from the surface of the tissue slab using a Shandon Finesse ME+ microtome (Thermo Scientific), using the same surface measured previously. Sections were then mounted on glass microscopy slides, haematoxylin & eosin stained, and imaged at 20× magnification using a digital pathology slide scanner (Aperio Scanscope brightfield scanner, Leica Biosystems Inc., Ontario, Canada). Microscopic SM distance was measured from this digital image at 20× magnification using digital callipers and recorded as the pathology margin (Fig. 2D).

Statistical analysis

A mean and range for SM length at the four different stages (CT, gross pre-formalin fixation, gross post-formalin fixation and final pathology) were calculated. Categorical variables were summarized as numbers and percentages. Continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviation. The % SM change between each stage as well as overall change was also quantified. Limits of agreement analysis was performed using the Bland–Altman method and interclass correlation coefficient was calculated to evaluate agreement between the two measurements. CT-to-pathology SM discrepancy was also evaluated by the one-way analysis of variance with a Bonferroni correction between the following subgroups: CT margin less than 10 mm, 10–20 mm and more than 20 mm. A test result was considered statistically significant if the P value was less than or equal to 0.05. All statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

RESULTS

A total of 50 resected fresh pig lung specimens were analysed. In all cases, CT imaging of inflated specimens clearly visualized agarose pseudotumours, allowing accurate measurement of the SM. Sample characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Mean maximum dimension of the surgical specimens was 53.2 mm (range, 25–80 mm). Pseudotumour injection was performed in the right cranial lobe in 10 cases (20%), right middle lobe in 6 cases (12%), right caudal lobe in 10 cases (20%), left cranial lobe in 12 cases (24%) and left caudal lobe in 12 cases (24%). The mean distance between the pseudotumour and visceral pleura was 5.1 mm (range, 2.8–9.8 mm). The mean SM at each evaluation point were as follows: CT 14.0 mm (range: 4.5–28.3 mm), gross pre-formalin fixation 13.0 mm (4.0–25.0 mm), gross post-formalin fixation 12.1 mm (2.5–26.0 mm) and final pathology 10.9 mm (1.0–23.4 mm).

DISCUSSION

In the present study, we investigated the relationship between CT-based SM measurement of resected lung specimens compared to pathologically measured SM using pig ex vivo sublobar resected lung samples. Our study demonstrated that there was excellent agreement between CT and pathology measurement; however, an approximately 20% discrepancy between the two techniques was also observed.

Due to the increased prevalence of CT screening, lung cancer is being detected earlier. Consequently, the demand for sublobar resection is expected to increase. Based on two multicentre phase III trials, sublobar resection was at least non-inferior to lobectomy [4, 5]. In addition, sublobar resection plays a role on lung function preservation, comparing postoperative pulmonary function tests with preoperative baselines and contrasting outcomes following lobectomy [13]. Sublobar resection will likely become the standard of care for small-sized peripheral NSCLC without lymph node metastasis. However, the SM is still a major concern for local control, even in cases with ground-glass opacity nodules [14].

According to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, segmentectomy and wedge resection should achieve parenchymal resection margins ≥20 mm or the size of the nodule [7]. If the SM is insufficient, additional interventions such as completion lobectomy or adjuvant radiation may be required. To date, there is no established method for confirming whether sufficient margins have been obtained while the patient is undergoing surgery. Approaches to assess SM status intraoperatively are sorely needed. Two studies have investigated the utility of using micro-CT and CT to evaluate fresh lung resection specimen [11, 12]. Both of these studies have shown that CT assessment of the resected specimen is beneficial to identify the tumour inside and to measure SM length during surgery. However, whether intraoperative CT-derived SM length is consistent with postoperative pathology-derived SM has not been assessed. Before integrating intraoperative CT assessment into clinical practice, understanding the relation between CT- and pathology-derived SM measurement is essential.

Comparison between CT images and histopathology of the exact same slice/cut surface can be challenging because of difficulties in obtaining consistent sectioning angles. Various methods have been studied to allow for a more accurate comparison between the two measurements. In our study, we performed whole-mount specimen embedding for comparison of the pathology findings to the CT imaging [15]. Resected lung specimens were embedded in gels to prevent movement during processing. Moreover, the gel enabled us to leverage the CT axial alignment laser to ensure bisection occurred across the identical plane. Different concentrations of agarose were previously described as the preferred media for embedding specimens in previous studies [16]. The melting point for agarose is around 88°C (85–89°C) and tissue embedment should be delayed until the gel temperature drops to 45 to 55°C [17]. This adds substantial time to prepare the gel solution and still has potential risk of tissue damage due to use of heated solutions. By contrast, silicone gel, as used in our study, can be prepared at room temperature and sets quicky.

In our study, a 23.8% discrepancy between CT- and pathology-based SM measurement was observed. Major factors affecting margin discrepancy include air escaping from the specimen and formalin fixation. CT measurements were based upon dimensions obtained by CT following lung specimen inflation. Meanwhile, pathology SM measurements were obtained from deflated lung tissue, which has decreased volume. In addition, shrinkage is an inevitable change with formalin fixation. Previous reports found that formalin fixation can shrink normal lung parenchyma and the tumour itself in NSCLC specimens [18, 19]. In our subgroup analysis, the specimens with shorter SM (≤10 mm category) demonstrated the highest shrinkage rate (32.5%) while the specimens with a longer SM (>20 mm category) showed the largest actual differences from CT to pathology. A certain amount of SM length shortening secondary to staples removal would be inevitable, and this may have more of an effect in the setting of short margins. Furthermore, with a short SM, the tension along the staple line may be greater due to closer proximity to the solid tumour; release of the staple line in this scenario may result in greater recoil of the adjacent lung parenchyma. Although CT- and pathology-derived SM were closely correlated, surgeons must be aware that CT-derived SM tends to overestimate the pathology-derived SM.

There are two major advantages when aiming to measure SM length prior to the final pathologic analysis. First, intraoperative SM assessment enables real-time decision-making regarding the need for additional resection. This could potentially reduce the rates of additional treatments such as radiation and surgery for close or involved margins. Second, an increasing number of non-solid lung tumours are being detected, which are challenging to identify by visual inspection intraoperatively or even on gross pathology. Radiological evaluation during surgery might help direct specimen sectioning, including optimal sectioning for SM assessment. Intraoperative CT imaging of resected lung specimen can be a low-risk tool that provide beneficial information to physicians and pathologists resulting in better patient outcomes.

There are limitations to this study that must be considered. First, the SM assessment in different phases was performed using ex vivo pig sublobar lung resection models. Tissue shrinkage due to formalin fixation, embedding and staining in pig lungs may vary from those in human lungs. Second, agarose pseudotumours were used to mimic peripheral lung nodules. The border between pseudotumour and surrounding lung tissue is extremely distinct. On the other hand, microscopic extension is commonly observed in in vivo lung tumour, especially in ground-grass opacity nodules, making it challenging to measure SM length on CT. This missed microscopic extension on CT may increase its discrepancy with final pathology. Third, CT-derived SM length is affected by the degree of inflation of the specimen. In case with air leakage from the staple line, CT-derived SM length is shortened because of insufficient aeration. In the present study, a pressure transducer was used to minimize differences in specimen inflation, which may be helpful in clinical practice if specimen inflation is included in any future protocols.

CONCLUSIONS

Our preclinical study using ex vivo sublobar lung resection models showed that SM length on average shrinks by 23.8% from initial CT measurement to final pathology measurement, even though both techniques were strongly correlated. Although CT examination of resected lung cancer specimens can be a promising and low-risk tool for real-time SM measurement during lung sublobar resection, surgeons must be aware that CT-derived SM generally overestimated the pathology-derived SM.
