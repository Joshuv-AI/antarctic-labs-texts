Treat the patient, not the grade of injury: modern management of solid organ trauma in children
Authors: Fatma Özcan Sıkı, Mehmet Sarıkaya, İsmail Yağmurlu, Samet Ünal, Fatma Betül Yiğit, Metin Gündüz, Tamer Sekmenli, İlhan Çiftci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTrauma remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in childhood, and intra-abdominal solid organ injuries represent a major clinical challenge in pediatric trauma care. Although multiple international guidelines define diagnostic, follow-up, and treatment strategies, management decisions may still vary depending on institutional experience, patient characteristics, and imaging findings. This study aimed to evaluate the management process, treatment strategies, and clinical outcomes of pediatric patients followed for posttraumatic intra-abdominal solid organ injuries in a tertiary referral center.MethodsPediatric patients aged 0–18 years who were admitted to our hospital between 2014 and 2024 due to blunt or penetrating trauma and diagnosed with intra-abdominal solid organ injuries were retrospectively evaluated. Patients with gastrointestinal perforation and those who died during emergency intervention after trauma were excluded. Demographic characteristics, trauma mechanisms, injured organs, transfusion requirements, and treatment approaches were analyzed to assess current management practices.ResultsA total of 1043 pediatric patients were examined. Blunt trauma was present in 97% of the patients, while penetrating trauma was observed in 3%. The most common causes of blunt trauma were falls from heights and traffic accidents. Approximately 76.8% of the patients were male, with a mean age of 9.16 ± 3.4 years. The average hospital stay was 7 ± 2 days. The transfusion requirement was determined to be 8.2%, and the emergency laparotomy rate was 3.4%. Patients with stable vital signs were treated non-operatively. The most commonly injured organs were the spleen, liver, and kidney.ConclusionsIn pediatric patients with solid organ injuries, treatment strategies should primarily be guided by hemodynamic stability and clinical assessment rather than injury grade alone. Conservative management in hemodynamically stable patients effectively reduces surgical intervention rates and supports favorable clinical outcomes. Careful patient selection, structured clinical follow-up, and adherence to guideline-based nonoperative management protocols may further optimize outcomes and minimize unnecessary surgical interventions in pediatric trauma care.

Introduction

Trauma remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in childhood and is recognized as a major public health problem in both developed and developing countries. In pediatric trauma cases, abdominal trauma is among the most common causes of death following head injury and is responsible for a substantial proportion of trauma-related hospitalizations and long-term sequelae. In this context, intra-abdominal solid organ injuries represent some of the most severe and potentially life-threatening clinical conditions encountered after pediatric trauma [1].

In the pediatric population, the spleen, liver, and kidneys are the most frequently affected organs in intra-abdominal solid organ injuries, with the vast majority of these injuries resulting from blunt trauma. Anatomical and physiological characteristics unique to children—such as relatively reduced protection of abdominal organs and a more elastic thoracic cage—facilitate the transmission of traumatic energy to the abdominal cavity, thereby increasing the risk of solid organ injury. These features constitute fundamental differences that distinguish pediatric trauma management from that of adult patients.

In recent years, a significant paradigm shift has occurred in the management of pediatric intra-abdominal trauma, with conservative treatment strategies increasingly replacing traditional surgery-based approaches. Current literature emphasizes that, particularly in hemodynamically stable pediatric patients, clinical status and physiological response should guide treatment decisions rather than the grade of organ injury alone [2–4]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that this approach reduces unnecessary surgical interventions, lowers complication rates, and shortens hospital stay.

Despite the widespread adoption of conservative management, accurate assessment of the trauma mechanism, rapid and precise diagnosis, identification of associated injuries, and appropriate patient selection remain critical determinants of successful outcomes. Although advances in imaging techniques provide more precise information about injuries and have significantly improved trauma management in pediatric patients by allowing for treatment decisions based on the patient’s hemodynamic profile in the diagnosis and monitoring of solid organ injuries, clinical practices continue to vary between centers. This variability underscores the need to evaluate institutional outcomes and compare them with existing literature.

Within this framework, sharing up-to-date clinical data on the monitoring and management of pediatric intra-abdominal solid organ injuries following trauma is essential, both to assess the effectiveness of current approaches and to inform future treatment strategies. In this study, we evaluated the follow-up and treatment processes of pediatric patients who presented to our institution with trauma-related intra-abdominal solid organ injuries and compared the clinical outcomes with current literature in light of evolving trauma management strategies in recent years.

Materials and methods

This study was designed as a single-center, retrospective, observational investigation. Medical records of pediatric patients aged 0–18 years who presented to our institution with trauma and were diagnosed with intra-abdominal solid organ injury between 2014 and 2024 were reviewed. Patients with isolated gastrointestinal perforation due to penetrating injury, as well as those who presented with severe hemodynamic instability or cardiac arrest and died before a complete diagnostic evaluation could be performed, were excluded from the study. The study was conducted with approval from the local institutional ethics committee (document number E-70632468-050.04-795494 and decision number 2024/403). Consent has been obtained from the parents or legal guardians of participants under the age of 18. Permission has been obtained from the owners or parents of the images included in the article.). All data were anonymized prior to analysis, and the study was performed in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Demographic and clinical data were obtained from patient files and the hospital information management system. Recorded variables included age, sex, type of trauma (blunt or penetrating), mechanism of injury, involved organ(s) (spleen, liver, kidney, and/or multiple organ injuries), treatment modality (conservative management or surgical intervention), need for blood transfusion, length of hospital stay, and clinical follow-up outcomes. Treatment management was categorized as conservative or surgical based on patients’ hemodynamic status and clinical course, and follow-up data were verified through patient records.

Hemodynamic assessment was performed based on age-adjusted vital signs and neurological status. In patients younger than 13 years, hypotension and tachycardia were defined according to age-specific criteria; for hypotension, widely accepted thresholds were used (< 70 mmHg for 0–1 year, < 70 + [2 × age] mmHg for 1–10 years, and < 90 mmHg for patients older than 10 years). Tachycardia was evaluated according to age-specific reference ranges. Neurological status was assessed using the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), and a GCS score ≤ 13 was considered indicative of impaired consciousness. For patients aged 13 years and older, assessments were performed according to Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) criteria [5–7].

Post-traumatic diagnostic evaluation was based on clinical findings in conjunction with abdominal ultrasonography and computed tomography. Ultrasonography was recorded as the primary modality used during initial assessment and follow-up, whereas computed tomography findings were accepted as the reference standard for grading solid organ injuries. Solid organ injuries were graded according to the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) classification [6, 7], and in patients with multiple organ injuries, the AAST grade was recorded separately for each affected organ.

Descriptive and comparative statistical methods were used for data analysis. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages. The chi-square test was used to assess associations between categorical variables, and Fisher’s exact test was applied when expected cell frequencies were low. Relationships between continuous variables and ordinal data (such as AAST injury grades) were evaluated using Spearman’s correlation analysis. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses, which were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 1,043 pediatric patients were included in the study. Of these, 76.8% were male and 23.2% were female, with a mean age of 9.16 ± 3.4 years. The mean length of hospital stay was 7 ± 2 days.

Evaluation of trauma type revealed that 97% of patients sustained blunt trauma, whereas 3% had penetrating trauma. The most common causes of blunt trauma were falls from height (e.g., balconies/windows, bicycle accidents) and traffic accidents. Penetrating trauma mechanisms included injuries caused by penetrating objects, firearms, and falls onto pointed objects.

Solid organ injuries resulting from penetrating trauma accounted for 3% of all cases. Surgical intervention was performed in only 5 of these patients, including one renal injury and four liver injuries. Nephrectomy was performed for the renal injury, while primary repair was performed in patients with liver injuries.

Analysis of the distribution of solid organ injuries showed that isolated organ injuries most frequently involved the spleen (50%), followed by the liver (29%) and the kidney (9%). Statistical analyses demonstrated significant associations between organ injury grade and the need for surgical intervention and blood transfusion in splenic, hepatic, and renal injuries. In particular, when low-grade (Grade 1–3) and high-grade (Grade 4–5) injuries were compared, a significant difference was observed with respect to surgical intervention and transfusion requirements (p = 0.067 for surgical intervention; p < 0.05 for transfusion requirement). In contrast, no significant differences were identified between Grade 4 and Grade 5 injuries in terms of surgical intervention or transfusion requirements for splenic, hepatic, or renal injuries (p > 0.05).

Discussion

Trauma remains one of the leading causes of accident-related mortality in childhood, and abdominal solid organ injuries are associated with a high potential for morbidity and mortality. In pediatric patients, the increased elasticity of the abdominal wall and the relatively less protected anatomical position of solid organs facilitate the transmission of traumatic energy into the abdominal cavity. Consequently, solid organ injuries are not only more frequent in children but also necessitate management strategies that differ fundamentally from those applied in adults [2]. In our study, the distribution of solid organ injuries and the predominance of blunt trauma as the primary mechanism clearly reflect the clinical implications of these anatomical and physiological characteristics.

In recent years, a pronounced paradigm shift has occurred in the management of pediatric intra-abdominal solid organ injuries, with nonoperative management (NOM) strategies based on hemodynamic stability increasingly replacing surgery-oriented approaches. The World Society of Emergency Surgery (WSES) and the American Pediatric Surgical Association (APSA) guidelines emphasize that treatment decisions should be guided primarily by the patient’s clinical condition rather than the grade of organ injury alone [3–6]. Surgical intervention rates in the literature range between 4.1% and 14.9% [7, 8]. In our cohort, the surgical intervention rate of 3.4%, which is below the reported range, suggests that NOM is not only safe but can be implemented more extensively with appropriate patient selection and disciplined clinical monitoring. Our clinical practice, decisions regarding patient monitoring, transfusion, or surgery are made at the surgeon’s discretion, in in line with AAST recommendations.

Splenic injuries are the most common solid organ injuries encountered in pediatric abdominal trauma (Fig. 1). The WSES 2017 splenic trauma guidelines report that the widespread adoption of NOM is the principal factor underlying the decline in splenectomy rates among pediatric patients [9]. Previous studies have demonstrated conservative management success rates exceeding 90%, with splenectomy rates generally ranging from 3% to 10% [10, 11]. The splenectomy rate of 2.7% observed in our study, which is lower than most rates reported in the literature, indicates that conservative management can be advanced to a more organ-preserving level when supported by adequate institutional experience.

Liver injuries in children most commonly result from blunt trauma, and contemporary guidelines state that the vast majority of these injuries can be managed nonoperatively (Fig. 2). The WSES 2020 liver trauma guidelines and APSA recommendations define NOM as the gold standard for pediatric liver injuries [4, 12, 13]. Surgical intervention rates reported in the literature are generally below 1%. In our series, the surgical intervention rate for liver injuries was approximately 0.5%, suggesting that, beyond guideline adherence, selective clinical decision-making and close monitoring can further reduce the need for surgery to levels even lower than those previously reported.

Although renal injuries are less common in pediatric trauma, surgical decision-making is particularly critical in high-grade injuries (Fig. 3). The WSES–AAST 2019 guidelines recommend that surgery be largely limited to Grade 5 injuries and cases of uncontrollable hemorrhage (Fig. 3) [7, 8]. Studies focusing on pediatric urological trauma have reported low nephrectomy rates, with most cases managed conservatively [14]. In our study, nephrectomy was performed in only one patients with Grade 5 renal injuries, supporting the notion that surgery should be reserved as an exceptional option and that clinical management can be applied even more selectively than suggested by current guidelines.

Multiple solid organ injuries represent a more complex patient population and reflect higher trauma severity. The literature indicates that falls from height and traffic accidents are the most common mechanisms of injury and that a substantial proportion of multiple organ injuries can still be managed nonoperatively [11, 15]. While the distribution of trauma mechanisms and multiple organ injuries in our cohort was consistent with these reports, the ability to maintain low surgical intervention rates further demonstrates that a conservative approach can be safely applied even in patients with multiple organ involvement, provided that careful patient selection is ensured.

Appropriate use of imaging modalities during diagnosis and follow-up constitutes a cornerstone of pediatric trauma management. Although computed tomography is regarded as the gold standard for evaluating abdominal trauma, selective use is recommended in children due to concerns regarding radiation exposure [16]. In our study, the use of CT as the primary tool for diagnosis and injury grading, combined with ultrasonography for follow-up, reflects a guideline-concordant approach that prioritizes patient safety without compromising diagnostic accuracy.

Concerning surgical techniques, laparoscopy has increasingly emerged as a less invasive option in selected cases, offering advantages such as shorter hospital stays, fewer complications, and improved cosmetic outcomes [17, 18]. One of the limitations of this study is that a limited number of laparotomies were performed in our cohort as the primary surgical intervention method.

Given the age-dependent variability of hypotension and tachycardia in pediatric trauma patients, all data were systematically evaluated against age-adjusted reference ranges. Hypotension was defined according to established age-specific thresholds, while tachycardia was defined as heart rates exceeding the 95th percentile for age; neurological status was assessed using the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) [6]. This analysis demonstrated that hypotension, tachycardia, and decreased GCS scores were more frequently observed in patients requiring surgical intervention.

The need for blood transfusion in pediatric solid organ injuries should not be regarded as a direct indicator of surgical necessity but rather as an important clinical marker reflecting trauma severity and the need for close monitoring. The literature highlights the role of transfusion requirements—particularly in splenic and hepatic injuries—in guiding intensive care and clinical decision-making processes [19]. In our study, the absence of surgical intervention in a substantial proportion of patients requiring transfusion suggests that transfusion is not an inevitable pathway to surgery but can be integrated into a controlled and deliberate clinical monitoring strategy.

Gastrointestinal tract perforations following blunt pediatric trauma are known to be rare but high-risk lesions [20]. Accordingly, such cases were deliberately excluded from our study, allowing the analysis to focus exclusively on solid organ injuries. This approach enabled a clearer evaluation of clinical decision-making processes within a more homogeneous patient population.

Finally, the importance of multidisciplinary approaches and education-based practices in trauma management is increasingly emphasized. Simulation-based training has been shown to improve team coordination and clinical decision-making [21]. The findings of our study support the premise that, with experienced teams and structured monitoring protocols, the need for surgical intervention in pediatric solid organ injuries can be safely minimized.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective and single-center design may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader pediatric trauma population. Nevertheless, the large sample size and extended study period represent significant strengths, allowing for an assessment of how institutional experience translates into clinical practice. Additionally, the study focused exclusively on intra-abdominal solid organ injuries, with gastrointestinal perforations and certain severe associated injuries intentionally excluded. While this facilitated interpretation within a homogeneous cohort, it does not encompass the entire spectrum of abdominal trauma. Furthermore, potential changes in imaging and follow-up protocols over the study period may have partially influenced treatment approaches. However, these factors do not detract from the primary objective of the study, which was to delineate contemporary clinical trends and real-world practice patterns.

Conclusion

In the management of solid organ injuries following trauma in children, the primary determinant of treatment strategy appears to be the patient’s hemodynamic status and clinical course rather than the grade of organ injury. In hemodynamically stable patients, nonoperative management was found to be safe and effective, with the need for surgical intervention largely limited to high-grade injuries.

Based on these findings, a selective nonoperative approach under close clinical monitoring appears to be appropriate in the management of pediatric solid organ injuries. The implementation of this approach by specialized trauma teams, along with the integration of current guidelines into clinical practice, may contribute to improved standardization of care and better clinical outcomes in pediatric patients.
