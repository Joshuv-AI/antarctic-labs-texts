Effect of music therapy on vital signs and heart rate variability of paediatric patients during the extubation process in the paediatric intensive care unit: a multicentre randomised clinical trial protocol

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionA successful extubation process is critical for the future health outcomes of paediatric patients, as it tests the functioning of the respiratory system without the support of mechanical ventilation. However, extubation can cause stress, pain, anxiety or discomfort in patients, which may sometimes lead to an increased likelihood of reintubation. Music-based interventions and therapies have been shown to be effective in reducing anxiety and stress levels in ventilated patients in the paediatric intensive care unit (PICU), but studies evaluating the effect of music therapy during the extubation process in the PICU are scarce.Methods and analysisThis is a pragmatic multicentre randomised clinical trial with two parallel arms. Introduction

Mechanical ventilation (MV) is a fundamental strategy in the treatment of patients with respiratory failure in the paediatric intensive care unit (PICU).1 MV is replacing the patient’s own breathing efforts with a ventilator that pushes air into the lungs through a tracheal tube.2 This intervention is essential to guarantee adequate oxygenation and pulmonary ventilation in the patient until their existing disease indicates an improvement that allows them to ensure autonomous breathing.3 One of the main reasons for MV in children is acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). ARDS is a condition in which the lungs swell up and fluids cause damage to the alveolar membranes, restricting them from filling with enough air. Underlying causes of ARDS are multiple, including sepsis, pneumonia, trauma, haemorrhagic shock or acute pancreatitis, among others.4 While ARDS is of relatively low incidence, mortality continues to be high, with studies reporting rates between 33.5% and 50%.5 6 Once the clinical criteria of the patients indicate sufficient improvement of the medical condition (ie, readiness testing), patients undergo weaning of MV (ie, decreasing the airflow of the ventilator).7 If the weaning process is successful, the patient will be extubated, which means that the endotracheal tube will be removed or a tracheostomy will be performed, allowing the patient to breathe independently.

While the extubation process is an important step towards recovery, it is often challenging, particularly in patients with difficulties in oxygenation, alveolar ventilation, airway patency and secretion control.8 In case the patient is not able to sustain spontaneous breathing and again requires the assistance of MV within 24–72 hours, extubation has failed.9 Factors that increase the likelihood of extubation failure are multiple, including prolonged MV, increased sedation, neurological comorbidities or elevated anxiety levels.10,12 Anxiety, agitation and delirium have been found to hinder weaning from MV, and recent reviews recommend alternative and complementary therapies, including music therapy, as potential strategies to aid the extubation process.12 13

Music therapy has been widely applied for adult patients during MV in the intensive care unit,14 15 and evidence-based guidelines recommend music therapy for this population.16 However, with respect to the paediatric population, conclusive evidence is still lacking. Huang et al17 investigated the effect of parent-selected prerecorded soothing music that was played back to infants with MV after paediatric surgery three times a day for 1 hour. The results showed shorter MV duration, less delirium and a trend towards higher success of MV withdrawal in the intervention group (IG).17 In a randomised pilot study, Liu et al18 found that infants during MV improved their vital signs and had a shorter ventilation time after listening to their favourite music three times a day for 60 min. Similarly, in the study by Garcia Guerra et al,19 children listened to 30 min of classical music three times a day during MV, and results showed a reduction in heart rate (HR) in the music group and no adverse events were reported. In one of the few music therapy studies, Bush et al20 investigated the differences between live music therapy versus recorded music on sedated MV patients in the PICU, resulting in a greater HR reduction for the live music therapy group.

While these studies show preliminary evidence for music’s potential to assist paediatric patients during MV, no study was found that tested the effectiveness of a live music therapy intervention during the extubation process with this population. This study seeks to close this knowledge gap by determining whether entrained live music therapy combined with standard care has a greater effect on improving vital signs and HR variability of paediatric patients during the extubation process than standard care alone.

Methods and analysis

Objectives

The main objective of this study is to determine the effect of live music therapy on vital signs and HR variability during the extubation procedure of paediatric patients in the PICU.

Study design

This is a pragmatic, multicentre randomised clinical trial (RCT), with two parallel arms.21 The IG will receive standard care + music therapy, and the control group (CG) will receive standard care only during the extubation process. This study follows the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials guidelines for reporting RCT protocols.22 The planned start date for this study is 1 September 2025. Data collection is expected to last 16 months, until 31 December 2026.

Participants and setting

This study will include paediatric patients hospitalised in the PICU of two hospitals in Colombia.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria are MV patients between 1 month and 44 weeks corrected gestational age and 17 years plus 364 days, and who are undergoing an extubation procedure. Patients with accidental extubation prior to randomisation will be excluded.

Sample size

The G*Power 3.1.9.7 programme was used to calculate the sample size, with the primary outcome being the HR. For this, the results of a previous pilot RCT of personalised music in paediatric ventilated patients were considered, reporting a mean HR difference of 4.7 and a SD of 7.9 between the music and CG.18 Based on these findings and assuming an effect size of 0.29, an alpha error of 0.05, a power of 90%, two comparison groups and three measurements over time, the sample size calculation resulted in 82 patients.

Participant selection and randomisation

Participant selection will be consecutive. Parents or legal guardians of patients evaluated as eligible for reduction of MV requirements and meeting the eligibility criteria by a critical care physician will be contacted 48 hours prior to extubation to explain the aims, purpose and interventions of the study. If parents give their consent for the child’s participation in the study, and after signing the informed consent, the patient will be randomly assigned to either the control or the IG. A block randomisation with block sizes of 4 and six possible combinations of group assignments will be performed: AABB, ABBA, ABAB, BBAA, BABA, BAAB. One of these arrangements is selected randomly, and four participants are assigned accordingly in each of the study sites. This process will be repeated until all participants are randomised. It will be established that intervention A is music therapy + standard care and intervention B is standard care alone.

Ethics and dissemination

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Fundación Universitaria Sanitas (CEIFUS 1356-24, date of approval: 3 May 2024). All parents or legal guardians of patients will sign a written informed consent, and if applicable, assent from participants will be sought (onlinesupplemental material 1 2). The written informed consent will be collected by a research assistant. This study was registered in clinicaltrials.gov, NCT06591533, trial registration date: 10 September 2024. The dissemination of this study will take place through publication of full results in peer-reviewed journals, and through presentations in local, national and international conferences and the hospitals’ clinical committees.

Blinding

Due to the nature of the intervention, it is not possible to blind the music therapist, the participant or the medical and therapy staff who is going to perform the extubation. 1 hour after the extubation procedure has been performed, a research assistant will review the monitors and take notes of the recorded vital signs, guaranteeing blinding of data collection. Data analysis will be carried out by a member of the research team blinded to the result of the randomisation. All data will be stored in a secure REDCap database.

Concealment

The concealment of the randomisation sequence will be guaranteed using opaque, sealed and sequentially numbered envelopes. The envelopes will be opened sequentially only after the parents or legal guardians of the patient who has met the eligibility criteria have signed the informed consent. The envelopes will be kept in a folder in a locked shelf inside each PICU that will be in the custody of the operational coordinator of the project.

Primary outcome measure

The primary outcome measure in this study is changes in HR. HR will be measured through pulse oximetry every minute from 5 min pre-extubation, during the extubation procedure, and up to 10 min postextubation. The data of primary and secondary outcome measures will be stored on hospital monitors and retrospectively extracted for maintaining blinding of the data collection.

Interventions

CG: standard care alone

Standard care during the extubation process includes providing the usual medical care for patients according to each hospital’s guidelines. Extubation always begins with a thorough evaluation of the patient’s condition to determine the suitability of the procedure. After sedation is lowered and muscle paralysis is reversed, ventilatory support will be gradually decreased. A spontaneous breathing test is then performed by disconnecting the patient from ventilatory support, allowing him/her to breathe freely through the orotracheal tube connected to a humidified oxygen source. The indices used to assess weaning from MV are measured after the patient has successfully passed the spontaneous breathing tests. These indices assess the patient’s ability to maintain airway reflexes and determine whether the respiratory centres are functioning properly, helping to predict the success or failure of the extubation procedure. If the patient tolerates the test well and maintains adequate oxygenation, extubation is performed. This stage implies that the patient has successfully completed the weaning process, is conscious, can maintain the protective mechanisms of the airway and is haemodynamically stable. Prior to extubation, the patient is preoxygenated with 100% oxygen for 1–2 min, followed by a final suction to remove secretions. The tracheal tube is then gently removed during the first cough reflex or, if the patient is unable to cough, at the end of expiration. Close monitoring of the patient is performed during and after extubation to prevent potential complications. An extubation is considered successful if the patient can sustain spontaneous breathing for more than 48 hours from the withdrawal of MV. Extubation failure is defined as the need for reintubation or ventilatory support within 48 hours of removal from MV.

IG: standard care + music therapy

The following description of the intervention follows the updated guidelines for reporting music-based interventions in clinical trials.23 The music therapy intervention will be provided by a certified music therapist and consists of improvised live music based on the principles of entrainment.24 25 Entrainment consists of the conscious synchronisation and modification of musical elements in relation to the patient’s biological rhythms and has been shown to improve well-being and reduce symptoms in a variety of medical populations, including young children in the PICU.26,29 In this study, entrainment will be used with the aim of supporting the self-regulation responses of the patients and for promoting a calm and comfortable auditory environment during the extubation process. The musical instruments will be a classical guitar with nylon strings (Yamaha C-40) and the therapist’s voice.

The intervention will be divided into two phases:

Pre-extubation and extubation phase: before starting the intervention, the music therapist will clean the guitar and comply with the necessary safety measures according to a published disinfection protocol.30 Then, the music therapist will greet the patient and explain the nature of the intervention according to the patient’s age and, in case the patient is fully awake. Then the improvised music will start. In this phase of the intervention, the musical characteristics will include a slow to medium tempo, stable pulse, simple harmonic structures (eg, tonic–subdominant movements), a simple melodic motif with subtle variations and avoiding large intervals or abrupt changes of tonalities. The music therapist might accompany the improvised music by using vocal humming or toning without lyrics or words. The aim of the music in this phase is to seek greater tranquillity and help patients maintain a calm and relaxed state.

Data analysis

All randomised patients will be analysed based on intention-to-treat analysis.31 A descriptive analysis will be performed using absolute and relative frequencies for qualitative variables, and measures of central tendency and dispersion for quantitative variables. Both will be evaluated through the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test in accordance with their distribution. To compare the basic quantitative sociodemographic and clinical data between the two groups, the Student’s t-test or its non-parametric equivalent (Mann-Whitney U test) will be used. For categorical variables, the χ2 or Fisher test will be used when applicable. HR, OS, RR and BP of each participant will be averaged according to the three time points (5 min before, during and up to 10 min after the extubation process). A mixed factorial analysis of variance of 2×3 will be used for between-subject and within-subject analysis. If a statistically significant interaction is identified, a subgroup analysis (sex, age and clinical diagnosis) will be proposed. Post hoc comparisons will be made to explore significant differences between the student t-test groups for independent samples. To control the error rate (type I error), the CIs will be adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. The results will be considered statistically significant with a p value <0.05. The statistical analysis will be carried out with the Stata V.16 software.

For the analysis of HRV, an ECG analysis will be carried out to infer the activity of the sympathetic and parasympathetic branches of the autonomic nervous system. For this, ECG’s R-peaks will be detected through the Pan-Tompkins algorithm,32 from which the time interval between each R-peak will be estimated, producing a tachogram. Then, we will calculate the Fourier transform of the tachogram and estimate the low and high frequency (HF) components of the time series. According to previous research,33 34 low frequency components are linked to sympathetic activity, while the HF) band is associated with parasympathetic influence of the autonomic nervous system. This procedure provides an estimate of the sympathetic-vagal activity during extubation, allowing the comparison of possible differences when patients experience music therapy intervention or not.

Harms or adverse events

While harms are not expected in this study, all potential adverse events will be fully documented and reported to the ethics committee.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not directly involved in the design, outcomes or recruitment of the study. However, this study derives from daily clinical practice, in which the music therapy service regularly supports the extubation process of PICU patients. The positive feedback from staff, patients and caregivers motivated the elaboration of this study.

Discussion

This study aims to investigate the effects of music therapy on vital signs and HRV in paediatric patients during the extubation process. During MV and weaning from MV, patients may experience anxiety and stress secondary to the feeling of shortness of breath, frequent sucking or inability to speak, among others.35 In infants, irritability and fear during MV can lead to a HR and oxygen consumption increase.17 The results of previous reviews suggest that music therapy and other music-based interventions are a viable option for anxiety management in adult patients with MV,14 15 but studies related to paediatric populations are still limited. While a handful of studies suggest benefits of prerecorded music for infants during MV,17,19 just one small-scale study reported the use of live music therapy.20 No study was found that focuses on the extubation process itself. This is surprising, as live music therapy has been shown to be effective in improving HR, RR and comfort in non-MV patients in the PICU,29 36 37 suggesting a potential positive effect also during the extubation process.

The intervention in this study will consist of improvised live music based on the principles of entrainment. A recent case study with infants with congenital heart disease suggests that music therapy entrainment may improve physiologic stability in infants, particularly through lowering the rate of change of the HR, leading in this way to a more stable heartbeat.38 In adult MV patients, entrainment has also been shown to reduce agitation, pain and HR.39 In healthy subjects, rhythmically entrained live music has been found to increase peripheral blood flow and subjective well-being.40 Thus, we consider entrained live music the most viable and safest intervention option, considering the medical state of critical care patients.

Potential limitations

There are several potential limitations to this study protocol: First, while the principles of entrainment allow for a greater individualisation of the music based on real-time responses and needs of each patient, the music cannot be fully standardised. This could result in a variation of the intervention and influence its effect. In the intervention protocol, we have tried to highlight replicable and adaptable components: replicable features include delivery by a certified music therapist, initiation and termination of the intervention in relation to the extubation procedure, a fixed session duration, live music as a delivery modality, and structured therapeutic goals (to promote relaxation and physiological stability). Adaptable components include the choice of musical elements based on entrainment (eg, tempo, dynamics and tonal structure) and therapist strategies, individualised according to the patient’s clinical condition and observable responses. Second, while sample size calculation resulted in a larger sample as reported in previous studies,17,20 generalisation of the results will be restricted, as both hospitals are located in Bogotá, Colombia, and cultural and contextual factors that might influence the results cannot be excluded. Third, as the staff who perform the extubation process cannot be blinded to the intervention, the music might have indirect positive, negative or neutral effects on the extubation performance of the staff itself. In a future study, a qualitative evaluation of the staff’s experience during music therapy might be helpful to control for this factor. And lastly, in this study, parents or caregivers might or might not be present during the extubation. This might further influence potential outcomes and will be controlled for during the statistical analysis.
