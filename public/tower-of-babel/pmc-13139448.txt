AI-based autism identification from hyperspectral imaging detection of oxidative stress in pediatric red blood cells
Authors: Roupen Vartian, Anna Sansone, Gessica Batani, Antonia Parmeggiani, Enzo Grossi, Marina Marini, Vincenzo Davide Catania, Mario Lima, Alessandro Ghezzo, Cristina Panisi, Marida Angotti, Provvidenza M Abruzzo, Carmela Serpe, Arianna Vignini, Ellas Spyratou, Efstathios P Efstathoupoulos, Chryssostomos Chatgilialoglu, Carla Ferreri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOxidative stress (OS) plays a key role in many pathologies, yet the non-invasive, label-free, and cost-effective detection remains a challenge. This study evaluates Hyperspectral Imaging (HSI) combined with AI to detect OS by identifying changes in red blood cell (RBC) membranes.MethodsAn OS model for the HSI procedure is established by treating EDTA-anticoagulated whole blood with 1.5% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) to induce stress without cell lysis. Membrane fatty acid composition (lipidome) is analysed via gas chromatography, while HSI in dark-field microscopy captures spectral signatures and their distributions in healthy and insulted RBC. The HSI methodology is then applied to RBC samples from 31 neurotypical (NT) children and 27 children with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), a condition linked to OS. A deep learning algorithm is used to classify the clinical samples based on the identified OS signatures.ResultsHere, we show that significant spectral distribution differences are present in OS-exposed RBCs, which correlate with membrane lipidome remodelling. Notably, the OS-induced spectral differences in the H2O2 model mirror those observed between the ASD and NT groups. The AI-assisted analysis successfully classifies the pediatric cohort, achieving 93.2% accuracy in identifying ASD subjects.ConclusionsHSI, guided by OS-specific modeling and integrated with AI, provides a robust, scalable method for membrane diagnostics. This approach offers a promising pathway for personalized medicine and the non-invasive monitoring of oxidative stress-related conditions.Subject terms: Predictive markers, Membrane lipids

Introduction

Oxidative stress (OS) profoundly impacts red blood cell (RBC) lifespan and function, with polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) of membrane phospholipids being primary targets of this process1–3. OS is involved in a broad spectrum of pathologies4–7, highlighting the critical need for robust diagnostic and prognostic tools. The OS-related changes of the RBC membrane lipidome and its fatty acid composition result in risk factors for cardiovascular and other OS-related diseases in population studies4–10. At the same time, the Peroxidation Index (PI) offers valuable insights into the OS impact on aging, indicating cell membranes as a valuable target for investigation of stress exposure during life span11–13. Considering the whole scenario of OS-related transformations in a living organism, a recent review has affirmed the importance of using a panel of biomarkers, rather than a single indicator, to ensure accurate and comprehensive evaluations of OS levels3. Beyond the challenges of detecting subtle, early signs of oxidative stress due to methodological limitations, cost constraints also present a significant hurdle.

Hyperspectral imaging (HSI) is an advanced imaging technique that combines the principles of spectroscopy with traditional imaging. Unlike conventional cameras, hyperspectral cameras capture detailed spectral information across hundreds of narrow, contiguous spectral bands of the electromagnetic spectrum for each pixel. By analyzing the light reflected or emitted by a specimen, hyperspectral imaging (HSI) can reveal necessary information about its material composition and chemical properties, but also enables the detection of geometrical and organizational features of the sample, not visible in other imaging methodologies14,15.

Hyperspectral imaging (HSI) was initially developed for remote sensing applications, particularly on Earth-observing satellites, where it was used to monitor geological activity, assess environmental conditions, and support military surveillance. Over time, HSI has expanded into various fields, including agriculture, environmental monitoring, biomedical imaging, and molecular biology. In particular, when imaging biological specimens that interact with light, HSI captures a detailed spectral signature or “spectral fingerprint” for each pixel in the image. These signatures typically span wavelengths from the visible to near-infrared (VNIR) and short-wave infrared (SWIR) regions of the electromagnetic spectrum16,17. For hyperspectral imaging, a three-dimensional (3D) hyperspectral datacube is created for each pixel, with two spatial dimensions, similar to those in a regular photograph, and the third dimension containing the spectral information for each pixel (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b)15–17. The Spectral Angle Mapper (SAM) is a widely used algorithm for spectral classification in hyperspectral imaging. It operates through a complex mathematical process in which the spectral signature of each pixel is treated as a vector in a high-dimensional space, with each dimension corresponding to a specific spectral band. SAM then calculates the angle between these spectral vectors to assess their similarity—smaller angles indicate greater similarity. Typically, if the angle between two spectra is less than 0.10 radians, they are considered a match or classified as the same. When accompanied by SAM, HSI can identify materials by their unique spectral signature, differentiating between substances that might appear the same to the naked eye, thus detecting differences or anomalies introduced in the original sample that would otherwise be invisible. In the case of a biological specimen, such as blood, HSI allowed to distinguish blood cell morphology (for example, leukocytes from erythrocytes), to automate erythrocyte count, and also to get spectral fingerprints of molecular components, such as hemoglobin in the different redox states, the membrane lipid cluster (phospholipids, cholesterol, fatty acids), and proteins (spectrin, ankyrin, actin, protein 4.1, and protein 4.2)18–21. Using hyperspectral dark field microscopy (HDFM) the healthy RBC membrane scattering properties were first reported, obtaining a spectral signature, made of eight graphs, showing light intensity versus wavelength in the range 400–1000 nm; a simple statistical treatment of such spectral features combined with clinical status afforded initial insights of the power of this methodology to examine health conditions22. When the architecture of the RBC membrane is considered, any lipid alteration, as well as degradation or aggregation of proteins, not destructive for the cell survival, causes a rearrangement in the membrane bilayer organization, changing its refractive index, anisotropy, or geometry. It can be inferred that light scattering from the cell membrane serves as a signature of the specific cell type or status, and provides comprehensive information about changes resulting from the specific insult23–25.

Building on advances in HSI-based biological measurements and the relevance of OS as a molecular basis of cellular changes, we develop the first HSI-based methodology for direct monitoring changes in RBC due to OS. Here, we show the use of HSI for mapping RBC membranes before and after a non-destructive oxidative insult induced by H2O2, a well-characterized biologically related oxidant26–30. Moreover, we integrate lipidomic analysis into HSI endmembers to demonstrate spectral changes that correlate with the specific changes of the fatty acid compositions, which are known in the literature6,7,13. This procedure allows to have an easily reproducible and reference model of OS for a comprehensive understanding of OS-mediated membrane alterations through biophotonic measurements. Given the established link between OS and several pathologies, including Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)31–35, our investigation extended the analysis of RBC membrane HSI data comparing subjects with ASD against neurotypical (NT) controls. The severe malfunction of a membrane-embedded ionic pump, previously reported in children with ASD31, is here correlated with HSI signatures. Our work employs a deep learning algorithm to analyze extensive hyperspectral imaging (HSI) datasets14,36, assessing by artificial intelligence (AI) tool the potential of these data for accurate clinical predictions.

This study presents evidence showing that (a) specific changes in the distribution of the hyperspectral library of healthy RBC membranes exposed to OS correlate with specific fatty acid modifications; (b) the extent of such HSI changes are similar to those observed in RBC of individuals with ASD compared to the RBC of neurotypical individuals; (c) HSI spectra hold information on the membrane organization that may reveal structural adaptations to biochemical alterations; (d) AI analysis of HSI data allows for accurate grouping of individuals with ASD. This groundbreaking approach establishes HSI, enhanced by AI, as a robust and scalable approach leveraging RBC membrane analysis to advance membrane-based diagnostics, paving the way for advancements in OS research and personalized medicine.

Methods

Blood sample collection for the OS model experiment

A fresh capillary blood sample (500 μL) was collected from nine healthy volunteers in a Vacutainer EDTA tube (Microvette® 500 K3E, Sarstedt, Numbrecht, Germany). A signed agreement exists with the Department of Immunohematology and Transfusion Medicine (Metropolitan Area of Bologna, SIMT AMBO) to receive blood samples from healthy blood donors (Ethical Committee approval, Comitato Etico Area Vasta Emilia Romagna #0083892), accompanied by a signed informed consent for research purposes.

Preparation of the blood sample and HSI analysis

This is a modification of the previously reported procedure for analyzing RBC images using hyperspectral dark-field microscopy (HDFM)21. Figure 1a shows the workflow of HSI analysis for untreated blood samples.

The HDFM system consisted of a CytoViva 150 dark field condenser (CytoViva, Auburn, AL) attached to an Olympus microscope (EDFM) in place of the microscope’s original condenser, via a fiber optic light guide to the lamp source. A 60× oil immersion colour-corrected objective was integral to the system. A 150 W quartz-halogen light source (Dolan-Jenner DC-950, Massachusetts, USA) was used, covering the full spectral range from 400 to 2500 nm. The calibration of the measurements is performed using two commercially available standard references (NIST polystyrene standard and the White reference standard).

All blood samples were used within 5 h after withdrawal, and reference tests were run every day before the samples arrived. In all cases, no adverse events occurred during the index test or the reference standard.

For RBC image detection, a glass slide bearing a 2 μL drop of whole blood in EDTA is placed on the microscope stage. A drop of special oil is then placed on the condenser below the slide to couple the glass slide to the condenser optically. The 10× lens was used initially to obtain an overview of a broader area of the sample and to set the focus along the z-axis. Identifying suitable locations or regions of interest (ROI) for image acquisition that contain round, distinct, and well-illuminated cells requires careful examination to minimise cell-cell interaction effects. Additionally, the selected ROIs must, to the extent possible, be free of background noise (e.g., from nanoparticles or plasma debris). After checking these criteria, the switch to the 60× lens is made after placing a drop of oil on the slide to couple it to the objective lens optically. In this way, the refractive index of the optical apparatus (condenser-slide-lens) is approximately constant, and the image is much sharper. In the Supplementary Information, further details of lamp-spectrum elimination and luminescence removal are provided (see Supplementary Fig. 3). Once the area is selected and the exposure time is set, the image is captured and displayed on the screen. Nine blood samples from healthy donors were used, yielding three RBC images from three different optical fields per sample, for a total of 81 images. The number of cells per blood sample (9 RBCs) is sufficiently large to yield low standard errors of the measurements, while remaining small enough to be handled in practice during analysis. The RBC images were collected on a range from 400 to 1000 nm (VNIR), and the Environment for Visualizing Images (ENVI, Exelis Visual Information Solutions) software version 4.4 was used for analyzing the acquired hyperspectral images and generating hyperspectral graphs. Using the Spectral Angle Mapper (SAM) algorithm, a spectral classification image was obtained, indicating the degree to which each pixel in the input image matches the endmember pixels. Subsequently, binary maps were produced to identify pixels whose similarity values exceed a predefined threshold (for more details, see SAM classification algorithm section in Supplementary Information). An optimization process was performed to establish the match between the optical and hyperspectral images. During this optimization, we used a large (up to 15) or a reduced number of endmembers to see the outcomes. Suboptimal outcomes are possible: some endmembers exhibit 0% class distribution with an under-mapping (optical image of RBC membrane partially covered) —rendering them redundant—or cause an “over-mapping” effect, where pixels outside the cell membrane (e.g., intracellular or plasma regions) are incorrectly classified (see Supplementary Fig. 1e). After the optimization, eight different hyperspectral graphs were identified, and a colour code was attributed to each of them, showing a satisfactory match with the optical images (see Fig. 1b for the images and Fig. 1c for the eight endmembers). For n = 9 blood samples, the percentage distributions of the eight endmembers in the healthy RBC images (81 images) were obtained and expressed as median ± standard error of the mean (SEM) in the Table shown in Fig. 1e. All analyses were performed under the conditions that prevent bias from the test performers. Experimentally, analyses were rerun to assess the extent to which the results changed. The narrow standard errors of the mean for the endmembers’ distribution values indicated the method’s reproducibility. The healthy RBC spectral library identified by the user and saved in ENVI is then used to map RBC in the H2O2-treated blood samples from the experiments described in the next section.

Blood oxidative experiment

According to the workflow in Fig. 1d, 50 μL of EDTA-treated whole blood from nine healthy volunteers was mixed with 50 μL of 1.5% or 3% (v/v) H2O2 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at pH 7.4. The final oxidant concentrations in the mixture were 0.75% and 1.5%, respectively. The sample was left with the reagent at room temperature for 1 h, after which 2 μL were taken for HSI analysis. The remaining sample was used for membrane fatty acid analysis, as described below. Image acquisition of the nine 1.5% H2O2-treated blood samples yielded hyperspectral endmember distribution results shown in Fig. 1e. In Supplementary Table 2, the non-significant results for 0.75% H2O2 are shown.

Isolation of fatty acids from RBC membrane phospholipids and gas chromatographic analysis

The nine samples of EDTA-treated whole blood, which remained from the HSI analysis (approximately 0.4 mL), were used to perform fatty acid-based membrane lipidome analyses before and after the oxidative experiments, following a protocol described in previous studies22. The fatty acids are expressed in relative quantitative percentages (% rel. quant.) as mean ± SEM, and the data before and after the 1.5% H2O2 treatment are shown in Supplementary Table 3.

Supervised ANNs work to learn patterns from examples related to a specific condition (like in our case, distinguishing between subjects with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) and Neurotypical (NT) subjects). In the training phase, the network is fed data (inputs) and a predefined target (desired output). It computes an error function (the distance between its production and the target) and iteratively adjusts its internal parameters (weights) to minimise this error. The network’s operation can be simplified as y = f(x, w*), where x is the input, y is the output, and w* is the optimized set of parameters that best perform the prediction function f. To make the predictive model as efficient as possible, with the TWIST System we eliminated the inclusion of irrelevant variables from the spectral data—those that don’t help in the classification37. TWIST combines two powerful techniques:

Training & Testing (T&T): A robust data re-sampling method that splits the primary dataset into multiple sub-samples (for training, testing, and validation), ensuring they all have similar statistical characteristics (probability density functions).

Both T&T and IS are driven by a powerful evolutionary computing method called the Genetic Doping Algorithm (GenD) developed at the Semeion Research centre (Rome, Italy)37.

A critical step is optimally dividing the entire dataset into two main parts: a Training Sub-sample (A) and a Testing Sub-sample (B). The goal is to ensure that the statistical distributions of Subsets A and B are as similar as possible. GenD’s evolutionary process is used to find the perfect split. Each potential division (or “hypothesis”) is represented by an “individual” in the genetic population—a vector of 1 s and 0 s indicating which records go into Sub-sample A (1) or Sub-sample B (0).

In the Fitness Evaluation (the 5-Step Check), a fitness function is used to determine whether a proposed split is good. A better split is one that generates two sub-samples (A and B) with the most similar statistical properties. This evaluation uses two independent Supervised Neural Networks (SNNs) in five steps:

Step 1: SNN_A is trained only on Subset A to learn the patterns in A.

Step 2: SNN_A is applied “blindly” to Subset B to check if what it learned from A works on B.

Step 3: SNN_B is trained only on Subset B to learn the patterns in B (independent of A).

Step 4: SNN_B is applied “blindly” to Subset A to check if what it learned from B works on A.

Step 5: The minimum of the two accuracies (from steps 2 → 4) is set as the split’s fitness to ensure the split is robust and both sub-samples are statistically similar.

This “Fitness Evaluation” is repeated for every proposed split in every generation of the GenD algorithm.

The auto-contractive map (Auto-CM) and minimum spanning tree (MST)

These tools are used to explore and visualize relationships within the data, rather than for prediction.

The Auto-CM identifies intricate, nonlinear relationships, clusters, and hidden patterns among variables in the dataset (see Mathematical representation and Theoretical consideration in the Supplementary Information). Therefore, the auto-contractive map function is designed to visualize the most plausible underlying interconnection schema among the variables under study. It is a unique neural network that minimizes a special cost function that includes third-order effects, allowing it to detect deeply hidden similarities. It initializes connections uniformly (making results highly reproducible) and only produces positive weights (avoiding inhibitory relationships).

The Minimum Spanning Tree (MST) simplifies and interprets the complex output matrix generated by the Auto-CM. Based on Kruskal’s algorithm, it filters the Auto-CM’s connections, keeping only the essential links needed to connect all variables with the minimal overall “edge weight”. This creates a concise, easily interpretable graph that highlights the most critical connections in the system (a semantic connectivity map)37–44.

Results and discussion

Non-invasive methodology to gather the scattering profiles of RBC membranes in healthy individuals, containing molecular and spatial information on protein and lipid assemblies

HSI is a strong candidate technology in non-invasive medical imaging using a readily available RBC sample20,45, providing combined information of the molecular composition and spatial arrangement of cell components, which can be advantageously used, which enables observation of the mechanisms of stress adaptation in membrane compartments46.

From a small volume (2 µL) of whole blood obtained from nine healthy individuals with ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) as the anticoagulant, RBC images in dark-field microscopy are analysed using HSI. This analysis can be performed directly on the blood sample, without any additional labelling, according to the workflow shown in Fig. 1a. EDTA is an excellent metal and, in particular a Calcium chelator47; beside the anticoagulant effect, it is also known to preserve the RBC samples from the Ca-dependent phospholipase enzyme activation, which leads to membrane lipid remodelling and release of polyunsaturated fatty acids-containing phospholipids48. The concentration of EDTA in the commercially available hematology tubes is known (1.8 mg per mL of blood). It ensures the uniformity of the chelation effect as well as of any other type of interaction of this chelator in the sample.

In applying HSI to the samples in the present work, we have introduced modifications to previously established methods to improve data quality21,22. In particular, we address the exposure time to achieve a maximum intensity of 5000 a.u. at 640 nm (red color for our hyperspectral imaging system), the smoothing of the original data signals using the Savitzky–Golay filtering method, and the removal of lamp spectrum and luminescence background. Details are provided in “Methods” and Supplementary Information (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3).

Nine different EDTA-treated blood samples derived from healthy subjects are used to establish the HSI protocol. Nine RBC images are acquired from each sample, as described in the “Methods”, according to the workflow depicted in Fig. 1a. Therefore, a total of n = 81 RBC images are obtained, representing a total number of cells evaluated per blood sample sufficiently large to yield low standard errors, while remaining small enough to be handled in practice during analysis. As described in the “Methods” for optical and hyperspectral image acquisitions, the hyperspectral library for healthy RBC was created in ENVI based on a large-scale analysis of the 81 RBC images per blood sample. The library, saved in ENVI, is used by the SAM algorithm to obtain the percent distribution of the spectral endmembers (recognized by a color code), as shown in Fig. 1b. It is worth underlining that the superimposition of the optical image with the spectral endmembers results from a very careful optimization procedure of the experimental configuration for the best HSI mapping performance, aiming at obtaining the class distribution of the endmembers with a -desired- “Gaussian-like” profile (see “Methods”). After optimization avoiding under- or over-mapping effects (see Supplementary Fig. 1e), as described in the “Methods”, the HSI analysis yields eight spectral signatures (endmembers with arbitrary numbers and color codes) as reported in Fig. 1c, showing a strong scattering behaviour in the range 450–800 nm, with spectra 2 and 3 having the highest intensities. Spectrum 2 is in the blue-green region, and spectrum 3 is shifted toward the red. The other graphs are characterized by a moderate scattering efficiency at shorter wavelengths (see the list of the bands for each spectrum in Supplementary Fig. 1d). By the color codes used in the HSI detection, it is possible to appreciate the distribution of the spectral endmembers. It is worth recalling that light scattering from the RBC membrane, which is composed of clusters of molecular elements46, encodes comprehensive information about not only the RBC’s membrane composition, but also its morphology and organisation. Using the total class distributions of the endmembers (the sum of pixels across the eight signatures) as 100%, the SAM algorithm produced a class distribution report quantifying the amount of each endmember that is mapped in each RBC sample image. The results are listed in the Table of Fig. 1e (see the “Untreated” column), reported as mean ± SEM. Notably, the narrow deviations in the registered spectral endmember distribution values across the 81 images indicate limited variability and high precision in the protocol for repeated measurements. Indeed, it is gratifying to observe how homogeneous the information from the nine blood samples from different healthy individuals was. These results are obtained using blood samples kept at room temperature and analyzed within a maximum of 5 h of blood withdrawal. Any deviation from the specified blood temperature and storage time (i.e., longer storage at room temperature or in the refrigerator at 4 °C) increased variability, thereby reducing the repeatability of the measurements. Frozen samples do not respond due to membrane alterations. In the “Methods” and Supplementary Information, the methodology is detailed, including the image acquisition procedure, the SAM classification algorithm for evaluating the spectral endmembers distribution in the RBC images, and the removal of lamp spectra and luminescence backgrounds. The analytical guide for treating the collected spectra to remove lamp spectra and luminescence is shown in Supplementary Fig. 2.

In connection with previous work, molecular components representative of the RBC membrane and sub-membrane compartments are used to examine their hyperspectral characteristics, under the same experimental conditions21. HSI analysis is performed on aqueous solutions or suspensions containing a single compound, such as cholesterol, phospholipids (using egg lecithin), spectrin, hemoglobin. The first two elements account for up to 50% of the membrane composition12,46; the latter two are associated with the sub-membrane regions and are involved in various physiopathological conditions49. Additionally, to examine molecular differences for oxidation by-products, two other compounds are analysed: the commercially available 7-ketocholesterol, as a representative for cholesterol oxidation, and oxyhemoglobin formed by the known reaction of hemoglobin with H2O250. The aqueous solutions or suspensions are prepared at a molarity similar to that found in biological systems21, as detailed in the Supporting Information. These components are characterized by scattering features, with bands in the 450–800 nm window, some of which are high-intensity (indicated with ‘s’, sharp), as shown in Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 1A. The scattering data on hemoglobin and its oxidised derivative by HSI are new information, to the best of our knowledge, to combine with that reported by other methodologies. The visible absorption in the region 575–600 nm (Q bands) of hemoglobin due to the heme portion is known in the absorption spectroscopy of RBC51. Moreover, the bands at 542 and 577 nm are reported in the diffuse reflectance spectroscopy measurements52, the latter being shifted to 572 nm in the HSI analysis. For hemoglobin, a sharp band at a higher wavelength (595 nm) was reported when the basic amino acids (arginine, but also histidine and lysine) display new electrostatic interactions in an acidic environment53. A complete understanding of the hyperspectral features of pure biomolecules and their relationship with the complexity of the membrane assembly is beyond the scope of this work and warrants further investigation in the context of hyperspectral imaging and erythrocyte analysis20,54–56. It is worth noting that cholesterol emerges as the most influential contributor to the RBC spectral profile in the VNIR region. It dominates the 549–694 nm range, where the most prominent peaks occur, and also appears in both the blue region (450–500 nm) and the red-IR region (675–800 nm). Interestingly, spectroscopic studies using Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) and vibrational frequency analysis report no data for cholesterol in the visible region57. All data of the scattering bands for each of the studied components are shown in the Supplementary Table 1A. At the same time, Supplementary Table 1B lists the spectral bands of the eight RBC spectral endmembers in connection with those of the single compounds. Although not exhaustively describing the molecular complexity of the membrane structural components, it can be recognized that the spectral endmember 2 contains features of phospholipids (egg lecithin), cholesterol, spectrin, and does not involve hemoglobin and cholesteryl oxide. In the spectral endmember 3, spectral features of oxidized hemoglobin, cholesterol and 7-ketocholesterol are present. The spectral endmember 5 presents spectral features of hemoglobin, cholesterol, spectrin, phospholipids, and the oxidized counterparts of hemoglobin and cholesterol, with sharp bands at 554 (and 671 nm), 627, 629, 694 (and 728, 785 nm) nm. We are aware that the unmixing of a single compound cannot be achieved using such RBC membrane spectral information. Based on spectral unmixing methods, which have recently enhanced HSI for fluorescence-guided brain tumor surgery58, further steps are needed to deepen the understanding of the individual spectral contributions arising from the complexity of the RBC membrane.

The RBCs of the healthy blood samples (n = 9) are then isolated and undergo work-up for the extraction of their membrane phospholipids, derivatization of all glycerol-ester-based phospholipids into their corresponding fatty acid methyl esters (FAME), according to an accredited protocol in compliance with the ISO/EIC 17025 regulation6,7,22. Using gas chromatographic (GC) analysis, the fatty acid composition of RBC membrane lipidome is obtained and expressed as relative quantitative percentages (mean ± SEM; see the results of untreated RBC in Supplementary Table 3). It is worth recalling that the fatty acids more sensitive to oxidative conditions are the polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), representing reliable biomarkers of such a process1–3. Statistical correlations are examined between the eight spectral distributions of healthy RBCs (reported in the Table of Fig. 1e) and the quantities of fatty acids in the RBC membrane lipidome of healthy individuals. In Fig. 2a, the correlation heatmap is shown (full R and p-values are reported in Supplementary Table 4). Four out of eight spectra (spectra 1, 4, 7 and 8) have distributions that correlate with the fatty acid values, whereas spectra 2, 3, 5, and 6 do not show correlations. We note that this is not an attempt to disentangle the contribution of specific fatty acids from the RBC membrane spectral features. Rather, our results suggest that HSI can characterize the membrane’s overall structural organization and correlate with fatty acid composition, and we proceed to apply these findings to the OS experiment, discussed in the next section.

Correlation heatmaps of the HSI spectral distributions (values in Fig. 1e) and the RBC membrane fatty acids (values in Supplementary Table 1) for untreated (a) and 1.5% H2O2-treated RBC (b). Significant positive and negative correlations are indicated with asterisks, and the full correlation data are reported in Supplementary Table 4.

The oxidative treatment of blood samples determines changes in HSI spectral data and in fatty acid-based lipidomics of RBC membranes

The EDTA-collected blood samples from nine healthy volunteers are treated with hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) under conditions that mimic OS induced by this reagent under various physiological and pathological stressors4,59,60. Blood samples are examined using HSI and lipidomic analysis. Different H2O2 concentrations, prepared from a commercially available stock solution in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and titrated according to literature data61,62, are tested for this oxidative treatment. The two concentrations of 0.75% or 1.5% H2O2 (as final concentrations in the samples) are chosen, after checking the absence of RBC hemolysis63 and the non-significant increase in oxidized hemoglobin64, which are typical indicators of severe and irreversible RBC membrane damage. The workflow of the OS experiment is shown in Fig. 1d and described in the “Experimental” section. Briefly, we incubate 50 μL of blood with an equal volume of the oxidant at the defined concentration for 1 h at room temperature; 2 μL of the mixture is used for HSI acquisition, and the remaining sample is used for RBC membrane lipidome analysis, as described above. In the optical image, no evident morphological changes are evidenced with respect to the previous healthy RBCs. From the SAM analysis of the HSI spectral graphs, the percentage distributions of the eight spectral endmembers in the RBC images are obtained (mean values ± SEM) for the 1.5% H2O2 experiment (Table in Fig. 1e, see column “Treated”, with the reported delta (%) variations and p-values compared to the healthy “Untreated” RBC). Again, narrow deviations are observed in the repeated measurements. Five endmembers are significantly different in their distribution compared with the healthy RBCs: spectra 1, 2, 5, 6, 7, with spectra 2 and 5 showing the highest percent variations, as follows: (a) spectrum two, having an average percent distribution of 11.07 ± 0.84% in untreated samples, shows a net decrease to 6.28 ± 0.44% in the oxidized samples (delta = −4.78%; p-value = 0.0001); (b) spectrum five, with an initial value of 27.62 ± 0.67%, shows an increase in the oxidized samples up to 31.65% ± 0.59% (delta = +4.03%; p = 0.0004). On the other hand, when RBCs are exposed to 0.75% H2O2 concentration, the spectral distribution values do not significantly change compared to the untreated RBC (Supplementary Table 2).

The H2O2-treated blood samples are also analysed for the RBC fatty acid composition of the membrane lipidome. Statistically significant changes are found compared to the untreated RBC only for the 1.5% H2O2, and reported in Supplementary Table 3: the total PUFA value decreases by about 25% (p = 0.024), with parallel decreases in the omega-6 and omega-3 families, resulting in a ratio that remained unchanged. At the same time, saturated fatty acids (SFA) increase (p = 0.028) by about 15%, causing a significant change in the SFA/MUFA ratio (p = 0.04). This compositional change results from the combined effects of PUFA peroxidation catalysed by oxidants and the exchange of damaged phospholipids with other phospholipids in the cellular pool that are rich in SFA4,6,60. It is worth recalling that changes in the unsaturated fatty acids of membrane phospholipids can alter the nanomechanical properties of the lipidome assembly, as evidenced by RBC PUFA rearrangements in obesity25.

Under our experimental conditions, the blood samples contained EDTA as the anticoagulant, a known iron chelator that could reduce iron levels, as observed in several processes, including ferroptosis65. However, the amount of EDTA is standardised in the hematology test tube; therefore, this effect is consistent across all samples. Other anticoagulants are not assessed at this stage, as EDTA is prioritised as an efficient calcium chelator after withdrawal, as previously explained.

Following H2O2 treatment and RBC isolation for membrane lipidome analysis, the correlation between the RBC membrane fatty acid composition and the percentage distribution of the eight spectral endmembers is re-evaluated. Interestingly, spectrum 5, whose distribution value increases after the OS insult, shows a different behaviour in the correlation map (Fig. 2b) compared with that reported for untreated blood samples (cfr, Fig. 2a; full R and p-values in Supplementary Table 4). Its distribution values now correlate with the membrane fatty acid values, showing: a positive correlation with the SFA stearic acid (C18:0, R = +0.833, p-value = 0.008), and negative correlations with different PUFAs, such as linoleic acid (C18:2; R = − 0.7, p-value = 0.04), docosahexaenoic acid (C22:6, R = −0.833, p-value = 0.008), with total PUFA (R = − 0.700, p-value = 0.043), total PUFA omega-3 (R = − 0.850, p-value = 0.0006), UI (unsaturation index, R = −0.783, p-value = 0.017) and PI (peroxidation index, R = −0.783, p-value = 0.017). Overall, the increased spectral distribution of endmember 5 in HSI mapping of oxidised RBCs correlates with decreases in PUFA and an increase in SFA, suggesting that this spectral endmember primarily reflects changes in membrane organisation induced by OS insult to the PUFA moieties. On the other hand, when considering the results with a 0,75% oxidant concentration, we can assume that this experiment serves as a “control” for the extent of perturbation, which significantly influences the membrane response. This is the first time that OS-related changes in RBC membranes have been mapped by HSI, employing a biologically relevant, well-characterised model of oxidative insult and rigorous experimental conditions. We are aware that further work, using a variety of stress insults, will improve the specificity of the endmember set, highlight the independent spectral components, and validate the use of HSI across different biological contexts.

The HSI features due to oxidative stress are also found in RBC from children with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)

The OS-induced changes in RBC detected by the HSI analysis prompt us to assess whether similar spectral changes could be observed in a pathological condition involving OS. Notably, our own and others’ research has identified OS markers in the blood and urine of children diagnosed with ASD conditions31–35. Thus, we examine RBCs from subjects with this clinical condition using HSI, compared with those from neurotypical (NT) subjects. The subjects discussed in this study are part of a larger investigation named “Complexity in Autism”, collecting and evaluating a wide range of clinical, biochemical, and biological parameters in male children with idiopathic autism, and comparing these parameters with those of NT subjects, matched for sex, age (3 to 8 years of age) and BMI. Details of the sampling are given in the “Methods”. Table 1 summarizes the main clinical and demographic characteristics of both cohorts.

Blood samples in EDTA of both NT (n = 31) and ASD (n = 27) groups undergo HSI analysis using the above-reported procedure (Fig. 1a). Figure 3a reports the results of the spectral distribution of the eight endmembers found for these two groups.

HSI data from ASD patients and NT controls and comparison with the RBC oxidative experiment.

Variations (delta) in ASD children are expressed in %, and significant p-values (≤0.05) are shown. Statistical comparisons were performed by applying unpaired two-sided t-test. b Comparison of the variations (+ or − delta %) of the 8 spectral endmembers for the H2O2-treated vs untreated RBC samples (OS, red bars (Fig. 1e) and for RBC samples from ASD vs NT subjects (Clinical study, yellow bars a). Five spectra show significant differences, four of them with p-values ≤ 0.0001 (spectra 1, 2, 5, 7, 8). Having the RBC model of OS as a reference for the HSI changes under an oxidative condition, we can make a comparison, evidencing a shared subset of significant spectral signatures affected in both ASD and following induction of OS (i.e., spectra 1, 2, 5, and 7; cfr, Figs. 1e and 3a). We focus our interest on spectra two and five of RBCs from subjects with ASD: spectrum two decreases by 3.43% (p = 0.0001) and spectrum five increases by 4.10% (p < 0.0001) compared with NT subjects. The ASD-associated spectral distribution changes of these two spectra mirror in the percent (%) and sign (+ or −) those found in the H2O2-treated RBCs, as shown in Fig. 3b, giving support to the hypothesis that common membrane alterations occur in the two conditions. This result provides pioneering evidence that HSI can detect changes in the components and architecture of RBC membranes, which correlate with OS-related health conditions in human subjects. It is worth emphasising that the OS model used in this work closely mimics the oxidative exposure of RBCs during blood circulation. The HSI analysis of such experimentally induced oxidative insults to RBC is linked here with a health condition known to be associated with oxidative stress. Our data points to spectrum five as the best indicator of the membrane cluster affected by OS. It must also be clarified that this result does not directly connect the use of HSI for diagnosing ASD. On the other hand, the cost-effectiveness, non-invasive nature, and broad applicability of HSI give support for extending this observational study to larger clinical settings, thereby enabling a comprehensive evaluation of its diagnostic capabilities and limitations.

Na+/K+-ATPase activity is severely decreased in ASD children and correlates with HSI spectral characteristics

To further investigate the relation of RBC membrane perturbation with the HSI patterns, we measure Na+/K+-activated Mg2+-dependent ATPase activity66 in RBC from ASD (n = 26) and neurotypical (NT, n = 30) subjects. RBCs are obtained from the same blood samples examined by HSI; one sample from the NT group and one from the ASD group are missing. Previous research demonstrated a significant decrease in this enzyme activity in individuals with ASD compared to NT31, which is attributed to OS-related alterations in plasma membrane organization, after having rigorously excluded a wide range of other possible causes67. As shown in Fig. 4a (complete data in Supplementary Table 5), the RBC Na+/K+-ATPase activity decreases in the ASD children cohort (−57%; p < 0.0001), with no overlapping values between NT and ASD (NT: mean 7.053; min. 5.245; max. 7.577; ASD: mean 3.002; min. 1.197; max. 3.865). This result is an essential confirmation of previous results obtained in a different cohort of NT and ASD children31.

Na+/K+-ATPase activity and its correlations with HSI spectral distribution in RBC.

a The activity of Na+/K+-ATPase is compared between NT (n = 30) and ASD (n = 26) children, showing a net difference between the two groups. Statistical comparison was performed by applying a non-parametric two-sided Mann–Whitney test (p = 1.149E-34); the significant correlations between Na+/K+-ATPase values and the HSI spectral endmembers distributions are shown for NT (b) and ASD subjects (c). Full data are reported in Supplementary Table 5.

Interestingly, Na+/K+-ATPase activity does not correlate with age-corrected ADOS-2 scores or with the subject’s age (data not shown). The observed differences in Na+/K+-ATPase activity among the human cohorts support the involvement of oxidative stress, which is known to impair membrane protein function, as previously discussed31,67. The values of the Na+/K+-ATPase activity are correlated with the HSI spectral distributions in NT and ASD subjects, with significance for some of them (cfr, Fig. 4): spectra three and five associate with protein activity in both NT and ASD subjects, whereas the significant correlation seen for spectrum 2 in the NT subjects is lost in the ASD subjects. This finding aligns with the understanding that membrane structure and organization play crucial roles in the function of the embedded proteins, which are influenced by dynamic changes in protein-lipid interactions within the membrane68.

Data analysis of HSI signatures with artificial intelligence Tools reveals a high sensitivity and specificity in stratifying ASD and NT subjects

Artificial Intelligence tools are used to corroborate the results of hyperspectral imaging of RBC in the stratification of NT and ASD subjects and to develop a predictive model capable of distinguishing between Autism and Typical Development conditions using the available spectral data. To evaluate the feasibility of predicting outcome (autistic vs. neurotypical) from selected variables in the study, we use supervised Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) with a backpropagation algorithm, utilising the training and validation/test protocol developed at the Semeion Research Centre37–44. Figure 5a–d shows the results of ANN analysis combined with TWIST, which indicates that HSI analysis can discriminate between the two sets of subjects (NT and ASD) with high sensitivity (92.31) and specificity (94.12), overall accuracy = 93.21 and AUC = 0.92 (Fig. 5c). For details of the mathematical and technical characteristics of back propagation ANN see Table 2.

The semantic connectivity map of the variables in the study is explored by Auto-contractive map (Auto-CM), a fourth-generation artificial neural network coupled with a minimum spanning tree filter37,38. This tool yields the semantic connectivity map shown in Fig. 5d, where two digits identify each node. The first one refers to the spectrum (Sp) number (N.1-8), while the second refers to the spectrum replica number (N = 1–9). As shown, the spectrum replicas tend to cluster, except for two outliers (see arrows in Fig. 5d). The ASD group is connected to spectrum 5. In contrast, the NT group (i.e., Controls) is connected to the spectrum 4 family (variables “Controls” and “ASD” are enclosed by the black circles). Coloured areas identify replicas of the same spectral endmember. Although these results are preliminary, they suggest that spectrum 5 is the most relevant for detecting differences between ASD and NT individuals. These differences, which relate to a model of RBCs, appear to be associated with oxidative conditions.

In the “Methods” and Supplementary Information sections, further details of the AI protocol and AutoCM are given.

Conclusions

Figure 6 shows the entire workflow, from HSI data collection using RBC images from a small sample of EDTA-treated whole blood to the AI-based group classification of OS-affected RBC.

The pipeline of HSI data acquisition and AI-aided analysis.

starting from a small sample of EDTA-treated whole blood, the pipeline includes: HSI acquisition and definition of the RBC endmember library collection, SAM classification with the endmember distributions in healthy and OS-related RBC, the AI-aided group classification output, recognizing OS-altered RBC. Figure created by the authors.

This study establishes a pioneering HSI protocol for identifying OS in red blood cells by utilizing a standardized OS model of H2O2-induced modifications as a reference against RBC from healthy donors. Specifically, this approach enables the detection of OS in children and, employing AI-driven analysis of HSI data, achieves an accuracy of 93.2% in stratifying ASD subjects. However, it is essential to underline that this methodology is not yet a diagnostic tool for identifying ASD. To fully realize its clinical potential, extensive large-scale studies are required to examine RBCs across a broader spectrum of OS-related conditions and pathologies. On the other hand, the ability of HSI to detect membrane modifications in a minimally invasive manner—requiring only two microliters of blood—and without any labeling, holds significant promise for future membrane-based diagnostics. Such a technique could guide personalized intervention aimed at promoting the natural membrane remodeling and monitoring the restoration of antioxidant protection. Notably, HSI technology provides insight into the 3-D organization of the cell membrane, an organization likely altered by OS. This explains the observed correlation between HSI features and the activity of enzymes embedded in the membrane. Ultimately, the robust and accessible nature of this HSI procedure, combined with the rapid evolution of AI, is poised to accelerate translational medical breakthroughs.
