Radiological and multidisciplinary management of extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcoma: A case report
Authors: Finidya Septiani, Leksmana Arry Chandra, Waluyo Sugito, Familia Bella Rahadiati, Vininta Fazharyasti, Yulia Rachmawati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcoma (EMC), a very rare subtype of soft tissue sarcoma (STS), distinguished by its histopathological profile, has unique clinical prognosis of potential for late local recurrences and distant metastases due to its molecular alterations. The infrequent occurrence of EMC, coupled with its radiological characteristics that often overlap with those of other myxoid-rich neoplasms, may impose considerable diagnostic difficulties for clinicians. A 43-year-old male who presented with a progressively enlarging mass in the distal right thigh and knee region since six-month prior. Initial imaging studies, including computed tomography (CT) and non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), suggested a diagnosis of liposarcoma. However, further evaluation including a comprehensive contrast-enhanced MRI revealed a large multilobulated, well-defined soft tissue mass at the distal anteromedial femur. The lesion demonstrated isointense to hypointense signal on T1-weighted sequence and hyperintensity on T2-weighted/STIR sequences reflecting myxoid matrix, and prominent peripheral and septal enhancement post-gadolinium administration. The mass also exhibited local bone erosion and infiltration into surrounding musculature and the knee joint. A core needle biopsy followed by immunohistochemical profiling was consistent with a diagnosis of EMC. Following multidisciplinary consultation, the patient underwent successful limb salvage surgery, involving wide excision of the tumor and reconstruction using prosthesis. This case underscores the critical role of advanced imaging techniques in characterizing soft tissue masses and suggesting EMC in the differential diagnosis prior to histopathological confirmation. Moreover, multidisciplinary approach for the effective management of this rare sarcoma, including complex surgical reconstruction to preserve limb function is essential.

Introduction

Soft tissue sarcoma (STS) consists of heterogeneous group of malignant neoplasms with mesenchymal origins, representing about 1% of all adult cancers worldwide [1]. Within this group, extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcoma (EMC) emerges as a distinctly rare and diagnostically complex type. Classified as a very rare form of STS, EMC exhibits an annual incidence of under one case per million individuals and accounts for at most 1–3% of all soft tissue sarcomas [1,2]. This rarity, combined with its singular biological behavior and a clinical course that is often indolent yet tenaciously persistent, create a difficult in pointing accurate diagnosis and treatment strategies.

Extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcoma is defined by its histopathologic characteristics, marked by a proliferation of rounded or spindle-shaped cells situated within an abundant myxoid matrix, frequently organized into a multinodular or cord-like configuration [1,2]. Despite its name implying cartilaginous differentiation, EMC does not form true hyaline cartilage, different from conventional skeletal chondrosarcomas. A fundamental aspect of EMC's pathogenesis and a key diagnostic indicator is the presence of specific chromosomal translocations, most notably t(9;22) (q22;q12), leading to the creation of the EWSR1-NR4A3 fusion gene. This genetic anomaly is regarded as the primary mutation driver, with alternative partners such as TAF15 at TCF12 fusing to NR4A3 in other cases [[1], [2], [3]].

Classic EMC usually presents as a slow-growing mass in the soft tissues of the proximal extremities, especially at the thigh and popliteal fossa, with its predilection in middle-aged adults, with a slight male bias [1,[3], [4], [5]]. Although often initially painless, the mass may enlarge and result in local compression effects, pain, and compromised function. A particularly notable characteristic of EMC is its tendency for both local recurrence and distant metastasis, even following adequate treatment. Metastases most frequently affect the lungs, but can also manifest in other soft tissues and bone, sometimes appearing years or even decades after the primary diagnosis, signifying the need of extended period of follow-up [6,7].

The contribution of radiology, especially magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), is paramount in the diagnosis and treatment of EMC. MRI affords precise characterization of the morphology, extent, and relationship to adjacent neurovascular structures, thereby guiding biopsy planning and surgical resection. The classic MRI appearance of EMC is well-defined, multilobulated mass, hypointense to isointense on T1-weighted images and markedly hyperintense on T2-weighted images, a consequence of the high water content within the myxoid matrix. Post-gadolinium administration, a characteristic peripheral and septal enhancement pattern is often evident, reflecting the more cellular areas and fibrous septa within the tumor. However, it may also overlap with other myxoid-rich soft tissue tumors [1,4].

The management of EMC typically revolves around wide local excision with negative margins, which optimal opportunity for local control, while adjuvant radiotherapy may be considered in cases with positive margins or when wide excision cannot be performed [1,3,6]. This report aims to present a case of EMC which emphasize the diagnostic challenge from initial radiological suspicion to definitive histopathological and immunohistochemical confirmation, and the subsequent multidisciplinary management, including limb salvage surgery with megaprosthesis reconstruction.

Case report

A 43-year-old male patient was admitted to our hospital with a six-month history of worsening pain and a palpable, enlarging mass in his right knee. The pain was initially dull and localized, but it had become more severe in the two months prior to admission with visual analog score of 7-8/10. The patient said that the mass was initially small but progressively increased in size. His past medical history was notable for an anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction on the same knee ten years earlier. He denied any trauma to the area, fever, and unintentional weight loss. Prior to the admission, he already had a X-ray examination and CT scan of his right knee, revealing a large soft tissue mass, approximately 10 cm x 8 cm in his medial distal femur. The mass was observed to be eroding the medial femoral cortex, displacing the vastus medialis muscle, and infiltrating the lateral condyle, extending into the knee joint, suspected to be a liposarcoma. Subsequently, the patient was referred to other hospital, where a non-contrast MRI of the right knee was performed, confirming the presence of a soft tissue mass in the distal femur, involving both the medial, anterior, and posterior aspects of the femur, supporting the suspicion of liposarcoma further (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Knee non-contrast CT-scan showing 10×8 cm mass eroding medial femoral cortex and pressing the vastus medialis muscle.Fig 1 – dummy alt text

Upon arrival at our center, the patient was fully alert with stable vital signs. Inspection of the right lower extremity revealed a prominent, firm, fixed mass in the distal anteromedial right femur, measuring approximately 15×10 cm at its greatest dimension. The overlying skin was intact, without erythema or ulceration. There was intact sensation and motor function in the foot and ankle. The patient was able to bear weight and ambulate, albeit with discomfort and a slight antalgic gait. Initial laboratory investigations were all within normal limits. In order to establish a definitive histopathological diagnosis, a core needle biopsy of the mass was performed, showing a cellular tumor composed of cells arranged in sheets, solid cords, and pseudotubular structures. The cells exhibited pleomorphic, hyperchromatic and vesicular nuclei with coarse chromatin, and occasional abnormal mitotic figures. The cytoplasm was eosinophilic, and the cells were embedded within an amorphous basophilic myxoid matrix, interspersed with fibromyxoid connective tissue and skeletal muscle fibers The initial histopathological impression was a "round cell tumor," with differentials including metastatic carcinoma or plasmacytoma. Furthermore, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining reported the results to be as consistent with extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcoma (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Pathological images showing results consistent with extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcoma (left: H&E staining, 100x magnification, right: H&E staining, 400x magnification).Fig 2 – dummy alt text

For a detailed preoperative assessment of the mass and its local extension, a contrast-enhanced MRI of the right knee was performed. In this examination, a heterogeneously enhancing soft tissue mass in the distal anteromedial right femur measuring 13×11,2×9,2 cm was found. The mass was isointense to hypointense on T1-weighted images and markedly hyperintense on T2-weighted (TSE), TIRM, and PD fat-saturated sequences, with well-defined, lobulated margins. Following intravenous gadolinium, there was strong peripheral and septal enhancement, with central non-enhancing areas suggestive of necrosis. During the examination, it was found that the lesion had obliterated the vastus medialis and vastus intermedius muscles, extended into the medial knee joint, involved the medial collateral ligament and medial patellofemoral retinaculum, and displaced the popliteal artery and vein posteriorly. It also eroded and destroyed the cortex and medulla of the distal right femoral metadiaphysis and extended into the anteromedial subcutaneous tissue. Staging investigations, including a non-contrast thoracoabdominal CT-scan were performed to evaluate for distant metastases, revealing no evidence of metastasis (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Contrast enhanced MRI examination of the right knee.Fig 3 – dummy alt text

Following multidisciplinary discussion involving orthopedic oncology, radiology, and pathology, the decision was made for surgical resection. The patient underwent a limb salvage procedure consisting of wide excision of the tumor followed by reconstruction with a prosthesis. The tumor was excised in en bloc fashion with wide margins of healthy tissue with frozen section analysis intraoperatively. Intra-articular extension necessitated detachment of the joint capsule, cruciate ligaments, collateral ligaments, and menisci. An osteotomy was performed approximately 18 cm from the distal femoral articular surface. Adjuvant cryotherapy with liquid nitrogen was applied to the patellar articular surface and healthy muscle margins. Subsequently, a prostehsis was implanted to reconstruct the femur and tibia. A drain was placed, and the wound was closed in layers. A week follow-up post-operative X-rays of the right knee and femur confirmed satisfactory prosthesis position and fixation, with no acute complications like fracture or loosening (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Intraoperative images and postoperative X-ray images of the patient.Fig 4 – dummy alt text

Final histopathological examination of the resected specimen, confirmed the diagnosis of extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcoma. The tumor showed a lobular growth pattern with islands and cords of malignant cells within a prominent myxoid stroma, consistent with previous biopsy findings. The post-operative recovery period was uneventful, with patient discharged a week following the surgery. The patient was scheduled for regular follow-up for clinical and radiological monitoring following the discharge.

Discussion

This case of extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcoma (EMC) of the distal right femur illuminates several critical aspects of this rare neoplasm, including its diagnostic challenges, radiological and histopathological features, and the complexities of its multidisciplinary management. EMC, comprising of about 1-3% of all soft tissue sarcomas, is a very rare entity, making its initial recognition and accurate diagnosis a formidable challenge for both clinicians and radiologists [1]. The initial presentation of enlarging mass and progressive pain in the proximal lower extremity aligns with the typical clinical profile of EMC, which most commonly affects deep soft tissues of the thigh and popliteal region in middle-aged adults, with a slight male bias. Moreover, the dominant mass in the soft tissue with secondary bone involvement, cortical erosion without primary medullary center, and no aggressive periosteal reaction indicated that the mass was of extraskeletal origin [1,[3], [4], [5]].

Initial radiological evaluations at referring hospitals, including CT and MRI, suggested liposarcoma. This misdiagnosis underscores the significant overlap in imaging characteristics between EMC and other myxoid-rich soft tissue tumors, particularly myxoid liposarcoma (MLS). Both entities can appear as large, well-defined, multilobulated masses with high T2 signal due to their myxoid content. However, careful MRI analysis can provide crucial distinguishing clues [4]. It is only after contrast enhanced MRI that highly suggestive features of EMC was found. Erosion of the femoral cortex and medullary involvement, noted on CT and MRI, can occur in larger EMCs and was a critical finding here, broadening the initial differential to include primary bone sarcomas like osteosarcoma. This bone involvement, while not universal in EMC, indicates locally aggressive behavior and necessitates careful surgical planning [1,2,4].

The cornerstone of definitive EMC diagnosis remains histopathological examination. The core needle biopsy initially yielded a "round cell tumor" diagnosis, with differentials including metastatic carcinoma or plasmacytoma. This highlights the morphological spectrum of EMC, which sometimes mimics other round cell neoplasms. Subsequent immunohistochemical (IHC) profiling played a pivotal role in narrowing the diagnosis [[1], [2], [3]]. Other identifying characteristic of EMC is chromosomal translocations involving the NR4A3 gene. The most common translocation, t(9;22) (q22;q12), results in the EWSR1-NR4A3 fusion gene, present in almost all of the cases, with alternative fusion partners for NR4A3 include TAF15 (t(9;17)(q22;q11)), TCF12 (t(9;15)(q22;q21)) [3].

The management of localized EMC primarily involves wide surgical excision with negative margins, offering the best chance for local control and long-term survival [2,6]. In this case, limb salvage surgery with wide excision and reconstruction using a prosthesis was successfully performed. This approach is particularly valuable for large tumors involving major bones and joints, allowing for resection with preservation of limb function which was crucial for quality of life.

The role of radiotherapy in EMC is not as firmly established as in some other high-grade STS. It may have some function improving local control, particularly in cases with close or positive margins, or when wide excision is compromised [1,7]. The decision regarding adjuvant radiotherapy in this case would have been based on final margin status, tumor biological aggressiveness, and multidisciplinary discussion. Similarly, adjuvant chemotherapy is not routinely recommended for EMC due to the lack of evidence. However, it might be considered in high-risk cases or within clinical trial contexts

There are some limitations in this study. First, there is no long-term follow-up examination results of the patient. Despite a generally more favorable prognosis than many other STS, EMC is characterized by a significant risk of late local recurrence and distant metastasis [1,3]. Second, only non-contrast abdominal imaging was performed in this patient. Although metastases in EMC most commonly affect the lungs, it may also involve soft tissues (such as liver nodules), bone, and lymph nodes, thus contrast imaging for initial and follow-up staging remains essential. A particularly challenging feature of EMC is the potential for metastases to emerge many years, even decades, after initial diagnosis and treatment. This necessitates long-term, often lifelong, clinical and radiological surveillance. Recommended follow-up typically involves regular physical examinations and chest imaging (CT scans) every 3-6 months for the first 2-3 years, then every 6 months up to 5 years, and annually thereafter, or as clinically indicated [1,4].

This case report, therefore, not only illustrates a typical presentation and management pathway for EMC but also implicitly highlights the ongoing need for research into this rare disease. The initial diagnostic dilemma, reliance on specialized histopathological and molecular techniques for confirmation, challenges in adjuvant therapy decision-making, and ever-present risk of late recurrence all point to the complexities inherent in caring for EMC patients. A multidisciplinary team approach is paramount for optimal patient outcomes. Continued efforts to understand the molecular drivers of EMC, identify reliable prognostic biomarkers, and develop more effective systemic therapies are essential to further improve the outlook for patients with this enigmatic sarcoma.

Conclusion

Successful management of EMC requires a high degree of clinical suspicion, accurate multimodal diagnostic workup integrating radiology, histopathology, and molecular genetics, a multidisciplinary team for comprehensive treatment planning, and a long-term commitment to follow-up care.
