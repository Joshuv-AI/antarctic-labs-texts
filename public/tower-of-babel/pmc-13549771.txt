Evaluation of polyampholyte thin film hydrogels for the prevention of Ralstonia pickettii adhesion in microgravity
Authors: Adrienne Shea, Skyler Oneida, Kristopher V. Waynant, Matthew T. Bernards
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Human health is impacted by bacteria within drinking water. This study investigates polyampholyte coatings and their resistance to Ralstonia pickettii adhesion. Zwitterionic crosslinkers significantly enhanced the resistance of the hydrogel coatings, with Serine–Serine dimethacrylate demonstrating the best performance. These coatings show promise for reducing bacterial adhesion in drinking water systems.

For the first time since the International Space Station (ISS) began seeing human occupancy, the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) had to undertake an emergency evacuation of astronauts due to an undisclosed medical concern in January 2026. While the ISS has existing procedures for emergency evacuation, this requires coordination of a high-stakes, unscheduled departure.1 Additionally, NASA has stated goals of sending a crewed mission to Mars. A trip to Mars would have no hope of executing an emergency return within the timeframe necessary to treat a medical emergency or mechanical failure requiring parts not already aboard the spacecraft.2 Therefore, it is imperative to add as many layers of protection as possible to strengthen contamination preventative measures employed by NASA.

A common cause of both mechanical failures and illness is the presence of bacteria on board space craft. Biofilm formation follows the initial adhesion of individual bacterial cells, after they multiply and form a surrounding protective layer of extracellular polymeric matrix.3 This barrier complicates combative measures to eliminate biofilm since it prevents antimicrobial or disinfection treatments from reaching the interior bacterial cells.4 With the inner layer of bacteria surviving and reproducing, complete elimination of biofilm becomes virtually impossible.

One mechanical system functionally impacted by biofilm, while also distributing microbes to astronauts, is the potable water system. In 2010, a portion of the potable water system aboard the ISS had to be replaced due to a mechanical failure from biofouling. During early habitation of the ISS a survey was conducted to establish a baseline microbial presence, and twenty-seven bacterial colony types, including Gram negative Ralstonia (R.) pickettii, were isolated from potable water samples.5–11R. pickettii has previously exhibited adaptability to low nutrient environments, like the conditions within water reclamation systems.12–14 These bacterial isolates are also known to be strong biofilm formers that have shown increased resistance to disinfectants and antimicrobials under microgravity conditions.2,15–17 Additionally, these pathogens have demonstrated enhanced expression of genes responsible for virulence factors and more rapidly enter the log phase of the bacterial growth curve.18–23

The US National Institutes of Health report that biofilms are causally identified in 80% of microbial infections seen within the human body on Earth.24 Astronauts see further health risks including observed changes in the human microbiome.1,24 It was documented during the Soviet Union operation of both space stations Salyut and Mir, that reductions in intestinal bacteria were seen after two weeks of confinement.25,26 Spaceflight is also known to suppress functionality of both the innate and adaptive immune response.1 NASA has extensive countermeasures to combat these issues including but not limited to extensive medical screening of astronauts and isolation prior to missions.2 However, given the contamination concerns identified, and despite these stringent countermeasures, it is of the utmost importance to bolster the current methods of microbial containment to reduce risk to astronauts and mechanical systems.

One promising avenue of biofilm prevention is nonfouling polymers.27 Nonfouling polymer coatings are utilized to prevent initial bacterial adhesion to surfaces. Preventing biofilm adds a layer of protection to systems that current methods of mitigation such as antimicrobials cannot achieve.28–33 Within the category of nonfouling polymers, one subset, zwitterions, has emerged demonstrating the highest resistance to bacterial adhesion.27 Zwitterionic materials combine cationic and anionic charges in equal numbers to produce a net neutrally charged molecule. The polyampholyte polymers of focus here fall under the zwitterionic umbrella as they combine a cationic monomer, 2-(acryloyloxy)ethyl] trimethylammonium chloride (TMA), and anionic monomer, 2-carboxyethyl acrylate (CAA), in equimolar concentrations to create a net neutrally charged copolymer. Previous work has demonstrated the ability of TMA/CAA copolymers to resist bacterial adhesion and that zwitterionic crosslinkers further enhance their ability to resist bacterial adhesion.33,34 These studies were conducted under gravity impacted and simulated microgravity conditions.33,35 In this study, the bacterial resistance of polyampholyte polymers and the impact of crosslinker chemistry will be evaluated under true microgravity conditions aboard the ISS. The control crosslinker is polyethylene glycol (PEG) based; diethylene glycol dimethacrylate (DEGDMA) was selected since it is a widely evaluated standard for nonfouling coatings.36–40 Two zwitterionic crosslinkers, carboxybetaine dimethacrylate (CBMAX)40 and [N-(2-ammonio-3-(methacryloyloxy) propanoyl)-O-methacryloylserinate (Ser–Ser), are compared to the DEGDMA control samples and uncoated stainless steel to evaluate crosslinker chemistry impacts on bacterial adhesion. All three crosslinker species can be seen in Fig. 2.

In this study, a payload was designed to test the ability of TMA/CAA thin film hydrogels to prevent R. pickettii adhesion to stainless steel substrates under microgravity aboard the ISS. The results obtained under microgravity are also compared to an identical experiment executed under gravity impacted conditions on Earth. Stainless steel was chosen since it is one of the main components of the water reclamation system aboard spacecraft and in high purity water systems globally. Mirror polished 304 stainless steel coupons were coated with TMA/CAA thin film hydrogels crosslinked with either one of the zwitterionic crosslinkers or the non-ionic control, DEGDMA. The coatings were then evaluated for their ability to resist adhesion of R. pickettii over 58 days and compared to both uncoated stainless steel and the parallel Earth-based payload.

The first stage of this work was focused on the design of an experimental platform that would meet the requirements necessary for bacterial experimentation aboard the ISS to assess the efficacy of thin film polymer hydrogel resistance to bacterial adhesion under microgravity conditions. This included the primary requirement of no interaction from the crew aside from the initial connection of the experimental unit to power. The final design of the system and detailed design and operational conditions are described in the SI. A second requirement of the design was to ensure that no bacteria was introduced to the coated and uncoated experimental coupons until the payload was experiencing microgravity conditions aboard the ISS. This objective was achieved by storing lyophilized R. pickettii within pistons that were released into the experimental chambers upon power up. From 24 August until 29 December 2025, the payload was refrigerated to maintain dormancy of the lyophilized bacteria contained in the system. Based on estimated food to mass ratio, each experimental chamber was designed to maintain nutrients for the population for approximately 28 days. However, due to a crew member medical emergency, the schedule for return to Earth was shifted and the payload remained in operation for 58 days. An identical experimental system was fabricated and operated concurrently on Earth, to serve as a gravity impacted control.

The TMA/CAA thin film hydrogels placed in both the ISS and Earth based boxes were synthesized directly on the underlying ½” diameter stainless steel coupons using silane surface couplers consistent with previous work. Based on previous evaluation under both gravity impacted and simulated microgravity, this study focused on the highest performing conditions which were found to be a 5 mM silane concentration for surface coupling and a spin coater speed of 3000 rpm.33,35 Thorough characterization of the surface properties under these coating conditions including atomic force microscopy for surface roughness and contact angle goniometry for hydrophilicity have also been outlined for each of the three different crosslinked polyampholyte copolymer systems and the stainless-steel controls.33

The performance of the polyampholyte thin film copolymers was evaluated after 55 days of R. pickettii exposure in microgravity and three days of gravity exposure while the payload returned to Earth and travelled back to the University of Idaho. The Earth-based control box was evaluated after 58 days of gravity exposure. It is acknowledged that this extended experimental time length likely impacted overall bacterial viability, but the bacterial surface coverage between samples remains directly comparable because the timing was consistent across all results. The levels of bacterial adhesion were evaluated based on percent surface coverage from confocal microscopy imaging after the experimental coupons were stained using Baclight red. Previous investigations have shown that under gravity impacted conditions all three species of crosslinked hydrogels had a greater than 99% reduction in R. pickettii surface coverage after seven days of exposure.33 Further, under simulated microgravity Ser–Ser crosslinked hydrogels only had ∼0.002% overall fluorescence coverage after seven days of exposure to R. pickettii.35

In this study, it is apparent that both the DEGDMA and CBMAX lose some of their bacterial resistance over the extended evaluation. The overall best performance under both microgravity and gravity impacted conditions was seen from the Ser–Ser crosslinked thin film hydrogels. Some of the bacterial adhesion seen on the thin film hydrogels can be attributed to the nanoscale surface features created during the spin coating process, so further optimization of the coating procedures would enhance the Ser–Ser crosslinked thin performance. For example, previous studies of bulk Ser–Ser hydrogels have shown bacterial adhesion resistance in excess of 99.99% after 28 days of repeat exposure to R. pickettii.34

Fig. 5 shows the relative reduction in overall surface coverage from Earth to microgravity aboard the ISS. While there was a reduction in bacterial adhesion across all of the experimental coupons from Earth to Space, the reduction in adhesion to the uncoated stainless steel was the most significant. This reduction was most likely due to the lack of sedimentation due to the samples being mounted on the sides of the chambers to minimize experimental differences from the gravity impacted to microgravity payload. Sedimentation effects can be neglected for some small microorganisms like viruses but cannot be neglected for larger macromolecules such as bacteria.41 Additionally, the uncoated controls had the most surface coverage to begin with which further enhanced the reduction percentage. This is contrary to many studies which indicate bacterial virulence and proliferation increase in space1 suggesting there may be a balancing act between an increase in virulence and contact with the exposed surface.

Fig. 6 provides a normalized evaluation of the experimental results relative to the uncoated stainless-steel controls. These values were determined by dividing the individual mean bacterial surface coverage by the bacterial surface coverage for the appropriate stainless-steel results (Space or Earth). The results indicate that both the DEGDMA and CBMAX coatings performed better under gravity-impacted environments, as they had lower normalized surface coverage relative to their respective micro-gravity-based results. Interestingly, the results for the Ser–Ser coated substrates indicate that the micro-gravity environment had no impact on the coating performance, as the Ser–Ser samples maintained ∼30% of the bacterial surface coverage relative to the uncoated controls under both conditions.

Bacterial surface coverage normalized to the uncoated stainless steel control substrate (none).

Overall, Ser–Ser crosslinked thin films had a significantly superior performance compared to all other coatings under both gravity impacted and microgravity conditions. These results show that the TMA/CAA copolymer hydrogel crosslinked with the zwitterionic Ser–Ser crosslinker shows the most promise because it had the greatest reduction in bacterial adhesion after this long term 58 days study. However, the adhesion that was quantified on the Ser–Ser crosslinked copolymers could be further reduced through the optimization of the coating process. The bacterial adhesion of 0.9% can be further reduced by eliminating remaining nanoscale surface features of the thin film hydrogels based on recent results quantifying bacteria adhesion to bulk polymer hydrogels with an identical formulation.34 Spin coating leads to some nanoscale surface features which could be eliminated with a different coating procedure such as dip coating, which would also be more suitable for larger scale applications. Despite this drawback, the Ser–Ser crosslinked system had statistically lower levels compared to all other substrates and represents a promising path forward for reducing bacterial adhesion during long duration space missions.

Bacterial adhesion and the subsequent biofilm formation present significant impediments to long duration spaceflight. By the nature of the timing, access to medical care and spare mechanical parts during long duration missions are strictly limited to what can be carried aboard the spacecraft. TMA/CAA polyampholyte copolymer thin film hydrogels were evaluated in this study for their ability to prevent adhesion of R. pickettii under microgravity conditions aboard the ISS and the results were compared to gravity impacted conditions here on Earth. After 58 days of exposure to bacterial culture, all of the experimental coupons exposed to microgravity conditions aboard the ISS showed significantly less adhesion than their counterparts exposed to gravity on Earth. Additionally, Ser–Ser crosslinked thin film hydrogels showed statistically superior bacterial adhesion resistance compared to all other crosslinked thin films and the stainless-steel control. These results suggest that Ser–Ser crosslinked thin films demonstrate promise for increasing the bacterial resistance of stainless-steel surfaces for widespread water distribution systems and warrant further investigation into optimization of the thin film hydrogel coating properties.
