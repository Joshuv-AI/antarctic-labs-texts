Evaluating an innovative support programme for older long-term cancer survivors (IMPULS-A): protocol for a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Till Johannes Bugaj, Charlotte Flock, Elena Scapinello, Joachim Wiskemann, Dirk Jäger, Jürgen M Bauer, Beate Wild, Eva Winkler, Volker Arndt, Julien Frick, Daniela Doege, Katja Mehlis, Sarina Carter, Annette Eidam, Martin Bongartz, Rea Kuehl, Tabea Bertram, Sabrina Scherbarth, Bernhard Ortseifen, Imke Veit-Schirmer, Mechthild Hartmann, Hans-Christoph Friederich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMore than 5 million people in Germany are living with cancer or a history of cancer, 40% of them aged 75 years or older. Cancer and its treatment may lead to reduced physiological reserves, multiple somatic comorbidities and a combination of functional and psychosocial health problems in addition to the long-term and late effects of cancer and its treatment. Therefore, providing tailored care and support through an interdisciplinary care network is crucial. The aim of the IMPULS-A (Implementation of a Support Programme for Long-Term Cancer Survivors in Old Age) study is to evaluate an innovative survivorship programme designed to address the specific needs of older cancer survivors by improving access to regional care networks.Methods and analysisIMPULS-A is a prospective, two-arm, randomised controlled trial. Altogether, n=724 cancer survivors ≥70 years, who have completed the course of first line/primary treatment, with an expected lifespan of >3 years, identified support needs and live in proximity (≤100 km) of the National Center for Tumor Diseases Heidelberg, Germany, will be randomly allocated (1:1) to an intervention or control group. All eligible participants complete study evaluation questionnaires— either via LimeSurvey or paper-based — at three time points: T0 (at randomisation), T1 (T0+9 months) and T2 (T0+18 months). The primary outcome is health literacy. Secondary outcomes are digital health literacy, quality of life, effects on preventive health measures and healthcare utilisation, personal resources, treatment satisfaction, acceptability, appropriateness and feasibility of the survivorship care model and support needs of relatives in the long-term survival phase. For participants in the intervention group, a care navigator provides appropriate referrals and information based on a biannual biopsychosocial screening. Participants with complex needs are reviewed and discussed in an interdisciplinary survivor board. Participants in the control group receive the current standard treatment. Data analysis will be performed according to the intention-to-treat principle. The primary hypothesis will be analysed using a multilevel model. Missing values will be handled using the mixed models for repeated measures method. Additionally, qualitative, semi-structured interviews with patients from both study arms will explore which aspects are important to older people in relation to a life worth living after cancer.Ethics and disseminationWritten informed consent will be obtained from all participants prior to study enrolment. The protocol, informed consent forms and information letters have been approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical Faculty of Heidelberg University (S-692/2024), the Ethics Committee of the State Medical Association of Baden-Württemberg (B-F-2025-056) and the Ethics Committee of the State Medical Association of Rhineland-Palatinate (2025–18207). The study is conducted in accordance with the current version of the Declaration of Helsinki. Study results will be disseminated through publication in peer-reviewed journals and presentations at relevant scientific congresses and conferences.Trial registration numberISRCTN14717755.Keywords: ONCOLOGY, Survival, Patient-Centered Care, Quality of Life, Psychosocial Intervention, Frailty

Introduction

The term cancer survivor is widely used in English-speaking contexts to refer to people living with cancer or who have experienced it at some point in their lives.1 Demographic ageing, advances in early cancer detection, modern cancer therapy and age-appropriate treatment planning2 have contributed to an increasing number of older adults surviving cancer.3

In Germany, more than 5 million people are living with or after cancer and about 40% are aged ≥75 years.4 This group often suffers from reduced physiological reserves and complex overlaps of multiple somatic comorbidities. Older cancer survivors also face functional and psychosocial health problems, along with numerous disease- and treatment-related long-term and late effects.5 6

Health literacy is understood as a multidimensional concept encompassing individuals’ ability to access, understand and use health-related information and services to make informed decisions and manage health-related challenges.7 Various studies have demonstrated that older adults tend to have lower levels of health literacy.8

Recent evidence indicates that health literacy plays a critical role in shaping health outcomes among cancer survivors. A large cross-sectional study including 4589 survivors demonstrated that low health literacy is independently associated with substantially poorer quality of life.9 Similarly, data from the Cancer and Aging Resilience Evaluation Registry — comprising 876 older adults with cancer — show that limited health literacy is strongly associated with increased frailty, impaired physical and mental health-related quality of life and higher hospitalisation rates after adjustment for major confounders.10

Together, this growing body of evidence highlights limited health literacy as an independent and modifiable risk factor in older cancer survivors, consistently associated with important survivorship-related outcomes.9 10 As a modifiable factor, it may facilitate access to adequate information, healthcare services and supportive care resources. Improved health literacy may support cancer survivors in navigating the (complex) healthcare system and making informed health-related decisions,11 12 although this potential relationship has not yet been established.

Targeted interventions to strengthen health literacy may contribute to more favourable survivorship experiences in this vulnerable and expanding population. Improving health literacy within this population could also enhance understanding and promote more effective utilisation of available services. Therefore, it is important to provide care and support services that comprehensively address these issues in an interprofessional manner and help prevent further long-term and late effects.

Most cancer survivorship programmes aim to support patients with cancer after the end of acute medical treatment, particularly in the transition from illness to survivorship,13 and to provide adequate individual care that addresses the existing need for advice and treatment. The main goals of these programmes14 are to detect and treat medical and psychosocial long-term and late effects, provide interventions to mitigate the consequences of cancer and its treatment, improve coordination between aftercare specialists and primary care providers, and prevent tumour recurrence, second cancers and other late complications. Due to the high complexity of care needs, survivorship programmes should maintain an interprofessional network to provide care tailored to patients’ individual needs.

In recent years, two major projects in Germany have evaluated interventions to improve health literacy, communication and self-determination in oncology patients. In the OSCAR15 project (Oncological Social Care Project), social care nurses supported patients and relatives for 1 year after inpatient treatment. Quality of life improved at 6 months but not at 12 months, partly due to limited coordination with outpatient physicians. In the PIKKO16 project (Patient Information, Communication and Competence Promotion in Oncology), oncological counselling was offered through so-called oncological pilots, to address patients’ psychosocial needs. In this non-randomised, controlled study, with a total of 1004 patients included, there were no group differences after 12 months.

The present study IMPULS-A (Implementation of a Support Programme for Long-Term Cancer Survivors in Old Age) aims to develop, implement and evaluate a survivorship programme for older cancer survivors aged 70 and over. This survivorship programme primarily aims to improve cancer survivors’ access to, awareness of and perceived availability of relevant health and support information. Accordingly, the Health Literacy Questionnaire (HLQ) subscale ‘Having sufficient information to manage my health’ was selected to assess the primary outcome because it directly reflects the information-related mechanism through which the intervention is expected to exert its effects. The subscale specifically captures whether participants perceive the information available to them as sufficient for managing their health.17

The primary hypothesis of this study is to evaluate whether the IMPULS-A intervention improves health literacy among older cancer survivors compared with usual care. An important component of IMPULS-A is the development of tailored informational materials for this population. Although digital health information is increasingly common, digital literacy varies widely18 and many older adults may still prefer printed materials or in-person communication, offering information in multiple formats and actively fostering digital skills may therefore better accommodate the diverse needs of older cancer survivors. Therefore, secondary objectives also include assessing digital health literacy, as well as quality of life, healthcare utilisation and patient satisfaction. The study will also evaluate implementation outcomes and explore perspectives on survivorship in older adults.

The programme can start immediately after the end of the first line/primary treatment and builds on a network of regional care services for older cancer survivors. IMPULS-A aims to achieve several clinical innovations, including regulated access and standardised biopsychosocial screenings, quality-assured and guideline-compliant treatment of long-term and late effects, and interdisciplinary, personalised care plans and coordination of care for survivors provided by a specialised so-called care navigator and an interprofessional survivor board. The programme also aims to support coordination of complex care, facilitate the transition from acute therapy to aftercare, include the patient’s social environment and take into account age-related needs. Additionally, it fosters structured exchange and cooperation between practitioners and enables care to be provided close to home through regional networking and the use of digital services.

The study intentionally includes a heterogeneous population of older cancer survivors with different cancer diagnoses, treatment histories and survivorship trajectories. This pragmatic approach aims to enhance the generalisability of the findings to routine care settings. As the intervention is based on individually identified biopsychosocial support needs, participants with different cancer entities and disease stages may benefit. Nevertheless, the heterogeneity of the study population has to be considered when interpreting the trial results.

Methods and analysis

Study design

The study design is an interventional, randomised controlled trial.

Trial setting

The study is conducted at the National Center for Tumor Diseases (NCT) Heidelberg in collaboration with existing follow-up care facilities and selected outpatient oncology practices in Baden-Württemberg, Hessen and Rhineland-Palatinate. Patients may also self-refer, for example, through patient organisations or study websites. Eligibility assessment

Study candidates are preliminarily screened to assess their biopsychosocial support needs using a combination of self-report validated (screening) instruments with predefined cut-off values and selected single items from established questionnaires, for which cut-offs were defined by expert consensus (table 1). Eligibility is determined based on these (standardised) values, thereby supporting a transparent and reproducible screening process. Meeting the predefined threshold in at least one need domain is sufficient for study inclusion. Candidates for whom no support needs are identified will not be included in the study but will be offered repeated screening after 6 months to re-evaluate support needs, ie, eligibility. Given the wide heterogeneity of treatment pathways across cancer entities, eligibility in IMPULS-A is based on whether participants are considered to be in the follow-up care setting rather than in an acute, closely monitored oncological treatment phase. In cases where treatment status or follow-up care status cannot be clearly determined, eligibility is assessed in consultation with an experienced physician from the relevant oncology specialty.

Recruitment

Participants will be recruited at NCT Heidelberg and at University Hospital Heidelberg, as well as through cooperating oncologists, specialists and general practitioners, rehabilitation facilities and hospitals, and via self-referral. Outpatient recruitment involves an initial pre-selection by medical staff at cooperating practices, rehabilitation facilities, and hospitals based on the eligibility criteria of age, treatment completion, place of residence and expected life expectancy, but not biopsychosocial support needs. Eligible individuals are subsequently contacted by IMPULS-A staff, who further inform them about the study, and obtain written informed consent. Individuals expressing interest independently may also participate. Recruitment materials will be distributed through print and digital media, and collaborations with relevant partners, for example, clinics and patient organisations will be maintained. Recruitment progress will be continuously monitored, and additional outreach measures will be implemented if enrolment falls below expectations. These measures are expected to enable recruitment of a sufficient number of participants within the planned 18-month period (15 August 2025 to 15 February 2027). For the qualitative interviews, participants will be purposively sampled from both study arms using a maximum variation approach to capture heterogeneity in sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, as well as survivorship experiences.

Randomisation

A total of 724 older survivor participants with biopsychosocial needs will be randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to one of the two arms: intervention group (n=362) and control group (n=362). Participants in the intervention group will receive the IMPULS-A intervention and participants in the control group will receive the current standard care for long-term survivors (treatment as usual; TAU). The randomisation is stratified by age (<80 or ≥80 years), sex and time since (first) cancer diagnosis (<5 or ≥5 years). Randomisation is done by REDCap (a web application for building and managing online surveys and databases). Concealment of allocation sequence is ensured.

Blinding (masking)

Given the nature of the intervention, neither the study team nor the participants are blinded to group allocation. Participants are informed of their allocation (intervention or control) after randomisation. The care navigator responsible for intervention delivery also knows the allocation status.

As all outcomes are collected using self-administered questionnaires, the participants themselves are the outcome assessors. Questionnaire instructions, assessment time points, modes of contact, reminder procedures and the type of assistance available are identical across trial groups. Intervention delivery and outcome data collection are organisationally and personally strictly separated. Similarly, the staff members responsible for randomisation and allocation have no role in the analysis or interpretation of outcomes. In addition, data analysts will be blinded to group allocation.

Intervention and comparator

Intervention development

The IMPULS-A intervention was developed by an interdisciplinary team integrating oncology, geriatrics, psycho-oncology, exercise and nutrition care. An interdisciplinary survivor board — following the model of long-established and well-proven tumour boards, drawing on extensive clinical expertise within the project team — was established to manage complex cases, ensuring comprehensive, age-appropriate support.

The programme was piloted to ensure feasibility and acceptability before full implementation.

Intervention description

Participants who receive the intervention are screened every 6 months to determine their existing biopsychosocial needs and whether they require support using (adapted) established instruments and questionnaires. If care needs are identified based on predefined cut-offs (see table 1) — a care navigator provides appropriate, tailored counselling concerning treatment options and assists participants with referrals, primarily to existing, individually tailored services close to their homes. The care navigator also provides tailored support materials in print, online, or video format, selected and created by interdisciplinary experts in geriatrics, psycho-oncology, exercise and movement, survivorship, medical ethics, as well as by patient representatives. In addition, a study website with a login area has been created, allowing participants in the intervention group to access resources specifically created or selected to support the needs of older cancer survivors. The IMPULS-A intervention follows a stepped-care approach based on participants’ individually identified support needs. Complex cases with multifaceted biopsychosocial needs are presented by the care navigator to an interdisciplinary survivor board, which facilitates coordinated, cross-professional care. Examples of tailored support include provision of information and recommendations to seek psycho-oncological support in participants reporting depressive symptoms or psychological distress in the Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4; see table 1 and below), or recommendations to seek nutritional counselling for participants with elevated scores in the Simplified Nutritional Appetite Questionnaire (SNAQ; see table 1 and below). Follow-up contacts conducted after the second or third screening may be used to discuss barriers to service utilisation, provide additional guidance where required, and offer further information and referrals in the event of deterioration or the emergence of new needs. Additionally, participants in the intervention group may contact the care navigator at any time to discuss any questions or concerns related to survivorship. The specific intervention components are determined individually based on each participant’s support needs and preferences.

Comparator

Participants in the control group receive usual care within the German healthcare system. This typically includes guideline-based oncological follow-up care according to tumour-specific recommendations. While certain medical follow-up routines are widely established, there is no standardised survivorship programme that routinely identifies biopsychosocial support needs and systematically connects cancer survivors with supportive care services. Access to these is generally organised on an individual basis, potentially with assistance from general practitioners or treating physicians. Participants in the control group will receive this current standard of care for cancer survivors, naturally allowing them to access all available support services. They will not receive systematic screenings or tailored information or referral as provided in the intervention group.

Modifications

Older cancer survivors enrolled in the study who experience a cancer relapse or develop a new tumour requiring additional acute treatment will continue to participate in the study and will receive the intervention if they are assigned to the intervention group. Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to evaluate possible associations between relapse and outcome.

Adherence

To ensure timely completion of study assessments at T0, T1 and T2, all participants will be regularly followed up by staff in charge of participant recruitment. They will provide support and guidance for completing questionnaires, whether online or on paper. Reminders will be sent at each assessment time point, and participants can contact the study team at any time for assistance. This approach is intended to maximise adherence to the trial protocol and completion of study evaluations.

Adherence to the intervention will be assessed by documenting the number, setting, duration and content of participant contacts and contact attempts throughout the intervention period. In addition, relevant written communication and support activities are recorded.

Concomitant care

Participants in both the intervention and control groups may continue to receive any usual care or support services available to long-term cancer survivors, including medical follow-up, psychosocial support, rehabilitation or counselling. There are no prohibited concomitant interventions, as the trial does not involve administration of drugs or other experimental therapies. Participation in the study will not interfere with these routine services.

Outcomes

Participating patients will be asked to complete a self-administered (study) evaluation at three time points: at randomisation (T0), 9 months (T1) and 18 months (T2) after T0, with T2 being the main measurement time point.

Participants’ relatives may also take part in the study, if interested. They are asked to complete a study evaluation at the time of inclusion (T0) and 18 months (T2) later. The support needs of relatives of participants included in the intervention group will be measured as well. The aim is to recruit relatives comprising 10%–20% of the total number of older survivor participants.

Practitioners and care providers of participating survivors will be asked to complete study evaluations once participants disclose them at study inclusion, and again at the time point when all study procedures have been completed by all participants (last patient out).

Furthermore, patients from both study arms will be invited to participate in qualitative interviews to explore their individual conceptions of a ‘life worth living’ after cancer.

Additionally, a qualitative part of the study will explore which aspects are important to older people in relation to a life worth living after cancer. The adequacy of the interview sample size will be continuously assessed using the information power framework,38 evaluating study aim specificity, sample characteristics and dialogue quality. Operationally, data analysis will be performed iteratively and parallel to recruitment. The research team will hold regular consensus meetings to evaluate code depth. Recruitment will cease within the planned range (n=10–22) when additional interviews no longer yield new conceptual insights regarding the participants’ dimensions of a ‘life worth living’. An interview guide will be developed, focusing on factors that contribute to ‘good survivorship’ as well as relevant needs and priorities.

Baseline data are collected at the very beginning of the study during baseline screening. These data include sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, which are beyond the sample description used to assess eligibility and to obtain information necessary for randomisation.

The self-report questionnaires and assessment forms are available either paper-based or online, depending on participants’ preference and capacity. Online data will be collected via the LimeSurvey platform. The qualitative interviews will be audio-recorded and transcribed for subsequent analysis.

All data will be collected and stored in pseudonymised form and will be anonymised at the end of the study.

To maximise retention and complete follow-up, the recruitment staff will maintain contact with participants throughout the study, providing reminders and support to ensure that questionnaires and assessments are completed. Flexible options for completing forms (paper or online) will be offered based on participants’ preferences and capacities.

For participants who withdraw from the trial, no further information will be collected from the date of withdrawal.

Personal and sensitive participant data (eg, contact information, randomisation information) are linked to a pseudonym code and stored securely in REDCap, which is encrypted and access-controlled. Questionnaire responses collected via LimeSurvey, as well as audio recordings and transcripts, are pseudonymised.

Access to personal and study data is restricted to authorised study personnel only. Participant timeline

Study candidates who meet the pre-screening inclusion criteria (age ≥70 years, expected life expectancy ≥3 years, residence within 100 km of the NCT Heidelberg and completion of first line/primary treatment) are invited to provide written informed consent. After consent, participants complete a baseline screening to assess their biopsychosocial support needs. Candidates with identified needs are enrolled in the study and randomised. Candidates without needs are not included but are re-screened every 6 months and enrolled if needs emerge. Participants are randomly allocated either to the intervention group or to the control group (TAU). Intervention participants are screened every 6 months to assess existing needs and identify any requests for support. For study evaluation, participants complete questionnaires at three time points. Relatives who consent to participate complete a questionnaire on their needs at inclusion (T0) and 18 months after (T2). Practitioners and care providers complete study evaluations once participants have disclosed them to the study team at study inclusion, and again at the time point when all study procedures have been completed by all participants (last patient out).

Sample size

The sample size calculation is based on the expected effects of the intervention after 18 months. In previous studies, the OSCAR15 study and PIKKO16 study, effect sizes of 0.4 and 0.25, respectively, were anticipated for changes in quality of life. Only PIKKO16 demonstrated a statistically significant effect size of 0.27. For IMPULS-A, the main outcome is health literacy, and a smaller effect size of 0.25 is expected, based on preliminary studies.

To detect a statistically significant effect of d=0.25 with a two-sided t-test, 80% power, and a 5% significance level, n=253 participants per group are required. We anticipate approximately 5% mortality over the study period.39 In addition, some participants may not complete the final assessment. Therefore, a conservative loss-to-follow-up rate of 30% after 18 months was assumed. Taking this into account, the required initial sample size is n=362 participants per group.

For the qualitative component of the study, we aim to recruit n=10–22 participants for interviews. This range is based on empirical recommendations for achieving thematic saturation in individual interviews.40

Statistical methods

Outcomes

Data analysis will be performed according to the intention-to-treat principle.

For testing the primary hypothesis, a multilevel model will be used. Mean values and treatment condition will be treated as fixed effects, and the baseline value of HLQ subscale 2 will be included as a covariate. Stratification factors — age group (<80 vs ≥80 years), sex and time since cancer diagnosis (<5 vs ≥5 years) — will also be included as fixed effects. The analysis will evaluate whether the intervention group shows a significant difference in HLQ at T2 compared with the control group.

Secondary hypotheses will also be tested using multilevel models. Health service utilisation will be compared using a generalised linear model with a Gamma distribution. The extent to which participants engaged with the intervention will be included in an additional sensitivity analysis. For all parameters, effect sizes with CIs will be estimated.

Analysis population

The analysis population is defined according to the intention-to-treat principle and includes all participants who were randomised, regardless of adherence to the intervention or completion of follow-up assessments.

Missing data

Missing values, for example, due to participant death or other reasons, will be handled using the MMRM method.41 A multivariate model will be specified, modelling time and within-patient correlations as unstructured. In this mixed-effects model, missing values are not explicitly imputed; instead, all available observations from the previous measurement time points are used to estimate missing values at the main assessment point.

Additional analyses

Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to evaluate possible associations between relapse and outcome. The extent to which participants engaged with the intervention will be included in an additional sensitivity analysis.

The patient interviews will be analysed using a structured qualitative content analysis according to Kuckartz42 with a combined deductive–inductive approach, supported by the software MAXQDA.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and patient representatives were actively involved as equal partners in all phases of the project. They also contributed their experience to the ethics application and helped to develop and adapt patient information materials to ensure appropriateness and comprehensible language for the target group.

Patient representatives also serve as ambassadors for the project. The aim is to find suitable ways to reach and involve older survivors and their relatives in IMPULS-A and to further adapt its content to the needs and expectations of cancer survivors. Patients, survivors and their relatives will also play a central role in the dissemination of the results by addressing relevant patient organisations and the public and by supporting the potential implementation of IMPULS-A into routine care.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethics approval and consent

The protocol, informed consent forms, and the information letters have been approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical Faculty of Heidelberg University (S-692/2024), the Ethics Committee of the State Medical Association of Baden-Württemberg (B-F-2025-056) and the Ethics Committee of the State Medical Association of Rhineland-Palatinate (2025-18207).

The study will be conducted in accordance with the current version of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participation in the study is voluntary. Consent can be withdrawn by the study participants at any time without giving reasons and without disadvantages for further medical care.

Before giving consent, each potential participant receives written information about the study, including all information and details about the study, incl. procedures, potential risks and benefits, and their rights as participants. Participants will have the opportunity to ask questions prior to signing the consent form. The participant information sheet and the informed consent form are provided as online supplemental files 1 and 2.

Safety monitoring

Given that the study involves a low-risk intervention and is conducted at a single centre, no formal external monitoring of trial conduct or recruitment sites is planned. Trial oversight, including adherence to the protocol, data quality, regulatory compliance and occurrence of adverse events will be performed by the study principal investigator and the study team at the coordinating centre.

No physical risks or harms are anticipated from participation in this behavioural trial. Participants may experience temporary emotional burden when discussing their cancer history or psychosocial needs. In such cases, participants can access low-threshold psychological support via the psycho-oncological outpatient clinic at the NCT Heidelberg or, outside opening hours, through the on-call physician at the Department for General Internal Medicine, Psychosomatics and Psychotherapy at Heidelberg Medical University Hospital. All participants will be explicitly informed about these support options. As the study only includes cancer survivors who have completed the acute therapy, the majority of participants is expected to have already come to terms with their cancer, making such reactions less likely.

Adverse events that may be related to the study intervention will be recorded throughout the study. The proportion of participants experiencing such adverse events in each group will be routinely monitored and reported in the study results.
