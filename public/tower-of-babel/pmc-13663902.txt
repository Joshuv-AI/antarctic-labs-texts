Boosting T cell antioxidant capacity: A strategic approach to overcome immunosuppression in solid tumors
Authors: Fan Liu, Yuxin Pang, Luoyang Wang, Christabel Kechiyerunda Dimkpa, Bei Zhang, Jie Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Adoptive T cell therapy faces significant hurdles in solid tumors due to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), wherein aberrant accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) serves as a critical driver of therapeutic resistance. Conventional systemic antioxidant strategies are constrained by their non-selectivity and potential to disrupt physiological ROS signaling. Here, we systematically delineate the inherent heterogeneity among T cell subsets—including effector, memory, and regulatory T cells—with respect to metabolic preferences, mitochondrial dynamics, and antioxidant gene expression, revealing their divergent susceptibilities to oxidative stress. Furthermore, the intricate interplay between ROS and diverse immune subsets within the TME, such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), collectively orchestrates an immunosuppressive network that underscores the imperative for selective intervention. Building upon this foundation, we propose a multi-layered intervention framework encompassing: (i) reinforcement of intrinsic antioxidant defenses; (ii) metabolic reprogramming to potentiate redox capacity; and (iii) nanomaterial-based remodeling of the pro-oxidative microenvironment. Notably, we introduce the first combinatorial framework centered on nanomaterial-enabled TME modulation integrated with T cell-intrinsic orchestration—a strategy rationally designed to synergistically preserve stem-like memory phenotypes and augment antitumor persistence through multidimensional redox regulation. This framework emphasizes the profound coupling between metabolic and redox homeostasis while critically addressing current challenges, including cellular selectivity, therapeutic windows, and the risk of reductive stress. By providing an integrated mechanistic roadmap and a clear translational trajectory, this review aims to guide future efforts toward subset-selective interventions, spatiotemporally controlled redox modulation, and synergistic integration with existing immunotherapies, ultimately advancing adoptive T cell therapy for solid tumors.

Introduction

T cell-based adoptive cell therapy (ACT) mainly includes chimeric antigen receptor therapy (CAR-T), tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte therapy, and engineered T cell receptor-T cells (TCR-T), and has achieved remarkable success in the treatment of hematological malignancies [[1], [2], [3]]. Unfortunately, ACT has limited efficacy in solid tumors (e.g., breast cancer), with exhaustion of tumor-infiltrating T (TIL) cells, oxidative stress-induced cell death, and limited infiltration [[4], [5], [6]]. The immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a crucial role in this process. Due to abnormal tumor cell metabolism, the TME exhibits distinct features compared to normal tissues, including hypoxia, persistent antigen stimulation, and elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS)—all of which are intimately linked to its potent immunosuppressive properties [[7], [8], [9]].

These interconnected features collectively establish a unique pro-oxidative niche that imposes sustained oxidative stress on infiltrating T cells. Extracellular hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), present at high concentrations within the TME, can enter T cells either via aquaporins or through passive diffusion, directly elevating intracellular ROS levels [10,11]. Concurrently, chronic hypoxia exacerbates mitochondrial ROS generation [12]. Persistent antigen stimulation further amplifies ROS production via TCR-dependent mitochondrial NADH accumulation [13]. Together, these mechanisms expose TILs to oxidative stress levels that far exceed their endogenous antioxidant capacity, ultimately compromising their survival and effector function.

At physiological levels, ROS activate multiple signaling pathways, regulating T cell activation, differentiation, and effector function [14]. However, within the TME, pathologically elevated ROS inflict a broad spectrum of damage on T cells, including DNA damage, lipid peroxidation, and irreversible protein oxidation [15,16]. These impairments manifest through multiple interconnected mechanisms: telomere shortening and dysfunction, disruption of TCR downstream signaling, impairment of mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) and energy metabolism, and oxidative modification of cytoskeletal proteins such as cofilin and L-plastin that compromise T cell motility. Collectively, these pathological effects diminish the infiltration of functional T cells into solid tumors and drive antitumor T cells toward exhaustion and apoptosis.

Beyond their direct cytotoxic effects on T cells, elevated ROS also shape the broader immune landscape by promoting the polarization and recruitment of immunosuppressive subsets, including M2-type macrophages and MDSCs [17,18]. These populations not only tolerate high-ROS environments through upregulated antioxidant systems but further reinforce the immunosuppressive state by secreting inhibitory cytokines and competing for metabolic resources, thereby amplifying the overall immunosuppressive network within the TME [19].

However, the most formidable challenge lies in the unequal impact of this oxidative stress on different cell types. Tumor cells and immunosuppressive cells within the TME often upregulate their endogenous antioxidant systems to adapt to and even benefit from the high-ROS environment, thereby gaining a selective survival advantage within the hostile TME [18,20,21]. Regulatory T (Treg) cells exemplify this phenomenon: they display notable metabolic adaptability alongside up-regulated antioxidant gene signatures, which underpin their relatively competitive survival capacity under oxidative-stress conditions [22,23]. This creates a fundamental therapeutic paradox: antitumor effector T cells become vulnerable to oxidative stress, while their intended targets—tumor cells—and the cells that suppress their function—immunosuppressive populations—have greater resilience. This paradox of bidirectional imbalance in redox homeostasis represents a core bottleneck limiting the efficacy of ACT in solid tumors.

Recently, strategies targeting oxidative stress to enhance the efficacy of adoptive T cell therapy in solid tumors have garnered considerable attention. However, existing reviews have predominantly focused on metabolic reprogramming or genetic modification in isolation, with few addressing the integration of intrinsic antioxidant defense and metabolic remodeling as an interconnected framework. Moreover, within the field of nanomaterials, the emphasis has largely been placed on pro-oxidative strategies for direct tumor killing, while the protective role of antioxidant-oriented nanomaterials in preserving T cell function remains systematically underexplored.

The TME constitutes a complex ecosystem in which ROS simultaneously empower immunosuppressive populations and impair effector T cells (Fig. 1), creating a bidirectional redox imbalance that selectively disadvantages antitumor immunity. Therefore, the central scientific question lies in how to precisely augment the antioxidant capacity of antitumor T cells, rather than indiscriminately altering the overall redox state of the TME. An ideal intervention strategy should possess three layers of selectivity: at the cellular level, targeting effector T cells while sparing immunosuppressive subsets such as Tregs; at the spatial level, focusing on the tumor locale rather than systemic application; and at the temporal level, protecting T cells without disrupting the transient ROS signals required for their activation. The unique contributions of this review are threefold. First, grounded in the intrinsic heterogeneity of T cell subsets—wherein naïve/effector, memory, and Treg cells exhibit divergent susceptibilities to oxidative stress due to differences in metabolic preferences and antioxidant gene expression—we underscore the imperative for cell type-selective intervention strategies. Second, we propose a multi-layered intervention framework that integrates reinforcement of intrinsic antioxidant defenses, metabolic remodeling, and microenvironmental modulation into a synergistic system, with metabolic-redox coupling as its central concept. Third, we provide the first systematic synthesis of the dual functions of nanomaterials as both "microenvironmental scavengers" and "precision delivery vehicles", enabling end-to-end redox regulation from ex vivo priming to in vivo persistence (spatial and temporal selectivity). Furthermore, this review systematically examines these strategies through the dual lenses of T cell subset heterogeneity and TME ecological complexity, while critically assessing their translational potential. The ultimate objective extends beyond mere ROS scavenging to the restoration of redox homeostasis in T cells within the TME—alleviating pathological suppression while preserving physiological signals, and protecting effector T cells while sparing immunosuppressive subsets—thereby charting new directions for solid tumor immunotherapy.

Systematic dissection of T cell redox regulation: ROS functions, subset heterogeneity, and microenvironmental ecosystem

The antitumor function of T cells is intricately linked to their intracellular redox status. ROS play a dual role: at physiological levels, they serve as essential signaling molecules for T cell activation and effector function; but within the TME, excessive ROS inflict oxidative damage that drives T cells toward dysfunction and death. Importantly, this redox regulation does not uniformly affect all T cells. Distinct subsets—naïve, effector, memory, and Tregs—exhibit divergent susceptibilities to oxidative stress due to inherent variations in metabolic preferences, mitochondrial status, and antioxidant gene expression. Adding another layer of complexity, other immune cells within the TME—such as ROS-producing. MDSCs and TAMs, as well as ROS-sensitive NK cells and DCs—form intricate networks with T cells that collectively shape the immunosuppressive landscape. This chapter will: (i) examine the physiological and pathological mechanisms of T cell redox regulation; (ii) systematically analyze the redox characteristics of distinct T cell subsets; and (iii) extend this discussion to the other immune cells in the TME, thereby establishing a foundation for targeted antioxidant intervention strategies.

The dose-dependent effects of ROS in T cell fate determination: spatiotemporal dynamics from physiological signaling to pathological injury

ROS encompass a range of highly reactive molecules derived from distinct subcellular sources. In T cells, ROS are generated from three primary compartments: mitochondria ROS (mtROS), NADPH oxidases (NOX)-derived ROS, and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-derived ROS, each with unique spatial and temporal dynamics that dictate their physiological versus pathological roles.

In physiological states, ROS within T cells are tightly regulated by antioxidant systems, including the catalase (CAT) system, the GSH system, and the TRX system. Specifically, CAT directly decomposes H2O2 into water and oxygen. Glutathione peroxidase (GPx) utilizes GSH to eliminate H2O2. The TRX system reduces oxidized proteins, maintains the thiol redox balance, and regulates the activity of redox-sensitive transcription factors such as NF-κB [24]. Single-cell signaling network profiling has shown that upon T cell activation, the expression of redox-related proteins increases, contributing to the maintenance of ROS homeostasis. The notably upregulated components include antioxidants (GPx4, CAT), NADPH generators (nicotinamide nucleotide transhydrogenase, NNT), protein disulfide bond formation (QSOX1), and redox signaling mediators (p38MAPK and pNFκB), thereby sustaining normal T cell responses [25]. This delicate balance, however, is disrupted within the pro-oxidative environment of solid tumors.

mtROS are primarily produced at electron transport chain (ETC) Complexes I and III during oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). The primary species generated at both complexes is superoxide anion (O2•-), which rapidly undergoes dismutation to H2O2—the principal effector of redox signaling. Upon TCR stimulation, mitochondria translocate to the immunological synapse, where localized mtROS production is required for signal transduction [26,27]. Complex I-derived H2O2 promote IL-2 and IL-4 production via NF-κB and AP-1 activation, which are critical for T cell activation and proliferation [28,29]. While Complex III-derived H2O2 regulate nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT) signaling and are essential for naïve and memory T cell generation [30,31]. Complex III-derived ROS also activate hypoxia-inducible factors, support Treg cell function, and serve as a prerequisite for antigen-specific T cell activation [32]. Additionally, mtROS activate mTOR and Myc, supporting T cells metabolic reprogramming and proliferation [33]. Conversely, when mtROS production exceeds antioxidant capacity—due to hypoxia, persistent stimulation, or mitochondrial dysfunction—they cause oxidative damage to mitochondrial DNA and ETC components, creating a vicious cycle that drives T cell exhaustion and apoptosis [9,[34], [35], [36]].

NOX2-derived ROS was found to contribute to T cell differentiation from Th1 phenotype toward Th2, with decreased GATA-3 and STAT5 phosphorylation identified as potential redox-dependent mechanisms [37]. NOX-mediated superoxide anion production shape the characteristic Th cell response from a Th17-biased phenotype to a Th1-biased response [38]. These seemingly divergent outcomes may reflect context-dependent roles of NOX-derived ROS, governed by differences in genetic background and the cellular sources of ROS involved. In CD8+ T cells, NOX-derived ROS regulate CD39 expression and IFN-γ production via JNK and NF-κB pathways [39,40]. DUOX1, another NOX family member induced upon TCR stimulation, produces H2O2, which acts in a positive feedback loop to enhance ζ-chain-associated protein kinase (ZAP-70) phosphorylation, Ca2+ influx, and ERK activation, ultimately promoting the secretion of IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-4, and IL-10 [41]. At the immunological synapse, NOX2-derived ROS provide localized signaling that supports T cell activation, but when overproduced, they contribute to activation-induced cell death (AICD). Mechanistically, Complex I-derived superoxide anion induces FasL expression, and subsequent Fas-linked NOX2 activation likely executes apoptosis via H2O2-mediated AKT activation and MEK inhibition [42].

ER-derived ROS arise primarily from oxidative protein folding within the ER lumen, where ERO1α re-oxidizes PDI and transfers electrons to molecular oxygen, generating H2O2 as a byproduct [43]. The ER-resident peroxiredoxin (Prdx) 4, GPx7 and GPx8 eliminate this H2O2 and prevent ER stress under physiological conditions [44,45]. However, in rapidly proliferating effector T cells, sustained protein synthesis may drive chronic protein folding within the ER, engaging the PERK–ATF4/CHOP–ERO1α axis and leading to excess ERO1α production. Heightened ERO1α activity exacerbates oxidative burden and drives oxidative stress. In addition, activation of the chronic PERK axis consumes cellular energy. To compensate for ATP depletion, OXPHOS may be stimulated. Sustained mitochondrial activity consequently drives the accumulation of mtROS, which serves as a hallmark of ER-induced mitochondrial exhaustion [46].

Factors such as hypoxia, metabolic stress, and persistent antigen stimulation drive intracellular ROS beyond the endogenous antioxidant defense capacity, resulting in the cumulative damage in T cell through multiple mechanisms [47]. First, oxidative stress disrupts signal-transduction cascades in activated T cells. Even moderately elevated ROS triggered by glutathione deficiency can inhibit phosphatases by oxidizing their catalytic residues and can interfere with phosphatase-sensitive MAPK- and NFAT-associated pathways that drive Myc expression. Consistently, higher ROS levels correlate with impaired NFAT nuclear translocation and promote cytoplasmic retention of NFAT. The resultant decline in NFAT-driven activity subsequently down-regulates Myc and CD98 expression. The reduction in Myc abundance further leads to defective proliferation and functional impairment of T cells [[48], [49], [50]]. Importantly, the transcriptional outcome of NFAT signaling is highly dependent on its co-activator partner AP-1: sustained NFAT activity decoupled from AP-1 co-activation initiates a dysfunction-associated transcriptional program [51]. Oxidative stress upregulates TOX expression, and antigen-driven ROS is associated with sustained NFAT activity, with both events potentially contributing to T cell terminal exhaustion [9,52,53]. In addition, superoxide anion derived from TCR stimulation can induce FasL expression and trigger FasL-dependent AICD. Binding of FasL to Fas subsequently activates caspase cascades to drive apoptosis [14,54].

Beyond signaling disruption, excessive ROS impair ADP-coupled oxidative phosphorylation and block cell proliferation as well as effector functions by limiting ATP synthesis [9,35]. Oxidative stress also promotes tunneling nanotube formation, enabling tumor cells to hijack functional mitochondria from T cells while donating dysfunctional ones—a process mediated by oxidative stress dependent regulation of cofilin [34]. Oxidative stress further drives telomere dysfunction—telomeric DNA is highly susceptible to oxidative damage, and mtROS accumulation recruits DNA damage response factors to telomeres, leading to T cell dysfunction and terminal differentiation even without overt telomere shortening [55,56]. Conversely, singlet oxygen (1O2) generated at telomeres also induces mitochondrial dysfunction, suggesting a coordinated interplay between these organelles in T cells under oxidative stress [56]. Compromising cellular architecture, H2O2-driven oxidative stress oxidizes cytoskeletal regulators such as cofilin, L-plastin, and F-actin, disrupting actin dynamics and impairing T cell migration, immunological synapse formation, and mechanosensing [[57], [58], [59], [60], [61], [62]]. Furthermore, excessive H2O2 generates hydroxyl radicals (•OH) via Fenton chemistry, driving lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis [27,[63], [64], [65]].

In summary, ROS from mitochondria, NOX enzymes, and the ER exhibit distinct spatiotemporal distribution patterns: mtROS and NOX-derived ROS are concentrated at the immunological synapse during activation, whereas ER-derived ROS accumulate under conditions of protein folding stress. Physiological ROS levels are tightly regulated by antioxidant systems (GSH, TRX, CAT, GPx4) and support T cell activation and differentiation. However, when these systems are overwhelmed by sustained oxidative stress within the TME, ROS inflict cumulative damage on DNA, mitochondria, cytoskeleton, and membranes, ultimately driving T cell exhaustion, dysfunction, and death.

It should be noted that most current studies do not routinely distinguish between distinct ROS subtypes and tend to refer to them collectively as ROS. Generally, superoxide anion, hydroxyl radical, and peroxynitrite (ONOO−) are highly reactive short-lived species that primarily drive localized oxidative damage, though they can also contribute to redox-dependent signalling in certain biological contexts. However, their rapid decay limits long-range intercellular communication [27,66,67]. By contrast, H2O2 is relatively mild in its reactivity and can serve as a signalling molecule both intracellularly and within the extracellular microenvironment [66]. However, at elevated concentrations, H2O2 can react with metal ions or other substrates to generate more potent and deleterious species, thereby instigating oxidative injury [47].

Metabolic and redox heterogeneity among T cell subsets

T cell subsets differ markedly in differentiation state, functional positioning, and metabolic profiles, which collectively determine their unique redox characteristics. This section systematically reviews the subsets most relevant to antitumor immunity, organized by their lineage and functional classification. CD4+ and CD8+ effector T cells are discussed separately due to their distinct metabolic and functional features. In contrast, naïve and memory T cells—where CD4+ and CD8+ lineages exhibit largely similar redox profiles or available evidence is limited—are covered collectively. Tregs are addressed as a distinct subset given their unique immunosuppressive function and metabolic adaptation. Finally, tissue-resident memory T cells (TRM) and γδ T cells, for which lineage-specific data are scarce or unique functions transcend CD4/CD8 classification, are also discussed as unified populations.

Naïve T cell

Naïve T cells are quiescent lymphocytes capable of differentiating into effector T cells upon antigen stimulation. Their low energy demands met primarily by low-level OXPHOS [68,69] and fatty acid oxidation (FAO) [70], may resulting in minimal ROS production and stable redox homeostasis. Moreover, naïve T cells express higher levels of TRX compared with memory T cells [71], and possess substantial GSH reserves [72], both contributing to their antioxidant capacity. Furthermore, naïve T cells exhibit enhanced autophagy, which facilitates the clearance of damaged mitochondria and preserves mitochondrial quality [73,74], thereby minimizing ROS accumulation [75].

Given their low basal oxidative stress due to their quiescent metabolism, minimal ROS production, robust antioxidant reserves, and efficient mitochondrial quality control, redox interventions targeting naïve T cells are likely unnecessary. In fact, excessive antioxidant exposure may be detrimental: naïve T cells depend on rapid metabolic and ROS upregulation upon antigen stimulation to initiate differentiation. Premature or excessive antioxidant exposure could suppress this initial activation step, compromising subsequent effector T cell generation.

Effector T cells

Effector T cells arise from naïve T cells following antigen recognition and differentiation. This process entails metabolic reprogramming from OXPHOS toward aerobic glycolysis, generating abundant biosynthetic intermediates to meet the demands of rapid proliferation and effector functions [69,76]. Consistent with this, effector cells undergo mitochondrial fission and exhibits fragmented mitochondria with looser cristae that support glycolysis [77]. Effector T cells can maintain their activated state through selective autophagy, but their autophagic activity is relatively weaker than that of naïve and memory T cells, which may lead to inferior mitochondrial quality control [73]. Fragmented mitochondria promote mitochondrial polarization and increase mtROS production [78]. These polarized mitochondria, along with damaged mitochondria escaping clearance, serve as important pathological ROS sources, and the resulting ROS accumulation drives oxidative damage and necrosis [75]. Accordingly, effector T cells display higher Δψm, indicating elevated ROS generation [79].

Beyond aerobic glycolysis, metabolic pathways such as the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) and glutaminolysis also support effector T cell growth, expansion, and function. These metabolic pathways provide substrates for mitochondrial OXPHOS to generate ATP and supply biosynthetic precursors for proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids [68]. Through ROS generation and scavenging, they also contribute to redox balance maintenance [68]. Although all effector T cells engage these pathways, distinct subsets exhibit differential reliance on specific pathways based on their unique energy and redox requirements.

This subset-specific metabolic heterogeneity is particularly evident among effector CD4+ T cells. Th1 cells predominantly rely on aerobic glycolysis while utilizing partial OXPHOS via high glucose transporter 1 (Glut1) expression [14,80,81]. This glycolytic may promote Th1 differentiation by supporting pro-inflammatory cytokine production [[82], [83], [84]]. Th2 cells mainly depend on aerobic glycolysis, whereas Th17 cells require both aerobic glycolysis and glutaminolysis [14]. Effector CD8+ T cells also primarily utilize aerobic glycolysis, with additional support from enhanced OXPHOS. They exhibit increased lipid uptake; however, under both homeostatic and inflammatory conditions, de novo lipogenesis is required for optimal CD8+ T cell function [85,86]. Notably, within the TME—characterized by lipid overload and a pro-oxidative milieu—this enhanced uptake may promote lipid peroxidation, increasing the risk of ROS-mediated apoptosis and ferroptosis [87,88].

Overall, the heightened susceptibility of effector T cells to oxidative stress arises from multiple intrinsic factors: increased mtROS from mitochondrial fragmentation, compromised mitochondrial quality control due to diminished autophagy (compared to memory subsets), and relatively weak antioxidant gene expression. Within the TME, metabolic stressors—such as hypoxia, nutrient competition, and persistent antigenic stimulation—likely exacerbate this vulnerability. Consequently, antioxidant interventions targeting effector T cells hold therapeutic promise: enhancing GSH levels, preserving mitochondrial function, and inhibiting ferroptosis may prolong their survival in the TME. However, timing is critical—excessive H2O2 scavenging during early activation may impair effector function, while intervention at terminally exhausted stages is unlikely to reverse established damage.

Memory T cells

Memory T cells maintain immunological memory after antigen clearance and can rapidly mount antigen-specific responses upon rechallenge. Based on surface marker expression, human memory T cells are categorized into stem cell memory (TSCM), central memory (TCM), and effector memory (TEM) subsets [89].

Memory T cells utilize extracellular glucose to support FAO and OXPHOS [90], meeting their relatively low energy demands [68]. This metabolic configuration underpins their enhanced resistance to oxidative stress. Memory T cells are highly dependent on FAO and can also suppress intracellular ROS elevation by increasing glycerol uptake via aquaporin 9 (AQP9) [89]. Consistent with this metabolic preference, mitochondrial fusion in memory T cells remodels cristae architecture, facilitating the reassembly of ETC complexes into configurations favorable for OXPHOS and FAO [77]. This enhanced mitochondrial integrity may also contributes to redox homeostasis maintenance, given that fragmented mitochondrial networks are commonly associated with elevated mtROS output [35,75]. Compared to naïve T cells, memory T cells have increased mitochondrial mass and spare respiratory capacity—the latter associated with FAO—enabling rapid energy supply upon restimulation [91,92]. Furthermore, cardiolipin synthesis supports mitochondrial fitness in circulating memory CD8+ T cells [93], and memory T cells display enhanced mitophagy facilitating mitochondrial quality control [73,74].

Notably, memory T cells exhibit lower Δψm than effector T cells [91]. CD8+ T cells with low Δψm possess reduced levels of oxidative stress and show upregulated mRNA expression of ROS-detoxifying enzymes, including CAT, GPx4, and superoxide dismutase (SOD) 1 and 2 [79]. TSCM cells also exhibit active GSH metabolism [72], which may underlie their enhanced antioxidant capacity. Additionally, CD45RO+ T cells—a marker of the memory lineage—display elevated expression of phosphoglycerate kinase 1, fructose-bisphosphate aldolase A, and manganese superoxide dismutase, which may contribute to energy metabolism and redox maintenance within this subset [71].

Overall, memory T cell possess an intrinsically superior capacity to maintain redox homeostasis, attributed to their highly fused mitochondrial networks, enhanced mitochondrial mass and spare respiratory capacity, and elevated antioxidant gene expression. Accordingly, interventions targeting memory T cells should focus on preserving their mitochondrial function and metabolic flexibility, rather than further upregulating intracellular antioxidant levels. Excessive antioxidant treatment may disrupt the low-level ROS signaling required for memory T cell quiescence, potentially compromising their long-term survival and self-renewal capacity.

Tissue-resident memory T cells

TRM cells are a specialized subset that acquire fatty acids through a shift in fatty acid-binding proteins isoforms [[94], [95], [96]]. They primarily rely on mitochondrial oxidation of exogenous fatty acids to sustain long-term persistence in peripheral tissues [94]. Notably, TRM cells can synthesize coenzyme Q via the mevalonate-cholesterol pathway [97], and this antioxidant production enhances their resistance to oxidative stress. Cardiolipin synthesis has also been shown to support CD8+ TRM cells activation [98].

Whether the maintenance of tissue-resident properties depends on specific levels of ROS signaling remains unclear. Given the critical role of TRM cells in local tumor immunosurveillance, any systemic antioxidant intervention warrants careful evaluation of its potential impact on this subset.

Regulatory T cells

Tregs primarily rely on FAO and OXPHOS to meet their low energy demands [68]. This metabolic profile is closely linked to Foxp3 [99], the lineage-defining transcription factor that programs enhanced mitochondrial respiration in this subset [99,100]. Compared to naïve T cells, Tregs exhibit higher mTORC1 activity, which potentiates mitochondrial metabolism and promotes the expression of ICOS and CTLA-4, thereby establishing their suppressive function [101,102]. Tregs also display enhanced autophagosome formation relative to conventional CD4+ T cells [103], collectively suggesting increased mitochondrial mass and oxidative stress resistance.

Regarding antioxidant defense, Tregs elevate TRX1 expression and secretion [104], which may contribute to their superior antioxidant capacity. TMED4 maintains intracellular ROS homeostasis through the IRE1α/XBP1 axis and NRF2-related antioxidant responses, sustaining Treg stability and suppressive function [23]. Furthermore, Tregs resist lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis via GPx4 induction, which regulates mitochondrial homeostasis and maintains redox balance [[105], [106], [107]].

Notably, ROS-dependent signaling is essential for maintaining the suppressive capacity of Tregs. Specifically, H2O2 and other intracellular ROS act on SUMO-specific protease 3 (SENP3) by preventing its ubiquitination-mediated degradation, thereby stabilizing SENP3 protein levels. Stabilized SENP3 subsequently mediates the deSUMOylation of BACH2, a transcription factor that represses effector-T-cell-associated transcriptional programs and preserves the unique gene signature required for Treg-mediated immune suppression. Upon TCR and CD28 co-stimulation, SENP3 accumulates within Tregs in a ROS-dependent manner, and pharmacological scavenging of intracellular ROS has been shown to impair Treg immunosuppressive function [16,108]. In addition, moderate ROS elevation can trigger indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase production, further potentiating Treg-derived immunosuppressive responses [109].

Consistent with the notion that Tregs exploit ROS signaling to reinforce their immunosuppressive capacity, Tregs exhibit higher basal ROS levels than effector T cells [110]. Concurrently, bolstered by a more robust overall antioxidant defense system, Tregs show greater resistance to oxidative stress-induced cell death [111]. However, this protection has an upper tolerance limit. Studies have shown that intratumoral Tregs display reduced expression of NRF2 and its downstream antioxidant target genes. Although this reduction merely compresses the oxidative stress tolerance threshold without reversing their overall antioxidant advantage, when the TME experiences focal extreme ROS loads, the excessive oxidative pressure can breach this defense ceiling, thereby inducing mitochondrial damage and subsequent apoptosis in intratumoral Tregs. Intriguingly, this oxidative stress-induced apoptosis paradoxically reinforces local immunosuppression rather than alleviating it. Mechanistically, apoptotic Tregs release ATP via pannexin-1 channels, while the enzymatically active CD39/CD73 retained on their plasma membrane rapidly convert this ATP into adenosine. Adenosine then binds to the A2A receptor on effector T cells, suppressing the secretion of IFN-γ, TNF, and IL-2 by both CD4+ and CD8+ effector T cells, thereby establishing a potent immunosuppressive milieu that contribute to immunotherapy resistance [112].

Collectively, the Treg–ROS interplay exhibits a duality that complicates redox-based therapeutic interventions: viable Tregs depend on ROS signaling to sustain their suppressive function, whereas the pro-oxidative tumor microenvironment can induce apoptosis in a subset of Tregs, which in turn amplifies local immunosuppression via adenosine signaling. Because the ATP released by apoptotic Tregs represents a one-time substrate reserve, the adenosine-mediated inhibitory effect eventually wanes as ATP becomes depleted. In contrast, persistently viable Tregs can continuously exert long-lasting immunosuppressive activity. Thus, non-selective ROS-scavenging interventions may paradoxically promote Treg survival and expand intratumoral Treg pools, resulting in sustained immunosuppression. Therefore, precise modulation of ROS levels constitutes a challenging yet essential task. This complexity further underscores the necessity of developing targeted strategies that selectively improve the redox homeostasis and function of effector T cells while avoiding unintended enhancement of Treg immunosuppressive activity.

γδT cells

γδT cells are unconventional lymphocytes that function independently of classical TCR engagement and major histocompatibility complex (MHC) antigen presentation, operating as innate-like T cells [113,114]. In humans, γδ T-cell development requires sustained Notch signaling, modulated by specific Notch receptor-ligand interactions, in particular Jagged2/Notch3 signaling [115]. Evidence on the redox status of γδ T cells remains limited. One study reported heightened sensitivity to hypoxia within the brain TME relative to αβ T cells [116,117], and another showed that CD27− γδ T17 cells exhibited low glutathione levels and were suppressed by ROS derived from tumor-associated neutrophils [118]. These observations may indicate relatively limited antioxidant capacity in γδ T cells. However, current evidence remains insufficient to guide subset-specific interventions.

Summary

T cell subsets display distinct metabolic profiles, mitochondrial states, and antioxidant capacities shaped by their unique functional properties and energy demands (Table 1). Effector T cells are most vulnerable to oxidative stress due to mitochondrial fragmentation and weak antioxidant reserves, while Tregs gain a survival advantage through enhanced antioxidant programs. This subset-specific hierarchy of oxidative stress resistance underscores the need for selective redox-targeted interventions that protect effector T cells without reinforcing Tregs or disrupting other subsets (e.g., TRM and memory T cells). Redox characteristics and metabolic preferences of major T cell subsets.

The redox ecosystem of the tumor microenvironment

The vulnerability of functional T cells to oxidative stress, detailed in Section 2.2, is exacerbated by a broader ecosystem of ROS-shaped immune dysregulation. Within the TME, the impact of ROS is paradoxically selective: MDSCs and M2-type TAMs are equipped with robust antioxidant machinery, enabling them to thrive and suppress T cell immunity using ROS as a weapon. Conversely, NK cells and DCs are highly susceptible to oxidative damage, rendering them dysfunctional and incapable of supporting T cell activation. Therefore, the TME redox status creates a “perfect storm” for T cell failure—directly damaging effectors while simultaneously empowering their suppressors and disabling their allies. A comprehensive intervention strategy must account for this complex interplay.

Myeloid-derived suppressor cells

MDSCs are key ROS producers within the TME while simultaneously possessing robust antioxidant mechanisms that enable their survival in this high-ROS environment. This heterogeneous population of immature myeloid cells is primarily composed of monocytic (M-MDSCs) and polymorphonuclear (PMN-MDSCs) subsets, both endowed with potent immunosuppressive capacity [121]. A defining feature of MDSCs is their ability to exploit elevated ROS levels to maintain their undifferentiated state and execute their suppressive functions, all without succumbing to oxidative damage. This is achieved through the coordinated regulation of multiple factors—including Nrf2, HMGB1, IDO1, calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase 2, hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1, and phosphoenolpyruvate—which together upregulate glycolysis while simultaneously suppressing excessive ROS production and promoting its clearance. However, H2O2 constitutes the dominant fraction of elevated ROS released by MDSCs under pathological conditions [122].

Elevated ROS levels maintain MDSCs in an undifferentiated state, as CAT treatment or NOX2 loss drives their differentiation toward TAMs and DCs [123,124]. MDSCs rely on NOX2-mediated ROS production to execute immunosuppressive [124]. Mechanistically, peroxynitrite suppresses antitumor T cell immunity in part through nitrating the TCR-CD8 complex, lymphocyte-specific protein tyrosine kinase, and chemokine ligand 2—the latter impairing antigen-specific T cell infiltration [125,126]. H2O2 reduces TCR ζ chain expression and diminishes IFN-γ production, thereby compromising T cell functionality [124,127]. Consistently, ROS inhibitors abolish MDSCs-mediated T cell suppression, confirming ROS as critical immunosuppressive mediators [128].

Beyond T cells, MDSCs also suppress other immune compartments. PMN-MDSCs inhibit NK cells via ROS, reducing IFN-γ and granzyme B secretion [129,130]. Both PMN-MDSCs and M-MDSCs suppress B cell responses: PMN-MDSCs inhibit B cell proliferation and antibody production, while M-MDSCs release ROS (superoxide, peroxynitrite, and nitric oxide, but not H2O2) to achieve similar effects [131,132]. Furthermore, PMN-MDSC-derived myeloperoxidase generates lipid peroxides that impair DC cross-presentation, compromising T cell priming from the outset of the antitumor response [133]. Beyond direct immunosuppression, peroxynitrite also alter the tumor MHC class I peptidome, reducing immunogenicity and diminishing T cell recognition of tumor targets [134].

Collectively, MDSCs exploit ROS as both a shield for self-preservation and a sword for suppressing antitumor immunity.

Tumor-associated macrophages

TAMs represent another abundant immune population in the TME that exhibit remarkable resistance to oxidative stress. This resilience is attributed to the NRF2-heme oxygenase-1 axis and adaptive autophagy, enabling their survival under significantly elevated ROS levels [135,136]. ROS also promote macrophage recruitment to the TME via the ROS/PI3K/Akt pathway and ROS-mediated basement membrane damage [137,138]. Furthermore, ROS drive macrophage polarization toward the immunosuppressive M2 phenotype, and inhibiting ROS prevents this process [[139], [140], [141]]. Mechanistically, ROS promote M2 polarization through multiple signaling pathways, including MAPK [139,142], PI3K/Akt [137], NF-κB [143], STAT3 [144], and IL-4/STAT6/HDAC2 [145]. Mitochondria-localized Cu, Zn-SOD elevates H2O2 to drive M2 polarization [146], and H2O2 scavenger Prx5-deficient macrophages also exhibit M2 skewing [147]. While one study reported ROS-induced M2-to-M1 repolarization [148], the preponderance of evidence supports ROS in promoting M2 polarization.

However, the classical M1/M2 dichotomy does not fully capture the complexity of TAM heterogeneity. TAMs comprise functionally distinct subsets with different spatial distributions, phenotypic profiles, and likely divergent redox sensitivities [149]. For instance, CX3CR1+ TAMs have been identified as a non-redundant subset that shapes antitumor immunity and mediates resistance to immune checkpoint blockade [150], highlighting the functional diversity that exists beyond the M1/M2 paradigm. The redox-metabolic axis is increasingly recognized as a key determinant of TAM functional reprogramming. Notably, CLEVER-1+ TAMs accumulate in advanced tumors and contribute to anti-PD-1 resistance through PPARγ-driven lipid metabolic programs; blocking CLEVER-1 reprograms these TAMs toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype by suppressing this metabolic pathway [151]. This example illustrates how targeting specific metabolic-redox circuits within defined immunosuppressive subsets can overcome immunotherapy resistance—a principle that directly parallels the subset-selective logic applied to T cells throughout this review.

Beyond this subset-level heterogeneity, ROS also endow TAMs with broader immunosuppressive functions that reshape the TME. Oxidative stress drives monocyte-to-myofibroblast transdifferentiation, which generates a unique subset of cancer-associated fibroblasts [152]. Moreover, it promotes PD-L1 expression on macrophages via NF-κB pathway activation, as reflected by elevated p65 S536 phosphorylation [153], and increases TAMs-derived TNF-α secretion, thereby impairing the function of antitumor T cells and enhancing tumor invasiveness [154]. This increase in TNF-α secretion may be ascribed to ROS-facilitated nuclear translocation of PPAR-γ, a process mediated by MAPK/ERK kinase 1. These observations further illustrate how ROS-driven alterations in macrophage function, converge on suppressing antitumor T cell immunity and facilitating tumor progression.

The TAMs-ROS relationship is bidirectional. TAMs not only respond to oxidative stress but also actively contribute to the ROS pool in the TME, harnessing these molecules to potentiate their immunosuppressive functions. For instance, various tumor cells can induce Irg1 expression in peritoneal-resident macrophages, leading to elevated itaconate levels. In a related context, the abundance of long-chain fatty acids declines within these macrophages, and their oxidation is functionally linked to the regulation of OXPHOS activity, a major source of cellular ROS. The increased macrophage-derived ROS can subsequently activate the ERK pathway in tumor cells to promote tumor growth [155]. Moreover, tumor cell-induced macrophages display increased lipid biosynthesis, which in turn elicits ROS responses [156]. These TAM-derived ROS suppress T cell activation, proliferation, and effector functions while promoting Treg induction [[157], [158], [159], [160]]. Beyond their effects on T cells, monocyte-derived H2O2 can down-regulate CD16 ζ-chain expression, which is critical for NK-cell signal transduction. This down-regulation may, in part, account for the impaired NK-cell effector functions [161]. Moreover, in the setting of surgical intervention, LPS-induced, macrophage-derived ROS can damage vascular endothelium, which in turn enhances tumor-cell adhesion capacity and increases the risk of colorectal-cancer liver metastasis [162]. Of note, M1 macrophages also produce substantial ROS while exerting their antitumor functions [163].

The dual role of ROS—as both a weapon for antitumor macrophages and a shield for immunosuppressive TAMs—highlights the complexity of redox regulation and reinforces the imperative for cell type-selective interventions, particularly given the emerging recognition of TAM subset heterogeneity.

NK cells

In contrast to MDSCs and TAMs, which thrive in high-ROS environments, NK cells are highly susceptible to oxidative stress and constitute primary targets of ROS-mediated damage within the TME [164]. Indeed, NK cells within the tumor core exhibit higher intracellular ROS levels compared to their peritumoral counterparts [165], suggesting their dysfunction is largely attributable to the pro-oxidative milieu.

Oxidative stress impairs the effector functions, metabolic fitness, and survival of NK cells through multiple mechanisms. In a murine melanoma model, ROS generated by NOX2-expressing myeloid cells suppress NK cells antitumor immunity and promote pulmonary metastasis, an effect linked to reduced IFN-γ release by NK cells [166]. Moreover, oxidative stress associated with lipid peroxidation also impairs glucose metabolism, leading to functional deficits in human NK cells [167]. In addition, ROS, particularly superoxide anion and H2O2, compromise NK cell antitumor activity, potentially through modifications to their surface charge that hinder their contact with negatively charged cancer cells [168]. Beyond functional inhibition, NK cells are also highly susceptible to ROS-induced cell death. H2O2 induces NK cell apoptosis through a protein kinase-dependent pathway [169] and neutrophil-derived ROS triggered by galectin-3 stimulation further reduce NK cell survival [170]. Consistent with this, exogenous H2O2 at concentrations exceeding 1 μM induces NK cell apoptosis at a substantially lower threshold than for T cells [171]. This heightened susceptibility may partially explain the decline in NK cell frequency during tumor progression.

Antioxidant-based interventions effectively restore NK cell function. Activating the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway restores NK cell metabolism and function within the TME [167]. NOX2 inhibitors or myeloid NOX2 knockout restore NK-mediated, IFN-γ dependent melanoma regression in mouse models [166]. TRX1 upregulation enhances NK cell resistance to H2O2-induced apoptosis [172], and other antioxidant molecules, including thiols and nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase, also protect NK cells [165,173]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that boosting antioxidant capacity is a promising strategy to counteract ROS-mediated NK cell dysfunction, thereby preserving their antitumor activity within the pro-oxidative TME.

Dendritic cells

DCs are the most potent antigen-presenting cells, playing a critical role in priming naïve T cells and establishing antitumor immunity. ROS exert dual, dose-dependent effects on DC function. At low levels, ROS promote DC maturation and antigen presentation. ROS generated by the xanthine-xanthine oxidase system upregulates CD80, CD83, and CD86 while downregulating endocytic activity partly via NF-κB activation, driving early DC maturation [174,175]. In addition, low-level sustained ROS production by NOX2 also regulates phagosomal and endosomal pH, facilitating cross-presentation [176]. Furthermore, low-dose ROS support DC function and even facilitate STING-mediated antitumor responses via SENP3 modulation [177]. Thus, physiological ROS signaling is essential for optimal DC function.

Conversely, elevated ROS within the TME are detrimental to DCs. In particular, H2O2 enters DCs through transmembrane diffusion or tumor-derived extracellular vesicles [178], inducing oxidative stress. Additionally, excessive ROS promote lipid peroxide generation [88], byproducts such as 4-HNE fuel XBP1 activation, which in turn triggers an aberrant triglyceride biosynthetic program. This leads to abnormal lipid accumulation and compromises DC homeostasis and antigen-presenting ability [179]. Moreover, excessive ROS generated by aged DCs may directly impair cross-presentation-related molecules, further affecting their ability to activate antitumor T cells [180].

Beyond their vulnerability to ROS-mediated damage, DCs can also provide a certain degree of antioxidant protection to T cells within the TME. During antigen presentation, human monocyte-derived DCs can uptake cystine, convert it intracellularly into cysteine, and release it into the extracellular space, thereby establishing a reducing microenvironment. This process occurs independently of antigen-specific T cell interaction and is accompanied by upregulated TRX synthesis within DCs. In contrast, the secretion of TRX into the extracellular space by DCs occurs exclusively upon antigen-specific DC-T cell contact. Given that naïve T cells are defective in cystine uptake, the exogenous thiols provided by DCs are critical for T cell activation and the initiation of normal immune responses [181].

Summary

The TME redox status exerts differential effects on diverse immune populations through distinct mechanisms. MDSCs and M2-polarized TAMs possess robust antioxidant capacity, enabling them to tolerate high ROS levels and actively utilize ROS as mediators suppress T cells. In contrast, NK cells and DCs are highly susceptible to oxidative stress, with ROS directly compromising their antitumor activity.

This functional dichotomy implies that redox-targeted interventions may yield complex and even opposing outcomes. On one hand, ROS scavenging could relieve MDSC/TAM-mediated T cell suppression while protecting NK/DC function. On the other hand, it might dampen the ROS signals required for T cell activation or inadvertently enhance the adaptive advantage of Tregs within the TME.

Therefore, any strategy aimed at modulating TME redox status must achieve high selectivity across three dimensions: cellular, spatial, and temporal. Building on this principle, we propose a three-tiered intervention framework: (i) reinforcing intrinsic antioxidant defenses during ex vivo T cell expansion (Section 3); (ii) metabolic reprogramming to generate oxidative stress-resistant T cell subsets (Section 4); and (iii) nanoparticle-mediated remodeling of the TME to protect T cells in vivo (Section 5). The following sections critically evaluate each tier.

Reinforcing the endogenous antioxidant defense system

The most straightforward approach to countering persistent oxidative stress in the TME is to enhance the intrinsic antioxidant capacity of T cells. Multiple interconnected antioxidant systems operate within cells, with GSH, TRX, and CAT as core components. Reinforcing these defense systems can be achieved through multiple strategies: supplementing key biosynthetic precursors, enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity, genetic overexpressing, or transcriptional activation. This section systematically reviews the mechanisms, target cell types, and potential risks of these strategies.

Glutathione system

GSH is the most abundant non-protein thiol in cells and plays a central role in maintaining T cell redox balance. As a key reducing agent and cofactor, GSH is directly involved in scavenging H2O2 and lipid peroxides, while maintaining protein thiols in their reduced state [182]. Strategies to enhance GSH function fall into two main categories: supplying biosynthetic precursors and enhancing peroxide clearance through exogenous mimetics.

NAC serves as a critical GSH precursor and a direct radical scavenger via its thiol group, exerting dual antioxidant functions [183,184]. NAC also enhances cell surface thiol (c-SH) expression, expands the intracellular cysteine pool, elevates GSH levels, and augments GSH-dependent H2O2 detoxification [185].

Beyond precursor supply, NAC orchestrates T cell fate decisions through integrated redox and metabolic reprogramming. By scavenging ROS in activated CD8+ Tn cells, NAC attenuates ROS-induced terminal differentiation and promotes the generation of a TSCM phenotype, characterized by enhanced self-renewal capacity following adoptive transfer in syngeneic and xenograft models. Consistent with this, these TSCM cells show increased TCF-1 levels, suggesting sustained stem-like properties under chronic antigen exposure [72,186]. Elevated c-SH levels following NAC treatment correlate with improved T cell persistence and antitumor activity [187,188]. NAC also modulates glucose and lipid metabolism in CD8+ T cells and promotes long-lived memory differentiation [50,189].

Preclinical studies further underscore NAC's promise as an ACT adjuvant. In murine models, T cells cultured with NAC show resistance to AICD and confer enhanced tumor control and prolonged survival post-adoptive transfer [190,191]. Similarly, in human TIL13838I TCR-transduced T cells, NAC supplementation during in vitro expansion reduces senescence (CD57) and exhaustion (PD-1) markers. This intervention concurrently attenuates DNA damage (γH2AX) during rapid proliferation and suppresses the FOXO1-EOMES axis via PI3K/Akt activation, thereby downregulating terminal exhaustion drivers. Concomitantly, NAC enhances granzyme B production, which correlates with superior tumor target cytotoxicity [190,192]. In addition, NAC upregulates cytotoxic effector genes (IFNG, PRF1, GZMB) in cytokine-induced killer (CIK) cells and amplifies their tumoricidal efficacy against K562 and CL6 [193].

While NAC enhances T cell redox resilience and antitumor activity during in vitro expansion, it exhibits concentration-dependent, biphasic effects on T cells. Low concentrations are immunostimulatory, promoting T cell function and antitumor activity, while high concentrations become immunosuppressive, paradoxically attenuating granzyme B, TNF-α, and IFN-γ secretion in CD8+ T cells and potentially compromising cytotoxicity [189]. Although the overall evidence supports NAC supplementation during in vitro expansion, its narrow therapeutic window necessitates systematic characterization of optimal dosing regimens to maximize benefits while minimizing functional trade-offs [194].

In addition to precursor supplementation, pharmacological enhancement of the GSH system can also be achieved through enzyme mimetics. Ebselen—a selenium-containing GPx mimetic, directly enhances peroxides clearance [195]. In vitro supplementation with 30 μM ebselen increases the intracellular GSH/GSSG ratio and reduces oxidative DNA damage in human CD4+ T cells, while promoting lymphocyte proliferation [183]. While various synthetic GPx mimetics have been developed, direct evidence for their role in T cells remains limited [[196], [197], [198]]. Beyond GPx mimicry, low-dose selenite upregulates thioredoxin reductase (TrxR) expression, inhibits ROS accumulation, and promotes T cell expansion [199,200]. These observations suggest that selenium compounds enhance T cell antioxidant capacity not only through GPx mimicry but also by supporting the TRX system.

The thioredoxin system

The thioredoxin (TRX) system, composed of NADPH, TrxR, TRX itself, and thioredoxin-interacting protein, constitutes another core redox regulatory network within the cell [201]. TRXs is a class of 12-kDa redox regulators that are predominantly localized to the cytoplasm and contain a evolutionarily conserved -Cys-Gly-Pro-Cys-redox catalytic motif. It participates in the reaction of redox enzymes as a reductant through a dithiol-disulfide exchanger.

Among TRX family members, TRX1 has emerged as a pivotal immune regulator, orchestrating thiol-dependent redox signaling critical for T cell survival and functional integrity [202,203]. TRX1 directly associates with the p50/p65 heterodimer and reduces reactive thiols within the Rel DNA-binding domain, an event that promotes NF-κB binding to κB consensus sequences in target gene promoters [204]. Upon cellular activation, TRX1 translocates to the nucleus, where it associates with p50/p65 and other transcription factors either directly or via APE/Ref-1 [205]. Interestingly, the subcellular localization of TRX1 determines its mode of NF-κB regulation: cytoplasmic TRX1 suppresses IκBα degradation, whereas nuclear TRX1 directly associates with the p50 DNA-binding domain through its catalytic cysteine residues [206]. These observations suggest that TRX1 may contribute to T cell redox regulation and functional integrity, at least in part, through thiol-dependent modulation of NF-κB activity.

Beyond its described role in NF-κB modulation, genetic overexpression of TRX1 in murine T cells has been shown to confer significant functional enhancements in antitumor immunity, associated with metabolic adaptation and favorable effector phenotypes. Pmel-Trx T cells exhibit elevated levels of PPP metabolites that support nucleotide precursor and NADPH generation. These cells also possess enhanced glutamine uptake and utilization, facilitating rapid replenishment of ATP and biosynthetic precursors. Increased levels of the TCA cycle metabolite α-KG in Pmel-Trx T cells may counteract the accumulation of repressive histone marks and DNA methylation, thereby favouring the retention of stem-like properties. TRX-mediated metabolic reprogramming may further remodel the epigenetic landscape, with upregulation of epigenetic modifiers including HDAC11, SETD5, and SETD1B, whichunderpins sustained tumour control and long-term immunological memory. Concurrently, TRX1 overexpression elevates thiol levels, reduces sensitivity to oxidative stress, and expands the TSCM population. This stemness program is mediated by upregulated transcription factors Lef1, Oct4, β-catenin, and Bcl6, which collectively maintain self-renewal and inhibit differentiation toward exhausted states. Following antigen stimulation, Pmel-TrxT cells significantly secrete soluble TRX, which subsequently synergizes with mitogens or cytokines to potentiate lymphocyte proliferation and extend functional longevity [207,208].

In human T cells, genetic augmentation of TRX1 elicits profound improvements in redox homeostasis, cellular survival, and phenotypic preservation, thereby enhancing antitumor potency. TRX1-engineered T cells have enhanced antioxidant levels, reduced cell death after TCR stimulation, and enriched central memory phenotypes-manifested by increased Δψm, suppressed NO accumulation, and upregulated stem cell gene expression. These phenotypic adaptations collectively suggest enhanced potential for in vivo tumor control [207]. Mechanistically, TRX1 prevents downregulation of TCF7, a gene encoding TCF1. This transcription factor governs T cell development and differentiation. By preserving TCF1-dependent transcriptional programs, TRX1-overexpressed T cells retain self-renewal potential and effector functionality, indicating improved therapeutic outcomes and patient survival in preclinical studies. Furthermore, human CAR-T cells with upregulated TRX1 demonstrate robust cytolytic synapse formation, augmented secretion of effector molecules like perforin, IFN-γ, and granzyme A, and preferential expansion of CD45ROlo central memory cells within oxidative TME, culminating in superior tumor-killing capacity in co-culture models [202,209,210].

The catalase system

CAT represents another core antioxidant enzyme responsible for ROS elimination within cells. Unlike the GSH and TRX systems, which require continuous consumption of reducing equivalents, CAT directly decomposes H2O2 into water and oxygen, playing a critical role in antioxidant defense in eukaryotic cells [211,212].

Within the context of T cell immunity, CAT transduction has emerged as a promising strategy to enhance cellular resilience against redox-mediated dysfunction. Studies demonstrate that CAT overexpression in both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells alleviates H2O2-induced apoptosis and restores functional competence under oxidative stress conditions. Furthermore, CAT-overexpressing T cells display improved resistance to oxidative stress-induced cell death upon co-culture with activated granulocytes or exposure to the oxidized lipid 4-hydroxynonenal. This enhanced resilience is accompanied by attenuated suppression of IFN-γ production and improved proliferative capacity following anti-CD3 stimulation, thereby preserving both effector functions and metabolic fitness [213].

The therapeutic potential of CAT is prominently explored in tumor-specific CAR T cell engineering. Co-expression of CAT in CAR-T cells confers superior antioxidant capacity compared to conventional CAR-T cells. These engineered cells maintain reduced ROS levels and a lower oxidative state in both quiescent and active states, sustain functional activity under high H2O2 conditions, and have superior antigen-specific tumor cytotoxicity. Notably, a unique feature of CAR-CAT T cells is their secretion of CAT. This extracellular enzyme exerts a paracrine effect: it protects non-engineered bystander T cells and NK cells from local ROS-mediated dysfunction, thereby enabling ROS-sensitive NK cells to retain effective tumor-clearance capacity under oxidative stress [214,215]. This paracrine capacity may also contribute to reducing local ROS levels following adoptive transfer. Modest alleviation of oxidative pressure within the TME may suppress the formation of immunosuppressive phenotypes in TAMs and MDSCs, and mitigate the direct inhibitory effects of excessive ROS on antitumor cells [123,146]. However, these beneficial outcomes remain largely hypothetical, as the precise redox setpoint that optimally balances antitumor efficacy and immune protection—and the strategies to achieve it—are currently undefined. Moreover, non-selective ROS scavenging carries the risk of inadvertently protecting tumor cells and immunosuppressive populations.

Given these challenges, an alternative strategy of in vitro CAT supplementation has emerged to enhance T cell antitumor functionality while minimizing the complexities associated with genetic modification. Co-culturing T cells with CAT promotes the generation of CD8+ T cells with reduced exhausted phenotypes, increased expression of activation and memory markers, and enhanced bioenergetic capacity. This potential memory-promoting effect may be associated with the activity of H2O2-sensitive epigenetic regulators, such as DNMTs, HATs, and HDACs. Furthermore, during acute activation, CAT selectively inhibits the differentiation of TSCM and naïve T cells into a TCM-like phenotype. This preservation of a less differentiated state contributes to improved T cell activation and polyfunctionality [216].

Collectively, these observations highlight CAT as a promising tool for reinforcing T cell antioxidant capacity, though its clinical applicability awaits further investigation into dosing and safety margins.

Nrf2 activators: transcriptional regulation of antioxidant defense

Unlike the strategies targeting individual effector molecules discussed above, another class of interventions achieves broader defensive enhancement by activating transcription factors that coordinate the expression of multiple antioxidant genes. Auranofin (AUF, Ridura), a gold(I)-containing phosphine compound, potently activates the transcription factor Nrf2, whose downstream target genes encompass a wide range of antioxidant enzymes—including CAT, glutathione reductase (GSR), glutathione S-transferase (GST), and NQO1—playing a central role in cellular resistance to oxidative stress [217].

At low concentrations, AUF significantly reduces ROS within TILs and enhances their oxidative stress resistance. This protective effect persists for 48–72 h post-compound removal. Moreover, AUF treatment potentiates caspase-dependent tumor spheroid apoptosis. Consequently, AUF-boosted CD19-specific CAR-T cells and TILs have enhanced effector function, resulting in superior lysis of CD19+ tumor cells and autologous tumor targets under pro-oxidative conditions. CAR-T cells pre-treated with AUF and subsequently infused into patients also exhibited superior antitumor efficacy [218]. However, the role of Nrf2 in T cells extends beyond mere pro-survival signaling. Paradoxically, studies have shown that Nrf2-deficient cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) exhibit resistance to ROS and are capable of maintaining effector function within the TME. Knockdown of Nrf2 in human CAR-T cells similarly enhanced intratumoral CAR-T cell survival and function, effectively suppressing tumor growth [219]. This apparent paradox likely stems from the pleiotropic nature of Nrf2 as a transcriptional regulator—its activation not only upregulates antioxidant genes but also influences broader gene networks governing cellular metabolism, proliferation, and effector function. Consequently, Nrf2 deficiency may, under specific conditions, inadvertently enhance T cell fitness by relieving certain functional constraints or by triggering compensatory antioxidant pathways. These observations underscore that strategies targeting Nrf2 must carefully balance its potential as a master antioxidant regulator against the complex, multifaceted effects it exerts; the timing, intensity, and cellular context of intervention all require precise optimization. Ascorbic acid: a cofactor for the endogenous antioxidant network

In contrast to the broad-spectrum antioxidant strategies achieved through transcription factor activation described above, certain micronutrients function as essential cofactors for enzymatic reactions, directly participating in the maintenance and regeneration of antioxidant networks. Ascorbic acid (AA) represents an archetypal example of such strategies.

AA also known as vitamin C, is an organic acid mainly derived from fresh vegetables and fruits. As a reductant, it exerts multifaceted antioxidant functions through both direct and indirect mechanisms: as a redox-active compound, it neutralizes ROS via hydroxyl groups on its lactone ring and by maintaining GSH in its reduced state, while regenerating vitamin E in lipid membranes through using reducing equivalents and GSH to quench lipid radicals and protect membrane from peroxidative damage [220]. Concurrently, AA modulates SOD and GPx activity to limit H2O2-induced DNA damage, establishing a coordinated antioxidant network that balances cellular redox homeostasis [221].

AA exhibits concentration- and context-dependent dual effects on T cell fate. At moderate concentrations (e.g., 450 μM), AA protects T cells against apoptosis via suppressing multiple death pathways (growth factor withdrawal-, spontaneous-, and steroid-induced apoptosis). Meanwhile, pro-proliferative effects of AA have been observed at lower concentrations (e.g., 341 μM), which may be associated with the inhibition of pro-apoptotic signaling [222]. However, conflicting evidence indicates that low AA concentrations (62.5–125 μM) during in vitro activation do not critically affect T cell proliferation or viability, whereas higher doses (250–500 μM) paradoxically suppress both T cell expansion and survival [223].

This discrepancy likely stems from differences in T cell status and the timing of AA addition. Campbell et al. [222] demonstrated that AA inhibits spontaneous apoptosis in freshly isolated resting T cells and promotes S phase entry in pre-established effector T cells. Under these conditions, AA may exert its anti-apoptotic effects by scavenging ROS-mediated death signals as a free radical scavenger, or by providing protection upon growth factor withdrawal. In contrast, Maeng et al. [223] applied AA concurrently with the initial mitogenic stimulation of resting T cells—a phase critically dependent on ROS signaling. In this setting, AA may, on one hand, interfere with ROS-mediated activation signals, and on the other hand, its oxidation products may introduce exogenous oxidative stress [224,225], thereby suppressing proliferation and survival at higher concentrations (250–500 μM). Collectively, these seemingly contradictory findings reflect the strict context-dependent actions of AA rather than substantive conflicting evidence.

Furthermore, AA enhances antitumor T cell responses by promoting pro-inflammatory differentiation and inducing metabolic reprogramming. In CD4+ T cells, AA promotes the polarization of naïve mouse CD4+ T cells toward Th17 [226], a pro-inflammatory phenotype that with stem cell-like properties that can orchestrate pronounced CD8+ T cell-dependent antitumor responses [[227], [228], [229]]. In γδ T cells, AA treatment triggers metabolic reprogramming characterized by increased aerobic respiration and glycolysis [230], which may support energy-demanding effector functions. This metabolic shift coincides with reduced post-stimulation apoptosis, augmented cytokine secretion, enhanced proliferation, and functional activity [230].

Conversely, the stable AA derivative l-ascorbic acid 2-phosphate (“phospho-vitamin C″, pVC) primarily augments γδ T cell plasticity and expansion efficiency. It upregulates the co-expression of transcription factors T-bet and GATA-3 in γδ T cells, which are Th1 and Th2-specific transcription factors, respectively, reflecting a high level of cellular plasticity. Critically, pVC enhances the proliferation and cytokine production in phosphoantigen-activated cells while reducing apoptotic rates during in vitro expansion. Following TCR restimulation in short-term expanded γδ T cell lines, pVC promotes clonal expansion of surviving γδ T cells [230,231].

Dose optimization is a universal prerequisite for small-molecule immunomodulators. For example, NAC exhibits biphasic, concentration-dependent effects—enhancing T cell responses at lower doses yet becoming suppressive or even cytotoxic at elevated concentrations. This underscores the need for precise dosing regimens tailored to distinct applications, such as in vitro expansion versus in vivo administration.

Long-term safety remains unresolved for genetic strategies. TRX1 or CAT overexpression offers durable antioxidant enhancement, but whether constitutive overexpression affects T cell homeostasis or increases transformation risk remains unknown due to the lack of long-term follow-up data.

Context-dependent efficacy complicates transcriptional targeting. While Nrf2 activation by AUF provides broad-spectrum antioxidant effects, the observation that Nrf2-deficient CTLs can exhibit ROS resistance under certain conditions highlights that the outcome of Nrf2 targeting depends on cellular context and microenvironmental factors, necessitating individualized rather than one-size-fits-all approaches.

Combination strategies offer synergy but carry antagonism risks. Pairing interventions at different regulatory levels—such as an Nrf2 activator (AUF) with NAC, or co-expression of TRX1 and CAT—holds promise for enhanced efficacy. However, overactivation of multiple antioxidant systems may induce reductive stress, disrupting redox homeostasis. Moreover, certain interventions (e.g., high-dose NAC) may suppress effector molecule secretion, potentially antagonizing the cytotoxic function required for CAR-T therapy. Such risks merit systematic evaluation in future studies.

Collectively, successful clinical translation will require not only efficacy validation but also rigorous optimization of dosing, delivery, and combination regimens to balance therapeutic benefit against potential harm.

Other small molecule compounds with antioxidant activity

Beyond the mechanistically well-defined strategies discussed above, a variety of naturally derived small molecule compounds have been reported to possess antioxidant activity that may modulate T cell redox status. However, these studies remain scattered and mostly confined to preliminary in vitro observations. Unlike NAC, ebselen, or AA—which directly augment specific antioxidant systems—the mechanisms of these natural compounds are often pleiotropic and less well characterized. Systematic evaluation of their selectivity toward specific T cell subsets, underlying mechanisms, and synergy with existing immunotherapies is still lacking. Remodeling metabolic foundations and signaling pathways

The metabolic state of T cells is intimately linked to their redox balance. Metabolic pathways not only govern energy production but also directly influence antioxidant capacity by generating reducing equivalents and biosynthetic precursors. The PPP is the primary source of intracellular NADPH, which provides essential reducing power for the GSH and TRX systems. One-carbon metabolism supporting de novo GSH synthesis by generating glycine through serine metabolism. Amino acid metabolism further contributes: arginine. influences mitochondrial function through mTOR signaling, while cysteine uptake directly limits GSH synthesis. The activity of these metabolic pathways collectively determines the reserve capacity of T cells to cope with oxidative stress.

Mitochondria serve as a critical hub connecting metabolism and redox status. On one hand, they meet the energy demands of T cells through OXPHOS and FAO. On the other hand, ETC leakage represents a major source of intracellular ROS. In healthy mitochondria, ROS act as signaling molecules involved in TCR activation and cytokine production. However, when mitochondrial function is compromised, decreased electron transfer efficiency leads to excessive ROS accumulation, which in turn oxidizes mitochondrial DNA and respiratory chain complexes, creating a vicious cycle that ultimately drives T cells toward exhaustion. In general, metabolic programmes favouring mitochondrial integrity and fatty-acid oxidation over aerobic glycolysis may be associated with reduced ETC leakage and diminished mitochondrial ROS output, thereby supporting cellular resilience against oxidative stress.

Therefore, stabilizing redox balance through metabolic remodeling represents a core strategy for enhancing T cell antitumor function. We propose a three-level intervention framework. First, targeting upstream signals–such as cytokines and hormones–to regulate metabolism and antioxidant gene expression (Section 4.1). Second, intervening in core metabolic nodes and pathways that couple metabolism with redox regulation (Section 4.2). Third, providing direct support for antioxidant defense through nutrient substrate supplementation (Section 4.3). The following sections critically evaluate each level.

Reprogramming of upstream signals: regulation by cytokines and hormones

As the first level of our intervention framework, targeting upstream signals offers a powerful strategy to reprogram T cell metabolism and enhance antioxidant capacity. Extracellular signaling molecules—including cytokines and hormones—initiate intracellular cascades by binding to their receptors, thereby regulating metabolic flux (e.g., the balance between glycolysis and FAO), mitochondrial biogenesis, and antioxidant gene expression.

Three members of the common gamma chain (γc) family of cytokines—IL-7, IL-15, and IL-21—share the receptor subunit γc (CD132) and signal through the JAK/STAT pathways [239,240]. This common signaling mechanism underpins their ability to promote T cell survival, metabolic fitness, and stem-like memory phenotypes. In addition to these γc cytokines, IL-6 and estrogen also modulate T cell redox balance, albeit through distinct pathways.

This section focuses on these key regulators. For each, we discuss how their signaling pathways enhance T cell metabolic fitness and redox homeostasis. We also examine combination strategies that leverage synergistic and complementary effects to optimize T cell persistence and antitumor function.

IL-15

IL-15 enhances the intrinsic antioxidant defenses of T cells through both transcriptional regulation and metabolic reprogramming. For instance, it upregulates the expression of multiple antioxidant defense-related genes, including GSR, TXNRD1, PRDX, and SOD, while also elevating the levels of cell-surface thiols, intracellular GSH, and TRX. Collectively, this panel of antioxidant enzymes boosts superoxide-scavenging activity and sustains cellular peroxide-detoxification systems. Simultaneously, IL-15 suppresses the expression of pro-oxidant enzymes such as NOX, COX, TPO, and MPO [241], thus curbing excessive ROS production.

Beyond these direct redox-regulatory effects, IL-15 reshapes T cell metabolism, with metabolic remodeling acting as an upstream driver of improved oxidative-stress tolerance. Metabolic reprogramming analyses reveal that IL-15 reduces the activity of the glycolysis regulator mTORC1 in CAR-T cells, thereby limiting glycolysis [242], and promoting mitochondrial OXPHOS and FAO [243]. This metabolic shift improves mitochondrial fitness, which may mitigate ETC leakage and enhance oxidative-stress resilience, and also enhances effector functions, as IL-15 increases perforin, granzyme B, and IFN-γ expression [241,244,245]. These functional improvements translate to clinical relevance, as IL-15-expanded CD8+ T cells show enhanced expansion capacity and persistence in patients with synovial sarcoma and myxoid/round cell liposarcoma [246].

Consistent with this metabolic rewiring, IL-15 also promotes the expansion of T cell subsets with favorable metabolic profiles and intrinsic oxidative stress resistance. It supports memory CD8+ T cell survival and homeostatic expansion by enhancing the expression of anti-apoptotic molecules [244] and regulates the self-renewal of TCF-1+ PD-1+ CD8+ T cells [247] —a population with stem-like properties critical for durable antitumor immunity [209,248]. Notably, T cells expanded in IL-15 can produce their own IL-2 and maintain CD62L expression during antigen restimulation, characteristics of TCM cells [249]. This IL-15-mediated suppression of mTORC1 activity also contributes to maintaining the TSCM phenotype [242].

IL-15 is particularly effective in γδ T cell engineering for immunotherapy. It significantly enhances the proliferation and survival of γδ T cells in vitro, with cells retaining improved anti-apoptotic capacity even after IL-15 withdrawal—an effect especially pronounced in neuroblastoma patient-derived cells and primarily mediated by Mcl-1. IL-15 also boosts the activation phenotype and cytotoxic potential of γδ T cells, leading to enhanced antitumor activity [250]. These effects can be further amplified by combining IL-15 with isopentenyl pyrophosphate or zoledronate, which respectively enhance Th1-polarized cytotoxicity and increase IFN-γ+ γδ T cell frequencies [251].

IL-21

IL-21 exerts multifaceted effects on T cells, coordinating antioxidant gene upregulation, mitochondrial optimization, and metabolic rewiring to reinforce oxidative stress resistance and promote memory-like phenotypes. Functionally, IL-21 reduces mitochondrial and intracellular ROS levels, attenuates oxidative stress damage, and enhances resilience to ROS-induced cell death. Mechanistically, IL-21 upregulates key antioxidant molecules including heme oxygenase-1, the glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic and regulatory subunits (GCLC/GCLM), and CAT. Concurrently, it enhances mitochondrial function by increasing mitochondrial biogenesis and Δψm. Notably, IL-21-driven metabolic reprogramming fundamentally differs from IL-2-induced glycolysis: it prioritizes FAO, sustains a metabolically quiescent state, and preserves intact mitochondrial morphology, whereas IL-2 stimulation promotes the formation of fragmented, punctate mitochondria [252,253]. Maintenance of an interconnected, non-fragmented mitochondrial network is associated with comparatively lower levels of mtROS [78,254]. This FAO preference has been shown to be required for the generation of central memory-like T cells [252].

Beyond metabolic reprogramming, IL-21 enhances T cell antitumor functionality through transcriptional regulation. It appears to mitigate T cell exhaustion and senescence, as reflected by reduced PD-1 and CD57 expression [252]. Concurrently, IL-21 retains a less-differentiated molecular signature, characterized by elevated transcripts of early-differentiation markers including Lef1, Sell, Itgae, and Tcf7, alongside repression of Eomes [255]. However, this effect is context-dependent: in resting T cells, IL-21 can directly upregulate PD-1 in an antigen-independent manner [256], whereas under chronic activation it reduces PD-1. IL-21 signaling activates the JAK/STAT, PI3K/AKT, and MAPK pathways [255,[257], [258], [259]] and is accompanied by altered transcription factors critical for memory formation (NOTCH, TBX21, SOCS1) and downregulation of terminal differentiation markers (EOMES, GATA3), supporting the development of CD8+ TSCM cells and sustaining cell plasticity [258]. IL-21 also directly augments cytotoxicity by upregulating the expression of perforin、granzyme B、IFN-γ [260,261].

IL-21 driven stem-like programming can be exploited to optimize engineered adoptive T-cell products and may be combined with additional interventions to further reinforce stemness. Consistent with this notion, IL-21 enhances the expansion of poorly differentiated CAR-T cells, increasing the proportion of naïve subsets within the CAR-T product [262]. Furthermore, supplementation with IL-21 significantly augments the antitumor efficacy of AFP-specific TCR-T cells, promoting their persistence, cytolytic capacity (increased IFN-γ secretion), proliferative potential and stemness maintenance. Following adoptive transfer, these cells show improved longevity and tumor infiltration, leading to enhanced antitumor activity against hepatocellular carcinoma [263]. Co-culture of naïve CD8+ T cells with IL-21 and a lactate dehydrogenase inhibitor generates TSCM phenotype (CD44ˡᵒ CD62Lᴴᴵ Sca-1ᴴᴵ) with low expression of inhibitory receptors (LAG3, PD-1, 2B4, TIM3) [253].

IL-7

IL-7 is a homeostatic cytokine critical for T cell survival and the generation of long-lived memory T cells. Signaling through its heterodimeric receptor (IL-7Rα/γc), IL-7 activates the JAK/STAT and PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathways [264], orchestrating T cell survival, homeostatic expansion, and memory formation.

In CD8+ T cell biology, IL-7 fosters the generation and maintenance of less-differentiated, antioxidant-rich subsets through coordinated signaling and metabolic reprogramming [72]. It synergizes with Notch activation to imprint naïve-like transcriptional programs in recently activated CD8+ T cells, and drives naïve CD8+ T cell expansion at optimal concentrations [265,266]. Notably, IL-7 can promote a less-differentiated state in TCM and TEM cells, shifting them toward a more naïve-like phenotype that resembles previously described stem-memory T cell populations. Epigenetic analyses revealed that these reprogrammed cells exhibit increased accessibility of cytokine-induced mediators such as STAT, whereas BATF-dependent sites that drive terminal differentiation become closed [267]. IL-7 also promotes the survival of naïve and memory CD8+ T cells via STAT5-dependent upregulation of anti-apoptotic proteins, including Bcl-2, Bcl-xL, and Mcl-1 [268]. Beyond its signaling functions, IL-7 also orchestrates metabolic reprogramming, thus supporting memory T cell survival. In vitro, IL-7 enhances glucose uptake via GLUT1 upregulation and increased HK2 activity—an effect associated with TSCM generation and expansion [269]. Additionally, IL-7 induces AQP9 expression and promotes TAG synthesis, replenishing lipid stores and sustaining memory T cell survival [270]. These minimally differentiated cells exhibit reduced oxidative stress levels, which contributes to their functional persistence within the pro-oxidative TME and improves tumor control in ACT.

Beyond CD8+ T cells, IL-7 treatment of antigen-stimulated CD4+ T cells induces a polyfunctional phenotype—marked by co-expression of IFN-γ, IL-2, TNF-α, and granzyme B—with enhanced cytotoxicity and stemness potential. Mechanistically, this effect may be linked to increased histone acetylation at effector gene promoter regions [271].

It should be noted that the metabolic effects of γc cytokines may vary by T cell subset (CD4+ vs. CD8+), differentiation stage (naïve, effector, memory), and activation status. Most current studies have focused on bulk CD8+ T cells, leaving subset- and state-specific responses incompletely characterized. This represents an important knowledge gap for optimizing cytokine cocktails in ACT.

IL-6

In contrast to the γc family cytokines, IL-6 plays a complex and often paradoxical role in T cell biology. It signals through two distinct modes: classical signaling (via membrane-bound IL-6R) and “trans-signaling” (via soluble IL-6R), both leading to GP130 dimerization and JAK/STAT3 activation [272]. IL-6 is not a classic antioxidant cytokine, yet it may exert protective effects against oxidative stress. An antioxidant response element has been identified in the IL-6 gene [273], and IL-6 has been shown to activate Nrf2 and link antioxidant responses with autophagy to alleviate oxidative stress in certain cell types [273,274]. These observations suggest a potential, albeit indirect, role for IL-6 in counteracting oxidative stress.

In the context of antitumor immunity, IL-6 presents a double-edged sword. On one hand, it can support T cell function. Early studies showed that IL-6 administration eliminated tumor metastases in mice, an effect correlated with the generation of tumor-specific CTLs [275]. IL-6 supplementation enhanced the proliferation and cytotoxicity of CIK cells, increased TEM (CD45RO + CD62Llow CCR7low) phenotypes, and reduced Treg proportions [276]. IL-6 also promotes Th17 cell memory while limiting the engraftment of tumor-specific Treg [277], and both preclinical and clinical evidence indicates that IL-6 signaling supports CAR-T cells expansion and antitumor efficacy [278,279]. Mechanistically, IL-6 upregulates genes related to T cell migration, early memory differentiation, and GP130/STAT3 signaling in CAR-T cells [272].

On the other hand, IL-6 is a major driver of toxicity. Engineered IL-6-expressing CAR-T cells induce severe graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), likely due to activation of non-transduced alloreactive T cells [272]. Elevated systemic IL-6 is also associated with cytokine release syndrome and GVHD following allogeneic CAR-T therapy [[280], [281], [282], [283]]. Notably, restricted activation of IL-6 signaling—such as expressing GP130 alone within CAR-T cells—preserved antitumor activity without inducing GVHD [272].

These findings highlight both the therapeutic potential of targeting the IL-6 pathway and the critical need to control IL-6 concentrations in vivo. This balance is essential not only for IL-6-based antitumor therapies but for ACT in general. Intratumoral IL-6 blockade has been shown to suppress the antitumor efficacy of therapeutic T cells in pancreatic cancer models and patients [284], further underscoring the context-dependent nature of IL-6 signaling. Elucidating the mechanisms and safe dosing ranges of IL-6 in ACT remains a priority for clinical translation.

Estrogen

Beyond cytokines, endocrine factors also modulate T cell redox balance. Estrogen, in particular, integrates hormonal signaling with oxidative stress resistance in T cells, serving as an important modulator of antioxidant capacity and antitumor activity.

Estrogen exerts its physiological effects mainly through specific nuclear receptors α (ER-α) and β (ER-β) on the cell membrane [285]. Mechanistically, it enhances T cell function through both redox regulation and pro-inflammatory pathways. At the molecular level, estrogen upregulates antioxidant enzyme activity—including SOD, CAT, and GPx—in a concentration-dependent manner, thereby fortifying cellular resistance to oxidative stress [286]. Complementary studies indicate that estrogen promotes antitumor immunity by enhancing the secretion of cytotoxic and pro-inflammatory cytokines. Signaling via ER-α induces the expression of TNF-α, granzyme B, IL-4, and IFN-γ [287]. IFN-γ is critical for Th1 CD4+ T cell generation and phenotype maintenance, as well as for Treg functional suppression. Moreover, IFN-γ signaling enhances CD8+ T cell expression of IL-2 receptor, the transcription factor T-bet, and granzyme [288]. Furthermore, estrogen upregulates CCR5 and CCR1 expression on CD4+ T cells, a change that may enhance their migratory capacity and promote tumor infiltration [289]. Concurrently, signaling via ER-β promotes T cell polyfunctionality, as reflected by increased frequencies of CD107a+IFNγ+TNFα+ populations following activation—a phenotype associated with enhanced memory cell generation and durable antitumor responses [287,290]. In a mouse model of HCC, estrogen enhanced antigen-specific T cell infiltration, survival, and activation, while also boosting cytokine production and effectively reducing tumor burden [287].

Notably, estrogen's immunomodulatory effects exhibit marked dose dependency [291]. Low-dose estrogen promotes Th1 cell differentiation, bolstering cell-mediated immunity, while high-dose exposure drives Th2 polarization, favoring humoral immune responses [289]. This biphasic relationship may inform the development of targeted therapeutic strategies leveraging estrogen signaling.

Combination strategies

The γc family cytokines IL-7, IL-15, and IL-21 have been extensively studied in combination. These cytokines often function cooperatively to regulate T cell activity within the oxidative TME, enhancing antitumor immunity. The rationale for combining them stems from their synergistic capacity to address divergent limitations in T cell fitness: IL-7 ensures survival and homeostatic proliferation, IL-15 maximizes effector potency and mitochondrial resilience, and IL-21 prevents exhaustion by maintaining a stem-like reservoir. Functionally, they cooperatively enhance antioxidant defenses and induce an antioxidant stem-like phenotype, reducing oxidative stress, maintaining metabolic homeostasis and cytotoxicity, while minimizing exhaustion and cell death.

IL-7 and IL-15 synergistically enrich CD8+ CD45RA+ CCR7+ TSCM-like cells with enhanced homing capacity [292]. The triple combination of IL-7, IL-15, and IL-21 maintains primitive T cell states while boosting proliferation in umbilical cord blood-derived and CD45RA-rich peripheral blood T cells, particularly within TSCM populations. When applied to CD45RA-derived CAR-T cells, this cytokine cocktail preserves less-differentiated immunophenotypes, extending in vivo persistence, and enhances CD4+ T cell polyfunctionality (TNF-α, IL-2, IFN-γ) and CAR-T cell transduction efficiency [293].

IL-7 and/or IL-21 can attenuate IL-15's capacity to induce stem-like T cells [242]. Furthermore, the addition of IL-7 to high-dose IL-2 promotes Fas-mediated apoptosis [266,294], while IL-2 combined with IL-21 drives TSCM differentiation toward a terminal effector phenotype [295].

Co-stimulation with IL-15 and IL-21 promotes cytotoxic CD8+ T cell proliferation and function while expanding naïve and memory CD8+ T cell pools. This regimen is considered the most effective for achieving optimal T cell expansion and maximal cytotoxic potency [262,296]. Note that this combinatorial approach mitigates the functional trade-offs inherent to single-cytokine therapies—such as survival at the expense of effector function or cytotoxicity leading to premature exhaustion—creating a balanced microenvironment for T cell adaptability in the pro-oxidative TME.

Although studies on combination estrogen with cytokines remain limited, mechanistic complementarity warrants exploration. Estrogen upregulates antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT, and GPx) [286], while γc family cytokines promote metabolic reprogramming (e.g., IL-15 promotes FAO; IL-21 enhances mitochondrial function) [243,244,252]. Their combination may yield synergistic effects at multiple levels: (1) dual enhancement of metabolism and antioxidant capacity (estrogen provides basal antioxidant enzyme support while γc cytokines optimize energy metabolism and mitochondrial health, collectively improving T cell persistence within the TME); (2) subset complementarity (estrogen promotes CD4+ T cell polyfunctionality and chemokine receptor expression [287,289,290], and γc cytokines drive CD8+ T cell expansion and stemness); and (3) signaling crosstalk between estrogen receptors and JAK-STAT pathways, which may cooperatively enhance downstream expression of antioxidant and memory-associated genes. Future studies could explore combining low-dose estrogen with IL-15/IL-21 in CAR-T or TILs expansion protocols to simultaneously achieve enhanced antioxidant enzyme activity and metabolic optimization.

Collectively, γc family cytokines share the common feature of promoting oxidative stress-resistant, stem-like T cell phenotypes through metabolic reprogramming and antioxidant gene upregulation. IL-6 and estrogen offer complementary mechanisms but require careful context-dependent application. Core metabolic nodes and pathway regulation

The coupling of metabolism and redox regulation provides multiple actionable therapeutic entry points for enhancing T cell antioxidant capacity. Intervention strategies targeting these key nodes can be categorized into four classes: (i) modulating core antioxidant metabolic pathways (Section 4.2.1); (ii) targeting metabolic-redox coupling hubs molecules (Section 4.2.2); (iii) pharmacological activation of redox-sensitive signaling nodes (Section 4.2.3); and (iv) regulating energy and nutrient sensors (Section 4.2.4). The following sections discuss each category in turn.

Modulating key antioxidant metabolic pathways

The PPP is the primary intracellular source of NADPH, providing essential reducing power for the GSH and TRX systems. However, under nutrient-deprived conditions within the TME, excessive PPP flux may distort metabolic precursor allocation. Recent studies demonstrate that enasidenib, an isocitrate dehydrogenase 2 inhibitor, restricts glucose flux through the PPP. This metabolic constraint preserves T cell metabolic fitness and alleviates oxidative stress, particularly under nutrient limitation. Concurrently, enasidenib reduces cytoplasmic acetyl-CoA levels, thereby suppressing histone acetylation that promotes memory T cell formation. Through these dual mechanisms, enasidenib enhances the generation of memory CAR-T cells, ultimately improving ACT efficacy against solid tumors [297]. This strategy suggest that precise modulation—rather than simply enhancing—of PPP flux may be key to optimizing T cell antioxidant capacity, ensuring adequate NADPH supply while avoiding excessive allocation of metabolic resources.

Metabolic-redox coupling hubs

The coupling of metabolism and redox regulation is achieved through multiple key molecular nodes and metabolic pathways. Beyond directly modulating metabolic flux, targeting hub molecules that simultaneously influence both metabolic and redox signaling provides strategic intervention points for enhancing T cell function. This section focuses on NIK overexpression as a representative example of this approach.

NIK (NF-κB-inducing kinase) is a key regulator of non-canonical NF-κB pathway activation, thus modulating immune and inflammatory responses [298]. Notably, NIK also regulates T cell homeostasis through an NF-κB-independent mechanism involving hexokinase 2 (HK2). Preclinical evidence supports a pivotal role for NIK upregulation in enhancing T cell antitumor immunity by stabilizing redox balance and metabolic fitness in effector T cells [299].

Mechanistically, NIK overexpression promotes G6PD phosphorylation and activation, thereby facilitating NADPH generation via the PPP. NADPH, in turn, serves as an essential cofactor for antioxidant defense, and this enhanced reductive capacity helps limit aberrant ROS accumulation, thus preventing autophagic degradation of HK2—a key enzyme that sustains the bioenergetic demands of effector T cells during antitumor responses. In parallel, sustained NADPH levels contribute to an elevated GSH/GSSG ratio, indicative of reduced oxidative stress. Moreover, NIK overexpression increases extracellular acidification and oxygen consumption rates, suggesting enhanced bioenergetic capacity that may support T cell effector function [[299], [300], [301]].

Functionally, NIK-transgenic (NIKiTg) T cells have augmented cytotoxic potential. Specifically, NIKiTg Pmel1 CD8+ effector T cells demonstrate potent cytotoxicity against B16F10 tumor cells in vitro and resistance to PD-L1-mediated suppression of IFN-γ production. Moreover, this ectopic NIK expression alleviates CD8+ T cell exhaustion and restores IFN-γ production in PD-1+Tim-3+ tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells. Following adoptive transfer into tumor-bearing mice, these engineered T cells elevate tumor-infiltrating CD8+ effector T cell frequency and IFN-γ-producing CD8+ T cells within tumors, culminating in profound tumor growth inhibition [299].Collectively, these findings establish NIK as a dual-function regulator of T cell metabolism and redox homeostasis, underscoring its therapeutic potential in cancer immunotherapy through genetic engineering of both metabolic and antioxidative pathways.

Regulating energy and nutrient sensors

Targeting cellular energy and nutrient sensors represents another class of therapeutic strategies that influence redox balance through metabolic remodeling. mTORC1 and AMPK serve as core sensors of cellular energy status, responding to nutrient sufficiency and energy stress signals, respectively. By modulating mitochondrial function and metabolic flux, these sensors indirectly influence T cell redox homeostasis.

Rapamycin, a macrocyclic lactone produced by Streptomyces hygroscopicus [302], specifically inhibits mTOR, which is a serine/threonine kinase that has been identified as a crucial kinase acting downstream of phosphatidylinositol 3 kinase (PI3K) activation [303]. It elevates cell-surface thiol levels, upregulates the expression of key antioxidant genes including CAT, NRF-2, SOD-1, and TRX-2, and alleviates H2O2-induced oxidative stress and apoptosis in human T cells. Moreover, rapamycin treatment reduces the metabolically active state of CD62Llo TEM-like cells while reinforcing their antioxidant capacity, potentially promoting their persistence in the TME [188].

Beyond antioxidant properties, rapamycin induces a distinct metabolic state in activated CD8+ T cells, marked by the concurrent upregulation of both glycolysis and OXPHOS. This dual reprogramming is associated with the generation of long-lived memory T cells and improved T cell survival upon glucose withdrawal or growth factor deprivation. Mechanistically, enhanced OXPHOS appears essential for sustaining mitochondrial integrity and viability. Conversely, the parallel increase in glycolysis promotes the maintenance of antioxidant intermediates—such as pyruvate and glutathione precursors—thereby limiting ROS accumulation and oxidative damage. Together, this mixed metabolic phenotype may represent an optimized bioenergetic configuration that promotes T cell persistence under bioenergetically demanding conditions [304]. In γδ T cells, rapamycin (0.5 nmol/L) boosts activation markers (e.g., CD69, Lamp-1), increases IFN-γ production, and potentiates cytotoxicity against tumor targets [305].

Importantly, rapamycin also suppresses Th17 differentiation and promotes Treg generation, likely through inhibition of glycolysis in induced Th17 cells and enhancement of FAO in Tregs. Thus, when applying rapamycin during ex vivo T cell expansion, it is critical to optimize culture conditions to maximize its oxidative protective benefits for effector and memory CD8+ T cells while minimizing the risk of Treg induction [306,307].

A parallel strategy for metabolic reprogramming involves metformin, an AMPK activator. Mechanistically, metformin promotes memory CD8+ T cells formation via the AMPK-miR-107-Eomes-PD-1 axis, with expanded TSCM and TCM subsets within both CD8+ T cell and HER2 CAR-T cell populations—subsets characterized by reduced oxidative stress levels. Functionally, metformin-treated HER2 CAR-T cells show enhanced CD107a expression and specific cytotoxicity against lung cancer cell lines (A549, H460, and HCT-116) in vitro, and improved antitumor efficacy in mouse models. Metformin also enhances T cell anti-apoptotic capacity and Ki-67 expression, particularly in TSCM cells [308].

Nutrient substrate supplementation

Direct supplementation of nutrients required for metabolic reactions offers another strategy for enhancing T cell redox balance. By providing biosynthetic precursors for antioxidant systems or effector molecules that modulate metabolic flux, these interventions offer more direct support for maintaining T cell function under oxidative stress. This section focuses on amino acids and their metabolites involved in antioxidant metabolism. Additionally, membrane transport systems such as the cystine/glutamate antiporter (system xc−) regulate GSH synthesis in tumor cells [309,310], but studies in antitumor T cells remain limited, making this a potential target for future investigation.

Pretreatment with specific amino acids and their metabolites effectively mitigates oxidative damage in T cells, enhances mitochondrial adaptability and promotes stem-like phenotypes. For instance, serine-derived metabolites (formate and glycine) can rescue the survival and function of aged T cells [311], an effect that may be linked to improved mitochondrial fitness. l-arginine reprograms glucose metabolism in activated T cells from glycolysis toward oxidative phosphorylation, facilitates the emergence of long-lived TCM-like cells, and enhances the post-activation survival of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, providing a foundation for sustained antitumor immunity [312].

Lactate suppresses histone deacetylase activity and enhances H3K27 acetylation at the Tcf7 super-enhancer, leading to increased Tcf7 expression. Ex vivo, lactate supplementation reduces apoptosis and expands the TCF-1+ CD8+ T cell pool; adoptive transfer of these cells confers effective tumor control. Importantly, these effects are pH-dependent—under acidic conditions, lactate neither upregulates TCF-1 nor supports CD8+ T cell survival, and instead induces cell death [313].

Remodeling TME redox status with nanomaterials

The strategies discussed above primarily focus on optimizing T cells themselves:enhancing their oxidative stress tolerance or remodeling metabolic foundations during in vitro expansion. However, even after ex vivo optimization, adoptively transferred T cells still confront a persistently pro-oxidative pressure within the TME characterized by elevated ROS and sustained hypoxia, which collectively impair their function. Therefore, ameliorating the pro-oxidative TME requires simultaneous targeting of both core issues. Nanomaterials offer a unique advantage to address these challenges by remodeling the TME redox landscape. Based on their mechanisms of action, current nanoplatforms fall into three main categories: (i) functional nanomaterials with intrinsic catalytic or ROS-scavenging activity; (ii) advanced nanoplatforms designed for dynamic, responsive, or targeted modulation of the TME; and (iii) delivery carriers designed to transport active molecules to the TME. This section also discusses the research progress, challenges, and future prospects of these strategies.

Functional nanomaterials: from metal nanozymes to organic polymers

These nanomaterials directly modulate the TME by leveraging their inherent physicochemical properties to scavenge ROS and/or generate oxygen.

Metal-based nanozymes: Mn, Ce, and Zr

Metal-based nanozymes have garnered considerable attention due to their multifunctionality. Metal oxides such as manganese (Mn), cerium (Ce), and zirconium (Zr) can mimic SOD and CAT activities, directly scavenge excess ROS while generate oxygen as a catalytic byproduct to alleviate TME hypoxia. Building on this design principle, researchers have developed various manganese-based nanoplatforms that achieve synergistic ROS clearance and oxygen production. Wang et al. reported a self-oxygen-generating nanoparticle containing functional CaO2 and MnO2 components. CaO2 reacts with water to generate H2O2, which is subsequently catalytically decomposed by MnO2 into oxygen, thereby continuously alleviating local hypoxia. This action can shift the TME from pro-tumorigenic to antitumorigenic: hypoxia relief attenuates adenosine-mediated immunosuppression, reduces Treg activity, and simultaneously triggers CD8+ and CD4+ T cell-mediated antitumor immune responses [314]. Beyond cascade catalysis design, polymer encapsulation strategies offer an alternative approach for sustained hypoxia modulation. David A. Murphy et al. employed poly(lactic [50]-co-glycolic [50] acid) (50:50 PLGA) to encapsulate MnO2 nanoparticles, achieving sustained hypoxia control. These nanoparticles reduced HIF-1α levels, decreased Treg numbers in tumor bone metastases, and enhanced NK cell cytotoxicity, ultimately improving survival in tumor-bearing mice [315].

Cerium oxide (CeO2) nanoparticles have also demonstrated ROS-scavenging capacity. In a mouse model of angioimmunoblastic T cell lymphoma, CeO2 nanoparticles reduced the population of pathogenic CD4+ PD-1high T cells, promoted the functional recovery of exhausted CD8+ T cells, and improved survival without observable toxicity [316].

To further expand their functionality, researchers have begun to combine different metals. Mo et al. reported that Zr-CeO nanozymes possess both potent SOD-like and CAT-like activities, converting superoxide anion to H2O2 and subsequently to water and oxygen. Their application enhanced T cell infiltration and IFN-γ production within the TME. In parallel, Zr-CeO-mediated intracellular ROS scavenging alleviates MDSC-driven immunosuppression via suppression of the unfolded protein response, and blocks M2-like TAM polarization through ERK and STAT3 signaling, thereby facilitating TME remodeling [317].

Metal-organic frameworks

Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), as a class of structurally tunable hybrid nanozymes, further expand the boundaries of this field. Unlike traditional metal oxide nanozymes, MOFs exhibit unique advantages in catalytic activity and functional integration by virtue of their periodic pore architecture and programmable metal nodes. A recent study developed cisplatin-encapsulated Ce-Mn MOF nanoparticles. These NPs catalytically decompose tumor-associated H2O2 and hydroxyl radical ROS into oxygen, thereby ameliorating both hypoxia and the pro-oxidative TME. This nanoparticle potentiates immunogenic cell death (ICD) elicited by the combined treatment of cisplatin and microwave hyperthermia, and concurrently induces macrophage polarization from the immunosuppressive M2 phenotype to the antitumor M1 phenotype. Furthermore, it enhances infiltration of TILs and augmentes cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells [318].

Another example is a PEG-coated Zr-based MOF nanozyme, Pt@PCN-224-Fe, which simultaneously mimics SOD and CAT activities. By neutralizing excess ROS, it activates the PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling pathway and suppresses Foxp3 expression, thereby blocking the polarization of naïve CD4+ T cells toward Treg differentiation under polarizing conditions and inhibiting their TGF-β1 secretion [319]. This mechanism exhibits particular translational value in preventing postoperative tumor recurrence. In a surgical trauma model, a single intraperitoneal injection of Pt@FeMOF-PEG effectively reversed the immunosuppressive state, reducing Tregs, increasing Th17 cells, restoring the normal CD4+/CD8+ T cell ratio, and suppressing M2 macrophage polarization. Concomitant with these changes, pro-inflammatory cytokine levels increased while anti-inflammatory cytokines decreased, indicating a systemic reconstitution of both innate and adaptive immunity and predicting a reduced risk of postoperative tumor progression. Notably, this nanozyme have excellent biocompatibility, with minimal hemolysis and no observed organ damage [319].

Organic polymer materials

In addition to metal-based systems, organic polymer materials have also been investigated for TME redox modulation, leveraging their structural versatility and biocompatibility. Deng H. et al. established a pH-sensitive, polyethylene glycol-covered nanosensor (PEG-TECM-NS) designed to ROS within the TME. This system maintains high stability in blood circulation while enabling anchorage and retention in ECM. Through H2O2 scavenging, PEG-TECM-NS enhances T cell persistence and proliferation, restores immune response and counteracts the suppression of ICD in the TME. Notably, when loaded with oleandrin, it increases the number of CD8+ T cells expressing IFN-γ in tumor-bearing mice, promotes the infiltration of CD8+ T cells and memory T cells, while reducing tissue and organ damage due to its excellent biocompatibility [320].

Advanced nanoplatforms for dynamic redox modulation

The strategies described in Section 5.1 primarily rely on sustained ROS scavenging or hypoxia relief to remodel the TME. While effective in principle, this protection-centered approach may come at a cost—moderate levels of ROS within tumor cells are indispensable for direct tumor killing and for the induction of ICD. In this regard, Tan et al. introduced a crosslinked nanoplatform constructed from selenolipoic acid (cSeLAN) that bidirectionally modulates intracellular and extracellular ROS levels within the TME. Upon accumulation in tumors, cSeLAN responds to and scavenges extracellular H2O2, thereby restoring T cell proliferation and reversing the immunosuppressive milieu. Concurrently, internalized cSeLAN is degraded in a GSH-dependent manner, generating dihydroselenoleic acid, which enhances mitochondrial OXPHOS and increases intracellular ROS via the Fenton reaction, ultimately promoting ICD. This bidirectional regulation strategy significantly amplifies overall immune activation and, when combined with immune checkpoint blockade, markedly enhances the antitumor and anti-metastatic effects in immunologically "cold" tumors [321].

Beyond the need for dynamic regulation, another critical challenge lies in cellular selectivity. Broad-spectrum ROS scavenging may inadvertently protect immunosuppressive subsets such as Tregs, highlighting the need for strategies that preferentially safeguard effector T cells while sparing suppressive populations.

An attempt at cell type-selective protection is the T cell-targeted fusogenic liposome (T-Fulips) developed by Shi et al. These nanoparticles are designed to target T cells and resist ROS-induced oxidation of cell-surface thiol groups, creating a favorable reducing microenvironment on the T cell surface. This protection reduces intracellular ROS accumulation, improves T cell survival and IFN-γ secretion. In a mouse model of radiotherapy, T-Fulips treatment increased intratumoral infiltration of CTLs (CD45+CD8+IFN-γ+) and Th cells (CD45+CD4+IFN-γ+). Concurrently, T-Fulips enabled MRI-based monitoring of T cell activity. However, Tregs can also derive benefits with respect to their surface redox status under this treatment in a TGF-β-dependent manner [322], likely because the anti-CD3-based targeting strategy does not discriminate between effector T cells and Tregs.

Nanomaterials as delivery vectors: targeted intervention via functional payloads

Beyond their intrinsic functionalities and advanced responsive designs, nanoparticles serve another core application as delivery vectors for precisely transporting functional molecules to the TME to ameliorate its pro-oxidative state. Based on the nature of the payload and design objectives, these carrier strategies can be broadly categorized into four types: the delivery of small-molecule antioxidants or regulatory agents, platforms for the temporal regulation of ROS, the delivery of mRNA to confer endogenous antioxidant capacity, and the delivery of oxygen to alleviate hypoxia.

The delivery of small-molecule antioxidants or regulatory agents represents the most common strategy in this context. Zhang et al. designed a redox-responsive nanoplatform for the co-delivery of a Toll-like receptor agonist, CAT, and PD-L1 siRNA to the TME. This platform simultaneously repolarizes TAMs toward the M1 phenotype, scavenges the abundant ROS generated during this reprogramming process via CAT, and downregulates PD-L1 expression on tumor cells. Through these coordinated actions, it alleviates the immunosuppressive microenvironment and rescues CD8+ T cell function. In orthotopic Hepa1-6 hepatoma and 4T1 tumor-bearing mouse models, this treatment significantly increased intratumoral IFN-γ and granzyme B expression in CD8+ T cells, promoted the infiltration of CD8+ effector and CD4+ TCM cells, effectively suppressed tumor growth, and prolonged survival [323].

This multi-target synergy approach can also be achieved by integrating metal ions with small molecules. Chen et al. developed a liposomal nanofactory co-loaded with manganese ions and spermidine (L@Mn@SPD). This nanoplatform operates through multiple parallel mechanisms that collectively relieve immunosuppression and support T cell function. First, Mn2+ activates DCs via the STING pathway, enhancing antigen presentation and subsequently activating CD8+ T cells. Second, the nanoplatform generates oxygen, effectively alleviating intratumoral hypoxia. This process reduces the expression of HIF-1α, further mitigating CD8+ T cell exhaustion. L@Mn@SPD also enhances mitochondrial respiration and ATP production, promoting CD8+ T cell activation and alleviating T cell exhaustion phenotypes, including impaired proliferation and reduced production of cytotoxic effector molecules [324].

If the aforementioned two examples represent "synergistic intervention" strategies that integrate multiple targets within the same temporal window, Xu et al. have taken a step further by employing a temporally separated design to sequentially achieve two distinct functions within a single platform: ROS generation for tumor killing and ROS scavenging for immune protection. They developed an intelligent yolk-shell nanoplatform (SPCCM) that dynamically regulates ROS levels within the TME. This platform employs star-shaped silica as a core loaded with the natural antioxidant proanthocyanidin, encapsulated within an acid-sensitive calcium carbonate shell co-loaded with the photosensitizer Ce6. In the acidic TME, the platform releases the photosensitizer Ce6 and the antioxidant proanthocyanidin. Under laser irradiation, Ce6 generates a transient ROS burst to kill tumor cells and induce ICD, while proanthocyanidin continuously scavenges excess ROS to protect T cells from immunosuppression. In a bilateral tumor model, SPCCM combined with laser therapy achieved 86.8% and 69.5% inhibition rates in primary and distant tumors, respectively, while increasing intratumoral CD8+ and CD4+ T cell infiltration and serum IL-12, TNF-α, and IFN-γ levels. This dynamic regulation strategy thus balances tumor killing with T cell protection [325].

Recent advances in mRNA nanomedicine have opened new avenues for in vivo T cell engineering, with studies demonstrating that targeted lipid nanoparticles can efficiently deliver functional mRNAs to T cells, enabling endogenous expression of exogenous proteins such as CARs [[326], [327], [328]]. This platform technology provides a compelling foundation for delivering antioxidant enzyme mRNAs—such as CAT or GPx4—into TILs, enabling them to upregulate antioxidant enzymes endogenously within the TME. Although direct delivery of antioxidant enzyme mRNAs to TILs has not yet been reported, the rapid maturation of T cell-targeted nanocarriers positions this strategy as a promising avenue for reversing T cell dysfunction.

Oxygen-carrying carriers take an alternative approach by directly improving TME oxygenation to break the hypoxia-driven vicious cycle of oxidative stress. Carriers such as perfluorocarbons enable direct oxygen delivery [329], while more biomimetic designs draw inspiration from erythrocytes. Liu et al. constructed artificial erythrocytes by encapsulating hemoglobin along with polydopamine, and photosensitizers within reassembled erythrocyte membranes. These artificial erythrocytes accumulate in tumor tissues, enabling efficient local oxygen delivery and significantly enhancing photodynamic therapy efficacy against hypoxic tumors, ultimately achieving complete tumor regression [330]. This work provides important technological insights for restoring TME oxygenation through biomimetic oxygen-delivery platforms.

The nanoplatforms discussed above differ substantially in material composition, mechanism of action, drug loading capacity, and targeting strategy, rendering them suitable for different application scenarios. Some directly remodel the TME redox state through inherent catalytic activity, while others function indirectly by delivering functional molecules to enhance T cell antioxidant capacity. Challenges and future directions for clinical translation

Nanomaterials have demonstrated considerable therapeutic potential across a range of diseases [[342], [343], [344]], yet the complexity of the TME presents substantial hurdles for their clinical translation. These challenges span the entire journey from systemic administration to functional engagement within the tumor, encompassing delivery efficiency, distribution, cellular selectivity, and long-term safety.

The enhanced permeability and retention effect is the foundation for nanoparticle accumulation in tumors, but its pronounced heterogeneity across patients and tumor types limits clinical utility; robust efficacy in animal models often fails to translate to human [345]. In response, researchers have pursued optimization strategies at the material design level: tuning particle size and morphology to enhance tumor penetration, incorporating active targeting moieties that recognize tumor-specific cues to improve selectivity and reduce reliance on passive accumulation, and employing PEGylation to prolong circulation and reduce nonspecific uptake [[346], [347], [348], [349]]. Alternative administration routes, including intratumoral injection and inhalation, have also been explored to bypass systemic delivery barriers [[350], [351], [352]].

Even after successful tumor accumulation, heterogeneous distribution of nanoparticles within the tumor mass remains a critical barrier. Hypoxic regions distant from blood vessels often receive inadequate support, and the dense tumor stroma impedes nanoparticle penetration [353]. One promising approach lies in the development of microenvironment-responsive drug delivery systems. By exploiting tumor-specific cues such as elevated ROS levels, acidic pH, or heightened enzymatic activity, these nanoplatforms can be designed to release their cargo on demand within defined regions [354], thereby overcoming physical barriers and enabling deeper tissue penetration. Alongside such strategies, the transient disruption of physical barriers using stroma-modulating agents—including collagenase, hyaluronidase, pulsed high-intensity focused ultrasound, and hyperthermia—can create temporary channels that facilitate nanoparticle infiltration [346,355,356].

Beyond heterogeneous distribution, the risk of off-target effects leading to non-specific cell protection presents another major concern. Most current strategies target the TME broadly, aiming to remodel its redox landscape. Consequently, their effects extend beyond T cells to influence diverse immune populations, some of which—such as Tregs —may benefit from the same protective interventions intended for effector T cells. Achieving bulk accumulation at the tumor site is therefore insufficient; intervention strategies must be refined to the level of specific cell types. Active targeting of T cells, for example using antibody-modified liposomes, offers a direct approach, while specific blockade of suppressive subsets (e.g., inhibiting Foxp3 to prevent Treg differentiation) represents a more sophisticated strategy.

Beyond physical barriers and heterogeneous distribution, nanoparticles must contend with recognition by the immune system upon entering the body. Inorganic nanoparticles and MOFs are particularly prone to recognition as foreign entities, triggering clearance that limit the repeated administration. Biomimetic membrane coating offers a strategy to address this issue: encapsulating nanoparticles within cell-derived membranes mimics self-signals, enabling immune evasion [357]. From a translational perspective, prioritizing biocompatible materials with low immunogenicity and toxicity—such as liposomes, PLGA, and lipid nanoparticles—represents another effective approach to mitigate immunogenic risks.

The biosafety of nanomaterials themselves represents a fundamental prerequisite for clinical translation. Even approved nanomedicines can sometimes trigger unexpected side effects due to their material properties. Potential risks—including metal ion accumulation following degradation of metal-based platforms, toxicity arising from degradation products of organic materials, and the immunostimulatory potential of various material classes—must be systematically evaluated during early-stage development. Moreover, nanoparticles are predominantly cleared through the liver and kidneys [358,359], raising concerns about cumulative organ toxicity. This necessitates the establishment of comprehensive databases encompassing physicochemical properties, toxicokinetics, and tissue distribution profiles of nanomaterials, alongside deeper fundamental insights into material-biological interactions. Incorporating advanced strategies such as predictive toxicology, high-throughput screening, multi-omics approaches, and cellular pharmacokinetics into preclinical studies offers a pathway toward designing for safety from the outset. For metal-based platforms, establishing long-term monitoring systems for tissue distribution of metal ions and systematically assessing chronic toxicity profiles are essential steps toward clinical application. Concurrently, several material design strategies can mitigate toxicity risks: developing biodegradable materials, constructing systems capable of rapid metabolic clearance after fulfilling their function, and employing biomimetic coating or surface passivation modifications. Beyond synthetic approaches, nanomaterials derived from engineered microorganisms with low or no toxicity, such as bacteriophages or bacteria, are emerging as promising platforms with unique antitumor potential [360,361].

The scalable manufacturing and quality control of sophisticated nanoplatforms present inevitable hurdles on the path to clinical translation. Multifunctional systems such as metal-organic frameworks, multi-component carriers, and biomimetic platforms involve complex synthetic processes, exhibit substantial batch-to-batch variability, and face limitations in available characterization methods, all of which impede compliance with Good Manufacturing Practice requirements. Modular design, which decomposes multifunctional platforms into independently producible functional modules, offers a strategy to reduce process complexity. Establishing standardized assays for key quality attributes—including particle size, drug loading capacity, and responsiveness—provides the foundation for effective quality control.

Most of the strategies described above follow an open-loop design logic: materials respond in a predetermined manner upon entering the body but are incapable of adapting to dynamic changes in the microenvironment. This single-threshold activation mode risks either excessive ROS scavenging or insufficient intervention, potentially disrupting normal redox signaling. Notably, preliminary explorations of dynamic ROS regulation have emerged in non-tumor disease models, where material design enables graded release based on ROS concentration gradients and selective elimination [362,363]. These advances offer important insights for the oncology field: the development of intelligent platforms whose scavenging rate is proportional to local ROS levels, coupled with feedback mechanisms that automatically attenuate intervention upon microenvironmental recovery, could enable truly on-demand antioxidant therapy. However, such closed-loop regulation remains a forward-looking concept, awaiting breakthroughs at the intersection of materials science and immunology. An even more creative direction involves engineered cellular carriers that leverage the natural chemotaxis of cells such as mesenchymal stem cells or neutrophils to actively transport nanoparticles to tumor sites [364,365], thus enabling living delivery systems with dynamic responsiveness.

Although current research on nanomaterials has largely focused on direct tumor killing [366,367], strategies that enhance T cell function by remodeling the redox landscape of the TME—such as hypoxia relief using manganese-based nanozymes [314,315], ROS scavenging with ceria-based nanozymes [316], and immunosuppressive cells modulation via MOFs [317,319]—have demonstrated unique translational potential. Concurrently, the carrier function of nanomaterials provides a platform for the precise delivery of the antioxidant molecules and metabolic regulators discussed in previous sections. Preliminary studies have validated the feasibility of this approach [368,369], although such investigations remain in early stages and will be discussed in detail in the following section.

Integrative analysis, clinical translation, and future perspectives

The preceding sections have outlined multi-layered strategies for enhancing T cell antioxidant capacity—reinforcing endogenous defenses, remodeling metabolism, and ameliorating the TME. Building on this foundation, this chapter synthesizes the deeper connections among these interventions, revealing how their mechanisms converge on core signaling hubs and proposing synergistic therapeutic concepts. We also review the current clinical landscape, note the early-stage trials and their preliminary outcomes, and examine the translational hurdles that lie between the laboratory and the clinic, addressing core challenges including safety, selectivity, and model heterogeneity. Finally, we look toward future directions, emphasizing the integration of fundamental biological, cutting-edge technological innovations, and precision medicine strategies, with the ultimate goal of achieving spatiotemporally precise modulation of the redox state of antitumor T cells.

Mechanistic convergence and strategic synergy

Although the intervention strategies described above differ in their operational modalities, their mechanisms converge on a limited set of intracellular signaling hubs. Identifying these convergence points not only helps to elucidate the intrinsic connections between different interventions but also provides a theoretical foundation for designing synergistic strategies. The following sections first classify the mechanisms of these convergence points and their engagement across various interventions (Fig. 6). Metabolic states, intrinsic antioxidant systems, and microenvironmental pressures converge on core redox decision hubs to dictate T cell fate.

In the TME, T cell fates are shaped by the intersection of intrinsic antioxidant machinery (GSH, TRX, CAT), metabolic states (OXPHOS/FAO versus glycolysis), and microenvironmental pressures (high ROS, hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and chronic antigen). These inputs converge onto a set of core signaling hubs—including Nrf2/KEAP1, FOXO, AMPK/mTOR, and mitochondrial quality control machinery—which act as the critical redox decision points. Collectively, these hubs orchestrate T cell fate through transcriptional regulation, metabolic switches, and mitochondrial quality control. A favorable balance drives T cells toward effector/memory phenotypes, whereas persistent oxidative stress pushes them into exhaustion and apoptosis. Multi-layered intervention strategies (NAC, IL-15/21, rapamycin, NIK overexpression, nanomaterials) targeting these hubs illustrate how rewiring the oxidative-metabolic axis can tip the balance toward favorable outcomes.

The convergence points can be broadly divided into three categories. Core transcriptional hubs, such as Nrf2 and FOXO, achieve broad-spectrum defense by regulating the expression of antioxidant genes. Energy and metabolism sensing axes, including AMPK and mTOR, couple cellular nutrient status with redox homeostasis. Organelle quality control systems, centered on mitochondria and autophagy, indirectly safeguard antioxidant capacity by maintaining cellular structural integrity. In addition, specific metabolic pathways such as NADPH homeostasis and ferroptosis regulation also serve as common targets for multiple intervention strategies.

Core transcriptional hubs play particularly critical roles in orchestrating antioxidant responses. The Nrf2/KEAP1 axis serves as a central regulatory pathway for cellular antioxidant defense. AUF acts as a direct activator of Nrf2, engaging this pathway as described in Section 3.4. Certain cytokines, such as IL-6, have also been reported to potentially associate with Nrf2 activation (Section 4.1.4). Selenium compounds, acting as GPx mimetics (e.g., ebselen) and potentiating the GSH system, concurrently support the TRX system by upregulating TrxR expression (Section 3.1), thereby establishing functional links with the network governed by Nrf2. Similarly, the FOXO family of transcription factors occupies a pivotal position in redox regulation within T cells. NAC suppresses the FOXO1-EOMES axis through PI3K/Akt signaling, thereby mitigating terminal differentiation (Section 3.1). IL-7 and IL-21 influence T cell differentiation and survival via JAK-STAT signaling, with mechanisms involving the regulation of FOXO and related transcription factors (Sections 4.1.2, 4.1.3).

Post-translational modification networks represent another layer of regulation engaged by multiple intervention strategies. Modifications such as oxidation, acetylation, and phosphorylation play important roles in this context. NIK signaling suppresses autophagic degradation of HK2, thereby contributing to the maintenance of bioenergetic supply in effector T cells (Section 4.2.2). Oxidative stress itself directly impacts T cell function through redox modification of cysteine residues on cytoskeletal proteins such as cofilin and L-plastin (Section 2.1).

The AMPK/mTOR signaling axis serves as a key node linking energy metabolism and redox regulation. For instance, pharmacological inhibition of mTORC1 by rapamycin has been shown to modulate the metabolic–redox interface in T cells (Section 4.2.3). Metformin facilitates the formation of memory CD8+ T cells through the AMPK-miR-107-Eomes-PD-1 pathway (Section 4.2.3). IL-15 limits glycolysis by lowering mTORC1 activity, while promoting mitochondrial OXPHOS (Section 4.1.1). IL-21-driven metabolic reprogramming is characterized by enhanced FAO and mitochondrial function, and is closely associated with the AMPK/mTOR axis (Section 4.1.2).

The mitochondrial quality control system serves as a common endpoint for multiple intervention strategies. Cytokines such as IL-15 and IL-21 promote mitochondrial biogenesis and functional optimization (Section 4.1.1, 4.1.2). NIK overexpression maintains mitochondrial function by stabilizing the glycolytic rate-limiting enzyme HK2 (Section 4.2.2). AA enhances γδ T cell function by supporting mitochondrial metabolism (Section 3.5). Nanomaterials indirectly protect mitochondrial homeostasis by alleviating hypoxia in the TME (Section 5.1, 5.3). Autophagy, as the central mechanism for clearing damaged mitochondria, plays a critical role in this quality control network. Naïve and memory T cells rely on enhanced autophagy to maintain mitochondrial quality, whereas effector T cells exhibit comparatively lower autophagic activity, relating to mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS accumulation in the TME (Section 2.2). NAC influences T cell fate through metabolic reprogramming, with its mechanism linked to autophagy regulation (Section 3.1). IL-7 and IL-21 improve mitochondrial health via metabolic remodeling, indirectly affecting autophagic demand (Section 4.1.2, 4.1.3).

NADPH serves as an essential electron donor for regenerating the reducing power of the GSH and TRX systems, with the PPP representing its primary source. Enasidenib restricts flux through this pathway, potentially enabling sustained NADPH maintenance under nutrient-deprived conditions (Section 4.2.1). NIK overexpression alleviates oxidative stress by stabilizing the G6PD-NADPH redox axis (Section 4.2.2). IL-15 and IL-21, while promoting mitochondrial function, provide a favorable metabolic context for NADPH-dependent antioxidant systems (Section 4.1.1, 4.1.2). NAC acts by supplementing the substrate for GSH synthesis, and its efficacy depends on sufficient NADPH availability to maintain GSH in its reduced state (Section 3.1).

Ferroptosis, a form of regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, has been implicated in T cell dysfunction (Section 2.1). Effector T cells, with their increased lipid uptake, may have heightened susceptibility to ferroptosis (Section 2.2.2). Selenium compounds, acting as GPx mimetics, may influence ferroptosis sensitivity (Section 3.1). The functional status of system xc−, a key transporter for cystine uptake, affects GSH synthesis (Section 4.3). Strategies aimed at improving mitochondrial function may lower the risk of lipid peroxidation by reducing mitochondrial ROS production, thereby potentially mitigating ferroptosis.

Based on the convergence points identified above, intervention strategies operating at different levels have natural complementarity in temporal scale, mode of action, and cellular outcome, providing a theoretical foundation for designing multi-target combination approaches. The following sections propose representative examples of combinatorial strategies from four dimensions: transcriptional regulation, metabolic optimization, stemness maintenance, and mitochondrial quality control.

The combination of AUF with CAT overexpression represents a synergy between a signaling hub and a terminal effector. AUF activates Nrf2, influencing the expression of a broad range of antioxidant genes including CAT, GSR, and NQO1 (Section 3.4). CAT overexpression directly decomposes H2O2 into water and oxygen without consuming reducing equivalents, and the secreted form of the enzyme can protect bystander cells (Section 3.3). These two strategies operate at different levels: one initiates a broad transcriptional antioxidant program, while the other provides precise clearance of a specific ROS species. Nrf2 activation ensures sustained baseline antioxidant capacity, whereas CAT overexpression addresses acute oxidative stress peaks, with secreted CAT extending protection to unmodified bystander T cells and thereby amplifying the synergistic effect. However, the complexity of Nrf2 function in T cells warrants caution, as some studies have shown that Nrf2-deficient CTLs paradoxically maintain resistance to ROS (Section 3.4), suggesting that the outcome of combined intervention may depend on cellular context. In addition, the long-term effects of constitutive CAT overexpression on T cell biology require careful consideration.

The combination of IL-15 and NAC offers a dual-axis synergy of metabolism and antioxidant defense. IL-15 upregulates GSR, TXNRD1, and PRDX systems, while simultaneously promoting mitochondrial OXPHOS and FAO, optimizing mitochondrial function and reducing ROS production at the source (Section 4.1.1). NAC, in turn, supplies cysteine as a precursor for GSH synthesis and directly scavenges existing ROS (Section 3.1). These two strategies operate on complementary fronts: one curbing ROS generation, the other reinforcing ROS elimination. Together, they are expected to yield a synergistic effect that combines the long-term antioxidant benefit of metabolic optimization with the immediate protection conferred by GSH replenishment. In addition, IL-15 promotes the expansion of TSCM cells, whereas NAC reduces terminal differentiation driven by oxidative stress, jointly preserving T cell stemness. A potential risk lies in the possibility that excessive antioxidant activity may interfere with the physiological ROS signaling required for T cell receptor activation. Moreover, the optimal concentration windows for IL-15 and NAC need to be individually defined, and the combined regimen may narrow these windows further.

The combination of IL-21 with low-dose NAC exemplifies a synergy between stemness maintenance and effector function enhancement. IL-21-mediated metabolic reprogramming contributes to the maintenance of T cell stemness and memory phenotypes, which is accompanied by elevated FAO activity and preserved mitochondrial integrity (Section 4.1.2). Low-dose NAC, when applied during ex vivo expansion, promotes the secretion of effector molecules such as granzyme B and IFN-γ (Section 3.1). These two strategies operate on complementary fronts: one sustains long-term functional capacity, while the other enhances effector potency. TSCM cells expanded with IL-21 may thus mount more effective tumor killing upon antigen encounter by leveraging the enhanced effector functions conferred by NAC. The suppression of the FOXO1-EOMES axis by NAC and the transcriptional repression of EOMES by IL-21 may act synergistically to inhibit terminal differentiation. However, the combination requires careful dose optimization, as high concentrations of NAC can suppress effector molecule secretion (Section 3.1). Furthermore, potential antagonism between cytokines should be considered, as IL-7 and IL-21 have been reported to attenuate the capacity of IL-15 to induce stem-like T cells (Section 4.1.6).

The combination of NIK overexpression with rapamycin constitutes a synergy between damage prevention and quality control. High concentrations of ROS can directly damage mitochondrial DNA, leading to dysfunction and metabolic bottlenecks (Section 2.1). To address this challenge, NIK overexpression maintains mitochondrial function and intracellular redox balance by stabilizing the G6PD-NADPH redox axis (Section 4.2.2). Rapamycin, in turn, elevates cell surface thiol levels and upregulates the expression of antioxidant genes including CAT, NRF-2, SOD-1, and TRX-2, thereby alleviating H2O2-induced oxidative stress and apoptosis (Section 4.2.3). These two strategies operate on complementary fronts: one prevents damage at its source by stabilizing mitochondrial function and limiting aberrant ROS production through metabolic regulation, while the other enhances the overall antioxidant capacity to help clear excess ROS. Together, they are expected to synergistically preserve mitochondrial network integrity and functional quality, thereby improving T cell survival and function under oxidative stress. A notable concern associated with rapamycin treatment is its inherent capacity to promote Treg generation (Section 4.2.3). Careful optimization of treatment regimens is therefore essential to avoid unintended immunosuppressive outcomes when combining these two interventions.

Nanomaterials offer two distinct advantages in the redox regulation of T cells. On one hand, they can sustain modulation of the TME redox state following T cell infusion (Section 5). On the other hand, they serve as carriers for targeted delivery of the antioxidant molecules discussed earlier to the intended cells [368]. This carrier function introduces a new dimension to existing intervention strategies, enabling more precise spatiotemporal control by overcoming the pharmacokinetic limitations of small molecule compounds through nanoscale delivery systems.

The feasibility of this concept has been supported by several recent studies. CAT, a core antioxidant enzyme (Section 3.3), has been successfully loaded into calcium carbonate nanoparticles (CAT@CaCO3NPs). Within the TME, these nanoparticles ameliorate local acidosis, scavenge H2O2, and generate oxygen. This redox-modulatory activity facilitates the repolarization of macrophages from the M2 to the M1 phenotype. Meanwhile, the accumulated Ca2+ derived from the nanoparticles triggers the release of DAMPs from tumor cells, which in turn orchestrates tumor antigen presentation, DC maturation, and T cell-mediated immune activation [370]. In parallel, an ROS-responsive lipid-based mRNA delivery platform has been developed for cancer vaccines, achieving efficient antigen expression and CD8+ T cell activation in draining lymph nodes [371], a finding that supports the feasibility of the antioxidant enzyme mRNA delivery strategy discussed earlier (Section 5.1). For cytokine delivery, cell-derived nanoparticles engineered to display membrane-bound IL-7 and IL-15 have been shown to enhance T cell activation, expansion, and stemness maintenance during in vitro culture without the need for soluble cytokines [372]. More recently, IL-21 was loaded onto acid-sensitive CaMnO3 nanoparticles (CMC-21) and anchored to the surface of CAR-T cells as a “backpack”, enabling sustained IL-21 release within the TME and enhancing CAR-T cell antitumor responses while alleviating hypoxia and acidosis [369]. Together, these studies suggest that nanocarriers can integrate the antioxidant molecules (such as CAT, NAC, and ebselen) and metabolic modulators (including IL-15, IL-21, and rapamycin) discussed in earlier sections into multifunctional delivery systems, enabling a layered synergistic strategy that encompasses ex vivopriming, in vivo maintenance, and localized protection.

Mitochondrial transfer has emerged as an intriguing avenue for modulating T cell metabolic fitness, offering a new entry point for nanomaterial applications. Tumor cells can acquire functional mitochondria from T cells through tunneling nanotubes while simultaneously delivering dysfunctional mitochondria to them, thereby enhancing their own metabolic capacity while weakening T cell function [373]. Studies have shown that combining L-778123 with PD-1 immune checkpoint inhibitors partially inhibits tunneling nanotube formation and mitochondrial transfer, thereby enhancing antitumor efficacy in breast cancer models [373]. However, given the broad target spectrum of L-778123 and the systemic exposure associated with free-drug administration, coupled with adverse events such as myelosuppression observed in early-phase clinical trials [374], this combination strategy faces considerable translational risks. The use of nanocarriers with improved targeting capabilities to deliver this inhibitor may offer a promising approach to enhance its in vivo application prospects. Conversely, promoting the acquisition of healthy mitochondria by T cells also holds therapeutic promise. Bone marrow stromal cells have been shown to transfer functional mitochondria to CD8+ T cells via tunneling nanotubes, significantly enhancing their mitochondrial respiration and spare respiratory capacity, ultimately improving antitumor efficacy [375,376]. These findings lay a foundation for nanomaterial-mediated mitochondrial transplantation strategies, suggesting potential applications such as delivering healthy mitochondria through T cell-targeted nanocarriers or enabling directional transport of mitochondrial components via engineered stromal cell-derived nanovesicles.

Beyond the delivery studies already reported, there remains substantial room for exploring the combination of nanocarriers with the strategies discussed in earlier sections. For instance, rapamycin-loaded nanoparticles can be enriched in tumor tissues through targeted modifications, protecting effector T cells while minimizing systemic exposure (Section 4.2.3). Co-loaded targeted liposomes containing NAC and IL-21 could simultaneously promote TSCM differentiation and enhance antioxidant reserve (Section 3.1, 4.1.2). Responsive nanoplatforms designed to exploit the high ROS levels characteristic of the TME could enable on-demand release of the Nrf2 activator AUF, initiating broad-spectrum antioxidant defense precisely at times of peak oxidative stress (Section 3.4). Looking ahead, nanoplatforms capable of delivering functional mitochondria or mitochondrial components could directly re-energize T cells and enhance their metabolic fitness within the tumor microenvironment. Realizing these concepts will require continued advances in nanomaterial science, including more precise targeting modifications, more controllable release mechanisms, and more comprehensive biosafety evaluations. Nevertheless, their potential merits systematic exploration in future studies.

Clinical translation and challenges

Several intervention strategies discussed in this review have advanced into early-phase clinical testing, with particularly notable progress in cytokine-enhanced ACT, cytokine-enhanced CPI, and metabolic modulation.

Recombinant human IL-15 (rhIL-15) has been evaluated in phase I trials in combination with CPIs, demonstrating acceptable safety and induction of peripheral T cell proliferation (NCT03388632). The IL-15 superagonist N-803, combined with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, has advanced to phase II testing across multiple solid tumor types (NCT03228667). In the context of T-cell malignancies, rhIL-15 has also been combined with mogamulizumab (an anti-CCR4 antibody) in a phase I trial, where it promoted the differentiation and activation of CD8+ memory T cells (NCT04185220). More directly relevant to ACT, IL-15 has been incorporated into CAR-T cell designs to enhance persistence. A phase I trial of GPC3-CAR-T cells co-expressing IL-15 (with or without IL-21) in hepatocellular carcinoma is ongoing (NCT04093648), and a similar approach for pediatric brain tumors (NCT07513194) is planned to initiate in 2026. Additionally, a clinical study is actively evaluating IL-15 supplementation in CAR-T cell therapy for malignant hematological tumors, with CAR-T cell survival time and copy number as key endpoints (NCT06783816). These ongoing trials are designed to test the principle that cytokine-mediated metabolic reprogramming—discussed in Section 4.1—can improve CAR-T cell persistence in both solid and hematological malignancies.

In addition to IL-15 and IL-21, another γc family cytokine, IL-7, has also shown clinical promise. The recombinant IL-7 CYT107 has been evaluated in a randomized phase II trial combined with atezolizumab in advanced urothelial carcinoma (NCT03513952). While the addition of CYT107 did not significantly improve the objective response rate compared to atezolizumab monotherapy (26.3% vs. 23.8%, p = 0.43), it achieved a doubling of complete responses (10.5% vs. 4.8%) and produced durable responses exceeding 21 months in three patients. Notably, CYT107 treatment significantly expanded circulating CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, with preferential expansion of TSCM cells [377], supporting the concept discussed in Section 4.1.3 that IL-7 promotes less-differentiated, oxidative stress-resistant T cell subsets.

Beyond cytokine-based strategies, metabolic modulators have also entered clinical testing. The metabolic modulator metformin has been investigated in combination with nivolumab in a phase II trial for non-small cell lung cancer (NCT03048500), though formal results have not been reported. A more recent phase II trial (NCT04931017) is evaluating metformin in high-risk lung cancer patients, with Treg PD-1 expression as a mechanistic endpoint, reflecting the potential of metabolic reprogramming to modulate T cell functional states (Section 4.2.3).

Parallel to metabolic modulation, direct antioxidant reinforcement has also been evaluated. A phase I trial is actively evaluating NAC as an adjuvant to CD19 CAR-T therapy (axicabtagene ciloleucel) in lymphoma patients (NCT05081479), with preliminary evidence suggesting that NAC enhances CAR-T cell persistence and reduces PD-1 expression. This trial provides the most direct clinical validation of the antioxidant reinforcement strategy discussed in Section 3.1.

Safety is paramount for any intervention. Genetic modifications such as TRX1 or CAT overexpression durably boost T cell antioxidant capacity, but their long-term effects on T cell homeostasis remain poorly understood, and follow-up data are scarce. Reports linking CAR-T therapy to tumour lysis syndrome underscore the need for rigorous safety controls [378]. Beyond these concerns, excessive antioxidant activity can itself cause reductive stress, which may mimic the detrimental effects of oxidative stress. This phenomenon could potentially enhance tumor cell drug resistance by increasing the intracellular pool of reducing agents [379]. In a lung cancer model, NAC or vitamin E supplementation accelerated tumor growth by reducing ROS, DNA damage, and inhibiting the tumour suppressor p53 [380]. Reductive stress stabilizes the redox-sensitive transcription factor BACH1 and promote the transcription of pro-metastatic factors and aerobic glycolysis [[381], [382], [383]]. Antioxidants may also aid metastasis by fostering angiogenesis [384].

The context-dependent nature of redox regulation adds another layer of complexity. For ROS themselves, the same signaling molecule can exert opposing effects depending on the timing and cellular context: physiological ROS levels are essential for T cell activation and effector function, whereas excessive or sustained ROS within the TME drive exhaustion and cell death. Consequently, the same intervention may yield distinct outcomes depending on whether it is applied during early T cell activation or at the effector stage. Moreover, scavenging pathologically elevated ROS may inadvertently compromise the transient ROS signals that are required for effective tumor cell killing. This context dependency extends to various antioxidant interventions themselves. For example, AUF inhibits TrxR activity (exerting a pro-oxidant effect) while simultaneously activating Nrf2 (serving an indirect antioxidant function). Its net impact on T cell function may vary depending on cell type, concentration, and treatment duration. Similarly, studies have reported that under different experimental conditions, IL-21 can either decrease or increase PD-1 expression on CD8+ T cells. Together, these observations underscore the necessity of evaluating redox modulators in relevant physiological or pathological contexts rather than assuming uniform effects.

Targeting selectivity represents a core challenge. At the cellular level, ensuring that interventions act specifically on intratumoral effector T cells rather than on peripheral T cells or Tregs requires strategies that integrate active targeting technologies, such as the antibody-modified nanocarriers discussed in Section 5, to distinguish between different T cell subsets. The design of molecules capable of discriminating effector T cells from Tregs is essential to avoid inadvertently targeting or benefiting the wrong cell subsets. At the patient level, identifying individuals most likely to benefit is equally critical. This necessitates the development of reliable predictive biomarker systems. The functional state of TILs themselves serves as a direct predictor. For example, the mitochondrial status of T cells determines their intrinsic vulnerability to oxidative stress, while the expression profiles of surface inhibitory receptors such as PD-1 and TIM-3, along with stemness-associated transcription factors like TCF-1, reflect the degree of exhaustion. The redox landscape of the TME provides an additional dimension of information. Local ROS levels, the extent of hypoxia, and the functional status of infiltrating MDSCs and TAMs collectively shape the oxidative stress burden experienced by T cells. The predictive value of these markers and the optimal timing for their assessment await validation through prospective clinical trials.

Heterogeneity among patient populations and preclinical models further complicates translational efforts. On one hand, the physiological status of patients varies considerably and influences how T cells respond to intervention. T cells from elderly patients often exhibit mitochondrial dysfunction and elevated baseline oxidative stress, which may lead to different treatment responses compared to their younger counterparts [385,386]. Moreover, patients with cancer frequently present with comorbidities such as diabetes or chronic inflammation, conditions that can themselves alter T cell metabolism and redox profiles, thereby adding another layer of interindividual variability. On the other hand, the predictive power of preclinical models represents a fundamental challenge in translational medicine, particularly in the field of immunotherapy. The TME contains a diverse array of immune cells and a complex stromal architecture, with intricate interactions among them. Even after humanization, immunodeficient mouse models or xenograft systems struggle to faithfully recapitulate the authentic immunological landscape of human tumors, raising the possibility that risks not apparent in animal studies may only emerge in clinical trials. Thus, alongside the advancement of candidate strategies, there is an urgent need to develop next-generation preclinical models that better reflect the true immunological context of human patients. Such efforts include validating findings with patient-derived T cells, introducing animal models that mimic aging or comorbid conditions, and exploring high-throughput screening platforms based on organoid co-culture systems.

Cognitive and technical limitations represent an even deeper barrier. Tools for real-time monitoring of T cell redox status in vivo remain technically challenging, particularly for longitudinal studies within the authentic tumor microenvironment, which limits our ability to precisely control the timing and dosage of interventions. Subcellular-specific regulation also remains an unresolved challenge. For instance, ROS derived from different organelles serve distinct signaling functions, yet current technologies have limited capacity to selectively eliminate pathological mitochondrial ROS while preserving physiological ROS signals originating.

Moreover, several mechanistic gaps persist. The roles of canonical mitochondrial stress pathways—such as the mitochondrial unfolded protein response and PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy—in determining T cell antioxidant capacity and exhaustion susceptibility remain largely unexplored. Redox-dependent epigenetic regulation (e.g., ROS-induced alterations in DNA methylation at exhaustion-related loci like Pdcd1 and TOX) and metabolic checkpoints (e.g., mTOR, NFAT) that drive T cell exhaustion have not been systematically integrated into current antioxidant frameworks.

Future directions

Future breakthroughs will depend on the deep integration of fundamental biological discoveries, cutting-edge technological developments, and clinically oriented therapeutic strategies.

Biological research should continue to focus on elucidating the regulatory mechanisms that govern T cell antioxidant capacity and on identifying associated biomarkers. The development of biomarkers capable of reflecting the redox state of T cells in patients in real time is a prerequisite for achieving targeted antioxidant interventions and advancing personalized treatment strategies. At the mechanistic level, dynamic inter-organellar communication, including mitochondrial transfer and mitochondria-ER contact sites, plays a critical role in determining the antioxidant resilience and fate of T cells. For instance, the transfer of functional mitochondria from bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells to CD8+ T cells via tunneling nanotubes markedly enhances their metabolic activity and mitochondria function, thereby supporting T cell survival under oxidative stress within the TME [375,387]. In addition, mitochondria-ER contacts sites serve as a hub that regulate signaling and metabolic components, contributing to the rapid recall responses of memory CD8+ T cells [91,388]. A deeper understanding of these inter-organellar communication pathways holds promise for identifying new targets to enhance T cell antioxidant capacity, delay exhaustion, and preserve stemness. For antioxidant molecules with well-defined dose-dependent effects, such as NAC and AA, rigorously designed clinical trials are needed to establish optimal therapeutic windows, thereby enabling precise enhancement of T cell antioxidant capacity while avoiding immune suppression or functional impairment resulting from improper dosing.

Technology-driven innovation serves as the core engine for overcoming current barriers. The development of redox biosensors will enable, for the first time, real-time monitoring and feedback regulation of T cell redox status, addressing the current lack of tools for in vivo dynamic assessment. Intelligent nanosystems, leveraging light, heat, or ultrasound can actively enhance tumor vascular permeability and penetrate dense stromal barriers, thereby improving both the tumor accumulation efficiency and deep tissue penetration of nanoparticles and offering new strategies for spatiotemporally precise antioxidant interventions [389,390]. Epigenetic editing technologies such as CRISPRa/i provide new tools for controllable expression of antioxidant genes, avoiding the long-term risks associated with constitutive overexpression. An even more ambitious direction is subcellular targeting, achieved through nanocarriers designed to localize to specific organelles such as mitochondria or the endoplasmic reticulum, enabling selective elimination of pathological ROS from diseased compartments while preserving physiological ROS signals.

At the level of therapeutic application, the scientific rationale and optimal timing for combining antioxidant interventions with existing therapies need to be clearly defined. For combination with ACT, a layered synergistic strategy encompassing ex vivopriming, in vivo maintenance, and localized protection is worth exploring. During the ex vivo expansion phase, IL-21 can initiate stem cell-like programs and optimize mitochondrial function (Section 4.1.2), while NAC pretreatment enhances immediate antioxidant reserves (Section 3.1). After infusion, nanoplatforms can facilitate in vivo maintenance and localized protection through targeted delivery of rapamycin to modulate mTOR signaling (Section 4.2.3) or delivery of CAT mRNA to endow T cells with intrinsic antioxidant capacity (Section 5.3).

When combined with radiotherapy or chemotherapy, strategies that alleviate hypoxia could theoretically enhance treatment efficacy. Hypoxia is a key driver of resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapy [391,392], and mitigating this condition using materials such as manganese-based nanozymes might improve the sensitivity of tumor cells to these modalities. Radiotherapy and certain chemotherapeutic agents kill tumor cells in part by generating ROS, which can simultaneously exacerbate oxidative stress in the TME and impair TILs. The temporally separated design of SPCCM offers a valuable reference for addressing this trade-off, where Ce6 generates a burst of ROS under laser irradiation to kill tumor cells and induce ICD, while procyanidins continuously scavenge excess ROS to protect T cell function (Section 5.3). Although a growing number of studies have focused on improving oxygen supply within the TME using nanomaterials to enhance the efficacy of radiotherapy and chemotherapy, relatively few have considered the impact on the functional state of TILs. Therefore, a temporally separated design that alleviates hypoxia while achieving effective tumor killing and simultaneously preserving or enhancing T cell immunity represents a promising avenue for further exploration.

The feasibility of combining antioxidant interventions with immune checkpoint inhibitors has been supported by several studies. In syngeneic mouse models of lung and pancreatic cancer, metformin combined with anti-PD-1 treatment showed synergistic antitumor effects, enhancing T cell infiltration and therapeutic efficacy [393]. The dual-regulation strategy of cSeLAN, which scavenges extracellular ROS and concurrently increases intracellular ROS, markedly improved the antitumor response of immunologically cold tumors when combined with PD-1 blockade (Section 5.2). A nanoplatform co-delivering PD-L1 siRNA and CAT synergistically improved T cell function by simultaneously reducing PD-L1 expression and scavenging ROS (Section 5.3). Despite these advances, one critical question remains regarding the optimal sequencing of these interventions. Whether antioxidant strategies should be applied first to improve T cell infiltration, followed by PD-1 blockade to unleash effector function, or administered concurrently for better tumor control, has not been systematically addressed and warrants further investigation.

Single-cell multi-omics-based patient stratification can not only guide the development of personalized treatment strategies but also provide a critical foundation for precise clinical trial design. By delineating the common principles and individual characteristics of T cell redox states across different tumor types, it becomes possible to identify patient subsets most likely to benefit from specific antioxidant interventions, thereby optimizing therapeutic strategies and improving tumor control and patient outcomes. Building on this foundation, umbrella or basket clinical trials tailored to these distinct subsets can be designed to facilitate clinical translation, advancing antioxidant interventions from the laboratory toward precision clinical application.

Conclusion

The efficacy of ACT in solid tumors is constrained by the pro-oxidative TME, where excessive ROS impair T cell function through multifaceted mechanisms, including disruption of signal transduction and transcriptional programs, damage the cytoskeleton, induction of mitochondrial dysfunction, acceleration of telomere shortening, and accumulation of toxic molecules such as unfolded proteins and lipid peroxides. Collectively, these injuries drive T cells toward functional exhaustion or death.

Crucially, not all T cells are equally vulnerable. Effector T cells are the most vulnerable due to their fragmented mitochondrial and weak antioxidant reserves. Memory T cells maintain redox homeostasis through fused mitochondrial networks and enhanced antioxidant gene expression. Tregs, by contrast, exploit metabolic adaptations and intrinsic antioxidant programs to gain a survival advantage under oxidative stress. Other immune cells within the TME also respond divergently: MDSCs and M2-type TAMs produce large amounts of ROS as immunosuppressive weapons, while NK cells and DCs are functionally impaired by oxidative stress. This complexity underscores the need for selective intervention—protecting effector T cells without reinforcing suppressive populations or damage to innate immune cells.

To address this challenge, we propose a hierarchical intervention framework. Reinforcing endogenous antioxidant defenses (Section 3) enhances the intrinsic ROS scavenging capacity of T cells. Metabolic remodeling (Section 4) reduces ROS production at the source and strengthens antioxidant reserves by optimizing energy metabolism and mitochondrial function. Nanomaterials (Section 5) create a favorable TME by scavenging ROS and alleviating hypoxia. These strategies are not mutually exclusive; their mechanisms converge on core hubs such as Nrf2, FOXO, and the AMPK/mTOR axis (Section 6.1), providing a rationale for combination regimens. Within this framework, nanomaterials serve as a critical bridge, enabling the targeted delivery of antioxidant molecules and metabolic modulators to the TME, thereby connecting the ex vivo T cell optimization with precise in vivo protection. This integrated redox modulation across the entire T cell therapy workflow focuses interventions on effector T cells and their local microenvironment, thereby minimizing the potential drawbacks associated with broad-spectrum antioxidant strategies and offering a promising multi-dimensional solution to overcome the current bottlenecks in solid tumor immunotherapy.

Translating these strategies into clinical practice will require overcoming multi-layered barriers related to safety, selectivity, patient heterogeneity, and technical limitations. Future breakthroughs will depend on deeper fundamental insights into inter-organellar communication, the development of redox biosensors and intelligent nanosystems, and the integration of single-cell multi-omics-guided patient stratification into clinical trial design.

Rather than simply eliminating ROS, the ultimate goal is to restore redox homeostasis in antitumor T cells within the TME—alleviating pathological suppression while preserving physiological signals, and protecting effector T cells while sparing suppressive subsets. Achieving this goal would mark a pivotal step toward advancing ACT therapy for solid tumors.
