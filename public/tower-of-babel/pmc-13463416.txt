Prognostic significance and optimal staging of supraclavicular lymph node metastasis in upper esophageal squamous cell carcinoma undergoing chemoradiotherapy
Authors: Bowen Zhang, Huan Zhang, Hanwen Zheng, Xiaoque Xie, Dongmei Ye, Liyang Zhu, Mei Kang, Liang Xiao, Yu Chen, Wanli Xia, Ying Dai, Yichun Wang, Jundong Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe optimal staging of supraclavicular lymph node metastasis (SCLNM) in upper esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (UESCC) remains debated. This study aimed to evaluate its prognostic significance and clinical implications in patients treated with definitive chemoradiotherapy.MethodsThis study included 435 newly diagnosed UESCC patients who received definitive chemoradiotherapy between January 2013 and October 2022. Survival and prognostic factors were analyzed by Kaplan-Meier and Cox regression methods.ResultsSCLNM was diagnosed in 49 patients (11.3%) and was strongly associated with metastasis at stations 101, 106rec, 106pre, and 106tb. Multivariate analysis confirmed SCLNM as an independent prognostic factor. Patients with SCLNM had poorer survival than those with M0 disease but better than those with other distant metastases (median overall survival: 24.0 vs. 67.0 vs. 8.0 months). Survival was significantly lower in SCLNM patients compared to N0M0 (P < 0.001) and N1M0 (P = 0.004) patients, but no significant difference was observed compared to N2M0 (P = 0.477) or N3M0 (P = 0.204) patients. After reclassifying SCLNM as a regional lymph node metastasis, N1 patients with SCLNM exhibited worse overall survival than those without SCLNM, although the statistical significance was marginal (P = 0.048); however, this difference was not seen in N2 or N3 categories. In the revised staging system where SCLNM was assigned to N2 or higher, survival differentiation across stages became more distinct, yielding a higher prognostic accuracy compared to the current AJCC staging system.ConclusionsSCLNM in UESCC patients undergoing definitive CRT shows prognostic similarity to N2–3 disease, but this finding is preliminary and requires further validation.Keywords: Esophageal squamous cell cancer, Cervical and upper thoracic, Chemoradiotherapies, Supraclavicular lymph node, Prognosis, Cancer staging

Background

Esophageal carcinoma (EC) is the 11th most common cancer and the 7th leading cause of cancer death worldwide, highlighting its significant public health burden [1]. The disease exhibits a distinct biological profile, marked by a strong tendency for lymph node metastasis. The dense and bidirectional lymphatic network of the esophageal wall allows for extensive lymph node spread from the neck to the abdomen, even in early-stage disease [2]. Multimodal treatment strategies involving definitive chemoradiotherapy (CRT) are widely adopted for non-metastatic upper EC. However, the management of supraclavicular lymph node metastasis (SCLNM), which frequently occurs in such tumors, continues to present challenges in both therapeutic strategy and disease classification.

Although SCLNM is often associated with poor prognosis, its role as an independent prognostic factor remains inconsistent [3–6]. Notably, patients with SCLNM show significantly better survival than those with visceral metastases [7, 8]. This uncertainty is reflected in major staging systems: both the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) and Japan Esophageal Society (JES) classifications have historically defined SCLNM inconsistently, not only between systems but also across their own successive editions [9]. SCLNM has variably been categorized as regional (M0) or distant metastatic (M1) disease, creating ongoing ambiguity in prognostic stratification and clinical management.

In upper EC, prevailing evidence supports classifying SCLNM as a regional rather than a systemic disease. A large-scale Japanese retrospective study found no survival impact from SCLNM [3], aligning with SEER and other analyses that exclude it as an independent prognostic factor [5, 10]. Conversely, another study associates SCLNM in cervical EC with poor survival, suggesting these nodes may represent extra-regional spread [4]. Whether to treat with curative-intent locoregional therapy or manage as systemic disease remains a critical and evidence-limited clinical decision.

Therefore, this study evaluates the prognostic impact of SCLNM in upper esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (UESCC) treated with CRT. Through survival and prognostic factor analysis, we aim to clarify whether SCLNM reflects a locally-advanced or systemically-dominant phenotype in this setting. Furthermore, we propose a clinically reasoned staging framework that may better guide risk stratification and therapeutic strategy for this distinct patient subgroup, ultimately contributing to more personalized and evidence-based management of UESCC.

Patients and methods

Patients

This retrospective cohort study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (approval no. PJ-2025-01-50). In accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, anonymized clinical data were retrospectively analyzed, and the requirement for informed consent was waived. From January 2013 to October 2022, a total of 1,649 patients with upper EC were identified by screening electronic records in our hospital’s medical record management system.

Anatomically, UEC was strictly defined as a tumor located between the cricopharyngeal muscle and the azygos arch. This segment is further subdivided by the thoracic inlet into cervical EC and upper thoracic EC. Tumor location was determined based on the central point of the lesion within the esophageal lumen.

Patients with pathologically confirmed UESCC were included if they met all the following criteria: (1) underwent curative-intent CRT; (2) had no distant metastasis other than SCLNM; (3) had baseline CT images available for review prior to treatment initiation; (4) had complete clinical data and maintained follow-up records. Patients were excluded according to the following criteria: (1) had previous or synchronous secondary or multiple primary tumors; (2) had synchronous tumors located in the middle or lower thoracic esophagus; (3) had previous esophagectomy or immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy; (4) had survival less than one months. During the same study period, we additionally enrolled 82 patients with de novo distant metastasis (excluding SCLNM) who underwent systemic treatment. The distribution of metastatic sites was as follows: lung (n = 29), bone (n = 15), distant lymph nodes (n = 6), liver (n = 2), multiple sites (n = 17), and other sites (n = 13).

Staging and lymph node station definitions

Staging of UESCC was performed in accordance with the 8th edition AJCC TNM classification. Clinical T and N stages were determined based on CT, PET/CT, endoscopic ultrasound (EUS), or a combination of these modalities. Due to the limited resolution of CT in differentiating early T-stage lesions, clinical T stage was categorized into T1-2 (combined), T3, and T4. The diagnostic criteria for lymph node metastasis in clinical N staging were as follows: (1) short-axis diameter ≥ 10 mm (≥ 5 mm for nodes located in the tracheoesophageal groove); (2) morphologic abnormalities (e.g., fusion, necrosis) or interval enlargement on serial imaging; and (3) SUVmax ≥ 2.4 on PET/CT. Biopsy of some superficial cervical lymph nodes was performed, with cytopathological confirmation. All staging assessments were independently performed by two researchers (Huan Zhang and Bowen Zhang), with any discrepancies resolved through consensus review (Yichun Wang).

Lymph node station classification adhered to the 12th edition of the Japanese Classification of Esophageal Cancer by the JES [11]. The supraclavicular lymph node region corresponds to station 104. Its anatomical boundaries are defined as: superiorly, the lower border of the cricoid cartilage; inferiorly, the clavicle; medially, the medial border of the carotid sheath; and posterolaterally, the anterior border of the trapezius muscle.

Treatments

All patients underwent radiotherapy delivered via either three-dimensional conformal radiation therapy (3DCRT) or intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT). The radiation fields encompassed elective nodal irradiation or involved-field irradiation. The gross tumor volume (GTV) comprised the primary esophageal tumor and radiologically involved regional lymph nodes. The clinical target volume (CTV) was generated by expanding the GTV to account for subclinical disease. For the primary tumor, the CTV expansion margins were 3.0 cm craniocaudally along the esophageal wall and 0.5–0.6 cm radially, with subsequent anatomical refinement. For radiologically positive lymph nodes, a uniform 0.5–0.6 cm isotropic expansion was applied to the GTV, followed by anatomical boundary adjustment. For elective nodal irradiation, the CTV included the bilateral supraclavicular and upper mediastinal nodal stations (e.g., stations 101, 104, 106recR/L, 105, 106pre). The planning target volume (PTV) was then created by applying an additional institution-specific margin of 0.5–0.8 cm to the CTV. Definitive radiotherapy was administered to a total dose of ≥ 50 Gy, delivered in daily fractions of 1.8–2.0 Gy. For elective nodal irradiation, a prophylactic dose of 45–50.4 Gy in 25–28 fractions was administered to the PTV, followed by a sequential boost to the esophageal tumor and involved lymph nodes, yielding a final cumulative dose of 59.4–66 Gy in 30–33 fractions.

Concurrent or sequential systemic chemotherapy was administered with radiotherapy, utilizing regimens based on taxanes, platinum agents, and/or fluoropyrimidines. The most frequently employed regimen consisted of a two-drug combination, most commonly a taxane (e.g., paclitaxel) paired with a platinum agent (e.g., cisplatin or carboplatin). Alternative protocols included a platinum agent combined with a fluoropyrimidine (e.g., cisplatin plus 5-fluorouracil). The choice of specific agents and dosing schedule was determined by the discretion of medical radiation oncologists.

Data collection and surveillance

Clinicopathological data were retrospectively extracted from institutional medical records. Collected variables included demographic characteristics (age at diagnosis, sex), tumor-specific features (location, length, TNM stage), the specific station(s) of involved lymph nodes, treatment details (systemic therapy and radiotherapy parameters), and follow-up outcomes.

The study follow-up concluded on July 31, 2025, with all outcome measures censored at this predefined cutoff date. The primary endpoint was overall survival (OS), defined as the interval from the date of diagnosis to death from any cause or the last confirmed follow-up. Vital status was ascertained using medical records, telephone follow-up, or official population registry data from local police stations.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R (version 4.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and SPSS (version 27.0.1; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) .Survival curves were constructed using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Prognostic factors were assessed via Cox proportional hazards regression models. Variables significant (P < 0.05) in univariate analyses were included in the multivariable model. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Correlation analysis was performed using the corrplot package. The timeROC package was used to conduct time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and compute time-specific area under the curve (AUC) values for assessing prognostic model accuracy.

Results

Patient characteristics

This study enrolled 435 patients with UESCC who underwent definitive CRT after stringent screening (Table 1). The median age was 69 years (range: 39–90), and the median tumor length was 4.0 cm (range: 0.3–16.0). The cohort was predominantly male (64.8%, n = 282), with most tumors located in the upper thoracic segment (73.6%, n = 320). Based on the 8th edition AJCC TNM staging system, T3 stage accounted for 76.6% (n = 333) and N0 stage for 49.2% (n = 214). SCLNM (station 104) was identified in 49 patients (11.3%). Lymph node metastases were also present in the following stations: 101 (6.4%, n = 28), 105 (7.8%, n = 34), 106rec (43.4%, n = 189), 106pre (3,2%, n = 14), 106tb (2.5%,n = 12), and 107 (5.5%, n = 21). Additionally, 82 contemporaneous patients who had received treatment for distant metastasis other than SCLNM (non-SCLNM M1) were enrolled (Table 1).

Prognostic factors for OS

For the cohort of 435 UESCC patients treated with definitive CRT, univariate Cox regression analysis identified gender (P = 0.015), T category (P < 0.001), N category (P < 0.001), tumor length (P < 0.001), and station 104 status (P < 0.01) as significant risk factors for OS (Fig. 2). In the multivariate analysis, however, only T category (P = 0.011), N category (P = 0.005), tumor length (P = 0.02), and station 104 status (P = 0.002) retained independent prognostic significance.

Survival outcomes of SCLNM according to current AJCC staging

As shown in Fig. 3A, patients with SCLNM had poorer survival outcomes compared to M0 but better than non-SCLNM M1 disease. The median OS (mOS) was 67.0 months (95% CI, 53.0–87.0) in patients with M0 stage, 24.0 months (95% CI, 15.0–36.0) in those with SCLNM, and 8.0 months (95% CI, 7.0–12.0) in those with non-SCLNM M1 disease, with significant differences observed across the three groups (P < 0.0001). Additionally, patients with SCLNM showed significantly lower survival than that of N0M0 (P < 0.001) or N1M0 patients (P = 0.004), whereas no significant difference was observed when compared to N2M0 (P = 0.477) or N3M0 patients (P = 0.204)(Fig. 3B).

The presence of SCLNM was associated with a worse prognosis in node-negative (N0) patients (mOS: 23.0 vs. 87.0 months; P = 0.002), but not in node-positive (N+) patients (mOS: 25.0 vs. 43.0 months; P = 0.28; Fig. 3C). Further analysis demonstrated that patients with metastasis limited to station 104 (SCLNM) exhibited a significantly poorer prognosis than those with metastasis confined to station 106rec (P = 0.0025).

Re-classifying SCLNM as regional nodal disease

We hypothesized that SCLNM could be considered as regional lymph node metastasis, and therefore incorporated the number of SCLNM into the total number of regional lymph node metastases for each patient. Based on this assumption, N-classification was reassigned according to AJCC staging criteria.

As shown in Fig. 4A, N1 category patients with SCLNM exhibited significantly poorer OS than those without SCLNM (P = 0.048). In contrast, no significant survival differences were observed between patients with and without SCLNM in the N2 or N3 categories (P = 0.72 and 0.54, respectively; Fig. 4B-C). Furthermore, among patients with SCLNM, OS did not differ significantly across different N stages (all P > 0.05; Fig. 4D).

Validation of the modified versus the AJCC staging system

Based on these findings, SLNM was reclassified as regional lymph node metastasis and assigned as N2 or higher. Consequently, N1 patients with SCLNM were upgraded to N2, while N2 and N3 patients with SCLNM retained their original stages. All cases were then restaged using this modified N-classification system.

Figure 5A presents the survival differences according to the modified N categories among the 435 UESCC patients who underwent definitive CRT. As shown in Figure 5B, the AJCC staging system fails to effectively differentiate survival outcomes between patients with SCLNM and those with stage IVA disease (P = 0.539). By comparison, application of the modified staging system resulted in clearly differentiated survival across stage categories, with all intergroup comparisons statistically significant (all P < 0.05; Figure 5C). Time dependent ROC cures analysis further demonstrated that the modified staging system provided better predictive performance for OS than the AJCC system (Figure 5D).

Discussion

This study reveals that SCLNM in UESCC patients treated with definitive CRT carries an intermediate prognosis between localized and systemic disease. We confirmed SCLNM as an independent prognostic factor, though its effect depends on concurrent regional nodal burden: it markedly worsens survival in N0-1 patients but not in N2-3 cases. Consequently, we propose a revised staging model that integrates SCLNM into the regional node, which outperformed the conventional AJCC 8th edition system in prognostic stratification, offering a refined risk-classification approach.

Supraclavicular lymph nodes serve as critical junctions in the esophageal lymphatic pathway prior to the bloodstream, as they receive drainage both from adjacent nodal stations and via direct connections to the upper esophagus and major lymphatic trunks [9]. The reported incidence of unilateral SCLNM in upper EC ranges from 7.0% to 11.6% [2, 12, 13], with a corresponding 5-year OS of 24.4%-35.3% [3, 14, 15], which is consistent with our finding of 28.4%.

Several studies have reported no significant survival difference between node-positive UESCC patients with and without pathological SCLNM. Wang et al. found no OS difference for UESCC patients (P = 0.077), although the study was limited by a small sample size [5]. Another study reported that neither clinical nor pathological SCLNM significantly affected OS in node-positive UESCC patients [14]. However, these studies lack N-stage subgroup analysis. A large JES surgical series showed that among node-positive upper EC patients, SCLNM did not significantly affect survival. Moreover, among patients with the same number of positive lymph nodes, SCLNM was not associated with survival [3]. Our study align with the intricate biology of lymphatic dissemination in EC and contribute to the persistent discourse on staging classification. Similar to previous studies on patients receiving definitive CRT, our results show that SCLNM in upper EC should be classified as regional nodal disease [15–17]. Furthermore, a multicenter retrospective study of 850 upper thoracic ESCC patients treated with definitive radiotherapy demonstrated that SCLNM overlapped with N2 (P > 0.05) but was clearly distinct from N0 and N1 (all P < 0.01), supporting the classification of SCLNM as N2 disease in this setting [18]. Similar to our results, SCLNM showed survival outcomes comparable to N2-3 patients, supporting its classification as a locally advanced N category rather than systemic disease.

Both clinically and pathologically diagnosed SCLNM in UESCC likely represents regional rather than systemic disease, and could remain amenable to curative-intent therapies. In surgically treated patients, pathological SCLNM shows no survival difference across N stages after adjusting for total nodal burden, suggesting it is a marker of nodal burden rather than an independent prognostic factor. In contrast, clinical SCLNM is often detected at a relatively advanced stage, sometimes with features such as lymph node fusion, larger mass, and higher node count. These characteristics are associated with high nodal burden in non-surgical patients, likely due to limited imaging resolution and stage migration. These findings underscore the need to consider diagnostic modality and total nodal burden when interpreting the prognostic impact of SCLNM.

Our findings further highlight the prognostic relevance of metastatic lymph node location in EC, showing that metastases confined to SCLNM (104) lead to a worse outcome than those limited to station 106rec. Furthermore, the SCLNM showed a strong association with metastasis in other regional lymph nodes (stations 101, 106rec, 106pre, and 106tb). Consequently, the status of station 106rec may serve as an indicator for considering supraclavicular lymph node assessment or dissection in upper EC [19].While prior research has reported that SCLNM confers a poorer prognosis than metastasis to the cervical paraesophageal nodes (station 101) in thoracic EC [20], a direct comparison between these two stations was not feasible in our cohort due to the limited number of patients with metastases confined to station 101 alone. These findings provide direct support for the 10th and 11th JES systems in classifying 106rec as N1 and 104 as N2 for upper EC [21, 22]. The clinical stage of the 12th edition of the JES classifies station 104 metastasis as M1a (N2-3) for upper thoracic EC, distinguishing it from M1b disease. This change mirrors our proposal to stage SCLNM as N2 or higher rather than M1b [11]. Our study has two main clinical implications for non-surgical UESCC. First, incorporating SCLNM into regional nodal assessment improves staging consistency and prognostic accuracy. Second, this framework enables personalized treatment: SCLNM with high nodal burden (N2–3) supports curative-intent CRT, similar to N2–3M0 patients. In contrast, SCLNM with limited nodal involvement (N0–1) defines a high-risk subgroup warranting intensified systemic therapy or surveillance. These findings avoid both undertreatment and overtreatment of SCLNM, aligning with existing literature [14, 18, 23].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective, single-institution design carries inherent risks of selection bias and unmeasured confounding. Second, lymph node status was primarily determined by clinical imaging, which may affect staging precision (e.g., false positives or negatives). Third, heterogeneity in treatment details (e.g., chemotherapy regimens, radiation fields, and elective nodal irradiation criteria) may have introduced bias, as supraclavicular nodes were not routinely covered by involved-field irradiation, potentially disadvantaging SCLNM-negative patients by leaving their potential subclinical disease untreated. Fourth, our findings are derived exclusively from patients receiving definitive CRT, and their applicability to surgical populations remains uncertain. Finally, the proposed staging model requires external validation in independent prospective or multicenter cohorts.

Conclusions

In our cohort of UESCC patients undergoing definitive CRT, SCLNM showed characteristics of an advanced locoregional disease entity. Its adverse impact appeared most pronounced in patients with limited regional nodal involvement, whereas it did not show additional risk in those with high nodal burden. One might hypothesize that integrating SCLNM into regional nodal classification as N2 or higher could enhance prognostic stratification. However, given the significant limitations of this retrospective study (clinical staging, treatment heterogeneity, small sample size), these findings are hypothesis-generating only and requires validation in larger, pathologically staged cohorts before clinical application.
