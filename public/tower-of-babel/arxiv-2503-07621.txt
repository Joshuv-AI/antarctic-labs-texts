Calculus for Functions with Fuzzy Inputs and Outputs: Applications to Fuzzy Differential Equations
Laécio Carvalho de Barros, Estevão Esmi, Francielle Santo Pedro Simões, Mina Shahidi

Abstract
This article presents a theory of differential and integral calculus for mapping between Banach spaces formed by subsets of fuzzy numbers called A A -linearly correlated fuzzy numbers ( ℝ ℱ ⁡ ( A ) \mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)} ), where both the domain and codomain are spaces composed of fuzzy numbers. This is one of the main contributions of this study from a theoretical point of view, as well-known approaches to fuzzy calculus in the literature typically deal with fuzzy number-valued functions defined on intervals of real numbers. Notions of differentiability and integrability based on complex functions are proposed. Moreover, we introduce the study of ordinary differential equations for which the solutions are functions ℝ ℱ ⁡ ( A ) → ℝ ℱ ⁡ ( A ) \mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}\to\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)} or D ⊆ ℝ → ℝ ℱ ⁡ ( A ) D\subseteq\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)} . For the latter case, we present an initial study of the solution and its phase portrait for two-dimensional differential equation systems. In particular, for the former case, we examine the Lotka-Volterra model and analyze its phase portrait.

Most approaches to fuzzy calculus presented in the literature are developed for functions whose domain is an interval of

Hence, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first time that a calculus theory is proposed for functions where both the input and output are fuzzy numbers.
To this end, we shall consider the space
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
introduced by Esmi
et al.

[

Using the

In this approach, the term fuzzy curve refers to fuzzy number-valued functions defined on an interval of
\mathbb{R}
, and the term fuzzy mapping refers to functions that map fuzzy numbers to fuzzy numbers.

Problems where the input may be fuzzy for a variety of reasons, such as intrinsic uncertainties associated with assessing the value of the independent variable, among others, arise frequently in mathematical modeling.

For example, the time variable in the study of the spread of diseases or wildfires can also be considered fuzzy, as the moment of infection or the initiation of a fire is uncertain. Another example is the study of the concentration of substances at the border (or at the bottom) of a river, which also may be modeled as a phenomenon with fuzzy inputs since the border (or the bottom) of a river is uncertain and can be described by fuzzy sets.

From a mathematical point of view, a motivation for studying functions that map fuzzy numbers to fuzzy numbers is that it is possible to confront compositions
f\circ g
, where
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}}}
and
f:\mathbb{\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}}}\to\mathbb{\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}}}

Note that

It is worth noting that compositions of functions naturally appear in the study of differential equations, as the
n

x
and its derivatives of lower order with a given function
f

such that

) in a first-order system of classical (crisp) differential equations involves assessing the derivative of

with respect to

Beyond the fact that the formulation of many problems may involve products of fuzzy numbers, the notions of multiplication operation and reciprocal are also important if one intends to define derivative as a limit of quotients.
In this context, the usual multiplication operator on the class of fuzzy numbers, which coincides with the Zadeh extension of the real number multiplication operator, can not be used here because
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is not closed with respect to it.
In other words, for

does not belong to
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
.
Recently, this problem has been overpassed by using the

\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}

A
, denoted by

, is a unit set, say

[

.
In addition, the
\Psi

[
21

. However, the notion of a reciprocal or inverse element, that is, for
B\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
there exists
B^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
such that the
\Psi
-cross product of
B
and

is equal to the real number
1
, is not defined for all

such that

does not represent the number zero (

For example, if

Mathematical Background

Let
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
be the set of fuzzy numbers. For the sake of simplicity, we use the same symbol to denote a fuzzy number and its membership function, that is, for
A\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
and for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, the membership degree of
x
in
A
is given by
A(x)
. Recall that the
\alpha
-levels of
A
are non-empty close intervals of
\mathbb{R}

[
2
,
36
]
, that we denote here by
[A]_{\alpha}:=[\underline{a}_{\alpha},\overline{a}_{\alpha}]
for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Moreover, the space
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
forms a complete metric space with the metric
d_{\infty}
defined for every
A,B\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
as follows
[
10
]
:

d_{\infty}(A,B)=\sup_{0\leq\alpha\leq 1}\max\{|a^{-}_{\alpha}-b^{-}_{\alpha}|,|a^{+}_{\alpha}-b^{+}_{\alpha}|\}.

Trapezoidal and triangular fuzzy numbers are well-known and used examples of fuzzy numbers that can be defined levelwise as follows. For
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{R}
such that
a\leq b\leq c\leq d
, we can defined a trapezoidal fuzzy number
A
, which is also denoted by the symbol
(a;b;c;d)
, such that
[A]_{\alpha}=[a+\alpha(b-a),d-\alpha(d-c)]
for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
.
If
b=c
, we speak of a triangular fuzzy number and denote by the symbol
(a;b;d)
instead.

The usual addition and scalar product can be defined level-wise as follows:

[C+D]_{\alpha}=[\underline{c}_{\alpha}+\underline{d}_{\alpha},\overline{c}_{\alpha}+\overline{d}_{\alpha}]

(1)

and

[\lambda C]_{\alpha}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\left[\lambda\underline{c}_{\alpha},\lambda\overline{c}_{\alpha}\right]&,\mbox{ if }\lambda\geq 0,\\
\left[\lambda\overline{c}_{\alpha},\lambda\underline{c}_{\alpha}\right]&,\mbox{ if }\lambda<0.\end{array}\right.

(2)

for all
B,C\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.

We say that a fuzzy number
A
is asymmetric (or nonsymmetric) if for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
there exists
y\in\mathbb{R}
such that
A(x-y)\neq A(x+y)
.
An alternative definition of this concept of asymmetry is provided by Lemma 3.5 in
[
14
]
which states that
A
is asymmetric if
\underline{a}_{\alpha}+\overline{a}_{\alpha}
is not constant with respect to
\alpha
.
An immediate consequence of this lemma is that the set of asymmetric fuzzy numbers is open. Since
A
is asymmetric, there exist
\alpha\neq\beta

such that
\epsilon:=|(\underline{a}_{\alpha}+\overline{a}_{\alpha})-(\underline{a}_{\beta}+\overline{a}_{\beta})|>0
. For all
B\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
with
d_{\infty}(B,A)<0.2\epsilon
we obtain
|(\underline{b}_{\alpha}+\overline{b}_{\alpha})-(\underline{b}_{\beta}+\overline{b}_{\beta})|\geq 0.2\epsilon>0
. Therefore, all elements in the open ball of radius
0.2\epsilon
centered at
A
are also asymmetric.
One can also prove that the set of asymmetric fuzzy numbers is dense in
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
w.r.t.
d_{\infty}

[
12
]
. Thus, from a practical point of view, this set is sufficiently large to approximate any fuzzy number and, furthermore, asymmetry is preserved under small perturbations.

In
[
14
]
, Esmi et al. prove that the mapping
(r,q)\mapsto r+qA
is injective if, and only if,
A
is asymmetric.
By composing this mapping and the usual bijection between
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\mathbb{C}
, we obtain a bijection from
\mathbb{C}
to its range. The following section focuses on this bijection.

3
The space of
A
-Linearly Correlated Fuzzy Numbers -
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}

For each
A\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
, we can define the operator
\Phi:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
that associates
each complex number
(r+iq)\in\mathbb{C}
with the fuzzy number
\Phi_{A}(r+iq)
given by

\Phi_{A}(r+iq)=r+qA,q,r\in\mathbb{R}.

(3)

For notational convenience, we use the symbol

\Phi
instead of
\Phi_{A}
.

As in
[
14
]
, it is easy to verify that
\Phi
is well defined since
\Psi
is defined in terms of the usual arithmetic operations. Moreover, one can easily verify that
[\Phi(r+iq)]_{\alpha}=\{r+qx\in\mathbb{R}\,|\,x\in[A]_{\alpha}\}
for all
\alpha\in[0,1].
Let us denote the range of the operator
\Phi
by the symbol
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
, that is,
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}=\{\Phi(r+iq)\mid(r+iq)\in\mathbb{C}\}
.

An important fact that is worth emphasizing is that
\mathbb{R}
can be embedded in
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
since every real number
r
can be identified with the fuzzy number
\Phi(r+i0)\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
, that is,
\mathbb{R}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
.
Another interesting property is that for every
B\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}\setminus\mathbb{R}
, we have
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(B)}=\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}

[
14
]
. In other words,
the range of
\Psi_{B}
does not depend on the choice of
B\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}\setminus\mathbb{R}
and is equal to
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Given
A\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
, consider the operator
\Phi:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
given by

\Phi(r+iq)=r+qA

(4)

for all
z=(r+iq)\in\mathbb{C}
.
The operator
\Phi
is a bijection if, and only if,
A
is asymmetric.

Proof.

It follows analogously from Theorem 3.6 of
[
14
]
.
∎

Informally and roughly speaking, the space
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is obtained from
\mathbb{C}
simply replacing the imaginary unit
i
by the asymmetric fuzzy number
A
. However, unlike the complex case, the quantity
r+qA
can be seen as a sum of fuzzy numbers
r
and
qA
that results in a fuzzy number
B=r+qA
.
Note that, in general, any fuzzy number
B
can be written as
b+B_{0}
, where
b
is a real number and
B_{0}(x-b)=B(x)
for all
x
. For example, the triangular fuzzy number
(0.5;2;5)
is equal to
2+3(-0.5;0;1)
. However, for a fixed fuzzy number
A
, only elements of
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
can be decomposed as a sum of a real part
r
and a fuzzy part
qA
.

For an asymmetric
A
, if the codomain of the operator
\Phi
is restricted to its range (
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
), then
\Phi
is a bijection from Theorem
3.1
.
Using this bijection, one can induce isometric isomorphism from the field
\mathbb{C}
to
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
. This is stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
A
be an asymmetric fuzzy number. The set
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is a vector space over the scalar field
\mathbb{R}
with the usual scalar product and the addition operator given by

\displaystyle B\oplus C=\Phi(\Phi^{-1}(B)+\Phi^{-1}(C))=(r+s)+(q+p)A

for all
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
,
B=(r+qA)\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
and
C=(s+pA)\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
.
In addition,
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is isometric to
\mathbb{C}
with the norm

\|B\|_{\Phi}=|\Phi^{-1}(B)|=\sqrt{r^{2}+q^{2}}.

(5)

Proof.

First of all, note that for every
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
and
B=(r+qA)\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
, we have that

\displaystyle\eta B

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta(r+qA)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\eta r)+(\eta q)A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi((\eta r)+(\eta q)i)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi(\eta(r+iq))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi(\eta\Phi^{-1}(B)).

This implies that the usual scalar product coincides with the scalar product induced by the bijection
\Phi
.
Therefore, the bijection
\Phi
becomes an isometric isomorphism from
\mathbb{C}
to
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
over the field
\mathbb{R}
since
all operations and the norm on
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
are induced by the bijection
\Phi
.
∎

Notice that if
r\in\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}\subset\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
, we have that
r\oplus B=r+B
, where “
+
" is the usual addition given by (
1
).
As usual for every vector space, for every
B,C\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
, we simply denote the product
(-1)B
by
-B
and
the difference of
C
and
B
is given by

C\ominus B=C\oplus(-B).

(6)

From now on, unless otherwise stated, we assume that the fuzzy number
A
that generates the space
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is asymmetric and, therefore,
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is a vector space with the operations given as in Theorem
3.2
.

Remark 1
.

Through the isomorphism
\Phi
, we can define the product and division operators between the fuzzy numbers
B=(r+qA)\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
and
C=(s+pA)\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
, as the set of complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
already possesses these operations
[
7
]
. More precisely, we have

\displaystyle B\odot C

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi(\Phi^{-1}(B)\cdot\Phi^{-1}(C))

(7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(rs-qp)+(sq+rp)A,

and

\displaystyle B\odiv C

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi(\Phi^{-1}(B)\div\Phi^{-1}(C))

(8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi\left(\frac{rs+qp}{s^{2}+p^{2}}+\frac{qs-rp}{s^{2}+p^{2}}i\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{rs+qp}{s^{2}+p^{2}}+\frac{qs-rp}{s^{2}+p^{2}}A,

provided that
C\neq 0
. For notational convenience, we also denote the quotient
B\odiv C
by the usual symbol
\frac{B}{C}
.

In analogy with the so-called complexification process (see
[
1
,
26
]
) and in view of Theorem
3.2
and of the fact that
\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
, the vector space
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
equipped with the product given by (
7
) could be called “fuzzyfication” of the vector space
\mathbb{R}
.

It is worth noting that the
\odot
multiplication operator extends the scalar product. More precisely, let
r\in\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
and
B=(s+pA)\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
, from (
7
), we have that

r\odot B=(r+0A)\odot(s+pA)=rs+rpA=r(s+pA)=rB.

Since every structure in
\mathbb{C}
can be carried to the space
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
through the isomorphism
\Phi
, we can establish a parallel between these two spaces.
In fact, in the same way that
\mathbb{C}
is a 2-dimension vector space over real field (
\mathbb{R}
) with
\{1,i\}
as the canonical basis, the vector space
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
over
\mathbb{R}
is
2
-dimension with
\{1,A\}
as a basis.
Recall that every field is a 1-dimension over itself.
Thus, the set of complex numbers is a
1
-dimension vector space over itself, that is, over the complex field (
\mathbb{C}
), and its canonical basis is
\{1\}
. Similarly, using the isomorphism between
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
, we also obtain that

\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is a field and, hence, it is an
1
-dimension vector space over itself (i.e. the field
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
) and, in this case,
\{1\}
is a basis for
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
.
This last result is stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.3
.

The space
(\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)},\oplus,\ominus,\odot,\odiv)
is field isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

It follows from the fact that all these operations on
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
are induced by the isomorphism
\Phi
.
Note that, from Equation (
8
), for every
C=(s+pA)\neq 0
, we have that

\frac{1}{C}=\frac{s}{s^{2}+p^{2}}+\frac{-p}{s^{2}+p^{2}}A.

∎

It is worth noting that
B\circledast C=\Phi(\Phi^{-1}(B)\ast\Phi^{-1}(C))
, where
\ast
is one of the arithmetic operations on the field
\mathbb{C}
.

3.1
Interpretations and Representations

In the context of uncertainty modeling, the set
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is composed of uncertain quantities given by fuzzy numbers that are linearly correlated with each other since each element is linearly correlated with
A

[
3
]
. Additionally, we can treat each element as fuzzy noise around the expected value “
r
." In statistical terms, “
r+qA
" is a typical additive uncertainty model where “
r
" is a population expected value, and “
qA
" is an individual variability term. In this sense, according to
[
9
]
, we can say that we are facing an epistemic approach. This approach aligns with the interpretation provided by Dubois.

On the other hand, due to the isomorphism between the set of complex numbers and the
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
space, with
A
being asymmetric, all interpretations and concepts of complex numbers can be transferred to the space
(\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)},\oplus,\odot,\|\cdot\|_{\Phi})
. As in
\mathbb{C}
(and here too), many concepts are purely abstract, such as the derivative at a point. It does not have a geometric meaning like a slope.

Multiplication in polar coordinates has an interesting representation. If
z_{1}=\|z_{1}\|_{\Phi}(\cos{\theta_{1}}+\sin{\theta_{1}}A)
and
z_{2}=\|z_{2}\|_{\Phi}(\cos{\theta_{2}}+\sin{\theta_{2}}A)

\sqrt[n]{z}=\sqrt[n]{r}(\cos{\frac{\theta}{n}}+\sin{\frac{\theta}{n}}A)
.
To illustrate, consider the fuzzy number

\sqrt{z}=\sqrt{2}(\cos{\frac{\pi}{6}}+\sin{\frac{\pi}{6}}A)=\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}A\right)=B.

and

, then
B=\sqrt{2}\left(-1+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2};\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2};2+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)
.

From the isomorphism
\Phi
, we have that the fuzzy number
A
in
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
plays a similar role to the imaginary unit (

) in
\mathbb{C}
. But, unlike the imaginary components of complex numbers that are not represented in the set of real numbers, the uncertain components, which are associated with the fuzzy number

,
are represented by fuzzy numbers on
\mathbb{R}
. Thus, an element

can be interpreted as a “fuzzyfication” of the deterministic/predictable value

.
But, unlike complex numbers, here there is no need to add an additional axis to graphically represent a component of an element of
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
.
This is the main difference in
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
.

3.2
Fuzzy Mappings in
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}

A fuzzy mapping
f
in
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is nothing more than a function from
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
to
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
.
Let
z=(x+y)A\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
, with
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
.
The bijection
\Phi
ensures that there exist unique functions
u,v:\bar{D}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

u(x,y)=\text{Re}(f(x+yA))\quad\text{ and }\quad v(x,y)=\text{Fu}(f(x+yA)),

(9)

for all
(x,y)\in\bar{D}=\Phi^{-1}(D)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Using the bijection
\Phi
, many of the important functions in
\mathbb{C}

[
7
]
can be defined similarly in
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
. More precisely, a mapping
f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
can be carried to the space
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
by taking
g=\Phi\circ f\circ\Phi^{-1}:\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}\to\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
.
For example, if
f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
is given by
f(r+iq)=e^{r+iq}=e^{r}\cos(q)+ie^{r}\sin(q)
(note that this last equality follows from the Euler’s formula in
\mathbb{C}
),
then
g=\Phi\circ f\circ\Phi^{-1}:\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}\to\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is given by

\displaystyle g(r+qA)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi\left(f(\Phi^{-1}(r+qA))\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi\left(f(r+iq)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi\left(e^{r}\cos(q)+ie^{r}\sin(q)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{r}\cos(q)+e^{r}\sin(q)A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{r}(\cos(q)+\sin(q)A).

Based on the expression in the last equality, Euler’s formula, and the fact that
\Phi
is an isomorphism, we define
e^{z}
, for any
z=r+qA
in
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
, as follows.

Definition 3.1
.

For every
z=(r+qA)\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
, the exponential of
z
is defined by the fuzzy number
e^{z}\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
given by

e^{z}=e^{r}(\cos(q)+\sin(q)A).

(10)

Equation (
10
) is called Euler’s formula in
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
and the mapping
z\mapsto e^{z}
is called
the
exponential function
in
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
.

Some properties of exponential function follow directly from Euler’s formula in
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}:

i.

e^{z_{1}}e^{z_{2}}=e^{z_{1}+z_{2}},\forall z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)},

ii.

(e^{z})^{n}=e^{nz},\forall z\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)},n\in\mathbb{Z}.

Example 1
.

For
z=4+3A
, by Definition
3.1
, the exponential of
z
is given by

e^{z}=e^{4+3A}=e^{4}(\cos(3)+\sin(3)A)=e^{4}\cos(3)+e^{4}\sin(3)A.

In a similar way to the exponential function, other fundamental mappings from
\mathbb{C}
to
\mathbb{C}
can also be defined in
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
as follows:

Logarithmic function:

For every
z=x+yA\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
, the logarithmic function is given by
\ln(z)=\ln{\|z\|_{\Phi}}+A(\theta+2n\pi)
, where
n=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots
,
z\neq 0
,
{\|z\|_{\Phi}}
is the modulus of
z
and
\theta=\text{arg}(z)
is the argument (angle) of
z
with the
x
axis.

Power function:

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}
. For every
z\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
,
z\neq 0
, we define
z^{a}=\exp{(a\ln(z))}
.

Polynomial function:

Given
a_{0},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
. For every
z\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
, we define the polynomial function of
n
degree as
f(z)=a_{0}\oplus a_{1}\odot z\oplus a_{2}\odot z^{2}\oplus\ldots\oplus a_{n}\odot z^{n}
.

Example 2
.

Consider
z=x+yA\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
.
The real and fuzzy part of the function
f(z)=exp(z^{2}\oplus z)
are given by
\text{Re}(f(z))=u(x,y)=exp(x^{2}-y^{2}+x)\cos{(2xy+y)}
and
\text{Fu}(f(z))=v(x,y)=exp(x^{2}-y^{2}+x)\sin{(2xy+y)}
.

Remark 2
.

For a given
z=r+qA\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
, the symbol
e^{z}
refers to the fuzzy number in
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
as defined by Eq. (
10
), which does not coincide with the Zadeh extension of the exponential function
e^{x}
at
z
. In general, the Zadeh extension of the exponential function
e^{x}
applied to a non-crisp element of
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
results in a fuzzy number that does not belong to
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
.
This observation also applies to other functions, such as logarithmic, power, polynomial, etc, which do not coincide with their Zadeh extension. This outcome is expected because
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is a Banach space with interactive arithmetic operations, as induced by the isomorphism
\Phi

[
3
]
.
In contrast, the Zadeh extension principle for arithmetic operations leads to the usual or the non-interactive fuzzy arithmetic
[
8
]
.

4
Calculus for Fuzzy Mapping in
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}

Once again, such concepts can be “transferred" from the space of complex numbers to the
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
space via the
\Phi
operator. Note that
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is now equipped with product and quotient operations between its elements, so it makes sense to discuss the limit, continuity, differentiability, and integrability of functions in
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
.

4.1
Limit and continuity

Definition 4.1
.

Let
f:D\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}\to\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
and
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
. We say that there is the limit of
f
in
z_{0}
if there is
L\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
such that for each
\epsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that

z\in D,0<\|z\ominus z_{0}\|_{\Phi}<\delta\Rightarrow\|f(z)\ominus L\|_{\Phi}<\epsilon.

(11)

In this case, we denote it by

\lim_{z\to z_{0}}{f(z)}=L.

(12)

Since
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}
, whenever it exists, the limit is unique.
Moreover, it is worth noting that the limit is independent of the way that
z
approaches
z_{0}
in the fuzzy plane (
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
).

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
f,g:D\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}\to\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
be functions such that
\displaystyle\lim_{z\to z_{0}}{f(z)}
and
\displaystyle\lim_{z\to z_{0}}{g(z)}
exist. For every
a\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
, we have

i)

\displaystyle\lim_{z\to z_{0}}{[a\odot f(z)\oplus g(z)]}=\left(a\odot\lim_{z\to z_{0}}{f(z)}\right)\oplus\lim_{z\to z_{0}}{g(z)}
;

ii)

\displaystyle\lim_{z\to z_{0}}{[f(z)\odot g(z)]}=\lim_{z\to z_{0}}{f(z)}\odot\lim_{z\to z_{0}}{g(z)}
;

iii)

\displaystyle\lim_{z\to z_{0}}{[f(z)\odiv g(z)]}=\lim_{z\to z_{0}}{f(z)}\odiv\lim_{z\to z_{0}}{g(z)}
if
\displaystyle\lim_{z\to z_{0}}{g(z)}\neq 0
.

Proof.

From isomorphism,
\Phi
, the proof for the
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
space remains analogous to the case in the complex space
\mathbb{C}

[
7
]
.
∎

Definition 4.2
.

Let
f:D\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}\to\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
and
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
. We say that
f
is continuous in
z_{0}
if

\lim_{z\to z_{0}}{f(z)}=f(z_{0}).

(13)

We say that
f
is continuous in
D
if
f
is continuous at all points of
D
.

Note that the notion continuity can alternatively be defined in terms of open sets since
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is also a topology space induced by the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\Phi}
. Recall that a subset of
Y
of
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is said to be open if for every
B\in Y
there exists
\epsilon>0
such that for all
C\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
satisfying
\|B-C\|_{\Phi}<\epsilon
implies that
C\in Y

[
28
]
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
f:D\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}\to\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
,
u(x,y)=\text{Re}(f(z))
and
v(x,y)=\text{Fu}(f(z))
and
z_{0}=x_{0}+y_{0}A\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
,
x_{0},y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. For the limit of
f
to exist at
z_{0}
, it is necessary and sufficient that there are limits
of
u
and
v
in
(x_{0},y_{0})
. Then, we have

\lim_{z\to z_{0}}{f(z)}=\lim_{(x,y)\to(x_{0},y_{0})}{u(x,y)}+\lim_{(x,y)\to(x_{0},y_{0})}{v(x,y)}\,A.

(14)

Proof.

Immediate consequence of this property in
\mathbb{C}
and the isomorphism
\Phi
.∎

4.2
Derivatives

Definition 4.3
.

Let
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
be an open set,
f:D\to\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
and
z_{0}\in D
. The derivative of
f
at
z_{0}
is the limit

f^{\prime}(z_{0})=\lim_{z\to z_{0}}{\frac{f(z)\ominus f(z_{0})}{z\ominus z_{0}}}

(15)

when it exists. Moreover, the derivative
f^{\prime}(z_{0})
is also denoted by
\frac{df}{dz}(z_{0})

The basic formulas used to find the derivatives below can be obtained through the complex case
[
7
]
and the isomorphism
\Phi
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
be open,
z_{0}=x_{0}+y_{0}A\in D
,
f,g:D\to\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
, and
a\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
. If
f
and
g
are differentiable in
z_{0}
then

i)

\displaystyle(a\odot f\oplus g)^{\prime}(z_{0})=a\odot f^{\prime}(z_{0})\oplus g^{\prime}(z_{0})
;

ii)

\displaystyle(f\odot g)^{\prime}(z_{0})=f(z_{0})\odot g^{\prime}(z_{0})\oplus f^{\prime}(z_{0})\odot g(z_{0})
;

iii)

\displaystyle(f\odiv g)^{\prime}(z_{0})=\left(f^{\prime}(z_{0})\odot g(z_{0})\oplus f(z_{0})\odot g^{\prime}(z_{0})\right)\odiv g(z_{0})^{2}
, when
g(z_{0})\neq 0
.

Proof.

It follows from the properties of complex calculus and from the isomorphism
\Phi
.
∎

Example 3
.

Consider

Proposition 4.4
.

Let

\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x_{0},y_{0})=\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}(x_{0},y_{0})\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}(x_{0},y_{0})=-\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}(x_{0},y_{0}).

(16)

Furthermore,

f^{\prime}(z_{0})=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x_{0},y_{0})+\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}(x_{0},y_{0})A=\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}(x_{0},y_{0})-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}(x_{0},y_{0})A.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
be open,
z_{0}=x_{0}+y_{0}A\in D
,
x_{0},y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, and

a
function such that,
u=u(x,y)=\text{Re}f(z)
and

have continuous first-order partial derivatives in
(x_{0},y_{0})
. If
u
and
v
satisfy the Cauchy-Riemann equations (
16

f^{\prime}(z_{0})
and it is given by (
17
).

Example 4
.

Consider

and

\displaystyle f(z)=e^{-z}=e^{-x}\left(\cos{y}-\sin{y}A\right).

(18)

We can rewrite

\displaystyle u(x,y)=e^{-x}\cos{y}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad v(x,y)=-e^{-x}\sin{y}

Remark 3

Calculating this derivative does not involve the analysis of

(20)

4.3
Antiderivative Function

The following results are adapted from the complex case
[
7
]
.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
f:\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}\to\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
. We say that
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is an antiderivative (primitive) of
f
if
F^{\prime}(z)=f(z)
for all
z\in\Omega
.

In this case, it is common to denote
F
by
\int{f(z)dz}
.

Theorem 4.2
.

If
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is a simply connected set and
f
is an analytical function then, fixed
z_{0}\in\Omega
there exists
C\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
such that the general form of a primitive
f
is given by

F(z)=\int_{z_{0}}^{z}{f(\zeta)d\zeta+C},\quad z\in\Omega.

(21)

Given
z_{0}
, formula (
21
) indicates that the integral of
f
is independent of the path that connects
z_{0}
to
z
.

From Theorem
4.2
, it immediately follows that

\int_{z_{1}}^{z_{2}}{f(z)dz}=F(z_{2})\ominus F(z_{1}).

(22)

Example 6
.

Consider
f:\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}\to\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
. The continuous function
f(z)=z^{2}
has an antiderivative
F(z)=\displaystyle\frac{z^{3}}{3}
throughout the plane. Thus

\int_{0}^{1+A}{z^{2}\,dz}=\frac{z^{3}}{3}\bigg]_{0}^{1+A}=\frac{1}{3}(1+A)^{3}=-\frac{2}{3}+\frac{2}{3}A.

(23)

5
Fuzzy curves and ordinary differential equations in
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}

A fuzzy curve in
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is a fuzzy-number-valued mapping
w:I\subseteq\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
given by

w(t)=x(t)+y(t)A,\quad t\in I\subseteq\mathbb{R}.

(24)

Since addition and multiplication are defined in
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
, we can define the pointwise sum and product of fuzzy curves as follows:

\displaystyle(w_{1}\oplus w_{2})(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w_{1}(t)\oplus w_{2}(t)\quad\text{and}

(25)

\displaystyle(w_{1}\odot w_{2})(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w_{1}(t)\odot w_{2}(t)\quad\forall t\in I.

5.1
Derivatives and Integrals of the fuzzy curve

The derivative of the function (
24
) at a point
t_{0}
is defined as

\frac{dw}{dt}\bigg|_{t=t_{0}}=\lim_{h\to 0}{\frac{w(t_{0}+h)\ominus w(t_{0})}{h}}.

(26)

or, equivalently, as

\frac{dw}{dt}\bigg|_{t=t_{0}}=w^{\prime}(t_{0})=x^{\prime}(t_{0})+y^{\prime}(t_{0})A,\quad t_{0}\in I,

(27)

provided the existence of the derivatives
x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
at
t_{0}

[
14
]
.

The linearity of the derivative operator (
26
) with respect to the addition
\oplus
and the scalar product in the real field follows immediately from the results of
[
14
]
combined to the standard bijection between
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

In what follows we state some properties with respect to the multiplication
\odot
given as in Equation (
7
).

Theorem 5.1

(Chain rule)
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
be an open set,
w:(a,b)\to\Omega
, and
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
.
If the fuzzy curve
w
is differentiable at
t_{0}\in(a,b)

and
f
is differentiable at
w(t_{0})
, then
the fuzzy curve
\gamma=f\circ w:(a,b)\to\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is differentiable at
t_{0}
with

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime}(t_{0})=f^{\prime}(w(t_{0}))\odot w^{\prime}(t_{0}).

(28)

Proof.

Let
x,y:(a,b)\to\mathbb{R}
and
u,v:D\to\mathbb{R}
such that

w(t)=x(t)+y(t)A
and
f(p+qA)=u(p,q)+v(p,q)A
, with
D=\Phi^{-1}(\Omega)
.
Note that, from the bijectivity of
\Phi
, these functions are unique.

On the one hand, we have

\displaystyle\gamma(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(w(t))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(x(t)+y(t)A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u(x(t),y(t))+v(x(t),y(t))A.

Using (
27
) and chain rule for real functions, we obtain

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime}(t_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x(t_{0}),y(t_{0}))x^{\prime}(t_{0})+\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}(x(t_{0}),y(t_{0}))y^{\prime}(t_{0})\right)+

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}(x(t_{0}),y(t_{0}))x^{\prime}(t_{0})+\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}(x(t_{0}),y(t_{0}))y^{\prime}(t_{0})\right)A.

This last equality proves that
\gamma
is differentiable at
t_{0}
since every derivative and partial derivative and right side exist.

On the other hand, we have that
w^{\prime}(t_{0})=x^{\prime}(t_{0})+y^{\prime}(t_{0})A
and, from Proposition
4.4
, we have

f^{\prime}(x(t_{0})+y(t_{0})A)=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x(t_{0}),y(t_{0}))+\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}(x(t_{0}),y(t_{0}))A,

and the Cauchy-Riemman equations is satisfied at
x(t_{0})+y(t_{0})A
.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(w(t_{0}))\odot w^{\prime}(t_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x(t_{0}),y(t_{0}))+\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}(x(t_{0}),y(t_{0}))A\right)\odot\left(x^{\prime}(t_{0})+y^{\prime}(t_{0})A\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x(t_{0}),y(t_{0}))x^{\prime}(t_{0})-\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}(x(t_{0}),y(t_{0}))y^{\prime}(t_{0})\right)+

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}(x(t_{0}),y(t_{0}))x^{\prime}(t_{0})+\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x(t_{0}),y(t_{0}))y^{\prime}(t_{0})\right)A.

From the Cauchy-Riemann conditions, it follows that

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(w(t_{0}))\odot w^{\prime}(t_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x(t_{0}),y(t_{0}))x^{\prime}(t_{0})+\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}(x(t_{0}),y(t_{0}))y^{\prime}(t_{0})\right)+

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}(x(t_{0}),y(t_{0}))x^{\prime}(t_{0})+\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}(x(t_{0}),y(t_{0}))y^{\prime}(t_{0})\right)A.

Therefore, we conclude that (
28
) holds true.

Linear Ordinary Differential Equations

Consider the linear differential equation given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl }\frac{d}{dt}w(t)&=&\lambda\odot w(t)\\

(31)

By computing its derivative, one can verify that the curve

In what follows, we present some considerations about the fuzzy function

Consider

a.

If
\lambda_{1}\neq 0

b.

If
\lambda_{1}=0

c.

If

\lambda_{2}

Alternatively, assuming

\cos{(\lambda_{2}t)}\end{array}\right).

Therefore, the

and
y(t)

Given the equations

\displaystyle w(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x_{0}+y_{0}A)\odot e^{(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}A)t}=w_{0}\odot e^{\lambda t}.

6
Application to Two-dimensional System of Fuzzy Differential Equations

This section presents an initial study on two-dimensional systems of fuzzy differential equations whose main goal is to show some important aspects of the calculus developed in this article. The first one is the chain rule for functions with fuzzy inputs, which involves the product operation between fuzzy numbers. The other one is the division between fuzzy numbers presented in this article.
To this end, we will investigate the dynamics of two-dimensional models through their phase portrait.

Consider the following autonomous system of fuzzy differential equations in terms of the deterministic independent variable
t

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\frac{dx}{dt}&=&g(x(t),y(t))\\
\frac{dy}{dt}&=&h(x(t),y(t))\end{array},\right.

where
g
and
h

\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}^{2}

\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}

[

[

20

and the references therein.

A qualitative study on the system given in Eq. (

In fact, in systems of two-dimensional differential equations with state variables
x
and
y

x(t)
and
y(t)
of the system of equations in question, omitting the independent variable
t

Thus, it is very common to represent the curves of the solutions in the phase space under the form
y=f(x)

\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{h(x,y)}{g(x,y)}.

Note that the meaning of
\frac{dy}{dx}
is given in Definition
4.3

y

\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
to
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}

\frac{h(x,y)}{g(x,y)}

8

In the following subsection, we study a classical and important model in Ecology called Lokta-Volterra which plays a central role in prey-predator systems
[
5
,
11
]
.

6.1
The Fuzzy Lotka-Volterra Model

The Lotka-Volterra equation is a well-known model in Ecology that is used to describe the dynamic of a predator-prey system.
Here, we consider the generalization of this model by allowing uncertain parameters and variables to take values in the space
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}x^{\prime}&=\alpha\odot x\ominus a\odot x\odot y,\\
y^{\prime}&=-\beta\odot y\oplus b\odot x\odot y,\\
x(0)&=x_{0},\\
y(0)&=y_{0},\end{array}\right.

(47)

where
x(t)=r(t)+p(t)A\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
and
y(t)=s(t)+q(t)A\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
correspond respectively to the population of the prey and predator for
t\in[0,T]
,
\alpha=r_{\alpha}+q_{\alpha}A\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is the fuzzy growth rate of the prey,
\beta=r_{\beta}+q_{\beta}A\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is the fuzzy death rate of the predator,
a=r_{a}+q_{a}A\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is the predation rate per predator in a unit of time,
\beta=r_{b}+q_{b}A\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is the fuzzy growth rate of predator caused by the presence of prey in a unit of time,
x_{0}=r_{0}+p_{0}A
and
y_{0}=s_{0}+q_{0}A
are the fuzzy initial conditions.

Using the fact that every element of
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}\setminus\{0\}
has multiplicative inverse with respect the multiplication
\odot
, we have that equilibrium points of the system are

P_{1}=(0,0)\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}^{2}
and
P_{2}=\left(\frac{\beta}{b},\frac{\alpha}{a}\right)\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}^{2}
, since

x^{\prime}=x\odot(\alpha\ominus a\odot y)=0\Leftrightarrow x=0\mbox{ or }y=\frac{\alpha}{a},

and

y^{\prime}=y\odot(-\beta\oplus b\odot x)=0\Leftrightarrow y=0\mbox{ or }x=\frac{\beta}{b}.

The case of interest is the non trivial equilibrium point
P_{2}
which involves divisions of elements of
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
.

It is worth noting that, in the classical case, the Lokta-Volterra model can be simplified by considering its linearized version around the non trivial equilibrium point.
This simplification provides an easier system of equations such that its solution in the phase portrait is given by a convenient formula of an ellipse.
So, if we consider fuzzy version of its linearization around the non trivial equilibrium point, then we obtain the following system:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}u^{\prime}&=-\frac{a\odot\beta}{b}\odot v,\\
v^{\prime}&=\frac{b\odot\alpha}{a}\odot u.\end{array}\right..

(48)

The analysis of the phase portrait of this equation is similar to that done for Equation (
45
).
In fact, Eq. (
48
) corresponds to Eq. (
45
) if

1=\frac{a\odot\beta}{b}
and
1=\frac{b\odot\alpha}{a}
.
So, we do not delve deeper into this fuzzy linearized system.

Suppose that initial conditions of Eq. (
47
) are not in equilibrium points. In this case, as we shall see in the phase portrait, the solutions have periodic behavior around to the non-trivial equilibrium point
P_{2}=\left(\frac{\beta}{b},\frac{\alpha}{a}\right)
of Eq. (
47
).
To analyze the phase portrait, we consider the following equation that follows by applying the rule chain and Eq. (
8
):

\displaystyle y^{\prime}=\frac{dy}{dx}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\beta\odot y\oplus b\odot x\odot y}{\alpha\odot x\ominus a\odot x\odot y}=\frac{y\odot\left(-\beta\oplus b\odot x\right)}{x\odot\left(\alpha\ominus a\odot y\right)}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\left(\frac{\alpha\ominus a\odot y}{y}\right)\odot y^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-\beta\oplus b\odot x}{x},

by integrating with respect to
x

\alpha\odot\mathrm{ln}y\ominus a\odot y=-\beta\odot\mathrm{ln}x\oplus b\odot x\oplus k,

(49)

for some

chain rule given in Proposition
4.5

The parameter

where
x(0)=x_{0}
and
y(0)=y_{0}
are the the initial conditions.
Although this system more sophisticated than that of Eq. (

7
Conclusions

This article presents a fuzzy calculus theory for functions that map fuzzy numbers to fuzzy numbers. One of the main benefits of this approach is that it allows for modeling phenomena where both inputs and outputs are uncertain. It is worth noting that, in fuzzy differential equations, the inputs of a direction field represent the state of the solution and, therefore, an uncertain quantity.

Although a broader class of fuzzy sets can be considered, this study focuses on the space
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
that is composed of all fuzzy numbers that are linearly correlated with an asymmetric fuzzy number
A
(i.e.,
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}=\{r+qA\mid(r,q)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\}
. This space has two important mathematical characteristics: 1) it is a Banach space, which is an ideal framework for developing a theory of differential and integral calculus and, at the same time, 2) it allows us to model phenomena such that uncertainty (or noise) cannot be disregarded in the task of obtaining an adequate solution for the problem at hand.

Since each
B\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
can be written as
B=r+qA
, in analogy to the language used in statistics (or even in stochastic calculus),
B
could be understood as the “composition” of the real part “
r
” and the uncertain part “
qA
”. In this context, the elements of
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
could be interpreted from an epistemic point of view.
On the other hand, from a more technical point of view, such a representation of
B
also resembles the representation of a complex number, where
A
plays the role of an imaginary unit “
i
”.

Thus, the proposed approach is to associate the space
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
with the set of complex numbers through the bijection
\Phi
, thereby equipping
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
with the structure of a vector space of complex numbers,
\mathbb{C}
. Consequently, other concepts such as differentiability and integrability are also inherited from
\mathbb{C}
. Additionally, from a practical standpoint, this approach enables the application of conformal maps in the fuzzy domain.

One of the advantages of the proposed approach is to equip
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
with the multiplication
\odot
which is induced by the multiplication of complex numbers. Unlike what happens with the
\Psi
-cross product, every element of
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
that is different from zero has a reciprocal, that is, a multiplicative inverse. Such a property is essential to define the derivative of a function
f:\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}\to\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
at some
T\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
in terms of the limit of
H^{-1}\odot(f(T+H)\ominus f(T))
as
H\to 0
, where
H^{-1}
stands for the reciprocal of
H
with respect to the
H
, i.e,
H\odot H^{-1}=1
.

The vector space
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
(and the vector space
\mathbb{C}
) over the real field (
\mathbb{R}
) has 2 dimensions, and
\{1,A\}
(and
\{1,i\}
) is a basis. However, the set of complex numbers is a vector space over the complex field (
\mathbb{C}
) that has 1 dimension, with its canonical basis being
\{1\}
.
Using the isomorphism
\Phi
between
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
, we show in Theorem
3.3
that
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is a field and, hence, it is a
1
-dimension vector space over the filed
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
with
\{1\}
as a basis.
Note that in this last case, the multiplication operation on
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is defined as in Equation (
7
).
In contrast, the notion of multiplication is not unique if we regard
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
as vector space over
\mathbb{R}
and, therefore, we need to indicate which one is being used.
Depending on the multiplication adopted, the
\Psi
-cross product or that induced by complex numbers, the solutions are qualitatively different.
For instance, in the linear model with the
\Psi
-cross product, the uncertainty (or the noise) in the growth rate does not affect the dynamic of the real part
x(t)
(see Equation (
41
)), whilst, with the multiplication
\odot
given in Equation (
7
), the uncertainty in this parameter affects both the real and fuzzy parts of the solution, as can be seen in Equation (
35
).
In addition, if we regard
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
as a
1
-dimension vector space over itself, we can consider differential equations whose solutions are fuzzy mappings, which is the case of the
solution (
43
).

We would like to present some brief comments comparing our proposal with the theory of fuzzy calculus based on the concept of generalized Hukuhara derivative or, for short,
gH
-derivative.
On the one hand, at first glance, the notion of the generalized Hukuhara derivative seems to be more general, as it is not restricted to special classes of fuzzy numbers
[
6
]
.
However, to use it (mainly when solving fuzzy differential equations), firstly, we need to identify
a priori
the switching points of the function, that is, the points where there is a change in the behavior of the widths of the
\alpha
-levels. Secondly, since its calculation is done in the endpoints of
\alpha
-levels, it is necessary to check whether the conditions of Stacking Theorem
[
6
]
are satisfied to ensure that the derivative is well-defined at a given point. In general, checking such conditions is a very hard task in practice. Therefore, although there is no restriction in the codomain of the functions, its use could be restrictive due to the comments above.
On the other hand, our approach to fuzzy calculus can be applied to functions taking values in subclasses of fuzzy numbers. These subclasses are subsets of fuzzy numbers whose elements are autocorrelated. Among them, we could highlight those based on linear (or complete) correlation
[
8
]
, for which the space
\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
is included
[
3
]
.
Under certain conditions, these spaces have the structure of Banach spaces and, therefore, they are closed with respect to the addition, subtraction, and scalar product. This fact eliminates the need to examine the requirements mentioned above when using the
gH
-derivative. Indeed, the theory of differential and integral calculus in Banach space is already well-established. Finally, our approach has explored the Lotka-Volterra model from the perspective of fuzzification of
\mathbb{R}
.
This involves treating

\mathbb{R}_{\mathcal{F}(A)}
as a field over itself and has included a brief analysis of the solution’s phase portrait, facilitated by the chain rule applicable to functions with fuzzy arguments. As a future perspective, this introductory study can be extended for higher dimension of fuzzy differential equation systems and analysis of stability.
