The role of autoimmune gastritis in gastric cancer risk: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xinyi Liu, Jun Xu, Yun Wu, Xiaolei Zhao, Shiwei Wang, Lin Su, Shan Cao, Ning Chen, Yulan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

This process leads to mucosal atrophy, which is a key precursor lesion in the established Correa cascade of gastric carcinogenesis. The primary purpose of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to evaluate the precise relationship between AIG and the risk of gastric cancer.MethodsWe performed a comprehensive search of relevant databases and included studies that assessed GC risk in patients diagnosed with AIG. The data were synthesized using random-effects models to calculate pooled hazard ratios (HRs). Studies were stratified based on H. pylori infection status and histological confirmation of AIG. Risk of bias was evaluated using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool.ResultsThe meta-analysis included 8 studies with a total of 10 cohorts. Background

Autoimmune gastritis (AIG) is a chronic, progressive condition characterized by the immune system’s attack on the gastric parietal cells, leading to mucosal atrophy, particularly in the corpus and fundus of the stomach [1, 2]. The clinical presentation of AIG is often subtle and may include symptoms of dyspepsia, iron deficiency anemia, or pernicious anemia (PA). Over time, the progressive destruction of parietal cells can result in malabsorption of critical nutrients, including vitamin B12 and iron [2–4]. Although AIG can lead to significant complications, it is frequently underdiagnosed due to its often asymptomatic early stages and the lack of proactive screening [5–7].

AIG has long been considered a precursor to gastric cancer, primarily through its association with atrophic gastritis, a condition widely regarded as a precursor lesion in the Correa cascade, the canonical pathway of gastric carcinogenesis [2, 8]. In this cascade, chronic gastritis progresses through stages of mucosal atrophy, intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, and eventually carcinoma [8]. The atrophy in AIG predominantly affects the oxyntic mucosa, where parietal cells are targeted by autoimmune mechanisms. This can lead to significant alterations in the gastric mucosa, including the development of intestinal metaplasia, a well-known precancerous change [2, 9]. The potential for gastric cancer development in AIG patients underscores the importance of early diagnosis and regular surveillance.

Studies have suggested an association between AIG and an increased risk of gastric cancer, particularly in advanced stages of AIG, especially when accompanied by PA and intestinal metaplasia [10, 11]. However, recent evidence has challenged this association. Some studies argue that the presence of intestinal metaplasia in AIG patients, particularly the complete or pseudo-pyloric type, may be associated with a lower cancer risk compared to the incomplete type commonly found in H. pylori-related atrophic gastritis [12–15]. A recent study showed that AIG patients, particularly those with early-stage atrophy and enterochromaffin-like (ECL) -hyperplasia, exhibit lower levels of genomic damage markers (such as 8-OHdG) compared to multifocal atrophic gastritis patients, suggesting a reduced cancer risk in AIG [16]. Despite these findings, the relationship between AIG and gastric cancer remains debated, with some studies indicating a higher risk in specific subgroups, while others suggest a more modest risk compared to H. pylori-associated conditions. Additionally, some research has found no significant association between AIG and gastric cancer. In contrast, H. pylori infection has been consistently shown to have a strong positive correlation with gastric cancer development [17–19]. However, the existing studies exploring the relationship between AIG and gastric cancer often lack proper stratification for H. pylori infection, making it difficult to clearly delineate the independent role of AIG in cancer risk.

This study aims to systematically review and meta-analyze existing research to assess the relationship between AIG and the risk of gastric cancer. By synthesizing available data, this analysis seeks to provide clearer insights into whether AIG should be considered a significant risk factor for gastric cancer, and if so, under which conditions.

Subjects and methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

This meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Statement. Relevant studies were selected through searches of PubMed, Embase, Cochrane, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) using MeSH terms including “risk” and “Stomach Neoplasms.” The complete search strategy for PubMed was as follows: (Risk [Mesh] OR Risks OR Relative Risk OR Relative Risks OR Risk, Relative OR Risks, Relative) AND (AIG OR Gastritis, Atrophic) AND (Autoimmune Diseases) AND (Stomach Neoplasm OR stomach cancer OR Neoplasm, Stomach OR Gastric Neoplasms OR Gastric Neoplasm OR Neoplasm, Gastric OR Neoplasms, Gastric OR Neoplasms, Stomach OR Cancer of Stomach OR Stomach Cancers OR Cancer of the Stomach OR Gastric Cancer OR Cancer, Gastric OR Cancers, Gastric OR Gastric Cancers OR Stomach Cancer OR Cancers, Stomach OR Cancer, Stomach OR Gastric Cancer, Familial Diffuse). All terms were searched in the Title/Abstract fields, and all potentially eligible studies were considered, regardless of primary outcome or language. We also conducted a manual search using references of major articles published in English.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were considered eligible for inclusion in this meta-analysis if they met the following criteria: (1) Study Design: Original cohort studies or case-control studies. (2) Outcome: Reported a risk estimate for gastric cancer development in patients with AIG, quantified as a Hazard Ratio (HR), Standardized Incidence Ratio (SIR), or Relative Risk (RR), along with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). (3) Diagnosis of AIG (Crucial Quality Filter): Defined AIG based on rigorous diagnostic criteria, requiring a combination of evidence, including histological findings (oxyntic atrophy and/or intestinal metaplasia predominantly in the corpus/fundus) and/or serological evidence (positive parietal cell antibodies, intrinsic factor antibodies, and/or reduced serum Pepsinogen I/II ratio).

Studies were excluded if they: (1) Were review articles, case reports, editorials, letters, animal studies, or in vitro studies. (2) Lacked a clear definition of AIG or only diagnosed AIG based solely on PA without concurrent histological or serological confirmation. These studies were excluded because PA represents a late-stage, symptomatic manifestation, and relying solely on it is a known source of bias that tends to inflate risk estimates in historical cohorts. (3) Did not provide sufficient data to calculate the pooled risk estimates.

Data extraction

Reference lists of retrieved articles were also reviewed for additional studies not identified by the database search strategy. Two investigators (X.Y.L. and J.X.) independently reviewed the articles for relevance and availability of basic information, with any disagreements resolved by a third reviewer (Y.W.). After screening the titles and abstracts, the full text of selected articles was obtained for data extraction.

Studies included in the final analysis were those that reported case counts or risk estimates such as SIR, HR, RR, and odds ratio. The following data were extracted from each study: first author, publication year, study design (cohort, case-control, cross-sectional, or registry-linkage), country, effect type (SIR, HR, RR, OR, incidence rate), effect value, 95% CI (Lower CI, Upper CI), adjusted variables, statistical model used, follow-up duration (in years), baseline age (mean and standard deviation), percentage of females, autoimmune comorbidities (such as thyroiditis, type 1 diabetes mellitus, vitiligo, or others), outcome definition (incidence of gastric adenocarcinoma), median time from AIG to cancer, cancer site (cardia, non-cardia, or unspecified), and number of deaths due to gastric cancer.

Statistical analysis

Summary estimates preferentially included the associations for the total population in a study, rather than for specific subgroups (e.g., combining males and females rather than analyzing each sex separately, and including overall gastric cancer rather than anatomical subsites). If no overall value was presented, associations for subgroups (e.g., males and females) were treated as separate studies. Standardized ratios or adjusted rates were used when available, but if only observed and expected case counts were provided, associations were derived based on the study design. Random-effects regression models were used to meta-analyze the summary risk estimates as RR with corresponding 95% CIs. Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and Higgins’s I² statistics. Publication bias was evaluated using Begg’s funnel plots and Egger’s tests. Funnel plot asymmetry was examined using the trim and fill method of Duval and Tweedie. To explore sources of heterogeneity, meta-regressions were conducted according to study design (case-control, retrospective cohort, or prospective cohort), geographic area (Europe, non-Europe) and sex (male or female).

Statistical analyses were performed using RevMan software (Cochrane, version 5.4). All statistical tests were two-sided, and p-values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

The process of identifying relevant studies for this systematic review and meta-analysis began with an initial search of 258 records from databases. After removing duplicates, 242 records were screened for eligibility. Following the title and abstract screening, 157 records were excluded for various reasons, including being classified as systematic reviews (n = 43), case reports (n = 16), or deemed irrelevant at the title and abstract level (n = 97).

Subsequently, 85 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. From these, 77 articles were excluded for the following reasons: irrelevant to the research question (n = 37), no available data (n = 14), and inappropriate outcome measures (n = 26). As a result, 8 studies were ultimately included in the quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis), contributing to the final analysis of gastric cancer risk in patients with AIG (Fig. 1). However, two of these studies (Song et al. and De Re et al.) included two separate cohorts, yielding a total of 10 independent cohorts included in the final synthesis. This systematic approach ensures that only the most relevant and high-quality studies were included in the final analysis, providing reliable insights into the relationship between AIG and the risk of gastric cancer. The studies were published between 23 September 2019 and 4 December 2025 (Table 1).

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed for all nine study datasets using the Cochrane risk-of‐bias domains. Overall, the methodological quality was moderate (Fig. 3). Most datasets were judged at low risk of bias for random sequence generation and allocation concealment, indicating that participant selection within cohorts or nested case–control sets was generally appropriate. Blinding of outcome assessment and completeness of outcome data were also predominantly rated as low risk, suggesting that gastric cancer outcomes were ascertained in a consistent manner and losses to follow-up were limited or adequately accounted for. In contrast, blinding of participants and personnel was frequently rated as unclear, which is expected given the observational design of these studies. Selective reporting was mostly low to unclear, reflecting that prespecified analysis plans were not always available. The domain of “other bias” showed the greatest concern, with several studies judged at high risk because of potential residual confounding, imbalance in baseline characteristics, or possible misclassification of AIG and H. pylori status. Taken together, these patterns indicate that while the included studies provide reasonably robust estimates, the predominance of observational designs and remaining unmeasured confounding must be considered when interpreting the pooled results.

Heterogeneity analysis

The overall I² value was 77%, which indicates substantial heterogeneity across the studies included in this meta-analysis. The Chi-squared test for heterogeneity was significant (p < 0.0001), further confirming the presence of substantial variability in the results.

First, there is considerable variation in study design types. Prospective cohort studies (e.g., Esposito 2021, Miceli 2019) minimize recall bias but impose strict inclusion criteria, whereas retrospective cohort studies (e.g., Kang 2023,) are more susceptible to selection bias due to reliance on historical data retrieval. Additionally, nested case-control studies (e.g., Song 2021, Butt 2022, Jayasekara 2025) use ORs, which are not directly comparable to HRs from cohort studies, thus distorting the pooled effect sizes. Second, differences in follow-up duration and outcome ascertainment further contribute to heterogeneity. The follow-up periods range from 1.29 years (De Re 2023) to 25 years (Jayasekara 2025), with shorter follow-up periods potentially underestimating long-term neoplasm risk. Third, the diagnostic criteria for AIG are inconsistent across studies. Some studies rely solely on histological criteria (Miceli 2019), others on serology such as anti-parietal cell antibodies (e.g., Song 2021, Jayasekara 2025), and some use combined diagnostic approaches (Guo 2023), which may lead to exposure misclassification. Furthermore, the handling of Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) — whether exclusion or inclusion — further modifies the risk estimates.

These variations in study design and methodology collectively undermine the consistency of effect sizes across studies, highlighting the need for subgroup analyses by design type and baseline characteristics.

Sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis employed a “leave-one-out” strategy to evaluate the robustness of the pooled results and determine the influence of potentially high-impact studies. After sequentially excluding the 8 studies (10 cohorts), the pooled risk estimates remained stable, ranging from 1.59 to 2.33, with all corresponding 95% confidence intervals excluding 1.0. This consistency indicates that the overall effect size and statistical significance were not materially altered by the exclusion of any individual study. However, exclusion of the ATBC cohort from Song 2021 resulted in a marked increase in the pooled HR to 2.17 (95% CI: 1.77–2.68), representing a 12.4% increase in effect size. Notably, the 95% CI no longer overlapped with the full dataset, demonstrating a substantial dilution effect exerted by the ATBC cohort on the pooled estimate.

Further investigation revealed that the ATBC cohort consisted exclusively of male participants, introducing a sex-specific confounding factor. Given that male sex is an established risk factor for gastric cancer, the inclusion of a 100% male cohort may have attenuated the observed association between AIG and gastric cancer, thereby lowering the pooled effect size. These findings underscore the importance of accounting for demographic characteristics such as sex distribution, and highlight the need for stratified analyses to validate the stability of the overall conclusions.

Subgroup analysis

To explore the substantial heterogeneity (I²=77%) in the meta-analysis and validate the robustness of the association between AIG and gastric cancer risk, subgroup analyses were performed using a fixed-effects model, stratified by key study characteristics.

A subgroup analysis was conducted on the six included cohort studies (De Re 2023 Validation 1 & 2, Esposito 2020, Guo 2023, Kang 2023, and Miceli 2024). The individual study HRs mostly favored the AIG group (HR > 1), with Kang 2023 carrying the largest weight (44.4%). Using a Fixed Effects model, the pooled Total HR for this subgroup was determined to be 4.09 (95% CI: 2.46–6.78) (Fig. 5). This finding was statistically highly significant (Z = 5.44, P < 0.00001), supporting a strong association between the intervention and the outcome in cohort studies. Furthermore, the analysis demonstrated no heterogeneity across the included studies (I2 = 0%, P = 0.75), suggesting a highly consistent effect across all cohorts.

For European studies (a total of eight cohorts from Italy, Netherlands and Finland), the pooled HR was 2.28 (95% CI: 1.77–2.93, P = 0.0001), indicating a significantly increased gastric cancer risk among patients with AIG (Fig. 6). Heterogeneity remained moderate to high (I² = 76%, Chi² = 29.22, df = 7, P < 0.00001), suggesting substantial variability across studies. Subgroup exploration showed no meaningful differences between Southern and Northern European cohorts, supporting a generally consistent association between AIG and gastric cancer across Europe. The observed heterogeneity was largely attributable to methodological divergence—particularly differences between population-based datasets and tertiary-care cohorts—rather than geographic variation.

The subgroup analysis of studies diagnosing AIG through histology revealed a strong association between AIG and gastric cancer risk, with a pooled HR of 4.82 (95% CI: 2.44–9.51; P < 0.00001) (Fig. 8). This result indicates a significant positive correlation between AIG, diagnosed histologically, and an increased risk of gastric cancer. Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to assess the relationship between AIG and the risk of gastric cancer. Our key finding was that the pooled HR for gastric cancer in patients with AIG was 1.93 (95% CI: 1.59–2.33), indicating a moderate increase in cancer risk. These findings are consistent with some previous studies, though they also offer a more nuanced interpretation. For instance, a recent meta-analysis by Chen et al. (2025) reported a much higher relative risk of 11.05 (95% CI: 6.39–19.11) for gastric cancer in AIG patients [20]. However, the higher relative risk observed in that study may be attributed to the inclusion of many studies published before 2015, which primarily focused on PA and its association with gastric cancer [21].

While PA is a clinical manifestation of advanced AIG, using PA as a substitute for AIG diagnosis is not methodologically rigorous, as it fails to account for the broader spectrum of AIG pathology [3, 22–24]. This discrepancy highlights the importance of distinguishing AIG from PA when assessing the true cancer risk associated with AIG. Corroborating this, recent large-scale population-based studies have consistently reported elevated gastric cancer risks in patients diagnosed specifically with PA. For instance, a UK study by Murphy et al. [25] reported an OR of 2.75 (95% CI: 2.19–3.44), while a US SEER-Medicare analysis by Song et al. [26] demonstrated an OR of 1.93 (95% CI: 1.80–2.06). Because PA represents an advanced stage of AIG with higher neoplastic potential, these findings validate our methodological decision to exclude PA-only cohorts. This ensures our pooled HR of 1.93 more accurately reflects the risk for the broader AIG population.

However, it is crucial to recognize that our pooled HR of 1.93 may conversely represent a conservative estimate. Our sensitivity analysis revealed that excluding the ATBC cohort—which consisted exclusively of male smokers—raised the pooled HR to 2.17. Given that AIG is a female-predominant disease [15, 27], the inclusion of a large, all-male cohort likely diluted the observed association. Therefore, the ‘true’ cancer risk for the typical AIG population likely lies closer to 2.17, positioning it well below historical overestimates derived from PA cohorts but higher than what unstratified data might suggest.

The risk of gastric cancer in patients with AIG should not be underestimated. A recent multicenter study found that gastric cancer occurred in both multifocal atrophic gastritis (MF-AG) and corpus-restricted atrophic gastritis (CR-AG) patients, with no significant difference in overall cancer risk between the two groups [28]. Biologically, the long-term damage to the oxyntic glands caused by AIG, along with the resultant low-acid environment and elevated gastrin levels, can promote ECL cell hyperplasia and neuroendocrine changes [29, 30]. Furthermore, prolonged mucosal remodeling in AIG can induce intestinal metaplasia and genomic instability, both of which are known precursors to cancer. Recent findings also reveal that AIG triggers a diverse array of metaplastic cell types, including a cancer-related subtype resembling incomplete intestinal metaplasia, which shares transcriptional features with gastric cancer. This cancer-like metaplastic subtype, characterized by the expression of the cancer-associated biomarker ANPEP/CD13, suggests that AIG may not only promote gastric carcinogenesis through inflammatory and structural changes but also by inducing a cellular environment conducive to neoplastic transformation [31]. Immunohistochemical markers such as aberrant p53 accumulation, loss of E-cadherin expression, and increased Ki-67 proliferation index provide additional evidence that autoimmune-driven mucosal remodeling creates a permissive environment for neoplastic transformation [32, 33]. Incorporating these molecular features into risk assessment may help distinguish indolent lesions from those with aggressive potential. However, due to the often asymptomatic nature of the disease, the prevalence of AIG and its associated gastric cancer may be underrecognized. Multiple studies have reported that some AIG patients already present with gastric cancer at the time of diagnosis [20, 34–44]. Thus, early identification of AIG and its associated gastric lesions is crucial. Given that AIG-related early gastric cancers may exhibit distinct clinicopathological features—such as a tendency toward polypoid morphology or location in the upper stomach—special vigilance is warranted from endoscopists during surveillance [40, 45].

Our finding that AIG patients who are H. pylori–negative exhibit a markedly higher risk of gastric cancer (HR = 4.31), compared with a more moderate risk in H. pylori–positive AIG patients (HR = 2.36), supports the hypothesis that, in the absence of concurrent H. pylori infection, this autoimmune-driven cascade of oxyntic gland destruction, acid suppression, hypergastrinemia, and mucosal remodeling may exert a more predominant and unmasked carcinogenic effect [46–48]. A recent retrospective analysis of 65 cases of gastric cancer with AIG further supports this hypothesis [49], demonstrating that H. pylori–naïve GC-AIG patients are younger, with tumors more frequently located in the upper and middle stomach, and a higher proportion of pure undifferentiated-type adenocarcinoma compared to those with H. pylori infection. These findings suggest that the absence of H. pylori infection may create a distinct clinicopathological profile in GC-AIG patients, potentially contributing to a more aggressive and earlier-onset form of gastric cancer. Biologically, H. pylori–negative AIG represents a milieu of long-standing oxyntic gland destruction, resulting in chronically reduced gastric acidity, hypergastrinemia, and consequent ECL cell hyperplasia or neuroendocrine changes [46], along with sustained mucosal remodeling that may promote intestinal metaplasia and genomic instability—all processes potentially accelerating neoplastic transformation.

In contrast, AIG patients with coexisting H. pylori infection inhabit a more complex, multifactorial carcinogenic environment: bacterial-induced chronic inflammation, immune responses, and atrophic changes may interact with autoimmune injury, potentially diluting or modifying the pure autoimmune effect [15, 18, 34, 50]. The lower relative risk in this subgroup might reflect an overlap of pathological pathways where H. pylori-related atrophic gastritis mechanisms predominate over isolated autoimmune processes, or conversely, that H. pylori–associated mucosal changes mask or modulate the effect of AIG. This differential pattern underscores the clinical importance of carefully stratifying AIG patients by H. pylori status. Our finding that the risk is highest in H. pylori-negative patients refines and strengthens existing recommendations: according to MAPS III [51], AIG patients, particularly those who are H. pylori–negative, should undergo endoscopic follow-up every 3 to 5 years. This provides robust evidence for the importance of monitoring AIG patients at regular intervals, especially in those without H. pylori infection, where autoimmune mechanisms may play a more direct role in gastric cancer development.

The biological plausibility of this H. pylori-independent risk is strongly corroborated by recent molecular evidence. A 2025 translational study by Hoft et al. utilizing single-cell RNA sequencing demonstrated that chronic inflammation in AIG drives specific gastric metaplastic lineages [31]. Importantly, they unveiled cancer-related metaplastic cells originating directly from the AIG microenvironment, providing a robust cellular mechanism for the oncogenic potential of AIG that operates entirely independently of H. pylori infection. This molecular evidence further justifies the necessity of stringent endoscopic surveillance for the AIG population, regardless of their H. pylori status.

The markedly elevated hazard ratio (HR = 4.82) for gastric cancer observed in patients with histologically confirmed AIG underscores the critical importance of histopathological diagnosis in assessing cancer risk. This strong association suggests that serological markers alone, such as parietal-cell and intrinsic factor antibodies, are insufficient to fully capture the cancer risk conferred by AIG. Serological testing can identify exposure to autoantigens and autoantibodies, but it does not reliably reflect the structural and morphological changes in the gastric mucosa [6, 9, 52]. Histological evaluation, through systematic endoscopic biopsy of the gastric body and fundus, remains the gold standard for diagnosing AIG, as only histology can definitively demonstrate the atrophic process, the disappearance or replacement of parietal cells, the presence of pseudopyloric or intestinal metaplasia, and ECL-cell hyperplasia or dysplasia—all of which represent the architectural and cellular substrate on which neoplastic transformation may occur [2]. Recent updates to diagnostic criteria for AIG, such as those proposed by Japanese investigators, emphasize the necessity of both autoantibody positivity and characteristic endoscopic or histological findings [53]. This reflects a growing recognition that reliance on serological markers alone may lead to underdiagnosis or misclassification, particularly given AIG’s often asymptomatic course.

Based on the stratified findings of this meta-analysis, we propose a shift in the clinical management paradigm for AIG patients, moving from a general surveillance approach to a risk-stratified strategy centered on two key elements: H. pylori status and histological confirmation. Our demonstration that H. pylori-negative AIG patients carry the highest relative risk (HR 4.31) strongly supports the existing recommendations (such as MAPS III) that these patients should be enrolled in rigorous endoscopic surveillance every 3 to 5 years, highlighting that the absence of concurrent infection does not equate to a benign prognosis. Crucially, the discovery that the cancer risk is nearly five-fold higher in patients with histologically confirmed AIG emphasizes the inadequacy of relying solely on serological markers for long-term risk assessment. Therefore, clinical guidelines should be updated to prioritize histological confirmation via systematic gastric body/fundus biopsy over serology alone when establishing a surveillance program. Serological testing is suitable for initial screening; however, a definitive diagnosis and subsequent risk stratification for cancer prevention must be based on documented mucosal damage (atrophy, intestinal metaplasia, ECL cell hyperplasia). Adopting this dual-criteria approach ensures that limited healthcare resources are concentrated on the highest-risk subgroup: AIG patients with histological atrophy and negative H. pylori status. Beyond these foundational criteria, recent large-scale evidence further identifies age > 65 years, intestinal metaplasia, and elevated gastrin-17 as additional key risk factors [54]. Integrating these specific clinical parameters with our proposed framework reinforces the urgent need to shift from a non-stratified surveillance approach to a highly personalized, comprehensive endoscopic monitoring protocol.

This study has several distinct strengths compared to previous reviews. Primary among them is the focus on modern definitions of AIG rather than relying on PA as a surrogate. Previous meta-analyses often included older studies where PA was the primary inclusion criterion. Since PA represents a late-stage clinical manifestation, relying on it excludes a large proportion of AIG patients who are asymptomatic or in earlier stages, potentially skewing risk estimates [1, 55, 56]. By synthesizing data from 2015 to 2025, our study reflects more contemporary diagnostic and management practices. Furthermore, this is one of the few analyses to rigorously stratify risk by H. pylori status. The ability to demonstrate that H. pylori-negative AIG patients carry a significantly higher risk (HR 4.31) compared to mixed or positive populations adds crucial nuance to the understanding of gastric carcinogenesis. Finally, the robustness of our findings was validated through sensitivity analysis. The “leave-one-out” method confirmed that the overall positive association remained stable even after excluding individual studies.

Despite these strengths, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, substantial between-study heterogeneity (I² = 77%) was observed in the primary analysis. However, our subgroup analyses provided important insight into the sources of this variability. When the analysis was restricted to studies with histologically confirmed AIG [39, 57–59], heterogeneity decreased to 0%, indicating that inconsistent diagnostic definitions (serology-based versus histology-based AIG) are a major driver of between-study differences. Likewise, exclusion of the ATBC cohort [60], which consisted exclusively of male smokers, substantially reduced the I², suggesting that extreme demographic imbalances in specific cohorts can attenuate or distort the overall effect estimate. Taken together, these patterns suggest that the observed heterogeneity is driven predominantly by differences in diagnostic methodology and imbalances in cohort composition. While such variability is inherent to meta-analyses of observational studies, the stability of the association in multiple sensitivity analyses (including leave-one-out analyses) mitigates concerns that heterogeneity would invalidate the overall positive relationship between AIG and gastric cancer risk. Second, the total number of studies included in the final quantitative synthesis was relatively small (N = 8). This limited sample size restricts the statistical power available for more granular subgroup analyses and may affect the generalizability of some findings. Third, demographic and clinical imbalances in specific cohorts may have introduced bias. In particular, the ATBC cohort, which consisted exclusively of male smokers, appeared to attenuate the pooled effect size, as shown in our sensitivity analyses. Given that AIG predominantly affects women whereas gastric cancer is more common in men, such sex- and lifestyle-related imbalances may have distorted the association to some extent. Finally, residual confounding remains a concern. Because AIG patients are more likely to undergo repeated endoscopic assessments than the general population, surveillance bias may have inflated the observed association to some extent, although the magnitude of the HR and the consistency across sensitivity analyses suggest that this bias alone is unlikely to fully explain the increased risk. While most studies adjusted for essential covariates such as age and sex, variations in the control for other lifestyle and environmental factors (e.g., smoking, diet, family history) and the rigorousness of H. pylori exclusion across studies cannot be fully controlled for in a meta-analysis.

In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis provides robust evidence that AIG is an independent and significant risk factor for gastric cancer (HR 1.93). Our findings reveal that this risk is highly dependent on patient stratification: it is most pronounced in patients with histologically confirmed AIG and those who are negative for H. pylori infection. These data necessitate a shift towards rigorous risk-stratified surveillance in clinical practice, particularly for the highest-risk subset. Future prospective studies with standardized diagnostic criteria and long-term follow-up are essential to further refine optimal surveillance intervals and to quantify absolute cancer risks in different AIG subgroups.
