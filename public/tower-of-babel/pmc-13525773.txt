Laparoscopic Cervical Cerclage Band Erosion into the Rectal Lumen Complicated by Recto-Uterine Fistula: A Case Report
Authors: Ziqian Tang, Qunzhi Zhang, Qi Li, Desheng Qi, Huan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLaparoscopic abdominal cervical cerclage (LACC) is a well-established intervention for cervical insufficiency. Long-term complications involving delayed band migration and erosion into adjacent viscera are exceptionally rare; the most frequently reported site is the urinary bladder. To the best of our knowledge, complete erosion into the intestinal lumen has not been previously reported. This report describes a case of complete cerclage band erosion into the rectal lumen, later complicated by recto-uterine fistula formation, and highlights the associated diagnostic and management challenges.CaseA 44-year-old asymptomatic woman with prior LACC presented for elective band removal. Preoperative evaluation, including pelvic ultrasound, was unrevealing. During laparoscopy, two cerclage bands were found entirely within the rectal lumen, necessitating unplanned rectal repair and diverting transverse colostomy. Three months later, pre-colostomy reversal imaging revealed a recto-uterine fistula. A multidisciplinary team recommended delayed surgical repair; the patient opted for conservative management.ConclusionTo the best of our knowledge, this case represents the first report of complete transmural erosion of an LACC band into the rectum with subsequent recto-uterine fistula formation. This case demonstrates that cerclage removal in patients with complex surgical histories warrants careful preoperative planning and multidisciplinary preparedness. Given the extreme rarity of this complication, the decision regarding elective cerclage removal should be individualized.Keywords: laparoscopic abdominal cervical cerclage, case report, erosion, recto-uterine fistula, complication

Introduction

Cervical insufficiency is a well-established cause of mid-trimester pregnancy loss, affecting approximately 1% of obstetric populations.1 Cervical cerclage is one of the few effective interventions for preventing preterm birth.1 For patients with a history of failed transvaginal cerclage or altered pelvic anatomy, laparoscopic abdominal cervical cerclage (LACC) offers excellent obstetric outcomes.2,3 Major international guidelines provide structured recommendations for cerclage placement based on obstetric history and cervical length assessment.4–6 A comprehensive review of clinical guidelines confirmed consensus on cerclage indications across professional societies.7

While acute perioperative complications of LACC are rare,8 the long-term behavior of retained cerclage bands remains poorly characterized. For transabdominal cerclage (TAC), the Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine (SMFM) reports complication rates of 0.7–4.5% for laparoscopic placement.9 The largest TAC systematic review to date (83 studies, 3398 patients) found no reports of erosion or fistula formation.10 Delayed complications of cervical cerclage have been documented, primarily involving erosion into the urinary bladder, which may lead to stone formation, recurrent urinary tract infections, and vesicovaginal fistula.11–13 Other reported sites of erosion include the vagina and ureter.14–19 To the best of our knowledge, intestinal involvement has not been described in the existing literature. Current international guidelines do not provide specific recommendations regarding the long-term management of retained abdominal cerclage or criteria for elective removal after completion of childbearing.4–6,9,20

The removal of a cervical cerclage is generally considered a routine procedure. However, in patients with extensive pelvic adhesive disease, the risk of band migration or erosion into adjacent viscera presents a significant diagnostic and surgical challenge. Preoperative imaging in such cases may fail to accurately delineate the complex anatomical relationships. We present a case of complete erosion of an LACC band into the rectal lumen, subsequently complicated by recto-uterine fistula formation, to increase clinical awareness and highlight the need for multidisciplinary preparedness.

Case Report

A 44-year-old woman (gravida 5, para 1) was admitted on June 28, 2025, for elective removal of a cervical cerclage band. Her obstetric history included a cesarean delivery in 2004 for a twin pregnancy and three subsequent spontaneous abortions at approximately 16 weeks of gestation, leading to a diagnosis of cervical insufficiency. In July 2018, the patient had undergone LACC with concomitant adhesiolysis at an external hospital. The operative record documented placement of a polyester cerclage tape at the level of the internal cervical os, medial to the uterine vessels, with tightness confirmed using a 6.5 Hegar dilator. Dense omental adhesions to the abdominal wall and uterine fundus were noted intraoperatively. Active bleeding at the right posterior uterine wall at the cerclage site was controlled with a continuous 1/0 absorbable suture. The procedure was performed under general anesthesia with 50 mL estimated blood loss over 86 minutes; the postoperative course was uneventful.

Following the cerclage, the patient had one pregnancy in 2020, which was terminated due to a missed abortion requiring dilation and curettage. She had no subsequent pregnancies and no further desire for childbearing. She had been asymptomatic in the years following the cerclage. On admission, vital signs were: temperature 36.5°C, heart rate 73 bpm, respiratory rate 19/min, blood pressure 120/86 mmHg, SpO2 98%. Height was 153 cm and weight 55.5 kg. Preoperative laboratory investigations revealed: white blood cell count 6.07×109/L with 71.9% neutrophils, C-reactive protein 3.79 mg/L, hemoglobin 111 g/L, and interleukin-6 33.9 pg/mL. D-dimer was 1.54 μg/mL. Gynecological examination, including triple examination (rectovaginal-abdominal), revealed no significant findings. A pelvic ultrasound on June 30, 2025, demonstrated a ring-shaped hyperechoic structure at the internal cervical os but did not identify any abnormality suggestive of band erosion or fistula formation. The patient did not receive bowel preparation prior to surgery.

On July 7, 2025, the patient underwent laparoscopic surgery. Intraoperative findings revealed dense adhesions between the anterior uterine wall and the bladder, between the anterior abdominal wall peritoneum and the uterus, and between the omentum and the uterine fundus and anterior wall. The posterior uterine wall was densely adherent to the rectum, bilateral adnexa, and the pelvic floor. Adhesiolysis was performed using an ultrasonic scalpel. The vesicouterine peritoneal reflection was opened; however, the cerclage band was not immediately visible. Further dissection along the posterior uterine wall revealed that the anterior wall of the rectum was densely adherent at the cervico-uterine junction without a clear plane of separation. Upon opening the left broad ligament posterior leaflet and the left serosal layer posterior to the cervix, a portion of a white, cord-like band was visualized. The cerclage knot could not be identified. The ultrasonic scalpel was used to dissect along the course of the band, revealing that two cerclage bands were intimately associated with the adherent rectal wall. The bands were transected; upon retrieving one cut end, segments of both bands including the cerclage knots were found within the rectal lumen (Figure 1A), with the surface coated with old fecal material (Figure 1B). The extracted cerclage band measured approximately 6 cm in length. This confirmed the diagnosis of ectopic erosion of the cervical cerclage band into the rectal lumen. Estimated blood loss for this portion of the procedure was 80 mL over 140 minutes.

Intraoperative photographs. (A) Upon retrieving one cut end, the cerclage band knot were found within the lumen of the adherent rectum segment (green arrowheads indicate the band knot within the rectal lumen). (B) The extracted cerclage band with surface coated with old fecal material.

A colorectal surgery consultation was obtained intraoperatively. Rectal perforation repair was performed using 3–0 Vicryl interrupted sutures. No intraoperative leak test was performed. Given the finding of rectal perforation by the cerclage material, the absence of preoperative bowel preparation, and the presence of fecal contamination, a diverting transverse loop colostomy was created. This second portion of the procedure lasted 5 hours and 5 minutes, with an estimated 100 mL blood loss. Perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis with cefuroxime was administered.

The patient’s postoperative recovery was satisfactory. Follow-up laboratory values on post-operative day 6 showed: WBC 6.07×109/L, CRP 3.79 mg/L, hemoglobin 111 g/L, and IL-6 33.9 pg/mL. She was discharged on July 15, 2025 (hospital stay: 10 days). The colostomy was managed with regular appliance changes and stoma care, and a plan was made for colostomy closure three months postoperatively.

The patient was readmitted on October 6, 2025 for planned colostomy reversal. Pre-reversal evaluation included: (1) an iodinated oil enema, which revealed contrast leakage from the rectum into the vagina, suggestive of a recto-uterine fistula; (2) pelvic MRI, which demonstrated an irregular and locally discontinuous area in the posterior-superior wall of the uterine body with an indistinct connection to the rectum (Figure 2A and B); (3) contrast-enhanced CT, which showed only a small amount of pelvic fluid without clear visualization of the fistula tract; and (4) colonoscopy, which showed findings consistent with colitis. A multidisciplinary team (MDT) consultation was held. Considering that the fistula had been present for approximately three months and given the history of extensive pelvic adhesions and prior surgeries, the MDT concluded that local inflammation and edema might not have fully resolved and recommended surgical intervention at 6 to 12 months, provided no active acute inflammation was present. After considering the complexity and risks associated with the proposed surgical repair, the patient and her family decided to forgo further surgical intervention and opted for conservative management. Subsequent telephone follow-up was attempted but the patient declined further contact.

Discussion

Cervical insufficiency remains a significant cause of preterm birth, and cervical cerclage improves perinatal outcomes in appropriately selected patients.1,4,5 LACC is generally regarded as safe. The largest TAC systematic review (83 studies, 3,398 patients) did not identify any case of erosion or fistula formation;10 however, rare long-term complications have been documented. A comprehensive review of all cerclage-related fistulas identified exclusively urogenital types—vesicovaginal, vesicocervical, vesicouterine, and ureterovaginal—with no rectal or bowel involvement reported.21 The most frequently reported site of delayed cerclage band erosion is the urinary bladder;11–13 other affected sites include the vagina and ureter.14–19 The mechanism of erosion is hypothesized to involve a combination of chronic inflammation and foreign-body reaction at the tissue-band interface, coupled with sustained mechanical pressure leading to progressive tissue ischemia and necrosis. Analogous processes have been well characterized in surgical mesh complications, where foreign-body inflammatory responses with activation of matrix metalloproteinases degrading collagen and elastin have been demonstrated.22 Mesh cut edges may trigger inflammatory reactions leading to weakening and erosion of adjacent organs.23 However, histopathological confirmation is rarely available, and these mechanisms remain putative.

In contrast to the well-documented bladder erosion cases, the present case demonstrates complete transmural erosion of the cerclage band into the rectal lumen with subsequent fistula formation. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first report of intestinal involvement following LACC. While intraoperative bowel injury during cerclage placement has been reported24 and postoperative infections have been documented,25 delayed migration and erosion into the gastrointestinal tract represents a distinct clinical entity. This pattern of foreign-body erosion bears similarities to mesh-related complications following pelvic surgery, where synthetic materials can migrate into the bladder, bowel, or vagina through processes of chronic inflammation and mechanical erosion.22,26,27 The Gupta et al description of the LACC technique explicitly acknowledges this risk, noting that the polyester tape is secured with a 2.0 silk suture specifically to prevent a theoretic risk of erosion into the bladder.28

The fistula in this case developed after cerclage band removal, and several factors likely contributed. First, the chronic erosion process had created a transmural communication between the uterus and rectum; while the rectal defect was repaired at the time of band extraction, the corresponding uterine wall defect may not have been separately identified or closed, leaving a residual epithelialized channel susceptible to fistulization. Second, the rectal repair was performed under suboptimal conditions—in the absence of preoperative bowel preparation, with fecal contamination of the operative field, and in inflamed, densely adherent tissue—predisposing to microscopic suture-line dehiscence and subsequent fistula tract maturation. Third, compromised local vascular supply in the setting of dense pelvic adhesions likely impaired tissue healing at the repair site. Fourth, persistent postoperative inflammation in the pelvis may have further contributed to breakdown at the repair interface. The German S3-Guideline notes that pelvic fistulas can arise from suture insufficiency at repair sites and that technical problems and local infections caused by foreign materials play an important role.29 The absence of histopathological examination of the fistula tract precludes definitive establishment of any single etiology.

The diagnostic capabilities and limitations of various imaging modalities in this case warrant discussion. Preoperative pelvic ultrasound identified the cerclage band at the level of the internal cervical os but failed to detect its ectopic intraluminal location or the associated rectal wall abnormality. Three-dimensional ultrasound-based virtual cystoscopy has shown diagnostic value for detecting eroded cerclage sutures within the bladder,30 though its role in detecting rectal involvement is unknown. In contrast, postoperative MRI and iodinated oil contrast enema successfully demonstrated the recto-uterine fistulous tract. CT identified only a small amount of pelvic fluid without clear fistula visualization. Colonoscopy provided additional mucosal assessment but was non-specific, showing only colitis. A tailored multimodal imaging approach incorporating MRI and contrast studies should be considered when cerclage-related complications are suspected in patients with complex surgical histories.29,31

This case underscores several important clinical lessons. First, laparoscopic cerclage removal should not be approached as a uniformly straightforward procedure. In patients with extensive adhesive disease and a history of multiple pelvic surgeries, preoperative consultation with colorectal surgery and consideration of bowel preparation should be undertaken. Second, multidisciplinary collaboration—involving gynecology, colorectal surgery, radiology, and stoma care—is essential. Third, the decision to perform elective cerclage removal in asymptomatic patients should be individualized. Current evidence indicates a notable absence of guideline-based recommendations on this matter. The SMFM states that the long-term management of a TAC in situ after completion of childbearing is not well defined and that there are no outcome data to support routine TAC removal.9 The RCOG guideline explicitly acknowledges this gap, noting the absence of published studies on long-term outcomes of TAC removal versus retention.5 A systematic review of 20 guidelines confirmed that none address criteria for cerclage removal or long-term management of retained cerclage.20 Even in the acute setting of preterm premature rupture of membranes, no consensus exists among international guidelines: ACOG does not provide a strong recommendation, RCOG advocates removal after corticosteroid administration, and FIGO leaves the decision to clinician discretion.32,33 This lack of consensus, even for an emergent indication, further underscores the absence of evidence for elective removal in asymptomatic patients. The decision should therefore be based on individualized risk assessment through shared decision-making between patient and surgeon.

This report has several important limitations. As a single case report, it cannot establish causal relationships between the cerclage band and the observed complications, nor can the findings be generalized. The absence of histopathological examination of the eroded tissue limits definitive characterization of the underlying mechanisms. Long-term follow-up data are limited, as the patient opted for conservative management and declined further surgical intervention and telephone follow-up. Despite these limitations, this report adds valuable information by documenting an extremely rare complication and highlighting important clinical considerations for managing patients with retained cerclage bands and complex pelvic surgical histories.

In summary, this report describes complete transmural erosion of an LACC band into the rectal lumen complicated by recto-uterine fistula formation—a clinical scenario not previously described in the literature. This case illustrates that in patients with a history of severe pelvic adhesions, the potential, albeit extremely rare, risk of delayed cerclage band erosion into adjacent hollow viscera should be considered. It advocates for heightened vigilance during cerclage band removal, including thorough preoperative imaging assessment, preparedness for unplanned visceral repair with appropriate surgical expertise, and the indispensable role of a multidisciplinary team.
