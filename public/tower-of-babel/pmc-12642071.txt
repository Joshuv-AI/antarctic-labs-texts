Effects of kinesio taping applied over the biceps muscle on muscle activity and muscle fatigue
Authors: Gülseren Bebek, Yücel Ocak, Hasan Sözen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKinesio taping (KT) is commonly used to support muscle function, enhance performance, and reduce fatigue in both athletic and clinical settings. Understanding its effects on agonist and antagonist muscle groups during resistance exercises is essential for optimizing training and rehabilitation strategies. This study aimed to investigate the effects of kinesiotaping (KT) applied to the biceps brachii on the activation and fatigue of the biceps brachii (BB) and triceps brachii (TB) muscles during bicipital curl exercise.MethodsA total of 40 participants (10 female athletes, 10 male athletes, 10 sedentary females, and 10 sedentary males), aged 18–24 years, were included in the study. The participants performed isotonic muscle activation and isometric fatigue protocols targeting the BB (agonist) and TB (antagonist) muscles during the bicipital curl movement following KT application. Surface EMG signals were collected via wireless Ag/AgCl electrodes and a Noraxon system.ResultsA statistically significant difference was observed in the pre- and posttest values of the BB muscle during the bicep curl exercise (p < 0.05). Additionally, analysis of median frequency (MF) values revealed no overall pre–post differences. However, a significant posttest between-group difference was observed in the BB muscle, with athletes showing lower MF values than sedentary participants, indicating relatively greater fatigue in athletes.ConclusionsThe 48-hour application of Kinesio tape led to increased electrical activation of the agonist muscle (BB), and sedentary individuals demonstrated higher MF values compared to athletes, indicating relatively lower fatigue levels after taping. These findings suggest that short-term KT may alter neuromuscular function by modulating muscle activation and fatigue patterns. Furthermore, the results highlight the differential impact of KT depending on training status, suggesting that while KT may facilitate performance in trained individuals, it could accelerate fatigue in untrained individuals. Future research should explore optimal taping duration, tension, and placement strategies to tailor KT interventions for specific functional goals.Keywords: Surface electromyography, Kinesio tape, Muscle fatigue, Muscle activation, Antagonist muscle

Background

The Kinesio Taping (KT) technique was discovered and produced in 1973 by Dr. Kenzo Kase, an acupuncture and chiropractic scientist. While measured tape applications support joint and muscle structure, they limit the functional activities and movements of the joint. Additionally, KT applications do not provide a basis for deep tissues such as tissue fascia damaged by compression effects on the skin, but they also slow the healing of damage [1]. The philosophy of the emergence of this term is that a banding method such as human flexibility can be obtained without affecting joint movements, and more successful results can be obtained in the structural properties of human skin. Kase et al. started the search for a banding method that did not limit joint mobility while helping with tissue healing, as opposed to the effects of conventional tapes, and after two years of research, he designed kinesological tape and started to apply the methods he developed in different body regions [2]. The first used was called “KinesioTex Gold”. The sticky face of this tape, which is still the most widely used species, has a sinusoidal wavy structure. The area between the waves allows sweat and air to pass through the tape easily. The sticky face of the “Kinesio Tex Platinum” band, which was later developed, is shaped like a diamond slice. It is usually recommended for sports injuries, and it is used by experienced practitioners [3]. The most important factor that has made the band internationally recognized is its use during the 2008 Beijing Summer Olympics by many athletes in different branches during competitions. The use of these tapes by elite and well-known professional athletes during training and competitions increased the popularity of the tape [4]. There is an increase in the number of professionals who apply kinesio tape, especially all physicians, and sports specialists, nurses, chiropractors, and physiotherapists who deal with lymphology and musculoskeletal diseases worldwide [5].

The mechanisms affected by kinesological banding are the main problems caused by the musculoskeletal system. After excessive attrition or injury, the elastic property of the muscle deteriorates, and the muscle loses its function. KT therefore involves the flexibility of the muscle, with the effects of adhesiveness on the skin and lifting on the skin in a way that allows air navigation between the skin’s external environments [6, 7].

Kinesio tape can extend up to 140% of the original length of the tape, thereby strengthening the muscles with a constant cutting force on the skin and improving muscle function [8]. It mimics human skin in weight, thickness, and elasticity and contains no medication. A latex‑free material made with acrylic adhesive is considered hypoallergenic and breathable. In common practice, it can remain on the skin for approximately 3–5 days (sometimes up to 4) without interfering with daily hygiene, although longer wear may increase the risk of skin irritation. According to Fu et al., kinesio taping techniques are claimed to reduce pain, swelling, and muscle spasms and are recommended for sport‑injury prevention and rehabilitation [9]. KT is theorized to help reposition subluxed joints by reducing abnormal muscle tension and restoring fascia‑muscle function. It is proposed to modulate muscle tone and enhance athletic performance. Recent evidence further suggests that it may regulate muscle tone and support muscle function, although effects appear to be condition‑dependent and vary according to the individual and injury context [10–12].

Surface electromyography (sEMG) is a widely used, noninvasive technique for assessing skeletal muscle activity. It captures the electrical potential generated during muscle contraction, providing objective, quantitative data that reflect neuromuscular control and motor unit recruitment. Modern applications of sEMG include clinical diagnostics, sports science, rehabilitation, movement analysis, and fatigue assessment [13]. It enables clinicians and researchers to monitor activation patterns, symmetry between paired muscles, and responses to interventions such as rehabilitation exercises or strength training [14]. Recent research has examined the influence of KT on muscle activation via surface EMG. In athletes and healthy individuals, studies have shown inconsistent sEMG responses: some have reported no significant changes in muscle activation or fatigue patterns following KT application [15], whereas others have reported increased muscle activation when KT is applied in a specific direction and tension [16].

This study aimed to investigate the effects of KT applied to the biceps brachii on the activation and fatigue of both the agonist (biceps brachii) and antagonist (triceps brachii) muscles during bicipital curl exercise.

Methods

Participants

A total of 40 individuals (10 female athletes, 10 male athletes, 10 sedentary females, and 10 sedentary males) aged between 18 and 24 years voluntarily participated in this study. All participants were informed about the purpose and procedures of the study prior to testing, and written informed consent was obtained through a signed volunteer form. They were instructed to avoid caffeine, nicotine, alcohol, intense physical activity, and similar substances 48 h before the measurements. The physical characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1. A priori sample size calculation was performed using GPower (version 3.1). Assuming an effect size of d = 0.65 based on previous literature, with an alpha level of 0.05 and a desired power of 0.80, the minimum required sample size was 34 participants. Our study included 40 participants, which exceeded this requirement and was therefore considered adequate to ensure sufficient statistical power.

Demographic characteristics of the participants

The participants were screened using a demographic information form that included questions regarding previous sports injuries, chronic or acute muscle pain, musculoskeletal disorders, and skin conditions such as allergies. Individuals with such conditions were excluded from the study. The experimental procedures began upon the participants’ second visit to the laboratory. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Afyon Kocatepe University Health Sciences Ethics Committee for Clinical Research (Decision Code: 2011-KAEK-2, Approval Number: 2018 − 209).

Research design

The participants completed isotonic muscle activation and isometric fatigue protocols targeting the biceps brachii (BB) and triceps brachii (TB) muscles, which act as agonists and antagonists, respectively, during the bicipital curl movement. The Dumbbell Biceps Curl (DBC) exercise was employed to assess BB muscle activation and fatigue (Fig. 1) [17]. Each participant performed three sets of ten isotonic repetitions, using a dumbbell corresponding to 30% of their individual one-repetition maximum (1RM). Each participant’s 1RM was determined using a standardized submaximal testing protocol. The 1RM was estimated by recording the maximum load lifted for 6–10 repetitions and applying validated prediction equations (Brzycki formula). This procedure ensured safety while providing an accurate measure of maximum strength. To standardize the movement tempo, a metronome set at 30 beats per minute was used, with 4 s allocated per repetition (2 s for concentric flexion and 2 s for eccentric extension). Isotonic exercises are dynamic muscle contractions performed against a constant load throughout the full range of joint motion. During the concentric phase of an isotonic contraction, the muscle shortens while maintaining a consistent resistance.

DBC movement is used to assess biceps brachii (BB) muscle activation and fatigue. A Starting position of movement. B End position of the movement

For the assessment of BB muscle fatigue, participants were instructed to maintain an isometric contraction at a 90° elbow flexion angle until volitional exhaustion (Fig. 2). The load used corresponded to 30% of each participant’s individual one-repetition maximum (1RM). The trial was terminated when the 90-degree joint angle could no longer be maintained.

Representative posture during the bicep brachii (BB) muscle fatigue phase in the isometric contraction task

During DBC movement, which is used to assess the activation and fatigue of the BB muscle, sEMG data are collected from the dominant arm. Measurements were recorded from both the agonist muscle (BB) and its antagonist (TB) when the muscle was in its most active contraction phase. A Noraxon device (myoMUSCLE, Noraxon Inc., Scottsdale, AZ, USA) equipped with wireless Ag/AgCl surface electrodes was used for signal acquisition. In addition, joint angle control was performed via the MyoMotion system (Noraxon Inc., Scottsdale, AZ, USA).

EMG analysis and signal processing

The maximum voluntary contraction (MVC) method, an amplitude-based EMG analysis technique, was used to normalize muscle activation data collected from the biceps brachii (BB) and triceps brachii (TB) muscles. In accordance with the procedure described by Konrad [18], participants were instructed to gradually increase muscle force, reach maximal effort within 3–5 s, maintain maximum contraction for 3 s, and then relax rapidly. This procedure was performed three times, with 30–60 s of rest between trials. The MVC protocol was used to normalize the EMG values derived from the root mean square (RMS) amplitude, expressed as a percentage of the MVC (%MVC). This normalization allows for intersubject comparisons of muscle activation levels. Following the MVC trials, EMG data were collected during three sets of ten isotonic contractions, with a 2-minute rest interval between sets. These measurements were taken approximately 3 min after the MVC protocol. The raw EMG signals were sampled at 1500 Hz and processed via a 20–500 Hz band-pass Butterworth filter (4th order) to remove motion artifacts and baseline drift. The signals were then full-wave rectified, and RMS values were calculated with a 512-sample window and 50% overlap. Median frequency (MF) was computed from the power spectral density within a 15-second analysis window (5th–20th second of contraction) to represent muscle fatigue. Since raw EMG signals are not directly suitable for interpretation, they were processed prior to analysis. Finally, a smoothing filter was applied to the RMS processed signal to generate a cleaner and more interpretable EMG amplitude curve.

Fatigue protocol

To assess fatigue in the BB muscle, participants were instructed to maintain an isometric contraction at a 90° elbow flexion angle until volitional exhaustion. sEMG signals were recorded at a sampling frequency of 1500 Hz and analysed at a 10 Hz frequency resolution. The raw EMG signals were processed via a Butterworth high-pass filter, which is designed to provide a maximally flat frequency response within the passband. The median frequency (MF) values were calculated within a 15-second time window, specifically between the 5th and 20th seconds of isometric contraction. The MF is commonly used in sEMG-based muscle fatigue analysis, as it represents the frequency at which the power spectrum is divided into two equal halves. A decrease in MF over time is considered an indicator of muscle fatigue, especially during sustained isometric contractions [19]. The fatigue index was calculated on the basis of the decline in MF values to quantify the progression of neuromuscular fatigue. For fatigue analysis, the 5th–20th second of isometric contraction was selected. The first 5 s were excluded to avoid transient signal instability at contraction onset, while the terminal phase was excluded due to variability associated with imminent exhaustion. The 15-second window thus represents a stable contraction period, consistent with previous EMG fatigue studies [19–21].

Kinesio tape application

Following the initial EMG recordings of muscle activation and fatigue, kinesiology tape (Kinesio® Tex Gold™ Tape, 5 cm) was applied to the BB via the neural facilitation technique (I-strip method) with 50% tension (Fig. 3) to increase muscle activation [22]. The tape was applied by a certified practitioner using a standardized procedure. The tape length and placement were kept consistent across all participants, and to ensure reproducibility, anatomical landmarks were marked with indelible ink to maintain electrode and tape consistency between sessions. The participants were instructed to continue their normal daily activities but to refrain from any sporting or strenuous physical activity during the 48-hour tape application period. After 48 h, posttest sEMG measurements were performed, including isotonic (3 sets of 10 repetitions) and isometric contraction protocols, excluding MVC trials. To ensure consistency in electrode placement between the pre- and posttest sessions, all electrode locations were marked via an indelible skin-safe pen during the initial measurement. The MVC values obtained during the first visit were used for normalization throughout the study. This allowed the investigation of potential changes in muscle activation attributable to the 48-hour Kinesio tape application.

KT was applied to the biceps brachii muscle via the neural facilitation technique (I-strip method)

Statistical analysis

All the data obtained in the study were transferred to a computer environment and recorded for analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics, including the arithmetic mean (X̄) and standard deviation (SD), were used to summarize the data. The Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to assess whether the muscle activation and fatigue values of the biceps brachii (BB) and triceps brachii (TB) muscles followed a normal distribution.

For normally distributed data, paired-samples t tests were used to compare pretest and posttest measurements, and independent-samples t tests were applied for between-group comparisons (e.g., sedentary vs. athlete, male vs. female). When data were not normally distributed, nonparametric alternatives were employed: the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for within-group comparisons, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for between-group comparisons. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Results

During the DBC exercise, a statistically significant increase was observed in the isotonic muscle activation (%MVC) of the BB following the 48-hour Kinesio taping intervention (pretest: M = 30.04, SD = 12.98; posttest: M = 34.28, SD = 15.05; t = − 3.402, p = 0.002). These findings suggest that the application of Kinesio tape positively influences agonist muscle activity during dynamic movement. In contrast, although there was a numerical increase in TB activation from pretest to posttest (pretest: M = 20.69, SD = 11.21; posttest: M = 22.79, SD = 12.29), the difference was not statistically significant (t = − 1.707, p = 0.096). This may indicate that kinesiotaping had a more pronounced effect on the primary (agonist) muscle involved in the exercise task, whereas antagonist muscle activation remained relatively unaffected (Table 2).

When comparing sedentary and athletic participants during DBC movement, no statistically significant differences were found between groups in the %MVC values of BB or TB muscles at either the pretest or posttest phases (p > 0.05). Although sedentary individuals consistently demonstrated numerically higher muscle activation values than athletes did, these differences did not reach statistical significance. As shown in Table 4, no statistically significant differences were found between male and female participants in terms of %MVC values for both BB and TB muscles during the pre- and posttest phases of DBC movement (p > 0.05). Although the mean activation values for all the parameters were slightly greater in the female group than in the male group, these differences did not reach statistical significance. The highest mean difference was observed in the posttest TB values, where females showed greater activation (26.19 ± 14.00) than males (19.40 ± 9.48). This difference was not statistically significant, but it trended toward significance (t = 1.797, p = 0.080), suggesting a possible sex-related effect. These findings suggest that while gender-based variations in muscle activation following kinesiotaping exist numerically, they do not yield statistically meaningful differences in this sample. Further investigations with larger cohorts or different loading protocols may be needed to clarify potential sex-related neuromuscular responses.

As shown in Table 5, no statistically significant differences were observed in the median frequency (MF) values of BB and TB muscles between pretest and posttest isometric contractions following 48 h of kinesiotaping (p > 0.05). For the BB muscle, the MF slightly decreased from 54.11 ± 5.80 Hz to 53.88 ± 5.46 Hz, whereas the TB muscle showed a negligible increase from 56.43 ± 7.03 Hz to 56.54 ± 6.93 Hz. These findings suggest that 48-hour kinesiotaping did not significantly alter the neuromuscular fatigue characteristics of the BB or TB muscles during isometric effort, as reflected by stable MF values. The median frequency, a key indicator of muscle fatigue, appears to remain unaffected by short-term taping protocols under isometric loading conditions.

Discussion

To evaluate muscle activity and fatigue in both the pre- and posttest phases, sEMG and MVC analyses were used. These methods provide amplitude-based measurements of muscle signals in the BB and TB. In this study, we investigated the effects of KT applied to the BB muscle, which serves as an agonist during the bicipital curl movement, and assessed its influence not only on the activation and fatigue of the BB but also on the TB, which acts as the antagonist muscle.

When the body composition data of all the volunteers who participated in the study were examined, no statistically significant differences were found between sedentary and athletic individuals in terms of height, weight, age, or BMI. This suggests that the study sample was homogeneous with respect to physical characteristics. Although sEMG is a widely accepted method for evaluating muscle activation, measurements can be influenced by numerous intrinsic and extrinsic factors, such as electrode placement, subcutaneous fat thickness, skin‒electrode impedance, and task-specific dynamics (e.g., joint position and contraction type) [23]. According to the literature, muscle activation may vary on the basis of joint position [24], time of day when measurements are taken [25], anatomical placement of the joint [26], and the involvement of synergistic muscles [27]. Furthermore, psychological factors such as participant motivation have also been reported to influence muscle activity. In our study, the physical characteristics of the sedentary and athletic groups were controlled, and measurements were conducted at consistent times during the day to minimize potential confounding effects related to motivation or circadian influences.

When the MVIC-normalized RMS (µV) values obtained from BB and TB muscles during the pretest and posttest DBC activations (based on three sets of ten repetitions) were examined, a statistically significant increase was observed in the BB muscle, which acts as the agonist during movement. This finding suggests that the BB muscle was more actively recruited during the same exercise after 48 h of kinesiotaping. As expected, the agonist muscle demonstrated greater participation than did the antagonist muscle throughout the DBC task. Although the posttest activation values of the antagonist muscle (TB) were also numerically greater than the pretest values, this difference was not statistically significant. Recent research provides mixed support for our findings regarding increased agonist (BB) activation following 48 h of kinesiotaping. Lemos et al. [16] demonstrated significantly elevated mean and maximal sEMG amplitudes in the rectus femoris muscle when the tape was applied from the origin to the insertion site at low-to-moderate tension (~ 10%), supporting facilitative mechanisms similar to those we observed in BB activation. Similarly, a study on healthy subjects reported increased concentric elbow torque, suggesting possible underlying increases in neural drive to the biceps brachii following KT application [8]. Slupik et al. [28] investigated the effects of KT on the vastus medialis muscle via sEMG. They reported increased bioelectrical activity after 24 h, with statistically significant changes occurring after 72 h. In contrast, our study demonstrated a statistically significant increase in BB muscle activation after 48 h, suggesting that the effect of KT may vary by muscle group, taping technique, or duration of application. Similarly, Lee et al. [24] examined the sEMG activity of the BB muscle during a strength protocol consisting of seven sets of ten repetitions and reported a decrease in sEMG activity at 24 and 48 h posttaping, followed by an increase at 72 h. While our results differ slightly, the upwards trend in muscle activation we observed at 48 h may be attributed to the specific taping method employed. In our study, the I-strip tap technique designed to stimulate muscle activity was applied, which may have contributed to earlier facilitation of muscle recruitment. However, other recent studies have reported no significant changes in activation or strength on the basis of tape direction, despite the use of similar facilitation techniques. For example, a controlled trial revealed that KT application direction (proximal‒distal vs. distal‒proximal) did not significantly influence EMG activation or muscle strength in asymptomatic individuals [29]. Additionally, Králová et al. [30] assessed the impact of KT on the isokinetic strength of the elbow joint but reported no significant differences in flexion and extension force measurements after 24–72 h. Notably, their study did not evaluate sEMG activity, making direct comparisons with our findings limited. Together, these findings suggest that activation gains after KT may depend critically on factors such as muscle group, task type (static versus dynamic), athletic status, direction and tension of taping, and duration postapplication.

When the muscle activation rates (%MVC-normalized RMS) of BBs and TBs during isotonic DBC exercises were compared, sedentary participants exhibited numerically greater activation in both muscles than did athletes, although these differences were not statistically significant. This trend suggests that sedentary individuals may require greater neuromuscular recruitment to perform the same task, a finding that is consistent with previous sEMG comparisons across training statuses. For example, Syed‑Abdul et al. [31] compared TB activation during push-ups among sedentary and football or gymnastics athletes; sedentary individuals again presented higher EMG amplitudes, with the lowest TB activation in the gymnastics group. This study supports the interpretation that athletes may execute the same movements more efficiently, with lower relative activation. Furthermore, the neuromuscular adaptation literature shows that elite athletes tend to exhibit smaller acute sEMG responses to standardized stimuli than recreational or sedentary individuals do because of neural and muscular efficiency adaptations over time [32]. Therefore, although our results did not reach statistical significance, the trend of greater BB and TB activation in sedentary individuals mirrors the established literature: less trained populations often display greater relative muscle activation for equivalent loads, likely reflecting lower neuromuscular efficiency.

When the pretest and posttest muscle activation levels of the BB and TB were examined during isotonic DBC exercises, female participants demonstrated higher average activation values in both muscles than male participants did. However, these differences were not statistically significant. This trend may suggest that female participants required greater neuromuscular effort to perform the same task, possibly due to differences in relative strength or neuromuscular recruitment strategies. Previous studies have shown mixed results regarding sex differences in muscle activation. For example, White et al. [33] reported greater muscle activation in certain lower extremity muscles in male participants. In contrast, Garrison et al. [34], who investigated muscle activation in male and female football players via MVC normalization, reported no significant gender-based differences. Similarly, Keller et al. [35] reported no significant differences in muscle activation between males and females across various upper limb tasks. Our findings are consistent with these studies, indicating that although females may show slightly greater activation on average, these differences are not always statistically significant and may depend on task-specific factors such as movement complexity, relative load, or fatigue level.

When the MF values obtained from the participating muscles were examined for muscle fatigue during the preliminary and posttest isometric muscle activation of the subjects who participated in the study, no significant difference was detected in the preliminary and final test values of the BB and TB muscles. This result suggests that after 48 h of kinesio band application, the muscles show increased bioelectric activity during isotonic contraction but no change in fatigue values during static isometric contraction. The muscles cause an increase in their bioelectric activity during moving (isotonic) contraction but do not cause a change in muscle fatigue values during static contraction (isometric). Isometric exercise is called isometric contraction, which continues against resistances too great for the muscle to resist without a change in muscle length and movement in the joint [36]. Since there is no mobility in the articulated joint, it can be preferred in cases where joint movement cannot be performed [37]. It is widely accepted that muscle fatigue during isometric contractions indicates a decrease in MF values obtained from sEMG [20, 21]. In one study, the effects of kinesiotaping on muscle fatigue were evaluated via median frequency values derived from sEMG, as in our study. Researchers have examined fatigue in the upper trapezius, lower trapezius, and serratus anterior muscles of senior athletes. The results indicated that KT did not produce any significant changes in muscle fatigue [38]. Our study shows results similar to those of this study. The reason for these similar results may be the participation of healthy volunteers both in our study and in the studies of Hsu et al. [39] and Su et al. [40], who examined the effects of kinesiyo band application on muscle fatigue in athletes with athlete injuries and reported that the kinesiyo band caused changes in the intensity of disabled muscle groups. It should also be noted that the placebo effect is a well-recognized factor in kinesio taping research. Several studies have demonstrated that participants’ expectations and the mere presence of tape on the skin can influence perceived muscle performance, activation, or fatigue, independent of any physiological mechanism. Since our study did not include a sham or placebo taping condition, it is possible that some of the observed changes in EMG activity or fatigue may partly reflect psychological rather than purely neuromuscular effects. This consideration highlights the importance of incorporating placebo-controlled designs in future KT research to better distinguish between physiological adaptations and expectation-driven outcomes.

Overall, the findings indicate that 48 h of KT application did not consistently alter muscle fatigue as measured by MF values. While sedentary participants exhibited relatively lower fatigue levels compared with athletes, most pre–post comparisons showed no significant changes. Thus, KT appears to influence fatigue responses in a subgroup-dependent manner rather than producing a uniform effect.

When the median frequency (MF) values obtained from the muscles were examined to assess muscle fatigue during the pre- and posttest isometric contractions of sedentary and athletic participants, a statistically significant difference was found only in the posttest BB muscle. When comparing sedentary and athletic groups, posttest MF values indicated that sedentary individuals had higher MF values than athletes, which reflects relatively lower fatigue levels. In contrast, athletes demonstrated lower MF values, suggesting a greater fatigue response following KT application. These results highlight that training status may differentially influence the fatigue response to KT, with sedentary participants showing relative resistance to fatigue, whereas athletes appear more susceptible.

When the MF values obtained from the muscles participating in the study were examined for muscle fatigue during the preliminary and posttest isometric muscle activation of the male and female volunteers who participated in our study, the values obtained from the pretest and posttest BB and TB muscles were not significantly different. This result reveals that after 48 h of kinesio tape application, muscle fatigue values do not differ according to sex.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the sample consisted of healthy young adults aged 18–24 years, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other age groups or clinical populations. Second, the study used a single KT technique (I-strip with 50% tension) applied to one muscle group (biceps brachii), which may not reflect the variety of techniques, tensions, or anatomical regions used in clinical and athletic practice. Third, the duration of KT application was limited to 48 h, while a significant increase in muscle activation was observed, the longer-term effects on neuromuscular function and fatigue remain unknown.

Additionally, although sEMG is a widely accepted method for evaluating muscle activity and fatigue, it is sensitive to external factors such as skin preparation, electrode placement, subcutaneous fat, and participant motivation. While precautions were taken to standardize the measurement conditions (e.g., consistent testing times, skin markings for electrode location), some variability in signal quality may still exist. Finally, the study did not include a control group without taping or with sham taping, which limits the ability to isolate the specific effects of Kinesio taping from placebo or learning effects.

Future research should include larger and more diverse populations, compare different taping techniques and durations, and incorporate control groups to better isolate the physiological and functional impacts of KT.

Another limitation of this study is the reliance on multiple pairwise t-tests across different factors (time, muscle, training status, and sex), which may increase the risk of Type I error. A factorial approach, such as repeated measures ANOVA or linear mixed-effects models, would provide a more comprehensive and statistically robust framework, particularly in future studies conducted with larger sample sizes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, 48 h of KT application increased electrical activation of the biceps brachii during dynamic exercise, indicating enhanced neuromuscular recruitment. However, KT did not consistently alter muscle fatigue as measured by MF values, with sedentary participants showing relatively lower fatigue levels compared to athletes. These findings suggest that KT may facilitate muscle activation but has inconsistent effects on fatigue, depending on training status.
