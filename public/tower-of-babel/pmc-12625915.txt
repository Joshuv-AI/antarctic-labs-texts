Evaluating the impact of the Medicaid Balancing Incentive Programme on long-term care utilisation by living arrangement: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Ah-Reum Lee, Yulin Yang, Thomas Rapp, Jacqueline Torres
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the impact of the Medicaid Balancing Incentive Programme (BIP) on long-term services and supports utilisation among older adults, focusing on differences in living arrangements.DesignQuasi-experimental study using a generalised difference-in-differences approach.SettingStates that participated and completed BIP (treatment group: 18 states) and states that were eligible but did not participate (control group: 17 states).ParticipantsOlder Medicaid beneficiaries from the Health and Retirement Study (2006–2018) across states that participated in BIP and those that did not. A negative control analysis was conducted using non-Medicaid beneficiaries.OutcomesWe examined the probability of long-term nursing home stays (over 100 days), professional home healthcare and three types of home care services for activities of daily living: paid professional caregiving, paid informal caregiving and unpaid informal caregiving. Analysis was stratified by living arrangement (living alone vs with others).ResultsBIP participation was associated with a 5 percentage point (pp) decrease in long-term nursing home stays among Medicaid beneficiaries living alone (average treatment effect on the treated, ATT=–0.06, 95% CI –0.10 to –0.02; p<0.01). Among Medicaid beneficiaries living with others, BIP was associated with a 5 pp increase in paid informal caregiving (ATT 0.05, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.08; p<0.01). No significant effects were observed for non-Medicaid beneficiaries.ConclusionsBIP contributed to a reduction in institutionalisation for individuals living alone while increasing support for family caregivers in multiperson households. These findings highlight the importance of tailoring long-term care policies to the specific needs of populations based on their living arrangements.

Introduction

In most Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries, institutional care accounts for most of total long-term care (LTC) spending.1 While older adults often prefer to age at home or in the community, several factors can pose barriers, such as the lack of home and community-based services (HCBS), fragmented care systems and the increasing unavailability of informal care providers.2 3 In response to the growing care needs of ageing populations, countries have introduced policies to rebalance LTC spending, but success has been limited. On average, approximately 38% of total LTC spending in Europe is now allocated to HCBS, with only a few countries (such as the UK, Belgium, Italy and the Czech Republic) nearing a balance between institutional and HCBS spending.1

Similarly, the USA has aimed to move away from institutional care, advancing beyond Europe by introducing policies explicitly targeting spending thresholds. Over the past two decades, several initiatives have sought to expand Medicaid HCBS, including Money Follows the Person (MFP) and 1915(c) waivers. However, these programmes had limitations. MFP primarily focused on transitioning individuals already in institutional care to community settings,4 while 1915(c) waivers allowed states with flexibility to implement HCBS but often imposed enrolment caps and waitlists, restricting access.5 Neither programme required states to shift overall long-term services and supports (LTSS) expenditures toward HCBS, limiting their impact on systemic rebalancing.

To address these gaps, the federal government introduced the Medicaid Balancing Incentive Programme (BIP), implemented from 2011 to 2015, as a key initiative to fundamentally restructure LTC delivery.6 Unlike previous efforts, BIP focused on systemic reform rather than individual transitions, requiring states to commit to infrastructure changes and increase HCBS expenditures as a share of total LTSS spending. The BIP sought to address imbalances in state LTC expenditures by enhancing the Federal Matching Assistance Percentage (FMAP), offering states additional federal funding to implement structural reforms and expand HCBS. To participate in the BIP, states had to spend less than 50% of their Medicaid LTSS funds on HCBS in fiscal year 2009. Of the 38 eligible states, 21 joined the programme, although 3 states—Nebraska, Indiana and Louisiana—later withdrew.7 Most participating states, except for Mississippi, spent between 25% and 50% of their LTSS budget on HCBS in 2009, making them eligible for the 2% enhanced FMAP. By the end of 2015, the 18 remaining states in the programme increased their average HCBS spending from 38% to 52% of total LTSS expenditures, outpacing non-participating states.7 Additional information on the institutional background of BIP is available in online supplemental file 1.

Although the programme’s success in meeting expenditure goals has been documented,6 8 its effectiveness in expanding HCBS provision remains unclear, particularly for individuals with varied living arrangements. According to family decision-making and health-production perspectives,911 living arrangement can be considered an enabling resource that changes the relative cost of care and the balance between institutional care and home and community-based care. Co-residence lowers the shadow price and coordination costs of monitoring and daily help, allowing families to substitute away from some institutional care; living alone removes this household resource, raising the effective cost of remaining at home without paid help and increasing the use of institutional care.1214 Accordingly, BIP, which reduces system-level frictions and the price of accessing HCBS, should differentially affect utilisation: for those living alone, HCBS is more likely to substitute for absent proximate support, increasing care at home and reducing institutional use; for those living with others, HCBS can often complement family caregiving (eg, respite and self-directed options that compensate relatives), sustaining community living while easing caregiver burden. While prior research has found no significant effects of BIP on overall Medicaid beneficiaries’ institutionalisation risks15 and family caregiving,16 these studies did not account for differences by living arrangements. Given that individuals living alone and those in multiperson households experience fundamentally different caregiving dynamics, failing to stratify results by household structure may obscure potential subgroup effects.

To address this knowledge gap, this study evaluates the effects of state participation in the BIP on individual LTSS utilisation, focusing on differences in living arrangements. We hypothesise that states' participation in the BIP will reduce institutional care, particularly for individuals living alone, by expanding access to HCBS. Additionally, we expect that BIP will enhance support for family caregivers of those living with others, further reducing the need for institutionalisation.

We employed a generalised difference-in-difference (DID) model,17 which is well-suited for analysing staggered implementation of the BIP across multiple time frames.17 18 We further conducted a negative control analysis to validate our findings, comparing Medicaid and non-Medicaid beneficiaries to validate that the observed effects were specific to the Medicaid population. If BIP had significant spillover effects on non-Medicaid beneficiaries, it would suggest that factors beyond the programme influenced outcomes.

Methods

Participants and data sources

Our analysis uses data from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS; 2006–2018), a nationally representative, longitudinal survey of US adults aged 50 and older.19 HRS employs a multistage area probability sampling framework, incorporating geographic stratification, clustering and the oversampling of specific demographic groups to enhance representativeness. HRS refreshes its sample by enrolling new birth cohorts every 6 years to maintain its representativeness of the ageing US population. The survey collects extensive data on demographics, socioeconomic status, health and LTC utilisation. We linked the RAND HRS Longitudinal File 2018 (V2) public-use dataset with the restricted HRS Cross-Wave Geographic Information (Detail) (1992–2018) to determine the respondents’ state of residence.20 The average response rate during the study period was 87%, with minimal variation across years. Additionally, we incorporated state-level sociodemographic characteristics from the American Community Survey (ACS) and healthcare workforce data from the Annual Social and Economic Supplement of the Current Population Survey (ASEC-CPS) to account for time-varying state characteristics throughout the study period.

Although our analysis focuses on 2011–2017, when the BIP was implemented across states, we leveraged multiple waves (2006–2018) to establish preintervention trends and track participants over time. The study included respondents from 18 states that fully participated in BIP, constituting the treatment group, and 17 eligible states that did not participate, serving as the control group. Three states (Indiana, Louisiana and Nebraska) that initiated but did not complete the programme were excluded due to small sample sizes. Medicaid enrolment status (measured at each HRS interview wave) and demographic characteristics were determined based on self-reported survey responses.

Among 14 665 older adults aged 65 or above in the HRS, 2354 individuals were enrolled in Medicaid, and 12 311 were not (table 1). Of the Medicaid beneficiaries, 1594 individuals (63%) lived in states that participated in BIP, whereas 940 (37%) lived in non-participating states. For non-Medicaid beneficiaries included in the negative control analysis, 7060 individuals (57%) resided in participating states, while 5251 (43%) were in non-participating states.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in designing, conducting, reporting on, or planning to disseminate our research.

Outcomes

Nursing home utilisation

In the HRS data, nursing home services include a wide array of care provided in residential facilities, intended for individuals needing substantial support and assistance, based on self-reported or proxy-reported data. Although Medicare covers up to 100 days of skilled nursing facility care per benefit period, long-term stays exceeding this limit are commonly covered by Medicaid for eligible beneficiaries.21 Therefore, we created a binary outcome variable indicating whether respondents used nursing home services for over 100 days in the past 2 years. Because this measure is retrospective, the outcome variable is lagged to accurately capture long-term nursing home stays.

Home care for activities of daily living

Home care refers to assistance with daily activities or functional limitations provided by family, friends or professional helpers. In the HRS, respondents report whether they received help within the last month, the helper’s relationship and whether the helper was paid. We classify home care into three mutually exclusive categories: (1) paid professional care (care delivered by paid, non-family professionals), (2) paid informal care (care delivered by family members or friends who are compensated) and (3) unpaid informal care (care delivered without financial compensation, typically by family). Using these distinctions, we construct lagged binary indicators capturing receipt of each care type in the prior month. An important qualification is that the HRS records whether paid care was covered by public insurance (Medicare/Medicaid) or other insurance but does not identify the specific payer or programme within these categories. In addition, only a limited subset of Medicaid programmes reimburses informal caregivers (especially family caregivers). Accordingly, we interpret paid care as financing-sensitive home help that may respond when BIP reduces the user cost of HCBS or expands self-direction options, and interpret changes primarily as shifts in the composition of home care rather than programme take-up. We also include unpaid informal care to capture potential substitution or complementarity with paid services (ie, whether expanded HCBS displaces unpaid family help or is combined with it to meet higher care needs).

Professional home healthcare

In the HRS, home healthcare refers to services delivered by trained professionals (eg, nurses, physician assistants, therapists and hospice staff). It excludes chore/homemaker services and does not distinguish short-term postacute rehabilitation from longer-term supportive care. Because the measure emphasises medically oriented and acute services (often covered by Medicare), this limitation may understate Medicaid BIP’s impact on this outcome. For analysis, we construct a binary indicator for any home healthcare use in the prior 2 years.

Covariates

We controlled for respondents’ self-reported age in years, sex, race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic Black/African American, non-Hispanic White/Caucasian, Hispanics or other race or ethnicity), educational attainment (less than high school, high school/GED, some college and 4 years of college or above), urbanicity (classified as urban, suburban, rural areas based on county-level population density), the Langa-Weir classification of probable cognitive impairment or dementia (normal vs cognitive impairment with no dementia vs probable dementia),22 and baseline health status, including indicators for chronic conditions (high blood pressure, diabetes, cancer, lung diseases, heart problems, stroke, psychiatric problems, arthritis) and the number of functional limitations. Functional limitations were summarised from five Activities of Daily Living (ADLs: bathing, dressing, eating, getting in and out of bed, walking across a room) and five Instrumental ADLs (IADLs: using the telephone, managing money, taking medications, shopping for groceries and preparing hot meals). Additionally, we controlled for the following time-varying state characteristics based on the ACS23 and the ASEC-CPS24: total population, sex, and racial composition, education, median household income, poverty rates, unemployment rates and LTC workforce (nurses and personal care workers providing help at home or in institutions).

Subgroups

We stratified the analysis based on self-reported household composition to examine subgroup differences by living arrangement. Respondents were categorised as living alone if they reported a household size of one and living with others if they reported a household size greater than one. This classification was derived from self-reported data in the HRS, which captures household composition at each survey wave.

Statistical analysis

The Medicaid BIP, officially authorised from 2011 to 2015, had varying implementation timelines across states. While most states began participation between 2012 and 2013, end dates ranged from 2015 to 2017, with nine states extending their participation until 30 September 2017. This staggered rollout presents an opportunity to evaluate the programme’s impact by leveraging differences in timing across states. See online supplemental file 1 for more details about the implementation timeline by state.

Given this variation in policy adoption, we employ a staggered DiD design17 to estimate the programme’s effects. This approach compares states that adopted BIP at different times (treated units) to those that either never implemented the programme (never treated) or had not yet adopted it during the study period (not yet treated), with the latter serving as the default comparison group. Including not-yet-treated states as controls allows for comparisons among states that are more similar in characteristics, helping to reduce selection bias.25 This design also ensures that changes in LTC utilisation are more likely attributable to BIP participation rather than unrelated time trends. In the main analysis, we estimate the average effect of programme participation using both not-yet-treated and never-treated states as control groups. Additionally, results using only the ‘never-treated’ states as the control group are available in the online supplemental file 2—the results from both cases were similar.

Our estimates capture the group-time average treatment effect for states at a given time, grouping them based on their initial BIP adoption period (eg, 2011/2012 vs 2013/2014 adopters). The estimated treatment effects for each group and time period are then aggregated to derive an overall estimate of the average effect of programme participation over time. We report an aggregate measure that reflects the policy intervention’s impact across multiple periods and staggered treatment adoption—specifically, the ‘average treatment effect on the treated (ATT)’. This approach aligns with event-study estimands, providing insights into the dynamic effects of BIP implementation. Further methodological details can be found in online supplemental file 3.

We assess pretreatment trends to confirm that states with different BIP implementation timelines exhibited similar LTC utilisation patterns before programme adoption, supporting the parallel trends assumption. To evaluate this, we report the average pretreatment effect alongside our ATT estimates to identify any systematic divergence between treated and control states before BIP implementation. A non-zero or statistically significant pretreatment effect would suggest a potential violation of the parallel trends assumption, indicating that observed differences in LTC utilisation may not be solely attributable to BIP participation.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted a negative control analysis to validate our findings, comparing Medicaid and non-Medicaid beneficiaries to ensure that the observed effects were specific to the Medicaid population. This approach allows us to rule out the possibility that broader state-level factors, such as simultaneous policy changes or unmeasured trends, drove the observed changes in LTC utilisation. If BIP had significant spillover effects on non-Medicaid beneficiaries who were not directly targeted by the programme, it would indicate that factors beyond BIP participation, such as shifts in overall LTC policy or market-wide changes in service availability, may have influenced the results. Conversely, a lack of significant effects among non-Medicaid individuals strengthens the validity of our causal estimates, confirming that the identified impacts on Medicaid beneficiaries are likely attributable to BIP rather than external confounding influences.

Additionally, we re-estimated the model on a restricted sample of pre-BIP enrollees (ie, individuals whose first observed Medicaid enrolment predates BIP in their state) and followed this fixed cohort through the preperiods and postperiods. Whereas the main analysis includes all Medicaid enrollees observed before and after each state’s BIP start (thereby capturing both changes in use among existing beneficiaries and potential shifts in who enters Medicaid), that approach can dilute programme effects by conflating utilisation changes with compositional changes in enrolment. The pre-BIP cohort specification serves as a sensitivity analysis to reduce composition bias and to isolate how much of the total effect reflects within-beneficiary changes.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

Table 1 presents descriptive statistics for respondents aged 65 and older from the HRS (2006–2018), stratified by Medicaid enrolment and state BIP participation.

Among Medicaid beneficiaries, demographic characteristics were largely similar between treatment (BIP-participating) and control (non-participating) states, indicating balanced baseline characteristics. Age, race/ethnicity, education, sex distribution and health conditions (including cognitive impairment prevalence, ADL/IADL limitations and number of chronic diseases) showed minimal variation; standardised mean differences were small (generally <0.10, with modest values for race/ethnicity ≈0.11 and urbanicity ≈0.13), and two-sided t-tests indicated no statistically significant differences. However, clear differences emerged when comparing Medicaid and non-Medicaid beneficiaries. Medicaid enrollees were more likely to be female (68% vs 58%) and included higher proportions of Black (35% vs 16%) and Hispanic (23% vs 5%) individuals and fewer non-Hispanic Whites (23% vs 5%). They had significantly lower educational attainment, with 57% lacking a high school diploma compared with 20% of non-Medicaid individuals. Additionally, Medicaid enrollees had higher cognitive impairment (63% vs 28%), a greater number of ADL and IADL limitations (ADL: 0.99 vs 0.23; IADL: 1.25 vs 0.37), were more likely to live alone (42% vs 28%), and had significantly lower household assets, reflecting socioeconomic disparities.

For nursing home stays of 100 days or more, BIP participation corresponds to a modest, statistically indistinguishable decrease of 1.9 percentage points (pp) among Medicaid beneficiaries overall (β= −0.019, 95% CI −0.068 to 0.031). The pattern varies by living arrangement: for beneficiaries not living alone, BIP participation shows no detectable association with long-term nursing home stay (β= 0.014, 95% CI −0.018 to 0.046), whereas among those living alone it is linked to a 5.9-point lower probability (β= −0.059, 95% CI −0.095 to –0.023; p<0.01). Average pretreatment effects hover around zero in each stratum, consistent with parallel pretrends.

Turning to paid professional home care for ADL, we find no statistically significant association between BIP participation and use of services—either overall or by living arrangement. At the aggregate level, BIP participation is associated with a 9.9-point increase (β=0.099, 95% CI −0.052 to 0.251). Among individuals not living alone, the association is negative and insignificant (β= −0.022; 95% CI −0.101 to 0.058), while among those living alone BIP participation is associated with a 5.4-point increase that is marginal (β= 0.054, 95% CI −0.002 to 0.110). In all cases, confidence intervals include zero.

For paid informal home care, while states’ BIP participation is linked to a small, statistically insignificant 1.3 pp increase overall (β= 0.013, 95% CI −0.020 to 0.045), stratified estimates reveal heterogeneity. BIP participation is associated with a 5.2-point greater likelihood of paid informal care among those not living alone (β= 0.052, 95% CI 0.025 to 0.079; p<0.01), and with a statistically insignificant 2.2-point lower likelihood among those living alone (β= −0.022, 95% CI −0.071 to 0.027). Pretreatment averages remain close to zero across groups.

Estimates for unpaid informal care and professional home healthcare services are imprecise and centred near the null. Overall, BIP participation is related to a statistically insignificant 9.3-point higher probability of using unpaid informal care (β= 0.093, 95% CI −0.109 to 0.296) and a 4.6-point decrease in the use of professional home healthcare services (β= −0.046, 95% CI −0.255 to 0.164). Stratified estimates are also small and imprecise for both those not living alone and those living alone.

Lastly, in the negative-control sample of non-Medicaid beneficiaries aged 65 and older, BIP participation is not associated with changes in LTC use (online supplemental table 1). Across outcomes, ATTs are near zero and statistically indistinguishable from zero overall and by living arrangement.

Additionally, restricting the sample to pre-BIP enrollees yields a similar pattern to the main findings (online supplemental table 2). For long-term nursing home stays, BIP participation is associated with a 6.7-point decrease among beneficiaries living alone (β= −0.067, 95% CI −0.129 to –0.006), whereas the overall effect remains small and statistically indistinguishable from zero (β= −0.020, 95% CI −0.050 to 0.011) and the estimate for those not living alone is close to null (β= 0.025; 95% CI −0.013 to 0.063). Across the home-care outcomes, point estimates are near zero and imprecise in all strata, indicating no clear changes in paid or unpaid care under the cohort-restricted specification. We note, however, that several pretreatment averages differ modestly from zero (eg, a small negative pretrend in the overall nursing-home model and positive pretrends in some home-care models), so these results should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

This study provides new evidence on how Medicaid’s BIP influenced LTC utilisation, particularly in relation to living arrangements. While no significant effects were observed for Medicaid beneficiaries overall—a finding consistent with prior research on BIP’s effects on long-term institutionalisation risks15—our results reveal meaningful differences based on living circumstances.

Several factors may explain the limited overall impact of BIP on older adults. Although BIP advanced LTSS rebalancing efforts in many states, the relatively short duration of BIP may have limited its ability to achieve substantial systemic changes in HCBS provision or to observe significant reductions in nursing home transitions.24 Systemic improvements, such as expanding services and improving access, often require sustained efforts and long-term investments that the programme’s timeline did not allow. Additionally, states have historically prioritised HCBS funding for individuals with intellectual and developmental disabilities, allocating fewer resources to older adults and those with physical disabilities—a trend that persisted during BIP.6 15 While BIP mandated infrastructure reforms to broadly support LTSS consumers, its lack of a targeted focus on older adults likely limited its ability to address their specific needs. For many older adults and their families, particularly those with severe health conditions or functional needs, nursing homes may have remained the preferred or more practical option, as HCBS often failed to adequately address their specific challenges.26

However, our analysis indicates that BIP participation significantly reduced nursing home stays for individuals living alone. This finding highlights BIP’s potential to address gaps in informal caregiving networks for this subgroup, where the absence of family caregivers often increases the likelihood of institutionalisation. By enhancing access to HCBS, BIP may have provided critical formal support that enabled these beneficiaries to remain in their homes longer, delaying or avoiding the need for nursing home care. Conversely, for those not living alone, BIP was associated with increased paid informal home care, suggesting that BIP may have supplemented existing family caregiving by offering additional paid support. This finding aligns with evidence that BIP increased the availability of professional caregiving hours while reducing family care hours, particularly from non-spousal caregivers.16 This likely provided relief for family members and enhanced their capacity to continue providing care at home.

Finally, we found that BIP participation did not significantly affect the likelihood of non-Medicaid beneficiaries entering nursing homes or receiving paid informal care, regardless of their living arrangements. This suggests limited spillover effects from the programme, as BIP primarily targets Medicaid beneficiaries, who are generally at a higher risk for institutionalisation. Moreover, this finding strengthens the robustness of our primary results, given that evidence of association for non-Medicaid beneficiaries may have indicated residual confounding bias.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The reliance on publicly available data from the HRS means that respondents were not surveyed specifically about their use of HCBS services, making the results best interpreted as intent-to-treat estimates at the population level. Second, for the nursing home outcome (over 100 days in nursing home in the past 2 years, measured with a lag), interviews conducted near a state’s BIP start date have a 2-year look-back window that may span preimplementation and postimplementation. Thus, estimates should be interpreted as average utilisation over that 2-year window, not point-in-time use. Third, we acknowledge that other policies and contextual factors may have influenced nursing home use during the study period. For instance, over the past two decades, states have increasingly adopted Managed Long-Term Services and Supports (MLTSS) programmes to deliver LTSS through managed care arrangements, aiming to improve care coordination and control costs. Although we account for state-level pre-BIP implementation trends, such as sociodemographic characteristics, economic conditions and LTC workforce data, and ensure the parallel trends assumption is met, the concurrent implementation of MLTSS and other policy initiatives during the study period may introduce confounding factors. Finally, the findings apply primarily to Medicaid-eligible older adults and may not reflect LTC use in the general population. Additionally, results may not be generalisable to non-participating states or regions with different policy environments.

Conclusions

Our findings highlight the importance of targeting LTC interventions toward vulnerable populations at risk of institutionalisation, particularly those who might benefit most from enhanced community-based services like those offered under BIP. By demonstrating that BIP reduced nursing home stays for individuals living alone and increased paid informal caregiving for those in multiperson households, this study underscores the need for tailored policy approaches that account for differences in caregiving networks and living arrangements.

Future research should examine how such programmes can be refined to meet the varied needs of Medicaid beneficiaries, considering factors such as living arrangements, access to informal support and quality of care. Additionally, further investigation into BIP-like initiatives’ long-term sustainability and effectiveness could help policymakers design more comprehensive strategies to expand HCBS and reduce reliance on institutional care.
