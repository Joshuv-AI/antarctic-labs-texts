Comparative efficacy of anteroposterior and lateral X-ray based deep learning in the detection of osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture
Authors: Chulho Kim, Minjae Kang, Woon Tak Yuh, Seung-Lee Lee, Jae Jun Lee, Jong-Uk Hou, Suk Hyung Kang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Magnetic resonance imaging remains the gold standard for diagnosing osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCF), but the use of X-ray imaging, particularly anteroposterior (AP) and lateral views, is prevalent due to its accessibility and cost-effectiveness. We aim to assess whether the performance of AP images-based deep learning is comparable compared to those using lateral images. This retrospective study analyzed X-ray images from two tertiary teaching hospitals, involving 1,507 patients for the training and internal test, and 104 patients for the external test. The EfficientNet-B5-based algorithms were employed to classify OVCF and non-OVCF group. The model was trained with a 1:1 balanced dataset and validated through 5-fold cross validation. Performance outcomes were compared with the area under receiver operating characteristic (AUROC) curve. Out of a total of 1,507 patients, 799 were included in the training dataset and 708 were included in the internal test dataset. The training and internal test datasets were matched 1:1 as OVCF and non-OVCF patients. The DL model showed comparable classifying performance with internal test data (N = 708, AUROC for AP, 0.915; AUROC for lateral, 0.953) and external test data (N = 104, AUROC for AP, 0.982; AUROC for lateral, 0979), respectively. The other performances including F1 score and accuracy were also comparable. Especially, The AUROC of AP and lateral x-ray image-based DL was not significantly different (p for DeLong test = 0.604). The EfficientNet-B5 algorithms using AP X-ray images shows comparable efficacy for classifying OVCF and non-OVCF compared to lateral images.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-79610-w.

Introduction

Osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCF) are common causes of chronic back pain in the elderly, associated with increased disability and mortality. The incidence of OVCF is rising as life expectancy increases due to improvements in healthcare access and technology, nutritional status, and preventive education for chronic diseases. This gradual increase in OVCF cases is contributing to a rising socioeconomic burden, encompassing health-related costs, familial care costs, and productivity losses1. Therefore, early detection of OVCF is crucial to prevent further complications and improve patient outcomes2.

Though spinal MRI is considered the gold standard for diagnosing OVCF, X-rays are frequently used in both emergency and routine clinical situations due to their cost-effectiveness, speed, and broad availability. Simple radiographies, including both anteroposterior (AP) and lateral views, are especially advantageous for assessing multiple fractures, which is common in osteoporosis patients. The lateral view is particularly useful for diagnosing OVCF as it offers less interference from other internal organs and better distinguishes vertebral body shape (Fig. 1). Moreover, X-ray images allow for the evaluation of the entire spine, aiding in the rapid assessment of vertebral shape changes such as wedging, height loss, or deformities.

Recently, clinicians’ decisions were improved by 7.9–22% with the help of deep learning algorythm using convolutional neural network (CNN). It is remarkable in terms of quantitatively showing the effect of CNN networks3. Machine learning (ML) technologies have been increasingly applied to medical imaging, including X-ray, CT, MRI, and DEXA, improving the diagnosis of OVCF2,4–8. Research using ML for detecting OVCF shows that its performance can be comparable to or better than specialized radiologists. Dong et al. reported a high performance of AUROC 0.99 in detecting moderate to severe compression fractures in X-rays of 4,461 patients using GoogLeNet. However, this study only analyzed lateral X-ray images, not AP X-rays. Chen et al. performed deep learning classification based on ResNet-50 to classify between acute compression fracture and non-compression fracture X-rays. After analyzing the X-rays of 1099 patients, lateral X-rays performed better than AP X-rays (AUROC = 0.83 vs. 0.77, respectively). However, this study was not designed with AP and lateral X-ray performance comparison as a primary outcome. AP X-ray imaging is routinely performed not only in patients with back pain, but also for a variety of abdominal problems such as abdominal pain, stones, post-abdominal surgery monitoring, and foreign body ingestion, allowing for opportunistic evaluation of additional spinal status. Therefore, we aim to compare predictive values of OVCF using deep learning in both AP and lateral views, with a focus on comparability in diagnosis accuracy between these views.

Methods

Participants

This is a retrospective analysis of the two tertiary teaching hospital’s image data. All experimental protocols were approved by a named institutional and informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board/Ethics Committee of Chuncheon Sacred Heart Hospital (IRB No., 2023-03-009) because we only used deidentified image data, which was performed in routine clinical practice previously. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

We initially screened patients who diagnosed with OVCF as the case group, and those who visited at spine clinic with chronic back pain as the control group from 2008 January to 2022 December. Inclusion criteria were patients aged 15–90 years with no surgery or other spinal intervention between the time of the x-ray and MRI measurements. Among these, we only included patients who were performed spinal MRI within 7 days of taking an x-ray. We excluded who performed vertebroplasty or instrumental insertion previously, and who were diagnosed with severe scoliosis, tuberculotic spondylitis, Schumann’s deformity, and ankylosing spondylitis. Finally, we excluded patients who had poor quality X-rays or MRIs, or whose medical history could not be reliably identified. The test dataset was also composed of patients with the same inclusions and exclusions from another hospital over the last three years. Both hospitals are tertiary teaching hospitals and are responsible for the final surgical care at the spinal center. Since class imbalance in DL tasks is an important issue for model development, we constructed the dataset to match OVCF and non-OVCF patients with 1:1 ratio, and the detailed flowchart is shown in Fig. 2 (Flow diagram).

Radiologic parameters

All patients who performed anteroposterior and lateral x-ray images with the following parameters: Voltage, 70-82kVp; currents, 20-22 mA. Spine MRI was performed using the Intera or Ingenia model, a Phillips 1.5 or 3.0T model, with detailed parameters shown in Supplemental Table 1.

Labeling

The presence of a compression fracture on X-ray was determined by a spinal MRI performed at the same time. We defined the OVCF as a loss of height and bone marrow signal changes on the MRI9. A spinal radiologist evaluated for a compression fracture, which was confirmed by another spinal surgeon.

Preprocessing

When opening and visualizing DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine) files directly in Python, important details may not be visible due to the absence of window settings. To address this issue, this study implemented a preprocessing procedure that adjusts pixel values using the Window Center and Window Width. The WindowCenter and WindowWidth values were extracted from the DICOM file metadata. If these values were multi-valued, the first value was used. In the absence of these values, the minimum and maximum values of the pixel array were utilized. The pixel values were then clipped within the window range: values below lwin (left window) were set to lwin, and values above rwin (right window) were set to rwin. Finally, all pixel values were adjusted by subtracting lwin, ensuring the window starts at zero.

Training deep learning model

A classification model based on EfficientNetV2-s10, one of the latest models of CNN, was trained. The training dataset was divided into 5 folds for cross-validation. To prevent overfitting and improve performance, we employed the RandAugment Technique, which randomly selects and applies 2 out of 14 data augmentations to each image11. Figure 3 visually demonstrate the data before and after applying the preprocessing methods described in the paper. The classification results from the models trained on the 5 folds were aggregated to derive the final fracture classification results. We optimized the binary cross-entropy function (BCE) using a cosine scheduler and the Adam12 optimizer, with a learning rate of 5e-5. The weights were fine-tuned from a model pre-trained on ImageNet13. The network was trained for 20 epochs per fold with a batch size of 4, and the weights were saved at the epoch with the lowest BCE loss. Training and testing were conducted using an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU. In test phase, the results were predicted by ensembling the outputs from 5 folds (Fig. 4).

Statistical analysis

Comparisons of the demographics of the training and test datasets were performed using t-test or chi-square test. The performance of deep learning classification is presented as the area under receiver operating characteristic (AUROC) curve, f1-score and accuracy in the test dataset. We performed a deep learning classification task using spinal MR images and visualized them using the t-distributed stochastic neighbor embeddings (t-sne) method. In medical image-based DL with many voxels, this method can help understand the intrinsic structure of an image through dimensional space reduction, and can help identify distinct classes or subtypes in an image dataset. We performed an AUROC comparison with the deLong test to assess whether the performances of X-ray AP images and lateral images were statistically differed.

Result

Out of a total of 1,507 patients in our hospital, 799 were included in the training dataset and 708 were included in the internal test dataset. The training and internal test datasets were matched 1:1 as OVCF and non-OVCF patients. We used a 5-fold CV in our training, where 160 patients, one fifth of the 799 patients in one of the 5 folds, were used to calculate the validation loss at each step. 708 patients were internal test datasets collected from the same hospital and not used in the training or validation process, and 104 patients were external datasets collected from different hospitals. Discussion

In this study, we aim to investigate diagnostic performance of DL algorithms in classifying OVCF and non-OVCF using AP and lateral X-ray images. Our results show that DL algorithms using x-ray images can perform well enough to predict MRI-proven compression fractures, and that the performance is comparable when predicting using only AP images rather than lateral images. While this study demonstrates that lateral images have better diagnostic accuracy than AP images in detecting OVCFs, incidental AP images, which are often acquired in situations where spinal disease is not suspected, can also be used to opportunistically assess vertebral compression fractures with relatively high accuracy.

Simple radiography is readily accessible and cost-effective. They are capable of detecting vertebral fractures by assessing changes in vertebral shape, such as wedging, height loss, or deformities. Moreover, X-rays can evaluate the entire spine, helping identify multiple fractures at once, which is especially advantageous when considering the potential presence of multiple fractures in osteoporosis patients. It is widely available, making it a convenient tool for primary care physicians, and can be performed quickly, aiding in rapid assessments in emergency situations.

Chen et al. reported that 1306 AP images were classified for OCVF using an algorithm based on ResNet-50 as a backbone and showed an accuracy of 73.59%, sensitivity of 73.81%, and specificity of 73.02%4. This is the first study to report on the usefulness of AP images for the classification task of OVCF, although the classification performance of AP images is significantly lower than our results. Another group of researchers analyzed AP images using the ImageNet-based DL algorithm and reported an AUROC of 0.80, an accuracy of 74%, a sensitivity of 80%, and a specificity of 68%14. Why is there such a difference in performance of AP X-ray images when classifying OVCFs from the others using DL strategy? First, we utilized transfer learning based on EfficientNet rather than ResNet al.gorithm. While it is well known that CNN algorithms are useful in reflecting the local characteristics of an image15, the EfficientNet al.gorithm is a multi-scaling algorithm that can simultaneously consider scaling parameters such as depth, width, and resolution16. This advantage of EfficientNet is likely to have contributed to the performance improvement rather than the disadvantage of the ResNet al.gorithm, which can fall into a local minima due to its deep depth. Second, we minimized class imbalance by matching OVCFs and non-OVCFs 1:1 ratio. Class imbalance is an issue that must be considered when training algorithms in real-world clinical situations where disease and normal conditions do not exist in a 1:1 ratio. Third, the results of the previous studies focus on the performance of the algorithm in classifying OVCFs, while our study focuses on comparing the performance of AP and lateral images, which may explain the difference. Fourth, it seems that the characteristics of the data may affect the classification performance. For example, in our external test dataset, there are more differences in age and gender between OVCF and non-OVCF than in the training dataset. Therefore, the differences in clinical characteristics of these test datasets may be partially reflected in the algorithm’s performance improvement.

The accuracy of medical imaging research through ML is getting better. In 2019, Kolanu et al. trained ML for OVCF detection using abdominal and thoracic CT images. At that time, they trained ML to detect images with more than 25% change in the vertebral height, and as a result, sensitivity was 54%, specificity was 92%, and accuracy was 83%17. In 2021, Yabu et al. conducted a study on AI for diagnosing fresh OVCFs on MRI and reported an accuracy of 88% and an AUROC of 0.94918. In this study, we used simple x-ray for the external test dataset, the results for AP view were as follows: f1 score, 0.935; AUROC, 0.982; accuracy, 0.932; and for lateral view; f1 score, 0.944; AUROC, 0.979, and accuracy, 0.941. Recent reports using only X-ray lateral images to detect OVCFs have reported AUROC or accuracy of over 90%19–21, this shows similar performance metrics to our results using lateral x-ray images. Future studies will be focused to assess if additional information such as gender, age, osteopenia, and bone mineral density can improve the accuracy of the diagnosis of OVCF.

For our AP X-ray images DL, there was not significant difference in performance when detecting OVCFs compared to lateral images, which could be associated with the following possibilities. As mentioned earlier, CNN-based models are characterized by their ability to reflect localized characteristics. AP imaging, like lateral imaging, will implicitly contain a variety of findings related to OVCF. As in studies that use X-ray images to predict bone age or osteoporosis severity22,23, DL using AP images can also perform well because AP images contain information such as bone integrity that is relevant to the occurrence of compression fractures. Second, our study excluded patients who had undergone surgery in the past, such as vertebroplasty or instrumental insertion, or who had various spinal conditions such as severe scoliosis, tuberculous spondylitis, or Schumann’s deformity. In practice, these patients are a difficult obstacle to diagnose OVCF on AP imaging, even visually, so it is conceivable that AP imaging might have improved performance in our study.

Early and accurate detection of OVCF is crucial in preventing subsequent complications such as chronic pain and reduced mobility, which significantly affect the quality of life in elderly patients. The use of X-ray-based DL strategy could potentially be implemented the diagnostic screening process in clinical settings where MRI is unavailable or impractical. This could also lead to more timely interventions, ultimately improving patient outcomes. In addition, since AP X-ray images can be taken in a variety of clinical situations other than back pain, it may be possible to utilize these images for opportunistic detection of OVCF.

Our study had several limitations. First, the retrospective nature of the analysis may be associated with selection bias, potentially influencing the generalizability of our findings. Second, the study was conducted within two tertiary teaching hospitals, which may not accurately represent the broader clinical settings.

DL models offer significant benefits for image analysis, but require large datasets for training to achieve optimal accuracy and reliability, reflecting the complexity and diversity of X-ray images. However, recent studies have demonstrated that several technical methods can improve model performance even with smaller datasets3,24,25.

Clinically, we should be careful in generalizing our findings because our study excluded patients with refracture and previous spinal surgery. Patients who were unable to undergo lateral x-rays due to systemic illnesses or deterioration of the underlying disease and only AP x-rays were excluded from the study, so it is possible that patients with multiple OVCFs were relatively excluded in our analysis.

Technically, additional models need to be refined and validated to reflect a variety of clinical situations, including patients who were excluded from the study. For examples, we excluded refracture or old compression fracture, one should be focused because they could be the pain. The former may be a cause of pain, while the latter is unlikely to cause pain. Whether this clinically important fact can be technically distinguished is a challenge that needs to be overcome through further research.

Conclusions

This study shows that AP x-ray images, when analyzed using EfficientNet-based DL algorithm, offer comparable efficacy for classifying OVCF and non-OVCF compared to lateral X-ray images. These findings suggest that AP X-ray images could be useful for the opportunistic detection of OVCF, thereby improving patient care in routine clinical practice.
