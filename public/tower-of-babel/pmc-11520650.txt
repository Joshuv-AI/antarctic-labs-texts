Nurses’ experiences in neonatal sepsis prevention and management: a qualitative cross-sectional study at selected hospitals in a metropolitan area
Authors: Kennedy Diema Konlan, Ruth Nimota Nukpezah, Abdul Razak Doat
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeonatal sepsis represents a significant cause of morbidity and mortality among infants in healthcare settings. This study explored nurses’ experiences in preventing and managing neonatal sepsis in the Tamale metropolis.MethodsAn exploratory, descriptive qualitative cross-sectional study design was used, and data was collected using semi-structured interviews. The sample size was determined at the point of data saturation of fifteen participants. Thematic analysis was employed to identify codes, subthemes, and themes within the interview transcripts.ResultsThree major challenges nurses face in preventing and managing neonatal sepsis, including (1) the absence of a protocol for handling newborns upon admission, (2) an excessive workload that made it difficult for staff to follow infection prevention protocols, and (3) inadequate, obsolete and/or lack of equipment that caused devices to be overused. The nurses also identified critical psychological and emotional difficulties they encounter as a result of working within such a stressful environment. Other activities nurses conducted were handwashing before and after every procedure, infection prevention, and separating the babies in the incubator. In managing babies with neonatal sepsis, nurses provided daily treatment, hygiene care, and supported activities of living. The nurses also assert that the availability of resources, appropriate staff, and critical skills are important for preventing and managing neonatal sepsis.ConclusionThe lack of basic working consumables like gloves, syringes, and needles, inadequate staff, and lack of defined protocol of care were identified as critical challenges that hamper the care provided in neonatal care units. Hospital authorities must focus attention on addressing the critical challenges faced by neonatal care units. Also, measures should be instituted to address the emotional and psychological concerns associated with nursing care. Future research must consider the level of influence of each of the challenges enumerated on nurses’ overall health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-11811-5.Keywords: Nurses, Neonatal, Experiences, Prevention, Management, Sepsis

Background

Neonatal sepsis, characterised as a systemic inflammatory response syndrome due to suspected or confirmed infections—be they bacterial, viral, fungal, or rickettsia—remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality among newborns globally [1–3]. Despite advancements in medical science, neonatal sepsis continues to lead to substantial illness and death within the first twenty-eight days of life, a period during which 40% of all under-five child mortality occurs [4]. In low- and middle-income countries, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, the burden of neonatal sepsis is even more pronounced. These regions account for approximately 69% of global neonatal mortality, with an estimated 380,000 to 2,000,000 cases of newborn infections annually, resulting in around 270,000 deaths [5, 6]. In Ghana, the primary causes of neonatal deaths include infections (31%), preterm births (29%), and complications following birth (27%) [7].

The transition of neonates from a sterile intrauterine environment to a diverse and dynamic microbiological milieu results in significant changes in immune function, contributing to the high prevalence and mortality rates associated with neonatal sepsis [8, 9]. This transition is particularly critical for premature neonates, whose underdeveloped immune systems render them especially susceptible to severe infections. Consequently, severe illnesses such as sepsis and pneumonia account for over one-third of the predicted 2.8 million newborn fatalities annually [10, 11]. Nurses play a critical role prevention, early detection and management of neonatal sepsis. Their constant presence with infants during the first critical hours and days after birth positions them uniquely to recognize the early, subtle signs of sepsis. Experienced nurses can intervene promptly, potentially halting the lethal progression of the condition [7, 12]. This role is, especially, important in resource-limited settings, where delays in illness recognition and intervention can be fatal.

In many low-resource settings, including Ghana, the challenges are compounded by a lack of illness recognition, delays in seeking care, inadequate access to skilled health professionals, and low-quality health services [6, 13]. The probability of a serious illness is approximately 11 times higher in high-mortality nations compared to low-mortality countries [14, 15]. Factors contributing to high case fatality rates in these settings include delayed care-seeking, insufficient healthcare infrastructure, and high treatment costs. The variability in diagnostic capabilities further impedes accurate assessment of the impact of neonatal sepsis in sub-Saharan Africa. Past sources estimate that there are 380,000 to 2,000,000 cases of newborn infection annually in sub-Saharan Africa, resulting in approximately 270,000 deaths [5]. The variability in diagnostic capabilities and incidence hinders the precise measurement of neonatal sepsis’ impact in this region. In Ghana, neonatal deaths are predominantly caused by infections (31%), preterm births (29%), and post-birth complications (27%) [7].

Despite these challenges, many neonatal deaths are preventable. Improving maternal access to skilled professional care during delivery, ensuring hygienic delivery practices and proper cord care, and facilitating immediate mother-newborn contact can significantly reduce mortality rates [15]. Continuous care in mother and child health is vital to address neonatal mortality, yet neonatal care is often overlooked in health initiatives, leading to deficiencies in care provision [11, 15]. Nurses, with their direct and sustained interaction with neonates, are well-positioned to identify early signs of sepsis and other complications. Their clinical experience and ability to take decisive actions in ambiguous situations are critical in managing infections and improving outcomes [16]. This qualitative cross-sectional study explores nurses’ perspectives, challenges, and strategies related to neonatal sepsis prevention and management at selected hospitals in a metropolitan area in Ghana.

By examining nurses’ experiences, we aim to inform policies that enhance healthcare quality for newborns with sepsis, ultimately reducing neonatal mortality rates and associated complications. Nurses’ insights are pivotal for developing effective interventions and ensuring continuous maternal and child health care, especially in resource-constrained settings where neonatal care often remains overlooked [11, 15]. The findings of this study will contribute to the formulation of targeted interventions and policies aimed at reducing neonatal sepsis and improving overall neonatal health outcomes in Ghana and similar contexts.

Methods

Research design

This study was a descriptive cross-sectional study using a qualitative research approach. This is because the study aimed to learn, more specifically, contextually, in-depth information on a particular real-world subject using a phenomenological design.

Study area

The research was conducted in the Tamale metropolis. The metropolis has two facilities, which are described as secondary and tertiary health facilities. A simple random selection method was employed to select Tamale Teaching Hospital, and Tamale Central Hospital in the Northern region. Tamale Teaching Hospital is one of the country’s five teaching hospitals and the only one in Ghana’s northern region. Tamale Teaching Hospital is found on the Tamale Yendi / Salaga route in the eastern portion of the Metropolis. In 2005, it was designated as a teaching hospital. According to the hospital’s 2017 performance rating, it has a bed capacity of 450 and provides general in-patient and out-patient care. Tamale Central Hospital is one of the several government health facilities in the Metropolis, serving the health needs of residents. Tamale Central Hospital was established as a polyclinic in April 1998. In the same year, it was elevated to the position of district hospital.

Study population

The study participants were registered nurses with at least two years of working experience in the neonatal care setting. Purposeful sampling was employed to ensure diverse perspectives. A purposive sampling technique was used to identify respondents that matched the inclusion criteria until the necessary representative sample size of 15 was obtained [17]. The researchers using the purposive sampling technique certified multiple variability sampling by ensuring certain critical characteristics of the respondents. In the neonatal care units, the researchers identified nurses belonging to various categories (auxiliary and professional), professional types (general nurses, midwives, specialist nurses), and different job ranks (staff nurses, nursing officers, and senior nursing officers). Other characteristics considered were age (ranging from 22 to 34 years), religious affiliations (Muslim, Christian, and atheist), ethnicity, and marital status (Single, and married).

Data types and sources

The interviews were conducted face to face within the health facility directly not in the presence of other nurses and patients ' relatives. All interviews were conducted in a quiet place where both the respondents and the interviewer agreed it was conducive to conducting the interviews. The respondents were encouraged to relax during the sessions. They were also informed that the sessions were to be audio recorded, and should they wish to stop the interview at any point in time, they needed to alert the interviewer. The interview sessions started with a self-introduction by the researcher to the respondents. The researcher used interview guides to lead the interviewees in in-depth, extensive discussions on the topic of discussion, usually when the respondents were less busy. The length of each interview was between ten to fifteen minutes. During the interview process, the researcher captured the interviewees’ comments using recording and note-taking as a technique. The primary data consisted of information on the ‘nurses’ lived experiences of prevention and managing neonatal sepsis at the Tamale Teaching Hospital and Tamale Central Hospital. Again, information on the structural factors, health providers’ practices, and level of prevention and management of neonatal sepsis were collected. The interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. The benefits of interviews include receiving relevant, understandable information from participants and providing opportunities for follow-up questions and deeper digging.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis was employed to identify patterns, themes, and categories within the interview transcripts. This involved coding, categorising, and interpreting the data. Two authors (RNN and ARD) independently listened to the first two interview sessions and developed separate codes. The codes were then compared and agreed upon. Where the two authors disagreed about the codes generated the third author (KDK) was invited to arbitrate. All disagreements were resolved through consensus. Before carefully analysing the data and “open coding,” the researcher read through the interview notes numerous times. Multiple researchers were involved in the data analysis process to ensure the credibility and reliability of the findings. Large amounts of data were first reduced or condensed into more digestible categories, codes, and themes through a process known as open coding [18]. The codes that result from the data analysis are shown in supplementary file 1. The stage of selective coding came next. Before relabelling and developing broader and more important themes for final descriptions and explanations, the key themes generated at the previous stage as well as the codes, were critically inspected to uncover additional parallels and contrasts [18]. Emerging sub-themes from the data were used to derive the themes. In merging the codes into subthemes and subsequently the themes the three researchers in several meetings agreed on the eventual themes. Participants were allowed to review the findings and provide feedback to validate the accuracy and authenticity of their experiences.

Ethical considerations

The ethical review committee of the University for Development Studies granted ethical clearance. All participants provided both written and verbal informed consent and were assured of anonymity and voluntary participation. The respondents were informed that they could terminate the interview at any point in time if they so wished to. Confidentiality was maintained as participant names and identifying information were not disclosed. The nurses who participated in this study voluntarily decided to participate after being informed of the research objectives. The study’s participants received no payment for their participation. After generating the codes, subthemes, themes, and supporting quotes, the transcripts and the above-listed information were returned to the respondents for verification. All the respondents [5] agreed with the codes generated and the supporting information. None of the respondents offered to withdraw any information reported to them.

Results

Demographic characteristics of respondents

A total of 15 nurses with at least two years of working experience in neonatal care settings participated in this study. The findings of the study revealed that the ages of the nurses ranged from 22 to 34 years, 22–24 years (33.3%), 25–27 years (20%), 28–30 years (20%), 31–33 years (20%), and 34 years (6.7%). The mean age was 27.33 ± 2.32 years, and the modal ages were 23, 30, and 31 years. The level of education attained by the respondents ranged from diploma (40.0%), degree (46.7%), and certificate (13.3%). Also, 46.7% of the nurses were Muslims, Christians (40.0%), and those who preferred not to disclose their religion (13.3%). Also, (40.0%) of the respondents were single, and (60.0%) were married. Most respondents had three years of work experience (33.3%). However, one [1] person each had six and eight years of working experience. The demographic characteristics of the participants are shown in supplementary file 2.

Experiences of nurses regarding prevention and management of neonatal sepsis

The findings from the data are presented under the main themes and the sub-themes and are also supported with verbatim quotations from the interview transcripts. The data from the study generated seven [7] major themes and seventeen [17] sub-themes. The themes that emerged from the analysis were (1) ‘Nurses’ experiences regarding neonatal sepsis prevention and management, (2) challenges nurses encounter in managing neonatal sepsis, (3) strategies nurses employ to prevent and manage neonatal sepsis, (4) the impact of ‘Nurses’ experiences on neonatal sepsis outcome, (5) emotional and psychological concerns of nurses, (6) improvements and recommendations in neonatal sepsis care, and (7) ethical considerations.

Nurses’ experiences regarding neonatal sepsis management

This theme presents nurses’ experiences regarding the care rendered to manage or prevent the occurrence of neonatal sepsis. These experiences highlight the nurse’s role and the protocols available in a facility for preventing and managing neonatal sepsis. The nurses had varied experiences regarding the care of neonates with sepsis as they played multiple and diverse roles while implementing already established protocols of care. The two sub-themes were (1) the role and experience of a nurse in the neonatal unit at the hospital and (2) the overview of the neonatal sepsis prevention and management protocols currently in place at the hospitals.

The role and experience as a nurse in the neonatal unit.

The role and experience as a nurse in the neonatal unit.

The roles and experiences of nurses in the neonatal care units were multiple and varied. These roles of nurses ranged from administration of medication, provision of health education, ensuring daily care needs, managing the unit, and assisting neonates and families through the activities of daily living. The type of diseases nurses managed ranged from those with respiratory distress like airway blockage, temperature regulation deficiencies like hypothermia or hyperthermia, and those with metabolic distress manifesting hypoglycaemia or hyperglycaemia. The neonatal intensive care units were identified as places for emergency and resuscitation care for preterm babies, small for gestational age (SGA), and those who have severe illnesses resulting in fever. Also, children who were SGA or preterm were cared for by nurses in these units.

“We receive babies from 0 to 28 days of life suffering from atresia, abnormal malformations, and sepsis. We also receive babies all over the northern belt, including Tamale central and west hospitals” (Resp. 1).

“My role and experience in NICU has been very interesting. Here we work in cubicles. We have four cubicles where we keep our babies. Each cubicle has its own work or management protocols depending on the conditions admitted there “. (Resp. 12).

The nurses working in the care of children were happy that they could protect and provide services to neonates at the very beginning of life. The sense of fulfilment of nurses was in tandem with their professional calling of providing care and supporting life. The general experiences of nurses working in these units were satisfaction and fulfilment.

“We do many things, preparing for the babies, serving medications, feeding the babies, and also providing intensive care” (Resp. 1).

“We take care of babies within 0 to 28 days of life, we feed the babies, we do cord care, changing their diapers, serving their medications and providing warmth for them” (Resp. 8).

b.Overview of the neonatal sepsis prevention and management protocols.

Overview of the neonatal sepsis prevention and management protocols.

The nurses provided varied and multiple services to neonates admitted to the neonatal intensive care units. An overview of the neonatal sepsis prevention and management protocols currently in place at the hospital was not specifically identified. There was no specific protocol that the respondents could readily remember, but the services they provided were almost consistently the same for all clients. However, there was some consistency in how the care of babies was conducted. The nurses could readily show that they provided consistent care even though they could not readily remember or show a written protocol. They admitted this was due to the nature and rigour of their professional training before working in the unit.

“I’d remember all, but I know that when we receive babies with high temperatures, we expose the baby and check the vital signs. We also take blood samples to do blood CS, FBC, and culture for infection. We give oxygen to babies who need oxygen” (Resp. 5).

“Yes, when we get a referral, we check vital signs, provide warmth for the baby, take samples to the laboratory, and prescribe medication. We also give oxygen to babies that need them” (Resp. 11).

Challenges nurses encounter in managing neonatal sepsis

One of the themes was the challenges nurses encounter in managing neonatal sepsis. The challenges encountered by nurses in the care of neonates with sepsis were varied. They ranged from those bordering on regulation, activities of superiors and administrators, lack and shortage of hospital supplies, uncooperative patient relatives, influences of cultural beliefs and practices, to the lack of security in the various units. The theme highlights the challenges in managing and handling neonates, showing lack of equipment and space, specific incidences of challenges, and the factors leading to the high impact of these challenges. Some participants also highlighted their specific incidents and experiences while working in the neonatal intensive care units. Three [3] sub-themes emerged and included (1) major challenges nurses face in preventing and managing neonatal sepsis, (2) specific incidents or cases that highlight these challenges, and (3) factors that contribute to the occurrence of neonatal sepsis, in your opinion.

Challenges nurses face in preventing and managing neonatal sepsis.

Challenges nurses face in preventing and managing neonatal sepsis.

Participants identified several factors that contributed to the spread of infection in the neonatal unit. The challenges ranged from overcrowding and lack of infection prevention measures to the influence of cultural practices in the care of neonates. These factors included the absence of a protocol for handling newborns on admission, an excessive number of admissions that resulted in physical overcrowding, an excessive workload that made it difficult for staff to adhere to infection prevention protocols, and a lack of equipment that caused devices to be overused. One of the codes identified in this study is nurses’ major challenges in preventing and managing neonatal sepsis.

“We do not have any written protocol in this ward. We are trained and work according to our professional practices” (Resp. 8).

Also, the continuous shortage, spoilage, and obsolescence of equipment and consumables in the neonatal care units were identified as a critical challenge in the care of neonates. The availability of logistics, personal hygiene for healthcare workers, and patient relatives, and lack of designated time for visitors were challenges highlighted by the study participants. There is a lack of logistics and equipment related to infection prevention.

“Available logistics to work with. We sometimes have to use ‘’’used’ gloves because they are insufficient. Also, hand washing for the mothers before taking their babies is a major challenge” (Resp. 14).

Another critical challenge was the interference of patients’ relatives, parents, and visitors, especially during critical periods when invasive sterile procedures are to be conducted. The birth of a child in a family setting is usually regarded as a joyous occasion. It warrants the visitation of family members to the baby and mother as they are still in the hospital. Participants had some reservations in respect of visiting time, which distracted their work and general communication with patients and relatives.

“The major challenge we have is visiting time; we tend to get a lot of visitors walking in and out of the unit who are not dressed properly (Resp. 4).

“It’s very difficult to manage them, so they even get angry and insult us for preventing them from entering the ward, and I think because we don’t have a security man is causing all this.” (Resp. 1).

b.Specific Incidents or Cases that Highlight These Challenges.

Specific Incidents or Cases that Highlight These Challenges.

Specific incidents or cases that highlight the challenges encountered by nurses in the care of patients with neonatal sepsis. Nurses were asked to share specific incidents or cases that highlight these challenges. The challenges highlighted were when patients lacked money for medical bills, inadequate staff on duty with many patients, lack of knowledge on hand washing, and uncooperative patient relatives.

“There was a case of a parent who did not have money to take care of the baby …. they couldn’t even pay for their laboratory tests” (Resp. 11).

“On this day, I was the only nurse in my cubicle, caring for 13 babies. One of the babies was having a seizure, so I ran to get the house officer, but the baby’s IV line was dislodged, and I spent hours trying to get an IV line” (Resp. 15).

Nurses reiterated the verbal and sometimes physical abuse they encounter during interaction with patient relatives while providing services at the neonatal intensive care unit. For doing the right things at the workplace, some nurses suffer verbal abuse from patient relatives. Nurses are challenged to control the inflow of patients.

“Yes, some patients’ relatives are very stubborn. When you ask them out to go and wash their hands, they become angry and even start to insult you” (Resp. 8).

“Yes, I remember one man came to visit his relative’s baby, and we were giving medications, and I said he can’t see the baby now, but this man just got angry …” (Resp. 2).

c.Factors contributing to the occurrence of neonatal sepsis.

Factors contributing to the occurrence of neonatal sepsis.

Factors contributing to the occurrence of neonatal sepsis are another major sub-theme. The nurses argued that even as neonates are in the unit, they are still prone to neonatal sepsis. This can be associated with the nature of care or how the caregiver handles the baby by implementing infection prevention measures.

“Cord infection is one of the major factors when the mothers are discharged …. they do not take care of the cord, some mothers even apply all sorts of things in cleaning the cord like toothpaste, cow-dung, etc.” (Resp. 12).

Environmental factors and visitors walking about were seen as factors increasing the occurrence of neonatal sepsis. The key factors relate to mothers not washing their hands well before breastfeeding, inadequate cord sheets, and poor personal hygiene. Cross-contamination was also revealed to be a factor that influences the occurrence of neonatal sepsis.

“Some come with the infection. Thus, babies aspirate before coming and some are acquired in the ward, sometimes we don’t wash our hands in between babies” (Resp. 9).

“Poor hand washing by mother and nurses before touching the babies. Allowing visitors to be touching the babies, wearing baby dirty cloth, putting all sorts of things on babies’ cord” (Resp. 13).

Effective strategies nurses employ to prevent and manage neonatal sepsis

Nurses working in the neonatal intensive units and facilities were engaged in diverse duties to promote childcare and welfare. Hand hygiene procedures, proper management of central venous catheters (CVC), precise diagnostic techniques, and appropriate antimicrobial drug administration are among the successful practices in minimising the incidence of infection in NICUs. Nosocomial infections continue to be a significant issue even though the incidence of infections has decreased due to the application of these measures. Hence, effective strategies nurses employ to prevent and manage neonatal sepsis are identified in this theme. The sub-themes were (1) strategies or practices nurses employ to prevent neonatal sepsis daily, (2) specific protocols or guidelines that nurses follow and communicate, and (3) collaboration with other healthcare professionals to ensure effective sepsis prevention and management.

Strategies or practices nurses employ to prevent neonatal sepsis daily.

Strategies or practices nurses employ to prevent neonatal sepsis daily.

Established effective techniques for managing and preventing neonatal sepsis generally included hand washing, barrier nursing, limiting antibiotics use, and rationalising admission to neonatal facilities. Also, other critical roles played by nurses are to ensure that children receive the required stations and to limit the number of visitors to the baby. This includes nurses’ daily routines for managing and preventing neonatal sepsis. The aim of daily activities during the care of neonates in the units is to ensure and promote a limited risk of infection.

“We make sure we do the head-to-toe examination. We examine the babies to see if there is any abnormality. We feed off the babies, change their diapers, clean the cord with spirit and cotton, and keep the babies warm” (Resp. 2).

“We wash our hands before and after every procedure, changing our gloves between babies, feeding the babies with warm breast milk, changing their diapers and serving the medications” (Resp. 1).

“We do hand washing, wearing gloves in-between babies, educating mothers to avoid taking their baby with unwashed hands. We separate the babies in the incubator, and wash their hands before and after each procedure. We change gloves in-between babies; we fed babies with warm breast milk and cleaned the cord to prevent sepsis’ (Resp. 6).

b.Specific Protocols or guidelines that nurses follow.

Specific Protocols or guidelines that nurses follow.

The actions taken by nurses ensured the articulation of consistent best-practice methods, nursing standards, guidelines, and protocols, raising the standards of nursing care delivery. They also facilitate effective staff training, improve and standardise client care, define and explain roles, legitimacy, and responsibility, give care auditing and assessment bases, and act as teaching tools.

“Yes, it is just what I spoke about in our daily work activities” (Resp. 2).

“Yes, let us start with triaging when the babies come… we have to triage to see if the baby needs emergency care or not, it’s just our normal care” (Resp 15).

If it is an emergency, you attend to the baby using the ABC airways, breathing, and circulation. The D is for disability/ dehydration, so per the quick assessment, then we will know whether the baby needs emergency care or the baby can wait for some time” (Resp 9).

c.Communicate and collaborate with other healthcare professionals.

Communicate and collaborate with other healthcare professionals.

Healthy teamwork may be promoted, decision-making confidence can be increased, and working relationships can be enhanced with effective communication and collaboration between healthcare professionals. The nurses assert that they are the pivot for this teamwork by incorporating and liaising with all other professionals to ensure the baby gets the required care. The interaction between healthcare professionals greatly influences successful clinical outcomes and job satisfaction.

It’s our daily duty to ensure that the parent takes the blood sample to the lab and follows up on the result. Also, our daily duty is to administer all prescribed medication and other Interventions for the baby, but house officers are always around with us” (Resp. 13).

“We work as a team with the other healthcare professionals. We work well and communicate well” (Resp. 14).

“We work as a team together. We share ideas for the management of the babies. The only problem is when new house officers come, we try to work with them even though we have challenges.” (Resp. 15).

Impact of nurses’ experiences on neonatal sepsis outcome

Another theme identified by the participant was the impact of nurses’ experiences on neonatal sepsis outcomes. The experiences of nurses mainly served as resources and efficient use of limited resources to ensure maximum impact in the care of babies with neonatal sepsis. Also critical was the training of nurses for efficient patient care. Another important experience of nurses was their ability to effectively collaborate with hospital authorities, ward-in-charge and supervisors to ensure they effectively managed children with neonatal sepsis. Three [3] main sub-themes were (1) resources or tools available to assist in preventing and managing neonatal sepsis, (2) training or continuing education related to sepsis prevention, and (3) support nurses have from supervisors or the hospital administration in addressing sepsis-related challenges.

Resources or tools available in preventing and managing neonatal sepsis.

Resources or tools available in preventing and managing neonatal sepsis.

The study sought any resources or tools available to assist nurses in preventing and managing neonatal sepsis. The nurses had mixed reactions about the experiences of resources or tools available in preventing and managing neonatal sepsis. As acknowledging the presence of the tools, many still identified its relative absence or obsolesce. All the participants confirmed the availability of oxygen cylinders and incubators for the babies. However, nurses identified that these and the equipment were not adequately available to meet the number of babies in the unit.

“Yes, we don’t have enough, but the oxygen cylinder, incubators, etc. are there to help in our work” (Resp. 7).

“Yes, hmmm, mostly the syringe and needle are available, but the cannula we write for them to go out and buy” (Resp. 9).

“Hmmm, sometimes we have everything available, other times we have to write for the parents to go out and buy everything, even gloves. We had a few monitors, incubator, and warmer working well” (Resp. 1).

Our insufficient staff strength makes the work difficult” (Resp. 13).

b.Training or continuing education related to sepsis prevention.

Training or continuing education related to sepsis prevention.

The study asked the participants whether they received any training or continuing education on sepsis prevention and management. The nurses confirmed they received training or continuing education on sepsis prevention. Training and continuous education introduce new concepts, spurring nurses to investigate them further or demonstrate and encourage the practice of actual methods.

“Yes, we do. Even among the staff, we sometimes organise seminars and workshops” (Resp. 1).

“Yes, we receive training, we have workshops and other pieces of training” (Resp. 3).

“Yes. We have workshops and other training programs on preventing and managing sepsis” (Resp. 11).

c.Support nurses receive in addressing sepsis-related challenges.

Support nurses receive in addressing sepsis-related challenges.

All the nurses had varied responses regarding the support they received from supervisors or the hospital administration in addressing sepsis-related challenges. Nurses felt that these supports were not adequate to meet the needs of the babies who were admitted to the unit. Nurse revealed that they do not have all the support needed to carry out their work effectively. Other challenges were inadequate logistics and tools.

“As for the hospital administration from the top, they make sure we have the logistics to work with, and our supervisors make them available for use in the ward” (Resp. 8).

“No, we don’t have everything, but our person in charge makes sure to release the things needed to work for us. Those that are not available, we write for the parents to go outside and buy” (Resp. 4).

“Our supervisors try to provide and make available all the logistics needed for the work to go on” (Resp. 5).

“We don’t have any source of motivation; our supervisors try to make available all the logistics to work within the hospital. They try to get us the things to work with and encourage us to work hard” (Resp. 9).

Emotional and psychological concerns of nurse

Nurses in neonatal care units must work in an environment in the healthcare industry that is complicated and stressful. Patients’ conduct and complaints, nurses’ responsibility for the health and wellness of others, and dealing with death and injuries all contribute to the stress that nurses experience. Two (2) sub-themes emerged: (1) the emotional challenges of nurses in the care of neonatal sepsis and (2) Specific incidents or situations related to the care of babies with neonatal sepsis.

Emotional Challenges of the nurse in the care of neonatal sepsis.

Emotional Challenges of the nurse in the care of neonatal sepsis.

Both internalising behavioural issues, such as emotional reactivity and anxiety/depression, as well as externalising behavioural issues, such as oppositional and violent behaviour, appeared to be associated with caring for children with neonatal sepsis. It was shown that dealing with neonatal sepsis can be emotionally challenging. After expected cases of neonatal sepsis, nurses talked about how concerned they were, but with other staff members’ support, they felt more optimistic about the cases they were managing. The nurses identified that they were challenged in managing their emotions, especially when it is related to neonatal sepsis. Most nurses show much compassion and understanding to their patients during treatment.

“We develop a passion for the work, but sometimes when the logistics to work with are not available and a baby is struggling to survive, and there is nothing …, then you become very sad and breakdown” (Resp: 4).

“Hmmm, we sometimes feel very sad or bad when we lose a baby” (Resp: 9).

“Hmmm, working in NICU can be very trying, too close to babies and all of a sudden one dies it’s very sad” (Resp. 15).

b.Specific incidents or situations related to the care of babies with neonatal sepsis.

Specific incidents or situations related to the care of babies with neonatal sepsis.

Another sub-theme that emerged was specific incidents or situations related to neonatal sepsis. All the nurses had reactions as far as specific incidents or situations related to neonatal stress are concerned. The nurses highlighted specific experiences they identified while caring for a case of neonatal sepsis.

“It took time, so when the light went off for almost an hour when the light came back, we lost a baby, and we were all sad, but what can we do about that” (Resp. 10).

“Yes, I remember there was an emergency with a dehydrated baby, and we needed to check the RBS, but we didn’t have the strips, so I wrote for the mother to go out and buy it. Before she could come, we lost the baby” (Resp. 6).

“… a mother fed a baby with breast milk, and he aspirated, but she lied about feeding the baby, and before we could know, the baby passed away. We were all very sad we could have saved her if she told us the truth” (Resp. 5).

Again, a participant stated: “Yes, this woman had three children, and her husband was not in town. She had to be sleeping in the hospital with her three other children because there was no one home to take care of them. (Resp. 1).

“… We were nursing this baby for almost one month. We all got used to the baby, and he was getting better until one day, I went for the afternoon schedule and was told the baby had passed. I felt like crying; it was very sad for all of us” (Resp. 1).

Improvements and recommendations in neonatal sepsis care

Improvements and recommendations for preventing and managing neonatal sepsis were other themes identified. This theme focuses on the suggestion or advice that is offered to help make the management of neonatal sepsis better. The critical actions for improving the care of neonatal sepsis were ensuring improved attitudinal changes, increasing and allocating resources to the neonatal care units, including staff, fostering collaboration between team members, improving training, and increasing the skills of nurses and other professionals in the neonatal care units. Two sub-themes were identified: (1) improvements or changes to enhance neonatal sepsis prevention and (2) management practices and specific resources, training programs, or support systems that would be beneficial.

Changes to enhance neonatal sepsis prevention and management practices.

Changes to enhance neonatal sepsis prevention and management practices.

Another sub-theme that emerged was improvements or changes suggested to enhance neonatal sepsis prevention and management. Nurses described how educating pregnant women could help manage and prevent neonatal sepsis cases. The care rendered to pregnant women at the antenatal clinics should emphasise the need for infection prevention, especially for the unborn child, and how to manage and cooperate with care if the baby is admitted to the neonatal care unit. Personal hygiene, effective hand washing, barrier nursing, efficient breastfeeding, bathing, and appropriate use of clothing are some of the critical care procedures expected to be implemented by mothers to prevent neonatal sepsis and ensure effective management.

“We need to educate the mother on prevention and how to take care of the cord, this education should start from ANC; the mothers should be told how to manage aspiration, and also, mothers should wash their hands with soap and water before touching their babies” (Resp. 14).

“We need more logistics to help us do the work effectively” (Resp. 1).

“We also need a security man at the door to prevent visitors walking in and out of the ward, and we do more education mostly at the ANC for mothers to know the preventive measures” (Resp. 9).

b.Specific resources, training programs, and support systems to manage neonatal sepsis.

Specific resources, training programs, and support systems to manage neonatal sepsis.

The nurses also assert that the availability of critical resources, appropriate staff and critical skills are important for caring for, managing, and preventing neonatal sepsis. In-service training for nurses, improved education, and specialisation in neonatal care were critical interventions that can improve the care of babies in the units. Also, assisting nurses to attain the skill of using interpretation data from specialised equipment was another important intervention to improve the care of babies with neonatal sepsis. Media campaigns and presentations are usually designed to reach specific target groups and achieve certain goals, such as increasing awareness. Hence, nurses believe these activities will help manage and prevent neonatal sepsis.

“Yes. Like educating mothers on the care of their newborn, what they should do to avoid sepsis. We also have radio presentations and education at ANC” (Resp. 6).

“Language barriers are a key problem. I don’t understand Dagbani, so it’s very difficult to explain some things to the mothers who can’t understand English or Twi” (Resp. 13).

“Yes, Education, workshops for mothers, radio presentations, and TV shows can be beneficial to help manage the situation” (Resp. 4).

Ethical considerations to manage neonatal Sepsis

Ethical consideration in managing neonatal sepsis was another theme identified in the study. Ethical considerations in managing neonatal sepsis refer to a set of principles that guide nurses and other health professionals in treating and managing neonatal sepsis cases. Usually, these principles include informed consent, anonymity, confidentiality, and communication results. Two sub-themes were identified: (1) parents or guardians of the neonate’s involvement in the prevention and management of neonatal sepsis and (2) confidentiality and privacy of the neonates and families.

Parents/guardians of the neonate’s involvement in the prevention of neonatal sepsis.

Parents/guardians of the neonate’s involvement in the prevention of neonatal sepsis.

All the participants educated parents and guardians on the needs and care of the baby. The extent of involvement of other family members was identified as a critical concern in the care of babies with neonatal sepsis. In critical situations, important ethical dilemmas involve the extent of disclosure of diagnosis and the level and depth of information that can be divulged to family members and sometimes even spouses. Nurses stated that they highlight the importance of breastfeeding to mothers and the need to constantly do so.

“We educate the parents and guardians on the needs and care of the baby” (Resp. 8).

“Educating the parents on the condition of their new babies and informing them of the interventions the whole team is taking or planning for their baby, so that they are also involved in the care of their baby, hence when they are discharged, they can manage the baby at home” (Resp. 2).

“Yes, by involving the mother in the care, how to hold the baby when feeding and cord care -cleaning the cord with alcohol, allowing it to dry, and fall off” (Resp. 11).

Young patients, kids, and babies cannot offer their consent for medical care in addition to having the right to privacy and secrecy. Nurses posited that the right to confidentiality includes the restricted use and sharing of personally identifiable information and access to medical records. During consultations, tests, treatments, and medical care, the right to privacy protects confidentiality, privacy, and dignity.

“Mostly we assess on the first day and then the parents depending on the finding and the state of the baby whether they will make it or not, but we call them aside into a room to counsel them” (Resp. 1).

“We call the parents privately to explain to them and reassure the mother” (Resp. 1).

“We mostly ensure confidentiality by counselling the parents on the condition of their babies. We do that by calling them privately into a room and talking about very sensitive information” (Resp. 5).

“We do that by keeping patients’ information private and not discussing it at our nurse’s station” (Resp. 10).

Discussion

This study assessed nurse’s experience in the management and prevention of neonatal sepsis using a qualitative research approach. Using a qualitative research approach was critical as it elucidated the important concerns and factors associated with caring for neonates diagnosed with sepsis. The perspectives and experiences of nurses directly influence the depth and nature of care usually rendered to critically ill neonates [19]. The nurse experienced various feelings, behaviours, and perspectives in the current study. The varied emotional reactions concerned nurses caring for babies with neonatal sepsis [14, 19, 20]. Neonatal care nurses provide care to infants with diverse conditions like congenital disabilities, surgical complications, and preterm birth in the first month after birth [21, 22]. Considering the daunting nature of nurses’ experiences in these units, pragmatic measures are instituted to address nurse’s emotional and psychological needs. This is particularly important because providing round-the-clock care to neonates and preterm or critically ill patients requires specialised skills, emotional stability and motivation [16]. The care rendered by nurses to neonates was comprehensive and encompassed all facets of human development. It was also identified that newborn care responsibilities included feeding and diaper changing, inserting intravenous lines, collecting samples for laboratory testing and administering medication, among other procedures [23, 24]. Conducting all these roles for neonates, preterm and sometimes SGA babies increases the work demand.

The challenges nurses face in preventing and managing neonatal sepsis were identified to be several and multiple. The interactions between these factors aggravate the already impoverished situation associated with neonatal care in low-resource settings. The challenges encountered by nurses included but were not limited to the absence of a protocol for handling newborn care needs, an excessive number of admissions resulting in physical crowding, excessive workload, continuous shortage of basic consumables, uncooperative patient relatives, interruption of care by patient relatives, and lack of equipment. These similar factors that influence the care rendered to neonates were espoused elsewhere [7, 15, 25], indicating the difficult working conditions of nurses. Not addressing these challenges may lead to the increased incidence of neonatal infection, prevalence of complications, effects on nurse health and generally poor quality of life. When neonatal care is not critically prioritised with several challenges, there is an increased risk of complications [26, 27].

The nurses identified that the prevalence of infection in NICUs is reduced by using hand hygiene practices, environmental control, effective venous catheter maintenance, accurate diagnostic techniques, administration of antimicrobial drugs, control of visitors, basic child hygiene, and proper education of mothers. Nurses must institute pragmatic measures to promote infection prevention, reduce complications, and provide adequate care to neonates [28, 29]. Visitors touching babies, assisting babies wearing diapers, babies in dirty clothing, and inappropriate cord care were identified as measures to avoid. This is because neonatal sepsis arises when pathogenic microorganisms enter the bloodstream, causing devastating systemic infection within the first 28 days [12, 30, 31]. Also, birth asphyxia, prematurity, low birth weight, and other factors such as delivery settings, type of delivery, antenatal care received, newborn mixed feeding, and some cultural practices for the cord were believed to contribute to the incidence of neonatal sepsis [10, 32]. Therefore, nurses in the direct care of neonates must identify and address these measures of infection transmission. This will require diverse strategies that ought to be tailored to clinical protocols. The strategies or practices nurses employ to prevent neonatal sepsis daily include efficient hand washing, barrier nurses, efficient education of mothers, and avoiding overcrowding in neonatal care units. These measures can be reconstituted into clinical protocols for caring for neonates with sepsis. Nurses must practice tested strategies like hand washing, barrier nursing, controlled use of antibiotics, and streamlining admissions to neonatal wards to curtail neonatal sepsis [33, 34]. Using protocols and clinical scientific measures facilitates effective staff training, improves and standardises patient care, defines and explains roles, legitimacy, and responsibility, gives care auditing and assessment bases, and acts as teaching tools [35–38]. Guidelines and protocols are valuable tools for fostering quality improvement and developing evidence-based practice, and they provide the best evidence to guide healthcare decisions [39, 40].

Another experience of nurses was the efficient collaboration and teamwork with and between other professionals facilitated by nurses. The role of nurses in this regard promoted healthy teamwork, effective decision-making, increased confidence, improved working relationships, enhanced communication, and collaboration between professionals. These are critical tenets for improved patient care, increased quality of services, and increased client outcomes [5, 40]. To have sustained cooperation and coordination of the clinical team, there has to be some form of consistency in nurses’ training [9, 17, 41]. Professional and continuous in-service training helps nurses adapt and overcome the stress associated with service delivery in neonatal care units. Neonatal care units are difficult to work in, demanding, and stressful by nature. Healthcare professionals, especially nurses, feel stressed due to their duty to others’ health and well-being, dealing with patients’ behaviour and complaints, and handling deaths and injuries [11]. The psychological health of nurses may have a negative knock-on effect on the calibre of services rendered [42]. It has been estimated that a significant number of neonatal and paediatric healthcare professionals [42, 43] report having high levels of anxiety, depression, psychosomatic symptoms, post-traumatic stress symptoms, and emotional exhaustion. Immediate measures must be instituted to address the psychosocial concerns of nurses, especially those working in neonatal care areas.

Strengths and limitations

This is one of the first studies in the northern part of Ghana that used in-depth qualitative methods to assess nurses’ experiences regarding neonatal sepsis. The study highlighted the individual, institutional and systematic measures that influence the care of babies with neonatal species. However, inherent in this study was that the interviews were conducted by nurses in the same professional category. The interviewer and the assistant had the same professional background as the respondents. Also, because of the cross-sectional nature of the study that required data collection at just a point in time, and no follow-up required it could have been influenced by social desirability bias. However, the researchers ensured critical methodological rigour to influence the impact of these biases. Consequently, we recommend that future studies could benefit from incorporating various methodologies like participant observation.

Conclusion

Improving neonatal sepsis outcomes does not only rely on better hospital protocols, resources, and staff training but also heavily depends on educating and involving parents in neonatal care. Incorporating parental roles in infection control and emphasizing this partnership could enhance the practical application of measures to improve the care of babies with neonatal sepsis. In addition, we recommend that healthcare providers should prioritise developing and implementing clinical protocols for newborn care. Measures must be instituted to address the emotional and psychological challenges encountered by nurses in neonatal intensive care units. Also, the availability of critical resources, appropriate staff and critical skills are important for caring for, managing, and preventing neonatal sepsis. In-service training and workshops on continuous professional development should cover the needs of nurses working in the neonatal intensive care units to facilitate effective team dynamics. Future research can focus on identifying the level of influence of each challenge experienced by nurses in providing care to neonates in care units.
