A low-power buffer-assisted 14T ternary SRAM
Authors: Shams Ul Haq, Erfan Abbasian, Abdolreza Darabi, Deepika Bansal, Sajad A Loan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTernary static random-access memory (TSRAM) has emerged as a promising solution for enhancing energy efficiency and information density beyond binary limits, particularly in data-intensive and low-power applications. This paper presents a power-efficient single-supply 14-transistor CNTFET-based ternary SRAM cell that uses a buffer-based topology with a single-bitline read/write scheme. The proposed design eliminates the need for dual supply rails and redundant voltage-division paths, significantly reducing dynamic power and delay while maintaining robust stability. Extensive HSPICE simulations using the Stanford CNTFET compact model demonstrate superior performance compared to state-of-the-art TSRAM cells, achieving the lowest power–delay product of 5.37 aJ at 0.9 V, along with a competitive static noise margin under process variations. To evaluate practical effectiveness, the proposed TSRAM is integrated into a ternary medical image processing framework using hardware-based signal mapping and a weighted k-nearest neighbor classifier. Application-level results show a 26.65% reduction in average energy consumption, a peak signal-to-noise ratio of 41.06 dB, a mean structural similarity of 99.83%, and a prediction accuracy of 97.86%. A comprehensive figure of merit confirms a 61.58% improvement over existing designs, highlighting the proposed architecture as a strong candidate for energy-efficient ternary biomedical processing systems.Keywords: CNTFET, Ternary logic, Ternary buffer (TBUF), Ternary SRAM (TSRAM), Static noise margin (SNM), Image processingSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Nowadays, digital image processing plays an important role in people’s daily lives as well as in many fields of science and technology, and it has countless applications in medical engineering, pattern recognition, robotics, satellites, and industrial systems, where memory architectures serve as fundamental building blocks1–3. On the other hand, in digital signal processing applications, due to the lower accuracy and the possibility of data loss in binary logic–based image processing, image processing using the concept of ternary logic—by increasing the gray levels of images (from binary to ternary)—has been introduced by researchers as a general solution with a significant data-preservation approach2,4,5. This improvement in preserving fundamental data can be highly important for designing more efficient binary-logic circuits with sensitive applications, such as in the field of bioelectronics and particularly in the processing of bioimages6,7.

Ternary (three-valued) logic extends binary logic by using three discrete signal levels (commonly encoded as 0, 1/2 VDD, and VDD or as symbols {0,1,2}), which increases information density per digit (log₂3 ≈ 1.585 bits per trit). The logic reduces the interconnect and gate count for certain arithmetic and memory operations compared with strictly binary implementations8. At the memory level, storing trits instead of bits increases information density and can decrease the number of memory accesses and peripheral overhead for a given information throughput, which often translates to lower energy per computation in memory-dominant applications9. In hardware, ternary circuits are realized using specialized ternary inverters/buffers, voltage-division or multi-threshold techniques, and three main cell-architecture families have emerged for ternary SRAM (TSRAM) cells: TBUF-based, cross-coupled STIs, and cycle-operator topologies; each topology trades off area, SNM, and peripheral complexity10. Practically, many designs use either a dual-supply scheme or precise threshold/voltage-divider networks to generate the intermediate logic level, which creates key design challenges for level restoration, read/write stability, leakage paths, and sensitivity to process, voltage, and temperature (PVT) variations11. Carbon nanotube (CNT)-based field-effect transistors (CNTFETs) are especially attractive for ternary implementations because their threshold voltages (Vth) can be engineered via nanotube diameter and chirality, enabling compact multi-threshold devices and reliable intermediate-level generation at lower supply voltages while offering high carrier mobility and near-ballistic transport for improved energy efficiency12. Consequently, CNTFET-based TSRAM designs reported in the literature demonstrate promising reductions in cell area and dynamic energy per stored trit but also highlight the need for careful device sizing, sense-amp/bitline schemes, and layout techniques to preserve SNM and tolerate soft errors—motivating ongoing work on CNTFET-aware cell topologies and peripheral circuits that balance power, stability, and manufacturability11. Additionally, integration of CNTFET-based TSRAM into larger systems will require tight co-design of peripheral circuits, standard-cell libraries and sense/restore schemes to ensure cell-level benefits scale at the array and system level13.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows. First, a single-supply buffer-based 14T CNTFET ternary SRAM cell is proposed, which employs a unified read/write single-bitline architecture and eliminates the need for auxiliary voltage rails, thereby reducing power consumption and circuit complexity. Second, an energy-efficient ternary buffer with controlled voltage-division is introduced to reliably generate and retain the intermediate logic level, achieving a low power–delay product while maintaining competitive static noise margin and improved tolerance to process variations. Third, a comprehensive circuit-level comparison against state-of-the-art TSRAM designs is carried out under identical simulation conditions, demonstrating superior power, delay, and PDP characteristics.

Finally, the proposed TSRAM is validated in a near-realistic application framework by integrating it into a ternary medical image processing system. This application-level framework is based on the developed hardware and a supervised learning weighted k-nearest neighbor (KNN) algorithm14. Using this hardware-algorithm co-design, the pixels of medical images are mapped to a signal composed of ternary pulses with three voltage levels, which are applied at the transistor level using the concept of the piecewise linear (PWL) function2. Circuit-level simulation results show a 26.65% reduction in the hardware efficiency factor for the proposed TSRAM architecture with the best quality, compared to other designs under the same simulation conditions. Additionally, the accuracy evaluation of the proposed memory structure based on the KNN algorithm demonstrates a prediction accuracy of 97.86%. A unified figure of merit, including circuit-level efficiency, quality, and accuracy metrics, is further employed to fairly assess cross-layer performance. The results confirm the effectiveness of the proposed design for energy-efficient ternary computing applications, indicating that the proposed architecture achieves an optimal trade-off between energy, quality, and accuracy and is a suitable candidate for ternary logic-based medical image processing.

Prior efforts on designing TSRAM cells

This section reviews prior research efforts dedicated to the design of TSRAM cells. A foundational approach employed by several early researchers involves using cross-coupled standard ternary inverters (STIs) as a data latch, complemented by separate reading and writing paths to enhance stability. For instance, Lin et al.15 implemented this architecture in an 18-CNTFET cell. This design was later echoed by Cho et al.16, who proposed two similar TSRAM cells utilizing 18 and 20 CNTFETs, and by Shrivastava et al.17, who developed a variant employing 17 CNTFETs. A critical drawback shared by these cells is their core STI circuit, which relies on a pair of transistor-based diodes to perform voltage division for generating logic ‘1’. This mechanism inherently leads to increased power dissipation and signal delay. Subsequent work has sought to optimize this STI configuration. Shrivastava et al.18, for example, proposed two TSRAM cells with topologies similar to Cho et al.16 but with a modified STI. Their design incorporated an additional diode pair in parallel with the existing one to reduce the logic ‘1’ delay. However, this improvement came at the cost of a higher transistor count, increasing the total to 22 and 24 CNTFETs. Meanwhile, other researchers explored different circuit elements. Srinivasu et al.19 proposed two types of TSRAM: one based on dual-VDD cycle operators using 20 CNTFETs, and another based on a single-VDD TBUF using 16 CNTFETs. The first cell requires an additional VDD/2 power supply, leading to greater design complexity, routing challenges, and higher power consumption. The second cell, while avoiding a dual supply, still employs a diode pair within its TBUF for voltage division, consequently increasing the power and delay for logic ‘1’. Similarly, Gadgil et al.11 proposed TBUF- and cycle operator-based cells employing 14 and 18/21 CNTFETs, which also utilize an additional voltage rail (VDD/2)—a feature that contradicts a primary goal of multiple-valued logic (MVL) circuit design, which is to reduce hardware complexity.

More recent designs continue to navigate these trade-offs. Shams et al.20 introduced a TSRAM cell based on back-to-back connected STIs, consisting of an NTI, PTI, and a couple of always-on transistors forming a voltage divider. This cell, which uses 15 CNTFETs and separates the read and write paths, doubles the number of voltage divisions during logic ‘1’ generation, resulting in higher power dissipation. Another cell proposed by Shams et al.21 is based on a dual-VDD TBUF and employs shared reading and writing paths, reducing the transistor count to just 10 CNTFETs. While this achieves a notable reduction in component count, the need for an additional VDD/2 power rail reintroduces increased design complexity. Building upon the landscape of single-VDD TSRAM designs, two other notable cells based on the TBUF were proposed by Rahbari et al.22 and Darabi et al.2. A key commonality of these cells is their use of a shared read/write access path and a single BL structure, which simplifies the interconnect but requires careful internal control of logic levels. The cell from22 uses a combination of NTI and PTI. To successfully write a logic ‘1’ using a single supply voltage, it incorporates transistor-based resistors that act as a voltage divider, thereby setting the correct middle-level voltage. This design is implemented with 12 CNTFETs. In contrast, the cell from2 employs a more complex approach to achieve greater stability and noise margin. It also uses NTI and PTI, but enhances them with n-type and p-type transistor stacking, which helps reduce leakage current. Furthermore, instead of simple resistors, it uses a complementary pseudo-resistor network biased with a reverse gate voltage, providing a more robust and controlled voltage level. This advanced technique comes at the cost of a higher transistor count, requiring 14 CNTFETs. Moving to a different architectural approach, a single-VDD TSRAM cell based on cross-coupled STIs was proposed by Abiri et al.23. This design, which uses 16 CNTFETs, departs from the shared-path model by employing differential reading and writing structures. This differential approach can offer better speed and noise immunity. A key technique in this cell is its method for resolving the output logic state: it uses voltage division, where the final output signal is derived from the average of the NTI and PTI outputs, ensuring the correct logic value is presented.

Proposed ternary SRAM design

The TSRAM cell introduced in this work is built around the proposed TBUF circuit, a transmission gate (TG), and a bitline (BL). As shown in Fig. 1, the transistor-level design of the TBUF circuit utilizes unary operators (NTI and PTI) and a power-enhanced voltage division technique to achieve a logic ‘1’ level. The input signal and output signal are referred to as In and Out, respectively. The operation of the proposed TBUF is illustrated in Fig. 2, with its behavior in different states described as follows. In = logic ‘0’ (see Fig. 2a): Transistors C1 and C3 turn on, pulling both NTI(In) and PTI(In) to a logic ‘2’. This activates C7 and C8, connecting the Out to ground to establish a logic ‘0’. In = logic ‘1’ (see Fig. 2b): Transistor C2 turns on, pulling NTI(In) to logic ‘0’, while C3 remains on, keeping PTI(In) at logic ‘2’. The voltage at In also directly activates C9 and C10. Consequently, the Out is simultaneously connected to VDD and GND through the C5-C9-C11 and C8-C10-C12 paths, respectively. This creates a voltage division, resulting in an intermediate logic ‘1’. The always-ON transistors C11 (in the pull-up network) and C12 (in the pull-down network) function as resistors for this division, also reducing the on-current to lower circuit power dissipation. In = logic ‘2’ (see Fig. 2c): Transistor C4 turns on, pulling PTI(In) to logic ‘0’, while C2 remains on, keeping NTI(In) at logic ‘0’. This activates C5 and C6, which connect the Out to VDD, establishing a logic ‘2’.

Transistor-based structure of the proposed TBUF circuit.

Status of the proposed TBUF circuit when (a) In = ‘0’, (b) In = ‘1’, and (c) In = ‘2’.

Figure 3 shows the output response of the proposed TBUF circuit from a transient simulation performed at a VDD of 0.9 V. As Fig. 3 demonstrates, the proposed TBUF circuit functions correctly across all defined states. Leveraging the proposed TBUF circuit, we constructed the TSRAM cell shown in Fig. 4a. The core of the cell is the TBUF (transistors C1–C12), configured with its input and output connected to the storage node (Q) to form a positive feedback loop. Access to this node is managed by a TG composed of transistors C13 and C14, which connects Q to the BL. The TG is controlled by the wordline (WL) and its complement (WLB), enabling all read and write operations through a single BL to minimize power consumption. This structure offers a key advantage over the conventional TSRAM design, which uses cross-coupled STIs. The conventional cell requires two separate voltage divisions to maintain a logic ‘1’, inherently leading to high short-circuit power dissipation—a drawback our TBUF-based design effectively avoids. Figure 4b presents the layout of the proposed TSRAM alongside the conventional 6T SRAM cell for comparison. Both layouts, created using based on the CAD electric-VLSI design tool and λ rules-based the technology-independent mocmos-cn library24,25 with only one polysilicon layer and two metal layers for estimating the percentage of area, and have successfully passed design rule check (DRC) and electrical rule check (ERC), where the tool supports the generation of HSPICE/VerilogA netlists. The proposed TSRAM cell measures 140λ × 78λ, compared to 94λ × 40λ lambda for the 6T cell. Consequently, the TSRAM occupies an area approximately three times larger than its conventional counterpart. The proposed TSRAM cell has three operational modes hold, read, and write. Each mode is detailed in the following explanation.

Hold mode

Data retention is ensured by pulling WL low and WLB high, which switches off the TG and disconnects the BL from the TBUF circuit. The previously stored value at node Q is maintained through the feedback path with good SNM. The SNM of the proposed TSRAM at 0.9 V is depicted in Fig. 5. Here, SNM0, SNM1, and SNM2 represent the SNM values while storing logic ‘0’, ‘1’, and ‘2’, respectively. The overall SNM of the circuit is taken as the minimum of these values. As shown in Fig. 5, the proposed TSRAM achieves an SNM0 and SNM2 of 0.226 V, and an SNM1 of 0.219 V, resulting in a final SNM of 0.219 V.

SNM of the proposed TSRAM at 0.9 V.

Read operation

The read operation is performed by precharging the BL to VDD, as shown in Fig. 6. When WL goes high (logic ‘2’), the TG switches on. Between 0 and 2 ns, node Q stores logic ‘0’, causing the BL to discharge through the TG to complete the read ‘0’ operation. Between 2 and 4 ns, node Q stores logic ‘1’. Once WL is activated, the BL charges from the previous logic ‘0’ to logic ‘1’ via the TG, completing the read ‘1’ operation. The read ‘2’ operation follows the same principle (Fig. 7).

Read operation in the proposed TSRAM cell at 0.9 V.

Write operation in the proposed TSRAM cell at 0.9 V.

Write operation

The complementary signals WL and WLB are used to control the two transistors (C13 and C14) of the TG. The trit (‘0’, ‘1’, or ‘2’) to be written to node Q is placed on the BL, and then WL is asserted. This switches on the TG and transfers the trit to node Q. The buffer maintains the data at node Q through positive feedback, latching the TBUF into one of three storage states: (‘0’ → ‘0’), (‘1’ → ‘1’), or (‘2’ → ‘2’).

For example, if node Q initially stores logic ‘0’:

- To write logic ‘1’, BL must be set to logic ‘1’ and WL raised to VDD. The TG transfers this value to the NTI and PTI inputs, producing outputs of ‘0’ and ‘2’, respectively.

This activates C5 and C8. The voltage division network through transistors C5-C9-C11 and C8-C10-C12 pulls node Q to 0.45 V (logic ‘1’) through voltage division.

- To write logic ‘2’, BL must be set to logic ‘2’ and WL raised to VDD. The TG transfers the value, causing both NTI and PTI outputs to become ‘0’. This activates C5 and C6, connecting node Q to VDD.

- To write logic ‘0’, BL must be set to logic ‘0’ and WL raised to VDD. The TG transfers the value, causing both NTI and PTI outputs to become ‘2’. This activates C7 and C8, connecting node Q to ground. The complete write operation, shown in Fig. 8, confirms the correct functionality of the proposed TSRAM cell (Fig. 9).

Results and discussion

Simulations employed the Stanford University CNTFET compact model, which characterizes enhancement-mode, unipolar devices of MOSFET-type operation26,27. Furthermore, the effects of layout geometry and fabrication process information, such as parasitic effects that emerge after physical layout, have also been extracted from the output netlist of the CAD electric-VLSI graphical tool for other layouts and incorporated into the simulations. The simulated performance metrics of the proposed TSRAM design are benchmarked against existing state-of-the-art TSRAM cells, such as the designs presented in2,17,18,20,22,23. All TSRAM designs were designed and simulated using 32-nm CNTFET model with the parameters outlined in21 at operating voltage of 0.9 V and room temperature.

A comparative summary of these results is provided in Table 1. The proposed TSRAM cell consumes least write and read power, measuring 178.77 nW and 180.33 nW, respectively. For all the designs, the power used to calculate the PDP is the average of the read and write power. Therefore, the average power of the proposed TSRAM cell is 179.38 nW. The TSRAM design in17 has an average power consumption of around 365 nW. Its dual-BL scheme results in double the bitline toggling and a sense amplifier, which roughly doubles the dynamic power. The TSRAM design in18 consumes an average power of 555 nW. Its additional mid rail and multiple bitlines lead to nearly three times the power of the proposed cell. The TSRAM design in20 consumes about 254 nW, which is fairly efficient but still around 1.4 times that of the proposed cell; their double division write and separate BLs incur extra leakage. The TSRAM in22 consumes an average power of 458 nW. The design in22 suffers from leakage through the constant-divider resistors. Its larger supply voltage margin results in higher swing current. The proposed TSRAM design uses complementary WL/WLB to isolate these paths, reducing leakage. It avoids using stacked transistors like those in design2, which lowers dynamic energy. By using a single BL and TG, the proposed TSRAM cell roughly halves power consumption. Overall, the proposed architecture’s single-bitline read/write path and careful transistor sizing produce the lowest power consumption among all designs.

To further quantify the performance advantages of the proposed TSRAM, relative improvements with respect to the best reported state-of-the-art designs are evaluated. Comapred to the the design with lowest write power20, the proposed design achieves 12.44% lower power. Also, the proposed design consumes achieves 40.7% reduction in read power compared to the design with lowet read power. The proposed design achieves a 57.7% reduction in PDP compared to the most efficient prior work20, which represents the most significant improvement among all metrics. In addition, the proposed cell provides a 14.5% reduction in average write delay and a 29.8% reduction in average read delay compared to the fastest existing design17.

This difference is primarily attributable to topology and sizing choices. Our TBUF uses always-ON transistors and parallel voltage-division paths (C11/C12 acting as resistive elements) to robustly create/hold the mid-level, which improves SNM and write/read speed but introduces a static leakage path between VDD and GND in the stored- ‘1’ condition. In contrast, the design in18 employs an alternate STI/TG-based arrangement and read/write isolation that reduces DC short paths in standby. Compared with general STI/STB-based designs, our lower leakage stems from using one voltage division (TBUF) and complementary WL/WLB isolation rather than multiple, simultaneous voltage-dividers and extra precharged bitlines that create DC short paths in hold. The proposed 14T TSRAM has the lowest write delay of 0.0342 ns. The TSRAM design in17 has a write delay of 0.040 ns. The proposed cell is quicker because it uses a single bitline and one TG access, while the design in17 employs cross-coupled inverters with separate read and write bitlines and additional TGs to manage bitline swing. This requires the design in17 to carry out two voltage divisions to write a “1,” which slows down the write operation. In contrast, the proposed design’s TBUF core with positive feedback directly drives Q. WL/WLB enables just one TG to inject the trit on one BL, which shortens the write path and cuts the delay. The proposed cell is more than twice as fast as the TSRAM design from18, which has a write delay of 0.073 ns. It depends on extra mid-VDD generation and implied dual-bitline operations. This adds capacitance and requires a two-step write operation. By avoiding a second supply/rail and using just one bitline, the proposed cell roughly cuts the write delay in half. Similarly, the STI-based design from20 shows a write delay of 0.048 ns; this design uses separate read and write paths along with two serial divisions for logic ‘1’. In contrast, the proposed TSRAM’s core buffer and TG inject data in one step. The buffer-based design from22 has a write delay of 0.0597 ns. It shares a bitline but uses internal transistors as resistors to create VDD/2 for writing ‘1’. This method adds extra RC delay. The STB-based cell design in2 is even slower at about 0.069 ns. This slowdown occurs because its multiple TGs increase effective resistance during the write operation. The proposed cell’s design avoids stacked “leaky” paths, resulting in a write path with lower resistance. The 16 T differential cell design in23 writes in about 0.052 ns. This design uses two bitlines and a comparator/averaging stage, which effectively doubles the capacitance. In contrast, the proposed cell toggles a single bitline with one TG, achieving around 35% faster write times. The TSRAM design in17 has a read delay of 0.043 ns. The proposed TSRAM’s single bit line read path pre-charges BL to VDD, then WL activates to turn on TG and discharge when Q equals 0. This path is straightforward. In17, the read operation uses a ternary sense amplifier on dual bit lines, which adds both precharge time and sense delay. The additional TGs and sense circuitry make the design in17 slower. The design in18 has a read delay of 0.125 ns; it uses two bitlines and mid-level generation, which leads to significant delay. The TSRAM in20 has separate BLs and a slower CNTFET drive. The proposed TSRAM’s single read path, along with complementary WL and WLB, allows it to discharge BL to threshold almost twice as fast. The design in22 has a write delay of 0.0597 ns. It shares a bitline but uses transistor-based resistive division for logic ‘1’, which causes the bitline to rise more slowly to VDD/2 when reading “1.” The proposed cell quickly identifies all three levels by directly connecting Q to the bitline through TG and allowing a strong TBUF drive to finish the operation, reducing the read delay. The TSRAM design in2 has a read delay of 0.079 ns and uses stacked CNTFETs and pseudo resistors, which slow down both the pull-up and pull-down of the bitline. The proposed cell avoids long series stacks, allowing its bitline to swing faster. The design in23, which has a read delay of 0.073 ns, uses dual bit lines and differential sensing. This extra capacitance and logic nearly double the read time. With just one TG and one bitline, the proposed cell completes the read much sooner. The SNM1 of various TSRAM cells has been compared because the TSRAM is most vulnerable to noise during the Write ‘1’ operation.

The proposed design offers an SNM1 of 0.219, which is second only to the design in23. The TBUF-based cell has much steeper voltage-transfer curves; its cross-coupled loop (C7, C12) provides strong regeneration. The TSRAM in17 uses standard cross-coupled inverters, which results in weaker feedback. The design in20 has an SNM of approximately 0.150 V, while even the cell in22 has an SNM1 of 0.215 V. The SNM1 of the proposed TSRAM slightly exceeds the value from22 due to balanced transistor sizing and a single-supply design, eliminating the need for a VDD/2 rail. The TSRAM cell in2 has a very uneven SNM, with the worst case being about 0.102 V, as it utilizes two current paths and pseudo-resistors. A statistical evaluation of the SNM1 is conducted to assess the robustness of the proposed TSRAM cell under process variations. A total of 1000 Monte Carlo simulations is performed, considering the dominant CNTFET process parameters, namely channel length, gate oxide thickness, CNT pitch, CNT diameter, and CNT density. Each parameter is independently modeled using a Gaussian distribution with a ± 15% variation at 3σ21.

Figure 8 depicts the resulting statistical distributions of SNM1 for the proposed design and previously reported TSRAM cells. It is observed that designs employing larger CNT diameters exhibit increased SNM1 variability due to the heightened sensitivity of threshold voltage and carrier transport characteristics to diameter-induced fluctuations. In particular, the design reported in16 demonstrates the highest normalized variability, which can be primarily attributed to its larger CNT diameter. Other reference designs exhibit moderate variability depending on their CNT diameter selection and device configuration. By contrast, the proposed TSRAM cell achieves a lower normalized SNM1 variability (σ/μ) while maintaining a competitive mean SNM1 value. This improvement is mainly attributed to the adoption of a uniform and optimized CNT diameter, along with a variation-aware cell topology, which effectively mitigates the propagation of process-induced fluctuations. Consequently, the proposed design demonstrates enhanced stability and reliability under realistic process variations, making it a suitable candidate for low-voltage and variation-sensitive memory applications.

Biomedical image processing: quality assessment and accuracy estimation

In this section, to investigate the effectiveness of other ternary memory architectures in a realistic environment, the efficiency and accuracy of these structures in ternary logic concept based-medical image processing using hardware and KNN algorithms have been evaluated. In this regard, the standard medical database, the VIA/I-ELCAP lung database (Vision and Image Analysis Group/International Early Lung Cancer Action Program)28, and each computer tomography (CT) image selected from the database, with a resolution of 512 × 512, served as input data. Similar to the works in29–32, the dataset with publicly accessible capability has been selected from CT-scan images from 10 different groups in the W0011-W0020 repository from Ref.28 (each image from a different group of the ELCAP database). For qualitative evaluation, the structures have been assessed based on the storage of numerical data corresponding to each pixel of the stored medical images, according to the hardware algorithm developed in reference2. Additionally, following reference33, to investigate the functional accuracy coefficient of the structures, the modified KNN algorithm (i.e., weighted KNN14,34) has been used, employing the concept of Hamming distance and the correct prediction of the output medical image stored with the test dataset.

Conclusion

This study investigated the effectiveness of a buffer-based 14T CNTFET ternary SRAM cell through combined circuit-level and application-driven evaluation. To emulate a near-realistic usage scenario, hardware-based algorithms were employed to convert pixel-wise medical image data into ternary voltage pulses and apply them directly to transistor-level memory circuits. Circuit performance metrics were extracted from HSPICE simulations, while image quality and functional accuracy were evaluated in MATLAB using a supervised weighted KNN framework, enabling consistent cross-layer assessment of different TSRAM architectures. The results indicate that the proposed memory structure maintains stable ternary storage with low delay and power consumption while preserving image fidelity during large-scale data storage and retrieval. Compared with existing TSRAM designs, the proposed architecture demonstrates a 26.65% reduction in average energy consumption and achieves high-quality image reconstruction, with a PSNR of about 41 dB and an average structural similarity of nearly 99.83%. In addition, a prediction accuracy exceeding 97.86% confirms reliable ternary data retention at the application level. By using a comprehensive figure of merit that simultaneously considers hardware efficiency, quality, and accuracy, the proposed design shows a 61.58% improvement over comparable architectures. These results highlight the suitability of the proposed TSRAM for energy-constrained, ternary logic-based on-chip medical image processing systems, where efficiency and data integrity are equally critical.
