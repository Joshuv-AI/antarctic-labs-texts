The Quest for Longevity in Pulmonary Valve Replacement: Translating Experimental Insights Into Surgical Excellence
Authors: Han Ki Park
Published: 2026

Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) remains one of the most common cyanotic congenital heart diseases. Over the past several decades, advances in cardiac surgery have transformed the prognosis of these patients: the vast majority of individuals undergoing corrective repair survive into adulthood and enjoy a good quality of life. However, in a substantial proportion of patients, right ventricular outflow lesions such as pulmonary valve regurgitation or stenosis lead to progressive right ventricular dilatation, ventricular dysfunction, tricuspid regurgitation, arrhythmias, and even sudden cardiac death.1) Consequently, pulmonary valve replacement (PVR) has become an increasingly frequent and indispensable component of lifelong TOF management. Numerous studies have demonstrated that timely PVR results in significant reductions in right ventricular volume, improvements in symptoms and exercise capacity, and favorable clinical trajectories.2),3) Because many patients require valve replacement during adolescence or young adulthood, the durability of the implanted prosthesis and the avoidance of repeated reinterventions have become central clinical challenges. This challenge raises two fundamental questions: What is the optimal valve substitute for the pulmonary position, and how should the right ventricular outflow tract be managed throughout a patient’s lifetime? While laboratory investigations continue to pursue the development of ideal prosthetic valve materials, equally important progress must occur at the bedside and in the operating room, where implantation strategies and surgical judgment directly shape long-term outcomes.

Bioprosthetic valves are now widely favored in the pulmonary position. The low-pressure, low-flow characteristics of the right-sided circulation predispose mechanical valves to thrombosis, necessitating lifelong anticoagulation, whereas tissue valves provide acceptable durability and eliminate the need for anticoagulation. In the contemporary era—when percutaneous pulmonary valve implantation is increasingly feasible—the preference for bioprosthetic valves has become even more compelling. Nevertheless, the limited durability of bioprosthetic valves remains an unavoidable reality, and many patients will require multiple valve replacements during their lifetime. Decades of advances in tissue preservation chemistry, leaflet biomechanics, and bioengineering have substantially improved structural durability, yet surgical decision-making continues to play a decisive role. Careful valve selection and meticulous management of right ventricular outflow tract pathology are essential components of successful long-term care.4) Long-term observational studies suggest that porcine bioprostheses may exhibit lower rates of structural valve degeneration than bovine pericardial valves,5),6) underscoring the continuing importance of evidence-based prosthesis selection.

In this issue of the Korean Circulation Journal, Kwak and colleagues7) present an important and clinically relevant study examining the effect of implantation angle on the durability of bioprosthetic pulmonary valves in patients with TOF. By analyzing computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, and angiographic data from 36 patients, the investigators quantified the implantation angle and demonstrated that valves positioned between 30° and 45° were associated with significantly lower failure rates. Their findings highlight a frequently underappreciated aspect of PVR: valve orientation. Because patients with repaired TOF often lack a well-defined native pulmonary annulus, surgeons have considerable flexibility in prosthesis positioning; however, this flexibility introduces variability in the valve orientation. Proper alignment, avoiding lateral tilt and ensuring posterior orientation along the natural course of the pulmonary artery, may be more important than previously recognized.

It is intuitively reasonable to assume that valves operating under more favorable hemodynamic conditions will maintain function longer. This hypothesis has been explored using computational fluid–structure interaction models. Ko and colleagues8) investigated pulmonary arterial hemodynamics and leaflet dynamics of prosthetic pulmonary valves with varying orientations and demonstrated that off-axis valve orientation produces higher pressure forces and greater energy asymmetry, factors that may adversely affect long-term valve durability. The report by Kwak and colleagues7) now provides clinical confirmation that these biomechanical insights are not merely theoretical but directly relevant to surgical practice.

Ultimately, the pursuit of the “ideal valve” does not end with material science or device engineering. Even the most advanced prosthesis cannot compensate for suboptimal implantation. The durability of pulmonary valve replacement depends not only on the valve implanted but also on how it is implanted. The work by Kwak and colleagues7) serves as an important reminder that surgical precision—thoughtful positioning, careful alignment, and meticulous technique—remains one of the most powerful determinants of long-term success. In the quest for longevity in pulmonary valve replacement, innovation must continue in the laboratory, but excellence must ultimately be delivered in the operating room.
