Initiation of immediate postpartum family planning and its determinants among women delivering in health facilities: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Uhawenimana Thierry Claudien, Muhayimana Alice, Ndikumana Edouard, Mbabazi Eugenie, Nsengiyumva Richard, Murekezi Josephine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe immediate postpartum period represents a critical yet underutilized opportunity to prevent unintended and closely spaced pregnancies, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. In Rwanda, despite high rates of facility-based delivery, initiation of contraception before hospital discharge remains suboptimal. Evidence on how sociodemographic characteristics, childbirth-related factors, male partner presence during childbirth, and health provider practices jointly determine immediate postpartum family planning (IPPFP) uptake is limited. This study assessed IPPFP uptake, examined male partner involvement, and identified associated factors.MethodsThe study was conducted in the maternity units of Muhima District Hospital and King Faisal Hospital in Kigali, Rwanda. Quantitative data were collected from 480 women aged ≥ 18 years within 96 h postpartum. Qualitative in-depth interviews were conducted with five male partners to explore perspectives on postpartum contraceptive decision-making. Immediate postpartum family planning was defined as self-reported initiation of a modern contraceptive method before discharge. Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and regression analysis, and qualitative data were analyzed thematically to complement quantitative findings.ResultsOverall, 60.6% (291/480) of women initiated a contraceptive method before discharge, with intrauterine devices being the most commonly chosen method (33.7%). Male partners were present during childbirth for 48.3% of women; however, partner presence was not significantly associated with IPPFP uptake (χ² = 1.409, p = 0.262). Qualitative findings from a small exploratory sample suggest that contraceptive decisions were largely made during pregnancy, driven by considerations such as maternal health, parity, and household economic capacity, rather than male partners’ physical presence at childbirth.ConclusionsIPPFP uptake in these two urban Rwandan hospitals was moderate. Initiation of contraception before discharge was associated with selected sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics, although these associations were based on unadjusted analyses. Male partner presence during childbirth was not statistically associated with IPPFP uptake, and qualitative findings suggest that contraceptive decisions are often made earlier in pregnancy. Strengthening postpartum counseling and clinical assessment may improve informed decision-making. However, findings should be interpreted in light of the study’s limited scope and design.Keywords: Immediate postpartum family planning, Male partner involvement, Rwanda, Contraception uptake, Maternity care

Introduction

The immediate postpartum period represents a uniquely critical yet frequently underutilized opportunity for preventing unintended and closely spaced pregnancies. Globally, an estimated 121 million unintended pregnancies occur annually, many of which arise during the postpartum period when sexual activity resumes in the absence of effective contraception [1]. These pregnancies contribute substantially to maternal morbidity and mortality, particularly in LMICs [2]. In sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), where fertility rates remain among the highest globally and maternal mortality is disproportionately elevated, low uptake of postpartum contraception continues to undermine reproductive health gains [3].

IPPFP, defined as the initiation of a modern contraceptive method before discharge following childbirth [4, 5], is recognized as a high-impact and cost-effective intervention for reducing preventable maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality [6, 7]. The World Health Organization recommends a minimum interpregnancy interval of 24 months to optimize maternal and child health outcomes, a target that is difficult to achieve without timely postpartum contraceptive initiation [8]. Scaling up IPPFP directly contributes to several Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) by reducing maternal deaths, preventing unsafe abortions, strengthening women’s reproductive autonomy, and mitigating the socioeconomic consequences of poor birth outcomes [9–12].

Despite these commitments, uptake of immediate postpartum contraception remains uneven across SSA, even in settings with high rates of facility-based delivery. Postpartum modern contraceptive use in low-income countries in the region remains suboptimal, leaving a substantial unmet need during the critical first year after childbirth [13, 14]. This gap perpetuates rapid repeat pregnancies, increases demand for unsafe abortion, and contributes to elevated maternal and infant morbidity and mortality. Family planning has been credited with contributing to nearly 40% of the reduction in maternal mortality observed in developing countries, highlighting its central role in maternal health improvement [13, 14]. Integrating family planning into maternity care before discharge therefore represents a strategic opportunity that may reduce preventable maternal deaths while leveraging routine contact with women during childbirth.

Missed opportunities for postpartum contraceptive initiation during maternity care remain common and are driven by multiple factors, including inadequate counseling, limited method availability, provider bias, insufficient clinical assessment, misconceptions regarding postpartum fertility, and sociocultural norms that constrain women’s decision-making [13, 15]. Increasing attention has also been given to male partner involvement as a potentially influential factor through shared decision-making, emotional support, and facilitation of access to services [16, 17]. However, evidence on the association of male partners during childbirth with women’s immediate postpartum contraceptive initiation remains inconsistent and context-specific [18–20].

Rwanda has achieved substantial progress in expanding access to maternal health services and modern contraception, characterized by high antenatal care attendance and facility-based delivery rates [20]. Nonetheless, unmet need for contraception during the postpartum period persists, and delayed initiation after childbirth remains common. Evidence indicates that immediate postpartum contraceptive initiation before discharge remains limited, despite the near-universal contact between women and health services at delivery.

Several studies conducted in Rwanda have examined immediate postpartum family planning utilization, primarily focusing on prevalence and individual-level determinants such as age, education, parity, and counseling exposure [4, 21, 22]. While informative, these studies were geographically limited and did not comprehensively examine childbirth-related contextual factors, including mode of delivery or male partner presence during labor and delivery. Other studies have assessed postpartum contraceptive use beyond the immediate postpartum period or focused on specific subpopulations or methods, such as postpartum intrauterine contraceptive devices [23–25]. These approaches do not fully capture women’s contraceptive decision-making at the point of childbirth among those delivering live births.

Moreover, although male partner involvement is increasingly recognized as an important determinant of reproductive health behaviors, evidence from Rwanda remains limited and fragmented. Existing studies often rely on indirect measures such as spousal communication or general partner support, without explicitly examining male partners’ presence and participation during labor and delivery and how this may influence women’s immediate postpartum contraceptive decisions [19, 26–28]. In addition, few studies have jointly assessed health provider practices—such as counseling on method options, mechanisms of action, side effects, contraindications, and clinical eligibility—in relation to contraceptive initiation before discharge, despite these being core components of quality postpartum family planning services [3].

Accordingly, a critical evidence gap remains regarding how sociodemographic characteristics, childbirth-related factors (including mode of delivery), male partner presence during childbirth, and health provider practices interact to determine women’s initiation of immediate postpartum family planning in Rwanda. Addressing this gap is essential for informing the integration of high-quality, rights-based family planning services into maternity care and for advancing national and global goals related to maternal health, gender equality, and sustainable development.

This study aimed to assess the uptake of immediate postpartum family planning among women delivering in health facilities in Rwanda, examine the extent and nature of male partners’ presence and participation during childbirth, and identify factors associated with women’s initiation of contraception prior to hospital discharge. By addressing contextual, relational, and health-system factors simultaneously, this study provides novel, policy-relevant evidence to strengthen postpartum family planning programming and accelerate progress toward the Sustainable Development Goals.

Methodology

The research was carried out in the maternity units of Muhima District Hospital and King Faisal Hospital; both located in Kigali City. The selection of these two healthcare facilities was based on practical considerations such as financial feasibility and ease of access to the study population.

Study design

A convergent mixed-methods design was adopted, where quantitative and qualitative data were collected concurrently and integrated during interpretation. The quantitative component measured prevalence and associated factors of IPPFP, while the qualitative component explored male partner perspectives to explain observed statistical patterns. A quantitative design alone was insufficient to explain underlying reasons for IPPFP uptake; therefore, qualitative interviews were used to explore decision-making processes and contextual factors.

Study population

The research sample consisted of women whose partners were present during the process of labor and delivery in specific healthcare facilities that allowed fathers to be physically present in the labor and delivery room. Women whose male partners were not present during labor and delivery were also included. Furthermore, it was a requirement for the women to have given birth within 96 h. For the qualitative component, five male partners of postpartum women were purposively selected to provide in-depth perspectives on family planning decisions.

However, certain criteria were used to exclude certain individuals from the study. Women who were below 18 years of age at the time of the study and were present in the facilities where the study was conducted were not included. Additionally, women whose newborns passed away after childbirth or whose babies were admitted to neonatal intensive care units were also not selected for the study.

Sampling procedure

The research employed a combination of purposive and convenient sampling techniques. Purposive sampling was utilized to select two specific hospitals, namely Muhima District Hospital and King Faisal Hospital. While both hospitals permit the presence of men during childbirth, King Faisal Hospital stands out for actively promoting male involvement in the birthing process. This is primarily due to the provision of a supportive environment within their labor and delivery wards, which surpasses that of Muhima District Hospital. On the other hand, convenient sampling was employed to choose women who would participate in the interviews. This sampling strategy was pragmatic rather than representative and was not intended to generate population-level estimates.

The qualitative component involved five male partners, a sample size determined based on the principle of information-rich cases rather than statistical representation. For the qualitative component, five male partners were purposively selected based on their availability and willingness to participate, as well as variation in their presence during childbirth given the Rwandan culture and context regarding men’s attendance at childbirth. They were selected as information-rich cases for exploratory qualitative inquiry. The study did not aim to achieve thematic saturation but to provide illustrative perspectives that complement quantitative findings. This small sample was intended to complement quantitative findings by providing contextual insights into contraceptive decision-making.

Data collection process

A face-to-face survey was conducted to collect quantitative data from eligible women who had delivered in the study facilities. The questionnaire was administered in person within 96 h postpartum to women present in the maternity wards at Muhima District Hospital and King Faisal Hospital between 15th May 2022 and 30th January 2023.

The study aimed to collect information on family planning initiation postpartum. Immediate postpartum family planning initiation was operationally defined as self-reported initiation of any modern contraceptive method prior to discharge from the health facility following childbirth. Male partner participation was assessed based on women’s self-reported perceptions of their partner’s emotional, physical, and decision-making support during labor and delivery. Responses were categorized as none, low, average, or sufficient. These categories were subjective and not derived from a validated measurement scale.

Initially, the survey tool was designed in English, but a Kinyarwanda version was also available for mothers who were unable to understand and respond to the questions in English. The questionnaire consisted of four sections: sociodemographic information, information about the obstetric history of the current pregnancy, male partners’ presence and level of participation, and information about immediate postpartum family planning initiation. The questionnaire did not comprehensively capture several potential confounding variables, including antenatal family planning counseling exposure, prior contraceptive use, partner communication prior to delivery, and facility-level readiness (e.g., availability of trained providers and contraceptive methods).

Before commencing the data collection process in the field, the research team ensured the face and content validity of the data collection tools. This involved a thorough review and proofreading of the questionnaire to identify any missing variables. Additionally, the team assessed whether the formulation of the questions aligned with the objectives and expected outcomes of the study. This quality control process aimed to ensure the reliability and accuracy of the collected data. A pretest was conducted to ensure that the questionnaire was comprehensible. Following the pretest, minor revisions were made to improve clarity of wording and sequencing of questions.

For the qualitative component, in-depth interviews (IDIs) were conducted with the five male partners between 15th March 2023 and 15th April 2023. Interviews were semi-structured, allowing flexibility to probe participants’ experiences and perceptions regarding:

Interviews were conducted in a private setting, audio-recorded with participants’ consent, and later transcribed verbatim for analysis.

Two data collectors were hired and trained to administer questionnaires and conduct interviews with recently delivered mothers. To ensure the credibility and reliability of the data, the collectors were registered midwives with experience in both academia and clinical settings. The data collection process was designed to minimize disruptions for the mothers, taking into consideration their convenience. Additionally, a pretest was conducted with five mothers to refine the study materials and ensure their accuracy. To maintain the participants’ honesty and confidentiality, the interviews were conducted in separate and private settings. Furthermore, daily checks were performed to ensure that the questionnaires were filled out accurately and without any errors that could compromise the quality of the collected data.

Data management

At the conclusion of each day, the data collection assistants ensured the safe delivery of all completed questionnaires to the project team in order to mitigate any potential data loss. The collected questionnaires and qualitative data were securely stored in a locked filing cabinet. The electronic data obtained from this study were exclusively shared among the project investigators and were stored in a cloud-based system called “OneDrive,” which required a password for access.

Data analysis

The data obtained through questionnaires were entered into the statistical software SPSS, version 25, for further analysis. In order to provide a comprehensive overview of the participants’ sociodemographic and obstetric history information, descriptive statistics were computed. Additionally, the association between the physical presence of partners during labor and delivery and the adoption of family planning was assessed, along with the identification of obstetric and demographic factors that could potentially influence couples’ initiation of contraception in the immediate postpartum period. To achieve this, chi-square tests and bivariate analysis were performed. It is important to note that the level of statistical significance was determined at a p-value of 0.05, ensuring that any observed associations or relationships were statistically meaningful. Furthermore, a multivariate logistic regression model was then fitted to identify independent predictors of immediate postpartum family planning initiation as these variables are known to influence postpartum contraceptive uptake through differences in access to information, socio-economic status, cultural norms, and healthcare utilization. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed to estimate the strength and direction of associations. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Qualitative data were analyzed using an inductive thematic analysis approach, allowing themes to emerge directly from participants’ narratives rather than being predetermined by existing frameworks. Audio-recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim and translated from Kinyarwanda into English. The transcripts were repeatedly read to ensure familiarity with the data before coding began.

Coding was conducted manually by two independent researchers to enhance analytical rigor. Initial codes were generated independently and then compared through discussion to resolve discrepancies and reach consensus, thereby strengthening inter-coder agreement. Codes were subsequently grouped into broader themes that reflected patterns in male partners’ perceptions of childbirth experiences and decision-making regarding postpartum family planning.

NVivo software was not used; instead, manual coding and organization of data were performed using structured matrices in Microsoft Word and Excel. To enhance credibility, the researchers engaged in regular peer debriefing sessions throughout the analysis process. However, member checking was not conducted due to logistical constraints, which may limit confirmability of the qualitative findings and is acknowledged as a study limitation.

The qualitative findings were used to complement and contextualize the quantitative results, particularly in explaining why male partner presence during childbirth was not significantly associated with immediate postpartum contraceptive uptake.

Ethical consideration

The current study involved human participants and was conducted according to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [29]. Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Rwanda IRB (Approval Notice: No 240/CMHS IRB/2022), followed by institutional permission from King Faisal Hospital. Prior to commencing the study, informed consent was obtained from each individual participant, who were fully informed about the study’s objectives. To ensure confidentiality, participants’ identifying information was kept anonymous by assigning them unique codes. The data collected was securely stored and only accessible to the researchers involved in the study.

Bivariate analysis: Factors associated with women’s choice of family planning during the immediate postpartum

However, there was no statistically significant difference between women whose partners attended childbirth and those whose male partners were not present, suggesting that the presence of male partners did not influence the women’s decisions regarding the method of family planning to use in the immediate postpartum period. Qualitative findings reinforced the quantitative result that male partner attendance at childbirth is not significantly associated with immediate postpartum contraceptive uptake. Across five interviews, participants emphasized that family planning choices were typically made during pregnancy, prior to labor or delivery, and were guided by broader considerations such as the mother’s health, existing number of children, and household financial capacity.

One male partner who did not attend childbirth reflected:

“The absence of male partners does not necessarily affect a couple’s decision to use contraception immediately postpartum. Decisions regarding family planning are often made during pregnancy, prior to labor, rather than as a result of attending labor or a cesarean section.” – Male partner 5.

Similarly, another participant stated:

“My absence during childbirth does not significantly influence our family’s decision to use immediate postpartum contraception…Even if I were present, I believe it would not strongly affect the decision, as family planning choices are typically made at home.” – Male partner 1.

While some participants noted that witnessing complicated or cesarean deliveries could make men more aware of the physical and emotional challenges of childbirth, this experience was described as a secondary influence, rather than a decisive factor for immediate postpartum contraceptive uptake.

As one participant explained:

“Male partners’ presence at childbirth may not significantly influence the couple’s choice of immediate postpartum contraceptive use, except possibly in cases of complicated deliveries…Witnessing labor and birth, however, does not appear to directly affect couples’ decisions regarding immediate postpartum contraception.” – Male partner 2.

Participants generally reported the importance of pre-labor planning and education, highlighting that informed discussions during pregnancy were more critical for initiating contraception than physical presence at birth.

Supporting this, a participant noted:

“Physical presence during childbirth can influence men’s perspectives, making them more supportive of family planning. Health care providers should proactively discuss contraceptive intentions and options with couples. While witnessing childbirth may motivate uptake, informed guidance from doctors and midwives remains crucial in supporting timely family planning decisions.” – Male partner 3.

Predictors of women’s initiation of family planning during the immediate postpartum

As presented in Table 6, women employed in the public sector were significantly more likely to initiate immediate postpartum family planning compared to those in other occupational categories (adjusted odds ratio [AOR]: 1.58; 95% CI: 2.21–10.82; p < 0.001). Regarding educational attainment, women with secondary education had higher odds of initiating family planning compared to those with university-level education (AOR: 0.67; 95% CI: 1.09–3.53; p = 0.02). Similarly, women with no formal education were significantly less likely to initiate postpartum family planning (AOR: 0.17; 95% CI: 0.03–0.88; p = 0.03). Mode of delivery was also a significant predictor. Women who had a normal vaginal delivery were less likely to initiate immediate postpartum family planning compared to those who underwent caesarean section (AOR: 0.49; 95% CI: 0.31–0.76; p = 0.02). Age category, religion, and husband’s presence during labour were not significantly associated with initiation of immediate postpartum family planning in the adjusted model.

Discussion

In the two hospitals where the study was carried out, 48.3% of women had their partners accompanying them during labor and/or childbirth. The study revealed various reasons for partners not being present, such as work obligations, taking care of other children at home, hospitals restricting access to labor and delivery rooms, and denial of paternity responsibility. This study suggests that the presence of male partners, as reported by women, went beyond mere physical presence. In fact, a substantial proportion of women reported moderate to high perceived partner participation; however, this measure was subjective and should be interpreted with caution. This finding is significant considering that previous evidence suggesting that male partners’ presence during childbirth was not common. However, the current findings offer promise as more women are now accompanied by their partners during labor and/or birth. However, the practice is not yet widespread in all health facilities. This is due to a small proportion of men who reported being denied access to the labor wards to be with their partners. Although this study did not directly investigate the reasons for denial of access, previous Rwandan studies suggest that the structural setup of labor and delivery wards, privacy concerns, and the lack of policies regarding birth companionship may limit men’s attendance during labor and birth, even when women prefer their partners’ presence [30–32].

The study found that 60.6% of women proactively opted for immediate postpartum contraception. This proportion is higher than that reported in some previous facility-based studies in Rwanda and other sub-Saharan African settings; however, differences in study design, population, and context limit direct comparability [33]. In that particular study, only 47.6% of women expressed a desire for contraception, and a mere 11.4% received postpartum family planning before being discharged [33]. Furthermore, our study suggested a promising trend in the utilization of immediate postpartum family planning, particularly when compared to other settings such as Ethiopia, and Uganda [34–36]. This is supported by a comprehensive review of 15 primary studies conducted in Ethiopia, which reported an overall pooled prevalence of immediate postpartum family planning utilization among postpartum women at 21.04% [34]. Another recent study from Ethiopia reported that of the total study participants, 30.7% [95% CI (26.1, 35.3)] utilized immediate postpartum family planning [35]. In Uganda, a study conducted in one hospital in Kampala reported that contraceptive uptake in the immediate postpartum period among these participants was 15.4% (61/397) [36]. It is important to note that while the current study provides valuable insights into contraception use immediately after childbirth, the findings are not generalizable to the broader population of postpartum women in Rwanda, particularly those in rural settings or lower-level health facilities. Additionally, the sample size did not include sufficient participants from rural areas. Therefore, in order to obtain an accurate understanding of the prevalence of immediate postpartum family planning choices among couples in Rwanda, a large-scale survey is necessary. This survey should include a representative number of health facilities, encompassing both private and public sectors, as well as a mix of urban and rural characteristics.

The study suggests that, compared to other contraceptive methods, intra-uterine devices (IUDs) were the preferred contraceptive method by women during the immediate postpartum period. Sociocultural and reproductive factors may explain this preference. Women with higher parity or those wishing to delay future pregnancies for extended periods may perceive IUDs as a suitable option. Additionally, misconceptions surrounding hormonal methods—such as concerns about side effects or impacts on breastfeeding—may lead some women to favor non-user-dependent or perceived “non-hormonal” options like copper IUDs. However, given that method choice is influenced by both provider counseling and availability, further research is needed to disentangle the extent to which women’s preferences versus provider practices drive this pattern.

The observed preference for IUDs in this study may also be understood in relation to women’s sociodemographic characteristics—particularly age, marital status, and occupation—which are closely linked to fertility intentions and the desire to delay subsequent pregnancies. Women in the predominant age groups in this study (21–40 years) are often in active reproductive phases but may wish to space births for health, economic, or social reasons. For such women, long-acting reversible contraceptives like IUDs may provide a reliable and convenient option for delaying pregnancy without requiring frequent user action. This finding contradicts a study conducted in Uganda by Nakiwunga et al. [36], which revealed that most women opted for the progestin-only pill instead of IUDs.

The study revealed that the level of joint decision-making between couples regarding family planning initiation during the immediate postpartum period remains low, as only a third of women have actively engaged in collaborative discussions with their male partners to determine the most suitable method. While previous studies have suggested an association between men’s attendance at childbirth and couples’ decisions to adopt immediate postpartum contraceptive use [6, 22, 33, 37], male partner attendance during childbirth remains uncommon. Moreover, even among men who do attend, knowledge and understanding of immediate postpartum family planning options for their female partners may be limited, which could affect couples’ joint decision-making regarding the choice of contraceptive methods. In addition, some women may choose not to involve their partners in contraceptive decision-making because of their personal perceptions and concerns regarding the effects of immediate postpartum contraceptive use on their sexual health [22]. Furthermore, like other patriarchal societies, within the cultural context of Rwanda, family planning decisions are still predominantly considered to be within the domain of women which may affect couples’ decision making regarding the uptake of immediate postpartum contraceptive use [38–40]. Lastly, the nature of the relationship between the woman and the father of the baby may play a significant role in determining whether she feels inclined to involve him in the decision-making process regarding her contraceptive choices and the level of his involvement. Therefore, strengthening couple-based counseling during antenatal care, encouraging male participation in reproductive health services, and addressing sociocultural norms that limit open communication between partners may help improve collaborative decision-making regarding postpartum contraception.

The findings of this study indicate that the provision of information to women regarding the mode of action, contraindications, and potential side effects of immediate postpartum family planning methods was insufficient in the hospitals surveyed. Several factors may explain these deficiencies in counseling. First, maternity wards in high-volume facilities are often characterized by heavy workloads and limited staffing, which may constrain the time available for comprehensive counseling. In such contexts, providers may prioritize clinical care over detailed contraceptive education, resulting in incomplete information delivery. Secondly, the immediate postpartum period presents unique clinical considerations, and providers may be cautious about recommending certain methods without thorough assessment, particularly in the absence of adequate time or resources. However, it is important to note that the current study did not delve into the underlying barriers that contribute to this issue. Consequently, further investigation is warranted to gain insights from healthcare professionals’ perspectives on the factors that influence their practice of providing information to couples about immediate postpartum family planning methods. Additionally, this research should aim to identify potential strategies that can be implemented to address these challenges.

According to the reports provided by women in this study, the practice of assessing the individual woman to determine the most suitable family planning method for her was found to be alarmingly low. Only approximately 31% of the women reported that healthcare providers conducted a comprehensive assessment of their health before offering them their preferred family planning method. This lack of thorough evaluation may have significant implications for the effectiveness of the chosen contraceptive methods. Therefore, in order to enhance the quality of immediate family planning services, healthcare facilities should prioritize the strengthening of this aspect by ensuring that a comprehensive assessment of women’s health is conducted prior to offering them their preferred family planning method.

The study identified several factors that can contribute to women in Rwanda opting not to use contraception during the immediate postpartum period. These factors include the need for sufficient time to recover from childbirth, the early stage of the postpartum period which may make it difficult for women to make a decision, concerns about potential side effects, the desire to conceive another child quickly, inadequate information provided by healthcare providers regarding immediate contraceptive options, and the refusal of their spouse. These findings align with previous studies conducted in Uganda, which also highlighted similar reasons for women’s reluctance to use contraception immediately after childbirth. For instance, a study conducted in Uganda revealed that approximately 97 (30.2%) of women preferred to delay contraception after the postpartum period due to various factors such as the need for healing time, waiting for their menstrual cycle to resume, discomfort and pain experienced during the immediate postpartum period, approval from their husband, and concerns about side effects of contraception [36]. However, unlike the Ugandan study, this study indicates that the most common reason for women in Rwanda not choosing a contraceptive method during the immediate postpartum period is that they perceive it as too early to make a decision, while another proportion of women prioritize their post-birth recovery experience.

The study revealed that the presence of a partner during childbirth was not statistically associated with the couple’s IPPFP uptake in this study. The reported denial of male partners’ access to labor and delivery wards has important implications for immediate postpartum family planning (IPPFP) initiation. The immediate postpartum period represents a critical window for contraceptive decision-making, often occurring within a limited timeframe before discharge. Excluding male partners from this setting may reduce opportunities for real-time couple communication and joint decision-making regarding contraceptive use. In situations where family planning decisions have not been fully discussed during pregnancy, the absence of male partners at the point of care may limit their ability to support or consent to contraceptive initiation, particularly in contexts where male involvement influences health decisions. The lack of association between male partner presence and IPPFP uptake observed in this study suggests that decision-making around postpartum contraception may often occur earlier, during pregnancy, rather than at the time of delivery. In this context, denial of access to the labor ward may have a limited direct effect on contraceptive initiation if prior discussions and decisions have already been made. However, for couples who have not engaged in antenatal family planning counseling or discussions, the absence of male partners during the immediate postpartum period may represent a missed opportunity for provider-facilitated couple counseling and shared decision-making. The finding from this study contradicts the evidence presented in previous studies conducted in Africa, which demonstrated that partner support can have a significant impact on women’s uptake of contraception [17, 41].

The study found that women employed in the public sector were more likely to initiate contraception compared to those in other occupational groups. This may reflect greater access to health information, increased interaction with healthcare services, and enhanced autonomy in reproductive decision-making among formally employed women.

Women with secondary education had higher odds of initiating postpartum family planning, while those with no formal education were significantly less likely to adopt a method. The higher odds of immediate postpartum contraceptive initiation among women with secondary-level education in Rwanda may be explained by several interrelated contextual and socioeconomic factors. Women at this educational level are often in transitional life stages where they aspire to pursue further education or vocational training beyond secondary school. In this context, avoiding an unintended or closely spaced pregnancy may be a priority in order to maintain educational continuity and future academic opportunities. Furthermore, this association may also be influenced by broader life-course considerations, including marital status, employment prospects, and perceived financial stability. Women with secondary education may be more likely to engage in early planning regarding family size and timing of births, particularly in relation to anticipated career progression and income generation after completing school. As a result, they may be more inclined to adopt postpartum contraception as a deliberate strategy to delay or space pregnancies in line with their socioeconomic goals. However, the current study did not explore in depth the underlying pathways through which education influences postpartum contraceptive uptake. Future research should therefore employ qualitative or mixed-methods designs specifically targeting women with different educational levels to better understand the decision-making processes, perceived opportunities, and contextual constraints that shape contraceptive behavior.

Mode of delivery was significantly associated with uptake. Women who had vaginal deliveries were less likely to initiate contraception compared to those who underwent caesarean section. Women who experience caesarean section are more likely to perceive their delivery as complicated or medically high-risk, which may increase their perceived vulnerability to closely spaced pregnancies. This heightened perception of risk can motivate a stronger intention to adopt contraception immediately after delivery as a preventive measure against potential adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes in future pregnancies. In addition, women undergoing caesarean delivery often receive more intensive clinical monitoring and postoperative counseling, which may include structured discussions on birth spacing and the importance of delaying subsequent pregnancies to allow adequate uterine healing and recovery. Conversely, women who deliver vaginally may perceive childbirth as a lower-risk or “normal” event, which could reduce their perceived urgency for immediate contraception. In such cases, both women and providers may place less emphasis on postpartum contraceptive counseling, particularly if the delivery is uncomplicated and recovery is rapid. As a result, these women may be more inclined to defer contraceptive initiation to a later postnatal period rather than adopting a method before discharge.

While employment in the public sector and attainment of at least secondary education were identified as predictors of immediate postpartum family planning uptake in this study, these findings contrast with reports from other settings such as Ethiopia, Uganda, and Rwanda [4, 36, 42–44]. Studies conducted in those countries identified different predictors of immediate postpartum contraceptive use, including grand multiparity [42], maternal age being over 30 years old [4, 42, 44], marital status [36], prior contraceptive counseling during antenatal care [36, 42, 43], and awareness of the immediate postpartum contraceptive use [43].

From men’s perspectives, our study found that couples’ uptake of immediate postpartum family planning is largely influenced by decisions made during pregnancy and by broader contextual factors—such as maternal health, parity, and economic capacity—rather than by the male partner’s presence at childbirth, although witnessing complicated or painful deliveries may reinforce some men’s support for immediate contraceptive use.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several important strengths that extend and complement the existing evidence on postpartum family planning in Rwanda. Unlike prior facility-based studies that focused on single hospitals, specific contraceptive methods, or selected clinical subpopulations, this study adopts a comprehensive approach by simultaneously examining sociodemographic characteristics, childbirth-related factors (including mode of delivery), male partner presence and participation during childbirth, and health-provider practices within the immediate postpartum period. This integrated analysis provides a more holistic and contextualized understanding of factors associated with immediate postpartum family planning (IPPFP) initiation than previously available in Rwanda.

Furthermore, whereas most Rwandan studies have emphasized method-specific uptake—particularly postpartum intrauterine contraceptive devices—or assessed contraceptive use beyond the immediate postpartum period, this study explicitly focuses on contraceptive initiation prior to hospital discharge. By capturing this critical and often underexamined decision-making window, the study enhances the relevance of its findings for strengthening maternity-based service integration and informing postpartum family planning policies and programs.

However, the study also has limitations. Conducted in only two urban hospitals in Kigali, the findings may not be generalizable to rural settings or other types of health facilities. Data collection within 96 h postpartum may not fully capture evolving contraceptive intentions or decisions made after discharge. The cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal inferences. Additionally, the study did not explore contextual factors such as cultural norms, women’s attitudes toward contraception, or healthcare provider perspectives, which may influence immediate postpartum contraceptive decision-making. While the study’s larger sample size improved statistical precision, the use of convenience sampling limits the representativeness of the sample, and findings should not be generalized to the broader population. Due to the small sample size of the qualitative component of this study, thematic saturation was not achieved, and findings are exploratory in nature.

The analysis was limited to variables captured in the questionnaire. Important potential confounders were not measured or adjusted for, including antenatal family planning counseling, prior contraceptive use, fertility intentions, partner communication prior to delivery, and facility-level readiness (e.g., staff training and contraceptive stock availability). Their omission may have introduced residual confounding. Furthermore, The qualitative component included only five participants, which is insufficient to achieve thematic saturation or generate comprehensive conceptual insights. The findings should therefore be interpreted as illustrative and exploratory rather than representative.

In addition, the study relied on self-reported data collected shortly after childbirth, which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias, particularly regarding sensitive topics such as contraceptive decision-making and partner involvement. The measurement of male partner participation was based on subjective, non-validated categories, which may have introduced misclassification.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence on immediate postpartum family planning uptake among women delivering in two urban hospitals in Kigali. Approximately six in ten women reported initiating contraception prior to discharge. Several sociodemographic and obstetric factors were associated with uptake in bivariate analysis, while male partner presence during childbirth was not statistically associated with contraceptive initiation.

However, given the cross-sectional design, convenience sampling, and restriction to two urban facilities, these findings should be interpreted as context-specific and non-causal. The results highlight potential gaps in counseling and clinical assessment that may influence postpartum contraceptive decision-making. Further research using representative samples and longitudinal designs is needed to better understand determinants of IPPFP uptake and inform targeted interventions.
