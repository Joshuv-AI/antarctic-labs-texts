A new proof of the Herzog-Hibi-Zheng theorem
Antonino Ficarra

Abstract
. We give a new, elementary proof of the celebrated Herzog-Hibi-Zheng theorem on powers of quadratic monomial ideals.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 13F20; Secondary 13F55, 05C70, 05E40.

1.
Introduction

Let
S=K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be the standard graded polynomial ring over a field
K
, and let
I\subset S
be a monomial ideal. One of the most fascinating problems in Combinatorial Commutative Algebra is to decide when
I
has
linear powers
. That is,
I
is generated in a single degree
d
, and all powers
I^{k}
have a
dk
-linear resolution. Naively, one would expect that if
I
has linear resolution, the same should be true for all its powers
I^{k}
. Very early on, counterexamples to this expectation were discovered by Terai, and, independently, by Sturmfels. See the introduction of
[
11
]
.

Nonetheless, using the classical Dirac theorem on chordal graphs
[
5
]
, and Fröberg theorem on edge ideals
[
8
]
, the following influential result was proved in
[
11
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

(Herzog-Hibi-Zheng).
Let
I\subset S
be a quadratic monomial ideal. The following conditions are equivalent.

(a)

I
has linear resolution.

(b)

I
has linear powers.

(c)

All powers of
I
have linear quotients.

We will explain any unexplained concept in the next section.

The original proof given in
[
11
]
, which shows only that (a) and (b) are equivalent, made use of the so-called
x
-condition, which is a condition on the Gröbner basis of the defining ideal of the Rees algebra of
I
, and the delicate computation of the Graver basis of certain edge rings. Only in
[
9
, Theorem 3.1 and Corollary 3.2]
it was realized that the conditions (a)-(b) are further equivalent to (c).

In the last 20 years, Theorem
1.1
inspired a vast amount of researches going into various directions. One of the most remarkable ones, is the conjecture of Nevo and Peeva
[
12
]
, which is still open, that aims to characterize those quadratic monomial ideals
I\subset S
such that
I^{k}
has linear resolution for all
k\gg 0
.

Besides of an alternative, but still rather tedious proof of Theorem
1.1
, which is due to Banerjee
[
1
, Theorem 6.16]
, no simple and elementary proof of Theorem
1.1
has been known. The goal of this note is to provide a short, simple and yet new proof of Theorem
1.1
. Moreover, a novel consequence of Theorem
1.1
will be derived in Corollary
3.1
, together with some related questions.

2.
From Dirac to Fröberg to Herzog-Hibi-Zheng

Let
G
be a finite simple graph on the vertex set
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
, with edge set
E(G)
. The
edge ideal
of
G
is the monomial ideal
I(G)\subset S
generated by those monomials
x_{i}x_{j}
such that
\{x_{i},x_{j}\}\in E(G)
. A graph
G
is said
complete
if every 2-subset
\{x_{i},x_{j}\}
of
V(G)
is an edge of
G
. The
open neighbourhood
of
x_{i}\in V(G)
is the set

N_{G}(x_{i})\ =\ \{x_{j}\in V(G):\ \{x_{i},x_{j}\}\in E(G)\}.

A graph
G
is called
chordal
if it has no induced cycles of length bigger than three. Recall that a
perfect elimination order
of
G
is an ordering
x_{1}>\dots>x_{n}
of its vertex set
V(G)
such that
N_{G_{i}}(x_{i})
induces a complete subgraph on
G_{i}
, where
G_{i}
is the induced subgraph of
G
on the vertex set
\{x_{i},x_{i+1},\dots,x_{n}\}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

(Dirac).
A finite
simple graph
G
is chordal, if and only if,
G
admits a perfect elimination order.

The
complementary graph

G^{c}
of
G
is the graph with vertex set
V(G^{c})=V(G)
and where
\{x_{i},x_{j}\}
is an edge of
G^{c}
if and only if
\{x_{i},x_{j}\}\notin E(G)
. A graph
G
is called
cochordal
if and only if
G^{c}
is chordal.

Theorem 2.2
.

(Fröberg).
Let
G
be a simple finite graph. Then,
I(G)
has a linear resolution, if and only if,
G
is cochordal.

Let
I\subset S
be a monomial ideal. We denote by
\mathcal{G}(I)
the (unique) minimal monomial generating set of
I
. We say that
I
has
linear quotients
if there exists an order
u_{1}>\dots>u_{m}
of
\mathcal{G}(I)
, called a
linear quotients order
of
I
, such that the colon ideals
(u_{1},\dots,u_{i-1}):u_{i}
are generated by variables, for all
1\leq i\leq m
.

Note that
(u_{1},\dots,u_{i-1}):u_{i}
is generated by the monomials
u_{j}:u_{i}=\textup{lcm}(u_{j},u_{i})/u_{i}
where
1\leq j<i
. Therefore,
I
has linear quotients order
u_{1}>\dots>u_{m}
, if and only if, for all
j<i
there exists
k<i
such that
u_{k}:u_{i}=x_{b}
is a variable dividing
u_{j}:u_{i}
.

It is well-known that if
I\subset S
is an equigenerated monomial ideal with linear quotients, then
I
has linear resolution
[
9
, Proposition 8.2.1]
.

We recall the
polarization
technique. For a monomial
u=x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}}\in S
, the
polarization
of
u
is the monomial
u^{\wp}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(\prod_{j=1}^{a_{i}}x_{i,j})
in the polynomial ring
K[x_{i,j}:1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq a_{i}]
. The
polarization
of a monomial ideal
I\subset S
is defined to be the squarefree ideal
I^{\wp}
with minimal generating set
\mathcal{G}(I^{\wp})=\{u^{\wp}:u\in\mathcal{G}(I)\}
, in the polynomial ring
S^{\wp}
over
K
in the variables
x_{i,j}
required to define
\mathcal{G}(I^{\wp})
.

The following well-known property can be found in
[
9
, Corollary 1.6.3]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
I\subset S
be a monomial ideal. Then
I
has linear resolution, if and only if,
I^{\wp}
has linear resolution.

The following technical lemma will be crucial for the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
I\subset S
be a quadratic monomial ideal having linear resolution. Then, up to relabeling, we can write
I=x_{1}P+J
with
J\subset P
, where

\displaystyle P\

\displaystyle=\ (u/x_{1}:\ u\in\mathcal{G}(I)\,\,\text{such that}\,\,x_{1}\,\,\text{divides}\,\,u),\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{and}

(1)

\displaystyle J\

\displaystyle=\ (u\in\mathcal{G}(I):\ x_{1}\,\,\text{does not divide}\,\,u),

(2)

and moreover
J
has again linear resolution.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.3
, there exists a finite simple graph
G
for which
I^{\wp}=I(G)
is an edge ideal with linear resolution. Then, by Theorem
2.2
,
G
is a cochordal graph. Fix
x_{1}>x_{2}>\dots>x_{n}
a perfect elimination order of
G^{c}
. Let
P^{\prime}=(x_{j}:x_{j}\in N_{G}(x_{1}))
and
J^{\prime}=I(G\setminus\{x_{1}\})
, where
G\setminus\{x_{1}\}
is the graph obtained from
G
by removing
x_{1}
from
V(G)
and all edges incident with
x_{1}
from
E(G)
. Since
x_{2}>\dots>x_{n}
is again a perfect elimination order of
(G\setminus\{x_{1}\})^{c}
, it follows by Theorem
2.1
that
G\setminus\{x_{1}\}
is cochordal and by Theorem
2.2
that
J^{\prime}
has linear resolution. We have

E(G)\ =\ \{\{x_{1},x_{j}\}:\ x_{j}\in N_{G}(x_{1})\}\cup E(G\setminus\{x_{1}\}).

So
I(G)=x_{1}P^{\prime}+J^{\prime}
. We claim that
J^{\prime}\subset P^{\prime}
. Let
x_{i}x_{j}\in J^{\prime}
be a monomial generator corresponding to an edge
\{x_{i},x_{j}\}\in E(G\setminus\{x_{1}\})\subset E(G)
. We must show that either
x_{i}
or
x_{j}
belongs to
N_{G}(x_{1})
. If this was not the case, then
\{x_{1},x_{i}\},\{x_{1},x_{j}\}\in E(G^{c})
. Since
x_{1}>x_{2}>\dots>x_{n}
is a perfect elimination order of
G^{c}
, it would follow that
\{x_{i},x_{j}\}\in E(G^{c})
, against the fact that
\{x_{i},x_{j}\}\in E(G)
. Hence
J^{\prime}\subset P^{\prime}
.

Now, let
P
and
J
as defined in the equations (
1
) and (
2
). It is then clear that
I=x_{1}P+J
,
P^{\wp}=P^{\prime}
and
J^{\wp}=J^{\prime}
. Hence
J\subset P
, because
J^{\prime}\subset P^{\prime}
. Finally, applying Lemma
2.3
,
J
has a linear resolution, because
J^{\prime}=J^{\wp}
has linear resolution.

∎

For the proof of the next result we recall some concepts. Let
u=x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}}
and
v=x_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{b_{n}}
. The
lex order

<_{\textup{lex}}
is the monomial order of
S
defined by setting
u>v
if
a_{j}=b_{j}
for all
j<i
and
a_{i}>b_{i}
. The
support
of a monomial ideal
I\subset S
is defined as the set
\textup{supp}\,I=\bigcup_{u\in\mathcal{G}(I)}\textup{supp}(u)
, where
\textup{supp}(w)=\{x_{i}:\ x_{i}\,\,\text{divides}\,\,w\}
for any monomial
w\in S
.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
I\subset S
be a quadratic monomial ideal having linear resolution. Then, up to relabeling, the following two properties hold.

(*)

If
i<j<k
and
x_{j}x_{k}\in I
, then
x_{i}x_{j}\in I
or
x_{i}x_{k}\in I
.

(**)

If
x_{i}^{2}\in I
and
x_{j}x_{k}\in I
for some
j<i
and some
k
, then
x_{i}x_{j}\in I
or
x_{i}x_{k}\in I
.

In particular, up to relabeling,
I
has linear quotients with respect to the lex order of its minimal generators.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
|\textup{supp}\,I|
. If
|\textup{supp}\,I|=1
, there is nothing to prove. Let
|\textup{supp}\,I|>1
. By Lemma
2.4
,
I=x_{1}P+J
where
P
and
J
are as in equations (
1
) and (
2
),
J\subset P
and
J
has linear resolution. Since
|\textup{supp}\,J|<|\textup{supp}\,I|
, by the inductive hypothesis
J
satisfies the properties
(*)
and
(**)
.

Proof of

(*)
. Let
i<j<k
such that
x_{j}x_{k}\in I
. If
i>1
, then the property
(*)
holds for
J
, and so it holds for
I
, too. Suppose now
i=1
. Then
x_{j}x_{k}\in J\subset P
. Thus
x_{j}\in P
or
x_{k}\in P
, and so
x_{i}x_{j}\in I
or
x_{i}x_{k}\in I
, as desired.

Proof of

(**)
. Let
i<j
and
k
such that
x_{i}^{2}\in I
and
x_{j}x_{k}\in I
. Since
i>1
, we have
x_{i}^{2}\in J\subset P
, and so
x_{i}\in P
. If
j,k>1
, then the property
(**)
holds for
J
, and so it holds for
I
too. Otherwise, if
j=1
or
k=1
, then
x_{j}x_{i}\in x_{1}P\subset I
or
x_{k}x_{i}\in x_{1}P\subset I
, as desired.

Finally, let
\mathcal{G}(I)=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{m}\}
be ordered such that
e_{1}>_{\textup{lex}}\cdots>_{\textup{lex}}e_{m}
. We claim that
e_{1}>\dots>e_{m}
is a linear quotients order of
I
. If
|\textup{supp}\,I|=1
, there is nothing to prove. Let
|\textup{supp}\,I|>1
, and
I=x_{1}P+J
as in the beginning of the proof. Then
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{s}\}=\mathcal{G}(x_{1}P)
and
\{e_{s+1},\dots,e_{m}\}=\mathcal{G}(J)
for some
s
. It is clear that
(e_{1},\dots,e_{r-1}):(e_{r})
is generated by variables for
r=2,\dots,s
. Now, let
r>s
. Since
x_{1}
does not divide
e_{r}
and
e_{r}\in P
, we obtain that

(e_{1},\dots,e_{r-1}):(e_{r})=(x_{1}P,e_{s+1},\dots,e_{r-1}):(e_{r})=(x_{1})+(e_{s+1},\dots,e_{r-1}):(e_{r}).

Since
|\textup{supp}\,J|<|\textup{supp}\,I|
, by induction
e_{s+1}>\dots>e_{m}
is a linear quotients order of
J
. Hence,
(e_{1},\dots,e_{r-1}):e_{r}
is indeed generated by variables.

∎

3.
The new proof

Let
I\subset S
be a quadratic monomial ideal having linear resolution. After a suitable relabeling of the variables, Lemma
2.4
and Corollary
2.5
guarantee that
I
satisfies the properties
(*)
and
(**)
.

Let
<_{\textup{lex}}
be the lex order on
S
induced by
x_{1}>x_{2}>\dots>x_{n}
, and let
\mathcal{G}(I)=\{e_{1}<_{\textup{lex}}\dots<_{\textup{lex}}e_{m}\}
ordered decreasingly according to
<_{\textup{lex}}
.

Let
y_{1},\dots,y_{m}
be a new set of variables, and let
\varphi:K[y_{1},\dots,y_{m}]\rightarrow S
be the
K
-linear map defined by setting
\varphi(y_{i})=e_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,m
. On the variables
y_{1},\dots,y_{m}
we consider the lex order induced by
y_{1}>\dots>y_{m}
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{G}(I^{k})
be a monomial of degree
2k
. Then
u=e_{i_{1}}\cdots e_{i_{k}}
for certain integers
i_{1}\leq\dots\leq i_{k}
. Such a presentation is not unique in general. Following ideas given in
[
10
, Section 2]
, we say that
u=e_{i_{1}}\cdots e_{i_{k}}
is the
standard presentation
of
u
if
y_{i_{1}}\cdots y_{i_{k}}
is the smallest monomial, with respect to
<
, such that
u=\varphi(y_{i_{1}}\cdots y_{i_{k}})
.

Fix
k\geq 1
. On the set
\mathcal{G}(I^{k})
we consider the following order. Let
u,v\in\mathcal{G}(I^{k})
, and let
u=e_{i_{1}}\cdots e_{i_{k}}
,
v=e_{j_{1}}\cdots e_{j_{k}}
be the standard presentations of
u
and
v
. We put
v>u
if
y_{i_{1}}\cdots y_{i_{k}}<y_{j_{1}}\cdots y_{j_{k}}
with respect to the lex order induced by
y_{1}>\dots>y_{m}
. We will show that
I^{k}
has linear quotients with respect to this order for all
k\geq 1
.

We are now ready to deliver the new elementary proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

(c)
\Rightarrow
(b)
\Rightarrow
(a) is true for any equigenerated monomial ideal.

To prove (a)
\Rightarrow
(c), we proceed by induction on
k\geq 1
and show that
I^{k}
has linear quotients with respect to the order introduced above.

Let
k=1
. Then, it follows from Corollary
2.5
that
I
has linear quotients order
e_{m}>_{\textup{lex}}e_{m-1}>_{\textup{lex}}\cdots>_{\textup{lex}}e_{1}
. Since each monomial
e_{i}
is its standard presentation and
y_{m}<y_{m-1}<\dots<y_{1}
, the base case of the induction is verified.

Now, let
k>1
, and let
u=e_{i_{1}}\cdots e_{i_{k}}
and
v=e_{j_{1}}\cdots e_{j_{k}}
be the standard presentations of two monomials
u,v\in\mathcal{G}(I^{k})
with
v<u
. Our job is to find a monomial
w\in\mathcal{G}(I^{k})
with
w<u
such that
w:v
is a variable that divides
v:u
.

If
\deg(v:u)=1
, there is nothing to prove. Suppose now
\deg(v:u)>1
.

We may assume that
i_{r}\neq j_{s}
for all
1\leq s,r\leq k
. Indeed, suppose
i_{r}=j_{s}
for some
r
and
s
. Set
u^{\prime}=u/e_{i_{r}}
and
v^{\prime}=v/e_{j_{s}}
. Then
v^{\prime}:u^{\prime}=v:u
. We claim that
u^{\prime}=e_{i_{1}}\cdots e_{i_{r-1}}e_{i_{r+1}}\cdots e_{i_{k}}
and
v^{\prime}=e_{j_{1}}\cdots e_{j_{s-1}}e_{j_{s+1}}\cdots e_{j_{k}}
are again standard presentations. Suppose this was not the case for
u^{\prime}
. Then, if
u^{\prime}=e_{\ell_{1}}\cdots e_{\ell_{k-1}}
is the standard presentation of
u^{\prime}
, we have
y_{\ell_{1}}\cdots y_{\ell_{k-1}}<y_{i_{1}}\cdots y_{i_{r-1}}y_{i_{r+1}}\cdots y_{i_{k}}
. Multiplying by
y_{i_{r}}
we obtain
y_{\ell_{1}}\cdots y_{\ell_{k-1}}y_{i_{r}}<y_{i_{1}}\cdots y_{i_{k}}
and
\varphi(y_{\ell_{1}}\cdots y_{\ell_{k-1}}y_{i_{r}})=u
, against the fact that
u=e_{i_{1}}\cdots e_{i_{k}}
is the standard presentation of
u
. We can proceed similarly for
v^{\prime}
. Hence
v^{\prime}<u^{\prime}
. By induction on
k
, there exists
w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}(I^{k-1})
such that
w^{\prime}:u^{\prime}
is a variable that divides
v^{\prime}:u^{\prime}
. Set
w=w^{\prime}e_{i_{r}}
and let
w^{\prime}=e_{t_{1}}\cdots e_{t_{k-1}}
and
w=e_{p_{1}}\cdots e_{p_{k}}
be the standard presentations of
w^{\prime}
of
w
. We have
w<u
, because
y_{p_{1}}\cdots y_{p_{k}}\leq y_{t_{1}}\cdots y_{t_{k-1}}y_{i_{r}}<y_{i_{1}}\cdots y_{i_{k}}
. Moreover,
w:u=w^{\prime}:u^{\prime}
is a variable that divides
v:u=v^{\prime}:u^{\prime}
, as desired.

Let
e_{i_{1}}
be the monomial appearing in
u
for which
y_{i_{1}}
is the biggest variable appearing in
y_{i_{1}}\cdots y_{i_{k}}
. By our assumption
i_{1}\neq j_{s}
for all
s
. We can write

fu=gv,\quad\quad\textup{where}\quad f=v:u\quad\textup{and}\quad g=u:v.

Since
\deg(u)=\deg(v)=2k
, we have
\deg(f)=\deg(g)=d\geq 2
. Let
x_{n+1}
be a “dummy” variable. Set
f^{\prime}=fx_{n+1}^{d}
and
g^{\prime}=gx_{n+1}^{d}
. Then
f^{\prime}u=g^{\prime}v
. We claim that there is a sequence of
2(k+d)
indices
v_{1},\dots,v_{2(k+d)}
with
v_{2(k+d)+1}=v_{1}
such that

(i)

x_{v_{1}}x_{v_{2}}=e_{i_{1}}
with
v_{1}\leq v_{2}
,

(ii)

f^{\prime}u=\prod_{\ell=1}^{k+d}(x_{v_{2\ell-1}}x_{v_{2\ell}})
and
\,g^{\prime}v=\prod_{\ell=1}^{k+d}(x_{v_{2\ell}}x_{v_{2\ell+1}})
,

(iii)

if both
v_{\ell},v_{\ell+1}\neq n+1
, then

x_{v_{\ell}}x_{v_{\ell+1}}\in\begin{cases}\,\{e_{i_{1}},\dots,e_{i_{k}}\},&\textup{if}\ \ell\ \textup{is odd},\\
\,\{e_{j_{1}},\dots,e_{j_{k}}\},&\textup{if}\ \ell\ \textup{is even}.\end{cases}

Proof of the Claim.
The trick which we use and which is well-known to the experts, is to associate to the relation
f^{\prime}u=g^{\prime}v
a so-called
even closed walk
of a certain graph, see also
[
9
, Lemma 10.1.4]
. Let
e_{i_{1}}=x_{v_{1}}x_{v_{2}}
with
v_{1}\leq v_{2}
. Since
x_{v_{2}}
divides
f^{\prime}u=g^{\prime}v
, then
x_{v_{2}}
divides
g^{\prime}
or
v
. If
x_{v_{2}}
divides
g^{\prime}
, we can write
g^{\prime}v=(x_{v_{2}}x_{n+1})h
where
h
is a suitable monomial of degree
2(k+d-1)
. Since
x_{n+1}
divides
f^{\prime}
we can find
x_{v_{3}}
dividing
f
and then
x_{v_{3}}
divides
h
. Otherwise,
x_{v_{2}}
divides
v
, so it divides
e_{j_{s}}
for some
s
, and
e_{j_{s}}=x_{v_{2}}x_{v_{3}}
. Iterating this reasoning, it is clear that we can find the desired sequence of indices satisfying the properties (i)-(ii)-(iii).
\square

Let
\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}<\cdots
be the integers
3\leq\ell\leq 2(k+d)
for which
v_{\ell}=n+1
. There are at least two such integers since
\deg(f)\geq 2
. We distinguish the possible cases.

Case 1.
Suppose
\ell_{1}
is even. Let
u^{\prime\prime}=u/[(x_{v_{1}}x_{v_{2}})\cdots(x_{v_{\ell_{1}-3}}x_{v_{\ell_{1}-2}})]
and set

w=(x_{v_{2}}x_{v_{3}})\cdots(x_{v_{\ell_{1}-2}}x_{v_{\ell_{1}-1}})u^{\prime\prime}.

(3)

Then
w\in\mathcal{G}(I^{k})
and
w:u=x_{v_{\ell_{1}-1}}
. Since
e_{i_{1}}
appears in the standard presentation of
u
, but not in that of
v
, by the property (iii) we have
e_{i_{1}}\neq(x_{v_{2}}x_{v_{3}}),\dots,(x_{v_{\ell_{1}-2}}x_{v_{\ell_{1}-1}})
. Let
e_{p_{1}}\cdots e_{p_{k}}
be the standard presentation of
w
, and let
e_{t_{1}}\cdots e_{t_{k}}
be the presentation given in (
3
). Then,

y_{p_{1}}\cdots y_{p_{k}}\leq y_{t_{1}}\cdots y_{t_{k}}<y_{i_{1}}\cdots y_{i_{k}}
because the
y_{i_{1}}
-degree of
y_{i_{1}}\cdots y_{i_{k}}
is strictly bigger than the
y_{i_{1}}
-degree of
y_{t_{1}}\cdots y_{t_{k}}
. We conclude that
w<u
. Since
x_{v_{\ell_{1}-1}}=w:u
divides
f=v:u
, we are done in this case.

Case 2.
Suppose both
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
are odd. Then, we consider the monomials
u^{\prime}=u/[(x_{v_{\ell_{1}+2}}x_{v_{\ell_{1}+3}})\cdots(x_{v_{\ell_{2}-2}}x_{v_{\ell_{2}-1}})]
and
v^{\prime}=v/[(x_{v_{\ell_{1}+1}}x_{v_{\ell_{1}+2}})\cdots(x_{v_{\ell_{2}-3}}x_{v_{\ell_{2}-2}})]
. Notice that
\ell_{2}-\ell_{1}\geq 4
. Otherwise,
\ell_{2}=\ell_{1}+2
and then
x_{\ell_{1}+1}
divides both
f
and
g
, which is not possible since
\gcd(f,g)=\gcd(v:u,u:v)=1
. Therefore
\ell_{2}-\ell_{1}\geq 4
, and so
\deg(u^{\prime})=\deg(v^{\prime})<k
. Since
e_{i_{1}}
divides
u^{\prime}
again but not
v^{\prime}
and
u^{\prime}
is in standard presentation, it follows that
v^{\prime}<u^{\prime}
. Thus by inductive hypothesis, there exists
w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}(I^{s})
where
s=\deg(u^{\prime})/2
with
w^{\prime}<u^{\prime}
such that
w^{\prime}:u^{\prime}
is a variable that divides
v^{\prime}:u^{\prime}=f/x_{v_{\ell_{1}+1}}
. Set
w=w^{\prime}(x_{v_{\ell_{1}+2}}x_{v_{\ell_{1}+3}})\cdots(x_{v_{\ell_{2}-2}}x_{v_{\ell_{2}-1}})
. Then
w\in\mathcal{G}(I^{k})
,
w<u
and
w:u=w^{\prime}:u^{\prime}
is a variable that divides
f=v:u
.

Case 3.
Suppose
\ell_{1}
is odd and
\ell_{2}
is even. Then, we consider the monomials
u^{\prime}=u/[(x_{v_{3}}x_{v_{4}})\cdots(x_{v_{\ell_{1}-2}}x_{v_{\ell_{1}-1}})]
and
v^{\prime}=v/[(x_{v_{2}}x_{v_{3}})\cdots(x_{v_{\ell_{1}-3}}x_{v_{\ell_{1}-2}})]
. Notice that
v^{\prime}<u^{\prime}
. If
\deg(u^{\prime})=\deg(v^{\prime})<2k
, by induction there exists
w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}(I^{s})
with
s=\deg(u^{\prime})/2
and
w^{\prime}<u^{\prime}
such that
w^{\prime}:u^{\prime}
is variable that divides
v^{\prime}:u^{\prime}=f
. Setting
w=w^{\prime}(x_{v_{3}}x_{v_{4}})\cdots(x_{v_{\ell_{1}-2}}x_{v_{\ell_{1}-1}})
, we have
w\in\mathcal{G}(I^{k})
and
w<u
. Moreover,
w:u=w^{\prime}:u^{\prime}
is a variable that divides
f=v:u
, as desired.

Suppose now that
u^{\prime}=u
and
v^{\prime}=v
. Hence
\ell_{1}=3
and
x_{v_{4}}
divides
f
. We may furthermore assume that for none of the integers
4\leq p\leq\ell_{2}-1
we have
v_{p}=v_{1}
or
v_{p}=v_{2}
. Indeed, assume, for instance, that
v_{p}=v_{1}
for some
4\leq p\leq\ell_{2}-1
. Suppose that
p
is odd. Consider the monomial

w=(x_{v_{4}}x_{v_{5}})\cdots(x_{v_{p-1}}x_{v_{p}})(x_{v_{p+1}}x_{v_{1}})[u/(x_{v_{1}}x_{v_{2}})(x_{v_{5}}x_{v_{6}})\cdots(x_{v_{p}}x_{v_{p+1}})]\in\mathcal{G}(I^{k}).

We claim that
w<u
. Indeed, suppose that
v_{p+1}\neq v_{2}
. Then
e_{i_{1}}
appears in the standard presentation of
u
but not in the above presentation of
w
. This implies that
w<u
in this case. Suppose now that
v_{p+1}=v_{2}
. Then
e_{i_{1}}^{2}
appears in the standard presentation of
u
, but
e_{i_{1}}
appears in degree one in the above presentation of
w
. This implies again that
w<u
. It is clear that
w:u=x_{v_{4}}
divides
f=v:u
. We can proceed similarly if
v_{p}=v_{1}
for
p
even, or if
v_{p}=v_{2}
for some
4\leq p\leq\ell_{2}-1
.

Summarizing our argument thus far, we may assume that
\ell_{1}=3
and that for all integers
4\leq p\leq\ell_{2}-1
we have
v_{p}\neq v_{1}
and
v_{p}\neq v_{2}
.

Subcase 3.1.
Suppose there is an integer
4\leq p<\ell_{2}-1
such that
v_{p}\neq v_{p+1}
.

Subcase 3.1.1.
Let
v_{1}\neq v_{2}
. Assume that
x_{v_{1}}x_{v_{p}}\in I
or
x_{v_{1}}x_{v_{p+1}}\in I
. For instance, say that
x_{v_{1}}x_{v_{p}}\in I
. Then, we consider the following monomial of
\mathcal{G}(I^{k})
,

w=(x_{v_{4}}x_{v_{5}})\cdots(x_{v_{p}}x_{v_{1}})\cdots(x_{v_{\ell_{2}-2}}x_{v_{\ell_{2}-1}})[u/(x_{v_{1}}x_{v_{2}})(x_{v_{5}}x_{v_{6}})\cdots(x_{v_{\ell_{2}-3}}x_{v_{\ell_{2}-2}})].

(4)

Since
e_{i_{1}}
appears in the standard presentation of
u
but not in the above presentation of
w
, we have
w<u
. Moreover,
w:u=x_{v_{4}}
divides
f
, as desired. We can proceed similarly if
x_{v_{2}}x_{v_{p+1}}\in I
for some
4\leq p<\ell_{2}-1
.

Suppose now that
x_{v_{1}}x_{v_{p}},x_{v_{1}}x_{v_{p+1}}\notin I
. Since
x_{v_{p}}x_{v_{p+1}}\in I
, by the property
(*)
it follows that either
v_{p}<v_{1}
or
v_{p+1}<v_{1}
. Let
v_{p}<v_{1}
. Since
v_{p}<v_{1}<v_{2}
,
x_{v_{1}}x_{v_{2}}\in I
and
x_{v_{1}}x_{v_{p}}\notin I
, again by the property
(*)
we obtain that
x_{v_{2}}x_{v_{p}}\in I
. Then, we can consider the following monomial of
\mathcal{G}(I^{k})
,

w=(x_{v_{4}}x_{v_{5}})\cdots(x_{v_{p}}x_{v_{2}})\cdots(x_{v_{\ell_{2}-2}}x_{v_{\ell_{2}-1}})[u/(x_{v_{1}}x_{v_{2}})(x_{v_{5}}x_{v_{6}})\cdots(x_{v_{\ell_{2}-3}}x_{v_{\ell_{2}-2}})].

As before,
w<u
and
w:u=x_{p_{4}}
divides
f=v:u
, as desired.

Subcase 3.1.2.
Now, let
v_{1}=v_{2}
. Our assumption on
e_{i_{1}}
ensures that
v_{1}\geq v_{p}
. Since
x_{v_{p}}x_{v_{p+1}}\in I
, the property
(**)
implies that either
x_{v_{1}}x_{v_{p}}\in I
or
x_{v_{1}}x_{v_{p+1}}\in I
. If
x_{v_{1}}x_{v_{p}}\in I
, we can consider again the monomial given in (

We conclude the paper with the following consequence and some questions.

Corollary 3.1
.

Let
I\subset S
be a quadratic monomial ideal with linear resolution and let
P\subset S
be a monomial prime ideal containing
I
. Then
P^{k}I^{\ell}
has linear quotients for all
k,\ell\geq 1
.

Proof.

We introduce the “dummy” variable
x_{0}
, and up to a suitable extension, we may assume that
S=K[x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
and that
\textup{supp}\,P\cup\textup{supp}\,I\subseteq\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
. Let
J=x_{0}P+I
. This is a rather trivial example of a Betti splitting
[
7
]
. Indeed,
J=x_{0}P+I
is an
x_{0}
-splitting (in the sense of
[
7
]
) because
x_{0}P
and
I
have 2-linear resolution. Since
x_{0}P\cap I=x_{0}(P\cap I)=x_{0}I
has a
3
-linear resolution, it follows from
[
4
, Proposition 1.8]
that
J
is a quadratic monomial ideal having a
2
-linear resolution. By the proof of Theorem
1.1
,
J^{k}
has linear quotients with respect to the order
<
described in the beginning of the section.

We now show that
P^{k}I^{\ell}
has linear quotients for all
k,\ell\geq 1
. It is equivalent to show that
x_{0}^{k}P^{k}I^{\ell}
has linear quotients. Let
u,v\in\mathcal{G}(x_{0}^{k}P^{k}I^{\ell})\subset\mathcal{G}(J^{k+\ell})
with
v<u
with respect to the order
<
. Then, there exists
w\in\mathcal{G}(J^{k+\ell})
with
w<u
such that
w:u=x_{q}
for some
q
and
x_{q}
divides
v:u
. Since the
x_{0}
-degree of
u
and
v
is
k
, it follows that
x_{q}\neq x_{0}
and the
x_{0}
-degree of
w
is less or equal to
k
. Since
\mathcal{G}(J^{k+\ell})
is the disjoint union
\bigsqcup_{i=0}^{k+\ell}\mathcal{G}(x_{0}^{i}P^{i}J^{k+\ell-i})
, it follows that
w=x_{0}^{i}w_{0}w_{1}
with
i\leq k
,
w_{0}\in\mathcal{G}(P^{i})
and
w_{1}\in\mathcal{G}(I^{k+\ell-i})
. If
i=k
, then
w\in\mathcal{G}(x_{0}^{k}P^{k}I^{\ell})
, as desired. Suppose now that
i<k
. By assumption
I\subset P
. Write
w_{1}=e_{1}\cdots e_{k+\ell-i}
with
e_{j}=x_{r_{j}}x_{s_{j}}\in I
and
x_{s_{j}}\in P
for all
j
. Then, we consider the monomial
w^{\prime}=x_{0}^{k-i}w_{0}^{\prime}w_{1}^{\prime}
with
w_{0}^{\prime}=w_{0}x_{s_{1}}\cdots x_{s_{k-i}}\in\mathcal{G}(P^{k})
and
w_{1}^{\prime}=w_{1}/(e_{1}\cdots e_{k-i})\in\mathcal{G}(I^{\ell})
. It follows from the definition of
<
that
w^{\prime}<w
. Moreover,
w^{\prime}:u
divides
w:u=x_{q}
. Hence
w^{\prime}:u=x_{q}
too, and since
w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}(x_{0}^{k}P^{k}I^{\ell})
, this concludes the proof.

∎

This result is no longer valid if
I
is not a quadratic monomial ideal with linear resolution, as the following example
[
3
, Example 4.3]
of Conca and Herzog shows. Let
S=K[a,b,c,d]
,
I=(a^{2}b,abc,bcd,cd^{2})
and
P=(b,c)
. Then
I
has linear quotients and
I\subset P
, but
PI
does not have linear quotients, not even linear resolution.

On the other hand, very recently it was shown in
[
6
, Lemma 3.3]
that
PI
has linear quotients for any edge ideal
I
with linear resolution and any monomial prime ideal
P
, independently from the assumption that
I\subset P
. Hence, we are left to ask whether the assumption that
I\subset P
is really needed in Corollary
3.1
. This raises the following question.

Question 3.2
.

Let
I\subset S
be a quadratic monomial ideal with linear resolution, and let
P\subset S
be a monomial prime ideal. Is it true that
P^{k}I^{\ell}
has linear quotients for all
k,\ell\geq 1
?
