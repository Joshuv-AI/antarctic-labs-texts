Abstract
We present a proof-theoretical study of the interpretability logic 𝖨𝖫 \mathsf{IL} , providing a wellfounded and a non-wellfounded sequent calculus for 𝖨𝖫 \mathsf{IL} . The non-wellfounded calculus is used to establish a cut elimination argument for both calculi. In addition, we show that the non-wellfounded proof theory of 𝖨𝖫 \mathsf{IL} is well-behaved, i.e., that cyclic proofs suffice. This makes it possible to prove uniform interpolation for 𝖨𝖫 \mathsf{IL} . As a corollary we also provide a proof of uniform interpolation for the interpretability logic 𝖨𝖫𝖯 \mathsf{ILP} .

Thus, the contributions of this paper are threefold:

1.

We refine our previous work on developing a general proof theory of non-wellfounded local progress calculi. In particular, we introduce the notions of admissible, locally admissible, eliminable, and locally eliminable rules and study their relationship.

2.1
Interpretability Logic

In this subsection we will define the interpretability logic that we will be working with. We will also prove that certain formulas, which will be useful to us in the next sections, are theorems of this logic.

The syntax of interpretability logic is given by

\phi::=p\ |\ \bot\ |\ \phi\to\phi\ |\ \phi\rhd\phi,

where
p
ranges over a fixed infinite countable set of propositional variables.
We call formulas of this language
\mathsf{IL}
-formulas.
When it is clear from the context that we are talking about
\mathsf{IL}
-formulas, we will just write formula instead of
\mathsf{IL}
-formula.
Other Boolean connectives can be defined as abbreviations as usual, i.e.,
\neg\phi=\phi\to\bot
,
\phi\vee\psi=\neg\phi\to\psi
,
\phi\wedge\psi=\neg(\phi\to\neg\psi)
.

\Box\phi
can be defined as an abbreviation, namely
\Box\phi=\neg\phi\rhd\bot
and we set
\Diamond\phi=\neg(\phi\mathbin{\rhd}\bot)
.
We will also use the abbreviation
\mathop{\rule[0.5pt]{6.0pt}{6.0pt}}\phi=(\phi\mathbin{\rhd}\bot)\wedge\phi
and

\boxdot\phi=\phi\wedge\Box\phi
.

A formula of the form
\phi\rhd\psi
will be called a
\rhd
-formula.
Given a
\mathbin{\rhd}
-formula
\phi\mathbin{\rhd}\psi
, we wil say that
\phi
is its
antecedent
, also denoted as
\mathrm{an}(\phi\mathbin{\rhd}\psi)
, and
\psi
is its
succedent
, also denoted as
\mathrm{su}(\phi\mathbin{\rhd}\psi)
.
Given a multiset
\Sigma
of
\mathbin{\rhd}
-formulas, we will write
\mathrm{an}(\Sigma)
to mean the
multiset
of antecedents in
\Sigma
and
\mathrm{su}(\Sigma)
to mean the
multiset
of succedents in
\Sigma
..

We use lower case Latin letters
p
,
q
, …, possibly with subscripts, for propositional variables
and lower case Greek letters
\phi
,
\psi
, …, possibly with subscripts,
for
\mathsf{IL}
-formulas.
To avoid too many parentheses in longer formulas,
we treat
\rhd
as having higher priority than
\rightarrow
, but lower than other Boolean connectives.
Unary operators
\Box
,
\Diamond
and
\neg
have the highest priority.

The idea of interpretability logics originates by extending the usual interpretation of modal logic inside arithmatic
T
by adding a binary modality
\mathbin{\rhd}
.
Then
\phi\mathbin{\rhd}\psi
is understood as
T+\psi
is relative interpretable in
T+\phi
.
Some interpretability logics are sound and complete with respect to this semantics, e.g.,
\mathsf{ILM}
when we choose
T
to be Peano Arithmetic.

\mathsf{IL}
, the logic we are going to study, is sound with respect to many arithmetical theories, but incomplete.
However, it contains a good portion of the rest of interpretability logics and it is an appealing logic from the modal point of view.
For details the reader is encouraged to read
[
22
]
.

In some proofs we will use the following auxiliary definition of a size of an
\mathsf{IL}
-formula.

Definition 2.1
.

The
size

|\phi|
of an
\mathsf{IL}
-formula
\phi
is defined recursively as follows:

|\bot|=1,\qquad|p|=1,\qquad|\phi\to\psi|=|\phi\mathbin{\rhd}\psi|=|\phi|+|\psi|+1.

We define the interpretability logic we will consider in this paper.

Definition 2.2
.

Interpretability logic
\mathsf{IL}
is the smallest set of
\mathsf{IL}
-formulas that contains all the classical propositional tautologies and axioms

\displaystyle(\mathrm{K})\quad\Box(\phi\rightarrow\psi)\rightarrow(\Box\phi\rightarrow\Box\psi),

\displaystyle(\mathrm{4})\quad\Box\phi\rightarrow\Box\Box\phi,

\displaystyle(\mathrm{L})\quad\Box(\Box\phi\to\phi)\to\Box\phi,

\displaystyle(\mathrm{J1})\quad\Box(\phi\rightarrow\psi)\rightarrow(\phi\rhd\psi),

\displaystyle(\mathrm{J2})\quad(\phi\rhd\chi)\wedge(\chi\rhd\psi)\rightarrow(\phi\rhd\psi),

\displaystyle(\mathrm{J3})\quad(\phi\rhd\psi)\wedge(\chi\rhd\psi)\rightarrow(\phi\vee\chi)\rhd\psi,

\displaystyle(\mathrm{J4})\quad\phi\rhd\psi\rightarrow(\Diamond\phi\rightarrow\Diamond\psi).

\displaystyle(\mathrm{J5})\quad\Diamond\phi\rhd\phi

and is closed under modus ponens and necessitation:

\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 14.57222pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\phi\to\psi$}\hskip 4.0pt}\hbox{\hskip 14.45377pt}\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\phi$}\hskip 4.0pt}}\vskip 2.0pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to59.4398pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}}\vskip 2.0pt\hbox{\hskip 22.46294pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\psi$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}}\ignorespaces\ ,\qquad\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 14.47221pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hskip 3.8889pt\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\phi$}\hskip 4.0pt}}\vskip 2.0pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to21.73615pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}}\vskip 2.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Box\phi$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}\ignorespaces\ .

Sometimes we will be referring to axiom (L) as
Löb axiom
.
Note that the definition of
\mathsf{IL}
is not minimal.
In particular
(\mathrm{J4})
is derivable from
(\mathrm{J2})
.

In the following lemma we will put together some basic properties of
\mathsf{IL}
.
These results will be used in some proofs in the remainder of this paper.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\phi,\psi
be formulas and
\Sigma
be a non-empty finite multiset of formulas.
Then

1.

\mathsf{IL}\vdash\phi\to\psi
implies
\mathsf{IL}\vdash\phi\mathbin{\rhd}\psi
.

2.

(Löb’s rule in
\mathsf{IL}
)
\mathsf{IL}\vdash\psi\wedge\bigwedge(\Sigma\mathbin{\rhd}\bot)\to\bigvee\Sigma
implies
\mathsf{IL}\vdash\psi\mathbin{\rhd}\bigvee\Sigma
.

3.

\mathsf{IL}\vdash\phi\mathbin{\rhd}\mathop{\rule[0.5pt]{6.0pt}{6.0pt}}\phi
.

Proof.

The proof of 1. is trivial using necessitation and (J1).
Let us prove 2. and 3. in detail.

Assume
\mathsf{IL}\vdash\psi\wedge\bigwedge(\Sigma\mathbin{\rhd}\bot)\to\bigvee\Sigma
.
From classical propositional reasoning we obtain

\mathsf{IL}\vdash\psi\to\bigvee\Sigma\vee\bigvee(\Diamond\Sigma)
, so by using 1. we obtain

\mathsf{IL}\vdash\psi\mathbin{\rhd}\left(\bigvee\Sigma\vee\bigvee\Diamond\Sigma\right)

(i)

Let
\Sigma=\{\phi_{0},\ldots,\phi_{m}\}
and notice that
\mathsf{IL}\vdash\Diamond\phi_{j}\mathbin{\rhd}\phi_{j}
for each
j\leq m
thanks to (J5).
For each
j\leq m
we also have by 1. that
\mathsf{IL}\vdash\phi_{j}\mathbin{\rhd}\phi_{j}
,
so we get by (J3) that
\mathsf{IL}\vdash(\phi_{j}\vee\Diamond\phi_{j})\mathbin{\rhd}\phi_{j}
.
Also from
\mathsf{IL}\vdash\phi_{j}\to\bigvee_{i\leq m}\phi_{i}

by 1. we get
\mathsf{IL}\vdash\phi_{j}\mathbin{\rhd}\bigvee_{i\leq m}\phi_{i}
.
Then, using (J2) we obtain
\mathsf{IL}\vdash(\phi_{j}\vee\Diamond\phi_{j})\mathbin{\rhd}\bigvee_{i\leq m}\phi_{i}
for each
j\leq m
and, by (J3),

\mathsf{IL}\vdash\left(\bigvee_{i\leq m}(\phi_{i}\vee\Diamond\phi_{i})\right)\mathbin{\rhd}\bigvee_{i\leq m}\phi_{i}.

(ii)

Since the formulas
\left(\bigvee_{i\leq m}\phi_{i}\right)\vee\left(\bigvee_{i\leq m}\Diamond\phi_{i}\right)
and
\bigvee_{i\leq m}(\phi_{i}\vee\Diamond\phi_{i})
are equivalent in classical propositional logic by 1. we obtain

\mathsf{IL}\vdash\left(\bigvee\Sigma\vee\bigvee\Diamond\Sigma\right)\mathbin{\rhd}\left(\bigvee_{i\leq m}(\phi_{i}\vee\Diamond\phi_{i})\right)

(iii)

where we used that
\left(\bigvee_{i\leq m}\phi_{i}\right)\vee\left(\bigvee_{i\leq m}\Diamond\phi_{i}\right)
is just the same formula as
\bigvee\Sigma\vee\bigvee\Diamond\Sigma
.
So using (
i
), (
ii
), (
iii
) and the (J2) axiom gives us

\mathsf{IL}\vdash\psi\mathbin{\rhd}\bigvee_{i\leq m}\phi_{i},

as desired.

Proof of 3.
By Löb’s axiom we obtain that
\mathsf{IL}\vdash\Box(\Box\neg\phi\to\neg\phi)\to\Box\neg\phi
.
Unfolding some definitions of
\Box
we get
\mathsf{IL}\vdash\neg(\Box\neg\phi\to\neg\phi)\mathbin{\rhd}\bot\to\neg\neg\phi\mathbin{\rhd}\bot
.
Since
\mathsf{IL}\vdash\neg\neg\phi\leftrightarrow\phi
and
\mathsf{IL}\vdash\neg(\Box\neg\phi\to\neg\phi)\leftrightarrow(\Box\neg\phi\wedge\phi)
, using 1. and (J2) we get
\mathsf{IL}\vdash(\Box\neg\phi\wedge\phi)\mathbin{\rhd}\bot\to\phi\mathbin{\rhd}\bot
, or in other words
\mathsf{IL}\vdash((\neg\neg\phi\mathbin{\rhd}\bot)\wedge\phi)\mathbin{\rhd}\bot\to\phi\mathbin{\rhd}\bot
.
Using again that
\mathsf{IL}\vdash\neg\neg\phi\leftrightarrow\phi
with 1. and (J2) (multiple times) we obtain
\mathsf{IL}\vdash((\phi\mathbin{\rhd}\bot)\wedge\phi)\mathbin{\rhd}\bot\to\phi\mathbin{\rhd}\bot
, i.e.,
\mathsf{IL}\vdash\mathop{\rule[0.5pt]{6.0pt}{6.0pt}}\phi\mathbin{\rhd}\bot\to\phi\mathbin{\rhd}\bot
.
Adding
\phi
on both sides we have
\mathsf{IL}\vdash\phi\wedge(\mathop{\rule[0.5pt]{6.0pt}{6.0pt}}\phi\mathbin{\rhd}\bot)\to\phi\wedge\phi\mathbin{\rhd}\bot
, or analogously,
\mathsf{IL}\vdash\phi\wedge(\mathop{\rule[0.5pt]{6.0pt}{6.0pt}}\phi\mathbin{\rhd}\bot)\to\mathop{\rule[0.5pt]{6.0pt}{6.0pt}}\phi
.
Using 2. we conclude the desired
\mathsf{IL}\vdash\phi\mathbin{\rhd}\mathop{\rule[0.5pt]{6.0pt}{6.0pt}}\phi
.
∎

2.2.2
The Method of Translations.

In
[
20
]
we developed a method to construct translations between local progress calculi, i.e., to provide functions transforming proofs of one calculus into proofs (not necessarily of the same sequent) in another calculus.
Here, we will introduce informally the concepts and methods, the interested reader should consult
[
20
]
for more details.

The idea goes as follows.
Given a proof
\pi
in a local progress calculus
G
we can define a partition of its nodes, the elements of the partition will be called
local fragments
.
Two nodes will belong to the same local fragment if the smallest path between them does not go through progress.
Here, with passing through progress we mean going from the premise to the conclusion of a rule instance, or from conclusion to premise, such that the premise is progressing in the rule instance.
Thanks to the condition that any infinite branch progresses infinitely often, it is easy to see that each local fragment will be a finite tree, in other words, this slices the non-wellfounded tree into (possibly infinitely many) finite trees.
Figure
2
shows how the slicing can look in this setting, where each triangle represents a local fragment.

The bottom-most local fragment, i.e., the one to which the root belongs to, is called the
main local fragment
.
We define the
local height
of a proof
\pi
, denoted as
\lheight(\pi)
, as the height of its main local fragment (which is a finite tree, so indeed it has a height).

Finally, the translation method goes as follows.
To define a function from local progress Gentzen calculus
G
to local progress Gentzen calculus

, it suffices to provide another function (called

) that, given a proof
\pi
in

1.

a local fragment in
G^{\prime}

where every leaf is either axiomatic or a progressing premise and every progressing premise is a leaf;

2.

for each non-axiomatic leaf (of the local fragment) with sequent
S

S
in
G
.

Then, the desired translation function is obtained by extending this corecursive step via corecursion.

Properties of Rules.

Finally we introduce some properties of rules and proofs that will be fundamental to show cut elimination.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
R
be an
n
-ary rule,
\mathcal{G}
be a local progress Gentzen calculus and
\pi
a proof in
\mathcal{G}+R
.
We say that

1.

R
is
derivable
in
\mathcal{G}
if for any
(S_{0},\ldots,S_{n-1},S)\in R
there is a derivation in
\mathcal{G}
with assumptions
\{S_{0},\ldots,S_{n-1}\}
and conclusion
S
.

2.

R
is
admissible
in
\mathcal{G}
if for any instance
(S_{0},\ldots,S_{n-1},S)
of the rule
R
,
\mathcal{G}\vdash S_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{G}\vdash S_{n-1}
implies that
\mathcal{G}\vdash S
.

3.

R
is
invertible
if for each
i<n
, the rule

R^{-1}_{i}=\{(S_{n},S_{i})\mid\text{Exists }S_{0},\ldots,S_{i-1},S_{i+1},\ldots S_{n-1}.\ (S_{0},\ldots,S_{n})\in R\}

is admissible.
In words, if each of the rules which says that from the conclusion you can infer the premises is admissible.

4.

R
is
eliminable
in
\mathcal{G}
if for any sequent
S
if
\mathcal{G}+R\vdash S
then
\mathcal{G}\vdash S
.

5.

\pi
is
locally
R
-free
if it contains no instances of
R
in its main local fragment.

6.

R
is
locally admissible
in
\mathcal{G}
if for any instance
(S_{0},\ldots,S_{n-1},S)
of the rule if
\mathcal{G}\vdash S_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{G}\vdash S_{n-1}
with locally
R
-free proofs, then there is a locally
R
-free proof of
\mathcal{G}\vdash S
.

7.

R
is
locally eliminable
if for any
S
, if
\mathcal{G}+R\vdash S
then there is a locally
R
-free proof in
\mathcal{G}+R
of
S
.

The (local) admissibility/eliminability properties can be understood as asserting the existence of a proof
\pi
from the assumption that some proofs
\pi_{0},\ldots,\pi_{n-1}
exist.
Let
P
be a property of proofs, we say that any of the properties above holds
preserving
P
if, adding the extra assumption that
\tau_{0},\ldots,\tau_{n-1}
fulfill
P
,
\pi
also fulfills
P
.
In particular, we will say that we have (local) admissibility/eliminability of a rule
R
preserving height if
\height(\pi)\leq\max(\height(\pi_{0}),\ldots,\height(\pi_{n-1}))
and similarly for local height.

Note that derivability implies eliminability which implies admissibility.
The fundamental lemma to show cut elimination is the following.

Lemma 2.8
.

For any local progress sequent calculi, the following holds

R\text{ eliminable iff }R\text{ locally eliminable iff }R\text{ locally admissible}.

Proof.

That
R
is eliminable trivially implies that
R
is locally admissible.
To show that
R
locally admissible implies
R
locally eliminable it suffices to do an induction in the local height.
Finally, to show that
R
locally eliminable implies that
R
is eliminable it suffices to apply the method of translations using local eliminability to define a corecursive step.
∎

In addition we notice the following facts.

1.

In a local-progress calculus
\mathcal{G}
,
R
is locally admissibile preserving local height implies that
R
is eliminable preserving height.

2.

In a wellfounded calculus
\mathcal{G}
,
R
is admissibile preserving local height implies that
R
is eliminable preserving height.

3.

In a local-progress calculus
\mathcal{G}
,
R
is locally admissibile preserving local
R^{\prime}
-freeness implies that
R
is eliminable preserving local
R^{\prime}
-freeness.

4.

In a wellfounded calculus
\mathcal{G}
,
R
is admissibile preserving
R^{\prime}
-freeness implies that
R
is eliminable preserving
R^{\prime}
-freeness.

Let

8
Future work

There are multiple directions for future work.
First, not much has been done with respect to uniform interpolation of bimodal (an exception being
[
10
]
) and unary interpretability logics.
Due to the generality of our method, it should be possible, once the correct sequent calculi for these logics are found, to adapt the presented results for these logics.

In a different direction, it should be possible to extend our techniques to show Lyndon uniform interpolation.
This will imply that multiple provability logics would have one of the strongest possible interpolation property.

Finally, it is worth noting that attempts to demonstrate uniform interpolation of
\mathsf{IL}
by semantic methods have been unsuccessful.
Hence, our new techniques based on non-wellfounded proofs have created a gap between what can be achieved by semantic and syntactic methods.
It will be interesting to find a semantic proof of uniform interpolation and close this gap, as this would yield semantics tools that corresponds to our non-wellfounded proofs.
