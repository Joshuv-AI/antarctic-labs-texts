NLRC5 expression within tumor cells is critical to activate adaptive and innate antitumor immune responses
Authors: Akhil Shukla, Mohammad Moradzad, Akouavi Julite Irmine Quenum, Jean-François Lucier, Anny Armas Cayarga, Dominique Lévesque, François-Michel Boisvert, Thomas A Kufer, Sheela Ramanathan, Subburaj Ilangumaran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTumors downregulate NLRC5 expression to reduce MHC-I and evade CD8+ T cell–mediated killing. Introducing full-length NLRC5 (N5FL) or NLRC5-CIITA chimeric protein (N5CA) can restore tumor immunogenicity. Here, we elucidated the underlying mechanisms and also evaluated the requirement of NLRC5 expression in antigen-presenting cells (APCs) of the host for tumor control.Materials and methods Nlrc5−/− and Nlrc5+/+ mice were implanted with B16-F10 melanoma expressing N5FL or NC5A. Tumor growth, activation of lymphoid cells, their infiltration into tumors and the requirement for tumor control were assessed. The proteomes of B16-F10 cells expressing N5FL or N5CA were analyzed.ResultsNLRC5-expressing tumors were efficiently controlled in both Nlrc5+/+ and Nlrc5−/− mice, characterized by infiltration of CD8+ and CD4+ cells, natural killer (NK) cells, NK T (NKT) cells and invariant NKT (iNKT) cells, increased tumor vascularization and upregulation of Ccl4 and Cxcl9 genes. Both CD8+ and NK1.1+ cells contribute to NLRC5-dependent tumor control. NLRC5 expression in the Tumor Cancer Genome Atlas–skin cutaneous melanoma correlated with NK cell markers and nonclassical MHC-Ib expression. Proteomes of NLRC5-expressing tumor cells showed downmodulation of dominant tumor antigens, but displayed significant changes in the lysosome and the proteasome pathways, upregulated the protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum pathway, and downregulated the autophagy pathway, all of which could impact processing and presentation of subdominant tumor antigens.ConclusionNLRC5 expression in APCs is dispensable for NLRC5-dependent antitumor immunity. NLRC5-dependent tumor control requires both CD8+ and NK1.1+ cells. Tumor-intrinsic NLRC5 promotes vasculature that is permissive to infiltration by antitumor immune cells. In addition to upregulating MHC-I, NLRC5 may enhance alternative antigen-processing pathways and attenuate autophagic degradation of MHC-I, thereby augmenting tumor immunogenicity.

Introduction

A central cancer immune evasion mechanism involves the downregulation of major histocompatibility Class I (MHC-I) and antigen-processing machinery (APM) genes [1]. This defect impairs tumor antigen presentation and renders tumors less immunogenic, allowing them to evade killing by CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and remain unresponsive to immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy. Restoring MHC-I expression to increase tumor immunogenicity remains a challenging endeavor [2].

Downmodulation of MHC-I in tumors mainly results from the loss of NLRC5 (“NOD-Like Receptor CARD domain containing 5”) [3]. NLRC5 is the master transcriptional activator of MHC-I and several APM genes and is required for cancer immunosurveillance and cancer immunoediting [4]. Expressing NLRC5 in MHC-I-low tumors upregulates MHC-I and APM genes and restores tumor immunogenicity [5]. Reduced NLRC5 expression in tumors correlates with diminished CTL infiltration and unresponsiveness to ICI therapy [6]. These studies raised the possibility of exploiting NLRC5 for immunotherapy against MHC-I-low immune evasive cancers [7, 8]. As the large molecular size of full-length NLRC5 (NLRC5-FL; N5FL) hampers its translational utility, we tested a smaller engineered NLRC5-CIITA fusion protein (NLRC5-CA; N5CA) that efficiently induces MHC-I [7].

Activation of naive antitumor CD8+ T cells occurs in tumor-draining lymph nodes (DLNs) by antigen-presenting cells (APCs) [9]. Activated CD8+ T cells proliferate and differentiate into effector CTLs, enter the circulation, traffic to the tumor, recognize cancer cells, and release cytotoxic granules to kill them. Release of tumor antigens and their presentation to naive CD8+ T cells perpetuates the cancer immunity cycle, leading to tumor control. Thus, the loss of NLRC5 and downmodulation of MHC-I in cancers will not only impair tumor cell killing but also compromise the tumor immunity cycle and restrain the breadth of antitumor CTL responses. Dendritic cells (DCs) acquire tumor antigens from dead tumor cells and cross-present tumor antigenic peptides on their own MHC-I molecules along with costimulatory ligands. However, the impact of NLRC5 expression in cancer cells and APCs on antitumor immune responses has not yet been addressed.

In addition to T cells, innate immune cells such as natural killer (NK) cells also contribute to tumor immunosurveillance [10]. NK cells kill cancer cells by recognizing the absence of self-MHC-I molecules on their surface. In addition to upregulating classical MHC-Ia molecules, NLRC5 induces nonclassical MHC-Ib molecules that are recognized by activating and inhibitory NK cell receptors [11]. As NLRC5 deficiency compromises the expression of both classical and nonclassical MHC-I molecules, it is unclear why tumors that downregulate NLRC5 are not controlled by NK cells.

To investigate how NLRC5 expression in tumor cells impacts the activation of immune cells and to determine the role of NLRC5 expressed in host APCs on antitumor immune responses, we studied tumor formation by B16-F10 melanoma cells expressing NLRC5-FL or NLRC5-CA in NLRC5-deficient and control mice and the associated cellular and molecular immune mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and growth assay

B16-F10 murine melanoma cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (Virginia, USA). B16-F10 cells expressing NLRC5-FL (B16-N5FL), NLRC5-CA (B16-N5CA), or the control vector (B16-V) have been described [5, 7]. Cell growth was assessed using the WST-8 (water-soluble Tetrazolium-8) assay kit (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, #CK04).

Mice and husbandry

NOD.scid.gamma (NSG) mice (NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ) were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME USA). Nlrc5−/− and Nlrc5+/+ mice in the C57BL/6J background have been previously described [4]. Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions in ventilated cages according to the “Guide to Care and Use of Experimental Animals, Canadian Council on Animal Care.” All experiments were performed during daytime. Both male and female mice were used and the data were pooled, as there was no significant difference in tumor growth control by NLRC5 between the sexes. All experiments on mice were carried out following the ARRIVE guidelines with the approval of the “Université de Sherbrooke Ethics Committee for Animal Care and Use” under protocol number 2023-4043.

Tumor growth and tissue processing

B16-V (control), B16-N5FL, and B16-N5CA cell lines were injected subcutaneously into the right flanks of 8-week-old NSG, Nlrc5−/−, and Nlrc5+/+ mice. After palpable tumor formation, tumor length and width were recorded every 2 days. Tumor volume was calculated using the ellipsoid formula: V = ½×Length × Width2. At the tumor endpoint (2 cm in diameter), mice were euthanized with CO2 under isofluorane anesthesia, and the tumors were excised and photographed. Tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for paraffin embedding, snap-frozen for protein analysis, or preserved in RNAprotect Tissue Reagent (Qiagen, #76106) for gene expression studies. Fresh tumor tissues were used for flow cytometry.

Histochemistry

Tumor sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Stained sections were scanned using the Digital NanoZoomer (Hamamatsu Photonics 2.0-RS). For collagen matrix staining, deparaffinized tissue sections were immersed in 0.05% fast green (Sigma, #F7252) and then in 1% acetic acid, followed by picrosirius red staining. Slides were dehydrated, mounted, and imaged using the Digital NanoZoomer. Images were analyzed using the Nanozoomer Digital Pathology (NDP View 2) software. Necrotic areas in H&E-stained sections and collagen deposition in Sirius red-stained sections were quantified from at least three tumors and nine random-field areas using the NIH ImageJ software (version 1.53e; https://imagej.net/ij/download.html).

Immunofluorescence

Deparaffinized tissue sections were rehydrated, and antigen retrieval was performed using a Retriever (Electron Microscopy Sciences, 62700). After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in 0.1% Triton-X (Sigma, T8787), sections were incubated with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table S1) overnight. After staining with fluorochrome-conjugated secondary antibodies (Supplementary Table S2) and Hoechst (Invitrogen, H3570) nuclear staining, the slides were washed and mounted in DAKO mounting medium (DAKO S3023). Slides were visualized under a Zeiss Axioscope fluorescence microscope. Positively stained areas were quantified using the NIH ImageJ software.

Flow cytometry

Fresh tumor tissues were mechanically disrupted using a Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotech, 130-096-730) to isolate tumor infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs). Inguinal lymph nodes from the tumor-draining side and tumor nondraining contralateral side were processed to obtain single-cell suspensions. TILs and tumor-draining and nondraining lymph node cells were suspended in 2% FBS–PBS, counted and stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies (Supplementary Table S3). Stained cells were analyzed using CytoFLEX-30 (Beckman Coulter) flow cytometer. Data were analyzed using the FlowJo™ software (BD Biosciences).

Gene expression by RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from tumor tissues using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, #4134). cDNA was synthesized using QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen, #205311). Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was conducted using specific primers (Supplementary Table S4) and SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, 1725274) in a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR system (Bio-Rad). Cycle threshold (Ct) values were used to normalize gene expression levels between different samples and the housekeeping gene m36B4 (Rplp0). Fold induction was calculated by comparing the expression levels in B16-V tumors of Nlrc5+/+ control mice.

Cell depletion

CD8+ and NK1.1+ cells were depleted using anti-CD8α (clone 53-6.7, #BE0004-1, BioXcell; 25 μg/mouse) and anti-NK1.1 (PK136, #BE0036, BioXcell; 200 μg/mouse) monoclonal antibodies, respectively. Rat IgG2a (clone 2A3, #BE0089, BioXcell) was used as an isotype control. Antibodies were administered intraperitoneally on days −1 and +1 relative to tumor implantation and every 7–8 days thereafter. Cell depletion was confirmed using flow cytometry.

Statistics

Data analysis and graphic plotting were carried out using the GraphPad Prism (San Diego, CA, USA; version 10.4.1).

Tumor Cancer Genome Atlas–skin cutaneous melanoma transcriptomic data analysis

Correlation between the expression of NLRC5 and candidate genes in the human Tumor Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)–skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM) dataset was analyzed using the cBioPortal platform [12].

Proteomic analysis

Protein samples prepared from frozen cell pellets were digested with trypsin, and peptides were profiled by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. Proteins were identified as described in the Supplementary Methods. The mass spectrometry data are deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE [13] partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD060523. Significantly modulated proteins were identified using the cut-off values of fold change (FC) ≤ −1 and ≥1 and adjusted P-value <.05. Proteomic data analysis is described in the Supplementary Methods.

Results

Immune-mediated tumor control requires NLRC5 expression in tumor cells

B16-N5FL, B16-N5CA, and B16-V cells showed similar viability and in vitro expansion, indicating that NLRC5-FL or NLRC5-CA did not affect cell growth (Supplementary Fig. S1a). Subcutaneous tumors formed by B16-V, B16-N5FL, and B16-N5CA cells in NSG mice showed comparable growth and reached the endpoint within 14 days (Supplementary Fig. S1b and d). Upon implantation into immunocompetent C57Bl/6 mice, B16-V tumors reached only half the size of those growing in NSG mice at 14 days post-implantation, indicating that a functional immune system can restrain MHC-I-low B16-V tumors (Supplementary Fig. S1c). B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA cells formed significantly smaller tumors than B16-V cells in C57Bl/6 mice (Supplementary Fig. S1c and d). To distinguish the impact of NLRC5 expression in tumor cells from that of endogenous NLRC5 expressed in APCs, we compared B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA tumors in Nlrc5−/− and littermate Nlrc5+/+ mice. Whereas B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA tumors were controlled in Nlrc5+/+ hosts as expected [7], Nlrc5−/− hosts also efficiently controlled these tumors (Fig. 1a–d; Supplementary Fig. S2). B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA tumors contained prominent areas of necrosis compared with B16-V tumors in both Nlrc5+/+ and Nlrc5−/− hosts (Fig. 1e, f; Supplementary Fig. S3). These necrotic areas are unlikely to arise from nutrient and oxygen deprivation caused by tumor growth, as they were not evident in larger B16-V tumors. Alternatively, these necrotic lesions could arise from immune cell–mediated killing, which is supported by abundant CD45+ cell infiltration (Fig. 1g and h). These results indicate that tumor-intrinsic NLRC5 expression is necessary, whereas NLRC5 expression in APCs is not required, for immune cell infiltration and tumor control, which can be achieved by expressing NLRC5-CA in tumor cells.

Tumor-intrinsic NLRC5 expression is associated with increased immune cell infiltration and activation

Next, we characterized the cellular composition of TILs, tumor DLNs and tumor non-DLNs (NDLNs) (Fig. 2). As the tumors differed in size, TILs were normalized to the tumor mass. CD45+ cells were significantly increased in B16-N5FL tumors compared with B16-V tumors in Nlrc5+/+ and Nlrc5−/− mice, whereas CD45+ cells in B16-N5CA tumors were abundant only in Nlrc5−/− mice (Fig. 2a). However, the DLN of B16-N5CA tumors harbored elevated numbers of CD45+ cells in both Nlrc5+/+ and Nlrc5−/− hosts (Fig. 2a). Immunophenotyping of TILs and tumor draining and nontumor draining lymph node cells was performed following the gating strategy shown in Supplementary Fig. S4. The absolute numbers of lymphoid cell subsets were calculated based on their proportions (Supplementary Fig. S5). B16-N5FL TILs harbored significantly more CD3+, CD4+, and CD8+ T cells than B16-V TILs in Nlrc5+/+ but not in Nlrc5−/− hosts, whereas these cell numbers were not increased in B16-N5CA TILs in either host (Fig. 2b–d). However, total CD3+, CD8+, and CD4+ T-cell numbers were significantly elevated in the draining lymph nodes of B16-N5CA tumors in both Nlrc5+/+ and Nlrc5−/− hosts (Fig. 2b–d). These cells were increased in the draining lymph nodes of B16-N5FL tumors only in Nlrc5−/− mice. Subset analysis of CD8+ T cells showed significantly more CD69+ activated effector (TE) cells in B16-N5FL TILs of Nlrc5+/+ mice and in TILs and draining lymph nodes of B16-N5CA tumors in Nlrc5+/+ and Nlrc5−/− hosts (Fig. 2e). CD44+CD62Llo effector memory (TEM) cells were also enriched in B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA TILs of both Nlrc5+/+ and Nlrc5−/− hosts (Fig. 2f). CD44+CD62L+ central memory (TCM) and CD44loCD62L+ naive (TN) were increased in B16-N5FL but not in B16-N5CA TILs of Nlrc5+/+ mice, whereas these cells were not increased in Nlrc5−/− hosts (Fig. 2g and h). Tumor-intrinsic NLRC5 expression also promotes CD4+ T-cell activation and tumor infiltration. Notably, CD69+ TE, CD44+CD62Llo TEM, and CD44+CD62L+ TCM CD4+ cells were increased in B16-N5FL TILs, whereas all CD4+ T-cell subsets were increased in the draining lymph nodes of B16-N5CA tumors in Nlrc5+/+ hosts (Supplementary Fig. S6a–d). All CD4+ T-cell subsets were increased in the draining lymph nodes of B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA tumors in Nlrc5−/− hosts. These data indicate that NLRC5-FL and NLRC5-CA expressed in tumor cells promote the activation and differentiation of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells and their infiltration into tumors and that these events can occur independently of NLRC5 expression in host APCs.

NK1.1+ cell subsets displayed differential enrichment in TILs and draining lymph nodes of B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA tumors. NK1.1+CD3− NK cells were significantly enriched in B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA TILs in Nlrc5+/+ and Nlrc5−/− hosts (Fig. 2e). NLRC5 expression in tumor cells promotes NK cell–mediated tumor control

Activation of NK cell subsets is regulated by activating and inhibitory receptors [14]. The expression of activating receptor genes Klrb1c (NK1.1), Klrk1 (NKG2D), and Klrc2 (NKG2C) was significantly elevated in B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA tumors compared with B16-V tumors in Nlrc5+/+ mice, although another activating receptor, Ncr1 (NKp46), was not upregulated (Fig. 3a, left panel). Notably, the inhibitory receptor Klrd1 (CD94) was also upregulated in B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA tumors in Nlrc5+/+ mice. Nlrc5−/− mice showed a similar pattern of NK activating and inhibitory receptor gene expression in B16-N5FL tumors as in B16-N5CA tumors (Fig. 3a, right panel). Expression of Klra10 (Ly49j), a pseudogene in mice, and that of the NK cell ligand Rae1 were not affected in B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA tumors in both hosts (Fig. 3a). Interferon gamma (IFNγ) is a key effector cytokine produced by NK, NKT, and iNKT cells. B16-N5CA tumors from Nlrc5+/+ mice showed elevated Ifng and Ifnb expression compared with B16-V tumors (Fig. 3b, left panel). Upregulation of these genes in B16-N5FL tumors was not statistically significant. In contrast, B16-N5FL tumors showed significant induction of Ifng and Ifna genes in Nlrc5−/− hosts (Fig. 3b, right panel). These observations indicate that tumor-intrinsic NLRC5 expression promotes NK, NKT, and iNKT cell recruitment and activation, which could be modulated by endogenous NLRC5 expression in host cells.

NLRC5 expression has been associated with the recruitment and activation of CD8+ T cells but not NK cells in human melanoma, based on the lack of correlation between NLRC5 and the NK cell marker CD56 (NCAM1) expression [3]. Therefore, we evaluated the correlation between the expression of NLRC5 and NK cell receptor genes in the TCGA–SKCM dataset. We found a strong positive correlation between NLRC5 and several NK-activating receptor genes KLRC2 (NKG2C), KLRK1 (NKG2D), NCR1 (NKp46), and KLRB1 (NKR-P1C, the human equivalent of NK1.1) as well as the inhibitory receptor KLRD1 (CD94) (Fig. 3c). No correlation was observed between NLRC5 and NCAM1 expression levels. NLRC5 expression significantly correlated with CD8A and CD4, and transcription factors TBX21 (TBET), EOMES, GATA3, and RORC (RORγT), which regulate the differentiation of CD4 and CD8 T cells, NK cells, and ILC cells (Supplementary Fig. S7a and b). Accordingly, NLRC5 strongly correlated with IFNG expression but minimally with IFNB1 or IL22 (Supplementary Fig. S7c). Moreover, NLRC5 expression correlated with the classical MHC-Ia genes HLA-A, B, and C as well as with the nonclassical MHC-Ib genes HLA-E, F, and G, and MHC-II genes HLA-DRA, DRB1, and DPA1 (Supplementary Fig. S8a–c).

Next, we investigated the requirement for NK1.1+ cells in tumor control. Depletion of either CD8+ or NK1.1+ cells significantly increased the growth of B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA tumors in Nlrc5+/+ hosts (Fig. 3d–f). CD8+ T-cell depletion increased the growth of B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA tumors in Nlrc5−/− mice as well (Fig. 3g–i). However, depletion of NK1.1+ cells in Nlrc5−/− mice increased the growth of B16-N5CA but not B16-N5FL tumors (Fig. 3g and i). These data indicate that both CD8+ T cells and NK1.1+ cells are critical for NLRC5-dependent tumor control and that the C-terminal end of endogenous NLRC5 expression in host cells regulates the antitumor functions of NK cells.

Tumor-intrinsic NLRC5 expression establishes an immune-supportive tumor microenvironment

B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA tumors from both Nlrc5+/+ and Nlrc5−/− hosts were firmer in consistency than B16-V tumors, suggesting an altered tumor microenvironment (TME). Sirius red staining revealed markedly elevated collagen matrix deposition and abundant collagen-producing α-smooth muscle actin-positive myofibroblasts in NLRC5-expressing tumors (Supplementary Fig. S9a–d). We also observed that B16-N5FL tumors were reddish in NSG, Nlrc5+/+, and Nlrc5−/− hosts compared with the black color of B16-V tumors, whereas B16-N5CA tumors were reddish-brown (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. S1d). NLRC5 expression in endothelial cells (ECs) can promote angiogenesis [15]. As B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA tumors harbored more immune cells than B16-V tumors, we hypothesized that NLRC5-FL and NLRC5-CA promote tumor vascularization, facilitating immune cell infiltration. Tumor vascularization can arise from angiogenesis through the proliferation of ECs, as well as through nonangiogenic mechanisms such as vasculogenic mimicry (VM), wherein cancer cells acquire EC-like properties and form vessel-like structures [16]. CD31 expression characterizes vascular ECs, whereas tumor cells undergoing VM can be detected using tomato lectin (TL) that binds luminal glycocalyx [17, 18]. B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA tumors displayed increased staining for CD31 and TL compared with B16-V tumors in both Nlrc5+/+ and Nlrc5−/− hosts (Fig. 4a–c). CD31 and TL staining markedly overlapped in B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA tumors in Nlrc5+/+ mice but minimally in Nlrc5−/− hosts (Fig. 4a). NLRC5 also showed a positive correlation with PECAM1 (CD31) in the TCGA–SKCM transcriptomic data (Fig. 4d). As immune cell infiltration into tumors is influenced by chemokines [19], we evaluated the expression of CCL and CXCL chemokine genes in tumor tissues. B16-N5FL tumors displayed elevated Ccl5 expression in Nlrc5+/+ mice, whereas B16-N5CA tumors showed Ccl4 upregulation compared with B16-V or B16-N5FL tumors in Nlrc5+/+ and Nlrc5−/− hosts (Fig. 4e). B16-N5FL tumors also showed significantly increased Ccl17 expression compared with B16-V and B16-N5CA tumors in Nlrc5+/+ mice but not in Nlrc5−/− mice. Among the CXCL chemokines, Cxcl9 was elevated in B16-N5CA tumors from Nlrc5+/+ mice, whereas Cxcl9 and Cxcl11 were upregulated in B16-N5FL tumors from Nlrc5−/− mice (Fig. 4f). These findings indicate that tumor-intrinsic NLRC5 expression increases tumor vascularization and alters chemokine expression, facilitating immune cell recruitment.

NLRC5 expression in tumor cells modulates protein processing and peptide transport machineries

To understand the molecular mechanisms that contribute to NLRC5-dependent tumor control, we performed a total proteome analysis of B16-V, B16-N5FL, and B16-N5CA cells. Quality control data are shown in Supplementary Fig. S8. Approximately 6000 proteins were detected in each sample with a combined total of 6208 individual proteins (Supplementary Fig. S10). Compared with B16-V cells, B16-N5FL cells showed significant modulation of 1372 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs), with 583 upregulated and 789 downregulated proteins. B16-N5CA cells showed 862 DEPs compared with B16-V cells with 412 upregulated and 450 downregulated proteins (Fig. 5a and b). Notably, PMEL, TYR, DCT, and TYRP1 proteins, which carry dominant CTL epitopes [20], were downregulated in B16-N5FL cells, and the latter three in B16-N5CA cells compared with B16-V cells (Fig. 5a and b). A comparison of B16-N5CA and B16-N5FL cells revealed 1200 DEPs with 740 upregulated and 460 downregulated proteins, with relative upregulation of PMEL, TYRP1, and DCT in B16-N5CA cells (Fig. 5c). Both B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA cells harbored a significant number of uniquely modulated proteins (Fig. 5d).

Gene ontology (GO) analysis of DEPs revealed notable enrichment of the GO:cellular compartment (CC) terms “melanosome” and “pigment granule” among the downregulated proteins of B16-N-5FL and B16-N5CA cells (Supplementary Fig. S11a and b). DEPs [FC ≥ 1, false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05] in these GO terms included proteins involved in all steps of melanin biosynthesis including key enzymes that catalyze melanin synthesis (TYR, DCT, and TYRP1), melanosome structure and biogenesis (PMEL/gp100), trafficking, ion transport, and regulatory molecules (Fig. 5e). The significant downmodulation of TYR, DCT, TYRP1, and PMEL, which harbor dominant CTL epitopes [20] in B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA cells suggest that tumor-intrinsic NLRC5 expression may facilitate efficient processing and presentation of the limiting amounts of dominant tumor antigens, and possibly subdominant antigens, and their loading onto the upregulated MHC-I molecules.

Peptides for loading MHC-I in the ER are generated mainly by cytosolic proteasomes and NLRC5 is known to increase the expression of Psmb8 and Psmb9 genes that code for the 20S core immunoproteasome components PSMB8 (β5i/LMP7) and PSMB9 (β1i/LMP2) [5, 21, 22]. Therefore, we examined DEPs in B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA cells within the functional modules of proteasomes, namely the 20S core proteasome, 19S regulatory particles, the immunoproteasome, and PA28 activators in B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA cells (Supplementary Fig. S12a). N5FL caused a significant upregulation of all three immunoproteasome components PSMB8, PSMB9, and PSMB10 (β2i/MECL1), whereas B16-N5CA cells did not upregulate these proteins. Moreover, B16-N5FL cells showed marked upregulation of the proteasome activator PA28αβ components PSME1 and PSME2, which are inducible by IFNγ and play a key role in antigen processing [22], with a concomitant reduction in the expression of PA200 (PSME4) (Supplementary Fig. S12). B16-N5CA cells not only showed a decrease in the expression of PSME4 but also downregulated PMSE1 and PSME2. N5FL also caused marked changes in the composition of the 19S regulatory particle [22], which were not observed in B16-N5CA cells. Consistent with these changes, unbiased gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) proteasome pathway showed a positive but nonsignificant enrichment [normalized enrichment score (NES) 1.219, FDR 0.159] in B16-N5FL cells suggesting a trend toward higher proteasome-related protein abundance, whereas B16-N5CA cells showed a significant negative enrichment (NES −1.528, FDR 0.0287), suggesting coordinated downregulation of proteasome components (Supplementary Fig. S12b). These observations suggest that the changes in proteasome composition caused by NLRC5 may alter proteasomal antigen processing, contributing to CD8+ T cell–mediated control of B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA tumors.

Another protein processing pathway that could impact tumor antigen presentation by MHC-I and is enriched in the upregulated proteome of B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA cells is the “lysosome” (Supplementary Fig. S11a and b). However, this pathway is also represented in the downregulated proteome of B16-N5FL cells. Moreover, the GO:CC terms “lytic vacuole” and “lysosome” are enriched among the upregulated proteins in B16-N5FL cells (Supplementary Fig. S11a) and among the downmodulated proteins in B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA cells (Supplementary Fig. S11b and d). KEGG pathway analysis also showed a similar enrichment of the “lysosome” pathway in B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA cells (Supplementary Fig. S13a, b, and d). Heat map representation of DEPs modulated within the GO:CC terms lysosome and lytic vacuole revealed the profound impact of N5FL and N5CA on various components of the lysosome pathway and lytic granules (Fig. 5f), reflecting their representation in both upregulated and downregulated proteomes of NLRC5-expressing cells (Supplementary Figs S11 and S13). Notably, N5FL caused an upregulation of cathepsin proteases CSTB, CSTC, and CSTA, while downregulating CSTD and CSTL, and a marked upregulation of several hydrolases and glycosidases (Fig. 5f). These changes were less pronounced in B16-N5CA cells. CSTB and CSTD have been implicated in MHC-I degradation, although cathepsins may also be involved in MHC-I antigen presentation [23, 24]. However, both B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA cells displayed marked downregulation of several V-ATPase isoforms (Fig. 5f) that cause lysosomal acidification, which is required for optimal protease activity involved in vacuolar pathways of MHC-I antigen presentation [25]. GSEA of KEGG lysosome pathway proteins showed significant negative enrichment in both B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA cells (Fig. 5g). These data suggest that NLRC5 expression within tumor cells may attenuate protein degradation in lysosomes, thereby augmenting the vacuolar pathway of MHC-I antigen presentation [26].

The upregulated proteome of B16-N5FL cells showed an enrichment of the “protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum (ER)” pathway (Supplementary Fig. S13a). This pathway regulates protein homeostasis through chaperone-assisted folding, glycosylation and sorting to different cellular compartments, and by recognizing misfolded proteins and promoting their ubiquitination, which is coupled to translocation to cytosol for proteasomal degradation [27]. This ER-associated degradation (ERAD) pathway is also implicated in MHC-I antigen presentation in cancer cells and antigen cross-presentation in APCs by promoting reverse translocation of antigenic peptides into the ER for loading onto MHC-I [24, 28]. Several proteins involved in various steps of the ER protein processing pathway, namely translocation, chaperone function, N-glycosylation, folding, quality control, and ERAD, were significantly upregulated in B16-N5FL cells and to a variable extent in N5CA cells (Fig. 5h). Notably, SEC61A1, SEC61A2, and SEC63 components of the SEC61 translocon that are implicated in the presentation of cytosolic peptides in TAP-deficient cancer cells [24] are highly upregulated in B16-N5FL cells (Fig. 5h). These findings suggest a potential contribution of NLRC5 in promoting alternate routes of peptide transport to the ER lumen for loading onto MHC-I molecules.

NLRC5 expression in tumor cells attenuates autophagy pathway

Next, we examined the DEPs shared between B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA cells (Fig. 6a). Among the 465 shared DEPs, the GO terms lytic vacuole, lysosome, melanosome, and pigment granule were highly enriched in B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA cells (Fig. 6b). Pathway analysis showed significant enrichment of phagosome and autophagy in B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA cells (Fig. 6c). Cancer cells use autophagy not only to remove damaged and stressed organelles and recover cellular biomass to fuel their growth but also as an immune evasion mechanism by promoting the degradation of MHC-I in autophagolysosomes [29]. Heat map analysis of DEPs in the autophagy pathway showed marked downmodulation of several key proteins in all stages of autophagy in B16-N5FL cells, many of which also showed reduced expression in B16-N5CA cells (Fig. 6d). These proteins include the ATG14, BECN1 and AMBRA1 of the phagophore initiation complex, several cargo receptors (SQSTM1/p62, NBR1, TAX1BP1, and OPTN) and proteins involved in ATG conjugation and elongation of the autophagosome (MAP1LC3, GABARAPL2, and ATG5). Among these proteins, NBR1, MAP1LC3B (LC3B), and GABARAPL2 are implicated in the degradation of MHC-I in autophagosomes [30, 31]. Additional proteins involved in autophagosome–lysosome fusion and lysosomal protein degradation were downregulated in N5FL cells (Fig. 6d), some of which were also represented in the vesicle trafficking module of the lysosome pathway (Fig. 5f). GSEA of the KEGG autophagy pathway showed significant negative enrichment in B16-N5FL (NES −1.574, FDR 0.00469) and B16-N5CA (NES −1.584, FDR 0.00588) cells compared with control cells (Fig. 6e). These data suggest that NLRC5 expression in tumor cells may attenuate MHC-I degradation through the autophagy pathway, contributing to enhanced tumor immunogenicity.

In addition to causing transcriptional upregulation of MHC-I and APM genes, tumor-intrinsic NLRC5 alters the expression of key proteins in proteasome components, lysosomal protein degradation compartments, the ERAD module and the autophagosome, which could impact cytosolic tumor antigen processing, potentially expanding the peptide diversity that could impact cross-dressing of APC and T-cell activation. Furthermore, tumor-intrinsic NLRC5 increases tumor vascularization and chemokine expression, establishing an immune-supportive TME that facilitates T- and NK-cell infiltration into tumors and efficient killing of cancer cells. The release of more tumor antigens can amplify antitumor T-cell responses and iteration of this process could lead to effective tumor control.

Discussion

Downmodulation of NLRC5 and its correlation with low MHC-I expression in many cancers have raised the prospects of exploiting NLRC5 to restore tumor immunogenicity [3, 6–8]. We have previously shown that the introduction of NLRC5-FL or NLRC5-CIITA fusion protein into B16 melanoma and EL4 thymoma upregulates the expression of MHC-I, B2m, and APM (Psmb8, Psmb9, Tap1, and Tapbp) genes, enhances tumor immunogenicity and promotes tumor control [5, 7]. Here, we demonstrate that tumor-intrinsic NLRC5 expression facilitates the activation of T and NK cells and their infiltration into tumors through establishing an immune-supportive TME, for which NLRC5 expression in APCs is not required. We also show that NLRC5 expression in cancer cells reduces proteolytic enzymes in lysosomes while increasing ERAD pathway proteins, which could increase peptide availability for MHC-I and alter the MHC-I peptide repertoire. Moreover, NLRC5 reduces the expression of proteins that promote MHC-I degradation in autophagosomes. Collectively, these NLRC5-induced changes could synergize with the transcriptional upregulation of MHC-I to boost tumor immunogenicity, reinforce the cancer immunity cycle and its iteration to establish effective antitumor immunity (Fig. 6f).

Mice lacking NLRC5 in DCs showed reduced MHC-I-restricted viral antigen specific CD8+ T-cell responses [32]. Antitumor CD8+ T cells are primed by type 1 conventional DCs (cDC1) via antigen cross-presentation in DLN [33]. Hence, we anticipated that the cross-presentation of tumor antigens by APCs would be less efficient in Nlrc5−/− mice than in Nlrc5+/+ mice. However, both B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA tumors were efficiently controlled in Nlrc5−/− hosts, indicating that tumor cell-intrinsic NLRC5 is sufficient to induce efficient antitumor immune responses. Indeed, the “cross-dressing” of tumor-derived MHC-I:peptide complexes, acquired by cDC1 cells via trogocytosis of tumor cells, is a key pathway to prime naive CD8+ T cells [34]. Even though direct evidence linking tumor-intrinsic NLRC5 expression to antigen cross-dressing is currently lacking, the dispensability of host NLRC5 in controlling NLRC5-expressing tumors suggests that elevated expression of MHC-I and tumor antigen presentation in these tumors may facilitate antigen cross-dressing of host APCs.

Our data also suggest that NLRC5 expression in tumor cells may widen the repertoire of tumor antigens presented to CD8+ T cells. Despite showing significant downmodulation of PMEL1, DCT, TYR and TRP1 bearing dominant CTL epitopes [20], B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA tumors displayed increased CD8+ T-cell activation in tumor draining lymph nodes and infiltration into tumors. Because reduced competition from dominant tumor antigens and increased antigen cross-presentation can elicit efficient antitumor responses toward subdominant tumor antigens [35], our data suggest that NLRC5-mediated tumor control could result from improved processing and presentation of subdominant tumor antigenic peptides. This could arise not only from the upregulation of MHC-I and the proteasomal antigen processing but also from reduced protein degradation in vacuolar compartments and increased peptide availability in the ER, resulting in improved antigen cross-dressing of APCs by NLRC5-expressing tumor cells. NLRC5 could attenuate vacuolar protein degradation pathways by reducing the activity of proteolytic enzymes and the expression of V-ATPases (Fig. 5f), which cause lysosomal acidification [25]. In parallel, NLRC5 could enhance the pool of peptides available for loading onto MHC-I in the ER lumen not only by upregulating TAP and TAPBP but also by upregulating the SEC61 translocon (Fig. 5h), which is implicated in the reverse translocation of cytosolic peptides into the ER [24, 28]. Even though peptide transfer via SEC61 is thought to be more efficient in TAP-deficient cancer cells [24], whether increased NLRC5 availability improves the efficiency of this pathway remains to be tested.

Increased tumor antigen presentation in NLRC5 expressing tumors may also arise from rescuing MHC-I from degradation in autophagosomes, resulting in improved cross-dressing in vivo. Even though autophagy can promote MHC-I-dependent antitumor immune response by boosting antigen presentation, it can also dampen this response by degrading MHC-I in autolysosomes [29–31, 36]. Autophagy induction in B16 melanoma cells reduces MHC-I expression, which can be overcome by IFNγ treatment [37]. Even though the latter could result from the induction of NLRC5 and the consequent increase in MHC-I gene transcription, our findings suggest that NLRC5 may also prevent the degradation of MHC-I in autophagosomes. Both B16-N5FL and B16-NC5A cells showed downmodulation of proteins involved in the formation of autophagosomes and in protein degradation within autolysosomes. These include MAP1LC3B (LC3B), GABARAPL2, and NBR1, which mediate key steps of phagophore formation, membrane expansion, and maturation of autophagosomes in the macroautophagy pathway, and proteolytic enzymes of the autolysosomes such as cathepsin L (CTSL) (Fig. 6e). Pancreatic cancers evade CTLs by upregulating NBR1 to promote MHC-I degradation in autophagosomes, whereas liver cancer cells achieve this goal by upregulating IRGQ that interacts with LC3B and GABARAPL2 [31]. Inhibiting CSTL promotes CTL response against head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [38]. Hence, delivering NLRC5 to tumors would promote MHC-I-dependent antitumor CTL response not only by upregulating canonical and noncanonical antigen presentation pathways but also by dampening MHC-I degradation through the autophagy pathway.

We observed enrichment of NK, NKT, and iNKT cells within the TILs of B16-N5FL tumors in Nlrc5+/+ mice and in the TILs of B16-N5CA tumors in Nlrc5−/− hosts, suggesting that NLRC5 promotes antitumor response of these innate immune cells that it is likely influenced by NLRC5 expressed in host cells. Moreover, NK1.1+ cells are needed for the control of B16-N5FL tumors in Nlrc5+/+ and Nlrc5−/− hosts. These findings stand in contrast to an earlier study suggesting that NLRC5 does not promote NK cell infiltration into tumors based on the lack of correlation between NLRC5 and the NK cell marker CD56 in the TCGA–SKCM transcriptomic data [3]. Even though NK1.1 is a widely used marker for NK and NKT cells, it is also expressed on subsets of γδ T cells and CD8+ T cells [39–41]. NLRC5 expression in tumor cells can promote γδ T-cell activation via induction of butyrophilin family proteins BTN3A1-3, which bind altered self-proteins such as phospho-antigens in cancer cells that are recognized by the γδ TCR in an MHC-independent manner [42]. Hence, it is possible that the increased growth of B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA tumors following depletion of NK1.1+ cells could result from the loss of not only NK, NKT, and iNKT cells but also subsets of γδ T and CD8+ T cells. NLRC5 also induces nonclassical MHC-Ib molecules such as Qa-1 that is recognized by the NK cell activating receptor NKG2C [11, 43]. NKG2C is upregulated in NLRC5 expressing B16 tumors (Fig. 3a), suggesting a role for MHC-Ib-driven NK cell responses in NLRC5-dependent tumor control. Dissecting the contribution of different NK1.1+ innate immune cells in tumor-intrinsic NLRC5-mediated tumor control would require the use mice specifically lacking NK, NKT, or γδT cells. Intriguingly, depletion of NK1.1+ cells enabled B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA tumors to grow aggressively in Nlrc5+/+ hosts but only that of B16-N5CA tumors in Nlrc5−/− mice (Fig. 3f and i). The reasons for the inability of NK1.1+ cell depletion to abolish the control B16-N5FL tumors in Nlrc5−/− hosts, despite elevated numbers of NK cells in these tumors (Fig. 2i), remain unclear. Because MHC-Ib molecules are implicated in NK cell education and function [44], our findings raise the possibility that NLRC5 expression in host cells may modulate NK cell education and their antitumor responses and that the latter could be impacted by NLRC5 expressed in tumors to modulate the overall efficacy of antitumor NK cell responses.

Our data show that NLRC5-expressing tumors show increased vascularity accompanied by efficient immune cell infiltration and tumor control in both Nlrc5+/+ and Nlrc5−/− hosts. In general, neo-vascularization is a hallmark of cancer that occurs mainly via neo-angiogenesis involving EC proliferation [45]. Transcriptional signatures of tumor ECs vary from those of ECs in physiological angiogenesis, which not only facilitates stromal remodeling and vessel growth but also causes endothelial anergy [16]. The latter alters trans-endothelial migration of leukocytes, diminishes their response to proinflammatory signaling, and promotes accumulation of immunosuppressive cell types and apoptosis of CTLs and NK cells. Vessel normalization and endothelial reprogramming approaches aim at disrupting such immune-hostile EC-to-immune cell crosstalk to restore an immune-supportive TME [46]. Tumor vascularization can also occur via nonangiogenic mechanisms including VM, wherein cancer cells acquire EC-like properties and form vessel-like structures that can be detected using TL [17, 18]. Increased number of CD31+ ECs and CD31−TL+ cells are observed in B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA tumors (Fig. 4a–c), suggesting that NLRC5 expression in tumors may increase both neo-angiogenesis and VM. Studies on EC-specific NLRC5-deficient mice have revealed that NLRC5 promotes angiogenesis in ischemic tissues through increasing EC proliferation by cooperating with STAT3 to induce angiopoietin-2 [15]. EC-specific Nlrc5−/− mice exhibited reduced growth of B16 tumors, suggesting that NLRC5-mediated angiogenesis may promote tumor growth [15]. However, our findings show that increased vascularization caused by tumor cell-intrinsic NLRC5 expression is associated with efficient tumor control by immune cells in both Nlrc5+/+ and Nlrc5−/− mice. Proteomes of B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA cells did not show enrichment of angiogenesis or other vascularization pathways, suggesting that increased vascularization in NLRC5-expressing tumors likely arises from the interaction between tumor cells and host cells. Clearly, further studies are needed to elucidate the mechanisms by which NLRC5 expressed in tumor cells modulates host ECs and promotes VM to establish an immune-supportive TME.

In addition to increasing tumor vascularity that allows immune cells gain access to the tumor, antitumor immune response elicited by tumor-intrinsic NLRC5 expression can facilitate their infiltration into tumors by inducing chemokines. Ccl4 and Cxcl9 chemokine genes are upregulated in B16-N5CA tumors in Nlrc5+/+ mice, whereas Cxcl9 and Cxcl11 are upregulated in B16-N5FL tumors in Nlrc5−/− hosts (Fig. 4e and f). CCL4 and CXCL9 chemokines promote the recruitment of cDC1 cells and NK and T effector cells, respectively [19, 47, 48]. Even though a previous study has shown NLRC5-dependent upregulation of CXCL10 in pancreatic beta cells in response to Type I IFN stimulation [49], CXCL10 was not upregulated in B16-N5FL or B16-N5CA tumors, despite increased expression of Ifna and Ifnb genes (Figs 4f and 3b). CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 form a triad of ligands produced by myeloid cells, ECs, fibroblasts, and cancer cells in the TME [50]. These chemokines bind to the CXCR3 receptor, which is highly expressed in Th1, CD8+ TE, NK, and NKT cells, promoting their recruitment to the TME. IFNγ is a strong inducer of CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 genes, whereas Type I IFNs have a differential impact on these genes [50]. Elevated expression of Ifng in B16-N5CA tumors from Nlrc5+/+ hosts and in B16-N5FL tumors from Nlrc5−/− hosts and parallel upregulation of Cxcl9 expression (Figs 3b and 4f) suggest that IFNγ produced by activated CD8+ T cells and NK cells within NLRC5-expressing tumors may induce Cxcl9 to establish a feed forward loop and further augment the recruitment of CD8+ TE, NK, and NKT cells.

One of the limitations of our study is that immune cell profiling of TILs and DLN, and gene expression studies in B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA tumors were performed when control B16-V tumors reached the endpoint in Nlrc5+/+ hosts. It is likely that immune cell activation in B16-N5FL or B16-N5CA tumors occurred earlier than in B16-V tumors, and at different rates in Nlrc5+/+ and Nlrc5−/− hosts. Studying the immune parameters in TILs and tumor draining lymph nodes at earlier time points in mice bearing B16-N5FL or B16-N5CA tumors will inform how NLRC5-FL and NLRC5-CA impact the kinetics of immune cell activation, their infiltration into tumors, and the establishment of antitumor immunity. Another limitation of our study is the focus on lymphoid cells, based on NLRC5-dependent transactivation of classical MHC-Ia and nonclassical MHC-Ib genes. Characterization of the myeloid cell compartment in TILs and tumor draining lymph nodes in B16-N5FL or B16-N5CA tumors when control B16-V tumors are in exponential growth phase would shed light on the dynamics of myeloid cells impacted by tumor-intrinsic NLRC5 expression, and how they contribute to establishing an immune-supportive TME. Similarly, further studies are needed to determine whether the NLRC5-mediated downregulation of proteins in the vacuolar and ER protein degradation pathways in cell lines occurs at the transcriptional or posttranscriptions level, and to elucidate the functional consequences of the impacted pathways on antigen cross-presentation, cross-dressing and tumor control in vivo.

Overall, our findings reveal important tumor cell-intrinsic roles of NLRC5 in mediating tumor control by CD8+ and NK1.1+ cells through efficient activation of adaptive and innate immune cells and their recruitment into tumors by establishing an immune-supportive TME, independently of NLRC5 expression in APCs. Moreover, the shared proteomes of B16-N5FL and B16-N5CA cells suggest novel roles for tumor cell-intrinsic NLRC5 in modulating protein degrading vacuolar compartments that could impact tumor antigen processing and, consequently, the repertoire of antigenic peptides on tumor cells and the profile of cross-dressed tumor antigens in APCs. Hence, our study further strengthens the foundation for developing NLRC5-based cancer immunotherapies, complementing ongoing efforts to reinstate antitumor immunity in immune evasive cancers.
