The autophagy-inhibitory tissue hormone DBI/ACBP is essential for glucocorticoid-induced immunosuppression
Authors: Zhe Shen, Hui Pan, Liwei Zhao, Peng Liu, Oliver Kepp, Maria Chiara Maiuri, Yuting Ma, Isabelle Martins, Guido Kroemer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSystemic administration of glucocorticoids (GCs) has immunosuppressive effects that involve the upregulation of the transcription factor TSC22D3 in dendritic cells (DCs), thereby reducing their capacity for antigen presentation to T lymphocytes. Recently, we found that this effect is not mediated by direct action on the GC receptor in DCs but rather involves an indirect signaling circuitry. Indeed, GCs act on the GC receptor expressed by many cell types to cause the upregulation and release of the tissue hormone DBI/ACBP (diazepam binding inhibitor, acyl-CoA binding protein). DBI/ACBP, which is an inhibitor of macroautophagy/autophagy, then acts on the benzodiazepine-binding site of the gamma-aminobutyric acid type A receptor (GABAAR) to elicit the upregulation of TSC22D3. The indirect, DBI/ACBP-dependent upregulation of TSC22D3 by GCs is observed both in vivo (mice) and in vitro, in murine splenocytes and bone marrow-derived DCs, as well as in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells and monocyte-derived DCs. Inhibition of human mixed lymphocyte reactions (confronting DCs and lymphocytes from distinct donors) by DCs is reduced by DBI/ACBP neutralizing antibodies. Similarly, the suppression of antitumor immune responses (elicited by vaccination with dying cancer cells, immunogenic chemotherapy or PDCD1/PD-1 blockade) by GCs is reversed by DBI/ACBP neutralization. Epistatic experiments indicate that knockout of Tsc22d3 in DCs and inhibition of DBI/ACBP act on the pathway to reverse GC-mediated inhibition of cancer immunosurveillance. Of note, the benzodiazepine diazepam restores GC-induced immunosuppression when DBI/ACBP is inhibited. Altogether, these findings support a role for the DBI/ACBP-GABAAR system in immunosuppression by GCs.

Main text

Glucocorticoids (GCs) are endogenous steroid hormones released by the adrenal cortex that tonically regulate most bodily functions following a complex pattern of ultradian and circadian oscillations. In response to acute or chronic stress, GC levels increase to facilitate adaptive and maladaptive responses, respectively. Moreover, natural or synthetic GCs (such as cortisol, dexamethasone and prednisolone) are therapeutically used to dampen excessive inflammation in the context of severe infectious, allergic, autoinflammatory and autoimmune diseases. In its most extreme form, chronic overstimulation of GC receptors by endogenous or exogenous GCs causes Cushing syndrome, consisting of a wide compendium of cutaneous, metabolic, musculoskeletal, cardiovascular, immunosuppressive and neuropsychiatric symptoms that include a moon face, skin atrophy, cutaneous striae, alopecia, hirsutism, diabetes, lipodystrophy, visceral adiposity, hepatosteatosis, sarcopenia, osteoporosis, dyslipidemia, hypertension, thromboembolism, susceptibility to infections, impaired cognition and depression.

Although it has been widely thought that these GC effects would be mediated by the direct action of GCs on GC receptors expressed in most, if not all, nucleated cell types of the body, it has recently become clear that they involve the obligate contribution of another stress hormone, DBI/ACBP. DBI/ACBP is a phylogenetically ancient peptide hormone (which likely precedes GCs in evolution) that is produced by many different cell types scattered throughout all major organs and that is released in response to stress and damage to inhibit autophagy and to elicit trophic signals and compensatory proliferation. In response to GCs, hepatocytes upregulate DBI/ACBP expression and secrete this leaderless protein through a nonconventional, autophagy-dependent pathway. Importantly, in patients with Cushing syndrome, circulating DBI/ACBP levels are supraphysiological until elevated cortisol levels are reduced by surgical intervention. In mouse models of Cushing syndrome, the administration of antibodies neutralizing DBI/ACBP abolishes all quantifiable phenotypic, metabolic, musculoskeletal and depressive symptoms. The anti-Cushing effects of DBI/ACBP neutralization are fully phenocopied by the inducible knockout (KO) of Dbi either at the whole-body level or only in hepatocytes, as well as by the mutation (Gabrg2F77I) of the benzodiazepine-binding site of the gamma-aminobutyric acid type A receptor (GABAAR), which blocks the binding of DBI/ACBP. These latter findings strongly argue in favor of the interpretation that the anti-Cushing effects of anti-DBI/ACBP antibodies are mediated by on-target effects.

Driven by these observations, we wondered whether DBI/ACBP might also be involved in the immunosuppressive effects of GCs (Figure 1). For this, we resorted to several models of immunosurveillance that were either spontaneous and hence relying on the capacity of the immune system to slow down the growth of orthotopically transplanted tumors (such as EO771 mammary carcinomas, MCA205 fibrosarcomas or TC1 non-small cell lung cancers) without further therapeutic manipulation or elicited by specific treatments (Figure 1(A)). In this latter case, anticancer immune responses were induced by vaccination with dying cancer cells, administration of immunogenic chemotherapies (such as oxaliplatin and mitoxantrone), or immunotherapy by injection of antibodies blocking the immune checkpoint inhibitor PDCD1/PD-1 (programmed cell death 1). In all cases, GCs (either corticosterone, which is the principal endogenous GC in mice or dexamethasone) accelerate tumor growth as a sign of immunosuppression. This GC-induced tumor acceleration is at least partially inhibited by antibody-mediated neutralization of DBI/ACBP, dbi knockout or mutation of its receptor (Gabrg2F77I). Moreover, the anti-immunosuppressive effect of DBI/ACBP neutralization is lost in mice receiving periodic injection of diazepam (which, like DBI/ACBP acts as a positive allosteric modulator of GABAAR), again arguing in favor of the specificity of the experimental intervention (Figure 1(A)). In a next step, we performed a series of in vitro experiments designed to recapitulate these in vivo results in a simplified (and more artificial) setting (Figure 1(B)). Bone marrow-derived mouse dendritic cells (BMDCs) can engulf ovalbumin protein, digest it into peptides and present one particular MHC class I-restricted octapeptide (SIINFEKL) to B3Z hybridoma cells causing them to produce IL2 (interleukin 2). In this system, GCs reduce antigen presentation by BMDCs, and this effect is partially restored by anti-DBI/ACBP antibody. In mixed lymphocyte reactions (MLRs) confronting human monocyte-derived DCs with T lymphocytes from another donor, GCs inhibit the stimulation of T cells to produce IFNG (interferon gamma) and to proliferate. The inhibition of such MLRs by GCs is again attenuated by an antibody specific for human DBI/ACBP (Figure 1(B)).

Mechanistically, we found that GCs cause mouse BMDCs to transcriptionally upregulate both DBI/ACBP and the immunosuppressive transcription factor TSC22D3/GILZ (TSC22 domain family member 3). This GC-induced upregulation of TSC22D3 is blunted by anti-DBI/ACBP antibody, not only in BMDCs, but also in purified mouse splenocytes, freshly drawn human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and in human monocyte-derived DCs. In a final twist, we compared the effects of immunochemotherapy (oxaliplatin plus PDCD1 blockade) against fibrosarcomas developing in mice lacking TSC22D3 in myeloid cells (including DCs) and wild-type (WT) controls subjected to GC treatment and/or DBI/ACBP blockade. Removal of TSC22D3 from DCs interferes with the chemoimmunotherapy-subversive effects of GCs, as does DBI/ACBP blockade in WT animals. However, simultaneous TSC22D3 and DBI/ACBP blockade does not confer a better reversal of GC-mediated immunosuppression than either manipulation alone. These epistatic experiments strongly suggest the existence of a linear pathway connecting GCs to the upregulation of DBI/ACBP, which then elicits TSC22D3 induction in DCs to mediate immunosuppression (Figure 1(C)).

In conclusion, it appears that DBI/ACBP is not only involved in the metabolic and body composition-modifying side effects of GCs but that it also contributes to GC-mediated immunosuppression [1]. This is at least in part due to an action on DCs, which upregulate the immunosuppressive transcription factor TSC22D3. Although it is well established that autophagy is required for the function of DCs (and other immune cell subtypes), it remains to be determined whether the upregulation of TSC22D3 is required for autophagy inhibition by DBI/ACBP in DCs. Irrespective of this detail, it will be important to evaluate the capacity of DBI/ACBP inhibitors to improve cancer immunosurveillance in patients, in particular in those that have high levels of endogenous GCs or receive synthetic GCs for the treatment of immune-related adverse effects.
