Dietary index for gut microbiota (DIGM) and cardiometabolic, inflammatory, and gut–brain-related biomarkers in adults with obesity: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Amr Ali Mohamed Abdelgawwad El-Sehrawy, Ismail Kandil, Safa labib, Ihab M Abdelrahim, Mansuor A Alanazi, Marwah Suliman Maashi, Mona Ahmed Alzahrani, Hussam Ghurman Alshehri, Kateryna Dykalo, Aya Ahmed Fathy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground The gut microbiota plays an important role in metabolic homeostasis and systemic inflammation, and dietary habits are major determinants of its composition and function. This study investigated the association between adherence to the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DIGM) and cardiometabolic, inflammatory, and gut microbiota-related biomarkers in adults with obesity. Methods In this cross-sectional study conducted from January to December 2025, 637 adults with obesity aged 20–55 years were recruited from clinics affiliated with Mansoura University, Egypt, through clinic-based recruitment and public advertisements. Dietary intake was assessed using a semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire, and the DIGM score was calculated based on predefined favorable and unfavorable dietary components according to the original scoring framework. Participants were categorized into tertiles according to their DIGM score. Anthropometric measurements, blood pressure, glycemic indices, lipid profile, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and C-reactive protein (CRP) were assessed. Multivariable analyses were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, physical activity, dietary energy intake, education, occupation, marital status, and history of depression. Results Higher DIGM adherence was associated with lower SBP and higher high density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) after multivariable adjustment. Participants in the highest DIGM tertile showed lower circulating LPS and CRP concentrations and higher BDNF levels compared with lower tertiles (all adjusted P < 0.001). In multivariable linear regression models adjusted for confounders, higher DIGM scores were independently associated with lower SBP (β = −0.057; 95% CI: −0.011 to − 0.006), higher HDL-C (β = 0.100; 95% CI: 0.004 to 0.011), higher BDNF (β = 0.504; 95% CI: 0.295 to 0.407), and lower LPS (β = −0.468; 95% CI: −0.208 to − 0.146) and CRP (β = −0.048; 95% CI: −0.028 to − 0.003). Conclusion Greater adherence to the DIGM was independently associated with a more favorable inflammatory profile, higher circulating BDNF concentrations, lower LPS and CRP levels, and lower blood pressure among adults with obesity. These findings suggest that dietary patterns characterized by higher DIGM adherence may be associated with cardiometabolic and inflammatory profiles. Further prospective studies incorporating direct assessment of gut microbiota composition, microbial metabolites, and clinical outcomes are warranted to clarify the underlying mechanisms and temporal relationships. Keywords: Gut microbiota, Diet score, Obesity, Inflammation, Blood pressure, BDNF, Metabolic profile, Cardiometabolic risk. Introduction

Obesity has emerged as a critical global public health issue, with the age-standardized prevalence among adults rising from approximately 6.6% in 1990 to 15.8% in 2022 worldwide1. Recent research indicates that over 1 billion people globally now live with obesity, reflecting a more-than-twofold increase among adults and a fourfold rise in childhood obesity between 1990 and 20221,2. The etiology of obesity is multifactorial, encompassing genetic predispositions, behavioral patterns, and environmental influences3. Among these, dietary factors are paramount: sustained intake of energy-dense, nutrient-poor processed foods, combined with low consumption of nutrient-rich options, drives chronic energy surplus and adiposity4.

The human gut microbiota, composed of trillions of microorganisms including bacteria, archaea, viruses, and fungi, plays an integral role in maintaining host health. It contributes to nutrient metabolism, synthesis of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), regulation of immune responses, protection against pathogens, and modulation of systemic inflammation5,6. Alterations in the composition or function of the gut microbiota—commonly referred to as dysbiosis—have been implicated in the pathogenesis of various chronic conditions such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and inflammatory bowel disorders7,8.

Emerging evidence suggests that the gut microbiota also influences host energy balance, lipid metabolism, and insulin sensitivity, thereby acting as a critical intermediary in metabolic regulation. Among the modifiable factors shaping the gut microbiome, diet is one of the most influential.

Dietary patterns rich in dietary fiber, complex carbohydrates, polyphenols, and unsaturated fats have been associated with favorable gut microbial profiles and microbial metabolite production, including SCFAs, which have been proposed to contribute to immune regulation and maintenance of gut barrier integrity9,10. In contrast, diets high in saturated fats, animal proteins, added sugars, and ultra-processed foods have been associated with reduced microbial diversity and an increase in pro-inflammatory microbial metabolites11. These findings have led to increased interest in identifying specific dietary factors that beneficially influence gut microbiota composition and function.

Given the strong influence of diet on gut microbial composition, researchers have increasingly sought to quantify dietary patterns that promote microbial health. The Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DIGM) was developed as a composite tool to assess adherence to food-based dietary components previously associated with gut microbial health. The index typically includes microbiota-friendly food groups such as avocado, chickpeas, broccoli, coffee, fermented dairies, soybean and whole grains. At the same time, it penalizes the intake of microbiota-disrupting items, including red and processed meats, refined grains and high fat diet12. These components were selected based on previous evidence linking them with dietary patterns associated with gut microbial characteristics and health-related outcomes13,14(Fig. 1).

The components of Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DIGM).

Previous studies investigating DIGM and microbiome-related outcomes have reported associations between higher DIGM scores and gut microbial characteristics, including microbial diversity and specific bacterial taxa which contribute to immune regulation and anti-inflammatory effects15. Moreover, higher DIGM scores have been associated with favorable metabolic profiles, including insulin sensitivity, lipid parameters, and inflammatory markers16.

The gut–brain axis (GBA) is a bidirectional communication pathway between the gastrointestinal tract and the central nervous system, strongly influenced by the gut microbiota17. Two key biomarkers in this context are lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF). LPS, a pro-inflammatory endotoxin derived from gram-negative bacteria, has been suggested as a circulating marker related to microbial-derived inflammatory signaling and is associated with systemic inflammation and neuroinflammatory conditions18,19. Conversely, BDNF is a neurotrophin involved in neuronal function, and circulating BDNF levels have been investigated as a biomarker potentially related to metabolic and gut–brain axis-related processes20. Reduced BDNF and elevated LPS levels have been linked to metabolic and neurological disorders, highlighting their potential relevance as circulating biomarkers in metabolic and gut–brain-related research21.

Previous studies have reported that higher adherence to the DIGM was associated with more favorable metabolic profiles, including lower risk of metabolic syndrome22, diabetes-related outcomes23 and has also been investigated in relation to cancer-related outcomes24,25.These findings suggest a potential association between gut microbiota–oriented dietary patterns and metabolic health; however, the underlying mechanisms require further investigation. However, limited evidence exists regarding the relationship between DIGM and key markers of the gut–brain axis and cardiovascular outcomes, particularly blood pressure, in adults with obesity. Given the growing burden of obesity and its link with both hypertension and neuroinflammatory pathways, understanding the role of microbiota-related dietary patterns in these mechanisms is essential. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between adherence to the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DIGM) and circulating biomarkers related to gut health and inflammation, including LPS, BDNF, and C-reactive protein (CRP), as the primary outcomes. Secondary outcomes included blood pressure, glycemic markers, lipid profile, and anthropometric measurements in adults with obesity.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted among adults with obesity recruited using a convenience sampling approach between January to December 2025 from clinics affiliated with Mansoura University, Egypt, as well as through public advertisements inviting eligible individuals to participate in the study. Obesity was defined as a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m². Eligible participants were between 20 and 55 years of age. Individuals with a history of cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, cancer, renal, hepatic, thyroid, or gastrointestinal disorders were excluded. Participants who were pregnant, lactating, or postmenopausal, as well as those who had followed any special dietary or weight-loss regimen or received dietary counseling during the previous three months, were also excluded. To minimize the potential influence of medications and supplements on gut microbiota and inflammatory biomarkers, individuals who had used antibiotics, prebiotics, anti-inflammatory medications, glucose-lowering medications, lipid-lowering medications, or nutritional supplements within the preceding three months were not eligible for inclusion. Alcohol consumption was not assessed because its use is legally and culturally restricted in the study setting, making self-reported data potentially unreliable.

Sociodemographic and lifestyle characteristics, including age, sex, educational level, occupation, marital status, smoking status, and history of depression, were collected using a self-administered questionnaire designed specifically for this study. Information regarding these variables was obtained through interviews conducted by trained researchers, and participants’ responses were recorded using predefined categories. The study flowchart is shown in Fig. 2.

The study flowchart.

After confirming eligibility criteria, all participants were informed about the study procedures and provided written informed consent before enrollment. The study protocol received ethical approval from the Deanship of Research and Graduate Studies at Mansoura University, Egypt (Ethical approval No. R.26.05.3722). All procedures were performed in compliance with the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent revisions.

Dietary assessment and construction of DIGM score

Dietary intake was evaluated using a semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ), which was adapted from a previously validated instrument developed for Arab populations and successfully applied in large epidemiological studies, including the Mutaba’ah Study26. The adapted FFQ comprised 146 food items representing the major food groups, including cereals, dairy products, vegetables, fruits, legumes, meat, fish, eggs, fats and oils, mixed dishes, beverages, sweets, nuts, and seeds. Participants were asked to report their habitual dietary intake during the preceding 12 months by specifying both the frequency of consumption and the usual portion size for each food item. Reported portion sizes were converted into grams using standardized household measures, and average daily intakes (g/day) were calculated. Dietary data were subsequently analyzed with Nutritionist IV software (First Databank, San Bruno, CA, USA) to estimate daily energy intake, macronutrient intake, and food group consumption.

The DIGM was developed to characterize dietary patterns associated with gut microbial composition and function based on previously reported evidence linking specific dietary components with microbial diversity, richness, and metabolite production12–14,25. The DIGM score included 14 dietary components, consisting of 10 components considered favorable for gut microbiota and 4 components considered unfavorable (Fig. 1). The favorable components included chickpeas, whole grains, soybeans, cranberries, fermented dairies, broccoli, coffee, avocado, fiber, and green tea. For each favorable component, participants received a score of 1 if their intake was at or above the sex-specific median and 0 otherwise. The unfavorable components included processed meat, red meat, refined grains, and high-fat diet. For processed meat, red meat, and refined grains, participants received a score of 0 if intake was at or above the sex-specific median and 1 otherwise. The high-fat diet component was scored according to the original DIGM algorithm, with a score of 0 assigned to participants consuming ≥ 40% of total energy from fat and a score of 1 assigned to those with lower fat intake12,25. The individual component scores were summed to generate the overall DIGM score, with higher scores indicating a more microbiota-supportive dietary pattern. In the present study population, the observed DIGM scores ranged from 1 to 11. For categorical analyses, participants were classified into tertiles based on the rank distribution of the continuous DIGM score, resulting in three mutually exclusive groups: T1 (n = 212), T2 (n = 212), and T3 (n = 213). The continuous DIGM score was used as the primary exposure variable in multivariable regression analyses, whereas tertile-based categories were used for descriptive comparisons.

Anthropometric assays, clinical measurements and physical activity

Body weight was measured using a calibrated digital scale, and height was measured using a stadiometer to the nearest 0.1 kg and 0.5 cm, respectively. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). Body composition parameters, including fat mass (FM) and fat-free mass (FFM), were assessed using bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) with the InBody device (InBody Co., Ltd., Seoul, Republic of Korea) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Participants were assessed under standardized conditions, including measurements performed in the morning after an overnight fast and with participants barefoot, to minimize measurement variability. Waist circumference (WC) was measured at the midpoint between the lower margin of the last palpable rib and the top of the iliac crest using a non-stretchable measuring tape, while hip circumference was measured at the level of the widest portion of the buttocks with participants standing upright and feet together. The waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) was calculated as waist circumference divided by hip circumference. All anthropometric and body-composition measurements were performed by trained personnel using standardized procedures, and repeated measurements were obtained to ensure measurement reliability. Blood pressure was measured in a seated position after at least 10 min of rest using an automated digital sphygmomanometer (Omron, Japan). Two measurements were obtained at 5-minute intervals, and the mean value was used for statistical analyses.

Physical activity level was evaluated using the validated International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ)27. The overall physical activity score was calculated as MET-hours/week by summing the energy expenditure values derived from walking, moderate-intensity, and vigorous-intensity physical activities.

Biochemical analyses

Following an overnight fasting period (8–12 h), venous blood samples were collected and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min. Serum and plasma samples were separated, aliquoted, and stored at − 80 °C until biochemical analyses. Inflammatory and gut-related biomarkers, including CRP and LPS, were measured using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (ZellBio, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Serum BDNF concentrations were assessed using an ELISA kit (IBL International, Germany). The reported analytical ranges were approximately 0.1–10 mg/L for hs-CRP, 0.1–50 EU/mL for LPS, and 0.2–100 ng/mL for BDNF. The intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation were within the acceptable ranges reported by the manufacturers (generally < 10%). All ELISA measurements were performed under standardized laboratory conditions, and samples were analyzed using consistent procedures to minimize analytical variability. Serum lipid profile parameters, including total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and triglycerides (TG), were measured using an automated biochemical analyzer (Hitachi 902, Roche Diagnostics, Germany). Fasting serum glucose (FBS) was determined using the glucose oxidase method. HbA1c concentrations were measured using ion-exchange high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with a Bio-Rad D-10 system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA). Fasting serum insulin concentrations were determined using a commercial ELISA kit (Monobind Inc., USA).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Participants were categorized into tertiles according to the DIGM score for descriptive comparisons. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, inspection of histograms, Q–Q plots, and evaluation of skewness and kurtosis values. Variables with significant deviation from normality were analyzed using non-parametric methods. Differences in continuous variables across DIGM tertiles were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed variables and the Kruskal–Wallis test for variables that were not normally distributed. When appropriate, Tukey’s post hoc test was applied following ANOVA, whereas Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons were performed following the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test. For dietary components included in the DIGM scoring algorithm, comparisons across tertiles were performed only to describe the distribution of score components and were not considered as independent validation analyses. Multivariable general linear models (GLM) were applied to examine the associations between the continuous DIGM score and anthropometric, cardiometabolic, biochemical, and inflammatory variables. The results of regression analyses were reported as standardized regression coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and corresponding P-values. For categorical comparisons, both crude and adjusted P-values were reported when applicable. Adjusted models were used to evaluate whether observed associations remained after accounting for potential confounders.

Covariates included in the multivariable models were selected a priori based on their established associations with both dietary intake (DIGM score) and cardiometabolic outcomes, as identified from previous literature. The predefined confounder set included age, sex, BMI (where appropriate), smoking status, physical activity, total energy intake, education level, occupation, marital status, and history of depression. To account for multiple comparisons across cardiometabolic, biochemical, and inflammatory outcomes, the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure was applied to the adjusted P-values obtained from the fully adjusted models. Both nominal P-values and FDR-adjusted q-values are reported, and statistical significance after multiple-testing correction was defined as q < 0.05. Outliers were assessed using boxplots and standardized residuals. No influential outliers requiring exclusion were identified; therefore, all observations were retained in the analyses.

Multicollinearity among independent variables was evaluated using tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF) statistics. Tolerance values > 0.20 and VIF values < 5 were considered indicative of acceptable collinearity. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Results

The baseline demographic and anthropometric characteristics of the participants across DIGM tertiles are presented in Table 1. Sex, smoking status, physical activity, education, occupation, marital status, history of depression, age, weight, height, waist circumference, hip circumference, and waist-to-hip ratio were comparable across DIGM tertiles (all P > 0.05). In contrast, significant differences were observed in body composition measures. Body mass index differed significantly across tertiles, with participants in the highest DIGM tertile having lower values than those in the lower tertiles (crude P = 0.003; adjusted P = 0.008). This association remained significant after adjustment for age, sex, smoking status, physical activity, dietary energy intake, education, occupation, marital status, and history of depression. Post hoc analysis indicated a significant difference between the second and third tertiles. Fat mass also differed across tertiles (crude P = 0.040; adjusted P = 0.047). Likewise, fat-free mass remained significantly different in both crude (P = 0.049) and adjusted analyses (P = 0.037), with post hoc analysis showing a significant difference between the first and third tertiles.

The cardiometabolic, biochemical, and inflammatory characteristics of participants across DIGM tertiles are presented in Table 2. Participants in the highest DIGM tertile had lower SBP than those in the lower tertiles, and this association remained significant in the fully adjusted model (P = 0.015). In contrast, although dbp differed significantly in the crude and partially adjusted analyses, the association was no longer significant after full adjustment (P = 0.511). Among glycemic indices, neither FBS nor HbA1c remained significantly associated with DIGM tertiles after full adjustment (P = 0.910 and P = 0.157, respectively), and insulin concentrations were comparable across tertiles. Regarding lipid profile, participants in the highest DIGM tertile had significantly higher HDL-C concentrations than those in the lower tertiles, and this association remained significant after full adjustment (P < 0.001). TC also showed a significant association in the fully adjusted model (P = 0.011), whereas LDL-C reached statistical significance after full adjustment (P = 0.027). No significant association was observed for TG concentrations (P = 0.275). Marked differences were observed for inflammatory and gut microbiota-related biomarkers. BDNF concentrations increased progressively across increasing DIGM tertiles, whereas LPS and CRP concentrations decreased. These associations remained highly significant after full adjustment (all P < 0.001). Post hoc analyses demonstrated significant differences between all tertiles for BDNF, LPS, and CRP.

To account for multiple comparisons across cardiometabolic, biochemical, and inflammatory outcomes, the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR procedure was applied to the fully adjusted analyses. The associations for SBP, DBP, HDL-C, HbA1c, BDNF, LPS, and CRP were significant after FDR adjustment (all q < 0.05; Supplementary Table 1).

Table 3 presents the multivariable linear regression analyses evaluating the associations between the DIGM score and anthropometric and biochemical variables. All biochemical models were adjusted for age, sex, BMI, smoking status, physical activity, dietary energy intake, education, occupation, marital status, and history of depression. Among the anthropometric variables, higher DIGM scores were significantly associated with lower FM (β = −0.115, P = 0.047) and lower FFM (β = −0.144, P = 0.005), whereas no significant associations were observed for weight, height, BMI, WC, HC, or WHR. Among the biochemical variables, higher DIGM scores were significantly associated with lower SBP (β = −0.057, P = 0.039), higher HDL-C (β = 0.100, P < 0.001), higher BDNF (β = 0.504, P < 0.001), lower LPS (β = −0.468, P < 0.001), and lower CRP (β = −0.048, P = 0.017). No significant associations were observed for DBP, FBS, TC, TG, LDL-C, insulin, or HbA1c (all P > 0.05). Also, the results of collinearity diagnostics for variables included in the multivariable regression model are presented in Supplementary Table 2. No evidence of problematic multicollinearity was observed among the variables. All tolerance values were above 0.20 and all VIF values were below 5, indicating acceptable levels of collinearity. The highest VIF values were observed for LPS (VIF = 4.670) and BDNF (VIF = 4.592), which remained below the predefined threshold.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of adults with obesity, higher adherence to the DIGM, a dietary index reflecting food components previously associated with gut microbial health, was associated with a more favorable cardiometabolic and inflammatory biomarker profile. Participants in the highest DIGM tertile showed lower blood pressure, lower circulating CRP and LPS concentrations, and higher circulating BDNF levels compared with lower tertiles. These associations remained significant after adjustment for relevant demographic, lifestyle, dietary, and socioeconomic confounders. However, because of the cross-sectional design, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of causal effects or microbiota-mediated mechanisms.

Our finding that higher DIGM scores were associated with a more favorable blood pressure profile, particularly lower systolic blood pressure, is consistent with previous evidence linking dietary patterns rich in fiber, plant-based foods, and other microbiota-related dietary components with cardiometabolic health. For example, high-fiber diets, which may influence the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), have been reported to reduce blood pressure in hypertensive animal models through alterations in gut-derived metabolic pathways28. Moreover, plant-rich dietary patterns, which share several components with the DIGM, have been associated with favorable blood pressure and LDL cholesterol profiles in clinical trials and systematic reviews29,30. Although these pathways may provide potential explanations for the observed associations, direct assessment of gut microbiota composition and microbial metabolites was not performed in the present study; therefore, these mechanisms remain to be confirmed in future investigations.

Participants with higher DIGM adherence exhibited significantly lower circulating CRP and LPS concentrations. These findings are consistent with previous experimental studies suggesting that dietary patterns rich in fiber and fermented foods may influence gut-derived inflammatory pathways and endotoxin-related signaling31. In human studies, diets characterized by higher intake of plant-based and microbiota-related food components have also been associated with more favorable inflammatory and metabolic profiles22,32. However, whether these associations are mediated through changes in gut barrier function, microbial composition, or microbial metabolites requires further investigation.

Although lipid and glycemic markers showed generally favorable patterns across higher DIGM categories, these associations did not reach statistical significance after multivariable adjustment. Therefore, these estimates should be interpreted with caution, as they may reflect random variation or limited statistical precision rather than true underlying associations. Previous meta-analyses have reported associations between plant-based and DASH-style dietary patterns and improved blood pressure, lipid profiles, and glycemic parameters29,30,33,34. However, the present cross-sectional study was not designed to evaluate causal or longitudinal effects. Future prospective and intervention studies are warranted to determine whether adherence to microbiota-related dietary patterns is associated with long-term metabolic outcomes.

A major strength of this study is the application of the DIGM, which reflects dietary patterns associated with gut health. Another strength is the comprehensive evaluation of anthropometric, cardiometabolic, inflammatory, and gut–brain axis related biomarkers within the same study population. However, several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal interpretation, and the possibility of reverse causation cannot be completely excluded. Although individuals with recent dietary counseling, special diets, or medications and supplements that could affect metabolic or inflammatory outcomes were excluded, residual confounding may remain. Second, dietary intake was assessed using an FFQ, which may be influenced by recall bias and measurement error. Third, obesity duration and alcohol consumption were not available, and unmeasured confounding cannot be fully ruled out despite adjustment for predefined covariates. In addition, gut microbiota composition and microbial metabolites, including SCFAs, were not directly measured; therefore, proposed microbiota-mediated mechanisms should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation in future studies incorporating microbiome and metabolomic analyses. Finally, the study population included adults with obesity recruited from clinics and public advertisements within a single geographic region, which may limit the generalizability of the findings.

In conclusion, higher adherence to the DIGM was associated with a more favorable cardiometabolic, inflammatory, and gut–brain related biomarker profile among adults with obesity (Fig. 3). The observed associations, including lower blood pressure, CRP and LPS concentrations, and higher BDNF levels, highlight the potential role of gut-health-oriented dietary patterns in association with obesity-related metabolic and inflammatory status. However, given the cross-sectional design and the lack of direct assessment of gut microbiota composition and microbial metabolites, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Future longitudinal and intervention studies incorporating microbiome and metabolomic analyses are warranted to confirm these associations and elucidate the underlying biological pathways.
