Bifid Mandibular Condyle Seen in a Patient With Posterior Disk Displacement
Authors: Yosuke Iijima, Nami Nakayama, Takumi Takahashi, Miki Yamada, Shunsuke Hino, Norio Horie, Takahiro Kaneko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis report highlights the rare bifid mandibular condyle (BMC). Clinicians should consider BMC as a potential factor in complex temporomandibular joint mechanics.

Introduction

The bifid mandibular condyle (BMC), an uncommon anatomical variation where the mandibular condyle presents with two distinct heads, is a rare condition. These two articulating surfaces are separated by a groove, which can be oriented either mediolaterally or anteroposteriorly. While most BMC cases are unilateral, bilateral occurrences have also been reported [1, 2]. BMC is typically an incidental finding on diagnostic imaging, often discovered during the retrieval of existing images. Imaging studies have estimated the prevalence of BMC to be around 1%, with no significant difference observed between males and females [2].

Various etiological possibilities have been proposed for BMC, including developmental abnormalities, degenerative processes, tumors, and trauma [3]. It is hypothesized that the anteroposterior pattern of BMC might result from childhood facial trauma, whereas the mediolateral type could be associated with the persistence of fibrous septa within the condylar cartilage [2, 4]. However, exceptions to this classification exist; for example, some mediolateral bifid condyles have been reported following sagittal fractures through the condylar head [5, 6].

The most common and predominant symptoms of BMC are temporomandibular joint (TMJ) sounds [5]. Pain, restricted mandibular movement, trismus, swelling, ankylosis and facial asymmetry have also been reported [7] However, in 67% of patients with BMC, no symptoms are observed in the affected condyle area [5].

This report describes a case of a patient who presented with symptoms clinically suggestive of posterior articular disk displacement after TMJ dislocation, and computed tomography (CT) imaging revealed a BMC.

Case History

A 50‐year‐old man presented to our department after experiencing right TMJ dislocation following a yawn, which could not be successfully reduced at a local dental clinic. The patient reported no previous history of maxillofacial trauma or symptoms of TMJ disorder, and his general health was good.

At the time of the initial examination, the patient did not present with a total inability to close his mouth, as is usually seen with TMJ dislocation. There was severe discomfort in the right TMJ. He was partially able to close his mouth, but his mandibular midline was displaced to the left, and his upper and lower dentition could not achieve an intercuspal position. Palpation of the right TMJ, which was described as dislocated, revealed that the mandibular condyle was not positioned anterior to the articular tubercle. Similarly, the left TMJ showed no evidence of the mandibular condyle crossing over the articular tubercle. Given these findings, traumatic changes or other pathological conditions were suspected, prompting a CT scan.

Differential Diagnosis

CT findings revealed that the right mandibular condyle was situated directly below the articular tubercle. Remarkably, the right mandibular condyle was divided into two heads by a deep mediolateral groove, confirming the presence of a BMC. There were no other abnormal findings such as erosion or osteophytes in the right mandibular condyle, glenoid fossa, or articular tubercle. Outcome and Follow‐Up

Based on the clinical presentation and CT findings, a diagnosis of open lock due to suspected right posterior disk displacement after TMJ dislocation was made. Manual disk reduction was attempted as a treatment, but it did not improve the disk displacement. The patient was advised to avoid wide mouth opening and chewing hard foods, and the treatment session was concluded for the day. At a follow‐up visit 7 days later, the patient still experienced some discomfort in the right TMJ, but the mouth opening and closing disorder, including the malocclusion, had significantly improved.

Although we recommended a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to confirm the disk position, the patient declined further examination because he was no longer inconvenienced in daily life and wished to avoid the long waiting period (approximately 1 month) required for a non‐emergency MRI at our institution.

Discussion

This case report details the rare coexistence of a BMC in a patient presenting with symptoms highly suggestive of posterior articular disk displacement.

Posterior disk displacement is considerably less common than anterior displacement, with a reported incidence of approximately 0.9% among all TMJ disorder patients [4, 8, 9]. In this condition, the articular disk is positioned posteriorly to the condyle, typically between the posterior aspect of the condyle and the postglenoid tubercle [4, 10], even when the condyle is within the glenoid fossa in a closed‐mouth position [11]. The opening disturbance in anterior disk displacement without reduction is clinically termed closed lock because of the inability to open the mouth after closure, whereas the closing disturbance in posterior disk displacement without reduction is termed open lock because of the inability to close the mouth after maximal opening. The precise causes and risk factors for posterior disk displacement are not fully understood [8]. However, it is recognized that locking often occurs after activities involving large mouth openings, such as yawning [12, 13]. This suggests that excessive condylar movement can lead to an over‐internalization of the articular capsule and lateral ligaments, resulting in the disk being displaced posteriorly.

It is crucial to differentiate posterior disk displacement from TMJ dislocation. In TMJ dislocation, the condylar head is displaced anterior to the articular tubercle and fails to return to the glenoid fossa. In contrast, with posterior disk displacement without reduction, the mandibular condyle returns to the glenoid fossa, and the limitation of movement occurs near the closed‐mouth position.

Clinical features associated with posterior disk displacement include TMJ pain, clicking, crepitation, open lock, and TMJ luxation [4]. Other reported features include an average maximum interincisal distance, a history of luxation and subluxation, and a lack of occlusal contact on the affected side [8]. However, these features are not consistently present. Initial treatment for posterior disk displacement is typically conservative, focusing on lifestyle modifications (e.g., avoiding wide openings), self‐exercise, cognitive‐behavioral therapy, and sometimes manual manipulation with or without pumping [14].

BMC is known to potentially cause issues with TMJ movement, particularly during opening and closing, as the additional bony part of the condyle can impede normal joint mechanics [2]. To date, there have been no documented reports describing a direct association between BMC and posterior disk displacement. In the present case, the right mandibular condyle was slightly larger due to the bifid morphology, and it is plausible that this anatomical variation contributed to the altered TMJ movement, potentially predisposing the patient to posterior disk displacement. However, as this is a single case report, further studies are necessary to ascertain the precise influence of BMC on the development of posterior disk displacement.

In the present case, based on the patient's initial report of being unable to close his mouth after yawning, the primary suspicion was a right TMJ dislocation. However, the subsequent clinical course and CT findings strongly suggested that the difficulty in closing his mouth evolved into an open lock caused by posterior disk displacement, rather than a sustained TMJ dislocation.

This case report has certain limitations. Firstly, a three‐dimensional CT reconstruction was not performed, which would have provided a more comprehensive understanding of the complex anatomical relationship between the BMC and the surrounding TMJ structures. Secondly, MRI was not conducted. An MRI would have unequivocally demonstrated the exact position and condition of the articular disk, confirming the posterior displacement and assessing any associated soft tissue pathology.

In conclusion, this case reports the rare occurrence of suggesting posterior disk displacement in a patient with an extremely rare bifid mandibular condyle. This unique presentation underscores the importance of thorough diagnostic imaging in complex TMJ disorders. Further detailed case studies and research are warranted to fully understand the potential influence of BMC on the etiology and clinical course of temporomandibular disorder including posterior disk displacement.
