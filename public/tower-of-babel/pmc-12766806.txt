Overcoming impaired antigen presentation in tumor-draining lymph nodes facilitates immunotherapy
Authors: Meghan J. O’Melia, Lutz Menzel, Pin-Ji Lei, Hengbo Zhou, Xingjian Zhang, Neian Contreras-Alvarado, Johanna J Rajotte, Lingshan Liu, Mohammad R Nikmaneshi, James W Baish, Jessalyn M Ubellacker, Genevieve M Boland, Sonia Cohen, Timothy P Padera, Lance L Munn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmunotherapies have revolutionized cancer care in recent decades, but approved therapies often fail and currently only target specific steps in the generation of anti-cancer immune responses. Notably, the majority of approved immunotherapies do not target antigen processing and presentation, which are key steps in the development of immune responses and harbor potential as targets to improve immunotherapy. Here, we hypothesize that tumor-mediated alterations in cytokine concentrations alter antigen presentation, which can be normalized by locoregional cytokine delivery or targeted immunological adjuvant delivery.MethodsWe used mouse models of breast cancer, with analysis by flow cytometry, immunofluorescence, confocal imaging, and single-cell RNA sequencing to address the impacts of tumors on locoregional antigen presentation, along with mechanisms to remedy these impacts.ResultsHere, we demonstrate that breast tumors induce locoregional impairments in dendritic cell antigen presentation that limits anti-cancer antigen-specific T cell responses. Antigen processing was not impaired in dendritic cells within the tumor-draining lymph node. A reduction of the cytokine IL-1β in tumor-draining lymph nodes was responsible for impairments in antigen presentation by dendritic cells. As such, we tested the ability of dendritic cells in lymph nodes at various distances from the primary tumor to be activated utilizing an antigen-agnostic immunological adjuvant delivery strategy. We observed improved antitumor T cell responses when immunological adjuvant was delivered to cancer antigen-positive lymph nodes distant from the tumor, suggesting that these lymph nodes can be targeted to improve anti-cancer immune responses. When combined with immune checkpoint blockade, delivery of immunological adjuvant to distant lymph nodes led to long-term survival and protection from recurrence. Antigen presentation by dendritic cells and T cell responses could also be recovered by exogenous delivery of IL-1β via intratumoral injection, with improved survival when combined with immune checkpoint blockade.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that tumor-induced impairments in antigen presentation in tumor-draining lymph nodes can be overcome by the appropriate introduction of immunological adjuvant to tumor-distant lymph nodes or by restoring IL-1β to the tumor-draining lymph node. These strategies can induce high-quality, durable immune responses and have clinical implications for expanding the efficacy of immunotherapies.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed malignant cancer in women. In the USA, one in eight women will be diagnosed with breast cancer during their lifetime.1 Fortunately, many breast cancers are detected early through screening programs; however, treatments for late-stage breast cancer are currently insufficient, with only approximately 31% of advanced breast cancer patients surviving long-term.1 Immunotherapies have revolutionized the treatment of many cancers due to their potential to elicit long-lasting cures, induce protection from recurrence, and impact distant metastatic disease.26 The most successful immunotherapies for cancer include immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), engineered T cell therapies, and vaccines. However, ICB—the most commonly applied clinical immunotherapy—has resulted in responses in only ~12%–33% of breast cancer patients.7 Another commonly used strategy, engineered T cell therapies, has met with poor efficacy when tested in solid tumors, with only ~33% of breast cancer patients in limited clinical trials demonstrating responses.8 Further, cancer vaccines, which have been underutilized clinically, have only resulted in responses in ~26%–40% of melanoma patients911 with little clinical application in breast cancer.1214 These therapies have struggled for many reasons, including tumor-mediated immune evasion, dysfunctional T cell activation, lack of appropriate cytokines, systemic pre-suppression due to prior treatment, and continual tumor mutation.1520 Additionally, all of these therapies have focused on mechanisms of immune function downstream of initial activation of anti-cancer immune responses, leaving the critical steps of antigen processing and presentation underexplored as therapeutic targets.

To improve immunotherapy responses, we first considered the various steps in the tumor immunity cycle.21 In short, for an efficient immune response to develop against a tumor, cancer antigen must access an antigen-presenting cell, and that antigen must then be presented to a cognate T cell alongside costimulatory molecules.21 A cancer antigen-specific T cell then becomes activated, proliferates, and recirculates to reach the tumor, where it finds the correct antigen-bearing cancer cell and kills it.21 The activation portions of this process typically occur in secondary lymphoid organs, such as lymph nodes (LNs), which are specialized organs that co-localize antigen, dendritic cells (DCs), and naïve T cells. The currently available therapeutic modalities (ICB, engineered T cells) focus on the T cell dynamics after (or without the need for) T cell activation, with few clinical approaches focusing on antigen presentation and T cell activation dynamics. As such, therapies targeting antigen processing and presentation are underexplored and have the potential to elicit substantial improvements in immunotherapy.

In this work, we demonstrate that breast cancer impairs the ability of tumor DCs or DCs in tumor-draining LNs (tumor-dLNs) to efficiently present cancer neoantigen, which limits the potential to develop a productive anti-cancer immune response. We used LN-targeted immunological adjuvant delivery strategies to induce antigen presentation and elicitation of an antigen-specific T cell response, which resulted in long-term cures when combined with ICB. We also identified reduced IL-1β levels within tumor-dLNs as a potential cause for tumor-mediated impaired antigen presentation. We demonstrated that localized delivery of IL-1β improves the capacity of DCs to present antigen and thus T cells to mount tumor antigen-specific responses. As a whole, this study demonstrates the potential of targeting antigen presentation in an antigen-agnostic manner to expand immunotherapy efficiency.

Results

Tissue-resident DCs in tumor-draining LNs are impaired in their capacity to present neoantigens

The presentation of neoantigens is a critical step for developing and sustaining anti-cancer immune responses. Thus, we measured the ability of DCs from the spleen and LNs to present neoantigens in tumor-bearing and naïve animals. The LNs included in the analyses were the inguinal LN, which directly drains the fourth mammary fat pad (MFP) where the primary breast tumor was implanted, and the axillary LN, which is directly connected to the inguinal LN via the inguinal-axillary lymphatic vessel. We evaluated LNs local to the tumor (ipsilateral inguinal LN—referred to throughout as local dLN—and ipsilateral axillary LN) or distant from the tumor (contralateral axillary LN—referred throughout as distant LN—and contralateral inguinal LN) (figure 1A). To measure the ability of conventional DC-1s (cDC1, defined as CD45+CD3−CD11c+CD11b−CD8+Xcr1+), conventional DC-2s (cDC2, defined as CD45+CD3-CD11c+CD8-CD11b+major histocompatibility complex (MHC)II+), and plasmacytoid DCs (pDC, defined as CD45+CD3−CD11c+B220+) (online supplemental figure 1) to process antigen, we implanted E0771 mammary carcinoma cells in the fourth MFP of immunocompetent C57/Bl6 mice. On day 7 of tumor growth, the relevant LNs (figure 1A), along with the spleen, were excised and single-cell suspensions were generated. Notably, metastatic burden is quite low in this model at this time point.22 To evaluate antigen processing, we stimulated cells from tumor-bearing animals compared with tumor-naïve animals ex vivo with DQ-OVA, a modified OVA protein in which the BODIPY fluorophore is self-quenched and emits no fluorescence in its native state but emits green fluorescence in its cleaved state, for 15 mins or 6 hours. Cells stimulated with DQ-OVA at 4°C were not DQ-OVA+, demonstrating that our interpretation of the DQ-OVA signal was valid (online supplemental figure 2A). Flow cytometry revealed that, as expected,2325 cDC2s were more likely to process DQ-OVA when compared with cDC1s or pDCs (figure 1B, (online supplemental figure 2C). However, we saw no difference in the ability of each DC subset to process DQ-OVA based on their tissue of origin or whether the DCs were from a tumor-bearing or tumor-naïve animal (figure 1B, (online supplemental figure 2B and C), indicating that the tumor had no effect on antigen processing.

We next assessed the ability of these DCs to present processed antigen using the 25D1.16 monoclonal antibody (mAb), which detects the OVA257-264 octapeptide (amino acid sequence: SIINFEKL) within a MHCI molecule. This revealed that cDC1 were most likely to present SIINFEKL within the MHCI, followed by pDC and cDC2 (figure 1C), consistent with the major functions of these distinct DC subsets.2325 When evaluating different DC subsets within these tissues, we found that cDC1s in the local tumor dLN had markedly reduced presentation of SIINFEKL compared with cDC1s in any other LN analyzed (figure 1C,D), despite no difference in antigen processing (figure 1B). The lack of antigen presentation in the tumor-dLN has potential ramifications for the development of an antigen-specific CD8+ T cell response. Among cDC1s within any LN not directly draining tumors, we were not able to see any differences relative to naive LNs (figure 1C,D), indicating that the impairment was local. pDCs within the local tumor dLN presented lower SIINFEKL within the MHCI in comparison to pDC within the contralateral inguinal and naive inguinal LN, mimicking the trends seen among cDC1s in the local tumor dLN. Finally, cDC2s had markedly lower presentation of SIINFEKL within the MHCI, as expected,2325 but we did identify some differences in MHCI:SIINFEKL staining with cDC2s within the local tumor-dLNs and Naïve inguinal LNs, which had lower SIINFEKL presentation compared with those in the contralateral inguinal LN. Further, cDC2s within the ipsilateral axillary (secondary tumor-dLN) LN had lower MHCI:SIINFEKL staining compared with those in the naïve axillary LN (figure 1C). Notably, the phenotype of DCs in these tissues was also altered by the presence of a tumor, with similar spatial dependence. DCs in the LN most proximal to the MFP tumor had the lowest levels of CD80 and CD86—markers of activation—among all CD11c+ cells and those presenting and processing cancer antigen. Interestingly, DCs in the local tumor dLN had higher MHCI and MHCII levels (online supplemental figure 3 and 4). We additionally measured IL-12 and CD83, markers of mature DCs, which were not different among all DCs or DC processing antigen (online supplemental figure 3 and 5). Within DC presenting antigen, IL-12 was highest among naïve LNs (online supplemental figure 5).

As a whole, a closer distance relative to the primary tumor seemed to correlate with decreases in antigen presentation, with the local tumor dLN being most impaired. The LN farthest from the tumor was unaffected by the presence of a tumor (online supplemental figure 2B). The impaired antigen presentation observed in the tumor-dLNs (figure 1C,D) was not due to issues in antigen processing (figure 1B), but rather a deficiency in the mechanisms involved in antigen presentation. As such, we examined expression of genes involved in MHC recycling, which did not reveal any differences that could explain the differences in antigen presentation (online supplemental figure 6),2632 suggesting the impairment is not transcriptionally regulated.

In order to assess the functional ramifications of this impaired antigen presentation, we isolated CD11c+ cells from relevant tissues (the local and distant LNs from E0771-bearing animals, compared with LNs from naïve animals, (online supplemental figure 7)). Due to concerns about the impacts of flow cytometry-associated sorting, we only evaluated total CD11c+ cells isolated by magnetic sorting and not DC subsets. The isolated CD11c+ cells were incubated with OVA for 6 hours, washed, and then co-incubated with CD8+ T cells isolated from OT-I animals, which all have antigen specificity towards the SIINFEKL antigen. OT-I cells were then stained for phenotypic changes. This revealed that OT-I CD8+ T cells incubated with DCs isolated from the local LN, which directly drains the tumor, had lower granzyme B (GzmB) and Tim-3 expression (figure 1E,F, online supplemental figure 7)—indicating lower functional CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity—when compared with OT-I CD8+ T cells incubated with DCs from the distant or naïve LNs. Mean fluorescence index of these markers was not different in these cells (online supplemental figure 2D and E).

To directly measure cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells educated by these DCs, we performed a co-culture imaging experiment to measure T cell killing of tumor cells. As above, DCs were isolated from relevant tissues, incubated with OVA, washed, and then co-incubated with CD8+ OT-I T cells. ZsGreen-SIINFEKL-E0771 cells were then added to these cultures and ZsGreen area was measured over time, which quantifies the number of live tumor cells in the culture. This revealed that T cells educated by DCs from the local tumor-dLN killed substantially fewer cells at 24 hours postaddition of cancer cells compared with T cells educated by DCs from the distant or naïve LN (figure 1G,H). Notably, uneducated T cells perform similarly to T cells educated by DCs from the local LN, indicating that education by DCs in the local tumor-dLN is quite poor (figure 1G,H). These data demonstrate that the deficits we measured in the ability of DCs to present antigen in tumor-dLNs have functional ramifications for the animal to activate antigen-specific CD8+ T cells.

Finally, we assessed the persistence of the local LN antigen presentation impairment after tumor removal. To this end, we orthotopically implanted immunocompetent C57/Bl6 mice with E0771 tumors. On day 14 of tumor growth, tumors were resected. Animals were sacrificed 1 day (to assess immediate impacts of tumor removal), 9 days (to assess a timepoint in which acute tumor-mediated effects could be ameliorated) or 28 days (to assess potentially metastatic lesions) later and antigen presentation was assessed by flow cytometry. On day one after tumor resection, antigen presentation impairment was similar to that seen in animals with intact tumors (figure 1G, online supplemental figure 8). On day nine post-tumor resection, antigen presentation was improved and was similar in local tumor-dLNs and distant LNs in tumor-bearing mice and LNs from naïve animals (figure 1I, online supplemental figure 8 and 9). On day 28 after tumor resection, antigen presentation was impaired in LNs with metastatic lesions, but in tumor-dLNs without metastasis, antigen presentation was similar to distant LNs (figure 1I, online supplemental figure 8 and 9). Thus, the presence of cancer or cancer-disseminated factors interferes with antigen presentation.

Phenotype of tumor-dLN-resident DCs is altered

To test the hypothesis that tumor-disseminated factors were responsible for the alterations in tumor antigen presentation, we performed multiplexed cytokine assays on tissues (tumors or naïve MFP, local dLNs, and distant LNs) derived from tumor-bearing or naïve animals (figure 2A, online supplemental figure 10). This revealed differences in IL-1β (total, includes both pro and active forms) in the local dLN in tumor-bearing versus naïve animals (figure 2A, online supplemental figure 10), but not the distant LN. To test the effects of these cytokines, we measured differences in antigen presentation after ex vivo culture of cells from tumor-naïve animals in the presence of IL-1β, IL-33, MCP, or IP10 with OVA (figure 2B, online supplemental figure 10). The only clear difference in antigen presentation occurred with the addition of IL-1β, in which antigen presentation was enhanced (figure 2B, online supplemental figure 10). As IL-1β concentration was decreased in the tumor and local dLN of tumor-bearing animals compared with naïve animals, but unchanged in the distant LN (figure 2A), we hypothesized that changes in IL-1β levels could explain the impaired DC antigen presentation (figure 1). As such, we isolated CD11c+ cells from tissues of relevance from tumor-bearing vs naïve animals. These cells were then incubated with OVA in the presence of IL-1β or vehicle (saline) for 6 hours, washed, and co-cultured with OT-I CD8+ T cells for 18 hours. The addition of IL-1β enhanced GzmB production in CD8+ T cells educated by CD11c+ cells from both the local and distant LNs from tumor-bearing animals (figure 2C,D, online supplemental figure 11), without differences in Tcf1 or programmed death-1 (PD1) expression (online supplemental figure 10), indicating that the addition of IL-1β in tumor-bearing animals has the potential to induce a functional antitumor T cell response.

As in vitro cultures do not completely recapitulate the complexity of the immune system and immune reactions, we next tested the effects of IL-1β on in vivo antigen presentation in tumor-naïve animals. To do so, we injected the flank skin of immunocompetent animals with IL-1β for 4 days (day 0–3), with the addition of DQ-OVA on day 3. Animals were then sacrificed on day four to assess antigen (OVA) presentation or anti-OVA T cell responses (figure 2E). Our data revealed that IL-1β had no impact on antigen processing in DCs as assessed by DQ-OVA positivity (figure 2E,F, online supplemental figure 12). However, the addition of IL-1β enhanced antigen presentation measured by the frequency of CD11c+MHCI:SIINFEKL+ DCs in the LN draining the IL-1β injection (figure 2G, online supplemental figure 12). We next assessed the impacts of these changes in DC antigen presentation on T cell responses. The LN draining the IL-1β injection showed no change in the number of total endogenous CD8+ T cells compared with vehicle administration or to distant LNs in the same animal (non-draining LN, NdLN) (figure 2H). However, GzmB+, PD1+, and Tcf1+ CD8+ T cells were enhanced in LNs that received IL-1β compared with animals receiving vehicle control (figure 2H). Thus, in cancer-naïve animals, IL-1β was able to enhance antigen presentation and T cell activation compared with vehicle controls.

We next assessed the impact of IL-1β injection targeting the tumor-dLN in a cancer context in vivo (figure 2I). A similar experimental plan compared with the above was used, with the addition of a WT E0771 tumor prior to the IL-1β administration. In short, animals were implanted with WT E0771 tumors (day −7), then IL1β was delivered daily on days 0–3 with DQ-OVA delivered on day 3 (figure 2I). Animals were then sacrificed on day four to measure antigen presentation and day 11 to assess T cell responses. IL-1β injection into the local flank skin did not change the number of DCs taking up and processing the antigen as assessed by DQ-OVA (figure 2J, (online supplemental figure 12) but did increase the number of DCs presenting antigen (MHCI:SIINFEKL+) within the local LN compared with mice that received vehicle control (figure 2K, online supplemental figure 12). There were also more CD80+ cells presenting SIINFEKL after IL-1β delivery in the local LN, indicating more reactivity by these DCs (figure 2L). Finally, we measured the development of antigen-specific T cells using SIINFEKL-specific tetramers. These data show that there were more antigen-specific T cells in both the local LN and tumor of animals that received IL-1β compared with those that received vehicle control (figure 2M, (online supplemental figure 12). Production of interferon (IFN)-γ by CD8+ T cells was also enhanced in the local tumor-dLN with delivery of IL-1β (figure 2N), indicating T cell functionality. Thus, IL-1β was able to improve the antigen presentation by DCs and generate a T cell response, potentially overcoming the antigen presentation impairment in tumor dLNs identified in figure 1.

Delivering immunological adjuvant to distant LNs enhances antigen presentation and antigen-specific T cells

Given that antigen presentation was impaired in local tumor-dLNs, we next tried a strategy aimed at initiating immunity in distant LNs,33 thus bypassing the immunosuppression that exists near the tumor. We hypothesized that the introduction of antigen and/or adjuvant to these distant, non-suppressed nodes would enhance antigen presentation and T cell activation. To determine the appropriate location for injection, we first mapped the distribution of a surrogate antigen (FITC-conjugated 2 MDa dextran) after injection in either the local flank skin or distant forelimb (figure 3A). After injection into the flank skin, surrogate antigen accumulated in the local dLN and ipsilateral axillary LN (which drains the local dLN) at approximately 102–103 fold higher rates compared with the distant LNs (figure 3B,C). When the surrogate antigen was injected in the forelimb distant from the tumor location, it accumulated in the tumor-distant LN (draining the injection site) at approximately 10-fold higher rates compared with any other LN (figure 3C). Thus, these injection sites could be used to target specific LNs.

To test the impact of an altered delivery strategy on response to immunization, E0771-OVA tumors were implanted into immunocompetent C57/Bl6 mice and OVA in combination with lipopolysaccharide (LPS)—an immunological adjuvant commonly used preclinically—(OVA+LPS) was injected into either the local flank (local OVA+LPS) or distant forelimb (distant OVA+LPS) on day 8 of tumor growth followed by a boost on day 14 (figure 3A). When the LPS+OVA combination was injected in the local flank, there were few differences in antigen presentation by CD11c+ cells in the targeted LN (the local LN), the non-targeted LN (distant LN), or the tumor (figure 3D, online supplemental figure 13). When the LPS+OVA were injected in the distant forelimb, antigen presentation by CD11c+ cells was enhanced in the targeted LN (the distant LN), but not the non-targeted LN (local LN) or the tumor (figure 3D, online supplemental figure 13). Neither intratumoral (i.t.) delivery of OVA+LPS nor vehicle administration altered antigen presentation by CD11c+ cells (figure 3D, online supplemental figure 13). When comparing across groups, only the distant OVA+LPS induced an increase in antigen presentation in any tissue when compared with the saline group (figure 3D, online supplemental figure 13). We also observed a very similar trend when examining the DC activation marker CD80 in these tissues, with only DCs in the distant LN demonstrating an enhancement in CD80 after forelimb delivery of OVA+LPS (figure 3E). As a whole, these data indicate that therapies targeting LNs that do not directly drain the tumor have the potential to enhance both quantity and quality of antigen presentation.

We next assessed the impacts of different delivery strategies on the development of an antigen-specific response using SIINFEKL tetramers to identify antigen-specific T cell activation. OVA+LPS injections targeted to a distant LN did not change the frequency of antigen-specific T cells in the local or distant LNs compared with the saline group (figure 3F, online supplemental figure 13). However, because antigen-specific T cells should move to the tumor after education in LNs, we evaluated these effects in this tissue, which revealed that distant LN-targeted OVA+LPS (distant OVA+LPS) did increase infiltration of antigen-specific T cells into the tumor when compared with the saline group (figure 3F, online supplemental figure 13). Likewise, the frequency of cancer antigen (SIINFEKL)-specific T cells expressing PD1 increased in the local LN when treated with Distant OVA+LPS compared with local or i.t. OVA+LPS, indicating enhanced antigen experience of those cells (figure 3G). When OVA+LPS was delivered locally or intratumorally, no changes in antigen-specific T cells were observed in the local or distant LNs or in the tumor when compared with the saline group (figure 3F, online supplemental figure 13). Thus, only distant OVA+LPS was able to enhance infiltration of antigen-specific T cells into the tumor, which has been shown to positively correlate with survival.3438 Interestingly, the ipsilateral axillary LN, which directly drains the local LN and tumor via the inguinal-axillary lymphatic vessel, showed very similar trends in the presence of antigen-specific T cells, indicating that improvements after local injections could persist beyond the very first tumor-dLN into a secondary LN (online supplemental figure 13). Because immunological adjuvants are typically utilized in vaccines to increase the development of antibody responses, we measured anti-OVA IgG in serum of these animals, which did not reveal differences in the development of anti-OVA antibodies based on the location of injection (though a clear difference was noted based on the formulation of injection) (online supplemental figure 14).

Delivering immunological adjuvant to distant LNs delays tumor growth

Strategies for vaccination typically require the availability and knowledge of a tumor-associated antigen. In the clinic, this is a challenge because of the wide variety of mutations leading to the development of continued antigen mutation throughout disease progression.3942 Because of this limitation, we next tested the ability of two clinically relevant immunological adjuvants, aluminum hydroxide salt (alum) used in TDaP (tetanus, diphtheria, and pertussis), hepatitis, and human papillomavirus vaccines; and Titermax Gold (Titermax), an analog to MF59 used in influenza vaccines,4346 to slow tumor growth in two breast tumor models (4T1 and E0771) without addition of exogenous antigen, that is, in an antigen-agnostic manner (figure 4A). Delivery of either adjuvant slowed tumor growth and enhanced survival when delivered distantly from the tumor, but not locally or intratumorally (figure 4B,C, online supplemental figure 15 and 16). We sacrificed animals treated with these adjuvants on day 28 of tumor growth to analyze the infiltration of cells into the tumor. This revealed that distant delivery of immunological adjuvant increased the infiltration of CD8+ T cells into the tumor (figure 4D, online supplemental figure 15). Additionally, we measured a higher proportion of GzmB+ cytotoxic CD8+ cells within the tumor (figure 4E, online supplemental figure 15). There were no differences in PD1+ or Tcf1+ infiltrates (online supplemental figure 15). Thus, distant delivery of immunological adjuvant alone (ie, antigen-agnostically) was able to slow tumor growth in two different preclinical models of breast cancer and enhance animal survival, independent of the type of immunological adjuvant itself, and in an antigen-agnostic manner.

As these antigen-agnostic strategies did not result in curative responses, we sought to combine immunological adjuvant delivery with ICB in the form of systemic αPD1 mAb delivery. Mice bearing E0771-ZsGreen-SIINFEKL tumors were vaccinated with LPS+OVA on day 8, followed by a boost on day 14 (figure 4F, online supplemental figure 16). On day 21, a time point in which antigen-specific T cells infiltrated the tumor to a higher degree after distant OVA+LPS treatment compared with vehicle control (figure 3), the first of 3 doses of αPD1 mAb or isotype IgG was given intraperitoneally. Tumor growth slowed in animals receiving the combined distant OVA+LPS treatment and αPD1 when compared with those receiving combined local OVA+LPS and αPD1 (figure 4G, online supplemental figure 17 and 18). Distant OVA+LPS (antigen+adjuvant) combined with αPD1 induced long-term survival in 100% of these animals (figure 4H, online supplemental figure 17 and 18), with protection from three different rechallenges (figure 4I, online supplemental figure 17 and 18), demonstrating an effective and long-lasting antitumor immune response. Finally, to investigate the effects of a less immunogenic and more physiological antigen source, we implanted mice with the MCaP-1362 breast carcinoma line and administered a “vaccine” composed of lysed MCaP-1362 cells combined with LPS, delivered either locally or distantly (figure 4J). This revealed that delivery of antigen+adjuvant to distant LNs slowed tumor growth more than delivery of antigen+adjuvant to local tumor dLNs (figure 4K). These data indicate that even in a translationally relevant scenario in which a lysed tumor sample with adjuvant is used, delivery to a distant LN could be more beneficial than local delivery. Overall, our studies have shown that combining distant delivery of immunological adjuvant with ICB reduces tumor burden, enhances animal survival, and induces the development of a robust and long-term, curative immunological memory response.

IL-1β in combination with other immunotherapies, but not as a monotherapy, slows tumor growth

We noted that distant adjuvant delivery induced higher IL-1β within the tumor, which may be responsible for the effects we saw in antigen presentation, antigen-specific T cell development, and tumor growth dynamics with distant adjuvant delivery (online supplemental figure 19). Based on these results, we sought to explore local IL-1β delivery as an alternative therapeutic option. Our data showed that IL-1β delivery to LNs could improve antigen presentation by DCs, so we tested the hypothesis that IL-1β supplementation could improve tumor responses. To measure the capacity of IL-1β to impact tumor growth, we orthotopically implanted animals with E0771 tumors and delivered IL-1β intratumorally (figure 5A). IL-1β monotherapy showed only a very slight slowing of tumor growth but did reach significance in overall survival (figure 5B, online supplemental figure 20). However, only one of seven animals survived long-term (figure 5B), which we hope to enhance.

As our data showed that IL-1β enhanced DC antigen presentation and subsequent T cell activation, we hypothesized that tumor-mediated impairment of T cell effector function could be preventing ultimate anti-cancer effector immune responses that would improve survival. Thus, we combined IL-1β delivery with ICB (αPD-1 mAb) to maintain functionality of effector T cells after IL-1β assisted activation (figure 5A). The data show a slowing of tumor growth in animals treated with this combination (figure 5C, online supplemental figure 20). Survival curves indicated some variability in long-term survival in the IL-1β+αPD1 group but indicated that this combination improved survival compared with both monotherapies (figure 5C, online supplemental figure 20). Notably, the αPD1 alone group performed worse than expected in this experiment.33 47 All animals surviving the original tumor implantation (treated in both the IL-1β monotherapy and IL-1β+αPD1 therapy groups) survived tumor rechallenge with 2×106 E0771 cells with no evidence of disease at any point (monitored until at least 100 days post rechallenge), indicating development of a durable immune response. Thus, combining IL-1β with αPD1 induced an improved response in a subset of animals and has the potential to improve clinical outcomes.

Discussion

The generation of effective anti-cancer immunity relies on intact antigen presentation within tumor dLNs. However, tumors often modulate localized immune microenvironments, leading to suppression of T cell priming in tumor dLNs. Our data show that breast tumors impair MHCI-mediated antigen presentation in local tumor dLNs, and this impairment can be compensated via either IL-1β delivery to the tumor-dLN or via targeted immunological adjuvant delivery strategies to distant LNs not suppressed by the tumor. These findings reveal the importance of an understudied and underappreciated step in the development of cancer immunity.

We identified reduced IL-1β as a key barrier to the process of antigen presentation by DCs in tumor-dLNs and showed that i.t. delivery of IL-1β in combination with αPD1 enhanced survival and slowed tumor growth. IL-1β is a potent activator of DCs, promoting the expression of costimulatory molecules and release of cytokines to support CD8+ T cell priming.4851 The tumor appears to dampen this pathway within tumor-dLNs, limiting the quality of T cell responses to disseminated antigen. Restoration of IL-1β signaling thus improved DC function and antigen presentation. However, IL-1β is a pleiotropic and highly inflammatory cytokine, with established roles in the pathogenesis of autoimmune and inflammatory diseases.52 53 Systemic administration of IL-1β or IL-1β agonists has been linked to toxicity and poor clinical tolerability,54 55 demonstrating the challenges of translating cytokine-based approaches directly. We propose that locoregional administration strategies can mitigate these potential systemic issues while improving response rates. Our results provide mechanistic evidence that IL-1β-dependent signaling is important in DC activation and that stimulation of antigen presentation in LNs that access tumor-disseminated antigen but are less immunosuppressed can be beneficial—even when using antigen-agnostic adjuvant.33 This approach has significant translational promise due to its simplicity and potential to enhance antitumor immunity without relying on direct cytokine administration or knowledge of tumor-specific neoantigens.56

Our findings also carry significant clinical implications in the context of ongoing preclinical and clinical efforts to change paradigms in LN resection in breast cancer. Increasingly, axillary LN dissection is being reduced or omitted altogether, reflecting a shift toward minimizing surgical morbidity.5759 Our results suggest that LNs are active participants in shaping systemic antitumor immunity. Tumor dLNs are more directly affected by the local tumor, while more distant LNs retain their ability to induce an anti-cancer immune response. These findings imply that preservation of portions of the lymphatic network may be beneficial for inducing systemic anticancer immune responses and for facilitating responses to immunotherapy. We have recently shown that resection of tumor-dLNs does not impair responses to ICB,60 indicating that more distant LNs may be more important in the development of these sustained immune responses. Overall, our data provide a rationale for continued evaluation of the potential of local vs more distant LNs to induce immunological responses in the context of immunotherapies for cancer.

It is important to recognize that cytokine and immunological adjuvant therapies exert broad immunological effects beyond modulation of DCs. These agents can influence stromal remodeling, angiogenesis, and metastatic dissemination in addition to their direct immunological effects. In our models, the duration of treatment was necessarily limited due to the nature of preclinical models and therefore may not fully capture longer-term effects such as metastatic progression and systemic inflammation. While our findings establish a clear mechanistic link between IL-1β signaling and antigen presentation along with distant adjuvant delivery effects on elicitation of an anti-cancer CD8+ T cell response, it is possible that neither of these strategies will yield durable systemic control in all settings. Future studies in models allowing for evaluation of metastatic outcomes, along with systemic responses due to chronic IL-1β exposure, will be critical to determine the full therapeutic potential of these interventions.

While these findings represent a clear conceptual advantage, translational challenges exist. The complexity of the human lymphatic network makes targeted LN delivery more difficult than in mice, where each drainage basin is simpler and more discrete. For example, in a mouse, there is typically one axillary LN on either side of the mouse, while in the human, there can be dozens of interconnected LNs.6164 Likewise, the length scales of these interactions differ substantially. We utilized the most distant LN that was in the same general lymphatic network in these studies, but acknowledge that the length scales in mice are still not directly comparable to humans. These differences make the targeting of the specific LNs of interest more challenging in the clinic. Computational approaches can be used to understand ideal LNs to target for an individual patient60 and improve chances of durable responses even in late-stage breast cancer patients. Future clinical translation could also leverage image-guided injection or biomaterial-based delivery systems to reach optimal LNs on a patient-specific basis.6572 Additionally, our studies were limited to preclinical models of breast cancer, with most using the E0771 model of luminal breast cancer (and some experiments performed in the 4T1 triple-negative breast cancer and MCaP-1362 breast cancer lines). However, we nonetheless hypothesize that these results could translate to other types of cancer, as the majority of our findings are immune cell-intrinsic, not cancer cell-intrinsic, implying the potential for commonalities and implications for additional cancer types. Additional limitations include our focus on MHCI antigen presentation and elicitation of CD8+ T cell responses; thus, tumor-induced alterations in MHCII presentation and CD4+ T cell responses remain to be explored.

Overall, this work demonstrates that local antigen presentation in tumor dLNs is impaired in the cancer context, in a process mediated by IL-1β. This, in turn, impairs the development of an anti-cancer T cell response, which allows the tumor to progress. However, by targeting immunological adjuvant to a distant LN or locally delivering IL-1β, we demonstrate prolonged survival. This work has potential implications for therapeutic interventions and shines light on an underexplored and exciting mechanism by which to target the tumor immunity cycle.

Materials and methods

Sex as a biological variable

Only female animals were included in this study, as the study focuses on breast cancer, which presents quite differently in males compared with females.

Animals and tumor models

We used three different syngeneic breast tumor lines in this work. E0771 mammary carcinoma cells (ATCC), MCaP-1362 mammary carcinoma cells (developed within Edwin L. Steele Laboratories73), and 4T1 mammary carcinoma (ATCC) were grown in vitro in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (Gibco, Invitrogen Life Technologies) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Atlanta Biologicals) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Invitrogen Life Technologies). Cells were maintained in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator at 37°C. All cells were recently authenticated and mycoplasma-free. E0771 cells (0.5×106), MCaP cells (106), or 4T1 cells (0.5×106) in saline (total volume 30 µL) were implanted in the fourth MFP of female immunocompetent C57/Bl6 (E0771) or BALB/c (4T1 and MCaP) mice (aged 6–10 weeks). Animals were monitored every day throughout tumor development and progression. For any survival experiments, surviving animals were monitored for at least 100 days without evidence of disease.

Flow cytometry

Animals were sacrificed in accordance with protocol guidelines. Tissues were resected and maintained on ice in Hank’s Buffered Salt Solution (HBSS). Tissues were processed by mechanical dissociation through a 70 µm cell strainer and washed with HBSS. For spleen samples, cells were resuspended in ACK lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher) for 7 min at room temperature and then quenched with approximately 30 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Samples were spun down for 5 min at 350 g and plated in a 96-well U-bottom plate. Plates were spun down for 5 min at 350 g and Zombie UV/NIR/Red (based on experiment) Live/Dead (1:100, Biolegend) was added to samples and allowed to incubate for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Samples were spun down for 5 min at 350 g and washed with PBS. Fc blocking antibody (anti-CD16/32 1:200, Tonbo Bioscience) was added to samples and allowed to incubate for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Samples were spun down for 5 min at 350 g and surface stain antibodies (online supplemental Table 1) were added. Samples were allowed to incubate for 30 min on ice in the dark. Samples were spun down for 5 min at 350 g and washed with PBS. For experiments without intracellular stains, BD fixation buffer was added to each sample and allowed to incubate for 30 min on ice in the dark. Samples were spun down for 5 min at 350 g, washed with FACS buffer (1% FBS in PBS), and maintained at 4°C in FACS buffer until analysis. For samples with intracellular stains, fixative/permeabilization buffer (eBioscience) was added to each sample and allowed to incubate for 30 min on ice in the dark. Samples were spun down for 5 min at 350 g, washed with eBioscience buffer, and spun down again. Intracellular stains (online supplemental Table 1) were then added and samples allowed to incubate for 30 min on ice in the dark. Samples were spun down for 5 min at 350 g, washed with eBioscience buffer, and spun down again. FACS buffer was added to each sample, and then samples were spun down for 5 min at 350 g, washed, and maintained at 4°C until analyzed via a customized BD LSRII or Cytek Aurora flow cytometer. Data were then analyzed using FlowJo V.10.

Ex vivo stimulation

Animals were sacrificed in accordance with MGH guidelines and tissues excised. Tissues were mechanically dissociated through 70 µm filters to generate single cell suspensions. For spleen and blood samples, cells were resuspended in ACK Lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher) and allowed to incubate for 7 min at room temperature. Samples were then quenched with~30 mL PBS and spun down for 5 min at 350 g. Cells were plated in 96-well U-bottom plates, spun down for 5 min at 350 g, and resuspended in 100 µL of appropriate media (Roswell Park Memorial Institute media, RPMI (Gibco, Invitrogen Life Sciences)+10% FBS for unstimulated samples; RPMI (Gibco, Invitrogen Life Sciences)+10% FBS+10 µg OVA (Sigma Aldrich) for OVA-stimulated samples; RPMI (Gibco, Invitrogen Life Sciences+10% FBS+10 µg DQ-OVA (Thermo Fisher) for DQ-OVA-stimulated samples; RPMI (Gibco, Invitrogen Life Sciences)+10% FBS+1 ng cytokine (Recombinant IL-1β, IL-33, MCP, or IP10; Biolegend) +10 µg OVA (Sigma Aldrich) for cytokine experiments) and allowed to incubate for 6 hours in a 5% CO2, humidified incubator at 37°C. Cells were then stained for flow cytometry as above.

Tumor resection

Animals were given preprocedure analgesic (0.1 mg/kg buprenorphine) and anesthetized using ketamine-xylazine (80–100 mg/kg ketamine+5–10 mg/kg xylazine). Eyes were lubricated with Lubricant PM ointment (AACE Pharmaceuticals). Animals were then placed on a warming bench, and the surgical site prepared. Any remaining hair around the surgical site was shaved, and then the site was cleaned alternately between betadine scrub and alcohol a total of three times. Once animals were prepared, an incision was made around the tumor site, and blunt dissection was used to separate the tumor from underlying fascia. The tumor was then resected and any bleeding controlled via pressure. The skin was then closed with wound clips. Post-procedure analgesic (0.1 mg/kg buprenorphine) was maintained for 72 hours after termination of the procedure. Wound clips were removed 12–14 days postprocedure.

Analysis of MHC recycling genes

The single-cell RNA sequencing data were obtained from NCBI GEO database GSE168181.26 The datasets of naïve LNs and tumor-dLNs were integrated using the “FindIntegrationAnchors” function in the Seurat R package to obtain anchors for batch bias normalization. Subsequently, the “IntegrateData” function was employed to integrate data and correct for technical differences. Next, the top 30 Principal Components (PCs) were selected for Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection analysis and clustering. Cell types were annotated based on the top highly expressed genes in each cluster. The MHC recycling genes were selected based on a literature search.2732

Biodistribution analysis

Two MDa FITC-dextran (Sigma-Aldrich) and 10 kDa TRITC-dextran (Sigma-Aldrich) in saline were coinjected into locations of interest (forelimb, flank, MFP, hindlimb) in a total volume of 30 µL. Animals were sacrificed 24 hours later, and tissues immediately frozen in optimal cutting temperature (OCT, Tissue-Tek) gel, and then maintained at −80°C. Blocks were then sliced into 10 µm sections using a cryostat. Slides were immediately covered with Vectashield (Vector Labs) to avoid diffusion. Vectashield was allowed to harden overnight, and slides were imaged using an Axioscan (Zeiss). Images were processed on a PC using the public domain National Institutes of Health (NIH) ImageJ program (developed at the U.S. NIH and available on the Internet at http://imagej.net/nih-image/).

Vaccination strategies

Several different vaccination strategies were used in this work: OVA+LPS (10 µg OVA (Sigma Aldrich)+10 µg LPS (Sigma-Aldrich) in saline for a total volume of 30 µL), LPS alone (10 µg LPS (Sigma-Aldrich) in saline for a total volume of 30 µL), Titermax (10 µL Titermax emulsified with 10 µL saline), alum (0.5 mg alum (VWR International) in saline for a total volume of 30 µL), and lysed cells (MCaP cells from culture, after five freeze/thaw cycles via dunking in liquid nitrogen) combined with 10 µg LPS and saline in a total volume of 30 µL. Treatments were prepared on the day of vaccination. Treatments were injected intradermally using a 31G insulin needle in the wrist skin of the distant forelimb relative to the tumor location, intradermally in the flank skin local to the tumor location, or intratumorally. Pressure was applied to the syringe plunger as the needles were removed from the injection site to avoid any backflow of the solution from the injection site. Saline injected intratumorally served as a control.

Antibody ELISA

Serum was collected for antibody ELISAs. In short, blood was taken from animals at sacrifice into 1.5 mL uncoated Eppendorf tubes. Blood was allowed to clot at 25°C for 30 min and then samples spun down at 1000 g for 10 min. Serum was removed from the tops of samples and maintained at −80°C. To prepare ELISAs, Nunc MaxiSorp high-affinity plates (Thermo Fisher, Inc.) were incubated with OVA (10 µg OVA (Sigma Aldrich) in 100 µL PBS) or PBS alone overnight. Plates were then washed with 300 µL PBS+Tween 20 (1% Tween 20 in PBS) three times. Serum (5 µL serum in 95 µL PBS) samples were then added to plates and allowed to incubate 2 hours at room temperature. Plates were then washed with 100 µL PBS three times. Streptavidin-conjugated anti-mouse IgG antibodies (1:1000, Southern Biotech) were then added to plates and allowed to incubate for 2 hours at room temperature. Plates were washed with 100 µL PBS. Biotinylated horseradish peroxidase (100 uL, R&D Biosystems) was then added to each sample and allowed to incubate for approximately 20 min at room temperature. Stop solution (50 µL, R&D Biosystems) was then added to each sample, and absorbance was read at 450 nm and 540 nm using a multiplex plate reader (MesoScale Discovery).

Immunofluorescence staining

Animals were sacrificed, and dissected tissues were placed into OCT gel (Tissue-Tek) and frozen. Tissues were maintained at −80°C. Blocks were then sliced into 10 µm slices using a cryostat. Slides were placed in −20°C acetone for 10 min and then air dried. Slides were then washed 3 times for 5 min in PBS+TritonX (PBS with 0.5% TritonX-100). 1% normal donkey serum (Millipore Sigma) in PBS was then added to samples and allowed to incubate for 1 hour at room temperature. Slides were then washed. Primary antibodies (online supplemental Table 2) were added to samples and allowed to incubate overnight at 4°C. Slides were then washed with PBS+TritonX. Secondary antibodies were added along with DAPI counter-stain and allowed to incubate for 1 hour at room temperature in the dark. Slides were washed, and then coverslipped with Vectashield (Vector Labs) and allowed to harden overnight. Slides were then imaged using an Axioscan (Zeiss). Images were analyzed using QuPath-0.4.3.74

Statistical analysis

Data are represented as the mean accompanied by SE of the mean (SEM), and statistics (one-way, two-way, or repeated measures (RM) analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test for grouped analyses; log-rank test for survival analyses; or t-tests, as indicated in figure legends) were calculated using Prism V.10 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, California, USA). Statistical significance was defined as p<0.05, 0.01, 0.005, and 0.001, respectively, unless otherwise specified.
