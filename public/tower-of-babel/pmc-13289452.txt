Association between the oxidative balance score and biological aging measured by epigenetic clock: insight from the NHANES 1999-2002
Authors: Mingming Lv, Zhihui Jiang, Changjiang Deng, Zhiyan Du, Bingxin Bai, Zhilong Wang, Adilai Adilijiang, Ying-Ying Zheng, Xiang Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOxidative stress is a key factor leading to oxidative damage in cells and tissues, and is a major driving force behind aging and various age-related diseases. Epigenetic clock and oxidative balance score (OBS) serve as reliable indicators for assessing an individual’s aging process and oxidative stress levels, respectively. However, no study has comprehensively assessed the association between epigenetic clock and OBS.MethodsThis study analysed 1,797 subjects from the 1999–2002 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). Multivariate regression models were used to assess the relationship between OBS and epigenetic age as well as epigenetic age acceleration (EAA). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses were used to visualize the dose-response relationship between OBS and EAA. Subgroup analysis and interaction tests were used to investigate whether this association was stable across populations.ResultsMultiple linear regression analyses showed that higher OBS levels were significantly associated with lower epigenetic age. Further analyses showed that both the dietary OBS and the lifestyle OBS were significantly negatively correlated with epigenetic age. RCS analysis revealed a significant negative linear dose–response relationship between OBS and EAA. The association between OBS and EAA was homogeneous across subgroups.ConclusionsThis study suggests higher OBS levels are associated with reduced epigenetic age and decelerated biological aging, and antioxidant-rich diets and lifestyles may delay biological aging.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-026-02141-w.

Introduction

As of 2019, 88 out of 188 countries have been defined as aging societies and population aging has increased the global burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), including death and disability from disease [1]. Aging is characterized by a progressive loss of physiological integrity, leading to impaired function and increased susceptibility to disorders [2]. Geroscience research has indicated that aging is the predominant risk factor for most age-related diseases, and that slowing the aging process can delay or prevent many chronic diseases [3]. Aging is a multifaceted process, and in order to slow down or even reverse it, there is a need for effective and reliable biomarkers of aging.

One hallmark of aging is epigenetic changes or age-related DNA methylation changes [4]. In recent years, epigenetic clocks based on DNA methylation of selected sites have emerged as a precise tool for assessing aging [2]. Its age prediction, known as DNA methylation age or epigenetic age, is compared with chronological age to indicate accelerated or decelerated aging. Studies have shown that epigenetic age acceleration (EAA) is significantly associated with the incidence and mortality of various age-related diseases [5–8]. Individuals with EAA may face a higher risk of adverse health outcomes, indicating that epigenetic clocks are valuable aging biomarkers for predicting biological age and mortality risk, as well as for evaluating interventions that could potentially extend human lifespan.

Oxidative stress is a key factor leading to the oxidative damage of cells and tissues and plays a crucial role in the development and progression of aging [9]. Oxidative stress induces cellular senescence through various pathways, such as mitochondrial dysfunction, DNA damage and telomere shortening, which in turn lead to a range of age-related diseases [9, 10]. However, the level of oxidative stress in the body is influenced by various factors, and an indicator that comprehensively reflects the redox state of an organism is crucial for stratifying the risk of aging. The oxidation balance score (OBS), calculated based on 20 antioxidant and pro-oxidative components in dietary and lifestyle factors, serves as a comprehensive indicator for assessing an individual’s oxidative/antioxidative status [11]. Generally, higher OBS indicates stronger antioxidant capacity and lower levels of oxidative stress in the body. Studies have shown that higher OBS are associated with a reduced mortality rate from various age-related diseases, including hypertension [12], diabetes and prediabetes [13], chronic kidney disease [14], and cancer [15].

In conclusion, epigenetic clock and OBS serve as reliable indicators for assessing an individual’s aging process and oxidative stress levels, respectively. However, no study has comprehensively assessed the association between OBS and biological aging measured by epigenetic clock. Therefore, the present study aims to investigate this association using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).

Methods

Study population

This study included subjects from the NHANES 1999–2000 and 2001–2002, because DNA methylation was measured from biospecimens collected from participants during these cycles. The NHANES is a nationally representative cross-sectional study conducted in the United States by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), with the primary objective of assessing the health and nutritional status of individuals. Data collection followed a complex, stratified, multistage probability sampling design, and all participants provided written informed consent. We merged data from two NHANES cycles, which included a total of 21,004 participants. The following individuals were excluded : (1) participants without OBS component data (n = 9,149); (2) participants without DNA methylation data (n = 9,694); (3) participants with extreme energy intake (n = 35); (4) participants without demographic data (n = 329). Ultimately, the study included 1,797 participants (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Flowchart for selecting participants.

DNA methylation and epigenetic biomarker production

DNA was extracted from whole blood of NHANES participants aged 50 and above from the 1999–2000 and 2001–2002 cycles and stored at −80°C. DNA methylation was measured using Illumina EPIC BeadChip arrays in the laboratory of Dr. Yongmei Liu at Duke University. The IDAT files generated by the Illumina EPIC BeadChip arrays were used to convert the red and green intensity signals into methylated and unmethylated signals, followed by quality control through color correction, background subtraction, and outlier removal. After imputation and normalization of the methylation data matrix, the coefficients provided by the authors for each epigenetic biomarker were applied to the corresponding subset of probes to generate various epigenetic biomarkers, including HorvathAge [16], HannumAge [17], SkinBloodAge [18], PhenoAge [19], GrimAge [20], GrimAge2 [21], ZhangAge [22], LinAge [23], WeidnerAge [24], VidalBraloAge [25], HorvathTelo [26], DunedinPoAm [27]. All pre-processing and DNA methylation epigenetic biomarker production were completed in the RStudio environment using the R language. In July 2024, NHANES publicly released these data files, from which we directly extracted the precomputed epigenetic biomarker variables for analysis. This dataset has been used in prior published studies [28].

Definition of OBS

According to previous literature, the calculation of the OBS includes 16 nutrients and 4 lifestyle factors, comprising 15 antioxidants and 5 pro-oxidants [29]. The 16 nutrients were obtained through a 24-hour dietary recall survey and include dietary fiber, carotene, vitamin B2, niacin, vitamin B6, total folate, vitamin B12, vitamin C, vitamin E, calcium, magnesium, zinc, copper, selenium, total fat, and iron. The four lifestyle factors include physical activity, body mass index (BMI), alcohol consumption, and smoking. Smoking is estimated by cotinine levels, and physical activity data include detailed information on moderate and vigorous leisure activities, with intensity quantified using metabolic equivalent (MET) scores. The 5 pro-oxidant components include total fat, iron, BMI, alcohol consumption, and cotinine, while the remaining factors are classified as antioxidants. For alcohol consumption, non-drinkers, non-heavy drinkers (female 0–15g/day, male 0–30g/day), and heavy drinkers (female ≥ 15g/day, male ≥ 30g/day) are assigned scores of 2, 1, and 0 points, respectively. Physical activity was classified as low (< 600 MET-minute per week), moderate (600–3000 MET-minute per week), and high physical activity (≥ 3000 MET-minute per week), and these were assigned 0, 1, and 2 points, respectively [30]. We determined scores for the other components based on gender and the tertiles of each component. Antioxidants were assigned points from 0 to 2 for the lowest to the highest tertile group, respectively. The point assignment for pro-oxidants was inverse, with 0 points for the highest tertile group and 2 points for the lowest tertile group. Covariate assessment

In this study, the covariates comprised age, gender (male/female), race/ethnicity (Mexican American, non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, other race), education (less than high school, high school diploma, some college or higher), marital status (divorced/separated/widowed, married/living with a partner, never married), poverty income ratio (PIR) and energy intake.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were expressed by quartiles of the OBS. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Group differences across OBS quartiles were assessed using chi-square tests for categorical variables and ANOVA for continuous variables. Pearson correlation coefficients were used to examine the association between chronological age and epigenetic age. For epigenetic clocks other than HorvathTelo and DunedinPoAm, the residual values obtained from the linear regression model comparing epigenetic age to chronological age represent aging. Positive values constitutes EAA, indicating a higher epigenetic age relative to chronological age and thus faster biological aging. Given the complex sampling design of NHANES, we developed three weighted multivariate linear regression models to evaluate the relationship between OBS and epigenetic age. Model 1 was unadjusted, Model 2 adjusted for age, gender, and race, and Model 3 further adjusted for education, marital status, PIR, and energy intake. To thoroughly investigate the relationship between OBS and epigenetic age, OBS was analyzed both as a continuous and a categorical variable. To further examine the relationship between OBS and EAA, we also constructed three weighted multivariable logistic regression models using the same adjustment sets. We constructed restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses based on the fully adjusted logistic regression model to visualize the dose-response relationship between OBS and EAA. To determine the optimal number of knots, we calculated the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) for different knot numbers (3-6) to guide model selection. Subgroup analyses and interaction tests were performed using the fully adjusted model to explore potential effect modification. Additionally, sensitivity analyses were conducted to separately assess the associations of dietary OBS and lifestyle OBS with epigenetic age. Statistical analysis followed the CDC analysis guidelines. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.3). A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Correlations between epigenetic age and chronological age

Figure 2 presents the correlation coefficients (r) between chronological age and 12 epigenetic clocks. Except for WeidnerAge and VidalBraloAge, we observed a strong positive correlation between chronological age and epigenetic age, ranging from r = 0.77 (LinAge or PhenoAge) to r = 0.89 (ZhangAge). HorvathTelo was negatively correlated with chronological age (r = −0.60), as telomere length shortened with the increase of age. Distributions of epigenetic age residuals stratified by OBS quartiles

Figure 3 presents the distribution of residuals derived from regressing epigenetic age on chronological age, stratified by OBS quartiles. For PhenoAge, residuals decreased progressively across increasing OBS quartiles (P = 0.004), suggesting that higher OBS levels are associated with decelerated biological aging as measured by PhenoAge. A similar pattern was observed for GrimAge and GrimAge2 residuals across OBS quartiles (both P < 0.001), indicating a significant slowdown in the aging process. Likewise, untransformed DunedinPoAm values showed a declining pattern with higher OBS levels (P < 0.001), suggesting that the pace of biological aging similarly slows down as OBS levels increase. In contrast, no statistically significant differences were found across OBS quartiles for the remaining epigenetic clocks (all P > 0.05).Fig. 3Distribution of epigenetic age residuals across oxidative balance score (OBS) quartiles. Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to assess the relationship between OBS and epigenetic clocks based on a relatively large sample from NHANES. We found significant inverse associations between OBS levels and both epigenetic age and accelerate biological aging. RCS analysis revealed a negative dose-response relationship between OBS and accelerated biological aging as measured by GrimAge and GrimAge2. The association between OBS and biological aging acceleration was homogeneous across subgroups. Sensitivity analysis showed that both dietary OBS and lifestyle OBS were negatively correlated with epigenetic age. These findings indicate that higher OBS levels are associated with reduced epigenetic age and decelerated aging. This evidence underscores that maintaining a high level of antioxidative status through diet and lifestyle may represent a viable strategy for anti-aging interventions.

We conducted a comprehensive study on the relationship between OBS and 12 different epigenetic clocks among middle-aged and older adults in the U.S. Our results indicate that GrimAge, GrimAge2, HorvathTelo, and DunedinPoAm exhibit stronger associations with OBS compared with other clocks. Epigenetic clock estimates biological age by applying machine learning algorithms to DNA methylation patterns at specific cytosine-guanine dinucleotides (CpG) sites [16–27]. However, heterogeneity in the composition of epigenetic clocks including selected CpGs and clinical biomarkers contributed to different association strengths with outcomes. The development of first generation epigenetic clocks was primarily based on DNA methylation data of selected CpGs, such as Hannum clock (71 CpGs) and Horvath clock (353 CpGs) [16, 17]. In contrast, the second generation clocks incorporated not only more CpGs but also clinical biomarkers. For example, Levine’s clock PhenoAge incorporates 513 CpGs, chronological age, and a set of biomarkers strongly associated with mortality [19]. The Horvath group’s GrimAge is a composite biomarker based on seven DNA methylation-based estimators of plasma proteins and a DNA methylation -based estimator of smoking pack years [20]. DunedinPoAm, a novel algorithm developed using aging data and whole-genome methylation data to predict the rate of aging, may actually be the first of the third generation of clocks [27, 31]. Previous study found that the second generation epigenetic clocks were more strongly associated with an increased risk of both arsenic-related cardiovascular disease incidence and mortality than the first generation epigenetic clocks [32]. Consistently, our results suggest that the second generation epigenetic clocks are more effective in capturing the impact of oxidative stress on aging.

Being the first study addressing OBS and epigenetic clocks, we can only compare our results with previous research on oxidative stress and other biological ages. Phenotypic age is a novel indicator of biological aging, calculated based on a combination of chronological age and nine clinical chemistry biomarkers [33]. Wu et al. reported that higher OBS levels are significantly associated with slower phenotypic age acceleration, suggesting that higher levels of OBS may be associated with a slower biological aging process, which is consistent with our findings [34]. In addition, our subgroup analyses demonstrated a consistent association between OBS and biological aging measured by GrimAge and GrimAge2 across all subgroups. This suggests that populations with varying demographic characteristics may still delay aging by enhancing their OBS levels. Telomere length, a well-established indicator of biological aging, exhibits a negative association with the aging process. Zhang et al. reported a positive association between OBS and telomere length measured by qPCR (qPCRTL) in females but not males [29]. In our study, OBS also showed positive associations with DNA methylation-based telomere length (DNAmTL) in all models. Previous study demonstrated that DNAmTL exhibited a stronger correlation with chronological age compared to qPCRTL (r = −0.58 vs. r = −0.25) [28]. Additionally, ROC analysis showed that DNAmTL was superior to qPCRTL for predicting long-term all-cause mortality (AUC: 0.74 vs. 0.62, P < 0.001) and CVD mortality (AUC: 0.75 vs. 0.64, P < 0.001). Therefore, compared with qPCRTL, DNAmTL may be a more valuable biomarker for aging.

The association between OBS and epigenetic clocks may be explained by biological mechanisms involving oxidative stress and DNA methylation. DNA methylation is a significant epigenetic alteration during the aging process, with CpG being the primary targets of DNA methylation modifications in mammals [35]. Mounting evidence indicates that oxidative stress dynamically regulates methylation/demethylation patterns of genes and their expression. O'Hagan et al. discovered that oxidative damage induces the relocalization of DNMT1, DNMT3B, SIRT1, and EZH2 from low GC-content regions togene promoter regions containing CpG islands, forming a large multi-protein complex(es) that promotes DNA methylation [36]. Oxidative stress induced aberrant DNA methylation patterns through DNA strand breakage and damage, while also regulating DNA demethylation by DNA oxidation or TET-mediated hydroxymethylation [37, 38]. In addition, oxidative stress promote DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT) expression, which leads to site-specific hypermethylation in neurodegeneration [39]. A recently research showed that aging and oxidative stress lead to aberrant methylation of the CpG island in the LEDGF promoter, resulting in the silencing of LEDGF [40]. Additionally, chronic inflammation is also one of the Hallmarks of aging, and oxidative stress can lead to age-related tissue damage by promoting inflammation [41]. Considering the critical role of oxidative stress in modulating DNA methylation, the OBS, as a comprehensive measure of oxidative homeostasis, may capture the DNA methylation modifications underlying the construction of epigenetic clocks. Subsequent research needs to s further investigate aging-related gene loci and their methylation patterns, particularly focusing on their regulatory mechanisms by oxidative stress.

Diet and lifestyle factors represent practical interventions for modulating aging processes, with accumulating evidence linking them to epigenetic aging. For example, higher dietary intake of creatine was associated with reduced biological age acceleration and mortality risk as estimated by epigenetic biomarkers [42]. Inadequate intake of specific nutrients, including vitamin B12 and vitamin C, has been associated with accelerated epigenetic aging [43]. Exercise training has been shown to shift epigenetic and transcriptomic patterns toward a younger profile in human skeletal muscle, while muscle disuse accelerates aging-related changes [44]. A 6-month endurance exercise training intervention demonstrated that GrimAge decreased by 7.44 months relative to the expected trajectory, and this change was correlated with improvements in cardiorespiratory fitness [45]. In a thymic regeneration study, the average epigenetic age was approximately 1.5 years lower than baseline after one year of treatment, along with improvements in risk indices for many age-related diseases [46]. A retrospective analysis reported that 7-month supplementation with α-ketoglutarate turned back epigenetic age by 8 years [47]. Wang et al. reported that an antiinflammatory and/or antioxidant diet may mitigate biological aging [48]. In the present study, further analyses showed that both the dietary OBS and the lifestyle OBS were significantly negatively correlated with epigenetic age measured by four epigenetic clocks. Our findings suggest that higher antioxidant dietary and lifestyle exposures are associated with attenuated biological aging, consistent with the existing literature. In mice, the overexpression of antioxidant enzymes can reduce ROS production and protect DNA from its harmful effects, which is associated with increased lifespan [49]. Moreover, it is reported that increased intake of dietary antioxidants, such as vitamin E, are associated with reduced risk of atherosclerotic diseases and Alzheimer's disease [10]. Bouzid et al. reported that regular physical activity can enhance antioxidant defenses and reduce lipid peroxidation levels in both young and elderly individuals, highlighting the importance of regular exercise in slowing down aging-related damage [50]. To our knowledge, no prior study has specifically examined the association between the oxidative balance score and multiple epigenetic aging markers. Given the results of this study, we recommend that future research on aging consider using epigenetic clocks as efficacy indicators for antioxidant-based interventions.

This study has several strengths. First, the complex sampling design used in NHANES makes our findings generalizable to the U.S. population. Second, by using both the OBS and epigenetic age as indicators, we can more intuitively understand the relationship between an individual’s oxidative stress levels and aging. Third, we conducted rigorous and comprehensive statistical analyses, demonstrating the robustness of our key findings. There are also several limitations to this study. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal inferences between OBS and epigenetic age. Second, the dietary and lifestyle data used to calculate OBS come from recall interviews, which may introduce recall bias. Third, although OBS is a comprehensive indicator of oxidative stress, certain antioxidant components such as flavonoids and resveratrol were not included in its calculation. Fourth, despite identifying a linear dose-response relationship between OBS and biological aging through RCS analysis, further clinical validation is needed to confirm the impact of interventions targeting OBS on biological aging. Fifth, despite adjusting for multiple covariates, residual confounding due to unmeasured factors (e.g., genetic background, medication use) cannot be completely excluded.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study is the first to reveal a negative correlation between OBS and biological aging measured by multiple epigenetic clocks. Antioxidant-rich diets and lifestyles may reduce epigenetic age and delay biological aging, emphasizing the importance of antioxidants in anti-aging strategies. These findings need to be further validated in prospective studies with larger samples and different populations in the future.
