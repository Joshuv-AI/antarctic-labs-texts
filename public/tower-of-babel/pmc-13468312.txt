Identifying and Managing Pre-Myopia: A Narrative Review
Authors: Amy H Y Chow, Deborah A Jones, Brie M McConnell, Natalia Vlasak, Rakhee Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProactive management of children at risk of developing myopia (pre-myopia) can be more impactful than treatment after onset. The aim of this narrative review is to identify risk factors associated with pre-myopia, explore predictors of myopia development in pre-myopic children, and discuss effective prevention strategies.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted in several databases and clinical trial registries, using keywords related to pre-myopia prevention and control.Results266 studies were included. Risk factors associated with pre-myopia development include parental myopia, female gender and insufficient outdoor time. Near work and screen time have a modest link to myopia development, and the role of physical activity remains unclear. Predictive models can categorize children as low, moderate, or high risk of developing myopia. Lifestyle changes, such as increasing outdoor time and limiting near work may be sufficient to stave off myopia onset. Low-dose atropine or myopia control spectacles may be the most effective options for clinicians considering active intervention. Although effective for myopia prevention, repeated low-level red light should be used cautiously in children until its safety profile is better understood.ConclusionEffective myopia prevention involves managing the hyperopic reserve alongside axial elongation. Behavioural, pharmacological and optical treatments can be effective for preventing myopia, though more evidence is needed before it changes standard practice for pre-myopia management.

Introduction

Myopia is becoming increasingly prevalent worldwide, affecting nearly 30% of the world as of 2020 and estimated to rise to 50% by 2050.1 Myopia typically develops in childhood between 6–10 years of age and continues to progress through adolescence and sometimes adulthood.2 Several approaches (such as atropine, myopia control spectacles and contact lenses (soft and ortho-keratology) are known to be effective in slowing myopia progression in myopic patients, and as such, the World Council of Optometry has advocated for changing the standard of care from merely correcting myopic refractive error with standard optical correction to implementation of myopia management.3

The next major challenge lies in preventing or delaying the onset of myopia. An earlier age of onset is often associated with a more severe myopic refractive error by adulthood.4,5 For each year that myopia onset can be delayed, the resultant level of myopia by adulthood can be reduced by up to 0.97D for East Asian children and up to 0.50D for non-East Asian children.4,6 Thus, shifting the focus towards prevention may be more impactful, as achieving a similar reduction in an already myopic patient would require years of myopia control interventions.6

In 2019, the International Myopia Institute introduced the concept of “pre-myopia” to describe children who are highly likely to develop myopia, based on their age, refractive status (cycloplegic refractive error being ≤ +0.75D and > −0.50D), and risk factor profile.7 Presently, there is no consensus among clinicians regarding the management strategy for pre-myopic children. The aim of this narrative review is to identify risk factors associated with pre-myopia, explore predictors of myopia development in pre-myopic children, and discuss effective prevention strategies.

Methods

A comprehensive search was conducted by a medical librarian (BMM) in January 2025 and re-run in November 2025. The databases and registries used for this search included Medline OVID (Ovid MEDLINE and Epub Ahead of Print, In-Process, In-Data-Review and Other Non-Indexed Citations, Daily and Versions 1946 to November 1 2025); OVID Embase (1947 – November 1 2025); Pubmed (1966 – November 1, 2025); Web of Science (1900 – November 2025); and Europe PMC (1946 – November 1, 2025). The search included a combination of keywords and controlled vocabulary (MeSH terms) relating to prevention and control of myopia and pre-myopia in children. The full search strategies for each database are included in Appendix 1. Animal studies were excluded in the search strategies and chronological filters (2020–2025, present) were applied. The timeframe included in the search strategies is because the term “pre-myopia” is a relatively new term and gained prominence through a 2019 white paper published by the International Myopia Institute.7 The formal search was accompanied by a hand search for myopia risk factors to capture publications prior to 2020. The reference lists of associated scoping reviews, systematic reviews, meta-analyses and IMI white papers were also manually screened for additional records. Publications were excluded if: (1) full-text was not available; (2) English translation was not available; (3) publication was retracted; (4) population did not include children; (5) wrong patient population (myopic children); (6) no intervention or (7) was an editorial/correspondence. Additional keyword searches were run in the registries for clinicaltrials.gov, and the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP) for the World Health Organization, as well as in the preprint server, medRXiv.

Search results were imported into Covidence (https://www.covidence.org, Melbourne, Australia) for screening. A total of 1177 publications (1054 from search strategy, 123 from manual review of cited references of related studies) were considered (see Figure 1). After the removal of duplicate references and irrelevant studies, 348 full-text studies were screened for eligibility. Eighty‑two studies were excluded, resulting in the inclusion of 266 studies for review. Publication types included guidelines, expert consensus, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, evidence summaries, randomized clinical trials and longitudinal studies. Narrative Synthesis

Prevalence of Pre-Myopia

The prevalence of pre-myopia varies by age, and appears to be most prevalent in children between the ages of 5 and 10 (Figure 2).8 A large population-based cross-sectional study found that pre-myopia was the most common refractive error in children aged 5–6 years old in Taiwan, with a prevalence of 52%.9 Table 1 details a summary of published prevalence studies, delineating the studies using the IMI definition of pre-myopia. Pre-myopia is more prevalent in Asian children (47.8%) than non-Hispanic White children (26.2%).10 The prevalence of pre-myopia has also been increasing over time. In a longitudinal study in China, rates of pre-myopia in children aged 4–6 years have increased significantly from 19.0% in 2005 to 26.5% in 2021, with a corresponding decrease in the prevalence of hyperopia (from 65.4% to 51.1%) alongside an increase in myopia rates (15.6% to 22.4%) over the same period.11 Similarly, in Slovakia, the percentage of pre-myopic eyes increased from 31.9% in 2019 to 48.9% in 2022 in children aged 7–12 years.12 As children grow older, the proportion of pre-myopic tends to decrease, as children may be becoming myopic instead.13 The average age of myopia onset has decreased from 10.6 years in 2005 to 7.6 years in 2021.11 The rate of conversion from pre-myopia to myopia is estimated to be between 30% and 35%.14,15 Table 1Comparison of Prevalence Studies of Pre-MyopiaStudyAge (Yrs)Pre-Myopia CriteriaRefractionPre-Myopia PrevalenceCountryYin et al, 202383-6−0.50D < SER ≤ +0.75DCycloplegic286/1308 = 21.9%ChinaZhu et al, 2025163-6−0.50D < SER ≤ +0.75DCycloplegic311/1141 = 27.3%ChinaHu et al, 2025173-6−0.50D < SER ≤ +0.75DCycloplegic513/2109 = 24.3%ChinaWang et al, 202295-6−0.50D < SER ≤ +0.75DCycloplegic11318/21761 = 52.0%TaiwanMukazhanova et al, 2022186-710-11−0.50D < SER ≤ +0.75DCycloplegic598/769 = 77.8%541/768 = 70.4%KazakhstanYam et al, 2020196-8−0.50D < SER ≤ +0.75DCycloplegic2062/4257 = 48.4%Hong KongLiu et al, 2022136-12–0.50D < SER < + 0.50DCycloplegic235/778 = 30.2%ChinaModrzejewska & Durajczyk, 2024208−0.50D < SER ≤ +0.75DCycloplegic704/1155 = 60.9%PolandPršová et al, 2023127-12−0.50D < SER ≤ +0.75DCycloplegic15/47 = 31.9% (2019)23/47 = 48.9% (2022)SlovakiaDeng et al, 2024216-12−0.50D < SER ≤ +0.75DNon-cycloplegic1020/2253 = 45.3%ChinaJiang et al, 2020226-11−0.50D ≤ SER ≤ +0.50DNon-cycloplegic866/2097 = 41.3%ChinaNotes: Bold face denotes the studies that used the IMI definition of pre-myopia. Identifying Pre-Myopic Children

Insufficient Hyperopic Reserve

Part of the process of emmetropization is the progressive decline of hyperopia with age. However, when hyperopia decreases too quickly or at an unusually early age, children are at an increased risk of developing myopia.23 Chinese children with a hyperopic reserve of less than 1.50D were at an increased risk of developing myopia in the following year, regardless of age and gender.11 Among Chinese children aged 7–9 years with a refractive error of ≤+0.50D, the incidence of myopia is 33% within one year and 50% within two years.24 Similarly, in a longitudinal cohort study of 7 year old children in China, the 5-year incidence rate of myopia was lowest in children who had a baseline refractive error >+2.00D (4.4%), as compared to children with baseline refractive errors between 0.00 and >-0.50D (92.0%).25 Thus, a larger hyperopic reserve is associated with a lower risk for developing myopia in a dose-dependent manner.14,25

Central to the definition of pre-myopia is a lower-than-expected hyperopic reserve for a child’s age.7 However, what constitutes an adequate hyperopic reserve appears to vary across ethnicities, with children of East Asian descent likely needing a greater hyperopic reserve than non-East Asian children. This is consistent with their greater axial elongation rates as compared to Caucasian children.26,27 Table 2 details recommended hyperopic reserve thresholds as suggested in the CLEERE and Chinese studies.28,29 A large population study found that only 10% of East Asian children had this higher reserve threshold, which may explain the high rates of myopia in Chinese populations.26 Thus, definitions of pre-myopia may need to include a broader range of hyperopic reserves to mitigate myopia development across different ethnic groups. Recommended Hyperopic Reserve Levels Found Across Different Ethnicities

Accurate evaluation of the hyperopic reserve is critical for assessing whether a child is pre-myopic or at risk of becoming pre-myopic. Thus, cycloplegia plays a critical role in determining a child’s refractive error. Cycloplegic refractive error provides a reliable measure of baseline spherical equivalent refraction, and is a strong predictor of future myopia onset.25,28,30 The use of cycloplegia helps detect an accelerated loss of hyperopia (ie refractive shifts towards myopia), which is known to take place up to 3–4 years prior to myopia onset in the pre-myopic years.14 A longitudinal study of 3–6 year old children found steep declines in hyperopic reserves of between 0.2D to 0.45D per year once below a threshold of +1.00D.31 Cycloplegia can also help avoid miscategorization of borderline emmetropes and hyperopes as pre-myopic children. The use of atropine for cycloplegia results in less overestimation of pre-myopia prevalence and reveals a more hyperopic measure of refractive error as compared to cyclopentolate (8.7% vs 21.6% of truly pre-myopic patients within the same study sample).32 Furthermore, it can help with detecting pseudomyopia (≤-0.50DS before cycloplegia, but >-0.50DS after cycloplegia), which is an independent risk factor associated with myopia onset. Pre-myopic children who presented with pseudomyopia had a 2.66 times greater risk of developing myopia.33 These refractive characteristics are best detected through the use of cycloplegia to determine the level of risk in pre-myopic children.

Accelerated Axial Elongation

In addition to a steep loss of hyperopic reserve, pre-myopic children who subsequently become myopic also experience accelerated axial elongation prior to myopia onset.14,27,34,35 This acceleration in axial elongation appears to start around +1.00D as children enter the pre-myopia range, as compared to growth rates for children with over +1.00D of hyperopia.14,35,36 While children with longer axial lengths are more likely to be pre-myopic than hyperopic children, axial length alone may not be a sufficient differentiator between pre-myopic and emmetropic refractive states.16,37,38 A long axial length alone does not indicate impending myopic refractive error, especially with corneal and lens power compensation.5,39 While axial elongation slows in adolescence for children who remain emmetropic, children who subsequently become myopic continue along the same rate of growth or even accelerated growth (exceeding normal growth rates of 0.2 mm/year).27,35,39–44 Axial elongation rates exceeding 0.2 mm/year effectively predicts myopia onset for pre-myopic children between 6–10 years of age, especially when used in conjunction with cycloplegic refractive error measurements.45,46 On the other hand, pre-myopic eyes that remained non-myopic only exhibited growth rates between 0.15 and 0.21 mm year.45 This abnormal growth rate can be detected in children up to 2–3 years before myopia onset and accelerates the year before myopia onset, making it a reliable indicator of myopia development.22,35,47 While axial elongation is typically greater in Asian children compared to non-Asian children, the acceleration in growth rates appears to be common across ethnic groups.27,35,36,48,49 This suggests that changes in eye growth regulation may play a role in the transition from pre-myopia to myopia. Thus axial length can be monitored as children approach emmetropia to detect an abnormal rate of change and referenced against gender and ethnic-specific normative growth charts to determine a child’s percentile for myopia risk.5,39,41,50 Any progression from a lower percentile position to a higher percentile position elevates the risk of myopia development.5,41 Axial length measurements can be obtained relatively quickly and accurately, and cycloplegia is not required.51 While axial length biometers primarily designed for adults with cataracts are also well-tolerated by children and provide the most accurate and reliable measurements, newly-developed multifunction instruments (using partial coherence interferometry or optical low‑coherence interferometry) provide sufficient performance for tracking axial length.51,52 These multifunction instruments also include software that track axial length changes over time and incorporate normative datasets to aid practitioners in determining appropriate management.52

Increased Axial Length to Corneal Radius (AL/CR) Ratio

Another useful screening attribute is to consider the ratio of axial length to corneal radius of curvature (AL/CR ratio), which can help in distinguishing pre-myopic children from emmetropic children. Corneal radius refers to the measurement of the anterior corneal curvature in keratometry readings, and often reported as a mean of steep and flat meridians. Pre-myopic children have longer axial lengths as well as AL/CR ratios than hyperopic children.16 Children with a higher baseline axial length and AL/CR ratio have an increased risk of becoming pre-myopic.37 Children who subsequently developed myopia had a higher baseline AL/CR ratio than those who did not develop myopia.53–57 Children with an axial length/corneal radius ratio beyond the 75th percentile are almost 3x more likely to develop myopia than those below the 25th percentile.37 However, there is no singular threshold that can predict myopia onset as the axial length/corneal radius ratio differs across age groups. As this ratio is a relatively new metric, normative age- and gender-specific percentile growth curves have only been published only for Chinese children.56 Further research should focus on determining normative growth curves for this ratio in children of other ethnic origins. Nevertheless, the AL/CR ratio may be a useful screening marker for identifying pre-myopic children who may be at higher risk of developing myopia. However, the use of the AL/CR ratio may not be prognosticative of myopia onset, as the rate of change in this ratio did not differ between children who were pre-myopic and children who remained hyperopic.34

Risk Factors Associated with Myopia Development in Pre-Myopic Children

Parental Myopia

Parental myopia is a significant non-modifiable risk factor that is associated with pre-myopia and subsequent development of myopia.58 Children are more likely to be pre-myopic if one parent (odds ratio 1.5 times more likely) and both parents are myopic (odds ratio around 2.6 times more likely) compared to if neither parent was myopic.59,60 Children with two myopic parents were more likely to be pre-myopic and subsequently myopic (hazard ratio of 1.7) compared to children with neither parent being myopic.37 While having parents with mild myopia does not significantly increase myopia risk, the risk becomes nearly 12 times higher when both parents are highly myopic.61

Parental myopia reflects both genetic susceptibility and shared environmental factors. Genetics accounts for only 10–35% of an individual’s refractive error.62,63 Susceptibility also varies by ethnicity, with East Asian children having a greater predisposition to myopia compared to European Caucasians.64–66 Beyond shared genes, parents with myopia may be passing on a myopiagenic lifestyle, shaped by cultural educational pressures and preferences for indoor versus outdoor activities. Children with parental myopia, especially parents with moderate to high myopia, may be a higher risk group for pre-myopia and require closer monitoring.

Gender

Evidence suggests that the impact of gender on the development of pre-myopia may be mixed. While pre-myopia appears to be more prevalent in boys than girls across several studies., the differences are not statistically significant.8,9,17,21 A cohort study found that when a child’s refractive error enters the pre-myopia range (around +0.63D), the risk of myopia development within the next year is 7-fold if the child is male as compared to 4.7-fold if female.67 However, in another study of Taiwanese children, while boys had a greater risk of being myopic (OR 1.24), girls were more likely to have new onset myopia in subsequent years within the study.68 This may be partly attributable to differential growth patterns across genders. Mean corneal radius was found to increase with age in boys but not girls during preschool years,69,70 and boys aged 3–5 years have a marginally higher AL/CR ratio as compared to girls.71 However, girls experience a more rapid decline in hyperopic reserve than boys,72 and thus more likely to develop myopia.72,73 A meta-analysis found that myopia incidence is 1.28 times higher in girls than boys.64,74 Overall, gender may interact with other environmental and behavioural factors triggering the transitioning from pre-myopia to myopia, highlighting the importance of closer monitoring during this period.

Outdoor Time

Insufficient outdoor time is another significant but modifiable risk factor for pre-myopic children. Children who spend less than two hours a day outdoors have an increased risk of becoming pre-myopic.21 A study using wearable wristwatches in Shanghai, China found that pre-myopic children tended to spend less than 90 minutes per day outside, which may explain the high regional rates of myopia prevalence.75 In contrast, in regions with low myopia prevalence, children spend nearly 4 hours outdoors each day.76 Each additional hour spent outdoors per week reduces the odds of developing myopia by between 2% and 5%.77–80 The risk of developing myopia increases from 10% to 30% when children engage in less than 5 hours of outdoor time a week as compared to more than 14 hours a week.81 Similarly, increasing time outdoors is effective in lowering the incidence of myopia.65,82–85 For instance, daily 40-minute outdoor sessions led to a significant reduction in myopia incidence (30.4% vs 39.5%) in children 6 years of age over the course of 3 years.83 Increasing outdoor time to 80 minutes a day reduced myopia incidence rates from 17.65% to 8.41%86 and from 17.4% to 14.5%85 in one year. Children who spent more time outdoors have a 2.4% lower incidence rate after one year, 4.2% after two years, and 9.3% after three years, as compared to peers who had less outdoor time.87

However, the protective effect of time outdoors may require at least 120 mins per day for pre-myopic children.15 The protective effect of outdoor time was modest for pre-myopic children and reached statistical significance only at the longest exposure levels. For the same duration of outdoor exposure, the protective effect on hyperopic reserve appears greater in hyperopic children than in pre-myopic children.15 Each additional hour of outdoor time was associated with a reduced risk of myopia onset in a dose-dependent manner (hazard ratio of 0.97 for 1–2 hours, 0.90 for 2–3 hours, 0.86 for >3 hours).88 Evidence remains mixed as to whether outdoor time has an impact on axial elongation or perseveration of hyperopic reserves, possibly due to the variability of outdoor time protocols.77,87 Rates of axial elongation did not differ between myopic and pre-myopic children following light exposure.89

Part of the protective effect of outdoor time has been attributed to the higher luminance levels outdoors during daytime, which typically exceeds 10,000 lux. Evidence suggests that spending at least 1–2 hours daily with this level of light intensity may be sufficient to significantly reduce myopia risk.90,91 In contrast, pre-myopic children tended to be exposed to light levels below 2500 lux.75 Emerging evidence indicates that lower light levels (between 2000–5000 lux) can still be effective to limit myopic shifts and axial elongation, provided that the duration of exposure is extended.75,92 It is estimated that achieving 700,000 to 850,000 cumulative lux per day can reduce myopia incidence by 21–30% over two years.91 This would be achievable with 140–170 minutes under a tree shade, which typically ranges from 5556 to 7876 lux.93 Even brief durations can be protective against myopic shifts in refractive error as long as light intensity is sufficient.75 While increased duration and higher light intensity are independently effective, optimizing with both provides an additive effect on myopia prevention.

Near Work

Near work appears to have a modest impact on myopia development. It encompasses activities at short to intermediate working distances, such as reading, studying (doing homework, writing), using computers, playing video games or watching TV.94 Near work increases the odds of developing myopia by 31%, and children who perform more near work are more likely to be myopic.95–97 The duration of near work appears to have an impact on the risk of myopia development. A meta-analysis found that the risk of myopia development increases by 2% for each additional dioptre-hour of near work per week.94 Pre-myopic children who spent more time reading were found to have greater myopic shifts in refractive error and axial elongation.98 Spending more than one hour per day on near work was significantly associated with an increased risk of myopia by between 1–2 times.64,99 Each additional diopter-hour of near work increased the risk of developing myopia by 1–1.43 times (odds ratio).99 Each additional hour of reading time was associated with a slightly increased risk of myopia onset in a dose-dependent manner (hazard ratio of 1.02 for 1–2 hours, 1.03 for 2–3 hours, 1.05 for >3 hours).88 Children who became myopic performed significantly more near work per week than those who remained non-myopic (19.4 vs 17.6 hours), though this association was observed only in the younger subgroup consisting of 6-year-olds.65 Nevertheless, several other studies have found no evidence that longer durations of near work elevates the risk of myopia development.24,65,98,100

Of the various types of near work activities, homework and reading have consistently been linked to increased myopia risk as well as linked to less hyperopic refractive errors.96 Children who became myopic tended to perform more near work, particularly reading and homework, compared to children who did not develop myopia.96 Compared to children who read between 4–5 hours per day, children who read more than 6 hours per day had 1.65 times the risk of developing myopia.101 Greater homework demands and extracurricular tutoring was also significantly associated with an increased risk of developing myopia.102 Younger children who developed myopia tended to spend less time doing intermediate distance activities.96 For older children, those who developed myopia spent more time using computers as compared to those who remained non-myopic.96 Reading at night-time with low illumination may also increase myopia risk, particularly for older children.103 Overall, the risk of myopia development increases by 14% for studying, reading and writing, and 7% for watching TV.99 However, one confounding factor is that these activities occur at different working distances.104 The odds of myopia development are 51% lower if reading is done at a distance of at least 30 cm.105,106 Smaller losses in hyperopic reserve were associated with activities with greater working distances, such as watching TV or viewing projectors in online teaching as compared to the use of tablets or smartphones.12 It is also possible that it is not necessarily near work itself that produces myopia, but rather less time spent outdoors, as it explains nearly 40% of the effect of education on myopia development.107,108 While outdoor time and reading time appear to be independent contributors to myopia risk, myopia risk increases significantly when reading time exceeds time spent outdoors.88

Screen Time

The use of digital devices has been associated with an increased risk of pre-myopia and subsequent myopia development. Children are more likely to be pre-myopic when spending more than 1–2 hours per day using screens.9,59 In a study of children between 6–7 years of age, more than 2 hours of screen time was associated with being pre-myopic (odds ratio 2.4).59 Similarly, another study involving children aged 6–12 found more than 2 hours of screen time was also associated with becoming pre-myopic (odds ratio 1.6).21 In younger children (ages 5–6), more than 1 hour of screen time was associated with an increase in the prevalence of pre-myopia (odds ratio 1.1).9 An age-appropriate hyperopic reserve was associated with spending less than one hour per day on digital devices (such as computers, video game consoles, iPads, smartphones and TV).59 Screen time is also associated with myopia incidence, where children between the ages of 6–14 with screen use exceeding 2 hours per day had an increased risk of developing myopia (hazard ratio of 2.21 for 2–4 hours/day, 3.5 for >4 hours/day).109 This association persisted even after accounting for outdoor activity and parental myopia. Recent systematic reviews have found that each additional hour of screen time was associated with 21–31% greater odds of becoming myopic.97,110 The risk of myopia development increases sharply for the first 4 hours of daily screen time, followed by a more gradual increase with additional screen time.

Evidence remains mixed as to whether screen time plays a causal role for the development of myopia. A systematic review that found only half of the included studies found an association between screen time and myopia.111 Another meta-analysis found that screen time on computers and TV was associated with myopia development but not for smartphone use.112 Screen time is similar among young children regardless of whether they subsequently developed myopia.96 It may be that screen time is only replacing reading as a near activity, with no evidence that screen use poses a greater risk.95 Similar to near work, the concern may be that time spent on screens is time not spent outdoors.

Physical Activity

It remains unclear whether insufficient physical activity is a risk factor for pre-myopia and subsequent myopia. Several studies find that physical activity levels do not differ between emmetropes and myopes.113–115 However, increased physical activity has been associated with reduced risk of developing myopia, with each additional hour per day reducing myopia risk by 12%.97 While some studies report a protective effect of physical activity,81,116,117 other studies suggest that the risk of myopia onset is confounded by outdoor time instead.64,118 A recent study suggests that the protective effect of physical activity may depend on the amount of outdoor time children are getting. In non-myopic children aged 6–9, the protective effect of physical activity indoors was only evident for children who had at least 2 hours of outdoor exposure per day.15 For children who had limited outdoor exposure (<2 hours/day), outdoor activities had a greater protective effect, and there was no additional benefit of indoor activity.15 Physical activity may be another modifiable risk factor that can be considered after ensuring adequate outdoor time.

Sleep

There is inconclusive evidence in the literature as to whether sleep alters the risk for pre-myopia. A study using parental reports of children’s sleep behaviour found that children who awaken during the night, or reluctant to sleep at bedtime, or exhibited daytime sleepiness had an increased risk for pre-myopia, as well as myopia.119 However, late bedtimes and early wake-up times on weekdays were associated only with myopia but not pre-myopia.119 Insufficient sleep is not significantly associated with myopia incidence.120 Sleep duration and quality are also confounded by educational pressure, as children who have heavy study loads after school are more likely to get less sleep.107 However, sleep deprivation is less common for early years of grade school when pre-myopia typically begins.

Risk Stratification for Pre-Myopia

Risk stratification for pre-myopic patients is crucial for determining an appropriate clinical management strategy to delay or possibly prevent myopia onset altogether. Myopia prevention requires both an encouragement of healthy lifestyle behaviours and an avoidance of negatively associated risk factors. A study conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic found that while it was difficult for children to achieve numerous positively associated factors (such as adequate sleep, a balanced diet, regular outdoor activity) within a single day, it was more common for children to engage in negatively associated factors (such as poor desk posture, insufficient indoor illumination, uninterrupted or prolonged screen time).121 Hypothetical optimal control of both positive and negative factors through the pandemic could potentially have reduced myopia prevalence by 27.8%, myopia incidence by 9.5%, and myopia progression by 4.6% in Chinese children and adolescents.121 Myopia was more likely to develop when a cumulative score of modifiable risk factors, including outdoor time, visual and lifestyle habits, and environmental influences, reached seven.122 Ultimately, myopia development is multifactorial, with modest individual attributes that cumulatively increase the risk of pre-myopia.9

Detecting Pre-Myopia

While cycloplegic refractive error is most effective in identifying pre-myopic children, not all eye care practitioners have the scope of practice to perform cycloplegic refraction. The best non-cycloplegic approach involves considering both non-cycloplegic spherical equivalent refractive error with the AL/CR ratio, which results in an acceptable discrimination performance for detecting pre-myopia (area under curve (AUC) = 0.72–0.74).8,60 This approach had better detection sensitivity compared to relying on any single indicator alone: AL/CR (AUC 0.69–0.71), non-cycloplegic SER (AUC 0.67–0.70), axial length (AUC 0.63–0.67) parental myopia (AUC 0.59) or uncorrected visual acuity (AUC 0.42–0.52).8,60

Predicting Myopia Development in Pre-Myopic Children

Being able to predict which pre-myopic children are most likely to progress, based on their risk factors and clinical data, would optimally guide clinical management. The use of modelling has identified several key risk factors associated with the development of myopia. Pre-myopia in preschool age emerges as the strongest predictor of subsequent myopia onset (OR 9.64), followed by female gender (OR 3.1), higher AL/CR (OR 2.3), longer AL (OR 1.7), and parental myopia (OR 1.4–1.82).25,123,124 Increased near work (>21h/week), insufficient outdoor activity (0h/week) and insufficient sleep (<9.25 hours/day) around Grade 2 were associated with depletion of hyperopic reserve, which may be a critical tipping point as early as 3–4 years prior to myopia onset.125 Losses of hyperopic reserve by more than 0.375D or axial elongation greater than 0.22mm over 1 year was found to have high sensitivity (95.7%) as a screening threshold for pre-myopic patients.126 Another approach using retinal fundus images found that the length of Bruch’s membrane between the macula and the optic disc could be used as a predictor of myopia development in pre-myopic children even at 3–6 years of age.127 Specifically, the Bruch’s membrane was shorter in pre-myopic children who later developed myopia within 3 years. This is likely due to the more prolate/curved retinal shape in myopia.

One of the strongest predictive models incorporating baseline cycloplegic SER, gender and parental myopia achieved excellent discrimination of which pre-myopic children subsequently develop myopia, with AUCs of 0.941, 0.921, and 0.879 for 1-year, 2-year, and 4-year predictions.128 Another model used non-cycloplegic SER, uncorrected visual acuity, AL, AL/CR demonstrated similar performance even without cycloplegic measures (AUC 0.921).129 A model combining choroidal thickness with SER, colour fundus photography, and other variables such as age and weight, achieved good discrimination performance (AUC 0.845).130 Choroidal thinning, particularly, in the macular region, may serve as early signs of myopia development.130 Other complex models have been developed for children of different ethnicities, including the Collaborative Longitudinal Evaluation of Ethnicity and Refractive Error (CLEERE) model (C-statistic of 0.83–0.92),131 Northern Ireland Childhood Errors of Refraction (NICER) study (AUC of 0.87),132 Singapore Cohort Study of the Risk factors for Myopia (SCORM; AUC 0.91),30 Wenzhou Medical University Essilor Progression and Onset of Myopia (WEPrOM; AUC 0.826)133 and Predicting Myopia Onset and progression (PreMO; AUC 0.834–0.926).134 PreMO uses baseline cycloplegic refraction, axial length, and parental myopia data to generate a risk score ranging from no risk (0) to high risk (7–9), predicting whether children will develop myopia by age 15. Using a PreMO score ≥ 4 had high sensitivity in predicting myopia onset in children in the UK and Hong Kong, with a low rate of false positives.135

Machine learning and deep learning algorithms have also been able to pick up on non-obvious patterns within large datasets. A machine-learning based predictive model achieved an AUC of 0.963 for predicting myopia onset in pre-myopic children within 1 year.136 Baseline SER was the most significant predictor, followed by parental myopia, baseline subfoveal choroidal thickness and age, while factors such as gender, baseline AL and AL/CR ratio did not have significant predictive value in this model. A subset of machine learning, deep learning, uses multi-layer neural networks for modelling complex patterns in datasets. One such deep-learning based model, DeepMyopia, has been engineered to predict myopia onset.137 Using retinal fundus images in conjunction with non-cycloplegic clinical data (age, gender and axial length), it achieved good 1-year discrimination performance (AUC 0.796) with an external validation set. DeepMyopia was also able to sort pre-myopic children into low and high-risk groups. Another deep learning algorithm developed in Croatia was able to predict refractive error for Croatian children for up to 7 years with an error of 0.10 ± 0.15D, using only cycloplegic refractive error and parental history information.138 High predictive accuracy was achieved without axial length or corneal curvature measurements, or lifestyle attributes such as time spent outdoors, near work/screen time and room illumination. Other deep learning algorithms have attempted to estimate cycloplegic refractive error from non-cycloplegic clinical data, offering a solution for areas where access to cycloplegic drops or suitably qualified health care providers is limited.139

Management of Pre-Myopia

There is currently no consensus on the management strategy for pre-myopic children. Behavioural interventions involving increased outdoor time and limiting near work have demonstrated modest effectiveness. Increasing awareness for children and their caregivers and frequent reminders may be key to behavioural changes. Public health interventions involving weekly school-based health education led to a modest reduction in the 2-year incidence of myopia, though it had no effect on axial elongation when compared to those who did not receive this educational program.140 Another novel approach involved incorporating lifestyle and vision habits in a song that children would be able to recall. Children who were introduced to the myopia song spent less time on near work and more time outdoors, and had significantly reduced prevalence of myopia (30.5%) than the control group (41%).141 Reminders against prolonged near work, close working distance and inadequate light combined with feedback (in the form of weekly reports, praise and rewards) were effective in lowering myopia incidence (13.3% vs 27.8%) as well as limiting loss of hyperopic reserve and axial elongation (0.52D vs 0.73D; 0.3 mm vs 0.4 mm respectively).142 Thus, myopia prevention involves a comprehensive approach improving awareness among children and their caregivers as well as encouraging consistency of behavioural changes.

Behavioural Interventions

Increase Outdoor Time

Public health programs in China, Taiwan and Singapore encouraging children to spend at least 2 hours outdoors daily have demonstratable efficacy in lowering myopia incidence.92 The amount of time spent outdoors plays a critical role in preventing pre-myopic children from becoming myopic.143 Spending 40–80 minutes a day outdoors (exceeding 3000 lux) is effective in reducing myopia incidence.83,85,89 Spending 30–120 minutes outdoors daily in the preschool years was able to reduce the odds of developing myopia by between 15–50%.124,144 Spending 120–150 minutes a day outdoors with at least 5000 lux (approximately 600,000–750,000 lux total per day) was effective to reduce myopia risk by between 11–16%, as well as limit loss of hyperopic reserve and axial elongation.91 Each additional hour of outdoor time correlates to an additional 2% decrease in myopia risk.79

Increased outdoor exposure may mitigate the impact of risk factors such as near work and parental myopia. Children who spent more than 3 hours on near work could decrease their risk of myopia by 5-fold by spending more than 2.8 hours per day outside.113 Similarly, children aged 6 years old had a 16-fold decreased risk of developing myopia by spending >23 hours/week outdoors combined with low levels of near work (≤13 hours).65 Children living in areas with greater sunshine exposure had a lower prevalence of myopia than those living in less sunny areas, though this protective effect diminished as educational pressures increased with progression through grade school.145 In Inuit populations transitioning to more formal education, studies found that 1 hour of daily outdoor time was insufficient to prevent myopia.146,147 For children with elevated risk due to parental history of myopia, reaching 2 hours of outdoor time daily can reduce their risk to be similar to that of children without parental myopia.81

Attempts to mimic the outdoors with outdoor scene classrooms (with images of trees and sky) have a modest impact.89,148 While outdoor scene classrooms do not significantly impact myopia incidence, it reduced hyperopic reserve loss and axial elongation.149,150 Increasing indoor illumination with ambient light levels of 558 lux of illumination on the desk and 440 lux for the blackboard in classrooms seem to be sufficient to confer a protective effect.148 Even modest increases in classroom illumination (from 100 to 500 lux) reduced the risk of myopia development in primary- and middle-school students by 6% within one year.148 This intervention also reduced the magnitude of myopia shift in refraction by 0.22D as well as reduced axial elongation by 0.07 mm. The use of artificial lighting mimicking the natural spectrum of outdoor light has also been found to reduce the incidence of myopia and slow axial elongation in Chinese children.151 Similarly, the use of light therapy spectacles emitting a spectrum similar to natural sunlight led to a slightly thicker choroid in adults, which is considered protective against myopia, despite the glasses emitting only 506 lux.152 Spending time in green spaces also appears to have a protective effect and are associated with a reduced risk of myopia development.153,154 The protective benefit of green spaces appears to be most impactful for children between the ages of 7–12.155 Decreased proximity to green spaces was associated with a lower risk of myopia development, even after adjusting for parental myopia, outdoor time, reading time and screen time.156,157

Overall, children with limited time outdoors may be at higher risk of becoming pre-myopic and encouraging time outdoors prior to becoming pre-myopic may be the most effective way to mitigate this risk.

Limit Near Work

Efforts to balance near work demands with outdoor time have also been effective. A ratio of near work to outdoor time exceeding 1:2 is associated with an increased prevalence of myopia.158 Working distance should be at a minimum of 30 cm, and activities preferably further away if possible, such as watching TV or using projectors at school.105,106 Desks and chairs should be at suitable heights for children to maintain a healthy desk posture.74

Atropine

While atropine is known to be an effective form of myopia control, it is also able to mitigate the progression from pre-myopia to myopia. Concentrations of 0.01%, 0.025% and 0.05% atropine have helped pre-myopic children retain an average of 0.58D of hyperopia and slow axial elongation by 0.19 mm compared to a non-treated group over the course of 1–2 years.179 Stronger concentrations appear to be more effective, as a meta-analysis that compared 0.01%, 0.025% and 0.05% atropine dosages found that 0.05% atropine reduced the myopia incidence rate by 25% over two years.179 A concentration of 0.05% atropine has also been shown to be more effective than 0.01% atropine in retaining hyperopia and reducing axial elongation for pre-myopic children.180 A randomized controlled trial in Hong Kong found that only 0.05% atropine (and not 0.01%) significantly reduced myopia incidence relative to a non-treated group.165 Both 0.05% and 0.025% atropine reduced the proportion of children who experienced an accelerated loss of hyperopic reserve relative to control groups.160,181

While 0.01% atropine has been shown to be effective in reducing loss of hyperopic reserve and axial elongation, it may not be effective for pre-myopic children with lower levels of hyperopic reserve.159,162,164 The use of 0.01% atropine helped children retain around 0.40D of hyperopia reserve over a period of two years.182–184 While 0.01% was able to retain the hyperopic reserve for children who ultimately remained emmetropic, it was insufficient for children with lower levels of hyperopic reserve, who subsequently became myopic.159 In particular, the ATOM3 study found limited efficacy with 0.01% atropine likely due to the inclusion of children with at least one parent <-3.00D who were at a higher risk of pre-myopia. The axial elongation in the pre-myopic children included in their study was greater than reported in most population studies (0.37 mm vs 0.08–0.14 mm).42 Thus, it is important to monitor myopia risk as hyperopic reserve decreases and consider a dose that may be best suitable for the child’s risk profile, and tailor the dose accordingly.

The mechanism of action for atropine remains unclear, though it likely involves dopamine signalling in the retina to reduce axial elongation. Atropine appears to increase choroidal thickness in both myopic and non-myopic children185 and reduces the choroidal thinning associated with hyperopic defocus.186 It also appears to increase superficial retinal vascular density in the retina.176 Atropine does not seem to influence corneal or crystalline lens power.187

Myopia Control Spectacles

Optical interventions, such as the use of myopia control spectacle lenses, also appear effective in slowing progression from pre-myopia to myopia. These spectacle lenses induce peripheral myopic defocus around a central clear zone. For instance, the Defocus Incorporated Multiple Segments (DIMS) spectacle lens uses 396 segments with +3.5D defocus power scattered across a mid-peripheral zone in a honeycomb pattern, whereas the Diverse Segmented Defocus Optics (DSDO) spectacle lens has 256 lenslets arranged radially with add power decreasing from +4.00D to +3.00D towards the peripheral outer rings. A study comparing these two spectacle lens designs (DIMS and DSDO) found pre-myopic children using DSDO spectacle lenses experienced less axial elongation (0.16 mm) than children using DIMS spectacle lenses (0.28 mm) after a year of usage.167 DSDO spectacle lenses were more effective in slowing axial elongation in pre-myopic children than DIMS spectacle lenses, though with a small sample. There was no statistical difference between these two spectacle lenses in terms of preserving the hyperopic reserve (DSDO −0.13 DS vs DIMS −0.26 DS). DSDO lenses were also effective in reducing incidence rates (5.8% vs 15.3%) and the number of children experiencing greater than 0.50D of loss in hyperopic reserve in 1 year (15.7% vs 42.7%) when compared to wearing SV spectacles.166

Another myopia control spectacle design using Highly Aspherical Lenslets (HAL) has also been studied in pre-myopic children. Pre-myopic children using HAL spectacle lenses had significantly reduced axial elongation (0.19 mm) compared to children wearing single‑vision spectacle lenses (0.24 mm) after 12 months of use.168 Across two other studies, plano HAL spectacle lenses significantly reduced axial elongation and helped pre-myopic children retain more hyperopia relative to control groups.169,170 As a result, fewer pre-myopic children experienced accelerated axial elongation (50% vs 81.6% in the control group).170 Another study of children with low hyperopia (plano to +2.00D) found that HAL spectacles lenses significantly slowed axial elongation compared to single‑vision lenses.168

Other spectacle designs that similarly incorporate peripheral defocus in lenslet array designs have also been effective in pre-myopic children. Hexagon Optimized Reticular Integration (HORI) spectacles limited hyperopic reserve loss and axial elongation more effectively than 0.01% atropine alone.161 Similar to atropine, HORI spectacle lenses reduced the incidence of myopia as well as the proportion of fast progressors. Using both HORI spectacles and 0.01% atropine together had the greatest efficacy in terms of limiting loss of hyperopia and axial elongation. Non-myopic children (plano to +2.00D) using Optical Technical Of Guiding Emmetropization (OTOGE) spectacle lenses (with peripheral defocus on both front and back surfaces) experienced smaller hyperopic losses and reduced axial elongation than children who did not wear the lenses.171 Non-myopic children using Multizone-Defocus-Plano (MDP) spectacles during near work experienced limited hyperopic losses and axial elongation than children who did not wear the lenses.188

Beyond peripheral defocus, another form of myopia control intervention alters perceived contrast through Diffusion Optics Technology (DOT) spectacles. While untested directly in pre-myopic children specifically, DOT spectacle lenses could induce short-term choroidal thickening in the central and parafoveal retinal regions in emmetropic children even for wear times as short as 30 minutes.189 This is critical, as efficacy of the aforementioned lenses (DIMS, DSDO, HAL, HORI, OTOGE) requires a minimum wear time of 10 hours/day, with greater efficacy correlated with wear time.161,167–169,171 Ensuring compliance with spectacles may be challenging, especially for an asymptomatic child who may not notice a tangible benefit through the spectacles.

More randomized clinical trials for the use of myopia control spectacles in pre-myopic children have been registered and are presently underway. One such investigation involves the use of DIMS spectacle lenses in pre-myopic children of preschool age,190 and another uses “direct emmetropia with focus out glasses” (DEFOG; known to incorporate peripheral myopic defocus using multiple segments of a positive power) in non-myopic children between 6–8 years old.191

Low‑Level Red Light

Repeated low-level red light (RLRL) has been used for myopia control and also appears effective for pre-myopic children. The treatment consists of viewing a bright red light (650 nm) for 3 minutes, twice daily. Studies using RLRL in pre-myopic children have been able to reduce myopia incidence by 16.1–20.4% within one year.173,174,177 In alignment with these estimates, a meta-analysis has found that RLRL exposure reduces myopia incidence by 17%, and limits loss of hyperopic reserve by 0.36D and axial elongation by 0.18mm over 1 year.192 The protective effect of RLRL is thought to be by reducing choroidal thinning with increases to both superficial and deep retinal vascular density.175,176,192–194 The average choroidal thickness, especially the subfoveal area, was noticeably thicker by 21.36 ± 21.56 μm after 3 months of RLRL treatment.195 This effect of choroidal thickening remained with maintenance of repeated sessions (3 minutes twice daily, 5 days a week), but thinned back to baseline levels within 3 months of discontinuation of therapy.194 Despite these promising results, there are concerns about the long-term safety when using RLRL. Prolonged exposure to the light intensity used in RLRL treatment exceeds recommended safety limits.196 More recently, regulatory bodies in China have introduced new certification requirements requiring extensive testing in animals prior to human clinical trials. At present, RLRL is approved only for myopia control in children aged 8 and older and is not authorized for pre-myopic children.

Comparing Intervention Efficacy

A recent network meta-analysis that included 19 randomized controlled clinical trials examined the effects of different interventions (0.01% atropine, red light therapy, increased outdoor time of between 3–10 hours per week, physical activity and myopia awareness programs) on myopia prevention, focusing on outcomes such as incidence, axial length, and changes in refractive error.197 Studies incorporating myopia control spectacles were not considered in the analysis, perhaps due to timing of publication. Only children who were non-myopic (cycloplegic refractive error ≤ −0.50DS) were included. Low-dose atropine was the most effective in reducing myopia incidence whereas low-level red light was most effective in reducing myopic shifts in refractive error and axial elongation. For reducing myopia incidence, low-dose atropine had the greatest impact (45% relative risk reduction relative to placebo), followed by low-level red light (41% vs placebo), increased outdoor time (18% vs placebo) and myopia awareness programs (12% vs placebo). In terms of refractive error, low-level red light preserved 0.48D (95% CI [0.38; 0.59]) more hyperopia compared to placebo versus low-dose atropine which retained 0.33D [0.23; 0.43] more hyperopia than placebo. Outdoor time only retained 0.11D [0.03; 0.19] more than placebo whereas physical exercise and myopia awareness programs may have no significant effect. Relative to no intervention, axial elongation rates were slowed by 0.23 mm/year [0.19; 0.27] using low-level red light and by 0.12 mm/year [0.08; 0.16] with low-dose atropine. It appears outdoor time and myopia awareness programs may have no additional impact on axial elongation compared to placebo as per confidence intervals. Overall, it appears that low-dose atropine and low-level red-light therapy are the most effective interventions for myopia prevention, followed by moderate impact of outdoor time and myopia awareness programs. It is currently difficult to estimate the impact of myopia control spectacles as published clinical trials either have no placebo group167 or use single‑vision spectacles as the comparison group.168

Evidence remains mixed as to whether combining myopia control spectacles with atropine confers any additional benefit. While one study found no further benefit as compared to myopia control spectacles alone,166 another study found that combination therapy had superior efficacy.161

Discussion

Effective pre-myopia management requires moving beyond static refractive thresholds toward a risk-stratified approach. Pre-myopic children with a similar baseline refractive error may have different risks of developing myopia depending on demographic characteristics, clinical attributes, and environmental exposures. The focus should be on maintaining a sufficient level of hyperopic reserve, varying by age and ethnicity. Clinicians should consider demographic factors (such as parental myopia and female gender) and behavioural factors (such as insufficient outdoor time and increased near work) that may deplete the hyperopic reserve. Axial length should be monitored and referenced against gender and ethnic-specific growth charts to detect excessive growth rates. With the advent of improved predictive models that can categorize children into low, moderate, or high risk of developing myopia, clinicians can better identify at-risk children who may benefit from earlier intervention.

The critical window for myopia prevention may be during the period preceding the decline in hyperopic reserve. In the normal course of emmetropization, axial elongation is counterbalanced by crystalline lens power loss in a synchronized process.31,198 Children who remain emmetropic experience slower rates of axial elongation (<0.2mm/year). However, in children who develop myopia, axial elongation accelerates, resulting in progression towards a pre-myopic and subsequently myopic state.31 Thus, a loss of concordance between lens power and axial length may be involved in the development of myopia.31 Acceleration of axial elongation appears to occur before children enter the conventional pre-myopia refractive error range, suggesting that clinicians should try to keep a hyperopic reserve of at least +1.00D.14 Indeed, in large multinational population‑level studies (including China, India, Nepal, South Africa, Chile and Malaysia), populations were either predominantly mildly hyperopic (between +0.50D to +2.00D) or substantially myopic.83,199,200 A large cohort study in China found that the refractive error tends to converge around +1.00D for young non-myopic children.198 Specifically, children with more than +1.00D of hyperopia showed modest myopic shifts in their refractive development, whereas children with less than +1.00D of hyperopia experienced slight hyperopic shifts, resulting in final refractive error converging towards +1.00D. Under normal emmetropization, it appears the feedback loop between lens power and axial length may remain intact, converging towards this small amount of hyperopia.

If the goal is to maintain refractive status in the mildly hyperopic range and prevent children from entering the pre-myopic range, timely behavioural and prophylactic interventions may be considered. Personalized prevention strategies can be implemented based on individual risk. Lifestyle changes, such as increasing outdoor time to 40–80 minutes daily and limiting near work to 1–2 hours per day with a minimum working distance of 30 cm may be sufficient to stave off myopia onset. Outdoor time should be considered for hyperopic children prior to reaching the pre-myopia range. The use of 0.01% atropine may also confer some benefit with a limited side effect profile. For children who have entered the pre-myopic range or children who are experiencing rapid depletion of hyperopic reserve, 0.05% atropine or myopia control spectacles may be more effective to slow progression towards myopia onset. While repeated low-level red light has demonstrated efficacy, it should not be considered with caution until its safety profile is better understood.

Challenges and Future Directions

Clinicians may be hesitant to employ pharmacologic or optical interventions without strong evidence, similar to historical hesitancies surrounding myopia control. While active interventions such as low-dose atropine or myopia control spectacles have demonstrated immediate-term efficacy, the long-term benefit remains to be determined. Presently, there is insufficient longitudinal data linking baseline patient characteristics with subsequent refractive outcomes to evaluate overall efficacy, both for behavioural and prophylactic interventions. It remains unclear whether early intervention during a child’s pre-myopic years can reduce the overall severity of myopia or merely postpones progression to later years. More data is needed to determine the critical windows when interventions may be most impactful, which may also vary across interventions depending on their mechanism of action. There is presently no consensus on when to initiate or taper pharmacological or optical treatment of pre-myopic patients, and more research may be needed to guide this level of intervention. Although trends suggest near work and screen time impact myopia development, it is unclear whether interventions to reduce near work and/or screen time directly alter the trajectory of myopia development, in terms of incidence or depletion of hyperopic reserve. While randomized clinical trials are studying various interventions in pre-myopic children directly, comparisons between interventions remain limited. New randomized clinical trials need to be designed with an appropriate control condition to allow for inclusion in meta-analyses. A better understanding of underlying mechanisms for each intervention would help determine whether combining interventions (eg outdoor time with atropine or optical interventions) may have an additive effect if greater efficacy is required. Overall, data on pre-myopia prevalence and interventions remain limited for non-East Asian populations, more data across a range of ethnicities are needed improve risk prediction and guide management for pre-myopic children. A broader understanding of risk across ethnicities is needed to reduce prediction uncertainty and improve management outcomes on an individual patient basis, taking into consideration the cost-effectiveness of early intervention programs.

Conclusion

Pre-myopia management should involve an early individualized risk assessment, focus on preserving the hyperopic reserve and monitor for axial elongation. The use of predictive models can guide discussions with pre-myopic children and their caregivers to determine whether children may benefit from timely intervention. Clinical management should be guided by a cost-benefit analysis to determine what level of intervention may be sufficient. Proactive management of pre-myopia can have a greater impact than implementing myopia control treatment after onset. Timely intervention may meaningfully reduce the burden of myopia on both individuals and society.
