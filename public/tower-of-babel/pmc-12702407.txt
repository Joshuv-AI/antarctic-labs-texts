Cavitation-enhanced aroma and browning: A multiscale study of star anise (Illicium verum Hook. f.) pretreated by ultrasound and electrolyzed water

Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Spices and aromatic crops constitute a major category of high-value agricultural commodities, whose market competitiveness is largely dictated by their sensory quality, bioactive composition, and process stability[1]. Illicium verum Hook. f. (star anise) is an economically and culturally important spice widely distributed in subtropical regions of Asia and the Americas, where it serves as a key ingredient in culinary, pharmaceutical and functional food applications owing to its distinctive aroma and high levels of phenolics, flavonoids, and essential oils. Its global demand continues to rise, yet the postharvest processing of star anise remains challenging. [2,3]. Conventional processing often leads to heterogeneous color formation, prolonged dehydration time, and substantial aroma loss, which together undermine product quality and restrict industrial upgrading [4].

Pretreatment prior to drying has therefore become a key step in maintaining the physicochemical and sensory integrity of star anise. Growing evidence has demonstrated that pre-drying operations such as blanching or thawing can significantly influence tissue structure, enzymatic activity, and mass transfer behavior, thereby determining the final color and aroma quality of dried products [5,6]. Hot-water blanching (HWB) has been widely applied to soften plant tissues, inactivate enzymes, and reduce microbial loads [7]. However, for enzyme-dependent color-forming materials such as star anise, HWB presents inherent limitations. High-temperature exposure often destroys heat-sensitive volatiles and phenolics[8,9], while complete enzyme inactivation interrupts the oxidative reactions essential for its desirable reddish coloration. Consequently, the development of non-thermal pretreatments capable of fine-tuning enzymatic activity while preserving volatile compounds has attracted increasing attention.

Among emerging non-thermal technologies, ultrasound (US) and electrolyzed water (EW) have individually shown potential in modifying food matrices and improving product quality. US generates cavitation bubbles that induce localized microstreaming and microfractures, which can disrupt cell walls, enhance solvent penetration, and accelerate mass transfer even at mild temperatures [10,11,12]. EW, in contrast, is a mildly alkaline, redox-active aqueous solution capable of altering cell membrane permeability and microbial viability through electrochemical oxidation–reduction processes [13]. Previous studies have demonstrated its potential to improve surface decontamination and quality retention in fruits and vegetables[13,14,15]. Despite these advances, existing research has predominantly focused on either US or EW applied individually, and few attempts have been made to explore their combined effects, particularly within spice or aromatic crop processing.

Notably, star anise is commonly stored in frozen form to extend postharvest shelf life, making thawing an essential preparatory step prior to drying. This provides an opportunity to incorporate mild physical and electrochemical treatments into the thawing stage. The integration of US and EW-termed ultrasound-assisted electrolyzed water thawing (UEWT)-is hypothesized to achieve synergistic effects by coupling cavitation-induced mass transfer enhancement with electrochemical modulation of enzymatic and structural responses. However, the combined impact of these two technologies on the quality evolution of star anise, or similar spice matrices, has not been systematically investigated. Moreover, how aqueous-based pretreatments mediate the interplay among microstructural integrity, enzymatic oxidation, antioxidant retention, and volatile composition during subsequent drying remains largely unexplored [16,17].

To address these gaps, the present study systematically compared the effects of five pretreatments-deionized water thawing (DWT), electrolyzed water thawing (EWT), ultrasound-assisted deionized water thawing (UDWT), ultrasound-assisted electrolyzed water thawing (UEWT), and hot-water blanching (HWB)–on the drying kinetics, color attributes, microstructure, enzyme activities, antioxidant capacity, and volatile profiles of star anise. By contrasting thermal and non-thermal aqueous pretreatments, this work elucidates the underlying mechanisms by which thawing-induced biochemical and structural changes govern the color-flavor quality formation of star anise. The findings aim to provide a scientific basis for developing efficient, non-thermal pretreatment strategies for high-value spice processing.

Materials and methods

To clarify how different pretreatments influence the drying kinetics and quality formation of Illicium verum, five representative methods-DWT, EWT, UDWT, UEWT, and HWB-were applied prior to drying. Subsequent analyses focused on water migration, color development, enzymatic activity, antioxidant retention, microstructure, and volatile compounds to establish the relationships between processing conditions and quality attributes.

Materials and chemicals

Fresh star anise (Illicium verum Hook. f.) was obtained from Yunnan Zhongmu Agriculture and Forestry Development Co., Ltd. (Funing County, Wenshan, Yunnan Province, China) in April 2025. The selected green star anise was of uniform size and was stored at 4 °C for no more than 30 days prior to the experiment.

Folin reagent, crosslinked polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVPP), Triton X-100, catechol, p-phenylenediamine, hydrogen peroxide and toluidine blue staining solution were all purchased from SOLA Biosciences (Natick, MA, USA). Anthrone and acetonitrile were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical (Shanghai, China). Sodium dihydrogen phosphate was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent (Shanghai, China), while sodium chloride was purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology (Shanghai, China). n-alkanes (C3–C25) were obtained from O2si (Charleston, SC, USA).

Pretreatment methods

Freezing

Star anise was subject to freezing at −20 °C in a forced-air freezing system (Jingchuang Electric, ECB-5060 electric control box, Jiangsu, China) until its core temperature also reached around −20 °C. Noted, however, that star anise sample used for HWB treatment experiment was fresh and had not undergone any prior freezing.

Thawing

Frozen star anise was thawed using four different methods: immersion thawing with deionized water (DWT), immersion thawing with alkaline electrolyzed water (EWT), ultrasound-assisted thawing with deionized water (UDWT) and ultrasound-assisted thawing with alkaline electrolyzed water (UEWT). All thawing treatments were conducted at room temperature (23 ± 1 °C). The thawing process was considered complete when the core temperature of a sample reached or exceeded 4 °C, as monitored using a thermocouple-temperature recorder (Teans Electronic Industrial, TA612C, Suzhou, China). Only one sample was thawed at a time; each treatment was performed in triplicate. During the thawing process, each sample was fully submerged in the corresponding liquid medium and was not wrapped in any film.

Alkaline electrolyzed water (AEW) was generated using a membrane-free electrolysis unit (Shui Xiong Technology, SX-G30, Zhejiang, China) using a mixed solution of sodium chloride and tap water. Electrolysis was conducted at a constant current of 8.0 A for 20 min. The pH, oxidation–reduction potential (ORP) and available chlorine concentration (ACC) of AEW were measured using a pH/ORP meter (Shanghai Sanxin Instrument, PHB-1, Shanghai, China) and a colorimetric chlorine analyzer (Hanna Instruments, HI97771, Sălaj, Romania). The resulting AEW had a pH of 10.29 ± 0.02, an ORP of 754.21 ± 3.45 mV and an ACC of 32.45 ± 0.67 mg/L.

For ultrasound-assisted thawing (UEWT and UDWT), each sample was placed in an ultrasonic cell disruptor (Xiaomei Ultrasonics, XM-1200 T ultrasonic cell disruptor, Kunshan, China), which contained a 500-mL beaker filled with either AEW or deionized water. The ultrasonic cell disruptor was operated at 25 kHz with an output power of 240 W. Each sample was completely submerged and subject to continuous ultrasonic treatment until its internal temperature reached 4 °C (Fig. S1).

Drying

To ensure uniform dehydration and enable comparison among different pretreatments, all samples were dried under identical heat-pump conditions. Heat pump drying was employed in the present study due to its reported ability to well preserve the quality of plant-derived materials [18]. Based on the methods described by Shi et al. [19] with slight modification, star anise samples were dried at 55 °C and 30 % relative humidity until the final moisture content was below 17.5 % on a dry basis (d.b.).

Water state distribution determination

To characterize moisture migration and redistribution behavior during drying, the distribution of water states in star anise was determined using low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR). The Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill (CPMG) multi-echo sequence was employed with the following parameters: a resonance frequency of 23 MHz and an offset frequency of 400,694.42 Hz. The duration of the 90° excitation pulse was 7.4 μs, while the 180° refocusing pulse lasted 12.24 μs. The acquisition consisted of 1,440,042 sampling points, with an echo time (TE) of 0.8 ms and a total of 3600 echoes. Each scan was repeated 8 times, with a recycle delay of 10,000 ms. The signal amplification was set to an analog gain of 20.0 dB and a digital gain of 3 dB.

After blanching and drying, each sample was weighed using a precision electronic balance and loaded into a cylindrical tube (50 mm in diameter). Measurements were performed using a low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) spectrometer (Niumag, NMI20-040, Shanghai, China). Transverse relaxation (T2) data were collected and analyzed using inverse Laplace transform algorithms to generate relaxation spectra[20].

Enzyme activities determination

To elucidate the enzymatic mechanism associated with color formation and oxidative reactions, the activities of polyphenol oxidase (PPO) and peroxidase (POD) were determined. The activities of polyphenol oxidase (PPO) and peroxidase (POD) were measured using a modified protocol based on the methods reported by Yi et al. [22]. Briefly, 2 g of star anise was extracted with 10 mL of 0.2 M phosphate buffer (pH 6.5) containing 4 % (w/v) PVPP, 1 % (v/v) Triton X-100 and 1 M sodium chloride. The mixture was thoroughly homogenized and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was used as the crude enzyme extract.

For PPO activity determination, 0.5 mL of the enzyme extract was mixed with 2.5 mL of 0.05 M phosphate buffer (pH 6.5) containing 0.07 M catechol. Change in the absorbance at 420 nm was continuously monitored for 1 min using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Persee, T9, Beijing, China). One unit of PPO activity was noted as an increase in the absorbance of 0.001 per min per mL of the extract.

POD activity was measured by mixing 0.2 mL of the enzyme extract with 0.2 mL of phosphate buffer containing 1 % (w/v) p-phenylenediamine, 0.2 mL of 1.5 % (v/v) hydrogen peroxide and 1.5 mL of 0.05 M phosphate buffer (pH 6.5). Absorbance at 485 nm was recorded over 1 min. One unit of POD activity was again noted as an increase in the absorbance of 0.001 per min per mL of the extract.

Total phenolics content determination

To assess the retention of antioxidant compounds after various pretreatments, total phenolic content (TPC) was measured. A total of 8.0 g of fresh star anise or 2.0 g of its dried form was initially subject to extraction at 25 °C using a constant-temperature shaker set to 180 rpm for 16 h, and 80 % (v/v) methanol was used as the extraction solvent. The extract was then diluted 100-fold with 80 % (v/v) methanol. To enhance the extraction efficiency, ultrasonic treatment was subsequently applied at ambient temperature using a power density of 20 W/L and a frequency of 40 kHz for 20 min. Afterwards, the mixture was centrifuged at 8,000 × g for 5 min at 25 °C; supernatant was collected for further analysis.

The total phenolics content (TPC) was determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu method, following a modified protocol of Rizvi et al. [23]. Specifically, 50 µL of the diluted extract was mixed with 500 µL of pre-diluted Folin reagent (1:10, v/v) and incubated for 5 min. Then, 450 µL of 7.5 % (w/v) sodium carbonate solution was added; the reaction was allowed to proceed in the dark for 1 h. Absorbance was measured at 765 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer (Agilent, EPOCH2NS-SN, Santa Clara, CA, USA). TPC is expressed as mg of gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per g dry mass for both fresh and dried samples.

Total flavonoids content determination

To complement TPC analysis and further evaluate antioxidant retention, total flavonoid content (TFC) was quantified. The extract used for the analysis was obtained following the procedures outlined in Section 2.7 (Matic et al., 2017). The total flavonoids content (TFC) was quantified by a colorimetric assay based on a sequential reagent addition method. Specifically, 15 µL of 5 % (w/v) sodium nitrite was first added and allowed to react for 5 min. This was followed by the addition of 15 µL of 5 % (w/v) aluminum chloride with a 6-min incubation. Finally, 100 µL of 1 M sodium hydroxide was introduced; the mixture was kept in the dark for 30 min. The absorbance was then recorded at 510 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer (Agilent, EPOCH2NS-SN, Santa Clara, CA, USA). TFC is expressed as mg of rutin equivalents per g dry mass for both fresh and dried samples.

Microstructure determination

Optical microscopy

A star anise sample was cut into uniform cubes with the dimensions of 10 mm × 10 mm × 10 mm. From these, thin tissue sections, approximately 1 mm in thickness, were obtained and immersed in a 0.05 % (w/v) toluidine blue solution for 15 min for histological staining. Following the staining process, the sections were examined under an optical microscope (Leica, DM500 RH, Wetzlar, Germany) at 100 × magnification. Optical microscopy was aimed at visualizing cellular structural alterations resulting from the various pretreatment methods applied to the star anise tissue.

Field emission scanning electron microscopy

To further investigate the microstructural characteristics of star anise, dried lobes were carefully halved, ensuring that each fragment was smaller than 1 cm. Sample preparation was conducted according to a modified protocol of Wang et al. [24]. The tissue fragments were fixed onto aluminum stubs, coated with a thin layer of gold via sputtering and subsequently examined using a field emission scanning electron microscope (Zeiss, Sigma 300, Oberkochen, Germany) equipped with an Oxford Xplore 30 energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer. Observations were carried out at an accelerating voltage of 1.5 kV, with images captured at 500 × magnification.

Volatile compounds determination

Electronic nose analysis

To analyze an aroma profile of star anise, 5.0 g of a powdered sample was transferred into a 20-mL sealed headspace vial and equilibrated at ambient temperature. A metal needle from an electronic nose system (Bosin, C-Nose, Shanghai, China) was inserted into the vial headspace without contacting the powder or inner vial walls. Between measurements, the instrument was flushed with clean air to prevent cross-sample contamination. Each sample was analyzed in four replicates. The key operational parameters were: detection time of 100 s, gas flow rate of 1.0 L/min and purge interval prior to each run of 120 s (Zhu et al., 2020).

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry

1.0 G of powdered star anise was accurately weighed into a headspace vial and spiked with 100 μL of a 4 mg/mL octanol solution, which served as the internal standard. The sample was equilibrated at 50 °C for 20 min, after which a white polyacrylate (PA) solid-phase microextraction (SPME) fiber (85 μm) was exposed to the headspace and used to adsorb volatile compounds for 40 min at the same temperature. The fiber was then thermally desorbed in the GC injector at 250 °C

Chromatographic separation was carried out using the following temperature program. The oven was initially held at 50 °C for 3 min, then ramped to 220 °C at a rate of 4 °C/min and maintained at 220 °C for an additional 10 min[25]. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min.

Mass spectrometric detection was conducted using an Agilent 7000E system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) under electron impact (EI) ionization at 70 eV, with the ion source temperature set to 200 °C. The scanned mass range covered m/z ratio of 35–500. Retention indices (RI) were computed based on C5–C25 n-alkane series analyzed under identical GC conditions. Identification of volatile constituents was accomplished by comparing the calculated RI values and mass spectral data with entries in the NIST database [26].

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were subject to the analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple range test at a confidence level of 95 %. The data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Origin 2019 software (Origin Lab Inc., Northampton, MA, USA) was used to plot the data. The GC–MS data and physicochemical indices of star anise were subject to principal component analysis (PCA).

Results

Color changes

Fig. 1 illustrates the appearances of dried star anise samples. In terms of the a* value, UEWT sample exhibited the highest value at 27.3; this was followed by UDWT sample at 22.8. These values were significantly higher (p < 0.05) than those observed for the EWT (19.1) and DWT (17.9) samples, indicating that ultrasound-assisted treatments markedly enhanced red color development. HWB sample possessed the lowest a* value at 15.5, reflecting the weakest red coloration.

Appearances and color differences of star anise undergone different thawing methods. UEWT: ultrasound combined with electrolytic water thawing; UDWT: ultrasound combined with deionized water thawing; EWT: electrolyzed water thawing; DWT: deionized water thawing; HWB: hot-water blanching (HWB).

In terms of the L* value, HWB sample exhibited the highest lightness value at 65.7. EWT and DWT samples exhibited moderate lightness levels, at 58.2 and 60.3, respectively. In contrast, UEWT (53.4) and UDWT (55.1) samples showed significantly lower (p < 0.05) L* values compared to HWB, suggesting a more deeply pigmented surface.

In the case of the b* value, DWT (33.7) and EWT (31.9) samples retained the highest values, significantly higher than those in the cases of UEWT (28.8) and UDWT (29.5) samples. HWB sample exhibited the lowest b* value at 25.4, showing a reduction in the yellowness, possibly due to pigment degradation or enzyme inactivation.

The total color difference (ΔE*) was most pronounced in case of UEWT sample, reaching 26.8; this was followed by that of UDWT sample at 24.3, indicating that ultrasound-assisted treatments led to the most significant visual deviation from the untreated reference. Moderate ΔE* values were observed in EWT (14.7) and DWT (11.9) sample, while HWB sample ezhibited the smallest color change, with a ΔE* value of 7.6.

Enzyme activities, TPC and TFC contents

The activities of PPO and POD in star anise under different thawing and blanching treatments are shown in Fig. 2-A and Fig. 2-B, respectively. PPO activity exhibited significant differences among treatments (p < 0.05). UEWT resulted in the highest PPO activity at 273 U/mL/min. This was followed by UDWT, which resulted in a moderate PPO activity level of around 150 U/mL/min. Both EWT and DWT resulted in relatively low PPO activities, with no significant difference among them (p > 0.05), maintaining levels around 88 U/mL/min and 76 U/mL/min, respectively. In contrast, HWB resulted in the lowest PPO activity, nearly inactivated, with values close to 3 U/mL/min.

UEWT: ultrasound combined with electrolytic water thawing; UDWT: ultrasound combined with deionized water thawing; EWT: electrolyzed water thawing; DWT: deionized water thawing; HWB: hot-water blanching (HWB).

For POD activity, both UEWT and UDWT resulted in significantly elevated activity levels of 1945 U/mL/min and 1883 U/mL/min, which were significantly higher than those of EWT and DWT at 1299 U/mL/min and 1419 U/mL/min. HWB again resulted in the lowest activity (54 U/mL/min), indicating near-complete inactivation of POD. Statistical analysis indicated that both ultrasound-assisted treatments (UDWT and UEWT) significantly resulted in higher PPO and POD activities compared to the non-ultrasound-assisted treatments. HWB showed consistently extensive inactivation of both enzymes.

As shown in Fig. 2-C and 2-D, TPC of star anise varied significantly, depending on the treatment method after freezing. In Fig. 2-C, it is seen that TPC was significantly lower (p < 0.05) for the sample subjected to freezing and then UEWT, with the value of 1.13 mg GAE/g. TPC of the sample treated by EWT was 4.02 mg GAE/g, while DWT resulted in TPC of 4.38 mg GAE/g. Star anise treated with UDWT possessed TPC of 4.18 mg GAE/g, while HWB resulted in TPC of 4.52 mg GAE/g.

Fig. 2-D illustrates TPC of star anise after drying following different treatment methods. TPC of star anise undergone EWT was 141.46 mg GAE/g, while that of the sample undergone DWT was higher at 165.73 mg GAE/g. When combined with ultrasound-assisted thawing, TPC decreased to 45.94 mg GAE/g in the case of using electrolysis water and 44.04 mg GAE/g in the case of using deionized water. The lowest TPC was observed in the case of HWB at 31.26 mg GAE/g. Overall, star anise dried after thawing with electrolyzed water or deionized water showed higher TPC than that treated with ultrasound-assisted thawing. HWB sample exhibited the lowest TPC.

In Fig. 2-E, TFC of star anise after various treatments is presented. After freezing and thawing with electrolyzed water in combination with ultrasound, TFC was significantly lower, at 2.35 mg GAE/g (p < 0.05), similar to the results observed for TPC. TFC of star anise treated with EWT was 4.17 mg GAE/g, while that of the sample treated with DWT was 3.79 mg GAE/g. Star anise treated with UDWT exhibited TFC of around 6.6 mg GAE/g, while that of the sample treated with HWB was 13.13 mg GAE/g, the highest among all the treatments.

Fig. 2-F presents TFC of star anise after freezing and thawing with different treatments, followed by drying. TFC of EWT sample was 250.60 mg GAE/g. TFC of DWT sample was 249.08 mg GAE/g. TFC of UEWT sample was 51.82 mg GAE/g, while that of UDWT sample was 59.02 mg GAE/g. TFC of star anise treated with HWB was 92.87 mg GAE/g.

Microstructure

Fig. 3-A shows microscopic images of star anise subjected to different pretreatment methods. The images reveal notable variations in the cell structures of the different samples. Both EWT and DWT caused visible disruptions in the cell structures, with EWT showing slightly less deformation, likely due to changes in the ionic composition of the surrounding medium. In contrast, DWT resulted in more significant cell wall ruptures, likely due to physical stresses imposed by ice crystal damage during the freezing and thawing processes. When ultrasound was applied, both UEWT and UDWT caused more pronounced structural damages. The cells treated with UEWT showed severe deformation, suggesting that acoustic cavitation in combination with electrolysis induced stronger mechanical disruption. UDWT also resulted in noticeable deformation, but the damage was less severe. HWB resulted in a relatively less disruption of the cell structure, with some deformation due to thermal softening; overall, however, the cell structure appeared more intact compared to the samples undergone the freeze–thaw treatments.

(A) Microstructural changes of star anise undergone different pretreatments, (B) field emission scanning electron microscopy images of dried star anise undergone different pretreatments.

Field emission scanning electron microscopy images of dried star anise samples subjected to various pretreatment methods are shown in Fig. 3-B. DWT sample exhibited relatively intact, but irregular cell walls, with uneven pore distribution; some cellular collapse was evident. In contrast, EWT sample exhibited more defined and smoother pore boundaries, indicating a milder impact of the treatment method on the cell structure. HWB sample displayed significant structural disruption, with numerous shrunken and deformed pores as well as severely compacted tissue. UDWT sample had large, polygonal pores with clear and expanded structures, suggesting that acoustic cavitation promoted uniform expansion and partial breakdown of the cellular matrix. UEWT sample exhibited a highly porous microstructure with well-preserved pore integrity and uniform size distribution, along with relatively thin and distinct cell walls.

Drying characteristics

Fig. 4-A shows the drying curves of the star anise samples undergone different pretreatment methods. UEWT sample most rapidly reached the target moisture level (∼8h); this was followed by UDWT (∼11 h) and EWT and DWT samples (12–12.5 h). HWB sample exhibited the lowest drying rate, requiring nearly 17 h. Fig. 4-B shows the drying rate curves of the differently treated samples. UEWT sample exhibited the highest peak drying rate (∼0.86 kg/kg·h); this was followed by HWB sample (∼0.39 kg/kg·h) and UDWT sample (∼0.48 kg/kg·h). EWT and DWT samples exhibited the drying rate of ∼ 0.39–0.45 kg/kg·h.

(A) Drying curves, (B) drying rate curves, (C) moisture distribution characterized by NMR signal intensity vs. transverse relaxation time (T2) curves and (D) peak area percentage of star anise undergone different thawing methods. UEWT: ultrasound combined with electrolytic water thawing; UDWT: ultrasound combined with deionized water thawing; EWT: electrolyzed water thawing; DWT: deionized water thawing; HWB: hot-water blanching (HWB).

T2 relaxation spectra of star anise subjected to different pretreatments are shown in Fig. 4-C. Four distinct peaks were observed for all the samples, corresponding to the different physical states of water and oil. Based on their relaxation times and prior information on aromatic plant tissues, the peaks could be assigned as follows: T21 (0.1–1 ms, bound water), T22 (1–10 ms, immobilized water), T23 (10–100 ms, capillary/free water) and T24 (>1000 ms, oil-associated protons). Quantitative analysis of the peak area proportions is then presented in Fig. 4-D. UEWT sample exhibited the highest proportion of free water (T23 of 48 %) and oil components (T24 of 23 %), while bound water (T21 of 11 %) and immobilized water (T22 of 3 %) were minimal. UDWT sample exhibited a similar pattern, with T23 of 41 % and T24 of 16 %, along with T21 (34 %) and T22 (9 %). EWT and DWT samples displayed more balanced distributions. In the case of EWT, T21 was 33 %, T22 was 29 %, T23 was 23 % and T24 was 15 %. DWT sample possessed the highest bound water proportion (T21 of 35 %) and similar oil content (T24 of 15 %), with T22 and T23 of 32 % and 19 %, respectively. HWB sample differed markedly from the others, with the highest proportion of bound water (T21 of 35 %) and the lowest oil-associated signal (T24 of 9 %). Its capillary water (T23 of 36 %) remained moderate, while immobilized water content (T22 of 20 %) was slightly higher.

Additionally, peak positions in the spectra exhibited noticeable shifts. For UEWT and UDWT samples, T23 and T24 slightly shifted to the longer relaxation times, indicating increased mobility of water. In contrast, HWB sample exhibited left-shifted T22 and T23, consistent with its tighter binding and restricted mobility of water.

Volatile compounds

Total ion chromatograms of volatile compounds of star anise undergone different treatments are shown in Fig. 5. A total of 49 volatile compounds were identified, including 1 acid, 5 esters, 7 alcohols, 1 aldehyde, 3 ethers, 8 monoterpenes, 19 sesquiterpenes and 5 non-standard hydrocarbon skeletons. The number of volatile compounds identified in each sample group was as follows: HWB (36), EWT (38), DWT (32), UDWT (38) and UEWT (45). This indicates a clear advantage in terms of aroma retention and complexity of UEWT.

The radar chart (Fig. 6-A) illustrates the E-nose response profiles of the dried star anise samples undergone different pretreatments. The sample undergone UEWT exhibited the most prominent flavor fingerprint, particularly with high response intensities recorded by sensors 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 9, 14, 15, 16 and 17. In contrast, sensors 3, 7, 10 and 13 showed negligible response signals for all the samples, indicating limited detection of certain volatiles. Among the detected volatiles, terpenes and esters were the dominant contributors to the characteristic woody, herbal and spicy aroma of star anise, as detailed in Table S1. Fourteen key aroma compounds with distinct flavor-contributing potential were selected for quantitative comparison, as shown in Fig. 6-C. These compounds were classified into six structural categories: esters (benzoic acid, methyl ester; 2-propanone, 1-(4-methoxyphenyl)-), alcohols (linalool, terpinen-4-ol, α-terpineol), ethers (estragole, trans-anethole), monoterpenes (β-myrcene), sesquiterpenes (caryophyllene, cis-β-farnesene, α-farnesene, β-bisabolene, caryophyllene oxide) and non-standard hydrocarbons ((1R)-2,6,6-trimethylbicyclo[3.1.1]hept-2-ene).

Among the above-mentioned volatiles, trans-anethole, the most representative aromatic compound in star anise, exhibited the highest odor activity value (OAV). Its content reached 1100.40 μg/kg in the UEWT sample; this was followed by HWB (869.81 μg/kg), UDWT (505.98 μg/kg), DWT (394.60 μg/kg) and EWT (358.87 μg/kg) samples. Of nearly all 14 key aroma compounds, UEWT sample showed significantly higher concentrations (p < 0.05) than other samples. Notably, α-terpineol was detected exclusively in the UEWT sample. On the other hand, benzoic acid methyl ester was significantly elevated in EWT sample, while terpinen-4-ol was preserved only in UEWT and HWB samples. Overall, the concentration trends of the 13 other key volatiles followed the order: UEWT > UDWT > EWT > DWT > HWB.

Discussion

Influences of pretreatments on color characteristics

The color variation observed among different pretreatments reflects the distinct biochemical pathways triggered by physical and electrochemical stimuli during thawing. The color characteristics of star anise are strongly influenced by the enzyme activities and availability of phenolic and flavonoid substrates, both of which are affected by the applied pretreatment methods [27]. The present study demonstrates that the treatments involving ultrasound, particularly UEWT and UDWT, resulted in significantly higher redness (a*) value, suggesting the desirable formation of red-brown pigments via enzymatic oxidation pathways. Such enhanced coloration under ultrasound-assisted treatments may not only result from increased PPO/POD exposure but also from a more favorable micro-oxygenation environment that accelerates phenolic oxidation. The cavitation-induced microbubbles could intermittently introduce oxygen into the cellular matrix, thereby intensifying enzymatic polymerization reactions. Xin et al. [28] indeed reported that the use of ultrasound was conducive to the preservation of color of broccoli. Zhang et al. [29] also noted that ultrasound-treated Porcine longissimus muscles displayed a redder color over air frozen and immersion frozen samples. Our observed effect on color is closely associated with the higher PPO and POD activities as a result of the ultrasound-assisted treatments, in turn resulting in the more extensive browning during the subsequent drying process [30], which is desirable. Note that ultrasound is known to promote structural disruption through acoustic cavitation, facilitating the release of enzymes and their substrates from intracellular compartments[31]. Moreover, mechanical stimulation and localized microthermal effects induced by ultrasound might also have enhanced enzyme conformational activation[32]. In the case of UEWT, the presence of reactive species in the electrolyzed water might have further accelerated enzyme-substrate interactions.

Although UEWT and UDWT resulted in substantial reduction in TPC and TFC when compared with DWT and EWT[33], the former more significantly enhanced the color intensity of star anise. The accelerated enzymatic oxidation reactions rendered by UEWT and UDWT consumed phenolics and flavonoids to produce quinones and polymeric pigments responsible for the color transformation. In contrast, DWT and EWT resulted in milder physical effects that preserved the cellular structure, limiting both enzymes and substrates releases, in turn reducing the enzymatic browning, while retaining more antioxidant compounds. Interestingly, although electrolyzed water contains oxidative species, the absence of ultrasound resulted in minimal enzyme release and activation; the impact on color formation was therefore limited.

HWB effectively inactivated PPO and POD[34], thereby suppressing enzymatic browning reactions. As expected, HWB sample exhibited the lowest a* value and highest L* value, indicating minimal pigment formation and pale appearance [19]. Nevertheless, although HWB initially retained higher levels of phenolics and flavonoids by avoiding enzymatic oxidation, these compounds might have subsequently degraded or leached out during the drying process, leading to the lowest post-drying retention. Overall, the combined ultrasound and electrolyzed water treatment achieved a controlled balance between enzymatic activation and phenolic oxidation, resulting in a desirable reddish hue characteristic of premium-grade star anise.

Influences of pretreatments on drying characteristics

Drying efficiency and moisture migration behavior are closely linked to cell microstructure integrity and the physicochemical interactions established during pretreatment. The differences in drying and water migration behaviors of the differently treated star anise samples can be attributed to the extent and nature of cell structural disruption. As indicated by field emission scanning electron microscopy and histological images, UEWT sample exhibited the most porous and severely disrupted tissue microstructure, characterized by interconnected cavities, fractured cell walls and thin boundary membranes. The higher drying rate of UEWT samples can thus be attributed to the synergistic interplay of mechanical and chemical effects—ultrasound cavitation weakened the cell wall, while electrolyzed water altered membrane lipid oxidation, both reducing internal diffusion resistance. This can be explained by the synergistic mechanisms wherein freezing induced intracellular ice crystals that mechanically ruptured the cells, while subsequent ultrasound treatment generated cavitation forces that further expanded intercellular spaces and damaged membrane integrity [35]. The alkaline and oxidizing nature of electrolyzed water might also help destabilize membrane lipids and proteins, further weakening structural cohesion and hence increasing membrane permeability (Ryglova et al., 2023). These structural alterations enhanced moisture diffusivity during drying, as evidenced by the rapid moisture loss from the sample. Microstructural breakdown also contributed to the outward migration of polyphenols and flavonoids from vacuoles to the surface, where they became exposed to oxygen and PPO, promoting enzymatic browning and the more intense surface coloration [36].

The aforementioned structural changes were also reflected in the water distribution profiles derived from LF-NMR spectroscopy. UEWT sample exhibited the highest proportion of free water and a clear shift from bound (T21) and immobilized water (T22) to free water (T23), hinting that intracellular water was effectively released into newly formed capillary networks. A similar but less pronounced effect was observed in UDWT sample. While the absence of electrolyzed water limited the extent of membrane destabilization, ultrasound-induced cavitation still promoted moderate loosening of the tissue structure, resulting in visible pore expansion and partial membrane rupture [37].

In contrast, EWT and DWT samples retained intact and compact cellular architectures, with lower porosity and less extensive membrane rupture. Without mechanical stresses from freezing or cavitation, water remained confined within intracellular spaces, resulting in the lower drying rates and higher proportions of bound water, as reflected in their LF-NMR spectra. Moreover, the reduced migration of phenolic compounds likely limited PPO-mediated oxidation, resulting in the lower extent of surface coloration [38].

HWB sample, despite being treated in the fresh state, exhibited extensive tissue compaction due to heat-induced protein denaturation and membrane aggregation (Ye et al., 2021). This led to reduced pore continuity and restricted moisture diffusion. Consequently, HWB sample exhibited the lowest drying rate and free water signal, consistent with the observed inefficient mass transfer within the sample. Collectively, these findings confirm that structural disintegration and membrane permeability are the dominant determinants of moisture mobility, providing a mechanistic basis for pretreatment optimization in spice dehydration.

Influences of pretreatments on volatile compounds

Aroma preservation and transformation during drying are largely determined by the accessibility of volatile precursors and the stability of terpenoid compounds under oxidative and mechanical stress. The findings from the E-nose and volatile compounds analyses collectively demonstrate that thawing pretreatment significantly influenced the aroma profiles of dried star anise samples. Among all methods tested, UEWT was noted as the most effective in preserving and enhancing the aroma-related volatiles. The high E-nose sensor response intensities and clear separation in the PCA plot indicate more aroma complexity and intensity of UEWT sample, likely due to the synergistic effect of the oxidizing properties of electrolyzed water and ultrasound-induced microstructural disruption. Acoustic cavitation facilitated cell wall disruption, thereby promoting the release of bound volatile precursors and their extractability [39]. Additionally, the high oxidation–reduction potential of electrolyzed water might help alter the enzyme activities, particularly those of glycosidases and oxidoreductases, which are known to modulate the release or degradation of aroma precursors[40].

UEWT significantly enhanced the contents of terpenoids, especially those of sesquiterpenes and monoterpenes, which included trans-anethole, β-myrcene and caryophyllene. Trans-anethole, the principal compound responsible for the characteristic sweet and licorice-like aroma of star anise (Li et al., 2024), was significantly more abundant in UEWT sample. The synergistic microstructural disruption by ultrasound and mild oxidative-reductive environment provided by electrolyzed water likely maintained a redox balance favorable for terpenoid stability while enhancing precursor release. This compound is known to be thermally labile and susceptible to oxidation[41]. The combination of ultrasound and mild redox buffering provided by electrolyzed water likely reduced its degradation[42,43]. The exclusive presence of α-terpineol in UEWT sample further suggests that this pretreatment not only preserved but may have facilitated biosynthesis transformation pathways, such as those mediated by terpene synthases or post-harvest enzyme activities, which have been reported to be sensitive to both thermal and oxidative conditions[44].

Interestingly, while UDWT also resulted in enhanced aroma preservation, its performance was consistently inferior to UEWT, indicating that the chemical nature of the thawing medium, particularly the reactive species in the electrolyzed water, plays an important role beyond just the mechanical effect of ultrasound. In contrast, HWB, despite being applied to fresh (unfrozen) sample, resulted in considerable losses of key volatiles. Such observation supports previous studies that HWB can cause leaching and thermal degradation of aroma compounds in plant materials [45,46]. The high-temperature environment likely induced volatilization and thermal degradation of heat-sensitive compounds, including esters and monoterpenes. In summary, UEWT not only preserved key aroma-active molecules such as trans-anethole but also promoted secondary volatile transformations, revealing its potential as a targeted pretreatment strategy for aroma enhancement in spice processing.

Conclusion and future perspectives

The present study demonstrates that ultrasound-assisted thawing, particularly when combined with electrolyzed water (UEWT), could significantly enhance the drying efficiency, enzymatic browning intensity and aroma compounds retention due to its ability to induce extensive microstructural disruption, promote the release of water and enzyme substrates as well as volatile compounds migration. Microstructural images and LF-NMR spectra confirm that UEWT sample possessed the most porous cellular architecture and the highest proportions of free water and oil-associated protons. Such structural advantages translated into more rapid drying, more extensive coloration and richer volatile profiles, particularly with respect to such key terpenoid compounds as trans-anethole and α-terpineol. Corresponding to the benefits in terms of aroma enhancement and improved drying behavior, UEWT was noted to result in marked reduction in total phenolics and flavonoids contents, confirming the hypothesis of more substantial substrate consumption via enzymatic oxidation reactions. EWT and DWT, on the other hand, better maintained structural integrity, resulting in higher antioxidants retention but lower drying efficiency and aroma release. HWB, while effective in terms of enzyme inactivation, severely restricted mass transfer and caused significant losses of volatiles and antioxidants, leading to the lowest quality outcome. This study still has some limitations, as it was conducted at a laboratory scale under fixed processing parameters and focused only on star anise. In addition, the generation and maintenance of electrolyzed water may increase operational costs and complexity in large-scale applications. Overall, UEWT emerges as an effective pretreatment strategy to improve both the drying kinetics and aroma retention of star anise.

Future studies should explore process optimization and scale-up potential to balance the drying efficiency, which is related to energy consumption and greenhouse gas emissions, and quality development of star anise. Extending the proposed pretreatment strategy to other spices is also worth consideration. From an industrial perspective, the UEWT pretreatment offers potential for application in continuous or semi-continuous drying lines for spice and herbal materials. Its ability to accelerate moisture removal while preserving desirable aroma compounds could reduce energy consumption and drying time by approximately 40–50 %, thereby improving production efficiency and product uniformity. The technology could also be adapted for other aroma-sensitive commodities, contributing to the development of greener and more sustainable spice-processing systems.
