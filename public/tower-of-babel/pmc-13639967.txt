High-speed electric-scooter crash causing a tibial spine fracture with physeal extension in an adolescent: a case report
Authors: Abir Manni, Yacine Zouirech, Mounir Kisra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTibial spine fractures are pediatric anterior cruciate ligament–equivalent avulsion injuries that can be radiographically subtle, leading to delayed or missed diagnosis. We report the case of a healthy 14-year-old girl who sustained a high-speed electric-scooter crash, landing on both knees in flexion. Initial radiographs obtained at a community hospital were interpreted as normal despite persistent pain, swelling, and complete functional impairment of the right lower limb. On re-evaluation, comparative anteroposterior and lateral radiographs revealed subtle elevation of the right tibial intercondylar eminence, and computed tomography confirmed a tibial spine avulsion with a Meyers–McKeever type II pattern, extending into the proximal tibial physis and associated with a nondisplaced tibial plateau fracture. The patient was treated nonoperatively with gentle closed reduction under sedation followed by six weeks of immobilization in a long-leg plaster cast in slight flexion. Post-reduction imaging showed anatomic alignment of the tibial spine fragment and maintained plateau congruity. At six weeks, radiographs documented union without secondary displacement, and by eight weeks she had symmetric range of motion and a stable Lachman test, with no pain or instability. This case report highlights the need for repeat and cross-sectional imaging when clinical suspicion persists and illustrates that carefully selected type II tibial spine fractures with associated injuries may be successfully managed without surgery.Keywords: Tibial spine fracture, Intercondylar eminence, Pediatric knee, Electric-scooter injury, Physeal extension, Nondisplaced tibial plateau fracture

Introduction

Tibial spine fractures (TSFs) are avulsion injuries of the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tibial footprint that occur predominantly in children and early adolescents [1]. The radiographic Meyers–McKeever (MM) classification remains a practical basis for displacement-driven treatment decisions, while contemporary reviews emphasize the complementary role of cross-sectional imaging in detecting subtle displacement, comminution, and soft-tissue interposition [1], [2]. MRI is particularly useful for identifying meniscal or intermeniscal-ligament entrapment and associated chondroligamentous lesions, and MRI-based schemes such as the Green–Tuca classification can modify treatment recommendations when compared with MM alone [1], [3].

Diagnostic delay is not uncommon: pain-limited examination, hemarthrosis, and near-anatomic fragment positions may mask TSFs on standard radiographic views, which underscores the value of comparative radiographs and CT/MRI when symptoms persist despite “normal” films [1], [4]. Management is typically nonoperative for type I injuries, with an attempted closed reduction for type II fractures and conversion to fixation if reduction is irreducible or unstable, while most type III/IV lesions require operative treatment [1], [5], [6]. Among operative options, functional outcomes are broadly similar; however, screw constructs have been associated with higher reoperation or implant-removal rates than suture constructs in pooled analyses [6], [7].

Case presentation

A previously healthy 14-year-old girl sustained a high-energy electric-scooter crash at approximately 40 km/h, landing directly on both knees in flexion. She immediately developed acute right-knee pain, swelling, and complete functional impairment of the limb; the left knee was contused but less symptomatic. There was no head trauma or loss of consciousness. She initially attended a community hospital, where anteroposterior and lateral knee radiographs were obtained and interpreted as normal. Because pain and functional limitation persisted, she presented to our unit for a second opinion.

On arrival, the right knee exhibited a tense effusion (probable hemarthrosis), joint-line tenderness, and pain-limited range of motion. Distal pulses, sensation, and motor function were normal. Lachman and anterior-drawer tests were limited by pain but suggested increased anterior tibial translation. The left knee showed superficial ecchymosis without clinical instability.

Comparative anteroposterior and lateral radiographs of both knees were obtained (Fig. 1). These showed subtle elevation of the right tibial intercondylar eminence compared with the contralateral side, raising suspicion for a tibial spine avulsion, while no acute osseous injury was seen in the left knee. Computed tomography of the right knee with multiplanar and three-dimensional reconstructions (Fig. 2) confirmed a tibial spine avulsion with a Meyers–McKeever type II pattern, characterized by an elevated tibial spine fragment with anterior tilt and an intact posterior bony hinge. The fracture line extended into the proximal tibial physis. No additional fractures were identified.

The patient was managed nonoperatively. Under analgesia and sedation, a gentle closed reduction was performed using progressive knee flexion combined with a controlled anterior-drawer maneuver, achieving anatomic repositioning of the fragment. The limb was then immobilized in a long-leg (thigh-to-foot) plaster cast with the knee in slight flexion (approximately 10°). Post-reduction radiographs in the cast demonstrated anatomic alignment of the tibial spine fragment and maintained congruity of the tibial plateau without secondary displacement. Serial neurovascular examinations remained normal throughout the immobilization period, and the cast was maintained for six weeks.

At six weeks, radiographs confirmed fracture union of both the tibial spine and the nondisplaced plateau component without secondary displacement (Fig. 3). The patient transitioned to a hinged knee brace and began supervised rehabilitation focusing on gradual range-of-motion recovery and quadriceps strengthening. At eight weeks, clinical examination showed symmetric knee motion compared with the contralateral side and a stable Lachman test, findings illustrated by the standing, flexion, and squatting photographs in Fig. 4. Return to running, and subsequently to pivoting sports, was planned contingent on sustained clinical stability and radiographic consolidation.

Discussion

This case underscores several important points for clinicians who manage pediatric knee trauma. First, diagnostic vigilance is essential. TSFs can be radiographically subtle—particularly injuries with a preserved posterior hinge and near-anatomic fragment position—and may be overlooked when swelling, pain, or suboptimal positioning obscures the intercondylar eminence on standard views [1], [2]. In our patient, the initial radiographs were interpreted as normal despite persistent pain, effusion, and functional impairment. Such clinical features should prompt repeat imaging, including comparative anteroposterior and lateral views and, when doubt persists, cross-sectional imaging [1], [3], [4]. CT is particularly valuable for clarifying fragment tilt, displacement, comminution, physeal extension, and associated plateau fractures, thereby guiding both the assessment of reducibility and the choice of casting position [1], [2]. MRI is especially useful when meniscal or intermeniscal-ligament entrapment or concomitant chondroligamentous injury is suspected, as these factors can transform an apparently reducible lesion into an irreducible fracture that requires arthroscopic reduction and fixation [3], [4].

Second, this case illustrates that even when the fracture pattern is more complex than a “pure” type II lesion—here, a tibial spine avulsion with a type II radiographic pattern but with physeal extension—carefully selected patients may still benefit from nonoperative management. Contemporary algorithms endorse an initial attempt at closed reduction for type II injuries, with conversion to surgery if anatomic alignment cannot be achieved or maintained [1], [5]. Our patient achieved anatomic reduction with gentle closed manipulation, and subsequent immobilization in a long-leg cast resulted in uneventful healing of both the tibial spine and the nondisplaced plateau component. At short-term follow-up, she demonstrated symmetric range of motion and a stable Lachman test, in line with the favorable outcomes reported in recent systematic reviews and state-of-the-art guidance for appropriately selected cases [1], [5], [6].

When surgery is indicated—for example, in irreducible fractures, unstable reductions, or cases with soft-tissue interposition—multiple fixation techniques are available. Both suture and screw constructs yield good functional results, but meta-analytic data indicate higher reoperation and implant-removal rates with screw fixation, which supports the preferential use of suture-based techniques in skeletally immature patients when internal fixation is required [6], [7]. Regardless of the treatment strategy, preventing arthrofibrosis remains a major concern. Multicenter studies have identified postoperative casting parameters, prolonged immobilization, and certain implant characteristics among the risk factors for stiffness [8], whereas pediatric series emphasize the benefits of early, structured mobilization once reduction or fixation is secure [8], [9].

Finally, epidemiologic work indicates a nontrivial case burden of tibial spine fractures in early adolescence [10]. In the context of increasing use of high-speed personal mobility devices such as electric scooters, this case underscores the ongoing need for emergency and primary-care providers to recognize tibial spine fractures promptly, to pursue appropriate imaging when clinical suspicion persists despite normal initial radiographs, and to consider nonoperative management when anatomic and stable reduction can be achieved.

Conclusion

Tibial spine fractures may be overlooked at initial presentation, particularly when radiographs are interpreted as normal despite persistent symptoms. This case illustrates that ongoing pain, effusion, and functional limitation after knee trauma should prompt repeat imaging and cross-sectional studies to avoid missed diagnoses, including fractures with physeal extension. When a tibial spine avulsion with a Meyers–McKeever type II pattern is reducible and radiographically stable, even in the presence of a nondisplaced plateau component, closed reduction followed by casting can represent a safe and effective orthopedic treatment strategy, achieving anatomic union and rapid functional recovery in adolescent patients while avoiding surgical risks.
