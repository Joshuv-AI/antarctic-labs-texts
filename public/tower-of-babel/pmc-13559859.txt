Combining Phytochemical and Computational Approach on Citrus hystrix DC. (Kaffir Lime) Peel Extract: Anti‐Diabetic and Anti‐Inflammatory Activities
Authors: Malathy Soundara Rajan, Sankarganesh Palaniyandi, Swetha Sunkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe peel of Citrus hystrix DC. (kaffir lime), an underexploited agro‐industrial by‐product, may have bioactives. This work tested the ethanolic extract of C. hystrix peel's (EeChP) phytochemicals by GC‐MS profiling, and the antidiabetic effect was evaluated using the alpha‐amylase inhibitory test, and the anti‐inflammatory activity was done using the human red blood cell (HRBC) membrane model. The antibacterial effect was determined using the agar well diffusion method against Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli. Major constituents subjected to pharmacokinetic and ADMET filtering, molecular docking, Gene Ontology (GO), and KEGG pathway analysis for mechanistic plausibility. The GC‐MS detected 44 phytoconstituents, including limonene, γ‐sitosterol, and β‐amyrin. EeChP inhibited α‐amylase (IC50 = 80.97 µg/mL) and the stabilized HRBC membrane (IC50 = 64 µg/mL). The extract demonstrated substantial antibacterial action against S. aureus (IZD = 6 mm at 200 µg/well) but failed against E. coli. The β‐amyrin and scoparone were found to be non‐toxic. Scoparone and carbonic anhydrase 12 were connected with β‐amyrin and protein tyrosine phosphatase non‐receptor type 1. EeChP has anti‐diabetic, multi‐target, modulatory, and anti‐inflammatory effects. The results recommend C. hystrix peel's bioresource potential, but enzyme kinetics, antimicrobial MIC, and in vivo research are necessary. The research has contributed to accomplishing SDG 3 and SDG 12.

Introduction

Metabolic diseases, such as type 2 diabetes mellitus and chronic inflammatory diseases, are an increasing health burden in the world due to sedentary lifestyles, nutritional imbalance, and oxidative‐stress‐induced dysfunction in cells [1, 2, 3]. These diseases are mechanistically multifaceted and entail a dysregulation of carbohydrate metabolism, inflammatory signaling, and redox homeostasis, which constrains the actionability of single‐target therapies. As a result, more interest grows in multicomponent, multitarget agents of natural origin that are capable of targeting multiple biochemical pathways at the same time and modulating.

Antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and metabolic regulatory properties of plant‐based bioactive compounds are not a new discovery [4, 5]. Specifically, citrus residues that result from food processing have become excellent sources of structurally diversified phytochemicals, such as monoterpenes, triterpenoids, sterols, and coumarins. Such agro‐wastes, when valorized, do not only facilitate the operations of sustainable bioprocessing but also provide new opportunities for finding functional food products and therapeutic leads.

Citrus hystrix DC., or kaffir lime, is popular in Southeast Asian traditional medicine and food [6]. Essential oils, pectin‐associated phytochemicals, and lipophilic bioactives [7, 8], which are commonly abundant in kaffir lime peel, in particular, have been reported to have antioxidant, antimicrobial [9], and anti‐inflammatory activities [10, 11]. Despite such reports, the majority of available studies are still fragmented, concentrating either on phytochemical identification, in many instances with no validation of the mechanism involved and without integrative analysis of experimental and computational data. Several previous researches have investigated the essential oil derived from the peel of C. hystrix DC for various biological applications [11, 12, 13, 14]. But the current study used only the direct ethanolic extract of peels.

Efficient methods of predicting molecular targets and explaining possible mechanisms of action of complex plant extracts are molecular docking and network‐based bioinformatics, and examples include Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses [15, 16]. Such integrative approaches can be used to fill the gap between empirical bioactivity and the target‐level mechanistic knowledge when combined with in vitro functional studies. Nevertheless, studies using this integrated framework for C. hystrix peel are scarce, particularly comprehensive studies.

It is against this backdrop that the current research study seeks to offer a comprehensive estimation of the ethanolic extract of C. hystrix peel in terms of (i) identifying the phytochemical composition of the extract using GC‐MS techniques; (ii) determining the antidiabetic, anti‐inflammatory, and antibacterial properties of the extract through the conventional in vitro assays; (iii) estimating pharmacokinetic and drug‐ like qualities of the major bioactive components; and (iv) combining molecular docking with both the GO and KE. This integrative method aims at reinforcing the mechanistic foundation of the therapeutic potential of C. hystrix peel and follows up on its further evolution as a bioactive resource with added value. Apart from this, the sustainable valorization approach, which presents kaffir lime peel waste as a mass produced agro‐industrial waste, suitable for nutraceutical development and functional food applications, may not have been highlighted in previous studies, introduces a translational dimension to the development of functional foods and nutraceuticals in harmony with Sustainable Development Goals 3 and 12. These elements constitute a new contribution to understanding, rather than merely reporting on composition or activity.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Kaffir lime fruit was collected from local supermarkets located in Sholinganallur, Chennai, Tamil Nadu, India. The authentication was done at the Department of Medicinal Botany, National Institute of Siddha, Chennai‐600047, Tamil Nadu, India. The specimen certificate number is NISMB9502026 (Figure 1). Analytical‐grade biochemicals were procured from Sigma Aldrich.

Citrus hystrix DC. authentication certificate.

Ethanolic Extract of Kaffir Lime Peel

The peels were washed and dried at ambient temperature (25°C–28°C) until the weight was constant and then milled into fine powder in a laboratory grinder (Figure 2a). Peel powder was combined with 50 mL of absolute ethanol (solid‐to‐solvent ratio 1:25 w/v). The mixture received a maceration time of 24 h at room temperature (Figure 2b) where it was stirred at constant intervals to maximize the penetration of the solvent and maximize the efficiency of the extraction process. After extraction, the mixture was filtered using Whatman No. 1 filter paper, and the filtrate was concentrated under low pressure using a rotary evaporator at 40°C to eliminate solvent. To measure the extraction yield, the dried crude extract was weighed and kept at 4°C in airtight containers until it was analyzed again. Triple extractions were done to achieve reproducibility.

Kaffir lime peel ethanolic extraction steps. (a) Preparation of peel powder. (b) Ethanolic extraction of peel powder.

GC‐MS Analysis

The analytical approaches of phytochemical profiling of an ethanolic extract of C. hystrix peel (EeChP) were performed using an Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph with a mass selective detector (MSD). The separation was done by placing an HP‐5MS capillary column (30 m × 250 m × 0.25 mm 5% phenyl methyl siloxane). A constant flow rate of 1 mL/min was maintained for the carrier gas, which was helium with a purity of 99.90%. The injection volume was 0.5 µL, and the injector was in split mode. The programmed temperature of the oven was displayed as follows: 110°C (held 2 min) then raised at a rate of 10°C/min to 200°C then raised at a rate of 5°C/min to 280°C followed by a 9 min hold. The MS detector was run under electron‐impact mode having a 70 eV scan interval of 0.5 s [11]. Tentative identifications were made on the basis of the comparison of the mass spectra with those in the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) library. Relative abundance was then given as a proportionality of the total peak area. Putative identifications were accepted, realizing the lack of authentic standards of reference.

Computational Analysis:

Pharmacokinetic Properties and Ligand Preparation

The PubChem database was searched with the identified compounds using GC‐MS, and the three‐dimensional structures of the compounds were downloaded in SDF format. The conversion of the structure to geometries and geometry optimization was also done with OpenBabel. SwissADME, ADMETlab 2.0, and DataWarner were used to predict drug‐like properties and pharmacokinetic values such as absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) [17, 18, 19]. Two compounds, scoparone and β‐amyrin, were chosen based on their favorable ADMET profiles (absence of mutagenicity, tumorigenicity, or inflammatory effects) and underwent additional molecular docking studies.

Target Prediction and DEGs Screening

The SwissTarget Prediction database was used to predict the target of the selected ligands, scoparone and β‐amyrin, at their SMILES notations. Identification and validation of the potential target proteins were also done on the STITCH database. The biological relevance of these targets was investigated by differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis through the utilization of gene datasets in the NCBI database of Homo sapiens and further compared using the STITCH, DAVID, and ShinyGO programs as bioinformatical tools [20, 21]. The biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components related to the predicted genes were done using Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis. Also, no fewer than four KEGG pathway analyses were conducted to investigate potentially related signaling pathways, and protein‐protein interaction (PPI) networks were created to display functional connections. The SRPlot software was used to produce the resultant plots. According to the results of the DEG analysis and target prediction, the best protein structures were obtained in the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). The selection of the target proteins was based on the GO analysis: protein tyrosine phosphatase non‐receptor type 1 (PDB ID:3A5J) and carbonic anhydrase 12 (PDB ID:6QNG). Three target proteins were chosen due to the antimicrobial activity of the extract: Staphylococcus aureus (SPA protein, PDB ID: 1DEE), Escherichia coli (AidA‐I family adhesin, PDB ID: 7KO9). Prediction of protein structure was performed with the help of the I‐TASSER (Iterative Threading ASSEmbly Refinement) server [22]. Protein sequences were sent to the 3D modelling and functional annotation. The models that had been generated were checked with the help of PROCHECK and MolProbity servers by analyzing the Ramachandran plot to check on the stereochemical quality.

Molecular Docking

AutoDock4.2 with the Lamarckian genetic algorithm was used for molecular docking. The structure of the target proteins PTPN1 (PDB ID: 3A5J) and CA12 (PDB 6QNG) was downloaded through the RCSB Protein Data Bank. The preparations of protein structures were done by eliminating water molecules, attaching polar hydrogens, and assigning Gasteiger charges. Proteins remained rigid, and ligands were taken as being flexible. Docking grids with a size of 12 Å × 12 Å × 12 Å were centered on the active sites of the proteins. Docking was done by ranking of the binding energy, and the interaction was analyzed by use of BIO via Discovery Studio [22]. The results of docking were viewed as confirmatory and hypothesis‐generating, rather than predictive.

In Vitro Assays

Anti‐Diabetic Activity (Alpha‐Amylase Activity)

The α‐amylase inhibitory activity of EeChP was determined by the utilization of a colorimetric technique founded on the hydrolysis of starch [23]. In short, 0.5 mg mL−1 of 0.2 M phosphate buffer was added, and EeChP (20—100 µg/mL) incubated with 0.5 mg mL−1 of 0.2 M phosphate buffer (pH 6.9). Soluble starch (1% concentration) was added, and incubation at 37°C was done. To finish the reaction, 3,5‐dinitro salicylic acid reagent and heating to 100°C were added to the reaction, and the resultant percentage inhibition was measured against a control that did not have an extract.

Absorbance was measured at 540 nm, and percentage inhibition was calculated relative to a control without extract. All experiments were conducted thrice: %inhibition=AbsControl−AbsSample/AbsControl×100

Anti‐Inflammatory Activity (HRBC Membrane Stabilization Assay)

The human red blood cell (HRBC) membrane stabilization assay was used to examine the anti‐inflammatory activity. Informed blood samples were taken by means of healthy volunteers. Asever's solution was added to the blood, and the mixture was centrifuged to have an erythrocyte suspension. EEChP (0.2 to 100 µg/mL) was added to the suspension of the HRBC and left to incubate with the suspension in 37°C.

After centrifugation, a measurement of hemoglobin concentration in the supernatant was done at 560 nm. The percentage hemolysis was determined relative to the control. The assay was done thrice, and the results were shown in mean/SD: Hemolysis%=[ODSample/ODControl]×100

Antibacterial Analysis

For antibacterial analysis against Staphylococcus aureus (MTCC 737) and Escherichia coli (MTCC 443), the agar well diffusion method was used to test the antibacterial activity. The bacteria cultures were adjusted to the 0.5 McFarland standard (1.5 × 108 CFU/mL− 1) and the cultures were swabbed on the Mueller‐Hinton agar plates. EeChP (100, 150, and 200 µg/mL) was loaded into wells. Positive control was done using streptomycin.

Plates were left at 37°C and 18–24 h, and the measurement of zones of inhibition was in millimeters. The findings were analyzed qualitatively, as the diffusion‐based assays have certain limitations.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were conducted three times, and the results were presented in the form of a mean. Dose‐response curves have been used to determine the values of IC50. The analysis of the data was conducted with the help of Microsoft Excel. Due to the exploratory research design, statistical comparisons could not be done apart from descriptively.

Computational Analysis

ADMET Properties

The gas chromatography‐mass spectral (GC‐MS) analysis of the EeChP screened forty‐four compounds. The PubChem database was used to retrieve the data, and accessing their pharmacokinetic characteristics was done with the help of ADMETlab 2.0 and Swiss ADME. Out of 44 compounds screened, 22 compounds have significant biological activity, which was listed in the pharmacokinetic property list (Table 2). From that most potent active, Scoparone (C11H10O4), a member of the class of coumarins, and Β‐Amyrin (C30H50O), a pentacyclic triterpenoid, showed nil mutagenic, tumorigenic, and irritant effects. Scoparone has a Hydrogen Donor (HD) value of 0, a Hydrogen Acceptor (HA) value of 4, TPSA Å of 48.67, and a drug‐likeness score of −2.56. β‐Amyrin has a hydrogen donor (HD) and hydrogen acceptor (HA) value of 1, a TPSA Å value of 20.23, and a drug‐likeness score of −8.25. Hence, these two compounds were considered ligands for further molecular docking studies.

Functional Annotation and KEGG Pathway Enrichment Analysis of DEGs

These powerful compounds, scoparone and β‐amyrin, were then studied in a detailed analysis in order to clarify their hypothetical molecular target and biological functions using Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment and KEGG enrichment analyses (Figure 4). With reference to scoparone, GO analysis showed that the genes related to it are involved in such metabolic pathways as protein autophosphorylation, and the corresponding proteins were mainly localized in the cytoplasm (Figure 4a). Its molecular activity was primarily enriched with catalytic activity and carbonate dehydrogenase activity. Conversely, β‐amyrin‐linked genes were identified to be involved in the cellular response to chemical stimuli and regulate the metabolic processes involving small molecules, and the corresponding proteins were located in the plasma membrane, cytosol, and other intracellular structures (Figure 4b).

Gene Ontology characters of the compounds (a) scoparone and (b) β‐amyrin.

The analysis of KEGG pathways showed that scoparone was related to the nitrogen metabolism pathway via nuclear receptors, for example, EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance (Figure 5a), whereas β‐amyrin was related to steroid biosynthesis and PPAR networks (Figure 5b). Both of these compounds were referred to endocrine resistance. Scoparone, in turn, was connected to the C‐type lectin receptor signaling pathway, the MAPK signaling pathway, the TNF signaling pathway, the PI3K‐Akt signaling pathways, the Rap1 signaling pathway among other pathways that are implicated in cancer β‐amyrin on the other hand, was linked with the prolactin signaling pathway, PD‐L1 expression and the PD‐1 checkpoint pathway in cancer, the calcium signaling pathway, the cAMP signaling pathway and The high values of the fold enrichment and the quantity of genes per pathway (between 9 and 21 genes per pathway in scoparone and 5 and 20 genes in β‐amyrin) and the scores of ‐log10(FDR) are signs of accounts and specific interactions being significant.

KEGG pathway for the compounds (a) scoparone and (b) β‐amyrin.

Molecular Docking

The potent bioactive compounds of kaffir lime peel extract were tested using their molecular docking analysis with selected target proteins. Ligand β‐amyrin was docked with protein 3A5J (tyrosine‐protein phosphatase non‐receptor type1) and produced a binding energy ‐8.01 kcal/mol, which is also a strong binding energy (Figure 6).

β‐Amyrin ligand binding with 3A5J protein. (a) 2D structure; (b) 3D structure.

Figure 7 rather demonstrates that the ligand scoparone has been docked onto protein 6QNG (carbonic anhydrase 12) with a binding energy of −5.97 kcal/mol which is a moderate strength of interaction. This value of the estimated inhibition constant (Ki) was 41.97 7 µm real time, which depicts a high‐binding affinity. AspA252 and ArgA254 were hydrogen‐bonded with the distances of 2.09781 and 2.84581 Å, respectively, and others: GlyA9, GlyB24, GlyA12, Gly13 and LysB250.

Scoparone ligand binding with 6QNG protein. (a) 2D structure; (b) 3D structure.

Preliminary Biological Predictions (In Vitro) Based on Computational Analysis

Anti‐Diabetic Activity

EeChP produced concentration‐dependent inhibition of α‐amylase activity, with an IC50 value of 80.97 µg/mL, indicating moderate inhibitory potential (Figure 8a,b).

Anti‐diabetic activity: (a) α‐amylase inhibitory activity of ethanolic extract of the Kaffir lime peel powder and (b) dose‐dependent curve for the activity.

Anti‐Inflammatory Activity

The HRBC membrane stabilization assay demonstrated that EeChP inhibited hypotonicity‐induced hemolysis in a dose‐dependent manner, with an IC50 of 64 µg/mL (Figure 9a,b).

Anti‐inflammatory activity: (a) HRBC activity of ethanolic extract of the Kaffir lime peel powder, (b) dose‐dependent curve for the hemolysis inhibition activity.

Antibacterial Activity

EeChP exhibited detectable antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus aureus (MTCC 737) (Figure 10a) but not against Escherichia coli (MTCC 443) (Figure 10b) under the tested conditions.

The further purification of EeChP may produce improved α‐amylase inhibitor activity.

Given the chemical complexity of EeChP, the observed activity is unlikely to arise from a single dominant inhibitor (Figure 8a,b). Instead, monoterpenes, triterpenoids, and phytosterols present in the extract may collectively contribute through weak but complementary interactions with digestive enzymes and metabolic regulators. To contextualize these functional observations, molecular docking was employed as an exploratory tool. Among screened compounds, β‐amyrin showed favorable binding affinity toward protein tyrosine phosphatase non‐receptor type 1 (PTPN1), a well‐established negative regulator of insulin signaling [34].

It is important to emphasize that this interaction does not demonstrate enzymatic inhibition or physiological modulation. Rather, it suggests a theoretical molecular compatibility that aligns with reported insulin‐sensitizing effects of triterpenoids in experimental models. Supporting this interpretation, pathway enrichment analysis associated β‐amyrin‐linked targets with metabolic and PPAR‐related signaling pathways [4]. Taken together, these data suggest that the antidiabetic effect of EeChP is more plausibly linked to multi‐target metabolic modulation than to direct α‐amylase inhibition alone [31, 35].

Since erythrocyte membrane stabilization (Figure 9a,b) is commonly used as a surrogate indicator of lysosomal membrane protection, this result suggests a moderate capacity of the extract to mitigate membrane destabilization under inflammatory stress conditions [36].

This effect is consistent with the presence of sterols, triterpenoids, and coumarin derivatives identified by GC‐MS, which are known to influence membrane fluidity and inflammatory mediator release. To explore potential molecular correlates, scoparone—a coumarin derivative with favorable ADMET properties—was subjected to docking analysis against carbonic anhydrase 12 (CA12), a protein implicated in inflammatory microenvironment regulation. The moderate binding affinity observed indicates possible molecular engagement but does not establish functional inhibition.

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses further associated scoparone‐linked targets with MAPK, TNF, PI3K–Akt, and related signaling pathways [37]. These associations should be interpreted cautiously, as pathway enrichment reflects statistical overrepresentation rather than direct biological action. Nonetheless, when considered alongside HRBC stabilization data, the results provide mechanistic plausibility for the anti‐inflammatory effects observed at the cellular level.

This selective response is consistent with known permeability differences between Gram‐positive and Gram‐negative bacteria and the hydrophobic nature of many citrus‐derived phytochemicals (Figure 10a,b). While docking analyses suggested potential interactions between selected compounds and bacterial proteins, these findings should be interpreted strictly as hypothesis‐generating. In the absence of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values and time–kill kinetics, no conclusions regarding antibacterial mechanisms or therapeutic relevance can be drawn. Accordingly, EeChP should not be regarded as a broad‐spectrum antibacterial agent based on the current data. Conclusion

In the study, phytochemical profiling, functional bioassays, and computer studies are correlated to question the biochemical possibilities of Citrus hystrix peel ethanol extract. The occurrence of diverse bioactive compound classes, which have the potential to regulate metabolic and inflammatory pathways, was confirmed by gas chromatography‐mass spectrometry profiling. In vitro tests showed moderate levels of α‐amylase inhibition and stabilization of the erythrocyte membrane and hence proved the ability of the extract to affect the process of postprandial glucose regulation and inflammatory stress.

Computational docking and pathway enrichment analyses were utilized mainly to put the functional data into perspective as opposed to defining cause and effect actions. The former predictions of β‐amyrin and scoparone interactions with metabolic and inflammatory targets provide a logical guide to future hypothesis‐directed studies, although they will remain to be confirmed using enzyme kinetics, cellular, and in vivo models.

Translational‐wise, the results present C. hystrix peel as a potential candidate for additional research in terms of functional food and nutraceutical product development, since it is a mass‐produced agro‐industrial by‐product. Nonetheless, the medium strength was noticed, and the dependence on in vitro and in silico methods emphasizes the necessity to conduct strict pharmacological validation, bioavailability research, and safety tests before implementing the respective practice, which can be taken into consideration.

The research supports the aims of Sustainable Development Goal 3, Good Health and Well‐Being, by promoting plant‐based nutraceutical strategies for addressing metabolic and inflammatory‐determined cases. Simultaneously, it corresponds with Sustainable Development Goal 12, Responsible Consumption and Production, by valorizing Citrus hystrix peel waste into bioactive compounds, thereby supporting the circular bioeconomy and the development of sustainable food systems.
