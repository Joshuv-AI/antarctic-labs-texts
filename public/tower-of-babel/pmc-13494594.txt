Weight loss medication prescribing practices among fertility specialists: a cross-sectional survey study
Authors: Elizabeth L Wolfe, Lucas Bartl, Heather Burks, LaTasha B Craig, Jennifer D Peck, Karl R Hansen
Published: 2026

Objective

Obesity (body mass index >30 kg/m2) affects >40% of reproductive age women and is associated with adverse reproductive outcomes (1). Until recently, durable weight loss options were largely limited to lifestyle interventions and bariatric surgery, with only modest success from pharmacotherapy. However, the 2021 introduction of next generation GLP-1 receptor agonists, which report mean body weight reductions of 15%–21%, mark a significant paradigm shift in the modern management of obesity (2, 3).

Fertility specialists are uniquely positioned to prescribe preconception weight loss interventions, yet evidence on the reproductive benefits of immediate preconception weight loss is mixed (4, 5). This study uses a cross-sectional survey design to assess current anti-obesity pharmacotherapy prescribing practices among reproductive endocrinology and infertility providers. In light of recent advances and growing interest in obesity management, assessing provider opinion and baseline practice patterns will promote important discussion that can inform future research and guideline development.

Study design

This study was approved by the University of Oklahoma Health Sciences Center institutional review board (#17676). A 15-item cross-sectional anonymous survey was developed to assess respondent demographics, prescribing patterns, motivations, and barriers to the medical management of obesity. Content validation was provided by a pilot group of consulting experts who reviewed the survey for word choice, clarity, and alignment with the intended aims. The survey was distributed virtually and in-person via QR code link to MD/DO/APRN/PA attendees of the Obesity and Reproduction Journal Club Global as part of the 2024 American Society for Reproductive Medicine Scientific Congress. Descriptive statistics were used to analyze demographic information and response patterns. Fisher’s exact test was performed to assess response variation between categorical variables including practice location (inside vs. outside the United States), practice setting (academic, hybrid, or private practice) and number of years in practice (≤10 years, >10 years).

Results

The survey was distributed to 199 people and had a response rate of 17.5% (n = 35 respondents). Of the respondents, 91% (32/35) were M.D. or D.O., and 63% (22/35) practiced in the United States. The largest percentage of respondents (12/35) had been in practice for ≥21 years, and more than half (16/35) practiced in a university or academic institution.

Figure 1 displays responses to practice patterns. More than half (51%) of respondents believed that prescribing weight loss medications falls within the scope of fertility care, but only 33% currently or previously prescribed weight loss medications as part of their practice. A total of 51% (18/35) are planning to continue to prescribe or start prescribing weight loss medications within the next 1–2 years. There were no statistically significant differences in response patterns by practice location, practice setting, or number of years in practice.

Responses to practice patterns.

Respondents additionally were asked to select motivations and barriers that exist to prescribing weight loss medications. A lack of clear prescribing guidelines (57%) and a lack of familiarity with drug mechanism of action, contraindications, and monitoring (52%) were the most common barriers to prescribing. Of respondents who already prescribe weight loss pharmacotherapy, the most cited motivation was a desire to improve a patient’s metabolic health before conception (92%, 12/13).

Conclusion

This study highlights the increasing interest, yet lack of consensus regarding the evolving role that fertility specialists have in the preconception medical management of obesity. Prescription of weight loss medications among fertility specialists currently is uncommon, although there appears to be increasing interest in incorporating pharmacotherapy into patient care. Of the barriers that exist, the two most common are a lack of professional society prescribing guidelines and a lack of medication familiarity.

The study strengths include a timely and clinically relevant question regarding fertility specialists’ prescribing practices in the setting of rapidly evolving anti-obesity pharmacotherapy and using a content-validated survey instrument distributed to a targeted audience of fertility specialists. In addition, the inclusion of respondents across practice settings and years in practice provides useful preliminary insight into variation in attitudes toward pharmacologic weight management in reproductive medicine.

However, the findings should be interpreted in light of several important limitations. The overall sample size was small, and the survey was distributed during an obesity-focused journal club, thereby increasing susceptibility to nonresponse and selection bias, thus limiting the external validity of the study. The respondent population may not be representative of the broader fertility workforce, because only approximately two-thirds of participants practiced in the United States and more than half were affiliated with academic institutions, potentially restricting generalizability to practitioners based in other settings.

This study provides a baseline assessment of modern prescribing practice opinion among fertility specialists after the recent advent of next generation GLP-1 receptor agonists and underscores the need for further research that supports evidence-based weight management recommendations in reproductive medicine.
