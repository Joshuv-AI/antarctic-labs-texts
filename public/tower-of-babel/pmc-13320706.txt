Sustainable healthcare knowledge, experience and curricular perspectives: a cross-sectional survey within the European Young Family Doctors Movement network
Authors: Zineb Bentounsi, Ikbal Humay Arman, Carolina Cordovil, Ana Cristina Franco Spinola, Lukasz Reczek, Ozden Gokdemir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe healthcare sector contributes substantially to greenhouse gas emissions, and family doctors are well placed to reduce this impact, but their preparedness depends on adequate education in sustainable healthcare.ObjectivesThe objective of this study was to describe self-reported knowledge of sustainable healthcare and perceived educational gaps among family doctors and trainees affiliated with the European Young Family Doctors Movement (EYFDM) network.MethodsA cross-sectional, web-based survey was distributed through EYFDM. The instrument comprised one open-ended question, five knowledge items (four single-best-answer and one multiselect), and two yes/no items. Open-ended responses were scored independently by three authors using a rubric anchored to the World Health Organization definition of environmentally sustainable health systems. Data were analysed in SPSS v27.ResultsA total of 130 family doctors and trainees from 15 European countries responded; 83.8% came from three countries (the Netherlands, Türkiye, and the UK). Of those who answered, 64.7% (75/116) reported no prior experience with sustainable healthcare, and 72.5% (58/80) had received no related teaching during training. The median total knowledge score was 3/5 [interquartile range (IQR) 2]. Scores were higher in participants aged under 45 years (P < .001), in female respondents (P = .014), and in trainees (P < .001). Nearly everyone who responded agreed that planetary health should be included in the family medicine curriculum.ConclusionsWithin this EYFDM sample, prior formal education in sustainable healthcare was uncommon, and interest in further training was high. Findings are exploratory and not generalizable to all European family doctors, but they support the development of validated instruments and the integration of planetary health into family medicine curricula.

Introduction

The European Definition of Family Medicine was revised in 2023 by the World Organization of Family Doctors (WONCA) Europe [1]. One Health, Planetary Health, and Sustainability were added to the discipline's context to create the bedrock for a new integrated approach.

Sustainable healthcare is defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a health system that maintains, improves, or restores health while minimizing negative environmental impacts and leverages opportunities to improve it for the benefit of the health of current and future generations [2]. The healthcare sector itself contributes an estimated 4%–5% of global greenhouse gas emissions and a comparable share of air pollutant emissions in many high-income countries [3, 4]. Primary care has been identified as a key setting in which clinicians can both reduce environmental impact and prepare patients for climate-related health risks [5, 6].

Surveys of general practitioners and trainees in different countries have consistently reported limited formal education in planetary health and a high level of interest in receiving such training [7–10]. Family doctors are in a unique position to promote knowledge about One Health, Planetary Health, and Sustainability, to encourage changes in behaviour, and to inform policy and educational change [11].

Building on a pilot survey and a peer dialogue at the WONCA Europe Conference in 2023 [12], both indicating a need for wider exploration, this paper describes self-reported knowledge of sustainable healthcare among family doctors and trainees within the EYFDM network, identifies perceived educational gaps, and explores preferences for further education.

Materials and methods

Study design and reporting

We conducted a cross-sectional, web-based, self-administered survey. The manuscript is reported in line with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for cross-sectional studies [13]. A completed STROBE checklist is provided as Supplementary material.

Setting and sampling frame

The accessible population was family doctors/general practitioners (GP) and family medicine trainees affiliated with the European Young Family Doctors Movement (EYFDM), a subnetwork of WONCA Europe, which includes predominantly early-career family doctors. We used a convenience, nonprobability sampling approach: an open invitation was distributed within the EYFDM network through e-mail lists, EYFDM-related WhatsApp groups, and two issues of the EYFDM newsletter. Several follow-up reminders were sent through the same channels, and additional invitations were e-mailed directly to national EYFDM country representatives, who were asked to forward the survey to their national networks. The data was collected from September 2023 to January 2024. No formal sample-size calculation was performed; we aimed to maximize responses across as many European countries as possible within this network. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and responses were collected through Google Forms. No IP addresses or other identifiers were recorded, and no incentives were offered.

Survey content and variables

The survey was designed by the authors based on the WHO definition of environmentally sustainable health systems [2] and on topics highlighted in WONCA position statements [11] and prior literature [7, 8]. It was reviewed for face validity by all coauthors before distribution and administered in English only; formal pilot testing and psychometric validation were not performed. The survey contained, in order, the following: an open-ended question (‘In your opinion, what is sustainable healthcare?’); five knowledge items covering healthcare's contribution to global carbon emissions, deaths from climate-sensitive diseases, the benefit-to-cost ratio of climate action, actions to reduce healthcare's carbon emissions (multiselect), and healthcare waste (identify the false statement); a repeat of the open-ended question; two nonmandatory closed questions on inclusion of planetary health in the curriculum and awareness of national initiatives; and nonmandatory demographics (age category, gender, country, training status, prior experience, and prior teaching). Verbatim wording, response options, and sources for each knowledge item are provided as Supplementary Appendix S1. The nonmandatory placement of demographic and closed items explains the variation in denominators reported in the Results section.

Scoring

Each single-best-answer item (Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q5) was scored 1 point for the correct answer and 0 points otherwise. Question 4 was a multiselect item in which respondents could identify all correct statements from a list of four and an ‘other’ option and was scored proportionally (0.2 per correct statement, including adding one more correct item for a total maximum of 1.0).

Open-ended responses were scored from 1 (very poor) to 5 (very competent) using a rubric developed a priori and anchored to the five components of the WHO definition of environmentally sustainable health systems [2] (maintaining or improving health, minimization of environmental impact, responsibility towards current and future generations, systems-level thinking, and clinician-level actions). Three authors (Z.B., C.C., and Ö.G.) scored each response independently; discrepancies were resolved by averaging the three scores. Formal interrater reliability statistics (intraclass correlation coefficient) were not calculated; this is acknowledged as a limitation. When a participant's second open-ended response was ‘same as before’, the first score was carried over. Fourteen responses (one in Turkish and 13 in Dutch) were translated into English by bilingual members of the author team before scoring; two participants left both open-ended questions blank and were excluded from the open-ended analysis (n = 128 for the paired open-ended comparison; n = 130 for the knowledge score).

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed with IBM SPSS Statistics version 27. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages, with the denominator stated for each variable. Continuous variables were tested for normality using histograms and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Normally distributed variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation; nonnormally distributed variables are reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). The Mann–Whitney U test and Kruskal–Wallis test were used to compare nonnormally distributed continuous variables between two and more than two independent groups, respectively. The chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables. Spearman's rank correlation and Pearson's correlation were used to assess the relationship between the total knowledge score and continuous independent variables. A linear regression model with age (three categories), gender, training status, prior experience, prior teaching during training, and awareness of national sustainability efforts as independent variables was used to explore predictors of the total knowledge score. A two-sided P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant. Given the limited sample size and the exploratory nature of the study, all subgroup analyses and regression results are interpreted as hypothesis generating.

Missing data and handling of partial responses

Because the demographic items and the two closed (yes/no) questions were not mandatory, some items have a smaller denominator than the overall sample of 130. Denominators are reported for every variable in Table 1. We did not impute missing values, and analyses were performed on complete cases for each item.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from Izmir University of Economics, Ethics Committee for Health Sciences Research (Date: 11.09.2023, No: B.30.2.İEÜSB.0.05.05-20-260). Participation was voluntary and anonymous. The first page of the online survey contained an information statement describing the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, the absence of identifiers, and contact details of the research team. Submission of the completed survey was taken as informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Participants and descriptive data

A total of 130 participants completed the survey. A total of 61.5% were female, 34.6% were male, and 3.9% preferred not to say. The mean age was 39.9 ± 11.2 years (median 35; range 26–65). A total of 64.6% were qualified family doctors, and 35.4% were trainees.

Participants came from 15 countries in Europe. The top three contributing countries were the Netherlands (57.7%, n = 75), Türkiye (16.9%, n = 22), and the UK (9.2%, n = 12); together these accounted for 83.8% of the sample. The remaining 12 countries each contributed five or fewer respondents (Table 1).

Most participants had no prior experience in sustainable healthcare (64.7%, 75/116), and the majority of participants did not receive any teaching about it as part of their family medicine training (72.5%, 58/80) (Table 1).

Knowledge score and item-level performance

The median total knowledge score was 3 of 5 (IQR 2–4; mean 2.97 ± 1.14; range 0–5). Ten per cent of participants achieved a perfect score, and 1.5% scored zero (Table 1). The frequency of correct answers varied markedly between items (Table 2): Question 5, on healthcare waste, was the most frequently answered correctly (76.9%, 100/130), whereas Question 1 (rank of healthcare as a global carbon emitter) and Question 2 (climate-sensitive deaths) were the least frequently answered correctly (26.2% and 27.7%, respectively). For the multiselect Question 4 (actions to reduce healthcare's carbon emissions), the mean proportional score was 0.67 ± 0.20 (median 0.80; IQR 0.20).

Female respondents scored higher than male respondents (P = .014), trainees higher than qualified family doctors (P < .001), respondents who had received teaching about sustainable healthcare during training higher than those who had not (P = .011), and respondents aware of national initiatives higher than those who were not (P = .005). Prior personal experience was associated with a slightly nonsignificant trend (P = .052). No significant difference was observed between high-income and upper–middle-income countries (P = .383). In an exploratory linear regression including age, gender, training status, prior experience, prior teaching during training, and awareness of national efforts, age category (P = .001) and awareness of national efforts (P = .030) were the strongest independent predictors of the total knowledge score (overall model P = .007; R2 = 0.262). Given the limited sample size, these results are interpreted as hypothesis generating.

Attitudes towards inclusion of planetary health in the curriculum

Eighty participants answered the question ‘Do you think it is important to include planetary health in the family medicine curriculum?’, and 92.5% (74/80) said ‘yes’. This question was placed at the end of the survey and was nonmandatory, which explains the smaller denominator compared with the 130 respondents who answered the knowledge questions. Of the 117 respondents who answered the corresponding question, 71.8% (n = 84) reported being aware of initiatives in their country aimed at making healthcare more sustainable.

Open-ended responses: ‘what is sustainable healthcare?’

Of 130 respondents, two participants left both open-ended questions blank and were excluded from the open-ended analysis. Fourteen responses written in a language other than English (one in Turkish and 13 in Dutch) were translated into English by bilingual members of the author team before scoring, so that 128 paired open-ended responses contributed to the comparison. The mean score for the first iteration of the question (at the start of the survey) was 2.66 of 5; the mean score for the second iteration (at the end of the survey) was 2.26. A total of 43.9% of participants gave the same answer to both iterations, 10.6% obtained a higher score in the second iteration, while the remainder obtained a similar or lower score. The decline among the latter group most often reflected a shift from describing the concept of sustainable healthcare to commenting on its importance after exposure to the closed-ended items, rather than any actual loss of knowledge. The absence of a control group prevents any causal inference about a learning effect.

As an example, one answer that scored 4 of 5 from the rubric-based scoring was: ‘In my view, healthcare should aim for minimal environmental impact, fostering a harmonious coexistence between hygiene and climate preservation. This necessitates a transformative journey, where new innovations drive sustainability, reducing post-surgery waste, ensuring the responsible disposal of medications to safeguard natural and drinking water, and promoting the use of reusable medical products’.

Discussion

In this convenience sample of 130 family doctors and family medicine trainees affiliated with the EYFDM network, most respondents reported limited prior education in sustainable healthcare. The median knowledge score was 3 of 5, and scores were higher in younger respondents, in female respondents, and in trainees than in their respective comparator groups. Among the 80 respondents who answered the curriculum question, 92.5% supported the inclusion of planetary health in the family medicine curriculum. These findings are exploratory and apply primarily to the surveyed network; they should not be interpreted as a representative description of European family doctors as a whole.

The finding that participants aged under 45 years scored higher than those aged 45 and above is consistent with reports from other primary care contexts. In an Australian survey of general practitioner registrars, 61.8% agreed that climate change content should be integrated into their training programme [7]. Several plausible mechanisms could contribute to this age-related pattern: more recent undergraduate and postgraduate curricula increasingly include planetary health content [14, 15]; younger clinicians may have greater exposure to climate change as a salient public health issue through media and professional networks [16]; and self-selection into surveys distributed through early-career networks (such as the EYFDM) may have produced a particularly engaged group of younger respondents. The finding that trainees scored higher than qualified family doctors is consistent with these mechanisms. These hypotheses cannot be tested with the present data and should be examined in future studies with broader sampling and validated instruments.

Continuing professional development for more experienced colleagues is also important, both because they currently care for the largest share of patients and because the climate–health intersection has emerged within their working lifetime rather than during their training. Highlighting the link between planetary health and patient outcomes, e.g. through the lens of climate-sensitive infectious diseases, heat-related morbidity, and air pollution-driven respiratory and cardiovascular disease, may be a particularly effective entry point for clinicians whose primary professional motivation is patient care [17, 18].

Encouragingly, our survey found a high level of interest in further education on this topic. The 92.5% supporting planetary health inclusion in the curriculum represents 74/80 respondents to a nonmandatory item rather than 92.5% of all 130 participants, which is consistent with findings in other surveys of primary care clinicians already engaged with the topic [7–10]. Some training bodies, such as the Royal College of General Practitioners in the UK, have begun to integrate planetary health and sustainability into the workplace-based assessment framework [19], and a scoping review is currently mapping the integration of planetary health into family medicine training across Europe [20].

Limitations

This study has several important limitations that constrain how its findings should be interpreted.

Sampling frame and representativeness

The survey was distributed predominantly through the EYFDM network, an early-career subnetwork of WONCA Europe, so the accessible population was a specific subgroup rather than European family doctors. The sample size (n = 130) is small relative to the target population; the geographical distribution is uneven (83.8% from three countries; several countries contributing a single respondent); and although 32.3% of participants were aged over 45 years, the EYFDM is structured around early-career doctors. The findings should therefore be interpreted as descriptive of an engaged, self-selected subgroup connected to the EYFDM.

Selection bias

The voluntary, self-selected nature of participation is likely to have overrepresented respondents with a preexisting interest in sustainable healthcare; baseline knowledge is therefore likely to be overestimated.

Instrument validity and reliability

The instrument was developed by the authors using the WHO definition and prior literature but was not piloted and not subjected to formal psychometric validation, and Cronbach's alpha was not calculated; construct validity therefore cannot be confirmed. The rubric-based scoring of open-ended responses was performed independently by three raters, but formal interrater reliability statistics (Fleiss’ kappa or intraclass correlation coefficient) were not calculated, and discrepancies were resolved by averaging rather than through structured consensus discussion; this may have introduced interrater variability.

Open-ended analysis

The analysis of open-ended responses relied on rubric-based scoring rather than on a formal qualitative method. Comparisons between the open-ended score and the total knowledge score should be interpreted cautiously as the two instruments measure different constructs.

Language

The survey was administered in English only, which may have affected both who chose to participate and the depth of free-text responses among nonnative English speakers. Non-English open-ended responses (one in Turkish and 13 in Dutch) were translated into English by bilingual members of the author team and included in the analysis; however, translation by the same team that designed and scored the rubric is a potential source of bias, and a fully independent translation pipeline would be preferable in future work. Future studies should also administer multilingual versions of the instrument from the outset.

Conclusion

Among family doctors and trainees engaged with the EYFDM network, self-reported knowledge of sustainable healthcare was modest, prior formal training was uncommon, and there was strong interest in including planetary health in the family medicine curriculum. These findings are exploratory and not generalizable to all European family doctors, but they support the development and validation of standardized educational tools, the integration of planetary health into family medicine curricula, and the design of larger, multilingual, and more representative studies of European primary care clinicians.
