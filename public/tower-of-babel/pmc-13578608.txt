Prevalence and Determinants of Work‐Related Ocular Injuries Among Welders in Small‐Scale Industries of Debre Markos Town, East Gojjam Zone, Northwest Ethiopia: An Institution‐Based Cross‐Sectional Study

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsWelding activities expose workers to ultraviolet radiation, high temperatures, and airborne particles, making ocular injuries one of the most common occupational health problems in this profession. These injuries may result in temporary or permanent visual impairment, negatively affecting workers' productivity and quality of life. Identifying the burden of ocular injuries and their contributing factors is essential for designing effective preventive interventions. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of work‐related ocular injuries and identify associated determinants among small‐scale welders in Debre Markos town, Northwest Ethiopia, in 2023.MethodsAn institution‐based cross‐sectional survey was carried out among 414 welders in Debre Markos town from September 10 to 30, 2023. Participants were selected using a systematic random sampling technique. Data were gathered through a structured and pretested interviewer‐administered questionnaire. The dataset was entered into EpiData version 4.6 and analyzed using SPSS version 25. Logistic regression analyses were performed to determine factors associated with ocular injuries. Variables with statistical significance in the multivariable model were reported using adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsThe results showed that 46.7% of the 414 welders experienced ocular injuries. The mean age of participants was 27 ± 4.9 years, and 76.6% were male. While 81.4% of participants had access to eye protective devices, only 71.5% used eye protective devices. Significant factors associated with ocular injury included working as an arc welder (AOR = 4.96, 95% CI: 2.05–9.74), workplace supervision (AOR = 2.41, 95% CI: 1.97–6.51), lack of health and safety training (AOR = 6.43, 95% CI: 3.02–9.46), not using protective eyewear (AOR = 3.79, 95% CI: 2.44–9.29), and low awareness of ocular injury risks (AOR = 4.03, 95% CI: 2.11–9.43).ConclusionTo reduce ocular injuries, the study recommends providing health and safety training, strengthening workplace supervision, promoting consistent use of eye safety device, and raising awareness of welding‐related eye hazards.

Background

Work‐related ocular injuries are an important occupational health problem because even brief exposure to workplace hazards can cause pain, temporary disability, visual impairment, or permanent loss of sight. Common mechanisms include foreign‐body penetration, chemical and thermal burns, radiation exposure, and blunt or penetrating trauma. Beyond their clinical consequences, these injuries may lead to treatment costs, absenteeism, reduced productivity, and long‐term effects on workers' livelihoods [1, 2].

Welding is widely used in construction, manufacturing, vehicle repair, and metal fabrication [3]. However, the welding process generates ultraviolet and infrared radiation, intense visible light, heat, sparks, molten metal, fumes, and high‐velocity particles [4, 5]. These hazards may produce acute conditions such as photokeratitis, corneal abrasions, conjunctival injury, and foreign bodies, as well as chronic ocular problems associated with repeated occupational exposure [6, 7]. The risk is influenced by the type of welding, duration of exposure, workplace layout, proximity to other workers, and the quality and consistency of protective‐equipment use.

Eye injuries remain a significant global public health problem. Worldwide estimates indicate that millions of individuals suffer from visual impairment or blindness attributable to ocular trauma, with hundreds of thousands of new cases reported each year [8]. Studies conducted in different countries have reported varying prevalence of welding‐related ocular injuries, often influenced by safety practices and work environments [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14].

In sub‐Saharan Africa, ocular injuries among welders remain a major public health issue, with significant impacts on productivity, income, and quality of life [15, 16, 17, 18]. The burden is further aggravated by limited access to protective equipment, inadequate occupational safety training, and poor awareness of preventive practices [19, 20, 21, 22]. However, evidence from previous research shows that the prevalence of ocular injuries can be considerably reduced by applying simple preventive measures such as institutional safety training, consistent use of protective eyewear, and effective workplace supervision [17, 23, 24, 25].

In Ethiopia, the industrial sector is expanding rapidly as part of national development initiatives [26]. Despite this, occupational safety, particularly in small‐scale industries, has received minimal attention. Government efforts mainly focus on financial support rather than incorporating occupational health and safety measures [27, 28]. Although some studies have been conducted on occupational injuries in Ethiopia [2, 29, 30, 31, 32], there is limited knowledge about the prevalence and determinants of work‐related ocular injuries among welders, especially in the East Gojjam Zone, where numerous small‐ and medium‐scale factories operate.

Therefore, this study aims to assess the prevalence and determinants of work‐related ocular injuries among welders in Debre Markos town, Northwest Ethiopia. The findings will help fill the knowledge gap and provide evidence to guide occupational health and safety interventions for welders and other industrial workers.

Methods and Materials

Study Area, Design, and Study Period

An institution‐based cross‐sectional study was undertaken among welders operating in Debre Markos town, Northwest Ethiopia, between September 10 and September 30, 2023. Debre Markos, the administrative center of East Gojjam Zone in the Amhara National Regional State, is located approximately 296 km northwest of Addis Ababa. Based on projections from the Ethiopian Central Statistical Agency, the city's population was estimated at 160,837, comprising 79,608 males and 81,229 females [33]. Information obtained from the Debre Markos town Technical and Vocational Enterprises Development Office indicated that 1,643 welders were employed across 79 registered welding workshops during the study period. Eye care services in the city are provided through two private ophthalmic clinics and Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital.

Study Population and Participant Selection

The source population comprised all welders employed in registered welding workshops within Debre Markos town. Welders who had been working in the selected establishments for at least 6 months and were available during the data collection period were considered eligible. Individuals assigned to quality‐control sections and newly recruited workers with less than 6 months of service were excluded from the study. Workers aged 15–17 years were eligible for inclusion only under the minor‐participant consent and assent procedures approved by the Ethical Review Board.

Participants were selected using a systematic random sampling approach. Data were collected from eligible welders through face‐to‐face interviews and workplace observations using structured data collection instruments.

Sample Size Determination and Sampling Technique

The sample size required to estimate the prevalence of ocular injury was calculated using the single population proportion formula: n=(Zα/2)2×p(1−p)d2 where n denotes the required sample size, p represents the estimated prevalence of ocular injury (47.9%) reported in a previous Ghanaian study [17], Z corresponds to the standard normal value at a 95% confidence level (1.96), and d is the margin of error set at 5%.

The resulting sample size for the primary objective was 383 participants. For the assessment of factors associated with ocular injury, a separate calculation yielded a sample size of 234. Since the larger sample provides greater statistical precision, 383 were selected. After adding a 10% allowance for possible nonresponse, the final sample size became 421 welders.

To obtain a representative sample, 59 welding workshops were randomly selected from the 79 registered workshops using a lottery method. The total sample size was then proportionally distributed across the selected workshops according to the number of welders employed in each establishment. Finally, eligible participants were chosen systematically from the employee lists.

Data Collection Tools and Procedure

Information was gathered using a structured interviewer‐administered questionnaire complemented by an observational checklist. The questionnaire was developed after reviewing relevant scientific literature on occupational ocular injuries and workplace safety practices [2, 29, 30, 31, 32]. Initially prepared in English, the instrument was translated into Amharic for field use and subsequently back‐translated into English to ensure consistency and accuracy.

The questionnaire includes sociodemographic information, workplace‐related information, and questions about awareness, history of ocular injury and knowledge of protective eyewear. Data collection was carried out by four trained Bachelor of Science ophthalmic nurses with prior experience in field surveys.

To ensure the tool's reliability and validity, a pre‐test was conducted with 21 respondents from the neighboring town of Dejen. During pretesting, the awareness and knowledge scales demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha 0.72 and 0.77, respectively) [34]. Consequently, the questionnaire's reliability in this study was confirmed as adequate.

Data Quality Assurance and Statistical Analysis

Several measures were implemented to ensure data quality. Data collectors and supervisors received intensive training before fieldwork commenced. The questionnaire was pretested, and daily supervision was conducted throughout the data collection period. Completed questionnaires were reviewed regularly for completeness, consistency, and accuracy.

Following data cleaning and verification, the dataset was entered into EpiData version 4.6 and subsequently exported to SPSS version 25 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were generated to summarize participant characteristics and study variables. Binary logistic regression analysis was initially performed to identify candidate variables associated with ocular injury. Variables with a p‐value less than 0.20 in the bivariable analysis were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model.

Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to measure the strength of associations between explanatory variables and ocular injury. Statistical significance was determined using a p‐value threshold of less than 0.05. The goodness‐of‐fit of the final multivariable logistic regression model was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, which indicated an adequate fit of the model to the observed data (p = 0.23). Additionally, multicollinearity among the independent variables was evaluated using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), and no evidence of significant multicollinearity was detected.

Operational Definitions

Small‐scale industry: A manufacturing or construction enterprise utilizing a combination of manually operated and electrically powered machinery for production activities [2].

Work‐related ocular injury: Any injury affecting the eye or its associated structures that occurred during welding activities within the 12 months preceding the interview [35].

Awareness of ocular injury: Awareness was measured using five items related to occupational eye hazards and preventive practices. Participants scoring at or above the mean value were categorized as having good awareness, whereas those scoring below the mean were classified as having poor awareness [30].

Smoking status: Individuals who reported smoking at least one cigarette were categorized as current smokers, whereas those who did not smoke were classified as non‐smokers [36].

Knowledge of eye safety device: Knowledge was assessed using four questions concerning the purpose and proper use of eye safety devices. Respondents with scores equal to or above the mean were considered to have good knowledge, while those scoring below the mean were categorized as having poor knowledge [30].

Alcohol consumption: Weekly alcohol intake was calculated using standard drinking units, where one bottle of beer, one glass of wine, or one serving of spirits was considered equivalent to approximately 10 g of ethanol. Participants were classified as non‐drinkers, light drinkers (1–14 units/week), or heavy drinkers (> 14 units/week) [2].

Results

Socio‐Demographic Characteristics of Participants

A total of 421 welders were initially targeted based on the calculated sample size. Of these, 414 participants were successfully included in the final analysis, yielding a response rate of 98.3%. The remaining seven participants were considered non‐respondents due to non‐participation and incomplete responses during data collection. The respondents had a mean age of 27 years (SD ± 4.9 years). The largest proportion of participants (32.7%) belonged to the 26–28 years age group. Most respondents were male (76.6%), while females accounted for 23.4% of the study population. Regarding marital status, more than two‐thirds (70.3%) were single. The overwhelming majority identified as Orthodox Christians (97.6%). In terms of educational attainment, approximately two‐thirds (64.1%) had completed secondary education or above. Monthly income varied among participants, with 29.3% earning between 3,501 and 5,000 Ethiopian Birr (Table 1).

Behavioral Characteristics of Study Participants

Of the 414 welders surveyed, 337 (81.4%) reported having access to eye protective devices at their workplace. However, only 296 (71.5%) indicated that they routinely used such equipment during welding activities. Among these 296 welders who reported using eye‐ and face‐protective equipment, sunglasses were the most frequently reported device (34.5%), followed by goggles (19.9%), sunglasses combined with face shields (18.9%), welding helmets (11.8%), goggles combined with face shields (10.1%), and face shields alone (4.8%).

Regarding lifestyle‐related factors, nearly half of the respondents (48.0%) consumed alcoholic beverages. Of these, 117 (28.2%) were classified as light drinkers and 82 (19.8%) as heavy drinkers. Khat chewing was reported by 97 participants (23.5%), whereas 77 respondents (18.6%) identified themselves as current smokers (Table 2).

Workplace Characteristics of the Study Participants

Among the study participants, 191 (46.1%) were engaged primarily in arc welding, while 149 (36.0%) performed gas welding and 74 (17.9%) were involved in both welding methods. Workplace supervision was reported by only 34.5% of welders, whereas nearly two‐thirds (65.5%) worked without regular supervision.

Health and safety training was relatively uncommon, with 287 participants (69.3%) reporting that they had never received formal occupational safety training. Most welders (74.2%) worked 8 h or less per day. In terms of occupational experience, 44.9% had between three and 4 years of work experience, followed by 24.7% with five to 6 years of experience (Table 3).

Magnitude of Work‐Related Ocular Injury

The study found that 193 of the 414 welders had experienced at least one work‐related ocular injury during the previous 12 months, corresponding to a prevalence of 46.7% (95% CI: 41.9–51.5). Among those reporting ocular injury, 101 (52.4%) had sustained a single episode, while 69 (35.7%) and 23 (11.9%) reported two and three injury episodes, respectively. Slightly more than half of the participants (53.3%) reported no history of ocular injury during the reference period (Figure 1).

Factors Associated With Work‐Related Ocular Injury

The bivariable logistic regression analysis identified several candidate variables associated with ocular injury, including sex, marital status, educational status, workplace supervision, job training, work experience, welding type, awareness of ocular injury, and knowledge of eye protective devices. These variables were subsequently entered into the multivariable logistic regression model.

After adjusting for potential confounders, five variables remained significantly associated with work‐related ocular injury: welding type, workplace supervision, occupational health and safety training, utilization of eye protective devices, and awareness of ocular injury.

Welders engaged in arc welding were approximately five times more likely to experience ocular injuries than those involved in gas welding (AOR = 4.96, 95% CI: 2.05–9.74). Likewise, the odds of ocular injury were significantly higher among workers who lacked workplace supervision compared with those who received regular supervision (AOR = 2.41, 95% CI: 1.97–6.51).

The absence of occupational health and safety training was another strong factor associated with ocular injury. Welders who had not received such training were more than six times as likely to sustain ocular injuries as those who had undergone training (AOR = 6.43, 95% CI: 3.02–9.46).

Similarly, participants who did not regularly use eye protective devices had significantly higher odds of ocular injury compared with those who used protective eyewear (AOR = 3.79, 95% CI: 2.44–9.29). In addition, welders with poor awareness of ocular hazards were approximately four times more likely to experience ocular injuries than their counterparts with good awareness (AOR = 4.03, 95% CI: 2.11–9.43).

In contrast, variables such as sex, educational status, work experience, marital status, and knowledge of eye protective devices did not retain statistical significance in the final multivariable model (Table 5).

Discussion

This study assessed the prevalence and determinants of work‐related ocular injuries among welders in Debre Markos town. Nearly half of the participants had experienced an occupational ocular injury, demonstrating that eye injuries remain a substantial and potentially preventable occupational health problem in small‐scale welding workplaces. Welders are exposed to ultraviolet radiation, intense heat, sparks, molten metal, fumes, and high‐velocity particles. The resulting injuries may range from transient irritation and photokeratitis to corneal trauma, visual impairment, and permanent ocular damage.

The prevalence of ocular injury in this study was 46.7% (95% CI: 41.9–51.5). This estimate was comparable to the prevalence reported among welders in Ghana, where 47.9% experienced work‐related eye injuries [17] and study conducted in Yenagoa, Nigeria (43.4%) [37]. However, it was lower than the 62.2% reported in a recent study from Nepal [7] and higher than the 31.4% reported among small‐scale industry workers in Gondar, Ethiopia [2]. Differences between studies may reflect variation in study populations, welding techniques, sample sizes, recall periods, definitions of ocular injury, and methods of case ascertainment. The use of self‐reported symptoms in some studies and clinical examination in others may also contribute to differences in prevalence estimates. A recent review emphasized that occupational eye injuries are frequently underreported and inconsistently classified across settings [6].

The relatively high prevalence observed in Debre Markos may be related to the characteristics of small‐scale welding workplaces, where formal occupational health programs, regular supervision, and certified protective devices may be limited. In Nepal, for example, most welders reported using some form of eye protection, but only 1.8% used certified protective goggles, indicating that reported use does not always represent adequate protection [7]. Similarly, a study in Hosanna found that although 99% of welders had access to eye‐protection equipment, only 43.6% demonstrated good protection practice [30]. These findings suggest that the effectiveness of eye protection depends not only on availability, but also on equipment quality, correct use, workplace enforcement, and workers' understanding of occupational hazards.

The multivariable analysis identified several factors that were independently associated with ocular injury, including welding type, occupational health and safety training, use of eye protective devices, workplace supervision, and awareness of ocular hazards.

In the present study, arc welders had approximately five times higher odds of experiencing ocular injury than gas welders. This finding is consistent with the study from Ghana, in which eye injury was considerably more common among electric or arc welders than among gas welders [17]. Arc welding produces intense ultraviolet and infrared radiation together with sparks and flying metal particles. Inadequate shielding or improper use of welding helmets may therefore expose workers to both radiation‐related and mechanical ocular injuries. The increased risk among arc welders supports the need for task‐specific risk assessment and stricter protection during high‐intensity welding activities.

Lack of occupational health and safety training was another important determinant. Welders who had not received training were more than six times as likely to report ocular injury as trained workers. Training enables workers to recognize radiation, sparks, fumes, and flying particles as potential hazards and teaches them how to select, fit, maintain, and correctly use protective devices. Evidence from sub‐Saharan Africa supports this relationship: welders who received occupational safety training were more likely to practice ocular protection, although the meta‐analysis also reported substantial variation between the included studies [4]. In Nepal, lack of formal workplace safety training was significantly associated with ocular trauma [7]. These findings indicate that training should be continuous and practical rather than limited to informal instructions provided when workers begin employment.

The use of eye protective devices was also found to be a significant determinant of ocular injury. Participants who did not regularly use protective eyewear had nearly fourfold greater odds of injury compared with those who consistently used such equipment. This finding supports previous studies from Ghana [17], Nigeria [38], and Nepal [39]. Protective eyewear serves as the primary barrier against ultraviolet radiation, infrared rays, sparks, dust particles, and metal fragments generated during welding activities. Despite its proven effectiveness, noncompliance remains common due to factors such as discomfort, poor fit, limited availability, financial constraints, and inadequate awareness regarding its protective benefits.

Workplace supervision emerged as another significant factor associated with ocular injury. Welders who worked in settings lacking regular supervision were more than twice as likely to experience eye injuries compared with those employed in supervised environments. Effective supervision helps ensure adherence to occupational health and safety guidelines, promotes the consistent use of personal protective equipment, and facilitates the early identification and correction of unsafe work practices [40]. In the absence of supervisory oversight, workers may become less compliant with safety procedures and more likely to engage in risky behaviors. Similar associations between inadequate supervision and increased occupational injury rates have been reported in previous workplace safety studies [41, 42].

Awareness regarding welding‐related ocular hazards was also significantly associated with injury occurrence. Participants with poor awareness were approximately four times more likely to sustain ocular injuries than those with adequate awareness. Comparable findings have been documented in studies from Kenya [43], and Ghana [17]. Workers who lack awareness of the harmful effects of welding radiation, flying debris, and other occupational hazards may be less inclined to adopt preventive measures or consistently use protective equipment. Consequently, inadequate knowledge can contribute directly to unsafe workplace behaviors and increased injury risk [13, 44].

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

One important strength of this study is the inclusion of welders from small‐scale industries, a group that is often overlooked in occupational health research despite their substantial exposure to workplace hazards. The institution‐based cross‐sectional design and adequate sample size also strengthened the statistical power and reliability of the findings. In addition, by examining a broad range of occupational, behavioral, and workplace‐related factors, the study provides a more comprehensive understanding of the factors associated with ocular injuries.

Some limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. First, work‐related ocular injuries were identified through self‐reported information, which may have introduced recall and social desirability biases. Participants may not have remembered previous injuries accurately, and minor injuries may have gone unreported if they were not considered medically serious. Moreover, because the reported injuries were not clinically or ophthalmologically verified, some cases may have been misclassified, affecting the accuracy of the estimated prevalence and observed associations.

Second, the cross‐sectional design does not allow causal relationships to be established between the identified factors and ocular injuries. Furthermore, welders who had left the occupation because of severe eye injuries were not included, which may have led to an underestimation of the true burden of occupational ocular injuries. Despite these limitations, the study offers valuable baseline evidence to guide occupational eye‐health interventions in the study area.

Conclusion

This study revealed that almost half of the participants (46.7%) experienced ocular injury. Being arc welder, absence of workplace supervision, lack of health and safety training, not using eye safety device and having poor awareness of welding‐related ocular injury were significantly associated with work‐related ocular injury. We recommend providing regular occupational health and safety training, strengthening workplace supervision, encouraging the consistent use of eye protective devices, and raising awareness of the potential hazards of welding to minimize and prevent welding‐related ocular injuries.

Ethics Statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Ethical Review Board of Debre Markos University, College of Medicine and Health Sciences (Reference No. IRB/688/2023). Official permission letters were secured from the relevant institutions before commencement of data collection. Prior to participation, the objectives and procedures of the study were clearly explained to all respondents, and verbal informed consent was obtained from each participant. For participants aged 15–17 years, verbal informed permission was obtained from a parent or legally authorized guardian, and verbal assent was obtained from each minor participant before participation, in accordance with the procedure approved by the Ethical Review Board. The verbal consent and assent were documented and witnessed as required by the Board. Participation was entirely voluntary, and respondents were informed of their right to decline participation or withdraw from the study at any stage without any consequences. To protect confidentiality, personal identifiers were excluded from the data collection instruments and coded identifiers were used throughout the study. Participants identified with ocular health problems during the survey were advised and referred to nearby eye care facilities for further evaluation and management.
