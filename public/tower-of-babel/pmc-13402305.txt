Targeted plasma proteomics reveals a central role of upregulated TNFRSF proteins in HIV-associated stroke
Authors: Tailin Chen, Haijiang Lin, Xiaoxiao Chen, Miaochen Wang, Haijun Zhang, Shanling Wang, Liangyou Wang, Tingting Wang, Yali Xie, Yating Wang, Jiyuan Ren, Zhiyi Zhang, Na He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic inflammation drives vascular aging and stroke risk, yet circulating proteins linking immune activation to cerebrovascular events remain elusive. People with HIV (PWH) face elevated stroke risk beyond traditional vascular risk factors, even under antiretroviral therapy. We aimed to identify circulating proteomic signatures of HIV-associated stroke, characterize underlying biological pathways, and assess their longitudinal stability. We conducted a nested case–control study of 135 PWH with stroke (HIV + /Stroke + , mean age 64.2 years, 85.9% ischemic) and two matched control groups (HIV + /Stroke− and HIV − /Stroke − , n = 135 each), with longitudinal validation in 37 PWH who subsequently developed stroke (78.4% ischemic). Among 184 plasma proteins measured using Olink Cardiovascular panels, 16 were associated with stroke in PWH, centrally involving the tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily (TNFRSF). TNFRSF proteins (TNFRSF10A, TRAIL-R2, TNFRSF14, and TNF-R1) were strongly inter-correlated (ρ > 0.77), with their co-expression module associated with stroke (adjusted OR = 1.75, P = 0.018). TNFRSF expression increased monotonically across HIV − /Stroke − , HIV + /Stroke − , and HIV + /Stroke+ groups. Longitudinal analysis confirmed post-stroke upregulation of these TNFRSF members, alongside MMP12 and TFF3, particularly in ischemic subtypes. These findings suggest coordinated TNFRSF upregulation as a potential signature associated with both HIV-related inflammation and cerebrovascular events, providing candidate biomarkers for future investigation.Subject terms: HIV infections, Proteomics, Stroke, Prognostic markers

Introduction

With the widespread implementation of early and effective antiretroviral therapy (ART), HIV infection has shifted from a progressive, life-threatening condition to a manageable chronic disease. However, this shift has been accompanied by a growing burden of non-communicable comorbidities, which often manifest clinically as premature aging phenotypes1. Even under sustained viral suppression, people with HIV (PWH) face a 1.5- to twofold increased risk of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), including myocardial infarction, heart failure, and stroke, compared to the general population2. Within this population, ischemic stroke predominates, with an incidence of 31.50 per 10,000 person-years compared with 4.43 per 10,000 person-years for hemorrhagic stroke3. A growing body of evidence supports HIV infection as an independent risk factor for stroke beyond conventional vascular risk factors4,5. As the HIV-positive population ages globally—with a substantial and growing proportion now aged 50 years and older—stroke represents an increasingly significant source of morbidity and mortality in this group5, underscoring the urgent need to understand the specific mechanisms through which HIV confers excess cerebrovascular risk.

Among older PWH, traditional vascular risk factors remain the primary drivers of stroke risk, as in the general population; however, the excess cerebrovascular risk observed in PWH beyond what is attributable to these conventional factors points to additional disease-specific mechanisms6. Stroke pathogenesis is multifactorial and involves the interplay of endothelial dysfunction, atherosclerotic plaque rupture, thrombosis, and neuroinflammation7. Among these, inflammation serves as a central pathological driver spanning the onset, progression, and outcomes of stroke. Inflammatory responses can promote atherosclerosis and plaque instability, thereby contributing to ischemic stroke, while also compromising the integrity of the cerebral vasculature and increasing the risk of hemorrhagic stroke. Thus, both acute and chronic inflammation play central roles in the development of cerebrovascular events8. While limited proteomic studies in the general population have linked ischemic stroke to dysregulated inflammatory pathways (e.g., interleukins)9, this inflammatory drive is particularly pronounced in the context of HIV. Despite effective ART, PWH exhibit persistent low-grade systemic inflammation and chronic immune activation—a state that closely mirrors the inflammaging observed in the elderly population. This pro-inflammatory milieu can induce endothelial injury, reduce vascular compliance, and increase blood–brain barrier permeability. This may accelerate vascular aging and arteriosclerosis, weaken the neurovascular unit, and ultimately increase the risk of both ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke, beyond what can be explained by traditional risk factors alone5. Dissecting how HIV-associated chronic inflammation contributes to cerebrovascular pathogenesis may therefore yield molecular insights relevant not only to PWH but also to the broader aging population in whom immunosenescence and chronic inflammation play similarly critical roles.

Although epidemiological data suggest a critical role for HIV-related inflammation in stroke pathogenesis5, the molecular mechanisms underlying this relationship are still not fully understood, particularly in terms of inflammatory signaling pathways and their regulation at the protein level. Recent advances in high-throughput proteomics using Olink proximity extension assay technology have provided a powerful platform for identifying biomarkers and pathogenic pathways relevant to cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases10,11. To capture the interplay between systemic immune activation and vascular pathology, we selected the complementary Olink Cardiovascular II and III panels, each designed to simultaneously measure 92 protein biomarkers related to CVD. While the Cardiovascular II panel focuses on established cardiovascular markers, the Cardiovascular III panel is optimized to provide comprehensive coverage of inflammatory and emerging candidate proteins. Given the current knowledge gaps regarding the molecular mechanisms linking HIV infection to stroke, this study aimed to identify plasma protein biomarkers, signaling pathways, and protein interaction networks associated with stroke and specifically with ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke in PWH. Furthermore, by leveraging a longitudinal cohort with paired samples collected before and after stroke onset, we aimed to validate key proteins and characterize the temporal dynamics of proteomic changes in relation to stroke onset. Our findings characterize the circulating proteomic landscape of HIV-associated stroke and reveal coordinated inflammatory protein alterations at the convergence of chronic immune activation and cerebrovascular pathology, with potential relevance to the broader aging population affected by chronic inflammatory conditions.

Results

Participant characteristics

Table 1 presents the baseline clinical and demographic characteristics of the study participants. The HIV+/Stroke+ group (n = 135) was predominantly male (82.2%) with a mean age of 64.2 ± 11.1 years, of whom 85.9% (116/135) had ischemic stroke. Compared with the HIV+/Stroke− group, HIV+/Stroke+ individuals were older (64.2 ± 11.1 vs. 59.6 ± 12.4 years, P = 0.002), while sex distribution and the prevalence of hypertension and renal impairment were comparable between groups. In terms of biochemical profiles, the HIV+/Stroke+ group exhibited higher levels of body mass index (BMI), glucose (GLU), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and total bilirubin (TBIL), but lower levels of triglycerides (TG) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) compared with the HIV+/Stroke− group. Regarding HIV-specific characteristics, both groups acquired the virus predominantly through heterosexual contact. The HIV+/Stroke+ group had lower CD4 + T cell counts and a lower rate of virological suppression, and a greater proportion were receiving tenofovir- or efavirenz-based regimens. Variables showing significant between-group differences were carried forward into multivariable analysis, and those that remained independently significant were incorporated as covariates in subsequent proteomic screening (Supplementary Table 1).

Proteomic signatures and enriched pathways of stroke in PWH

Stroke-related proteins in PWH

After adjusting for the identical set of covariates detailed in the “Methods” and Supplementary Table 1, 16 proteins were identified as significantly differentially expressed between the HIV+/Stroke+ and HIV+/Stroke− groups. All 16 stroke-associated proteins were upregulated in HIV+/Stroke+ (Fig. 1a). These included tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily members (TNFRSF10A, TRAIL-R2, TNFRSF14, TNF-R1), interleukins and chemokines (IL18, IL6, MCP-1, CCL3), matrix metalloproteinases (MMP9, MMP12), and other mediators of vascular and cellular injury (CSTB, Trefoil factor 3 (TFF3), IGFBP-2, LOX-1, KIM1, PD-L2). Among these, TFF3 exhibited the strongest association with stroke risk (adjusted OR = 4.39 [2.02–9.53]; Supplementary Data 1). Tenfold cross-validated receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis revealed that the 16-protein proteomic signature achieved a cross-validated AUC (CV-AUC) of 0.763, significantly outperforming a basic clinical model comprising age, sex, BMI, hypertension, and renal impairment (CV-AUC = 0.598; P = 1.46 × 10−4; Fig. 1b).

Stroke-related pathways in PWH

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of the 16 stroke-associated proteins, performed against a background of the 184 panel-quantified proteins, identified Viral protein interaction with cytokine and cytokine receptor as the most significantly enriched pathway (Supplementary Table 2 and Fig. 2a), with TNFRSF10A, TNFRSF14, TRAIL-R2, TNF-R1, MCP-1, IL18, IL6, and CCL3 as the contributing proteins (Fig. 2b). Influenza A and Salmonella infection were also significantly enriched.

PPI network analysis using the STRING database (filtered for interactions supported exclusively by experimentally determined evidence, curated databases, and protein homology) revealed three distinct functional clusters among the 16 proteins (Fig. 2c). Cluster 1 comprised IL6, IL18, MCP-1, and CCL3, enriched in Interleukin-10 signaling. Cluster 2 was characterized by Death receptor activity, comprising key members of the tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily (TNFRSF10A, TRAIL-R2, and TNF-R1). Cluster 3 comprised MMP9 and MMP12, sharing Hemopexin-like repeats. Several identified stroke-associated proteins (such as TFF3, LOX-1, and KIM1) lacked high-confidence interactions and were not assigned to any cluster.

Expression profiles and correlation of the stroke-associated proteins

To assess the impact of HIV infection and stroke on the relative abundance of the 16 identified proteins, we evaluated their expression patterns across the HIV−/Stroke−, HIV+/Stroke−, and HIV+/Stroke+ groups (Fig. 3a). Thirteen of the 16 proteins were expressed at significantly higher levels in PWH than in people without HIV; only KIM1, MCP-1, and MMP12 showed no significant difference between these two groups. The vast majority of the identified proteins—including TNFRSF10A, TRAIL-R2, CSTB, TFF3, IL18, TNFRSF14, IL6, MMP9, IGFBP-2, TNF-R1, CCL3, LOX-1, and PD-L2—exhibited a stepwise increase across the HIV−/Stroke−, HIV+/Stroke−, and HIV+/Stroke+ groups.

Based on their relative expression patterns across all individual samples, the standardized expression heatmap confirmed this pattern of progressive upregulation aligned with advancing disease status (Fig. 3b). Furthermore, correlation analysis revealed a tightly interconnected protein cluster comprising the TNFRSF members, TFF3, IGFBP-2, CCL3, and PD-L2. Within this highly correlated local network, the TNFRSF proteins exhibited strong positive pairwise correlations with one another and with TFF3 (Spearman’s ρ ranging from 0.77 to 0.90), forming a tight protein cluster (Fig. 3c).

Proteomic co-expression network analysis

WGCNA was performed based on all 184 detected proteins in PWH. The analysis identified three major co-expression modules (MEblue, MEbrown, and MEturquoise), alongside an unassigned background module (MEgrey; Fig. 4a). As depicted in the topological overlap matrix heatmap, proteins within each module were highly correlated, while correlations across modules were minimal (Fig. 4b). Module-trait relationship analysis revealed that the MEblue module was positively associated with stroke occurrence in PWH, whereas the MEbrown module showed a negative association (Fig. 4c). After multivariate adjustment, individuals with higher expression of proteins in the MEblue module had a significantly increased risk of stroke (adjusted OR = 1.75 [1.10–2.79]; Fig. 4d). Cytoscape visualization of the stroke-associated MEblue module revealed a tightly interconnected network comprising 14 proteins, predominantly composed of TNFRSF members (TNFRSF10A, TNFRSF11A, TNFRSF14, TNF-R1, TRAIL-R2, TNF-R2), which were highly co-expressed. Notably, all four differentially expressed TNFRSF proteins in HIV+/Stroke+ (TNF-R1, TNFRSF10A, TRAIL-R2, TNFRSF14) were included in this module (Fig. 4e).

Validation of stroke-related proteins by longitudinal stroke cohort

Longitudinal analysis of the 37 HIV+/Stroke+ individuals confirmed significant post-stroke upregulation of six proteins (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). Four TNFRSF proteins showed consistent elevation: TNF-R1 (OR = 1.45 [1.12–1.87], P = 0.004), TNFRSF10A (OR = 1.47 [1.10–1.97], P = 0.010), TRAIL-R2 (OR = 1.50 [1.07–2.11], P = 0.019), TNFRSF14 (OR = 1.40 [1.02–1.92], P = 0.038). TFF3 (OR = 1.53 [1.13–2.07], P = 0.006) and MMP12 (OR = 1.49 [1.14–1.93], P = 0.003) were also significantly upregulated following stroke onset.

Proteomic signatures of subtype strokes in PWH

Subtype stroke–related proteins in PWH

Stratified analyses further identified distinct sets of differentially expressed proteins for ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke in PWH (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). Baseline characteristics of the HIV+/Ischemic stroke+, HIV+/Hemorrhagic stroke+, and matched HIV+/Stroke− control groups are summarized in Supplementary Table 3. Notably, six TNFRSF family members (TNF-R1, TNFRSF10A, TRAIL-R2, TNFRSF14, TNFRSF11A, and TNF-R2) were linked to ischemic stroke. For hemorrhagic stroke, associated proteins included mediators of vascular remodeling and inflammation, such as osteoprotegerin (OPG), osteopontin (OPN), MER proto-oncogene tyrosine kinase (MERTK), myeloperoxidase (MPO), and pentraxin-3 (PTX3). Among the 16 stroke-related proteins initially identified in PWH, four (IGFBP-2, MCP-1, MMP9, and TNF-R1) were significantly associated with both stroke subtypes, while the remaining 12 were specific to ischemic stroke (Supplementary Fig. 2c).

Subtype stroke–related PPI network in PWH

PPI network analysis of the ischemic stroke-specific proteins revealed four distinct functional clusters (Supplementary Fig. 3). Clusters 2 and 3 comprised key TNFRSF members—TNF-R1, TNF-R2, TRAIL-R2, TNFRSF10A, and TNFRSF11A—enriched for TNF- and TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL)-mediated signaling, with dense intra- and inter-cluster connectivity. Cluster 1 contained cytokines and chemokines associated with viral protein interactions, including IL6, IL18, and CCL3. Cluster 4 comprised the matrix metalloproteinases MMP9 and MMP12. In contrast, hemorrhagic stroke-associated proteins did not form a connected interaction network.

Validation of ischemic stroke-related proteins by longitudinal stroke cohort

In a subgroup of 29 individuals with ischemic stroke, TRAIL-R2, TNF-R1, TNFRSF10A, and TNFRSF14 remained elevated after ischemic stroke, along with TFF3, MMP12, CSTB, KIM1, and IGFBP-2 (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d).

Discussion

This study characterized circulating proteomic signatures of stroke in PWH. Among 184 plasma proteins, 16 were significantly upregulated in PWH with stroke, with TNFRSF members (TNFRSF10A, TRAIL-R2, TNF-R1, and TNFRSF14) forming a tightly co-expressed hub at the center of the identified signature. Pathway and network analyses identified viral cytokine–receptor interaction and death receptor signaling as the most prominently enriched axes. These proteins exhibited a graded increase across the HIV−/Stroke−, HIV+/Stroke−, and HIV+/Stroke+ groups. Longitudinal within-individual analysis corroborated the cross-sectional findings, confirming post-stroke upregulation of TNFRSF members, MMP12, and TFF3. Subtype stratification revealed that ischemic stroke was predominantly associated with TNFRSF-mediated signaling, whereas hemorrhagic stroke exhibited a distinct program centered on oxidative stress and vascular remodeling. Collectively, our data suggest coordinated TNFRSF upregulation as a potential molecular signature associated with HIV-related inflammation and cerebrovascular events, a finding that may have relevance to the broader aging population in whom inflammaging plays a similarly central role, pending validation in independent cohorts.

Stroke in PWH was closely linked to four TNFRSF proteins—TNF-R1, TNFRSF10A, TRAIL-R2, and TNFRSF14—which mainly formed a distinct functional cluster characterized by death receptor activity in PPI network analysis. TNFRSF proteins mediate canonical death receptor signaling through ligand binding (e.g., TNF-α), activating downstream adaptor proteins and initiating caspase cascades that induce apoptosis12. While previous studies in the general population have primarily focused on TNF-α as a driver of vascular inflammation and stroke risk13, our findings build on this evidence by showing a coordinated upregulation of multiple TNFRSF members in PWH. TNF-R1 is the primary receptor mediating TNF-α signaling. Elevated serum soluble TNF-R1 impairs endothelial function, destabilizes atherosclerotic plaques, and is strongly associated with increased risk of CVD and stroke14,15. TNFRSF10A and TRAIL-R2 mediate TRAIL-induced apoptosis and contribute to cardiovascular pathology, with TRAIL-R2 serving as a marker of plaque cell apoptosis and adverse cardiovascular events16. TNFRSF14, also known as Herpesvirus Entry Mediator, regulates T-cell proliferation, immune activation, and inflammation17. Its ligands influence vascular and cardiac inflammatory states and have been independently linked to cardiovascular events in patients with stable coronary artery disease18. In WGCNA analysis, six TNFRSF proteins formed a highly co-expressed module, whose elevated expression was significantly associated with an increased risk of stroke in PWH. This integrative finding suggests that HIV-related immune activation may engage a broader network of TNFRSF-mediated signaling, with potential downstream effects on NF-κB and MAPK pathways, inflammation, and cerebrovascular integrity19.

Beyond the TNFRSF cluster, four cytokines and chemokines—IL6, IL18, MCP-1, and CCL3—were also significantly upregulated in PWH with stroke. These proteins may interact with death receptor signaling pathways to collectively amplify inflammation and vascular injury. In KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, these proteins, together with the four TNFRSF members, collectively drove the most significantly enriched pathway: Viral protein interaction with cytokine and cytokine receptor. Consistently, PPI network analysis grouped IL6, IL18, MCP-1, and CCL3 into a distinct functional cluster enriched in interleukin-10 signaling, highlighting their coordinated role in immune regulation and vascular inflammation. IL6, a prototypical proinflammatory cytokine, is induced via NF-κB activation by TNF-R1 and TNFRSF10A. It contributes to vascular inflammation and elevates the risk of cardiovascular events, including stroke20. IL18, regulated by TNF signaling, promotes plaque progression and cardiac remodeling, while in stroke, it exacerbates cerebrovascular inflammation and tissue injury by enhancing Th1 responses and the release of proinflammatory mediators21,22. MCP-1 (CCL2) is a potent monocyte and macrophage chemoattractant; elevated circulating MCP-1 promotes monocyte infiltration into vessel walls, accelerates atherosclerotic plaque formation, and has been independently associated with increased stroke risk23. Chemokines such as CCL3 and MCP-1 recruit monocytes, macrophages, and T cells, facilitating their infiltration into ischemic brain tissue, where they amplify local neuroinflammation and exacerbate tissue injury24. The co-identification of these cytokines and chemokines alongside the TNFRSF cluster in both pathway and network analyses is consistent with a coordinated inflammatory program in PWH, in which death receptor signaling and cytokine-driven immune recruitment may operate within a shared network associated with cerebrovascular pathology.

The stepwise upregulation of TNFRSF proteins and inflammatory mediators across HIV−/Stroke−, HIV+/Stroke−, and HIV+/Stroke+ groups reveals a distinctive pathophysiological pattern in PWH. This finding suggests that chronic HIV infection is associated with persistent activation of death receptor signaling. This activation may lead to the release of cytokines, the degradation of the extracellular matrix, and endothelial dysfunction, potentially creating a permissive environment for stroke. Previous evidence for this mechanism has largely come from experimental studies. Dysregulated expression of TNF-α and its receptors in PWH indicates sustained activation of the TNF system, which contributes to accelerated CD4 + T-cell depletion, enhanced HIV-1 replication, and the development of comorbidities25. HIV-related proteins such as gp120, Tat, and Nef can mimic or disrupt TNFR signaling, enabling persistent viral replication in infected cells while suppressing T-cell apoptosis to maintain viral proliferation. At advanced stages of disease, elevated TNF-α induces apoptosis of large numbers of uninfected T cells, leading to profound immunosuppression26,27. Furthermore, HIV proteins may amplify TNFR-mediated cytotoxic signaling, resulting in chronic tissue injury and immune exhaustion28. In parallel, plasmacytoid dendritic cells produce IFN-α upon viral stimulation, which can increase TRAIL expression on CD4 + T cells by up to tenfold, thereby inducing apoptosis of vascular smooth muscle cells, accelerating plaque destabilization, and elevating the risk of thrombosis and stroke29. These mechanisms may converge on a self-reinforcing immune–inflammation–vascular injury cascade, a model consistent with our KEGG enrichment finding that TNFRSF members and inflammatory proteins collectively participate in viral protein interaction with cytokines and cytokine receptors. Our study provides population-level observational evidence that TNFRSF-associated inflammatory signaling is associated with the severity of HIV-associated immune dysregulation and cerebrovascular disease burden in PWH, consistent with a potential cumulative process linking chronic immune activation to cerebrovascular injury.

We also identified additional proteins associated with stroke in PWH. PPI network analysis identified a third functional cluster comprising MMP9 and MMP12, both sharing hemopexin-like repeats. MMP9, an inducible matrix metalloproteinase, degrades vascular basement membranes and tight junction proteins, disrupts the blood–brain barrier, and destabilizes atherosclerotic plaques, thereby contributing to infarct expansion and hemorrhagic transformation30. MMP12, predominantly expressed in macrophages, promotes extracellular matrix remodeling and vascular inflammation, and has been implicated in plaque vulnerability and arterial wall remodeling in the context of chronic inflammatory conditions31. Their co-clustering in the PPI network suggests coordinated extracellular matrix degradation as a downstream effector of the broader inflammatory program identified in this study. PD-L2, an immune checkpoint molecule, regulates T-cell activation and immune responses. Experimental models of ischemic stroke have shown that mice deficient in PD-L1 and PD-L2 exhibit markedly reduced infarct volumes, along with lower levels of proinflammatory microglia, infiltrating monocytes, and CD4 + T cells32. LOX-1, the principal receptor for oxidized LDL, is upregulated after ischemic stroke and induces MMP9 expression, contributing to cerebral edema, infarct expansion, and neurological decline33. IGFBP-2, a regulator of the insulin-like growth factor (IGF) pathway, modulates angiogenesis and metabolic homeostasis. Its elevation has been linked to increased carotid intima–media thickness (cIMT)34. CSTB, a cysteine protease inhibitor, has been implicated in neuronal protection and inflammatory regulation35; its upregulation in the context of HIV-associated stroke may reflect a compensatory response to heightened proteolytic activity and neuronal stress. KIM1, a marker of tubular injury and systemic inflammation, has been associated with cardiovascular risk and endothelial dysfunction36, and may reflect the broader cardiorenal inflammatory burden in PWH. TFF3 exhibited the strongest association with stroke risk in our study (adjusted OR = 4.39). Recent studies have highlighted TFF3 as an emerging biomarker of CVD, showing associations with myocardial infarction and heart failure37; our findings provide evidence linking elevated plasma TFF3 levels to stroke, underscoring its potential involvement in cerebrovascular injury and systemic vascular risk38.

Subtype-stratified analyses revealed both shared and divergent proteomic features between ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke in PWH, with the primary combined analysis designed to maximize statistical power and the subtype analyses interpreted as complementary characterization of etiology-specific molecular features. Ischemic stroke remained closely associated with most TNFRSF proteins and inflammatory mediators. Importantly, our longitudinal ischemic stroke cohort confirmed the post-stroke upregulation of TNFRSF proteins, supporting the association between TNFRSF-mediated death receptor signaling and ischemic brain injury in the context of HIV infection. These findings align with observations in the general population, where TNFRSF signaling and apoptosis are central to ischemic stroke pathogenesis15, but our results are consistent with a potentially more pronounced activation in PWH, which may reflect chronic immune activation and priming of death receptor pathways. In contrast, hemorrhagic stroke shared only four proteins with the primary signature (IGFBP-2, MCP-1, MMP9, and TNF-R1), indicating a partial mechanistic divergence from ischemic stroke. The hemorrhagic stroke-specific proteins—ITGB1BP2, MERTK, MPO, OPG, OPN, PGLYRP1, PTX3, and XCL1—collectively reflect a distinct but equally inflammation-driven pathological program. MPO is a mediator of oxidative stress and contributes to microglial activation and vascular wall injury. MPO inhibition has been shown to improve outcomes in intracerebral hemorrhage39. OPN and OPG are matricellular proteins involved in vascular remodeling and calcification; their dysregulation has been linked to vascular fragility and cerebrovascular events40. PTX3, an acute-phase pentraxin, reflects amplified innate immune activation and has been implicated in cerebrovascular inflammation41. Other proteins—MERTK42 and PGLYRP143—together implicate innate immune activation, impaired inflammatory resolution, and lymphocyte-mediated vascular injury in cerebrovascular events but have not been systematically examined in HIV-related hemorrhagic stroke44. Taken together, while ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke share a common theme of dysregulated inflammation, the molecular drivers diverge substantially: ischemic stroke is characterized by coordinated TNFRSF-mediated death receptor activation, whereas hemorrhagic stroke reflects a broader program of oxidative stress, vascular remodeling, and impaired inflammatory resolution—a subtype-specific pattern that, if confirmed, may have implications for risk stratification in this high-risk population.

Longitudinal analysis confirmed the cross-sectional findings at the within-individual level, demonstrating significant post-stroke upregulation of TNFRSF members, MMP12, and TFF3 in PWH—indicating that these proteins not only distinguish stroke cases from controls cross-sectionally, but also change dynamically in relation to stroke onset within the same individuals. Although the longitudinal sub-cohort (n = 37) is nested within the HIV+/Stroke+ group rather than constituting an independent external cohort, the self-controlled paired design provides a methodologically distinct form of validation: by comparing each individual’s pre- and post-stroke proteomic profiles, this approach eliminates stable between-individual confounding and directly captures the temporal dynamics of protein expression relative to stroke onset45. The consistency between cross-sectional and longitudinal findings strengthens confidence in the identified signatures; nonetheless, replication in an independent external cohort remains an important priority for future work. These findings identify TNFRSF proteins as candidate molecular correlates of stroke in PWH that warrant prospective evaluation to determine their predictive value, and raise the possibility that TNFRSF-related signaling pathways may represent targets for future mechanistic investigation.

Several limitations need further consideration. First, although the two Olink Cardiovascular panels provided biologically targeted and analytically sensitive coverage of 184 proteins, this represents a fraction of the total plasma proteome. To mitigate ascertainment bias inherent to a cardiovascular-focused panel, pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the 184 quantified proteins as the background reference rather than the whole proteome; nonetheless, findings should be interpreted as pathway signals within a targeted protein space rather than an unbiased proteome-wide discovery, and future studies incorporating broader platforms would help contextualize these results. Second, although the longitudinal within-individual analysis provides a methodologically distinct form of validation, all analyses were conducted within a single cohort; no independent external cohort was available for replication. This lack of external validation limits the generalizability of the identified proteomic signatures, and replication in independent prospective cohorts with standardized pre-event sampling remains an important priority to confirm their robustness and establish temporal precedence. Third, subgroup analyses—particularly for hemorrhagic stroke—were constrained by relatively small sample size, which may have attenuated statistical power. Therefore, larger studies are needed to confirm and extend our observations. These limitations notwithstanding, the convergent evidence from cross-sectional discovery, pathway, and network analyses, longitudinal within-individual validation, and subtype stratification provides a multi-layered basis for the identified proteomic signatures, which warrant further investigation in larger and more diverse cohorts.

Our proteomic investigation revealed a potential coordinated TNFRSF-centered inflammatory signature associated with stroke in PWH, with viral cytokine–receptor interaction and death receptor signaling identified as prominently enriched axes in pathway analyses. The alterations in TNFRSF proteins were further supported by longitudinal within-individual analysis. Collectively, these findings suggest that coordinated TNFRSF-mediated and cytokine-driven inflammatory signaling tracks with cerebrovascular disease burden in PWH, providing a molecular framework for guiding future mechanistic and prospective investigation of HIV-associated stroke. Based on our population-based cohort, TNFRSF proteins and related inflammatory mediators merit prospective evaluation as potential indicators of cerebrovascular risk in PWH. Crucially, given the shared biology between HIV-associated immune activation and age-related immunosenescence, this inflammatory axis likely extends beyond HIV, offering a molecular framework that warrants investigation in the broader aging population burdened by chronic inflammation.

Methods

Study population and design

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the School of Public Health, Fudan University, China (IRB#2015-06-0553, IRB#2017-07-0629, IRB#2017-07-0629-S, IRB#2024-11-1179). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. No monetary compensation was provided to participants; however, all participants received free health examinations as part of the cohort study protocol. We selected 135 PWH who had experienced a stroke (HIV+/Stroke+) from the Comparative HIV and Aging Research in Taizhou cohort, which was described earlier46. Matching was conducted in two sequential steps using the MatchIt package in R. First, each HIV+/Stroke+ individual was matched 1:1 to an HIV-positive, stroke-free individual (HIV+/Stroke−) by age and sex using nearest-neighbor matching without replacement; this comparison was designed to identify stroke-associated proteomic signatures within the HIV-infected population, with HIV status held constant to isolate stroke-specific effects. Second, each HIV+/Stroke− individual was subsequently matched 1:1 to an HIV-negative, stroke-free control (HIV−/Stroke−) enrolled in the same cohort using identical criteria; this comparison was designed to characterize the independent contribution of HIV infection to the circulating proteomic profile, independent of stroke. No age caliper was imposed in either step, as our primary objective was to retain all available stroke cases (n = 135) to ensure adequate statistical power. A statistically significant difference in continuous age remained after matching; continuous age was therefore included as an obligatory a priori covariate in all subsequent multivariable models.

For cross-sectional proteomic analysis, blood samples collected closest to the date of stroke occurrence were used for the HIV+/Stroke+ group, as no standardized enrollment timepoint existed for stroke cases; samples collected at the baseline enrollment were used for both control groups. For longitudinal proteomic analysis, 37 individuals in the HIV+/Stroke+ group who had one or more stored blood samples collected after HIV confirmation but prior to stroke onset were included (18 with one sample and 19 with two samples); these represented all individuals within the cohort for whom paired pre- and post-stroke proteomic data were available. As this sub-cohort is nested within the HIV+/Stroke+ group by design, the longitudinal analysis serves as a within-individual, self-controlled validation of the cross-sectional findings, with each participant serving as their own control across the pre- and post-stroke timepoints; this paired design inherently controls for stable between-individual confounders and provides greater statistical power per participant than independent cross-sectional comparisons47,48. A flowchart of the study design is shown in Fig. 5.

Flowchart of study participants and analytical procedures.

Schematic overview of the study design. Three age- and sex-matched groups of 135 individuals each were enrolled: people with HIV who had documented stroke (HIV+/Stroke+), people with HIV without stroke (HIV+/Stroke−), and HIV-negative individuals without stroke (HIV−/Stroke−). Plasma from all participants was profiled with the Olink Cardiovascular II and III panels (184 unique proteins) and used in four analyses. Analysis 1 identified stroke-related proteins through differential expression between HIV+/Stroke+ and HIV+/Stroke−, followed by KEGG pathway enrichment and protein–protein interaction analyses. Analysis 2 applied weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) among PWH to distinguish stroke-related from HIV-related signatures. Analysis 3 separately compared ischemic (n = 116) and hemorrhagic (n = 19) cases with newly age- and sex-matched HIV+/Stroke− controls at 1:1 and 1:4 ratios, respectively. Analysis 4 used generalized estimating equation models in 37 HIV+/Stroke+ individuals (18 with one pre-stroke sample, 19 with two) to test within-individual changes from pre-stroke to post-stroke timepoints.

Data collection and measurements

Data were collected through standardized face-to-face interviews using structured questionnaires, and physical examinations were performed by trained health professionals. Biological sex, determined via self-report during standardized face-to-face questionnaire interviews, was explicitly considered in the study design as both a matching variable for control selection and a covariate in all multivariable analyses. The study population was predominantly male (82.2% in the HIV+/Stroke+ group). Due to the limited number of female participants, sex-stratified proteomic analyses were not performed. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). Fasting blood samples were collected during the survey and were immediately stored at −80 °C. Serum creatinine (SCR), TG, TC, HB, GLU, ALT, AST, and TBIL were measured using a Beckman Coulter automated biochemical analyzer.

HIV-related clinical information, including mode of transmission, time since diagnosis, ART regimens, and CD4 + T cell counts, was retrieved from the Chinese National HIV/AIDS Case Reporting and Information Management System.

Stroke diagnosis, including subtype classification and date of onset, was ascertained from the local Center for Disease Control and Prevention chronic disease registration and management system, which captures stroke events diagnosed at designated hospitals as part of China’s national chronic disease monitoring program. All included stroke cases were required to have a formal diagnosis certificate or neuroimaging documentation issued by a secondary or higher-level medical institution (hospital level II or above). To ensure diagnostic validity, the medical records of all included stroke cases were clinically adjudicated by qualified neurologists, requiring definitive neuroimaging confirmation (CT or MRI) of the acute event and its subtype, in accordance with the Chinese diagnostic criteria for cerebrovascular diseases and consistent with the World Health Organization definition of stroke. Cases lacking definitive neuroimaging evidence were excluded. This structured registry, combined with institutional certification requirements and neuroimaging-based adjudication, established a standardized and clinically verified basis for case ascertainment.

Proteomic profiling

Plasma samples were collected in EDTA tubes, stored at −80 °C without prior thawing, and subjected to proteomic analysis. To maximize analytical coverage, all samples were analyzed simultaneously using both the Olink Target 96 Cardiovascular II and Cardiovascular III panels. Because these two panels consist of entirely distinct, non-overlapping sets of 92 target proteins, using two different panels inherently does not introduce inter-panel batch effects for any individual protein abundance measurement. This platform enables the comprehensive quantification of 184 unique cardiovascular-related protein biomarkers. Each target protein was recognized by a pair of antibodies conjugated with unique DNA oligonucleotide barcodes, followed by quantification using the Biomark HD system (Fluidigm, South San Francisco, CA), a high-throughput microfluidic real-time PCR instrument.

Rigorous quality control (QC) procedures were applied at both the plate and individual sample levels. Samples were distributed across 6 assay plates per panel. Each sample included four internal controls (two incubation controls, one extension control, and one detection control) to accurately monitor the immunoreaction, extension, and amplification/detection steps within each specific well. Each plate also contained dedicated external controls in separate wells: three inter-plate controls, three negative controls for background noise monitoring and limit of detection (LOD) calculation, and duplicated sample controls (pooled plasma) for variance assessment. Sample plates were evaluated based on the standard deviation of internal controls (< 0.2 NPX), and individual sample quality was assessed by evaluating deviations from the median control values (<0.3 NPX). Based on the dedicated sample controls, overall intra-assay coefficients of variation (CV) were 4% for both panels (reference threshold: <15%), and inter-assay CVs were 11% and 10% for Cardiovascular II and III, respectively (reference threshold: <25%), demonstrating satisfactory technical reproducibility. For the longitudinal sub-cohort, paired pre-stroke and post-stroke samples from the same individual were assigned to the same analytical plate to fundamentally eliminate plate-to-plate batch effects for within-subject comparisons.

Protein expression levels were reported as normalized protein expression (NPX) values, which are log2-scaled and relative measures, not reflecting absolute concentrations. Higher NPX values correspond to higher protein abundance. Values below the LOD were retained for downstream analyses in accordance with Olink’s official guidelines, which indicate that inclusion of sub-LOD values does not inflate false-positive discovery rates; the proportion of sub-LOD data points per protein is deposited in the Proteomics Identifications Database (PRIDE). Furthermore, to eliminate potential intra-panel batch effects across the 6 different assay plates, raw data were processed using Olink’s Intensity Normalization (v.2) method. Briefly, intra-plate technical variations were adjusted using built-in internal extension controls, and inter-plate variations were normalized by aligning the plate-specific median of each assay to the overall global median. The full quantification matrix containing NPX values, LOD thresholds, and individual sample QC results is also deposited in PRIDE (see “Data Availability”).

Statistical analysis

Baseline non-proteomic characteristics were compared across three groups: HIV+/Stroke+, HIV+/Stroke−, and HIV−/Stroke−. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. For continuous variables, those with a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using independent sample t-tests; non-normally distributed variables were presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) and analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Identification of differentially expressed proteins

Differential expression analysis was conducted cross-sectionally between the HIV+/Stroke+ and HIV+/Stroke− groups. Prior to screening for differentially expressed proteins, we rigorously controlled for non-proteomic covariates. Candidate covariates were first screened from sociodemographic, biochemical, HIV-related, and comorbidity variables using univariate analysis; those with P < 0.05 were subsequently entered into a multivariable model. Variables that remained independently significant were retained, alongside established a priori stroke risk factors, as the final covariate set. Each protein was evaluated using the following multivariable logistic regression model: logit(P(Stroke = 1)) = β0 + β1(Protein NPX) + Σβk(Covariatesk), where Covariatesk includes age, sex, hypertension, and renal impairment (included a priori as established stroke risk factors irrespective of univariate significance), and time since HIV diagnosis, TG, ALT, AST, TBIL, BMI, and virological suppression (retained following univariate screening). Significance was defined as a false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P < 0.05. Because Olink NPX values are inherently log2-scaled, the log2 fold change (log2FC) was calculated directly as the mean NPX difference between groups (log2FC = Mean NPXHIV+/Stroke+–Mean NPXHIV+/Stroke−), yielding fold change magnitudes consistent with those reported in comparable Olink-based proteomics studies49.

Because circulating inflammatory biomarkers often operate in highly co-regulated networks, conventional univariate approaches may fail to capture the multivariate structure of stroke-associated proteomic signatures in the presence of high inter-protein collinearity. We therefore concurrently applied orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), a supervised multivariate method widely used in metabolomic and proteomic biomarker discovery to address inherent multicollinearity50. This multivariate technique robustly isolates predictive variation associated with stroke from orthogonal, non-predictive systemic noise, enabling the extraction of true biological signatures amidst the high heterogeneity of PWH. Features with a variable importance in projection (VIP) score > 1 were considered meaningful contributors to group separation. To maximize analytical stringency and minimize false positives, proteins were classified as significantly differentially expressed only if they met both the independent statistical criterion (FDR < 0.05) and the multivariate discriminative threshold (VIP > 1). This dual criterion ensures that reported proteins are statistically significant at the individual level and contribute meaningfully to the overall multivariate separation, reducing the risk of identifying proteins driven by correlated noise rather than true biological signal (Supplementary Fig. 4).

To evaluate discriminative performance, tenfold cross-validated ROC analysis compared three models: the proteomic signature, a combined model, and a basic clinical model. To reflect practical utility rather than exhaustive biological confounding, the clinical model only included readily accessible, routinely collected baseline factors (age, sex, BMI, hypertension, and renal impairment). CV-AUCs were compared using DeLong’s test with Bonferroni correction.

Protein-protein interaction network and pathway enrichment analysis

To explore the functional connectivity of the differentially expressed proteins, we constructed a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network using the STRING database (v12.0)51,52. To ensure high reliability, network edges were strictly limited to interactions supported by experimentally determined evidence, curated databases, and protein homology, applying a minimum required interaction score of 0.400. Functional clusters were identified using the Markov Cluster (MCL) algorithm based on stochastic flow simulation, with an inflation parameter of 3.

For functional evaluation, we performed KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. To prevent the analytical bias inherent to targeted proteomics, the enrichment background was restricted exclusively to the 184 unique proteins measured by the two Olink panels, rather than the entire human proteome. This custom background ensures that the enriched pathways reflect true disease-driven biological differences rather than the predefined cardiovascular focus of the assay. Pathways with FDR < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Correlation and clustering of differentially expressed proteins

To examine how the differentially expressed proteins relate to each other, we evaluated their localized expression patterns across the HIV+/Stroke+, HIV+/Stroke−, and HIV−/Stroke− groups, utilizing boxplots to visually compare their relative expression levels and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests to evaluate statistical significance. We calculated Spearman correlation coefficients specifically among these 16 significant proteins to assess their direct pairwise relationships. Hierarchical clustering based on Spearman correlation distance and the Ward.D2 agglomeration method was then applied to group these proteins into distinct clusters sharing similar expression trends.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

In a separate analysis aimed at the global proteomic profile, we applied WGCNA to the complete dataset of all 184 quantified proteins to identify broader co-expression modules associated with stroke risk. We first constructed a signed network by calculating pairwise correlations across all 184 proteins. The resulting adjacency matrix was raised to a soft-thresholding power to emphasize strong connections and penalize weak ones. This was then transformed into a topological overlap matrix (TOM) to quantify network interconnectedness. Modules of co-expressed proteins were identified through hierarchical clustering of TOM-based dissimilarity.

To evaluate the association between these global protein modules and stroke, the module eigengene (the first principal component summarizing the expression profile of a given module) was extracted. Both univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to determine the OR and 95% confidence interval for each module eigengene with respect to stroke status. Multivariable models were adjusted for the identical set of covariates previously described in the differential expression analysis. Highly interconnected modules were exported for visualization in Cytoscape. In these network diagrams, connection strength (edge weight) between two proteins represents their pairwise TOM value, which captures topological overlap and shared network neighborhood within the module. To highlight the most robust co-expression relationships and filter out weak associations, a minimum TOM threshold of 0.02 was applied for edge visualization.

Subgroup and longitudinal validation analyses

To account for the distinct pathophysiological mechanisms underlying different stroke etiologies, subgroup analyses were conducted separately for ischemic (HIV+/Ischemic stroke+) and hemorrhagic stroke (HIV+/Hemorrhagic stroke+) cases. Due to the reduced sample sizes in these subgroups, the threshold for differential protein expression was adapted to adjusted P < 0.05 and VIP > 1 to maintain discriminative robustness. Covariates were independently re-evaluated for each subtype using the same screening procedure described above (Supplementary Fig. 2). In this analysis, 116 HIV+/Ischemic stroke+ participants were 1:1 matched by age and sex with HIV+/Stroke− controls, while 19 HIV+/Hemorrhagic stroke+ participants were age- and sex-matched at 1:4 with HIV+/Stroke− controls to maximize statistical power.

To validate the cross-sectional proteomic findings, longitudinal self-controlled analyses were conducted using 37 individuals nested within the HIV+/Stroke+ group who had paired plasma samples before and after stroke onset. Generalized estimating equation models with a binomial distribution and logit link function were used to assess within-individual changes in protein expression across the pre- and post-stroke timepoints, with stroke occurrence as the binary outcome. An exchangeable working correlation structure was specified to accommodate the unbalanced repeated-measures design, in which participants contributed either two or three longitudinal observations, and robust sandwich standard errors were used to ensure valid inference. To adjust for variable sampling intervals, natural cubic splines were introduced to model the time variable flexibly. A significance threshold of P < 0.05 was applied. An identical analysis was performed in the subset of 29 HIV+/Ischemic stroke+ individuals with longitudinally paired samples to examine subtype-specific temporal protein dynamics.
