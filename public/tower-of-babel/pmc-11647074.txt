Autologous simple limbal stem cell transplantation with conjunctival autograft in chronic unilateral total LSCD with severe symblepharon due to severe alkali burn
Authors: Shefali Mazumdar, Richa Chauhan, Shelly Chaudhary
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis report details the case of a middle-aged man with chronic unilateral total limbal stem cell deficiency with severe symblepharon in his left eye with upper lid mechanical ptosis, restricted ocular movements and visual acuity limited to counting fingers at 2 feet. 9 months prior to his first visit, he sustained an accidental unilateral alkali injury to the left eye. He underwent autologous simple limbal epithelial transplantation combined with conjunctival autograft in the left eye. Complete ocular surface epithelialisation was achieved within 24 days post surgery. After 24 months of follow-up, the patient exhibited a stable ocular surface, normal palpebral fissure and full range of eye movements, with a best-corrected visual acuity of 6/12p in the left eye.Keywords: Ophthalmology, Transplantation

Background

The corneal epithelium is a specialised, transparent, non-keratinized and stratified squamous epithelium that is constantly regenerating and renewing its cells. Limbal stem cells, which are located in a specific spot called the limbal Palisades of Vogt, are crucial for the maintenance of corneal transparency and the repair and renewal of the corneal epithelium.1 If the Limbal stem cells are directly damaged or if the Palisades of Vogt are destroyed, it can lead to limbal stem cell deficiency (LSCD). This, in turn, can compromise the barrier function of the limbus and the corneal epithelium is replaced with conjunctival epithelial cells, which is a characteristic feature of LSCD.2 This can cause progressive vascularisation, pannus formation and corneal scarring, leading to visual impairment and even blindness. LSCD can be primary, caused by genetic mutations such as aniridia, or secondary, caused by various external factors that directly destroy LSCs such as chemical or thermal injuries, ocular cicatricial pemphigoid, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, multiple surgeries or cryotherapies, contact lens wear and extensive microbial infection.3 Ocular burns, which can be caused by chemicals or heat, are responsible for a significant percentage of eye injuries, ranging from 7.7% to 18%.4 They are one of the main causes of secondary LSCD. Severe burns can cause extensive damage to the ocular surface and limbal stem cells, leading to visual impairment and disfigurement even with the best possible management in the acute stage. In the chronic phase, to reverse LSCD and restore a normal corneal surface, there are various treatment options available such as conjunctival limbal autograft, keratolimbal allograft, cultivated limbal epithelial transplantation and simple limbal epithelial transplantation (SLET).1 5 Among these, SLET is a simple yet advanced technique developed by Dr Sangwan to manage total limbal stem cell damage.6 Depending on the donor tissue source, SLET can either be autologous (from the unaffected eye of the same person) for unilateral total LSCD or allogeneic (from another person, preferably a live related donor or cadaveric) for bilateral total LSCD.7 Here, we present this case of total unilateral LSCD following chemical injury, treated successfully with autologous SLET (Auto-SLET) with conjunctival autograft (CAG).

Case presentation

A middle-aged man presented with chronic total LSCD in his left eye, accompanied by upper lid mechanical ptosis and restricted movements in downward and outward gaze, with visual acuity of counting fingers at 2 feet (figure 1). His right eye was normal, with unaided visual acuity of 6/6. 9 months prior, he suffered a severe alkali injury to the left eye while whitewashing his house, treated conservatively with oral antibiotics,anti inflammatory drugs, topical antibiotics and topical lubricants in his town but no surgery due to COVID-19 lockdown. Over time, a mass covered his cornea, leading to vision decline. He visited our department in 2022 and was enrolled in a trial on Auto-SLET and registered with CTRI (CTRI/2021/11/037724). On slit-lamp examination, the left eye exhibited 360° of corneal vascularisation and grade 3 symblepharon from 10 to 5 o’clock (figure 2).

Treatment

Due to severe symblepharon extending to the centre of the cornea, an autologous limbal stem cell transplantation (SLET) combined with a CAG was planned in a single-stage surgery to minimise the recurrence of symblepharon risk. After obtaining written consent, the procedure was performed 12-month postchemical injury under local anaesthesia, using a peribulbar block with a mixture of 2% lidocaine and 0.5% bupivacaine for both eyes. Pannus resection in the recipient’s eye

Brimonidine tartrate 0.15% was applied for decongestion 5 min prior to surgery. Due to severe symblepharon, a limited peritomy was performed to release it before placing the speculum. Deep dissection commenced 1–2 mm behind the superior limbus, using Vannas scissors and Lim’s forceps, to remove the pannus from the whole of the cornea and clear fibrosed tissues up to 2–3 mm from the limbus while preserving healthy conjunctiva. The eye was then closed, and a wet gauze was placed over the eyelids.

Harvesting the CAG and the limbal biopsy from the donor’s (fellow) eye of the patient

After inserting the speculum, a 10 mm section at the superior limbus was marked. A 0.25 mL injection of 2% Xylocaine was given 5 mm behind the limbus to separate the conjunctiva from Tenon’s capsule. A limbus-based conjunctival flap was then raised using Vannas scissors and McPherson forceps, followed by dissection into the clear cornea with a crescent knife to obtain a 4 mm limbal biopsy. The 10 mm conjunctival graft and the 4 mm limbal biopsy were preserved in a balanced salt solution (BSS). Finally, gentamicin and dexamethasone were injected into the superior conjunctiva, and a bandage contact lens (BCL) was placed for 1 week.

Placement of CAG, amniotic membrane and limbal biopsy pieces over the recipient cornea

After reinserting the speculum in the LE, the harvested 10 mm CAG was cut into two horizontal pieces, placed around the limbus between 10- 5 o’clock (the site of preoperative symblepharon) and secured with fibrin glue. Then human amniotic membrane was positioned with the basement membrane side up over the ocular surface and CAG. Amniotic membrane was secured with fibrin glue, trimmed as needed and tucked into the conjunctival pockets all around. A running suture with 10–0 nylon was placed around the cornea, starting just behind the CAG, for extra support to the CAG and amniotic membrane. The limbal biopsy tissue was then cut into eight tiny pieces and positioned equidistantly in a circle on the cornea in the mid-periphery, secured with fibrin glue. After 1 min for polymerization, a soft BCL was applied, followed by temporary central tarsorrhaphy with 4–0 silk.

Outcome and follow-up

On the day of surgery, oral antibiotics and anti-inflammatory medication were initiated for 5 days. In the donor eye, moxifloxacin 0.5%, CMC 0.5% and loteprednol 0.5% drops were administered four times a day for a week postoperative. In the recipient’s eye, prednisolone acetate 1% drops were given every hour for 15 days, tapering over 6 months to a maintenance dose of two times per day for 1 year, then once daily. Moxifloxacin 0.5% was prescribed six times per day until complete ocular surface epithelialisation, and sodium hyaluronate 0.1% was used six times per day for 1 year, then four times a day thereafter. Follow-ups included weekly visits for the first month, biweekly for 3 months and monthly thereafter. Tarsorrhaphy was opened on day 15, with complete epithelialisation achieved in 24 days and a UCVA of 5/60 (figure 5). A small granuloma developed at the symblepharon site within 2 months, which persisted despite increased topical steroid use. At 4 months, oral steroids (60 mg per day) were introduced, with a weekly taper, leading to granuloma resolution (figure 6). After 1 year, tacrolimus 0.1% ointment was added. At 24 months, the patient had a stable ocular surface, normal eye movements and a best-corrected visual acuity of 6/12p in the left eye (figures7 8).

Discussion

The objective of this case report is to share our experience with ocular surface reconstruction using autologous stem cell transplantation (SLET) combined with CAG in a patient with total LSCD with severe symblepharon following a severe unilateral alkali burn. A retrospective study by Kate et al identified alkali burns (chuna injuries) as the most common cause of chemical injuries in India.8 In our case, the patient received no surgical intervention or even adequate ocular surface irrigation with BSS during the acute phase, highlighting a concerning lack of awareness among general ophthalmologists about the critical need for copious irrigation to normalise pH, as well as the role of amniotic membrane transplantation and tenonplasty in acute chemical injuries. These interventions could prevent blinding sequelae and enhance the success rates of subsequent Auto-SLET procedures in the chronic phase.9 10 In this patient, the interval between the chemical injury and SLET surgery was 12 months. Singh and Sangwan emphasised in their minireview the importance of timing for Auto-SLET and its correlation with procedural success.7 During SLET surgery, the most critical steps are the resection of the pannus and the management of symblepharon. Releasing symblepharon close to the cornea with Vannas scissors is essential, as this approach minimises conjunctival excision compared with release near the fornix, which can result in the unnecessary removal of larger conjunctival sections. Preserving as much healthy conjunctiva as possible is advisable to optimise surgical outcomes.11 In our case, the simultaneous Auto-SLET with CAG performed as a single-stage surgery was uneventful and effective. Despite the development of a large granuloma in the postoperative period, no additional surgical intervention was required, as the CAG served as a barrier, preventing the granuloma from reaching the cornea, which ultimately resolved with medical therapy alone. Shanbhag et al emphasised the necessity of additional CAG in cases of severe symblepharon, either concurrently or in a staged procedure after 3–4 months.12 13 For our patient, the 4 mm limbal biopsy was divided into 8 small pieces, while many studies suggest using 10–16 pieces.12,14 Although Shanbhag et al recommended tarsorrhaphy for children, we opted for it in this adult patient as an additional protective measure for the SLET grafts.12 The ocular surface achieved epithelialisation and stabilisation within 24 days post-surgery, comparable to other studies that reported epithelialisation times ranging from 8 to 42 days.15,17 A pyogenic granuloma developed at the site of severe preoperative symblepharon, which resolved with increased topical steroid treatment (1% prednisolone acetate hourly) and the addition of oral steroids with a tapering regime over 6 weeks. This case highlights the crucial role of CAG in preventing granuloma formation that could affect the cornea, given that the conjunctiva is a mature membrane acting as a barrier. The donor’s fellow eye remained healthy throughout the follow-up period, with no reported serious adverse outcomes. Our patient completed 2 years of postoperative follow-up with a stable ocular surface and best-corrected visual acuity of 6/12p in the left eye. Sayan Basu’s extensive series at LVPEI demonstrated that autologous SLET is a long-lasting solution for ocular surface reconstruction.18

Patient’s perspective

I sustained an accidental chuna injury to my left eye three years ago while whitewashing at home. Initially, I took treatment from a local private practitioner, who diagnosed the injury as serious and provided medical treatment. Over time, a mass developed and covered the cornea, causing my vision to deteriorate to one meter despite consulting with several eye specialists. The mass also caused difficulty in fully opening and moving my left eye. I then sought treatment at S.N. Medical College and consulted with Dr Shefali, who performed surgery on my eye. She explained that she would take a small limbal tissue from my other healthy eye. Although I had concerns, she assured me that there would be no harm to my other eye. I have completed two years of follow-up, and my both eyes are perfectly healthy with good vision. I am immensely grateful to Dr. Shefali for everything she has done for me.

Learning Points

Autologous simple limbal epithelial transplantation (SLET) with conjunctival autograft is a straightforward, reproducible and effective surgical procedure. It gives promising surgical and visual outcomes in cases of unilateral total limbal stem cell deficiency with severe symblepharon.

For optimal results, the affected eye should be quiet at the time of Autologous SLET surgery and there should be a minimum interval of 1 year between the initial injury and the SLET procedure.
