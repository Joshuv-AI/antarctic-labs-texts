Insights into Enzymes Involved in the Biosynthesis of Protopanaxadiol-Type Ginsenosides in Panax Species
Authors: Na Li, Xingyi Jiang, Shisong Gao, Qin Chen, Feng Ge, Xiuming Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Protopanaxadiol (PPD)-type ginsenosides, as the primary bioactive components of Panax medicinal plants, have become a research hotspot in natural products due to their diverse pharmacological activities. The biosynthetic pathway of PPD-type ginsenosides in Panax species and the related enzymes is becoming increasingly clear. However, for most enzymes, research has been largely limited to identifying their functions in the pathway, with insufficient exploration of their biochemical properties, structures, and mechanisms. This review, from the perspective of enzymatic catalysis, systematically elucidates the biosynthetic pathway of PPD-type ginsenosides in Panax species. It focuses on the functions, sequence, and biochemical features of the enzymes and uses homology modeling and AlphaFold predictions to generate testable hypotheses regarding their structures and catalytic mechanisms, while also summarizing directed evolution strategies, aiming to provide a theoretical foundation for the construction of in vitro or microbial “cell factories” and to promote the efficient synthesis and application of highly bioactive PPD-type ginsenosides.

Introduction

Panax ginseng C.A. Meyer, Panax quinquefolius L., Panax notoginseng (Burk.) F.H. Chen, and Panax japonicus (T. Nees) C.A. Mey. are among the best-studied medicinal Panax species, and their major pharmacological activities are primarily attributed to ginsenosides. Ginsenosides are triterpenoid saponins composed of an aglycone and one or more sugar moieties and are generally classified into dammarane-, oleanane (OA)-, and ocotillol (OT)-type compounds. Among them, dammarane-type ginsenosides dominate, with substitution at C3/C20 producing protopanaxadiol (PPD)-type ginsenosides and substitution at C6/C20 producing protopanaxatriol (PPT)-type ginsenosides (Tables and ). Depending on the type and number of sugar moieties attached, these can be further divided into various ginsenosides (Tables and ).

PPD-type ginsenosides display broad pharmacological activities, including anticancer, anti-inflammatory, hepatoprotective, antidiabetic, antistress, antiaging, and antioxidant effects, as well as mitigation of chemotherapy-associated side effects. Recent studies have further highlighted the medicinal and food-related value of P. ginseng products and P. notoginseng-derived preparations and saponins. − Their bioactivity is closely linked to glycosylation pattern and aglycone structure. For example, CK and Rh2 differ in glycosylation position and have each been reported to exhibit diverse biological activities. − Rare ginsenosides are generally more bioavailable and more active than major ginsenosides, yet they occur only in trace amounts in Panax plants. , This limitation has intensified interest in synthetic biology and engineered microbial cell factories for the production of target PPD-type ginsenosides. Because enzyme selectivity and catalytic efficiency directly determine the aglycone oxidation pattern and the position, identity, and number of appended sugars, systematic understanding of these enzymes is essential not only for explaining structure–activity differences but also for the rational biosynthesis of target PPD-type ginsenosides with defined glycosylation patterns.

The biosynthesis of PPD-type ginsenosides mainly proceeds through the cytosolic mevalonate (MVA) pathway, although the plastidial MEP pathway may also contribute to precursor supply. As outlined in Figure , acetyl-CoA is converted into isopentenyl pyrophosphate (IPP) and dimethylallyl diphosphate (DMAPP); these C5 units are elongated to farnesyl diphosphate (FPP), which is then transformed into squalene, 2,3-oxidosqualene, and dammarenediol-II (DAD), followed by cytochrome P450 (CYP)-mediated oxidation to PPD and uridine diphosphate glycosyltransferase (UGT)-mediated glycosylation to diverse PPD-type ginsenosides. Accordingly, the pathway can be divided into three modules: the MVA pathway, isoprenoid chain elongation and polymerization, and the cyclization, oxidation, and glycosylation of 2,3-oxidosqualene.

Although many studies and reviews have addressed ginsenoside biosynthesis in Panax species, most have emphasized gene discovery, expression profiling, and pathway regulation. ,− In contrast, this review focuses on the catalytic logic of the enzymes involved in PPD-type ginsenoside biosynthesis, with emphasis on biochemical features, structural characteristics, catalytic mechanisms, and recent progress in enzyme engineering. For structural interpretation, protein sequences were modeled using experimentally resolved homologues and, where available, cross-checked against AlphaFold predictions, while putative active-site residues were assigned through sequence alignment, conserved-motif analysis, and structural superposition; because high-resolution experimental structures are unavailable for most Panax enzymes, these models and residue assignments should be regarded as testable hypotheses rather than experimentally validated conclusions. Such knowledge is directly relevant to both microbial production and in vitro cascade synthesis of target ginsenosides.

Enzymes of the same class are generally highly conserved among Panax species. By contrast, branch-point enzymes such as DDS and β-amyrin synthase, although homologous, show lower sequence similarity (Figure ), likely because differences in their active-site contours alter carbocation stabilization and thereby enforce distinct cyclization outcomes. , Comparative analysis of enzyme function, structure, and catalytic logic is therefore central to pathway optimization and enzyme engineering.

Mevalonate Pathway

The MVA pathway is the principal cytosolic route supplying C5 units for triterpenoid saponin biosynthesis. Starting from acetyl-CoA, AACT, HMGS, and the rate-limiting HMGR generate mevalonate, which is subsequently phosphorylated by MK and PMK and decarboxylated by MVD to form IPP; IDI then interconverts IPP and DMAPP. Because this module controls precursor supply and upstream carbon flux, it is a major target in metabolic engineering strategies for ginsenoside production.

Acetyl-CoA Acyltransferase

AACT catalyzes the entry step of the cytosolic MVA pathway and primarily determines upstream precursor supply rather than downstream product specificity. Transcriptomic and spatial studies indicate that AACT expression is associated with ginsenoside biosynthesis in P. ginseng and is preferentially enriched in roots of P. japonicus, supporting a role in root-biased saponin formation. ,−

The in vitro catalytic properties and structural features of AACTs from Panax species remain largely unexplored. Structural modeling of PnAACT (AIK21787.1) based on Aspergillus fumigatus thiolase (afCT, PDB ID: 6ARG) suggests a putative tetrameric architecture, with His364 as a predicted catalytic residue corresponding to His354 in afCT (Figure a). Ala259, corresponding to Ala249 in afCT, lies in a loop region and is predicted to interact with coenzyme A; together with K+, it may contribute to stabilization of both the catalytic His and the substrate. Cl– at the dimer interface has also been implicated in active-site stabilization,49 suggesting that KCl-containing buffers may enhance activity in vitro. Compared with plant AACTs, microbial AACTs are better characterized and may therefore provide useful mechanistic references or even alternative enzymes for in vitro biosynthetic applications. ,

Mechanistically, AACT belongs to the biosynthetic thiolase family and catalyzes the Claisen condensation of two acetyl-CoA molecules through an acetyl-enzyme intermediate. As in homologous thiolases, a conserved Cys-Asn-His-Cys framework is expected to stabilize tetrahedral intermediates via oxyanion–hole interactions (Scheme ). This conserved catalytic logic provides a useful basis for future in vitro application and precursor-pathway engineering.

3-Hydroxy-3-Methylglutaryl-CoA Synthase

HMGS catalyzes the conversion of acetoacetyl-CoA to HMG-CoA and functions as an upstream MVA-pathway enzyme that primarily contributes to precursor supply. In Panax, HMGS is associated with tissue-dependent and stress-responsive metabolic regulation, including root-preferential expression and multicopy organization within spatially coordinated upstream biosynthetic networks. ,−

PnHMGSs contain the characteristic HMG-CoA synthase domain, but direct structural information from Panax is still scarce. Homology modeling of PnHMGS1 (AKP55622.1) based on the Brassica juncea S359A variant (PDB ID: 7CQT) suggests a conserved dimeric architecture and indicates that Cys119, Glu85, and His249 are putative catalytic residues (Figure b), with Cys119 predicted to attack the carbonyl carbon of acetyl-CoA. The model also suggests that substitution of Ser361 with Ala may reduce steric hindrance and improve acetyl-CoA accommodation; this prediction requires experimental validation.

Mechanistically, HMGS proceeds through acetyl-enzyme formation, Claisen condensation with acetoacetyl-CoA, and hydrolytic release of HMG-CoA. Its catalytic core is therefore best described as a nucleophilic Cys coupled to a catalytic Glu and a residue network that stabilizes substrate positioning and transition states, rather than as a simple catalytic triad (Scheme ).

3-Hydroxy-3-Methylglutaryl-CoA Reductase

HMGR is the key rate-limiting enzyme of the MVA pathway and catalyzes the NADPH-dependent reduction of HMG-CoA to mevalonate, thereby controlling isoprenoid precursor supply and flux toward triterpene biosynthesis. , In P. notoginseng, HMGR has been linked to altered terpenoid profiles and increased accumulation of specific ginsenosides such as Re and Rb1, underscoring its value as a central metabolic engineering target.

HMGR is also a highly conserved plant enzyme with potential value for herbicide development. Structural modeling based on Arabidopsis thaliana HMG1 (PDB ID: 7ULI) suggests that the Panaxenzyme (AIX87979.1) may adopt a tetrameric architecture with putative conserved catalytic residues, including Glu252, Lys384, Asn448, and Asp460, corresponding to the active-site network of the Arabidopsis template (Figure c). The model also preserves two HMG-CoA-binding motifs, two NADPH-binding motifs, and two transmembrane segments. , In particular, Lys384 is predicted to be positioned to polarize the carbonyl oxygen of the substrate and the mevaldehyde-like intermediate.

As shown in Scheme , HMGR catalyzes a two-step, four-electron reduction using two molecules of NADPH. The reaction proceeds through a mevaldehyde-like intermediate and is typically organized by a conserved Glu-Lys-Asp-His proton-transfer network in class II HMGRs, suggesting that Panax HMGR follows a similar catalytic strategy.

Mevalonate Kinase and Phosphomevalonate Kinase

MK and PMK catalyze the sequential ATP-dependent phosphorylation of mevalonate and phosphomevalonate, ultimately generating mevalonate-5-pyrophosphate. In P. notoginseng, MK is most highly expressed in roots, whereas PMK is more abundant in flowers and leaves; in P. ginseng, both enzymes are present in multiple copies, reflecting gene duplication commonly observed in upstream triterpenoid pathways. ,

Because MK and PMK are subject to feedback inhibition by downstream metabolites, they are important targets for building terpenoid-producing cell factories. The structural model of PnMK (GenBank: AFN02124.1) from P. notoginseng was generated using recombinant rat MK as a template and predicts a loosely associated dimer with an active site located between two domains (Figure d). By contrast, the predicted PnPMK (AIK21784.1) monomer, modeled using PMK from Cuscuta australis as a template, resembles MK at the fold level (Figure e). In PnMK, Asp204 is predicted to act as the catalytic base, whereas Glu193 together with Mg2+ may stabilize the phosphoryl-transfer environment. ,

Mechanistically, MK catalyzes a Mg2+-dependent in-line transfer of the ATP gamma-phosphate to the C5 hydroxyl of mevalonate (Scheme ). PMK appears to employ similar phosphoryl-transfer chemistry, but substrate binding is thought to promote a more closed conformation that aligns ATP and phosphomevalonate for catalysis (Scheme ). , These enzymes therefore represent promising targets for antifeedback engineering.

Mevalonate Diphosphate Decarboxylase

MVD converts MVAPP to IPP and occupies a critical position at the end of the MVA pathway. In P. ginseng, PgMVD overexpression enhanced PgCAS expression and phytosterol accumulation, while having little effect on total ginsenoside content, suggesting preferential redirection of flux toward the sterol branch. This observation highlights the need to consider branch-pathway balance when engineering upstream flux.

Structural and biochemical studies of Panax MVDs remain limited. Homology modeling of PnMVD (AIK21783.1) based on AthalMDD (PDB ID: 6N10) predicts a dimer, similar to PnMK, with a relatively limited interface between the two monomers (Figure f) and a typical GHMP α/β fold. Putative conserved residues within the catalytic pocket include 168Arg and 312Asp, together with the hydrogen-bonding side chains 163Ser, 165Ser, and 218Ser. Residues 28Lys and 80Arg are predicted to recognize and bind the β-phosphate group of (R)-MVAPP.

Mechanistically, MVD is not a simple decarboxylase. Instead, it couples ATP-dependent activation of the C3 hydroxyl group to decarboxylation and elimination, thereby generating the IPP double bond. Conserved Lys and Asp residues are thought to organize phosphate handling and the associated proton-transfer steps during catalysis (Scheme ).

Isopentenyl Diphosphate Isomerase

The IDI (EC 5.3.3.2) catalyzes the reversible interconversion of IPP and DMAPP, thereby maintaining the C5 precursor ratio required for terpene biosynthesis. In P. notoginseng, PnIDI shows substantial expression in roots, flowers, and leaves, while in P. vietnamensis var. fuscidiscus different IDI copies display tissue-dependent expression, suggesting functional diversification. , Similar partitioning between plastidial and cytosolic isoforms has been demonstrated in Artemisia annua, supporting the broader role of IDI in balancing precursor pools across compartments.

A structural model of PnIDI (AIK21782.1) was constructed using human hIPPI2 (PDB ID: 2i6k) as a template. The model predicts a gated active-site architecture in which the N-terminal α-helix regulates substrate entry and binding-induced conformational changes activate the catalytic site. In Figure g, Ser162, Cys161, Trp273, and the N-terminal α-helix cap are predicted to define a dynamic active-site region in which substrate binding induces local backbone movement and side-chain flipping. This model provides a tentative structural rationale for the gating behavior of type I IDIs.

Mechanistically, type I IDI catalyzes double-bond migration through protonation of the alkene, formation of a carbocation-like intermediate, and deprotonation at the alternate position. This conserved acid–base chemistry is typically supported by Cys/Glu/Tyr catalytic residues together with divalent-metal-assisted active-site organization (Scheme ). ,

Isopentenyl Pyrophosphate Polymerization Pathway

The IPP/DMAPP pool generated by the MVA pathway feeds the isopentenyl pyrophosphate polymerization pathway, which produces longer prenyl diphosphates such as GPP, FPP, and GFPP. In the PPD-type ginsenoside pathway, FPS produces FPP, SS converts two FPP molecules into squalene, and SE oxidizes squalene to 2,3-oxidosqualene, thereby linking C5 precursor supply to triterpene scaffold formation. ,,,,

Farnesyl Pyrophosphate Synthase

The FPS (EC 2.5.1.10) is a trans-1,4-prenyltransferase that catalyzes the condensation of IPP and DMAPP to form FPP, a key branch-point intermediate for sesquiterpenes, sterols, and triterpenoid saponins. Functional studies in Panax indicate that FPS strongly influences metabolite accumulation: overexpression of PjFPS increased total saponin content in P. japonicus, and FPS overexpression combined with CAS silencing enhanced triterpene saponin production in P. notoginseng cells. ,−

FPSs from Panax species are highly conserved and all contain the canonical Asp-rich FARM and SARM motifs harboring the DDxxD sequence that defines the catalytic core. , Structural modeling of PnFPS (Q4JHN6.1) based on EuFPS1 (PDB ID: 7bux) suggests a putative dimeric structure, with each monomer possessing TPT-specific domains containing two Asp-rich motifs (DDIMD and DDYLD) (Figure a), that are predicted to contribute to substrate binding. The side chains of conserved residues 88Tyr and 89Phe protrude between α5 and α6, resulting in a marked bending of α5, which may contribute to dimer formation.

Squalene Synthase

The SS (EC 2.5.1.21) is a bifunctional and rate-limiting enzyme that converts two molecules of FPP into squalene via presqualene diphosphate, using NADPH and Mg2+ in the second step. In Panax, SS expression is inducible by methyl jasmonate, and coexpression of PnHMGR and PnSS can substantially increase total saponin accumulation, underscoring its importance in flux control. ,

PnSS contains several conserved regions involved in the two-stage reaction, including regions I–IV associated with presqualene diphosphate formation and region V involved in the NADPH-dependent reduction step. The catalytic center is formed by a large hydrophobic cavity lined by antiparallel α-helices and enriched in DxxxD motifs; domain V also contains candidate NADPH-binding sequences, including FCAIPQT/VMAM/IATL and downstream V/IKIRK. Most Panax SS proteins share more than 91% sequence similarity with PnSS, supporting strong conservation of this hydrophobic reaction channel. In addition, transmembrane helices place SS at the endoplasmic reticulum, although C-terminal truncation can still yield catalytically competent soluble enzyme. ,

As shown in Scheme , SS converts two FPP molecules to squalene through head-to-head condensation to presqualene diphosphate followed by NADPH-dependent rearrangement/reduction. Its catalytic center therefore functions as an integrated hydrophobic reaction channel supported by conserved Asp, Tyr, and Phe residues rather than by a simple acid–base pair. This mechanistic interpretation is consistent with the region-based functional partitioning described above.

Squalene Epoxidase

The SE (EC 1.14.99.7) is a key rate-limiting enzyme in sterol and triterpenoid saponin biosynthesis that catalyzes the conversion of squalene to 2,3-oxidosqualene using FAD as a cofactor. In P. ginseng, PgSE1 (originally reported as PgSQE1) and PgSE2 (originally reported as PgSQE2) differ in sequence, transmembrane organization, and responsiveness to methyl jasmonate, with PgSE1 appearing to play the dominant role in ginsenoside biosynthesis. Similar functional differentiation has been reported for PnSE1 and PnSE2 in P. notoginseng. In addition, co-overexpression of SE and PAL in hairy roots reduced Rg3 accumulation, illustrating the importance of balancing competing branches during metabolic engineering.

A structural model of PnSE (AGI19257.1) based on human SQLE (PDB ID: 6c6n) suggests that Gln117 and Tyr144 may contribute to activity (Figure b), whereas Tyr281, Ile111, and Glu114 are predicted to contribute to the interaction network around flavin N5 through a water-mediated arrangement. The predicted structure contains a conserved FAD-binding pocket and substrate channel. These features provide a tentative structural explanation for how the FAD pocket is organized for oxygen activation and epoxidation chemistry.

As shown in Scheme , SE is a FAD-dependent monooxygenase in which reduced FAD activates O2 to generate reactive flavin oxygen species, followed by oxygen insertion into the 2,3 double bond of squalene to form the epoxide. Conserved aromatic residues near the FAD pocket are therefore considered important for oxygen activation and epoxidation efficiency.

Cyclization and Hydroxylation of 2,3-Oxidosqualene

The cyclization and hydroxylation of 2,3-oxidosqualene constitute the downstream committed steps of ginsenoside biosynthesis and define the divergence between sterol and triterpenoid branches. Different oxidosqualene cyclases determine the first committed scaffold, while CYPs introduce the oxidation patterns required for specific ginsenoside aglycones.

Dammarenediol-II Synthase

The DDS (EC 4.2.1.125) is the first dedicated cyclase in dammarane-type ginsenoside biosynthesis and catalyzes the conversion of 2,3-oxidosqualene into dammarenediol-II. Functional studies have demonstrated its value for heterologous production, including engineering rice to produce dammarane-type sapogenins and constructing a tobacco-based Rh2 biosynthetic cell factory. ,

DDSs from Panax contain typical oxidosqualene cyclase motifs, including the QW motif, the MWCYCR signature sequence, and the DCTAE substrate-binding motif. , Furthermore, structural modeling of PnDDS (AED99865.1) based on TwOSC (PDB ID: 8j5z) suggests a putative fold divided into an N-terminal region, domain 1, and domain 2 (Figure c). The model highlights conserved cyclase architecture but also predicts residue differences relative to TwOSC, including Val535, Val484, and the Cys731-Lys732 dyad, which may contribute to the distinction between tetracyclic and pentacyclic triterpene formation.

Mechanistically, DDS initiates catalysis by protonating the epoxide of 2,3-oxidosqualene, thereby triggering a multistep cationic cyclization cascade that ultimately yields dammarenediol-II. By analogy with experimentally characterized oxidosqualene cyclases, the Asp residue within the DCTAE motif is proposed to initiate epoxide protonation, whereas the QW motifs may help stabilize the cyclase fold and the high-energy carbocationic cascade. ,

Cytochrome P450

CYPs, especially members of the CYP716 family, are indispensable downstream tailoring enzymes in Panax ginsenoside biosynthesis because they convert common triterpene intermediates into PPD-, PPT-, and oleanolic acid-type backbones. In P. ginseng, CYP716A47 (also termed protopanaxadiol synthase) functions as a dammarenediol-II 12-hydroxylase that converts dammarenediol-II to PPD, whereas CYP716A53v2 functions as a PPD 6-hydroxylase that converts PPD to PPT. , Beyond their catalytic roles, CYPs are also major determinants of chemotypic divergence among Panax species. Comparative omics analyses have linked differential expression of CYP716 members to species-specific ginsenoside profiles, and additional studies have revealed strong environmental, developmental, and tissue-specific regulation of CYP expression. ,,−

A model of PgCYP716A47 (AEY75212) based on CYP725A4 (PDB ID: 8X1W) suggests that the overall fold may be conserved but that many substrate-channel residues differ, which may reflect substrate diversity and product specificity among CYP subfamilies. The homology model (Figure d) is broadly consistent with the AlphaFold prediction (Figure e). The residues corresponding to the template channel positions are largely nonconserved in PgCYP716A47, with only Met123 retained among the compared set. This pattern suggests possible active-site remodeling around a conserved P450 scaffold. Because no substrate-bound high-resolution structure of a Panax CYP716 enzyme is currently available, all residue-level assignments remain putative.

Mechanistically, CYP catalysis follows the classical P450 cycle involving substrate binding, electron transfer, O2 activation, formation of Compound I, and subsequent hydrogen abstraction/oxygen rebound or related oxidative chemistry. Thus, the conserved heme-coordinating cysteine and the I-helix acid–alcohol pair remain central mechanistic features even when residue-level assignments in Panax CYP716 enzymes remain incomplete. Site-directed mutagenesis of predicted substrate-channel residues, kinetic analysis in reconstituted CYP-reductase systems, and substrate-bound structural studies will be required to establish the determinants of regioselectivity in the CYP716 family.

Spatiotemporal Regulation and Putative Membrane-Associated Metabolons

From squalene synthesis onward, several pathway enzymes are associated with the endoplasmic reticulum: SS and SE contain membrane-associated regions, whereas CYP enzymes are membrane anchored and depend on electron transfer from cytochrome P450 reductases. This colocalization may facilitate substrate channeling and reduce diffusion of hydrophobic intermediates. Direct interactions between squalene epoxidases and oxidosqualene cyclases have been demonstrated in another plant triterpene pathway, supporting the plausibility of a membrane-associated metabolon model. However, a stable ginsenoside-biosynthetic metabolon has not yet been isolated from Panax, and its composition, stoichiometry, and dynamic regulation therefore remain hypothetical.

Spatiotemporal regulation is also important for pathway output. Tissue-dependent expression of upstream enzymes, CYPs, and UGTs, together with competition between DDS and cycloartenol synthase for 2,3-oxidosqualene, can redirect carbon toward ginsenosides or sterols. For example, PgMVD overexpression increased sterol accumulation while reducing ginsenoside production, and single-cell or spatial omics studies have revealed cell-type-specific distributions of ginsenoside-pathway transcripts and metabolites. , Future studies should combine spatial transcriptomics and metabolomics with coimmunoprecipitation, proximity labeling, fluorescence-complementation assays, and isotope-resolved flux analysis to test enzyme-complex formation and quantify branch competition.

Cross-Species Differences and Chemotypic Divergence

The available evidence indicates that upstream MVA-pathway enzymes are generally highly conserved among Panax species, whereas differences in paralog expression, branch-point cyclases, CYPs, and UGTs are more likely to contribute to species-specific ginsenoside profiles. P. vietnamensis var. fuscidiscus contains two tissue-differentially expressed IDI genes and the functionally characterized oxidosqualene cyclase PvOSC4, illustrating diversification at both precursor-balancing and scaffold-forming steps. , Nevertheless, quantitative kinetic parameters, experimentally determined structures, and matched expression data remain scarce for P. vietnamensis and several less-studied species. Consequently, current comparisons are largely qualitative, and systematic cross-species assays under identical conditions are needed to distinguish effects of enzyme sequence from transcriptional and cellular regulation.

Glycosyltransferases for the Glycosylation of PPD-Type Ginsenosides

Glycosyltransferases (GTs) are key determinants of the structural diversity and bioactivity of ginsenosides because they catalyze the transfer of sugar moieties onto triterpene aglycones and partially glycosylated intermediates (Figure ). According to CAZy classification, most Panax ginsenoside glycosyltransferases belong to the GT1 family, namely UDP-dependent glycosyltransferases (UGTs), which typically use UDP-glucose and related UDP-sugars as donors. Plant UGTs generally adopt a GT-B fold composed of two Rossmann-like domains (α-helix-β-turn-α-helix), and the N-terminal and C-terminal domains are connected by a flexible loop that forms the catalytic cleft for O-glycosylation reactions. −

UGTs mediates distinct glycosylation modifications at different positions of PPD-type saponins.

UGTs involved in PPD-type ginsenoside biosynthesis in P. ginseng, P. quinquefolius, and P. notoginseng are mainly distributed in the UGT71, UGT74, and UGT94 families. Although these enzymes share the same overall GT-B scaffold, they often show low sequence similarity, and variations in the donor- and acceptor-binding clefts strongly influence regioselectivity, sugar-donor specificity, and product distribution. For example, different sugars attached to the C20-O-Glc group of Rd generate Rb1, Rb2, and Rb3, which may exhibit different biological properties because of their distinct terminal sugar moieties. − And the UGTPj3 and UGTPj20 derived from P. japonicus catalyzed the glycosylation of C3 and C20 positions of PPD aglycone to produce ginsenoside Rh2 and CK, respectively (Figure ). This functional diversity is closely related to the PSPG motif in the C-terminal domain and neighboring residues that influence donor recognition and active-site conformation.

At the mechanistic level, plant UGTs catalyze SN2-like inverting glycosyl transfer, in which the acceptor hydroxyl group is activated and attacks the anomeric carbon of the UDP-sugar donor while UDP leaves simultaneously. This chemistry is typically supported by an N-terminal His–Asp catalytic dyad, whereas the C-terminal PSPG motif primarily governs donor recognition and sugar-donor specificity. In addition, multienzyme cascades combined with UDP-sugar regeneration systems have already shown promise for efficient in vitro production of target ginsenosides such as Rh2. , However, the in vitro system places high demands on enzyme activity and stability. Future work should integrate enzyme engineering, metabolic engineering, and cell-free synthesis platforms to enable efficient large-scale production of target ginsenosides. In the following sections, UGTs from representative Panax species are reviewed to provide deeper insight into their catalytic properties and to guide future engineering and application.

Although the genus Panax contains additional medicinal species, experimentally validated UGTs for PPD-type ginsenoside biosynthesis are concentrated in P. ginseng, P. quinquefolius, and P. notoginseng. Only a few enzymes from P. japonicus have been functionally characterized, whereas comparable biochemical data sets for P. vietnamensis and other species remain limited. Accordingly, the detailed discussion below focuses on the three species for which sufficient substrate, product, and kinetic evidence is available, while explicitly identifying the remaining cross-species knowledge gaps.

UGTs with C3 Position as the Glycosylation Site

As shown in Table , the aglycone backbone of dammarane-type ginsenosides is a tetracyclic triterpenoid with a hydroxyl group at C3 that serves as a key glycosylation site. An eight-carbon alkyl side chain attached at C17 confers substantial lipophilicity. Accordingly, the catalytic pocket of UGTs must accommodate both hydrophilic UDP-sugar donors and the lipophilic aglycone side chain, and the polarity of this microenvironment, together with reaction conditions, strongly influences catalytic efficiency.

UGTs Derived from P. ginseng

Members of the UGT74 family in P. ginseng are mainly responsible for C3–OH glycosylation (Table ). For example, UGTPg45 catalyzes the C3–O-glycosylation of PPD to form Rh2 but showed little or no activity toward PPT under the tested conditions. Recombinant UGTPg45 expressed in E. coli BL21 displayed a catalytic efficiency of 2 mM–1 s–1 for PPD-to-Rh2 conversion at pH 7.5 and 40 °C. Another UGT74 enzyme, PgUGT74AE2, exhibits broader substrate specificity: it converts PPD to Rh2 and also glycosylates CK at C3 to produce F2 (Figure ), with the reported kinetic parameters shown in the source study. PgUGT74AE2 can also glycosylate PPT aglycone at C3 to generate a novel triterpene saponin.

Identified UGTs From Panax ginseng and Panax notoginseng

Sugar-chain elongation at C3 in P. ginseng is mainly catalyzed by the UGT94 family (Table ). UGTPg29 extends a glucose moiety on the C3–O-Glc group of Rh2 and F2 to yield Rg3 and Rd, respectively (Figure ), and its catalytic efficiency toward Rh2 varies markedly with assay conditions. , PgUGT94Q2 catalyzes the same two reactions and differs from UGTPg29 by only one residue (N5 in PgUGT94Q2 versus K5 in UGTPg29), yet it shows distinct kinetic behavior. PgUGT94Q2 also shares 50% sequence identity with GpUGT35 from Gynostemma pentaphyllum, suggesting possible ginsenoside-related activity in this homologue. Another homologue, PgUGT94Q18, displays relatively high specific activity toward Rh2.

UGTs Derived from P. quinquefolius

In P. quinquefolius, Pq3-O-UGT1 (GenBank accession number: KR028477) catalyzes conversion of PPD to ginsenoside Rh2 under mildly alkaline conditions and is highly similar to PgUGT74AE2 and UGTPg45 from P. ginseng. Pq3-O-UGT2, a UGT94 family enzyme, catalyzes sugar-chain elongation at the C3–O-Glc position of Rh2 and F2 to produce Rg3 and Rd, respectively, but is markedly more efficient toward F2 than Rh2. , Structural analysis further showed that the Nα5-Nα6 linker is open in the apo form and moves inward upon substrate binding, indicating that this conformational change is important for substrate accommodation and catalysis.

UGTs Derived from P. notoginseng

Members of the UGT74 family also mediate the initial C3 glycosylation step in P. notoginseng (Table ). Pn1–31 shares 96.2% sequence identity with PgUGT74AE2 and catalyzes C3–OH glycosylation of PPD to yield Rh2. PnUGT74AE1 is likewise highly similar to PnUGT5 and PgUGT74AE2 and catalyzes the same reaction. Among five PnUGT1–5 proteins heterologously expressed in E. coli, all except PnUGT3 were active toward PPD-type ginsenosides. UGTPn17 can glycosylate the C3–OH of PPD, CK, and LXXV to produce Rh2, F2, and XVII, respectively (Figure ). Compared with UGTPg45, UGTPn50 lacks two amino acids yet displays 1.7-fold higher catalytic efficiency toward PPD-to-Rh2 conversion, although the structural basis for this enhancement remains unclear.

As in P. ginseng and P. quinquefolius, C3 sugar-chain elongation in P. notoginseng is mainly catalyzed by UGT94 family members (Table ). Pn3–31 specifically glycosylates the C3–O-Glc position of Rh2 to yield Rg3. Although Pn3–32 and its isoform Pn3–32-i5 share over 90% sequence identity with Pn3–31, they show markedly different substrate specificity. Pn3–32 glycosylates the C20-O-Glc position of Rd to produce Rb1, whereas Pn3–32-i5 glycosylates the C6 position of the PPT-type ginsenoside Rg1 to generate notoginsenoside R1. These differences likely arise from changes in the active-site pocket.

PnUGT2, UGT94C, and UGTPn42 also catalyze C3 sugar-chain elongation, converting Rh2 and F2 into Rg3 and Rd, respectively. , PnUGT33, another UGT94 family member, can extend sugar chains at both C3 and C20, converting Rh2 and CK into Rg3 and LXXV, respectively (Figure ). Under pH 8.0 and 35 °C, its kinetic parameters toward CK were reported as Km = 280.113 μM and kcat = 3.996 × 10–3 s–1. PnUGT33 lacks transmembrane domains, and 265Ser together with residues 17Ala–19Ser and 49Ser–53Arg may contribute to substrate binding and catalysis.

UGTPn87 exhibits especially broad substrate scope. It can add glucose moieties to the C3–O-Glc position of Rh2, XVII, notoginsenoside Fd, and notoginsenoside Fe to form Rg3, Rb1, Rb3, and Rc, respectively (Figure ). It can also convert F2 to Rb1 via two-step glycosylation. In addition, UGTPn87 can transfer xylose to the C3–O-Glc position of F2, XVII, Fd, and Fe, producing ginsenoside R16, notoginsenoside L, Fd-Xyl, and Fe-Xyl, respectively. Further mechanistic analysis of this broad substrate tolerance could provide valuable guidance for substrate-specificity engineering.

UGTs with C20 as the Glycosylation Site

As shown in Table , PPD-type saponins such as CK, Rd, and F2 carry glycosyl groups at C20 and therefore require C20 glycosylation during biosynthesis. The formation of ginsenoside Rb1, a typical tetrasaccharide ginsenoside, involves sequential glycosylation at both C3 and C20. For example, in P. notoginseng, PPD is first glycosylated at C3 by Pn1–31 to form Rh2, then further glycosylated by Pn3–31 to produce Rg3; Pn3–29 subsequently glycosylates the C20 position of Rg3 to yield Rd, and Pn3–32 finally extends the C20 sugar chain of Rd to produce Rb1 (Figure ). Because studies on C20-targeting UGTs from P. quinquefolius remain scarce, the following discussion mainly focuses on P. ginseng and P. notoginseng.

UGTs Derived from P. ginseng

In P. ginseng, C20 glycosylation is mainly catalyzed by UGT71 family members, whereas a few UGT94 enzymes participate in sugar-chain elongation. For example, PgUGT94Q6 extends the sugar chain at the C20 position of Rd to form Rb1 (Table ). UGTPg101 (PgUGT71A55) and UGTPg1 (PgUGT71A53), both UGT71 family members with 98.1% sequence identity, catalyze C20 glycosylation of several PPD-type substrates, including PPD, Rg3, and Rh2, to form CK, Rd, and F2, respectively (Figure ). However, except for PPT aglycone, they do not efficiently glycosylate PPT-type substrates, probably because the C6 sugar group introduces steric hindrance during entry into the catalytic pocket.

UGTPg71A29 (GenBank accession number: MH638345) encodes a 475-amino-acid UGT in which 283Gln maintains the active conformation through hydrophobic interactions, whereas 251Gln forms weaker hydrogen bonds with the substrate; mutation of either residue reduces activity. Despite sharing 96.47% sequence identity with UGTPg1, UGTPg71A29 has acquired the additional ability to extend the C20 sugar chain of Rd to form Rb1 (Figure ).

UGTs Derived from P. notoginseng

Many UGT71 family members from P. notoginseng catalyze C20–OH glycosylation of both PPD- and PPT-type ginsenosides (Table ). For example, Pn3–29 converts PPT and PPD into F1 and CK, respectively, whereas UGTPn35 and UGT71A act on PPT, Rh2, Rg3, and PPD to produce F1, F2, Rd, and CK. , PnUGT71A1 shares 99% sequence identity with PnUGT1, and both glycosylate PPT, Rh2, Rg3, and PPD at C20; however, neither converts Rh1 to Rg1, suggesting that the C6 sugar moiety of Rh1 hinders binding of the C20–OH group to the active site. By contrast, PnUGT3 shares 85% sequence identity with PnUGT71A1 but catalyzes C6–OH glycosylation of PPT and F1 to form Rh1 and Rg1. , PnUGT71A3, which shares 77.5% sequence identity with PnUGT3, catalyzes the same C6 reactions and also glycosylates the C20–OH of Rg3 to yield Rd.

GT95syn, another UGT71 family member from P. notoginseng, catalyzes 20­(R)–OH glycosylation of 20­(R)-Rg3, 20­(R)-PPD, and 20­(R)-Rh2 to produce 20­(R)-Rd, 20­(R)-CK, and 20­(R)-F2, respectively. Sequence comparison indicates that GT95syn differs from UGTPg1 at only nine amino acid positions, which are thought to determine substrate stereospecificity. In addition, UGRdGT catalyzes conversion of Rd to Rb1 with a Km of 0.14 mM under optimal conditions of pH 8.5 and 35 °C and is activated by Mn2+, Ca2+, and Mg2+.

Directed Evolution of UGTs

Research on UGTs involved in PPD-type ginsenoside biosynthesis in Panax species has progressed from simple functional identification to performance optimization and application expansion. For example, an error-prone PCR-derived mutant, Q222H/A322V, strengthened interaction with PPD and thereby improved catalytic efficiency. Guo et al. further used sequence-guided mutagenesis and computational design to obtain mutants with higher UDP-xylose specificity, longer half-life, and greater catalytic efficiency; notably, the combined mutant C140A/P101S/L200C/G255D synergistically enhanced sugar-donor selectivity, thermal stability, and catalytic performance, thereby improving the biosynthetic capacity for ST4. A semirational design strategy based on ΔΔG calculation, Km/Kcat prediction, and substrate-binding analysis also yielded a UGTPg29 R91M/D184M/A287V/A342L mutant with improved stability and catalytic activity.

Protein engineering of SgUGT74AC1 from Siraitia grosvenorii produced a 4.17 × 104-fold increase in catalytic efficiency toward mogroside, a compound structurally similar to ginsenoside Rh2. Inspired by this result, a semirational strategy was used to engineer Pq3-O-UGT2 by targeting residues in the N-terminal acceptor-binding region. The resulting S49R/I50M/H85Y triple mutant showed a 1.30-fold increase in kcat/Km toward Rh2 relative to the wild-type enzyme. Crystal-structure analysis of Pq3-O-UGT2 further showed that 358Leu, 181Leu, 185Phe, and 117Phe form hydrophobic interactions with Rh2. Mutation of 181Leu to Trp increased catalytic efficiency toward Rh2 to 90.17 s–1 mM–1 while decreasing activity toward F2; the double mutant S175A/L181W further reduced activity toward F2 but enhanced activity toward Rh2, indicating that 175Ser and 178Asn influence substrate specificity.

Conclusion

PPD-type ginsenosides are major bioactive constituents of Panax species, and their biosynthesis depends on a coordinated enzyme network that governs precursor supply, triterpene scaffold formation, oxidation, and glycosylation. The core framework of this pathway has now been largely established. AACT, HMGS, HMGR, MK, PMK, MVD, and IDI form the upstream precursor-supplying module; FPS, SS, and SE link precursor generation to oxidosqualene formation; DDS catalyzes the first committed cyclization step; and downstream CYPs and UGTs create structural diversity through oxidation and glycosylation.

Recent studies have moved beyond single-gene cloning toward integrated analyses involving genome annotation, multiomics profiling, structural prediction, catalytic characterization, and metabolic engineering. In particular, detailed studies of UGT function and directed evolution have provided a solid basis for the in vitro synthesis of rare ginsenosides and the development of microbial cell factories for industrial production. Nevertheless, experimentally determined structures remain available for only a small subset of ginsenoside-related enzymes, and most residue-level assignments for Panax proteinsparticularly CYP716 enzymesremain predictions that require direct validation.

Challenges and Prospects

Despite substantial progress, several bottlenecks still constrain efficient biosynthesis of PPD-type ginsenosides in Panax, including competition with sterol and other branch pathways, incomplete understanding of spatiotemporal and feedback regulation, limited structural and biochemical characterization of many enzymes, and the generally modest activity and stability of native biocatalysts, limited evidence for membrane-associated enzyme complexes, and the scarcity of directly comparable kinetic data across Panax species.

Future work should combine CRISPR/Cas or RNAi with multiomics approaches to dissect metabolic flux distribution, suppress competing branches, and establish regulatory networks for key enzymes. In parallel, transferring biosynthetic modules into microbial chassis cells and constructing cell-free multienzyme cascades may help bypass endogenous metabolic interference and improve production efficiency. Advances in cryo-EM, X-ray crystallography, native mass spectrometry, proximity labeling, and AI-assisted structure prediction should be integrated with mutagenesis and kinetic assays to clarify catalytic mechanisms, enzyme-complex organization, and structure–function relationships. On this basis, rational design, directed evolution, and enzyme immobilization should accelerate the development of robust biocatalysts with improved activity, stability, and substrate adaptability, thereby promoting efficient and economical production of target ginsenosides.
