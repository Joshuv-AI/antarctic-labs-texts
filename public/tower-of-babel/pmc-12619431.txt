Effect of preoperative mental health status on spine surgery outcomes: a systematic review
Authors: Tarek Nahle, Mohammad Daher, Ziad Zalaquett, Marven Aoun, Marc Moussallem, Remy Daou, Alan H Daniels, Amer Sebaaly
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecent data reveals an association between preoperative mental health and postoperative outcomes in spine surgery. Patients with poor mental health often experience less favorable recovery trajectories, though long-term quality of life improvements may still be achieved. Understanding this relationship is essential for optimizing surgical planning and patient care.Recent findingsSeveral studies have shown a clear correlation between poor preoperative mental health and poor postoperative outcomes in adult spinal deformity, spinal degenerative disease and cervical spine surgery. Furthermore, treating preoperative depression was shown to result in better outcomes in depressed patients, pointing out the importance of evaluating and treating psychiatric comorbidities before performing the surgery. To do so, the EuroQol-5D (EQ-5D) demonstrated its potential for application in identifying psychological discomfort in spine surgery patients.SummaryPreoperative mental health status plays a critical role in predicting postoperative recovery and satisfaction in spine surgery. Routine screening and treatment of psychiatric comorbidities should be incorporated into preoperative assessment to enhance surgical outcomes and overall patient well-being.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03303-y.

Introduction

Spinal surgery is growing, demonstrating a 2.4-fold increase in prevalence over the past 16 years [1]. For instance, it is widely used in managing spinal deformity [2], trauma, infections, and lumbar spinal stenosis, which is the most common cause of spinal surgery in patients over 65 years of age [3]. Among many of the factors that can influence the natural history of spinal diseases, psychological factors were shown to play an important role in the onset, maintenance, and treatment of chronic back pain [4]. Moreover, the role of mental health scores in affecting surgical outcomes should be considered. Notably, individuals with a low baseline mental health score (LMH) tend to experience more challenging postsurgical outcomes compared to those with a high baseline mental health score (HMH). Interestingly, despite these initial differences, the ultimate endpoint of Health-Related Quality of Life (HRQOL) remains consistent, with both groups reporting similar scores [5, 6]. This observation underscores the complex interplay between mental health and surgical outcomes. Because of this potential contribution, we hypothesized that mental health status could potentially affect the result of these operations. Therefore, the goal of this article is to review the effect of preoperative mental health status on all types of spine surgery outcomes.

Material and methods

Search strategy

The PRISMA standards were followed in this investigation to study the impact of mental health on spine surgery. PubMed, Google Scholar (page 1–20), and Embase were queried till December 2024 in search of the qualified papers. A combination of the keywords “mental health”, “depression”, “anxiety”, “spine surgery”, “spinal surgery”, and “fusion” was used in all 3 databases. Reference lists from papers and online searches were also used to look up the literature. One author extracted the data, while another verified the article selection.

Inclusion criteria were randomized controlled trials, prospective clinical trials, retrospective studies, and case series about patients with mental health issues and undergoing spine surgery. The studies with the following characteristics were excluded from this study: case reports, narrative or systematic reviews, theoretical research, conference report, expert comment, economic analysis and studies with non-relevant outcomes.

Data extraction

Eligibility of the studies was determined by two reviewers independently with an agreement rate of 0.9. Extraction of the analyzed data was made from the included studies. The extracted data included the basic information such as the authors, title, year of publication, journal, study design, sample size, prevalence of the injuries, their types, and its possible risk factors. Any arising difference between the investigators was resolved by discussion.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias was assessed by two authors independently by means of the ROBINS-I tool for assessing risk of bias in non-randomized studies of interventions, excluding studies with a critical risk of bias.

Results

Study inclusion

The database search (PubMed, Embase, Google Scholar pages 1–20; through December 2024) identified 3,386 records. After de-duplication and screening per predefined criteria, non-eligible items (e.g., case reports, editorials, economic analyses, reviews) were excluded. Full texts were assessed by two independent reviewers with discrepancies resolved by discussion. Nineteen primary studies met inclusion criteria and were synthesized qualitatively.

Factors affecting spine surgery patients’ mental health

Two main preoperative factors seem to impact mental health preoperatively and thus can potentially impact the surgical outcome of spine surgery. A nonmodifiable factor such as gender was seen to be related to preoperative mental health. Women were shown to be more likely to have lower preoperative HRQOL mental health scores due to lower pain tolerance than men [7]. Furthermore, while HRQOL improved postoperatively in both males and females, males showed a significant reduction in disability and showed a closer correlation between HRQOL and disability (Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) Low Back Pain [8]. In addition, spinal deformity can be by itself an isolated cause of mental health deterioration. Schwab et al. [9] demonstrated worse SF-36 scores in adult spinal deformity patients when compared to the general US population. Another study by Diebo et al. [10] reported the comorbid mental health disorders in such patients, showing that patients undergoing four levels or more of lumbar spine fusion had at least one psychological comorbidity.

Mental health in surgery

Adult spinal deformity surgery (ASD)(Table 1)

Several studies assessed the effect of mental health on ASD. First of all, it was shown that patients with higher preoperative expectations for back pain relief reported less pain 2 years after ASD surgery than patients with low preoperative expectations [11]. In fact, both the numeric rating scale (NRS) and Scoliosis Research Society–22r (SRS-22r) were better in the high expectation group [11]. Conversely, it was shown by Sebaaly et al. [12] in their study in a Lebanese population that patients’ satisfaction was inversely correlated to their own expectations, and positively correlated with the presence of pre-operative radicular pain. Another pre-operative factor to consider is resilience. As for the impact of pre-operative mental health, when poor, it was associated with worse postoperative HRQOL in ASD [15]. Moreover, the post-operative satisfaction levels of patients undergoing ASD surgery were shown to be lower in patients with a low baseline mental component score (MCS) [16]. On the other hand, higher preoperative MCS predicted lower postoperative pain after spinal fusion surgery [17]. Furthermore, given the fact that one of spinal deformities’ burdens is the patient’s own perception of external morphological esthetics, it was shown that mental health status, in addition to spinopelvic alignment, correlate with self-image in patients with ASD before and after corrective surgery [18].

Spinal degenerative diseases (Table 2)

In lumbar spinal stenosis, low preoperative MCS-12 was correlated with lower improvement in ODI and VAS leg pain after lumbar fusion [19]. Furthermore, pre-operative SF-36 was shown to correlate with post-operative ODI after lumbar spinal stenosis surgery [32]. Moreover, the short form mental component score (SF-12), was demonstrated to be an independent predictor for extended length of stay after Anterior Lumbar Interbody Fusion [23] and patients with low SF-12 had increased odds of reporting higher NRS leg pain post-operatively [22]. In minimally invasive transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (MiTLIF), patients with SF-12 MCS < 50 had no long term improvement at 2 years after the procedure in the VAS back and leg, ODI, and PROMIS-PF [33]. In addition, Urban-Baeza et al. showed that patients with depression had worse pain and disability post-operatively when compared to patients with no depression [26]. Those results were reinforced by the fact that when the depression was treated pre-operatively, the post-operative improvement was similar compared to the depression free group [26]. In addition, depression was independently associated with patient dissatisfaction 2 years post-operatively [24]. On the other hand, Mayo et al. demonstrated that although a better preoperative mental health status was associated with lower postoperative perceived disability, it was not a predictive component of patient reported outcomes at any point in the timeline after MiTLIF [25]. Concerning cervical spinal surgery, a low postoperative mental state was demonstrated to be a risk factor for postoperative axial neck pain after controlling for radiographic parameters [31]. However, pre-operative low mental state was not shown to be correlated with it [31]. This finding was contrasted by Skeppholm et al., reporting worse outcomes after surgery for cervical radiculopathy in patients with preoperative hospital anxiety and depression (HAD) [28]. Additionally, psychiatric pathology can also affect the outcomes of the surgery, as shown by Tetreault et al. reporting that depression and bipolar disorder have a more modest functional quality of life improvement, assessed by comparing the Neck Disability Index (NDI) and MCS, after degenerative cervical myelopathy surgery when compared to individuals who do not have psychiatric comorbidities [29]. Furthermore, psychiatric disorders were shown to be associated with increased risk for perioperative adverse events and increased post-hospitalization care [30]. Moreover, depression, anxiety, and sleep disturbance were shown to be a significant predictor of any post-operative complication [35]. It was also shown that the proportion of patients reporting satisfaction with cervical spine surgery was lower in patients with low MCS compared to High MCS patients [36].

Discussion

This review highlights the significant impact of preoperative mental health status on postoperative outcomes in spine surgery. Across adult spinal deformity, degenerative disease, and cervical procedures, poor baseline mental health was consistently associated with worse health-related quality of life (HRQOL), increased pain, greater disability, and lower satisfaction after surgery. The adopted biopsychosocial model of mental health makes the baseline mood status of the patients influenceable by several biologic, psychological, and socioenvironmental factors surrounding the individual. This framing helps contextualize why mental health interacts with symptom burden, perception of pain, and recovery trajectories. Although poor baseline mental health predicted slower and more challenging postoperative recovery, long-term improvements in HRQOL were still achievable, suggesting mental health is a modifiable risk factor rather than a contraindication to surgery.

The EuroQol-5D (EQ-5D) emerged as a practical tool for detecting psychological distress in surgical patients, particularly the anxiety/depression domain, while also providing a global self-rated health score via the visual analogue scale. Incorporating the EQ-5D alongside validated tools such as the PHQ-9 or SF-12 may provide a more complete profile of preoperative well-being and better predict recovery. Standardizing such assessments in preoperative pathways could support shared decision-making, improve patient counseling, and identify individuals who may benefit from targeted psychological support prior to surgery.

Since mental health has been shown to be a potential predictive factor for postoperative outcomes, physicians are encouraged to assess patients’ mental health status pre-operatively. In practice, many patients presenting for complex spine procedures have at least one psychological condition, yet presurgical psychological screening remains underused; validated instruments (EQ-5D anxiety/depression, PHQ-9, SF-12/VR-12 MCS) show good concordance with established distress measures and are feasible to deploy.

Therefore, considering the substantial influence of preoperative mental health on the success of spine surgery, it is advisable for healthcare providers to routinely assess the mental health status of patients before these procedures. By taking this comprehensive approach to patient care, healthcare providers can ensure not only better surgical outcomes but also a more positive overall experience for their patients. This approach reflects a commitment to addressing both the physical and emotional aspects of patient health, ultimately leading to improved results and satisfaction for those undergoing spinal surgery.

Consequently, we recommend universally screening any patient undergoing spine surgery with the EQ-5D prior to performing the procedure, with the possibility of using the SF-36 (or alternatively the SF-12) in spinal degenerative disease surgery, and any of the SF-12 MCS, PHQ-9, or VR-12 MCS for cervical spine. Future research should prioritize prospective, multicenter studies with standardized mental-health instruments (including EQ-5D) to clarify causal pathways and identify effective prehabilitation or treatment strategies. Integrating mental-health professionals within spine care teams may further optimize perioperative management and long-term outcomes.

Conclusion

This investigation highlights the important role of preoperative mental health in affecting the outcomes of spine surgery. Despite the conflicting results, it’s becoming clearer that a patient’s pre-operative mental well-being is closely linked to their post-operative recovery. Thus, underlining the importance of including a preoperative mental health evaluation and, if possible medical management in patients undergoing spine surgery.
