Rheo-Impedance Measurements for the Evaluation of Thermoresponsive Polymer Gels
Authors: Isao Shitanda, Haruna Tsunegi, Yuichi Takasaki, Noya Loew, Yoshifumi Yamagata, Keisuke Miyamoto, Kazutake Takada, Hikari Watanabe, Masayuki Itagaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Although various thermoresponsive properties of poly­(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM) hydrogels have been investigated during phase transitions, the effects of the changing gel structure on the electrochemical properties of PNIPAM gels are poorly understood. Therefore, this study uses rheo-impedance measurements under simultaneous temperature modulation and shear deformation to examine the mechanism governing the conductivity of these gels. The impedance spectra of the PNIPAM hydrogels exhibit a distinct transition near the lower critical solution temperature, indicating that the internal conductive pathways dynamically reconstruct, associated with gel deswelling. Meanwhile, the nonlinear behavior of the impedance response with the varying shear rate suggests shear-induced degradation of the gel structure, followed by conductive network reformation. These results highlight the strong correlation between the internal microstructure of the hydrogel and its electrochemical properties, thus demonstrating the effectiveness of rheo-impedance analysis in determining the coupled mechanical–electrical responses of stimuli-responsive soft materials. Unlike conventional static measurements, this approach enables dynamic in situ evaluation of functional transitions within hydrogels. This study provides new insights into the design and analysis of intelligent hydrogel systems, with potential implications for soft actuators, and establishes a methodological foundation for extending rheo-impedance analysis to other responsive polymer systems, thereby broadening its applicability in smart material research.

Introduction

Recent years have seen significant advances in materials technology, such as smart materials that respond reversibly to environmental changes. − Most smart materials are polymers with expected applications in aqueous media. Such smart polymers form expanded three-dimensional (3D) solvent-retaining structures, similar to those of natural fibers. Smart materials are used in various biomedical applications, including as carriers in drug delivery systems and artificial muscles. −

Poly­(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM) gels exhibit temperature responsiveness, − with a positive thermosensitivity and the existence of a lower critical solution temperature (LCST). − The LCST is the temperature at which the polymer in solution undergoes a phase transition from a soluble random coil phase to an insoluble globule phase, which is determined by the balance of hydrophilic and hydrophobic groups in the polymer chains (Figure ). In PNIPAM, the amide groups are hydrophilic, and the isopropyl groups are hydrophobic. The hydrophilic amide groups enhance the affinity of PNIPAM for water at low temperature; however, as the temperature increases, hydrogen bonding weakens, while hydrophobic interactions between the hydrophobic groups strengthen, which together result in a volume phase transition (VPT). To control the LCST, the balance between the hydrophilic and hydrophobic groups can be adjusted through copolymerization with other monomers, whereas regulating the pH can modulate the LCST of polymers bearing ionic groups. The LCST and VPT temperatures of PNIPAM are typically 32 and 34 °C, respectively; thus, PNIPAM is suitable for biomedical applications because these temperatures are close to that of the human body, which enables phase transitions under physiological conditions. ,

Schematic of the phase transition of a PNIPAM gel from hydrophilic to hydrophobic at the LCST. PNIPAM can be transported as a swollen gel carrying active ingredients within it, which are released upon deswelling. It is thus expected to serve as a carrier in drug delivery systems.

PNIPAM gel has potential as a biomaterial with significant medical and engineering applications; however, extensive swelling of the gel in aqueous media decreases its polymer chain density and mechanical strength. Additionally, the application of PNIPAM gel in drug delivery is hampered by its insufficient drug-loading capacity and immediate drug release. The copolymerization and grafting of PNIPAM with various monomers and polymers can address these issues and have therefore attracted considerable attention in pharmaceutical research.

The LCST of PNIPAM typically decreases upon copolymerization with hydrophobic polymers and increases upon copolymerization with hydrophilic polymers. Among various copolymer systems, PNIPAM-co-acrylic acid (AA) microgels display distinctive pH-responsive behavior: at pH values above 4.25, the deprotonation of AA moieties (pK a ≈ 4.25) induces negative charges within the network, leading to osmotic swelling via Coulombic repulsion. , Conversely, the incorporation of polycationic polymers results in charge neutralization and cross-linking within the microgels, causing depuffing through electrostatic interactions. Building on such dual responsiveness, Schilli et al. successfully synthesized PNIPAM–poly­(acrylic acid) (PAA) block copolymers using reversible addition–fragmentation chain transfer (RAFT) polymerization, which enabled the synergistic control of structural transitions by both temperature and pH.

Beyond fundamental studies, PNIPAM-based systems have been explored in biomedical applications. For instance, Lin et al. developed PNIPAM nanogels with an LCST near 40 °C as the dispersed phase in a triphasic composite material for wound healing. This system enabled the staged release of anti-inflammatory agents and growth factors, thereby promoting both the inflammatory and tissue regeneration stages and achieving high therapeutic efficacy in animal models. Beyond drug delivery, PNIPAM gels have been investigated as multifunctional platforms across various fields. Research has focused not only on improving their mechanical strength and controlling their phase transitions but also on imparting them with electrical conductivity. Etika et al. incorporated PNIPAM with single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWNTs) and montmorillonite, which yielded a stable aqueous suspension. The enhanced dispersion of SWNTs and subsequent gelation increased the storage modulus, reflecting improved mechanical reinforcement.

The advances summarized above underline the versatility of PNIPAM-based systems and highlight their potential as stimuli-responsive materials for applications ranging from drug delivery to advanced functional composites. In addition, PNIPAM gels have been extensively characterized using a variety of techniques, including differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), dynamic light scattering, spectroscopic methods, and mechanical testing. However, despite this extensive research, the conductive mechanisms operating during the phase transitions and the electrochemical properties of PNIPAM gels are poorly understood. Furthermore, no studies have observed microscopic structural changes in situ within the gel under shear stress conditions relevant in real-world environments or variations in electrical conductivity within the gel arising from macroscopic structural changes during phase transitions. Changes in the gel structure are expected to alter the ion migration pathways, which would in turn affect drug diffusion processes within gels under shear stress in biological environments or drug release processes from gels in drug delivery systems. Thus, a thorough understanding of these structural changes is expected to help develop future applications.

To that end, this study simultaneously evaluated the electrochemical impedance and rheological properties of a material under shear stress by evaluating the gel structure using rheo-impedance measurements. This technique enables the dynamic and nondestructive observation of internal structural changes in dispersed systems; thus, rheo-impedance is gaining attention as a new analytical tool for visualizing and quantitatively evaluating the dispersion states and network structures of conductive nanomaterials and high-concentration slurries. For example, Shitanda et al. used rheo-impedance measurements to evaluate the dispersibility of carbon slurries used to fabricate the electrodes in lithium-ion batteries and fuel cells;=the resulting Nyquist plots captured the effect of the MC concentration on changes in the interparticle contact resistance and bulk resistance at different shear rates. Ishikawa et al. evaluated the sintering of copper–nickel-based conductive inks using an in situ method combining rheo-impedance and thermogravimetric–differential thermal analysis, which allowed monitoring the formation of conductive pathways and oxidation behavior in real time during sintering. Adding Ni was found to improve the oxidation resistance of the inks. Mehrazi et al. quantified the interactions between ionomers and carbon in inks for preparing fuel cell catalysts using rheo-impedance, evaluating the network formation and changes in conductivity. This approach facilitated the development of electrodes with a low Pt content by optimizing the three-phase boundary. Meanwhile, Yamagata et al. simultaneously measured the changes in the elastic modulus (G′) and impedance during the gelation of a gelatin solution, thereby identifying the gelation point and its relationship with the molecular weight. Similarly, Yamagata et al. observed a correlation between the length and impedance of cellulose nanofibers prepared by mechanical processing, demonstrating that the impedance response was independent of the shear rate but depended on the nanostructure. Their study demonstrated the utility of this method as a nondestructive technique for determining the fiber length. Using rheo-impedance and optical observations, Al-Shaibah et al. revealed that various aggregation mechanisms were in operation near the critical concentration (4 wt %) in carbon black dispersions. Changes in the conductivity associated with microstructural changes under flow conditions were also observed, which was expected to provide a basis for electrode design.

In a similar vein, the current study aimed to quantitatively evaluate the changes in the internal conductive pathways associated with the phase transition behavior of PNIPAM gels under shear flow conditions using rheo-impedance measurements. The structural changes and phase transition points of PNIPAM gels are conventionally evaluated using DSC and swelling rate measurements; however, these methods do not reliably capture the dynamic behavior under flow conditions. Therefore, this study simultaneously measured the AC impedance and rheological properties under various temperatures and shear stress conditions to elucidate the correlation between the reconstruction process of the gel structure near the phase transition temperature and the formation of conductive pathways. Furthermore, equivalent circuit models were proposed by evaluating the structural changes using small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) under the same conditions while applying shear stress, termed rheo-SAXS. In addition, the dependence of the gel structure on the magnitude of the applied shear stress was investigated. This is the first application of rheo-impedance to analyze PNIPAM gels, and the findings are expected to be highly useful for understanding the functional mechanisms of temperature-responsive polymer gels under flow conditions.

Experimental Section

PNIPAM was synthesized by mixing the monomer N-isopropylacrylamide (NIPAM, 0.45 mol/L), cross-linking agent N,N′-methylenebis­(acrylamide) (MBAA, 0.016 mol/L), polymerization initiator ammonium thiosulfate (ATS, 0.018 mol/L), and polymerization accelerator N,N,N′,N′-tetramethylethylenediamine (TMEDA, 0.047 mol/L). All reagents were acquired from Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation. The solvent is ultrapure water. The mixture was then cooled for 2 h and gelated in a mold to form a coin-shaped gel with dimensions of Ø40 mm × 1.5 mm. The reaction temperature was set at room temperature, 25 °C.

The phase-transition behavior of the PNIPAM gel was evaluated by performing rheo-impedance measurements using the apparatus shown in Figure . In our previous studies, we used a stainless steel (SUS) sample stage and parallel Ti plates; however, the low electrical conductivities of SUS and Ti increase the contact resistance and reduce the accuracy of the impedance measurements. This study therefore adopted gold-plated components, including the fixture, the shaft portion of the fixture, and the sample stage, which significantly decreased the contact resistance owing to the excellent electrical conductivity of gold.

Rheo-impedance measurement apparatus.

Rheological and electrochemical impedance measurements were performed simultaneously by electrically connecting a rheometer (Anton Paar, MCR102) to a frequency response analyzer ((FRA)-integrated potentiostat Bio Logic, SP-200) using gold springs (Anton Paar) as fixtures. The use of springs enabled performing measurements in vibration mode without introducing noise into the wiring.

The rheometer measurement conditions were as follows: shear strain of 1%, vibration frequency of 1 Hz, temperature change from 20 to 50 °C at a rate of 70 °C/s, followed by a 210 s hold, and then cooling to 20 °C at the same rate. The sample thickness and gap between the fixture and plate were both 1.5 mm, and one measurement was taken every 10 s. The potentiostat measurement conditions were as follows: an initial potential of 0 V, a frequency range of 7 MHz to 100 mHz, a potential amplitude of 10 mV, and a logarithmic sweep of one digit over five points. Samples were characterized at shear strains of 1, 3, 5, 8, 10, 15, and 20% under otherwise identical conditions. The shear strain measurement is illustrated schematically in Figure S1.

Rheo-SAXS measurements were performed using an apparatus combining an Anton Paar SAXSPoint 5.0 with a DSR 502 dynamic viscoelastic measurement device based on an MCR rheometer to observe the structural changes in the prepared gel. The samples of the gel solution (8 mL) had the same compositions as those analyzed using the rheo-impedance device and were transferred to a measurement cup prior to analysis (Figure S2). The SAXS measurements were performed using a Cu Kα X-ray source, with a measurement time of 15 min and a distance of 500 mm between the sample and detector. Rheometry was performed at 25 and 40 °C at a heating rate of 60 °C/s, a constant shear strain of 1%, and a frequency of 1 Hz under an applied shear force.

Results and Discussion

The rheo-impedance of the PNIPAM gel was measured as a function of temperature, and the resulting 3D Nyquist plot is shown in Figure . During the heating process, a new capacitive semicircle appeared on the high-frequency side. Dividing the Nyquist plots into 5 °C intervals shows that blocking behavior occurred during heating between 20 and 30 °C, and a capacitive semicircle appeared on the high-frequency side between 35 and 50 °C, which became larger with increasing temperature (Figure (a)). During the cooling process, the capacitive semicircle gradually decreased, disappearing below 30 °C (Figure (b)). These observations confirm the temperature dependence of the impedance.

This fitting was based on the contribution of each equation owing to the large number of parameters, with a particular focus on the slope on the small-angle side to determine the macroscopic structure. As shown in Figure , the contribution of the Ornstein–Zernike function increased at 25 °C, indicating density fluctuations caused by the emergence of a network structure within the gel. At 40 °C, the contribution of the Debye–Bucher function increased, suggesting that an inhomogeneous biphasic system similar to a phase-separated structure formed owing to contraction of the gel. ,

An equivalent circuit analysis was performed based on the above results. The proposed equivalent circuit is shown in Figure .

Proposed equivalent circuits for the swollen and contracted gel structure.

Before the phase transition, the system is represented by a parallel circuit consisting of elements corresponding to the solution resistance (R sol) within the gel, the solution resistance (R ct) at the gel/electrode interface, and the electric double-layer capacitance (C dl). Upon phase transition, we propose a new parallel circuit represented by the gel resistance (R gel) within the hydrophobic domains and the capacitance of the gel (C gel), which is in series with the initial circuit. The structural causes of R gel and C gel are illustrated in Figure . Upon heating above 35 °C, the gel contracts during the phase transition, forming hydrophobic domains (Figure , top right). Pathways for ion and electron conduction form between the hydrophobic domains and the electrolyte, which are expressed by the new parallel components in the equivalent circuit representing the electrolyte capacitance and electrolyte resistance (Figure , bottom right). R gel is the resistance associated with ion movement, and C gel is the capacitance arising from charge accumulation; however, the obtained spectra were distorted owing to the influence of the current-line distribution. Accordingly, based on the rheo-SAXS measurements, the spectra were corrected by substituting the C components with constant phase elements (CPEs) during the fitting. Thus, in Figure , the electric double layer capacitance C dl and the gel capacitance C gel are denoted as CPEdl and CPEgel, respectively.

Next, rheo-impedance measurements were performed while varying the magnitude of the shear strain at various temperatures. The spectra obtained at 40, 45, and 50 °C were fitted using the equivalent circuit model described above, and R gel was plotted as a function of the shear strain at each temperature (Figure ).

R gel as a function of shear strain at 40, 45, and 50 °C (left to right) (n = 3, error bars added using Student’s t test with a 90% confidence interval).

Figure shows that as the strain increased, R gel decreased, increased, and then decreased again. The structural changes giving rise to this behavior are shown in Figure . At small strains of 1–5%, the electrolyte solution was expelled from the hydrophobic domains by shear stress, increasing the proportion of the electrolyte solution. This increased the number of ion conduction pathways, thereby reducing R gel. At moderate strains of 5–10%, as the shear stress further increased, the electrolyte solution was expelled from the gel interior to the exterior, thus reducing the total volume of the electrolyte solution within the gel, which inhibited ion movement and increased R gel. At large strains of 10–20%, the shear stress degraded the hydrophobic domains, which changed the gel structure. Gaps formed between the chains and domains within the gel, altering the conductive pathways (Figure , right). In this case, the number of hydrophobic domains hindering ion movement within the gel decreased, thereby reducing R gel.

Structural changes in the gel depending on the magnitude of shear strain.

Such nonmonotonic shear-rate-dependent changes in conductivity have been observed in conductive dispersion and gelation systems, and structural relaxation and reconfiguration processes influence the electrical conductivity characteristics of such materials. , Similarly, the flow history of PNIPAM gels affects the topology of conductive pathways, and rheo-impedance measurements have proven effective in providing a quantitative understanding of these dynamics. The findings of this study demonstrate that the temperature-responsive behavior of PNIPAM gels is not limited to a simple swelling–shrinking phase transition phenomenon but is also strongly influenced by the internal conductive network structure, providing a new perspective that will facilitate the design of functional gel materials. In particular, elucidating the interaction between the nonlinear changes in the impedance characteristics associated with phase transitions and shear responsiveness has implications for the design of PNIPAM-based sensors, soft actuators, and bioelectronic devices designed to operate in dynamic environments.

Although PNIPAM gels have been applied in drug delivery, cell scaffolds, and microactuators, functional control over the formation of conductive pathways within their internal structures has not been sufficiently investigated. The current rheo-impedance method provides a means to dynamically and nondestructively evaluate structure–function correlations and therefore represents a foundational technology for the functionalization of next-generation temperature-responsive materials. In addition, this method can be extended to other stimulus-responsive polymers (hydrogen bonding, pH, light response, etc.), thereby potentially establishing a versatile analytical platform.

Conclusions

A novel rheo-impedance spectroscopy method was introduced to analyze the phase transition behavior of a temperature-responsive PNIPAM gel. Nyquist plots obtained from the rheo-impedance measurements of PNIPAM gels at various temperatures revealed a new capacitive semicircle on the high-frequency side above 35 °C, which was higher than the phase transition temperature during both heating and cooling. Furthermore, simultaneous measurement of the elastic modulus suggested that the appearance and disappearance of the semicircle was attributable to a phase transition.

The gel structure was evaluated using rheo-SAXS measurements to determine the phase transition behavior of the gel. Fitting the scattering curves with various equations suggested that the gel network was in a fluctuating state before the phase transition but adopted an inhomogeneous phase-separated structure consisting of hydrophilic and hydrophobic domains after the phase transition.

An equivalent circuit analysis showed that the gel impedance could be represented by resistance and capacitance components associated with ion movement within the network structure prior to the phase transition. By contrast, after the phase transition, the hydrophobic domains formed by gel contraction introduced insulating properties, leading to the accumulation of charge and the emergence of additional resistance and capacitance.

Furthermore, the influence of the magnitude of the applied shear strain during the rheo-impedance measurements suggested that changes in the internal resistance occurred within the gel, enabling the detailed monitoring of changes that are not visible through rheological measurements alone. This study demonstrated the use of rheo-impedance measurements to evaluate the phase transition and conductive behavior of PNIPAM gels under shear stress, thereby affording insights that can be used to control the phase transition behavior of PNIPAM gels.
